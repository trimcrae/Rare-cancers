## Background
Lung ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI) is a major contributor to primary graft dysfunction (PGD) after lung transplantation. Mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) have emerged as promising therapeutic agents in inflammatory diseases by ameliorating tissue damage and promoting repair. However, the anti-inflammatory efficacy of these approaches and the underlying mechanisms in lung ischemia–reperfusion injury remain incompletely understood.

## Methods
The protective effects of mesenchymal stromal cell-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) against lung ischemia–reperfusion injury were evaluated using two delivery approaches, inhalation and intravenous injection. Both in vivo and in vitro models were employed to assess the biological activity of MSC-EVs and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms. In addition, a rat orthotopic lung transplantation (OLT) model was established to further examine the translational relevance of MSC-EVs.

## Results
MSC–EVs treatment significantly ameliorated lung IRI, with inhalation showing superior efficacy over intravenous delivery. Mechanistically, miR-22-3p within MSC-EVs targeted macrophage NLRP3, suppressing activation of the NLRP3/Caspase-1/IL-1β pathway and promoting M2 polarization. The protective efficacy was confirmed in a clinically relevant rat OLT model, underscoring their translational potential

## Conclusions
Our findings indicate that inhaled MSC-derived extracellular vesicles attenuate lung ischemia–reperfusion injury by promoting macrophage polarization via the miR-22-3p/NLRP3/IL-1β pathway, supporting their potential as a cell-free therapeutic approach to mitigate primary graft dysfunction after lung transplantation.

## Introduction
Lung ischemia - reperfusion injury (IRI) is a pivotal pathological process underlying the development of primary graft dysfunction (PGD), which remains the leading cause of early morbidity and mortality following lung transplantation [ , ]. PGD typically manifests within 72 h post-transplant as diffuse alveolar damage, severe hypoxemia, and pulmonary edema, and remains a major obstacle to improved post-transplant outcomes [ ]. Mechanistically, lung IRI is initiated during the ischemic phase by metabolic derangements, which are further exacerbated upon reperfusion by a burst of oxidative stress, inflammatory mediator release, and immune cell infiltration [ ]. Despite decades of investigation, no effective pharmacological agents are currently available to prevent or treat lung IRI in the transplant setting.
Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)-based therapies have emerged as promising candidates for mitigating IRI, owing to their potent immunomodulatory properties, capacity to promote tissue repair, and favorable safety profile observed in early-phase clinical trials [ – ]. Increasing attention has focused on MSC-derived extracellular vesicles, which are nanosized lipid bilayer vesicles enriched in proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, and which mediate much of the paracrine activity of MSCs [ , ]. Compared with direct MSCs administration, MSC-EVs offer several advantages: they retain MSCs bioactivity without the risk of uncontrolled differentiation, display superior tissue penetration due to their small size, and exhibit low immunogenicity, rendering them particularly suitable for allogeneic applications [ – ]. Recent studies have demonstrated that MSC-EVs exert tissue-protective and reparative effects by transferring bioactive cargoes such as microRNAs (miRNAs) or mitochondria and modulating immune cell function, thereby alleviating injury and promoting regeneration [ , – ]. Beyond identifying the therapeutic cargoes of EVs, it is essential to elucidate the mechanisms by which they exert therapeutic benefits, including the regulation of immune responses, mitigation of oxidative stress, and promotion of tissue regeneration. However, the role and mechanisms of MSC-EVs in lung IRI remain poorly defined [ ]. Clarifying these effects is crucial for the potential application of MSC-EVs in alleviating PGD after lung transplantation.
Given the central role of immune responses in IRI, macrophages have attracted particular attention as key regulators of inflammation [ ]. Among the diverse immune cell populations involved, macrophages orchestrate the inflammatory cascade during IRI. In this setting, macrophages predominantly polarize toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, secreting cytokines that exacerbate vascular permeability and damage to endothelial and epithelial cells [ , ]. Accordingly, therapeutic strategies that shift the macrophage balance from the M1 toward the M2 phenotype, thereby enhancing anti-inflammatory and reparative functions, may offer substantial benefits in lung IRI.
Here, we investigated the therapeutic effects of MSC-EVs in lung IRI using two delivery routes: inhalation and intravenous injection. MSC-EVs significantly attenuated lung IRI in a murine left hilar clamp model, as evidenced by reduced pulmonary damage, diminished inflammatory responses, and improved lung function. Importantly, MSC-EVs were found to promote macrophage polarization from the M1 toward the M2 phenotype. Mechanistically, we identify microRNA-22-3p (miR-22-3p) as a critical mediator within MSC-EVs that directly targets NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3), thereby suppressing activation of the NLRP3/Caspase-1/interleukin-1β (IL-1β) pathway and facilitating M2 polarization. Furthermore, the protective efficacy of MSC-EVs was confirmed in a clinically relevant rat orthotopic lung transplantation (OLT) model, underscoring their preclinical and translational relevance. Collectively, this study provides novel mechanistic insights into the therapeutic potential of MSC-EVs in lung IRI and offers a rationale for the development of innovative treatment strategies to mitigate PGD after lung transplantation.

## Materials and methods

## MSC–EVs isolation and identification
MSC‐EVs were provided by Cellular Biomedicine Group (CBMG, Shanghai, China) and prepared according to previously reported methods [ ]. Briefly, the human adipose-derived MSCs were cultured to fourth passage and were seeded at 1.0 – 1.5 × 104/cm2 density. When cells reached 90% confluence, the complete medium (α-MEM supplemented with human platelet lysate) was replaced with EV-depleted conditioned medium, which had been prepared by ultracentrifugation of the complete medium at 120,000 g for 6 h to remove pre-existing EVs. After 48 h of incubation, the supernatant was sequentially centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min, 2000 g for 20 min, and 10,000 g for 30 min to remove cells and debris. The clarified supernatant was then incubated with 12% polyethylene glycol for 24 h and centrifuged at 3,000 g for 1 h at 4 °C. The resulting pellet was resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and ultracentrifuged at 120,000 g for 70 min to deplete free proteins and other soluble impurities. Finally, the MSC-EVs were re-suspended in PBS.
Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) showed that particle size distribution ranged from 50 to 150 nm (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed a double-layer membrane and typical cup-shaped morphology (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Western blotting confirmed the presence of EV markers TSG101, CD9, CD63 and HSP70 and the absence of the endoplasmic reticulum marker Calnexin (Supplementary Fig. 1C).

## Animals
All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital (FKDS-22-1-067), and were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 wild-type (WT) mice were purchased from GemPharmatech company (Jiangsu, China). For orthotopic left lung transplantation, male inbred Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats aged 8 - 12 weeks were purchased from Charles River (Beijing, China) and used as both donors and recipients.
All animals were kept in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment at the Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital Animal Facility (Shanghai, China), with unrestricted access to water and standard laboratory chow. At the end of each experiment, animals were humanely euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (200 mg/kg) until complete loss of reflexes, followed by exsanguination to ensure death. All experiments were performed independently to ensure reliability and avoid cross-interference among assays.

## Labelling of MSC-EVs
PKH67 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), a green fluorescent lipophilic membrane dye, was used to label MSC-EVs. Briefly, MSC-EVs were incubated with PKH67 (3 μL per 100 μg of MSC-EVs, approximately 1 × 1011 particles) in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. Then, the samples were quenched with PBS containing 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA), the labelled MSC-EVs were extracted with ExoQuick Reagent (SBI, USA), resuspended in PBS. Two hours after administration of PKH67-labelled MSC-EVs via inhalation or tail vein injection, mice were sacrificed and lung tissues were harvested. The distribution of labelled EVs in lung sections was examined using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Germany).

## Mouse left hilar-clamp model for lung IRI
Mice were randomized to receive sham surgery or lung IRI. Anesthesia was induced by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg; Sinopharm, China). Orotracheal intubation was performed using a 20 G catheter, and mechanical ventilation (KW-100-2; KEW Basis, China) with ambient air was initiated at a tidal volume of 0.8 mL and a respiratory rate of 120 breaths/min. Body temperature was maintained with a heating pad. After left thoracotomy via the fourth intercostal space, the left pulmonary artery, vein, and bronchus were occluded using a microvascular clamp. After 1 h of ischemia, the clamp was removed to initiate reperfusion. The chest was then closed, and animals were allowed to recover with 3 h of reperfusion. For sham-operated controls, mice underwent identical surgical procedures, including anesthesia, intubation, thoracotomy, and exposure of the left pulmonary hilum, but without vascular clamping. At the end of reperfusion, arterial blood was collected from the left ventricle for blood gas analysis, and left lung tissues were harvested and stored at − 80 °C. Additionally, to better assess injured lung function, we ligated the right hilum after left lung reperfusion so that the mice relied solely on the injured lung, and determined the 1-h survival rate. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

## MSC-EVs treatment
C57BL/6 mice received 6 × 105 particles of MSC-EVs suspended in either 30μL PBS (for aerosol inhalation via a nebulizer needle) or 100 μL PBS (for tail vein injection). For rat experiments, 6 × 106 particles of MSC-EVs were resuspended in 200 μL sterile PBS and administered to SD rats, with the dosage adjusted according to body weight and volume conversion. Two hours after administration, animals either underwent induction of the lung IRI model or were sacrificed for lung tissue collection. EVs extracted from fibroblasts as vehicle control (Veh). For in vitro assays, RAW264.7 cells were treated with MSC-EVs at a concentration of 1 × 10⁸ particles/mL.

## Lung histology
Lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h at 4 °C, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and paraffin-embedded. Serial 4–5 µm sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Lung injury was scored according to the American Thoracic Society (ATS) workshop report criteria [ ], evaluating alveolar congestion, hemorrhage, neutrophil infiltration, and alveolar wall thickening. Ten randomly selected non-overlapping fields per mouse were scored under light microscopy by an investigator blinded to the experimental groups.

## Lung wet/dry weight ratio
After reperfusion, the left lung was excised, gently blotted to remove surface moisture, and immediately weighed to obtain the wet weight. To minimize variability due to the small size of individual lobes, three lungs were pooled to form one sample. The tissues were then dried at 60 °C for 48 - 72 h until a constant weight was achieved, which was recorded as the dry weight. The wet-to-dry (W/D) weight ratio was calculated as an indicator of the severity of pulmonary edema.

## Measurement of pulmonary function
At the end of reperfusion, animals were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) and tracheostomized using an 18 G catheter. Mechanical ventilation was set at a tidal volume of 250 μL and a respiratory rate of 250 breaths/min. Pulmonary resistance and compliance were measured using a resistance–compliance plethysmograph with a pneumotachograph connected to a pressure transducer (EMMS, Hants, UK). Measurements were recorded for 5 min, and instantaneous values were calculated using system software.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
The concentrations of TNF-α (SEKM-0034; Solarbio, China), IL-6 (SEKM-0007; Solarbio, China), and IL-10 (SEKM-0010; Solarbio, China) in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and cell culture supernatants were quantified using ELISA kits, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, after reperfusion, the right hilum was ligated and the left lung underwent lavage with 0.5 ml of cold PBS. The BALF was centrifuged at 4 °C, 500g for 5 min, and the supernatant was divided into aliquots and stored at − 80 °C. Cell culture supernatants were collected after treatment, centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min to remove debris, and stored under the same conditions. For ELISA, standards and samples (50 - 100 μL per well) were added in duplicate to antibody-coated 96-well plates and incubated at 37 °C for 60 - 120 min. After washing, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied, followed by substrate solution (TMB). The reaction was terminated with stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, USA). Cytokine concentrations were calculated from the standard curves generated in each assay.

## Flow cytometry and cell sorting
Single-cell suspensions were prepared from the left lungs of sham-operated or IRI mice by mechanical dissociation followed by enzymatic digestion with collagenase type I (1 mg/mL) and DNase I (50 μg/mL) at 37 °C for 30 min. The digested tissue was passed through a 70 μm cell strainer, and red blood cells were lysed with ammonium-chloride-potassium buffer. Cells were resuspended in flow cytometry staining buffer (PBS containing 2% fetal bovine serum). For immunophenotyping, cells were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies at 4 °C for 30 min in the dark. The gating strategy for macrophage identification is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2A. The following antibodies were used: fixable viability dyes (123115; BioLegend, USA), anti-CD11b (101215; BioLegend, USA), anti-F4/80 (123115; BioLegend, USA), anti-CD11c (337234; BioLegend, USA), and anti-CD206 (141706; BioLegend, USA). After staining, cells were washed and resuspended for analysis. Flow cytometric acquisition and sorting were performed on a BD FACSAria II cell sorter (BD Biosciences, USA), and post-sort purity was consistently > 90% (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Pyroptosis was assessed by staining with a caspase-1 activity probe (FAM-FLICA® Caspase-1 Assay Kit, ImmunoChemistry Technologies, USA) and propidium iodide (PI) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Immunofluorescence
Paraffin-embedded lung section (4-5 µm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 15 min. Paraffin sections or paraformaldehyde-fixed cultured cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Gibco, USA) for 30 min at room temperature. Samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer: F4/80 (29414-1-AP; Proteintech, China), iNOS (ab283655; Abcam, USA), NLRP3 (68102-1-Ig; Proteintech, China), and IL-1β (16906-1-AP; Proteintech, China). After washing, sections were incubated with appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, China) for 5 min. Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Germany).

## Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining
Paraffin-embedded lung section (4-5µm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100/0.1% sodium citrate on ice for 2 min. Apoptotic nuclei were detected using the In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sections were incubated with TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark, rinsed in PBS, and counterstained with DAPI. Positive controls were pretreated with DNase I, and negative controls were processed in the absence of TdT enzyme. Images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (LeicaDM2000, Germany), and TUNEL-positive cells were expressed as a percentage of total nuclei.

## Western blotting (WB)
Proteins were extracted from cells or lung tissues using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, China) and quantified using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD9 (ab92726; Abcam, USA), TSG101 (ab125011; Abcam, USA), CD63 (ab134045; Abcam, USA), HSP70 (ab2787; Abcam, USA), Calnexin (ab92573; Abcam, USA), iNOS (18985-1-AP; Proteintech, China), Arg1 (16001-1-AP; Proteintech, China), GSDMD-FL (ab125011; Abcam, USA), GSDMD-NT (ab215203; Abcam, USA), Caspase-1 (ab207802; Abcam, USA), IL-1β (16906-1-AP; Proteintech, China), NLRP3 (68102-1-Ig; Proteintech, China), ASC (ab309497; Abcam, USA), GAPDH (60004-1-Ig; Proteintech, China) and β-actin (AC026; Abclonal, China). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Bands were visualized using a Bio-Rad imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA), and densitometric analysis was performed with ImageJ software.

## Cell culture
The mouse macrophage cell line RAW264.7 (ATCC® TIB-71™, USA) was used in this study. Cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (100 U/mL penicillin and 100μg/mL streptomycin; Gibco, USA). Cultures were maintained under standard conditions in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

## Uptake of MSC-EVs by RAW264.7 cells
MSC-EVs were labeled using a PKH67 Green Fluorescent Cell Linker Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. PKH67-labeled MSC-EVs were added to RAW264.7 cells at 1 × 10⁸ particles/mL and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove unbound vesicles and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, pH 7.4) for 30 min at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, China) in the dark for 5 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Germany) under identical acquisition settings.

## Hypoxia–reoxygenation model
Cells were placed in a sealed hypoxia chamber (Billups-Rothenberg, CA, USA) and subjected to purging with 95% N2 and 5% CO2 at a flow rate of 40 l/min for 4 min to induce hypoxia following the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, the chamber was placed in a cell culture incubator for 12 h, after which it was opened for 4-h reoxygenation.

## Induction of M2 polarization in RAW264.7 cells
For M2 polarization, RAW264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well in 6-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then stimulated with recombinant mouse interleukin-4 (IL-4, 10 ng/mL) and interleukin-13 (IL-13, 10 ng/mL) for 24 h.

## Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)
The extraction of total RNA was carried out using Trizol reagent, followed by reverse transcription into cDNA utilizing a TaKaRa PrimeScript RT reagent kit (TaKaRa, Japan). Quantitative PCR was conducted on a Roche Light Cycler 480 (Roche, USA) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (TaKaRa, Japan). The levels of mRNA expression were calculated using the 2−△△Ct method and normalized to GADPH gene. The primers used were summarized in Supplementary Tables 1.

## Dual luciferase reporter assay
Bioinformatics analysis using the Target-Scan database indicated that NLRP3 is a potential miR-22-3p target. RAW264.7 cells were transfected with 100 ng of either a wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) NLRP3 3'-UTR reporter vectors using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. After 48 h, firefly and renilla luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

## Detection of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitochondrial damage
Intracellular ROS levels and mitochondrial integrity were assessed using fluorescent probes. RAW264.7 cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA from a ROS Assay Kit (Highly Sensitive DCFH-DA, R252; Dojindo, Japan) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, followed by three washes with serum-free medium to remove excess dye. For mitochondrial staining, cells were incubated with MitoTracker Green (Beyotime, China) at a final concentration of 100 nM for 30 min at 37 °C, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Germany).

## Orthotopic left lung transplantation rat model for lung IRI
SD rats were used as both donors and recipients. After induction of anesthesia, donor rats were intubated and mechanically ventilated with ambient air at a tidal volume of 10 mL/kg and a respiratory rate of 80 breaths/min. Following median sternotomy, the left auricle was incised, and the lungs were perfused via the pulmonary trunk with 20 mL of 4 °C Celsior preservation solution (Genzyme Corp, USA). The heart–lung block was excised and preserved in Celsior solution at 4 °C for 18 h. The left bronchus, pulmonary artery, and pulmonary vein were cuffed with 14 G, 16 G, and 18 G catheters, respectively. For the recipient, left thoracotomy was performed, and the corresponding structures were clamped with microvascular clamps. Cuffed bronchus, artery, and vein were anastomosed and secured with 5 - 0 silk ligatures. After clamps were released to initiate reperfusion, the chest was closed. Recipients were extubated upon spontaneous respiration. After 2 h of reperfusion, blood from the graft pulmonary vein was collected for arterial blood gas analysis, and graft lungs were harvested. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

## Statistical analysis
The statistical difference between two groups was determined using an independent t test for data with a normal distribution and a Mann - Whitney test for data that did not follow a normal distribution. For comparisons involving more than 2 groups, data with a normal distribution were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test, while data not following a normal distribution were assessed using the post hoc test with Dunnett’s T3. Comparison of survival curves was analyzed by log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2) software. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Inhaled MSC-EVs confer enhanced anti-inflammatory and reparative effects compared with intravenous delivery in lung IRI
To investigate the pulmonary distribution of MSC-EVs administered via different routes, PKH67 labeling was used to trace their localization within lung tissues. A greater accumulation of PKH67-labelled MSC-EVs was observed in the lungs of mice receiving inhalation delivery compared with those receiving tail vein injection (Fig.  A). Next, we employed a left hilar clamp model to investigate the effects of MSC-EVs and different administration routes on lung IRI (Fig.  B). Pathological examination with H&E staining revealed reduced edema and inflammatory cell infiltration in the MSC-EVs-treated groups, with inhalation delivery producing a visibly milder injury than intravenous injection (Fig.  C and Supplementary Fig. 3A). We assessed the lung wet/dry weight ratio as an indicator of pulmonary edema. Compared with the Veh group, both MSC-EVs treated groups showed a significantly lower wet/dry weight ratio, with inhalation delivery exerting a greater protective effect than intravenous injection (Fig.  D). Lung function was evaluated by measuring airway resistance and lung compliance. MSC-EVs improved airway resistance and compliance in IRI mice, and inhalation delivery further enhanced these effects compared with intravenous injection (Fig.  E, F). Next, cytokine expression in BALF was assessed by ELISA. Both delivery routes decreased TNF-α and IL-6 and increased IL-10, with inhalation delivery showing a more favorable cytokine profile (Fig.  G–I). Additionally, to better assess injured lung function, we ligated the right hilum after left lung reperfusion so that the mice relied solely on the injured lung, and determined the 1-h survival rate [ ]. Inhaled MSC-EVs improved short-term survival compared with intravenous delivery (Supplementary Fig. 3B). 
Taken together, these findings indicate that MSC-EVs ameliorate pathological alterations, improve lung function, and attenuate inflammatory responses in lung IRI, with inhalation delivery conferring a more pronounced protective effect than tail vein injection.

## MSC-EVs modulate macrophage polarization and pyroptosis following lung IRI
Given the critical role of macrophages in mediating tissue injury after IRI [ , ], we investigated the role of macrophages in MSC-EVs therapy. First, we examined the quantity of macrophages within lung tissue after lung IRI by flow cytometry. The results showed that the proportion of CD11b+ F4/80+ macrophages was significantly greater in the lung tissue of the IRI group than in that of the sham-operated group, whereas MSC-EVs treatment did not alter the total macrophage population compared with that in the Veh-treated group (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). MSC-EVs reduced M1 macrophages and increased M2 macrophages in lung tissues (Fig.  A - C). TUNEL staining showed that MSC-EVs effectively mitigated cell death - associated tissue injury in lung IRI (Fig.  D, E). Moreover, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that MSC-EVs notably reduced the increase in M1 macrophages resulting from lung IRI (Fig.  D, F). Western blot confirmed decreased iNOS and increased Arg1 in lung macrophages following MSC-EVs treatment (Fig.  G and Supplementary Fig. 4C). In addition, flow cytometry revealed a higher rate of macrophage pyroptosis in the IRI group compared with the sham group, whereas pyroptosis was reduced in the IRI + MSC-EVs group relative to the IRI + Veh group (Fig.  H and Supplementary Fig. 4D). Consistently, Western blot showed reduced GSDMD-NT, Caspase-1, and IL-1β expression following MSC-EVs treatment (Fig.  I). 
These results indicate that MSC-EVs shift macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype and attenuate macrophage pyroptosis during lung IRI.

## MSC-EVs regulate macrophage polarization and pyroptosis in vitro
To further investigate the direct effects of MSC-EVs on macrophages, PKH67-labelled MSC-EVs were added to RAW264.7 cultures, and confocal microscopy confirmed their internalization (Fig.  A). Hypoxia-reoxygenation (H/R) was induced in RAW264.7 cells to simulate IRI in vitro, and MSC-EVs or vehicle were added to the cultures. After 6 h of hypoxia and 12 h of reoxygenation, the concentrations of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10 in the culture supernatants were measured. Compared with the control group, the H/R group exhibited significantly higher levels of TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-10. Moreover, IL-10 levels were higher and TNF-α and IL-6 levels were lower in the H/R + MSC-EVs group than in the H/R + Veh group (Fig.  B - D). Flow cytometry analysis further revealed that the proportion of M1 macrophages was significantly lower in the H/R + MSC-EVs group compared with the H/R + Veh group (Fig.  E, F). Consistently, western blot and RT-qPCR analyses showed that MSC-EVs inhibited H/R-induced iNOS expression and upregulated Arg1 expression (Fig.  G - I). In addition, we assessed the impact of MSC-EVs on macrophage pyroptosis and found that MSC-EVs markedly reduced the proportion of pyroptotic macrophages (Fig.  J and Supplementary Fig. 4E). Western blot analysis demonstrated that the expression of GSDMD-NT, Caspase-1, and IL-1β was decreased in the H/R + MSC-EVs group compared with the H/R + Veh group (Fig.  K).
These results indicate that MSC-EVs regulate macrophage polarization and pyroptosis in vitro.

## MSC-EVs-mediated macrophage polarization was mediated by miR-22-3p
MSC-EVs play an important role in cellular communication by exchanging miRNAs or proteins between cells. To elucidate the mechanisms by which MSC-EVs regulate macrophage polarization, we analyzed the miRNA composition of MSC-EVs using a miRNA microarray. Based on the miRNA profiles, we focused on miRNAs previously reported to be involved in ischemia–reperfusion injury (miR-21, miR-125-5p, miR-221-3p, miR-22-3p, miR-20a-5p, miR-144-3p, miR-760-3p, and miR-1) [ – ] and macrophage polarization (miR-21, miR-125-5p, miR-221-3p, miR-22-3p, miR-30d-5p, miR-155, miR-210, and miR-223) [ – ] that were enriched in MSC-EVs. Among these candidates, miR-221-3p and miR-22-3p were identified as potential regulators of macrophage polarization (Fig.  A). We subsequently quantified the expression levels of miR-221-3p and miR-22-3p in MSC-EVs and vehicle controls. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that miR-22-3p was significantly enriched in MSC-EVs, whereas no significant difference was observed for miR-221-3p (Fig.  B). In contrast, endogenous expression levels of miR-22-3p and miR-221-3p in lung macrophages isolated from sham and IRI mice showed no significant differences among groups (Supplementary Fig. 4F). 
To investigate the functional role of miR-22-3p in macrophage polarization, M1 and M2 markers were analyzed by flow cytometry. The results showed that miR-22-3p significantly reduced the proportion of M1 macrophages under H/R conditions (Fig.  D, E). To further confirm the contribution of miR-22-3p within MSC-EVs, MSCs were transfected with a miR-22-3p inhibitor, and EVs were subsequently isolated from the culture supernatants. RT-qPCR analysis confirmed that miR-22-3p levels were significantly reduced in MSC-EVs derived from MSCs transfected with a miR-22-3p inhibitor compared with those derived from MSCs transfected with a negative control inhibitor (Fig.  C). RAW264.7 cells were then subjected to H/R stimulation and treated with inhibitor-NC MSC-EVs or miR-22-3p inhibitor MSC-EVs for 48 h, followed by flow cytometric analysis. MSC-EV treatment reduced the proportion of M1 macrophages under H/R conditions; however, this effect was reversed when miR-22-3p was inhibited in MSC-EVs (Fig.  F, G).
Collectively, these findings demonstrate that macrophage polarization induced by MSC-EVs relies on miR-22-3p.

## The protective effect of MSC-EVs against lung IRI is mediated by miR-22-3p
We next examined the in vivo contribution of miR-22-3p to the protective effects of MSC-EVs using a mouse left hilar clamp model. Inhibition of miR-22-3p in MSC-EVs resulted in more pronounced pulmonary edema and increased inflammatory cell infiltration compared with unmodified MSC-EVs (Fig.  A, B). Consistently, the lung wet/dry weight ratio was significantly higher in mice treated with miR-22-3p-depleted MSC-EVs than in those treated with control MSC-EVs (Fig.  C). Arterial blood gas analysis showed that inhibition of miR-22-3p in MSC-EVs impaired the recovery of gas exchange function (Fig.  D, E). In addition, lung function assessment revealed that miR-22-3p inhibition attenuated the beneficial effects of MSC-EV treatment, as evidenced by increased airway resistance and reduced lung compliance compared with the MSC-EVs group (Fig.  F, G). Flow cytometry further demonstrated that the proportion of M1 macrophages was higher in the miR-22-3p inhibited MSC-EVs group than in the MSC-EVs group (Fig.  H, I). 
Collectively, these results indicate that the protective effects of MSC-EVs against lung IRI are mediated by miR-22-3p.

## NLRP3 is a direct target of miR-22-3p
To explore the molecular mechanism by which miR-22-3p facilitates macrophage polarization, we performed bioinformatic analysis using the TargetScan database and identified NLRP3 as a potential target (Fig.  A). The results of the luciferase assay, which was used to study the binding of miR-22-3p to the 3’-UTR of NLRP3, revealed a significant decrease in luciferase activity compared with the negative controls (Fig.  B). Overexpression of miR-22-3p in RAW264.7 cells using a miR-22-3p mimic (Fig.  C) markedly reduced NLRP3 mRNA levels (Fig.  D), whereas inhibition of miR-22-3p increased NLRP3 expression (Fig.  E, F). Furthermore, attenuation of miR-22-3p expression reversed the miR-22-3p-induced suppression of NLRP3, as confirmed by immunofluorescence (Fig.  G) and western blot analyses (Fig.  H and Supplementary Fig. 4G). 
Although MSC-EVs contain multiple bioactive cargos, our cellular assays consistently identified miR-22-3p as the principal mediator of NLRP3 suppression. Inhibition of miR-22-3p enhanced NLRP3 expression and promoted M1 polarization. whereas co-transfection with NLRP3 siRNA rescued this phenotype by partially restoring M2 markers (Fig.  I). These findings demonstrate that the protective and pro-M2 effects of miR-22-3p are NLRP3-dependent, providing direct functional evidence that MSC-EV-delivered miR-22-3p acts through NLRP3 to regulate macrophage polarization.
NLRP3 serves as a critical regulator of cellular pyroptosis [ ]. We therefore evaluated NLRP3 expression in macrophages in the lung IRI model. NLRP3 expression was significantly decreased in lung macrophages isolated from IRI mice treated with MSC-EVs relative to the Veh group (Fig.  J, K). Taken together, these findings consistently demonstrate that NLRP3 is a direct downstream target of miR-22-3p.

## miR-22-3p shuttling by MSC-EVs modulates macrophage phenotype by regulating pyroptosis via the NLRP3/Caspase-1/IL-1β pathway
We further investigated the NLRP3/caspase-1/IL-1β pathway after modulating miR-22-3p levels in macrophages. Western blot analysis showed that transfection with the miR-22-3p mimic markedly reduced the expression of NLRP3 and its downstream effectors, including ASC, Caspase-1, and IL-1β, in H/R-stimulated RAW264.7 cells (Fig.  A). Consistently, MSC-EV treatment suppressed this pathway, whereas inhibition of miR-22-3p within MSC-EVs largely reversed these effects (Fig.  B). Immunofluorescence staining showed that MSC-EVs attenuated the H/R-induced upregulation of the pyroptosis-related proteins NLRP3 and IL-1β (Fig.  C and Supplementary Fig. 4H). As alterations in mitochondrial membrane potential and ROS production are central to pyroptotic injury, we next assessed mitochondrial function and oxidative stress. MitoTracker staining revealed that MSC-EVs improved mitochondrial function and alleviated cellular damage (Fig.  D and Supplementary Fig. 4I). Additionally, MSC-EVs reduced ROS production during hypoxia–reoxygenation (Fig.  E and Supplementary Fig. 4J). 
These results suggest that MSC-EVs, through the transfer of miR-22-3p, modulate macrophage polarization by regulating pyroptosis via the NLRP3/Caspase-1/IL-1β pathway.

## Efficacy in a clinically relevant OLT model
Although the hilar clamp mouse model has been widely used to study lung IRI, the OLT model is more relevant to human PGD [ ]. Therefore, we established a left OLT rat model as previously described [ ]. The Prolonged IR group was subjected to left donor lung harvesting and preservation at 4 °C for 18 h before transplantation, followed by 2 h of warm reperfusion prior to euthanasia. The control group underwent the same procedure without lung preservation, and MSC-EVs were administered via tail vein injection 2 h prior to donor lung harvesting (Fig.  A). H&E staining revealed that in the Prolonged IR group, MSC-EVs treatment significantly improved lung transplantation outcomes, as evidenced by reduced immune cell infiltration, alveolar wall thickening, interstitial edema, and capillary congestion in the MSC-EVs group compared with the Veh group (Fig.  B). Similarly, pulmonary function tests supported these findings: airway compliance was significantly improved, and airway resistance was reduced in the MSC-EVs group (Fig.  C, D). Arterial blood gas analysis showed higher PaO₂ and lower PaCO₂ values in the MSC-EVs group (Fig.  E, F), indicating better preservation of oxygenation and ventilation. 

## Discussion
In contrast to MSCs, stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles are not directly influenced by the compromised microenvironment. Owing to their distinctive characteristics, including small size and low immunogenicity, these vesicles exert pivotal biological functions, including mitigation of cellular apoptosis, attenuation of inflammatory responses, facilitation of angiogenesis, inhibition of fibrosis, and enhancement of tissue repair potential [ ]. Consequently, stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles have emerged as promising therapeutic agents due to their capacity for precise and effective modulation of tissue regeneration [ ]. In this context, inhalation delivery of MSC-EVs offers a direct and noninvasive route to the lungs, ensuring high local bioavailability while limiting systemic exposure. Compared with intravenous administration, aerosolized inhalation enhances EV retention within the injured pulmonary microenvironment and facilitates uptake by alveolar macrophages. This localized delivery strategy not only augments therapeutic efficacy but also has practical advantages for clinical translation, particularly in lung transplantation and IRI-related PGD.
Macrophages are key orchestrators of the inflammatory response, and their polarization state critically regulates both the initiation and resolution of inflammation [ ]. NLRP3, a core component of the inflammasome complex, is a central regulator of pyroptosis and contributes to downstream macrophage fate decisions [ ]. The inflammatory milieu generated by NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis, notably through the release of IL-1β and IL-18 together with other mediators, promotes polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype [ , ]. Conversely, genetic deletion or pharmacologic inhibition of NLRP3 can skew macrophage polarization toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, characterized by anti-inflammatory cytokine production and promotion of tissue repair and regeneration [ ]. Our findings extend these mechanistic insights to the setting of lung transplantation, in which PGD remains a major unresolved clinical challenge. By integrating a mouse hilar clamp model with a rat OLT model, we provide converging preclinical evidence that MSC-EVs attenuate IRI and PGD through modulation of macrophage pyroptosis and polarization.
While our data highlight extracellular vesicle miR-22-3p as a pivotal determinant of immunomodulatory and tissue-protective effects of MSC-EVs, we acknowledge that other vesicular cargoes likely contribute. Notably, endogenous miR-22-3p levels in macrophages did not change after IRI, indicating that its functional impact arises from EV-mediated supplementation rather than intrinsic transcriptional regulation. Although our mechanistic studies consistently identify miR-22-3p as the principal mediator of NLRP3 suppression, numerous additional components are undoubtedly biologically active and may cooperate to produce the observed protective phenotype. In addition, although the rat OLT model reinforces the translational promise of inhaled MSC-EVs, several challenges must be addressed before clinical application. Key considerations include maintaining EV stability during aerosolization, optimizing human dosing and delivery efficiency, and establishing scalable, GMP-compliant production with consistent quality. Addressing these issues will be essential for advancing inhaled MSC-EV therapy toward clinical translation. Finally, a limitation of our study is the relatively small sample size used in the in vivo experiments, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Larger and more comprehensive studies will be necessary to further validate and extend these results.

## Conclusion
Our study demonstrates that MSC-EVs represent a promising therapeutic approach for lung IRI. Inhalation delivery alleviated pulmonary injury more effectively than intravenous administration, and efficacy was confirmed in both a murine hilar clamp model and a rat OLT model, supporting translational relevance to PGD. Mechanistically, MSC-EVs delivered miR-22-3p into macrophages, leading to downregulation of NLRP3 and suppression of the ASC/Caspase-1/IL-1β axis, thereby attenuating macrophage pyroptosis and facilitating polarization toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. These effects collectively reduced cytokine-driven injury and enhanced tissue repair. Taken together, our findings position MSC-EVs as a promising preclinical strategy for PGD prevention in lung transplantation.
