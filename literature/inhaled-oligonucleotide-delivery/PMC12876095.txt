## Abstract
Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of cell death driven by lipid peroxidation, plays a critical role in the progression of asthma, yet its therapeutic modulation remains underexplored. Luteolin (Lut), a natural flavonoid known for its anti-inflammatory properties, presents a potential candidate for targeting ferroptosis in asthma. However, the precise mechanisms underlying its therapeutic effects are unclear. This study used a combination of bioinformatics, network pharmacology, molecular docking, and in vitro and in vivo experiments to investigate the role of luteolin in the treatment of ferroptosis in asthma. A collection of natural flavonoid compounds with high oral bioavailability and drug-like properties was assembled from the Chinese herbal medicine database. Through network pharmacology analysis, it was discovered that these flavonoids can effectively treat ferroptosis in asthma by regulating Arachidonic acid 15-lipoxygenase (ALOX15) and arachidonic acid metabolism. Further investigation through virtual docking revealed that luteolin is a key compound in the treatment of ferroptosis in asthma, targeting ALOX15. Molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated that the ALOX15-luteolin complex is stable and flexible. Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA) confirmed the thermal stability of luteolin and ALOX15, while Drug affinity response target stability (DARTS) assay showed that luteolin inhibits the proteolysis of ALOX15 by pronase. In in vitro experiments, it was observed that luteolin treatment reduced Fe2+ content and lipid ROS levels in a dose-dependent manner, while also downregulating ALOX15 and ACSL4 and upregulating SLC7A11 and GPX4, effectively alleviating ferroptosis induced by house dust mite (HDM) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in 16HBE cells. ALOX15 was specifically knocked down in 16HBE cells, and it was found that ALOX15 silencing and luteolin treatment could also inhibit ferroptosis in asthma. In vivo experiments and serum metabolomics analyses further confirmed that luteolin inhibits ferroptosis by suppressing ALOX15 expression and regulating arachidonic acid metabolism, ultimately alleviating asthma symptoms in mice. This study found that luteolin inhibited ALOX15-mediated ferroptosis in bronchial epithelial cells to alleviate asthma, highlighting the potential of luteolin as a promising therapeutic agent for asthma treatment.

## Introduction
Asthma is a multifaceted and heterogeneous airway disorder characterized by pulmonary inflammation, heightened airway hyperresponsiveness, excessive mucus production, and structural remodeling of the airways [ ], which affects approximately 1%–18% of the population worldwide [ ]. The principal therapeutic approach for asthma presently depends on the administration of inhaled corticosteroids and bronchodilators to mitigate symptoms; however, approximately 5% to 10% of asthma patients fail to attain adequate control over their condition [ ]. The lack of development of natural medicines targeting the underlying mechanisms of asthma means that exploring effective medicines with stable access and low economic costs is critical.
Ferroptosis is a recently identified type of regulated cell death (RCD), whose regulation depends on the lipid repair enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), and is triggered by severe lipid peroxidation, involving reactive oxygen species (ROS) and iron overload [ ]. Ferroptosis has recently been found to be associated with a variety of pathologic conditions in the lungs including asthma [ – ]. Additionally, research indicates that asthma triggered by the environmental allergen house dust mite (HDM) and pulmonary ferroptosis resulting from HDM inhalation are associated with increased airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR), heightened airway inflammation, elevated lipid peroxidation, and higher levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [ ]. Arachidonic acid 15-lipoxygenase is a protease corresponding to the ALOX15 gene and is an oxygen-containing lipase with pro-oxidative and esterification effects. It catalyzes the polymerization of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) on the cell membrane [ ]. Studies have found that ALOX15 is highly associated with ferroptosis. There are three main metabolic pathways associated with ferroptosis, including iron metabolism, lipid peroxidation, and glutathione metabolism. ALOX15 can selectively catalyze PE-AA oxidation to produce lipid hydroperoxides, ultimately leading to ferroptosis [ ]. At the same time, ALOX15 is present in airway epithelial cells and may be associated with chronic airway inflammation in asthma by catalyzing the conversion of arachidonic acid to 15-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid (15-HETE), which is associated with various airway inflammatory diseases [ ]. These studies have shown the potential and essential role of ferroptosis in asthma, indicating that targeting ALOX15 could provide new treatment options for asthma.
Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) has a long history of use spanning thousands of years, and natural compounds in herbal formulas, herbs, or herbal extracts have been found to relieve the inflammatory symptoms of asthma. Flavonoids are commonly present in these materials and act as potent antioxidants and nutrients that help combat allergies. They can inhibit the release of chemical mediators, reduce the synthesis of Th2 cytokines (such as IL-4 and IL-13), prevent the accumulation of eosinophils in the airways and lung tissue, and lower the expression of high-affinity IgE receptor cells (such as mast cells and basophils) on CD40 ligand [ ]. In the management of respiratory diseases, numerous flavonoids exhibit the capability to diminish reactive oxygen species (ROS), inhibit redox-related agents, and chelate iron that contributes to ROS production, thereby reducing the accumulation of lipid peroxidation products (LPO) and Fe2+ [ , – ]. Luteolin has the natural advantages of low toxicity and high efficacy, and has become a candidate drug for a variety of diseases, with different potential mechanisms of action and considerable potential for clinical application [ ]. Compared with other flavonoids, Lut has a stronger research basis for its anti-asthmatic properties and has been shown to relieve a range of asthma symptoms, including airway inflammation [ ], airway hyperresponsiveness, bronchoconstriction [ ], airway mucus hypersecretion [ ], and lung autophagy [ ], as well as neutrophil asthma in OVA/LPS-stimulated asthmatic mice [ ]. However, there is a lack of research on the mechanism of how natural flavonoids can treat asthma by regulating ferroptosis, and there is no research on Lut in this regard.
Systems pharmacology has emerged as a crucial approach for identifying the biological targets and chemical compounds of drugs that influence network interactions within the body, as it highlights the extensive nature of drug interactions, aligning well with the multi-target and multi-pathway features inherent in natural products [ , ]. Based on basic theory, clinical observations, and in vivo and in vitro experimental results, the study systematically revealed the advantages of natural flavonoids in regulating ferroptosis. At the same time, it is notable that luteolin was found to modulate the effect of ALOX15 on ferroptosis in asthma, providing a potential natural flavonoid inhibitor luteolin for the treatment of ALOX15, a key target of ferroptosis in asthma.

## Methods

## Construction of Natural Flavonoids Library from Chinese Herbal Medicine
Natural flavonoids compounds were obtained from TCMSP (http://tcmspw.com/tcmsp.php), ITCM (http://itcm.biotcm.net/index.html), BATMAN-TCM (http://bionet.ncpsb.org.cn/batman-tcm/) and TCMID (http://www.megabionet.org/tcmid) databases and the compounds’ results and CAS numbers were analyzed against the PubChem databases, leading to the acquisition of their corresponding PubChem CID [ – ]. Molecular structures in compound form were retrieved from the PubChem database and stored in mol2 format for subsequent analysis. Considering the ADMET pharmacokinetic characteristics (absorption, distribution, metabolism, elimination, and toxicity) of the compounds, candidates for active ingredients were chosen based on oral bioavailability (OB) being greater than or equal to 30% and drug-likeness (DL) being at least 0.18. At the same time, certain compounds that exhibit lower oral bioavailability (OB) or distribution level (DL) were chosen due to their remarkable pharmacological properties or significant content, including Tephrosin and Isoflavones.

## Candidate Compound and Disease Potential Targets Prediction
The candidate flavonoids were consistently saved in SDF format using ChemBioDraw software. The SMILES representations of these compounds were then uploaded to the SwissTargetPrediction database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch) to identify target similarities, allowing for the selection of the top 100 targets for each compound, all with a probability greater than 0 for a specific parameter [ ]. In addition, relevant targets were added through the literature. With “asthma” as keywords, potential targets were investigated in the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org). Moreover, we retrieved 564 genes associated with ferroptosis from FerrDb V2 (https://ferrdb.org).

## Acquisition and Processing of Asthma of Gene Expression Profile
We searched for RNA-seq analysis of human asthma patients in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), including datasets collected from clinical studies (age, gender, disease extent, receiving steroids or not). GSE136587 contained 33 asthma patient samples, 6 health people samples. Differential gene analysis was performed on the processed gene expression matrix of the GSE136587 dataset using the R package “limma” to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), (|Log2FC| > 0.5, p < 0.05) [ ]. The ggplot2 R program and the heatmap R package were used to create volcano plots and heatmaps of DEGs [ ].

## Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis
In order to identify genes that may be linked to the clinical characteristics of asthma, we selected the gene set of asthma patients in GSE136587 for WGCNA analysis via the “WGCNA” package in R software [ ]. Initially, we utilized the top 19,200 genes identified by median absolute deviation (MAD) to create the expression matrix. Subsequently, we transformed the correlation matrix into a weighted neighborhood matrix using an appropriate soft threshold. We subsequently utilize the pickSoftThreshold function within WGCNA to determine the best parameter β for building a scale-free network. In conclusion, we employ hierarchical clustering to detect modules of closely linked genes by analyzing their connectivity and covariance coefficients.

## Analysis of the Protein-Protein Interaction Network and Enrichment Assessment of Intersection Genes
We selected modules from WGCNA that were highly correlated with clinical phenotypes to crosswalk with database-associated gene sets and DEGs for potential asthma targets. With the help of the R package “Venny”, we mapped the Venny diagrams of flavonoids targets, potential asthma targets and ferroptosis-related genes to sort out the potential natural flavonoid targets for treating asthma through ferroptosis. To investigate the connections among intersection genes, data on protein-protein interactions (PPI) for the target proteins were retrieved from the STRING database. Cytoscape version 3.7.2 offers visual representations of biomedical interaction networks involving proteins, genes, and various interactions to illustrate the connections among target genes. Additionally, it utilizes the MCODE algorithm to identify and filter the most significant genes within cytoHubba [ ]. In order to gain deeper insights into the key processes involving interaction targets, we uploaded the list of target protein genes to the Metascape database (https://metascape.org/) for analysis of gene ontology (GO) and enrichment analysis of KEGG pathways. The threshold for the p-value was established at 0.05, suggesting a significant enrichment of target genes within biological processes.

## Network Construction and Analysis
In order to clarify the potential pharmacological benefits of natural flavonoids in treating asthma, we constructed compound-target (C-T) and compound-target-pathway-disease (C-T-P-D) networks. These networks were designed to systematically explore the intricate relationships between compounds, targets, diseases, and pathways at a systemic level. The CytoHubba tool was utilized to assess three parameters of network topology (degree of connectivity, betweenness centrality, and closeness centrality), which helped in identifying the crucial nodes within the network.

## Molecular Docking
Molecular docking can better reveal the binding mode and binding activity between compounds and targets. Molecular docking analyses were performed utilizing the Maestro software. Each protein was tested with the Schrodinger Protein Preparation Wizard. The filling conditions of missing side chain residues and rings were added, the hydrogen bond network of protein amino acid residues was optimized, and water molecules were removed. The ligands were generated using the LigPrep module from Schrödinger. Catechin, Baicalein, Licochalcone A, Quercetin, Luteolin were selected as a small molecular ligand for molecular docking verificatio. For the molecules with high docking score, pymol is used to visualize the docking mode.

## Molecular Dynamics
A molecular dynamics simulation lasting 100 ns was conducted to investigate the stability and flexibility of luteolin in the binding sites of ALOX15, GPX4, and SLC7A11. The molecular dynamics simulations are conducted using the GROMACS 2020.1 software package [ ]. The simulation system was built utilizing the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field along with the SPC216 water model. The parameters for the luteolin force field were derived from the ACPYPE website (https://www.bio2byte.be/acpype/) utilizing the AMBER99SB-ILDN force field. To maintain overall charge neutrality in the system, sodium and chloride ions are introduced. The energy of the complete system is minimized (EM) over 50,000 steps at a temperature of 300 K. Following energy minimization, the system’s pressure and temperature are maintained at a stable state through a 100 ps equilibration period at 300 K. Ultimately, all three systems performed the concluding production run of 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation at a temperature of 300 K. Following the simulation, an analysis is conducted on the root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the system as well as the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) in atomic positions.

## Cell Culture
The 16HBE cell line was obtained from the Type Culture Collection at the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The 16HBE cell line was maintained in MEM medium (Gibco, Grand Island, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Grand Island, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Grand Island, USA), and incubated at a temperature of 37 °C with an atmosphere of 5% CO2.

## Lipid Peroxide and Ferrous Ion Level Measurement
BODIPY-C11 staining from ABclonal China was used (cat#RM02821), following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. After treatment according to the experimental grouping, 16HBE cells were exposed to 10 µM BODIPY-C11 for 60 min. Lipid peroxide levels were assessed using flow cytometry (DxP Athena 1–3 L, Cytek Biosciences, USA). In addition, The FerroOrange probe, sourced from Dojindo in Shanghai, China (cat#F374), was utilized to measure the levels of intracellular Fe2+. Following the specific treatment, PBS was used to wash the 16HBE cells. The 16HBE cells were subsequently incubated in FerroOrange solution at a concentration of 1 µmol/L for a duration of 30 min. The stained cell samples were then examined with a multifunctional microplate detection system. (CY-TATION5, BIOTEK, USA).

## Western Blot Assay
Total protein was extracted from cultured 16HBE cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China, cat#R0020) obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology Institute, which included protease and phosphatase inhibitors also sourced from Beyotime. The extraction procedure was conducted for 30 min at a temperature of 4 °C. Afterward, the protein concentrations in the collected supernatants were determined using the BCA protein Assay Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China, cat#C503021), adhering to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each sample contained 20 µg of protein and was briefly denatured with boiled SDS upwelling buffer for a duration of 5 min. The samples were subsequently subjected to a 12% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Following this, the protein bands were transferred onto NC membranes and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used in the experiment included ACSL4 (ABclonal, diluted 1:1000, cat#A16848), GPX4 (CST, diluted 1:1000, cat#52455), ALOX15 (ABclonal, diluted 1:1000, cat#A6864), SLC7A11 (CST, diluted 1:1000, cat#12691), and GAPDH (Sangon Biotech, diluted 1:1000, cat#D110016). After extensive washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with the relevant enzyme-conjugated secondary antibodies for one hour. Subsequently, protein development was performed using BeyoECL Moon (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, cat# P0018FM).

## Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA)
On the following day, 16HBE cells were seeded in well plates and treated with either DMSO or Luteolin for a duration of 24 h. Afterward, the cells were harvested using PBS and transferred into EP tubes. The cells were subjected to heating at a specified temperature range of 40 to 80 °C for a duration of 10 min, followed by cooling at 4 °C for 3 min. The supernatant was then harvested for Western blot analysis.

## Drug Affinity Response Target Stability (DARTS) Assay
Untreated 16HBE cells were lysed on ice for 10 min using M-PER lysis solution, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for another 10 min to obtain the supernatant. Incubate the lysis solution with equal volumes of DMSO and varying doses of Luteolin for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were treated with pronase at a ratio of 1:300 to total protein and incubated for 30 min. The lysate samples were combined with 5 × loading buffer, boiled for 5 min at 100 ℃, and analyzed using western blots.

## si-RNA Assay
16HBE cells were inoculated into 12-well plates at a density of 8 × 104 cells/well. 16HBE cells were transfected with ALOX15-targeting siRNA oligonucleotides (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) for 48 h using jetPRIME® transfection reagent (Polyplus, France). Si-NC was used as a negative control (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China).

## Animal Model
Animals received care in accordance with the Guidance Suggestions for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangdong Medical University (GDY2402733). This study utilized animal models only and did not involve human participants. Clinical trial registration: not applicable. A total of 21 female C57 mice (8 weeks old, 20–22 g), were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd(Beijing, China). All experiments were conducted in accordance with established guidelines and approved by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Medical University. The mice were raised at the School of Marine and Tropical Medicine, Guangdong Medical University. They have free access to both food and water, following a 12-hour light and dark cycle. After a one-week acclimation period, the experimental subjects were randomly divided into three groups, each containing seven subjects. During the study period, the control group received PBS (40 µl) tracheal instillation from day 0 to day 2. The HDM + LPS group and HDM + LPS + Luteolin group were administered with PBS (40 µl) containing HDM 20 µl (10 mg/ml) and LPS 5 µl (5 mg/ml) to establish asthma mouse models. From day 3 to day 6, all three groups were given no treatment. From day 7 to day 11, with the exception of the control group, the remaining two groups received an airway infusion consisting of 40 µl PBS mixed with HDM 20 µl (10 mg/ml) and LPS 5 µl (5 mg/ml). HDM + LPS + Luteolin group was given Luteolin (10 mg/kg) one hour later. Finally, all mice were sacrificed on the 15th day and their blood and lung tissues were collected.

## HE and IHC Staining
Paraffin Sect. (5 μm) of lung tissue were stained with hematoxylin (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and eosin (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) for 8 min and 1 min, respectively, and then visualized with BioTek cytting 5 (BioTek, USA) fixed neutral gel (Solarbio, Beijing, China, cat#G8590). Paraffin sections of lung tissues were repaired with sodium citrate antigen repair solution (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), followed by inactivation of endogenous catalase with hydrogen peroxide solution and blocking with droplets of goat serum (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The primary antibodies were stored at 4 °C during incubation and included ALOX15 (ABclonal, cat#A6864), a-SMA (ABclonal, cat#A2235), GPX4 (ABclonal, cat#A11243), SLC7A11 (ABclonal, cat#A25302), ACSL4 (ABclonal, cat#A6826), IL-1β (CST, #12242), IL6 (CST, #944070), TNF-α(ABclonal, cat#A21265), and MDA (abcam, #ab27642). On the following day, a secondary antibody labeled with HRP was applied dropwise and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, utilizing an immunohistochemistry kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China, cat#D601037). The samples were subjected to Diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining for 15 min, followed by hematoxylin staining for 4 min. Visualization was carried out using a BioTek Cytation 5 (USA).

## RNA Sequencing
16HBE cells were evenly distributed across eight culture dishes and organized into groups. The drug treatment group received Lut for a duration of 24 h. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and subsequently analyzed through high-throughput sequencing conducted by LC-BIO. The control samples were designated as HDM1, HDM2, HDM3, and HDM4, while the treated samples were labeled Lut1, Lut2, Lut3, and Lut4. The raw image data from the high-throughput sequencing underwent CASAVA base calling analysis to convert it into original sequenced reads. FeatureCounts software was utilized to compute the FPKM values (the expected number of Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) for each gene in every sample. Differential expression analysis was performed using edgeR software with criteria set at P < 0.05 and | log2(fold change) | > 0.5 for identifying differentially expressed genes. For GO (Gene Ontology, http://www.geneontology.org/) and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, http://www.kegg.jp/) enrichment analyses, we employed the R package clusterProfiler.

## Metabolomics Analysis
The metabolic profiles were subjected to logarithmic (LOG) transformation and mean centering using the SIMCA software (V16.0.2), followed by Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA). Metabolites identified as differentially expressed were those with a P-value below 0.05 and a Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) score exceeding 1, based on the analyses previously mentioned. The visualization was conducted with R software (version 4.3.0). Following this, differential metabolites underwent enrichment analysis through the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and the Small Molecule Pathway Database (SMPDB), utilizing MetaboAnalyst (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/).

## Statistical Analysis
The statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5 (San Diego; CA, United States). All data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between two groups were assessed using the t-Test, while differences among three groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance. A value of P < 0.05 indicates statistically significant difference, with */# indicates P < 0.05; **/## indicates P < 0.01, ***/### indicates P < 0.001, ****/#### indicates P < 0.0001.

## Results

## Screening of Natural Flavonoids and Acquisition of their Potential Targets
Based on the candidate natural flavonoids in 1037 Chinese herbal medicines in the databases, a database containing 126 natural flavonoids with high oral availability and drug-like properties was constructed by ADMET pharmacokinetic characterization screening (supplemented by literature retrieval). We classified the mother nuclei into 12 distinct types based on their flavonoid substitution sites and the types of their substituents, assigning numbers to each category. The details regarding their characterization can be found in Supplementary Table . At the same time, the active compound is matched with its related molecular information and specific pharmacokinetic parameters, and the distribution of natural flavonoids is shown in Fig.  B, we found that the compounds of the highest proportions in the compound library were Flavones, Isoflavones, and Flavonones. The structural formulas of natural flavonoid compounds were entered into SwissTargetPrediction for the purpose of identifying potential targets, resulting in the identification of 814 protein targets with a probability parameter greater than 0. The C-T network was constructed by inputting natural flavonoids and their targets into Cytoscape 3.7.2 (Fig.  A). By analyzing the C-T network, we found the top 20 natutal flavonoids, as shown in Supplementary Table 2. The interaction between these compounds and targets illustrates the relationship between flavonoids and many the synergy of multiple targets exerts a therapeutic effect. Through DO (DOSE Supports Disease Ontology) enrichment analysis of protein targets, we found that potential targets of flavonoid compound action may be involved in related diseases such as asthma (Fig.  C). At the same time, pathway enrichment analysis found that their potential targets are mainly concentrated in “protein phosphorylation”, “cellular response to lipid”, “Signaling by Interleukins”, “positive regulation of programmed cell death”, “inflammatory response” and other related pathways (Fig.  D).
 

## Screening of ferroptosis-related Asthma Targets and Functional Enrichment Analysis
After preprocessing the experimental dataset (GSE136587) and removing outliers (NA or 0), we obtained 26,365 gene expression profiles from 39 samples, and then we analyzed samples from both asthmatic patients and healthy controls utilizing the “limma” R package, resulting in the identification of 639 DEGs, comprising 330 genes that were up-regulated and 308 that were down-regulated. (Fig.  H). In order to pinpoint asthma-related genes that show a highly association with clinical phenotypes, we conducted WGCNA utilizing the datasets. After excluding abnormal samples, a hierarchical clustering dendrogram was constructed with hierarchical clustering method for clinical grouping features, and then we chose β = 8 as the optimal soft threshold (Fig.  A and B). After the soft threshold value was determined, the adjacency matrix was calculated and then converted into a topological overlap matrix (TOM). Using the TOM, a hierarchical clustering was performed to create a hierarchical tree, which resulted in the formation of 9 distinct modules (Fig.  C). Finally, the associations between modules and clinical phenotypes were calculated using the characteristic gene (ME) values of the modules (Fig.  D). Through the heat map of module-trait relationships, we found strong associations between MEred and age, MEgreen and steroid, and MEgrey and disease extent (Fig.  D). As shown in Fig.  E, in the red module, a significant correlation was found between gene significance related to age and module membership (cor = 0.27, p < 0.01). Similarly, in the green module, there was also a strong correlation coefficient observed between gene significance for steroids and module membership (cor = 0.30, p < 0.01) (Fig.  F). The analysis of the grey module (correlation = 0.69, p < 0.01) showed a strong correlation between the significance of genes related to disease severity and their membership in the module (Fig.  G). We identified 1,223 potential asthma genes related to clinical phenotypes by cross-analyzing the module genes associated with clinical phenotypes in WGCNA and asthma-related genes from the database. Subsequently, we merged DEGs with potential asthma genes for clinical phenotypes to obtain potential asthma genes, and also took intersections with ferroptosis-related targets to obtain 84 ferroptosis-related asthma targets (Fig.  I). In addition, we imported ferroptosis-related asthma targets into R package “clusterProfiler” for enrichment analysis. Enrichment analysis showed (Fig.  J) that these targets may be involved in “inflammatory response”, “cellular response to lipid”, “cellular response to cytokine stimulus”, “Ferroptosis”, “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway”, “MAPK signaling pathway”, “Asthma”, etc. We hypothesize that the biological processes underlying the genetic enrichment of asthma are intricately linked to the pathways involved in the development and progression of ferroptosis.
 

## Analysis of Natural Flavonoids in the Treatment of Ferroptosis in Asthma
We identified the targets of natural flavonoids treated to ferroptosis in asthma and created a Venn diagram (Fig.  A). This analysis revealed 38 potential targets for natural flavonoids in addressing ferroptosis associated with asthma. As shown in the graph of Fig.  A, a network of protein-protein interactions involving natural flavonoids in asthma treatment has been established. The yellow nodes, potentially representing the more significant genes, as shown in the middle panel of Fig.  B among the 14 genes were obtained by setting the parameters (Degree > 10, BetweennessCentrality > 0.011, ClosenessCentrality > 0.512) in the network graph on the left of Fig.  B. Then, the key genes were obtained using the MCODM algorithm in the middle panel of Fig.  B, as shown in the right panel of Fig.  B, including PTGS2, STAT3, NFE2L2, HIF1A, PPARG, HMOX1, RELA, NQO1, GPX4, GCLC, SLC7A11, and ALOX15. As shown in Supplementary Table 3, the GO enrichment analysis based on the 38 intersecting genes yielded a total of 897 GO items, including biological process (BP) items, cellular component (CC) items and molecular function (MF) items. Figure  E shows the distribution of three categories of GO terms. The target gene products were primarily found in various cellular locations, including the plasma membrane rafts, nuclear envelope, lumen of the endoplasmic reticulum, and oxidoreductase complexes. The molecular functions mainly involve glutathione peroxidase activity, iron ion binding, oxidoreductase activity, and glutathione binding. The enrichment scores of the three strongly correlated BP terms consisting of “cellular response to oxidative stress”, “lipoxygenase pathway”, and “response to iron ion” also converged to 1. In addition, 218 relevant pathways of action were identified by KEGG, Reactome, and WikiPathways enrichment analyses, of which 10 key KEGG pathways are shown in Fig.  C, with bar lengths representing the target counts, and the KEGG pathways with higher relevance were Th17 cell differentiation, “HIF-1 signaling pathway”, “IL-17 signaling pathway”, “PI3K-Akt signaling pathway”, “NF-kappa B signaling pathway”, “TNF signaling pathway”, “Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation”, and so on. Reactome analysis indicated that the intracellular localization of the regulatory pathway associated with the target gene was predominantly distributed in signaling by “Sphingolipid signaling pathway”, “Glutathione metabolism”, “Arachidonic acid metabolism” (Fig.  D). WikiPathways enrichment analysis revealed that “Interleukin-4 and Interleukin-13 signaling”, “Synthesis of 15-eicosatetraenoic acid derivatives”, “Metabolism of lipids” (Fig.  D).
 In addition, we constructed a network of ferroptosis-related pathways, and analyzed the interaction of the networks based on MCODE algorithm. It was found that natural flavonoids may treat ferroptosis in asthma through arachidonic acid metabolic pathway, and the key target was ALOX15 (Fig.  F). A network linking compounds, targets, pathways, and diseases (C-T-P-D) was created by compiling information on the relevant compounds, targets, and associated pathways (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Topological analysis of the network showed that the top five therapeutic targets in the network were ALOX15, ALOX5, PTGS2, AKR1C3, and AKR1C1, and the flavonoid categories that showed a strong association with the potential targets were primarily found in A -Flavones, B-Flavonones, and C-Flavonols. Network analysis revealed that natural flavonoids exert their therapeutic effects on asthma mainly through Arachidonic acid metabolism, Interleukin-4 and Interleukin-13 signaling, Th17 cell differentiation, Metabolism of lipids, and IL-17 signaling pathway. Meanwhile, taking the intersection of the results of the C-T and C-T-P-D network analyses (Supplementary Table 4), we identified that Baicalein (A10), Luteolin (A23), Quercetin (C18), Licochalcone A (E4), and Catechin (J2) have potentially strong pharmacological activities in the treatment of ferroptosis in asthma (Supplementary Fig. 1B). In addition, we found that ALOX15 was a key target in the PPI network, ferroptosis-related pathway network and C-T-P-D network, so we took ALOX15 as the main target for future study (Fig. ). 
 

## Molecular Docking and Molecular Dynamics Simulations
We selected ALOX15 and key markers of ferroptosis (GPX4, SLC7A11) as docking proteins and conducted virtual docking screening with five potential natural flavonoids. The results demonstrated that luteolin (A23) exhibited the highest docking scores with ALOX15, GPX4, and SLC7A11 (Supplementary Table 5). The molecular docking results are presented in Fig.  and Supplementary Fig. 2. Visualization using PyMol revealed that luteolin formed hydrogen bonding interactions with the residues ARG-599, ILE-400, and PHE-175 of the ALOX15 protein (Fig.  A). RMSD quantifies variations in atomic positions, serving as an indicator of the overall system’s stability. Conversely, RMSF measures the root mean square displacement of each amino acid within a given framework conformation compared to its average state, which aids in assessing the flexibility of specific protein regions. Molecular dynamics simulations revealed that the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) values for the Lut/ALOX15 complex ranged between 0.04 nm and 0.37 nm. Notably, the RMSF values for the critical active binding residues ARG-599, ILE-400, and PHE-175 were measured as 0.16 nm, 0.13 nm, and 0.34 nm, respectively (Fig.  C). The findings suggest that luteolin establishes stable interactions with the three residues of ALOX15. Furthermore, RMSD analysis revealed that, during the 100 ns simulation, a stable state between luteolin and ALOX15 was achieved after 20 ns. The RMSD values remained relatively constant, indicating a high level of stability in the complex formed by luteolin and ALOX15 (Fig.  B). In summary, we found that luteolin binds effectively to ALOX15 and key markers of ferroptosis (GPX4, SLC7A11). Luteolin is a key compound for ferroptosis in asthma treatment via natural flavonoids and holds potential as an ALOX15 inhibitor.
 

## Luteolin Attenuated Ferroptosis in 16HBE Cells Treated with HDM and LPS by Inhibiting ALOX15
To further confirm whether luteolin attenuates ferroptosis by inhibiting ALOX15 expression, we used an in vitro model of HDM + LPS-induced 16HBE cells. 16HBE cells were analyzed using laser confocal microscopy following FerroOrange staining. The findings indicated that co-treatment with HDM and LPS led to a significant increase in Fe2+ levels in 16HBE cells. Compared with the HDM + LPS group, Fe2+ levels were significantly reduced in a dose-dependent manner in the HDM + LPS + Luteolin group (Fig.  A). Following luteolin treatment, flow cytometry analysis demonstrated a significant decrease in lipid ROS levels (Fig.  B-C), suggesting that luteolin exerted an inhibitory effect on ferroptosis.To further elucidate the mechanism underlying luteolin-mediated ferroptosis inhibition in asthma, we analyzed the expression levels of associated proteins using Western blotting. The results showed that ACSL4 and ALOX15 expression levels were upregulated, whereas SLC7A11 and GPX4 levels were downregulated in HDM + LPS-induced asthma models, suggesting that ferroptosis plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of asthma. Additionally, luteolin treatment downregulated ALOX15 and ACSL4 expression while upregulating SLC7A11 and GPX4, suggesting that luteolin-mediated ALOX15 inhibition alleviates ferroptosis in 16HBE cells (Fig.  D-H). To verify the interaction and stability of luteolin binding to ALOX15, we performed a CETSA-Western blot assay using protein extracts from 16HBE cells treated with DMSO or luteolin. The results showed that ALOX15 exhibited increased thermal stability in the luteolin treatment group (Fig.  D and E). DARTS assay revealed that luteolin inhibited ALOX15 proteolysis by pronase (Fig.  F and G). These results suggest that luteolin attenuates ferroptosis in 16HBE cells by inhibiting ALOX15 expression.

## Comprehensive Transcriptome Analysis of Luteolin Treatment
To further confirm our results, we performed a comprehensive transcriptome analysis along with a differential expression analysis of 16HBE cells following luteolin (Lut) treatment [P < 0.05, log2(Fold change) > 0.5]. The volcano plot indicated that 1,663 genes were significantly downregulated, while 1,088 genes were significantly upregulated (Fig.  A). Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated distinct differences in the transcriptomic profiles between the luteolin-treated and HDM groups (Fig.  B). GO analysis showed that luteolin-associated genes were significantly enriched in pathways related to fatty acid metabolism, cholesterol metabolism, and oxidative stress, suggesting a correlation with ferroptosis (Fig.  F). Furthermore, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of ferroptosis, fatty acid metabolism, and chemical carcinogenesis-reactive oxygen species pathways in differentially expressed genes (Fig.  E), indicating that luteolin treatment modulates gene transcription and has strong potential for regulating ferroptosis. Next, by integrating differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with ferroptosis-related genes, we identified 59 genes associated with luteolin treatment in ferroptosis-related asthma. A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed, revealing 16 genes with significantly altered expression levels (Fig.  C and D). Finally, transcriptomic analysis demonstrated that luteolin has the potential to regulate ALOX15 in ferroptosis-associated asthma treatment (Fig.  G). Additionally, we retrieved RNA-seq datasets of bronchial epithelial cells from asthmatic patients and healthy controls from the GEO database and compared ALOX15 expression levels between asthmatic and healthy samples. We found that ALOX15 expression was significantly upregulated in asthmatic airway epithelial cells (Fig.  H and I).
 

## ALOX15 Silencing Inhibits Ferroptosis in 16HBE Cells Induced by HDM and LPS
To further investigate whether the ferroptosis-inhibitory effect of luteolin is dependent on ALOX15, we performed ALOX15 knockdown in 16HBE cells. Western blot analysis confirmed that ALOX15-specific siRNA effectively reduced ALOX15 expression (Fig.  A). We then assessed lipid ROS and Fe2+ levels. The results demonstrated that both ALOX15 silencing and luteolin treatment significantly reduced Fe2+ accumulation and lipid ROS levels in HDM + LPS-induced 16HBE cells (Fig.  B and C). Furthermore, compared to the HDM + LPS + si-NC + Luteolin-treated group, ALOX15 silencing or luteolin treatment significantly upregulated GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression while downregulating ACSL4 levels (Fig.  D). In conclusion, ALOX15 silencing and luteolin treatment exert comparable inhibitory effects on ferroptosis in asthma.
 

## Luteolin Alleviates Ferroptosis in HDM and LPS-induced Asthma Mice by Inhibiting ALOX15
To investigate the therapeutic effect of luteolin on asthma, hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining was performed on lung tissue from mice. Compared with the control group, the HDM + LPS group had significantly thickened airway walls, narrowed lumina, significant peribronchial inflammatory cell infiltration, epithelial cell damage, and increased goblet cell density, which were consistent with the pathological characteristics of allergen-induced asthma. Compared with the HDM + LPS group, the HDM + LPS + Lut group had reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, less thickening of the bronchial wall, expanded lumina, and improved ventilation function, suggesting that it had a certain repair effect on the airway structure (Fig.  A). To assess the effect of luteolin on lung tissue inflammation and ferroptosis in an asthma mouse model, immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was performed. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in the expression of α-SMA in myofibroblasts, accompanied by a reversal in the expression of key ferroptosis-related proteins, including ALOX15, ACSL4, GPX4, and SLC7A11 (Fig.  B and C). Additionally, MDA staining confirmed that HDM + LPS exposure promoted lipid peroxidation in the lung tissue, whereas luteolin treatment significantly inhibited this process (Fig.  D). Furthermore, luteolin administration led to a marked reduction in the levels of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, thereby attenuating the inflammatory response in the lung tissues of HDM + LPS-induced asthmatic mice (Fig.  D). In summary, luteolin may attenuate ferroptosis and alleviate asthma by inhibiting ALOX15 expression.
 

## The Impact of Luteolin on the Serum Metabolic Profile of Mice
To further investigate the mechanisms underlying changes in the serum metabolic profile of mice induced by luteolin treatment, we performed an analysis of the relative content of differential metabolites. The volcano plot indicated that 32 metabolites showed a significant increase, while 23 metabolites displayed a notable decrease (Fig.  A). A comparison between the luteolin and HDM groups revealed that 29 primary metabolites exhibited significant differences (VIP > 1, P < 0.05) (Fig.  B). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that luteolin influences lipid metabolism pathways, including arachidonic acid metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, and alpha-linolenic acid metabolism (Fig.  C and D). The SMPDB enrichment analysis confirmed this result and revealed that luteolin is associated with glutathione metabolism (Fig.  E). By ranking the abundance of differential metabolites, we identified the top 20 metabolites with the highest absolute abundance values (Fig.  F). Subsequently, we observed significant variations in the relative content of the metabolites lithocholylglycine, ergothioneine, and gossypol following luteolin administration (Fig.  G-I). In summary, we conclude that luteolin is closely associated with lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in asthma.
 

## Discussion
Asthma has significant adverse effects on public health, with increased morbidity and mortality rates among individuals with severe asthma [ ]. Annually, this disease accounts for an estimated 250,000 deaths annually worldwide [ ]. Currently, inhalable corticosteroids and beta-adrenergic agonists are widely used in clinical practice to treat asthma, but some patients have difficulty controlling their condition and experience frequent exacerbations, and current treatment regimens do not fully meet the needs of these patients [ ]. Flavonoids are an important component in natural herbs, and are increasingly being recognized for their potential role in the treatment of asthma due to their anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-ROS properties. Recent studies have found that asthma patients exhibit airway inflammation, lipid peroxidation, and increased ROS levels, alongside the onset of ferroptosis [ ]. Therefore, in this study, we focused on flavonoids, a group of compounds with anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. We constructed a library of natural flavonoids with high oral bioavailability and drug-likeness derived from traditional Chinese herbal medicine. Bioinformatics was used to screen potential ferroptosis-related asthma targets, and the drug discovery process was demonstrated, highlighting the mechanisms through which natural flavonoids target ferroptosis in asthma via network pharmacological modeling. Finally, we identified ALOX15 as a key target for natural flavonoids in the treatment of ferroptosis in asthma.
In the respiratory system, ALOX15 is expressed at elevated levels in airway epithelial cells. It releases chemokines that influence immune cells like eosinophils and plays a role in regulating mucus secretion [ ]. In the progression of asthma, it has been observed that the expression of ALOX15 in bronchial epithelial cells rises with increasing severity of the condition. This heightened activity of ALOX15 may contribute to the production of mucus protein MUC5AC associated with asthma [ ]. In asthma model mice deficient in ALOX15, there was a observed decrease in airway inflammation when compared to the wild-type asthma model mice [ ]. At present, many studies show that targeted regulation of ALOX15 can obviously alleviate ferroptosis in diseases [ – ], but Acyl coenzyme A synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), a key enzyme involved in fatty acid metabolism, has been identified as an important biomarker and driver of ferroptosis, and upregulation of ACSL4 leads to an increase in polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) content of phospholipids. Meanwhile, the mammalian family of arachidonic acid lipoxygenases (ALOXs) can catalyze the oxidation of PUFA to generate AA/AdA-PE-OOH, which exerts a tissue- or cellular-level-dependent role in triggering ferroptosis [ , ]. In this study, we extracted samples from bronchial epithelial tissues and observed a significant increase in ALOX15 expression in individuals with asthma, which was not seen in healthy individuals. We then established asthma models induced by house dust mite and lipopolysaccharide, encompassing both in vitro and in vivo experiments. The results indicated that both 16HBE cells and asthma mice underwent ferroptosis following stimulation by HDM + LPS. We also observed elevated expression levels of ALOX15 and ACSL4 in asthma, while GPX4 and SLC7A11 showed reduced expression. Our findings are consistent with previous studies, suggesting that ALOX15 plays a role in modulating ferroptosis in asthma, making it a potential drug target for therapeutic intervention in this condition [ – ]. Additionally, we found that luteolin alleviated ferroptosis in 16HBE cells by inhibiting ALOX15 expression. After luteolin treatment, ALOX15 and ACSL4 were downregulated, while SLC7A11 and GPX4 were upregulated in 16HBE cells treated with HDM and LPS. The same phenomenon was observed in lung tissues from HDM + LPS-induced asthma model mice. GPX4 is an antioxidant enzyme that, together with glutathione (GSH), functions as a key cofactor to neutralize LOOH and maintain membrane fluidity, thereby protecting cells and membranes from peroxidative damage [ ]. Luteolin can enhance GPX4 activity, which helps to reduce lipid peroxidation and thus reduce ferroptosis-related damage in lung tissues. The reduction in SLC7A11 expression leads to reduced glutathione synthesis, resulting in oxidative stress and ferroptosis [ ]. By upregulating SLC7A11, luteolin may enhance the antioxidant capacity of bronchial epithelial cells and reduce inflammatory responses and oxidative stress in asthma. Furthermore, compared to the HDM + LPS + si-NC + Luteolin groups, ALOX15 silencing or luteolin treatment significantly upregulated GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression, while downregulating ACSL4 expression. Both ALOX15 silencing and luteolin treatment similarly inhibited ferroptosis in asthma.
Luteolin (3’,4’,5,7-tetrahydroxyflavone) is a typical flavonoid, mainly in the form of glycosides in a variety of plants and Chinese herbal medicine, which are high in Dracocephali Integrifolii Herba., Chrysanthemum indicum L., Lonicera japonica Thunb., Perilla frutescens (L.) Britt. It is frequently used for inflammatory disease treatment, especially asthma [ ]. In conscious guinea pigs that are sensitized to ovalbumin (OA) and exposed to aerosolized OA, the effectiveness of flavones in treating asthma is ranked as follows: luteolin ≥ apigenin > baicalein > chrysin > flavone [ ]. Wang’s study found that both the group receiving high doses of luteolin and the dexamethasone group improved lung function in mice with asthma, reduced lung inflammation, and significantly decreased the levels of Th2 cytokines like IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, as well as lowering IgE levels [ ]. Recent studies have shown that baicalin and luteolin can reduce the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and malondialdehyde (MDA) within cells, reduce the expression levels of ASCL4 and PTGS2 mRNA, restore the protein content of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) in cardiomyocytes that has decreased due to ferroptosis inducer, and alleviate myocardial infarction caused by ischemia-reperfusion [ ]. Based on a multi-level network analysis of natural flavonoids in the treatment of asthma-related ferroptosis, we identified that luteolin (A23) exhibits potential pharmacological activity and may treat asthma via multiple targets and pathways. To further elucidate its mechanism in asthma-related ferroptosis, we performed virtual docking screening combined with molecular dynamics simulations, which revealed that luteolin binds effectively to ALOX15 and maintains the protein in a relatively stable conformation, thereby potentially mitigating ferroptosis in asthma by inhibiting ALOX15. Subsequently, CETSA-WB experiments and DARTS assays confirmed that ALOX15 in the luteolin-treated group exhibited enhanced thermal stability. Luteolin was also found to inhibit the proteolysis of ALOX15 by pronase, indicating its direct binding to ALOX15. In vitro studies further confirmed that luteolin inhibits ferroptosis in asthma by reducing lipid peroxidation and Fe2+ accumulation, and by modulating the expression of ALOX15 and key ferroptosis-related genes (ACSL4, GPX4, SLC7A11). Additionally, our results demonstrated that luteolin treatment reduced peribronchial and perivascular inflammatory cell infiltration and histopathological damage in HDM and LPS-treated mice, alleviating bronchospasm and reducing inflammatory cytokine levels. Furthermore, our in vivo studies indicated that luteolin alleviated ferroptosis in HDM and LPS-treated lung tissues by reversing the expression patterns of ALOX15, ACSL4, GPX4, and SLC7A11. In 16HBE cells, specific knockdown of ALOX15 demonstrated that both ALOX15 silencing and luteolin treatment inhibited ferroptosis. Moreover, RNA sequencing analysis of luteolin-treated asthma cell models and serum metabolic profiling of mice revealed that luteolin suppresses ALOX15 expression and modulates arachidonic acid metabolism. Collectively, these results indicate that luteolin mitigates ferroptosis and ameliorates asthma by inhibiting ALOX15 expression.
In this study, we established a library of natural flavonoids with notable oral bioavailability and pharmacological potential, and we analyzed the potential targets and associated pathways through which these compounds mitigate asthma-induced ferroptosis using network pharmacology. Our study identified ALOX15 as a key target in asthma-related ferroptosis, and it demonstrated that luteolin mitigates asthma-induced ferroptosis by targeting ALOX15 (Fig. ). However, it is important to note that asthma is a highly heterogeneous disease with complex diagnostic criteria and a diverse range of phenotypes [ ]. The underlying mechanisms contributing to asthma pathogenesis are multifactorial, including cellular senescence [ ], oxidative stress [ ], and ferroptosis [ ]. Previous studies investigating the therapeutic effects of luteolin in asthma have primarily focused on its anti-inflammatory, anti-allergic, and immune-modulatory properties. For example, luteolin has been shown to promote M2 macrophage polarization and inhibit M1 polarization [ ], enhancing induced Treg function [ ]. It also alleviates neutrophil-driven asthma by modulating IL-36γ and MAPK pathways [ ]. However, the potential role of luteolin in airway epithelial cells, particularly its effects on ferroptosis, has not been fully elucidated. Notably, only Quan et al. have demonstrated that luteolin attenuates airway remodeling in asthma by inhibiting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) through β-catenin regulation [ ]. Our study extends these findings by demonstrating that luteolin alleviates ferroptosis in asthmatic bronchial epithelial cells through targeting ALOX15, thereby providing new insights into potential treatment strategies. Despite these promising findings, our study has several limitations. First, although our results indicate that luteolin alleviates ferroptosis by inhibiting ALOX15, we did not assess the effects of ALOX15 knockdown or overexpression in animal models. Second, our evaluation of the therapeutic effects of luteolin in animal models warrants further investigation into its impact on immune cell function. In summary, although our study positions luteolin as a promising candidate for asthma treatment, the development of more complex in vitro and in vivo models is necessary to validate its clinical relevance and further elucidate the mechanistic pathways through which luteolin modulates asthma and ferroptosis. Addressing these gaps will provide a clearer understanding of the therapeutic potential of luteolin relative to current treatments and help define its role in future asthma management strategies.
 

## Conclusion
Taken together, our findings suggest that luteolin, a natural flavonoid, is a potential natural compound for the treatment of ferroptosis in asthma, and luteolin alleviates ferroptosis in asthmatic airway epithelial cells by inhibiting ALOX15.
