## Abstract
Acetyltransferases play a crucial role in biological processes by modifying a variety of substrates. However, their roles in the virulence of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tb) are poorly understood. To systematically investigate the roles of acetyltransferases in M. tb, we constructed an acetyltransferase mutant library using CRISPR-assisted genome editing and screened for genes that are essential for mouse infection. Seven acetyltransferases were identified as essential for lung infection of M. tb. cysE, encoding a serine acetyltransferase, was confirmed to be required for virulence of M. tb in mice and its replication in macrophages. Further experiments revealed that mutation of cysE or inhibition of CysE by small molecular chemical increased sensitivity to clofazimine treatment. Finally, we demonstrated that cysE is involved in mitigating oxidative stress, which modulates the virulence and drug resistance of M. tb. Our study suggests that targeting cysE offers potential for the development of anti-tuberculosis drugs, particularly for enhancing treatment regimens for drug-resistant tuberculosis through the synergistic effect with clofazimine.

## Introduction
Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tb), resulted in an estimated 10.6 million active cases and 1.3 million deaths in 2022 . Although substantial progress has been achieved in elucidating the biological basis of M. tb pathogenicity, the specific contributions of M. tb physiology and metabolism to persistence, pathogenesis, and drug resistance remain poorly understood.
Acetylation is a widespread modification used to regulate various cellular pathways, including gene expression and protein synthesis . Acetyltransferase enzymes are present in all domains of life and typically transfer the acetyl group (acetyl coenzyme A) to the target substrate in order to regulate its activity or stability . Acetyltransferase enzymes have been demonstrated to modify various small-molecule substrates, such as tRNAs, polyamines, cell wall components, antibiotics, and toxins. They are also involved in detoxification, synthesis, and transcription processes . There are 47 acetyltransferase-encoding genes in the genome of M. tb. Some of these enzymes have been implicated in M. tb virulence. For instance, Rv0859 can modulate the immune response to suppress the host’s pro-inflammatory reaction . Rv2170 encodes an aminoglycoside acetyltransferase that acetylates and alters various antibiotics, leading to drug resistance . Rv3423.1 functions as an epigenetic regulator that manipulates anti-inflammatory genes to promote bacterial survival . eis upregulates IL-10 through histone H3 acetylation, which activates the Akt/mTOR/p70S6K pathway and hampers host innate immune defenses by modulating autophagy, inflammation, and cell death in a redox-dependent manner – . Although some M. tb acetyltransferases have been implicated in host-pathogen interactions, the functions of the majority of these enzymes in M. tb are still unclear.
To systematically study essential genes in vivo and in vitro, various molecular genetic tools have been developed in M. tb – . Transposon insertion sequencing has been widely used to screen the essential genes of M. tb in vivo and in vitro , . However, this method is limited to whole-genome screening and cannot be used to investigate genes associated with specific cellular pathways. Recently, CRISPR interference and CRISPR knockout methods have been developed in M. tb and used for genome-wide screening to investigate genetic and chemical-genetic interactions in M. tb in vitro – . However, a CRISPR screen for investigation of the genes essential for M. tb virulence in vivo has not been conducted.
The role of acetyltransferases in M. tb virulence was investigated by knocking out acetyltransferase-encoding genes in M. tb using the CRISPR-NHEJ method. The genes essential for survival in vivo were identified through aerosol infection. cysE, which encodes a serine acetyltransferase, was found to be crucial for M. tb survival in macrophages and animals as well as resistance to clofazimine (CFZ). Mutation of cysE altered global gene expression, reducing the pathogen’s ability to counter host-induced redox stress, thereby impacting its survival and drug tolerance. Overall, our study suggests that CysE could serve as a potential drug target.

## Results

## Screening of M. tb acetyltransferases essential for in vivo infection
Bioinformatic analysis identified 47 potential acetyltransferases in M. tb , , five of which were essential for viability – (Supplementary Table  ). Given the established role of acetyltransferases in bacterial pathogenesis , , we systematically investigated their functions in M. tb by generating knockout mutants of the non-essential acetyltransferase genes using CRISPR-NHEJ . We successfully constructed 37 mutants (Supplementary Table  ), which were pooled with two wild-type (WT) control strains (expressing non-targeting sgRNAs) and delivered via aerosol infection into adult C57BL/6N mice (~1 × 103 CFUs/lung at day 1 post-infection; p.i.) (Supplementary Fig.  ). Bacterial loads were assessed by plating lung homogenates on 7H10 agar at days 14 and 28 p.i., ~3 × 104 colonies were harvested for CRISPR sequencing and analysis (Fig.  ).
 The average Pearson’s correlation coefficients of the mean read counts for each gene across the three libraries in mice on days 14 and 28 p.i. were 0.295–0.908 and 0.60–0.85, indicating strong correlations among the libraries (Supplementary Fig.  ). Mutants displaying a ≥10-fold reduction in sgRNA abundance compared to the WT were classified as attenuated in virulence (Supplementary Data  ). By applying these criteria to the data from each time point, we identified three genes (Rv0819, Rv1347c, and Rv2335) as crucial for lung infection by M. tb at day 14 p.i., and four additional genes (Rv0428c, Rv0859, Rv1323, and Rv2775) as essential at day 28 p.i. (Fig.  and Supplementary Data  ). Notably, the identification of Rv1347c (mbtK) and Rv0859 (fadA) as essential genes for M. tb survival in mice supported the validity of our screening approach , .

## cysE is required for M. tb survival in macrophages and mice
Rv2335 (cysE), which encodes a serine acetyltransferase and is involved in the de novo cysteine biosynthesis pathway , , emerged as one of the most critical genes for in vivo infection in our screening. To confirm the screening results, we constructed a cysE deletion mutant (ΔcysE) and its complemented strain (ΔcysE::cysE) (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). While cysE deletion caused mild growth impairment in 7H9 medium (Supplementary Fig.  ), this defect was rescued by supplementation with O-acetylserine (OAS), the direct metabolic product of CysE (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ) . In infection assays using THP-1 macrophages, the ΔcysE strain showed ~3-fold reduced survival compared to WT and complemented strains in resting macrophages (Fig.  ). Notably, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) + IFN-γ activated macrophages restricted M. tb survival by ~20-fold relative to resting macrophages, with the ΔcysE mutant exhibiting an additional 2-fold reduction compared to WT strains in this activated state (Fig.  ).
 The virulence of the cysE mutant was further evaluated in a murine aerosol infection model. The ΔcysE strain exhibited significantly impaired proliferation in both lungs and spleens, showing 25- and 62-fold reductions in bacterial loads compared to WT at day 28 p.i., and 41- and 45-fold reductions at day 42 p.i., respectively (Fig.  ). Genetic complementation restored bacterial burdens to WT levels in both organs at day 42 p.i. Histopathological analysis demonstrated substantial lymphocytic infiltrates in lungs of mice infected with either WT or complemented strains at both time points, while ΔcysE infected lungs displayed markedly attenuated pathology with reduced lesion formation and inflammatory responses (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). In conclusion, these findings support the requirement of cysE for M. tb survival in macrophages and mice.

## Disruption of the cysE gene heightens sensitivity of M. tb to oxidants
M. tb is exposed to multiple stresses within host macrophages . To identify the specific factors affecting ΔcysE survival in vivo, we systematically evaluated its response to various stressors. The ΔcysE mutant showed normal resistance to nutrient starvation, acidic pH (5.5) (Fig.  ), and SDS-induced membrane stress (Fig.  ). However, ΔcysE exhibited markedly compromised survival (~10-fold reduction) upon exposed to peroxide radicals (Fig.  ), with heightened susceptibility to all tested peroxide stressors (H₂O₂, MND, and CHP) (Fig.  ). This survival defect was partially reversed by OAS supplementation (Fig.  ). Notably, the reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenger thiourea (TU) not only restored ΔcysE survival under peroxide stress but also ameliorated its growth defect in 7H9 medium (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). These findings indicate that ROS sensitivity primarily accounts for the observed growth and survival impairments of the ΔcysE mutant under in vitro conditions.
 To further investigate whether ROS are a primary determinant of killing of ΔcysE by macrophages, we examined if the ROS scavenger N-acetylcysteine (NAC) promoted resistance of ΔcysE to macrophages. NAC, an antioxidant that furnishes the thiol (-SH) group, can act directly on ROS and free radicals . Both NAC and OAS supplementation restored ΔcysE survival in both resting and LPS + IFN-γ-activated THP-1 macrophages to WT levels (Fig.  ). Overall, these findings support the significance of cysE in enabling M. tb to combat oxidative stress both in macrophages and in vitro.

## cysE is required for regular production of free thiols in M. tb
To investigate if the growth defect and increased sensitivity to oxidants observed in ΔcysE are linked to metabolic dysfunction, we analyzed the metabolomes of the WT and ΔcysE strains. Metabolites were detected by untargeted, relative quantification liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) (Supplementary Fig.  ). In ΔcysE, 180 metabolites were modified compared with the WT, with 82 upregulated (~9.4%) and 98 downregulated (~11.2%) (Supplementary Data  ). Notably, L-serine and acetyl-CoA, the substrates of CysE, were accumulated significantly in ΔcysE (Fig.  ) , while L-cysteine levels remained comparable to WT (Supplementary Fig.  ). L-cysteine-derived molecules such as mycothiol (MSH), ergothioneine (EGT), H2S, and other Fe-S cluster-containing proteins alleviate oxidative stress . Strikingly, EGT was significantly reduced in ΔcysE (Fig.  ), while MSH became undetectable.
 Free thiols, particularly MSH, play a crucial role in the redox balance, antioxidant stress, and drug susceptibility of M. tb . To investigate whether cysE-mediated regulation influences L-cysteine-derived free thiols, we estimated the free thiol content in WT, ΔcysE, and ΔcysE::cysE. The ΔcysE mutant showed a 25% reduction in free thiols compared to WT and complemented (ΔcysE::cysE) strains under normal conditions. This deficiency was completely rescued by OAS supplementation (Fig.  ). Taken together, these findings indicate that cysE is necessary to preserve the redox balance in M. tb, which is a crucial mechanism to combat oxidative stress.

## Deletion of cysE enhances the susceptibility of M. tb to CFZ
The redox status and metabolic state of M. tb significantly influence its susceptibility to anti-TB drugs , , . To evaluate the effect of cysE mutation on drug sensitivity, we determined the 90% minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC90) of first- and second-line anti-TB drugs against WT, ΔcysE, and ΔcysE::cysE strains using microplate alamarBlue assay. While most drugs showed comparable MIC₉₀ values across all strains, we observed significant differences specifically for CFZ and bedaquiline (BDQ) (Table  ).
 The impact of cysE on the susceptibility of M. tb to BDQ and CFZ was further evaluated by analyzing Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) (Fig.  ) and performing colony-forming unit (CFU) assays (Fig.  ). Consistent with MIC₉₀ data (Table  ), ΔcysE exhibited marginally lower MIC₅₀ values for both BDQ and CFZ compared to WT (Fig.  ). There were no significant differences in the CFU counts of the WT, ΔcysE, and ΔcysE::cysE strains upon treatment with BDQ or CFZ individually for 3 days, whereas the CFU counts of ΔcysE was notably reduced after 7 days of CFZ exposure, but not BDQ exposure (Fig.  ). In line with previous studies , combined treatment with BDQ and CFZ for 3 and 7 days demonstrated increased efficacy in killing M. tb, and resulted in more than 10- and 103-fold reductions in viability of the WT, respectively (Fig.  ). Intriguingly, ΔcysE resulted in more than 100- and 105-fold decreases in viability following 3 and 7 days of combined BDQ and CFZ treatment, respectively (Fig.  ). The supplementation of OAS or TU can rescue the survival defect of ΔcysE strain on CFZ or BDQ combined with CFZ (Fig.  ). The increased drug susceptibility phenotype persisted in macrophages, where ΔcysE showed reduced survival following both mono- and combination therapies (Fig.  ).
 CFZ treatment enhances the ROS level, which has been considered as a mechanism to kill M. tb . CysE might affect the ROS level in M. tb and thereby affect resistance to CFZ. To test this hypothesis, ROS levels were measured in M. tb cultures treated with CFZ or BDQ by flow cytometry. Consistent with previous findings , treatment with CFZ but not BDQ strongly increased ROS levels in M. tb. Mutation of cysE did not increase ROS levels upon treatment with BDQ or without drug treatment but significantly enhanced ROS levels upon treatment with CFZ or combined treatment with CFZ and BDQ (Fig.  ). These findings indicate that mutation of cysE enhances bactericidal activity by elevating ROS levels during CFZ treatment and combined CFZ and BDQ therapy.
BDQ targets AtpE, which is involved in ATP synthesis, and therefore treatment with BDQ can decrease ATP levels in mycobacteria, which is a major mechanism underlying the bactericidal activity of BDQ . To ascertain whether drug treatment changes ATP levels, we also quantified ATP in cells treated with or without drugs. Consistent with previous findings, BDQ treatment for 48 h significantly reduced ATP levels in M. tb, while CFZ treatment did not affect ATP levels. Intriguingly, the lack of cysE decreased ATP levels in the initial stages (3 and 24 h) of BDQ treatment but did not continue to decrease ATP levels upon longer treatment (Fig.  ), which potentially explains why cysE mutation did not enhance the bactericidal effect of BDQ on M. tb.

## The cysE gene orchestrates transcriptomic changes in response to oxidative stress and energy metabolism
We then examined if any transcriptional reactions were linked to the redox, metabolic, and bioenergetic alterations observed in ΔcysE. RNA-sequencing analysis was conducted to assess mRNA levels in the WT and ΔcysE strains under standard cultivation conditions. Compared with the WT transcriptome, ΔcysE showed altered expression of 1669 genes [q ≤ 0.05, absolute fold change ≥ 2], with 874 genes upregulated (~21.97%) and 795 genes downregulated (~19.98%) (Fig.  and Supplementary Data  ). Functional categorization of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the ΔcysE mutant revealed significant enrichment in key metabolic processes including: (i) Translation, ribosomal structure and biogenesis; (ii) Energy production and conversion; (iii) Transcription; and (iv) Amino acid transport and metabolism. Conversely, genes associated with “Replication, recombination and repair” were significantly underrepresented (Fig.  ). Seven differentially regulated genes were selected and verified via quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), validating the RNA-sequencing results (Fig.  ). In addition, supplementation of OAS could partially rescue the transcript levels of cysE mutant (Fig.  ).
 Genes related to energy production and conversion (e.g., ATP synthase, energy, and respiration genes) were significantly upregulated in ΔcysE compared with the WT (Fig.  ), while central carbon metabolism genes were downregulated, indicating that reduced ATP levels in ΔcysE disrupt energy metabolism homeostasis. The cysE mutation increased expression of genes associated with the oxidative stress response (Fig.  ). Furthermore, genes involved in iron acquisition were downregulated, with iron-responsive repressors upregulated in the absence of cysE (Fig.  ), suggesting that iron homeostasis is impacted by oxidative stress. Intriguingly, the cysE mutation triggered expression of DosR and DosR-dependent genes (Fig.  ), which are typically induced during dormancy . The reduced energy level in the cysE mutant may be responsible for inducing DosR-related genes. Overall, comprehensive transcriptomic analysis indicates that cysE mutation significantly influences gene expression in M. tb in response to oxidative stress and energy metabolism.

## Serine acetyltransferase inhibitor can effectively enhance the efficacy of CFZ against M. tb
Compound 1 (C1) was identified as a putative CysE inhibitor in M. tb through in-silico screening (Fig.  ). To explore whether CysE might serve as an important target for adjunct TB therapy, we assessed the synergistic effects of C1 on the bactericidal activity of CFZ and BDQ. While C1 monotherapy showed no bactericidal activity, it significantly potentiated the lethal effects of CFZ and CFZ-BDQ combination therapy against WT (Fig.  ). Notably, this synergistic effect was absent in the ΔcysE mutant (Fig.  ), demonstrating that C1’s adjuvant activity requires functional CysE. This indicates that the CysE inhibitor C1 might work in conjunction with CFZ and BDQ to effectively eliminate M. tb.
 

## Discussion and conclusion
Genome-wide CRISPR-based screening technology is a powerful functional genomics tool for multiple investigations of biological processes in M. tb – , . In this study, we constructed a small library of 37 acetyltransferase mutants in M. tb using the CRISPR-NHEJ method and systematically investigated the roles of acetyltransferases during M. tb infection in C57BL/6N mice. To our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically evaluate M. tb genes associated with a specific pathway through aerosol infection. Our study identified seven acetyltransferase genes, including cysE (Rv2335), mshD (Rv0819), mbtK (Rv1347c), Rv2775, Rv1323, fadA (Rv0859), and Rv0428c, required for survival in the mouse lung (Fig.  and Supplementary Data  ). Among them, mbtK and fadA were previously reported to be necessary for M. tb survival in mice , .
Consistent with previous transposon library screening , , cysE is required for survival of M. tb in macrophages and mice (Fig.  ). The acetyltransferase CysE is crucial for synthesis of OAS, the precursor of L-cysteine . The absence of OAS in the ΔcysE mutant may interfere L-cysteine production, leading to reduced levels of L-cysteine-derived antioxidants like free thiols, thereby increasing vulnerability to oxidative stress and decreasing M. tb survival in vivo. This hypothesis is supported by the following three findings: (i) mutation of cysE decreased the thiol level in M. tb and increased sensitivity of M. tb to oxidative stress (Fig.  and Fig.  ); (ii) OAS supplementation restored thiol levels and oxidative stress tolerance in the cysE mutant (Figs.  , ); and (iii) addition of NAC to macrophages rescued the survival defect of the cysE mutant (Fig.  ).
CFZ, a generator of reactive oxygen intermediates, is believed to eradicate M. tb by elevating ROS levels . Consistent with the finding that cysE mutation heightens sensitivity of M. tb to oxidative stress, the cysE mutation significantly boosted CFZ-induced killing of M. tb (Fig.  ). This increased bactericidal activity might be due to the increased ROS level in the cysE mutant upon CFZ treatment (Fig.  ). Mutation of cysE in M. tb decreased the MIC90 and MIC50 values of BDQ, but did not affect its bactericidal activity (Fig.  ). This disparity may be because the absence of cysE in M. tb decreased ATP levels only in the initial stages of BDQ treatment, not upon prolonged BDQ exposure (Fig.  ). BDQ and CFZ have shown potential synergy in M. tb elimination . Importantly, the absence of cysE further intensifies the bactericidal efficacy of BDQ and CFZ in vitro and in macrophages, suggesting that CysE is a potential promising target for developing anti-TB drugs, especially in conjunction with BDQ and CFZ. This hypothesis is further supported by the observation that C1, a small molecular chemical inhibitor of CysE identified through in silico screening, enhances the drug efficacy of BDQ and CFZ (Fig.  ). However, whether C1 specifically target CysE and exhibits similar function in vivo remains to be investigated.
We constructed a small acetyltransferase mutant library in M. tb using CRISPR-assisted genome editing and screened for acetyltransferase-encoding genes required for survival of M. tb in vivo. CysE was identified as one of most required acetyltransferases for M. tb growth in lungs of mice. In addition, the absence of CysE in M. tb enhances the bactericidal effect of CFZ alone or in conjunction with BDQ. Therefore, our study suggests that the CysE is a potential attractive target for the design of anti-mycobacterial drugs and provides new insights for design of effective combined drug therapy regimes to eliminate TB.

## Materials and methods

## Bacterial strains and culture conditions
The bacterial strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table  . Unless noted otherwise, Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37Rv and its derivatives were grown at 37 °C in Middlebrook 7H9 broth (Difco) supplemented with 0.2% glycerol (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.05% Tween 80 (Sigma-Aldrich) and 10% Oleic Albumin Dextrose Catalase (OADC; Becton Dickinson) or on 7H10 plates (Difco) supplemented with the appropriate antibiotics, 0.5% glycerol, and 10% OADC. Sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), H2O2, Menadione (MND), Cumyl Hydroperoxide (CHP), L-Cysteine, OAS, NAC were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Hygromycin B (Hyg), Kanamycin (kana), Zeocin (zeo), Anhydrotetracycline (ATc), Rifampicin (RIF), Isoniazide (INH), Levofloxacin (LFX), Amikacin (AMK), Ethambutol (EMB), CFZ, BDQ, Linezolid (LZD), Dramani (DLM) were purchased from MedChemExpress. Compound 1 (C1, CAS: 875007-19-5) was purchased from Topscience. All antibiotic stocks were prepared in DMSO. When indicated, antibiotics and small molecules were used at the following concentrations: kana (25 μg/mL), hyg (50 μg/mL), zeo (50 μg/mL), ATc (50 ng/mL), and C 1 (0.5 mg/mL) . Primers and sgRNAs used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table  .

## Acetyltransferase knockout library construction and CRISPR screen
M. tb competent cells were prepared and transformed as described previously . Briefly, H37Rv cells were grown in roller bottles (Greiner Bio-one, #681072) containing 300 mL of 7H9 medium at 37 °C for 7–10 days. When the culture reached an OD600 of 0.7–0.8, 30 ml of 15% glycine stock solution was added, yielding a final concentration of 1.5%, and the cultures were incubated in roller bottles at 37 °C for an additional 20–24 h. The cells were collected by centrifugation, washed three times at room temperature in 10% glycerol, and resuspended in 15 ml of 10% glycerol, divided into 200 μL aliquots.
A total of 42 sgRNA expression plasmids targeting nonessential acetyltransferase genes were constructed on the integrative plasmid pYC1446 individually, as previously described . The prepared competent cells were mixed with 200 ng of plasmid and transferred to a 0.2-cm electrode gap electroporation cuvette (Bio-Rad, #1652082). Cells were electroporated using a Gene Pulser Xcell electroporation system (Bio-Rad, #1652660) at an optimized setting of 2.5 kV, 25 μF, and 1000 Ω. The cells were subsequently recovered in 2 ml of 7H9 medium supplemented with OADC and incubated for 24 h and spread onto 7H10 agar plates supplemented with kanamycin, zeocin and ATc. The resulting transformants were first picked for analysis of PCR and sequencing analysis to verify genome editing. Finally, 37 acetyltransferase mutant strains were obtained and 2 control strains with non-target sgRNA were mixed for the knockout library (Supplementary Table  ).
The female C57BL/6N mice aged 4–6 weeks were infected with M. tb acetyltransferase knockout library. 11 mice were infected with 500–1000 CFU/animal using an inhalation exposure system (099 C A4224, Glas-Col, USA) as previously described . Briefly, M. tb cells were cultured in 10 ml of 7H9 medium supplemented with 0.05% Tween 80, 0.2% glycerol, and 1 ml OADC, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 5–7 days. M. tb cells were harvested at an OD600 ~ 0.8, washed twice with PBS-T (PBS medium supplemented with 0.05% Tween 80), and resuspended in 5 mL PBS-T with ~4.2 × 107 M. tb cells for aerosol infection. Three mice were sacrificed at day 1 p.i., and four mice were sacrificed at days 14 and 28 p.i., respectively. Tissues from infected mice were harvested and homogenized in 1 ml of PBS. The bacterial counts in the lungs were detected by plating 10-fold serial dilutions on 7H10 agar plates. Aliquots containing 3 × 104 colonies from three mice at day 14 and 28 days were collected for genomic DNA extraction and sequencing analyses, respectively.

## Genomic DNA extraction and library preparation for Illumina sequencing
Ethanol inactivated M. tb pellets were resuspended in 950 μL of GTE buffer (50 mmol/L glucose, 25 mmol/L Tris-HCl pH8, 10 mmol/L EDTA) containing 20 mg/mL lysozyme and incubated at 37 °C overnight . To each sample was added 50 μL of 10% SDS and 80 μL of 2.5 mg/mL proteinase K, and the samples were incubated at 55 °C for 2 h. Add the same amount of DNA extract as the sample and let stand for 10 min. Each isolated genomic DNA sample was resuspended in 45 μL RNase-free water, and each gDNA was electrophoresed on 1% agarose gels, and its concentration was measured by Nanodrop. Genomic DNA was used as a template for PCR amplification of the sgRNA-encoding region. The resulting ~ 240 bp PCR fragments were sent to GENEWIZ, Inc. (Suzhou, China) for amplicon sequencing. Briefly, the purified PCR fragments were treated with End Prep Mix for end repair, 5’ phosphorylation, and dA-tailing in one reaction, followed by T-A ligation to add adaptors to both ends. The adaptor-ligated DNAs were purified using sample purification beads (SPBs) and PCR-amplified for eight cycles using P5 and P7 primers. The PCR products were cleaned up using SPBs, validated by Qsep100 (Bioptic, Taiwan, China), and quantified by Qubit3.0 Fluorometry (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Libraries with different indexes were multiplexed and loaded onto an Illumina NovaSeq instrument in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed using a 2 × 150 paired-end configuration; image analysis and base calling were performed with HiSeq Control Software (HCS) + OLB + GAPipeline-1.6 (Illumina) on the HiSeq instrument. Approximately 200 thousand reads per library were collected.

## NGS data analysis
Sequencing results were analyzed using the MAGeCK robust rank analysis method (version 0.5.9.4) . During CRISPR screening, the read count was normalized relative to the reading of total sgRNAs in each sample. For comparisons of enrichment or depletion, sgRNAs with fewer than 10 read counts in each control sample replicate were filtered out of the analysis. The MAGeCK gene summary output results can be found in Supplementary Data  .

## Mice infection and analysis
The female C57BL/6 N mice aged 4–6 weeks were infected with M. tb WT, ΔcysE and ΔcysE:: cysE strains. 13 mice of each group (Total: 39) were infected with 500–1000 CFUs/animal using an inhalation exposure system (099 C A4224, Glas-Col, USA) as previously described . The mice were sacrificed at days 1, 28 and 42 p.i., the bacterial counts in the lungs and spleens were detected by plating 10-fold serial dilutions on 7H10 agar plates. The lung tissue from mice infected with various strains at days 28 and 42 p.i. was stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological analysis. The GEMINI liquid-based cytology artificial intelligence-assisted diagnostic system (BIOPIC, China) was used to digitally capture the images of the lung lobes. The inflammatory infiltration area was analyzed using ImageJ2.

## Generation of M. tuberculosis mutant
To generate an unmarked ΔcysE deletion mutant, we constructed the ΔcysE mutant using a replicated plasmid which was further cured from the mutant . Briefly, competent M. tb cells harboring the pYC1759 vector (pNHEJ-recX-sacB) were prepared as previously described . The sgRNA expression plasmid targeting the cysE gene was constructed and electroporated into these competent M. tb cells. Transformed cells were cultured in 1 mL of 7H9 medium supplemented with OADC at 37 °C for 48 h, then plated on 7H10 agar plates containing 25 μg/mL kanamycin, 50 μg/mL zeocin, and 50 ng/mL ATc. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 3–4 weeks. Recombinant colonies were verified by PCR amplification and sequencing (Supplementary Table  ). Helper plasmids were further cured through selection on 7H10 agar plates supplemented with 2% sucrose. The construct was verified by DNA sequencing. Sequence of the oligonucleotides used in the study are provided in Supplementary Table  .
For complementation experiments, the full-length cysE gene (Rv2335) and the 500 bp region immediately upstream were amplified from M. tb genomic DNA using specific primers. Full-length hsp60 was amplified from pMV261 using specific primers and two fragments cloned into the Not I/Xba I sites in pYC874-L5(pYC2125), to generate pYC874-L5-cysE, electricity transfer to ΔcysE strain to produce ΔcysE::cysE strain. The construct was verified by DNA sequencing (Supplementary Table  ).

## Macrophage infection experiments
The THP-1 cells preserved in our laboratory were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C. THP-1 cells were treated with 20 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (Sigma, USA) for 48 h, followed by a 12 h rest before infection. Activating the cells with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/ml) and IFN-γ (20 ng /ml) for 24 h. M. tb WT, ΔcysE and ΔcysE::cysE strains were grown to OD600 ~ 0.8 in 7H9-OADC medium. Then, centrifuged and resuspended in RPMI to obtain a single cell suspension. Infect THP-1 macrophage at a 1:10 (cells: bacteria) multiplicity of infection (MOI) and incubate in CO2 incubator at 37 °C. Cells were subsequently washed 3 times with PBS after 6 h to removed extracellular M. tb, lysed with 0.5% triton, CFU were counted on 7H10 plates. Continue to incubate in other wells for 48 or 72 h, then calculate CFU as above.

## In vitro growth phenotype
To evaluate the impact of cysE disruption in vitro growth of M. tb. WT, ΔcysE and ΔcysE::cysE strains were inoculated into 7H9, PBS containing 0.05% Tween 80 (PBS-T), and 7H9 (pH 5.5) at OD600 ~ 0.04. Aliquots were withdrawn to measure OD600 at the indicated time points, and draw the growth curve. For detecting the growth of different strains on 7H10 solid plates, bacterial suspensions were serially diluted and take 50 μL bacteria serially diluted on the plates to monitor the growth.

## MIC determination
The MICs of drugs against M. tb WT, ΔcysE and ΔcysE::cysE strains were determined using the broth microdilution technique. Various anti-TB drugs was serially diluted 2-fold in 96-well clear plates, an 100 μL of M. tb culture (OD600 ~ 0.01) was added to each well, and the assay plates were incubated at 37 °C for 7 days. OD600 values were recorded using an EnVision multimode microplate reader (PerkinElmer), and IC50 curves were plotted using GraphPad Prism 9 software. On day 7, 20 μL of Alamar blue and 50 μL of 5% Tween 80 were sequentially added to the wells. Each MIC was performed twice independently, with triplicate sample per experiment.

## Cell viability assays
THP- 1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 5 × 104/well. 20× MIC drugs were added into wells with five replicates for each drug. Cell viability was assayed 48 h later using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays (Transgen Biotech. Beijing, China) as per the instruction of the manufacture.

## Kill-kinetic assays
M. tb WT, ΔcysE and ΔcysE::cysE strains were grown to mid-log-phase (OD600 ~ 0.8) in 7H9. Bacteria cells were collected by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS-T, and inoculated into 7H9 medium at OD600 ~ 0.3. H2O2 (10 mmol/L), Menadione (MND, 0.05 mmol/L), Cumyl Hydroperoxide (CHP, 0.1 mmol/L), or 0.05% SDS were added separately for 24 h of treatment, control cells did not receive any stress agent. Bacteria cells were collected by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS-T, and inoculated into 7H9 medium at OD600 ~ 0.04. 20 × MIC BDQ, CFZ, or BDQ + CFZ were added separately for 7 days of treatment. Bacteria cells were collected by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS-T, CFUs were enumerated after 3–4 weeks of incubation at 37 °C.

## Disk diffusion assays
M. tb WT, ΔcysE and ΔcysE::cysE strains were grown in 7H9 broth before dilution to an OD600 ~ 0.7. 500 μL of bacterial culture was mixed with 2.5 mL of 7H9 broth and 3 mL of 7H9 soft agar before plating onto 7H10 agar plates containing the appropriate antibiotics. Sterile filter paper disks saturated with 10 μL of 5% SDS, H2O2 (10 mmol/L), MND (5 mmol/L) or CHP (10 mmol/L) were placed onto the hardened agar, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 weeks. The diameters of the zones of growth inhibition were recorded and analyzed statistically, experiment was performed in duplicate.

## Quantitation of cellular ATP levels
The ATP assay kit was from Beyotime and the assay was performed according to manufacturer’s instruction (S0026B). M. tb WT, ΔcysE and ΔcysE::cysE strains density was adjusted to an OD600 ~ 0.4, and drugs were added to the desired concentrations subsequently. After incubation for 3, 24 and 48 h, samples were mixed with 200 μL ATP detection of lysate and incubated at 100 °C for 5 min. The samples were centrifuged, and the supernatants were transferred to fresh tubes. 100 μL of each supernatant was mixed with an equal volume of ATP detection reagent for 5 min in the dark. Luminescence was measured using an EnVision multimode microplate reader (PerkinElmer).

## Measurement of ROS by flow cytometry
Intracellular ROS accumulation was measured using an ROS-sensitive fluorescence dye as described previously . Carboxy-H2DCFDA (final concentration 10 μmol/L) was added to cultures for detection of total intracellular ROS. A culture lacking the fluorescent dye was included as a control for autofluorescence (little was detected). After drug treatment for M. tb WT, ΔcysE and ΔcysE::cysE strains 1, 3 and 24 h, the fluorescence intensity of bacterial cells was measured using fluorescence-based flow cytometry with a CytoFLEX A00-1-1102 flow cytometer. All tubes with cultures were wrapped with aluminum foil to avoid light. Samples (200 μL), taken at various times, were chilled on ice and subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry. A total of 100,000 cells was analyzed at a speed of 35 μL/min for each sample to determine fluorescence values. The detection parameters were 20-mV laser power, 533/30-nm band pass filter (FL1-channel). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software. Each data point represents the average of at least three independent measurements.

## Thiol estimation
Protein thiol was estimated using a commercially available kit (Invitrogen-Cat no. EIARSHF) as per the manufacturer’s instructions . The kit contains a non-fluorescent molecule that covalently binds free thiol in the sample to produce a fluorescent product. M. tb WT, ΔcysE and ΔcysE::cysE strains were either left untreated or subjected to OAS for 6 h. WCLs were prepared in the presence of DTT to reduce oxidized thiols. The concentration of thiols in μmol/L/mg lysate was estimated with respect to the standard curve obtained using different concentrations of NAC.

## L-cysteine estimation
Use the Cytosine (Cys) Colorimetric Assay Kit for detection. Product code: E-BC-K352-M. Detection principle: L-cysteine reduces phosphotungstic acid to produce tungsten blue, which has an absorption peak at 600 nm. By measuring the absorbance value at 600 nm, the content of L-cysteine can be calculated. M. tb WT and ΔcysE strains collected 1 × 109 bacteria, respectively, add 0.5 mL of reagent 1, perform mechanical homogenization 3 times. Centrifuge at 4 °C and 10,000 × g for 5 min, then take the supernatant and place it on ice for testing. Add 50 μL of standard or sample to the 96 well plate, add 100uL of test agent 2 and 100 μL of test agent 3. Shake the plate on the enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) reader for 5 s and let it stand at room temperature for 10 min. Measure the OD values of each well at 600 nm on the ELISA reader.

## Metabolite extraction and analysis
Metabolite extraction from M. tb WT and ΔcysE strains was performed as outlined . Briefly, exponentially growing M. tb WT and ΔcysE cultures (OD600 ~ 0.6) were quenched with four volumes of 60% methanol for 5 min in a dry ice-methanol bath, followed by centrifugation at 4500 r/min for 5 min. The pellet was resuspended in 700 μL of 60% methanol and centrifuged at 4500 r/min for 5 min. The pellet obtained was resuspended in 1 mL of 75% ethanol and incubated at 80 °C for exactly 3 min, with intermittent mixing at 1.5 min intervals, followed by incubation on ice for 5 min and centrifugation at 13,000 r/min for 15 min. The final supernatant was lyophilized and then stored at −80 °C until further analysis.
The LC-MS/MS analysis of sample was conducted on a Thermo UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system equipped with an ACQUITY HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.8 μmol/L; Waters, USA) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The pretreatment of LC/MS raw data was performed by Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) software, and a three-dimensional data matrix in CSV format was exported. All the analyses of metabolomics data were performed on Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://cloud.majorbio. com) which include Partial Least Square-Discriminant Analysis (PLS- DA), Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) analysis, t-test analysis and KEGG enrichment . The metabolites with VIP > 1, p < 0.05 were determined as significantly different metabolites between the WT and ΔcysE strains based on the VIP obtained by the PLS-DA model and the p-value generated by student’s t test.

## RNA isolation and qRT-PCR
RNA was extracted according to the FastRNA® Pro Blue Kit manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 10 mL of M. tb WT and ΔcysE cultures (OD600 ~ 1.0) were resuspended in RNAproTM solution, suspensions were processed in the FastRNA® instrument (MP FastPrep system, MP Biomedicals) for 40 s at a setting of 6.0. The lysed cell mixture was extracted with chloroform, and the RNA pellet was dissolved in RNase-free water. A total of 200 ng of purified RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using HiScript II Reverse Transcriptase (Vazyme, Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. qRT-PCRs were performed using PowerUPTM SYBR TM Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Inc.) in the ABI 7900 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Inc.). Data were normalized to 16 s rRNA (rrs gene) expression levels. The 2(−∆∆Ct) method was used to analyze relative changes in gene expression.

## RNA sequencing and analysis
Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol Reagent/RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). rRNA was depleted from total RNA using rRNA removal Kit. The ribosomal depleted RNA was then fragmented and reverse transcribed. The first cDNA strand was synthesized using ProtoScript II Reverse Transcriptase with random primers and Actinomycin D. The second cDNA strand was synthesized using Second Strand Synthesis Enzyme Mix. dA-tailing was performed in one reaction, followed by a T-A ligation to add adaptors to both ends. Size selection of Adaptor-ligated DNA was then performed using beads, and fragments of ~400 bp were recovered. Each sample was then amplified by PCR using P5 and P7 primers. The PCR products were validated using an Qsep100 (Bioptic, Taiwan, China), and quantified by Qubit3.0 Fluorometer. Libraries with different indices were multiplexed and loaded on an Illumina HiSeq/Novaseq instrument according to the manufacturer’s instructions, sequencing was carried out using a 2 × 150 PE configuration.

## Statistics and reproducibility
Unless mentioned otherwise, experiments described in the manuscript were executed independently at least twice, being performed minimally in triplicates each time. Statistical significance was determined with unpaired two-tailed t test and one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for comparison of multiple groups. The error bar in the experiment was expressed as the difference between the three values. GraphPad Prism 9 was used to plot the acquired data sets and calculate statistical significance and modified using Adobe Illustrator. P values < 0.05 were considered as statistically significant.
