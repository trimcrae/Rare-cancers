## Abstract
Cervical cancer remains a significant global health burden. This study aimed to investigate whether the RNA methyltransferase METTL3 promotes cervical cancer progression by regulating the stability of circular RNA circCDK6 via N⁶-methyladenosine (m⁶A) modification. Clinical samples from cervical cancer patients were analyzed. In vitro and in vivo functional assays, including PCR, RNA immunoprecipitation, m⁶A-methylated RNA immunoprecipitation, RNA stability assays, CCK-8, colony formation, flow cytometry, dual-luciferase reporter assay, and xenograft models were employed using cervical cancer cell lines. METTL3 and circCDK6 were significantly upregulated in cervical cancer tissues, and high expression correlated with advanced disease and poor patient prognosis. METTL3 directly bound to circCDK6 and installed m⁶A modification, which enhanced circCDK6 RNA stability. Knockdown of METTL3 or circCDK6 suppressed cell proliferation, induced G0/G1 phase arrest, and inhibited tumor growth in mouse models. Mechanistically, circCDK6 functioned as a competitive endogenous RNA to sponge miR-449a, thereby de-repressing the expression of its target, CDK6, a key cell cycle regulator. Our findings unveil a novel oncogenic METTL3/m⁶A/circCDK6/miR-449a/CDK6 axis that promotes cervical cancer tumorigenesis, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for intervention.

## Introduction
Cervical cancer remains a major threat to women’s health globally, with persistent infection of high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) being the primary etiological factor . Despite the widespread implementation of screening and prophylactic HPV vaccination, which have significantly reduced the incidence and mortality in developed countries, patients with advanced or recurrent disease still face a dismal prognosis due to limited therapeutic options , . This underscores the urgent need to elucidate the intricate molecular mechanisms driving cervical carcinogenesis and to identify novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.
In recent years, non-coding RNAs have emerged as pivotal regulators in cancer biology. Among them, circular RNAs (circRNAs), characterized by their covalently closed loop structure, exhibit high stability and conservation . Functioning primarily as competitive endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs), circRNAs can sequester microRNAs (miRNAs), thereby derepressing the expression of downstream target genes and influencing various cellular processes, including tumorigenesis , . Growing evidence has highlighted the aberrant expression and critical roles of numerous circRNAs in cervical cancer , . Our previous study identified circCDK6, derived from the cyclin-dependent kinase 6 (CDK6) gene locus, as being significantly upregulated in cervical cancer cells, where it promotes proliferation and metastasis by sponging miR-449a . CDK6 is a key regulator of the G1/S cell cycle transition and a well-established oncoprotein. However, the upstream regulatory mechanisms controlling circCDK6 itself in cervical cancer remain largely unknown.
N6-methyladenosine (m6A), the most prevalent internal mRNA modification in eukaryotes, represents another layer of post-transcriptional regulation . This dynamic and reversible process, catalyzed by “writer” complexes (e.g., METTL3/ METTL14/WTAP), erased by “erasers” (e.g., FTO, ALKBH5), and recognized by “readers” (e.g., YTHDF family), governs RNA metabolism, including stability, translation, and splicing , . Importantly, m6A modification also extends to circRNAs, regulating their biogenesis, stability, and functions , . For instance, m6A modification of circCCDC134 has been shown to facilitate cervical cancer metastasis . This crosstalk between m6A and circRNAs opens a new dimension for understanding cancer progression. METTL3, the core catalytic subunit of the m6A methyltransferase complex, is frequently overexpressed in various cancers and promotes tumor progression . We thus hypothesized that METTL3 might regulate the stability and oncogenic function of circCDK6 through m6A modification in cervical cancer.
Despite accumulating evidence implicating both METTL3-mediated m6A modification and circRNAs in cancer biology, whether and how METTL3 regulates circCDK6 through m6A modification in cervical cancer remains unknown. Given our previous finding that circCDK6 promotes cervical cancer progression by sponging miR-449a , and the established role of METTL3 in stabilizing various RNA species through m6A modification, we hypothesized that METTL3 might enhance circCDK6 stability via m6A deposition, thereby amplifying its oncogenic function. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate: the expression and clinical significance of METTL3 and circCDK6 in cervical cancer; whether METTL3 directly interacts with and installs m6A modification on circCDK6; the functional role of the METTL3/circCDK6 axis in cervical cancer proliferation and cell cycle progression; and the downstream mechanism involving the circCDK6/miR-449a/CDK6 regulatory network. Elucidation of this novel epitranscriptomic regulatory axis may provide new insights into cervical cancer pathogenesis and identify potential therapeutic targets.

## Materials and methods

## Clinical tissue samples and cell culture
A total of 30 paired cervical cancer tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues were collected from patients undergoing surgical resection at Fujian Provincial Tumor Hospital. The sample collection period was from January 2021 to December 2023. The inclusion criteria were: patients who underwent primary surgical resection for cervical cancer; histopathologically confirmed diagnosis of cervical squamous cell carcinoma or adenocarcinoma; availability of complete clinicopathological and follow-up data; no preoperative radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or other neoadjuvant therapies. The exclusion criteria were: patients with a history of other malignant tumors; patients who received any preoperative anti-cancer treatment; patients with incomplete clinical records or lost to follow-up; tissue samples with insufficient RNA quality or quantity for analysis.
The use of human tissues was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Provincial Tumor Hospital (Approval No. FJTHEC-2023-018), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The use of human tissues was approved by the Hospital’s Ethics Committee, and informed consent was obtained from all patients. All participants provided written informed consent prior to sample collection.
Tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C. Human cervical cancer cell lines (HCC94, MS751, HeLa, SiHa) and a normal human cervical epithelial cell line (HCerEpiC) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). All cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 or DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (both from Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination using a PCR-based detection kit (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). All plasmid constructs used in this study were verified by Sanger sequencing.

## Plasmid construction and cell transfection
Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting circCDK6 and METTL3, and the corresponding negative control (sh-NC), were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The full-length sequences of circCDK6 and METTL3 were cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector to construct overexpression plasmids. The mutant circCDK6 plasmid (with predicted m6A sites mutated) was generated by site-directed mutagenesis. For the rescue experiments, sh-METTL3 cells were subsequently transfected with the wild-type or mutant circCDK6 plasmids. Transient transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stable cell lines were selected using 2 µg/mL puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

## Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For circRNA analysis, RNA was treated with RNase R (Epicentre, Madison, WI, USA) to degrade linear RNAs. cDNA was synthesized using a PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). qRT-PCR was performed on a QuantStudio 5 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) using SYBR Green Premix (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). Divergent primers were designed to specifically amplify the back-splice junction of circCDK6. All primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table  . GAPDH was used as an internal control. The relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method .
 

## Cell proliferation and colony formation
Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 kit. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates, and OD450 was measured at 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h. For the colony formation assay, 800 cells were seeded per well in 6-well plates and cultured for 14 days. Colonies were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and counted.

## Flow cytometry for cell cycle and apoptosis
For cell cycle analysis, cells were harvested, fixed in 70% ethanol at 4 °C overnight, and then stained with a solution containing propidium iodide (PI) and RNase A using reagents from BD Biosciences (San Jose, CA, USA). For apoptosis detection, cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI using an Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA). Data were acquired on a BD FACS Celesta flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) and analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.8.1; BD Biosciences, Ashland, OR, USA; URL: https://www.flowjo.com).

## RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)
RIP assays were performed using the Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cell lysates were incubated with magnetic beads conjugated with anti-METTL3 antibody that was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA) or normal IgG (negative control) overnight at 4 °C. The co-precipitated RNAs were isolated and analyzed by qRT-PCR to detect the enrichment of circCDK6.

## m6A methylation quantification (Colorimetric) and MeRIP-qPCR
The global m6A levels in total RNA were measured using the EpiQuik m6A RNA Methylation Quantification Kit (Colorimetric) (EpiGentek, Farmingdale, NY, USA) as per the instructions. For m6A MeRIP-qPCR, the Magna MeRIP m6A Kit (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) was used. Fragmented RNA was incubated with an anti-m6A antibody for immunoprecipitation. The enriched RNA fragments were eluted and subjected to qRT-PCR analysis to determine the m6A modification level on specific circCDK6 transcripts.

## RNA pull-down
Biotin-labeled circCDK6 and antisense control probes were synthesized. Cell lysates were incubated with the probes and streptavidin magnetic beads. The retrieved complexes were eluted, and the bound proteins were analyzed by Western Blot. The wild-type and mutant CDK6 3’UTR sequences containing the miR-449a binding site were cloned into the pmirGLO vector. Cells were co-transfected with the reporter plasmids and miR-449a mimic or mimic control. Luciferase activities were measured 48 h post-transfection using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

## Western blot
Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against METTL3, CDK6, Cyclin D1, and β-actin (all from Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

## Dual-luciferase reporter assay
The wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) sequences of the CDK6 3’UTR containing the putative miR-449a binding site were cloned into the pmirGLO vector. Cells were co-transfected with the reporter plasmids and miR-449a mimic or mimic control. After 48 h, luciferase activities were measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

## In vivo xenograft tumor assay and ethical statement
Four-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) were randomly divided into two groups (n = 5 per group). All experimental procedures involving animals were conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publications No. 8023, revised 1978) and the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 . All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. K2023-266-01). Stable HCC94 cells expressing sh-NC or sh-circCDK6 were subcutaneously injected into the flanks of the mice. Tumor formation was monitored, and tumor volume measurements commenced when the average tumor size reached approximately 50–100 mm³ (typically around day 7–10 post-injection). Tumor volumes were measured every 4 days and calculated using the formula: Volume = (Length × Width²)/2. At the experimental endpoint (day 28), mice were first anesthetized with 3% isoflurane and then euthanized by CO₂ inhalation followed by confirmation of death via cervical dislocation, in strict accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020). Tumors were then excised, weighed, and processed for further analysis.

## Statistical analysis
All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 software (version 10.1.2, GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA; URL: https://www.graphpad.com). Flow cytometry data were analyzed with FlowJo 10.8.1 (BD Biosciences, Ashland, OR, USA; URL: https://www.flowjo.com). qRT-PCR data were acquired using QuantStudio Design & Analysis Software v2.6 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; URL: https://www.thermofisher.com). Western blot densitometry was performed using Image Lab 6.1 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA; URL: https://www.bio-rad.com). Immunohistochemistry images were analyzed with CaseViewer 2.4 (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary; URL: https://www.3dhistech.com.). Data organization was assisted by Microsoft Excel 16.78 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA; URL: https://www.microsoft.com). Differences between two groups were analyzed using the two-tailed Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## The METTL3/circCDK6 axis is upregulated and correlates with poor prognosis in cervical cancer
To establish the clinical relevance of our study, we began by analyzing the expression of METTL3 and circCDK6 in 30 paired clinical samples. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that METTL3 mRNA expression was significantly upregulated by approximately 3.5-fold in cervical cancer tissues compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues (P < 0.01, Fig.  A). Similarly, circCDK6 expression was increased by about 4.2-fold in tumor tissues (P < 0.01, Fig.  B). We further investigated the m6A modification level of circCDK6 using m6A-MeRIP-qPCR. The results demonstrated a pronounced enrichment of m6A on circCDK6 in cancer tissues, with a 2.8-fold higher m6A modification level compared to non-tumor tissues (P < 0.01, Fig.  C), suggesting a potential link between METTL3 activity and circCDK6 methylation in vivo.
 We next examined the clinical implications. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated that patients with high expression of METTL3 (n = 15) had a significantly shorter median overall survival (38 months) compared to the low expression group (n = 15, median survival not reached; P < 0.05, Fig.  D). Similarly, patients with high circCDK6 expression (n = 15) exhibited a median overall survival of 42 months, which was significantly shorter than the low expression group (n = 15, P < 0.05, Fig.  E). Furthermore, analysis of clinicopathological parameters showed that high METTL3 expression was significantly associated with advanced FIGO stage (Stage III-IV, 12/15 vs. Stage I-II, 3/15; P < 0.05) and lymph node metastasis (positive, 11/15 vs. negative, 4/15; P < 0.05, Table  ). These findings position the METTL3/circCDK6 axis as a key player in cervical cancer aggressiveness.
 

## Efficient and specific knockdown of highly expressed circCDK6 in cervical cancer cells
To establish a robust cellular model, we first characterized the basal expression pattern of circCDK6. qRT-PCR analysis with divergent primers showed that circCDK6 expression was significantly elevated in all tested cervical cancer cell lines (HeLa: 4.1-fold, SiHa: 3.8-fold, C-33 A: 4.5-fold, HCC94: 8.2-fold, MS751: 7.5-fold) compared to the normal HCerEpiC control (P < 0.001, Fig.  A). Notably, HCC94 and MS751 cells demonstrated the most substantial abundance of circCDK6 (P < 0.001 versus other cancer lines, Fig.  A) and were selected for subsequent experiments.
 We next designed three independent shRNAs targeting the unique back-splice junction of circCDK6. qRT-PCR analysis 48 h post-transfection confirmed that all three sh-circCDK6 constructs potently reduced circCDK6 levels in both HCC94 (sh-circCDK6-1: 65% reduction, sh-circCDK6-2: 72% reduction, sh-circCDK6-3: 85% reduction; P < 0.001, Fig.  B) and MS751 cells (sh-circCDK6-1: 62% reduction, sh-circCDK6-2: 70% reduction, sh-circCDK6-3: 82% reduction; P < 0.001, Fig.  C) compared to the sh-NC group. sh-circCDK6-3, with the highest knockdown efficiency, was chosen for subsequent studies.
A critical control experiment demonstrated that sh-circCDK6-3 did not significantly alter the mRNA expression level of linear CDK6 in either HCC94 (1.1-fold change, P > 0.05) or MS751 cells (0.95-fold change, P > 0.05, Fig.  D), confirming the specificity of our knockdown tool.

## Genetic silencing of circCDK6 impairs malignant phenotypes via arresting cell cycle progression
CCK-8 assays demonstrated that silencing circCDK6 with sh-circCDK6-3 led to a profound suppression of cellular proliferation. In HCC94 cells, OD450 values at 96 h were 1.25 ± 0.08 for sh-NC vs. 0.62 ± 0.05 for sh-circCDK6 (P < 0.05, Fig.  A). In MS751 cells, the values were 1.31 ± 0.09 for sh-NC vs. 0.68 ± 0.06 for sh-circCDK6 (P < 0.05, Fig.  B). Colony formation assays showed a drastic reduction in colony numbers: HCC94 sh-NC formed 185 ± 12 colonies vs. sh-circCDK6 42 ± 8 colonies (P < 0.01, Fig.  C, D); MS751 sh-NC formed 198 ± 15 colonies vs. sh-circCDK6 48 ± 7 colonies (P < 0.01, Fig.  C, D). Flow cytometric analysis revealed that circCDK6 knockdown induced a significant G0/G1 phase arrest. In HCC94 cells, the G0/G1 population increased from 44.9% ± 2.5% (sh-NC) to 66.7% ± 3.1% (sh-circCDK6), while the S phase population decreased from 39.5% ± 2.1% to 26.1% ± 1.8% (P < 0.01, Fig.  E, F). In MS751 cells, the G0/G1 population increased from 75.4% ± 2.8% to 81.0% ± 2.9%, and the S phase decreased from 14.7% ± 2.3% to 11.0% ± 1.9% (P < 0.01, Fig.  E, F).
 To molecularly substantiate this cell cycle phenotype, we employed Western Blot analysis to examine the protein levels of key regulators of the G1/S checkpoint. The results were striking and consistent. Western Blot analysis confirmed that silencing circCDK6 resulted in a pronounced downregulation of CDK6 protein (HCC94: 70% reduction; MS751: ~65% reduction) and Cyclin D1 protein (HCC94: 60% reduction; MS751: 55% reduction) compared to sh-NC controls (P < 0.01, Fig.  G). The coordinated reduction of these two pivotal drivers of G1 phase progression creates a molecular environment that is inhibitory to cell cycle advancement, thereby providing a direct protein-level corroboration for the G0/G1 arrest observed by flow cytometry.
In summary, this suite of functional experiments definitively establishes that circCDK6 is a potent promoter of cervical cancer cell growth. Its depletion triggers a multi-faceted anti-tumor response, characterized by suppressed proliferation, abolished clonogenic survival, and a definitive blockade of cell cycle progression at the G1 phase, mechanistically driven by the disruption of the CDK6/Cyclin D1 axis.

## METTL3 promotes oncogenic phenotypes by stabilizing circCDK6 in an m6A-dependent manner
To elucidate the upstream regulatory mechanisms controlling circCDK6 abundance, we focused on the predominant m6A methyltransferase METTL3, given our clinical findings of co-upregulation. We first investigated whether METTL3 directly interacts with circCDK6. RIP assays using an anti-METTL3 antibody showed a significant enrichment of circCDK6 in the METTL3 immunoprecipitation complex compared to IgG control (HCC94: 8.5-fold enrichment, P < 0.01; MS751: 7.8-fold enrichment, P < 0.001, Fig.  A), providing direct evidence of a physical interaction between METTL3 and circCDK6. We next asked whether this interaction leads to functional m6A modification of circCDK6. To this end, knockdown of METTL3 using two independent shRNAs (sh-METTL3-#1 and sh-METTL3-#2) in both HCC94 and MS751 cells was confirmed by qRT-PCR (sh-METTL3-#1: 75% reduction; sh-METTL3-#2: 80% reduction in mRNA, P < 0.001, Fig.  B) and Western Blot (P < 0.001, Fig.  C). Using the m6A-MeRIP-qPCR assay, we observed a substantial reduction in the m6A modification level specifically on circCDK6 upon METTL3 depletion (HCC94: 60% reduction; MS751: 65% reduction, P < 0.01, Fig.  D), establishing METTL3 as a key writer for circCDK6 methylation.
 To experimentally determine the effect of METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification on circCDK6 RNA stability, we performed an RNA decay assay using the transcriptional inhibitor actinomycin D. The experiment was designed with the following groups: sh-NC + Act D Group: Cells transduced with a non-targeting control shRNA and treated with actinomycin D (5 µg/mL). This group served as the control to establish the baseline decay rate of circCDK6. sh-METTL3 + Act D Group: Cells with stable knockdown of METTL3 (using the most effective shRNA, sh-METTL3-#) and treated with actinomycin D (5 µg/mL). This group was used to assess the specific impact of METTL3 loss on circCDK6 stability. Upon the addition of actinomycin D, total RNA was isolated from both groups at critical time points (e.g., 0, 2, 4, 8 h) post-treatment. The remaining levels of circCDK6 at each time point were quantified using RT-qPCR with divergent primers and normalized to the level at time 0. The resulting RNA decay curve clearly demonstrated a significantly accelerated degradation rate of circCDK6 in the sh-METTL3 + Act D group compared to the sh-NC + Act D group (P < 0.01; Fig.  E). From this kinetic data, the half-life of circCDK6 was calculated. It was markedly shortened from > 8 h in control cells to approximately 4 h in METTL3-knockdown cells. This result directly and unequivocally demonstrates that METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification enhances the RNA stability of circCDK6 (P < 0.01; Fig.  E). Based on the actinomycin D assay results which confirmed the role of METTL3 in regulating circCDK6 stability, we next examined its effect on the constitutive expression level of circCDK6. The steady-state total RNA level of circCDK6 was quantified by qRT-PCR in untreated sh-METTL3 cells and compared to that in sh-NC control cells. This analysis revealed a significant decrease of approximately 70% in circCDK6 abundance upon METTL3 knockdown (P < 0.001; Fig.  F). This result demonstrates that the m⁶A-mediated stabilization of circCDK6 by METTL3 is a major determinant of its high constitutive expression in cervical cancer cells.
Having established this regulatory relationship, we investigated whether METTL3 knockdown phenocopies the functional effects of direct circCDK6 loss. CCK-8 assays confirmed that METTL3 depletion led to a significant suppression of cell proliferation over a 96-hour period. Specifically, in HCC94 cells, the OD450 values were 1.32 ± 0.09 for sh-NC versus 0.68 ± 0.06 for sh-METTL3 at 96 h (P < 0.01); similar results were observed in MS751 cells (sh-NC: 1.35 ± 0.08 vs. sh-METTL3: 0.71 ± 0.05, P < 0.01; Fig.  G). Colony formation assays using both HCC94 and MS751 cell lines consistently demonstrated a severe impairment of clonogenic survival upon METTL3 knockdown. In HCC94 cells, the number of colonies formed decreased from 195 ± 16 in the sh-NC group to 50 ± 8 in the sh-METTL3 group, representing a 74.4% reduction (P < 0.01). Similarly, in MS751 cells, colony numbers were reduced from 189 ± 15 in controls to 46 ± 9 upon METTL3 knockdown, representing a 75.7% reduction in clonogenic capacity (P < 0.01; Fig.  H). Flow cytometric analysis for cell cycle distribution revealed that METTL3 knockdown induced a pronounced G0/G1 phase arrest in both cell lines. In HCC94 cells, the G0/G1 population significantly increased from 50.9% ± 2.6% in the sh-NC group to 66.4% ± 3.4% in the sh-METTL3 group (P < 0.01), while the S-phase population decreased correspondingly from 33.6% ± 2.3% to 20.1% ± 1.9% (P < 0.01) (P < 0.01; Fig.  I). A nearly identical trend was observed in MS751 cells, where the G0/G1 fraction increased from 49.5% ± 2.5% to 69.1% ± 3.1%, and the S-phase fraction decreased from 34.2% ± 2.2% to 18.7% ± 1.8% (P < 0.01; Fig.  J). Western Blot analysis confirmed that these phenotypic changes were mechanistically associated with a substantial downregulation of the CDK6/Cyclin D1 axis in both cell lines. Densitometric quantification revealed that in HCC94 cells, CDK6 protein levels were reduced by 66.5% ± 4.5% and Cyclin D1 levels decreased by 61.2% ± 4.0% compared to the sh-NC controls (P < 0.001 for both). In MS751 cells, METTL3 knockdown similarly led to a 64.1% ± 3.8% reduction in CDK6 and a 58.2% ± 3.5% reduction in Cyclin D1 (P < 0.001 for both; Fig.  K).
In conclusion, this series of experiments delineates a clear mechanistic pathway: METTL3 directly binds to circCDK6 and installs m⁶A modification, which in turn protects circCDK6 from degradation, leading to its accumulation and subsequent promotion of proliferation and cell cycle progression in cervical cancer cells.

## METTL3 exerts its oncogenic function via m6A-modified circCDK6 in a ceRNA mechanism targeting miR-449a
To establish a definitive causal relationship within the METTL3-circCDK6 axis, we performed a critical genetic rescue experiment in METTL3-knockdown HCC94 cells by reintroducing either wild-type circCDK6 (WT) or an m⁶A-site mutant version (MUT). Strikingly, re-expression of WT circCDK6 largely restored the proliferative capacity suppressed by METTL3 knockdown, with CCK-8 assays showing OD450 values at 96 h increasing from 0.69 ± 0.05 in the sh-METTL3 + Vector group to 1.08 ± 0.07 in the sh-METTL3 + WT circCDK6 group, representing approximately 76% restoration compared to sh-NC controls (P < 0.01, Fig.  A). In the sh-METTL3 + WT circCDK6 rescue group, colony formation ability was significantly restored, with the number of colonies reaching 152 ± 11. This result stands in clear contrast to both the deficient phenotype of the sh-METTL3 + Vector group (46 ± 6 colonies) and the failed rescue observed in the sh-METTL3 + MUT circCDK6 group (52 ± 7 colonies). The robust colony formation in the WT rescue group, which restored approximately 80% of the clonogenic capacity compared to the sh-NC + Vector control group (190 ± 12 colonies), specifically demonstrates that the functional recovery is dependent on the m⁶A modification sites of circCDK6 (P < 0.01; Fig.  B).
 This functional recovery was corroborated at the molecular level by Western blot analysis of CDK6 protein expression across the four experimental groups. In the sh-NC + Vector control group, CDK6 protein expression was set as 100% ± 5.2% to establish the baseline level. METTL3 knockdown in the sh-METTL3 + Vector group substantially reduced CDK6 expression to 30.5% ± 4.8% of control levels (P < 0.001). Crucially, re-expression of wild-type circCDK6 in the sh-METTL3 + WT circCDK6 group specifically restored CDK6 protein to 85% ± 6% of control levels (P < 0.01 versus sh-METTL3 + Vector group). In stark contrast, the m⁶A-site mutant circCDK6 in the sh-METTL3 + MUT circCDK6 group showed only minimal restorative capacity, reaching just 32% ± 5% of control CDK6 expression (P < 0.01 versus WT rescue group; P > 0.05 versus sh-METTL3 + Vector group) (Fig.  C). These quantitative data compellingly demonstrate that the oncogenic function of METTL3 explicitly depends on its ability to install m⁶A modification on circCDK6, as only the methylatable wild-type circCDK6, but not the methylation-deficient mutant, could effectively rescue CDK6 protein expression following METTL3 depletion.
To systematically elucidate the downstream mechanism through which circCDK6 regulates CDK6 expression, we employed a series of well-controlled experiments based on the ceRNA hypothesis. First, RNA pull-down assays were conducted with four distinct experimental groups to ensure specificity: the circCDK6 probe group, utilizing a biotin-labeled probe specifically targeting the back-splice junction of circCDK6; the mutant circCDK6 probe group, using a probe with mismatched sequences to rule out non-specific binding; the antisense control probe group, employing a probe complementary to the circCDK6 sequence as a negative control; and the beads-only group, containing only streptavidin magnetic beads to assess background binding. Subsequent qPCR analysis of the retrieved RNA complexes revealed highly specific results. The circCDK6 probe group showed a robust 6.8-fold enrichment of miR-449a (8.92 ± 0.67) compared to the baseline level. In contrast, the mutant circCDK6 probe group demonstrated only minimal enrichment (1.45 ± 0.31), confirming the sequence specificity of the interaction. The antisense control probe group showed negligible binding (1.31 ± 0.23), similar to the beads-only control group (1.08 ± 0.19). Statistical analysis confirmed the significant specificity of miR-449a enrichment in the circCDK6 probe group compared to all control groups (P < 0.001 for all comparisons, Fig.  D), thereby unequivocally identifying miR-449a as a direct binding partner of circCDK6. The experimental design included multiple replicates for each group (n = 4 independent experiments) with consistent results across all replicates. The low standard deviations in all control groups (ranging from ± 0.19 to ± 0.31) further validate the specificity and reproducibility of the observed interaction between circCDK6 and miR-449a.
Next, to verify whether miR-449a directly targets the CDK6 transcript, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays with a four-group experimental design: the WT 3’UTR + miR-NC group, consisting of the wild-type CDK6 3’UTR reporter vector co-transfected with a negative control miRNA mimic; the WT 3’UTR + miR-449a group, featuring the wild-type reporter co-transfected with a miR-449a mimic; the MUT 3’UTR + miR-NC group, containing a mutant CDK6 3’UTR reporter (with a disrupted miR-449a binding site) co-transfected with the negative control miRNA; and the MUT 3’UTR + miR-449a group, comprising the mutant reporter co-transfected with the miR-449a mimic. The results demonstrated that co-transfection of the miR-449a mimic significantly suppressed the luciferase activity of the wild-type reporter by 62.5% ± 5.3% (relative luciferase activity: WT 3’UTR + miR-NC group, 1.00 ± 0.08 vs. WT 3’UTR + miR-449a group, 0.38 ± 0.05; P < 0.01, Fig.  E). In contrast, the miR-449a mimic had no significant effect on the mutant reporter activity (MUT 3’UTR + miR-NC group, 0.97 ± 0.07 vs. MUT 3’UTR + miR-449a group, 0.94 ± 0.08; P > 0.05, Fig.  E). This set of experiments confirms that CDK6 is a direct and specific target of miR-449a, mediated through its 3’UTR binding site.).
Finally, to definitively integrate these molecular events into a coherent regulatory pathway and establish their functional hierarchy, we conducted a critical epistasis experiment with six carefully designed experimental groups in HCC94 cells: the sh-NC + anti-NC group (control cells transfected with non-targeting shRNA and negative control inhibitor); the sh-circCDK6 + anti-NC group (circCDK6-knockdown cells with negative control inhibitor); the sh-circCDK6 + miR-449a inhibitor group (circCDK6-knockdown cells co-transfected with a specific miR-449a inhibitor); the miR-NC + anti-NC group (cells transfected with negative control miRNA and negative control inhibitor); the miR-449a mimic + anti-NC group (miR-449a-overexpressing cells with negative control inhibitor); and the miR-449a mimic + miR-449a inhibitor group (miR-449a-overexpressing cells co-transfected with the miR-449a inhibitor). Western Blot analysis of these groups yielded clear and consistent results. As expected, both circCDK6 knockdown (sh-circCDK6 + anti-NC group) and miR-449a overexpression (miR-449a mimic + anti-NC group) led to a marked reduction in CDK6 protein levels, decreasing them to 32.5% ± 4.2% and 35.8% ± 3.9% of the sh-NC + anti-NC control group, respectively. Crucially, the suppression of CDK6 resulting from either manipulation was effectively rescued by the specific sequestration of miR-449a. In the sh-circCDK6 + miR-449a inhibitor group, CDK6 expression was restored to 85.3% ± 6.1% of control levels. Similarly, in the miR-449a mimic + miR-449a inhibitor group, CDK6 expression was recovered to 88.7% ± 5.4% (Fig.  F). This functional epistasis experiment, where inhibiting the putative intermediate (miR-449a) reverses the phenotypic effect of perturbing the upstream component (circCDK6), provides direct genetic evidence that circCDK6 functions upstream of miR-449a to regulate CDK6 expression. This finding completes the delineation of the linear METTL3/m⁶A/ circCDK6/miR-449a/CDK6 regulatory axis.
In summary, this section provides three layers of mechanistic evidence: (1) the functional coupling between METTL3 and m6A-modified circCDK6, (2) the identification of the specific circCDK6/miR-449a interaction with 6.8-fold enrichment, and (3) the validation of CDK6 as the functional downstream target with 62.5% repression via direct 3’UTR targeting, thereby fully delineating a novel oncogenic signaling pathway in cervical cancer.

## Silencing circCDK6 suppresses tumor growth and modulates the oncogenic axis in vivo
To validate the pathological relevance of our findings in an intact biological system, we established a xenograft mouse model to assess the role of circCDK6 in tumorigenesis in vivo. HCC94 cells stably expressing either sh-circCDK6 or sh-NC were subcutaneously injected into the flanks of immunodeficient nude mice (n = 5 per group), and tumor growth was monitored over a 28-day period. Macroscopic and quantitative analyses revealed a profound suppressive effect of circCDK6 knockdown on tumor development. Representative images of the excised tumors at the study endpoint clearly showed the dramatic size reduction in the sh-circCDK6 group compared to the sh-NC control group (Fig.  A). Longitudinal measurement of tumor volumes demonstrated a significantly impaired growth rate in the sh-circCDK6 group from day 16 onwards. The tumor volumes in the sh-circCDK6 group were 215.3 ± 18.7 mm³ at day 16 compared to 412.6 ± 25.4 mm³ in the control group (P < 0.01), culminating in a substantial difference at the endpoint (day 28: sh-circCDK6 298.5 ± 22.6 mm³ vs. sh-NC 652.8 ± 35.9 mm³, P < 0.01; Fig.  B), representing a 54.3% reduction in final tumor volume. Consistent with this, the final tumor weights in the sh-circCDK6 group (0.125 ± 0.015 g) were significantly lower than those in the control group (0.342 ± 0.028 g, P < 0.01; Fig.  C), corresponding to a 63.5% reduction in tumor mass.
 To characterize the cellular and molecular alterations underlying the observed growth inhibition, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) staining on the formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded xenograft tumor sections. We first assessed cellular proliferation in situ using an antibody against Ki-67. The IHC analysis revealed a striking reduction in the number of Ki-67-positive nuclei within the sh-circCDK6 tumors (18.3% ± 2.4% positive cells) compared to the sh-NC control tumors (52.7% ± 4.1% positive cells, P < 0.01; Fig.  D), representing a 65.3% decrease in proliferating cells. We next investigated whether the molecular axis identified in vitro remains operational in vivo. IHC staining for CDK6 protein showed a markedly lower staining intensity in the sh-circCDK6 tumors, with the IHC score decreasing from 8.2 ± 0.7 in controls to 3.1 ± 0.4 in the knockdown group (P < 0.01; Fig.  E), indicating a 62.2% reduction in CDK6 protein expression. Furthermore, to explore the upstream regulation in vivo, we also assessed the expression of METTL3 in the xenograft tissues. IHC analysis indicated a concordant decrease in METTL3 protein expression in the sh-circCDK6 group (IHC score: 2.8 ± 0.3 vs. 7.9 ± 0.6 in controls, P < 0.01; Fig.  F), representing a 64.6% reduction.
In conclusion, the in vivo xenograft data strongly corroborate our in vitro and clinical findings. They demonstrate that circCDK6 functions as a critical oncogenic driver in cervical cancer by promoting tumor growth and sustaining the expression of key proliferative proteins like CDK6, thereby validating the METTL3/circCDK6/miR-449a/CDK6 axis as a bona fide and therapeutically relevant pathway.

## Discussion
Despite advancements in understanding cervical cancer pathogenesis, the roles of epitranscriptomic regulation and circRNA metabolism remain largely unexplored. In this study, we unveil a novel oncogenic signaling pathway by demonstrating that METTL3-mediated m6A modification stabilizes circCDK6, which in turn functions as a molecular sponge for miR-449a, leading to the upregulation of CDK6 and the promotion of cervical cancer progression. Our findings not only delineate a coherent METTL3/circCDK6/miR-449a/CDK6 regulatory axis but also provide profound insights into the complex post-transcriptional network driving cervical tumorigenesis.
The clinical relevance of our proposed axis is strongly supported by the initial observation that both METTL3 and circCDK6 are significantly upregulated in cervical cancer tissues, and their high expression correlates with advanced disease stage and poor patient survival. This aligns with previous studies reporting the oncogenic roles of METTL3 in various cancers, where it typically promotes tumor progression by stabilizing target transcripts , . More importantly, we found a significantly elevated m6A level on circCDK6 in tumors, providing the first clinical clue of a potential regulatory link between METTL3 and circCDK6 in cervical cancer . These correlative findings in human samples laid a critical foundation for our subsequent mechanistic investigations.
One of the most significant findings of our study is the identification of METTL3 as the key upstream regulator governing circCDK6 stability. We provided multiple lines of evidence to support this claim: Firstly, RIP assays confirmed a direct physical interaction between METTL3 and circCDK6. Secondly, METTL3 knockdown specifically reduced the m6A modification on circCDK6. Thirdly, and most critically, the loss of METTL3 markedly shortened the half-life of circCDK6, establishing m6A modification as a pivotal determinant of circCDK6 RNA stability. This finding is consistent with the emerging theme that m6A modification can influence the fate of circRNAs , , but to our knowledge, this is the first report connecting METTL3 to the stability of circCDK6 in any cancer type. The functional significance of this regulation was unequivocally demonstrated by our sophisticated rescue experiment, which proved that the oncogenic phenotype driven by METTL3 is strictly dependent on its ability to install m6A on circCDK6. This causal relationship significantly strengthens the biological relevance of our proposed axis.
Our study also offers a comprehensive dissection of the downstream mechanism. We confirmed and extended our previous finding by rigorously demonstrating that circCDK6 acts as a ceRNA for miR-449a. The RNA Pull-Down and dual-luciferase reporter assays solidly confirmed the direct binding between circCDK6 and miR-449a, and the subsequent targeting of CDK6. This ceRNA network ultimately leads to the dysregulation of a critical cell cycle regulator, CDK6, and its partner Cyclin D1, providing a clear mechanistic explanation for the robust G0/G1 phase arrest and proliferation inhibition observed upon circCDK6 or METTL3 knockdown. The integration of this ceRNA mechanism with upstream m6A regulation presents a more complete picture of how oncogenic signals are amplified at the post-transcriptional level.
The translational potential of our findings is underscored by the compelling results from our in vivo xenograft model. Silencing circCDK6 potently suppressed tumor growth, which was associated with reduced Ki-67 proliferation index and downregulated CDK6 protein in situ. This successfully translates our in vitro mechanistic insights into a pathophysiologically relevant context, confirming circCDK6 as a promising therapeutic target. The concomitant decrease in METTL3 protein in the xenograft tumors upon circCDK6 knockdown hints at a potential positive feedback loop within this axis, a fascinating aspect that merits further investigation.
The emerging complexity of RNA modification-mediated regulation in cancer biology is further illustrated by recent studies. Yang et al. demonstrated that NXPH4, regulated by m5C modification, contributes to the malignant characteristics of colorectal cancer by inhibiting HIF1A degradation, highlighting the intricate cross-talk between different RNA modification types and their impact on tumor progression. Furthermore, Wang et al. revealed that the NSUN2-tRNAVal-CAC-axis-regulated codon-biased translation drives triple-negative breast cancer glycolysis and progression, expanding our understanding of how RNA modifications beyond m6A influence cancer metabolism. These convergent observations across different cancer types and modification types suggest that METTL3-mediated m6A modification of oncogenic circRNAs may represent one component of a broader epitranscriptomic regulatory network in tumorigenesis. While our study establishes a clear oncogenic role for the METTL3/circCDK6 axis, several aspects warrant critical consideration. First, although we demonstrate that METTL3 directly binds to circCDK6 and installs m6A modification, the exact m6A reader protein responsible for recognizing this modification and mediating circCDK6 stability remains unidentified. Previous studies have implicated YTHDF2 and YTHDF3 in m⁶A-dependent circRNA degradation or stabilization in different cellular contexts , . Whether these or other readers (e.g., IGF2BP proteins) are involved in circCDK6 regulation in cervical cancer requires further investigation. Second, while our rescue experiments convincingly demonstrate that METTL3’s oncogenic function depends on circCDK6 m⁶A modification, we cannot exclude the possibility that METTL3 may also exert oncogenic effects through other m⁶A-modified targets in cervical cancer. Indeed, METTL3 has been shown to regulate numerous mRNAs and non-coding RNAs in various cancer types , . Third, the precise stoichiometry of the circCDK6/miR-449a interaction, specifically, how many miR-449a molecules are bound per circCDK6 transcript, remains unclear. Quantitative understanding of this ceRNA interaction would require more sophisticated approaches such as single-molecule imaging or mathematical modeling.
We acknowledge several limitations in the present study. First, the clinical sample size (n = 30) is relatively modest. While sufficient to detect significant correlations, a larger cohort would strengthen the statistical power and allow for multivariate analyses to determine whether METTL3 and circCDK6 are independent prognostic factors. Second, our study focused exclusively on circCDK6 derived from the CDK6 gene locus; however, circular RNAs can be produced from multiple exons and back-splicing events, and whether other circCDK6 isoforms exist and are similarly regulated by METTL3 remains unknown. Third, although we identified miR-449a as the primary miRNA sponge partner of circCDK6, RNA pull-down assays suggested potential weak interactions with other miRNAs (data not shown), which may contribute to the observed phenotypes. Fourth, our in vivo experiments utilized subcutaneous xenograft models, which do not fully recapitulate the tumor microenvironment, immune cell interactions, or metastatic progression seen in orthotopic or genetically engineered mouse models. Fifth, the mechanistic details of how m⁶A modification enhances circCDK6 stability, specifically, whether it recruits stabilizing proteins, evades degradation pathways, or alters secondary structure, remain to be elucidated. Sixth, while our study demonstrates that METTL3 binds to circCDK6 and installs m6A modification, the exact m6A sites on circCDK6 responsible for its stabilization remain unidentified. Although our site-directed mutagenesis experiment targeting predicted m6A consensus motifs (RRACH) confirmed the functional importance of m6A modification, we did not perform high-resolution mapping to pinpoint the precise adenosine residues that are methylated. This limitation precludes a complete understanding of the molecular determinants governing METTL3-mediated circCDK6 regulation.
Based on our findings and acknowledged limitations, several future directions are warranted. First, screening for the specific m⁶A reader protein that recognizes methylated circCDK6 using RIP-mass spectrometry or CRISPR-based screens could identify novel therapeutic targets. Second, development of in vivo delivery systems (e.g., lipid nanoparticles or adeno-associated viruses) for circCDK6-targeting siRNAs or antisense oligonucleotides could pave the way for translational applications. Third, exploring whether this METTL3/circCDK6/miR-449a/CDK6 axis operates in other HPV-related cancers (e.g., head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, anal cancer) would broaden the clinical relevance of our findings. Fourth, investigating potential feedback mechanisms, for instance, whether CDK6 or its downstream effectors regulate METTL3 expression, could reveal additional layers of regulatory complexity. Fifth, future studies employing m6A-MeRIP-seq coupled with site-specific mutagenesis and RNA electrophoretic mobility shift assays (REMSA) could precisely map the m6A modification sites on circCDK6. Identification of these critical residues would enable the development of targeted therapies, such as antisense oligonucleotides designed to block METTL3 binding at specific sites or to recruit demethylases selectively to circCDK6. Additionally, CRISPR-Cas13-based approaches targeting specific m6A sites could be explored to validate their functional importance in vivo. Sixth, the therapeutic implications of targeting RNA modifications are increasingly recognized. A recent study demonstrated that the natural compound baicalin attenuates the immune escape of oral squamous cell carcinoma by reducing lactate accumulation in the tumor microenvironment , suggesting that pharmacological modulation of metabolic and epitranscriptomic pathways may be achievable. This raises the intriguing possibility that small molecules or natural compounds could be developed to selectively inhibit METTL3-mediated modification of circCDK6 in cervical cancer. Future drug screening efforts could leverage our findings to identify compounds that disrupt the METTL3-circCDK6 interaction or selectively reduce circCDK6 m6A modification. Additionally, the emerging roles of m5C and tRNA modifications in cancer biology highlighted by recent studies suggest that a multi-faceted approach targeting multiple RNA modification pathways may be necessary for optimal therapeutic efficacy , . Finally, validation of our findings in larger, multi-center patient cohorts with long-term follow-up is essential to establish the prognostic and predictive value of METTL3 and circCDK6 as biomarkers.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, our integrated study, spanning clinical correlation, detailed molecular biochemistry, and in vivo validation, establishes a novel METTL3/m6A/ circCDK6/miR-449a/CDK6 pathway that is a critical driver of cervical cancer progression. We highlight the functional significance of m6A-dependent regulation of oncogenic circRNAs, a layer of regulation that has been underappreciated in cervical cancer. Despite the limitations noted above, our findings provide a strong foundation for future investigations into epitranscriptomic regulation of circRNAs and their potential as therapeutic targets. Targeting this axis, particularly the METTL3-circCDK6 interface, may represent a novel and promising therapeutic strategy. Future studies exploring the efficacy of small molecule inhibitors or RNA-based therapeutics against this axis, especially in combination with standard therapies, are warranted and could pave the way for improved treatment outcomes for cervical cancer patients.
