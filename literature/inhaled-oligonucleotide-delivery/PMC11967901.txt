## Abstract
Itaconate was initially identified as an antimicrobial compound produced by myeloid cells. Beyond its antimicrobial role, itaconate may also serve as a crucial metabolic and immune modulator. We therefore examined the roles of aconitate decarboxylase 1 (Acod1) and itaconate in house dust mite (HDM)-sensitized and –challenged mice, a model of T helper 2 (Th2)-driven allergic airways disease. HDM treatment induced lung Acod1 mRNA expression and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) itaconate levels in wild-type C57BL/6 mice. Acod1 knockout mice (Acod1-KO) with negligible BAL itaconate showed heightened HDM-induced type 2 cytokine expression, increased serum IgE, and enhanced recruitment of Th2 cells in the lung, indicating a shift towards a more pronounced Th2 immune response. Acod1-KO mice also showed increased eosinophilic airway inflammation and hyperresponsiveness. Experiments in chimeric mice demonstrated that bone marrow from Acod1-KO mice is sufficient to increase type 2 cytokine expression in wild-type mice, and that restitution of bone marrow from wild type mice attenuates mRNA expression of Th2 cytokines in Acod1-KO mice. Specific deletion of Acod1 in lysozyme-secreting macrophages (LysM-cre+Acod1flox/flox) recapitulated the exaggerated phenotype observed in whole-body Acod1-KO mice. Adoptive transfer of Acod1-KO bone marrow-derived macrophages also increased lung mRNA expression of Th2 cytokines. In addition, treatment of Th2-polarized CD4 cells with itaconate impeded Th2 cell differentiation, as shown by reduced expression of Gata3 and decreased release of IL-5 and IL-13. Finally, public datasets of human samples show lower Acod1 expression in subjects with allergic asthma, consistent with a protective role of itaconate in asthma pathogenesis. Together, these data suggest that itaconate plays a protective, immunomodulatory role in limiting airway type 2 inflammation after allergen challenge by attenuating T cell responses.

## Introduction
Metabolites are low molecular weight chemical compounds that play critical roles in shaping immune responses. Of particular interest is itaconate, which is produced by macrophages upon activation by bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS). LPS upregulates aconitate decarboxylase (Acod1, also called Irg1), the protein product of which, cis-aconitate decarboxylase 1 (Acod1), catalyzes the production of itaconate from the citric acid cycle intermediate cis-aconitate. Itaconate was initially identified as an antimicrobial compound produced by macrophages against intracellular pathogens. , Itaconate has been shown to have immunomodulatory effects on mammalian cells. LPS-activated bone marrow-derived macrophages from Acod1−/− (Acod1-KO) mice produce more NO, IL-6, IL-12 and mature IL-1β, IL-18 and HIF-1α, demonstrating that itaconate attenuates Toll-like receptor-mediated inflammatory cytokine production. LPS-activated macrophages from Acod1-KO mice show reduced succinate accumulation, , evidence that itaconate acts as a competitive inhibitor of succinate dehydrogenase. While competitive inhibition of succinate dehydrogenase (complex II of the mitochondrial electron transport system) increases the pro-inflammatory signals of succinate, it also decreases mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate, attenuating oxidative phosphorylation and generation of reactive oxygen. Itaconate-depleted Acod1-KO macrophages exhibit increased NLRP3 inflammasome activation. In addition, itaconate activates the Nrf2 pathway by post-translational modification of the Nrf2 inhibitor Keap1, enhancing the expression of antioxidant and anti-inflammatory genes Upregulation of Acod1 and itaconate have been shown to play a role in immune tolerance. Most recently, Acod1 elevation been shown to induce both anti- and pro-inflammatory responses, depending on context (reviewed in ). For example, macrophages from Acod1-KO mice show impaired expression of interferon-stimulated genes which is restored by itaconate. Downregulation of Acod1 by siRNA reduces reactive oxygen species, inflammatory cell infiltration and lung injury in respiratory syncytial virus-infected mice. Together these data suggest that itaconate should be considered an immunoregulatory rather than strictly immunosuppressive metabolite, the effects of which may vary under specific conditions.
In addition to its intracellular effects in macrophages, emerging evidence suggests that itaconate can be secreted, impacting neighboring cells within tissue environments. For example, treatment of naive CD4+ T cells with itaconate inhibits Th17 and promotes Treg cell differentiation. Adoptive transfer of T cells treated with itaconate mitigated experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, underscoring the pivotal role of itaconate as a metabolic regulator in maintaining Th17/Treg cell equilibrium. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells secrete itaconate that is taken up by CD8 + T cells and suppresses their proliferation, cytokine production and cytolytic activity. In a murine model of lung fibrosis, Acod1-deficient mice show prolonged pulmonary inflammation, and administration of inhaled itaconate alleviates lung fibrosis. Itaconate also reduces the proliferation and wound healing capabilities of lung fibroblasts in vitro. Secreted itaconate stimulates Oxgr1 to promote mucociliary clearance during Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection. Taken together, these data demonstrate the immunomodulatory role of itaconate and its capacity to affect fibroblast metabolic phenotype. Given the ability of itaconate to affect T cell differentiation and cytokine production, we examined the role of itaconate in a mouse model of T helper 2 (Th2)-driven allergic airways disease, employing whole body and tissue specific Acod1 knockout mice as well as adoptive transfer of wild-type and Acod1-KO macrophages.

## Results

## Itaconate deficiency exacerbates HDM-induced allergic airways disease.
To investigate the role of itaconate in allergic airways disease, we administered HDM intranasally, sensitizing on days 1 and 4 and challenging on days 8, 10 and 12 ( ). We analyzed HDM-induced airway responses in wild-type and Acod1-KO mice which are deficient in itaconate. Two days after the last challenge, there was a significant increase in the lung mRNA expression of Acod1 ( ) and concentration of itaconate in the BAL ( , Supplemental Fig. 1) of wild-type mice. BAL itaconate increased from 3.1-10.7 nM at baseline to 35.2–79.2 nM after HDM sensitization and challenge. HDM challenge failed to increase Acod1 mRNA expression or BAL itaconate concentration in Acod1-KO mice. HDM-challenged Acod1-KO mice exhibited significant elevations of mRNAs encoding the Th2 cytokines IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13 ( ). mRNAs encoding Muc5ac and CCL11, an eosinophil chemoattractant, were also increased. In contrast, Acod1-KOs showed reduced levels of BAL neutrophils and lung mRNAs encoding IL-1β and TNF-α, suggesting a general shift towards type 2 and away from a type 1 immune response. To further compare the immune profile changes induced by HDM between wild-type and Acod1-KO mice, we quantified Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells by flow cytometry. Consistent with the lung mRNA expression data, Acod1-KO mice exhibited an increase in Th2 cells, but not Th1 or Th17 cells, compared to wild-type mice after HDM treatment ( , ).
 Compared to wild-type mice, Acod1-KO mice treated with HDM showed increased levels of serum IgE and IgG1 ( ) and elevated BAL eosinophils ( ). PAS staining of the lungs showed mucous metaplasia in HDM-exposed wild type mice, with increased peribronchial inflammation and PAS staining in Acod1-KOs ( ). There were no obvious phenotypic changes in the immune cells of Acod1-KO mice (not shown). The M2 macrophage markers Arg1, Retnla, CD206 and Chil3 were increased in lungs of Acod1-KO mice ( ), consistent with increased type 2 inflammation. Finally, Acod1-KO showed increased cholinergic airways responsiveness compared to wild-type mice ( ). Taken together, these data suggest a protective role of Acod1 and itaconate in the development of allergic airways disease.
 

## Bone marrow-derived itaconate attenuates allergic airway inflammation.
Acod1 has been reported to be expressed in macrophages, , bone marrow-derived dendritic cells, neutrophils , and neurons. To determine the requirement and sufficiency of bone marrow-derived itaconate for protection against allergic inflammation, we employed a chimeric mouse model. Wild-type and Acod1-KO mice were irradiated and reconstituted with bone marrow from either wild-type and Acod1-KO mice. Mice were then sensitized and challenged with HDM. Bone marrow from Acod1-KO mice was sufficient to increase Il5 and Il13 mRNA levels in wild-type mice ( ). Conversely. bone marrow from the wild-type mice decreased Il5 and Il13 mRNA expression in Acod1-KO mice ( ). This observation underscores the role of itaconate-derived from bone marrow cells in protecting against type 2 inflammation.
 Bone marrow-derived macrophages express Acod1 in response to LPS treatment. , To further determine the source of bone marrow-derived itaconate, we differentiated bone marrow cells to macrophages under the influence of M–CSF and stimulated with HDM. HDM increased Acod1 mRNA expression ( ) and protein abundance in bone marrow-derived macrophages ( ), consistent with the notion that bone marrow-derived macrophages are a significant source of itaconate. Alveolar macrophages express Acod1 in response to bacterial infection. , HDM also increased Acod1 mRNA expression in alveolar macrophages ( ).
 Next, we utilized transgenic mice with the Acod1 knockout targeted to specific cell types. LysM-cre+Acod1flox/flox mice were used to target macrophage itaconate production. – LysM is also expressed in neutrophil populations. , 
Cd11c-cre+Acod1flox/flox mice were used to deplete itaconate from dendritic cells. CD11c is also highly expressed in alveolar macrophages. In comparison, macrophages recruited macrophages from the bone marrow express high levels of CD11b and low levels of CD11c. 
Cd4-cre+Acod1flox/flox mice were used to block T cell itaconate. Compared to HDM-treated cre-negative Acod1flox/flox mice, HDM-treated LysM-cre+Acod1flox/flox mice exhibited the most closely-aligned phenotype to the whole body Acod1-KOs, with heightened levels of mRNAs encoding IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, CCL-11, and Muc5ac ( ). Cd11c-cre+Acod1flox/flox mice displayed increased Il4 and Il5 but no change in Il13, Ccl11 or Muc5ac. Finally, no discernible differences were noted between cre−Acod1flox/flox and Cd4-cre+Acod1flox/flox mice. These observations are consistent with a protective role of itaconate from bone marrow-derived macrophages in the mitigation of allergic airways disease.

## Adoptive transfer of bone marrow-derived macrophages from wild-type and Acod1-KO mice.
To examine the sufficiency of macrophage itaconate to regulate allergen-induced type 2 inflammation during the allergen challenge phase, we differentiated bone marrow cells from wild-type and whole-body Acod1-KO mice to macrophages using M–CSF in vitro. We exclusively used Acod1-KO mice as the recipients for adoptive transfer experiments to avoid the interference of endogenous itaconate from wild-type mice. Wild-type or Acod1-KO bone marrow-derived macrophages were administered intranasally to whole-body Acod1-KO mice three days after two intranasal sensitizations with 100 μg HDM. Two days later, mice were challenged with a third dose of HDM or PBS and lungs were collected 6 h later for measurement of lung type 2 cytokine mRNA. HDM-treated Acod1-KO mice receiving wild-type bone marrow-derived macrophages showed significantly lower lung mRNA expression of Il4, Il5 and Il13 compared to mice receiving itaconate-deficient bone marrow-derived macrophages ( ).
 

## Exogenous itaconate inhibits Th2 cell differentiation.
T helper 2 (Th2) cells are key contributors to the development of allergic asthma. While our studies showed that CD4 + cells are not the source of itaconate, we hypothesized that itaconate produced by myeloid cells impacts neighboring T cells. , We isolated CD4 + spleen cells and cultured them under Th2-polarizing conditions, with or without itaconate at concentrations of 0.1, 1, or 5 mM ( ). Both 1 and 5 mM exogenous itaconate inhibited Th2 cell differentiation, as evidenced by mRNA expression and protein levels of Gata3 ( , ). The ELISA results indicate that Th2 cells cultured with itaconate also exhibited decreased secretion of IL-5 and IL-13 ( ). Itaconate had no effect on cell viability ( ). These data are consistent with the notion that itaconate limits type 2 inflammation during HDM treatment by attenuating Th2 cell differentiation.
 

## Potential role of macrophage-derived itaconate in human asthma.
To investigate the role of itaconate in human asthma, we performed data mining on two publicly available datasets. The first dataset (GEO accession number GSE118761) used RNAseq analysis to compare RNA expression in the nasal brushings of atopic non-asthmatic and atopic asthmatic subjects. Patients with atopic wheezing exhibited a −3.85 log2 fold change in Acod1 expression (padj value 0.0065) compared to atopic non-wheezing subjects (Supplemental Fig. 2A), consistent with the notion that itaconate protects against wheezing in atopic individuals. The second dataset employed single-cell RNAseq to compare RNA profiles from the lower airway mucosa of allergic asthmatics and allergic non-asthmatics. 
Acod1 expression was found exclusively in mononuclear phagocytes, with a −2.11 log2 fold change (though not significant) in allergic asthma compared to allergic non-asthma (Supplemental Fig. 2B). Specifically, Acod1 expression was observed in Cxcl10- and Areg-expressing monocyte-derived cells and Fabp4- expressing macrophages (Supplemental Fig. 2C). In contrast, no Acod1 expression was found in DCs within their annotated cell clusters. Overall, data obtained from human clinical samples is consistent with our hypothesis that monocyte/macrophage-derived itaconate deficiency contributes to asthma development, and that macrophage-derived exacerbates asthma.

## Discussion
In this study, we investigated the role of itaconate, a secreted metabolite with anti-inflammatory properties, in the context of Th2-mediated allergic airways disease. We found that itaconate deficiency exacerbates HDM-induced airway responses. Acod1-KO mice exhibited elevated levels of type 2 cytokine expression and serum IgE, and enhanced recruitment of Th2 cells in the lung, indicating a shift towards an enhanced Th2 immune response. Further experiments examining the cellular source of itaconate revealed its protective role to originate from bone marrow cells, particularly macrophages. Experiments in chimeric mice demonstrated that restitution of bone marrow from wild type mice attenuates mRNA expression of Th2 cytokines in Acod1-KO mice, and that bone marrow from Acod-KO mice increases Th2 cytokines in wild-type mice. Specific deletion of Acod1 in lysozyme-secreting macrophages (LysM-cre+Acod1flox/flox) recapitulated the exaggerated phenotype observed in whole-body Acod1-KO mice. HDM-treated Acod1-KO mice undergoing adoptive transfer of wild-type bone marrow-derived macrophages showed significantly lower lung type 2 cytokine expression compared to mice receiving itaconate-deficient bone marrow-derived macrophages. Together these data provide evidence that itaconate from bone marrow-derived macrophages plays a pivotal role in mitigating allergic airways disease.
In allergic asthma, after allergen exposure, there is rapid recruitment of monocytes and monocyte-derived macrophages into the airways, promoting allergic inflammation. , Airway allergen challenge in human volunteers with mild allergic asthma induces a rapid shift in an infiltrating population of cells in BAL fluid by 48 h, consisting mainly of eosinophils and macrophages. In HDM-sensitized and –challenged mice, F4/80 + Ly6C+CD11b-diphtheria toxin receptor-sensitive macrophages are required for mucus metaplasia, eosinophil recruitment and lung mRNA expression of type 2 cytokines and IL-13-dependent genes. Macrophage depletion impaired effector CD4 + T helper type 2 (Th2) cell homing and activation within the inflamed lung, consistent with the notion that after allergen exposure, macrophages promote Th2 effector functions. We found that supplementation of naïve CD4 + T cells with 1–5 mM itaconate under Th2-polarizing conditions reduced expression of crucial Th2 developmental factor Gata3 and reduced secretion of the Th2 cytokines IL-5 and IL-13. 2.5–5 mM itaconate has previously been shown to attenuate Il5 and Il13 expression from mature Th2 cells. These data suggest that secreted itaconate directly modulates pathways crucial for both Th2 cell differentiation and cytokine production.
We also found that macrophage M2 markers were increased in the lungs of Acod1-KO mice. 4-octyl itaconate (4-OI), a cell-permeable itaconate derivative, has previously been shown to block M2 polarization of bone marrow-derived macrophages. Blood monocyte-derived macrophages from HDM-treated mice and HDM-allergic patients show TNF-dependent transcriptional reprogramming and enhanced production of CCL17, a T2 inflammatory mediator. Thus, downregulation of macrophage M2 polarization by itaconate could also indirectly attenuate Th2-mediated allergic airways disease.
Our findings align with recent studies examining the role of itaconate in allergic airways disease.
In one study, adoptive transfer of HDM-pulsed DC from Acod1-deficient mice into naïve wild type mice induced the asthma-like phenotype, suggesting that itaconate attenuates dendritic cell immune priming. In a second study, Acod1-KO mice showed increased macrophage NLRP3 activation and oxidative stress in allergic airway inflammation. Two additional studies employed 4-OI to block airway responses. , However, in each case, mice were sensitized with intraperitoneal allergen, which does not resemble human sensitization and regulates numerous dendritic cell signaling and metabolic pathways. , Finally, these studies did not confirm that allergic sensitization and challenge increases extracellular itaconate in vivo.
It is important to review the limitations of our study. First, while we determined that 1 mM itaconate attenuates Th2 cell development in vitro, this concentration of itaconate did not approach that which we found in the BAL of HDM-treated mice (35.2–79.2 nM). Smaller concentrations of itaconate had no effect on Th2 differentiation, likely due to its poor membrane permeability. We chose not to use itaconate derivatives such as 4-OI or dimethyl itaconate, as they are not converted into intracellular itaconate and elicit distinct effects compared to endogenous itaconate. In addition, quantifying physiological “free” itaconate poses technical challenges due to the dilution by bronchoalveolar lavage. Not only that, concentrations of itaconate in the BAL, which may reflect those in the mucosal surface epithelial lining fluid, may be dissimilar to the those which occur in lung-associated lymphoid tissue or submucosal regions where T cell differentiation occurs.
Second, we used LysM-cre+Acod1flox/flox and Cd11c-cre+Acod1flox/flox mice to narrow down the source of itaconate production. However, LysM or Cd11c expression is not confined to a single cell type. LysM is expressed by macrophages and neutrophils, and Cd11c by dendritic cells and alveolar macrophages. In comparison, macrophages recruited macrophages from the bone marrow express high levels of CD11b and low levels of CD11c. The non-specificity of these markers makes it difficult to interpret such KO studies independently. For example, in our study HDM-treated LysM-cre+Acod1flox/flox mice exhibited the most closely-aligned phenotype to the whole body Acod1-KOs, consistent with the notion that macrophages or neutrophils were the source of itaconate.
Cd11c-cre+Acod1flox/flox mice showed a partial reduction in type 2 inflammation, in keeping with a partial role for DCs or alveolar macrophages. However, in combination with experiments showing increased HDM-induced type 2 inflammation upon adoptive transfer of bone marrow-derived macrophages from Acod1-KO mice, we believe these cells to be the primary source of itaconate upon HDM treatment.
In conclusion, we confirmed using intranasal sensitization and challenge that itaconate deficiency exacerbates allergic airways disease in mice. While Acod1 and itaconate production were induced by HDM treatment, Acod1-KO mice exhibited enhanced Th2 cytokine expression, IgE production, eosinophilic infiltration and mucous metaplasia, indicating that itaconate plays an immunomodulatory role. Similar type 2 cytokine expression was stimulated by adoptive transfer of bone marrow-derived macrophages. We also showed that itaconate attenuates Th2 cell differentiation and function. Further studies examining the interplay between metabolic pathways and immune regulation in asthma may lead to novel therapeutic strategies for this disease.

## Materials and methods

## Animals.
Global Acod1−/− (Acod1-KO) and myeloid-specific Acod1-KO (Acod1fl/fl-LysMcre) were provided by Richard Mortensen (University of Michigan, Ann Arbor) and generated as described previously. , C57BL/6J, B6.Cg-Tg(Cd11c-cre)1-1Reiz/J, and B6.Cg-Tg(Cd4-cre) 1Cwi/BfluJ mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). Dendritic cell specific Acod1-KO (Acod1fl/fl-Cd11cCre) and CD4 + T cell specific Acod1-KO (Acodfl/fl-Cd4Cre) were generated by crossing floxed Acod1 mice with the indicated Cre lines. All mice were generated on C57BL/6 background, including global and cell specific knockouts. Mice were bred in house in pathogen-free facility within the Unit for Laboratory Animal Medicine at the University of Michigan. All animal usage was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and followed guidelines set forth in the Principles of Laboratory Animal Care from the National Society for Medical Research. Mice were euthanized via CO2 chamber and death was verified with cervical dislocation.

## House dust mite (HDM) treatment.
All mice were intranasally treated with low-endotoxin HDM (100 ug, Stallergenes Greer, 390844) or PBS on days 1 and 4 and challenged with HDM (50 ug) or PBS on days 8, 10, 12 before harvest on day 14.

## Real-time quantitative PCR.
Cell or lung RNA was extracted with Trizol (Invitrogen, 15596018) and isolated using an RNAeasy kit (Qiagen, 74004). cDNA was synthesized from 2 mg of RNA using high-capacity cDNA synthases kit (Applied Biosystems, 4368814) and subjected to quantitative real-time PCR using specific primers ( ) for mRNA. The level of gene expression for each sample was normalized to Gapdh. Acod1-KO had no effect on Gapdh mRNA levels.
 

## Flow cytometric analysis.
Lungs were perfused with PBS and minced and digested with Liberase (100 μg/mL; Roche), collagenase XI (250 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich), hyaluronidase 1a (1 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich), and DNase I (200 μg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were filtered and washed with RBC lysis buffer (BD Biosciences), and stimulated with cell stimulation cocktail (eBioscience, 00–4970-93) containing PMA, ionomycin and protein export blockers for 3 h at 37C. After stimulation, dead cells were stained with Pac-Orange Succinimidyl Ester (Invitrogen, P30253). Nonspecific binding was blocked with 1 % fetal bovine serum with 1 % LPS-free bovine serum albumin in DMEM, and 5 μg rat anti–mouse CD16/32 (BioLegend,101302) was added. To identify helper T cells, cells were stained with anti-CD45-PE/Cyanine7 (BioLegend, 103114), anti-CD3ε-BV605 (BioLegend, 100351), anti-CD4-FITC (BioLegend, 100406) and negatively gated for anti-CD8a-APC/Cyanine7 (BioLegend, 100714). Th1, Th2, and Th17 were further identified from helper T cells with anti-IFNγ-Pacific Blue (BioLegend, 505818), anti-IL13-PE (BioLegend, 159403), and anti-IL17A-PerCP/ Cyanine5.5 (BioLegend, 506920) respectively.

## Lung histology and bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL).
Lungs were fixed with 10 % formaldehyde overnight and paraffin embedded. Blocks were sectioned at 500 mm intervals at a thickness of 5 mm, and each section was deparaffinized, hydrated and stained. To visualize mucus, sections were stained with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) stain kit (Abcam, ab150680). PAS staining in the airway epithelium were quantified by QuPath v0.5.0 software.
For cell counting, BAL was collected with 1 mL PBS and red blood cells were lysed with BD Pharm Lyse™ Lysing Buffer (BD Biosciences, 555899) for 1 min at 4 °C. BAL cells were re-suspended in 200 uL PBS and spun down on slides. Cells were counts after hematoxylin and eosin stain.

## Measurement of BAL itaconate by mass spectroscopy.
200 uL BAL supernatant was mixed with 800 uL ice-old methanol. Samples were then centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to pellet insoluble material, and the resulting metabolite supernatant was transferred to a fresh 1.5 mL tube. The metabolites were then dried on a SpeedVac until the methanol/water had evaporated, and the resulting pellet was re-suspended in a 50:50 mixture of methanol and water.
To quantify itaconate concentrations, a standard ladder was created and run on the LCMS platform alongside samples. Standard ladder was created with itaconate standard (Sigma Aldrich, I29204) dissolved in 1:1 v/v MeOH/H2O. Concentrations ranged from 10uM to 10 pM. Ion counts were quantified by area under the curve (AUC) of the metabolite chromatogram, and then assigned to their respective concentrations to create a concentration curve. Samples were compared against this to derive itaconate levels.
Samples were run on an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC –6470 Triple Quadrupole (QqQ) tandem mass spectrometer (MS/MS) system with the following parameters: Agilent Technologies Triple Quad 6470 LC-MS/MS system consists of the 1290 Infinity II LC Flexible Pump (Quaternary Pump), the 1290 Infinity II Multisampler, the 1290 Infinity II Multicolumn Thermostat with 6 port valves, and the 6470 triple quad mass spectrometer. Agilent Masshunter Workstation Software LC/MS Data Acquisition for 6400 Series Triple Quadrupole MS with Version B.08.02 is used for compound optimization, calibration, and data acquisition.

## HDM-specific IgE and IgG1.
96-well plates were coated with 0.01 % HDM and blocked with 1 % BSA for 24 h. Serum samples were added to the plates for 1 h at room temperature. The plate was then washed with PBS with 0.05 % Tween-20 for 6 times followed by incubation with biotinylated anti-mouse IgE (BD Biosciences, 553414) or IgG1 (BD Biosciences, 553441) for 1 h. The plate was then washed with PBS with 0.05 % Tween-20 for 6 times before incubation with streptavidin-HRP (Thermo Scientific™, N100) for 30 min. The addition of TMB substrate (Thermo Scientific™, N301) determined bound HDM-specific antibodies by the spectrometer (ELX800, Bio-Tek) at 650 nm.

## Measurement of airway responsiveness.
Mice were anesthetized, intubated, and ventilated with a Buxco FinePointe System (Data Sciences International). Methacholine was administered via retroorbital injection. 

## Chimeric mouse generation.
Wild type (WT) and Acod1-KO mice were treated with 13 Gy total body irradiation (TBI) using a 137Cs irradiator, followed by tail vein injection of 5 x 106 whole bone marrow cells from either WT or Acod1-KO mice. Mice were given the following bone marrow transplantation regimens: 1) TBI WT mice + bone marrow from WT mice; 2) TBI Acod1-KO mice + bone marrow from WT mice; 3) TBI WT mice + bone marrows from Acod1-KO mice; and 4) TBI Acod1-KO mice + bone marrow from Acod1-KO mice.

## Isolation of bone marrow-derived and alveolar macrophages.
Femur and tibia were removed from mice to flush bone marrow cells with 5 mL RPMI 1640 media (Gibco, 22400089) through a 27 g needle and strained using a 100 μm filter. Cell suspension was then centrifuged at 1250 RPM for 10 min to pellet cells following by incubation with BD Pharm Lyse™ Lysing Buffer (BD Biosciences, 555899) and washing with PBS to remove red blood cells. Supernatant was decanted and replaced with fresh bone marrow-derived macrophage differentiation media (RPMI 1640 + 5 ng/ mL M–CSF (Peprotech, 315–02) + 10 % FBS+penicillin streptomycin). Cells were allowed to differentiate for a total of 6 days with media replacement at day 4. Bone marrow-derived macrophages were treated with HDM (10 ug/mL) or PBS for 24 h before collection. Alveolar macrophages were taken from BAL cells of naïve wild-type and adherent cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 + 5 ng/mL M–CSF for a total of 2 days.

## Macrophage adoptive transfer.
Acod1-KO mice were sensitized with HDM (100 ug intranasally) on days 1 and 4. A total of 0.5 x 106 cultured wild-type or Acod1-KO bone marrow-derived macrophages were instilled intranasally on day 8. Mice were challenged with HDM (100 ug) or PBS on day 10. Lungs were harvested 6 h after treatments on day 10.

## T cell proliferation and differentiation.
Mouse CD4 + T cells were isolated from spleens of naïve C57BL/6J mice with mouse CD4 + T cell isolation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130–104-454). In brief, CD4 + T cells were isolated by depletion of non CD4 + T cells using a cocktail of biotin-conjugated antibodies against CD8a, CD11b, CD11c, CD19, CD45R (B220), CD49b (DX5), CD105, MHC-class II, Ter-119 and TCRγ/δ as primary labeling reagent. The cells were then magnetically labeled with Anti-Biotin MicroBeads. The magnetically labeled non-target cells are depleted by retaining them on a MACS Column (Miltenyi Biotec, 130–042-201) in the magnetic field of a MACS Separator, while the unlabeled target cells pass through the column.
Isolated CD4 + cells were resuspended in prewarmed RPMI1640 medium containing 20 ng/mL murine IL2 (Peprotech, 212–12), 50 uM 2-mercaptoethanol (Gibco, 21985023), and 1 ug/mL anti-mouse CD28 (BD Biosciences, 553295). For Th2 cell differentiation, cells were cultured with anti-mouse IFN-γ antibody (3 ug/mL, Biolegend, 517905) and murine IL-4 (100 ng/mL, Peprotech, 214–14) with or without itaconate at concentrations of 0.1, 1, or 5 mM. Cells were plated into U-bottom 96-well plates coated with 10 ug/mL anti-mouse CD3e (BD Biosciences, 553295) for up to 72 h before collection. Cells were collected for qPCR and Western blot analysis. Cell culture supernatants were collected for ELISA and cytotoxicity assay. IL-5 and IL-13 were measured by ELISA (Rogel Cancer Center Immunology Core, University of Michigan). Cytotoxicity was assayed using the LDH Cytotoxicity Detection Kit (Takara, MK401). Itaconate solution was prepared by dissolving itaconic acid powder (Sigma Aldrich, I29204) in PBS, followed by pH adjustment to 7.2 with NaOH.

## Western blot assay.
Cells were dissolved in lysis buffer and homogenized for Western blot assay using anti-Gata3 (Abclonal, A19636), anti-Acod1 (Abcam, ab222411), and anti-β actin (Millipore Sigma, A1978).

## Statistical analysis.
All data were represented as mean ± standard error (SE). Statistical significance was assessed by two-tailed unpaired t test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). For ANOVA, group differences were pinpointed by the Tukey multiple comparison test.

## Human data analysis.
We performed data mining on two publicly available datasets. The first dataset (GEO accession number GSE118761) used RNAseq analysis to compare RNA expression in the nasal brushings of atopic non-asthmatic and atopic asthmatic subjects. Differentially expressed genes between atopic asthma and atopic non-asthma subjects were analyzed with GEO2R from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus. The sample IDs of atopic asthma subjects (n = 10) are GSM3346629, GSM3346631, GSM3346633, GSM3346635, GSM3346637, GSM3346639, GSM3346641, GSM3346643, GSM3346645, and GSM3346647. The sample IDs of atopic non-asthma (n = 14) are GSM3346600, GSM3346602, GSM3346604, GSM3346606, GSM3346608, GSM3346610, GSM3346612, GSM3346614, GSM3346616, GSM3346618, GSM3346620, GSM3346622, GSM3346624, and GSM3346627. The second dataset employed single-cell RNAseq to compare RNA profiles from the lower airway mucosa of allergic asthmatics and allergic non-asthmatics. The transcriptomics data of Acod1 for each of the manually curated cell clusters was visualized using the Villani Labratory online tool (https://villani.mgh.harvard.edu/allergy-asthma/).
