## Summary
Here, we present a protocol to identify nucleotide motifs that predict the pro-inflammatory property of microRNAs (miRNAs) using machine learning. We describe steps for cell culture, miRNA transfection, pro-inflammatory classification, and k-mer discovery. We detail procedures for combining in vitro macrophage assays with exhaustive motif searches and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to define nucleotide sequence features that distinguish pro-inflammatory miRNAs. This workflow enables systematic motif discovery and biomarker prioritization directly from miRNA sequences, streamlining translational applications without extensive functional screening.
For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Ren et al.

## Before you begin
Recent evidence demonstrates that certain extracellular microRNAs (miRNAs) function as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) to activate innate immune response via Toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7) signaling. , However, determination of the pro-inflammatory property of miRNA relies on an in vitro readout of cytokine production in a cell culture, which involves miRNA mimic transfection and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). To predict if a given miRNA possesses such pro-inflammatory property, we employed a machine learning-guided computer exhaustive search algorithm to identify specific nucleotide sequences (i.e., motifs) that are uniquely present in pro-inflammatory, but not in non-inflammatory, miRNA structures. The set of miRNA motifs identified via this protocol predicts the pro-inflammatory property of a miRNA with 80–90% sensitivity and 70–80% specificity. The newly identified miRNA motifs help identify pro-inflammatory miRNAs and guide selection of miRNA candidates for biomarker discovery.
The current protocol describes the complete experimental and computational workflow of miRNA pro-inflammatory motif discovery, including miRNA mimic synthesis, macrophage transfection, cytokine detection, binary outcome classification, motif discovery, and performance evaluation. The protocol provides a standardized approach for generating custom training and validation datasets of pro-inflammatory and non-inflammatory miRNAs and for executing the motif discovery algorithm to identify sequence motifs uniquely enriched in pro-inflammatory miRNAs. In addition, the computational algorithm and associated code can be adapted to discover sequence motifs across any oligonucleotide sequence dataset with binary classification outcomes (e.g., 0 and 1).

## Innovation
This protocol employs a machine learning strategy to identify nucleotide motifs that predict pro-inflammatory property of miRNAs. By leveraging motif-based classification, it enables efficient biomarker prioritization directly from sequence thus reducing the need for extensive functional screening.

## Macrophage transfection with miRNA mimics

Timing: 2 days

CRITICAL: Procedures from steps 1–15 should be performed in a biological safety cabinet using sterile technique.

1.Culture RAW 264.7 mouse macrophages in 10-cm cell culture dish until 80% confluency.
2.Scrape off cells using a cell lifter with soft rubber blade and transfer cell suspension into a 50 mL conical tube.
3.Centrifuge at 300 g for 5 min at 20°C–25°C.
4.Remove supernatant and resuspend cells in 5 mL culture medium.
5.Mix cells 1:1 (v/v) with Trypan Blue, count viable (non-blue) cells using a hemocytometer, then dilute to a final density of 3×105/mL.
6.Dispense 100 μL cells per well into non-edge wells of a 96-well plate as shown below.
7.To maintain consistent humidity and minimize edge effects, fill the outermost wells of the plate with PBS (or water).
8.Incubate cells at 37°C with 5% CO2.
9.On the next day, cells are ready for transfection. 

Note: Check the cell morphology and density under microscope at 10x objective. Optimal conditions are around 70% confluency ( ), and cells typically appear bright and round. Confluency <50% at transfection may lead to suboptimal growth conditions and cell loss/detachment during medium changes.

10.Cell starvation.a.Remove the previous medium carefully from the well, slowly add 100 μL pre-warmed starvation medium.
b.Incubate for 30 s.
c.Remove starvation medium, add 90 μL starvation medium.
d.Keep culturing for 2 h before transfection.

CRITICAL: Work quickly to prevent cell desiccation when aspirating medium and add fresh medium gently along the flask wall to avoid detaching cells.

Note: Confirm cells are not detached after starvation under 10x objective microscope.

11.miRNA working solution preparation.a.Add 18 μL DMEM in a series of 3 tubes (labeled as tube A1, B1 and C1).
b.Add 2 μL 100 μM miRNA stock solution to tube A1.Note: For positive control, add 2 μL hsa-miR-146a-5p; For test group, add 2 μL miRNA mimics; For negative control, add 2 μL DMEM.

c.Mix tube A1 with vortex, transfer 2 μL of tube A1 solution to tube B1.
d.Mix tube B1 with vortex, transfer 2 μL of tube B1 solution to tube C1.
e.Mix tube C1 with vortex.

12.Lipofectamine working solution preparation.a.Add 0.15 μL lipofectamine 3000 is used for transfection per well.

Note: To prepare Lipofectamine for 60 wells of transfection as shown above, add 9 μL lipofectamine 3000 into 291 μL of DMEM, gently mix. (Scale accordingly to sample size.) An extra 10% of reagent is typically prepared to account for potential pipetting errors.

13.Nucleic acid-lipofectamine complex preparation.a.Dispense 12.5 μL of miRNA working solution from tube A1, B1 and C1 into new tubes A2, B2 and C2 respectively.
b.Dispense 12.5 μL lipofectamine working solution to each of the tube A2, B2, C2. Mix gently by flicking the tube.
c.Incubate the mixture for 15 min at 20°C–25°C.

14.Transfection.a.Dispense 10 μL miRNA-lipofectamine 3000 complex from the tube A2 into the appropriate RAW 264.7 well for a final transfection concentration of 500 nM. The complexes are enough for duplicate wells.
b.Dispense 10 μL complex from tube B2 to wells for 50 nM.
c.Dispense 10 μL complex from tube C2 to wells for 5 nM.

15.Incubate at 37°C with 5% CO2.
16.18 h later, carefully transfer 60 μL of supernatant from each well into centrifuge tubes without disturbing the cell layer at the bottom.
17.Centrifuge at 400 g for 10 min at 4°C.
18.Transfer the supernatant to new tubes.
19.The supernatant samples can be used immediately for the next step.

Pause point: Save supernatant samples in −80°C freezer. Samples are stable for at least 2 months.

## K-mer discovery and optimization (K-mer_discovery.py)

Timing: <1 min with parallel computing enabled for calculating 2- to 10-mers

Note: In this part, we generate all theoretical k-mers that include wildcard positions within miRNAs from Step 23, where k is a value defined by the user. The value of k can range from 2 to N (any integer within the length of miRNA). While k can start at 2, using a very small value may yield low-complexity motifs that are common across various miRNAs. Additionally, N should be determined based on the available computational resources, as the number of candidate motifs increases exponentially with k. To manage memory usage and reduce processing time, only motifs that meet the specified criteria in the provided sequences are retained for further analysis.

Required input file: training_testing_sequence.xlsx: Provide the Excel sheet containing summary of miRNA nucleotide sequences and classification results (Step 23). Ensure that the file is accessible.Note: We list only the critical steps of the k-mer discovery workflow here. 24.Configure parameters.Note: Configure the required parameters before executing the Python script “K-mer_discovery.py”.
a.k-mer range (kmin, kmax): Set the inclusive bounds for the search and ensure that kmin is less than or equal to kmax.

Note: Given a set of unique k-length strings, we generate all possible wildcard patterns by replacing any subset of positions with the dot character (.). For each input motif candidate, we apply each mask to produce a pattern with dots inserted at the specified masked positions. Finally, we eliminate any duplicate motifs by storing them in a set.

>   this_str = list(key)
>   for index in comb:
>    this_str[index-1] = '.'

## Expected outcomes
This protocol generates a validated set of nucleotide motifs that predict pro-inflammatory activity of miRNAs directly from sequence. Expected outcomes include a validated LASSO model for classifying pro-inflammatory miRNAs, a ranked list of sequence features enabling flexible feature selection, and 5 essential motifs distinguishing pro-inflammatory from non-inflammatory miRNAs. The pipeline produces highly reproducible results with motifs, model coefficients, and performance metrics. This approach streamlines therapeutic miRNA candidate screening by eliminating extensive wet-lab functional assays.

## Limitations
We utilize a mouse macrophage cell line to assess the pro-inflammatory property of miRNAs. While previous research has demonstrated conserved mechanisms of miRNA sensing between mouse and human immune cells, incorporating human cell lines in future experiments may enhance the predictive reliability of motif-guided miRNA classification for human applications.
For motif discovery, we have derived over half a million motifs from 33 training miRNAs in our study. To identify motifs relevant to inflammation, we employed LASSO regression. This approach is effective because the features with non-zero coefficients in the LASSO model are the most important factors for predicting the outcome. However, this method may not fully capture the complexities of motif interactions. Other machine learning approaches, such as Elastic Net or deep learning techniques, may offer improved motif discovery by recognizing complicated motifs and interactions. In addition, the optimization of hyperparameters, such as the regularization parameter λ (denoted as alpha in the code script) in the LASSO model, plays a crucial role in the model’s performance. While we optimized these parameters based on our training dataset, further research is needed to explore the robustness of our findings across different hyperparameter values and to identify the optimal settings.

## Troubleshooting

## Problem 1
Low CXCL2 production. In RAW 264.7 macrophage cells transfected with PS-modified hsa-miR-146a-5p as positive control, CXCL2 concentration should follow a dose-dependent pattern. In our previous experience, the expected concentrations (mean ± SD) would be 5 nM miR: 5136 ± 1530 pg/mL, 50 nM miR: 30202 ± 12777 pg/mL, 500 nM miR: 67016 ± 28383 pg/mL (summarized from six independent transfections, with each transfection performed in two wells). If the measured CXCL2 is significantly lower than the above range, consider following the solutions below for troubleshooting.

## Potential solution
Allow cells to grow for an extended period (24–48 h) before seeding them into the 96-well plate (step 1). Slightly increase the initial cell seeding density (step 5) and ensure that the plates used are cell- or tissue-culture treated (step 6). If cells consistently show poor attachment, reduce the starvation period to approximately 1 h (step 10). Check for microbial contamination (step 1) and assess cell viability with Trypan Blue staining: if viability is low, optimize growth conditions so that live (unstained) cells account for more than 90% of total cells (step 5). For lyophilized miRNA mimics, centrifuge briefly at >10,000 g for 1 min before opening to prevent sample loss, then add water, mix thoroughly, and incubate at 20–25°C for 10 min to ensure complete resuspension before use (Before You Begin, step 5).

## Problem 2
Cell detachment during culture or plate handling.

## Potential solution
Add medium slowly and at an angle by pipetting gently against the plate wall (step 10). Always use pre-warmed medium when refreshing cultures (step 10). For a full 96-well plate or larger setups, use multichannel pipettes to minimize air-drying time between procedures, or refresh the medium in regional passes to reduce cellular stress (step 10). If cells detach before starvation, check for microbial contamination and confirm that the plate surface is properly cell-culture coated (step 9). If detachment occurs after transfection, reduce the amount of Lipofectamine used for each transfection (steps 12–15).
