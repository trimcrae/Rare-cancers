## Introduction
Benign tracheal stenosis (BTS) is characterized by granulation tissue hyperplasia, driven by abnormal fibroblast activation and excessive extracellular matrix deposition. Targeting these mechanisms with novel therapies may offer a promising approach to addressing or preventing re-stenosis. Our previous study demonstrated that airway basal stem cell (BSC) transplantation can inhibit granulation hyperplasia in BTS.

## Objectives
Given the therapeutic potential of extracellular vesicles (EVs) as mediators in cell-based therapies, we investigated the in vivo efficacy of airway BSC-derived EVs (BSC-EVs) and explored their underlying mechanisms.

## Methods
BTS was induced in rabbits via tracheal mucosal injury. BSC-EVs were isolated, characterized, and intratracheally delivered. Then, therapeutic effects were evaluated using bronchoscopy, 18F-FAPI PET/CT, histology, and RNA-seq. The underlying mechanisms were investigated in vitro focusing on miRNA regulation of human airway fibroblasts (AFs).

## Results
Our findings revealed that BSC-EVs significantly reduce granulation hyperplasia, promote ECM remodeling and improve collagen fiber alignment, leading to enhanced airway patency and prolonged survival. Mechanistically, we identified miR-30a-5p as a key functional cargo within BSC-EVs, which directly targets the fibroblast activation protein (FAP) gene. In vitro experiments demonstrated that miR-30a-5p mediates the anti-fibrotic effects of BSC-EVs by attenuating fibroblast activation, collagen contraction, and ECM deposition.

## Conclusions
These findings suggest that BSC-EVs represent a promising therapeutic strategy for BTS by inhibiting fibroblast activation and promoting ECM remodeling via the miR-30a-5p/FAP axis, offering a novel advancement beyond current treatment approaches.

## Introduction
Airway fibrosis is a defining feature of many chronic respiratory diseases, driven by the accumulation of activated fibroblasts and extracellular matrix (ECM), resulting in airway obstruction and diminished lung function [ , ]. Recurrent benign tracheal stenosis (BTS) presents a typical example of this pathological process. It is characterized by excessive collagen deposition and granulation tissue overgrowth, which often leads to a high rate of re-stenosis [ ]. Despite the availability of traditional treatments, such as surgical and bronchoscopic procedures, these interventions are associated with significant complications, offer only temporary relief, and fail to prevent disease progression [ ]. These limitations emphasize the urgent need for new therapeutic approaches targeting the root causes of tracheal fibrosis. One critical factor in the development of airway fibrosis is airway epithelium injury, which impairs the stable signaling pathways that regulate the balance between epithelial cells and mesenchymal cells (i.e. fibroblasts) [ ]. This disruption activates fibroblasts and promotes excessive ECM deposition, and ultimately accelerates airway stenosis. Therefore, this evidence suggests a promising therapeutic strategy targeting the regulation of mesenchymal-epithelial interactions and ECM homeostasis.
In line with this therapeutic concept, recent efforts have leveraged the regenerative potential of airway basal stem cells (BSCs) to treat fibrotic respiratory diseases [ , ]. As progenitors of bronchial epithelial cells, TP63 + BSCs play a pivotal role in repairing damaged lung tissue and promoting epithelial and alveolar regeneration following respiratory injury [ ]. Our previous work demonstrated that BSC transplantation effectively inhibits granulation hyperplasia in BTS, suggesting the potential of BSC-based therapy [ ]. Despite their advantages, the therapeutic effects of stem cell therapies are increasingly attributed to paracrine signaling, especially through extracellular vesicles (EVs), which mediate key molecular interactions between cells [ ]. In addition, EVs offer a safer and more feasible therapeutic approach compared to direct cell transplantation, addressing challenges such as immune rejection and low cell survival. Previous studies have shown that EVs derived from bronchial epithelial cell or lung spheroid cells carry miRNAs that mitigate lung fibrosis by inhibiting fibroblast activation [ , ]. However, the heterogeneity of EVs derived from mixed epithelial cell populations complicates their therapeutic efficacy and limits consistent production and precise application. To overcome these limitations, a targeted approach using a well-defined, single-source population of bronchial epithelial-derived EVs-such as BSC-derived EVs (BSC-EVs)-appears to be a more superior strategy. Similarly, airway BSC-EVs have been used to modulate epithelial-mesenchymal interaction in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, supporting their potential anti-fibrotic effects [ ]. Moreover, miRNAs contained within EVs have been confirmed as key cargo for regulating the behavior of recipient cells, with several studies showing their ability to ameliorate fibrosis [ , ].
Therefore, we hypothesize that BSC-EVs comprising functional miRNAs may improve airway fibrosis in BTS. In this study, we induced tracheal mucosal injury in a rabbit model to simulate BTS and investigated the therapeutic effects of BSC-EVs, as well as underlying mechanisms. We further confirmed the effective retention of BSC-EVs within the tracheal tissues and observed that BSC-EV treatment inhibited fibroblast activation and optimized ECM remodeling. Through miRNA sequencing, we identified miR-30a-5p as a key component of BSC-EVs, which targets fibroblast activation protein (FAP) and exerts anti-fibrotic effects in vitro. These findings suggest that BSC-EVs serve as effective therapeutic agents that inhibit fibroblast activation and promote adaptive ECM remodeling through the miR-30a-5p/FAP axis in BTS.

## Material and methods

## Human primary airway BSCs and AFs cultures
This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (Ethics Review Number: ES-2024-K075-01), and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Human airway tissue samples were obtained from the 3rd to 5th bronchial segments of healthy lungs. The inclusion criteria for donors were: (a) age below 60 years with no history of smoking; (b) normal lung function and absence of respiratory symptoms; and (c) undergoing bronchoscopy due to suspicious nodules identified on chest imaging. Primary human airway BSCs were isolated from epithelial samples and cultured in BSC complete medium according to the patent (Chinese patent CN114958727B). Additionally, primary human AFs were isolated from the airway epithelium of patients diagnosed with BTS and cultured in complete DMEM medium.

## Isolation, identification, and labeling of BSC-EVs
BSC-EVs were isolated using an ultrahigh-speed centrifugation method [ ]. Briefly, BSCs were cultured in exosome-isolation medium, and the culture medium was collected. The medium was first centrifuged at 300g for 10 min, then at 10,000g for 30 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-μm filter and ultracentrifuged at 100,000g for 70 min. Finally, 5 × 10^10 BSC-EVs was resuspended in 100 μL of PBS. Morphological characterization of BSC-EVs was performed using transmission electron microscopy, and their size distribution and particle concentration were analyzed by Nano-Flow Cytometry. Western blot identified EV markers, including CD9, CD63, CD81, HSP70, and TSG101. BSC-EVs were labeled with the cell membrane dye PKH26 (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA).

## Animal experiment procedure
To establish the rabbit BTS model, three-month-old white New Zealand rabbits (3.0–3.2 kg) were purchased from Longgui Xingke, Guangzhou. The animal experiment protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee (license number: No. XK202401003). BTS was induced by mechanical scraping of the tracheal mucosa using a nylon brush, as previously described [ ]. Airway secretion clearance was performed on days 3 and 5, and tracheal stenosis was confirmed by bronchoscopy on day 7. The rabbits were randomly divided into two groups: PBS-treated and BSC-EVs-treated. Each group received 600 µL of PBS or 5 × 10^10 BSC-EVs particles via intratracheal injection. PKH-26-labeled BSC-EVs were injected into the trachea and tissue was harvested over the following 2 weeks to assess distribution of BSC-EVs. Distribution was examined using macroscopic analysis, fluorescent stereomicroscopy, and fluorescence microscopy. The tracheal stenosis index was calculated using the formula: stenosis index = [1 − (d1 + d2)/(D1 + D2)] × 100 %. where dn and Dn represent the diameters of the stenosed and normal tracheal segments, respectively [ ]. The lumen diameter was measured following tissue section scanning.

## F-FAPI PET/CT imaging and analysis.
PET/CT scans were performed using a Panorama PET/CT scanner (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China) 40 min after injecting 1.48–2.22 MBq of 18F-FAPI. Radioactivity was expressed as the SUVmax in %inject-dose per gram (%ID/g). Tracheal stenosis was assessed using the stenosis index, based on the tracheal diameters from the CT scan. The anterior-posterior dimension was measured as D1, and the transverse dimension as D2 in the axial plane. The stenosis index was calculated from these measurements in the mediastinal window.

## HE staining and immunohistochemistry
Tracheal segment tissues were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde and sectioned into 4-μm slices. For HE, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence staining, paraffin sections were first baked, dewaxed, and rehydrated. HE staining was performed according to standard protocol: tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin solution for 5 min, followed by 5 dips in 1 % acid ethanol and rinsed in distilled water. The sections were then stained with eosin solution for 3 min, dehydrated with graded alcohol, and cleared in xylene. For immunohistochemistry, antigen retrieval was performed using EDTA (pH 8.0). The primary antibodies against FAP (1:50, CST), α-SMA (1:400, Abcam), FN1 (1:100, Abcam), and Collagen I (1:100, Abcam) were incubated with the sections overnight at 4 °C. Semi-quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

## Masson trichrome-staining and extracellular matrix structure analysis
Masson trichrome staining was performed following a standard protocol. The collagen volume fraction was calculated as the ratio of the total area of fibrosis to the total area of the rabbit tracheal segment in the specimens. CVF was quantified using ImageJ software. The TWOMBLI Fiji macro was employed to analyze the structural characteristics of collagen fibers [ ]. Five metrics were examined, including area, total length, endpoints, curvature, and high-density matrix of the collagen fibers.

## Next generation sequencing and bioinformatics analysis
RNA sequencing of rabbit trachea tissue (Sham control: n = 4, BTS: n = 5, BSC-EVs: n = 8) and small RNA sequencing of BSC-EVs (n = 6) were performed on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Guangzhou, China). Total RNA was extracted using the Trizol reagent kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and checked with RNase-free agarose gel electrophoresis. Detailed bioinformatic analysis was performed using Omicsmart, a real-time interactive online platform for data analysis (https://www.omicsmart.com).

## miRNA FISH assay
Oligonucleotide-modified probes labeled with FITC-TSA were used to detect hsa-miR-30a-5p. FISH assays were performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections. Briefly, 4-μm sections were preheated at 60 °C for 30 min, then digested with proteinase K at 37 °C for 15 min, and washed three times with PBS. The slides were refixed and incubated in a pre-hybridization solution at 37 °C for 1 h. This solution, containing a biotin-labeled hsa-miR-30a-5p probe, was used for overnight hybridization at 37 °C in a humidified chamber. After hybridization, washing was performed with 4×, 2×, 1×, and 0.2 × SSC at 37 °C for 30 min each. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DM6, Wetzlar, Germany).

## miRNA transfection and coculture
MiR-30a-5p transfection was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions for Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, CA, USA). To investigate the effect of BSCs on fibroblast activation and the role of BSC-EVs, BSCs and fibroblasts were co-cultured in a transwell system (0.4 μm). BSCs were transfected with either NC or miR-30a-5p inhibitors/mimics in the upper chamber for 24 h. Afterward, BSCs were placed above the AFs for an additional 24 h, with DMSO or 10 µM GW4869 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) added to the upper chamber. Additionally, 2 × 10^4 BSC-EVs particles were directly added to AFs and then transfected with NC inhibitors or miR-30a-5p inhibitors for 24 h.

## Luciferase reporter assay
We first utilized target gene prediction platforms (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_80/) to identify potential target genes of miR-30a-5p. Then, the 3′-UTR of FAP, containing the putative target site for miR-30a-5p, or its mutated form, was inserted into the pmirGLO luciferase reporter vector. Subsequently, the wild-type pmirGLO-FAP-3′-UTR or the mutant pmirGLO-FAP-3′-UTR was co-transfected with Lipofectamine 3000, along with scrambled miRNA and miR-30a-5p mimics. The luciferase activity was measured 48 h post-transfection using a dual luciferase reporter gene assay kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China).

## RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was extracted using the TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit (Transgen, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Reverse transcription was performed for miRNA using the miRNA First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) and for mRNA using the Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (Yeasen). qRT-PCR amplification was conducted using the MicroRNAs qPCR Kit (Sangon Biotech) and Hieff UNICON® qPCR SYBR® Green Master Mix (Yeasen) on a CFX Connection Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). Relative expression levels of miRNA and mRNA were normalized to U6 or GAPDH and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primers are listed in .

## Western blotting analysis
Total protein was extracted and concentrations were measured using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Beyotime). A total of 20 µg of protein was separated by 10 % SDS-PAGE, transferred to a PVDF membrane (Merck-Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany), and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (1:1000 dilution) against CD9 (SAB, Maryland, USA), CD63 (SAB), CD81 (SAB), TSG101 (SAB), HSP70 (SAB), Collagen I (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Vimentin (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), α-SMA (Abcam), FAP (CST, MA, USA), Matrix Remodeling Antibody Sampler Kit (CST), Phospho-SMAD2 (CST), Phospho-SMAD3 (CST), SMAD2/3 (CST), GAPDH (CST), and β-actin (CST). Protein bands were detected and quantified using ImageJ software.

## Immunocytochemistry
Cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.3 % Triton X-100, and blocked with 5 % bovine serum albumin for 1 h. Corresponding primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature and mounted in Vectashield mounting medium containing DAPI. Images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica DMi8, Wetzlar, Germany).

## Collagen gel contraction assay
Fibroblasts were suspended in serum-free medium and mixed with 3 mg/mL neutralized rat tail type I collagen (Corning, NY, USA) at a 2:1 ratio. The gels were allowed to solidify for 2 h at 37 °C, after which 1 mL of culture medium was added and incubated for 8 h at 37 °C. Gel areas were measured using ImageJ, and the gel area percentage was calculated as x/n (where x = area of each group with cells and n = area of the well).

## Statistics analysis
All results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). A two-tailed paired Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, while Mann–Whitney U test was employed for nonparametric analysis. Comparisons among three groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA. Differences were considered statistically significant at P ≤ 0.05. Statistical significance levels are indicated as follows: *P ≤ 0.05; **P ≤ 0.01; ***P ≤ 0.001.

## Results

## Fibroblast activation and collagen deposition underlie BTS pathology in human and rabbit models
Fibroblast activation and excessive collagen deposition are key features of BTS pathology, as shown by elevated levels of FAP, α-SMA, and collagen I in BTS patient tissues compared to normal controls ( a, b). To simulate these pathological features, we induced tracheal injury in rabbits using a nylon brush ( c). Bronchoscopic evaluation, 18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging, and histology revealed significant airway narrowing due to granulation tissue proliferation within two weeks (P < 0.05; d, e). The injured tracheal regions exhibited a lack of mature collagen and epithelial cells, further confirming airway remodeling in BTS ( d). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed upregulation of collagen-related pathways and downregulation of cilium-related pathways in BTS tissues ( a). In addition, 18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging demonstrated a progressive increase in the maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 18F-FAPI, reflecting persistent FAP uptake, which correlated with tracheal stenosis progression (R2 = 0.4822, P = 0.0041; f, g). FDG-PET imaging confirmed that FAP uptake was specific to fibrosis, excluding inflammation as a contributing factor ( b). Western blot analysis further validated a time-dependent increase in FAP expression ( h, i). Overall, these findings indicate that the rabbit BTS model faithfully replicates key pathological features of human BTS, and support FAP is a potential biomarker for monitoring disease progression. 

## Precise characterization of BSC-EVs and their effective retention in BTS
To confirm the stemness and differentiation potential of BSCs, we performed immunocytochemistry and hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining. We observed abundant KRT5+/P63+ and KRT5+/KI67+ nuclei, indicating their capacity for self-renewal and proliferation ( a). Under air–liquid interface (ALI) culture conditions, BSCs differentiated into mature airway epithelial cells, expressing MUC5B and AC-TUB proteins and forming a pseudostratified epithelium ( a, b). These findings confirm that BSCs possess the both self-renew and differentiation capabilities. Next, we isolated BSC-EVs from the culture supernatant by ultracentrifugation. Nanoflow cytometry and transmission electron microscopy revealed BSC-EVs exhibit the characteristic cup-shaped morphology, with an average size of 70 ± 20 nm ( c, d). Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of EV-associated surface markers, including CD9, CD63, CD81, HSP70, and TSG101, further verifying the purity of isolated BSC-EVs ( e). To investigate intratracheal delivery and retention, we administered 600 µL of 5 × 10^10 PKH26-labeled BSC-EVs into the tracheal submucosa of stenosis via bronchoscopy. Fluorescent stereomicroscopy and microscopy confirmed that BSC-EVs were retained within the granulation tissue for up to 14 days ( f). These findings demonstrate the feasibility of intratracheal BSC-EVs delivery and their sustained retention in fibrotic tracheal tissues, supporting their potential as a localized therapy for BTS. 

## BSC-EVs alleviate BTS progression and enhance ECM remodeling for structural restoration
To assess the therapeutic potential of BSC-EVs, we evaluated their effects in a rabbit BTS model ( a). Survival analysis showed that the median survival time was 12 days in the PBS group and 14 days in the BSC-EVs group, with survival rates of 20 % and 75 % at day 14, respectively (P = 0.038; b). Bronchoscopic evaluation, 18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging, histological, and immunohistochemical analysis confirmed the therapeutic efficacy of BSC-EVs ( c). By day 14, bronchoscopy revealed a significant reduction in granulation hyperplasia and improved luminal patency in the BSC-EVs group ( d). Although no significant difference in SUVmax was observed between groups on day 14, the BSC-EVs group showed a marked reduction by day 21 ( e). These results suggest that BSC-EVs alleviate or delay disease progression following BTS induction. 
Given these promising outcomes, we further analyzed the histopathological changes post-treatment. Collagen deposition was significantly reduced in the BSC-EVs group (mean 43.608 %) compared to the PBS group (mean 55.116 %) at day 14, with continued increase at days 21 (49.088 %) and 28 (64.876 %) ( f). Additionally, collagen I protein levels in tracheal tissue decreased significantly in treated animals at day 14 ( g). Notably, the BSC-EVs group initially showed an increase in collagen fiber curvature and number of endpoints, followed by a progressive decline by day 28, suggesting dynamic collagen remodeling ( h). Meanwhile, the high-density collagen matrix progressively increased, indicating enhanced fiber organization and alignment at the later stages ( h and ). Together, these data highlight that BSC-EVs promote ECM remodeling by improving collagen fiber organization and structure integrity in BTS models, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating airway fibrosis and enhancing tracheal tissue restoration.

## BSC-EVs modulate MMP/TIMP balance to restore ECM dynamics in BTS
To assess the impact of BSC-EVs on ECM remodeling, we performed RNA sequencing on tracheal tissues from sham control, PBS-treated, and BSC-EVs-treated groups. Differentially expressed genes were visualized using a volcano plot ( a), revealing significant changes in collagen fibril organization ( b) and suggesting that BSC-EVs influence key ECM remodeling pathways. Specifically, BSC-EVs treatment significantly reduced FAP expression, as confirmed by qRT-PCR analysis of tracheal tissue ( c, d). Additionally, BSC-EVs modulated the expression of key ECM proteins, including collagen I, MMPs, and TIMPs ( e). Notably, MMP9 expression was upregulated, while Collagen I expression was downregulated following BSC-EVs treatment ( f), indicating that BSC-EVs promote collagen degradation. Schematic figure showing the ECM dynamics restore pathway of BSC-EVs ( g). These findings demonstrate that BSC-EVs help restore ECM homeostasis by modulating the MMP/TIMP balance, with MMP9 playing a key role in mitigating excessive collagen deposition, thereby contributing to ECM remodeling in BTS. 

## miR-30a-5p is highly enriched in BSC-EVs and targets FAP
Given the therapeutic effects of BSC-EVs on tracheal fibrosis, we investigated the miRNA cargo within BSC-EVs to identify potential mediators of their anti-fibrotic activity. miRNA sequencing of six BSC-EVs samples identified 60 abundant miRNAs, with a subset of 34 co-expressed miRNAs ( a). To identify the key miRNA responsible for the therapeutic effects, human airway fibroblasts (AFs) were treated with BSC-EVs, followed by qRT-PCR analysis to evaluate differentially expressed miRNAs in PBS- and BSC-EVs-treated groups ( ). Six miRNAs, including let-7a-5p, let-7f-5p, miR-30a-5p, miR-125a-5p, miR-146a-5p, and miR-222-3p, were significantly upregulated following BSC-EV treatment ( b). Considering the roles of FAP and MMP9 in fibroblast activation, we tested miRNA mimics in AFs, revealing that miR-30a-5p mimics significantly suppressed FAP expression while increasing MMP9 levels ( c). These findings pinpoint miR-30a-5p as a key regulator of fibroblast activation and collagen deposition in BSC-EVs. 
To further investigate the localization of miR-30a-5p in tracheal tissue, we performed HE staining and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) assays, which demonstrated that higher miR-30a-5p expression in normal airway epithelium compared to BTS epithelium ( d). Bioinformatics analysis predicted FAP as a potential target of miR-30a-5p, which was validated by a dual-luciferase reporter assay, confirming that miR-30a-5p binds to the 3′ UTR region of FAP ( e). Additionally, we modulated miR-30a-5p expression in AFs using miR-30a-5p mimics and inhibitors, further supporting its role in fibroblast regulation ( f). Collectively, these results demonstrate that miR-30a-5p, enriched in BSC-EVs, plays a pivotal role in modulating fibroblast activation and ECM remodeling in BTS by directly targeting FAP.

## MiR-30a-5p in BSC-EVs drives fibroblast-mediated degradation of ECM in vitro
To elucidate the role of miR-30a-5p in mediating the anti-fibrotic effects of BSC-EVs, we first established a co-culture system of BSCs and AFs ( a). Inhibition of miR-30a-5p expression was confirmed by qRT-PCR analysis ( a). The results showed that inhibiting miR-30a-5p significantly increased FAP expression and reduced collagen gel contraction, indicating enhanced fibroblast activation ( b). Further analysis revealed elevated collagen I and FAP levels, along with decreased MMP2 and MMP9 expression ( b and c). In rescue experiments, the inhibition of miR-30a-5p-containing BSC-EV secretion using GW4869 ( c) significantly reduced the gel area, further confirming the role of miR-30a-5p in ECM remodeling ( d). qRT-PCR and western blot analysis revealed decreased FAP and increased MMP2/MMP9 expression in the miR-30a-5p mimic groups, which was attenuated by GW4869 treatment ( d and e). To further validate these findings, BSC-EVs were directly applied to AFs transfected with miR-30a-5p inhibitors or negative controls (NCs) ( a). As expected, miR-30a-5p inhibitors attenuated the anti-fibrotic effects of BSC-EVs ( b). Western blot analysis confirmed that ECM degradation induced by BSC-EVs was reversed by miR-30a-5p inhibitors ( c, d). These findings underscore the critical role of miR-30a-5p in BSC-EVs, driving anti-fibrotic effects by inhibiting fibroblast activation and promoting ECM degradation. 
In summary, our findings demonstrate the therapeutic potential of BSC-EVs in BTS, supported by by comprehensive in vivo and in vitro analyses. Mechanistically, RNA sequencing identified miR-30a-5p as a key functional component of BSC-EVs, which directly targets FAP. Functionally, miR-30a-5p-containing BSC-EVs effectively inhibit fibroblast activation and regulate ECM remodeling in vitro ( ). These findings highlight miR-30a-5p as a key regulator of ECM dynamics, providing valuable insights into potential therapeutic strategies for treating BTS. 

## Discussion
Airway fibrosis in BTS poses a significant clinical challenge, as current treatments focus primarily on symptomatic relief without addressing the underlying pathological mechanisms. This study highlights the therapeutic potential of airway BSC-EVs in treating BTS, emphasizing their superior efficacy in mitigating airway fibrosis. Specifically, BSC-EV treatment effectively decreased granulation tissue formation, stabilized ECM dynamics, and improved airway patency and survival in a preclinical BTS model. Multi-dimensional analyses revealed that inhibiting fibroblast activation was a critical prerequisite for these therapeutic effects, effectively halting the pathological cascade driving fibroproliferation. Importantly, we identified miR-30a-5p as a key functional cargo within BSC-EVs, which directly targets FAP to mediate collagen degradation and restore airway structure. These findings represent the first preclinical evidence supporting the use of BSC-EVs in airway fibrosis and serve as proof of concept for regenerative therapies in tracheal fibrosis treatment. Overall, this comprehensive study provides a paradigm-shifting approach to managing BTS and offers a promising foundation for expanding BSC-EVs-related therapies to other fibrosis/tissue remodeling-driven respiratory diseases.
Tracheal fibrosis, characterized by abnormal wound healing, involves a tightly regulated yet highly susceptible ECM remodeling process. Any disruption in the balance between ECM deposition and degradation can lead to excessive collagen accumulation and granulation tissue formation [ , ], ultimately resulting in airway narrowing and functional impairment. Current therapeutic strategies, including the use of mitomycin C or pirfenidone, primarily focus on inhibiting fibroblast proliferation and inflammation without addressing the remodeling phase of granulation tissue [ , ]. This limitation leaves ECM imbalance unresolved, perpetuating fibrosis progression. Our study demonstrates that BSC-EVs directly modulate ECM remodeling pathways, restoring ECM homeostasis. BSC-EVs effectively downregulate excessive collagen fibril organization while promoting collagen degradation through increased MMP2/MMP9 expression and coordinated regulation of TIMPs. Unlike conventional treatments, BSC-EVs intervene at a critical juncture, directly targeting ECM restoration, which is essential for reversing fibrosis and promoting functional tissue repair. This unique ability underscores the transformative potential of BSC-EVs in treating fibrotic airway disorders. Notably, BSC-EV treatment induced denser and more aligned collagen fibers, which resulted in a more stable ECM structure and reduced airway wall reversibility in BTS. This optimized remodeling of ECM is critical for achieving long-term stability and resolution of stenosis.
MicroRNAs serve as essential functional cargoes in EV-based therapies, mediating therapeutic effects by orchestrating complex cellular behaviors [ ]. Among the six identified anti-fibrotic miRNAs in BSC-EVs, miR-30a-5p emerged as a pivotal regulator in our study. Specifically, miR-30a-5p directly targets FAP, the initiating trigger for fibroblast activation, effectively halting the molecular cascade that drives excessive collagen deposition and fibrosis progression. This “first-step inhibition” underscores its potential in disrupting airway fibrosis initiation. In tracheal stenosis, severe epithelial damage leads to the loss of miRNA-mediated inhibitory signals, laying the foundational environment for uncontrolled fibroblast-driven aberrant repair processes [ , ]. Notably, miR-30a-5p is significantly downregulated in stenotic tissue but widely expressed in normal airway epithelium and mesenchyme, further implying its protective roles in maintaining ECM homeostasis. Furthermore, miR-30a-5p-containing BSC-EVs restored ECM stability by modulating MMP/TIMP expression, thereby promoting adaptive ECM remodeling and collagen organization. Similar findings have been reported for the miR-30a family in pulmonary fibrosis [ ]. Additionally, Chen et al. demonstrated that miR-30a alleviated liver fibrosis by downregulating α-SMA, collagen I, and TIMP-1 [ ]. Collectively, these findings highlight miR-30a-5p as a critical effector within BSC-EVs, capable of restoring ECM stability by orchestrating fibroblast behavior and protease activity. Beyond miR-30a-5p, other potentially therapeutic miRNAs identified in BSC-EVs, such as the let-7 family and miR-125[ , ], warrant further investigation to explore synergistic mechanisms for maximizing therapeutic efficacy.
FAP is a hallmark of activated fibroblasts, whose elevated expression has been associated with dysregulated ECM turnover and impaired tissue repair in pulmonary, cardiac, and hepatic fibrosis [ , ]. While prior studies suggest FAP as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target, its role in tracheal stenosis remains unclear [ ]. Our study identifies FAP as a critical driver of BTS progression and validates its therapeutic value in airway fibrosis. Using 18F-FAPI PET/CT imaging, we observed a progressive increase in FAPI uptake that correlated with BTS severity, supporting FAP as a dynamic biomarker of fibrotic activity. In early BTS (days 3–7), FAPI and FDG uptake increased in parallel, indicating fibroblast recruitment and initial ECM remodeling as part of physiological repair. By days 7–14, sustained FAP upregulation led to pathological fibrosis, coinciding with peak PET signals. BSC-EV-derived miR-30a-5p suppressed FAP expression and halted fibrosis progression, confirmed by reduced FAPI-PET signals post-treatment. By day 21, the decline in SUVmax reflected ECM stabilization and diminished fibrotic activity, underscoring the therapeutic effects of BSC-EVs. These findings indicate that FAPI-PET could serve as a dynamic approach for monitoring BTS progression and BSC-EV treatment response, facilitating precision intervention strategies (e.g. dosage optimization, treatment timing). Similar applications have been explored in interstitial lung diseases and cardiac fibrosis, reinforcing the clinical utility of FAP-targeted imaging and therapy [ , ].
In recent years, the anti-fibrotic properties of EVs derived from common sources such as MSCs and epithelial cells have been widely attributed to their regulatory effects on fibroblast activation or inflammation [ , , ]. In contrast, BSC-EVs possess a unique mechanism that actively drives adaptive ECM remodeling by promoting both the degradation of excessive collagen and the reorganization of collagen fibers. This process is crucial for maintaining airway wall structural stability and reducing the risk of recurrent airway narrowing. In terms of origin, BSC-EVs offer a site-specific advantage due to their parent cells residing in the airway epithelium, where EVs are typically expressed. This enhances the homing efficiency of the EVs to the damaged tracheal microenvironment and facilitates rapid integration into signaling pathways, a capability not inherently present in EVs from extra-pulmonary sources [ ]. Moreover, as a superior subpopulation of bronchial epithelial cells, BSCs provide a more stable and reproducible EV composition than those from heterogeneous epithelial [ ], ensuring greater consistency in therapeutic applications. Collectively, these mechanistic advantages provide valuable insights for developing EV-based therapies for airway fibrosis.
The relatively small size of BSC-EVs observed in this study may be attributed to both intrinsic biological characteristics and extrinsic technical factors. Stem-cell-derived vesicles are inherently smaller than those produced by differentiated cells, which enhances the efficiency of intercellular communication and the delivery of molecular cargo [ , ]. Additionally, spatial constraints imposed by adjacent ciliated and goblet cells may further select for compact vesicles capable of penetrating basement membranes [ , ]. Moreover, the technologies employed for isolation and evaluation significantly influence the size of EVs, as detailed in the MISEV2023 guidelines [ ]. Overall, our findings indicate that these BSC-EVs are relatively small, which may improve information transmission and drug delivery efficacy.
Despite these promising findings, this study has several limitations. First, we only evaluated a single administration and fixed dose of BSC-EVs, as determined by prior experience. Future studies should explore multiple BSC-EVs administrations and dose–response relationships to optimize therapeutic strategies. Second, our assessment of ECM alterations was limited to collagen deposition and fiber structure. A comprehensive analysis of additional ECM components (e.g., elastin, laminin, fibronectin, and fibulin-1) and structural properties (e.g., stiffness) is needed to better elucidate ECM changes in tissue repair. Third, while we identified miR-30a-5p as a key therapeutic cargo within BSC-EVs and investigated its anti-fibrotic effects in vitro, further studies are required to evaluate the therapeutic potential of miR-30a-5p-enriched engineered EVs in vivo to enhance clinical relevance. Additionally, other miRNAs and proteomic cargoes within BSC-EVs may exert additive therapeutic effects. Developing an integrated co-delivery system combining multiple agents could represent a superior therapeutic strategy.

## Conclusions
In summary, our study highlights the promising therapeutic potential of BSC-EVs in treating BTS, demonstrating improved airway patency and ECM remodeling in a preclinical model. Mechanistically, we identified miR-30a-5p as a key regulatory cargo within BSC-EVs targeting FAP, which is a critical factor driving fibroblast activation and excessive ECM deposition. By modulating FAP expression, miR-30a-5p plays a pivotal role in restoring ECM dynamics and preventing fibrosis progression. These findings provide strong evidence supporting BSC-EVs as a novel and effective strategy for managing BTS and offer a broader implication for the treatment of airway fibrosis.
