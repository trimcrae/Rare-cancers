LAM cells carry TSC1/TSC2 mutations, causing mTORC1 hyperactivation and uncontrolled cell growth. mTORC1 inhibitors stabilize lung function; however, sustained efficacy requires long-term administration, and some patients fail to tolerate or respond to therapy. Although the genetic basis of LAM is known, mechanisms underlying LAM pathogenesis remain elusive. We integrated single-cell RNA sequencing and single-nuclei ATAC-seq of LAM lungs to construct a gene regulatory network controlling the transcriptional program of LAM cells. We identified activation of uterine-specific HOX-PBX transcriptional programs in pulmonary LAMCORE cells as regulators of cell survival depending upon HOXD11-PBX1 dimerization. Accordingly, blockage of HOXD11-PBX1 dimerization by HXR9 suppressed LAM cell survival in vitro and in vivo. PBX1 regulated STAT1/3, increased the expression of antiapoptotic genes, and promoted LAM cell survival in vitro. The HOX-PBX gene network provides promising targets for treatment of LAM/TSC mTORC1-hyperactive cancers.

## INTRODUCTION
Lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM) is a rare metastasizing lung disease that almost exclusively affects women. Pulmonary manifestations of LAM are characterized by the proliferation of abnormal smooth muscle–like cells, leading to progressive cystic lung destruction. LAM is caused by deleterious mutations in TSC1 or TSC2 ( ), which results in the hyperactivation of the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1). This discovery led to clinical trials demonstrating that mTORC1 inhibitors (mTORi) stabilize lung function in most patients with LAM ( ). However, challenges remain in optimizing treatments for patients with LAM. Because mTORi are cytostatic, some patients are poorly responsive. Moreover, because sustained treatment is required, side effects can develop secondary to chronic drug exposure ( , ). The development of better treatments for LAM will require a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular pathogenesis of the disease.
LAM lesions are stimulated by female hormones and are thought to be metastatic, most likely originating from uterine cells ( – ). The most definitive evidence supporting a metastatic mechanism for the disease is the recurrence of LAM lesions composed of recipient cells in the donor allograft following lung transplantation ( ). A uterine source of LAM is supported by predominance in women, histopathological characteristics of LAM lesions consistent with uterine smooth muscle–like cells, a gradient of axial lymph node involvement from pelvis to thorax ( ), and multiple case reports and case series providing evidence of LAM lesions in the uterus ( , ). Recently, we provided further evidence supporting the notion of a uterine source based on single-cell transcriptomic analysis of explant lungs from patients with LAM and identified a unique population of cells (termed LAMCORE cells) that are readily distinguishable from endogenous lung cell types and share strong transcriptomic similarity with uterine cells ( ). LAMCORE cells selectively express a panel of uterine-specific transcription factors (TFs), including homeobox TFs HOXA10, HOXA11, HOXD11, PITX2, EMX2, and pre–B cell leukemia homeobox 1 (PBX1), known to play important roles in reproductive and endocrine system development and diseases ( ). The TF PBX1 forms dimers with other HOX proteins to influence transcriptional regulation of target genes ( ). Particularly relevant to the estrogen dependence of LAM, HOX/PBX dimers have been implicated in oncogenesis of steroid-responsive cancers and are of interest as potential therapeutic targets ( , ).
To explore further the implications of PBX1 induction in LAM, in the present study, we used single-cell multiomic data to construct a LAM cell–specific gene regulatory network and performed biochemical and functional validation experiments using LAM models in vitro and in vivo. We identify the molecular interplay among HOX and PBX TFs in LAM and define a role for HOX/PBX1 in regulating signal transducer and activator of transcription 1/3 (STAT1/3) signaling to promote LAM cell survival and lung colonization.

## RESULTS

## Integrative single-cell analysis of gene expression and DNA accessibility identifies the activation of uterine-selective homeobox family of TFs in the LAM lung
Using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of lung samples from patients with LAM, a unique population of cells, termed LAMCORE, was identified, which was readily distinguishable from endogenous lung cell types and shared the closest transcriptomic similarity to uterine myocytes ( ). In the present study, we generated paired measurements of single-cell chromatin accessibility and gene expression using 10x Multiome, single-nucleus assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (snATAC-seq) and existing 10x scRNA-seq ( ) to produce an integrated analysis of single-cell gene expression and chromatin accessibility in human LAM tissues. Mapping of integrated LAM single-cell data using the LungMAP CellRef ( ), 33 different cell types were identified in LAM lung, including the previously identified LAMCORE cell population ( ). Integrative analyses enabled the identification of LAM cells, cell type–specific chromatin accessibility peaks, and enriched TF binding sites or motifs in a cell type–specific manner. We found that LAMCORE cell–specific ATAC-seq peaks accessible regions were highly enriched in motifs for uterine selective homeobox family of TFs, including PBX1, PBX3, HOXA9, HOXA10, and HOXD11, Melanocyte Inducing Transcription Factor (MITF), STAT family (STAT1 and STAT3), Nuclear Receptor Subfamily 2 Group F Member 1, Nuclear Receptor Subfamily 2 Group F Member 2 (NR2F2), and TEA Domain Transcription Factor (TEAD)family (TEAD1, TEAD2, and TEAD3) ( ). HOXD11, PBX1, MITF, and TEAD2 were predicted as signature genes with enriched expression in LAMCORE cells ( , pink bar), while MYH11, ACTA2, STAT1, and STAT3 were expressed in multiple cell types, with their average expression levels increased in LAMCORE cells ( , blue bar). MITF is a known oncogenic driver for kidney angiomyolipomas (AMLs) ( ), and NR2F2 is a nuclear receptor recently identified by genome-wide association studies as a modifying genetic locus in LAM ( ). After obtaining differentially accessible peak sets of LAMCORE cells, Hypergeometric Optimization of Motif EnRichment (HOMER) was used to assign peaks to the nearest or overlapping genes, enabling the association of chromatin accessibility with gene expression. The nearest genes associated with LAMCORE cell–selective peaks were functionally enriched in muscle structure development and extracellular matrix organization ( ). Among the nearest genes, expression of uterine TFs (PBX1, PITX2, ESR1, and HAND2), genes encoding muscle and collagen proteins (ACTA2, MYH11, COL1A2, and COL4A1), and genes involved in apoptosis (CRYAB, PTGIS, and PDE3A) were increased in LAM ( ). ATAC accessibility and RNA expression patterns across all cell types were highly concordant ( ). We analyzed peak-to-gene (P2G) links in genomic regions of HOXA10 and PBX1 ( ) and identified unique accessible peaks in the promoter regions of HOXA10 and HOXA11 in LAMCORE cells, consistent with the selective expression of HOXA10 and HOXA11 RNAs in LAMCORE cells ( ). PBX1 is predicted as one of the signature genes of LAMCORE cells, its gene expression and chromatin accessible peaks were detected in other cell types, and the selectivity of PBX1 in LAMCORE cells was likely influenced by its dimerization with HOX proteins ( ). Thus, the present integrative scRNA-seq/snATAC-seq data analyses identified an increased expression and activity of uterine-specific transcriptional programming (i.e., homeobox family of TFs) in the LAMCORE cells, supporting the concept that activation of HOX-PBX signaling plays an important role in the transcription and epigenetic regulation of LAMCORE cells.
 

## Expression of HOXs, PBX1, and target genes is prominent in LAM
To validate the single-cell findings, we measured PBX1 gene expression levels in tissues from five LAM participants and three non-LAM participants ( ) using reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). Our findings show that PBX1 mRNA is increased in LAM lung tissue ( ). This confirms our analysis of publicly available PBX1 gene expression data from laser capture microdissected LAM lung cells [n = 14, GSE12027 ( )] versus lung tissue from female patients with cancer [n = 38, GSE10072 ( ) and GSE19804 ( )] ( ). Uterine LAM lesions have been identified in patients with pulmonary LAM ( ). The uterine tissue was obtained at the time of hysterectomy from a patient with sporadic LAM. Histopathology of the LAM uterus revealed multiple well-circumscribed, firm nodules with a whorled trabecular pattern bulging from the myometrial surface, consistent with leiomyomas ( ). Immunohistochemical staining showed positive immunoreactivity with Actin Alpha 2, Smooth Muscle (ACTA2). Immunofluorescence staining showed PBX1 in the nuclei of ACTA2-positive uterine leiomyoma cells ( ). Renal AMLs are frequently present in patients with pulmonary LAM. Our published scRNA-seq analysis identified three major cell types: ACTA2+ cells, immune cells, and endothelial cells ( ). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed a distinct epithelial cell characteristic of the ACTA2+ population in AML ( ). Immunofluorescence staining showed PBX1 in the nuclei of ACTA2-positive renal AML cells ( ).
 Next, we examined PBX1 protein levels in a xenograft tumor model of tuberous sclerosis complex 2 (Tsc2)–null rat uterine leiomyoma–derived ELT3 (Eker rat uterine leiomyoma-derived) cells ( , ). Both ELT3-V3 (expressing empty vector) and ELT3-T3 (expressing TSC2) cells developed subcutaneous tumors 6 weeks after inoculation. The percentage of PBX1-positive nuclei was increased in Tsc2-null ELT3-V3 tumor cells compared to Tsc2-positive ELT3-T3 cells ( ). Last, in a lung metastatic mouse model ( ), PBX1 accumulation was more prominent in estrogen-induced metastatic lung lesions, compared to placebo treatment ( ). Consistent with the single-cell multiomics findings, our data demonstrated increased PBX1 expression and PBX1 protein levels in LAM tissues and TSC2-null mouse tumor models.

## Integrative analyses of PBX1 ChIP-seq and scRNA-seq of LAM lung identify HOXs and STATs as PBX1 targets
PBX1 is a pioneer factor that opens chromatin to recruit estrogen receptor α in breast cancer cells ( ). PBX (PBX1 to PBX4) and HOX (HOX1 to HOX11) are homeobox domain TFs that form functional dimers to promote proliferation, suppress apoptosis, induce angiogenesis, and drive metastasis in different types of cancers ( ). We applied integrative analyses of breast cancer PBX1 chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) (GSE28008) and scRNA-seq of LAM lung ( ) to identify potential PBX1 targets in LAMCORE cells. Genes with positive PBX1 binding peaks and selective expression in LAMCORE cells include HOXD11, PITX2, ESR1, and NR2F2 ( ). STAT3 is predicted as a PBX1 transcriptional target. While STAT3 is expressed in both LAM and control lung cells, its overall expression (pseudo-bulk) is significantly increased in LAM versus control lungs ( ). Genes with positive PBX1 binding sites and selectively expressed in LAMCORE cells were functionally enriched in pathways related to LAM pathogenesis, including the activation of estrogen, extracellular signal–regulated kinase (ERK)/mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and mTOR signaling in the LAMCORE cells ( ), supporting the concept that PBX1 is involved in the regulation of genes and pathways leading to the development of LAM. Estrogen treatment increased the transcript levels of ESR1 and PGR, as expected, but did not affect the expression of PBX1 in 621-101 cells ( ), suggesting the lack of negative feedback regulation toward PBX1 expression in TSC2-null cells.
 

## PBX1 interacts with HOXD11 in TSC2-null cells
Dimerization of PBX/HOX family members determines tissue-specific gene activation ( ). The precise PBX/HOX dimer combinations in LAM cells are unknown. To identify LAM-specific HOX/PBX interactions, we performed IP using two independent anti-HOXD11 antibodies to detect PBX1 protein in the HOXD11 immunocomplexes from rat uterine leiomyoma–derived ELT3 cells ( ). Confocal microscopy showed that PBX1 and HOXD11 immunostaining colocalized in the nuclei of TSC2-null cells ( ). Immunofluorescence staining showed that PBX1 and HOXD11 colocalized in the nuclei of smooth muscle actin (ACTA2)–positive lung cells from a LAM participant ( ). We generated a genetic mouse model of uterine-specific Tsc2 deletion ( ) and identified uterine enlargement, cyst formation, and development of uterine tumors in PRCre;Tsc2f/f (Tsc2KO) mice at 27 weeks of age ( ), consistent with the published findings ( ). Histopathology showed the endometrial and myometrial cells in Tsc2KO mice ( ). Immunoblotting analysis demonstrated loss of Tsc2 protein and increased phosphorylation of S6 (Ser235/236), indicative of mTORC1 activation in uterine tissues from Tsc2KO mouse compared to wild-type (WT) mice ( ). Immunofluorescence staining showed that PBX1 is prominent in the nuclei of myometrial cells and HOXD11 is abundantly expressed in a perinuclear pattern in myometrial tumor cells from a PRCre;Tsc2f/f mouse model of LAM ( ). Collectively, our findings identify the interaction of PBX1 and HOXD11 in TSC2-null cells as suggested by the integrative genomic analyses.

## HOXD11 and PBX1 are active transcriptional regulatory networks in LAMCORE cells
To reveal the genomic and epigenomic regulatory circuits that determine LAM-specific cell fate, we constructed LAMCORE cell transcriptional regulatory networks (TRNs) using paired expression and chromatin accessibility (PECA), a statistical model inferring context-specific TRN from paired gene expression and chromatin accessibility data ( ). The model calculates the probability of the expression of target genes based on the accessibility of regulatory elements and expression of chromatin regulators. To ensure that the TRN is LAMCORE specific, we required that TFs are abundantly expressed in LAMCORE cells and that target genes are either LAMCORE signature genes or genes induced in LAMCORE compared to control mesenchymal cells ( ). We identified TFs hubs with the most interactions in the LAMCORE TRN (top 1% of the network) including hormone receptors PGR (progesterone receptor), androgen receptor, and homeobox TFs (HOXA10, HOXA11, HOXD11, PBX1, and PBX3) ( ). Given our finding that PBX1 binds to HOXD11 in human and rodent TSC2-null cells ( ), we next focused on a PBX1-HOXD11 centered TRN by extracting gene nodes directly connected to both PBX1 and HOXD11 in the network, including common upstream regulators, cofactors, and targets ( ). Shared PBX1-HOXD11 targets were functionally enriched for “muscle proliferation/development,” “cell fate commitment,” “wound healing,” “response to hormone,” “Wnt signaling pathway,” “apoptotic signaling pathway,” and “focal adhesion” and “phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)–Akt-mTOR signaling pathway” ( ). Our TRN analysis identified the precise regulatory circuits upstream and downstream of PBX1-HOXD11 centered TRN in LAMCORE cells and provided the in silico blueprint for functional validation and identification of the molecular and cellular mechanisms by which PBX1-HOXD11 regulates LAM pathogenesis.
 Because STAT family TFs (STAT1 and STAT3) were highly enriched motifs identified in the cell type–specific chromatin accessibility regions of LAMCORE cells ( ) and were predicted to be direct transcription targets of PBX1 ( ), we constructed PBX1/3 and STAT1/3 common networks to identify shared targets ( ). Because families of TFs largely share TF binding sites and motifs, we combined PBX1 and PBX3 as PBX family and STAT1 and STAT3 as STAT family. “Regulation of programmed cell death” was the most enriched function for PBX/STAT common target genes. Most of these genes were associated with “negative regulation of cell death” ( ). Our network analysis suggested that the PBX and STAT family of TFs work cooperatively to suppress LAM cell apoptosis and promote LAM cell survival. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated that PBX1, HOXA11, and HOXD11 proteins were present in ACTA2-positive cells in LAM lung nodules ( and ). Phosphorylated STAT1 (phospho-STAT1) and phospho-STAT3 were localized in the nuclei of cells in LAM lung nodules ( ). RT-PCR validated the increased expression of several PBX-STAT common targets involved in the negative regulation of apoptosis in LAM, including CRYAB and HAND2 ( ). Consistent with single-cell analysis and network prediction, we demonstrated increased levels of PBX1, HOXA11, HOXD11, phospho-STAT1/3 proteins and increased expression of the predicted PBX-STAT gene targets inhibiting apoptosis (CRYAB and HAND2) in LAM nodules.

## Suppression of PBX1 attenuates STAT1/3 phosphorylation in LAM-derived cells
PBX1 was depleted in 621-101 cells using lentiviral short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs). PBX1 silencing attenuated phospho–Ser727-STAT1 by 45 and 88%, and phospho–Ser727-STAT3 by 80 and 75% ( ). Protein levels of the cell cycle protein cyclin D1, a STAT3 transcriptional target, were not affected by depletion of PBX1 ( ), suggesting that PBX1 does not control cyclin D1–dependent proliferative signals in LAM-derived cells. Moreover, PBX1 silencing decreased the transcript levels of PBX1 and ESR1 in LAM-derived cells ( ). Together, our results demonstrated that increased expression of STAT1, STAT3, and estrogen receptor alpha (ESR1) in TSC2-null LAM cells is PBX1 dependent.
 

## Inhibition of STAT1 promotes the death of TSC2-null LAM-derived cells in vitro
LAM patient–derived cells were treated with Fludarabine (NSC 118218, FaraA, Fludarabinum), which depletes STAT1 mRNA and protein ( ). Fludarabine treatment also significantly increased the transcript levels of ESR1 within 24 and 48 hours in TSC2-null 621-101 cells ( ). Fludarabine significantly decreased the viability of TSC2-null 621-101 cells [Growth inhibitory dose 50% (GI50) = 56.1 μM], compared to TSC2-expressing 621-103 cells (GI50 = 852.2 μM) within 72 hours ( ). We next treated both 621-101 and 621-103 cells with increasing concentrations of Fludarabine for 72 hours. Phase-contrast microscopy showed that 55.6 to 500 μM Fludarabine treatment caused significantly more cell death in 621-101 cells, compared to 621-103 cells ( ). 621-101 cells treated with Fludarabine exhibited decreased crystal violet staining compared to 621-103 cells ( ). Fludarabine treatment for 48 hours reduced phospho-STAT1 levels, compared to vehicle control, as well as decreased protein levels of PBX1 ( ). Marked poly(adenosine 5′-diphosphate–ribose) polymerase (PARP) cleavage was found in Fludarabine-treated TSC2-null 621-101 cells ( , and ), indicating apoptosis of TSC2-null cells. Collectively, our network analysis and functional studies demonstrate that PBX1 and STAT family of TFs work cooperatively to suppress LAM cell apoptosis and promote LAM cell survival, an effect reversed by the inhibition of STAT signaling.
 

## Suppression of STAT1 does not affect lung colonization of Tsc2-null rat uterine leiomyoma–derived cells in vivo
To assess the potential therapeutic effect of inhibiting STAT1 activation in vivo, Tsc2-null ERL4 cells were treated with Fludarabine for 16 hours. Fludarabine (100 μM) did not alter luciferase activity in Tsc2-null ERL4 cells compared to vehicle control ( ). Immunoblotting analysis showed that Fludarabine treatment reduced phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT3 ( ), indicating suppression of STAT1 in vitro. Next, we tested the effect of Fludarabine on lung colonization of ERL4 cells. Female NOD scid gamma (NSG) mice were injected with ERL4 cells. While bioluminescence intensities at the chest regions were similar in all mice within 1 hour (baseline), bioluminescence intensities were moderately increased 6 hours after cell inoculation in all mice. Fludarabine treatment with 72 hours did not affect lung colonization of ERL4 cells ( ). Collectively, these data demonstrate that suppression of STAT1 activation is not sufficient to inhibit the survival and/or lung colonization of Tsc2-null ERL4 cells in vivo.
 

## Inhibition of HOX-PBX1 dimerization promotes the death of TSC2-null cells
To assess the potential therapeutic effect of inhibiting HOX-PBX1 dimerization in LAM, TSC2-null 621-101 and ERL4 cells were treated with increasing concentrations of HXR9 (Bio-Synthesis Inc., Lewisville, TX, USA), a cell-permeable synthetic peptide which inhibits HOX-PBX interaction ( ). HXR9 (50 μM) killed TSC2-null 621-101 cells ( ), indicating the cytotoxic effect of blocking HOX-PBX1 dimerization. HXR9 treatment increased the levels of cleaved caspase-3 and phosphorylation of receptor-interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1) in TSC2-null 621-101 cells but not in TSC2-reexpressing 621-103 cells ( ), indicating enhanced apoptosis and necroptosis. Next, we tested the effect of HXR9 on lung colonization of ERL4 cells. Pretreatment with HXR9 suppressed lung colonization 24 and 48 hours after cell inoculation ( ). Female C.B-Igh-1b/IcrTac-Prkdcscid (SCID) mice were injected with luciferase-expressing LAM-derived 621-101–luciferase (621L9) cells transfected with two independent PBX1-shRNAs ( ). While bioluminescence intensities at the chest regions were similar in mice inoculated with pLKO.1 or PBX1-shRNA cells within 1 hour (baseline), bioluminescence intensities were significantly reduced in mice inoculated with PBX1-shRNA cells 24 and 48 hours after inoculation ( ). Collectively, these data demonstrate that the PBX1-HOX interaction regulates the survival and lung colonization of TSC2-null LAM patient–derived cells in vivo.
 

## DISCUSSION
While the recurrence of LAM following lung transplantation originating from recipient cells supports the concept that LAM is a low-grade metastatic neoplasm ( ), the source of LAM metastasis remains elusive. Our previous scRNA-seq analysis of LAM lesions identified a unique population of cells termed LAMCORE cells that were readily distinguishable from endogenous lung cells and shared the closest transcriptomic similarity with uterine myocytes. LAMCORE cells in the lung and uterus share the same biallelic TSC2 mutations and are morphologically indistinguishable, suggesting that the uterus is a likely source of origin of LAMCORE cells ( ). The present integrative analysis of scRNA-seq and ATAC-seq data of LAM lung identified enrichment of HOX, PBX, and STAT family TF binding sites in the open chromatin of LAMCORE cells as well as the increased expression of the corresponding TFs in LAMCORE cells, indicating activity of uterine-specific HOX-PBX transcriptional programming in pulmonary LAMCORE cells. We constructed a TRN using paired scRNA-seq and snATAC-seq data and showed that HOX and PBX family of TFs serve as important regulatory hubs in LAMCORE cells, regulating the STAT1/3 signaling cascade to promote LAM cell survival. Integrative analysis of PBX1 ChIP-seq and LAM scRNA-seq predicted functional PBX1 targets in LAMCORE cells that were functionally enriched in the activation of prosurvival mTOR, estrogen, and ERK/MAPK signaling. We showed that PBX1 regulated LAMCORE cell behavior and metastatic potential of TSC2-deficient cells, mimicking findings in human LAM. We have previously demonstrated that estrogen augments survival and lung metastasis of Tsc2-null ERL4 cells in SCID mice ( ). Here, we predicted ESR1 as a PBX1 target that was induced in LAMCORE cells.
Consistent with our integrative genomic analysis predictions regarding the role of a HOX/PBX dependent network in LAM, we detected the accumulation of HOXA11, HOXD11, PBX1, phospho-STAT1 and phospho-STAT3 in the nucleus of LAM cells. These findings provide evidence that HOXD11 and PBX1 interact in TSC2-null LAM models and establish a functional link between PBX1 and STAT1/3 activation. Phosphorylation of STAT1 at Ser727 is required for transcriptional regulation of STAT1 target genes, including cell cycle regulators ( ). PI3K and mTOR pathways regulate STAT1-mediated transcription of proapoptotic genes in a rapamycin-insensitive manner ( ). Increased expression and phosphorylation of STAT1/3 were demonstrated in cells lacking TSC1 or TSC2 and in mTORC1 hyperactive mouse embryonic fibroblasts in vitro and in vivo ( ). Increased expression, phosphorylation, and nuclear localization of STAT3 were previously shown in LAM-derived cells in culture, in xenograft tumors of Tsc2-null cells, and in smooth muscle–like cells in LAM explants ( ). Previous findings showed increased STAT1/3 signaling in TSC2-null cells, TSC-associated and sporadic LAM lung lesions, and renal AMLs ( – ). In the present study, we found that phospho-STAT1 (Ser727) was primarily localized in the nucleus of ACTA2-positive LAM cells but not in neighboring lung cells. Phospho-STAT3 (Ser727) was less prominent in the nuclei of LAM cells compared to phospho-STAT1 ( ).
LAM is a life-threatening disease in urgent need of a cure. Development of previously unidentified drugs to treat any disease is a complex, expensive, and laborious process. Because of this fact, rare diseases like LAM rarely garner the interest of pharmaceutical industries, thereby reducing the chances of developing a cure. Hence, repurposing existing drugs with similar signaling targets or pathways has been beneficial for rare diseases ( ). This approach shortens the extensive timeline required for new compounds development, testing, and approval. In addition, most approved drugs already have established safety and toxicology profiles, resulting in more focused and more streamlined preclinical and clinical trials. Patients with LAM have benefited from repurposed drug, as Sirolimus (rapamycin) is currently the optimal medication for managing LAM. Rapamycin, which was originally developed for kidney transplant patients ( ), antirestenosis ( ), and later as an antineoplastic drug for mTOR-associated cancers, improved lung function in patients with LAM ( ) and currently is the only U.S. Food and Drug Administration–approved drug for LAM.
Fludarabine is a STAT1 inhibitor that is currently approved in the United States and European Union as an antineoplastic agent for B cell chronic lymphocytic leukemia ( ). We demonstrated that Fludarabine has potential as a previously unidentified repurposed therapeutic for the treatment of LAM. Fludarabine is a nucleotide analog that prevents DNA synthesis through inhibition of DNA polymerase, causing cell death ( ). In the present study, we found that Fludarabine treatment reduced STAT1 phosphorylation and induced the death of LAM patient–derived cells in vitro. This is consistent with previous findings from Goncharova et al. ( ) who showed that molecular and pharmacologic blockade of STAT3 reduced cell proliferation and induced apoptosis in TSC2-null LAM patient–derived cells and rat uterine leiomyoma–derived (ELT3) cells. Fludarabine treatment [40 mg kg−1 day−1 intraperitoneally (ip)] with 72 hours did not affect lung colonization of Tsc2-null ERL4 cells ( ), indicating that STAT1 inhibition using Fludarabine at the current concentration and duration is not sufficient to attenuate the survival and/or lung colonization of Tsc2-null ERL4 cells in vivo. Collectively, our data, together with studies by others ( ), indicate that STAT1 and STAT3 activation is an important cell survival mediator in LAM pathogenesis.
HOX and PBX genes are potent pro-oncogenic TFs that promote prostate, breast, renal, ovarian, and lung cancer as well as melanoma, myeloma, and acute myeloid leukemia ( , ). However, the high level of functional redundancy among HOX and PBX family members has prevented the targeting of these proteins individually. As an alternative, targeting the interaction between HOX and PBX proteins is more feasible, since it is mediated by a highly conserved hexapeptide sequence in HOX proteins and a hydrophobic binding pocket within PBX ( ). HXR9 is a cell-permeable peptide acting as a competitive antagonist of HOX/PBX binding. HXR9 has been reported to reduce tumor cell survival and shrink tumors in melanoma, ovarian cancer, breast cancer, non–small cell lung cancer, prostate cancer, and mesothelioma cell lines ( – ). Here, we showed nuclear colocalization of HOXD11/PBX1 in LAM lung and demonstrated that HXR9 treatment induces TSC2-null cell death as compared to TSC2-expressing cells. We also show that HXR9, but not Fludarabine, attenuates lung colonization of Tsc2-null ERL4 cells in our intravenous mouse model. Together, our data indicate that HOX/PBX interaction is critical for LAM cell survival and lung colonization, thus highlighting the potential therapeutic benefit of targeting HOX/PBX dimerization in LAM.
There are several limitations in the present study. Because of the rarity of LAM disease and limited availability of fresh tissue samples, we combined available single-cell data from patients of different racial background and age (before and after menstrual cycle), and different 10x Genomics platforms (scRNA-seq, snATAC-seq, and 10x Multiome). Therefore, our integrative analysis results may be influenced by these effects, although we applied multiple algorithms including mutual nearest neighbor matching, reciprocal principal components analysis in Seurat, and Harmony to remove/minimize batch effects ( – ). To address these concerns, we performed functional validation to test our single-cell predictions. We also recognize that the cells used for functional studies were not LAM-lung derived because of the rarity and survival disadvantage of LAM lung cells in culture conditions despite efforts from multiple groups to establish LAM cell lines. A recent study by Johnson Lab revealed that LAM lung stromal components including LAM-associated fibroblasts (LAFs) contribute to LAM progression in vitro and in vivo ( ). LAFs secrete cytokines that promote the growth of LAM lung cells in culture and in three-dimensional microtissues ( ). In our in vitro and in vivo studies, we used immortalized LAM patient–derived cells to demonstrate the prosurvival roles of HOXD11-PBX1 predicted by integrative single-cell genomic analyses of LAM lung cells. We postulate that the immortalization process produces factor(s) essential for LAM cells growth or reprograms LAM cells to gain growth advantage. Nonetheless, our in vivo studies, in combination with in vitro data, strongly support the single-cell omics prediction that the HOX-PBX gene network promotes survival of mTOR hyperactive cells in LAM.
In the present study, we predicted and validated that HOXD11-PBX1 dimers are present in LAM models. HXR9, or the molecular depletion of PBX1, induced death of LAM patient–derived cells in vitro and in vivo and decreased lung colonization of TSC2-null cells in vivo. Inhibition of STAT signaling by Fludarabine recapitulated HXR9 effects, demonstrating that STAT signaling is a key downstream regulator of HOXD11-PBX1–mediated LAM cell survival and lung metastasis. By identifying a previously unidentified HOXD11-PBX1-STAT regulatory circuit that inhibits apoptosis and promotes survival of LAM cells, we provide a proof of concept for the potential therapeutic benefit of targeting HOX-PBX dimerization and STAT signaling in LAM- and TSC-related diseases.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Human participants and tissue collection
Freshly resected lung, uterine, and renal AML tissues were obtained from the National Disease Research Interchange with the assistance of The LAM Foundation. Publicly available scRNA-seq data from six female donor lungs were downloaded from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) ( ) and included as a normal control for integrative analyses. Publicly available snATAC-seq data from two female donor lungs (GSE161381) were downloaded and included as normal controls. 10x Multiome snATAC + gene expression (GEX) sequencing of LAM human lungs was performed by Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center Gene Expression Core. All steps were performed according to the 10x Multiome sequencing workflow and protocol.

## Analysis of 10x Multiome snATAC + GEX data
RNA and ATAC reads from 10x Multiome single-nucleus RNA (snRNA) and ATAC sequencing of the LAM lungs (n = 2) were demultiplexed and processed using the Cell Ranger Analysis, Reconstruction, and Clustering (ARC) software package v 2.0.0 (10x Genomics) and human reference hg38. Nuclei that passed Cell Ranger ARC filters were used as input to Signac ( ) for the following RNA and ATAC quality controls (QCs), including RNA data that have 500 to 10,000 genes and less than 10% mitochondrial reads as well as ( ) ATAC data that have 1000 to 100,000 unique molecular identifiers, less than two nucleosome signal values, greater than two transcription start site enrichment, and less 5% reads in The Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE) blacklist ratio. In addition, Scrublet ( ) was applied to the RNA data to identify and remove potential doublets. SoupX (v1.5.2) ( ) was applied to each multiome sample to remove technical ambient RNA counts from the RNA data. Cell types were identified using Seurat’s label transfer algorithm using the snRNA-seq data as reference. Cell type predictions were validated using expression of cell type marker genes.

## snATAC-seq analysis
snATAC-seq of LAM lung was processed using Cell Ranger ATAC with GRCh38 reference (10x Genomics). ATAC cells passing the following QC criteria were included in downstream analysis using Signac ( ), including: (i) at least 1000 but less than 20,000 fragments in peaks, (ii) greater than 10% of fragments in peaks, (iii) less than 5% of fragments in ENCODE blacklist regions for GRCh38 reference, (iv) ratio of mononucleosomal to nucleosome-free fragments of less than 2, and (v) transcription start site enrichment score greater than 2. The QC criteria were selected on the basis of inspection of distributions of each QC metric. Unbiased cell clustering based on ATAC peaks was performed using the Leiden algorithm. Cell clusters were assigned to cell types using label transfer method in Signac using our analyzed scRNA-seq of LAM lung. Cell type–specific differentially accessible peaks and overrepresented motifs were identified using the FindMarkers function in Seurat using the “logistic regression” method. Signac ClosestFeature function was used to find closest genes to ATAC peak regions. HOMER was used to annotate peak regions with nearest genes under default parameters. Motif enrichment was performed with HOMER using the hg38 assembly.

## Bioinformatic analysis of scRNA-seq, snATAC-seq, and 10x Multiome data
We performed integrative scRNA-seq, snATAC-seq), and 10x Multiome data analysis of human lung with pulmonary LAM. To improve data integration, we reprocessed all data using the GRCh38 reference. In total, we combined sc/snRNA-seq (n = 4) and snATAC-seq (n = 3) for downstream analysis integration. Seurat 3 ( ) and Monocle 3 ( ) were used for data analysis and integration. Data were normalized using SCTransform function in Seurat after filtering out the effects of cell cycle and mitochondrial percentage. The mutual nearest neighbor matching based single-cell batch correction method ( ) implemented in Monocle 3 was used to integrate data from different samples. Unbiased cell clustering was performed using the Leiden algorithm. Cell clusters were mapped to cell types based on expression of known cell type markers and signature genes from our previous LAM and normal lung single-cell studies ( , , ).

## Integrative analysis of PBX1 ChIP-seq and LAM scRNA-seq
PBX1 ChIP-seq was downloaded from GEO browser (accession number: GSE28008). The reads were aligned using the ELAND software. The Model-based Analysis of ChIP-Seq peak-calling algorithm was used to call significantly enriched peaks using default settings (P < 10−5) and specifying the peak size  =  200 base pairs (bps). De novo motif discovery analysis on PBX1 islands was performed using MEME-ChIP ( ). Identification of islands containing all TFs was performed using the HOMER mergePeaks function. To identify the potential PBX1 targets in LAMCORE cells, we performed overlap analysis of target genes with positive PBX1 binding sites and genes differentially expressed in LAMCORE cells by scRNA-seq analysis. Genes with positive PBX1 binding peaks and selectively expressed in LAMCORE cells were subject to functional enrichment analysis using ToppGene Suite ( ). Functional enrichment results were visualized using R (version 4.1.0) and the ggplot2 (version 3.3.5) library.

## TRN analysis
TRN analysis was performed using paired RNA-seq (n = 4) and ATAC-seq (n = 3) of the LAMCORE cells. Gene regulatory network inference was performed using the PECA 2.0 (version 3.1) statistical model ( ). The edge regulation score cutoff was 99th percentile for the network display and 90th percentile for the functional enrichment analysis. Networks were visualized using the Cytoscape (version 3.7.2) software package ( ).

## Cell culture and reagents
ELT3 cells ( ) were provided by C. Walker, Institute of Biosciences and Technology Texas A&M University, Houston, TX. ELT3 cells stably expressing luciferase (ERL4) and LAM patient–associated AML-derived (621-101) cells were provided by E. P. Henske, Brigham and Women’s Hospital, Harvard Medical School, Boston, MA ( , ). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin–amphotericin B.

## Cell viability assay
Cell viability was estimated using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. TSC2-null 621-101 and TSC2-addback 621-103 cells were plated in 96-well plates (2 × 103 cells per well). Cells were incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 atmosphere for 24 hours before treatment with escalating concentrations of Fludarabine (0 to 500 μM; no. 118218, Selleck Chemicals LLC, TX, USA) in a final volume of 100 μl for 72 hours. Twenty-five microliters of MTT solution (2.5 mg/ml growth media) was added to each well, followed by 4-hour incubation of plates at 37°C. Formation of formazan crystals was observed under the microscope and solubilized with the addition of 125 μl of 0.04 N HCl in isopropanol, followed by 90 min of incubation at 37°C. Cell viability was determined by measuring absorbance at 560 nm with a reference of 650 nm. Growth inhibitory power was analyzed using CompuSyn software.
Alternatively, cell viability was assessed using crystal violet staining. TSC2-null 621-101 and TSC2-addback 621-103 cells were plated in 24-well plates (n = 3 per treatment group). Cells were incubated in 37°C CO2 incubator for 24 hours before treatment with escalating concentrations of Fludarabine (0 to 500 μM) for 72 hours or HXR9 (0 to 100 μM; Bio-Synthesis Inc., Lewisville, TX, USA) for 5 hours at 37°C. After treatment with inhibitors, cells were washed with PBS and stained with crystal violet stain [0.1% w/v crystal violet, 50% v/v methanol, and 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)] at room temperature for 15 min. Stain was removed and washed, and plates were allowed to air dry in the dark. Crystal violet stain was dissolved with 100% methanol for 15 min, and absorbance (optical density) was measured at 590 nm using a microplate reader.

## Expression array analysis
Reanalysis of previously published expression array data [GEO accession numbers GSE10072 ( ), GSE19804 ( ), and GSE12027 ( )] was performed using the online tool GEO2R. Transcript levels of PBX1 were compared among LAM cells collected by laser capture microdissection from LAM nodules ( ) and non-LAM lungs ( ), including lung cancer/tumor.

## Immunoprecipitation
Cells were lysed and the nuclear fraction was isolated using Subcellular Protein Fractionation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, no. 78840). Nuclear extracts were incubated with two independent HOXD11 antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, no. sc-81969 and Sigma-Aldrich, no. SAB1403944) at 4°C overnight with rotation. Normal mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, no. sc-2025) was used as a negative control. Fifty microliters of protein A agarose bead slurry (Invitrogen) were added to the IP complexes and incubated at 4°C for 2 hours. The beads were washed twice with in lysis buffer, pelleted, eluted in 2× SDS Laemmli buffer, and separated by electrophoresis. Protein levels of PBX1 were assessed by immunoblotting in HOXD11-IP complexes, protein lysates, and nuclear fractions of ELT3 cells.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Sections were deparaffinized and incubated with primary antibody [1:100 in PBS + 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA)] and the secondary antibodies (1:1000; Invitrogen, no. A-21202 and no. A10042). Images were captured with fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX60).

## Confocal microscopy
LAM-derived 621-101 cells were plated overnight on glass coverslips in 12-well tissue culture plates. Cells were serum-starved overnight and then treated with 20 nM rapamycin for 24 hours. Cells were rinsed with PBS twice, fixed with warm 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, blocked in 3% BSA/PBS for 1 hour, and then incubated with primary antibody in 1% BSA in PBS for 1 hour, followed by secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Images were captured with a FluoView FV-10i Olympus Laser Point Scanning Confocal Microscope.

## Quantitative RT-PCR
RNA from cultured cells was isolated using RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN). Gene expression was quantified using One-Step qRT-PCR Kits (Invitrogen) in the Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System and normalized to human β-actin (ACTB). Primers used include: PBX1, HOXD11, HOXA11, HAND2, CRYAB, ACTB, ESR1, and PGR ( ).
 

## Phase-contrast microscopy
TSC2-null 621-101 and TSC2-addback 621-103 cells were seeded into 24-well plates 24 hours before treatment with increasing concentrations of Fludarabine or HXR9. Cell morphology images were captured using phase-contrast microscopy.

## Immunohistochemistry
Immunohistochemistry was performed on paraffin-embedded 10-μm sections using antibodies against target proteins, followed by secondary antibodies for detection. Slides were deparaffinized, and antigen retrieval was performed using sodium citrate retrieval solution pH 6. The immunoperoxidase technique was used with 3,3′-Diaminobenzidine substrate (Invitrogen) and Gill’s hematoxylin counterstain.

## Immunoblotting
Protein lysis of TSC2-null 621-101 cells treated with Fludarabine was done using mammalian protein extraction reagent buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, no. 78501) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Lysates were resolved on 4 to 20% Mini-PROTEAN TGX Precast gels (Bio-Rad, no. 4561094) and transferred using a wet electrophoretic transfer unit (Bio-Rad) onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 1 hour of blocking with 5% nonfat dry milk, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. Primary antibodies include: phospho-STAT1 (no. 8826), STAT1 (no. 14994), phospho-STAT3 (no. 9145), STAT3 (no. 30835), PBX1 (no. 4342), cleaved caspase-3 (no. 9661), PARP (no. 9532), cyclin D1 (no. 55506), phospho-RIPK1 (no. 44590), and β-actin (no. 3700) from Cell Signaling Technology. Secondary antibody incubation was done using horseradish peroxidase–linked antibodies for 2 hours. Signal was detected with SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, no. PI34580).

## shRNA down-regulation
293T packaging cells were transfected with PBX1 shRNA or nontargeting shRNA vector pLKO.1 using Mirus TransIT-TKO Transfection reagent (Mirus). 621-101 cells were transduced with lentiviruses for 48 hours and then selected with puromycin. Stable clones were harvested for experiments.

## Animal studies
Female SCID (Taconic Biosciences, Germantown, NY) or NSG (the Jackson Laboratory) mice at 6 to 8 weeks of age were injected with 2 × 105 cells intravenously, as previously described ( ). Animal health was monitored daily during the tumor experiments. Mice inoculated with Tsc2-null cells were randomized and treated with HXR9 [2 mg kg−1 day−1 intravenously (iv)], Fludarabine (40 mg kg−1 day−1 ip), or vehicle for 2 days before cell inoculation and 1 day after cell injection. Uterine-specific Tsc2 knockout mice were generated by crossing mice containing a floxed Tsc2 gene with mice expressing Cre recombinase driven by the PR promoter, and ear notch DNA was isolated for genotyping using appropriate primers. Animals were euthanized at 27 weeks of age. Uteri were removed, followed by gross dissection and fixation with 10% formalin. For uterine histological analysis, sections from paraffin-embedded tissue were stained with either H&E or immunohistochemistry. All mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide (CO2) inhalation via compressed gas after the last bioluminescence image was taken.

## Bioluminescent reporter imaging
Ten minutes before imaging, mice were given d-luciferin (120 mg/kg ip; PerkinElmer Inc., no. 122799). Bioluminescent signals were recorded using the Xenogen IVIS Spectrum System. Total photon flux of chest regions was analyzed as previously described ( ).

## Statistical analyses
Statistical analysis and graphing were performed with Prism 9 (version 9.4.1, GraphPad Software, LLC). Data are presented as means ± SD, unless otherwise specified. Statistical analyses were performed using two-tailed Student’s t test when comparing two groups for in vitro and in vivo studies and analysis of variance (ANOVA) test (Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test when comparing multiple groups with control group and Tukey’s multiple comparisons test when making multiple pairwise comparisons between different groups) for multiple group comparisons. P value less than 0.05 was considered significant.
