## Summary
Adult-onset Still’s disease (AOSD) is characterized by an overwhelming inflammatory response and hyperactivation of monocytes/macrophages, which may cause macrophage activation syndrome (MAS). Here, we demonstrate the forkhead box protein O3 (FOXO3), a transcriptional factor downregulated by interferon, as an important regulator of inflammation in AOSD-MAS. FOXO3 expression is downregulated in monocytes/PBMCs from patients with AOSD, especially in those developing MAS. A negative correlation between FOXO3 expression with disease activity and inflammatory level is identified. FOXO3 downregulation can be induced by TLR9 activation both in the murine MAS model and TLR9 agonist-stimulated macrophages in vitro, through transcriptional regulation and phosphorylation by AKT. Depletion of Foxo3 protected mice from hyperinflammatory response and organ damage in MAS, and mechanistically, alleviated NLRP3 inflammasome activation in macrophages. Our study reveals mechanisms of FOXO3 in facilitating AOSD-MAS development and identifies the critical role of FOXO3 in the self-negative regulation of inflammation in AOSD through suppression by TLR9 signaling.

## Introduction
Adult-onset Still’s disease (AOSD) is a systemic autoinflammatory disorder typically characterized by spiking fever, evanescent skin rash, polyarthralgia, leukocytosis, and hyperferritinemia. The pathogenesis of AOSD mainly includes the activation of innate immune cells, especially neutrophils and the monocytes/macrophages lineage. One of the major complications of AOSD is macrophage activation syndrome (MAS), a situation referring to secondary haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) that emerged in rheumatic diseases such as AOSD and systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA). As a life-threatening cytokine storm syndrome featured by continuous fever, pancytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, hyperferritinemia, and hemophagocytosis, MAS can lead to multiorgan dysfunction and even death. Early diagnosis and aggressive treatment are needed to prevent high mortality. 
Though an overwhelming inflammatory response caused by the hyperactivation of monocytes/macrophages has been implicated in AOSD-MAS, how the inflammation was self-regulated to prevent excessive inflammatory damage was still undetermined. In this case, there is an urgent need to explore mechanisms of how the inflammatory response cascade is precisely regulated during MAS development. Our and others’ studies have shed insight into the transcriptomic landscape of monocytes/PBMCs in MAS developed from AOSD or sJIA, revealing an essential involvement of type I interferon (IFN-I) signaling. , , Therefore, it is intriguing to explore the downstream regulation of IFN-I in the pathogenesis of AOSD-MAS.
One of the immunoregulatory molecules known to be downregulated by IFN-I is the forkhead box protein O3 (FOXO3), a well-known member of the family of FOXO transcription factors, playing an evolutionarily conserved role in senescence, apoptosis, oxidative stress detoxification, and so on. , Except for its classical function in anti-aging and anti-oxidant, accumulating evidence has indicated its role in immunoregulation, and its effect in various inflammatory diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), rheumatic arthritis (RA), ankylosing spondylitis (AS), and inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) has been investigated. , , , Initially, FOXO3 was suggested to be an anti-inflammatory factor. For example, FOXO3 can inhibit NF-κB activation and regulate helper T cell activation and tolerance, and its deficiency was reported to lead to lymphoproliferation with multi-organ inflammation. FOXO3 was also reported to control T cell expansion after viral infection by modulating dendritic cell function. FOXO3 was also identified as a negative regulator of a subset of antiviral genes, such as interferon regulatory factors. In SLE, FOXO3 was significantly downregulated and was critical for the glucocorticoid-mediated inhibition of NF-κB activity. Downregulated FOXO3 expression was also confirmed in AS, and it was revealed to alleviate the inflammation and oxidative stress. Besides, in RA and IBD, FOXO3 is also linked to inflammation on a genetic background. A human FOXO3 noncoding single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP, rs12212067: T>G), which increases its transcription, was found to be associated with milder Crohn’s disease (CD) and RA. , However, there were also considerable findings suggesting a pro-inflammatory role of FOXO3. For instance, it was reported that inflammatory arthritis required FOXO3 to prevent neutrophil apoptosis, and FOXO3 deficient mice are resistant to models of inflammatory arthritis and peritonitis. FOXO3 was also involved in neutrophil/T cell survival in RA, with its surprising overexpression in blood and synovial tissue. Moreover, FOXO3 was able to transcriptionally activate CXCL16 and accelerated cardiac inflammation and dysfunction in myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury. It was even reported to activate cGAS/STING and IFN-I response in neural stem/progenitor cells. Considering the roles of FOXO3 in immunoregulation and inflammatory diseases mentioned above, we are curious whether FOXO3 plays a regulatory role in the process of AOSD or AOSD-MAS development, which is obviously an autoinflammatory situation with immunoregulatory dysfunction.
In the current study, we were interested in the role of FOXO3 in the regulation of inflammation during AOSD-MAS. Therefore, we first investigated the correlation of FOXO3 mRNA and protein expression with AOSD disease activity and inflammatory cytokine levels, especially with AOSD-MAS. As toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) treatment could lead to hemophagocytes differentiation and MAS-like disease in mice, , FOXO3 expression after TLR9 stimulation was detected both in vitro and in vivo. Combined with murine models, we further explored the effect of FOXO3 on inflammatory regulation during MAS development. In our previous and others’ work, NLRP3 inflammasome activation was also indicated as a driver of AOSD inflammation. , Therefore, we finally analyzed the influence of FOXO3 on NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

## Results

## Downregulated forkhead box protein O3 assesses adult-onset Still’s disease activity and severity
To identify the alteration of transcriptional profiles in monocytes from patients with AOSD, we have performed RNA-seq of monocytes from 10 HCs to 12 patients with AOSD. A total of 667 upregulated and 426 downregulated DEGs were identified in AOSD compared to the HC group ( A). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed an obvious enrichment of IFN-I related pathways, which was consistent with our previous findings ( B). To confirm whether FOXO3 was negatively regulated by IFN-I in monocytes from patients with AOSD, we did the gene set variation analysis (GSVA) and correlation analysis, and found a significant negative correlation between IFN-I production pathway and the FOXO3 fragments per kilobase million (FKPM) level ( C, r = −0.5426, p = 0.0091). Besides, a downregulated FOXO3 FKPM expression in AOSD monocytes was shown ( D, p = 0.0071). A validation analysis by Q-PCR also confirmed decreased mRNA expression levels of FOXO3 in AOSD monocytes ( E, p = 0.0011). 
We then compared FOXO3 mRNA levels in PBMCs from patients with AOSD (n = 67) and HCs (n = 57). The clinical characteristics of these subjects were detailed in . The result showed a significantly decreased level of FOXO3 mRNA expression in PBMCs from patients with AOSD ( F, p < 0.0001). We next investigated whether FOXO3 level could assess disease activity of patients with AOSD. We first divided patients with AOSD into active (n = 42) and inactive subgroups (n = 25), and detected a markedly lower FOXO3 mRNA level in PBMCs from patients with active AOSD than inactive patients ( G, p = 0.0009). We also compared the FOXO3 mRNA level before and after treatment in 6 patients with AOSD, and found FOXO3 expression recovered during this serial follow-up ( H, p = 0.313). 
We also studied the correlation between FOXO3 expression level and routine clinical parameters, Pouchot systemic disease severity score, laboratory inflammatory markers, and various cytokine levels. The results showed the FOXO3 mRNA level was strongly and negatively correlated with Pouchot score (r = −0.3298, p = 0.0096), CRP (r = −0.3564, p = 0.0095), ferritin (r = −0.321, p = 0.0169), and AST level (r = −0.3205, p = 0.0264) ( I, upper panel). It was also negatively correlated with cytokine levels including IL-10 (r = −0.4008, p = 0.0282), TNF-α (r = −0.5394, p = 0.0001), IL-18 (r = −0.5057, p = 0.0003), and the reported inhibitor of FOXO3, IFN-α (r = −0.4165, p = 0.0143) ( I, lower panel). On the other hand, the Western blot of FOXO3 in PBMCs from 16 patients with AOSD and 16 HCs also validated the reduction ( J, p < 0.0001). Thus, we speculated that FOXO3 was downregulated during the overwhelming inflammatory condition in AOSD and could be a biomarker to assess disease activity and severity.

## Forkhead box protein O3 depletion in adult-onset Still’s disease-macrophage activation syndrome
We then further explored the association between FOXO3 and MAS development in patients with AOSD. Among 42 patients with active AOSD, 12 could be diagnosed as AOSD-MAS according to the 2016 systemic JIA-MAS criteria. The FOXO3 mRNA level was significantly reduced in AOSD-MAS compared to those without MAS ( A, p = 0.0004). Moreover, HScore, a metric for estimating secondary hemophagocytic syndrome, was also negatively correlated with FOXO3 expression ( B, r = −0.3315, p = 0.0091), indicating a critical role of FOXO3 in the development of MAS in patients with AOSD. 
We then compared FOXO3 expression between AOSD-MAS (n = 9) and AOSD-nMAS (n = 3) groups in monocyte RNA-seq ( C). As FOXO3 is a transcription factor, we assessed whether its transcriptional activity was also decreased. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) identified a tendency of downregulation of the FOXO3 activating targets pathway (BAKKER_FOXO3_TARGETS_UP, NES = −0.94, p = 0.5504) as well as an upregulation of the FOXO3 inhibiting targets pathway (BAKKER_FOXO3_TARGETS_DN, NES = 1.18, p = 0.1893) in the AOSD-MAS group compared to the AOSD-nMAS group ( D and 2E). These results indicated that FOXO3 was downregulated in monocytes during the development of MAS.

## Downregulation of forkhead box protein O3 by toll-like receptor 9 activation through transcriptional regulation and phosphorylation by AKT
To further investigate how FOXO3 was downregulated in MAS development, we administered murine CpG Class B ODN 1826, a TLR9 agonist, intraperitoneally to wild-type mice to induce a MAS model in vivo as previously described. We first confirmed an elevated expression of IFN-I inducible genes (ISGs) in mice with MAS ( A). Notably, the protein levels of FOXO3 in the livers and BMDMs of mice with MAS were decreased (rearranged B upper panel; B lower panel, p = 0.0255). PCR also confirmed the downregulated mRNA level of FOXO3 in livers from CpG-treated mice ( C, p = 0.0284). We then detected the effect of TLR9 signaling on FOXO3 protein and mRNA expression in vitro. Firstly, TLR9 stimulation by human CpG Class B ODN 2006 with concentrations from 1 μM to 10 μM for 6 h led to reduced FOXO3 expression in THP1 derived macrophages ( D). We also carried out a time-course TLR9 stimulation within 6 h with 1 μM CpG ODN 2006 on THP1 derived macrophages and found that FOXO3 protein level was sustained repressed ( E). While at the transcriptional level, FOXO3 mRNA level as well as its promoter activity were analyzed by the promoter-luciferase reporter system, both significantly decreased after TLR9 activation ( F and 3G). Besides, after validating an upregulation of the TLR9 pathway in monocytes of the AOSD-MAS group compared to the AOSD-nMAS group with GSEA ( A and S1B), we discovered a strong negative correlation between FOXO3 expression and the TLR9 signaling pathway through GSVA analysis ( C, r−0.5031, p = 0.017). Taken together, these results indicated that TLR9 activation led to FOXO3 downregulation, both at the protein and transcriptional levels. 
Next, we investigated the mechanism of the effect of TLR9 signaling on regulating FOXO3. Firstly, FOXO3 phosphorylation and activation of TLR9 downstream pathways are detected. We found FOXO3 downregulation happens along with its increasing phosphorylation level, after a rapid burst of TLR9-downstream inflammatory pathways activation of IRF7, mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) (ERK, JNK, and p38), and the serine-threonine kinase AKT pathways ( E). Notably, the downregulation of FOXO3 following CpG stimulation recovers after AKT inhibition by MK-2206, but not MAPK inhibition by SP600125, SCH772984, and SB203580, indicating AKT activation is crucial in CpG-induced FOXO3 downregulation ( H and ).
Next, we investigated the mechanism of the effect of TLR9 signaling on regulating FOXO3. As for transcriptional regulation, we tried to find transcription factors that may be involved in regulating FOXO3 transcription by TLR9 activation. We first screened potential transcription factors that may regulate FOXO3 expression through various human transcription factor-related databases, including ChIP-X Enrichment Analysis (ChEA), Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE), Jason’s Animal Species-specific Regulatory Interaction and Network DATABASE (JASPAR), database of human transcription targets (hTFtarget), and KnockTF. Combining these databases, it is indicated that cyclic AMP-responsive element-binding protein 1 (CREB1) and endothelial transcription factor GATA-2 (GATA2) may be transcriptional factors regulating FOXO3 promoter ( ). It is reported that TLR9 activation by CpG-DNA can lead to CREB1 phosphorylation and activation. So, we applied CREB1 inhibitor (KG-501, 5 μM, Med Chem Express) to stimulate THP1-derived macrophages for 60 min before CpG stimulation. However, the mRNA expression of FOXO3 after CpG stimulation with or without the treatment of KG-501 does not show a significant difference ( ). Further studies, such as DNA pulldown analysis, may better determine the transcription factors involved in regulating FOXO3 transcription after TLR9 activation.
We also investigated the cellular localization of FOXO3 since it mainly exerts its regulatory effect on downstream target genes through nuclear export. Our findings revealed that the cytoplasmic FOXO3 was gradually repressed after TLR9 stimulation, mainly in the final 2 h; while nuclear FOXO3 presented a gradual increase in the first 4 h, but significantly reduced again within 4th to 6th hours ( ). This may also be attributed to the transcriptional downregulation and enhanced phosphorylation of FOXO3 by TLR9 activation, as well as a negative feedback mechanism.
Whether the protein degradation pathways of FOXO3 are affected by TLR9 activation is also determined. We applied bortezomib (BTZ, 100 nM) as the proteasome inhibitor and hydroxychloroquine (HCQ, 10 μM) as an autophagy inhibitor to stimulate cells 30 min prior to TLR9 activation. The level of FOXO3 does not obviously change after inhibition, indicating degradation and autophagy pathways are not necessary for the downregulation of FOXO3 after CpG stimulation ( ).

## Forkhead box protein O3 ablation ameliorates MAS inflammation
We then induced the MAS model on wild-type and Foxo3−/− mice to investigate the role of FOXO3 on MAS inflammation. Foxo3−/− mice with MAS displayed less splenomegaly (p = 0.0073), thrombocytopenia (p = 0.0454), and anemia (p = 0.0469) compared to WT mice ( A–4C), though there was no significant improvement in hepatomegaly and leukopenia. Besides, serum levels of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-10, MCP-1, and IFN-γ were all significantly or mildly attenuated in Foxo3−/− mice than in WT mice ( D). 
Consistently, Foxo3−/− mice with MAS did not show a marked expansion of total (p = 0.0256) or inflammatory (Ly6Chi) monocytes (p = 0.0381) in the peripheral blood ( E). Besides, a dramatic reduction in the inflammatory cell infiltration within the liver of Foxo3−/− MAS mice was observed by H&E staining ( F, p = 0.0276). F4/80 immunohistochemistry also revealed a reduced infiltration of macrophages in the liver ( G, p = 0.0002). Flow cytometry validated a reduction of infiltrating monocytes (p = 0.037), inflammatory (Ly6Chi) monocytes (p = 0.429), and macrophages (p = 0.0296) in the liver ( H). In line with this observation, Foxo3 deficiency led to attenuated liver damage with lower ALT activity ( I, p = 0.0424). Collectively, these results indicated that FOXO3 was critical for TLR9-triggered MAS hyperinflammation and liver injury.
To further verify the regulatory role of TLR9 on FOXO3 in vivo, we also construct TLR9 gene knockout mice and detect the protein expression level of FOXO3, as well as FOXO3-related physiological or pathological processes such as inflammation and apoptosis. We found MAS model cannot be successfully induced in Tlr9ko mice with repeated CpG stimulation, as the sizes of the liver and spleen and levels of the three cell lineages in peripheral blood were comparable to those of the negative control mice ( A–S7C). The inflammation levels in Tlr9ko mice after MAS modeling, obviously, were also comparable to the negative control, indicated by serum inflammatory factors level, total or inflammatory monocytes amounts in blood, and inflammatory cell or macrophages infiltration within liver ( D–S7G). We also found that the FOXO3 protein and mRNA level in Tlr9ko mice do not decrease after MAS modeling as they do in wild-type mice, validating our conclusion that TLR9 activation led to FOXO3 downregulation ( H–S7I). After MAS model induction, the proportion of BMDMs experiencing apoptosis significantly decreased in WT mice, accompanied by the decrease of FOXO3 expression; while in Tlr9ko mice, this difference is not so significant ( J).

## Forkhead box protein O3 facilitates macrophage activation syndrome inflammation by activating NLRP3 inflammasomes through transcriptionally regulating NLRP3 expression
We then investigated how FOXO3 mediated inflammation in MAS. We found significantly diminished expression levels of Il1b and Tnfa in the liver of Foxo3−/− MAS mice with Q-PCR ( A). NLRP3 inflammasome activation serves as an important source of IL-1β. Several studies have reported the regulatory role of FOXO3 in NLRP3 inflammasome activation, but there were inconsistent results, including both promoting and suppressing effects. Through Western blot of liver tissue lysate, we detected a decreased level of NLRP3, cleaved caspase-1, and cleaved IL-1β in the liver of Foxo3−/− MAS mice, indicating an attenuated activation of NLRP3 inflammasome activation ( B). We next activated NLRP3 inflammasome in BMDMs in vitro with LPS followed by ATP stimulation, and found diminished levels of active caspase-1 p20 and IL-1β production in both the supernatant and lysate of Foxo3−/− BMDMs ( C), indicating impaired activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. We also observed reduced NLRP3 inflammasome activator-induced ASC speckle formation in Foxo3−/− BMDMs through immunofluorescence ( D). To further validate our conclusions, we have also performed rescue experiments by overexpressing Foxo3 again in Foxo3−/− BMDMs. Upon the restoration of FOXO3 expression levels in BMDMs, we observed a concomitant recovery in the previously compromised NLRP3 expression and activation ( E). Taken together, we concluded that FOXO3 played an essential role in NLRP3 inflammasome activation ( ), contributing to MAS inflammation. 
We further investigated the mechanisms of the role of FOXO3 on NLRP3 inflammasome activation. Applying co-IP experiments, we first determine that there is no obvious protein interaction between FOXO3 and NLRP3 ( ). Then we study the effect of FOXO3 on the upstream signaling pathway of NLRP3. MAPK signaling is well illustrated to be interface canonical NLRP3 inflammasome activation, involving three steps of priming, licensing, and activation. So, we then study the effect of FOXO3 on the upstream signaling pathway of NLRP3, i.e., the MAPK pathway. Though IL-1β expression was reduced after the inhibition of all three MAPK signaling, with the cleavage of caspase-1 even totally blocked after the inhibition of JNK, we found no significant differentiation in the effect of MAPK inhibition on NLRP3 expression, IL-1β expression, and maturation of caspase-1/IL-1β in wildtype and Foxo3-ko BMDMs ( ). The effect of FOXO3 on the protein stability of NLRP3 was also studied by protein synthesis inhibitor experiments and ubiquitination detection, which both showed no significant differentiation between wild-type and Foxo3−/− BMDMs ( ).
Given that the expression of NLRP3 is reduced in the liver of Foxo3ko mice, we then analyze the regulation of FOXO3 on the transcriptional level of NLRP3. Firstly, we detected the NLRP3 mRNA level in WT and Foxo3−/− BMDMs by qPCR. Consistent with the protein level detection, NLRP3 mRNA level was decreased in Foxo3−/− BMDMs. Then, we carried out ChIP experiments, which validate an interaction between the transcription factor FOXO3 and the DNA of NLRP3, indicating FOXO3 transcriptionally activates NLRP3 in BMDMs ( F and 5G).

## Discussion
As an intriguing autoinflammatory syndrome, AOSD demonstrates excessive activation of monocytes/macrophages and a rebellious cytokine storm. Since there is a high tendency to develop MAS in patients with AOSD, much attention has been paid to uncover the monocyte molecular spectrum during AOSD-MAS development. In the present study, we identified FOXO3 to be downregulated in AOSD and negatively correlated with disease activity. Moreover, patients with AOSD with MAS exhibited markedly depleted FOXO3 expression. Using a combination of murine models with human studies, we further revealed that FOXO3 promotes inflammation progression in MAS by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome. In conclusion, our work illustrates that FOXO3, inhibited by TLR9 activation through phosphorylation and transcriptional regulation, plays a critical role in the self-negative regulation of inflammation in AOSD.
Under circumstances such as acute or chronic inflammation or infection, human myeloid cells are precisely self-regulated to prevent excessive activation and uncontrollable organ damage. In AOSD, there is an amplification of inflammation activated by the innate immune system, leading to an uncontrollable inflammatory cascade reaction. Previous studies have focused on the mechanisms of the “inflammatory loops” resulting in the refractory cytokine storm in AOSD. For instance, our previous study has revealed enhanced neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation in AOSD to promote the NLRP3 inflammasome activation in macrophages and thus produce a collection of pro-inflammatory mediators, which could enhance NETs formation again. Similarly, we have reported that ferritin, the typical acute-phase biomarker of AOSD and MAS, triggered NETs-mediated cytokine storm in AOSD through Msr1 signaling, leading to a vicious spiral to amplify inflammation. However, a few studies have paid attention to the self-feedback mechanism to control inflammation in AOSD. It was reported that placenta specific 8 (PLAC8), a molecule found to be extremely upregulated in AOSD utilizing RNA-seq, suppressed the production of IL-1β and IL-18 by inflammasome activation via enhancement of autophagy, playing a protective role in AOSD. We have also reported an elevated level of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10 in AOSD. Transcriptional signature of monocytes from “high ferritin” sJIA also showed an attempt of monocytes to compensate for systemic hyperinflammation, and display both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory properties. Herein, our observation of decreased levels of FOXO3 in AOSD and its pro-inflammatory function through NLRP3 inflammasome activation led to the conclusion that FOXO3 downregulation was important to the self-control of AOSD inflammation.
Our findings indicating a pro-inflammatory role of FOXO3 may challenge the initial hypothesis that it can alleviate inflammation by inhibiting NF-κB activation or negatively regulate antiviral responses. , However, there were also theoretical supports to explain this outcome. For instance, FOXO3 depletion has been reported to ameliorate inflammation in myocarditis, and the noncoding rs12212067 mutation of FOXO3 is linked to poorer prognosis due to insufficient viral clearance, which contradicts its protective role in CD and RA. , Combining these findings, we conclude that the negative regulatory effect of FOXO3 on antiviral responses may be disadvantageous for controlling the acute inflammatory storm caused by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), considered a pathogenesis of AOSD. On the other hand, inflammatory arthritis requires FOXO3 to prevent neutrophil apoptosis, , and FOXO3 has been found to be involved in neutrophil/T cell survival in RA, showing unexpected overexpression in blood and synovial tissue. This suggests that the effects of FOXO3 on inflammation may vary depending on cell types. Moreover, FOXO3 can activate CXCL16, worsening cardiac inflammation in ischemia/reperfusion injury, and enhances cGAS/STING and IFN-I responses via post-translational modifications. These reports indicate that there may be undiscovered transcriptional targets of FOXO3 involved in the pro-inflammatory process. Further experiments, such as chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq), are needed to identify potential unknown transcriptional targets of FOXO3 in mediating hyperinflammation.
As a self-negative regulation of inflammatory response in AOSD-MAS, it is also a key question to identify which inflammatory mediator specifically downregulates FOXO3. FOXO3 was reported to be repressed by a large variety of cytokines and signals, including IFN-β, TNF-α, IL-1β, and toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) activation by LPS. , , Herein, in addition to confirming the correlation between IFN-I and FOXO3, we validated that FOXO3 was downregulated in the MAS model induced by TLR9 agonist, CpG-ODN 1826, and by ODN 2006 in vitro as well. These were both B-class ODNs, which strongly activated TLR9-dependent NF-κB signaling but weakly stimulated IFN-α secretion. There had been similar reports describing the suppression of FOXO3 by ODN 1668, which was also a B-class CpG-ODN. TLR9 preferentially senses and binds DNA in bacteria and viruses and interacts with MyD88, thereby triggering a signaling cascade leading to a pro-inflammatory cytokine response, including IRF7 signaling, MAPK signaling, and NF-κB signaling. Notably, clues indicated that TLR9 played a critical role in the development of MAS. It was previously proposed that TLR9 activation may lead to the cytokine storm syndrome in EBV-HLH, SLE, or SJIA. In EBV-HLH, high viral loads in those patients might serve as a source of TLR9 stimulus. In SLE, exposure to amounts of self-DNA/RNA might also serve as a source of MAS development through TLR9 activation. Patients with SJIA, another population at risk for MAS, show gene expression profiles consistent with repeated TLR receptor activation. As in AOSD, evidence reported that TLR9-positive inflammatory cells infiltration was higher in skin lesions from patients with AOSD than HC. And as we illustrated by our genetic profile data, the TLR9 signaling pathway is upregulated in AOSD-MAS compared to those without MAS. The TLR9 activation in AOSD-MAS might be attributed to the DAMPs such as NET-DNA or ferritin. For example, in our previous work, we found an elevated circulation NET-DNA level in patients with AOSD because of the neutrophil overactivation, which was mainly composed of oxidative mitochondrial DNA (ox-mtDNA). These self-DAN might be able to bind to TLR9 to induce IFN-I production in patients with AOSD. , Also, TLR9 expression can also be induced by ferritin, which is a critical biomarker and pro-inflammatory modulator in AOSD-MAS. On the other hand, repeated TLR9 stimulation results in MAS-like disease in mice, as the model we applied. Given the importance of TLR9 in AOSD-MAS, we concluded that FOXO3 performs as a sentinel to negatively control the overwhelming inflammation through suppression of TLR9 signaling.
Given the unexpected pro-inflammatory role of FOXO3 in AOSD-MAS, it was intriguing to explore by which mechanism FOXO3 mediates inflammation. The NLRP3 inflammasome is one of the most extensively studied inflammasomes, particularly in the context of autoinflammatory diseases. It plays a crucial role in the immune response by mediating the activation of caspase-1, which leads to the processing and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18. This process is essential for the induction of pyroptosis, a form of programmed cell death that is characterized by the swelling and lysis of cells, thereby contributing to the inflammatory response. NLRP3 is primarily activated in myeloid/macrophages, through various stimuli including mitochondrial DNA, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and post-translational modifications. Mutations in the NLRP3 gene are known to cause a classical monogenic autoinflammatory syndrome called cryopyrin-associated periodic syndromes (CAPSs), which encompasses a range of disorders characterized by recurrent fever and systemic inflammation. The significance of NLRP3 extends beyond CAPS, as it has been implicated in several other autoinflammatory conditions, including systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA) and AOSD. The increased expression of the NLRP3 and elevated serum levels of IL-1β and IL-18 have been reported in patients with AOSD and positively correlated with disease activity, while the expression of other NLRPs, such as NLRP2, NLRP7, and NLRP12, was decreased in patients with AOSD compared with the controls. This complexity suggests that therapeutic strategies targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome may provide new avenues for treating autoinflammatory diseases. In our research, we suggested that FOXO3 promoted MAS inflammation by activating NLRP3 inflammasomes. It was noteworthy that the reported effect of FOXO3 on NLRP3 inflammasome activation was also discordant. For example, FOXO3 was indicated to decrease caspase-1 activation and IL-1β release by increasing the PINK1 mediated mitophagy and clearance of mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) or mtDNA. Overexpression of FOXO3 improved inflammation in necrotizing colitis mice by affecting NLRP3-mediated cell coronation. It was also suggested to limit alcohol-induced liver inflammation by suppressing LPS-induced inflammasome activation. However, in myelodysplastic syndromes, overexpression of FOXO3 was reported to promote NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and the mechanism may involve abnormal autophagy. A promoting role of FOXO3 in NLRP3 inflammasome activation was also reported in atherosclerosis. Above all, further investigation was needed to understand the underlying mechanism.
In conclusion, our findings demonstrate a pro-inflammatory role of FOXO3 in AOSD and its negative feedback mechanism in AOSD. FOXO3 induces the inflammation in MAS development in an NLRP3 inflammasome dependent way, which then contributes to systemic and hepatic inflammation. Accordingly, abolishing FOXO3 expression could abrogate the inflammatory process in MAS. Our study highlights the importance of FOXO3 in the self-regulation of inflammatory response in AOSD-MAS.

## Limitations of the study
In this study, we revealed a downregulation of FOXO3 in AOSD, especially AOSD-MAS. However, due to a lack of samples, we did not compare the expression of FOXO3 in PBMCs or monocytes with other autoimmune or autoinflammatory diseases, such as SLE or dermatomyositis, or MAS developed from other causes, including distinct rheumatic diseases, infection, or lymphoma. There might be a possibility that the phenomenon is common among inflammatory conditions in secondary MAS. Nevertheless, we were unable to characterize the role of FOXO3 in all cellular types in the MAS model as we used Foxo3 full-knockout mice. The influence of FOXO3 on immunoregulation in MAS may not be limited only to monocytes/macrophages. Hence, more should be systematically explored in the future. We were also enabling to observe the expression changes of FOXO3 and its downstream genes using Foxo3 overexpression mice, so alternatively, we have performed rescue experiments by overexpressing FOXO3 again in BMDMs from Foxo3ko mice.

## Experimental model and study participant details

## Human participants
A cohort of 12 AOSD (9 with MAS and 3 without MAS) and 10 healthy controls (HCs) were used for monocyte RNA sequencing. Another cohort of 67 AOSD patients (42 active and 25 inactive AOSD patients) and 57 HCs were included for PBMC FOXO3 expression analysis, whose clinical characteristics were summarized in . All patients fulfilled Yamaguchi’s criteria and were excluded from those with neoplastic, infectious, and autoimmune disorders. Active AOSD was defined as presenting fever, and/or arthritis, and/or skin lesions, and/or sore throat. All HCs were age- and sex-matched, with no history of rheumatic or other diseases. Information on demographic and clinical data was recorded together with the laboratory test results. The 2016 EULAR/ACR/PRINTO classification criteria for MAS complicating systemic JIA (sJIA-MAS criteria) were used to define AOSD complicated with MAS (AOSD-MAS). The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the principles of Good Clinical Practice. The experimental design was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University (2016-62). All subjects signed written informed consent. All samples were stored at −80°C immediately after collection.

## Mice
Wild-type FVB/N (#215) mice and C57BL/6N (#213) mice were purchased from Vital River Laboratories (Beijing, China). FOXO3-deficient (Foxo3−/−) mice on an FVB/N background were provided from Professor Ping Hu in Guangzhou Laboratory. 
Tlr9 knockout mice (C57BL/6Smoc-Tlr9em1Smoc, NM-KO-190168) were purchased from Shanghai Model organisms. Animals were maintained under pathogen-free conditions with a 12h light/dark cycle, ambient temperature 22-24°C and humidity 50-70%, with ad libitum food and water in our animal facility. All experiments were performed on sex-and 8 to 12-week-old age-matched animals, to minimize the influence of sex or age on the results or the study. All experimental protocols described in this study were approved by the Animal Care Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (ADM-018-T01). All experiments conformed to the relevant regulatory standards. The number of animals used and their suffering were minimized.

## MAS model
Wild-type and Foxo3 deficient FVB/n mice (8-12 weeks old, male/female, sex and age matched and randomly allocated to experimental groups), as well as wild-type C57BL/6N and C57BL/6Smoc-Tlr9em1Smoc mice (2 months old, male/female, sex and age matched and randomly allocated to experimental groups) were treated with intraperitoneal injections of murine CpG Class B ODN 1826 (5 OD, one OD per day) or PBS on days 0, 2, 4, 7, and 9. On day 10, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation for body weight measurement and blood collection, and then sacrificed by rapid cervical dislocation for liver and spleen collection. The size and weight of liver and spleen were measured. Blood was collected with EDTA-containing tubes and analyzed using a SYSMEX Hematology Analyzer (pocH-100iVD, Sysmex, Japan). Blood and serum were harvested for flow cytometry and cytokine analysis. Liver samples were collected for flow cytometry and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for immunohistochemistry. Additional liver samples were stored at −80°C.

## Cell culture and macrophage differentiation
THP1 and HEK293T cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (#TIB-202 and #CRL-3216, Manassas, VA, USA). These cells were cultured in the RPMI-1640 medium (supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 100 units of penicillin, and 100 mg/ml streptomycin) at 37°C and 5% CO2. THP1 cells were differentiated into macrophages in RPMI-1640 medium containing PMA (100nM, Sigma) for 48 h, and rested for 3 days. PMA-differentiated THP1 were seeded in a 6-well plate (2×10ˆ6 per well) and stimulated with human CpG Class B oligonucleotide (ODN) 2006 (1μM to 10μM) for up to 6 hours. Mouse bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDMs) were differentiated by 5ng/mL M-CSF (CB34, Novoprotein) at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 7 days in from bone marrow cells from wild-type and FOXO3 deficient FVB/n mice.

## Monocyte isolation
Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from patients with AOSD and HC were isolated using Lymphoprep (Serumwerk Bernburg AG) under sterile conditions following the manufacturer’s instructions. Monocytes were isolated with CD14 positive magnetic sorting (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany).

## Method details

## RNA sequencing
mRNA was purified with Oligo(dT)-attached magnetic beads and subsequently fragmented at appropriate temperature. cDNA was generated using random hexamer-primed reverse transcription. Afterwards, RNA Index Adapters and A-Tailing Mix were added to end repair. PCR was used to amplify the cDNA fragments and Ampure XP Beads were used to purify the products. The quality of the products was assessed using the Agilent Technologies 2100 bioanalyzer. The double-strand PCR products were denatured and circularized with the splint oligo sequence to create the final library, which was amplified by phi29 to produce DNA nanoballs (DNBs) containing over 300 copies per molecule. The DNBs were loaded onto a patterned nanoarray, and single-end 50 base reads were generated on the BGIseq500 platform (BGI-Shenzhen, China). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as genes with a log2 fold change > 1 and adjusted p value < 0.05 using edgeR analysis package.

## Q-PCR
Total RNA was extracted from samples with Trizol reagent (Takara, Japan) following manufacturer’s instructions, and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara). cDNA was amplified by real-time quantitative PCR (Q-PCR) with specific primers (see Supplementary texts). The relative expression levels of mRNA were normalized against GAPDH/β-actin.

## Promoter activity analysis
The pGL4.14-human FOXO3 promoter plasmid was obtained from Shanghai Xitubio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. THP1 derived macrophages were transfected with the promoter-luciferase reporter and control plasmids, using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer's instructions. After 48 hours, cells were treated with or without 1μM CpG-PDN 2006 for 6 hours. Luciferase activity was then measured using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay System (11402ES60, Yeason, Shanghai) and normalized to Renilla luciferase activity as an internal control.

## Lentivirus construction and transfection
The lentivirus plasmid pCDH-EF1aL-murine FOXO3-Puro, psPAX2 and pMD2G was obtained from Shanghai Xitubio Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The above plasmids were transfected into HEK293T cells, and lentivirus was collected and filtered from the supernatants after 48h. Then BMDMs were infected with lentivirus at 10 MOI for 48h, followed by priming and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome.

## ChIP assay
Briefly, BMDMs at D7 were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min, and crosslinking reaction was quenched by addition of 0.125M glycine for 5 min. Chromatin was fragmented using 2000 gel units/μl MNase (BeyoChIP™ Enzymatic ChIP Assay Kit, P2083S, Beyotime), and the chromatin fragmentation effect was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. 2% sample was saved as input. Remaining samples was incubated with antibodies for FoxO3 (12829, D19A7 clone, CST) for IP and normal rabbit IgG (A7016, Beyotime) as negative control, at 4°C for overnight. Then samples were incubated with magnetic beads at 4°C for an hour (Protein A/G Magnetic Beads/Salmon Sperm DNA, Beyotime), followed by elution. Purification of DNA was conducted using purifying column according to the manufacturer's instructions (DNA Purification Kit, D0033, Beyotime). Results were detected using qRT-PCR. Primers used are: Forward-ACACAGTACCCATTCCATACCC; Reverse-AAGGAATGAAGAGGCAGGGAAG. Percent input (%input) was calculated.

## Cell stimulation
For NLRP3 inflammasome activation, BMDMs and PMA-differentiated THP1 were primed with LPS (1000 ng/ml, Sigma) for 4 h, followed by stimulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome activator ATP (5 mM, Invivogen, 30 min). For inhibition of MAPK and AKT signaling, SP600125 (JNK inhibitor, 15 μM, Med Chem Express), SCH772984 (ERK inhibitor, 1.5 μM, Med Chem Express), SB203580 (p38 inhibitor, 15μM, Med Chem Express), and AKT inhibitor (MK-2206, 2μM, 30min) were added 30 min before CpG stimulation. For inhibition of proteasome or autophagy pathway, bortezomib (BTZ, 100nM) and hydroxychloroquine (HCQ, 10μM) were added 30 min prior to TLR9 activation. For inhibition of protein synthesis, wild-type and Foxo3-/- BMDMs were stimulated with cycloheximide (CHX, 20ug/ml) for 0, 4, 8 and 24 hours.

## Immunoprecipitation
Antibody for FOXO3 (12829, D19A7 clone, CST) is incubated at 1:50 of protein lysate on the rotator at 4°C overnight. The next day, 20 μl of washed protein G-sepharose beads were added to capture immune complexes at 4°C overnight. Immunoprecipitated complexes were washed thrice and collected with lysis buffer at 3000g for 2min. Finally, 25 μl of 2× loading buffer was added to beads and boiled for 10 min at 95°C.

## Western blotting
Human PBMCs, mouse livers, PMA-differentiated THP1 cells and BMDMs were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Yeason, Shanghai, China). For the localization detection of FOXO3, cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins were extracted from cells using a protein extraction kit (KeyGen BioTECH, KGB5300). Proteins in cell lysates were first resolved using sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, then transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membrane, and subsequently incubated with the primary antibodies against rabbit anti-FOXO3a (1:1000, 12829, D19A7 clone, CST), rabbit anti-phospho-FoxO3a Ser253 (1:1000, 13129, D18H8 clone, CST), rabbit anti-IRF7 (1:1000, 4920, CST), rabbit anti-phospho-IRF7 (1:1000, 5184, CST), rabbit anti-ERK (1:1000, 4695, 137F5 clone, CST), rabbit anti-phospho-ERK (1:1000, 4370, D13.14.4E clone, CST), rabbit anti-JNK (1:1000, 9258, 56G8 clone, CST), rabbit anti-phospho-JNK (1:1000, 4668, 81E11 clone, CST), rabbit anti-p38 (1:1000, 8690, D13E1 clone, CST), rabbit anti-phospho-p38 (1:1000, 4511, D3F9 clone, CST), rabbit anti-NLRP3 (1:1000, 15101, D4D8T clone, CST), rabbit anti-AKT (1:1000, C67E7 clone, 4691, CST), rabbit anti-phospho-AKT (1:1000, C31E5E clone, 2965, CST), mouse anti-Caspase-1 (1:1000, AG-20B-0042, Adipogen), and rabbit anti-IL-1 beta (1:1000, 5128, Biovision), rabbit anti-Ubiquitin (1:500, GB115700, ServiceBio) overnight at 4°C. After incubation with peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies, the signals were detected by ECL assays (WBKLS0500, Millipore, USA). Anti-GAPDH (1:1000, AF1186, Beyotime, China) or β-actin antibody (1:1000, 3700, CST) was used as an internal control for total cytoplasmic protein, and histone H3 (1:300, GB11102, ServiceBio) were used as an internal control for nuclear protein. Bands were quantitated with Image J (v1.53, Bio-Rad, USA). Results are expressed as fold change to the internal control.

## Histology and immunohistochemistry
Liver tissue sections were stained with H&E staining. To detect macrophages in the liver tissue, paraffin-embedded mouse liver sections were stained by antibodies against F4/80 (GB11027, 1:1000, Servicebio, China). Infiltration area was quantitated with Image J (v1.53, Bio-Rad, USA). For HE staining, areas of inflammatory cell infiltration, as judged visually, were manually selected at 100× field of view, and the area was measured. For F4/80 immunohistochemical staining, after converting the image to 8-bit, the cells with positive staining were automatically identified by adjusting an appropriate threshold value, and then the overall area was measured. For some of the supplemented part, full sectional electron scanning (ServiceBio) was applied.

## Serum ALT and cytokine measurement
Murine serum was collected from whole blood by centrifugation at 845 × g for 10 min. Serum ALT measurement were performed in the clinical biochemistry lab of our hospital. Serum IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-6, MCP-1, IL-10, and IL-12p70 were measured with Cytokine Bead Array (552364, BD Biosciences, USA). 50μl of the standard dilutions or samples were incubated with capture beads and detection reagent at room temperature for 2 h. Assays were performed by a FACS Canto II cytometer (BD).

## Flow cytometry
Murine blood and hepatic cells were stained at 4°C for 15 min using the following antibodies: PerCP-conjugated anti-mouse CD45 (1:100, 557235, 30-F11 clone, BD), FITC-conjugated anti-mouse CD11b (1:100, 101206, M1/70 clone, Biolegend), PE-Cy7-conjugated anti-mouse Ly6G (1:100, 560601, 1A8 clone, BD), APC-conjugated anti-mouse Ly6C (1:100, 128016, HK1.4 clone, BD), and PE-conjugated anti-mouse F4/80 (1:100, 123110, BM8 clone, Biolegend). For apoptosis detection, staining and flow cytometry were conducted using Annexin V-EGFP/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (KeyGen BioTECH, KGA1101) following the manufacturer’s instructions. All assays were performed by a FACS Canto II cytometer (BD).

## Immunofluorescence
Immunofluorescence staining of BMDM was performed with a mouse monoclonal anti-ASC antibody (1:200, sc-514414, B-3 clone, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) to detect inflammasome speckle formation. DNA was stained with Hoechst 33342 (1:2000, H3570, Invitrogen) for 5 min. Finally, images were obtained using an Olympus microscope (IX73, Tokyo, Japan).

## Quantification and statistical analysis
All data were statistically analyzed using SPSS version 20.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and Graphpad Prism v8.0 software. Quantitative data are expressed as means ± SD (standard deviation) as indicated. Data with Gaussian distribution was analyzed using an unpaired two-sided t-test, while nonparametric data were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The number of animals used in each experiment and the number of images that were analyses are specified in the Methods and/or indicated in each figure legend. All tests were two-sided, and p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. ∗, p<0.05; ∗∗, p<0.01; ∗∗∗, p<0.001; ∗∗∗∗, p<0.0001; ns, not significant.
