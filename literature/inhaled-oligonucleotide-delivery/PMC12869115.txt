## ABSTRACT
Peripheral arterial disease progression to critical limb ischemia remains a significant burden in the aged, necessitating revascularization. Therapies to enhance neovascularization, such as angiogenesis or arteriogenesis, may provide an option for patients not suitable for surgery. The thrombospondins (TSPs) are secreted matricellular glycoproteins, with TSP‐1, TSP‐2, and TSP‐5 implicated in neovascularization. Currently, there is little data regarding the effects of TSP‐2, TSP‐5, sex, and aging on angiogenesis and arteriogenesis. In vitro, the effect of TSP‐2 or TSP‐5 on early or late passage endothelial cell (EC) tubule formation or disruption was assessed. In vivo, angiogenesis, and arteriogenesis in male and female, young (14–16 weeks) and old (105–110 weeks) wild‐type, TSP‐2 and TSP‐5 knockout mice were compared. To assess the effect of sex hormones, neovascularization was assessed in ovariectomized young female mice. After 14 days, angiogenesis and arteriogenesis were quantified using immunohistochemistry. Laser Doppler was used to confirm > 50% decrease in blood flow. In vitro: TSP‐2 inhibited angiogenesis in early passage ECs and disrupted tubules in late passage ECs. TSP‐5 prevented early passage tubule disruption. Late passage cells showed greater disruption than early passage. In vivo: TSP‐2 was anti‐angiogenic in young mice and anti‐arteriogenic in old mice; TSP‐5 had no effect on angiogenesis but largely negatively impacted arteriogenesis. Females showed reduced angiogenesis/arteriogenesis versus males, aging blunted both responses, and TSP‐2/TSP‐5 knockouts altered sex‐ and age‐specific ischemic responses. In conclusion, we broadly elucidate the differential effects of TSP‐2, TSP‐5, sex and aging on neovascularization after inducing limb ischemia.

## Introduction
The progression to critical limb ischemia (CLI) from asymptomatic peripheral arterial disease (PAD) represents a significant burden of disease in the aging population with an estimated 500,000 to 1 million cases per year [ ]. Surgical revascularization is the standard of care to prevent limb loss or life‐threatening complications [ , ]. However, not all patients are candidates for invasive interventions, and currently, there is a lack of alternative treatment options. A possible remedy to this significant obstacle in patient care includes developing therapies that increase neovascularization. Furthermore, although the prevalence is similar between sexes, female patients tend to present with more advanced disease and at an older age [ , , ]. The direct effects of aging and sex together on vascular recovery from ischemia have not been fully elucidated. An important clinical question is whether neovascularization is similar in aging males and females, and if proposed medical therapies would equally affect both sexes.
In a coordinated response to an ischemic insult to the lower extremity, there are two compensating physiologic responses involved in the vascular remodeling process: angiogenesis and arteriogenesis [ , ]. Angiogenesis, the primary source of new vessels, is a process by which de novo endothelial channels are formed from sprouting of endothelial cells (ECs) from preexisting capillaries [ , , ]. Arteriogenesis, on the other hand, is the positive remodeling of preexisting arteriole collateral channels. Typically, these preexisting channels are high resistance. However, when blood flow is redirected through these channels, the ensuing hemodynamic stimulus provokes an increase in the arteriole diameter and wall thickness, with proliferation of vascular cells and turnover of the vascular matrix [ ]. Although these processes were once thought to be governed by the same mechanism, now it is accepted that these processes are different, yet functionally connected [ , ]. For example, angiogenesis is dependent on hypoxia/ischemia but arteriogenesis is driven by fluid shear stress [ , ]. These processes are distinct but remain interdependent, with studies demonstrating the distal microvascular networks from angiogenesis are capable of retrograde communication to the more proximal arterial bed, influencing arteriogenesis via backward signaling and augmenting flow velocity and shear rates [ , ].
Thrombospondins (TSPs) are a family of five matricellular glycoproteins that are synthesized, secreted by multiple cell types and incorporated into the extracellular matrix to perform a variety of distinct and non‐redundant actions, including neovascularization [ , ]. There are two subfamilies, where subgroup A contains TSP‐1 and TSP‐2, whereas subgroup B contains TSP‐3, TSP‐4, and TSP‐5. TSP‐1 and TSP‐2 are trimeric proteins that share similarities in structure, whereas TSP‐3, TSP‐4, and TSP‐5 are pentamers [ ]. Numerous in vitro and in vivo studies have identified TSP‐1 and TSP‐2 as endogenous inhibitors of angiogenesis through their antagonism of VEGF, suppression of nitric oxide signaling, inhibition of EC migration, and induction of EC apoptosis [ , ]. Emerging in vitro evidence suggests that TSP‐5 (also known as cartilage oligomeric matrix protein) may, in contrast, be proangiogenic [ ]. In regard to arteriogenesis, TSP‐1's role has been investigated; there remains a paucity of data regarding the effects of TSP‐2 or TSP‐5 on this process [ , ]. Furthermore, a limited understanding of the role TSP‐2 or TSP‐5 have in vivo with respect to vascular recovery from ischemia, sex differences, and aging exists.
The vascular response to injury in the context of aging is extremely complex. Due to this complexity, studies in vivo have attempted to reduce the confounding variables by using genetically identical animals with or without knockout of individual genes to focus on the interaction of these genes with the process of aging. This approach is not perfect but does allow for insight into the individual factors involved in the sequelae of aging. In this regard, there is very little literature exploring the roles of TSP‐2 and TSP‐5 in the aged vascular response to hypoxic injury. One study has shown that increased expression of TSP‐2 is associated with impaired angiogenesis in old mice; there is little data on TSP‐5's effect on aged vascular remodeling [ , ].
Given the limited literature on TSP‐2 or TSP‐5, in comparison to TSP‐1, the purpose of our study was to determine how TSP‐2 and TSP‐5, advancing age and sex impact angiogenesis and arteriogenesis after hindlimb ischemia. We hypothesized that angiogenesis and arteriogenesis are increased in males compared to females, young compared to aged, and arteriogenesis increases when angiogenesis decreases. In addition, TSP‐2 null mice have increased angiogenesis and arteriogenesis, which are reduced in TSP‐5 null mice.

## Materials and Methods
2.1.

## Materials
Endothelial cell growth medium was purchased from Cell Applications Inc. (San Diego, CA). Trypsin and trypsin neutralizing solution were purchased from Cell Applications Inc. (San Diego, CA). Matrigel was from Corning Inc. (Corning, NY). Recombinant TSP‐2 and TSP‐5 were purchased from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN).

2.2.

## Cell Culture
Human aortic endothelial cells (Cell Applications Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) were used in early passage (P3–P8) to represent young cells or late passage (P15–P18) to represent aged cells. Cell viability was determined with the Trypan blue exclusion assay using the Countess cell counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

2.3.

## Endothelial Tubule Assay
To assess the role of aging, TSP‐2 and TSP‐5 on EC tubule formation, a Matrigel‐based endothelial tubule formation assay (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) was used. Standard matrigel was plated into a 96 well plate. Endothelial cells (2 × 104 cells in 1 mL) with or without TSP‐2 (5 μg/mL) or TSP‐5 (5 μg/mL) were incubated overnight (18 h). In another set of experiments to assess tubule disruption, EC tubes were allowed to form overnight and then TSP‐2 (5 μg/mL) or TSP‐5 (5 μg/mL) was added for 24 h. Photographs were taken at 10× using an EVOS imaging system (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA) and the number of tubule branch points counted manually (Figure  ). Data were recorded as number of branch points per well. Experiments were performed in triplicate three times, and each data point is an average of each experiment.

2.4.

## Hind Limb Ischemia Model
All animal protocols were approved by the Loyola University IACUC and Edwards Hines Veteran Affairs Hospital IACUC. Male and female mice (wild type [WT]; C57BL/6, JAX Laboratories, Bar Harbor, ME) were used across the following age groups: young (14–16 weeks old) and old (105–110 weeks old). Each of these groups was repeated with age‐ and sex‐matched THBS2 or THBS5 knockout mice. THBS2+ heterozygote mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME), bred to homozygote THBS2−/− at Loyola University Chicago Comparative Medicine Facility and genotyped at Loyola University Chicago. THBS5−/− mice were provided by Jacqueline Hecht, PhD (UT Health McGovern Medical School, Houston, TX), bred at Loyola University Chicago Comparative Medicine Facility. Both animal lines were genotyped at Loyola University Chicago and germline knockout confirmed by performing qPCR.
The murine hindlimb ischemia model with femoral artery ligation was performed in the standard fashion [ , ]. The surgical site and instruments were prepped for aseptic technique. Animals were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane. The surgical site was removed of hair using hair clippers and hair removal cream (Nair). The skin was disinfected with betadine swabs and alcohol pads. Gentamycin concentration (1.25 mg/mL, dose 5 mg/kg) and analgesic extended‐release buprenorphine (concentration 3.25 mg/kg, 0.05 mL/20 g mouse) were administered prior to incision. Ligation was performed proximally on the femoral artery as close to the inguinal ligament as possible and then distally on the femoral artery just proximal to the branch of the superficial caudal epigastric artery. Procedural and postprocedural monitoring was performed by the operating surgeon.
A laser Doppler was used to confirm ligation of the femoral artery and assess blood flow recovery (Moor Instruments VMS v4.2.1 software, Wilmington, DE). The laser Doppler assessed blood flow in the proximal femoral artery for 1–2 min prior to arterial ligation, immediately after arterial ligation and on day of euthanasia. The 10 s of flow with the lowest variation was selected for analysis, and a ratio of ischemic to nonischemic leg was calculated. A decrease in blood flow by ≥ 50% compared to nonoperative leg was considered adequate ligation. On postoperative day 14 after femoral artery ligation, the mice were euthanized following standard guidelines and included anesthetic overdose using inhaled isoflurane with decapitation under anesthesia [ ]. Following, the gastrocnemius and the medial thigh muscles (adductor magnus, semimembranosus, and semitendinosus) were harvested.

2.5.

## Ovariectomy Model
To assess the effect of sex hormones without the effect of aging on angiogenesis and arteriogenesis, ovariectomy was performed on young female mice (WT, THBS2−/−, THBS5−/−) 7–10 days prior to femoral artery ligation. As previously described, the surgical site and instruments were prepped for aseptic technique. Animals were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane, hair removed, skin disinfected, and pretreated with gentamicin and extended‐release buprenorphine. A left flank incision was performed, the left ovary and uterine horn exteriorized, and the distal uterine horn suture ligated with a 4–0 silk. The ovary was then removed. The left incision was closed and the right ovary removed in similar fashion using a right flank incision.

2.6.

## Immunohistochemistry
The gastrocnemius and the medial thigh muscles, including the adductor magnus, semimembranosus, and semitendinosus, were collected for evaluation of angiogenesis and arteriogenesis, respectively. Tissues collected from mice were fixed in 10% buffered formalin and processed for routine histological examination. For angiogenesis immunohistochemistry, gastrocnemius muscles were processed in the Loyola University Medical Center Pathology Lab. Tissue blocks of the gastrocnemius muscle were sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Immunohistochemical stains were performed at Loyola University Histology Core facility on the Ventana Benchmark XT automated immunostaining platform (Ventana Medical Systems Inc., Tucson, AZ). Prior to staining, the slides were deparaffinized in sequential baths of xylene, transferred to sequential baths of 100% ethanol, followed by sequential baths of 95% ethanol, and then rinsed in deionized (DI) water. Immunohistochemical stains of the gastrocnemius muscle were performed using rabbit antimouse polyclonal anti‐CD31 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) with a dilution of 1:200 to examine angiogenesis. Medial thigh muscles were stained with anti‐alpha smooth muscle actin (HistoWiz Inc., Brooklyn, NY) to examine arteriogenesis. Gastrocnemius slides were blinded, and photographs were captured at 10× magnification of five random fields per sample. Positive staining with clear demonstration of a lumen (Figure  ), as well as myofiber quantity of the five random fields, were counted using ImageJ 1.54 g (Wayne Rasband and contributors, National Institutes of Health, USA). To identify myofibers, all IHC samples were counterstained with hematoxylin nuclei stain, and we counted muscular structures that were multinucleated. Angiogenesis was defined as capillary/myofiber ratio. Medial thigh samples were blinded and photographs captured by HistoWiz Inc. (Brooklyn, NY). Positive staining (Figure  ) with clear demonstration of an arteriole structure (defined arteriole wall and lumen) was counted on all slides at 20× magnification of five random fields using ImageJ 1.54 g (Wayne Rasband and contributors, National Institutes of Health, USA).

2.7.

## Statistical Analysis
Data are presented as the mean ± SEM. The in vitro data were analyzed by Student's t‐test with p < 0.05 considered significant. For blood flow recovery on postoperative day 14, data were analyzed using two‐way ANOVA, with post hoc testing performed using Fisher's Least Significant Differences; p < 0.05 was considered significant. POD14 results are reported as mean ± SEM. For analysis of angiogenesis and arteriogenesis, the data were transformed using a natural log transformation to decrease skewness, then a linear regression model applied. Least square means (LS means) and 95% confidence limits were obtained and then back‐transformed onto the original scale of the data. Type III ANOVA was performed to assess overall differences. Pairwise comparisons are presented between genes by age and sex and oophorectomy by gene and age with p < 0.05 considered significant. Results are reported as LS means [95% lower confidence interval, 95% upper confidence interval]. Data analysis was performed using R 4.3.1 (Vienna, Austria).

## Results
3.1.

## 
TSP‐2 Inhibits Angiogenesis in Early Passage ECs, and TSP‐5 May Protect Against Tubule Disruption
In pretreated ECs, TSP‐2 inhibited angiogenesis in early passage (44.7 ± 2.3 vs. 81 ± 1.5, p = 0.0005) ECs, but TSP‐2 did not inhibit angiogenesis in late passage (69.7 ± 2.4 vs. 86 ± 0.6, p = 0.14). Early passage TSP‐5 pretreated ECs demonstrated no change in angiogenesis compared to nontreated cells (p = 0.18); TSP‐5 pretreated late passage ECs inhibited angiogenesis (72.0 ± 3.1 vs. 86 ± 0.6, p = 0.04). Findings are summarized in Figure  . Qualitative images of tubule branch points can be found in Figure  .
 A second group of ECs was given 24 h to form tubules, and then was left untreated (control), or treated with TSP‐2 or TSP‐5 for 24 h. TSP‐2 promoted tubule disruption in late passage ECs (16.7 ± 0.9 vs. 27.7 ± 2.3, p = 0.03) but not in early passage ECs (49 ± 2.3 vs. 56.7 ± 1.5, p = 0.058). TSP‐5 prevented tubule disruption in early passage ECs (64.3 ± 1.8 vs. 56.7 ± 1.5, p = 0.03) but not late passage ECs (34 ± 2.3 vs. 27.7 ± 2.3, p = 0.13). Additionally, early passage ECs had less tubule disruption than late passage ECs in each treatment group, p < 0.05. Findings are summarized in Figure  . Qualitative images of tubule branch points can be found in Figure  .

3.2.

## Inactivation of THBS2 Increases Angiogenesis in Young Mice, and Inactivation of THBS2 or THBS5 Increase Arteriogenesis in Old Mice
In young animals, THBS2−/− male and female mice had increased angiogenesis compared to WT male (0.25 [0.20, 0.31] vs. 0.17 [0.13, 0.23], p = 0.037) or WT female (0.18 [0.14, 0.24] vs. 0.11 [0.08, 0.15], p = 0.018) mice, respectively. In old age, no difference existed between WT and THBS2−/−. Compared to WT, THBS5−/− demonstrated no difference in angiogenesis in young or old mice. Findings are summarized in Figure  .
 In young age, only THBS5−/− demonstrated differences in arteriogenesis. THBS5−/− males had reduced arteriogenesis compared to WT males (2.66 [2.11, 3.36] vs. 3.86 [3.15, 4.74], p = 0.006). THBS5−/− females had increased arteriogenesis compared to WT females (3.75 [2.89, 4.86] vs. 2.40 [1.85, 3.11], p = 0.006). Old age increased arteriogenesis in THBS2−/− male (4.79 [3.75, 6.13] vs. 1.98 [1.53, 2.57], p < 0.001) and female (4.80 [3.85, 6.00] vs. 1.87 [1.46, 2.39], p < 0.001) mice compared to WT, respectively. Old age also increased arteriogenesis in THBS5−/− male (3.28 [2.56, 4.19] vs. 1.98 [1.53, 2.57], p = 0.001) and female (3.75 [2.89, 4.86] vs. 1.87 [1.46, 2.39], p < 0.001) mice. Findings are summarized in Figure  .

## Sex and Age Differences Exist in Angiogenesis and Arteriogenesis Within WT, THBS2
−/− and THBS5
−/− Mice
In WT mice, young WT females and young ovariectomized females (0.11 [0.08,0.15]; 0.09 [0.07, 0.11]) had reduced angiogenesis compared to young WT males (0.174 [0.13, 0.23]) (p = 0.04; p < 0.001). No sex differences in angiogenesis were found in old age. Angiogenesis was reduced in old males compared to young males (0.11 [0.08, 0.15] vs. 0.174 [0.13, 0.23]) (p = 0.04). Young WT females had reduced arteriogenesis than young WT males (2.40 [1.85,3.11] vs. 3.86 [3.15,4.74]) (p = 0.005); however, there was no difference between young WT males and ovariectomized females (3.60 [2.80, 4.63]). Sex differences did not exist in old age for arteriogenesis; however, old males had reduced arteriogenesis compared to young males (1.98 [1.53, 2.57] vs. 3.86 [3.15, 4.74], p < 0.001). Results are summarized in Figure  .
 In THBS2−/− mice, only ovariectomized young females had reduced angiogenesis compared to young males (0.15 [0.12, 0.20] vs. 0.25 [0.20, 0.31]) (p = 0.001). This sex difference persisted in old age, in which old female THBS2−/− had less angiogenesis than old male THBS2−/−. Within sexes, aging reduced angiogenesis (male: 0.15 [0.09, 0.23] vs. 0.25 [0.20, 0.31], p = 0.04; female: 0.06 [0.04, 0.097] vs. 0.18 [0.14, 0.24], p < 0.001). In arteriogenesis, young females had reduced arteriogenesis than young males (2.44 [1.88, 3.17] vs. 2.95 [2.41, 3.62]) (p < 0.05); however, after ovariectomy this difference was negated (3.13 [2.40, 4.09]). Within sexes, aging increased arteriogenesis (males: (4.79 [3.75, 6.13] vs. 2.95 [2.41, 3.62], p = 0.003); females: (4.80 [3.85, 6.00] vs. 2.44 [1.88, 3.17], p < 0.001)). Sex differences did not exist in old age. Results are summarized in Figure  .
In THBS5−/−, young females had reduced angiogenesis compared to young males (0.08 [0.06, 0.11] vs. 0.18 [0.14, 0.23], p < 0.001); however, this difference was negated with ovariectomy (0.17 [0.13, 0.22]). Aging only decreased angiogenesis in male mice (0.12 [0.09, 0.16] vs. 0.18 [0.14, 0.23], p = 0.048) and sex differences were not apparent. For arteriogenesis, no differences existed between sexes or with aging. Results are summarized in Figure  .

3.4.

## Laser Doppler Blood Flow Demonstrates Variable Blood Flow Recovery Depending on Age, Sex and Gene Inactivation
Laser Doppler blood flow analysis demonstrated > 50% reduction of blood flow following femoral arterial ligation in all models (Figure  ). On postoperative day 14, blood flow recovery was compared. Blood flow was significantly reduced in old WT males compared to young WT males (0.54 ± 0.23 vs. 0.76 ± 0.17; p = 0.008). Blood flow was not different between young or old THBS2−/− or THBS5−/− males (Figure  ). In young males, THBS2−/− and THBS5−/− males had reduced blood flow recovery than WT males (0.54 ± 0.06 vs. 0.76 ± 0.05; 0.47 ± 0.06 vs. 0.76 ± 0.05; p < 0.05). There was no difference in blood flow recovery in old age between WT, THBS2−/− or THBS5−/− male mice.
 In females, blood flow was significantly reduced in old WT females compared to young WT females (0.40 ± 0.02 vs. 0.63 ± 0.07, p = 0.0003), in OVX females compared to young WT females (0.42 ± 0.02 vs. 0.63 ± 0.07, p = 0.0007), and in young THBS5−/− females compared to OVX THBS5−/− females (0.33 ± 0.03 vs. 0.49 ± 0.06, p = 0.004) (Figure  ). Blood flow was not different in young, old or OVX THBS2−/− females. Only young THBS5−/− females had reduced blood flow recovery than young WT females (0.33 ± 0.03 vs. 0.63 ± 0.07; p = 0.00001). No differences were appreciated in old age between WT, THBS2−/− or THBS5−/− female mice.
When comparing male and female mice, young WT females had reduced blood flow recovery compared to young WT males (0.63 ± 0.07 vs. 0.76 ± 0.05, p = 0.04). Young THBS5−/− females also had reduced blood flow recovery compared to young THBS5−/− males (0.33 ± 0.03 vs. 0.47 ± 0.06; p = 0.01). There was no difference between young male and female THBS2−/− mice. In old age, only WT females had reduced blood flow recovery compared to WT males (0.40 ± 0.02 vs. 0.54 ± 0.08; p = 0.04). No differences were appreciated between old THBS2−/− or THBS5−/− male and female mice.

## Discussion
A valuable treatment option for PAD patients unfit for surgery will be therapies targeted to effectively increase neovascularization. In this study, we determined how TSP‐2, TSP‐5, advancing age and sex impact angiogenesis and arteriogenesis after hindlimb ischemia. In vitro, we found (1) TSP‐2 reduces tubule formation in early passage ECs and disrupts tubules in late passage ECs; (2) TSP‐5 prevents tubule disruption in early passage ECs; and (3) late passage cells demonstrate increased tubule disruption than early. In vivo we showed that (1) TSP‐2 inhibits angiogenesis in young mice and inhibits arteriogenesis in old mice; (2) TSP‐5 has no effect on angiogenesis, but differentially affects arteriogenesis in young males and females; (3) TSP‐5 inhibits arteriogenesis only in old mice; (4) WT females demonstrate reduced angiogenesis and arteriogenesis compared to WT males; and (5) blood flow recovery varied depending on age, sex, and gene knockout. In summary, systemic knockdown of THBS2 or THBS5, and ovariectomy change the response to hindlimb ischemia. The findings and their potential implications are summarized below.
4.1.

## 
TSP‐2's Effect on Angiogenesis and Arteriogenesis in Young and Old Mice
TSP‐2 is a known potent inhibitor of angiogenesis and the literature demonstrates THBS2−/− mice have increased angiogenesis after inducing hindlimb ischemia [ , , , ]. However, the literature is conflicting regarding TSP‐2's effect on arteriogenesis. For example, Krady et al. demonstrated that THBS2−/− mice had increased angiogenesis in the ischemic limb and increased baseline collateral formation in the control limb; ischemic limbs of THBS2−/− mice compared to WT mice exhibited no difference in arterialization [ ]. Meanwhile, another study showed decellularized biologic scaffolds from THBS2−/− mice promoted arteriogenesis in diabetic wounds, but not angiogenesis [ ]. The discrepancies in the literature and the possible compensatory relationship between angiogenesis and arteriogenesis prompted our initial investigation: whether angiogenic or arteriogenic responses are modulated by TSP‐2 in young and old age.
Comparable to the existing literature, we found in vitro, TSP‐2 inhibited tubule formation or angiogenesis in early passage cells (Figure  ). In vivo, THBS2−/− mice differentially modulated angiogenesis and arteriogenesis depending on age. The findings in our study may be related to the complex molecular pathways that regulate neovascularization [ , ]. For example, angiogenesis is promoted by moderate doses of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), but arteriogenesis is optimally stimulated by higher doses of VEGF and platelet derived growth factor‐BB (PDGF‐BB) [ ]. TSP‐2's anti‐angiogenic properties have been associated with its ability to enhance uptake of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP)‐2 and ‐9. Tissues void of TSP‐2 have increased MMP2/9, with an associated increase of VEGF and an altered collagen ultrastructure [ ]. MMP regulation of the ECM is a critical process in vascular remodeling in vascular disease [ , ]. The altered ECM is thought to improve cellular migration and spreading [ , , ]. Furthermore, TSP‐2 silenced VSMCs have a diminutive response to PDGF‐BB [ ]. We suspect young THBS2−/− mice have a microenvironment with doses of VEGF permissive to angiogenesis, but not sufficient to enhance arteriogenesis. Additionally, in consideration of the intricate relationship between angiogenesis and arteriogenesis, the increased angiogenesis in our model may diminish the hemodynamic shear stress and lack the appropriate response to PDGF‐BB required for arteriole remodeling.
In old age, angiogenesis did not significantly differ between THBS2−/− and WT mice. Old THBS2−/− mice had reduced angiogenesis compared to young THBS2−/−. We suspect the differences in angiogenesis in old age are likely due to cell senescence [ , , ]. This finding is captured in our in vitro work, in which old (late passage) pretreated cells did not have a significant reduction in angiogenesis compared to young cells (Figure  : 19% vs. 45%, respectively). We did find that tubules formed by late passage cells were less stable than those formed by early passage cells, most likely due to increased senescence (Figure  ). Furthermore, TSP‐1, a potent inhibitor of angiogenesis, accumulates in the ECM in old age [ ]. Thus, angiogenesis in old WT and THBS2−/− may be blunted due to the increased levels of TSP‐1 in the microenvironment. Arteriogenesis, on the other hand, was significantly increased in old THBS2−/− mice compared to old WT mice. As previously mentioned, lack of TSP‐2 contributes to increased levels of MMP2/9, which has been implicated in facilitating arteriogenesis [ , ]. This effect is magnified in old age due to accumulation of TSP‐2 [ ]. Although angiogenesis was significantly different from old WT mice, which is likely a result of decreased angiogenic factors, we found arteriogenesis to be significantly elevated. We suspect this result represents the contribution and importance of MMPs to collateral formation.

4.2.

## 
TSP‐5's Effect on Angiogenesis and Arteriogenesis in Young and Old Mice
TSP‐1 and TSP‐2 have been and continue to be studied, but there is a paucity of data on the effects of TSP‐5 on neovascularization after hindlimb ischemia. TSP‐5 is known to be a biomarker for vascular remodeling and is involved in the endothelial response to disturbed flow [ , ]. In a previous study, we demonstrated TSP‐5 in vitro is proangiogenic, rescues the antiangiogenic effects of TSP‐2, and upregulates the proangiogenic gene HEY1 [ ]. We hypothesized THBS5−/− mice would have reduced angiogenesis. In the current study, we confirmed TSP‐5 is protective against tubule disruption in early passage ECs (Figure  ). However, TSP‐5 in young (early passage) cells had no effect on tubule formation, which conflicts with our previous study that demonstrated TSP‐5 promoted tubule formation. We suspect this is a result of assessing tubule formation at different time points. In the previous study, we assessed tubule formation at 12 h; in the current study, tubule formation was assessed at 24 h. The varying in vitro effect suggested TSP‐5 may be protective of capillary development in a time dependent manner, with its effect more robust in preventing capillary disruption rather than formation. The in vitro results demonstrated promise of TSP‐5 promoting angiogenesis; in vivo, angiogenesis was not significantly different than WT, suggesting physiologic levels of TSP‐5 in the WT are likely not sufficient to significantly contribute to the angiogenic process.
Conversely, the lack of TSP‐5's effect on angiogenesis may be related to its influence on arteriogenesis. For example, THBS5−/− mice displayed increased or reduced arteriogenesis depending on age and sex. Previous studies have demonstrated TSP‐5 is expressed in VSMCs and maintains the homeostasis of mature VSMCs [ , , , ]. Rodents deficient of TSP‐5 have enhanced VSMC migration due to VSMC dedifferentiation, increased neointimal hyperplasia, and atherosclerotic plaque [ , ]. Given TSP‐5 deficiency increases VSMC migration, we hypothesized THBS5−/− mice would exhibit increased arteriogenesis. Interestingly, our hypothesis was confirmed in THBS5−/− females and old THBS5−/− males, but young THBS5−/− males had reduced arteriogenesis. To our knowledge, there is no existing literature on sex differences in TSP‐5 deficient mice. We suspect there are hormonal or chromosomal differences between male and female that contribute to the unexpected results. Furthermore, these sex differences are likely diminished in old age due to the contribution of MMPs in arteriogenesis [ , ]. For example, previous studies have shown TSP‐5 deficiency increases MMP‐9, which likely facilitates a microenvironment that promotes arteriogenesis independent of sex [ ].
Sex differences in angiogenesis and arteriogenesis although the patterns of angiogenesis and arteriogenesis are similar between males and females, the apparent blunted angiogenic response in females and differences in THBS5−/− arteriogenesis guided us to further assess sex differences. In the current study, WT young females exhibited reduced angiogenesis and arteriogenesis compared to males. In previous studies, these findings have been attributed to females' reduced expression of angiogenic genes, such as VEGF and eNOS [ , ]. To assess the effect of sex hormones, we performed ovariectomy on young female mice and found young WT ovariectomized mice trended toward less angiogenesis compared to non‐ovariectomy females, but had significantly increased arteriogenesis. In regard to the angiogenic response, studies have demonstrated that estradiol accelerates functional endothelial recovery by increasing VEGF expression, with ovariectomized females demonstrating reduced neovascularization and reduced eNOS expression [ , , ]. Our study follows this trend in ovariectomized and old‐aged WT females; however, we found a robust arteriogenic response demonstrated in the ovariectomized mice. Given male and female endothelial cells differentially express genes under static and shear stress conditions, we suspect female sex hormones negatively modulate pro‐arteriogenic gene expression [ ]. With this notion, it would be anticipated that aged females, similar to ovariectomized mice, would have increased arteriogenesis than young. However, our findings demonstrated no difference in arteriogenesis between the two groups, suggesting the effects of cell senescence may counteract the hormonal effects, which may also explain the lack of sex differences identified among old mice.
In comparison to the WT sex differences, THBS2−/− and THBS5−/− mice demonstrated some similar patterns, but also differences that may elucidate sex‐related variations. For example, in regard to angiogenesis, we found young ovariectomized and old female mice had reduced angiogenesis compared to young or old THBS2−/− males, respectively. We suspect sex hormones, in the absence of TSP‐2, may positively modulate angiogenesis. Whether androgens or estrogens may be contributing to this process is inconclusive; however, androgens are proangiogenic in male mice, and we speculate may positively modulate angiogenesis in the absence of TSP‐2 [ , , ]. Meanwhile, arteriogenesis in THBS2−/− mice demonstrated similar patterns to WT, suggesting THBS2−/−'s effect on arteriogenesis is independent of sex and may be a result of another etiology such as accumulated MMPs in the microenvironment.
In THBS5−/− mice, young females had reduced angiogenesis compared to males, but we did find that young ovariectomized mice had significantly increased angiogenesis. Interestingly, within sexes, TSP‐5 demonstrated no effect on angiogenesis. We suspect ovariectomy in young age, which abruptly disrupts sex hormones in the female mice, and the lack of TSP‐5 may enable expression of pro‐angiogenic genes that otherwise are suppressed. In regard to arteriogenesis in THBS5−/− mice, no sex‐related differences were found. For example, arteriogenesis was reduced in young THBS5−/− males and increased in young THBS5−/− females compared to WT. Yet, when comparing arteriogenesis between males and females, sex‐related differences were not identified. The increase in arteriogenesis among young females and old mice may reflect the effect of TSP‐5 deficiency, which includes increased VSMC migration and accumulation of MMPs. The reduction identified in young males, on the other hand, may suggest male sex hormones in the absence of TSP‐5 negatively regulate arteriogenesis. Unfortunately, to our knowledge, no other study has investigated sex differences and the interaction of thrombospondins in hindlimb ischemia, so future studies are crucial to understand these interactions.

4.3.

## Blood Flow Recovery
In our study, blood flow recovery was measured at the proximal thigh to assess the adequacy of femoral artery ligation. No other study has assessed blood flow recovery in THBS5−/− mice. One study assessed blood flow recovery in THBS2−/− mice and found increased blood flow recovery at 2 weeks [ ]. However, this study assessed flow at the level of the gastrocnemius, thereby assessing the functionality of the arteriolar collateralization developed within the thigh. Given we assessed flow in the proximal thigh, we are unable to extrapolate our flow results to the arterioles measured. However, it is interesting to note that young, old, and OVX females displayed reduced blood flow recovery compared to males, suggesting there exists a chromosomal effect regulating the differences between the male and female hemodynamic response. Another factor that may contribute to the differences we found in arteriogenesis and blood flow recovery may be related to the functionality of the arterioles measured. Our study found that structurally, arterioles are formed, but functionality is yet to be determined.

4.4.

## Strengths, Limitations and Clinical Implications
There are strengths and limitations to our study. First, to our knowledge, this is the first study assessing the effect of aging, sex, TSP‐2, and TSP‐5 on angiogenesis and arteriogenesis. While we assessed angiogenesis and arteriogenesis development through immunohistochemistry, a limitation includes the lack of measurement of functional blood flow recovery. Although blood flow recovery would complement our results, the purpose of this study was to assess the more granular formation of capillaries and arterioles. Second, we did not measure sex hormones or standardize hindlimb ischemia to the female estrus cycle, which may increase the variability in results. Despite the lack of standardization, our WT results are comparable to previous studies. Last, we did not assess the interactions of specific angiogenic or arteriogenic genes with sex hormones. However, we did broadly elucidate sex differences not only in structural angiogenesis and arteriogenesis but within the genes, THBS2 and THBS5.
Future work is necessary to understand the molecular pathways causing the differential effects of THBS2 and THBS5 on angiogenesis and arteriogenesis; the current results have clinical implications. Given PAD is a disease of the aging, our results suggest arteriogenesis may be modulated through selective targeting of THBS2 or THBS5 in the proximal arterial bed. A mechanism to accomplish silencing of these genes would be through the local administration of small interfering RNA (siRNA) [ ] However, future work is necessary to determine whether selective targeting, rather than systemic, has similar effects seen in our study. Furthermore, given angiogenesis was unchanged in aging mice, our study suggests in the aging PAD population, targeting THBS2 or THBS5 may not be the best therapeutic option.

5.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, TSP‐2, TSP‐5, sex, and aging differentially regulate angiogenesis and arteriogenesis. We found (1) TSP‐2 is anti‐angiogenic in young mice and anti‐arteriogenic in old mice; (2) TSP‐5 does not significantly contribute to angiogenesis, but differentially affects arteriogenesis, with its effect largely anti‐arteriogenic; (3) females have reduced angiogenesis and arteriogenesis compared to males; (4) aging blunts the angiogenic and arteriogenic response; and (5) gene knockout modulates the sex‐specific and age‐specific responses to neovascularization. The results presented in this study broadly elucidate the role of TSP‐2, TSP‐5, and sex differences in PAD. In order to adequately develop therapies targeted to increase neovascularization in males and females, future studies assessing differences in male–female gene expression and hormonal interactions will be instrumental.
