## Introduction
Digestive system malignancies, including those affecting the stomach, colon, liver, and pancreas, rank among the prevalent solid neoplasms [ ]. The incidence of these conditions has significantly increased with the widespread adoption of high-fat, high-sugar diets and unhealthy lifestyle habits [ ]. Currently, while surgery remains the mainstay of curative treatment, patients with advanced-stage disease often require non-surgical treatments due to tumor metastasis and spread [ , ]. Traditional radiotherapy and chemotherapy have demonstrated efficacy to some extent but face two significant challenges [ ]. On one hand, unlike normal tissues, tumor margins lack clear boundaries, making it challenging for surgeons to determine the tumor's edges during surgery, which may result in incomplete removal [ ]. On the other hand, the severe systemic toxicities of chemotherapy drugs and the collateral damage radiotherapy causes to healthy cells significantly impact the quality of life for cancer patients [ ]. Therefore, developing new treatment strategies to address the shortcomings of traditional therapies is urgent [ ]. Sonodynamic therapy (SDT) has attracted significant interest in recent years, given its ability to harness sound waves to penetrate tissues and generate cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) to kill cancer cells, showing particular promise for deep-seated tumors [ ]. However, its application faces significant limitations [ ]: Indeed, the short half-life and limited diffusion range of ROS, make it challenging to effectively induce immunogenic cell death (ICD) [ ]. On the other hand, the poor water solubility of sonosensitizers hinders their intratumor accumulation [ , ]. During treatment, these agents exacerbate the tumor's hypoxic environment, which not only weakens the drug's efficacy but may also trigger metastasis, creating a vicious cycle [ , ]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop new treatment strategies to improve the tumor microenvironment, enhance the immune system's anti-cancer capabilities, and achieve precise targeting of cancer cells [ ]. These innovative therapies aim to improve the hypoxic environment, enhance drug efficacy and immune response, precisely identify and eliminate cancer cells, significantly reduce the risk of recurrence, and increase cure rates, providing safer and more effective treatment options for patients with gastrointestinal tumors [ , ].
Significant advancements in nanobiotechnology over the past few years have offered new approaches to overcoming these challenges [ , ]. Due to their unique size (1–100 nm) and high surface area-to-volume ratio, nanomaterials exhibit significant biological selectivity, enabling precise drug delivery to targets, significantly improving drug solubility, and extending half-life [ , ]. Compared to standalone SDT, nanosonosensitizers offer advantages through synergistic mechanisms. For example, when combined with immunotherapy, they activate anti-tumor immune responses and inhibit pancreatic cancer metastasis [ , ]. When integrated with photothermal therapy, they significantly reduce or even completely ablate tumor volume [ ]. Besides, their combination with chemodynamic therapy, is conducive to the tumor immune microenvironment remodeling in liver and colorectal cancers and decreased cancer cell antioxidant capacity [ , ]. Furthermore, when combined with drug delivery receptors, they enhance tumor-specific accumulation and reduce systemic toxicity [ ]. This paper will focus on the application of various nanosonosensitizers in combination with SDT for the treatment of gastrointestinal tumors, and will evaluate and compare the efficacy of different therapies.

## Principles and mechanisms of sonodynamic therapy

## Ultrasonic cavitation effect
Ultrasound (US) technology, characterized by its non-invasive nature and excellent tissue penetration, has become widely used in the medical field, particularly in cancer treatment, where it holds huge potential [ , ]. It not only enables the precise delivery of nanocarrier drugs to deeply situated tumor cells but also facilitates for targeted therapy by activating sonosensitizers [ , ]. The core mechanism of the ultrasound-induced cavitation effect [ , ]. This encompasses several complex processes, including bubble effects caused by mechanical oscillation, the generation of singlet oxygen and free radicals, sonoluminescence, and heat production [ , ]. The ultrasound cavitation effect refers to the process by which ultrasound can converts sound energy into a rapidly increased mechanical pressure, leading to the formation of tiny gas bubbles in the bodily tissue fluid environment [ , ]. Baased on the varying behavior of these bubbles, the cavitation effect can be divided into inertial cavitation and non-inertial cavitation [ , ]. Inertial cavitation is an extremely intense process in which bubbles rapidly expand and ultimately collapse and burst under continuous ultrasonic wave influence [ , ]. Conversely, non-inertial cavitation is relatively gentle process,which occurs when the sound field intensity is insufficient to cause bubble rupture, leading the remaining bubbles to oscillate and undergo periodic expansion or contraction [ ]. Firstly, under ultrasonic action, cavitation microbubbles near the cell membrane generate mechanical oscillations, causing the formation of transient micro-pores on the membrane surface. These micro-pores alter the state of the cell membrane, significantly enhancing its permeability and thereby improving the efficiency of intracellular drug transport [ , ] (Fig.  ). Secondly, through inertial cavitation, microbubbles can activate sonosensitizers to produce singlet oxygen via chemical effects [ , ]. In other words, the sonosensitizer, when stimulated by ultrasound energy, reacts with oxygen to form singlet oxygen. This form of oxygen serves as the primary harmful agent in sonodynamic therapy. Once its concentration reaches a sufficiently high level, it can cause irreversible damage to cancer cells [ , ] (Fig.  ). In addition, inertial cavitation bubbles can split to generate hydrogen atoms and hydroxyl radicals [ , ].Under the influence of ultrasound, these radicals further interact with nearby oxygen molecules, leading to the formation of peroxide radicals, alkoxy radicals, and other types of free radicals [ , ].This cascade of reactions ultimately results in the death of cancer cells [ , ] (Fig.  ). Meanwhile, the light emitted during bubble collapse can activate semiconductor-based sonosensitizers, which further generate ROS through oxidative stress [ , ]. This leads to DNA fragmentation, membrane damage, and the induction of immunogenic cell death [ ] (Fig.  ). Furthermore, under high-power ultrasound irradiation, some sonosensitizers can directly kill cancer cells through thermal effects, offering additional mechanistic support for SDT therapy [ ] (Fig.  ). 

## Induction of cancer cell apoptosis
Inducing cancer cell death is a core strategy in cancer therapy [ , ]. SDT not only activates sonosensitizers through ultrasound but may also exert its effects by inducing apoptosis in cancer cells [ ]. However, its specific mechanisms remain incompletely understood [ ]. The main pathways that promote tumor cell death are as follows: (1) the mitochondrial pathway; (2) the death receptor pathway; and (3) the intrinsic pathway [ , ]. Firstly, mitochondria, as the energy factories of cells, play a crucial role in the process of cancer cell self-destruction [ ]. When external stimuli increase mitochondrial membrane permeability, apoptotic inducing factors are released from the mitochondria into the cytoplasm, activating the apoptosis signaling cascade, which promotes cancer cell death [ ]. Besides, the death receptor pathway plays a crucial role in cancer cell apoptosis. The death receptors on the surface of cancer cells trigger a signaling cascade upon binding with their ligands, ultimately leading to cancer cell apoptosis [ , ]. Furthermore, the intrinsic pathway plays an irreplaceable role in cancer cell apoptosis. This pathway primarily relies on the direct or indirect actions of apoptotic regulatory genes, such as P53. By regulating the cell cycle and gene expression, it guides cancer cells toward apoptosis [ , ]. The mechanisms underlying SDT-induced apoptosis are as follows: (1) Ultrasound can induce damage to tumor endothelial cells, leading to platelet aggregation and blockage of nutrient supply. This affects cancer cell glucose metabolism, thereby triggering apoptosis (Fig.  ) [ ] (2) US can modulate the expression of the Bcl-2 gene, leading to the generation of significant amounts of ROS, which disrupt the oxidative phosphorylation process in cancer cells [ , ]. Oxidative phosphorylation is a crucial pathway for energy production in cancer cells [ , ]. Once this process is disrupted, the mitochondrial membrane potential in cancer cells is decreased, ultimately leading to cell apoptosis (Fig.  ) [ , ]. (3) SDT can induce to calcium overload within the mitochondria, altering membrane permeability, leading to release of cytochrome c and other apoptotic factors, as well as reducing mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby triggering cell death [ , ] (Fig.  ). (4) The expression of FAS/FASL in malignant cells is upregulated, activating the extrinsic apoptotic pathway and driving cancer cell death [ , ].

## Nanosonosensitizers combined with sonodynamic therapy
Sonosensitizers are key agents in enhancing the anti-cancer effects of ultrasound. Once activated by sound waves, they produce ROS, which kill cancer cells by inducing oxidative stress and immunogenic cell death, while simultaneously activating anti-tumor immune responses [ ]. With the advancement of nanobiotechnology, novel nanosonosensitizers have overcome the limitations of traditional drugs through precise targeting capabilities [ , ]. Their nanostructure can extend circulation time in the bloodstream, improve accumulation efficiency at tumor sites, and reduce damage to normal tissues due to high biological selectivity [ , ]. Given their unique electrical, magnetic, and optical biochemical properties, nanosonosensitizers further optimize ROS diffusion efficiency and synergistically enhance the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect [ , ]. This significantly improves treatment outcomes for advanced tumors and advances the development of anti-tumor therapies, bringing new hope to the field of cancer treatment [ , ].

## Organic nanosonosensitizers improve tumor hypoxia
Metal–organic nanomaterials, characterized by their tunable structures, high porosity, and diverse functionalities, demonstrate revolutionary potential for the technological advancement of sonodynamic therapy [ ]. Currently, metal–organic nanoframes used to the limitations of SDT's include smart probes like Ce6-Leu@Mn2+, TiO2/C nanocomposites, polymethyl methacrylate-coated CaO2 nanoparticles, and TBP@MOL, among others [ , ]. The design of these materials aims to precisely modulate the tumor microenvironment (TME) and systematically address the three major technical challenges faced by traditional SDT: tissue hypoxia, low efficiency of ROS generation, and insufficient targeting [ , ].
Metal–organic nanomaterials exhibited great potential in overcoming the limitations of sonodynamic therapy SDT. with the following two typical improvement strategies [ ]. Firstly, TiO₂ nanomaterials are widely recognized for their ultrasound-responsive properties, enabling the transfer of energy to O₂ molecules under ultrasound stimulation to generate ROS. [ ] (Table  ). However, pure TiO₂ materials face challenges such as instability and low energy conversion efficiency [ ] (Table  ). To address the inherent limitations of such materials, the team led by Cao Jing developed TiO2/C for the treatment of pancreatic cancer [ ] (Table  ). This organic nanocomposite material achieved a performance leap by enhanciing material stability and specifically adsorbsing H₂O₂ in the TME [ ] (Table  ). The mechanical stress from the ultrasonic cavitation effect triggers a cascade reaction, converting H₂O₂ into hydroxyl radicals (·OH) [ ] (Table  ). On the other hand, the sonoluminescence effect induces DNA double-strand breaks, significantly enhancing the apoptosis efficiency of pancreatic cancer cells [ ] (Table  ). Besides, the low toxicity, weak immunogenicity, and high biocompatibility of this nanomaterial ensure a safe option for long-term treatment of pancreatic cancer patients [ ] (Table  ; Fig.  ). Secondly, Ce6-Leu@Mn2⁺represents a powerful nanoprobes capable of significantly enhancing SDT effects under ultrasound activation [ ] (Table  ). Upon the dual consumption of leucine aminopeptidase (LAP) and reducing glutathione (GSH), the probe undergoes an enzyme-catalyzed condensation reaction to form smaller nanoparticles [ ] (Table  ). These nanoprobes not only enhance tissue penetration but also activate photoacoustic signaling pathways [ ]. It can degrade hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) in cancer tissue into oxygen (O2), alleviating tumor hypoxia and improving the SDT efficacy in liver cancer treatment [ ] (Fig.  ; Table  ). 
Conversely, pH-sensitive polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA)-coated CaO₂ nanoparticles (RBcNp) have demonstrated great anticancer potential [ ] (Table  ). Research has shown that the polymethacrylate coating of these nanoparticles rapidly hydrolyzes in the weakly acidic environment of the TME (pH 6.4–7.0), releasing the CaO₂ core [ ]. The reaction between CaO₂ and H⁺ produces Ca2⁺ and H₂O₂, which not only neutralizes the acidic tumor microenvironment but also generates O₂ through catalase-mediate, alleviating tumor hypoxia. Following hydrolysis, the release of Ca2⁺ can induce calcium overload in the mitochondria of cancer cells, thereby reducing mitochondrial membrane potential and inducing cancer cell death [ ] (Table  ). Furthermore, these nanoparticles exhibit a robust SDT-mediated abscopal effect on off-target tumors, showing significant efficacy even against metastatic pancreatic cancer [ ] (Table  ). Metastatic pancreatic cancer represents a highly malignant and therapeutically challenging tumor, and the development of RBcNp offers new hope for its treatment [ ] (Table  ). Moreover, TBP@MOL nanomaterials have shown unique advantages in treating digestive tract tumors [ ] (Table  ). Research conducted using a mouse colorectal cancer model- revealed that this nano-sonosensitizer undergoes triplet–triplet Dexter energy transfer from the excited-state DBB-Ir bridging ligand to the TBP SS anchored at the SBU, effectively capturing sonoluminescence generated by ultrasonic cavitation. Simultaneously, it enhances the generation and utilization of O2 by the excited-state TBP sensitizer, thereby effectively mitigating the hypoxia induced by the standalone TBP sonosensitizer in tumor tissues [ ] (Fig.  ; Table  ). Compared to the pure TBP sonosensitizer, TBP@MOL exhibits stronger cytotoxicity, thus offering a clear advantage in therapeutic outcomes [ , ]. These organic nanomaterials not only enhance the efficacy of SDT but also alleviate tumor hypoxia, providing new approaches for the treatment of malignant digestive tract cancers [ , ] (Table  ).
 

## Non-metal nanosonosensitizers regulate anti-tumor immunity
Non-metal organic nanomaterials offer a novel approach to overcoming the inherent limitations of sonodynamic therapy SDT. IR780@O2-FHMONs and protease B-degradable glutamic acid-tyrosine copolymer nanoparticles (HPNP) have significantly enhanced the treatment efficacy for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma through synergistic mechanisms [ , ]. On one hand, organic silica nanoparticles (IR780@O2-FHMONs) utilize the unique structure of their Fc chains to provide efficient binding sites for O2 [ ] (Table  ). By employing a dual oxygen-supply mechanism, they overcome the hypoxic barrier of tumors: ultrasound radiation activates the conversion of adsorbed oxygen into free oxygen while the nanoparticles continuously release stored oxygen [ ] (Table  ). This not only mitigates the tumor microenvironment's resistance to SDT due to hypoxia but also enhances ROS generation, precisely targeting and killing pancreatic cancer cells [ ] (Table  ). This material elevates the efficacy of SDT in highly aggressive tumors to a new level [ ] (Table  ; Fig.  ). On the other hand, addressing the immune-suppressive microenvironment characteristic of pancreatic cancer, HPNP nanoparticles act by targeting regulatory T cells (Tregs) [ ] (Table  ). They specifically inhibit the activity of CTLA-4-expressing Tregs, alleviating the suppression on dendritic cell function and restoring the anti-tumor immune response of effector T cells [ ] (Table  ). Moreover, these nanoparticles enhance acoustic energy deposition efficiency, thereby synergistically increasing the cytotoxicity of SDT, significantly inhibiting cancer cell proliferation and colony formation capabilities [ ] (Table  ). By optimizing the tumor microenvironment and increasing treatment efficiency, these non-metal organic nanomaterials provide a robust complement to SDT treatment strategies and offer a new paradigm for the precise treatment of pancreatic cancer, a malignancy known for its aggressive and immune-suppressive characteristics [ , ] (Tables  , ).

## Inorganic metal nanonosensitizers effectively promote cancer cell death
Inorganic metal nanocomplexes, owing to their excellent acoustic responsiveness and chemical properties, have become important research hotspots in SDT [ , ]. Current research focuses mainly on Ru(II)-polypyridine complexes, Ir(III)-porphyrin complexes, MnO₂ composite hydrogel systems, and collagenase-loaded TiO₂ nanoparticles, which have shown remarkable performance in enhancing reactive oxygen species ROS generation and modulating the tumor microenvironment TME [ , ]. Firstly, Ru(II)-polypyridine complexes due to their chemical stability and sonosensitivity,serve as efficient sonosensitizers [ ]. Under ultrasound irradiation, they can stably generate singlet oxygen and disrupt tumor cell metabolic pathways by converting NADH⁺ to NAD⁺ through oxidative phosphorylation mechanisms [ ]. Besides, their water solubility and biocompatibility allow them to remain active in complex in vivo environments, achieving precise anti-cancer effects [ ] (Fig.  ). Secondly, water-soluble Ir(III)-porphyrin complexes exhibit a unique sonophotodynamic synergy: the luminescence wavelength induced by ultrasound matches the absorption spectrum of the sonosensitizer, significantly enhancing ROS production (Fig.  ). This material also efficiently generates oxygen, alleviating TME hypoxia and thus enhancing the inhibitory effect of SDT on tumor growth [ ] (Table  ). Thirdly, a nanosystem composed of alginate, MnO₂, and the glutathione (GSH) inhibitor BSO can be targeted to tumors via local injection [ ] (Table  ). MnO₂ releases oxygen and consumes GSH, while BSO blocks the antioxidant defense system, synergistically increasing ROS levels and significantly accelerating colorectal cancer cell death [ ] (Table  ). Finally, using collagenase-loaded hollow TiO₂ nanoparticles (Col-H-TiO₂ NPs) in conjunction with SDT presents multiple advantages [ ] (Fig.  ; Table  ). This material enhances the ultrasound cavitation effect through its mesoporous structure, promoting the release of collagenase to degrade tumor stromal fibers, reducing stromal interstitial pressure, and increasing vascular density [ ] (Fig.  ; Table  ). The nanoparticles also provide more active sites by increasing the effective surface area, further boosting the catalytic activity of collagenase, efficiently converting H₂O₂ into O₂, alleviating hypoxia, and inducing a large amount of ROS, demonstrating potent cytotoxic effects against solid tumors such as pancreatic cancer [ ] (Fig.  ; Table  ). The application of these inorganic metal nanomaterials significantly enhances the efficiency of SDT in tumor treatment, especially in terms of increasing ROS generation and promoting cancer cell death [ , ] (Table  ). These materials hold great potential in improving the therapeutic efficacy of SDT, offering new hope for the treatment of aggressive cancers [ , ] (Table  ).

## Nanosonosensitizers combined with immunotherapy enhance immune response efficacy
Tumor immunotherapy functions by activating the immune system to recognize cancer cells, and the advent of immune checkpoint blockade therapy has significantly improved the treatment outcomes for gastrointestinal tumors [ , ]. However, challenges such as hypoxia and immune suppression in the TME continue to limit efficacy [ , ]. The introduction of novel nanomaterials offers innovative solutions to overcome these bottlenecks [ , ]. On one hand, sonication-activated semiconductor nanoremodelers (SPNDNH) play a crucial role in tumor immunotherapy [ ]. Activated by ultrasound to produce a sonodynamic effect, these nanoremodelers degrade hyaluronic acid in the TME while releasing oxygen, alleviating hypoxia and promoting nanoparticle accumulation [ ] (Fig.  ; Table  ). Their dual-action mechanism includes: 1.inducing ICD to activate dendritic cells; 2.simultaneously delivering IDO inhibitors to block the tryptophan metabolism pathway, reversing immune suppression [ ] (Fig.  ; Table  ). Besides, SPNDNH release H₂S through cavitation effects, inhibiting cytochrome c oxidase, reducing oxygen consumption, and further improving oxygen supply in the TME, significantly enhancing the anti-pancreatic cancer immune response [ ] (Fig.  ; Table  ). On the other hand, research on Fe₃O₄@TiO₂ nanoparticles (FTV NPs) also has yielded new breakthroughs in the field of immunotherapy [ ] (Fig.  ; Table  ). First, this nanosystem integrates VISTA monoclonal antibodies with piezoelectric material properties, offering abundant nanobinding sites. Under ultrasound radiation, it generates a large amount of ROS and releases NO gas through the piezoelectric response to mechanical stress, synergistically destroying cancer cells with ROS [ , ] (Fig.  ; Table  ). Second, VISTA monoclonal antibodies bind to ligands on the cancer cell membrane, initiating an immune response, functioning as immune checkpoint blockers, and activating T cells to kill cancer cells [ ] (Fig.  ; Table  ) Moreover, its mesoporous structure can adsorb hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) in the TME, enhancing the ultrasound cavitation effect and promoting immune cell infiltration, thereby boosting the anti-tumor immune response [ ] (Fig.  ; Table  ). Therefore, the synergistic effect of FTV NPs significantly boosts the efficiency of immune therapy for pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and shows promising application prospects, especially for innovative treatment strategies targeting deep pancreatic cancer [ ] (Fig.  ; Table  ). These innovative nanocarriers not only enhance the efficacy of both SDT and immunotherapy but also provide new directions for the in-depth study of cancer immunotherapy, driving advances in tumor treatment [ , ] (Table  ).

## Sonodynamic therapy combined with multiple techniques and carriers
The clinical application of nanosonosensitizers for tumor treatment faces dual challenges: drug penetration barriers caused by the heterogeneity of the TME and the difficulty of eradicating advanced metastatic tumors [ ]. Research has shown that the limitations of single treatment modalities in eliminating advanced malignant tumors [ ]. Current combination therapies primarily exhibit three synergistic mechanisms: combining with PTT to achieve both tumor ablation and recurrence inhibition; (Tables  , ) integrating with CDT to overcome the tumor's antioxidant defense system by reducing intracellular GSH levels; (Tables  , ) and incorporating PD-L1 inhibitors to induce ICD via sonodynamic effects, significantly enhancing anti-tumor immune responses [ , ] (Tables  , ). This multi-dimensional intervention not only overcomes the efficacy limitations of single therapies but also produces a synergistic sensitization effect by improving TME characteristics, such as correcting hypoxia and reshaping the immunosuppressive microenvironment [ , ]. With the ongoing process in precision medicine, this pathology-based combination therapy paradigm is expected to shift tumor treatment from a broad-spectrum approach to personalized diagnosis and therapy, providing new solutions for metastatic malignant tumors [ , ].
 

## Nanosonosensitizers combined with photothermal therapy (PTT) can significantly shrink tumor volume
Photothermal therapy (PTT), as an important innovative approach in tumor treatment, demonstrates substantial anti-cancer potential through its unique mechanism [ ]. PTT harnesses specific photothermal agents to convert near-infrared light energy into heat, rapidly raising the local temperature of the tumor, thereby effectively killing cancer cells [ ]. Recently, the combination of SDT and PTT has been reported to enhance anti-cancer effects [ ]. Relevant nanoparticle-based sonosensitizers include Pt-CuS-Janus, 131I/AN@D/IX, and CR-PEG-GBP [ ].The key role of photothermal agents (PTAs) lies at the core of PTT. PTAs absorb light energy and quickly convert it into heat energy [ ]. Compared to organic materials, inorganic materials exhibit significantly better photothermal conversion performance and photothermal stability, making them more promising for PTT applications [ ]. Firstly, inorganic materials like Pt-Cus-Janus have become central to PTT due to their excellent photothermal properties [ ]. The hollow structure of Cus facilitates the loading of the sonosensitizer TAPP, and under 808 nm laser irradiation, the Pt nanoparticles catalyze the conversion of H₂O₂ into oxygen, alleviating tumor hypoxia and enhancing the synergistic effects of SDT and PTT [ ]. This approach achieves thorough tumor eradication with a very low recurrence rate [ ] (Table  ). Secondly, 131I/AN@D/IX, composed of polydopamine-modified gold nanostars loaded with protoporphyrin IX and 131I, offers biocompatibility, high photothermal efficiency, and drug-loading capacity [ ]. In pancreatic cancer models, the combination of SDT and PDT minimizes tumor volume with significant efficacy [ ] (Table  ). Furthermore, integrating photoacoustic imaging with PTT/SDT enhances the targeting and efficacy of liver cancer treatments, overcoming the limitations of single therapies [ ] (Table  ). Similar studies have demonstrated the high-efficiency synergistic effects of using graphene's thermal conductivity and TiO₂'s sonosensitive properties in various tumor treatments [ ] (Table  ). Notably, the hydride titanium nanomodel (TIH1.924) possesses both sonosensitizing and photothermal properties. Under ultrasound activation, it not only generates a significant amount of ROS but also enhances intratumoral blood flow and oxygenation through a mild photothermal effect. Furthermore, this nanocarrier exhibits a high clearance rate in vivo. As a result, the design of this nanocarrier not only demonstrates a highly effective synergistic effect in cancer therapy but also significantly reduces its toxicity [ ]. However, there are still limitations to these combination therapies [ , ]. Research indicates that the localized heating effect of the photothermal process can lead to the expression of heat shock proteins, which may reduce the effectiveness of subsequent treatments by disrupting tumor cell membrane structures [ , ] (Table  ). Besides, some photothermal materials may remain in the body, potentially causing inflammatory responses [ , ] (Table  ). 

## Nanosonosensitizers combined with chemodynamic therapy (CDT) can reduce the antioxidant capacity of cancer cells
The synergistic integration of chemodynamic therapy (CDT) and sonodynamic therapy (SDT) has paved the way for a new therapeutic approach against cancer treatment [ , ]. By activating catalysts and H₂O₂ in the tumor microenvironment, CDT can induce the Fenton reaction to produce highly cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals (·OH), offering lower systemic toxicity compared to traditional chemotherapy [ , ]. The limitations of SDT in hypoxic microenvironments have led to breakthrough advancements in multifunctional nanomaterials [ , ]. Firstly, the novel AIPH@Cu-MOF nanosystem demonstrates potential for multimodal therapy [ ] (Table  ). The Cu2+-mediated Fenton-like reaction continuously generates ROS [ ] (Table  ). Its metal–organic framework (MOF) structure not only enables precise drug release but also promotes the decomposition of AIPH into cytotoxic radicals under ultrasound activation, simultaneously depleting glutathione (GSH) to weaken the tumor's antioxidant defenses [ ] (Table  ). The nitrogen gas bubbles produced can enhance tumor penetration depth, showing significant efficacy in pancreatic cancer models [ ] (Table  ).
Chlorin e6 (Ce6) is a highly effective sonosensitizer;however,its therapeutic efficacy is limited due to its high clearance rate and low accumulation within tumors when in its free form [ ]. To address the delivery challenges of sonosensitizers, the biomimetic nanocatalyst MBFC, featuring an Fe₃O₄ core modified with bovine serum albumin and camouflaged with a cancer cell membrane, has demonstrated significantly enhanced targeting treatment efficiency [ ] (Table  ). Under the synergistic action of SDT/CDT, its ability to catalyze the conversion of H₂O₂ to ·OH potently inhibits colon cancer [ ] (Table  ). Similarly, the Janus-Au-MnO nanoplateform employs an ultrasound/GSH dual-responsive mechanism to enable controlled release of Mn2+ at the tumor site, enhancing the CDT effect while generating gold nanoparticles for second near-infrared (NIR-II) photoacoustic imaging [ ] (Table  ). This multimodal system significantly enhances the apoptosis rate of liver cancer cells and provides real-time treatment monitoring capabilities [ ] (Table  ). Interestingly, H@PLA@CCM demonstrates excellent stability in saline solutions while retaining the activity of CCM proteins, enhancing internalization and phagocytosis efficiency in CT26 cells [ ] (Table  ). When administered intravenously, this carrier exhibits a prolonged half-life and, when combined with SDT, significantly increases the mortality rate of colorectal cancer cells,thus advancing the field [ ] (Table  ). Although combination therapies have demonstrated significant advantages, two major technical bottlenecks must be overcome: the systemic toxicity caused by the premature release of metal ions needs to be controlled through targeted modifications, and the heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment pH necessitates the development of adaptive catalytic materials [ , ] (Table  ). Current research has demonstrated that the combination of CDT and SDT can produce a cascade amplification effect with—SDT-induced microbubble cavitation enhancing nanoparticle penetration and disrupting cell membranes through mechanical stress, promoting ROS diffusion [ , ] (Table  ). This dual-mode physical–chemical attack strategy shows high local control rates in the treatment of solid tumors such as colorectal cancer and liver cancer [ , ] (Table  ).

## Nanosonosensitizers embedded with drugs boost effectiveness and elevate safety standards
Traditional chemotherapy faces clinical challenges due to severe side effects, drug resistance, and insufficient drug accumulation at tumor sites. In recent years, nanosonosensitizer platforms have opened new avenues for the treatment of gastrointestinal tumors by integrating SDT, targeted delivery, and immune modulation functions [ ]. Various drug-loaded nanomaterials have been developed, including SOR-TCPP@PEG-FA multifunctional nanoparticles (STP NPs), ZTC@M nanoparticles, and magnetic-responsive microbubbles (MagMB) [ , ]. Firstly, STP NPs, activated by ultrasound, generate oxygen radicals to effectively kill liver cancer cells while simultaneously releasing sorafenib, achieving a synergy between chemotherapy and SDT [ ].This approach significantly reduces drug dosage while greatly enhancing therapeutic efficacy.[2]ZTC@M nanoparticles utilize a ZIF-8 framework to encapsulate Ce6 and tirapazamine, leveraging the hypoxic characteristics of the TME to achieve ROS-activated dual cytotoxic effects [ ]. The synergy between SDT and chemotherapy significantly induces gastric cancer cell death, offering more advanced treatment strategies for gastric cancer patients [ ]. Besides, MagMB can be precisely guided to pancreatic cancer sites under an external magnetic field [ ]. In mouse experiments, the intravenous delivery of MagO₂Mb combined with SDT showed significantly better efficacy than SDT alone, demonstrating high specificity and safety, thus offering a novel solution for pancreatic cancer patients [ ] (Table  ). Moreover, BaTiO₃ nanoparticles exhibit significant piezoelectric catalytic activity under ultrasound irradiation, generating a large amount of ROS to kill tumor cells [ ] (Table  ). The piezoelectric effect of BaTiO₃ NPs also enhances cell membrane permeability, promoting the intracellular accumulation of chemotherapeutic drugs [ ] (Table  ). This dual synergistic effect allows for reduced dosages of chemotherapy drugs, providing a new treatment strategy for colorectal cancer patients [ ] (Table  ). However, a limitation of these therapies is that they do not significantly reduce drug resistance, as tumor stem cells may escape combination therapy through multidrug resistance genes [ , ] (Table  ). This highlights the need for further research to address the challenge of drug resistance, particularly in targeting tumor stem cells, to fully realize the potential of these advanced therapeutic strategies [ , ]. 
Oral nanomedicines offer favorable patient compliance and can directly target the colorectal mucosa [ ]. However, their main drawback is their poor penetration, which hinders effective delivery to deep tumor tissues, leading to poor anti-cancer efficacy [ ]. This limitation significantly affects their use in cancer therapy and restricts their clinical application [ ]. To overcome this issue, researchers have developed a novel approach using nanomotors (CS-ID@NMs) [ ]. These nanomotors can be activated by US radiation in the TME, allowing them to penetrate the mucus layer of the colon and reach the cancerous tissue, addressing the shortcomings of traditional nanomedicines [ ]. Initially, the colorectal mucus barrier is traversed through CD44-mediated targeted endocytosis, facilitating deep tumor penetration under ultrasound activation [ ]. Next, under specific stimuli (such as H+, ROS, or US), the nanomotors release bioactive agents that specifically target the cancer cells, exerting anti-cancer effects [ ]. In vivo experiments have shown that the nanomotors not only significantly inhibit the growth of primary tumors but also enhance the overall immune response and stimulate long-term immune memory, thereby inhibiting tumor growth and reducing the risk of recurrence, providing long-term protection for the patients [ ] (Table  ).
PD-1 (Programmed Cell Death Protein 1, CD279) is a critical immune checkpoint receptor primarily expressed on the surface of T lymphocytes [ , ]. In many types of cancer, especially gastrointestinal tumors, the expression of PD-L1 (Programmed Cell Death Ligand 1) is significantly upregulated in the TME [ , ]. Through binding with PD-1, PD-L1 inhibits T cell proliferation and activation, primarily via two key signaling pathways: PI3 K/AKT and Ras/MAPK/ERK [ , ]. PD-L1 not only plays a role within the TME but is also expressed on dendritic cells (DC), natural killer (NK) cells, B cells, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, further exacerbating the tumor’s immunosuppressive environment [ , ]. The interaction between PD-1 and PD-L1 drives T cells into an immune tolerance state, thereby weakening their ability to attack cancer cells [ , ] (Fig.  ). Nanobubble-based sonosensitizers represent an emerging cancer therapy [ ] (Fig.  ). By using US to activate encapsulated sonosensitizers, these agents can induce Immunogenic Cell Death (ICD) and release tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) [ ] (Fig.  ). This activation stimulates DCs and tumor-specific cytotoxic T cells (CD8 + T cells). Furthermore, activated T cells express PD-1 and CD80 on their surface [ ] (Fig.  ). The binding of PD-1 to PD-L1 disrupts immune checkpoint signaling, thereby potentiating T cell-mediated tumor cell lysis and apoptotic induction [ ] (Fig.  ). On the other hand, the interaction between CD80 and PD-L1 facilitates reverse inhibitory signaling, promoting T cell interaction with tumor cells, thereby enhancing immune responses and aiding in the elimination of the primary tumor [ ] (Fig.  ). For instance, multifunctional nanobubbles like sPD-1/ce6-NBs,which combine soluble PD-1 (sPD-1) with the sonosensitizer Ce6, have been used to deliver sPD-1 in a targeted manner [ ]. The ultrasound-activated SDT further enhances the effects of immune checkpoint blockade by driving the secretion of cytokines, boosting NK cells and CTLs to kill cancer cells, thereby significantly improving the synergistic effect of immunotherapy [ ] (Table  ). In addition, the IR@siRNA nanosystem enhances DNA damage by silencing the Nrf2 gene, inducing ICD in colorectal cancer cells and activating CD8 + T cells, significantly promoting ICD in these cancer cells [ ]. In vivo experiments have shown that this nanodelivery system effectively inhibits the growth of colorectal cancer cells in mice and enhances T cell infiltration into the tumor, offering a novel therapeutic strategy for metastatic colorectal cancer patients [ ] (Table  ). Moreover, TiO2-Ce6-CpG nanobubbles combine SDT with the CpG immune stimulant to enhance immune responses and improve the efficacy of immunotherapy [ ]. When combined with PD-L1 blockade, the synergistic effects of SDT demonstrate potent tumor-suppressive activity in both primary and metastatic liver cancers, providing new prospects for liver cancer treatment [ ] (Table  ). Besides, PMPS NDs enhance vascular penetration through the ultrasound cavitation effect [ ]. After several hours, the drug accumulates in the tumor, releasing therapeutic components that induce endoplasmic reticulum stress, ICD, and dendritic cell maturation. Through these synergistic actions, PMPS NDs significantly enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy in pancreatic cancer and its metastatic variants [ ]. Furthermore, NCG nanomedicines, loaded with Ce6 and β-receptor inhibitors, generate ROS under ultrasound guidance to trigger ICD and provoke immune responses [ ]. These nanomedicines enhance immune responses within the TME, promote dendritic cell maturation, and support the immune system's reaction to tumors [ ]. By releasing galactose (Gal), they induce the differentiation of MDSCs, disrupting the immune protective mechanisms within the tumor and further improving the efficacy of anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy [ ]. Therefore, NCG holds huge potential for treating refractory colorectal cancer [ ]. Similar studies with ICG-C3 F8-R848 nanobubbles have shown that they not only trigger strong apoptotic effects but also promote the conversion of macrophages from the M2 to the M1 phenotype, enhancing tumor defense immune responses [ ]. Research also indicates that this nanomedicine can significantly inhibit liver cancer growth and achieve highly effective and comprehensive therapeutic outcomes, providing a breakthrough for liver cancer treatment [ ] (Table  ). 
In summary, the highly efficient synergistic effects of drug-loaded nanobubble sonosensitizers not only reduce the required dosage of chemotherapy drugs and their associated side effects but also effectively overcome the immune escape mechanisms of tumors [ , ] (Table  ). These therapies enhance ICD and trigger stronger T cell-mediated immune responses [ , ] (Table  ). However, this therapy still faces challenges, such as the potential for SDT-induced inflammatory responses to exacerbate PD-1 inhibitor-related pneumonia/enteritis, and the possibility of tumor stem cells escaping combination treatment through multidrug resistance genes [ , ] (Table  ). If these bottlenecks can be overcome, leveraging nanotechnology for targeted delivery and combining SDT with immune checkpoint blockade therapy and immune stimulants, there is significant potential toenhance the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy [ , ] (Table  ). This would provide cancer patients with more effective and safer treatment options [ , ] (Table  ).

## Toxicity and side effects of nanomaterials
Nanomaterials pose significant risks of side effects in practical applications [ , ]. Firstly, aerosolized nanoparticles can enter the body through the respiratory tract, penetrate the alveolar-capillary barrier, and interact with the microbiome, triggering the release of pro-inflammatory factors that lead to airway inflammation, pneumonia, and even pulmonary fibrosis [ , ]. Carbon-based nanomaterials have been shown to induce the death of alveolar macrophages, triggering localized inflammatory responses [ , ]. Secondly, owing to their ultra-small size, specific nanoparticles (such as single-walled carbon nanotubes) can penetrate the blood–brain barrier after intravenous injection, causing neural damage and resulting in neurotoxic symptoms such as dizziness and vomiting [ ]. More seriously, these particles may accumulate in cardiac tissue within the circulatory system, inducing oxidative stress and myocardial dysfunction by disrupting mitochondrial membrane potential and interfering with calcium ion balance [ , ]. Silicon dioxide nanoparticles, for example, can reduce mitochondrial membrane potential, hinder oxidative phosphorylation, and generate excessive free radicals leading to myocardial hypoxia, and even cause fatal arrhythmias by interfering with sodium and potassium ion channels [ ]. Moreover, nanomaterials have the potential to disrupt the skin barrier, with their surface adhesion stimulating epithelial cells and causing dermatitis and allergic reactions [ , ]. Notably, the multiple toxic mechanisms of silicon dioxide nanoparticles illustrate the complexity of nanotoxicology—by disrupting cardiac electrophysiological stability and affecting ion channel function, which escalates the difficulty of health risk assessment due to their multi-target effects [ , ]. Current research confirms that the toxicity of nanomaterials is closely related to their size, surface charge, and chemical properties, indicating the need to establish more precise toxicological evaluation systems in medical applications [ , ].

## Clinical translation of SDT
Based on the aforementioned theories, SDT demonstrates huge potential for clinical applications in gastrointestinal tumors, especially when combined with nanobiotechnology, which holds significant clinical value for treating these cancers. Although there are currently no reported clinical applications, the prospects for translation are highly optimistic. For example, the integration of novel sonosensitizers, such as dihydroporphyrins and porphyrin derivatives, with nanocarriers not only enables responsive release in the tumor microenvironment but also enhances the specific targeting and destruction of gastrointestinal tumors while simultaneously generating ROS and inhibiting autophagy. Moreover, these technologies can improve the accumulation efficiency of the drugs in tumors, reduce systemic toxicity, and promote the advancement of precision treatment for gastrointestinal cancers, offering new strategies for targeting deeply situated tumors. Future clinical studies may leverage wearable ultrasound patches and other devices to achieve continuous and controlled treatment of deep tumors. Coupled with artificial intelligence-driven dynamic adjustments of ultrasound parameters, this approach could enhance treatment accuracy and minimize damage to normal tissues. Besides, the ability of SDT to induce the release of tumor antigens may improve the response rates to immunotherapy. Future research endeavors should assess the specific regulatory mechanisms of SDT on immune cells within the tumor microenvironment, such as T cells and macrophages, and explore its potential applications in activating immunity in"cold tumors". Importantly, there is a need to establish standardized clinical evaluation systems to identify the optimal patient populations for SDT and to validate its long-term efficacy and safety through large-scale, multicenter clinical trials. Furthermore, interdisciplinary collaboration, including fields such as materials science, imaging, and immunology, will be key to advancing the clinical translation of SDT.

## Conclusion and prospects
As SDT emerges as a promising new approach to cancer treatment, it demonstrates advantages over traditional therapies, including greater tissue penetration, broader therapeutic range, and lower cost. When combined with other technologies, the benefits of SDT become even more pronounced, resulting in enhanced efficacy, reduced side effects, and improved safety. In recent years, with the advancement of medical research, there has been an increasing focus on combining SDT with nanosensitizers for treating gastrointestinal tumors. Currently, the integration of multiple carriers and technologies in SDT has become a focal point of research, showing significant synergistic effects in the treatment of gastrointestinal cancers. However, despite some positive outcomes in the field of tumor treatment, translating the advantages of SDT into practical clinical applications still faces numerous challenges. There is an urgent need to develop nanosensitizers with better biocompatibility, enhanced stability, increased targeting capabilities, and improved safety to advance the development and application of SDT. Overall, optimizing strategies for SDT in treating gastrointestinal tumors requires ongoing exploration. Researchers must focus on developing nanosensitizers that offer high stability, biocompatibility, efficacy, safety, prolonged half-lives, and minimal side effects. Looking ahead, the combination of SDT with nanomaterials holds promise for minimally invasive treatment of difficult-to-treat deep gastrointestinal tumors, as well as for postoperative adjuvant therapy and prevention of recurrence. Designing multimodal nanoplatforms based on tumor molecular subtyping to achieve a"demand-driven"combination therapy strategy will be a key area of focus in research. These advancements will provide critical support for clinical translation, offering new hope and improved quality of life for cancer patients.
