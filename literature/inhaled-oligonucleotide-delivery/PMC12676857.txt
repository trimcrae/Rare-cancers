## Abstract
Immunotherapy has achieved remarkable progress in treating cancers that evade immune surveillance. Among the components of the tumor microenvironment, macrophages play important roles in maintaining homeostasis, preventing pathogen invasion, engulfing and promoting the adaptive immune response. It is acknowledged that blocking the CD24–Siglec-10 interaction significantly enhances the macrophage phagocytosis ability. In this study, we identified CD24 as an over-expressed molecule in ovarian and breast cancer using data from the GEO database and clinical samples. We then developed genetically programmable extracellular vesicles displaying CD24 single-chain variable fragment (CD24 scFv-EVs) and evaluated their ability to restore macrophage phagocytic activity and eliminate CD24-overexpressing cancer cells. Our findings demonstrate that CD24 scFv-EVs effectively block CD24 on ovarian cancer cells as well as breast cancer cells, thereby inhibiting the CD24–Siglec-10 pathway and enhancing macrophage-mediated clearance of cancer cells. Furthermore, CD24 scFv-EVs promote the polarization of tumor-infiltrated macrophages toward an M1-like phenotype. These results highlight CD24 blockade as a novel therapeutic strategy to target ovarian and breast cancer cells and enhance macrophage-driven tumor cell clearance.

## Introduction
The intricate cell interactions and communications between tumor cells and neighboring immune cells involves complex competitive-cooperative patterns rewired through immune signals, which are essential for shaping the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) [ ]. It is vital to exploit the novel therapeutic strategies based on the cross-talks and communications among the tumor microenvironments.
Ovarian cancer is the most challenging malignancies in reproductive system and remains the leading cause of gynecologic cancer-related deaths [ ]. While debulking surgery combined with platinum-based chemotherapy is the standard treatment, recurrence occurs in 25% to 80% of patients highlighting the urgent need for more effective therapeutic strategies [ , ].
Breast cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths among women worldwide [ ]. It is a highly heterogeneous disease, characterized by distinct molecular subtypes, including hormone receptor-positive (HR+), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive (HER2+), and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). These subtypes differ significantly in their biological behavior, prognosis, and response to therapy. Despite advances in early detection, surgical techniques, and systemic therapies such as endocrine therapy, chemotherapy, and targeted agents, breast cancer remains a major health challenge, particularly in advanced or metastatic stages [ ].
Increasing evidence had witnessed that the remarkable success of immune checkpoint therapies (including PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and CTLA-4 related therapies) in activating immune responses against cancers and subsequent significant improvements in patient outcomes. However, most of the currently immune checkpoint inhibitors acquired limited progress in ovarian cancer field. CD24 is a heavily glycosylated glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored surface protein [ ]. Several studies showed that CD24 and Siglec-10 could selectively repress tissue damage–induced immune responses [ , ]. Some studies have shown that CD24 could interact with Siglec-10 on innate immune cells and subsequently inhibit the innate immune system as well as to avoid clearance by macrophages to achieve the immune escape [ ]. Since some studies reported the CD24 expression in several solid tumors including ovarian cancers [ ] and breast cancers [ ] is higher than counterpart normal tissues [ , ], which are associated to the outcome of these cancers [ ]. CD24-Siglec10 immune checkpoint has been recognized as a promising therapeutic target in ovarian cancer therapy [ ] and breast cancers therapy [ – ]. Increasing studies reported that the CD24 monoclonal antibody (mAb) could act as blockade [ ] to promote the anti-tumor immunity [ ] and subsequently inhibit the tumor growth. Increasing studies showed that antibody-based treatments against CD24 have been designed [ ] and evaluated. However, the uncertain side effects and safety concerns still limited the application of the antibody-based therapies and antibody-drug conjugate-based therapies [ ].
The strategy of genetic engineered extracellular vesicles (EVs) has been acknowledged as a novel drug delivery approach for some innate advantages including excellent biocompatibility and high stability and possess the unique ability to deliver therapeutic agents to specific locations within the body without triggering immune reactions or unexpected toxicity [ ]. Increasing studies showed that the engineered EVs could express diverse ligands outsider the EVs membrane, and activate/block the signaling pathways between cells communications [ , ].
Thus, in this study, we analyzed the CD24 expression in ovarian cancer and breast cancer. Subsequently we genetically engineered EVs, which presenting CD24 scFv as immune inhibitory molecules, to suppress the “phagocytosis-blocked” signal via blocking CD24. And we explored the therapeutic effects of CD24 scFv-EVs in restoring the phagocytic effect as well as regulating the immune micro-environment, to suppress the progression of cancers. Overall, the present study introduces a novel and effective strategy utilizing CD24 scfv-EVs to enhance antitumor efficacy, offering valuable insights into cellular immunotherapy for cancer treatment.

## Methods and materials

## Cell culture
SKOV-3 cells (SCSP-5214), MCF-7 cells (SCSP-531) and THP-1 cells (SCSP-567) were generously provided by Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences. SKOV-3mCherry cells were gifts from Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University. Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, MA, USA) with 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin-Gentamicin Solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and 20% FBS (Gibco, MA, USA).

## Clinical samples
Fresh tissues were collected from ovarian cancer patients who underwent ovarian cancer debulking surgery (n = 10) and non-ovarian cancer participants (for benign disease samples) at Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University from 2019 to 2023 (Supplementary Table 1). Fresh tissues were collected from women diagnosed as breast cancer (n = 10) and non-breast cancer women (for benign disease samples) at Affiliated Hospital of Youjiang Medical University for Nationalities from 2019 to 2024 (Supplementary Table 2). Tissues were made of section to detect the CD24 expression through the immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay.

## Immunohistochemistry
For histological analysis, tissues were dissected and fixed in a mix of 4% Paraformaldehyde (PFA), followed by embedding and section. Tissue sections were subjected to IHC analysis as described previously. The primary antibodies were as follows: anti-CD24 (1: 100, ab202073, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). The secondary antibodies were horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit immunoglobulin-G antibody (1: 1000, ab6721, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). The intensity and extent of immunostaining were evaluated for all tumor samples by two pathologists under double-blinded conditions. In brief, the percentage of positive staining was scored as 0 (0–9%), 1 (10–25%), 2 (26–50%), or 3 (51–100%), and the intensity as 0 (no staining), 1 (weak staining), 2 (moderate staining) or 3 (dark staining). The total immunostaining score was calculated as the product of extent and intensity, ranging from 0 to 9.

## Microarray data
The SuperSeries GSE14407, which includes gene expression profiles from 12 healthy ovarian surface epithelia samples and 12 serous ovarian cancer epithelia samples, was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database [ ].

## Survival analysis
The overall survival (OS) analysis and gene expression analysis were conducted through Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA,http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/) [ ] and TNMplot dataset (https://tnmplot.com/analysis/) [ ]. The gene expression analysis in tumor and normal tissues was also analyzed in the databases.

## Data processing
Gene expression data were obtained from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). High-throughput functional genomic data are stored in the GEO database for free research and are normalized using various methods or tools. The dataset [GSE14407] was selected for analysis based on relevance to ovarian cancer. To facilitate the analysis of these large-scale datasets, GEO provides an integrated, user-friendly web tool called GEO2R, which enables researchers to perform differential gene expression analysis directly on selected datasets without requiring advanced bioinformatics skills or the need to download raw data. GEO2R is an online interface that leverages the power of the Bioconductor R package limma (Linear Models for Microarray Data) to analyze expression profiles by comparing two or more experimental groups within a GEO Series (GSE) dataset. The general workflow of GEO2R begins with the user selecting the relevant GEO dataset and defining groups of samples according to the experimental design—for example, disease vs. control or treated vs. untreated groups. GEO2R automatically performs data normalization and background correction if necessary, ensuring comparability between samples. It then fits a linear model to the expression data, estimates fold changes between groups, and applies empirical Bayes moderation to improve statistical reliability, particularly for small sample sizes. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using GEO2R, an online tool provided by NCBI that enables comparison between two or more sample groups within a GEO Series, and processed for Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), and heatmaps showing clustering analysis of DEGs. Specifically, the bulk microarray dataset GSE14407 was analyzed through GEO2R’s web interface. GEO2R automatically utilizes the platform information associated with the dataset; for GSE14407, the platform used was GPL570 [HG-U133_Plus_2] Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array. Data normalization was conducted internally by GEO2R using the RMA (Robust Multi-array Average) method or the method originally applied in the dataset, ensuring comparability across samples. Quality control and outlier detection are integrated within GEO2R’s preprocessing pipeline, and samples flagged as poor quality in the original dataset were excluded from our analysis.
We selected the relevant samples based on the experimental groups defined within the dataset metadata; no additional sub-series were separately analyzed unless explicitly stated. For differential expression analysis, GEO2R applies the limma package to fit linear models, computing fold changes and statistical significance. We set the significance threshold at an adjusted p-value (Benjamini-Hochberg FDR) < 0.05 and a fold change cutoff of |log2FC| >1 to identify significantly differentially expressed genes.

## Identification of EVs scaffold proteins for presenting CD24 ScFv
The plasmids “CD63-NanoLuc”, “CD81-NanoLuc”, “CD9-NanoLuc” and “Lamp2b-NanoLuc” were constructed and were used to transfect the HEK-293T cells to produce the EVs. The bioluminescent signaling of each EVs were tested by using the NanoLuc® Luciferase kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Meanwhile, the cells treated with different EVs were also tested by using NanoLuc® Luciferase kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

## Preparation of CD24 scFv-EVs
The HEK-293T cells were seeded into T175 flask (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and were transfected with “Lamp2b-CD24 scFv” plasmid by BeyoPEI™ (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the protocol provided by the manufacturer (PEI: plasmid = 2:1). Five hours after the transfection, we replaced the medium as Opti-MEM (Gibco, MA, USA) with 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin-Gentamicin Solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 48 h. The producing process of engineered EVs is based on our previous studies [ – ]. The conditioned medium was collected and centrifuged at 2000 g for 20 min and then was filtered through a 0.22 μm BeyoGold™ Vacuum Filters (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) to get rid of the cell debris. The tangential flow filtration (TFF, MicroKross) was used to isolate the extracellular vesicles (EVs) from filtered conditioned medium. Particles exceeding the cutoff of the TFF system would be collected and concentrated. TFF could achieve adequate removal of contaminants and increase the purity level of the EVs [ , ]. Then, the Millipore Amicon Ultra-15 spin filters (30 kDa) were used to concentrate the EVs by centrifugation at 3800 g for 2 h at 4 °C. These procedures enabled the isolation of engineered EVs overexpressing CD24 scFv (CD24 scFv-EVs), which were utilized as fresh EVs within one weeks.

## Characterization of engineered CD24 scFv-EVs
The concentrated EVs were collected in maxirecovery 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes (Axygene, USA) and quantified using transmission electron microscope (TEM) (FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit Twin, Philips, NL), nanoparticle tracking analysis, using NanoSight NS300 (Malvern, Amesbury, GB). And the markers of EVs including CD63, Alix, Tsg101 and Lamp2b were detected by western blot assay as below. Experiments were conducted in biological triplicate and technical triplicate.

## Western blot
Proteins were extracted from EVs (109 particles/sample were used) and cell lysates using Radio Immunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) buffer (Solarbio, Beijing, China), supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min with intermittent vortexing every 5 min to ensure complete lysis. After incubation, samples were centrifuged at 12,500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to pellet cellular debris. The protein concentration of cell lysate was quantified by Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) to keep 1000 ng/µL. The protein samples were mixed with SDS (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), then were heated at 100 °C for 10 min in a metal bath to ensure complete denaturation and linearization of the proteins. After cooling to room temperature, the samples were ready for loading onto the Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel. The protein samples (1000ng) were loaded onto the gel and subjected to electrophoresis at 130 V, which were separated by SDS-PAGE (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) following standard procedures. The Polyvinylidene Fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Merck Millipore, MA, USA) was blocked with blocking buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 40 min at room temperature, then incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. After three times washes with 1× Tris-buffered Saline with Tween 20 (TBST) (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 5 min, the membrane was treated with Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies and visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). EV markers were detected with antibodies as below [ ]: anti-CD63 (1: 1000, ab134045, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-Alix (1: 1000, ab275377, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-Tsg101 (1: 5000, ab133586, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and anti-Lamp2b (1 µg/mL, ab18529, Abcam, Cambridge, UK).

## Isolation of primary bone marrow derived macrophages
The fresh bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were collected from the sacrificed C57BL/6 mice, aged 6 weeks. Both male and female mice were used for the isolation of BMDMs. Bone marrow was flushed out from femurs and tibias by using 5 ml of BMDM culture medium consisting of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin-Gentamicin Solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) plus 10 ng/mL of recombinant macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Red blood cells in bone marrow were lysed via RBC Lysis Buffer. Briefly, cells were incubated in the RBC Lysis Buffer (1X, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 5 min at 4 °C and then centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 2 min to remove the lysed red blood cells and collect the remaining BMDMs. And the remaining cells were suspended with BMDM culture medium at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL and seeded into 6 well plate (Corning, NY, USA). The fresh BMDM medium (containing 10 ng/mL M-CSF) was used to replace the medium for every 2 days until day 7. On day 7, the fully differentiated bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were gently washed and allowed to attach to the culture surface overnight in fresh differentiation medium, which was replaced the next day. After overnight attachment, the cells were treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) as a macrophage activator (100 ng/mL) for 24 h. Differentiation efficiency was validated by flow cytometry using markers (F4/80 and CD11b) to confirm differentiation into macrophages. All experiments were performed triplicates to ensure the robustness of the results.

## In vitro detection of phagocytosis by macrophages
The SKOV-3mCherry cells (105 cells/well) were seeded onto cell slides (Corning, NY, USA) placed inside a 24-well-plate (for subsequent immunofluorescence analysis). After 24 h, added with PBS, Blank EVs (EVs derived from HEK-293T) or CD24 scFv-EVs into the wells and incubated for 6 hours. Then we removed the medium and washed the cell slides via PBS (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), then added fresh DMEM containing THP-1 cells (2 × 106 cells per well) or BMDMs (2 × 106 cells per well) and incubated for 12 h.
For THP-1 cells phagocytosis evaluation, the conditioned medium was removed, and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) was added to fix the cells. The cells were with FITC conjugated anti-CD11b (4µL for 106 cells, ab269333, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), then be analyzed by immunofluorescent microscopy. For BMDMs phagocytosis evaluation, the conditioned medium was removed, and 4% PFA was added into the wells to fix the cells. After incubated with FITC conjugated anti-F4/80 (1: 100, ab60343, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), the cells were analyzed by immunofluorescent microscopy. Experiments were conducted in biological triplicate and technical triplicate.
In the flow cytometry assay, we selected the ovarian cancer cells to test the phagocytosis ability of macrophages, 5 × 104 SKOV-3mCherry cells were collected after treated with diverse EVs. We used PBS (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) to wash the excessed EVs, then added THP-1 cells or BMDMs for subsequent coculturing. Then the phagocytosis ability of THP-1 cells (FITC conjugated anti-CD11b, 4µL for 106 cells, ab269333, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) or BMDMs (FITC conjugated anti-F4/80, 1: 100, ab60343, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) was tested by flow cytometry. The gating strategy is as below: lymphocytes, single cells, live cells, FITC positive mCherry positive cells. Macrophages were distinguished by surface markers (FITC), and cancer cells were distinguished by fluorescent labels (mCherry). The phagocytic percentage was used as the readout. Experiments were conducted in biological triplicate and technical triplicate. The flow cytometry results were subsequently analyzed by the FlowJo software (10.8.1).

## Mice
Female BALB/c−/− nude mice (6 weeks) were purchased and housed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions in Animal Center of Fudan University (Shanghai, China). The experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committees of Fudan University. In the ovarian cancer assay, SKOV-3 cancer cells (104 cells/mouse) were subcutaneously injected the nude mice subcutaneously for 3 days to allow for initial tumor establishment, and the mice were grouped into 4 groups (N = 4): PBS group, Blank EVs group, CD24 mAb group and CD24 scFv-EVs group. Mice in each group were intravenously treated with PBS (100µL), Blank EVs (109 particles/mouse in 100µL PBS), CD24 mAb (200 µg) and CD24 scFv-EVs (109 particles/mouse in 100µL PBS) for 18 days (every three days, 6 times in total). Then, mice were sacrificed for the further detection. In the breast cancer assay, MCF-7 cell line is estrogen receptor-positive (ER⁺), estradiol (E2) supplementation is critical for tumor establishment in vivo. E2 was administered via subcutaneous implantation of 17β-estradiol pellets (0.72 mg/pellet, 60-day release; Innovative Research of America) one day prior to tumor cell injection. MCF-7 cancer cells (5 × 106 cells/mouse, as previous studies [ , ]) were subcutaneously injected the nude mice subcutaneously for 3 days to allow for initial tumor establishment, and the mice were grouped into 4 groups (N = 4): PBS group, Blank EVs group, CD24 mAb group and CD24 scFv-EVs group. Mice in each group were intravenously treated with PBS (100 µL), Blank EVs (109 particles/mouse in 100µL PBS), CD24 mAb (200 µg) and CD24 scFv-EVs (109 particles/mouse in 100µL PBS based on our previous studies [ , ]) for 18 days (every three days, 6 times in total). The EVs number was based on protocols from our previous published references [ , ] for similar experiments in which the effects of EVs on cellular responses. The sample size of n = 4 per group was chosen based on several factors: this sample size would provide sufficient power to detect statistically significant differences between the groups, based on expected effect sizes derived from similar studies in the literature. Similar studies [ , ] in the field, often use sample sizes of n = 4 per group to ensure statistical robustness while balancing practical considerations such as resource constraints and biological variability. The volume of tumors was measured by using a vernier caliper. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = 1/2 (Length)*(Width)^2. Then, mice were sacrificed via cervical dislocation (complied with institutional animal care guidelines) at Day 21. The animals were euthanized using CO₂ inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, in accordance with the guidelines of our Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Tumor volumes were monitored every 3 days, tumor size, weight, histological analysis, immunostaining were conducted after mice sacrifice.

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Analyses were performed using Prism 10.4 (GraphPad Software, LLC, San Diego, CA, USA), with biological replicates. Experiments were conducted in biological triplicate and technical triplicate. Data were tested for normality and homogeneity of variances before applying one-way ANOVA. Prior to ANOVA, the assumption of homogeneity of variance was evaluated. When this assumption was satisfied, ANOVA was performed. Significant differences were determined at a significance level of p < 0.05. For significant ANOVA results, Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc test, implemented through the (TukeyHSD) function, was used to identify specific pairwise group differences. One-way ANOVA was used for multiple group comparisons, P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant (*: < 0.05, **: < 0.01, ***: < 0.001, ****: < 0.0001).

## Results

## The expression of CD24 predicts patient prognosis of ovarian cancer and breast cancer
To investigate the relationship of dysregulated expression level of the CD24 expression and patient prognosis in ovarian cancer and breast cancer, we firstly determined the CD24 expression as well as combination of “CD24 and Ki67 expression” in patients with ovarian cancer and breast cancer based on The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets. As depicted in Fig.  A, the CD24 expression level is much higher in ovarian cancer tissues. Ovarian cancer patients with higher level of CD24 are related to inferior prognosis (Fig.  C). As depicted in Fig.  D, the CD24 expressed higher in breast cancer tissues. Breast cancer patients with higher level of CD24 are also related to inferior prognosis (Fig.  F). In addition, according to the TNMplot database includes samples from GEO, GTex, TCGA, and TARGET databases, CD24 expressed higher level in ovarian cancer (Fig.  G) and breast cancer (Fig.  H).
 

## The CD24 expression and phagocytosis-blocked macrophages function in ovarian cancer and breast cancer
The effect of the upregulated CD24 expression and the dysregulated macrophages function in ovarian cancer and breast cancer was analyzed. Firstly, the bulk microarray dataset analysis based on publicly available data sets was conducted, which including 12 ovarian cancer (OC) samples and 12 normal ovarian (NC) samples [ ]. To identify the potential functional molecules of ovarian cancer, differentially expressed gene (DEG) was analyzed, and the results showed that CD24 expressed significantly higher in ovarian cancer (Fig. A and B). EPCAM is a well-established epithelial marker in various cancer types, including ovarian cancer, which plays a role in cell-cell adhesion and is involved in tumor progression and metastasis. As depicted in Fig. C, CD24 and EPCAM expressed higher in ovarian cancer samples than normal ovarian samples. Furthermore, we detected the CD24 expression in the clinical samples from patients with ovarian cancer and breast cancer. The ovarian cancer samples showed significantly increased percentage of CD24 expression, which was similar in the breast cancer samples (Fig. D). The western blot results also exhibited that ovarian cancer and breast cancer expressed higher level of CD24 (Fig. E, Supplementary Figure ). Since CD24 are reported to act as “do not eat me” signals between tumor cells and macrophages through CD24-Siglec-10 signaling, we explored the phagocytosis-blocked macrophages in ovarian cancer and breast cancer. Intriguingly, the Siglec-10 + CD68+ (we defined as “phagocytosis-blocked” macrophages) expressed higher in ovarian cancer (Fig. F) and in breast cancer (Fig. G). Above all, ovarian cancer and breast cancer expressed higher level of CD24 and subsequently block the macrophages to avoid the phagocytic clearance. Thus, it is reasonable to engineer EVs presenting CD24 scFv to block the CD24 on the tumor cells and promote the phagocytic clearance of macrophages.
 

## Identifications of scaffold proteins for producing CD24 scFv-EVs
We identified the scaffold proteins by detecting the bioluminescence signaling of different EVs as well as EVs treated cells. Some published scaffolds including CD63 [ ], CD81 [ ], CD9 [ ] and Lamp2b [ ] were enrolled to compare the presenting ability. As demonstrated in Figure , the Lamp2b-NanoLuc EVs exhibited higher presenting ability into EVs. Meanwhile, the Lamp2b-NanoLuc EVs treated recipient cells also showed higher bioluminescent signaling. Based on these, we selected Lamp2b as scaffold protein in this study. Following the preparation of CD24 scFv-EVs, the characteristics of the collected EVs were detected through TEM, NTA as well as western blot. The CD24scFv-EVs exhibited distinct biconcave morphological features of EVs (Fig. C) and ranged from 30 nm to 300 nm (Fig. D). Furthermore, CD24 scFv-EVs stably expressed CD63, Alix and Tsg101 (Fig. E, Supplementary Figure ), which are regarded as classic EVs markers. In addition, CD24 scFv-EVs also stably expressed the scaffold protein Lamp2b (Fig. E, Supplementary Figure ). What’s more, the CD24 scFv-mNG EVs (EVs presenting Anti-CD24 scFv-mNG-Lamp2b on surface, mNG is an abbreviation for “mNeonGreen”, a fluorescent fusion protein to visualize and track EVs) and CD24 scFv-mCherry EVs (EVs presenting Anti-CD24 scFv-mCherry-Lamp2b on surface) from HEK-293T cells were observed by confocal microscopy, the results (Fig. F) showed that the genetically engineered EVs could express mCherry and GFP fluorescence consistently. These results indicated that CD24 scFv- EVs could be produced and isolated as well as be characterized.
 

## The binding ability of CD24 scFv-EVs on ovaria cancer cells and breast cancer cells in vitro
The binding ability of CD24 scFv-EVs was tested by incubating with SKOV-3 cells, which expresses CD24 on the cell membrane (Supplementary Figure ). To this end, CD24 scFv-mNG EVs) (Fig.  A) were genetically produced to treat the SKOV-3 cells. Meanwhile, the CD24 scFv-mCherry EVs were genetically produced to treat MCF-7 cells (Fig.  B). This experiment is designed to show the genetic EVs could bind to the recipient cells. Since the fluorescent proteins were fused on the surface of EVs, the receipt cells binding the EVs could be detected by flow cytometry. As depicted in Fig.  C, SKOV-3 cells treated with CD24 scFv-mNG EVs exhibited higher percentage of GFP + cells than those treated with PBS, Blank EVs and mNG EVs. In addition, the effect of CD24 scFv-mNG EVs could be inhibited by pre-incubated with CD24 antibody while the mNG EVs could not be inhibited (Figre 4E). As demonstrated in Fig.  D, MCF-7 cells treated with CD24 scFv-mCherry EVs exhibited higher percentage of mCherry + cells than those treated with PBS, Blank EVs and mCherry EVs. Encouragingly, the effect of CD24 scFv-mCherry EVs could be inhibited by pre-incubated with CD24 antibody while the mCherry EVs could not be inhibited (Fig.  F). Furthermore, we also produced EVs expressed HiBiT (CD24 scFv-HiBiT EVs) (Fig.  G) to treat SKOV-3 cells. Cells treated with CD24-HiBiT EVs exhibited higher bioluminescent signal than those treated with HiBiT EVs or PBS (Fig.  I), while there is no significant difference between CD24-HiBiT EVs and HiBiT EVs (Fig.  H). These findings suggested that CD24 scFv-EVs exhibited the ability to bind to the tumor cells.
 

## CD24 scFv-EVs promote phagocytic clearance by macrophages
The ovarian cancer cells SKOV-3mCherry (SKOV-3 stably expressing mCherry) were incubated with CD24 scFv-EVs for 6 h, then the SKOV-3mCherry were co-cultured with macrophages for 12 h to detect the phagocytic clearance of macrophages (Fig.  A). The macrophages were labeled by the F4/80-antibody, the phagocytosis events were visualized as red cells inside the green cells (mCherry+GFP+) by immunofluorescence microscopy and calculated by flow cytometry. As demonstrated in Fig.  C, we found that compared with the PBS treatment and Blank EVs treatment, the treatment of CD24 scFv-EVs showed a remarkable increase in THP-1 macrophages phagocytic clearance. The size of tumor in CD24-scFv EVs group is smaller than the size of tumor in anti-CD24 antibodies group, the treatment of CD24 scFv-EVs showed a remarkable increase in THP-1 macrophages phagocytic clearance. The flow cytometry assay showed the similar result (Fig.  E and F). Furthermore, the primary BMDMs were isolated and used to detect the phagocytic clearance (Fig.  B). The treatment of CD24 scFv-EVs also exhibited a remarkable enhancement in primary BMDMs phagocytic clearance (Fig.  D). The flow cytometry assay showed the similar result (Fig.  G and H). These findings suggested that CD24 scFv-EVs could promote the phagocytic clearance of macrophages.
 

## CD24-EVs reduce ovarian cancer burden and breast cancer burden in the mouse model and prolong the survival of cancer-bearing mice
To determine CD24 scFv-EVs in inhibiting ovarian cancer and breast cancer in vivo, nude mice for ovarian cancer model were injected human ovarian cancer cell lines SKOV-3 subcutaneously, nude mice for breast cancer model human breast cancer cell linkes MCF-7 subcutaneously. Then mice were subsequently treated with CD24 scFv-EVs, CD24 mAb, Blank EVs or PBS (Fig.  A). As depicted in Fig.  B and C, SKOV-3 bearing mice treated with CD24 scFv-EVs exhibited better tumor clearance than other groups. Furthermore, in the SKOV-3 tumor-bearing mouse model, mice treated with the PBS and mice treated with Blank EVs groups exhibited a remarkable loss of body weight. Encouragingly, the mice treated with CD24 scFv-EVs showed a steady gain of body weight (Fig.  D and E). Consistently, MCF-7 bearing mice treated with CD24 scFv-EVs had better tumor clearance than those treated with Blank EVs, PBS or CD24mAb (Fig.  F and G). Furthermore, in the MCF-7 xenograft mouse model, mice in both the PBS and Blank EVs groups experienced a significant decline in body weight in the later stages of the experiment due to tumor growth. In contrast, the CD24 scFv-EVs group showed a steady increase in mouse body weight (Fig.  H and I). These results suggest that CD24 scFv-EVs could exhibit inhibiting performance in ovarian cancer growth, with no significant treatment-related toxicity effects. Importantly, we also found that the expression of Ki67 in tumor from CD24 scFv-EVs group is lower than Blank EVs group and PBS group (Fig.  J and K). These results showed that CD24 scFv-EVs play vital roles in eliminating tumor cells in vivo, subsequently improve the survival outcome of both ovarian cancer tumor bearing mice and breast cancer tumor bearing mice.
 

## CD24 scFv-EVs modulate tumor microenvironment
We investigated the immune cell fluctuation in the tumor microenvironment of the SKOV-3 and MCF-7 mouse model. In the macrophage (CD11b+) population, CD11b + CD86 + cells are regarded as M1-like macrophages (M1), while CD11b + CD206 + cells are regarded as M2-like macrophages (M2). After CD24 scFv-EVs treatment, there is a remarkable fluctuation in the percentages of M1 and M2 cells in both the ovarian and the breast cancer microenvironment. In ovarian cancer microenvironment, compared with the PBS group and Blank EVs group, the percentage of M1 cells increased in the CD24 mAb group and the CD24 scFv-EVs while the percentage of M2 cells decreased, which indicates the CD24 scFv-EVs could reverse the M2 immunosuppressive macrophages infiltrating (Fig.  C, D and E). In addition, macrophages treated with CD24 scFv-EVs, expressed higher levels of IL-6 and TNF-α (Figure ). Similarly, in breast cancer microenvironment compared with the PBS group and Blank EVs group, the percentage of M1 cells increased in the CD24 scFv-EVs while the percentage of M2 cells decreased, which indicates the CD24 scFv-EVs could reverse the M2 immunosuppressive macrophages infiltrating (Fig.  F, G and H). These results suggested that the phenotype of tumor infiltrated macrophage could be reversed by CD24 scFv-EVs. These findings indicated that the CD24 scFv-EVs could not only block the also modulate tumor microenvironment.
 

## Discussion
As the well development of immune checkpoints inhibition in cancer therapeutic field, increasing evidence witnessed the remarkable achievements in different cancers. PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and CTLA-4 inhibitors are most popular ICIs in clinical practices. Recently, some “do not eat me” signaling molecules including CD47 and CD24 are reported to help cancer cells evade from immune surveillance. Given that many solid tumor cases exhibited limited response to the ICIs and chemotherapies, there is a pressing need to explore the new therapeutic approaches. Considering ovarian cancer and breast cancer expressed high level of CD24, and exhibited limited response to current immunotherapies, it is urgent to exploit the potential strategy for therapy and illustrate the underlying mechanism.
In this study, firstly we explored that the higher expression level of CD24, dysregulated macrophages function and the correlated poor prognosis in ovarian cancer and breast cancer by bioinformatics based on TCGA database and GEO database. We also verified the remarkable higher expression level of CD24 in ovarian cancer and breast cancer based on the patients’ samples, which were highly consistent with previous studies [ , , ] that CD24 might be a promising target for cancer immunotherapy via CD24-Siglec-10 axis [ , ].
Based on these scenarios, we reconstructed the plasmids encoding various scaffold proteins and used them to transfect the producing cells. Some widely reported scaffold proteins including CD63 [ ], CD81 [ ], CD9 [ ] and Lamp2b [ ] were enrolled as potential scaffold candidates to produce the EVs and were compared to identify the scaffold protein for presenting the CD24 scFv on the EVs. Since Lamp2b exhibited better presenting performance than other scaffold proteins, it was selected to produce the endogenous engineered EVs, which overexpressed immunosuppressive ligands CD24 scFv on the membrane. Lamp2b has been widely reported to engineer EVs to deliver proteins, siRNA [ ] and other cargos [ ]. In vitro, engineered CD24 scFv-EVs exhibited the ability to bind and block the CD24 on cancer cells and promote the phagocytic clearance by macrophages in vitro and ex vivo. Furthermore, our studies showed that CD24 scFv-EVs could increase the proportion of infiltrating M1-like macrophages, which contributed to the regulation of immune dysfunction. These lead to the inhibiting effect of the cancer progression. In the presented delicate algorithm, the engineered EVs were produced to block the “do not eat me” signaling on both the ovarian cancer cells and the breast cancer cells and subsequently suppress the cancer progression. Consequently, the engineered EVs could promote the phagocytic clearance of macrophages upon both the ovarian cancer cells and the breast cancer cells to suppress the cancer progression.
In this study, we selected the HEK-293T cells to produce the EVs. Since some cell types including MSCs [ ], macrophages [ ] were reported to produce the engineered EVs, it was also reported that HEK-293T cells are perfect candidate as producing cells to produce the engineered EVs. The bioengineered EVs derived from HEK-293T cells could be used in vitro assays as well as in vivo assays, with lower immunogenicity and toxicity. Since the critical roles of HEK-293T derived engineered EVs in therapy field, we leveraged them to produce engineered EVs that exhibit overexpression of CD24 scFv through genetic endogenous engineering design.
Extracellular vesicles are acknowledged acting as a kind of therapeutic agents for drug delivery, which present remarkable advantages compared to conventional medications. What’s more, we produced the EVs through the plasmids transfection method and collected the endogenous EVs presenting the CD24 scFv. Some studies that EVs isolated from the extrusion methods could exert the ligand presenting function and signal activating/blocking function [ – ]. However, the endogenous method used in this study could isolate much purer EVs without cells component of cytoplasm (organelles, nucleus and cytoplasmic components). According to the EVs endogenous principles, after the transfection of the plasmids, the EVs sorting domains (transmembrane domain including CD63 [ , ], CD9 [ ], CD81 and Lamp2b [ , ], etc.) could fuse the proteins/ligands of interests and help these proteins display outside the EVs membrane. This principle could help researchers produce the endogenous ligands on the surface of the EVs and apply this therapeutic approach to diverse diseases.
Immune checkpoint blockade therapy has drawn increasing attention in cancer treatment [ , ]. Nevertheless, these negative regulatory immune checkpoints also serve a vital function in preserving immune tolerance within healthy tissues and organs. The CD24/Siglect-10 signaling axis also exerts a significant influence on various diseases including inflammation [ ], metabolic disorder [ ] and cancer immunotherapy [ ]. It is widely acknowledged that blocking the inhibitory ligands of immune checkpoints is becoming a new therapeutic approach for malignancies [ , ]. Previous clinical trials showed that chemotherapy combined with PD-1 blockade can be a treatment option in patients with diverse cancers [ , ]. Some studies showed that the activation of the Gal-9/Tim-3 pathway inhibits Th1 effector function and encourages the participation of Treg cells in the immune evasion process observed in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) [ ]. Some clinical studies showed CD24Fc could be potential therapy for prevention of graft-versus-host disease [ ] and COVID-19-associated systemic immunopathology [ ]. However, most studies related to the CD24 still remain pre-clinical phase. In our study, we also enrolled the CD24 mAb to evaluate the effect of CD24 scFv-EVs and CD24 mAb in restoring the phagocytic effect of macrophages. According to our findings, the CD24 scFv-EVs performed similar to the CD24 mAb for therapeutic efficacy in ovarian cancer while slightly better than CD24 mAb in breast cancer. This could be explained by that CD24 scFv-EVs might be likely to maintain longer half-time in the circulation than CD24 mAb and could better restore the phagocytic effect of macrophages. However, this study is limited by lack of pharmacokinetic data, which are needed to support these assumptions in further pharmacokinetic related studies to support these assumptions.
Moreover, compared to conventional full-length anti-CD24 antibodies, CD24-scFv engineered EVs offer several potential advantages. The smaller size of scFv enhances tissue penetration and may reduce immunogenicity. Additionally, EVs presenting CD24-scFv may exhibit reduced toxicity and side effects compared to traditional CD24 mAbs [ , ]. These characteristics suggest that CD24-scFv EVs represent a more flexible and safer platform for targeting CD24-expressing cells, offering translational potential beyond conventional antibody-based therapies.
In this study, we introduced mCherry at two different parts for different designing. In the engineered CD24 scFv-mCherry EVs, mCherry is a fluorescent fusion protein to visualize and track EVs. In this context, CD24 scFv-mCherry EVs were used coculture with wild-type SKOV-3 cells and wild-type MCF-7 cells, and we detected the percentage of mCherry positive cells to evaluate the binding ability of CD24 scFv-mCherry EVs. One the other hand, for SKOV-3mCherry (SKOV-3 stably expressing mCherry) cells, they are designed for phagocytic clearance assay. In this context, SKOV-3mCherry could be phagocytosed by macrophages. After staining macrophages with specific antibodies conjugated FITC, we could detect double FITC positive and mCherry positive cells to evaluate the phagocytic clearance ability of macrophages. In addition, to further support our phagocytosis assay, we primarily relied on flow cytometry, which enables accurate and quantitative detection of double-positive cells. Flow cytometry allows for high-throughput and objective analysis of phagocytic events, avoiding the ambiguity of 2D imaging artifacts. The combination of fluorescent labeling and flow cytometry provides a robust and reliable approach for assessing phagocytic clearance in this context.
CD24 scFv-EVs exhibited a more pronounced effect in reversing the M2-like polarization of macrophages compared to the conventional CD24 monoclonal antibody. This enhanced activity may result from both improved delivery efficiency and mechanistic differences between the two therapeutic modalities. First, the EV-based delivery system may facilitate more efficient and targeted delivery of the CD24-scFv molecules to immune cells, especially in the tumor microenvironment, due to the natural tropism and membrane fusion capability of EVs. Second, the scFv format lacks the Fc region, which may reduce undesired Fc receptor-mediated effects and allow for more specific Siglec-10 blockade. Additionally, the multivalent display of CD24-scFv on the EV surface may promote stronger or more stable interactions with Siglec-10, enhancing functional outcomes. However, in this study, we only observed an increased proportion of M1 macrophages and a decreased proportion of M2 macrophages in the tumor microenvironment following CD24 scFv-EV treatment. We did not further investigate the underlying mechanisms driving this polarization shift. It remains unclear whether the observed effect is a direct consequence of CD24 blockade, a secondary result of enhanced phagocytosis, or a combination of both. Elucidating these mechanistic pathways will require further investigation in future studies, and this represents one of the limitations of the current work.
There are some limitations in the present study. Firstly, diverse origin cells for producing engineered EVs should be enrolled to compare for optimizing the origin cells. Secondly, the half-life time of CD24 mAb and CD24 scFv-EVs should be further detected in the future study. Thirdly, humanized immune system mouse should be used to explore the roles of CD24 scFv-EVs in T cells in the future study. Another limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size (n = 4 per group) used in the animal experiments. While this is consistent with prior studies in the field, such small cohorts may limit statistical power, particularly for endpoints such as survival analysis and immunophenotyping. Although the current study provides important proof-of-concept data, formal power calculations were not performed at the outset. Future studies will include larger sample sizes and prospective power analyses to validate these findings with greater statistical confidence. Additionally, we analyzed phagocytosis was assessed using fluorescence microscopy, which should be better using confocal fluorescence microscopy. To minimize misinterpretation due to overlapping cells, images were captured at multiple focal planes, and only punctate intracellular signals clearly located within cell boundaries were considered positive. All samples were thoroughly washed to remove non-internalized particles, and quantification was performed through flow cytometry. Confocal fluorescence microscopy is needed in the future related study.
Totally, this study would expand the application of genetic endogenous engineered EVs inevitably. The engineered EVs not only could deliver the proteins of interest (including RNA and proteins) but also could express the ligands of interest on the surface to display with cells in immune system. The genetically engineered EVs presenting CD24 scFv could restore the phagocytic effect of macrophages and regulate the immune micro-environment, which inhibit the progression of cancers.
