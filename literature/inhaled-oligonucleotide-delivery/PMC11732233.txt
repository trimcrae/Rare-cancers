## Abstract
Emerging evidence suggests that lipid-laden macrophages (LLM) participate in lung damage in various clinical conditions. However, the mechanisms involved in LLM formation are not fully understood. In this study, we aimed to investigate the link between reactive oxygen species (ROS) and LLM formation. We found that ROS triggered by cigarette smoke extract (CSE) or H2O2 significantly promoted LLM formation. Given the key role of ROS in LLM formation, we further demonstrated that LLM formation is induced by various ROS-producing stimuli, including bacteria, oxidized low-density lipoprotein (OxLDL), hyperoxia, and E-cigarette vapor extract (EVE). Meanwhile, cytochrome P450 family-1 subfamily B member 1 (CYP1B1) was highly upregulated in lung macrophages from chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) patients and CSE-treated macrophages. Functionally, CYP1B1 contributes to the CSE-induced lipid accumulation and LLM formation. CYP1B1 expression and LLM formation were effectively suppressed by antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC) and carvedilol. The formation of LLM was also associated with classically activated M1 but not the M2 state. CSE-induced LLM showed time-dependent alterations in inflammatory response and phagocytic ability. In summary, our study highlights the role of oxidative stress in LLM formation. CYP1B1 contributes to ROS-induced LLM formation and may serve as a therapeutic target for reducing LLM-induced lung damage.
Keywords: CYP1B1, COPD, Cigarette smoking, E-Cigarette, Reactive oxygen species, Carvedilol

## Introduction
Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), the third leading cause of death worldwide, affects over 400 million individuals, bringing considerable health and economic burden [ ]. COPD is an irreversible respiratory condition characterized primarily by restricted airflow due to chronic inflammation, airway remodeling, and alveolar damage [ ]. The diagnosis of COPD adheres to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) criteria, which involves spirometry measurement of forced expiratory volume in 1 s/forced vital capacity (FEV1/FVC) [ ]. Risk factors of COPD include both genetic predispositions such as alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency and environmental factors such as exposure to noxious airborne particles [ ]. Among these factors, cigarette smoke (CS) stands out as the predominant cause of COPD [ , ]. Inhaled CS-induced oxidative stress triggers immune cell recruitment and sustains inflammation [ ]. Alveolar macrophage (AM), the predominant immune cell in the lung, plays a critical role in regulating the invaded noxious particles such as CS [ ]. Reports show that COPD and asthma patients experience altered lipid metabolism, specifically altering the surfactant lipids-cholesterol, fatty acid, and phospholipid levels [ , ]. While functional AMs clear damaged or oxidized lipids and facilitate lipid transport, CS impairs lipid clearance by AMs [ , ]. These lipid-laden macrophages (LLM) have been observed in both CS-exposed mice and patients with chronic cough as well as those with mild or severe asthma [ , ].
Although the presence of CS-induced LLM in patients exhibiting COPD symptoms is evident, the underlying mechanism remains unclear. Our previous work demonstrated that miR-103a modulates CS-induced LLM formation, a process associated with reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in macrophages [ ]. Hence, we hypothesized that the CS-induced ROS could facilitate the formation of LLM during altered lipid metabolism. In this study, we aimed to further investigate the role of ROS in the formation of LLM. Our study addresses that the increased ROS promotes the formation of LLM, which is mitigated by the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC). Our further investigation of the relationship between macrophage polarization and LLM formation revealed a stronger association between LLM formation with the pro-inflammatory M1 state rather than the M2 state. In addition, our data indicated that various ROS-producing stimuli can induce LLM formation. Mechanistically, we identified cytochrome P450 family 1 subfamily B member 1 (CYP1B1) as a potential key regulator of LLM formation through in vitro and in vivo models. Evidently, this LLM formation was blunted by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved β-blocker, carvedilol, which has been shown to possess antioxidant properties. In summary, our research elucidates that the increased expression of CYP1B1 induced by ROS leads to changes in lipid metabolism in macrophages, which results in LLM formation. Additionally, re-purposing carvedilol emerges as a potential therapeutic option for preventing LLM formation and mitigating LLM-induced lung injury.

## Materials and methods
2.1.

## Human specimens and ethics approval
In this study, unidentifiable human samples were used. Human sample research was approved and determined as non-human subject research by the institutional review board at Augusta University and Brigham and Women's Hospital (IRB reference number: 1889310–1 and 2,070,085–1). The human samples from the bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) cohort used in the current study were obtained, processed, and analyzed as described previously [ ]. The relative mRNA expression of CYP1B1 was measured by the quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) in nonsmokers (n = 6), smokers (n = 11), and COPD patients (n = 8). The demographic and clinical characteristics of the BAL cohort are listed in . The double immunofluorescence staining of CYP1B1 and CD68 was performed in lung sections from the immunostaining cohort, in which 4 non-smokers and 6 COPD patients were included (demographic and clinical characteristics are shown in ). The lung sections in this cohort were provided by the Lung Tissue Research Consortium (LTRC) or the Department of Pathology at Brigham and Women's Hospital [ ].

2.2.

## Animal studies
All of the animal experiments were approved by the Charlie Norwood Veterans Affairs Medical Center Institutional Animal Ethics Committee and followed the ARRIVE guidelines. C57BL/6 wild-type mice of both sexes (8–10 weeks old) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). To induce LLM formation in the lung, mice were anesthetized with 2 % isoflurane (Covetrus, Portland, ME, USA). Then, 50 μg Oxidized Low-density Lipoprotein (OxLDL) (Athens Research & Technology, Athens, GA, USA) in 50 μL sterile PBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was intratracheally administered to mice on day 0 [ ]. After 24 h, 50 μL PBS with or without 0.2 mg/mL carvedilol (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) or 0.2 mg/mL timolol (MedChemExpress) was intratracheally given to OxLDL-treated mice (n = 7 per group) on days 1, 3, and 5. Isoprenaline (MedChemExpress) was delivered intratracheally on days 1, 3 and 5. On day 7, BAL cells were isolated as previously described [ ]. To track the OxLDL distribution in the lungs, the PKH26 Fluorescent Cell Linker Kit (MilliporeSigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) was used according to the manufacturer's protocol. Unincorporated dye was removed using a 3 kDa Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter (MilliporeSigma). 50 μg PKH26-labeled OxLDL in 50 μL sterile PBS was intratracheally administered to mice. On days 1 and 3 post-treatment, mice were euthanized with 5 % isoflurane and BAL cells were collected.

2.3.

## Plasmid construction and siRNA oligonucleotides
Full length of human CYP1B1 and mouse Cypb1b cDNA amplified by PCR was subcloned into the BamHI and SalI sites of pBABE-puro vector (Plasmid #1764, Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) [ ] to obtain pBABE-CYP1B1 and pBABE-Cyp1b1, respectively. The DNA oligonucleotide sequences used for cloning were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA, USA) and the sequences are listed in . A commercial siRNA of CYP1B1 (siCYP1B1, Catalog # AM16708, Assay ID 145515) and siRNA control (siCon) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

2.4.

## Cell culture and transfection
THP-1, U937, and MH-S cell lines were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and cultured in RPMI 1640 complete medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 10 % FBS (MilliporeSigma) and 1 % Penicillin-Streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Differentiated THP-1 or U937 cells were induced by 40 nM phorbol myristate acetate (PMA) (MilliporeSigma) as we described previously [ ]. Macrophage polarization was induced as previously described [ ]. 10 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (Escherichia coli O111:B4, MilliporeSigma) and 20 ng/mL of recombinant IFN-γ (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) were used to induce M1 macrophage, and 20 ng/mL IL-4 (R&D Systems) was used for inducing M2 macrophage. The M0 macrophage was incubated with Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) (MilliporeSigma) and served as a control. Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for transfection of siCYP1B1, siCon, pBABE, and pBABE-CYP1B1 according to the manufacturer's protocol.
Hydrogen Peroxide (H2O2), Cigarette Smoke Extract (CSE), E-cigarette Vapor Extract (EVE), Hyperoxia, OxLDL, LPS, Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. p), 2,4,3′,5′-tetramethoxystilbene (TMS), Antioxidant and FDA-approved Drug Treatment.
For the H2O2 treatment, macrophages were incubated with 200 or 500 μM H2O2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as indicated. CSE was prepared as described before [ ]. Briefly, a research cigarette (3R4F, University of Kentucky) was used to bubble its smoke into 10 mL of RPMI, creating a 100 % CSE solution. This freshly prepared CSE solution was then filtered and diluted to achieve the specific concentrations required for each experiment. E-cigarettes (Juicy Bar JB5000 Disposable Vape-clear flavor) were purchased from Jvapes (Prescott, AZ, USA). The e-cigarette liquid has a 13 mL capacity with 5000 puff counts, and a rechargeable battery is included. The product contains a 1.2 Ohm Mesh Coil and a nicotine strength of 50 mg/mL (5 %). EVE was prepared as described before [ ]. Briefly, negative pressure from a syringe was given to the activated e-cigarette. After the e-cigarette was exhaled, this procedure was repeated for a total of 30 puffs. This process produced a 10 mL media, which constituted a 100 % EVE stock solution. The 100 % EVE stock was then diluted to the desired concentration for the experiment. To apply hyperoxia conditions, cells were exposed to hyperoxia (95 % O2 and 5 % CO2) in modular exposure chambers. 5 μg/mL OxLDL was added to the differentiated THP-1 or U937 to induce LLM formation. All experiments with antioxidant treatments were conducted with or without the addition of 5 mM NAC (MilliporeSigma) to the culture medium 2 h before the CSE treatment. 10 ng/mL LPS was used as an inducer for testing LLM formation in this study. In vitro K. p (#43186, ATCC) infection was conducted as previously described [ ]. Briefly, the concentration of K. p was measured by a plate reader (OD600). 106 cells were incubated with 5 × 106 CFU K. p for 1 h. The bacteria were then removed and eliminated by washing with PBS and 50 μg/mL gentamicin (MilliporeSigma) [ ]. TMS, the FDA-approved carvedilol, isoprenaline, propranolol, timolol purchased from MedChemExpress, and tamsulosin (Tocris, MN, USA) were incubated together with or without other treatments.

2.5.

## ROS detection
5 μM CellROX Green dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to the cultured cells for 30 min to determine the cellular oxidative stress. Cells were washed with PBS three times and then fixed with a 4 % formaldehyde solution (MilliporeSigma) for 1 h before imaging. The nuclei of the cells were stained using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA). Images were taken using a Carl Zeiss Observer Z1 microscope (Jena, Germany). The fluorescence intensity of these images was quantified using ImageJ software (v1.52a, National Institutes of Health, USA).

2.6.

## Oil red O (ORO) and lipid TOX staining
ORO staining was performed as described before [ ]. 100 % isopropanol-saturated ORO (MilliporeSigma) was freshly prepared as a stock solution. The ORO working solution was prepared by making 6 mL stock and 4 mL ddH2O. Fixed cells were washed with ddH2O three times and stained at room temperature for 30 min. After staining, the cells were washed with ddH2O, and images were taken using the Carl Zeiss Observer Z1 microscope. The percentage of ORO-positive cells in each group was subsequently counted and analyzed using ImageJ software. LipidTOX staining was performed as previously described [ ]. Fixed cells were washed with ddH2O 3 times and incubated with LipidTOX dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min. DAPI mounting solution was used to stain the nucleus.

2.7.

## Triglyceride and cholesterol quantification
The total amount of triglyceride and cholesterol in macrophages was measured using RayBio® Triglyceride Colorimetric Assay Kit (Catalog #: MA-TG) and RayBio® Total Cholesterol Colorimetric Assay Kit (Catalog #: MA-TC) purchased from RayBiotech (Peachtree Corners, Georgia, USA). The experiments were conducted according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, 200 μL of the enzyme mix solution per well was added to a 96-well plate, followed by adding the standard solution provided by the experimental kit and 10 μL cell lysates. The plate was incubated at 37 °C before reading at the plate reader.

2.8.

## Phagocytosis assay
The Vybrant™ Phagocytosis Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to determine the phagocytic ability of the macrophages. Briefly, cells were first cultured and treated with the desired conditions. E. coli particles were added to the cells and incubated together for 2 h. Trypan blue was prepared based on the manufacturer's protocol and then incubated with cells for 1 min. The fluorescence images were captured by Carl Zeiss Observer Z1 microscope.

2.9.

## Western Blot analyses
Western blot experiments were performed as previously described [ ]. Cells were collected and lysed with RIPA buffer (RPI Research Products, Mount Prospect, IL, USA) with the addition of protease inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The protein lysates were first separated using SDS-PAGE gels and then transferred onto PVDF membranes (MilliporeSigma). The PVDF membranes were blocked and incubated with primary polyclonal CYP1B1 antibody (PA5-95277, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 1: 500 dilution overnight. The membrane was subsequently washed with TBST solution and incubated with a secondary anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated antibody (HAF008, R&D Systems) for 1 h. To detect β-actin, the membrane was stripped with stripping buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated overnight with a primary monoclonal anti-β-actin antibody (A5441, MilliporeSigma) at 1:1000 dilution. This was followed by incubation with an anti-mouse HRP-conjugated antibody (HAF007, R&D Systems). Images were taken using the ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). ImageJ software was used to quantify the intensity of the bands.

2.10.

## Immunofluorescence staining and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining
Immunofluorescence staining was performed as described previously [ ]. Macrophages were incubated with or without 5 % CSE for 3 days. Fixed cells or lung sections were incubated with CYP1B1 antibody (PA5-95277, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a 1:250 dilution or CD68 antibody (ab955, Abcam) at a 1:250 dilution overnight at 4 °C. Then the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with IgG-Alexa Fluor 488 or 594 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The DAPI containing mounting medium (ab104139, Abcam) was used for mounting and staining the nuclei. Imaging was conducted using a Carl Zeiss Observer Z1 microscope, and the fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software. H&E staining was conducted in the Histology Core Laboratory at Augusta University. Images were taken using a Carl Zeiss Observer Z1 microscope.

2.11.

## Measurement of lung macrophage size
The diameters of human lung macrophages in H&E-stained lung tissues were determined using the ImageJ Software. Approximately 100 macrophages were randomly picked and the mean size of the lung macrophages from each sample was used to plot the graph.

## RNA preparation, reverse transcription, RT-qPCR, and PCR array
The total RNA was extracted using the RNAqueous™ Total RNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was synthesized using the high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer's instructions. Human and mouse TBP is used as housekeeping genes. Primers used in the study were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA, USA) and the sequences are listed in . PowerUP Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used for RT-qPCR. RT-qPCR was performed and analyzed using QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The RT2 Profiler PCR Array for Mouse Extracellular Matrix & Adhesion Molecules was performed following the protocol from the manufacturer (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA).

## Bioinformatics methods for CYP1B1 expression analysis in AMs
The expression profiles of genes in AM from COPD patients and control subjects were obtained from the GEO database (GSE16972) [ ]. CYP1B1 expression data were acquired and analyzed using GEO2R. The fold change of gene expression in AM from COPD patients and control subjects was included in .

2.14.

## Statistics analysis
All data in this study were presented as means ± SD and were analyzed with SigmaPlot (Systat Software). The difference between the two experimental groups was analyzed using a two-tailed unpaired student t-test for variables that fall in the normal distribution, while data that were not normally distributed were analyzed by using Mann-Whitney U tests. Differences between multiple (three or more) experimental groups were compared using one-way ANOVAs with Tukey's tests for data with a normal distribution; Mann-Whitney U tests were used for analyzing data that were not normally distributed. Groups were considered to be statistically significantly different when p ≤ 0.05.

## Results
3.1.

## Oxidative stress promotes LLM formation
To assess the association between ROS production and LLM formation, we employed the established in vitro model in which LLM formation was induced by CSE incubation. As shown in A–C, CSE exposure promoted the formation of LLM and the accumulation of triglycerides and cholesterol in MH-S cells. NAC, a ROS inhibitor, significantly inhibited CSE-induced LLM formation regardless of whether it was used as a pretreatment or administered simultaneously with CSE ( D–G). Studies have shown that pulmonary lipids are damaged by cholesterol oxidation and lipid peroxidation when exposed to CS, leading to their accumulation in pulmonary macrophages [ ]. Given that FBS is a rich source of lipids and cholesterol [ ], a higher FBS concentration in the culture medium provides more damaged lipids upon H2O2 treatment, serving as an in vitro model that mimics the increased amount of damaged lipids induced by CS. As shown in H, H2O2 significantly increased the percentage of LLM in the presence of 20 %–30 % FBS. To be noticed, this is an in vitro model that assesses the relationship between ROS and LLM formation. Long-term H2O2 treatment significantly causes cell death. The relative cell numbers in control, 200 μM H2O2, and 500 μM H2O2 under 5 % FBS were approximately 100 % versus 65 % versus 11 %, respectively.
 
3.2.

## Various stimuli can trigger LLM formation
Considering the critical role of ROS in LLM formation, we tested if LLM formation can be induced by various ROS-producing stimuli. We found that cellular ROS production can be induced by 10 % EVE, 95 % O2, 5 μg/mL OxLDL, 10 ng/mL LPS, and K. p at MOI 5 in MH-S and THP-1 macrophages ( A–D). Meanwhile, an increased number of LLM in these treated cells was observed ( E–H).
 
3.3.

## CS exposure induces CYP1B1 expression in macrophages
Increased size of lung macrophages has been observed in patients with COPD, which is characteristic of LLM [ ]. Consistent with the latter report, we found the size of lung macrophages in COPD patients was increased compared with the nonsmokers (NSC) using H&E staining ( A and B). To identify the target gene involved in LLM formation, we accessed the GSE database (GSE16972), where the authors compared gene profiles in AM between healthy controls and individuals with COPD [ ]. Among all the up-regulated genes, CYP1B1 exhibited the greatest induction in AM from COPD patients than in AM from control subjects ( C and ). Interestingly, CYP1B1 is considered a mediator of lipid peroxidation and has been reported as a potential regulator of ROS [ ]. Therefore, we confirmed the CYP1B1 mRNA level within the BAL cohort and observed a significant increase of CYP1B1 expression in the COPD patients compared to the nonsmokers ( D). However, the relative mRNA level of CYP1B1 did not significantly correlate with FEV1/FVC (p = 0.0836) ( E). Double immunofluorescence staining showed significantly increased CYP1B1 fluorescence intensity of lung macrophages (CD68 positive) in COPD patients compared with those in non-smoker control ( F and G). The increased CYP1B1 at both mRNA and protein levels was confirmed in CSE-treated THP-1 and MH-S macrophages ( H–L). In addition to CSE, CYP1B1 expression was significantly increased in macrophages by H2O2 treatment ( M) and other ROS-inducing stimuli, including CSE, EVE, 95 % O2, LPS, and K. p ( ), suggesting that CYP1B1 is a ROS-responsive gene.
 
3.4.

## CYP1B1 regulates LLM formation
To understand the role of CYP1B1 in LLM formation, we overexpressed CYP1B1 in MH-S cells ( A and B). The overexpression of CYP1B1 promoted LLM formation ( C and D) and lipid accumulation ( E and F). In addition, we inhibited CYP1B1 expression in 5 % CSE-treated THP-1 cells using siRNA ( A–C). CYP1B1 knockdown significantly suppressed CSE-induced LLM formation ( D and E) and lipid accumulation ( F and G). Consistently, CSE-induced LLM formation was rescued by TMS ( H and I), which is a known CYP1B1 inhibitor [ ]. Taken together, our data suggests that CYP1B1 plays a role in LLM formation.
 
3.5.

## Carvedilol suppresses LLM formation
FDA-approved β-blocker carvedilol has antioxidant properties [ ] and it reduces the number of foam cells in animal models of hyperlipidemia [ ]. Then, we evaluated the effect of carvedilol on LLM formation and found that carvedilol at 5–20 μM significantly reduced the percentage of ORO-positive U937 ( A and B). Carvedilol significantly attenuated CSE-induced ROS production and CYP1B1 expression in THP-1 macrophages ( C–E). Similarly, carvedilol at 10 μM inhibited both CSE and OxLDL-induced LLM formation ( F–I). Next, we assessed whether carvedilol could inhibit LLM formation in vivo. As shown in , OxLDL was labeled with PKH26, a fluorescent lipophilic dye, to track the distribution of OxLDL after intratracheal administration. In murine lungs, the colocalization of PKH26-positive cells and CD68-positive cells indicated that OxLDL was taken up by lung macrophages ( ). In addition, the delivery of OxLDL induced CYP1B1 expression ( ) and promoted LLM formation in murine BAL cells ( ). After confirming LLM formation in vivo, we administrated carvedilol to OxLDL-treated mice ( J). Consistent with in vitro results, carvedilol administration significantly reduced the percentage of ORO-positive AMs ( K and L).
 
3.6.

## Adrenergic receptor signaling pathway in LLM formation
Given that carvedilol is a known non-selective β-blocker with additional blockade of α1-adrenergic receptors [ , ], there is a possibility that carvedilol inhibition of LLM formation may also involve these signaling pathways in addition to its antioxidant properties. To explore this, we assessed the effect of adrenergic agonists and antagonists on LLM formation. First, we observed that the adrenergic agonist isoprenaline increased ROS production in macrophages, which was rescued by NAC addition ( A and B; ). In contrast, adrenergic antagonists propranolol and timolol suppressed CSE-induced ROS production ( A and B; ). Consistent with the altered ROS level, isoprenaline induced the formation of LLM in macrophages ( C and D; ). While adrenergic antagonists propranolol and timolol suppressed LLM formation ( C and D; ; ). The effects of isoprenaline and timolol were further confirmed in vivo ( ). In addition, we found that another adrenergic antagonist tamsulosin neither reduced ROS production ( E and F) nor LLM formation in CSE-exposed MH-S cells ( G and H).
 
3.7.

## Macrophage polarization influences LLM formation
It is known that macrophages can be polarized into M1 (pro-inflammatory) and M2 (anti-inflammatory) states. To investigate the relationship between LLM and macrophage polarization statuses, we polarized MH-S macrophages into M0, M1, and M2 states and found that M1-polarized macrophages showed higher ROS levels ( A and B). In line with the increased ROS level, there are more ORO-positive LLM in M1-polarized macrophages ( C and D). The results were reproducible in human THP-1 macrophages ( ). In addition, the number of LLM in M1-polarized macrophages was slightly reduced by NAC and carvedilol ( E and F). In M1-polarized THP-1 cells, NAC and carvedilol down-regulated CY1PB1 expression ( ). Meanwhile, NAC and carvedilol inhibited pro-inflammatory marker genes and promoted anti-inflammatory marker CD206 expression in M1-polarized THP-1 cells ( ).
 
3.8.

## CSE exposure alters the innate immune response of macrophages
We next assessed the innate immune response of macrophages in response to CSE exposure. Interestingly, the expressions of pro-inflammatory markers were highly induced by CSE compared to the control group on day 3 and reduced on days 6 and 9 ( A–F). The phagocytosis ability was also changed in a time-dependent manner in CSE-treated MH-S ( G and H). Our data suggests that the differences between short-term and long-term CSE exposure need to be considered. To be noticed, the expression of CSE-induced pro-inflammatory genes was significantly lower than those in M1-polarized MH-S cells ( I–K). Similar observations were found in THP-1 macrophages ( ).

## Discussion
COPD, a chronic inflammatory condition, continues to pose a significant global burden due to its rising incidence, prevalence, and associated morbidity, and mortality rates [ ]. While non-pharmacological interventions such as smoking cessation and increased physical activity hold promise for enhancing the quality of life of COPD patients [ , ], the irreversible nature of the disease means that no existing medical therapies can cure COPD symptoms. The complex molecular mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of COPD are not fully understood.
Dysregulation of CYP1B1 was previously observed in lung epithelial cells [ , ] and rat lung tissues [ ] when exposed to CS. In our study, we found that CYP1B1 is significantly upregulated in AMs from patients with COPD. CYP1B1 belongs to the cytochrome P450 superfamily of enzymes, which are predominantly expressed in the liver and lungs [ ]. Studies indicate that the CYP enzymes may influence ROS production and detoxification processes, thereby impacting lipid peroxidation and DNA damage [ , ]. Cyp1b1 deletion in myeloid cell lineage attenuated macrophage M1 polarization [ ]. While CYP1B1 is known to be activated by polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) found in cigarettes and is elevated in smokers compared to nonsmokers [ , ], its role in the pathogenesis of COPD is poorly understood. In the study, we employed both gain- and loss-of-function approaches utilizing siRNA and pBABE-CYP1B1 transfection to silence or overexpress CYP1B1 in macrophages. We observed that silencing CYP1B1 inhibited the CSE-induced LLM formation, and LLM formation was promoted by overexpressing CYP1B1; suggesting a critical role for CYP1B1 in this process. A key strength of our data lies in not only verifying the increase of CYP1B1 at mRNA and protein levels accompanied by lipid drop formation in macrophages but also in identifying the functional significance of CYP1B1 in this context. Based on our findings, CYP1B1 has enormous potential to serve as a target gene modulating the LLM formation, offering potential avenues for therapeutic intervention in COPD.
Oxidative stress is characterized by increased ROS levels within cells, which inflicts damage on the proteins and lipids [ ]. However, the role of ROS in LLM formation in the lung has been unclear. Our research is the first to elucidate the molecular mechanism governing the modulation of LLM by oxidative stress. We have demonstrated the correlation between LLM formation by various ROS-generating stimuli. Furthermore, we have shown that the formation of LLM due to CSE exposure can be mitigated by the antioxidant NAC in both murine and human macrophages.
AMs serve as the first line of defense in the lungs, responsible for clearing inhaled irritants and pathogenic microbes [ ]. Their pivotal role in the pathophysiology of COPD has been well-established [ ], with chronic inflammation in COPD closely associated with AM dysfunction [ ]. Transcriptomic and lipidomic analyses have highlighted a significant alteration in AM lipid metabolism among COPD patients, a phenomenon associated with the formation of LLM [ ]. Growing evidence suggests that LLM participate in lung damage under various clinical conditions including not only COPD but also fibrosis, tuberculosis, and pediatric aspiration [ , , , ]. Moreover, apart from cigarette smoking, an increased number of LLM was found in the lungs of E-cigarette users and is proposed as a clinical feature of E-cigarette or vaping product use-associated lung injury [ , ]. Evidence of LLM as a potential biomarker for vaping-associated lung injury has been reported [ ].
Using in vitro models, we exposed macrophages to a range of stimuli including CSE, LPS, hyperoxia, OxLDL, and bacteria. Not surprisingly, we observed the presence of LLM in these treated macrophages along with significantly increased cellular ROS levels, suggesting the vital role of ROS generation in LLM formation. It indicates that LLM can be induced by oxidative stress and inflammation, which are triggered by various stimuli, such as CS, vaping, or infection. Based on this observation, we posit that ROS serves as a key inducer of LLM formation, representing a shared mechanism across diverse clinical conditions. However, further evidence, particularly from in vivo studies, is warranted to bolster our findings.
Multiple studies have highlighted the detrimental effects of LLM formation in various diseases, yet the strategies to block this process remain insufficiently explored [ , , , ]. Considering the potential beneficial effect of blocking LLM formation, the administration of CYP1B1 inhibitors may protect the lung from LLM-caused damage. To confirm our hypothesis, we treated the macrophages with CSE incubation with CYP1B1 inhibitor, TMS [ ]. We found that TMS treatment has inhibited the formation of CSE-induced LLM. Wang et al. reported that the FDA-approved drug, carvedilol serves as a novel CYP1B1 inhibitor as well as a promising anti-cancer agent [ ]. To investigate whether carvedilol can reduce the formation of LLM, which will enable the drug to be re-purposed for COPD and other inflammatory diseases, we performed a dose-dependent test in CSE-induced LLM formation. We observed that carvedilol treatment reduced the number of LLM in human U937 and THP-1 macrophage cultures. To understand whether carvedilol has a similar effect in vivo, we conducted a comparison between the carvedilol-treated group with the PBS-treated control group in wild-type C57BL6 mice. Similarly, we observed that the number of OxLDL-induced LLM was significantly reduced in the carvedilol-treated group. Thus, administering carvedilol to the lungs might safeguard against LLM-induced inflammation and injury during the progression of COPD.
Adrenergic antagonists such as propranolol [ ] and timolol [ ] exhibit antioxidant effects. However, another antagonist, tamsulosin, did not inhibit either ROS production or LLM formation. To our knowledge, no study has reported that tamsulosin has a direct antioxidant effect. Although our results indicate that the adrenergic receptor signaling pathway is involved in LLM formation, the process appears mainly mediated by ROS production in macrophages. Carvedilol, a non-selective β blocker with antioxidant properties [ ], may inhibit LLM formation not only through CYP1B1 inhibition but also via its antioxidant effect. Functionally, CYP1B1 is an essential player in oxidative metabolism and synthesizes cholesterol and other lipids [ ]. It is reported that CYP1B1 contributes to superoxide production in vascular smooth muscle cells [ ] and the deletion of Cyp1b1 suppresses lipid droplets in sinusoidal hepatocytes [ ]. Therefore, further studies are warranted to determine whether both mechanisms contribute to the carvedilol effect on LLM formation.
Macrophages exhibit remarkable plasticity and sensitivity to their surroundings [ ]. Traditionally, M1 macrophages, characterized by classical activation, present pro-inflammatory characteristics, whereas M2 macrophages, marked by alternative activation, tend to display anti-inflammatory properties [ ]. As of today, the relationship between LLM and macrophage polarization states remains incompletely understood. The classification of LLM is subject to debate, with some studies reporting M2 activation aiding the promotion of LLM [ , ], while others reporting an increased number of pro-inflammatory macrophages contributing to LLM formation [ ]. Additionally, research indicates that the macrophages in COPD patients secrete increased inflammatory proteins compared to those in healthy smokers and nonsmokers, suggesting altered immune function [ ]. Conversely, M2 macrophages were unable to form LLM. Interestingly, CSE-induced LLM displayed characteristics consistent with the pro-inflammatory macrophages. Our findings demonstrate that the expression of pro-inflammatory markers was increased in CSE-incubated macrophages. These findings collectively suggest that LLM favors an M1-like phenotype. Given the association between LLM and pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, it is imperative to evaluate their innate immune response in future studies.
Our study has several limitations that warrant attention. Firstly, electrophiles such as aldehydes [ ], NO [ ], and heavy metals [ ] are well-known inducers of oxidative stress. Our study hypothesized that the increased cellular ROS level is a key contributor to LLM formation. From this perspective, both CSE-derived electrophilicity and the secondary produced ROS can induce oxidative stress and contribute to LLM formation. However, distinguishing the specific effects of CSE-derived electrophilicity from those of CSE-induced secondary ROS on LLM formation is challenging due to difficulties in separating electrophiles and accurately determining their concentrations. Secondly, although an increased CYP1B1 expression in AMs was found in COPD patients, it was not significantly upregulated in smokers compared to non-smokers. On the other hand, the correlation between the relative mRNA levels of CYP1B1 and FEV1/FVC was not statistically significant, although a positive trend was noted. This may be due to the small sample size and variations among smokers, such as pack-years of smoking. Therefore, further studies with a larger sample size are needed to confirm the observed results with greater confidence. Thirdly, our study lacks detailed molecular mechanisms. For instance, it is unknown how CYP1B1 modulates lipid accumulation and LLM formation in response to CSE exposure. A previous study revealed that the expression of genes involved in fatty acid transport and synthesis, such as differentiation 36 (CD36), fatty acid synthase (FAS), and stearoyl CoA desaturase 1 (Scd1) is regulated by CYP1B1 [ ]. Thus, we aim to further explore the molecular mechanism by which CYP1B1 modulates fatty acid homeostasis and contributes to the pathogenesis of COPD using the Cyp1b1 myeloid-specific conditional knockout mouse model [ ]. Additionally, our study demonstrated that carvedilol reduced ROS production and CYP1B1 expression in macrophages. However, our study did not evaluate whether carvedilol suppresses LLM formation through the inhibition of CYP1B1 activity since a study has shown that carvedilol is a novel CYP1B1 inhibitor [ ]. Lastly, the pathological role of LLM was not fully explored in our current study. In the future, we aim to uncover the contribution of LLM to the development of COPD.
In summary, our findings demonstrate that ROS production in macrophages can be induced by external stimuli, such as CS, e-cigarette aerosol, or bacterial infection. Elevated oxidative stress promotes LLM formation through the actions of CYP1B1. Carvedilol, with antioxidant properties, suppressing CSE-induced CYP1B1 expression, emerges as a promising agent capable of curtailing LLM formation and potentially attenuating LLM-induced lung inflammation and injury.
