## ABSTRACT

## Objectives
Preclinical models are necessary to study the pathophysiology of laryngotracheal diseases and novel treatments. Currently, there is no consensus on species, injury mechanics, and study duration This review provides a contemporary overview of the studies of vocal fold and laryngotracheal injuries in preclinical models.

## Methods
A search was performed in PubMed and Web of Science involving various animal models undergoing vocal fold, trachea, and laryngotracheal injury and/or wound healing studies from 2013 to 2023. Articles were independently screened and selected by four reviewers.

## Results
Of 1279 filtered studies, 197 studies were identified and categorized by species, study duration, and mechanism of injury (MOI): vocal fold stripping, intubation, lasers, microtools, scraping, and other MOIs. Within each MOI, studies were grouped by animal model, with subsections discussing tissue target, research aim, analysis method, and study duration. Leporine and rat models were the most employed animal models for both vocal fold wound healing and laryngotracheal models. Leporine studies had the broadest diversity in MOIs, with lasers, microtools, scraping, and other methods used. Comparatively, rat studies primarily utilized vocal fold stripping as a MOI, perhaps second to anatomic limitations. Larger species, specifically porcine models, were most prevalent in intubation injury studies. Ferret, mouse, and ovine models were less utilized. Study duration varied from 1 day to 3 months in vocal fold injury models and 30 min to 12 months in laryngotracheal injuries.

## Conclusion
This review provides a foundational framework to collate and interpret available data on preclinical vocal fold and laryngotracheal injury models to guide future study design.

## Level of Evidence
Five scoping review/NA.

## Vocal Fold Stripping
About 54 studies were identified (Table  ) that used methods to excise the vocal fold epithelium and lamina propria, exposing the underlying thyroarytenoid muscle. Techniques included 25‐gauge needles and microforceps, following the injury model established by Tateya et al. which has become a foundational reference in subsequent research. The mice model followed the injury inducing protocol by Yamashita et al., with a 14‐day follow‐up [ ]. Rats were the predominant model used in this injury method, accounting for 37 of the 54 studies [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ]. Chronic vocal fold scars in rats typically mature by 2 months post‐injury. Transition from the proliferative to the remodeling phase begins at 3 days post‐injury, with significant extracellular matrix (ECM) reorganization occurring at 2 and 4 weeks. Study periods ranged from 1 to 90 days. Canine models were used in only 4 of the 54 studies, exploring injury outcomes over longer timeframes [ , , , ]. The injury methods in these studies were originally developed by Hirano et al. [ ] and Ohno et al. Leporine models account for 9 of 54, with research durations ranging from 7 to 60 days [ , , , , , , , , , ]. Both porcine [ ] and murine [ ] models were used to examine wound healing, with the porcine model studied for 30 days.

## Scraping
One method for inducing tracheal stenosis involved scraping the tracheal mucosa with a brush or other stiff instrument (Table  ). Nylon brushes were employed in 17 studies, ranging from 7 days to 8 months, to evaluate injury responses [ , , , , ], develop injury models [ ], and assess treatment outcomes [ , , , ]. Using this model, McIlwain et al. reported instances of morbidity and mortality in leporine, with one death occurring < 24 h post‐injury due to acute edema and blood clots, and two additional deaths from severe subglottic stenosis. In a similar rat model, severe stridor developed 48 h post‐injury, leading to mortality [ ]. Four studies incorporated rat models, focusing on injury response and treatment for 4–8 days [ , , ]. A polypropylene brush was used in one leporine model study [ ] over 8 days. Another study used a plastic interdental brush for leporine tracheal injury model, assessing injury histology [ ]. A spatula‐induced injury in a leporine model was employed in one study over 42 days for treatment [ ]. Two studies from the same research group developed a mouse model for subglottic stenosis by causing a mechanical injury with wire brush [ , ].
 Specific to the method of scraping the trachea, most researchers scraped the brush 10 or 20 times; Wycherly et al. scraped 16 times (four times each in four quadrants) [ ], Ghosh et al. and Nguyen et al. scraped 15 times [ , ]. Steehler et al. scraped four times [ ], and McIlwain et al. did not specify the number and instead scraped until injury was visually obtained to the naked eye [ ]. Brush sizes ranged from 1.8 to 7 mm. Wilson et al. initially attempted to induce stenosis via intubation, but after failing, they switched to brush scraping [ ]. McIlwain et al. used a propylene brush after inconsistent results with a nylon brush [ ], and Latahir et al. used an interdental brush [ ].

## Laser
About 16 studies used lasers to create vocal fold injury and/or LTS (Table  ). Carbon dioxide (CO2) lasers were used in five studies targeting canine [ ] and leporine [ , , , ] models of vocal fold and subglottic stenosis injuries. In five studies, potassium titanyl phosphate (KTP) lasers were applied to leporine [ , , ] and rat vocal folds [ ] or tracheas to create injuries [ ]. Yttrium aluminum garnet (YAG) lasers were used in two studies for model comparison and development, one on the canine trachea [ ] and the other on leporine vocal folds [ ]. Diode lasers, reported in two studies, focused on wound healing in leporine vocal fold injuries [ , , ]. Diode lasers, reported in two studies, focused on wound healing in leporine vocal fold injuries [ , ], while semiconductor lasers were mentioned in two studies on canine and leporine vocal fold treatments [ , ].
 Across these studies, treatment efficacy was a common aim in several studies (five studies involving CO2 [ , , ] and semiconductor lasers [ , ]). Model development and comparison were central in three studies (CO2, KTP, and YAG lasers [ ]). Wound healing analysis was the focus in five studies (CO2 [ ], KTP [ , , ], KTP [ , ], Diode lasers [ , ]). The energy delivered measured by power, time, and distance varied across and within the laser types. Outcome measures included direct visualization [ , , , , , , , , , ], immunostaining [ , , ], histology [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , ], biomechanics [ ], and gene expression [ , , , ]. Study durations ranged from 1 day to 3 months.

## Microtools
About 35 studies used microtools to create precise vocal fold injuries compared to comprehensive stripping (Table  ). Specifically, microscissors were employed in nine studies to create vocal fold injuries or tracheal stenosis in leporine [ , ], rat [ , , , , ], and ovine [ , ] models. Microforceps were used in 14 studies across multiple preclinical animal types (leporine [ , , , , , , , ], canine [ , ], rat [ ], and porcine [ , , ] and ovine [ , ]) to create tracheal stenosis and vocal fold injuries. Two porcine animals developed postoperative hyperthermia from anesthetic complications and were treated with IV non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs [ ]. Sickle knives and a scalpel were used in three studies involving leporine [ ] and rat [ ] models for injury response [ ] and treatment [ , ]. Wires were mentioned in one study on rat vocal fold wound healing [ ], while needles were utilized in three rat studies aimed at wound healing [ ], treatment [ ], and model development [ ]. Biopsy forceps were used in a single leporine study for vocal fold injury treatment [ ]. Additionally, unspecified tools were used in various capacities across rat, leporine, and canine studies for varying purposes including wound healing [ , , , , ] and model development [ , ]. The primary objectives in the microtools group examined vocal fold and tracheal stenosis, with 16 studies solely focusing on wound healing involving microscissors [ , ], microforceps [ , , , , , , ], wires [ ], and needles [ ], and unspecified tools [ , , , , ]; treatment efficacy in 15 studies involving all mentioned tools except wires; and model development and comparison in four studies involving microforceps [ ], needle [ ], and unspecified tools [ , ]. Outcome measures included direct visualization, immunohistochemistry, histology, gene expression, and biomechanics. Studies used IHC post‐injury to look for expression and distribution of ECM proteins and infiltration of inflammatory cells through surface markers. Study duration ranged from 1 day to 12 months.

## Intubation
About 18 studies used the intubation method to create laryngeal, subglottic, and tracheal stenosis (Table  ). Nine studies focused on maintaining variable endotracheal tube (ETT) cuff pressure over varying lengths of time, while three examined the effects of rotation, displacement, deflation, and reinflating the ETT on stenosis [ , , ]. These studies, which included canine, porcine, leporine, and rat models, aimed to develop models [ , , , , , , , ], compare models [ , ], and test devices [ , , , , ]. Outcomes measured included direct visualization [ , , , , ], histology [ , , , , , , , , , , , , ], and tissue biomechanics of primarily the trachea [ , ]. Study durations ranged from 4 h to 12 weeks. Longer intubation periods showed increased complications. In a leporine study, three out of eight rabbits were excluded due to respiratory distress‐related mortality before completing the 12‐week period [ ]. Similarly, in a porcine model, mortality occurred within 24 h post‐op due to tracheal obstruction by mucus and necrotic tissue [ ]. In a specific application, one porcine study utilized tracheal dilators for 12 weeks to examine the effects of dilation on the tracheal structure via histology [ ].

## Other Methods
Several alternative methods that did not fit the prior categories were identified that cause vocal fold and laryngotracheal injuries outside of conventional surgical and mechanical interventions (Table  ). These include chemical exposure to methyl isocyanate [ ], formaldehyde [ ], and sulfur mustard [ ] in rat studies lasting 6 h to 13 weeks and hydrochloric acid [ , ] and naphthalene [ ] in mouse models lasting 1–14 days. Histology [ , , , , ], direct visualization [ ], immunohistochemistry [ ], and gene expression [ ] assessment were performed. Other methods included chemomechanical injury using a bleomycin‐coated wire brush (six mouse studies [ , , , , , ]), argon plasma coagulation (one canine study [ ]), silver nitrate (one leporine study [ ]) heat inhalation (six studies across leporine [ , , , , ], canine, and porcine [ ]), and pressure‐induced tissue resection (one canine study [ ]), with outcomes measured over periods ranging from 1 to 90 days. Electrocautery was used in canine, ferret, mice, and rat studies for tracheal [ , , , ] and subglottic stenosis [ ] and vocal fold injury [ , , , , ], with outcomes measured over 1–16 weeks. The defect method was applied in three studies under various conditions, with outcomes measured from 21 to 90 days [ , , ]. In the leporine study by Jorge et al., muscle‐encapsulated abscesses were observed at the tracheal incision site. These animals survived until euthanasia at 8 weeks but exhibited symptoms of stridor and tachypnea. However, mortality from respiratory failure was reported 4–6 weeks post‐injury in two animals. Two rat studies used irradiation for laryngeal stenosis [ , ], with outcomes measured from 12 h to 12 weeks, and stent usage was noted in three studies for canines [ ] and rats [ ], and leporine [ ] with tracheal and laryngeal stenosis. Lipopolysaccharide injection (LPS) was used in a porcine model study [ ] for vocal fold wound healing, with outcomes assessed over 1–5 days. Two studies from the same research group on LTS development in mouse models saw an increase in premature study mortality rate in the groups injured using a bleomycin‐coated wire brush (16 out of 40 and 10 out of 44, respectively) in comparison to a non‐coated wire brush control group (8 out of 32 and 8 out of 42, respectively) [ , ].

## Immunostaining Analysis
We identified a diverse set of immunostaining biomarkers to evaluate the molecular and cellular mechanisms involved in post‐injury processes. These biomarkers are essential for exploring injury response, treatment efficacy, and wound healing, depending on the specific research aims. Key ECM proteins, most commonly collagen types I and III, were frequently used to assess wound healing. Collagen I [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ] was associated with evaluating treatment outcomes, while collagen III [ , , , , , , , ] was linked to early healing stages and injury modeling. Cytoskeletal markers like α‐SMA [ , , , , , , , ] were crucial in assessing myofibroblast differentiation and ECM production. Vimentin [ ] and E‐cadherin [ , ] were vital for investigating epithelial–mesenchymal transition and fibrosis, further elucidated by their spatial distribution in tissue sections through immunostaining. ## Discussion
This scoping review presents a comprehensive evaluation of the various methods to induce vocal fold and laryngotracheal injuries in preclinical models. It offers a clear and concise overview of the current state of knowledge and application of these techniques in animal models. In the past decade, treatments like siRNA and novel drug delivery systems, such as drug‐eluting stents and ETTs, have shown promise, further justifying the need for preclinical development in clinically relevant models [ ]. This review can serve as a repository for comparative analysis to develop and design studies to evaluate novel therapeutics and treatments for vocal fold scarring and LTS.
To date, the literature predominantly focuses on leporine models, used across various MOIs including lasers, microtools, scraping, and other methods, due to their ease of handling and anatomical similarity to pediatric populations (< 2‐year‐old). However, they are limited by their size and a chronic vocal fold scar maturation period of up to 6 months [ ]. Rats are valuable for vocal fold studies due to their human‐like vocal fold anatomy, which includes a three‐layer lamina propria with similar hyaluronic acid distribution and sparse collagen type III. They were utilized primarily in vocal fold stripping studies, which entailed excision of the entire vocal fold mucosa and lamina propria of the thyroarytenoid muscle. Vocal fold stripping was documented in the literature for studying wound healing processes, treatment efficacy, and protein expression in response to vocal fold injury. Vocal fold stripping was performed using instruments such as a 25‐gauge needle and micro‐forceps as reported by Tateya et al. The ease of handling and short lifespan of rats enables rapid maturation of chronic vocal fold scarring within about 8–12 weeks, facilitating investigation of treatment outcomes [ ]. Porcine models were mostly used for creating tracheal stenosis injuries via intubation but were not used for scraping injuries. They are advantageous due to their similarity in collagen and elastin distribution and laryngeal size but are less well‐characterized and present experimental challenges [ ]. Canine models, while comparable in laryngeal size to humans, differ in structural layers and biomechanical properties, with chronic vocal fold scarring taking up to 6 months [ ]. These studies show variability in MOI, with the highest number mentioned in other methods and notable absence of scraping methods, indicating potential limitations of scraping techniques for both porcine and canine models. The development and utilization of other models were less prevalent in the past decade, leading to 12 mouse studies, 3 ferret studies, and 2 ovine studies. Mouse models were employed to develop mostly laryngotracheal injuries using chemical and mechanical MOIs, offering a cost‐effective approach for studying upper airways pathophysiology or developing novel treatments. However, their anatomical size can present challenges and require the use of smaller instruments. Ferret and ovine models are generally less common in biomedical research, but the studies found demonstrated their utility to study novel therapeutics in vocal fold injury for precise drug delivery [ , , ]. In contrast to smaller animal models, they are closer to resembling human laryngeal anatomy and size, so they may be more informative for clinical potential in translational research. Deciding on the optimal animal model for a study on vocal fold or laryngotracheal injury warrants considering multiple questions that include but are not limited to what the research question is being asked, what biomarkers are of interest, what resources the lab and institution have, what the availability or costs of a specific animal to purchase and maintain are, among many others.
Results showed that only Ujvary et al. conducted a review on vocal fold or laryngotracheal injury using rat models with no period restriction, discussing surgical injury to the rat vocal fold regardless of the study scope [ ]. Ujvary et al. organized their tables by including major components such as visualization, anesthetization, lesion induction, positioning of animal, and type of lesion. This scoping review expands on their work by including several other commonly used species for vocal fold injury as well as looking at laryngotracheal injury models. In addition, we created tables based on the mechanism of injury and further divided these tables by components such as tissue target, study aim, result analysis, and study length. Some studies utilized immunostaining following injury to examine the expression and distribution of ECM proteins, alongside inflammatory cell infiltration via surface markers. Others utilized gene expressions to find reference genes that are upregulated in vocal folds and stenotic injury such as Tenascin‐C (Tnc) [ ] and Smad3 [ ]. These genes can be targets for injury treatments. Complications most frequently occurred within the first 48 h post‐injury, particularly in cases of tracheal injury or stenosis. Injury‐derived mortality and complications, though generally uncommon from these studies, are multifactorial and can be associated with the anatomical size of the animal, instruments or medications used, among others. To address these risks, Berges et al. recommended continuous monitoring, suctioning, bronchoscopy every 4 h, and airway humidification to ensure survival during the critical 24 to 48‐h period [ ].
The purpose of this review was to assess the broad area of animal models in laryngotracheal injury studies, with the aim of identifying trends and uncovering potential knowledge gaps that can guide future research as well as serve as an opportunity to leverage existing methodologies to allow for future development of therapeutic and treatment trials.
The scope of this review is limited by the constraints of the scoping review format, which restricted a detailed exploration of each study's unique characteristics within the allotted word limit. Another limitation is not utilizing a biomedical librarian for this wide‐encompassing review, which would help account for varying terminology in other studies that may have been considered. Moreover, constraints imposed by the table guidelines prevented the inclusion of additional factors, such as commonly discussed aspects like the types and dosages of administered anesthetic. However, the overall importance of each injury mechanism and their lessons are appropriately addressed.
One potential confounding issue is the principle of using the smallest possible species to achieve study aims, which, along with economic considerations, likely explains the prevalence of rat and leporine studies. Despite these limitations, the results highlight the variability in injury mechanisms, outcome measures, and study durations in preclinical animal models of vocal fold and LTS, ultimately revealing opportunities for optimizing future research.

5

## Conclusion
This scoping review provides a framework for organizing and interpreting existing preclinical vocal fold and laryngotracheal injury models to guide experimental designs and allow investigators to select optimal animal models, injury methodologies, and outcome measures for their clinical questions. In addition, this review creates a reference for selecting ideal models to evaluate and test innovative therapeutics and devices intended for vocal fold wound healing and laryngotracheal reconstruction.
