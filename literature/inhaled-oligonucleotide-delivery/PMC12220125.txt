## Abstract

## Background
Drug degradation poses a significant challenge in the pursuit of effective gene therapies for cancers.

## Methods
Here we have developed a bioactive nanosized composite that utilizes human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hucMSCs) derived small extracellular vesicles (sEVs), to carry tumor suppressor miR-145 alongside erbium-doped rare earth nanoparticles (ErNPs). This approach not only enhances in vivo delivery but also facilitates real-time fluorescence tracking of nucleic acid drugs in the near infrared (NIR) II window. With this technique, we are able to realize and visualize the effective inhibition of colorectal cancer (CRC) progression in a xenografted murine model.

## Results
Our results revealed that the efficient loading of miR-145 into sEVs could be achieved through a dynamic combination of sonication and electroporation. The resulting miR-145-encapsulated sEVs (i.e., miRNA@sEVs) exhibited a profound ability to hinder tumor growth by effectively downregulating the expression of fascin actin-bundling protein 1 (FSCN1), both in vitro and in vivo. Additionally, the circulation half-time of miRNA@sEVs was measured to be ~ 4 h and the fluorescence at the tumor sites reached a peak intensity at ~ 8 h after intravenous injection of sEVs particles. Finally, the fluorescent signals of miRNA@sEVs were predominantly localized in the mouse liver and spleen, with substantial accumulation in tumors.

## Conclusions
Our results illuminated the excellent biosafety of miRNA@sEVs and their high accumulation in tumors, leading to efficient suppression of tumor progression. This research heralds a promising advancement in gene therapy, paving the way for more effective and safer treatment options.

## Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide. While its incidence typically rises with age, an alarming trend shows that younger adults are increasingly at risk as well [ ]. In the United States, CRC tragically remains the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths, with 153,020 new cases diagnosed and 52,550 death tolls in 2023 [ ]. The carcinogenesis of CRC is characterized by a series of progressive genetic changes through multiple pathways, leading to tumor formation, growth, migration and local or distant metastasis, often resulting in diagnoses at advanced stages when treatment options become very limited [ , ]. Conventional treatments for CRC include surgery, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, playing essential roles in combating this disease. In addition, gene therapy is emerging as an innovative alternative. Targeting the frequently altered TP53 and KRAS, this cutting-edge approach offers hope for many facing CRC [ ]. This gene intervention holds promise as a safe and reliable treatment strategy with reduced side effects, lighting a path forward in the quest for effective cancer treatments in clinical settings [ ].
MicroRNA (miRNA) as a non-coding small RNA molecule that regulates gene expression, can interfere with specific oncogenic pathways [ ]. Among the most studied miRNAs in CRC, miR-145 has been identified as a potent suppressor of tumorigenesis, progression, and metastasis, which intricately engages with various tumor-specific pathways, including KRAS and P53 signaling [ ]. The expression level of miR-145 was found significantly correlated with poor prognosis of patients with a diversity of malignant tumors, including colorectal, gastric, and ovarian cancers [ , ]. Simultaneously, the level of miR-145 in the blood plasma predicted the CRC risk in the general population, making it a practical biomarker in early diagnosis of CRC [ ]. However, the gene targets of miR-145 can be multiple, as the primary target in CRC progression remains elusive, which may otherwise enrich therapeutic regimen greatly.
As many gene therapy techniques require viral vectors (e.g., adeno-associated virus) to carry therapeutic nucleic acids, it raises concerns of immunogenicity and cytotoxicity in clinical contexts. Alternatively, non-viral formulations, primarily composed of lipids and polymers, have emerged as safe and effective vehicles for delivering genetic substances. Among those biocompatible materials, extracellular vesicles (EVs) are a group of membraned particles secreted by most types of living cells. They exhibit a range of physicochemical properties, such as different sizes and densities, and biological characteristics related to their diverse origins and the resulting surface and interior compositions [ ]. EVs can be categorized into small EVs (sEVs), measuring less than 200 nm in diameter, and medium/large EVs that exceed this size [ ]. Often referred to as exosomes, sEVs possess a typical ‘cup-shaped’ structure and exist in most biological fluids, including saliva, blood, and urine [ ]. Their surfaces are enriched with transmembrane proteins that are capable of relocation within the cellular matrix, facilitating the exchange of materials and signaling between cells [ ]. Compared to artificially synthesized particles of similar size, sEVs own naturally derived membrane structures of lipid bilayer, so offering many unmatched features as a delivery platform, including low immunogenicity, non-toxicity, exceptional biocompatibility and stability [ ].
In this study, we utilized sEVs derived from hucMSCs as gene carriers. Those sEVs were loaded with miR-145 and ErNPs for in vivo miRNA delivery and fluorescence tracking. The employment of ErNPs as an NIR II emissive fluorescent probe provided an unprecedented imaging window for biological tissues [ ]. Compared to traditional NIR I dyes, which have an emission wavelength of 650–900 nm, ErNPs emit a peak wavelength at 1540 nm. This allows deeper tissue penetration with minimized spontaneous fluorescence, so enabling precise localization of the transported particles in murine CRC models. Concurrently, this study validated the interaction between the tumor suppressor miR-145 and its cancerous targets in CRC, shedding light on the anti-cancer mechanisms of gene therapies.

## Materials and methods

## Human CRC tissue samples
Sixty-two CRC samples (24 rectum and 38 colon tissues) were clinically diagnosed and histopathologically confirmed at theAffiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University (AHJU) between March 2023 and February 2024, and preserved at -80 °C, together with their corresponding paracancerous tissues. All cancerous tissues were histologically adenocarcinomas surgically removed from patients that received no prior treatment. Patient information was summarized in Table . Informed consent was acquired from patients and ethical approval was obtained from the research ethics committees of AHJU.

## Isolation, characterization and culture of hucMSCs
HucMSCs were isolated as previously reported [ , ]. In brief, following approval from the Research Ethics Committee of AHJU and with informed consent from patients, fresh umbilical cord tissues were obtained. HucMSCs were isolated using the tissue block adhesion method and cultured in an alpha minimum essential medium (α-MEM, Invitrogen, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA). The isolated hucMSCs were characterized as reported [ ].

## Isolation and characterization of sEVs
HucMSCs were transferred to a 10 cm culture dish with the number of 1 × 106 cells. When the cell density reached 50–70% confluence in 1–2 days, the medium was replaced with α-MEM containing 10% EV-depleted FBS for 8 mL/dish. Then the cells were cultured at 37 °C in the incubator with 5% CO2 for 48 h. Next, the supernatant from the cell culture of hucMSCs was collected and centrifuged at 1,500 g for 20 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. Subsequently, centrifugation was continued at 10,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C to eliminate cell organelles. The supernatant was then transferred to 100 kDa molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) ultrafiltration tubes (Millipore, USA) and centrifuged at 1,500 g for 30 min at 4 °C for multiple times until the concentration of vesicles reached 100× of original solutions, i.e., the volume of the concentrated solution is 1/100 of the initial amount when cell supernatant was first collected. Next, the concentrated solution was transferred to high-speed centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 4 °C, 100,000 g for 1.5 h (Beckman, USA). Then the supernatant was carefully discarded, and fresh PBS was added to the tubes to wash the precipitate, followed by centrifugation again at 4 °C and 100,000 g for 1.5 h. After centrifugation, the hucMSC-derived sEVs were obtained as precipitates. To characterize, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Hitachi, Japan) was utilized to examine the morphology of sEVs, nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) (NanoSight, Malvern, UK) was used to measure the concentration, size and surface charge of sEVs, and Western blotting experiments were applied to verify their protein contents.

## Western blot
Total proteins in cells, sEVs and tissues were extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. Proteins of equal quantity in each group were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), followed by transfer of the gel onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. After the transfer, the membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h. Subsequently, the PVDF membrane was incubated overnight with primary antibodies specific to each protein at 4 °C, including anti-Alix, anti-TSG101, anti-CD9, anti-Calnexin (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), anti-FSCN1 (1:1000, Abcam, USA), and anti-GAPDH (1:3000, Abcam, USA). Later, the PVDF membrane was incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibody (1:2000) (Vazyme, China). After washing, the membrane was detected by chemiluminescence imaging instrument (GE, USA).

## qRT-PCR analysis
Trizol lysis buffer was employed to extract mRNA from cells and tissues. The exosomal RNA purification kit (Qiagen, Germany) was utilized to isolate miRNA from sEVs, and the reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, China) was used for reverse transcription of miRNA and mRNA. Subsequently, QuantStudio 3 was employed for qRT-PCR analysis. The primer sequences used in the experiment were shown in Table  .
 

## Transfection of miRNA and plasmids
All the miRNA mimics, miRNA inhibitors and negtive control (NC) used in the experiment were obtained from Genepharma Co., Ltd., China. The synthetic miRNA comprised of 21-base pair nucleotides. NC has a scrambled sequence of nucleotides that undergoe random rearrangement (i.e., in a shuffling order) while maintaining the same length and GC content as the miR-145. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates (2 × 105 cells/well). When the cell density reached 60–70%, cells were transfected with miRNA and plasmid using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) in serum-free medium according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Firstly, the original culture medium was gently discarded and the cells were washed twice with fresh PBS. Then 0.5 mL of culture medium without FBS was added, and the cell plate was cultured in the cell incubator for 1–2 h. During the incubation, the transfection reagent was prepared, as 5 µL of Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) was added to 245 µL Opti MEM medium, with gently mixing, then left at room temperature for 5 min. At the same time, 5 µL miRNA or plasmid was added to 245 µL Opti MEM medium, with gently mixing, also left at room temperature for 5 min. Subsequently, the above two mixed solutions were transferred to the same tube and mixed gently, left at room temperature for 20 min. Then, 500 µL of mixed solution was added to each well and the plate was cultured in the cell incubator for 6–8 h. Next, the culture medium containing transfection reagent was carefully discarded and replaced with the fresh medium containing 10% FBS, and the plate was cultured in the incubator for 24–48 h.

## Dual-luciferase reporter gene analysis
Human CRC cell lines HCT116 and SW480 were purchased from Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology at the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) containing 10% FBS at 37°C with 5% CO2. The dual-luciferase reporter plasmids were constructed by inserting the wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) 3’-untranslated region (3’UTR) of FSCN1 into the GP-miRGLO vector. The WT plasmid contained the putative miR-145 binding sites, while the MUT plasmid carried site-directed mutations in the seed regions. The experiments were conducted in four groups, each with three replicates (n = 3). The groups were FSCN1-WT plasmid + miR-145 mimics negative control (NC), FSCN1-WT plasmid + miR-145 mimics, FSCN1-MUT plasmid + miR-145 mimics NC, and FSCN1-MUT plasmid + miR-145 mimics. Plasmids were transfected into the corresponding cells. Following transfection, 100 µL of 1 × passive lysis buffer (PLB) was added to the cells. After thorough lysis, the cell suspension was transferred to Eppendorf (EP) tubes and placed under a biochemical luminescence detector (Turner Biosystems, USA), where the first reading was recorded. To each tube, 50 µL of reaction termination solution was added, gently mixed, and then retested, recording the fluorescence signal ratio, followed by statistical analysis.

## Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) for viability tests
Cells from each treatment group were seeded into 96-well plates, with 2 × 103 cells per well. Each group had 6 replicates, and the plates were then incubated in a cell incubator for 24–96 h. Every 24 h, one plate was taken out. The original culture medium was carefully discarded, and the plate was washed with fresh PBS. Subsequently, 100 µL of fresh culture medium containing 10% CCK-8 detection reagent was added to each well, and the plate was incubated in the dark for 2 h in the incubator. The absorbance of each well was then measured using a microplate reader at the wavelength of 450 nm.

## Cell migration assay
Cells were resuspended in serum-free medium, adjusting the cell concentration to 2.0 × 105 cells/mL. Then, 200 µL of cell suspension was added to the upper layer of the chamber (sterile transwell with 8.0 μm pore polycarbonate membrane insert, purchased from Corning, USA), while 600 µL of cell culture medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower layer. The culture plate was then placed in the incubator for 16 h. After PBS washing, the chamber was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, followed by staining with 0.5% crystal violet for 10–15 min. Unmigrated cells in the upper layer were carefully removed with cotton swabs, and the number of migrated cells was observed and counted under a microscope.

## Cell invasion assay
The matrix gel was pre-melted on ice and diluted with serum-free medium at a ratio of 1:4. Then, 50 µL per well was added to the upper layer of the chamber at 37 °C for 30 min to solidify the gel for later use. The cells were resuspended in serum-free culture medium, where the cell concentration was adjusted to 2.0 × 105 cells/mL. Then, 200 µL of cell suspension was added to the upper layer of the chamber, while 600 µL of cell culture medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower layer. The culture plate was then placed in the cell incubator for 24 h. After PBS wash, the chamber was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, followed by staining with 0.5% crystal violet for 10–15 min. Non-invasion cells in the upper layer were carefully removed with cotton swabs, and the number of invading cells was observed and counted under a microscope.

## Cell colony formation assay
Cells were seeded into 3.5 cm cell culture dishes with 1 × 103 cells per dish and then cultured in a cell incubator. The growth status and density of the cells were monitored under a microscope on a daily basis. When significant cell colony formation was observed, cell culture was immediately halted. After washing with PBS, 4% paraformaldehyde was used for fixation for 20 min, followed by 0.5% crystal violet staining for 10–15 min. The staining solution was then discarded, and the unbound staining solution was rinsed away using PBS. The cells were left to air dry at room temperature. The size and number of cell colonies were recorded. A cell count > 50 was considered a positive colony, and the number of cell colonies in each group was calculated.

## Preparation and characterization of miRNA-encapsulated sEVs (miRNA@sEVs)
Freshly extracted sEVs and miRNA mimics were thoroughly mixed in a centrifuge tube, resulting in a final concentration of 1 × 1010/mL sEVs and 100 nM miRNA mimics. The centrifuge tube was placed in the ice water, and the tip sonication parameters were set as 500 V, 2 kHz, 20% amplitude, 6 cycles, with 5 s pulse/3 s pause (QSonicator 500, SONICS, USA). This process was repeated twice with a 2-min interval on ice to cool down. Next, the solution was transferred to an electrode cup, and the electroporation conditions were set to 110 V and 940 µF, using the CUY21EDIT II electroporator (BEX, Japan). After electroporation, the mixture was centrifuged at 100,000 g for 2 h to remove unbound miRNA, resulting in the precipitation of miRNA@sEVs.

## Fluorescent labeling of miRNA@sEVs
6.4 mg/mL sEVs and 1 mg/mL ErNPs were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min. ErNPs were purchased from Nirmidas Biotech, USA, and well characterized as reported [ ]. Basically, they were synthesized as α-phase Er-doped NaYF4 nanocrystal and functionalized with PEG-NH2 group to give off enhanced bright downconversion fluorescence upon excitation at 980 nm (size = ~ 14 nm; λex/λem=980 nm/1540 nm). Subsequently, the mixture was placed in an ultrasonic water bath and sonicated for 3 min. Then, the liquid was transferred to 100 kDa MWCO ultrafiltration tubes and centrifuged at 1,500 g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove unbound dyes. We also investigated the possible dye leakage in the fresh cell-free media over time after sEVs were stained by ErNPs. Specifically, the fluorescence of the newly stained miRNA@sEVs was recorded as the initial fluorescence intensity and kept incubated at 37 °C up to 24 h. The samples were taken at different time points and transferred to the ultrafiltration tube and centrifuged at 1500 × g for 30 min to separate the dye stained sEVs and supernatant. The fluorescence of supernatant was measured by the DeepVision NIR-II in vivo imaging system (Nirmidas Biotech, USA). The fluorescence intensity of the obtained supernatant at each time point was normalized to the initial fluorescence intensity, obtaining the cumulative release of dyes from stained sEVs as a function of time.

## Animal experiments
BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks old female) were purchased from the Cavens Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., China. Animals were housed and maintained in the Animal Facility of Jiangsu University, China, and all animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Jiangsu University. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. A total of 10 mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 5 for each group) to establish the tumor model, and 5.0 × 106 HCT116 cells were suspended in 200 µL PBS and injected subcutaneously into both sides of each mouse. Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane (2.5% in O2 at 2 L/min). When tumors were about ~ 0.5 cm in diameter, ErNPs-labeled sEVs, and ErNPs-labeled miRNA@sEVs were injected into the mice via the tail vein. DeepVision NIR-II in vivo imaging system (Nirmidas Biotech, USA) was applied. During the experiment, all animals were anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation (2.5% in O2 at 2 L/min). Imaging monitoring was conducted on mice at various time points post-injection, specifically at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24 and 48 h. Whole-body images of mice were acquired, mouse blood was collected, and fluorescence intensities in tumor sites were analyzed at each time point. For each tumor of one mouse, the fluorescence intensity at various time points was normalized to that acquired at initial time point. The mice were euthanized using carbon dioxide in an induction chamber, and major organs and tumors were immediately extracted for ex vivo imaging to assess the biodistribution profiles. In a separate experiment, a total of 15 mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 5 for each group), and ErNPs, ErNPs-sEVs and ErNPs-miRNA@sEVs were injected into mice via the tail vein, at 3-day intervals, for a total of four injections. Fluorescence accumulation at the tumor sites was monitored at 1, 2, 4, 8, 12 and 24 h post each injection, along with records of the body weights and tumor sizes. In parallel, a total of 20 mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5 for each group), and PBS, miRNA, sEVs and miRNA@sEVs were individually injected into each group of mice via the tail vein, every three days for a total of four injections. The weights of the mice and the sizes of their tumors were measured after each injection. After mice were euthanized, the blood was quickly collected for the assessment of liver and kidney function parameters, specifically aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (CREA). Simultaneously, major organs and tumors were processed and sliced for pathological analysis.

## Statistical analysis
All data presented in this article were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons between two groups were analyzed by Student’s t-test, while multiple group comparisons were analyzed by two-way ANOVA using GraphPad Prism 8.3.0 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

## Results

## Validation of miR-145 expression and its gene targets
A total of 62 human CRC tissue samples were previously extracted and preserved, and the expression of miR-145 in each pair of cancerous and paracancerous tissues was retrospectively examined using qRT-PCR. The results indicated that the miR-145 expression was significantly lowered in CRC tissues compared to that in adjacent (paracancerous) tissues (Fig.  A). Consequently, miR-145 overexpression and knockdown were performed in colorectal carcinoma derived cell lines HCT116 and SW480, respectively. As a result, in comparison to the control, overexpression of miR-145 significantly inhibited cell proliferation, colony formation and migration/invasion capabilities. Reversely, knockdown of miR-145 promoted those cellular activities (Supplementary File 1: Figures and ). Those results integrated that miR-145 may play a suppressive role in the CRC progression.
 Next, we explored the potential targets of miR-145 through databases including miRDB, TargetScan, miRTarBase and miRWalk. The top 10 targets were identified with the highest prediction probabilities by intersecting these datasets (Fig.  B), and these predictions were validated in HCT116 and SW480 cells (Fig.  C). The results revealed that FSCN1 and ADD3 were significantly downregulated in both cell lines, where FSCN1 was the lowest expression. Simultaneously, the results from the dual-luciferase reporter gene analysis displayed that miR-145 significantly reduced the luciferase activity of wild-type FSCN1. Moreover, following mutations at the corresponding binding sites, the interaction between miR-145 and FSCN1 mRNA was diminished (Fig.  D). For this reason, we assessed the expression of FSCN1 in clinical samples from patients with CRCs. As a result, in comparison to that in the paracancerous tissues, FSCN1 was significantly upregulated in cancerous tissues (Fig.  E), exhibiting its negative correlation with the expression of miR-145. Those findings supported that FSCN1 in the human CRC tissues and human colorectal carcinoma derived cell lines was the primary gene target of miR-145.
We next explored the interaction between miR-145 and FSCN1 at both gene and protein levels. MiR-145 mimics and inhibitors were individually transfected into either HCT116 or SW480 cells. Then qRT-PCR results indicated that the FSCN1 expression was significantly downregulated in case of miR-145 overexpression, while the level of FSCN1 was substantially upregulated upon miR-145 inhibition (Fig.  A and C). Consistently, Western blot results corroborated that miR-145 overexpression lowered the protein expression level of FSCN1 while miR-145 inhibition increased it (Fig.  B and D). Furthermore, FSCN1 siRNA was synthesized and transfected into CRC cell lines, silencing FSCN1 and reducing FSCN1 protein levels (Supplementary File 1: Figure ). Consequently, the FSCN1 silencing led to a notable inhibition on cell proliferation, colony formation, and cell migration and invasion capacities (Supplementary File 1: Figure ).
 

## Preparation and characterization of miRNA@sEVs
To obtain efficient and stable loading of miRNA into sEVs, various methods were implemented to assess and improve the loading efficiency. These methods included direct incubation, electroporation, sonication, electroporation followed by sonication, and sonication followed by electroporation, respectively. The qRT-PCR was utilized to validate the loading efficiency when miRNA expression in each group was measured and compared. Compared to the direct incubation, the electroporation resulted in a 2.6-fold increase of miR-145 loading, the sonication had a 2.2-fold increase, the electroporation plus sonication achieved a 5.6-fold increase, and the sonication followed by electroporation attained a 5.9-fold increase. Collectively, there was yet statistical difference of miR-145 loading efficiency in terms of different sequential orders when combining sonication and electroporation (Fig.  A).
 We then characterized the physicochemical and biological properties of miRNA@sEVs. Before and after miR-145 loading using sonication in combination with electroporation, sEVs consistently expressed surface protein markers such as CD9, TSG101 and Alix, but no intracellular Calnexin (Fig.  B). TEM images revealed that miRNA@sEVs maintained a cup-shaped vesicle structure and remained intact during modification (Fig.  C). NTA results indicated that the loading method had minimal impact on the size and zeta potential of sEVs, measuring a diameter of (157.6 ± 1.5) nm (Fig.  D) and zeta potential of (-17.3 ± 0.2) mV.
To further validate the successful loading of miR-145 into sEVs, the interaction between miRNA@sEVs and its intracellular target FSCN1 was then examined. First, miRNA@sEVs (6.4 mg/mL sEVs + 0.5 µM miR-145) were added to HCT116 and SW480 cells for 48 h, and RNAs and proteins were then extracted from the cells to detect changes of FSCN1 at both gene and protein levels. Concurrently, the untreated group, the group treated with sEVs (6.4 mg/mL) alone, the group directly added with miR-145 mimics (0.5 µM), and the group transfected with miR-145 mimics (0.5 µM) using Lipofectamine 2000, designated as miR-145 (lipo), were set up in the same experiments for comparison. Our findings revealed that the gene and protein expression of FSCN1 in the miRNA@sEVs group and the group transfected with miR-145 were significantly lowered compared to those in the untreated group (Fig.  E and F), demonstrating a notable tumor-suppressing effect that inhibited cell proliferation, colony formation, migration and invasion capabilities (Supplementary File 1: Figure ). These results further endorsed the successful and efficacious loading of miR-145 into sEVs, which subsequently exerted its anti-tumor effects through efficient intracellular delivery by sEVs.

## In vivo profiles of miRNA@sEVs
We investigated in vivo profiles of miRNA@sEVs in murine cancer models. NIR-II fluorescent probes ErNPs were adopted to stain sEVs or miRNA@sEVs, and their fluorescent properties were measured to determine the dye concentration and physical stability (Supplementary File 1: Figure A). Firstly, the fluorescent linear range of ErNPs was examined. The original solution of ErNPs was diluted at ratios of 1:1, 1:2, 1:5, 1:10, 1:20, 1:50, 1:100, 1:200, and 1:500 in PBS. The NIR-II small animal live imaging instrument was applied to measure the fluorescence intensity versus each concentration at λex/λem=980 nm/1540 nm. The results showed that the fluorescence intensity of ErNPs increased linearly when the concentration fell in the range of 0–1 mg/mL. Thus, 1 mg/mL of ErNPs was determined for the working concentration in the following experiments.
Subsequently, 1 mg/mL ErNPs were incubated with sEVs and miRNA@sEVs, respectively, and the labeled particles were transferred to ultracentrifugation tubes at different time points (i.e., 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h after labeling), followed by thorough PBS washing and fluorescence determination of ErNPs-labeled sEVs or miRNA@sEVs. The fluorescence intensity of the obtained supernatant at each time point was normalized to the initial fluorescence intensity of ErNPs-stained sEVs or miRNA@sEVs, and the cumulative release of ErNPs from stained particles was obtained over time. The results demonstrated that the initial release of ErNPs from stained sEVs or miRNA@sEVs showed a linearly increasing trend over time but finally reached a plateau after 15 h release (Supplementary File 1: Figure B). Thereby, for later animal experiments, 1 mg/mL ErNPs were first incubated with sEVs and miRNA@sEVs, respectively, for > 15 h to ensure the staining stability (i.e., no further dye leakage). Afterwards, ErNPs-sEVs and ErNPs-miRNA@sEVs were prepared and injected into mice bearing HCT116 subcutaneous tumors through the tail vein. At time of injection, the dimension of tumors reached 0.8–0.9 cm in all groups.
The in vivo images of mice at various time points were illustrated in Fig.  A. Concurrently, venous blood was collected from mice to assess the pharmacokinetics of sEVs and miRNA@sEVs (Fig.  B), while the accumulation of sEVs and miRNA@sEVs at the tumor sites was monitored over time (Fig.  C). Our findings revealed that ErNPs-sEVs and ErNPs-miRNA@sEVs exhibited comparable circulation time (i.e., 4.0 ± 0.4 h and 4.1 ± 0.2 h). Following injections, fluorescent accumulation at the tumor sites increased, reaching a peak at ~ 8 h when the fluorescence intensity attained ~ 1.5 times the initial value. After mice were sacrificed, major organs were examined ex vivo to investigate the biodistribution of injected particles. These results indicated that the fluorescent signals of ErNPs-sEVs and ErNPs-miRNA@sEVs were predominantly localized in the liver and spleen. By normalizing the fluorescence intensity of each tissue to that of the liver as the largest uptake upon tail vein injection, it was observed that the tumors in the ErNPs-sEVs group and ErNPs-miRNA@sEVs group accounted for (15.4 ± 3.1)% and (20.7 ± 3.0)% of liver uptake, respectively, although no statistical difference was noticed between the two groups (p > 0.05) (Fig.  D). All the results pointed out that the incorporation of miR-145 into sEVs via electroporation and sonication in our study kept the membraned structure intact, minimizing the impact on its in vivo profiles, including pharmacokinetics, tumor accumulation and biodistribution.
 

## The therapeutic effect of miRNA@sEVs in murine models of human CRC
To explore the therapeutic effect of miRNA@sEVs in vivo, 200 µL of ErNPs (1 mg/mL), ErNPs-sEVs (1 mg/mL ErNPs + 6.4 mg/mL sEVs) and ErNPs-miRNA@sEVs (1 mg/mL ErNPs + 6.4 mg/mL sEVs + 0.5 µM miR-145) were injected into mice bearing subcutaneous tumors via the tail vein, respectively, subject to treatments administered every 3 days for a total of 4 times (Fig.  A). At time of the first injection, the dimension of tumors reached ~ 0.5 cm in all groups. As a result, over each course of 4 periodic treatments, the fluorescence intensity in the tumors of ErNPs group declined rapidly, and this fluorescence intensity after 24 h became barely detectable. For ErNPs-sEVs and ErNPs-miRNA@sEVs groups, the fluorescence accumulation at the tumor sites climbed after each injection, and it gradually decreased after the peak fluorescence accumulation was attained to be 1.3–1.4 times the initial value at ~ 2 h post-injection. The time point that corresponds to the highest fluorescence intensity following each injection was different from previous pharmacokinectics experiments, where it reached peak intensity at ~ 8 h post-injection, possibly due to much smaller tumor sizes applied here (0.5 cm versus 0.8 cm).
 When four treatment sessions ended, mice were sacrificed where major organs and tumor tissues were extracted for fluorescence imaging. Biodistribution results displayed that in all three groups the fluorescence signals were predominantly localized in the liver and spleen, followed by tumors (Fig.  B). Compared to the ErNPs group where the tumor accumulation was (2.1 ± 2.9)%, the ErNPs-sEVs and ErNPs-miRNA@sEVs groups had much higher tumor accumulation of (13.9 ± 5.9)% and (12.6 ± 2.9)%, respectively, with no statistical difference between the two groups. For all three groups, there was no noticeable weight loss in mice through different treatments over time (Fig.  C). In parallel, the tumor volumes in the ErNPs-sEVs and ErNPs-miRNA@sEVs groups were suppressed to (9.6 ± 18.1)% and (61.6 ± 12.7)% of that in the ErNPs group, respectively, with the inhibition on tumor growth due to ErNPs-miRNA@sEVs treatment being the most pronounced (Fig.  D).
Next, 200 µL of PBS, sEVs (6.4 mg/mL), miR-145 mimics (0.5 µM) and miR-145@sEVs (6.4 mg/mL sEVs + 0.5 µM miR-145) were individually injected into mice with subcutaneous tumors via the tail vein. Over the 30-day treatment, tumor growth in mice of each group was monitored and recorded. Compared to the PBS group, the sEVs, miR-145, and miRNA@sEVs group significantly suppressed tumor growth to a greater extent, with the miRNA@sEVs group exhibiting the most noted inhibitory effect (Fig.  A). By comparing ex vivo tumor sizes after mice were sacrificed and tumor tissues were extracted, it was observed that tumor volumes in the sEVs and miR-145 groups were suppressed to (16.1 ± 37.2)% and (43.3 ± 15.0)% of that in the PBS group, whereas the miRNA@sEVs treatment inhibited the tumor growth to (74.1 ± 8.3)% (Fig.  B).
 We further analyzed the expression of FSCN1 in tumor tissues from each group. The qRT-PCR results depicted that compared to the PBS group the sEVs group showed no noticeable difference in the gene expression level of FSCN1 in tumors, whereas the miR-145 group and miRNA@sEVs group exhibited significant FSCN1 downregulation to (75.8 ± 6.1)% and (51.3 ± 9.4)%, respectively (p < 0.05) (Fig.  C). Consistently, Western blotting experiments yielded similar results, as the miR-145 group and miRNA@sEVs group displayed substantial decrease in FSCN1 protein expression to (72.5 ± 3.1)% and (44.8 ± 1.7)%, respectively (p < 0.001) (Fig.  D). Furthermore, immunohistochemical staining of tumor samples from each treatment group revealed that the sEVs group, miR-145 group and miRNA@sEVs group had much smaller numbers of FSCN1-positive cells than the PBS group, where miRNA@sEVs group demonstrated the most pronounced FSCN1 reduction (Fig.  E). Those results corroborated effective downregulation of FSCN1 by miR-145 in both gene and protein levels when delivered by sEVs.
In parallel to validating the tumor suppression of miRNA@sEVs in vivo, its biological safety was also assessed. Through the treatment, the body weights in mice were continuously monitored, and there were no significant changes among the PBS, sEVs, miR-145 and miRNA@sEVs treated mice (Supplementary File 1: Figure A). At the same time, no pathological changes were found in tumor tissues of each group (Supplementary File 1: Figure B). Before mice were sacrificed, venous blood from each group of mice was collected to measure the liver and kidney functions. No noticeable abnormalities were observed under all treatment conditions (Supplementary File 1: Figure C). Put together, miRNA@sEVs as prepared through a combination of sonication and electroporation techniques, exhibited the most significant suppression on CRC growth and progression with excellent biocompatibility.

## Discussion
In this study we first verified that miR-145 was an effective tumor suppressor in clinical samples from patients with CRCs, and it exerted its tumor-inhibitory effect by downregulating its primary target FSCN1. Then, we utilized sEVs derived from hucMSCs as delivering carriers to encapsulate miR-145 and enhanced the loading efficiency of miR-145 in sEVs by employing a strategic combination of sonication and electroporation techniques. The resulting miRNA@sEVs demonstrated exceptional biocompatibility, which contributed to prolonged circulation in the bloodstream and increased accumulation within tumors. Consequently, these nanoscaled miRNA@sEVs showed substantial suppression of tumor progression both in vitro and in vivo by effectively downregulating the expression of FSCN1 protein.
Recent research suggests that miR-145 is implicated in various malignant tumors by regulating oncogenes and signal transduction related to cell proliferation, angiogenesis, tumor progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance [ ]. For instance, miR-145 has been identified as a diagnostic marker for chondrosarcoma, where it may inhibit the tumor progression by downregulating the expression of FSCN1 [ ]. Similarly, miR-145 was found to be downregulated in hepatocellular carcinoma, reducing cancer cell proliferation by targeting insulin receptor substrate 1 [ ]. Moreover, miR-145 can suppress non-small cell lung cancer by downregulating its dual targets, GOLM1 and RTKN [ ].
One miRNA may target multiple gene transcripts within one specific cancer type, and the primary targets can vary in different stages of the same cancer or across different types of cancers [ ]. In human CRCs, miR-145 may modulate a pool of gene targets at each step of tumor development [ ]. For example, miR-145-5p, one form of mature miR-145, suppresses cell growth, metastasis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition in CRC by targeting cell cycle-associated protein-3 (CDCA3) [ ]. Also, miR-145 inhibits the invasion and migration of CRC cells by targeting the twist family bHLH transcription factor 1 (TWIST1) [ ]. However, conflicting results have been reported. MiR-145 may bind directly to and suppress AF9, decreasing expression of gluconeogenic genes and promoting glycolysis, potentially leading to carcinogenesis and CRC development [ ]. Moreover, while miR-145-5p was found tumor suppressive in the early stages of CRC, it promoted metastasis in the late stages by regulating AKT signaling [ ]. Further research is needed to clarify whether and how miR-145 can bind its various gene targets and regulate tumor development during different stages of CRC.
Here we delineated that miR-145 deliverd by hucMSC-derived sEVs primarily targeted FSCN1 and effectively lowered its expression in CRCs, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation, colony formation, migration and invasion, which eventually led to substantial tumor suppression. Notably, the interaction between miR-145 and FSCN1 has been reported in several malignant tumors, where FSCN1 expression was negatively correlated to miR-145 [ , ]. Specifically downregulated by certain long non-coding RNA inhibition [ ] or by promoter hypermethylation [ ], the loss of miR-145 expression, which directly interacts with 3’-untranslated region of FSCN1 messenger RNA, upregulates FSCN1 expression at the mRNA and posttranscriptional levels, so promoting tumor progression [ , ]. Our results here validated the role of miR-145/FSCN1 axis in CRC progression, which stood well in line with those reports.
Highly conserved across species, human FSCN1 protein is widely expressed in various organs, which cross-links actin microfilaments into well-structured bundles, providing rigidity and stability for cytoskeleton remodeling and cell adhesion/migration [ ]. Aberrantly elevated levels of FSCN1 have been found in a variety of cancer types, closely associated with promoted cell prefiltration, migration and invasion, and neovascularization, thus being recognized as a poor prognostic indicator and a potential druggable target [ – ]. In prostate cancer high expression of FSCN1 induces cytoskeleton reorganization, aggregating actin bundles into filamentous pseudopodia, therefore promoting migration, invasion and lymph node metastasis [ ]. Alternatively, FSCN1 contributes to lung adenocarcinoma development through activation of PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, stimulating cell proliferation and inhibiting cell apoptosis [ ]. In hepatoblastoma, FSCN1 activates the Wnt/β-catenin pathway by stabilizing the entry of β-catenin into the nucleus and driving the transcription of oncogenes [ ]. Nevertheless, with regards to the ensuing FSCN1-mediated tumor progression in CRC, it requires further investigation to explore the exact mechanism or pathway following miR-145 regulation.
Derived from their parental cells, sEVs possess high biocompatibility and low immunogenicity due to their endogenous origin. Their surface proteins help evade immune detection, reducing clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system. HucMSC-derived sEVs inherit the homing characteristics of parental cells and can actively recognize the tumor microenvironment through adhesion molecules such as integrins [ ]. In contrast, artificial particles at similar nano to micro scale, liposomes for example, albeit surface modification such as tunable PEGylation may reduce immune recognition, can still provoke an array of immune responses and induce local or systemic toxicity [ ]. At the same time, the targeting capacity of liposomes relies on the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, whereas tumor vascular heterogeneity may greatly limit their efficiencies [ ]. Furthermore, cell-derived sEVs and synthesized liposomes can both efficiently carry xenobiotic drugs for delivery, while sEVs own unique therapeutic pleiotropy as it can inherit biologically active molecules from their parental cells. For this reason, with similar efforts to load exogenous cargos of pharmaceutical interest into both drug carriers, endogenous substances in sEVs can be exploited to take synergistic effects for enhanced treatment [ ].
Efficiently loading therapeutic substances (such as chemical drugs and exogenous nucleic acids) into sEVs and precisely delivering them to the specific target in the body may govern the effectiveness of cancer therapy. To begin, gene cargos can be encapsulated into sEVs through direct or indirect method. The direct method employs physical or/and chemical techniques to import the cargo directly into sEVs, including incubation, electroporation, sonication, extrusion and freeze-thaw cycles. In contrast, the indirect loading first integrates the gene into the donor cells through gene transfection and co-incubation, followed by the secretion and isolation of sEVs [ ]. In this study we selected sEVs derived from hucMSCs as the delivery platform to encapsulate miR-145 via a combination of sonication and electroporation. Compared to direct incubation with very low efficiency, this combinational method achieved a 5.9-fold improvement of miRNA loading, much higher than electroporation or sonication alone. This loading capacity was also superior to our previous result when miR-193 was loaded into hucMSC-derived sEVs via sonication with optimized energy input [ ]. Simultaneously, encapsulation of miRNAs by sEVs protected them from immature degradation before reaching the target, exerting tumor suppression to the best of their capacities [ ]. Put together, sEVs prove themselves as exceptional gene drug delivery vehicles in cancer therapy.
To date, NIR-II fluorescence bioimaging has found widespread applications in the biomedical field, with single-walled carbon nanotubes, quantum dots, gold nanoparticles, and rare earth nanoparticles commonly implemented as contrast agents. Compared to NIR-I, the NIR-II fluorescence imaging window offers higher spatial resolution and allows deeper tissue penetration [ ]. To monitor and track the circulation of miRNA@sEVs in vivo in a real-time manner, we utilized ErNPs as a tracer probe, examining the pharmacokinetics and tumor accumulation of miRNA@sEVs. Our results indicated that miR-145 encapsulation had no noticeable impact on the pharmacokinetics of husMSC-derived sEVs, displaying a circulation half-life of approximately 4 h, consistent with our previous findings [ ]. Similarly, the presence or absence of miR-145 did not significantly alter the accumulation of sEVs at tumor sites, showing an initial increase followed by a gradual decline until clearance from the body, aligning with our earlier reports [ , ]. Importantly, miR-145 loaded and delivered by sEVs successfully inhibited tumor progression by downregulating FSCN1 expression in the xenografted murine model of human CRC. Those results further demonstrated the excellent biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, high stability, and minimized toxicity of sEVs as a delivery platform of bioactive materials, such as therapeutic nucleic acids.

## Conclusion
In this study, we fabricated miRNA-encapsulated sEVs using a combination of sonication and electroporation. We employed ErNPs as an NIR-II fluorescence tracer for miRNA@sEVs, allowing us to elucidate their biological profiles in vivo, including the circulation time, biodistribution, and tumor accumulation. Our results demonstrated that miRNA@sEVs, after effectively loading of miR-145 into sEVs, greatly inhibited the progression of CRC by downregulating the expression of FSCN1 as its primary target. This study offers a new strategy that utilizes sEVs as a biosafe and reliable delivery platform, enabling non-invasive real-time monitoring of gene therapy during tumor treatment under an NIR-II fluorescence regimen.
