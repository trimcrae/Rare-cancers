## Abstract
Patient behavior and physiology can directly affect cancer metabolism. Smoking is the leading risk factor for non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). In this study, we identified that smoking modulates lung cancer cell metabolism through altered protein post-translational modification. Proteomic analyses identified elevated K251 succinylation (K251-Su) of GAPDH, a key enzyme in glycolysis, in NSCLC samples, and GAPDH K251-Su was significantly higher in patients who smoke compared with nonsmokers. Exposure of lung cancer cells to cigarette smoke extract led to increased uptake of glutamine and enhanced GAPDH K251-Su. Glutamine uptake by cancer cells in hypoxic and nutrient-deficient microenvironments provided succinyl-CoA donors for GAPDH succinylation at K251, which was catalyzed by acyltransferase p300. K251-Su increased GAPDH stability by suppressing TRIM4-mediated K254 ubiquitination. GAPDH K251-Su enhanced glycolysis and glutamine reductive carboxylation to meet the demands for cell growth and to support survival in hypoxic and nutrient-depleted conditions, promoting tumor growth and metastasis. These findings indicate that tobacco smoking mediates metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells through succinylation of GAPDH to drive NSCLC progression.

## Significance:
Smoking-induced GAPDH succinylation coordinates glycolysis and glutamine metabolism and supports lung cancer cell survival in stressful microenvironments to promote tumor progression, highlighting quitting smoking as a potential strategy to target cancer metabolism.

## Introduction
Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer death, among which, 85% are non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Approximately 40% of patients with NSCLC have distant metastases at the time of diagnosis because of no obvious clinical symptoms in the early stages ( ). Tobacco smoking is the leading risk factor for lung cancer, accounting for nearly two thirds of all lung cancer cases globally ( ). Quitting smoking at or around diagnosis was significantly associated with improved overall survival ( ). Multiple studies have revealed the effect of tobacco smoking on genomic mutations, epigenetic variation, and immune escape in promoting cancer progression ( ). However, the mechanism of smoking-induced metabolic reprogramming of cancer cells remains unclear.
The reprogramming of energy metabolism is an important feature of cancer ( ). Metabolic processes of glucose ( ), amino acids ( ), nucleic acids ( ), and lipids in tumor cells undergo adaptive changes to maintain the energy supply, anabolic precursors, and redox homeostasis that are required for tumor malignant progression ( – ). Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) is a key enzyme in glycolysis and catalyzes the conversion of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate to 1,3-diphosphoglycerate ( ). In its native state, GAPDH is a tetramer structure composed of four identical 37 kDa subunits ( ). As a housekeeping gene necessary for cell metabolism, it has been widely used as an internal reference for protein and nucleic acid detection. However, recent studies found that GAPDH is abnormally highly expressed in a variety of cancers. For example, high expression of GAPDH in colon cancer promotes the metastasis of colon cancer cells by upregulating the expression of snail ( ). Increased expression of GAPDH in breast cancer, lung cancer, and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma is closely related to cell proliferation and invasion ( , ). An integrative omics study in lung adenocarcinoma found that high expression of GAPDH protein was associated with poor prognosis of patients ( – ). In terms of the regulation mechanism of GAPDH expression, most studies focus on the gene transcriptional level. For example, insulin, hypoxia-inducible factor 1, and p53 promote the mRNA expression of GAPDH ( , ). However, the role of post-translational modification in regulating the stable expression of GAPDH has not been reported.
Lysine succinylation modification was first identified by Zhao’s study in 2010 ( ). In eukaryotes and prokaryotes, succinylation is a conserved modification that causes significant changes in the charge of the lysine residue side chain (from +1 to −1) and molecular weight (+100.0186 Da). Therefore, succinylation causes more remarkable changes in protein conformation and function than other modifications ( ). The succinyl donors are derived from succinyl-CoA, which comes from a variety of metabolic pathways, including the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, catabolism of some branched chain amino acids and odd chain fatty acids, and porphyrin synthesis ( , ). It has been reported that lysine acetyltransferase 2A (KAT2A/GCN5; ref. ), carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A; ref. ), α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex [main subunit is dihydrolipoamide succinyltransferase (DLST); ref. ] and p300/CBP have succinyltransferase activity ( , ). By comprehensive use of protein PTMomics and bioinformatics analysis, it is proved that succinylation is widely present in the enzymes of cell energy metabolism and is involved in the regulation of multiple metabolic pathways including the TCA cycle, the pentose phosphate pathway, amino acid metabolism, and fatty acid metabolism ( – ). In the study of liver cancer, gastric cancer, breast cancer, and renal cancer, it has been further confirmed that succinylation plays an important role in regulating the energy metabolism of tumor cells by modulating key enzymes involved in cell metabolism such as pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2; ref. ), 2-hydroxyglutarate dehydrogenase (OGDH; ref. ), glutaminase (GLS; ref. ), and succinylate dehydrogenase complex subunit A (SDHA; ref. ).
In this study, we show that tobacco smoking is related to increased uptake of glutamine by cancer cells under hypoxic and nutrient-deficient (Nu-Def) tumor microenvironment. Enhanced glutamine catabolism provides more succinyl-CoA donors for the succinylation of a key glycolytic enzyme, GAPDH. Elevated GAPDH K251 succinylation (K251-Su) readjusts cell glucose and glutamine metabolism to meet the demands for cancer cell growth, resulting in the progression and metastasis of NSCLC.

## Materials and Methods

## Cell lines and cell culture
Cells were obtained from the ATCC (A549, sex: male, RRID: CVCL_0023; NCI-H2170, sex: male, RRID: CVCL_1535; NCI-H460, sex: male, RRID: CVCL_0459; NCI-H520, sex: male, RRID: CVCL_1566; IMR-90, sex: female, RRID: CVCL_0347; and BEAs-2B, sex: female, RRID: CVCL_0168). The cell lines were tested and authenticated using short tandem repeat DNA profiling by Beijing Microread Genetics and were cultured at 37°C under 5% CO2 in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 2 mmol/L L-glutamine. A549 and NCI-H2170 cells were used to generate cells transiently overexpressing p300 and GAPDH wild-type (WT) and mutant proteins. Cell lines with a stably silenced GAPDH gene and then overexpressed WT or mutant GAPDH genes were generated from A549 and NCI-H2170 cells (A549/NCI-H2170-GAPDH-WT; A549/NCI-H2170-GAPDH-K251R/E; and A549-GAPDH-K251R/ K254R). The Mycoplasma detection of cells was carried out using the PCR Mycoplasma Detection Set (Takara, catalog no. 6601), which is a regular detection conducted by the cell bank in our laboratory. The cells were generally passaged two or three times per week, and the cell culture time was determined according to specific experimental requirements.

## Mouse models and ethics approval

The animal experiments were performed in strict accordance with the People’s Republic of China Legislation Regarding the Use and Care of Laboratory Animals. All protocols used in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Fourth Military Medical University.

## IP and co-IP
IP and coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) were performed using the Pierce Crosslink Immunoprecipitation Kit (cat. #26147, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the Pierce Co-Immunoprecipitation Kit (cat. #26149, Thermo Fisher Scientific), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and a mixture of deacetylase inhibitors was added in lysis buffer when detecting GAPDH succinylation. Anti-Flag tag (Proteintech, cat. #66008-3-Ig, RRID: AB_2749837) and anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, cat. #60004-1-Ig, RRID: AB_2107436) antibodies were crosslinked onto a Protein A/G resin for IP and co-IP. Anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, cat. #60004-1-Ig, RRID: AB_2107436) and anti-His tag (Proteintech, cat. #66005-1-Ig, RRID: AB_11232599) antibodies were used for antibody immobilization for co-IP.

## Detection and analysis of GAPDH succinylation
According to the above IP scheme, GAPDH protein was purified from human NSCLC cells and then the protein solution was separated on an SDS-PAGE gel. The band was cut from the gel according to the molecular weight position of GAPDH (∼36 kDa) for downstream detection.

## In-gel digestion
In-gel tryptic digestion was carried out as previously described ( ). The gel pieces were destained in 50 mmol/L NH4HCO3 in 50% acetonitrile (v/v) until clear and dehydrated with 100 μL of 100% acetonitrile for 5 minutes. The liquid was removed, and the gel pieces were rehydrated in 10 mmol/L dithiothreitol and incubated at 56°C for 60 minutes. Gel pieces were again dehydrated in 100% acetonitrile, liquid was removed, and gel pieces were rehydrated with 55 mmol/L iodoacetamide. Samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 45 minutes. Gel pieces were washed with 50 mmol/L NH4HCO3 and dehydrated with 100% acetonitrile. Gel pieces were rehydrated with 10 ng/μL trypsin in 50 mmol/L NH4HCO3 on ice for 1 hour. Excess liquid was removed, and gel pieces were digested with trypsin at 37°C overnight. Peptides were extracted with 50% acetonitrile/5% formic acid, followed by 100% acetonitrile. Peptides were dried to completion and resuspended in 2% acetonitrile/0.1% formic acid.

## LC/MS-MS analysis
The tryptic peptides were dissolved in 0.1% formic acid (solvent A) and directly loaded onto a homemade reverse-phase analytic column with C18 resin (15-cm length, 75-μm i.d.). The gradient was from 6% to 23% solvent B (0.1% formic acid in 98% acetonitrile) over 16 minutes, 23% to 35% over 8 minutes, and climbing to 80% over 3 minutes and then holding at 80% for the last 3 minutes, all at a constant flow rate of 400 nL/minute on an EASY-nLC 1000 ultra performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The peptides were subjected to nano spray ionization (NSI) source, followed by tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) in Q Exactive Plus (Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID: SCR_020552) coupled online to the UPLC. The electrospray voltage applied was 2.0 kV. The m/z scan range was 350 to 1,800 for full scans and intact peptides were detected in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 70,000. Peptides were then selected for MS/MS using an normalized collision energy (NCE) setting of 28 and the fragments were detected in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 17,500. A data-dependent procedure was used that alternated between one MS scan followed by 20 MS/MS scans with 15.0 s dynamic exclusion. The automatic gain control was set at 5E4.

## Data processing
Succinylation modification was identified in the original MS data using Proteome Discoverer 2.4 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID: SCR_014477). The protein sequence of 335 amino acids of human GAPDH (P04406) in the UniProt (RRID: SCR_002380) database was used as the search object. Trypsin/P was specified as the cleavage enzyme and allowed up to two missing cleavages. The mass error was set to 10 parts per million for precursor ions and 0.02 Da for fragment ions. Carbamidomethyl on Cys was specified as a fixed modification and oxidation on Met and succinylation on Lys were specified as variable modifications. Peptide confidence was set at high, and the peptide ion score was set at >20.

## Western blotting
Each protein sample was extracted from cultured NSCLC cells using RIPA lysis buffer (cat. #P0013, Beyotime Biotechnology) with a mixture of deacetylase inhibitors (cat. #P1113, Beyotime Biotechnology) when detecting GAPDH succinylation. Samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE (10–100 μg) and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Millipore). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the corresponding primary and secondary antibodies were incubated to visualize the protein. The images were obtained with a Gel Doc EZ Imager (Bio-Rad) and analyzed with Image Lab software (Bio-Rad, RRID: SCR_014210).

## Dot blots
The antigenic peptide was placed on the polyvinylidene difluoride membrane according to the concentration gradient. After the surface of the membrane was dry, the blocking solution was added at room temperature for 60 minutes. The membrane was washed with 1× Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween 20 (TBST) for 10 minutes. GAPDH K251-Su antibody was diluted with 5% non-fat milk, incubated at room temperature for 2 hours, and washed three times with 1× TBST for 10 minutes each time. The goat anti–rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody was diluted and added, incubated at room temperature for 1 hour, and washed three times with 1× TBST for 10 minutes each time. After washing the membrane, a chemiluminescence chromogenic substrate was added for exposure.

## Real-time PCR
Real-time PCR was carried out as previously described ( ). The total RNA of the cells was extracted using an RNA separation kit (cat. #D6934-01, Omega Bio-tek) and reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (cat. #RR037A, Takara). Real-time PCR analysis of cDNA was performed using an ABI 7200 Analyzer (Applied Biosystems). TB Green (cat. #RR8820, Takara) was used as the fluorescent probe and actin as the internal control. The amplification reaction was performed on the MiniOpticon system (Bio-Rad). The relative mRNA expression of the target gene was normalized to actin. All primers were synthesized by Beijing Genomics Institute, with the following sequence information:
GAPDH forward 5′-AGA​AGG​CTG​GGG​CTC​ATT​TG-3′, GAPDH reverse 5′-AGG​GGC​CAT​CCA​CAG​TCT​TC-3′; actin forward 5′-CAC​CAT​TGG​CAA​TGA​GCG​GTT​C-3′, and actin reverse 5′-AGG​TCT​TTG​CGG​ATG​TCC​ACG​T-3′.

## NSCLC tissue MS analysis
MS analysis of NSCLC tissues was carried out as previously described ( ). Briefly, lung cancer tissues were treated with RIPA lysate, protein was extracted, and the protein sample was run on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel. After the protein molecular weight distribution was identified by Coomassie bright blue staining, the gel was divided into five equal parts. The gel sample was evaporated with acetonitrile and reconstituted in 1% (v/v) formic acid. The protein peptide was then digested by trypsin, and the peptide sample was injected into the mass spectrometer (model LTQ, Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID: SCR_020551). The mass spectrometer was calibrated using standard compounds and operated in the data-dependent mode, in which the instrument cycled between full MS scans (m/z 300–2,000), and the MS data were collected by targeting MS scans on the 10 most abundant ions occurring in the MS scan. Finally, the mass spectrometry raw data of NSCLC tissues were used for further analysis of differential proteins and succinylation modification.

## Histopathologic analysis
Briefly, the tissue was fixed overnight with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sliced into 5-mm sections. Slices were baked at 70°C for 1 hour, xylene dewaxing was performed, and gradient hydration (100%, 90%, 80%, and 70% ethanol and distilled water in turn for 2 minutes, respectively) was done. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, dehydration, transparency, and sealing were performed sequentially. Changes in histopathologic features were observed under the microscope and photographed.

## IHC analysis
Samples for IHC analysis were obtained from serial tissue microarrays of NSCLC purchased from Shanghai Outdo Biotech and paraffin sections of human or mouse animal models were made in our laboratory. Paraffin sections were dewaxed and hydrated and then sequentially subjected to 10 μmol/L citrate buffer at pH 6.0 for antigen repair and a methanolic solution containing 3% hydrogen peroxide was used for endogenous peroxidase blocking. The sections were then blocked with goat serum for 30 minutes and incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4°C. Following the antibody incubation, immunoperoxidase staining was conducted using a General SP kit (catalog no. SP-9000, ZSGB-BIO), and the sections were treated with 3,30-diaminobenzidine (catalog no. ZLI-9019, ZSGB-BIO) to detect the target proteins. Hematoxylin was used to counterstain the nuclei. The IHC results were evaluated by two independent pathologists who were blinded to the experiment. The total score of IHC results was obtained based on the intensity and density of positive cells. The intensity of positive cells was categorized as follows: 0 (no staining; defined as negative), 1 (weak), 2 (medium), and 3 (strong). The density of positive cells was classified as 0 (staining ≤ 5%; defined as negative), 1 (5% < staining ≤ 25%), 2 (25% < staining ≤ 50%), 3 (50% < staining ≤ 75%), and 4 (75% < staining ≤ 100%). According to the total score of the results, “0 < total score ≤ 5” was classified as being in the low expression group, and “5 < total score ≤ 7” was classified as being in the high expression group.

After incubation, the diluted oligonucleotide and diluted Lipofectamine 2000 were mixed, and then the oligomer–Lipofectamine 2000 mixture was added to each well containing cells and medium was added after 20 minutes of incubation at room temperature. The cells were incubated in a CO2 incubator at 37°C for 72 hours for subsequent testing of the gene interference efficiency of siRNA. ## Immunofluorescence staining
The cells were inoculated in the cell culture chamber, and after overnight adhesion, the medium was discarded, and cells were washed with 1× PBS three times for 5 minutes each. The cells were fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution at room temperature for a duration of 10 minutes, followed by three consecutive washes with 1× PBS for 5 minutes each. Subsequently, the cells were blocked with a 5% goat serum at room temperature for 30 minutes and then incubated with the primary antibodies overnight at a temperature of 4°C. Following this, the cells were washed three times with 1× PBS for 5 minutes each and finally incubated with the appropriate fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies and DAPI mixture. Following three washes with 1× PBS for 5 minutes each, the samples were examined using a confocal fluorescence microscope (Nikon) to evaluate colocalization.

## Generation of anti–GAPDH K251-Su antibody
The anti–GAPDH K251-Su antibody was custom-ordered from PTM Biolabs and was generated in rabbits using the peptide TSRLE-(succinyl)K-PAKYDC (KLH conjugated) as an antigen. A total of four immunizations were performed. The first immunization dose was 0.5 mg, whereas half of the first dose was used for the next three immunizations. Finally, the serum was obtained for purification with peptides TSRLE-(succinyl)K-PAKYDC and CRLEKPAKYDDK.

## Cigarette smoke extract preparation
The sponge filter from one cigarette (The Shaanxi Yan’an brand cigarettes from China containing 1.0 mg of nicotine, 10 mg of tar, and 11 mg of carbon monoxide per cigarette) was removed. After igniting the cigarettes, a 50 mL syringe pre-filled with 10 mL of glutamine-free and glucose-free RPMI-1640 medium was utilized to draw in an equivalent volume of smoke. Following each inhalation, the syringe was agitated horizontally 10 times rapidly to facilitate the dissolution of cigarette smoke into the medium. This process was continued until the cigarette was fully consumed. After the collected smoke was dissolved in 10 mL of medium, the pH of the medium was adjusted to 7.5. Subsequently, bacteria were filtered using a filter with a 0.22-μm pore size. The resulting solution, which exhibited an optical density (OD) of 1.40 ± 0.12 at 320 nm as measured by an ultraviolet spectrophotometer, was designated as the 100% cigarette smoke extract (CSE) stock solution. Subsequent concentrations of CSE were prepared by diluting the stock solution with glutamine-free and glucose-free RPMI-1640 medium.

## Glutamine colorimetric assay
The A549 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing varying concentrations of glucose (0.5 and 4.5 g/L) and FBS (1% and 10%) to induce nutrient deficiency and nutrient sufficiency conditions, respectively. Additionally, the cells were exposed to 0% and 15% CSE for 24 hours in an incubator at 37°C, 5% CO2, and 2% O2. After the abovementioned treatment, a glutamine colorimetric assay kit (E-BC-K853-M; Elabscience Biotechnology) was used to determine the concentration of glutamine according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Glutamine undergoes catalysis by enzymes, leading to the reduction of NAD+ to NADH. NADH reduces WST-8 to an orange-yellow formazan under the action of a hydrogen-transmitting substance, which exhibits a maximum absorption peak at approximately 450 nm. The concentration of glutamine within the system can be quantified by monitoring the change in light absorption at 450 nm.

## Human succinyl CoA test
Cells were incubated at 37°C in an incubator with 5% CO2 for 24 hours and treated with different doses of glutamine (0.5 and 6 mmol/L) or glutamate (10 and 80 mg/mL) under hypoxic/Nu-Def (2% O2, 1% FBS, and 0.5 g/L glucose) or normoxic/nutrient-sufficient (20% O2, 10% FBS, and 4.5 g/L glucose) culture conditions. The cell precipitates were collected and subjected to analysis using the Human Succinyl Coenzyme A ELISA Assay Kit (cat. #ML716772-2, MLBio Biotechnology) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Establishment of NSCLC nude mouse model
BALB/c nude mice (male, aged 4 weeks, RRID: IMSR_APB:4790) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technologies. The animal study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Fourth Military Medical University. Mice were housed in a standard animal laboratory under constant environmental conditions, including a 12-hour light and dark cycle at 22°C to 25°C with free access to water and food. Nude mice were subjected to subcutaneous injections of GAPDH WT and GAPDH-K251R/K251E–overexpressing cells (A549 or NCI-H2170), with a dose of 5 × 106 cells per mouse for A549-derived cells and 2 × 106 cells per mouse for NCI-H2170–derived cells. One week after injection, the tumor was well established, and its volume was measured every 3 days. The longest longitudinal diameter (L) and the longest transverse diameter (W) of the tumor were recorded, and the formula for calculating the tumor volume was V = 0.52 × L × W2. When the diameter of the tumor exceeded 15 mm, the tumor was removed from the mouse. The tumor volume and weight were measured and statistically analyzed. The tumor tissue was fixed and embedded in paraffin for pathologic sectioning and H&E staining analysis.

## Cell proliferation and colony formation assay
Cell numbers were measured spectrophotometrically using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (cat. #E1CK-00208, EnoGene) assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After the cells were inoculated on a 96-well culture plate and allowed to adhere for 6 hours, CCK-8 solution was added, and the absorbance at 450 nm was determined as the basic value of cell growth (day 0). After that, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured after adding CCK-8 solution every 24 hours for 4 days (days 1, 2, 3, and 4). The relative growth rate each day was calculated as follows: OD450 absorbance (days 1, 2, 3, and 4 – blank) – (day 0 – blank)/OD450 absorbance day 0 – blank. For the colony formation assay, 1,000 cells were seeded in six-well plates and cultured for 14 days, and the culture medium was changed every 3 days. The colonies were fixed with methanol and then stained with 0.2% crystal violet. Dot-shaped clone balls were counted after taking photos.

## Cell migration assay
Cells were seeded into six-well plates to achieve 80% to 90% confluence. The cell monolayer was wounded with a sterilized pipette tip and washed twice with a serum-free medium. The cell culture continued in a serum-free medium for 12, 24, or 36 hours. The gap lengths were measured using photomicrographs.

## Absolute quantitative targeted metabolomics analysis
Lung tissues were extracted from KrasG12D GAPDH WT and KrasG12DGAPDHK251R lung cancer model mice and then snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. Before processing the sample, the sample was thawed in an ice bath to reduce sample degradation. Then 10 mg of tissue sample was weighed, 20 μL of deionized water and 10 grinding beads were added, the sample was homogenized for 3 minutes, 120 μL of precooled methanol solution (including internal standard) was added, and the solution was homogenized again for 3 minutes. Centrifugation was done at a speed of 18,000 g at 4°C for 15 minutes. Into a 96-well plate, 20 μL of the supernatant was taken. Then 20 μL 200 mmol/L 3-NPH and 20 μL 120 mmol/L EDC were added into each hole in turn. Centrifugation was performed at 30°C at 1,450 rpm for 60 minutes. For dilution, 350 μL glacial methanol solution was added. Centrifugation was performed at 4,000 g at 4°C for 20 minutes. To a new 96-well plate, 150 μL the supernatant was transferred, waiting for sample injection analysis. In this experiment, we used an ultra-high pressure liquid chromatography-triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (ACQUITY UPLC/Xevo TQ-XS, RRID: SCR_025677/RRID:SCR_020890) from Waters Corporation to detect energy metabolism–related metabolites. The parameters and methods of the instrument were as follows. For high-performance liquid chromatography, column used was BEH C18 1.7 μmol/L analytic column (2.1 × 100 mm), column temperature was 40°C, sample manager temperature was 10°C, mobile phases were A = 5 mmol/L DIPEA solution and B = acetonitrile (ACN):isopropanol (IPA) = 7:3, gradient conditions were 0 to 1 minute (1% B), 1 to 9.5 minutes (1–15% B), 9.5 to 13 minutes (15–62% B), 13 to 14 minutes (62–100% B), 14 to 16 minutes (100% B), 16 to 16.2 minutes (100–1%B), and 16.2 to 18 minutes (1% B), flow rate was 0.3 mL/minute, and injection volume 5.0 μL. For MS, capillary 3.0 (ESI-) Kv, source temperature was 150°C, desolvation temperature was 500°C, and desolvation gas flow was 1,000 L/hour. The raw data files generated by UPLC/MS-MS were processed using the MassLynx software (Waters Corporation, v4.1, RRID: SCR_014271) to perform peak integration, calibration, and quantitation for each metabolite.

## Micro-CT imaging and processing
CT imaging was performed using a micro-CT machine (PerkinElmer Quantum GX2, RRID: SCR_025839) with six mice per group. Briefly, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital and placed on a micro-CT carrier stage for image acquisition. The settings for micro-CT image acquisition were as follows: voltage 90 kV, current 80 μA, resolution 72 μm, filter Cu0.06 + Al0.5, shot duration 4 minutes, field of view 36 mm, imaging mode high resolution, and radiation dose 842 mGy. The images were processed for lung reconstruction using SimpleViewer, an image analysis tool accompanying the instrument, and the data were analyzed using 3D Slicer (open source, RRID: SCR_005619) to display the lung tissue in three dimensions (3D). Rendering and quantification were performed using rendering tools and measurement tools. The long (L) and transverse (W) diameters of the lung tumor were measured, and the tumor volume was calculated as V = 0.52× L × W2.

## PET/CT scanning and analysis
PET/CT imaging analysis was performed in mice after fasting and water restriction for 8 hours. The mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and 200 to 255 μCi (0.2 mL) 3′-deoxy-3′-([18F]Fluoro)-fluorothymidine (18F-FLT) was injected into the tail vein. After injection, the anesthetized mice were placed in the PET animal cabin, the head was placed in the oxygen mask, and the limbs were fixed with medical tape. After 40 ± 5 minutes of resting metabolism, a PET/CT instrument (MadicLab PSA094, Shandong Madic Technology, RRID: SCR_025838) was used to collect images with the following parameters: PET scan collection 15 minutes, PET spatial resolution 0.5 mm, CT spatial resolution 50 μm, CT tube voltage 80 kV, and CT tube current 60 mA. After data collection, PET data reconstruction parameters were set as follows: accuracy of 0.943, iteration time of 25, and CT data reconstruction accuracy of 200 μm. PMOD 4.3 software (PMOD Technologies, RRID: SCR_016547) was used for PET/CT fusion image processing, delineating areas of interest, and measuring standard uptake value.

## Establishment of KrasG12D-driven NSCLC GAPDH WT/K251R mouse model
Intranasal injection was carried out according to a previously established protocol ( ). ## Seahorse XF cellular mitochondrial stress test, glutamine oxidative stress test, and ATP production rate test
Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were measured using a Seahorse XFe96 analyzer (Agilent Technologies, RRID: SCR_ 019545). Briefly, A549 GAPDH WT and A549 GAPDHK251R cells were inoculated (10,000 cells/well) in prehydrated 96-well plates. The cells were then incubated in RPMI-1640 medium with 0.5 g/L glucose and 1% FBS and 4.5 g/L glucose and 10% FBS, respectively, for 24 hours in an incubator at 37°C, 5% CO2, and 2% O2. In addition, the cells were treated with 6 mmol/L glutamine supplementation in culture. On the second day, the medium was replaced with a pre-warmed Seahorse XF assay medium. To simulate Nu-Def and Nu-Suff environments, we used medium with 0.5 and 4.5 g/L glucose in RPMI-1640 basal medium with 6 mmol/L glutamine and 1 mmol/L sodium pyruvate at pH 7.4. Cell culture microtiter wells were incubated at 37°C without CO2 for 1 hour after the assay medium was changed. In the mitochondrial stress test, 1 μmol/L oligomycin, 0.5 μmol/L carbonyl cyanide 4-(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone (FCCP), and 0.5 μmol/L rotenone/antimycin were sequentially injected, and the OCR of the cells was measured. In the glutamine oxidative stress test, 3 μmol/L BPTES (or an equal volume of basal medium), 1 μmol/L oligomycin, 0.5 μmol/L FCCP, and 0.5 μmol/L rotenone/antimycin were injected sequentially, and the OCR of the cells was measured. In the ATP production rate test, before starting the XF assay, the assay medium was discarded and freshly pre-warmed assay medium was added to each well. Oligomycin (1.5 μmol/L) and 0.5 μmol/L rotenone/antimycin were injected sequentially, and cell-based OCR and ECAR were measured. Addition of oligomycin resulted in the inhibition of mitochondrial ATP synthesis, thereby causing a decrease in OCR, which allowed quantification of the rate of mitoATP production. ECAR data, combined with the buffer coefficient of the assay medium, enabled the calculation of the total proton efflux rate. Complete inhibition of mitochondrial respiration with rotenone and antimycin A enabled the calculation of acidification associated with mitochondria, which, in combination with proton efflux rate data, enabled the calculation of glycoATP production rates. Experiments included at least three biological replicates. The data were normalized to the relative number of cells per well (the number of cells in an area of view photographed by the microscope) and analyzed in WAVE (Agilent Technologies, RRID: SCR_014526). Detailed steps for the above experiments can be found in the Agilent Seahorse XF Cellular Mitochondrial Stress Test (cat. #103015-100), Glutamine Oxidative Stress Test (cat. #103674-100), and Real-time ATP Rate Assay (cat. #103592-100) kit instructions.

## Seahorse XF cellular glycolysis stress test
ECAR was measured using a Seahorse XFe96 analyzer (Agilent Technologies, RRID: SCR_019545). The glycolysis stress test uses the same amount of cells as the mitochondrial stress test (10,000 cells/well). In the experiment of measuring the effects of different concentrations of glutamine (0.5 and 6 mmol/L) on the glycolysis ability of A549 and NCI-H2170 cells, the same Nu-Def and Nu-Suff culture conditions as in the mitochondrial stress test were used. The steps were similar to the OCR analysis except for the different treatments. In the experiment measuring the difference in glycolysis ability between GAPDH WT and GAPDHK251 mutant of A549 and NCI-H2170 cells, the cells were incubated at 37°C, 5% CO2, and 20% O2 in RPMI-1640 complete medium (2 g/L glucose, 2 mmol/L glutamine, and 10% FBS) for 24 hours. ECAR was measured before (basal level) and after sequential injections of 10 mmol/L glucose, 1 μmol/L oligomycin, and 50 mmol/L 2-DG. The detailed steps were carried out according to the instructions of the Agilent Seahorse XF glycolysis stress test kit (cat. #103020-100). Experiments included at least three biological replicates. The data were normalized to the relative number of cells per well (the number of cells in an area of view photographed by the microscope) and analyzed in WAVE (Agilent Technologies, RRID: SCR_014526).

## Tracing of metabolic fate of 13C-labeled glutamine
A549 GAPDH WT and A549 GAPDHK251R cells (1 × 107/sample) were adhered for 6 hours at 37°C, 20% O2, and 5% CO2 in RPMI-1640 complete medium. The medium was replaced with glutamine-free and glucose-free RPMI-1640 supplemented with 6 mmol/L (U-13C5) glutamine, 0.5 g/L glucose, and 1% FBS for 24 hours. Cell pellets were collected from culture dishes, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and placed at −80°C for downstream metabolite assay analysis. Metabolites were quantitated by Metabo-Profile Biotechnology. Samples were thawed in an ice bath to reduce sample degradation before processing. To each tube, 400 μL of 80% methanol solution was added and the cells were sonicated and then centrifuged at 18,000 g for 15 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was concentrated by centrifugation and then 100 μL of 80% methanol was added. For LC/MS analysis, samples were analyzed by UPLC coupled to an MS/MS (UPLC/MS-MS) system (ACQUITY UPLC/Xevo TQ-XS, Waters, RRID: SCR_025677/RRID: SCR_020890). The optimized instrument settings were as follows. For high-performance liquid chromatography, column used was UPLC HILIC (4.6-mm column, Amide XBridge, Waters), column temperature was 40°C, sample manager temperature was 10°C, mobile phases were A = 5% acetonitrile in water (with 20 mmol/L ammonium hydroxide and ammonium acetate) and B = acetonitrile, gradient conditions were 0 to 3.5 minutes (85–32% B), 3.5 to 12 minutes (32–2% B), 12 to 16.5 minutes (2% B), 16.5 to 17 minutes (2–85% B), and 17 to 25.5 minutes (85% B), flow rate was 0.40 mL/minute, and injection volume was 5.0 μL. The raw data files generated by UPLC/MS-MS were processed using MassLynx software (Waters Corporation, v4.1, RRID: SCR_014271) to perform peak integration, calibration, and quantitation for each metabolite.

## Statistical analyses
All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, RRID: SCR_002865) and GraphPad 8.3 (GraphPad, RRID: SCR_002798). The Mann–Whitney test was used for analyzing the difference between two independent samples, and the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for analyzing the difference among multiple independent samples. Significant differences were analyzed by an unpaired t test or log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. Data are displayed as dot plots of at least three independent experiments. Plots include each data point, mean, and SD. All P values are reported in the figure legends. P < 0.05 is considered statistically significant. The mice were assigned to each group randomly by body weight. Biological samples, IHC analysis of human NSCLC tissues, and tumorigenicity experiments were conducted in a blinded fashion. Sample sizes were predetermined by power analysis.

## Results

## Identification of elevated GAPDH K251-Su in the tumor tissues of smoker patients with NSCLC
Tumor and adjacent normal tissues from patients with NSCLC (n = 14) were collected, and MS was used to generate proteomic data and evaluate differential protein expression ( ). Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and IPA pathway enrichment analyses showed that differentially expressed proteins were significantly enriched in glucose metabolism as well as the glycolysis pathway (Supplementary Fig. S1A and S1B). String protein interaction analysis by Cytoscape v3.6.1 software revealed that GAPDH, a key protein in glycolysis, was in the core position of the interaction network, with the highest interaction frequency and the closest contact with other differential proteins (Supplementary Fig. S1C). Our MS data demonstrated that the expression level of GAPDH in lung cancer tissues was significantly higher than that in adjacent tissues, and GAPDH protein levels were significantly higher in NSCLC tumors cells compared with IMR-90 (normal embryonic lung fibroblast) and BEAs-2B (normal lung epithelial) cells (Supplementary Fig. S1D and S1G). Furthermore, proteomics data of patients with NSCLC from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) database further supported this conclusion (Supplementary Fig. S1E and S1F). It is noteworthy that based on the analysis results from the GEPIA, LOGpc, and OSppc databases, the level of GAPDH mRNA showed no significant correlation with the prognosis of patients with lung cancer (Supplementary Fig. S1H). However, elevated GAPDH protein levels were significantly associated with poor overall survival in patients with NSCLC (Supplementary Fig. S1I).
 Our MS analysis on tumor and adjacent normal tissues from patients with NSCLC revealed a high abundance of GAPDH lysine succinylation at multiple potential sites in NSCLC tumor tissues and cell lines ( ; Supplementary Fig. S2A–S2M). GAPDH K251-Su occurred significantly more frequently in tumor tissues than in normal tissues ( ; Supplementary Fig. S3). We purified GAPDH protein from the A549 lung adenocarcinoma cell line and the NCI-H2170 lung squamous cell carcinoma cell line, and succinylation of GAPDH was verified using a pan-succinylation antibody ( ). We performed lysine (K) to arginine (R) mutations at seven potential lysine succinylation sites and found that mutation of K251 reduced the succinylation level of GAPDH more significantly compared with other lysine sites ( ). This indicates that K251 constitutes the predominant succinylation modification site of GAPDH.
A site-specific antibody for GAPDH K251-Su was prepared by PTM BioLab, Inc. Dot blot assays showed that the GAPDH K251-Su antibody specifically bound to modified peptide while hardly recognizing the nonmodified peptide ( ). The results of Western blotting indicated that the GAPDH K251-Su antibody specifically recognized a band slightly larger than 35 KDa in Hela cells ( ), which corresponds to the molecular weight of the GAPDH protein (37 KDa). The results of the immunoblotting showed that the GAPDH K251-Su antibody premixed with a modified peptide (Peptide-Su-2) failed to bind GAPDH protein, whereas the GAPDH K251-Su antibody premixed with a nonmodified peptide (Peptide-NC) could detect a visible band of GAPDH ( ). Furthermore, we expressed the WT and K251R-mutant GAPDH in A549 and NCI-H2170 cell lines, and the results showed that the antibody recognition efficiency was significantly reduced in the K251R-mutant compared with the WT GAPDH ( ). IHC staining was performed on tumor and normal tissues from 90 cases of lung squamous cell carcinoma and 92 cases of patients with lung adenocarcinoma. In lung squamous cell carcinoma, the positive rate of GAPDH K251-Su was 91.11% in tumor tissues and 22.22% in adjacent normal tissues (Supplementary Table S1). In lung adenocarcinoma, the positive rate of GAPDH K251-Su was 92.39% in tumor tissues and 57.95% in adjacent normal tissues (Supplementary Table S1). Representative IHC staining images of tumor and normal tissues are shown in . The results of the statistical analysis indicated that the level of GAPDH K251-Su was significantly higher in tumor tissues than in normal tissues, and the expression level showed a significant elevation in the early stages of NSCLC ( ). The survival analysis indicated that a high level of GAPDH K251-Su significantly correlated with poor prognosis in patients with NSCLC ( ). We then analyzed the correlation between the level of GAPDH K251-Su and the clinical characteristics of patients with NSCLC. In lung squamous cell carcinoma, high GAPDH K251-Su significantly correlated with old age (P = 0.000484), large tumor size (P = 0.025), advanced clinical T stage (P = 0.027), and advanced American Joint Committee on Cancer stage (P = 0.028). In patients with lung adenocarcinoma, high GAPDH K251-Su significantly correlated with old age (P = 0.035), large tumor size (P = 0.048), and advanced clinical T stage (P = 0.049; ). Moreover, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, along with a nomogram prediction model, showed that GAPDH K251-Su was an independent unfavorable prognostic biomarker in patients with NSCLC (P < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. S4A and S4B). It is particularly noteworthy that the GAPDH K251-Su levels were significantly higher in smokers than in nonsmokers in both patients with lung adenocarcinoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma ( ). Pathologically, long-term smoking leads to anthracosis in lung tissue. Interestingly, we found that the level of GAPDH K251-Su was significantly positively correlated with the degree of anthracosis in the tumor tissue of NSCLC ( ). Moreover, moderate and severe anthracosis was significantly correlated with poor prognosis in patients with NSCLC (Supplementary Fig. S1J).
 

## Smoke induced increased cell uptake of glutamine and enhanced GAPDH K251-Su level
We dissolved different doses of CSE in cell culture medium to treat A549 cells and found elevated levels of GAPDH K251-Su in response to increased doses of CSE under hypoxic and Nu-Def conditions, whereas this effect was not observed under normoxic or Nu-Suff conditions ( ; Supplementary Fig. S5A). The effect of CSE on the GAPDH protein level showed a similar upward trend to that of GAPDH K251-Su (Supplementary Fig. S5B). Several studies have demonstrated that cigarette smoke influences cell glutamine metabolism ( – ). Then we compared the gene and protein expression levels of glutamine transporters (SLC1A5, SLC7A5, and SLC38A1) between smoker and nonsmoker patients with NSCLC using data from TCGA and cBioPortal databases and found that the both gene and protein expression levels of SLC1A5, SLC7A5, and SLC38A1 were significantly higher in the tumor tissue of smoker patients than that in nonsmoker patients ( ). We compared the differential metabolites from lung tissues of mice exposed to cigarette smoke and filtered air by bioinformatics analysis from the Metabolights database and found that the concentration of glutamine in the lung tissue was significantly elevated after 3 and 6 months of continuous cigarette smoke inhalation compared with the filtered air group ( ). Furthermore, we found elevated levels of intracellular glutamine in response to treatment with CSE in A549 cells under hypoxic and Nu-Def conditions, and this effect was not observed under normoxic and Nu-Suff conditions ( ); this effect was markedly attenuated after treatment with the glutamine transporter SLC1A5 inhibitor GPNA (Supplementary Fig. S5C). The donor of succinylation, succinyl-CoA, is a metabolite mainly from the TCA cycle of amino acid metabolism. So, we detected the effects of common amino acids, including glutamine, glutamate (a catabolite of glutamine), methionine, proline, valine, leucine, and isoleucine, and found that the removal of glutamine and glutamate reduced the level of GAPDH-K251 succinylation compared with other amino acids under hypoxic and Nu-Def conditions ( ). Then we added different doses of glutamine and glutamate to A549 and NCI-H2170 cells and found elevated levels of GAPDH K251-Su in response to increased doses of glutamine under hypoxic and Nu-Def conditions, and this effect was not observed under normoxic and Nu-Suff conditions ( ). Similar effect was also found for adding increased doses of glutamate, a product of glutamine catabolism ( ). Adding methionine, proline, valine, leucine, and isoleucine did not affect the levels of GAPDH K251-Su and GAPDH under either hypoxic and Nu-Def or normoxic and Nu-Suff conditions (Supplementary Fig. S5D and S5E). Then, we measured the concentration of succinyl-CoA after adding different doses of glutamine and glutamate. The results showed that adding increased doses of glutamine or glutamate significantly enhanced the production of succinyl-CoA in A549 and NCI-H2170 cells under hypoxic and Nu-Def conditions, whereas no effect was observed under normoxic and Nu-Suff conditions ( and ). In summary, smoke induces increased cell uptake of glutamine and enhances GAPDH K251-Su levels through the glutamine TCA cycle metabolism pathway to provide donor succinyl-CoA for GAPDH succinylation under hypoxic and Nu-Def conditions ( ).
 

## GAPDH K251-Su increases the stable expression of GAPDH by suppressing TRIM4-mediated K254 ubiquitination
We constructed WT GAPDH (WT GAPDH), mutant K251R GAPDH (unable to undergo succinylation modification), and K251E GAPDH (it mimics the charge alteration induced by the succinyl group but differs in both chemical structure and steric hindrance effects from succinylated lysine) with flag tags in NSCLC cells (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Immunoblotting results demonstrated that the expression of GAPDH decreased significantly in K251R/E mutants compared with WT ( ), and endogenous mutation by CRISPR/Cas-mediated genome engineering introducing the K251R of GAPDH also resulted in a decrease in GAPDH protein expression (Supplementary Fig. S6B). We examined the GAPDH mRNA levels by qPCR to investigate whether the decrease in protein expression was caused by decreased gene expression, and the results showed that the level of GAPDH mRNA in K251R/E was not lower than that in WT ( ). To verify whether GAPDH protein stability was affected by K251 succinylation, we purified expressed GAPDH WT and mutant proteins from A549 and NCI-H2170 cells and measured the ubiquitination level using a pan-ubiquitin antibody. The level of GAPDH ubiquitination was significantly increased in both K251R and K251E mutants ( ). To investigate whether ubiquitinated GAPDH enters the proteasomal degradation pathway, the MG132 proteasome inhibitor was used to treat GAPDH WT cells and K251-mutant cells. The protein levels of K251R and K251E GAPDH were gradually increased with MG132 treatment time (0, 6, and 12 hours) in A549 and NCI-H2170 cells, whereas MG132 treatment did not affect GAPDH levels in the WT group ( and ). In addition, the protein levels of K251R and K251E GAPDH gradually decreased with a prolonged cell treatment of cycloheximide, a protein synthesis inhibitor, but this effect was not found in the WT group ( and ). These results suggest that GAPDH K251-Su may enhance GAPDH protein stability by inhibiting the ubiquitination of GAPDH.
 Next, we identified the ubiquitination modification site K254 of GAPDH by MS analysis, and the mass spectrogram of K254 ubiquitination is shown in . In addition, using the protein structure analysis software Discovery Studio to simulate the tetramers of GAPDH, we found that both K254 and K251 were on the outer surface of the GAPDH spatial structure, and they were adjacent to each other ( ). We purified expressed WT and K254R-mutant GAPDH from A549 and NCI-H2170 cells and measured the ubiquitination level using a pan-ubiquitin antibody. The result showed that the level of GAPDH ubiquitination was significantly decreased in K254R mutants ( ). Affinity purification coupled with quantitative MS was used to analyze proteins that interacted with GAPDH in GAPDH WT, GAPDHK251R-mutant, and GAPDHK251E-mutant cells. The results demonstrated higher MS abundance ratios of E3 ubiquitin ligases TRIM4, TRIM21, and TRIM23 in GAPDHK251R/E cells compared with GAPDH WT cells (Supplementary Fig. S7A and S7B), suggesting that TRIM4, TRIM21, and TRIM23 are potential E3 ubiquitin ligases for GAPDH. To further clarify the E3 ubiquitin ligases of GAPDH, we measured the interaction between GAPDH and TRIM4, TRIM21, and TRIM23 by co-IP and immunoblotting and found that GAPDH exclusively interacted with TRIM4 in NSCLC cells ( ). In addition, the binding ability between GAPDHK251R/E and TRIM4 was much stronger than GAPDH WT ( ). We next constructed a GAPDHK251R/K254R double-mutant A549 cell and found that K251R/K254R double mutation impaired the increased ubiquitination caused by the K251R mutation and double mutation significantly reduced the interaction between TRIM4 and GAPDH compared with the K251R or K251E single mutations ( ). The GAPDH enzyme activity assay revealed that the catalytic activity of GAPDH was significantly higher in the GAPDH WT group than that in GAPDHK251R/E NSCLC cells, which may be attributed to increased protein stability of GAPDH ( and ). The assumption that GAPDH K251-Su increases the stable expression of GAPDH by suppressing TRIM4-mediated K254 ubiquitination is shown in .

## Acyltransferase p300 catalyzes GAPDH K251-Su
We performed siRNA on the previously reported succinyltransferases GCN5, CPT1A, DLST, and p300/CBP in A549 cells and found that knockdown of p300 significantly decreased the level of GAPDH K251-Su, whereas knockdown other succinyltransferases had no effect on GAPDH K251-Su ( ). Moreover, remarkable colocalization of p300 and GAPDH was detected in A549 and NCI-H2170 cells by immunofluorescence colocalization assay ( ). In addition, the p300 interaction with GAPDH in A549 and NCI-H2170 cells was confirmed by co-IP assay ( ). We coexpressed His-p300 and Flag-GAPDH in A549 and NCI-H2170 cells, and the interaction between His-p300 and Flag-GAPDH was verified by co-IP assays ( ). We overexpressed His-p300 in WT, K251R-, and K251E-mutant GAPDH A549 cells, respectively, and found that the level of GAPDH K251-Su was significantly elevated caused by overexpression of p300 in WT cells other than in mutant cells ( ). Furthermore, GAPDH enzyme activity analysis demonstrated that overexpression of p300 caused increased enzyme activity exclusively in WT cells ( ). Then we treated NSCLC cells with A485, an acyltransferase inhibitor of p300, and found that A485 suppressed the ascending tendency of GAPDH K251-Su induced by glutamine ( ). Notably, when we compared the gene and protein expression of p300 between smoker and nonsmoker patients with NSCLC based on TCGA and cBioPortal databases, we found that both gene and protein expression levels of p300 were significantly higher in the tumor tissues of smoker patients than in nonsmoker patients ( ). In summary, we conclude that acyltransferase p300 catalyzes GAPDH K251-Su in NSCLC cells and suggests that this catalytic role may be more pronounced in the tumor tissue of smoker patients.
 

## GAPDH K251-Su promotes cell glycolysis and enhances glutamine reductive carboxylation
Previous studies revealed that the KrasG12D mutation is a common mutation that is related to the smoking habit of patients with NSCLC ( ). So, we established the B6-Loxp-Stop-Loxp KrasG12D (B6-KrasLSL-G12D) mouse model to investigate the role of GAPDH K251-Su in metabolic rewiring. Intranasal inhalation of the AAV6-Cre and AAV6-Gapdh WT/K251R adeno-associated viruses sequentially at 7-day intervals was conducted on 40-day-old B6-KrasLSL-G12D mice to induce endogenous on-expression of KrasG12D and to induce the expression of WT and K251R Gapdh in the tumor tissue of NSCLC. Then in vivo absolute quantitative targeted metabolomics assay of tumor tissue was performed ( ). The enrichment of differential metabolites in GAPDH WT versus K251R-mutant groups demonstrated that the top differential metabolic pathways were the citrate cycle, amino acids (includes glutamine, glutamate, alanine, aspartate, arginine, and proline) metabolism, glycolysis or gluconeogenesis, pyruvate metabolism, nitrogen metabolism, and butanoate metabolism ( ). Further quantitative analysis demonstrated that in glycolysis, glutamine and citrate cycle metabolism, pentose phosphate pathway, and carbon and nitrogen anabolism pathway, the concentration of metabolites was significantly higher in WT than that in mutant groups ( ). It is worth noting that glutamine ranks with the highest concentration in absolute quantitative analysis, and in the comparison of metabolites between WT and mutant groups, glutamine showed the most significant difference among all differential metabolites ( ). The Seahorse glycolysis stress test showed that GAPDHK251R and GAPDHK251E mutations significantly suppressed the glycolysis capability in NSCLC cells ( ). The glycolysis stress test also demonstrated that adding increased doses of glutamine significantly enhanced the glycolytic capacity in NSCLC cells under hypoxic and Nu-Def conditions, whereas this effect becomes less pronounced under Nu-Suff conditions ( ). Seahorse real-time ATP rate assay showed that GAPDHK251R mutation significantly increased the rate and percentage of mitochondrial ATP production and decreased the percentage of glycolytic ATP production upon adding glutamine under Nu-Def conditions ( ). The Seahorse mitochondria stress test showed that GAPDHK251R mutation significantly enhanced the oxidative phosphorylation ability compared with WT cells upon adding glutamine under Nu-Def conditions ( ). The above effects were not significant under Nu-Suff conditions ( and ). Moreover, the Seahorse glutamine substrate oxidation stress test showed that treatment with the glutaminase 1 inhibitor BPTES under Nu-Def conditions significantly suppressed glutamine oxidative phosphorylation in GAPDHK251R-mutant cells, whereas no significance was found in GAPDH WT cells ( ). We conducted metabolic flux analysis to trace NSCLC cell uptake of [U13-C5]-labeled glutamine under hypoxic and Nu-Def conditions. The results showed that the levels of glutamine oxidative phosphorylation pathway intermediates, including glutamate (M5), succinate (M4), malate (M4), and aspartate (M4), were higher in GAPDHK251R cells than in GAPDH WT cells. However, the levels of glutamine reductive carboxylation pathway intermediates, such as citrate (M5), malate (M3), pyruvate (M3), and lactate (M2), were lower in GAPDHK251R cells than in GAPDH WT cells ( ). In summary, the above results suggest that GAPDH K251-Su promotes cell glycolysis and glutamine reductive carboxylation and suppresses the glutamine oxidative phosphorylation ( ).
 

## GAPDH K251-Su promotes tumor growth and metastasis in NSCLC
We investigated the effect of GAPDH K251-Su on the malignant progression of NSCLC in KrasG12D-driven mice using micro-CT scanning and 3D dynamic reconstruction in vivo 130 days after virus infection. Representative pictures are shown in . Further analysis demonstrated a significantly larger tumor size and more tumor lesions in the GAPDH WT mouse compared with the GAPDHK251R mouse ( ; Supplementary Videos S1 and S2). Indeed, we have found lung nodules in the early stage of virus infection (63 days after infection) in GAPDH WT mice but not in GAPDHK251R mice by micro-CT assay (Supplementary Fig. S8A). PET-CT scanning of lung tissues 175 days after virus infection showed that the GAPDH WT mice had a stronger 18F-FLT uptake signal than the GAPDHK251R mice ( ; Supplementary Videos S3 and S4). Mice were dissected and we found more and larger tumor lesions in the lung tissue of GAPDH WT mice than in GAPDHK251R mice ( ). H&E assays showed that GAPDH WT mice had obvious more metastasis lesions in lung and distal liver tissues ( ). IHC staining demonstrated that GAPDH WT mice had a higher level of GAPDH K251-Su, Ki-67 (a biomarker of cell proliferation), and CD31 (a biomarker of angiogenesis) in lung tissues than in the GAPDHK251R mice, whereas the level of the eGFP-tagged protein, which represents the efficiency of virus infection and expression, did not exhibit significant differences ( ). In addition, the subcutaneous tumor-bearing assay demonstrated a stronger ability of tumor situ growth and metastasis in GAPDH WT mice than that in GAPDHK251R/E-mutant groups (Supplementary Fig. S8B–S8E). In vitro analysis confirmed stronger proliferation and migration capacity of GAPDH WT cells than GAPDHK251R/E-mutant cells (Supplementary Fig. S8F–S8H). In summary, GAPDH K251-Su induced by tobacco smoking coordinates glycolysis and glutamine metabolism to promote NSCLC progression ( ).
 

## Discussion
Tobacco smoke strongly increases the risk of lung cancer ( ). Evidence has shown the benefits of smoking cessation on prolonged survival after cancer diagnosis across cancer types ( ). The major feature of cancer metabolism is that patient physiology, including age, diet, exercise, and gut microbiota, has a clear impact on cell metabolism ( ). However, the effect of smoking habit on the metabolic reprogramming of NSCLC is largely unknown. Previous studies reported the impact of cigarette smoke on the metabolism of lung epithelial cells and bronchial epithelial cells to drive the progression of inflammatory lung diseases ( ). In this study, bioinformatics analysis showed that the expression level of glutamine transporter in the tumor tissue of NSCLC was significantly higher in smokers than in nonsmokers, and we further confirmed that increased glutamine uptake by lung cancer cells caused by smoke. In fact, several studies have demonstrated that cigarette smoke influences cell glutamine metabolism. Metabolomic analysis showed that exposure to cigarette smoke condensates significantly increases glutamine concentrations in various cells from lung tissue and oral tissue ( – ). Furthermore, results from glutamine metabolic flux analysis show that the cell TCA cycle metabolism shifts from oxidative to reductive pathway ( ). Our findings are consistent with those of previous studies. More importantly, previous studies on the impact of smoking focused on inducing genetic mutation, transcriptional regulation, epigenetic remodeling, or from the perspective of toxicology ( , – ), which drives the development of lung cancer. Our study revealed that an increase in cell glutamine uptake caused by smoking alters the level of protein succinylation in lung cancer by providing succinyl-CoA donors. Altered protein post-translational modification is a novel mechanism through which tobacco smoke reprograms cell metabolism and a novel perspective to understand the impact of the smoking habit in promoting the progression of lung cancer.
Protein post-translational modifications respond quickly to altered metabolites in the tumor microenvironment, readjusting cell metabolism to support cell growth ( , , ). Cell metabolites provide chemical groups for protein post-translational modifications such as acetyl-CoA, lactic acid, and succinyl-CoA ( , ). Glutamine is a crucial metabolite in supporting cell growth and maintaining redox homeostasis in NSCLC ( ). In this study, we think that smoking habit induced a hypoxia microenvironment and increased uptake of glutamine by cancer cells. The glutamine–glutamate–α-ketoglutaric acid–succinyl-CoA catabolism pathway provides succinyl-CoA as a donor for lysine succinylation. Evidence has shown that the concentration of glutamine in lung cancer tissues is especially high compared with other cancer types, such as colon or stomach cancer ( – ). However, whether glutamine accumulation is associated with a smoking habit remains unknown. We assume that smoking causes changes in the composition and concentration of metabolites in the tumor microenvironment. Further systematic metabolomic analyses are still needed to clarify smoking-induced metabolic reprogramming in lung cancer.
Kiran Kurmi and colleagues ( ) found that GAPDH succinylation occurred at multiple lysine sites in 293T cells. However, the significance of GAPDH succinylation in tumor progression and the key functional succinylated sites remain unclear. In this study, by comparing the differential acylation modifications between tumor tissues and paracancerous tissues using MS, we found a high abundance of GAPDH K251 succinylation in tumor tissues. It is worth noting that as a stably expressed housekeeping gene, protein degradation pathways of GAPDH and the mechanism by which GAPDH is persistently and highly expressed in tumor cells remain unclear. We found that GAPDH K251-Su increases GAPDH protein stability by suppressing the proteasome degradation pathway. Furthermore, we verified GAPDH-K254 ubiquitination and identified the ubiquitin ligase TRIM4 that mediates ubiquitination at K254. In 3D structural reconstruction analysis, K251 and K254 are spatially close to each other and both sites are near the outer surface of the GAPDH tetramer. This spatial distribution suggests possible cross-talk between K251 and K254 modifications. We speculate that the succinylation of K251 hinders the interaction between ubiquitin ligase TRIM4 and GAPDH K254 through a steric hindrance effect. TRIM4 belongs to the tripartite motif (TRIM) protein family ( ). Contrasting with other proteins in this family, the expression level of TRIM4 is lower than that in adjacent tissues in NSCLC ( ), breast cancer ( ), and liver cancer ( ). Our study provides clues for further research on the mechanism by which TRIM4 regulates cancer progression.
p300 is an acyltransferase that catalyzes acetylation, propionylation, crotonylation, and lactic acidification. Most studies report that the activity of p300 in the cell nucleus catalyzes the modification of nuclear proteins such as histones and transcription factors. Our study confirmed the activity of p300 in the cytoplasm to catalyze protein succinylation and that p300-catalyzed GAPDH succinylation plays a vital role in regulating cell metabolism. A previous study reported that p300 catalyzed GAPDH acetylation to regulate the nuclear translocation of GAPDH, suggesting an interaction between p300 and GAPDH ( ). Our study further demonstrates the catalytic activity of succinylation in this interaction. Several natural compound drugs and small-molecule inhibitors targeting p300 have been developed ( ). Notably, we found that the expression level of GAPDH K251 succinylation was decreased after cell treatment with the p300 acyltransferase activity inhibitor A485. This suggests the possible shared catalytic domains between the succinylation and acetylating catalytic activity of p300, which provide clues for the development of inhibitors targeting p300.
Metabolic plasticity is a main driver for tumor evasion ( , ). Glycolysis and glutamine metabolism are two important pathways in remolding tumor metabolism ( , ). However, few studies have reported promotion of tumor progression by glycolysis and glutamine metabolism. Our metabolomics results in the KrasG12D mutation mouse model showed that GAPDH K251-Su promotes glycolysis to produce energy for tumor cells. Meanwhile, reductive carboxylation of glutamine was enhanced. Metabolic flow analysis revealed that tumor cells uptake glutamine in hypoxic and Nu-Def microenvironments, and elevated GAPDH K251-Su inhibits the glutamine oxidative phosphorylation decarboxylation and promotes the glutamine reductive carboxylation pathway. Our results are in accordance with previous studies that rapidly proliferating tumor cells are more likely to produce energy via the glycolytic pathway, and glutamine is more inclined to enter the reductive carboxylation pathway than the TCA oxidation pathway to provide sufficient substrates for anabolism ( , , , ). One possible reason is that in a hypoxic and Nu-Def microenvironment, tumor cells are more inclined to choose a pathway with less reactive oxygen species production, whereas mitochondrial oxidative respiratory pathways increase ROS production, causing damage to tumor cells ( ). Our results suggest a role of GAPDH K251-Su in this synergistic effect. Several studies have shown that glycolysis promotes glutamine reductive carboxylation by providing a reduced form of NADH or NADPH ( ). Further study demonstrated that GAPDH and malate dehydrogenase 1 (MDH1) coordinate in a mitochondrial dysfunction cell model. On the one hand, GAPDH enhanced the production of a reduced form of NADH during the glycolysis pathway, which can be used by MDH1 to promote glutamine reductive carboxylation. On the other hand, the oxidative NAD+ obtained from MDH1 reductive catalytic reactions, in turn, promotes the activity of GAPDH in glycolysis ( ). Therefore, we speculate that GAPDH K251-Su may enhance the interaction between GAPDH and MDH1, which coordinates glycolysis and glutamine metabolism in cancer cells.
In summary, this study reveals that altered protein posttranslational modification is a novel mechanism of cigarette smoke-induced cell metabolism rewiring. GAPDH succinylation plays a vital role in maintaining its persistently high expression in tumor cells. Elevated GAPDH K251-Su induced by smoking habit coordinates glycolysis and glutamine metabolism and maintains the balance of energy, substance, and redox homeostasis for cancer cell survival in a stressful microenvironment. Interestingly, the results of our bioinformatics analysis indicated that both the expression level of GAPDH mRNA and GAPDH K251-Su was negatively correlated with the infiltration of CD8+ T cells in the tumor microenvironment, suggesting that GAPDH may be involved in the formation of the tumor immune-suppressive microenvironment. The mechanism remains to be further investigated. Considering the substantial changes of reshaping the tumor microenvironment and cancer metabolism induced by smoking habit, quitting smoking is an effective way to prolong the patient's survival period.
