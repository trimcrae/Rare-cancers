## Abstract

## Background
Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors have changed the therapeutic landscape for diabetic kidney disease (DKD) patients, but their underlying mechanisms are complicated and not fully understood. Mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum membranes (MAMs), the dynamic contact sites between mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), serve as intracellular platforms important for regulating cellular fate and function. This study explored the roles and mechanisms of SGLT2 inhibitors in regulating MAMs formation in diabetic podocytes.

## Methods
We assessed MAMs formation in podocytes from DKD patients’ renal biopsy samples and induced an increase in MAMs formation in cultured human podocytes by transfecting OMM-ER linker plasmid to investigate the effects of MAMs imbalance on podocyte injury. Empagliflozin-treated diabetic mice and podocyte-specific SGLT2 knockout diabetic mice (diabetic states were induced by streptozotocin and a high-fat diet), empagliflozin-treated podocytes, SGLT2-downregulated podocytes, and SGLT2-overexpressing podocytes were used to investigate the effects and mechanisms of SGLT2 inhibitors on MAMs formation in diabetic podocytes.

## Results
MAMs were increased in podocytes and were associated with renal dysfunction in DKD patients. Increased MAMs aggravated HG-induced podocyte injury. The expression of SGLT2 was increased in diabetic podocytes. In addition, empagliflozin-treatment and podocyte-specific SGLT2 knockout attenuated MAMs formation and podocyte injury in diabetic mice. Empagliflozin treatment and SGLT2 knockdown decreased podocyte MAMs formation by activating the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway, while SGLT2 overexpression had the opposite effect.

## Conclusions
Inhibition of SGLT2 attenuates MAMs imbalance in diabetic podocytes by activating the AMPK pathway. This study expands our knowledge of the roles of SGLT2 inhibitors in improving DKD podocyte injury and provides new insights into DKD treatment.

## Background
Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a serious complication that decreases the quality of life and survival of diabetic patients. It occurs in approximately 30-40% of patients with diabetes [ ]. DKD is a leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and is considered a public health problem worldwide [ ]. In recent decades, people with DKD had limited therapeutic options. Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors have changed the therapeutic landscape for these patients and recently become a first-line treatment of DKD [ ]. Several randomized controlled clinical trials such as EMPA-REG OUTCOME, CREDENCE, and DAPA-CKD, have shown that SGLT2 inhibitors significantly improve proteinuria and glomerular hyperfiltration and reduce the risk of renal endpoint events in chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients with or without diabetes [ – ]. SGLT2 inhibitors are a class of novel anti-diabetic drugs that lower blood glucose levels by inhibiting glucose and sodium reabsorption at proximal tubules [ ]. However, recent studies fonud that SGLT2 inhibitors were less effective at lowering blood glucose levels when the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was low [ ], but they still exhibited renal protective effects in diabetic patients with low eGFR [ , ]. Besides, another study reported that SGLT2 inhibitors showed similar benefits on renal end points in DKD patients regardless of baseline hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) level, including those with HbA1c < 7% at baseline [ ]. Therefore, it is now believed that the mechanisms underlying the renal benefits of SGLT2 inhibitors on DKD are far beyond lowering blood glucose levels [ ]. But the mechanisms of SGLT2 inhibitors still remain unclear and require further exploration.
Podocyte damage and loss disrupt the normal glomerular basement membrane structure, leading directly to proteinuria, which is the most common clinical sign of DKD [ ]. Because podocytes are terminally differentiated cells that are highly susceptible to damage and lack regeneration capacity [ ], there is an urgent need to explore effective therapeutic targets that protect podocytes in DKD. It is widely believed that SGLT2 is expressed primarily in renal tubules [ ]. Inhibiting SGLT2 increases the delivery of glucose and sodium to the macula densa, which activates tubuloglomerular feedback and promotes afferent arteriole constriction. This, in turn, reduces intraglomerular capillary hypertension and hyperfiltration, which can cause proteinuria [ ]. Interestingly, Cassis et al., Zhao et al., and Ge et al. reported that podocytes also expressed SGLT2, and SGLT2 inhibitors alleviated podocyte damage in proteinuric nondiabetic nephropathy, experimental Alport syndrome, and lupus nephritis [ – ]. In addition, Hudkins et al. reported that treating BTBR ob/ob mice with empagliflozin exhibited structural and functional benefits on podocytes [ ]. Our hypothesis is that the protective effect of SGLT2 inhibitors against proteinuria in DKD patients may be associated with their direct effects on podocytes. Further exploration of the relationship between SGLT2 and podocytes may provide new insights into the pathogenesis of DKD. However, additional evidence is needed to fully understand how SGLT2 inhibitors affect podocytes.
Mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum membranes (MAMs) are dynamic contact sites, with a distance of 10–30 nm, between the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondria. MAMs serve as platforms for the exchange of important metabolic substances and messengers between the two organelles and coordinate essential cellular processes such as calcium and lipid homeostasis, apoptosis, inflammation, ER stress, mitochondrial dynamics, and bioenergetics [ ]. The area and length of MAMs are important determinants of the efficiency of material exchange between the ER and mitochondria. MAMs are highly dynamic structures, whose area and length constantly fluctuate within a small range under normal physiological conditions. MAMs imbalance (a constant increase or decrease) significantly affect the efficiency of material exchange between two organelles, leading to cellular physiological dysfunction [ , ]. Because MAMs play an important role in regulating cellular fate and function, MAMs are now known as key intracellular hubs for the progression of several diseases, such as cancer, neurodegenerative diseases, and cardiovascular diseases [ ]. We and Wei et al. previously demonstrated that MAMs were increased in diabetic podocytes and contributed to podocyte injury [ , ]. Thus, MAMs dysfunction may be a core pathogenesis of podocyte damage in DKD. SGLT2 inhibitors have been previously reported to protect mitochondrial and ER functions [ , ]. Therefore, we wondered whether SGLT2 inhibitors could protect against podocyte damage by regulating MAMs formation. However, the effects of SGLT2 inhibitors on podocyte MAMs formation have not been clearly described.
AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a trimeric complex consisting of a catalytic subunit (the α-subunit, the main subunit with enzyme activity) and two regulatory subunits (the β- and γ-subunits). It is a highly conserved master regulator of metabolism that restores cellular energy balance during metabolic stress [ ]. Available data suggest that a reduction in AMPK activity in podocytes is pivotal for the progression of obesity-related kidney disease and DKD [ , ]. SGLT2 inhibitors can induce a cellular fasting-like state and enhance nutrient deprivation signaling such as AMPK activation [ ]. In addition, several studies have shown that AMPK is a key regulator of MAMs formation in cardiomyocytes and HeLa cells [ , ]. AMPK may play a critical role in regulating MAMs formation in diabetic podocytes, thereby contributing to the protective effect of SGLT2 inhibitors.
In this study, we explored the relationship between SGLT2 inhibitors and MAMs formation in diabetic podocytes. Our findings may provide new insights into the mechanisms of SGLT2 inhibitors in the treatment of DKD and other podocyte diseases.

## Methods

## Human renal biopsy samples
Renal biopsy samples from diabetic kidney disease (DKD) patients and normal kidney tissue (control group; adjacent healthy tissue from surgical resection of renal cell carcinoma samples without diabetes or other renal diseases) were obtained. DKD renal biopsy specimens were obtained from type 2 diabetes patients with a pathological diagnosis of DKD, accompanied by clinical syndrome characterized by persistent albuminuria (> 300 mg/24 h). All renal biopsy samples form patients diagnosed with DKD were classified in accordance with a new pathologic classification provided by the Renal Pathology Society. From mild to severe, DKD is divided into four hierarchical glomerular lesions as follows: Class I, glomerular basement membrane thickening: isolated glomerular basement membrane thickening and only mild, nonspecific changes by light microscopy that do not meet the criteria of classes II through IV. Class II, mild or severe mesangial expansion: glomeruli classified as mild or severe mesangial expansion but without nodular sclerosis (Kimmelstiel–Wilson lesions) or global glomerulosclerosis in more than 50% of glomeruli. Class III, nodular sclerosis (Kimmelstiel–Wilson lesions): at least one glomerulus with a nodular increase in the mesangial matrix (Kimmelstiel–Wilson) without changes described in class IV. Class IV, advanced diabetic glomerulosclerosis: global glomerulosclerosis in more than 50% of glomeruli with other clinical or pathologic evidence that sclerosis is attributable to DKD [ ]. In this study, the renal specimens from patients with DKD we collected were from Class II- IV patients. The simplified (Chinese) MDRD equation was used to calculate the eGFR as follows: MDRD = 175× serum creatinine (mg/dL)−1.234 × years− 0.179 × (0.79, if female) [ ]. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fifth Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University (Zhuhai, China, 2022, No. K179-1), and all protocols were conducted in accordance with approved guidelines after informed patient consent was obtained. The basic clinical information of the human donors is shown in Supplementary Table .

## Generation of podocyte-specific SGLT2 knockout mice and genotyping
C57BL/6J mice with an SGLT2 targeting vector were generated by Cyagen Biosciences Inc (Guangzhou, China). Homozygotes (SGLT2flox/flox) were generated by mating heterozygous (SGLT2flox/+) mice with each other. SGLT2flox/flox mice were further crossed with podocin promoter (NPHS2)-driven Cre recombinase transgenic (NPHS2-Cre+) mice in two successive breeding rounds to generate homozygous podocyte-specific SGLT2-deficient (SGLT2CKO) mice.
Mouse tail DNA was isolated using a Tissue Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR was performed according to the PCR kit protocol (P131, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and the primers used for PCR genotyping are shown in Supplementary Table . The sizes of the products were as follows. Flox 1: A 146-bp fragment was produced in the wild-type (WT) mice, and a 205-bp fragment was produced in SGLT2flox/flox or SGLT2CKO mice. Flox 2: A 172-bp fragment was produced in the WT mice, and a 238-bp fragment was produced in SGLT2flox/flox or SGLT2CKO mice. Cre: A 200-bp fragment was produced in SGLT2CKO mice, but no fragment was produced in WT or SGLT2flox/flox mice. The products were resolved on a 2% agarose gel using a TAE buffer system and a 50 bp DNA ladder (MD108, TIANGEN, Beijing, China) was used to determine the size of the products.

## Animal protocol
The studies involving mice were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-Sen University (No.00314, Guangdong, China). Healthy four-week-old male wild-type C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (Foshan, Guangdong). Four-week-old podocyte-specific SGLT2 knockout male mice were generated as described above. All mice were maintained on a high-fat diet (HFD; 60 kcal% from fat, D12492, Research Diets) or a standard chow diet (12 kcal% from fat) and housed at 22–26 ℃ in a quiet room with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. After 4 weeks of HFD feeding, diabetic mice were induced by intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ; S0130, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Before receiving daily STZ injections, the mice were fasted for 12 h. STZ was intraperitoneally injected at a dose of 50 mg/kg body weight for 5 consecutive days. The control mice received an equal volume of 0.1 mol/L citrate buffer (pH = 4.5) at the same time. One week after the last injection, blood samples were taken from the tail vein of all mice to detect fasting blood glucose. Mice with fasting blood glucose levels ≥ 300 mg/dL were considered diabetic (marked as 0 week).
For the study of empagliflozin treatment in STZ/HFD-induced diabetic wild-type mice: Starting from the day when the diabetic model was confirmed to be successfully established through fasting blood glucose detection (marked as 0 week), and until the mice were euthanized (marked as 12 weeks), they were administered 10 mg/kg/d empagliflozin (HY-15409, MedChemExpress, USA) by daily oral gavage. The control mice also received an equal volume of 0.9% saline by daily oral gavage for 12 weeks.
All mice were anesthetized via isoflurane (RWD Life Science Co., China) inhalation and euthanized 12 weeks after the diabetic status was established. On the day before the mice were euthanized, they were housed in metabolic cages for 24 h and all urine was collected. Urine and serum were collected, and the kidneys and other organs were harvested for subsequent analysis.

## Isolation of primary mouse podocytes
Mouse kidneys were removed and cut into pieces on ice, digested in culture medium containing collagenase A (COLLA-RO, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and dispase (COLLDISP-RO, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 37 °C, and then passed through 100 μm and 70 μm sieves in succession. The filtered cells were coincubated with two podocyte-specific marker antibodies for 1 h (the antibodies used are shown in Supplementary Table ), and then isolated by using streptavidin-conjugated M280 magnetic Dynabeads (Invitrogen, USA) [ , ]. The magnetically attracted cells were considered to be primary podocytes and were processed for RNA or protein extraction.

## Urine and serum analysis
Urine albumin was measured using an Albuwell M kit (1011, Exocell, Philadelphia, PA, USA), and the values were normalized to urine creatinine levels, which were quantified in the same samples with a urinary creatinine assay kit (1012, Exocell, Philadelphia, PA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The urine albumin excretion rate was calculated as the urine albumin/creatinine ratio (UACR). Blood urea nitrogen was measured in serum using a Urea Nitrogen Colorimetric Detection Kit (EIABUN, Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Histopathology analysis by light microscopy
Mice were perfused with 0.9% sodium chloride solution before kidney removal. Kidney tissues for light microscopy were immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China) overnight, embedded in paraffin and further subjected to hematoxylin-eosin (H&E; DH0006, Leagene, Beijing, China), periodic acid-Schiff (PAS; DG0005, Leagene, Beijing, China) and Masson’s trichrome staining (DC0033, Leagene, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Light microscope analyses were based on 10 randomly selected glomeruli per mouse in each group using ImageJ software. The degree of glomerular mesangial expansion was assessed using PAS staining and the mesangial matrix index was quantified with ImageJ software as follows: mesangial matrix index = (mesangial area/total glomerular area)×100%. The mesangial area was defined as the PAS-positive and nucleus-free area, and the glomerular area was traced along the outline of the capillary loop and calculated using ImageJ. The assessment of glomerulosclerosis was based on the area of collagen deposition detected by Masson’s trichrome staining. The collagen deposition area was defined as the blue collagen-positive and nucleus-free area and calculated using ImageJ. The percentage of Masson’s trichrome staining area was quantified with ImageJ software as follows: Masson’s trichrome staining area = (collagen deposition area/total glomerular area)×100%.

## Observation of glomerular basement membraney podocyte foot process MAMs and mitochondrial morphology by transmission electron microscopy
Renal tissue samples for electron microscopy were cut into 1mm3 sections and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Alfa Aesar, USA) at 4 °C for 2 h. After washing, the tissue was post-fixed with 1% osmic acid (TED PELLA, USA) at 4 °C for 2 h. The tissue blocks were then dehydrated with ethanol and acetone and embedded in Epon812 Resin (TED PELLA, USA). Ultrathin 50–70 nm sections were cut using the cryo-ultramicrotome (Leica EM UC7, Leica, Germany), and then double-stained with 2% uranyl acetate (EMS, USA) and 3% lead citrate (TED PELLA, USA). Transmission electron microscope (JEM-1400 PLUS, Japan Electron Optics Laboratory Co., Ltd.) with an accelerating voltage of 100 kV was used to observe and photograph podocyte ultrastructure. Images were taken from randomly selected fields of view at 30,000× magnification. We obtained 3 different views for each sample.
ImageJ was used to quantify the average thickness of the glomerular basement membrane, and evaluate the average extent of foot process fusion and effacement.
MAMs and mitochondrial morphology were also assessed by transmission electron microscopy. Alterations in ER-mitochondrial contact points in podocytes were assessed using ImageJ software. MAMs were measured by the length of the mitochondrial membrane in contact with the ER (distance between the ER and mitochondria within 50 nm) and normalized to the mitochondrial perimeter, as previously described [ , ]. Notably, some mitochondria in the images were not surrounded by the ER. In this case, the MAMs length would be recorded as 0. Then, we took the average of all the quantitative MAMs measured in all the random images as the final result for each sample. Besides, mitochondrial morphology was assessed using two mitochondrial fission-related parameters: mitochondrial aspect ratio and mitochondrial length. The mitochondrial aspect ratio was defined as the ratio of the major axis to the minor axis of the ellipse. The mitochondrial length was the length of the major axis. Then, we took the average of all the quantitative mitochondrial aspect ratio and mitochondrial length measured in all the random images as the final result for each sample.

## SGLT2 immunofluorescence staining
The paraffin-embedded renal tissue sections and frozen renal tissue sections were sliced to approximately 5 μm thickness and mounted onto slides. The paraffin-embedded tissue slides were first deparaffinized and rehydrated. After antigen retrieval, the sections were blocked with donkey serum at 37 °C for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The sections were washed 3 times in PBS and incubated with the secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h on the next day. The primary antibodies and the secondary antibodies used for immunofluorescence are shown in Supplementary Table . DAPI staining was used to visualize the nuclei. Images were taken from randomly selected fields by confocal microscopy (LSM880, Carl Zeiss), and the mean fluorescence intensity was measured using ImageJ software.

## Cell culture and treatment
Conditionally immortalized human podocytes, a gift from Prof. Saleem, were cultured as previously described [ ]. Podocytes were incubated in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA). Podocytes were cultured at 33 °C in 5% CO2 (permissive conditions) to induce cell proliferation.
To induce differentiation, the cells were maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2 (non-permissive conditions) for at least 2 weeks. To prepare for the experiments, differentiated podocytes were exposed to normal glucose (NG, 5.5 mmol/L) or high glucose (HG, 30 mmol/L) medium for 72 h.
To further investigate the effects of mitochondrial calcium overload in MAMs-induced podocyte apoptosis, after incubation with NG or HG for 48 h, podocytes were treated with 100 nmol/L empagliflozin (HY-15409, MedChemExpress, USA) under NG or HG conditions for 24 h.
To further investigate the effects of empagliflozin, podocytes were pretreated with pretreated with BAPTA-AM (10 µmol/L, HY-100545, MedChemExpress, USA), or 2-APB (20 µmol/L, HY-W009724, MedChemExpress, USA), or Ru360 (10 µmol/L, 557440, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 1 h.
To investigate the roles of other selective SGLT2 inhibitors, after incubation with NG or HG for 48 h, podocytes were treated with 100 nmol/L canagliflozin (HY-10451, MedChemExpress, USA) or 100 nmol/L dapagliflozin (HY-10450, MedChemExpress, USA) under NG or HG conditions for 24 h.
In addition, to investigate the role of AMPK in SGLT2-mediated MAMs formation, after incubation with NG or HG for 48 h, podocytes were treated with 1 mmol/L of the AMPK activator AICAR (HY-13417, MedChemExpress, USA), or 5 µmol/L of the AMPK inhibitor Compound C (HY-13418, MedChemExpress, USA), or 100 nmol/L of empagliflozin together with 5 µmol/L of Compound C under NG or HG conditions for 24 h.

## Small interfering RNA (siRNA) and plasmid transfection
SGLT2 (also known as SLC5A2) siRNA was purchased from HanYi Bio (Guangzhou, China). The siRNAs used in this study were as follows: SGLT2 sense, AGAAGGCCCUGAUUGACAATT; SGLT2 anti-sense, UUGUCAAUCAGGGCCUUCUTT. The siRNAs were dissolved in ddH2O to prepare a 20 µmol/L stock solution. Differentiated podocytes grown in 6-well plates were transfected with siRNA in transfection medium containing Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, USA). For each transfection, 100 µL of transfection medium containing 5 µL of siRNA stock solution was gently mixed with 100 µL of transfection medium containing 5 µL of transfection reagent. After incubation for 15 min, the complexes were added to podocytes in 1.8 mL of transfection medium. Podocytes were incubated with this mixture for 6–8 h at 37 °C, after which the transfection medium was replaced with normal medium.
SGLT2 (also known as SLC5A2)-3×Flag plasmid was purchased from HanYi Bio (Guangzhou, China) and constructed using the pcDNA3.1 vector. The pcDNA3.1-3×Flag negative control plasmid was used as a control. OMM-ER linker plasmid (also known as OMM-RFP-ER, a synthetic linker encoding a monomeric red fluorescent protein (RFP) between the N-terminal outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) mAKAP1 targeting sequence and the C-terminal ER yUBC6 targeting sequence) and DsER plasmid (control plasmid) were gifts from Prof. Liu [ , ]. Based on the size of the fluorescent protein (4.2 × 2.4 nm), the maximal length of this construct was < 5 nm. Therefore, this construct can decrease the distance between these organelles and increase the number of ER-mitochondrial contact sites [ , ]. Podocytes grown in 6-well plates were transfected with plasmid in transfection medium containing Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). For each transfection, 100 µL of transfection medium containing 2 µg of plasmid and 4 µL of p3000 was gently mixed with 100 µL of transfection medium containing 4 µL of transfection reagent. After incubation for 15 min, the complexes were added to podocytes in 1.8 mL of transfection medium. Podocytes were incubated with this mixture for 6–8 h at 37 °C, after which the transfection medium was replaced with normal medium.

## In situ proximity ligation assay (PLA)
Labeling the classical MAMs structure of the Inositol-1,4,5-triphosphate receptor (IP3R) - Glucose regulated protein 75 (GRP75) - Voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1) complex by in situ PLA is a classical method for identifying MAMs and has been widely used in the detection and quantification of MAMs in the last decade [ , – ]. In this study, the status of MAMs was evaluated using IP3R - VDAC1 PLA assays. The primary antibodies used were anti-IP3R and anti-VDAC1 (the details of the antibodies are shown in Supplementary Table ). Immunofluorescent IP3R-VDAC1 PLA assays were performed using the Duolink® In Situ Detection Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (DUO92002, DUO92004, DUO92008 or DUO92014, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Besides, immunohistochemical IP3R-VDAC1 PLA assays were done using the Duolink® In Situ Detection kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (DUO92002, DUO92004, DUO92012, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA). Images were taken from randomly selected fields of view using confocal microscopy (LSM880, Carl Zeiss, Germany) or Olympus BX53 microscope (Olympus, Japan). PLA signals were quantified using the “Particle Analysis” function of ImageJ software.

## Flow cytometric analysis of apoptosis
Podocytes (2 × 105 cells/well) were harvested using 0.25% Trypsin without EDTA for 4 min. After double-staining with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) according to the instructions of the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (A211, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), the cells were subjected to flow cytometry (CytoFlex LX, Beckman, USA). A total of 105 cells were analyzed for each sample, and each condition was tested in three independent samples. FlowJo v10 software was used to calculate the percentage of apoptotic cells. The apoptosis rate = Q3 (Annexin V-FITC+/PI–, early apoptotic cells) + Q2 (Annexin V-FITC+/PI+, late apoptotic cells).

## TUNEL apoptosis assays
A TUNEL BrightGreen Apoptosis Detection Kit (A112, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was used based on the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, frozen renal sections were dried at room temperature for 20 min and then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. The sections were incubated with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 15 min and equilibrated with equilibration buffer for 30 min, followed by incubation with 50 µL of the TdT reaction mixture at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark. Subsequently, the sections were counterstained with DAPI. Images were taken from randomly selected fields of view using confocal microscopy (LSM880, Carl Zeiss, Germany). The number of apoptotic cells in the glomeruli for each sample was counted for quantification.

## Measurement of calcium fluorescence
Specific calcium fluorescent probes Mag-Fura-2 AM (2 µM, Invitrogen, USA) and Rhod-2 AM (5µM, Invitrogen, USA) were used to measure the ER and mitochondrial Ca2+ concentrations, respectively. Podocytes were co-incubated with Mag-Fura-2 AM and Rhod-2 AM in calcium-free Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (Biosharp, China) at 37 °C for 30 min, and then washed to remove the extraneous dye. Hoechst 33,342 (Beyotime, China) was used to stain nuclei. Cells were immediately measured ER and mitochondrial Ca2+ concentrations using confocal microscopy (LSM880, Carl Zeiss, Germany) at 458 nm and 543 nm excitation wavelengths, respectively. Image mean fluorescent intensity was quantified using Image J software.

## Isolation of MAMs from podocytes
The MAMs, mitochondrial, and ER fractions were isolated from cultured podocytes by Percoll gradient fractionation. Briefly, podocytes from twenty 100 mm cell culture dishes were digested with trypsin and centrifuged at 600 ×g for 5 min twice to collect the cells. Then, the podocytes were lysed in buffer 1 (225 mM mannitol, 75 mM sucrose, 0.1 mM EGTA and 30 mM Tris-HCl, pH = 7.4) supplemented with protease inhibitors. The homogenate was centrifuged twice at 600 ×g for 5 min to collect the supernatant. The supernatant was then recentrifuged for 10 min at 7,000 ×g to separate the resulting pellet (crude mitochondrial fraction) and supernatant (ER fraction). Using a 41.1 SW rotor in the Beckman ultracentrifuge, the supernatant was centrifuged at 100,000 ×g for 1 h to obtain the resulting pellet (ER fraction). The crude mitochondrial fraction was resuspended in buffer 2 (225 mM mannitol, 75 mM sucrose and 30 mM Tris-HCl, pH = 7.4) and centrifuged twice at 7,000 ×g for 10 min to collect the resulting pellet. Then, the pellet was resuspended using a 30% Percoll gradient in mitochondrial resuspending buffer (MRB; 250 mM mannitol, 5 mM HEPES pH = 7.4 and 0.5 mM EGTA) and centrifuged at 95,000 ×g for 30 min. The pure mitochondrial fraction was obtained at the bottom of the tube, and the crude MAMs fraction was localized to the intermediate layer. Then, the pure mitochondrial fraction was resuspended in MRB buffer and centrifuged twice at 6,300 ×g for 10 min to collect the resulting pellet (mitochondrial fraction). The crude MAMs fraction was also resuspended in MRB buffer and centrifuged at 6,300 ×g for 10 min to collect the supernatant. Then, the supernatant was centrifuged at 100,000 ×g for 1 h to collect the resulting pellet (MAMs fraction) [ , ]. Equal amounts of protein (25 µg) from each fraction were used for further western blot analysis.

## Adenosine diphosphate (ADP)/ adenosine triphosphate (ATP) ratio assay
Podocytes were grown and differentiated on a white 96-well plate at a density of 3000–4000 cells per well. The ADP/ATP ratio was measured via an ADP/ATP Ratio Assay Kit-Luminescence (A552, Dojindo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

## Quantitative real-time PCR
Total RNA from cultured human podocytes was obtained using a Total RNA Kit I (R6834, Omega, Guangzhou, China). Total RNA was isolated from mouse tissue using TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa Biotechnology, Dalian, China). RNA purity and concentration were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was prepared using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (RR047A, TaKaRa Biotechnology, Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time PCR was performed in a CFX96 Touch Real-time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) with 12.5 µL of TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (RR820A, TaKaRa Biotechnology, Dalian, China), 0.4 µM primers, 2 µL of cDNA and 8.5 µL of ddH2O. The cycling conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression was normalized to that of the β-actin internal control and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCq method in Microsoft Office Excel. The primers used for quantitative real-time PCR are shown in Supplementary Table .

## Western blot analysis
Podocytes were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime Technology, Shanghai, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime Technology, Shanghai, China) at 4 °C. The suspension was centrifuged at 12,000×g and the supernatant containing the proteins was collected. A total of 30 µg of protein lysate was diluted in loading buffer, separated by 10% SDS‒PAGE (EpiZyme, Shanghai, China), and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) at 100 V for 2 h. The membranes were rinsed in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST, Beyotime Technology, Shanghai, China) for 5 min three times. Then, the membranes were immersed in blocking buffer (5% milk powder in TBST) for 1 h and then rinsed in TBST for 5 min three times. The membranes were incubated with the primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After rinsing in TBST for 5 min three times, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature and developed using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (WBKLS0500, Merck Millipore, USA). The primary antibodies and the secondary antibodies used are shown in Supplementary Table . Densitometric analyses were conducted using ImageJ software.

## Statistics
The data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation, and all experiments were performed at least three independent times, unless stated otherwise. The data were evaluated with GraphPad Prism (version 9.0, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Comparisons between two groups were performed by two-tailed Student’s t tests. Comparisons among multiple groups were tested using one-way or two-way ANOVA with a multiple comparisons post hoc test. A normal distribution was assessed using a Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test and normal data distribution was assumed when P > 0.05. Non-normal distributed data were analyzed by Mann‒Whitney test. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

## Results

## MAMs are increased in podocytes and associated with renal dysfunction in DKD patients
Observing the contact sites between mitochondria and the ER by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and labeling the classical MAMs marker Inositol-1,4,5-triphosphate receptor (IP3R) - Glucose regulated protein 75 (GRP75) - Voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1) complex by in situ proximity ligation assay (PLA) are two widely used methods for observing and quantifying MAMs [ ]. TEM revealed increased MAMs in podocytes from subjects with DKD compared with those from control individuals (Fig.  A, B). The number of MAMs in podocytes gradually increased from class II to IV (DKD glomerular pathological classification is indicated by PAS staining) (Fig.  A, B). In addition, similar trends as TEM experiment were found in glomerulus from DKD patients by PLA (Fig.  C, D). Furthermore, the increase in MAMs in podocytes from DKD patients observed by TEM was positively correlated with the 24 h proteinuria and the urine albumin creatine ratio (UACR), and negatively correlated with the eGFR (Fig.  E-G), which indicates that increased MAMs in podocytes might contribute to podocyte injury and renal dysfunction in DKD patients.
 

## Increased MAMs aggravate high glucose-induced podocyte injury
To further clarify the association of increased MAMs with podocyte injury, we transfected podocytes with OMM-ER linker plasmid (a synthetic linker encoding a < 5 nm protein between the N-terminal outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM) targeting sequence and the C-terminal ER targeting sequence), which is widely used to increase the number of ER-mitochondrial contact sites [ , ]. PLA showed that MAMs were significantly increased in OMM-ER linker plasmid-transfected podocytes when compared with control plasmid-transfected podocytes (Supplementary Fig.  A, B). Besides, the pro-apoptosis markers Bax and cleaved Caspase-3 were significantly upregulated while the anti-apoptosis marker Bcl-2 was downregulated in OMM-ER linker plasmid-transfected podocytes (Supplementary Fig.  C, D). The apoptosis rate was increased in podocytes treated with high glucose (HG), while it was further aggravated in podocytes transfected with OMM-ER linker plasmid (Supplementary Fig.  E, F). These results further indicate that increased MAMs in podocytes may contribute to HG-induced podocyte injury.
Besides, it has been well established that calcium is transferred from ER to mitochondria through MAMs, which is essential for maintaining calcium homeostasis [ ]. Continuously increased MAMs will lead to excessive ER-mitochondrial calcium transfer, resulting in cellular mitochondrial calcium overload and ultimately leading to increased apoptosis [ , , ]. In order to explore whether mitochondrial calcium overload is involved in MAMs-induced podocyte apoptosis, we pretreated the OMM-ER linker plasmid-transfected podocytes with BAPTA-AM (cytoplasmic calcium chelator, reducing cytoplasmic free Ca2+ concentration), 2-APB (IP3R inhibitor, effectively inhibiting ER Ca2+ efflux) or Ru360 (a cell-permeable oxygen-bridged dinuclear ruthenium amine complex, effectively inhibiting mitochondrial Ca2+ influx), respectively. We found that 2-APB or Ru360 pretreatment significantly reduced apoptosis induced by OMM-ER linker plasmid transfection, while no statistically significant reduction of apoptosis was found in BAPTA-AM pretreatment group (Supplementary Fig.  G, H), suggesting that increased MAMs facilitate calcium transfer between ER and mitochondria and thus aggravate mitochondrial calcium overload, which may be involved in MAMs-induced podocyte apoptosis.

## SGLT2 is increased in podocytes under diabetic conditions in vivo and in vitro
SGLT2 mRNA expression was increased in the glomeruli of DKD patients according to the Nephroseq v5 online database (https://nephroseq.org) (Fig.  A), suggesting that SGLT2 is not only specific to tubules, but also expressed in glomeruli. Then, we further explored the expression of SGLT2 in podocytes under diabetic conditions. Using double-label immunofluorescence for SGLT2 and the podocyte-specific marker synaptopodin (Synpo), we observed that SGLT2 expression in podocytes from DKD patients was significantly upregulated when compared with that in podocytes from control individuals (Fig.  B, C). Similar trends could also be found in podocytes from diabetic mice induced by streptozotocin (STZ) and a high-fat diet (HFD) (Fig.  E-H) and cultured human podocytes treated with HG (Fig.  I, J). These results suggest that SGLT2 is increased in podocytes under diabetic conditions both in vivo and in vitro. Furthermore, increased SGLT2 was positively correlated with podocyte MAMs level in DKD patients (as evaluated by the IP3R-VDAC1 PLA in Fig.  C, D) (Fig.  D). These results suggest that increased SGLT2 may play a role in promoting MAMs formation in DKD.
 

## Empagliflozin protects podocyte from HG-induced cell injury by reducing MAMs formation
Next, we explored the effects of SGLT2 inhibitor empagliflozin on MAMs formation and podocyte injury. In the dose- and time-dependent experiments of empagliflozin on diabetic podocyte protection (podocyte damage was indicated by decreased expression of the podocyte marker nephrin), we found that empagliflozin showed dose- and time- dependent protective effect on podocyte injury, while there was no significant statistical difference on podocyte protection between 100 nM and 500 nM, as well as between 24 h and 48 h (Supplementary Fig.  ). Based on these results, we selected 100nM treatment dose and 24 h treatment time of empagliflozin.
SGLT2 was increased in HG-incubated podocytes, which was decreased after empagliflozin treatment (Fig.  A, B). Then, we found that empagliflozin treatment significantly reduced podocyte MAMs formation induced by HG (Fig.  C, D). In addition, similar trends were also found in podocytes treated with canagliflozin and dapagliflozin, two other SGLT2 inhibitors (Supplementary Fig.  ). Besides, ER Ca2+ and mitochondrial Ca2+ concentrations were examined using Mag-Fura-2 AM and Rhod-2 AM, respectively. The ratio of mito Ca2+/ER Ca2+ was increased in HG-treated podocytes, which was significantly reduced after empagliflozin treatment (Supplementary Fig.  A, B), indicating that empagliflozin treatment can reduce HG-induced mitochondrial calcium overload. And increased podocyte apoptosis induced by HG was attenuated after empagliflozin treatment (Fig.  E-H). Furthermore, we also found that upregulation of MAMs formation by transfecting OMM-ER linker plasmid significantly reversed the protective effect of empagliflozin on diabetic podocyte apoptosis (Fig.  I-L). These results suggest that empagliflozin treatment improves HG-induced podocyte injury by reducing MAMs formation.
 

## Genetic manipulation of SGLT2 affects HG-induced MAMs formation and cell injury in human podocytes
Then, we explored the effects of the genetic regulation of SGLT2 on podocyte MAMs formation. We knocked down SGLT2 expression in human podocytes using small interfering RNA (siRNA) and induced SGLT2 overexpression (OE) by transfecting podocytes with SGLT2 plasmid (Supplementary Fig.  A-C; Supplementary Fig.  A-C). SGLT2 siRNA reduced MAMs formation induced by HG (Supplementary Fig.  D, E), while SGLT2 OE further aggravated MAMs formation induced by HG (Supplementary Fig.  D, E). In addition, SGLT2 siRNA reduced HG-induced mitochondrial calcium overload and apoptosis in podocytes (Supplementary Fig.  C, D; Supplementary Fig.  F-I), while SGLT2 OE showed the opposite effects (Supplementary Fig.  E, F; Supplementary Fig.  F-I). These results further suggest that SGLT2 is a key target for regulating MAMs formation and injury in podocytes under diabetic conditions.

## Empagliflozin attenuates excessive podocyte MAMs formation and podocyte injury in diabetic mice
The diabetic status in mice was induced by HFD feeding followed by intraperitoneal injection of STZ. The mice were then administered empagliflozin by gavage to block SGLT2. SGLT2 was increased in podocytes isolated from diabetic mice, which was decreased after empagliflozin treatment (Fig.  A, B). We observed that diabetic mice exhibited lower body weights and higher blood glucose levels (Fig.  C, D), indicating successful establishment of the diabetic model. Although no significant difference in body weight was observed, empagliflozin treatment significantly reduced blood glucose levels (Fig.  C, D).
 Besides, compared with those in STZ/HFD-induced diabetic mice, lower kidney/body weight ratios, 24 h urine albumin excretion, UACR, and serum BUN were found in diabetic mice treated with empagliflozin (Fig.  E-H), indicating that empagliflozin administration ameliorated albuminuria and renal dysfunction in STZ/HFD-induced diabetic mice. The histopathological characteristics observed in diabetic mice induced by STZ/HFD, including severe mesangial matrix expansion and glomerulosclerosis, increased glomerular basement membrane thickness, and aggravated podocyte foot process effacement were significantly attenuated by empagliflozin treatment (Fig.  I-M).
Compared with control mice, increased MAMs (detected by TEM and IP3R-VDAC1 PLA) were found in diabetic mice induced by STZ/HFD, which was decreased in diabetic mice treated with empagliflozin (Fig.  A, B, E, F). Besides, we also evaluated mitochondrial morphology in podocytes using TEM. We found that two mitochondrial fission-related parameters (mitochondrial aspect ratio and mitochondrial length) were decreased in podocytes from diabetic mice, which were restored in podocytes from diabetic mice treated with empagliflozin (Fig.  C, D), suggesting that empagliflozin treatment can improve mitochondrial morphological abnormalities such as excessive mitochondrial fission in diabetic podocytes. And podocyte injury (indicated by decreased expression of the podocyte markers nephrin and synaptopodin (Synpo)) was observed in diabetic mice induced by STZ/HFD, which was was significantly ameliorated after empagliflozin treatment (Fig.  G, H). TUNEL staining and apoptosis-related proteins detection revealed that STZ/HFD-induced diabetic mice exhibited severe podocyte apoptosis, which was alleviated by empagliflozin treatment (Fig.  I-L).
 These data suggest that empagliflozin attenuates excessive MAMs formation and podocyte injury in diabetic mice induced by STZ/HFD, which supports our hypothesis.

## Podocyte-specific SGLT2 knockout attenuates excessive podocyte MAMs formation and podocyte injury in diabetic mice
Since SGLT2 is significantly expressed in renal tubules, the role of renal tubules in the empagliflozin- treated mouse model cannot be ruled out. Therefore, to assess the contribution of podocyte SGLT2 to MAMs formation and podocyte damage in vivo, podocyte-specific SGLT2 knockout mice were generated using the Cre-loxP system (Supplementary Fig.  A-C). Immunofluorescence and western blotting were performed to confirm the conditional knockout of SGLT2 in podocytes. Compared to that in SGLT2flox/flox mice (SGLT2f/f mice), SGLT2 expression was abolished in the podocytes of SGLT2flox/flox, NPHS2-Cre+ mice (SGLT2CKO mice) (Supplementary Fig.  D-F), while no significant difference in SGLT2 expression was found in renal tubules between SGLT2f/f and SGLT2CKO mice (Supplementary Fig.  G).
To determine the effects of podocyte-specific SGLT2 deficiency under diabetic conditions, SGLT2f/f and SGLT2CKO mice were induced to a diabetic state by HFD feeding and intraperitoneal injection of STZ. The diabetic mice exhibited lower body weights and higher blood glucose levels (Fig.  A, B). There was a slight decrease in blood glucose levels in the SGLT2CKO diabetic group compared to those in the SGLT2f/f diabetic group, while no significant differences were observed in body weight between the SGLT2f/f and SGLT2CKO diabetic groups (Fig.  A, B). Besides, we found that the SGLT2f/f diabetic group exhibited increased kidney/body weight, 24 h urine albumin excretion, UACR, and serum BUN, which were decreased in the SGLT2CKO diabetic group (Fig.  C-F). Furthermore, SGLT2f/f diabetic mice exhibited more severe mesangial matrix expansion and glomerulosclerosis, increased glomerular basement membrane thickness, and aggravated podocyte foot process effacement, while these histopathological features improved in SGLT2CKO diabetic mice (Fig.  G-K).
 Besides, increased MAMs (detected by TEM and IP3R-VDAC1 PLA) and excessive mitochondrial fission (indicated by decreased mitochondrial aspect ratio and mitochondrial length) were found in podocytes from SGLT2f/f diabetic mice showed excessive podocyte MAMs formation, which were both restored in SGLT2CKO diabetic mice (Fig.  A-F). And severe podocyte injury (indicated by decreased expression of the podocyte markers nephrin and synaptopodin (Synpo)) was found in SGLT2f/f diabetic mice, which was ameliorated in SGLT2CKO diabetic mice (Fig.  G, H). TUNEL staining and apoptosis-related proteins detection revealed that SGLT2f/f diabetic mice exhibited severe podocyte apoptosis, which was alleviated in SGLT2CKO diabetic mice (Fig.  I-L).
 These results further suggest that SGLT2 contributes to the regulation of podocyte MAMs formation and podocyte injury under diabetic conditions.

## The regulation of AMPK signaling is required for SGLT2-mediated MAMs formation in human podocytes under diabetic conditions

We then investigated the mechanisms underlying the protective effect of SGLT2 inhibition on MAMs formation in podocytes. Empagliflozin and SGLT2 siRNA reversed the HG-induced reduction in AMPK activity (as assessed by phospho-AMPK/AMPK ratio), while SGLT2 OE exacerbated the decrease in AMPK activity (Fig.  A-F). Moreover, AMPK activity was also decreased in podocytes isolated from diabetic mice induced by STZ/HFD, while empagliflozin administration and podocyte-specific SGLT2 knockout significantly restored AMPK activity (Supplementary Fig.  ). These data suggest that AMPK signaling is activated downstream of SGLT2 inhibition in diabetic podocytes. AMPK is a sensor of cellular energy status and can be activated by increases in adenosine monophosphate (AMP) or adenosine monophosphate (ADP) relative to adenosine triphosphate (ATP) [ ]. Since the cellular AMP level is low and difficult to measure in cultured cells, we measured the ADP: ATP ratio instead of the AMP: ATP ratio [ ]. We found that empagliflozin and SGLT2 siRNA increased the ADP/ATP ratio in podocytes, while SGLT2 OE decreased the ADP/ATP ratio in podocytes (Fig.  G). These data suggest that SGLT2 inhibition may activate AMPK by increasing the ADP/ATP ratio in podocytes.
 Next, we wondered whether AMPK plays a role in regulating podocyte MAMs formation. We used a biochemical fractionation method to obtain the ER, mitochondrial, and MAMs fractions from podocytes. And we found that AMPK was enriched in the mitochondrial and MAMs fractions but was barely detectable in the ER fraction (Fig.  H). Therefore, AMPK is located in podocyte MAMs. Podocytes treated with AICAR, an AMPK activator, reversed the increase in MAMs formation induced by HG, while the AMPK inhibitor Compound C (CC) had the opposite effect (Fig.  I, J), which indicates that AMPK is a key regulator of podocyte MAMs formation.
To further investigate the relationship between SGLT2 and AMPK in podocyte MAMs formation, we used CC to inhibit AMPK activity in podocytes treated with empagliflozin. CC treatment reversed the protective effect of empagliflozin against increased MAMs formation (Fig.  I, J). We also found that the MAMs-related proteins IP3R, GRP75, and VDAC1 were increased in podocytes treated with HG, but were significantly reduced after empagliflozin treatment (Fig.  K, L). However, CC treatment significantly reversed the effects of empagliflozin on the expression of these proteins (Fig.  K, L).
These data indicate that the regulation of AMPK signaling is required for SGLT2-mediated MAMs formation in human podocytes under diabetic conditions.

## Discussion

Diabetic kidney disease is the leading cause of end-stage renal disease, which inevitably ends with dialysis and kidney transplantation. It poses a significant challenge to health-care systems worldwide [ ]. Clinical practice guidelines now recommend SGLT2 inhibitors as a first-line therapy for the management of DKD, which provides significant benefits for cardiovascular and renal protection beyond glycemic control [ ]. Various hypotheses have been proposed to explain the nephroprotective effects of SGLT2 inhibitors, including normalizing tubuloglomerular feedback, lowering intraglomerular pressure, stimulating erythropoiesis, improving organ oxygen delivery, improving inflammation and oxidative stress, increasing autophagy, and promoting metabolic reprogramming [ ]. However, the mechanisms underlying the protective effects of SGLT2 inhibitors in DKD still require further exploration. In this study, we revealed novel mechanistic details about SGLT2 inhibitors’ protective effect on regulating MAMs formation through activating the AMPK pathway in diabetic podocytes, which may expand our knowledge of the effects of SGLT2 inhibitors in improving DKD podocyte injury and provide new insights into DKD treatment.

Recent studies have shown that there are close structural and functional connections between mitochondria and the ER, called MAMs, which are physical platforms that facilitate communication between the ER and mitochondria [ ]. Increasing evidence suggests that MAMs contain abundant functional proteins and have emerged as key hubs for signaling molecules that integrate intracellular homeostasis and biological functions [ , ]. The roles of ER and mitochondrial dysfunction in podocytes under diabetic conditions in DKD progression have been extensively studied [ , ], but the role of podocyte MAMs in DKD pathophysiology is not yet fully understood. We and Wei et al. previously demonstrated that MAMs were increased in podocytes under diabetic conditions [ , ]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report that podocyte MAMs gradually increased in different stages of DKD and increased podocyte MAMs were positively correlated with the levels of 24 h proteinuria and UACR, and negatively correlated with GFR. Furthermore, in vitro experiments also confirmed that the upregulation of MAMs in podocytes further aggravated diabetic podocyte injury. These data indicate that increased MAMs in podocytes may play a vital role in DKD development and progression. Besides, It’s well known that MAMs play an important role in maintaining calcium homeostasis [ ]. IP3R-GRP75-VDAC1 complex is a critical hub in the regulation of ER-mitochondrial calcium exchange [ ], and we found that IP3R-GRP75-VDAC1 complex was significantly increased in diabetic podocytes detected by PLA assays. Besides, Mag-Fura-2 AM and Rhod-2 AM staining results suggested that excessive ER-mitochondrial calcium transfer existed in diabetic podocytes. Transient basal fluctuations in mitochondrial calcium drive mitochondrial metabolism for ATP production, maintaining optimal cellular bioenergetics [ ]. However, excessive calcium uptake into the mitochondrial matrix may lead to mitochondrial calcium overload and trigger mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening and subsequent cell apoptosis [ , ]. We found that pharmacological inhibition of ER calcium efflux or mitochondrial influx significantly improved MAMs-mediated podocyte apoptosis, rather than inhibiting cytoplasmic calcium, suggesting that increased MAMs exacerbate podocyte apoptosis by aggravating mitochondrial calcium overload in podocytes, which may be a core pathogenic mechanism of DKD.
SGLT2 inhibitors are an approved class of anti-hyperglycemic agents for the treatment of DKD that block renal glucose reabsorption and promote glycosuria by inhibiting SGLT2, the primary glucose transporter in proximal tubules [ ]. It is generally believed that SGLT2 is specifically expressed in renal proximal tubules and that SGLT2 inhibitors reduce proteinuria in DKD patients by increasing glucose and sodium delivery to the macula densa and subsequently activating tubuloglomerular feedback [ ]. Interestingly, consistent with several other recent studies [ – , , ], we found that SGLT2 was expressed in podocytes. And we also found that SGLT2 was increased in podocytes under diabetic conditions, which was restored after empagliflozin treatment both in vivo and in vitro. These results suggest that the direct effect of SGLT2 inhibitors on podocytes may be related to their Renal protective effects. And there are several studies that may help us explain the mechanism by which SGLT2 was increased in diabetic podocytes and decreased after empagliflozin treatment. Analysis of the promoter region of SGLT2 gene identified five binding sites of SP-1 and one binding site of hepatocyte nuclear factor − 1α (HNF-1α) upstream of the transcriptional start site [ ], and transcription factor HNF-1α and SP-1 have been proved to be crucial to activate SGLT2 transcription [ , ]. Freitas et al. found that SGLT2 and HNF-1α mRNA expression, HNF-1α binding activity, and the effect of HNF-1α binding into the promoter region of SGLT2 gene were increased by 51%, 30%, 100%, and 77% respectively in diabetic kidney, which were reduced after a short-term SGLT2 inhibitor treatment [ ]. Zhang et al. found that SP-1 was increased in diabetic podocytes [ ], while Liu et al. found that empagliflozin treatment decreased SP-1 expression in diabetic kidney [ ]. Besides, Fu et al. confirmed that another transcription factor nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) bound to the region of SGLT2 promoter and participated in the activation of SGLT2 expression [ ]. NF-κB was actived in diabetic podocytes [ ], which was inhibited after SGLT2 inhibitors treatment [ , ]. However, further explorations are still necessary to determine the mechanisms by which SGLT2 was increased in diabetic podocytes and decreased after empagliflozin treatment.

Durcan et al. reported that DKD patients receiving SGLT2 inhibitors showed less podocyte damage and less podocyte excretion into the urine, which suggests that SGLT2 inhibitors may have structural and functional benefits for glomerular health [ ]. Besides, Cassis et al. reported that dapagliflozin alleviated podocyte damage in proteinuric nondiabetic nephropathy [ ], while Klimontov et al. found that empagliflozin alleviated podocytopathy and enhanced nephrin expression in db/db diabetic mice [ ]. Hudkins et al. also reported that empagliflozin treatment exhibited structural and functional benefits on podocytes in BTBR ob/ob mice [ ]. Consistent with the above studies, we demonstrated that empagliflozin protected against podocyte injury under diabetic conditions both in vivo and in vitro. Besides, we generated podocyte-conditional SGLT2 knockout diabetic mice, which also exhibited similar protective effects as empagliflozin treatment on podocytes in vivo. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of generating podocyte-conditional SGLT2 knockout mice to study the role of SGLT2 in podocytes under diabetic conditions, which contributed to further understanding the roles of podocyte SGLT2 in diabetic podocyte injury.

However, there are few studies on the mechanisms of SGLT2 inhibitors in podocytes, which still remains controversial. Yang et al. reported that dapagliflozin alleviated advanced glycation end product-induced podocyte injury by inducing podocyte autophagy [ ]. Ge et al. found that empagliflozin reduces podocyte lipotoxicity in experimental Alport syndrome [ ]. Guo et al. showed that dapagliflozin suppressed epithelial-mesenchymal transition in podocytes under diabetic conditions [ ]. However, Locatelli et al. reported that although SGLT2 was expressed in podocytes from BTBR ob/ob mice, the glomerular protection provided by empagliflozin was not directly related to its activity in podocytes. This might be due to a limitation of the paracrine signaling of podocyte-derived VEGF-A, which resulted in a reduction in abnormal endothelial caveolin-1 and PV-1 and preservation of glomerular endothelial function and permeability [ ]. Thus, further investigations are necessary to evaluate the mechanisms of SGLT2 inhibitors in diabetic podocytes. In the present study, we found that empagliflozin and SGLT2 downregulation reduced MAMs formation in diabetic podocytes in vivo and in vitro, while SGLT2 OE had the opposite effect. We demonstrated for the first time that SGLT2 was a key target for regulating MAMs formation in podocytes under diabetic conditions, which might expand our knowledge of the mechanisms by which SGLT2 inhibitors improve podocyte injury and DKD progression. Similar trends could also be found in podocytes treated with two other SGLT2 inhibitors canagliflozin and dapagliflozin, which suggested that regulating MAMs formation might be a class effect of all SGLT2 inhibitors.

We also explored the underlying mechanisms of SGLT2 in regulating MAMs formation in diabetic podocytes. AMPK is an important nutrient sensor, whose activity decreases in DKD podocytes [ , ]. A previous study reported an inverse relationship between SGLT2 activity and AMPK activation in the kidney [ ]. Tan et al. studied transcriptomic changes in response to empagliflozin based on RNA-sequencing (RNA-Seq) in eight different tissues from a spontaneous hypertension rat model. The AMPK signaling was enriched in the differentially-expressed genes of the empagliflozin-treated model [ ]. Liu et al. also reported that empagliflozin activated the AMPK pathway in diabetic renal tubules [ ]. In this study, we found that the AMPK signaling was activated downstream of SGLT2 inhibition in podocytes. We also found that SGLT2 inhibition might activate AMPK by increasing cellular ADP/ATP ratio in diabetic podocytes, which was consistent with previous studies showing that SGLT2 inhibitors activated AMPK through increasing cellular AMP or ADP in cardiac microvascular endothelial cells and HEK-293 cells [ , ]. Therefore, AMPK activation may be a potential protective mechanism of SGLT2 inhibitors [ ]. Several studies showed that AMPK was a key regulator of MAMs formation in cardiomyocytes and HeLa cells [ , ]. Thus, we speculated that AMPK might be involved in SGLT2-mediated MAMs formation in podocytes. In this study, we found that AMPK was located in podocyte MAMs and that regulating AMPK activity affected MAMs formation in podocytes. Furthermore, inhibition of AMPK activity reversed the protective effects of empagliflozin on podocyte MAMs formation. Therefore, AMPK activation is necessary for the protective effects of SGLT2 inhibitors on the regulation of podocyte MAMs formation under diabetic conditions.

There are also several limitations to our results as follows: (1) The regulation of podocyte MAMs formation by canagliflozin and dapagliflozin, two other SGLT2 inhibitors, requires further study, especially in vivo. (2) A comparison of the protective effects on MAMs formation and podocyte injury between SGLT2 inhibitors and other glucose-lowering therapies unrelated to SGLT2 inhibition in vivo will be required in the future. (3) Detection of the protective effects of empagliflozin on podocyte injury in proximal tubules-specific Sglt2 knockout mice administered with empagliflozin will be required in the future, in order to further demonstrate empagliflozin’s direct protective effects on podocytes without the influence of renal tubular SGLT2 in vivo. (4) Podocyte-specific SGLT2 overexpressing mice will be needed in the future to explore the effects of podocyte SGLT2 overexpression on diabetic podocytes in vivo. (5) Due to the difficulties in obtaining renal biopsy samples from early DKD patients with glomerular hyperfiltration, we lack sufficient data and evidence for the correlation analysis between elevated eGFR and podocyte MAMs in patients with glomerular hyperfiltration in the current study, which may not follow a linear distribution and requires to collect more data for further analysis in the future. (6) The relationship between AMPK and MAMs formation in podocytes deserves further investigation.

## Conclusion

Our study revealed that MAMs were increased in diabetic podocytes and contribute to podocyte injury. The expression of SGLT2 was increased in diabetic podocytes. In addition, empagliflozin-treatment and podocyte-specific SGLT2 knockout attenuated MAMs formation and podocyte injury in diabetic mice. Empagliflozin treatment and SGLT2 knockdown decreased podocyte MAMs formation by activating the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway both in vivo and in vitro, while SGLT2 overexpression had the opposite effect. Thus, inhibition of SGLT2 attenuates MAMs imbalance in diabetic podocytes by activating the AMPK pathway, which may expand our knowledge of the roles of SGLT2 inhibitors in improving DKD podocyte injury and provide new insights into DKD treatment and other podocyte diseases.
