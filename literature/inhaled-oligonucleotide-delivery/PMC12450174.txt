## Abstract
Connective tissue disease-related interstitial lung disease (CTD-ILD) refers to a range of pulmonary complications arising from connective tissue disorders, characterized by alveolar inflammation and interstitial fibrosis. Various factors, such as the specific type of connective tissue disease, infections, and hypoxemia, influence acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD. Treatment strategies typically include targeted interventions for precipitating factors, glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, and supportive care. Glucocorticoids play a key role in managing acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD; however, the optimal dosing and duration of treatment remain uncertain. Immunosuppressants show potential therapeutic value, but further studies are needed to determine the most effective regimens for CTD-ILD patients. Supportive care, including respiratory support and oxygen therapy, is crucial for symptom relief and correction of hypoxia. Despite recent treatment advancements in China, significant challenges remain in optimizing outcomes and improving survival rates in CTD-ILD, highlighting the need for a comprehensive, individualized management approach for Chinese patients. Future research should focus on elucidating the pathogenesis of CTD-ILD, tailoring treatment strategies, and establishing standardized diagnostic and management protocols based on the Chinese population.
Keywords: Systemic autoimmune rheumatic disease, Interstitial lung disease with systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases, Acute exacerbation, Glucocorticoids, Immunosuppressor

## Key Summary Points

 

## Introduction
Connective tissue disease-related interstitial lung disease (CTD-ILD) refers to pulmonary complications caused by connective tissue diseases, characterized by inflammation of the alveolar units and interstitial fibrosis [ ]. A variety of connective tissue diseases—including systemic sclerosis, rheumatoid arthritis, polymyositis/dermatomyositis, Sjögren’s syndrome, and systemic lupus erythematosus—can lead to the development of CTD-ILD [ ]. A systematic review has reported considerable global variation in the incidence and prevalence of CTD-ILD, with prevalence rates ranging from 7.5% in Belgium to 33.3% in Canada and 34.8% in Saudi Arabia [ ]. In China, interstitial lung disease (ILD) presents a significant health burden, and CTD-ILD is one of the most common subtypes, accounting for approximately 18.3% of ILD cases according to recent epidemiological data [ ]. Clinically, when a CTD-ILD patient experiences acute respiratory deterioration without identifiable risk factors for ARDS, an acute exacerbation is diagnosed; however, standardized diagnostic criteria and management protocols remain lacking [ ]. Acute exacerbations contribute significantly to the poor prognosis and high mortality rates among CTD-ILD patients [ ]. Pathophysiologically, acute exacerbation of interstitial lung disease (AE-ILD) resembles acute lung injury, with histopathological features marked by diffuse alveolar damage superimposed on pre-existing interstitial lung disease. Although the precise incidence of AE-ILD is unclear, a study by Suzuki et al. following 1019 ILD patients over an average of 3.4 years reported 193 cases of acute exacerbation. These patients frequently require ICU admission, with in-hospital mortality rates > 50% and lung infection being the leading cause of death within 3 years after discharge [ ].
Given the specific disease burden and clinical practice environment in China, this scoping review was primarily conducted based on research data involving Chinese patients. It aims to examine current treatment and management strategies for CTD-ILD in this population, with a focus on interventions such as glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, and supportive care. By summarizing available evidence and identifying gaps in clinical practice, this review seeks to provide insights to optimize clinical management strategies for Chinese CTD-ILD patients and inform future research directions.

## Methods

## Search Strategy
We searched three English electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science) and three Chinese electronic databases (CNKI, CBM, and Wanfang). We used variants and combinations of search terms relating to interstitial lung disease with systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases, risk profile, and therapy. We pilot-tested several sets of search terms to identify the most efficient method for locating relevant articles. All databases were searched on February 2025 for entries from 1 January 2000 to 31 December 2024. We also manually searched references of the included publications to identify relevant publications not captured by the electronic search strategies.

## Selection Criteria
The included publications must meet the following criteria: (1) empirical research; (2) the study subjects are patients with any type of CTD-ILD; (3) the study describes or evaluates the risk factors or treatment strategies for acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD; (4) the article is published in either Chinese or English; (5) the article was published between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2024. There are no restrictions on the study design. We excluded the following reports: (1) studies that only discuss other types of lung diseases related to CTD or do not address acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD; (2) studies focusing solely on other types of respiratory diseases; (3) studies that focus on other complications or treatment plans for CTD rather than CTD-ILD or its acute exacerbations; (4) abstracts without the full article.

## Ethical Approval
This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

## Risk Prediction Factors for Acute Exacerbation of CTD-ILD
Clinicians primarily diagnose acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD by assessing the significant deterioration of clinical symptoms, identifying new ground-glass opacities and/or pulmonary consolidation on high-resolution CT; ruling out pneumothorax, pulmonary embolism, pleural effusion, and other conditions; and excluding symptoms caused by factors such as heart failure, fluid overload, and antirheumatic drug toxicity. Additionally, medical staff focus on identifying potential precipitating factors [ , ]. Given the significantly increased mortality rate during acute exacerbations, identifying risk factors for early-stage exacerbations of CTD-ILD remains a key focus of clinical attention (Fig.  ).
 

## Different Types of Connective Tissue Diseases
CTD-ILD is a group of diseases caused by systemic autoimmune diseases that lead to pulmonary interstitial abnormalities or pulmonary fibrosis [ ]. Among these diseases, the overall prevalence of ILD in rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is 11%, in systemic sclerosis (SSc) is 47%, in idiopathic inflammatory myopathy (IIM) is 41%, in primary Sjögren’s syndrome (pSS) is 17%, in mixed connective tissue disease (MCTD) is 56%, and in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is 6%. Interstitial pneumonia is usually the most common type of ILD in rheumatoid arthritis (with an overall prevalence of 46%), while non-specific interstitial pneumonia is the most common type of ILD in all other CTD subtypes (with an overall prevalence ranging from 27 to 76%) [ ]. When ILD occurs, both incidence and mortality increase accordingly [ ] (Table  ). Therefore, early diagnosis is crucial. The prevalence of CTD-ILD varies significantly depending on the underlying primary CTD, with an overall range of 3% to 70% [ ]. ILD caused by different CTDs can manifest in various clinical, radiological, and pathological types, each with its own distinct development patterns and outcomes. The diagnosis and differential diagnosis of CTD-ILD are complex, and treatment plans vary greatly based on individual cases, requiring a multidisciplinary approach involving rheumatology, respiratory medicine, radiology, pathology, and rehabilitation nursing. CTD-ILD is most commonly manifested as one of the two imaging histopathological phenotypes, namely usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) or NSIP. For patients with CTD and confirmed UIP through biopsy, their survival rate is poor [ ]. However, because most CTD-ILD patients do not undergo surgical lung biopsy, other clinical indicators such as lung function and HRCT examination results are often the only available tools for making treatment decisions in such patients in clinical practice. Walsh et al. recently found that radiological diagnosis has predictive significance for survival in CTD-ILD patients with UIP confirmed by biopsy [ ]. Imaging parameters including the severity of traction bronchiectasis, the degree of increase in honeycomb like lesions, and the degree of decrease in Dlco are independent factors associated with increased mortality in CTD-ILD patients. The researchers cleverly confirmed that an increase in the degree of honeycomb-like lesions and the severity of traction bronchiectasis are independently associated with an increase in mortality rate in CTD-ILD patients [ ]. Compared with evaluating honeycomb-like lesions, assessing the absence/presence of traction bronchiectasis in both lungs of patients can provide more useful prognostic information. In addition, for patients diagnosed with UIP through histopathology, there are differences in survival rates between those with or without UIP pattern on HRCT, and the mortality rate was highest in patients who had UIP patterns consistent with HRCT. In summary, composite indices such as CPI can provide a more accurate overall assessment of the severity of ILD patients and their role in comorbidities such as emphysema. Some ILD patients may progress to progressive fibrosing interstitial lung disease (PF-ILD), where lung function deteriorates as the disease progresses, ultimately leading to respiratory failure. This severely impacts the patient's quality of life and may even be life-threatening. Based on domestic and international guidelines and clinical experience, the Rheumatology Branch of the Chinese Medical Association has developed this guideline to assist clinicians in improving diagnostic and treatment practices, with the goals of early diagnosis, early treatment, and better patient outcomes. In most cases of CTD-ILD, the underlying CTD is identified at the time of diagnosis. However, ILD may be the only manifestation of the underlying CTD [ ]. For example, in up to 10% of patients with RA, ILD is the predominant manifestation, while in clinical amyopathic dermatomyositis (CADM), anti-synthetase syndrome, and SSc, ILD may persist as the sole manifestation of CTD [ ]. Therefore, it is essential to assess the subtle and atypical features of CTD based on medical history and clinical examination in all ILD patients. Specific differences exist in the acute exacerbation of CTD-ILD among various types of connective tissue diseases. The incidence of acute exacerbation in CTD-ILD associated with rheumatoid arthritis is relatively high. A study led by Hozumi et al. reported that the 90-day mortality rate for CTD-ILD patients with rheumatoid arthritis during acute exacerbation is as high as 48.3%. Low baseline vital capacity and low oxygenation index during acute exacerbation are independent predictors of mortality [ ]. The incidence of acute exacerbation in CTD-ILD associated with polymyositis/dermatomyositis is 31.9%, while that associated with systemic sclerosis is 11.4% [ ]. Among inflammatory myopathies, the likelihood of patients with amyopathic dermatomyositis developing AE-ILD is 19.4%, higher than in dermatomyositis (10.8%) and polymyositis (5.7%) patients [ ]. In short, the specific immune system disorder mechanisms associated with different connetive tissue diseases may directly influence the pathological process of lung involvement. Some CTD-related molecular markers, such as IL-6 [ ], D-dimer [ ], and KL-6 [ ], may play a key role in the acute exacerbation of CTD-ILD and serve as predictors of poor clinical prognosis (Table  ).
 Additionally, disease activity status affects exacerbations. For instance, rheumatoid arthritis activity not only contributes to the progression of CTD-ILD but may also trigger acute exacerbation. A clinical disease activity index > 10 at 24 weeks in rheumatoid arthritis patients increases the risk of acute exacerbation of CTD-ILD [ ]. Similarly, an increase in the Myositis Disease Activity Assessment Tool (MDAAT) score is closely associated with acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD in patients with idiopathic inflammatory myositis [ ]. Therefore, due to the varying causes of CTD-ILD, factors leading to acute exacerbations may differ and require further clarification.

## Drug Toxicity
CTD-ILD patients have reduced immune function and weakened resistance to pathogens due to the disease’s immune disorder and long-term use of immunosuppressive agents, making them more susceptible to infections. Long-term use of immunosuppressive agents or biologics can suppress the body’s immune function and increase the risk of developing ILD, making ILD a potential complication of CTD treatment. The mechanisms of drug-induced lung injury may involve direct damage to alveolar epithelial cells or capillary endothelial cells, immune system dysregulation, systemic cytokine release, cell-mediated lung injury, and free radical generation caused by oxidative damage. Studies have shown that several drugs used to treat CTDs, such as methotrexate, tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors, penicillamine, leflunomide, and sulfasalazine, can induce ILD. It is currently estimated that the mortality rate associated with methotrexate-induced pneumonitis is approximately 20%. Reports indicate that patients receiving any of the five TNF inhibitors currently approved for the treatment of RA may develop new-onset ILD. The data on leflunomide increasing the risk of ILD remain controversial, while lung disease induced by sulfasalazine and penicillamine is less frequently reported. Additionally, a systematic review of published reports on drug-induced ILD in RA patients found that the overall risk of lung disease caused by biologics is 1%, but the associated mortality rate can be as high as 35% [ ]. Therefore, when developing treatment plans for CTDs, the risks and benefits of the drugs potentially causing ILD should be carefully weighed.

## Infection
In recent years, increasing evidence has suggested that different pathogens play important roles in the pathogenesis of CTD-ILD, but their mechanisms of action are still unclear. A retrospective study in 2020 showed a significant correlation between bacterial infection and ILD associated with idiopathic inflammatory myopathy in terms of short-term acute exacerbation and poor prognosis [ ]. In addition to bacterial infections, prospective observational studies have reported that 19.2% of CTD-ILD patients experience viral infections during acute exacerbation, with human herpesvirus seven and cytomegalovirus being the most common. The 60-day survival rate is significantly lower in patients with viral infections than those without [ ]. Particularly during the global COVID-19 pandemic, infection with the novel coronavirus can cause rapid deterioration of lung function in CTD-ILD patients [ ]. The interaction between COVID-19 caused by SARS-CoV-2 virus and pre-existing ILD induces lung inflammation through immune-mediated or direct damage to destroy lung tissue. Research shows that ILD patients with novel coronavirus infection have a higher risk of severe disease (OR value 1.34–2.73) and a higher risk of death (OR value 1.2–4.3) [ ]. Therefore, preventing or controlling infections is critical in avoiding acute exacerbations in these patients.

## Triggering Factors

## Genetic Factors
Recent studies have identified several genetic mutations associated with an increased risk of CTD-ILD, primarily including well-established telomere-related genes (such as TERT, TERC, RTEL1, PARN, TINF2, NAF1, and DKC1) and the mucin 5B gene (MUC5B), as well as the TOLLIP and HLA-DRB1 genes, which remain to be further clarified. The most commonly implicated genes in CTD-ILD are the telomere-related genes. Telomeres are crucial components at the ends of chromosomes that maintain chromosomal integrity and stability. Loss of function of the telomerase complex may impair the renewal and healing of alveolar epithelial cells in the early stages following injurious stimuli [ ]. A 2017 study showed that the mutation rate of telomere-related genes (including TERT, RTEL1, PARN, and SFTPC) in RA-ILD patients was higher than that in the control group (OR = 3.17), and patients with these mutations had shorter telomeres compared to controls [ ].

## Smoking
Smoking can induce the expression of peptidyl arginine deiminase in alveolar cells, promoting the production of citrullinated proteins, which disrupt the immune tolerance to citrullinated proteins in genetically susceptible individuals, thereby inducing the production of autoantibodies. This leads to pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis [ ]. A recent prospective cohort study also confirmed the association between smoking and ILD in RA patients (OR = 2.484) [ ]. Therefore, smoking cessation is a critical preventive and therapeutic measure for ILD in CTD patients.

## Other Diseases
Studies show that approximately one-third of RA-ILD patients, particularly those of African descent, have concomitant gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) [ ], suggesting a potential pathogenic role of GERD in CTD-ILD. Chronic microaspiration of gastric contents, such as gastric acid, bile, and pepsin, leads to repetitive direct damage to the pulmonary epithelial cells, which over time can result in pneumonia, increased epithelial permeability, and fibrotic proliferation.

## Environmental Factors
Long-term exposure to air pollution, particularly ozone and nitrogen dioxide, may trigger acute exacerbations of ILD [ ]. However, a recent case-control study analyzing the association between air pollutants and CTD-ILD found that ozone exposure was associated with a reduced risk of ILD in CTD patients (OR = 0.51 after adjusting for confounding factors) [ ].

## Outcomes of Acute Exacerbation in CTD-ILD
Patients with acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD often present with progressive dyspnea and hypoxemia. Hypoxemia is a key phenotype of acute exacerbation and is closely associated with disease severity and prognosis [ ]. The development of hypoxemia is primarily due to diffuse lung injury superimposed on the underlying interstitial lung disease, leading to systemic hypoxia. Consequently, monitoring arterial oxygen partial pressure (PaO₂) and carbon dioxide partial pressure (PaCO₂) is crucial for assessing disease severity. Studies have shown that PaO₂/FiO₂ ratios are lower in CTD-ILD patients who die from acute exacerbation, often accompanied by type II respiratory failure. Patients experiencing acute exacerbation typically have severe hypoxemia and may require mechanical ventilation for respiratory support [ ]. However, studies indicate that the in-hospital mortality rate for CTD-ILD patients with acute exacerbation requiring mechanical ventilation can range from 76 to 100% [ ]. In addition to the severity of the condition, some believe the high mortality rate may also be linked to the choice of pulmonary ventilation strategy. Research has demonstrated that the mortality rate for patients receiving non-invasive mechanical ventilation is significantly lower than for those undergoing invasive ventilation [ ]. Non-invasive ventilation can avoid tracheal intubation and ventilator-associated pneumonia, making it particularly suitable for CTD-ILD patients with acute exacerbation, a history of immunosuppressant use, and ineffective nasal oxygen therapy. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) can be used for CTD-ILD patients with acute exacerbation who have potentially reversible causes of severe respiratory failure or for those awaiting lung transplantation [ ]. For critically ill CTD-ILD patients at high risk of death who meet lung transplantation criteria, invasive mechanical ventilation and ECMO serve as effective bridging strategies [ ]. Therefore, hypoxemia is an important clinical prognostic indicator for CTD-ILD patients with acute exacerbation, reflecting disease severity and the effectiveness of clinical interventions.

## Treatment Strategies for Acute Exacerbation of CTD-ILD

## Treatment Targeting Precipitating Factors
The first step in managing acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD is identifying and addressing potential precipitating factors. Tailored treatment for these factors is essential to achieve the best outcomes. Infection is a common trigger, and broad-spectrum antibiotics should cover typical and atypical bacterial pathogens [ ]. Additionally, clinicians should consider the possibility of concurrent infections, including viral, Pneumocystis jirovecii, and fungal infections, and initiate appropriate treatment accordingly [ ]. In cases of suspected drug toxicity, discontinuing or reducing the use of offending drugs, such as antirheumatic medications, is essential [ ]. Research has shown that gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) may increase the risk of acute exacerbation in CTD-ILD patients, and anti-reflux treatment should be considered as part of the management plan [ ]. Exposure to environmental air pollutants, mainly ozone and nitrogen dioxide, may also contribute to acute exacerbations of ILD [ ].

## Glucocorticoids
Glucocorticoids play a crucial role in managing acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD, but their use remains controversial. Current practice largely relies on management strategies developed for acute exacerbations of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), although clear evidence supporting glucocorticoid use in CTD-ILD is lacking. Clinical guidelines weakly recommend systemic corticosteroids for acute exacerbations of IPF but do not specify the most appropriate type, dosage, or duration of treatment [ ]. A retrospective study found that glucocorticoid use did not improve treatment outcomes in patients with acute exacerbations of IPF [ ]. However, many CTD-ILD patients are already receiving corticosteroid treatment upon admission for acute exacerbation, but the clinical effectiveness of this treatment still requires further evaluation [ , ]. Therefore, when managing acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD, glucocorticoid indications should be carefully assessed, and treatment plans should be tailored to individual patient conditions and responses.

## Immunosuppressants
Immunosuppressants show potential in treating acute exacerbation of CTD-ILD; however, further research is needed to determine the optimal dosage, route of administration, and treatment duration (Table  ).
 Studies have reported that using cyclophosphamide in the acute exacerbation of CTD-ILD is associated with improved survival rates [ ]. Liu et al. demonstrated that combining cyclophosphamide with prednisone acetate to treat CTD-ILD significantly improves lung function and radiological findings, showing more notable effects compared to prednisone alone, without increasing adverse reactions and with good patient tolerance. Additionally, changes in serum KL-6 and 25(OH)D levels are associated with CTD-ILD, and these markers can reflect disease progression. They may serve as useful serological markers for diagnosing CTD-ILD and assessing treatment effectiveness and disease activity.
Rituximab (RTX) is a monoclonal antibody targeting the CD20 antigen on B cells, and it has been used to treat acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD, especially when other treatments are ineffective [ ]. The therapeutic effects of RTX vary across different CTD-ILD patients. Approximately 80% of PM/DM/CADM-ILD patients have certain specific types of MSAs. Especially anti-aminoacyl tRNA synthetase (ARs) antibodies and anti-melanoma differentiation associated gene-5 (MDA5) antibodies are common in PM/DM/CADM-ILD patients. Xu and Wang’s research indicated that patients with IIM-CTD (non-ASyS) or ASyS-ILD respond better to RTX than other CTD-ILD patients [ ]. Regarding side effects, most patients tolerate RTX well. Another study showed that both RTX and cyclophosphamide can improve forced vital capacity (FVC) and quality of life in CTD-ILD patients. Compared to cyclophosphamide, RTX treatment is associated with fewer adverse events and less corticosteroid exposure [ ]. For CTD-ILD patients requiring intravenous treatment, RTX should be considered an alternative to cyclophosphamide. Therefore, RTX should be considered a treatment option for severe or rapidly progressive CTD-ILD patients [ ]. Furthermore, several studies have confirmed that RTX has therapeutic effects on pSS-ILD. In a cohort study from the French AIR registry, among 78 pSS patients, 8 had concomitant ILD. All eight pSS-ILD patients were treated with RTX, of whom six showed improvements in lung function and systemic symptoms, while two had stable conditions. Additionally, belimumab, a B-cell activation factor inhibitor used for treating systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), has also shown potential in improving the exercise capacity and lung function in CTD-ILD patients [ ].
Tofacitinib, a selective tyrosine kinase inhibitor, has shown efficacy in reducing flare-ups in juvenile idiopathic arthritis and improving clinical symptoms, and it also shows potential for improving lung function in some CTD-ILD patients [ ]. Considering a JAK inhibitor, Chen et al. studied 18 MDA5-positive CADM-ILD patients treated with glucocorticoids combined with tofacitinib (5 mg, twice daily) and used 32 clinically and epidemiologically matched patients receiving conventional therapy as historical controls. The 6-month cumulative survival rate in the tofacitinib group was significantly higher than that in the baseline-matched historical control group (100% vs. 78%, P = 0.0363). The study suggests that tofacitinib may significantly improve the survival of MDA5-positive CADM-ILD patients in the early stages of disease progression. Based on the clear efficacy of JAK-targeted small molecule drugs in autoimmune diseases, they provide great potential for the treatment of CTD-ILD.
Abatacept, a soluble fusion protein used for the treatment of idiopathic arthritis, has shown potential in reducing the risk of ILD exacerbation in RA-ILD patients [ ]. Fernández-Díaz et al. studied 63 RA-ILD patients who received abatacept treatment and followed them for 9.4 ± 3.2 months. Among them, 67% of patients had stable FVC, 20% showed improvement in FVC, 67% had stable DLCO, and 25% showed improvement. The efficacy of abatacept in RA-ILD treatment is receiving increasing attention. The research results show that long-term use of abazil can significantly reduce KL-6 levels in RA patients with ILD, thereby delaying the progression of ILD. In addition, the progression rate of ILD in the abazil treatment group was significantly lower than that in the control group, further confirming the safety of abazil in RA patients with ILD [ ]. Currently, there are limited cases of abatacept used in CTD-ILD, and no large-scale clinical trials have been conducted, so its real efficacy and side effects remain unclear.
Anti-fibrotic drugs, such as pirfenidone and nintedanib, have also shown potential efficacy in the treatment of CTD-ILD, particularly in delaying the deterioration of lung function [ , ]. Experts suggest that pirfenidone combined with immunosuppressants could be an effective treatment for CTD-ILD, although the efficacy varies between subtypes. FVC% significantly improves in SSc and IIM patients, particularly those with SSc-uip and IIM-non-uip. In RA, subgroups with non-uip and lower baseline DLCO% benefit the most from pirfenidone therapy [ ]. This indicates that pirfenidone combined with immunosuppressants is a suitable choice for SSc, IIM, and RA patients [ ]. In the INBULD trial, nintedanib slowed down the decline of FVC in patients with progressive fibrosis RA-ILD, and the adverse events were generally controllable. The efficacy and safety of nidanib in these patients are consistent with the overall trial population [ ].
In conclusion, immunosuppressants have potential value in treating acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD, but their optimal dosage, route, and duration still require further research. Treatment choices should be based on the specific situation of individual patients and their response to initial therapy. More research is needed to provide evidence for the role of immunosuppressants in the treatment of acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD.

## Supportive Care
The primary objectives of supportive care in CTD-ILD are to alleviate symptoms, correct hypoxia, maintain electrolyte and acid-base balance, and reduce mortality following acute exacerbation. Given that nearly all patients with acute exacerbation of CTD-ILD experience varying degrees of hypoxia, respiratory support is a critical aspect of treatment. This may include nasal catheter or mask oxygen or high-flow nasal cannula oxygen therapy; in severe cases, mechanical ventilation or ECMO may be required [ ]. Studies have shown that direct hemoperfusion with polymyxin B can adsorb inflammatory mediators, such as endotoxin, TNF-α, and IL-6, and remove inflammatory cells, which may benefit patients with acute exacerbation of CTD-ILD [ ]. For diffuse alveolar hemorrhage, when conservative treatment and coagulation correction fail, plasma exchange has been successfully used in clinical practice [ ]. Lung transplantation is indicated for CTD-ILD patients who meet the criteria, improving quality of life and increasing 5-year survival rates [ ]. Long-term management of CTD-ILD includes regular follow-up, medication adherence, prevention of acute exacerbations, rehabilitation, oxygen therapy, treatment of gastroesophageal reflux, smoking cessation, vaccination, and infection prevention. Additionally, professional life and psychological counseling are recommended to enhance the quality of life.

## Discussion
CTD-ILD is a common pulmonary complication caused by connective tissue diseases. Acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD that lead to respiratory failure severely threaten patients’ health and life, making effective prevention and treatment a clinical focus. The risk factors for acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD include various types of connective tissue diseases, infections, and other factors that ultimately lead to dyspnea and hypoxemia. Identifying and treating possible triggers, such as infections and drug toxicity, is the first step in treatment. Acute exacerbation, as an important malignant event related to the outcomes of CTD-ILD, is of concern due to its high mortality rate and significant medical burden. Although there is a lack of consensus or guidelines specifically for CTD-ILD, valuable progress has been made in understanding and treating these patients under the guidance of AE-IPF official consensus or guidelines, which greatly benefit clinicians. However, many issues remain unresolved. For example, there is limited reporting on indicators for the progression of CTD-ILD. If easily accessible and cost-effective indicators such as cytokines, antibodies, and inflammatory mediators are incorporated into the diagnosis of CTD-ILD, it may improve the assessment of interstitial lung disease in CTD-ILD patients and facilitate earlier disease management. This would not only enhance the prevention and treatment rates of CTD-ILD by clinicians but also broaden the diagnostic approach to CTD-ILD.
Additionally, in terms of treatment, glucocorticoids still play an important role in the treatment of acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD, but their optimal dosage and duration remain unclear. While immunosuppressants show potential value in treating acute exacerbations of CTD-ILD, further research is needed to determine the best treatment regimen. In conclusion, the pathogenesis of CTD-ILD is not yet fully understood, and multiple cytokines and inflammatory mediators are involved in its development. A single biologic agent may not be sufficient to treat all CTD-ILD cases, as the onset and progression of CTD-ILD may be triggered by various factors, including medications, infections, and allergic responses. Whether biologic agents can be used in these patients, or how to exclude these factors before using biologics, remains one of the future directions for further research and clinical studies. Moreover, the appropriate dosage, duration, safety, resistance, and long-term clinical efficacy of biologic treatments still require further investigation. With the ongoing development of various biologic drugs and deeper research into the pathogenesis of CTD-ILD, the treatment and prognosis of CTD-ILD will reach new heights.

## Future Prospects
In recent years, the treatment methods for CTD-ILD have been continuously enriched, and the concept of comprehensive treatment has gradually been established. However, there are still many challenges in the management of refractory cases and acute exacerbations, and it is urgent to explore more precise and efficient intervention methods. Future research directions can focus on the following aspects. First, chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) therapy, as an important means of immune cell therapy, has shown good efficacy in hematological malignancies and some autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus [ , ]. CAR-T cells can specifically clear pathogenic B cell subsets, reshape abnormal immune microenvironments, and have potential regulatory effects on immune-mediated alveolar inflammation and fibrosis processes. Preliminary animal experiments and case reports suggest that CAR-T has certain therapeutic potential in autoimmune pulmonary fibrosis, especially for patients who have failed conventional immunosuppression or have repeatedly worsened [ ]. In the future, multicenter, prospective clinical studies are needed to systematically evaluate the safety, efficacy, and optimal timing of CAR-T in CTD-ILD.
In addition, inhalation therapy has the advantage of local drug delivery, which can effectively increase drug concentration in lung tissue and reduce systemic adverse reactions. In recent years, it has gradually been applied in the field of interstitial lung disease [ ]. Existing studies have shown that inhaled anti-fibrotic drugs, such as inhaled nintedanib and pirfenidone, can significantly inhibit the progression of pulmonary fibrosis in animal models. Meanwhile, novel delivery systems such as nanoparticles, liposomes, and microspheres are expected to further enhance drug retention time and targeting in the lungs [ ]. In addition, the development of inhaled biologics and interfering RNA drugs also has prospects, such as inhaled anti-IL-6, anti-TGF-β monoclonal antibodies, or siRNA inhibition of key fibrosis related gene expression [ ]. In the future, based on an animal model of acute exacerbation of ILD, a systematic evaluation of the pharmacokinetic characteristics, safety, and combination immunosuppressive therapy of inhalation therapy can be conducted to provide a basis for clinical translation.
Also, imaging examinations play an irreplaceable role in the diagnosis, evaluation, and efficacy monitoring of CTD-ILD. Lung ultrasound (LUS), as a non-invasive, bedside, and real-time feasible technique, has been increasingly used in ILD management in recent years. By detecting indicators such as the number of B-lines and changes in pleural lines, LUS can dynamically reflect the degree of pulmonary interstitial edema and fibrosis, which is helpful for real-time monitoring of disease changes and evaluation of treatment response [ ]. Future research can explore the correlation between LUS features and HRCT manifestations and prognosis, construct an ILD clinical scoring system based on LUS, optimize HRCT examination indications, and reduce unnecessary radiation exposure [ ]. At the same time, the application of artificial intelligence and radiomics technology is expected to achieve early warning, disease progression prediction, and quantitative assessment of fibrosis burden in CTD-ILD, helping to formulate personalized diagnosis and treatment strategies.

## Limitations
The findings of this study are primarily based on data from Chinese patients and therefore may not be fully generalizable to patient populations in other countries or regions. The conclusions drawn from the data may be influenced by regional, cultural, and healthcare system differences, limiting the external validity of the results. Consequently, future studies should consider incorporating diverse datasets from different geographical regions to validate the findings and enhance the generalizability of the conclusions.

## Conclusion
In summary, the treatment management of CTD-ILD in the future will present a trend towards multi-target, precision, minimally invasive, and intelligent approaches. The comprehensive application of CAR-T cell therapy, inhaled anti-fibrotic drugs and biologics, and pulmonary ultrasound combined with artificial intelligence-assisted monitoring methods is expected to improve the prognosis of refractory and acute exacerbation ILD patients and enhance clinical management level. Multicenter, large sample, prospective clinical research, and basic translational research will be the key development directions in the future.
