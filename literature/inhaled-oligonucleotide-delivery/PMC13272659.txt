## Abstract
Proteolytic stress frequently arises during disease and aging, particularly in long-lived, post-mitotic cells such as cardiomyocytes. To maintain proteostasis, cardiomyocytes depend on coordinated protein quality control pathways, including the ubiquitin-proteasome system and autophagy. Mechanisms that activate these pathways hold therapeutic potential for heart disease. Here, we demonstrate that transient activation of nuclear factor erythroid 2–like 1 (Nfe2l1, also known as Nrf1), a transcriptional regulator of proteasome activity, in cardiomyocytes during ischemia/reperfusion injury improves cardiac function. In addition to regulating the proteasome, we identify a critical role for Nrf1 in activating autophagy, which is essential for its cardioprotective effects. Through multi-omics analyses, we define both transcriptional and post-transcriptional functions of Nrf1 that underlie its cardioprotective activity. Loss-of-function studies in mice demonstrate that Nrf1, but not its homolog Nrf2, is required for autophagy and baseline cardiac function. Together, our findings establish a dual function of Nrf1 in promoting cardiac proteostasis by regulating both proteasomal and autophagic protein quality control pathways. Activating Nrf1 thus offers a therapeutic strategy for treating ischemic heart disease.

## Introduction
Maintaining proteostasis is essential for cellular and tissue health and relies on two major protein quality control systems: the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), which rapidly degrades misfolded proteins , and autophagy, which clears larger protein aggregates and damaged organelles through lysosomal degradation . Inadequate activity of these pathways leads to proteolytic stress that disrupts cellular homeostasis and drives maladaptive tissue remodeling and dysfunction , . In the heart, long-lived cardiomyocytes have limited regenerative capacity and operate under continuous mechanical and metabolic demands, making them highly vulnerable to proteolytic imbalance, especially during disease and aging – . Under these conditions, the accumulation of misfolded, aberrantly modified, or oxidized proteins imposes a significant burden on protein quality control systems. Enhancing mechanisms that preserve proteostasis holds the potential as a therapeutic strategy to improve outcomes of heart diseases.
Nrf1 (encoded by Nfe2l1) is a cap-‘n’-collar (CNC) basic leucine zipper transcription factor that regulates stress-adaptive responses to maintain cellular homeostasis , . Under basal conditions, Nrf1 is localized to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane and undergoes rapid degradation via the ER-associated degradation (ERAD) pathway , . Upon cellular stress, Nrf1 escapes from degradation and is cleaved by the protease DDI2 and translocates to the nucleus , where it binds antioxidant response elements (AREs) in the promoters of proteasome subunit and antioxidant genes and activates their expression , thereby increasing the ability of cells to combat oxidative and proteolytic stress. Unlike its paralog Nrf2, which primarily regulates antioxidant gene expression, Nrf1 plays a unique and indispensable role in regulating the de novo synthesis of proteasome subunits. This proteasome regulatory function of Nrf1 has been shown to confer protective effects in models of neurodegenerative diseases , , ischemic heart injury , and obesity . While Nrf1 is well recognized as a master regulator of protein homeostasis, its role in other protein quality control pathways, such as autophagy, is less understood. Emerging evidence indicates that Nrf1 can activate autophagy when proteasomal activity is inhibited as a compensatory mechanism to maintain protein homeostasis , , suggesting a dual function in coordinating both major arms of protein quality control. However, whether Nrf1 regulates autophagy in the heart under physiological or pathological conditions remains unknown.
Here, using gain- and loss-of-function mouse models as well as human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), we demonstrate that Nrf1 is both necessary and sufficient to regulate autophagy gene expression in cardiomyocytes. Transient overexpression of Nrf1 in cardiomyocytes via adeno-associated virus (AAV) during cardiac ischemia/reperfusion injury (I/R) injury elicited a protective effect and improved heart function. Conversely, pharmacological and genetic inhibition of autophagy attenuated the cytoprotective effects of Nrf1 under H₂O₂-induced stress. Nrf1 activation triggered broad transcriptomic and proteomic responses, including direct transcriptional activation of proteasomal and autophagy genes and attenuation of stress-induced increase of proteins involved in pathological remodeling. Collectively, our study uncovers a previously unrecognized role of Nrf1 in regulating autophagy in the heart and establishes it as a central coordinator of cardiac proteostasis, acting through both UPS and autophagy pathways. These findings highlight Nrf1 as a potential therapeutic target for promoting heart repair.

## Result

## Transient overexpression of Nrf1 protects adult hearts against ischemia/reperfusion injury
We engineered a cardiac-specific inducible AAV9 vector (iAAV) that enables temporally controlled gene overexpression. This system incorporates a bidirectional regulatory cassette, in which the reverse tetracycline transactivator (rtTA) is driven by the cardiomyocyte-specific cTnT promoter , and the gene of interest is under the control of a tetracycline-responsive element (TRE) (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). As a result, gene expression is specifically induced in cardiomyocytes upon doxycycline (Dox) administration. To test the efficacy of this system, we generated GFP-expressing iAAV (iAAV-GFP). We administered iAAV-GFP into neonatal mice at postnatal day 2 (P2) via subcutaneous injection. At 8 weeks of age, GFP expression was induced by two Dox injections, followed by Dox-supplemented drinking water for four days, and was subsequently detected specifically in cardiomyocytes of mice that received both iAAV and Dox (Fig.  ). To assess the temporal dynamics of gene induction, we collected hearts at various time points before, during, and after Dox treatment. GFP expression was detectable 1 day after Dox administration, increased by day 4, and plateaued by day 8 (Supplementary Fig.  ). Upon Dox withdrawal, GFP levels decreased within two days, returning to baseline levels comparable to no-Dox controls (Supplementary Fig.  ), confirming the Dox-dependent and reversible activation of the iAAV system.
 We next generated an iAAV construct to overexpress Nrf1 fused with an HA tag (iAAV-Nrf1-HA) and examined its effect on adult heart repair in an I/R injury model. While our previous study showed that Nrf1 overexpression confers cardioprotection by improving cardiomyocyte survival after ischemic injury , sustained activation may carry long-term risks by persistently elevating proteasome activity. Indeed, chronic activation of Nrf1 and its downstream proteasome pathway has been implicated in skeletal muscle atrophy . Therefore, we tested whether temporally activating Nrf1 around the time of injury would enhance its therapeutic benefit by minimizing potential adverse effects. To this end, we treated 12-week-old mice injected with either iAAV-Nrf1-HA or control iAAV-GFP at P2 with Dox starting 6 days prior to I/R or sham surgery, and continued Dox treatment until 1 day post-surgery (Fig.  ). Successful induction of GFP and Nrf1 expression was confirmed in Dox-treated hearts (Fig.  ). At baseline, heart function measured by echocardiography was similar between iAAV-GFP and iAAV-Nrf1-HA animals (Fig.  ). However, iAAV-Nrf1-HA mice exhibited significantly increased cardiac ejection fraction and reduced left ventricular end-diastolic volume compared to iAAV-GFP controls at 7, 14, and 21 days post-injury (Fig.  ). These mice also showed marked reduction in myocardial fibrosis (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ) and cardiomyocyte hypertrophy (Fig.  ). These results demonstrate that transient Nrf1 overexpression during ischemic injury provides significant cardioprotective benefits. Notably, the functional improvement achieved using the iAAV system was significantly greater than that observed with our previous constitutive Nrf1 overexpression approach (Supplementary Fig.  ), highlighting the importance of temporal control in activating stress-adaptive factors like Nrf1 for therapeutic benefit.

## Nrf1 coordinates transcriptional and proteomic remodeling in cardiomyocytes under oxidative stress
Given the dual role of Nrf1 in transcriptional regulation and protein turnover, we next investigated the global effects of Nrf1 activation on the transcriptome and proteome of cardiomyocytes to elucidate the mechanisms underlying its cardioprotective function. We previously showed that Nrf1 overexpression protects murine cardiomyocytes from H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress , . We thus performed RNA sequencing and tandem mass tag (TMT) proteomics on primary murine cardiomyocytes overexpressing either Nrf1 or LacZ (control), with and without H₂O₂ treatment (Supplementary Fig.  , Supplementary Data  , ). As a first step, we examined the effect of H₂O₂ on the transcriptome of control cardiomyocytes. H₂O₂ treatment upregulated genes involved in leukocyte differentiation, cytokine production, and inflammatory responses, indicating that oxidative stress activates immune-related pathways in cardiomyocytes (Supplementary Fig.  ). In contrast, genes downregulated by H₂O₂ treatment were enriched for pathways related to action potential generation, actin filament–based processes, cardiac contraction, and calcium signaling (Supplementary Fig.  ), suggesting that oxidative stress suppresses gene programs essential for contractile function. We then assessed the effect of Nrf1 overexpression in modulating these transcriptomic responses. Notably, Nrf1 restored the expression of both H₂O₂-upregulated and downregulated genes to near-baseline, untreated levels (Supplementary Fig.  ), indicating that Nrf1 overexpression attenuates the broad transcriptomic dysregulation in cardiomyocytes induced by oxidative stress.
To delineate the specific contribution of Nrf1 to proteome regulation that occurs independently of transcriptional changes, we compared Nrf1-induced alterations in protein abundance with corresponding changes in RNA levels in H₂O₂-treated cardiomyocytes. This analysis identified 1,192 genes with significant changes at either the RNA or protein level, or both, in Nrf1-overexpressing cells compared to control cells (Fig.  , Supplementary Data  ). Among these, 114 genes were significantly downregulated at the protein level without corresponding changes in RNA expression. These genes were enriched in pathways related to supramolecular fiber organization, integrin signaling, and microtubule cytoskeleton organization, and included cardiac stress marker Ankrd1 as well as genes involved in cardiac hypertrophy, such as Camk2d and Pdlim5 (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). In contrast, only 26 genes were upregulated at the protein level without significant changes in RNA, and they include mitochondrial-related genes such as Atp5if1, Mtch2, and Tmem223 (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Genes upregulated at both RNA and protein levels were enriched for pathways associated with proteasome function and autophagy (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ), indicating a coordinated transcriptional upregulation of protein turnover mechanisms. Conversely, genes downregulated at both RNA and protein levels were associated with collagen fiber organization and oxidative stress responses (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). These results demonstrate the ability of Nrf1 to differentially regulate the proteomic and transcriptomic landscape in cardiomyocytes and underscore the importance of understanding proteome-level responses that underlie its cardioprotective effects.
 To directly study the impact of Nrf1 overexpression in mitigating H₂O₂-induced proteomic changes, we identified proteins significantly affected by H₂O₂ treatment and focused on those that were rescued by Nrf1 overexpression. This analysis revealed 67 proteins that were upregulated by H₂O₂ in control cells but were partially restored toward baseline levels in Nrf1-overexpressing cells (Fig.  ). These proteins were enriched in pathways related to immune activation (e.g., Stat3, Map2k3), cell death, and cardiac stress and hypertrophy (e.g., Camk2d, Ankrd1, Nppa). Notably, most of these were not transcriptionally altered by Nrf1 overexpression (Nrf1 versus LacZ) (Fig.  ), suggesting that Nrf1 acts post-transcriptionally to attenuate stress-induced pathological remodeling and inflammation. Western blot confirmed that Nppa, a cardiac stress marker, was strongly induced by H₂O₂ in control cells but significantly reduced by Nrf1 overexpression (Supplementary Fig.  ). Conversely, we identified 27 proteins that were downregulated by H₂O₂ but significantly restored by Nrf1 overexpression (Fig.  ). In contrast to the previous group, most of these proteins were transcriptionally regulated by Nrf1 and enriched for pathways involved in proteasome function (e.g., proteasome subunit genes) and autophagy (e.g., Cttn, Sqstm1) (Fig.  ). These findings suggest that oxidative stress causes a protein level reduction of essential regulators of proteasomal and autophagic pathways in cardiomyocytes, and that Nrf1 overexpression partially recuse these protein quality control pathways through transcriptional activation.

## Nrf1 regulates the transcription of autophagy genes in cardiomyocytes
Our data point to a role of Nrf1 in coordinating both proteasomal and autophagic pathways in cardiomyocytes under oxidative stress. While the proteasomal regulatory role of Nrf1 is well established, its contribution to autophagy has only recently been recognized and remains uncharacterized in the heart , . To investigate whether Nrf1 regulates autophagy in cardiomyocytes, we first validated autophagy gene expression in primary mouse cardiomyocytes overexpressing Nrf1. Sqstm1, also known as p62, functions as a selective autophagy adaptor that binds ubiquitinated cargo, facilitating the autophagic degradation of damaged or misfolded proteins . Our proteomic data showed that the Sqstm1 protein level decreased significantly in LacZ-overexpressing control cells after H₂O₂ treatment, whereas this reduction was markedly rescued by Nrf1 overexpression (Supplementary Fig.  ). In fact, Nrf1 overexpression increased Sqstm1 at both the protein and RNA levels regardless of H2O2 treatment (Supplementary Fig.  ), validated by RT-PCR and Western blotting (Fig.  ), suggesting that it is a direct transcriptional target of Nrf1. Nrf1 overexpression also upregulated additional core autophagy genes, including Gabarapl1 (a member of the Atg8 family that facilitates cargo recognition and autophagosome maturation), Tmem41b (essential for autophagosome formation), and Vcp (also known as p97, an ATPase regulates the extraction of misfolded proteins from the ER for degradation) (Fig.  ). Sqstm1 and Gabarapl1 expression was also upregulated in iAAV-Nrf1 treated hearts (Supplementary Fig.  ), confirming the role of Nrf1 in activating autophagy gene expression.
 To determine whether Nrf1 directly regulates the transcription of autophagy-related genes, we performed reporter assays using promoter regions of Sqstm1, Gabarapl1, and Vcp. These promoter regions were selected based on the presence of the active histone mark H3K27ac from our previous ChIP-seq data in cardiomyocytes and putative Nrf1-binding motifs (Fig.  ). Overexpression of Nrf1 significantly increased the reporter activity from wild-type (WT) promoters of Sqstm1 and Gabarapl1 compared to LacZ controls, suggesting their transcriptional activation by Nrf1 (Fig.  ). Although Nrf1 overexpression did not significantly increase Vcp promoter activity, the promoter exhibited high basal activity that may mask its responsiveness to further Nrf1 overexpression (Fig.  ). To assess whether this transcriptional activation is dependent on Nrf1 binding, we generated mutant promoter constructs in which putative Nrf1-binding sites were deleted. Mutation of these motifs significantly reduced promoter activity in Nrf1-overexpressing cells across all three genes (Fig.  ), demonstrating that Nrf1 binding is required for their transcriptional activation. Notably, deletion of Nrf1 motifs also reduced basal promoter activity for Gabarapl1 and Vcp, indicating that endogenous Nrf1 contributes to their baseline expression. These results support that Nrf1 directly binds and activates autophagy gene promoters, thereby establishing it as a transcriptional regulator of autophagy.
We next asked whether the Nrf1-dependent upregulation of autophagy genes increases autophagic flux in cardiomyocytes. LC3B (microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 beta) is essential for autophagy response and is a common marker for autophagy activity . LC3B is synthesized as pro-LC3B, which is cleaved to form LC3B-I, and then lipidated to generate LC3B-II . The lipidated LC3B-II associates with autophagosome membranes and is ultimately degraded when autophagosome fuses with lysosome . As such, LC3B-II level increases either when autophagy is activated (greater LCB3I to LC3B-II processing) or when lysosomal degradation is blocked (accumulation of LC3B-II). However, if LC3B-II level further increases when the lysosomal activity is inhibited, it would demonstrate that there is an increase in autophagy flux . We treated cardiomyocytes overexpressing LacZ control or Nrf1 with or without chloroquine (CQ), an inhibitor of autophagosome fusion. Analysis of LC3B-I and LC3B-II by Western blot showed that Nrf1 overexpression markedly increased both LC3BI and LC3BII compared with LacZ control in untreated cells (Fig.  ). Notably, this increase was not due to transcriptional upregulation of LC3B, as the mRNA level of its coding gene Map1lc3b was not affected by Nrf1 overexpression (Supplementary Fig.  ). Importantly, CQ treatment further increased LC3B-II in Nrf1-overexpressing cells compared to untreated controls (Fig.  ), suggesting that Nrf1 overexpression increases autophagic flux in cardiomyocytes.
Mitophagy is a specialized form of autophagy in which damaged or depolarized mitochondria are ubiquitinated by the E3 ligase Parkin, marking them for autophagosome recruitment and subsequent lysosomal degradation , . To determine whether Nrf1 overexpression enhances mitophagy, we expressed a Parkin-GFP reporter in cardiomyocytes and treated the cells with FCCP, a mitochondrial uncoupler that induces acute depolarization and activation of mitophagy . As expected, FCCP treatment increased the percentage of cells showing Parkin-GFP puncta colocalized with the mitochondrial outer-membrane protein Tom20 in both LacZ- and Nrf1-expressing cells (Fig.  ). Notably, this percentage was significantly higher in cardiomyocytes overexpressing Nrf1, indicating that Nrf1 enhances the recruitment of Parkin to damaged mitochondria (Fig.  ), a key initiation step in mitophagy. Consistent with increased mitophagy activity, Tom20 immunofluorescence intensity was reduced in Nrf1-overexpressing cells following FCCP treatment (Fig.  ), reflecting loss of mitochondria undergoing degradation. Importantly, co-treatment with CQ rescued Tom20 levels (Fig.  ), confirming that the decreased Tom20 signal by Nrf1 overexpression results from accelerated mitophagy flux, rather than reduced mitochondrial biogenesis. These results demonstrates that Nrf1 overexpression augments mitochondrial turnover under stress.

## Nrf1 is required for autophagy gene expression and heart function
We next investigated whether Nrf1 is required for maintaining autophagy gene expression in cardiomyocytes in vivo. To this end, we generated cardiomyocyte-specific Nrf1 knockout (Nrf1 cKO) mice by crossing αMHCCre transgenic mice with Nrf1 floxed mice , . Nrf1 shares transcriptional target genes with its homolog Nrf2 , , which is also expressed in adult cardiomyocytes, albeit at much lower levels (Supplementary Fig.  ). To assess potential functional redundancy, we also generated cardiomyocyte-specific Nrf2 knockout (Nrf2 cKO) and Nrf1/Nrf2 double knockout (dKO) mice. At 2 months of age, both Nrf1 cKO and Nrf2 cKO mice were viable and exhibited normal cardiac function comparable to WT controls (Supplementary Fig.  ). In contrast, dKO mice showed significant cardiac dysfunction, including reduced cardiac ejection fraction and fractional shortening, as well as increased diastolic and systolic ventricular volumes (Supplementary Fig.  ). Interestingly, while Nrf1 cKO mice maintained normal heart function at 2 months, by 4 months they developed reduced ejection fraction and fractional shortening as well as increased systolic ventricular volume (Supplementary Fig.  ). Nrf2 cKO mice, however, continued to exhibit normal cardiac function at 4 months (Supplementary Fig.  ).
At 4 months, dKO mice developed cardiac hypertrophy, evidenced by elevated heart weight–to–body weight (HW/BW) ratios (Supplementary Fig.  ) and increased cardiomyocyte cross-sectional area compared to controls (Supplementary Fig.  ). Although Nrf1 cKO hearts also exhibited increased cardiomyocyte cross-sectional areas, their HW/BW ratios were not significantly altered (Supplementary Fig.  ). Transcript levels of autophagy-related genes, including Sqstm1, Tmem41b, and Vcp, were significantly downregulated in both Nrf1 cKO and dKO hearts (Fig.  ), indicating impaired autophagy. Notably, autophagy gene expression was not further reduced in dKO hearts compared to single Nrf1 cKO hearts (Fig.  ), suggesting that Nrf2 does not compensate for the loss of Nrf1 in regulating autophagy. Additionally, Nrf2 transcript levels were not significantly changed in Nrf1-deleted cardiomyocytes and were instead reduced upon Nrf1 overexpression (Supplementary Fig.  ), further indicating that Nrf1 regulates autophagy gene expression independently of Nrf2. These results demonstrate that Nrf1 is required for normal cardiac physiology and function and plays an essential role in regulating autophagy gene expression in the heart.

## Autophagy activity is required for the cardioprotective function of Nrf1
To assess whether the autophagy regulatory function of Nrf1 is required for its cytoprotective effects, we treated cardiomyocytes with the autophagy inhibitor CQ and examined its impact on Nrf1-mediated protection during H₂O₂-induced stress. Cardiomyocyte survival was assessed using Calcein-AM live cell staining as well as cytotoxicity analysis by measuring lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release from damaged cells. As expected, Nrf1 overexpression enhanced cardiomyocyte survival after H₂O₂ treatment (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). However, CQ treatment significantly attenuated the protective effect of Nrf1 overexpression during H₂O₂ treatment, as seen by reduced percentage of live cells and increased LDH release (Nrf1 + H₂O₂ + CQ v.s. Nrf1 + H₂O₂, Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). We observed a moderate increase in cytotoxicity following CQ treatment alone in LacZ cells (Supplementary Fig.  ), but not in Nrf1-overexpressing cells, supporting the cytoprotective effect of Nrf1 against proteolytic stress. We additionally treated cardiomyocytes with bafilomycin A1 (BafA1), a V-ATPase inhibitor that blocks autophagosome–lysosome fusion and lysosomal degradation, and is a more specific inhibitor of autophagy . Similar to CQ, BafA1 abrogated the protective effect of Nrf1 overexpression in H₂O₂-treated cells, as seen by reduced cell survival compared to cells treated with H₂O₂ alone (Fig.  ). Notably, BafA1 alone had no significant effect on cell viability in either LacZ- or Nrf1-expressing cells compared to untreated cells (Fig.  ), showing that autophagy inhibition specifically compromised Nrf1-mediated cytoprotection against H2O2-induced oxidative stress.
 We next used a genetic approach to inhibit autophagy via shRNAs targeting Sqstm, a transcription target of Nrf1, and Atg5, a critical autophagy gene required for the formation of autophagosomes . We observed a marked reduction in the transcript level of Atg5 and Sqstm1 in cells with shRNAs (Supplementary Fig.  ). Cardiomyocytes with Atg5 shRNA showed a significant loss of LC3-II formation under both baseline and CQ-treated conditions, as well as a marked reduction in the LC3B-II/LC3B-I ratio (Fig.  ), confirming effective blockade of autophagy. Although Sqstm1 shRNA did not significantly affect LC3B-II levels, it almost completely abolished Sqstm1 protein expression (Fig.  ), thereby impairing the delivery of ubiquitinated cargo to autophagosomes . To assess whether Atg5 and Sqstm1 knockdown affects Nrf1-mediated cytoprotection, we co-expressed Nrf1 or LacZ with or without shRNAs followed by H₂O₂ treatment. As expected, Nrf1 overexpression reduced the cytotoxicity induced by H₂O₂ treatment, compared to H₂O₂-treated LacZ controls (Fig.  ). However, knockdown of either Atg5 or Sqstm1 attenuated the protective effect of Nrf1, resulting cytotoxicity comparable to H₂O₂-treated LacZ controls (Fig.  ). Calcein-AM live-cell staining showed no significant difference in the viability between Nrf1-overexpressing cells with or without H₂O₂ treatment (Supplementary Fig.  ). In contrast, the percentage of live cells was significantly reduced after H₂O₂ treatment by Atg5 or Sqstm1 shRNA knockdown (Supplementary Fig.  ). Importantly, neither Atg5 nor Sqstm1 knockdown affected cardiomyocyte viability under normal condition without H₂O₂ (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Together, by pharmacological and genetic inhibition of autophagy, we demonstrate that autophagy is required for the protective effect of Nrf1 in cardiomyocytes under oxidative stress.

## NRF1 regulates autophagy in human cardiomyocytes
Lastly, to determine whether NRF1 regulates autophagy in human hearts, we analyzed gene expression data from 431 human left ventricular samples in the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) dataset . Although these human samples likely varied by ventricular region and clinical status, we observed strong positive correlations (spearman correlation co-efficient 0.75–0.86) between NRF1 and multiple autophagy-related genes, including SQSTM1, GABARAPL1, and VCP, supporting a role for NRF1 in autophagy regulation in the human heart (Fig.  ). To validate this, we overexpressed NRF1 in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs). NRF1 overexpression increased transcript levels of SQSTM1, GABARAPL1, and VCP (Fig.  ) as well as protein levels of SQSTM1(Supplementary Fig.  ). To study whether NRF1 overexpression increases autophagy flux in hiPSC-CMs, we analyzed LC3B by immunoblotting in hiPSC-CMs overexpressing NRF1 or LacZ, with or without CQ treatment. Similar to our findings in mouse cardiomyocytes, NRF1 overexpression increased both LC3B-I and LC3B-II compared to LacZ controls (Fig.  ). Additionally, CQ treatment further elevated LC3B-II in NRF1 overexpressing cells, showing enhanced autophagy flux (Fig.  ).
 Decline in proteasomal activity is associated with heart diseases and aging , , leading to accumulation of misfolded and unwanted proteins. Cells can compensate for the impaired protein clearance by activating aggrephagy, a type of selective autophagy that removes ubiquitinated protein aggregates . Indeed, treating hiPSC-CMs with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 resulted in elevated LC3B-II levels, which were further increased by co-treatment of MG132 and CQ (Supplementary Fig.  ), verifying MG132-activated autophagic flux. We next examined the clearance of ubiquitinated protein aggregates in response to MG132-mediated proteasome inhibition. As expected, MG132 caused a marked accumulation of polyubiquitinated proteins in control cells overexpressing LacZ (Fig.  ), consistent with impaired proteasome function. NRF1 knockdown via shRNA, which effectively reduced NRF1 transcripts by >90% as well as autophagy gene expression (Supplementary Fig.  ), resulted in further accumulation of polyubiquitinated proteins after MG132 treatment (Fig.  ). In contrast, NRF1 overexpression enhanced aggregate clearance, as shown by significantly reduced polyubiquitin proteins compared to MG132-treated controls (Fig.  ). Together, these findings demonstrate that NRF1 facilitates the removal of ubiquitinated proteins during proteasome inhibition and plays a key role in mitigating proteotoxic stress in human cardiomyocytes.

## Discussion
Our study identifies Nrf1 as a key regulator of autophagy in both mouse and human cardiomyocytes and that this function is essential for its cardioprotective effects. By uncovering its role in regulating autophagy, we establish Nrf1 as a central coordinator of cardiac proteostasis, integrating both proteasomal and autophagic pathways. We demonstrate that transient activation of Nrf1 during I/R injury confers therapeutic benefit to the adult mouse heart and is superior to its constitutive activation, providing a proof-of-principle for time-controlled interventions in heart diseases by targeting stress-adaptive mechanisms. Using integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses, we delineate the function of Nrf1 in orchestrating both transcriptional and post-transcriptional mechanisms that underlie its cardioprotective benefit under oxidative stress. Our study advances fundamental understanding of cardiac proteostasis and highlights Nrf1 as a promising therapeutic target for enhancing heart repair.
While UPS and autophagy were traditionally viewed as two independent protein degradation pathways, accumulating evidence suggested a regulatory link between them, where autophagy is activated as a compensatory pathway when proteasome is impaired – . Nrf1 has been recently proposed to be responsible for this compensatory activation to promote autophagic clearance of ubiquitinated protein aggregates , . In supporting this, our data show that in primary cardiomyocytes with impaired proteasome activity by MG132 treatment, shRNA knockdown of Nrf1 led to further accumulation of ubiquitinated protein aggregate, whereas Nrf1 overexpression reduces that. The function of Nrf1 in activating autophagy helps cells to maintain proteostasis and provide survival fitness. Indeed, both pharmacological and genetic blockade of autophagy abolished the cytoprotective effect of Nrf1 against H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress. The ability of Nrf1 to induce autophagy under proteolytic stress provides a mechanism to couple the proteasome “bounce-back” response with coordinated induction of autophagy. By doing so, Nrf1 provides two complementary pathways to restore proteostasis in which the proteasome clears soluble proteins and autophagy removes aggregates and damaged organelles. This layered regulation could be especially advantageous in long-lived, low-turnover tissues such as the heart, which cannot dilute damaged proteins by cell division.
Our data show that Nrf1 regulates multiple components of the autophagy pathway spanning initiation (Tmem41b), cargo recognition (Sqstm1), formation and maturation of autophagosome (LC3/Gabarap family), and aggregate processing (Vcp), suggesting that Nrf1 functions as a multi-node coordinator of autophagy. Consistent with this idea, our data show that knockdown of Sqstm1 alone is sufficient to abolish the cytoprotective effect of Nrf1. Conversely, individual activation of Sqstm1 or Gabarapl1 failed to rescue the autophagy defect caused by Nrf1 deficiency . Mechanistically, we identified putative Nrf1-binding sites in the promoters of these autophagy genes that are required for their transcription, supporting a module-level co-regulation of these autophagy genes by Nrf1. It is unclear how such convergent regulation of autophagy genes has evolved. Future studies examining whether this regulatory module is conserved across species will help determine whether a shared cis-regulatory mechanism underlies this stress-adaptive proteostasis network. Additionally, we observed that Nrf1 overexpression increased both LC3B-I and LC3B-II levels, however the mRNA level of Map1lc3b, which encodes LC3B protein, is unchanged, suggesting a post-transcriptional increase of LC3B by Nrf1. How does Nrf1 overexpression lead to the marked protein level increase of LC3B warrens further investigation. We speculate that Nrf1 overexpression primarily expands the total LC3B pool, rather than specifically promoting LC3B-I to LC3B-II processing, and that its concurrent activation of other autophagy components, including Tmem41b, Sqstm1, and Vcp collectively enhances the autophagy activity.
Although Nrf1 and Nrf2 belong to the same transcription factor family, Nrf1 is more specialized in proteostasis regulation, whereas Nrf2 primarily controls anti-oxidant responses. Prior cell-based studies showed that while both factors can induce autophagy genes, Nrf1 drives their expression to a greater extent than Nrf2 . Consistent with this, our in vivo data show that Nrf1 deletion reduced the expression of multiple autophagy genes, and importantly, these genes were not further reduced by additional Nrf2 deletion. In cardiomyocytes, Nrf1 is expressed at substantially higher levels than Nrf2, suggesting it is the dominant stress regulator. Indeed, our data show that mice with Nrf2 deletion in cardiomyocytes are phenotypically normal at 2–4 months, whereas Nrf1 deletion developed systolic dysfunction by 4 months, despite having normal cardiac output at 2 months. The age-dependent decompensation in Nrf1 cKO suggests that there is modest early compensation, likely via Nrf2. Supporting this, mice with double deletion of Nrf1 and Nrf2 have reduced cardiac functional decline at 2 months. However, the functional compensation by Nrf2 in Nrf1 cKO hearts becomes insufficient at 4 months, potentially due to increased physiological load and proteotoxic stress with age.
Our integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses enabled us to dissect the post-transcriptional effects of Nrf1 on proteome remodeling. Under oxidative stress, Nrf1 overexpression attenuated the induction of proteins involved in immune responses, cell death, and cardiac hypertrophy, including the canonical cardiac stress markers Nppa, Ankrd1, and Camk2d. Whether the downregulation of these stress-responsive proteins reflects Nrf1-induced selective clearance, such as through enhanced proteasome activity and/or macroautophagy/aggrephagy, remains to be determined. Supporting this possibility, studies in adipocytes have shown that proteins involved in mitochondrial respiration are preferentially degraded through the Nrf1-mediated UPS during cold adaptation , indicating that upregulation of Nrf1-dependent protein degradation pathways can exert pathway-specific effects. Future studies using targeted inhibition of individual protein quality control pathways, coupled with ubiquitin proteomics, will help clarify the specific mechanisms of degradation and identify proteins that are preferentially sensitive to Nrf1-mediated turnover. Although we cannot exclude the possibility that some stress-responsive proteins are indirectly regulated by Nrf1 overexpression, their selective reduction at the protein, but not RNA, level indicates that Nrf1 exerts a predominantly post-transcriptional regulatory control of these genes.
The inducible AAV platform we used in our study enabled temporally precise and reversible activation of Nrf1 during ischemic injury, allowing activation of the protective gene program precisely when the injured heart requires it most. This transient activation of a stress-adaptive factor is likely essential for maximizing therapeutic benefit, because constitutive activation could chronically elevate protein turnover and, paradoxically, disrupt proteostasis. Indeed, our data show that this transient Nrf1 activation yielded superior functional recovery compared with constitutive overexpression reported previously . In our study, Nrf1 was activated before injury and maintained through 1 day post-injury. Future studies will test whether initiating Nrf1 activation immediately after injury is also beneficial, as this timing is more translatable to patient treatments. Our study shows that the cardioprotective effect of Nrf1 in ischemic heart disease is attributed to its coordinated enhancement of proteasome and autophagy function. Because UPS and autophagy capacity decline with aging and in diverse diseases, Nrf1-based therapy could offer a broadly applicable strategy to restore proteostasis.

## Methods

## Mice
Mouse experiments complied with all relevant ethical regulations and were performed according to protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees at Boston Children’s Hospital IACUC regulations (protocol number 00002604). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. All mice used in this study were housed at the pathogen-free Animal Resource Center and were bred inside an SPF facility with 12 h light/dark cycles with a temperature of 18–24 °C and humidity of 35–60% and monitored daily with no health problems. All animals were housed in groups of a maximum five per cage with ad libitum access to food and water. Nrf1fl/fl mice in C57BL/6 background were obtained from Dr. Gokhan S. Hotamisligil’s laboratory (Harvard Medical School). Nrf2fl/fl and αMHC-Cre mice in C57BL/6 background were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (#025433 and #011038, respectively). The sex and age of animals used are specified in each experimental section. Euthanasia procedures for mice follow the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals: 2020 Edition ad performed by trained personnel. Neonatal mice (P1-P7) are euthanatized by rapid decapitation. Neonates aged P8-P10 are first euthanized by CO2 inhalation followed by rapid decapitation. Mice older than P10 are euthanized by CO2 inhalation followed a secondary physical method of euthanasia, such as cervical dislocation.
We followed a standard CO2 procedure using a fill rate of 30-70% displacement of the chamber volume per minute for 5 min. Upon completion of the procedure, death is confirmed by an appropriate method, such as ascertaining cardiac and respiratory arrest.

## Generation of inducible AAV system
Cardiomyocyte-specific Tet-on inducible AAV system was cloned by replacing the hPGK promoter of the AAVPro Tet-One inducible expression system (Takara #634310) with the cardiomyocyte-specific chicken cTnT2 promoter . GFP or HA-tagged Nrf1 coding sequences were inserted at the multiple cloning site downstream of the TRE3G3 promoter. Vector map is shown in Supplementary Fig.  . Plasmids are deposited to Addgene (ID: 254458 and 254459).

## AAV Production, Purification and Tier Quantification
Adeno-associated viruses (AAVs) were produced in HEK293T cells (ATCC CRL-3216) using a triple transfection method following previously established protocols . Briefly, cells were seeded in 15-cm dishes and transfected at ~70–80% confluency using polyethylenimine (PEI) with a 1:1 PEI:DNA mass ratio. Each plate was transfected with 140 µg of the rep-cap plasmid, 320 µg of the adenoviral helper plasmid, and 140 µg of the AAV plasmid, using Opti-MEM as the transfection medium. After 72 h, cells were harvested and lysed in lysis buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), and 1 mM MgCl₂. Lysates were subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles and treated with Benzonase (≥50 U/mL, MilliporeSigma) for 30 min at 37 °C to degrade unpackaged DNA. Viral particles were purified using iodixanol density gradient ultracentrifugation as previously described . The AAV-containing fraction was extracted and buffer-exchanged into PBS containing 0.001% Pluronic F-68 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using Amicon Ultra-15 centrifugal filters (MWCO 100 kDa). Aliquots were stored at –80 °C until use.
Viral genome titer was determined by quantitative PCR (qPCR). AAV samples were first treated with DNase I (10 U/µL, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C for 30 min to degrade residual plasmid DNA. Samples were then heat-inactivated and incubated with Proteinase K (10 mg/mL, Thermo Fisher) at 56 °C for 30 min to release viral genomes from the capsid. Following enzyme inactivation at 95 °C for 10 min, 2 µL of the resulting lysate was used as the template for qPCR. qPCR was performed using primers targeting the cardiac troponin T (cTnT) promoter region of the AAV transfer plasmid (Forward: 5′-TCGGGATAAAAGCAGTCTGG-3’ and Reverse: 5′-CCCAAGCTATTGTGTGGCCT-3′). A standard curve was generated from 10-fold serial dilutions (10¹ to 10⁸ copies/µL) of a linearized AAV plasmid of known concentration. Viral titer was calculated based on Ct values and are reported as vector genomes (vg) per milliliter.

## Adult I/R
Timed-pregnant wildtype C57BL/6 mice were used to deliver pups for systemic iAAV9 injection on P2-P4, followed by I/R surgery at 12 weeks of age. Pups were randomly assigned to GFP or Nrf1-HA groups and iAAV9 expressing GFP or Nrf1-HA were delivered to pups at a titer of 5E13 vg/kg by subcutaneous injection. Six days before the surgery, dox treatment was started and continued till 1 day post-surgery. Mice were administered two injections of doxycycline hyclate (Sigma-Aldrich, D9891-1G) by intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 50 µg per gram of body weight. Following injection, mice were kept on doxycycline-supplemented drinking water (2 mg/mL doxycycline hyclate in 10 mg/mL sucrose solution) for four days. At 12 weeks of age, I/R surgery was performed on male mice pre-injected with iAAV9. For the I/R procedure, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane blended with oxygen, intubated, and ventilated with a MiniVent mouse ventilator (Hugo Sachs Elektronik; stroke volume, 250 μL; respiratory rate, 105 breaths per minute). Care is taken to extend the neck prior to intubation of the trachea to avoid obstruction. Respiratory rate, body temperature, and heart rate are monitored continuously during the procedure. A heating pad is used to maintain body temperature within a range of 37 ± 0.2 °C. An oblique 8 mm incision is made 2 mm away from the left sternal border toward the left armpit (~1 cm below it). The underlying muscles are gently separated, taking care to avoid damaging blood vessels. The rib cage and moving left lung are visualized. The 4th intercostal space, between those ribs, represents the area where the lowest part of the lung is observed. The chest cavity is opened cautiously to avoid damaging the lung. A sterile chest retractor is inserted (~1 cm) and gently opened to spread the wound ~8 mm in width. The heart, partially covered by the lung, is visualized. The pericardium is gently picked up with sterile curved and straight forceps, pulled apart, and placed behind the arms of the retractor. This maneuver pushes the lung up slightly, immobilizing it and providing better exposure of the heart. The LAD coronary artery appears as a pulsating bright red spike, running from underneath the left atrium toward the apex. The LAD artery is ligated 1 − 2 mm below the tip of the left auricle in its normal position, inducing roughly 30−40% ischemic area of the LV. Once the site of ligation is determined, curved forceps are used to gently press on the artery just below the ligation site to enhance visibility and stabilize the heart. A suture ligature (e.g., 7-0 Prolene) is passed underneath the LAD coronary artery with a tapered needle. The ligature is tied, and occlusion is confirmed by the change in color (becoming pale) of the anterior wall of the LV. The mouse remains ventilated with anesthesia for 45 min on a heating pad. After this period, the suture around the LAD is removed, and the lung is reinflated. For the Sham surgery, the procedure is identical except that the LAD is not ligated. The mouse remains ventilated with anesthesia for 45 min on a heating pad before the chest cavity is closed. The operator performing the procedure was blinded to genotype assignments.
Pain or distress during surgery is alleviated with anesthesia with Isoflurane blended with oxygen (2-4% induction, 1-3% maintenance) and after surgery with a single injection of Ethiqa XR (3.25 mg/kg, SQ), which provides analgesia for 72 h. After surgery, mice are examined daily after procedures for three days to look for evidence of weight loss (>20%), decreased movement, respiratory distress, or wound infection. Severe weight loss, anorexia, severe shortness of breath, severe lethargy will be considered adequate symptoms to induce euthanasia, and these animals are not included in the study. Additionally, some degree of lethargy can be observed in the first few days following surgery due to reduced cardiac output from infarct injury to the ventricle. A 30% mortality rate associated with the surgery was observed within the first three days following surgery, and these animals are not included in the study.

## Transthoracic echocardiography
Echocardiography is performed on anesthetized mice (1-3% isofluorane mixed with oxygen, titrated to anesthesia level and heart rate). Mice are placed on a heated stage and imaged. Fractional shortening (FS) and ejection fraction (EF) were used as indices of cardiac contractile function. M-mode tracings were used to measure LV internal diameter at end-diastole (LVIDd) and end-systole (LVIDs). FS was calculated according to the following formula: FS = [(LVIDd−LVIDs)/LVIDd]×100%. EF was calculated as EF  =  [(LVEDV − LVESV)/LVEDV]×100%. LVESV, left ventricular end-systolic volume; LVEDV, left ventricular end-diastolic volume. All measurements were performed by an experienced operator blinded to genotype assignments.

## Analysis of fibrotic tissue
Hearts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS at 4 °C for 24–48 h, followed by cryoprotection in 30% sucrose in PBS at 4 °C until tissues sank. Cryoprotected tissues were then embedded in optimal cutting temperature (O.C.T.) compound (Scigen), frozen, and stored at −80 °C. Hearts were cross-sectioned at 10 μm thickness using a cryostat. Sectioning was performed starting from the ligation suture, and tissue sections were collected at defined depths of 200, 300, 400, and 500 μm below the suture to ensure consistent anatomical sampling. To identify fibrosis, Masson’s Trichrome staining was performed on cryosections using the Modified Masson’s Trichrome Stain Kit (ScyTek Laboratories) following the manufacturer’s instructions, with minor adaptations for OCT-embedded tissues. Briefly, 10 μm cryosections were air-dried, fixed in pre-chilled 4% paraformaldehyde, rinsed in distilled water, and stained sequentially with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin (for nuclear staining), Biebrich scarlet-acid fuchsin, phosphomolybdic/phosphotungstic acid, and aniline blue. Sections were then dehydrated through graded alcohols, cleared in xylene, and mounted. Collagen fibers appeared blue, muscle fibers red, and nuclei black
Images were acquired using ImageXpress Pico Automated Cell Imaging System (Molecular Devices). After image acquisition of whole heart sections, the fibrotic region indicated in blue was quantified in ImageJ. Especially, the freehand selection tool was used to trace the fibrotic region, and the area was measured (Analysis → Measure). The total heart cross-sectional area was also traced using the freehand selection tool and then measured. The fibrotic area was calculated as the fibrotic region normalized by the heart section area.

## Generation of adenoviruses
Adenovirus was generated using the Adeno-X Adenoviral System 3 (Clontech, 632267). Coding regions of β-galactosidase (lacZ), mouse Nrf1 and human NRF1, Parkin-GFP (gift from Edward Fon, Addgene #45875 ) were cloned into the pAdx-CMV vector containing a red fluorescent protein. Adenovirus vectors expressing an array of three shRNAs targeting human NRF1, mouse Atg5 and mouse Sqstm1 were purchased from VectorBuilder, GmBH. Adenoviruses were generated by transfecting PacI-linearized recombinant adenoviral plasmids into a mammalian packaging cell line Adeno-X 293. Primary lysates were used to re-infect Adeno-X 293 cells to generate higher-titer viruses. The viral titer was determined in 293 T cells using Adeno-X RapidTiter Kit (Clontech, 632250). Adenoviruses were stored in 20 μL aliquots at −80 °C.

## Cardiomyocyte isolation, culture, and autophagy inhibition
Neonatal ventricular cardiomyocytes (NRVMs) were isolated from 1- or 2-day-old Sprague-Dawley rats with the Isolation System for Neonatal Rat/Mouse Cardiomyocytes (Cellutron, nc-6031) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Approximately 30 pups (both male and female) were used for isolation. Isolated NRVMs were plated at a density of 3 × 105 cells/well to gelatin-coated 12-well plates and were maintained in DMEM/199 medium (3:1, 3% FBS) for 48 h before adenoviral infection. Adenoviruses were diluted in DMEM/199 medium containing 3% FBS and were added to NRVMs at an MOI of 50. Two days after viral infection, NRVMs were treated with 50 μM H2O2 for 1 h followed by sample collections for RNA sequencing and proteomics analyses. Cell seeding, adenovirus infection and H2O2 treatment were randomized across groups.
Neonatal ventricular mouse cardiomyocytes were isolated from 1- or 2-day-old C57BL/6 mice following a previous protocol . Approximately 6-8 pups (both male and female) were used for cardiomyocyte isolation, yielding roughly 5 million cells. Isolated cardiomyocytes were plated at a density of 3 × 105 cells/well in 0.1% gelatin-coated 24-well plates and were maintained in DMEM/199 medium (3:1, 3% FBS) for 48 h before adenoviral infection. Adenoviruses were added at a concentration of 4 μL of virus per 1 mL of media to cardiomyocytes. Two days after viral transfection, cardiomyocytes cells were treated with 400 μM chloroquine or 1 μM Bafilomycin A1 for 30 min prior to H2O2 treatment (150 μM) for 70 min. CQ and BafA1 treatments were continued for the duration of H2O2 treatment. At the end of the treatments, culture medium from treated cells was collected, and the level of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) in the medium released from damaged cells was quantified using the LDH Assay Kit (Cayman Chemical, 601170). Cardiomyocytes were next washed with PBS and incubated with Calcein AM dye (Thermo Fisher, L3224) for 20 min following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cell viability was then visualized on the ImageXpress Pico Automated Cell Imaging System (Molecular Devices).
For shRNA inhibition of autophagy genes, neonatal ventricular mouse cardiomyocytes were similarly isolated and cultured as above and co-infected with Ad-LacZ or Ad-Nrf1 with or without Ad-Atg5 shRNA or Ad-Sqstm1 shRNA. Two days after infection, cells were collected for RNA extraction and quantification of Atg5 and Sqstm1 expression was performed by RT-qPCR using primers listed in Supplementary Table  . For cell survival analysis, adenovirus infected cells were treated with 400 μM chloroquine for 30 min prior to H2O2 treatment (150 μM) for 70 min. CQ treatment was continued for the duration of H2O2 treatment. LDH assay and Calcein AM staining were performed at the end of the treatment, same as above.
To analyze mitophagy, neonatal ventricular mouse cardiomyocytes were infected with Ad-LacZ along with a mitophagy reporter, Ad-Parkin-GFP, or Ad-Nrf1 with Ad-Parkin-GFP, and treated with FCCP at 10 μM or FCCP plus CQ (400 μM) two days after infection. One hour after the treatment, cells were fixed in 4% PFA for 15 min and washed with PBS three times before antibody staining. Cell seeding, adenovirus infection and H2O2 treatment were randomized across groups.

## Immunohistochemistry and Immunocytochemistry
Heart samples were fixed and sectioned as described above. Heart sections were air-dried for 30 min at room temperature, fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min, washed twice with PBS, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. Sections were then blocked in 5% goat serum/3% BSA/0.025% Triton X-100/PBS blocking solution for 1 h and stained with the indicated primary antibodies prepared in blocking solution at 4 °C overnight using the following dilutions: cTnI (Abcam, ab47003, 1:200) and GFP (Abcam, ab290, 1:200). Sections were subsequently washed with 0.025% Triton X-100/PBS three times and incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies: Goat anti-mouse IgG Alexa 647 (ThermoFisher Scientific A32728, 1:500), Goat anti-mouse IgG Alexa 488 (ThermoFisher Scientific A32723, 1:500), Goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa 647 (ThermoFisher Scientific A32731, 1:500), Goat anti-rat IgG Alexa 488 (ThermoFisher Scientific A-11006, 1:500) and Goat anti-mouse IgG Alexa 647 (ThermoFisher Scientific A32733, 1:500) and WGA (Vector Laboratories FL-1021, 1:100), prepared in blocking solution at room temperature for 1.5 h. After secondary antibody incubation, sections were washed with PBS, incubated with DAPI at room temperature for 10 min, and washed twice with PBS before mounting. Images were obtained using an Olympus FV3000 RS confocal microscope.
For analyzing mitophagy activity, neonatal mouse ventricular cardiomyocytes infected with Ad-LacZ plus Ad-Parkin-GFP or Ad-Nrf1 plus Ad-Parkin-GFP with FCCP treatment, FCCP + CQ treatment or untreated conditions were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min. Fixed cells were washed with PBS three times and treated with 0.025% Triton X-100/PBS for 20 min at room temperature to permeabilized the cell membranes. Cells were then blocked with 10%BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies against GFP (Abcam, ab290, 1:250) and Tom20 (Abnova, H00009804-M01, 1:50) in blocking solution at 4 °C overnight. Cells were subsequently washed with PBS three times and incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1.5 h. Cells were washed with PBS, incubated with DAPI at room temperature for 10 min, and washed twice with PBS before imaging. Images were obtained using the ImageXpress Pico Automated Cell Imaging System (Molecular Devices).

## Protein isolation and immunoblot analysis
Dissected heart ventricles or cultured cells after treatment were homogenized on ice for 15 min in RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher, J63306-AP) containing protease inhibitor (Thermo Fisher, 78438) and phosphorylase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, P0044), followed by 15 min centrifugation at 16,000×g to pellet cell debris. Protein concentration in cell lysates was measured by BCA protein assay (Thermo Fisher, 23225). 2–20 µg of protein was used for SDS–PAGE gel electrophoresis. After gel to membrane transfer, membranes were blocked in 5% milk for 30 min followed by primary antibody incubation at 4 °C overnight using the following dilutions: ubiquitin (P4D1) (Cell Signaling Technology, #3936, 1:5000), α-tubulin (11H10) (Cell Signaling Technology, #2125, 1:3000), SQSTM1/p62 (ABclonal, A19700, 1:2000-4000), LC3B (ABclonal, A19665, 1:1000), NPPA (Abcepta, AP8534A, 1:500), GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology, # 2118 1:5000), GFP (Rockland, 600-101-215, 1:500), HA tag (Invitrogen, 71-5500, 1:1000), After the primary antibody incubation, the protein-membrane was washed with 1× TBST buffer and incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP): Goat anti-mouse IgG HRP (abcam, ab6789, 1:5000) and Goat anti-rabbit IgG HRP (abcam, ab6721, 1:5000). Chemiluminescent signal was developed using SuperSignal™ West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher, 34577).

## Bulk RNA sequencing in NRVMs and data analysis
NRVMs were isolated as described in the above section. Isolated NRVMs were infected with adenoviruses expressing either Nrf1 or LacZ, and treated with 50 μM H₂O₂ for 1 h. Parallel samples without H₂O₂ exposure served as controls. For each experimental group (n = 3 biological replicates), total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Cat# 74104) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 TapeStation. Stranded mRNA-seq libraries were prepared with the KAPA mRNA HyperPrep Kit (Roche, KK8581), according to the provided protocol. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NextSeq 500 platform using a 75-base single-end high-output run configuration. Sample collection and processing were randomized across groups and performed on the same day to minimize batch effects.
For computational analysis, raw sequence data were first quality-checked using FastQC (v0.11.4). Reads were aligned to the Rnor 6.0 rat reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.0.4) with default parameters and the option --rna-strandness F. Duplicate reads were removed using Samtools (v0.1.18), and gene-level counts were obtained with featureCounts (v1.6.0) using Ensembl gene annotations. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using edgeR (v3.20.5) in R, employing a generalized linear model (GLM) framework. Batch effects were accounted for with a design matrix specifying batch and treatment groups. Genes with >1 count per million (CPM) in at least three samples were retained for normalization and downstream analysis. Normalized count matrix in FKPM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) is provided in Supplementary Data  . Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the normalized count matrix and identified one LacZ control sample as an outlier, which was excluded from subsequent analyses based on predefined quality-control criteria. Significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with an absolute |log₂ fold change| > 0.5 and a false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05. Functional enrichment analyses were conducted via Metascape (https://metascape.org/) using either the upregulated or downregulated gene sets as input.

## Tandem mass tag proteomics analysis
Primary NRVMs were isolated as described above and transduced with adenoviruses expressing either Nrf1 or LacZ and subsequently exposed to 50 μM H₂O₂ for 1 h. Control samples (LacZ or Nrf1 without H₂O₂) were also collected. For each biological replicate (n = 3), 10 million cells were harvested for proteomic profiling. Sample collection and processing were randomized across groups and performed on the same day to minimize potential batch effects. Sample processing and proteomics analysis follow same protocols as we previously described . Specifically, cell lysates were concentrated to half their initial volume using a SpeedVac. Equal volumes of 10% SDS in 100 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) were added, followed by the addition of 10 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP) and incubation at 56 °C for 30 min. Proteins were then alkylated with 20 mM iodoacetamide at room temperature for 30 min in the dark. Acidification was performed with 12% phosphoric acid, and six volumes of S-Trap binding buffer (90% methanol in 100 mM TEAB) were added. Samples were loaded onto S-Trap mini columns (Protifi), washed three times with 150 μl binding buffer, and digested overnight at 37 °C using trypsin at a 1:10 ratio in 50 mM TEAB. Peptides were sequentially eluted with 50 mM TEAB, 0.2% formic acid, and 50% acetonitrile in 0.2% formic acid. Eluted peptides were fractionated into 8 parts using the Pierce High pH Reversed-Phase Peptide Fractionation Kit.
Dried peptide fractions were reconstituted in 2% acetonitrile and 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid prior to mass spectrometry analysis. Samples were analyzed on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer coupled with an Ultimate 3000 RSLC-Nano LC system. Peptides were separated on a 75 μm internal diameter, 75 cm long EasySpray column (Thermo) using a linear gradient of 0–28% buffer B over 180 min. Buffer A consisted of 2% acetonitrile and 0.1% formic acid, while buffer B contained 80% acetonitrile, 10% trifluoroethanol, and 0.1% formic acid. MS1 spectra were collected in positive ion mode at 120,000 resolution with a spray voltage of 1.8 kV and a capillary temperature of 275 °C. The top 10 precursor ions (z = 2–6) were selected for MS2 via CID (collision energy: 35%), and subsequent MS3 analysis was performed using SPS-MS3 with HCD fragmentation at 55% energy. Dynamic exclusion was enabled with a 25-second window following precursor selection.
Raw data were processed using Proteome Discoverer v2.4 (Thermo). Sequest HT was used to search against the UniProt rat reference proteome. Search parameters included 10 ppm precursor and 0.6 Da fragment mass tolerances, allowing up to three missed cleavages. Variable modifications included methionine oxidation, while static modifications accounted for TMT6plex labeling on lysine residues and N-termini. Reporter ion intensities were analyzed using the msms.edgeR package first generate normalized counts. Potential batch effects were accounted for by including design matrix specifying batch and treatment groups as a factor in the Generalized Linear Model (GLM) with the edgeR. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were further analyzed using a cutoff of |log₂(fold-change)| > 0.25 and FDR  <  0.05.

## Promoter reporter assay
The multiplex reporter assay was performed as described before , , with modifications. The GFP-tag sequence from the 13 DNA-tag vectors reporter system was individually cloned into a modified pGL4.23 vector (Promega) that contains a minimal basal promoter SCP1 . The promoter regions of Sqstm1, Gabarapl1, Vcp based on H3K27ac ChIP-seq were PCR-amplified and cloned into the multiple cloning sites upstream of the SCP1 promoter. An empty vector without additional DNA insert was used as the negative control. Plasmids were transfected into HEK293 cells, in which plasmids with different tags were mixed at equal molar ratios. The negative control vector was co-transfected in each batch. Cells were collected 48 h after transfection. DNA and RNA were extracted using AllPrep DNA/RNA Micro Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. QPCR experiments were performed on both DNA and cDNA templates using primers specific to each tag-DNA . To calculate the enhancer activity, the relative expression level of each tag compared to the negative control (result from cDNA) was normalized to the amount of transfected DNA . Primers used to amplify promoter are: Sqstm1 forward: 5’- AACCAACTCCAAGAGAGGTCC-3’, Sqstm1 reverse: 5’- GCTCTTCCGGGCCTTTAACTG-3’, Gabarapl1 forward: 5’- CTCCCAAGATAAAGGTCTGGAG- 3’, Gabarapl1 forward: 5’- AAATTGTGGTTCACCTCGCTC- 3’, Vcp forward: 5’- GGTGGAGTAAGAGGATCCCTAGG- 3’, Vcp reverse: 5’- GGTCTTCTGTATATGGTCGGCG-3’. Mutant promoter sequences were generated by deleting the predicted Nrf1 binding sites shown in Fig.  .

## Human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) culture and cardiac differentiation
A wild-type human male hiPSC line WTC-11 (Coriell Institute: # GM25256) is used for hiPSC culture . hiPSC culture and cardiac differentiation was performed as published previously using a spinner flask-based suspension system (Pfeiffer CELLspin Spinner System, Cole Palmer®; CELLSPIN CONTROL UNIT #501960613; CELLSPIN STIR PLATFORM 4 POS #501960614) . In short, hiPSCs were cultured in T80 cell culture flasks pretreated with 1:100 (v/v) diluted Geltrex (Life Technologies, #A1413302) incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C, with 5% CO2 at a density of 15,000 cells/cm2. Cells were maintained in E8 medium (Life Technologies, #A1517001) with daily medium change until 90% cell confluency was reached. For dissociation, T80 flasks were washed once with PBS, incubated with 5 mL Versene (Life Technologies, #1540066) for 15 to 20 min at 37 °C and dissociation was stopped by adding 5 mL E8 medium supplemented with 10 μM of ROCK inhibitor Y-27632. 50 million single cells were resuspended in 100 mL E8 medium supplemented with 10 μM of ROCK inhibitor and inoculated into one spinner flask for 24 h (Day −1). Cardiac differentiation was induced by a complete change of the medium to RPMI 1640 with B27 (w/o Insulin; basic medium) supplemented with 7 μM After 24 h (Day 1), the complete medium was changed to basic medium, and cells were incubated for an additional 24 h. On day 2, the complete medium was changed again to basic medium containing 5 µM IWR-1-endo for 48 h. On day 4, the complete medium was changed to basic medium, and cells were incubated for an additional 72 hours. From day 7 on cells were cultured in basic medium supplemented with 1:1000 (v/v) insulin. Finally, cells were enzymatically dissociated on day 15 for 3–4 hours, depending on EB size and density, and were then frozen using a controlled rate freezer as described previously .
For functional experiments cryovials containing spinner-derived hPSC-CMs were thawed as described previously and plated into 24-well plates pretreated with 1:100 (v/v) diluted Geltrex at 400,000 cells per well. hiPSC-CMs were transfected with adenoviruses expressing human NRF1 and lacZ (control) the two days after plating with MOI 25–50. Adenoviral infection efficiency was assessed 30 h after the infection and over 70% of cells were infected as indicated by a red fluorescent reporter. 48 h after the infection, hiPSC-CMs were collected for RNA and protein analysis. To achieve NRF1 knockdown in hiPSC-CMs, adenoviruses expressing three shRNAs targeting human NRF1 was purchased from VectorBuilder and were used to infect hiPSC-CMs at MOI 25–50. The viral infection efficiency was verified by GFP reporter and reduction of NRF1 transcripts (Supplementary Fig.  ). To induce autophagy, 100 µM MG132 was added to hiPSC-CM cultures for 12-16 h.

## Quantitative real-time PCR analysis
Total RNA was extracted from cells or heart tissues using Trizol and Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Kits (Zymo Research, R2052) and reverse transcribed using iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad) with random primers. The quantitative polymerase chain reactions (qPCR) were assembled using FastSYBR Mixture (CoWin Biosciences, CW2621). Assays were performed using a QuanStudio 3 0.2 ml qPCR system (Thermo Fisher). Expression values were normalized to GAPDH or TUBA mRNA and were represented as fold change. Oligonucleotide sequences of qPCR primers are listed in Supplementary Table  .

For sample size determination, sample sizes were chosen based on prior experience with the experimental models and assays , , , – . For randomization, animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Investigators performing I/R surgery and echocardiographic acquisition and analysis were blinded to group assignment. Cell-based experiments were randomized across conditions when feasible, and samples were processed in parallel to minimize batch effects.
All statistical analyses used an alpha level of 0.05. Statistical tests were selected based on the experimental design and number of factors. For experiments with two factors (e.g., genotype ±H₂O₂), analyses were performed using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. For experiments with three factors (e.g., genotype × H₂O₂ × shRNA condition), analyses were performed using three-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Where only two groups were compared, two-tailed unpaired tests were used. For RNA-seq and proteomics differential analyses, multiple testing was controlled using false discovery rate (FDR) procedures in edgeR as described in the Methods. No data were excluded unless pre-specified outlier criteria were met. Outliers were identified using GraphPad Prism (ROUT method, Q = 5%) and PCA analysis for omic samples. For the adult I/R model, mortality can occur within the first three days post-surgery, therefore mice die during data recording are excluded from analysis.
