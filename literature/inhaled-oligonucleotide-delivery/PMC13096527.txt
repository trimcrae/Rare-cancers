To elucidate its functional role, we establish MCT4 homozygous knockout BALB/c mice (MCT4-/-) using CRISPR/Cas9-EGE technology. Compared with wild-type counterparts, MCT4-/- mice exhibit ~40% reduction in allograft tumor volume, which is partly attributable to diminished IGF1 production, as circulating and tumor interstitial IGF1 levels decrease by 40–45%. Exogenous IGF1 supplementation restores tumor growth, confirming this dependency. Moreover, MCT4 deficiency enhances antitumor immunity, characterized by increased infiltration of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, NK cells, and macrophages, with a pronounced shift from immunosuppressive M2 to pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages. Consistently, across three independent carcinogenesis models (breast, lung, oral squamous cancers), MCT4-/- mice develop fewer and smaller lesions, underscoring its critical role in tumor incidence. Collectively, these findings identify MCT4 as a key driver of carcinogenesis through IGF1 regulation and immune modulation, highlighting its therapeutic potential.

## Introduction
Highly proliferative cells, including cancerous, embryonic, activated immune, and regenerating tissue cells, preferentially use glycolysis as a primary energy-producing process, leading to the production of a large quantity of lactate as the end product , . Tumor cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) have limited oxygen supply, but even with ample oxygen supply, highly proliferating tumor cells preferentially rely on glycolysis for energy production, a phenomenon widely known as the “Warburg effect” , . Although the amount of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) generated by glycolysis is significantly lower than that produced by oxidative phosphorylation for each glucose molecule, the metabolic flow through the glycolytic pathway, and hence the rate of ATP generation, is much faster, which better suits the high energy demands of rapidly proliferating tumor cells . As glycolytic intermediates are essential raw materials for the synthesis of cellular biomacromolecules, the Warburg effect benefits cancer cells in bioenergetics and biosynthesis .
To avoid intracellular acidification, cancer cells upregulate the expression of monocarboxylate transporters (MCTs) to export lactate into the TME – . MCT1 (encoded by SLC16A1) serves as both a lactate importer and exporter, whereas MCT4 (encoded by SLC16A3) serves primarily as a lactate exporter, even against high lactate concentrations in the TME , . Under physiological conditions, MCT4 expression is primarily limited to highly glycolytic tissues, such as the skeletal muscle, and astrocytes . Overexpression of MCT1 and MCT4 has been reported in various cancers, including breast, colon, pancreatic, and prostate cancers; glioblastoma; and renal cell carcinoma . Upregulated MCT1 and MCT4 expression is strongly correlated with metastasis and poor prognosis in patients with cancer – . Inhibitors to MCT1 and/or MCT4 have shown promise for cancer therapy , ; however, no therapeutics have yet been approved .
The molecular mechanisms of MCT in tumorigenesis and progression remain elusive. We previously expressed MCT1 and MCT4 in non-oncogenic L929 cells and demonstrated that overexpression of MCT4, but not MCT1, enhanced the migration and invasion of non-oncogenic L929 fibroblasts . Furthermore, we identified that MCT4 promotes cell migration by upregulating the expression of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF1) and activating its downstream signaling pathways . IGF1 is a peptide hormone mainly produced by the liver in response to growth hormone. It plays a key role in regulating growth, development, and metabolism by promoting cell proliferation, survival, and differentiation through activation of the IGF1 receptor and downstream PI3K–AKT and MAPK signaling pathways. Circulating IGF1 also mediates many of the systemic effects of growth hormone and is involved in tissue repair, muscle maintenance, and metabolic homeostasis. Other proposed mechanisms include elevated interaction of MCT4 with β1-integrin, which modulates focal adhesions , the promotion of integrin β4 signaling to SRC-FAK and MEK-ERK , the upregulation of invasion-related genes such as VEGF, CD147, MMP2, and MMP9 , and the upregulation of trafficking protein particle complex subunit 5 . However, in vivo studies that verify the various proposed mechanisms are still missing. To address this gap, we constructed MCT4 homozygous knockout (MCT4-/-) BALB/c mice and used wild-type (WT, MCT4+/+) BALB/c mice as a control for in vivo validation, with the aim of investigating the mechanism by which MCT4 promotes tumorigenesis and development.
We also investigated the effect of MCT4 expression on the immune cell population and function in the TME of mice. Metabolic abnormalities of tumor cells have been proposed to reduce immune cell activity in the TME and lead to immune escape of tumor cells . From a nutrient supply perspective, the competitive uptake of key nutrients, such as glucose, amino acids, and fatty acids, by tumor cells may limit nutrient supply to immune cells and disrupt their functions. From a metabolic byproduct perspective, the buildup of lactate results in acidification of the TME, which may disrupt the immune response to tumor cells, such as by inducing apoptosis of naïve T cells and inhibiting NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity – .
Immune evasion is a hallmark of tumors. A correlation between MCT4 and the TME has been well established. LKB1-deficient tumors promote M2 macrophage polarization and dysfunctional T cell proliferation. These effects can be recapitulated by the addition of exogenous lactate and can be abolished through MCT4 knockout or inhibition of the lactic acid receptor GPR81 on immune cells. Notably, MCT4 knockout restores sensitivity to PD-1 blockade, thereby overcoming the resistance induced by LKB1 deficiency . T cell infiltration increases after treatment with an MCT4 inhibitor, leading to an enhanced immune response that improves the efficacy of hepatocellular carcinoma immunotherapy . These findings highlight the intricate relationship between MCT4 and the immune microenvironment; however, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.
Two in vivo tumorigenicity models were employed in this study. One was a allograft tumor model, in which the grafted murine tumor cell line possessed the functional MCT4 gene, whereas the host MCT4-/- mice lacked the MCT4 gene. The other model was the carcinogenesis induction model, in which no functional MCT4 gene existed during tumor incidence and development in MCT4-/- mice. The latter model resembled the process of spontaneous cancer in humans.
In this study, we sought to elucidate the role of MCT4 in tumor incidence and MCT4-/- and WT BALB/c mice. Two complementary models—a allograft tumor model and a chemical carcinogenesis induction model—were employed to assess the impact of MCT4 on tumorigenesis and the immune microenvironment. By integrating genetic models with functional assays, this work aims to clarify the oncogenic potential of MCT4 and provide ninsights into its therapeutic relevance in cancer.

## Results

## MCT4 overexpression in tumor tissues
We analyzed two databases, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC), for MCT4 transcription and expression in different tumors (Supplementary Table  for a list of all tumors). Of the 24 tumors included in TCGA, 18 showed elevated MCT4 transcription compared with their corresponding healthy tissues, four showed lower transcription, and two showed no significant difference (Fig.  ). At the protein level, the expression of MCT4 in all 10 types of tumors included in the CPTAC database was higher than that in their corresponding healthy tissue (Fig.  ). Elevated transcription (Fig.  ) and expression (Fig.  ) were observed in different subtypes of breast cancer, including luminal, Her2-positive, and triple-negative cancers. High MCT4 expression is also correlated with poor prognosis for patients with breast cancer. As illustrated by the Kaplan–Meier Plotter (https://kmplot.com) , patients with elevated MCT4 experienced lower overall survival (OS), distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), post-progression survival (PPS), and recurrence-free survival (RFS) (Fig.  ).And High MCT4 expression is also correlated with poor prognosis for patients with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) (Supplementary Fig.  ). 

## Generation of MCT4-/- mice
MCT4 homozygous knockout (MCT4-/-) BALB/c mice were generated in Beijing Biocytogen Corporation using CRISPR/Cas9-EGE technology, based on the strategies outlined in Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  . Two sgRNAs were designed to knock out approximately 2.0 kb of SLC16A3 spanning from part of exon 3 to exon 5 (Fig.  ). Transcript-201 (NM_030696.3, NP_109621.1, ENSMUST00000070653.13) was used as the template. Following microinjection, 285 zygotes were planted, which resulted in 61 pups, of which 15 were confirmed with designed MCT4 knockout (Supplementary Fig.  ). After subsequent crossbreeding, seven F1 generation mice with heterozygotes and stable genotypes were successfully obtained (Supplementary Fig.  ). Using F1 generation mice with stable genotype heterozygotes for breeding, five F2 homozygous knockout mice were identified and sequenced (Supplementary Fig.  ). F2 offspring were used in subsequent investigations. MCT4-/- mice are similar to WT mice in terms of their regeneration capabilities and lifespan, but two minor differences were observed between the MCT4-/- and WT mice. One was that the weight of both male and female MCT4-/- mice was approximately 9% less than that of their corresponding WT counterparts (Fig.  ). The daily consumption of food and water for both adult male and female MCT4-/- mice was lower than that for WT mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). The other difference was that both male and female adult MCT4-/- mice exhibited higher blood glucose levels than their WT counterparts (Fig.  ). 

## MCT4 homozygous knockout mitigates allograft 4T1 tumor growth and metastasis in mice
We previously used murine L929 cells to demonstrate that MCT4 overexpression enhances the migration and invasion of L929 cells . The 4T1 cell line is a highly metastatic murine breast cancer model derived from a BALB/c mouse mammary tumor. Based on the growth capability of 4T1 cells in mice compared with L929 cells, we used 4T1 cells in the allograft study. We confirmed that 4T1 behaved similarly to L929 when it overexpressed MCT4 (Supplementary Fig.  ), and MCT4 in MDA-MB-231 cells deletion similarly down regulates IGF1 expression (Supplementary Fig.  ).
The 4T1 cells were injected subcutaneously into both WT and MCT4-/- mice. The growth of 4T1 tumors was significantly slower in both female and male MCT4-/- mice than in their WT counterparts (Fig.  ). On day 21 after transplantation, the tumor size was approximately 40% smaller in MCT4-/- mice. Similar results were observed when comparing fluorescence intensity through in vivo imaging of female mice implanted with 4T1-Luc cells, a cell line that overexpresses luciferase in 4T1. The average radiance of 4T1 tumors in MCT4-/- mice was significantly lower than WT mice (Fig.  ). Immunofluorescence analysis indicated that the expression of Ki67 and CD31 in 4T1 tumors from WT mice was higher than that observed in MCT4-/- mice, suggesting more pronounced tumor growth and angiogenesis in WT mice (Fig.  ). The MCT4-/- 4T1 tumor-bearing mice also lived longer than their WT counterparts. WT mice started to die 21 days after implantation of 4T1 cells, while MCT4-/- mice started to die 2 weeks later (Fig.  ). To study the effect of MCT4 expression on metastasis, 4T1-Luc cells were injected into the tail vein of female mice. Figure  g, h indicates that both WT and MCT4-/- mice developed lung metastasis; however, the average radiance intensity of lung metastasis in MCT4-/- mice was 43% lower (Fig.  ). Next, we examined MCT4 expression in 4T1 tumor tissues in WT and MCT4-/- mice. Compared with the control 4T1 cells with low MCT4 expression, MCT4 levels were significantly elevated in 4T1 tumors of WT mice, whereas MCT4 expression remained low in MCT4-/- mice (Fig.  ). Also, we performed western blotting analysis on key organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, stomach, endothelium, muscle) from tumor-bearing WT and MCT4-/- mice. As expected, MCT4 protein was not detectable in these non-glycolytic tissues in WT mice, but expression of MCT4 was observed in endothelial and muscle tissues, and its expression was absent in all tissues from MCT4-/- mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). The 4T1 tumor tissue itself showed a clear upregulation of MCT4 compared to the in vitro cultured cells. And to address MCT4 expression in the tumor, we performed Western blotting analysis on both superficial and deep tumor tissues, using a 5 mm depth as the boundary. MCT4 expression was detected in both the deep tissues and the tumor surface (a site prone to immune infiltration) (Supplementary Fig.  ). Concurrently, we conducted flow cytometry on tumor tissues. The results showed that 4T1 tumors from both MCT4-/- and WT mice formed similar cell clusters without distinct subpopulations. However, a clear separation into distinct subpopulations only appeared after MCT4 staining (Supplementary Fig.  ). 
A second cell line, M109 cells (a mouse lung cancer cell line), was used to confirm the findings of the 4T1 allograft study. Similar to the 4T1 study, the M109 allograft tumor size was approximately 40% smaller in MCT4-/- mice (Fig.  ). Furthermore, similar to the 4T1 study, the expression of MCT4 was significantly elevated in M109 tumors in WT mice, whereas MCT4 expression remained low in MCT4-/- mice (Fig.  ).

## Low IGF1 concentrations contribute to slower tumor growth in MCT4-/- mice
Serum IGF1 concentration in MCT4-/- mice was approximately 45% lower than that in WT mice under physiological conditions (Fig.  ). Meanwhile, the serum lactate concentration in MCT4-/- mice was approximately 12% lower than that in WT mice (Fig.  ). After 4T1 implantation, the IGF1 and lactate concentrations in the tumor interstitial fluid (TIF) were significantly lower (by approximately 40%) in MCT4-/- mice (Fig.  ). 
During the experiment, it was verified that injecting IGF1 can restore the concentration to a level similar to that in WT mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). Then when IGF1 or lactate was supplemented in MCT4-/- mice, tumor growth was partially restored, although not at the same rate as in WT mice (Fig.  ). Our findings indicate that IGF1 and lactate partially contribute to the function of MCT4 in promoting tumor growth.

## MCT4-/- mice exhibit a stronger anti-tumor immune response than WT
SLC transporters play an irreplaceable role in immune cell development, homeostasis, differentiation, and activation by regulating key physiological processes such as nutrient absorption, ion balance, and waste elimination . So, we analyzed the correlation between SLC16A3 and immunity using SangerBox 3.0(http://sangerbox.com) . It revealed that SLC16A3 is significantly correlated with multiple immune regulatory genes and immune checkpoint genes across various cancers. Significant correlations were observed in most tumors about the infiltration scores for B cell, T cell CD4, T cell CD8, Neutrophil, Macrophage, and DC. SLC16A3 showing a significant positive correlation with M2 macrophages in BRCA (Supplementary Fig.  ). Immune cell populations were first analyzed in healthy WT and MCT4-/- mice and then in WT and MCT4-/- mice bearing 4T1 tumors. We observed no significant differences in the peripheral blood populations of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and macrophages between WT and MCT4-/- mice under normal physiological conditions (Fig.  ). Upon 4T1 tumor implantation, MCT4-/- mice exhibited a marked increase in peripheral blood immune cell populations, with CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and macrophages increasing by approximately 30%, 54%, 63%, and 18%, respectively (Fig.  ). Similarly, after implantation of 4T1 tumors, MCT4-/- mice showed significant increases in immune cell populations within the tumor, with CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and macrophages increasing by approximately 38%, 56%, 36%, and 28%, respectively (Fig.  ). Enhanced infiltration of these immune cells in tumors of MCT4-/- mice was further validated using IF microscopy (Fig.  ). Gating strategies for all major immune populations are provided in Supplementary Fig.  . 
M1 polarized macrophages exert anti-tumor effects, whereas M2 polarized macrophages promote an immunosuppressive environment and are believed to facilitate tumor growth . Macrophage polarization was assessed in both peripheral blood and tumor-infiltrating tissues. Under physiological conditions, no significant differences in M1 and M2 polarization were observed between WT and MCT4-/- mice (Fig.  ). However, after 4T1 transplantation, MCT4-/- mice exhibited a 60% increase in M1 polarized macrophages and a 40% decrease in the M2 population compared with WT mice (Fig.  ). Similarly, tumor tissues exhibited a 20% increase in infiltrating M1 macrophages and an 11% decrease in M2 macrophages (Fig.  ). The decreased expression of CD206, an M2 macrophage marker, as revealed by IF confirmed the reduced infiltration of M2 macrophages in the TME of MCT4-/- mice (Fig.  ). To investigate potential factors that influence macrophage polarization, a murine macrophage cell line, RAW264.7, was cultured with and without IGF1 or lactate supplementation. When RAW264.7 cells were cultured with conditioned medium from 4T1 cells supplemented with IGF1 or lactate, the surface expression of CD206 was elevated by 28% and 10%, respectively (Fig.  ). After RAW264.7 polarized to M2 cells, IGF1 expression was upregulated (Supplementary Fig.  ). Chemokines regulate the differentiation and infiltration of immunosuppressive, pro-tumorigenic cells into the tumor, specifically regulatory T cells (Tregs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and tumor-associated macrophages(TAMs) – . We screened for chemokines with significant differences in expression between 4T1 and 4T1-MCT4 cells. The transcription of multiple chemokines showed significant variation, especially for CCL28, which was upregulated by approximately 50-fold in 4T1-MCT4 cells (Fig.  , j). CCL28 regulates the distribution of immune cells and promotes tumor development primarily by affecting Tregs and MDSCs , . The concentration of CCL28 in the culture supernatant of 4T1-MCT4 cells was elevated by 53% compared with that of 4T1 cells (Fig.  ), while MCT4 deletion reduced it in MDA-MB-231 cell (Supplementary Fig.  ). We confirmed using MCT4 inhibitor (VB124) in 4T1-MCT4 cells that pharmacological or genetic inhibition of MCT4 reduces CCL28 secretion. And siRNA interference of CCL28 also did not affect the expression of MCT4(Supplementary Fig.  ). For MCT4-/- mice, their serum CCL28 concentration was 52% lower than that of WT mice (Fig.  ). In 4T1 allograft mice, the CCL28 concentration in the TIF showed a consistent trend, with a 35% reduction compared with that in WT mice (Fig.  ). The population of MDSCs, defined as CD45.2+ cells, was studied to investigate the downstream effects of different concentrations of CCL28 in MCT4-/- and WT mice. In 4T1 allograft MCT4-/- mice, the MDSCs population was reduced by 32% in the peripheral blood and by 9% within tumors compared with 4T1 allograft WT mice (Fig.  ).

## MCT4-/- mice develop fewer tumors and metastases than WT mice in carcinogenesis induction models
In addition to employing allograft tumor models to investigate the role of MCT4 in tumor incidence and metastasis, we used carcinogenesis induction models to simulate natural tumor occurrence. WT and MCT4-/- mice were fed carcinogens, including 7,12-dimethylbenz[a]anthracene (DMBA), to induce breast cancer; urethane to induce lung cancer; and 4-nitroquinoline N-oxide (4NQO) to induce oral squamous cell carcinoma (Fig.  , k). 
In the DMBA-induced breast cancer model, all six WT mice developed tumors, whereas in MCT4-/- mice, tumors were developed in five of seven mice (Table  ), with the remaining two exhibiting only subcutaneous tissue hyperplasia (Fig.  ). Regarding tumor metastasis, all six WT mice developed metastatic lesions, with an average of 2.3 lesions per mouse, primarily in the heart, lungs, and intestines. In contrast, only two MCT4-/- mice developed metastasis with one and two lesions in the heart and lungs, respectively (Table  ; Fig.  ). In the Urethane- induced lung cancer model, lung cancer lesions developed in both WT and MCT4-/- mice (Fig.  ), but the number of tumor lesions was only 11 on average in MCT4-/- mice compared with 25 on average in WT mice (Fig.  ). Additionally, lesions occupied an average of 29.5% of the lung surface in WT mice but only 13% of the lung surface in MCT4-/- mice (Fig.  ). Hematoxylin and eosin staining (H&E) analysis was performed on lung tissue sections. In the absence of urethane induction, no significant differences were observed between lung sections of WT and MCT4-/- mice, however, WT mice developed more extensive and larger tumor lesions following urethane treatment (Fig.  ). The expression of Ki67 and CD31 in the lung tissues of urethane-treated WT mice was higher than that in MCT4-/- mice, indicating higher proliferative activity and vascularization in WT mice (Fig.  ). In the 4NQO- induced oral squamous cell cancer study, all mice developed oral squamous cell cancer, but the lesions were larger in WT mice (Fig.  ). The observation was confirmed by staining of the tongue sections. After 4NQO induction, WT mice developed more extensive lesions (Fig.  ). The expression of Ki67 and CD31 in the tongue tissues of WT mice was also more pronounced after 4NQO induction (Fig.  ). 

## Discussion
MCT4 overexpression has been shown to be closely associated with malignant progression and poor prognosis in various cancers. In this study, we established an MCT4 knockout (MCT4-/-) BALB/c mouse model to elucidate the mechanisms of MCT4 in tumorigenesis and progression.
Mechanistically, MCT4 promotes tumor incidence and progression by upregulating IGF1 expression. We demonstrate that MCT4 deficiency significantly reduces IGF1 concentrations in both peripheral blood and TIF, resulting in delayed tumor growth. Exogenous IGF1 supplementation partially restored tumor growth in MCT4-/- mice, indicating that IGF1 is a mediator of MCT4-driven oncogenic effects. IGF1 regulates cellular proliferation, metabolism, and differentiation, and its deficiency has been linked to growth retardation in mice and humans , . Indeed, MCT4-/- mice display slightly reduced body weight and elevated blood glucose levels, likely due to diminished IGF1 signaling. Exogenous IGF1 supplementation partially rescued growth in MCT4-/- mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). Our data suggest that IGF1 signaling contributes substantially to cell proliferation in MCT4-deficient conditions, but IGF1 and lactate treatment does not fully rescue the wild-type phenotype. The partial rescue by IGF1 alone is indeed likely because MCT4 deletion affects tumor growth through TWO parallel axes: 1) the IGF1-mediated growth/survival axis, and 2) the CCL28-mediated immune suppression axis. IGF1 promotes glucose uptake and glycolysis in cancer cells, leading to lactate production. We supplemented IGF1 in mice and measured serum lactate levels. Results indicated that while IGF1 supplementation did not alter lactate levels in WT mice, it transiently restored them in MCT4-/- mice. Conversely, lactate supplementation in MCT4-/- and WT mice led to a transient decrease in serum IGF1 levels (Supplementary Fig.  ). In this study, our findings indicate that altered IGF1 expression downstream of MCT4 deficiency is associated with at least two major biological consequences. Another effect is to modulate systemic glucose metabolism by promoting glycogen uptake and biosynthesis. Thus, MCT4-mediated upregulation of IGF1 represents a nexus between tumor progression and metabolic homeostasis.
In addition to upregulating growth factor expression, MCT4 contributes to a tumor-friendly environment through immune suppression. Immunoprofiling of transplanted tumors reveals that MCT4 knockout enhances anti-tumor immunity, characterized by increased infiltration of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and macrophage polarization toward pro-inflammatory M1 subtypes. The strong anti-tumor environment in MCT4-/- mice correlates with decreased levels of multiple chemokines, including CCL28. CCL28 recruits MDSCs into the TME via CCR10, thereby dampening effector immune responses , – . Our in vivo and in vitro results are the first to provide evidence that MCT4 expression is positively associated with and functionally influences CCL28 expression. The expression of MCT4 might foster an immunosuppressive niche through the CCL28-MDSC axis, accelerating tumor progression.
However, further investigations are warranted to delineate the molecular interactions and explore how to regulate the expression of CCL28. We analyzed the human CCL28 core promoter using the EPDnew (Eukaryotic Promoter Database) Search Motif tool of library “Transcription Factor Motifs (JASPAR CORE 2018 vertebrates)”. The queried region spanned −1000 to +100 bp relative to the annotated transcription start site (TSS). Based on the significance threshold of P < 0.001, the EPDnew identified three significant HIF1A motif matches within the CCL28 promoter region, located at positions +31, ‑58, and ‑61 relative to the transcription start site (TSS) (Supplementary Fig.  ). This suggests the possible presence of a HIF‑1α response element in this region, and that the transcription factor HIF‑1α may be involved in regulating CCL28 expression. Further verification requires further research and discussion.
In summary, our study positions MCT4 at the intersection of three important tumor-related properties, namely metabolic reprogramming, growth factor regulation, and immune remodeling. Figure  shows a preliminary mechanism of MCT4 expression on tumor incidence and progression. MCT4 exerts a multifaceted influence on the TME by regulating both IGF1-driven tumorigenesis and progression and CCL28-mediated immune suppression. This dual regulatory capacity underscores the unique therapeutic potential of MCT4 as a target capable of disrupting multiple tumor-promoting pathways. 
Furthermore, we identify the co-expressed genes of MCT4 using TCGA-BRCA omics data to investigate how MCT4 regulates the expression of IGF1 and CCL28, and to indicate more clues of evidence. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses of these genes revealed significant enrichment in pathways directly relevant to our study, including “carbohydrate kinase activity,” “leukocyte migration,” “mitochondrial protein complex,” and “receptor ligand activity,” and “carbohydrate binding,” and “neutrophil mediated immunity,” and “response to interferon-gamma” (Supplementary Fig.  and Supplementary Data  ). This bioinformatic evidence independently supports our experimental findings linking MCT4 to metabolic reprogramming, hypoxia response (a potential link to IGF1 regulation), and immune modulation (via chemokines like CCL28). These TCGA-based analyses provide complementary, hypothesis-generating evidence supporting the functional programs associated with MCT4 expression and help to contextualize our experimental findings within a broader transcriptional landscape.
MCT4, as a therapeutic target simultaneously blocking multiple tumor-promoting pathways, also requires significant consideration for its safety. MCT4-/- mice have a normal lifespan, fertility, and immune system development, with only modest reductions in body weight and mild hyperglycemia that may be mediated by decrease IGF1. These phenotypes are in agreement with the observations in patients with congenital IGF1 deficiency syndromes (e.g., Laron syndrome), who display reduced cancer incidence – . This underscores the safety of MCT4 inhibition when a chemical entity or an antibody is used to block the function of MCT4 for cancer treatment, especially in adults. Nevertheless, potential metabolic side effects, particularly in pediatric or diabetic populations, warrant careful clinical evaluation. Given the limited success of prior IGF pathway inhibitors due to toxicity and suboptimal efficacy – , MCT4-targeted strategies hold promises to overcome these barriers by attenuating IGF1 activity and reshaping tumor immunity.
It should be noted that the current research design has certain limitations. Firstly, while our in vitro findings show IGF1 can induce M2-like macrophage polarization, demonstrating that this mechanism directly drives the altered immune landscape in MCT4-/- mice would necessitate in vivo rescue experiments where IGF1 is restored in the knockout TME. Our data position IGF1 downregulation as a consequence of MCT4 loss and a potential mediator of the altered immune landscape, rather than definitively proving it as the sole effector in vivo. Future studies employing inducible IGF1 expression systems or localized IGF1 delivery in MCT4-KO tumor models will be crucial to dissect the precise functional weight of IGF1 within the broader metabolic-immune regulatory network governed by MCT4. Secondly, we state that the observed phenotypes could result from a combination of tumor cell-extrinsic (systemic IGF1 reduction, developmental immune priming) and intrinsic effects, and that future studies using tissue-specific knockout models will be valuable to dissect these contributions. And in carcinogen models, we acknowledge that differences in carcinogen metabolism or systemic immune status in MCT4-/- mice could contribute to the observed reduction in tumor incidence. However, the primary and robust finding of our study remains a significantly reduced tumor burden in the knockout model under standardized conditions. This provides a strong foundation for future mechanistic studies to precisely dissect the relative contributions of these potential factors.

## Methods

## Expression and clinical correlation analysis of MCT4 (SLC16A3) across cancers
UALCAN (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu) is an open platform designed for user-friendly analysis of data from TCGA and CPTAC databases. We used UALCAN to conduct a pan-cancer analysis of SLC16A3 at both the transcriptional and protein levels, using TCGA and CPTAC data, respectively, with the gene specified as “SLC16A3” and the assay set to “Pan-Cancer Review.” To further investigate the expression of SLC16A3 in breast cancer, the cancer type was set to “breast cancer,” and the assay method to “expression assay.” Patients with breast carcinoma in TCGA and CPTAC were further stratified by stage and molecular subtype to assess differences in expression among these groups.
The Kaplan–Meier plotter (www.kmplot.com) is a web-based database that is used to assess the prognostic value of genes in various types of cancer. It integrates gene chip and RNA-Seq data from GEO, EGA, TCGA, and other public sources, enabling the evaluation of 70,632 genes across 21 cancer types, including breast (6234 cases), ovarian (2190 cases), lung (3452 cases), and gastric cancer (1440 cases). In this study, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used to assess the prognostic value of MCT4 in breast cancer by analyzing its association with OS, DMFS, progression-free survival, and RFS. Hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals and log-rank p-values were calculated to quantify the results.

WT (MCT4+/+) BALB/c mice were purchased from Liaoning Changsheng Biotechnology Co, Ltd. under the animal license SCXK (Liao) 2020-0001. MCT4 homozygous knockout (MCT4-/-) BALB/c mice were developed by Beijing Biocytogen using CRISPR/Cas9-EGE technology, under the animal license SCXK (Jing) 2020-0007. SLC16A3 knockout alleles were generated by microinjection of in vitro-transcribed guide ribonucleic acids (gRNAs) containing the protospacer sequences 5′Guide #1 (5′-GACGCTGACCGCCTTGGGGA AGG) and 3′Guide #2 (5′-GCTTGACACCTTTACACACG GGG). PCR primers flanking the target site (WT-F 5′-GGCTGAGATGGTCACCCTGGCAACC; WT-F1 5′-TCCTGCTTGAGCCCAAAGTCACTG; WT-R 5′-GAGGGTATAAGGAGAGCAGATAGGG; WT-R1 5′-TGGGGTGTCAGGTCCTTAGTGCAGG) and a primer within the knockout sequence (Mut-R: 5’-GAGGGTATAAGGAGAGCAGATAGGG) were used to amplify and identify the modified alleles.
Mice were housed in individually ventilated cages under the specific pathogen-free facility of Shenyang Pharmaceutical University. They were housed in groups on a 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water at 20–22 °C. All experiments were performed during the day cycle. Unless otherwise specified, both male and female mice were included in the analysis, and all mice were 5–6 weeks old. Mice were randomly assigned to groups.

## Cell culture
The 4T1 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). Stable 4T1 cells overexpressing MCT4 (4T1-MCT4) were generated via plasmid transfection by electroporation, as previously described . The 4T1-Luc cells were purchased from Hunan Fenghui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hunan, China). Professor Cong Luo (Shenyang Pharmaceutical University) provided RAW264.7 cells. All cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. M109 cells obtained from Shanghai Guan&Dao Biological Engineering Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cell lines were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

## Food and water intake and body weight monitoring
WT and MCT4-/- mice (4 weeks old; seven males and seven females) were used in the experiments. The weekly food and water intake was recorded to calculate the daily consumption per mouse, and body weight changes were monitored to assess the effect of MCT4 knockout on BALB/c mice.

## Glucose tolerance test
Three WT and MCT4-/- mice of each sex were randomly selected to undergo a 12-h fasting period before participating in the intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test. A bolus of glucose (2 g/kg) was injected intraperitoneally. The blood glucose levels of each mouse were measured at 0, 15, 30, 60, and 120 min.

## Tumor allograft model
To establish 4T1 and M109 tumor models, 3 × 106 cells (0.1 mL) were subcutaneously injected into the right upper abdomen of 6-week-old WT and MCT4-/- mice (four males and four females per genotype). The long and short diameters of the tumor were recorded every 7 days using digital calipers, and the tumor size was determined using the formula (width2 × length)/2. In growth curve studies, the animals were sacrificed 21 days after implantation when the tumor was growing exponentially. Female mice were transplanted with 4T1-Luc cells using a consistent protocol, followed by in vivo imaging and quantification of average fluorescence intensity to assess differences in tumor growth. To document the survival duration of tumor-bearing mice, the animals were monitored daily over 40 days, with survival analysis conducted at the conclusion of the observation period.
The effect of IGF1 and lactate on tumor growth was assessed using 4T1 allograft models. Briefly, IGF1 or lactate was administered to MCT4-/- mice via subcutaneous injection at a dosage of respectively 3 and 15 mg/kg on days 7, 10, 13, 16, 19, and 22 after engraftment. The tumor size was determined using the formula (width2 × length)/2; mice were euthanized when the tumors were ulcerated or when the tumor volume reached 1500 mm3.

## Breast cancer-based lung metastasis model
To generate a breast cancer mouse model, 4T1-Luc cells (1 × 106 cells/mouse) were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and slowly injected into the tail vein of 6-week-old female WT and MCT4-/- mice divided into two groups of four. Three weeks after injection, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and administered D-fluorescein potassium salt. Subsequently, in vivo imaging was performed, and the mean fluorescence values of the lung metastases were quantified.

## Protein extraction and western blotting
Cells or tissues were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay kit (Cat. No. AR0197A, Wuhan Boster Biological Technology, Ltd.).
To perform western blot analysis, lysates (15–50 µg/lane) were resolved using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis with a 12% gel. The resolved proteins were transferred to a 0.45-µm polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Cat. No. IPFL00010; Merck KGaA). Subsequently, the membrane was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature to reduce background staining. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (anti-MCT4, Cat. No. 22787-1-AP; anti-beta actin, Cat. No. 66009-1-Ig; Proteintech). After washing with TBST, horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse, Cat. No. 31430; anti-rabbit, Cat. No. 31460; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (Cat. No. KGP1127, Nanjing KeyGen Biotech Co., Ltd.) with 5200 Multi Automatic Chemiluminescence (Tanon Science and Technology Co., Ltd.).

## TIF isolation
TIF was isolated from tumors using a previously described centrifugal method . Tumor-bearing animals were euthanized via cervical dislocation, and the tumors were rapidly excised. Following dissection, the tumors were weighed, rinsed briefly in saline (150 mM NaCl) at room temperature, and blotted on filter paper. An equivalent mass of tumor tissue was placed into 1.5-mL conical tubes and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. TIF was collected from the conical tube and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

## Measurement of lactate and IGF1
The lactate concentration in the culture supernatant, serum, and TIF was measured using the L-lactate detection kit (Cat. No. A09-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bio-Engineering Institute) following the manufacturer’s protocol. A Mouse IGF1 ELISA Kit (Cat. No. EK0378, Wuhan Boster Bio-Engineering Co., Ltd) was used to quantify the IGF1 concentration in these samples using a double-sandwich method.

## Analysis of lymphocyte subsets
The populations of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and macrophages were analyzed using fluorescence-activated cell sorting. For immune cell population analysis in peripheral blood, approximately 300 µL of blood was collected from the retro-orbital sinus of mice into heparinized tubes. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using the mouse peripheral blood lymphocyte isolation kit (Cat. No. C009, Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The staining protocol for peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets was as follows: anti-CD45-FITC/CD3-APC/CD4-PE antibody for labeling CD4+ T cells; anti-CD45-FITC/CD3-APC/CD8-PE antibody for labeling CD8+ T cells; anti-CD45-FITC/CD3-APC/CD49b-PE antibody for labeling NK lymphocytes; anti-CD45-FITC/CD11b-APC/F4/80-PE antibody for labeling macrophages; anti-F4/80-PE/CD80-FITC antibody for labeling M1-polarized macrophages; anti-F4/80-PE/CD206-FITC antibody for labeling M2-polarized macrophages; and anti-CD45-FITC/F4/80-PE/ Ly6G Ly6C-APC antibody for labeling MDSCs. The antibodies used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table  . The stained cells were washed, resuspended in 0.1% BSA-containing PBS, and analyzed using a BD FACSAria™ III flow cytometer (BD Biosciences). To analyze immune cell populations in fresh tumors, single cells were isolated using collagenase IV and stained, and the immune cell populations in peripheral blood were analyzed using the same methodology.

## In vitro macrophage polarization experiments
The effect of MCT4 on macrophage polarization was investigated using RAW264.7 cells. The cells were adjusted to a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium and treated with IL-4 (100 ng/mL) to induce M2 polarization. Subsequently, the cells were cultured for 24 h with 4T1 culture supernatant, 4T1-MCT4 culture supernatant, and 4T1 culture supernatant containing IGF1 (300 ng/mL)/lactate (10 mM) for 24 h. Anti-mouse CD206-APC antibody was used to assess M2 polarization. The stained cells were washed, resuspended in 0.1% BSA-containing PBS, and analyzed using a BD FACSAria™ III flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

## Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)
Total RNA was extracted from 4T1 and 4T1-MCT4 cells using TRIzol Universal Reagent (Cat No. DP424, Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd.). Complementary DNA was synthesized from the RNA using the HiFiScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Cat No. CW2569, CoWin Biosciences) under the following conditions: 42 °C for 15 min and 85 °C for 5 min. RT-qPCR analysis was then performed using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Cat No. RR820A, TAKARA) on a CFX96 system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc.) with the appropriate primers (Supplementary Table  ). The RT-qPCR conditions were as follows: initial activation at 95 °C for 3 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing at 60 °C for 30 s. mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt relative quantification method . The expression level of each target gene was normalized to that of β-actin mRNA in the same sample.

## Breast, lung, and oral carcinogenesis induction mouse model
WT and MCT4-/- mice (6 weeks old) were used to establish models for tumor carcinogenesis induction. In the breast cancer induction model , mice were injected subcutaneously with 100 μL of DMBA (2 mg/mL) every 2 weeks over a total duration of 20 weeks, followed by 8 weeks of plain water supplementation. In the lung cancer induction model , mice received intraperitoneal injections of 100 μL of urethane (100 mg/mL) weekly for 18 weeks, followed by plain water supplementation for 7 weeks. In the oral carcinogenesis induction model , mice were fed 4NQO at a final concentration of 50 μg/mL in their drinking water for 22 weeks, followed by 7 weeks of plain water supplementation. The lesions were examined weekly, and their weight was monitored as an indicator of pain. Mice were sacrificed at the end of the experiment.

## Hematoxylin and eosin staining
Fresh tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by gradient alcohol dehydration and embedding in paraffin. Sections (5 μm) were dewaxed in xylene, rehydrated using graded alcohol, and heat-treated at 62 °C for 2 h. After antigen retrieval, the slides were stained with hematoxylin, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol, and counterstained with 5% eosin. Dehydration was performed using graded alcohol (80%, 95%, and 100%) and xylene, followed by neutral gum mounting. A Nikon ECLIPSE-E200 microscope was used for histological analysis; three non-overlapping cortical fields were randomly selected for imaging.

## Immunofluorescence (IF)
Fresh tumor tissues were fixed in a 4% polymethyl aldehyde solution for 24 h. All samples were paraffin-embedded and sectioned. Briefly, 5-μm tumor sections were deparaffinized, subjected to antigen retrieval, blocked, and stained with primary antibodies. Subsequently, the sections were stained with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies, and the nuclei were stained with DAPI. Random observations of the tumor tissue cortex were performed at 400× magnification using a fluorescence microscope (NIM900 Ningbo Yongxin Optics Co., Ltd.).

## Statistical analysis
Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo 10.8.1 software. Data representation, statistical testing, and outlier identification were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. The results are represented as the mean ± standard error of the mean. Two-tailed unpaired t-tests were used to compare results between two groups. One- or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used to compare results among more than two groups. Correlation was assessed by Pearson’s coefficient. P-values < 0.05 were considered to indicate a statistically significant difference. Exact p-values and biological replicates (N) are reported in the figure legends.

## Ethics statement
All animal experiments performed in this study were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shenyang Pharmaceutical University (Approval Number: SYXK [Liao] 2021-0009). This study was conducted in strict compliance with the institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.
Animal Welfare and Euthanasia: All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and distress. Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under a 12-hour light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. At the experimental endpoints, animals were euthanized humanely. The primary method of euthanasia for tissue collection was cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia induced by isoflurane inhalation (5% for induction, 1.5–2% for maintenance). For in vivo imaging procedures requiring terminal anesthesia, euthanasia was ensured by an overdose of isoflurane followed by cervical dislocation as a secondary physical method. All procedures were performed by trained personnel.
Tumor Burden Monitoring: The maximal tumor volume permitted by our approved protocol was 1500 mm³. Tumor size was measured every 2-3 days using a digital caliper, and volume was calculated using the formula: (width² × length)/2. Animals were monitored daily for signs of distress (e.g., lethargy, hunched posture, difficulty moving, or ulceration). In no instance throughout this study was the maximal permitted tumor volume exceeded. Any animal showing signs of significant discomfort or reaching the predefined humane endpoint (tumor volume ≥ 1500 mm³ or ulceration) was euthanized immediately and excluded from subsequent longitudinal analyses.
