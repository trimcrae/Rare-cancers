## Background
Interstitial lung disease (ILD) is a common and severe comorbidity of rheumatoid arthritis (RA), yet reliable diagnostic biomarkers remain lacking. Alveolar type II (ATII) cells are widely recognized to play a key role in the pathogenesis of ILD. This study first aimed to screen and validate potential autoantigens involved in RA-ILD using ATII cells.

## Methods
Sera from multiple patient groups were subjected to immunoprecipitation with ATII cells and analyzed for candidate autoantigens related to RA-ILD through proteomic methods. Antibody concentrations in the sera were subsequently measured using protein chips and ELISA. Expression levels of target antigens in the lung tissues of collagen-induced arthritis-bleomycin (CIA-BLM) mice were evaluated via immunohistochemistry, while their expression in ATII-BLM cells was assessed using indirect immunofluorescence. The diagnostic value of these antibodies was further examined by ROC curve analysis. Preliminary studies also explored the cellular mechanisms involved.

## Results
We identified exosome component 4 (EXOSC4) as a potential autoantigen in RA-ILD, with significantly higher levels of EXOSC4 antibodies found in the sera of RA-ILD patients. Moreover, EXOSC4 expression was elevated in the lung tissues of CIA-BLM mice and in ATII-BLM cells. Clinical assessments showed that the area under the curve (AUC) for EXOSC4 antibody in diagnosing RA-ILD was 0.809, increasing to 0.890 (95% CI:0.812–0.967) when combined with other clinical indicators. Notably, EXOSC4, Wnt 3a, and β-catenin expression were significantly upregulated in ATII-BLM cells as compared to ATII cells. Additionally, we found a significant interaction between naïve CD4 + T cells and ATII cells within the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, suggesting a possible mechanistic role of EXOSC4 in RA-ILD pathogenesis.

## Conclusion
We identified EXOSC4 as a novel autoantigen associated with RA-ILD, which may assist in its clinical diagnosis.

## Introduction
RA is a systemic inflammatory autoimmune disorder, with approximately 50% of RA patients developing extra-articular manifestations, the most common of which is ILD [ – ]. Reported incidence rates of RA-ILD vary widely, ranging from 2 to 58%, likely due to differences in patient populations and diagnostic standards across studies [ – ]. RA-ILD is marked by a progressive decline in lung function and a higher risk of infections, both of which substantially raise mortality among affected individuals [ , , ]. Evidence suggests that mortality rates in RA-ILD are 2 to 10 times higher than in RA patients without ILD [ , ]. A systematic review encompassing 78 studies reported a median survival of 2 to 14 years for RA-ILD patients, with 5 to 10 year survival rates at roughly 50% [ ]. Early diagnosis plays an essential role in recognizing disease progression and refining treatment approaches, which can improve quality of life and survival [ ].
Clinical laboratory science is crucial for modern healthcare, enabling the accurate and timely diagnosis of diseases [ ]. At present, high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) is regarded as the gold standard for diagnosing and monitoring the progression of RA-ILD. HRCT findings indicate that over 50% of patients with RA exhibit lung abnormalities, with approximately 10% advancing to clinically significant ILD [ ]. However, by the time HRCT detects RA-ILD, lung function may already be impaired, complicating early identification and delaying intervention. The broader application of HRCT is further limited by concerns regarding ionizing radiation and financial cost. As a result, there is growing interest in alternative diagnostic tools, including lung ultrasound [ – ], biomarkers [ – ], and novel autoantibodies [ – ].
Autoantibodies play a key role in the diagnosis, classification, and monitoring of rheumatic immune diseases. They act as biomarkers that help clinicians confirm disease presence and also reflect disease activity and prognosis. For example, anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies can be detected in the early stages of RA and are strong, independent predictors of early radiologic progression in RA [ , ]. Anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB antibodies exhibit a strong association with Sjögren’s syndrome (SS), whereas anti-DNA topoisomerase I antibodies are frequently observed in patients with scleroderma [ , ].In the context of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), the concentration of anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) antibodies typically correlates closely with disease activity [ ]. Early identification of RA-ILD through autoantibody testing is vital for prompt treatment, yet few studies have explored autoantigen screening specifically for RA-ILD. It is necessary to conduct relevant research [ ].
The pathogenesis of ILD is closely associated with ATII cells [ ]. Under normal physiological conditions, ATII cells possess both self-renewal and differentiation capacity, allowing them to transform into alveolar type I epithelial cells, which are essential for preserving the structure and function of the alveolar epithelium. In disease states, damaged ATII cells undergo significant changes in phenotype and function, including activation and the release of a range of cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors. These factors stimulate lung fibroblasts, triggering their proliferation and transformation into myofibroblasts [ ]. The accumulation and persistent activation of myofibroblasts lead to excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition and the progression of pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [ ]. Recent findings indicate that immunoglobulin A (IgA) secreted by ATII cells is captured by the ECM, exacerbating lung fibrosis through the activation of fibroblasts [ ]. These findings imply that ILD-associated autoantibodies may originate in ATII cells. However, no studies have yet screened for autoantigens in ATII cells.
Here, we Co-immunoprecipitated (Co-IP) sera from different patients with ATII cells and subsequently screened for potential autoantigens in RA-ILD by Pro data-independent acquisition (DIA) quantitative proteomics. To verify the reliability of autoantigen, we assessed the levels of relevant antibody in patient sera by protein chips and ELISA, and further validated autoantigen expression in both in vivo and in vitro experiments. Additionally, we assessed the diagnostic capability of relevant antibody for RA-ILD using ROC curve. Finally, single-cell sequencing was employed to explore the associated cellular mechanisms.

## Participants and methods

## Participants
All RA patients met the 2010 American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) criteria for RA [ ], and RA-ILD diagnoses were confirmed by high-resolution chest CT scans. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Guang’anmen Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (2023-203-KY), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.
Serum samples were collected at Guang’anmen Hospital and included 37 RA-non-ILD patients, 40 RA-ILD patients, and 37 healthy controls (HC). Five samples from each group were used for Co-IP, three RA-ILD samples for single-cell sequencing, and the remaining 32 samples per group were used for ELISA validation.

## Animals and modeling
DBA/1 female mice (16–18 g, 7 weeks old; Viton Lihua, Beijing, China) were used for the study. Eighteen mice were randomly divided into 3 groups (control group, CIA group, and CIA-BLM group, with 6 mice in each group. Briefly, the mice were housed under standard conditions with free access to water and food. Bovine type II collagen (Chondrex, USA) and complete Fuchs’ adjuvant (Chondrex, USA) were mixed in equal proportions, and the collagen emulsion was prepared on ice at a concentration of 2 mg/mL. In addition to the 6 mice in the NC group, 12 mice were injected subcutaneously with 200 µL of collagen emulsion at the root of the tail to construct the CIA model, and the initial immunization was recorded on day 0, followed by a booster vaccination on day 7 with an equivalent dose of the same modeling agent, avoiding the site of the initial immunization model. Six mice in the CIA-BLM group were injected with bleomycin solution 2 mg/kg via a tracheal drip on day 1. After 30 days of normal rearing, the mice were euthanized, and then lung tissues were obtained for histological analysis and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. Approval was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Guang’anmen Hospital China Academy of Traditional Chinese Medicine (2023-204-KY-01).

## Cell culture
Immortalized human ATII cells were obtained from Jinyuan Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). ATII cells were cultured in DMEM (VivaCell, Shanghai, China) supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS (ExCell Bio, Shanghai, China), 100 iu /mL penicillin (Gibco, USA) and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin (Gibco, USA) and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The cell culture medium was changed every two days and the cells were passaged with 0.05% trypsin/EDTA (Gibco, USA).

## Co-IP
ATII cells were washed twice with pre-cooled PBS and then lysed on ice using a protease inhibitor-containing RIPA buffer for 5 min. After lysis, the cells were scraped from the culture bottle. The cell suspension was then transferred into an Eppendorf tube and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, thoroughly mixed, and aliquoted into separate tubes in equal volumes. One tube was designated as a blank control. The other tubes were incubated with 5 µL of serum in an oscillating incubator at 4 °C for 1 h, followed by incubation at room temperature for 30 min. Wash and mix the protein A/G. 100 µL of protein A/G was added to each tube and then rotated at 4 °C for 1 h, followed by rotation at room temperature for 30 min. Centrifuge at 4,000 rpm at 4 °C for 3 min, remove the supernatant, and wash the precipitate three times with pre-cooled PBS.

## Pro DIA quantitative proteomics
The proteomic data analysis was performed by OE Biotech (Shanghai, China). All assessments were carried out using a Tims TOF Pro mass spectrometer (Thermo, USA) equipped with an Easyspray source (Thermo, USA). Sample introduction was achieved via a C18 column (15 cm×75 μm ID, 1.6 μm C18) on an EASY-nLCTM 1200 system (Thermo, USA). The flow rate was set at 400 nL/min, and the linear gradient was programmed as follows: 0 ∼ 20 min, 5–22% B; 20 ∼ 24 min, 22–37% B; 24 ∼ 27 min, 37–80% B; 27 ∼ 30 min, 80% B. Ion mobility was set within the range of 0.7 to 1.3 Vs/cm², and the collision energy ranged from 20 to 59 eV. The MS/MS spectra were acquired within the range of 100 to 1700 m/z.

## Protein chips Preparation
Keep the polymer 3D substrates (CapitalBio, Beijing, China) at 4 °C, take them out before use, and let them to return room temperature. The PersonalArrayer™16 contact printing device (CapitalBio, Beijing, China) is used to attach pre-printing adhesion microscope slides (Citotest, Jiangsu, China) and polymer 3D substrates, with the program parameters configured for pre-printing 90 iterations, followed by repeating the printing process 3 times. After the printing process has concluded, it is essential to assess the status of the substrate regarding the printed proteins. Any proteins that have not been successfully printed onto the substrate must be reapplied. Finally, the chips were placed at room temperature to avoid light overnight.

## Serum assays on protein chips
The prepared chips were blocked with a 5% BSA solution at room temperature for 30 min. Following the blocking step, the chips were washed three times with PBS for 5 min each. The serum was incubated with 50 µL of 1:500 dilutions of RA patients, RA-ILD patients, and HC for 1 h. The serum was discarded and washed three times with PBST. The protein chips were then incubated with 1:400 dilution of Cy3-coupled goat anti-human IgG antibody(Bioss, Beijing, China)and washed three times with PBST. The microarrays were scanned using a microarray scanner (CapitalBio, Beijing, China).

## ELISA
The 96-well plates were coated with 0.1 µg/mL of recombinant EXOSC4 proteins (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), overnight at 4℃, followed by 1 h block with 5% BSA at 37℃. After three washes with PBST, added 100 µL of serum diluted at 1:100 to each well and incubated the plates at 37℃ for 1 h. After washing again, peroxidase-conjugated affinipure goat anti-human IgG (1:10000) (Jackson ImmunoResearch, USA) was added to the wells and incubated at 37℃ for 1 h. After washing the samples three times with PBST, add the color development solution, observe the resulting color change, and then add the stop solution promptly to halt the reaction. Lastly, measure the absorbance at 450 nm.

## Indirect Immunofluorescence
ATII cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 5 min at room temperature. Fixed cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 solution for 2 min and washed with PBS. Cells were closed with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature and washed three times with PBS-T. The cells were incubated with RA, RA-ILD patient serum (1:20), EXOSC4 Polyclonal antibody (1:200) (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) for 0.5 h at 37℃, respectively, washed three times with PBS-T, and fluorescence-labeled secondary antibody (1:500) was incubated at room temperature protected from light for 1 h. Subsequently, a coverslip was affixed to the glass with a drop of fluorescent sealer containing DAPI (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Images were taken with a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX71, Japan).

## IHC
Lung tissues taken from mice were also subjected to IHC analysis after routine fixation, decalcification, paraffin embedding and cutting. After deparaffinization, the tissue sections were sequentially incubated with an antibody specific for mouse EXOSC4, followed by incubation with an appropriate secondary antibody. After staining, the sections were washed, dehydrated and mounted with synthetic resin, then scanned under a microscope (Zeiss, Germany) and the target proteins were quantified using Image J software.

## Cytoplasmic protein extraction
The procedure was performed according to the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit instructions (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Briefly, the cells were rinsed with PBS and subsequently scraped using a spatula. The cell precipitate was then collected via centrifugation. Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Reagent A, supplemented with a protease inhibitor, was added to the precipitate. Following a vigorous vortexing for 5 s, the mixture was subjected to an ice bath for 15 min. Subsequently, Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Reagent B was introduced to the precipitate, followed by another vigorous vortexing for 5 s and a subsequent ice bath for 1 min. Finally, the mixture was vortexed again for 5 s and centrifuged at 12,000 g at 4℃ for 5 min. The supernatant was collected into a pre-cooled freezing tube to obtain the cytoplasmic proteins.

## WB
The cellular proteins were transferred to PVDF membrane (Absin, Shanghai, China) by SDS-PAGE. Following the blocking of the PVDF membrane with a 5% skim milk solution, it was incubated with sera from different patients, α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), Wnt3a, β-catenin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), EXOSC4 (Proteintech, wuhan, China), β-actin and GAPDH (ABclonal, Wuhan, China) antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Following three PBS-T washes, the secondary antibody was incubated at room temperature for 30 min, and again washed three times with PBS-T before imaging using electrochemiluminescence.

## Luminex
This assessment was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Bio-Plex Pro Human Cytokine Screening Panel, 48 Plex Kit (Bio-Rad). In summary, magnetic beads were placed into a 96-well plate and subsequently washed with a designated wash buffer. Following this, sera from different patients were introduced into the same 96-well plate containing the magnetic beads and allowed to incubate at room temperature for 1 h. After further rinsing, antibodies were introduced and incubation continued for an additional 30 min. Following another rinse, Streptavidin PE was introduced, and after 10 min incubation with agitation, the mixture was rinsed once more before the addition of a detection solution. Measurements were then conducted using the Bio Plex 200 system (Bio-Rad), with the minimum threshold for each sample of cytokine being established at 50 beads. A standard curve was established based on the diluted standard concentration described in the instruction manual, allowing for the conversion of detection data into concentrations measured in pg/mL.

## Single-cell RNA sequencing
The NovelCyto Single-Cell Analysis System (NovelBio, Shanghai, China) was utilized to capture transcriptomic data by initially distributing a single-cell suspension across more than 100,000 microwells using a limiting dilution technique. Subsequently, oligonucleotide barcoded beads were added until saturation was achieved, ensuring that each bead was paired with a cell within a microwell. Following cell lysis in the microwells, mRNA molecules hybridized with the barcoded capture oligonucleotides on the beads. These beads were then collected into a single tube for reverse transcription and ExoI digestion. During cDNA synthesis, each cDNA molecule was tagged at the 5′ end (corresponding to the 3′ end of the mRNA transcript) with a unique molecular identifier (UMI) and a cell barcode, which served as an indicator of cell origin. The entire transcriptome library was assembled by employing the NovelCyto single-cell whole-transcriptome amplification (WTA) process, which encompassed random priming and extension, amplification PCR, and WTA library index PCR. The quantity of the libraries was assessed using a High Sensitivity DNA chip (Agilent) on a Bioanalyzer 4200 and a Qubit High Sensitivity DNA assay (Thermo, USA). Ultimately, sequencing was carried out on an Illumina sequencer (Illumina, USA) utilizing a 150 bp paired-end mode.

## Cell communication analysis
The single-cell dataset GSE132771 was sourced from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/info/datasets.html). From this dataset, we selected single-cell data pertaining to three normal lung tissues and three lung tissues affected by IPF. We employed CellChat to analyze interactions involving peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) and ATII cells. The interaction relationships were quantified by assessing both the strength (weight) and the frequency (count) of interactions between the cells, thereby enabling us to observe the activities and effects of various cell types in the context of the disease.

## Statistical analysis
All the data were analyzed via SPSS software (version 10.0). Differences between groups were compared using one-way ANOVA or independent-samples T test. Correlation analysis was performed using Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001.

## Results

## EXOSC4 is a potential autoantigen in RA-ILD patients
Initially, peripheral blood sera were collected from five cases each of healthy individuals, RA-non-ILD patients, and RA-ILD patients, along with two blank controls, for Co-IP using ATII cells. Subsequently, Pro DIA quantitative proteomics analysis was conducted to identify potential antigens within ATII cells. The proteomics analysis yielded a total of 1,117 quantifiable proteins and 8,464 peptides. The experimental stability and sample grouping were evaluated, with principal component analysis (PCA) indicating that RA-ILD samples exhibited distinct grouping characteristics compared to other sample groups. Significant differences in antigens expression were observed among the different groups (Fig.  A). Utilizing criteria of |log2FC|>1 and P < 0.05 for differential expression, 68 significantly differentially expressed antigens were identified in the RA-ILD versus RA control comparison, with 56 antigens showing increased expression and 12 showing decreased expression (Fig.  C). Further analysis revealed that the expression of EXOSC4 in the RA-ILD group was significantly higher than that in the RA-non-ILD group, demonstrating high specificity (Fig.  B). Previous studies have demonstrated that alterations in RNA function constitute one of the crucial mechanisms underlying pulmonary fibrosis [ , ]. Also, inhaled RNA therapy has shown potential in treating this condition [ ]. EXOSC4, a critical component of the RNA exosome complex, plays a vital role in nucleic acid metabolism. Therefore, we speculate that EXOSC4 may be a potential antigen for RA-ILD. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that the pathogenesis of RA-ILD is likely associated with signaling pathways such as nucleoplasmic transport, extracellular exocytosis, and RNA degradation, which further supported the possibility of EXOSC4 as a potential autoantigen for RA-ILD (Fig.  D).

 

## Elevated serum EXOSC4 antibody levels in RA-ILD patients
We conducted an incubation of WB bands derived from ATII cells with sera from various patients, and among all the bands, we found one band with the same molecular weight as EXOSC4, which was obtained by incubating RA-ILD patients sera (Fig.  A). To substantiate the hypothesis that EXOSC4 serves as a potential autoantigen in RA-ILD, we employed a microarray scanner to assess the signal intensity of patient sera bound to the EXOSC4 antigen. The protein chips analysis indicated that sera from RA-ILD patients demonstrated the highest signal intensity upon binding to the EXOSC4 antigen (Fig.  B).
Subsequently, to ensure the reliability of the experimental results, we increased the sample size for testing. The levels of EXOSC4 antibody in the sera of different patients were further compared by ELISA. Comprehensive details regarding the clinical samples are provided in Table  . The study’s findings revealed that the optical density (OD) values of EXOSC4 antibody were significantly elevated in RA-ILD patients compared to RA-non-ILD patients (P < 0.01) and HC (P < 0.0001) as depicted in Fig.  C. Consequently, we confirmed that serum EXOSC4 antibody levels are elevated in RA-ILD patients, establishing EXOSC4 as an autoantigen in this patient population.

 

 

## Increased expression of EXOSC4 in lung tissues of CIA-BLM mice
In this study, DBA/1 female mice were employed for in vivo validation. Firstly, a mouse model of CIA was established using bovine type II collagen, and a fibrosis model was developed via tracheal administration of BLM solution. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining of lung tissue revealed that the lung architecture in the negative control (NC) group was largely normal, while the CIA group exhibited mild abnormalities without edema or inflammatory cell infiltration. In contrast, the CIA-BLM group displayed severe abnormalities, including a mass of inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig.  A). Subsequently, IHC was utilized to examine the expression of EXOSC4 in lung tissue, revealing positive nuclear staining. In the CIA-BLM group, notable alveolar epithelial hyperplasia and significant damage to the alveolar lumen were observed, with extensive fusion and occlusion of the alveolar spaces due to fibroblastic tissue proliferation. The number of EXOSC4-positive cells was elevated compared to the NC and CIA groups (Fig.  B). Quantitative analysis indicated that the positive area was significantly greater in the CIA-BLM group than in the CIA and NC groups (P < 0.01) (Fig.  C). These findings suggest that EXOSC4 expression is markedly increased in the CIA-BLM group relative to the CIA group.

## Increased expression of EXOSC4 in ATII-BLM cells
We treated ATII cells with BLM to create an in vitro model that simulates fibrotic cells. To investigate changes in EXOSC4 expression, we incubated ATII and ATII-BLM cells with sera from NC, RA, and RA-ILD patients, along with an EXOSC4 antibody.
Antigens expression were increased in ATII-BLM cells as compared to ATII cells (Fig.  D). Notably, the autoantigens expression pattern of the RA-ILD group was consistent with that of the EXOSC4 antibody group in ATII-BLM cells, transitioning from the nucleus to the cell membrane and ultimately demonstrating substantial expression at the cell membrane. In contrast, the RA-non-ILD group did not exhibit a comparable alteration in autoantigens expression. Quantitative analysis showed that the relative mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was significantly higher in the RA-ILD group than in the RA-non-ILD and NC groups, both before and after BLM treatment (Fig.  E, F). Additionally, the relative MFI significantly increased in the NC and EXOSC4 groups after BLM treatment, but no significant difference was found between the RA-non-ILD and RA-ILD groups (Fig.  G). 

## EXOSC4 antibody levels were significantly negatively correlated with PNI
As demonstrated in Table  , patients with RA-ILD were significantly older than those with RA-non-ILD (P < 0.001), and exhibited higher age at onset, elevated ESR, CRP, and LDH levels compared to RA-non-ILD patients (P < 0.05). Additionally, RA-ILD patients had a significantly lower PNI than RA-non-ILD patients (P < 0.01), while no significant differences were observed in other indicators. To evaluate the clinical relevance of EXOSC4 antibody levels, we analyzed correlations between the clinical data and OD values of EXOSC4 antibody. Concerning basic demographic factors (Fig.  A), EXOSC4 antibody le、vels showed a low positive correlation with age (P = 0.050, r = 0.246), but no significant correlation with age at onset, disease duration, or BMI (P > 0.05). In terms of disease activity (Fig.  B), EXOSC4 antibody levels demonstrated a low positive correlation with DAS28-CRP (P = 0.033, r = 0.267), but no significant correlation with DAS28-ESR, CDAI, or SDAI (P > 0.05). Regarding inflammatory markers (Fig.  C), EXOSC4 antibody levels exhibited a low positive correlation with CRP (P = 0.002, r = 0.299) and ESR (P = 0.014, r = 0.305), but no correlation with PLT (P > 0.05). No association was found between EXOSC4 antibody and RF or anti-CCP antibody (P > 0.05) (Fig.  D). Notably, EXOSC4 antibody levels showed a medium positive correlation with LDH (P = 0.006, r = 0.337) and significant negative correlated with PNI (P < 0.001, r = -0.705) (Fig.  E).

 

## EXOSC4 antibody combined with clinical indicators significantly improve diagnostic accuracy for RA-ILD
We conducted an in-depth investigation into the diagnostic potential of EXOSC4 antibody as a biomarker. By integrating data from the existing literature and the results of univariate analyses, we generated receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for LDH, AO, PNI, and EXOSC4 antibodies for distinguishing between RA-ILD and RA-non-ILD patients, as depicted in Fig.  . The AUC for EXOSC4 antibody was 0.809 (95% CI: 0.704–0.913), indicating superior predictive capability. Subsequent logistic regression analysis incorporating LDH, AO, and EXOSC4 antibody demonstrated that the combined use of these three indicators enhanced the model’s predictive performance, achieving an AUC of 0.890 (95% CI: 0.812–0.967). This shows that EXOSC4 antibody can be a reliable diagnostic marker for RA-ILD.

 

## Communication between Naïve CD4 + T cells and ATII cells May affect EXOSC4 antibody generation
To investigate the the source of EXOSC4 antigen production, we conducted an interaction analysis between peripheral blood cells and ATII cells. We obtained single-cell data from three cases of Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) and three normal human lung tissues from the GEO database (GSE132771) to use as controls. We first clustered various cell types (Figs.  A-C), analyzed their gene expression profiles (Fig.  D), and then examined the interactions between these cells and ATII cells. In the Figs.  E and F, the circle size indicates the number of cells, while the line thickness reflects the strength of the interaction. It can be seen that the naïve CD4 + T cells interacted with the largest number of ATII cells.
Given that abnormal cell interactions are often associated with the activation of key signaling pathways in disease development, we preliminarily speculated that the extensive interactions between naïve CD4 + cells and ATII cells might be involved in some important signaling pathways. Among them, the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which is closely related to cell proliferation, differentiation, and fibrosis, was suspected to play a role [ ]. Further analysis involved scoring the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway across different cell types, revealing that naïve CD4 + cells exerted the most substantial influence on ATII cells within this pathway (Fig.  G). Therefore, we hypothesized that the elevated levels of A antibodies in RA-ILD patients are likely a consequence of extensive interactions between naïve CD4 + cells and ATII cells, and this interaction might be related to the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway.

 

## EXOSC4 antigen May cause RA-ILD through activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway
To further validate the hypothesis that EXOSC4 serves as a autoantigen in patients with RA-ILD, we conducted a comparative analysis of cytoplasmic EXOSC4 expression in ATII cells. Our findings, illustrated in Fig.  A, the expression of EXOSC4 in the cytoplasm of ATII-BLM was elevated compared to the ATII cells. We performed preliminary mechanistic explorations and found that the expression of α-SMA, Wnta3a, and β-catenin were increased in ATII-BLM cells compared with ATII cells (Fig.  B). The multi-cytokine analysis revealed that sera levels of IL-10 and IL-27 were significantly lower in RA-ILD patients, whereas IL-15 and Rantes levels were significantly higher compared to HC and RA-non-ILD patients (Fig.  C). Therefore, we propose that EXOSC4 is likely to contribute to RA-ILD by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which is associated with the interaction between naïve CD4 + cells and ATII cells.

 

## Discussion
EXOSC4, a component of the RNA exosome complex, plays a crucial role in RNA processing and degradation. The complex consists of multiple subunits and is essential for maintaining stable intracellular RNA levels. It also influences a variety of biological processes, including gene expression, cell growth, and programmed cell death [ ]. EXOSC4 may be important in the development and progression of cancers, including pancreatic [ ], colorectal [ ], and ovarian cancers [ ]. Zang et al. analyzed RNA-seq data from 14,645 samples as well as 917,294 single-cell expression profiles of 13 cancer types and identified EXOSC4 as a potential therapeutic target in malignant tumors [ ].
While its primary function has been extensively studied in the context of RNA metabolism, its involvement in autoimmune diseases, particularly in RA-ILD, has not been well explored. Recent studies have highlighted the potential role of autoantigens in the pathogenesis of various autoimmune diseases, including RA and SLE [ , ]. However, the identification of specific autoantigens in RA-ILD remains limited. Our study is among the first to identify EXOSC4 as a novel autoantigen in RA-ILD, providing a new perspective on the molecular mechanisms underlying this severe comorbidity.
We incubated WB bands from ATII cells with sera from RA-ILD patients and observed bands matching the molecular weight of EXOSC4. Protein microarray analysis further revealed that sera from RA-ILD patients produced the strongest signal intensity when bound to the EXOSC4 antigen, initially supporting the potential of EXOSC4 as a RA-ILD autoantigen. To strengthen this finding, we expanded the sample size and conducted ELISA assays, which confirmed that RA-ILD patient sera contained significantly higher levels of EXOSC4 antibodies, reinforcing the reliability of EXOSC4 as a disease-related autoantigen. Notably, in vivo experiments showed that EXOSC4 expression was significantly increased in the lung tissues of CIA-BLM mice. In vitro analysis demonstrated elevated antigen expression in ATII-BLM cells. Both the RA-ILD serum group and the EXOSC4 antibody group exhibited the same expression pattern, characterized by the translocation of autoantigens from the nucleus to the cell membrane, ultimately resulting in significant membrane expression. These observations collectively confirm that EXOSC4 functions as an autoantigen in RA-ILD.
In addition, we delved into the correlation between EXOSC4 antibody and clinical indicators. The results showed that EXOSC4 antibody was correlated with a variety of clinical indicators, especially strong correlation with LDH and PNI. Studies have reported that LDH activity is significantly increased in patients with pulmonary fibrosis and is a potential systemic biomarker [ ]. EXOSC4 antibody combined with age of onset and LDH to construct a logistic regression model with a significantly higher ability to predict RA-ILD with an AUC of 0.89. Numerous studies have shown that elevated LDH may be associated with poor prognosis in patients with pulmonary fibrosis [ , ]. The PNI index is an important indicator for assessing the nutritional status of the patient and the disease prognosis [ ]. The significant negative correlation between EXOSC4 antibody levels and PNI highlights its potential as a predictive marker, which is more likely to be associated with the prognosis of RA-ILD, which needs to be confirmed by further studies.
Cellular interactions, particularly the involvement of immune cells, play an important role in the progression of diseases [ , ].The interaction between CD4 + T cells and ATII cells is crucial in modulating the immune response and influencing the pathogenesis of pulmonary diseases [ – ]. Upon activation, CD4 + T cells secrete a diverse array of cytokines that not only facilitate their own proliferation and differentiation but also modulate the behavior of adjacent cells by regulating the Wnt signaling pathway. For instance, CD4 + T cells can induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition in alveolar cells through the secretion of cytokines such as transforming growth factor-β(TGF-β), thereby impacting alveolar repair and regeneration processes and contributing to lung fibrosis [ , ]. We found that EXOSC4 expression was significantly upregulated in the cytoplasm of ATII-BLM cells after stimulation with sera from RA-ILD patients. Additionally, there was an observed increase in the expression of αSMA, Wnt3a, and β-catenin. Multi-cytokine analysis showed that sera levels of IL-10 and IL-27 were significantly lower in RA-ILD patients, whereas IL-15 and Rantes levels were significantly higher than in HC and RA-non-ILD patients. Furthermore, cellular communication analysis has demonstrated a significant association between naïve CD4 + T cells and ATII cells within the Wnt/βcatenin signaling pathway. We propose that the upregulation of EXOSC4 may result from cytokines secreted by initial CD4 + T cells, which modulate the Wnt signaling pathway and subsequently affect ATII cell function. Consequently, elucidating the interaction between CD4 + T cells and ATII cells may be pivotal for the pathogenesis of RA-ILD.
Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays a key role in cell proliferation and differentiation, and its abnormal activation in ILD can lead to the abnormal proliferation and differentiation of alveolar epithelial cells and fibroblasts, which in turn promotes the fibrotic process of lung tissues [ , ]. Pan Y et al. found that EXOSC4 was involved in the regulation of Wnt/β-catenin pathway in colorectal cancer [ ]. Specifically, knockdown of EXOSC4 significantly inhibited Wnt/β- catenin pathway activity by reducing the expression of β-catenin and Wnt/β-catenin pathway-related genes. Similarly, in ovarian cancer, knockdown of EXOSC4 also significantly inhibited the activity of Wnt/β-catenin pathway [ ]. Therefore, we speculate that in RA-ILD patients, EXOSC4 may exacerbate the fibrosis process by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which in turn affects the proliferation and differentiation of ATII cells.
The limitations of this study include the following points. Firstly, the sample size, although sufficient for initial validation, is relatively small. Larger cohorts are needed to confirm the diagnostic value of EXOSC4 autoantigen in RA-ILD. Secondly, while we identified the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway as a potential mechanism, the exact molecular interactions between EXOSC4 and this pathway remain to be fully elucidated.
Future research should focus on several key areas to further elucidate the role of EXOSC4 in RA-ILD. Firstly, larger-scale studies involving diverse patient populations are needed to validate the diagnostic and prognostic potential of EXOSC4 antibodies. Secondly, mechanistic studies should be conducted to explore the precise role of EXOSC4 in the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and its interaction with other molecular pathways involved in RA-ILD pathogenesis. Furthermore, the role of EXOSC4 in other autoimmune diseases and lung pathologies should be investigated to determine if its involvement is specific to RA-ILD or if it plays a broader role in autoimmune and fibrotic diseases. Finally, longitudinal studies are needed to assess the dynamic changes in EXOSC4 expression and antibody levels over the course of the disease and in response to treatment, which could provide valuable insights into disease progression and therapeutic efficacy.

## Conclusion
Our study identifies EXOSC4 as a novel autoantigen in RA-ILD and provides preliminary evidence of its diagnostic and mechanistic significance. While further research is needed to fully understand its role, our findings open new avenues for the development of diagnostic tools and targeted therapies for RA-ILD.
