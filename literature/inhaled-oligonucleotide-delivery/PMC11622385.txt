ASFV is a large, enveloped, double-stranded DNA virus (average diameter: approximately 250 nm) and the sole member of the Asfarviridae family. Different ASFV strains exhibit genome sizes of 170–194 kb, and encoding 151–174 open reading frames [ , ], although the functions of more than half of these viral-encoded genes remain unknown [ ]. Since ASF was first identified in Kenya in 1921 [ ], it has spread to 41 countries, resulting in the death of more than 1 million animals in the past 2 years [ ]. No effective vaccine or antiviral compound has been available for ASF until now.
Cepharanthine (CEP) is a natural small molecule alkaloid extracted from Stephania cepharantha, Hayata [ ]. In the 1950s, CEP was approved in Japan for clinical use in treating various acute and chronic conditions, including radiation-induced leukopenia, alopecia areata, exudative otitis media, and snakebites, with few serious side effects reported [ ]. Over decades of research, CEP has been found to exhibit a range of biological and pharmacological activities, such as an anti-oxidant [ , ], anti-inflammatory [ , ], and immunomodulatory effects [ , ]. It also stabilizes cell membranes [ , ], inhibits drug efflux transporters [ , ], and potentially exerts antiproliferative, and proapoptotic effects on tumors by inhibiting NF-κB activation [ , ]. Furthermore, CEP possesses broad-spectrum antiviral capabilities, including activity against HIV [ , ], SARS [ ], HBV [ ], HSV-1 [ ], SARS-CoV-2 [ , ], and other viruses [ ]. However, few studies have investigated the antiviral effects of CEP against ASFV, and its underlying mechanism remains unclear.
The identification of potent ASFV antivirals is of great interest. In this study, we screened a natural compound library. CEP was found to significantly inhibit ASFV replication in porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs). To further elucidate the mechanism of CEP’s anti-ASFV effect, high-throughput transcriptome sequencing was performed. According to the results, CEP may inhibit ASFV entry by modulating cholesterol metabolism and trafficking within macrophages. Time-of-addition (TOA) experiments demonstrated that CEP disrupts the entire ASFV replication process, particularly during the viral entry stage. Additionally, we discovered that CEP-induced upregulation of EHD2 gene expression in the clathrin-mediated pathway may be a key mechanism underlying its inhibition of ASFV entry. Taken together, our findings detail the intracellular transcription changes in ASFV-infected PAMs treated with CEP and reveal a novel possible mechanism of CEP’s antiviral effect, positioning it as a promising antiviral candidate for ASF prevention and control.

## Results

## Identification of CEP as a potent antiviral compound against ASFV through screening of anti-ASFV compounds
A total of 803 compounds from a Selleck natural compound library were screened by conducting the established cell-based ELISA, and 6 compounds demonstrated potential anti-ASFV activities ( A and 1B). After measured the CC50 and IC50 of these candidate anti-ASFV compounds ( C and 1D), CEP was identified as the most effective (IC50 = 0.3223 μM, CC50 = 15.86 μM). Next, RT-qPCR and Western blotting were performed to confirm CEP’s anti-ASFV effect. Both ASFV-p72 mRNA and protein levels were significantly upregulated at 24 hpi in ASFV-infected PAMs. However, this upregulation was abolished upon CEP treatment ( B-2E). To confirm the effect of CEP on virus titer, the virus titer was measured upon CEP treatment in PAMs. Indeed, CEP significantly reduced the number of intracellular ASFV copies in PAMs ( C). Consistent with these findings, IFA results revealed a significant decrease in p72-positivie fluorescent PAMs when treated with CEP compared with the ASFV-infected group (DMSO treated) ( F). Next, a time-of-addition (TOA) assay was conducted to determine the ASFV life cycle stages affected by CEP ( G). CEP effectively inhibited ASFV replication within the first 2 h of administration, targeting the viral entry stage. Prolonged exposure to CEP the viral replication cycle led to a more pronounced inhibitory effect ( H). Specifically, ASFV-p72 levels decreased significantly in groups that included the viral binding/entry stages, such as groups 2, 3, and 5. Viral replication was nearly abolished in the group that received CEP treatment throughout the entire replication cycle (group 8). To further investigate the impact of CEP on viral entry, we examined the localization of two early ASFV proteins ( I). CEP treatment significantly reduced the fluorescence levels of ASFV-p30 and ASFV-I73R in PAMs at 2 hpi, confirming its inhibitory effect on viral entry. These results suggest that CEP effectively inhibits ASFV replication, particularly by targeting the viral entry stage.
 
 

## Bioinformatics analysis of the ASFV-CEP transcriptome
After confirming CEP’s anti-ASFV effect, we used transcriptome sequencing to investigate the mechanism through which CEP inhibits ASFV entry. We prepared 12 cDNA libraries from four groups: Mock (Virus-, CEP-), Virus (Virus+, CEP-), CEP (Virus-, CEP+), and CEP + Virus (Virus+, CEP+), each in triplicate ( A). In total, 560,337,290 trimmed sequence reads were generated, comprising 85.42 Gbp clean bases (Table S1). Between 95.68% and 96.23% of the trimmed reads were successfully mapped to the Sus scrofa genome and assembled into over 13,375 genes with functional annotation. To explore the relationship between replicates in the individual groups, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) ( B). The data showed that the 12 group samples clustered into three sets: Mock set (Mock1, Mock2, Mock3), Virus set (Virus1, Virus2, Virus3), and CEP treated set (CEP1, CEP2, CEP3, and CEP + Virus1, CEP + Virus2, CEP + Virus3) along principal component axis 1 (PC1 = 85.95%). Moreover, DEGs were identified between groups using criteria of a q value < 0.05 and log2FC fold change > 1. The number of significant DEGs across key comparisons was also presented ( C).
 

## ASFV infection causes a severe inflammatory response to PAMs
After summarizing the preliminary data, we performed a comprehensive comparison between the Virus and Mock groups (Virus-Mock, Case–Control) to investigate cellular changes following ASFV infection. In total, 182 upregulated DEGs and 32 downregulated DEGs were identified and visualized using both a volcano map and a heatmap ( D and S1A). Immune response-related genes (IFIT2, IFIT5, IFIT3, DDX58, IFI44L, IFIT1, MX2, BST2, OAS1, and ISG15), along with RNA- or DNA-binding-related genes (DDX58, DDX60, DHX58, IFIH1, and TLR3) were significantly upregulated. By contrast, genes associated with intracellular signal transduction (ASB2, GSG2, and RASA3) and metal ion binding (DLL3, MATN2, PSPH, ATP2A3, and LIMS2) were downregulated. KEGG enrichment analysis unveiled that the upregulated DEGs in the Virus Group were predominantly enriched in the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway, RIG-I-like receptor, and Toll-like receptor signaling pathway ( E). Specifically, genes associated with viral defense response, including members of the SLA and ATP families involved in phagosome function were significantly upregulated (Figure S1B). The GSEA further supported these findings, highlighting significant DEGs enrichment in multiple signaling pathways (Figure S1C), including regulation of immune response (GO:0009615, GO:0045071, and GO:0050776), interferon signaling (GO:0060337, GO:0034341, and GO:0035455), and RNA binding (GO:0003725). To further explore the functionally linked gene ontology and interactions between these signaling pathways, network analyses were performed using the Metascape website and a Cytoscape plug-in, ClueGO. Key representative terms from the enriched clusters were displayed in a network layout (Figure S1D). Among the virus-upregulated DEG-enriched pathways, the pathways associated with response to virus infection and regulation of response to biotic stimuli occupied upstream positions within the network and showed extensive interaction with other pathways. Using the MCODE algorithm to identify densely connected neighborhoods, we observed genes such as IRF7, OAS2, and ISG20 were central within the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network (Figure S1E). These data indicate that ASFV infection induces a severe inflammatory response in PAMs, driven by significant alterations in immune and viral response pathways.

## Transcriptional response of PAMs after CEP treatment
To date, studies employing transcriptome sequencing to investigate the functional mechanisms of CEP are few, especially in terms of its antiviral effects. Thus, we compare the CEP and Mock groups (CEP-Mock) to explore the potential anti-viral mechanisms of CEP. In total, 110 upregulated and 219 downregulated DEGs were identified ( C). Among these, cholesterol-biosynthesis related genes (HMGCS1, LDLR, and HMGCR) were significantly upregulated, whereas many immune response-related genes were downregulated ( A and 4B). The GO enrichment analysis revealed that CEP-upregulated DEGs were enriched in processes such as cholesterol biosynthesis, lipid metabolism, and isoprenoid biosynthesis ( C). Conversely, downregulated DEGs were chiefly enriched in innate immune response, and monocyte and neutrophil chemotaxis signaling ( D). These findings were supported by the heatmap analysis, which confirmed that cholesterol biosynthesis-related genes were significantly upregulated, whereas ATP-binding- and innate immune response-related genes were downregulated in CEP-treated PAMs ( E). Using the Metascape tool to analyze DEGs, we observed that ontology clusters related to cholesterol biosynthesis, SREBF regulation, and tube morphogenesis were enriched in CEP-upregulated pathways ( F). Genes such as FASN, CCNF, and CAD were identified as central nodes within the PPI network ( G). By contrast, CEP-downregulated ontology clusters included pathways related to cell chemotaxis, cell migration, and defense response to viral infection (Figure S2A and S2B). The GSEA further demonstrated that DEGs in the CEP-Mock comparison were significantly enriched in various signaling pathways (Figure S2C). Among the TOP-ranked pathways, cholesterol biosynthesis (GO:0006695 and GO:0008203) was prominent in the upregulated gene sets, whereas mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I (GO:0032981) was involved in the downregulated gene sets.
 

## ASFV infection poorly alters the transcription status of CEP-treated PAMs
Following the analysis of CEP treatment alone on PAMs, DEGs in the (CEP + Virus)-Mock comparison were examined. Remarkably, 169 of the same DEGs were identified when compared to those in CEP-Mock group ( A and S3A). Among these, over 40% of the upregulated DEGs (45 shared DEGs) were consistent between the CEP-Mock and (CEP + Virus)-Mock groups, whereas more than 57% of the downregulated DEGs (125 shared DEGs) were also identified. Moreover, only 7 DEGs were detected in the (CEP + Virus)-CEP group comparison ( B), indicating a strikingly low number of changes. This extremely small number of DEGs suggests that PAMs in the CEP + Virus group maintained a transcriptional profile highly similar to those in the CEP-treated group. In other words, ASFV infection had little to no impact on the transcriptional state of CEP-treated PAMs. These findings imply that CEP effectively suppresses the virus’s ability to alter host gene expression, maintaining cellular homeostasis despite viral presence.
 
Next, GO enrichment analysis was conducted on the common DEGs in the two CEP-treated groups to elucidate how CEP enables PAMs to resist ASFV infection. The results revealed that the commonly downregulated DEGs were enriched in the innate immune response GO0045087, ( C), whereas the commonly upregulated DEGs were enriched in the cholesterol biosynthesis pathway WP197, ( D). The heat map of the cholesterol metabolic pathway specifically demonstrated that CEP significantly alters genes within this pathway in in PAMs, regardless of ASFV infection status ( E). Given that cholesterol in the cell membrane is essential for ASFV infection, as reported previously [ ]. We further examined the effect of CEP on cholesterol metabolism through RT–PCR. The data indicated that CEP treatment significantly upregulated these cholesterol metabolism-related genes, including SREBF2, HMGCR, HMGCS1, FASN, and SQLE across all four groups ( F). This suggests that CEP impairs ASFV entry by modulating cholesterol metabolism in host cells.
In addition to the shared DEGs, unique DEGs could also contribute to CEP’s anti-ASFV effect. Therefore, we performed GO analysis on the unique DEGs in the CEP-Mock and (CEP + Virus)-Mock comparisons (Figure S3B-S3E). Results indicated lipid metabolism pathways were enriched among the uniquely upregulated DEGs in both groups, whereas negative regulation of chemotaxis was observed in the uniquely downregulated DEGs. We also presented the gene expression heat map of chemotaxis across four groups (Figure S3F). However, considering that this pathway was weakly associated with CEP’s role in inhibiting ASFV entry, further studies may explore this in more specific contexts.

## EHD2 is a target for CEP to inhibit ASFV entry
Previous studies have reported that EHD2 inhibit virus replication at the entry or post-entry stage through caveolae [ ]. The regulatory effect of CEP on cholesterol may be a mechanism by which CEP exerts its inhibitory effect on ASFV entry. Therefore, we analyzed to analyze the genes upregulated by the virus infection that were reversed after CEP treatment. The GSEA indicated that a portion of the genes downregulated by the virus were upregulated by CEP (NES = 2.67, P < 0.001, FDR < 0.001). By contrast, most genes upregulated by the virus were significantly downregulated after CEP treatment (NES = −3.94, P < 0.001, FDR < 0.001; A). Specifically, among the 182 genes upregulated by the virus, 146 genes (80.22%, including only significantly enriched core genes) were downregulated by CEP ( B and S4A). The GO enrichment analysis of these reversed genes unveiled that the TOP enriched pathway was primarily associated with the immune response (Figure S4B). However, this result provides limited insight into the anti-ASFV effects of CEP, as it is expected that immune-related genes will be reversed when ASFV replication is suppressed by CEP.
 
We also observed that 10 genes (31.25%) among the 32 genes downregulated by the virus were upregulated by CEP ( B). Interestingly, EH Domain Containing 2 (EHD2), identified as one of the 7 DEGs in the (CEP + Virus)-CEP comparison, was included in this list. Additionally, several genes involved in the clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) pathway were reversed by CEP ( C). Considering that EHD2 was upregulated after CEP treatment but downregulated during ASFV infection in the transcriptome analysis, we further validated these findings at the protein ( D) and mRNA levels ( E and 6F), and the results were consistent. Furthermore, CEP increased EHD2 expression in a dose-dependent manner (Figure S4C-S4D, and S4F in WSL cells), whereas EHD2 expression slightly decreased during the late stages of ASFV infection (Figure S4E). Studies have reported that different forms of EHD2 vectors can inhibit transferrin entry into COS-1 cells via the CME pathway 30, which is also involved in ASFV entry [ , ]. Therefore, we speculated that EHD2 upregulation might contribute to the inhibition of ASFV entry. To further confirm this, we constructed various EHD2 plasmids as reported in the literature [ ]. Due to the low transfection efficiency in PAMs, another ASFV-infected cell line, WSL cells, was used for ASFV infection after EHD2 truncates overexpression. As shown in H and 6I, intracellular ASFV levels were decreased in the groups overexpressing EHD2 truncates. Additionally, CEP treatment reduced ASFV levels in WSL cells while increasing EHD2 expression (Figure S4F). We also conducted knockdown experiments to verify the role of EHD2 in ASFV entry by transfecting EHD2 siRNA or nontargeting scrambled siRNA. siRNA effectively reduced intracellular EHD2 levels in both PAMs ( J) and WSL cells (Figure S4G), significantly increasing the number of ASFV copies entering the cells ( K and S4H). It has been reported that Caveolin-1 (Cav1) is one of the binding proteins of EHD2 [ ]. To further elucidate EHD2’s role in ASFV entry, we first confirmed the Cav1-EHD2 interaction by conducting Co-IP assay, with LC3, a known Cav-1-binding protein [ ], as a control. As expected, overexpressed Flag-EHD2 was present in the anti-HA (Cav1) immunocomplex ( L–6M). Subsequently, we conducted IFA assays to detect Cav1 and ASFV-p30 localization in cells overexpression or silencing EHD2. As depicted in N–6S, the co-localization of Cav1 and ASFV-p30 was significantly reduced in EHD2-overexpressing cells ( P), while it increased in EHD2-silenced cells ( R). This indicates that EHD2 overexpression decreases virion entry into the cells, whereas EHD2 silencing enhances viral entry. Thus, we concluded that CEP-mediated upregulation of EHD2 likely contributes to the inhibition of ASFV entry via CME. ( T).

## CEP extended the survival time of ASFV-infected pigs
To further evaluate the protective efficacy of CEP against ASFV in pigs, an animal challenge protection experiment was conducted ( A). As shown in B, the rectal temperature of pigs in the ASFV group rose rapidly on the third-day post-infection (dpi), peaking at 41.5℃ by 9 dpi. The pigs in the CEP + ASFV group displayed similar temperature trends during the challenge. Moreover, the survival curves revealed that the mean time of death was 9.67 and 16.67 days for the ASFV group and CEP + ASFV group, respectively ( C). Organ lesion analysis revealed no significant differences in the degree of lesions in organs such as the heart, lungs, and most lymph nodes between the ASFV group and CEP + ASFV group. However, some lymph nodes around the intestine and gastrointestinal tract in the CEP + ASFV group exhibited only mild lesions ( D). Histopathological analysis showed that both groups displayed typical pathological changes, including disordered myocardial fiber arrangement and bleeding in the heart, acute splenitis in the spleen, and the presence of multiple eosinophilic exudates, red blood cells, and numerous inflammatory cells in the lungs ( E). Additionally, viral load measurements across various organs and swabs showed no significant differences between the CEP and CEP + ASFV groups ( F and 7G). Overall, while CEP provided partial protection by delaying mortality in infected with a virulent ASFV strain, it did not offer complete protection against virus infection or death.
 

## Discussion
ASF is a severe viral disease characterized by high mortality rates and hemorrhagic symptoms, posing a substantial threat to the global pig industry, particularly in China. Urgent measures are required for preventing and controlling ASF. Currently, vaccine development is the primary focus for ASF control, with several vaccine types demonstrating effective protection for pigs. However, few have been thoroughly evaluated for safety and efficacy [ ]. Consequently, additional research into screening and developing effective anti-ASFV drugs is critically important.
In this study, we screened a natural compounds library using the cell-based ELISA method and identified CEP as a promising anti-ASFV compound ( ). Our data indicate that CEP effectively inhibits ASFV replication at the entry stage with low cytotoxicity ( ). We further explored the potential anti-ASFV mechanism of CEP through transcriptomic sequencing. Results revealed that both ASFV infection and CEP treatment uniquely altered the transcriptional state of PAMs. In the comparison between ASFV-infected and mock-infected PAMs (ASFV-Mock), ASFV infection triggered a severe inflammatory response ( D-3E and S1). By contrast, the comparison between CEP-treated and mock-treated PAMs (CEP-Mock) showed that CEP modulated cholesterol metabolism-related genes ( and S2). Interestingly, similar DEGs were identified in the (CEP + Virus)-Mock comparison as those in the CEP-Mock group, with only 7 unique DEGs found in the (CEP + Virus)-CEP comparison. This consistency in transcriptional status across two CEP-treated groups indicates that ASFV infection has minimal influence on the transcriptional state of CEP-treated PAMs ( ). Moreover, CEP regulates cholesterol metabolism in PAMs, regardless of ASFV infection. Given that the presence of cholesterol in the cell membrane is necessary for ASFV infection, we further verified the regulatory effect of CEP on cholesterol metabolism through RT-qPCR. Our results demonstrated that CEP significantly upregulates key genes such as HMGCS1 and HMGCR in PAMs, regardless of ASFV infection status. Unfortunately, further analysis of this mechanism was limited by experimental conditions. Therefore, we shifted our focus to genes that were altered by ASFV infection but reversed by CEP treatment for the next phase of the study. Mapping the DEGs between Virus-Mock and CEP-Virus ( A) revealed that CEP could reverse the expression of immune response-related genes (Figure S4B) and those involved in the CME pathway during ASFV infection ( C). Among these, a gene named EHD2 garnered our attention. Identified as one of 7 DEGs in the previous (CEP + Virus)-CEP comparison, EHD2 plays a significant role in regulating endocytosis. We observed that EHD2 was upregulated following CEP treatment but downregulated by ASFV infection ( D–6F). Further experiment showed that overexpressing various truncations of EHD2 led to a significant reduction in intracellular ASFV copies in all groups ( I). Conversely, EHD2 knockdown resulted in an increased number of intracellular viral copies ( K and S4H). These findings suggest that EHD2 upregulation may be one of the mechanisms through which CEP inhibits ASFV entry. Finally, we evaluated the protective effect of CEP in ASFV-challenged pigs in vivo. Although intramuscular administration of CEP did not completely protect pigs from the ASFV challenge, it significantly prolonged their survival time, from 9.67 days to 16.67 days. These results confirm that CEP exerts anti-ASFV effect by blocking viral entry and suggest that it may be a promising candidate for ASF prevention and control.
Typically, the viral life cycle comprises several stages: attachment, entry, uncoating, replication, assembly, release, and cell-to-cell transmission. Some of these stages can be targeted by antiviral drugs for therapeutic purposes. CEP is known to inhibit viral replication at the entry [ ] or post-entry stages [ ]. In our TOA experiments, results demonstrated CEP’s potential to inhibit ASFV entry into PAMs. Interestingly, ASFV replication was also efficiently suppressed when CEP was administered during the post-entry phase ( H, lane 4). This phenomenon was attributable to CEP’s continuous inhibition effect, which limits the number of ASFV virions that can enter the cells and replicate. Consequently, ASFV infection had a minimal impact on the transcriptional state of CEP-treated PAMs ( ). Additionally, most of the genes altered by ASFV infection but reversed by CEP treatment were immune-related genes (Figure S4B). This is likely because only a limited number of virions replicate, which may not be sufficient to trigger a strong activation of the intracellular immune system. Despite, CEP significantly impeding ASFV entry into host cells, a small number of virions may still accumulate to the infective dose after continuous replication. This could explain why CEP prolongs the survival time of ASFV-infected pigs but does not fully mitigate the symptoms associated with ASFV infection ( ).
The broad-spectrum antiviral function of CEP has been demonstrated through various mechanisms, including inhibition of NF-κb [ ], PI3 K/Akt [ ] signaling pathways, modification of cell membrane fluidity [ ], and reversion of heat shock protein responses [ ]. However, in our study, CEP did not significantly affect heat shock proteins or NF-κb-induced inflammation, suggesting that CEP’s antiviral mechanism may vary depending on the virus. Nevertheless, we did observe an effect of CEP on cell membrane fluidity through its regulation of cholesterol metabolism. Cholesterol efflux plays a crucial role in ASFV infection, as the virus requires cholesterol to exit the endosome and enter the cytoplasm for productive replication. Lowering cellular cholesterol severely impairs ASFV infectivity during early infection [ , , ]. According to studies, agent such as Nystatin (Nys) and methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD), which disrupt cholesterol distribution, can reduce ASFV infectivity in macrophages by up to 80% during early infection [ ]. CEP, also known as a cholesterol trafficking inhibitor, has been shown to suppress angiogenesis and tumor growth by causing intracellular cholesterol accumulation [ ]. In this study, CEP was found to upregulate cholesterol metabolism-related genes, including SREBF2, NPC1, and HMGCS1. The sterol regulatory element-binding proteins (SREBPs) are master regulators of lipid homeostasis, and low cholesterol concentrations promote SREBP2 processing, thereby activating the transcription of cholesterol biosynthesis-related genes, such as HMGCS and ACAT [ ]. Our RT-qPCR results confirmed that CEP upregulates these genes. Unfortunately, due to equipment limitations, we were unable to obtain CEP-ASFV images with cholesterol staining, but this could be explored in future studies under better conditions. In addition to cholesterol-related pathways, we identified EHD2 as a potential target for CEP. EHD2 is a plasma membrane-associated protein that regulates transport and recycling from the plasma membrane and plays a critical role in various ASFV entry mechanisms, including caveolae, micropinocytosis, and clathrin-mediated pathways [ , , ]. High EHD2 levels are known to distort and reduce endogenous caveolae [ ], while EHD2 overexpression lead to the confinement of cholera toxin B within caveolae [ ]. Based on our findings, we propose that EHD2 may not only serve as a potential target for CEP’s anti-ASFV activity but could also be a key factor in understanding the entry mechanisms of ASFV. Further research on EHD2 could provide deeper insights into ASFV pathogenesis and help develop targeted antiviral strategies.
Nevertheless, our study has several specific limitations. First, while transcriptome analysis suggested a significant regulatory effect of CEP on cholesterol metabolism pathways, we provided no direct evidence to demonstrate how CEP regulates cholesterol to inhibit ASFV entry. The specific impact of CEP on cholesterol accumulation or depletion remains to be clarified. Second, although we observed that CEP reversed the expression of several genes involved in the CME pathway, such as EGFR and AP2, we did not further investigate CEP’s regulatory effect on these genes. Additionally, given EHD2's role in caveolae and micropinocytosis pathways, EHD2 gene regulation by upstream factors also requires further validation. Third, our study had areas for improvement in animal challenge experiments. For instance, optimizing the route of administration (e.g. switching from intramuscular to oral or inhalation methods), adjusting the dosage of the compounds, and increasing the number of experimental pigs could improve the experimental design. Future studies should further explore the anti-ASFV activity of CEP in more refined settings.
In conclusion, we screened 6 effective anti-ASFV compounds and, for the first time, reported that CEP can inhibit ASFV replication by blocking viral entry. We elucidated the antiviral mechanism of CEP through transcriptomic analysis and identified EHD2 as a key target. Although CEP offers only partial protection against ASFV in pigs, it holds promise as a potential component of combination therapy, working with other drugs to inhibit ASFV at various stages of its life cycle. Our findings provide novel insights and potential strategies for ASF prevention and control.

## Ethics statements
All ASFV-related experiments were carried out in the BSL-3 laboratory, and the animals were handled in strict accordance with good animal practices as outlined in the Animal Ethics Procedures and Guidelines of the People’s Republic of China. The study was approved by the Animal Welfare Committee of the China Animal Health and Epidemiology Center (DWFL-2023-03).

## Cells, virus, and cepharanthine
Porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs) were isolated from healthy specific pathogen free (SPF) Bama miniature piglets as described in the previously published paper with minor modifications [ ]. Briefly, alveolar macrophages were collected from 40-day old piglets confirmed to be free of Porcine circovirus (PCV), Porcine parvovirus (PPV), Porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV), and ASFV by SYBR green based qPCR. The cells were maintained in fresh Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Hyclone), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 50 mg/mL streptomycin (Penicillin–Streptomycin Solution, Hyclone). PAMs were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The ASFV China/LN/2018/1 strain (GenBank accession number OP856591.1) used in this study was maintained by China Animal Health and Epidemiology Center (CAHEC), Shandong province, China. Cepharanthine (S4238, CAS: 481-49-2, Selleck, Shanghai China) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as stocks.

## Anti-ASFV compound screening and determination of the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of compounds
The cell-based enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (cell-based ELISA) was performed to screen effective anti-ASFV compounds. PAMs were seeded at a density 2 × 105 cells/well in 96-well plates. After 24 h, the cells were pretreated with various compounds (at a concentration of 4 μM) for 2 h. Following pretreatment, the cells were infected with ASFV at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 for 2 h at 37°C. The cells were gently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and maintained in a DMEM medium containing the same compounds used for pretreatment. After an appropriate incubation period, the cells were fixed and permeabilized using 4% formaldehyde and 0.1% Triton X-100, respectively, at 37°C for 30 min. The cells were washed with Glycine-PBS (0.02 M glycine in PBS) and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h at 37°C. Subsequently, the cells were incubated sequentially with primary and secondary antibodies, following the manufacturer’s instruction. Finally, 100 µL of TMB solution was added to each well, and the cells were incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 50 μL/well of 2% H2SO4. The OD450 value was measured to calculate the inhibition rate. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was calculated based on the OD450 values.

## Cytotoxicity assay
The cytotoxic effect of CEP on PAMs was measured by conducting the CCK-8 assay according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well in a 96-well plate. After 12 h of culture, the cells were treated with various concentrations of CEP (0, 5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 μM). After 24 h, fresh DMEM containing 10% CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader to assess cell viability. The CEP concentration that induced 50% cytotoxicity (CC50) was calculated based on the absorbance data.

## Western blotting
PAMs were lysed in 2×SDS sample buffer (100 mM Tris-Cl, pH 6.8, 20% glycerol, 4% SDS, 10% β-mercaptoethanol, and 0.12% bromophenol blue). The lysates were boiled at 96°C for 10 min. The samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE and then transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (Pall Corp). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in PBS with 0.5% Tween 20 (PBST) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated with the corresponding primary antibody overnight at 4°C. Following primary incubation, the membrane were incubated with secondary antibodies for 6 h at 4°C. Protein bands were visualized using the enhanced ECL reagent.

## Co-immunoprecipitation
Cells were collected in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.05% NP-40 supplemented with PIC). The lysates were incubated with anti-Flag antibody and protein A/G agarose beads (Sigma), at 4°C for 4 h to allow for the formation of immune complex. The beads were washed five times with ice-cold lysis buffer to remove unbound proteins and nonspecific interactions. The precipitated protein complexes were mixed with 2xSDS sample buffer. The samples were then subjected to Western blot analysis to detect the presence of the target proteins.

## Immunofluorescence microscopy
PAMs were seeded on coverslips and treated according to the specific experimental requirements. The cells were permeabilized with 4% formaldehyde and 0.1% Triton X-100 at 37°C for 30 min. Following permeabilization, the cells were washed with 0.02 M glycine-PBS to neutralize residual formaldehyde. PAMs were blocked with 5% BSA in PBS at 37°C for 30 min. The cells were incubated with the primary antibody at 37°C for 1 h. After primary antibody incubation, the cells were washed thrice with PBST, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody at 37°C for 30 min. The cells were washed thrice with PBST. The nuclei were stained with DAPI containing an anti-fade Dabco solution. The images were obtained using a laser confocal microscope (Leica STELLARIS, LAS X software, Germany).

## Assay of the effect of CEP on ASFV entry
For the ASFV entry assay, PAMs were pretreated with CEP (4μM) for 2 h. The treatment involved incubating the cells at 37°C for 1 h, followed by 4°C for 1 h. The cells were washed with ice-cold PBS to remove any residual CEP. The PAMs were then infected with ASFV (5 MOI) for 2 h at 4°C. The culture medium was replaced with fresh culture medium with or without CEP (4 μM). The PAMs were incubated for an additional 2 h at 37°C to allow for virus entry. Non-internalized virions were washed out with citrate buffer (pH 3.0) to ensure only internalized ASFV was present. The IFA assay was performed to check the localization of ASFV early proteins, specifically ASFV-p30 and ASFV-I73R.

## RNA-Seq library preparation
The PAMs were seeded at 4 × 106 cells/well in 6-well plates. The cells were divided into four groups in triplicates, namely Mock group, Virus group, CEP group, and CEP + Virus group, for RNA preparation for the transcriptome analysis ( A). In the pretreatment stage, Mock and Virus groups were pretreated with DMSO (4 μM). The CEP and CEP + Virus groups were pretreated with CEP (4 μM) for 2 h. In the virus infection stage, Virus and CEP + Virus groups were infected with ASFV (1 MOI) for 2 h. In the post-infection stage, the supernatants of each group were replaced with a medium containing DMSO or CEP. The cells were then maintained until 24 h post-infection (hpi). Finally, the cells were harvested for RNA extraction using TRIzol. Total RNA was sent for transcriptome sequencing to OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). RNA purity and quantification were evaluated using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). Its integrity was assessed using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Libraries were constructed using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA LT Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Transcriptome sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform, generating 125 bp/150 bp paired-end reads. The raw reads in FASTQ format were processed using Trimmomatic [ ] to remove low-quality bases and adapter sequences from reads with parameters LEADING:3 TRAILING:3 ILLUMINACLIP:TruSeq3-PE- for paired-end reads. Trimmed reads for each sample were mapped to the reference genome of Sus scrofa (pig), Sscrofa11.1 (GCA_000003025.6) using HISAT2 (v2.1.0) with parameters –rna-strandness rf –fr [ ].
To clarity in comparing groups in our study, the format A-B (A Case- VS- B Control) was used. For example, CEP-Mock represents the CEP group versus the Mock group, where the CEP group is the case and the Mock group is the control.

## RNA-Seq analysis
Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were analyzed using the DESeq2 in R [ ]. The false discovery rate (FDR) q value was calculated by adjusting P values using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Adjusted P value < 0.05 and fold change > 2 or < 0.5 were established as thresholds for identifying statistically significant DEGs. Hierarchical clustering of DEGs was performed to explore gene expression patterns across in different groups and samples. GO enrichment and KEGG [ ] pathway enrichment analyze of DEGs were conducted using R. The volcano plots and heatmaps were generated using the ggplot2 package in R and the website tool provided by OE Biotech (https://cloud.oebiotech.cn/task/). For the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), the background files used as the reference files for gene set enrichment included Virus-Mock, CEP-Mock, (CEP + Virus)-Mock, and CEP-reversed DEGs. Gene set information and the KEGG database were provided by OE Biotech (Date: June 2023). Gene set enrichment P values, normalized enrichment score (NES) values, and FDR values were calculated as recommended and run in the Signal to Noise mode. Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks were constructed using Metascape (https://metascape.org/, v3.5.20230101) with default parameters, utilizing the human genome database [ ]. The networks were visualized using Cytoscape [ ].

## Quantitative real-Time PCR
The total RNA was extracted from the cells by using the Simply P Total RNA Extraction Kit (BioFlux, CN) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA sample integrity was confirmed, ensuring the absence of DNA or protein contamination. For reverse transcription, the Hifair II 1st Strand cDNA synthesis kit with a gDNA digester was utilized, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative Real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed on a CFX96Touch (Bio-rad) system using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix. Primers, synthesized by Ruibo Biotech (Shandong, China), are detailed in Supplementary Table 2. RT-qPCR amplification was conducted with an initial heating step at heating to 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles consisting of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 30 s. A melting curve was generated to confirm the specificity of amplification. Each sample was analyzed in independent triplicates.

## Animal experiments
The animal experiments were conducted at the Animal Biosafety Level 3 facility at China Animal Health and Epidemiology Center, with ethics approval from the Animal Ethics Committee. Chinese Bama minipigs (42 days old) were randomly divided into three groups, namely Mock group (ASFV-/CEP-, n = 2), ASFV group (n = 3), and CEP + ASFV group (n = 3). The CEP + ASFV group was intramuscularly injected with CEP (4.44 mg/kg) daily for 2 days before the ASFV challenge. The injection was continued every 48 h after the virus challenge until the end of the experiment. The ASFV and CEP + ASFV groups were infected intramuscularly with 50 HAD50 ASFV China/LN/2018/1 on the challenge day. During the experiment, the animal’s rectal temperature was monitored and recorded daily, along with clinical signs and survival rates. Moreover, oral, nasal and rectal swabs were collected for virus copy detection. Necropsy was performed in a timely manner on decreased or moribund pigs, with the collection of tissue samples, including heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and submaxillary lymph node for histopathological examination and viral load analysis.

## Statistical analysis
All experiments were performed at least in triplicate. Results were analyzed using GraphPad Prism and are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD). Statistical significance was determined using the two-tailed Student unpaired-sample t-test (*, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001).
