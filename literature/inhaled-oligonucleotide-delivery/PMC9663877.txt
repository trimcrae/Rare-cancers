## Abstract
Herein, we present the structure-based design, synthesis and biological evaluation of novel mono- and di-carboxylic 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-2(1H)-one derivatives as potential heme-independent activators of soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC). Docking calculations of several known sGC agonists by utilizing both a homology model of human sGC β1 Η-ΝΟΧ domain and a recent cryo-EM structure of the same domain guided the structural optimization of various designed compounds. Among these, mono- and di-carboxylic 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-2(1H)-one derivatives arose as promising candidate sGC activators. A series of such compounds was synthesized and assessed for their effect on sGC activity. None of them was able to trigger any detectable activation of native sGC in prostate cancer (LnCaP) or rat aortic smooth muscle (A7r5) cells, even after loss of heme by treatment with the heme oxidant ODQ. Furthermore, selected derivatives did not exhibit any antagonistic effect against the known heme-independent sGC activator BAY 60-2770 nor any additive or synergistic effect with the heme-dependent NO donor sodium nitroprusside (SNP) on heme-associated sGC in A7r5 cells. However, when tested in vitro using purified recombinant sGC enzyme, the dicarboxylic 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-2(1H)-one derivative 30d was able to increase the enzymatic activity of both the wild-type α1/β1 sGC dimer (by 4.4-fold, EC50 = 0.77 μΜ) as well as the heme-free α1/β1 His105Ala mutant sGC (by 4.8-fold, EC50 = 1.8 μΜ). Notably, the activity of compound 30d towards the mutant α1/β1 Η105A enzyme was comparable with that previously reported by us for the bona fide activator BAY 60-2770, using the functionally equivalent wild-type sGC preparation treated with ODQ. These results indicate that compound 30d can indeed act as a promising sGC activator and may serve as a basic structure in the design of novel, optimized analogues with enhanced sGC agonistic activity and improved efficiency in cell-based and in vivo systems.
Keywords: Nitric oxide; Soluble guanylyl cyclase; sGC activators; 3,4-Dihydroquinoxalin-2(1H)-one

## Introduction
Soluble guanylyl cyclase (sGC), a α/β-heterodimeric enzyme, is the primary receptor of endogenously produced nitric oxide (NO) or of NO delivered by donor molecules and catalyzes the conversion of guanosine triphosphate (GTP) to the second messenger cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) [ ]. The prosthetic heme moiety, located in the Heme-NO OXygen domain (H-NOX) of the regulatory β1 subunit, binds NO with high affinity, triggering an increase of catalytic activity [ ]. Through this mechanism, the NO/sGC/cGMP pathway promotes vasodilation and inhibits smooth muscle proliferation, platelet aggregation and negative vascular remodeling by modulating the activity of a number of downstream targets, such as protein kinases, cGMP-gated ion channels and phosphodiesterases [ ]. There is significant preclinical and clinical evidence that dysregulation of this signaling pathway, either by impaired NO bioavailability or by heme oxidation and conversion of sGC to its NO-unresponsive apo-enzyme state in oxidative stress conditions, contributes to various disorders, such as pulmonary hypertension, heart failure, glaucoma and chronic kidney disease [ ]. During the last two decades, novel treatments of the aforementioned disorders have targeted the sGC, aiming to restore or augment its enzymatic activity.
Through these efforts, two new classes of compounds that are capable of activating sGC in an NO-independent manner have been discovered, distinct from NO donors; heme-dependent sGC “stimulators” and heme-independent sGC “activators” ( ) [ ]. Because of their broad clinical potential, sGC stimulators attract increasing interest from research groups and pharmaceutical companies [ , ], exemplified by two approved per os drugs, riociguat and vericiguat [ , ]. Riociguat (Adempas®) was approved in 2013 for the treatment of two forms of pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) and chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) [ , , , ]. More recently, FDA approved vericiguat (Verquvo®) for the treatment of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) [ , ]. Besides these two compounds, a rising number of sGC stimulators have been introduced in clinical studies for the treatment of various diseases, e.g., praliciguat is in clinical phase II for the treatment of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and diabetic nephropathy, whereas olinciguat is in clinical phase II trials for the treatment of achalasia and sickle cell disease [ ].
 Despite the progress in the clinic, there is still insufficient information regarding the mechanism of action of stimulators. Recent studies, involving Nuclear Resonance Spectroscopy (NMR) and cryogenic Electron Microscopy (cryo-EM) methods, applied to complexes of known sGC stimulators with either bacterial H-NOX domains or with human sGC, pointed to two main hypotheses regarding their binding mode to the enzyme. The first postulates that the stimulators’ binding pocket resides in the β1 Η-ΝΟΧ domain, whereas the second suggests that the binding pocket is formed by the interface of three different domains: the β1 Η-ΝΟΧ, the α1 subunit and the β1 Coiled-coil (CC) domain [ , ].
In comparison, sGC activators’ mode of action has been better characterized in numerous publications over the years [ , ]. All of these studies are in agreement with the hypothesis that these compounds activate the NO-insensitive forms of sGC through interaction with residues that bind heme in a pocket located in the β1 Η-ΝΟΧ domain. More specifically, the development of crucial hydrogen-bonding interactions with Tyr135, Ser137 and Arg139, also known as the Y-S-R motif, mimics those formed by the two propionic groups of the heme moiety.
In contrast to the stimulators, the clinical development of sGC activators has encountered several challenges. A typical example is Cinaciguat, the most clinically advanced sGC activator. Phase IIb clinical trials of this compound, administered intravenously for the treatment of acute heart failure, were terminated due to a marked hypotensive adverse effect observed even at low doses [ ]. Another interesting sGC activator, ataciguat, which reached Phase II clinical trials for aortic valve stenosis, was also discontinued.
In an effort to reduce such systemic effects, the development of current sGC activators focuses, among others, on other pathophysiological indications, where local application is a possibility. Recent examples are ocular instillation of MGV354 and inhalation of BAY 1211163, which were introduced in Phase II clinical studies for the treatment of open-angle glaucoma and acute respiratory distress syndrome, respectively [ , ].
Another interesting approach to deal with the poor clinical profile of sGC activators was presented by Elgert et al. [ ]. Based on this, second generation of sGC activators, mainly consisting of monocarboxylic derivatives, act as partial agonists of sGC through the formation of a non-stabilizing dead-end complex with sGC. Due to their binding and activation profile, monocarboxylic sGC activators may not cause hypotension, as dicarboxylic derivatives do. This hypothesis was further supported by point mutation studies of the Y-S-R motif residues, which indicated differences in the binding mode between a full (BAY 60-2770) and a partial sGC agonist (BAY-543). The first formed hydrogen-bonding interactions with all three Y-S-R motif residues, whereas the second selectively developed hydrogen-bonding interactions with Tyr135 only [ ]. Based on the aforementioned pharmacological studies and focusing on a sGC activator with improved pharmacokinetic properties, Bayer recently identified the orally administered monocarboxylic acid runcaciguat via high-throughput screening. This second generation sGC activator is currently investigated in a clinical phase II trials in patients with chronic kidney disease and nonproliferative diabetic retinopathy [ ].
Motivated by these observations, we decided to focus on the discovery of novel sGC activators through a structure-based design approach. Initially, we performed in silico analysis of the binding mode of known full and partial sGC agonists in the heme binding pocket. The absence of PDB-submitted complexes of these compounds with the human sGC β1 Η-ΝΟΧ domain led us to the utilization of computational methods. Thus, a homology model of human sGC β1 Η-ΝΟΧ domain was constructed and used docking calculations of known sGC activators. Detailed analysis indicated crucial structural features of these molecules that were involved into residue-specific interactions with the β1 Η-ΝΟΧ domain. Based on these findings, further docking calculations of several designed compounds were performed, among which monocarboxylic 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-2(1H)-one derivatives exhibited promising binding modes. Further attempts to increase the binding affinity of these derivatives through enhancement of hydrogen-bonding and van der Waals interactions with the Y-S-R motif and a previously unoccupied hydrophobic pocket, led to the design of potentially more effective dicarboxylic 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-2(1H)-ones. Herein, we describe the results of our computational studies, the synthesis of selected designed compounds and the evaluation of their ability to stimulate sGC activity.

## Results and discussion
2.1.

## Computational studies and design
Homology modeling of human β1 H-NOX domain (hH-NOX) was carried out using the X-ray crystal structure of the corresponding domain from the cyanobacterium Nostoc sp. as template (NsH-NOX, PDB ID: 2O09) [ ]. Based on their sequence identity of 35.1% (Figure S1) an acceptable homology model of hH-NOX domain was obtained using Modeller [ ]. Subsequently, the homology model was subjected to energy minimization using UCSF Chimera [ ]. Superimposition of the 3D structures obtained before and after the energy minimization indicated subtle changes, mostly at the helices forming the heme binding pocket (Figure S4). Before docking of the designed compounds, we assessed the performance of AutoDock (v.4.2.6) [ ] to identify the binding pose of the heme prosthetic group. Indeed, AutoDock predicted the crystallographic pose of NsH-NOX heme within a mean root-mean-square deviation of 0.7 Å in 99/100 docking rounds and with a mean estimated free energy of binding (ΔGest) of –17.9 kcal/mol. Inspection of the complex revealed hydrogen-bonding interactions between heme's propionate groups and residues Tyr2, Tyr135, Ser137, Arg139, as well as van der Waals interactions with residues of helix aF (Leu101, Leu104, His105, Leu108) and helix aG (Leu142, Ile145, Val146, Ile149).
Subsequently, we carried out molecular docking of two known activators with hH-NOX, ataciguat (HMR-1766) and cinaciguat. The top-ranked cluster of ataciguat (54% population, mean ΔGest = –10.23 kcal/mol) displayed hydrogen-bonding interactions between the amide –NH of the ligand and the imidazole side chain of His105, and between the morpholine ring of ataciguat and the side chain of Arg139. A number of van der Waals interactions were also formed with residues of the aF and aG helices, similar to those formed by heme (Figure S5). In contrast to ataciguat, the high flexibility of cinaciguat (7 and 17 rotatable bonds respectively) gave rise to a large number of different bound conformations (17 clusters with >1 pose). Visual investigation of the 2 top-ranked clusters (12% and 6% populations with mean ΔGest of –13.97 kcal/mol and –13.24 kcal/mol, respectively) revealed hydrogen-bonding interactions between the carboxylate groups of cinaciguat and the Y-S-R motif (Figure S6), in accordance with previously published data.19 Hydrophobic-aromatic interactions between the phenoxy or terminal phenethyl moiety of cinaciguat and residues of helices αF and αG were also formed.
The increased flexibility of known sGC activators within the heme binding pocket, combined with their moderate mean binding energies, led us to design new compounds with rigid heterocyclic skeletons that can be functionalized appropriately. First, considering the relatively short distance between the amide and sulfonamide –NH groups of ataciguat (equivalent to 2–3 C–C bonds) we decided to incorporate them into a fused bicyclic heterocycle. Then, we incorporated carboxylic substituents in specific positions of the bicyclic scaffold in order to enhance hydrogen-bonding interactions with the Y-S-R motif residues (Figure S7A). In an attempt to exploit the available hydrophobic cavities of hH-NOX (Figure S7B), we introduced suitably substituted heteroaromatic rings at either NH positions of the skeleton.
Furthermore, the spatial arrangement of the –NH groups in the bicyclic skeleton was probed aiming at the optimal orientation of their substituents within the heme binding pocket. On this basis we designed 4 compounds and performed docking calculations (C1–C4, Table S1). Comparison of their top-ranked poses indicated the 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-2(1H)-one derivative C4 as the most promising ligand (28% population, mean ΔGest = –10.60 kcal/mol). As predicted for cinaciguat, C4 displayed hydrogen-bonding interactions between the amide-attached carboxylate and residues Tyr2, Arg116 and Arg139 ( A, B). Additionally, the pyrrole substituent, attached to the secondary amide through amide linkage, displayed similar van der Waals interactions with Leu101 (aF helix) and Val146, Ile149 (aG helix) as those observed for heme.
 We also investigated the potential replacement of the pyrrole moiety of C4 by other heteroaromatic rings with the designed compounds C5–C9. However, their docking results did not display any significant improvement of the estimated binding affinities (Table S1). Therefore, we maintained the pyrrole substituent as a structural feature of our compounds during the optimization process and we planned to synthesize monocarboxylic 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-2(1H)-one derivatives decorated with selected heteroaromatic substituents at the N4 position of the skeleton to gain insights into structure activity relationships.
In order to enhance binding affinity of C4, a thorough investigation of the heme binding site was undertaken. During this process, two previously unoccupied cavities by C4 were revealed. These involve, a hydrophilic cavity, that incorporates residues Tyr135, Ser137 of the Y-S-R motif, as well as a hydrophobic one, formed mostly by alkyl and aromatic side chains of residues, situated in helices αA and αF ( C). In an attempt to exploit the hydrophilic cavity, the decoration of the 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-2(1H)-one skeleton involved the introduction of several phenyl- or phenoxy-substituents with an additional carboxylic group, at position C7 of the basic skeleton. The newly designed compounds C10–C13 were introduced to docking studies (Table S1). Among them, dicarboxylic 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-2(1H)-one derivative C13 exhibited enhanced binding affinity, as the 4-oxy-benzoic acid substituent at C7 presented optimal proximity to the aforementioned residues.
Furthermore, the addition of a phenyl group at position C6 of the basic skeleton contributed to the efficient exploitation of the hydrophobic cavity and led to the optimized dicarboxylic derivative C14, which showed increased stability (population C4 = 29% vs C14 = 70%) and binding affinity (mean ΔGest
C4 = −10.60 kcal/mol vs C14 = −15.65 kcal/mol) within the heme binding pocket. The improved binding mode of C14 were basically attributed to the increased amount of hydrogen-bonding and van der Waals interactions with residues of heme binding pocket ( A). Notably, superimposition of the docked conformations of heme and C14 revealed a similar orientation, especially regarding the two carboxyl groups of C14, which are crucial for the establishment of a hydrogen-bonding network with the Y-S-R motif ( B, C).
 Interestingly, during the course of this project, various cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of human sGC were resolved, in active and inactive state [ , ]. We decided to verify our initial design rationale by utilizing this available information and conducted docking calculations of the known sGC activators and designed compounds C1–C14 with β1 H-NOX domain, obtained from a cryo-EM structure of human sGC in the heme-oxidized state (PDB ID: 6JT1) [ ], which is targeted by sGC activators. Analysis of the results revealed a similar classification of the known and designed compounds based on their mean estimated binding energy and population of their top-ranked clusters (Table S1), supporting our scaffold optimization strategy. Notably, the interaction analysis of selected designed compounds (e.g. C14) bound to the heme binding pocket revealed their similar orientation in the heme cavity as well as the formation of hydrogen-bonding interactions with the Y-S-R motif, especially with Arg139, and other residues nearby, such as Tyr2, Arg116 and Glu138 (Figure S8). Based on these results, we proceeded to the synthesis of a series of mono- and di-carboxylic 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-2(1H)-one derivatives which incorporated structural features, capable of promoting binding properties and activation of sGC, as it was indicated by our molecular design studies. Thus, various substituents were introduced at position N4, C6 and C7 of the bicyclic quinoxalin-2(1H)-one skeleton of the target molecules, depending on the synthetic challenges might emerged from their introduction, in order to elucidate the relationships between the ligand estimated binding affinities and the sGC agonistic activity.

2.2.

## Synthesis
Synthesis of the target compounds was accomplished through four 3,4-dihydroquinoxalinone intermediates (4, 8, 20, 22) which were diversified in the final steps to yield the desired mono- and di-carboxylic 3,4-dihydroquinoxalinone derivatives.
Initially, the construction of the appropriately substituted 3,4-dihydroquinoxalinones 4 and 8 that would lead to the target monocarboxylic compounds was attempted. Condensation of commercially available o-phenylenediamine 1 with ethyl glyoxalate following previously described conditions afforded quinoxalin-2(1H)-one 2 in 85% yield ( ) [ ]. Treatment with freshly synthesized benzyl 4-(bromomethyl)benzoate (9) and sodium hydride provided the N-benzylated lactam derivative 3 in 64% yield [ ]. Finally, imine reduction with sodium cyanoborohydride afforded the 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-1(2H)-one derivative 4 in excellent yield (98%). Alternatively, selective bromination of 2 using bromine in acetic acid gave the C7 brominated quinoxalin-2(1H)-one 5 in 90% yield which upon benzylation under the aforementioned conditions furnished the corresponding N-benzylated lactam 6 in 48% yield [ ]. Subsequently, 6 was introduced to Suzuki cross-coupling conditions with phenylboronic acid providing the C7-phenylated derivative 7. The latter was reduced efficiently using sodium cyanoborohydride to the dihydroquinoxalinone 8 in 98% yield.
 Another route, outlined in , was employed for the appropriate decoration of the 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-1(2H)-one core that eventually would furnish the target dicarboxylic compounds. Thus, commercially available 3-bromoaniline 10 was introduced in a series of previously reported transformations, including p-bromination (11), amine acetylation (12), o-nitration (13) and acetamide acidic hydrolysis, to furnish the 4,5-dibromo-2-nitroaniline 14 with a total yield of 51% [ ]. Subsequently, selective etherification of 14 at C5 position was achieved by treatment with freshly prepared benzyl 4-hydroxybenzoate (23) affording the diphenyl ether 15 [ ]. Next, chloroacetylation of 15 to the anilide 16, followed by reduction of the nitro group using metallic iron in acidic conditions, provided the substituted aniline 17. The latter was introduced to a halogen-exchange Finkelstein reaction using sodium iodide and the generated intermediate alkyl iodide was cyclized in situ via intramolecular nucleophilic attack by the amine group to the resulting substituted quinoxalin-2(1H)-one derivative 18. Upon treatment with 9 and NaH, N-benzylated lactam 19 was obtained in 68% yield. Finally, imine reduction with sodium cyanoborohydride afforded the 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-1(2H)-one derivative 20, while Suzuki cross-coupling with phenylboronic acid followed by imine reduction furnished the corresponding C7-phenylated derivative 22.
 Further diversification of the dihydroquinoxalinone cores 4, 8, 20 and 22 was achieved by amide coupling of secondary amine with several heteroaromatic acyl chlorides (24–26) in the presence of triethylamine as base. Following this methodology, amide derivatives 27(a–e) and 29(a–e) were obtained with overall good yields (23-99 %). Finally, cleavage of the benzyl ester protective group by applying catalytic hydrogenolysis conditions using ammonium formate and palladium on activated carbon as catalyst furnished the target mono- and dicarboxylic 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-1(2H)-one derivatives, 28(a–e) and 30(a–e), respectively. Notably, the deprotection of di-benzyl ester derivatives 29(a–b) was accompanied by unavoidable debromination of the skeleton ( ).

## Evaluation of bioactivity
The biological activity of the synthesized derivatives was evaluated via a cGMP ELISA assay in LNCaP and A7r5 RAoSM cells, which naturally express the soluble guanylate cyclase (sGC) α1β1 isoenzyme [ , ]. The assay was performed both in cells pretreated or not with the heme oxidant 1H-[1,2,4]oxadiazolo[4,3-a]quinoxalin-1-one (ODQ), which has been previously described to potentiate the effect of heme- and NO-independent activators on the function of the sGC, while it reduces those of heme-dependent activators, such as the NO donor sodium nitroprusside (SNP) [ ]. Results were determined as cGMP levels normalized for total protein (pmol/mg) in the respective well and are represented as means ± SD of fold-induction relatively to untreated (Control, CTL) cells.
2.3.1.

## Bioactivity of monocarboxylic 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-1(2H)-one derivatives in LnCaP cells
In LNCaP cells, the oxidant ODQ had no significant effect by itself on basal cGMP levels. Both the NO donor SNP and the heme-independent sGC activator BAY 60-2770 raised cGMP levels similarly in ODQ-untreated (naïve) cells. As expected, the effect of SNP was abrogated by pretreatment with ODQ, while the effect of BAY 60-2770 was enhanced ( ). None of the monocarboxylic derivatives tested elicited a significant response, either in the absence or presence of ODQ. Therefore, at the concentrations and cellular background we conducted our tests, the synthesized molecules are unable to trigger by themselves detectable activation of sGC. Indicatively, we present the results of the compounds 28a, 28c and 28d in .
 
2.3.2.

## Bioactivity of dicarboxylic 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-1(2H)-one derivatives in A7r5 RAoSM cells
The oxidant ODQ did not significantly affect basal cGMP levels in A7r5 RAoSM cells, either. The control sGC agonists, SNP and BAY 60-2770, exhibited an identical pattern of biological activity and sensitivity to ODQ to that seen in LNCaP cells. None of the dicarboxylic derivatives tested elicited a raise in cGMP levels, either on naïve or ODQ-treated cells. Indicatively, we present the results of the compounds 30b, 30d and 30e in .
 
2.3.3.

## Determination of potential antagonism or synergy with BAY 60-2770 and SNP
Since the new 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-1(2H)-one derivatives, primarily designed to activate the heme-free sGC, were unable to elicit cGMP generation on their own, we also examined the possibility that they act as inhibitors of the enzyme. For this purpose, two selected mono- and di-carboxylic derivatives, 28e and 30d respectively, were tested at a concentration of 30 μM for their ability to modify the effect of 1 μM BAY 60-2770, in the presence of ODQ (i.e. absence of heme). The selected derivatives were added 15 min before BAY 60-2770. The cGMP-raising effect of BAY 60-2770 alone (7.06 ± 2.02-fold increase) remained unaltered by pretreatment with 30-fold Molar excess of either 28e or 30d (7.20 ± 2.79-fold and 6.38 ± 1.24-fold, respectively) ( ). Consequently, we conclude that these compounds, in the concentrations and context used, do not antagonize the binding mode of and thus the cGMP generation by BAY 60-2770.
 The sGC activators can typically stimulate cellular sGC activity even in the absence of ODQ ( ) and can evoke an additive or synergistic effect when NO is present [ ]. Based on this, we sought to determine whether a potentiating effect of the newly synthesized 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-1(2H)-ones could be detected towards an NO donor, such as SNP. For this, among the synthesized derivatives, we selected the dicarboxylic derivative 30d and compared its ability to modify the effect of SNP (therefore in the absence of ODQ) with that of the bona fide sGC activator BAY 60-2770. This time, SNP and BAY 60-2770 individually induced cGMP levels (2.78 ± 1.34 and 4.02 ± 1.23-fold of Control, respectively) and produced an additive effect (9.29 ± 3.14-fold) when added together ( ). In contrast, the effect of SNP was not affected by the simultaneous addition of 30d (2.19 ± 0.68-fold). Therefore, compound 30d, under the conditions used, does not enhance or inhibit the effect of an NO donor on the heme-associated enzyme.
 
2.3.4.

## Bioactivity of new 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-1(2H)-one derivatives on purified recombinant sGC
We hypothesized that the inactivity of our 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-1(2H)-one derivatives to act as sGC agonists in cell-based assays might be due in part to suboptimal physicochemical properties, such as impaired cellular permeability, and therefore we decided to test their activity in vitro using purified recombinant sGC enzyme [ ]. To this end, we assayed selected derivatives which were substituted at position N1, N4, C6 and C7 of the 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-1(2H)-one skeleton with moieties which may display enhanced sGC binding affinity, such as the monocarboxylic derivatives 28a and 28e, and the dicarboxylic compounds 30b and 30d. Experiments were performed in preparations of the wild-type α1/β1 as well as with the heme-free α1/β1 His105Ala mutant enzyme [ ]. Results are shown as sGC activity expressed in nmol cGMP/(mg x min) generated, as means ± SD of three independent experiments ( ).
 Among the four tested compounds, the dicarboxylic derivative 30d bearing a N4 furanoyl, a C6 phenyl and a C7 4-carboxyphenoxy moiety, exhibited potent sGC agonistic activity, followed by 30b, whereas the monocarboxylic derivatives displayed no activity. However, the latter molecule showed statistically-significant activity only on the mutant enzyme and only at the highest concentration (10 μΜ). Specifically, compound 30d increased the activity of the wild-type sGC α1/β1 enzyme by as much as 4.4-fold (from 61.59 ± 15.45 nmol cGMP/mg x min to 271.0 ± 113.8 nmol cGMP/mg x min, at 10 μΜ), exhibiting an apparent EC50 value = 0.77 μΜ ( A). Crucially, the ability of 30d to increase the activity of the heme-free sGC α1/β1 His105Ala mutant enzyme was undiminished, since it raised sGC activity by 4.8-fold (from 112.0 ± 63.38 nmol cGMP/mg x min to 541.5 ± 55.21 nmol cGMP/mg x min) at the same maximal concentration, with an apparent EC50 value = 1.8 μΜ ( B).
 Comparing the activity of dicarboxylic derivatives 30b and 30d, it is clearly evident that not only the N1 and C7 carboxylic substituents, but also the presence of the C6 phenyl group significantly improved the sGC agonistic activity. These results are in agreement with the information obtained from docking simulations of designed analogues, where C6-phenylated dicarboxylic derivative C14 presented better binding mode compared to the non-phenylated one, C13. This observation is also in line with our applied design approach which indicated that the introduction of an aromatic moiety at the C6 position of the 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-1(2H)-one skeleton could not only enhance the binding affinity through efficient van der Waals interactions with specific residues of the heme binding pocket, but also improve the activation of sGC elicited by this compound.
It is noteworthy that the compound 30d induces maximum enzyme activity at the highest-tested concentration (10 μM) comparable with that of the bona fide activator BAY 60-2770, as it was determined previously by us using the functionally equivalent (i.e. heme-free) ODQ-treated wild-type enzyme [ ].

3.

## Conclusions
In summary, guided by a structure-based design approach we synthesized appropriately substituted mono- and di-carboxylic 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-2(1H)-one derivatives as potential sGC activators, i.e. molecules capable of stimulating the heme-free enzyme. Although the initial design strategy was based on a homology model of hH-NOX, we have verified our approach using a recent cryo-EM structure of the same domain. A series of synthesized compounds were subsequently evaluated in intact LnCaP prostate cancer or A7r5 vascular smooth muscle cells, however we were unable to detect enzyme activation in either the absence or presence of the prosthetic heme group, effected by the use of the heme-oxidant ODQ. Furthermore, the monocarboxylic derivative 28e and the dicarboxylic compound 30d, both at 3-fold excess concentrations, did not exhibit any antagonistic effect against the heme-independent sGC activator BAY 60-2770 in the presence of ODQ, whereas derivative 30d did not display any additive or synergistic effect with the known NO donor SNP on the heme-associated enzyme in A7r5 RAoSM cells.
In contrast, among four selected mono- and dicarboxylic derivatives, the dicarboxylic molecules compounds 30b and 30d alone upregulated the activity of the purified recombinant sGC enzyme in vitro. Significantly, 30d increased the activity of both the wild-type α1/β1 (4.4-fold) as well as the mutant α1/β1 His105Ala (4.8-fold) sGC at the maximal concentration tested (10 μΜ), with EC50 values of 0.77 and 1.8 μΜ, respectively. It is also noteworthy that the ability of 30d to stimulate the α1/β1 Η105A (heme-free) enzyme is comparable with that of the known activator BAY 60-2770, reported previously by us, against the functionally equivalent ODQ-treated wild-type purified enzyme (i.e. in the absence of the heme moiety), over the same range of concentrations [ ]. These last bioactivity data indicate that the addition of appropriate substituents at positions N1, N4, C6 and C7 of the 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-2(1H)-one skeleton, as indicated by our structure-based design approach, may result to generation of new compounds with sGC agonistic activity characteristic of sGC “activators”. The future incorporation of optimal structural features to the new 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-2(1H)-one derivatives is expected not only to enhance their sGC binding and agonistic properties, but also to improve some of their suboptimal physicochemical characteristics, which may account for the lack of their activity in the cell-based assays. Undoubtedly, among all compounds presented herein, the dicarboxylic 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-2(1H)-one 30d seems the most promising starting point for the design of additional, optimized analogues with enhanced sGC agonistic activity and improved efficiency in intact cells.

4.1.

## Computational methods
The coordinates of the NsH-NOX crystal structure in complex with heme were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank with accession code: 2O09 [ ]. Residues 1-182 from chain A were used as template for the modeling of hH-NOX domain residues 1-184 (UniProt assessing code: Q02153) according to the sequence alignment obtained with Clustal Omega (Figure S1). Modeller v9.14 was used to produce 200 models of hH-NOX and the model with the lowest DOPE score was selected [ ]. Its quality was assessed using the Structural Analysis and Verification Server v6.0 (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/), which revealed a well-defined model (Figure S2). Next, hH-NOX coordinates were superimposed with the template structure of NsH-NOX using the MatchMaker extension of UCSF Chimera 1.10.1 [ ], for the preparation of hHNOX complex with the heme moiety at its crystallographic position. The complex was subjected to energy minimization using the AmberTools extension of UCSF Chimera 1.10.1 [ ], which was carried out with 1,000 and 4,000 steps of the steepest descent and the conjugate gradient methods, respectively (quality assessment in Figure S3). The energy-minimized model of hH-NOX – heme complex was used without heme for the molecular docking calculations of Ataciguat, Cinaciguat and of the designed compounds C1–C14. Structure coordinates of human sGC complex with heme moiety in oxidized state were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank with accession code: 6JT1 [ ]. Residues 1-184 from chain B were used without heme for the molecular docking calculations of the designed compounds C1–C14.
The initial 3D coordinates of ligands were obtained from SMILES representations using the program OMEGA v4.0.0.4 [ ]. Protein and ligands were assigned with united-atom types and Gasteiger partial charges using AutoDockTools v1.5.7. The search space was defined at the center of the heme-binding cavity as a grid box of 18.75 Å in each dimension (50 points × 0.375 Å spacing). One hundred docking rounds were performed with AutoDock v.4.2.6 using the Lamarckian genetic algorithm with the default parameters [ , ]. The maximum number of energy evaluations was set to 2.5×106 and the resulting conformations were clustered within a 2-Å cutoff. Hydrogen-bonding and van der Waals interactions were visually inspected using Discovery Studio Visualizer v20.1.0.19295 [ ], whereas rendering of figures was performed with PyMOL 2.4.0 [ ].

## Chemistry
All reagents were obtained commercially from Alfa Aesar, Sigma-Aldrich or Merck and used without further purification. Reactions involving moisture-sensitive reactants were conducted in flame-dried glassware under an atmosphere of argon. Reagents and anhydrous solvents were transferred via syringe. Analytical TLC was performed on Merck Silica gel 60 F254 on precoated silica gel plates. Visualization was accomplished under UV light (254 and 365 nm), by exposure to iodine vapors and by use of Seebach staining solution. Flash column chromatography was performed on silica gel (SDS 60A, 40–63 lm). Melting points were determined on an Haake Buchler melting point apparatus in capillary tubes and are uncorrected. The IR spectra were recorded on a FT-IR Jasco spectrophotometer and a FT-IR Spectrum100 Perkin Elmer spectrophotometer equipped with KBr pellet accessory. NMR spectra (1H and 13C) were recorded on a Bruker DPX 400 MHz (400 MHz for 1H, 100.6 MHz for 13C), a Bruker 600 MHz (600 MHz for 1H, 150.9 MHz for 13C) Avance III HD Ascend TM and a Bruker 700 MHz (700 MHz for 1H, 176.1 MHz for 13C) Avance III HD Ascend TM spectrometer. The chemical shifts (δ) are reported in parts per million (ppm) and the residual solvent signal is used as an internal standard. The following abbreviations are used for the proton spectra multiplicities: singlet (s), doublet (d), triplet (t), quartet (q), multiplet (m), broad signal (br) and combinations thereof. Coupling constants (J) are given in Hertz (Hz). Mass spectra were obtained on a single quadrupole LC/MS system of Agilent LC/MSD1260 Infinity II equipped with an ESI ion source.

## General procedure for the synthesis of the mono- and di-carboxylic 3,4-dihydroquinoxalin-1(2H)-one derivatives 28(a–e) and 30(a–e)
To a solution of appropriate mono- or di-benzyl ester (27a-e or 29a-e) in dry THF and EtOH abs. (50:50, v/v) were added Pd, 10% on activated carbon (0.5 equiv) and HCOONH4 (6 equiv) and the resulting mixture was stirred at rt for 4 h. The reaction mixture was filtered in vacuo and the obtained filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure. The resulting residue was acidified to a pH of 1 by addition of conc. HCl and the aqueous phase was extracted with EtOAc (3 × 15 mL). The combined organic layers were washed with brine, dried over Na2SO4 and filtered. The crude was concentrated in vacuo and purified via recrystallization with EtOAc and MeOH.

## Evaluation of bioactivity
4.3.1.

## Cell-based assays
A7r5 rat aortic smooth muscle cells (RAoSM) and LNCaP human prostate cancer epithelial cells (both from ATCC, Rockville, MD) were cultured in 48-well clusters in DMEM and RPMI growth media (Gibco™), respectively, supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (Gibco™) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Biosera).
After reaching confluence, cells were serum-starved for 2 h in serum-free media containing 0.1% BSA, whereupon they were pretreated with the heme-oxidizing compound 1H-[1,2,4]oxadiazolo [4,3-a]quinoxalin-1-one (ODQ, Cayman Chemicals) at 10 μΜ or its vehicle control (DMSO) for 20 min. After a washout with serum-free media, cells were treated with the non-selective phosphodiesterase (PDE) inhibitor isobutyl-methyl-xanthine (IBMX, 1 mM, Applichem) for 5 min. They were then exposed to the sGC activator BAY 60-2770 (10 μM) (a generous gift of Bayer AG), the heme-dependent NO donor sodium nitroprusside (SNP, 100 μΜ, Applichem) or novel synthetic test compounds (10 μΜ–30 μΜ) for 15 min (Figure S9A). sGC activators and test compounds were diluted in DMSO, then further diluted in assay buffer prior to adding to cells.
In the experiments where the additive or antagonistic effect of test compounds was assessed, cells were exposed, after IBMX treatment, to the test compounds either simultaneously with SNP (Figure S9B), or at the indicated time before BAY addition (Figure S9C), until cell extract collection.
The extracts were collected by 0.1 M HCl and analyzed by a commercial ELISA kit (Cayman Chemicals) for total cell cGMP content, following the manufacturer's instructions. cGMP levels were normalized for total protein in the respective well, determined by a Micro BCA Protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific).

4.3.2.3.

## Cloning of constructs and mutagenesis
For infection of Sf-9 cells human α1 and β1 were cloned into the pFASTBAC vector as it was described by Koglin und Behrends [ , ]. For the purification of sGC a C-terminally strep tagged or N-terminally twin-strep-TEV tagged α1 subunit was used. For the C-terminally strep tagged α1 subunit synthetic DNA encoding the C-terminal region of α1 and a strep tag II (PHGEPIKMRIGLHSGSVFAGVVGVKMPRYCLFGNNVTLANKFESCSVPRKINVSPTTYRLLKDCPGFVFTPRSREELPPNFPSEIPGICHFLDAYQQGTNSKPCFQKKDVEDGNANFLGKASGIDWSHPQFEK) was purchased from GeneArt® (Invitrogen™, Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, USA) and cloned Ncol/Kpnl into α1 human in pFASTBAC [ ]. To purify the wildtype α1 containing constructs by Nterminal fusion of a TEV protease-cleavable twin strep tag II an artificial gene encoding a twin strep tag II, a TEV protease-cleavage side and the N-terminus of the α1 subunit (LGSATMSAWSHPQFEKGGGSGGGSGGSAWSHPQFEKSGGGGGENLYFQGMFCTKLKDLKITGECPFSLLAPGG) was cloned BamHI/SexAI into the α1 construct in pFASTBAC. This artificial gene was obtained from GeneArt® (Invitrogen™, Thermo Fisher Scientific; Waltham, USA). The mutation of histidine at position 105 to alanine in the β1 subunit was introduced into the human β1 in pFASTBAC.

4.3.2.4.

## Protein purification
For peptide purifications Sf-9 cells, cultivated at 27 °C and 140rpm, were infected with recombinant baculoviruses encoding for a twin strep TEV tagged α1 and β1 wildtype or an C-terminally strep-tagged α1 subunit and β1H105A. 300–400ml of cell suspension with a concentration of 3 × 106 cells/ml were coinfected with virus. After 72h cells were harvested at 3020xg for 4min and resuspended in 9–12ml lysis buffer (50 Mm triethanolamine/HCl, 10 mM DTT, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, pH 7.4) containing complete™ protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Mannheim, Germany). The resuspended cells were sonified and centrifuged at 15,000×g and 4 °C for 90 min. The supernatant was clarified by filtration through 0.45 μM and 0.2 μM filters before purification via affinity chromatography.

4.3.2.5.

## SDS-PAGE and Coomassie Blue staining
For sample preparation purified protein was diluted in phosphate buffered saline (PBS; 1.7 mM monopotassium phosphate, 5.2 mM disodium phosphate, 150 mM sodium chloride, pH 7.4). SDS sample buffer (1% SDS, 100mM DTT, 50 mM tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane, 30% glycerol) was added in 1:1 ratio to the diluted cytosol reaching a protein concentration of 1 μg in 20 μl. The samples were treated 3 min with a temperature of 99 °C before storage at −20 °C. After resolution on a 10% SDS polyacrylamide electrophoresis gel the samples of the purified protein were stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue as described in Kang et al [ ].

4.3.2.6.

## Densitometric analysis and determination of purified protein concentration
The protein concentration was determined by the Warburg-Christian method with a NanoPhotometer™ (Implen, München, Germany) [ ]. As there were clear differences in protein concentrations detected by Coomassie blue staining between the different α1S/β1H105A Proteins, we adjusted the concentration by densitometric analysis. The densitometric volume was quantified with ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For determination of purified protein concentration from densitometric volume after Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining, a standard curve was generated in triplicate. BSA was loaded on a 10% SDS polyacrylamide electrophoresis gel in amounts ranging from 0.125 mg to 4 mg. A standard curve was created by linear regression with Prism5 (GraphPad, La Jolla, USA), relating the loaded protein amount to the densitometric volume after staining with Coomassie Brilliant Blue. To determine purified sGC concentration, the sum of the densitometric volume of both subunits was determined.

4.3.2.7.

## sGC activity assay
sGC activity assays were performed as described previously in Kraehling et al. [ ]. In short, cGMP forming activity of purified protein was determined by incubation for 10 min at 37 °C in the presence of 50 mM triethanolamine/HCl buffer pH 7.4, containing 3 mM DTT, 1 mM 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine, 1 mM cGMP, 5 mM creatine phosphate, 10units/tube creatine phosphokinase, 0.5 mM [α-32 P]GTP and 3 mM magnesium chloride. The reaction was stopped by adding 500 μL of 125 mM zinc acetate and 500 μL of 120 mM sodium carbonate which results in a co-precipitation of zinc carbonate and 5′-nucleotides. The mixture was centrifuged at 18.000g for 9 min. The supernatant was purified on short alumina columns before eluting the enzyme formed cGMP with natrium acetate (pH 6.5; 250 mM). CGMP was quantified by liquid scintillation counting in a Beckman Coulter Counter LS6500 (Beckmann GmbH, Krefeld, Germany) or a Hidex 600 SL Automatic TDCR Liquid Scintillation Counter (Hidex Oy, Turku, Finland) with a counting time of 2 min [ ].

4.3.2.8.

## Statistical analysis
All results are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent experiments. In sGC activity assay samples were analysed as duplicates and averaged for each assay. Analyses and curve fitting were performed with Prism 5 (GraphPad, La Jolla, USA). For comparison of sGC activity one-way ANOVA was performed followed by Tukey's multi comparison test with Prism 5. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
