## Background
KRAS G12C-targeted therapies have transformed the treatment of KRAS G12C-mutant lung adenocarcinoma. However, acquired resistance to these therapies, whose underlying molecular mechanisms are not fully understood, presents a major obstacle to achieving long-term therapeutic success. The purpose of this study was to elucidate the mechanisms of acquired resistance to KRAS G12C inhibitors and identify potential regulators of resistance in lung adenocarcinoma.

## Methods
Two lung adenocarcinoma cell lines (H23 and H2122) were exposed to escalating doses of two novel KRAS G12C inhibitors, fulzerasib and garsorasib, to generate resistant variants. Transcriptomic profiling was conducted to identify genes consistently upregulated in resistant cells. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout (MXD4-KO) and siRNA-mediated knockdown of MXD4 were performed to assess its role in drug resistance. Mechanistic investigations employed inhibitors of ferroptosis, apoptosis, and necrosis, along with assays measuring lipid peroxidation and malondialdehyde levels. Further analysis included ferroptosis-related gene expression profiling, lipidomic profiling, ChIP-Seq, ChIP-qPCR, and dual-luciferase reporter assays. The findings were validated in patient-derived organoids (PDOs) and nude mouse models.

## Results
Transcriptomic profiling identified 11 genes consistently upregulated in resistant cells, with MXD4 emerging as a key resistance regulator. CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout of MXD4 restored sensitivity to fulzerasib, garsorasib, sotorasib, and adagrasib, an effect fully reversed upon MXD4 re-overexpression. Similarly, siRNA-mediated knockdown of MXD4 in resistant cells restored drug sensitivity. Mechanistic studies revealed that MXD4 specifically suppresses ferroptosis, rather than apoptotic or necrotic pathways, by repressing ACSL4 expression and blocking its catalytic synthesis of phosphatidylethanolamine-polyunsaturated fatty acids (PE-PUFAs). ACSL4 knockout or overexpression abolished MXD4’s ability to promote ferroptosis suppression and resistance to KRAS G12C inhibitors. ChIP-Seq, ChIP-qPCR, and dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed that MXD4 directly binds to and represses the ACSL4 promoter. These findings were validated in PDOs and nude mouse models, where MXD4 knockout restored therapeutic sensitivity, while ACSL4 knockout blocked MXD4’s resistance-promoting effects.

## Conclusions
This study uncovers a novel resistance mechanism in which MXD4 transcriptionally silences ACSL4 to suppress ferroptosis, enabling cancer cells to evade KRAS G12C inhibitors. Targeting the MXD4-ACSL4 axis represents a promising strategy to overcome therapeutic resistance in KRAS G12C-mutant lung cancer, potentially improving long-term treatment outcomes.

## Background
Lung cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with lung adenocarcinoma representing the most common histological subtype, accounting for more than half of newly diagnosed cases each year [ – ]. Among oncogenic drivers, mutations in Kirsten Rat Sarcoma Viral Oncogene Homolog (KRAS) occur in approximately 10–30% of lung adenocarcinomas and play a central role in tumor initiation, progression, and maintenance [ , ]. For decades, KRAS was considered “undruggable” due to its smooth molecular surface, posing major challenges for therapeutic targeting [ – ].
Recent years have witnessed a paradigm shift in KRAS-directed therapy. Covalent KRAS G12C inhibitors, including sotorasib and adagrasib, were the first agents to achieve clinical success and regulatory approval, ending a decade-long therapeutic impasse [ , ]. In parallel, fulzerasib, garsorasib, and glecirasib have been approved in China, further expanding treatment options for patients with KRAS G12C-mutant cancers [ – ]. Moreover, next-generation inhibitors such as zoldonrasib (RMC-9805) and daraxonrasib (RMC-6236) have exhibited remarkable clinical trial outcomes by employing pioneering allosteric inhibition strategies that extend KRAS targeting beyond the G12C mutation [ , ]. Collectively, these advances have substantially improved outcomes for patients with lung adenocarcinoma and other aggressive malignancies.
Despite these breakthroughs, the clinical benefit of KRAS G12C inhibitors remains limited by the rapid emergence of drug resistance, with median progression-free survival typically ranging from 6 to 12 months [ , ]. Both intrinsic and acquired resistance mechanisms have been reported, including pathway reactivation, adaptive transcriptional responses, and altered cell death programs. However, the molecular determinants governing adaptive resistance-particularly those involving transcriptional regulation—remain incompletely understood.
MAX dimerization protein 4 (MXD4) is a transcriptional regulatory protein whose biological functions in cancer, particularly in the context of targeted therapy response and drug resistance, have not been previously defined [ ]. As adaptive transcriptional reprogramming is increasingly recognized as a central mechanism underlying resistance to oncogene-targeted therapies, MXD4 may represent an unappreciated regulatory component of this process. Acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), a key enzyme in lipid metabolism, governs the incorporation of polyunsaturated fatty acids into cellular membranes and serves as a major determinant of ferroptosis sensitivity [ – ]. Dysregulation of ACSL4 has been linked to tumor progression and therapeutic response; however, the upstream transcriptional mechanisms controlling ACSL4 expression during acquired resistance to KRAS G12C–targeted therapies remain largely unknown.
It is worth noting that fulzerasib and garsorasib, although already approved for clinical use in China, have scarcely been fully investigated regarding their resistance mechanisms. In our study, we took a pioneering step by constructing fulzerasib- and garsorasib-resistant lung adenocarcinoma cell lines and, through transcriptomic sequencing, identified marked upregulation of MXD4 in these resistant cells. Notably, while the role of MXD4 in tumor progression remains poorly defined, its involvement in resistance to KRAS-targeted therapies was entirely unexplored. Our mechanistic studies further revealed that MXD4 enhances resistance to KRAS G12C inhibitors (not only fulzerasib and garsorasib, but also sotorasib and adagrasib) by inhibiting ACSL4-mediated ferroptosis. These results offer novel insights and highlight MXD4 as a potential therapeutic target to overcome resistance and improve the efficacy of KRAS-targeted cancer therapies.

## Methods

## Reagents
A range of reagents was procured from various suppliers, including KRAS G12C inhibitors such as fulzerasib (#T73190, TargetMol, Boston, Massachusetts, USA), garsorasib (#E1578, Selleck, Shanghai, China), sotorasib (#T8684, TargetMol), and adagrasib (#T8369, TargetMol); Mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway inhibitors including trametinib (#T2125, TargetMol) and SCH772984 (#T6066, TargetMol); the pan-RAS inhibitor RMC-7977 (#T81263, TargetMol); ferroptosis inducers, including RSL3 (#R302648, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) and Imidazole ketone erastin (IKE) (#T5523, TargetMol); as well as other inhibitors of cell death such as ferrostatin-1 (fer-1) (#T6500, TargetMol), deferoxamine (DFO) (#T1637, TargetMol), Z-VAD-FMK (Z-VAD) (#T7020, TargetMol), and necrosulfonamide (necro) (#T6904, TargetMol).

## Cell culture
Lung adenocarcinoma cell lines H23 (#SCSP-581) and H2122 (#SCSP-598) harboring KRAS G12C mutations, along with the human embryonic kidney cell line 293T (#SCSP-502), were sourced from the cell bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). These cells were cultivated in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (KeyGen Biotech, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China), which was fortified with 10% fetal bovine serum (Every Green, Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China), 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and 100 U/ml penicillin (Beyotime). The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator with an atmosphere consisting of 95% air and 5% CO2.
Based on their respective proliferation rates, the cell lines were sub-cultured every five to seven days. To avoid the contamination of mycoplasma, a polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based detection method, as suggested in previous studies [ , ], was employed to test the cells every two months. Only those cells that yielded negative results for mycoplasma were selected for use in subsequent experimental procedures.

## Construction of fulzerasib- and garsorasib-resistant cells
To investigate resistance mechanisms to KRAS G12C inhibitors, we generated resistant variants of H23 and H2122 cells through gradual dose escalation. Cells were initially exposed to 10 nM fulzerasib or garsorasib, with concentrations increased stepwise (10 nM → 30 nM → 100 nM → 300 nM → 500 nM) at more than a month interval. Upon reaching 500 nM, cultures were maintained for three months with weekly medium replacement. Parental cell lines cultured in drug-free medium served as controls.
To characterize genomic alterations in parental and KRAS G12C inhibitor-resistant cell lines, targeted next-generation sequencing was performed using a 520-gene cancer panel (OncoScreen® Plus, Burning Rock Dx, Guangzhou, China). Genomic DNA was extracted from parental and resistant H23 and H2122 cells according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Library preparation, sequencing, and variant calling were conducted by Burning Rock Dx using validated pipelines. Detected variants were annotated and compared between parental and resistant cell lines to identify newly acquired mutations associated with drug resistance.

## Construction of MXD4 knockout and re-overexpressing cells, ACSL4 knockout cells, as well as MXD4 knockdown cells
This study employed CRISPR/Cas9 technology to achieve MXD4 knockout (KO) and ACSL4-KO in H23 and H2122 cell lines. Specifically, a single-guide RNA (sgRNA) targeting the MXD4 coding sequence (5’-ACAACGAGCTAGAAAAGCAC-3’) was cloned into the GV392 lentiviral vector (GeneChem, Shanghai, China), which co-expresses human codon-optimized SpCas9 (hSpCas9) and a puromycin resistance gene under separate promoters for producing lentivirus. For ACSL4-KO, a sgRNA (5’-ACCACTAGTATACATAACAA-3’) was cloned into a different lentiviral vector, and this vector, which co - expresses hSpCas9 and a geneticin resistance gene under separate promoters, was utilized to generate the corresponding lentivirus (GeneChem). Then cells were infected with lentivirus supplemented with Hitrans A (GeneChem) to enhance transduction efficiency. At 72 h post-infection, cells were cultured in complete medium containing 5 µg/mL puromycin for 48 h or 500 µg/mL geneticin for 7 d to eliminate non-transduced cells. Then, surviving colonies were expanded, and MXD4 knockout efficiency was validated by Western blot analysis.
For MXD4 re-overexpression experiments, a sgRNA-resistant MXD4 overexpression lentivirus with a geneticin resistance gene was generated by introducing silent mutations in the sgRNA target site and the following protospacer adjacent motif (5’-ATAATGAACTGGAGAAGCACAGA-3’) to disrupt Cas9 binding while preserving the wild-type protein sequence. Then the lentivirus was transfected into MXD4-KO cells, and stable re-overexpressing cell lines were established by geneticin selection and verified by Western blot.
To achieve efficient knockdown of MXD4, a cocktail of three siRNAs targeting distinct regions of the MXD4 gene was employed. The siRNA sequences were as follows: 5’-GAGGCCATCACCGTGTCTATA-3’, 5’-GGCCAAGGTGCACATCAAGAA-3’, and 5’-GGCTTCTGCTCTCATCTCTGA-3’. Each siRNA was diluted to a final concentration of 100 nM and combined in equal volumes to form the transfection cocktail. Transient transfection was performed using Lipo8000™ Transfection Reagent (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Negative controls included two non-targeting siRNAs (NC #1 and NC #2) obtained from RiboBio (Guangzhou, China).

## Cytotoxicity assays
Cells were plated into 96-well microplates at a density of 2,000 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h under standard incubation conditions. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to varying concentrations of KRAS G12C inhibitors or other reagent for a 72-hour treatment period if not specified. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (TargetMol) assay, which measures the activity of cellular dehydrogenases as a surrogate marker of metabolic activity. Absorbance values were recorded at 450 nm using a SpectraMax iD5 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA), and viable cell counts were calculated based on the optical density.

## RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)
RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was conducted by OE Biotech (Shanghai, China) as previously reported [ , ]. Briefly, total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China). The purity and concentration of the extracted RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), while its integrity was evaluated with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). For transcriptome library construction, the VAHTS Universal V10 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) was employed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The prepared libraries were then sequenced on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the limma package in R software. Genes were considered differentially upregulated if they met the following criteria: a Fold-change > 1.5, and an adjusted p-value < 0.05.

## Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA isolation was performed using the TRIzol Reagent (TIANGEN) according to the standardized protocol provided by the manufacturer. First-strand complementary DNA was generated through reverse transcription of the isolated RNA templates utilizing the Hifair® II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (YEASEN Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Quantitative analysis of target mRNA expression levels was conducted via quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) using the Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (YEASEN) on an ABI QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher). All reactions were performed in technical triplicates, and relative mRNA expression was normalized to the endogenous reference gene β-actin (ACTB) using the comparative 2⁻∆∆Ct method. Oligonucleotide primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), with specific sequences detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

## Western blot
Cells were lysed on ice for 10 min using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (TargetMol) to prevent protein degradation. Total protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (YEASEN) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For protein separation, samples were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), followed by electroblotting onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck-Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After three 10-minute washes with TBST, membranes were probed with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using the BeyoECL Moon Ultra-Sensitive ECL Chemiluminescence Kit (Beyotime). For quantitative analysis, band intensities were measured by densitometry using ImageJ software (v1.54 g, https://imagej.net/software/imagej/). The signal intensity of each target protein was normalized to the corresponding control β-actin.
The antibodies were obtained from Abnova (Taibei, Taiwan, China), Abways (Shanghai, China), Affinity (Changzhou, Jiangsu, China), Beyotime, Proteintech (Wuhan, Hubei, China), and Servicebio (Wuhan, Hubei, China), with the details including the dilution ratio in Supplementary Table S2.

## Lipid peroxidation and malondialdehyde (MDA) quantification
Cells cultured in 12-well plates were pretreated with various agents, followed by three washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cells were then resuspended in DMEM containing 200 µM BODIPY™ 581/591 C11 dye (Thermo Fisher) for lipid peroxidation detection as previously reported [ – ]. After a 30-min incubation at 37 °C, the cells were rinsed three times with PBS to remove excess dye. Lipid peroxidation levels were quantified using the FITC channel of an Accuri™ C6 Plus flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA).
Ferroptosis progression was also assessed by measuring intracellular malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations. Cell lysates were prepared, and MDA levels were determined using a MDA Assay Kit (Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s guidelines.

## Lipidomic analysis
Lipidomic analysis was performed by Shanghai Lumin Biological Technology (Shanghai, China) as previously reported [ ]. Briefly, 10⁶ cells were washed, scraped, quenched with liquid nitrogen, and resuspended in methanol/water with isotope-labeled internal standards, followed by chloroform addition, ultrasonication, and incubation at 4 °C. Then, chloroform layers were collected, lyophilized, and re-extracted. Final samples were reconstituted in isopropanol/methanol, vortexed, ultrasonicated, and centrifuged. The supernatants were analyzed using an ExionLC™ System (SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA) coupled with a QTRAP® 6500⁺ hybrid triple quadrupole/linear ion trap mass spectrometer (SCIEX).

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay
The chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay was performed using the SimpleChIP® Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Magnetic Beads) (Cell Signaling Technology, CST, Danvers, MA, USA) as previously reported [ – ]. Briefly, Flag-tagged MXD4 overexpression lentivirus with a puromycin resistance gene (Genechem) was used to constructed stable transfected cell lines for ChIP assays. Then, 5 × 10⁶ cells overexpressing Flag-tagged MXD4 were subjected to cross-linking with 1% formaldehyde, followed by quenching with glycine. Cell membranes were disrupted using the provided lysis buffer, and isolated nuclei were treated with micrococcal nuclease (CST) for 20 min to shear chromatin. Nuclear membranes were further disrupted by sonication using a UP250 Ultrasonic Sonicator (Qiqian, Shanghai, China).
For immunoprecipitation, cross-linked chromatin was incubated with anti-Flag antibody (CST, #14793S) overnight at 4 °C. Positive and negative controls were included using anti-Histone H3 (CST, #4620) and IgG (CST, #2729), respectively. Each reaction was supplemented with Protein G Magnetic Beads (CST) and incubated overnight at 4 °C to capture antibody-chromatin complexes. After extensive washing, DNA-protein complexes were isolated via magnetic separation and eluted using kit-provided buffer. DNA purification involved sequential treatment with RNase A (CST) and Proteinase K (CST), followed by quantitative PCR analysis using Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (YEASEN) on an ABI QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher).

## Dual-luciferase reporter assay
Luciferase reporter assays were performed to analyze the regulatory activity of ACSL4 promoter regions as previously reported [ , ]. Genomic fragments spanning − 2000 to + 500 bp relative to the transcription start site were used, along with corresponding mutant sequences containing alterations at MXD4 binding sites which were predicted by JASPAR (https://jaspar.elixir.no/)(34) within MXD4 binding peaks. Both wild-type and mutant sequences were cloned into firefly luciferase reporter vectors by GeneChem.
For transfection, 293T cells were co-transfected with either wild-type or mutant firefly luciferase plasmids (GeneChem) and Renilla luciferase control plasmids (Genomeditech, Shanghai, China) using Lipo8000™ Transfection Reagent (Beyotime). Dual-luciferase activities were measured 48 h post-transfection with the Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Beyotime) and SpectraMax iD5 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices), following the manufacturers’ protocol.

## Patient-Derived Organoids (PDO)
Surgical specimens of fresh lung adenocarcinoma tissues were collected from patients admitted to Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University. These patients had pathologically confirmed KRAS G12C mutations and had not received preoperative chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy. Tissue fragments were processed by mechanical dissociation, followed by three washes in ice-cold PBS and enzymatic digestion using Human Lung Cancer Organoid Culture Medium Kits (Absin, Shanghai, China). The resulting cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min, and the pellet was resuspended in Matrigel® (YEASEN) at a 1:25 ratio. The Matrigel-cell mixture was dispensed into 24-well plates and allowed to polymerize at 37 °C for 10 min before supplementation with 500 µL PDO-specific culture medium per well.
For drug sensitivity testing, established organoids were seeded into 96-well plates and allowed to recover for 24 h prior to genetic manipulation. Lentiviral particles encoding MXD4 knockout constructs or corresponding control vectors were then added directly to the organoid cultures at a multiplicity of infection of 20. To minimize selection bias and preserve the intrinsic heterogeneity of patient-derived organoids, no puromycin selection or single-clone isolation was performed following lentiviral transduction. Forty-eight hours after lentiviral transduction, organoids were exposed to 100 nM fulzerasib for 72 h. Cell viability was quantified using the CellTiter-Lumi™ 3D Luminescent Cell Viability Assay Kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

## Animal experiments
Four-week-old male BALB/c nude mice were procured from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center (Shanghai, China) and maintained under sterile, pathogen-free housing conditions throughout the study.
For tumor inoculation, a suspension of 1 × 10⁶ cells in 100 µL of ice-cold Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (KeyGen) was subcutaneous injected into the right flank of each mouse. Tumor growth was monitored every two days using digital calipers, with volumes calculated using the modified ellipsoid formula: V = (length × width²)/2.
Once tumor volumes reached approximately 150 mm³, mice were randomly assigned to two treatment groups: a vehicle control group receiving normal saline, and a fulzerasib treatment group receiving 30 mg/kg via oral gavage once daily for 14 consecutive days. Following the treatment period, all mice were humanely euthanized via controlled CO₂ inhalation, and the excised tumors were immediately weighed.

## The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database analyses
RNA-seq expression data for lung adenocarcinoma were obtained from The TCGA database (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga). Patients were stratified into MXD4-high and MXD4-low groups based on the median MXD4 expression level. Differentially expressed genes between the two groups were identified using standard statistical criteria. Hallmark pathway enrichment analysis and Gene Ontology (GO) biological process enrichment analysis were performed using gene sets curated in the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, Broad Institute, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp) as previously reported [ ].
Publicly available gene expression datasets derived from KRAS-targeted therapy-treated cells and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models were retrieved from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds/). Specifically, transcriptomic datasets of KRAS G12C inhibitor–treated lung cancer cell lines (GSE229070, GSE288707, and GSE282795) [ – ] were analyzed to evaluate MXD4 expression changes following pharmacological inhibition of KRAS signaling in Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1) wild-type cells. In addition, dataset GSE204753 [ ], which includes treatment-naïve (n = 3) and sotorasib-resistant lung cancer PDX models (n = 6), and dataset GSE305318 [ ], which includes control (n = 3), adagrasib-resistant (n = 3), and RMC-4998 (n = 3) -resistant lung cancer PDX models, were analyzed. For each dataset, MXD4 expression levels were extracted and compared between groups.

## Statistical analysis
All experiments were conducted independently and replicated a minimum of three times to guarantee the robustness and reliability of the findings. For comparing continuous variables across two groups, an unpaired Student’s t-test was employed; for comparisons among multiple groups, either one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA was utilized, depending on the experimental design. To address the issue of multiple comparisons, the Bonferroni correction method was applied. Unless stated otherwise, the results are reported as mean values, with error bars indicating the standard deviation. All statistical analyses were carried out using R software (Version 4.4.0; accessible at https://www.r-project.org/). All p-values were two-tailed, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

## Results

## MXD4 was significantly upregulated in KRAS G12C-targeted therapy-resistant, responsive, and drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells of lung adenocarcinoma
To systematically explore the molecular mechanisms underlying resistance to KRAS G12C-targeted therapy in lung adenocarcinoma, we first established drug-resistant cell lines from two well-characterized KRAS G12C-mutant lung adenocarcinoma cell lines, H23 and H2122. This was achieved through a gradual, stepwise escalation of two KRAS G12C inhibitors, fulzerasib and garsorasib, over a six-month period, followed by long-term maintenance at a stable concentration of 500 nM for an additional three months (Fig.  A). The acquisition of resistance was rigorously validated by measuring the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50), which revealed a statistically significant ten-fold or greater increase in drug resistance in the resistant cells compared to their parental counterparts (p < 0.001) (Fig.  B-C).

 

To further determine whether resistance was associated with newly acquired genomic alterations, we performed targeted next-generation sequencing using a 520-gene cancer panel in both parental and resistant cell lines. In H23 cells, canonical alterations were detected in KRAS (p.G12C), serine/threonine kinase 11 (STK11; p.W332Ter), KEAP1 (p.Q193H), and tumor protein p53 (TP53; p.M246I), along with additional alterations in ataxia telangiectasia mutated (ATM), fibroblast growth factor receptor 2 (FGFR2), catenin delta 1 (CTNND1), and bone morphogenetic protein 10 (BMP10). In H2122 cells, mutations were identified in KRAS (p.G12C), STK11 (p.P281RfsTer6), and KEAP1 (p.A170_R204del).
Importantly, no newly acquired mutations in the 520-gene panel were observed in the resistant derivatives compared with their corresponding parental lines, including genes involved in the Mitogen-activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway. These results indicate that resistance in our models is unlikely to be driven by additional genomic alterations, but instead supports an adaptive, non-genetic mechanism of resistance, prompting further investigation into transcriptomic and functional changes.
To uncover the transcriptional changes driving this acquired resistance, we performed transcriptome sequencing on both resistant and sensitive cell populations (Fig.  D). Through cross-analysis of the sequencing data, we identified a set of 11 genes that were consistently and significantly upregulated across all resistant clones, including complement C1q Tumor Necrosis Factor-Related Protein 6 (C1QTNF6), Caspase 9 (CASP9), Cluster of Differentiation 24 (CD24), Dual Specificity Phosphatase 8 (DUSP8), Inhibitor of Dna Binding 2 (ID2), Interferon Regulatory Factor 1 (IRF1), MXD4, NUMB Like Endocytic Adaptor Protein (NUMBL), Programmed Cell Death 4 (PDCD4), RAS Homolog Family Member V (RHOV), SRY-Box Transcription Factor 12 (SOX12) (Fig.  D). These genes are involved in diverse biological processes, including cell differentiation, apoptosis, immune regulation, and signal transduction, suggesting that resistance to KRAS G12C inhibitors may arise from coordinated transcriptional reprogramming rather than a single molecular alteration. Among these candidates, MXD4 emerged as a compelling factor due to its limited prior characterization in KRAS G12C-mutant lung adenocarcinoma and the absence of any reported role in resistance to KRAS-targeted therapies, leading us to prioritize MXD4 for further mechanistic investigation.
To validate the transcriptional upregulation of MXD4 observed in the RNA-seq data (Fig.  E-F), we conducted targeted qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses. These experiments unequivocally confirmed that MXD4 expression was significantly elevated at both the mRNA and protein levels in fulzerasib- or garsorasib-resistant H23 and H2122 cells, when compared to their parental controls (Fig.  G-H), supporting a role for MXD4 in acquired resistance.
To gain deeper insights into the temporal dynamics of MXD4 expression during therapy, we treated H23 and H2122 cells with IC50 concentrations of fulzerasib or garsorasib for 72 h. Interestingly, we observed a significant elevation in MXD4 levels even in cells that were still responsive to therapy (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). This suggests that MXD4 upregulation may also be an early event in the adaptive response to KRAS G12C inhibition. To further explore this, we extended the drug exposure period to 10 days using 100-fold IC50 concentrations—a condition known to select for drug-tolerant persister (DTP) cells, a transient subpopulation that survives sustained drug pressure and serves as a reservoir for resistance evolution. Consistent with our initial observations, DTP cells exhibited significantly higher MXD4 expression compared to untreated controls (Fig.  I-J), implicating MXD4 in both persistence and resistance evolution.
Then to exclude the possibility that MXD4 upregulation was a compound-specific effect, we treated cells with IC50 concentrations of two additional KRAS G12C inhibitors, sotorasib and adagrasib. Notably, both agents also induced significant elevation of MXD4 expression (Supplementary Fig. 1C-D), indicating that MXD4 upregulation is a conserved response across distinct KRAS G12C inhibitors.
Given that KEAP1 co-mutations/NRF2 activation have been identified as major drivers of primary resistance to KRAS G12C-targeted therapies [ ], and considering that both H23 and H2122 cell lines harbor KEAP1 mutations, we analyzed publicly available transcriptomic datasets from lung cancer cell lines exposed to KRAS G12C inhibitors to further investigate whether MXD4 induction occurs in models without KEAP1 alterations. In the H358 cells, a KRAS G12C mutant lung cancer cell line reported to harbor wild-type KEAP1, MXD4 expression was increased under sotorasib-resistant datasets (GSE229070 and GSE288707) (Supplementary Fig. 1E). Similarly, analysis of the adagrasib-treated LU99 dataset (GSE282795), which also lacks reported KEAP1 mutations, revealed elevated MXD4 expression upon adagrasib treatment (Supplementary Fig. 1F). These findings suggest that MXD4 upregulation following KRAS pathway inhibition is not restricted to KEAP1-mutant backgrounds and can also occur in KEAP1 wild-type lung cancer models.
To determine whether MXD4 induction merely reflects KRAS wild-type reactivation, we examined MXD4 expression under conditions in which wild-type KRAS signaling is pharmacologically suppressed. Treatment with the active-state pan-RAS inhibitor RMC-7977, which inhibits both mutant and wild-type RAS signaling, resulted in a marked MXD4 upregulation in both H23 and H2122 cells (Fig.  K and Supplementary Fig. 1G).
Given that MAPK pathway reactivation is a well-established adaptive resistance mechanism, we next asked whether MXD4 induction reflects MAPK signaling status or occurs downstream of pathway suppression. Pharmacological inhibition of MAPK signaling using the Mitogen-activated Protein Kinase Kinase (MEK) inhibitor trametinib or the ERK inhibitor SCH772984 led to significant MXD4 upregulation at the transcript level (Supplementary Fig. 1H). Western blot analysis further confirmed increased MXD4 protein expression despite effective suppression of ERK1/2 phosphorylation and compensatory feedback elevation of MEK1/2 phosphorylation, without changes in total protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 1I). These findings indicate that MXD4 induction occurs as an adaptive response downstream of MAPK inhibition rather than as a consequence of pathway reactivation.
Finally, to assess the clinical relevance of MXD4 induction, we reanalyzed publicly available transcriptomic datasets from patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models of acquired resistance. MXD4 expression was significantly elevated in sotorasib-resistant PDXs in GSE204753 and in adagrasib- and RMC-4998-resistant PDXs in GSE305318 (Fig. L) [ , ]. Together, these patient-derived datasets support MXD4 upregulation as a recurrent feature of acquired resistance to KRAS-targeted therapies in vivo.
Collectively, these data demonstrate MXD4 as a convergent, non-genetic adaptive resistance node downstream of RAS–MAPK pathway inhibition, providing a strong rationale for subsequent functional and mechanistic interrogation of its role in KRAS G12C-targeted therapy resistance.

## MXD4 promoted resistance to KRAS G12C-targeted therapies
To investigate the role of MXD4 in KRAS G12C-targeted therapy resistance, we generated MXD4 knockout (KO) clones in H23 and H2122 lung adenocarcinoma cells using CRISPR/Cas9 technology. Western blot analysis confirmed complete ablation of MXD4 protein expression (Fig.  A). To rule out potential off-target effects, we also performed rescue experiments by re-overexpressing MXD4 cDNA harboring silent mutations at the sgRNA target sites (ROE) in MXD4-KO cells (Fig.  A and Supplementary Fig. 2A).

 

The following drug sensitivity assays revealed that MXD4 depletion significantly enhanced cellular responsiveness to four clinically advanced KRAS G12C inhibitors—fulzerasib, garsorasib, sotorasib, and adagrasib—resulting in significant reductions in IC50 values compared to wild-type controls (Fig.  B-C and Supplementary Fig. 2B-C). Notably, MXD4 knockout also significantly sensitized cells to the active-state pan-RAS inhibitor RMC-7977 (Supplementary Fig. 2B-C), indicating that the resistance-promoting role of MXD4 is not restricted to a single KRAS G12C inhibitor or OFF-state targeting strategy. Meanwhile, genetic restoration of MXD4 fully reversed the hypersensitivity phenotype, thereby confirming the specificity of MXD4’s resistance-promoting function (Fig.  B-C and Supplementary Fig. 2B-C).
Furthermore, we employed a cocktail of three siRNAs to knock down MXD4 in drug-resistant cells, with the knockdown efficacy subsequently validated by both qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses (Fig.  D and Supplementary Fig. 2D). Drug sensitivity assays demonstrated that MXD4 knockdown significantly reduced the IC50 values of resistant cells relative to parental lines (Fig.  E-F).
To directly assess whether MXD4 contributes to resistance acquired under KRAS G12C inhibitor selection pressure, we next evaluated cross-resistance profiles in fulzerasib- and garsorasib-resistant cells. All four resistant derivatives exhibited significantly reduced sensitivity to both sotorasib and adagrasib compared with their parental counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 2E), demonstrating robust cross-resistance among structurally and mechanistically related KRAS G12C inhibitors. Strikingly, siRNA-mediated depletion of MXD4 consistently and significantly restored sensitivity to both sotorasib and adagrasib across fulzerasib- and garsorasib-resistant cells (Supplementary Fig. 2F), indicating that MXD4 is required for maintenance of the resistant phenotype.
When subjected to prolonged KRAS G12C inhibitor treatment over 60 days, we observed that knockout of MXD4 cells exhibited a consistently stronger and sustained reduction in tumor cell proliferation throughout the 60-day period (Fig.  G-H). Notably, the reduction was completely rescued upon MXD4 re-overexpression, establishing a direct causal relationship between MXD4 and therapeutic resistance (Fig.  G-H).

## MXD4 contributed to resistance to KRAS G12C-targeted therapy by inhibiting ferroptosis
To further delve into the mechanism through which MXD4 induces resistance to KRAS G12C-targeted therapy, we investigated its impact on different types of cell death during KRAS G12C-targeted treatment. MXD4-KO cells, MXD4-ROE cells, and the corresponding control cells were treated with KRAS G12C inhibitors (fulzerasib and garsorasib) in combination with various cell death inhibitors for 72 h. These inhibitors included ferroptosis inhibitors ferrostatin-1 (fer-1) and deferoxamine (DFO), an apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK (Z-VAD), and a necrosis inhibitor necrosulfonamide (necro).
The relevant results revealed that only the ferroptosis inhibitors significantly reduced the differences in cell viability between the MXD4-KO or MXD4-ROE cells and the control cells under the treatment of KRAS G12C inhibitors (Fig.  A-B and Supplementary Fig. 3A-B). In contrast, the apoptosis or necrosis inhibitors had no significant impact on the disparities of cell viability among the MXD4-knockout or MXD4-reoverexpressing groups (Fig.  A-B and Supplementary Fig. 3A-B). This finding suggested that MXD4 conferred resistance to KRAS G12C-targeted therapy via the inhibition of ferroptosis.

 

To further assess whether MXD4 is linked to ferroptosis-related biological programs in human tumors, we analyzed transcriptomic data from the TCGA lung adenocarcinoma cohort. Tumors were stratified into MXD4-high and MXD4-low groups based on the median MXD4 expression level, followed by differential gene expression analysis. Hallmark pathway enrichment and GO biological process enrichment analyses were then performed using curated gene sets from the MSigDB database. These analyses revealed significant enrichment of pathways related to reactive oxygen species signaling, oxidative stress response, and metal ion response in MXD4-high tumors (Supplementary Fig. 3C-D), all of which are mechanistically linked to ferroptosis regulation.
Therefore, we further examined MXD4’s role in ferroptosis. When cells were exposed to ferroptosis inducers RSL3 and IKE, MXD4 knockout led to a substantial decline in cell viability (Fig.  C). Conversely, re-overexpression of MXD4 significantly enhanced cell viability, demonstrating its protective effect against ferroptosis-induced cell death (Fig.  C).
After treatment with these inducers, the MXD4-knockout group exhibited notably elevated levels of intracellular lipid peroxidation and malondialdehyde (MDA), both of which are classic indicators of ferroptosis, under the treatment of fulzerasib or garsorasib (Fig.  D-F). This suggests that the absence of MXD4 renders cells more susceptible to ferroptosis in KRAS G12C-targeted therapy. Notably, re-overexpressing MXD4 reversed the increases in lipid peroxidation and MDA levels (Fig.  D-F), further confirming its inhibitory role in ferroptosis. Overall, these results strongly support the conclusion that MXD4 functions as an inhibitor of ferroptosis.

## MXD4 inhibited ferroptosis and resistance to KRAS G12C-targeted therapy by suppressing ACSL4 expression
Given that MXD4 functions as a transcriptional regulator, we hypothesized that it may promote resistance to KRAS G12C-targeted therapies by suppressing ferroptosis through regulation of key ferroptosis-related genes. To test this, we first examined the mRNA and protein levels of critical ferroptosis regulators—ACSL4, glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1, also known as apoptosis inducing factor mitochondria associated 2, AIFM2), and dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH)—in MXD4 knockout (MXD4-KO) and MXD4 overexpression (MXD4-ROE) cells using qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis. Among these candidates, only ACSL4 expression was significantly increased upon MXD4 depletion and markedly suppressed upon MXD4 re-expression, whereas the expression of GPX4, SLC7A11, FSP1, and DHODH remained largely unchanged (Fig.  A and Supplementary Fig. 4A-B), suggesting that ACSL4 represents a specific downstream target of MXD4.

 

Because ACSL4 catalyzes the conversion of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) to PUFA-Coenzyme A (CoA), a critical step in generating phosphatidylethanolamine (PE)-PUFA species that serve as primary substrates for ferroptosis-associated lipid peroxidation, we next performed comprehensive lipidomic profiling to investigate how MXD4 influences PUFA-containing lipid metabolism. MXD4-KO cells displayed a pronounced accumulation of multiple PE-PUFA species, including PE(14:0–22:4), PE(16:0–20:4), PE-O(16:0–20:4), and PE-P(16:0–22:4), whereas these lipid species were significantly reduced in MXD4-ROE cells (Fig.  B and Supplementary Fig. 4C). These data indicate that MXD4 suppresses PUFA-enriched membrane lipid synthesis via inhibition of ACSL4, thereby limiting ferroptotic vulnerability.
To functionally validate the role of ACSL4 in MXD4-mediated ferroptosis regulation, we generated ACSL4 knockout cells in control and MXD4-KO backgrounds (Fig.  C). Loss of ACSL4 abolished the enhanced lipid peroxidation and malondialdehyde (MDA) accumulation observed in MXD4-KO cells following treatment with KRAS G12C inhibitors (Fig.  D-E and Supplementary Fig. 4D). Consistently, ACSL4 knockout eliminated the differential sensitivity between control and MXD4-KO cells to ferroptosis inducers (RSL3 and IKE) as well as to multiple KRAS G12C inhibitors, including fulzerasib, garsorasib, sotorasib, and adagrasib (Fig.  F-H and Supplementary Fig. 4E). These results establish ACSL4 as an essential mediator of MXD4-regulated ferroptosis and drug response in drug-sensitive cells.
To determine whether this regulatory axis is maintained in drug-resistant contexts, we next extended our analysis to fulzerasib- and garsorasib-resistant cell lines. Transcriptomic profiling revealed a consistent downregulation of ACSL4 in resistant cells compared with their parental counterparts, which was further validated by qRT-PCR and western blot analyses (Supplementary Fig. 5A-C). Importantly, siRNA-mediated knockdown of MXD4 in resistant cells significantly restored ACSL4 expression, while other ferroptosis-related regulators remained largely unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 5D-F).
To further assess the clinical relevance of this observation, we analyzed TCGA lung adenocarcinoma samples stratified by MXD4 expression. Patients were divided into MXD4-high and MXD4-low groups based on the median MXD4 level, and ACSL4 expression was found to be significantly lower in the MXD4-high group (Supplementary Fig. 5G-H).
To directly assess the functional relevance of ACSL4 suppression in resistant cells, in which MXD4 expression is elevated while ACSL4 expression is reduced, we knocked down MXD4 and/or overexpressed ACSL4 in these resistant cells. qRT–PCR and western blot analyses confirmed efficient MXD4 knockdown and ACSL4 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 6A–B). Functionally, both MXD4 knockdown and ACSL4 overexpression significantly restored the sensitivity of resistant cells to KRAS G12C inhibitors. Importantly, ACSL4 overexpression largely counteracted the resensitizing effect of MXD4 knockdown upon treatment with fulzerasib, garsorasib, sotorasib, and adagrasib (Supplementary Fig. 6C–D). These results demonstrate that ACSL4 acts as a key downstream effector of MXD4 in regulating therapeutic responses in KRAS G12C inhibitor–resistant cells.
Collectively, these results establish the MXD4–ACSL4 axis as a central regulator of ferroptosis suppression and resistance to KRAS G12C-targeted therapies, operating consistently in both drug-sensitive and acquired drug-resistant lung adenocarcinoma cells.

## MXD4 suppressed ACSL4 transcription by binding to its promoter region
To validate the transcriptional regulation of ACSL4 by MXD4, we first analyzed publicly available ChIP-Seq datasets for MXD4 from the ENCODE project (https://www.encodeproject.org/) [ ]. Consistent with our hypothesis, these data revealed prominent MXD4-binding peaks within the ACSL4 promoter region (-2000 ~ 500 bp), particularly around the transcription start site (Fig. A). Complementing these findings, interrogation of the GTRD database (http://gtrd.biouml.org/) [ ], a comprehensive repository integrating transcription factor binding profiles across diverse cell types, demonstrated significant enrichment of MXD4 binding motifs in the ACSL4 promoter (Supplementary Table 3).

 

To further substantiate these observations experimentally, we designed three primer pairs targeting the MXD4-binding peaks identified in the ChIP-Seq data (Fig.  A). Our ChIP-qPCR assays demonstrated significant enrichment of MXD4 binding at regions flanking primer pair 1 and primer pair 3 in both H23 and H2122 cell lines overexpressing Flag-tagged MXD4 (Fig.  B-C). These combined results not only establish ACSL4 as a direct transcriptional target of MXD4 but also precisely map the approximate binding regions within the ACSL4 promoter.
To investigate the functionality of MXD4 binding to the ACSL4 promoter, we generated luciferase reporter constructs by cloning the human ACSL4 promoter region into the PGL4-luciferase reporter vector for luciferase reporter activity assays. Meanwhile, we predicted and mutated the high-probability MXD4-binding sites around primer pair 1 and primer pair 3 based on consensus motif of MXD4 from the JASPAR database [ ] to disrupt these motifs (Supplementary Information). The results showed that MXD4-KO 293T cells significantly promoted the luciferase activity of the reporter containing the wild-type (WT) ACSL4 promoter sequences, while MXD4-ROE reversed this promoter activity to levels significantly lower than those in control cells (Fig. D). In contrast, the reporter with mutant (MU) ACSL4 promoter constructs did not exhibit an alteration in luciferase activity in response to MXD4-KO or MXD4-ROE overexpression (Fig. D). These findings elucidate the interaction mechanism between MXD4 and ACSL4, indicating that MXD4 strongly binds to the ACSL4 promoter and inhibits its transcription.

## MXD4 suppressed the efficacy of KRAS G12C-targeted therapy in organoids and nude mouse tumorigenesis models
Given that there are well-recognized disparities between tumor cell lines and clinical tumors, along with the distinct growth conditions in vitro and in vivo, we decided to further validate the impact of MXD4 on KRAS G12C-targeted therapy in patient-derived tumor organoid models and nude mouse tumorigenesis experiments (Fig.  A).

 

To assess the functional relevance of MXD4 in patient-derived tumor models, we established lung adenocarcinoma organoids from tumor specimens of five patients harboring KRAS G12C mutations. Organoids were expanded and seeded into parallel wells prior to any genetic manipulation to ensure equivalent starting populations. MXD4 knockout or control lentiviral transduction was then performed in parallel across these organoid cultures, followed by treatment with the KRAS G12C inhibitor fulzerasib. Importantly, no puromycin selection or clonal isolation was applied after lentiviral transduction, thereby minimizing artificial selection pressure and preserving the intrinsic heterogeneity of patient-derived organoids.
Under these conditions, MXD4 knockout significantly enhanced the sensitivity of patient-derived organoids to fulzerasib treatment (Fig.  B-C). Consistent with our mechanistic model, qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses confirmed effective reduction of MXD4 protein levels in MXD4-knockout organoids, accompanied by increased ACSL4 expression (Supplementary Fig. 7A-B). The incomplete depletion of MXD4 reflects the absence of antibiotic selection or clonal isolation, resulting in a heterogeneous organoid population with mixed editing efficiency and residual transcripts undergoing nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (Supplementary Fig. 7A-B). These findings indicate that MXD4 functionally contributes to KRAS G12C inhibitor response in patient-derived organoid systems, supporting its role in modulating therapeutic sensitivity at the patient-tumor level.
In parallel, we performed nude mouse xenograft experiments using H23 control or MXD4-KO cells with or without ACSL4 knockout, followed by treatment with either vehicle or fulzerasib. Under vehicle-treated conditions, ACSL4 knockout modestly reduced tumor growth irrespective of MXD4 status (Fig.  D-F), consistent with a role of ACSL4 in supporting basal tumor proliferation in vivo. Importantly, upon fulzerasib treatment, MXD4 knockout markedly enhanced the antitumor efficacy of KRAS G12C inhibition. This sensitizing effect was completely abolished when ACSL4 was concomitantly knocked out in tumor cells (Fig.  D-F). These results strongly indicates that MXD4 regulates the in vivo efficacy of KRAS G12C-targeted therapy through ACSL4, providing new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the response to KRAS G12C-targeted drugs (Fig.  G).

## Discussion
Our study reveals that MXD4 is significantly upregulated in KRAS G12C-targeted therapy-resistant, responsive, and DTP cells of lung adenocarcinoma, and this upregulation is a common response across different KRAS G12C inhibitors. MXD4 promotes resistance to such therapies by inhibiting ferroptosis, specifically through suppressing ACSL4 expression via binding to its promoter region. Furthermore, MXD4 suppresses the efficacy of KRAS G12C-targeted therapy in organoid and nude mouse models, highlighting its potential as a broad-spectrum biomarker and therapeutic target.
KRAS mutations, particularly the G12C subtype, present in 10–30% of NSCLC, were long deemed “undruggable” due to structural challenges, until breakthroughs like sotorasib achieved a 32.2% overall response rate (ORR) in pretreated NSCLC patients in 2020 [ ]. Future innovations, such as KRAS-targeting proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) and T cell receptor-engineered T cell (TCR-T) therapies against mutant neoantigens, aim to deliver deeper, durable remissions in this aggressive disease [ – ]. Despite these advances, resistance mechanisms—including EGFR pathway reactivation, compensatory downstream signaling, and adaptive reactivation of the KRAS-MAPK axis—continue to limit therapeutic efficacy, necessitating combination strategies [ , , – ]. In response to this challenge, active-state pan-KRAS inhibitors, such as RMC-7977 and RMC-6236, as well as other emerging RAS(ON)-state inhibitors, have been developed to more comprehensively suppress both mutant and wild-type KRAS signaling [ – ]. Our study reveals MXD4 is upregulated in resistant cells and attenuates the efficacy of KRAS G12C-targeted therapy, thereby highlighting MXD4’s potential as both a broad-spectrum biomarker for resistance and a promising target for developing combination strategies.
The function of MXD4 in tumors is still not thoroughly investigated. Several studies indicate that MXD4 exerts tumor-suppressing and immunoregulatory roles by inhibiting MYC activity. For example, Hu et al. [ ] found that in AML, UHRF1 boosts the self-renewal of LICs by repressing MXD4; Yang et al. [ ] observed that in glioblastoma, Sin3B deficiency triggers MXD4 degradation, activating the MYC pathway and fostering tumor growth; Heckler et al. [ ] noted that in NSCLC, CDK4/6 inhibitors elevate MXD4 levels, suppress MYC activity, and enhance the memory formation and long-term immune protection of CD8 + T cells. However, research on MXD4’s role in KRAS-targeted therapy or other targeted therapies is currently lacking, while our study, for the first time, reveals that MXD4 promotes resistance to KRAS G12C-targeted therapy in lung adenocarcinoma. Furthermore, the absence of significant MYC expression changes in our cell sequencing results from KRAS G12C-targeted therapy-resistant cells implies that MXD4 may operate through alternative pathways beyond MYC. Additionally, gene fusions involving MXD4 and NUTM1 have been detected in various rare tumors, and MXD4 can also fuse with genes such as MGA and MXI1, forming complexes with unique transcriptional regulatory functions that drive tumorigenesis [ – ].
Our study demonstrates that MXD4 drives resistance to KRAS G12C-targeted therapy in lung adenocarcinoma by downregulating ACSL4 expression and suppressing ACSL4-mediated ferroptosis. As a newly recognized form of regulated cell death, ferroptosis holds significant promise for cancer treatment [ – ]. Previous research has shown that KRAS-mutant tumor cells are particularly vulnerable to ferroptosis, and ACSL4—a key regulator of this process—amplifies lipid peroxidation by promoting the phospholipidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids, thereby serving as a crucial target for enhancing the efficacy of KRAS-targeted therapies, despite also supporting tumor proliferation under basal conditions [ – ]. Therefore, modulating the MXD4-ACSL4-ferroptosis signaling pathway could offer a promising approach to overcome resistance and improve clinical outcomes in KRAS-mutant lung adenocarcinoma.
Our study has several limitations. First, our investigation focused specifically on resistance mechanisms to KRAS G12C-targeted therapy, and the role of MXD4 in mediating responses to therapies targeting other KRAS mutations remains to be determined. Second, because KRAS G12C inhibitors are primarily used in advanced-stage patients who are often not eligible for surgical resection, we were unable to evaluate the prognostic significance of MXD4 using post-treatment or drug-resistant patient tumor specimens. To partially address this limitation, we employed patient-derived organoids and complemented our findings with analyses of publicly available KRAS inhibitor-resistant PDX datasets, both of which supported MXD4 upregulation in resistant tumors; however, these approaches cannot fully substitute for prospective patient-derived resistance samples. Third, although the immune microenvironment is known to influence responses to KRAS G12C-targeted therapy, experimental constraints prevented a systematic investigation of the role of MXD4 in modulating antitumor immunity during treatment. Future studies incorporating longitudinal patient specimens and immune-competent models will be required to address these limitations.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, our study identifies MXD4 as a key mediator of resistance to KRAS G12C-targeted therapy by suppressing ACSL4-mediated ferroptosis in lung adenocarcinoma. Given its consistent upregulation and impact on treatment efficacy in preclinical models, MXD4 holds promise as a broad-spectrum biomarker and therapeutic target to overcome resistance.
