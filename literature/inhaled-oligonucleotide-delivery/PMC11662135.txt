## Abstract
Globally, people widely recognize cancer as one of the most lethal diseases due to its high mortality rates and lack of effective treatment options. Ongoing research into cancer therapies remains a critical area of inquiry, holding significant social relevance. Currently used treatment, such as chemotherapy, radiation, or surgery, often suffers from other problems like damaging side effects, inaccuracy, and the lack of ability to clear tumors. Conventional cancer therapies are usually imprecise and ineffective and usually develop resistance to treatments and cancer recurs. Cancer patients need fresh and innovative treatment that can reduce side effects while maximizing effectiveness. In recent decades several breakthroughs in these, and other areas of medical research, have paved the way for new avenues of fighting cancer including more focused and more effective alternatives. This study reviews exciting possibilities for mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), nanomaterials, and microbial agents in the modern realm of cancer treatment. Nanoparticles (NPs) have demonstrated surprisingly high potential. They improve drug delivery systems (DDS) significantly, enhance imaging techniques remarkably, and target cancer cells selectively while protecting healthy tissues. MSCs play a double role in tissue repair and are a vehicle for novel cancer treatments such as gene treatments or NPs loaded with therapeutic agents. Additionally, therapies utilizing microbial agents, particularly those involving bacteria, offer an inventive approach to cancer treatment. This review investigates the potential of nanomaterials, MSCs, and microbial agents in addressing the shortcomings of conventional cancer therapies. We will also discuss the challenges and limitations of using these therapeutic approaches.

## Introduction
Cancer is distinguished by irregularities in the regulatory mechanisms of the cell cycle, resulting in the survival and rapid growth of cancer cells. Cancer typically causes changes in signaling pathways. Preventing natural apoptosis encourages cancer growth and increases its resistance to chemotherapy and radiation [ – ]. The prevalence of cancer is on the rise as a result of shifts in the environment and the adoption of modernized lifestyles. Breast cancer (BC) and lung cancer are the most frequently identified malignancies globally and the primary contributors to cancer-related mortality in both males and females. Concurrently, factors that increase the likelihood of developing cancer can stem from external sources such as chemicals, tobacco, radiation, and infectious agents, as well as internal factors, including genetic predispositions and immune system disorders [ , ]. Researchers are expressing concern regarding the rising incidence of cancer. A recent report published by the World Health Organization (WHO) indicates that cancer ranks as the primary or secondary leading cause of mortality for individuals under the age of 70 in 112 out of 183 countries [ ]. Approximately 9.6 million cancer deaths occurred in 2018, impacting 18.1 million people globally. By 2040, it is anticipated that more than two-thirds of cancer cases worldwide will be identified [ ]. The global outbreak of COVID-19 in 2019 has resulted in impediments to the timely diagnosis and treatment of cancer, stemming from the closure of healthcare facilities, interruptions in employment and health insurance, and concerns about potential exposure to the virus. A delay in the identification and management of a medical condition can result in the progression of the disease and higher rates of mortality. The long-term impacts of the pandemic, including its secondary effects, will require several years to be accurately assessed at the population level, as there is a delay of 2–3 years in obtaining population-based data on cancer incidence and mortality. It has been previously determined that the pandemic has a significantly greater direct and indirect effect on communities of color [ , ].
Chemotherapy, surgery, radiation therapy, and immunotherapy are the current treatments for treating cancer, as shown in (The graphical abstract). Nevertheless, each therapeutic approach has limitations that necessitate further intervention or lack precision in targeting, resulting in additional adverse effects for the individual. For instance, radiation and chemotherapy treatments lack specificity for targeting cancer cells, resulting in the elimination of both healthy and diseased cells and causing adverse side effects. Moreover, these treatments are ineffective in completely eradicating tumors and their metastases [ , ]. Substantial advancements in immunotherapy, biology, and contemporary pharmaceutical research and production have rendered the pursuit of a cure for cancer a realistic and attainable objective [ ]. Currently, scholars have examined novel approaches, including nanotechnology, cell therapy, and the utilization of microbial approaches. Discoveries in stem cell biology in recent decades have provided new potential ways to treat cancer patients [ – ]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are among the most frequently utilized cell types in regenerative medicine. MSCs are regarded as a favorable reservoir of stem cells for individualized cell-based treatments. The intrinsic tumor-homing and immunomodulatory characteristics of MSCs can be harnessed to direct their activity toward cancer cells [ – ]. Currently, there have been significant advancements in the field of nanoparticle (NP) technology, particularly in the development of various pharmaceuticals. The utilization of NPs holds promise for the improved efficacy of treatments and diagnostics for a wide range of diseases, such as cancer [ , ]. Metal NPs have been widely regarded as potential agents for the treatment of cancer. Due to the abundant presence of natural metal oxides in the environment, the production and creation of these NPs can be considered a cost-effective synthetic approach [ , ]. Recently, bacteria alone and substances produced by bacteria have been described as having potential anticancer effects. Furthermore, bacteria have been effectively targeted to cancerous tissues using different strategies. Furthermore, the vast taxonomic diversity of bacteria presents many opportunities for the facile genetic manipulation of novel molecules, thereby indicating the potential of bacteria and bacterial anticancer compounds as a viable option for cancer therapy. Clostridium, Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Salmonella, Escherichia, Pseudomonas, Caulobacter, Proteus, Streptococcus, and Listeria are genera of bacteria that have already been reported as anticancer [ , ].
The purpose of this study is to examine the roles of three promising therapeutic avenues-nanomaterials, MSCs, and microbial agents in cancer treatment. We will review current literature, critically assess the therapeutic potential, and discuss advancements in each field. Additionally, this review aims to provide a comparative analysis of emerging cancer therapies, highlighting their unique mechanisms, advantages, limitations, and potential synergies for more effective, targeted cancer treatments.

## Cancer and cancer treatments
An essential part of proper patient management for many types of cancer is determining the extent of the disease to determine whether local therapies, “combination” approaches, or systemic therapy should be considered initially. Radiographic and other imaging studies can be helpful in clinical staging; the pathological stage documents the histological appearance of the tumor in tissue biopsies obtained through a surgical procedure. Sampling of lymph nodes for BC, melanoma, lung, head, neck cancer, and other abdominal cancers can provide important information [ , ]. The staging systems have developed to delineate a “T” factor associated with the size of the tumor or its invasion of nearby structures, an “N” factor related to the quantity and characteristics of lymph node clusters near the tumor, and indications of tumor dissemination, and an “M” factor based on the existence of local or distant metastatic locations. The different TNM components are subsequently amalgamated into stages, typically categorized as stages I–III or IV, contingent upon the anatomical site. The survival outcomes of combined TNM groups in numerical stages demonstrate comparable long-term results following treatment adjustments for each stage. In most cases, stage I tumors are characterized by being T1 (indicating a small size), N0 or N1 (indicating no or minimal spread to nearby lymph nodes), and M0 (indicating no presence of metastasis). Typically, these primary tumors exhibit a favorable response to localized therapeutic interventions. In contrast, stage IV tumors have spread to distant locations or invaded nearby internal organs in a way that cannot be surgically removed. They are treated with palliative intent, except for diseases exceptionally sensitive to systemic therapy, such as chemotherapy or immunotherapy. The TNM staging system is not applicable for conditions like leukemia, in which bone marrow infiltration is not localized, or for central nervous system (CNS) tumors, where tumor histology and the extent of resection are more significant factors for prognosis [ , ]. The primary approach for addressing cancer involves the surgical removal of tumors, followed by the administration of chemotherapy and radiation therapy. In certain instances, when the cancer, particularly pancreatic cancer, has advanced to a point where surgical intervention is no longer feasible, chemotherapy continues to be the established method of treatment [ , , ]. Many chemotherapy medications can eliminate both normal and malignant cells within the body. Hence, the administration of these chemotherapeutic agents may lead to severe adverse reactions and, in some cases, even mortality of the individual [ , ]. Numerous alternative methods have been suggested for the treatment of various cancer types, such as hormone therapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy. However, existing treatments remain inadequate [ , ]. Induction of apoptosis and inhibition of tumor cell growth and proliferation have been the mainstays of cancer treatment yet [ – ]. Cancer cells have changed signaling pathways (a problem that led researchers to treat with enzyme inhibitors). Cancer cells develop unconventional metabolic pathways to acquire the necessary energy and raw materials to support their rapid proliferation and mobility, leading to the potential for enzyme therapy. Cancer cells utilize unconventional metabolic pathways to generate power due to their heightened metabolic activity compared to healthy cells [ ]. On the other hand, a critical problem with current therapies is tumor hypoxia. Most solid tumors contain a hypoxia zone (which causes spore formation) [ , ]. Cancer cells also secrete various chemoattractants (which lead to the attraction of MSC migration into the tumor microenvironment (TME) [ ]. Bacterial cells have been found to employ multiple communication methods with tumor cells, in addition to metabolites. However, bacterial metabolites are widely recognized as the primary means bacterial cells exert their anti-cancer effects on cancer cell viability [ , ]. We examined metabolic substances currently under extensive investigation for their potential in cancer therapy, including pigments, enzymes, enzyme inhibitors, biosurfactants, bacterial extracts, exopolysaccharides, and nanomaterials. These substances hold promise for enhancing current anticancer treatments and facilitating favorable clinical outcomes while minimizing systemic adverse effects.

## Nanomaterials

## Synthetic nanoparticles (NPs)
Nanobiotechnology represents an emerging area within the field of nanotechnology, garnering widespread attention on a global scale. Since this discipline is relatively new, it has grown since its birth and has applications in many scientific fields [ – ]. Natural chemicals called NPs are stable, biocompatible, and non-toxic, making them suitable for use as an efficient drug delivery method [ , ]. NPs range in size from 1 to 100 nm [ , ]. NPs have been widely used in various fields. Since the shape, size, and composition of metallic nanomaterials are significantly related to their physical, chemical, and optical properties, techniques based on nanoscale materials have been used in several fields ranging from chemistry to medicine [ ]. These NPs are utilized in the creation, planning, and advancement of medicinal medications and drug delivery mechanisms [ ].
NPs are commonly categorized into three groups: organic NPs such as liposomes, carbon-based NPs like fullerenes, and metal nanoparticles (MNPs), including metal oxide NPs. The utilization of nanotechnology in the examination of MNPs has experienced substantial growth in the past twenty years [ ]. MNPs may be able to get around problems with conventional therapy. It is stated that MNPs enhance targeting, drug administration, and gene silencing, serving a beneficial and effective role in cancer therapy. Targeted ligand-functionalized MNPs improve tumor energy deposition control [ ]. MNPs can be categorized into various groups based on their elemental composition, including metal sulfides, metal oxides, organometallic frameworks, doped metals, and MNPs [ ]. Most MNPs are produced through chemical synthesis processes that generate harmful waste products [ ]. To mitigate the potential hazards of nanotechnology, the optimal approach involves the utilization of green nanotechnology in both the manufacturing and application processes. One of the significant advancements in the fields of materials science and nanotechnology is the development of engineered nanomaterials [ , ]. NPs have been produced through the metabolic activities of various microorganisms, such as bacteria, fungi, yeasts, and plants [ ]. Implementing “green synthesis” is essential to prevent the generation of undesirable or hazardous by-products by employing dependable, sustainable, and environmentally conscious synthetic methodologies. Utilizing ideal solvent systems and organic resources is essential for attaining this objective. The environmentally friendly synthesis of metallic NPs has been employed to incorporate many biological entities, such as bacteria, fungi, algae, and plant extracts [ , ]. It has been shown that both microorganisms, through intracellular synthesis, and their secreted molecules, through extracellular synthesis, can be utilized safely to synthesize MNPs [ ]. The process of biosynthesis is commonly denoted as the synthesis of molecules within the cell as well as outside the cell. Nevertheless, due to the labor-intensive isolation methods and the unnecessary consumption of time and resources associated with intracellular synthesis, extracellular biosynthesis appears to be a more favorable option. The process of producing MNPs through biosynthesis shows potential as a viable alternative to chemical synthesis, as it is believed to have a reduced environmental impact [ – ]. Because of their ability to reduce inflammation, their compatibility with living organisms, and their ability to fight against microorganisms, as well as their capacity to interact with biological systems, deliver drugs effectively, be absorbed by the body, and target tumors, NPs are frequently utilized in various biological, medical, and environmental contexts [ ]. The modification of MNPs with specific ligands enhances the regulation of energy accumulation within tumor cells. Furthermore, MNPs are employed not only for therapeutic purposes but also as a diagnostic modality for visualizing cancer cells. Therapeutic platforms utilizing MNPs not only facilitate concurrent diagnosis and treatment but also allow for precise and targeted drug delivery, thereby transforming the landscape of cancer treatment and patient care [ – ]. The use of copper NPs as potential therapies has been a recent discovery, as they have been shown to cause cancer cell death as a result of oxidative stress [ ]. Research into using gold NPs as delivery systems for other therapeutic agents such as siRNA and chemotherapy drugs has progressed [ , ]. The shape and size of gold NPs significantly affect the optical and electrical properties. In addition, gold NPs have a negative charge and can be easily functionalized with many biomolecules, including genes and targeting ligands [ ]. The utilization of silver (Ag) NPs as a secure and efficient therapy for cancer presents a complex issue. Collaborative research efforts encompassing chemists, physicists, engineers, and biologists are essential for comprehensively assessing the impact of Ag NPs on cellular systems. This evaluation should encompass the cytotoxic effects of Ag NPs and their potential to induce damage in malignant cells [ , ]. One of the cutting-edge nanomaterials that has greatly advanced the uses of noble metal nanomaterials in biomedicine is the palladium (Pd)-based NPs. Compared to other noble metal nanostructures, Pd-based nanomaterials provide several benefits. Among these benefits are strong photothermal stability and biocompatibility. Pd-based nanomaterials are remarkable and promising in biomedicine because of these features [ – ]. Also, Pd-based NPs have shown great promise in the field of cancer imaging contrast agents and therapeutic agents [ , ]. Because of their remarkable magnetic characteristics, ideal surface-to-volume ratio for effective functionalization, and biocompatibility, iron oxide-based NPs are often employed in biological applications. The majority of the emphasis is directed toward the development of these NPs for magnetic particle hyperthermia, or MRI, because they are now used in the medical system as heating mediators and contrast agents [ , ]. Various therapeutic NP platforms, including liposomes, albumin NPs, and polymeric micelles, have received approval for cancer treatment. Additionally, numerous other nanotechnology-assisted therapies, such as radiotherapy, hyperthermia, chemotherapy, gene or RNA interference (RNAi) treatment, and immunotherapy, are currently undergoing clinical trials [ ].
Specially designed intelligent NPs can gather at the location of a tumor either using passive targeting mechanisms, such as the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects, or through active targeting strategies mediated by receptors. These NPs offer an alternative method for independently regulating their reaction to each stimulus, enabling precise management of drug release in response to the combined influence of multiple triggers. To activate the NPs in the TME by introducing external stimuli like laser and ultrasound, these NPs incorporated several pulses. One of these stimuli loads the medication into the NPs in these systems, causing the medicine to be released. Additionally, extrinsic stimuli like temperature, magnetic fields, light, or ultrasonography might activate medication release at the target spot. Smart NPs have been designed particularly for anticancer medications because of the complicated TME, which includes aberrant expression of numerous receptors, high redox potential, and abnormal metabolic circumstances. For the treatment of lung cancer, for instance, a clever dual-responsive and targeted nano platform was created to co-administer chemotherapeutic drugs (DOX and PTX) [ – ]. Folic acid was employed in this nano platform as a receptor-mediated targeting chemical to help the nano platforms enter the tumor cells. Additionally, the pH- and GSH-sensitive drug release behavior of the nano platform was provided by the acid-responsive block copolymers and disulfide linkages in the TME. Specifically, during the transfer from the physiological environment to the TME, the prepared nano platforms exhibited a shift in surface charge from negative to positive, which may facilitate tumor cell internalization. Following that, endosomal escape of the nanoplatforms was accomplished using a proton sponge and an acidic endo/lysosomal milieu. This smart nano platform demonstrated significant cellular internalization, improved tumor cell suppression, and outstanding biocompatibility, as predicted. Furthermore, in tumor-bearing animal models, the nano platform showed synergistic effects and increased the killing efficiency of solid tumors compared to monotherapy. This implies that the hybridization and combination of cancer nanomedicine has enormous potential for treating tumors [ , ]. Kabiri et al. [ ] intend to biosynthesize gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) using Amycolatopsis sp. KMN and investigate its potential cytotoxic activities. The result demonstrated cytotoxic solid activity against two cell lines, MCF-7 and HT-29. The MTT test result displayed that throughout 48 h, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of AuNPs was 600 and 300 mg/ml for MCF-7 and HT-29 cell lines, respectively.
Ebadi et al. [ ] reported that they can synthesize ZnO NPs using the cell extract of the cyanobacterium Nostoc sp. EA03 (CEN-ZnO NPs) through a rapid and eco-friendly approach. MTT assay indicated that CEN-ZnO NPs had less cytotoxicity on the MRC-5 lung fibroblast cells than cancerous-treated A549 cells [ ]. Table provides a comprehensive overview of NPs utilized in cancer therapy. While nanomaterials offer innovative solutions in targeting and treating cancer, integrating biological elements such as MSCs can enhance the therapeutic efficacy of these approaches.
 

## Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs)
Stem cells can be employed in regenerative medicine, therapeutic carriers, drug targeting, immune cell production, and anticancer treatment. They have distinct biological functions such as self-renewal, directed migration, differentiation, and regulatory effects on other cells [ – ]. Among these many sources are (i) embryonic stem cells (ESCs), which may develop into any kind of cell except those found in the placenta when separated from the embryo's undifferentiated inner cell mass [ ]. Adult stem cells (ASCs) can develop into various specialized tissues, organs, and cell types. This subset frequently uses neural stem cells (NSCs), hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), and MSCs to treat cancer. (iii) Epigenetic alterations in precursor/progenitor cells or normal stem cells give rise to cancer stem cells (CSCs). CSCs are found in tumor tissues and are crucial for the development, spread, and recurrence of cancer [ ]. Numerous tissues and organs contain these MSCs, essential for tissue regeneration and repair (Fig.  ). They can increase quickly and generate various distinct cell types in vitro, including chondrocytes, adipocytes, and osteocytes. Because of their particular biological characteristics, MSCs are frequently employed to deliver therapeutic drugs or complement other therapies in treating different types of cancer [ – ].
 Other diseases treated with stem cells include liver disease, peripheral nervous system damage, cancer, cardiovascular disease, connective tissue repair, osteoarthritis, obesity, and diabetes [ , – ]. The primary advantage of employing MSCs for therapeutic reasons in both acute and chronic conditions is their readily available nature, which allows them to be expanded to the clinically necessary numbers [ , ]. The impact of MSC treatment on cancer cells is debatable; depending on the kind of MSC and its secretions, it may either prevent or accelerate the growth of cancer. MSC affects the apoptotic and proliferative pathways in cancer cells, as well as signaling pathways [ ]. Many methods of using stem cell therapy to treat cancer have been established, such as immune effector cell generation, HSC transplantation, MSC infusion for post-cancer treatment, stem cells in therapeutic carriers, and vaccine development [ ]. The active interaction between the stem cell CXCR4 receptor and the SDF-1 gradient, released by the cells lining the bone marrow stem cells, is the primary molecular mechanism underlying the HSC homing process. Other molecular signals include ceramide-1-phosphate, sphingosine-1-phosphate, extracellular ATP or UTP, and Ca2 + and H + ions [ – ]. Furthermore, HSC cells must engage with endothelial cells via LFA-1, VLA-4/5, and CD44 to migrate through blood vessels, and they must also secrete the matrix-degrading enzyme MMP-2/9 [ ]. Tumor development and invasion are determined by the extracellular matrix (ECM) and secreted paracrine substances in the TME. These factors attract the directed migration of many cell types, including MSCs, endothelial cells, and infiltrating immune cells. Tumors are thought to be chronic wounds that never heal and are always hypoxic, inflammatory, and under oxidative stress [ , ]. As a result, it is anticipated that MSC migration into the TME would resemble that of MSC migration to ischemia or damaged locations. Actually, by secreting different chemoattractive molecules, tumor cells and tumor-associated immune cells can both participate in this process [ , ]. Examples of substances that encourage MSC cell migration into the TME are CXCL16, SDF-1, CCL-25, and IL-6, which are released by cells with prostate cancer, osteosarcoma, multiple myeloma, and BC, respectively [ , ]. Tumor-associated immune cells produce proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-1β), which are crucial for the migration and differentiation of MSC cells within the tumor [ , ].
MSCs can differentiate into myofibroblasts or endothelial cells at tumor locations, which encourages the growth of tumor stroma. There is mounting evidence that stem cells work by secreting various paracrine substances, such as soluble materials and extracellular vesicles (EVs), which can affect the survival, metastasis, and development of tumors. Nanosized exosomes are a crucial modulator of cell-to-cell interactions among the three forms of EVs. They are produced in exocytosis of an intracellular multivesicular body, which contains various biological molecules such as proteins, mRNA, and miRNA [ – ]. Exosomes that are produced from MSCs can enter the tumor niche, where they are taken up by cancer cells by endocytosis or interactions between receptors and ligands. Then they release their cargo into recipient cells (Table  ) [ ]. The reasons for using stem cell carriers in cancer treatment are as follows: (i) protection of therapeutic agents against rapid biodegradation, reduction of systemic side effects, and (iii) increase of local levels of therapeutic agents due to the intratumoral action of stem cells. The antitumor efficacy of this system is based on the number of stem cells localized in the TME [ ]. This is how viruses are transduced into stem cells, such as MSC and NSC, to boost the production and secretion of soluble factors, such as prodrug-converting enzymes or cytokines/chemokines that are harmful to tumors [ ]. This system is known as “suicide gene therapy” or “gene-based enzyme prodrug therapy (GDEPT)” [ , ]. Prodrugs can be changed into active compounds that are more harmful to cancer cells by an enzyme secreted by live stem cells. For example, upon infusion of cytosine deaminase (CD)-expressing MSCs or NSCs, 5-fluorocytosine (5-FC) is effectively converted to the tumor-toxic 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) [ – ]. Similarly, in carboxylesterase (CE), the less effective prodrug irinotecan can be converted to a 1000-fold more lethal molecule (SN-38). Tumor grafts in a mouse model of neuroblastoma were more responsive to irinotecan and CE-expressing NSCs used in combination than they were to either drug alone [ , ]. It has been discovered that transduced MSCs and prodrugs work well together and can potentially stabilize the cancerous state [ , ]. Furthermore, secretory cytokines and chemokines can be expressed genetically by stem cells. After systemic administration, the tumor-toxic TNF-α-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) has a brief half-life. It's interesting to note that NSCs employed as gene carriers for TRAIL sustained release have been demonstrated to decrease brain tumor spread and increase treated mice's survival [ , ]. This system was used in a clinical trial to treat human lung adenocarcinoma (NCT03298763, enrollment). Co-expression of a suicide gene (i.e., CD) and a cytotoxic gene (i.e., IFN-β, inhibits tumor growth and induces their apoptosis) in stem cells show promise for improving treatment efficacy [ , ].
 NPs have been used for cancer drug delivery for a long time. Disadvantages include their quick elimination from the body, inability to operate specifically, and uncontrollably high absorption by normal cells [ ]. The fact that stem cells may effectively transport NPs to tumor locations is an intriguing finding. This method allows for injecting NPs into cells or their conjugation to the cell surface. The process of NP internalization involves either passive absorption or active endocytosis, contingent upon the NPs' size, surface properties, concentration, and duration of incubation [ , ]. The management of medication loading and possible toxicity to the cell carrier are the primary issues. Additionally, uncontrolled release of therapeutic medicines to non-target locations may result from the quick exocytosis of NPs from cells. Without compromising the survival and functionality of the cells, Roger et al. [ ] showed how to incorporate lipids and PLA-NPs into MSCs effectively. Following direct tumor injection, MSCs transported these NPs to brain tumors in a mouse model of human glioma [ ]. For now, NPs can attach to stem cells and target malignancies by engaging with amine or thiol functional groups on the cell surface [ , ]. According to a recent study by Layek et al. [ ], conjugating cyclooctyne-modified NPs with azide-functionalized MSCs to generate an ambient-stable triazole can increase NP loading efficiency and retention on cells [ ]. Although MSCs provide significant benefits in targeting tumors and modulating immune responses, the bacteria present an additional layer of therapeutic potential, a theme that we will explore in the subsequent section.

## Bacteria-driven cancer therapy
The necessity for developing potent treatment techniques and therapeutic agents is increasing due to the shortcomings of current cancer treatments and the need to improve their efficacy. Scientists have been investigating the possibility of using helpful bacteria and their byproducts as innovative medications to treat and prevent cancer cells in recent decades. The benefit of these bacterial-based treatments is that they are less harmful to healthy cells and have fewer adverse effects [ – ]. Promising results have been observed in treating malignancies using bacterial compounds called endotoxins (lipopolysaccharides). Furthermore, it has been shown that bacterial toxins may specifically destroy cancer cells, and bacteria-derived immunotoxins are frequently used in cancer vaccinations. Through the use of bacteria's distinct modes of action and immunogenic qualities, combining bacterial therapeutics with conventional chemotherapy may improve treatment results [ ]. The first classes of bacteria to be employed as live anticancer medications were Streptococci and Clostridium; however, bacteria have a wide range of anticancer properties [ , ]. Given the various anticancer processes and tactics, genetically modified bacteria are currently mainly employed in anticancer therapy [ , , ]. The primary anticancer techniques employed by cancer bacteria include toxicity of live bacteria, gene transfer, generation of anticancer metabolites, RNA interference, prepharmaceutical cleavage, and usage in conjunction with other medicines [ , ]. Anticancer treatment involves the use of common genera such as Streptococcus, Salmonella, Clostridium, Escherichia, Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, Mycobacterium, Pseudomonas, Caulobacter, Listeria, and Proteus, which have been proven to have anticancer action [ ]. Mandatory anaerobes, such as Clostridium spp., may develop in the hypoxic environment of solid tumors, making them suitable for use in anticancer treatment. Reportedly, orthotopically transplanting Clostridium novyi-NT into rats inhibits the formation of glioblastomas. Furthermore, a recent study found that injecting C. novyi-NT spores intratumorally also has anticancer effects [ , , ]. Mycobacterium bovis strain BCG (Bacillus Calmette-Guerin) has been used as a TB vaccine in several nations since 1921; yet, its significance in cancer regression has only lately been identified [ , ]. Clinical research indicates that when BCG is injected intravenously, cancer recurrence is extremely uncommon following resection or chemotherapy; nevertheless, the length and dosage of treatment vary according to the disease's stage [ , ]. BCG treatment is beneficial in lowering tumor recurrence and enhancing patient outcomes in non-muscle invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) patients [ , ].
The distinct physiological features of the TME, such as thick stroma, diverse vascular leakage, and hypoxic circumstances, decrease the effectiveness of NP-based tumor targeting [ , ]. The use of NPs alone in cancer treatment is limited since their transport mostly depends on passive accumulation at tumor sites through systemic circulation, lacking active driving factors to penetrate deeply into tumors. This lowers the therapeutic efficacy of NPs [ ]. Tumor targeting by bacteria is a potential approach to cancer treatment, especially when paired with other anti-cancer therapies. By optimizing medication targeting, this strategy can increase the effectiveness of cancer treatment while also drastically lowering adverse effects [ ]. Researchers use anaerobic bacteria' innate ability to target anaerobic environments as drug delivery vehicles to deliver therapeutic medicines to tumor-hypoxic areas [ , ]. By affixing medications to microorganisms, this method achieves tailored cancer treatment. Bifidobacterium, Salmonella typhimurium, and Escherichia coli are often employed bacterial carriers for tumor treatment. Research has demonstrated the effective use of attenuated strains of Salmonella in conjunction with nanotechnology for the treatment of melanoma [ , ]. Moreover, liposomes conjugated with various bacterial strains have demonstrated encouraging outcomes in cancer treatment and diagnostic applications [ ]. Using anaerobic bacteria for tumor-targeted therapy represents an innovative and productive approach to cancer treatment, owing to their distinctive ability to target anaerobic environments. Numerous research investigations have employed specific bacterial strains as carriers for pharmaceuticals, including anticancer agents, and as transport mechanisms for drug-encapsulated nanomaterials. This approach aims to improve the targeting of tumors and to augment the therapeutic effectiveness of traditional cancer treatments [ ]. As a result, bacterial carriers are becoming a new medication delivery frontier [ ]. Nanomedicines are delivered to the deep areas of solid tumors using the anaerobic targeting and autonomous movement of bacteria. At the same time, nanomaterials may be affixed to the surface of bacteria in a variety of ways to create bacterium-NP hybrids that facilitate the delivery of NPs [ ]. The utilization of a bacteria-NP hybrid strategy in cancer therapy has facilitated significant progress in the field of bacterial research about tumor treatment. The utilization of bacteria as vehicles for nanomedicines is garnering heightened interest among researchers engaged in developing novel anticancer therapeutics [ ].

## Anaerobic bacteria’s spores
The capacity of anaerobic bacteria to generate incredibly durable spores has been revealed, allowing them to survive in oxygen-rich settings. Until they come into favorable environments, such as hypoxic regions seen in tumor tissues, these spores stay dormant. Because of this exceptional quality, spore-producing anaerobic bacteria are a desirable target for cancer cells [ , ]. Anaerobic bacteria's spores can create, develop, and manufacture anti-cancer medications. Moreover, they can be carriers to transfer drugs and genes to tumor tissues in hypoxic necrotic tissues; these bacterial spores can germinate, increase, and have anticancer effects [ ]. The Clostridium genus of anaerobic bacteria is well known for its capacity to accurately germinate and eliminate hypoxic malignancies that are resistant to treatment. Most solid tumors have a hypoxic zone, which offers an excellent ecological habitat for the development of anaerobic bacteria [ ]. Genetically modified C. novyi-NT strains do not produce systemic adverse effects in the injected host and do not possess deadly toxins [ ]. Tumor tissue was lysed in mice models of uterine cancer when C. histolyticum spores were directly injected into the uterus. Similarly, tumor tissue lysis was also seen with intravenous (IV) delivery of C. sporogenes spores [ ]. Comprehensive toxicological and pharmacological analyses of C. novyi-NT spores have demonstrated their rapid clearance from the circulatory system by the reticuloendothelial system (RES). In healthy persons, this effective clearance system is essential to avoiding any possible damage caused by the spores. Additionally, comprehensive tests on mice and rabbits subjected to large dosages of these spores also revealed no toxicities that were clinically detrimental, demonstrating their good safety profile in non-tumor-bearing individuals [ ]. Bacterial spores have shown their promise in targeted medicine delivery by emerging as novel carriers of anti-cancer drugs [ ].

## Bacterial metabolites
As a novel antineoplastic tactic, scientists and researchers have investigated the possibility of using bacteria and their metabolites to kill tumor cells throughout the previous ten years. They discovered that the bacteria and the compounds they produce are not only minimally harmful but also have little adverse impacts on healthy, normal cells. Cancer patients have been treated using a variety of bacterial species in different forms and bacterial metabolites (living, genetically modified, bacterial products, gene therapy vectors, cancer medication transporters, anaerobic bacteria, etc.). The medicines' ability to specifically increase and stop the growth of tumors was demonstrated by the results [ , ]. Natural products are mostly produced by common producers, which include filamentous actinomycetes, cyanobacteria, myxobacteria, Pseudomonas, and Bacillus species. More than 10,000 bioactive chemicals are produced by Actinomycetales species, making them the largest class of bioactive microbial metabolites [ ]. Microbes create auxiliary substances called secondary metabolites, which are advantageous to the cells but not necessary for regular cell development. These substances could be involved in the uptake of nutrients, inhibition, communication, or other interactions with nearby species or the environment. These compounds include degrading enzymes, quorum-sensing molecules, antibiotics, and siderophores, to name a few [ ]. Prathiba et al. [ ] found that a bacterial extract of the halophilic Bacillus VITPS16 has a cytotoxic effect on Hella cells. The MTT assay showed that metabolite fractions (S2-85.74%) have cytotoxic effects. The cytotoxic potential of the fractions was validated by a clonogenic test, and apoptosis experiments demonstrated that, at 10 mg/ml, 83.37% of cells underwent apoptosis (MF-S2). A portion of the metabolite interacts with DNA to produce cytotoxicity, according to research on DNA binding. One potential mechanism by which MF-S2 generates antiproliferative effects and apoptosis is DNA binding. Even at 10 mg/ml, the metabolite fractions are not harmful, according to a zebrafish toxicity test. Glycerophospholipids, phosphoethanolamines, apocarotenoids, sphingolipids, enigma and its analogs, ancaflavin, and flavonoid metabolites that have been linked to anticancer activity were all detected in fraction MF-S2 [ ]. Researchers have demonstrated the anti-cancer properties of lactic acid bacteria. Cancer cell growth can be impacted by nisin, bacterial cytoplasmic extract, and the cell walls of Lactococcus lactis. Since cyclin D1 is crucial to the advancement of the cell cycle, controlling it may also be a therapeutic strategy. The cancer cells treated with nisin 2000 μg, cytoplasmic extract, and cell wall had survival rates of 34%, 47%, and 49%, in that order. The cyclin D1 mRNA expression in nisin-treated sw480 cells is dramatically decreased (P < 0.05), according to real-time PCR data [ ]. Dysbiosis, or changes in the normal composition of the intestinal microbiota, can impact the concentrations of metabolites, which can be one of the basic processes behind the development of colorectal cancer (CRC) and influence carcinogenesis in other settings. The breakdown products of long-chain fatty acids, short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), tryptophan, polyphenol compounds, and polyamines are among the other metabolites produced by bacteria that are of great interest because of their shown impacts on immunological and cellular signaling [ ]. Dietary fiber and other fermentable, non-digestible carbohydrates are major sources of SCFAs gut bacteria produce [ ]. The overall concentration of SCFAs, which comprise propionate, acetate, and butyrate, may surpass 100 millimolars in the colon [ ]. SCFAs are generated by two primary bacterial groups: Firmicutes produce butyrate, while the Bacteroidetes phylum produces propionate and acetate [ ]. According to the clinical case studies, the SCFA concentrations in the feces of CRC patients were lower than those of the control group. This is consistent with a lower concentration of bacteria synthesizing SCFAs, including Lachnospiraceae, Roseburia sp., Bifidobacterium sp., and others [ , ]. Microbial metabolites called polyamines (PAs) are mostly produced in the gut by Firmicutes [ , ]. Moreover, PAs can be produced in host cells and taken up from meals in the upper intestine [ ]. It was suggested that changes in intracellular PAs and alterations in their metabolism are linked to breast, lung, colon, prostate, and skin malignancies based on clinical findings and research on cell cultures and animal models [ ]. Actively proliferating cells, including chicken embryos, regenerated rat liver, and different malignancies, were shown to have increased PA production [ ]. The capacity of probiotic bacteria to modify the amount of PAs in the intestinal lumen is one of their anticancer properties. The amounts of PAs decreased when rats were fed a probiotic cocktail enriched with Bifidobacterium sp., Streptococcus sp., and Lactobacillus sp. [ ]. Khoshakhlagh et al. [ ] found in a study that the secondary metabolite of three new actinomycete strains Streptomyces griseofavus, Streptomyces calvus, and Kitasatospora phosalacineus isolated from soil was able to induce apoptosis in A549 cells by 25, 14.5, and 7.58%, respectively, by the flow cytometry method.

## Biosurfactant
A variety of microorganisms, including bacteria (Bacillus, Acinetobacter, and Pseudomonas), filamentous fungi (Aspergillus, Penicillium, Fusarium, Trichoderma, and Ustilago), yeasts (Rhodotorula, Pseudozyma, Kluyveromyces, Torulopsis, Saccharomyces, and Candida), and certain animals and plants, can produce biosurfactants [ ]. Amphiphilic substances called biosurfactants are produced by bacteria extracellularly or as a component of the cell membrane [ ]. They are primarily produced as secondary metabolites and have a variety of functions that are crucial to the location, survival, and expansion of the microorganisms that produce them, including motility, metabolism, surface attachment and detachment, defense against microbial antagonists, formation and collapse of biofilms, and resistance to harmful substances [ ]. Biosurfactants have had a significant impact in the fields of medicine, food, and bioremediation. Chemical surfactants have dominated the market, while recent attention has focused on extracting less toxic and more biodegradable biosurfactants [ ]. Biosurfactants may be effective as anticancer medications due to their intriguing biological activity profile. The molecules turn into up-and-coming medication candidates for treating a variety of infectious disorders; biosurfactant, for example, exhibits both antibacterial and antiproliferative properties against specific pathogens and human lung cancer cell lines in vitro [ ]. Recently, research has been done on the mechanisms of action of some biosurfactants, such as bolalipids, glycolipids, and acid and lactonic sophorolipids, against cancer cells. Three distinct cell lines were used for the experiments: the mouse skin melanoma cell line (B16F10), the lung cancer cell line (A549), and the BC cell line (MDA-MB 231The findings suggest that glycolipids prevent the migration of tumor cells, perhaps through the disruption of actin filaments, and that reactive oxygen species are produced in cells by both glycolipids and lactone sophorolipids. Furthermore, these biosurfactants altered the potential of the mitochondrial membrane, which ultimately resulted in necrosis and cell death [ ]. Bacillus halotolerensis produces a lipopeptide (LP) that, at deficient concentrations, has demonstrated encouraging anticancer action against the human BC cell line MCF-7. Biosurfactants impede critical signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt, JAK/STAT, and MAPK signaling, which in turn impair processes involved in cancer development. Furthermore, biosurfactants can trigger apoptosis via cancer cell death receptors, suppress angiogenesis, and increase natural killer (NK) cells [ ]. By halting cell cycle arrest, metastasis, and apoptosis, surfactin, a cyclic lipopeptide biosurfactant, has demonstrated anticancer properties. One significant benefit of their improved transport when targeting cancer cells is the capacity to combine these surfactin molecules into nanoformulations, including polymeric NPs, micelles, and liposomes [ ]. Significant biofilm and anticancer activity against T47D BC cells and B16F10 murine melanoma cells was also demonstrated by Bacillus safensis F4 surfactin [ ]. Biosurfactants are considered active chemicals since they prevent cancer cells from synthesizing DNA and are non-toxic. Both cytotoxicity and antiproliferative efficacy against cancer cell types were assessed in vitro. The lung cancer cell line A549 demonstrated the anti-proliferative activity of the biosurfactant obtained from Acinetobacter indicus M6 bacteria, and the non-tumor cell line MC-3 T3-E1 demonstrated the biosurfactant's non-toxicity. The stoppage of the G1 phase in A549 cells suggests that the mechanism of cell death is due to the suppression of DNA synthesis. Because of its detergent action, the protein portion of the biosurfactant caused the cell membrane to break down [ ]. A further biosurfactant, rakicidins, derived from the marine bacteria Micromonospora, exhibits hypoxia-selective toxicity against many cell lines, including HCT-8 colon cancer cells, A549 lung cancer cells, SGC7901 gastric cancer cells, HeLa cervical cancer cells, and HepG2 hepatocellular carcinoma cells. Rachidins cause apoptosis by blocking signaling pathways like JNK/p38 and MAPK and activating caspase-mediated pathways [ ]. A summary of biosurfactants isolated from bacteria is shown in Table  .
 

## Exopolysaccharides (EPS)
High-molecular-weight carbohydrate polymers known as exopolysaccharides (EPS) are produced by either extracellular or intracellular routes and then released by microbes extracellularly [ ]. As bacterial growth is a prerequisite for EPS generation, batch mode monitoring of maximum EPS yields may be done during both exponential and stationary bacterial growth [ ]. Additionally, EPS can shield cells from phagocytosis, toxic metals, pH, antibiotics, and other dangerous substances [ ]. Recent years have seen a variety of microbiological sources of EPS being examined and researched [ ]. EPSs comprise substances, including proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, DNA, vesicles, or pieces of cells. Exposure to various species and, or environmental factors might alter the composition of EPSs [ ]. Owing to the usefulness of EPS in real-world applications, it has garnered interest in various industries, including pharmaceuticals, nutraceuticals, cosmetics, beneficial foods, and pesticides and herbicides [ ]. EPS has attracted widespread attention due to the environmental properties and applications of EPS, such as nontoxic, antioxidant, antitumor, stable, emulsified, viscous, antibiofilm, and antimicrobial [ – ]. Numerous studies have documented polysaccharides' capacity to cause cancer cells to undergo apoptosis, whereas their cytotoxicity against normal cells is negligible. Commercial polysaccharide products and combinations, such as grifolin, PSP (polysaccharide-peptide complex), and crestin (polysaccharide-protein complex), are beneficial to many cancer patients [ ]. Two distinct mechanisms allow polysaccharides to exert their antitumor effects: the death receptor pathway, also known as the extrinsic pathway, is activated by the binding of tumor necrosis factor receptor (TNFR) family members to the intracellular death domain, whereas the BCL2-regulated pathway, also known as the intrinsic, mitochondrial, or stress pathway, is activated by cytokine deprivation, ER stress, or DNA damage [ ]. The BCL2-regulated apoptotic pathway is responsible for promoting cell death in response to overexpression of the proapoptotic BH3-only members (BIM, PUMA, BID, BMF, BAD, BIK, NOXA, and HRK) of the BCL2 protein family. The pro-apoptotic multi-BH domain members of the BCL2 family, BAX and BAK, are activated when the BH3-only proteins interact with and inhibit pro-survival BCL2 proteins (BCL2, BCLXL, MCL1, BCLW, and A1/BFL1) [ ]. It has been demonstrated that some BH3-only proteins directly activate BAX/BAK. The activation of BAX/BAK results in the permeabilization of the mitochondrial outer membrane (MOMP), which activates cysteine proteases specific to aspartate and its activator, APAF-1, which lyses cells. On the other hand, the down-regulation mechanism causes apoptosis by attaching to and triggering procaspase-8 at death receptors connected to the plasma membrane via the adapters FADD and, seldom, TRADD. To properly cause apoptosis in so-called type 1 cells, caspase eight activations must come first, and then effector caspases (caspases 3 and 7) (e.g., thymocytes). However, for so-called type 2 cells (hepatocytes, for example) to undergo effective cell death, the caspase cascade must be amplified by crossover activation of the BCL2-regulated apoptotic pathway. This is achieved by Caspase 8-mediated proteolytic activation of the ordinarily inert BH3-only protein BID [ ]. Owing to their excellent hydrogel-forming capacity, EPSs generated by lactic acid bacteria (LAB) have a variety of additional beneficial functions that are used in therapeutic medicine as drug carriers and in adjuvant therapy for the treatment of cancer and inflammation [ – ]. For instance, because of their apparent lack of toxicity and biocompatibility, Moscovici [ ] tried to insert medicines and biomolecules into the internal structure of EPS for use as an intelligent drug delivery system (DDS) for the treatment of neurological illnesses, including brain tumors [ ]. The L. plantarum strain's EPS exhibited exceptional anticancer efficacy against HepG-2, BGC-823, and particularly HT-29 cancer cells [ ].

## Enzymes
Essential amino acids are necessary for healthy cell metabolism and development. These amino acids can function as a limiting factor, preventing tumor cells from growing uncontrollably and rapidly. Another anti-cancer therapy is a deficiency of these vital amino acids. To achieve this, bacteria generate a wide variety of enzymes that act on these vital amino acids and prevent several cellular processes that are required for the formation of tumors. The often-researched bacterial enzymes are arginine decarboxylase, l-asparaginase, and arginine deiminase [ ]. The hydrolysis of asparagine is catalyzed by l-asparaginase (l-ASNase), whereas arginine catabolism is carried out by enzymes such as arginine deiminase and arginine decarboxylase. These enzymes deplete the amino acids required for protein synthesis, which ultimately results in the demise of tumor cells [ ]. Treatments for acute lymphoblastic leukemia, neoplasia, lymphosarcoma, and other malignant tumors have demonstrated the efficacy of l-ASNase [ ]. Commercial asparaginases are antigenic, produce hypersensitivity responses, and have l-glutaminase activity. l-ASNase lacks l-glutaminase co-activity, it is far more successful than other FDA-approved asparaginases at treating acute lymphoblastic leukemia with fewer side effects and less toxicity. l-ASNase is produced by species such as Bacillus subtilis, Escherichia coli, Streptomyces, and Erwinia. The enzyme l-ASNase reduced blood asparagine levels in research by Nguyen et al. [ ], which stopped protein synthesis and caused cell cycle arrest in leukemia cells in the G1 phase [ ]. Alrumman et al. [ ] isolated 40 bacteria from the Saudi Arabian Red Sea and used a qualitative fast plate test to screen them for ASNase production. Of these, 28 bacteria generated significant l-asparagine hydrolysis sites. Compared to freely floating cells, immobilized bacterial cells produced more ASNase. Bacillus licheniformis KKU-KH14 was a promising strain that lacked glutaminase activity among these isolates. Purified ASNase had a noteworthy toxic impact on HepG-2 cells (IC50 11.66 μg/ml) in comparison to MCF-7 (IC50 14.55 μg/ml) and HCT-116 cells (IC50 17.02 μg/ml) due to its anticancer action [ ]. Recently, l-glutaminase has been the subject of investigation as another anticancer enzyme. l-glutaminase's cytotoxic action showed exceptional safety in Vero cells with strong anticancer activity against NFS-60, HepG-2, and MCF-7 cancer cell lines, according to research by Orabi et al. G1 phase cells are significantly reduced, S phase cells were marginally reduced by increasing the concentration of the l-glutaminase enzyme, and G2 phase cells were reduced when treated with 10, 15, and 20 mg/ml l-glutaminase in comparison to untreated cells [ ]. The synthesis of l-methionine gamma-lyase was investigated in soil harboring Pseudomonas sp. ON993898.1. ## Enzymes inhibitors
Numerous biological activities, including cell differentiation and proliferation, signal transmission, metabolism, DNA damage repair, apoptosis, and stress response, depend on enzymes. Enzyme dysregulation is thought to be one of the primary causes of a wide range of illnesses, including cancer. Enzyme inhibitors have drawn more attention in recent years for their potential application in pharmacology. They are excellent candidates for treating pathogenic processes [ ]. Common pharmacological targets include proteases, kinases, and phosphatases [ ]. Several natural product-derived kinase inhibitors, such as staurosporine and roscovitine, and the mTOR kinase inhibitor rapamycin (sirolimus), have been authorized for clinical usage as anticancer medications. Early in the 1970s, the soil bacteria Streptomyces hygroscopicus produced the naturally occurring macrocyclic polyketide rapamycin, which was then shown to have antifungal properties. It is now being employed as a single agent or in combination with other chemotherapies in several clinical studies for anticancer treatment [ – ]. Amylase inhibitors are helpful treatments for conditions including obesity, diabetes, and hyperlipemia dependent on carbohydrates [ ]. Protease activity is controlled at two levels: post-translational modification (activation of inactive zymogens) and transcription (reduction in protease expression and rise in endogenous inhibitors). Proteases are a significant class of therapeutic targets. In terms of the overall number of inhibitors found, the hunt for protease inhibitors from nature and structure-based research has been highly fruitful. Based on the amino acids involved in catalysis, these proteolytic enzymes are categorized into five classes: metalloproteases (34%), aspartic (4%), cysteine (26%), threonine (5%), serine (31%), and threonine (5%). In the active site of serine, threonine, and cysteine proteases, the matching amino acid residue functions as a nucleophile; in the case of asparagine and metalloproteases, an activated water molecule serves as the nucleophile. 62% of the clinically authorized protease inhibitors have a natural product connection, even when synthetic natural products are excluded; if natural products are taken into considered, this percentage increases to 89% [ ]. Human chronic myelogenous leukemia (K562) cell lines treated with a pure trypsin inhibitor demonstrated apoptosis, and TI doses of 25 µM and 50 µM inhibited the growth of K562 cells. ## Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs)
Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are minute peptides that possess significant importance in the natural defense mechanism of the host against an extensive array of microorganisms, encompassing bacteria, fungi, parasites, and viruses [ – ]. Additionally, bacterial peptides can stop tumor cell development [ ]. Because of their cationic qualities, AMPs primarily attach to cancer cells, breaking down their lipid membranes and ultimately leading to the death of the cells [ ]. Certain AMPs can cause cancer cells to undergo apoptosis by turning on several cell death-related pathways. As a result, cancer cells may be eliminated without endangering healthy cells. By interfering with signaling pathways involved in blood vessel creation, certain AMPs can restrict angiogenesis, stopping cancer cells from growing and spreading. By stimulating immune cells like NK cells and macrophages, which aid in the destruction of cancer cells, other AMPs can modify the immunological response [ ]. Here we have mentioned some of the antimicrobial peptides produced by bacteria that are observed to have anti-cancer properties: The Lactococcus lactis subspecies, which produces nisin, has 34 amino acid residues. Lactis has been demonstrated to be cytotoxic to MCF-7 cells, a human BC cell line, and to have antibacterial action against most Gram-negative bacteria [ ]. Laterosporulin 10, a defensin-like peptide produced by Brevibacillus species, is cytotoxic to cancer cell lines, including HT1080, a fibrosarcoma cell line, HeLa cells, HEK293T cells, and MCF-7 (a human breast adenocarcinoma cell line) [ ]. Zhao revealed that the HPRP-A1 peptide, isolated from Helicobacter pylori, has anticancer properties [ ]. Furthermore, the potential for augmenting the anticancer activity of HPRP-A1 and iRGD (homopeptide) was explored through their interaction. The findings demonstrated that iRGD contributed to higher penetration of HPRP-A1 in A549 MCS [ ]. Sulfolobus islandicus-isolated VLL-28 suppresses tumor cells in mice and humans [ ]. By reducing the expression of early growth response protein 4, gramicidin A can cause tumor cell necrosis and prevent the proliferation of cholangiocarcinoma cells (EGR-4) [ ]. In gastric cancer cells (SGC-7901 and BGC-823), gramicidin has also been demonstrated to have an anticancer impact due to its ability to promote cell death by blocking the G2/M cell cycle, downregulating the expression of cyclin D1, B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2), and phosphorylating Forkhead Box O1 (FOXO1) [ ]. In summary, the potential of microbial agents in cancer treatment illustrates the diversity of therapeutic strategies available.

## Challenges and limitations
Despite the promising applications of MSCs, nanomaterials, and microbial agents in cancer treatment that are encouraging, it is essential to recognize challenges and several inherent limitations. Bacteria that target tumors can enter primary tumors, spread throughout the body, and multiply in necrotic regions. The limitations of current clinical cancer treatments, including radiotherapy, surgery, and other physical therapies that cannot target potential metastatic sites, and chemotherapy, molecular targeted therapy, cell therapy, and antibody therapy, which cannot enter the interior of tumors, are successfully overcome by this unique proliferation mechanism. However, the outcomes of clinical trials have demonstrated that bacterial monotherapy has not yet produced notable anticancer benefits [ – ]. This is mainly because the released therapeutic chemicals cannot permeate across the complete solid tumor, and the cytotoxicity of bacteria that target tumors is insufficient to eradicate tumor cells. Furthermore, certain bacteria's ability to increase may encourage the growth and spread of tumors, which presents serious difficulties for therapeutic use [ ]. Therefore, for its ultimate therapeutic applicability, bacterial targeted therapy must be combined with the benefits of current clinical therapies [ ]. Potential bacteria-based biotherapy hazards need to be properly and adequately evaluated. It is critical to address biosafety concerns since these conjugates include live bacterial cells. A robust immunological response may be triggered if many bacterial cells are quickly introduced into the human body [ , ]. Additionally, there is a need to increase the binding stability of bacteria and NPs during in vivo transport, and performing clinical trials is fraught with challenges. Moreover, another issue impeding the anti-cancer effectiveness of bacteria is their low drug-loading efficiency. Genetically altering bacteria to manufacture anti-cancer medications may improve the therapeutic impact in addition to further refining the manufacturing techniques. Lastly, compared to small molecule anti-cancer medications, the manufacturing procedure for live bacteria is more intricate [ , ].
Nanomaterials are easy to produce on a lab scale. Still, large-scale manufacture appears to be a complex undertaking because of the advanced experimental setup's constraints and the lack of adequate knowledge of scale-up methods. Adverse effects are also seen as a result of the challenges faced in recreating the preparation process and scaling up the procedure [ ]. Contaminants, inadequate batch-to-batch variability, poor quality control, biocompatibility, chemical instability, low production yield, high cost, scalability issues, lack of infrastructure, government regulations, and lack of funding are some of the difficulties encountered during the manufacturing of nanomaterials [ – ].
One of the primary issues with nanomaterials is still their toxicity. Surface area, particle size and shape, solubility, and aggregation are some variables that affect toxicity. Physiological barriers may be broken down due to their minuscule size, posing health risks [ , ]. There is proof that NP-induced free radicals damage DNA, organelles, and cellular membranes [ ]. Interacting with cell surface receptors, intracellularly administered nanomaterials may elicit an immunological response [ , ]. Titanium dioxide NPs can traverse the placental barrier, potentially leading to anatomical malformations in the developing fetus when pregnant mice are exposed to them [ ]. Furthermore, Ag NPs have been shown to decrease plasma levels of estrogen, leading to an increase in the number of resorbed fetuses and affects embryonic development [ , ].
A further complex challenge involves circumventing the "mononuclear phagocytic system (MPS)." In biological fluids, NPs tend to adsorb proteins, resulting in the formation of a protein corona (PC), which facilitates the uptake of NPs by the MPS. To mitigate this issue, NPs have been coated with various materials designed to inhibit the formation of the PC. They haven't, nonetheless, produced any noteworthy outcomes. To get around this issue, NPs that target "macrophages" can be designed and used as novel drug carriers [ ].
One of the clinical challenges of the therapeutic use of MSCs is the culture and manufacturing conditions. Since MSCs are not common after isolation, they must be expanded ex vivo to a large quantity before being used. Consequently, the culture and manufacturing conditions may significantly affect the characteristics of MSCs and should be thoroughly characterized [ ]. Animal-derived growth supplements, including fetal bovine serum (FBS), have been primarily utilized to expand MSCs. However, there are severe drawbacks and safety issues with using items obtained from animals. In particular, animal welfare, the possibility of triggering xenogeneic immunological responses, and the danger of contamination and infectious agent transmission should all be taken into considered. Furthermore, it is still unknown exactly what makes up FBS, and lots frequently differ significantly from one another. Therefore, it is vital to identify viable substitutes for animal serum that meet all pertinent clinical standards and supply the correct number of high-quality cells while maintaining the requisite characteristics [ , ].
Depending on the sources of MSCs and the kind of tumor, MSC-derived EVs play a wide range of dual roles in oncogenesis, tumor development, and chemoresistance. As a result, the safety debate around native MSC-derived EVs has persisted for a long time and is seen to be their "Achilles' heel" in terms of therapeutic applications. It is essential to understand the mechanics underlying how one type of EV affects a particular malignancy. Therefore, to treat the specific cancer type with minimal side effects, the most suitable MSC source may be selected for bioengineered EV generation. However, to ascertain the possible therapeutic scope, the same kind of EVs must also be applied to various tumors. One crucial and innovative technical approach may be creating techniques to deactivate or eliminate undesirable and hazardous EV contents. To ensure treatment safety, optimize dosing schedules, and hinder medication distribution, an in vivo monitoring platform is also necessary [ ].
A significant challenge associated with utilizing MSCs is their intrinsic capacity to migrate towards tumor locations. Although targeted medication delivery can benefit from this trait, there is a danger that it could encourage tumor development by improving the TME or, in some instances, unintentionally encouraging metastasis. Furthermore, MSCs' production of growth factors and cytokines might occasionally promote tumor development and survival, making safe clinical translation difficult [ – ].
Another challenge associated with using MSCs pertains to the necessary administration route of application and dosage. The method of administration for MSCs is significantly influenced by the intended therapeutic outcome. Typical routes for MSC delivery include systemic approaches, such as intravenous (IV), intra-arterial (IA), and inhalation methods, as well as local techniques, which encompass topical application, direct tissue injection, intramuscular injection, trans-epithelial, trans-endocardial, and intra-articular administration [ ]. Among the factors affecting the safety and effectiveness of the treatment are the method of administration, the precise and adequate quantity of MSCs, the number of doses (single or repeated doses), and the time between each dosage [ , ].
Another challenge associated with the utilization of MSCs pertains to their hemocompatibility. Research has shown that MSCs are ABO-neutral and do not naturally express ABO blood-type antigens. Nonetheless, the application of human AB plasma (ABP) in conjunction with MSCs resulted in the adsorption of ABO antigens in proportion to the serum antigen concentration and adsorption duration. Therefore, it is advised to wash and infuse MSCs with nonimmunogenic human serum albumin, especially when treating immunocompetent individuals or patients with blood type O [ , ]. MSCs can sometimes trigger immediate blood-mediated inflammatory responses (IBMIRs). The failure of allogenic graft survival and function is primarily caused by this. Procoagulant tissue factor (TF), which strongly correlates with the start of IBMIR, is the primary determinant of MSC hemocompatibility. As a result, MSCs are frequently administered intravenously in clinical studies to reduce the incidence of IBMIR and increase engraftment survival. However, studies have examined how various variables (such as culture medium, freeze–thaw cycles, and cell growth) affect MSCs' capacity to cause IBMIR [ ]. However, it is advised to combine anticoagulant factors with MSC transplantation for therapeutic applications [ ].

## Regulatory and ethical considerations
Nanomedicine presents questions about safety, effectiveness, and ethical issues even though it has enormous potential to improve healthcare. Consequently, control of nanomedicine becomes essential for several reasons. "Efficacy and Safety" is the first justification. Nanomedicine is the application of manufactured nanomaterials that, because of their tiny size and unique characteristics, can interact with biological systems in novel ways. Both positive and perhaps negative impacts on the human body might result from these interactions. Before being authorized for clinical use, nanomaterials must pass stringent testing to determine their safety and effectiveness, made possible by regulation [ – ]. The second reason is "Risk Assessment." Nanomaterials may behave differently in the body due to differences in their characteristics from their bulk counterparts. Evaluating the possible hazards of nanomedicine exposure, including toxicity, long-term consequences, and environmental impact, is crucial. Regulation helps to safeguard the environment and public health by providing standards and procedures for risk assessment [ , ]. The third reason is “Ethical Considerations.” Nanomedicine involves ethical problems about privacy, informed consent, equity of access, and potential unintended effects. Regulatory frameworks can effectively mitigate these issues by establishing guidelines for ethical practices, safeguarding patient rights, and fostering transparency and accountability within research and development [ , , ]. The fourth rationale pertains to "International Harmonization." Nanomedicine represents a global domain with significant cross-border ramifications. Establishing harmonized regulations and standards promotes international collaboration, facilitates the exchange of knowledge, and ensures consistent oversight of nanomedicine products across various jurisdictions. This approach aims to optimize the advantages of nanomedicine while mitigating potential risks and discrepancies [ , ].
The regulatory bodies are appropriate to exercise caution since, in the past, medical imaging NPs that received market clearance were later withdrawn when unexpected patient effects occurred after injection [ ]. The European Medicines Agency (EMA) rejected a recommendation for marketing authorization. It removed Sinerem®, an ultra-small superparamagnetic iron oxide (USPIO) contrast agent for magnetic resonance imaging, off the market in 2008 because of issues found in clinical studies. The issues raised pertained to significant adverse effects, notably myalgia in the lower back region, as well as, more alarmingly, allergic reactions that culminated in a fatality. Consequently, it was determined that the risks linked to this specific nanomolecule substantially exceeded any prospective advantages, leading to the denial of its marketing authorization [ ].
Stem cell research is inherently associated with ethical dilemmas that are prevalent globally. On one hand, the pursuit of practical and novel therapies for treating severe medical conditions renders this research essential. Conversely, ethical concerns arise regarding the utilization of embryos, which necessitates carefully considering the boundaries and stipulations governing such research. It's critical to comprehend how gametes, embryos, or somatic cells donated by donors will be used and how donor privacy will be protected. As a result, employing them presents concerns about personal information [ ]. Concurrently, there is a discussion over the safety and effectiveness of ongoing therapies and the patient's comprehension of the source of the chemicals employed in them. There are two primary concerns with stem cells extracted from the umbilical cord: (a) when donor agreement should be sought for the use of the ensuing medical data, and (b) the associated concerns with cold storage and maintenance in certain banks. There are concerns about the donor's discomfort and safety throughout the cell isolation procedure regarding hBMSCs [ , ]. The subjects' full information consent for the entire experimental procedure is then questioned. The comprehensive evaluation of the intellectual property rights governing stem cell research about medical treatments and ethics is one of the institutional problems that is important. Actually, because of this, the International Society for Stem Cell Research (ISSCR) has developed the recommendations throughout time [ , ]. Reviewing the goals of the information and consent procedure for cell and tissue donors is crucial, as is gathering data after that. Lastly, there are a few unique issues with collecting other somatic cells (such as adult skin cells), including the patient's physical danger during the isolation procedure and minimal pain. In reality, the surplus fertilized eggs from in vitro fertilization serve as the initial source of stem cell generation, and the fertilized eggs are destroyed after the pluripotent stem cells are recovered. At this point, two moral dilemmas emerge: (a) it is unacceptable to destroy (or "kill") fertilized eggs that have the potential to become human beings, and (b) it is ethically wrong to utilize these "potential" human beings for any purpose. However, a personalized life does not yet exist in the fertilized egg of the first fourteen days, according to medical and biological research, as it forms fourteen days after fertilization. Therefore, it is not about "killing" by extracting stem cells; instead, it is about the fetus not having a "human person" that can be assigned to it in the initial few days, and it is not subject to obligations and rights to be able to perceive a scenario like that given to the human being [ – ].
The science of medicine views the difference between pluripotent and omnipotent stem cells as being of utmost ethical importance. Pluripotent stem cells may evolve into complete, equal beings like the original embryo after fully developing [ , ]. Therefore, it is conceivable that a reproduction technology will be created in the future to produce individuals with comparable exterior qualities. This is ethically wrong since the creation of human beings would interfere with their autonomy and the free development of their personalities. Therefore, since the gamete donors' fertilized eggs are a part of their personalities, their written authorization is required to collect stem cells from these eggs. This agreement must state that the fetus will not be utilized for the reproduction of other parties and that rejection will not hurt future medical care requirements [ ].
The application of bacteria in cancer treatment presents complex regulatory and ethical challenges. While certain bacterial strains demonstrate the potential to target and eradicate tumor cells specifically, they also pose risks of infection and adverse immunological reactions. Regulatory frameworks must ensure that the chosen bacterial strains are genetically stable, non-pathogenic, and devoid of the risk of horizontal gene transfer. Furthermore, there is a need for consensus regarding acceptable risk thresholds. Ethical considerations must navigate the consent process, weighing the uncertainties associated with the long-term risks of bacterial therapies and potential immunological responses against the benefits of providing patients with treatment options when conventional therapies have been exhausted [ , , – ].

## Conclusion
In conclusion, cancer is the consequence of defects in the control of the cell cycle, allowing the cells to divide in an uncontrolled manner and an invasive capacity for forming metastasis. These defects have been further developed or brought forward due to a series of environmental changes typical for the development of a multicultural society and a product of the consumer society in general. These factors include external ones such as substances in the atmosphere, inhalation of tobacco, the composition of food, radiation, infectious agents, etc., or they are internal, like genetic frame and immunology. Most treatment strategies developed to date have limitations and significant side effects resulting in tumor elimination failure or insufficient eradication of metastasis. The developments above provide possible cancer therapies using an NP, MSC, or bacterium. Progressively, these combined methods with therapeutic agents not only show great potential in their development but also help to significantly reduce the consequences of toxicity.
Looking forward, understanding the basic mechanisms of action of some of these emerging therapies is critical. Investigating the synergistic effects of combining MSCs, NPs, and bacteria could lead to more targeted and effective treatment strategies. Personalized medicine based on defined genetics, nutrients, microbiome, environment, and other predictors that influence cancer has the potential for establishing effective, productive, and safe strategies that are highly targeted. Importantly, patients will have successful outcomes with fundamentally improved therapeutic efficacies and minimal side effects. Lastly, defining these unique approaches and strategies has the potential to contribute to the evolution and continuous advancements in the field of cancer management.
