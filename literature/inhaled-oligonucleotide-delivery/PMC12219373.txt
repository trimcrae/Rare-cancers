## Summary

## Background
Cellular senescence contributes to ageing and age-related diseases. While miR-106b-5p is elevated in centenarians and GH-deficient models of healthy ageing, its role in senescence was unclear.

## Methods
Senolytic effects of miR-106b-5p were evaluated in etoposide-induced senescent IMR90 fibroblasts and HUVECs, and in male naturally aged mice using liposome-mediated delivery. Cellular assays, qPCR, Western blotting, and RNA-seq were performed to assess senescence and SASP markers, apoptosis pathways, and molecular mechanisms.

## Findings
miR-106b-5p selectively eliminated senescent cells without affecting non-senescent cells. It enhanced p53 K120 acetylation and upregulated PUMA, while reducing PCAF expression. In male aged mice, systemic delivery of miR-106b-5p reduced markers of senescence and SASP in multiple tissues and lowered serum IL-6 levels.

## Interpretation
miR-106b-5p functions as a senolytic miRNA via modulation of the p53-PUMA axis and SASP suppression. It holds promise as a therapeutic agent to mitigate age-related cellular dysfunction and inflammation.

## Research in Context

## Evidence before this study
Cellular senescence is a hallmark of ageing and contributes to the development of many chronic diseases. Therapeutic approaches targeting senescent cells, such as senolytics and senomorphics, have shown potential in improving healthspan. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are emerging regulators of cellular ageing, yet the therapeutic potential of individual miRNAs remains underexplored. miR-106b-5p has been reported to be elevated in long-lived Ames dwarf mice, individuals with isolated growth hormone deficiency (IGHD), and centenarians, but its specific role in senescence regulation and inflammation in ageing had not been fully elucidated.

## Added value of this study
This study identifies miR-106b-5p as a senolytic miRNA that selectively induces apoptosis in senescent human fibroblasts and endothelial cells, sparing non-senescent counterparts. Mechanistically, miR-106b-5p enhances p53 acetylation at lysine 120 and upregulates PUMA, while suppressing PCAF—a cofactor that promotes senescence over apoptosis. Systemic delivery of miR-106b-5p in aged mice reduced senescence and SASP markers in multiple tissues, demonstrating both cellular and organismal efficacy.

## Implications of all the available evidence
Our findings establish miR-106b-5p as a promising senolytic candidate with therapeutic potential to alleviate age-related inflammation and tissue dysfunction. The selective action of miR-106b-5p on senescent cells supports the feasibility of miRNA-based senotherapies, providing a new class of tools to target cellular senescence in ageing and chronic disease contexts.

## Introduction
Ageing is the major risk factor for developing numerous chronic diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes, neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer's disease and cancer. , The ageing process is mediated by alterations in specific activities for functions, termed hallmarks of ageing, that influence lifespan and healthspan. , Of these hallmarks, cellular senescence as a driver of ageing has received considerable attention since it is a potentially druggable hallmark to prevent or treat multiple ageing co-morbidities and extend healthspan. , , Senescence is a cell fate that typically includes irreversible loss of proliferative potential, resistance to cell death and increased metabolic activity. , Persistent mitogenic signals, telomere shortening, DNA damage, high glucose, increased reactive oxygen species or protein aggregation activate the p53/p21CIP1 and/or p16INK4a/Rb tumour suppressor pathways to initiate senescence. Senescent cells (SnCs) are often metabolically very active with a robust secretome termed the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). SASP factors include proinflammatory interleukins, chemokines, growth factors, proteases, receptors, enzymes that modify the extracellular matrix, stem cell/progenitor toxins, haemostatic factors, reactive metabolites, bioactive lipids, microRNAs (miRNAs), non-coding nucleotides, and extracellular vesicles (EVs). , Even a relatively low number of chronic SnCs with an inflammatory SASP is sufficient to cause sterile inflammation, loss of stem cell function and tissue homoeostasis and reduced resilience.
Current therapeutic strategies targeting SnCs consist of two types of compounds: senomorphics and senolytics. , , , Senomorphic compounds modify the senescent cell phenotype, usually through suppression of the SASP, without inducing cell death. In contrast, senolytics selectively trigger apoptosis in SnCs, eliminating their adverse effects on tissue functionality and regenerative capacity. Recent studies have highlighted the potential of these therapies in extending healthspan and mitigating age-related diseases. For example, a senolytic cocktail comprising dasatinib plus quercetin (D + Q) effectively eliminated senescent cells, , leading to improvements in many age-associated diseases and disorders in mice , , , , and has been tested in several human clinical trials such as Alzheimer's disease, , idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, and chronic kidney disease. , In contrast, compounds such as rapamycin, metformin, , and NF-κB inhibitors function as senomorphics.
MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are endogenous, small non-coding RNA molecules typically 21–25 nucleotides in length that function as post-transcriptional regulators of gene expression by binding to specific messenger RNA (mRNA) targets to either inhibit translation or promote mRNA degradation. miRNAs regulate various cellular, physiological, and developmental processes including cellular senescence. miRNAs also regulate age-related processes and pathologies across various tissues, including the neural, cardiac, and skeletal systems. , , , , For example, miR-155 is downregulated during vascular ageing, and restoration of miR-155 rescues the phenotype related to vascular ageing. , In addition, the miR-34 family has been implicated in the modulation of cell cycle arrest, telomere length maintenance and in driving senescence. Furthermore, miR-146a also has been shown to accelerate cellular senescence in different tissues. 
To identify miRNAs that could impact ageing, we previously performed sequencing of small RNAs extracted from serum of young and old long-lived Ames dwarf mice and in humans with congenital GH deficiency (IGHD). We observed that although miR-106b and miR-361-3p were down-regulated with age in human serum, they were up-regulated in Ames Dwarf mice and in IGHD subjects. Similarly, analysis of expression of miRNAs in B cells from centenarians identified a set of miRNAs including miR-106b-5p that were elevated in centenarians compared to non-centenarian controls. Given that miR-106b-5p was the one miRNA elevated in Ames Dwarf mice, IGHD subjects and in centenarians, we examined the function of this specific miRNA. Here we demonstrate that miR-106b-5p has senolytic activity when tested on senescent human IMR90 fibroblasts through modulation of the activity of p53 and enhancing the expression of PUMA. Additionally, we demonstrate that miR-106b-5p has senolytic activity in naturally aged mice, reducing expression of senescence and SASP markers in multiple tissues after liposome-mediated delivery. These results suggest that miR-106b-5p has therapeutic potential to reduce senescence and inflammation with ageing and age-related diseases.

## Methods

## Cell culture and transfection
Human lung fibroblast cells (IMR90, RRID: CVCL_0347) were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA). Cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum, 1% Sodium Pyruvate (Gibco), and 1% MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids (Gibco). All cells were split before the establishment of confluence and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. To induce senescence, IMR90 cells were treated with 20 μM Etoposide (Sigma) for 48 h. Etoposide was removed and replaced with fresh media after the two-day treatment. On day six after etoposide removal, approximately 60% of IMR90 cells exhibited characteristics of senescence, as evidenced by positive SA-β-Gal staining.
Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVECs, RRID: CVCL_2959) were obtained from Lonza and cultured in Endothelial Cell Growth Medium (ScienCell Research Laboratories) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 1% endothelial cell growth supplement (ScienCell Research Laboratories). The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. Senescent HUVECs was induced by serial passaging at weekly intervals with a seeding density of 5 × 103 cells/cm2. The cells were experimentally transfected with miR-106-5p mimic at late passages 14–16.
IMR-90 and HUVEC cell lines were obtained from ATCC and Lonza, respectively. Both cell lines were authenticated by the providers using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and were tested negative for mycoplasma contamination prior to use.
To determine the senolytic activity of miR-106b-5p, senescent IMR90 cells and HUVECS were transfected with mirVana® (Ambion) miR-106-5p mimic. To investigate the role of PUMA on the effects of miR-106b-5p, senescent IMR90 cells were transfected with ON-TARGETplus Human PUMA siRNA (Dharmacon) at the doses indicated. The transfection was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer's protocol.

## Animals
Wild-type male C57Bl/6 mice, 95 weeks old, were purchased from NIA and housed under pathogen-free and controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C) and light conditions (12-h light/12-h dark cycle). To verify the senolytic effect of miR-106b-5p, animals were randomly assigned to either the control group (n = 6) or the miR-106b-5p mimic treated group (n = 6). miRNA negative control and miR-106b-5p mimic were prepared using the in vivo-jetPEI® transfection reagent (Ployplus Transfection, Illkirch, France) at an N/P ratio of 7 (microRNA mimic to anionic phosphate), following the manufacturer's protocol and our previous study, half an hour before each injection. The mice received three intraperitoneal injections (I.P.) of miRNA mimics at a dose of 8 mg/kg, with a 2-day interval between each injection. Two days after the third injection, the mice were euthanised via CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation as a secondary method, and their tissues were collected and promptly frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent gene expression and histological analyses.

## Ethics
All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Minnesota (Protocol #2108-39356A). All experiments were conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and institutional ethical guidelines for animal research.

## C12FDG analysis of senescence
For fluorescence analysis of the percentage of SA-β-Gal positive senescent or non-senescent IMR90 cells and HUVECs, 5 × 103 cells/well respectively were seeded in black 96-well plate and treated after 24 h with miR-106-5p mimic. After treatment, cells were first rinsed with PBS and then incubated with Bafilomycin (100 nM) for 1 h and subsequently with C12FDG (100 μM) for 2 h. After C12FDG staining procedure, their nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (2 μg/ml) for 15 min. For quantitative analysis of cell number (Hoechst staining) and the number of C12FDG positive senescent cells, images were acquired at Cytation 1 image reader (BioTek). Thresholds for SA-β-Gal positive staining were determined based on the integrated fluorescence intensity of C12FDG in each cell type. All samples were analysed in triplicate, with 9 fields captured per well, covering most of the well's surface.

## SA-β-Gal staining in animal tissues
Frozen liver, from mice treated with miR-106b-5p mimic or control, were cut at a thickness of 5 μm, followed by air-drying on slide and fixing in 10% formalin. Following fixation, the slides were incubated in SA-β-Gal staining solution containing 1 mg/ml 5-bromo-4-chloro-3-indolyl-beta-d-galactopyranoside (X-gal), citric acid/sodium phosphate buffer, 5 mM potassium ferrocyanide, 150 mM NaCl, and 2 mM MgCl2. The incubation took place at 37 °C in a CO2-free incubator for 16–18 h. After staining, the slides were washed with PBS and mounted with ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant with DAPI (Invitrogen) and allowed to air-dry for 30 min. For quantitative analysis of cell number (DAPI), slides were scanned at Cytation 1 image reader (BioTek). To obtain the full slide image, slides were scanned at the Panotiq Digital Slide Imaging System. To overcome the difficulty of distinguishing blue colour belonging to one or several cells in tissue samples, the full slide blue pixel (Adobe Photoshop) was counted and then divided by the total number of cells to obtain the relative percentage of SA-β-Gal positive cells.

## Immunofluorescence analysis
Frozen liver slides were fixed with 4% formaldehyde in PBS for 10 min at room temperature. Following fixation, slides were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 15 min at room temperature. After blocking, cells were incubated with the primary antibody γ-H2AX (1:200, Cell Signalling Technology) at 4 °C overnight, followed by three times PBS washing and incubation with the secondary antibody Alexa Fluor® 488 (1:200, Invitrogen) for 2 h at room temperature. Finally, the slides were scanned to identify and count the γ-H2AX positive foci using the Cytation 1 Image Reader (BioTek). Cells with three or more γ-H2AX foci are considered positive for γ-H2AX staining.

## Western blots and antibodies
Protein samples at a concentration of 25∼50 μg were separated into 10% gradient SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked in Intercept® blocking buffer (Li-COR) for 1 h at room temperature. After blocking, membranes were probed overnight with rabbit anti-p53 (acetyl K120) (1:500, Abcam), anti-PUMA (1:1000, Cell Signalling Technology), anti-BAX (1:1000, Cell Signalling Technology), anti-p53 (ser15) (1:500, Cell Signalling Technology), anti-p53 (1:500, Cell Signalling Technology), and anti-β-actin (1:2000, Cell Signalling Technology) was used as a housekeeping. After three washes with TBS-T, the blots were incubated with the appropriate fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Following incubation, the membranes were washed with TBS-T and were detected by iBright1500 (Thermo Fisher). All antibodies used in this study were commercially available and validated by the manufacturers for their indicated applications. Specificity was confirmed by single-band detection in Western blot.

## Real-time PCR
Total RNA and miRNA were isolated using miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) to enrich miRNA quantity. The concentration and purity of RNA were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher). Template complementary DNA was synthesised from total RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems) or TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcription (Applied Biosystems) according to the manufacturer's protocols. Real-time PCR was performed using Powerup SYBR Green Master Kit (Applied Biosystems) for mRNA or TaqMan Fast Advanced master mix (Applied Biosystems) for miRNA. miRNA expression was normalised to that of U6 snRNA and mRNA expression was normalised to GAPDH and 18S ribosomal RNA. All retro-transcriptase (RT) reactions, including no-template controls and RT minus controls, were run in a QuantStudio 5 PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Each sample was tested in quadruplicate and data were analysed according to the △△Ct method, normalising the Ct values of the target gene to a reference gene and comparing the normalised values between different samples or conditions.

## Cell viability and caspase 3/7 assay
Senescence or non-senescent IMR90 cells were seeded in 96-well plate (5000 cells/well) and treated after 24 h with miRNA-106-5p mimic. Cell viability and Caspase 3/7 activity were determined using ATPase assay (Abcam) and Caspase-Glo 3/7 Assay Systems (Promega), respectively.

## IL-6 ELISA assays for conditioned media and serum
Conditioned media from treated and untreated cells were collected and concentrated using Ultra-15 centrifugal filters (Amicon). The concentrated samples were diluted in sample diluent NS buffer. Serum was obtained from clotted blood from treated and untreated mice and diluted as 1:2 by sample diluent NS buffer. IL-6 level was measured using a commercially available ELISA kit (R&D) following the manufacturer's protocol.

## RNA library preparation and sequencing
Total RNA was isolated with miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen), and the quality of RNA was checked using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher). Samples were quantified by the University of Minnesota Genomics Center (UMGC) using fluorimetry (RiboGreen assay) and RNA was assessed using capillary electrophoresis (Agilent 4200 TapeStation). Total RNA samples were converted to Illumina sequencing libraries using Illumina's TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Cat. # RS-122-2001) or Stranded mRNA Sample Preparation Kit (Cat. # RS-122-2101) and further sequenced on Novaseq S4 with 20 million reads per sample by UMGC.

## RNA sequencing data analysis
Quality control on raw sequence data for each sample was performed with FastQC. Read mapping was performed via Hisat2 (v2.1.0) using the human genome (Homo sapiens GRCH38) as reference. Gene quantification was done via Feature Counts for raw read counts. Differentially expressed genes were identified using the edgeR (negative binomial) feature in CLCGWB (Qiagen, Redwood City, CA) using raw read counts. The list of Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) was generated based on a minimum 2× Absolute Fold Change and FDR corrected p < 0.05.
To further analyse these DEGs, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed with default settings (1000 permutations for gene sets). The gene set database employed was c2.cp.kegg_legacy.v 2023.2.Hs.symbols. We consider pathways to be significantly enriched when absolute normalised enrichment score (NES) > 1, nominal p-value < 0.05, and False Discovery Rate (FDR) q-value < 0.25 following the GSEA User Guide. To visualise the results, a bubble chart was created using GraphPad Prism, which included the enriched pathways that met the above conditions.
DEGs were also analysed by comparison with the SenMayo senescence gene set (125 genes) and the KEGG apoptosis gene set (166 genes). Heatmaps were generated based on these selected genes to highlight the expression changes in senescence- and apoptosis-related pathways.

## Statistical analysis
Results are expressed as mean ± Standard Error of Mean (SEM) of three separate replicate experiments unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10. Differences between two groups were evaluated for statistical significance using Student's t-test. ANOVA was used to compare statistical differences among multiple groups. p-values < 0.05 were regarded as significant.

## Results

## miR-106b-5p selectively targets and eliminates senescent cells
Our previous study analysing miRNAs in plasma from long lived Ames Dwarf mice and IGHD subjects identified miR-106b-5p as remaining high as compared to normal ageing in mice and human. In addition, when profiling miRNAs in B-cells from Ashkenazi Jewish centenarians and 70-year-old controls without a family history of longevity, we demonstrated that miR-106-5p also remained high in centenarians in contrast to non-centenarian controls. Based on the fact that miR-106b-5p is differentially regulated in longer lived mice and human, we examined the potential function(s) of this specific miRNA. Given that we already have identified sets of miRNAs able to either drive or prevent cellular senescence, some of which are overexpressed in centenarians as compared to older individuals with family history of longevity, we initially evaluated the effect of miR-106b-5p on etoposide-induced senescent IMR90 cells. Non-senescent, pre-senescent (day 2 post-etoposide treatment), and senescent IMR90s (day 8 post-etoposide treatment) were transfected with varying doses of a miR-106b-5p mimic. To evaluate the efficiency of miR-106b-5p mimic transfection, we measured the level of intracellular miR-106b-5p post-transfection using qRT-PCR ( ). miR-106b-5p mimic at 50 nM significantly reduced the percentage of senescent cells at 96 h and over 16 days without impacting the viability of the non-senescent and pre-senescent IMR90 cell population ( a, ). This effect was dose-dependent with higher concentrations of miR-106b-5p leading to a greater elimination of senescent cells, but not non-senescent IMR90 cells ( b, ). In addition to a reduction in the percent of SA-β-gal positive cells, the miR-106b-5p mimic downregulated expression of the key senescence marker p21CIP1, but not p16INK4a, as well as reduced expression of the inflammatory SASP factors IL-1β, IL-6, CXCL1, CXCL8, and Pai-1 ( c, ). To further assess the cell type specificity of miR-106b-5p, we repeated the experiments in senescent human vascular endothelial cells (HUVECs) with similar results ( ). These results suggest that miR-106b-5p preferentially targets p21CIP1+ senescent cells, consistent with a study demonstrating that p21CIP1 is a direct target of the miRNA. 
 

## miR-106b-5p induces apoptosis in senescent IMR90 cells
To determine if miR-106b-5p was inducing cell death through apoptosis of senescent IMR90 cells, a caspase 3/7 assay was used to measure the extent of apoptosis. Transfection of senescent IMR90s cells with the miR-106b-5p mimic increased apoptotic activity, particularly at higher concentrations ( a). In addition, an ATPase-based assay was used to confirm a dose-dependent decrease in cells viability in senescent cells ( b). To understand the molecular mechanisms underlying this apoptosis, we examined the mRNA levels of several apoptosis-related genes by qPCR. Treatment with miR-106b-5p mimic increased the pro-apoptotic PUMA (p53 upregulated modulator of apoptosis), a Bcl-2 homology 3 (BH3)-only Bcl-2 family member important for p53-dependent and -independent apoptosis ( c). Interestingly, we also observed an increase in anti-apoptotic genes Bcl-2, BCL-xL, and MCL-1 ( c, ), and downregulation of the pro-apoptotic gene FASTK, which is reported to be a direct target of miR-106b-5p. Western blot analysis confirmed the elevated levels of PUMA, but not BAX and APAF1 in miR-106b-5p treated senescent cells ( d and e). In non-senescent IMR90 cells, qPCR analysis showed that miR-106b-5p only induced downregulation of p21CIP1 but not PUMA ( ). These results demonstrate the ability of miR-106b-5p to modulate the apoptotic pathway in senescent cells, potentially through the upregulation of PUMA.
 

## miR-106b-5p promotes senescent cell apoptosis via PUMA upregulation and p53 pathway activation
To confirm the role of PUMA in conferring the apoptotic effect of miR-106b-5p in senescent cells, we utilised siRNA-mediated knockdown of PUMA with miR-106b-5p. Reduction in PUMA also reduced miR-106b-5p-mediated apoptosis, further implicating PUMA as a mediator of the senolytic activity of miR-106b-5p ( a, ). Interestingly, the knockdown of PUMA did not lead to any significant changes in the levels of expression of p21CIP1, IL-1β, and IL-6, suggesting that while PUMA is essential for the senolytic activity of miR-106b-5p, the senomorphic effects of miR-106b-5p are independent of PUMA-regulated apoptosis ( b and c, ). This result also suggests that miR-106b-5p is able to down-regulate expression of p21CIP1, IL-1β, and IL-6 independent of apoptosis. We also observed that miR-106b-5p transfection increased acetylation of p53 at lysine 120 (K120), a key modulator of cell fate decisions between apoptosis and cell cycle arrest, , known to enhance the binding affinity of p53 to pro-apoptotic gene promoters such as PUMA, thereby promoting apoptosis, while not affecting the level of total p53 and p53 ser15 phosphorylation ( d and e). Interesting, in non-senescent IMR90 cells, Western blot analysis showed that miR-106b-5p decreased p53 acetylation (p53 k120) in a time-dependent manner ( ). These results suggest a complex regulatory mechanism where miR-106b-5p modulates the p53 pathway, amplifying its pro-apoptotic effects in senescent cells, while reducing p21CIP1 expression.
 

## Identification of pathways affected by miR-106b-5p
To further examine the pathways regulated by miR-106b-5p, RNAseq was performed at different time points on senescent IMR90 cells either transfected with or without miR-106b-5p. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of RNA-seq data post-treatment identified significant enrichment in pathways related to senescence, apoptosis, and cellular stress responses, demonstrating the impact of miR-106b-5p on multiple cellular processes ( a). Most of the enriched downregulated pathways are related to SASP pathways. Interestingly, the apoptosis pathway becomes apparent at 48 h following miR-106b-5p treatment but disappears after 96 h. This suggests that the senolytics effect of miR-106b-5p is initiated on the gene expression level at 48 h and reaches completion by 96 h. In non-senescent cells, apoptotic pathways were not enriched, and minimal pathway enrichment was observed at 48 h, indicating specificity of miR-106b-5p for senescent cells ( ). Instead, its effects were primarily focused on suppressing inflammation-related pathways, consistent with miR106b-5p having senomorphic activity. Heatmap analysis further highlighted this specificity, demonstrating significant enrichment of apoptosis-related genes in senescent cells treated with miR-106b-5p, whereas such enrichment was absent in non-senescent cells ( ). The heatmap based on the SenMayo senescence/SASP gene set revealed significant differential expressions in genes involved in metalloprotease activity, cytokine and chemokine signalling, and growth factor interactions, further illuminating the broad spectrum of miR-106b-5p on senescent cells ( ). Moreover, qPCR analysis revealed significant changes in genes like BNIP2, SQSTM1, and PCAF, which are direct target genes of miR-106b-5p ( b). BNIP2 is implicated in apoptosis regulation, while SQSTM1 (p62) plays a critical role in autophagy, a key process in cellular ageing. , PCAF (P300/CBP-Associated Factor) is known to play a significant role in ageing, particularly through its effects on the p53 signalling pathway. , Furthermore, we observed a pronounced reduction in IL-6 levels, a component of the SASP, in the supernatant of senescent IMR90 cells after 96-h transfection ( c). Together, these results further confirm the senolytic activity of miR-106b-5p.
 

## Senolytic activity of miR-106b-5p in aged mice
qPCR analysis of miR-106b-5p expression levels in the liver of young and old mice demonstrated that expression of miR-106b-5p significantly decreased with ageing ( g). To evaluate the potential senolytic activity of miR-106b-5p in vivo, the miRNA was delivered to 2-year-old natural aged C57/BL6 mice using the in vivo-jetPEI transfection reagent. After three intraperitoneal (I.P.) injections of the miR-106b-5p mimic or a miRNA negative control at two-day intervals, the mice were euthanised, and tissues were harvested for analysis. We confirmed transfection of the miRNA-106b-5p mimic by qPCR analysis of the liver of the treated mice ( ). There was a significant decrease in SA-β-gal positive cells in the liver in the miR-106b-5p treated mice ( a and b) compared to the control. Similarly, the percent of cells staining positive for γ-H2AX staining, a marker for DNA damage and senescence, also was reduced ( c and d). Additionally, there was a trend towards reduced IL-6 levels in the serum ( e). Supporting these histological findings, Western blot and qPCR analyses confirmed significant changes in key senescence-related proteins and mRNA levels in the treated mice. Western blot results demonstrated a notable increase in PUMA protein levels in the liver, while total p53 and p53 K120 acetylation remained unchanged ( h and i). Consistently, qPCR analysis showed significant upregulation of Puma mRNA, with no significant changes in p300 and Tip60, further highlighting PUMA as a key mediator of the senotherapeutic activity of miR-106b-5p ( ). Moreover, a lower expression of senescence-related genes such as p16Ink4a, IL-1β, Mcp-1, and CXCL1 along with a trend of reduced p21CIP1, IL-6, Pai-1, and Tgf-β expression was observed in the liver of treated mice ( f, ). Several of these key senescence genes and SASP markers also show a significant decrease in the kidney, spleen, brain, and lung of treated mice ( ). RNA-seq analysis confirmed the effect of miR-106b-5p on downregulating the SenMayo panel ( j). In addition, miR-106b-5p also affected expression of genes involved cell cycle regulation and, in particular, suppressed inflammatory signalling pathways across multiple tissues ( ). Taking together, these results suggest that miR-106b-5p is a senolytic miRNA that could be used to reduce senescence and inflammation with ageing and age-related diseases in multiple tissues.
 

## Discussion
Given that miRNAs have been implicated in the regulation of many, if not all, cellular processes including ageing, we have been examining the role(s) of miRNAs in senescence and inflammation. Analysis of miRNAs in extracellular vesicles from functional adult stem cells identified a cocktail of miRNAs, miR-181a-5p, miR-92a-3p, miR-21-5p, and miR-186-5p, with senomorphic activity in cell culture and in aged mice. Similarly, we previously demonstrated that miR-449a reduces GH-associated senescence in cell culture studies whereas miR146a-5p has pro-senescence activity. Screening for miRNAs that either go up or down with senescence using a miRNA biosensor lentiviral library identified a class of miRNAs able to drive senescence in the absence of stress, some of which were identified previously as tumour suppressor miRNAs. Based on our recent identification of senescence-regulating miRNAs, we were interested to determine if miRNAs that are positively associated with successful ageing in the serum of long lived Ames Dwarf mice and in humans with GH deficiency and in B cells from centenarians, had any effect on senescence and its inflammatory SASP. The only miRNA that was found to remain high in these three murine and human groups was miR-106b-5p. 
Analysis of miR-106b-5p on etoposide-induced senescent IMR90s showed a strong senolytic activity by a dose-dependent reduction in the number of C12FDG positive cells with evidence of apoptosis and loss of senescent cell markers including SASP factors. Importantly, there was no effect on non-senescent cell viability even at high doses of miR-106b-5p, demonstrating the senolytic specificity of the miRNA. At least in senescent IMR90 cells, the effect seems to be specific for p21CIP1, but not p16INK4a positive senescent cells, based on the qPCR analysis. This is consistent with a previous study demonstrating that miR-106b-5p targets p21CIP1 directly. However, given that we observe a significant decrease in p16INK4a expression in mice treated with miR-106b-5p, this may not be true for all types of senescent cells.
Examination of the mechanism of senolysis conferred by miR-106b-5p suggested that the activation of the p53 pathway and, in particular, an increase in the pro-apoptosis gene PUMA were important for apoptosis. Indeed, siRNA knockdown of PUMA reduced the senolytic activity of miR-106-5p. It is important to note that although the inhibition of PUMA blocks the senolytic action of miR-106b-5p, the expression of p21CIP1 and SASP genes such as IL-1ß and IL-6 remained comparatively low following treatment with miR-106b-5p. This suggests that miR-106b-5p also possesses senomorphic properties in senescent cells that don't undergo senolysis.
Moreover, our data suggest that miR-106b-5p enhances acetylation of p53 at lysine 120 (K120), a modification known to promote transcription of pro-apoptotic genes such as PUMA, without altering total p53 levels. In parallel, miR-106b-5p suppresses the expression of PCAF, which mediates p53 acetylation at K320—an event associated with senescence rather than apoptosis. These findings indicate that miR-106b-5p may shift the p53 response from cell cycle arrest toward apoptosis, thereby enhancing its senolytic activity.
Although miR-106b-5p is high in serum and in B cells from healthy older mice and individuals including centenarians, previous studies on miR-106b have been focused primarily on its overexpression in multiple tumour types including breast, prostate, lung cancer, cervical, and colorectal cancers as well as hepatocellular carcinoma and oesophageal squamous cell carcinomas, where it has been reported to have effects on cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and/or metastases. Here potential targets for miR106b-5p include PTEN/AKT, Deleted in liver cancer-1 (DLC-1) and RE-1 Silencing Transcription Factor (REST-1), VEGFA and BTG anti-proliferation factor 3 (BTG3). However, it is likely that any pro-tumour role for miR-106b-5p is in cells that already have escaped senescence through mutation of key genes such as p53 or Rb.
Our study has several strengths. First, we applied both in vitro and in vivo models to validate the senolytic and senomorphic activities of miR-106b-5p, using primary senescent cells and naturally aged mice. Second, mechanistic insights were provided by dissecting the p53–PUMA signalling axis and its epigenetic regulation, supported by siRNA, Western blotting, and transcriptomic analyses. Third, we demonstrated the organ-specific transcriptional effects of miR-106b-5p through both qPCR and RNA-seq data, corroborated across multiple tissues.
Nonetheless, our study also has limitations. The in vivo experiments were performed only in male mice, and thus potential sex-based biological differences in miRNA responses remain unexplored. Furthermore, while we demonstrated molecular changes and senescence marker reduction, long-term outcomes—such as lifespan extension or age-related functional improvement—were not evaluated.
Importantly, miR-106b-5p was able to reduce markers of senescence and SASP in naturally aged mice after short term repeated liposome-mediated delivery compared to a miRNA control. miRNA-106b-5p conferred a significant reduction in senescence markers across various organs, including the liver, kidney, and spleen and partially in the brain and lung. This systemic reduction in senescence was complemented by a trend toward decreased serum IL-6 levels, suggesting potential of miR-106b-5p to reduce systemic inflammation associated with the accumulation of SnCs. Importantly, these findings are consistent with the fact that the level of miR-106b-5p naturally declines with ageing in multiple tissues. Thus miR-106b-5p represents a potent senotherapeutic to reduce the burden of SnCs and chronic inflammation.
