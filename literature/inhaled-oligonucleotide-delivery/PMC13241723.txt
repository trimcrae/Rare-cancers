## Summary
Precise genome engineering in human pluripotent stem cells remains inefficient, limiting endogenous fluorescent tagging needed to study phase-separated membraneless nuclear compartments. Here, we present a protocol for generating precise knockin alleles of essential genes in human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) via CRISPR-Cas9 editing. We describe steps for combining transient p53 inhibition, optimized transfection conditions, and fluorescence-activated cell sorting. This protocol enables recovery of viable edited clones that would otherwise be lost due to editing-induced stress or essential gene perturbation.
For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Li et al.

## Before you begin
Efficient CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing in human pluripotent stem cells depends critically on careful guide RNA (gRNA) and donor template design. In this protocol, gRNA sequences are cloned into Cas9 and gRNA co-expression plasmids to enable transient but robust Cas9 activity during the editing window.
Donor plasmids are constructed using NEBuilder HiFi Assembly or an equivalent Gibson-style cloning strategy. For degron-based protein degradation systems, the E3 ligase auxin receptor F-box protein (AtAFB2) is commonly introduced by viral transduction or safe harbor locus knockin. However, viral methods can cause variable expression and promoter silencing, leading to cellular heterogeneity. We therefore attempted knockin at the safe harbor Adeno-associated virus integration site 1 (AAVS1) locus, but this did not yield robust expression in our system. To obtain stable expression, we integrated AtAFB2 downstream of the endogenous SUPT16H gene using an Internal Ribosome Entry Site (IRES), enabling simultaneous SUPT16H tagging and AtAFB2 expression in a single knockin step ( ).
 gRNA target sites are selected such that the Cas9 cleavage site lies within 50 bp of the intended insertion site, with optimal efficiency typically observed when the cut site is within approximately ±25 bp. Based on repeated empirical observations, knockin efficiency is highest for insertions smaller than 3 kb, although larger insertions can still be recovered using fluorescence-activated cell sorting-based enrichment.
For studies involving phase-separated condensates or live-cell imaging, careful selection of fluorescent tags is critical. GFP variants are bright and suitable for low-abundance proteins, whereas commonly used red fluorescent proteins, such as mCherry, are dimmer and often limit resolution. For single-molecule or ligand-based imaging, self-labeling tags such as HaloTag may be preferable. Tag choice should also consider endogenous expression levels and spectral separation when labeling multiple proteins.
When incorporating degron-based perturbations, the choice of degradation system should be carefully matched to protein abundance and experimental requirements. The auxin-inducible degron (AID) system provides efficient and reversible protein depletion and is particularly well suited for essential genes, as degradation can be titrated by adjusting auxin concentration. , However, for highly abundant proteins, AID-mediated depletion may be incomplete or slow. In such cases, alternative systems such as dTAG (degradation tag, an FKBP12_F36V-based degron) can achieve more rapid and robust degradation. These advantages must be balanced against potential limitations, including higher cost, prolonged effects after ligand removal, and reliance on endogenous E3 ligases, which may perturb native ubiquitin-proteasome pathways.
An additional key consideration in degron design is the choice of N- or C-terminal tagging. Tag placement can significantly influence protein stability, localization, and function (For example, , ), and should therefore be evaluated in the context of the protein’s structure and biological role. Careful assessment of tagging strategy is essential to ensure effective degradation while preserving native protein behavior prior to induction.

## Innovation
Mechanistic studies of transcription and chromatin regulation have often relied on mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs), as precise genome engineering remains inefficient in human pluripotent stem cells due to low homology-directed repair (HDR) efficiency and poor survival after single-cell dissociation. As a result, endogenous fluorescent tagging of transcription factors in native human chromatin, which is required to study phase-separated nuclear condensates, has been difficult to achieve.
Targeting essential genes such as SUPT16H or Nucleosome Destabilizing Factor (NDF, also called Glyr1) further exacerbates these challenges, as the combined stress of Cas9-induced double-strand breaks and single-cell isolation frequently triggers p53-dependent apoptosis before edited clones can be recovered. , , , , This protocol mitigates these barriers through three key modifications. First, transient suppression of the p53 response is achieved by co-transfection of the dominant-negative p53 construct pCE-mp53DD, reducing apoptosis during the critical editing window without permanently altering checkpoint control. Second, DNA repair outcomes are biased toward homology-directed repair through the use of the non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) inhibitor SCR7, thereby increasing the likelihood of precise knockin events. Finally, survival during single-cell isolation is supported by the use of CloneR2 or RevitaCell Supplement, which is essential for maintaining viability during FACS-based clonal selection. Together, these features enable reliable recovery of precise knockin clones at essential loci in human iPSCs.

## Maintain human iPSCs under optimal culture conditions

Timing: 2–3 days

Before initiating genome editing experiments, it is important to establish appropriate culture conditions for human iPSCs. Cells should be maintained under feeder-free conditions using a defined medium such as Essential 8 (E8 or E8 Plus; Thermo Fisher Scientific) or an equivalent formulation (e.g., TeSR; STEMCELL Technologies). , Alternative approaches, including homemade E8 formulations supplemented with defined growth factors, may also be used if properly optimized. , 
Culture conditions should be selected to support stable pluripotency, minimize spontaneous differentiation, and ensure high cell viability, particularly prior to transfection. Maintaining cells at appropriate density and avoiding excessive mechanical stress are critical, as both overconfluency and harsh dissociation can compromise cell health and reduce editing efficiency.
For genome editing applications, cells should be in a robust proliferative state with consistent morphology and minimal differentiation. Optimization of culture conditions, including medium choice, coating substrate, and passaging strategy, may be necessary depending on the cell line and experimental design.
Detailed step-by-step procedures for iPSC culture and maintenance are provided in the following section.

## Genomic DNA extraction
Column-based genomic DNA extraction using DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit.
This section describes two alternative approaches for genomic DNA extraction from human iPSCs. Column-based purification yields high-quality genomic DNA suitable for sequencing, cloning, and detection of low-frequency genome editing events, whereas the HotSHOT method provides a rapid and low-cost preparation compatible with PCR-based genotyping of clonal cell lines. However, due to its lower sensitivity, the HotSHOT method is not suitable for detecting low-frequency editing events in bulk cell populations after transfection.

## Thawing and freezing cells
These steps describe thawing human iPSC lines to generate healthy colonies suitable for downstream applications such as cell transfection, as well as freezing iPSC lines for long-term storage. The procedures are described for one well of a 6-well plate. See for other plate formats.

## Thawing of iPSC cell line

Timing: 1 h (hands-on time: 30 min)

53.Warm 4 mL of E8 medium at room temperature for approximately 30 min. Alternatively, E8 medium may be warmed in a 37 °C water bath for up to 5 min immediately before use.

CRITICAL: Certain growth factors in E8 medium, including Fibroblast Growth Factor 2 (FGF2), are temperature sensitive and lose activity upon prolonged exposure to 37 °C. Therefore, warming time should be minimized, and only the required volume of medium should be warmed immediately before use. Any unused warmed medium should be discarded.

54.Supplement the warmed E8 medium with ROCK inhibitor to a final concentration of 10 μM by adding 4 μL of 10 mM stock solution.

Note: Inclusion of ROCK inhibitor is critical to reduce actin-myosin contraction during cell plating, thereby decreasing anoikis-associated cell death and promoting cell attachment. After cells have attached to the plate and established stable cell-cell contacts, typically within 24 h after plating, ROCK inhibitor can be removed from the culture medium.

55.Remove a cryovial containing frozen iPSCs from liquid nitrogen and immediately immerse the vial in a 37 °C water bath without submerging the cap. Gently swirl the vial until the cell suspension is completely thawed.

Note: Submerging the cryotube cap in the water bath will increase the likelihood of contamination. And keeping the cryotube in the water bath too long will reduce cell viability.

56.Disinfect the exterior of the cryovial with 70% ethanol, then transfer it to a biosafety cabinet. Using a sterile pipette, gently transfer the thawed cell suspension into a 15 mL conical tube containing 2 mL of warmed E8 medium.
57.Centrifuge the cells at 200 × g for 2 min at room temperature. Aspirate the supernatant carefully. At the same time, aspirate the coating solution from a pre-prepared Matrigel-coated well of a 6-well plate.
58.Gently resuspend the cell pellet in 2 mL of E8 medium supplemented with 10 μM ROCK inhibitor. Slowly dispense the cell suspension into the Matrigel-coated well.
59.Return the plate to a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Gently move the plate in short back-and-forth and side-to-side motions to evenly distribute the cells across the well surface.

## Freezing of iPSCs

Timing: 1 h

These steps describe freezing iPSCs for long-term storage in liquid nitrogen.
60.Prepare freezing medium consisting of 90% KnockOut Serum Replacement (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 10% DMSO. Approximately 500 μL of freezing medium is needed per cryovial. Freezing medium may be stored at −20 °C for several months.
Note: In many protocols, DMSO is added to the cell suspension immediately before freezing, a step that can generate localized heat and requires careful handling. In this protocol, DMSO is pre-mixed with the storage medium to a final concentration of 10%, which simplifies handling and improves reproducibility. Under these conditions, no adverse effects on post-thaw cell viability or pluripotency were observed. Pre-mixing DMSO also reduces variability associated with manual addition immediately before freezing.
61.Ensure that a Mr. Frosty freezing container is equilibrated to room temperature before use.
Note: Using a cold container alters the cooling rate and reduces cell viability.
62.Warm Versene to 37 °C in a water bath before use.
Note: Versene may also be used at room temperature; however, cell dissociation may require a longer incubation time under these conditions.
63.Aspirate culture medium from the well to be frozen and wash once with 2 mL of pre-warmed Versene. Aspirate the wash and add a second 2 mL of pre-warmed Versene. Incubate the plate at room temperature for approximately 4 min, or until cells begin to detach.
Note: If cells were maintained in medium containing ROCK inhibitor before freezing, detachment may require longer incubation. Monitor cell detachment under a microscope to ensure efficient dissociation. Versene incubation time may require optimization depending on the cell line. If cells detach rapidly, reduce the incubation time in future passages. If detachment is inefficient, extend incubation slightly.
64.Aspirate the Versene.
65.Gently resuspend the cells in 1 mL of freezing medium by pipetting up and down three to four times until a homogeneous suspension is obtained.
Note: Excessive pipetting can reduce cell viability. Limit pipetting to the minimum number required to resuspend the cells.
Note: Enzymatic dissociation using reagents such as TrypLE is possible; however, this requires additional steps, including neutralization with E8 medium and centrifugation at 200 × g for 2 min to remove the enzyme before resuspension in freezing medium. Because these extra manipulations can reduce viability, enzymatic dissociation is generally not recommended for routine freezing of iPSCs.
66.Adjust the cell suspension to a final volume of 500 μL freezing medium per cryovial. We recommend freezing at least 2 × 105 cells per cryovial. Aliquot immediately into cryovials, cap securely, and place the vials into the Mr. Frosty container.
Note: Do not overtighten the container lid.
67.Transfer the freezing container to a −80 °C freezer for 8-24 h to allow controlled cooling. On the following day, move cryovials to liquid nitrogen for long-term storage.
Note: Prolonged storage at −80 °C is not recommended.

## Maintenance of iPSCs
These steps describe daily maintenance of human iPSCs and passaging using Versene. iPSCs are typically passaged at approximately 60-70% confluency every 2–3 days, with split ratios commonly around 1:6 unless otherwise noted. The GM25256 iPSC line has an approximate doubling time of ∼18 h during exponential growth in E8 medium; therefore, split ratios may be adjusted depending on experimental timing and downstream applications.
Note: Because key growth factors in E8 medium are temperature sensitive, the culture medium should be replaced daily. For extended culture intervals, such as over weekends, E8 Flex medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) may be used due to its increased growth factor stability, allowing maintenance without daily medium changes for up to 48 h. When using E8 Flex, cell density should be carefully controlled to maintain optimal cell health.
Note: Ensure iPSCs are in an optimal state before genome editing. Cells should display uniform, compact colony morphology with minimal spontaneous differentiation and be at appropriate confluency, as overgrown or stressed cultures can significantly reduce editing efficiency.

Timing: 40 min (hands-on time: 10 min)

68.Warm E8 medium at room temperature for approximately 30 min.
69.Aspirate spent medium from each well of a 6-well plate and replace with 2 mL of fresh warmed E8 medium. Repeat medium replacement daily until cells are ready for passaging.

## Passaging iPSCs using Versene

Timing: 40 min (hands-on time: 20 min)

70.Prepare a Matrigel-coated 6-well plate in advance (see above).
71.Warm E8 medium at room temperature for approximately 30 min.
72.Transfer 3 mL of E8 medium to a 15 mL conical tube and supplement with Y-27632 ROCK inhibitor to a final concentration of 10 μM by adding 3 μL of 10 mM stock solution.
73.Loosen cells from the culture plate using Versene by following steps 62-63.
74.Aspirate the Versene and gently resuspend the cells with 1 mL of E8 medium supplemented with ROCK inhibitor.
75.Transfer 200 μL of the cell suspension into the conical tube containing the remaining E8 medium supplemented with ROCK inhibitor and gently mix to achieve the desired split ratio.
76.Aspirate the coating solution from a fresh Matrigel-coated 6-well plate.
77.Seed 2 mL of the diluted cell suspension into one well of the Matrigel-coated 6-well plate.
78.Return the plate to a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Gently move the plate in short back-and-forth and side-to-side motions to evenly distribute cells across the well surface.
79.On the day following seeding, assess cell attachment and morphology by microscopy and replace the medium with fresh pre-warmed E8 medium without ROCK inhibitor.

Note: The optimal split ratio may vary depending on cell line and growth rate. When using Versene, split ratios between 1:5 and 1:10 are commonly appropriate. Adjust the split ratio based on colony morphology and density. If colonies appear crowded, increase the split ratio; if sparse, decrease it. Under optimal conditions, iPSCs should be passaged every 2–3 days.

Note: For cell lines that exhibit poor survival or increased sensitivity during passaging, supplements such as RevitaCell (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or CloneR2 (STEMCELL Technologies) may be used in place of Y-27632. These formulations provide more selective ROCK inhibition and include additional components, described by the manufacturers as antioxidants or free-radical scavengers, as well as pro-survival factors, which may improve cell viability during passaging of difficult-to-culture iPSC lines.

## Fluorescence detection

Timing: 1 h

When a fluorescent tag is knocked into an endogenous protein-coding gene, fluorescence microscopy serves as an efficient primary screening tool to rapidly identify positive clones. However, the feasibility and sensitivity of visual screening depend on several factors. First, plate material and optical properties strongly influence signal detection. Although 96-well plates are convenient for clone expansion, they are suboptimal for detecting weak endogenous fluorescent signals. The small well diameter creates a pronounced liquid meniscus that introduces optical distortion and light reflection, and standard polystyrene plastic exhibits relatively high autofluorescence. Second, the effectiveness of visual screening is directly determined by the endogenous expression level of the target gene. For example, proteins with nuclear concentrations on the order of ∼0.5 μM, such as NDF, produce a distinct but very faint GFP signal. For proteins expressed at low levels, clones could be transferred to glass-bottom imaging dishes and, if necessary, fixed and imaged using a high-sensitivity widefield or confocal microscope to reliably confirm successful knockin events.

## Expected outcomes
This protocol enables recovery of viable human iPSC clones carrying precise knockin alleles at essential transcription factor loci. By combining transient p53 inhibition using p53DD and suppression of non-homologous end joining with the SCR7 inhibitor, approximately 0.1–1% of parental cells can be successfully edited and sort out by fluorescence-activated single-cell sorting ( ).
Recovered clones maintain characteristic iPSC morphology, normal growth behavior, and expression of pluripotency markers after expansion. Endogenously fluorescently tagged proteins are expressed at physiological levels and display subcellular localization patterns consistent with known biology, with signal intensity varying according to endogenous protein abundance and fluorophore choice (for example, ). For degron-tagged alleles, ligand addition induces robust and reversible protein depletion within 1-4 h, with depletion kinetics depending on protein abundance and the degradation system used. In Western blot analysis, this is reflected by a progressive decrease in target protein levels with increasing ligand concentration or time (for example, Fig. 6e of the ref. ).

## Limitations
Insertion size strongly influences editing efficiency; insert larger than ∼3 kb significantly reduces the frequency of edited cells as assessed by FACS. Targeting essential genes can compromise cell viability, further decreasing overall editing efficiency.
Knockin efficiency in human iPSCs remains substantially lower than in mouse embryonic stem cells. For point-mutation knockins, hundreds of Cas9-mCherry-positive cells typically must be isolated and expanded to identify correctly edited clones, which requires substantial hands-on time and effort.
When the E3 ligase is expressed as a fusion with an endogenous gene, its expression level depends on that of the host gene. As a result, the efficiency and kinetics of degron-mediated protein degradation may vary between targets.

## Troubleshooting

## Problem 1
Extensive cell death after transfection or nucleofection (Steps 92-123).

## Potential solution
High mortality typically results from poor DNA quality, excessive nucleic acid dosage, or inadequate post-transfection survival support. Human iPSCs are uniquely sensitive to impurities and the physical stress of membrane disruption. To mitigate toxicity, ensure all plasmids are prepared using endotoxin-free kits with an A260/A280 ≈ 1.8–2.0 and A260/A230 > 2.0. High concentrations of DNA or transfection reagents often drive apoptosis. Therefore, titrating the total DNA amount by 25%–50% while maintaining component ratios can significantly improve viability. Ensure iPSCs are healthy, undifferentiated, and seeded at 60–70% confluency before transfection. Avoid over-dissociation and include ROCK inhibitor, CloneR2, or RevitaCell during the post-transfection recovery period.

## Problem 2
Low frequency of edited or fluorescent cells detected by FACS (Step 146).

## Potential solution
Low editing efficiency is typically driven by poor gRNA design, sub-optimal donor template architecture, or inherent biological constraints such as low target gene expression and protein intolerance to terminal tagging. Select gRNAs with high predicted on-target activity and position the Cas9 cut site within ±25 bp of the intended insertion site. Ensure donor templates include sufficiently long homology arms and incorporate PAM- or gRNA-disrupting mutations to prevent re-cutting. Reduce insertion size if feasible. Seed cells at the recommended density (60–70% confluency) and ensure the cells are evenly distributed prior to transfection to optimize DNA uptake and editing efficiency. Prior to FACS, validate editing via microscopy or PCR. However, note that low-abundance proteins may not yield detectable fluorescence at this stage. If the signal remains undetectable, reconsider the tagging terminus (N- vs. C-terminal) to avoid disrupting protein stability or folding. While antibiotic selection can enrich for edited populations, iPSCs are highly sensitive to selection pressure. Thus, fluorescent-based enrichment is preferred. For low-expression genes, ensure imaging equipment is optimized for high signal-to-noise ratios to distinguish true positives from autofluorescent background.

## Problem 3
Poor survival or few colonies formation after single-cell sorting (Steps 147-151).

## Potential solution
Low clonal recovery is usually caused by physical damage during cell preparation, the high pressure of the sorting machine, or poor growth conditions after sorting. Human iPSCs are large and fragile; additionally, cells that are still stressed from the initial transfection are often unable to survive the added pressure of being dissociated and sorted into single wells.
To improve survival, minimize the time cells spend in dissociation reagents and perform FACS immediately after preparation. Add DNase I to the buffer to prevent clumping and handle the cells gently by pipetting slowly. Avoid using tip-mounted strainers, as these create high pressure that can kill fragile cells. We highly recommend passaging the cells at least once after transfection but before sorting. This “recovery passage” allows the most stressed cells to be cleared out naturally, ensuring you only sort the healthiest, most robust cells. During sorting, use a large nozzle (e.g., 100 μm) to reduce shear stress. While specialized recovery media is expensive, we found that using 100 μL per well in a 96-well plate increases colony formation by 20% compared to using 50 μL. Under these conditions, you should expect to recover 20–30 colonies per 96-well plate. For the best recovery, ensure plates are freshly and evenly coated with Geltrex. Supplement the recovery media with RevitaCell or CloneR2 during and after the sorting process. It is important to continue including these supplements in the media to support the developing colonies.

## Problem 4
Incomplete or slow degron-mediated protein depletion (Step 199).

## Potential solution
Confirm that the degron tag is correctly integrated. For example, sequencing the junction to rule out frame-shifts that might reduce AID function. Confirm AFB2/E3 ligase expression by immunoblotting or RT-PCR if possible. If AFB2 expression is low, the host gene context might not produce enough ligase; consider alternate strategies such as a stronger promoter or using a different degron system, such as dTAG, if appropriate. Extending the auxin treatment time or increasing the auxin concentration within non-toxic ranges may increase the rate of degradation. Note that degradation kinetics may vary between targets.
