## Introduction
Melanin, a byproduct of tyrosinase-catalyzed oxidation of L-tyrosine, is physiologically packaged into melanosomes and transferred to keratinocytes via melanocyte dendrites to maintain epidermal homeostasis. However, the equilibrium can be disrupted by endogenous hormonal fluctuations during pregnancy [ , ] or exogenous stressors such as ultraviolet radiation [ ], which upregulate tyrosinase activity and drive excessive melanin synthesis. While epidermal turnover typically clears surplus pigment, age-related metabolic decline, barrier dysfunction, and impaired antioxidant capacity hinder the process and result in aberrant melanin deposition in epidermis and dermis, which is stated as the most defining feature of melasma [ , ]. Clinically, melasma affects more than 30% of Asian women of reproductive age manifesting as symmetric hyperpigmented macules and inflammatory response, and accompanied by histopathological evidence of melanin accumulation and elongated melanocyte dendrites [ , ]. Chronic ultraviolet (UV) exposure activates TLR3-mediated inflammation in keratinocytes, which in turn releases pro-inflammatory mediators (e.g., TNFα, α-MSH) that enhance melanogenesis [ , ]. Simultaneously, dermal mast cell degranulation triggers VEGF-driven increased vascularity and infiltration of inflammatory cells, thereby perpetuating a cycle with “inflammation, angiogenesis, and pigmentation" [ , ].
Traditional phototherapy modalities target melanosomes through photo-thermolysis. However, many articles reported that using photo-thermolysis for the clinical melasma treatment raises the risk of complications such as epidermal barrier disruption and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, which can worsen the condition [ , ] especially in the Asian population. In contrast, low-fluence Q-switched Nd: YAG (LFQSNY) 1064 nm laser has been proven a promising alternative treatment with non/sub-thermal energy, and the near-infrared wavelength enhances dermal penetration [ , ]. This might induce photo-biomodulation (low-level laser therapy, LLLT) through a non-thermal mechanism, modulating cellular function without collateral damage. Emerging evidence suggests that LLLT can modulate inflammation and immune responses [ , ]. For example, 810 nm laser irradiation has been shown to suppress NF-κB activation in dendritic cells, while 650 nm and 808 nm lasers enhance macrophage phagocytosis through cytochrome c oxidase (CCO)-mediated reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [ , ]. However, despite numerous studies demonstrating effective clinical results by LFQSNY laser treatment, the precise mechanism, particularly the role of LFQSNY laser in regulating melanocyte melanogenesis, anti-inflammatory effect, and phagocytic activity of macrophage in the melasma, remains unclear and hasn’t been reported. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the mechanisms of LFQSNY laser treatment on melanogenesis and anti-inflammation in cells and mice, furthermore, to elucidate its therapeutic potential for disrupting the pathogenic cycle in melasma.

## Results

## Effects of LFQSNY 1064 nm laser irradiation on the temperature, cytoskeleton, and viability of melanocytes in a dose-dependent manner
To determine whether the effect of LFQSNY laser on melasma goes through the photo-biomodulation instead of the photo-thermolysis, the temperature changes on the surface of B16F10 cells under the laser irradiation were evaluated by infrared thermography. There was no significant temperature increase observed in the irradiation by LFQSNY laser across the total energy densities of 0.5 to 2.0 J/cm² with 2-pass. However, the irradiation at 2.0 J/cm² with 4-pass induced a significant temperature rise (p < 0.01). Notably, even the irradiation at the lowest energy density, 0.5 J/cm², with 8-pass caused a temperature increase (p < 0.01). The results indicate that LFQSNY with 0.5 to 2.0 J/cm² with 2-pass could lead to a non-thermal effect rather than a thermal effect. The higher fluence setting of Q-switched Nd: YAG potentially caused thermolysis when heating was pronounced with cumulative irradiation (≥ 4-pass at 2.0 J/cm² or ≥ 6-pass at 1.0 J/cm², Fig.  a-d). To appraise the cellular function, the morphology and viability of B16F10 cells were detected by rhodamine-phalloidin staining and CCK-8 assay. The cytoskeletal morphology retained a normal spindle in cells post-irradiation at irradiation of 1.0–2.0 J/cm² with 2-pass. However, the same doses laser irradiation with 8-pass that increased total fluence induced marked cytoskeletal disruption, including enlarged cell contours, shortened/thickened pseudopodia, and widened intercellular gaps, particularly up to 2.0 J/cm², suggesting high-fluence laser can cause thermal damage to actin microfilament organization (Fig.  e). CCK-8 assays showed that cell viability remained unaffected after irradiations across the densities from 0.5 to 2.0 J/cm² with 2-pass and 4-pass. However, a significant reduction of viability was observed after irradiations with 6-pass and 8-pass, even at the lowest energy density (0.5 J/cm², p < 0.01). This progressive inhibition was consistent with cumulative thermal cytotoxicity (Fig.  f- g). Based on those results, we selected LFQSNY with 1.0 J/cm², 2-pass as the standard treatment for the following mechanical studies, which would avoid photo-thermolysis.

 

## Effects of LFQSNY 1064 nm laser irradiation on melanogenesis of melanocyte at varying energy densities
To understand the effect of LFQSNY laser on melanogenesis, melanin content and tyrosinase activity of B16F10 cells were assessed. Cells were subjected to repeated low-fluence irradiation (each session with 1.0 J/cm², 2-pass scans, 12-hour intervals) up to 4 sessions (4T) by simulating clinical protocols for treating melasma that typically involve low-energy irradiation with multiple sessions. The melanin levels and tyrosinase activity in the treatment groups with 4 T were significantly reduced compared to those in the controls (p < 0.05, Fig. a-b). Meanwhile, the melanogenesis-related gene (TYR) and tyrosinase-related protein 1 and 2 (TYRP1, TYRP2) genes were significantly suppressed by treatment (Fig. c). Western blot (WB) analysis revealed no signs of apoptotic activation at 1.0 J/cm² with 4T. In contrast, a higher fluence density (2.0 J/cm²) induced cell apoptosis as evidenced by a reduced Bcl-2/Bax ratio and elevated Cleaved-caspase-3 levels (Fig.  d). The findings demonstrate that low-fluence irradiation (1.0 J/cm², 2-pass, 4 T) effectively suppresses melanin synthesis through a non-thermal mechanism, including tyrosinase inhibition and transcriptional repression of pigment-related genes, rather than triggering apoptosis. The higher fluence densities (≥ 2.0 J/cm²) reduce melanin via cytotoxic pathways that rely on cell apoptosis. On the contrary, highlighting the low-fluence irradiation will benefit patients in clinical applications.

 

## LFQSNY 1064 nm laser Irradiation increases the gene expression and protein level of DHX9 while the suppression of melanin in vitro
RNA sequencing of B16F10 cells subjected to LFQSNY 1064 nm laser irradiation (1.0 J/cm², 2-pass, 4 T with 12-hour intervals) identified a total of 145 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 73 upregulated and 72 downregulated genes (Fig. a-b). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses revealed significant enrichments in pathways related to energy metabolism, oxidative stress, mitochondrial autophagy, and glycine/serine/threonine metabolism (Fig. c-d). Our data suggests that laser irradiation modulates melanogenesis-related metabolic networks through alterations in those key biological processes. Based on the RNA-seq data and literature implicating genes involved in pigmentation, we validated mRNA expression in B16F10 cells subjected to LFQSNY laser irradiation. qRT-PCR confirmed a significant upregulation of DHX9 mRNA following irradiation, which was consistent with the RNA-seq results (Fig. e). DHX9, an ATP-dependent RNA helicase (also known as MLE), belongs to the DExD/H-box subfamily of SF2 superfamily helicases. DHX9 interacts with key regulatory factors to modulate gene expression, reinforcing its function as a critical regulator of cellular equilibrium [ ]. Dysregulation of DHX9 has been associated with various human pathologies, emphasizing its physiological importance. Meanwhile, the protein level of DHX9 was elevated by WB analysis (Fig. f). Functional validation using siRNA-mediated knockdown (sequences: siRNA-1: 5’-GUGGUUCAUUGUAGUUUAA-3’; siRNA-2: 5’- GGUUCGAAUAAAUGAAAUA-3’) demonstrated efficient inhibition of DHX9 expression (Fig. g-h). To investigate the role of DHX9 in melanogenesis, we employed a DHX9-knockdown B16F10 cell model and evaluated cellular melanin content. The results demonstrated a statistically significant increase in melanin production following DHX9 silencing (Fig. i), whereas laser irradiation partially reversed this effect (Fig. j), suggesting DHX9 plays a critical role in regulating melanin synthesis.

 

## DHX9 modulates melanogenesis via interacting with TRIB3 during LFQSNY 1064 nm laser irradiation in vitro and in vivo
Our RNA-seq analysis revealed that the downregulated TRIB3 expression, in contrast to the DHX9 upregulation, was observable in LFQSNY-irradiated cells. To dissect whether DHX9 interacts with TRIB3 during LFQSNY laser treatment, the DHX9-silenced B16F10 cells were also evaluated after irradiation. The results showed that LFQSNY laser irradiation increased protein of DHX9 while decreasing TRIB3 protein levels, and DHX9 silencing could reverse TRIB3 gene suppression, which was partially rescued by subsequent irradiation. Similar trends were observable in protein levels and mRNA expressions, which confirmed the inhibitory effect of LFQSNY laser on melanogenesis by DHX9 interaction with TRIB3 (Fig. a-b). Furthermore, dual-luciferase reporter assays were employed to validate DHX9’s transcriptional regulation of TRIB3. HEK293T cells were co-transfected with DHX9 overexpression plasmids or empty-vector as controls. TRIB3 wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) luciferase reporters and alongside pRL-TK (internal control) were used. DHX9 plus TRIB3-WT cells exhibited significantly reduced luciferase activity ratios (P < 0.01), whereas DHX9 plus TRIB3-MUT showed no changes in luciferase activity ratios (P > 0.05), post-irradiation at 48 h (Fig. c). The data indicateDHX9 acts as a transcriptional repressor of TRIB3 via promoter binding.

 

To further confirm the results from the cells, C57BL/6J mice were selected to detect the tractable melanocyte distribution in hair follicles post-irradiation of LFQSNY laser (1.0 J/cm², 2-pass, 3 T with 1-week intervals). Histological analysis in mouse skin also showed marked reduction in melanin granules in the hair follicles and shafts post-treatment (Fig.  f-g). WB and qPCR confirmed elevated DHX9 and reduced TRIB3 expression in irradiated skin (1.0 J/cm², 2-pass, 3 T with 1-week intervals) (Fig.  d-e), recapitulating the findings from in vitro. Furthermore, the expression of DHX9 and TRIB3 in skin tissues was detected using IHC (Fig.  h). The results showed that the expression of DHX9 in the DHX9 siRNA group was significantly lower than that in the NC siRNA group, indicating that DHX9 siRNA effectively knocked down the expression of DHX9 in skin tissues. In contrast, the expression of DHX9 in the DHX9 siRNA + LFQSNY group was significantly higher than that in the DHX9 siRNA group, suggesting that LFQSNY treatment could upregulate the expression of DHX9. Additionally, the expression of TRIB3 in the DHX9 siRNA group was higher than that in the NC siRNA group, while the expression of TRIB3 in the DHX9 siRNA + LFQSNY group was lower than that in the DHX9 siRNA group. We further detected the inflammatory expression levels and found that the TNF-α and NLRP3 in the DHX9 siRNA group were higher than those in the NC siRNA group (Fig.  i). Meanwhile, the expression levels of TNF-α and NLRP3 in the DHX9 siRNA + LFQSNY group were lower than those in the DHX9 siRNA group. Collectively, these results establish the interaction of DHX9 with TRIB3 as a pivotal mediator of LFQSNY laser-induced melanogenesis suppression.

## Effects of LFQSNY laser irradiation on inflammatory mediator production and phagocytic function in macrophages
To model persistent inflammatory conditions in melasma, RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with 100 ng/µL of TNF-α and IFN-γ for 48 h. The significant increases in the mRNA expressions and protein levels of pro-inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) in macrophages were detected by qRT-PCR and ELISA (Fig. a-b). Augmented TNF-α and NLRP3 protein levels were confirmed by WB (Fig. c), validating the establishment of the inflammatory model. Laser safety was assessed using CCK-8 assays, the results demonstrated no cytotoxicity across the energy densities of treatment (0.5–2.0 J/cm², 2-pass; Fig.  d). For therapeutic efficacy, 1.0 J/cm² was selected as the optimal dose for intervention, the effectively reduced TNF-α and NLRP3 protein levels were seen in M1-macrophages (Fig. e). LFQSNY laser irradiation (1.0 J/cm², 2-pass) applied to M1-macrophages significantly suppressed the mRNA expressions of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 (Fig. f). ELISA further confirmed a reduction in IL-1β and IL-6 protein secretion (Fig.  g), demonstrating the effects of anti-inflammation by LFQSNY laser. To further understand those in vivo, the content of M2 and M1 macrophages in skin tissues was examined using IH. Compared to the NC siRNA group, the number of M2 macrophages in the DHX9 siRNA group was reduced. In contrast, the number of M2 macrophages in the DHX9 siRNA + LFQSNY group was significantly higher than that in the DHX9 siRNA group (Fig. j). Interestingly, compared to the NC siRNA group, the number of M1 macrophages in the DHX9 siRNA group was increased. In contrast, the number of M1 macrophages in the DHX9 siRNA + LFQSNY group was significantly lower than that in the DHX9 siRNA group (Fig. k).

 

Macrophages contribute to skin homeostasis since their capacity to phagocytose melanin. Phagocytosis using fluorescent microspheres was used to evaluate the phagocytic ability of RAW264.7 cells with their orange-fluorescent polystyrene microsphere. The results of the phagocytosis assay showed that the laser treatment significantly enhanced particle uptake in an energy-dependent manner (Fig.  h). To confirm the data from phagocytosis, a neutral red assay further was used and showed an increased dye internalization in cells at 1.0 J/cm² of laser treatment (Fig.  i). The results suggest that LFQSNY laser promotes macrophage-mediated melanin clearance through activation of phagocytosis, which will benefit to clinical potential in melasma treatment.

## Discussion
We used an integrated approach of cellular and mouse models to elucidate the mechanisms underlying LFQSNY 1064 nm laser treatment for melasma and showed that (1) low-fluence irradiation effectively suppresses melanogenesis, including tyrosinase inhibition and transcriptional repression of pigment-related genes, through upregulating DHX9 and repressing TRIB3 expression via non-thermal biomodulation; (2) LFQSNY laser significantly reduced inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α and NLRP3 and enhanced the phagocytic activity in macrophages. Our data demonstrated that LFQSNY laser corresponds to macrophages that reduce inflammation and have ingested melanosomes for improving pigment clearance in melasma. At clinically optimized parameters (1.0 J/cm², 2P, 4 T), LFQSNY laser irradiation achieves pigment reduction without inducing cytotoxicity or apoptosis, distinguishing it from high-fluence protocols (≥ 2.0 J/cm²) that rely on photothermal melanocyte ablation. This fluence-dependent biphasic response underscores the importance of parameter precision in balancing efficacy and safety.
 DHX9, interacting with TRIB3, plays a critical role in regulating the suppression of melanogenesis during LFQSNY laser treatment as a pivotal regulator. DHX9, a multifunctional helicase, was increased and transcriptionally repressed TRIB3 in B16F10 cells post-irradiation. TRIB3, a member of the tribbles family of serine/threonine protein kinase-like genes and functioning as a pseudokinase and nuclear gene, promotes apoptosis and inhibits mitosis [ ]. TRIB3 regulates keratinocyte proliferation, potentially linking to psoriasis pathogenesis in skin [ ], suggesting its involvement in photoaged skin, hyperkeratosis, and pigment deposition. As we know that TRIB3 is a stress-responsive gene implicated in ROS generation and NF-κB-mediated inflammation [ – ]. The DHX9-TRIB3 axis attenuates oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling, indirectly disrupting tyrosinase activity and melanogenic gene expression (TYR, TYRP1, TYRP2). This effect aligns with DHX9 in stress granule dynamics and RNA repair [ ], suggesting its potential to stabilize melanocyte homeostasis by LFQSNY laser irradiation. Moreover, we used a murine models which confirmed that marked reduction in melanin granules in the hair follicles and shafts after laser treatment. In this study, we demonstrated that the low-fluence protocol achieved pigment suppression via DHX9-TRIB3 pathway with non-thermal damage. In contrast, high-fluence settings (≥ 2.0 J/cm²), though effective in melanin removal, lead highly risk of inflammation-driven pigmentation possibly caused by thermal damage.
Accumulating evidence suggests that Macrophage polarization is regulated by cytokines, pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), and so on. M1 polarization is induced by IFN-γ, LPS, or TNF-α through STAT1 and NF-κB signaling pathways. M1 macrophages are characterized by high expression of IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12 and NLRP3, which mediate pro-inflammatory responses. In contrast, M2 macrophages exhibit elevated expression of Arg1 and CD206, and primarily function in tissue repair and immunosuppression [ ]. In this study, we observed that LFQSNY irradiation (1.0 J/cm², 2-pass) exerted anti-immunomodulatory effects on macrophages, as evidenced by suppressing TNF-α/IFN-γ-induced pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, NLRP3) and mimicking the effects of low-level laser therapy (LLLT) in rheumatoid arthritis [ ]. In addition, macrophages contribute to skin homeostasis because of their capacity to phagocytose cellular debris, pigment particles, and apoptotic cells. In our data, an enhanced the phagocytic activity of macrophages after LFQSNY laser irradiation was demonstrated by Phagocytosis assays using fluorescent microspheres and neutral red particles. Macrophages can capture some exogenous particulates and ingest self-derived particulates, such as “melanophages” that is an example by the melanin-laden macrophages in the human dermis [ , ]. LFQSNY irradiation likely promotes an M2-like macrophage phenotype, characterized by upregulated efferocytosis receptors (MerTK, CD206) and anti-inflammatory cytokine secretion (TGF-β, IL-10) [ ]. M2 macrophages are critical for dermal melanin clearance via scavenger receptors (CD36, CD163), yet their persistence as melanophages in conditions like post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) [ ]. Clinical studies show that laser efficacy correlates with melanophage recruitment [ ], but their role in pigment recycling warrants further exploration. Macrophage phagocytosis is tightly linked to metabolic shifts as M1 polarization favors glycolysis, while M2 polarization relies on oxidative phosphorylation [ ]. LFQSNY-induced phagocytic enhancement may involve metabolic rewiring, potentially mediated by melanin degradation byproducts or HIF-1α/mTOR pathway activation [ ]. However, the endogenous melanin’s impact on macrophage metabolism still remains unrevealed, highlighting a critical knowledge gap. More future studies on the interactions between melanocytes and macrophages will be conducted.

## Conclusion
In summary, our data revealed that LFQSNY laser therapy mitigates melasma through synergistic mechanisms, which is inhibited melanogenesis through biomodulation with increasing DHX9 and repression TRIB3, thereby reducing the melanogenic drive. Furthermore, LFQSNY laser irradiation reduced inflammatory response by inhibiting production of inflammatory mediators and enhanced the phagocytic activity of macrophages for pigment clearance. These findings suggest that low-fluence Q-switched Nd: YAG 1064 nm laser therapy have clinical relevance with noninvasive photodamage and elucidate therapeutic efficacy for treating melasma.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture and treatment
B16F10 murine melanoma cells (catalog number: CRL-6475) and Raw264.7 murine macrophage cells (catalog number: TIB-71) were obtained from ATCC and were cultured in complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA), supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Sangon Biotech, China) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA). Cells were passaged at 90% confluence using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Servicebio, China) and 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Experimental procedures were performed using cells between passages 3 and 5, with all cultures maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO₂. Q-switched 1064 nm Nd: YAG laser (SPECTRA PRO, Lutronic, Korea) with spot size (8 mm) and frequency (1 Hz). The effects of varying energy densities (0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0 J/cm²) and irradiation passes (2, 4, 6, 8 passes) on temperature changes were systematically analyzed.

## Cytoskeletal architecture analysis
Phalloidin staining was used to assess cytoskeletal alterations induced by laser irradiation under standardized conditions (8 mm spot size, 1 Hz frequency). The study examined the effects of two energy density levels (1.0 vs. 2.0 J/cm²) and two irradiation protocols (2 vs. 8 passes) on actin filament organization and cellular morphological changes.

## Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)
Cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay under fixed laser parameters: spot size (8 mm) and frequency (1 Hz). The effects of varying energy densities (0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0 J/cm²) and irradiation passes (2, 4, 6, 8 passes) on cellular activity.

## Assessment of melanin content and tyrosinase activity
Melanin content was quantified using an NaOH alkaline lysis method. Briefly, cells were lysed with 1 mol/L NaOH containing 10% DMSO, based on cell count, and incubated at 80 °C for 60 min. The absorbance of the lysate was measured at 405 nm using a microplate reader. Tyrosinase activity was analyzed according to the manufacturer’s protocol of a tyrosinase detection kit (Solarbio, China). Samples were processed, and the absorbance (A1) was measured at 475 nm using a microplate reader. The reaction plate was then immediately transferred to a 37 °C incubator for 3 min, and the absorbance was re-measured and recorded as A2. The ΔA value was calculated as ΔA = A2 – A1. ## Transcriptome sequencing (RNA-Seq) and bioinformatics analysis
RNA-Seq and bioinformatics analyses were performed on B16F10 cells treated with LFQSNY irradiation and untreated controls to investigate their regulatory effects on melanogenesis. Comparative transcriptomic profiling of the irradiated and non-irradiated groups was conducted to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and pathways associated with melanin synthesis.

## Dual-luciferase reporter assay
The plasmids employed in this study were commercially sourced from GeneChem (Shanghai, China). For transfection, HEK 293-T cells were co-transfected with the respective plasmids for 6 h, followed by a 48-hour incubation under standard culture conditions. Luciferase activity was quantified using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
 The levels of inflammatory markers (interleukin (IL)−6 and IL-1β) were measured using ELISA (Thermofisher scientific 88–ID="EN1">7064-88and BMS6002-2).

## Assessment of macrophage phagocytic function
Macrophage phagocytosis was evaluated via neutral red uptake and fluorescent microsphere assays. For the neutral red assay, cells were cultured in dishes (37 °C, 5% CO₂), treated per experimental groups, and incubated with 10 µg/ml neutral red for 1 h. Unincorporated dye was removed by PBS washing, followed by lysis in 200 µl buffer (30 min). Absorbance was measured at 540 nm. For the fluorescent microsphere assay, macrophages (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL) were incubated with PBS-resuspended microspheres (Rigor Science, FS01N100O, China) for 24 h (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Post-washing with ice-cold PBS, cells were trypsinized, centrifuged (1,000 ×g, 5 min, 4 °C), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), and analyzed via flow cytometry. Data were processed using FlowJo.

## Animal experimental procedure
Male C57BL/6J mice (8–10 weeks old, 20–25 g, Shanghai Slac biotechnology) were maintained under SPF conditions (12 h light/12 h dark, 22 ± 2 °C) with free access to food and water. The animal study protocol was approved by the Committee on Animal Use of the Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital and met the National Institutes of Health guidelines for the Use and Care of Animals (SH9H-2025-A73-1,2025-03−07). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and all methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).
After induction of anesthesia with 2% isoflurane, each mouse was positioned prone, and a 2 × 2 cm dorsal area was shaved and disinfected with 75% ethanol. Animals were randomly assigned to two groups (n = 6 per group). The experimental group received an intradermal injection of DHX9-siRNA (100 µL), while controls received an equal volume of FAM-labelled negative-control oligonucleotide (FAM-NC).
72 h later, the experimental group was exposed to low-fluence Q-switched Nd: YAG 1064 nm laser (LFQSNY; 1.0 J cm⁻²; two passes) every other day for three sessions. Control mice were handled identically with the laser shutter closed (sham irradiation). 24 h after the final session, animals were humanely euthanized by CO₂ inhalation in a gradual-fill chamber (20% chamber volume min⁻¹) followed immediately by cervical dislocation to ensure death. Dorsal skin was excised: one portion fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (24–48 h) for histology and immunohistochemistry, the remainder snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for RNA, protein and biochemical assays.

## EnVision-based immunohistochemistry (IHC)
The levels of DHX9, TRIB3, TNF-α, and NLRP3 in skin tissues was detected using IHC. Tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and underwent antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked, followed by a brief protein blocking step. Slides were incubated with the primary antibody, then with an HRP-conjugated polymer detection system. Color development was achieved using a chromogen, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted for light microscopy.

## TSA-based multiplex immunofluorescence (IF)
The content of M2 and M1macrophages in skin tissues was examined using IH. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked, and non-specific binding sites were blocked with serum. Multiplex staining was performed using a TSA kit, involving sequential cycles of primary antibody incubation, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, and fluorophore deposition. After each cycle, antibody stripping was performed to allow for additional markers. Nuclei were counterstained, and slides were mounted for multispectral imaging and analysis.

## Statistical analysis
All experiments were repeated a minimum of three times (n ≥ 3). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t-test (for two-group comparisons) or One-way ANOVA (for multi-group comparisons) in GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
