## Background
Hypoxic–ischemic brain damage (HIBD) is a predominant cause of neuronal injury and mortality in newborns. Current preventive and therapeutic interventions demonstrate limited clinical efficacy. Emerging evidence reveals ferroptosis as a critical mechanism within HIBD pathophysiology, positioning it as a promising therapeutic target. Dimethyl malonate (DMM), a competitive inhibitor of succinate dehydrogenase, has demonstrated neuroprotective properties across multiple models of neurological disorders. However, the impact of DMM on the neonatal HIBD has not been studied.

## Aim
To investigate the neuroprotective effects of DMM against neonatal HIBD and elucidate its mechanisms of action.

## Methods
We created a model of HIBD in neonatal male C57BL/6J mice and administered various doses of DMM or vehicle control. Quantitative assessments included cerebral infarct volume measurement, Nissl staining for neurons, neurological behavior, ferrous ion (Fe2+), malondialdehyde (MDA) level, 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) expression, and solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11, system Xc−)/glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) antioxidant axis expression level. Parallel studies in vitro employed oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion-treated HT22 cells to investigate the effects of DMM on ferroptosis and its underlying mechanisms. Moreover, key factors of ferritinophagy, including nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4), SQSTM1/p62, ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), and microtubule-associated protein light 3 II (LC3II) were analyzed by western blotting. Molecular interactions between NCOA4 and FTH1 in brain cortical tissues of DMM-treated HIBD mice were analyzed by coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP). Ferroptosis regulation by DMM was further investigated via Fth1 knockdown in cellular models. Immunofluorescence staining was used to evaluate the capacity of DMM to suppress ferritin degradation and lysosomal Fe2+ accumulation at the organelle level.

## Results
DMM treatment demonstrated its neuroprotective efficacy in HIBD models, as evidenced by a reduction in cerebral infarct volume, an increase in the number of Nissl-positive neurons, and improved cognitive and motor functions in neonatal mice compared with controls. Additionally, the DMM intervention significantly modulated ferroptosis-related biomarkers in brain cortical tissues and HT22 cells, decreasing ferrous ion (Fe2+) accumulation, reducing lipid peroxidation products (MDA and 4-HNE), and enhancing SLC7A11/GPX4 antioxidant system activity. Importantly, DMM specifically regulated core ferritinophagy components: suppressing NCOA4 and LC3II expression while upregulating FTH1 and p62 levels. Co-IP revealed that mechanistically, DMM disrupted the protein interaction between NCOA4 and FTH1, effectively inhibiting ferritinophagy progression. The effects of antiferroptosis were FTH1-dependent, as demonstrated by reversal of the DMM protective effect following Fth1 knockdown in vitro. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that DMM decreased the colocalization of FTH1-lysosome-associated membrane protein 2, and FerroOrange/LysoTracker Green dual staining confirmed its inhibition of lysosomal iron accumulation, collectively indicating regulation of DMM at the organelle level.

## Conclusion
DMM suppressed ferroptosis-induced neuronal death by specifically targeting FTH1 and disrupting the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction, thereby mitigating HIBD. These findings position DMM as a promising therapeutic candidate for the clinical management of neonatal hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy.

## Introduction
Hypoxia–ischemia (HI)-induced neonatal hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), a subset of neonatal encephalopathy, is one of the most common causes of neonatal death, with the incidence in developed countries estimated to be 1.5 % [ ], and as high as 26 % in less developed countries [ ] [ ]. Oxidative stress is a key component of the newborn infant's brain response to HI injury, determining the ultimate severity of the disease. The newborn brain has more free iron than the mature nervous system. This iron exposure results in the generation of ·OH radicals (Fenton reaction), potent free radicals that can cause severe damage. Thus, the newborn brain is more susceptible to the adverse effects of oxidative stress than more mature brains [ ]. Cerebral ischemia induces iron deposition and inappropriate accumulation of ferritin in the ischemic brain [ ]. Studies aimed at reducing oxidative stress by regulating iron levels have shown encouraging results in both mature and immature brains. For example, deferasirox, a trivalent iron chelator, significantly suppressed the accumulation of intracellular ferrous ions (Fe2+) and cell death caused by hemin exposure in models of hemorrhagic stroke. These effects were related to the suppression of reactive oxygen species production, lipid peroxidation overproduction, and autophagy [ ]. In a rat model of chronic cerebral hypoperfusion simulating vascular dementia, iron deposition was observed in the hippocampus of rats in the model group, accompanied by an increase in the number of autophagosomes and upregulation of autophagy marker protein expression in the hippocampus [ ]. In a neonatal rat model of HIE using mitophagy agonists and inhibitors, BNIP3-mediated mitophagy attenuates hypoxic–ischemic brain damage by inhibiting ferroptosis thereby maintaining iron and redox homeostasis [ ]. However, autophagy can either promote or suppress ferroptosis, depending on the context. Some types of autophagy (e.g., ferritinophagy, lipophagy, and clockophagy) initiate or execute ferroptotic cell death by selectively degrading anti-injury proteins or organelles. Conversely, other forms of selective autophagy (e.g., reticulophagy and lysophagy) enhance the cellular defense against ferroptotic damage [ ]. Despite these findings, the clinical translation of the autophagy signaling as a therapeutic strategy for neonatal HIE still faces numerous difficulties.
Mottahedin et al. revealed cerebral succinate accumulation during ischemia and subsequent succinate oxidation upon reperfusion in a mouse model of ischemic stroke/mechanical thrombectomy. Targeting inhibition of succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) during the reperfusion phase significantly attenuated acute cerebral injury [ ]. In ischemia, inhibition of SDH results in the accumulation of succinic acid [ ]. Succinic acid is a proinflammatory circulating citric acid metabolite that accumulates in ischemic tissues. The oxidation of succinic acid after ischemia–reperfusion results in the reversal of the electron transport chain and the production of reactive oxygen species. Dimethyl malonate (DMM) is a competitive inhibitor of succinate dehydrogenase that can penetrate cells and can protect various tissues by reducing succinate levels [ ], including the kidney [ ] [ ], liver [ ], and heart [ ], under ischemia–reperfusion conditions. Infusing DMM intravenously before cardiopulmonary resuscitation can improve neurological performance associated with resuscitation after cardiac arrest in rats [ ]. However, despite the promising results, the timing of DMM administration in previous studies was mainly before and during ischemia to limit succinate accumulation, or immediately at the beginning of reperfusion to inhibit the rapid oxidation of succinate. This narrow treatment time window may limit the clinical translational approach of DMM.
Sangineto et al. explored the mechanism of action of DMM in vivo using a model of Alzheimer disease (AD). They used 6-month-old and 18-month-old male 3 × transgenic (Tg)-AD mice with three mutated human genes (APPSwe, PS1M146V, and tauP301L) and corresponding control non-Tg mice. The 3 × Tg-AD mouse is a mature AD model that exhibits AD-like plaques and tangles associated with synaptic dysfunction. Mice were intraperitoneally injected with DMM (160 mg/kg) or vehicle, Dulbecco's phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), once a day for 3 days, and then euthanized. These investigators demonstrated that inhibiting SDH with DMM can eliminate brain inflammation, promote an anti-inflammatory phenotype, and regulate microglial metabolic changes in 3 × Tg-AD mice [ ]. Their findings suggest that the neuroprotective effect of DMM is achieved through a succinate-independent pathway.
Cellular free iron (particularly the ratio Fe2+/Fe3+) is an important factor that regulates lipid peroxidation in the brain [ ] [ ]. Consistent with this action, the Fe2+ chelator ferene could inhibit methemoglobin- and ferric iron-mediated central nervous system (CNS) lipid oxidation, reflecting the reduction of Fe3+ by some component of the CNS [ ]. Notably, another study using rat brain homogenates in vitro showed that malonate can chelate iron(II) and the malonate–iron complex(es) is(are) active as measured by its(their) activity on deoxyribose degradation assay [ ]. Taking into account observations in vivo and in vitro that maintaining iron and redox homeostasis alleviates hypoxic–ischemic brain damage (HIBD) in neonatal rats [ ] [ ], it is reasonable to assume that the mechanism underlying DMM-induced neuroprotection involves the iron metabolism signaling pathway, which inhibits ferroptosis.
Based on ferroptosis, the regulatory effect of ferritinophagy (autophagic degradation of the major intracellular iron storage protein ferritin) on ferroptosis has become a focus of drug target research. The role of DMM in neonatal HIBD and its impact on iron metabolism and ferroptosis in the damaged cortex has remained poorly defined. The present study aimed to determine whether DMM is a potential therapy for ferroptosis-related brain injury in a murine model of HIBD in vivo, and to investigate the role of ferritinophagy in the inhibition of ferroptosis in vitro using Co-IP and immunofluorescence. We hypothesized that DMM inhibits ferroptosis by regulating ferritinophagy-mediated iron metabolism, thereby protecting against HIBD.

## Methods
2.1

## Ethics statement
All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Soochow University in compliance with national and institutional guidelines for animal care and use. Detailed methodologies for experimental models, both in vitro and in vivo, are systematically elaborated in the following sections.

2.2

## Animals and ischemia–reperfusion model
Ten-day-old male C57BL/6J mice were procured from Zhaoyan (Suzhou) New Drug Research Center Co. (Suzhou, China, license No. SCXK (Su) 2023-0004) and maintained under controlled environmental conditions (12:12 h light–dark cycle) with free access to a standard rodent diet and water. For Nissl staining (n = 6) and behavioral analyses (n = 8), the mice were assigned without any special selection to four experimental groups: Sham, Sham + DMM, HIBD, and HIBD + DMM. The measurement of Fe2+ content (n = 8), malondialdehyde (MDA) assay (n = 8), 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) expression analysis (n = 8), coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) (n = 3), and the Western blot assay (n = 6), which comprised three groups: Sham, HIBD, and HIBD + DMM.
A neonatal mouse model of HIBD was established using a modified Vannucci approach, as previously described [ ]. In brief, anesthesia was induced through spontaneous inhalation of 3 % isoflurane (RWD, China) and maintained at 1.5 %–2 % in air. Surgically prepared mice were immobilized supine, followed by a midline cervical incision to expose the right common carotid artery. The isolated vessel was double-ligated with a 5-0 silk suture and transected between ligation points, with subsequent wound closure. Next, the mice were returned to their mothers for a 1-h recovery period. Subsequently, the mice underwent hypoxic exposure (37 °C, 8 % O2/92 % N2) for 2 h before being returned to dam housing. Sham controls received identical surgical manipulation, excluding vessel ligation and induced hypoxia. The neonatal mice were then euthanized using isoflurane overdose before brain resection at predetermined intervals post-HIBD or cervical dislocation in the case of mice used for behavioral experiments.

2.3

## Drug administration in vivo
Dimethyl malonate (DMM, purity ≥98 %) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The DMM (2.5–20 mg/kg body weight (bw)−1) was dissolved in 50 μL of 1 % (v/v) dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Shanghai, China) and subsequently diluted with sterile saline (Huarena Pharmaceutical Co., Shenzhen, China) to achieve the final concentrations. All experimental animals received either saline vehicle or DMM solution by intraperitoneal injection 15 min before the surgical procedures.

2.4

## Staining with 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride
2,3,5-Triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining was employed to assess cerebral infarction areas following established protocols [ ]. In brief, after 24 h of HIBD modeling, mice in the various groups were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane. The brain tissues were rapidly extracted (<1 min) and frozen at −30 °C for 15 min. The frozen brain tissues were cut into five consecutive coronal slices (2 mm thick per slice). All sections were immersed in 1 % TTC solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) prepared in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Biosharp, Beijing, China), followed by incubation in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min with periodic inversion [ ]. Poststained slices underwent 24-h fixation in 4 % paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at ambient temperature. ImageJ (version 1.53; NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used by observers blinded to the treatments to analyze the staining quantitatively, with nonischemic necrotic areas exhibiting a light red color and ischemic necrotic tissues appearing white [ ]. The infarct volume percentage was calculated as:(sum of ischemic volumes of each slice)/(sum of volumes of each slice) × 100 %.

2.5

## Nissl staining
Nissl staining was performed to assess structural alterations in hippocampal and cortical neurons, as well as the integrity of Nissl bodies, in the neonatal mice. After 24 h of HIBD modeling, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. Brains were resected and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde at ambient room temperature for 24 h. Tissues were subsequently dehydrated through a graded series of increasing ethanol concentrations, cleared with xylene, and subsequently embedded in paraffin. Serial coronal sections (4–5 μm thickness) were mounted on glass slides and dried at 60 ± 5 °C using a precision slide drier (PSJ Medical Instruments, Changzhou, China) [ ]. Before staining, sections underwent two 5-min xylene immersions for dewaxing, followed by rehydration through a graded ethanol series (100 %–75 %). After two washes in deionized water, sections were stained with Nissl solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Stained sections were rapidly dehydrated in absolute ethanol, permeabilized with xylene, and sealed with neutral resin. Histological images were acquired using a light microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The Nissl body refers to the large granular body of rough endoplasmic reticulum with rosettes of free ribosomes [ ]. Nissl-positive neurons refer to cells with dark blue Nissl bodies, clear nuclei, and normal cell morphology after Nissl staining. Histopathological damage of the cortex and hippocampus was assessed by counting the number of Nissl-positive neurons [ ]. Cortical and hippocampal damage severity was inversely proportional to the number of Nissl-positive neurons.

2.6

## Behavioral evaluation of mice
To investigate the long-term behavioral effects of DMM following HIBD, behavioral assessments were conducted between postoperative weeks 4 and 5 according to a predefined experimental timeline ( ). All tests were completed in a quiet room to minimize the impact of loud noises, which can startle and stress the mice. To reduce anxiety-related confounders associated with transportation, handling, and environmental novelty, mice underwent a 60-min free exploration period in the testing apparatus before formal assessment. Between experimental subjects, testing equipment was thoroughly sanitized with 70 % ethanol to eliminate olfactory cues. All behavioral tests were performed by two observers who were blinded to the experimental group descriptions and treatments. All groups are tested in no particular order to avoid time effects. 
2.6.1

## Neurodevelopmental reflexes
Cliff avoidance testing was employed to assess depth perception capabilities and instinctive avoidance responses in mice. The mice were placed at the edge of a flat surface, such that their forepaws and snouts were over the edge. Withdrawal latency was recorded as the time interval between their initial positioning and complete body retraction from the cliff edge. To avoid injury, the experimenter's hand or a foam landing was positioned beneath the cliff during trials.
A negative geotropic reflex test was conducted to evaluate proprioceptive and vestibular function. Each mouse was blindfolded and placed head-down on a board inclined at 45°. The time taken to complete a full 180° body rotation and initiate upward movement along the incline was measured and recorded for each mouse [ ].
A forelimb grasp reflex trial was used to assess locomotor coordination. The forepaws of the mice were placed on a blunt rod positioned 50 cm above the ground. Timing was commenced immediately, with the trial terminated upon complete disengagement of the forelimbs, and the duration of rod grasping was recorded [ ].
A righting reflex test was used to assess balance and coordination in mice. Each mouse was manually restrained in a supine position with its ventral surface parallel to the testing platform. A successful righting response was defined as rolling or flipping over onto all four paws, with each paw perpendicular to the body [ ]. The time from release to stabilization upon surface contact was recorded.

2.6.2

## Open field test
An open field test (OFT) was performed in a black square arena (100 cm × 100 cm × 45 cm) [ ] to assess exploratory behavior, spontaneous activity, and anxiety-like behavior [ ]. The arena floor was divided into central and peripheral zones. A ceiling-mounted video camera connected to a digital recording system captured all behavioral parameters. During a habituation phase, individual mice were placed at the center of the arena and allowed to explore freely for 5 min to acclimate to the testing environment [ ]. After a 24-h interval, formal OFT trials were conducted with each mouse undergoing a 5-min recording session. Quantitative measurements included total distance traveled, frequency of entries into the central zone, duration spent in the central zone (dwell time) [ ] [ ], and frequency of grooming episodes.

2.6.3

## Novel object-recognition test
The novel object-recognition (NOR) test was used to assess recognition memory and is based on the innate preference of rodents for novel stimuli. Testing was conducted in a dimly lit environment using a matte gray plastic arena (45 cm × 45 cm × 45 cm). The protocol comprised three sequential phases: (1) a 5-min habituation session in the empty arena conducted 24 h before testing (acclimation phase) [ ]; (2) on the first day (familiarization phase), two identical objects (same shape, size, and color) were placed spaced along two thirds of the diagonal axis of the arena floor, and each mouse was allowed to explore freely for 5 min [ ] [ ]; (3) on the second day (24 h later, test, recognition phase), one of the familiar objects was replaced with a novel object differing in shape and color, and mice were again allowed to explore freely for 5 min. Exploratory behavior was defined operationally as nasal contact within 2 cm of the object or active forelimb manipulation, excluding passive contact or climbing [ ]. Recognition memory was quantified using the discrimination index: (novel object-exploration time)/(total object-exploration time) × 100 % [ ].

2.6.4

## Foot-fault test
A foot-fault test was used to evaluate sensorimotor function. The apparatus consisted of an elevated wire mesh grid (50 cm in height) with uniformly distributed 2 cm2 apertures. All the mice underwent a 5-min acclimatization trial to traverse the grid surface before testing. Following a 60-min interval, formal testing commenced with continuous video recording of each mouse's locomotion. A foot-fault error was defined as complete placement or falling of the left hind paw and heel below the level of the grid [ ]. Error frequency was independently quantified across 50 consecutive steps per mouse by two observers blinded to treatment allocation [ ].

2.7

## Measurement of Fe2+ content for experiments in vivo
At 24 h post-HIBD induction, the brain was removed from the mice, and cortical tissues were dissected and processed for Fe2+ content analysis. Tissue specimens were suspended in 1 mL ice-cold PBS and triturated 20 times using a pipette, followed by 10-s vortex homogenization in iron assay buffer. The samples were then centrifuged (20 min, 12,000×g, 4 °C) to obtain clarified supernatants. Protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), whereas Fe2+ content was measured using a commercial iron assay kit (I291, Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) following manufacturer specifications. All sample preparations were conducted on ice to avoid enzymatic degradation, and the absorbance of the ferrozine complex with Fe2+ was measured at 593 nm.

2.8

## Malondialdehyde assay for experiments in vivo
Lipid peroxidation levels were quantified by detecting malondialdehyde (MDA) with an MDA assay kit (S0131 M, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Cortical tissues harvested by dissection 24 h after HIBD modeling were washed three times with PBS, followed by precise gravimetric measurement. Tissue homogenates prepared in ice-cold PBS were centrifuged (20 min, 12,000×g, 4 °C) to obtain protein-rich supernatants. Protein concentration was determined via BCA assay. To quantify MDA, 100 μL tissue lysate was combined with 200 μL MDA detection working solution, incubated at 100 °C for 15 min, and clarified by centrifugation. The supernatant (200 μL) was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance was measured at 532 nm. Final MDA concentrations were normalized to total protein content using the formula: MDA (μmol/mg protein) = (Sample MDA concentration)/(protein concentration).

2.9

## Western blotting for experiments in vivo
Western blot analysis was performed according to established protocols. Cortical tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China). Lysates were centrifuged (20 min, 12,000×g, 4 °C), and supernatant protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay. Equal protein quantities (10 μg/lane) were resolved on 4 %–20 % gradient SDS-PAGE gels and electrophoretically transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (0.45 μm, Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). Nonspecific binding sites on the membranes were blocked with 5 % non-fat dried milk in Tris-buffered saline–Tween 20 (TBST) for 2 h at room temperature. Primary antibody incubations were performed overnight at 4 °C using: rabbit anti-4HNE (1:1000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-GPX4 (1:1000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-SLC7A11 (1:500, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-NCOA4 (1:500, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-FTH1 (1:1000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-p62 (1:20000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), rabbit anti-LC3B (1:1000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), and rabbit anti-β-actin (1:10000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China). Membranes were subsequently incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit IgG, 1:10,000; ABclonal) for 1 h at room temperature. The developed blots were scanned with an Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.53).

2.10

## Coimmunoprecipitation
Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) analysis was used to examine NCOA4-FTH1 interactions in cortical tissues of HIBD model mice treated with DMM. Tissue lysates were prepared using ice-cold immunoprecipitation buffer supplemented with a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail [ ]. Following centrifugation (5 min, 12,000×g, 4 °C), protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA assay. BeyoMag Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (P2108, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) were preconditioned using three Tris-buffered saline washes with magnetic separation [ ]. Precleared lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-NCOA4 antibody (DF4255, Affinity Biosciences, USA) or species-matched control IgG (sc-2025, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA). Antibody-bound complexes were captured using 40 μL bead suspension per 1 mg total protein, with overnight incubation at 4 °C. After five washes with IP buffer, bead-bound immunoprecipitates were boiled at 100 °C for 5 min to release bound proteins. The beads were magnetically separated, and the protein mixture in the supernatant was collected for SDS-PAGE resolution and immunoblotting as previously described.

2.11

## Cell model to mimic ischemia–reperfusion
HT22 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; C11995500BT, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Suzhou, China) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (S615JJ; Moregate Biotech, Brisbane, Australia) and 10 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin (C022; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) under an atmosphere of 5 % CO2. Oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) was used to model ischemic conditions using standardized protocols [ ]. For immunofluorescence studies (n = 5), four experimental groups were established: CON (control), CON + DMM (control with dimethyl malonate), OGD/R, and OGD + DMM. The measurement of Fe2+ content (n = 8), MDA assay (n = 8), and western blotting (n = 6) used three groups: CON, OGD/R, and OGD + DMM. Upon reaching 70 %–80 % confluence, cells were washed twice with PBS, and the complete medium was replaced with glucose-free DMEM (PM150270; Procell Life Science, Wuhan, China). Subsequent OGD was induced by transferring cultures to a hypoxia chamber (MIC-101; Billups Rothenburg, CA, USA) pre-equilibrated with 95 % N2/5 % CO2 via continuous gas exchange (>15 min) to establish hypoxia. The chamber was sealed and maintained at 37 °C for 6 h. Reperfusion was initiated by replacing the glucose-free medium with complete growth medium, followed by normoxic incubation (37 °C, 5 % CO2) for specified durations.

2.12

## Drug administration for experiments in vitro
For studies in vitro, DMM was prepared as 5 mM stock solutions(58.2 μL DMM + 41.8 μL DMSO) and serially diluted in a complete medium or glucose-free DMEM before experimental use. Two intervention paradigms were implemented to evaluate the effects of DMM on OGD-treated cells.
OGD + DMM group: during preconditioning, cultures were incubated with glucose-free DMEM supplemented with DMM (0.625–10 mM). Following 6 h of hypoxia, the medium was replaced with a complete medium without DMM during normoxic incubation.
R + DMM group: after 6 h of hypoxia, reperfusion was initiated by replacing glucose-free DMEM with a complete medium containing DMM (0.625–10 mM), maintaining treatment throughout normoxic incubation.

2.13

## Cellular viability assay
Cellular viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, CK04; Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Kumamoto, Japan) to evaluate DMM-mediated protection against OGD/R-induced cytotoxicity and establish an optimal therapeutic regimen. HT22 neuronal cultures were seeded in 96-well plates and maintained under standard conditions. Following experimental interventions, cells underwent 24-h incubation at 37 °C under an atmosphere of 5 % CO2. Subsequently, 10 % (v/v) CCK-8 working solution (90 μL culture medium + 10 μL CCK-8 reagent) was added to each well [ ]. After 1–2 h incubation under physiological conditions, optical density was quantified at 450 nm [ ]. Viability was calculated using the formula: viability (%) = ( ), where Ablank represents background absorbance from cell-free wells [ ].

2.14

## Measurement of Fe2+ content for experiments in vitro
Intracellular Fe2+ levels were quantified using the fluorogenic probe FerroOrange (F374; Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Kumamoto, Japan). HT22 neuronal cells were seeded in a glass-bottomed cell culture dish. Following experimental treatments, cells were washed three times with PBS. Then the cells were incubated with 1 μM FerroOrange working solution (diluted in PBS) and maintained at 37 °C under 5 % CO2 for 30 min. Finally, the fluorescence was quantified using laser scanning confocal microscopy.

2.15

## Malondialdehyde assay for experiments in vitro
Following experimental treatments, cells were washed three times with ice-cold PBS and were mechanically detached using cell scrapers. Cell suspensions were centrifuged (20 min, 12,000×g, 4 °C) to pellet cellular debris, and supernatants were retained for MDA analysis. Subsequent processing protocols were identical to those used for mouse tissue measurements.

2.16

## Western blotting for experiments in vitro
Following experimental interventions, cells were washed three times with chilled PBS and mechanically harvested using sterile cell scrapers. Cellular suspensions were centrifuged (20 min, 12,000×g, 4 °C) to separate insoluble components, with clarified supernatants preserved for western blotting analysis. All subsequent analytical procedures followed protocols previously established for western blotting of murine proteins.

2.17

## Immunofluorescence staining
Cells were cultured in 24-well plates containing sterile glass coverslips [ ]. Following experimental treatments, samples were fixed using 4 % paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature, followed by three washes with PBS [ ]. Cells were subsequently permeabilized using 0.1 % Triton X-100 for 10 min [ ]. Nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) in PBS for 1 h at ambient temperature. Primary antibodies against LAMP2 (mouse anti-LAMP2, 66301-1-lg, Proteintech; 1:200) and FTH1 (rabbit anti-FTH1, 83428-1-RR, Proteintech; 1:200) were applied and maintained at 4 °C overnight [ ]. Following PBS washes, fluorescent secondary antibodies (488-goat anti-mouse IgG, RGAM002, Proteintech; 1:500 and 594-goat anti-rabbit IgG, GRAR004, Proteintech; 1:500) were applied for 1 h under light-protected conditions [ ]. Nuclear counterstaining was achieved through 5 min incubation with 1 μg/mL 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, D9542, Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS. Fluorescence was imaged using a laser scanning confocal microscope.

2.18

## Distribution of ferrous iron in LysoTracker staining
HT22 neuronal cells were seeded in glass-bottomed cell culture dishes. Following experimental treatments, cellular samples were washed three times with PBS. Then all cells were stained with 1 μM FerroOrange working solution at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 incubator for 30 min [ ]. Following careful removal of the fluorophore solution via aspiration, cells were washed three times with PBS. Lysosomal staining was performed subsequently using 1 μM LysoTracker working solution (MD01; Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Kumamoto, Japan) for 30 min under identical incubation conditions [ ]. Fluorescent signals were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope.

## Results
3.1

## DMM alleviates histopathological injury in mice subjected to HIBD
We established a HIBD model to investigate the therapeutic potential of DMM in neonatal hypoxic–ischemic brain injury. Neonatal mice received various doses of DMM before inducing HIBD. TTC staining after 24 h of HIBD modeling ( A and B). TTC staining of brain sections revealed significantly increased infarct volumes in the HIBD group compared with the Sham group, confirming the establishment of a successful model. Pretreatment with DMM (5, 10, and 20 mg/kg) 15 min before surgical procedures markedly reduced cerebral infarction, with the 10 mg/kg dose demonstrating the strongest protective effect ( C and D). 
Nissl staining analysis after 24 h of HIBD modeling ( A), which reveals substantial neuronal degeneration in the lesioned cortex and ipsilateral hippocampal regions of HIBD mice, characterized by cellular shrinkage and morphological abnormalities ( B). DMM treatment preserved normal neuronal architecture and regular cellular morphology ( B). Quantitative analysis demonstrated significant reductions in Nissl-positive neuron counts within the lesioned cortex and hippocampal CA1, CA3, and DG subfields of the HIBD group compared with the Sham group ( C). DMM administration reversed these deficits, significantly increasing the number of Nissl-positive neurons across all examined regions ( C). 
These findings indicate that DMM effectively mitigates histopathological damage induced by HIBD in neonatal mice.

3.2

## DMM ameliorates cognition and mobility after HIBD modeling in neonatal mice
To evaluate the response of the mice to perception, locomotion, and coordination of the neonatal mouse HIBD model, we conducted neurodevelopmental reflex testing on the 24th day after successfully establishing the model (postnatal day 34). The HIBD group exhibited significantly prolonged response times for cliff avoidance ( A–a), negative geotropism ( A –b), and righting reflexes ( A–d) compared with the Sham group. Furthermore, forelimb grasp reflex ( A–c) durations were markedly reduced in HIBD mice compared with the Sham group. DMM administration significantly improved all HIBD-affected reflexes ( A). These results demonstrate that HIBD-induced impairments in perceptual, locomotor, and coordinative functions are substantially mitigated by DMM treatment. 
To assess exploratory behavior, spontaneous activity, and anxiety-like behavior in neonatal mice with HIBD, OFT was performed on the 25th and 26th day after establishing the model (postnatal days 35 and 36). HIBD group exhibited significantly reduced active distance compared with the Sham group, a deficit substantially reversed by DMM treatment ( D). Quantitative analysis revealed three key behavioral impairments in the HIBD group: decreased total distance traveled ( E–a), reduced center zone occupancy time ( E–b), and diminished central area entries ( E–c). DMM administration significantly ameliorated these abnormalities ( E–a, E-b, E-c). Anxiety-like behaviors, quantified through self-grooming frequency, showed an increase in the HIBD group compared with the Sham group ( E–d). DMM intervention reduced grooming events ( E–d). Collectively, these results demonstrate that HIBD induces significant deficits in exploratory behavior, spontaneous locomotion, and anxiety emotional regulation, all of which are effectively mitigated by DMM treatment.
To evaluate the memory and cognitive effects of DMM in neonatal mice with HIBD, NOR testing was conducted on the 27th to 29th day after establishing the model (postnatal days 37–39). Quantitative analysis revealed a reduction in the discrimination index in the HIBD group compared with the Sham group ( B). DMM administration restored exploratory preference, demonstrating an enhanced the discrimination index in treated mice ( B). These results indicate that HIBD induces significant deficits in object-recognition memory, which were substantially mitigated by DMM intervention.
Sensorimotor function was evaluated using a foot-fault test on the 30th day after the model was established (postnatal day 40). The HIBD group exhibited significantly more foot-fault errors compared with the Sham group ( C). DMM-treated mice demonstrated a reduction in foot-fault error frequency compared with untreated HIBD mice ( C). These results indicate that HIBD induces sensorimotor coordination deficits, which were significantly ameliorated by DMM intervention.

3.3

## DMM improves cell viability of HT22 cells subjected to OGD/R
To determine the optimal DMM therapeutic regimen for in vitro experiments, we conducted CCK-8 assays evaluating HT22 cell viability under OGD/R conditions ( A). Cells exposed to 1.25 and 2.5 mM DMM during the 6-h OGD phase exhibited greater viability compared with the untreated OGD/R group ( B). This protective effect displayed dose-dependency, with 1.25 mM DMM showing intermediate efficacy, 2.5 mM showing the best efficacy, and concentrations >2.5 mM showing no significant effect ( B). No significant viability differences were observed when administering DMM (0.625–10 mM) during reperfusion ( C). These results establish 2.5 mM DMM administration during the OGD phase as the optimal therapeutic regimen for subsequent in vitro investigations of neuroprotective mechanisms. 

3.4

## DMM protects against HIBD-induced injury via inhibition of ferroptosis in mice
To investigate the neuroprotective mechanisms of DMM against HIBD, we quantified ferroptosis-related biomarkers in cerebral cortical tissues. Current evidence establishes ferroptosis as an iron-dependent process characterized by Fe2+ accumulation. The data revealed that the levels of Fe2+ in the HIBD group were significantly higher than those in the Sham group, whereas DMM treatment notably reduced the Fe2+ content ( A). Subsequently, we evaluated the lipid oxidation products (MDA and 4-HNE). HIBD-induced elevation in MDA levels ( B) and increased 4-HNE expression compared with the Sham group ( C). DMM administration attenuated these lipid peroxidation markers, respectively ( A, B, C). 
In addition to the accumulation of iron-dependent lipid oxidation products, ferroptosis is also characterized by the depletion of the antioxidant system. To elucidate the differential expression of protective components of ferroptosis, SLC7A11 and GPX4, we examined and compared brain tissues from the lesioned cortex of mice in the Sham group at various times (6, 12, and 24 h) after modeling. As shown in D and E, the expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4 was significantly lower in the lesioned cortex of mice 24 h after HIBD modeling. Based on the above results, we chose 24 h after HIBD as the sampling time for subsequent western blotting. In the analysis, the effect of DMM on protein expression characteristics related to ferroptosis, we observed that the expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4 was significantly decreased in the HIBD group, whereas it was markedly increased after DMM treatment ( F and G).
Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DMM exerts neuroprotective effects against HIBD through multiple mechanisms, including the suppression of ferroptosis, mediated by iron overload suppression, lipid peroxidation suppression, and the restoration of antioxidant defense systems.

3.5

## DMM protects against OGD/R-induced injury via inhibition of ferroptosis in HT22 cells
We assessed the content of Fe2+ and MDA in HT22 cells to verify the efficacy of DMM in suppressing ferroptosis in vitro. The experimental results recapitulated our previous observations in vivo. OGD/R-exposed cells demonstrated significantly elevated Fe2+ ( A and B) and MDA levels ( C) compared with the CON group. DMM treatment during OGD significantly attenuated intracellular Fe2+ accumulation ( A and B) and reduced MDA production ( C). 
We further investigated the changes in the expression levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4 at various times (6, 12, and 24 h) after modeling. As shown in D and E, the expression levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4 were significantly lower in HT22 cells 12 h after OGD/R modeling. Therefore, 12 h after OGD/R was chosen as the time for cell collection. Additionally, we investigated whether DMM could restore the antioxidant system by using western blotting to measure the expression levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4. Western blotting analysis revealed reductions in SLC7A11 and GPX4 protein expression levels, respectively, in the OGD/R compared with the CON group ( F and G). Conversely, the expression levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4 were significantly increased in the cells in the OGD + DMM group compared with those in the OGD/R group ( F and G).
These findings demonstrate that DMM mitigates OGD/R-induced ferroptosis in HT22 cells through dual mechanisms of iron steady-state regulation and potentiation of the antioxidant system.

3.6

## DMM affects ferroptosis in HIBD by inhibiting ferritinophagy
We have confirmed that DMM suppresses ferroptosis by lowering the amount of bioavailable intracellular Fe2+. Given that NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy can alter iron homeostasis, we assumed that DMM inhibits ferritinophagy. To prove this hypothesis, we determined the levels of ferritinophagy-related proteins by western blotting. First, we assessed the differential expression of NCOA4 and FTH1 proteins at different time points (6, 12, and 24 h) after modeling. As shown in A and B, the expression of NCOA4 was significantly higher, and the expression of FTH1 was significantly lower in the brain tissues from the lesioned cortex of mice 24 h after HIBD modeling. Based on these results, we chose 24 h after HIBD as the sampling time for subsequent western blotting. Furthermore, results demonstrated that in the HIBD group, the expression levels of NCOA4 and LC3II were higher than those in the Sham group ( C and D). The expression of FTH1 and p62 in the HIBD group was significantly lower than that in the Sham group ( C and D). However, upon the addition of DMM, the levels of NCOA4 and LC3II decreased, and the levels of FTH1 and p62 increased ( C and D). These findings establish that DMM attenuates ferroptosis in HIBD by suppressing ferritinophagy. 

3.7

## DMM affects ferroptosis in OGD/R by inhibiting ferritinophagy
We analyzed ferritinophagy-related proteins (NCOA4, p62, FTH1, and LC3II) in HT22 cells under OGD/R conditions to validate the ferroptosis-suppressing mechanism of DMM by inhibiting ferritinophagy in vitro. First, in the analysis of protein expression of NCOA4 and FTH1 protein at different times (6, 12, and 24 h) after modeling, we observed that the expression of NCOA4 was significantly higher, and that of FTH1 was lower in HT22 cells 12 h after OGD/R modeling ( A and B). Therefore, 12 h after OGD/R was chosen as the time for cell collection. Compared with those in the CON group, the expression levels of NCOA4 and LC3II were significantly increased in the OGD/R group ( C and D). Furthermore, the expression of FTH1 and p62 was decreased in the OGD/R group ( C and D). However, DMM reversed the increase in NCOA4 and LC3II expression; it also reversed the reduced expression of FTH1 and p62 ( C and D). These results demonstrate that DMM mitigates ferroptosis by modulating ferritinophagy pathways in neuronal cells.

## DMM inhibits ferritinophagy by disrupting the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction in a mouse model of HIBD
Existing studies revealed that ferritinophagy is mediated by NCOA4, which directly recognizes FTH1 and then transports the ferritin complex to autophagosomes [ ]. The NCOA4-FTH1 protein–protein interaction (PPI) is essential for ferritinophagy. Our previous experimental results showed that DMM could inhibit ferritinophagy. Therefore, with Co-IP assays, we investigated whether DMM abolished the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction to inhibit ferritinophagy. As shown in , NCOA4-FTH1 binding affinity is reduced following DMM treatment compared with that in the untreated HIBD group. These findings provide mechanistic evidence that DMM exerts its ferritinophagy suppression effects through targeted interference with the NCOA4-FTH1 PPI. 

3.9

## Knockdown of Fth1 alleviates the ferroptosis-inhibiting effect of DMM in HT22 cells
We knocked down Fth1 in HT22 cells to examine in more detail whether FTH1 mediates the ferroptosis-inhibiting effect of DMM. First, we examined Fth1 knockdown efficiency by western blotting, which showed that the inhibitory effect of siRNA#3 was the most pronounced ( A and B). Therefore, we chose siRNA#3 for subsequent experiments. The cellular viability assay shows that the downregulated FTH1 partially reversed the protective effect of DMM in OGD/R-treated HT22 cells ( C). Moreover, Ferro Orange fluorescent probe staining shows that Fth1 knockdown partially reversed the effect of DMM that inhibited Fe2+ accumulation ( D and E). Similarly, the result of the MDA assay revealed that Fth1 knockdown partially reversed the decreased MDA levels induced by DMM treatment ( F). As revealed by western blotting, the upregulated expression of GPX4 and SLC7A11 induced by DMM treatment was abolished after Fth1 was knocked down ( G and H). These findings suggest that FTH1 is a crucial target for the ferroptosis-inhibitory action of DMM in HT22 cells. 

3.10

## DMM inhibits ferritinophagy and the accumulation of Fe2+ in lysosomes
We have revealed that DMM abolished the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction to inhibit ferritinophagy and targeted FTH1 to inhibit ferroptosis. We performed immunofluorescence experiments to observe the colocalization of FTH1 (a critical subunit of ferritin, which is used as a ferritin marker) and LAMP2 (a protein in lysosome membranes, which is used as a lysosomal-specific marker) to explore in more detail the potential of DMM to inhibit ferritin degradation at the organelle level. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed distinct spatial distribution patterns of FTH1 across experimental groups ( A). Control groups (CON and CON + DMM) exhibited diffuse FTH1 staining, whereas OGD/R-treated cells demonstrated pronounced FTH1-LAMP2 colocalization and reduced FTH1 fluorescence intensity ( A, B, C). DMM intervention during OGD significantly reduced this colocalization ( A and B) and restored FTH1 fluorescence intensity ( C), consistent with the results of western blotting analysis. LAMP2 fluorescence intensity remained unchanged across groups ( D), excluding the number of alterations of lysosomes as a confounding factor. 
We investigated lysosomal iron quantification using FerroOrange/LysoTracker Green dual staining, which revealed higher intralysosomal Fe2+ accumulation in the OGD/R group compared with the CON group ( E and F). By contrast, DMM treatment reduced the Fe2+ accumulation in lysosomes ( E and F). Notably, lysosomal fluorescence intensity remained unchanged across groups ( G), excluding the number of alterations of lysosomes as a confounding factor.
Together, these results suggest that DMM disrupted the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction to inhibit the transportation of ferritin complexes to autophagosomes. Subsequently, the suppression of ferritin degradation and iron release decreased the amount of intracellular free Fe2+, eventually reversing ferroptosis.

## Discussion
Although the role of ferroptosis in the pathophysiology of neurological diseases, such as HIE, has been confirmed, compounds that can effectively activate or inhibit ferroptosis, thereby regulating it in vivo remain scarce [ ]. Martin et al. compared species-specific metabolic changes in mouse, pig, and human hearts during warm and cold ischemia. They found that succinic acid accumulation is a major cross-species characteristic in ischemic hearts, and that ischemic injury can be improved by metabolic inhibitors such as DMM, which can prevent succinic acid accumulation and oxidation [ ]. Preclinical studies have shown that DMM has therapeutic potential in preventing brain damage, including in epilepsy [ ], Alzheimer disease [ ], ischemic stroke [ ], brain injury after cardiac arrest [ ], and obsessive–compulsive disorder [ ]. In animal models of ischemic stroke in vivo and oxygen and glucose deprivation/reperfusion in vitro, we have recently demonstrated that DMM can prevent ischemic brain injury [ ]. Zhang et al. also found that DMM reduced the infarct volume in a mouse model of ischemic stroke using transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO) [ ]. These findings suggest that DMM has a protective effect on brain damage induced by ischemic hypoxia. Here, we investigated the therapeutic efficacy of DMM in preclinical models of HIBD. Significant cerebral infarction and typical ischemic histopathological changes were observed in the injured brain tissue of neonatal mice 24 h after HIBD. DMM treatment significantly reduced the volume of cerebral infarction and reduced the pathological damage of cortical and hippocampal tissues, demonstrating the acute neuroprotective effect of DMM on HIBD. The studies in vitro demonstrated that DMM exhibits a significant protective effect when administered before OGD. However, this protective effect loses significance when administered following cellular injury. Further investigation is required to establish whether a therapeutic window exists for post-insult administration in a model in vivo. Additionally, we assessed the long-term impact of DMM through behavioral testing. The results showed that DMM treatment effectively reversed HIBD-induced neuroreflex and motor disorders, as well as cognitive and spatial memory disorders, consistent with early histomorphological results. These results suggest that DMM promotes long-term behavioral improvement by protecting neurons in the acute phase of HIBD. Our results are consistent with the findings of Zhang et al. in a rat model of epileptic seizure induced by kainic acid. They observed that epileptic seizures led to aggravated damage to hippocampal and cortical neurons, increased iron content, and abnormal expression of iron metabolism-related proteins, whereas DMM treatment corrected these damaged phenotypes [ ].
Stockwell et al. discovered that the oncogenic rat sarcoma (RAS)-selective lethal small molecule erastin triggers a unique iron-dependent nonapoptotic form of cell death that relies on iron within the cell rather than other metals. This form of death differs morphologically, biochemically, and genetically from apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy, and is therefore named ferroptosis [ ]. It is now widely recognized that ferroptosis is a regulated form of cell death characterized by the accumulation of intracellular iron and reactive oxygen species, inhibition of system Xc−, depletion of glutathione, and induction of lipid peroxidation [ ]. Ferroptosis is associated with pathological cell death related to degenerative diseases, stroke, cerebral hemorrhage, traumatic brain injury, ischemia–reperfusion injury, and other conditions [ ]. Hypoxic–ischemic brain injury can trigger neuronal ferroptosis through the accumulation of Fe2+ and lipid peroxides [ ]. In particular, it has been found that iron chelators have neuroprotective effects during neonatal brain injury, indicating that the iron-dependent pathway may be a new target for intervening in neonatal brain injury [ ]. Jiang et al. investigated the role and potential mechanism of ferroptosis in sevoflurane-induced developmental neurotoxicity in pregnant rats treated with or without 3.0 % sevoflurane on the 13th day of pregnancy (G13). Their results indicate that repeated exposure to sevoflurane during the mid-trimester can induce neurotoxicity caused by ferroptosis in the developing brain [ ]. More recently, Tschuck et al. discovered that inhibiting ferroptosis with vitamin A or radical-trapping antioxidants is necessary to obtain mature neurons and proper laminar organization in cortical organoids [ ]. In line with the study by Tschuck et al., ferroptosis inhibitors Fer-1 and deferoxamine B (DFO) reversed the accumulation of iron and lipid peroxides caused by mitochondrial division inhibitor-1 (Mdivi-1), hence reducing ferroptosis triggered by neonatal cerebral HI [ ]. As an extension of this work, here, we further evaluated ferroptosis by detecting iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation, and protein levels of GPX4 and SLC7A11 after HI. We found that after 24 h of HIBD and 12 h of OGD/R, Fe2+ and lipid peroxidation levels (MDA and 4-HNE) significantly increased, whereas DMM intervention significantly reduced Fe2+ accumulation and lipid peroxidation.
The glutathione pathway (SLC7A11/GPX4 axis) is a typical defense mechanism against ferroptosis, crucial for reducing lipid peroxidation and maintaining cellular antioxidant status. Therefore, disruption of the antioxidant system is a key contributor to ferroptosis. GPX4 is a selenocysteine-containing protein that plays an essential role in repairing peroxidized phospholipids [ ]. Genetic studies performed in cells and mice established GPX4 as the key and negative regulator of ferroptosis [ ]. Adult mouse Gpx4 ablation results in a lethal phenotype, accompanied by loss of brain neurons [ ]. SLC7A11 is a critical gateway for redox homeostasis, maintaining intracellular reduced glutathione levels to counteract cellular oxidative stress and inhibit ferroptosis [ ]. Using the ferret whole-hemisphere OGD model, Kolnik et al. showed that vitamin E neuroprotection is mediated by restoring reduced glutathione and decreasing amplification of certain markers of oxidative stress (CHAC1, SLC7A11) and inflammation after neonatal HI [ ]. Yuan et al. demonstrated that OGD/R reduced the levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4 while decreasing endogenous antioxidants in neurons. Activation of nuclear factor-E2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)/SLC7A11/GPX4 signaling enhanced antioxidant capacity, inhibited lipid peroxidation accumulation in OGD/R-treated neurons, and reversed OGD/R-induced ferroptosis [ ]. In addition, HI in neonatal rats can significantly reduce the expression levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4, leading to a decrease in cellular antioxidant capacity and ultimately resulting in ferroptosis of cortical neurons [ ]. Moreover, in the HIBD model and PC12 cell OGD model in neonatal rats, Fer-1 significantly reduced the cerebral infarction volume, improved histopathological damage, and significantly increased the expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4 [ ]. Consistently, we also observed downregulation of protein expression of several glutathione pathway ferroptosis markers (SLC7A11 and GPX4) in mice treated with HIBD and HT22 cells treated with OGD/R. By contrast, DMM intervention enhanced the antioxidant pathway both in vivo and in vitro (upregulating the expression levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4), further confirming that HIBD and OGD/R induce neuronal ferroptosis, which DMM could inhibit.
Ferroptosis was previously considered independent of autophagy; however, increasing evidence suggests that it is an autophagic cell death process involving ferritin degradation, and a complex relationship exists between ferritin autophagy and ferroptosis. Recently, it has been recognized that NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy is necessary for ferroptotic cell death, and regulating NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagic flux can alter sensitivity to ferroptosis [ ] [ ]. Using a cerebral ischemia–reperfusion mouse model with NCOA4 silencing, Li et al. found that early activation of NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy promoted autophagy and apoptosis, ultimately leading to exacerbated brain damage [ ]. Iron storage protein ferritin is composed of light chain and heavy chain subunits, encoded by FTL and FTH1, respectively. FTH1 is a ferritin heavy chain gene. FTH1 heterozygous nonsense variants can cause neuroferritinopathy in children and adults [ ] [ ]. Using ferritin H (Fth) knockout (KO) mice Gao et al. demonstrated that Fth depletion significantly exacerbated the inflammatory response after traumatic brain injury (TBI) and eliminated the anti-inflammatory effects of melatonin both in vivo and in vitro. In addition, the anti-inflammatory effect of melatonin after TBI depends on Fth [ ]. Similarly, Rui et al. found that Fth-KO mice were more prone to ferroptosis after TBI, and the neuroprotective effect of melatonin was eliminated in Fth-KO mice, which was also confirmed by si-Fth experiments in vitro [ ]. Dang et al. investigated the cellular heterogeneity of AD by utilizing scRNA seq bioinformatics analysis of AD patients and healthy controls in the Gene Expression Omnibus database. Gene ontogeny and KEGG analyses revealed that astrocytes are enriched in the ferroptosis pathway, and FTH1 was identified as a hub mRNA that affects ferroptosis in astrocytes [ ]. Notably, the synergistic effect of NCOA4-FTH1 is involved in the pathophysiological processes of multiple neurological disease models. For example, in a rat model of ischemic stroke, inhibiting ferroptosis by disrupting the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction can significantly improve the ischemic–refusion injury [ ]. Using tMCAO mouse models of ischemic stroke and HT-22 cell models of OGD/R, Yu et al. found that melatonin improves brain injury phenotype by inhibiting autophagy-dependent ferroptosis mediated by the binding interaction between NCOA4 and FTH1 [ ]. Notably, the expression of NCOA4 and FTH1 often exhibits opposing trends in brain injury. Using a controlled cortical impact model of TBI in rats, Zhou et al. detected an increase in NCOA4 and a decrease in FTH1 in injured white matter. Moreover, NCOA4 deletion notably improved white matter injury following TBI [ ]. In parallel, Zhang et al. found that in mice, intestinal injury induced by TBI resulted in significant upregulation of NCOA4, LC3II/LC3I, and p62, while GPX4 and FTH1 were significantly downregulated in intestinal tissues [ ]. In the present study, the expression of FTH1 and p62, a type of autophagy receptor that delivers substrates into autophagosomes for degradation through interaction with LC3, was significantly reduced, whereas the expression of NCOA4 and LC3II (autophagy markers) was significantly increased in the in vivo model of HIBD, and was reversed by DMM treatment. The in vitro study using HT22 cells showed similar results after OGD/R and DMM treatment. The results indicate that HIBD and OGD/R activate ferritinophagy and drive neuronal ferroptosis by upregulating NCOA4/LC3II and downregulating FTH1/p62. DMM intervention may maintain ferritin stability by disrupting the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction, thereby achieving resistance to ferroptosis. However, the specific mechanism by which DMM inhibits ferroptosis by disrupting the interaction between NCOA4 and FTH1 needs further clarification. To address this issue, we detected the endogenous NCOA4-FTH1 interaction in the damaged cortical tissue using Co-IP. We found that under HIBD conditions, the interaction between NCOA4 and FTH1 was enhanced, whereas DMM weakened the interaction between NCOA4 and FTH1. Our results are consistent with those of a previous study on renal ischemia–reperfusion injury, which also found an enhanced interaction between NCOA4 and ferritin, promoting ferritinophagy, whereas disrupting the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction effectively blocked ferritinophagy [ ]. Therefore, our results suggest that NCOA4 and FTH1 may be key targets for the protective effect of DMM. DMM specifically disrupts the NCOA4-FTH1 protein interaction, blocking the transport of ferritin to autophagosomes, thereby inhibiting Fe2+ release and the subsequent ferroptosis cascade reactions. However, whether NCOA4 or FTH1 plays the key role remains unclear. DMM competitively inhibits SDH, blocking succinate oxidation during reperfusion. This inhibition reduces mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) production by interrupting electron transfer at Complex II. Mitochondrial ROS modulation can influence cellular signaling cascades, including those regulating ferroptosis and autophagy. Altered ROS levels induced by DMM may indirectly affect NCOA4-FTH1 interaction through ROS-sensitive pathways. Although the role of DMM in disrupting the NCOA4-FTH1 complex is demonstrated, current research does not clarify whether this disruption occurs indirectly through the metabolic actions of DMM or by separate pathways.
Considering that Fth is mainly expressed in neurons, especially the important role of Fth1 in combating neuronal ferroptosis [ ], and combined with reports that Fth deficiency counteracts the inhibitory effect of melatonin on ferroptosis in a mouse model of TBI [ ], we speculate that FTH1 may mainly mediate the inhibitory effect of DMM on ferroptosis. To verify this hypothesis, we transfected HT22 cells with Fth1 siRNA (siFTH1) and then subjected them to OGD/R in vitro. FTH1 deficiency reversed the protective effect of DMM: the ability of DMM to restore cell survival was abolished, its effect on reducing Fe2+ accumulation was blocked, and its inhibitory effect on MDA accumulation was counteracted. In addition, its effect of upregulating GPX4 and SLC7A11 expression was lost. Silencing the Fth1 gene eliminated the neuroprotective effect of DMM, confirming that FTH1 may be a key molecular target through which DMM inhibits ferroptosis.
Lysosomes are crucial for the interaction between NCOA4 and FTH1. NCOA4 specifically recognizes arginine on the surface of FTH1 through a C-terminal helical domain, mediating the transport of ferritin-autophagosome complex to lysosomes [ ]. This process leads to the degradation of ferritin and the release of Fe2+ [ ], ultimately exacerbating lipid peroxidation and inducing ferroptosis through the Fenton reaction. We speculate that the mechanism by which DMM blocks the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction may influence FTH1-mediated transport of ferritin to lysosomes. To verify this hypothesis, we observed the colocalization of FTH1 and lysosome marker LAMP2 through an immunofluorescence assay [ ], as well as the colocalization of Fe2+ probe FerroOrange and the lysosome probe LysoTracker [ ]. We found that HT22 cells exhibited punctate colocalization of FTH1 and lysosomal marker LAMP2 after OGD/R treatment, whereas after DMM intervention, the cells showed diffuse staining of FTH1. In addition, OGD/R treatment enhanced the colocalization of Fe2+ and lysosomes, whereas the DMM treatment significantly reduced this colocalization. These findings suggest that DMM attenuated lysosomal Fe2+ release into the cytosolic iron pool and suppressed ferroptosis cascade reactions. This effect occurred through two interconnected mechanisms: disruption of the NCOA4-FTH1 protein interaction, thereby inhibiting NCOA4-mediated lysosomal transport of the ferritin-autophagosome complex; and reduction of ferritin degradation, thereby preventing lysosomal iron overload. Therefore, we speculate that DMM inhibits ferroptosis by disrupting the NCOA4-FTH1 protein interaction, thereby blocking lysosomal degradation of ferritin.

## Conclusions
Ferroptosis plays a key role in HIBD, and DMM confers neuroprotection by regulating ferritinophagy to inhibit ferroptosis. DMM disrupts the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction, thereby blocking ferritin degradation and targeting FTH1 to suppress ferroptosis. DMM inhibits lysosomal transport of the ferritin-autophagosome complex and inhibits iron accumulation in lysosomes. The potential therapeutic value of DMM in HIBD provides a basis for its future development and research as a treatment strategy.

6

## Limitations
Although this study provides compelling evidence for the neuroprotective effects of DMM, several methodological limitations warrant consideration. First, the therapeutic intervention was administered solely 15 min preoperatively, without assessing post-model establishment treatment protocols. Although this approach was informed by preliminary data from our prior investigations [ ] and the pharmacological characteristics of DMM, the absence of delayed intervention groups may constrain its clinical application. Second, all outcome measures (TTC/Nissl staining and behavioral assessments) were intrinsically linked to predetermined therapeutic windows. Subsequent model incorporation would necessitate repeating the entire experimental procedure, requiring approximately 112 additional animals and 1.5–2 months of additional work—a scenario that presents both ethical concerns and practical challenges. Future investigations that implement multipoint intervention strategies to evaluate temporal treatment effects comprehensively are warranted.
The exclusive use of male mice in this study, whereas common in preclinical models to avoid estrogenic effects in mature animals, represents a significant limitation given the sex differences in neurodevelopmental processes and injury responses. Future investigations incorporating pups of both sexes to account for potential sexual differences in therapeutic outcomes are warranted.
Although LC3II and p62 serve as established markers for assessing autophagy, they are not specific to ferritinophagy. Subsequent experiments to evaluate autophagic flux and additional markers (ATG5, ATG7, NBR1), and adding Fer-1 intervention, Baf-A1 intervention, and combined intervention (Fer-1+Baf-A1) as pharmacological controls to differentiate general autophagy modulation from selective ferritinophagy regulation are warranted.
Although our data demonstrate targeting of FTH1 by DMM to inhibit ferroptosis, direct molecular interaction remains unproven. Employing GST-pulldown assays to verify direct binding and subsequently identify the interaction site through targeted mutagenesis is warranted. Moreover, the precise mechanism by which DMM exerts its effects remains unclear, particularly whether it also acts indirectly through upstream regulation of iron metabolism or by succinate-mediated signaling pathways. Subsequent experiments to assess the effects through a combination of iron chelators such as deferoxamine (DFO) and an additional SDH inhibitor, aptenin A5 (AA5), serving as experimental controls, are warranted.
To determine the primary molecular target of DMM, we performed Fth1 gene knockout experiments to assess whether DMM retained its ferroptosis-inhibitory activity when this putative target was compromised. The rationale posited that persistent ferroptosis inhibition after knockout would indicate potential off-target mechanisms or nonspecific toxicity. Genetic ablation of Fth1 abolished the antiferroptotic effects of DMM, providing mechanistic evidence that FTH1 serves as a critical molecular target for suppressing DMM-mediated ferroptosis. Nevertheless, comprehensive evaluation of dose-dependent toxicity profiles and potential off-target effects at elevated pharmacological concentrations remains to be fully elucidated.
