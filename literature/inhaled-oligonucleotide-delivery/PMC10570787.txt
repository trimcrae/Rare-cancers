## Abstract

## Purpose
Although estrogen receptors (ERs) signal pathways are involved in the pathogenesis and development of asthma, their expressions and effects remain controversial. This study aimed to investigate the expressions of ERα and ERβ as well as their mechanisms in airway remodeling and mucus production in asthma.

## Methods
The expressions of ERα and ERβ in the airway epithelial cells of bronchial biopsies and induced sputum cells were examined by immunohistochemistry. The associations of ERs expressions with airway inflammation and remodeling were evaluated in asthmatic patients. In vitro, the regulations of ERs expressions in human bronchial epithelial cell lines were examined using western blot analysis. The epidermal growth factor (EGF)-mediated ligand-independent activation of ERα and its effect on epithelial-mesenchymal transitions (EMTs) were investigated in asthmatic epithelial cells by western blot, immunofluorescent staining, and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction.

## Results
ERα and ERβ were expressed on both bronchial epithelial cells and induced sputum cells, and the expressions showed no sex difference. Compared to controls, male asthmatic patients had higher levels of ERα on the bronchial epithelium, and there were cell-specific expressions of ERα and ERβ in induced sputum. The expression of ERα in the airway epithelium was inversely correlated to forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) % and FEV1/forced vital capacity. Severe asthmatic patients had significantly greater levels of ERα in the airway epithelium than mild-moderate patients. ERα level was positively correlated with the thickness of the subepithelial basement membrane and airway epithelium. In vitro, co-stimulation of interleukin (IL)-4 and EGF increased the expression of ERα and promoted its nuclear translocation. EGF activated the phosphorylation of ERα via extracellular signal-regulated kinase and c-Jun N-terminal kinase pathways. ERα knockdown alleviated EGF-mediated EMTs and mucus production in airway epithelial cells of asthma.

## Conclusions
ERα contributes to asthmatic airway remodeling and mucus production through the EGF-mediated ligand-independent pathway.

## INTRODUCTION
Asthma is a chronic respiratory disease and can be typically characterized by airway inflammation, irreversible airway remodeling, and airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR). , , Airway remodeling contributes to the poor response of treatment in severe asthma patients, which account for most of the disease burden. Target therapies involving different biomarkers are experimentally and clinically significant for understanding the pathogenesis and treatment of airway remodeling in asthma.
Sex hormones and receptors act as important driving factors in the pathogenesis of various diseases including lung adenocarcinoma, breast cancer, and Alzheimer’s disease, 
etc., resulting in sexual dimorphism in some diseases. For asthma, the roles of estrogen and receptors have been studied. It has been prove that estrogen can enhance interleukin (IL)-4-induced macrophages M2 polarization and thereby contributes to the asthmatic allergic airway inflammation. Estrogen receptor (ER) α signaling plays a critical role in severe asthma by increasing IL-17A protein expression in Th17 cells. In asthma mouse models, ERβ-knockout asthmatic mice show increased AHR and remodeling compared to wide-type and ERα-knockout groups. Carey et al., however, proposed that ERβ-knockout mice exhibited a rare change in AHR, while ERα-deficient mice had spontaneous AHR. These studies indicated that ERs (including ERα and ERβ) play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of asthma, but exact expressions, effects, and underlying mechanism need to be further investigated.
Both ERα and ERβ belong to the nuclear receptor of transcription factors. They have 2 transcriptional activation function domains: AF-1 (in A/B domain) and AF-2 (in F domain). , The AF-2 region interacts with co-regulatory proteins in a ligand-dependent pathway to activate or repress transcription of genes with estrogen response elements. , However, the AF-1 region can be activated in a ligand-independent manner. , Growth factors, including epidermal growth factor (EGF), can lead to the activation of several phosphorylation sites of the ERs by mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK). , , Indeed, estrogen is responsible for the sex-oriented differences in morbidity, severity, and therapeutic responses of asthma. Females show increased prevalence of asthma around the time of puberty, which is commonly interpreted by the climbing level of estrogen. , , However, a cohort study with 17-year follow-up showed that hormone replacement therapy was associated with a reduced risk of developing late-onset asthma in menopausal women. As fluctuation in estrogen does not align with all clinical manifestations of asthma, it remains unclear whether ERs contributes to asthma through the ligand-independent pathway and be the possible target for asthma treatment.
Airway remodeling, as generally described as a change in the composition, thickness or volume of airway walls, is a critical characteristic of asthma. In asthma, impaired airway epithelium can be activated by EGF, resulting in secretion of cytokines, epithelial-mesenchymal transitions (EMTs), then enhancing airway inflammation and remodeling. , Aravamudan et al. reported that asthmatic patients had higher levels of ERα and ERβ in airway smooth muscle (ASM) cells compared with controls. However, the levels of ERα and ERβ in asthmatic airway epithelial cells and inflammatory cells remain unclear. In this study, a hypothesis was proposed that the expressions of ERα and ERβ in asthmatic patients differed from those in healthy controls, and that ERs could contribute to airway remodeling through the ligand-independent pathway.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Subjects
With the approval of the Ethics Review Board of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology, and informed consent of the participants in this study, we enrolled 108 asthmatic patients and 22 healthy controls with induced sputum, and 21 asthmatic patients and 4 healthy controls with bronchial biopsies from July 2015 to April 2018 (IRB ID: 20150406). All subjects were older than 18, some of whom had smoking history. According to the Global Initiative for the Asthma 2022 guideline, all asthma subjects were clinically diagnosed with symptomatic asthma and had accumulated dosage of methacholine provoking a 20% fall in forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) (PD20) less than 2.5 mg and/or bronchodilation FEV1 change ≥ 200 mL and 12% following inhalation of 200 mg salbutamol. Exclusion criteria for asthma patients were as follows: 1) acute episode; 2) respiratory tract infection in the last 2 weeks; 3) lung cancer, bronchiectasis, tuberculosis, and other respiratory diseases; 4) pregnancy; 5) severe organ failures; 6) being not able to have the bronchoscopy examination or sputum induction. Inclusion criteria for controls were as follows: 1) no history of respiratory disease (e.g., asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, lung cancer, and bronchiectasis); 2) no abnormalities on chest computed tomography scan or X-ray examination; 3) no respiratory tract infection in last 2 weeks; 4) no serious organ dysfunction; 5) consent to receiving bronchoscopy examination or sputum induction. We recorded their demographic information and clinical features including asthma control test (ACT), routine blood testing, fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FENO), spirometry, and serum total immunoglobulin E (IgE).

## Bronchoscopy, sputum induction, and sample collection
All subjects received bronchoscopy with endobronchial biopsies and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) from the fourth to fifth generation airways as previously described. , Bronchial biopsy specimens were fixed with formalin and paraffin-embedded. Sections of paraffin-embedded human bronchial biopsies were treated by hematoxylin-eosin and imaged with a microscope (Olympus BX 51, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Then, the Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software was used to measure the thickness of airway epithelium and basement membrane. Cytospins of BALF cells were prepared using a cytocentrifuge and cells were stained (Wright-Giemsa); a differential cell count was obtained from 800 cells. The remaining cells were stored in TRIzol (Takara, Dalian, China) at −80°C until extraction for gene expression analyses.
Sputum induction was processed as described previously. Cells were stained (Wright-Giemsa), and a differential cell count was obtained from 400 non-squamous cells. Cytospins were prepared using a cytocentrifuge for further analysis.

## Murine models for ovalbumin (OVA)-induced allergic airway inflammation
Both male and female C57BL/6 mice (Charles River, Beijing, China) aged at 8 weeks were sensitized with an intraperitoneal injection of OVA grade V (dissolved in saline, Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) mixed with aluminum hydroxide (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) as an adjuvant on days 0 and 12. Because males were 4–6 g heavier than females at the same age, males were sensitized using OVA at a concentration of 125 μg/mouse in 125 μL aluminum hydroxide, and females were sensitized using OVA at a concentration of 100 μg/mouse in 100 μL aluminum hydroxide. Control mice received the same volume of saline. From days 18 to 23, all mice were daily challenged with the 5% OVA grade II (Sigma-Aldrich) in saline (aerosolized for 20 minutes), or with saline as controls. Mice were killed at 24 hours after the last serial OVA (or saline) administration on day 24. The protocols were as previously described with some modifications. Lung tissues were collected for histological analysis, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR), western blotting, and immunostaining. Cytospin of BALF cells were prepared and stained with Liu’s solution (Promoter biotech, Wuhan, China) to examine the cell differentials in a blinded manner. Animal experiments were approved by the ethics committee of Tongji Hospital, Huazhong University of Science and Technology.

## Cell culture and treatment
Human bronchial epithelial cell lines BEAS-2B (ATCC, CRL-9609) and HBE4-E6/E7 (ATCC, CRL-2078) were used in all cell experiments. BEAS-2B and HBE4-E6/E7 cell lines were cultured in DMEM (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and RPMI 1640 (Hyclone) medium with 10% FBS, respectively.
BEAS-2B cells were stimulated with or without recombinant human IL-4 (20 ng/mL, PeproTech, Rocky Hill, CT, USA) or recombinant human EGF (100 ng/mL, PeproTech).
Small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting ERα and the negative control siRNA were produced by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). Cells were seeded on 12-well plates and after 24 hours were transfected with siRNA at 50 nM by Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Twenty-four hours later, the Lipofectamine 3000 was removed and replaced with a new cell culture medium with stimulus. The sequence of the sense strand of ERα siRNA was 5′-CGAGTATGATCCTACCAGA-3′. Cell lines were transfected with 0.125 μg of ERα plasmids with Lipofectamine 3000 and P3000. Six hours later, the transfection mixture was removed and replaced with a new cell culture medium. Treated cells were harvested for further investigation. Data of western blots were normalized by comparing with the corresponding controls.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and analysis
Four μm sections of paraffin-embedded human bronchial biopsy and mouse lung, and cytospins of induced sputum were prepared for IHC staining. Briefly, slides were deparaffinized, rehydrated, antigen retrieved, incubated with hydrogen peroxide for 30 minutes, and then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin at room temperature for 1 hour. Slides were then incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4°C. After washing, the slides were incubated with horseradish peroxidase conjugated secondary antibody (Boster Biotechnology, Wuhan, China), developed with diaminobezidin 3 or 3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole (Boster Biotechnology), counterstained with hematoxylin, and imaged with a microscope (Olympus BX 51, Olympus). Consecutive sections of IHC were stained to define the relationship between ERα positive cells and the expression of EGF receptor (EGFR) of bronchial epithelial cells. As for human bronchial biopsy, primary antibodies against ERα were purchased from Proteintech (1:200 dilution, Wuhan, China), primary antibodies against ERβ were purchased from Proteintech (1:200 dilution), and those against EGFR was purchased from Cell Signal Technology (1:30 dilution, Danvers, MA, USA). As for mouse lung sections, a primary antibody against ERα was purchased from Abcam (1:100 dilution, Wuhan, China), the primary antibody against ERβ was purchased from Proteintech (1:400 dilution), and that against α-smooth muscle actin was purchased from Boster Biotechnology (1:400 dilution). Average optical density determined by the Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (EPIX Vision, Silver Spring, MD, USA) was used to quantitatively analyze of epithelial ERα and ERβ levels.

## Immunofluorescent staining
Tissue slides of mouse lung were deparaffinized and antigen retrieved for immunofluorescent staining, and then were blocked with 5% donkey serum for 1 hour at room temperature and incubated with the primary antibodies of interest (ERα, 1:50 dilution, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA; pEGFR, 1:800 dilution, Cell Signal Technology). On the second day, slides were incubated with cocktails of Alexa Flour 488- and 594-conjugated secondary antibody (1:200, Abbkine, Redlands, CA, USA) for 1 hour at room temperature, and then nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 5 minutes. Slides were imaged with a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX 51), and the images were recorded at the same time of exposure.
For cell immunofluorescence, BEAS-2B cell lines after stimulation by IL-4 or/and EGF were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 minutes. Cells were then rinsed with PBS, blocked with 5% donkey serum for and incubated overnight with mouse monoclonal anti-human ERα antibody (1:50 dilution, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and goat monoclonal anti-human pEGFR antibody (1:200 dilution, Cell Signal Technology). Afterward, the sections were incubated with an Alexa Fluor 488-labeled anti-mouse antibody (1:400, Abbkine) and an Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated anti-rabbit antibody (1:400, Abbkine). Cells were counterstained with DAPI for 5 minutes. The images were captured as mentioned above.

## Quantitative real-time PCR
The total RNA of BEAS-2B, HBE4-E6/E7 cell lines, BALF cells, and mouse lung tissues were extracted, and each RNA sample was quantified using a spectrophotometer (NanoDrop 1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara). Real-time PCR assays were performed using the SYBR Premix ExTaq (Takara) on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). IL-5 and interferon (IFN)-γ mRNA levels were normalized to β-actin. β-actin, sense: 5′-AGAAAATCTGGCACCACACCT-3′, antisense: 5′-GATAGCACAGCCTGGATAGCA-3′. IL-5, sense: 5′-TCTACTCATCGAACTCTGCTGA-3′, antisense: 5′-CCCTTGCACAGTTTGACTCTC-3′. IFN-γ, sense: 5′-TCGGTAACTGACTTGAATGTCCA-3′, antisense: 5′-TCGCTTCCCTGTTTTAGCTGC-3′. Muc5Ac, sense: 5′-AGCCGGGAACCTACTACTCG-3′, antisense: 5′-AAGTGGTCATAGGCTTCGTGC-3′. The relative gene expression level was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, and the IL-5 and IFN-γ mRNA levels were calculated using the 2−ΔCT method.

## Western blot analysis
Radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer containing phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, cocktail, and phosphorylation protease inhibitors was used to collect the proteins of mice lung tissues and cells. The cells and tissue lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4°C for 10 minutes. The supernatants were collected, and the BCA kit was used to detect the protein concentration. The proteins were then boiled with the addition of a 5×loading buffer. Next, the proteins were isolated on sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gels and then were shifted to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Following immunoblotting using primary and secondary antibodies, the bands were incubated with enhanced chemiluminescence reagent and visualized using the ChemiDocTM XRS+imaging system. Anti-mouse ERα antibody was purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (1:200 dilution); anti-human ERα antibody was from Cell Signal Technology (1:1,000 dilution); primary antibodies against ERβ were purchased from Proteintech (1:2,000 dilution). Primary antibodies including anti-pERα (Ser118), anti-pEGFR (Tyr1068), and anti-EGFR were from Cell Signal Technology; primary antibodies including anti-fibronectin, anti-E-cadherin and anti-vimentin were purchased from Proteintech; primary antibodies including anti-β-actin and anti-GAPDH were from Sungene (Tianjin, China); primary antibody against Histone 3 was from Abbkine. The densitometry of the western blots was quantified by using the Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed by the JMP software package (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (normally distributed variables), medians and interquartile ranges (nonnormally distributed variables), or counts and percentages (categorical variables). Mann-Whitney U test was performed for continuous variables. Multigroup comparisons were conducted by one-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post-test (normally distributed data) or Kruskal-Wallis tests with Dunn intergroup comparisons (non-normally distributed data). Pearson’s χ2 test was used for categorical variables. The correlation was performed by Spearman’s rank order correlation. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

## RESULTS

## Clinical features
In this study, there were 108 asthmatic patients (61 females and 47 males) and 22 healthy controls (11 females and 11 males) having induced sputum, their clinical features are summarized in . Although healthy controls were younger than asthmatic patients, the differences in age between males/female asthmatic patients and healthy controls were not significant. For both males and females, asthma patients had higher levels of serum total IgE and sputum eosinophil counts, and worse lung function compared to healthy controls. Male patients had greater values of body mass index (BMI) and sputum eosinophils counts and lower values of FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC) (%). In addition, bronchial biopsy tissues of 22 asthmatic patients and 4 healthy controls were also collected, with corresponding clinical features shown in . There was no difference in age, BMI, the counts of eosinophils in peripheral blood, FENO, ACT scores, lung function, or asthma severity between the female and male asthmatics.
 

## Bronchial ER expression in asthma
The expressions of ERα and ERβ protein on bronchial biopsy tissues and induced sputum cells in asthmatic patients and healthy controls were analyzed by IHC staining ( ). The results revealed that both ERα and ERβ were expressed on bronchial epithelial cells and inflammatory cells ( ). There was no sex predisposition in the expressions of ERα or ERβ in epithelial cells and induced sputum cells ( ). Since all enrolled healthy controls were male, the expressions of ERα and ERβ protein of male asthmatic patients and healthy controls were compared. Compared to controls, male asthmatic patients had higher levels of ERα in bronchial epithelial cells (P = 0.0090) and showed a trend of increased ERβ expression (P = 0.0630). Although the percentages of ERs positive of cells in sputum showed no difference between patients and controls, asthmatic patients had greater positive ratios of lymphocytes and granulocytes than controls ( ). The expressions of ERα protein in the bronchial epithelium of biopsy and induced sputum cells were both positively correlated with the ERβ level (r = 0.6183, P = 0.0022, ; r = 0.7088, P < 0.0001, ).
 Lung homogenates from OVA-exposed mice showed a significant increase in the expression of ERα protein by western blot analysis compared to controls in the female and male groups (P = 0.0210 and 0.0076, respectively, ), while no notable difference in the ERβ level was noted between the asthmatic mice and controls. Besides, the expression of ERα (not ERβ) by IHC stain on bronchial epithelial cells of asthmatic mice increased significantly compared with controls (P = 0.0076 for the female group and P = 0.0185 for the male group, ). In OVA induced asthma mice model, there were no sex difference in the expressions of ERα/ERβ of lung homogenates or epithelial cells ( ).
 

## Enhanced bronchial epithelial ERα expression is associated with poor lung function and asthma severity
The expression of ERα on bronchial epithelium was inversely correlated with FEV1% predicted (r = −0.5438, P = 0.0050) and FEV1/FVC (r = −0.5400, P = 0.0053) in all enrolled subjects ( ), whereas the expression of ERβ was not correlated with lung function ( ).
 According to the criteria of American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society 2014, patients were classified into mild-moderate and severe asthma groups. Compared to mild-moderate asthmatic patients (n = 14), severe asthmatic patients (n = 7) had higher levels of ERα (but not ERβ) on bronchial epithelial cells (P = 0.0430, ).

## Enhanced bronchial epithelial ERα expression is associated with airway remodeling in asthma
As shown in , the expression of ERα on bronchial epithelial cells was positively correlated with the thickness of basement membrane (r = 0.6217, P = 0.0012) and airway epithelium (r = 0.4870, P = 0.0252) in all enrolled subjects. The results also showed no notable correlation between bronchial epithelial ERα expression and airway inflammation markers (IL-5 and IFN-γ transcript levels) in any enrolled subjects ( ). Moreover, bronchial epithelial ERβ expression was correlated with neither airway remodeling ( ) nor airway inflammation ( ).
 In OVA-induced asthmatic mice, results demonstrated that the expression of ERα but not ERβ on bronchial epithelial cells was associated with the percentage of airway wall area, percentage of periodic acid-Schiff stain-positive cells, fibrosis ratio, and thickness of ASM ( ).

## Bronchial epithelial ERα expression is regulated by IL-4 and EGF
Recent research has demonstrated that the expressions of EGFR and phosphorylated EGFR were enhanced in the bronchial epithelium from asthmatic subjects. Results in the study showed that the EGF mRNA expression of lung homogenate from OVA-induced asthmatic mice was increased compared to that from controls ( ).
With comparison to the median of ERα expression of epithelial cells in asthmatic patients, ERαHi patients were defined as ERαHi level greater than the median; otherwise they were ERαLow patients. As shown in , the brown areas indicated cells positive for ERα or EGFR staining. The IHC staining of ERα and EGFR by continuous section revealed that the percentage of ERα+EGFR+ epithelial cells was higher in ERαHi patients than in ERαLow patients ( ). Similarly, co-immunofluorescence of ERα and activated (phosphorylated) EGFR showed that the expressions of ERα and pEGFR in the epithelium were also increased in OVA challenged mice compared to controls ( ).
 
In vitro, the combination of IL-4 and EGF increased the percentage of ERα and pEGFR double-positive cells ( ) in BEAS-2B cell lines. ERα expression was upregulated after stimulation with IL-4, EGF, and both in a time-dependent manner ( ). Specifically, separate stimulation with IL-4 and EGF increased the expression of ERα with the enhancement effect of the expression starting at 24 hours, peaking at 48 hours, and slightly decreasing at 72 hours. Instead, co-stimulation of IL-4 and EGF increased the ERα expression at earlier starting time at 18 hours, then peaking at 48 hours, and show a stable enhancement effect at 72 hours. EGF and the combination of IL-4 and EGF also increased ERβ expression, while stimulation with IL-4 alone has no such effect. the increase in ERβ expression after stimulated with EGF did not differ from that stimulated with the combination of IL-4 and EGF ( ) in BEAS-2B cell lines. Notably, co-stimulation of IL-4 and EGF increased ERα expression at an earlier peak time ( ). Also, ERα expression in BEAS-2B cells showed a concentration-dependent increasing trend after stimulation with house dust mite ( ).

## ERα is activated and nuclear translocated by EGF through the MAPK pathway in epithelial cells of asthma
We observed that EGF or the combination of EGF and IL-4 increased ERα nuclear protein expression compared to the negative control group ( ). Additionally, stimulation with IL-4 increased the expression of EGFR in a time-dependent manner, with the phosphor-EGFR expression peaking at 5 minutes and returning to basal levels by 30 minutes ( ).
 In BEAS-2B cell lines, the stimulation with EGF, but not with IL-4 caused phosphorylation of ERα ( ). c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and P38 were activated by EGF in BEAS-2B cells ( ). Moreover, pretreatment of ERK inhibitor U0126 and JNK inhibitor SP600125 both attenuated the phosphorylation of ERα induced by EGF in BEAS-2B cell lines, while P38 inhibitor SB203580 did not ( ).

## ERα modulated EMT and mucus production in the bronchial epithelium
Loss of E-cadherin as well as increased proteins of fibronectin and vimentin were observed in BEAS-2B cell lines treated with the combination of EGF and IL-4 ( ). As shown in , ERα silencing significantly decreased the expression of fibronectin stimulated with the combination of EGF and IL-4. Additionally, overexpression of ERα increased the levels of fibronectin, vimentin, and E-cadherin ( ).
 To verify the roles of ERα in mucus production of airway epithelial cells, HBE4-E6/E7 cell lines were transfected with ERα siRNA and ERα plasmid as in vitro models. Notably, HBE4-E6/E7 instead of BEAS-2B cell lines were used because EGF regulated the Muc5Ac expression in HBE4-E6/E7 cell lines, but not in BEAS-2B cell lines ( ). The knockdown of ERα blunted the enhancing effect of IL-4 with EGF on the expression of Muc5Ac mRNA ( ), and the overexpression of ERα increased the expression of Muc5Ac mRNA ( ). The overexpression of ERα, however, did not strengthen the enhancing effect of IL-4 with EGF. This might be due to potentially various pathways of IL-4 with EGF in enhancing the expression of Muc5Ac mRNA.

## DISCUSSION
In the present study, asthmatic patients had higher levels of ERα in airway epithelial cells compared to controls, while the expressions of ERs in induced sputum cells of asthmatic patients and controls showed no difference; the expression of ERs in epithelium and sputum both showed no sex difference. In addition, the increased expression of ERα in the epithelium was associated with decreased lung function, severity, and airway remodeling in asthmatic patients. In vitro, co-stimulation with IL-4 and EGF enhanced the expression of ERα and promoted its nuclear translocation. EGF promoted the phosphorylation of ERα via the pathways of ERK and JNK. Activation of ERα through the EGF-mediated ligand-independent pathway was involved in the regulation of EMT and mucus production ( ). These results provided a novel insight into understanding the roles and mechanism of ERα in the airway remodeling of asthma.
 Expressions of ERα and ERβ have been reported in asthma. Expressions of ERα and ERβ were higher (especially ERβ) in ASM tissue samples from asthmatic patients than from non-asthmatics. However, the expression of ERα in alveolar macrophages from male mice was reduced following the challenge with OVA, but ERβ expression remained stable. A previous study has reported that ERα and ERβ were expressed in cytosol, plasma as well as membrane of human bronchial epithelium isolated from bronchial tissue. In this study, the ERα and ERβ levels of epithelium and sputum of asthmatic patients and OVA-induced asthma mouse models were examined. The results showed that the expressions of ERα and ERβ (especially ERα) were both higher in bronchial epithelial cells of asthmatic patients than of healthy controls; the expressions of ERα and ERβ in induced sputum cells of asthmatic patients and controls showed no difference, but showed difference between different inflammatory cells, especially lymphocytes and granulocytes. Also, male asthmatic patients had higher levels of ERβ in macrophages and had a trend toward higher levels of ERα. Consequently, ERs were expressed in a cell-specific manner in asthma, which could have been associated with differentiated reactions to asthmatic inflammation.
This study showed that ERα, but not ERβ, was correlated with clinical features of enrolled subjects, which could have been the result of the different structures and functions of ERα and ERβ in the AF-1 region. Specifically, the AF-1 region among the 5 distinct transcriptional activation function domains of ERα and ERβ is activated in a ligand-independent manner. Comparison of the AF1 domains of the 2 ERs has revealed that, in ERα, this domain actively stimulates reporter-gene expression from a variety of ERE-reporter constructs in different cell lines, but that the activity of the AF-1 domain of ERβ under the same conditions is negligible. , , 
To investigate factors affecting the expressions of ERs in epithelial cells, IL-4 and EGF were selected to stimulate BEAS-2B cells in vitro. IL-4, a typical type 2 cytokine of asthma, has frequently been used to simulate the type 2 inflammatory environment of asthma and has proven to be effective in damaging the barrier of the epithelium. In addition, EGF can be secreted on the basolateral side and blocked by the intact barrier of the airway epithelium. EGF is elevated in asthmatic patients and induces airway remodeling especially when the tight junctions of epithelium are disrupted by type 2 cytokines (IL-4) or other stimulants, permitting basolateral EGF to reach and activate the apical EGFR. , This study demonstrated a synergistic effect of IL-4 and EGF on improving the expression of ERα in the airway epithelium at an earlier time-point and with longer duration in vitro. This could be associated with the IL-4 enhanced phosphorylation of EGFR, which further resulted in the earlier and greater effect on increased ERα expression under the co-stimulation of EGF and IL-4. Moreover, there was a trend toward positive correlations between the expression of ERα in epithelial cells and the expression of IL-5 mRNA in BALF cells (r = 0.3844, P = 0.0853, ). In OVA-induced asthmatic mouse models, the ERα expression of epithelia was positively correlated with the expressions of both IL-4 and IL-13 mRNA expression ( ). It could be speculated that the co-stimulation of type 2 inflammation and EGF could promote ERα expression. However, the expression of ERβ showed significant change after exposure to IL-4 neither in vitro nor in the OVA-induced asthma mouse model, indicating that type 2 cytokines alone may not affect the expression of ERβ in the airway epithelium. Males generally have extremely low levels of estrogen, but male asthma patients had higher levels of ERα, indicating that ERα could participate in the pathogenesis of asthma via the ligand-independent pathway.
Consistent with previous studies, , mice challenged with OVA had higher EGF mRNA expression in lung tissues compared to controls ( ). In asthma, epithelial EGF is responsible for airway remodeling characterized by increased mucous metaplasia, subepithelial fibrosis, and EMT through the EGFR signaling pathway. , , , , Moreover, we observed that EGF enhanced ERα expression and promoted ERα nuclear translocation in bronchial epithelial cells. Previous studies have demonstrated that ERα could be phosphorylated at the Ser118 by EGF and then be activated (i.e., ligand-independent ERα activation pathway) in mammary cancer cells. Similarly, in this study, it was shown that EGF could phosphorylate ERα at Ser118 via the ERK and JNK signal pathways in epithelial cells. , 
It has been demonstrated that ERα-deficient mice had no change in airway inflammation , and that female ERα-knockout mice exposed to the mixed allergen had decreased airway remodeling compared to wild-type mice. Airway remodeling comprises thickening of the basement membrane accompanied by sub-epithelial fibrosis, epithelial shedding, and smooth muscle hypertrophy/hyperplasia. , In this study, it was found that the expression of ERα on epithelial cells was closely associated with the thickness of epithelium and basement membrane, but rarely correlated to airway inflammation. Moreover, knockdown of ERα blunted EMT and mucus production in vitro. Since EMT can contribute to airway remodeling and then lead to poor asthma control, it is concluded that ERα can enhance airway remodeling in asthma by promoting EMT and mucus production of epithelial cells. Airway inflammation is a consequence of combined and complicated inflammatory changes in structural and immune cells of the airways with structural cells. Previous studies have indicated that ERs can be expressed in structural and immune cells, but that the ERs in structural cells including epithelia and ASM cells were not significantly associated with airway inflammation. , , The role of ERs in the interactions between airway structure cells and immune cells need to be further investigated.
In this study, even though the enrolled controls were younger than the asthmatic patients, there was no correlation between age and expression of ERα and ERβ in asthmatic patients. Previous studies showed that female asthmatic mice suffered from more pronounced AHR, airway inflammation, and remodeling compared to males. , , In this study, no sex difference in airway inflammation and remodeling in the asthmatic mouse models ( , and ). This inconsistency could be due to different dosages of allergens (weight-specific dosages of OVA rather than equal dosages per mouse were used for sensitization in this study) and protocols of asthmatic mouse models.
There are some limitations in this study. First, a larger number of samples are needed to verify the expressions of ERα and ERβ in the asthmatic airway. Secondly, this study proved an important role of ERα activation via the ligand-independent pathway in affecting the airway remodeling and mucus, but the crosstalk between the ligand-dependent and -independent activation of ERα and the validations in animal models by knockout of ERα and ovariectomy method are needed in future studies. Thirdly, cells-specific expressions of ERα and ERβ in induced sputum were observed in this study, and the detailed pathways and mechanism of ERα and ERβ in lymphocytes and granulocytes need to be further investigated.
In summary, the expressions of ERα and ERβ in the airway epithelium and induced sputum of asthma patients may not show sex difference, and the increased expression of ERα in the epithelium can be associated with decreased lung function, severity, and airway remodeling of asthma patients. Activation of ERα through the independent-ligand pathway could be involved in EMT and mucus production of asthmatic airway epithelial cells. The results provide a novel insight into ERα, which may serve as a useful biomarker and a potential therapeutic target for asthma treatment.
