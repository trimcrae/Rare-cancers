## BACKGROUND/OBJECTIVES
Supplementation of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) precursors has been investigated for its potential to counteract age-related physiological decline. While decreased NAD+ level is associated with conditions such as obesity and dyslipidemia, few studies have explored its metabolic impact in relatively young animals. Here, we hypothesized that nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) could alleviate high-fat diet-induced metabolic stress in young mice and sought to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

## MATERIALS/METHODS
We first conducted a secondary analysis of hepatic transcriptome datasets from multiple NMN intervention studies to identify target pathways. Next, 11-week-old lean and obese C57BL/6N mice administrated NMN (500 mg/kg/day) for 1 mon were analyzed for metabolic parameters and gene expression for the target pathways. In vitro study using AML12 cell line was then conducted to confirm the effects of NMN. Additionally, transcriptomic data from liver-specific nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT)-deficient mice were analyzed to examine the relationship between NAD+ metabolism, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and circadian rhythm.

## RESULTS
The secondary analysis of hepatic transcriptome datasets revealed potential target pathways related to circadian regulation and hepatic ER stress. In mouse study, short-term NMN administration significantly decreased body weight and fat mass. NMN also alleviated hepatic ER stress response in vivo and in vitro. Additional analysis revealed that the mice with diet-induced metabolic stress or NAMPT deficiency led to phase shifts of the hepatic circadian core clock oscillation by advancing the peak time, while time-restricted feeding shifted the opposite, indicating the metabolic regulation of the circadian phase.

## CONCLUSION
Short-term NMN administration effectively attenuated obese condition in young mice and mitigated hepatic ER stress. The altered ER stress was regulated by NAMPT, potentially through the phase shift of the hepatic circadian rhythm.

## INTRODUCTION
Metabolic disorders are no longer confined to older adults; their prevalence is increasingly observed among younger populations, largely driven by sedentary lifestyles and the widespread adoption of Western-style, high-calorie diets [ ]. This shift has been accompanied by a rise in key indicators of metabolic syndrome, such as dyslipidemia and abdominal obesity [ ]. Early-onset metabolic abnormalities not only accelerate biological aging but also elevate the long-term risk of chronic diseases. As a result, there is growing interest in dietary supplements marketed to enhance physiological function and reduce disease risk [ ].
Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) is an endogenous molecule that plays a central role in metabolism. In yeast, increased NAD+ levels induced by calorie restriction have been shown to extend lifespan [ ]. As a well-established cofactor for enzymes involved in energy production and various metabolic pathways, NAD+ is also recognized as an important component in human nutrition and metabolic health [ ]. NAD+ can be synthesized de novo from dietary tryptophan through the kynurenine pathway, but it is mainly synthesized through the NAD+ salvage pathway, which recycles nicotinamide (NAM) by the action of NAD+-consuming enzymes, such as sirtuin (SIRT) and poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) [ ]. Considering the important contribution of the NAD+ salvage pathway to NAD+ synthesis [ ], nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase (NAMPT), the first rate-limiting enzyme in the salvage pathway, is essential in maintaining the NAD+ level and relies on 2 main precursors nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) and nicotinamide riboside (NR) [ ]. The physiological benefits of boosting NAD+ metabolism have been recently explored, particularly in middle-aged and older humans [ ] and mice [ ]. Boosting NAD+ through NMN or NR treatment has been shown to decrease blood inflammatory cytokines [ ], maintain the biological age of blood [ ], improve insulin sensitivity, as well as increase NAD+ levels [ ]. In mouse models, 20-week NR supplementation reduced liver fibrosis in middle-aged mice [ ]. One week of NMN administration improved metabolic function in aged-induced type 2 diabetic mice [ ], and long-term supplementation has been associated with delayed aging [ ]. Moreover, liver-specific deprivation of NAMPT weakened fatty liver disease in middle-aged mice [ ].
Despite the promising biological benefits of boosting NAD+ for healthy aging and disease prevention, research in younger adults remains limited. Given the link between NAD+ decline and other metabolic diseases, such as obesity, dyslipidemia, and hepatic steatosis [ ], enhancing NAD+ level may also confer metabolic benefits in younger individuals experiencing metabolic stress. Based on the hypothesis that short-term NMN administration has metabolic effects in young mice, we investigated the effects of NMN on the hepatic metabolism of control diet (CD) and high-fat diet (HFD)-fed young mice.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Hepatic secondary data analysis of publicly available transcriptome data

## Differential expression analysis of the murine liver following long-term NMN supplementation
To investigate potential molecular targets of NMN, we analyzed the microarray dataset GSE85718, obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gds/). This dataset was used to investigate effects of long-term NMN supplementation in an aged mouse model. In this data, 5-mon-old wild type mice were fed CD ad libitum, and mice were administered 300 mg/kg/day NMN in drinking water for 6 or 12 mon. To analyze this dataset, log2 fold changes (log2FC) were calculated following quantile normalization. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) by 6- and 12-mon supplementation were identified using the thresholds of |log2FC| > 1.5 and P-value < 0.05. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA v. 4.2.0; Broad Institute, Cambridge, MA, USA) was conducted with expression levels of all probes from both 6- and 12-mon intervention, and transcription factor (TF) analysis for DEGs of 12-mon intervention was performed using the EnrichR website (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/).

## Rhythmicity analysis of hepatic transcriptome data for validation
In order to validate our results, we analyzed rhythmicity of multiple publicly available transcriptomic data of the liver from 3 mouse models: 1) Hepatic RNA sequencing data (GSE133989) of the liver-specific Nampt knockout (KO) mice were obtained from the GEO dataset. The data were normalized with DESeq2's median of ratios method using the DESeq2 R package (v. 1.34.0; EMBL, Heidelberg, Germany), then log-transformed. 2) Microarray dataset GSE52333 was used for the analysis of DEGs oscillated by HFD feeding. Probe-level summarized data were downloaded from the GEO dataset, and quantile-normalized for circadian statistics. 3) Hepatic time-restricted feeding (TRF) data (GSE190389) was obtained from the GEO dataset. A quality check was done using FASTQC (v. 0.11.9; Babraham Institute, Babraham, England). Selective alignment was conducted with Salmon (v. 1.2.0; Stony Brook University, Stony Brook, NY, USA), and the transcript-level count was imported using the R package tximport (v. 1.26.1; University of Zurich, Zurich, Switzerland).
Rhythmicity graphs were created using the R package LimoRhyde2 (v. 0.1.0; Vanderbilt University, Nashville, TN, USA), and the JTK_CYCLE analysis was conducted for circadian statistics using JTK_CYCLE v. 3.1 in R (v. 4.1.1; Yale University, New Haven, CT, USA).

## Animal experiments
The animal study protocol was approved and reviewed by Ewha Laboratory Animal Genomic Center (EWHA IACUC 21-083-3). Four-week-old 32 C57BL/6N male mice (RaonBio, Yongin, Korea) were purchased and maintained with 12 h light/12 h dark cycles. After 1 week of adaptation, mice were randomly divided into 2 groups (n = 16) and fed CD (AIN-93G, D10012G; RaonBio) and HFD (D12451; RaonBio) for 6 weeks. Ingredients of experimental diets are shown in . From week 7, NMN (Oriental Yeast Co., Tokyo, Japan) was intraperitoneally injected (500 mg/kg/day) every other day for 1 mon (n = 8 for each group). Body weight and food intake were measured every week. The intraperitoneal glucose tolerance test (IPGTT) was conducted after 15 h fasting. At 15 weeks old, mice were sacrificed by carbon dioxide inhalation at zeitgeber time (ZT) 12. Liver tissue was collected for molecular analysis.
 

## 
In vitro experiments
AML12 cells were maintained in a growth medium containing Dulbecco's modified essential medium (DMEM, LM002-08; Welgene, Gyeongsan, Korea) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Corning, Glendale, AZ, USA), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA), 0.1% insulin-transferrin-selenium (Corning), 40 ng/mL dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and 15 mM HEPES (Welgene). For siRNA transfection, cells were transfected with either nontargeting control siRNA (D-001810-10-05; Dharmacon, Lafayette, CO, USA) or Nampt siRNA (M-040272-00-0005; Dharmacon). The transfection was performed once or twice during the experiments using the DharmaFECT1 reagent (T-2001-03; Dharmacon). Transfection knockdown efficacy was confirmed after 48 h treatment in all experiments. For NMN treatment, NMN (Oriental Yeast Co.) was dissolved in PBS and diluted in culture medium to final concentrations of 0.1 or 1 mM NMN. Cells were seeded on 96-well plates and harvested after 48 h NMN treatment for RNA isolation.

## RNA isolation and quantitative reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was extracted from mouse liver and cells by TRIzol reagent (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA) according to the accompanying manual. Total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA, which was then used for qPCR analysis with specific primers, the sequences of which are available upon request. Gene expression was normalized to the mouse Tbp gene. Rotor-Gene Q (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) equipment and the QuantiNova SYBR Green PCR Kit (Qiagen) or AccuPower 2X GreenStar qPCR Master Mix (Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea) were used according to the accompanying manual.

## Statistical analysis
All results are presented as mean ± SE of the mean or minimum, 25th percentile, median, 75th percentile, and maximum, showing individual points in each graph. Statistical analysis of all experiments was conducted with at least 3 biological replicates, and statistical significance was determined at P-value < 0.05 by 2-way analysis of variance (SAS v. 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

## RESULTS

## Transcriptomic identification of hepatic targets of NMN
To explore possible cellular targets of NMN in the liver, we analyzed hepatic transcriptomic data of mice with NMN supplementation. Firstly, we investigated DEGs ( ) and potential TFs to drive the differential expression upon a long-term NMN supplementation. Among about 25K probes, expression of 155 and 196 genes were altered following 6-mon and 12-mon supplementation, respectively ( ). The pathway analysis using GSEA revealed significant downregulation of the unfolded protein response pathway ( ). Notably, this included a marked reduction in heat shock protein family A (Hsp70) member 5 (HSPA5/BiP), a key regulator of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, suggesting that ER stress may be modulated by NAD+ metabolism. In addition, the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids was also significantly downregulated, in line with previous studies reporting a long-term NMN-induced reduction in hepatic triglyceride level [ ].
 TF analysis of upregulated genes revealed 2 main components in the circadian clock, namely aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator-like protein 1 (ARNTL, also known as BMALl) and circadian locomoter output cycles protein kaput (CLOCK) (the left panel, ), indicating that NMN was associated with enhancing circadian rhythm regulation. The analysis of downregulated genes showed HIC1 and TFDP1 as top candidates of transcriptional factors, followed by DNA damage-inducible transcript 3 (DDIT3) (the right panel, ). These results motivated to investigate the association between the effects of short-term NMN and possible target pathways, including the ER stress response and circadian rhythm in the liver.

## Phenotypic effects of NMN administration in young mice
To determine the effects of short-term NMN in young mice, an animal experiment was conducted ( ). We chose a dose level of 500 mg/kg/day due to its confirmed therapeutic effects on diabetes and age-related metabolic disorders [ ]. As expected, HFD-fed mice exhibited higher body weight compared to CD-fed mice, which short-term NMN administration significantly reduced in both diet conditions ( ). The accumulation of fat pads (gonadal white adipose tissue and inguinal white adipose) was also significantly repressed by NMN ( ).
 In terms of diet intake, HFD-fed mice consumed less food (g/day) but had a higher calorie intake (kcal/day) ( ) compared to the CD group, and NMN administration lowered food intake in both diet conditions. The alteration of water intake by NMN was dependent on the diets ( ), with a marginal increase in the CD-fed groups (P = 0.051) and no change in the HFD-fed groups. Results from the IPGTT showed HFD-induced glucose intolerance, with NMN not significantly affecting glucose levels ( ), though a tendency of marginal reduction of glucose levels upon NMN was observed particularly around 1:00−1:30 h.

## Changes in hepatic NAD+ metabolism by NMN administration
To determine the molecular effects of NMN, we initially explored hepatic NAD+ metabolism, which is briefly described ( ). We first checked the gene expression of nicotinamide mononucleotide adenylyltransferases (Nmnat1 and Nmnat2), which utilize NMN as a substrate. Nmnat1 expression was decreased in the HFD-fed groups and increased by short-term NMN in both diet conditions ( ). In contrast, Nmnat2 expression was reduced by NMN administration but remained unchanged by HFD feeding ( ). Although HFD feeding did not change Nampt expression, short-term NMN significantly decreased its expression under both diet conditions ( ). Gene expression of nicotinamide N-methyltransferase (Nnmt), which was associated with fatty liver [ ], was induced in the HFD-fed group compared to the CD-fed group (P = 0.022, ). With short-term NMN administration, Nnmt expression was not altered in CD-fed mice but significantly decreased in HFD-fed mice (P = 0.018). The results suggested that NMN alleviated the induction of Nnmt expression caused by HFD feeding.
 

## Attenuation of ER stress response by NMN in vivo and in vitro
To investigate whether short-term NMN alleviates HFD-induced ER stress, we analyzed the hepatic mRNA expression of ER stress-related genes. NMN significantly reduced most of the ER stress-regulated genes in HFD-fed mice, except the X-box binding protein (Xbp1) gene ( ). The results suggested that short-term NMN administration alleviated HFD-induced hepatic ER stress, possibly through the PRKR-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) and activating transcription factor 6-signaling pathways rather than the inositol-requiring enzyme 1-signaling pathway, which targets Xbp1. We conducted an experiment in vitro to confirm the effects of NMN treatment on the alleviation of ER stress using the AML12 cell line ( ). The 1 mM NMN significantly decreased the expression of Hspa5 and Ddit3, indicating that NMN effectively attenuated ER stress response in vivo and in vitro.
 

## Alteration of ER stress by Nampt knockdown
Then, we knocked down NAMPT using siRNA to assume a blockage of NMN production, and we confirmed that the knockdown efficiency was 70%−80% ( ). We observed that Nampt knockdown significantly induced expression of ER stress-related genes, particularly Hspa5 and Ddit3 ( ), which is consistent with the ER stress response-associated genes that also appeared affected by NMN ( ).
 Interestingly, alteration of NAMPT function might link to changes in oscillation of ER stress response ( ). The liver-specific Nampt KO mice exhibited changes in oscillation of ER stress response genes. JTK_CYCLE analysis ( ) confirmed that the peak time of the Hspa5 and Ddit3 genes was 6 h phase-advanced and 2 h delayed, respectively, indicating that hepatic Nampt knockdown led to alteration of the ER stress response. Collectively, NAMPT has a role in the ER stress response, which was confirmed in vivo and in vitro.
 

## Phase shifts of circadian core clock genes under genetic modification- or diet-induced ER stress
In light of the increased transcriptional activity of circadian core factors with NMN supplementation revealed by the secondary data analysis ( ), and disrupted oscillation of ER stress-response genes by liver-specific Nampt deletion ( ), we further analyzed whether the circadian clock was involved in the regulation of the ER stress response. We investigated the hepatic oscillation patterns of circadian clock genes in liver-specific Nampt KO mice and HFD-fed mice, which showed disrupted ER stress response. Intriguingly, both data showed similar patterns of oscillation across the circadian clock genes ( ). The Nampt deletion caused phase shift of all the major circadian clock genes ( ), confirmed by circadian statistics ( ). The HFD-fed mice showed a clear advanced phase-shift of the circadian clock gene pattern ( ), confirmed by circadian statistics ( ). The peak time of Arntl/Bmal1 and period circadian regulator 2 (Per2) genes was statistically advanced, and the period length of Clock and D-box binding PAR BZIP transcription factor (Dbp) was altered, similar to those of Nampt KO mice ( ). Notably, both models showed only minimal phase shifts of the circadian rhythm rather than complete loss of its circadian clock genes.
 Then, using GSE190389, we investigated whether TRF, which has been reported to prevent metabolic disorders caused by HFD [ ], recovers the advanced phase shift. Arntl and Dbp gene expression showed phase delay, and Clock and Per2 oscillation became more distinct with increased amplitude in the TRF group ( ). Circadian statistics in showed delayed peak times of all circadian clock genes. Therefore, metabolic stress and TRF have opposite impacts on the phase shift of the hepatic circadian clock.

## DISCUSSION
We investigated the effects of short-term NMN administration on hepatic metabolism in a young mouse model. Short-term NMN affects the physiological state of the mild obese condition in young mice, especially by reducing the weights of the body and fat pads. The molecular analysis revealed that the enhancement of the NAD+ pathway through NMN alleviated hepatic ER stress, both in vivo and in vitro. Moreover, ER stress caused phase shifts of the circadian core clock gene oscillations in both genetic and diet-induced models. These findings suggest that NMN has the potential for application in preventing chronic diseases in younger adults via regulating circadian clock and ER stress response.
Our initial screening of the pathways primarily targeted by NMN suggested that NMN induced changes in the ER stress response pathway and enrichment of circadian clock-controlled genes using the aged mice ( ). However, the screening was conducted using a dataset from long-term interventions lasting 6 or 12 mon, which may differ from a short-term intervention planned for our animal experiment. To assess whether these molecular effects also emerge in short-term NMN administration, we further analyzed an independent publicly available transcriptomic dataset from a HFD-induced obese mouse model treated with NMN for one week (GSE31647). In this short-term intervention expermient, ARNTL was again identified as a significantly enriched TF (data not shown), supporting the idea that NMN can alter the transcriptional activities of genes that drive the circadian rhythm regardless of the type of diet and duration of NMN supplementation. Given the overlap in key TFs identified in both short- and long-term NMN treatment datasets, we suspected that the molecular targets of NMN treatment might be conserved irrespective of treatment duration. Based on these observations, we directly examined the effect of NMN on the candidate pathways under short-term NMN treatment conditions.
The ER stress response pathway was confirmed as a target of NMN in vivo and in vitro ( ). It was consistent with a previous in vitro study that NAM treatment ameliorated palmitic acid-induced ER stress [ ]. ER stress, induced by metabolic stress, contributes to metabolic diseases such as diabetes, cancer, and cardiovascular disease [ ]. In our study, the NMN effect was observed in HFD-fed mice that is one of factors to have low NAD+ level [ ], but not CD-fed mice, which had low levels of ER stress ( ). Hepatic Nampt deletion model supported that NAMPT was required for proper ER stress response, which was confirmed by showing dramatic alterations to their expression in the Nampt-deleted models ( ). Interestingly, the alteration was not simply differential expression but rather changes in the oscillating patterns of the genes, indicating that NMN affected the circadian rhythm. This was supported by analyzing the publicly available hepatic Bmal1 deletion dataset, confirming disruption of circadian rhythm, and showing aberrant expression patterns of ER stress-response genes ( ). Especially, activating transcription factor 4 (Atf4) expression was upregulated in the KO mice, and the oscillating patterns of the other ER stress-response genes were notably changed compared to wildtype (WT) mice. These results suggest that circadian controlling components might mediate regulation of the ER stress response.
A link between NAD+ metabolism and circadian regulation has been well established [ ]. SIRT1, a NAD+-dependent deacetylase, is recruited to the promoter of NAMPT leading to regulate the activity of the circadian clock. The NAD+-mediated circadian regulation, accompanied by the result from transcriptomic analysis ( ), supports the possibility that circadian controlling components might influence ER stress response. Earlier studies suggested that Clock gene might be involved in liver aging through ER stress pathway [ ], and ER stress might affect the transcription of circadian clock genes via ATF4-dependent manner [ ]. Interestingly, restoration of the hepatic clock in global Bmal1 KO mice exhibited recovered oscillation of metabolites of NAD+ metabolism to the WT level [ ], indicating that the hepatic circadian clock regulates NAD+ metabolism. These lines of evidence suggest that the relationship between NAD+ metabolism and circadian regulation is not unidirectional but rather involves reciprocal interactions through feedback mechanisms.
To validate the link, we analzyed circadian gene regulation in the liver-specific Nampt-deficient mouse and found only marginal phase shift in oscillation of circadian clock components ( ). Considering calorie restriction-mimicking effect of NAD+ boosting [ ], the lack of NAMPT might be similar to metabolic stress by excessive energy intake. Indeed, HFD altered oscillating patterns of circadian clock genes [ ] and particularly resulted in advanced phase shift of liver clock, which was reversible by change to CD [ ]. The results consistently indicated HFD-induced changes in hepatic circadian clock machinery, especially with phase shifts. Moreover, TRF, the release of the metabolic stress, appeared to recover this pattern ( ). As a result of investigating hepatic gene expression of circadian clock in NMN-administered mice in our study, we observed no significant alteration ( ). Nevertheless, expression of Per2 showed an interaction between the diet and NMN administration ( ), similar to the patterns we expected according to the phase shifts in the Nampt KO mice and HFD-fed mice models ( ). Taken together, this suggested that short-term NMN administration did not dramatically change the hepatic oscillating patterns of the circadian clock genes and, if any, was limited to circadian phase shifts.
NAD+ metabolism is significantly affected by short-term NMN administration ( ), however, NMN administration did not alter the total NAD+ levels or the NAD+/NADH ratio in the liver ( ). Although their increase was anticipated because NAD+ is the downstream product of NMN, another report showed a limited impact of NMN on the hepatic NAD+ level, even with long-term supplementation [ ]. A possible reason for this might be a feature of NAD+ retaining the balanced state even if NAD+ salvage pathway changes occur [ ]. NMN administration enhanced Nmnat1 expression and reduced Nmnat2 expression ( ). This corresponds to alcohol-induced liver injury, where hepatic Nmnat1 expression was recovered by NR [ ]. Although the metabolic function of hepatic NMNAT2 is not yet known, hepatic NMNAT1 and NMNAT2 may have opposite functions considering that compartmentalized NMNAT1 and NMNAT2 in preadipocytes are involved in inhibiting and promoting adipose differentiation, respectively [ ]. In terms of Nampt expression, short-term NMN significantly decreased its expression under both diet conditions ( ). This downregulation likely reflects a feedback response in which sufficient NMN supply reduces the need for endogenous NMN production via Nampt expression, since its protein level showed tendency to increase by NMN administration ( ). Interestingly, hepatic expression of Nampt gene is known to show circadian oscillation, which peaks at ZT12 [ ], the onset of the dark phase when mice become metabolically active. The discrepancy between the decreased Nampt mRNA and increased protein levels might be due to Nampt's own oscillation [ ]. HFD significantly induced NAMPT protein expression ( ). Similarly, HFD-induced hepatic expression of NAMPT was previously reported, which was demonstrated as an adaptive mechanism to HFD with up-regulation of SIRT1 expression and consequential mitochondrial oxidation [ ]. In contrast, chronic HFD feeding decreased NAMPT expression [ ], suggesting that changes in the NAMPT level depend on the duration of HFD feeding. Genetically obese mice exhibit increased Nnmt expression, showing that Nnmt expression level is associated with fatty liver [ ]. This was consistent with its up-regulation by HFD feeding ( ), which might be mediated by the ER stress response [ ].
In addition, NMN administration significantly reduced body weight in both diet conditions ( ), similarly with previous research in aged mice [ ] and in HFD-fed mice [ ]. Body weight reduction might be attributed to the loss of fat ( ). However, 12-mon NMN supplementation significantly decreased body weight but with a minimal decrease in fat mass percentages [ ]. The notable loss of fat pads was likely to be a short-term, not long-term, effect. NMN administration has a marginal effect on improving glucose tolerance ( ). Previously, 1-week NMN significantly improved glucose tolerance in mice fed with chronic HFD [ ], contrary to a long-term NMN supplementation, which did not show differences in the glucose tolerance test of mice during the aging process [ ]. Therefore, NMN effects on glucose tolerance differ depending on metabolic status and duration of the administration. NMN administration reduced both food and calorie intake in both diet conditions ( ), which might partially cause the body weight reduction. However, long-term NMN supplementation enhanced food intake in aging mice [ ], implying that NMN might affect appetite depending on the metabolic status and duration of NMN supplementation, which have yet to be studied. NMN administration increased water intake, particularly in CD-fed mice ( ). This was consistent with long-term NMN-supplemented aged mice showing enhanced water intake when fed CD during the aging process [ ]. Further investigation is needed because high water intake is usually considered to provide metabolically beneficial effects [ ]. It is also required to examine side effects of NAD+ precursors to consider uses of the supplement in humans. In animal studies, long-term NMN-supplemented mice did not show any side effects [ ] and oral administration of NMN was also well-tolerated [ ]. Clinical research reported that both NR and NMN supplementation was well-tolerated in humans [ ].
There are several remaining questions. Considering the differential effects of compartmentalized NAD+ on metabolism [ ], additional intracellular compartmentalized NAD+ level measurement is required to determine how the effects of NMN are mediated through changes in the NAD+ pathway. Moreover, further investigation is necessary to determine whether NMN-induced mitigation of ER stress was directly or indirectly regulated by the circadian rhythm. Nevertheless, our study demonstrated the short-term effects of NMN administration on weight and fat loss of mild obese young mice by alleviating hepatic ER stress. The oscillation of hepatic ER stress genes was modulated by NAMPT, possibly through a circadian clock phase shift within the liver.
