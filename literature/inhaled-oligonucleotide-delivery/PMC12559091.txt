Years of basic research conducted worldwide have allowed to identify potential mechanisms involved in brain damage after an asphyxia episode, but effective therapies are still lacking [ ]. Neonatologists still only use therapeutic hypothermia (TH); however, in developing countries, this method is not widely available and, above all, not all cases qualify for it [ ]. There is an urgent need of introducing supportive or alternative therapies to TH to enable normal motor and cognitive development [ ]. The problem is so significant that children affected by asphyxia will, to varying degrees, require specialized care from physicians and therapists throughout their lives. Among the consequences of severe asphyxia are cerebral palsy [ ], epilepsy [ ], or intellectual disability [ ]. HI episode may also increase the risk of developing autism spectrum disorders (ASD) [ ]. This places a serious burden on their families and the health care system.
HIE covers the major joint symptom of the wide spectrum of asphyxia-correlated developmental disorders. The patterns of the injury depend on the duration and severity of hypoxia and the maturity stage of the brain. The predominant form of pathophysiological changes in HIE (over 40% of affected children) is cerebral white matter injury (WMI) including reduced total white matter volume and altered myelin microstructure [ , ]. The white matter consists of tracts of axons enwrapped with myelin. Myelin sheath is essential for efficient and fast transmission of nerve impulses which is required for effective functioning of the central nervous system. Myelin segments are in fact the large extensions of oligodendrocytes. The process leading to myelination is an energy-consuming strategy, involving strictly timed onset of proliferation, migration, differentiation, and maturation of oligodendrocyte progenitor cells [ ]. Oligodendrogenesis and myelination are regulated through sequence of changes in the expression and activity of specific transcription factors and epigenetic modifications [ ]. Therefore, any alterations to the neural tissue microenvironment may affect proper oligodendrocyte differentiation and white matter development.
The rat model of perinatal asphyxia was developed by Rice and Vanucci [ ]. Their research initiated years of research into the pathophysiological mechanisms of neonatal brain damage. Since Back et al. established the similarities of oligodendrocyte maturation between rodents and humans [ ], researches got valuable models to study pathologies in OPCs and myelination accompanying HIE in rat model. Among factors involved in developing HIE, it is now a general agreement that they include excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, and inflammatory response with microglial activation that can broadly modulate every cell’s fate [ ]. But there are not many reports concerning possible involvement of signaling through HIF-1α pathway. In our previous studies, we demonstrated that in a rat model of neonatal HI, oligodendrocytes are affected by the injury through an unspecified pathway. Oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) proliferated intensely shortly after HI; they were able to produce myelin proteins, but the myelination process was insufficient [ ]. We confirmed these results in the in vitro model, mimicking HI with oxygen–glucose deprivation (OGD) procedure on oligodendrocyte rat primary cells as well as rat hippocampal slice cultures [ , ]. Moreover, we studied the biology of oligodendrocytes to define the optimal culture conditions for primary OPCs. Comparing cultures maintained at normoxia for neural tissue (5% O2) and standard culture conditions (21% O2), we observed a strong impact of oxygen on oligodendrocyte growth. Although differentiation and maturation in both conditions proceeded properly, normoxia promoted cell proliferation and supported the abundance of the progenitor fraction in the cultures, while in standard conditions, OPCs earlier expressed markers of mature OLs. Moreover, immature oligodendrocytes in 5% O2 had more complex morphology, by means of higher average number of intersections in Sholl analyses of immature oligodendrocytes, but the enclosing radius indicating average cell’s size was significantly bigger in cells cultured in 21% O2, which may indicate their improved differentiation [ ].
Considering the fact that both the acute delimitation of access to oxygen and glucose during OGD as well as culture of oligodendrocytes under different aerobic conditions affect their growth, we decided to further explore the contribution of the HIF-1α signaling pathway in the observed effects. HIFs (hypoxia-induced factors) are basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factors that are the main regulators of cell response to hypoxic conditions. HIF-1α is an oxygen-sensitive molecule, which in normoxic conditions is continuously degraded by the proteasome, while under hypoxia, an enzyme HIF-prolyl-hydroxylase, which induces its degradation, is inhibited. This causes stabilization of HIF-1α and its translocation to the nucleus, where it dimerizes with HIF-1β (ARNT). Complex binds to hypoxia response element, the specific site on DNA, and activates the expression of genes further involved in adaptation to hypoxia, including those associated with glucose metabolism and angiogenesis [ ].
In the following study, we aimed to verify the effect of treatments applied directly after OGD injury to primary oligodendrocyte progenitor cells. We evaluated whether further stimulation of hypoxia with cobalt chloride (CoCl2) or its inhibition with KC7F2 which suppresses HIF-1α accumulation through inhibition of its translation may be beneficial for impaired oligodendrocyte maturation. Obtained results will provide a valuable background to instigate novel research questions about direct or indirect regulation of oligodendrogenesis, through a possible hypoxia-mediated pathway. Until now, therapeutic hypothermia (TH) is still the only one treatment strategy of asphyxiated newborns proved to have a beneficial effect, through reducing neuronal death, but it does not reduce white matter injury [ ]. We aim to provide to the neuroscience community the confirmation if modulation of hypoxia-signaling may be considered as possible target for novel therapies of hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy.

## Methods

## In Vivo Model of Rat Neonatal Hypoxia–Ischemia
The in vivo model of neonatal hypoxia–ischemia (HI) was induced in P7 rat pups (N = 8), according to the procedure described by Rice et al. [ ] and adapted by Ziemka-Nalecz et al. [ ]. Briefly, rats were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane (Iso-Vet, Piramal Critical Care B.V.) in oxygen (induction 4% and maintenance 2%), and the left common carotid artery was prepared and double-ligated inducing unilateral cerebral ischemia. Rats were caged with the dam for 1 h and then placed in a hypoxic chamber (7.6% O2 in N2, 33.2 °C) for 60 min. After HI induction, animals were returned to the cage, with access to water and food ad libitum, under artificial light conditions. Control animals (N = 8) underwent sham surgery which involved keeping the animals under anesthesia at the same time as the operated animals. Twenty-four hours after injury induction, animals were sacrificed to isolate brain hemispheres for immunoblotting. Seventy-two hours after injury, animals were sacrificed and brains were isolated and snap-frozen for immunofluorescent microscopic imaging. Brains were cut into 10-μm-thick slices. Sections covering hippocampal formation were placed on silane-coated slides, dried, and then fixed in 4% PFA (paraformaldehyde; MP Biomedicals) in PBS (phosphate-buffered saline; Gibco).

## OPC Isolation and Culture
OPCs were obtained from 1- to 2-day-old Wistar rat pups of both sexes (N = 70, of an average 67.5% female and 32.5% male rats per isolation). An average weight of the animal was 7.34 ± 1.02 g and an average weight of the brain was 0.227 ± 0.03 g. Cells were isolated with the method of magnetic sorting, according to the protocol described in details elsewhere [ ]. Briefly, brain hemispheres were dissociated with the Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit P (Miltenyi), and the obtained cell suspension was labeled with anti-A2B5 magnetic microbeads (Miltenyi). After incubation, the suspension was applied onto columns placed in the magnetic field, which enabled removing cells that were not labeled with magnetic microbeads (negative fraction). Columns were washed with the PBSA solution (0.5% BSA [Bovine serum albumin; Sigma] in PBS [Gibco]) and taken from the magnetic field to allow the retrieval of A2B5-labeled cells (positive fraction). Obtained cells were cultured in medium allowing OPC proliferation, which consisted of MACS Neuro Medium (Miltenyi), 0.5 mM l-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich), 1% ITS (insulin-transferrin-selenium; Gibco), 1% AAS (antibiotic–antimycotic solution; Sigma-Aldrich), 10 ng/ml human PDGFAA (platelet-derived growth factor AA; Miltenyi), and 10 ng/ml mouse FGF-1 (fibroblast growth factor 1; Miltenyi). Cells were plated onto culture vessels coated with poly-l-lysine, at a density of approximately 3 × 104 cells/cm2. Eight-well culture chambers with plastic bottom (FLux, SPL) were used for immunofluorescence microscopy; six-well plates (Thermo Scientific Nunc) were used for gene expression study and protein analysis. Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% O2 and 5% CO2.

## OGD Procedure
Three to four days after isolation, cells were subjected to the procedure of oxygen–glucose deprivation (OGD), which mimics the hypoxic-ischemic injury in the in vitro conditions. Culture medium was removed and replaced with OGD buffer (124 mM NaCl; 4.8 mM KCl; 1.2 mM KH2PO4; 1.5 mM CaCl2; 1 mM MgCl2; 10 mM mannitol; 25 mM NaHCO3; all reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich) of pH 7.4 and deprived of dissolved oxygen by pumping a stream of N2 (95%)/CO2 (5%) gas mix into the solution for 10 min. Culture vessels were placed in the Modular Incubator Chamber (Billups-Rothenberg, Inc.), connected to N2 (95%)/CO2 (5%) gas mix for 10 min to remove an atmospheric oxygen, and then incubated at 37 °C for 40 min.

## Modifications of HIF-1α Activity
After the OGD procedure, the buffer was removed and a medium of the following composition was added: DMEM Glutamax high glucose (Gibco), 1% ITS (Gibco), and 1% AAS (Sigma-Aldrich). This medium is later named “blank.” In order to sustain hypoxic-mimicking conditions, blank medium was supplemented with 100 µM CoCl2 (cobalt chloride; Sigma-Aldrich). In order to inhibit the HIF-1α pathway, the culture medium was supplemented with 30 µM KC7F2 (N,Nʹ-[2,5-dichlorosulfonyl]cysteamine; Sigma-Aldrich).

## Protein Isolation and Analysis
In order to evaluate the effect of OGD on the expression of HIF-1α at different time points after HI in vivo and OGD in vitro, the Western Blot (WB) analysis of whole-cell lysates or nuclear and cytosolic cell fractions was performed. Whole-cell lysates were obtained with modified RIPA buffer supplemented with 1% Triton X-100 (Sigma) and cOmplete protease inhibitor (Roche) and sonicated with Bioruptor Plus (Diagenode). The separate cellular fractions were obtained with NE-PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Kit (Thermo Scientific) according to manufacturer’s protocol. Total protein concentration in samples was estimated with DC Protein assay (Bio-Rad). Samples of 15 µg (tissue lysates), 30 µg (whole cell lysates), or 10 µg (cell fractions) of protein in 4 × Laemli loading buffer (Bio-Rad) were separated by SDS-PAGE (7.5% polyacrylamide) and transferred onto 0.45-μm nitrocellulose (Amersham). Membranes were stained with 0.2% pons red solution to verify protein loading and estimate total protein for WB normalization. Images of stained membranes were collected with G-Box System (Syngene). The staining was then removed with washing in TBST. Membranes were blocked with 0.1% casein in TBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary HIF-1α antibody (rabbit polyclonal; AF1009; Affinity) was diluted 1:500 in TBST supplemented with 0.02% sodium azide and incubated with membranes overnight in cold room. After three washes in TBST, membranes were incubated in the solution of anti-rabbit secondary antibody (IRDye 800CW; 926–3221; LI-COR) in 0.1% casein in TBS + 0.02% Tween for 1 h in room temperature. Blots were detected in Odyssey FC system (LI-COR). Original uncropped blot images are provided in the Supplementary file .

## Immunofluorescent Microscopy
At selected time points after OGD cells were fixed with 4% PFA in PBS and labeled for immunofluorescence microscopy according to the detailed protocol described previously [ ]. Preparations were blocked with 10% normal goat serum (Sigma) in PBS supplemented with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Serva). Primary antibodies diluted in PBS were incubated overnight in 4 °C. List of primary antibodies included anti-NG2 (chondroitin sulfate proteoglycan 4, oligodendrocyte progenitor marker [1:100; Santa Cruz sc-20162]), anti-OLIG2 (basic-helix-loop-helix transcription factor, oligodendrocyte marker [1:500; Merck ABN899]), anti-MBP (myelin basic protein [1:100; Chemicon MAB381]), anti-Ki67 (marker of proliferation [1:100; Novocastra NCL-L-Ki67-MM1), and anti-HIF-1α ([1:200; Santa Cruz sc-13515]). Tissue sections were labeled with anti-HIF-1α (1:50; Santa Cruz sc-13515) and anti-PDGFRα (platelet-derived growth factor alpha, marker of oligodendrocyte progenitor cells; Santa Cruz, sc-338; 1:200). The corresponding secondary antibodies (goat anti-mouse or goat anti-rabbit), at a dilution 1:750, were applied for 2 h of incubation at room temperature in the dark. To label the cell nuclei, Hoechst 33258 solution in PBS (Sigma-Aldrich; 1:150) was applied for 15 min of incubation in the dark. Microscopic images were acquired with Cell Observer SD system (Zeiss) in the Laboratory of Advanced Microscopy Techniques, Mossakowski Medical Research Institute Polish Academy of Sciences. The degree of differentiation of cells expressing the mature oligodendrocyte marker MBP + was assessed by measuring the area occupied by individual cells in microscopic image and the average fluorescence intensity of these cells with the ImageJ software.

## Cell Viability Assays
Different assays were applied in order to evaluate the impact of OGD and modifications of HIF-1α activity on OPC viability. The activity of an enzyme lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) released to the culture medium from damaged or dead cells was evaluated with CyQUANT LDH Cytotoxicity Assay (Invitrogen). The volume of 50 µl of culture medium was collected 3 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 96 h after OGD, and the LDH activity was measured according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was read using Fluorostar Omega (BMG Labtech) at 490 nm and 680 nm. Results were normalized to the absorbance obtained in the same culture well in the first time point.
The ability of living cells to reduce resazurin to resorufin, as an indicator of cellular metabolic activity, was evaluated with PrestoBlue HS Cell Viability Reagent (Invitrogen). Immediately after OGD and 93 h after OGD, the culture medium was changed to the one containing 10% of the PrestoBlue solution. Cells were then incubated for 3 h in a cell culture incubator. Fluorescence was measured using Fluorostar Omega (BMG Labtech) at the following parameters: excitation 530 nm, emission 620 nm, gain 2800. Results were normalized to the mean fluorescence of cell culture medium of the same condition at 3-h time point.
Cell membrane permeability present in necrotic and late apoptotic cells was assessed with the propidium iodide (PI) incorporation assay. At 96 h after OGD, before fixing the cells for immunofluorescent microscopy, the propidium iodide solution (Invitrogen) was added to cell cultures at the final concentration 2 µg/ml for 5 min of incubation in the dark. Immunofluorescent labeling was then continued, or cultures were labeled with Hoechst and imaged directly. Results were acquired through counting PI-positive cells among all Hoechst-labeled cells in microscopic images. An average of five images were acquired from each culture condition, from two to three experiments.

## Gene Expression Analysis with qPCR
In order to evaluate the oligodendrocyte differentiation rate, the expression of selected oligodendrocyte-specific genes as well as Arnt and Hif-1α was evaluated with RT-qPCR (reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction). Total RNA was isolated 24 h after OGD using the column method, with NucleoSpin RNA Plus XS kit (Macherey Nagel) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Purity and concentration of the RNA solution was determined using a NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific) spectrophotometer. Five hundred nanograms of RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA with an iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Primers used in the qPCR reaction were synthesized in the DNA Sequencing Laboratory of Institute of Biochemistry and Biophysics of the Polish Academy of Sciences and validated previously [ ]. The genes and primer sequences used in the experiment are listed in the Supplementary file . The qPCR reaction was performed using a iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix reaction mixture (Bio-Rad) and an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast thermocycler according to the following protocol: polymerase activation and DNA denaturation (20 s/95 °C), 40 amplification cycles (denaturation 1 s/95 °C; annealing and plate read 20 s/60 °C), melting curve analysis (65–95 °C). Differences in gene expression were normalized with the B2M (beta-2 microglobulin) and Rpl13 (ribosomal protein L13; ribosomal protein L13) genes using the qBase model, taking into account reaction efficiency and two reference genes [ ].

## Statistical Analysis
Results of the obtained and analyzed data from three biological replicates (separate cell isolations) and appropriate number of technical repetitions in a given experiment are presented as bar plots, box and whisker plot, spaghetti plot, or heat map plot. Bar plot represents mean value with error bars reflecting standard deviation and dots representing single values. In box and whisker plots, the bottom edge of the box corresponds to the first quartile of the results obtained, the middle of the box corresponds to the median, the top edge of the box corresponds to the third quartile, whiskers represent the 10–90 percentile range of the values obtained, and values below and above the whiskers are drawn as individual dots. Spaghetti plot represent average values for the group with whiskers for standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad PRISM 10.0 software. For the analysis of the data obtained from microscopic images, the ROUT test (Q = 5%) was applied to identify and remove outliers. The normal distribution was tested with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Depending on the analyzed data, results were evaluated with one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s correction, two-way ANOVA, or non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s correction test. Differences at p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Details of the test used can be found in the figure legends.

## Results

## HIF-1α Expression in the Neural Tissue After Hypoxia–Ischemia In Vivo
In order to verify whether neonatal asphyxia is sufficient to induce HIF-1α overexpression, we first examined samples collected from animals exposed to the injury in the well-established rat model. HIF-1α was detected in samples with immunoblot. In protein samples collected from cortices and adjacent white matter of both hypoxic-ischemic (ipsilateral, left) and hypoxic (contralateral, right) hemispheres, there was a tendency toward an increased HIF-1α expression (Fig.  a). No differences were observed within hippocampi (Fig.  b). At the 3-day time point after HI, these differences were still observed and even more pronounced with immunofluorescence labelling of the tissue (Fig.  c). Interestingly, increased expression of HIF-1α within cortex and thalamus covered the same areas of the brain, where an increased expression of OPC marker, PDGFRα, was observed after the injury (Fig.  d). 

## HIF-1α Expression in OPCs After OGD In Vitro
HIF-1α was also detected in cultured OPCs, and it tended to increase immediately after finishing the OGD procedure (Fig.  a). In order to evaluate whether there is a nuclear translocation of this protein and its inhibited degradation, we measured HIF-1α content in the cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions of cell lysates 0.5 h and 2 h after OGD (Fig.  b) by means of immunoblotting. Obtained results were however not significant and inconclusive. In the following experiments, we decided to verify a nuclear translocation through confocal microscopy, with immunofluorescence labeling using anti-HIF-1α and anti-NG2 (OPCs marker). After OGD, we applied a chemical compound to sustain hypoxia-mimicking conditions (100 µM CoCl2) or to inhibit HIF-1α accumulation through inhibition of its translation through supplementation of culture medium with 30 µM KC7F2. We observed HIF-1α expression and counted cells with nuclear translocation 1 h (Fig.  c) and 3 h (Fig.  d) after OGD. Translocation was observed as early as 1 h after OGD, since over 10 times more HIF-1α+ nuclei were observed in OGD culture vs CT (Fig.  c; p < 0.0001). One hour after injury was also not yet enough to modify HIF-1α activity with CoCl2 neither KC7F2, since the proportion of HIF-1α+ nuclei was also significantly higher than in non-treated cultures (Fig.  c; blank-CT 1.4 ± 2.36% vs OGD-CoCl2 18.73 ± 13.04 [p < 0.0001]; blank-CT 1.4 ± 2.36% vs OGD-KC7F2 7.66 ± 5.07% [p < 0.01]). Exemplary images of immunofluorescence labeling of OPCs fixed 1 h after OGD are presented in Fig.  e–j, with split channels to better visualize subcellular localization of HIF-1α in applied conditions. Interestingly, besides the clear nuclear localization of HIF-1α in every OGD-treated culture, we can observe the withdrawal of cell protrusions after the injury (Fig.  h–j). Three hours after OGD, HIF-1α translocation, and thus transcriptional activity, was significantly reduced with KC7F2, comparing both to blank (Fig.  d; an average of 69% reduction of nuclear HIF-1α; p < 0.05) and CoCl2-supplemented OGD-treated cultures (Fig.  d; an average of 71% reduction of nuclear HIF-1α; p < 0.01). Exemplary images of immunofluorescence labeling of OPCs fixed 3 h after OGD are provided in the Supplementary Fig. 1. 

## HIF-1α Modulation Does Not Affect Oligodendrocyte Viability After OGD
We considered if applied treatments may affect the viability of cultured cells. In LDH release assay, the only variable of statistical significance was the time of sampling (Fig.  a; p < 0.0001). The amount of released enzyme did not differ between samples collected from different treatments in the same time point. Another way to evaluate culture condition was PrestoBlue assay, where the level of fluorescence corresponds to viability of culture or its cells’ metabolic activity. In this assay, 3 h after OGD, any cultures were affected, but 96 h after OGD, both CoCl2 and KC7F2 treatment to control cultures caused significant decrease in fluorescence, which means lower metabolic activity or induction of cell death (Fig.  b, p < 0.01 and p < 0.05 respectively). What is worth notice, neither OGD itself caused oligodendrocyte death, neither OGD-treated cultures were significantly different from OGD-blank, but considerable effect of OGD was observed in cultures treated with CoCl2 and KC7F2 (Fig.  b; normalized fluorescence value in arbitrary units; CT-CoCl2 vs OGD-CoCl2 0.85 ± 0.02 vs 0.907 ± 0.03 p < 0.05; CT-KC7F2 vs OGD-KC7F2 0.87 ± 0.03 vs 0.945 ± 0.034 p < 0.001). 
To verify the assumptions from LDH and PrestoBlue viability assays, we performed the immunofluorescence staining of proliferating cells with anti-Ki67 antibody (Fig.  a, b) and propidium iodide incorporation assay (Fig.  c). Six hours after OGD, an average of 28.6 ± 14.47% cells was dividing in control cultures in blank culture medium. OGD treatment followed by KC7F2 supplementation caused immediate inhibition in cell proliferation, to less than 10 ± 4.3% of dividing cells in culture (Fig.  a; p < 0.05). This compound actively inhibited oligodendrocyte proliferation in control cultures even 96 h after OGD (Fig.  b; p < 0.05). In OGD-treated cultures, this effect was not observed. Exemplary images of proliferating cells in all analyzed culture conditions at 96-h time point are provided in Fig.  d–i. Supplementary Fig. 2 provides exemplary images of cells analyzed at the 6-h time point. 
Lack of negative effect of applied treatments on culture viability was finally confirmed with propidium iodide incorporation assay, where any significant differences where observed (Fig.  c, j–o).

## HIF-1α Inhibition Promotes Oligodendrocyte Differentiation After OGD
Considering the potential use of the proposed strategies in a therapy of hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, it was most interesting to investigate, whether HIF-1α modifications can improve oligodendrocyte differentiation and maturation. To evaluate this, we performed immunofluorescence labeling with markers of OPCs (NG2), oligodendrocyte-lineage cells (OLIG2), and mature oligodendrocytes (MBP).
NG2-positive OPCs were counted from images collected after co-immunostaining with HIF-1α, as shown previously in exemplary images provided in Fig.  e–j. The highest percentage of OPCs 1 h after OGD was observed in OGD-CoCl2 cultures (almost 40 ± 13.8% of cells in culture with the expression of NG2), significantly more than in CT-CoCl2, OGD-blank, and OGD-KC7F2 cultures in this time point (Fig.  a). After a further 2 h. these differences were no longer significant; the cells slowly lost NG2 expression, which is normal during oligodendrocyte differentiation, with an average of 23.75% positively labeled cells (Fig.  b). 
Oligodendrocyte-lineage cells are expressing OLIG2, a transcription factor involved in its differentiation. Nuclear localization of this factor is lost in fully matured, myelinating oligodendrocytes. Ninety-six hours after OGD, there was a slight decrease in the number of OLIG2 + cells with nuclear localization in OGD-KC7F2 cultures compared to OGD-CoCl2 (p < 0.05) and CT-blank (p = 0.0773) (Fig.  c). This could happen, for example, in the case of enhanced differentiation. And it was confirmed with MBP immunolabeling. KC7F2 induced the most significant improvement in oligodendrocyte maturation, resulting in a concomitant increase in oligodendrocyte number (Fig.  d), individual cell size (Fig.  e), and overall MBP protein expression levels (Fig.  f). Interestingly, despite the clear evidence of inhibition of oligodendrocyte maturation only after OGD in our previous study [ ], data currently obtained did not confirm the statistically significant negative effect of OGD injury on differentiation at the MBP protein level. We assume the fact that having cultures enriched in OPCs resulted in overall low contribution of mature oligodendrocyte in cultures (for example 0.95 ± 0.82% in CT-blank and 0.78 ± 0.45% in OGD-blank).
In order to evaluate the effect of OGD and applied treatments on the expression of genes coding HIF proteins (Arnt and HIF-1α), transcription factors involved in the differentiation of neural progenitors into oligodendrocytes (Ascl1, and Olig2), transcription factors involved in the differentiation into mature oligodendrocytes (Olig1, Nkx2.2, and Myrf), and myelin-specific proteins (Cnp, Mag and Plp), we isolated total RNA 24 h after OGD and performed qPCR analysis. Such analysis would allow us to confirm whether the rate of oligodendrocyte differentiation is changing based on transcript levels before we see the expression of characteristic proteins. Every condition analyzed (CT vs OGD) as well as the applied treatment (CoCl2 or KC7F2) induced significant changes to the gene expression profile, which was assessed with two-way ANOVA analysis (7.13% of the total variance; p = 0.0001; F = 5.32; DFn = 5, DFd = 299). KC7F2 tend to increase the expression of all analyzed genes, which means that it can promote oligodendrocyte differentiation (Fig.  ). Significant differences were observed within the expression of Mag, where the greatest fold change was observed between samples: CT-blank and OGD-KC7F2 (p = 0.0002), CT-CoCl2 and CT-KC7F2 (p = 0.0284), CT-CoCl2 and OGD-KC7F2 (p < 0.0001), OGD-blank and OGD-KC7F2 (p < 0.0001), and OGD-CoCl2 and OGD-KC7F2 (p < 0.0001). 

## Discussion
HIF-1α is a transcription factor, stabilized during hypoxic conditions. HIF-1α signaling may act as a very important cog in the complex machinery of the response of the cell to the hypoxic-ischemic injury. It may regulate the adaptive response to mild hypoxic conditions through the activation of angiogenesis and erythropoietin (EPO) synthesis. EPO is known to induce several neuroprotective and anti-apoptotic pathways [ ]. During severe hypoxia, HIF-1α signaling may however contribute to cell death through activation of p53 [ ]. Accordingly, HIF-1α has both neuroprotective and neurodegenerative modes of action, depending mainly on the severity of the injury. However, it is worth keeping in mind that OPC-mediated HIF-1α signaling is also necessary in developing a healthy brain, where it acts as a regulator of white matter angiogenesis. In this context, HIF-1α acts as an inhibitor of oligodendrocyte maturation, because it maintains activeness until brain tissue is provided with enough nutrients from blood supply [ ]. Moreover, during early development, OPCs require vasculature in order to migrate to the target areas of the brain to be myelinated [ ]. Targeting HIF-1α in the asphyxiated brain should therefore take into account the potential beneficial effect of promoting OPC differentiation at a specified time point, but not cause side effects for other neurorepair effects, such as restoration of vascularization.
The role of HIF-1α signaling in the pathology of neurodevelopmental disorders was recently reported by several groups. Wang et al. showed that the exposure of pregnant rats hypoxia may lead to development of autistic behavior in offspring [ ]. Findings from clinical studies indicate that preterm newborns with brain injury have higher levels of serum HIF-1α, which also correlates with the severity of white matter injury [ ]. In another study, an increased serum level of HIF-1α was observed in children at mean age 10 with ASD, however without well-documented birth history [ ]. These make the HIF-1α pathway an interesting target for further research in neonatal hypoxia–ischemia.
In the performed study, we reported that neural tissue of neonatal rats after hypoxic-ischemic injury shows elevated HIF-1α expression. Interestingly, this effect is not observed in the brain hemisphere affected by hypoxia only (right hemisphere, contralateral). Since this effect persisted for up to 3 days after the damage, its consequences may be in the long term. On the other hand, it opens a wide therapeutic window to target HIF-1α as potential therapy of HIE. It is worth to notice that during the first days after the injury, an intensive repair process takes place, including the proliferation of oligodendrocyte progenitor cells [ ]. Modulation of certain processes could therefore have long-term effects on improving brain development, particularly the white matter. The involvement of HIF-1α after hypoxic-ischemic injury is not fully evaluated; thus, research on its modulation brings conflicting results. An effective neuroprotection was previously shown with hypoxic preconditioning in models of ischemic injury [ , ], but it is important to realize that this approach has no practical translation into clinical applications. Another approach applied in similar models was to stabilize HIF-1α; however, the positive outcome was limited [ , ].
The potential of HIF-1α inhibition for neuroprotection after ischemia was evaluated by Chen et al. over a decade ago on a middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion rat model with animals treated with intraparenchymal injection of siRNA. The observed effect of reduced mortality and reduced brain damage was obtained through decrease in the expression of HIF-1α target genes—VEGF (involved in blood–brain barrier disruption) and p53 (induction of apoptosis) [ ]. Another group tested the HIF-1α inhibitor 2ME2 (2-Methoxyestradiol) administered early after the injury in the model of neonatal asphyxia and proved a long-term neuroprotective effect through reduction in brain edema, preservation of blood–brain barrier integrity, and reduced neuronal loss [ ]. Tang et al. recently demonstrated the potential positive effect of HIF-1α inhibition through KC7F2 to hamper the pathological neovascularization as well as to reduce inflammation in the model of oxygen-induced retinopathy in mice and rats [ ]. Considering the fact that effective treatment for HIE should target ways to reduce oxidative stress, limit activation of apoptosis, and decrease excitotoxicity, several studies showed that upregulated HIF-1α signaling may be involved in these processes [ , ].
In our experimental in vitro model, HIF-1α signaling was activated in OPCs immediately after OGD injury. Although we were not able to measure the HIF-1α translocation rate through the biochemical analysis of cellular fractions, we confirmed the effect of OGD with confocal microscopy. Injury caused rapid translocation of this transcription factor to the area of the cell covered by the nucleus in the microscopic images. This may have affected multiple targets in the cells, causing changes to their viability and differentiation. In our study, OGD did not induce cell death, which was confirmed by three independent assays—LDH release, PrestoBlue, and PI incorporation assay. Thus, we confirmed our previous observations of low sensitivity of OPCs to this model of injury [ ]. What has not been confirmed is significantly increased proliferation after OGD. We assume that this is due to slightly different cell culture phenotype obtained with different method of cell isolation. Similarly, in our previous projects, OGD induced inhibition of oligodendrocyte maturation, such as by reducing the proportion of immature oligodendrocytes in a culture with concomitant increased expression of selected oligodendrocyte genes [ ]. This effect was also not replicated in this study. This does not change the fact that KC7F2 has been shown to significantly affect oligodendrocyte proliferation and differentiation. The effect was particularly significant after OGD was triggered. Moreover, over a decade ago, Cui et al. also confirmed the potential role of HIF-1α in regulation of Olig1 in hypoxic brain tissue [ ].
In our study, HIF-1α inhibition in vitro with 30 μM KC7F2 improved oligodendrocyte maturation assessed with immunolabeling of mature oligodendrocytes with anti-MBP as well as qPCR analysis of myelin protein’s mRNA. KC7F2 effectively blocked HIF-1α translation which can be suspected from the accumulation of mRNA transcript for this protein. On the other hand, further stimulation of HIF-1α signaling with CoCl2 did not have a significant effect on OPC differentiation, but accelerated cell proliferation, opposite to the KC7F2 effect. Interestingly, HIF-1α modification on oligodendrocyte gene expression revealed that CoCl2 can strongly inhibit the expression of Mag gene comparing to KC7F2 treatment. However, both treatments did not induce significant effect when compared to blank culture variant. Nevertheless, this may indicate the significant importance of the HIF-1α transcription activity in the in vitro cultures maintained in control conditions of physiological normoxia.
KC7F2 was discovered during searching for novel treatments for solid tumors. It is a selective inhibitor of HIF-1α translation through the suppression of phosphorylation HIF-1α protein synthesis regulators: 4E-BP1 (eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E binding protein 1) and p70S6K (p70 S6 kinase). It does not affect the HIF-1α protein degradation, neither its transcription, which may be beneficial to maintain the basic level of this molecule. Since its discovery published in 2009, KC7F2 has been tested in several preclinical cancer studies [ ]. It is the most interesting for the studies on solid tumors, where hypervascularization due to hypoxic environment occurs. Its antiangiogenic properties were tested with HUVECs (human umbilical vein cells), where it induced reduced expression of VEGF and reduced synthesis of ROS [ ]. The HIF-1α inhibitor also acts on cancer cells through regulating glucose metabolism which makes cancer cells more susceptible to traditional chemotherapy [ ]. It also inhibits cell migration and proliferation [ , ]. OPCs under the influence of this factor had also inhibited proliferation in control conditions and increased differentiation after OGD. We did not study cell migration after KC7F2 administration to damaged OPCs. This effect, however, may be crucial for the regeneration processes of neural tissue and limit its repair. What is worth noticing is that KC7F2 is preferable cytotoxic to cancer cell lines, but not to mouse neurons, astrocytes, or human endothelial cells [ ], and what we have shown in our study—also not to primary rat OPCs. Moreover, KC7F2 was previously shown to have anti-inflammatory properties, reducing the level of IL-1β, TNF-α, and Il-β after hypoxic treatment of monocyte THP-1 cells [ ], which makes it an interesting candidate for further studies on modulation of inflammatory response in an in vivo model.
Although the compound we used to inhibit an activation of the HIF-α pathway in our perinatal hypoxia–ischemia in vitro model has not yet entered clinical trials, we believe that this study opened new perspectives for finding the potential therapies for HIE. Nevertheless, the obtained results require further confirmation in the animal model, verifying its mode of action, optimization of the time of treatment, verifying the impact on neural tissue, and finding the molecular background of the observed effect.
