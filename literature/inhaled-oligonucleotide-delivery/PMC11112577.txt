The year 2023 marked two important
milestones in the field of nucleic acids research: the 100th anniversary
of the birth of Har Gobind Khorana and the 70th year of the publication
of the double-helical structure of Deoxyribose Nucleic Acid by James
D. Watson and Francis H. C. Crick.
The biological role of DNA
and its structure that would enable
its function was a hot topic during the late 19th century (1900–1950).
Several biologists and chemists were involved in contributing to a
growing body of knowledge about DNA. The elucidation of the double-helical
structure of DNA by James D. Watson and Francis H. C. Crick in 1953,
alongside important contributions by Maurice H. F. Wilkins and Rosalind
Franklin, is widely regarded as one of the most significant scientific
breakthroughs of the 20th century. This discovery revolutionized the
field of biology by revealing how genetic information is stored, replicated,
and transmitted from generation to generation and laying the foundation
for the development of modern genetics and biotechnology. Watson,
Crick, and Wilkins shared the Nobel
Prize in 1962 “for their discoveries concerning
the molecular structure of nucleic acids and its significance for
information transfer in living material”.
Khorana began
his work in the field of biologically relevant phosphate
esters and nucleic acids at the University of British Columbia. He
ventured toward the synthesis of nucleotide building blocks during
1950–1960, when it was established that genetic information
is transferred from DNA to RNA. His success in the chemical synthesis
of nucleotides and their triphosphate enabled him to build different
RNA chains, something that had eluded scientists for a long time.
In subsequent years, he extended this ability to synthesize nucleotide
triphosphates even further by developing a laborious but meticulous
method for synthesizing RNA fragments of a known sequence in solution.
Using enzymes discovered in parallel by other scientists, he synthesized
a set of proteins, whose amino acid sequences solved the important
puzzle of deciphering the genetic code. Khorana’s pioneering
work on deciphering the genetic code laid the foundation for the development
of modern biotechnology and genetic engineering. He shared the 1968
Nobel Prize for Physiology or Medicine with Marshall W.
Nirenberg and Robert W. Holley “for their interpretation of
the genetic code and its function in protein synthesis”.
Letsinger ( ) and his colleagues developed solid-phase methods for rapid synthesis
of oligonucleotides using phosphotriester chemistry, which became
the basis for much of the current biotech industry (read more about
it in the retrospective included in this collection: 10.1021/acsomega.3c05177). The work of Khorana and Letsinger, along with that of Marvin Caruthers
later in phosphoramidite chemistry, made possible modern biotechnology
and genetic engineering which has profoundly impacted our understanding
of the fundamental principles of life.
 In celebration of these two important milestones in the field of
nucleic acids, which have enabled far reaching major consequences
in biology, this virtual special
issue provides a breadth of high-quality research on evolving
topics related to nucleic acids.
This virtual special
issue includes papers discussing historical perspective,
oligonucleotide synthesis and secondary structures (triplex and tetraplex),
chemical modifications and cellular stability, molecular mechanisms
of biological activity and interactions with proteins and ligands,
tools and techniques to study nucleic acids, and biotechnological
applications of DNA.
Our authors come from around the world—United
States, India,
Japan, Spain, Czech Republic, Italy, Brunei Darussalam, Portugal,
Australia, Switzerland, China, Norway, and Mexico—and include
leaders in the field alongside a number of rising stars. We’re
excited about the discoveries shared in these papers. We hope this
collection motivates you and others to look for opportunities to address
unsolved challenges in nucleic acids research. In the following, we
highlight the scientific summary of articles presented in the virtual special
issue.
To set the ball rolling, Martin Egli has provided
an excellent
personal retrospective (10.1021/acsomega.3c05177) based on a presentation at the symposium “Bob Letsinger, PhD–100 Years of History” in 2021 of the Oligonucleotide Therapeutics Society (OTS).
Along with Khorana, Robert Letsinger is considered as the father of
synthetic DNA chemistry, and both have left an unerasable legacy in
terms of not only the synthesis of genetic material but also the number
of chemists that they have trained, making innumerable contributions
to this field. In another article connecting the history to the present,
Balaram (10.1021/acsomega.3c07196) has shared a nice historical
account of the emergence of the macrocycle cucurbituril, first reported
in 1905 by Robert Behrend by chemical treatment of a bicyclic urea
derivative that remained a dry powder despite absorbing water, with
the ability to absorb dyes from solution. After 70 years, William
L. Mock showed it to be cucurbituril, a remarkably symmetric molecule
with a central cavity welcoming diverse guests. Since then, the versatility
of cucurbituril in the field of supramolecular chemistry has grown,
including a demonstration of the Huisgen click reaction in a molecular
cavity. Supramolecular chemistry is also a hallmark of DNA/RNA structure
and properties.
The specificity of hydrogen bonding among the
complementary bases
is the key attribute of DNA for carrying and transmitting information
for almost all functions of cells, executed through proteins. Thus,
hydrogen bonding between nucleobases is a crucial noncovalent interaction
for life on Earth. While the canonical nucleobases form base pairs
in the form of a Watson–Crick pair that enables the static
functions of nucleic acids (storing of genetic information), the Hoogsteen
pairing facilitates the dynamic functions of these biomacromolecules.
Osifová et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c03244) show that 8-(NH2)/Oxo-substituted purine derivatives
change the Hoogsteen pairing modes with their hydrogen-bonding partners.
Their stability has been analyzed experimentally using NMR and theoretically
by DFT calculations to conclude that for intermolecular H-bonds, while
dictating the stability, no strong preferences for either Watson–Crick
or Hoogsteen were found.
Nucleosides and their analogues also
exhibit potential antiviral
and antiproliferative activity. Martín-Nieves et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c06231) demonstrate that the enantiomers of unnatural l-nucleosides
have comparable biological activity but more favorable toxicological
properties and greater metabolic stability than natural d-nucleosides. l-Oligonucleotides have identical physical
properties in terms of solubility, hybridization kinetics, and duplex
thermal stability as d-oligonucleotides but are completely
orthogonal in nature. Importantly, they are resistant to nuclease
degradation, nontoxic, and immunologically passive which are desirable
properties for biomedical applications. In this context, Martín-Nieves
et al. describe the synthesis of several 2′-O-methyl/2′-O-MOE-l-nucleoside pyrimidine derivatives and their incorporation
into G-rich oligonucleotides. These new l-G-quadruplexes
showed enhanced nuclease resistance with a minimal impact on the nucleic
acid structural properties.
The nucleic acid therapeutics that
have evolved over the past 40
years hinge on DNA/RNA analogues, with modification of sugar residues
and backbone. Jestřábová et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c05202) report the synthesis of a set of 2′-deoxyribonucleoside
3′-phosphoramidites derived from 5-phenylethynyluracil, 5-(pentyn-1-yl)cytosine,
7-(indol-3-yl)ethynyl-7-deazaadenine, and 7-isopropylethynyl-7-deazaguanine.
These nucleoside phosphoramidites were incorporated into oligonucleotides
by solid phase synthesis. The hypermodified aptamers that incorporated
these modifications showed biophysical properties similar to sequence-specifically
displayed amino-acid-like hydrophobic substituents.
Among the
several backbone-modified nucleic acids, Peptide Nucleic
Acids (PNAs) are the “understudies of DNA”, and their
modified derivatives are antisense molecules with excellent polynucleotide
hybridization properties. They are resistant to nuclease degradation
but often have poor cell permeability, leading to moderate cellular
activity and limited clinical results. Podlaski et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c03184) describe the synthesis and polynucleotide hybridization properties
of a novel cationic/amino-alkyl nucleotide-base-modified PNA (OPNA).
The studies reveal a favorable 10- to 30-fold increase in affinity
for a single cationic modification on adenine, cytosine, or guanidine
OPNA sequence compared to the nonmodified PNA strand, promoted by
a favorable additional enthalpy of–80 kJ/mol.
Peptide
nucleic acid (PNA) and its modified analogues are also
emerging as promising ligands for triple-helical recognition of folded
biologically relevant RNA under physiological conditions. Kumpina
et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c09775) compare two modified PNA nucleobases, 2-aminopyridine (M) and 4-thiopseudisocytosine
(L), as alternatives to unfavorable protonated cytosine (under physiological
conditions) to form more stable triplets than C+·G-C.
They found that both nucleobases formed M+·G-C and
L·G-C triplets of similar stability, but the L-modified PNAs
showed reduced sequence specificity. With M, the pKa is increased to favor partial protonation for improving
their solubility and cellular uptake. Sulfur substitution in L enhanced
favorable hydrophobic interactions, thus avoiding off-target effects
caused by cationic modifications.
Nucleic acids and proteins
possess encoded “languages”
used for information storage or to direct function, but each biopolymer
is limited to encoding its respective “language”. In
the PNA scaffold, nucleobase and amino acid residue glycine are installed
on a singular backbone, to enable a single biopolymer to encode both
languages. Argueta-Gonzalez et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c05528) developed a “bilingual” PNA biopolymer that incorporates
a sequence-specific nucleic acid code interspersed with hydrophobic
(alanine) and hydrophilic (lysine) amino acid residues at defined
positions to produce amphiphilic character. Here, the authors demonstrate
that the amphiphilic amino acid residues in such a biopolymer undergo
self-assembly into micelle-like structures, while the nucleic acid
recognition was harnessed for disassembly. Judicious variation of
amino acid residues (lengths, functional group charges, hydrophobicity,
and spacings) indicated that negative charges in the hydrophilic block
or increased bulkiness of the hydrophobic side chains led to assembly
into similarly sized micelles and increased critical micelle concentration.
Varying PNA sequences led to self-assembly of smaller structures.
Thus, the amino acid and nucleic acid sequences of amphiphilic bilingual
biopolymers can be customized to finely tune the assembly and disassembly
properties with implications for applications with encapsulation and
delivery of cargo for therapeutics.
Conjugation of DNA/RNA/PNA
with functional ligands either as a
sensing probe or for enhancing cell permeability has expanded the
field of application of chemically modified nucleic acids. The transport
of antisense oligonucleotides into appropriate cellular compartments
is a limiting factor in their potency. Hill et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c05144) have synthesized previously reported splice-switching 2′-O-methoxyethyl phosphorothioate oligonucleotides (SSOs)
to treat the rare disease erythropoietic protoporphyria. The SSOs
were conjugated with nuclear localization signal (NLS) peptides using
thiol–maleimide chemistry. The chemical ligation–quantitative
polymerase chain reaction (CL-qPCR) was used to quantify SSOs in cells
and subcellular compartments. However, compared to the parent SSO
which altered the splicing of the target pre-mRNA, the conjugates
were inactive at splice correction under free uptake conditions in
vitro. However, the splice-switching activity could be conferred on
the conjugates by delivery into cells via cationic lipid-mediated
transfection or by treating the cells after the conjugate’s
delivery with chloroquine, an endosome-disrupting agent. Thus, the
major barrier to the activity of the peptide–oligonucleotide
conjugates was endosomal entrapment.
Poly(amidoamine) (PAMAM)
dendrimers and peptide-conjugated dendrimers
are known to be successful as nucleic acid delivery vectors. Dannert
et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c05140) studied the effect of conjugating two peptides’ tails to
G2-PAMAM dendrimers on DNA condensation and polyplex formation. They
have shown that the neutral peptide tails sterically hinder the formation
of DNA-G2 polyplexes composed of multiple DNA chains. When tails are
negatively charged, the interaction of G2 with DNA is electrostatically
hindered. Net positive charge on the tails restored the complexation
capacity of G2 with the DNA. While DNA complexation is obtained for
a similar net charge balance for G2, G2 conjugates with positive tails
were more effective than G2-DNA complexes with fewer molecules required
to achieve a comparable condensation degree. Tuning of the net charge
of conjugated peptide tails to PAMAM dendrimers helps to control the
complexation capacity of DNA that is optimal for gene delivery vehicles.
Fuchs’ corneal endothelial dystrophy (FECD) is a major cause
of vision loss, and corneal transplantation is the only effective
curative treatment but with some limitations. FECD is caused by an
expanded CUG repeat within intron 2 of the TCF4 RNA, and agents that
recognize the expanded repeat can reverse the splicing defects associated
with the disease. Hu et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c05634) evaluated anti-CUG morpholinos conjugated to cyclic cell-penetrating
peptides and showed that morpholino conjugates can enter corneal endothelial
cells and block the CUG RNA foci associated with the disease. The
results have important implications in developing anti-CUG therapies
for FECD.
In addition to the antisense mechanism, splice switching,
ribo
switches, aptamers, and CRISPR/Cas have evolved for important ancillary
tools for the development of nucleic acid therapeutics. Splice switching
antisense oligonucleotide (ASO)-based therapeutics have gained significant
interest after approval for clinical use for treating Duchenne muscular
dystrophy and spinal muscular atrophy. Le et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c07618) provide a comprehensive review of modified nucleic acid analogues
explored for developing splice switching ASOs, including drugs that
have entered human clinical trials.
Riboswitches have emerged
as one of the effective controls of gene
expression. Flavin mononucleotide riboswitches, which are common among
many pathogenic bacteria, are an attractive target for antibiotics
development. The binding mechanism of this riboswitch that binds to
riboflavin (RBF, vitamin B2), deep inside the RNA structure, is not
yet known. Bekker et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c06826) have used the multicanonical molecular dynamics (McMD)-based dynamic
docking protocol to identify the native binding configuration. They
found that RBF initially binds the interface between P4 and P6 including
U61 and G62, which forms a gateway which then opens to allow the ligand
to slip into the hidden binding site involving A48, A49, and A85.
Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) is widely used in
detection of nucleic acids but has limitations in terms of sequence
specificity and is expensive due to the use of chemically modified
DNA probes. Goyal et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c07599) have designed a protocol with a DNA aptamer and light-up dye-based
chemistry for quantitative PCR, in which an exponential decrease in
fluorescence is observed upon DNA amplification. The technique was
validated by quantifying gene targets from Streptococcus zooepidemicus (SzhasB) and Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MtrpoB),
with a DNA detection limit low as 800 copies/μL.
The self-organization
of G-rich DNA into G-quadruplexes is important
not only for stabilizing the ends of DNA during cell replication but
also as a key regulatory element. G-quadruplexes in disease-related
genes are associated with various biological processes regulating
the disease progression. Although ligands stabilizing G-quadruplexes
are well studied, destabilizing G-quadruplexes are also important
and remain limited in applications. Tateishi-Karimata and Sugimoto
(10.1021/acsomega.3c08087) evaluated such an approach using
choline dihydrogen phosphate (dhp), a highly biocompatible hydrated
ionic liquid, and found that it destabilized G-quadruplexes more effectively
than KCl. The destabilization resulted from dominant entropic contribution,
indicating its noncoordination with G-quartets, due to their large
radii. Subsequently, in the derived nuclear extracts from living cells
where G-quadruplexes repressed transcription, choline dhp increased
transcription. The transcription of template DNA containing the G-quadruplex
was greatly enhanced in living human breast cancer MDA-MD-231 cells
cultured with choline dhp. Thus, choline dhp is useful in regulating
target disease-related genes.
Kumar et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c07574) report the stabilization of G quadruplex structures using estrone-based
derivatives that have a nonplanar core and a cationic alkyl side chain
as G4 stabilizers. Interestingly, these ligands preferentially stabilized
parallel c-MYC and c-KIT1 G4s over the other G4s and duplex DNAs.
The ligand Est-3 shows cytotoxicity against cancer cell lines and
effectively downregulates the c-KIT gene in HepG2 cell lines. Est-3
was shown to prefer stacking over the 5′-quartet of c-MYC G4
with its aromatic ring. The binding of G4 with nonplanar scaffolds
opens up new avenues for G-binding ligand design.
CRISPR/Cas
has made a revolution in gene editing technology, giving
unprecedented control and accuracy. Janudin et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c04473) report a CRISPR/Cas12a-mediated recombinase polymerase amplification
(RPA) detection method that combines RPA with Cas12a cleavage for
the detection of porcine DNA in 5 pg/μL, which is a global
concern of food adulteration.
Nucleic acids are notorious for
self-organization through hydrogen
bonding interactions, leading to several types of secondary and tertiary
structures. The nonspecific ion atmosphere around the nucleic acids
influences nucleic acid folding and structural stability. While single
ion effects on nucleic acid secondary structure stability have been
examined, studies on how mixed ions and the competitive occupancy
of different ions in the ionic atmosphere influence nucleic acid secondary
structure and stability are not known. Arteaga et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c07563) studied optical melting of six oligonucleotides (DNA/RNA) in buffers
containing molar quantities of either XCl (X = Li, K, Rb, or Cs) or
a mixture with NaCl. They found that in a 1:1 mixture of XCl/NaCl
one alkali metal chloride contributed more to duplex stability than
the other.
Many biomolecules can form dynamic aggregates in
water, whose nanometric
structures often reflect the chirality of the monomers in unexpected
ways. Their twisted organization can be further propagated to the
mesoscale, in chiral liquid crystalline phases, and even to the macroscale,
where chiral, layered architectures contribute to the chromatic and
mechanical properties of various plant, insect, and animal tissues.
At all scales, the resulting organization is determined by a subtle
balance among chiral and nonchiral interactions, whose understanding
and fine-tuning are fundamental also for applications. Caimi and Zanchetta
(10.1021/acsomega.3c01822) present recent advances in the
chiral self-assembly and mesoscale ordering of biological and bioinspired
molecules in water, focusing on systems based on nucleic acids or
related aromatic molecules, oligopeptides, and their hybrid structures.
The common features and key mechanisms governing this wide range of
phenomena are reported together with novel characterization approaches.
The rapid and precise selective sensing of DNA nucleobases is a
long-pursued goal necessary to advance the field of genomics and related
medical biotechnology. Algharagholy et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c04945) present a new type of nanoscale sensor based on carbon nanotubes
with a specific geometry that can discriminate not only the type of
nucleobase but also its angle of orientation. They employ first-principle,
quantum transport simulations to calculate the transmission, conductance,
and current of the nanotube-based nanoscale sensor to effectively
discriminate between the four nucleotides through their angle of orientation
to determine the precise sequential DNA chains.
The mechanistic
aspects of nucleic acid function and their interaction
with proteins are of primary importance in understanding the cellular
functions. SIRT6 protein is an emerging regulator of longevity, whose
overexpression extends the lifespan of mice. SIRT6 knockout mice exhibited
severe metabolic defects and a shortened lifespan. Kang et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c04859) demonstrate that the deacetylase activity of SIRT6 can be stimulated
by DNA strand breaks for synthetic peptide and histone substrates.
SIRT6 can be preferentially activated by DNA lesions harboring a 5′-phosphate,
and the N- and C-termini of SIRT6 are strictly required for DNA break-induced
activation. The study sheds important light on the cellular regulation
of diverse SIRT6 activities and suggests possible strategies for effective
SIRT6 activation. Antigen receptor (AgR) diversity is central to the
ability of adaptive immunity in jawed vertebrates to protect against
pathogenic agents. The production of highly diverse AgR repertoires
is initiated during B and T cell lymphopoiesis by V(D)J recombination,
which assembles the receptor genes from component gene segments in
a cut-and-paste recombination reaction. Hoolehan et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c05601) review the presently known mechanisms that facilitate assembly
of recombination activating proteins RAG1/2 with recombination signal
sequences RSSs, the ensuing conformational changes required for DNA
cleavage activity, and how the readout of the RSS sequence affects
reaction efficiency.
In spite of the remarkable similarity of
the RNA polymerases (RNAPs)
in different bacteria, a wide variation is found in the promoter–polymerase
interaction, transcription initiation, and termination, and the rate
of elongation could vary in different bacteria. Tare et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c04754) measure the transcription rates for the RNAPs from three bacteria, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Mycobacterium smegmatis, and Escherichia coli, using surface plasmon resonance
through a computational flux flow model. The results revealed different
rates of transcription elongation among the above pathogens with different
rate profiles, 10 nt/s, 18 nt/s, and 23 nt/s, respectively, for the
three RNAPs.
Extraction of pure DNA from natural sources such
as vegetables,
leaves, and fruits of different varieties is important for sequencing.
Teixeira et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c02717) present a miniaturized module for DNA purification based on μSPE
using a borosilicate glass microfiber filter as the solid phase, with
a recovery percent of 50%. Resistance development in food-borne (Salmonella)
pathogens in hypertonic environments is an increasing concern, particularly
in the poultry industry, which necessitates a need to understand the
mechanism of resistance. Work by Ligong Zhai’s group (10.1021/acsomega.3c04257) preliminarily identified four hypertonic tolerance-related genes
identified in S. derby as otsA, proV, proW, and omsV.
The hypertonic-adapted S. derby exhibited resistance
to OXA, AMP, PEN, and CEP antibiotics, and the biofilm-forming ability
was 1.25 times that of nonadapted S. derby. The expression
of virulence-related genes in hypertonic-adapted S. derby increased by 2–3 times, and that of biofilm-related genes
increased by 2–4 times.
Ohashi et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c08516) have studied the quantitative conversion of 4-thiouridine to cytidine
by different methods and show that activation by 2,4-dinitrofluorbenzene,
followed by amination, works out best even on an RNA template. The
results are useful for studying transcriptome dynamics without harming
cells. Rodriguez et al. (10.1021/acsomega.3c07379) report procedures to condense DNAs extracted from different sources
such as human peripheral blood, the Lambda virus, and plasmid pBR322
and precipitate them on a glass slide, during which process DNA undergoes
autonomous self-assembly, leading to DNA hyper structures. The relative
morphological features of such DNA hyper structures from healthy individuals
and those diagnosed with different medical conditions or illnesses
provide an efficient diagnostic tool.
The articles presented
in this virtual special
issue serve as representative samples, albeit not exhaustive,
of how the synthesis and structure of DNA have profoundly influenced
areas far beyond the ideas that existed 70 years ago.

## Biographies
 Prof. Krishna N. Ganesh is a leading researcher in the
chemistry
and biology of nucleic acids with a special focus on the applications
of DNA analogues, structural biology of collagen peptides, and DNA
nanotechnology. He is recognized for his contributions to the design
of Peptide Nucleic Acid (PNA) analogues for effective cell permeation.
His scientific contribution towards the understanding of the chemical
principles of DNA molecular recognition and DNA interaction with drugs
and proteins was recognized with the CSIR Shanti Swarup Bhatnagar
Prize in chemical sciences (1998). He is a Fellow of all three Science
academies in India and The Fellow of The World Academy of Sciences
(TWAS, 2006). Prof. Ganesh is also one of the Founding Co-Editors
of ACS Omega since the inception of the journal in
2016.
He was most recently awarded a civilian honor of Padma
Shri (2023)
by the Government of India. He is the Founding Director of two of
the seven IISERs that are institutes of national importance in India,
namely, IISER Pune (2006–-2015) and IISER Tirupati (2017–2023).
He recently moved to Jawaharlal Nehru Centre for Advanced Scientific
Research (JNCASR) in Bangalore as the National Science Chair of Science
and Engineering Research Board (SERB-DST), New Delhi, to continue
contributing to the scientific community as an active researcher.

 Dr. Muthiah (Mano) Manoharan is a renowned chemist in oligonucleotide
chemical modifications, conjugation chemistry, and delivery platforms
(lipid nanoparticles, polymer conjugates, and complex-forming strategies).
Dr. Manoharan and his research group demonstrated for the first time
the human therapeutic applications of GalNAc-conjugated oligonucleotides,
a platform that has revolutionized nucleic acid-based therapeutics.
He received the M. L. Wolfrom Award from the American Chemical
Society Carbohydrate Chemistry Division in 2007 for his contributions
to this field. Dr. Manoharan earned his Ph.D. in Chemistry at the
University of North Carolina—Chapel Hill with Professor Ernest
L. Eliel and did postdoctoral research at Yale University and the
University of Maryland with Professor John A. Gerlt. He currently
serves as a Senior Vice President and a Distinguished Research Scientist
at Alnylam Pharmaceuticals, Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA. In March
2024, Dr. Manoharan was honoured by American Chemical Society with
the award of Breslow Prize.
