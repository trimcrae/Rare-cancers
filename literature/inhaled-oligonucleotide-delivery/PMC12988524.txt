## Objective
Emerging evidence has shown that inflammatory synovial macrophage and anabolism-impaired chondrocytes play essential roles in osteoarthritis (OA). The present work aims at uncovering the pathogenic mechanism of how the damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released from inflammatory synovial macrophage promote extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation of chondrocytes and developing feasible strategies to counter its detrimental effects.

## Methods
We identified pyroptosis of synovial macrophages in the synovium of OA human and mouse. The effect and mechanism of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) released from pyroptotic synovial macrophage in ECM degradation of chondrocytes and cartilage degeneration was further explored in cellular and animal models. Finally, the ameliorative effect of folic acid-modified poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles in OA was elucidated by in vivo experiments.

## Results
Mitochondrial dysfunction in synovial macrophages leads to the release of mtDNA into the cytoplasm, which promotes macrophage pyroptosis, thereby facilitating extracellular release of mtDNA and creating an inflammatory microenvironment unfavorable to cartilage in OA. DDIT3 deficiency inhibits mtROS production by enhancing PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy, which constraining the mtDNA release into the cytoplasm. The decreased cytosolic mtDNA, in turn, dampens macrophage pyroptosis. In vivo, DDIT3 deficiency significantly alleviates synovial inflammation and cartilage degeneration in OA progression, and targeting inhibition of macrophage pyroptosis by folic acid-modified PLGA nanoparticles mimics the protective effects of DDIT3 deficiency against OA progression.

## Conclusions
Our findings identified the pathological role of mtDNA released from pyroptotic synovial macrophages through DDIT3-mediated mitophagy in OA, and demonstrated the efficacy of using folic acid-modified PLGA nanoparticles as a delivery for OA treatment.

## The translational potential of this article
This study highlights the pivotal role of mtDNA released from pyroptotic synovial macrophages through DDIT3-mediated mitophagy in OA. Targeting inhibition of macrophage pyroptosis by folic acid-modified PLGA nanoparticles might serve as a potential therapeutic target for alleviating cartilage degeneration in OA.

## Introduction
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a degenerative joint disorder characterized by cartilage damage, alterations in the synovium, and the degradation of subchondral bone. Research indicates that during early stage of OA, macrophages accumulate in synovium and release a substantial quantity of inflammatory mediators and proteases, which act on chondrocytes in a paracrine manner, leading to cartilage destruction and accelerating OA progression [ , , ]. Thus, targeting the inhibition of macrophage paracrine mediators is a potential approach to alleviate osteoarthritic cartilage damage. However, the release pathways and signaling mechanisms of macrophage paracrine mediators are still need to be clarified.
Mitochondria are essential organelles involved in cellular energy metabolism. Inflammatory stimuli can induce mitochondrial dysfunction in macrophages, characterized by decreased mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), and increased release of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) [ ]. mtDNA that leaks into the cytoplasm is oxidized by ROS and binds to NLRP3 in the cytoplasm, leading to NLRP3 inflammasome activation and caspase-1-dependent pyroptosis [ ]. When the cell membrane ruptures, mtDNA released extracellularly, acting as a novel damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), mediates inflammatory responses [ , ]. Increasing evidence suggests that mtDNA released under mitochondrial stress can act as a paracrine mediator between cells [ , ]. Studies have shown that synovial macrophages exhibit mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative DNA damage during arthritis, with large amounts of extracellular mtDNA detected in synovial fluid [ , ]. Moreover, synovial macrophage pyroptosis has been suggested as a contributing factor in the progression of OA [ ]. Therefore, we hypothesize that during OA, synovial macrophages release mtDNA into the cytoplasm, promoting NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, which in turn leads to extracellular release of mtDNA, creating an inflammatory microenvironment detrimental to cartilage. However, the molecular mechanism mediating the release of mtDNA from stressed mitochondria into the cytoplasm in synovial macrophages remain unclear.
DNA damage-inducible transcript 3 (DDIT3), also referred to as CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein (C/EBP) homologous protein (CHOP), is a well-established marker of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and closely related to inflammatory responses. Previous studies have demonstrated that DDIT3 participates in regulating macrophage inflammatory responses and polarization in inflammation-related diseases [ , ]. Additionally, we previously found that DDIT3 promoted osteoblast pyroptosis in an inflammatory environment [ ]. Given these findings, it is speculated that DDIT3 may modulate synovial macrophage pyroptosis, thereby aggravating OA progression. Upon activation of pyroptosis signaling, mitochondrial undergo irreversible damage and produce abundant ROS, which are pivotal in modulating the efficiency of GSDMD cleavage [ ]. In addition, it was recently reported that glutamine deprivation induced a pool of mitochondrial localized DDIT3 that functioned to suppress mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and ROS levels [ ]. These findings suggested that DDIT3 may promote macrophage GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis through mitochondrial dysfunction. Mitophagy is a process that selectively removing damaged or dysfunctional mitochondria through autophagy mechanisms to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis [ ]. Recent studies have shown that defected mitophagy promoted mtDNA release into the cytoplasm, activating inflammatory signaling pathways [ , ]. Nonetheless, the role of DDIT3 in mitophagy, mtDNA release and its regulatory influence on synovial macrophage pyroptosis remains unclear.
Recent advancements have highlighted the potential of PLGA nanoparticles modified with folic acid as an effective therapeutic tool for modulating inflammatory response of macrophages [ ]. These nanoparticles possess biological properties, including superior biocompatibility, extended circulation, and targeted delivery capabilities, which demonstrate moderate therapeutic efficacy in inflammatory diseases such as arthritis [ ]. Thus, generating folic acid-modified PLGA nanoparticles is a promising strategy for enhancing drug targeting to activated synovial macrophages, thereby alleviating synovitis for the treatment of OA.
The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is among the most commonly affected joints in OA and the animal TMJ-OA model induced by complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA) has been developed to study the key role of synovitis during OA progression [ , ]. In this study, we report that during TMJ-OA, synovial macrophage with mitochondrial dysfunction leads to mtDNA release into the cytoplasm, which promotes NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, thereby facilitating extracellular release of mtDNA and creating an inflammatory microenvironment detrimental to cartilage. DDIT3 deficiency inhibits mtROS production by enhancing PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy, which constraining the mtDNA release into the cytoplasm. The decreased cytosolic mtDNA, in turn, dampens macrophage pyroptosis. In vivo, DDIT3 deficiency significantly alleviates synovial inflammation and cartilage degeneration in OA progression, and targeting inhibition of macrophage pyroptosis by folic acid-modified PLGA nanoparticles mimics the protective effects of DDIT3 deficiency against OA progression. These findings highlight the specific mechanism of the crosstalk between pyroptotic synovial macrophages and cartilage degeneration, thus providing a potential therapeutic target for OA that modulates the pyroptosis of synovial macrophages by modified PLGA nanoparticles.

## Materials and methods
2.1

## Clinical specimen collection
Synovial tissue samples from two distinct groups, ‘OA’ and ‘Non-OA’, were collected from patients undergoing arthroplasty. The ‘OA’ group samples were obtained from eight patients diagnosed with TMJ-OA accompanied by disc perforation, and the ‘Non-OA’ group samples, confirmed as non-inflammatory based on pathological diagnosis following Murakami's criteria, were collected from eight patients with condylar hypertrophy. Patients with other joint diseases or those who had received prior medication were excluded from the study. Detailed information regarding the samples is provided in . Informed consents were obtained from all participants prior to surgery, and the study protocol received approval from the Human Research Ethics Committee at the School and Hospital of Stomatology, Wuhan University (WDKQ2024-B39).

2.2

## Mice and TMJ-OA animal model
Twenty-four male wild-type C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) were purchased from the Experimental Animal Centre of Hubei Province, China. To construct the TMJ-OA animal model, 20 μL of complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA) (Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was injected into the upper compartment of the bilateral TMJs of each mouse, while the control groups were administered an equivalent volume of saline. Briefly, twenty-four mice were randomly allocated into the saline group (n = 6) and CFA group (1W: n = 6; 2W: n = 6; 4W: n = 6) using an online randomizer based on their individual tattooed number. Sample numbers were empirically determined in accordance with previously published data under similar experimental conditions. At 1, 2, or 4 weeks following the intra-articular CFA injection, TMJ samples were collected from each group mice (n = 6).
The global Ddit3 deficiency (Ddit3−/−) mice were generated by the CRISPR/Cas9 system in C57BL/6J background mice as previously described [ ]. Eight-week-old male Ddit3−/− mice (n = 8) and their littermates wild type mice (Ddit3+/+, n = 8) were initially paired and utilized to establish TMJ-OA mouse model. All mice were sacrificed 2 weeks following injection with saline or CFA.
All protocols and procedures adhered to the NIH guidelines and received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Wuhan University (S07918110E).

2.3

## Histomorphology, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and immunofluorescence (IF) staining
The TMJ specimens from the mice and patient synovial samples were initially fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, and the mouse specimens required decalcification. Subsequently, all samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin wax, and sectioned into 5 μm sequential slices. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was then conducted to evaluate cartilage degradation and synovial inflammation, utilizing the modified Mankin score and synovitis score. Additionally, Safranin O and toluidine blue staining were employed on the slices to detect proteoglycan in the cartilage matrix.
For IHC staining, slices were performed according to the manufacturer's instruction (DIG-3009, Maixin, Fuzhou, China). Detailed information regarding the primary antibodies was available in . For visualization, sections were treated with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB-0031, Maixin), followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin.
IF staining was conducted in a manner analogous to IHC staining, with the exception that nuclei were stained with DAPI. Images were captured using an inverted fluorescent microscope (Leica, Germany) and then analyzed by ImageJ software.
For all histological analyses, sections were evaluated by researchers who were blinded to the experimental conditions. After scoring, the data were unblinded and analyzed by the researchers conducting the experiments.

2.4

## Cell culture and treatment
Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from the femurs and tibias of 6-week-old male Ddit3−/− mice and their littermates Ddit3+/+ mice as previously described [ ]. The bone-marrow cells were cultured in α-MEM (Gibco, MD, USA) with 10 % fetal bovine serum and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (v/v) for 24 h. Cells in the supernatant were collected and incubated with 15 ng/mL M-CSF (Novoprotein, Suzhou, China). After 5–7 days, the adherent cells were identified as BMDMs. To induce cell pyroptosis, cells were pretreated with 0.25 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Sigma–Aldrich, USA) for 6 h, followed by stimulation with 5 mM adenosine triphosphate (ATP, MedchemExpress, New Jersey, USA) for 30 min. For the mtDNA deletion (ρ0) group, BMDMs were pretreated with 100 ng/mL ethidium bromide (EtBr) for 7 days, then cultured with LPS for 6 h, and 5 mM ATP was added for another 30 min. For the inhibitor group, MitoTEMPO (10 μM, MedchemExpress, New Jersey, USA) or Mdivi-1 (5 μM, MedchemExpress, New Jersey, USA) was added for 2 h before LPS/ATP stimulation.
Chondrocytes were harvested from the TMJ condylar cartilage as described in our previous study [ ]. Then, the chondrocytes were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, MD, USA) with 15 % fetal bovine serum (Gemini BioProducts, USA) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (v/v). The culture medium was changed at 48 h intervals until harvesting.

2.5

## Conditioned medium (CM)-based coculture
CM-based coculture was employed to evaluate the paracrine effect of macrophages on chondrocytes. In brief, after BMDMs induction with LPS and ATP, the medium was discarded and washed with PBS. Subsequently, the fresh DMEM medium was added to the cells for an additional 24 h, and the supernatants were collected. The CM was centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min to remove cell debris and stored at − 80 °C until use. Chondrocytes were cultured in a mixture of CM and DMEM/F12 (1:1) for 48 h.

2.6

## Western blot analysis
Cellular protein was extracted using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing PMSF. Protein samples were separated via 12 % SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5 % non-fat milk and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, details of which were provided in . After incubation with secondary antibodies (1:10000, Biosharp), the protein bands were detected by ECL reagent (Advansta, CA, USA) and quantified by ImageJ software.

2.7

## Mitochondrial function
The generation of mitochondrial ROS in cells was assessed using the MitoSOX Red Mitochondrial Superoxide Indicator (Yeason, Shanghai, China). Cells from each experimental group were incubated with MitoSOX Red (200 nM, 30 min, 37 °C), followed by washing with PBS buffer. Fluorescence imaged of the cells were acquired by a confocal fluorescence microscope (Olympus, IX83, Tokyo, Japan) and the mean fluorescence densities of MitoSOX Red were quantified by ImageJ software.
Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was assessed utilizing a JC-1 staining kit (Beyotime, China). In healthy mitochondrial, JC-1 accumulated to form red-fluorescent aggregates, whereas in damaged mitochondrial to form green-fluorescent monomers. The ratio of red to green fluorescence served as an indicator of MMP alterations. Cells were washed with PBS and subsequently incubated with the JC-1 working solution (20 min, 37 °C). Fluorescence was examined using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, IX83, Tokyo, Japan), and the ratio of aggregates/monomers was quantified using ImageJ software.

2.8

## Measurement of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)
For mtDNA staining, cells were incubated with 200 nM Mitotracker Red (Beyotime, China) and 0.5 % v/v PicoGreen (for dsDNA staining, Yeason, Shanghai, China) in serum-free DMEM for 30 min at 37 °C to co-localize mitochondrial and dsDNA. Then, the fluorescence was observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, IX83, Tokyo, Japan).
For the relative quantification of cytosolic mtDNA, the Cell Mitochondrial Isolation Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was employed to isolate cytosolic fractions. DNA from both whole cell and cytosolic fractions were extracted using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (QIAGEN, Duesseldorf, Germany). qRT-PCR was conducted on both whole cell extracts and cytosolic fractions utilizing mtDNA primers (D-loop, Cox1, Non-numt), with nuclear DNA (Tert, 18S, B2m) serving as normalization references. The primer sequences were provided in . The Ct values obtained for mtDNA in whole cell extracts served as normalization controls for the mtDNA values derived from the cytosolic fractions.
DNA from culture supernatant of BMDMs was extracted using the QIAmp DNA mini kit (QIAGEN, Duesseldorf, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer's protocol. qPCR was performed to quantify mtDNA.

2.9

## mtDNA treatment in CFA-induced TMJ-OA mouse model with macrophage depletion
Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were depleted of macrophages by intraperitoneally injected with 200 μL clodronate-containing liposomes (Clo-lip, Yeason, China), 2 days prior to CFA induction, and maintained throughout the 14-day experimental period. This intervention led to a rapid reduction in macrophage populations, with an 80 % decrease in F4/80+ cells observed within 24–48 h post-injection, as previously reported. For mtDNA injection group, mtDNA was extracted from the liver of wild type C57BL/6J mice using a mitochondrial DNA extraction kit (Phygene, Fuzhou, China), following the manufacturer's protocol. The concentration of mtDNA was determined using NanoDrop 2000. To explore the functional role of mtDNA in modulating osteoarthritic cartilage degeneration, mtDNA (400 ng or 2000 ng) was injected into the condylar joint cavity every 2 days. All the mice were euthanized 2 weeks following CFA injection.

2.10

## Synthesis of MCC950@PLGA-FA nanoparticles (NPs)
Firstly, 10 mg of MCC950 (MCE, Shanghai, China) and 60 mg of PLGA (ester terminated, Sigma–Aldrich) was dissolved into 5 mL of dichloromethane (DCM), sonicating for 3 min and, subsequently, mixed with PEG-bis-amine (NH2-PEG-NH2, Sigma–Aldrich). After stirring for 6 h at room temperature, DCM was completely evaporated and the water-suspended MCC950@PLGA NPs were obtained. The NPs in excessive sizes were discarded by 0.45 μm filters and the unloaded MCC950 were removed by repeated centrifugation (8000 g for 5 min).
Folic acid (FA) was conjugated with NH2-PEG-NH2 by the chemical reaction between the active ester of NH2-PEG-NH2 and the amino group at the N-terminus of FA. In brief, 24 mg of folic acid (Aladdin, Shanghai, China) dissolved in 5 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to the solution containing 40 mg of NH2-PEG-NH2 in deionized water under continuous stirring. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 6 h, followed by dialyzing against deionized water. The resultant product was dried by the vacuum freeze dryer. The resultant NPs were collected and washed with deionized water by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min to obtain the FA-enriched nano-delivery system.

2.11

## Long-term toxicity test
The wild type C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into two groups and intra-articularly injected with 20 μL saline, PLGA-FA NPs or MCC950@PLGA-FA NPs, respectively, for 14 days with a dose of 2.5 μg/mL every 3 days. At the endpoint of the experiment, all mice were sacrificed and their hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys were processed for histological sectioning and H&E staining.

2.12

## In vivo imaging
To study the rate of drug metabolism in vivo, TMJ-OA mice were intra-articularly injected with Cy5-PLGA-FA NPs and Cy5-MCC950@PLGA-FA NPs (corresponding Cy5 concentration 1 mg/mL), respectively. Thereafter, the mice were anaesthetized and imaged by IVIS imaging system (Ex: 650 nm, Em: 670 nm, PerkinElmer) at different time points (0, 1, 3 and 5 days). Moreover, 48 h after inhalation of the Cy5-labeled NPs, mice were sacrificed and organs were harvested and imaged on an IVIS system.

2.13

## Therapeutic efficacy of NPs in the TMJ-OA mice model
Eight-week-old male Ddit3−/− mice and their littermates wild type mice (Ddit3+/+) were firstly paired and intraarticularly injected with 20 μL CFA to establish TMJ-OA mouse model. The next day, mice were injected with PLGA-FA NPs or MCC950@PLGA-FA NPs for 14 days. After collecting TMJ specimen, histomorphology and IF staining were utilized to study the therapeutic efficacy of NPs in TMJ-OA mice model.

2.14

## Statistical analysis
Experimental data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism Version 8.0. Data were checked for normal distribution and homoscedasticity before a statistical analysis. For normally distributed data, two groups were compared by paired or unpaired two-tailed Student's t tests. For multiple comparisons, a one-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparisons test was employed. When variances were different, Brown-Forsythe and Welch's ANOVA tests followed by Dunnett's T3 multiple comparisons test was performed. P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.
Additional materials and methods are provided in the Appendix file.

## Results
3.1

## Pyroptosis of synovial macrophages in the synovium of TMJ-OA human and mouse
Firstly, our study identified a substantial elevation in the protein levels of NLRP3 and GSDMD-N/GSDMD-F in OA synovial tissue from patients compared to healthy synovial tissue ( A and B). Especially, significant synovial hyperplasia and extensive cellular infiltration in human osteoarthritic synovial tissues were noted ( C). Immunofluorescence double-labeling staining revealed a significant increase in the proportions of CD68+/GSDMD+ and CD68+/NLRP3+ cells in the OA group ( C and D). Also, CFA-induced TMJ-OA mice exhibited marked pathological alterations, including severe condylar cartilage degradation and inflammatory synovium ( E). Correspondingly, both the modified Mankin score and synovitis score were significantly elevated in the OA model compared to the saline group ( F). Toluidine blue and safranin O/fast green staining indicated a reduction in cartilage proteoglycan in the CFA group ( E and F). Similarly, immunofluorescence double-labeling staining results indicated a significant increase in the proportions of F4/80+/GSDMD+ and F4/80+/NLRP3+ cells in the 2w-CFA-OA group relative to the control group ( G–J). Collectively, these findings suggested a specific accumulation of pyroptotic macrophages in the synovial tissue of patients and TMJ-OA mice. 

3.2

## Synovial macrophage pyroptosis-induced mtDNA release aggravates cartilage degeneration
Primary BMDMs were isolated from WT mice and treated with LPS combined with ATP to construct an in vitro pyroptotic model, which was confirmed by TEM results that displayed pyroptotic morphology including cell swelling and bubbles generation ( A). Both IF staining and qPCR results demonstrated that the mtDNA released into cytoplasm increased over time ( B and C). To determine the role of mtDNA in inducing macrophage pyroptosis, macrophages were exposed to EtBr to generate ρ0 cells with depleted mtDNA. After 7 days of EtBr treatment, cells exhibited approximately 80 % mtDNA loss, as confirmed by IF staining and PCR ( D and E). The ρ0 cells with depleted mtDNA alleviated the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis induced by LPS/ATP treatment ( F and G). Moreover, the elevated levels of mtDNA in the supernatant of cultured macrophages following LPS/ATP treatment were mitigated by mtDNA depletion ( H). Collectively, these findings suggested that mtDNA cytosolic accumulation enhanced NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD-dependent macrophage pyroptosis, subsequently leading to extracellular mtDNA release. 
To simulate the effect of inflammatory synovial macrophages on chondrocytes, we collected conditional medium (CM) from pyroptotic macrophages for in vitro co-coculture with chondrocytes. Our results demonstrated a significant reduction in the mRNA expression levels of Acan, Sox9, and Col2a1 in chondrocytes cocultured with the CM, alongside a marked increase in Mmp13 expression. However, the CM pretreated with DNase I to digest the mtDNA elicited the opposite effects in cocultured chondrocytes ( I). Toluidine blue staining also confirmed that the CM from LPS/ATP-stimulated macrophages exacerbated extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation of chondrocytes, which was significantly alleviated in DNase I group ( J). These findings suggested that synovial macrophage pyroptosis-induced mtDNA release promoted ECM degradation of chondrocytes.
To investigate the functional role of mtDNA in modulating osteoarthritic cartilage degeneration, TMJ-OA mice with macrophage depletion were constructed by intraperitoneally injected with clodronate-containing liposomes (CLOD), and then mtDNA (400 ng or 2000 ng) was injected into the condylar joint cavity. Compared with the CLOD and CLOD + mtDNA (400 ng) group, injection of 2000 ng mtDNA in the TMJ cavity of mice induced obvious histopathological alterations, evidenced by a thinner cartilage layer and disordered cellular arrangement, as confirmed by the modified Mankin score. Safranin O/fast green and toluidine blue staining also demonstrated a reduction in cartilage proteoglycan content in the CLOD + mtDNA (2000 ng) group ( K and L). Additionally, there was an upregulation of COL10A1 expression and a downregulation of ACAN expression in the CLOD + mtDNA (2000 ng) group ( M and N). These findings suggested that synovial macrophage pyroptosis-induced mtDNA release could aggravate cartilage degeneration.

3.3

## DDIT3-deficiency-mediated cytosolic mtDNA release inhibits macrophage pyroptosis
DDIT3 is a downstream transcript factor of ER stress, and the analysis of a publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing dataset from human synovial tissues (GSE216651) showed that DDIT3 high expression macrophage subset associated with NOD-like receptor signaling pathway ( ). Therefore, we examined the role of DDIT3 in synovial macrophage pyroptosis in TMJ-OA patients and mice. First, upregulation of DDIT3 was found in the synovial tissues from TMJ-OA patients, as compared with non-OA individuals ( ). Additionally, immunohistochemical analysis indicated elevated DDIT3 expression in the synovium from TMJ-OA mice ( ). Immunofluorescence double-labeling staining revealed a significant increase in the proportions of F4/80+/DDIT3+ cells in the 2w-CFA-OA group compared to the control group ( ). These results indicated the potential role of DDIT3 in modulating synovial macrophages in the TMJ-OA.
To further illustrate the role of DDIT3 in macrophage pyroptosis, BMDMs were isolated from Ddit3−/− and their littermates Ddit3+/+ mice, then induced into pyroptotic state. As expected, DDIT3 deficiency inhibited the protein expression levels of NLRP3, cleaved-caspase-1, and cleaved-GSDMD ( A and B). Hoechst 33343/PI double fluorescent staining showed that, compared with the control group, DDIT3 deficiency cells inhibited membrane rupture as the PI positive cells were lower ( C and D). In addition, LDH release and ELISA analysis showed that, following treatment with LPS plus ATP, BMDMs from Ddit3−/− mice secreted less LDH, IL-1β, and IL-18 ( E and F). Collectively, these findings indicated that DDIT3 deficiency inhibited macrophage pyroptosis in vitro. 
Then, we investigated the role of DDIT3 in facilitating release of cytosolic mtDNA. Following LPS/ATP treatment, positive staining for cytosolic dsDNA was detected in the vicinity of the mitochondria, a phenomenon that was mitigated by DDIT3 deficiency ( G and H). qRT-PCR further revealed a reduction in cytosolic mtDNA levels in LPS/ATP-treated BMDMs from Ddit3−/− mice ( I). Moreover, BMDMs from Ddit3−/− mice released less mtDNA in the supernatant ( J). To elucidate the role of cytosolic mtDNA in regulating DDIT3-inhibited macrophage pyroptosis, we depleted mtDNA from the macrophages using Etbr, confirmed by IF staining and qRT-PCR ( ). As expected, blockade of mtDNA generation effectively suppressed macrophage pyroptosis ( K and L). Thus, these results suggested that DDIT3-deficiency-mediated cytosolic mtDNA release inhibited macrophage pyroptosis.

3.4

## DDIT3-induced mitochondrial injury leads to the production of mtROS to trigger the cytosolic release of mtDNA in macrophages
Next, we analyzed whether the increased cytosolic release of mtDNA in Ddit3+/+ BMDMs following LPS/ATP treatment was attributable to aggravated mitochondrial injury. As shown by TEM imaging, macrophages exposed to LPS/ATP exhibited a substantial number of mitochondria with disrupted or lost cristae and a swollen morphology, which was alleviated in DDIT3 deficiency cells ( A and B). Besides, MMP level and mtROS production were assessed as indicators of mitochondrial injury. The analysis demonstrated that LPS/ATP treatment resulted in the decreased ratio of red to green fluorescent intensity of JC-1, suggesting loss of MMP. However, the reduction was alleviated by DDIT3 deficiency ( C and D). Conversely, the fluorescence intensity of MitoSOX, an indicator of mtROS production, was markedly elevated, whose increment was attenuated in Ddit3−/− BMDMs ( E and F). These results suggested that DDIT3 deficiency alleviated mitochondrial injury in pyroptotic macrophages. Considering that mtROS was known to impair mitochondrial functions and facilitating the cytosolic release of mtDNA, we then examined whether excessive generation of mtROS influenced DDIT3-mediated mtDNA release in BMDMs utilizing mitoTEMPO, a specific mtROS scavenger. As expected, mitoTEMPO significantly mitigated the cytosolic release of mtDNA in Ddit3+/+ BMDMs ( G, H, I). Moreover, mitoTEMPO markedly attenuated pyroptosis in Ddit3−/− and Ddit3+/+ BMDMs under LPS/ATP treatment ( J and K). These findings demonstrated that DDIT3-induced mitochondrial injury led to the production of mtROS, subsequently triggering the cytosolic release of mtDNA in macrophages. 

3.5

## DDIT3 deficiency alleviates mtROS-mediated cytosolic release of mtDNA in macrophages via enhanced mitophagy
We firstly examined the levels of mitophagy-associated proteins. In LPS/ATP-stimulated Ddit3−/− BMDMs, there was a significant increase in the expression levels of LC3B-II/I, PINK1, and Parkin, while the expression of p62 was markedly decreased, in comparison to Ddit3+/+ BMDMs ( A and B). Furthermore, confocal microscopy analysis revealed that numerous mitochondria were colocalized with or adjacent to lysosomes in LPS/ATP-stimulated BMDMs, indicating the occurrence of mitophagy. And this colocalization was augmented by DDIT3 deficiency ( C and D). We further examined the changes in mitophagy by coimmunostaining PINK1 with Mitotracker Red, the mitochondrial marker. Confocal microscopy analysis demonstrated a substantial colocalization of PINK1 with or adjacent to mitochondrial in LPS/ATP-treated Ddit3−/− BMDMs, compared to Ddit3+/+ BMDMs ( E and F). These results indicated that DDIT3 deficiency enhanced mitophagy in LPS/ATP-treated macrophages. 
To further test whether mitophagy was implicated in decreased mtROS production and cytosolic release of mtDNA in DDIT3 deficiency macrophages, we treated Ddit3−/− and Ddit3+/+ BMDMs with Mdivi-1, a widely utilized mitophagy inhibitor. Mdivi-1 effectively inhibited the expression of PINK1 and Parkin and the conversion of LC3I to LC3II ( G and H), which confirming its efficacy in suppressing PINK1-dependent mitophagy. Notably, mitophagy blockade led to an increased mtROS production in LPS/ATP-treated Ddit3−/− BMDMs, as evidenced by MitoSOX staining ( I and J). Additionally, Mdivi-1 significantly increased cytosolic release of mtDNA in Ddit3−/− BMDMs, whereas no significant effects were observed in Ddit3+/+ BMDMs ( K). To further demonstrate the direct causal relationship between DDIT3 deficiency and PINK1-dependent mitophagy, genetic knockdown of PINK1 in Ddit3−/− macrophages by siRNA was performed and the results showed that such knockdown reversed the protective phenotypes with increased mtROS and mtDNA ( ). These results collectively demonstrated that DDIT3 deficiency alleviated mtROS-mediated cytosolic release of mtDNA in macrophages via enhanced mitophagy.

3.6

## DDIT3-deficiency-inhibited macrophage pyroptosis alleviates ECM degradation of chondrocytes and TMJ-OA progression
To determine the impact of DDIT3-mediated macrophages pyroptosis on ECM degradation of chondrocytes in vitro, primary chondrocytes were co-cultured with CM from Ddit3−/− and Ddit3+/+ BMDMs. qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the mRNA levels of Acan, Sox9, and Col2a1 were decreased, while that of Mmp13 was increased following LPS/ATP treatment ( A), which implied that the anabolism of ECM was inhibited by CM from pyroptotic cells compared with the control cells. This phenomenon was alleviated by DDIT3 deficiency ( A). Alcian blue staining corroborated that the suppression of ECM synthesis in chondrocytes due to the paracrine effects of pyroptotic macrophages was attenuated by DDIT3 deficiency ( B). These results were further validated by IF staining for COL2A1 expression in chondrocytes ( C and D). 
To elucidate the role of DDIT3-mediated synovial macrophage pyroptosis in vivo, Ddit3−/− and their littermates Ddit3+/+ mice were applied to construct TMJ-OA model. It was found that Ddit3+/+ mice present marked proliferation of synovial lining cells, apparent infiltration of mononucleated cells, proliferative and dilated blood vessels, and abundant sub-synovial lipid droplets within the inflamed synovium, however, DDIT3 deficiency mitigated the synovitis score ( E and F). Furthermore, IF staining results showed that F4/80+/GSDMD+/+ and F4/80+/NLRP3+/+ cells were drastically decreased in synovium of Ddit3−/− TMJ-OA mice ( G and H). Additionally, compared to Ddit3+/+ mice, Ddit3−/− mice showed reduced condylar cartilage degeneration and a modified Mankin score, characterized by diminished chondrocyte clustering and cartilage matrix loss ( I and J). And the IF staining showed the restrained expression of COL10A1 (catabolic mediator) as well as heightened ACAN (anabolic mediator) in the degraded condylar cartilage of Ddit3−/− mice relative to Ddit3+/+ mice ( K and L). Collectively, these findings suggested that DDIT3 deficiency alleviated TMJ-OA progression by inhibiting synovial macrophage pyroptosis.

3.7

## Targeting inhibition of synovial macrophage pyroptosis alleviates synovial inflammation and cartilage degeneration in TMJ-OA mice
To confirm the therapeutic effect of inhibiting synovial macrophage pyroptosis on TMJ-OA progression, PLGA nanoparticles (NPs) were adopted as the drug delivery system as previously reported [ , ]. MCC950, the NLRP3 specific inhibitor, was encapsulated within PLGA NPs, which were then functionalized with folic acid (FA) to specifically target activated macrophages ( A). TEM revealed that both PLGA-FA NPs and MCC950@PLGA-FA NPs possessed a uniform spherical shape and smooth surface morphology ( B). The dynamic light scattering analysis indicated that the mean particle size of PLGA-FA NPs was 146 nm, slightly larger than that of MCC950@PLGA-FA NPs (122 nm), suggesting that dense nanoparticles formed upon MCC950 loading ( C). The zeta potential of MCC950@PLGA-FA NPs increased from −27.67 ± 2.11 mV to −21.24 ± 3.74 mV compared to PLGA-FA NPs ( D). Drug release studies conducted at simulated extracellular pH of synovial fluid (7.8) and intercellular pH of M1 macrophages (7.5) revealed that MCC950 exhibited a cumulative release of 68 % at pH 7.5 and 48 % at pH 7.8 after 48 h incubation ( E). And CCK8 assay indicated that NPs had no effect on cell viability at concentration below 2.5 μg/mL ( F). Therefore, 2.5 μg/mL NPs were utilized for subsequent experiments. 
To evaluate the cellular uptake of the PLGA-FA NPs, BMDMs were exposed to FITC-labeled PLGA-FA NPs. Confocal microscopy analysis revealed that both FITC-labeled PLGA-FA NPs and MCC950@PLGA-FA NPs exhibited remarkably enhanced intracellular green fluorescence in LPS/ATP-activated BMDMs compared to untreated BMDMs ( G, ), confirming the facilitated uptake of FA-modified NPs by activated macrophages through the specific interaction between FA and folate receptor. Moreover, the anti-pyroptotic effects of MCC950@PLGA-FA NPs following internalization in vitro were validated by western blot analysis ( ).
Then, PLGA-FA NPs and MCC950@PLGA-FA NPs were administered intra-articularly in TMJ-OA mice to evaluate their targeting ability, biosafety, and metabolic rate. The results showed that the modified-FA efficiently enhanced the uptake of FITC-labeled NPs by F4/80+ synovial macrophages rather than chondrocytes ( ). No evidence of tissue damage was observed in mice treated with PLGA-FA NPs and MCC950@PLGA-FA NPs ( ). Additionally, the fluorescence intensity of Cy5-labeled NPs markedly decreased after 3 days, and was undetectable after 5 days in vivo by an IVIS imaging system ( H).
To assess the effects of NPs in vivo, PLGA-FA NPs and MCC950@PLGA-FA NPs were injected into the TMJ of Ddit3−/− and their littermates Ddit3+/+ mice every three days after induction of TMJ-OA, and all mice were sacrificed on day 14 ( I). Results found that intra-articular administration of MCC950@PLGA-FA NPs greatly relieved synovitis and synovial macrophage pyroptosis in Ddit3+/+ mice, which was more obvious in Ddit3−/− mice ( J, K, L, M). Besides, the area of safranin O and toluidine blue staining and expression level of ACAN in condylar cartilage of Ddit3+/+ mice treated with MCC950@PLGA-FA were greatly increased, while the expression level of COL10A1 was decreased ( ). Collectively, these results indicated that targeting inhibition of synovial macrophage pyroptosis by MCC950@PLGA-FA NPs mitigated synovial inflammation and cartilage degeneration in TMJ-OA mice, which mimicked the protective effects of DDIT3 deficiency against OA progression.

## Discussion
Chronic synovial inflammation is recognized as a primary contributor to cartilage degradation and plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of OA, but the precise mechanisms by which synovial macrophages initiate inflammation and aggravate OA remain controversial. In this study, we confirmed the pyroptosis of synovial macrophages in both OA patients and mice, and showed that pyroptotic macrophages could induce the ECM degradation of chondrocytes through the release of extracellular mtDNA, highlighting their crucial role in OA pathogenesis.
Pyroptosis is a form of lytic programmed cell death characterized by the rapid rupture of plasma membrane and the subsequent release of proinflammatory intracellular contents [ ]. In this study, our findings demonstrated that upon LPS/ATP treatment, increased mtDNA leakage into cytoplasm could promote NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD-dependent macrophage pyroptosis, and then facilitating the extracellular release of mtDNA. Moreover, DNase I treatment of CM from pyroptotic macrophages was found to mitigate ECM degradation of the cocultured chondrocytes. Previous research has indicated a positive correlation between synovial fluid mtDNA levels and the degree of inflammation in arthritis patients, with elevated mtDNA reflecting the extent of cartilage damage after articular joint [ , ], but whether inflammatory synovial macrophages could release mtDNA and exert paracrine effect on cartilage was still unclear. By injecting mtDNA into TMJ cavity of TMJ-OA mice with macrophage depletion, we discovered that the large dose of mtDNA could aggravate condylar cartilage degeneration.
DDIT3, as a key factor downstream of ER stress pathway, has been implicated in the regulation of macrophage inflammatory responses and polarization in inflammation-related diseases [ , ]. In this study, we observed a significant increase in the proportion of F4/80+/DDIT3+ cells within the synovial tissues from TMJ-OA mice. These findings suggested a potential role of DDIT3 in modulating synovial macrophage in TMJ-OA. It has been reported that DDIT3 could activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, subsequently initiating hepatocyte pyroptosis [ ]. Similarly, we demonstrated that DDIT3 deficiency inhibited macrophage pyroptosis in vitro. Although several studies have highlighted the pivotal role of DDIT3 in regulating mitochondrial dysfunction [ , , ], its involvement in the regulation of mtDNA release remains unknown. Our results demonstrated that DDIT3 deficiency suppressed cytosolic mtDNA release in macrophages with LPS/ATP treatment, and blockade of mtDNA generation effectively inhibited macrophage pyroptosis in Ddit3+/+ BMDMs, suggesting DDIT3-deficiency-mediated cytosolic mtDNA release inhibits macrophage pyroptosis.
Overproduced mtROS could contribute to mitochondrial membrane damage, facilitating the release of mtDNA into the cytosol and consequently inducing cytokine release and macrophage infiltrations [ , ]. Our results revealed that in LPS/ATP-treated macrophages, DDIT3 deficiency ameliorated mitochondrial injury, characterized by increased MMP and decreased mtROS production. Moreover, the application of mitoTEMPO to scavenge mtROS significantly reduced the cytosolic release of mtDNA in Ddit3+/+ BMDMs. Additionally, accumulating evidence indicates that disruption of mitophagy results in an accumulation of damaged mitochondria due to immune stimuli, which in turn increases mtROS production and mtDNA release, thereby inducing NLRP3 hyperactivation and leading to excessive inflammation and tissue damage [ , ]. A recent study revealed that LPS-primed NF-κB activation inhibited macrophage NLRP3 inflammasome activation by promoting p62-mediated Parkin-dependent mitophagy [ ]. Our results showed that DDIT3 deficiency activated PINK1/Parkin-dependent mitophagy in LPS/ATP-stimulated macrophages. Furthermore, inhibiting mitophagy drives macrophage activation in a mtROS-dependent manner [ ]. In this study, blocking mitophagy with Mdivi-1 increased mtROS production and cytosolic release of mtDNA in DDIT3 deficiency macrophages, suggesting that DDIT3 deficiency alleviated mtROS-mediated cytosolic release of mtDNA in macrophages via enhanced mitophagy. Thus, the mitophagy activation in pyroptotic macrophages results in decreased cytosolic mtDNA release from damaged mitochondria to alleviate inflammation, which may protect damaged cartilage in OA.
Utilizing a CM-based indirect co-culture model, we present compelling evidence that DDIT3-mediated macrophage pyroptosis acted as a major driver of ECM degradation of chondrocytes. Since our previous study has demonstrated that DDIT3 aggravated monosodium iodoacetate (MIA)-induced TMJOA cartilage degradation via directly inhibiting autophagy-mediated chondrocyte dysfunction [ ], in this study, we investigated the molecular mechanism of the indirect effect of DDIT3 on condylar cartilage injury by constructing the CFA-induced TMJ-OA mice model. The sustained inflammation of TMJ induced by CFA could lead to degenerative changes in TMJ synovium and disc, which is similar to the structural changes in individuals with TMJ-OA [ ]. The in vivo results demonstrated that DDIT3 deficiency exerted an anti-inflammatory effect on synovial macrophage pyroptosis and cartilage degeneration in TMJ-OA mice. Therefore, this study supported a novel role of DDIT3 in contributing to macrophage pyroptosis-amplified ECM degradation of chondrocytes and cartilage degeneration through a paracrine manner.
The present work also proposes viable therapeutic targets for alleviating OA. Focusing on the extracellular mtDNA-induced pathogenic pathway, potential therapeutic interventions may involve the prevention of synovial macrophage pyroptosis. Hence, the bioresorbable polyester PLGA was chosen to construct the inner core for drug loading due to its low toxicity, high loading capacity, and sustained release capability [ ]. Folic acid, which can bind the overexpressed folic receptor beta (FR-β) usually present on the cell membrane of activated macrophages [ ], is conjugated on the surface of PLGA NPs to specifically target activated macrophage. And the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 is incorporated to inhibit macrophage pyroptosis. By intra-articular injection, MCC950@PLGA-FA NPs effectively mimicked the protective effects of DDIT3 deficiency against OA progression.
However, it is imperative to acknowledge certain limitations and future directions of this study. Firstly, while we found that targeting inhibition of synovial macrophage pyroptosis by PLGA NPs could alleviate OA progression in DDIT3 knockout mice, myeloid cell-specific conditional knockout mice may provide a more appropriate model for elucidating the role and mechanisms of DDIT3-mediated macrophage pyroptosis in cartilage degeneration. Besides, this study is exclusively performed in a TMJ-OA model induced by CFA, whether this mechanism is universal to other OA contexts (e.g., post-traumatic, age-related) requires further investigation. Secondly, despite our validation that mtDNA represents a key DAMP released from pyroptotic macrophages that aggravates cartilage degeneration, the potential involvement of other secreted factors from pyroptotic macrophages in OA progression cannot be dismissed. Thirdly, the potential channels on the mitochondrial membrane involved in mtDNA cytosolic release from damaged mitochondria has not been investigated in this study. Besides, whether mtDNA is released by pyroptotic macrophages via extracellular membrane vesicles (EVs) and the relevance of mtDNA-containing EVs to cartilage damage in OA remain unclear. Moreover, how the exogenous mtDNA promotes cartilage degeneration warrants further clarified.
In conclusion, this study presented experimental evidence that synovial macrophage pyroptosis-induced mtDNA release played a pivotal role in the pathology of TMJ-OA. We elucidated that DDIT3-deficiency-enhanced mitophagy in macrophages alleviated mitochondrial damage, mtROS-mediated cytosolic mtDNA leakage, and the subsequent macrophage pyroptosis, thereby inhibiting the extracellular release of cytosolic mtDNA, and exerting protective effect in condylar cartilage ( ). Furthermore, interventions targeting the mechanism of synovial macrophage pyroptosis by modified PLGA nanoparticles is expected to provide new insights into alleviating OA.
