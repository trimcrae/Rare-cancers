## ABSTRACT
Epigenetic regulation of the angiotensin‐converting enzyme (ACE)—particularly CpG methylation within its promoter—may modulate gene expression and skeletal muscle adaptation to endurance exercise, complementing the known effects of the ACE I/D polymorphism (rs1799752). We hypothesized that ACE promoter methylation correlates with markers of aerobic metabolism and interacts with genotype and enzyme activity to explain interindividual variability in muscle oxidative capacity. 114 samples from healthy, physically diverse white Caucasian men (age: 29.5 ± 8.1 years; weight: 76.9 ± 14.5 kg) were analyzed for aerobic capacity, metabolic profiles and cellular composition of m. vastus lateralis. Molecular endpoints of the ACE system included ACE genotype, promoter methylation (23 CpG sites), mRNA expression, enzyme activity, angiotensin 2 levels, and markers of mitochondrial, glycolytic, and lipid metabolism. ACE promoter methylation in muscle was compared to the methylation in capillary blood from a second study group of 24 VO2max‐ and genotype‐matched male white Caucasian subjects. Statistical analyses comprised Pearson correlations, regression modeling (α = 0.05), network analysis, and ANOVA. Participants exhibited a wide range in VO₂max (2.1–5.4 L·min−1) and ACE promoter methylation (0.37 ± 0.18). ACE promoter methylation in muscle was higher than in blood, doubling with heart rate‐elevating physical activity > 6 h weekly and endurance exercise (p < 0.001, η
2 = 0.090 and η
2 = 0.059), and was lowest in inactive, aerobically unfit ACE I‐allele carriers. Exercise induced significant metabolic shifts, including a rise in blood angiotensin 2 from 18.1 to 45.6 pg/mL. ACE promoter methylation was inversely correlated with ACE activity (r = −0.406), angiotensin 2 (r = −0.447), and post‐exercise ACE mRNA expression (r = −0.745), and there was an influence of the ACE I/D gene polymorphism in interaction with the state of aerobically fitness (p = 0.046, η
2 = 0.297). Regression models incorporating ACE regulatory parameters significantly explained variance in slow‐twitch fiber percentage and cross‐sectional area, mitochondrial volume density, and key lipid/glucose metabolites (mean r = 0.493; power > 0.8). Seven metabolites, including acetyl‐CoA, adenosine monophosphate, and phosphocreatine, displayed opposing associations with ACE genotype and methylation, indicating divergent regulatory pathways. Notably, low promoter methylation exerted pronounced effects in ACE‐II homozygotes. ACE promoter methylation, integrated with other regulatory indexes of the ACE system, contributes to the slow aerobic muscle phenotype and its metabolic response to endurance exercise. Low ACE‐promoter methylation may override I‐allele–driven transcriptional silencing, offering a novel marker of individual training adaptation.

Weekly moderate‐to‐vigorous activity (≈6 h) induced a marked increase in ACE promoter methylation in exercised knee‐extensor muscle. In combination with the ACE I/D genotype, ACE methylation contributed to ACE gene activity regulation in ways aligning with the slow‐twitch, aerobic muscle phenotype and its metabolic response to endurance exercise. Strongest activity‐related effects on promoter methylation occurred in unfit ACE‐II homozygotes and those showing lowest promoter methylation. ACE promoter methylation may offer to refine personalized endurance‐training recommendations aimed at optimizing metabolic adaptations.

## Metabolic Compounds
Polar metabolites and non‐polar lipids in muscle biopsies were analyzed with MS, essentially as described [ ]. For the analysis of polar compounds, 5 mg of muscle sample was extracted in a corresponding volume of cold methanol:methyl‐tert‐butyl ether:H20 = 360:1200:348, and the polar was recovered, dried down and reconstituted in 100 μL of 50 mM ammonium acetate in acetonitrile: water 9:1 (v/v). 1 μL of sample was separated across a BEH‐Amide capillary column (200 μm × 150 mm, 1.7 μm particle size; Waters, Baden‐Dättwil, Switzerland) with a gradient of ammonium acetate in acetonitrile and water using nanoAquity UPLC (ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography; Waters, Baden‐Dättwil, Switzerland). Fractions were automatically injected into the coupled Q Exactive Hybrid Quadrupole‐Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Reinach, Switzerland) via a nano‐electrospray ionization source with an ion fragmentation over a range of 80–1200 m/z. Mass spectroscopy (MS) data were acquired using a negative polarization at a resolution of 70 000 (MS) and 25 000 (MSMS).
Non‐polar lipids were characterized in extracts being prepared from ~15 mg of muscle sample with the help of a ball mill into cold chloroform: methanol = 1:1. The non‐polar phase was flash‐frozen, and samples were directly infused into a Thermo LTQ Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) that was operated in negative electrospray ionization mode. The MS data were acquired with settings for independent fragmentation of all ions and stepped collision energy.
Datasets were processed with Progenesis QI (Nonlinear Dynamics, A Waters Company), followed by peak picking on an aggregated ion intensity map to detect and quantify the compound ions of interest based on the consensus retention times of observed mass‐to‐charge ratio (m/z) signals for the major adduct ions in all the samples. The detected compound ions were further annotated through database searches, including the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, www.hmdb.ca), to match observed fragmentation spectra with theoretical fragmentation spectra within a tolerance of 50 m Daltons. Lipid compounds were identified by comparison to the lipidmaps Database (lipidmaps.org) for a match in the neutral mass and hydrogen adducts, with a tolerance of 70 m Daltons. Determined total electron counts for the respectively identified compounds were normalized to the average of signals of all measures for the respective compound for subsequent quantitative analyses. The adenine nucleotide energy charge was calculated as described from the signals for ATP, ADP and AMP as described [ ]. Quality controls were run on individual and mixed samples to determine technical accuracy based on 20 selected compounds (amino acids, nucleotides, and metabolic intermediates) in mixed samples using Quan Browser (Xcalibur, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 61 further abundant ions using Progenesis QI software (Nonlinear Dynamics). The coefficients of variation for biological and technical replicas demonstrated values near or below 20%.
In addition, glycogen concentration in muscle biopsies was quantified in a volume of 1 mm3 tissue per total muscle protein using the Assay Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), as described [ ].

## Cellular Composition
The capillary‐to‐fiber ratio, the mean cross‐sectional area (MCSA) and percentage of slow (type I) and fast (type II) muscle fibers were determined in 15 μm‐thick cryosections from pre‐exercise biopsies on microscopic recordings with an Axioskop 2 system (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) and subsequent quantification using the ImageJ software (version 1.6.0_33; http://imagej.nih.gov/ij), based on immunological or histochemical methods and morphometric methodology as described [ , , ]. At least 24 representative fibers were analyzed per subject. In addition, the composition of muscle fibers in terms of the volume density of myofibrils, mitochondria and intramyocellular lipids was quantified using morphometric methodology on electron micrographs from frozen and/or glutaraldehyde‐fixed bioptic material as described and published in parts in other contexts [ , ].

2.9

## Substrates in Blood Serum
The concentrations of glucose, triacylglycerol (TAG), total cholesterol (T Chol), low‐density lipoprotein and high‐density lipoprotein (LDL and HDL), and total ketone (mainly bHb), were monitored in thirty microliters of capillary blood using a portable whole blood test system (CardioCheck ST Analyzer, Polymer Technology Systems; Indianapolis, IN, USA) as described [ ].

2.10

## Genotyping
Genomic DNA was extracted from bioptic material (group 1) and mucosa (group 2) as described [ , ]. DNA was subjected to specific polymerase chain reactions with defined reagents on a magnetic induction cycler (Mic Real‐Time Polymerase Chain Reactions (PCR) System, Bio Molecular Systems; Labgene, Châtel‐St‐Denis, Switzerland). The specific conditions included the use of 200 nM sense and antisense oligonucleotides in combination with a SensiFAST HRM Mix (Meridian Biosciences; BIO32005, Labgene, Châtel‐St‐Denis, Switzerland) and temperature protocols to detect the presence (I) or deletion (D) of the 287‐bp long Alu‐repeat sequence in intron 16 of the ACE gene on chromosome 17 as outlined [ , ]. Genotypes were identified based on high‐resolution melting curves against genotype references as established in previous PCR experiments based on Sanger sequencing of the amplicons by a commercial provider (Microsynth, Balgach, Switzerland).

2.11

## ACE and COX4 Transcript Expression
The transcript levels of COX4I1, ACE, and GAPDH mRNA were quantified as described previously [ ]. In brief, total RNA was isolated from 5 mm3 of biopsy sample using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hombrechtikon, Switzerland) and 600 ng was reverse‐transcribed using the Omniscript RT Kit with random hexamers (Qiagen, Hombrechtikon, Switzerland). cDNAs were amplified from 6 ng of cDNA and quantified via polymerase chain reactions. The primers used were as follows: For COX4I1 (forward, 5′‐GCC ATG TTC TTC ATC GGT TTC‐3′; reverse, 5′‐GGC CGT ACA CAT AGT GCT TCT G‐3′), for ACE (forward, 5′‐TCA CTA CGG GGC CCA GCA CA‐3′; reverse, 5′‐TGC GCC CAC ATG TTC CCC AG‐3′) and for GADPH (forward primer: 5′‐GGA GCG AGA CCC CAC TAA CA‐3′; reverse, 5′‐GCC TTC TCC ATG GTG GTG AA‐3′). Reactions were run at a primer concentration of 100 nM using KAPA SYBR Fast Universal Master mix (Ref# KK4601, Merck, Buchs, Switzerland) and run on a StepOne Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Fisher Scientific, Reinach, Switzerland). The thermal protocol consisted of 40 cycles of denaturation for 15 s at 95°C, annealing, and extension at 60°C. The specificity of amplification was assessed based on melting curves versus a reference that was constituted from a dilution of a PCR sample, for which specificity was confirmed by commercial microsequencing (Microsynth, Balgach, Switzerland). Relative transcript amounts were calculated using the comparative CT method, taking the amplification efficiency for each template into account using the comparative method for the threshold cycle for target amplification, as described [ ]. For each sample, transcript signals (in fluorescent counts) were related to the estimated input amount of total RNA in microgram and instances expressed per GAPDH transcript levels.

2.12

## ACE Activity
The enzymatic activity of ACE per muscle protein was determined with a fluorescence‐based assay based on the conversion of Abz‐Gly‐p‐nitro‐Phe‐Pro‐OH and expressed as conversion per microgram of muscle protein and relative to the concentration of the fluorogenic substrate Abz‐Gly‐OH (Bachem AG, Bubendorf, Switzerland) in muscle homogenates as described [ , ].

2.13

## Angiotensin 2 Concentration
The octapeptide angiotensin was determined on venous blood being collected before and immediately after one‐legged exercise under ice‐collected conditions in the presence of relevant inhibitors with a commercial ELISA system as described [ ].

2.14

## Scaling of the Data
To enable integrative analyses of muscle composition, selected morphometric parameters were scaled using molecular proxies and pooled across datasets. Missing values for mitochondrial volume density were imputed based on a validated relationship between transcript levels of the mitochondrial marker COX4I1 mRNA (expressed as counts per μg total RNA) and morphometric estimates of total mitochondrial volume density (expressed as % per muscle fiber) in resting biopsy samples of m. vastus lateralis. Similarly, the volume density of intramyocellular lipid (VV(li,f)) was estimated by integrating morphometric data with molecular concentrations of five representative triglyceride compounds (PE 38:5, FA 14:4; O3, PI 35:3, ACer 56:1; O2, PS 40:5) as detected by mass spectroscopy. This scaling approach was established in previous studies involving measurements in biopsy samples of m. vastus lateralis muscle at rest [ , , , ]. Muscle fiber cross‐sectional area (MCSA) was derived by combining light microscopic measurements with electron microscopic estimates. To account for tissue shrinkage induced by glutaraldehyde fixation, a correction factor of 27% was applied, consistent with established protocols for ultrastructural preservation and dimensional scaling [ ].

2.15

## Statistics
Statistical analyses were conducted using the JASP package (version 0.18.3; https://jasp‐stats.org/download/; 11.01.2024). Pearson's moment correlations were carried out to test linear relationships between the 8 aspects of ACE gene activity (i.e., model terms, Figure  ) versus the 102 assessed metabolic parameters as these were affiliated with 9 ontologies of general metabolic/energetic functioning (i.e., energy metabolism, lipid metabolism, ketone metabolism, substrate handling, muscle metabolism, contraction, systemic metabolism, signaling) and specific metabolic functioning (glycolysis, mitochondrial metabolism, respiration, energy metabolism, contraction, contraction per respiration, systemic metabolism, turnover, signaling). The numeric coding of the ACE promoter methylation and ACE genotype (rs1799752) for correlation analysis were according to the anticipated inhibitory influence on the expression/activity on the ACE transcript and enzyme; whereby a score of zero and one applied for a promoter methylation below or above the group average, respectively, and a score of one for each ACE I‐allele. The code for the combined effects of genotype and epigenotype was calculated by the sum of the score for promoter methylation and ACE I/D genotype. For a linear relationship to be deemed relevant the respective p values had to meet a threshold of < 0.05, categorized based on the following criteria: very strong > 0.8; strong > 0.6; moderate > 0.4; weak > 0.2. In general, weak correlations are not reported in the main manuscript. The connectivity of linear relationships was assessed for hierarchic organization and displayed using the Cytoscape software (version 3.10.1) [ ]. Linear regression analysis was carried out to estimate the in‐sample model‐fit of a dependent parameter based on the 8 covariates (model terms of ACE gene activity regulation, i.e., ACE promoter methylation, ACE genotype and ACE enzyme activity, and angiotensin 2 levels). Data were anonymized and missing data were assigned as NAN for the statistical evaluation. Data are presented as Mean ± standard deviation (SD), under provision of minima and maxima. Single or interaction effects of a genotype, physical activity, or aerobic fitness (based on a VO2peak cutoff value of 50 mL O2 min−1 kg−1) [ , ], on the assessed factors were assessed with ANOVA with respective post hoc tests of Tukey or Fisher. Effects of exercise on mRNA expression and metabolic parameters were evaluated using repeated‐measures ANOVAs. Interaction effects between genotype and promoter methylation were examined by categorizing ACE promoter methylation into two groups—higher or lower CpG methylation relative to the sample mean. Comparisons of ACE promoter methylation between capillary blood and vastus lateralis muscle were performed using a two‐factor ANOVA with the factors “tissue type” and “CpG dinucleotide number”. This analysis included 24 subjects from the second group who were matched for VO2max and ACE I/D genotype, allowing a maximum deviation of 7% in VO2max (Table  ).

## Results
3.1

## Subject Characteristics
Table  summarizes the anatomical and physiological characteristics of the studied men. The values exemplify the good health of the subjects, where twenty‐five of them met the criteria of a good aerobic capacity (> 50 mL O2 min−1 kg−1 reviewed [ ]).
 
3.2

## ACE Promoter Methylation
In order to assess methylation of promoter of the ACE gene we adapted a bisulfate sequencing‐based PCR procedure. The amplicon for the ACE promoter was detected as a broad peak, sometimes with two maxima, indicating a mix of DNA variants existed where cytosines are converted to thymidines during the bisulfate reaction. The ACE promoter exhibited a heterogeneous CpG‐methylation profile differentiating the degree of cytosine methylation for CpG site 9 from that of sites 12, 15, and 21 (Figure  ); and summing to a mean degree of CpG dinucleotide methylation of the ACE promoter of 0.284 ± 0.088.
 
3.3

## Association of ACE Promoter Methylation With Aerobic Physical Activity and Fitness
Variability in the mean degree of ACE promoter methylation in vastus lateralis muscle was associated with the weekly volume of moderate‐to‐vigorous physical activity (p < 0.001, η
2 = 0.090). Methylation levels were approximately twice as high in subjects performing more than 6 h of heart‐rate–elevating exercise per week over the past year compared with those performing less (Figure  ).
Consistently, ACE promoter methylation in vastus lateralis muscle was associated with habitual endurance exercise (p < 0.001, η
2 = 0.059), being 0.220 points higher in the endurance exercising subjects, and showed a trend towards an interaction with the ACE I/D genotype (p = 0.067, η
2 = 0.010). The lowest methylation levels were observed in ACE I‐allele carriers who did not engage in endurance exercise (Figure  ). Accordingly, ACE promoter methylation was higher in aerobically fit subjects compared with unfit subjects (0.451 ± 0.139 vs. 0.195 ± 0.139; p < 0.001, η
2 = 0.297), and this effect interacted with the ACE I/D genotype (p = 0.046, η
2 = 0.297). ACE‐DD genotypes exhibited the highest degree of ACE promoter methylation overall (p = 0.009), an effect that was further accentuated among aerobically unfit individuals (Figure  ). The ACE I/D genotype per se did not affect ACE promotor methylation (p = 0.083, η
2 = 0.033).

ACE promoter methylation in vastus lateralis muscle differed from that observed in peripheral blood in a second group of subjects matched for aerobic fitness and ACE I/D genotype (Table  , Figure  ; p = 0.047, η
2 = 0.004). This difference reflected a higher overall proportion of ACE promoter methylation in vastus lateralis muscle compared with capillary blood (0.325 vs. 0.270). Tissue dependence further interacted with CpG site number (p = 0.010, η
2 = 0.057): muscle showed greater cytosine methylation at CpG sites 6, 9, and 24, whereas CpG site 21 was more highly methylated in capillary blood.

3.4

## The Metabolic Stimulus of Exhaustive One‐Legged Exercise
Subjects achieved an average maximal performance of 188.9 ± 43.4 W and a maximal oxygen uptake of 3388.4 ± 672.0 mL O2 min−1 during the two‐phasic one‐legged exercise into exhaustion. Table  characterizes the metabolic characteristics of the exercise to exhaustion as assessed during and immediately after exercise with ergospirometry, from venous blood and 0.5 h after exercise in biopsies of vastus lateralis. It revealed that the exercise elevated respiration exchange ratio to an average maximal value of 0.904, increased the blood concentration of the metabolic substrates, glucose (+11.7%), HDL (+24.0%), while decreasing the ketones concentration (mainly bHb, −9.6%) immediately after cessation of pedaling.
 A metabolic contribution to exhaustion during one‐legged exercise into exhaustion was exemplified by alterations in compounds being associated with glucose and lipid metabolism in vastus lateralis muscle (p < 0.001 for the interaction between exercise × compound, Figure  ). Exercise‐induced changes being associated with glycolysis concerned the lowering of the concentration in glycogen (−65%), phospho‐enolypyruvate (−45%) and lactate (+34%) 30 min after exercise. Equally, 30 min after exercise lipid associated compounds were affected. This concerned strong increases in acetyl‐CoA (+453%) and ALCAR (+554%), as well as increased levels of 4 lipids (i.e., the glycerophospholipids PE 38:5‐LMGP02010538 (+319%), PS 41:6‐LMGP03010479 (+222%), SQDG 32:1‐LMGL05010007 (+77%) and the glycerolipid DG 35:6‐LMGL02010377 (+155%), Figure  ). Concomitantly, reduced levels were identified for 6 lipids, i.e., the three glycerolipids DG 35:3‐LMGL02010374 (−67%), TG 40:0‐LMGL03012632 (−73%), TG 64:11‐LMGL03012482 (−56%), the two fatty acyls FA 14:1‐LMFA01020132 (−25%), CAR 12:0‐LMFA07070062 (−42%), and the glycerophospholipid LPA 17:1‐LMGP10050002 (−94%).
 The concentration of the ketone, beta hydroxybutyrate, in vastus lateralis was not affected by exercise (p = 0.120). Energy metabolism related alterations 30 min post exercise concerned a reduction in AMP concentration (−32%), as well as increases in the concentration of phosphocreatine (+46%) and the ratio of phosphocreatine to creatine (PCr/Cr), reflecting phosphate‐charged creatine availability (+68%).
The concentration of angiotensin 2 in blood serum was increased immediately after the cessation of pedaling during the one‐legged exercise (pre: 18.1 ± 9.9 vs. post: 45.6 ± 10.3 pg. mL‐1; p = 0.023; η
2 = 0.245). ACE transcript levels per total RNA in vastus lateralis muscle tended to be increased 8 h post exercise (0.414 ± 0.246 vs. 0.534 ± 0.160 fluorescent counts; p = 0.051, η
2 = 0.080).

3.5

## Correlation Network of ACE Gene Activity Regulation in Muscle With Exercise
Aspects of ACE gene and activity regulation in muscle at rest and with exercise for the 44 subjects studied were inter‐related, defining a network of linear relationships. For ACE promoter methylation this included, at baseline, negative correlations with ACE mRNA per GAPDH (r = −0.743, p < 0.001), ACE activity in vastus lateralis (r = −0.406, p = 0.047) and angiotensin 2 concentration (r = −0.447, p = 0.036). Post exercise, ACE promoter methylation inversely correlated with ACE mRNA per GAPDH in vastus lateralis muscle 8 h after exercise (r = −0.745, p < 0.001) but was unrelated to angiotensin 2 concentration immediately after exercise (r = 0.242, p = 0.825). ACE promoter methylation was unrelated to the ACE genotype (r = −0.110, p = 0.488). Further relationships concerned positive relationships between blood angiotensin 2 concentration and ACE activity (r = 0.572, p = 0.008) and ACE mRNA concentration in muscle (r = 0.429, p = 0.043) at baseline; when ACE activity in vastus lateralis muscle correlated negatively with post‐exercise concentrations (r = −0.466, p = 0.030) and with exercise‐induced fold changes in circulating angiotensin 2 (r = −0.616, p = 0.004; Figure  ).
 
3.6

## ACE (Epi‐)genotype Associated Effect of Exercise on ACE Gene Expression
Exercise‐induced level alterations in ACE transcript levels in vastus lateralis muscle were affected by the interaction between the ACE gene polymorphism and ACE promoter methylation. Thereby ACE transcript levels tended to decrease alone in non‐I‐allele carriers (i.e., ACE‐DD genotypes) with a low promoter methylation when it was increased in I‐allele carriers with a low promoter methylation; and in subjects with a high promoter methylation, irrespective of whether they carried the I‐allele (Figure  ).
 Correlation analysis identified that the degree of cytosine methylation of CpG numbered 1–13 and 25 was significantly correlated with the ACE mRNA level in vastus lateralis muscle prior to/after exhaustive one‐legged exercise (Figure  ).

3.7

## Exploring Relationships Between (Epi‐)genetic Aspects of ACE Gene Activity and Muscle‐Related Parameters
We investigated whether associations exist between variables of 
ACE
 gene activity regulation in skeletal muscle and trait characteristics of the slow aerobic muscle phenotype.
Specifically, we assessed correlations between eight assessed ACE regulatory components—including ACE promoter methylation in muscle, ACE I/D genotype, their interaction term, ACE mRNA expression, ACE enzymatic activity in muscle, and venous concentrations of angiotensin 2 (pre‐exercise, post‐exercise, and fold change)—and 102 local and systemic parameters reflecting molecular and cellular aspects of muscle metabolism at rest and following exhaustive exercise. Figure  illustrates the distribution of significant linear relationships identified across 1984 possible correlations, whereby 23 very strong, 121 strong, 168 moderate, and 21 weak significant linear relationships were identified. Among these, we observed 37 correlations with ACE genotype, 57 correlations with ACE promoter methylation, and 54 correlations with the additive score combining genotype and methylation status (see Table  ). Further associations included 22 correlations with ACE mRNA expression, 32 correlations with ACE activity in vastus lateralis muscle, 56 correlations with venous angiotensin 2 concentration pre‐exercise, 39 correlations post‐exercise, and 36 correlations with the fold change in angiotensin 2 levels.
 Linear relationships between the assessed metabolic parameters to the 8 model terms of ACE gene activity regulation differed between the parameters (p = 0.006, η
2 = 0.086), and ontologies of cell functioning (p < 0.047, η
2 = 0.011) to which assessed parameters were affiliated, and this effect interacted with the model terms (p < 0.001, η
2 = 0.037, p < 0.001, η
2 = 0.053). Few of the effects could be resolved in post hoc differences between the R‐values of correlations between the model terms of ACE gene activity for the functional ontologies of mitochondrial metabolism, signaling and contraction (Figure  ). This concerned differences in ACE gene‐activity‐regulation related correlations between the ontologies of glucose metabolism and signaling, between the ontologies of energy metabolism and contraction, between energy metabolism and signaling, between glycolysis and turnover, as well as between mitochondrial metabolism and turnover.

ACE promoter methylation in vastus lateralis muscle, the blood serum angiotensin 2 concentration, and ACE mRNA levels (per GAPDH) demonstrated the highest degree connectivity in the metabolic variables in the network of correlations (Figure  ). Generally, correlations for the values from the different metabolic states, that is, prior or after one‐legged exercise or respective fold difference were comparable, or equal (Figure  ). Mentionable exceptions included the acylceramide LMSP02040006 (ACer 56:1; O2) and the nucleotide energy charge with considerably more relationships prior or after exercise, respectively (Figure  ). 14 parameters essentially reflecting molecular and cellular aspects of the slow oxidative phenotype, and the functional aspect of maximal aerobic performance during two‐legged exercise, demonstrated more than 25% of the possible, linear relationships to the eight assessed ACE regulatory components (Table  ).
 
3.8

## Regression Analysis of Metabolic Parameters Based on ACE Gene and Activity Regulation
Nine variables in m. vastus lateralis demonstrated a significant in‐sample model fit (p < 0.05) with good statistical power (> 0.8) for a regression based on the 8 covariates associated with ACE gene activity regulation—specifically ACE promoter methylation in skeletal muscle, ACE I/D genotype, their interaction term, ACE mRNA expression, pre‐exercise ACE enzymatic activity, and angiotensin 2 levels (pre‐exercise, post‐exercise, and fold change; Figure  ). This concerned the mean cross‐sectional area of slow type muscle fibers, the percentage area of slow type muscle fibers, mitochondrial volume density, the concentration of key substrates of glucose metabolism, glycogen and glucose‐6‐phosphate, AMP concentration, the volume density of intramyocellular lipids, and the levels of the lipidic compounds ACer 56:1; O2 (LMSP02040006) and FA 14:4; O3 (LMFA01150055).
 
3.9

## Low ACE Promoter Methylation Overrides Genetic Inhibition of Metabolic Responses
Nine of the metabolic parameters demonstrating reciprocal linear relationships with genetic and epigenetic aspects of ACE gene activity regulation (Figure  ). This included the glycolysis associated muscle metabolite G3P, four lipid metabolism associated species in muscle (Ac_CoA, PI 35:3, PG 43:4, LPC 21:0), HDL in blood serum, the muscle ketone bHb, and the charged (high) energy phosphate PCr and the discharged energy phosphate AMP. For the concentration of AMP prior to exercise, Ac_CoA post exercise, and fold changes in G3P and PCr in vastus lateralis muscle, differences resolved between categories of anticipated ACE inhibition by the ACE I‐allele and ACE promoter methylation (Figure  ).
 The concentrations of the four muscle metabolites that exhibited ACE‐promoter‐methylation–associated genetic differences were interrelated, and each showed a positive correlation with ACE transcript abundance at the corresponding time points: AMP (r = 0.444, p = 0.030), AcCoA (r = 0.586, p = 0.001), G3P (r = 0.385, p = 0.033), and PCr (r = 0.358, p = 0.048).

## Discussion
This study provides novel evidence that ACE promoter methylation contributes to the regulation of skeletal muscle metabolism and aerobic phenotype, independent of and in interaction with the ACE I/D genotype. Among eight modeled aspects of ACE gene regulation—including promoter methylation, genotype, enzyme activity, and angiotensin 2 levels—ACE promoter methylation emerged as the most consistent predictor of metabolic traits associated with slow‐twitch oxidative muscle fibers.
To our knowledge, this is the first investigation to characterize CpG methylation of the ACE promoter in human skeletal muscle. Previous work on (epi‐)genetic regulation in contractile muscle tissue has primarily examined global DNA methylation responses to exercise, microRNA expression or the associations between ACE genotype and ACE activity [ , , , ]. These approaches have provided valuable insights but offer limited resolution regarding promoter‐specific regulatory mechanisms. Our novel findings on the interplay between genetic and epigenetic aspects of ACE gene regulation in m. vastus lateralis suggest that ACE may contribute not only to vascular tone regulation but also to metabolic processes within skeletal muscle. This interpretation aligns with prior evidence linking ACE activity to systemic lipid and glucose metabolism [ , , ] and broader metabolic phenotypes [ , , , ]. Within this framework, we observed that physical activity level—particularly endurance exercise—is associated with variation in ACE promoter methylation. This relationship appears to interact with ACE I/D genotype and may help explain interindividual differences in slow‐twitch contractile and metabolic muscle fiber characteristics.
Linear correlations between ACE promoter methylation and metabolic parameters—including mitochondrial density, fiber composition, and lipid metabolites in knee extensor muscle (Figure  )—support its role as a biomarker of the oxidative muscle phenotype. Notably, ACE promoter methylation was inversely associated with ACE activity, angiotensin 2 levels, and post‐exercise ACE mRNA expression, and there was an influence of the ACE I/D gene polymorphism in interaction with the state of aerobic fitness (Figures  and ). These findings suggest that ACE promoter methylation may override genetic suppression of ACE transcription in homozygous I‐allele carriers.
Regression models revealed that ACE promoter methylation and angiotensin 2 levels in blood together explained substantial variability in aerobic performance and muscle composition (e.g., VO2max, area percentage and MCSA of slow type muscle fibers, mitochondrial volume density, as well as compounds associated with glucose (i.e., glycogen and the rate‐limiting metabolite for glucose uptake in muscle fibers, G6P [ ]) and lipid metabolism (i.e., IMCL, ACer 56:1; O2; FA 14:4; O3)). These findings are consistent with previous findings in White Caucasian men [ , , ].
These findings align with prior reports linking the ACE I/D genotype with conditionable metabolic traits of muscle tissue being favorable for endurance exercise in White Caucasian men [ , , , , ] and suggest that methylation status may refine genotype‐based predictions. This connects to findings on the correlation of ACE promoter methylation (and ACE I/D genotype) with body mass already at birth [ , ] and role of ACE in whole body metabolism via activity‐dependent glucose and lipid metabolism in muscle tissue [ , , , , ].
In this respect, the further observed associations between ACE promoter methylation and 22 lipid species (Figure  )—including glycerolipids DG 35:3, which decreased post exercise, the phosphoinositol PI 35:3, and the phosphoethanolamine PE 32:0, which also correlated with blood serum angiotensin 2—highlight a novel association of ACE with lipid‐related signaling in muscle tissue [ , ], in addition to lipid storage and β‐oxidation in skeletal muscle. Meanwhile, the lack of correlation with fat mass and associations with fat‐free mass suggest that the measured ACE methylation in vastus lateralis muscle is reflective of metabolic processes in intramuscular compartments rather than extramuscular aspects of angiotensin 2 regulated metabolism such as lipolysis in adipocytes [ ]. In this respect, the relationships of total cholesterol and HDL with angiotensin 2 concentrations in blood serum and post exercise with the ACE (epi‐)genotype, highlight a regulatory role for angiotensin 2 in hepatic cholesterol release that is of interest. They suggest a potential involvement of lipase‐mediated lipid uptake into muscle cells, a process closely tied to capillary function [ ].
Importantly, reciprocal relationships between ACE genotype and ACE promoter methylation were observed for several key metabolites (AMP, Ac‐CoA, G3P, PCr), indicating divergent regulatory mechanisms. In individuals with the ACE‐II genotype and low promoter methylation, metabolic responses to exercise were markedly altered, suggesting that methylation may, in some contexts, override genetic constraints on ACE expression and downstream metabolic regulation. Given that the affected metabolites are themselves tightly linked to nutritional and energetic status [ ], these patterns raise the possibility of a metabolic feedback loop influencing ACE promoter methylation. Together, the findings point to a mechanistic interface between epigenetic regulation and energy metabolism, potentially mediated by nucleotide charge and S‐adenosylmethionine availability [ ].
The ACE‐I/D– and methylation‐dependent regulation of aforementioned metabolites combined with the observed correlation between their concentration and the ACE transcript level suggests that ACE transcription is directly affected by the energetic status. In particular, AMP and PCr—core components of the phosphagen system—may contribute indirectly by modulating kinases that phosphorylate transcription factors in response to changes in energy charge [ ]. This interpretation aligns with the linear relationships identified between ACE mRNA levels in vastus lateralis muscle before and after exhaustive one‐legged exercise and the methylation status of CpG dinucleotides located between positions −456 and −255 of the ACE promoter. To date, specific transcriptional mechanisms acting on these cis‐regulatory regions (i.e., CpGs 1–13 and 25) have not been defined. However, several trans‐acting factors warrant consideration, including stimulatory protein‐1 (Sp1) – for which binding to GC‐rich regions in the ACE promoter has been predicted for a region closer to the transcription initiation site [ ], as well as AP‐2, KLF4, and CTCF [ ]. SP1 has been implicated in shifting transcriptional programs towards energy conservation, in a manner reminiscent of AMP‐activated protein kinase–driven responses [ , , ]. However, it remains unclear whether SP1 activity in skeletal muscle is modulated by the cellular energy state during exhaustive endurance exercise [ ], or whether such regulation specifically affects ACE transcription [ ].
The associations identified here between parameters of ACE gene regulation and variation in slow‐twitch fiber percentage, mitochondrial volume density, and key lipid and glucose metabolites support the view that ACE promoter methylation contributes to the regulation of still poorly understood metabolic pathways in skeletal muscle and its surrounding metabolic supply network [ ]. In this context, it is notable that ACE promoter methylation and genotype also correlate with the capillary fiber ratio and capillary density of vastus lateralis muscle (Figure  ). These structural associations align with the fact that ACE mRNA and protein in human skeletal muscle are largely localized to endothelial cells lining capillaries [ ], which play a central role in angiotensin 1‐to‐2 conversion [ , ].
We observed that post‐exercise angiotensin 2 concentrations in blood were associated with capillary density (Figure  ), consistent with prior work implicating angiotensin‐2–mediated capillary growth and findings from ACE‐inhibition studies suggesting angiotensin‐2–dependent angiogenic signaling and perfusion during exercise [ , , , ]. These results point to post‐transcriptional regulation of ACE expression and activity in endothelial cells as a potential contributor to vascular reactivity and muscle metabolic function. This interpretation further suggests that ACE‐related vascular processes may interact with metabolic pathways reflected in muscle fiber composition and metabolite profiles. Consequently, there is a clear need to examine how endurance‐type physical activity shapes ACE promoter methylation across biological compartments, including skeletal muscle in addition to the classical cardiovascular compartment, where endothelial ACE‐producing cells remain difficult to access [ , ]. In this context, it is notable that ACE promoter methylation differed between skeletal muscle and capillary blood in a matched cohort (Figure  ), indicating tissue‐specific CpG methylation patterns. Importantly, blood‐based measurements still yielded biologically interpretable signals.
With respect to the connection between the ACE system and the conditioning of metabolic and contractile traits in muscle tissue, it is notable that individuals who performed more than 6 h per week of moderate‐to‐vigorous activity and regularly conducted endurance exercise showed much higher ACE promoter methylation in the vastus lateralis, which interacted with the ACE I/D genotype. The identified threshold of weekly activity influencing ACE promoter methylation is close to the recommendation for moderate‐intensity cardiorespiratory exercise of ≥ 150 min per week (in sessions lasting more than 30 min) to improve and maintain metabolic fitness and health [ ]. Together with the established association of the ACE system with systemic metabolic regulation [ , ], as well as the observation of a pronounced influence of ACE promoter methylation on the muscle concentration of key metabolites post‐exercise in ACE‐II genotypes (Figure  ), these findings point to the suitability of ACE promoter methylation as a conceptual framework for personalized recommendations regarding metabolically effective exercise dose and modality. Inasmuch as ACE‐DD genotypes in skeletal muscle appear considerably less sensitive to methylation changes than I‐allele carriers, this pattern aligns with the widely accepted view of superior trainability of endurance in ACE‐I allele carriers [ , , ].
Longitudinal research designs will be essential to more fully address how ACE‐I/D–associated individual differences influence the physical‐activity‐mediated regulation of ACE gene activity and related metabolic traits. Such work should include early and late time points across periods of increased or decreased activity of varying intensity. A key question is whether changes in ACE promoter methylation precede or accompany shifts in the slow‐oxidative muscle phenotype, and whether methylation at specific CpG sites—such as sites 11–13 and 23 (Figure  )—modulates these adaptations through interactions with trans‐acting factors [ , ].
The potential reversibility of ACE promoter methylation also warrants investigation. Exercise‐responsive DNA methylation in human skeletal muscle is known to be dynamic and modifiable over time [ , , ], raising the possibility that ACE promoter methylation may exhibit similar temporal plasticity.
Limitations of our investigations include the cross‐sectional design and use of tissue homogenates, which preclude cell‐type‐specific resolution. Nonetheless, the reproducibility of methylation measurements (coefficient of variation ~12% across replicates from ~2 mm3 muscle samples) and their robust associations with metabolic traits support the biological relevance of ACE promoter methylation in skeletal muscle.
We also acknowledge that the supplementary analysis of ACE promoter methylation in capillary blood in comparison to an independent second cohort was likewise cross‐sectional and derived from whole‐blood DNA rather than isolated leukocyte subpopulations. The dried‐blood‐spot approach, while validated for whole‐blood methylation profiling, does not permit cell‐fraction–resolved interpretation. Even so, the observed CpG‐specific differences between muscle and whole blood provide biologically meaningful indications of tissue specificity and highlight promising directions for future work on cell‐type–resolved ACE regulation.
We also note that the absence of plasma ACE activity measurements constrains the interpretation of systemic versus tissue‐specific ACE gene regulation. In this regard, the observed inversion of the relationship between ACE activity in vastus lateralis muscle and circulating angiotensin 2 concentrations—from a positive association at baseline to a negative association post‐exercise (r = 0.572 vs. –0.466)—may suggest contributions from ACE sources other than skeletal muscle [ , ]. Future studies with dedicated sampling and analytical capacity will be required to address this question fully. Taken together, the current findings remain consistent with our hypothesis that ACE promoter methylation contributes to ACE gene regulation in skeletal muscle. They further suggest—through a mechanism whose cellular origin remains to be identified and for which we are not aware of existing literature—that this regulation is reflected in systemic angiotensin 2 responses.
In conclusion, ACE promoter methylation appears to modulate its transcriptional and enzymatic responses in skeletal muscle to exercise, correlates with oxidative muscle traits, and interacts with genotype and aerobic fitness to influence muscle's lipid and carbohydrate metabolism. Given that genetically modulated ACE activity affects muscle activity‐dependent metabolic processes in skeletal muscle through influences on capillary perfusion [ , ] and training adaptations [ , ], our findings position ACE promoter methylation as a candidate biomarker for personalized training adaptation and metabolic profiling in health and performance contexts, including those related to energy‐expenditure–based weight control.
