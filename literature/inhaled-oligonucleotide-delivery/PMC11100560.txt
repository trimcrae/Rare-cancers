HD is a late-onset disorder characterized by uncontrolled movements (chorea), emotional disturbances, and psychiatric symptoms. The most prominent pathological feature of HD is the preferential loss of neurons in the striatum ( , ). In patients with late-stage HD, neurodegeneration is found in other brain regions, such as the cortex, whereas neurons in the cerebellum are largely spared ( – ). Unraveling the mechanism underlying brain region–specific neurodegeneration is the key to understanding HD pathogenesis and developing effective HD therapies.
The causative gene for HD is Huntingtin (HTT), which encodes a large protein of more than 3000 amino acids. The HTT protein that contains more than 36 glutamines in the polyQ region is considered mutant and is prone to misfolding. Mutant HTT (mHTT) is cleaved by multiple proteases including caspase-3, caspase-6, and calpains ( – ). The resultant N-terminal mHTT fragments with expanded polyQ repeats are more toxic than full-length mHTT and form inclusion bodies that are typically found in HD patient brains ( , , ). While emerging evidence suggests that the transcript of HTT with extended CAG repeats can lead to RNA-mediated toxicity ( , ), the mHTT protein is still considered the primary cause of HD pathogenesis ( ).
The mHTT protein is widely expressed in the brain and peripheral tissues ( – ), yet preferentially causes striatal neurodegeneration in HD, with the medium spiny neurons (MSNs) being most vulnerable. Substantial efforts have been devoted to establishing a causal relationship between HTT expression level and neuronal vulnerability in different brain regions. Studies using postmortem human brain tissues have yielded inconsistent results. On a systemic level, there is no enrichment of HTT in the striatum ( , ). However, immunohistochemical studies reported brain region heterogeneity in HTT expression, with dense labeling in selected neurons in the striatum ( – ). The discrepancy could be caused by differences in sample conditions, which are hard to control for postmortem human tissues. On the other hand, in the widely used rodent models of HD, a lack of correlation between HTT expression and neuronal vulnerability was reported ( ).
The brain of nonhuman primates shares more similarities in structure and function with the human brain ( ). At the transcriptome level, the pattern of gene expression in the human brain is more similar to nonhuman primates than rodents ( – ). Therefore, nonhuman primates can be an ideal model to complement current knowledge on HD pathogenesis and explore previously unidentified pathogenic mechanisms. A transgenic monkey model expressing N-terminal mHTT exhibited obvious neurodegeneration and clinical features of HD, including dystonia and chorea ( ), which cannot be replicated by HD rodent models. This monkey model failed to reproduce due to severe pathological phenotypes. Nonetheless, wild-type (WT) HTT and mHTT showed a comparable expression pattern in HD patient brains ( , ), suggesting that the turnover of WT HTT and mHTT in the brain is comparable.
In the present study, we examined the expression of HTT in brain regions that are differentially affected in HD (cortex, cerebellum, striatum, and hippocampus), using adult WT cynomolgus monkeys (Macaca fascicularis). We found that HTT expression level positively correlates with neuronal vulnerability in different brain regions of monkeys, with the striatal neurons showing the highest level of HTT expression. The enrichment of HTT is caused by a slow degradation of HTT protein in the striatum. We also performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis using tissues from these four brain regions and identified tripartite motif-containing protein 37 (TRIM37) as a primate-specific E3 ubiquitin ligase for HTT, whose expression is specifically decreased in the striatum of nonhuman primates. TRIM37 is an E3 ligase that is known to ubiquitinate selective proteins, including histone H2A and PEX5 ( , ), and its mutations underly Mulibrey nanism, an autosomal recessive disorder with growth failure, cardiopathy, and hepatomegaly ( ). We found that TRIM37 modulates mHTT ubiquitination and degradation in cultured cells, HD140Q knock-in (KI) mice, and monkeys injected with the mHTT virus. Together, these results support the conclusion that the brain-regional differences in HTT expression contribute to HD neuropathology and highlight TRIM37 as a critical regulator of HTT degradation and a potential therapeutic target for HD treatment.

## RESULTS

## HTT expression is characterized in the monkey and mouse brain
First, we examined the expression of HTT in different types of cells in the monkey brain. We separated the monkey’s cerebral cortex into gray matter, which is enriched in neurons, and white matter, which is enriched in glial cells. By quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) and Western blotting, we found that both HTT mRNA and HTT protein are more abundantly expressed in the gray matter (fig. S1, A to C), indicating that HTT is enriched in neurons. This conclusion is further supported by immunostaining of monkey cortical slices, as HTT-positive signal predominantly colocalizes with NeuN, a marker of neurons, but rarely colocalizes with glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a marker of astrocytes (fig. S1D).
Next, we compared the expression of HTT in different monkey brain tissues, including the cortex, cerebellum, striatum, and hippocampus. Because HTT is enriched in neurons and the neuronal density varies among different brain regions (fig. S2A), we normalized the HTT band intensity to the NeuN band intensity in Western blotting to compare the relative enrichment of HTT in neurons of different brain regions. We used the MAB2166 antibody that recognizes HTT across different species ( ) and found that the monkey striatum has the highest ratio of HTT expression, and the cerebellum has the lowest ( ). In contrast, we did not find the enrichment of HTT in the mouse striatum ( ). To further confirm this finding, we used another neuronal marker neurofilament H. The expression pattern of neurofilament H and the ratio of HTT to neurofilament H is comparable to that of NeuN ( ). We also performed the same analysis using human brain samples and found a pattern similar to monkeys ( ). To validate this observation, we performed immunohistochemistry of monkey and mouse brain slices. Using the HTT antibody (EPR5526) that is optimal for staining both monkey and mouse HTT, we found that the striatum has the highest HTT staining intensity in the monkey brain ( ), but not in the mouse brain (fig. S2B). Double immunostaining using HTT and NeuN antibodies revealed that the highest level of HTT expression is in the monkey striatal neurons. ( ). To further distinguish different neuronal types in the striatum, we labeled HTT with either DARPP32, choline acetyltransferase (ChAT), or parvalbumin in the monkey striatal slice. The immunostaining result indicates that HTT is abundantly expressed in MSNs and parvalbumin interneurons, but not in cholinergic interneurons (fig. S2C). It is noteworthy that besides MSNs, the number of parvalbumin interneurons also decreases substantially in the HD striatum ( ), whereas the cholinergic interneurons are largely unaffected ( ). Therefore, in the primate brain, but not the rodent brain, the level of HTT expression positively correlates with striatal neuronal vulnerability in HD.
 

## HTT degradation, but not synthesis, contributes to the brain-regional variance of HTT expression in the monkey brain
We wonder if the brain-regional variance of HTT expression is caused by differences in HTT/Htt mRNA level. However, qRT-PCR analysis showed that in monkey, human, and mouse brains, the pattern of HTT/Htt mRNA is comparable, with the cerebellum showing the highest expression ( , and fig. S3A), so the HTT protein level does not correlate with the mRNA level. The HTT mRNA level could be affected by the cell density in different brain regions. To directly visualize and quantify HTT mRNA abundance in single neurons from different regions of the monkey brain, we performed RNA in situ hybridization (ISH) using an RNAscope probe that specifically targets monkey HTT. Co-labeling of HTT mRNA and NeuN protein revealed that the striatal neurons have significantly less HTT mRNA than neurons in the cortex, cerebellum, and hippocampus ( ). To further measure the abundance of HTT mRNA that is being actively translated, we performed sucrose-gradient centrifugation to enrich the ribosomes ( ). Western blotting analysis demonstrated that the ribosomes are enriched in the P3 fraction ( ). Therefore, we extracted mRNA from the P3 fraction and performed qRT-PCR, but again, we did not find the enrichment of actively translated HTT/Htt mRNA in the striatum ( and fig. S3B). One previous study combined bulk RNA-seq and ribosome profiling (Ribo-seq) to measure translation efficiency in the human brain. They identified HTT as being efficiently translated, and RIPK2 and PINK1 as being less efficiently translated ( ). Similarly, we found significantly more HTT mRNA than RIPK2 and PINK1 mRNA in the P3 fraction (fig. S3C), thereby confirming the validity of our ribosome purification approach. Together, these results indicate that the striatal enrichment of HTT protein is not determined by its mRNA.
 We then focused on HTT degradation in the monkey brain. We took advantage of an in vitro HTT degradation assay previously developed ( ), in which the same amount of full-length WT HTT or mHTT from stable human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cells is incubated with homogenate from various brain regions. As HTT is degraded by the enzymatic reaction in the brain homogenate, we terminated the reaction at different time intervals and measured HTT abundance by Western blotting to determine HTT stability in the brain lysate ( ). Therefore, this assay enables us to determine the rate of HTT degradation in different brain regions. We performed this assay using homogenates from the cortex, cerebellum, striatum, and hippocampus of adult monkeys. For Western blotting, we used a T7 tag antibody to specifically recognize HTT from the stable HEK293 cells but exclude the endogenous monkey HTT. Incubation of WT HTT in the cortex, cerebellum, and hippocampus lysate led to about 50% reduction within 240 min, whereas significantly more WT HTT remained in the striatum lysate ( ), suggesting a slow degradation rate in the striatum. We tried the same incubation time for mHTT but found that mHTT was almost completely degraded in 30 min (fig. S4A). This finding is consistent with previous publications reporting that mHTT is degraded faster than WT HTT ( , ). Therefore, we used a shorter incubation period and found that the degradation rate of mHTT was also the slowest when incubated with the striatum lysate, compared to other brain regions ( ). For a more rigorous comparison, we ran WT and mHTT at different time points on the same gel, which yielded results consistent with running WT or mHTT separately (fig. S4, B and C).
 To make sure that HTT degradation is caused by the brain homogenates, we incubated HTT in the lysis buffer only and found both WT HTT and mHTT were stable within the time frame (fig. S4D). We also compared the degradation of endogenous WT HTT in the monkey cerebellum and striatum and found rates similar to the exogenous HTT from the stable HEK293 cells (fig. S4, E and F). To determine whether HTT degradation can be boosted by a higher concentration of brain lysate, we mixed the HEK293 cell lysate and monkey striatum lysate at different ratios (1:1 or 1:3). The results showed that increasing the monkey striatum lysate led to faster degradation of mHTT (fig. S4, G and H). We performed the same assay using brain homogenates from 6-month-old mice and found that both WT HTT and mHTT were more rapidly degraded (fig. S5), suggesting that the mouse brain has a stronger capacity to degrade HTT than the monkey brain.

## The expression of TRIM37 is specifically decreased in the monkey striatum
The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and autophagy are two major protein quality control and recycling mechanisms ( ), and both mechanisms are involved in HTT degradation ( , ). We wonder if the overall activity of either mechanism is the lowest in the monkey striatum, thereby accounting for the slow degradation of HTT. For the UPS, we measured trypsin-like and chymotrypsin-like activities and found that both activities in the striatum were comparable to the cerebellum and hippocampus (fig. S6, A and B). For autophagy, we measured LC3-I to -II conversion, which is a marker for steady-state autophagy, and HSC70, which is an indicator of chaperone-mediated autophagy, respectively. We found that the LC3-I to -II conversion and HSC70 expression were similar among different brain regions (fig. S6, C to E). These results indicate that the slow degradation of HTT in the monkey striatum is not caused by brain-regional differences in the UPS or autophagy capacity.
To explore the molecular mechanisms more precisely, we performed RNA-seq using the cortex, cerebellum, striatum, and hippocampus tissues of four 5-year-old monkeys. The multidimensional scaling plot showed that each brain region has its unique transcriptomic signature ( and S7A). We were particularly interested in the transcripts whose expression was specifically changed in the striatum versus other brain regions. Comparison of the transcriptome of the striatum to that of the cortex, cerebellum, and hippocampus yielded 4987 (up, 2366; down, 2621), 8322 (up, 4216; down, 4106), and 4167 (up, 1952; down, 2215) significantly changed genes respectively (fig. S7, B to D). By overlapping these three gene lists via Venn diagram, we identified 1012 significantly up-regulated and 812 significantly down-regulated genes in the striatum ( ). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed that most of the up-regulated genes were related to pathways including cell growth and actin filament organization ( ), whereas the down-regulated genes were related to pathways such as ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis and glutamatergic synapse ( ). We specifically looked at the ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis pathway as it is closely linked to protein degradation. Within this pathway, UBA52, KLHL13, and WWP1 were significantly up-regulated; TRIM37, UBE2QL1, FBXW7, PIAS3, UBE2E3, and HERC4 were significantly down-regulated in the striatum ( ). To confirm the RNA-seq results, we performed qRT-PCR on individual genes within the ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis pathway and found that TRIM37, UBE2E3, UBE2QL1, FBXW7, HERC4, and PIAS3 had the lowest level of mRNA in the striatum when compared with other brain regions ( ).
 We further validated these targets at the protein level using antibodies commercially available. The expression of TRIM37 and UBE2E3 is specifically reduced in the striatum, whereas the expression of HERC4, FBXW7, and PIAS3 is indistinguishable among different brain regions ( , and fig. S7, E and F). Considering that TRIM37 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that should have a better substrate specificity than the E2 ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme UBE2E3, we focused on TRIM37 for the subsequent studies. The reduction of TRIM37 in the striatum was further supported by immunohistochemical staining of monkey brain slices ( ). TRIM37 is expressed in both neurons and astrocytes, and the staining intensity is significantly stronger in the astrocytes (fig. S8, A and B). This result indicates that TRIM37 is more enriched in the astrocytes, which also precludes us from using NeuN to normalize TRIM37 expression in different brain regions as we did for HTT. In addition, we examined the expression of TRIM37 in different brain regions of humans and mice. While the expression of human TRIM37 is significantly lower in the striatum, the expression of mouse TRIM37 is consistent among the brain regions (fig. S8, C to F).
 

## The interaction between TRIM37 and HTT is specific in the primate brain
To test the hypothesis that TRIM37 is an E3 ligase for HTT, we first studied the interaction between TRIM37 and HTT via coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP). We pulled down HTT from monkey cortical tissues using the EPR5526 antibody and detected abundant TRIM37 in the pull-down lysate ( ). Using mouse cortical lysate, we found that mouse HTT was unable to precipitate mouse TRIM37 ( ), indicating that TRIM37 is a primate-specific interacting protein for HTT. We repeated the co-IP experiment using human cortical tissues. Because postmortem human brain tissues may have undergone additional posttranslational modifications or degradation during sample collection and storage, compared to the freshly prepared monkey brain tissues, the human TRIM37 band appeared blurry. Nonetheless, human TRIM37 was also precipitated with HTT ( ).
The sequence homology between human and mouse TRIM37 is 92%. The N-terminal 391 amino acids of human and mouse TRIM37 are identical, whereas the C-terminal 573 amino acids are different in 73 amino acids ( ). To confirm whether the divergent C-terminal TRIM37 interacts with HTT, we constructed plasmids expressing full-length human TRIM37, the N-terminal 391 amino acids, and the C-terminal 573 amino acids ( ). These plasmids were separately transfected into HEK293 cells, and the cell lysate was used for co-IP assay. We found that both full-length TRIM37 and the C-terminal TRIM37 were coprecipitated with HTT, but not the N-terminal TRIM37 ( ). These results suggest that the C terminus of TRIM37 accounts for the primate-specific interaction with HTT.

## TRIM37 promotes HTT ubiquitination and degradation
To determine whether TRIM37 is an E3 ligase for HTT, we manipulated the expression of TRIM37 and examined the corresponding changes in HTT ubiquitination. Because polyubiquitinated WT HTT is rapidly degraded but polyubiquitinated mHTT can be detected, we transfected HEK293 cells stably expressing full-length mHTT with TRIM37 small interfering RNA (siRNA), treated the cells with MG132 to block the proteasome activity, and performed co-IP to measure the level of ubiquitination on mHTT. We found that knocking down TRIM37 led to a significant reduction in mHTT ubiquitination ( ). We also tried the opposite approach by transfecting HEK293 cells stably expressing full-length mHTT with the TRIM37 plasmid. The overexpression of TRIM37 significantly increased the level of mHTT ubiquitination ( ), indicating that TRIM37 is an E3 ligase that promotes HTT ubiquitination. The addition of MG132 inhibited the proteasome activity so that the overall expression of mHTT was unchanged. To further confirm this finding, we used another TRIM37 siRNA targeting its 3′ untranslated region (3′UTR), which reduces the expression of endogenous TRIM37 but does not affect the TRIM37 plasmid (fig. S9A). Rescuing the expression of TRIM37 in the cells with TRIM37 knockdown restored mHTT ubiquitination (fig. S9, B and C).
 We wonder if the half-life of HTT can be changed by modulating the expression of TRIM37. Given that our study and others have shown that mHTT is degraded faster than WT HTT ( , ), we performed a cycloheximide chase assay to measure mHTT degradation. To that end, we transfected HEK293 cells stably expressing mHTT with either TRIM37 siRNA or TRIM37 plasmid. Two days after transfection, the cells were treated with cycloheximide to block protein synthesis, and the levels of mHTT were measured at different time intervals after cycloheximide treatment. We found that knocking down TRIM37 significantly prolonged the half-life of mHTT ( ), whereas TRIM37 overexpression achieved the opposite effects ( ).

## TRIM37 modulates mHTT aggregation in vivo
As mouse TRIM37 does not interact with mouse HTT, we transfected the murine N2A cells with the plasmid expressing human TRIM37, and the co-IP result showed that endogenous mouse HTT can precipitate human TRIM37 ( ). Therefore, we packaged adeno-associated virus (AAV) expressing human TRIM37 (AAV-TRIM37) and injected the virus into the striatum of 24-week-old HD140Q KI mice. AAV green fluorescent protein (GFP) was injected into the other side of the striatum as a control ( ). HD140Q KI mice express mouse HTT with human exon1 containing 140Q and develop extensive mHTT aggregation in the striatum around 6 months of age ( , ). Four weeks after viral injection, we collected the striatum tissues and performed Western blotting using the mEM48 antibody, which preferentially recognizes aggregated mHTT ( , ), and the 1C2 antibody, which reacts with soluble full-length mHTT and its fragments ( , ). TRIM37 overexpression significantly reduced the aggregated form of mHTT and full-length mHTT in the striatum ( ). The Western blotting result was also supported by immunostaining and immunohistochemical study, as significantly less mHTT aggregates were found in the striatal areas with AAV-TRIM37 expression ( , and fig. S10A).
 To further confirm the result in the monkey brain, we packaged AAV expressing truncated N-terminal mHTT (first 1000 amino acids) with 114Q (AAV-HTT-114Q) and lentivirus expressing TRIM37 shRNA (Lenti-TRIM37-shRNA) (fig. S10B). These two viruses were mixed at a 1:1 ratio and injected into the striatum of adult monkeys ( ). Two months after viral injection, we found that Lenti-TRIM37-shRNA successfully reduced endogenous TRIM37 expression in the monkey striatum (fig. S10C) and promoted mHTT aggregates formation in the virus-infected area ( ).

## DISCUSSION
One prominent pathological feature of HD is the preferential loss of neurons in the striatum. The most straightforward explanation for this phenomenon is that HTT is enriched in the striatal neurons. However, previous work based on postmortem human brains failed to support this hypothesis, as the staining pattern of HTT varies among different studies ( – ). The discrepancy could be caused by variations in the HTT antibodies used or the postmortem sample conditions. Note that delayed sample preparation and staining could result in a loss of HTT immunoreactivity ( ). However, it is challenging to standardize the conditions for postmortem human brains. Nonhuman primates share close similarities with humans in terms of brain development, organization, and function. Their brains can be freshly prepared, ensuring uniform sample conditions. Therefore, nonhuman primate is a valuable model for studying HD. In the present study, we found that HTT is most abundantly expressed in monkey striatal neurons. Whereas in brain regions that are less affected in HD, such as the cerebellum, the expression of HTT is relatively low. Within the striatum, MSNs and parvalbumin interneurons, but not cholinergic interneurons, show relative enrichment of HTT, which is also in agreement with the preferential loss of MSNs and parvalbumin interneurons in HD. Therefore, our study suggests that the expression of HTT positively correlates with neuronal vulnerabilities in HD. Considering that HD is a late-onset disease, the mild but significant increase of HTT expression, coupled with age-related decline in protein clearance mechanisms ( ), could cause the gradual accumulation of toxic mHTT oligomers or aggregates over time, leading to the preferential degeneration selected neurons in the striatum.
The enrichment of HTT in the striatum could be caused by either enhanced protein synthesis or reduced protein degradation. We found that protein degradation is the major contributor to the brain-regional variations in HTT expression, as both full-length WT HTT and mHTT have the slowest rate of degradation in the monkey striatum lysate. Our result is consistent with a previous report that cortical neurons cleared mHTT faster than striatal neurons ( ). HTT is degraded much faster in the mouse brain lysate than in the monkey brain lysate, suggesting that the mouse brain has a stronger capacity to clear HTT. This species difference in degrading mHTT may also explain the fact that transgenic mouse models of HD rarely show obvious neurodegeneration ( – ).
The use of a nonhuman primate brain allows us to investigate the mechanism underlying the brain-regional differences in HTT clearance. RNA-seq analysis revealed that the monkey striatum has distinct transcriptional signatures compared with other brain regions, including significantly reduced expression of genes related to ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis. Among these targets, we identified TRIM37 as a primate-specific E3 ligase for HTT. Evidence in support of our conclusion is that the expression of TRIM37 is specifically reduced in the monkey striatum, but not in the mouse striatum. In addition, TRIM37 is coimmunoprecipitated with HTT in the monkey and human brain, but not in the mouse brain. TRIM37 belongs to the family of TRIM proteins that are defined by the presence of an N-terminal RING finger domain, B-box motifs, and a coiled-coil domain (RBCC). The RBCC domain is highly conserved, in which the RING domain catalyzes ubiquitin chain formation, and the BCC domain mediates oligomerization and ligase activities ( ). On the other hand, the C-terminal domain of the TRIM family is variable and confers substrate specificity ( ). Our observation that the N terminus of human and mouse TRIM37 is identical, while the divergent C terminus of TRIM37 mediates TRIM37-HTT interaction, is consistent with the previous structural studies.
We conducted several experiments to confirm the influence of TRIM37 on HTT stability. First, manipulation of TRIM37 expression alters mHTT ubiquitination and turnover in HEK293 cells. Second, given that human TRIM37 binds to mouse HTT, we observed a significant reduction of mHTT aggregation in the striatum of HD140Q KI mice with human TRIM37 overexpression. Third, in the monkey brain expressing an N-terminal fragment of mHTT, the knockdown of TRIM37 exacerbated mHTT aggregation. Their results established TRIM37 as a critical regulator of mHTT stability, which also explains the preferential enrichment of HTT in the striatal neurons of the monkey brain. In addition, other striatum-selective mechanisms, such as guanine nucleotide-binding protein RHES or G protein–coupled receptor GPR52, may also play important roles in mediating mHTT degradation ( , ). However, more work is needed to elucidate the relative contributions of these striatum-specific targets to modulate mHTT toxicity and to develop potential therapies for the treatment of HD.
Our work has established nonhuman primates as a valuable animal resource for HD research. While rodent models have greatly expanded our understanding of HD pathogenesis, inherent species differences make them incapable of addressing specific questions related to selective neurodegeneration. Therefore, studying nonhuman primates can fill in these conceptual gaps in current HD research. Although it is technically challenging and cost-prohibitive to establish a KI monkey model of HD, we used stereotaxic viral injection to express mHTT in the monkey brain. Future technological advances are highly desirable to make cloning of gene-edited monkeys more accessible ( ).

## Cell culture
HEK293, COS7, and N2A cells were purchased from ATAC (CRL-1573, CRL-1651, and CCL-131) and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Gibco, C11995500CP) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Biological Industries, 04-001-1B), penicillin and streptomycin (100 U/ml; Gibco, 15140163). HEK293 cell lines stably expressing full-length HTT with 23Q or 120Q were established previously in our laboratory and selected by hygromycin B (500 μg/ml; Invivogen, ant-hg-5) in the culture medium. For transient transfection of plasmids or siRNAs, Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000001) was used according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and the cells were harvested 48 hours after transfection. For proteasomal inhibition, 48 hours after transfection, MG132 (Solarbio, IM0310) was added to the culture medium for a final concentration of 10 μM. The cells were harvested 12 hours after MG132 treatment. For HTT half-life assay, 48 hours after transfection, cycloheximide (Sigma-Aldrich, C7698) was added to the culture medium for a final concentration of 10 μM. The cells were harvested at different time intervals after cycloheximide treatment.

## Viral vector construction and packaging
AAV-TRIM37 vector was generated using human cDNA library and the following primers: forward 5′-CCG ACC GGT GCC ACC ATG GAT GAA CAG AGC GTG GAG AG-3′, reverse 5′-CCG GAA TTC TTA AGC ATA ATC TGG AAC ATC ATA TGG ATA TCT TCC ACT ATT TTC ATC T-3′. The PCR product was cloned into the AAV plasmid under the control of a CMV promoter (purchased from PackGene Biotech). AAV-HTT-114Q was generated using full-length mHTT plasmid and the following primers: forward 5′-GTC AGA TCA CCG GTG CCA CCA TGG CTA GCA TGA CTG GTG GAC-3′, reverse 5′-AGA GAA GTT TGT TGC GCC GGA TCC TGT GCT GAC GGA GAA ATG AGA TG-3′. The PCR product was cloned into the AAV plasmid under the control of a CMV promoter. All the AAV plasmids were packaged into AAV serotype 9 by PackGene Biotech Inc. Lenti-TRIM37-shRNA vector was constructed (shRNA sequence 5′-GGA ACT GAT CAG CTT AGT TCA-3′) and packaged by IGEbio.

## Stereotaxic surgery
For mouse stereotaxic surgery, the mice were anesthetized with continuous 1.5% isoflurane inhalation and fixed on a stereotaxic instrument (RWD, 69100). The hair around the surgical site was removed using a disinfected scissor and the skin was sterilized with 70% alcohol. The skin was cut to expose the bregma of the skull, and the injection sites were identified according to the distance from the bregma. Microdrill was used to make small holes in the skull, and the AAV (1 × 10+13 GC/ml) was injected into the brain using a 33G microsyringe (Hamilton. NanoFil-10ul-1) at the speed of 200 nl/min. The microsyringe was left in the injection site for 10 min, and then slowly withdrawn. After suturing the wound, the mice were placed on a heated blanket until awakened. The injection coordinates used were as follows: striatum, anterior-posterior = +0.55 mm, medial-lateral = ±2 mm, dorsal-ventral = −3.5 mm. All postsurgical mice were monitored and given meloxicam (5 mg/kg) for analgesia for 5 consecutive days.
For monkey stereotaxic surgery, the monkey was anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 0.3 to 0.5 mg of atropine, followed by 10 to 12 mg of ketamine and 15 to 20 mg of pelltobarbitalumnatricum per kg body weight. The monkey was stabilized on a stereotaxic instrument (RWD, 68901). The precise positions of the cortex and striatum were determined by magnetic resonance imaging before injection. A microsyringe was used to deliver 10 μl of viruses into each location. Postoperative care was given by certified veterinarians at Guangdong Landau Biotechnology Co. Ltd.

## RNA sequencing and analysis
Monkey brain tissues (cortex, striatum, hippocampus, and cerebellum) were sent to Novogene for RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing. The raw data were quantified using Salmon software (Ver. 1.8.0) with the mapping-based mode ( ). The differentially expressed genes (Ver 3.1.6) were analyzed using the edgeR package with adjusted P < 0.01 and log |Fold Change| > 1 ( , ). The correlation map and Venn map were plotted using the corrplot (ver 0.92) and Venn (ver 1.11) packages, respectively. For KEGG pathway analysis, the clusterProfiler (ver 4.0.2) package was used on the up-regulated or down-regulated genes with a P value less than 0.01 ( ). All the analyses were performed using the R (Ver 4.1.0) and Rstudio (Ver 2022.07.1 Build 554) software.

## Western blotting
Cell samples were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) three times and collected; brain tissues were ground 20 to 30 times using Dounce Tissue Grinders (Thermo Fisher Scientific, K8853000002). The samples were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer [50 mM tris (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 1 mM EGTA (pH 8.0), 0.1% SDS, 0.5% DOC, and 1% Triton X-100] with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Mei5bio, MF182-plus-10), and sonicated six times. The samples were SurePAGE Bis-Tris gels (GenScript, M00652) for electrophoresis and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 1 hour and incubated with the corresponding primary antibodies in 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at 4°C overnight. The next day, the membranes were washed three times in 1× PBS and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibodies in 5% skim milk at room temperature for 1 hour. The signals were developed with ECL solution (Millipore, WBKLS0500) after washing three times in 1× PBS. The images were acquired digitally using Clinx ChemiScope 6300.

## Immunohistochemistry
The mice were anesthetized using isoflurane and intracardially perfused with warm 0.9% saline solution, followed by cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M PB solution. The monkeys were euthanized using isoflurane and intracardially perfused with warm PBS. The mouse brain was fixed overnight in 4% PFA solution and transferred to 30% sucrose for 48 hours. The monkey brain was dissected into different regions, fixed in 4% PFA solution for 7 days, and transferred to 30% sucrose until the samples sunk. The brain samples were embedded in the OCT solution (Sakura, 4583), sectioned at 30 μm in a cryostat (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and soaked in 0.1 M PB buffer containing 0.03% NaN3 for storage. Brain slices were blocked in blocking buffer (3% BSA/2% goat serum/0.1% Triton X-100/1× PBS) for 1 hour and incubated with primary antibodies in blocking buffer at 4°C overnight. After washing with 1× PBS three times, the brain slices were incubated with secondary antibodies for 10 min and developed with the Mouse and Rabbit Specific HRP/DAB (ABC) Detection IHC kit (Abcam, ab64264) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

## Immunofluorescent staining
Brain slices were penetrated with 0.5% Triton X-100 in 1× PBS for 30 min and blocked with a blocking buffer for 1 hour at room temperature. The brain slices were incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. The next day, the slices were washed with 1× PBS three times and incubated with secondary fluorescent antibodies for 1 hour and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole for 10 min. The slices were imaged using a Zeiss AX10 Axio Microscope or Olympus FV3000 confocal laser scanning microscope.

## RNAscope ISH
RNAscope Probe-Mfa-HTT (catalog #509121) and related products were purchased from ACDBio. The ISH procedure was performed following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, monkey brain slices underwent target retrieval in 1× Co-Detection Target Retrieval buffer at 100°C for 15 min. After washing four times in distilled water, the slices were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in a Co-Detection Antibody Diluent at 4°C overnight. The next day, the slices were treated with 10% Neutral Buffered Formalin and RNAscope Protease Plus sequentially. The fluorescent ISH assay was performed in the HybEZ Humidity Control Tray, followed by incubation in fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody diluted in a Co-Detection Antibody Diluent. The slices were imaged using an Olympus FV3000 confocal laser scanning microscope.

## HTT degradation assay
HEK293 cells stably expressing full-length HTT were grown to confluence in a 10-cm plate. The cells were collected and lysed in cold assay buffer [25 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.6), 10 mM MgCl, purified rabbit creatine kinase (100 g/ml), 50 mM phosphocreatine, and 1 mM adenosine 5′-triphosphate added at use). Brain tissues from WT mice and monkeys were rapidly collected and homogenized in a cold assay buffer using Dounce Tissue Grinders. Both cell and brain lysate were centrifuged 500g at 4°C for 10 min to pellet unbroken tissues and membranes. The supernatant was collected, and the protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To perform the test, 68 μg of cell lysate and 68 μg of brain lysate were mixed to a total volume of 400 μl. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for different time intervals. The reaction was stopped by the addition of a protease inhibitor cocktail, phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), protein loading dye, and bringing it to 95°C for 10 min.

## Sucrose fractionation
The brain tissues were lysed in cold fractionation buffer [0.32 M sucrose in RB buffer with 2 mM dithiothreitol, cycloheximide (100 μg/ml), 200 mM KCl, 20 mM Hepes-KOH, and 15 mM MgCl2] using Dounce Tissue Grinders. The homogenate was centrifuged at 300g for 5 min. The supernatant was transferred to a clean tube and centrifuged at 17,500g for 30 min. The pellet (P2) contains nucleus and mitochondria. The supernatant (S2) was transferred to the top of 28.5 ml of 30% sucrose in a 30-ml ultracentrifuge tube and centrifuged at 185,000g for 5 hours. The supernatant (S3) was considered as cytosol, and the pellet enriched with ribosomes (P3) was resuspended in 1 ml of TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for RNA extraction.

## Quantitative real-time PCR
PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara) was used to remove genomic DNA and synthesize cDNA from the extracted RNA. SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Qiagen, 208352) was used and the reaction was performed in the CFX Connect Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). The primers used are listed as follows: monkey HTT, forward 5′-CAA CCA CGA GAG CAC TCA CT-3′, reverse 5′-ATG GGA ACC AAG CTG ACG AG-3′; monkey TRIM37, forward 5′-CAA ACT TCG TCG GCG TCT CAT-3′, reverse 5′-ATT TCC CGA ACA CGC TCA TCT-3′; monkey UBE2QL1, forward 5′-GCG ACC GCT TCA TCT CCG-3′, reverse 5′-GTC CTG CCA CAG CAC CGA-3′; monkey FBXW7, forward 5′-TAC ATC TGT CCA GCC ACC-3′, reverse 5′-TTC ATC TAA AGC AAG CAA T-3′; monkey PIAS3, forward 5′-CAG CCG CCC CAT CAA CAT-3′, reverse 5′-AAT TCC GTC CGA ACT CAG-3′; monkey UBE2E3, forward 5′-GCT GTC TAT TTG TTC CCT T-3′, reverse 5′-GTC TGG CTA TCC TGT CGT-3′; monkey HERC4, forward 5′-AGG GTA AAT GAG AAA ACG-3′, reverse 5′-GAC ATC CAT TCC ATA GGC-3′, monkey Actin, forward 5′-ACA GAG CCT CGC CTT TGC-3′, reverse 5′-CAC GAT GGA GGG GAA GAC-3′; mouse Htt, forward 5′-GTC AGA ATG GTG GCT GAT GAG TGC-3′, reverse 5′-AGC CAG CTC AGC AAA CCT CCA CAG-3′; mouse Actin, forward 5′-TCA CTG TCC ACC TTC CAG CAG ATG-3′, reverse 5′-CTC AGT AAC AGT CCG CCT AGA AGC-3′.

## Immunoprecipitation
The cell or brain samples were collected and lysed in 1% NP-40 buffer [150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 2 mM EDTA, 50 mM tris (pH 8.0), and 1 mM PMSF] with a protease inhibitor cocktail. After preclearing with 50 μl of Dynabeads Protein G beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10004D), 500 μg of protein was incubated with the primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. The next day, the samples were incubated with 50 μl of Protein G beads at 4°C for 1 hour. The beads were washed with 1% NP-40 buffer three times and collected by the magnetic frame. The beads were boiled with 1× SDS loading buffer at 95°C for 10 min.

## Statistical analysis
The sample size determination was based on previous research. The animals were randomly assigned to different experimental groups, without exclusion. The experiments were performed unblinded. Each experimental group contains at least three biological replicates. The experiments were replicated in the laboratory at least three times.
The data were analyzed by the Prism 8 (GraphPad) software. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student’s t test was used. For three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparisons tests was used. For longitudinal studies, two-way ANOVA was used. The quantitative results were presented as means ± SEM. P < 0.05 was considered a significant difference.
