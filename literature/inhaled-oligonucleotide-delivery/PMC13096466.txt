## Abstract
p95HER2 is commonly expressed in human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive breast cancer (BC) and is generated primarily from shedding of the extracellular domain of HER2. p95HER2 is oncogenic, driving aggressive tumor growth, and conferring drug resistance. Targeting p95HER2 is challenging. Clinically approved HER2 inhibitors either cannot bind to p95HER2 or show limited inhibitory effects. We used cell lines and orthotopic tumor models (cell line xenografts and patient derived xenografts) to investigate the effects of HER2 inhibitors on p95HER2 and other key signaling proteins in HER2-positive BC, and to compare the therapeutic activities of different HER2 inhibitors. The HER2 inhibitors represent different mechanisms of actions, including trastuzumab, pertuzumab, tucatinib, and lapatinib, all of which are clinically approved, as well as PEPDG278D, a recombinant human protein which was previously shown to induce the degradation of HER2 and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). EGFR is closely related to HER2 and is implicated in drug resistance in HER2-positive BC. HER2-positive BC cells and tumors, despite p95HER2 expression, are exquisitely sensitive to targeted degradation of HER2 and EGFR by PEPDG278D. PEPDG278D-induced degradation of HER2 also consistently eliminates p95HER2. PEPDG278D is far more effective than the clinically approved HER2 inhibitors in inhibiting the oncogenic signaling and growth of HER2-positive BC cells and tumors expressing p95HER2. Most notably, in an orthotopic PDX of HER2-positive BC expressing p95HER2, which is insensitive to trastuzumab and tucatinib, PEPDG278D achieved complete and lasting tumor elimination. Despite p95HER2 expression and resistance to current HER2 inhibitors, HER2-positive BC cells and tumors are highly vulnerable to PEPDG278D-induced degradation of HER2 and EGFR. By inducing HER2 degradation, PEPDG278D eliminates p95HER2 in HER2-positive BC cells and tumors.

## Background
Approximately 20% of breast cancer (BC) cases strongly overexpress human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), typically showing 3 + immunohistochemical staining, which is known as HER2-positive BC. HER2-positive BC results primarily from HER2 gene amplification . HER2 is a receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) and when overexpressed, drives oncogenic signaling, which leads to aggressive tumor phenotype but also provides an opportunity for targeted therapy. Many HER2 targeted drugs have been developed, which have markedly improved the prognosis of patients with this disease. These drugs include monoclonal antibodies (trastuzumab, pertuzumab, and margetuximab), antibody drug conjugates (trastuzumab emtansine, and trastuzumab deruxtecan), and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), including lapatinib, neratinib, pyrotinib, and tucatinib , . However, drug resistance remains a significant problem, underscoring the need for new treatments . The drug resistance mechanism in HER2-positive BC is complex and not fully known, posing significant challenges to developing more effective therapies. Elucidating the mechanism of drug resistance, and in particular, identifying key therapeutic vulnerabilities are critical to advancing treatment for this disease.
Overexpression of human HER2 is accompanied in up to 80% of tumors by truncated membrane-anchored carboxy-terminal fragments (CTF) of 95–100 kD, known as p95HER2 – . p95HER2 is generated mainly from proteolytic shedding of the extracellular domain (ECD) of HER2 by metalloproteases, although it may also result from alternative initiation of translation of HER2 mRNA . Indeed, p95HER2 level strongly correlates with HER2 level in tumor tissues; high serum HER2 ECD level closely associates with tumor HER2-positive status; and only about 2% of BCs that lack HER2 express p95HER2 , , . Despite lacking the ECD, p95HER2 is constitutively active and oncogenic. For example, it was shown that the tyrosine kinase activity and transforming potency of a p95HER2 (deletion of N-terminal 621 amino acids) is 4–5 and 3–sevenfold higher than that of HER2, respectively . It was also shown that p95HER2 forms constitutively active homodimers and activates multiple pro-growth and pro-metastasis signaling pathways . p95HER2 has been linked to aggressive tumor behavior (invasion and metastasis) , , and poor patient survival , – . p95HER2 does not have the binding sites for the therapeutic HER2 antibodies (trastuzumab, pertuzumab, and margetuximab) and antibody–drug conjugates (trastuzumab emtansine, and trastuzumab deruxtecan). Trastuzumab has been the mainstay in the treatment of HER2-positive BC. Several studies have implicated p95HER2 in resistance of HER2-positive BC to trastuzumab and suggested that p95HER2 may be a biomarker of therapeutic efficacy of trastuzumab , , . A recent study shows that p95HER2 also confers resistance to trastuzumab deruxtecan . p95HER2 may be targeted by HER2 TKIs, but the therapeutic activities of these inhibitors are uncertain. Neratinib is inactive as a monotherapy in a mouse model of HER2-positive BC expressing p95HER2, and combining it with trastuzumab deruxtecan achieves only modest tumor growth inhibition . Lapatinib shows significant antitumor activity in mouse BC models overexpressing p95HER2 with or without HER2 overexpression, but tumors continue to grow despite the treatment , . Clinical benefit of lapatinib-based therapy is similar in patients with HER2-positive BC with or without co-expression of p95HER2 .
In this study, we use cell lines and tumor models of HER2-positive BC to investigate the effects of various HER2 inhibitors on p95HER2 and other key signaling proteins, and to compare the therapeutic activities of these inhibitors. The HER2 inhibitors include trastuzumab, pertuzumab, lapatinib, tucatinib, and PEPDG278D, representing different HER2-targeting mechanisms. Trastuzumab, pertuzumab, and tucatinib selectively targets HER2, whereas lapatinib targets both HER2 and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) . As mentioned before, trastuzumab and pertuzumab are monoclonal antibodies, and tucatinib and lapatinib are TKIs. PEPDG278D is an experimental antitumor agent, a recombinant human protein (an enzymatically inactive mutant of peptidase D, also known as prolidase) that induces the degradation of HER2 and EGFR by binding to the ECDs of the receptors and inducing their internalization and lysosomal degradation . EGFR is frequently overexpressed in HER2-positive BC and targeting both HER2 and EGFR may lead to better therapeutic outcome .

## Materials and methods

## Cell lines, and cell culture
The following cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection: HCC-1954 (Cat # CRL-2338), HCC-1569 (Cat # CRL-2330), HCC-1419 (Cat # CRL-2326), UACC-812 (Cat # CRL-1897), SW620 (Cat # CCL-227), and MCF10A (Cat # CRL-10317). MCF10A-p95HER2 cells were generously provided by Dr. Joaquin Arribas, Vall d’Hebron Institute of Oncology, Barcelona, Spain. HCC-1954 cells, HCC-1569 cells and HCC-1419 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). UACC-812 cells were cultured in L-15 medium supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine, 20 ng/ml human epidermal growth factor (EGF), and 20% FBS. MCF10A cells and MCF10A-p95HER2 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine, 15 mM HEPES, 0.5 µg/ml hydrocortisone, 20 ng/ml EGF, 100 ng/ml cholera toxin, 10 ng/ml insulin, and 10% FBS. SW620 cells were cultured in L-15 medium with 10% FBS. Penicillin and streptomycin were added as antibiotics to all culture media at 1%. All cell lines were cultured in humidified incubators at 37 °C with 5% CO2, except for UACC-812 cells and SW620 cells which were cultured in 100% room air. All cell lines were mycoplasma-free and were authenticated using short tandem repeats analysis. RPMI-1640 medium was purchased from Corning Cellgro (Cat # 10–040-CV). L-15 medium (Cat # 11,415–064), DMEM/F12 (Cat # 11,320,033), FBS (Cat # 10,437), HEPES (Cat # 15,630–080), and L- glutamine (Cat # 25,030,081) were purchased from Gibco. Hydrocortisone (Cat# H0888), and cholera toxin (Cat # C8052) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. EGF (Cat # 236-EG-200) was purchased from R&D Systems. Insulin (Cat # 12,585,014) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific.

## Western blot analysis
Cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) twice, and then mixed with 1 × cell lysis buffer from Cell Signaling Technology (Cat # 9803). The lysis buffer was supplemented with 2 mM phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF, Cat # 329–98-6), a protease inhibitor cocktail (Cat # P8215), phosphatase inhibitor cocktail 2 (Cat # P5726), and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail 3 (Cat # P0044), all of which were from Sigma-Aldrich. The mixture was placed on ice for 10 min, sonicated at 4 °C to enhance cell lysis using a Q800R3 sonicator (Qsonica), and finally centrifuged at 14,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant fraction was collected for analysis. Tumor samples were mixed with CelLytic™ MT Cell Lysis Reagent from Sigma-Aldrich (Cat # 3228), which was supplemented with 2 mM PMSF, the protease inhibitor cocktail, phosphatase inhibitor cocktail 2, and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail 3 mentioned above at 14.3 μl buffer per mg tissue, homogenized in a Dounce homogenizer, and sonicated at 4 °C to enhance cell lysis. The supernatant fraction is collected by centrifugation at 14,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration in each sample was measured by a BCA assay kit from Pierce (Cat # 23,228 and 1,859,078). Each sample was resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (8–10%). Proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membrane, probed with specific antibodies, and detected using Immobilon Classico Western HRP Substrate from Millipore (Cat # WBLUC0100) or Clarity Western ECL Substrate from Bio-Rad (Cat # 170–5060). X-ray films (Blu-Lite UHC, MTC Bio, Cat # A8815) were used to record the chemiluminescent signals from the membrane, which were then developed using an automated film processor (Optimax, Protec Medical). The band intensities of select proteins were analyzed using ImageJ (Fiji version, National Institutes of Health, USA) and normalized by control (GAPDH).

## siRNA knockdown of HER2
HCC-1954 cells were grown in six-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well with 2 ml medium) for 24 h and then transfected with nonspecific scramble siRNA or HER2 siRNA (25 nM), using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Cat # 13,778,075). Cells were harvested 48 h later and prepared for Western blot analysis. All siRNAs were purchased from Origene. Their sequences are as follows: rCrGrUrUrArArUrCrGrCrGrUrArUrArArUrArCrGrCrGrUA T (scramble siRNA, Cat # SR30004), and rUrGrUrArArUrUrUrUrGrArCrArUrGrGrUrUrGrGrGrArCrUrCrUr U (HER2 siRNA, Cat # SR320064A).

## Gene transfection
SW620 cells were seeded in six-well plates (1.5 × 105 cells/well) and 24 h later transfected with 1 µg of pCMV6-XL5-HER2 or pLPCX-p95HER2/611-CTF (expressing the 611-CTF of HER2) , using Lipofectamine 3000 from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Cat # L3000008). The cells were then treated with solvent or a HER2 inhibitor for 48 h, from which whole cell lysates were prepared for Western blot analysis.

## RT-PCR
Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy mini kit from Qiagen (Cat # 74,104), and 1 µg RNA per sample was reverse-transcribed to cDNA in 50 μl of reaction using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Cat # 4,374,966). The reverse transcription reaction was performed at 25 °C for 10 min, followed by heating at 48 °C for 30 min, and then 95 °C for 5 min. The primers were purchased from IDT (Integrated DNA Technologies), and their sequences are as follows: For human HER2, 5′-CTGTTTGCCGTGCCACCCTGAGT-3′ (forward), and 5′-CTTCTGCTGCCGTCGCTTGATGAG-3′ (reverse); for human EGFR, 5′- GCCTAAGATCCCGTCCATCG-3′ (forward), and 5′- TTCATGCCCTTTGCGATCTG-3′ (reverse); for human GAPDH, 5′-GACCACTTTGTCAAGCTCATTTC-3′ (forward), and 5′-GGTTGAGCACAGGGTACTTTAT-3′ (reverse). Each PCR amplification was carried out in 25 μl volume, containing 12.5 μl of DreamTaq PCR Master Mixes (2 ×) from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Cat # K1081), 1–4 μl of the reverse-transcribed mixture (cDNA), and 0.2 μM each of specific forward and reverse primers. The PCR conditions is as follows: For HER2, 95 °C for 2 min, 30 cycles at 95 °C (denaturation) for 30 s, 63 °C for 30 s (annealing), and 72 °C for 22 s (extension); for EGFR, 95 °C for 3 min, 32 cycles at 95 °C (denaturation) for 30 s, 58 °C for 30 s (annealing), and 72 °C for 30 s (extension); for GAPDH, 95 °C for 2 min, 25 cycles at 95 °C (denaturation) for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s (annealing), and 72 °C for 18 s (extension). For all the PCR reactions, final extension step was performed at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis with 1% agarose gel, stained by SYBR Safe DNA Gel Stain from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Cat # S33102), and visualized under ultraviolet light.

## Measurement of cell proliferation
Cell growth, except for UACC-812, was measured by the MTT assay in 96-well plates. Each well was seeded with 3 × 103 cells (MCF10A, MCF10A-p95HER2, HCC-1954, and SW620), 4 × 103 cells (HCC-1419), or 5 × 103 cells (HCC-1569) with 0.1 ml culture medium overnight and treated with solvent or a HER2 inhibitor in 0.2 ml medium for 72 h, followed by MTT assay. The HER2 inhibitors include PEPDG278D (5, 25 or 250 nM), trastuzumab (250, 1000, or 5000 nM), pertuzumab (250, 1000 or 5000 nM), tucatinib (10,100, or 1000 nM), and lapatinib (10, 100, or 1000 nM). MTT was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Cat # M2128). Cells were incubated with MTT at 50 µg/0.2 ml medium/well at 37 °C for 3 h. The cells were then washed with PBS and mixed with 0.15 ml/well dimethyl sulfoxide from Sigma-Aldrich (Cat # M81802). Cell density was measured by reading absorbance at 570 nm using a Synergy HTX Multi-Mode Microplate Reader from BioTek. UACC-812 cell growth was measured using the CellTiter-Glo Assay from Promega (Cat # G9241) in 96-well plates. Notably, UACC-812 cells grew relatively slowly, and the MTT assay produced low signal intensity and limited dynamic range. The CellTiter-Glo assay is far more sensitive than the MTT assay in this cell line. Cells were seeded at 4 × 103 cells per well with 0.1 ml culture medium overnight, treated with solvent or a HER2 inhibitor in 0.2 ml medium per well for 72 h. At the end of the treatment, 20 µl CellTiter-Glo reagent was added to each well, which was mixed for 2 min on a shaker to induce cell lysis and then incubated for 10 min to stabilize the luminescent signal. Luminescence was recorded using a Synergy HTX Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (BioTek).

## Mouse studies
NSG mice were purchased from the Cancer Mouse Models Core at the Massey Comprehensive Cancer Center of Virginia Commonwealth University. SCID/Beige mice (C.B-17/IcrHsd-Prkdc-scid) were purchased from Envigo. Mice at approximately 7 weeks of age were used for two tumor models, including orthotopic HCC-1569 cell line-derived xenograft (CDX) model, and HBCx-73 patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model.
Female C.B-17/IcrHsd-Prkdc-scid mice were used for the HCC-1569 CDX model. Approximately 5 × 106 cells in 0.1 ml RPMI-1640 medium without FBS were inoculated orthotopically into the inguinal 4th mammary fat pad. Female NSG mice were used for the HBCx-73 PDX model. The PDX was first expanded in donor mice. Tumor fragments (approximately 20 mm3) were implanted orthotopically into the inguinal 4th mammary fat pad using a trocar. Drinking water was supplemented with 1 μM 17β-estradiol (MedChemExpress, Cat # HY-B0141) to support PDX growth, which was changed twice weekly. Tumor volume was measured using length x width2 ÷ 2. Approximately 3 weeks after cells or tumor fragments were implanted, significant tumor growth was detected, and the mice were randomized cage-wise to different treatment groups using Research Randomizer (www.randomizer.org). The mice were treated with GD, trastuzumab, tucatinib, or GD + PEPDG278D. GD (40 mg/kg) was administered to the mice by intraperitoneal injection (ip) in a single dose, and was administered 1 h before the first dose of PEPDG278D when used in combination. PEPDG278D was administered ip at 5 mg/kg three times weekly (Monday, Wednesday, and Friday). Trastuzumab was administered ip at 10 mg/kg once a week. Tucatinib was administered at 50 mg/kg by oral gavage 5 days a week (Monday to Friday). Drug treatments lasted up to 3–4 weeks. In a separate experiment, to obtain tumor tissues for molecular analysis, mice bearing HCC-1569 CDX tumors were treated with a single dose of GD, plus three doses of PEPDG278D over 6 days. Investigators who measured tumors were not blinded to the treatments. GD was prepared at 13.3 mg/ml in 100 mM proline-acetic acid and 20 mM arginine, pH 5.0, and then diluted in PBS for use. PEPDG278D was prepared in PBS. Trastuzumab was first prepared in water at 21 mg/ml and diluted with PBS for use. Tucatinib was prepared at 10 mg/ml in saline containing 30% SBE-β-CD from MedChemExpress (Cat # HY-17031). Each agent was administered to mice in approximately 0.1 ml volume per 20 g body weight. All the mice were monitored closely for signs of adverse effects and were euthanized 24 h after the final treatment. Mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation. Tumors were promptly removed, snap-frozen, and stored at -80 °C for later analysis.

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prizm 10. For two-group comparison, we used unpaired two-tailed t test. For multi-group comparisons, we used One-Way ANOVA followed by Tukey test. P value of 0.05 or lower was considered statistically significant. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to confirm normality of data sets. No data were excluded from analysis.

## Results

## Growth inhibitory activities of HER2 inhibitors in BC cells with or without overexpression of HER2 or p95HER2
We began our study with four human HER2-positive BC cell lines, including UACC-812, HCC-1419, HCC-1569, and HCC-1954. Presence of p95HER2 in UACC-812, HCC-1419 and HCC-1954 was reported previously . For comparison, we included MCF10A cells which are spontaneously immortalized human breast epithelial cells and lack HER2, as well as MCF10A cells that are stably transfected with p95HER2 (MCF10A-p95HER2), specifically 611-CTF . The HER2-positive cell lines mentioned above carry 8–45 copies of HER2 gene per cell , . HER2 is strongly overexpressed in all four HER2-positive BC cell lines, while p95HER2 level is relatively low in these cells (Fig.  A). However, among the HER2-positive BC cell lines, the higher HER2, the higher p95HER2, consistent with HER2 being the primary source of p95HER2. Notably, two bands of HER2 were detected, which may be related to HER2 glycosylation. Using HCC-1954 cells, we show that both HER2 bands were knocked down by an HER2 siRNA (Fig. S1). Two bands of p95HER2 were also detected, which may also result from glycosylation and/or ECD cleavage at different sites. HCC-1569 cells are PTEN-deficient, and HCC-1954 cells carry activating PIK3CA mutation (H1047R) . PTEN loss and mutant PIK3CA are well known to confer drug resistance in HER2-positive BC. MCF10A cells show no expression of HER2 or p95HER2, but p95HER2 is strongly overexpressed in MCF10A-p95HER2 cells (Fig.  A). We also measured EGFR, which is implicated in drug resistance and poor patient survival in HER2-positive BC , . EGFR level is much higher in HCC-1954 cells than in other HER2-positive BC cells (Fig.  A). Overexpression of p95HER2 in MCF10A cells is associated with increased EGFR expression (Fig.  A), which was also reported previously .
 We examined the sensitivity of these cell lines to five HER2 inhibitors, including trastuzumab, pertuzumab, tucatinib, lapatinib, and PEPDG278D, which represent different mechanisms of actions as mentioned before. MCF10A cells are virtually insensitive to any of the inhibitors, with maximal cell growth inhibition of 18% achieved by PEPDG278D after 72 h treatment (Fig.  B). MCF10A-p95HER2 cells are also largely insensitive to the inhibitors, with maximal cell growth inhibition of 25% achieved by PEPDG278D after 72 h treatment (Fig.  C). The small inhibition of these cells by PEPDG278D may be related to EGFR loss as discussed later. Although p95HER2 is believed to be sensitive to HER2 TKIs, tucatinib and lapatinib inhibited the growth of MCF10A-p95HER2 cells only by15–16%. Even such small inhibition may be partly off-target effects, because tucatinib and lapatinib at the same concentrations (10 and 100 nM) also inhibited the growth of cells that express no HER2 and EGFR (colorectal cancer SW620 cells) by up to 10–16% (Fig. S2). At 1000 nM, the growth inhibitory effects of tucatinib or lapatinib are similar among SW620 (44.6–47.4% inhibition), MCF10A (33.4–38.6% inhibition), and MCF10A-p95HER2 cells (44.0–57.1% inhibition) (Fig. S2). Thus, previous finding that lapatinib is active in p95HER2-expressing cells and tumors , may result largely from off-target effects.
Among the HER2 inhibitors, PEPDG278D is the most effective in inhibiting the growth of the HER2-positive BC cell lines, inhibiting the growth of HCC-1954, UACC-812, HCC-1419, and HCC-1569 by up to 82, 60, 80 and 61%, respectively, after 72 h treatment (Fig.  D-G). Tucatinib and lapatinib are similar in inhibitory efficacy, inhibiting the growth of HCC-1954, UACC-812, HCC-1419, and HCC-1569 by up to 55–56, 27–31, 50–56 and 28–29%, respectively (Fig.  D-G). Trastuzumab is largely ineffective in HCC-1954 and HCC-1419, while inhibiting the growth of HCC-1569 and UACC-812 by up to 8% and 31%, respectively (Fig.  D-G). Pertuzumab is also ineffective in HCC-1954, while inhibiting the growth of HCC-1419, HCC-1569, and UACC-812 by up to 8, 27 and 33%, respectively (Fig.  D-G). Overall, trastuzumab is the weakest and PEPDG278D is the strongest among the HER2 inhibitors for inhibiting the growth of HER2-positive BC cells that express p95HER2.

## Response of p95HER2 and other oncogenic proteins to HER2 inhibitors in BC cells overexpressing HER2 or p95HER2
We next analyzed molecular changes in cells treated by HER2 inhibitors. Cells were treated by each inhibitor for 48 h, including PEPDG278D (25 nM), trastuzumab (250 nM), pertuzumab (250 nM), tucatinib (100 nM), and lapatinib (100 nM). The concentrations of these inhibitors were selected based on their cell growth inhibitory activities in sensitive cell lines. PEPDG278D is the only HER2 inhibitor that consistently eliminated HER2, p-HER2, EGFR, and p-EGFR in the four HER2-positive BC cell lines, although p-EGFR was below detection threshold in UACC-812 cells (Fig.  A). Using densitometric analysis of Western blot bands, we determined that PEPDG278D reduced HER2, p-HER2, EGFR, and p-EGFR in these cells by 87–91, 87–98, 93–97 and 96–99%, respectively (Fig.  B). We have previously shown that PEPDG278D induces the degradation of both HER2 and EGFR , . We confirmed in the present study using HCC-1954 cells that PEPDG278D has no effect on mRNA expression of HER2 and EGFR (Fig. S3). Neither trastuzumab nor pertuzumab significantly reduced HER2 or p-HER2 levels, and in UACC-812 cells, each agent actually significantly increased p-HER2 (Fig.  A). Densitometric analysis of the Western blot bands showed that trastuzumab and pertuzumab increase p-HER2 in UACC-812 cells 1.8-fold and 1.6-fold, respectively (Fig.  B). p-HER2 increase in UACC-812 cells after treatment with trastuzumab and pertuzumab is unusual, since these cells are somewhat more sensitive to these agents than the other HER2-positive cell lines (Fig.  ). A previous study shows that HER2 phosphorylation may remain high despite trastuzumab treatment due to compensatory signaling . None of the cell lines showed change in EGFR and p-EGFR levels after treatment with trastuzumab or pertuzumab, consistent with the fact that these antibodies are HER2-specific. Both tucatinib and lapatinib abolished p-HER2 and p-EGFR but did not reduce HER2 and EGFR expression levels, and in fact increased HER2 level in UACC-812 and HCC-1569 (Fig.  A). Densitometric analysis of the Western blot bands showed that lapatinib and tucatinib reduce p-HER2 78–88% and 81–97%, respectively, and reduce p-EGFR 65–92% and 96–99%, respectively, in the four HER2-positive BC cell lines, but increase HER2 level 1.6–1.8-fold and 1.8–2.5–fold, respectively, in UACC-812 and HCC-1569 (Fig.  B). Although tucatinib is HER2-specific, loss of p-EGFR could result from inhibition of HER2 by tucatinib in an EGFR-HER2 heterodimer. The inability of trastuzumab, pertuzumab, tucatinib, and lapatinib to lower EGFR and HER2 expression levels is noteworthy. There is compelling evidence that abolishing both kinase-dependent and kinase-independent functions of these receptors are critical to maximize inhibition of their oncogenic signaling , , which can only be achieved by eliminating their existence.
 We next measured the effects of HER2 inhibitors on p95HER2. p95HER2 phosphorylation could not be detected in any of the cell lines. PEPDG278D is the most effective eliminator of p95HER2 among the HER2 inhibitors, rendering it virtually undetectable (below the detection threshold) in all four HER2-positive BC cell lines (Fig.  A-B). However, PEPDG278D has no effect on p95HER2 level in MCF10A-p95HER2 cells (Fig.  A-B), suggesting that PEPDG278D elimination of p95HER2 in the HER2-positive BC cell lines results from HER2 depletion. We further evaluated the effect of PEPDG278D on HER2 and p95HER2 that were transiently expressed in cells, using trastuzumab and pertuzumab for comparison. SW620 cells lack both EGFR and HER2 (Fig. S2A), and in SW620 cells transiently overexpressing HER2, PEPDG278D depleted HER2, while trastuzumab and pertuzumab were ineffective (Fig. S4). In SW620 cells transiently overexpressing p95HER2, none of these agents altered p95HER2 expression (Fig. S4). These results further support that PEPDG278D eliminates p95HER2 by depleting HER2. Trastuzumab reduced p95HER2 level in every HER2-positive BC cell line, while pertuzumab did so only in HCC-1569 cells and actually elevated it in UACC-812 cells (Fig.  A). Densitometric analysis of the Western blot bands showed that trastuzumab reduced p95HER2 74–86% in the HER2-positive BC cell lines, and pertuzumab reduced p95HER2 74% in HCC-1569 cells but increased it 1.6-fold in UACC-812 cells (Fig.  B). Trastuzumab was previously shown to inhibit HER2 ECD cleavage . Indeed, neither trastuzumab nor pertuzumab alters p95HER2 level in MCF10A-p95HER2 cells or in SW620 cells expressing p95HER2 (Fig.  and Fig. S4). Both tucatinib and lapatinib strongly elevated p95HER2 level in every HER2-positive cell line, and appear to elevate p95HER2 in MCF10A-p95HER2 cells as well (Fig.  A). Densitometric analysis of the Western blot bands showed that tucatinib and lapatinib elevate p95HER2 1.4–2.6-fold and 1.5–3.8-fold, respectively, in the HER2-positive BC cell lines, and 1.3-fold and 1.7-fold, respectively, in MCF10A-p95HER2 cells (Fig.  B). Because tucatinib and lapatinib either had no effect on HER2 level or only slightly elevated it in the HER2-positive BC cell lines (Fig.  ), the above results suggest potential inhibition of p95HER2 degradation by tucatinib and lapatinib. Lapatinib was previously shown to elevate p95HER2 in tumors in vivo .
We also measured two major signaling proteins downstream of HER2, p95HER2, and EGFR, including AKT and ERK, which represent the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway and the RAS-ERK signaling pathway, respectively. PEPDG278D showed no effect on the expression of AKT and ERK but markedly reduced p-AKT and p-ERK in all four HER2-positive BC cell lines (Fig.  A). Loss of phosphorylation but not expression of AKT and ERK is consistent with suppression of HER2, p95HER2, and EGFR by PEPDG278D in these cells. It is interesting that PEPDG278D is able to inhibit p-AKT in HCC-1954 cells and HCC-1569 cells, because these cells either carry activating PIK3CA mutation or are PTEN-deficient as mentioned before. PEPDG278D also reduced the phosphorylation but not expression of AKT and ERK in MCF10A-p95HER2 cells, which likely resulted from EGFR depletion (Fig.  A). Neither trastuzumab nor pertuzumab showed any effect on AKT, p-AKT, ERK, and p-ERK in the cell lines, except a modest decrease in p-AKT in trastuzumab-treated HCC-1569 cells (Fig.  A). Tucatinib and lapatinib inhibited the phosphorylation of AKT and ERK in the HER2-positive BC cell lines, except p-ERK in tucatinib-treated UACC-812 cells. Decrease in p-AKT and p-ERK is correlated with decrease in p-HER2 and p-EGFR following treatment with tucatinib or lapatinib (Fig.  A). Neither tucatinib nor lapatinib inhibited the phosphorylation of AKT and ERK in MCF10A-p95HER2 cells (Fig.  A). Notably, in most instances, tucatinib and lapatinib are less effective than PEPDG278D in reducing p-AKT and p-ERK in the HER2-positive BC cell lines.
Collectively, the effects of the HER2 inhibitors on the signaling molecules as described above are consistent with their growth-inhibitory effects on the cell lines. Among the five HER2 inhibitors, PEPDG278D is by far the most effective in inhibiting the growth of HER2-positive BC cell lines and their oncogenic signaling. By depleting HER2, PEPDG278D consistently eliminates p95HER2.

## Therapeutic activities of HER2 inhibitors in mouse models of HER2-positive BC expressing p95HER2
First, we used one of the HER2-positive BC cell lines in our study, HCC-1569, to generate a CDX model. HCC-1569 cells were inoculated orthotopically to mammary fat pads in female SCID mice. We compared the antitumor activities of three HER2 inhibitors in this CDX model, including PEPDG278D, trastuzumab, and tucatinib, which represent different HER2-targeting mechanisms. We used dosing plans for these agents that were previously shown to be effective in tumor models of HER2-positive BC , , . Treatment of tumor-bearing mice was started when group average tumor volumes reached beyond 100 mm3, which was approximately 3 weeks after inoculating HCC-1569 cells. Mice were treated with PEPDG278D at 5 mg/kg three times weekly, trastuzumab at 10 mg/kg weekly, and tucatinib at 50 mg/kg daily (five days a week). PEPDG278D was administered in combination with garadacimab (GD). GD is a monoclonal antibody which was recently approved for treatment of hereditary angioedema by inhibiting activated coagulation factor XII (FXIIa) . PEPDG278D is degraded by plasma proteases triggered by FXIIa, but a single dose of GD blocks PEPDG278D degradation for 3 weeks . GD was administered to mice at 40 mg/kg 1 h before the first dose of PEPDG278D. GD itself does not show any antitumor activity . Tumors continued to grow in mice treated with GD, trastuzumab, or tucatinib, and tumor growth rates among the three groups are not statistically different (Fig.  A). In the PEPDG278D plus GD group, tumors began to regress after one week of PEPDG278D treatment, and six of seven tumors (85%) showed no sign of tumor presence after another 13 days of treatment based on visual inspection and palpation, at which point the entire experiment was stopped. However, the partially regressed tumor treated by GD plus PEPDG278D did not provide enough tissue for molecular analysis. Therefore, we performed a small new experiment with a shorter PEPDG278D treatment period and bigger tumors to treat. Specifically, mice bearing HCC-1569 orthotopic tumors, with group average tumor volume reaching approximately 160 mm3, were treated with three doses of PEPDG278D over six days. A single dose of GD was given 1 h before the first dose of PEPDG278D. The treatment caused rapid tumor regression and at the end of the treatment, average tumor regression reached 69.7% (Fig.  B). Initially, two different tumors per group were analyzed for levels of HER2 and p95HER2. Both HER2 and p95HER2 levels were drastically reduced in tumors treated by PEPDG278D (reduced approximately 10.8-fold and 16.1-fold, respectively, based on densitometric analysis of the Western blot bands) but did not appear to be significantly altered in tumors treated by tucatinib (Fig.  C and Fig. S5A). However, levels of HER2 and p95HER2 each varied considerably between the two samples from the GD group and the two samples from the trastuzumab group (Fig.  C and Fig. S5A). Therefore, additional different tumors from the GD and trastuzumab groups (four tumors/group) were analyzed for HER2 and p95HER2 to assess the extent of variation. Intra-group variation in HER2 expression level is relatively small (2.2-fold among GD-treated samples, and 1.9-fold among trastuzumab-treated samples), compared to that of p95HER2 (3.5-fold among GD-treated samples, and 6.1-fold among trastuzumab-treated samples) (Fig.  D and Fig. S5B). The reason for the considerable variation in p95HER2 level in tumors that grow at a similar rate is not known. Next, two different tumors from each treatment group were analyzed for other molecular changes, and also re-analyzed for HER2 and p95HER2. Notably, when the same tumors were analyzed in different experiments, they were given the same sample numbers. Besides eliminating HER2 and p95HER2 as mentioned before, PEPDG278D also abolishes p-HER2 and EGFR (Fig.  E), reducing each by approximately 4.6-fold (Fig. S5C). Notably, p-EGFR was below detection threshold. Trastuzumab inhibition of HER2 phosphorylation was marginal (Fig.  E and Fig. S5C). Surprisingly, unlike in cultured cells where tucatinib inhibited HER2 phosphorylation, it either did not inhibit HER2 phosphorylation or even stimulated it in the tumors (Fig.  E and Fig. S5C). In fact, in further analysis of 4 different tumors, tucatinib increased p-HER2 in three of the four tumors by up to 3.1-fold (Figs. F and S5D). EGFR remained largely unchanged in tumors treated by trastuzumab or tucatinib (Figs. E and S5C).
 A previous study suggests that even at low expression level, p95HER2 may activate a number of oncogenic proteins, such as increasing MET expression, and stimulating the phosphorylation of SRC, AKT and ERK . HER2 is also known to activate MET, SRC, AKT, and ERK . MET is an RTK which may be activated by heterodimerizing with HER2 , while SRC, AKT and ERK are downstream of the RTKs. In PEPDG278D-treated tumors, there was a marked loss of phosphorylation but not expression of MET, SRC, and AKT (Fig.  E). This is consistent with the loss of HER2 and p95HER2 in these tumors. However, p-ERK increased in PEPDG278D-treated tumors, in contrast to p-ERK decrease in PEPDG278D-treated cells in culture (Fig.  A). Because ERK is phosphorylated by MEK, and MEK (MEK1) is inhibited in PEPDG278D-treated tumors (loss of phosphorylation) (Fig.  E), the increased p-ERK level in these tumors may be related to dysregulation of an ERK-directed phosphatase. Increased ERK phosphorylation induced by PEPDG278D seems functionally insignificant, since tumor growth was profoundly inhibited by PEPDG278D. Neither trastuzumab nor tucatinib showed significant inhibitory effect on MET, SRC, AKT, ERK, MEK, and their phosphorylation (Fig.  E). In fact, in three of four tucatinib-treated tumors, p-AKT increased by up to 3.2-fold (Fig.  F and Fig. S5D), closely correlating with p-HER2 increase as described before.
We next compared PEPDG278D, trastuzumab, and tucatinib in an orthotopic PDX model. HBCx-73 (BC911LJ) is an PDX derived from a HER2-positive BC patient treated with trastuzumab in the neoadjuvant setting and harbors amplification of not just HER2 but also EGFR, SRC, and CCNE1 . Female NSG mice bearing orthotopic HBCx-73 were treated with GD, GD plus PEPDG278D, trastuzumab, and tucatinib. The doses and dosing schedules for these agents were the same as used in the HCC-1569 tumor model described before. Treatments were started about 3 weeks after implanting tumors, when the group average tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm3. Neither trastuzumab nor tucatinib significantly inhibited tumor growth, but GD plus PEPDG278D caused rapid tumor regression (Fig.  A). Figure  B shows spaghetti plots of individual tumor growth trajectories in the GD group and GD plus PEPDG278D group for up to 20 days following a single dose of GD or the first dose of PEPDG278D (Fig.  B). In order to obtain tumor tissues for molecular analysis, four tumors were removed after treatment with PEPDG278D for 9 days, while the remaining tumors continued PEPDG278D treatment. PEPDG278D treatment in all mice was stopped after another 7 days, because all tumors had disappeared completely (occurring within 8–14 days of treatment), based on visual inspection and palpation. Treatments in other groups were stopped at the same time. PEPDG278D-treated mice were followed for 72 days without additional treatment, but no tumor recurred as confirmed by necropsy, indicating lasting and complete tumor remission. We next evaluated treatment effects on various signaling proteins using two tumors per group. As in HCC-1569 tumors, PEPDG278D obliterated HER2 and p95HER2. PEPDG278D also obliterated the phosphorylation of HER2 and p95HER2 (Fig.  C). In addition, PEPDG278D obliterated EGFR and p-EGFR (Fig.  C). Densitometric analysis of the Western blot bands indicate that PEPDG278D reduced the levels of HER2, p95HER2, p-HER2, p-p95HER2, EGFR, and p-EGFR by 89–99% (Fig. S6A). Consistent with these changes, PEPDG278D abolished the phosphorylation but not expression of MET, SRC, AKT, and ERK. Notably, unlike HCC-1569 tumors where PEPDG278D increased ERK phosphorylation, PEPDG278D abolished ERK phosphorylation in HBCx-73 tumors. Neither trastuzumab nor tucatinib altered the expression levels of HER2 and p95HER2 in HBCx-73 tumors (Fig.  C). Tucatinib inhibited the phosphorylation of both HER2 and p95HER2 in HBCx-73 tumors, and also inhibited EGFR phosphorylation and decreased EGFR level in these tumors (Fig.  C). Densitometric analyses of the Western blot bands indicate that tucatinib inhibited the phosphorylation of HER2 and p95HER2 81–86% and reduced EGFR level 50% (Fig. S6A). However, the inhibitory effects of tucatinib on HER2, p95HER2, and EGFR did not translate consistently into inhibition of downstream signaling, i.e., inhibition of p-SRC, but no inhibition of p-AKT and p-ERK (Fig.  C). Trastuzumab did not consistently inhibited the phosphorylation of HER2, p95HER2, EGFR, and SRC, inhibiting their phosphorylation in one tumor but not in the other tumor, and even increasing p-p95HER2 in the other tumor (Fig.  C). In both tumors, trastuzumab showed no effect on MET, p-MET, SRC, AKT, p-AKT, ERK, and p-ERK (Fig.  C). Lack of changes in these proteins is consistent with tumor resistance to trastuzumab shown in Fig.  A.
 Because the two trastuzumab-treated tumors vary significantly in phosphorylation of several signaling proteins as described above, we expanded the analyses to four tumors/group to assess the variation, using four tumors treated by tucatinib for comparison (Fig.  D). The two tumors analyzed before were coded with the same sample numbers. Among the four trastuzumab-treated tumors, while EGFR levels are similar, the levels of HER2, p95HER2, p-HER2, p-p95HER2, and p-EGFR vary by twofold, 2.2-fold, 3.1-fold, 26-fold, and 57.1-fold, respectively (Fig.  D and Fig. S6B). These results show marked variation in tumor response to trastuzumab. Among the four tucatinib-treated tumors, the levels of HER2, p95HER2, and EGFR each does not differ significantly, whereas the levels of p-HER2, p-p95HER2 and p-EGFR vary by 5.3-fold, 45.8-fold, and 2.6-fold, respectively (Figs. D and S6B). Thus, there is also a marked variation in tumor response to tucatinib with regard to inhibition of its targets.

## Discussion
Despite decades of extensive research, drug resistance in HER2-positive BC has not been fully addressed. p95HER2 is commonly present in HER2-positive BC, which is generated primarily from ECD shedding of HER2. p95HER2 initiates oncogenic signaling, promotes tumor growth and progression, and confers drug resistance. It lacks binding sites for many clinically available HER2 inhibitors, including the monoclonal antibodies and antibody–drug conjugates (trastuzumab, pertuzumab, and margetuximab, trastuzumab emtansine, and trastuzumab deruxtecan). Indeed, neither trastuzumab shown in the present study nor trastuzumab deruxtecan shown in a recent study mentioned before inhibits the growth of BC tumors overexpressing p95HER2. Trastuzumab reduces p95HER2 by inhibiting ECD shedding of HER2. However, as shown in the present study, while trastuzumab reduces p95HER2 across HER2-positive BC cell lines in vitro, its p95HER2-lowering activity in tumors in vivo is limited and varies with individual tumors. Although HER2 TKIs may target p95HER2, the therapeutic activities of these inhibitors in BC cells and tumors expressing this oncogene are not clear. In the present study, neither tucatinib nor lapatinib significantly inhibits the growth of MCF10A-p95HER2 cells in vitro, and tucatinib does not show significant antitumor activity in two mouse models of HER2-positive BC expressing p95HER2. Neratinib is inactive in a mouse tumor model of HER2-positive BC expressing p95HER2, although it may induce proteasomal degradation of p95HER2, and combining it with trastuzumab deruxtecan achieves only modest tumor growth inhibition . While lapatinib shows significant antitumor activity in mouse BC models overexpressing p95HER2 with or without HER2 overexpression , , clinical benefit of lapatinib-based therapy is similar in patients with HER2-positive BC with or without co-expression of p95HER2 . Our present study also indicates that the growth-inhibitory activities of HER2 TKIs may derive partly from off-target effects. All HER2 TKIs as well as other HER2 inhibitors used in the clinic share a major weakness, i.e., their inability to significantly downregulate tumor HER2 level, as shown in the present study and previously , , allowing cancer cells to retain kinase-independent oncogenic functions of HER2 .
p95HER2 cannot be directly targeted by PEPDG278D due to lack of a binding site. However, we show that PEPDG278D efficiently eliminates p95HER2 in all HER2-positive BC cells in vitro and in tumors in vivo by inducing HER2 degradation. By inducing HER2 degradation, PEPDG278D also abolishes both kinase-dependent and kinase-independent oncogenic functions of HER2. In addition, PEPDG278D also induces the degradation of EGFR which contributes to drug resistance in HER2-positive BC. Of all the HER2 inhibitors evaluated in this study, only PEPDG278D shows such profound impact, and PEPDG278D is by far the most effective in inhibiting the growth of HER2-positive BC cells and tumors in vitro and in vivo. It is effective against cell lines and tumors that are resistant to trastuzumab and other clinically approved HER2 inhibitors. In both CDX and PDX models, PEPDG278D induces rapid tumor regression, despite resistance to trastuzumab and tucatinib. In the PDX model, PEPDG278D appears to achieve a cure, as the mice were observed for 72 days without treatment after apparently achieving complete tumor regression, and no recurrence occurred. This result is particularly encouraging, because orthotopic tumors grow in a physically relevant environment, and PDX is well known to conserve the heterogeneity of original tumors. Analysis of signaling changes in cell lines and tumors shows that PEPDG278D, but not any other HER2 inhibitors, obliterates oncogenic signaling orchestrated by p95HER2 and overexpressed HER2. These results suggest that drug-resistant HER2-positive BC is highly susceptible to pharmacological dual degradation of HER2 and EGFR. This also suggests that the PEPDG278D may significantly outperform current HER2 inhibitors in patients with HER2-positive BC. We have previously reported that PEPDG278D inhibits HER2-positive BC cells and tumors resistant to trastuzumab , . The present work extends the previous studies by comparing the therapeutic activities of PEPDG278D and a group of clinically used HER2 inhibitors and assessing their effects on p95HER2. However, it remains unclear to what extent the elimination of p95HER2 by PEPDG278D contributes to its antitumor efficacy in HER2-positive BC cells and tumors. A brief schematic summarizing the mechanism of action of PEPDG278D is presented in Figure S7. Adverse effects of PEPDG278D were not detected in mouse experiments. Previous studies have shown that PEPDG278D targets HER2 and EGFR only when they are overexpressed, enabled by its unique binding to the RTKs , . In discussing PEPDG278D therapeutic efficacy, it is important to consider the difference between drug-resistant HER2-positive BC and HER2-positive BC that loses HER2 expression during tumor progression or treatment and therefore is insensitive to HER2 inhibitors. PEPDG278D has not been evaluated against the latter, and it is not known if such tumor may overexpress EGFR and therefore may respond to PEPDG278D.
Degraders of HER2 and EGFR based on proteolysis targeting chimera (PROTAC) or lysosome-targeting chimeras (LYTACs) have been reported. A tucatinib-directed PROTAC degrader of HER2 (CH74C) was recently reported, but its antitumor activity in a mouse model of HER2-positive BC is not significantly better than tucatinib . Many degraders of EGFR and its mutants using PROTAC or LYTAC have been synthesized and evaluated, some of which show significant antitumor activities in mouse tumor models . However, these agents have been evaluated almost exclusively in lung cancer models. A lapatinib-directed PROTAC degrades EGFR and HER2 in HER2-positive BC cells and ovarian cancer cells in vitro but in vivo activity of this agent has not been reported .

## Conclusions
Despite expression of p95HER2 and resistance to clinically approved HER2 inhibitors, such HER2-positive BC cells and tumors are highly vulnerable to dual degradation of HER2 and EGFR induced by PEPDG278D. HER2 degradation induced by PEPDG278D also eliminates p95HER2 in HER2-positive BC cells and tumors. PEPDG278D is a highly promising antitumor agent in HER2-positive BC.
