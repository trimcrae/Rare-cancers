## Abstract
Vasohihibin‐2 (VASH2) is a homolog of vasohibin‐1 (VASH1) and is overexpressed in various cancers. Vasohihibin‐2 acts on both cancer cells and cancer microenvironmental cells. Previous analyses have shown that VASH2 promotes cancer progression and abrogation of VASH2 results in significant anticancer effects. We therefore propose VASH2 to be a practical molecular target for cancer treatment. Modifications of antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) such as bridged nucleic acids (BNA)‐based modification increases the specificity and stability of ASO, and are now applied to the development of a number of oligonucleotide‐based drugs. Here we designed human VASH2‐ASOs, selected an optimal one, and developed 2′,4′‐BNA‐based VASH2‐ASO. When systemically administered, naked 2′,4′‐BNA‐based VASH2‐ASO accumulated in the liver and showed its gene‐silencing activity. We then examined the effect of 2′,4′‐BNA‐based VASH2‐ASO in liver cancers. Intraperitoneal injection of naked 2′,4′‐BNA‐based VASH2‐ASO exerted a potent antitumor effect on orthotopically inoculated human hepatocellular carcinoma cells. The same manipulation also showed potent antitumor activity on the splenic inoculation of human colon cancer cells for liver metastasis. These results provide a novel strategy for the treatment of primary as well as metastatic liver cancers by using modified ASOs targeting VASH2.

We designed 2′,4′‐bridged nucleic acids‐based vasohihibin‐2 (VASH2)‐antisense oligonucleotide, showed its localization in the liver after systemic administration, and determined its anticancer effect in an animal model of liver cancer.

## Distribution of 2′,4′‐BNA‐based VASH2‐ASO in mice
We used PET analysis to examine the distribution of 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO in mice. The maximum intensity projection PET images of mice after i.v. injection of 64Cu‐labeled ASO, the TAC in the tissues, and the ex vivo distribution after the PET studies are shown in Figure  . Antisense oligonucleotide was rapidly cleared from the bloodstream, and the radioactivity in the bladder markedly increased with time, indicating that these probes were excreted mainly by renal clearance (Figure  ). The hepatic and renal accumulation of ASO were visualized in each PET image (Figure  ), and the renal TAC was higher than the hepatic TAC until 90 min after probe injection (Figure  ). The renal and hepatic TACs were almost steady for 15–90 min, whereas both TACs were decreased at 24 and 48 h after the injection. These data indicated that the renal and hepatic accumulation of ASO was high compared with other tissues (Figure  ).
 The plasma and tissue concentrations of ASO were analyzed by LC/MS at 5 min, 20 min, and 1.5, 24, and 48 h after i.v. administration. Antisense oligonucleotide was detectable in the plasma until 1.5 h, but undetectable at 24 and 48 h after administration. The greatest distribution was observed in the kidney at all times, and the concentration was almost steady until 48 h after administration. The second greatest distribution was observed in the liver, and the concentration in the liver also remained largely unchanged even at 48 h after administration (Figure  ).

## Effects of 2′,4′‐BNA‐based VASH2‐ASO on tumor growth in liver
We orthotopically inoculated HepG2‐luc cells in the liver. After confirming tumor engraftment by in vivo imaging, we i.p. injected 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO and examined its gene silencing ability in vivo. We observed that VASH2 expression in the liver tumor was markedly decreased on day 2, but returned to basal level on day 3 after the injection (Figure  ). We therefore started the injection of 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO when the luciferase activity in the liver reached 5 × 108 photons/s, and repeated every 3 days for the evaluation of antitumor effects. In vivo imaging indicated the tumor growth in the liver was significantly inhibited by the treatment (Figure  ). Consequently, the 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO treatment prevented body weight loss of mice by the tumor growth (Figure  ). Ex vivo imaging of the liver confirmed that the spread of tumors in the liver was inhibited by the treatment (Figure  ). Moreover, histological analyses revealed that large vacuoles were evident in the tumor of 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO treated mice, indicating tumor destruction (Figure  ). Epithelial–mesenchymal transition is involved in tumor spreading, and VASH2 plays a critical role in the regulation of EMT. , , , We therefore examined the expression of molecules related to EMT. As shown in Figure  , the expression of E‐cadherin, fibronectin, SNAIL1, and SNAIL2 were significantly correlated with that of VASH2.

## Effects of 2′,4′‐BNA‐based VASH2‐ASO on metastasis to liver
We injected HT29‐luc cells in the spleen of mice. Three days after this injection of HT29‐luc cells, we started the treatment with 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO every 3 days for the evaluation liver metastasis. We could start to detect liver metastasis in the control group on day 15, and significant inhibition of liver metastasis was evident on day 21 by the 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO treatment (Figure  ).

## DISCUSSION
Here we planned to develop VASH2‐targeting anticancer therapy, and for this purpose, we applied 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based modification of ASO. We determined the optimal sequence of ASO, developed 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based modification, confirmed its bioavailability in vivo, and used it for treatment. Because of its localization in the liver, we selected liver cancers and used orthotopic as well as metastatic liver cancer models. Obviously, our results revealed the effectiveness of VASH2‐targeting oligonucleotide‐based therapy for liver cancers.
Application of VASH2 as a molecular target for anticancer therapy has been proposed, as abrogation of the expression of VASH2 in various cancers showed significant anticancer effects. , , , , , , Indeed, the local injection of VASH2‐targeting siRNA showed anticancer activity in an ovarian cancer model. However, as siRNA must be delivered sufficiently in the tumor, we need to settle a number of issues on the systemic administration of siRNA, such as delivery and maintenance of its activity in vivo, for clinical use. To avoid such problems, here we applied 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based modification of ASO.
The usefulness of 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based modification is that the modification significantly improves the specificity and stability of ASO. When naked 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐ASO is systemically administered, it has been shown to be accumulated in the liver and exert its gene‐silencing activity without any carrier substance. Here we used PET analyses and confirmed this characteristic of 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO. Moreover, the LC/MS analysis and RT‐PCR analysis revealed that the tissue concentration of 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO in the liver was almost steady until 48 h after treatment, and the localized 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO maintained its gene‐silencing activity 48 h after treatment. These pharmacokinetics and gene‐silencing properties of VASH2‐ASO are consistent with what has been previously reported for 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based ASOs, which are stable in tissues for more than a week and exert sustained antisense effects because of their high nuclease resistance and affinity for the target RNA.
The pharmacokinetics of nucleic acid‐based medicines containing ASOs requires multifaceted evaluation due to a concern of their metabolic instability. Positron emission tomography imaging can provide minimally invasive quantitative pharmacokinetic data in the same individual with high sensitivity and spatial–temporal resolution, whereas LC/MS analysis distinguishes original drug molecules from their metabolites without using labeled compounds. Based on their complementary advantages, combining PET imaging and LC/MS analysis is expected to provide more precise pharmacokinetics of nucleic acid‐based medicines. For quantification of low concentrations of nucleic acid‐based medicines in tissue samples by LC/MS analysis, pretreatment for the removal of disturbing tissue components and efficient ionization conditions are essential but have not yet been standardized. In the present study, these conditions were optimized and an ASO evaluation with a detection limit of several tens of ng/mg was achieved. The LC/MS‐based pharmacokinetic data showed the maintained concentrations of VASH2‐ASO in the kidneys and liver up to 48 h postadministration, while PET‐based pharmacokinetic data showed decreased concentrations in the kidneys and liver at 24 and 48 h. This discrepancy is probably due to cleavage of the phosphodiester bond in the linker used for 64Cu‐labeling of VASH2‐ASO in the PET study. As a result, Cu‐64 was cleared from the tissues and the tissue concentration of VASH2‐ASO was estimated to be lower. The use of more nuclease‐resistant linkers will be necessary to enable long‐term pharmacokinetic evaluation by PET.
Because of this bioavailability after the systemic administration of 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO, we thought our approach would be appropriate for the treatment of liver cancers. Additionally, we have previously reported the close association between the expression of VASH2 and the progress of hepatocellular carcinomas. We therefore applied a mouse model of hepatocellular carcinoma, and clearly showed the anticancer effect of 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO. Importantly, the knockdown of VASH2 in hepatocellular carcinoma cells inhibited their EMT. We and others have shown that VASH2 is involved in the promotion of EMT in cancers. , , , It is well accepted that EMT associates with tumor invasion, metastasis, and conversion of tumors from low‐ to high‐grade malignancy, and numerous studies have shown the involvement of EMT in the progression of hepatocellular carcinoma. , , In particular, the knockdown of VASH2 in HepG2 cells abolishes the EMT phenotype with the decreased expression of Zeb1 and Zeb2. We reproduced these effects with 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO in HepG2 cells inoculated in the liver. We therefore propose that the inhibition of EMT is one of the mechanisms that contributes to the effect of 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO on hepatocellular carcinoma. We selected exon 7 of the VASH2 gene containing the TCP domain in order to target most of the VASH2 transcripts. However, as VASH2 is a multifunctional protein, the role of TCP activity in EMT is obscure. Further study is currently underway to clarify this point.
The liver is one of the most susceptible sites for cancer metastasis from other organs such as gastrointestinal, breast, lung, and genitourinary cancers. Clinicopathological analyses have revealed that VASH2 is involved in the progression of various cancers including pancreatic, breast, esophageal, , and gastric cancer. We therefore tested whether 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO would work on metastatic liver cancer as well. We injected human colon cells in the spleen of mice as a model of liver metastasis and could show the significant effect of 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based VASH2‐ASO on the liver metastasis.
In conclusion, the present study confirmed the value of VASH2 as the molecular target of cancer treatment, and showed an attractive way applying 2′,4′‐BNA/LNA‐based modification of ASO. We are confident that the present study will provide the strategy that can be exploited for the development of primary and metastatic liver cancer treatment targeting VASH2.
