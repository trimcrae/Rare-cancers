## Abstract

## Background
Osteoporosis (OP) is a prevalent age-related disease characterized by bone loss and increased fracture risk. Mitochondrial dysfunction is tightly associated with OP pathogenesis. This study aimed to explore the role of mitochondrial dysfunction-related genes (MDRGs) in OP.

## Methods
Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified from OP transcriptomic data sets, and weighted gene co-expression network analysis was performed to determine OP-associated modules. Machine learning algorithms (Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, and Lasso) were applied to identify hub MDRGs. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) and regulatory network analysis were performed to elucidate their functions and regulatory interactions. Hub gene's expression was validated in tissues using immunofluorescence, and osteogenic induction assays were conducted to validate the biological role and mechanism of the key gene VPS35 in osteoblast differentiation.

## Results
ALAS1, HSPB1, and VPS35 were identified as hub MDRGs, with VPS35 showing good diagnostic performance. GSEA highlighted potential molecular pathways associated with hub genes in OP, such as TNF–NFκB, RAS–ERK, and RAS–PI3K signaling pathways. In addition, a regulatory network involving hub genes, miRNAs, and lncRNAs was preliminarily constructed, providing insights into possible regulatory mechanisms underlying OP. Experimental validation demonstrated that VPS35 overexpression inhibited osteoblast differentiation by suppressing the ERK/PI3K/AKT signaling pathways, and this progression was regulated by miR-142-5p.

## Conclusions
This study reveals the potential roles of MDRGs in OP pathogenesis. VPS35 acts as a negative regulator of osteogenic differentiation by inhibiting ERK and PI3K/AKT signaling, providing preliminary insights into mitochondrial regulation of bone metabolism and potential therapeutic targets for OP.

## Introduction
Osteoporosis (OP), a prevalent skeletal disorder characterized by reduced bone mineral density (BMD) and increased fracture risk, has been a significant public health concern worldwide [ ]. With aging populations, its prevalence continues to escalate, resulting in approximately 200 million sufferers every year globally [ ]. A previous study reported that the prevalence of OP in Chinese women and men aged over 50 years was 29.13% and 6.46%, respectively [ ]. Hip fractures caused by OP significantly decrease patients’ life quality, with 40% of patients unable to walk independently and approximately 20% dying within 1 year [ ]. The multifactorial nature of OP involves a complex interplay of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors [ ]. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying this condition is crucial for developing effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
OP is a common aging-related disease, accompanied by metabolic process changes [ ]. The skeleton undergoes continuous remodeling and renewal. Under normal physiological conditions, a dynamic balance exists between the progress of bone resorption and formation. However, during aging or under stress conditions, the imbalance between bone resorption and formation leads to bone loss, ultimately resulting in OP [ – ]. Mitochondria, which generate the majority of the energy required by the organism, are essential for maintaining the balance between bone resorption and formation [ ]. Mitochondrial dysfunction disrupts cellular homeostasis, leading to oxidative stress, impaired osteoblast function, and enhanced osteoclast activity, ultimately causing bone loss and fragility fractures [ , , ]. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have indicated that mitochondrial dysfunction in osteoblasts and osteoclasts is associated with the progression of OP. For example, SIRT1 modulates osteoblast senescence via mitochondrial dysfunction during the OP progression [ ]. Cao et al. discovered that metformin can reverse mitochondrial dysfunction in osteoblasts, thereby reducing bone loss in patients with OP [ ]. Mitochondrial dysfunction has emerged as a compelling area of research in OP pathogenesis.
This study aimed to elucidate the involvement of mitochondrial dysfunction-related genes (MDRGs) in OP pathophysiology. Our research constructed gene co-expression networks and identified key hub genes associated with OP susceptibility and progression. Through comprehensive bioinformatics analyses, this study uncovered potential molecular pathways linking mitochondrial dysfunction to OP and prioritized candidate genes for further experimental validation.

## Methods

## Data acquisition
Two data sets, GSE56815 and GSE147287, were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE56815 cohort contained microarray analysis data from 40 OP and 40 normal samples, and the GSE147287 cohort included single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) from 1 osteoarthritis (OA) and 1 OP specimen. In addition, 2,392 MDRGs were downloaded from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org). Specifically, the keyword “mitochondrial dysfunction” was entered into the GeneCards search engine, and the retrieved genes were ranked according to the Relevance score provided by the database. Genes with a Relevance score ≥ 5 were selected for subsequent analyses.

## Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in OP
DEGs in the normal and OP samples in the GSE56815 cohort were identified using the Limma package (version 3.46.0) in R (p < 0.05 and |log fold change (FC)|≥ log 1.1). The DEGs were visualized through a volcano plot using the R package ggplot2 (version 3.3.5).

## Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)
WGCNA package (version 1.70–3.70) [ ] was utilized to identify gene modules associated with OP. To reduce noise and improve the stability of network construction, the median absolute deviation (MAD) was calculated for each gene to evaluate expression fluctuation, and the top 50% of genes with the highest MAD values (a total of 6,049 genes) were selected for WGCNA analysis. Hierarchical clustering of samples was first performed to remove outliers. Next, the optimal soft threshold was determined to achieve a network approaching a scale‐free distribution. Co-expression modules were then derived from the scale‐free network constructed using the optimal soft threshold, and a hierarchical clustering tree was generated. The minimum count of module genes was set to 50, softpower was 12, and the module merge threshold was 0.25. Correlation analysis was employed to identify key modules associated with OP. The top three module genes associated with OP were selected for further identification of hub genes.

## Identification of OP-related MDRGs
To identify OP-related MDRGs, DEGs, MDRGs, and genes from the top three WGCNA modules most strongly correlated with OP were intersected and shown in a Venn diagram. The Venn diagram was drawn using the R package VennDiagram (version 1.6.20).

## Hub MDRGs identification in OP using machine learning analyses
Three machine learning methods, RandomForest (RF) [ ], support vector machine (SVM) [ ], and Lasso [ ], were used to screen out characteristic MDRGs related to OP. The input features were the normalized expression matrix of differentially expressed MDRGs, and the sample grouping (OP vs. normal control) served as the classification label. In the RF model, implemented using the randomForest package (version 4.6–14.6), the random seed was set to 3 and the number of decision trees (ntree) to 500 to ensure reproducibility. Genes were ranked according to their importance scores (Mean Decrease Gini). In the SVM model, constructed using the e1071 (version 1.7–13.7) packages, a radial basis function kernel was applied. Recursive feature elimination (RFE) combined with fivefold cross-validation was applied to rank all genes based on their contribution to model performance. The average rank across cross-validation folds was then calculated for each gene. In the Lasso regression analysis, implemented via the glmnet package (version 4.1–7.1), tenfold cross-validation was used to determine the optimal penalty parameter (λ = 0.006). Finally, the top 10 genes ranked by feature importance in the RF, the top 10 genes with the lowest average ranks in the SVM, and the genes selected by the Lasso regression were intersected to obtain the final set of hub MDRGs.

## scRNA-seq analysis
Preprocessing and filtering of scRNA-seq data were conducted using the Seurat package. Quality control criteria were set as nFeature_RNA > 500, 1,000 < nCount_RNA < 20,000, and percent.mt < 20. After normalization using the Scale Data function, the data were processed to find significant principal components through principal component analysis (PCA). Then, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (tSEN) analysis was performed to screen out cell clusters. These cell clusters were annotated using SingleR 2.0.0 in R, and intercellular communication was analyzed using the CellChat package (version 1.6.0). The distribution of different cell types in OP and OA samples was explored through Fisher’s test. Finally, pseudo-time analysis was performed to acquire the cell trajectory using the Monocle2 package.

## Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)
GSEA was conducted using the GSEA software (version 4.4.0) to identify pathways associated with hub MDRGs. Genes were ranked using Pearson methods, and the KEGG gene set from MSigDB was used as the reference. We used a weighted enrichment statistic, 1,000 permutations (permutation type = gene_set), and filtered gene sets between 15 and 500 genes. Pathways with padj < 0.05 were considered significant.

## Construction of regulatory network, immune infiltration analysis, and drug sensitivity prediction
The miRNAs regulating hub genes were searched in the TargetScan database. Common miRNAs that regulate all hub genes were screened out to predict lncRNA in the LncACTdb database (http://bio-bigdata.hrbmu.edu.cn/LncACTdb/index.html). Subsequently, a regulatory network of lncRNA, miRNA, and hub genes was constructed.
To further explore the immune landscape associated with OP, immune cell infiltration analysis was performed based on the GSE56815 transcriptomic data set using the xCell algorithm. The relative enrichment scores of various immune cell types were quantified, and the differences in immune cell infiltration between OP and normal groups were compared.
Drug sensitivity prediction was performed using the “oncoPredict” R package, which employs ridge regression models trained on the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) data sets (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/). Gene expression profiles of GSE56815 were used as input to predict the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for each compound.

## Animals
C57BL/6 J mice (10-week-old female, n = 12) were purchased from the Zhejiang Academy of Medical Sciences (Zhejiang, China). All mice were maintained at 22 ± 2 °C with 45% humidity under a 12-h light/dark cycle and allowed ad libitum access to water and food for an additional 2 weeks. The Zhejiang University of Traditional Chinese Medicine Laboratory Animal Center Committee, ZJCLA (approval number: 202207-0530), approved the experimental protocols. All animal handling and sampling methods complied with animal welfare standards. Reporting in this manuscript follows the recommendations of the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

## Ovariectomy (OVX) model
All mice were randomly divided into the sham group and the OVX group (n = 6 for each group). Mice were anesthetized using 100 mg/kg ketamine combined with 10 mg/kg xylazine, and a 50 mm incision was made on their backs. After removing the muscle tissue to expose both ovaries, the ovaries of model mice were excised after ligating the fallopian tubes, and the incision was sutured. The sham mice undergo the removal of adipose tissue after bilateral ovarian exposure. Four weeks after surgery, all mice were euthanized by inhalation of CO2, and the femurs were extracted and placed in a 10% formalin hydrochloric acid solution for decalcification.

## Immunofluorescence
After decalcification, the femur tissue was embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 4 μm thick slices. Following deparaffinization and hydration with 100%, 95%, and 85% alcohol for 6 min each, the sections were subjected to a sodium citrate solution. After washing with phosphate buffer, the sections were incubated with 3% BSA at room temperature for 30 min. Next, the sections were incubated with primary antibody against VPS35 (1:400; #10236-1-AP, Proteintech, CA, USA) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (#HKI0029, HaoKe Biotechnology Co.Ltd, Hangzhou, China) at room temperature for 50 min in the dark. Then, the sections were incubated with DAPI solution at room temperature for 8 min in the dark. An inverted fluorescence microscope with an excitation wavelength of 490 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm was applied to observe the sections.

## Cell culture
Human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs, #CP-H166) were purchased from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

## Osteogenic differentiation
Osteogenic induction was performed using standard osteogenic induction medium (OIM) (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 50 µM ascorbic acid, and 10 mM β-glycerophosphate). The medium was changed every 2–3 days. For in vitro modelling of osteoporotic, OIM was supplemented with continuous 0.1 mM dexamethasone (Dex) for 7–4 days [ ].

## Cell transfection and treatments
Cell transfection was conducted using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher). For gain- and loss-of-function studies, hBMSCs maintained in OIM + Dex were transfected with VPS35 overexpression plasmid (oe-VPS35) or VPS35-targeting siRNA (si-VPS35) and their matched negative controls (oe-NC and si-NC). For miRNA experiments, cells were transfected with miR-142-5p mimics or miR-142-5p inhibitor and corresponding negative controls. In addition, oe-VPS35-transfected cells were treated with 30 μM ERK agonist for 24 h to probe pathway involvement [ ].

## Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. For mRNA analysis, cDNA was synthesized with the PrimeScript™ reverse transcription kit. qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix on an ABI 7500 system. The following thermocycling conditions were used for the qPCR: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min; followed by 40 cycles of 20 s at 95 °C and 45 s at 58 °C. Gene expression levels of VPS35, OCN, OPN, RUNX2, and miR-142-5p were quantified. GAPDH (for mRNA) and U6 (for miRNA) were used as internal controls. Relative expression was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method.

## Western blotting
Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was quantified with a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (20 µg) were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated with primary antibodies against VPS35 (1:2000, #SJ-AB2098, SanjingBio Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China), OCN (1:2000, #SJ-AB2608, SanjingBio), OPN (1:2000, #SJ-AB19795, SanjingBio), RUNX2 (1:10000, #SJ-AB6433, SanjingBio), ERK1/2 (1:2000, #SJ-AB0809, SanjingBio), phospho-ERK1/2 (1:2000, #SJ-AB3173, SanjingBio), AKT (1:2000, #SJ-AB0754, SanjingBio), phospho-AKT (1:5000, #SJ-AB6393, SanjingBio), and GAPDH (loading control; 1:10000, #10494-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) overnight at 4 °C. After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10000, #SA00001-2, Proteintech), bands were visualized using ECL substrate and quantified by densitometry with ImageJ.

## Dual-luciferase reporter assay
To validate the predicted miR-142-5p binding site in the 3′-UTR of VPS35, wild-type (WT) and mutated (MUT) VPS35 3′-UTR fragments were cloned downstream of the firefly luciferase reporter in the pMIR–REPORT vector. hBMSCs were co-transfected with WT or MUT reporter plasmids together with miR-142-5p mimics or NC mimics. At 24 h post-transfection, luciferase activity was measured using the dual-luciferase reporter assay system according to the manufacturer's instructions. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase (internal control).

## Statistical analysis
R software version 4.0.3, SPSS Statistics 25.0, and GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 were applied for statistical analyses. The diagnostic value of hub MDRGs was evaluated using the receiver operator curve (ROC). The Pearson method was used for correlation analysis. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was set as p < 0.05.

## Results

## Identification of OP-related MDRGs
First, 417 DEGs were identified between the OP and normal samples from the GSE56815. There were 173 up-regulated and 244 down-regulated genes (Table S1), and results were illustrated using a volcano plot (Fig.  A). Subsequently, WGCNA was performed on the GSE56815 data set to pinpoint gene modules that were most closely correlated with OP. A scale-free network was constructed with a soft threshold of 12, resulting in a high level of scale-free topology (R2 = 0.86, Fig.  B, C). This analysis revealed 11 modules through the average linkage hierarchical clustering (Fig.  D). The inter-module correlations were shown in a heatmap (Fig.  E). Moreover, the tan module eigengene was positively associated with OP (correlation coefficient = 0.26), whereas the module eigengenes of black (correlation coefficient = −0.25) and greenyellow (correlation coefficient = −0.24) were negatively associated with OP (p < 0.05, Fig.  F). These three modules, including 80, 606, and 81 genes, respectively, were the modules most strongly associated with OP and were further intersected with the DEGs and MDRGs. Finally, this intersection led to the identification of 12 key MDRGs crucially implicated in OP (Fig.  G and Table S2).
 

## Identification and cellular distribution of hub MDRGs in OP
Three machine learning algorithms were applied to the 12 OP-related MDRGs to identify potential hub genes. The ranking of these genes in the RF and SVM models is presented in Table S3. Lasso regression identified four genes (Fig.  A). The top 10 genes ranked by feature importance in the RF and the top 10 genes with the lowest average ranks in the SVM were intersected with the four Lasso-selected genes, resulting in three common hub MDRGs: ALAS1, HSP27 (also known as HSPB1), and VPS35 (Fig.  B). Furthermore, ROC curves were employed to assess the diagnostic value of three hub genes in OP. The findings demonstrated AUC values of 0.64, 0.71, and 0.79 for ALAS1, HSPB1, and VPS35, respectively (Fig.  C–E). These indicate that VPS35 exhibits good diagnostic performance, while HSPB1 and ALAS1 show modest ability in differentiating OP from normal samples.
 To further explore the role of hub MDRGs in OP, single-cell transcription profiling of OP was analyzed based on the GSE147287 cohort. Using tSNE clustering analysis, 18 cell clusters were acquired (Figure S1A), and 10 cell subpopulations were annotated by SingleR package, including monocyte, granulocyte–macrophage progenitors (GMP), macrophage, B-cell, pre-B-cell CD34−, myelocyte, tissue stem cell, T cells, pro-myelocyte, and erythroblast (Figure S1B). The count and weight of interactions among 10 cell types are shown in Figure S1C, D. The results indicated that the interaction between tissue stem cells and other cells was the strongest, followed by GMP and monocytes. Further analysis was conducted to determine the distribution of various cell types in specimens of OP and OA. Figure S1E illustrates the cell distribution in both sample types. We utilized pseudo-time analysis to further investigate the differentiation trajectory of human vertebral bone tissue derived from mesenchymal stem cells. We observed that tissue stem cells primarily accumulated in the anterior branch and gradually differentiated into pre-B-cell CD34−, macrophage, pro-myelocyte, and erythroblast. Subsequently, myelocytes, T cells, and B cells began to appear, ultimately dominated by GMP and monocytes (Figure S1F). The distribution of hub genes in these cells was investigated. Figure S2A reveals the expression of 3 hub genes in 18 cell groups. As shown in Fig. S2B–D, the 3 hub genes were mostly distributed in tissue stem cells, GMP, and monocytes.
Given the significance of immune cells observed in the above scRNA-seq analysis in OP progression, we further analyzed the immune cell infiltration analysis based on the microarray data set GSE56815 to explore specific roles of immune cells in OP. The fraction of 5 immune cells (basophils, conventional dendritic cell (cDC), macrophage M1, mast cells, and Th1 cells) significantly differed in the OP and normal samples (p < 0.05, Figure S3A). Basophils, macrophage M1, and mast cells were reduced, while cDC and Th1 cells were increased in the OP samples compared to the normal cells (Figure S3A). Moreover, to explore effective therapeutic drugs for OP patients, the oncoPredict package was used. As shown in Figure S3B, 9 drugs were significantly related to OP, including Camptothecin_1003, GSK2578215A_1927, lbrutinib_1799, OF_1_1853, TAF1_5496_1732, ML322_1629, LGK974_1598, AZD6482_2169, and GSK1904529A_1093.

## Molecular mechanism of OP based on hub MDRGs
Subsequently, GSEA was performed to investigate the potential function of hub genes in OP (Table S4). The findings showed that ALAS1 was positively associated with EGF/EGFR-actin, TLR1/2/4-NFκB, and TNF-NFκB signaling pathways (Fig.  A). Meanwhile, HSPB1 was positively related to CXCR–GNB-G–PI3K/AKT, GF–RTK–RAS–ERK, and RTK–PLCG signaling pathways (Fig.  B). The VPS35 was negatively linked to CXCR4–GNB-G–PLCB–PKC, GF–RTK–RAS–ERK, and GF–RTK–RAS–PI3K signaling pathways (Fig.  C).
 Considering that gene expression is finely regulated by noncoding RNAs, we further hypothesized that hub MDRGs might be modulated through a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network. Therefore, to explore the upstream post-transcriptional regulation of these genes, we constructed an integrated lncRNA–miRNA–hub gene regulatory network. Using TargetScan, 13 miRNAs targeting 3 hub genes were mined, and 18 lncRNAs targeting these miRNAs were obtained from LncACTdb (Table S5). The lncRNA–miRNA–hub gene regulatory network is shown in Fig.  .
 

## Validation of VPS35 expression in vivo and in vitro
According to the diagnostic model results, VPS35 demonstrated the optimal diagnostic value (AUC = 0.79). Therefore, we further validated its expression in the experiment. Immunofluorescence staining results showed a markedly enhanced VPS35 signal in the OVX group compared with the Sham group (p < 0.001, Fig.  ), suggesting an upregulation of VPS35 expression in osteoporotic bone tissues.
 To confirm these findings in vitro, an OIM using hBMSCs was established. As shown in Fig.  A–C, cell viability and the expression levels of osteogenic markers including OCN, OPN, and RUNX2 were significantly increased in the OIM group compared with the Normal group (p < 0.001), confirming the successful induction of osteogenic differentiation. Treatment with dexamethasone (OIM + Dex) notably suppressed cell viability and the expression of these osteogenic markers (p < 0.001), indicating its inhibitory effect on osteoblast differentiation. Therefore, the OIM + Dex group was used to mimic the osteoporotic condition in vitro, as glucocorticoid exposure induces bone loss and impairs osteogenic activity.
 In addition, both mRNA and protein levels of VPS35 were significantly increased in the OIM + Dex group compared to the OIM group (p < 0.001, Fig.  D). Moreover, activation of ERK and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways was also observed in the OIM groups, as indicated by elevated phosphorylation levels of ERK and AKT (p < 0.001, Fig.  E). However, levels of p-ERK/ERK and p-AKT/AKT were decreased in the OIM + Dex groups (p < 0.001). These findings suggest that VPS35 expression is positively associated with osteoporotic conditions and may be involved in Dex-induced regulation of osteogenic activity through the ERK/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

## VPS35 inhibits osteogenic differentiation by suppressing the ERK/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway
To elucidate the functional role of VPS35 in osteogenic differentiation, hBMDCs in OIM + Dex were transfected with oe-VPS35 or si-VPS35. As shown in Fig.  A, B, VPS35 expression was markedly increased in the oe-VPS35 group and significantly decreased in the si-VPS35 group (p < 0.001), confirming successful transfection.
 CCK-8 analysis revealed that VPS35 overexpression significantly reduced the proliferation of hBMDCs, whereas VPS35 knockdown promoted cell growth (p < 0.001, Fig.  C). Consistently, the mRNA and protein expression levels of OCN, OPN, and RUNX2 were markedly decreased in the oe-VPS35 group but upregulated after VPS35 knockdown (p < 0.001, Fig.  D, E), indicating that VPS35 negatively regulates osteoblast differentiation. Moreover, the phosphorylation levels of ERK and AKT were reduced in VPS35-overexpressing cells and increased upon VPS35 knockdown (p < 0.01, Fig.  F), suggesting that VPS35 inhibits the ERK/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.
Subsequently, feedback experiments were conducted by treating VPS35 overexpressed cells with ERK agonists. As shown in Fig.  A, ERK activation significantly restored the phosphorylation of ERK in oe-VPS35-transfected cells (p < 0.001), while p-AKT/AKT showed no significant differences. Furthermore, treatment with ERK agonists rescued the expression of osteogenic proteins OCN, OPN, and RUNX2 in oe-VPS35-transfected cells (p < 0.001, Fig.  B). These findings collectively demonstrate that VPS35 suppresses osteoblast proliferation and differentiation by inhibiting the ERK/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.
 

## VPS35 is directly targeted by miR-142-5p in osteogenic differentiation
The above bioinformatics analysis predicted a regulatory interaction between miR-142-5p and VPS35, and previous studies have reported that miR-142-5p participates in osteogenic differentiation. To verify this potential regulatory relationship, a dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed. As shown in Fig.  A, transfection of miR-142-5p mimics significantly reduced luciferase activity in the VPS35–WT reporter group, whereas no significant change was observed in the VPS35–MUT group, confirming that VPS35 is a direct target of miR-142-5p.
 Subsequently, hBMDCs cultured under OIM + Dex conditions were treated with miR-142-5p mimics or miR-142-5p inhibitor. qRT–PCR results showed that miR-142-5p expression was markedly increased by the mimics and decreased by the inhibitor (p < 0.01, Fig.  B), indicating effective transfection. Consistently, miR-142-5p mimics suppressed VPS35 expression, whereas miR-142-5p inhibitor upregulated VPS35 levels (p < 0.01, Fig.  C). Functionally, miR-142-5p mimics enhanced the expression of osteogenic markers OCN, OPN, and RUNX2, while opposite effects were observed following miR-142-5p inhibition (p < 0.001, Fig.  C). These findings suggest that miR-142-5p promotes osteogenic differentiation by downregulating VPS35 expression.

## Discussion
OP is a chronic metabolic bone disease, with the incidence of osteoporotic fractures being over three times higher than that of cancer or cardiovascular diseases, posing a significant threat to both patients and global health [ ]. Recent research suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction is associated with the occurrence and progression of OP. Mitochondrial dysfunction leads to the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), inducing damage to macromolecules such as nucleic acids and proteins within cells [ , ]. In addition, oxidative stress reduces the osteogenic potential of osteoblasts and increases the activity of osteoclasts [ ]. Targeting mitochondrial dysfunction is a potential approach for treating OP. In this study, we conducted an initial exploratory analysis to investigate the potential involvement of MDRGs in OP. Through integrated analyses of transcriptomic data, we identified 12 MDRGs showing potential relevance in OP. Among them, ALAS1, HSPB1, and VPS35 emerged as hub MDRGs.
ALAS1 is a highly conserved mitochondrial enzyme that catalyzes the condensation of glycine and succinyl coenzyme A to form 5-aminolevulinic acid [ ]. It is the rate-limiting enzyme in the heme biosynthetic pathway, regulating the first step of heme production [ ]. To date, there is a lack of research directly examining the role of ALAS1 in OP. However, heme is an essential molecule for almost all organisms, and excessive heme can lead to the generation of ROS, causing oxidative stress within cells [ ]. A previous study suggested a relationship between heme and BMD changes [ ]. In addition, increased expression of heme oxygenase-1, which breaks down heme, can reduce intracellular ROS levels and alleviate bone loss in OVX mice models [ ]. scRNA-seq analysis revealed the expression of ALAS1 in the vast majority of annotated cells. Thus, the potential role of ALAS1 in OP may be associated with heme homeostasis, although further validation is required.
HSPB1 is a stress-inducible chaperone protein that exerts cytoprotective effects by inhibiting apoptosis and autophagy processes [ ]. It is implicated in the pathogenesis of OP, as a preliminary study suggested a significant increase in HSPB1 levels in postmenopausal women with low BMD [ ]. Bhavna et al. found that increased phosphorylation of HSPB1 protein may promote osteoclastogenesis and subsequently lead to decreased BMD by enhancing monocyte migration to the bone microenvironment [ ]. Our research observed abundant expression of HSPB1 in monocytes and GMPs. Importantly, we found a significant correlation between HSPB1 and the RTK–RAS–ERK pathway, which has previously been shown to be associated with osteogenic differentiation in bone marrow stromal progenitor cells [ ]. These findings provide preliminary indications that HSPB1 may influence bone remodeling, but the exact mechanisms warrant further investigation.
VPS35 is a retrograde transport protein that is predominant in cargo selection for retrograde complexes [ ]. It coordinates the selective removal of mitochondrial DNA along with mitochondrial membrane proteins [ ]. Compared to the other two hub genes, VPS35 has been more extensively studied in OP. Previous research indicates a crucial role for VPS35 in bone remodeling. VPS35 is highly expressed in osteoclasts and osteoblasts, and its dysfunction increases osteoclast overactivity [ ]. VPS35 mutation in model mice leads to decreased bone formation and functional impairment resembling OP [ ]. Several studies suggest VPS35 as a diagnostic marker for OP [ , ], supported by our research, which found VPS35 to have good diagnostic value for OP patients, with the highest AUC value among the three hub genes at 0.79, determined through ROC analysis. Therefore, we further identified its expression in OVX mouse tissues. Results showed significantly upregulated expression of VPS35 in the model group compared to the sham group. In the in vitro experiments, we found that VPS35 overexpression significantly inhibited the viability of hBMSCs and the expression of osteogenic markers (OCN, OPN, and RUNX2), whereas VPS35 knockdown produced the opposite effect. These results indicate that VPS35 may function as a negative regulator of osteogenic differentiation, further supporting its pivotal role in the pathogenesis of OP.
Notably, VPS35 appears to be molecularly linked to the ERK/PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Previous studies have shown that VPS35 can steer RAS through the cytosol [ ], and VPS35 overexpression has been proven to down-regulate levels of ERK [ ]. Consistent with these findings, this study demonstrated that VPS35 overexpression significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of ERK and AKT, whereas treatment with an ERK agonist reversed the inhibitory effects of VPS35 on osteogenic differentiation. These observations provide preliminary evidence that VPS35 may suppress osteogenic differentiation through modulation of ERK-dependent signaling.
Building on these findings, we further explored the upstream regulatory mechanisms of VPS35. The ceRNA network analysis identified several potential miRNAs targeting VPS35, among which miR-142-5p attracted particular attention due to its reported involvement in bone formation. However, the functional role of miR-142-5p in osteogenesis remains controversial. Some studies have reported that miR-142-5p in BMSCs may promote OP [ ]. Du et al. further reported that miR-142-5p can inhibit osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs by targeting Lhx8 [ ]. Conversely, other reports indicated that miR-142-5p promotes osteogenesis and prevents OP by upregulating BMP2 [ , ]. In this study, miR-142-5p was shown to directly target VPS35. Functional experiments further demonstrated that miR-142-5p mimics downregulated VPS35 expression and upregulated osteogenic markers, whereas inhibition of miR-142-5p produced the opposite effects. Collectively, these preliminary observations suggest a potential miR-142-5p/VPS35/ERK–PI3K/AKT signaling axis. VPS35 functions as a downstream effector of miR-142-5p, participating in the regulatory network of bone formation and providing new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying OP.
Despite a series of meaningful findings, this study has several limitations. First, the scRNA-seq analysis included only one OP specimen, providing only preliminary observations of cellular heterogeneity. In addition, the DEG analysis was derived from a single mid-sized transcriptomic data set with modest transcriptional differences, and therefore, the robustness of downstream results requires validation in larger and independent cohorts. Second, most of the functional experiments in this study were conducted in vitro using hBMSCs. Although the results clearly demonstrated that VPS35 inhibits osteogenic differentiation, further in vivo experiments are required to confirm its physiological role in bone remodeling and OP progression.

## Conclusion
This study provides an initial exploratory analysis of MDRGs in OP by integrating transcriptomic analysis with machine learning. VPS35, ALAS1, and HSPB1 were identified as hub MDRGs with potential relevance to OP pathophysiology. Among them, VPS35 was the key negative regulator of osteogenic differentiation. VPS35 overexpression significantly suppressed hBMSC proliferation and osteogenic marker expression, while VPS35 knockdown had the opposite effect. Mechanistically, VPS35 is directly targeted by miR-142-5p and forms a potential miR-142-5p/VPS35/ERK–PI3K/AKT regulatory axis that promotes osteogenic differentiation. Overall, these findings provide preliminary insights into possible links between mitochondrial regulation and bone formation, and may help to generate hypotheses for future mechanistic and translational studies on OP.
