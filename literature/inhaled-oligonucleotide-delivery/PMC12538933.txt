## Abstract
The global morbidity and mortality associated with viral diseases pose a major threat to public health security and cause significant economic losses worldwide. Developing novel prophylactic and therapeutic interventions remains an urgent priority in contemporary virology research. Immunotherapy, initially developed for cancer treatment, has shown satisfactory efficacy in the management of viral infections. However, the clinical application of immunotherapy is still constrained by its inherent limitations, including poor stability, inadequate targeting ability, and systemic toxicity. Nanocarriers have emerged as a promising platform to address these challenges, with features such as protecting active substances from enzymatic degradation, delivering active substances specifically to the site of infection via ligand modification, and controlling the release behavior of active substances so as to maintain their controlled and therapeutic concentrations. Therefore, the combination of immunotherapy and nanocarriers is expected to overcome the shortcomings of immunotherapy and significantly improve their therapeutic efficacy. In this review, the classification, application, and combination of immunotherapy with nanocarriers in viral diseases are summarized. The challenges and the future prospects of this combination are also discussed.
Keywords: Immunotherapy, Nanocarriers, Viral diseases, Vaccine, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, T-cell immunotherapy

Mechanisms of actions of three mainly immunotherapies: vaccines, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and cell therapy. The mechanism of vaccine action is that after vaccines containing antigen enters into the body, B cells recognize the antigen, differentiate into plasma cells, and produce specific antibodies, who neutralize the pathogen and prevent it from invading the cell. ## Introduction
The emergence of viral infections poses a major challenge to global health issue ( ; ). Since the 20th century, various viral outbreaks, including human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), Ebola, and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), have caused widespread epidemics, infected millions of people annually, and lead to increased rates of illness and death ( ). In the case of acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), for example, there were more than 40 million people living with HIV globally by the end of 2024, with 1.3 million new infections and 630,000 deaths from AIDS-related illnesses in 2024 ( ).
Current treatment paradigms for viral infections predominantly rely on antiviral drugs that target viral gene transcription and replication ( ). Despite the verified efficacy in reducing incidence and morbidity, these traditional antiviral agents face critical limitations, that is, incomplete eradication due to viral genetic diversity and rapid mutations, drug resistance, and systemic toxicity (e.g., nephrotoxicity, myelosuppression) ( ; ). In contrast, immunotherapy represents a transformative approach by leveraging host immune mechanisms, and thereby immunotherapy displays distinct advantages including pathogen-specific targeting through adaptive immune activation and durable immune memory for long-term protection ( ). Superior safety profiles of immunotherapy is also verified compared to traditional antiviral drugs. However, clinical deployment of immunotherapy are still constrained by suboptimal response rates in immunosuppressive microenvironments, systemic immunotoxicity (e.g., cytokine release syndrome (CRS)), and logistical barriers (e.g., cold-chain dependency for biologics) ( ). Therefore, it is necessary to overcome these limitations by enhancing both the precision and practical applicability of the immunotherapy.
Nanocarriers, which represent a groundbreaking technology comprising nano-sized delivery systems such as lipid-based, polymeric, and inorganic particles, have demonstrated considerable potential in biomedical applications. They enhance drug stability, prolong circulation time, improve localized targeting, and consequently increase drug safety ( ). These systems enable precise delivery and controlled release of immunomodulatory agents, thereby addressing key limitations associated with conventional immunotherapy ( ). Several nanocarrier-based immunotherapies—including clinically approved lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-encapsulated mRNA vaccines—have exhibited significant therapeutic efficacy and favorable safety profiles. Innovative delivery platforms, such as inhaled LNPs utilizing charge-assisted stabilization technology, further highlight these advances. Preclinical and clinical studies have confirmed their ability to enhance immunotherapeutic outcomes. Thus, the integration of immunotherapy with nanocarriers effectively mitigates challenges inherent to monotherapeutic modalities—including drug resistance and inefficient delivery—thereby establishing a transformative platform for eradicating persistent viral infections.
In summary, this review highlights recent advances in immunotherapy and nanocarriers, with a focus on their combined application for treating viral diseases. It also discusses the associated challenges and future prospects of this integrated approach.

2.

## Immunotherapy
2.1.

## Overview of immunotherapy
Immunotherapy is a therapeutic strategy that effectively manages various diseases by modulating the activity of the immune system ( ). A hallmark of this approach is its ability to establish long-lasting immune memory, which helps sustain therapeutic efficacy while maintaining immune homeostasis ( ). The approval of ipilimumab for advanced melanoma ushered in the era of checkpoint blockade, catalyzing the rapid advancement of cancer immunotherapy ( ). Recent developments, such as Sinopharm's inactivated COVID-19 vaccine (WIBP-CorV), have further demonstrated potent and durable immune responses ( ). In addition, ICVAX, a therapeutic PD-1-based DNA vaccine against HIV-1 developed by Medchem Bio Group, has demonstrated the capacity to elicit broad and multifunctional T-cell responses across multiple viral subtypes. Phase I clinical trial results indicate that the vaccine exhibits an excellent safety profile and promising immunogenicity ( ). Recent reports indicate that Medchem Bio Group intends to utilize the WHO-prequalified PharmaJet Tropis® device for needle-free delivery of ICVAX. This initiative not only advances needle-free vaccination technology but also significantly accelerates the global commercialization process of the vaccine, thereby enhancing its accessibility and distribution potential. Overall, immunotherapy demonstrates a favorable safety profile, as its adverse effects typically originate from dysregulated immune activation rather than external toxic agents ( ). Consequently, this intrinsic safety, coupled with its ability to circumvent common pathogen resistance mechanisms, positions it as a highly promising modality for treating viral diseases.

2.2.

## Classification of immunotherapy
Immunotherapy is commonly categorized into active and passive modalities. Active immunotherapy aims to stimulate the host's immune system to recognize and eliminate specific pathogens or abnormal cells. Its core principle relies on antigen exposure to establish long-term immune memory, thereby enabling sustained disease control or prevention ( ). Typical agents include vaccines ( ) and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) ( ). In contrast, passive immunotherapy involves the direct administration of preformed immune components—such as antibodies, cytokines, or immune cells—to provide immediate immune-mediated protection or treatment ( ). Because it bypasses endogenous immune priming, passive immunotherapy generally confers transient effects without engaging the recipient's own immune memory ( ). This review focuses primarily on vaccines, ICIs, and cell therapies due to their broader clinical applicability ( ).
 2.2.1.

## Vaccines
Vaccines confer protection by mimicking natural infections, thereby activating the host's specific immune defenses and establishing lasting immunological memory ( ). They play a vital role in global public health, saving countless lives each year through durable protection against diverse pathogens ( ). Currently, at least four types of vaccines are available ( ): attenuated or inactivated pathogen vaccines, vector-based vaccines, nucleic acid vaccines (including DNA and mRNA vaccines), and recombinant protein vaccines. These vaccine platforms have proven effective in preventing and controlling infectious diseases, and are now extensively deployed worldwide.
 The well-documented efficacy of vaccines against viral infections is largely attributable to their ability to stimulate B cells, which elicits the production of critical neutralizing antibodies (nAbs) ( ). These nAbs operate by binding viruses in the plasma, blocking entry into host cells, and promoting viral clearance ( ). Vaccines targeting influenza, SARS-CoV-2, and hepatitis viruses function through this mechanism—eliciting robust antibody and cellular immune responses to prevent infection. Through iterative improvements, modern vaccines have demonstrated enhanced safety and efficacy, reduced manufacturing costs, and diminished public health burdens ( ). Furthermore, recent advances highlight the potential of mRNA-based strategies, such as trimer-encoding mRNA vaccines for HIV ( ) and multivalent mRNA candidates targeting monkeypox virus antigens for mpox and related orthopoxviruses ( ).
Despite their success, vaccines still face several limitations that hinder broader application. Certain viruses exhibit high mutation rates, enabling immune evasion even in vaccinated individuals. Additional challenges include serious adverse reactions, suboptimal efficacy in some populations, demanding storage conditions, and high production costs ( ; ). Therefore, continued innovation and optimization of vaccine platforms are urgently needed to overcome these barriers.

2.2.2.

## Immune checkpoint inhibitors
ICs are receptors expressed on the surface of immune cells that play a crucial role in maintaining immune balance and preventing autoimmunity by finely regulating T-cell activation ( ). ICIs function by blocking these receptors, thereby reactivating immune cells—particularly T cells—and enhancing immune responses ( ) ( ). Key molecular targets of ICIs include cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), lymphocyte-activation gene 3 (LAG-3), T-cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin and ITIM domains (TIGIT), as well as the co-stimulatory molecules CD40L and CD70 ( ). These inhibitors have demonstrated efficacy across a range of diseases and represent a major advancement in immunotherapy. For instance, CTLA-4 inhibitors, such as specific monoclonal antibodies, exhibit high-affinity binding to CTLA-4, resulting in the depletion or functional suppression of regulatory T cells (Tregs), which augments the responses of T cells ( ). Similarly, PD-1 inhibitors operate through a related mechanism. As illustrated in , PD-1 blockade can activate HIV expression in latent infected CD4+ T cells by relieving the inhibition of the positive transcription elongation factor b (P-TEFb) pathway ( ). Concurrently, PD-1 inhibitors potentiate T-cell signaling by preventing the interaction between PD-1 and its ligands, CD80, CD86, and programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1). Moreover, the modulation of PD-1 signaling exerts a broad influence on fundamental cellular processes, including cytokine secretion, survival, proliferation, and differentiation, thus playing a critical role in determining cell fate ( ). Collectively, ICIs represent a promising therapeutic strategy for the control and prevention of viral diseases.
 The clinical trial of combined PD-1 and CTLA-4 blockade conducted by , established its potent anticancer effects, underscoring the clinical utility of ICIs ( ). Beyond oncology, interest has grown in repurposing ICIs for the management of viral infections. A study by Paiardini et al. demonstrated that in SIV-infected rhesus macaques, dual blockade with ipilimumab (anti-CTLA-4) and nivolumab (anti-PD-1) was more effective than single-agent therapy. Not only did it enhance T-cell activation, but it also resulted in the reactivation of a larger viral reservoir, as evidenced by viral DNA sequencing in the blood ( ). Despite their potential, ICIs can cause immune-related adverse events (irAEs) due to excessive immune activation. Colitis is a frequently observed severe irAE, characterized by T-cell-mediated tissue damage and aberrant inflammatory cytokine production, which collectively contribute to impaired colonic epithelial barrier function ( ). Recent studies report that ICIs, particularly in combination regimens, can enhance HBV-specific T-cell responses ( ) and prevent HBV reactivation ( ). Furthermore, ongoing investigations explore the potential of ICIs to achieve sustained viral suppression or even a functional cure ( ).

2.2.3.

## T-cell immunotherapy
T-cell immunotherapy involves the genetic modification of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) ex vivo to express pathogen- or antigen-specific receptors, such as chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) or engineered T-cell receptors (TCRs). These reprogrammed cells are subsequently infused back into patients to target and eliminate viral infections ( ). Recent advances have extended this approach to chronic viral infections including hepatitis B virus (HBV) and COVID-19, utilizing NS3/HBV core-specific CARs and spike protein-targeting TCRs respectively ( ).
TCRs are naturally expressed on T lymphocytes, whereas CARs are synthetic constructs that enable T cells to recognize malignant or infected cells ( ; ). Engineered TCRs maintain the native α/β heterodimeric structure and integrate into the TCR-CD3 complex to initiate signaling ( , left). In contrast, CARs are synthetic, modular receptors constructed from an antigen-binding single-chain variable fragment (scFv), a hinge region, a transmembrane domain, and intracellular costimulatory signaling domains ( , right) ( ).
 T-cell immunotherapy offers several advantages, including high antigen affinity, efficient surface expression, and potent signaling capacity ( ). Although initially developed and successfully applied in oncology—with conceptual roots in T-cell therapies for HIV ( )—this strategy is now being harnessed against infectious diseases such as hepatitis B, AIDS, COVID-19, and influenza A.
Despite its therapeutic potential, T-cell immunotherapy faces several challenges, including limited persistence of engineered cells, risk of antigen escape, and high operational costs.

2.3.

## Application and limitation of immunotherapy in viral disease
2.3.1.

## Application of vaccines
Vaccines have a long-standing history in the prevention and control of viral infections, with remarkable success against diseases such as smallpox, influenza, and pneumonia ( ). Currently, multiple vaccine platforms—including inactivated, recombinant subunit, viral vector, mRNA, and DNA vaccines—are being utilized or tested for diseases including COVID-19, AIDS, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C.
Inactivated vaccines are manufactured through processes involving viral propagation, chemical inactivation, and purification. These steps abolish infectivity and pathogenicity while retaining structural epitopes essential for immunogenicity ( ). For example, the inactivated COVID-19 vaccine BBIBP-CorV (trade name Zhong Ai Ke Wei) demonstrated 78.1 % efficacy against symptomatic infection and 78.7 % against hospitalization in clinical trials ( ). Similarly, Covaxin, developed by Bharat Biotech, showed 77.8 % protection against symptomatic disease ( ). Although inactivated vaccines are stable and facilitate convenient storage, concerns remain regarding residual infectivity and reduced efficacy against emerging variants ( ). Another application includes plasma-derived hepatitis B vaccines, produced by purifying and inactivating subviral particles from chronic carriers—a method limited by cost and biosafety considerations ( ; ).
Recombinant subunit vaccines rely on antigenic proteins expressed in heterologous systems. Although development is often protracted ( ), subunit vaccines remain a pillar of preventive medicine. This is exemplified by the recombinant hepatitis B vaccine, which has become the most widely used platform against HBV since its introduction in 1986 ( ). Candidate vaccines for HCV that incorporate recombinant envelope glycoproteins gpE1 and gpE2 are currently undergoing clinical evaluation. These candidates have demonstrated the ability to induce both neutralizing antibodies and CD4+ T-cell responses ( ). A key limitation of subunit vaccines is their inherently weak immunogenicity, which frequently necessitates the use of adjuvants to potentiate immune responses and maintain antigen conformation ( ). The recombinant nanoparticle vaccine NVX-CoV2373 (Novavax), which incorporates the full-length spike glycoprotein and Matrix-M adjuvant, conferred 89.7 % protection against COVID-19, albeit with reported adverse effects including fatigue, headache, and myalgia ( ). Another candidate, V-01, developed by Livzon Biologics, exhibited favorable safety and potent humoral immunity as a heterologous booster ( ). Overall, recombinant subunit vaccines are associated with high safety and purity.
Viral vector vaccines utilize replication-deficient or attenuated viruses to deliver transgenes encoding target antigens. These platforms enable high-efficiency gene delivery, facilitate endogenous antigen presentation, and elicit both major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-I-restricted cytotoxic T-cell responses and neutralizing antibodies ( ). Clinical studies of Ad26.COV2·S (Johnson & Johnson's recombinant adenovirus type 26 vector encoding the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein) demonstrated that a single dose provided 52.9 % efficacy against moderate to severe/critical COVID-19. Moreover, robust protection was observed against critical disease, COVID-19-related medical interventions, and death, with effects lasting at least six months ( ). When evaluated for HCV, a prime-boost regimen based on a chimpanzee adenovirus vector (encoding NS3-NS5) and an MVA booster was shown to generate high-frequency, virus-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in volunteers ( ) An Ad26-based HIV-1 vaccine showed promising safety and immunogenicity in rhesus macaques, though translational relevance to humans remains under investigation ( ). Despite their potential, viral vector vaccines require stringent production standards and are associated with potential risks. Notably, reports of thrombotic events associated with AZD1222 (AstraZeneca) prompted temporary suspensions in several regions ( ).
mRNA vaccines function by delivering synthetic, codon-optimized mRNA sequences via LNPs, which enable cellular entry, endosomal escape, and ribosomal translation of the encoded antigen, thereby eliciting robust humoral and cellular immune responses ( ). The COVID-19 mRNA vaccines BNT162b2 (Pfizer/BioNTech) and mRNA-1273 (Moderna) both achieved >90 % efficacy in preventing infection ( ). mRNA technology is being actively applied to HIV vaccine development. For instance, Moderna's candidate, eOD-GT8 60mer (adjuvanted with AS01B), has shown promising results by eliciting broad neutralizing antibodies in 97 % of recipients, demonstrating favorable immunogenicity and safety ( ; ). Broader application of mRNA vaccines remains constrained by stability issues and demanding cold-chain requirements.
DNA vaccines introduce plasmid DNA encoding pathogen-derived antigens into host cells, enabling nuclear uptake, transcription, and antigen presentation via both MHC-I and MHC-II pathways ( ). The ZyCoV-D DNA vaccine (Zydus Cadila) showed promising safety and immunogenicity in early-phase trials and 66.6 % efficacy in a Phase III clinical study against COVID-19 ( ; ). DNA vaccines offer advantages in stability, production simplicity, and storage. However, challenges remain in improving delivery efficiency and addressing potential autoimmunity risks.
In summary, vaccines have served as a cornerstone in controlling viral infectious diseases, achieving notable success against COVID-19 and HBV. Continued research is imperative to overcome challenges like the genetic diversity of HIV and HCV. The promising candidates currently in development affirm a positive outlook for the future.

2.3.2.

## Application of immune checkpoint inhibitors
ICIs, particularly those targeting CTLA-4 and PD-1/PD-L1, have transformed cancer treatment by augmenting the immune system's ability to target tumors. To date, more than 20 ICIs have been approved for various malignancies ( ). In recent years, their application has expanded to viral infections, including hepatitis B and AIDS, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for chronic viral diseases.
Anti-CTLA-4 antibodies bind with high affinity to CTLA-4, leading to either depletion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) or functional inhibition, thereby enhancing T-cell activation and antiviral immunity ( ). For instance, ipilimumab—an anti-CTLA-4 antibody—was administered to HIV-positive patients with metastatic melanoma who were on antiretroviral therapy. Treatment resulted in increased unspliced HIV RNA within cells and reduced plasma HIV RNA, indicating that CTLA-4 blockade may suppress HIV replication ( ). Similarly, in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma and HCV infection, tremelimumab (another CTLA-4 inhibitor) significantly reduced viral load without serious immune-related adverse events, supporting its potential anti-HCV activity ( ).
PD-1/PD-L1 interaction delivers inhibitory signals that dampen T-cell function. Blocking this pathway can partially restore T-cell activity and sustain immune responses ( ). A study using a single low dose of the anti-PD-L1 antibody BMS-936559 in HIV-infected individuals showed no significant change in viral RNA or DNA levels after 28 days ( ). However, repeated administration of avelumab (anti-PD-L1) in SIV-infected macaques led to transient viral control, suggesting that higher and more frequent dosing may be necessary for effective HIV suppression ( ). Beyond HIV, in chronic hepatitis B (CHB), entecavir was found to upregulate PD-L1 on CD8+ T cells. Combining entecavir with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade enhanced virus-specific T-cell function in rats, promoting HBV clearance and seroconversion while reducing relapse risk and treatment costs ( ).
In summary, ICIs demonstrate considerable potential in the treatment of viral diseases. However, monotherapy with individual inhibitors often yields limited efficacy, prompting exploration of combination regimens ( ). Although combined ICIs may improve outcomes, they are also associated with increased toxicity ( ), underscoring the need for careful clinical management.

2.3.3.

## Application of T-cell immunotherapy
CAR-T cells are engineered to express CARs, enabling them to recognize diverse antigens and exert therapeutic effects without the need for repeated T-cell modification ( ). A series of broad neutralizing antibody (bNAb)-derived CARs—including 10E8, 3BNC117, PG9, PGT126, PGT128, VRC01, and X5—have been evaluated and shown to confer HIV-1-specific functional activity, enhance T-cell proliferation, and suppress viral replication ( ). Further studies confirmed that bNAb-based CAR-T cells targeting HIV-1 effectively inhibit viral replication and reduce virus-producing cells both in vitro and in vivo ( ). In a clinical study involving 14 antiretroviral-treated patients, third-generation HIV-1-specific CAR-T cells were developed by fusing single-chain variable fragments (scFvs) to intracellular signaling domains. The treatment was well-tolerated and imposed selective pressure on the HIV-1 reservoir, underscoring the clinical safety of this approach ( ). Additionally, a bimolecular CAR architecture (duoCAR) designed to target multiple epitopes on HIV envelope glycoproteins demonstrated potent antiviral activity, reducing viral infection by up to 99 % in vitro and 97 % in vivo when delivered via lentiviral vectors ( ; ). These findings highlight the considerable potential of CAR-T immunotherapy for HIV treatment.
By enabling T cells to recognize specific peptide–human leukocyte antigen (pHLA) complexes, TCRs have emerged as promising therapeutic tools for a range of diseases ( ). This TCR-T cell therapy is an adoptive immunotherapeutic approach that involves genetically modifying a patient's own T cells to express TCRs capable of specifically recognizing target antigens, thereby enhancing their antigen-specific cytotoxic activity. In the first clinical trial of TCR-T cells targeting HBV antigens for hepatocellular carcinoma (NCT03899415), most patients exhibited reduced or stabilized levels of HBsAg and HBV DNA, without grade 3 or higher adverse events reported, supporting the favorable safety profile of this strategy ( ). Similarly, SCG101, an autologous TCR-T product developed by SCG Biotech (previously Handel Biologics), exhibited notable antiviral and antitumor activities ( ). Despite these advances, TCR-T therapy remains constrained by risks of off-target effects and toxicity ( ). CAR-T and TCR-T cell therapies are also under investigation for COVID-19, with the goal of engineering T cells to recognize and eliminate coronavirus-infected cells. However, these therapeutic strategies are still in preliminary stages of development ( ; ).
Consequently, T-cell-based therapies hold considerable promise for treating viral diseases, notably against persistent infections such as HIV and HBV, as well as virus-associated malignancies.

## Nanocarriers
Nanocarriers are nano-dispersed systems composed of various materials that can be loaded with drugs or other active compounds for targeted delivery to specific therapeutic sites ( ). Typically ranging in size from 10 to 100 nm, these carriers exhibit enhanced capacity to penetrate biological barriers, including biofilms ( ). Several nanocarrier-based therapeutics have achieved clinical approval and commercial success. Notable examples include amphotericin B liposome (AmBisome®), the first nanodrug formulation approved in 1990 ( ); albumin-bound paclitaxel nanoparticles, a self-assembled nanodrug launched in 2005 ( ); and Onpattro®, the first siRNA therapeutic using cationic liposomes, approved in 2018 ( ).
Based on their core material composition, nanocarriers can be broadly classified into organic and inorganic categories. Among organic nanocarriers, lipid-based and polymeric systems are the most widely studied and clinically advanced. The properties and applications of these nanocarriers—specifically lipid-based, polymeric, and inorganic types—are summarized in , , .
 3.1.

## Lipid-based nanocarriers
Lipid-based nanocarriers are nanoparticles primarily composed of physiologically compatible lipids, which contribute to the low acute and chronic toxicity ( ). These systems are capable of encapsulating both hydrophilic and lipophilic drugs, thereby improving drug stability and bioavailability ( ). Through surface modification with hydrophilic polymers (e.g., PEG) and targeted ligands, lipid-based nanocarriers can extend their in vivo circulation time, enhance site-specific delivery, and reduce off-target toxicity and side effects ( ). Several lipid-based nanocarrier formulations have received clinical approval and achieved commercial success, including PEGylated liposomal doxorubicin (Doxil®) ( ) and PEGylated liposomal adriamycin ( ).
Based on their structural and functional characteristics, lipid-based nanocarriers can be categorized into several types, such as liposomes ( ), solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) ( ), nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) ( ), and lipid nanocapsules (LNCs) ( ). An overview of these commonly used lipid-based nanocarriers is provided in .

3.2.

## Polymer nanocarriers
Polymeric nanocarriers are nanoscale particles composed of natural or synthetic polymers, designed to encapsulate and deliver therapeutic agents ( ). They are capable of loading both hydrophilic and hydrophobic molecules, and offer tunable chemical and physical properties that enhance drug stability and prevent degradation ( ). The successful commercialization of these carriers is demonstrated by various formulations. Examples include sevelamer carbonate/hydrochloride tablets for hyperphosphatemia management ( ) and injectable paclitaxel-loaded polymeric micelles for treating non-small cell lung carcinoma ( ).
Based on polymer origin, polymeric nanocarriers can be classified into natural, synthetic, and semi-synthetic categories. This section reviews commonly used natural and synthetic polymer nanocarriers, including those based on chitosan and its derivatives ( ), albumin nanoparticles ( ), DNA nanostructures ( ), dendritic polymers ( ), peptide-based systems ( ), and poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles ( ). A summary of these systems is presented in .

3.3.

## Inorganic nanocarriers
Inorganic nanocarriers are drug delivery systems composed of inorganic materials, offering unique advantages for therapeutic applications ( ). Their versatility allows for precise customization of morphology, size, surface functionalization, and physical properties, enabling the rational design of tailored nanomedicines ( ). These systems have garnered significant attention for their potential in integrated diagnosis and therapy. Representative examples include a monodisperse mesoporous silica-coated superparamagnetic iron oxide platform (DOX@MMSN-SS-PEI-cit) used for cancer theranostics ( ), and doxorubicin/siRNA Bcl2-loaded gold nanoparticles (Dox-Bcl2-AuNPs) with antitumor efficacy ( ). Given their promising performance and multifunctionality, inorganic nanocarriers represent a highly promising area for continued research and development. 4.

## Combined application of immunotherapy and nanocarriers
Immunotherapy has been widely applied in disease prevention and treatment with substantial clinical benefits. However, its broader adoption is limited by inherent drawbacks of immunotherapeutic agents, such as structural instability, limited targeting specificity, reduced efficacy against viral mutations, systemic toxicity, and demanding storage conditions. Nanocarriers offer a promising strategy to overcome these challenges by enhancing drug solubility, shielding therapeutic compounds from degradation, controlling release kinetics, improving target specificity, reducing required doses, and minimizing adverse effects ( ; ). As a result, the integration of nanocarriers with immunotherapy is emerging as a powerful approach to amplify therapeutic outcomes and alleviate existing limitations. Currently, combination strategies—such as nanocarrier-delivered vaccines or immune checkpoint inhibitors—have been explored and demonstrate encouraging results.
4.1.

## Combined application of vaccines and nanocarriers
Compared to viral or mRNA vaccines, recombinant protein vaccines often exhibit lower immunogenicity and typically require potent adjuvants to achieve adequate preventive or therapeutic efficacy ( ). Nanocarriers can significantly enhance the adjuvant effect of vaccines through multiple mechanisms, primarily by improving antigen delivery efficiency, synergistically activating innate immune signaling pathways, and enabling spatiotemporally controlled immune activation. For instance, their tunable size and surface properties facilitate active targeting of lymph nodes and enhance dendritic cell uptake ( ); they allow co-delivery of immunostimulants to synergistically engage pattern-recognition receptors ( ); and they enable sustained antigen release through biodegradable materials (e.g., PLGA) to prolong immune stimulation ( ). These mechanisms collectively overcome the humoral bias of conventional adjuvants and promote robust cellular immunity with improved safety, offering a strategic platform for next-generation vaccine design.
To overcome the limitation of aluminum-based adjuvants, which preferentially induce Th2 over Th1 immune responses, Zhao et al. developed a nanocomposite adjuvant that integrates yam polysaccharide (CYP) with aluminum hydroxide nanoparticles (CYP-AlNPs). This system induced a balanced Th1/Th2 response in a PCV2 vaccine model by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome and TLR4–NF-κB pathways in dendritic cells, providing a rationale for adjuvant development against diseases requiring potent cellular immunity, such as AIDS and tuberculosis ( ). Deng et al. developed a nanoparticle vaccine, SCTV01B, which incorporates a chemically conjugated recombinant SARS-CoV-2 RBD, Streptococcus pneumoniae serotype 14 polysaccharide (PPS14), and a squalene-based emulsion adjuvant. The inclusion of PPS14 enables dendritic cell targeting via sugar receptors, thereby enhancing antigen internalization, processing, and presentation, which collectively substantially improved immunogenicity and antiviral efficacy ( ).
While mRNA and DNA vaccines have demonstrated high efficacy, their broad application is limited by instability, poor delivery, and suboptimal immune activation. To overcome these challenges, LNPs have been employed in COVID-19 vaccines such as BNT162b2 (Pfizer/BioNTech) and mRNA-1273 (Moderna), which incorporate modified mRNA encoding the spike glycoprotein. These LNPs protect mRNA from degradation, enhance delivery efficiency, and improve vaccine immunogenicity ( ). Furthermore, to circumvent cold-chain limitations, Voigt et al. developed a lyophilized self-amplifying mRNA (saRNA) vaccine encapsulated in NLCs, which retained immunogenicity after 6–8 months at room temperature ( ). Similarly, Moyo et al. used LNPs to deliver tetravalent HIVconsvM mRNA, inducing broad and potent T-cell responses ( ).
 Despite the success of preventive HBV vaccines, therapeutic options remain limited. Huang et al. developed ionizable lipid nanoparticles (RBP131) for the hepatocyte-specific delivery of modified siRNA. This approach achieved a significant reduction in HBV RNA, antigens (HBsAg, HBeAg), and DNA levels in both the circulation and liver tissues. These pronounced antiviral effects highlight the potential of RBP131 for treating chronic hepatitis B ( ). In another approach, a ferritin nanoparticle vaccine displaying HBV preS1 was designed to target SIGNR1+ antigen-presenting cells. This vaccine induced T-follicular helper cells, activated B cells, stimulated high-affinity and durable antibody responses, and reduced cccDNA reservoirs, demonstrating promise for functional cure strategies ( ).

4.2.

## Combined application of ICIs and nanocarriers
ICIs have demonstrated efficacy in treating a range of diseases. However, they are associated with significant toxicity and adverse effects, including severe and occasionally fatal immune-related reactions ( ). To address these limitations, researchers are increasingly turning to nanocarriers, whichoffer advantages such as enhanced tissue permeability, improved specificity, and targeted delivery of ICIs ( ).
PD-1-targeted therapies have exhibited modest antiviral effects in preclinical models of chronic viral infection. There is a pressing need for novel therapeutic strategies that amplify T cell immunity and improve viral control to counter T cell exhaustion and immunosuppression. In this context, nanoparticle-based vaccination was constructed to augment PD-1-targeted therapy in Friend retrovirus (FV) infection ( ). The results demonstrate that the combination therapy induced a synergistic reinvigoration of the exhausted virus-specific CD8 T cell immunity, and providing further information on how to improve PD-1-targeted therapies during chronic viral infection and cancer.
Recently, Virion Therapeutics reported interim Phase Ib trial results for VRON-0200—a novel checkpoint-modified immunotherapy designed to achieve a functional cure for chronic HBV. This therapy employs a heterologous chimpanzee adenovirus vector (e.g., AdC6, AdC7) to deliver selected HBV antigens (core and polymerase, excluding surface antigen) fused with herpes simplex virus glycoprotein D (gD), which acts as an early T cell checkpoint inhibitor. A single dose of VRON-0200 was shown to be safe, well-tolerated, and capable of significantly elevating T cell responses in patients with chronic HBV ( ; ).
In another study, Zhang et al. explored fibronectin (FN)-modified mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MNPs) for the targeted delivery of SIRT1 inhibitors to modulate macrophage polarization and reshape the hepatic immune microenvironment, with the goal of preventing hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) development following CHB. The FN-MNPs effectively delivered SIRT1 inhibitors and suppressed M2 macrophage polarization in vitro. In a CHB mouse model, treatment increased the hepatic M1/M2 macrophage ratio by 2.3-fold and reduced alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels by 45 %, effectively preventing the progression from hepatitis to HCC ( ).
Given the limitations of existing SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, Acúrcio et al. developed a next-generation multiepitope nanovaccine that co-delivers immunogenic peptides—selected via an immunoinformatic pipeline—along with adjuvants and regulators of PD-L1 expression ( ). This nanovaccine induced potent cellular and humoral immunity against SARS-CoV-2 at both local and systemic levels, as evidenced by immunoglobulin A (IgA) secretion and potent virus-neutralizing activity. Nanocarrier-based ICIs are also being advanced for the treatment of tumors linked to chronic viral infections. For instance, Liu et al. developed a tannic acid (TA)-based nanovaccine incorporating a engineered protein antigen for Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-associated tumors, adjuvanted with interferon-α (IFN-α) or CpG to break immune tolerance. Lymph node-targeted delivery of the CpG-adjuvanted nanovaccine provoked potent immune activation and effectively suppressed tumor formation. Furthermore, when combined with anti-PD-L1 therapy, the nanovaccine significantly reduced tumor size and extended survival in tumor-bearing mice by limiting regulatory T cell infiltration into tumors, indicating a capacity to reverse ICI resistance through remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. Thus, this study shows a promising strategy for the treatment of EBV-positive tumors in patients ( ).
In summary, the integration of nanocarrier technology with ICIs is advancing antiviral therapy toward greater precision, efficiency, and safety. Current strategies are highly diverse. They encompass targeted ICI delivery, combinations of nanovaccines with ICIs, and nanocarrier-mediated co-delivery of ICIs alongside oncolytic viruses, immunomodulators (e.g., siRNA), or antiviral agents. These multifaceted approaches are all supported by promising preclinical outcomes ( ). Although clinical translation remains limited, particularly for non-oncogenic viral infections, and most studies are still at the preclinical stage—often integrated with therapies for virus-related cancers—the potential of this approach is considerable. It offers innovative and promising avenues for combating viruses that establish chronic or latent infections and promote oncogenesis, such as EBV, HBV, HCV, HPV, and HIV.

4.3.

## Combined application of T-cell therapy and nanocarriers
In recent years, T-cell therapies, particularly CAR-T cell therapy, have achieved groundbreaking success in treating hematological malignancies, leading to approval by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) ( ). However, these novel therapies are associated with significant limitations. Current production typically relies on viral transduction, which constrains the size of deliverable genetic material and incurs high manufacturing costs ( ). In addition, toxicities such as CRS and CAR-T cell-related encephalopathy syndrome (CRES) remain major concerns ( ). CRS, driven by excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines from activated T cells, is nearly universal and commonly presents with sustained fever, myalgia, hypotension, and respiratory impairment ( ). While the mechanisms underlying CRES remain incompletely elucidated, clinical vigilance is warranted in patients presenting with symptoms such as headache, delirium, or seizures. Severe toxicities may lead to significant sequelae, including B-cell aplasia, hypogammaglobulinemia, and even death ( ). Consequently, improving safety and cost-effectiveness remains a central challenge in CAR-T immunotherapy ( ).
Precise targeting of T cells is a critical determinant of the efficacy and safety of CAR-T therapy. Nanocarriers can be engineered to incorporate various targeting biomaterials, thereby enhancing their targeting specificity and efficiency. Furthermore, chemical conjugation can be employed to improve their stability and extend shelf life ( ). Integrating nanocarriers with CAR-T therapy has shown promise in enhancing efficacy while reducing toxicity compared to monotherapy. Capstan Therapeutics has engineered targeted lipid nanoparticles (tLNPs) functionalized with anti-CD5 or anti-CD8 antibodies. These tLNPs are designed to deliver CD19 CAR mRNA specifically to CD8+ T cells in vivo, directly reprogramming them into CAR-T cells internally. This in situ approach eliminates the need for ex vivo T cell expansion required by conventional CAR-T therapy. Evaluated in non-human primate models, the strategy mediated rapid, dose-dependent B cell depletion without inducing significant hepatotoxicity or severe inflammatory responses ( ). This synergy has motivated growing research into combined nanocarriers and CAR-T strategies. For example, stimuli-sensitive cationic nanomicelles based on an mPEG-P(Asp-AED-g-HFB) block copolymer were developed to deliver the piggyBac transposon system into primary T lymphocytes, enabling sustained CAR transgene expression for up to 12 days ( ). In another study, non-classical LNPs—composed of novel PEGylated lipids (TA7AD8 LNP)—were used to produce CAR-T cells with durable CAR expression. This system successfully delivered minicircle nuclear-localizing transposons to primary human CD3+ T cells, resulting in persistent CAR expression and antitumor efficacy in xenograft models ( ). To address the issue of nonspecific organ accumulation due to lack of targeting ligands, Wang et al. developed spleen-selective organ targeting (SORT) LNPs for delivering CAR-encoding mRNA directly to T cells in a mouse model of B-cell lymphoma. These SORT LNPs facilitated in situ generation of CAR-T cells within the spleen, prolonging survival and reducing hepatic metastasis ( ).
With the aim of streamlining manufacturing and cutting costs, Hamilton et al. created membrane vesicles carrying monoclonal antibody fragments, CRISPR-Cas9, and gRNA, enabling efficient delivery of gene-editing tools ( ) ( ). This combination of T-cell therapy and nanocarriers represents a highly innovative direction in immunotherapy. By capitalizing on the advantages of nanotechnology—including targeted delivery, controlled release, multifunctional integration, and efficient gene transfer—this integrated approach shows significant potential to systematically overcome key limitations of current T-cell therapies, such as toxicity, high cost, and manufacturing complexity ( ). Zhu et al. constructed programmable nanovesicles (NVs) derived from bispecific CAR-T cells expressing two scFvs, CR3022 and B38, targeting SARS-CoV-2. These NVs co-expressing CR3022 and B38 exhibited stronger neutralization against Spike-pseudotyped virus than either scFv alone and reduced the incidence of viral resistance. Moreover, their specific binding to the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein enabled targeted delivery of remdesivir to spike-overexpressing 293 T cells, suggesting a potential strategy for enhancing antiviral efficacy and reducing adverse effects through site-specific drug delivery ( ). At the 13th IAS Scientific Meeting on HIV Science (IAS 2025), Garcia et al. demonstrated that HLA-E-restricted CD8+ T cells, primed against specific HIV epitopes, could identify and eliminate HIV-producing cells in mice, thereby marking an initial step toward reducing the latent reservoir and advancing curative strategies.
 CAR-T cell therapy has been explored for HIV (e.g., targeting CD4 or utilizing bNAbs) and HBV, yet research on viral-specific T-cell therapies remains in early stages. Nonetheless, integrating nanocarriers—with their favorable pharmacokinetic properties—is expected to overcome the limitations of standalone T-cell approaches and improve the treatment of viral diseases, with practical applications likely emerging in the near future.

5.

## Conclusions and prospective
Immunotherapy has demonstrated substantial efficacy in the treatment of viral diseases, with multiple immunotherapeutic agents already receiving clinical approval. Nevertheless, its broader application is hampered by challenges such as molecular instability, off-target effects, limited response rates, severe adverse reactions, and the emergence of drug resistance. Future developments are likely to focus on more precise immunotherapeutic strategies, including personalized neoantigen vaccines designed through AI-driven analysis, combination therapies incorporating epigenetic modulators or radiotherapy, and novel immune modulators targeting emerging checkpoints.
Nanocarrier systems provide significant advantages by protecting therapeutic agents from enzymatic degradation and enabling targeted delivery to specific tissues or cell types. The integration of immunotherapy with nanotechnology represents a promising direction, exemplified by the FDA-approved COVID-19 mRNA vaccine BNT162b2, which utilizes LNPs for effective antigen delivery. Advances in nanocarrier engineering are expected to facilitate multimodal therapeutic platforms. Examples include triple-component systems comprising antigen-loaded nanoparticles combined with sustained-release microparticles for single-administration prime-boost immunization, and xenogeneic cell membrane-coated nanoparticles that exploit cross-species immune recognition to circumvent immune evasion. Furthermore, the incorporation of advanced functional materials—such as stimuli-responsive polymers, autologous cell membranes, bacterial vectors, or nanorobots—into nanocarrier design may enhance the precision, efficacy, and safety of immunotherapies, while reducing toxicity and resistance. These innovations underscore the transformative potential of nanotechnology in immunotherapy, particularly through improved antigen presentation and targeted immune modulation. The application of AI-driven design to immunotherapy, nanocarrier development, and their combined use is anticipated to further accelerate progress. Ultimately, the future success of immunotherapy will rely on interdisciplinary collaboration across nanotechnology, artificial intelligence, and immunometabolism to develop durable and patient-tailored treatments—though challenges related to immune equilibrium and clinical translation must still be addressed.
Despite the significant benefits of nanocarrier-based immunotherapies, regulatory approval for these combination products entails navigating a multifaceted and evolving framework. Critical challenges inherent to such therapies—including precise characterization, rigorous manufacturing control, adequate nonclinical testing, and strategic clinical development—require meticulous evaluation. Moreover, particular consideration must be given to the dynamic regulatory landscape, as agencies worldwide continue to refine specialized expertise and guidelines to address the unique scientific and regulatory complexities presented by these advanced products.
