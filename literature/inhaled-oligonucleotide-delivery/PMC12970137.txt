Lifestyle interventions and pharmacotherapies have shown efficacy; however, limitations remain. The prevalence of MASLD has escalated significantly, mirroring the rising metabolic syndrome and obesity rates, which exert notable economic and social strains on public health systems.
Current evidence suggests that MASLD progression is closely associated with genetic, dietary and environmental determinants. Genetic factors, such as specific variations in the PNPLA3 and TM6SF2 genes, have been associated with an increased risk of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis. Additionally, dietary factors (eg, high-fructose and high-fat diets) and lifestyle factors (eg, obesity and physical inactivity) play crucial roles in MASLD pathogenesis. Recent studies suggest that environmental pollutants may also have a significant part in the progression as well as onset of MASLD, potentially exacerbating liver injury and fat accumulation by disrupting metabolic processes. The interplay between these factors and exposure to environmental pollutants may further exacerbate metabolic dysfunction in the liver.
Among the multitude of environmental pollutants, tris(2-butoxyethyl) phosphate (TBOEP), a widely used organophosphorus flame retardant, has seen a gradual increase in its usage in recent years. TBOEP is ubiquitous in the environment, particularly in water bodies and soil, where long-term exposure may pose potential toxic effects on organisms. The detection of TBOEP in drinking water is particularly concerning, as it signifies a direct route of human exposure via ingestion. Studies indicate that dermal exposure, dust ingestion, dietary intake and inhalation are likely the primary routes of human exposure to TBOEP. The harmful impacts of TBOEP on organisms are diverse and profound. Beyond affecting gonadal development and leading to reproductive dysfunction, the concentration of TBOEP in indoor air has also been linked to a higher risk of sick-building syndrome. Furthermore, TBOEP may disrupt the hypothalamic-pituitary axis, affecting hormone levels and leading to changes in endocrine function. Research has also revealed a strong positive association between serum TBOEP concentrations and the incidence of gestational diabetes. Chronic exposure to TBOEP increases the expression of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), thereby exacerbating inflammatory responses in the lungs. In aquatic environments, TBOEP leads to the accumulation of malondialdehyde and reactive oxygen species (ROS) in zebrafish, while a reduction in superoxide dismutase activity has been observed. In human liver cancer human hepatoma G2 (HepG2) cells, TBOEP causes cell cycle disruptions and DNA damage, along with elevated levels of ROS and calcium influx, triggering oxidative stress and proliferation inhibition. Additionally, in human cells, TBOEP may induce gene mutations and DNA/chromosome breaks; this process necessitates metabolic activation by the cytochrome (CYP) P450 enzyme system, particularly CYP1A1 and 3A4. Collectively, these outcomes highlight that TBOEP exerts multifaceted toxic effects on organisms, and its potential health risks warrant serious attention.
While prior research has investigated the effects of TBOEP across a range of organisms, its association with MASLD remains unexplored. This study uses a multidatabase analysis strategy to systematically identify and analyse the influence of TBOEP on MASLD. Additionally, it probes into its possible molecular mechanisms and biological relevance. This methodology integrates target identification, gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses, along with protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis, to furnish a thorough biological perspective. This study aims to help elucidate the potential relationship between TBOEP and the development of MASLD.

## Methods

## Target identification methodology
The potential biological targets of TBOEP were systematically identified through a multidatabase approach. Putative target proteins were predicted using three complementary computational platforms: (1) SwissTargetPrediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) employing cheminformatic similarity mapping; (2) PharmMapper (http://lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/index.html) using pharmacophore modelling; and (3) the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD; https://ctdbase.org/) through chemical-gene interaction analysis.
For MASLD-related targets, we conducted a comprehensive data mining strategy combining two authoritative biomedical resources: the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man database (OMIM; https://omim.org/) and GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/). Disease-associated genes were retrieved using the search terms ‘non-alcoholic fatty liver disease’ and ‘metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease’ with species restriction to Homo sapiens.

## Functional enrichment analysis
KEGG and GO pathway analyses were systematically conducted to characterise the biological relevance of identified targets. Computational workflows were implemented using R software (V.4.2.2) with the clusterProfiler package (V.4.5.0) through the Hiplot Pro platform (https://hiplot.com.cn/), an integrated bioinformatics toolkit for omics data visualisation and interpretation.

## PPI network analysis
Overlapping targets were submitted to the STRING database (Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes; V.11.5, https://string-db.org/) with the following parameters: (1) ‘Multiple proteins’ search mode, (2) organism restricted to Homo sapiens, (3) the threshold for the minimum required interaction score was established at a high confidence level of 0.400. To improve the biological significance of the network, nodes that were not connected were systematically removed. Cytoscape software was employed to visualise the protein interaction status. CentiscaPe V.2.2 was used to select the three conditions of degree, closeness and betweenness to obtain the key target genes.

## Molecular docking
The 3D crystal structures of the target proteins were retrieved from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics (RCSB) protein databank (http://www.rcsb.org/pdb/). Subsequently, preprocessing procedures, including the elimination of water molecules and ions, were executed using PyMOL software. Following this, Autodock software facilitated the addition of hydrogen atoms to the protein structure, the calculation of charges and the transformation of the file into pdbqt format. The compound’s sdf file was sourced from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Chem3D Pro software was used to determine the minimum energy and convert the file into mol format. Lastly, Autodock was used to convert the file into pdbqt format, and Vina was employed to conduct molecular docking. For proteins without a defined ligand-binding pocket, we used blind docking or focused our docking on known functional surfaces, such as cytokine-receptor interfaces for interleukin (IL)-6 or actin polymerisation sites for ACTB, to explore potential allosteric or PPI-disrupting interactions.

## Cell culture
The murine hepatocyte cell line alpha mouse liver 12 cells (AML12) was maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium/nutrient mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12) media supplemented with 40 ng/mL of dexamethasone, 10% foetal bovine serum and 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium. The cell suspension is plated at a 1:2 dilution and incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 environment to facilitate cellular proliferation.

## Cell viability assay
Cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 10 000 cells per well and left to incubate for 24 hours to facilitate cell adhesion. Subsequently, TBOEP (GLPBIO, GC26011) was added to the wells at varying concentrations of 0 µM, 5 µM, 10 µM, 20 µM, 40 µM, 80 µM, 160 µM, 320 µM, 640 µM and 1280 µM. Following a 48-hour exposure to TBOEP, 10 µL of CCK8 solution (Beyotime, C0037) was dispensed into each well. The plate was gently agitated to promote thorough mixing, and it was then incubated for an additional 2 hours. A microplate reader was then employed to measure absorbance (optical density) at 450 nm.

## RNA sequencing
Once the AML12 cells reached confluency, they were stimulated with 20 µM TBOEP for 48 hours. Following the extraction of total RNA from the samples, the Agilent 2100 bioanalyser was employed to evaluate the integrity of the RNA. RNA concentration was accurately determined using NanoDrop. Magnetic beads conjugated with oligo-dT were used to isolate mRNA from the total RNA, and the captured mRNA was fragmented. The first and second strands of cDNA were created using reverse transcriptase. End repair and the addition of A bases to the 3′ ends of the cDNA were performed. Subsequently, the fragments were ligated with sequencing adapters. The ligation products were purified to remove incomplete products and empty adapter dimers, followed by PCR amplification using primers complementary to the adapter sequences. The final library was purified using magnetic beads. After library construction, the concentration was measured using Qubit, and the fragment size was analysed using the Agilent Fragment Analyzer to guarantee library quality. The library was sequenced using PE150 sequencing on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform after passing quality control. The analysis of differential gene expression was conducted using the DESeq2 package within the R programming environment, where trimmed mean of M values (TMM) normalisation was implemented on the gene count data. Genes were classified as differentially expressed if they exhibited a padj value below 0.05 and an absolute log2(fold change) greater than 1.

## In vitro FFAs and TBOEP treatment model
AML12 hepatocytes were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2×105 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. To establish the steatosis model, cells were treated with a mixture of free fatty acids (FFAs) (oleic acid:palmitic acid at a 2:1 ratio) at a final concentration of 1 mM for 24 hours. To investigate the effect of TBOEP on lipid accumulation, 20 µM TBOEP was co-administered with FFAs. Cells were cultured under these induction conditions for 24 hours. Following treatment, cells were harvested for Oil Red O staining, triglyceride quantification and RNA/protein extraction.

## Oil Red O staining
AML12 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and washed with 60% isopropanol. Cells were then stained with a freshly filtered Oil Red O working solution (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at room temperature. After washing to remove excess dye, lipid droplets were visualised and imaged under a light microscope. For quantification, the stained dye was eluted with isopropanol, and the absorbance was measured at 510 nm.

## Triglyceride assay
Cellular triglyceride levels were measured using a commercial kit (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Following treatment, cells were lysed, and the supernatant was collected after centrifugation (12 000×g, 10 min, 4°C). The assay was performed by incubating the sample with the working reagent at 37°C for 10 min. The absorbance was read at 510 nm. Triglyceride (TG) concentrations were calculated from a standard curve and normalised to total protein content.

## Total RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR
Total RNA was extracted from AML12 cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). First-strand cDNA was synthesised from 1 µg of total RNA using a PrimeScript RT kit (Takara). Quantitative PCR was performed with the SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme) on a CFX96 system (Bio-Rad). The thermal cycling conditions were 95°C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s and 60°C for 30 s. A melt curve analysis confirmed product specificity. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, normalised to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). All primer sequences are listed in .

## Western blot assay
Cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer, and protein concentrations were determined by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking in 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against PPARγ, PPARα, SREBP1c, FASN, SCD1 and β-actin (all 1:1000, Abmart). This was followed by incubation with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody (1:3000) for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualised with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and quantified using ImageJ. β-actin served as the loading control.

## Small interfering RNAs transfection
To knock down gene expression, hepatocytes were transfected with specific small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) using the Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded into 6-well or 12-well plates and grown to approximately 60%–70% confluence. The siRNAs and the transfection reagent were separately diluted in Opti-MEM reduced serum medium and then mixed to form complexes. After incubating at room temperature for 15 min, the mixture was added to the cells. Small interfering RNA negative control (siNC) was used as a negative control. Transfection efficiency was assessed by qRT-PCR analysis 48 hours post-transfection. Sequences are listed in .

## Statistical analysis
GraphPad Prism V.8.0 software (GraphPad, San Diego, California, USA) was employed to examine the data. Student’s t-test was employed to assess the differences between the two groups. For comparisons among three or more groups, a one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) was applied to normally distributed data. If the ANOVA result was significant (p<0.05), a post hoc analysis was performed using Tukey’s multiple comparisons test to identify specific group differences. For non-normally distributed data with multiple groups, the Kruskal-Wallis test was used, followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. For the assessment of differentially expressed genes derived from RNA sequencing results, the DESeq2 package in R was used. For statistical significance, a threshold value of p<0.05 was set.

## Results

## Target acquisition
The chemical structure of TBOEP is shown in . Initially, 67 targets for TBOEP were obtained through the SwissTargetPrediction website, 83 targets were identified using PharmMapper and 1077 targets were retrieved from the CTD database. After merging and removing duplicates, a total of 1159 unique TBOEP targets were obtained. Additionally, 540 disease-related targets associated with MASLD were acquired from OMIM, and 1399 disease-related targets were obtained from GeneCards. On merging these two sets and removing duplicates, 1899 unique disease-related targets were identified. By comparing the predicted targets of TBOEP with the targets associated with MASLD, 154 common targets were found, suggesting that these targets might be critical to the mechanism by which TBOEP influences the progression of MASLD ( ).
 

## GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of targets
The KEGG and GO enrichment analysis revealed that the biological processes (BPs) involved in the development of MASLD influenced by TBOEP are diverse. The top five BPs include response to peptide, response to nutrient levels, response to extracellular stimulus, response to peptide hormone and cellular response to peptide ( ). The top five molecular functions (MFs) include RNA polymerase II-specific DNA-binding transcription factor binding, nuclear receptor activity, ligand-activated transcription factor activity, DNA-binding transcription factor binding and growth factor activity ( ). The top five cellular components (CCs) include secretory granule lumen, cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, vesicle lumen, collagen-containing extracellular matrix and membrane raft ( ). The top 10 KEGG pathways include insulin resistance, hypoxia-inducible factor-1 (HIF-1) signalling pathway, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, bile secretion, longevity regulating pathway, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signalling pathway, cholesterol metabolism, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) signalling pathway, alcoholic liver disease and lipid and atherosclerosis ( ).

## PPI network of key targets
A PPI network was built using the common targets of TBOEP and the disease to visualise the interactions between proteins ( ). Using the CentiscaPe V.2.2 plugin in Cytoscape, 29 important proteins were identified within the PPI network ( ). The colour intensity indicates the degree of interaction strength. The top six most important proteins identified were ACTB, IL6, PPARG, CASP3, PPARA and IGF1.

## Molecular docking of TBOEP with key targets
To investigate the binding affinities and modes of interaction between TBOEP and its anticipated targets, molecular docking analyses were performed ( ). The findings from the docking experiments indicated that the binding affinities of TBOEP towards ACTB, IL6, PPARG, CASP3, PPARA and IGF1 were −10.2, −6.7, −6.6, −7.0, −6.9 and −5.6 kcal/mol, respectively. Additionally, potential hydrogen bonds were observed: TBOEP may form two hydrogen bonds with LYS-86 on the IL6 protein, one hydrogen bond with TYR-477 on the PPARG protein, two hydrogen bonds with ARG-341 on the CASP3 protein and two hydrogen bonds with ASN-26 on the IGF1 protein.
 

## RNA-seq analysis of TBOEP’s impact on hepatocytes
To further explore the effects of TBOEP on hepatocytes, we first examined the cytotoxicity of varying concentrations of TBOEP on hepatocytes. It was observed that cell viability was altered at concentrations exceeding 80 µM ( ). Based on the CCK-8 results, a concentration of 20 µM was chosen for the subsequent RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) experiment. This sublethal dose was selected to capture the early transcriptional changes induced by the treatment, independent of secondary effects caused by cell death or significant stress responses. Subsequently, hepatocytes were cultured with 20 µM TBOEP for 48 hours before harvesting for transcriptome sequencing. Different group clustering was found using principal component analysis ( ). The TBOEP-treated group showed 56 upregulated genes and 270 downregulated genes in comparison to the dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) control group ( ). To clarify the BP, MF and CC impacted by TBOEP treatment, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed.
 The top five BPs included xenobiotic metabolic process, fatty acid metabolic process, cellular response to xenobiotic stimulus, arachidonic acid metabolic process and icosanoid metabolic process ( ). The top five MFs were monooxygenase activity, haem binding, oxidoreductase activity, tetrapyrrole binding and arachidonic acid epoxygenase activity ( ). The top five CCs were the apical part of cell, inflammasome complex, apical plasma membrane, peroxisome and microbody ( ). The top 10 KEGG pathways were chemical carcinogenesis—DNA adducts, steroid hormone biosynthesis, retinol metabolism, pentose and glucuronate interconversions, bile secretion, malaria, legionellosis, drug metabolism—CYP P450, ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, and metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450 ( ). Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis did not reveal any significant changes in pathways ( ).

## TBOEP-related pathways influencing MASLD development
By intersecting the KEGG pathways enriched from the database-predicted targets with those enriched from the RNA-seq differential genes, 10 pathways were identified that may be related to TBOEP’s influence on hepatocyte homoeostasis ( ). These pathways include lipid and atherosclerosis, metabolism of xenobiotics by CYP P450, PPAR signalling pathway, complement and coagulation cascades, chemical carcinogenesis—receptor activation, steroid hormone biosynthesis, bile secretion, taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, cocaine addiction and chemical carcinogenesis—DNA adducts. These pathways suggest a multifaceted impact of TBOEP on liver cell function and may provide insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying MASLD development.

## TBOEP induces hepatocellular lipid accumulation by activating PPARγ
To investigate the mechanism of TBOEP-induced steatosis, we performed in vitro assays. Following FFA induction, we added 20 µM TBOEP and incubated for 24 hours. The results showed that TBOEP exposure significantly increased lipid droplet accumulation ( ) and triglyceride content ( ) in hepatocytes. At the molecular level, TBOEP upregulated the mRNA expression of key lipogenic genes, SREBP1c, FASN and SCD1 ( ), while genes related to fatty acid β-oxidation (Pparα, Cpt1α) and inflammation (Il-6, Il-1β) remained unaffected ( ). Based on molecular docking and pathway enrichment analysis, we found that TBOEP significantly impacted the PPAR signalling pathway. We then verified the levels of PPARγ and PPARα, and found that TBOEP significantly increased PPARγ expression without affecting PPARα expression ( ), suggesting that TBOEP might promote hepatocellular lipid accumulation through PPARγ.
 To further validate whether TBOEP promotes lipid accumulation via PPARγ, we constructed siRNAs and confirmed the silencing efficiency of PPARγ ( ). Furthermore, the results revealed that after silencing PPARγ expression, the TBOEP-induced promotion of lipid droplet accumulation and triglyceride increase was reversed ( ). Concurrently, the TBOEP-induced upregulation of SREBP1c, FASN and SCD1 expression was also reversed ( ). Finally, PPARγ protein levels were measured by culturing the cells with TBOEP and then treating them with cycloheximide (100 µg/mL) for 0, 3 and 6 hours. The results showed that the degradation rate of the PPARγ protein in the TBOEP group was lower than that in the control group, indicating that TBOEP could further stabilise the level of PPARγ protein ( ). These results collectively demonstrate that PPARγ is a critical mediator of TBOEP-induced hepatocellular steatosis.
 

## Discussion
The rising prevalence of MASLD necessitates a deeper understanding of its underlying mechanisms and contributing factors. Current research has primarily focused on genetic and metabolic aspects of liver disease, yet the role of environmental chemicals, particularly TBOEP, remains underexplored. This investigation identifies 154 common targets between TBOEP and MASLD, revealing significant biological pathways that TBOEP may influence. The findings highlight the potential of environmental chemicals in exacerbating liver disease, suggesting that TBOEP could be a critical factor in the pathogenesis of MASLD. The recognition of these shared targets not only deepens our comprehension of the underlying molecular interactions but also highlights the need for additional research into the health impacts of environmental exposures.
This study identified common targets of TBOEP and MASLD and performed enrichment analysis, revealing significant enrichment in pathways such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, insulin resistance and cholesterol metabolism. These compelling pieces of evidence suggest a strong correlation between TBOEP and MASLD. Through PPI analysis, six proteins (ACTB, IL6, PPARG, CASP3, PPARA and IGF1) were identified as binding partners of TBOEP. Molecular docking outcomes highlighted strong binding affinities between the molecules and these proteins.
PPAR exhibits significant expression levels in hepatic tissues and is intricately involved in a multitude of pathological and physiological processes, such as oxidative stress responses, energy metabolism and inflammatory pathways, all of which are closely linked to the advancement of liver diseases. Notably, PPARα specifically stimulates the expression of genes related to fatty acid β-oxidation within the liver, thereby facilitating fatty acid breakdown and mitigating lipid accumulation. Furthermore, PPARα is instrumental in modulating lipoprotein synthesis, inflammatory responses and insulin sensitivity. In the liver, the excessive activation of PPARγ may promote lipogenesis and lipid deposition, but it can also improve insulin resistance and oxidative stress responses. PPARα and PPARγ maintain a dynamic balance in lipid metabolism, adjusting the metabolic state of the liver. Observations of the clinical efficacy of the pan-PPAR agonist lanifibranor suggest that it may become an important clinical drug in MASH treatment. Studies have found that in nematodes exposed to TBOEP, the PPAR signalling pathway and glycolipid metabolism-related pathways were also impacted. In the present study, we found that TBOEP promoted the accumulation of lipid droplets in hepatocytes by increasing the expression of PPARγ. However, the expression level of PPARα was not regulated.
CASP3, a cysteine aspartate protease functioning as an effector, induces the fragmentation of nuclear DNA during apoptosis. Additionally, CASP3 plays a role in the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signalling pathway and is implicated in inflammatory responses. Its overactivation is significantly associated with alcoholic hepatitis. The interaction between TBOEP and the CASP3 protein may enhance protein stability, potentially contributing to the onset of MASLD linked with TBOEP exposure. Furthermore, decreased IGF1 synthesis in the liver can result in disrupted fat metabolism, leading to fat accumulation within hepatic cells. In the context of paediatric obesity, IGF1 levels exhibit a negative correlation with the extent of liver fibrosis. Interventions targeting the growth hormone/insulin-like growth factor 1 (GH/IGF1) axis have been shown in animal models to alleviate fatty liver. The presence of TBOEP may disrupt the regulation of the GH/IGF1 axis, exacerbating the progression of MASLD.
An additional significant pathway that has been recognised is the metabolism of xenobiotics by CYP P450, which is essential for the biotransformation of certain environmental compounds, including TBOEP, into metabolites that exhibit reduced toxicity. The enzymes belonging to the cytochrome P450 family are integral to both drug metabolism and the detoxification processes of hazardous substances. Graphene family materials (GFMs), such as graphene oxide, have been found to inhibit CYP1A2, CYP2D6 and CYP3A4 enzymatic activity in hepatic cells, which subsequently interferes with drug metabolism and the elimination of xenobiotic compounds. This inhibitory effect, in conjunction with GFM-induced oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, highlights the risk of environmental pollutants impacting toxicological effects through the disruption of the CYP system. Polymorphisms in CYP enzymes can lead to variations in drug metabolism capacity, potentially increasing liver burden. For instance, the inhibition of CYP3A4 activity may prolong the retention of drugs in the liver, indirectly exacerbating the risk of lipid accumulation. Additionally, previous studies have found that TBOEP induces DNA breaks through the cytochrome P450 enzyme system. TBOEP exposure leading to the dysregulation of this pathway may result in the accumulation of toxic metabolites, further aggravating liver injury and inflammation. Understanding the impact of TBOEP on cytochrome P450 activity can guide strategies to enhance detoxification processes and reduce the risk of MASLD.
Ultimately, the pathways linked to lipids and atherosclerosis are of considerable importance, as they underscore the correlation between lipid metabolism and cardiovascular health. The build-up of lipids within the liver is a defining characteristic of MASLD, and the interaction between hepatic lipid metabolism and systemic atherosclerosis has been thoroughly documented. The effect of TBOEP on this pathway could not only affect liver function but also potentially increase cardiovascular risks associated with MASLD. Targeting this pathway could offer dual benefits for the treatment of liver disease and its cardiovascular complications.
The limitations of this study warrant careful consideration. First, while our in vitro findings establish a direct cell-autonomous effect of TBOEP, in vivo models are necessary to account for complex systemic factors such as the gut-liver axis, immune cell infiltration and whole-body metabolism, which are integral to MASLD pathogenesis. Moreover, the reliance on a single cell line (AML12) is a limitation; future studies using primary hepatocytes may better reflect the physiological metabolic state of the liver. Addressing these constraints in future research will strengthen our conclusions and improve the understanding of TBOEP’s role in MASLD.

## Conclusion
The present study elucidated the complex association between TBOEP exposure and MASLD progression through target identification, functional enrichment and molecular interactions. We demonstrated that TBOEP promoted lipid accumulation in hepatocytes via overexpression of PPARγ in vitro. This provides new insights into the link between environmental pollutants and the development of MASLD.
