## ABSTRACT
Antibody therapeutics have emerged as a cornerstone in modern biopharmaceutical development, revolutionizing treatments across diverse diseases. Recent breakthroughs in antibody discovery technologies, particularly single B-cell sorting and high-throughput sequencing, have significantly enhanced the ability to identify and isolate potent therapeutic antibodies. Despite these advances, widespread adoption of monoclonal antibody (mAb) therapies faces substantial challenges, including complex manufacturing processes, high production costs, and stringent cold-chain storage requirements. A promising solution to these limitations is nucleic acid-encoded antibody delivery, which enables in vivo production of functional antibodies. This technology delivers nucleotide sequences encoding mAbs instead of the antibody proteins themselves, effectively turning the body into a bioreactor for antibody production. By bypassing the complex purification and quality control processes associated with traditional recombinant protein production, this approach offers a more streamlined and potentially cost-effective alternative. Herein, we review current nucleic acid-based antibody delivery platforms, highlighting the unique advantages and technical challenges. We provide an in-depth analysis of the latest advancements in this field, including both viral and non-viral delivery methods, and discuss their implications for next-generation antibody therapeutics. We also examine the potential applications in infectious diseases and cancer immunotherapy, alongside regulatory and safety considerations for clinical translation. We aim to provide valuable insights and guidance for researchers and clinicians in advancing novel antibody-based therapies.
KEYWORDS: Passive immunity, nucleic acid antibody, adeno-associated virus, antibiotic therapy, gene delivery

## Introduction
Monoclonal antibody (mAb) therapy has advanced significantly since the hybridoma technology was established. In 1975, Köhler and Milstein pioneered the B lymphocyte hybridoma technique by fuzing antigen-specific mouse spleen cells (containing B lymphocytes) with myeloma cells (Köhler and Milstein ), laying the foundation for monoclonal antibody development. Over the past 40 years, antibody technology has evolved from murine monoclonal antibodies to chimeric antibodies, humanized antibodies, and finally fully human monoclonal antibodies. This transition from murine to human antibodies was enabled by breakthroughs such as antibody library technology, humanized mouse models, and single B-cell cloning. To date, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) have approved more than 80 mAb-based biologics for the treatment of oncology, immune diseases, infectious diseases, metabolic diseases, and neurological diseases (Baldo ).
Recombinant monoclonal antibody therapy, a passive immune approach that can provide near-immediate protection, is widely used in disease prevention and treatment. For example, during the Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, several mAb cocktails against SARS-CoV-2 were granted emergency use authorization to treat symptomatic patients (Copin et al. ). In contrast, active immunization caused by traditional vaccines requires time for the host to develop an effective immune response (Guimarães et al. ). Additionally, mAbs can also be used to protect immunocompromised individuals who may fail to mount a robust response to vaccines. Despite these advances, the large-scale production and global distribution of mAb as a biologic drug is still limited. First, mAb manufacturing is relatively complex involving cell culture, protein purification, and formulation (Vázquez-Rey and Lang ). Each stage requires strict quality control and high manufacturing costs. Second, recombinant mAbs require cold-chain storage and transportation, which greatly increases the difficulty of their application in underdeveloped regions and countries. Other limitations include short half-life, necessitating repeated administrations that may reduce patient compliance (Wei et al. ).
The significant potential and existing challenges of monoclonal antibody therapies underscore the critical need for innovations in their production and delivery. The emergence of in vivo antibody delivery can avoid these hurdles by administering nucleotide sequences encoding mAbs (Choi et al. ), rather than the mAb protein. This technology transforms the body into a bioreactor for antibody production and overcomes the inherent obstacles of in vitro production of recombinant proteins such as complex purification and cumbersome quality control processes. A single injection of antibody-encoding nucleic acids may cause prolonged expression of antibody molecules lasting from days to months (Kulkarni et al. ), which is a potential advantage for managing endemic diseases and recurrent infections. In summary, in vivo gene delivery platforms present a labor- and cost-effective alternative to the conventional production, purification, and administration of mAb proteins. This technology can increase patient access and provide new clinical options for this increasingly important class of biologics.
In this comprehensive review, we aim to provide an in-depth analysis of the current advancements in nucleic acid-encoded antibody technology, focusing on molecular mechanisms, delivery platforms, and potential applications in diverse disease areas. We further examine current challenges and future directions to offer critical insights for both researchers and clinicians.

## In vivo antibody gene delivery
Gene therapy has evolved significantly since its conceptual origins in the 1970s (Friedmann ). After decades of development, it has emerged as a critical therapeutic modality to treat genetic diseases, infectious diseases, and malignancies. The maturation of gene therapy technology has provided important technical support for in vivo antibody gene delivery (Bogdanenko et al. ). At present, two primary platforms for in vivo antibody gene transfer that have been developed. They are viral vector-mediated antibody gene transfer platforms and non-viral vector-mediated gene transfer platforms ( ). The former is usually categorized as DNA-encoded and RNA-encoded antibody expression platforms. Capitalizing on the versatility of antibody gene engineering, the diversity of mAb products that can be expressed in vivo has significantly expanded. It includes conventional recombinant antibodies such as antibody-fusion proteins (Patel et al. ; Clift et al. ), multispecific antibodies (bispecific or trispecific) (Wu and Demarest ; Lou and Cao ; Brinkmann and Kontermann ), single-chain variable fragment (scFv) (Chen et al. ), and single-domain antibodies (Tang et al. ; Rossotti et al. ; Liu and Huang ; Muyldermans ), as well as novel constructs like bispecific T-cell engagers (BiTEs), bispecific and trispecific killer cell engagers (BiKEs and TriKEs), and dual-affinity retargeting molecules (DARTs) have entered clinical trials.
 

## Viral vector-mediated antibody gene transfer
The use of viral vectors to express therapeutic proteins, including antibodies, has a long history. Although other viruses and bacterial systems have been reported the expression of therapeutic proteins, the viral vectors most commonly used for gene therapy are adenovirus (Adv) and adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors, which realize the in vivo transfer of antibody genes by deleting part of their genomes and inserting therapeutic antibody genes ( ) (Zhan et al. ). In preclinical animal models, both AAV and Adv have reached an impressive expression level of µg/mL.
 

## Adenoviral vectors
The adenovirus genome is a linear, non-segmented double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) molecule, typically 26–45 kb in size. It mainly includes E1a, E1b, E2a, E2b, E3, E4 (encoding viral regulatory proteins) in the early stage of viral DNA replication, L1-L5 (encoding viral structural proteins) in the late stage of DNA replication, and inverted terminal repeats (ITRs) at both ends. Adenovirus vectors can infect both mitotic cells and non-mitotic cells via receptor-mediated endocytosis, delivering their genome to the nucleus while remaining episomal. After decades of development, adenovirus-based genetic engineering drugs have relatively matured. Adv-encoded therapeutic monoclonal antibodies have been widely used in anti-tumor therapy (Ho et al. ; Liu et al. ; Lv et al. ; Wang et al. ), antibacterial (Guilleman et al. ), and anti-virus (van Lieshout et al. ). However, adenovirus vector may trigger anti-vector immune response due to immunogenicity, potentially limiting antibody expression efficiency.

## Adeno-associated viral vectors
Adeno-associated viral vectors is a small, non-enveloped, linear single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) virus, belonging to the genus Dependoparvovirus within the family Parvoviridae (Naso et al. ). Its genome is approximately 4.7 kb in length, packaged within an icosahedral capsid of 20–26 nm in diameter. In gene therapy, AAV is currently the most popular viral vector due to its low immunogenicity and serotype-dependent tissue tropism. At present, 13 human AAV serotypes and more than 100 isolates have been identified, distinguished by capsid protein modifications (Ertl ). In 2002, Lewis et al. reported the first example of producing antibody fragments (scFv) from AAV-based vectors (Lewis et al. ). Although only a low level of antibody was observed in the body (<10 μg/mL), this low antibody titer lasted at least 6 months. Subsequent innovations have dramatically improved efficacy: In 2017, Robert et al. utilized recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) vectors to deliver genes encoding the Ebola-targeting ZMapp antibody cocktail, achieving 90% protection in non-human primates via intramuscular administration (Robert et al. ). Recent advances in capsid engineering have demonstrated remarkable improvements in tissue specificity and therapeutic efficacy. A pivotal study by Wagner et al. systematically compared novel engineered myotropic AAV variant (AAVMYO) with conventional hepatotropic AAV8 for SARS-CoV-2 antibody delivery (Wagner et al. ). Their findings revealed that AAVMYO achieved peak serum antibody concentrations of 511 μg/mL in mice, sustaining levels over 100 μg/mL for over 52 weeks, representing a 3.7-fold higher peak titer than AAV8. This superiority increased afucosylation (up to 38%) and terminal galactosylation, enhancing Fc gamma receptor IIIa (Fc-γ RIIIa) binding by 2.1-fold. The study further advanced this paradigm by demonstrating tissue-specific expression dynamics: AAVMYO generated 1000-fold higher vector mRNA in muscle versus liver, while AAV8 showed exclusive hepatic tropism at 4 weeks. Importantly, no anti-drug antibodies (ADA) were detected by electrochemiluminescence assay during the 52-week follow-up, likely due to the antibody’s 94-98% homology to human germline sequence. These findings establish AAVMYO as a transformative platform for sustained antibody delivery.
Despite these advances, clinical challenges remain. Preexisting immunity to natural AAV serotypes is a major limitation, with 30-70% of populations showing neutralizing antibodies against common variants like AAV2 (Hareendran et al. ). Wagner et al. provided novel insights by demonstrating that engineered capsids like AAVMYO evade preexisting immunity through tissue-detargeting strategies, maintaining high transduction efficiency in muscle—a compartment less susceptible to antibody-mediated neutralization (Wagner et al. ). In addition to preexisting antibodies, certain non-antibody serum factors, such as platelet factor 4 (PF4), have also been reported to affect transgenic expression (Perdomo et al. ). Individuals without adaptive immunity will also be unable to repeat drug administration due to the initiation of adaptive immunity after inoculation of adeno-associated virus vector.

## RNA-mediated antibody gene transfer
RNA-based therapeutics have achieved remarkable advancements over the past decade, emerging as a highly efficient platform for in vivo encoding and production of therapeutic proteins. mRNA has been used to develop vaccines and nucleic acid drugs (Pardi et al. ), demonstrating significant clinical efficacy. In addition, novel RNA platforms such as self-amplifying RNA and circular RNA (Schmidt and Schnierle ) have been explored in RNA vaccine development in recent years, showing some potential applications superior to mRNA.

## mRNA-mediated antibody gene transfer
mRNA is synthesized by in vitro transcription (IVT) using a linear DNA template that contains a promoter, 5′ and 3′ untranslated regions (UTRs), and an open reading frame (Petkovic and Müller ). In addition, efficient translation and stability of mRNA requires a 5′ - terminal cap structure and a 3′ - terminal poly (A) tail. These can be added either co-transcriptionally or post-transcriptionally through enzymatic catalysis (Niu et al. ). As a transient protein expression platform, mRNA has many advantages over in vitro production of recombinant antibodies: 1) mRNA enables in vivo expression of therapeutic antibody genes in non-dividing cells, bypassing the need for nuclear phase activation; 2) The expression of mAb in vivo can be detected 2 hours after administration at the earliest due to cytoplasmic translation (Verbeke et al. ); 3) Unlike viral vectors, mRNA does not trigger anti-carrier antibodies, permitting repeat administration without diminished efficacy and safety concerns associated with immune responses. However, due to the rapid degradation of ubiquitous ribonuclease in the environment, the stable delivery of mRNA in vivo is a key problem to be solved. We can enhance the translation efficiency and achieve more stable protein expression by optimizing the mRNA synthesis and purification process, codon optimization, and the use of lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) to wrap RNA (Eygeris et al. ). These improvements, coupled with efficient delivery without equipment, greatly simplify and expand the application of mRNA in therapeutic applications, including antibodies.

## Research on mRNA-encoded antibodies
Lipid-based drug delivery systems have emerged as a versatile platform to target cells and tissues, delivering cargo from from small-molecule inhibitors to vaccine components. Compared with traditional drug delivery methods, it increases drug stability, bioavailability, and tissue distribution. In the past decade, mRNA-based studies have demonstrated significant potential for both treatment and vaccine applications (Sanz and Álvarez-Vallina ), in which antibody expression is mainly concentrated in the fields of anti-virus (including HIV, Influenza A/B, Chikungunya, Zika, and RSV), anti-tumor and antitoxin (Sanz and Álvarez-Vallina ). Several strategies have been used to express mRNA-encoded antibodies in vivo. Variable domain of Heavy-chain-only antibody (VHH) and other single-domain antibodies exhibit compact molecular sizes, conferring superior tissue penetration capabilities while retaining full antigen-binding affinity and specificity comparable to full-length antibodies (Stadler et al. ). However, their small molecular weight results in the half-life of only a few hours and a tendency to aggregate, which hinders their use (Stadler et al. ). Using mRNA to continuously express antibodies can greatly improve this situation and prevent aggregation during protein purification. Ying Wang et al. used the mRNA encapsulated on the clinically approved lipid nanoparticles platform to synthesize a bispecific single domain antibody that can double specifically bind and neutralize the catalytic factors CCL2 and CCL5 (BisCCL2/5i) in vivo (Wang et al. ). The mRNA encoding BisCCL2/5i forms a liver-tropic biomaterial, which allows BisCCL2/5i to be expressed in the diseased organs temporarily and effectively. This platform effectively reprograms tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward the anti-tumor M1 phenotype. The experimental results show that combined administration of mRNA-encoded BisCCL2/5i and PD-L1 inhibitor induces durable survival benefits in the murine models of primary liver cancer, colorectal cancer, and pancreatic cancer with liver metastasis (Wang et al. ). The discovery of LNPs and new lipids with better tolerance paved the way for mRNA-mediated antibody expression, resulting in a significant increase in antibody expression in non-human primates (Whitley et al. ). In addition, the progress of nucleotide modification reduces the innate immune stimulation of mRNA, enabling mRNA to be used for therapy. With the development of mRNA sequence design, manufacture, and purification, the first clinical trial of mRNA encoding antibody was finally completed (Deal et al. ), and the results proved the safety of this method and the potential to meet the requirements of clinical application.

## Self-amplifying RNA
As a new generation of mRNA, self-amplifying RNA (saRNA) vaccine has attracted wide attention (Blakney et al. ). Compared with traditional mRNA vaccines, saRNA vaccine adds replicable sequences encoding viral replicases that induce amplification of target sequences to the delivered mRNA sequences. Once saRNA is transported to the cytoplasm, it will replicate itself by using the host cells through the endogenous translation mechanism in the ribosome, Thus, in the case of extremely low injection dose (a fraction to a tenth of the dose of conventional mRNA vaccine), the high expression of antigen can be achieved, and the ability to generate a strong immune response can be achieved. This ultra-low injection volume has three important advantages: 1) It has greater production and manufacturing potential. The same origin and production equipment can produce more vaccines; 2) Because the dose is low, the risk of side effects is reduced; 3) Low doses can be used together with other vaccines.
One disadvantage of saRNA vaccines is the large and complex saRNA sequences. The total length of the saRNA vaccine is usually more than 9,000 bases. The larger structure leads to instability of the genome and easy fragmentation (Zhou et al. ; Huang et al. ), which also causes higher innate immune stimulation. Therefore, the large-scale manufacturing, storage, and characterization of these structures are quite complex and expensive (Zhou et al. ; Huang et al. ). The trans replication system (Blakney et al. ) reduces the structure of saRNA by separating replicable sequences and antibody genes into separate RNA molecules. However, the replicase mRNA still exceeds 7,000 nucleotides in length, which requires a vector with a large loading capacity to successfully deliver RNA. This cross replication system may be advantageous due to its potential advantages in versatility, security, and ease of mass production. Current research demonstrates that the self-amplifying mRNA (SAM) platform has been successfully applied to multiple therapeutic targets, such as HIV, RSV, and influenza virus. These applications have been rigorously validated across preclinical models including murine models, ferret models, and non-human primates (Maruggi et al. ). Recently, a binary vector trans-amplifying RNA (taRNA) system has been developed (Beissert et al. ). Using this system, animals can be protected from influenza at a dose as low as 50 ng, revealing that the activity of the taRNA-encoded replicase is far superior to that of saRNA. Although it has not been widely studied in infectious disease models (Beissert et al. ), emerging evidence demonstrates the taRNA system is superior to the saRNA system in terms of safety, translation efficiency, and versatility. Although the size of the trans-amplified RNA system is relatively compact before SAM becomes a practical choice for the clinical production of monoclonal antibodies in vivo, significant improvements are still needed in the stability, immunogenicity, and more complex manufacturing of long mRNA sequences (Deal et al. ).

## Circular RNA
Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are a class of non-coding RNAs that do not have a 5′ terminal cap and a 3′ terminal poly (A) tail, and form a circular structure with covalent bonds. They are generated by atypical RNA splicing events called back-splicing in eukaryotic cells (Whitley et al. ; Blakney et al. ; Kim et al. ). In 2012, Salzman first reported 80 circRNAs by the RNA sequencing (Salzman et al. ). So far, with the help of high-throughput sequencing technology, circular RNA has entered the scientific research field. A large number of circRNA molecules have been found successively. Jack and others have detected more than 25,000 kinds of circRNA in human fibroblasts (Jeck et al. ); Memczak et al. identified 1,950 human circRNAs, 1,903 mouse circRNAs (81 of which are conserved between humans and mice), and 724 nematode circRNAs through RNA-Seq data and human leukocyte database (Memczak et al. ). Although circRNA research remains relatively nascent, seminal studies have already been published in premier journals (including Nature, Science, and Cell). The field has gained remarkable momentum, primarily owing to the exceptional molecular stability of circRNA—its covalently closed circular structure confers resistance to exonuclease-mediated degradation (Zhou et al. ; Huang et al. ). So far, only a few endogenous circular RNA have been experimentally validated as functional protein translation templates (Huang et al. ). Although circRNA lacks the basic element of cap-dependent translation, it can achieve protein translation through internal ribosome entry sites (IRES) or m6A modification-driven translation initiation. Liang Qu et al. have developed circular RNA vaccines targeting SARS-CoV-2 and its variants, which induced potent neutralizing antibodies and robust T-cell responses in murine models (Qu et al. ). In addition, the vaccine did not show a significant decrease in potency after being placed at room temperature (25 °C) for two weeks, highlighting its superior thermostability compared to conventional mRNA vaccines.
Due to the low production efficiency, difficult purification, and weak protein expression of circRNA, it is necessary to further optimize its translation and enhance its expression capacity. R. Alexander Wesselhoeft et al. designed a self-splicing intron that enables RNA cyclization up to 5 kb in vitro by rationally designing ubiquitous auxiliary sequences that facilitate splicing (Wesselhoeft et al. ). The results showed that the optimized circRNA could produce a large amount of proteins and could be purified effectively by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). This study demonstrated that circRNA can produce more proteins over a longer period of time than unmodified linear RNA, which provides evidence for the possibility of stable expression of circRNA as a replacement protein for mRNA, and proves that circRNA is a promising alternative to linear mRNA (Moufarrej et al. ). However, more work needs to be done to fully investigate the potential of circRNA for therapeutic and non-therapeutic applications, including further optimization of circRNA protein translation, as well as comprehensive studies of the efficiency and stability of circRNA in translation in other cell types and tissues.

## DNA-encoded monoclonal antibody gene delivery
The earliest DNA therapy can be traced back to 1990. Wolf et al. injected naked plasmid DNA (pDNA) into mouse skeletal muscle and observed the expression of coding protein, which opened the curtain of the field of DNA therapy (Wolff et al. ). Compared with viral vectors and RNA, pDNA has the following advantages: 1) Low immunogenicity, absence of anti-vector immunity, and capacity for repeated administration are allowed; 2) Due to high thermal stability, the production, transportation, and storage costs are lower, and the shelf life is longer; 3) pDNA has no restriction on the size of transgene and can be designed to encode large and complex proteins; 4) Its modular nature allows direct molecular cloning, making it easy for genetic engineering operations (Casini et al. ). However, the main disadvantage of pDNA is that the amount of transgenic expression is low, which requires an effective delivery system. The application of electroporation system has greatly improved the amount of transgenic expression of pDNA, bringing DNA-encoded monoclonal antibody gene expression technology into clinical trials. In the ongoing or completed clinical trials of gene therapy (Hollevoet and Declerck ), about 17% used plasmids and showed good tolerance and safety. David B. Weiner pioneered DNA-encoded monoclonal antibody (DMAb) technology, a gene transfer flatform, and widely used it in cancer treatment and infectious diseases, such as antiviral and antibacterial interventions. After the outbreak of the global COVID-19 pandemic, his team used this technology to compare the DMAb form of the antibody cocktail (DMAb 2196 and DMAb 2130) with the corresponding clinical recombinant antibody cocktails, Tixagevimab and Cilgavimab. The functional characterization results showed that compared with the direct injection of recombinant protein, the antibodies encoded by DMAbs showed similar specificity, extensive antiviral efficacy, and equal protective effect in preventing multiple animal attack models of SARS-CoV-2[64]. In the pharmacokinetic study, DMAb delivery maintained a better serum antibody titer than the direct administration of the recombinant protein. The data showed that the DMAb cocktail had good recognition and antiviral activity against SARS-CoV-2 Omicron mutant (Patel et al. ). This research demonstrates that DMAb technology has great research potential in the research and development of next-generation biological therapeutics.

## Optimization of expression quantity

## Sequence optimization
The optimization and analysis of therapeutic antibody sequences during early-stage research can make the expression process twice as efficient. Almost every research will optimize the codon according to the species information of the antibody to be expressed, thereby increasing the expression of the antibody. Sequence optimization usually has two directions. The first approach involves identifying key amino acid residues in the variable region through sequence analysis and site-directed mutagenesis to enhance expression yield. Some bioinformatics methods are also used to predict key amino acid residues in antibody sequences. For example, the IMGT DomainGapAlign tool can be used to analyze sequences to determine potential expression optimization sites (Ehrenmann and Lefranc ). Nicholas J. Tursi et al. used DNA-encoded monoclonal antibodies in the malaria challenge model (Tursi et al. ). During the construction of the vector, the nucleotide sequences encoding both heavy-chain and light-chain Fab/Fc regions were codon-optimized by mouse/human-preferred codons to enhance transgenic expression, with additional RNA-level optimizations to improve stability. To further enhance the expression, they introduced N-terminal framework amino acid mutations in several anti-malarial gp protein-targeting DMAbs by reverting to germline Ig sequences from the IMGT database, thus enhancing both expression and stability of DMAb (Tursi et al. ).
Alternatively, the mutation in the Fc region of antibodies have been engineered to modulate interaction with both Fc receptor (FcRn) and Fc gamma receptor (FcγR). The binding of FcRn, in turn, affects the duration of antibody expression in vivo. Researchers have found that modifying the Fc region of antibodies can eliminate FcRn binding, thereby protecting mice from lethal infections with increased antibody dependence. Seleeke Flingai et al. (Flingai et al. ) described a method for expressing cross-reactive neutralizing antibodies against dengue virus (DENV) using DNA plasmid-mediated antibody gene transfer. By intramuscular injection of the DNA plasmid encoding anti-DENV human IgG1 neutralizing antibodies (nAbs), they introduced L234A/L235A mutations in the CH2 domain of the Fc region. This mutation eliminated FcRn binding and protected mice from lethal infection with increased antibody dependence (Flingai et al. ). Neonatal FcRn is a very crucial transport protein, which is widely involved in various physiological and immunological processes in vivo. Fc interacts with FcRn in a pH-dependent manner, which can slow down the degradation of IgG and prolong its serum half-life. In 2018, the FDA approved ravulizumab-cwvz, an Fc-engineered monoclonal antibody, which is a complement component 5 inhibitor developed by Alexion Pharmaceutical Company for the treatment of paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) and atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) (McKeage ). By adding two structural changes to Eculizumab and replacing two amino acids in the Fc region, the drug enhances the affinity of an antibody to human FcRn to create a new long-acting antibody. Eculizumab requires intravenous infusion every 2–3 weeks, which increases the infusion burden and related adverse events. The half-life of Ravulizumab is up to 42 days, and patients only need to receive intravenous injection once every 8 weeks (Kulasekararaj et al. ).

## Electroporation
The low transfection efficiency of naked DNA is the biggest obstacle to DNA-mediated gene transfer, which also fails to elicit a strong immune response in large mammals such as monkeys and humans. So the researchers developed electroporation (EP), a physical method to temporarily increase the cell membrane permeability. EP involves the use of transient electrical pulses, which lead to the generation of transient nanoscale pores in the lipid bilayer membranes of mammalian cells. This allows DNA and other macromolecules to be transported through cell membranes that are normally impermeable to these molecules. Therefore, EP has the ability to increase the uptake and extent of drugs and DNA delivered to target tissues. Electroporation is widely used in the field of gene therapy, such as nucleic acid vaccines and antibody gene transfer. The CELLECTRA®-3P is an intelligent device independently developed by INOVIO for subcutaneous delivery of vaccine INO-4800 (Amante et al. ). The CELLECTRA®-3P electroporation device is widely used. As a minimally invasive electroporation device, it targets the dermis and subcutaneous layer of the skin, so tissue damage is minimal. The device is widely used in DNA-encoded antibody gene transfer experiments (Germain and Malissen ). After administration, electroporation is performed at the injection site immediately, which increases the expression efficiency of antibody genes. CELLECTRA®-3P also demonstrated reliable safety in clinical trials. Shortly after COVID-19 broke out, India urgently authorized and approved a DNA-based vaccine for human use against COVID-19, and the first DNA vaccine ever approved for any infectious disease (Mahboob et al. ). In September 2022, Inovio Pharmaceuticals and GeneOne Life Sciences jointly developed the world’s first hepatitis C vaccine GLS-615[72]. The participants will receive intradermal administration of the DNA vaccine at either 1.0 mg or 2.0 mg, followed by electroporation with a CELLECTRA®-3P device. Compared with intramuscular injection, the reduction of minimally invasive electrode depth significantly improved the tolerance of electroporation. Using this equipment platform can provide preventive immunity with minimal invasiveness, which is the mainstream trend of electroporation technology in the future.

## Adjuvants for injection
An important factor for successful electroporation in vivo is the ability of plasmid DNA to enter muscle fibers through the fiber surface. Each muscle fiber is wrapped by the plasma membrane called sarcolemma and surrounded by collagen and hyaluronic acid, which collectively constitute a significant barrier to effective plasmid DNA delivery. Therefore, hyaluronidase or collagenase can reduce the activity of hyaluronic acid in vivo by decomposing extracellular matrix components, thereby improving the liquid permeability of tissues, thus increasing the distribution of plasmids in the whole muscle. Kevin Hollevoet et al. evaluated the role of hyaluronidase in the research related to clinical transformation of large animals. They transferred the pDNA encoding ovine anti-CEA monoclonal antibody (OVAC) to sheep through electroporation in vivo. After pretreatment with hyaluronidase at the injection site, the plasma OVAC concentration in sheep 4520 increased 6 to 10 times in the initial 14 days, with a peak of 1.6 µg/mL (Hollevoet et al. ). In addition to hyaluronidase, some other substances with similar effects were also included in the study, such as chondroitinase ABC, collagenase, and oleic acid. Nina N Schommer et al. applied chondroitinase ABC to the site of pDNA delivery in muscle, resulting in temporary destruction of the chondroitin sulfate scaffold barrier, thus enhancing gene distribution and expression in the whole tissue. This enzymatic preconditioning, when combined with CELLECTRA® intramuscular electroporation increased the gene expression of mice by 5.5 times and rabbits by 17.98 times (Schommer et al. ). In order to reduce the complexity of the operation and facilitate clinical transformation, Megan C. Wise et al. directly used hyaluronidase to prepare plasmid injection, eliminating the pretreatment step, and applied it to non-human primates (Wise et al. ).

## Double plasmid system and multiple injection sites
In murine models of antibody gene transfer, the most prevalent preclinical system, the tibialis anterior (TA) and quadriceps femoris (QF) muscles are preferentially selected as intramuscular injection sites. Due to the volume limitation of the injection site, large dose injection per unit point may affect the effective absorption of drugs. Therefore, when the injection dose is large, low-dose injection at multiple sites is usually used to improve the efficiency of gene transfer. In addition, when delivering pDNA-encoded antibody cocktails in vivo, different injection sites need to be selected for different muscles. Megan C. Wise et al. injected 25 μg of heavy chain and 25 μg of light chain plasmids, each prepared with hyaluronidase, into the tibialis anterior muscle and quadriceps femoris muscle, and then electroporated at the injection site. The experimental results showed that the expression level of single antibody in mice injected with antibody cocktail was not lower than that of single antibody delivered separately (Wise et al. ). It was found that compared with constructing the whole heavy light chain antibody gene into the same plasmid, constructing the heavy light chain into two separate plasmids can realize the self-assembly of the whole antibody in animals, and the expression efficiency is higher. Elizabeth M. Parzych’s research team used DMAbs technology to construct anti SARS-CoV-2 antibody plasmids and used a dual-plasmid system to construct the heavy chain and light chain genes of the antibody onto independent plasmids, and then co-transfected them into mice, making the dual-plasmid system self assemble into complete antibody molecules in vivo. Using a dual-plasmid system, the constructed antibody plasmid in this study significantly enhanced serum antibody titers in mice. Compared to the single-plasmid system, the dual-plasmid system achieved a 2–4 fold increase in peak antibody titers following in vivo delivery, with sustained high-level antibody expression lasting for at least six months (Parzych et al. ).

## Minicircle DNA
Microcircular DNA is the product of site-specific recombination of parental plasmid (PP) in vivo. Different from traditional plasmid vectors, because of the lack of prokaryotic DNA skeleton sequences, such as antibiotic genes, prokaryotic replication starting point. Microloop vectors can improve the level of therapeutic gene expression, the safety of gene therapy and reduce inflammation. In 1997, Darquet et al. The study of antibody gene transfer based on minicircle DNA (mcDNA) is relatively short (Darquet et al. ), and is currently limited to small animals. In 2014, Yi et al. evaluated the mcDNA used for antibody gene transfer for the first time. The antibody gene expressed was two kinds of monoclonal antibodies listed in the field of inflammatory diseases: tocilizumab, an anti-interleukin-6 receptor monoclonal antibody, and etanercept, an Fc fusion protein against soluble tumor necrosis factor (TNF) (Yi et al. ). The peak concentration of tolizumab reached 40–60 ng/mL at day 15 and decreased to below 20 ng/mL by day 30 after the administration of mcDNA. Although low antibody titer was detected, the process of arthritis symptoms was slowed down and improved in mice. In addition to inflammatory diseases, mcDNA is also used to express bispecific anti-CD3/CD20 monoclonal antibodies for the treatment of human B-cell lymphoma in a mouse cancer model. After injecting mcDNA into the tail vein of mice at a dose of 5 μg, the serum antibody titer reached a peak of 7 µg/mL in 24 hours and dropped sharply to 1.2 µg/mL after 3 days. By day 7, the antibody titer had dropped to 100 ng/mL, but it was enough to induce anti-lymphoma responses. Current literature reveals heterogeneous performance between mcDNA and plasmid DNA systems for antibody expression. Therefore, the characteristics of mcDNA compared with pDNA expressing antibodies still need to be further studied to form a convincing comparison (Liu and Zhang ).

## Safety and integration
The safety of the gene delivery platform is a necessary prerequisite for the clinical application of in vivo antibody gene delivery. Although the clinical safety data remain limited for both viral and non-viral vectors specifically engineered for antibody expression, the research on vaccine and gene therapy using this technology has been carried out for decades, which can provide some reference. Emerging clinical trials ( ) on gene-encoded monoclonal antibodies will further elucidate the safety profiles of these platforms.
 

## Safety of virus vector

## Safety of adenovirus vector
As preexisting immunity against viral vectors will limit the effectiveness and persistence of the corresponding vector, the researchers focus on the adenovirus serotype with low prevalence in the study population. Ronald Vogels et al. conducted extensive screening and found that among the 47 types of human adenoviruses detected, subgroup B members Ad35 and Ad11 were least neutralized by human serum (Vogels et al. ). The prevalence rate of Ad35 and Ad11 serum in Europe and America is less than 10% (22% and 30% respectively in Haiti). The prevalence rate in Asia is similar to that in Europe and America, but slightly higher in Africa (about 20% and 30% respectively); The prevalence rate of Ad26 serum among healthy people in China is about 35.3%, and that in Europe and the United States is less than or equal to 12%, but it is high in Africa (about 88% in Kamai) (Hadj Hassine ). The prevalence rate of other species such as chimpanzee adenovirus (ChAd) in the population is also low. For example, the prevalence rates of ChAd68, ChAd6, and ChAd7 serum among healthy people in China are 12.7%, 12.22%, and 13.13% (Palmer et al. ). Using rare human adenoviral serotypes or chimpanzee-derived adenoviruses as vaccine vectors can avoid the impact of preexisting immunity against Ad5 vector in the population to some extent (Venkatraman et al. ). Even so, the cross-reactive immunity between serotypes may compromise their immunogenicity. Studies indicate that adenoviral serotypes elicit a broad spectrum of T cell cross-reactivity. Thorner AR et al. pointed out that the sequence homology of adenovirus hexa adjacent amino acid hypervariable regions (Hexon HVRs) may be an important factor affecting cross-antibody responses (Thorner et al. ). For instance, studies have demonstrated cross-specific neutralizing antibody responses between Ad35 and Ad11, likely attributable to their 66% sequence homology in hexon hypervariable regions. In a limited number of studies, Ad vector had other adverse events, including lymphocytopenia and neutropenia (Ertl ). In addition, toxicity has been reported in the dose escalation study of Ad carrier, including one case of death due to the delivery of a high-dose carrier (Wise et al. ). At present, there is no relevant data in human clinical trials, but vectors such as Ad4, Ad6, Ad26 and Ad35 have been used in vaccine research (Duan ). Human rare adenoviral serotypes, especially those exhibiting low cross-reactive immunity with Ad5, retain significant potential for use.

## Safety of adeno-associated virus vector
AAV may also cause adverse reactions in animal and human clinical trials. The first is the immune response. When rAAV infects antigen-presenting cells, the activation of Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9) can cause a mild innate immune response. TLR9 recognizes the unmethylated Cytosine-phosphate-Guanine (CpG) in nucleic acid, thus activating classical and non-classical NF-κB pathways, and promoting the expression of inflammatory cytokines and interferon response genes. The expression of these genes will in turn produce a cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) reaction and stable neutralizing antibody titer (Horn and Fehse ). To avoid the immune reactions caused by AAV-mediated gene transfer, two methods are commonly used: 1) immunosuppressive drugs are used to avoid CTL reaction, which is successful in human factor IX; 2) In the design of rAAV vector, TLR9 inhibited DNA sequences are added, such as integrating multiple copies of TTAGGG from human telomere into rAAV genome to escape innate immune response (Nieto and Salvetti ). The second adverse reaction is the existence of genome integration. Nguyen et al. found that the therapeutic gene fragment (F Ⅷ) carried by AAV has been integrated into the genome of canine hepatocytes when AAV gene therapy was used to treat hemophilia A in canine, and some of them have even been integrated near the gene that affects cell growth (Nguyen et al. ). Some of these canine hepatocytes divide faster than other cells and form daughter cell clusters. Therefore, they suspected that these gene insertions activated growth-related genes, making these liver cells divide more rapidly. This finding confirms that AAV gene therapy can integrate therapeutic transgenes into host chromosomes. Although the integration frequency is not high, it still has potential carcinogenicity and may induce cancer. The third adverse reaction is that high-dose intravenous injection of AAV will produce severe toxicity. Hinderer et al. found that high-dose AAV injection caused serious toxicity in rhesus monkeys and piglets (Hinderer et al. ). Three rhesus monkeys showed obvious elevation of transaminase, and two of them disappeared after elevation without clinical sequelae, but the remaining rhesus monkeys suffered from acute liver failure and shock. However, most clinical studies focusing on moderate-dose support that AAV delivery is well tolerated in clinical practice and has achieved clinical effects in specific applications (Chandler et al. ). AAV is receiving more attention in the field of antibody gene delivery.

## Safety of mRNA delivery
Although the structure of exogenous IVT mRNA is similar to that of naturally synthesized mRNA, as a “non-self component,” it will still be recognized by the body’s immune monitoring mechanism and trigger a series of innate immune responses. IVT mRNA can be recognized by TLR-3, TLR-7, TLR-8 (Han et al. ), retinoic acid-inducible gene 1 and nucleus binding and oligomerization domain-containing protein 2 (NOD-2) in the TLR family. TLR is activated after binding with mRNA, and transmits signals to the downstream through specific junction proteins MyD88 and TRIF, activating a series of signal cascade reactions, thereby promoting the degradation of mRNA and inhibiting the expression of target protein (O’Neill et al. ). In this regard, researchers found that modified bases in natural RNA can not only inhibit TLR recognition in vivo but also increase the stability and translation of mRNA (Ashkar and Rosenthal ). A common modification is the replacement of uridine with pseudouridine, which has been shown to increase mRNA translation and reduce innate immune stimulation (O’Neill et al. ). In addition, strict mRNA purification by HPLC or Oligo(dT) affinity chromatography further reduces the stimulation of innate immunity and can remove abnormal RNA produced in IVT reaction. In March 2020, Moderna Company developed mRNA-1273, an LNP-encapsulated mRNA vaccine encoding the full-length spike glycoprotein (S protein) of SARS-CoV-2 with two proline mutations (2 P stabilization). In Phase I clinical trial results of the vaccine (Fraiman et al. ), mild to moderate adverse reactions including fatigue, chills, headache, and myalgia occurred, which is also a commonly induced side effect of the mRNA vaccine. In a small vaccination study, serious adverse events occurred after mRNA rabies vaccination (Alberer et al. ). The patient developed transient Bell’s palsy and recovered after the second vaccination. In addition, the researchers found that there were mild to moderate injection site reactions when mRNA was delivered (Alberer et al. ). If the injection frequency is high, it may cause more serious adverse reactions. Since mRNA currently requires LNP preparations, the safety of LNP preparation materials themselves is also worth paying attention to. At present, further research is being conducted to evaluate the safety of different preparations in clinical practice. The continuous research and development in this field will provide important guidance for the effectiveness and safety of this method.
To reduce systemic toxicity and anti-drug antibody responses, targeted organ delivery of mRNA therapeutics has become a research hotspot in many fields. The specificity of targeted organ drugs also reduces the drug dose reaching the effective therapeutic concentration, further reducing side effects, especially for the prevention or treatment of mucosal pathogens. Among them, the lungs and female reproductive tract (FRT) are potential targets of some infectious diseases, including influenza A, respiratory syncytial virus, and HIV. Van Hoecke et al (Van Hoecke et al. ) encoded an engineered bispecific VHH construct (RiboBiFE), which has previously been demonstrated to bind selectively to the extracellular domain of influenza A matrix protein 2 via a specific VHH, while targeting mouse Fc gamma receptor IV through a linked VHH. This dual-binding mechanism facilitates the recruitment of innate immune cells to influenza-infected cells. Compared with the RiboBiFE encoded by mRNA detected in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid fluid at 48 hours, the recombinant bispecific protein was barely detectable in the lung 24 hours after intratracheal injection, which proved the sustained expression of mRNA-encoded RiboBiFE in the lung. Moreover, mice treated with Fc-IV-M2e-RiboBIFE exhibited less weight loss, higher survival rates, and significantly reduced viral loads in the lungs following the influenza A virus attack.

## Safety of DNA delivery
To date, plasmid DNA has been extensively studied for more than 30 years, with no serious adverse events reported (Athanasopoulos et al. ). DNA plasmid injection have demonstrated good tolerability, and even at high doses, no adverse reactions similar to those observed with mRNA administration have been identified. The theoretical possibility of plasmid DNA integration is often considered as the risk factor of the platform. However, no matter what coding sequence and non-coding sequence exist in the plasmid, genome insertion has not been observed, which proves the safety and applicability of pDNA for research human use in various infectious disease prevention indications (Pagant and Liberatore ). Unlike the AAV vector, the plasmid encoding mAb lacks the immune response against the plasmid backbone. Therefore, different plasmids can be co-administered to target distinct epitopes without the risk of cross-inhibition. The technology was first validated in mice with anti-dengue virus monoclonal antibodies. The results showed that intramuscular administration of two plasmids encoding antibodies targeting distinct viral epitopes, combined with EP, increased the titer of total monoclonal antibodies in circulating blood, and increased the neutralization range of all four dengue virus serotypes. Furthermore, traditional plasmids usually contain bacterial components such as antibiotic resistance genes, which are prone to biosafety problems, and the regulatory authorities recommend avoiding these components in therapeutic applications (Pagant and Liberatore ). Consequently, some plasmid backbones without antibiotic resistance markers have been developed and validated for large-scale antibiotic-free production, including pCOR (plasmid with controlled orientation of replication), pORT (plasmid for oligonucleotide restriction and transcription), and npDNA (nanoparticle-associated DNA, NTC9385R) (Yu et al. ). Other constructs with fewer bacterial skeletons were also developed, such as microrings (Hollevoet and Declerck ). Collectively, these advances demonstrate that the plasmid DNA encoding antibody genes represents the safest delivery platform among current options, with significant therapeutic potential.

## Expression of human antibody in animals
Species differences pose inherent obstacles for pharmacokinetic characterization of antibody-encoding gene expression in animal models. The human antibody sequence, as a foreign substance, can elicit anti-drug antibody reactions in immunocompetent animals, significantly impacting both expression levels and persistence. To enable more accurate characterization of human antibody expression in animals, researchers usually employ the strategy of “immune shielding.” A common method is to use immunocompromised animal models for evaluation, such as RAG1 knockout mice or nude mice. David B. Weiner and colleagues (Patel and Weiner ) evaluated the expression of the DMAb-encoded BiSPA antibody against Pseudomonas aeruginosa in vivo using athymic nude mice (B6.Cg-Foxn1nu/J strain), which lack functional T-cells (Patel et al. ). According to the test, a 100 μg dose of DNA plasmid was administered via intramuscular injection into either the tibialis anterior or quadriceps femoris, followed by electroporation at the injection site. This approach achieved sustained transgene expression persisting for 100–120 days in nude mice.
The other method is T cell depletion, a well-established immunological technique. Transient immune suppression in mice can be achieved through administration of anti-CD4 and anti-CD8 monoclonal antibodies. Ami Patel et al. used the mouse infection model to carry out the in vivo transfer test of anti-Ebola virus DNA-encoded monoclonal antibodies, and used the method of T cell depletion to transiently suppress host immune responses at the early stage of DMAb injection, thus avoiding the murine anti-human antibody reaction. Within 14–21 days, the mouse immune cell function returned to baseline levels, and the antibody gene was expressed for a long time. In the control group without T cell depletion eveloped robust anti-drug antibody responses within 14 days post-DMAb administration, resulting in rapid decline of therapeutic antibody titers (Patel et al. ). The advantage of T cell depletion is that the depletion of immune cells is only temporary, and the immune function of mice can return to normal within a few days so that human antibodies can be expressed in the body of mice with normal immune function and the challenge protection experiment can be carried out.

## Methods to enhance the protection effect
Antibody gene transfer strategy, particularly DMAb technology, is emerging as a pivotal strategy in the treatment and prevention of infectious diseases. Researchers have adopted corresponding strategies to improve and enhance the protection effect of DMAb. For anti-virus infection, DMAb technology can be used to deliver antibody cocktails targeting either multiple viral variants or distinct conserved epitopes. To address the antigenic variability resulting from HIV-1’s error-prone reverse transcription, Megan C. Wise et al. co-delivered multiple monoclonal antibodies in a single mouse through DMAb technology to target different HIV-1 envelope epitopes (Wise et al. ). The experimental results demonstrated that mice receiving antibody cocktails, especially PGDM1400 and PGT121 (NCT03205917), exhibited significantly broader neutralization coverage across viral variants compared to those administered single antibodies. Moreover, DMAb technology allows single-mouse delivery of four antibodies (PGDM1400, PGT151, VRC01, and PGT121), achieving broader neutralization than other technologiest (Mandizvo et al. ).
DMAb technology, supported by electroporation technology in vivo, can realize rapid expression without requiring antigen-mediated immune stimulation, functioning as an “emergency vaccine” and when combined with conventional DNA vaccines, it achieves both rapid and durable immune responses against specific pathogens. Hyeree Choi et al. demonstrated that combining anti-Zika DMAb and DNA vaccine in A129 mice achieved 90% protection within 2 days and 100% protection by 2 weeks, confirming rapid and durable immunity against viral challenge (Choi et al. ). The results showed that 90% protection could be achieved two days after injection and 100% protection could be achieved by two weeks. The results show that the combined injection of DMAb and DNA vaccine can provide both timely and sustainable protection, offfering a new strategy for the control of new infectious diseases (Teran et al. ).

## Controllable expression of antibody genes
Beyond bypassing complex manufacturing, nucleic acid-delivered antibodies offer a fundamental advantage over conventional formats: programmable in vivo expression control. This capability is inherently enabled by the nucleic acid delivery platform itself, as engineered regulatory elements (e.g. tissue-specific promoters, degrons) can be co-encoded within the same vector. Precision temporal and spatial control of antibody expression mitigates risks of overdosing/underdosing and reduces immunogenicity from ectopic expression-issues inherent to recombinant protein therapy. Critically, this programmability allows dynamic adjustment to patient-specific factors (e.g. disease progression, metabolic clearance), optimizing efficacy while broadening therapeutic applicability. Thus, advancing controllable expression and targeted degradation is essential to realizing the clinical potential of nucleic acid-encoded antibodies, addressing key translational barriers including expression durability, safety thresholds, and personalized dosing regimens.

## Inducible promoter subsystem
The inducible promoter subsystem represent a well-established approach for controlled protein expression, enabling transcriptional activation or repression via specific molecular inducers. Common inducible promoter subsystems include the gene regulation system based on exon skipping. Cripe et al. developed an inducible gene therapy system (TransSkip) by engineering a defective KRAS exon flanked by spliceable introns into the αCD19-αCD3 bispecific antibody transgene. This exon, containing stop codons, silences the transgene until a complementary morpholino induces exon skipping, restoring functional antibody expression. In murine models, repeated morpholino administration achieved cyclical, on-demand antibody production for over 36 weeks, enabling tumor suppression in CD19+ lymphomas ( ). This strategy offers controllable, intermittent protein expression for therapies requiring temporal regulation (Cripe et al. ).
 

## Targeted protein degradation system
The control of antibody molecules expressed in vivo can be achieved through targeted protein degradation (TPD) technology. This technology uses two naturally existing protein degradation systems in cells: the ubiquitin proteasome system (UPS) and lysosomal degradation pathway to achieve specific and efficient degradation of disease related proteins and therapeutic antibodies when required ( ).
 

## Targeted protein degradation system based on UPS
Ubiquitin-mediated degradation pathways provide critical tools for controlling the half-life of nucleic acid-encoded antibodies. Among these, proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) represent clinically advanced bifunctional molecules that bridge target proteins to E3 ubiquitin ligases, triggering proteasomal degradation (Li and Crews ). By fuzing a PROTAC-recruiting degron tag to the antibody gene sequence within the delivery vector, antibody expression duration can be precisely regulated ( ). PROTACs’ clinical progress (e.g. Phase II trials in oncology) validates their translational potential (Chen et al. ). Molecular glues (e.g. FDA-approved lenalidomide) offer a simpler alternative by inducing E3 ligase-target proximity, though their rational design remains challenging (Domostegui et al. ). These targeted degradation technologies are not merely parallel systems but enableable components for nucleic acid antibody platforms, allowing programmable control of antibody persistence to optimize dosing and safety.
 

## Targeted protein degradation technology based on lysosomal pathway
Lysosomes degrade proteins mainly through three ways (Zhao et al. ): 1) cell surface proteins reach endosomes through endocytosis, and then are degraded by lysosomes, or transported to the plasma membrane or other organelles for recycling; 2) The cells engulf the invading pathogens, dead cells, and other large extracellular particles to form Phagophare and transport them to lysosomes for degradation; 3) The misfolded or aggregated proteins, damaged organelles and intracellular pathogens form autophagosomes and then are transported to lysosomes for degradation ( ). With advancing research into endo-lysosomal and autophagy-lysosomal degradation pathways, lysosome-targeted TPD technologies have emerged as powerful alternatives to ubiquitin-proteasome-based approaches. Recent developments include several innovative platforms (Amirian et al. ), such as LYTAC, AbTAC, ATTEC, AUTAC, and AUTOTAC. Compared with ubiquitin proteasome-based TPD, lysosomal-based TPD can degrade not only intracellular proteins but also protein aggregates, damaged organelles, and extracellular proteins, which has a wider potential in application scenarios.
 TPD technology offers novel avenues for drug discovery and therapeutic intervention across various diseases, including cancer, immune disorders, and neurodegenerative conditions (Békés et al. ). A landmark achievement was the clinical entry of ARV-110 (Arvinas) in 2019, the first PROTAC to reach human trials for prostate cancer (Jimenez et al. ). Subsequently, over a dozen PROTACs and molecular glues have advanced into clinical studies, predominantly targeting oncology indications. Notably, Phase I data for ARV-471 in advanced breast cancer demonstrated favorable safety, with no dose-limiting toxicity observed even at 700 mg (Rej et al. ), prompting its progression to Phase III trials in 2022. Despite existing challenges, TPD’s expansive target protein library underscores its potential as a transformative drug development platform, poised to address critical unmet clinical needs.

## CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene expression regulation
The CRISPR/Cas9 system is a versatile tool used not only for gene editing but also for regulating gene expression. Its mechanism relies on specifically designed single-guide RNA (sgRNA) to direct Cas9 protein to bind to the promoter region of a target gene, thereby activating or inhibiting gene expression. For CRISPR activation (CRISPRa), dead Cas9 (dCas9) is fused to a transcriptional activator-like VP64 to induce expression (Liu et al. ). For CRISPR interference (CRISPRi), dCas9 is linked to a transcriptional repressor like KRAB (Banerjee et al. ). This system offers high specificity and reversible regulation, works across diverse cell types and biological models, and is widely applied in gene function studies, disease modeling, and gene therapy (Zhou et al. ). Notably, its use in regulating antibody gene expression has not yet been reported.

## Nucleic acid-encoded antibody relevant clinical experiments
At present, most studies on antibody expression in vivo are still in the experimental and preclinical stages, and only a few have entered clinical trials. At present, Phase I clinical trials have been reported for the three antibody encoding platforms (Schnepp and Johnson ), in which Phase I clinical trials have been completed for the DNA-encoded antibody platform and the mRNA-encoded antibody platform, and the expected results have been achieved. At present, the COVID-19 is still at a high level. As an emerging antibody expression platform, a phase I clinical study with the open-label, single center, and the increasing dose is recruiting subjects. This study aims to evaluate the DMAb forms (DMAb AZD5396 and DMAb AZD8076) of the mAb AZD5396 and mAb AZD8076 monoclonal antibodies cocktails and the recombinant human hyaluronidase injection Hylenex® Safety, tolerability and pharmacokinetic characteristics after intramuscular injection.

## Expectation
The monoclonal antibody therapy is one of the most rapidly expanding domains in biopharmaceuticals. While extensive knowledge has been accumulated regarding recombinant mAb production and protein-based applications. However, only a few of the more than 70 licensed mAbs are targeted at infectious diseases, such as oblitoxaximab and raxibacumab for anthrax infection, and pallivizumab for respiratory syncytial virus. This is obviously not in direct proportion to its huge therapeutic potential, because in the case of antibiotic failure or absence of antiviral drugs, monoclonal antibodies are a powerful alternative to fighting infectious diseases (Schnepp and Johnson ; Li and Song ). The high cost associated with protein-based monoclonal antibodies is in sharp contrast to the cost of most small molecule drugs or antibiotics. However, the wide clinical introduction of antibody gene transfer still faces numerous challenges. First, it remains unclear whether therapeutic monoclonal antibody titers can be reliably obtained and maintained in humans. Second, the lack of control over monoclonal antibody expression may compromise safety. Third, the immunogenicity of the vector or the expressed monoclonal antibody could limit continuous expression. Fourth, the continuous innovation in traditional monoclonal antibody therapies directly competes with antibody gene transfer, potentially affecting the latter’s adoption. For these challenges, we discussed possible ways forward. Although challenges still exist, continuous innovation and the versatility of antibody gene transfer have expanded the clinical prospects. In the near future, clinical introduction can be accelerated by selecting the platform method that is most suitable for monoclonal antibody or disease indication at present. The innovation of expression platforms, drug delivery, and antibody technology is expected to further improve the safety and rapidity, and release the huge clinical potential of antibody gene transfer.

## Conclusion
The clinical potential of nucleic acid-encoded antibodies is substantial. The ongoing clinical trials and the continuous refinements of delivery platforms and antibody technologies are expected to further enhance the safety and efficacy of these therapies. Beyond the immediate goal of phase-II/III validation, three under-explored avenues are poised to define the next decade of nucleic-acid antibody therapy. (i) Personalized, “made-for-patient” formats: coupling single-B-cell repertoire mining with rapid DNA microarray synthesis now enables the design of patient-specific antibody sequences within 7–10 days; integrating these sequences into lipid nanoparticles or AAV vectors tailored to individual HLA haplotypes could create on-demand therapies for ultra-rare infections or refractory cancers. (ii) Combination regimens that exploit spatial and temporal control: layering inducible promoters, miRNA sensor cassettes and protein degradation tags onto the same construct will allow simultaneous or sequential expression of multi-specific antibodies (e.g. anti-PD-1 + anti-CTLA-4) in response to tumor-specific cues, while keeping systemic exposure below toxicity thresholds. (iii) Pan-pathogen rapid response platforms: establishing a plug-and-play library of validated vectors carrying neutralizing antibody cassettes against viral surface “consensus epitopes” would permit real-time swapping of payloads during pandemic outbreaks, analogous to annual influenza vaccine strain updates but achievable in weeks rather than months. Addressing these directions will require tighter integration of machine-learning-guided epitope prediction, scalable GMP-grade vector production, and adaptive regulatory pathways-yet the modular nature of nucleic-acid antibodies uniquely positions the field to meet these challenges. In conclusion, the nucleic acid-encoded antibody gene transfer represents a significant advancement in the field of biopharmaceuticals. While challenges persist, the continuous innovation and versatility of this technology are set to expand its clinical horizons, offering new hope for patients and new avenues for therapeutic intervention.
