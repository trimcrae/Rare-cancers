## Abstract
Transdermal drug delivery systems (TDDS) represent a non-invasive approach to achieve controlled drug release through the skin barrier, offering stable plasma concentrations while avoiding gastrointestinal and hepatic metabolism. However, the skin barrier poses physical challenges, making it difficult for most drugs to penetrate deep tissues using TDDS. This review systematically summarizes the research progress in nanocarrier design, physical technology application, and artificial intelligence (AI)-driven TDDS optimization design aimed at overcoming the key problem of skin barrier penetration. An in-depth analysis of nanocarriers, such as soft vesicles, nanoemulsions, rigid nanoparticles, and physically assisted enhancement technologies, and AI-driven research on skin penetration enhancement in TDDS was summarized. An integrated technical framework demonstrates how microneedle arrays, iontophoresis, electroporation, and photoporation synergize with nanoparticles to achieve spatiotemporally controlled transdermal drug delivery. Additionally, the review emphasizes the clinical application potential of these integrated technologies and offers a comprehensive, multidimensional perspective to advance innovation in TDDS.
Keywords: Transdermal drug delivery, Nanoplatforms, Physical enhancement technologies, Integrated strategies, Clinical applications, Artificial intelligence

## Introduction
Drug therapy requires long-term administration, with the main delivery routes being oral, intramuscular/subcutaneous, and intravenous routes [ , , ]. Each has drawbacks like low bioavailability or poor patient compliance [ , ], prompting research into new delivery strategies to enhance bioavailability and minimize side effects. Drug delivery systems (DDS) crystallize multidisciplinary theories and techniques like physics, chemistry, biology, and materials science. Drugs are transported to the desired tissues, organs, or cells through the DDS, thus maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing side effects [ ]. The delivery efficiency of the DDS is determined not only by the structure and composition of the loaded drug, but also by the route of administration. The routes of administration usually include oral administration, intravenous or intramuscular injection, pulmonary inhalation, and so on [ , , ]. In recent years, studies have shown that the transdermal drug delivery system (TDDS) enables drugs to be absorbed into the blood vessels through the skin, thus exerting drug effects [ , , , ]. Since the delivery route does not pass through the gastrointestinal tract, TDDS decreases the loss of the therapeutics owing to the first-pass impacts and can bypass the degradation from enzymes, pH, and intestinal bacteria [ ]. Additionally, TDDS can effectively regulate the release of drugs to maintain blood concentration [ ]. Most importantly, compared with some routes of administration, TDDS is one of the non-invasive routes of drug delivery, which can alleviate patients' pain and improve patient compliance [ , ]. Therefore, compared to the traditional injection-based drug delivery methods, TDDS have emerged as one of the most extensively studied of non-invasive routes for transdermal drug administration [ , ]. Current applications of TDDS in drug delivery have included pain management, hormonal therapy, and treatments for diseases of the cardiovascular or central nervous systems [ , , , ].
Although TDDS is a convenient and relatively safe method of drug administration, the presence of the stratum corneum (SC) within the skin inevitably reduces the bioavailability of drugs. Studies have shown that the ideal drugs for TDDS should have a molecular weight less than 500 Da, proper amphiphilic properties [ ], a short half-life, minimal skin irritation [ ], etc. These characteristics enhance the transdermal delivery efficiency and efficacy of drugs. Classically, molecular weight (MW) and partition coefficient (logP) are the two variables in Potts and Guy's equation: Log Kp = 0.71 LogP - 0.0061 MW - 6.3 (n = 93, r2 = 0.67) [ ]. Active drugs under 350 Da are preferred for normal skin, while 600 Da drugs nearly fully permeate inflammatory skin [ ]. The ideal logP for most drugs is between 1 and 3 [ ]. Certainly, hydrogen bonding numbers and dipolar interactions also influence the in vitro permeation of drugs [ ]. At present, various physical TDDS strategies (including skin puncture devices with probes, epidermal erosion, dry powder jets, microneedle arrays, and oscillating needle bundles etc.) and chemical TDDS strategies (including the use of prodrugs and penetration enhancers) have been developed and have made significant progress [ , ]. However, both physical methods and chemical methods possess their drawbacks. For example, some physical methods may cause skin allergies, itching, blisters, and other adverse reactions [ ]. Although some topical, transdermal formulations, such as oils, gels, creams, ointments, and pastes, have progressed, their penetration through the SC is poor, resulting in poor patient adherence [ , , ]. The design of prodrugs based on chemical methods also aims to overcome the skin barrier function. However, prodrugs in this method are essentially excess coarse stones that may cause side effects when released [ ]. Therefore, the current research still urgently needs to develop new TDDS to enhance the ability of drugs to penetrate the skin barrier and achieve therapeutic drug concentrations in target tissues.
The development of nanomedicine has provided ideas for the design of novel TDDS. Nanomedicines typically refer to the use of nanotechnology to make active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) to form nanoparticles or to combine drugs with suitable nanomaterials to produce further multifunctional nanoparticle formulations with sizes ranging from 1 to 1000 nm [ , ]. Nanomedicine has merits, including a smaller size, a bigger specific surface area, and a higher encapsulation capacity. Additionally, it can modulate the release rate, influence the distribution, and enhance the bioavailability of the loaded APIs. Recently, the application of nanomedicines in TDDS has received increasing attention, and many research achievements have been made. Therefore, this article reviews the research progress of nanomedicines in TDDS, not only introducing the impact of the skin's physiological barrier on drug permeation, but also elucidating the design of different nanomedicines to overcome the skin barrier, as well as summarizing the applications of nanomedicines in the TDDS field in recent years.

## Skin structure and pathways of drug delivery
The skin, the body's largest organ, has an outer epidermis, middle dermis, and subcutaneous tissues. The epidermis, a complex squamous tissue, includes keratinocytes and viable epidermis (VE) and is divided into four sublayers: SC, stratum granulosum, stratum spinosum, and stratum basale, with keratinocyte differentiation varying across layers. The superficial SC, 10–20 μm thick, contains keratinized, Langerhans, melanocytes, and Merkel cells [ ]. Meanwhile, it consists of dead keratinocytes (corneocytes) filled with keratin, tightly packed with hydrophobic lipids like ceramides and cholesterol, forming a barrier that prevents substance entry and water loss [ , ]. The skin acts as a barrier to drug absorption [ , , ], with the SC being the main obstacle. Small drugs can penetrate the skin [ ], which is influenced by factors like skin age and condition [ , ].
Drug penetration through the skin occurs via the trans-appendage and trans-epidermal pathways [ ], with the SC pathway divided into intercellular and intracellular routes. The intracellular route involves drug delivery through dead keratinocytes' cytoplasm and surrounding lipids. At the same time, the intercellular pathway allows drugs to bypass keratinocytes through the extracellular matrix [ , ], reducing cellular damage but hindering water-soluble molecules. Nanocarriers must have a suitable size and properties to penetrate the SC [ ], with polymer-based and amphiphilic nanoparticles being more effective than rigid ones. Skin appendages, like sweat glands (SGs) and hair follicles, facilitate penetration for rigid nanocarriers compared to the SC pathway [ ]. However, the skin appendages only occupy a small area of the skin. In general, each penetration route has its advantages and limitations, while the design of nanocarriers will achieve multi-path penetration and bring new choices to TDDS, as described in .

## New strategies for permeation improvement
Studies have shown that the SC, pharmacological characteristics, nanocarriers, and temperature can affect the transdermal penetration of the TDDS [ ]. Several new strategies have emerged to increase the amount of drugs delivered into the skin to improve the efficiency of transdermal transportation. Among the three pathways of drug penetration through the skin, the follicular route is an effective way to deliver APIs across the skin barrier. This pathway contributes to the penetration of APIs, especially those loaded by nanocarriers [ ]. In addition to nanotechnology-aided methods, TDDS also needs physically-assisted techniques and combinations with nanotechnology.
3.1.

## Nanocarriers
Nano-promoted penetration techniques minimize skin tissue damage and have high drug-loading capacity, biodegradability, and biocompatibility. The benefits of encapsulating the APIs into nanocarriers can be summarized as follows: (1) elongate retention of APIs in the skin [ ], (2) enhance the drug's specificity and provide sustained release of the drug [ ], (3) enable the co-delivery of hydrophobic and hydrophilic active ingredients [ ], (4) increase the targeted delivery and bioavailability [ ], (5) improve the stability of APIs and prevent degradation of APIs [ ]. Common nanocarriers include soft vesicles, nanoemulsions, and rigid nanoparticles ( ).
 3.1.1.

## Soft vesicles
3.1.1.1.

## Liposome
Liposomes, the most common vesicles in nano-promoted permeation technology, are self-organized phospholipids and can effectively load drugs using active or passive strategies [ , ]. However, the reticuloendothelial system quickly removes them from the bloodstream [ ], prompting the conjugation of their surfaces with small molecules or proteins for targeted drug delivery [ , ]. Their multi-functionality, biodegradability, and biocompatibility have led to widespread development in clinical trials. In the TDDS field, liposome systems are also considered promising nanocarriers for bypassing the epidermal barrier [ ]. Currently, the delivery mechanisms of liposomes through the skin mainly include vesicle skin penetration, adsorption effects, and liposome penetration via the trans-appendage pathway [ ]. Firstly, the liposomes can pass through both the trans-epidermal and trans-appendage routes to penetrate the skin [ ]. Secondly, as a key component of liposomes, phospholipids are easy to be fused and mixed with the lipids in SC, to realize local release of drugs [ ]. Briefly, the enhanced penetration of liposomes is due to the interaction with skin lipids, leading to the delivery of the APIs to the deeper layers of skin [ ]. While the trans-appendage pathway means that liposomes can also penetrate the skin through the skin appendages, such as SGs, sebaceous glands, or hair follicles [ ].
Recently, Xing et al. [ ] encapsulated high MW hyaluronan (HHA) into soft liposomes, combining the advantages of liposomes and HA to prepare a TDDS of HHA-containing liposome (HHL) ( A). The results showed that the HHL can carry more hyaluronic acid (HA) into the skin than a pure HHA solution. In addition, the HHL transdermal system had immunosuppressive and anti-inflammatory properties, which could promote skin cell growth and tissue repair ( C). More importantly, HHL statistically improved skin barrier dysfunction due to sunlight exposure, inhibited inflammatory responses, skin erythema, and oxidative stress in chronic photo-damaged skin models, and promoted collagen expression ( B). This undoubtedly provides a new choice for using TDDS in skin photodamage. In another study, Lim et al. [ ] synthesized a palmitoylated PMSP1 peptide, called Pal-RMSP1, to improve the efficacy and stability of the RMSP1 peptide (the number of speckled protein family) for atopic dermatitis. They further loaded the Pal-RMSP1 peptide into micelles (Pal-RMSP1 DP) and liposomes (Pal-RMSP1 LP). The studies showed that Pal-RMSP1 DP and Pal-RMSP1 LP exhibited enhanced therapeutic efficacy in vivo and anti-inflammatory features in vitro. Liang et al. [ ] developed a helical-blade-strengthened co-flow focusing (HBSCF) device that effectively synthesizes ultrasmall nanoliposomes (NLPUS) smaller than 40 nm, thereby enhancing skin penetration. Further experiments showed that when CoQ10 was loaded into these NLPUS for photo-aged skin models, the system not only inhibited ultraviolet B (UVB)-induced production of matrix metalloproteinase-1 (MMP-1), but also promoted collagen type I (Col-I) synthesis in skin cells, ultimately restoring vitality to photo-damaged skin. Although the application of liposomes can promote drug penetration through physiological barriers, it still faces problems such as poor stability, rapid clearance in vivo, and insufficient control of drug release. To address the above challenges, researchers have begun integrating liposomes into hydrogels. This integration effectively prevents drug leakage while reducing the liposome's premature clearance or off-target distribution. For instance, Guo et al. [ ] recently developed a biocompatible and intelligent injectable hydrogel system that encapsulates coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10)-loaded liposomes within the hydrogel matrix. Experimental results demonstrated that this approach achieved over 16.6-fold higher bioavailability of CoQ10 compared to conventional formulations.
 Photodynamic therapy (PDT) enhances immunotherapy by inducing tumor cell death, especially for superficial tumors. Wang et al. developed a transdermal-enhancing peptide (TD)-modified positive liposome, chlorine 6 (Ce6)/CpG@Lip-TD, encapsulating photosensitizer Ce6 and immunoadjuvant CpG for photo-immunotherapy via skin application. The TD peptide and positive charge improved skin penetration, while Ce6 and CpG synergized under laser irradiation. In melanoma and breast cancer models, Ce6/CpG@Lip-TD showed significant tumor suppression and boosted systemic immune response, offering a promising topical solution that minimizes regulatory and systemic side effects for clinical use [ ]. Shen et al. created a liposome-contained microneedle delivery system for berberine hydrochloride, significantly reducing psoriasis-like symptoms and inflammation in mice compared to the model group [ ]. Nevertheless, some studies suggest that liposomes may have toxic effects, such as hematopoiesis induction, bone marrow suppression, proliferation, and carcinogenesis [ ]. Therefore, further application and expansion of liposomes in transdermal drug delivery (TDD) should include the evaluation of their toxicity.

3.1.1.2.

## Ethosome
Classical ethosome is a modified vesicle of classical liposome, which is generally comprised of phospholipids, water, and relatively high concentrations of ethanol. Studies have shown that ethosomes are more efficient in enhancing skin penetration than liposomes and simple alcohol solutions [ ]. The preparation methods of ethosomes mainly include the cold method [ ], hot method [ ], thin-film dispersion hydration method [ ], and ethanol injection-sonication method [ ], etc. Jafari et al. have detailedly described methods in preparing ethosomes in an article published recently [ ]. The ethosome has negative zeta potential, reduced particle size, and higher encapsulation efficiency than classical liposomes [ ]. The mechanism by which ethosomes enhance permeability is due to the presence of ethanol. On the one hand, the high ethanol concentration gives the ethosomes soft and highly elastic characteristics, enabling them to achieve efficient transdermal permeation through the SC and improve drug deposition [ ]. On the other hand, ethanol will lead to the instability of C-C and C-H bonds in skin phospholipids, leading to the dissolution and extraction of intercellular lipids. In addition, ethanol can also change the transition temperature and fluidity of the SC layer [ , ]. Bondi et al. [ ] have used two production processes to design and prepare ethosomes for the transdermal delivery of gossypin (a plant-derived compound). The results showed that the ethosome prepared by the ethanol injection method could control the release of gossypin and improve the permeability of the drug.
Godin et al. investigated the penetration of bacitracin-loaded ethosomes into the skin and intracellular delivery of bacitracin. The data showed that this active antibiotic peptide could be evenly distributed in the skin's epidermis, SC, and dermis. Ethosomes loaded with bacitracin could penetrate cell membranes and release active peptides directly into the cytoplasm [ ]. Shumilov et al. [ ] found that ibuprofen exhibited prolonged plasma retention and showed a higher relative bioavailability when administered transdermally using ethosomes. Animal experiments showed that the nanogel acts for at least 12 h, 5 h longer than the time of oral treatment, but did not irritate the skin. Rajput et al. [ ] prepared ethosomes loaded with curcumin to overcome the inherent poor bioavailability and permeability of drugs. To enhance the transdermal absorption and antioxidant effects of total flavonoids derived from the traditional Chinese herbal plant Vernonia anthelmintica (L.) Willd., Qin et al. [ ] mixed their prepared ethosome containing total flavonoids into a gel matrix. The resulting ethosome demonstrated high encapsulation efficiency and remarkable stability in vitro. And in vivo experiments revealed significant therapeutic improvements in vitiligo treatment, with measurable enhanced levels observed in disease-related biomarkers, such as tyrosinase (TYR), cholinesterase (CHE), and monoamine oxidase (MAO) in mice, and reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) levels. These findings collectively validate the therapeutic efficacy of the developed ethosome for vitiligo management.
Transdermal insulin delivery offers controlled release for diabetes management, but insulin's considerable molecular weight limits its absorption through the skin, requiring high doses that affect commercial viability. Nabila et al. developed ethosome and trans-ethosome formulations with a lipid-based ionic liquid in ethanol, adding surfactants for trans-ethosome formulations (Tween-80, isopropyl myristate (IPM), or Span-20). These formulations were applied to diabetic mice at 30 IU/kg insulin, significantly lowering blood glucose levels for over 15 h, compared to 2 h with injections. The ethosome and trans-ethosome formulations reduced blood glucose by 62 % and 34 %, respectively, showing promise for transdermal insulin patches in diabetes treatment [ ]. Kusumawati et al. compared liposomal and ethosomal formulations of Curcuma heyneana extract for transdermal delivery, finding ethosomes penetrated deeper and showed superior anti-aging effects in rat skin histology [ ].

3.1.1.3.

## Niosome
Niosome is an efficient vesicle containing nonionic surfactants for TDDS, of which the nonionic surfactant is reasonably priced and biocompatible with the epidermis. Niosome prolongs the dermal retention time of encapsulated APIs in the SC and increases the concentration gradient caused by passive transport, thus improving the penetration efficiency of the drug. Additionally, niosomes can also act directly with the SC to reduce the resistance of the layer of dead cells to drug penetration, thus increasing drug penetration [ ].
Compared to other nanocarriers, niosomes can load hydrophilic and lipophilic drugs, achieve higher drug encapsulation, and achieve better target site specificity [ ]. Meng et al. [ ] investigated niosomes in the hydrogel to increase the transdermal rate of celastrol in treating psoriasis. The data showed that the niosome could enhance the delivery of celastrol into the skin and ameliorate the anti-psoriasis effects of the drug. Khalil et al. developed new amphiphilic vesicles, called liposomes, to load tizanidine hydrochloride (TZN). TZN loaded into nanovesicles has also shown better skin permeability to exert muscle relaxant effects [ ]. Alsaidan et al. [ ] attempted to optimize the niosome by incorporating the niosome loaded with apigenin (AN-NS), hoping to improve the low water solubility of AN and the transdermal delivery efficiency to enhance efficacy. In vitro evaluations demonstrated that the optimized AN-NS achieved 99.65 ± 0.53 % of AN content, with a significantly higher release rate than pure AN-gel. In paw edema of rat models, the enhanced swelling inhibition rate was observed in the optimized AN-NS. Therefore, this study suggested that niosome-laden gel can improve TDD, enhancing therapeutic efficacy.
Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a significant pathogen causing skin infections due to its biofilm formation. Niosome-based gels enhance transdermal drug delivery. Hemmati et al. studied a niosomal gel for topical teicoplanin (TEC) administration, finding it effective against MRSA in infected Wister rat wounds, suggesting its potential as a wound treatment [ ]. Cyclovirobuxine D has therapeutic benefits but suffers from low solubility, affecting bioavailability and causing toxicity. Ren et al. explored its skin penetration using chemical enhancers and niosomes. Azone was the most effective enhancer, increasing penetration by 4.55-fold, while niosome encapsulation improved it by 1.50-fold. Co-encapsulation with azone boosted penetration by 8.10-fold. These results support that co-encapsulation of cyclovirobuxine D into transdermal delivery can enhance the bioavailability of drug [ ]. It is widely recognized that peptides and proteins are the most crucial components of biological cells. Recent research in biotechnology has demonstrated that peptides and proteins are the foundation of new modern drug development [ ]. However, the high molecular weight limits effective delivery in biological systems. How to selectively deliver peptide and protein-based drugs to target cells, tissues, or organs remains a major therapeutic challenge. Niosomes, with their structural stability and economics, have become a preferred nanocarrier for such medications, effectively addressing the absorption of the drugs. Niosomes enhance drug penetration and stability when incorporated into topical or transdermal formulations. In a recent study, Pal et al. [ ] analyzed niosomes comprehensively as peptide and protein drug delivery carriers. Thus, niosomes have become a valuable candidate platform in the studies of macromolecule delivery.

3.1.1.4.

## Transfersome
Transfersome is also named transformable liposome or ultra-flexible, or elastic liposome. The structure of transfersomes is similar to that of liposomes; the difference is the incorporation of different edge activators, such as sodium cholate, into the phospholipid components during the preparation of transfersomes [ , , , , ]. For this reason, transfersomes have stronger deformability and skin affinity, differential pressure permeability, and higher safety compared to traditional liposomes [ , ]. This deformability allows transfersomes to penetrate pores, the sizes of which are much smaller than those of transfersomes. For this reason, transfersomes can intact deep skin layers [ ]. Studies have shown that transfersomes can be distributed between corneocytes via ICR and have also been found in subcutaneous tissue [ ]. Zhang et al. [ ] loaded itraconazole into transfersomes and compared the permeability of vesicles with liposomes on animal skin. The results exhibited that the penetration of transfersomes gradually increased over time. The cumulative permeation of transfersomes at 48h was approximately 2.2-fold that of conventional liposomes. Duangjit et al. compared the skin permeability of cationic transfersomes loaded with meloxicam with meloxicam suspension and traditional liposomal meloxicam. There is no doubt that cationic transfersomes provide greater skin permeability of the drug. Wang et al. [ ] prepared phloretin (Phl) transfersomes (Phl-TFs) using the thin-film dispersion-ultrasonic disruption method and determined the solubility using the Log P method. The results showed that the Log P of Phl-TFs was significantly higher than that of Phl, indicating that the coating of transfersomes improved the water solubility of Phl. Based on this, they further developed a Phl-TFs gel (PTG) and evaluated its transdermal performance. It was found that the Cmax and AUC of PTG were 1.39- and 1.97-fold higher than those of phloretin solution gel (PSG), respectively, indicating that the development of PTG can improve the transdermal efficiency and bioavailability of Phl, thereby better exerting its antioxidant and anticancer effects. The combination of transferosome and gel has also been applied in inhibiting the formation of hypertrophic scars (HSs). HSs are caused by prolonged inflammation and excessive fibrosis. Therefore, simultaneously inhibiting inflammation and fibrosis is an ideal strategy for forming HS. Based on the above background, Chen et al. [ ] prepared a triamcinolone acetonide (TA) and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) co-loaded transdermal transferosome nanogels (TA/Fu-TS). Co-delivering TA and 5-Fu to macrophages and HS fibroblasts (HSF) achieved the synergistic inhibitory effect on inflammation and fibrosis. This study also provides an effective therapeutic strategy for inhibiting the formation of HS. Moreover, Dragicevic-Curic et al. have confirmed that transfersomes are superior to conventional liposomes for enhancing drug penetration and vesicle interaction with the skin [ ].
Onychomycosis, a common nail infection caused by fungi, poses treatment challenges due to poor drug penetration and recurrence. Current antifungal treatments often show limited efficacy and resistance. Mao's team developed THT, a transfersome that penetrates nails to deliver a photosensitizer (HMME) and antifungal (tavaborole), achieving a chemo-photodynamic synergistic effect. An innovative rat model demonstrated efficacy, with cure rates around 90 % and reduced fungal presence. THT effectively penetrates deeper nail layers, making it a promising topical treatment for onychomycosis [ ]. Transfersomes have to face stability issues. Lu et al. developed lyophilized PNS-loaded transfersomes (PNS-FD-TFSs) to improve stability while maintaining skin permeation. The optimized formulation used a 3:2:1 ratio of trehalose, sucrose, and phospholipid, frozen at −80 °C and lyophilized at −45 °C. PNS-FD-TFSs showed stable properties for 6 months at 4 °C, with vesicle size under 100 nm and a PDI of 0.2. Encapsulation efficiencies for five saponins ranged from 65.46 % to 95.78 %. Pharmacokinetic studies indicated faster skin permeation of PNS-FD-TFS compared to PNS liposomes [ ].

3.1.1.5.

## Exosome
Exosomes are spherical lipid-bilayer vesicles released from cells, typically 40–150 nm in size. Because exosomes have an amphiphilic lipid bilayer structure around the water core that is similar to liposomes, some researchers have begun to explore exosomes as drug delivery carriers [ , ]. The proteins within the lipid bilayer of exosomes, such as α6β4, can bind cell adhesion molecule-1 (CAM-1) and CD44 to trigger the uptake of keratinocytes via the clathrin-aided pathway [ , ]. Additionally, phosphatidylserine (PS) in the exosome membrane fuses with the lipid bilayer of the skin cell membrane to directly release contents (such as small molecule drugs or nucleic acids) into the cytoplasm. The miRNAs within exosomes, such as miR-21 and miR-31, can decrease the levels of Claudin-1 and Occludin to enhance the permeability of SC [ , ]. As we know, endogenic exosomes are crucial for the skin's physiological and pathological development. At the same time, the exogenous exosomes also have the role of repair, regeneration, and restoration of skin tissue. Therefore, exosomes have been applied in dermatology and dermatological aesthetics. Xiong et al. have provided a detailed review of the novel mechanisms and applications of exosomes in dermatology and dermatological aesthetics [ ].
In recent years, plant exosome-like nanoparticles (PELNVs) have gradually attracted the attention of researchers due to the advantages such as easy extraction, high stability, good tissue penetration, and low immunogenicity [ ]. Compared to synthetic nanoparticles and exosomes derived from mammalian cells, the PELNVs have active constituents in a parent-of-origin plan and can also be used to deliver drug molecules. Furthermore, due to their natural origin and composition, the PELNVs are less likely to be recognized by the immune system, thereby exhibiting an enhanced circulation period and better immunotolerance. For example, Abraham et al. [ ] have produced cucumber-derived exosome-like vesicles and evaluated their transdermal efficiency. The results indicated that compared to traditional formulations, the transdermal efficiency of these cucumber-derived exosome-like vesicles was doubled, and they also achieved penetration into the dermal layer. In addition, Wang et al. [ ] also discussed the application of plant exosomes as biological shuttles in TDDS, including research progress on the source, extraction, separation, and chemical composition of plant exosomes. Meanwhile, this article provides a detailed overview of the recent research progress on plant exosomes as TDDS, offering comprehensive references for developing plant exosomes. Nonetheless, exosome-related details, such as action mechanisms, are urgently needed to direct the preclinical studies and apply for medication approval [ ].
Yang et al. used tumor microenvironment immunosuppressive mechanisms to treat psoriasis. Topical inactivated melanoma tissue reduced psoriatic lesions in mice via melanoma-derived exosomes, which modulated IL-17 signaling through miRNAs, lowering T cell activation [ ]. Hu's group created a hybrid nanovesicle (HL@Exo) by fusing exosome with liposomal carriers using ultrasonic incubation and membrane extrusion, enhancing transdermal delivery. In a laser-induced model, HL@Exo improved collagen regeneration, accelerated wound healing, and exhibited anti-inflammatory effects, suggesting its potential as a non-invasive treatment for clinical exosome application [ ].

3.1.1.6.

## Micelle
The micelle is a nanoscale assembly of surfactants with a core-shell structure, which can enhance the skin penetration of impermeable or insoluble molecules [ ]. Compared to the previous types of soft vesicles, there are relatively few studies on transdermal delivery using drug-loaded micelles, and the mechanism of permeability enhancement has not been fully elucidated. Roberts et al. [ ] proposed that drugs delivered through micelles into the skin occur via two pathways: the first involves micelles disintegrating on the skin surface to release their payload, then the released drugs permeate the epidermis in free forms. The second pathway delivers intact micelles directly into the skin tissue, decomposing at the outermost layer of skin tissue (the SC or upper epidermis) to release the drugs. The decomposition of the micelles in the skin tissue and the depth of its co-permeation determine the specific delivery site of the drug. Wang et al. [ ] developed a licochalcone A (LA)-loaded glycyrrhiza acid (GA) (GA + LA) micelles for local delivery of LA into the epidermis. They discussed its interaction with the skin, mechanism of action, and in vitro and in vivo whitening effects. The results showed that GA + LA micelles could decrease the tightness of the skin surface and enhance fluidization via lipid deformation and keratin denaturation, promoting drug permeation through intracellular or intercellular pathways. In addition, Wang et al. innovatively designed an ionic liquid self-assembled nanomicelle TDDS to enhance the permeability and anti-photoaging effect of signal peptides [ ].
Cannabidiol is a safe candidate for therapeutic molecules without psychoactive effects, which may be used to treat chronic diseases and neurological illnesses such as sleep disorders, depression, epilepsy, as well as Parkinson's disease. However, such problems as low solubility in water and poor oral bioavailability limit its application. Therefore, Chu et al. [ ] attempted to develop a transdermal patch by combining micelle vesicles to enhance its therapeutic effect. The results have shown that the bioavailability of the micelle group was significantly improved compared to free cannabidiol patches, which also demonstrated the value of micelles as a transdermal formulation for drug delivery. Similarly, valsartan is a vital anti-hypertensive drug with low absorption and weak oral bioavailability caused by poor solubility and dissolution speed. In the study of Yassin et al., micellar vesicles were also selected for the transdermal delivery of valsartan. The micellar vesicles containing Tween 80 could enhance the transdermal efflux of valsartan across the rat skin without chemical enhancers (68.84 ± 3.96 μg/cm2/h), statistically higher than the free drug [ ]. It can be seen that although the application of micelles as a TDDS is relatively limited, it is undeniable that micelles remain a promising nano-delivery system.
Eprinomectin shows potential against parasitic infections. Mao et al. developed mixed micelles for transdermal delivery to enhance efficacy. Results indicated improved transdermal absorption and bioavailability of the drug in rats compared to conventional formulations. The findings suggest that these mixed micelles could be a promising nanodelivery system [ ]. A new topical formula is urgently needed for mild psoriasis patients; deucravacitinib, an oral TYK2 inhibitor, is insufficient due to complex skin issues. Engineered smart nano-therapeutics can deliver deucravacitinib topically. Hydrophobic deucravacitinib was loaded into PEG-b-PPS micelles for transdermal delivery, significantly restoring psoriatic skin architecture and reducing splenomegaly in a model. The micelle inhibited STAT3 signaling and decreased Keratin 17 and Cyclin D1 levels while alleviating ROS generation and NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis [ ].

3.1.1.7.

## Bicelle
The bicelles are nanoscale disc-like micelles due to the assembly of different chain phospholipids, which have been widely explored owing to their special structure [ ]. They represent an up-and-coming model for membrane studies in dermatological research, attributed to their lipid composition, diminutive size, and adaptability, collectively enhancing drug permeation and altering the skin's barrier functionality. Their nanostructured formations can infiltrate the stratum corneum, fortifying lipid lamellae and facilitating the incorporation of various molecules [ ]. The proper amount of long and short-chain phosphatidylcholine is assembled to form a stable bicelle with less than 50 nm. Bicelle has a disc-like shape, longer circulation times, and stronger adhesive features for microvascular vessels than the spherical vesicle. It is easier for thin bicelles (<4 nm) to penetrate the intercellular gaps (<10 nm) but not internalize due to the bulk volume. Importantly, obtaining a kinetically stable bicelle and controlling its shape are still considerable challenges that need further exploration. Some strategies, such as covalent linkage of bilayers, assembly with stable gel-phase phospholipid materials, and metal-binding peptides, are mentioned in the literature [ ]. Physically, temperature can regulate the shape of bicelle from ellipsoidal discs (10 °C) to ribbon-like structures (38 °C). Chemically, the addition of single-chain n-dodecyl-β-d-maltoside reforms the ellipsoidal disks to a round structure and changes the flattened ribbon to a worm-like shape [ ]. The regulation of vesicle shape is of great significance to promote the penetration of vesicles in the TDDS development. In a recent report, researchers explored the impact of incorporating arginine-modified peptides into bicelles. They characterized the bicells system using multiple techniques, including dynamic light scattering (DLS), cryo-transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM), and small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) etc. When interacting between bicells and stratum corneum, severe disruption of stratum corneum, lipid rearrangement, and structural damage to bicells were observed. This indicated that bicells have high permeability on the skin, providing an effective strategy for future research on TDDS [ ]. Vergara et al. [ ] also conducted an interesting study based on bicells, in which they encapsulated bicells in liposomes to construct a novel co-encapsulation system called bicosomes. Research has shown that the bicosomes can co-encapsulate, solubilize, and protect antioxidant molecules, thereby improving the delivery performance of antioxidant molecules. This study also suggests that a synergistic combination of the two drug delivery systems may provide a new approach for innovative TDDS development.
The combinatory modulation of nanocarrier characteristics is a key topic in nanomedicine, focusing on how active targeting moieties and morphology enhance cancer therapy. Rad et al. synthesized and characterized lipid discoidal and vesicular nanoparticles under 50 nm, correlating them with cancer cell internalization and tumor accumulation. Folate-associated bicelles outperformed other carriers, indicating discoidal carriers' superior targeting efficacy [ ].

3.1.2.

## Nanoemulsions
Nanoemulsions are chemically unstable o/w emulsions with an average size of about 100–1000 nm. It is also called colloidal vesicles in the submicron size range. According to the different charges on the surface of the droplets, nanoemulsions are classified as neutral, anionic, and cationic nanoemulsions [ , ]. As DDS, nanoemulsions enhance APIs’ therapeutic effects and minimize their adverse and toxic reactions. The advantages of nanoemulsions include the increased bioavailability of drugs and the improved physical stability of the APIs. In addition, the properties of nanoemulsions, such as a large specific surface area, small particle size, and low surface tension, make them better for wetting to maintain close contact with the skin [ ]. Due to the ability to deliver drugs through lipophilic barriers, nanoemulsions have been increasingly used in TDDS. Nanoemulsions can be further formulated as foams, creams, liquids, and sprays, thus increasing the number of routes and forms of TDD [ ]. Sithole et al. [ ] showed that nanoemulsions had better features in the transdermal delivery of statins, proposing their potential as novel TDDS to address the suboptimal pharmacokinetic performance observed following oral administration of these therapeutic agents. In the transdermal delivery of diclofenac, nanoemulsions increased the release and diffusion of APIs compared to other DDS, such as nanoemulsions and nano-suspensions [ ].
Li et al. [ ] developed asiaticoside-loaded nanoemulsions (ASI-NEs) and nanoemulsion-based gels (ASI-NBGs) as the TDDS, and investigated the mechanisms for transdermal delivery. ASI, a compound treating human hypertrophic scars, has low penetration rates in the SC due to its high molecular weight, poor water solubility, and lipophilic properties. The in vivo pharmacokinetic studies demonstrated that when applied topically, both ASI-NEs and ASI-NBGs effectively overcome skin barriers while maintaining sustained release over extended periods. The authors propose that the mechanisms of nanoemulsion-based transdermal delivery primarily involve enhancing drug solubility, improving the SC hydration, and changing the lipid microstructure of the SC. In another study, nanoemulsions were applied as TDDS for antibiotic delivery. It is well known that antibiotic resistance and poor biofilm penetration remain major obstacles in treating skin infections. Applying nanoemulsion can enhance the loaded drug's permeability and biological activity inside the biofilms. Maurizi et al. [ ] developed levofloxacin-loaded nanoemulsion and evaluated the antimicrobial/antibofilm activity. The results showed that the levofloxacin nanoemulsion exhibited enhanced antimicrobial activity compared to free levofloxacin.
Raynaud's phenomenon involves excessive vasoconstriction in fingers and toes due to stress or cold. Jacob et al. developed a tadalafil (TDL)-loaded nanoemulgel for non-invasive transdermal delivery, addressing oral therapy limitations. TDL nanoemulsions were created using cinnamon oil and surfactants, then incorporated into a gel. Pharmacokinetic results indicated improved absorption and prolonged effects compared to oral forms. The TDL nanoemulgel presents a promising, patient-friendly alternative for treating Raynaud's phenomenon [ ]. Sophora alopecuroides L. empyreumatic oil (SoA) shows promise for eczema and psoriasis, but has poor skin permeability; a nanoemulsion gel was created to address this. In vivo tests revealed moderate irritation from SoA oil on rabbit skin, while nanoemulsion formulations were non-irritating [ ].

## Rigid nanoparticles
3.1.3.1.

## Lipid nanoparticles
Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) constitute colloidal DDS with lipid substances. LPNs are more advanced and complex nanocarriers when compared to liposomes, mainly including solid lipid nanoparticles (SLN), nanoscale lipid carriers (NLC), and some polymer nanoparticles consisting of lipid mixtures [ ]. The NLC is called the second generation of LNPs. The excipient of NLC is a combo of liquid and solid lipids. Compared with SLNs, NLC has improved the solubility of APIs because lipophilic active ingredients are more soluble in liquid lipids, and NLC generally has a better loading capacity for lipophilic APIs. Lipid nanoparticles have been alternatively used to prepare TDDS to deliver pharmaceutical and cosmetic compounds [ , ], even CRISPR/Cas9-sgRNA gene-editing complexes (CGECs). CRISPR/Cas9-sgRNA CGECs are safer than plasmid or mRNA delivery, while LNPs are preferable to viral vectors. Cationic LNPs demonstrated therapeutic potential for melanoma treatment through transdermal delivery of CRISPR/Cas9 ribonucleoprotein complexes specifically targeting the murine Braf oncogene. Adding PEI-DOCA gives LNPs a positive charge, enhancing skin penetration and RNP delivery into melanoma cells. CRISPR/Cas9-based technology offers hope for cancer treatment, but delivery challenges remain [ ].
NLC can be stabilized in aqueous dispersion using surfactants. The lipid composition forms a lipid film suitable for inflamed or damaged skin due to the formation of occlusive effects when attached to the skin surface [ ]. Therefore, the lipid composition is considered the safest delivery vehicle [ ]. In addition, the most significant difference between NLC and SLN is that NLC contains up to 30 % oil. Therefore, when NLC sticks to the skin surface, it increases skin hydration and promotes drug penetration by reducing keratinocyte layers and widening the spaces between keratinocytes [ ]. Therefore, NLC is also considered a suitable nanocarrier for long-term skin administration [ ]. For the application of lipid nanoparticles in TDDS, Babaei et al. [ ] compared the transdermal effects of NLC loaded with lidocaine with those of nanoethosomal lidocaine. The results showed that NLC and nanoethosomes exhibited higher drug distribution within the skin than the aqueous lidocaine solution in the control group. Regarding drug loading and skin-targeted delivery, NLC performed better than nanoethosomes.
Rani et al. [ ] developed an NLC-based hesperidin (HPD) gel to effectively treat psoriasis by enhancing HPD solubility and skin permeability. Both in vitro diffusion studies and in vivo experiments using imiquimod-induced psoriasis models demonstrated that the HPD-NLC gel effectively improved psoriasis symptoms such as hyperkeratosis. This validates the advantages of NLCs as topical delivery carriers for poorly soluble natural bioactive components, in the study of Bandiwadekar et al. [ ], SLNs loaded with resveratrol (RSV) were further integrated into microneedle patches to explore their therapeutic potential for Parkinson's disease (PD). The research demonstrated that RSV-SLNs microneedle patches could effectively penetrate the skin, significantly enhancing transdermal absorption of RSV. In vivo pharmacokinetic studies revealed that compared to the control group, this delivery system showed marked improvements in both peak plasma concentration (Cmax) and area under the curve (AUC), proving that the integration of SLNs with microneedle patches as a non-invasive, efficient systemic TDDS, paving a promising avenue for advanced PD therapy. In addition, NLC-containing TDDS is also used to treat basal cell carcinoma and diabetes mellitus, which is an up-and-coming controlled-release DDS [ , , ].
PDT is promising for psoriasis, but it activates high mobility group box 1 protein (HMGB1), worsening inflammation. Su et al. combined glycyrrhizic acid (GA) with PDT using chlorin e6 (Ce6) in GA-based SLNs (GC NPs/QCS-C). Transdermal GC NPs/QCS-C with near-infrared laser improved psoriasis symptoms, promoted apoptosis of epidermal cells, and reduced inflammation. The results indicate that this nanosystem effectively combines GA and Ce6 for psoriasis treatment [ ]. Diltiazem hydrochloride, a representative API with a short half-life, encounters difficulties in skin absorption. SLNs can enhance their bioavailability. Jaiswal et al. developed a transdermal patch loading diltiazem within these nanoparticles, which demonstrated an ex vivo steady-state flux of 6.9 μg/cm2/hr and a cumulative permeation of 814.885 μg after 24 h, adhering to a Higuchi-matrix release model [ ].

3.1.3.2.

## Polymeric nanocarriers
Polymeric nanocarriers are made of natural, semi-synthetic, or synthetic polymers with sizes ranging from 10 to 1000 nm [ ]. In recent years, polymeric nanocarrier-containing TDDS has received increasing attention. Compared to other DDS, polymeric nanocarriers exhibit better biodegradability, higher drug loading capacity, sustained release, reduced side effects, and relative stability [ ]. According to the architecture, polymeric nanocarriers are usually divided into polymeric nanoparticles, polymeric micelles, and dendrimers. Drugs are typically loaded into the nanocarriers by dissolved, entrapped, encapsulated, or attached methods [ ]. Due to the higher non-deformability and mechanical strength, polymeric nanocarriers can not pass through pores within the skin, whose size is smaller than or equal to the size of the particles. It is not easier for polymeric nanocarriers to leak drugs due to slow degradation, thus better retaining the drug and promoting the diffusion of drugs into deeper skin layers [ ].
The effects of ultraviolet radiation (UVR) on normal skin include sunburn, tanning, photocarcinogenesis, photoaging, etc. Sunscreen is one of the main practices prescribed by The Skin Cancer Foundation, The American Academy of Dermatology, and The American Cancer Society adopted to reduce exposure to UVR [ ]. Shetty et al. [ ] prepared a new sunscreen product using polymeric nanoparticles. They loaded morin (a flavonoid with UV protection and antioxidant properties) into polymeric nanoparticles to enhance the delivery of APIs that ultimately permeate the skin from the nanoparticle suspension. The results showed that polymeric nanoparticles enhanced drug penetration and led to higher skin deposition of morin. This study demonstrated that polymeric nanocarriers have good skin permeability and drug retention. Kosksi et al. [ ] used the advantages of polymeric nanoparticles to improve the stability, solubility, and skin permeability of natural compounds. They incorporated brown Phlomis crinita extract into polymer nanoparticles and demonstrated the effects of this formulation in treating wound healing. Zhang et al. [ ] introduced polymeric nanocarriers for insulin delivery, further combined with microneedle systems to enhance transdermal delivery of insulin. The nanocarriers can effectively encapsulate insulin, while the microneedle array avoids the pain caused by intravenous injection during SC penetration. This combination of polymeric nanocarriers and microneedle arrays provides an effective, safe, and low-toxicity option for diabetes treatment. In addition, dendrimers and polymeric micelles have also been used in TDDS. Nevertheless, the penetrating difficulty of the skin barrier inevitably reduces the efficacy of polymeric nanocarriers, thus limiting their application [ ].
Ionic liquids with imidazolium cations have beneficial properties like cell permeability and antimicrobial activity, aiding drug delivery and solubilization. Takahashi et al. prepared chitosan-decorated poly(dl-lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA) nanoparticles with 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium hexafluorophosphate for biofilm infection treatment, showing high antibacterial activity with surfactants [ ]. Lipid nanocapsules (LNC) enhance the transdermal drug delivery of ibuprofen, showing a flux rate of 21.9 ± 0.5 μg/cm2h, which is higher than that of polymeric carriers (10.62 ± 1.84 μg/cm2h); however, polymeric carriers have higher skin accumulation [ ].

3.1.3.3.

## Metallic nanoparticles
Metal nanocarriers are extensively applied for drug delivery owing to their narrow particle size, easy surface modification, adjustable shape, and good stability [ ]. At the same time, metal nanocarriers can also improve circulation stability, half-life, desired biodistribution, and targeting to the desired target location of the drug carrier [ ]. Among them, gold nanoparticles (GNPs) and silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are widely used in TDDS.
GNPs can be used as drug carriers, components of nanocomplexes, or penetration enhancers for transdermal delivery [ ]. Sonavane et al. [ ] explored the effects of particle size on the skin penetration rate of GNPs (198 nm, 102 nm, and 15 nm). The data exhibited that the GNPs passing through the skin progressively decreased within 6 h as the particle size increased in the rat experiment. Safwat et al. [ ] used GNPs modified with two cationic ligands, benzalkonium chloride (BC) or poly (ethyleneimine) (PEI), to load 5-fluorouracil (5-FU). In mouse experiments, the skin permeability of this complex was significantly increased when compared to the 5-FU solution. Zhao et al. [ ] developed an azelamide monoethanolamine-functionalized GNPs (Au-MEA NPs). By using psoriasis and rosacea animal models, they evaluated the transdermal delivery performance and therapeutic efficacy of Au-MEA NPs after transdermal administration. The results showed that Au-MEA NPs exhibited superior stability and permeability, demonstrating practical therapeutic effects on psoriasis and rosacea without toxic side effects. The authors suggested that these newly developed Au-MEA NPs could be a safe and effective topical nanomedicine for dermatological applications.
AgNPs are widely used because of their antibacterial, antiviral, and antioxidant properties [ ]. Makeen et al. [ ] developed AgNPs loaded with sesamol (SML-AgNPs) and conducted further optimized studies (Opt-SML-AgNPs). Then they incorporated Opt-SML-AgNPs into carbomer gel (Opt-SML-AgNPG) for wound healing. Experimental results demonstrated that Opt-SML-AgNPG significantly extended the release time and enhanced permeability compared to conventional SML-gel formulations. Compared to SML solutions, Opt-SML-AgNPG exhibited higher antioxidant activity, which is beneficial for wound healing. Nayak et al. [ ] prepared a complex material called Chitosan-AgNPs-Sericin, composed of AgNPs, sericin, and chitosan. After 50 μg of this preparation was applied to mouse skin for one week, the results showed that this complex helped to counteract the structural and cellular deformation of epidermoid A431 skin cancer cells. This experiment may provide a new strategy to treat skin cancer.
Salvianolic acid B (Sal B) has potential for treating skin photoaging, but poor skin permeability necessitates a novel transdermal formulation. To address this, chitosan-coated GNPs (CS-GNPs) were prepared using a microwave method, optimizing co-encapsulation of CS-GNPs and Sal B with Pluronic F-127 and HA(CAS@FH). CAS@FH showed a fluid state at 25 °C and gelation at 36.3 °C, enhancing skin permeability and retention. In vivo, a photoaging mouse model confirmed CAS@FH's efficacy in reducing photoaging and increasing collagen levels. Thus, CAS@FH enhances Sal B delivery and therapeutic effects on skin photoaging, offering potential clinical strategies [ ]. Friedman et al. created fluorescent GNPs with controlled size and shape and evaluated their follicular penetration through histological analysis of human skin sections. Their findings indicated that the optimal size of polymeric particles was 250 nm, and gold nanostars were superior to spherical ones in follicular penetration, highlighting the significance of physical properties in nanocarrier design for the pilosebaceous unit [ ].

3.1.3.4.

## Magnetic nanoparticles
Magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) are a class of DDS that can be manipulated by external magnetic fields, typically possessing sole physicochemical features, high surface area, and excellent biocompatibility [ ]. Nowadays, the application of MNPs in DDS has received extensive attention. Murthy [ ] and Murthy et al. [ ] attempted to enhance the permeation of APIs under magnetic fields, demonstrating that magnetophoresis is another practical approach for improving drug penetration through the SC. Nevertheless, since most APIs lack magnetic properties, more and more studies have begun to explore embedding drugs into magnetic nanocarriers to achieve magnetic characteristics. Iron oxide magnetic nanoparticles have been extensively studied in TDDS due to their sensitive response to external magnetic fields and outstanding biocompatibility. Choi et al. [ ] prepared hyaluronan (HA)-superparamagnetic microneedles (HA-MMN) with magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles and analyzed their surface, morphological features, and permeability in pig epidermis. The data confirmed that HA-MMN has strong mechanical strength for skin penetration and can be further applied to in vivo molecular imaging and therapy. Chen et al. [ ] prepared methotrexate-loaded silk fibroin magnetic nanoparticles and investigated the effects of alternating magnetic fields on the permeation of magnetic nanoparticle drugs. The study found that under the same intensity of the magnetic field, alternating magnetic fields increased drug penetration more effectively than the stationary magnetic field.
In addition, Rao et al. [ ] developed an anticancer agent for managing skin tumors based on MMN, linking epirubicin (EPI) to functionalized superparamagnetic iron-oxide nanoparticles (SPION). In vitro transdermal studies have demonstrated that EPI-SPION can penetrate deep into the skin driven by an external magnetic field. This study provides new ideas for the treatment of skin cancer.
Zhang et al. developed a transdermal nanoplatform (+)T-SiDs with a superparamagnetic iron oxide core, modified with cationic lipids, fluorescent dye (DiR), and peptide TD, and then loaded with doxorubicin, showing high transdermal efficiency and tumor accumulation in MR/NIR imaging. The combination of chemo-photothermal therapy is more effective than individual treatments, presenting a promising dual-modal imaging-guided approach for superficial tumors with low toxicity [ ]. Rakoczy et al. Further investigated how different electromagnetic fields (EMFs) influenced naproxen (NAP) permeability using pig skin. Results indicated that RMF at 50 Hz significantly increased NAP permeability (1461.40 ± 256.15 μg/cm2) when compared to the control (267.57 ± 41.74 μg/cm2), maximizing steady-state flux and permeability coefficient [ ].

## Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)
Recent advances in metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have highlighted their potential as versatile carriers for TDDS. As a novel drug delivery system, the MOF platform, incorporating biocompatible linkers and nanoscale pore architectures, enables controlled release kinetics and enhanced skin permeation. For instance, two transition metal-based MOFs described by Liang et al. demonstrated stable drug loading. They sustained transdermal release over 72 h, attributed to their optimized pore size and surface functionality [ ]. Similarly, a 3D porous Gd-based MOF engineered with hydrophilic surface modifications exhibits improved loading ratio and higher thermal stability than conventional liposomes [ ]. Moreover, Wang et al. presented a thermally and water-stable 3D anionic Zn-MOF (bcu topology) that loaded 17.2 wt% % of the anticancer drug 5-FU, released 93.3 % of the drug steadily over 72 h without a burst effect [ ]. Zhang et al. summarized the potential and challenges of University of Oslo (UiO)-based MOFs in antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer applications and proposed AI-assisted material development prospects [ ]. In a recently published review, Liu et al. [ ] explored the synergistic application potential of MOFs and hydrogel microneedles. As porous materials, MOFs have characteristics including high porosity, large specific surface area, biocompatibility, etc. Therefore, combining MOFs can enhance hydrogel microneedles' drug loading efficiency while improving microneedles' mechanical properties and the skin permeability of the drug. In addition, this article discusses the application of this strategy in different diseases, such as wound healing, scar management, acne treatment, and tumor suppression. It provides an in-depth analysis of its role in advancing percutaneous therapies.
Zhang et al. developed a transdermal nanoplatform (+)T-SiDs with a superparamagnetic iron oxide core, modified with cationic lipids, fluorescent dye (DiR), and peptide TD, and then loaded with doxorubicin, showing high transdermal efficiency and tumor accumulation in MR/NIR imaging. The combination of chemo-photothermal therapy is more effective than individual treatments, presenting a promising dual-modal imaging-guided approach for superficial tumors with low toxicity [ ]. Rakoczy et al. Further investigated how different electromagnetic fields (EMFs) influenced naproxen (NAP) permeability using pig skin. Results indicated that RMF at 50 Hz significantly increased NAP permeability (1461.40 ± 256.15 μg/cm2) when compared to the control (267.57 ± 41.74 μg/cm2), maximizing steady-state flux and permeability coefficient [ ].

## Industrial scale-up
Nanocrystals improve solubility and bioavailability via top-down, bottom-up, or combined methods. Patented products like Nanocrystals®, SmartCrystals®, and DissoCubes® utilize these methods. Nanosuspensions can be solidified through lyophilization or spray drying to prevent aggregation. Both techniques are known for scalability and continuous production [ ]. Cost and complexity have limited the industrial use of nanoparticles. Several key challenges arise in the industrial-scale production of nanocarriers such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, solid SLNs, and micelles, including particle size control, drug encapsulation efficiency, stability, and batch-to-batch consistency. For liposomes, the main challenges include maintaining consistent size and drug encapsulation efficiency. Traditional methods like thin-film hydration and reverse-phase evaporation are challenging to scale up and produce. Microfluidics and high-pressure homogenization offer promising solutions, providing better control over particle size and encapsulation efficiency. Polymeric nanoparticles face challenges such as size distribution control and solvent residence. Processes such as solvent evaporation, nanoprecipitation, and continuous flow reactors have been developed to improve scalability and ensure particle size and drug loading consistency. SLNs encounter issues related to particle aggregation and the control of drug release profiles. High-pressure homogenization and melt emulsification are commonly used in large-scale SLN production, addressing these issues. Micelles, on the other hand, suffer from challenges related to surfactant purity and drug loading capacity. Solvent evaporation and nanoprecipitation are used to enhance production efficiency and encapsulation efficiency. Overall, the industrial-scale production of nanocarriers requires careful optimization of processing conditions, innovative equipment, and novel manufacturing processes to overcome these challenges and achieve consistent, scalable, and cost-effective production [ , , ].
In recent years, microfluidic technology and three-dimensional (3D) printing have been increasingly applied in developing novel nanocarrier formulations. Microfluidic technology allows precise control over all operational parameters during processing. Once optimal conditions are set through experimental optimization, subsequent preparations can eliminate batch-to-batch variations [ , ]. Also, microfluidic systems enable production processes to use relatively small amounts of solvents, significantly reducing environmental impact [ ]. 3D printing is a technology suitable for large-scale preparation, enabling pharmaceutical production of personalized medicine [ ]. Continuous-flow microreactors represented a cost-effective fabrication tool prepared with 3D-printed molds and adhesive tape for PDMS channels, creating reactors with a 1 mm2 cross-section. Their flow rates were up to 20 ml/min, with fabrication time of <1 day and material costs around £5. The reactors could produce silver nanoparticles and liposomes, outperforming traditional methods. Numerical simulations indicated the method's scalability for biotechnology and nanomedicine [ ]. Sommonte et al. explored 3D printing (3DP) and microfluidic technology (MF) to create novel “diamond-shaped” equipment for producing lysozyme-loaded LPs. Using computer-aided design (CAD) software, various MF device geometries were designed and printed with high-resolution 3DP of digital light processing (DLP), optimizing MF-based DLP conditions. Lysozyme-loaded PEGylated liposomes were produced, passing stability tests, showing good encapsulation efficacy and great monodisperse. In vitro release studies indicated 93 % of cargo release within 72 h, demonstrating the effective synergy of MF and 3DP [ ]. Lin et al. developed a 3D-printed omnidirectional sheath flow-enabled microfluidics (OSEM) equipment to produce mRNA-laden LNPs using the same LNPs as Moderna's vaccine, encapsulating mRNA for the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein. The durable methacrylate-based OSEM equipment supports flow rates of 1 mL per minute. It features a four-channel hydrodynamic flow-focusing region integrated with a staggered herringbone mixer (SHM) structure for efficient LNP formation. The OSEM equipment achieves a fivefold enhancement in throughput (60 ml per min) compared to commercial mixers while maintaining precise control over particle characteristics. This system consistently produces LNPs with a size of sub-90 nm, narrow polydispersity indices (PDI, 2–8 %), and exceptional mRNA encapsulation efficiencies (exceeding 90 %). These performance metrics establish a technically superior and economically viable alternative for scalable manufacturing of therapeutic LNPs [ ].

## Artificial intelligence
The application of AI can clarify the influences of factors such as skin status and drug properties in the TDDS field ( A) [ , ]. With the rapid development of AI, scientists have begun to try to integrate TDDS with AI to achieve more accurate, personalized, and effective drug delivery using techniques such as Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Deep Neural Networks (DNN), COMSOL, BioSIM, Set Covering Machine (SVM), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), etc [ , ]. They utilize the aggregated conformal prediction models to predict chemical permeation rates through human skin, aiding decision-making. Models using random forest and support vector machine methods were based on data from 211 compounds [ ]. Researchers aim to accurately measure xenobiotic permeability coefficients while simplifying the process with predictive models. Most quantitative structure-permeability models rely on big data and AI-driven methods, and intense learning is advancing this goal. Ita et al. used various neural networks to predict skin permeability from a dataset of 476 records of 145 chemicals, demonstrating deep learning's effectiveness in this area ( B) [ ]. Furthermore, the integration of AI can optimize the formulation design of TDDS. For instance, AI-assisted drug development can enhance therapeutic outcomes by optimizing TDDS based on the data of pharmaceutical sciences and pathological information [ ]. AI can customize the diagnosis and treatment plan according to the patient's state and treatment needs, to adjust the drug delivery accordingly [ ]. Nevertheless, challenges such as formulation development and stability issues still warrant attention. Similarly, AI can examine the complex relationship between physiological factors, drug properties, and formulation components to foretell drug behavior. Therefore, AI can design new drug carriers, predict drug release profiles, and optimize drug dosages based on machine learning algorithms, significantly optimizing DDS [ , ]. Badger et al. [ ] combined digital light processing (DLP) printing technology with Quality by Design (QbD)-assisted AI algorithms to develop microneedles for delivering lipophilic APIs. The study demonstrated how AI-powered semi-supervised machine learning methods could optimize needle morphology and print fidelity. In a recently published review, Tarek et al. [ ] focus on psoriasis treatment and discuss the significant impact of machine learning and deep learning algorithms on drug discovery, drug reuse, and the design of new molecules or drug delivery carriers.
 With the continuous progress of AI technology, more and more research on AI in TDDS has been reported. For example, Takayama et al. [ ] developed an ANN model to optimize the transdermal hydrogel of ketoprofen ( C). First, a factorial experiment design was employed to create system models composed of multiple formulation factors. Subsequently, artificial neural networks were utilized to perform targeted predictions based on the response variables of these models. It should be noted that individual optimal responses vary across different parameter settings. Finally, some typical case studies were introduced. In optimization studies, additional computational analyses are often required to optimize all response variables simultaneously. Godavarthy et al. [ ] developed a screening algorithm to identify chemical permeation enhancers for TDD by integrating the neural networks' genetic algorithms with theory-based quantitative structure-property relationship models ( D). Suriyaamporn et al. developed crosslinked hydrogel microneedle patches (cHMNs) with 5-FU-HPβCD-laden PEGylated liposomes to improve treatment results and decrease toxicity, using AI for design and evaluation. Different AI models were evaluated for accuracy, leading to the selection of an optimal formulation with favorable mechanical and physicochemical properties, swelling phenomenon, effective skin permeation, and safety in cell culture, demonstrating potential for treating non-melanoma skin cancers due to deeper penetration into SC layers (467.87 ± 65.12 nm) and higher permeation rate (41.78 ± 4.26 %) [ ]. Ammar et al. engineered a novel TDDS comprising vardenafil hydrochloride (VRD)-encapsulated ethosomal systems for managing pulmonary arterial hypertension. The VRD-loaded ethosomal vesicle was prepared through systematic variation of phosphatidylcholine concentrations (15, 10, and 5 mg ml−1) and ethanol content (40 %, 30 %, and 20 %, v/v), optimized to invasomes with terpenes. The invasomes characterization, including zeta potential, vesicle size, entrapment efficiency, and permeation rates (cumulative percentage of drug permeation after hrs, Q), was performed based on the pharmacokinetic modeling. The optimized ethosomal system had a negative surface charge (−20.3 mV), spherical vesicles (159.9 nm), and high entrapment efficiency (81.3 % EE). The system displayed controlled release kinetics characterized by a limited initial burst (25.4 % Q0.5h) and sustained drug liberation (85.3 % cumulative release at Q12h), accompanied by enhanced transdermal permeation evidenced by a steady-state flux of 6.4 μg cm−2 h−1. This system exhibited minor histopathologic changes in rats and effective skin permeation. Transdermal application resulted in delayed Tmax, lower Cmax, and increased AUC0-24h in geriatrics and adults, which may be a promising TDDS for VRD [ ]. Overall, AI is revolutionizing drug delivery technologies by facilitating the development of personalized, targeted, and adaptive therapeutic strategies. The integration of AI and nanotechnologies holds great promise for the innovation and development of TDDS [ , ]. The AI-driven "AI-in-the-loop lifecycle for intelligent TDDS" system will enhance large-scale manufacturing and clinical wearable monitoring ( ). AI commonly controls nanomedicine-containing TDDS delivery based on physical assistive technology.
 Generally, we ignore the adhesion percentage of TDDS onto the skin surface. The current evaluation method is based on eye observation. Visually estimated percent adhesion by trained clinicians or trial participants creates variability and bias. To improve the accuracy and consistency of adhesion calculation of TDDS onto the skin, Wang et al. analyzed 796 images using the state-of-the-art -art deep learning models. The combination of U-net and ResNet-101 backbone network, and the model trained using training data augmentation (TDA), test-time augmentation (TTA), wrapped TTA (WTTA), and weighted cross entropy loss, achieved the highest mean intersection over union (mIoU) of 0.675 on the test data. More complex models such as Unet+ and DeppLabV3 did not further improve the mIoU value. The developed models are adept at segmenting a TDDS image to estimate adhesion percentage to assess TDDS efficacy [ ].

## Physical assistive technology
While nanocarriers effectively modulate drug release and enhance skin permeation through tailored carriers' properties, their full therapeutic potential is further unlocked when combined with physical assistive technologies that actively overcome the SC's formidable barrier function. uses color intensity to show the compatibility strength of the combination of nanocarriers and physical assistive technology, providing a comprehensive view for selecting combinations of carriers and assistive technologies. Physical assistive technologies refer to energy-based or mechanical methods that temporarily disrupt the skin barrier to enhance drug permeation [ , ]. These technologies overcome the SC's limitations by creating microchannels (e.g., microneedles) to bypass the SC or applying external energy (e.g., iontophoresis, electroporation, sonophoresis, magnetophoresis) to affect the structure of the SC or change drug distribution into the skin layer, as shown in . They enable the transport of small molecules and macromolecules (e.g., vaccines, peptides) with minimal invasive procedures and improved patient compliance.
 3.4.1.

## Microneedles
Microneedles are micron-sized needles that are widely used in the development of TDDS because of their advantages of being safe, painless, convenient, non-invasive, and highly effective for drug delivery. Microneedles can be used to promote drug penetration in the transdermal delivery field by breaching the SC to enhance the entry of drugs into the dermis and fortify their effects [ ]. Microneedles can be categorized into dissolving, solid, hollow, and coated [ ]. Solid microneedles create microchannels through meticulously designed small needles that penetrate the skin's outermost layer, enabling direct delivery of therapeutic agents [ , ]. Microneedles typically range from 100 to 3000 μm in length, with 250–1500 μm being most common. The tip diameter and base width vary for effective skin penetration and drug release [ ]. However, these solid microneedles are typically non-disposable, posing infection risks in clinical settings that require strict sterilization protocols post-use. Additionally, the microneedle-induced microchannels may also trigger inflammatory responses [ ]. Coated microneedles are composed of the same materials as solid microneedles, with the key difference being that therapeutics are coated onto their outer surfaces through methods such as spray coating, dip coating, or piezoelectric inkjet printing [ ]. Compared to solid microneedles, these coated microneedles enable more concentrated drug delivery. However, their relatively lower drug loading capacity makes them unsuitable for high-dose administration scenarios [ ]. In contrast, hollow microneedles have diversified manufacturing materials including polymers, metals, silicon etc [ ]. The hollow microneedles utilize their hollow structure as drug delivery channels [ ], enabling precise drug delivery into the skin to reduce injection-related discomfort and infection risks. Although hollow microneedles are now applied in glucose detection systems [ ] and vaccine administration [ , ], their production typically requires complex technologies such as 3D printing, photolithography, and etching. The high costs and technical complexities of these manufacturing processes and equipment requirements hinder the broader adoption of hollow microneedles. The emergence of dissolving microneedles has provided a safe, effective, and patient-friendly strategy for TDD [ ]. The preparation of dissolving microneedles first requires selecting biocompatible and degradable materials such as dextran, HA, chondroitin sulfate, polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), etc., followed by construction using techniques like micro-molding, stretch lithography, and 3D printing. Although the application of dissolving microneedles has been limited in transitioning from animal models to human trials due to size discrepancies and limited drug delivery capacity [ ]. However, they remain a promising TDDS. With technological advancements, we believe more innovative research on dissolving microneedles will be further revealed. Although different microneedles are used in TDD, each microneedle type has disadvantages. In recent years, hydrogels have begun to be used to design and produce new microneedles. Hydrogels are made of hydrophilic cross-linked polymers that absorb large amounts of water, which may respond to varied stimuli. Based on this mechanism, hydrogel-microneedles can control or sustain drug release out of the needles. This combination has advantages over conventional systemic and topical treatment [ ]. Liu et al. [ ] loaded an HA-based system containing β-glucan and fucoidan into a dissolving microneedle system. The microneedle system was applied in inflammatory disease management and antigen delivery by regulating immune responses and reactive oxygen species (ROS). For example, phenylethyl resorcinol (PR) is a potent complexinase inhibitor and is the most powerful whitening drug available [ ]. A randomized, double-masked clinical trial was carried out to observe the safety and efficacy of nanosized PR-containing microneedle-aided gel (NPRMG) for treating dark circles. Twenty patients recruited in the clinical trial were subsequently randomized into two parallel groups. In the experimental group, the right orbit and the left one of patients were treated with NPRMG twice a week and topical PR gel once a day, respectively. The control group received nano-microneedle-containing gel without PR (NMGWPR). The data showed that NPRMG treatment improved infraorbital hyperpigmentation better than PR gel alone. PR gel alone reduced infraorbital hyperpigmentation, but with poor durability. In contrast, NMGWPR showed no significant improvement in infraorbital pigmentation, suggesting that nano-microneedles improved dark circles by facilitating drug penetration rather than mechanical stimulation [ ]. This study showed that NPRMG provides a safe and reliable method to treat infraorbital dark circles. Wang et al. [ ] developed proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC)-len microneedle patches (PROTAC-MNs), which are used to treat androgenetic alopecia (AGA) by patching with a microneedling structure. The PROTAC-MNs can be thrust into the skin to create microscopic pores, releasing the drug into nearby hair follicles. Also, microneedles can be used to load nanocarriers for cancer treatment [ ], diabetes treatment [ ], and immunotherapy [ ]. In addition, studies have shown that microneedles can also be used in the treatment of acne [ ], wound healing [ ], viral infections [ ], vitiligo [ ], CLE [ ], psoriasis [ , ], and other diseases.

## Iontophoresis
Iontophoresis is a technique that promotes the transdermal diffusion of drugs by applying an electric field force. The most significant advantage of iontophoresis is that the magnitude and speed of the electricity can be controlled to achieve personalized treatment. It is difficult for large-molecule drugs to cross the skin barrier, whereas the traditional injection method often leads to adverse reactions such as pain, skin damage, and inflammation [ ]. Iontophoresis facilitates the transdermal delivery of macromolecules while mitigating injection-related drawbacks associated with conventional invasive administration methods [ ]. The efficiency of iontophoresis is influenced by several parameters: current density (typically between 0.5 mA/cm2 and 17 mA/cm2), drug concentration (with flux reaching a plateau at higher concentrations), and the physicochemical properties of the drug (such as size, charge, and lipophilicity) [ ]. Furthermore, applying a portable iontophoresis device has increased the skin's moisture content. This increase leads to improved drug absorption and deeper penetration, with a recorded penetration depth of approximately 320 μm [ ].
Fukuta et al. [ ] conducted a comparative analysis of therapeutic efficacy between transdermal drug delivery systems (TDDS) incorporating etanercept through iontophoresis and conventional intradermal injection for psoriasis treatment in mice models. The results showed that the therapeutic effect of enalapril-containing TDDS based on iontophoresis was significantly better than that of intradermal injection. Studies have shown that iontophoresis delivers ionic or peptide drugs more effectively. The FDA-approved lidocaine transdermal patch, fentanyl transdermal patch, and sumatriptan transdermal patch are based on this technique to improve the transdermal permeability of the drug [ ]. In addition, a clinical study showed that using growth factors with the iontophoresis technique could treat and prevent alopecia [ ]. Kim et al. [ ] developed a smart TDDS based on iontophoresis technology for cancer treatment. Iontophoresis can provide trigger and stop signals by alternating anodic and cathodic iontophoresis to control drug penetration precisely. This research undoubtedly provides a more intelligent idea for cancer treatment ( A) [ , , , , , ]. A CELLADEEP patch combining iontophoresis techniques and microneedle strategies accelerated the wound healing. This provides a promising non-invasive treatment option for wound healing [ ].
 Hypertrophic scarring (HS) is a raised, fibrotic, and indurated dermal lesion. Current clinical treatment using intralesional drug injections often results in uneven distribution of medications within the scar tissue. Chen et al. [ ] developed a paper battery-powered iontophoresis-driven microneedles patch (PBIMNP) based on iontophoresis technology. This device integrated microneedles with particle delivery techniques to achieve 90.19 % active drug delivery into scar tissue. This innovation provides an up-and-coming self-administration platform for HS treatment.

3.4.3.

## Laser
The laser-aided penetration technology utilizes laser light to form photomechanical waves to open dense pores in the skin, thus promoting transdermal absorption of the drug [ ]. These photomechanical waves are compression waves that enhance SC layers' permeability and destroy cell membrane integration. The waves caused by pressure differ from the cavitation formed by Ultrasound. Factors such as rise time, duration, and peak pressure can be changed to modulate the transport efficiency of macromolecules entering the deep skin [ ]. Furthermore, the changes in the above factors depend on the wavelength, fluency, and pulse duration [ ]. In laser-assisted delivery, the channel depth increases with laser fluence up to approximately 500–700 μm, beyond which drug uptake plateaus. On the other hand, increasing fractional density enhances uptake only up to approximately 5 % surface coverage. Additionally, the surrounding coagulation zone affects both absorption and safety, necessitating a balance of parameters depending on the specific indication [ , ].
Currently, the most used laser types are fractional carbon dioxide laser, erbium-infused yttrium aluminum garnet (Er: YAG) lasers, 755 nm ps alexandrite laser, narrow-band ultraviolet B (NB-UVB) phototherapy devices, and Q-switched neodymium-infused YAG laser (Q-switched Nd: YAG) [ ]. Olesen et al. [ ] exposed pig lumbar and abdominal skin to 6.4 mmol/L vismodegib using a carbon dioxide laser of 10600 nm at 0 or 80 mJ/microbeam and 5 % density, which results showed that carbon dioxide laser irradiation resulted in 7.6-fold higher maximum concentrations of vismodegib in the skin over 24 h than those of the control animals. This suggests that the laser helps to enhance drug penetration into the skin. Through the retrieval of different databases, Zhang et al. [ ] believed that local treatment of minoxidil-aided laser and light for alopecia was effective and safe. Yang et al. [ ] explored the promoting effects of carbon dioxide fractional laser irradiation on API permeation for hypertrophic scar management. In this study, rhodamine 110 (R110) and rhodamine 6G (R6G), as hydrophobic and hydrophilic model molecules, were loaded into silk nanofiber hydrogels (SNFs) (R6G/R110-laden SNFs). In the in vivo experimental protocol, hypertrophic scar models on rabbit ears were initially subjected to carbon dioxide fractional laser irradiation, followed by topical R6G/R110-encapsulated SNFs to the treated lesions ( B). The results showed that combining carbon dioxide fraction laser irradiation with R6G/R110-laden SNFs can effectively enhance the delivery efficiency of hydrophilic API.

3.4.4.

## Electroporation
Electroporation represents a biophysical technique that utilizes temporary high-voltage pulsed electric fields to induce the formation of transient, reversible small pores within the phospholipid bilayer structures of cellular membranes. This process facilitates enhanced permeability of APIs through temporary membrane destabilization [ ]. Key electrical pulse parameters, including field strength/voltage across the skin, pulse duration, and the number/sequence of pulses, are critical in regulating pore formation and molecular flux. Application of ≥5 pulses at ∼80–710 V significantly enhances transport. Higher settings, such as 300 V for 200 ms with 20 pulses, further improve the delivery of small molecules (such as 5-FU) but may also increase discomfort [ ]. The increased transport of molecules by electroporation is mainly based on the enhanced diffusion of drugs and electrophoresis parameters. The electroporation parameters include pulse wave lengths, duration, number of pulses, and distance between electrodes [ ]. This technique has been successfully applied to promote the permeation of several drugs, such as benzoic aci [ ], timolol [ ], etc. Due to the high-voltage pulses in electroporation, this method's short- and long-term safety is still to be investigated [ ]. Denet et al. [ ] found that high-voltage pulses sometimes induce muscle contractions. They could lead to side effects, including pain, increased skin hydration, transient impairment of barrier function, and transient increase in blood flow. Chen et al. [ ] developed a mathematical model of the relationship between cumulative permeation (Qn) and drug characteristics (including logP, pKa, S, and cumulative permeation time t). They elaborated on electroporation's duration and percutaneous depth in promoting drug absorption. Showed that electroporation promoted percutaneous absorption of the drug.
Hung et al. [ ] proposed a new electroporation method to deliver siRNA and DNA to overcome the difficulty of providing macromolecules to the skin. They developed a novel methodology by integrating a flexible interdigitated electroporation array (FIEA) with a commercial microneedle roller. As shown in C, the microneedle roller provoked the SC and formed many aqueous pores in the deeper epidermal/dermal layers. In contrast, FIEA could closely contact the tissue surface and generate a uniform electric field. This combination shows excellent potential in treating skin diseases or epidermal/dermal vaccines.

3.4.5.

## Magnetophoresis
Magnetophoresis is a transdermal permeation technique that enhances the diffusion of drugs into the skin by using a magnetic field to regulate the permeability of SC, thereby promoting the transdermal absorption of drugs [ ]. The magnetic field can be divided into static and pulsed electromagnetic fields. Murthy et al. assessed the effects of magnetic field strengths on the entry of lidocaine into the skin tissue, and there is a positive relationship between the permeation efficiency of drugs and magnetic strength [ ]. Data have confirmed that static magnetic fields can enhance the transdermal permeability of small-molecule drugs. The magnetophoresis technique can transdermally deliver medicines such as salbutamol sulfate, benzoic acid, terbutaline sulfate, lidocaine (a diamagnetic API), etc. [ ]. OBJ Ltd. combines a customized alcohol solvent-based formulation of ibuprofen hydrogel containing propylene glycol, isopropanol alcohol, and water with OBJ magnetically enhanced backing to promote ibuprofen absorption by enhancing the diffusion and partition of APIs in the skin [ ]. Because it does not require an external energy unit and can be miniaturized, this technique can act on transdermal patches or topical creams to improve the permeation of APIs into the skin.

3.4.6.

## Sonophoresis
Ultrasound can facilitate drug absorption into the epidermis, dermis, and skin appendages by stimulating micro-vibrations of components within the epidermis of the skin, as well as the movement of APIs due to the increasing kinetic energy of hydrophilic molecules and macromolecules, namely Sonophoresis. Cavitation and thermal effect are its two action mechanisms. Cavitation denotes the dynamic generation and collapse of vapor-filled microcavities within the SC layer, resulting in structural compromise of the SC's lamellar organization. In contrast, the thermal effect manifests as a transient elevation in epidermal temperature following ultrasonic exposure, facilitating enhanced transdermal permeation kinetics through temperature-dependent augmentation of molecular diffusion coefficients [ ]. Ultrasound, which was first utilized for TDD in 1954, operates within three distinct frequency ranges: low-frequency sonophoresis (LFS; 20–100 kHz), therapeutic sonophoresis (0.7–3 MHz), and high-frequency sonophoresis (HFS; >3 MHz). Initially, HFS was primarily studied for its potential in skin penetration. However, subsequent research revealed that LFS significantly enhances skin permeability. For example, a 20 kHz ultrasound (125 mW/cm2, 100 ms pulses) is up to 1000 times more permeability than a 1 MHz ultrasound (2 W/cm2). In 2004, LFS was FDA-approved for the delivery of lidocaine, notably reducing the onset of anesthesia from 60 min to just 5 min, with no significant side effects reported [ ].
In recent years, Boddu et al. reported using ultrasound-aided transdermal delivery-laden econazole nitrate to treat Raynaud's phenomenon [ ]. However, the lack of convenient ultrasound equipment is still a problem facing TDDS [ ]. Agomelatine (AGM), an antidepressant with low oral bioavailability due to hepatic metabolism, was investigated for enhanced transdermal delivery using LFS and novasomes. The optimized novasomal gel system demonstrated significantly improved drug permeability and bioavailability, achieving a flux of 26.7 μg cm−2 h−1 and an increase ratio of 11.9 when compared with AGM dispersion, indicating its potential for effective transdermal administration [ ]. Liu et al. [ ] conducted an intriguing study comparing the enhanced effects of microneedle transdermal delivery and low-frequency ultrasound on drug penetration of tetramethylpyrazine hydrochloride (TMPH) via transdermal delivery. Their findings confirmed that both vibration microneedles and low-frequency ultrasound systems could effectively enhance skin penetration of TMPH, but the vibrating microneedle system demonstrated significantly stronger penetration-enhancing effects.

## Combination of nanotechnology and physical assistive techniques
A combination of nanotechnology and physical techniques has been explored to improve the delivery efficiency of TDDS.
3.5.1.

## Nanocarriers and microneedles
Triptolide (TP) is a vital API purified from the herb Tripterygium wilfordii, which has confirmed its effectiveness in the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [ , ]. Nevertheless, the toxicity of TP to the gastrointestinal, liver, heart, and hematopoietic systems limits its application [ ]. Therefore, TDDS can be used to overcome its shortcomings and realize the clinical use of TP. Chen et al. [ ] developed a triptolide-laden liposome-containing hydrogel patch (TP-LHP). Afterward, TP-LHP was loaded into a microneedle array to promote transdermal absorption. The in vivo pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetic data of TP-LHP in the collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) rat model exhibited that the integration of TP-LHP and microneedles could alleviate the syndrome of articular swelling and decrease the levels of fetal liver kinase-1/4 (FLK 1/4) as well as hypoxia-inducible factor-1α. The microneedle enhances drug delivery while TP-LHP eliminates the inherent liver metabolism and digestive toxicity of tripterygium glycosides. Qu et al. [ ] investigated the effects of liposome size and surface charge in microneedles on the therapeutic efficacy. The results showed that 250 nm and 450 nm liposomes could elongate the retention time in the skin in the psoriatic skin, as well as enhance the long-term in vivo cellular internalization efficiency of APIs. Meanwhile, cationic liposomes with a size of 250 nm had an extremely long skin retention time (132 h) due to a stronger charge. It can be seen that the combination of nanotechnology and microneedle techniques may be a safer and more efficient treatment strategy. Zhang et al. [ ] also proposed a method for anti-acne therapy using ROS-responsive microneedle patches. The design of the microneedles can enhance the delivery to the dermis lesions. At the same time, the sustained release of ROS plays a crucial role in exerting antibacterial effects and minimizing side effects, thus overcoming the poor bactericidal effect of traditional creams due to the limited transmission via hair follicles and sebaceous glands.
It is well known that diabetes mellitus is recognized as a long-term metabolic disorder manifesting as chronically heightened blood glucose concentrations. Traditional diabetes management has predominantly relied on the recurrent subcutaneous administration of long-acting or short-acting insulin formulations to achieve therapeutic maintenance of euglycemic conditions, often leading to poor patient compliance. The glucose-sensing enzyme (GOx) can modulate the release of the loaded insulin through polymer degradation, structural transformation, or competition for glucose binding [ ]. Hu et al. [ ] developed H2O2-responsive polymeric vesicles (PVs) containing glucose oxidase (GOx) and insulin. Then, the PVs were further integrated into the percutaneous microneedle patch ( B). As shown in A, when microneedle patches are applied to diabetic mice, glucose can passively diffuse across the membrane and undergo enzymatic oxidation catalyzed by GOx within the cavity to produce H2O2. The produced H2O2 converts the structure of the methyl polyethylene glycol-branched phenylboronic ester (PBE)-modified poly serine, named mPEG-b-P (Ser-PBE), that constitutes the vesicle into water-soluble groups, leading to vesicle disintegration and the release of insulin. Both in vitro ( C and D) and in vivo ( E) tests have shown that the patch can rapidly and efficiently regulate glucose levels. At the same time, the efficiency of insulin release decreases when blood glucose levels reach normal, effectively avoiding hypoglycemia.
 In a recent study, Li and colleagues developed a dual-mode encapsulated dissolving microneedle patch to enable effective drug delivery. The tips of microneedles were loaded with diclofenac (DCF) nanoparticles and then coated with dexamethasone (DSP)-PLGA nanosuspensions. In vivo pharmacokinetic studies demonstrated that DCF concentrations in rat plasma maintained a sustained-release for 72 h compared to oral administration, with a prolonged half-life of 13.0 h and a bioavailability of 80.3 %. This innovative combination of microneedles and nanotechnology provides a high-capacity, sustained-release platform for dual-drug therapy, significantly improving patient medication adherence [ ].

3.5.2.

## Nanocarriers and iontophoresis
To efficiently deliver the drug, Hwang et al. [ ] developed a portable disposable reverse electrodialysis (RED) cell to push hydrogel-embedded nanocarriers to enter the skin using the ion electrophoresis-aided method. RED provides power for the movement of nanocarriers. The hydrogel, in turn, facilitated the electron transfer from the RED cell to the drug of mobile nanocarriers. In this study, the author loaded rosiglitazone or fluconazole into the nanocarriers and then modified them with charging-inducing molecules. The rosiglitazone system could effectively decrease body weight, glucose level, and the ratio of white adipose tissues to brown counterparts to reasonably control obese conditions reasonably, reflecting the effectiveness of the combination of nanotechnology and iontophoresis methods.
In another study, the nanoscale ethosomes and iontophoresis method were used to enhance the transdermal delivery efficiency of vancomycin hydrochloride. The current density, mode, ionic strength, and drug concentration were evaluated to optimize the transport efficiency. Results have shown that changing the current mode from ON/OFF to continuous or decreasing the current density could increase transdermal flux. Meanwhile, the transdermal delivery amount was increased with the increase in drug concentration. This also proves that the combination of ethosomes and iontophoresis can successfully deliver vancomycin transdermally [ ]. Sahraoui et al. [ ] investigated the feasibility of non-invasive delivery of negatively charged nanobodies (e.g., M7D12H and its variants) via anodal iontophoresis. The research revealed that electroosmosis (EO) is the key mechanism driving electro-migration (EM), enabling these negatively charged proteins to successfully penetrate the skin under anodal iontophoresis conditions. This study provided novel approaches for non-invasive delivery of nanobodies, particularly with potential applications in dermatological treatment.

3.5.3.

## Nanocarriers and magnetophoresis
Magnetic nanoparticles have also been recognized as a new delivery tool for therapeutic applications in recent years, which has been demonstrated in animal experiments to have corresponding therapeutic potential with magnetically-modulated mechanical stimulation [ ]. The magnetic nature of nanoparticles induces physical responses, including mechanical membrane damage and minute cellular signaling responses related to the magnetic, geometric, and physical properties. In addition, specific functionalization could induce the domain of cells to regulate their behavior in spatiotemporally modulated magnetic fields, which may be used to regulate the permeation of physical barriers, facilitating drug transport into the skin [ ]. Magnetic nanoparticle hyperthermia (MNH) is a new strategy to enhance TDD. The alternating current of a high-frequency magnetic field (on or off) can elevate the temperature of magnetic nanoparticles, which can regulate the release of APIs in the particles [ ]. Chen et al. [ ] developed magnetic silk fibroin nanoparticles loaded with methotrexate using supercritical carbon dioxide solvent dispersion. The alternating shift of a stationary to an alternating magnetic field offers a massage-like action to the skin, which changes the structure of SC and enhances the fluidity of lipids within bilayers in the SC, thus improving the penetration of methotrexate into the skin. The penetration improvement was caused by the changes of lipids within the SC bilayer by changing the gel state into a liquid-crystalline phase, which mainly increased follicular transport.

## Others
Conventional hydrogel dressings can modify the skin's chemical, physical, and biological properties. The bioadhesive capability, excellent water absorption features [ ], three-dimensional porous structure [ ], and adjustable degradation rate [ ] of hydrogels have a facilitating effect on drug loading and release [ ]. In an interesting study, Wu et al. [ ] constructed a TDDS with feedback control, a self-powered wearable iontophoretic TDDS ( A). The system used wearable triboelectric nanogenerators (TENG) as motion sensors and energy harvesters. The TENG could convert mechanical energy into electrical energy through the coupling effect of triboelectricity and electrostatic induction ( B), which was further integrated with hydrogel drug carriers to achieve a new type of wearable TDDS that provided on-demand treatment without an external power source ( C). This work offers new directions for non-invasive, electrically assisted TDDS with closed-loop sensing and therapy. In addition, hydrogel has also been extensively studied and applied in the treatment of diabetic wound healing [ ]. For example, Pan et al. [ ] prepared an injectable hydrogel (quaternized chitosan gel containing tannic acid, QT) that exhibited high hemostatic, antimicrobial, and ROS scavenging properties. This QT hydrogel had injectable and self-healing features, avoiding the squeezing of the irregular shape of the wound dressing. Meanwhile, the facile and convenient hydrogel benefited the coagulation, inflammation control, and collagen deposition in the wound skin tissue in a diabetic rat model. Taken together, the QT-treated group accelerated skin tissue regeneration and repair.
 For the treatment of periodontitis, Mou et al. [ ] prepared serum albumin microspheres loaded with zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs, 0.025 %) and minocycline (0.06 %). They incorporated them into a Carbopol 940® hydrogel with a pH-sensitive feature. Relevant experiments showed that the hydrogel exhibited a more substantial therapeutic effect and better self-repair of gingival tissues compared to 2 % minocycline ointment. Han et al. [ ] prepared an innovative ultrasound nanobubble by cross-linking the aminated HA of nanobubbles with the aldehyde-based HA of bubbles. The ultrasound nanobubble could deliver the drug to the deep layer of the skin by enhancing ultrasound-induced cavitation effects. The API reached an ideal penetration depth of 728 μm in a model of isolated porcine skin when the ultrasound parameters were set at 650 kHz, 2 W, and 50 % duty cycle with a 20-min application period.

## Clinical applications
4.1.

## Local delivery
Nanocarrier-based TDDS offers several advantages, including increased skin permeation, improved biodistribution, and effective control of drug release. Nanocarriers can be utilized for targeted delivery to hair follicles, thus enhancing the local treatment of follicle-related diseases [ ]. overviews the pathological characteristics, therapeutic difficulties of common skin diseases, nanocarriers, physical assistive techniques, and advantages of TDDS.
 4.1.1.

## Acne
Acne is a prevalent skin condition. As a chronic inflammatory follicular disorder of the skin, acne occurs in the facial follicular sebaceous unit, which consists of the follicular ducts, the underlying hairs, and the sebaceous glands [ ]. The increased androgen levels in acne patients modulate sebum secretion, leading to skin hyperkeratosis. Activation of Cutibacterium acne also leads to proliferation of keratinocytes and accelerates acne development [ , , ]. In summary, follicular hyperplasia and hyperkeratosis leading to obstruction and inflammation of the pilosebaceous unit are pathogenetic mechanisms of acne [ ]. Clinically, antimicrobials (clindamycin and erythromycin) and anti-keratinizing agents (azelaic acid and retinoic acid) are often chosen to treat acne topically [ ]. However, the occurrence of bacterial resistance inevitably affects the efficacy of these drugs. Nanoparticles offer a viable option for treating drug-resistant infections [ ]. Based on the targeted transport of nanocarriers to hair follicles, Lapteva et al. developed a stabilized polymeric micelle containing retinoic acid (RA) using a diblock poly (hexyl-modified lactic acid)-methoxy-poly(ethylene glycol) copolymer. They evaluated the ability of the micelle to deliver RA to the skin. It was shown that RA was successfully incorporated into the micellar nanocarriers that exhibited better delivery efficiency than conventional formulations in vitro experiments [ ]. Liposomal formulations of traditional acne-treating drugs, such as clindamycin and salicylic acid, have been shown to improve stability and intradermal penetration of the APIs, as well as to reduce skin irritation and other related side effects [ , ]. Niosome improved tretinoin's transdermal delivery and enhanced tretinoin's photostability [ ]. The composition and size of nanovesicles have an essential influence on the transdermal delivery of tretinoin due to the interaction between vesicles and skin based on the physicochemical properties of vesicular components. The SLN-containing tretinoin topical gel was less irritating to the skin than traditional dosage forms, which improved skin tolerance, enhanced penetration, and slowed drug release [ ]. Alvi et al. [ ] developed nanocomposites of liposomal gold nanoparticles encapsulating curcumin for dual-light-mediated acne treatment. After administration, nanocomposites are locally deposited via the follicular pathway, and photothermal effects were generated to destroy the sebaceous glands due to continuous irradiation with blue light and NIR. The results showed that the dual-light-mediated therapy of nanocomposites can significantly inhibit bacterial growth during the treatment of recurrent acne. Nicotinamide has also been used to treat acne because of its anti-inflammatory properties and reduced sebum secretion [ ]. Abd-Allah et al. [ ]. used chitosan nanoparticles encapsulated nicotinamide to treat 20 patients with acne. After 8 weeks of treatment, inflammatory lesions, comedones, and total lesions were reduced by 73.1 %, 20 %, and 68.9 %, respectively. These patients did not report side effects such as local irritation, burning, or dermatitis.
In a study that recruited 40 individuals with mild to moderate acne, clindamycin phosphate and salicylic acid (CLSA) mixed into ethosomal gel showed superior efficacy in inhibiting the proliferation of Propionibacterium acne and relieving the inflammatory reaction. The Institutional Ethics Helsinki Committee and the Israeli Ministry of Health permitted the studies. In this trial, patients were randomized to receive gels containing either a placebo (19 sicks) or CLSA (21 sicks) twice daily for eight weeks. The data exhibited that patients treated with the CLSA-containing gel showed significant improvement in acne symptoms, with a substantial reduction in pimples and pustules. At the same time, 48 % of the sicks in the placebo group reported no improvement in acne syndrome [ ]. Due to acne, post-acne scarring (PAS) significantly diminishes the quality of patients affected due to aesthetic concerns. The transdermal administration of botulinum toxin-A (BTXA) presents a promising avenue for treating PAS. In the study of Zhu's team, cholesterol/phosphatidylcholine (Chol/PC) nanoliposomes were employed for the encapsulation and TDD of BTXA. When compared to the treatment with HA hydrogel, the BTXA@liposome/HA hydrogel treatment could alleviate PSA to a certain degree [ ].

4.1.2.

## Hair loss
Hair loss can be divided into alopecia areata (AA), androgenetic alopecia (AGA), and other types. AGA represents the most prevalent form of hair loss disorder in clinical practice [ ]. Minoxidil remains the sole topical therapeutic agent approved by the FDA for AGA management [ ], the mechanism of which has not been fully elucidated. Studies have shown that the drug increases the blood circulation of the scalp and activates potassium channels, which results in the accelerating transition of the hair follicle from the resting phase to the growing stage [ ]. However, side effects such as itching and increased scalp flaking are often associated with the treatment [ ]. In addition, these drugs have low skin permeability [ ]. The nanosystems transport the drug, increase the deposition of therapeutic agents in the hair follicle, and enhance their penetration into the dermis, resulting in a better therapeutic effect [ ]. Vidlářová et al. found that the concentration (ideal concentration of 0.02 %) and the saturation solubility of curcumin determined the penetration efficiency of this molecule into the skin [ ].
López-Pinto et al. [ ] prepared ethosomes with α-DPPC, cholesterol, and ethanol to piggyback minoxidil. The nanovesicles allowed more drugs to reach deeper skin structures than either ethanol solutions or liposomes. Padois et al. [ ] prepared SLN loaded with 5 % minoxidil and found that SLN had better skin permeability and less skin corrosiveness when compared to other commercial products. NLCs are also excellent carriers for minoxidil. Wang et al. [ ] developed NLC loading with 2 % minoxidil, which increased retention of minoxidil in hair follicles without reverse skin reactions, such as erythema.
Liu et al. [ ] prepared a cardamonin (CAR)-loaded liposomal nanoformulation (CAR@Lip) that could significantly stimulate the expansion and migration of human epidermal-dermal papilla cells. Compared with CAR, the CAR@Lip had a better skin penetration capability and a higher local content in hair follicles ( A and B). Animal experiments have shown that this preparation can significantly promote hair growth and quicken the status change of hair follicles from the resting state to the growth mode ( C). In addition, Lgarashi et al. developed a cosmetic formulation containing nanoencapsulated active ingredients (AcPi) and investigated the mechanisms of treating follicular aging and hair loss. Their findings revealed that AcPi mitigated oxidative stress and restored mitochondrial function disrupted by UV radiation exposure. Additionally, AcPi facilitated the enhancement of the WNT/β-catenin signaling pathway, which fostered hair growth and prolonged the anagen phase of the hair cycle [ ].
 
4.1.3.

## Wound-healing
Wounds are usually caused by damage to the integrity of biological tissues, such as skin and mucous membranes, caused by diabetic ulcers, burns, osteomyelitis, pressure ulcers, and surgery [ ]. Among them, failure to heal wounds from diabetes and pressure co-induced ulcers is one of the significant factors of mortality in these patients [ ]. For diabetic patients, the healing of ulcer surfaces is delayed due to the down-regulated neovascularization induced by the decreased activity of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α). It has been shown that FDA-permitted deferoxamine (DFO) can enhance HIF-1α transactivation by inhibiting iron-triggered ROS, thereby promoting the healing of skin ulcers. To improve the activity of HIF-1α, Duscher et al. [ ] developed a novel TDDS incorporating DFO for targeted intervention in stress-induced ulcer pathogenesis within diabetic murine models. Research confirmed that in wounds treated with DFO, there was increased angiogenesis and collagen density, while decreased free radical levels and cell death rates. This suggests that transdermal administration of DFO could inhibit ulcer pathogenesis and promote tissue regeneration in diabetic wound healing processes. In addition, wounds from patients with diabetic foot ulcers (DFU) exhibit a more complex microenvironment when compared to normal skin [ ]. Hypoxia, excessive wound exudation, repeated bacterial infections, and high ROS levels may lead to the delayed healing of diabetic foot ulcers [ , ].
Ye et al. [ ] prepared a composite hydrogel by encapsulating near-infrared (NIR)-light-absorbing dye (indocyanine green, ICG), rifampicin, and phase-transition material (a eutectic mixture of fatty acids) with halloysite nanotubes and then incorporating them into alginate gel. Under NIR laser irradiation, nanotubes slowly released rifampicin to inhibit bacterial colonization, alleviate the inflammatory syndrome, promote collagen deposition, and enhance angiogenesis, accelerating wound healing. Wang et al. [ ] developed a pH-sensitive calcium alginate hydrogel containing protamine-loaded nanoparticles as well as hyaluronan oligosaccharides (HAOs) (protamine NPs/HAO Ca-alginate hydrogel) ( A). As a pH-responsive dressing, the hydrogel could more quickly complete the release of drugs in the alkaline environment of the wound. This hydrogel showed faster and better healing within 3 days in animal experiments. In addition, the closure rates of wounds within 5 days and 14 days were approximately 73.5 % and 96.8 %, respectively ( B). Thus, nanocarriers have been popularly applied in TDDS for the transdermal delivery of APIs to enhance wound healing [ ].
 Saghafi et al. [ ] investigated the effects of atorvastatin-laden nanoemulgel on postoperative wound healing and pain relief in a randomized, double-masked clinical trial that enrolled 60 sicks with post-cesarean section. The wound healing scores increased from 63 % (day 7) to 93 % (day 14) after they were treated with atorvastatin-laden nanoemulgel. In contrast, those were changed from 57 % to 89 % after the patients were administered atorvastatin-laden emulgel. The results also showed that topical atorvastatin-laden nanoemulgel effectively relieved postoperative wound pain after a cesarean section without causing intolerable side effects. The wound tissue is perpetually susceptible to infection. The recovery process of infected wound tissue could be hastened by applying to dissolve microneedles infused with nanozymes, which comprised a pullulan matrix. This matrix incorporated the antimicrobial peptide LL37, conjugated in situ onto the surface of metal-organic frameworks (ZIF-8) loaded onto the tip of the microneedles, alongside Prussian Blue, a compound known for its dual antimicrobial and antioxidative properties, thereby facilitating the healing of infected dermal wounds [ ].

4.1.4.

## Viral infections
TDDS demonstrates exceptional suitability for managing cutaneous viral infections through topical administration, which minimizes systemic drug absorption and associated adverse effects commonly observed with subcutaneous injection or oral administration routes [ ]. Unlike systematic administration, TDDS can provide lesion site-specific therapy for herpes simplex, varicella zoster, warts, etc.
4.1.4.1.

## Herpes labialis
Herpes labialis is predominantly associated with herpes simplex virus (HSV) infection and is currently treated with oral and topical acyclovir. However, the systemic administration of acyclovir may cause severe side effects such as nausea, vomiting, nephrotoxicity, and neurotoxicity [ ]. On the other hand, the local delivery of acyclovir requires frequent and prolonged use to achieve moderate efficacy [ ]. Transdermal delivery methods such as water-activated patches and dissolving polymer microneedles can be employed to improve patient compliance. A transbuccal patch has been designed to deliver acyclovir [ ]. The sustained release of the APIs from the patch was up to 22 h. When adding NaGC into the matrix of the patch, the system t(lag) and the steady state diffusion output were changed from 10.4 h to 5.6 h (53.8 %) and from 144.2 μg cm−2 h−1 to 758.7 μg cm−2 h−1 (526.1 %), respectively. Meanwhile, the amount of acyclovir loaded into this system was within the desired range, which suggests that acyclovir delivery via buccal mucosa is feasible.
Studies have shown that TDDS delivers much more acyclovir into the skin tissue than existing acyclovir creams. The data from the in vitro tests show that the advantages of niosomes loaded with acyclovir include higher loading efficiency, more extended drug release, and better stability than liposomes loaded with acyclovir [ ]. Chitosan nanospheres (NS) showed better results in delivering acyclovir than free molecules, including improved carrier penetration and higher antiviral activity against strains of HSV-1 and HSV-2 [ ]. Golestannejad et al. examined the effectiveness of acyclovir cream, a placebo nanofiber patch, and an acyclovir nanofiber patch in addressing recurrent herpes labialis. While acyclovir nanofiber patches are advocated for expediting symptom alleviation in recurrent labial herpes, they do not demonstrate efficacy in reducing either the crusting or healing duration [ ].

4.1.4.2.

## Varicella zoster
Herpes and Varicella zoster represent distinct clinical manifestations due to the varicella-zoster virus infection (VZV). Studies have shown that the VZV vaccine can be delivered via microneedles coated with the recombinant VZV gene. Reactivation of VZV usually causes postherpetic neuralgia (PHN), a chronic pain that can be attributed to inflammation and death of primary neurons caused by viral infection. The pain is mainly confined to the herpes zoster area, and the side effects of traditional systemic analgesics may outweigh the analgesic impact they provide. Studies have shown that local delivery of lidocaine via TDDS is effective in relieving moderate to severe PHN [ ]. As early as the 1990s, the FDA permitted a 5 % lidocaine patch to treat PHN [ ]. Small interfering RNA (siRNA) therapeutics can directly target viral mRNAs, thereby killing the herpes zoster virus. Recombinant siRNA targeting the Open Reading Frame 7 (ORF7), or r/siORF7, represents a promising therapeutic target against VZV employing an RNA interference (RNAi) strategy. The in vitro study showed that r/si-ORF7 could suppress VZV reproduction and significantly decrease the number of viral copies. Nanoliposomes carrying r/si-ORF7 could suppress VZV infection and guard epidermal structures against VZV damage [ ]. UL8 in the HSV-1 genome is another target against the HSV virus. Pei et al. [ ] expressed a recombinant UL8-siRNA and delivered it with flexible nanoliposomes. A mouse model of herpes zoster treated with the system showed a significant reduction in viral load at 2 days. The advent of new viral pathogens and the shortcomings of traditional antiviral treatments call for creative approaches to address ongoing and global health threats. Pu et al. summarized the impact of viral infections and the potential of antiviral nanomedicines, examining their mechanisms of action and therapeutic benefits, alongside the latest advancements in this innovative field [ ].

4.1.4.3.

## Warts
Cutaneous warts represent benign proliferative lesions of epidermal origin caused by human papillomavirus (HPV) infection. Current standard therapeutic approaches typically employ cryotherapeutic intervention utilizing liquid nitrogen to induce necrotic detachment of designed tissue through controlled freeze-thaw cycles. However, cryotherapy is relatively painful and may need to be performed multiple times [ ]. Studies have shown that patches with bleomycin-containing microneedles are comparable to cryotherapy for warts but are significantly less painful than cryotherapy [ ]. Intralesional injection of bleomycin is an excellent choice that realizes a cure ratio of up to 95 % before the patch was invented. However, side effects caused by intralesional bleomycin injection include tissue necrosis and pain. In contrast, microneedle delivery reduces side effects due to the precise control of infiltration depth. Meanwhile, microneedle delivery can cover a larger area of lesions, whereas intralesional injection can not. This suggests that TDDS can reduce pain and is easier and more convenient than intralesional injection [ ]. Photodynamic therapy (PDT) has emerged as a clinically validated therapeutic intervention for managing various cutaneous pathologies, functioning through the synergistic interaction of photosensitizing agents, light activation, and localized oxidative damage. However, recalcitrant Plantar Warts are more challenging to treat due to the hindrance of harder and thicker skin that severely sensitizes agents. Fadel developed a TDDS to treat warts topically: methylene blue (MB)-loaded transferosome-containing gel (MBTG). The results confirmed that MBTG showed a higher cure rate of disease than free MB gel (86.7 % vs. 53.6 %) at a lower time of repeated administration. In addition, patients did not report any adverse reaction to pain or inflammation. Last but not least, recurrence of the warts was not recorded [ ]. Genital warts are a type of sexually transmitted infection that severely impacts the health of patients. Shi et al. reported a successful treatment of a case involving persistent penile warts using podophyllotoxin-loaded nanostructured lipid carriers (POD-NLC). The POD-NLC demonstrated the ability to adhere to the skin surface, facilitating the gentle and controlled delivery of POD, thereby reducing irritation and toxicity [ ].

4.1.5.

## Skin cancer
Skin cancer encompasses non-melanoma, melanoma, etc. Skin cancer cells have a higher level of lipids, keratin, and erythematous papules when compared to normal skin. The changes result in the need to overcome more rigid barriers for transdermal absorption of the drug [ , , ]. Transdermal delivery of antitumor drugs with nanocarriers not only overcomes the transdermal absorption obstacles caused by pathological changes in skin cancer cells but also effectively inhibits tumor cells [ ]. Cervantes et al. [ ] utilized red LED light to trigger a photosensitizer (10 % aminolevulinic acid-loaded nanoemulsion-containing gel) to perform PDT in situ to treat squamous cell carcinoma. Clinical and histopathologic clearance was achieved at 4 weeks after treatment in all 12 patients who participated in the study. Dias et al. [ ] prepared an imiquimod-loaded nanoparticle (NPImq) that showed negative controls' approximately three-fold anti-angiogenic activity in animal experiments.
Additionally, the number and size of papillomas were significantly reduced in the group of NPImq-treated mice. Anirudhan's study showed that aminated dextran and β-CD nanoparticles were coated with alginate and chitosan and coupled by electrostatic forces after regulating the pH solution. The patch could realize co-delivery and a long-term release of curcumin and 5-fluorouracil. The advantages of the curcumin and 5-fluorouracil co-loaded into the TDDS for combination chemotherapy were, in turn, confirmed by the in vivo skin adhesion tests, skin irritation analysis, average visible transmittance, and water vapor permeability experiments [ ].
Tyrosinase (TYR) generates melanin by catalyzing the hydroxylation of L-tyrosine and oxidation of L-DOPA; thus, limiting its activity prevents excessive melanin deposition [ ]. Huang et al. [ ] combined quantum dots (QDs) of ZnO with BQ-788/HBTU to prepare BQ-788/NH ZnO for loading ellagic acid (EA), a highly potent TYR inhibitor ( C). The permeability of BQ-788/EAZnO on guinea pig skin was better than that of free EA, and this system showed good binding capability of endothelin ET receptor on melanocyte membrane. At a concentration of 12.5 μg ml−1, the inhibition rates of BQ-788/EAZnO against melanin deposition and TYR activity reached 37.50 % ± 5.23 % and 44.23 % ± 4.97 %, respectively, maintaining cellular viability without inducing significant cytotoxicity ( D and E).
In Ma's group [ ], a novel TDDS was developed to overcome the impact of multidrug resistance on chemotherapy. They first prepared PEI-decorated ethosomes (Eth−PEI) and sodium cholate-conjugated ethosomes (Eth−SC), then synthesized a new carrier complex (ETH−PEI/ETH−SC) through electrostatic adsorption. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments showed that ETH−PEI/ETH−SC loaded with cytotoxic drugs (doxorubicin) and chemosensitizers (curcumin) exhibited excellent antitumor effects against melanoma. From this, it can be seen that TDDS represents a promising therapeutic approach for melanoma treatment, exhibiting reduced systemic toxicity and better biocompatibility. On this basis, researchers are strenuously trying to improve the drug's skin permeation further. Zhu et al. [ ] used skin-penetrating peptides (TD-1) to modify curcumin-loaded liposomes to enhance their skin permeation capabilities. The experimental results demonstrated that the modification of TD-1 peptide enhanced the transdermal permeation efficiency of the combined delivery system by a factor of 4.48. The findings substantiate that this novel combination system integrating biological penetration enhancers with liposomes significantly improves cutaneous drug delivery, exhibiting significant translational potential for dermal carcinoma prophylaxis and therapeutic interventions. To improve the therapeutic efficiency of TDDS, Zhang et al. [ ] designed a unique self-assembled TDDS (Sup-TDDS). As shown in A, the Sup-TDDS consists of dithiolane-containing lipoic acid (LA), photosensitizers (Ce6), and chemotherapeutic agents (trametinib) to achieve synergistic effects of chemotherapy and photodynamic therapy (PDT). Significantly, the disulfides of the chain on Sup-TDDS facilitate thiol-disulfide exchange reactions with exofacial thiols on cell surfaces, thus enhancing stratum corneum penetration. In addition, the researchers observed that Sup-TDDS primarily accumulated within hair follicles ( B). This study shows that Sup-TDDS can effectively penetrate the skin and achieve a large amount of drug accumulation in subcutaneous tumors. Lipid nanoparticles are also promising for transdermal delivery of RNA. Piao et al. developed a target-oriented siRNA delivery system using an HA-coating on cationic SLNs (CSLNs). The siVEGF complex enabled precise delivery to skin cancer tissues, enhancing drug loading by electrostatically attaching nucleic acids to CSLN surfaces. HA also targeted cancer cells and improved transdermal delivery. The HA/CSLN/siVEGF complex enhanced absorption and tumor targeting, and reduced VEGF mRNA levels, which inhibited tumor growth. These effects make it a viable transdermal delivery vehicle for siRNA in cancer therapy [ ].
 
4.1.6.

## Vitiligo
Vitiligo is an acquired idiopathic skin disease. The presence of white spots associated with selective damage due to melanocyte apoptosis is characteristic [ , ]. Corticosteroids and calcineurin inhibitors are currently first-line drugs in traditional treatment regimens of vitiligo [ , ]. Palomo et al. [ ] prepared baicalin- or berberine-loaded ultra-deformable vesicles that showed better permeability in the in vitro experiments, thus increasing melanin and tyrosinase activities. Manosroi et al. found that the tyrosinase activity of human tyrosinase plasmid pMEL34 in the flexible cationic niosomes was fourfold that in plasmid-loaded common niosomes and free plasmid. The ex vitro studies have shown that the elastic cationic niosomes can promote tyrosinase gene expression and melanogenesis [ ]. Oxidative stress and autoimmune responses are key in vitiligo onset, where apoptotic melanocytes expose self-antigens and attract CD8+ T cells, leading to melanocyte destruction and melanin loss. CD8+ T cells release IFN-γ, activating the JAK-STAT pathway in keratinocytes, which further promotes vitiligo progression. Li's study introduced a nanodrug of antioxidant polydopamine (PDA)-janus kinase inhibitor (JAKi, tofacitinib), effectively reducing oxidative stress and T cell activation. PDA-JAKi decreased melanocyte apoptosis and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) release and inhibited the IFN-γ-JAK-STAT pathway. The nanodrug was delivered via microneedle patches, improving mouse vitiligo symptoms, including pigmentation restoration and increased melanocyte count [ ].

4.1.7.

## Cutaneous lupus erythematosus
CLE represents an autoimmune dermatological disorder that occurs independently or as a manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus [ ]. Keratinocytes from CLE patients undergo apoptosis induced by factors such as ultraviolet radiation [ ] and pro-inflammatory chemokines, which appear at the dermal-epidermal junction of the skin of CLE patients [ ]. In treating CLE patients, N-arachidonoylethanolamine (AEA) emerged as a potential active molecule due to its anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects. However, AEA is a lipophilic molecule poorly permeable to SC and easily oxidized. AEA is restricted in treating CLE patients due to its shortcomings [ , ]. McCormick et al. [ ] developed a silica nanoparticle with a loading rate of 4 wt% of AEA (AEA-NP). The nanoparticles showed good dermal permeability when topically treated CLE mice. They persisted in mouse dermal hair follicles for 24 h. Additionally, the severity score of skin lesions, histopathological score, and IBA-1 macrophage markers were significantly decreased in AEA-NP-treated mice. Recent studies identified miR-31, miR-485-3p, and miR-885-5p as key regulators in CLE. Joseph-Mullol et al. investigated a novel topical miRNA therapy using DDC642 elastic liposomes to target these pathways. Optimized miRNA-loaded liposomes showed effective cellular penetration with low cytotoxicity. Anti-miR-31-loaded liposomes reduced miR-31 and NF-κB but increased STK40 and PPP6C. Pre-miR-885-5p-loaded liposomes raised miR-885-5p and decreased PSMB5 and NF-κB. Anti-miR-485-3p-loaded liposomes lowered T-cell activation markers. Both anti-miR-31 and pre-miR-885-5p-co-loaded liposomes modulated inflammatory pathways in 3D-skin CLE models, indicating potential for topical genetic therapies in CLE, warranting further animal studies [ ].

4.1.8.

## Psoriasis vulgaris
Psoriasis vulgaris (PV) represents a prevalent chronic inflammatory dermatological condition, with topical corticosteroid therapy as the first-line therapeutic approach for managing mild to moderate PV in clinical practice [ , ]. Somlea et al. [ ] utilized high-frequency ultrasonography (HF-USG) to evaluate skin thickness in patients with psoriasis. The results showed that the thickness of the injured skin in psoriasis patients was increased by an average of 1180 ± 340 μm compared with adjacent normal skin. Compared with the skin damage caused by long-term use of the glucocorticoid ointment [ ]. Chinese medical cream brings better results in relieving dry skin rashes and itching in PV patients with fewer side effects. Better drug utilization can be obtained by delivering herbal preparations through nanoscale particles/vesicles. In a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial, 8 weeks of nanosized herbal runji ointment administration showed better results, including decreased lesion area and disease severity [ ]. A clinical study showed that using laser-aided drug delivery (LADD) to treat nail PV can significantly reduce the severity index of nails [ ]. The roles of exosomes derived from dendritic cells, neutrophil cells, macrophages, mast cells, T cells, keratinocytes, adipocytes, and fibroblasts are closely associated with PV [ ]. Keratinocyte exosomes can deliver LncRNA LOC285194 to inhibit the Th17 and cytokines, including IL-17A, IL-22, and TNF-ɑ, alleviating the PV syndrome of the skin [ ]. Elgueta et al. explored a novel therapy of a topical sponge composed of exosomes from Wharton's Jelly mesenchymal stem cells dispersed in the HA matrix. The sponges had a cross-linked structure that extended the dispersion time in water. Safety assessments showed no adverse effects in patients with atopic dermatitis and healthy volunteers. In a 30-day trial, 12 psoriasis patients had up to a 33 % reduction in modified Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (mPASI) scores and a 41 % decrease in plaque size, with transepidermal water loss (TEWL) decreasing by 30 % and skin elasticity improving by 43 %. This indicates the exosomes-HA sponge is a safe and effective treatment for psoriasis vulgaris [ ].

4.2.

## Whole-body delivery
4.2.1.

## Analgesia
Pain can be categorized into acute and chronic pain, while mixed pain syndromes are prevalent. Patients diagnosed with chronic pain usually require long-term use of analgesic medications. Therefore, finding a route of administration that improves pain and minimizes adverse effects is critical. When choosing an analgesic route, non-invasive administration is more acceptable to patients, especially for long-term chronic pain [ ].
4.2.1.1.

## Fentanyl
Fentanyl was the first drug to be used in the treatment of pain through TDDS. Delivery of fentanyl through TDDS allows the concentration of the drug up to a therapeutically effective level, even at lower doses. If fentanyl penetrates the blood-brain barrier, it would strongly stimulate the vomiting center, as well as inhibit the respiratory center. Therefore, respiratory depression is the main side effect of the drug [ ]. The administration of fentanyl through TDDS can effectively bypass the first-pass effect and reduce the side effects. It can also maintain a constant rate for 3 days [ ]. Although the Fentanyl patch is safer than the traditional formulation of the drug, improper use of Fentanyl TDDS may lead to death. Therefore, good instruction for patients is indeed needed. Transdermal fentanyl, however, can lead to conditions such as paralytic intestinal obstruction [ ] and asymmetric facial edema [ ], among other issues.

4.2.1.2.

## Lidocaine
Lidocaine, the most commonly used local anesthetic, is widely used in a variety of pain diseases [ ]. The current transdermal delivery of lidocaine has to face challenges such as slow onset time, inability to deliver the drug effectively through the skin, and insufficient local anesthetic effect [ ]. Microemulsions, as a kind of nanocarrier, can increase the solubility of the drugs, carry more drugs, enhance the impact of skin penetration, and augment transdermal drug permeation efficiency [ ]. Daryab et al. [ ] developed microemulsion-based gels as a new carrier for loading lidocaine. The system showed high stability and better anesthetic effects in vitro tests, suggesting that this system can be considered a more promising tool for transdermal transport of lidocaine. Additionally, Liu et al. [ ] developed a novel transdermal delivery system integrating elastic nanoliposomes (ENL) with microneedle (MN) technology to enhance the percutaneous absorption of lidocaine hydrochloride (LidH), shortening the onset time as well as providing longer anesthesia in mice ( C). Hasanpour et al. employed a nanostructured lipid carrier to act as a permeation enhancer for lidocaine. The researchers characterized the nanosystems through zeta potential analyses, particle size, crystallinity behavior, and encapsulation efficiency. Subsequently, they compared them with conventional dermal ointments. They further utilized 3D printed solid microneedles to optimize the delivery of lidocaine-loaded nanosystems [ ].

4.2.1.3.

## Ibuprofen
Ibuprofen is a popular medication for the treatment of fever and pain. Although its safety has been confirmed as a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID), it still has side effects related to irritation of the gastrointestinal mucosa, which finally leads to ulcers as well as bleeding. Ibuprofen works primarily by inhibiting cyclooxygenase (COX) 1 and 2, the enzymes associated with inflammatory signal pathways and prostaglandin formation. The delivery of ibuprofen via the TDDS prevents the drug from contact with the local mucus membranes, thus avoiding side effects [ ]. Shumilov et al. developed a new TDDS with ethosomal gel containing ibuprofen. The vesicle size was about 200 nm. Two hours after patch application, the maximum concentration of APIs in the blood (Cmax) was about 74.11 ± 18.52 μg ml−1. At 12 h after patch application, the body temperature of the fevered rat was down-regulated to normal values. The analgesic effect of API lasted for not less than 6 h. The authors hold that their works should be verified in humans to confirm the feasibility and efficacy of this new TDDS [ ]. In addition, Alkrad et al. used nonionic surfactants and cosolvents to prepare nanomulsions to enhance ibuprofen solubility and bioavailability. Results showed that droplet sizes of nanoemulsions were under 100 nm, and ibuprofen solubility reached 170 mg/mL. The flux of ibuprofen through rat skin was 94.6 ± 8.0 μg/cm2/h. After 8 h of transdermal administration, ibuprofen achieved a peak plasma concentration of 0.064 mg/mL, with bioavailability increased by approximately 1.5-fold compared to commercial ibuprofen gel, which indicates that the nonionic MEs could be effective nanocarriers for transdermal ibuprofen delivery [ ].

4.2.2.

## Enhancing the therapy of antibacterial drugs
Most antibiotics do not possess the favorable features required for passive transdermal delivery, and the efficiency of percutaneous delivery of these drugs may be improved by nanotechnology [ ].
4.2.2.1.

## Amoxicillin
Pérez-Martínez et al. [ ] created a new composite hydrogel fabricated with amoxicillin nanofibers called polyacrylamide hydrogels. This system could release the antibiotic after electrical stimulation with minimal toxicity. The data on in vitro cytotoxicity confirmed that the cell viability of composite hydrogel extracts on subcutaneous connective tissue in mice was greater than 80 %. For diabetic patients, their wound healing becomes slow due to the emergence of multidrug-resistant bacteria [ ]. Diab et al. [ ] explored the feasibility of amoxicillin-loaded fibroin/propolis nanoparticles for treating skin wounds in diabetic rats. Following 15 days of treatment, diabetic wounds healed completely, and bacteria in infected wounds were minimal (0–10 CFU ml−1). At the same time, the cumulative release percentage of amoxicillin in the nanoparticles reached 5 % after 2 h, which simultaneously allowed the long-term release of amoxicillin, thus avoiding the problem of the short half-life time of APIs. In addition to the applications of amoxicillin in wound healing, research is also being conducted in skin tissue engineering. A novel nanocomposite scaffold of gelatin, pectin, and amoxicillin-loaded UiO-66-NH2 MOF was developed for skin tissue engineering. The nanoparticles had a mean diameter of 218.5 nm and a surface charge of +44.1 mV, which changed to 248.8 ± 19 nm and +32.6 ± 2.5 mV, respectively, after drug loading. Scaffolds containing 3 % UiO-66-NH2@amoxicillin increased tensile strength by 37.6 % and Young's modulus by 56.7 % compared to controls. Antibacterial tests showed significant reductions in E. coli and S. aureus, while cell assays indicated over 85 % viability, as well as improved cell adhesion and migration with 3 % UiO-66-NH2@amoxicillin [ ].

4.2.2.2.

## Cephalosporins
Cefotaxime (CTX), a third-generation cephalosporin antibiotic, has broad-spectrum antimicrobial efficacy encompassing both Gram-negative (G-) and Gram-positive (G+) bacterial species. However, the increased occurrences of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria have progressively decreased the drug's efficacy. Hagbani et al. [ ] synthesized gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) loaded with CTX. Herein, CTX could reduce Au+ to Au and finally be coupled on the surface of Au nanoparticles. Most importantly, the couple of CTX onto nanoparticles did not reduce the antibacterial activity. On the contrary, Au nanoparticles loaded with CTX possessed better antimicrobial activity, including G bacteria (Klebsiella oxytoca, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Escherichia coli) and G+ bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus). Promisingly, the Au particles restored and/or enhanced the antibacterial effects of CTX against MDR bacteria. The encapsulation of ceftriaxone into chitosan nanoparticles also enhances the drug's antimicrobial efficiency against Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria. It provides sustained antibiotic release to specific targeted targets [ ]. Nasrollahzadeh et al. [ ] prepared SLN with α-tocopherol succinate to load Cephalexin. Free Cephalexin (7 %) and SLN loaded with Cephalexin (3 %) were further dispersed into the poly-iso-butylene adhesive matrix (90 %) to prepare the patch. The optimized TDDS had better inhibitory effects on the growth of S. aureus than the mixture of free Cephalexin (10 %) and poly-iso-butylene adhesive matrix (90 %), as well as promoting the development of human skin cells. Resistant bacterial infections complicate global clinical practices, prompting research into new antibiotics, but pathogens quickly develop resistance again. Rizvi et al. synthesized AuNPs in a one-pot method to deliver 2nd (cefotetan-CT) and 3rd (cefixime-CX) generation cephalosporins to resistant pathogens. AuNPs were tested against resistant E. coli and K. pneumoniae strains, showing effective MIC50 values of 0.5–0.8 μg/mL, suggesting that old antibiotics can be revitalized as potent nano-antibiotics [ ].

4.2.2.3.

## Tetracyclines
Tetracyclines represent a category of broad-spectrum antibiotics that are remarkably esteemed by dermatologists. Throughout the past decade, their therapeutic effectiveness has broadened to encompass a variety of skin disorders [ ]. A combination of tetracyclines and nanotechnology may further enhance these drugs' activities and clinical efficacy. Hasanpouri et al. [ ] investigated the potential of nanotransferosomes and nanoliposomes as carriers to deliver tetracycline hydrochloride to treat acne in 2018. The study chose the film hydration process to create vesicular nanocarriers. The effectiveness of this delivery method was demonstrated based on the tests of the in vitro drug release and the ex vivo drug permeation through rat skin. The results confirmed that the release of tetracycline from the nanoliposome was 2.6-fold more than that from nanotransfersomes, whereas the permeability of the nanoliposome was only 62.5 % of that of nanotransfersomes. Meanwhile, the solid lipid microparticles (SLM) produced by the spray-drying technique could also highly enhance tetracycline to penetrate the skin barrier, improving the therapeutic efficacy of acne and reducing the side effects associated with tetracycline [ ].

4.2.2.4.

## Roxithromycin
Roxithromycin is poorly water-soluble, and its solubility at 25 °C is 0.0335 mg ml−1. Therefore, the concentration of roxithromycin in TDDS needs more than 1 mg ml−1 to meet the penetrating requirement of this antibiotic [ , ]. Csongradi et al. [ ] tested the local permeability of roxithromycin (2 %) by encapsulating roxithromycin with different solid-state forms into four vesicles. The solid-state forms of roxithromycin include amorphous and crystalline. The four vesicle systems were proniosomes, niosomes, pro-ufosomes, and ufosomes. The results showed that vesicles could deliver the drug to the epidermal-dermal layer, thus playing a role in treating acne. Compared with other vesicle systems, niosomes could provide more APIs. APIs’ delivery efficiency in high to low vesicles was as follows: ufosomes, proniosomes, and pro-ufosomes. Antibiotics can be enhanced with growth factors for better wound healing. Ding et al. prepared nanoparticles (rhEGF/ROX/Oxi-αCD NPs) to combat S. aureus in wounds. The particles had a spherical structure with sizes of 190 ± 8 nm. In vitro tests revealed lower minimum inhibitory concentration for these NPs than roxithromycin, while in vivo studies indicated that rhEGF/ROX/Oxi-αCD NPs significantly improved healing of S. aureus-infected wounds compared to the control group [ ].

4.2.3.

## Attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder
Adult attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is mainly associated with reduced synaptic and diminished extra-synaptic dopamine related to the increased density of dopamine transporter protein (DAT) and deficient dopaminergic activity within the striatum and prefrontal cortex [ ]. Several animal studies and clinical trials have shown that nicotine can alter cognitive function through cholinergic agonists of nicotinic receptors within the brain and the augmented release of some neurotransmitters, such as dopamine (DA), norepinephrine (NE), acetylcholine (ACh), and 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) [ , , ]. A double-masked trial conducted by Conners et al. demonstrated that nicotine transport via TDDS improved attention in ADHD patients with fewer side effects, such as clinician-related comprehensive improvement, attention, timing accuracy performance, and self-assessed concentration and vigor [ ]. Poltavski et al. [ ] showed that transdermal delivery of nicotine improved sustained attention in non-smokers and may have different effects on the attention of various individuals, augmenting the optimal performance of attention. Hu et al. [ ] developed a cationic liposomal protein for piggybacking nicotine semi-antigens, and these nanoliposomes provide a new strategy for nicotine vaccines. Renukuntla et al. developed stearic acid-nicotine conjugate-loaded SLNs (SNA-SLNs) for transdermal nicotine-replacement therapy. Preliminary tests on rabbits indicated sustained release for 96 h, and suggested that SNA-SLNs are a promising option for nicotine transdermal delivery [ ].

4.2.4.

## Hypertension
Hypertension is a global health problem, and TDDS of conventional drugs such as hydrochlorothiazide and carvedilol has shown better results in randomized, single-center, and crossover trials. This transdermal administration effectively improves patient compliance while maintaining the levels of the administered drug in the vessels [ ]. Atenolol (ATE) is a β1-adrenergic receptor blocker used to treat hypertension and cardiovascular diseases. Zhang et al. [ ] developed MoS2 NPs with a three-dimensional (3D) flower-like structure that was coated with poly (acrylic acid) (PAA) for transdermal delivery of ATE (PAA-MoS2 NPs). The PAA-MoS2 NPs exhibited a high loading amount (55 %) and good photothermal conversion features. The carrier receives a laser signal to enhance drug release and transdermal penetration without skin irritation. Solubilization and transdermal delivery of nebivolol (NBV) by nanoscale ethosomes have shown an excellent transdermal delivery, which allowed the drug to penetrate the hair follicles and improved systemic absorption, making it a practical alternative route of oral administration for hypertensive individuals suffering from dysphagia [ ]. Thakkar et al. developed a cilostazol-laden transdermal patch to enhance drug solubility, stability, and bioavailability using nanomicelles of Soluplus and Poloxamer 188. The patch showed high entrapment efficiency (97.71 %), drug loading (48.86 %), and favorable mechanical properties, indicating its potential to improve cilostazol delivery by overcoming first-pass effects [ ].

5.

## Conclusions and future perspectives
TDDS offers an alternative to oral and subcutaneous drug delivery, with studies indicating its advantages over these methods. Nanomedicines also play an essential role in TDDS, and data have shown that nanocarrier-based TDDS has excellent drug utilization and better effects. Nanocarriers are pivotal in enhancing the efficacy of TDDS by facilitating the controlled release of drugs and improving skin penetration. Through their tunable size and surface properties, nanocarriers can significantly increase the bioavailability of drugs, offering targeted delivery to specific skin layers. Recent advancements in nanocarrier technology have shown promising results in overcoming the skin's natural barriers, thereby improving therapeutic outcomes in treating chronic diseases such as psoriasis and skin cancers.
Physical assistive technologies, such as iontophoresis and ultrasound, have demonstrated significant potential to enhance the efficiency and depth of transdermal drug delivery when combined with nanocarriers. These methods promote the transport of drug-loaded nanoparticles through the skin layers by applying mild electrical fields or ultrasound waves. This process increases the efficiency and speed of transdermal delivery. Moreover, the synergy between physical stimuli and nanocarriers could pave the way for more effective and non-invasive therapeutic strategies. Additionally, magnetic field-assisted technology has shown promise in enhancing the penetration and controlled release of drugs via nanocarriers, allowing for targeted and efficient drug delivery in TDDS. The synergistic application of magnetic field-responsive nanocarriers could further optimize therapeutic outcomes and open new possibilities in personalized medicine.
However, most studies on nanomedicines in TDDS are limited to in vitro tests and animal experiments, with fewer drugs undergoing clinical trials. Thus, accelerating the clinical evaluation of nanomedicines in TDDS is essential. Meanwhile, the development of more drugs for TDDS can also provide more possibilities for drug delivery. Small molecule penetration into the skin adheres to Fick's Law, and we must consider its factors when developing a TDDS. However, nanoparticles have many features, such as particle size, hindrance of particles' membranes or matrices, and deformation of vesicles. All these factors do not exist in Fick's Law as obeyed by small molecules. Therefore, we urgently need a new law to fit the penetration dynamics of nanoparticles across the skin based on the evolution of AI. AI is poised to revolutionize TDDS by enabling the design of personalized treatments. AI can analyze vast datasets to predict skin permeability, optimize drug formulations, and assist in patient-specific drug delivery strategies. Additionally, designing rational TDDS requires a comprehensive understanding of how pathological changes in the skin, such as epidermal thickening, inflammation, edema, hair follicle disorders like alopecia, and angiogenesis in skin tumors, impact nanoparticle penetration. While these factors are not fully understood, they must be considered when designing TDDS with AI assistance.
In recent years, wearable smart patches have garnered attention as a potential solution for TDDS. These patches integrate stimulus-responsive materials and electronic units to regulate the timing and mode of drug release. However, challenges remain in their practical application, including concerns about safety, development complexity, and consistent performance. Moreover, physical assistive technologies influence the skin barrier and impact the components within TDDS, particularly deformable vesicles. On an industrial scale, the production of TDDS faces significant challenges, such as product stability, batch-to-batch consistency, and regulatory standards. These issues must be addressed to support the widespread application of TDDS. Consequently, as we continue to develop and refine transdermal nanomedicines, personalized and highly efficient drug delivery systems are becoming increasingly promising due to their potential to tailor treatments to individual patient needs. Future studies should focus on refining the design of nanocarriers, using AI-driven predictive modeling to improve their specificity and reduce side effects by accurately predicting patient-specific responses. Additionally, innovations such as wearable smart patches, which integrate sensors and AI to monitor and control drug release in real time, are expected to be crucial in enhancing therapeutic outcomes. The ultimate goal is to create a fully integrated, patient-specific TDDS that can improve drug efficacy and enhance patient compliance through non-invasive, convenient treatments.
Looking to the future, developing a new theoretical framework to guide the evolution of TDDS and facilitate their clinical application is essential. With ongoing advancements in AI and other innovative technologies that support TDDS development, TDDS has immense potential to improve global healthcare by offering efficient, non-invasive personalized treatments for individual patients.
