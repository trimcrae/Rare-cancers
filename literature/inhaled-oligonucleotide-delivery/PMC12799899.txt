## Abstract
The accumulation of HIFs regulated by the PHD-pVHL pathway represents the classical mechanism that transcriptionally mediates cellular adaptation to hypoxia. Extensive hypoxic stress activates cell death. Comprehensive understanding the mechanisms of hypoxia-induced cell death is essential for treatment of several diseases. Here, we revealed that β-TrCP1 degradation is essential for hypoxia-induced cell death and tissue injury. Hypoxia promotes β-TrCP1 degradation via proteasome pathway in HIFs-independent manner, and SMURF2 is identified as the corresponding E3 ligase. Additionally, acetylation of β-TrCP1 decreases after hypoxia, which is required for β-TrCP1 degradation. Tip60 establishes the acetylation of β-TrCP1 under normoxic conditions and is prolyl-hydroxylated by PHD2. Prolyl Hydroxylation stabilizes Tip60 under normoxic conditions, while hypoxia promotes the degradation of Tip60 by decreasing its prolyl hydroxylation. HDAC8 catalyses the deacetylation of β-TrCP1, which is enhanced after hypoxia. Loss of β-TrCP1 acetylation after hypoxia promotes the binding of SMURF2 to β-TrCP1 and its degradation. p53 is a substrate of β-TrCP1, and loss of β-TrCP1 upon hypoxia results in the accumulation of p53, which is responsible for hypoxia-induced cell death and tissue injury. Thus, this study illustrates a previously unappreciated posttranscriptional hypoxia-responsive mechanism constituted by PHD2-Tip60-HDAC8-SMURF2-β-TrCP1 degradation axis to promote p53 accumulation to mediate cell death and tissue injury.

## Introduction
Hypoxia usually occurs under physiological or pathological conditions in which the oxygen availability in tissues and organs is decreased. Hypoxia is involved in multiple diseases, including cancers and ischaemia of the brain, heart, liver, kidney [ , ]. Hypoxia-inducing factors (HIFs), including HIF1α, HIF2α, HIF3α and HIF1β, which allow cells to adapt to hypoxic stimuli, are the most extensively studied molecules [ ]. Under normoxic conditions, HIF1α is prolyl-hydroxylated by prolyl hydroxylases (PHDs), and hydroxylated HIF1α is then recognized by the E3 ubiquitin ligase von Hippel Lindau (pVHL) to earmark HIF1α for proteasomal degradation [ ]. Under hypoxic conditions, the PHDs-mediated HIF1α prolyl hydroxylation process is inhibited because of the limited oxygen availability, resulting in the accumulation of HIF1α to promote cellular adaptation under hypoxic conditions [ ]. However, in addition to the HIFs-mediated classical hypoxia-responsive pathway, other explorations could be needed for a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms of cellular adaptation to hypoxia. Moreover, extensive hypoxic stress can induce cell death [ – ], but the underlying mechanisms still need to be further determined.
The SKP1-CUL1-F-box protein (SCF) complexes constitute the largest family of E3 ubiquitin ligases and contain multiple components [ ]. Beta-transducin repeat containing E3 ubiquitin protein ligase (β-TrCP) is the best characterized F-box protein in mammals and functions as the substrate receptor in the SCF complex [ ]. By collaborating with the scaffold protein cullin-1, the adaptor protein SKP1, and the RING protein RBX1, β-TrCP contributes to the formation of the SCF E3 ubiquitin ligase activity centre [ ]. Mammalian cells contain two isoforms of β-TrCP proteins, β-TrCP1 and β-TrCP2. Both proteins have similar structural and biochemical properties and play vital roles in several physiological and pathological processes, including cell cycle progression and tumorigenesis, by targeting distinct substrates for ubiquitination and degradation [ ]. Although many substrates of β-TrCP have been identified, the regulation of the stability of β-TrCP themselves and the response of β-TrCP to hypoxia are largely unclear.
In this study, we revealed that hypoxia promotes the degradation of β-TrCP1 in a HIFs-independent manner, which is required for hypoxia-induced cell death and tissue injury. Acetylation is essential for the stabilization of β-TrCP1 under normoxic conditions and is inversely regulated by the acetyltransferase Tip60 and the deacetylase HDAC8. The acetylation of β-TrCP1 prevents the SMAD specific E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 2 (SMURF2)-mediated degradation of β-TrCP1 via the 26S proteasome pathway. Moreover, Tip60 is stabilized by PHD2-mediated prolyl hydroxylation under normoxic conditions. However, under hypoxic conditions, Tip60 is degraded when prolyl hydroxylation is lost, and the binding of HDAC8 to β-TrCP1 is increased to eliminate the acetylation of β-TrCP1. SMURF2-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of β-TrCP1 is then accelerated. In addition, p53 has been identified as a substrate of β-TrCP1 and is accumulated after hypoxia, contributing to hypoxia-induced and β-TrCP1 degradation-accelerated cell death and tissue injury. Thus, our study demonstrates a novel mechanism constituted by Tip60, HDAC8, SMURF2, β-TrCP1 and p53 that transduces oxygen signaling via the PHD2-mediated hydroxylation on Tip60 to control hypoxia-induced cell death and tissue injury.

## Results

## Hypoxia promotes the degradation of β-TrCP1 via the 26S proteasome pathway, and loss of β-TrCP1 is essential for hypoxia-induced cell death and tissue injury
Extensive hypoxia can trigger cell death in mammals [ , , ]. To determine which types of cell death are involved in hypoxia-induced cell death, HepG2 and HeLa cells exposed to 1% O2 (hypoxia) were treated with multiple cell death inhibitors, including Z-VAD-FMK, Necrostain-1, Ferrostatin-1 and Chloroquine (C.Q.) to block apoptosis, necroptosis, ferroptosis and autophagy, respectively. Our data demonstrated that hypoxia-induced cell death was impaired after treatment with Z-VAD-FMK, Necrostain-1 and Ferrostatin-1, but not with C.Q. (Fig.  A, Fig. A, and B), suggesting that multiple types of cell death are involved in hypoxia-induced cell death. Moreover, we found that the protein levels of β-TrCP1 decreased after hypoxia in a time-dependent manner (Fig.  B) and in multiple cell lines, including MDA-MB-231, HeLa, HEK293T, MCF7, KYSE30, H1299 and HaCaT cells, which were challenged with hypoxia for 24 h (Fig. C). Moreover, the protein levels of transfected exogenous β-TrCP1 also decreased after hypoxia (Fig. D). Reoxygenation reversed the hypoxia-induced decrease in β-TrCP1 protein levels (Fig.  C). In contrast to those of β-TrCP1, the protein levels of β-TrCP2 did not obviously change upon hypoxia stimulation, supporting a specific response of β-TrCP1 in sensing hypoxia (Fig. E).
 To determine whether the decrease in β-TrCP1 is involved in hypoxia-induced cell death, we altered the expression of β-TrCP1 in HepG2 and HeLa cells. HepG2 and HeLa cells were challenged with hypoxia for different durations, and cell death was analysed. Prolonged (72 h) hypoxia killed cells with nearly 60% cell death, and depletion of β-TrCP1 slightly increased cell death even under normoxic conditions, whereas overexpression of β-TrCP1 strongly reduced cell death under hypoxic conditions, suggesting that β-TrCP1 protects cells from hypoxia-induced cell death (Fig.  D, Fig. F, and G). The hypoxia-induced changes in the levels of markers of each type of cell death, including phosphorylated RIPK1 and RIPK3 (p-RIPK1 and p-RIPK3) for necroptosis, cleaved caspase 3 for apoptosis, and SLC7A11 and GPX4 for ferroptosis, were reversed by β-TrCP1 overexpression, further supporting the protective roles of β-TrCP1 in hypoxia-induced cell death (Fig.  E). The decrease in β-TrCP1 was further validated in mouse tissues. Our data revealed that the protein levels of β-TrCP1 in the mouse liver and brain were also reduced when the mice were exposed to 7% O2 (hypoxia), although the reduced β-TrCP1 protein levels in the mouse brain slightly recovered after prolonged hypoxia (Fig.  F). Consistent with the observed cell death induced by hypoxia in cultured cell lines (Fig.  A, D and E, and Fig. A, B, F and G), hypoxia also resulted in obvious cell death and hydropic swelling in the mouse liver (Fig.  G), together with elevated levels of serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) but not bilirubin (Fig.  H). Moreover, hypoxia-induced cell death was completely rescued when the mice were re-exposed to oxygen (Fig. H). Thus, these data suggest that liver injury potentially occurs after hypoxia. Furthermore, knockout of β-TrCP1 (β-TrCP1−/−) (Fig. I and J) substantially enhanced hypoxia-induced cell death and hydropic swelling, whereas overexpression of β-TrCP1 by tail vein injection of the Flag-β-TrCP1-overexpressing adeno-associated virus serotype 8 (AAV8) vector under the control of a liver-specific Tbg promoter (Fig. K) in the β-TrCP1−/− mouse liver almost completely abolished the hypoxia-induced cell death and hydropic swelling disorders (Fig.  I). The levels of serum ALT and AST but not bilirubin were further increased in β-TrCP1−/− mice after hypoxia, whereas overexpression of β-TrCP1 in β-TrCP1−/− mice abolished the increases in serum ALT and AST levels after hypoxia observed in β-TrCP1−/− mice (Fig.  J). Thus, these data indicate that loss of β-TrCP1 is likely essential for hypoxia-induced cell death and liver injury.
To determine how the decrease in β-TrCP1 protein levels was regulated upon hypoxia, the mRNA levels of β-TrCP1 were first examined. We found that hypoxia did not alter the mRNA levels of β-TrCP1, suggesting that posttranscriptional regulation occurred in response to changes in β-TrCP1 protein levels after hypoxia (Fig.  K). Next, to determine whether protein degradation is responsible for the hypoxia-induced decrease in β-TrCP1 protein levels, hypoxia-stimulated HEK293T and HeLa cells were further treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 and the autophagy inhibitor Bafilomycin A1 (Baf A1) for 12 h. The decrease in β-TrCP1 protein levels after hypoxia was completely abolished by MG132 but not Baf A1, suggesting that the 26S proteasome-mediated protein degradation pathway is involved in the hypoxia-induced downregulation of β-TrCP1 protein levels (Fig.  L). Moreover, chase and ubiquitination assays revealed that both the protein degradation rate (Fig.  M) and the ubiquitination level (Fig.  N) of β-TrCP1 were increased under hypoxic conditions. Thus, these data demonstrated that hypoxia promotes the protein degradation of β-TrCP1 via the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway (Fig.  O).

## β-TrCP1 degradation after hypoxia is unrelated to HIFs and reactive oxygen species (ROS)
HIFs are the most classical hypoxia-responsive factors [ ]. We therefore wondered whether HIFs are involved in hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation. We depleted both HIF1α and HIF2α in HEK293T and HeLa cells and exposed the cells to hypoxia for 24 h. Our results showed that depletion of both HIF1α and HIF2α could not rescue the decrease in β-TrCP1 protein levels after hypoxia (Fig. S2A). Moreover, under normoxic conditions, overexpression of the HIF1α and HIF2α mutants, HIF1α-P402A/P564A and HIF2α-P405A/P531A, whose prolyl hydroxylation sites were mutated, allowing both HIF1α [ , ] and HIF2α [ , ] to be stabilized in HeLa cells under normoxic conditions, also had no obvious effect on β-TrCP1 protein levels (Fig. S2B). These data indicate that HIFs are not regulators of β-TrCP1 protein levels under either hypoxic or under normoxic conditions. Moreover, VH298 (a specific inhibitor of pVHL) was used to inhibit pVHL activity to accumulate HIF proteins in HEK293T cells. However, no obvious effect on β-TrCP1 protein levels was detected upon VH298 treatment under either normoxic or hypoxic conditions (Fig. S2C), further supporting the above findings. As ROS usually accumulate after hypoxia [ ], we next tested whether ROS affect hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 downregulation. H2O2 treatment was used to mimic ROS accumulation in HeLa cells. However, no obvious effect on β-TrCP1 protein levels was detected after H2O2 treatment (Fig. S2D). As ERK was reported to be activated upon H2O2 stimulation [ , ], phosphorylated ERK levels were increased after H2O2 treatment (Fig. S2D), suggesting the effectiveness of H2O2. Moreover, the ROS inhibitor NAC was used to inhibit ROS under hypoxic conditions. However, NAC also did not affect the protein levels of β-TrCP1 in either HEK293T or HeLa cells under normoxic or hypoxic conditions (Fig. S2E). Thus, these data demonstrated that the degradation of β-TrCP1 induced by hypoxia is independent of both HIFs and ROS.

## HDAC8-mediated deacetylation is essential for hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation
Acetylation has also been reported to regulate protein degradation by affecting ubiquitination [ , ]. We therefore examined whether the acetylation of β-TrCP1 affects hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation. First, two types of deacetylase inhibitors, Nicotinamide (NAM, a sirtuin (SIRT) family deacetylase inhibitor) and Trichostatin A (TSA, a histone deacetylase (HDAC) family deacetylase inhibitor), were used in HEK293T, HepG2 and HeLa cells under hypoxic conditions. Our results revealed that treatment with TSA completely blocked hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation, whereas NAM treatment had no obvious effect (Fig.  A and Fig. S3), suggesting that the HDACs-mediated deacetylation of β-TrCP1 is essential for hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation. Next, the acetylation levels of β-TrCP1 under normoxic and hypoxic conditions were examined in HEK293T and HepG2 cells. Consistently, the acetylation levels of β-TrCP1 decreased after hypoxia (Fig.  B), whereas TSA treatment completely abolished the decrease in β-TrCP1 acetylation after hypoxia (Fig.  C). To determine the HDACs responsible for hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 deacetylation, we depleted all the HDACs with their specific siRNAs in HEK293T cells. First, β-TrCP1 protein levels were tested after HDACs depletion. We found that depletion of multiple HDACs, including HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC4, HDAC5, HDAC6, HDAC7 and HDAC8, rescued hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation (Fig.  D), suggesting that these HDACs are likely either directly or indirectly involved in hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 deacetylation and degradation. To identify the direct deacetylase for hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 deacetylation, Co-IP assays were performed in HEK293T cells under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions to determine the interactions between β-TrCP1 and the above HDAC candidates and their responses to hypoxia. Among them, only HDAC7 and HDAC8 interacted with β-TrCP1, and the interactions were enhanced after hypoxia (Fig.  E), suggesting that these two HDACs could be deacetylases for β-TrCP1, especially under hypoxic conditions. Moreover, in vitro deacetylation assays demonstrated that both HDAC7 and HDAC8 could deacetylate acetylated β-TrCP1 in vitro, but HDAC8 was more efficient than HDAC7, suggesting that HDAC8 could be the predominant deacetylase of β-TrCP1 (Fig.  F). Purified His6-tagged HDAC8 and GST-tagged β-TrCP1 could also interact with each other in vitro (Fig.  G), further supporting the above findings. The effects of HDAC8 on β-TrCP1 acetylation levels, especially under hypoxic conditions, were also examined. We depleted HDAC8 in HEK293T cells and exposed the cells to hypoxia for 24 h or not. We found that loss of HDAC8 completely abolished the decrease in β-TrCP1 acetylation levels after hypoxia, suggesting that HDAC8 is required for the hypoxia-induced decrease in β-TrCP1 acetylation (Fig.  H). Thus, these data demonstrated that HDAC8 is a deacetylase of β-TrCP1 and that hypoxia promotes the deacetylation of β-TrCP1 by HDAC8. We next tested the effect of HDAC8 on the stability of β-TrCP1 under normoxic conditions. Consistent with the positive correlation between β-TrCP1 acetylation and its protein levels (Fig.  A and B) and the fact that HDAC8 is the bona fide deacetylase of β-TrCP1 (Fig.  F-H), overexpression of HDAC8 in HEK293T cells decreased the protein levels of β-TrCP1, and this decrease was reversed by both TSA and MG132 treatment (Fig.  I), suggesting that HDAC8 promotes the degradation of β-TrCP1 via the 26S proteasome pathway in a manner dependent on its deacetylase activity. Conversely, depletion of HDAC8 increased the protein levels of β-TrCP1 (Fig.  J). Moreover, HDAC8 positively regulates the degradation of β-TrCP1, as overexpression of HDAC8 increased the degradation rate of β-TrCP1 in HEK293T cells (Fig.  K), whereas depletion of HDAC8 had the opposite effect (Fig.  L). These data demonstrated that acetylation stabilizes β-TrCP1, especially under normoxic conditions, whereas hypoxia promotes the deacetylation of β-TrCP1. HDAC8 is the deacetylase of β-TrCP1, and HDAC8-mediated β-TrCP1 deacetylation is enhanced by hypoxia, resulting in the destabilization of β-TrCP1 (Fig.  M).
 

## Tip60 is the acetyltransferase of β-TrCP1 and hypoxia impairs the Tip60-mediated acetylation of β-TrCP1
To determine the acetyltransferase for β-TrCP1, the effects of multiple acetyltransferases on β-TrCP1 protein levels were first tested under normoxic conditions in HEK293T cells. Among them, overexpression of Tip60 was found to increase the protein levels of β-TrCP1 obviously (Fig.  A), and this positive regulation of Tip60 on β-TrCP1 protein levels was likely in an enzymatic activity-dependent manner, as treatment with the Tip60-specific inhibitor MG149 and overexpression of the dominant negative (DN) mutant lacking acetyltransferase activity both failed to increase β-TrCP1 protein levels in HEK293T cells (Fig.  B). Moreover, wild-type Tip60-overexpressing and Tip60-DN mutant-overexpressing HEK293T cells were challenged with hypoxia for 24 h, and we found that hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation was rescued by the overexpression of wild-type Tip60 but not its DN mutant (Fig.  C), suggesting that Tip60 could be the acetyltransferase of β-TrCP1 under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions. Moreover, consistent with the positive effect of Tip60 on β-TrCP1 protein levels (Fig.  A-C), the protein levels of Tip60 decreased after hypoxia, in parallel with the decrease in β-TrCP1 protein levels (Fig.  D). The mechanism of the decrease in Tip60 protein levels upon hypoxic stimulation will be examined in a later section. We next tested whether Tip60 interacts with β-TrCP1 and the dynamics of the interaction upon hypoxia. As expected, Co-IP assays revealed that both exogenous (Fig. S4A and B) and endogenous (Fig.  E and F) Tip60 and β-TrCP1 interacted with each other in HEK293T cells under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, and the interaction is impaired after hypoxia (Fig.  G). We next examined the direct catalytic effect of Tip60 on β-TrCP1 acetylation. The GST-tagged Tip60 and His6-tagged β-TrCP1 proteins were purified from E. coli, and the direct interaction between these two proteins was confirmed (Fig.  H). Tip60 indeed catalysed the acetylation of β-TrCP1 in vitro, suggesting that Tip60 likely functions as an acetyltransferase for β-TrCP1 (Fig.  I). Moreover, overexpression of wild-type Tip60, but not the DN mutant, increased the acetylation level of β-TrCP1 in HEK293T cells under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Fig.  J). Conversely, depletion of Tip60 decreased the level of β-TrCP1 acetylation under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Fig.  K). Next, the effect of Tip60 on β-TrCP1 protein stability was examined. First, in contrast to Tip60 overexpression (Fig.  A-C), depletion of Tip60 in HEK293T cells decreased the protein levels of β-TrCP1 under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, which was reversed by MG132 treatment (Fig.  L), suggesting that loss of Tip60 enhances the 26S proteasome-mediated protein degradation of β-TrCP1 and that Tip60 is a negative regulator of β-TrCP1 degradation. Moreover, chase assays were performed to determine the degradation rate of β-TrCP1. Overexpression of Tip60 inhibited the degradation of β-TrCP1 in HEK293T cells (Fig.  M), whereas depletion of Tip60 increased it (Fig.  N), further supporting the above notion that Tip60 negatively regulates β-TrCP1 degradation. These data demonstrated that Tip60 is the acetyltransferase of β-TrCP1 and that hypoxia impairs the Tip60-mediated acetylation of β-TrCP1. The regulation of β-TrCP1 acetylation by Tip60 further affects the protein stability of β-TrCP1 (Fig.  O).
 

## K96 and K294 are the acetylation sites of β-TrCP1 that are regulated by Tip60 and HDAC8 and are responsible for hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation
Next, we investigated the acetylation sites of β-TrCP1, especially the sites regulated by the Tip60-HDAC8 axis. First, we performed mass spectrometry analysis to determine the acetylated lysine (K) sites on β-TrCP1. Three lysine residues were identified, namely, K96, K220 and K294 (Fig.  A). We also predicted the acetylation sites using the GPS-PAIL database (http://pail.biocuckoo.org/index.php), and four residues were obtained: K11, K14, K268 and K430 (Fig.  B). First, all seven identified K residues were individually mutated to alanine (A), and the effect of each K-A mutation on the protein levels of β-TrCP1 was tested. HEK293T cells overexpressing wild-type (WT) β-TrCP1 or its K-A mutants were exposed to hypoxia and treated with or without TSA. Our results showed that TSA treatment failed to rescue the β-TrCP1 protein levels in the K96A and K294A mutants compared with those in the wild-type β-TrCP1 and other mutants (Fig.  C), suggesting that K96 and K294 could be potential acetylation sites of β-TrCP1 that are responsible for hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation. We next determined the effects of the K96A and K294A mutations on β-TrCP1 acetylation, and the levels of β-TrCP1 acetylation were indeed impaired in these two K-A mutants (Fig.  D), further supporting the above findings. The K96 and K294 sites and their neighbouring residues are conserved in multiple species (Fig.  E). Moreover, in contrast to the increase in β-TrCP1 acetylation caused by Tip60 overexpression, Tip60 overexpression failed to increase the acetylation levels of β-TrCP1 in both the K96A and K294A mutants, especially the K294A mutant (Fig.  F). Similarly, depletion of HDAC8 failed to increase the acetylation levels of β-TrCP1 in both the K96A and K294A mutants, which contrasts with the effect of HDAC8 depletion on wild-type β-TrCP1 (Fig.  G). Thus, these data suggest that the K96 and K294 sites are likely acetylation sites regulated by both Tip60 and HDAC8, whereas the K294 site is the major acetylation site regulated by Tip60. Consistent with the effect of acetylation on β-TrCP1 stability (Figs.  and ), our chase assays in wild-type β-TrCP1- and K-A mutant-overexpressing HEK293T cells revealed that both the K96A and K294A mutants were more unstable than the wild-type β-TrCP1 (Fig.  H), whereas K-A mutation on other lysines, including K11, K14, K220, K268 and K430, showed no obvious accelerating effect on the degradation rate of β-TrCP1 (Fig. S5). In contrast to the effect of the K-A mutation, the K-Q mutation of both the K96 and K294 residues, which mimic the acetylation status of the lysine residue, impaired the protein degradation of β-TrCP1 (Fig.  I), further supporting the positive effect of acetylation on the stability of β-TrCP1. Moreover, the ubiquitination levels of β-TrCP1 were increased in both the K294A and K96A mutants under hypoxic conditions, although the increase only weakly occurred in the K96A mutant, whereas in contrast to the K-A mutants, both K-Q mutants of β-TrCP1 showed decreased ubiquitination levels under hypoxic conditions (Fig.  J). Next, we tested the effects of K96 and K294 acetylation on β-TrCP1 protein levels in response to hypoxia in HEK293T cells. Intriguingly, the protein levels of both K-Q mutants were much higher than those of the wild-type and K-A mutants under hypoxic conditions, suggesting that both K-Q mutants can be stably existed even after hypoxia (Fig.  K). Moreover, HDAC8 depletion failed to rescue hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation in both K-A mutants, further supporting that the K96 and K294 acetylation sites could also be targets of HDAC8 (Fig.  L). Additionally, the increase in β-TrCP1 protein levels caused by Tip60 overexpression was impaired in the K294A mutant, but not in the K96A mutant, under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, suggesting that the K294 acetylation site could be the major target of Tip60 (Fig.  M and N). These data revealed that the K96 and K294 residues are the acetylation sites of β-TrCP1, the acetylation of which affects the ubiquitination and protein degradation of β-TrCP1. The acetylation of β-TrCP1 at both K96 and K294 is regulated by HDAC8, whereas K294 is the major acetylation site of β-TrCP1 that is regulated by Tip60 (Fig.  O).
 

## SMURF2 is the E3 ubiquitin ligase for hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation
SMAD specific E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 2 (SMURF2) was previously reported to be an E3 ligase that regulates the ubiquitination and degradation of β-TrCP1 in human cells under normoxic conditions [ ], and our recent study revealed that F-box and WD repeat domain containing 7 (FBXW7) also regulates its ubiquitination and degradation in mouse embryonic stem cells under normoxic conditions [ , ]. However, whether they are involved in hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation is unclear. To determine the E3 ubiquitin ligase responsible for hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation, siRNAs specific for SMURF2 and FBXW7 and siRNAs specific for other E3 ubiquitin ligases conserved in our laboratory were transfected into HEK293T cells, especially under hypoxic conditions (Fig.  A), and the RNAi efficiencies of the siRNAs were confirmed by RT-PCR (Fig. S6). We found that depletion of SMURF2 completely rescued the hypoxia-induced decrease in β-TrCP1 protein levels, suggesting that SMURF2 could be the major E3 ligase regulating hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation (Fig.  A). We next tested whether SMURF2 regulates the protein levels of β-TrCP1 under normoxic and hypoxic conditions. Our data demonstrated that overexpression of wild-type SMURF2 in HEK293T cells decreased β-TrCP1 protein levels under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, whereas overexpression of the mutant that lost its enzymatic activity (SMURF2-C716A) failed (Fig.  B). Moreover, the downregulation of β-TrCP1 protein levels by SMURF2 overexpression was completely abolished by MG132 treatment under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, suggesting that SMURF2 likely regulates β-TrCP1 protein levels through the 26S proteasome-mediated protein degradation pathway (Fig.  C). Next, the interaction between SMURF2 and β-TrCP1 was tested. Co-IP assays suggested that SMURF2 and β-TrCP1 interacted with each other in HEK293T cells under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Fig. S7A-C) and that their interaction was enhanced after hypoxia (Fig.  D). GST pull-down and His6 pull-down assays with the purified His6-tagged SMURF2 and GST-tagged β-TrCP1 proteins from E. coli confirmed the direct interaction between these two proteins (Fig. S7D). The domains required for the SMURF2-β-TrCP1 interaction were further determined. Our data revealed that the first WD40 domain of β-TrCP1 was required for the SMURF2-β-TrCP1 interaction (Fig. S7E). Conversely, β-TrCP1 likely associated with the 3 × WW domain and the HECTc domain of SMURF2 (Fig. S7F). We then examined the effect of SMURF2 on β-TrCP1 ubiquitination. Depletion of SMURF2 decreased the ubiquitination of β-TrCP1 (Fig. S7G) in HEK293T cells under normoxic conditions, whereas overexpression of wild-type SMURF2, but not the C716A-mutated SMURF2, whose enzymatic activity was lost, increased the ubiquitination of β-TrCP1 (Fig. S7H). Moreover, SMURF2 was required for hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 ubiquitination (Fig.  E), and SMURF2-mediated β-TrCP1 ubiquitination was increased after hypoxia (Fig.  F). Consistently, the protein degradation of β-TrCP1 was accelerated in SMURF2-overexpressing HEK293T cells under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions, with a shortened half-life (Fig.  G and H), but was inhibited after SMURF2 depletion, with an elongated half-life (Fig.  I and J). Moreover, C716A-mutated SMURF2 failed to regulate the protein degradation rate of β-TrCP1, suggesting that enzyme activity is required for the regulation of β-TrCP1 degradation by SMURF2 (Fig. S7I). Thus, these data indicate that SMURF2 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase of β-TrCP1 and contributes to hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 protein degradation.
 To determine whether acetylation affects the regulation of β-TrCP1 by SMURF2 during hypoxia, the effects of HDAC8 and Tip60 on the SMURF2-β-TrCP1 interaction in response to hypoxia were tested in HEK293T cells. Depletion of HDAC8 substantially abolished the hypoxia-induced increase in the SMURF2-β-TrCP1 interaction (Fig.  K), and a similar effect on the SMURF2-β-TrCP1 interaction was observed in Tip60-overexpressing cells (Fig.  L), suggesting that a decrease in β-TrCP1 acetylation is likely required for the increase in the SMURF2-β-TrCP1 interaction after hypoxia. Consistently, both the K96A and K294A mutants of β-TrCP1 showed increased associations with SMURF2 under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Fig.  M), whereas the K96Q and K294Q mutants of β-TrCP1 showed impaired associations with SMURF2 under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Fig.  N). Furthermore, the decrease in β-TrCP1 protein levels caused by SMURF2 overexpression under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions was abolished in both the K96Q and K294Q mutants (Fig.  O), whereas the opposite effect was observed in the K96A and K294A mutants (Fig.  P). Thus, these data indicate that loss of acetylation of β-TrCP1 at K96 and K294 after hypoxia enhances SMURF2-mediated β-TrCP1 protein degradation.

## Tip60 is prolyl-hydroxylated by PHD2, and prolyl hydroxylation stabilizes Tip60
We next explored the upstream regulatory mechanism of the observed “Tip60-HDAC8-SMURF2-β-TrCP1 degradation” hypoxia-responsive signaling. PHDs are the major factors involved in cellular oxygen sensing by regulating the prolyl hydroxylation of HIFs, therefore linking the oxygen signal to cellular actions [ ]. We therefore examined whether Tip60, HDAC8, SMURF2 and β-TrCP1 respond to PHD2. As prolyl hydroxylation is usually associated with the degradation of targeted proteins, such as HIFs [ ], we first tested the changes in Tip60, HDAC8, SMURF2 and β-TrCP1 protein levels upon pan-inhibition of PHDs or specific inhibition of PHD2 with CoCl2 or IOX2. HEK293T cells were treated with CoCl2 or IOX2 for different durations. Intriguingly, pan-inhibition of PHDs and even only PHD2 inhibition resulted in decreases in the protein levels of both Tip60 and β-TrCP1, whereas the protein levels of HDAC8 and SMURF2 were only slightly affected (Fig.  A). The mRNA levels of Tip60 and β-TrCP1 did not obviously change upon the inhibition of PHDs (Fig.  B), whereas MG132 treatment reversed the decreases in both proteins (Fig.  C), suggesting that the 26S proteasome-mediated protein degradation occurred on both Tip60 and β-TrCP1 after CoCl2 and IOX2 treatment. We therefore suspected that Tip60 and β-TrCP1 could be potential downstream targets of PHD2. To support this idea, we first examined whether PHD2 regulates the protein levels of Tip60 and β-TrCP1. As expected, overexpression of PHD2 in HEK293T cells increased the protein levels of both Tip60 and β-TrCP1 in a dose-dependent manner under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Fig.  D), whereas depletion of PHD2 decreased the protein levels of both Tip60 and β-TrCP1 (Fig.  E). Next, Co-IP analyses were performed in PHD2 together with Tip60- or with β-TrCP1- or with HDAC8- or with SMURF2-overexpressing HEK293T cells to determine the associations between PHD2 and Tip60, β-TrCP1, HDAC8 or SMURF2. We found that PHD2 interacted with Tip60 (Fig.  F), but not with β-TrCP1, HDAC8 or SMURF2 (Fig. S8A-C), in HEK293T cells, suggesting that only Tip60 could be a potential target of PHD2. The interaction of endogenous PHD2 and Tip60 was also confirmed (Fig. S8D). The results of the prolyl hydroxylation assays supported the above idea, as overexpression of PHD2 in Tip60-overexpressing HEK293T cells increased the prolyl hydroxylation of Tip60 (Fig.  G, left), whereas depletion of PHD2 decreased it (Fig.  G, right). The specificity of the used prolyl hydroxylation antibodies was also confirmed by testing the decrease in HIF1α prolyl hydroxylation upon hypoxia stimulation (Fig. S9). Moreover, inhibition of PHDs with both CoCl2 and IOX2 in Tip60-overexpressing HEK293T cells decreased the prolyl hydroxylation levels of Tip60 (Fig.  H). Thus, these data indicate that PHD2 likely functions as a prolyl hydroxylase for Tip60. Next, mass spectrometry analysis was performed to identify the hydroxylation sites of Tip60, and eight proline (P) residues in Tip60 were identified: P27, P126, P130, P159, P161, P374, P402 and P504. We individually mutated all the identified P residues to A, and all the P-A mutants were overexpressed in HEK293T cells for the hydroxylation assay. We found that the prolyl hydroxylation levels of Tip60 were markedly decreased in the P159A and P374A mutants, suggesting that P159 and P374 could be potential hydroxylation sites of Tip60 (Fig.  I). Consistently, overexpression of PHD2 failed to increase the prolyl hydroxylation levels of the P159A- and P374A-mutated Tip60 in HEK293T cells (Fig.  J). Thus, these data suggested that the P159 and P374 residues could be hydroxylation sites that are regulated by PHD2. As PHD2 positively regulates the protein levels of Tip60 (Fig.  D and E), we next determined whether PHD2 regulates Tip60 degradation via prolyl hydroxylation. Our results showed that the decrease in Tip60 protein levels after PHD2 depletion was reversed by MG132 treatment under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions in HEK293T cells (Fig.  K), suggesting that the proteasome-mediated protein degradation is indeed involved in PHD2-regulated changes in Tip60 protein levels. Consistently, our chase assays revealed that overexpression of PHD2 inhibited the protein degradation of Tip60, whereas depletion of PHD2 increased it (Fig.  L). Moreover, loss of P374 hydroxylation of Tip60 promoted the protein degradation of Tip60, as the P374A mutant, but not the P159A mutant, showed an elevated protein degradation rate (Fig.  M). Thus, these data demonstrated that PHD2-mediated P374 hydroxylation is essential for the stabilization of Tip60. Similar to the changes in Tip60 protein levels, the protein levels of β-TrCP1 are also regulated by PHD2 (Fig.  A, D and E), although β-TrCP1 fails to interact with PHD2 (Fig. S8A). Together with the notion that Tip60-mediated β-TrCP1 acetylation is essential for the stability of β-TrCP1 (Fig.  ), we therefore suspected that the changes in β-TrCP1 protein levels after interfering with PHD2 could be a consequence of altered Tip60 protein levels. As expected, the upregulation of β-TrCP1 protein levels after PHD2 overexpression in HEK293T cells was abolished when cells were treated with the Tip60-specific inhibitor MG149 (Fig. S10), supporting the idea that β-TrCP1 is likely changed following Tip60 in response to PHD interference. As hypoxia usually inhibits prolyl hydroxylation of substrates due to the limited supply of oxygen, we next tested the dynamics of Tip60 prolyl hydroxylation in response to hypoxia. As expected, hypoxia decreased the levels of prolyl hydroxylation of Tip60 in HEK293T cells (Fig.  N), which is consistent with the above observation that the protein levels of Tip60 decreased after hypoxia (Fig.  D). Thus, these data demonstrated that Tip60 is prolyl-hydroxylated and stabilized by PHD2 under normoxic conditions, whereas hypoxia decreased the prolyl hydroxylation of Tip60 and destabilized Tip60, suggesting that Tip60 likely functions as an indirect oxygen sensor mediated by PHD2 (Fig.  O).
 

## The Tip60-HDAC8-SMURF2-β-TrCP1 degradation regulatory axis is required for hypoxia-induced cell death by stabilizing p53
As Tip60 and HDAC8 are essential for the hypoxia-induced decrease in β-TrCP1 by regulating acetylation of β-TrCP1 (Fig.  and Fig.  ), we tested whether Tip60 and HDAC8 affect hypoxia-induced cell death through β-TrCP1. As expected, similar to β-TrCP1 overexpression (Fig.  D, Fig. F, and G), depletion of HDAC8 strongly reduced hypoxia-induced cell death in both HepG2 and HeLa cells, and the inhibition of cell death was β-TrCP1 dependent, as further depletion of β-TrCP1 partially reversed the inhibitory effect of HDAC8 depletion on hypoxia-induced cell death (Fig.  A, Fig. A, and B). Similarly, overexpression of Tip60 also reduced hypoxia-induced cell death in a β-TrCP1-dependent manner (Fig.  B, Fig. C, and D). Moreover, HepG2 and HeLa cells overexpressing the K96Q and K294Q mutants of β-TrCP1, which can be stably existed even under hypoxic conditions (Fig.  K), also showed reduced cell death upon hypoxia stimulation compared with cells overexpressing wild-type β-TrCP1 (Fig.  C, Fig. E, and F). Thus, these data indicate that acetylation of β-TrCP1 regulated by Tip60 and HDAC8 regulates hypoxia-induced cell death. As SMURF2 is the E3 ubiquitin ligase for β-TrCP1 in response to hypoxia (Fig.  ), the effect of SMURF2 on hypoxia-induced cell death was also examined. Similar to the effects of β-TrCP1 overexpression (Fig.  D, Fig. F, and G), HDAC8 depletion (Fig.  A, Fig. A, and B) and Tip60 overexpression (Fig.  B, Fig. C, and D), depletion of SMURF2 in HepG2 and HeLa cells reduced hypoxia-induced cell death in a manner dependent on the presence of β-TrCP1 (Fig.  D, Fig. G, and H).
 We next investigated the potential downstream mechanism by which loss of β-TrCP1 regulates cell death upon hypoxia. p53 is a downstream substrate of β-TrCP1 [ ], and as the most famous tumour suppressor, p53 is widely recognized as a “super” positive regulator that regulates distinct types of cell death, including apoptosis [ , ], necroptosis [ ], and ferroptosis [ – ]. We therefore suspected that p53 could be the downstream mechanism mediating the protective effect of β-TrCP1 on hypoxia-induced cell death. First, we confirmed the regulation of p53 stability by β-TrCP1. Consistent with a previous report [ ], β-TrCP1 indeed associated with p53 in HEK293T cells (Fig. S12A) and positively regulated the ubiquitination of p53, as overexpression of β-TrCP1 increased the ubiquitination level of p53 (Fig. S12B), whereas depletion of β-TrCP1 impaired it (Fig. S12C). β-TrCP1 also positively regulated the protein degradation of p53, as the chase assays revealed that overexpression of β-TrCP1 promoted the degradation of p53 (Fig. S12D), whereas depletion of β-TrCP1 inhibited the degradation of p53 (Fig. S12E). Next, we tested the changes in p53 protein levels upon hypoxia challenge. As expected, in parallel with the decrease in β-TrCP1 protein levels after hypoxia, hypoxia increased the protein levels of p53 in both HepG2 and HeLa cells (Fig.  E and F, and Fig. S13A and B), and overexpression of β-TrCP1 downregulated the p53 protein levels under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Fig.  E and Fig. S13A), whereas depletion of β-TrCP1 upregulated the p53 protein levels under both conditions (Fig.  F and Fig. S13B). Moreover, loss of β-TrCP1 accelerated hypoxia-induced cell death in both HepG2 and HeLa cells, whereas further depletion of p53 strongly inhibited the ability of β-TrCP1 depletion to accelerate hypoxia-induced cell death (Fig.  G and Fig. S13C). Conversely, p53 overexpression diminished the protective effect of β-TrCP1 overexpression on hypoxia-induced cell death in both HepG2 and HeLa cells (Fig.  H and Fig. S13D). Thus, these data suggest that the regulation of p53 stability by β-TrCP1 likely mediates the effect of β-TrCP1 on hypoxia-induced cell death.
To determine the effects of the observed Tip60-HDAC8-SMURF2-β-TrCP1-p53 regulatory axis on hypoxia-induced cell death in vivo, we examined the effects of several key components of this axis on cell death and tissue injury in wild-type or β-TrCP1−/− mice. Consistent with the effects of hypoxia on these factors observed in cultured cell lines (Figs.  D and  E), the protein levels of Tip60 decreased, and the protein levels of p53 increased in the mouse liver after hypoxia (Fig. S14A). Liver-specific depletion of Tip60 in mice by infection with the Tip60-specific shRNA (shTip60)-containing AAV8 under the control of a liver-specific Tbg promoter (Fig. S14B) substantially increased hypoxia-induced cell death and tissue injury in liver sections, whereas further overexpression of β-TrCP1 almost completely abolished hypoxia-induced cell death and liver injury (Fig.  I). In parallel, the levels of serum ALT and AST were further increased in shTip60-infected mice after hypoxia compared with those in wild-type mice, whereas overexpression of β-TrCP1 in shTip60-infected mice abolished the increase in the serum ALT and AST levels after hypoxia observed in shTip60-infected mice (Fig.  J). Liver-specific depletion of HDAC8 (Fig. S14C) in wild-type and β-TrCP1−/− mice showed that loss of HDAC8 abrogated hypoxia-induced cell death and liver injury in wild-type mice, but failed to rescue the lethal phenotype in β-TrCP1−/− mice (Fig.  K), underscoring the essential role of β-TrCP1 in HDAC8-regulated cell death and tissue injury. Consistently, hypoxia-induced elevations in serum ALT and AST were suppressed in HDAC8-deficient wild-type mice but remained elevated in HDAC8-depleted β-TrCP1−/− mice (Fig.  L). Moreover, the loss of β-TrCP1-enhanced cell death and liver injury (Fig.  I) was also abolished by further knockdown of p53 expression with the p53-specific shRNA (shp53)-containing AAV8 vector under the control of a liver-specific Tbg promoter (Fig.  M and Fig. S14D). Similarly, the increases in the levels of serum ALT and AST in β-TrCP1−/− mice after hypoxia were completely abolished by further knockdown of p53 expression (Fig.  N). Thus, these data indicate that the Tip60-β-TrCP1 degradation-p53 axis upon hypoxia stimulation in this study still functions in the regulation of the hypoxia-induced cell death and tissue injury in mice.

## Discussion
Our study reveals a previously unappreciated protein degradation pathway that targets β-TrCP1 in response to hypoxia stimulation independent of HIFs. Here, we suspected that the degradation of β-TrCP1 under hypoxic conditions could represent a separate “Yin” mechanism with respect to the accumulation of HIFs, which could represent a “Yang” mechanism for cellular adaptation to hypoxia. Both the so-called “Yin” and “Yang” mechanisms respond to hypoxia by further regulating the levels of distinct downstream targets. For example, HIFs regulate the transcription of many genes (e.g., VEGF, EPO, GLUT1, and ADRP) to coordinate cells to adapt to hypoxia [ ], whereas β-TrCP1 degradation promotes the accumulation of specific substrates (e.g., p53 in this study) via the proteasome-mediated protein degradation pathway to exert distinct responses to hypoxic challenges, such as cell death (Graphical Abstract). Therefore, the β-TrCP1 degradation pathway could represent a more direct mechanism than the canonical HIFs-mediated transcriptional regulatory mechanism. However, in addition to p53 investigated in this study, more substrates need to be identified to contribute to a comprehensive understanding of the roles of β-TrCP1 degradation signaling in hypoxia adaptation.
Hypoxia is a common feature of multiple physiological and pathological processes and is closely involved in many diseases, such as cancers, ischaemia injury- or ischaemia–reperfusion injury-related diseases, and inflammatory diseases [ , ]. In the present study, we found that hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation is essential for hypoxia-induced cell death and tissue injury. Mechanistically, hypoxia-induced β-TrCP1 degradation promotes p53 accumulation by regulating its degradation. p53 can then promote apoptosis, necroptosis or ferroptosis via distinct mechanisms [ – ]. Moreover, we also revealed that the PHD2-Tip60-HDAC8-SMURF2 axis is the upstream regulatory mechanism for β-TrCP1 degradation (Fig.  O). Therefore, targeting the above components may provide a potential effective strategy for the treatment of hypoxia-related diseases with characteristics of cell death, such as ischaemia injury or ischaemia–reperfusion injury, inflammatory diseases and even cancers.
In summary, our study revealed a previously unappreciated hypoxia-responsive mechanism involving the PHD2-Tip60-HDAC8-SMURF2-β-TrCP1 regulatory axis, which plays a key role in the regulation of hypoxia-induced cell death and tissue injury by targeting p53. In addition, this study contributes to the understanding of the upstream regulation of β-TrCP1 protein stability, especially under hypoxic conditions, as Tip60-HDAC8 axis-regulated acetylation and the E3 ligase SMURF2 were identified as key regulators of β-TrCP1 degradation.

## Limitations of the study
We determined the regulatory mechanism of β-TrCP1 degradation after hypoxia stimulation by the PHD2-Tip60-HDAC8-SMURF2 axis in detail and reported that Tip60 is likely a substrate of PHD2, suggesting that Tip60 could function as a more upstream regulator than β-TrCP1 in oxygen sensing. Moreover, both HIFs and Tip60 can be prolyl-hydroxylated by PHD2. However, opposite outcomes occur after prolyl hydroxylation of these two proteins. Prolyl hydroxylation of HIFs destabilizes HIFs [ ], whereas prolyl hydroxylation of Tip60 stabilizes Tip60. The exact mechanisms underlying these opposite effects of PHD2-mediated prolyl hydroxylation on protein stability are largely unclear. Moreover, the E3 ubiquitin ligases involved in PHD2-mediated prolyl hydroxylation-affected Tip60 degradation under hypoxic conditions were not identified in the present study. Therefore, more in-depth explorations are needed.

## Materials and methods

## Cell lines and cell culture
HEK293T human embryonic kidney cells, HeLa human cervical carcinoma cells, MDA-MB-231 and MCF7 human breast carcinoma cells, KYSE30 human oesophageal squamous carcinoma cells, H1299 human lung carcinoma cells and HaCaT human immortalized keratinocytes were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (SH30243.01, Cytiva, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.
For hypoxia stimulation, cells were cultured in an O2/CO2 incubator containing a gas mixture composed of 94% N2, 5% CO2 and 1% O2.

## Animals
β-TrCP1 knockout C57BL/6J mice were generated by GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China) using the CRISPR/Cas-mediated genome editing method. Two sgRNAs were used to generate the β-TrCP1 knockout mice, and their sequences were as follows: sgRNA-#1: 5’-TCTGTGCGTCACCATAGGTT-3’; sgRNA-#2: 5’-CAGAGCTTCGTAGGAACGAC-3’. Primers F1, R1 and F2, R2 used for genotyping are listed in Table . All the animals were housed in an environment with a temperature of 22 ± 1 °C, a relative humidity of 50 ± 1%, and a light/dark cycle of 12/12 h. Autoclaved food and water were provided ad libitum.
All animal experiments were authorized by the Animal Experimental Ethics Committee of Henan University (HUSOM2022-348, Kaifeng, Henan, PR China) and conducted according to the AAALAC and IACUC guidelines.

## Cell transfection
Cells were transfected with plasmids or siRNAs using Lipo8000 transfection reagent (C0533, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer's standard protocol.

## Plasmid construction
The cDNAs of human β-TrCP1, SMURF2, HDAC8 and PHD2 were amplified from total RNA extracted from HEK293T cells by RT-PCR. The β-TrCP1 and PHD2 cDNAs were inserted into the pCMV-HA vector. The SMURF2 cDNA was inserted into the pCMV-Myc vector. The β-TrCP1, PHD2 and HDAC8 cDNA sequences were inserted into the pCMV-Flag vector. The HA-Tip60, HA-Tip60-DN and GST-Tip60 plasmids were gifted by Dr. Chen-Song Zhang and Dr. Sheng-Cai Lin from Xiamen University. The Flag-p53 plasmid was a gift from Dr. Lingqiang Zhang from Beijing Institute of Radiation Medicine. The His-Myc-ubiquitin plasmid was a gift from Dr. Zhijie Chang from Tsinghua University. The β-TrCP1 and Tip60 cDNAs were inserted into the pET-42b(+) vector containing a GST tag. The Flag-β-TrCP1-K11A, -K14A, -K96A, -K220A, -K268A, -K294A, -K430A, and -K96A + K294A mutants; the Flag-β-TrCP1-K96Q, -K294Q, and -K96Q + K294Q mutants; and the Flag-TIP60-P27A, -P126A, -P130A, -P159A, -P161A, -P374A, -P402A, and -P504A mutants were amplified with a Quick Mutation Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (D0206, Beyotime, China). The sequences of the primers used for plasmid construction are listed in Table S2.

Knockdown of gene expression was performed by transfecting cells with gene-specific siRNAs. The sequences of the siRNAs are listed in Table S3.

## RNA extraction and quantitative RT-PCR assay
Total RNA was isolated using RNAiso Plus reagent (9108, Takara, Japan), and cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using a reverse transcription kit (RR036A, Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer's standard protocol. The cDNA was then diluted, and quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed with three repeated samples using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (RR820A, Takara, Japan) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). The final reaction volume for each qPCR was 25 µL, consisting of 12.5 µL TB Green Premix Ex Taq II, 1 µL each of the forward and reverse primers (10 μM), 8.5 µL sterilized water, and 2 µL cDNA template. The following thermal cycling procedure was used for qPCR: 95 °C for 30 s, 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s, which was repeated for 40 cycles. The sequences of the primers used for qPCR assays are listed in Table S4.

## Antibodies and western blot analysis
Antibodies against β-TrCP1 were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (4394, USA) and Santa Cruz Biotechnology (sc-390629, USA). Antibodies against HIF1α (ab179483), RIPK3 (ab305054), S277-phosphorylated RIPK3 (p-RIPK3-S227) (ab209384), GPX4 (ab125066) and Flag-tag (ab205606) were purchased from Abcam (USA). Antibodies against SLC7A11 (26864-1-AP), HA-tag (51064-2-AP), GST-tag (66001-2-Ig), His6-tag (66005-1-Ig) and Myc-tag (60003-2-Ig) were purchased from Proteintech (China). Antibodies against β-Actin (TA-09) and HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (ZB-2301 and ZB-2305) were purchased from ZSGB-Bio (China). Antibodies against hydroxyproline (bs-10389R) were purchased from Bioss (China).
For Western blot analysis, cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) containing 1 mM PMSF, followed by sonication for 10 s and incubation in an ice bath for 30 min. Then, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 12000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. An appropriate volume of 5 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer (P0015, Beyotime, China) was added to the supernatant such that the SDS-PAGE loading buffer reached a final concentration of 1 ×. The protein-loading buffer mixture was boiled for 10 min. The obtained samples were subjected to SDS-PAGE to separate proteins. After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes, which were hybridized with primary antibodies diluted at 1:1000. HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies were used at a dilution of 1:5000. Finally, the signals on the membranes were detected using an eECL Western Blot Kit (CW0049M, CWBiol, China) on a ChemiDoc™ XRS + imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA) or an ImageQuant LAS 4000 system (GE, USA).

## Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay
Cells transfected with the indicated constructs were harvested and washed with ice-cold PBS 3 times. Cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (P0013D, Beyotime, China) containing 1 mM PMSF. Immunoprecipitation was performed at 4 °C overnight with the indicated antibodies or with normal IgG (A7028, Beyotime, China) as a negative control. Protein A/G agarose beads (sc-2003, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA) were then added and incubated for another 3 h. The agarose beads were subsequently centrifuged, washed with ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer 5 times, and collected. The precipitated proteins were released from the agarose beads by boiling in 1 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer (P0015A, Beyotime, China) for 10 min, and subjected to Western blot analysis with specific antibodies as indicated.

## In vitro protein binding assay
In vitro protein binding assays were performed as previously described [ ]. Briefly, GST- or His6-tagged β-TrCP1 (GST-β-TrCP1 or His6-β-TrCP1) and GST-tagged Tip60 (GST-Tip60) proteins were expressed and purified from E. coli. His6-tagged HDAC8 (His6-HDAC8) proteins (Ag11692) were purchased from Proteintech (China). GST-β-TrCP1 or His6-β-TrCP1 proteins were mixed with His6-HDAC8 or GST-Tip60 respectively as indicated. For immunoprecipitation (IP), antibodies against β-TrCP1 or HDAC8 were added to the mixtures, which were subsequently incubated overnight. Protein A/G agarose beads were subsequently added and incubated for another 3 h. The solutions were centrifuged to collect the agarose beads after they were washed 3 times with 1 mL of ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (P0013D, Beyotime, China) containing 1 mM PMSF. For pull-down assays, Ni-beads or Sepharose 4B-beads were added to the protein mixtures and incubated overnight. The solutions were centrifuged to collect the Ni-beads or Sepharose 4B-beads after they were washed 3 times with 1 mL of ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer containing 1 mM PMSF. The precipitated proteins were released by boiling in 1 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer (P0015A, Beyotime, China) and separated by SDS-PAGE. Western blot analysis was performed with the related antibodies.

## In vitro deacetylation assay
HEK293T cells were transfected with Flag-tagged β-TrCP1 (Flag-β-TrCP1) for 24 h. Cells were then treated with 10 μM TSA for another 12 h. Cells were harvested and lysed with 800 μL of RIPA lysis buffer (P0013D, Beyotime, China) containing 1 mM PMSF after being washed with 1 mL of ice-cold PBS for 3 times. Cell lysates were centrifuged at 4 °C at 12000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatants containing the acetylated Flag-β-TrCP1 (Ac-Flag-β-TrCP1) were collected. Ac-Flag-β-TrCP1 proteins were enriched by immunoprecipitation with antibodies against Flag-tag. The precipitated Ac-Flag-β-TrCP1 proteins were resuspended with 300 μL of deacetylation reaction buffer and subsequently divided into 7 equal groups (approximately 40 μL in each group). TSA (10 μM) was added or not added as indicated. His6-tagged HDAC4 (His6-HDAC4) (31364), HDAC7 (His6-HDAC7) (31535) and HDAC8 (His6-HDAC8) (Ag11692) proteins were purchased from Proteintech (China). 1 μg of His6-HDAC4, His6-HDAC7 or His6-HDAC8 proteins were individually added to the reaction system with or without TSA as indicated. The mixtures were gently rotated for approximately 1 h at 30 °C. Next, 10 μL of 5 × SDS-PAGE loading buffer (P0015, Beyotime, China) was added to the reaction mixtures, and the mixtures were boiled for 10 min. The acetylation levels of Flag-β-TrCP1 were detected by Western blot assay.
Deacetylation reaction buffer: 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 9.0), 2 mM MgCl2, 50 mM NaCl, 0.2 mM PMSF, 0.5 mM DTT.

## In vitro acetylation assay
GST-tagged Tip60 (GST-Tip60) and His6-tagged β-TrCP1 (His6-β-TrCP1) proteins were purified from E. coli. GST-Tip60 and His6-β-TrCP1 proteins were then mixed with 10 μM TSA and 10 mM NAM together with or without 100 μM acetyl-CoA (Ac-CoA) as indicated in 40 μL of acetylation reaction buffer. The mixtures were rotated for approximately 2 h at 30 °C. Finally, 10 μL of 5 × SDS loading buffer (P0015, Beyotime, China) was added to the reaction mixtures, which were then boiled for 10 min. Western blot analysis was performed to detect the acetylation levels of Flag-β-TrCP1.
Acetylation reaction buffer: 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 20% glycerol, 100 mM KCl, 1 mM DTT, 0.2 mM EDTA, 10 μM TSA, 10 mM NAM, 100 μM Ac-CoA.

## Cell death analysis
Cell death was determined by propidium iodide (PI) staining assay according to the manufacturer's instruction (C1352, Beyotime, China). Briefly, cells were gently washed with 1 mL of PBS 3 times, followed by incubation with 1 mL of PBS supplemented with 100 μL of PI Staining Solution in the dark for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were then washed with 1 mL of PBS 3 times. The total number of cells and the number of PI-positive cells were counted with an inverted fluorescence microscope (ECLIPSE Ti2, Nikon, Japan). Cell morphology was imaged.

## Hypoxic animal models
C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks) were randomly grouped and exposed to normoxia (21% O2) or hypoxia (7% O2 and 93% N2) for 72 h. Mice were housed in a chamber (ProOx-100, TOW-INT TECH, Shanghai, China) with a mixture of oxygen and nitrogen. The oxygen concentration was monitored and controlled by the ProOx-100 chamber. Soda-lime granules were used to remove CO2. All the mice remained alive at the end of the assay.

## Viral infection to modulate gene expression in mice
The synthesized control shRNA and the HDAC8-, p53-, Tip60-specific shRNA and the β-TrCP1 cDNAs were cloned and inserted into the adeno-associated virus serotype 8 (AAV8) vector (AAV-shCont., AAV-shHDAC8, AAV-shp53, AAV-shTip60 or AAV-β-TrCP1). β-TrCP1 wild-type (β-TrCP1+/+) or β-TrCP1 knockout (β-TrCP1−/−) mice were pretreated with AAV-shHDAC8, AAV-shp53, AAV-shTip60 or AAV-shCont. at a dose of 100 μL and 5 × 1011 vg/mL per mouse for four weeks via tail vein injection. All AAV8 vectors are controlled by the liver-specific Tbg promoter.

## ALT, AST and bilirubin measurement
All cytokines were measured by removing eyeballs after anaesthesia. The mouse blood samples were collected and stood for 30 min. The samples were subsequently centrifuged for 15 min at 4 °C at 4000 × g to separate the serum for biochemical assays.
ALT levels were measured with an Alanine aminotransferase (GPT) test kit (BC1555, Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturers’ instruction. AST levels were measured with an Aspartate aminotransferase (GOT) test kit (BC1565, Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturers’ instruction. Briefly, a standard curve was constructed with the standards in the kits, and the reaction reagents were added to the serum samples to be measured as instructed. The absorbance of each sample was determined with a microplate reader at a wavelength of 505 nm. The concentrations of the serum ALT and AST were then calculated according to the standard curve.
Total bilirubin levels were measured with a Total Bilirubin Content Assay Kit (BC5185, Solarbio, Beijing, China). The absorbance of each sample was determined with a microplate reader at a wavelength of 450 nm.

## Histological analysis
Animals were anaesthetized by 2% isoflurane inhalation and then injected with phosphate-buffered saline. The liver tissues were extracted, soaked in 4% paraformaldehyde and sent to Servicebio (Wuhan, China) for HE staining, immunofluorescence staining and TUNEL staining assays. The slices were photographed with a digital pathology scanner (3DHISTECH, Hungary).

## Mass spectrometry analysis
HEK293T cells were transfected with Flag-tagged Tip60 (Flag-Tip60) or Flag-tagged β-TrCP1 (Flag-β-TrCP1) plasmids. Cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (P0013D, Beyotime, China) containing 1 mM PMSF, and immunoprecipitation was performed with the Flag magnetic beads. The precipitated Flag-Tip60 or Flag-β-TrCP1 proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE, and the gels were stained with Coomassie brilliant blue. Flag-Tip60 or Flag-β-TrCP1 bands were cut from the gel and analysed by mass spectrometry at BGI Genomics Company (Shenzhen, China) or PTM BIO (Hangzhou, China). Briefly, the bands containing Flag-Tip60 and Flag-β-TrCP1 were destained in 50 mM NH4HCO3 in 50% acetonitrile (v/v) until clear. The gel pieces were dehydrated with 100 μL of 100% acetonitrile for 5 min, the liquid was removed, and the gel pieces were rehydrated in 10 mM dithiothreitol and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. The gel pieces were again dehydrated in 100% acetonitrile, the liquid was removed, and the gel pieces were rehydrated with 55 mM iodoacetamide. Samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 45 min. The gel pieces were washed with 50 mM NH4HCO3 and dehydrated with 100% acetonitrile. The gel pieces were then rehydrated with 10 ng/μL trypsin resuspended in 50 mM NH4HCO3 on ice for 1 h. Excess liquid was removed, and the gel pieces were digested with trypsin at 37 °C overnight. Peptides were extracted with 50% acetonitrile/5% formic acid, followed by 100% acetonitrile. The peptides were subjected to tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS). MaxQuant software was used for all MS/MS spectra analyses.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0 software using unpaired Student’s t tests to determine the statistical significance of the differences between groups. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. The bars and error bars in the figures indicate the mean ± S.D. values, and a p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference. p values are indicated in the figures as asterisks or “ns” as follows: *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001; ns, nonsignificant.
