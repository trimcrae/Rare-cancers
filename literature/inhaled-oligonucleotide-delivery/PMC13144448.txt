## Abstract
Although cancer immunotherapy has recently revolutionized treatment, the low response rate to existing immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) underscores the need for new druggable targets. Here, we find that SOAT1 is selectively expressed in cancer stem cell (CSC) and pharmacological inhibition with STK results in robust anti-tumor effects across various preclinical mouse models, including colon, liver, lung, breast, and melanoma cancer, with low toxicity. Mechanistically, treatment with STK (or gene knockdown of Soat1) induces the release of 20(S)-Hydroxycholesterol (20SOHC) from the tumor cells, and downstream activation of the trans-cellular 20SOHC (tumor)- GPR132 pathway in regulatory T cell (Treg), ultimately resulting in the suppression of Treg functions and enhanced dendritic cells and cytotoxic CD8+ T cell responses. Importantly, STK treatment synergizes with anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4 ICB therapy. Thus, our findings identify SOAT1 as a CSC metabolism checkpoint that facilitates immune evasion and SOAT1 inhibition as a promising strategy for advanced cancer immunotherapy.
Subject terms: Immunosurveillance, Cancer immunotherapy, Cancer stem cells

Cancer stem cells contribute to cancer evasion and relapse by suppressing the immune response. Here, the authors identify sterol O-acyltransferase 1 (SOAT1) as a cancer stem cell immune checkpoint and propose that SOAT1 inhibition results in oxysterol-mediated reprogramming of intratumoral regulatory T cells via a trans-cellular 20SOHC-GPR132, ultimately enhancing anti-tumor immunity in various preclinical mouse models.

## Introduction
Immunotherapy with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) and adoptive T-cell therapy (ACT) has achieved remarkable success recently. Nonetheless, its therapeutic efficacy is often hindered by tumor immune evasion and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) – . Among tumor cells, recent studies suggest that cancer stem cell (CSC) play an essential role in tumor immune evasion and relapse . CSC interfere with immune cell activity through CSC–immune cell communication, such as niche-dependent metabolic programs. Among the various immune cells in the TME, regulatory T cell (Treg) are recognized as pivotal players in mediating tumor immunosuppression . Intratumoral Treg cells accumulation correlates with decreased patient survival and resistance to antitumor immunotherapy. Tregs exert immunosuppressive function through multiple mechanisms. Identifying the factors specifically expressed in Treg cells and/or that influence Treg cell homeostasis and function is vital to understanding cancer pathogenesis and seeking therapeutic targets . Notably, understanding the interactions of CSC with Treg populations holds great promise to improve the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy.
In this study, we develop an in vivo chemical high-throughput screening platform to identify agents capable of destroying CSCs for cancer immunotherapy. Using the screening platform, we identified STK as an effective anti-CSC candidate. STK targets sterol o-acyltransferase 1 (SOAT1), an enzyme selectively expressed in cancer stem cells. Inhibition of SOAT1 induces the release of 20(S)-Hydroxycholesterol (20SOHC), which activates a trans-cellular signaling pathway that suppresses Treg activity and revitalizes anti-tumor response. Pharmacological inhibition of SOAT1 with STK demonstrates robust efficacy across multiple preclinical models, synergizes with anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4 therapy. Thus, our data positions SOAT1 as a promising CSC therapeutic target for reprogramming cholesterol metabolism.

## Results

## An in vivo chemical screening identified STK as an ACAT1/SOAT1 inhibitor to eliminate stem cell tumors
We developed a screening system to identify anti-tumor immunotherapeutic compounds using Drosophila stem cell tumor models . This Drosophila system allows for high-throughput chemical screening by mobilizing the entire organism, akin to the immune response in mammals, to combat stem cell tumors through compound administration with food. In these models, we induced neoplastic stem cell tumors by expressing a constitutively activated Ras in kidney stem cells or a dominant-negative Notch in intestinal stem cells. This approach allowed us to selectively identify inhibitors that effectively targeted tumorigenic stem cell (referred to as CSC) while sparing normal stem cells – . Notably, our screening identified the compound STK773477 (hereafter referred to as STK), which demonstrated efficacy in eliminating stem cell tumors (Fig.  ). Further evaluation of STK in cultured cancer cell lines (CT26, B16-F10, 4T1, LLC) and normal mouse embryonic fibroblast (MEF) cells confirmed that it has a relatively mild impact on cell growth (Supplementary Fig.  ).
 To elucidate the target of STK, we initiated a reverse virtual screening process utilizing the Pharmmapper and Chemmapper databases, accompanied by drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) mass spectrometry assays (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Through this approach, we identified 61 candidates that overlapped between the two databases, and DARTS analysis uncovered six enriched proteins in the STK treatment group. By further cross-referencing these enriched proteins with the initial candidate proteins, we found that SOAT1 was significantly represented in both screenings (Fig.  ), leading us to hypothesize that STK exerts its anti-tumor effects by targeting SOAT1.
SOAT1 plays a crucial role in regulating cholesterol metabolism, mediating cholesterol esterification into neutral cholesterol esters to be stored in lipid droplets . To validate that STK specifically targets SOAT1, we treated LLC, CT26, and Huh7 cells with STK and measured the mRNA levels of genes involved in cholesterol metabolism, including uptake, synthesis, esterification, and conversion. The data revealed a significant reduction in these processes (uptake, synthesis, esterification) in the STK treatment group compared to controls (Supplementary Fig.  ). Additionally, to assess the effects of SOAT1 on cholesterol ester levels, we measured cholesteryl ester levels in CT26 cells with Soat1 knockdown or STK treatment. The results showed that cholesteryl ester levels were significantly decreased in both Soat1 knockdown groups and STK treatment compared to controls (Supplementary Fig.  ). Given that the SOAT1 inhibitor may suppress tumor progression by disrupting cholesterol metabolism, we evaluated other cholesterol inhibitors, including MβCD and Ro48-8071 fumarate (referred to as Ro48), either alone or combined with STK. Like STK, both MβCD and Ro48 could significantly inhibit tumor progression individually, but they did not exhibit synergistic effects when combined with STK (Supplementary Fig.  ). We also compared the binding affinity of Avasimibe, a commercial inhibitor of SOAT1, with that of STK through molecular docking. The binding energy indicated that STK exhibited a stronger binding affinity to SOAT1 than Avasimibe (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). To further test this hypothesis, we purified the SOAT1 protein and conducted an isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) analysis, and the lower KD parameter confirms that STK is more effectively bound to SOAT1 than Avasimibe (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Additionally, given the structural similarity between SOAT1 and SOAT2, we measured STK’s IC₅₀ against purified SOAT2 in vitro, the result showed STK incubation had no effect on the enzymatic activity of SOAT2 (Supplementary Fig.  ).
To determine whether STK exerts its anti-tumor effects by targeting SOAT1 in vivo, we treated both CT26 and LLC xenograft tumor-bearing mice with STK in both the control (ShScram) and Soat1-knockdown (ShSoat1) groups. STK treatment significantly reduced tumor volume and tumor weight in the control group, but no additional reduction in the Soat1-knockdown group (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). However, STK treatment exhibited additional reduction in the Soat2-knockdown (ShSoat2) group (Supplementary Fig.  ). Together, these results suggested that the tumor suppression function of STK treatment is specifically relied on SOAT1 inhibition.

## SOAT1 is selectively expressed in cancer stem cells and STK performed a robust anti-tumor efficacy across various cancer models
We evaluated the tumor-inhibitory effects of STK in three orthotopic transgenic and four grafted mouse syngeneic tumor models. In a CSC-like colon cancer stem cell model (Lgr5-CreERT2/Apcf/f) , mice were administered STK (30 mg/kg daily) for two weeks, resulting in a significant reduction in the number of GFP-labeled cancer stem cells compared to the control group (Fig.  ). Similarly, in a hepatocellular carcinoma model (Cebpb-tTA/TetO-c-Myc or MYC-ON) , STK treatment (30 mg/kg daily) led to a notable decrease in liver tumor cell numbers and a significant increase in survival rates compared to controls (Fig.  ). Besides, in a lung carcinoma model (LSL-Kras/P53f/f or KP), STK treatment (30 mg/kg daily) markedly reduced lung tumor cell numbers and extended the survival period compared to controls (Fig.  ). Notably, Soat1 knockdown in KP mice exhibited a decreased tumor suppression effect compared to control (Supplementary Fig.  ).
 Additionally, we assessed the anti-tumor efficacy of STK in four xenograft models: CT26 colon cancer, B16-F10 melanoma cancer, 4T1 breast cancer, and LLC lung adenocarcinoma. In all cases, STK treatment significantly inhibited tumor growth, effectively reducing tumor volume and tumor numbers (Supplementary Fig.  ). Furthermore, STK exhibited a dose-dependent tumor suppression effect in the CT26 xenograft mouse model (Supplementary Fig.  ). When comparing the tumor-inhibitory effects of STK with the commercially available SOAT1 inhibitor Avasimibe, though both compounds significantly reduced tumor volume by the experimental endpoint, STK demonstrated superior efficacy, suggesting more significant clinical potential in vivo using murine (Supplementary Fig.  ) and human PDX cancer models (Supplementary Fig.  ).
We further investigated the expression of SOAT1 protein in human lung, colon, and liver cancers using the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining revealed elevated SOAT1 levels in these cancers compared to controls (Fig.  ). Additionally, transcript expression analysis of Soat1 across a variety of cancers using the TCGA database indicated high Soat1 expression in multiple human malignancies, including GBM, GBMLGG, LGG, LUAD, COAD, BRCA, LIHC, and others (Supplementary Fig.  ). We also explored the correlation between cancer stages and SOAT1 expression through the GEPIA2 database, revealing that SOAT1 expression increases in the middle and late stages of various cancers, including ACC, CESC, COAD, DLBC, LUAD, LUAS, and STAD (Supplementary Fig.  ). Moreover, survival analysis indicated that low SOAT1 expression correlates with improved prognosis in patients with liver cancer, lung cancer, thymoma, breast cancer, and melanoma (Fig.  ). Using established cancer stem cell (CSC) markers, CD133 and CD44, we isolated CSCs and non-CSCs, finding that CD133+CD44+ tumor cells exhibited significantly higher SOAT1 levels than their CD133−CD44− counterparts (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Further analysis of 9 paired patient samples from non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) confirmed that SOAT1 expression was substantially higher in tumor samples, especially in cancer stem cells (CSC, CD133+CD166+ tumor cells) (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). However, SOAT1 expression showed no difference in stem cells and non-stem cells in normal lung tissue from human and mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). To determine SOAT1 enrichment in CSC, we performed immunofluorescence analysis in CT26 xenograft tumor tissue, and the result showed SOAT1 colocalizes with the established CSC marker CD133 (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Besides, we performed a commonly used limiting dilution assay and found that STK treatment robustly decreased tumor-initiating capacity (Supplementary Fig.  ). Together, our data suggested that SOAT1 is enriched in CSC and functions as the central regulator of homeostasis in CSC.
These findings collectively highlight SOAT1 as an enriched target in multiple human tumors, particularly within cancer stem cells. Our data suggest that SOAT1 represents a promising therapeutic target for pan-cancer treatment strategies. Thus, efforts focused on inhibiting SOAT1 expression in tumor cells or developing specialized SOAT1 inhibitors hold substantial promise for clinical application.

## STK treatment activates a potent anti-tumor immune response and reshapes Tregs
To investigate the impact of STK treatment on TME, we conducted single-cell sequencing on lung cancer samples from Kras/p53f/f mice and liver cancer samples from MYC–ON mice. Dimensionality reduction cluster analysis was performed on various immune cell populations, including myeloid cells, neutrophils, B cells, epithelial cells, T cells, endothelial cells, NK cells, fibroblasts, and plasmacytoid dendritic cells, utilizing marker genes such as Csf1r, Csf3r, Cd79a, Krt18, Cd3e, Pecam1, Ncr1, Dcn, and Siglech (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). STK treatment significantly increased the infiltration of immunostimulatory T cells, C1qc+ M1 macrophages, cDC1 dendritic cells, cDC2 dendritic cells, and cDC3 dendritic cells compared to control groups, indicating STK treatment induced immune cell subsets into pro-inflammatory immunophenotypes (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Additionally, T cell receptor (TCR) analysis revealed a significant increase in CD3 clone diversity in the STK-treated group compared to controls (Fig.  ).
 To determine whether STK’s anti-tumor effects are immune system-dependent, we treated CT26 tumor-bearing immunodeficient NSG mice with STK. In these trials, both Soat1 knockdown and STK treatment lost their anti-tumor efficacy, contrary to results observed in immunocompetent mice (Figs.  , ), indicating that STK’s anti-tumor effects rely on the immune system. Given that immune memory is a hallmark of adaptive immunity, we evaluated whether STK treatment induces immune memory. Mice with initial B16-F10 tumor inoculations received STK or control treatments for one week, followed by a second tumor challenge on the opposite side. The results showed that STK treatment effectively inhibited tumor growth on both sides compared to controls, and the number of tumor-free animals was two on both sides compared to controls, suggesting that STK treatment induces an immune memory response (Fig.  ). To further ascertain whether this effect depends on T cells, we treated CT26 xenografts with anti-CD4 and anti-CD8 antibodies prior to STK administration. While STK treatment significantly inhibited tumor growth in the IgG control group, this effect was completely abrogated in the anti-CD4 and anti-CD8-treated groups (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ), confirming that STK’s anti-tumor effects rely on T cells. Moreover, STK treatment resulted in a significant increase in the infiltration of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in tumors of the IgG group, which was effectively blocked in the anti-CD4 and anti-CD8-treated groups (Supplementary Fig.  ). However, T cell infiltration in the spleen remained unchanged for both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig.  ). Overall, our data suggest that STK’s anti-tumor effects are contingent upon the immune system, particularly T cell involvement, and produce substantial immune memory effects.
We then conducted a detailed analysis of T cell subpopulations and found STK treatment exhibited a significant increase in populations of immune-activating effector CD8+ T cells and NKT cells, alongside a marked decrease in immunosuppressive Treg cells (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Further flow cytometry analysis in CT26 colon cancer xenografts revealed significant increases in pro-inflammatory subsets, including CD8+ T cells, CD69+CD8+ T cells, IFNγ+CD8+ T cells, Gzmb+CD8+ T cells, CD44+CD62L+ T cells, DP+ T cells, and TCF1+PD1+ T cells. Conversely, the anti-inflammatory proportion of Treg cells was significantly diminished (Supplementary Fig.  ). Similar trends were observed across other xenograft models, where the infiltration of CD8+ T cells was increased while the infiltration of Treg cells decreased (Supplementary Fig.  ). Besides, Soat1 knockout KP mouse models revealed enhanced proportions of CD69+CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and cDC1 dendritic cells in lung tumor tissues, while the infiltration of Treg cells was decreased (Supplementary Fig.  ). Tregs in the tumor microenvironment inhibit the antigen presentation capability of dendritic cells and suppress the functions of effector CD8+ T cells, facilitating tumor immune escape. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) and differential gene analysis in tumor cells indicated a robust activation of T cell-mediated immune responses in the STK treatment group (Supplementary Fig.  ), which suggested that STK treatment may regulate tumor cells to enhance immune response. Then, we analyzed cell communication interactions between tumor cells and immune cells and found that the cell chat between tumor cells and Tregs was significantly changed in the STK treatment group compared to controls (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ), which suggested STK treatment reduces Treg immunosuppression through cell-chat between tumor cells and Tregs.
 To further investigate the mechanisms by which STK treatment reduces Treg immunosuppression, we conducted a detailed dimensionality reduction clustering of Treg subsets in both the STK-treated and control groups. Pseudotime Analysis of Treg subpopulations identified three distinct subsets: naive Tregs (nTreg), resting Tregs (rTreg), and effector Tregs (eTreg). In the STK treatment group, the proportion of eTregs significantly decreased, while the expression levels of the immunosuppressive checkpoint Ctla-4 and the cytokine Il-10 in Tregs were also significantly downregulated (Fig.  ). These findings suggest that STK treatment weakens the immunosuppressive functions of Tregs, indicating a transition from “cold tumors” to “hot tumors” and a restructuring of the immune landscape towards an activated TME.

## STK treatment reshapes fragile Tregs through the GPR132-JAK1-STAT1-IRF1 signaling axis
Analysis of differentially expressed genes in Tregs revealed a significant upregulation of Gpr132 expression in Tregs treated with STK in liver cancer (Fig.  ). NicheNet analysis of ligand-receptor activity in Treg-tumor cell interaction revealed that GPR132 might be a potential receptor on Tregs in lung cancer (Fig.  ). Consistently, we observed an increase in GPR132-positive Tregs in tumors from Soat1 knockout KP mice, alongside a marked reduction in effector Treg (eTreg) cells (Fig.  ). GPR132, a G protein-coupled receptor predominantly expressed in immune cells, serves as a receptor for oxidized lipids such as fatty acid 9(S)-HODE . Further analysis utilizing the GEPIA2 database showed a positive correlation between the GPR132-high Treg subset and improved overall survival and progression-free survival rates in both liver and lung cancer patients (Fig.  ). These findings suggest that STK treatment may alleviate immunosuppression by reshaping the GPR132+ Treg subset, thus enhancing the anti-tumor immune response.
 To evaluate the role of Tregs in the STK-mediated anti-tumor activity, we administered an anti-CD25 blocking antibody to deplete Tregs. The results indicated that the anti-tumor effect of STK treatment was significantly diminished in the absence of Tregs (Fig.  ), underscoring that STK’s anti-tumor efficacy primarily depends on Tregs. To further ascertain the importance of GPR132-positive Tregs in the anti-tumor function contingent upon SOAT1 inhibition, we generated Foxp3-Cre; Gpr132f/f mice, and found Soat1 knockdown inhibited tumor growth in Foxp3-Cre mice but not in Foxp3-Cre; Gpr132f/f mice (Fig.  ).
To probe the regulatory mechanisms of STK treatment on GPR132+ Tregs, we established a co-culture system with STK-treated CT26 tumor cells and Gpr132-knockdown Treg, co-culturing them with bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDC) or OT-1 T cells (Supplementary Fig.  ). The co-culture of Treg cells with either STK or Avasimibe-treated CT26 cells revealed a decrease in eTreg cell populations, with STK performing more pronounced efficacy (Supplementary Fig.  ). Following the co-culture of STK-treated CT26 tumor cells with BMDC, we observed a significant increase in CD80 maturation factors in cDC1, while CD86 and MHC II levels remained relatively unchanged. Only in the presence of Tregs did we notice substantial increases in maturation factors of CD80, CD86, and MHC II in BMDC, suggesting that STK treatment enhances the maturation factors of BMDC by alleviating Treg immunosuppression. (Supplementary Fig.  ). Furthermore, the IFN-γ levels in OT-1 CD8+ T cells increased significantly, while other proliferation and cytotoxic markers, such as Gzmb and IL-2, did not show significant changes. Only in the presence of Tregs did we notice substantial increases in the levels of IFN-γ, Gzmb, and IL-2 in OT-1 CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig.  ), suggesting that STK treatment enhances the cytotoxic functionality of CD8+ T cells by alleviating Treg immunosuppression. Notably, Gpr132 knockdown in Tregs reversed the increased levels of cDC1 maturation factors (CD80, CD86, and MHC II) (Supplementary Fig.  ), while the levels of IFN-γ, Gzmb, and IL-2 in OT-1 CD8+ T cells were significantly rescued (Supplementary Fig.  ). Importantly, treating naive CD4+ T cells with STK did not impact the GPR132 expression or their differentiation into Tregs, nor did treating Tregs with STK affect their GPR132 levels (Supplementary Fig.  ). These outcomes indicate that STK treatment increases cDC1 maturation and the cytotoxic potential of CD8+ T cells through remodeling of GPR132+ Tregs.
To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying STK-induced GPR132+ Treg fragility, we performed pathway enrichment analysis on single-cell samples, revealing significant enrichment of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway, previously associated with GPR132 regulation, among Tregs in the STK treatment group compared to controls (Fig.  ). The levels of immunosuppressive factors TGF-β and IL-10 were markedly decreased in Tregs cultured in conditioned medium from STK-treated CT26 cells or the Soat1 knockdown group, relative to controls (Fig.  ). However, the reductions in TGF-β and IL-10 within Tregs associated with STK treatment or Soat1 knockdown were significantly inhibited upon treatment with the GPR132 inhibitors Telmisartan or the STAT1 inhibitors Fludarabine (Fig.  ).
 To further investigate the regulation of the GPR132-JAK1-STAT1-IRF1 signaling axis by STK treatment, we collected conditioned medium (CM) from STK-treated CT26 cells and Soat1 knockdown CT26 cells, Tregs co-incubated with the CM were subsequently performed western blot analysis and FACS analysis. The results indicated that levels of GPR132, JAK1, STAT1, and IRF1 in Tregs were significantly increased with either STK treatment or Soat1 knockdown, while FOXP3 and LAG3 in Tregs were decreased considerably (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Treatment with telmisartan (GPR132 inhibitor) negated the upregulation of JAK1, STAT1, and IRF1 expression. Conversely, treatment with fludarabine (STAT1 inhibitor) increased GPR132 and JAK1 levels but abrogated the upregulations of STAT1 and IRF1 expression, alongside downregulations of FOXP3 and LAG3 expression (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Besides, GPR132 agonist ONC212 significantly activated the GPR132-JAK1-STAT1-IRF1 signaling axis (Supplementary Fig.  ). Additionally, Soat1 knockdown tumors activated this signaling axis and reduced eTregs in Foxp3-Cre mice, while Gpr132 knockout in Tregs inhibited the signaling activation and the Tregs remodeling (Supplementary Fig.  ). Altogether, these results support the conclusion that STK treatment reshapes GPR132+ Tregs, leading to fragile Tregs through the GPR132-JAK1-STAT1-IRF1 signaling pathway (Fig.  ).

## STK treatment induces tumor cells to release 20SOHC, facilitating the activation of GPR132 on Tregs
To investigate how SOAT1-mediated cholesterol metabolism impacts Treg cell immune recognition, we cultured CT26 cells with NBD-labeled cholesterol to track cholesterol metabolites. After washing out excess NBD-labeled cholesterol with PBS, we treated the CT26 cells with the SOAT1 inhibitor STK and collected the conditioned medium for co-culture with Tregs. Flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant increase in NBD signaling in the STK-treated group compared to controls (Fig.  ), indicating that STK treatment promotes the release of specific cholesterol metabolites from tumor cells, which are subsequently taken up by Tregs. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of the proteomic data from the STK treatment group further confirmed that oxidized cholesterol metabolism was significantly enriched (Fig.  ), suggesting that oxidized cholesterol released from STK-treated tumor cells may bind to oxidized lipid receptor GPR132 and influence Treg function. We then performed bulk RNA sequencing on Hepa1-6 and CT26 cells with Soat1 knockdown or STK treatment. The sequencing results indicated that the mRNA expression levels of various cholesterol oxidase enzymes—including Cyp11a1, Cyp27a1, Cyp7b1, and Cyp46a1—were significantly increased (Fig.  ). Targeted mass spectrometry analysis identified 20SOHC as the most significantly altered cholesterol oxidation product (Fig.  ). Furthermore, DARTS analysis confirmed that 20SOHC binds to GPR132 alongside the known ligand 9(S)-HODE (Fig.  ). Ligand-receptor docking analyses indicated strong binding affinities, with 20SOHC binding energy of −5.655 kcal/mol for human GPR132 and −33.51 kcal/mol for murine GPR132 (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Meanwhile, 9S-HODE showed a binding energy of −4.165 kcal/mol with human GPR132 (Fig.  ). Notably, 20SOHC treatment significantly enhanced GPR132 expression and activated the downstream JAK1-STAT1 signaling pathway in Tregs (Supplementary Fig.  ), indicating that 20SOHC acts as a tumor-derived ligand that activates the GPR132-JAK1-STAT1-IRF1 axis, thereby promoting anti-tumor immune responses.
 20SOHC is known to suppress SREBP1/2 and thereby inhibit cholesterol synthesis. To assess the modulating of SREBP signaling by STK/SOAT1, we quantified SREBP target gene expression after SOAT1 inhibition. The results showed SREBP1/2 signaling in CT26 cells is inhibited after treatment with STK (Supplementary Fig.  ). To further reveal whether SREBP suppression makes a meaningful contribution to the observed phenotype, we treated CT26 xenograft tumor-bearing mice with fatostatin treatment (SREBP signaling inhibitor) in combination with STK treatment. The result showed that both STK treatment and fatostatin treatment could inhibit tumor growth. The enhanced anti-tumor effect was detected in the combined group (Supplementary Fig.  ). These data suggested that SOAT1 inhibition with STK treatment might suppress the SREBP signaling in tumor cells. However, the anti-tumor effect of STK is not fully dependent on SREBP signaling suppression in tumor cells, but it might also function through the secreted 20SOHC/GPR132 signaling in Treg cells. Moreover, we also checked the expression of downstream targeted genes of SREBP signaling in Treg cells, either cocultured with conditioned medium from STK-treated tumor cells or treated with 20SOHC, the results showed SREBP signaling in Treg cells was not absolutely inhibited by cocultured with conditioned medium from STK-treated tumor cells or treated with 20SOHC (Supplementary Fig.  , ). In vivo, fatostatin pretreated-Tregs were mixed with CT26 tumor cells and inoculated into WT mice. Then, we treated mixture xenograft tumor-bearing mice in combination with STK773477 treatment. We found that both STK and fatostatin treatments could inhibit tumor growth. The enhanced anti-tumor effect was detected in the combined group (Supplementary Fig.  ). These data suggested that the function of 20SOHC might not be through the 20SOHC/SREBP signaling pathway in Treg cells, and anti-tumor effect of STK might not be dependent on SREBP signaling suppression in Treg cells. These data suggested that the anti-tumor effect of STK is not fully dependent on SREBP suppression induced by STK/20SOHC. Furthermore, we derived a 20SOHC-response gene set in Tregs and found GPR132/JAK/STAT1 genes were correlated with a favorable prognosis (Supplementary Fig.  ). Further analysis with human tumor datasets (LIHC, LUAD, COAD, BRCA, SKCM) showed that the higher GPR132/JAK/STAT1/IRF1 signatures are correlated with longer overall and disease-free survival (Supplementary Fig.  ).
To explore whether 20SOHC alone is necessary to recapitulate STK-induced immune stimulation, we established an in vitro co-culture system with 20S-treated Treg cells, co-culturing them with bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDC) or CD8+ T cells. Following the co-culture of 20SOHC-treated Treg cells with BMDC, we observed a significant increase in maturation factors (such as CD80, CD86 and MHCII) in cDC1 compared to the control group (Supplementary Fig.  ). Through targeted Western blots, we found NF-κB and MAPK pathways were activated, along with the transcription factor IRF8, which could drive DC maturation (Supplementary Fig.  ). When co-culturing of 20SOHC-treated Treg cells with CD8+ T cells, effector factors (such as IFN-γ, Gzmb and IL-2) were distinctly increased (Supplementary Fig.  ). However, treatment BMDC or CD8+ T cells with 20SOHC did not change the activated factors (Supplementary Fig.  ), suggesting 20SOHC is not directly sufficient for dendritic cell (DC) and CD8⁺ T-cell activation. Besides, treatment CAFs with 20SOHC did not change the activated factors, such as α-SMA and IL-6 (Supplementary Fig.  ). In vivo, when mixed 20SOHC pretreated Tregs with CT26 tumor cells and inoculated them into WT mice, we found that: compared with the blank group, adding Treg cells to the tumors significantly accelerated tumor growth; while compared with the DMSO group, the tumor growth in the group pretreated with Tregs with 20SOHC was significantly inhibited (Supplementary Fig.  ). When neutralizing 20SOHC via Cyp11a1 enzymatic degradation (ShCyp11a1), the anti-tumor effect of STK was abolished (Supplementary Fig.  ). These results indicated that exogenous 20SOHC alone could recapitulate STK-induced immune stimulation in vitro and in vivo, and neutralizing 20SOHC abolished the effect of STK treatment.
To map the sequence of events, we performed a time-course analysis for 20SOHC content in the tumor microenvironment. During STK treatment, the tumors were dissected, and the levels of 20SOHC in the tumor tissue fluid were analyzed by LC-MS on the 0th day, 6th day and 12th day, respectively. The results showed that the 20SOHC levels were increased in a time-course dependent pattern and reached peak dose after STK treatment for 12 days. The changes in Treg phenotype (pSTAT1, CLTA-4 and LAG3), DC maturation (CD80, CD86 and MHCII), and CD8⁺ T-cell function (IFN-γ, Gzmb and IL-2) appeared after 12 days of STK treatment. These results help a lot in clarifying causality and therapeutic scheduling guidance (Supplementary Fig.  ).

## STK enhances tumor rejection when combined with anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4 ICB therapy
To evaluate the combined effects of STK with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy, we conducted studies combining STK with anti-PD-1 therapy or CTLA-4 therapy in CT26 and 4T1 xenograft models. Results indicated that the STK and anti-PD-1 combination, or anti-CTLA-4, significantly enhanced therapeutic outcomes compared to control groups in both models (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ), suggesting strong potential for clinical applications of this combination in cancer treatment. Additionally, to validate the ability of STK to rejuvenate the antitumor CD8+ T cells via impairing Treg and synergize with anti-PD-1, antitumor T cell and Treg phenotype and functions have been assessed. As shown in the Supplementary Fig.  , the expressions of immunosuppressive factor (CTLA-4, LAG3) in the Tregs from the syngeneic treatment group were decreased, and the pSTAT1 levels in the Tregs were elevated while immune-activated factor of both the CD8+ T cell (GZMB, IFN-γ and IL-2) and the DC cells (CD80, CD86 and MHCII) were enhanced (Supplementary Fig.  ). Besides, to evaluate the safety and toxicity of STK, we tested body weight changes and histopathology of major organs and liver enzymes in the context of the combination of STK and anti-PD-1 therapy or as a single agent. The results showed MTD of STK is ＞1620 mg/kg (Supplementary Fig.  ), and STK treatment did not change body weight (Supplementary Figs.  , ) or impair major organs (Supplementary Fig.  ) or elevate liver enzymes (Such as ALT, AST and ALP) (Supplementary Fig.  ). Besides, STK treatment performed lower IC50 cytotoxicity than Avasimibe in HEK-293T cells (Supplementary Fig.  ). Additionally, we evaluated the pharmacokinetic properties of STK773477 in comparison to Avasimibe: STK performed a faster time to peak Tmax and a longer half-life T1/2, suggesting STK’s superiority over Avasimibe (Supplementary Fig.  ).
 In a pilot study for translational application, we treated patient-derived xenograft (PDX) tumor-bearing immune humanized NSG mice with STK (Fig.  ). The STK treatment effectively prevented PDX tumor growth (Fig.  ). Significantly, STK treatment was associated with a remarkable reduction in eTreg populations, an increase in GPR132-positive Tregs, and boosted activation of CD8+ T cells (Fig.  ).
Further analysis of 5 paired patient samples from non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) revealed that the GPR132 expression in Tregs was significantly lower in tumor samples and inversely correlated with SOAT1 expression (Fig.  ). Since CYP11A1 is identified as a key enzyme in synthesizing 20SOHC, we also analyzed the correlation between overall survival and Cyp11a1 expression. Our findings indicated that higher Cyp11a1 expression correlates with improved overall survival, highlighting its potential as a prognostic indicator (Fig.  ).
In summary, these findings illustrate how STK treatment induces a release of 20SOHC from tumor cells, which subsequently activates GPR132 on Tregs, contributing to enhanced anti-tumor immunity and suggesting a synergistic potential with existing immunotherapies such as PD-1 or CTLA-4 blockade.

## Discussion
Cholesterol plays a crucial role as a major component of cell membranes, and its metabolic reprogramming is essential for rapidly proliferating cancer cells to acquire the necessary building blocks for tumorigenesis. Targeting cholesterol metabolism has emerged as a popular strategy in cancer treatment, but so far, no specific targets or drugs have been developed for clinical use. This lack of effective options may be due, in part, to the diverse functions of cholesterol across different cell types. Recent studies have highlighted the heterogeneity of cholesterol metabolism within various cell subsets in the tumor microenvironment (TME), revealing notable differences in cholesterol profiles between immune cells and tumor cells – . For instance, dendritic cells (DC) and cytotoxic CD8+ T cells tend to be cholesterol-deficient, while tumor-associated macrophages (TAM) and tumor cells have higher cholesterol levels . 27-oxysterols secreted by cancer cells are known to inhibit SREBP2 (Sterol Regulatory Element-Binding Protein 2) activity and activate LXR (Liver X Receptor), leading to cholesterol deficiency in T cells. This deficiency can impede T cell proliferation and even induce autophagy-mediated apoptosis. Conversely, Tregs modulate SREBP1/2 activity within the tumor milieu to bolster their immunosuppressive functions . These dichotomous roles of cholesterol underline the complexity of targeted interventions, as exemplified by the clinical use of statins that have had mixed outcomes in cancer therapy.
An alternative and potentially more effective strategy is to target specific enzymes that regulate cholesterol homeostasis in selected cell types. In this study, we identify SOAT1 as a critical factor regulating tumor immune evasion and forming an immunosuppressive TME. Notably, SOAT1 was characterized as a cancer stem cell-specific metabolism immune checkpoint. SOAT1 inhibition impairs the quality maintenance of CSC, providing a chance to treat relapsed or resistant refractory tumors. The findings underscore the potential of SOAT1 as a therapeutic target in the pursuit of improved cancer immunotherapies, which could ultimately lead to better patient outcomes.
Regulatory T cells, known for their expression of the forkhead box transcription factor FOXP3, constitute a subset of CD4+ T cells with immunosuppressive activity in tumors . Tregs inhibit the maturation and activation of dendritic cells by downregulating essential signals critical for T cell activation. They wield additional immunosuppressive effects on conventional T cells and other immune cells via surface molecules like CD25, CD39, and CD73, and through the secretion of cytokines such as IL-10, IL-35, and TGF-β. Although Treg frequencies range from 5% to 10% in blood and lymphoid tissues under normal conditions, they can exceed 50% within tumors. Elevated levels of Tregs in the tumor microenvironment pose significant barriers to effective immunotherapy and are often linked to poor patient outcomes across various cancer types. Our study provides substantial evidence that SOAT1 acts as a cancer stem cell-specific metabolism checkpoint that facilitates immune evasion by tumors. Inhibition of SOAT1 led to increased 20S-Hydroxycholesterol (20SOHC) release from tumor cells, activating a trans-cellular signaling pathway through the GPR132 receptor on Tregs. This activation induced a downregulation of Tregs while enhancing the antigen presentation function of dendritic cells and activating the cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells. By targeting SOAT1 with the potent inhibitor STK treatment, a robust anti-tumor immune response was activated through a transcellular 20SOHC (CSC)-GPR132-JAK1-STAT1-IRF1 (Treg) pathway to downregulate Tregs. This strategy represents a promising avenue for leveraging cholesterol metabolism modulation for cancer immunotherapy, offering an approach to enhancing the efficacy of existing treatment modalities.
Though 20SOHC does not directly acting on dendritic cell (DC, myeloid cells), CD8⁺ T-cell and CAF (stromal cells) activation in vitro (Supplementary Fig.  ). However, we still could not rule out the possibility that STK might function through alternative routes other than the “tumor → 20SOHC → Treg(GPR132) → DC/CD8 activation” pathway. 20SOHC might act on myeloid or stromal cells directly, and STK may alter tumor antigenicity or cell death pathways, indirectly affecting immune infiltration.
Through in vivo chemical drug screen and target verification, we identified a highly robust SOAT1 inhibitor (STK). STK treatment reduces the cancer stem cells, which contribute to the recurrence of drug resistance in immunotherapy, and exhibits robust anti-tumor activities in liver, lung, colon, breast, and melanoma mouse tumor models, while enhancing tumor rejection when combined with anti-PD-1 or CTLA-4 therapy, suggesting the clinical potential for enhanced immunotherapy when combined with ICB. The high-affinity SOAT1 inhibitor STK could activate a strong antitumor immunity in a wide range of immunologically cold tumors and has synergistic effects with anti-PD1 or CTLA-4. The SOAT1 inhibitor STK may provide a cancer immunotherapy strategy as well as a synergy with existing ICBs, which will significantly enlarge the proportion of patients with cancer eligible for immunotherapy.

## Methods

## Mice
KP lung carcinoma mice were generated by breeding LSL-K-ras G12D mice (JAX: 008179) and B6.129P2-Trp53tm1Brn/J (JAX: 008462). Myc-overexpressing liver cancer mice were generated by crossing Cebpb-tTA mice (IMR Colony, The Jackson Laboratory) and tet-o-MYC mice (a gift from J. Michael Bishop, University of California, San Francisco), as previously described . Gpr132-flox mice (Stock#: S-CKO-12192) and Foxp3-YFP-cre mice (Stock#: C001074) were purchased from Cyagen (Guangzhou, China). Lgr5-EGFP-IRES-creERT2 mice were provided by Hans Clevers (Hubrecht Institute), and Apcf/f mice were from Tyler Jacks (Massachusetts Institute of Technology); both strains have been previously described . OT1 transgenic mice were generously provided by Prof. Yu Xiaofei at Fudan University (available upon request). C57BL/6JGpt (Strain NO. N000013), BALB/cJGpt (Strain NO. N000020) and NSG mice (Strain NO. T001475) were purchased from Gempharmatech Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China).

## Animal mouse model
In the Lgr5-CreER-GFP/Apcf/f mouse model, continuous intraperitoneal administration of 10 mg/kg tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# T5648) for three days induced specific APC knockout in Lgr5-expressing intestinal stem cells, resulting in the formation of in situ intestinal tumors. In the Cebpb-tTA/TetO-c-Myc model, doxycycline-mediated activation of the tTA promoter drove MYC expression, establishing in situ liver tumors. In the Adeno-Cre/f-stop-f-RasV12/p53f/f lung cancer model, nasal administration of adenoviral Cre triggered Kras and P53 knockout, leading to in situ lung tumors. Two weeks post-induction, mice were randomly assigned to receive oral treatment with STK773477 (30 mg/kg) or other compounds. After treatment, the intestines, livers, and lungs were dissected for analysis of tumor cell counts. Additionally, the survival rates of the treated mice were evaluated.
For the xenograft mouse model, 5 × 105 cells of CT26, B16-F10, LLC, and 4T1 cells were injected subcutaneously (s.c.) into the right flank of six-week-old male mice, thereby establishing CT26 colorectal cancer, B16-F10 melanoma, 4T1 breast cancer, and LLC lung adenocarcinoma tumor models. Once the tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, STK773477 (30 mg/kg) or other selected compounds were given to mice by gavage daily for approximately two weeks.
For Patient-Derived Xenograft (PDX) models, primary colon adenocarcinoma tumor specimens were obtained from CHANGHAI Hospital following surgical excision, with informed legal guardians from all patients. Under sterile conditions, the tumors were dissected into 2–4 mm³ fragments using forceps and subsequently transplanted subcutaneously into 11-week-old humanized mice. The implanted tumor models were regularly monitored for growth. Upon tumors reaching approximately 100 mm³, the mice were randomly assigned to treatment groups, receiving STK773477 (30 mg/kg) or DMSO daily. Tumor volume was monitored three times weekly.
All animals were maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions within a barrier facility at the Laboratory Animal Center of the Institute of Developmental Biology and Molecular Medicine, Fudan University. The housing environment was maintained on a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, with ambient temperature at 22 ± 2 °C and relative humidity at 50 ± 10%. Following genotyping at approximately 3 weeks of age, experimental and control mice were housed separately. All studies utilized male mice. Unless otherwise specified, experiments were initiated when mice were 5 weeks of age. Euthanasia was performed by CO2 asphyxiation. For all tumor studies, experiments were conducted in strict adherence to institutional ethical guidelines, which stipulated a maximum allowable tumor volume of 2000 mm³. Tumor size was measured every 3 days using a digital caliper; the longest diameter (length, a) and the perpendicular diameter (width, b) were recorded, and the tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = (a × b²)/2. Animals were monitored daily for overall health and tumor condition. Humane endpoints requiring early euthanasia included tumor volume exceeding the limit, weight loss >20%, or the appearance of significant distress signs such as hunched posture, lethargy, or ulceration. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Fudan University (ACUC, Fudan University; Animal Protocol Number, IDM2021051a).

## Human samples
Clinical specimens were prospectively obtained from 9 treatment-naive NSCLC patients (5 females, ages 45, 54, 55, 59, 71; 4 males, ages 51, 59, 59, 60) at Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital and its affiliated institutions. Participant sex was self-reported. All participants provided written informed consent prior to tissue acquisition; no compensation was provided. Tumor tissues were collected via intraoperative cryobiopsy, snap-frozen, and stored at −80 °C for analysis. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the exploratory nature and limited sample size, a formal sex-based analysis was not conducted. Disaggregated participant data are available in the Source Data.

## Cell Lines and cell culture
Mouse B16-F10, LLC, Hepa1-6 and human Huh7 cancer cell lines, human embryonic kidney 293 T (HEK 293 T) cells and human Plat-E cells were obtained from the American Tissue Culture Collection (ATCC) and maintained in complete Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 units mL−1 penicillin/streptomycin. Mouse CT26 and 4T1 cancer cell lines were purchased from the ATCC, cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 100 units mL−1 penicillin/streptomycin. All cell lines obtained from ATCC were authenticated by the provider using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination. All cells were cultivated in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The key chemical reagents used are listed in Supplementary Table  .
In vivo Screening identifies therapeutic cancer chemicals through Drosophila stem cell tumor models.
We performed a tiered high-throughput in vivo screening of compound libraries using two Drosophila stem cell tumor model . Adult flies were subjected to clonal tumor induction via PMML (Post-Mitotic Mosaic Labeling) and cultured at 29 °C for 4 days to generate RasV12-mutant renal stem cell (RSC) tumors or NDN-mutant intestinal stem cell (ISC) tumors. Boiled fly food cooled to 37 °C was aliquoted (99 μL/well) into a 96-well plate, followed by the addition of 1 μL test compounds (final concentration: 10 μM) and thorough mixing. After food solidification, three tumor-bearing flies were added per well and maintained at 29 °C for 3 days. Tumors were dissected post-treatment for comparative analysis of growth phenotypes between drug-treated and control groups. The screening strategy comprised three phases: (1) Primary screening using the RasV12-RSC tumor model, selecting compounds showing ≥50% reduction in GFP+ tumor area; (2) Secondary screening with three biological replicates of top candidates in the RasV12-RSC model, retaining compounds with consistent ≥50% tumor suppression; (3) Tertiary validation in the NDN-ISC tumor model, where compounds maintaining ≥50% tumor reduction were identified as lead candidates. This multi-phase approach ensured rigorous identification of compounds with cross-tumor efficacy in distinct Drosophila stem cell malignancies.

## Western blotting
Total protein was extracted from cell lysates using RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, supplemented with a protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail). Protein concentrations were determined via the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal amounts of protein were subjected to SDS-PAGE on an acrylamide gel and subsequently transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature to minimize non-specific binding. Following this, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight with shaking. After thorough washing with TBST, the membrane was exposed for 1 h at room temperature to HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. The membrane was then developed with Super ECL Detection Reagent (Yeasen, China) and imaged with an imaging system (Tanon 5200). The primary antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table  .

## Immunofluorescence
For tissues, freshly harvested tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight after the mice were euthanized via CO₂ inhalation. The tissues were dehydrated by 30% sucrose and then embedded in OCT (Tissue-Tek; Sakura Finetek USA, Cat# 4583). Then, the embedded tissues were stored at -80 °C and allowed to reach room temperature before being sectioned. 7 μm frozen sections were generated using a Leica CM1950. The sections on adhesive slides were washed in PBS (3 × 5 min) to remove OCT, permeabilized in 0.25% Triton X-100 (30 min), and blocked with 3% BSA (1 h) at room temperature. Then, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The following day, the sections were washed in PBS three times, each for 5 min. The frozen sections that had been incubated with primary antibodies were then stained with fluorescent secondary antibodies at room temperature and protected from light for 1 h. Sections were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg mL−1, 5 min) and mounted with Antifade Mounting Medium. Images were acquired with an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope. Image analysis was performed using Fiji/ImageJ (v.1.54f). The antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table .

## Single-cell RNA sequencing
Tumor tissues for cell sorting were processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions for a mouse tumor dissociation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat# 130-096-730) to isolate cells. Target cells (Zombie−CD45− and Zombie−CD45+ at the ratio of 1:5) were purified using a BD FACS (Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting) flow cytometer in 1X PBS with 0.04% BSA. Sorted cells were stained with Trypan Blue to evaluate viability and quality. Single-cell cDNA synthesis was followed by library amplification according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The library was qualified and sequenced on an Illumina Nova6000. Raw sequencing data (FASTQ files) were processed with Cell Ranger software v6.0.2 to perform essential pre-processing and quality control, including the removal of low-quality reads, elimination of primer contamination, and comprehensive quality assessment. More than 93.2% of bases in the unique molecular identifier (UMI) have Q30 or above. Seurat was used to read data processed by Cell Ranger. To eliminate potential dead cells and low-quality data, only specific criteria cells were selected. Cells with less than 200 genes detected were removed from the analysis, as well as cells with more than 6000 molecule counts, and a mitochondrial gene expression ratio not exceeding 20%. The Seurat object was normalized using NormalizeData. An unsupervised approach was used for cell clustering. Following this, highly variable genes were identified by FindVariableFeatures. Then the data were scaled across all genes using Scaledata, followed by Principal Component Analysis (PCA) conducted with RunPCA. Data visualization was performed using the UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) for nonlinear dimensionality reduction, enabling a graphical representation of the high-dimensional single-cell dataset that facilitates the identification of distinct cellular populations based on gene expression profiles. Cell clustering analysis was conducted using FindClusters to facilitate the characterization of various cell types. Differentially expressed genes were identified using the FindMarkers, which included only those genes detected in a minimum of 10% of the compared populations. SOAT1 significance threshold was set, requiring an absolute log fold change exceeding 0.25 and an adjusted p-value below 0.05. To identify tumor cells within single-cell sequencing data, the R packages copyKAT were utilized to analyze chromosomal copy number variations in epithelial cells. Cells exhibiting chromosomal copy number alterations were discerned and classified as tumor cells. Cellular ligand-receptor interaction analyses were conducted utilizing the Cellchat and NicheNet to predict ligands responsible for driving transcriptional alterations in target cells. Pseudotime analysis was performed with Monocle2 to investigate the developmental timeline and trajectory of Treg cells. T cell clonal analysis was performed with scRepertoire . Pathway enrichment analysis was carried out with PROGENy to predict the activity of 14 signaling pathways based on gene expression data, including androgen, estrogen, EGFR, hypoxia, JAK-STAT, MAPK, NF-kB, PI3K, p53, TGF-β, TNF, TRAIL, VEGF, and WNT. Data were visualized using the R package ggplot2. The key chemical reagents used are listed in Supplementary Table  . The primary antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table  .

## Primary cell isolation and culture
Primary cultures of mouse T cells from the spleen were performed as previously described . All primary mouse cells were isolated from 8- to 10-week-old male BALB/cJGpt mice. Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDC) were prepared according to the published procedure . To isolate primary T cells from mouse spleen, a mouse at 8–10 weeks old was euthanized in accordance with institutional guidelines. The spleen was carefully excised and mechanically disrupted using a sterile scalpel, and the resulting tissue was passed through a 40 µm cell strainer (JET BIOFIL, Cat# CSS013040) to obtain a single-cell suspension. The cell suspension was then washed twice with PBS and followed by centrifugation at 500 × g for 3 min. Mouse naive CD4+ T cells (CD4+CD44lowCD62Lhigh) were purified from mouse splenocytes by immunomagnetic negative selection with the EasySep™ Mouse Naive CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit(Stem Cell, CAS# 19765). Naive CD4+ T cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium, 10% FBS, 100 units mL−1 penicillin/streptomycin, 4 mM L-glutamine (Gibco, Cat#25030081), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, Cat# 11360070), 50 μM 2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma–Aldrich, Cat#M3180), 25 mM HEPES (Gibco, Cat#15630130), and 1 × MEM nonessential amino acid solution (Gibco, Cat#11140050) and plated into a 6-well plate previously coated with anti-CD3 antibodies (BioXcell, Cat# BE0001-1) at a final concentration of 10 μg mL−1 for 48 h. The cells were then induced for approximately 6 days using a culture medium supplemented with TGF-β at 5 ng mL−1 (Yeasen, Cat#92347ES50), IL-10 at 10 ng mL−1 (Yeasen, Cat#90149ES60), and anti-CD28 at 2 ng mL−1 (BioXcell, Cat# BE0015-1) to drive their differentiation into FOXP3-positive regulatory T cells. For isolating primary cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) from surgically resected tumor tissues, fresh tumor specimens are collected in cold PBS and mechanically minced into 1–2 mm³ fragments using sterile scalpels. Tissue fragments are then digested in DMEM containing 2 mg/mL collagenase IV (Yeasen, Cat# 40510ES60), 1 mg/mL hyaluronidase (Yeasen, Cat#20426ES60), and 2% FBS for 2–3 h at 37 °C with gentle agitation. The resulting cell suspension is filtered through 100 μm and 40 μm cell strainers to remove undigested tissue. Cells are seeded and allowed to adhere for 48 h in fibroblast culture medium, which is DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Cat#15140122). CAFs are purified through differential trypsinization and serial passaging, as fibroblasts exhibit faster adherence and greater resistance to trypsin compared to tumor cells and other stromal components. The key chemical reagents used are listed in Supplementary Table  . The primary antibodies used are listed in Supplementary Table  .

## LC-MS analysis of oxysterol in Hepa1-6 or CT26 cell conditioned medium
The LC-MS sample preparation protocol, including sterol extraction, hydrolysis, derivatization, and sample cleaning, was described previously . Hepa1-6 and CT26 cell lines were treated with STK. After 48 h of treatment, the conditioned medium of cells was collected and centrifuged at 4 °C at 300 × g for 10 min to clear dead cells. For the analysis of oxysterols in the tumor microenvironment, tumor tissues were harvested at day 0, day 6, and day 12 post-inoculation. The specimens were gently homogenized in DMEM using tissue grinder tubes, followed by centrifugation to collect the supernatant for subsequent analysis. 80 μl of blank (methanol) or conditioned medium sample was added to 1440 μl methanol/isopropanol 1:1 (v/v), mixed thoroughly for 1 min and centrifuged for 10 min at 12500 × g. Supernatants were taken and concentrated to dryness using a vacuum freeze concentrator. Dry extracts and standards were reconstituted in 200 μL of Methanol, followed by 10 min centrifugation using 3220 × g at 4 °C. Supernatants were finally transferred to HPLC glass vials. Oxysterol detection of medium samples was acquired using a LC-MS/MS system (QTRAP6500 + ). The key chemical reagents used are listed in Supplementary Table  .

## Fluorescence-based SOAT2 enzymatic assay
SOAT2 activity was quantified by detecting the fluorescence of CoA released from the acyltransferase reaction, based on its sulfhydryl group reacting with the fluorophore CPM . The assay was performed in a 20 μL reaction system containing mixed micelles composed of 2 mM cholesterol (MCE, Cat# HY-N0322), 10 mM POPC (MCE, Cat# HY-130462), and 18.6 mM taurocholate (MCE, Cat# HY-N0545) in a buffer of 100 mM HEPES and 150 mM NaCl (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# S3014) at pH 7.5. Each reaction consisted of 15 μl of the micelle mixture, 2.5 μl of protein at a concentration of 7.2 μM, and 2.5 μl of oleoyl-CoA (MCE, Cat# HY-109591A) at a concentration of 400 μM. After initiating the reaction by adding oleoyl-CoA, the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 3 min or as indicated, and then terminated with 3 μl of 10% SDS (MCE, Cat# HY-K1030). Subsequently, 90 μl of 50 μM CPM (MCE, Cat# HY-D0069) was added, followed by incubation at room temperature for 30 min. Fluorescence was measured using a BioTek Synergy Neo2 reader (excitation 355 nm, emission 460 nm). Relative fluorescence intensity was obtained by subtracting the value from an oleoyl-CoA-free control. For IC₅₀ determination, STK773477 was pre-incubated with the micelles and protein at 37 °C for 3 min before starting the reaction. The key chemical reagents used are listed in Supplementary Table  .
