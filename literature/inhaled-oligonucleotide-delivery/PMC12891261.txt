Currently, no effective therapeutic interventions are available. The aim of this study was to elucidate the role and underlying mechanisms of Caveolin-2 in sepsis-induced acute lung injury. To this end, a Caveolin-2 knockdown model was utilized both in vivo and in vitro, facilitating a comprehensive evaluation of several parameters, including lung tissue injury, inflammatory responses, oxidative stress markers, changes in apoptotic protein expression, and indicators of macrophage polarization. Additionally, the study investigated alterations in proteins associated with the Hippo signaling pathway and assessed the effects of Hippo pathway inhibitors on apoptosis and polarization. Our results indicate that the knockdown of Caveolin-2 facilitates the polarization of macrophages towards the protective M2 macrophage polarization and mitigates macrophage apoptosis. This process is associated with a reduction in oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, culminating in the activation of the Hippo signaling pathway. Conversely, the overexpression of Caveolin-2 intensifies inflammation and oxidative damage, while promoting apoptosis and M1 macrophage polarization. Our findings provide novel insights into the role of Caveolin-2 and Hippo signal pathway in sepsis-induced acute lung injury and establish a foundation for future research and the development of potential therapeutic strategies.

## Introduction
Acute lung injury (ALI) is a critical condition characterized by intense pulmonary inflammation, disruption of the alveolar-capillary barrier, leading to significant hypoxemia and respiratory distress [ ]. The mortality rate of ALI, including its most severe form acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), was reported to be approximately 40% [ ]. ALI can arise from diverse etiologies, including direct injury and hyper-inflammation-mediated indirect damage. Sepsis stands out as the principal risk factor for ALI, as the lungs are highly vulnerable to the severe immune response elicited by sepsis during multiply organ dysfunction. The precise mechanism underlying sepsis-induced ALI is complex, involving dysregulated inflammatory responses, endothelial dysfunction, alveolar damage, and various types of cell death. Despite extensive research, the translational application of pharmacological agents and biomarkers into clinical practice remains exceptionally rare [ ]. There is a clear lack of effective therapeutic agents and precise biomarkers for early diagnosis, treatment guidance, or prognostic evaluation [ ]. Consequently, innovative and efficacious therapeutic approaches for sepsis-induced ALI need to be established.
Macrophages are crucial to the immune system and can influence the body’s immune state through different polarization types [ ]. During the initial phase of sepsis-induced ALI, M1 macrophage polarization predominates. This leads to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-Iβ (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6). Conversely, in the advanced stages of sepsis-induced ALI, there is a shift towards M2 macrophage polarization, leading to the release of anti-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-10 [ ]– [ ]. The conversion of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype significantly alleviated sepsis-induced ALI [ ]– [ ]. Apoptosis is an evolutionarily conserved, energy-dependent form of cell death that activates and regulates complex genetic programs. The apoptosis process not only compromises the architectural integrity of pulmonary tissue but also modulates the magnitude and duration of the inflammatory response, thereby influencing the pathophysiological progression of sepsis-induced ALI. Furthermore, Macrophage apoptosis is pivotal in LPS-induced immunosuppression, significantly weakening the host’s antimicrobial defenses and ability to clear microorganisms, thereby prolonging inflammatory responses [ ]. Thus, modulating macrophage polarization and inhibiting macrophage apoptosis are promising approaches to attenuate sepsis-induced ALI progression.
Caveolae are a specialized and morphologically unique type of lipid rafts, enriched in cholesterol and glycosphingolipids [ ]. Caveolae are plasma membrane invaginations measuring 50–100 nm in size, and caveolins are their essential components [ ]– [ ]. The caveolin protein family includes caveolin-1 (Cav1), caveolin-2 (Cav2), and caveolin-3 (Cav3), with Cav1 being the most thoroughly studied variant. Besides its structural role in the plasma membrane, Cav1 acts as a key modulator of many proteins essential for regulating signal transduction pathways in ALI [ ]– [ ]. Cav1 and Cav2 extensively interact and frequently co-localize within cellular structures, and these caveolin proteins are broadly identified in diverse immune cell populations [ ]– [ ]. However, the relationship between Cav2 and sepsis-induced ALI has been relatively underexplored in the existing body of research. The Hippo signaling pathway is crucial for cell proliferation, tissue regeneration, and tumor inhibition. Recent studies have shown its involvement in regulating inflammation and related diseases [ ]– [ ]. Currently, no direct molecular interaction between Cav2 and the Hippo signaling pathway has been documented, and the correlation between sepsis-induced ALI and the Hippo pathway remains unclear.
In this study, we focused on the investigating whether Cav2 played a role in therapy of sepsis-induced ALI by affecting macrophages polarization and apoptosis, which associated with Hippo signaling pathway. The results of this research could offer a new approach to prevent the progression of sepsis-induced ALI.

## Results

## Cav2 Knockdown Alleviate the Inflammatory Response of Sepsis-induced ALI in Mice Model
Cav2 knockdown relieved sepsis-induced ALI. As validated by Western blotting (WB) and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), Cav2 was highly expressed in lung tissues of sepsis-induced ALI mice model. The sepsis-induced ALI mouse model was established via cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) method, as it serves as a classic approach for sepsis induction [ ]. To assess the effect of Cav2 knockdown on mice with CLP procedure, we conducted a loss-of-function experiment utilizing Cav2-specific siRNA (si-Cav2) in conjunction with an adeno-associated virus-9 (AAV-9). Mice in the AAV-si-Cav2 group showed significant reductions in Cav2 expression at both mRNA and protein levels compared to controls (Fig. A-B). The survival rate of mice subjected to the CLP procedure was significantly reduced compared to the Control group mice. Conversely, the knockdown of Cav2 resulted in a marked improvement in survival rate compared to the CLP group mice (Fig. C). To assess the progression of pulmonary pathology in CLP group mice subsequent to Cav2 knockdown administration, the efficacy was evaluated by examining histopathological outcomes using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of lung tissues across each experimental group. H&E staining showed that the characteristic features of ALI in CLP group mice, such as alveolar destruction and inflammatory cell infiltration, were remarkably reduced by Cav2 knockdown treatment (Fig. D). H&E staining results from each group were quantified using a lung injury score (Fig. E). To assess the extent of pulmonary edema and respiratory function among the different groups, the W/D ratio, arterial oxygen partial pressure (PaO2), arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), oxygen partial pressure/fraction of inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2) and the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) total protein concentration in the lung tissues were analyzed. In comparison to the Control group, mice subjected to CLP elevated the W/D ratio, PaCO2, total protein concentration, and neutrophil count in BALF, and simultaneously decreased PaO2 and PaO2/FiO2. In contrast, the si-Cav2 intervention effectively ameliorated these alterations by significantly decreasing the W/D ratio, PaCO2, total protein levels, and neutrophil count, while markedly enhancing PaO2 and PaO2/FiO2 (Fig. F-K). The influence of Cav2 knockdown on inflammatory response and oxidative stress in mice was further examined. Cav2 knockdown mitigated the CLP-induced elevation of malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in lung tissues and enhanced the levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity (Fig. L-M). Moreover, we measured the inflammatory cytokine in BALF and lung tissues using the ELISA assay and RT-qPCR, as it has been demonstrated that the expression of pro-inflammatory factors plays a crucial role in the progression of sepsis-induced ALI. The results of the ELISA assay demonstrated that mice subjected to the CLP procedure and treated with Cav2 knockdown exhibited a significant reduction in the levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. These cytokine levels were elevated in the CLP group compared to the Control group.(Fig. N-P). The mRNA expression trend of IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α detected by RT-qPCR was consistent with ELISA results (Fig. Q-S). Taken together, these data indicate that Cav2 knockdown attenuated sepsis-induced ALI in vivo. 

## Cav2 Knockdown Inhibited Pulmonary Apoptosis and Promoted M2 Polarization in Sepsis-induced ALI Mice Model Induced By CLP
The apoptosis of pulmonary cells and polarization of macrophages in lung tissue play a crucial role in pulmonary inflammation and tissue damage during sepsis-induced ALI progression [ ]– [ ].However, it remains unclear whether Cav2 knockdown exerts its protective effect against sepsis-induced ALI by promoting M2 polarization and macrophage apoptosis. Therefore, the role of Cav2 in macrophage polarization and apoptosis was further investigated in vivo. The expression of proteins related to polarization and apoptosis in lung tissues was detected using WB and RT-qPCR. Bak, Bax, and Caspase-3 are pro-apoptotic proteins, whereas Bcl-2 is an anti-apoptotic protein. WB analysis revealed that mice undergoing the CLP procedure exhibited a significant upregulation in the protein levels of Bak, Bax, and Caspase-3, alongside a reduction in Bcl-2 levels, relative to the Control group. Furthermore, the knockdown of Cav2 effectively mitigated the impact of CLP on the expression of Bax, Bak, Caspase-3, and Bcl-2 proteins. Meanwhile, WB results indicated that the M1 polarization markers iNOS, CD80 and CD86 were up-regulated in lung tissues of CLP mice compared with Control group, while the M2 molecules CD206, CD163 and Arg1 were down-regulated. Nevertheless, the knockdown of Cav2 resulted in a reduction of M1 markers, specifically iNOS, CD80, and CD86, while simultaneously increasing M2 markers, including CD206, CD163, and Arg1, in mice subjected to CLP (Fig. A). Subsequently, RT-qPCR analysis was employed to assess apoptosis and polarization, yielding results that were consistent with those obtained from WB analysis (Fig. B-K). In summary, these findings indicate that Cav2 knockdown mitigates macrophage apoptosis and facilitates the polarization towards the M2 phenotype in mice with sepsis-induced ALI. 

## Cav2 Knockdown Alleviated the Inflammatory Response of LPS-induced MHS Cells Injury
To investigate the role of Cav2 in modulating the macrophage polarization and inflammatory response in cell model, LPS-stimulated Mouse Alveolar Macrophage (MHS) cells were utilized. For this purpose, a lentivirus-transduced MHS cell line with stable Cav2 knockdown (LV-si-Cav2) and overexpression of Cav2 (LV-OE-Cav2) was established. The reduction and augmentation of Cav2 at the protein and mRNA levels were confirmed through WB and RT-qPCR analyses, as shown in Fig.  A-B. The effects of varying concentrations of LPS (0.1,0.5, 1, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100 ug/mL) on MHS cells were assessed by measuring changes in optical density (OD) values at 450 nm using the CCK-8 assay. The results in Fig.  C showed notable variations in OD values to control at the 10ug/mL LPS concentration. Subsequently, MHS cells were exposed to LPS at a concentration of 10 µg/mL for a duration of 24 h to induce macrophage polarization and establish an inflammatory model. A comparative analysis of the expression levels of oxidative stress-related indicators, MDA, SOD, and ROS, was conducted across various treatment groups. The results revealed that Cav2 knockdown administration exhibited a significant reduction in the levels of MDA and ROS, alongside an increase in SOD activity, in comparison to the LPS groups, which were reversed in the Cav2 overexpression treatment (Fig.  D-F). Furthermore, the levels of inflammatory factors in cell supernatant were measured by ELISA Kit. The levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were markedly elevated in the LPS group compared to the Control group. Conversely, the Cav2 knockdown exhibited significantly reduced cytokine levels relative to the LPS group, a trend that was reversed upon Cav2 overexpression (Fig.  G-I). The mRNA expression levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were significantly upregulated in the LPS group relative to the Control group. In contrast, the Cav2 knockdown group exhibited a reduction of these cytokines relative to the LPS-stimulated group, an effect that was reversed upon Cav2 overexpression (Fig.  J-L). Collectively, these findings imply that Cav2 knockdown alleviated inflammatory response and oxidative stress of MHS cells induced by LPS. 

## Cav2 Knockdown Mitigated Macrophage Apoptosis and Promoted M2 Polarization in MHS Cells Induced by LPS
Subsequently, the impact of Cav2 on macrophage apoptosis and polarization was investigated in MHS cells subjected to LPS treatment. As shown in Fig.  A-E, the results of WB and RT-qPCR showed that LPS significantly increase the protein levels of Bax, Bak, Caspase-3, and decreased the protein levels of Bcl-2 compared with the Control group in MHS cells. Remarkably, Cav2 knockdown inhibited the expression of apoptosis-associated proteins induced by LPS, whereas Cav2 overexpression aggravated the expression of these proteins. Additionally, the role of Cav2 in modulating macrophage polarization in MHS cells was examined utilizing WB, RT-qPCR and flow cytometry techniques. The mRNA expression levels of M1 and M2 marker genes in macrophages were quantified, revealing that si-Cav2 attenuated LPS-induced M1 polarization while enhancing M2 polarization. On the contrary, OE-Cav2 inhibited this polarization trend by enhancing M1 activation and suppressing M2 differentiation (Fig.  A and F-K). Furthermore, the macrophage polarization profile in each group was analyzed via flow cytometry, revealing concordance with the WB and RT-qPCR findings (Fig.  L-M). Based on the aforementioned findings, it can be concluded that Cav2 knockdown effectively facilitates M2 macrophage polarization while simultaneously inhibiting LPS-induced apoptotic processes in MHS cells. 

## Cav2 Knockdown Promoted M2 Polarization and Attenuated Macrophage Apoptosis by Regulating Hippo Signaling Pathway in Vitro and in Vivo
Immunofluorescence staining assessed nuclear expression levels of YAP and TAZ in different groups. Compared with the Control group, the LPS group showed increased nuclear fluorescence of YAP and TAZ. The administration of si-Cav2 led to a decrease in nuclear YAP and TAZ fluorescence, while OE-Cav2 resulted in an increase in nuclear YAP and TAZ expression. Conversely, VT02956 treatment reversed the fluorescence decrease caused by si-Cav2. Similarly, MY-875 reduced the fluorescence increase induced by OE-Cav2 (Fig.  A). Compared with Control group cells, the expression of Hippo signaling pathway marker proteins, including Sav1, Mst1, Lats1, Cytoplasm phosphorylated YAP (p-YAP), and Cytoplasm phosphorylated TAZ (p-TAZ), was decreased in LPS group cells. Meanwhile, the levels of Nucleus YAP and Nucleus TAZ were increased. The results were contrary to those observed in the LPS + LV-si-Cav2 group but identical to those in LPS + LV-OE-Cav2 group MHS cells (Fig.  B). To further elucidate the molecular mechanism by which si-Cav2 promotes M2 polarization and attenuates macrophage apoptosis, we treated cells from the LPS + LV-si-Cav2 group with VT02956, an inhibitor of the Hippo signaling pathway. Additionally, cells from the LPS + LV-OE-Cav2 group were treated with MY-875, an activator of the Hippo signaling pathway. The WB results showed that VT02956 treatment reduced the suppressive effect of Cav2 knockdown. This effect was seen in the expression of proteins associated with apoptosis and polarization. Additionally, MY-875 treatment reversed the worsening effect of Cav2 overexpression on the expression of proteins associated with apoptosis and polarization. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that VT02956 treatment counteracted the effects of Cav2 knockdown by reducing M2 macrophage polarization and increasing M1 polarization. In contrast, MY-875 administration reversed the effects of Cav2 overexpression by suppressing M1 polarization and promoting M2 polarization(Fig.  C-D). Furthermore, RT-qPCR was used to detect apoptosis and polarization related proteins associated with changes the Hippo pathway changes. The results, consistent with WB, showed that inhibiting the Hippo pathway promoted apoptosis and M1 polarization. In contrast, activating the pathway suppressed apoptosis and promoted M2 polarization (Fig.  E-N). Additionally, the effects of si-Cav2 in alleviating inflammation and OE-Cav2 in aggravating inflammation can also be modulated by a Hippo pathway inhibitor and an activator, respectively (Fig.  O-Q). 
To further confirm the involvement of the Hippo pathway in regulating Cav2, an in vivo rescue experiment targeting inflammation was conducted. Upon suppression of the Hippo signaling pathway by VT02956, the anti-apoptotic, M1-suppressing, and M2-promoting effects of Cav2 knockdown were attenuated (Fig.  A). IHC analysis further confirmed that Cav2 knockdown mitigated pulmonary apoptosis, whereas inhibition of the Hippo pathway counteracted this anti-apoptotic effect (Fig.  B-D). Furthermore, Changes in pulmonary injury and inflammation upon Hippo signaling inhibition were assessed via HE staining and RT-qPCR. Results showed that VT02956 application suppressed the Hippo pathway, counteracting the ameliorative impact of Cav2 knockout on pulmonary damage and its anti-inflammatory effects (Fig.  E-I). These results indicated that Cav2 knockdown inhibited macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory phenotype, promoted M2 polarization, and reduced macrophage apoptosis by modulating the Hippo signaling pathway (Fig. ). 

## Discussion
Despite a reduction in mortality rates, sepsis-induced ALI continues to be a significant contributor to both morbidity and mortality among critically ill patients [ ]. Sepsis-induced ALI is a complex pathological process involving excessive inflammation and oxidative stress, which cause severe lung damage and impaired gas exchange. The pathophysiology of this condition involves the infiltration of inflammatory mediators and the overproduction of ROS, which perpetuate tissue injury and exacerbate the inflammatory response [ ]. The immune and inflammatory response plays a crucial role in the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying ALI, with alveolar macrophages (AMs) being particularly vulnerable. Research indicates that AMs work in conjunction with various immune cells to modulate lung inflammation, and that severe pneumonia and the apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells are significant contributors to the development of acute lung injury [ ]– [ ]. Understanding the molecular mechanisms driving this process is essential for developing effective therapeutic approaches.
Caveolin protein, in particular the most studied Cav1, has been previously demonstrated to play a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of sepsis through its regulation of membrane transport and intracellular signaling pathways [ – ]. The association between Cav2 and ALI has been comparatively under-investigated within the current scholarly literature. Research has demonstrated that miRNA-144-3p can downregulate Cav2, thereby exacerbating ALI in the context of sepsis, as evidenced by studies using the LPS intraperitoneal injection model [ ]. However, in our study, we found that Cav2 was highly expressed in MHS cells and lung tissues of C57BL/6 mice stimulated by LPS treatment and CLP procedure. To investigate the role of Cav2 in sepsis-induced ALI, Cav2 loss-of-function experiments were performed in vivo and both overexpression and knockdown of Cav2 were conducted in vitro. Our results demonstrated that Cav2 knockdown in murine models and MHS cells markedly reduced the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, as well as the concentrations of MDA and ROS, while simultaneously increasing SOD activity, an indicator of antioxidant defense. Conversely, Cav2 overexpression in vitro exacerbated inflammation and oxidative damage, indicating its critical role in mediating inflammatory and oxidative stress responses in lung tissue. Consistent with the findings of this research, studies examining neuroinflammation have demonstrated that Cav2 knockdown can result in a decrease in THP-1 cell infiltration, thereby alleviating inflammation within the nervous system [ ]. The observed discrepancies can be ascribed to variations in the modeling methodologies utilized, and it is crucial to account for the intrinsic differences among these models and the variations in the stages of disease progression.
Cellular apoptosis is essential for regulating cell turnover and maintaining tissue homeostasis. During sepsis-induced ALI, excessive apoptosis in lung macrophages leads to tissue damage and a weakened immune response. Thus, reducing macrophage apoptosis can help alleviate lung injury and enhance recovery [ ]. Additionally, macrophages are highly adaptable and can differentiate into distinct phenotypes in response to micro-environmental stressors: M1 macrophages, which promote inflammation, and M2 macrophages, which possess anti-inflammatory characteristics. Previous studies have shown that encouraging the polarization of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype can further reduce sepsis-induced ALI by promoting tissue repair and resolving inflammation [ ]– [ ]. Interestingly, our current study revealed that Cav2 is associated with apoptosis and polarization of macrophages. Our findings indicated that sepsis-induced ALI was accompanied by macrophage apoptosis and M1 polarization phenomena. Moreover, our study demonstrated that Cav2 knockdown in mice not only reduced macrophage apoptosis but also significantly promoted M2 polarization, resulting in a marked reduction in ALI. These protective effects were mirrored in vitro, where the knockdown of Cav2 in alveolar macrophages led to decreased apoptosis and enhanced M2 polarization, thereby alleviating cellular damage. Conversely, overexpressing Cav2 increased apoptosis and shifted macrophages toward M1 polarization, exacerbating inflammatory responses and further damaging alveolar macrophages. Importantly, our findings establish a clear relationship between Cav2 expression and macrophage apoptosis and polarization. By modulating Cav2 levels, it is possible to influence the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotypes, thereby providing a potential avenue for therapeutic intervention in sepsis-induced ALI.
Further research was conducted to clarify the mechanism underlying Cav2 in sepsis-induced ALI. The Hippo signaling pathway is based on a kinase cascade involving key kinases such as Lats1/2, Mst1/2, and Sav1. These kinases regulate gene expression by phosphorylating the downstream transcriptional coactivators YAP/TAZ, thereby controlling cellular behavior. Previous studies have indicated that the Hippo pathway is crucial for regulating immune cell functions, including macrophage activation and polarization [ ]. Additionally, prior research found that the key Hippo pathway proteins YAP/TAZ are closely related to caveolae. In particular, YAP/TAZ proteins are essential for maintaining caveolae integrity. Caveolin, as a major component of caveolae, plays a crucial role in mediating the cellular response to shear stress, which in turn activates YAP/TAZ [ ]. Our study revealed that the levels of Lats1, Mst1, Sav1, Cytoplasm p-YAP, and Cytoplasm p-TAZ proteins were reduced, whereas the levels of Nucleus YAP and Nucleus TAZ proteins were increased in MHS cells stimulated by LPS. Conversely, Cav2 knockdown effectively reversed these LPS-induced changes by restoring the localization of p-YAP and p-TAZ in the cytoplasm while reducing their nuclear abundance. In contrast, the overexpression of the Cav2 protein reversed the cellular effects resulting from Cav2 knockdown.Thus, we speculated that Cav2 could regulate the Hippo pathway. No current literature documents a link between the Hippo pathway and Cav2. Whether Cav2 regulates macrophage apoptosis and polarization through the Hippo pathway is unknown. We further explored the molecular mechanisms by which Cav2 modulates macrophage polarization by employing VT02956, a Hippo pathway inhibitor, and MY-875, a Hippo pathway activator. We established that Cav2 knockdown in mice led to the activation of the Hippo signaling pathway, which correlated with a significant reduction in macrophage apoptosis and promoted M2 polarization. These findings suggest that Cav2 may act as a negative regulator of the Hippo pathway in the context of sepsis-induced ALI. Importantly, the anti-apoptotic effects and enhanced M2 polarization observed with Cav2 knockdown can be effectively blocked by VT02956 treatment, underscoring the role of this signaling cascade in mediating Cav2’s effects. Similarly, in vitro experiments corroborated our findings, demonstrating that Cav2 knockdown activated the Hippo pathway, resulting in reduced apoptosis and enhanced M2 polarization in alveolar macrophages. Conversely, overexpression of Cav2 led to the suppression of the Hippo pathway, which aggravated apoptosis and promoted M1 polarization. The Cav2 overexpression effect could be reversed by MY-875 treatment. These results confirmed that Cav2 regulates macrophage apoptosis and polarization through the modulation of the Hippo signaling pathway. By impacting these crucial cellular processes, targeting Cav2 and the Hippo pathway may offer promising therapeutic avenues for mitigating lung damage during sepsis-induced ALI, enhancing macrophage survival, and fostering a more favorable immune response. Further investigations are warranted to fully elucidate the intricate mechanisms involved in Cav2 and Hippo signaling interactions within the context of sepsis-induced ALI.
In conclusion, this study has clarified the essential role of Cav2 knockdown in the development of sepsis-induced ALI. We showed that Cav2 knockdown, both in vivo and in vitro, influences disease progression by inhibiting macrophage apoptosis and polarization through the regulation of the Hippo signaling pathway. Overall, these findings suggest that Cav2 and the Hippo pathway may play important roles in sepsis-induced ALI. Future studies should explore pharmacological modulation and translational models to validate Cav2 and Hippo pathway.

## Limitations
in the in vivo model comprising a variety of cell types, apoptotic signals observed at the tissue level provide a comprehensive overview; however, they lack the specificity necessary to identify contributions from individual cell types. Therefore, we limit the direct association between Cav2 and macrophage apoptosis to our in vitro MHS macrophage experiments, suggesting a potential involvement of Cav2 in macrophage apoptosis. The precise cell-specific function of Cav2 in sepsis-induced ALI in vivo awaits validation through future flow cytometry-based or transgenic animal investigations. Furthermore, while our study has yielded significant insights into the functional role of Cav2, the possibility of off-target and compensatory effects highlights the necessity of further investigation into these factors. To address these limitations, subsequent studies could benefit from the implementation of CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout, which provides a more accurate and lasting genetic alteration. Additionally, we intend to perform extensive RNA sequencing for gene expression profiling, with the aim of identifying overarching shifts in gene expression that may indicate compensatory responses. This approach will deepen our understanding of the cellular context of Cav2 and help identify any off-target or compensatory effects that might have influenced our results.

## Mice Treatment and CLP
Male C57BL/6 mice without pathogens were purchased from the CAVENS lab Animal Co.Ltd. and maintained under rigorously controlled environmental conditions (ambient temperature of 22±1°C, relative humidity of 50-60%) with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. The mice were provided with standard laboratory diet and water ad libitum. The study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing University School of Medicine, affiliated with Jinling Hospital. Mice were randomly divided into four groups with ten mice per group according to preliminary experiments: Control group, CLP group, CLP+AAV-si-Cav2 group (2 weeks after 40ul AAV-si-Cav2 intranasal instillation, CLP was performed), CLP+AAV-si-Cav2+VT02956 group (2 weeks following the intranasal administration of 40 µL AAV-si-Cav2, CLP was performed accompanied by pretreated with an intravenous tail vein injection of 5 mg/kg VT02956 for a duration of two days). All mice were euthanized, and their bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was harvested for enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The right pulmonary tissues were utilized for protein extraction to perform Western blotting analysis, while the left lung tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent histopathological examination.
To investigate the impact of Cav2 knockdown on the survival rate of mice with sepsis-induced ALI, an additional cohort of 30 mice was selected for a survival analysis experiment. These mice were randomly assigned to three groups: the Control group, the CLP group, and the CLP + AAV-si-Cav2 group. The treatment protocols applied were consistent with those used in previous experiments. The study concluded upon the death of the subjects, with survival rates being continuously monitored over a period of seven days.

## Intranasal Administration of Murine Adeno-associated Virus (AAV) serotype-9 Cav2
The mouse Cav2 gene knockdown AAV serotype-9 (GENECHEM, Shanghai, China) was a specialized viral vector designed for efficient gene delivery and knockdown of the Cav2 gene in mouse models. For the intranasal delivery of AAV in murine models, subjects were initially anesthetized using intraperitoneal administration of pentobarbital (1%，80 mg/kg). Subsequently, the mouse were positioned dorsally with its oral cavity gently sealed to ensure exclusive nasal respiration. The AAV suspension (1012GC/ml) was meticulously administered dropwise into each nostril using a micropipette, with an aliquot of 40 µL per nostril . The suspension was allowed to be inhaled passively, and the mouse were maintained in this posture for 30-60 seconds to facilitate optimal absorption into the nasal and respiratory epithelia. Post-procedure, the mouse were permitted to achieve full recovery prior to being returned to its housing. After administering AAV intranasally for 2 weeks, we assessed the transfection efficiency of the Cav2 gene using Western blotting and RT-qPCR analyses. Subsequently, acute lung injury was induced by administering CLP.

## Lentivirus (LV) Transfection of MHS Cells
Prepare a cell suspension of MHS cells at a concentration of 10⁵ cells/ml in complete culture medium. Seed the cells in a 24-well plate, aiming for a confluence of 50%-70% after 24 hours. Once the cells reach 50%-70% confluence, prepare the LV transduction mixture by adding 20µl of LV-OE-Cav2 and LV-si-Cav2 to each well. Add Polybrene to enhance transduction efficiency. Incubate the cells at 37°C with 5% CO₂ for 16 hours. After 16 hours, replace the medium with fresh complete culture medium to remove unbound virus particles. After 2-3 days, observe the transduction efficiency under a fluorescence microscope. Add puromycin (3ug/ml) to the culture medium to select for cells that have successfully integrated the LV construct. Maintain the cells in puromycin-containing medium for 7-10 days to allow for stable selection. Once stable cell lines are established, expand the cells by passaging them into larger culture vessels to obtain sufficient cell numbers for downstream experiments.

## Lung Weight and Dry(W/D) Ratio
Following the sacrifice of the mice, their wet left lungs were swiftly removed, weighed, and then dried in an oven at 60 °C for 24 hours. The dry lung weights were subsequently measured, and the wet-to-dry weight ratio was calculated.

## Cell Culture and Treatment
The mouse alveolar macrophage cell line (MHS), acquired from American Type Culture Collection, was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Sigma,USA) supplementedwith 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) under an atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37°C. Initially, we conducted lentiviral-mediated overexpression (LV-OE-Cav2) and knockdown (LV-si-Cav2) of Cav2 in MHS macrophages. Verification of successful transduction was achieved through qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses. Subsequently, the cells were subjected to LPS (10 µg/mL) stimulation for 24 h.. The MHS cells were divided into six groups for further expeiments: Control group, LPS group, LPS+LV-si-Cav2 group (20ul LV-si-Cav2 and 10ug/ml LPS), LPS+LV-si-Cav2+VT02956 group (20ul LV-si-Cav2, 10ug/ml LPS, preincubate with 2umol/L VT02956 for 1h), LPS+LV-OE-si-Cav2 group(10ug/ml, LPS and 20ul LV-OE-Cav2), LPS+LV-OE-Cav2+MY-875 group (10ug/ml LPS, 20ul LV-OE-Cav2 and 1mmol/L MY-875 with a incubate for 1h).

## Histopathology Examine and Immunohistochemical(IHC) Staining
Lung tissues were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently embedded in paraffin wax. The specimens were sectioned into 4 μm thick slices and affixed to glass slides, followed by histopathological staining using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). During the immunohistochemical examination, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were subjected to a deparaffinization process, followed by rehydration and antigen retrieval using a citrate buffer solution. To quench endogenous peroxidase activity, 3% hydrogen peroxide was employed, and non-specific protein binding was blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin. The sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies, washed, and then treated with MaxVision™ HRP-labeled secondary antibodies. Detection was performed using diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen, and the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for nuclear visualization.

## Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF) Analysis 
BALF was collected by injecting 2ml of pre-cooled PBS into the trachea in batches and gently pumping back and forth three times. Right after collecting the BALF, cells were separated by centrifuging at 500g for 10 minutes at 4°C after collecting the BALF. The cells were then re-suspended in 300ul of PBS and evenly divided into three portions after counting with an automated cell counter. The first portion was used to determine the protein concentration, the second to count the total number of cells in BALF and neutrophils following Rez-Giemsa staining, and the third to assess inflammatory factor expression.. ## Flow Cytometry
MHS cells from each group were collected for macrophage polarization analysis. A flow antibody mixture was prepared with FACS buffer, and 100ul flow antibody mixture was added to each well. After closure, the cells were centrifuged at 4°C, 300g, for 5min. After discarding the supernatant, 100 µL of the flow antibody mixture was added to each well. The mixture was gently blown and mixed thoroughly, ensuring no bubbles formed. The plates were then placed at 4°C, away from light, and stained for 30 minutes. The cells were stained with APC-conjugated anti-CD80 and PE-conjugated anti-MHC antibodies, or with APC-conjugated anti-CD206 and FITC-conjugated anti-CD163 antibodies for 10 minutes at 4℃. All cellular samples were subsequently subjected to analysis utilizing a Beckman CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter Inc., USA), with data processed and interpreted using FlowJo software.

## Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)
The concentrations of IL-6 (Cat No.E03S0260, BlueGene, Shanghai, China), IL-1β (Cat No.1210122, Dakewei Bio-engineering Co, Beijing, China) and TNF-α (Cat No.E03T0008, BlueGene, Shanghai, China) in the BALF and the culture supernatant of MHS cells were quantified using ELISA kits following the manufacturer's guidelines. Then, the OD values were measured with a Full-wavelength mulfifunctional microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Company, USA) on the wave length of 450nm.

## Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) Assay
To determine the optimal concentrations of LPS for the treatment of MHS cells, various dosages of LPS were administered over a 24-hour period. ## Real-time Fluorescent Quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)
RNAeasyTManimal long RNA isolation kit spin column were applied to extracted total RNA from lung tissues and MHS cells.The concentration of RNA was determined by NanoOne Microspectrophotometer(Youning, Hangzhou, China), and the ratio of A260/A280 between 1.8 -2.0 as well as the ratio of A260/A230 between 1.8-2.2 indicated a high purity. ## Western Blotting (WB) Analysis
The total protein from MHS cells and lung tissues were collected and lysed using RIPA buffer. The protein concentration was measured with a BCA assay kit and then separated by SDS-PAGE. Following electrophoretic transfer to a PVDF membrane (Millipore), the membranes were first incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight, followed by a 2-hour incubation with secondary antibodies at 37°C. Detection of protein bands were achieved using the BeyoECL Moon Kit and documented with the Tanon-5200 luminescent imaging system (Tanon, Science & Technology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China). Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) served as the internal control of cytoplasm proteins and Histone H3 served as the interal control of nucleus proteins.

## Pulmonary Function Testing
After anesthesia, arterial blood was immediately collected from the carotid artery of mice in each group. A portable i-STAT clinical analyzer (Abbott, USA) analyzed blood gases to measure arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO₂) and arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO₂), as well as to calculate the oxygenation index (PaO₂/FiO₂).

## Cells Immunofluorescence Assay
Cells from each group were gently washed three times with PBS. They were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (BL539A, Biosharp, China) at room temperature for 10 min. After another three PBS washes, the cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (P0096, Beyotime, Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) at 4℃ for 10 min. This was followed by five PBS washes. Non-specific binding was blocked with 1ml QuickBlock™blocking buffer for immunol staining (P0260, Beyotime, Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 30 min at room temperature, after which the cells were incubated with the primary antibody overnight. Fluorescent secondary antibody was applied and then incubated for 1 h in the dark. Following three additional PBS washes, the nuclei were stained with DAPI. The slides were mounted, and images were captured with a laser confocal microscope (LSM700, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

## Statistical Analysis
Data with a normal distribution are represented by the mean and standard deviation(SD), while data with a non-normal distribution are represented by the median and interquartile range. Group comparisons were conducted using a one-way analysis of variance, preceded by the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality and Bartlett’s test for homogeneity of variance. The analysis utilized GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software company, San Diego, CA, USA), with statistical significance determined at P < 0.05.
