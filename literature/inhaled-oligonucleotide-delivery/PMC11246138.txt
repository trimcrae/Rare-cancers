## Abstract
High-mobility group box 1 was first discovered in the calf thymus as a DNA-binding nuclear protein and has been widely studied in diverse fields, including neurology and neuroscience. High-mobility group box 1 in the extracellular space functions as a pro-inflammatory damage-associated molecular pattern, which has been proven to play an important role in a wide variety of central nervous system disorders such as ischemic stroke, Alzheimer’s disease, frontotemporal dementia, Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, epilepsy, and traumatic brain injury. Several drugs that inhibit high-mobility group box 1 as a damage-associated molecular pattern, such as glycyrrhizin, ethyl pyruvate, and neutralizing anti-high-mobility group box 1 antibodies, are commonly used to target high-mobility group box 1 activity in central nervous system disorders. Although it is commonly known for its detrimental inflammatory effect, high-mobility group box 1 has also been shown to have beneficial pro-regenerative roles in central nervous system disorders. In this narrative review, we provide a brief summary of the history of high-mobility group box 1 research and its characterization as a damage-associated molecular pattern, its downstream receptors, and intracellular signaling pathways, how high-mobility group box 1 exerts the repair-favoring roles in general and in the central nervous system, and clues on how to differentiate the pro-regenerative from the pro-inflammatory role. Research targeting high-mobility group box 1 in the central nervous system may benefit from differentiating between the two functions rather than overall suppression of high-mobility group box 1.
Keywords: central nervous system, damage-associated molecular pattern, ethyl pyruvate, glycyrhizzin, high mobility group box 1, inflammation, neural stem cells, neurodevelopment, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, redox status, regeneration

## Introduction
High mobility group box 1 (HMGB1, HMG1, HMG-1), otherwise known as amphoterin in the nervous system, ever since its first characterization as a non-histone DNA binding protein, has been extensively investigated in various fields of biology including neuroscience. HMGB1 was initially identified in the calf thymus as a nuclear protein that binds to DNA and aids its packaging into chromatin, and gained the name “high mobility group box” due to its high mobility during polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Goodwin and Johns, 1973). HMGB1 is an evolutionarily highly conserved protein with one polypeptide sequence containing two DNA-binding domains, the A-box and B-box. The nuclear form of HMGB1 protects DNA from mutagens and photons. Germline or uterine deletion of HMGB1 has been linked to embryonic lethality and adverse pregnancy outcomes. Successive studies have revealed that HMGB1 can also be secreted or released into the extracellular space and that this extracellular form of HMGB1 has a remotely different function from the nuclear form. Extracellular HMGB1 was first proposed to act as a mediator of endotoxin lethality in mice (Wang et al., 1999), and shown to be released from necrotic cells damaged by heating or freeze-thawing (Tang et al., 2023). It was later revealed that HMGB1 can also be released during apoptosis and other modalities of programmed cell death, such as necroptosis, ferroptosis, and pyroptosis (Chen et al., 2020, 2022b; Conche et al., 2023). Thus, extracellular HMGB1 was defined as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), and this definition has been widely accepted to date.
Following the establishment of HMGB1 as an inflammation-mediating DAMP, most studies on HMGB1 in human diseases have focused on the suppression of its pro-inflammatory properties, and central nervous system (CNS) diseases are no exception. There are numerous reports on the beneficial effects of HMGB1 antagonism through anti-HMGB1 antibodies which neutralize HMGB1 or drugs such as glycyrrhizin and ethyl pyruvate in various CNS diseases such as ischemic stroke, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), frontotemporal dementia (FTD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), multiple sclerosis, epilepsy, and traumatic brain injury (TBI) (Paudel et al., 2018; Gaikwad et al., 2021; Jin et al., 2021). Comprehensive reviews of the detrimental effects of HMGB1 and the benefits of HMGB1 antagonism in CNS disorders have also been published (Paudel et al., 2018; Mao et al., 2022). However, extracellular HMGB1 is not a protein with exclusively destructive functions, and evidence suggests that HMGB1 is capable of aiding tissue repair and regeneration, both in general and in the CNS. The regeneration-promoting properties of HMGB1 shed light on possibilities worth preserving. In this review, we focus on the regenerative role and possibility of HMGB1 in the CNS and discuss research providing clues on the circumstances and conditions under which HMGB1 can exert such repair-promoting functions.

## Search Strategy
We performed a comprehensive search of PubMed and Google Scholar search engines from their inception to 2023. A combination of multiple keywords among “HMGB1,” “history,” “CNS,” “ischemic stroke,” “Alzheimer’s disease,” “frontotemporal dementia,” “FTD,” “Parkinson’s disease,” “multiple sclerosis,” “epilepsy,” “traumatic brain injury,” “release,” “signaling,” “regeneration,” “repair,” “chemotaxis,” “neurogenesis,” “brain development,” “redox”, and “concentration.” The research articles, review articles, and references cited in the reviews were thoroughly examined.

## High Mobility Group Box 1 as an Inflammatory Damage-Associated Molecular Pattern in the Central Nervous System
HMGB1, initially identified as a nuclear protein, was rediscovered through subsequent research on its extracellular presence and function as a key mediator of inflammation. HMGB1 in the extracellular space was discovered to be released from both primary and RAW264.7 macrophages upon treatment with the endotoxin lipopolysaccharide (LPS), in a more delayed manner compared to the acute phase reaction proteins tumor necrosis factor (TNF) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and treatment of mice with LPS increased serum HMGB1. The administration of HMGB1 to mice produced signs of endotoxemia, such as lethargy, piloerection, and diarrhea, and proved to be lethal at high doses, whereas HMGB1 antagonism through repeated treatments with anti-HMGB1 antibodies could ameliorate the LPS-induced endotoxin lethality (Xue et al., 2021). HMGB1 was found to stimulate the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF, IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-1RA, IL-6, IL-8, macrophage inflammatory protein (MIP)-1α, and MIP-1β, in human monocytes. Furthermore, the intratracheal treatment of HMGB1 was documented to mediate acute lung inflammation, showing neutrophil accumulation, lung edema, and increased TNF-ɑ and IL-1β production in the lungs. It was later discovered that HMGB1 can mediate sterile inflammation and elevate serum alanine transaminase levels in a murine model of liver ischemia-reperfusion injury, and can also be released from non-immune cells such as HeLa cells or primary fibroblasts upon necrotic damage induced either pharmaceutically, via ionomycin, carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenylhydrazone, deoxyglucose, and sodium azide, or by freeze-thawing (Tang et al., 2023). Subsequent research has revealed that other programmed cell death modalities, such as late apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, can also induce the release of HMGB1 into the extracellular space (Chen et al., 2020, 2022b; Conche et al., 2023). Thus, HMGB1 was clarified as a mediator of inflammation released from damaged and dead cells, and gained the term “alarmin” or “damage-associated molecular pattern”.
The finding of extracellular HMGB1 as a pro-inflammatory DAMP launched a vast bulk of ongoing research targeting HMGB1 in various human diseases, and CNS diseases were also a subject of great interest. For example, in ischemic stroke, HMGB1 has been reported to be massively released into the extracellular space post-injury in human patients and animal models of middle cerebral artery occlusion resulting in neuroinflammation, whereas the suppression of HMGB1 activity by glycyrrhizin, anti-HMGB1 antibodies, and short hairpin RNA (shRNA) knockdown has been shown to ameliorate such destructive effects and decrease lesion size (Ye et al., 2019; Gao et al., 2023). Studies on HMGB1 and neurodegenerative diseases, such as AD, FTD, and PD, have also revealed the pathogenic roles of HMGB1. A network-based analysis of protein–protein interactions in animal models of AD and FTD revealed that HMGB1 is a core pathomechanism protein and that anti-HMGB1 antibodies could improve Morris water maze results and rescue neurite degeneration, neuronal DNA damage, and protein aggregation in FTLD mouse models (Jin et al., 2021). A different study by Gaikwad et al. (2021) reported that tau oligomers can trigger cellular senescence in the brain through HMGB1 and contribute to the pathology of AD and FTD and that inhibition of HMGB1 through glycyrrhizin and EP could block the development of the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, reduce neuroinflammation, and improve cognitive function in hTau mice. Regarding PD, HMGB1 levels were elevated in postmortem substantia nigra samples, cerebrospinal fluid, and serum samples of patients with PD, and inhibition of HMGB1 by glycyrrhizin and neutralizing anti-HMGB1 antibodies ameliorated dopaminergic neuron degeneration in animal models of PD induced by 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine and 6-hydroxy dopamine (Mao et al., 2022). HMGB1 has also been implicated in multiple sclerosis and experimental autoimmune encephalitis; elevated serum HMGB1 levels in multiple sclerosis and experimental autoimmune encephalitis have been documented, along with amelioration of the clinical experimental autoimmune encephalitis score, demyelination, and neuroinflammation, and the upregulation of serum IL-17 by the administration of an anti-HMGB1 monoclonal antibody (Paudel et al., 2019b; Mao et al., 2022). HMGB1 has been reported to be involved in seizure disorders and epileptogenesis (Vezzani et al., 2013; Paudel et al., 2019a), and HMGB1 antagonism has revealed therapeutic potential in preventing or suppressing seizures and epilepsy progression, in pilocarpine or kainate-induced seizure and status epilepticus animal models (Maroso et al., 2010; Li et al., 2019; Rusina et al., 2021; Mao et al., 2022). HMGB1 is also involved in the pathology of TBI, and strategies targeting HMGB1 in TBI models have been proven to have neuroprotective effects, improve post-TBI edema, attenuate glial activation, and ultimately facilitate neurological function recovery (Paudel et al., 2018; Tan et al., 2021). Finally, collective reviews have suggested that HMGB1 is a common biomarker of CNS disorders, indirectly implicating the importance of HMGB1 and neuroinflammation in the pathogenesis of CNS disorders (Paudel et al., 2018; Mao et al., 2022).

## Secretion and Release of High Mobility Group Box 1
HMGB1 can be released into the extracellular space either by active secretion due to stress stimuli or by passive release due to cell death. Stress stimuli, such as reactive oxygen species or reactive nitrogen species, endotoxins, calcium ions, and various other signals, have been shown to trigger the active secretion of HMGB1 in various cell types, mainly through non-canonical pathways other than the classical endoplasmic reticulum-Golgi secretory pathway, because of the absence of a leader sequence in the primary structure of HMGB1 (Kwak et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2022b). Alternatively, cell death modalities such as caspase-mediated apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and necrosis have been shown to cause passive release of HMGB1 (Chen et al., 2022b). Various post-translational modifications affect the secretion and release of HMGB1, such as acetylation and phosphorylation of nuclear localization signal 1/2, methylation (of Lys42), and N-glycosylation (of N37, N134, and N135), which alter the binding of HMGB1 to DNA (Yuan et al., 2020). The modulation of HMGB1 release has been reported to have a therapeutic effect in CNS disease models, which has been studied through the use of ethyl pyruvate, an HMGB1 inhibitor that suppresses the release of HMGB1 into the extracellular space (Chavali et al., 2020; Mao et al., 2021; Fan et al., 2022).

## High Mobility Group Box 1 Receptors and Downstream Intracellular Signaling
After secretion or release, extracellular free HMGB1 can bind to its corresponding receptors, toll-like receptor 2/4 (TLR2/4), receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE or AGER), CXC motif chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4), and T-cell immunoglobulin mucin-3 on the membranes of recipient cells to initiate a downstream signaling cascade.
TLRs are evolutionarily conserved transmembrane pattern recognition receptors primarily utilized by the innate immune system (Fitzgerald and Kagan, 2020), characterized by a leucine-rich repeat extracellular domain that binds to DAMPs or pathogen-associated molecular patterns and a cytosolic Toll/interleukin-1 receptor domain that carries out the downstream signaling pathway. Extracellular HMGB1 mainly interacts with TLR2 and 4 which are expressed on the cell membrane, and the binding of HMGB1 activates downstream junction molecules such as Toll/interleukin-1 receptor domain-containing adapter-inducing interferon-β and myeloid differentiation primary-response protein 88 (MyD88), which in turn activates interferon-regulatory factors and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), and culminates in the release of inflammatory cytokines such as interleukins, TNFs and interferons (Ren et al., 2023).
RAGE is a 40 kDa, multiligand pattern recognition receptors that was first proposed to bind to advanced glycation end products, as its name suggests (Reynaert et al., 2023). Additionally, RAGE can bind to DNA, RNA, LPS, amyloid-β fibrils, S100/calgranulins, and HMGB1. Upon ligand binding, the cytoplasmic domain of RAGE acts similarly as do membrane TLRs, recruiting the adaptor proteins diaphanous homolog 1, Toll/interleukin-1 receptor domain-containing adaptor protein, and MyD88 to activate numerous intracellular effector signals, including Ras-related C3 botulinum toxin substrate 1 (Rac1)/cell division control protein 42 (Cdc42), Ras-mediated extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2), phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt, stress-activated protein kinase/c-Jun-NH2-terminal kinase, p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), NF-κB, and caspases (Hudson and Lippman, 2018).
CXCR4 is a G-protein coupled receptor that has CXC motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12) or stromal cell-derived factor 1 as its conventional ligands. HMGB1 can bind to CXCR4 by forming a heterocomplex with CXCL12, a process that is dependent on the redox state of HMGB1; reduced HMGB1, but not oxidized disulfide HMGB1, can form the HMGB1-CXCL12 complex. CXCR4 is bound mainly to heterotrimeric Gi proteins, and ligand binding causes the heterotrimer to split into the guanosine triphosphate (GTP) bound Gαi and βγ subunits; the βγ subunits subsequently activate PI3K/Akt, and the Gαi subunit turns on PI3K/Akt and MAPK, NF- κB, Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription pathways (Bianchi and Mezzapelle, 2020).
As such, the downstream signaling of HMGB1 through TLRs, RAGE, and CXCR4 mostly converges into renowned signaling nexuses such as PI3K/Akt, ERK1/2, and NF-κB. Through these signals, HMGB1 exerts its detrimental pro-inflammatory and pro-cancer effects. However, these signaling pathways downstream of HMGB1 are multifunctional proteins, and investigations have revealed many more functions than those mentioned above, including favorable roles in cell survival, proliferation, migration, and differentiation. For instance, NF-κB has been proposed to exert a neuroprotective effect by inducing the production of anti-apoptotic proteins or antioxidants such as B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2), inhibitor of apoptosis proteins, and manganese superoxide dismutase (Mattson and Meffert, 2006). The chemokine receptor CXCR4 has been shown to mediate progenitor cell recruitment and tissue regeneration upon the binding of HMGB1, promote neurogenesis by contributing to the survival, proliferation, and migration of neural precursor cells (NPCs), and also facilitate remyelination by enhancing the proliferation, migration and differentiation of oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs) (Carbajal et al., 2011; Cheng et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2018; Tian et al., 2018; Tirone et al., 2018; Senf et al., 2021). Research has also shown that TLR2 and TLR4 play important roles in CNS repair, such as regulating the self-renewal and differentiation of NPCs, and promoting remyelination by promoting the survival and migration of OPCs (Choi et al., 2017, 2023; Sanchez-Petidier et al., 2022). Furthermore, the capacity of HMGB1 to induce cell proliferation in various cancer cell lines has been used as evidence that HMGB1 contributes to cancer progression; however, such research results can also indirectly imply that HMGB1 contributes to wound healing and tissue repair (Wang et al., 2020; Zhu et al., 2021).

## High Mobility Group Box 1 and Tissue Regeneration
The pro-regenerative role of extracellular HMGB1 is not merely based on assumptions made from downstream signaling pathways. Numerous studies have shown the beneficial role of HMGB1 in diverse organs and tissues. Considering that HMGB1 is released after injury, most evidence points to its role in tissue repair and regeneration. A study by Tirone et al. (2018) proposed that fully reduced HMGB1 can orchestrate muscle and liver regeneration by acting as a chemoattractant for stem cells via CXCR4; injured tibialis anterior muscles were treated with HMGB1 of various redox states, and injured muscles treated with fully reduced HMGB1 or a non-oxidizable synthetic form of HMGB1 exhibited increased expression of the satellite cell marker Pax7, myogenic markers myoD and myogenin, and pro-regenerative macrophage phenotype marker CD163 coupled with a decrease in tissue injury measured by the Evans blue dye. The regenerative effects were diminished by co-treatment of the CXCR4 antagonist AMD3100 (Tirone et al., 2018). Another study by Lee et al. (2018) showed that HMGB1, via CXCR4, promotes the repair of skeletal, hematopoietic, and muscle tissues by targeting endogenous stem cells and transitioning them to GAlert, which is an intermediate status between the G0 and G1 phases of the cell cycle, identifiable by increased ATP levels, mitochondrial DNA, cell size, faster entry into the cell cycle, and mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) dependency. Exogenous HMGB1 can induce regeneration of the myocardium in a mouse model of myocardial infarction through ligation of the left coronary artery, by enhancing the proliferation and differentiation of C-kit+ progenitor cells; hearts treated with recombinant HMGB1 showed an increase in α-sarcomeric cardiac cells and improvement in ejection fraction despite a lesion size comparable to glutathione s-transferase-treated controls (Belmadani and Matrougui, 2022). As such, the majority of research on the regenerative role of HMGB1 emphasizes its action on stem cells and progenitor cells to promote tissue regeneration.

## Regenerative Role of High Mobility Group Box 1 in the Central Nervous System
The CNS contains a pool of endogenous stem and progenitor cells that potentially benefit from the regeneration-promoting effects of HMGB1. Adult neural stem cells and NPCs remain in the hippocampus and subventricular zone after brain development, and OPCs are diffusely distributed throughout the brain and spinal cord, in both the gray and white matter. Neural stem cells, NPCs, and OPCs express HMGB1 receptors, which make them capable of responding to HMGB1 to promote CNS repair after injury. There is direct and indirect evidence that neural stem cells, NPCs, and OPCs express TLR2/4, RAGE, and CXCR4 and are capable of producing a response to HMGB1 treatment (Qin et al., 2008; Meneghini et al., 2010; Choi et al., 2017, 2023; Sanchez-Petidier et al., 2022).
Multiple studies have demonstrated that HMGB1 is involved in neurogenesis and normal nervous system development, which indirectly indicates its capacity to promote nervous system regeneration. Early studies on nervous system development discovered the presence of a 30-kDa, heparin-binding adhesive protein in perinatal rat brains, which could promote the outgrowth of neurites in in vitro N18 neuroblastoma cell cultures, termed p30 at the point of discovery and later revealed to be HMGB1 (Merenmies et al., 1991; Gou et al., 2020). Subsequent studies more clearly elucidated the spatiotemporal pattern of HMGB1 expression in the brain; HMGB1 was initially widely expressed in the cortical plate during early mouse development (embryo E14.5), followed by an increase in expression along with the expansion of the cortical plate by E16; at later stages (E18), HMGB1 was detectable by immunohistochemistry only in restricted areas where neurogenesis is preserved, such as the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus, olfactory bulb, and cells surrounding the telencephalic ventricles (Guazzi et al., 2003). HMGB1 has also been detected in the cerebral vesicles, neural tube, and notochord of amphioxus, as well as in lampreys during forebrain development (Zhao et al., 2020). The in vivo knockdown of HMGB1 through antisense morpholinos in zebrafish resulted in reduced animal size, brain size, and space between the eyes, as well as a deficiency in the catecholaminergic neural networks of the forebrain, hampering the survival and proliferation of neural progenitors involved in forebrain development (Gou et al., 2020). In vitro studies on HMGB1, neurite outgrowth, and cellular migration revealed that HMGB1 administration promoted the growth of neuronal processes and the migration of N18 neuronal cells and that antagonism of HMGB1 by glycyrrhizin, neutralizing antibodies, or antisense nucleotides suppressed this effect (Merenmies et al., 1991; Huttunen et al., 2000).
The regenerative function of HMGB1 in CNS diseases has also been evidenced, as shown in . Research by Chen et al. (2022a) showed that fully reduced or synthetic non-oxidizable HMGB1 can help promote neurite outgrowth and axon elongation in cultured primary rat cortical neurons, and ultimately attenuate cognitive impairment observed by open field and novel object recognition tests after experimental TBI (controlled cortex injury or controlled cortical impact). SH3-domain-containing ring finger 2 (SH3RF2) is upregulated in HMGB1-treated primary neurons, and genetic knockdown of SH3RF2 reverses the elongation of neuronal processes in vitro and improvement in neurological function (Chen et al., 2022a). A study by Fang et al. (2014) utilized a model of spinal cord injury by transection in zebrafish and observed that the application of antisense HMGB1 morpholinos impaired locomotor function, as examined by the distance the fish swam, and inhibited axon regrowth beyond the transection site by axon tracking with biocytin. Another study by Tian et al. (2017) proposed that HMGB1 acts through RAGE to promote the clearance of blood clots and facilitates neurovascular remodeling in a rat model of subarachnoid hemorrhage by autologous blood injection. In the same study, the inhibition of HMGB1 by ethyl pyruvate and glycyrrhizin or RAGE by FPS-ZM1 caused a decrease in the levels of growth factors, such as neuronal growth factor, brain-derived neurotrophic factor, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and the number of doublecortin-positive neurons in the cortex, which resulted in the suppression of functional recovery, as determined by appetite, activity and gait deficit (Tian et al., 2017).
 Studies have also shown that HMGB1 promotes angiogenesis in CNS diseases. HMGB1s’ angiogenetic property itself has been demonstrated in cancer research, which has shown that HMGB1 promotes vessel formation, increases VEGF levels, and attracts endothelial cells and mesangioblasts (Sasahira et al., 2007; Gou et al., 2020; Mo and Chen, 2022). A study on HMGB1 and post-stroke angiogenesis by Hayakawa et al showed that astrocytic HMGB1 promoted the proliferation of endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) in a mouse model of focal cerebral ischemia, and that inhibition of astrocytic HMGB1 or RAGE on EPCs reversed this effect (Hayakawa et al., 2010, 2012), and another study by Lei et al. (2015) showed that HMGB1 elevated VEGF levels in a rat model of intracerebral hemorrhage by collagenase injection, where the administration of ethyl pyruvate or the RAGE inhibitor FPS-ZM1 reduced VEGF levels and vessel density.
In the case of oligodendrocytes, OPCs, and myelin repair, studies conducted by Choi et al. (2017, 2023) showed that HMGB1 acts through TLR2 as an autocrine chemoattractant and trophic factor for OPCs in a rodent model of ischemic white matter injury. HMGB1 administration facilitated the migration of OPCs in an in vitro Boyden chamber migration assay and attenuated cell death in response to oxygen-glucose deprivation. Co-treatment with glycyrrhizin or TLR2 short interference RNA (siRNA) reversed these effects. In in vivo white matter injury models induced by endothelin-1 or N5-(1-iminoethyl)-L-ornithine injection, HMGB or TLR2 antagonism aggravated lesion progression and motor function deficits (Choi et al., 2017, 2023). Another study by Dong et al. (2013) suggests that HMGB1 interacts with RAGE and its downstream NF-κB and Rac1/Cdc42 to promote OPC migration in a gecko model of spinal cord injury. Thus, HMGB1 has a distinct pro-regenerative role in the CNS, which differs from its more well-known pro-inflammatory function, which mainly involves chemoattractant and trophic effects on stem cells or progenitor cells in the CNS.

## Differentiating “Good” and “Bad” High Mobility Group Box 1
The signaling pathway of HMGB1, its regenerative potential, and evidence of the favorable function of HMGB1 in promoting CNS regeneration are worth noting. However, without a strategy to effectively distinguish the pro-regenerative aspect of HMGB1 from its pro-inflammatory property of HMGB1 as a DAMP, the utilization of HMGB1 as a repair-promoting therapeutic agent is far from plausible. Drugs currently used in research to target HMGB1 in human diseases, such as glycyrrhizin, ethyl pyruvate, antibodies against HMGB1, and genetic knockdown do not distinguish the inflammation and regeneration caused by HMGB1; glycyrrhizin binds both A and B-box of HMGB1 and thereby blocks its interaction with receptors, and ethyl pyruvate inhibits the release of HMGB1 into the extracellular space (Xue et al., 2021). Therefore, it can be said that there are niches for improvement in drugs targeting HMGB1. However, to date, no clinically feasible pharmaceutical method can accurately differentiate between the detrimental and beneficial role of HMGB1, and HMGB1 remains a double-edged sword.
Research on HMGB1 has provided clues on how to separate this dual function. First, the activity of HMGB1 is dramatically influenced by its redox status. HMGB1 has three cysteine residues (C23 and C45 in the A-box and C106 in the B-box) that are subject to oxidation, and numerous studies have demonstrated that the oxidation or reduction of the cysteine residues causes a marked change in function. Alteration of the redox status of the cysteine residues with dithiothreitol, which adds a thiol side chain to the cysteine residues of HMGB1, created a reduced form of HMGB1 termed all-thiol or fully reduced HMGB1. HMGB1 purified without the presence of dithiothreitol showed the formation of a disulfide bond between the two cysteine residues in the A-box, and a terminally oxidized form of HMGB1 with all three oxidized cysteine residues could be purified in the presence of reactive oxygen species. Additionally, an oxidation-resistant synthetic form of HMGB1 could be created by replacing cysteine residues with serine, termed 1S-, 2S-, or 3S-HMGB1, depending on the number of residues replaced (Venereau et al., 2012). All-thiol or 3S-HMGB1 exhibited chemoattractant features through CXCR4; mouse 3T3 fibroblasts migrated toward 3S-HMGB1 but not oxidized HMGB1, and HMGB1 formed a heterocomplex with CXCL12, which was only true for all-thiol HMGB1 and not disulfide-HMGB1. Terminal oxidation of HMGB1 abrogated both aspects; neither chemoattractant nor inflammatory features were discerned in this state (Schiraldi et al., 2012; Venereau et al., 2012). In a model of muscle injury with injection of cardiotoxin into mice tibialis anterior muscles, an initial increase in reduced all-thiol HMGB1 was discovered, which was followed by oxidized disulfide-HMGB1. The addition of all-thiol or 3S-HMGB1 with CXCL12 was sufficient to induce the recruitment of inflammatory leukocytes to the injury site, although the wildtype all thiol-HMGB1 could be oxidized by reactive oxygen species in the microenvironment and lose its chemoattractant capacity (Venereau et al., 2012). In a study on the effect of HMGB1 on cancer cells, reduced HMGB1 bound RAGE to induce beclin-1-dependent autophagy in human pancreatic and colon cancer cell lines and induced resistance to ultraviolet radiation or chemotherapy by paclitaxel, melphalan, and oxalitaxel, whereas oxidized HMGB1 sensitized the cells to chemotherapy, showing increased apoptosis mediated by the caspase 9/3 intrinsic pathway (Tang et al., 2010). The redox status of HMGB1 was also shown to influence its secretion in response to inflammatory stimuli, as shown in studies by Kwak et al. (2019, 2020) utilizing periredoxin I and II knockout mice to block the oxidation of HMGB1, where the blockade of HMGB1 oxidation in periredoxin knockout mice resulted in decreased cytoplasmic translocation and secretion of HMGB1 from the bone marrow-derived macrophages of the animals. Thus, the redox status of HMGB1 may act as a switch that alters its function. Determining how CNS diseases or injuries of varying nature differentially impact the redox balance of HMGB1 and which types of redox signaling or oxidative stress are mainly involved in the alteration of HMGB1 redox status may help optimize HMGB1-targeting treatment. In addition, the potential influence of different post-translational modifications of HMGB1 other than oxidation/reduction, which have mainly been reported to alter the secretion and release of HMGB1, on the two opposing functions of HMGB1, may also be an intriguing subject for further expansion of this topic.
Another clue is the cell type on which HMGB1 takes action; more specifically, the differing activity HMGB1 can have on cells that do not belong to the immune system compared to cells that do. Various studies on HMGB1 in in vitro cell culture models, in which immune/inflammatory cells can be excluded from the system, have shown distinct responses in cells that differ from the more well-known results of the HMGB1 signaling pathway, such as leukocyte activation or inflammatory cytokine production. In the previous sections, we reviewed studies on HMGB1, showing that HMGB1 administration can induce cell proliferation in cancer cell lines (Zhu et al., 2021), enhance neurite outgrowth in rat primary neurons and neuroblastoma cell lines (Chen et al., 2022a), increase the proliferation of rat primary spleen EPCs (Hayakawa et al., 2012), and promote the survival and migration of rat primary OPCs (Choi et al., 2017, 2023). In addition, a study by Gao et al. (2011) utilized mixed cultures of brain cells treated with drugs used for modeling PD such as rotenone, and examined the effect of HMGB1, which revealed that HMGB1 did indeed exacerbate inflammation and cause the loss of dopaminergic neurons, but also that this phenomenon was only observed in cultures where microglia were present. HMGB1 did not have a destructive, inflammatory effect on neuron-enriched cultures with little or no microglia (Gao et al., 2011). Non-immune cells in the CNS also harbor receptors for HMGB1, which may serve as HMGB1 recipient cells and produce a response that differs from the classical inflammation-promoting effect. The precise reason non-immune cells react to HMGB1 in a manner distinct from that of immune cells is yet to be verified. Differences in the main receptor and downstream signaling of HMGB1 may exist between immune and non-immune cells. Another possibility is that the redox state of HMGB1 is modulated differently in these two cell types, which is worth noting considering that immune responses generally involve an increase in nitro-oxidative stress (Morris et al., 2022).
Previous studies also suggest that the concentration of HMGB1 may influence its effects. 3T3 fibroblasts respond to a lower concentration of HMGB1 (10 ng/mL) than do immune cells, such as leukocytes, and show increased migration, as determined by the Boyden chamber migration assay (Venereau et al., 2012). Another study demonstrated the effect of HMGB1 on polymorphonuclear leukocytes and discovered that HMGB1 at low concentrations (50–100 ng/mL) inhibited migration and chemotaxis, whereas at high concentrations (5000 ng/mL), exerted a chemoattractant effect on polymorphonuclear leukocytes (Berthelot et al., 2012). HMGB1 at low concentrations (1–10 ng/mL) promoted the proliferation of EPCs compared to high concentrations (100–1000 ng/mL) which did not have the same effect and could potentially damage endothelial cells owing to their pro-inflammatory effects (Hayakawa et al., 2012). A low level of HMGB1 (0.1–1 ng/mL) was sufficient to protect OPCs and augment their migratory capacity. No increase in OPC migration was observed at higher HMGB1 levels (100–1000 ng/mL; Choi et al., 2017, 2023). Conversely, research on HMGB1 and immune cells has revealed that low doses of HMGB1 cause T lymphocytes to adopt an inflammatory phenotype, showing increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and decreased expression of cytotoxic T-lymphocyte–associated protein 4, which is reversed at high doses (Ye et al., 2019). Collectively, HMGB1 may have different functions depending on its concentration, which is not merely proportional to the dosage. Further investigation is necessary to confirm the changes in HMGB1 levels at different concentrations and to determine the critical concentration at which the alteration in function occurs.
Prior research has provided clues on how to separate the favorable and unfavorable effects of HMGB1, such as the redox state of the molecule, recipient cell type, and concentration of HMGB1. Studies on how to incorporate these clues into strategies targeting HMGB1 in CNS disorders and the discovery of previously unknown mechanisms of HMGB1 action may provide additional information on how to approach HMGB1 antagonism to avoid the repression of its regenerative merit in CNS diseases.

## Conclusion
HMGB1 is a protein with diverse forms and functions. Among them, favorable regeneration-promoting functions apply to the CNS. A large amount of research on HMGB1 in CNS diseases has focused on the inhibition of its pro-inflammatory effects as a DAMP and hereby utilizes broad inhibitors that subdue the potential benefits. In this review, we elucidate the signaling pathway of HMGB1, which hints at the reparative potential of the protein, present research evidence on the pro-regenerative activity of HMGB1 in the CNS, and provide insights on how to distinguish the advantageous aspects of HMGB1 from its detrimental inflammatory effect ( ). Further research on how to incorporate these points that separate “good” and “bad” HMGB1 into pharmaceutical design, as well as investigation for additional aspects of HMGB1, which can be exploited to distinguish its dual effect is necessary to exploit the regeneration-promoting potential of HMGB1 that is hidden beneath the inflammatory guise. Targeting HMGB1 will become a much more effective approach for the treatment of CNS diseases once its harmful effects can be segregated from its potential benefits.
