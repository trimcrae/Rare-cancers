## Abstract
CD4+IFN-γ+IL-17+ T cells and their secreted cytokines play a critical role in the anti-mycobacterial response. However, the underlying synergistic mechanism of IFN-γ and IL-17 is little known. This study demonstrates that the downregulation of coronin-1A retention on the phagosome is a synergistic pathway for IFN-γ and IL-17 that kills Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) in macrophages. IL-17 alone does not play a role in intracellular Mtb killing but potentiates the anti-mycobactericidal pathway of IFN-γ. Co-treatment of IFN-γ/IL-17 inhibited phosphorylation of STAT3, induced LRG47 expression, and reduced retention of coronin-1A on phagosome, thereby eliciting phagolysosomal fusion and bacterial killing, which was proved through response analysis of IFN-γ/IL-17 in Mtb-infected macrophages transfected with small interfering RNA of coro1a, lrg47, or stat1. Determination of adjunctive therapeutic effect of IFN-γ, IL-17, or both with anti-mycobacterial drugs showed that the bacterial load in the tissue of mice co-treated with IFN-γ/IL-17, even at 1 ng/mL concentration each, was more rapidly reduced compared to those of IFN-γ injection or chemotherapy alone. It inhibited the re-growth after treatment termination. Our results shed new light on the synergistic mechanisms of IFN-γ/IL-17 for anti-mycobacterial responses, which might be targeted as an adjunctive chemotherapeutic method against tuberculosis.
Subject terms: Tuberculosis, Microbiology

IFN-γ and IL-17 act synergistically to enhance anti-mycobacterial immunity by inhibiting coronin-1A retention on Mtb-containing phagosomes through STAT signal-dependent LRG47 activation, offering new insights for cytokine-based tuberculosis therapy.

## Introduction
Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) poses a significant global public health challenge, necessitating the urgent development of effective prevention and treatment strategies. Mtb has evolved sophisticated mechanisms to evade immune surveillance, including manipulation of macrophage death pathways, inhibition of phagosome-lysosome fusion, and interference with inflammatory cytokine synthesis, contributing to chronic infection and host dissemination – . The ability of this bacterium to evade host immune responses and persist within host macrophages underscores the need for innovative approaches to fight against tuberculosis (TB) .
The World Health Organization recognizes antimicrobial resistance as a global health priority and emphasizes the importance of addressing this issue within national health systems , . The emergence of drug-resistant TB strains exacerbates this challenge by prolonging treatment duration, increasing healthcare costs, and increasing mortality rates . Conventional chemotherapy may suppress host immune responses, necessitating immunotherapeutic interventions to restore immune function, particularly in patients with multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) . With limited chemotherapeutic options for extensively drug-resistant TB and associated high mortality, immunomodulators have emerged as promising alternative treatment approaches. These agents can potentially restore the balance disrupted by infection and strengthen the host’s weakened immune system, either through immune stimulation or by reducing excessive immune responses .
Although cytokine therapy garnered significant attention in the 1980s and 1990s, its clinical application faced challenges and limitations, leading to alternative treatment modalities. However, this does not diminish the potential or importance of cytokine therapies in modern medicine. Challenges with cytokine immunotherapy include short half-lives and high costs, necessitating ongoing research efforts to optimize its therapeutic efficiency . Although patented cytokines with therapeutic relevance to TB exist, administering a single cytokine may not be sufficient to combat Mtb .
Traditionally, Th1 lymphocytes producing IFN-γ, capable of activating macrophages, are thought to play a central role in TB protection. This concept is supported by the occurrence of severe mycobacterial infections in humans and animals with defects in CD4+ cell populations or genes in the IFN-γ/IL-12 axis . However, the extent to which host protection depends on Th1/IFN-γ is currently debated. Recent studies, including our own findings, have reported no correlation or only a minimal role between the magnitude of Th1/IFN-γ response and the level of protection against TB – . Some researchers have found that excessive IFN-γ production by Th1 cells can be detrimental to the host .
However, the exact role of IL-17 remains unclear. Initially considered potentially pathological because of its ability to induce neutrophilic inflammation, which can mediate TB pathology, recent studies have demonstrated that IL-17 may also play an important role in TB defense. In mice, Th17 cells provided defense during experimental infections induced by the highly pathogenic Mtb strain HN878 and are important mediators of vaccine-induced protection , . The induction of Th1 lymphocytes, stimulation of their recruitment to infection sites, and formation of ectopic lymphoid follicles and granulomas have been suggested as the underlying mechanisms of action of IL-17 – . Research findings regarding the role of Th17 responses during TB infection in humans are conflicting. Some studies have suggested a protective effect of elevated Th17 responses in patients with latent TB infection (LTBI) , , whereas others have reported increased Th17 responses in patients with active TB – . Additionally, some studies have found no differences in the Th17 responses between LTBI and TB , and there are reports of very low frequencies of Mtb-specific Th17 lymphocytes in patients with TB . Consequently, it remains unclear whether Th17 lymphocytes play a protective, pathological, or non-significant role in the pathogenesis of TB.
Recent studies have focused on the interaction between IFN-γ and IL-17. Evidence suggests that T cells co-expressing IFN-γ and IL-17 (Th1/Th17) may play a crucial role in TB defense , . These cells express CXCR3 and CCR6, which are surface markers for Th1 and Th17 cells, respectively. Our research has also demonstrated the importance of IFN-γ/IL-17 co-expression in TB protection , . Notably, in vaccine studies, culturing infected macrophages with supernatants from HSP90-ESAT6-vaccinated T-cells stimulated with ESAT6 significantly inhibited Mtb growth. This inhibition was reversed upon neutralization with anti-IFN-γ and anti-IL-17 antibodies, suggesting a critical role for IFN-γ and IL-17 in Mtb clearance . Despite these promising findings, the precise molecular mechanisms with which IFN-γ and IL-17 synergistically enhance Mtb clearance have not been fully elucidated, warranting further investigation in this critical area of TB immunology.
To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the mechanism by which IFN-γ/IL-17 affects Mtb control. Through microarray analysis, we observed that simultaneous treatment with low concentrations of IFN-γ/IL-17 (1 ng/mL) significantly reduced coronin-1A expression compared to IFN-γ (10 ng/mL) alone, which is capable of killing Mtb in vitro. We also analyzed the relationship between coronin-1A and other associated molecules. Although treatment with IL-17 alone did not induce changes in infected macrophages, simultaneous treatment with IFN-γ rapidly inhibited coronin-1A retention through decreased STAT-3 and increased LRG47 expression, strongly promoting phagosome-lysosome fusion and leading to Mtb elimination. Our study elucidates the mechanism by which low doses of IFN-γ and IL-17 control Mtb.

## Results

## Identification of molecules involved in synergistic anti-mycobactericidal responses of IFN-γ and IL-17
We previously demonstrated the synergistic anti-mycobacterial response of IFN-γ and IL-17, which enhanced the co-localization of Mtb-containing phagosome and Lamp1 in macrophages . In the present study, we confirmed this finding, as shown in Supplementary Fig.  . Co-treatment of IFN-γ (1 ng/mL)/IL-17 (1 ng/mL) significantly inhibited intracellular Mtb growth, but IFN-γ or IL-17 alone (1 ng/mL) did not. Mtb growth was inhibited upon IFN-γ treatment but not IL-17 (10 ng/mL). This was more significantly inhibited in the co-treatment (IFN-γ/IL-17; 1 ng/mL) compared with 10 ng/mL of IFN-γ alone (Fig.  ). Confocal microscopy analysis revealed that co-localization of Mtb-containing phagosomes with late endosome marker Rab7 and lysosomal marker Lamp1 under IFN-γ/IL-17 stimulation was significantly increased compared to IFN-γ alone (Fig.  B−D). To confirm that the enhanced phagosome-lysosome fusion observed with IFN-γ/IL-17 treatment operates through a classical phagolysosomal maturation pathway rather than autophagy-mediated bacterial clearance, we examined autophagy markers in treated macrophages. Immunofluorescence analysis showed no significant increase in LC3 puncta formation, and Western blot analysis revealed no elevation of LC3-II levels in IFN-γ/IL-17-treated cells compared to rapamycin-treated positive controls (Supplementary Fig.  ). These findings indicate that the synergistic anti-mycobacterial effect of IFN-γ and IL-17 is mediated through direct enhancement of phagosome-lysosome fusion rather than autophagy-dependent mechanisms.
 Western blot also showed that Lamp1 was accumulated in Mtb-containing phagosomes isolated from IFN-γ/IL-17-treated macrophages, indicating that IFN-γ/IL-17 enhanced phagolysosomal fusion (Fig.  ). These results suggest that IL-17 enhances the phagosome-lysosome pathway of Mtb-killing mediated by IFN-γ.
Next, a microarray analysis was performed to identify the molecules involved in the synergistic pathway of IFN-γ/IL-17. Thirty upregulated and thirty downregulated genes were selected based on the significant differences between the infection alone and the IFN-γ (1 ng/mL) or IFN-γ/IL-17 (1 ng/mL) stimulation (Fig.  ). Among them, we found that socs6 (Kegg_MapID:04630) and coro1a (Kegg_MapID:04145) were significantly downregulated, whereas il11 (Kegg_MapID:04630), idi2 (Kegg_MapID:01100), and magi3 (Kegg_MapID:04015) were markedly upregulated in cells treated with IFN-γ/IL-17 (1 ng/mL) compared to IFN-γ alone (1 ng/mL). coro1a, socs6, idi2, magi3, and il11 exhibited the most prominent differences and were selected for further validation using qRT-PCR (Fig.  ). Expression of coronin-1A, encoded by the coro1a, was confirmed to be significantly decreased under IFN-γ-stimulated condition, which was further significantly decreased when co-treated with IFN-γ/IL-17. However, no significant decrease in the expression of other genes was observed in the qRT-PCR analysis. The role of coronin-1A in the synergistic effect of IFN-γ/IL-17 was investigated in the subsequent experiments.

## IFN-γ/IL-17 inhibits the retention of coronin-1A in the phagosomes
Reportedly, coronin-1A inhibits phagosome-lysosome fusion and is subject to IFN-γ-mediated regulation through phosphorylation , . Confocal microscopy to detect coronin-1A on phagosomes showed the retention of coronin-1A on phagosomes in Mtb-infected or IL-17-treated cells. This was significantly decreased in IFN-γ-treated cells and was completely depleted by IFN-γ/IL-17 co-treatment (Fig.  ). Confocal imaging of the orthogonal sections also showed that coronin-1A retention was synergistically inhibited by IFN-γ/IL-17 treatment (Fig.  ). Furthermore, western blotting showed that coronin-1A was not detected in Mtb-containing phagosomes isolated from IFN-γ/IL-17-treated cells (Fig.  ). However, no significant difference in coronin-1A expression was observed in the cytosol fraction among stimulating conditions, indicating that the IFN-γ/IL-17-induced changes in coronin-1A retention was limited to Mtb-containing phagosomes.
 Next, we performed small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection to investigate whether IFN-γ/IL-17-induced Mtb cell death was dependent on coronin-1A. Transfection of coro1a siRNA into Mtb-infected macrophages led to a significant reduction in coronin-1A expression in whole cells (Fig.  ) and coronin-1A retention in phagosomes irrespective of IFN-γ/IL-17 stimulation (Fig.  ). IFN-γ treatment at concentrations that could not inhibit Mtb growth induced significant Mtb growth inhibition in bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) transfected with coro1a siRNA (Fig.  ). IFN-γ/IL-17-mediated growth inhibition was potentiated, almost reaching a bacterial count of zero, by suppressing coronin-1A expression. Moreover, the co-localization of phagosome-Lamp1 induced by IFN-γ/IL-17 treatment was strongly enhanced in coro1a siRNA-transfected cells (Fig.  ). Taken together, our results suggest that regulation of coronin-1A expression on the phagosome is one of the underlying mechanisms for the synergistic effect of IFN-γ and IL-17 in inhibiting Mtb growth.

## IFN-γ/IL-17-induced LRG47 affects coronin-1A retention and bacterial killing
Next, we investigated the signal pathway related to the synergistic activity of IFN-γ and IL-17 for anti-mycobacterial response. Given that coronin-1A was significantly downregulated by IFN-γ/IL-17 treatment, we focused on the upstream signaling molecules , : STAT1 (the primary IFN-γ-activated transcription factor), LRG47 (an IFN-γ-inducible protein that regulates coronin-1A expression), and STAT3 (which can antagonize STAT1 signaling). The phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT3, along with the expression of LRG47, were assessed. IL-17 stimulation did not induce STAT1 phosphorylation, whereas IFN-γ or IFN-γ/IL-17 stimulation significantly increased STAT1 phosphorylation. Notably, a reduction in STAT3 phosphorylation was observed only in cells stimulated with IFN-γ/IL-17 (Fig.  ). In addition, IFN-γ/IL-17 stimulation led to a prominent increase in LRG47 expression, which is involved in regulating coronin-1A expression . Next, we investigated whether intracellular Mtb growth was affected in the BMDMs transfected with lrg47 siRNA. Western blotting data showed that LRG47 expression was suppressed from 0 to 72 h after transfection (Fig.  ). IFN-γ/IL-17-mediated reduction of coronin-1A retention was restored on the phagosome from Mtb-infected BMDMs transfected with lrg47 siRNA (Fig.  ). Furthermore, LRG47 suppression inhibited Rab7 localization into phagosome (Fig.  ) and significantly inhibited the co-localization of Lamp1 on Mtb phagosome induced by IFN-γ or IFN-γ/IL-17 (Fig.  ). Finally, IFN-γ/IL-17-induced inhibition of Mtb growth was abrogated in BMDMs transfected with lrg47 siRNA (Fig.  ). These results suggest that IFN-γ/IL-17 treatment induces LRG47 expression, which affects coronin-1A retention, thereby eliciting subsequent phagolysosomal fusion and bacterial death.
 

## Involvement of STAT1 and STAT3 in IFN-γ/IL-17-induced anti-mycobacterial response
IFN-γ activates STAT1 and inhibits STAT3 signaling, consistent with our observations when treated with IFN-γ or IFN-γ/IL-17, as shown in Fig.  . Next, we investigated whether STAT1 or STAT3 affected LRG47 expression, coronin-1A retention, and the Mtb-killing mechanism. The expression of LRG47 mediated by IFN-γ or IFN-γ/IL-17 was significantly decreased by STAT1 knockdown, but the STAT3 inhibitor induced a slight increase in the IFN-γ- or IFN-γ/IL-17-induced expression of LRG47; however, significance could not be measured (Fig.  ). Notably, in Mtb-infected cells with STAT1 knockdown, LRG47 expression increased regardless of IL-17 treatment, suggesting that this is due to the release of STAT-1 mediated suppression by Mtb-infection, which is not further altered by IL-17. IFN-γ/IL-17-induced reduction in coronin-1A retention on Mtb-containing phagosomes was restored by STAT1 knockdown, accompanied by a decrease in the co-localization of Rab7 or Lamp1 on phagosomes (Fig.  ). As expected, the STAT3 inhibitor significantly enhanced IFN-γ/IL-17-mediated Mtb growth inhibition, whereas STAT1 suppression led to a significant increase in Mtb growth in all conditions compared to non-transfected cells (Fig.  ).
 

## Effect of IFN-γ/IL-17 co-treatment on Mtb growth in the lung and spleen of mice
We investigated the adjunctive therapeutic effects of IFN-γ and/or IL-17 combination with anti-tuberculosis drugs in a murine model. As illustrated in Fig.  , Mtb-infected mice were treated with a mixture of isoniazid and rifampin (INH + RIF) for 4 wk starting at week 3 post-infection. Concurrently, IFN-γ (10 ng), IL-17 (10 ng), or IFN-γ/IL-17 (1 ng each) was administered intranasally at 3-day intervals for two weeks beginning at week 3 post-infection. Bacterial burden in the lungs and spleen were quantified at 3, 5, 7, and 11 weeks post-infection. At 11 weeks post-infection, the group receiving combined therapy of anti-tuberculosis drugs and IFN-γ/IL-17 (1 ng each) exhibited significantly reduced pulmonary inflammation compared to groups treated with anti-tuberculosis drugs alone or in combination with either IFN-γ (10 ng) or IL-17 (10 ng) (Fig.  B and C). Notably, the group treated with anti-TB drugs and IL-17 (10 ng) showed no difference from the infection control group. The efficacy of the combined therapy was characterized by a more rapid decrease in bacterial burden in mice treated with IFN-γ/IL-17 compared to IFN-γ alone or other conditions and by the lowest bacterial re-growth following cessation of chemotherapy (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). In contrast, the bacterial load in the INH + RIF treatment group began to increase in both tissues after the termination of drug therapy. IL-17 monotherapy showed no adjunctive effects. Although IFN-γ induced a faster reduction in bacterial load compared to chemotherapy alone, a rebound bacterial growth was observed after the cessation of drug treatment. Lung pathology images at 5, 7, and 11 weeks post-infection are shown in Supplementary Fig.  . Furthermore, confocal microscopic analysis of mouse lung tissues at 11 weeks post-infection revealed distinct changes in key molecular markers, including coronin-1A, LRG47, and phosphorylated STAT1. In line with our in vitro findings, combined IFN-γ/IL-17 therapy markedly reduced coronin-1A retention, accompanied by an increase in LRG47 expression and STAT1 phosphorylation. These changes were minimal in the infection control, anti-TB drugs only, and anti-TB drugs + IL-17 groups. Although anti-TB drugs + IFN-γ induced a moderate decrease in coronin-1A retention and increases in LRG47 expression and STAT1 phosphorylation, these effects were markedly more pronounced in the anti-TB drugs + IFN-γ/IL-17 groups (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). These findings suggest that the combination of IFN-γ and IL-17 could serve as an effective adjunctive therapy alongside conventional chemotherapy for TB treatment.
 

## Discussion
A comprehensive analysis of combined cytokine-based TB treatment underscores the complex interplay between host immune responses and bacterial pathogenesis in the context of TB therapy. This study demonstrates that the co-treatments of IFN-γ/IL-17 inhibit coronin-1A retention on the phagosome via STAT1, STAT3, and LRG47 signaling pathways, leading to synergistic anti-mycobacterial responses. Moreover, our results convey novel therapeutic approaches that can potentially enhance TB treatment outcomes, building on the existing knowledge of cytokine-mediated immunity.
In the complex landscape of TB management, the interplay between coronin-1A and LRG47 in promoting phagosome-lysosome fusion represents a fine-tuned layer of immune regulation. Coronin-1A, also known as tryptophan-aspartate-containing coat protein, is a protein encoded by coro1a and is present around the cell membrane. Mtb is internalized into phagosomes via phagocytosis, where coronin-1A surrounds the phagosomes and inhibits lysosomal fusion , . LRG47, also known as IRGM or IFI1 (Immunity-related GTPase family M protein, or interferon-inducible protein 1), is a regulatory protein involved in this process. It has been reported that IFN-γ activates STAT1 signaling , upregulates LRG47 , , decrease the retention of coronin-1A , and promotes phagosome-lysosome fusion .
Our discoveries highlight the paradox that while IFN-γ generally increases LRG47 expression through the STAT1 signaling pathway to promote phagosome-lysosome fusion, excessive IFN-γ levels elevate STAT3 , offsetting the beneficial effects and aiding Mtb survival . These conflicting results indicate that high concentrations of IFN-γ elevate STAT3 levels, inhibiting STAT1, suggesting that low concentrations of IFN-γ are insufficient for Mtb elimination, while high concentrations may activate signaling pathways that promote bacterial proliferation. This implies that precise cytokine regulation is crucial in TB treatment, with optimal dosing and timing being essential for harnessing the therapeutic benefits of cytokines without promoting bacterial resistance or survival.
Similarly, the role of coronin-1A as a target for intracellular Mtb eradication highlights an important area of potential therapeutic intervention. A study first identified in 2012 emphasized the structural regulation of coronin-1A by IFN-γ and its role in inhibiting autophagosome formation . However, the lack of follow-up studies raises important questions regarding the depth and scope of coronin-1A as a potential therapeutic target. We found that reducing the expression of coronin-1A in Mtb-infected macrophages resulted in co-localization with Lamp1, which was not statistically significant; however, it appeared to show a tendency toward increased induction of phagolysosomal fusion. Furthermore, although a reduction in coronin-1A expression did not result in a statistically significant decrease in bacterial growth in Mtb-infected macrophages during Mtb infection or IL-17 stimulation, there was an observable pattern of reduced Mtb growth. This suggests that coronin-1A substantially impacts phagosome-lysosome fusion, which is a crucial mechanism in the elimination of Mtb.
The functional importance of the LRG47-coronin-1A axis was further demonstrated in our knockdown experiments. Even with a moderate 30% reduction in LRG47 expression, the bactericidal effects of IFN-γ/IL-17 treatment were completely abolished, with CFU levels returning to infection-only controls rather than the typical reduction below initial levels observed with normal cytokine treatment. This was accompanied by restoration of coronin-1A retention and loss of phagolysosomal fusion markers, indicating that the entire antimicrobial pathway was disrupted.
While recent studies have shown that complete Irgm1 deficiency can be overcome through alternative immune interventions such as adoptive T cell transfer , our findings highlight the pathway-specific requirements for cytokine-mediated antimicrobial responses. Rather than indicating universal dispensability, these observations emphasize that different therapeutic approaches may rely on distinct molecular mechanisms with varying dependencies on specific immune factors. The high sensitivity of the IFN-γ/IL-17 pathway to LRG47 levels underscores its critical role as a non-redundant mediator in this particular therapeutic context. Further investigation is needed to elucidate the mechanisms by which coronin-1A enhances host defense against Mtb, potentially aligning with and expanding the strategic use of cytokine therapy against TB.
Our investigation into the interaction between IL-17 and IFN-γ within the domain of combinatorial immunology to amplify antimicrobial activity highlights complex, interlinked regulatory mechanisms that promote antimicrobial activity. The regulation of coronin-1A and LRG47 by IL-17/IFN-γ emphasizes the intricate and combined regulatory mechanisms that promote antimicrobial activity. Specifically, the regulation of tightly orchestrated STAT1 and STAT3 signaling through IFN-γ/IL-17 combination therapy underscores the potential of combinatorial immune modulation for TB treatment. Additionally, the role of IL-17 in recruiting immune cells during early infection suggests its utility as an early defense mechanism – despite its unclear direct effects on Mtb. The synergistic effects of IFN-γ and IL-17, particularly in relation to RpfE and HSP90-ESAT6 experiments, are compelling , . The observation that neutralizing cytokines restore bacterial growth further reinforces the crucial role of cytokines in controlling infection. Our findings emphasize the importance of targeted cytokine therapy that leverages the synergistic effects of IFN-γ and IL-17 to enhance phagosome-lysosome fusion for combating infection .
IFN-γ has long been recognized as a key cytokine in the immune response to TB . Clinical trials evaluating IFN-γ as an adjunctive therapy have shown significant benefits, particularly in MDR-TB , , . Meta-analyses support the effectiveness of IFN-γ in improving TB treatment outcomes . The various routes of IFN-γ administration (nebulized, aerosolized, subcutaneous, or intramuscular) highlight its versatility and potential for tailored therapeutic approaches . By contrast, the role of IL-2 in TB treatment appears to be more nuanced. Although some studies have reported positive outcomes, particularly in patients with MDR-TB, others have shown limited effects on bacterial clearance or symptom improvement . An ongoing large-scale clinical trial in China will provide valuable insights into the utility of IL-2 as an adjunct therapy for MDR-TB . Similarly, Granulocyte-Macrophage Colony-Stimulating Factor (GM-CSF) shows promise as an immunomodulator in TB treatment, with phase II clinical trial results indicating improved safety and accelerated bacterial clearance , . Despite significant progress in the treatment landscape, the disease remains a global health challenge. In this context, cytokine therapy, particularly including appropriate doses of two cytokines, IFN-γ and IL-17, has the potential to modulate immune responses at the site of infection. Strategic positioning of T cells in the lungs is crucial for macrophage activation, which is a critical step in the fight against Mtb infections. However, excessive accumulation of immune cells can create an unfavorable pathological environment, inhibit cytokine secretion and macrophage activity, and fail to induce Mtb clearance.
Our findings demonstrated that cytokine levels markedly decreased without periodic re-stimulation, suggesting the need for a nuanced approach to cytokine administration for therapeutic efficacy , . We conducted experiments using concentrations of IFN-γ and IL-17 sufficient to activate infected macrophages without causing excessive immune cell accumulation, aiming to reduce treatment duration. This approach emphasizes the delicate balance of cytokine therapy required to effectively control infections without exacerbating the lung pathology.
In vaccine experiments, studies conducted before and after infection without re-stimulation with antigens have shown markedly low cytokine levels , . Against this background, we conducted experiments to shorten the treatment period by administering IFN-γ/IL-17 at concentrations sufficient to activate infected macrophages without causing excessive immune cell accumulation. To demonstrate these experimental results, we treated the mice for only 4 weeks and confirmed that when only INH + RIF, an anti-TB drug, was administered, the lung burden of Mtb was reduced by approximately half. Similarly, when anti-TB drugs and IL-17 were administered, their efficacy was similar to that of the anti-TB drugs alone. When anti-TB drugs were co-administered with IFN-γ, the lung burden of Mtb was significantly reduced compared to the INH + RIF group, but the lung burden of Mtb increased 4 weeks after the termination of drug treatment (11 weeks after infection). However, when anti-TB drugs were co-administered with IFN-γ/IL-17, the lung burden of Mtb rapidly decreased from 5 weeks after infection, and this trend was maintained until 11 weeks after infection. This suggests that co-administration of low concentrations of IFN-γ/IL-17 cytokines and anti-TB drugs can dramatically shorten the TB treatment period.
To maximize the therapeutic potential of cytokine therapy, we administered cytokines intranasally, directly exposing the lungs to therapeutic agents. This approach demonstrates the effectiveness of local cytokine delivery, overcoming the short half-life of cytokines and minimizing systemic side effects, resulting in normalized pathology and a significantly reduced bacterial burden – . Our cytokine therapy strategy also focused on precise T-cell localization. Macrophage activation through vaccination or treatment is essential, and T-cells that activate infected macrophages must be present at the infection site, primarily in the lungs, to provide rapid, potent, and durable protection , . However, excessive accumulation of immune cells in the lungs can lead to adverse pathological outcomes and hinder an effective immune responses . Conversely, insufficient cytokine secretion impairs Mtb elimination and macrophage activity, thus preventing bacterial killing . This highlights the necessity of achieving a finely tuned immune response that is sufficiently robust to eliminate Mtb without causing excessive tissue damage during the treatment.
While our study successfully validated the STAT1-LRG47-coronin-1A pathway through in vivo immunofluorescence analysis, several areas warrant further investigation. Although we demonstrated that T cell positioning in the lung contributes to macrophage activation, we did not comprehensively analyze the impact of our cytokine therapy on lung T cell responses. A detailed characterization of T cell activation markers, spatial localization patterns, and cytokine production profiles in treated mice would provide valuable insights into the broader immunomodulatory effects of low-dose IFN-γ/IL-17 combination therapy. Understanding these T cell dynamics is crucial for optimizing dosing regimens and predicting therapeutic outcomes in clinical settings. Future studies should also examine the long-term effects of intranasal cytokine delivery on pulmonary immune memory and protection against reinfection. These comprehensive immune cell profiling studies, combined with our validated molecular mechanisms, represent critical next steps for advancing this therapeutic approach toward clinical application.
While our findings demonstrate the therapeutic potential of low-dose IFN-γ/IL-17 combination therapy, several translational challenges must be addressed before clinical implementation. Our intranasal delivery approach may require optimization for human respiratory anatomy and pathophysiology. Alternative delivery systems such as nebulization or sustained-release formulations could enhance therapeutic efficiency while maintaining the advantages of local pulmonary delivery . Given the pleiotropic nature of cytokines, comprehensive safety profiles must be established through preclinical toxicology studies, with particular attention to potential autoimmune reactions and long-term effects , . Despite these challenges, the demonstrated efficacy of low-dose cytokine therapy in our preclinical model supports the development of carefully designed clinical trials to evaluate this promising adjunctive therapeutic approach.
In conclusion, our findings suggest that IFN-γ-mediated inhibition of coronin-1A retention on the phagosome in Mtb-infected macrophages is enhanced by IL-17 co-treatment, resulting in synergistic mycobactericidal activity. It is crucial to remember that cytokines are pleiotropic proteins that can be toxic at high doses, necessitating precise administration to provide therapeutic benefits with minimal harm. Our study deepens the understanding of immune responses to Mtb infection and highlights the potential of cytokine-based therapies for TB treatment. This highlights the potential of cytokine-based adjunctive therapies in TB treatment at minimal concentrations, as demonstrated in our mouse experiments, although more sophisticated and tailored approaches are required. This approach could offer new hope for difficult-to-treat infections, such as multidrug-resistant TB, ultimately contributing to reducing the global burden of TB.

## Methods

## Sex as a biological variable
Our study exclusively examined female C57BL/6 mice based on their demonstrated stability in immunological responses to TB infection. While sex differences exist in TB susceptibility, the fundamental mechanisms of IFN-γ/IL-17-mediated antimycobacterial responses are expected to be relevant for both sexes.

## Mice
Six-to-seven-week-old specific pathogen-free female C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from Japan SLC Inc. (Shizuoka, Japan). All the mouse groups were maintained under barrier conditions in the ABSL-3 facility at the Medical Research Center of Chungnam National University (Daejeon, Korea) at a constant temperature (24 °C ± 1 °C) and humidity (50% ±5%). The animals were fed a regular sterile diet and had ad libitum access to water under standardized light-controlled conditions (12-h light–dark intervals).

## Cell culture
Murine BMDMs were cultured and prepared as previously previous . Briefly, BMDMs were cultured for 6 days in Dulbecco’s modified eagle’s medium (Welgene) containing 10% FBS, 50 ng/mL mouse macrophage colony stimulating factor (M-CSF) (R&D System, Minneapolis, MN, USA), and 100 unit/mL of penicillin/100 μg/mL of streptomycin. Cultured cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

## Bacterial culture
Mtb H37Rv (ATCC 27294) was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). The mycobacteria were grown in 7H9 medium supplemented with 0.5% glycerol, amphotericin B, 0.05% Tween-80 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and 10% oleic acid-albumin-dextrose-catalase (OADC; Sigma-Aldrich) cultivation media, harvested and aliquoted into cryovial tube, and frozen at –80 °C for further use. Additionally, the red fluorescent protein (RFP) expressing Mtb (RFP-H37Rv) was constructed in this study as previously described .

## Mycobacterial inhibition assay
BMDMs were infected with Mtb at a multiplicity of infection of 1:1 (bacteria: BMDMs) for 4 h. The infected BMDMs were treated with amikacin (200 μg/mL) for 2 h and then washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cells were stimulated with either PBS, IFN-γ (1 or 10 ng/mL), IL-17 (1 or 10 ng/mL), or IFN-γ/IL-17 (1 ng/mL). Three days later, the cells were lysed and intracellular infection was quantified by plating on 7H10 Middlebrook agar plates supplemented with OADC (Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The list of reagents used in this experiment is described in Supplementary Table  .

## Immunofluorescence
BMDMs were seeded onto 18-mm diameter round glass coverslips in a 12-well cell culture plate. The cells were then infected with RFP-Mtb at a multiplicity of infection of 1:1 (bacteria to BMDMs) for 4 h and washed with PBS. The cells were stimulated with PBS, IFN-γ (1 ng/mL), IL-17 (1 ng/mL), or IFN-γ/IL-17 (1 ng/mL) for indicated times. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, incubated with primary antibodies for 24 h at 4 °C, and then reacted with secondary antibodies at room temperature. 4′,6-Diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI) was used to stain the nuclei. Immunofluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser-scanning microscope (TCS SP8; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) with consistent excitation, emission, pinholes, exposure time parameters, and 3-dimensional analysis. The list of antibodies used in this experiment is described in Supplementary Table  .

## Immunoblotting
The infected, activated, inhibited, and transfected BMDMs were collected and lysed using a lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 137 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 10% glycerol, 1 mM PMSF, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1 mM NaF, a proteinase inhibitor cocktail tablet (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), and a protease inhibitor cocktail tablet (Roche). The lysates were resolved on sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gels and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). For proteins with different expression levels, separate blots were performed using optimized protein loading amounts for each target to prevent signal oversaturation while ensuring adequate detection sensitivity. The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA, incubated with primary antibodies for 24 h at 4 °C, and then incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature. Immunoblots were incubated with chemiluminescent substrates for horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), and the target protein was detected using the BioRad ChemiDoc imaging system. The list of antibodies used in this experiment is described in Supplementary Table  .

## Microarray analysis
BMDMs were infected with Mtb at a multiplicity of infection of 1:1 (bacteria: BMDMs) for 4 h. The infected BMDMs were treated with amikacin (200 μg/mL) for 2 h and then washed twice with PBS. Cultures were supplemented with either PBS, IFN-γ (1 ng/mL), IL-17 (1 ng/mL), or IFN-γ/IL-17 (1 ng/mL) for 24 h. Total RNA was isolated using Trizol reagent. RNA quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with an RNA 6000 Nano Chip (Agilent Technologies, Amstelveen, Netherlands), and RNA quantification was performed using an ND-2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). RNA amplification, labeling, array hybridization, scanning, and extraction of raw data were performed by Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, Korea). Microarray experiments were repeated thrice for each sample. An Affymetrix Clariom-S Array (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) from Agilent was used in this study. Selected gene signal values were logarithmically transformed and normalized using the quantile method. The heatmap was analyzed using the Morpheus software (https://software.broadinstitute.org/morpheus/) provided by Macrogen Inc. Raw Microarray data have been deposited in the ArrayExpress database under accession number E-MTAB-16005.

## Quantitative reverse-transcriptase polymerase chain reaction
BMDMs were infected with Mtb at a multiplicity of infection of 1:1 (bacteria to BMDMs) for 4 h and washed with PBS. The cells were stimulated with PBS, IFN-γ (1 ng/mL), IL-17 (1 ng/mL), or IFN-γ/IL-17 (1 ng/mL) for 4 h. Total RNA was isolated from BMDMs and mRNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using TRIzol reagent and Reverse Transcriptase Premix (ELPIS-BIOTECH, Daejeon, South Korea), respectively, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse-transcription PCR was performed using AccuPower® HotStart PCR PreMix (BIONEER, Daejeon, South Korea) to detect the mRNA levels of target genes. Synthesized cDNA and target gene (Supplementary Table  ) expression was quantified using SYBR green master mix (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands) in a CFX96 Real-time System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) to perform reverse transcription PCR. Data were analyzed using the 2ΔΔ threshold cycle (Ct) method, with beta-actin serving as the normalization control. Data are expressed as relative changes.

## Mtb-containing phagosome isolation
Mtb-containing phagosomes were prepared as previously described . Briefly, Mtb H37Rv was coated with NHS-biotin (10 mg/mL in DMSO) for 2 h at room temperature with constant shaking, followed by washing three times with sterile PBS. The biotin-coated bacteria were then incubated with streptavidin-conjugated magnetic beads (10 mg/mL) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing to remove unbound beads, coated bacteria were used to infect BMDMs at an MOI of 1:1 for 4 h. Following infection, cells were washed with PBS and incubated in complete medium containing amikacin (200 μg/mL) to kill extracellular bacteria. At designated time points, infected cells were lysed in phagosome isolation buffer (50 mM PIPES pH 7.0, 50 mM MgCl₂, 5 mM EGTA, 1 mM DTT, 10 μM cytochalasin B, with protease and phosphatase inhibitors), passed through a 26 G needle 15 times, and Mtb-containing phagosomes were isolated using a magnetic rack. Isolated phagosomes were washed three times with PBS and resuspended in appropriate buffer for subsequent analysis.

## Gene silencing by siRNA
Silencing of coronin-1A, LRG47, or STAT1 was achieved using siRNA (25 nM) for mouse coro1a, lrg47, or stat1 mRNA target sequences, and negative control siRNAs (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). siRNA oligonucleotides were transfected into cultured BMDMs using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. At 4 h post-transfection, the cells were cultured in fresh complete medium for 18 h, infected/activated, and bacterial growth was assessed by immunofluorescence and immunoblotting analyses.

## Evaluation of the adjunctive therapy protocol efficacy

## Challenge
Six weeks after the last subcutaneous vaccination, the C57BL/6 mice in each group were challenged with the H37Rv strain of Mtb via intratracheal injection. Briefly, following anesthetization with 1.2% 2,2,2-tribromoethanol (Avertin; Sigma-Aldrich), each mouse received 50 μL of 1 × 103 colony forming unit (CFU) Mtb H37Rv strain intratracheally via the small ventral midline cervical incision exposed in the tracheal cartilage rings caudal to the larynx. Immediately after the challenge, the open skin was sutured to prevent infection.

## Treatment
To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of IFN-γ and/or IL-17 after cytokine therapy and chemotherapy in Mtb-infected animals, mice were randomized into groups (5 animals per group). The control group received 10 μL PBS, whereas the treatment groups received IFN-γ (10 ng), IL-17 (10 ng), or IFN-γ/IL-17 (each 1 ng) per animal in a total volume of 10 μL. In the ‘short duration’ chemotherapy experiment, after 3 weeks of infection, the mice were given isoniazid (INH, 0.1 g/L) and rifampin (RIF, 0.1 g/L) in their drinking water ad libitum for 4 weeks . These mice were sacrificed 3, 5, 7, or 11 weeks later and their lungs were isolated for CFU and histopathological analyses.

## Bacterial enumeration
The mice were euthanized with CO2 and the lungs were homogenized. The number of viable bacteria was determined by plating serial dilutions of organ homogenates on Middlebrook 7H10 agar (Difco, Becton Dickson) supplemented with 10% OADC (Difco, Becton Dickson) and amphotericin B (Sigma-Aldrich). Colonies were counted after 4 weeks of incubation at 37 °C.

## Histopathology
To analyze histological changes, lung tissues were fixed in 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin blocks. Lung sections were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Paraffin sections were deparaffinized and endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched using 3% hydrogen peroxide. The level of inflammation in the lungs was evaluated using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

## Immunofluorescence staining of lung tissue
For the immunostaining of in vivo paraffin sections, mouse lung tissue sections harvested, fixed with 10% formalin and embedded in paraffin blocks. Paraffin sections were cut and immunostained with primary antibodies, followed by fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies. DAPI was used to stained the nuclei. Immunofluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser-scanning microscope (LSM 900 with airyscan 2; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) with consistent excitation, emission, pinhole, and exposure time parameters across all groups. The list of antibodies used in this experiment is described in Supplementary Table  .

## Statistics and reproducibility
Data for all experiments are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For CFU and histopathology analyses, the Mann–Whitney rank test was used to compare the differences between the two groups. GraphPad Prism version 8.0 for Windows was used for the statistical analyses (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA; www.graphpad.com). Differences with *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001 were considered significant. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times with similar results.
