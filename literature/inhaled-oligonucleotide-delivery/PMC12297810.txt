## Abstract
Brain injury following cardiac arrest (CA) is a significant cause of mortality and poor prognosis in patients, and effective treatment strategies remain limited. Stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs), a novel cell-free therapeutic approach, have recently demonstrated significant potential in the field of brain injury repair. EVs, key mediators of stem cell paracrine and autocrine signaling, are enriched with bioactive molecules such as non-coding RNAs and proteins. These EVs have the capacity to traverse the bloodstream, reach injury sites, and modulate various biological processes, including neuronal survival, oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, blood-brain barrier integrity, and neurovascular regeneration.This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the research history, structural characteristics, and in vivo distribution and metabolism of stem cell-derived EVs. The review further explores their therapeutic potential and underlying mechanisms in post-CA brain injury, including the inhibition of neuronal apoptosis, alleviation of oxidative stress and inflammation, promotion of blood-brain barrier repair, and enhancement of neurovascular regeneration. Additionally, the review highlights emerging directions and challenges in the clinical application of stem cell-derived EVs, offering theoretical insights and perspectives for future research and translational development. The potential of stem cell-derived EVs as a breakthrough strategy for treating post-CA brain injury is underscored, offering renewed optimism for enhancing patient outcomes.
Keywords: Stem cells, Extracellular vesicles, Cardiac arrest, Brain injury

## Introduction
Cardiac arrest (CA), which is characterized by the abrupt cessation of normal cardiac activity and blood perfusion to the organs, has a high mortality and disability rate. Globally, the average discharge survival rate for in-hospital CA patients is approximately 30% [ ], while this for out-of-hospital CA patients is less than 9% [ , ]. Subsequent to CA and resuscitation, ischemia and reperfusion injury impact systemic organs, with brain injury representing a primary cause of mortality and long-term disability. A variety of measures, including mild hypothermia treatment, have been employed with the aim of reducing secondary damage to the central nervous system in CA patients [ ]. Nevertheless, the long-term prognosis remains unchanged [ , ]. Advancements in stem cell research, particularly in extracellular vesicles (EVs), offer new potential treatment strategies for brain injury post-CA. This review provides an overview of the potential applications and mechanisms of stem cell-derived EVs in the context of brain injury following CA.

## Overview of EVs

## History of EVs study
The study of EVs can be traced back to the 1940s, when Chargaff and West identified small particles in blood with coagulant properties during their research on blood clotting mechanisms [ ]. In the 1980s, Harding, Pan, and colleagues observed that reticulocytes release transferrin receptors via vesicles during maturation [ , ]. They proposed that EVs serve as a pathway for disposing of obsolete membrane proteins, a view that has long been accepted in the field [ ]. In 1996, Raposo et al. demonstrated that EVs secreted by B lymphocytes play a role in antigen presentation, revealing their biological activity and suggesting their potential in cancer immunotherapy [ ]. Since then, the significance of EVs as intercellular communication tools has been increasingly recognized, not only in normal physiological processes such as immune regulation [ ] but also in the pathogenesis of diseases like cancer [ ], metabolic disorders [ ], and infectious diseases [ ]. These findings have laid a theoretical foundation for developing novel therapeutic strategies.

## Biogenesis of EVs
The International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) defines EVs as “particles that are released from cells, are delimited by a lipid bilayer, and cannot replicate on their own (i.e., do not contain a functional nucleus)” [ ]. EVs are characterized by a single-layer membrane structure, typically round or spherical in shape, and contain a diverse array of biomolecules including proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and metabolites (Fig.  B). Based on their biogenesis pathways, EVs are broadly classified into two major categories: exosomes (Exo) and ectosomes (or microvesicles) [ ].
 Exosome biogenesis is a highly complex and dynamic process that involves two membrane invagination events and the maturation of multivesicular bodies (MVBs). This process initiates with the formation of early sorting endosomes (ESEs) through plasma membrane endocytosis, followed by extensive cargo exchange and signaling interactions between ESEs and organelles such as the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, and mitochondria. As the endosomal system matures, ESEs undergo further membrane invagination to form late sorting endosomes (LSEs), which eventually develop into MVBs containing numerous intraluminal vesicles (ILVs). These MVBs can follow one of three potential fates: fusion with autophagosomes for lysosomal degradation, direct fusion with lysosomes for content degradation, or fusion with the plasma membrane to release ILVs as exosomes into the extracellular space (Fig.  A). In contrast, ectosome biogenesis is a more direct process mediated by outward budding of the plasma membrane, independent of the endosomal system. This fundamental difference contributes to marked heterogeneity in size and composition between the two vesicle types. Exosomes typically measure 40–160 nm in diameter, whereas ectosomes (also termed microvesicles) exhibit a broader size distribution of 50–1000 nm [ ]. Exosomal membranes are enriched in sphingomyelin, cholesterol, and tetraspanins (e.g., CD9, CD63, CD81), while their luminal cargo contains hallmark proteins such as TSG101, Alix, HSP70, and HSP90 [ ]. The additional sorting steps during exosome formation also selectively enrich non-coding RNAs (Fig.  B). In contrast, ectosomes exhibit membrane and cargo compositions that more closely resemble those of the parent cell. They are enriched in proteins associated with mitochondria, the endoplasmic reticulum, and the cytoskeleton, and can even encapsulate functional organelles such as mitochondria [ , ].

## Isolation of EVs
EVs can be isolated from a variety of sources, including cell culture supernatants, various bodily fluids such as blood, urine, and cerebrospinal fluid, as well as solid tissues. Current methods for EV isolation predominantly utilize non-selective strategies designed to obtain intact populations of EVs. Commonly employed techniques for isolation include ultracentrifugation, density gradient centrifugation, ultrafiltration, immunoaffinity capture, polymer-based precipitation, and size-exclusion chromatography.
Extensive comparative studies have demonstrated that each method possesses distinct characteristics. Ultracentrifugation offers relatively high recovery rates; however, it may compromise vesicle integrity. Ultrafiltration is operationally straightforward but carries risks of membrane clogging. Immunoaffinity provides strong specificity, yet it may overlook certain subpopulations. Polymer-based precipitation achieves high yields but often results in increased impurities. In contrast, size-exclusion chromatography better preserves vesicle activity, albeit at the cost of sample dilution. Notably, existing technologies universally confront the challenge of balancing yield with purity [ – ]. Table  summarizes the features and applications of EVs obtained through various isolation techniques.
 Considering methodological stability and reproducibility, ultracentrifugation remains the gold standard. In practical research, investigators should flexibly select isolation methods based on specific experimental requirements, and may employ combined technical approaches when necessary to achieve optimal isolation outcomes.

## Distribution of EVs
The distribution of EVs in the body is contingent upon the method of administration. Common methods include intravenous injection, intraperitoneal injection, etc., with intravenous injection being the most frequently used. Upon intravenous injection, EVs initially exhibit a rapid decline in the bloodstream, with a half-life of approximately 10 to 30 min, indicating a rapid distribution in tissues and organs [ , ]. Subsequently, a gradual decline ensues, primarily through excretion via the urinary system, with a duration of approximately six hours [ ]. Under physiological conditions, the intravenous administration of EVs predominantly results in accumulation within the liver and spleen, with subsequent distribution to the lungs. The distribution concentrations in vital organs such as the kidneys, gastrointestinal tract, heart, and brain are significantly lower [ ]. In pathological conditions, the distribution pattern of these particles undergoes significant changes, characterized by specific enrichment at the site of the lesion. (Fig.  )Grange et al. discovered that intravenously administered mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs (MSC-EVs) could accumulate to a significant extent in the damaged kidney region within 15 min in an acute kidney injury (AKI) model. In contrast, the same dose of EVs in healthy mice required 5 h to detect a weak signal in the kidneys [ ]. Similarly, Tieu et al. demonstrated that intravenously administered MSC-EVs exhibited obvious lung targeting in an acute lung injury (ALI) model. The fluorescence signal intensity of EVs in the lungs of ALI mice reached three times that of the spleen and liver 48 h after administration, while EVs in healthy mice was primarily distributed in the liver and spleen, with significantly reduced accumulation in the lungs [ ]. These findings demonstrate that EVs displaying an active homing tendency toward diseased tissues under pathological conditions.
 After intraperitoneal injection, EVs are primarily distributed to the gastrointestinal tract and absorbed by intra-abdominal lymph nodes [ ]. Intratracheal administration results in a predominantly pulmonary distribution, whereas intranasal administration promotes EVs distribution to the brain, with minor amounts also reaching the lungs, kidneys, and intestines [ ]. Despite variations in EVs sources, sizes, and tracking methods, the distribution pattern of EVs remains largely consistent, aside from differences in signal strength and decay time across different tissues and organs.

## Uptake of EVs
EVs interact with target cells and deliver their contents through a variety of mechanisms. Transmembrane ligands expressed on the surface of EVs have been shown to directly bind to membrane receptors of target cells, thereby activating downstream signaling pathways. This pathway has been implicated in the mediation of immune regulation and apoptosis [ ]. In the context of cargo delivery, EVs predominantly facilitate the intracellular transfer of their contents through two mechanisms. The membrane fusion is defined as the direct fusion of the EV membrane with the target cell plasma membrane, resulting in the release of the EV contents into the cytoplasm. The endocytosis is characterized by the internalization of intact EVs into endosomal compartments, followed by sorting and release of their contents [ , ].
Endocytosis represents the predominant pathway for the internalisation of EVs, encompassing clathrin-mediated endocytosis, phagocytosis, macropinocytosis, and lipid raft-mediated endocytosis [ ]. The process of clathrin-mediated endocytosis is initiated by the assembly of clathrin in non-raft regions of the plasma membrane, resulting in the formation of clathrin-coated pits. Subsequently, dynamin facilitates the scission of the membrane neck, thereby forming endocytic vesicles that subsequently fuse with endosomes [ ]. This pathway represents the most extensively studied EV endocytic mechanism, with confirmation in various cell types, including pancreatic cancer cells [ ], endothelial cells [ ], cardiomyocytes [ ], neurons [ ], and even plant cells [ ]. Lipid raft mediated endocytosis is dependent on lipid raft regions rich in cholesterol and sphingomyelin on the cell membrane. The enrichment of specific protein molecules, such as caveolin and flotillin stabilizes the endocytic vesicles formed in lipid rafts, thereby mediating caveolin-mediated endocytosis and flotillin-dependent endocytosis, respectively [ ]. Phagocytosis is primarily observed in cells with specialized phagocytic functions, including microglia, macrophages, and dendritic cells. These cells have the capacity to recognize “eat me” signals expressed on the surface of particles, extend pseudopodia to wrap the particles, and form phagosomes [ ]. While this pathway is predominantly targeted at large particles, such as pathogens and apoptotic cells, it can also effectively mediate the internalisation of EVs [ ]. Macropinocytosis is initiated by the stimulation of growth factors, leading to the formation of sheet-like pseudopodia on the cell membrane and subsequent invagination. This process results in internalisation of extracellular fluid and soluble macromolecules, forming macropinosomes with a diameter ranging from approximately 0.2 to 5 μm. This process does not depend on the recognition of specific receptors and has significant non-specific characteristics [ ].
Academic controversy persists regarding the primary mechanisms of uptake of EVs. Inhibiting a single endocytic pathway frequently fails to completely block the cellular uptake of EVs. During the uptake of endothelial progenitor cell-derived EVs by astrocytes, multiple mechanisms are involved, including macropinocytosis, clathrin-dependent and caveolin-dependent endocytosis. This phenomenon suggests that specific EV subpopulations may enter target cells through multiple parallel pathways [ ]. Costa et al. proposed that clathrin-independent endocytosis and macropinocytosis might be the predominant mechanisms for EVs to enter cells [ ]. The uptake of brain-derived EVs by glial cells is primarily accomplished through phagocytosis and macropinocytosis. Surprisingly, the inhibition of the lipid raft-mediated endocytic pathway resulted in the uptake of EVs by cells achieving a more rapid uptake through enhanced macropinocytosis [ ]. This finding reveals that cells have the rapid response ability to dynamically adjust their EV uptake strategies in response to microenvironmental changes.

## Stem cell-derived EVs
Stem cells are distinguished by their capacity for self-regeneration and differentiation into multiple cell types, rendering them a valuable resource for tissue regeneration and repair. This potential offers new avenues for addressing certain intractable diseases. Nonetheless, ethical concerns and the potential risk of tumor formation impedes the extensive implementation of stem cell transplantation in clinical practice [ ].
The therapeutic mechanisms of stem cells can be attributed to three primary aspects: differentiation to replace damaged cells [ ], restoration of cell function through cell fusion [ ], and the secretion of bioactive factors and EVs. Since the 1990s, the paracrine effects of stem cells have been extensively studied. A wide range of cytokines (e.g. IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α), chemokines (e.g. monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, MCP-1) and growth factors (e.g. IGF-1, VEGF) are secreted by stem cells, and these factors play crucial roles in immune modulation, tissue repair and angiogenesis [ ]. Additionally, stem cells are able to maintain their undifferentiated state and proliferative capacity through autocrine signaling, thereby enhancing their protective effects [ , ]. Studies have shown that the therapeutic benefits of stem cells are primarily attributed to paracrine factors and EVs [ ].
Preclinical studies have repeatedly demonstrated the superior safety profile of mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs). Adamiak et al. revealed that while both induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and their derived extracellular vesicles (iPSC-EVs) showed significant myocardial repair effects in a mouse model of myocardial infarction (MI), the iPSC injection group exhibited a teratoma incidence exceeding 50% within 6 weeks, whereas no tumor formation was observed in the iPSC-EVs treatment group [ ]. Costa-Ferro et al. confirmed that repeated intravenous and intraperitoneal administration of umbilical cord MSC-EVs (UCMSC-EVs) over a 6-week treatment course maintained all haematological parameters and hepatic/renal function markers within normal physiological ranges without statistically significant alterations [ ]. Extended safety evaluations demonstrated that subcutaneous injection of human embryonic stem cell-derived EVs (hESC-Exos) in nude mice for up to 40 weeks induced neither tumorigenesis nor pathological abnormalities at the injection sites upon histological examination [ ]. These collective findings provide substantial experimental evidence for the long-term safety of MSC-EVs, thus positioning MSC-EVs as a promising alternative to conventional stem cell therapies.
The functions of EVs derived from other cells have distinct characteristics. For instance, EVs derived from immune cells have a greater tendency to focus on their immunomodulatory effects, while EVs derived from neural cells are primarily utilized for the diagnosis and treatment of neurological diseases. Comparing EVs of different cell origins, stem cell-derived EVs possess a distinctive advantage in the domain of injury repair, which is primarily attributed to the self-renewal and differentiation potential inherent to stem cells. EVs contain active substances associated with stem cell self-renewal, which contribute to the functional restoration of various endogenous cells [ ]. Furthermore, stem cell-derived EVs have a longer residence in the target area and have the potential to promote cell differentiation, thereby replenishing cells depleted after injury and exerting their therapeutic effects [ ]. A growing body of evidence suggests that stem cell-derived EVs may offer a promising avenue for the treatment of a diverse array of diseases, including tissue damage [ ], tumors [ ], infections [ ], inflammatory diseases [ ], degenerative diseases [ ], etc.

## Mechanisms of brain injury after CA
The whole brain is susceptible to ischemia and hypoxia during CA, with neurons in regions with elevated metabolic activity, such as the hippocampus, cortex, striatum, and thalamus, being particularly vulnerable to damage [ ]. The “double-hit” model is a widely utilized framework for elucidating the pathophysiological alterations during CA [ ]. The initial injury occurs when tissue lacks blood perfusion during CA. At the cellular level, ischemia and hypoxia impede aerobic metabolism, leading to the reduction in adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels and dysfunction of the energy-dependent Na+/K+ pump. This results in the influx of water and sodium ions into cells, which in turn gives rise to toxic edema. Simultaneously, cell membrane depolarization triggers the opening of voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels, and the release of the excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate. These cascading events result in a release of calcium stores from the endoplasmic reticulum, and an intracellular calcium overload, which activats calcium-dependent proteases and lipases and damages cell structures. Furthermore, hypoxia induces anaerobic metabolism in cells, generating lactic acid and H⁺, which cause intracellular acidosis (Fig.  A). This exacerbates cellular dysfunction and ultimately culminates in cell death [ , ].
 The secondary injury occurs during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and after the restoration of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) in a state of hypoperfusion and reperfusion [ ]. Ca2+ enter the mitochondria, disrupting the electron transport chain. Upon reperfusion, electrons prematurely leak into O2, producing reactive oxygen species (ROS) that further exacerbate mitochondrial damage, and initiate programmed cell death [ ]. In addition, the immune system is activated, and activated microglia release pro-inflammatory mediators and chemokines, which recruit leukocytes in the blood to migrate to the brain tissue (Fig.  B). The inflammatory response serves to exacerbate the destruction of the blood-brain barrier, resulting in vasogenic cerebral edema and elevated intracranial pressure [ ]. Post-resuscitation complications such as seizures, hyperglycemia, and hyperthermia can increase the cerebral metabolic demands, further exacerbating secondary brain damage [ ].

## Biological functions of EVs in brain injury
Since 2010, the biological functions of EVs in brain injury have gradually become a research hotspot. As crucial mediators of intercellular communication, EVs function as a double-edged sword, with their composition and functionality heavily influenced by the physiological state of the source cells. In rats exposed to a hyperoxic environment, the cargo content of EVs in plasma increases, including surfactant protein C and the active form of Gasdermin D, changes that are closely associated with the occurrence of brain injury in neonatal rats [ ]. Similarly, in obese rats, endothelial cell-derived EVs are enriched with pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and IL-6, triggering an inflammatory cascade in astrocytes and exacerbating the extent of ischemic brain injury [ ]. Microparticles (MPs) released by microglia in response to traumatic brain injury (TBI) have been observed to contain pro-inflammatory mediators, including IL-1β and miR-155 [ ]. These have been shown to activate microglia in normal mice, resulting in progressive neuroinflammatory responses. Interestingly, when microglia are treated with brain extracts post-TBI, microglia-derived MPs have been found to be significantly enriched with miR-124-3p, thereby promoting the transition of microglia to an anti-inflammatory phenotype (M2) [ ]. Further studies have demonstrated that M2 microglia-derived EVs facilitates brain functional recovery through molecular mechanisms involving miR-135a-5p [ ], miR-124 [ ], and miR-23a-5p [ ]. This series of studies reveals the pleiotropy and spatiotemporal heterogeneity of EVs functions, which are closely associated with the timing of their release and the activation state of the source cells. Furthermore, Tian et al. discovered that neuron-derived microparticles released following brain injury have an impact on systemic coagulation function, promoting blood clotting in injured mice and helping to reduce acute blood loss. In normal mice, these microparticles exhibit a dose-dependent transition from a hypercoagulable state to consumptive coagulopathy. This finding highlights the importance of precise control of EVs dosage and its indications in clinical translation.
EVs derived from stem cells have been demonstrated to exhibit significant neuroprotective effects in various models of brain injury. These effects include immunomodulation, promotion of neural and vascular regeneration, and inhibition of glial scar formation, and this has led to stem cell derived EVs attracting widespread attention. The secretomes of diverse stem cell types, including bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UCMSCs), and Placental mesenchymal stem cells (PLMSCs), have been shown to ameliorate brain injury. However, it is noteworthy that the secretomes of these stem cell types exhibit notable differences. Shin et al. classified ADSCs and BMSCs as adult MSCs, while PLMSCs and UCMSCs were categorised as fetal MSCs. Through proteomic analysis, they found that the secretome of fetal MSCs is more diverse than that of adult MSCs, containing a greater abundance of proteins associated with tissue development and cell differentiation [ ]. Further research revealed that UCMSCs secrete higher levels of neurotrophic factors, such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and nerve growth factor (NGF), compared to the other three types of stem cells [ ]. Additionally, in differentiation induction experiments, UCMSCs demonstrated a higher efficiency in differentiating into neural progenitor cells, suggesting that UCMSC-derived EVs possess stronger neurotrophic potential [ ]. In contrast, BMSCs exhibited a significant advantage in secreting VEGF and demonstrated stronger immunomodulatory capabilities [ ]. These findings indicate that the primary mechanisms by which EVs from different stem cell sources mediate brain protection vary, likely due to differences in the expression levels of specific proteins and factors within their secretomes. In addition, researchers have noted that when extracting EVs from stem cells cultured in vitro, serum-free media are typically used to avoid interference from serum-derived EVs. Petrenko et al. observed that the metabolic activity of BMSCs significantly decreased after 24 h of serum starvation, while AD-MSCs and UCMSCs showed no significant changes [ ]. In comparison to in vivo stem cell therapy, stem cells cultured in vitro are typically exposed to an oxygen concentration of 21%, which differs markedly from the hypoxic microenvironment in vivo. Liu et al. found that stem cell-derived EVs under hypoxic conditions exhibited upregulation of 80 microRNAs and downregulation of 46 microRNAs compared to those under normoxic conditions, demonstrating superior repair capabilities in injury models [ ]. In a similar study, Gregorius et al. observed that treatment with hypoxic sEVs led to the upregulation of 3 miRNAs and downregulation of 3 miRNAs in cerebrovascular endothelial cells, compared to normoxic sEVs, platelet-derived sEVs, and sEV-free treatments. These differentially expressed microRNAs have been linked to pathways such as neurotrophic factor signaling and VEGF signaling, suggesting that EVs prepared under hypoxic conditions may offer distinct benefits in the repair of brain injury [ ].
The biological roles of EVs in brain injury repair are complex and multifaceted, with their functions depending not only on the type and state of the source cells, but also being significantly influenced by microenvironmental factors, as well as extraction and purification techniques. Future research should focus on elucidating the mechanisms of EVs under various pathological conditions, with the aim of precisely regulating their composition and functionality to provide novel strategies and tools for the treatment of brain injuries.

## Potential mechanism of stem cell-derived EVs against brain injury following cardiac arrest
The extant evidence substantiates the neuroprotective impact of EVs on brain injury subsequent to CA/CPR. This encompasses EVs procured from the plasma of rats subjected to remote ischemia adaptation [ ] as well as EVs derived from BMSCs [ ]. Nevertheless, the number of pertinent studies is insufficient, and the mechanism of action of EVs remains uncertain. The primary cause of brain injury following resuscitation is ischemia-reperfusion injury. This article reviews disease models with similar pathophysiological mechanisms, including ischemic stroke, ischemic-hypoxic brain injury, hemorrhagic shock, and other brain injury models, to explore the potential protective mechanisms of stem cell-derived EVs against brain injury after resuscitation.

## Inhibition of neuronal death
Apoptosis is a well-established mechanism of programmed cell death. In instances of acute cerebral ischemia and hypoxia, the inhibition of cell apoptosis, particularly neuronal apoptosis, presents a promising avenue for reversing the detrimental effects of brain damage following CA. EVs have been demonstrated to directly interfere with the process of apoptosis (Fig. A). For instance, EVs derived from neural stem cells (NSCs) can inhibit Caspase 2 and prevent neuronal apoptosis by transferring small noncoding RNA (microRNA, miRNA) miR-150-3p [ ]. These bioactive components target various regulatory molecules and modulate apoptosis signaling pathways. In a rat CA/CPR model, Li et al. demonstrated that BMSC-EVs could transfer miR-133b to neuronal cells, activating the AKT/GSK-3β pathway by targeting the JAK1/Wnt3 signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting neuronal apoptosis in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus [ ]. Han et al. discovered in a neonatal mouse model of hypoxic-ischemic brain damage (HIBD) that miR-410, present in UCMSCs-EVs, can be delivered to neurons. Once delivered, miR-410 targets and inhibits histone deacetylase 1 (HDAC1), resulting in an upregulation of EGR2 and Bcl2 expression. Consequently, neuronal activity is enhanced, and cell apoptosis is markedly inhibited [ ]. Furthermore, Shen et al. elucidated the significant role of miR-410 in the treatment of HIBD with BMSC-EVs. miR-410 offers effective protection against HIBD by targeting and inhibiting HDAC4 and modulating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway [ ]. Additionally, it was evidenced that miR-93 in BMSC-EVs protects hippocampal neurons from hypoxia-ischemia-induced damage by reducing the expression of HDAC4 and upregulating Bcl-2 [ ]. Furthermore, miR-93 has the potential to inhibit histone demethylase JMJD3. Through the JMJD3-mediated p53/KLF2 axis, miR-93 can reduce oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R)-induced neuronal apoptosis in vitro and alleviate HIBD in vivo [ ].
 Ferroptosis represents a novel form of programmed cell death that involves the intracellular accumulation of iron, which then facilitates the generation of ROS and lipid peroxidation. The accumulation of lipid peroxides can result in damage to the cell membrane, ultimately leading to cell death [ ]. Zhang et al. demonstrated that UCMSC-EVs facilitated the translocation of Nrf2 from the cytosol to the nucleus via the long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) TUBB6. This process inhibited both inflammation and ferroptosis, thereby conferring protection against post-TBI in mice [ ]. Additionally, Liu et al. investigated the potential therapeutic applications of inhibiting ferroptosis mechanisms via EVs. By integrating BMSC-EVs with the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), they crafted EVs that were enriched with Fer-1. These EVs potently curtailed ferroptosis via the upregulation of GPX4 and the downregulation of COX-2 [ ]. Moreover, evidence indicates that EVs may exert protective effects against pyroptosis [ , ]. However, emerging paradigms, such as cuproptosis [ ] and disulfidptosis [ ], remain understudied. The contribution of various cell death pathways in neurons following CA remains a matter of debate. Furthermore, additional research is required to elucidate the principal mechanisms through which EVs exert their effects.
Autophagy, a vital intracellular mechanism of degradation and recycling, plays an essential role in maintaining cellular stability. It eliminates damaged organelles and misfolded proteins, thereby ensuring the integrity and optimal functioning of cellular components. It is important to note that autophagy can become detrimental when excessive [ ]. The hyperactivation of autophagy following CA initiates neuronal demise, whereas its inhibition has been demonstrated to improve neurological outcomes [ ]. Conversely, preconditioning strategies, including hypothermia [ ], metformin [ ], and adiponectin [ ], stimulate autophagy via AMPK, conferring protection. EVs have been observed to exert a “dual-edged sword” effect on autophagy. In a model of MCAO, adipose-derived MSC-EVs delivered miR-25-3p to neurons, downregulating the p53/BNIP3 pathway to reduce autophagy and confer neuroprotection [ ]. Xu et al. discovered that components in BMSC-EVs, particularly lncRNA KLF3-AS1, facilitated autophagy via the ETV4/Sirt1 axis, which stabilized neurons and mitigated ischemia-reperfusion injury in MCAO mice [ ].

## Reducing oxidative stress and inflammation
The efficacy of EVs in mitigating oxidative stress and inflammation has been demonstrated consistently across various disease models [ ]. Their actions encompass the reduction of ROS generation, the suppression of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels in cells or tissues, the curtailment of infiltration by microglia and neutrophils in brain tissue [ ], and the effective diminishment of the circulation of monocytes and activated T lymphocytes [ ] (Fig. D). A prevailing hypothesis suggests that an ensemble of non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) housed within EVs, including lncRNAs [ , ], circular RNAs (circRNAs) [ ], and miRNAs [ , ], orchestrate these protective mechanisms. It is noteworthy that some lncRNAs and circRNAs function as competitive endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) to regulate miRNA-mediated pathways [ , ]. Furthermore, the involvement of various proteins and cytokines encapsulated within EVs cannot be ignored. Xia et al. identified the presence of transforming TGF-β, Smad2, and Smad4 proteins in sEVs derived from embryonic stem cells (ESC). These proteins, through stimulation of the TGF-β/Smad cascade in CD4+ T cells, facilitate regulatory T cell proliferation and exert immunomodulatory actions that favorably influence neurological recovery following ischemic stroke [ ].
Pathipati et al. employed tracing methodologies to ascertain that BMSC-sEV selectively migrate to injured brain regions in MCAO model. Regardless of the delivery mode, either intraventricular injection or intranasal route, BMSC-sEV were predominantly internalized by activated microglia/macrophages [ ]. This indicates that the therapeutic efficacy of BMSC-sEVs may originate from a complex interaction with microglia. In a related study, Xin et al. demonstrated that BMSC-EVs mitigate neuroinflammation by downregulating osteopontin expression in both resident microglia and infiltrating monocytes/macrophages. Following ischemic brain injury, MSC-EVs reduced the phagocytic activity of microglia surrounding neurons, thereby facilitating synaptic integrity restoration [ ].
Beyond their anti-inflammatory role in the acute phase, EVs also have the ability to alleviate chronic neuroinflammation. The miRNAs present in MSC-EVs are capable of regulating pro-inflammatory microglial activities, thereby playing a critical role in preventing chronic neuroinflammatory states and the subsequent development of long-term cognitive and emotional impairments [ ]. Notably, in a TBI mouse model, a single intranasal administration of MSC-EVs during the acute phase has a protective effect for 84 days. This prolonged benefit is believed to result from the unique ability of MSC-EVs to attenuate chronic neuroinflammatory responses. MSC-EVs may also inhibit synaptic deterioration and stimulate neurogenic processes within the hippocampus [ ].

## Regulation of microglial phenotypic polarization
The polarization of microglial phenotypes has become a focal point of contemporary research. As resident immune sentinels in the central nervous system, microglia exhibit dual roles. Pro-inflammatory (M1) microglia induce neuroinflammation and neurotoxicity, whereas anti-inflammatory (M2) microglia suppress inflammation and promote neurogenesis [ ]. Activation of M1 microglia is observed in various pathologies, including cerebral ischemia [ ], subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) [ ], TBI [ ], and neurodegenerative diseases [ ]. After CA, extensive microglial activation is detected and correlated with neurological impairment [ ]. Strategies to suppress microglial activation [ ] or induce a neuroprotective M2 phenotype [ ] have shown efficacy in improving hippocampal neuronal viability after CA. In the MCAO rat model, BMSC-EVs attenuate cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury-induced neuroinflammation by inhibiting M1 polarization and promoting M2 polarization of microglia. This process partially involves downregulation of cysLT2R and suppression of the ERK1/2 signaling pathway [ , ]. Zhou et al. employed cationic liposome-mediated transfection to generate EVs enriched with miR-145. The downregulation of FOXO1 expression by miR-145 facilitates the transformation of microglia towards an anti-inflammatory phenotype under OGD/R conditions [ ]. Furthermore, in a SAH mouse model, BMSC-EVs delivered miR-140-5p to microglia, which resulted in the suppression of HDAC7 expression. Consequently, this led to enhanced histone H3 acetylation at the AKAP12 promoter region, upregulating AKAP12 expression, and activating the cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling pathway [ ].
Other stem cell derived EVs have been observed to exhibit comparable mechanisms. For example, EVs derived from ADSCs have been demonstrated to facilitate the transformation of microglia M1 into M2 phenotype by inhibiting STAT1 and PTEN [ ]. In a study conducted by Yang et al., hypoxic-preconditioned ADSC-EVs were employed to treat MCAO mice. The results demonstrated that circ-Rps5, carried by these EVs, could enhance SIRT7 expression by sponge miR-124-3p, thereby modulating glial cell phenotypic transformation [ ]. Furthermore, Li et al. treated TBI rats with EVs secreted by dental stem cells (DSC) and observed that DSC-EVs transferred miR-330-5p to activated microglia. miR-330-5p targeted Ehmt2-H3K9me2-mediated CXCL14 transcription, modulating microglial polarization and consequently diminishing neuroinflammation and promoting brain injury repair [ ]. These findings elucidate the diverse mechanisms through which miRNAs in EVs regulate microglial inflammatory responses and identify potential novel targets for brain injury treatment (Fig. B).

## Improvement of blood-brain barrier
The blood-brain barrier (BBB) is constituted by tightly juncted endothelial cells of the cerebral vasculature and serves a pivotal role in safeguarding neural tissue and maintaining homeostasis. In pathological conditions, disruptions to the integrity of the barrier contribute to the exacerbation of neurological injury progression, which ultimately results in the development of vasogenic cerebral edema and cellular demise [ ]. The impairment of the BBB following CA has been consistently documented in both animal models and clinical cases [ , ]. The severity of BBB impairment correlates strongly with unfavorable neurological outcomes [ ].
The therapeutic application of EVs has been shown to enhance the integrity of the BBB. EVs derived from neural progenitor cells can mitigate the increase in matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) induced by MCAO. MMP-9, a mediator of inflammation secreted by activated astrocytes, upregulates ABCB1 expression in endothelial cells, thereby increasing BBB permeability [ ]. BMSC-EVs contribute to the maintenance of BBB integrity by increasing the expression of tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin in cerebral microvascular endothelium and inhibiting MMP activity, potentially through the suppression of the Caveolin-1/CD147/VEGFR2/MMP pathway [ ]. In a recent study, Li et al. revealed that sEVs derived from iPSCs not only mitigate BBB damage in aged mice with ischemic stroke but also exert an anti-aging influence on the BBB, reversing the expression of certain age-related proteins. The anti-aging mechanism of iPSC-sEVs on the BBB is partially attributed to the delivery of AKT1 and CALM, which activates the endothelial eNOS − Sirt1 axis [ ].
Tian et al. demonstrated that miR-124 within BMSC-EVs, by targeting the antioxidative enzyme PRX1, restrains the activation of astrocytes and microglia post-ischemia/reperfusion injury, thereby safeguarding the integrity of the blood-brain barrier [ ]. Upon internalization by endothelial cells, miR-132-3p suppresses the expression of GTPase-activating protein RASA1, thereby initiating the activation of the Ras/PI3K/Akt/eNOS signaling pathway. This alleviates hypoxia-reoxygenation (H/R)-induced oxidative stress and apoptosis, preserving the integrity of endothelial tight junctions [ ]. Additionally, BMSC-EVs enhance the expression of miR-666-3p in cerebral microvascular endothelial cells. Under OGD/R conditions, miR-666-3p promotes cell viability, mitigates apoptosis, suppresses inflammation, and exerts a protective function by directly targeting and disrupting the MAPK1 component of the MAPK pathway [ ] (Fig. C).

## Promoting nerve and blood vessel regeneration
Neurovascular regeneration following brain injury is of paramount importance for functional recovery. EVs enriched in specific miRNAs and growth factors have attracted significant interest due to their potential for neural injury repair. The therapeutic administration of such EVs elicits a notable increase in transcriptomic profiles associated with neurogenesis, neuronal maturation, synapse formation, and neural plastic adaptation [ ] (Fig. E). In a rat model of deep hypothermic circulatory arrest, EVs abundant in miR-214 demonstrated superior efficacy in facilitating neurovascular neogenesis within the hippocampal tissue compared to conventional BMSC-EVs. Furthermore, these EVs markedly enhance the rodents’ spatial learning, memory capabilities, and vestibulomotor functions [ ]. Similarly, EVs enriched with the miR-17-92 cluster have been demonstrated to significantly enhance angiogenesis and neurogenesis [ ]. Xin et al. provide further insight into the mechanisms of these EVs, which leverage the PTEN/PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway to amplify neuroplasticity in the ischemic border zone of the brain. This promotes axon elongation and myelination, thereby ameliorating electrophysiological responses and neurological functions in MCAO rats [ ].
Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which is ubiquitous in the central nervous system, plays a pivotal role in mitigating inflammation, apoptosis, promoting myelination, and neurogenesis [ ]. However, its translational application is impeded by the limited permeability of the blood-brain barrier due to its substantial molecular mass. The delivery of BDNF via EVs represents a crucial strategy for overcoming this limitation. Zhou et al. developed a lentivirus-mediated method to generate BDNF-enriched sEVs, which were administered nasally to stimulate the BDNF/TrkB signaling cascade in ischemic cerebral tissue, effectively reducing axonal injury and promoting neurovascular growth [ ]. Xu et al. observed that EVs from BDNF-induced BMSCs surpassed conventional BMSC-EVs in enhancing cell migration and inhibiting apoptosis, potentially through elevated expression of miR-216a-5p, employing a BDNF-induced BMSC approach [ ].
It is noteworthy that EVs have been observed to effectively mitigate the senescence of NSC. Through transcriptomic analyses, Liu et al. identified a notable correlation between genes that were differentially expressed in NSC following OGD/R injury and the processes of cellular aging. The treatment of OGD/R-injured NSC with small EVs derived from induced mesenchymal stem cells (iMSC-sEVs) resulted in the mitigation of NSC senescence and the restoration of regenerative capabilities. Subsequent in vivo investigations revealed that cognitive recovery was enhanced following ischemic stroke in mice, with this effect potentially linked to enhanced synaptic structure integrity [ ].

## Others
The transfer of mitochondria from EVs derived from various stem cell sources has been demonstrated to effectively ameliorate energy metabolism dysfunction in damaged cells. With regard to the distribution of EV size, EVs capable of mitochondrial transfer predominantly exhibit larger diameter characteristics (> 200 nm). Representative examples include EVs derived from iPSC-differentiated cardiomyocytes (204 nm) [ ], NSC-EVs (350.13 ± 25.70 nm) [ ], UCMSC-EVs (300–700 nm) [ ], and BMSC-EVs (200–400 nm) [ ]. These large-sized EVs that contain structurally and functionally intact mitochondria can be internalized by recipient cells. Phinney et al. confirmed that EVs derived from brain endothelial cells (BECs) could transfer functional mitochondria to OGD-injured BECs, thereby significantly improving mitochondrial function in recipient cells. Notably, EVs enriched with ATP5A have been observed to exhibit a heightened capacity to stimulate ATP synthesis [ ]. Furthermore, Dave et al. provided in vivo evidence that BEC-EVs mediated mitochondrial transfer significantly reduced cerebral infarction volume in MCAO models [ , ]. In contrast, EVs with diameters < 200 nm, while lacking intact mitochondrial structures, still carry mitochondrial-related proteins and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). Zhao et al. discovered that MSC-derived EVs could transfer mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) mRNA and mtDNA, exhibiting remarkable mitochondrial protective effects in both in vitro HK-2 cell models and in vivo AKI models, thus providing novel insights into the active components of EVs [ ].
Intracellular calcium overload serves as a trigger for cellular damage following cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury. Conversely, MSC-EVs have been demonstrated to inhibit the Ca²⁺ surge in neurons induced by OGD/R injury [ ]. Turovsky et al. observed that MSC-EVs induce regulated fluctuations in intracellular calcium concentration in astrocytes via PI3K/AKT pathway stimulation, thereby preventing calcium overload and protecting both astrocytes and neurons during OGD/R in vitro [ ]. This protective strategy is analogous to ischemic preconditioning, whereby cells are conditioned to tolerate minor Ca²⁺ perturbations and to enhance their defense against detrimental overload. Concurrently, ischemic hypoxic cells secrete glutamate, which potentiates an intracellular Ca²⁺ rise. Zhuang et al. demonstrated that BMSC-EVs upregulate glutamate transporter-1 expression in astrocytes, effectively alleviating glutamate excitotoxicity and improving neurological outcomes post-injury [ ]. Further investigation implicates a neuroprotective mechanism involving miR-124-3p, which suppresses the p38 MAPK signaling cascade [ ]. In conclusion, EVs derived from stem cells are capable of traversing the blood-brain barrier, delivering cargo such as ncRNA, proteins and functional mitochondria to a variety of neural cell types, including neurons, brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMEC), astrocytes, and microglia. This enables engagement with diverse signaling cascades. In the short term, EVs act rapidly to alleviate oxidative stress and suppress inflammation, significantly reducing cellular damage and mortality while simultaneously maintaining the integrity of the blood-brain barrier to prevent further injury. Over time, EVs contribute to the modulation of the immune microenvironment, alleviating chronic neuroinflammatory states. Additionally, they stimulate neurogenesis and angiogenesis, thereby reconstructing neural network coherence and enhancing cerebral circulation. Collectively, these effects facilitate the comprehensive tissue repair and functional recovery in the brain. ## Recent research directions and challenges

## New source of stem cells
The majority of current studies employ EVs derived from adult tissue-derived MSCs, particularly BMSCs and UCMSCs. However, these studies are constrained by the limited quantities of cells available and inter-batch variability. Ling et al. developed a non-invasive method for obtaining stem cells, successfully isolating them from human urine samples to generate USC-EVs that stimulate neurogenesis in MCAO rat models and accelerate NSC proliferation along with neuronal differentiation under OGD/R stress [ ]. Furthermore, additional non-invasive approaches for the acquisition of stem cells are being investigated. Examples include the retrieval of endometrial stem cells from female menstrual blood [ ] and dental pulp stem cells (DPSC) [ ]. Furthermore, the incorporation of iPSCs into studies of EVs has greatly expanded the range of available supply options (Fig.  A). Xia et al. demonstrated the capacity of iMSC-sEVs to facilitate angiogenesis following ischemic stroke and to enhance endothelial cell migration and tube formation after OGD/R [ ]. A significant challenge in the realm of iPSC applications pertains to the potential for tumorigenesis associated with undifferentiated iPSCs within the cell population. During the reprogramming process, there is a possibility of introducing genomic instability and mutations, which can augment immunogenicity. To ensure the safety of iPSC-EVs, it is imperative to optimize reprogramming methods and to meticulously regulate production standards. This will ensure that these EVs do not contain any reprogramming factors and are free from contamination by viruses and endotoxins in co-purified products [ ].
 

## Pretreatment of EVs
The composition of EVs is complex, exhibiting significant heterogeneity even among cells of the same origin. The contents of EVs produced can vary considerably as a result of variations in the cellular environment or exposure to specific stimuli [ ]. Chen et al. discovered that EP4 antagonists induce MSC-EVs to secrete high levels of 2’,3’-CNP. This enzyme converts 2’,3’-cAMP into neuroprotective adenosine, demonstrating remarkable efficacy in promoting neurogenesis and repairing cognitive, memory, and learning deficits caused by hippocampal damage [ ]. In a separate study, Chu et al. employed hydrogen sulfide pretreatment of BMSCs, resulting in a significant upregulation of microRNA-7b-5p in the derived EVs. These vesicles facilitated the conversion of activated microglia and monocytes/macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby alleviating hypoxic-ischemic brain injury in neonatal mice [ ]. These findings indicate that pretreatment strategies may enhance specific components in EVs, which could offer potential benefits for neuroprotection and repair. These results provide novel insights and avenues for future EVs therapy. However, further investigation is required to elucidate the specific components involved. Consequently, a considerable number of studies are employing genetic engineering techniques to produce EVs that are enriched with specific target miRNAs in order to elucidate their mechanisms of action. For example, Min et al. employed oligonucleotide plasmids to transfect BMSCs and obtain EVs enriched in miR-124-3p [ ]. Other common techniques include the transfection of stem cells with lentivirus or liposomes (Fig.  B). Compared to direct miRNA administration, EVs offer a natural protective shell for miRNAs, preventing their degradation by nucleases and thereby enhancing their stability and prolonging the duration of drug action. In addition, as natural carriers, EVs exhibit high biocompatibility and avoid immune rejection. However, several technical and production challenges remain. For instance, the yield of EVs is relatively low, making it difficult to meet the demands of large-scale clinical applications. Furthermore, the technology for efficiently loading RNA into EVs requires further optimization to ensure the stability and controlled release of RNA within the EVs.

## Improving the production of EVs
The production of EVs from stem cells is currently constrained by the lack of an ideal extraction technology. The widespread application of EVs has been impeded by the presence of common issues, including low yield and purity. A straightforward method for enhancing EVs production involves the direct addition of soluble small molecule compounds, such as fenoterol, norepinephrine, forskolin, N-methyldopamine, and mephenesin [ ], to the cell culture medium. Wang et al. achieved a threefold increase in EVs by treating mesenchymal stem cells with a combination of N-methyldopamine and norepinephrine [ ].
It has been demonstrated that specific external conditions can markedly enhance EVs production. These include, but are not limited to, mechanical stress, acoustic, electrical, thermal stimulation, as well as hypoxic and acidic environments [ – ]. These factors induce a state of cellular stress that mimics the conditions present in damaged tissue, thereby stimulating the production of EVs. Furthermore, in contrast to the traditional two-dimensional (2D) cell culture method, three-dimensional (3D) culture technology facilitates an enhanced platform for information exchange between cells. This not only enhances cell-to-cell interaction, but also increases the production of EVs. For example, Haraszti et al. observed that UCMSCs secreted 20 times more EVs in 3D culture than in 2D culture. Additionally, the combination of 3D culture technology with tangential flow filtration has the potential to further enhance EVs production, offering new avenues for the large-scale production and utilisation of EVs [ ].
Additionally, the production of EVs can be augmented through the manipulation of EVs biogenetic pathways via genetic engineering techniques (Fig.  C). Yoon et al. put forth an innovative approach utilizing gene editing technology to augment EV production. Tumor susceptibility gene (TSG) 101, a pivotal marker protein for EVs, is involved in the ESCRT-dependent mechanism of EVs production. The generation of EVs by neural stem cells with elevated TSG101 expression (NSC-TSG101) is approximately 10 times greater than that of parental NSCs, indicating the potential for reducing inflammatory responses and enhancing neurotrophic factor expression [ ]. However, further verification is necessary to confirm whether these physical, chemical and biological treatments affect the cellular phenotype and whether the composition and function of EVs undergo alteration.

## Alternatives to EVs
Novel technological procedures have been devised for the extraction of cell vesicles. Ilahibaks et al. derived nanovesicles (NVs) by extruding cells successively through filter membranes with increasingly smaller pore sizes. In comparison to EVs, NVs exhibit a significantly higher productivity, with a protein content that is 20 times greater than that of EVs, and a cargo composition more reflective of the cellular composition [ ]. Duan et al. have developed a freeze-thaw method for destroying cell membranes and then ultrafast centrifugation to obtain vesicles. This solution circumvents the process of cellular secretion, thus it can be designated as intracellular vesicles (IVs). IVs meet the general criteria of EVs, exhibit a comparable protein secretion profile, and display analogous functions in the domain of tissue repair. Moreover, the extraction efficiency of IVs is 16 times that of EVs, indicating that IVs are a promising substitute for traditional EVs [ ] (Fig.  D).

## Brain-targeted EVs
There is a consensus regarding the ability of extracellular vesicles to traverse the blood-brain barrier. Nevertheless, the challenge remains in enabling a greater number of systemically administered EVs to reach the injured brain region (Fig.  E). Tian et al. developed human neural progenitor cell-derived EVs that had been fused with a fusion protein containing an arginine-glycine-aspartate (RGD) peptide structure. The RGD peptide is designed to bind with integrin αvβ3, facilitating targeted delivery of EVs to the site of cerebral ischemia [ ]. Liu et al. employed lentiviral vectors to infect mouse BMSCs and generate brain-targeted EVs carrying the RVG-circ_0000075 cargo. By modulating the SMURF2/YY1/LEF1 pathway, this approach enhances neuronal survival post-cerebral ischemic injury and amelioration of the overall ischemic damage [ ]. Despite the significant potential of brain-targeting EVs to minimize retention and metabolic loss in organs such as the liver and spleen, there are several critical challenges. Firstly, there is a need to enhance the efficiency of crossing the blood-brain barrier with these particles. Secondly, optimization of drug loading efficiency and stability is required. Thirdly and finally, there is a need to achieve highly specific targeting of particular neural cell types or even organelles. Addressing these issues will greatly advance the application of EVs in the treatment of brain-related diseases.

## Conclusion
Extracellular vesicles derived from stem cells show considerable promise as a means to protect the brain from injury, offering an immediate defense during the acute stages and facilitating tissue repair. In chronic courses, it has been demonstrated to enhance neurological function and to represent a groundbreaking therapy for post-cardiopulmonary resuscitation brain injury. The manipulation of natural stem cells via induction or genetic editing has the potential to augment the yield of EVs, refine their composition, and enhance targeting specificity. Nevertheless, the precise composition of EVs remains unknown. How do EVs from different stem cell origins vary? What are the differences between EVs produced by cells in vivo and in vitro? Do EVs batche from the same source exhibit compositional differences? Elucidating these questions is crucial to deepen our understanding of EVs’ roles in post-resuscitation brain injury, paving the way for their clinical translation.
