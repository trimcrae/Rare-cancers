## Abstract

## Background
High-grade serous ovarian cancer (HGSOC) remains highly lethal due to late diagnosis and chemoresistance. Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) have emerged as critical regulators of tumor progression. This study investigates the role of the cytoplasmic lncRNA LINC02888 in HGSOC.

## Methods
We identified overexpression of LINC02888 in HGSOC tissues and cell lines through whole-transcriptome sequencing, qPCR, in situ hybridization, and analyses of TCGA/GTEx datasets. Functional assays including CCK-8, EdU, colony formation, wound healing, Transwell migration/invasion, and flow cytometry were performed after modulating LINC02888 expression. RNA pull-down coupled with mass spectrometry identified the RNA-binding protein PPIB as an interactor of LINC02888. RNA-seq following both LINC02888 and PPIB knockdown, along with Western blot, qPCR analyses and functional assays, revealed that LAPTM5 is a downstream effector. Furthermore, RIP-qPCR, deletion mapping, and ActD assays confirmed that LINC02888 binds PPIB, which stabilizes LAPTM5 mRNA via its 3’UTR. The effect on the RIG-I–like receptor pathway was evaluated by measuring the expression levels of RIG-I, IRF7, and ISG15. Finally, in vivo, the critical role of the LINC02888/PPIB/ LAPTM5 axis in ovarian tumor progression were explored by axillary subcutaneous injection of specifically transfected OVCAR3 cells into nude mice.

## Results
LINC02888 was significantly upregulated in HGSOC tissues and correlated with advanced disease and poor prognosis. Silencing LINC02888 inhibited proliferation, migration, and invasion, while inducing apoptosis; conversely, overexpression promoted these oncogenic behaviors. Mechanistically, LINC02888 interacts with PPIB to stabilize LAPTM5 mRNA via its 3’UTR, resulting in elevated LAPTM5 protein levels and suppression of RIG-I–like receptor signaling. Rescue experiments confirmed the critical role of the LINC02888–PPIB–LAPTM5 axis in HGSOC progression in vivo and vitro.

## Conclusions
Our findings reveal that LINC02888 drives HGSOC progression through a mechanism involving PPIB-mediated stabilization of LAPTM5 mRNA, which suppresses RIG-I–like receptor signaling. It also appropriately suggests that inhibiting this innate immune pathway may contribute to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, making the LINC02888–PPIB–LAPTM5 axis a promising therapeutic target for this aggressive malignancy.

## Introduction
Ovarian cancer is the seventh most common cancer in women worldwide, accounting for an estimated 22% of cases and 31% of cancer deaths in 2022 [ ]. High-grade serous ovarian carcinomas (HGSOC) represent the most prevalent subtype of ovarian cancer and are recognized to originate from the fimbrial ends of the fallopian tubes [ ]. Due to deficiencies in homologous recombination (HR) DNA repair, effective precision medicine strategies have been challenging to implement, resulting in frequent recurrence and acquired resistance in a substantial proportion of patients [ ]. Despite significant improvements in both biology and therapeutic solutions, the prognosis of patients with HGSOC remains poor [ ]. Therefore, in-depth investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying tumorigenesis and progression is essential to uncovering new therapeutic targets for HGSOC.
Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a large and relatively novel class of non-coding RNAs characterized by RNA transcripts > 200 nucleotides that the majority of expressed transcripts do not encode protein. Loci expressing lncRNAs exhibit many of the characteristics of protein-coding genes, including promoters [ ], multiple exons [ ], alternative splicing [ ], characteristic chromatin domains [ ], regulation by morphogens and conventional transcription factors [ ], altered expression in cancer and other diseases. lncRNAs can function distinctly and have different interaction partners based on localization, therefore, the roles of lncRNAs in the cytoplasm are expected to be different than in the nucleus [ ]. RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) are important regulatory molecules within cells that interact with RNA to form ribonucleoprotein complexes to regulate pre-mRNA splicing, cleavage and polyadenylation, RNA stability, RNA localization, RNA editing, and translation, thereby maintaining normal cellular physiological functions [ , ]. Studies have found that RBPs can influence mRNA stability through post-transcriptional regulation, playing a regulatory role in the occurrence, development and therapeutic resistance of malignant tumors [ ]. The stability of mRNA is related to the integrity of its structure, which includes the 5′cap, the 5′UTR, the open reading frame, the 3′UTR and the polyadenylated tail [Poly(A) tail] at the 3′end [ ]. lncRNAs can function as molecular scaffolds that facilitate the binding of specific proteins to the 5′or 3′UTRs of mRNAs. This interaction can modulate the maintenance or removal of the 5′cap and poly(A) tail, thereby directly influencing mRNA stability and degradation. For example, a recent study on brain function and neurodegenerative diseases identified a nuclear-enriched antisense lncRNA transcribed from the Uchl1 locus. This lncRNA overlaps with the 5′region of Uchl1 mRNA and harbors an inverted SINEB2 element, enabling it to serve as a molecular scaffold that recruits Uchl1 mRNA to the translation machinery [ ]. lncRNA also competitively binds to RBPs, thereby inhibiting their association with mRNAs and regulating mRNA stability. For example, host lncRNAs induced by SARS-CoV-2 infection may compete with the viral genome for binding to specific RBPs, thus modulating the cellular response and controlling the viral replication cycle [ ]. Similarly, Tan et al. demonstrated that lncRNA SNHG1 competitively interacts with hnRNPL to co-regulate E-cadherin expression at both the mRNA and protein levels [ ]. In this study, we sought to elucidate the previously uncharacterized functions of lncRNAs in HGSOC. By integrating whole-transcriptome profiling with targeted RNA sequencing and a suite of experimental validations, we identified LINC02888 (ENSG00000236318.2) as a novel oncogenic lncRNA overexpressed in HGSOC. Detailed clinical analyses revealed that elevated LINC02888 expression correlates with adverse prognostic features, while functional assays including gain-and loss-of-function experiments, RNA pull-down, and RNA immunoprecipitation—demonstrated that LINC02888 promotes HGSOC progression. Mechanistically, we found that LINC02888 binds to the RBP PPIB, which in turn stabilizes LAPTM5 mRNA, ultimately suppressing the RIG-I-like receptor pathway. These findings illuminate a new molecular axis in HGSOC pathogenesis and highlight potential therapeutic targets for combating this aggressive cancer.

## Materials and methods

## Patients and clinical specimens
In 2024, we collected 30 ovarian tissue specimens clinically and pathologically diagnosed as HGSOC (see Table  ) from the Department of Gynecology at Shengjing Hospital, China Medical University (Shenyang, China). Additionally, 30 normal ovarian tissue specimens were obtained from patients who underwent total hysterectomy and bilateral adnexectomy for benign uterine lesions. We then analyzed the correlation between LINC02888 expression and the clinicopathologic features of the HGSOC patients. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) postoperative histological confirmation of HGSOC (for the cancer group) or normal ovarian tissue (for the control group); (2) no administration of targeted medications, transcatheter arterial chemoembolization, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy prior to surgery; (3) no history of other malignancies; and (4) provision of informed consent prior to specimen collection. Patient clinicopathological data, including age, menopause, FIGO stage, tumor size, and lymph node metastasis, were systematically recorded. These specimens were primarily used for qPCR validation. Additionally, ovarian tissues from 3 HGSOC cases and normal ovarian tissues from 3 control individuals were obtained. Immediately after surgery, a portion of the resected samples was immersed in 5 mL of RNAlater (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, R0118FT) to preserve RNA integrity, while another portion was promptly immersed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF).
 

## RNA-sequencing and bioinformatic analysis
Total RNA was extracted from ovarian tissue samples using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and its quality was rigorously assessed via agarose gel electrophoresis, Nanodrop spectrophotometry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Only samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) ≥ 7.0 were processed further. To eliminate genomic DNA contamination, the RNA was treated with RNase-free DNase I (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Ribosomal RNA was then removed using the Ribo-Zero™rRNA Removal Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The rRNA-depleted RNA was fragmented into 250–300 bp fragments and used as a template for first-strand cDNA synthesis. Subsequently, RNase H (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to degrade the RNA strand. Second-strand cDNA synthesis was carried out using DNA Polymerase I (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) in the presence of dNTPs (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). The resulting double-stranded cDNA was purified, end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated with sequencing adapters. Size selection was performed with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) to isolate 200 bp fragments. The final library was quantified using qPCR and the Qubit® 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and then sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (paired-end 150 bp, S2 flow cell; Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Raw sequencing reads were processed using FastQC (v0.11.9, Babraham Bioinformatics, UK) for quality control, and DESeq2 (v1.32.0, Bioconductor) for differential expression analysis. Differentially expressed lncRNAs were defined as those with an adjusted P < 0.05 and a fold-change of ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5. A volcano plot of the differentially expressed lncRNAs was generated using Cluster 3.0 software. Furthermore, to investigate the downstream regulatory mechanisms of LINC02888 and peptidylprolyl isomerase B (PPIB), transcriptome sequencing followed by bioinformatic analysis was performed on OVCAR3 cells with LINC02888 and PPIB knockdown, using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with the NovaSeq 6000 S4 Reagent Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

## RNA extraction and qPCR
Total RNA was extracted from tissues and cells using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was then reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript® RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Japan) following the recommended protocol. qPCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems® Real-Time PCR System (QuantStudio 3, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™II (Tli RNaseH Plus) (Takara Bio, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: an initial incubation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 39 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 10 s. Relative gene expression was quantified using the 2 − ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table  .
 

## Cell culture
Human ovarian cancer cell lines (SKOV3, A2780, OVCAR3, and ANGLNE) were obtained from Servicebio (Wuhan, China), while human ovarian surface epithelial cells (HOSEpiC) were purchased from ScienCell Research Laboratories (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Human embryonic kidney 293 T cells (HEK293T) were obtained from China Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). Cells were passaged at least 3 times and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination prior to being used in experiments. SKOV3, OVCAR3, HEK293T, and HOSEpiC were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), whereas A2780 and ANGLNE were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). Both media were supplemented with 10% Superfine Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (Sbjbio, Nanjing, China) and 1% Antibiotic–Antimycotic (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂, and were passaged at a 1:3 split ratio once they reached 85–90% confluence.

## Plasmids, siRNA, and lentivirus transfection
Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting LINC02888, PPIB, and LAPTM5 were synthesized by RiboBio Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) using sequence-specific complementary base pairing for gene silencing. The siRNA sequences are listed in Table  . The cDNAs for LINC02888, PPIB, and LAPTM5 were cloned into plasmid vectors for overexpression, with technical support provided by Mibiotech Co., Ltd. (Shenyang, China). Furthermore, additional plasmid constructs were generated by Syngenbio Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), including those for expressing either the wild-type or deletion mutant versions of LINC02888, as well as plasmids encoding FLAG-tagged wild-type PPIB and separate constructs containing the F1-F3 domain fragments of PPIB. In addition, lentivirus-mediated systems were developed for PPIB overexpression and for delivering shRNA constructs targeting LINC02888 (Syngenbio Co., Ltd.), with stable transductants selected using 2 μg/mL puromycin. For transfection, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and grown to 60–70% confluence before being transfected with the respective plasmids or siRNAs using Lipo3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

## Western blot (WB)
Total protein was extracted from lysed cells using Cell Lysis Buffer for Western and IP (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (APExBIO, Boston, USA) and PMSF (Beyotime) to prevent protein degradation. The protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay (Sevenbio, Beijing, China). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per well) were subjected to 7.5–15% SDS-PAGE gels for separation, followed by electrotransfer onto Polyvinylidene Fluoride membrane (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 2 h at room temperature (RT) to minimize nonspecific binding. After blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at RT. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Beyotime), and images were captured using a chemiluminescence imaging system. All antibodies used are listed in Table  .
 

## Actinomycin D (ActD) assays
To assess LAPTM5 mRNA stability, SKOV3 cells were transfected with si-NC, si-PPIB, si-NC + pcDNA, si-NC + oe-LINC02888, or si-LAPTM5 + oe-LINC02888; OVCAR3 with either si-NC or si-PPIB; and ANGLNE cells received pcDNA, oe-LAPTM5, si-NC, or si-PPIB. After transfection, cells were treated with 5 µg/mL Actinomycin D (APExBIO) to halt transcription. Total RNA was extracted at 0,6,12, and 18 h post-treatment, and qPCR was performed. LAPTM5 transcript stability was determined by comparing its degradation kinetics over time.

## Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) with digoxigenin (DIG)-labeled probes
SKOV3 and OVCAR3 cells (2 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in 24-well plates, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sevenbio), and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Beyotime). The cells were then pre-hybridized using a fluorescent in situ hybridization kit (wuhan Genecreate Biological Engineeringco., Ltd) at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by overnight hybridization at 42 °C with digoxigenin (DIG)-labeled LINC02888 probes (Genecreate Biological Engineeringco., Ltd; sequence: 5′-TTTTCAGACTCTGTGGCGCA-3′). Post-hybridization washes were performed sequentially with 2 × SSC, 1 × SSC, and 0.5 × SSC. For probe detection, the samples were incubated with an anti-DIG-biotin antibody (1:100, Genecreate Biological Engineeringco., Ltd) at 37 °C for 1 h after PBS washes, followed by incubation with LINC02888-labeled streptavidin (1:100, Genecreate Biological Engineeringco., Ltd). Fluorescence signal amplification was achieved using a Tyramide Signal Amplification (TSA) kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Finally, the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were captured using a Nikon Eclipse 80i Fluorescence Microscope (Nikon, Japan).

## In situ hybridization (ISH)
The sections with a thickness of 3 μm were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Prehybridization was performed in a humidified chamber at 37 °C for 2 h using the appropriate prehybridization solution. Subsequently, the sections were hybridized overnight at 37 °C with a DIG‐labeled LINC02888 probe (Wuhan BOSTER Biological Technology Co., Ltd.). After hybridization, sections were washed and incubated with anti‐digoxigenin antibody at 4 °C overnight, followed by staining with a DAB substrate kit (DA1010; Solarbio). The LINC02888 probe comprised the following sequences:5′‐CATCTCCATTCCCAACAGTCCCCATTCTACTTGCAGAAAG‐3′,5′‐GACCACAGCTGCAGAGGGGATCGTGGTGAATTTCAATGAA‐3′, and 5′‐TAATGGAGGGATAGACTGAAGTTAAATGGGATCAGGTATG‐3′.

## RNA pull-down and mass spectrometry (MS)
RNA pull-down assays were performed using the RNA–Protein Pull Down Kit (Genecreate Biological Engineeringco., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Biotin-labeled RNA probes targeting LINC02888, LAPTM5, and a negative control (NC) were synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The target RNA was in vitro transcribed using the TranscriptAid T7 High Yield Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and subsequently biotinylated with the RNA 3′ End Biotinylation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The biotinylated RNA was then incubated with streptavidin-coated magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at RT for 1 h to facilitate efficient binding. Following extensive washing, the RNA-bound beads were incubated with SKOV3 and OVCAR3 cell lysates in lysis buffer for 2 h at 4 °C to allow for RNA–protein interactions. The LINC02888 RNA–protein complexes (OVCAR3 cell) were eluted and analyzed by silver staining for visualization, and by MS for protein identification (performed by Genecreate Biological Engineeringco., Ltd.). All procedures were conducted under RNase-free conditions to prevent RNA degradation.

## RNA–protein immunoprecipitation (RIP)
RNA Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation (RIP) Kit (Genecreate Biological Engineeringco., Ltd) was used to investigate the interaction between LINC02888, LAPTM5, and PPIB using an PPIB antibody. In brief, SKOV3 and OVCAR3 (2 × 107) were collected and lysed in RIP lysis buffer containing RNase inhibitors and a protease inhibitor cocktail. Protein A/G magnetic beads were incubated with PPIB antibody (for the IP group) or host-matched control IgG (for the IgG group) at RT for 2 h to allow antibody conjugation. Subsequently, the antibody-conjugated beads were mixed with the cell lysates and RIP buffer, and the mixtures were incubated overnight at 4 °C in a vertical mixer. After five washes with RIP wash buffer, RNA was isolated using TRIzol followed by chloroform extraction, isopropanol precipitation, and ethanol washes. The purified RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA and analyzed by qPCR, with agarose gel electrophoresis confirming the specificity of the qPCR products. The antibody information is presented in Table  .

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining
Following fixation in paraformaldehyde for over 24 h, the specimens were processed in 10% NBF before embedding. For immunohistochemical analysis, sections (3 µm thick) were deparaffinized in xylene and subsequently treated with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. The slides were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Ki-67, PPIB, LAPTM5, and RIG-I) at a dilution of 1:300. After washing, the sections received a 30‐minute incubation at RT with the appropriate secondary antibodies, and antigen detection was achieved using DAB (Solarbio). Counterstaining with hematoxylin was performed prior to digital scanning (Aperio, Leica Biosystems, Germany) for image analysis.

## Agarose gel electrophoresis
PCR amplification products or RNA samples were mixed with loading buffer and loaded onto a 2% agarose gel prepared in 1 × TAE buffer containing a nucleic acid stain. Electrophoresis was carried out at 100 V for 40 min. After separation, gels were examined under UV illumination and band sizes were determined by comparison with a DNA molecular weight marker.

## Subcellular fractionation
SKOV3, OVCAR3 and ANGLNE cells (5 × 106) were washed with ice-cold PBS and fractionated using the PARIS™ kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, pellets were lysed in cytoplasmic buffer on ice for 10 min, centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min to collect the supernatant (cytoplasmic RNA), then the pellet was resuspended in nuclear lysis buffer on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min to yield the nuclear RNA fraction. Fraction purity was confirmed by RT-qPCR for U6 (nuclear) and GAPDH (cytoplasmic), and LINC02888 abundance in each fraction was determined by RT-qPCR.

## Cell proliferation assay
To evaluate cell proliferation, three assays were conducted: Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8), colony formation, and 5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation. For the CCK-8 assay, transfected cells were seeded in triplicate at a density of 2,000 cells per well in 96-well plates and incubated overnight under standard culture conditions. On days 1 to 4, 10 μ L of CCK-8 reagent (C0038, Beyotime) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 1 h before measuring the absorbance at 495 nm with a microplate reader (Infinite® 200 PRO, Tecan, Switzerland).For the colony formation assay, 1 × 103 cells were plated in each well of a 6-well plate and cultured for two weeks until roughly 50 colonies formed per well. Colonies were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 1% crystal violet solution for 10 min to enhance visualization. In the EdU (Cy3) assay, cells were incubated with the EdU working solution from the EDU Imaging Kits (K1075, APExBIO) for 2 h, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min. After washing twice with PBS containing 3% BSA, the cells were permeabilized using 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min and washed again twice. The cells were then incubated with the click reaction solution for 30 min in the dark, followed by counterstaining with Hoechst for 15 min. Fluorescent images were captured using the ImageXpress Confocal High-Content Imaging System (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

## Cell migration assay
The upper chambers of 24-well transwell inserts (Corning, New York, USA) were plated with 2 × 104 cells in serum-free medium, and the lower chambers were filled with complete medium supplemented with 15% FBS. The upper chambers were collected after 24 h of culturing, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde fixation for 15 min and 0.3% crystal violet staining for 10 min. Migrated cells were subsequently counted in three randomly selected microscopic fields using ImageJ software. As for the wound healing assay, transfected cells were seeded in 6-well plates and grown until they reached 90–100% confluence. A sterile 10 μL pipette tip was used to make a wound in the cell monolayer. After washing three times with PBS to remove debris, the cells were incubated in medium supplemented with 2% FBS for 48 h. Images of the wound area were captured at 0 and 48 h using a microscope, and the migration rate was determined by measuring the reduction in the gap size over time.

## Cell invasion assay
For the invasion assay, Transwell inserts (Corning, New York, USA) were used as in the migration assay. One day prior to cell seeding, each upper chamber was pre-coated with 200 μ L of a Matrigel solution prepared at a 1:15 dilution (Matrigel: serum-free medium), taking care to avoid bubble formation. The plate was then incubated at 37 °C for 4 h to allow the Matrigel to polymerize into a thin, uniform matrix layer that simulates the extracellular matrix. After polymerization, Cells were seeded in the top part of the chamber, while the lower chamber was filled with complete medium containing 15% FBS. The invasive capacity of the cells was assessed by following the same procedures used in the migration assay.

## Cell apoptosis assay
Cells were processed using the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1062, Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s instructions and analyzed by flow cytometry. Data were statistically evaluated and visualized using GraphPad Prism software, with each group tested in triplicate.

## Xenograft mouse model
Stable transfected cell lines were generated and used to establish a subcutaneous tumor xenograft model. OVAR3 cells transfected with Lv-vector, Lv-sh-NC, Lv-sh-LINC02888, and Lv-oe-PPIB were utilized to assess the effects of LINC02888 knockdown and PPIB overexpression on tumor phenotype. Female BALB/c nude mice (5 weeks old, sourced from Huafukang, China) were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 per group). All animal procedures were conducted at China Medical University’s Animal Center. Each mouse received a subcutaneous injection in the axillary region with 150 μL of PBS containing 2 × 10⁶ cells. Tumor growth was monitored every three days, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: tumor volume (mm3) = 0.5 × length × width2. At 21 days post-injection, mice were euthanized via CO₂ inhalation in accordance with institutional guidelines. Excised tumors and harvested tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and subsequently processed for HE and IHC staining. The maximal tumor burden permitted by our ethics committee was a tumor diameter of less than 1.5 cm. Tumor sizes were monitored regularly throughout the study, and in no instance was the 1.5 cm limit exceeded. In cases where tumors approached the limit, animals were promptly euthanized in accordance with our predefined humane endpoints.

## Statistical analysis
All quantitative data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). For normally distributed data, group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA); for nonparametric data, the Kruskal–Wallis test and Mann–Whitney U test were applied. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test, as appropriate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), and ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used for quantifying wound areas, cell counts, and band densities. Original images from three independent Western blot experiments are provided in the Supplementary Material.

## Results

## LINC02888 is upregulated in HGSOC and acts as a predictor of poor prognosis
Whole-transcriptome sequencing of three HGSOC tissue samples and three normal ovarian tissue samples revealed differentially expressed lncRNAs. The significantly expressed lncRNAs (ENSG00000236318.2, LINC02888) from the 869 differentially overexpressed lncRNAs were selected for further analysis (Fig.  A), as supported by data from both the TCGA (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga)and GTEx (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/)databases (Fig.  B). It also was validated through RefSeq (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/refseq) (Fig.  D). Primers for LINC02888 were initially designed and used for qPCR analysis (Fig.  C), and in situ hybridization (ISH) was subsequently performed on paraffin-embedded tissue sections to detect LINC02888 expression (Fig.  E, ) in 30 HGSOC tissue samples and 30 normal ovarian tissue samples. The results confirmed that LINC02888 was significantly overexpressed in HGSOC tissues compared to normal ovarian tissues. LINC02888 expression was relatively higher in ovarian carcinoma cell lines (SKOV3, A2780, OVCAR3, ANGLNE) than in HOSEpiC cells (Fig.  G). Given the levels of LINC02888, SKOV3, OVCAR3, and ANGLNE cell lines were selected as candidates for follow-up studies. Then, the clinical correlation analyses suggest that high levels of LINC02888 contributed to Lymph node metastasis, advanced FIGO stage, and larger tumor size (Table  ). The FISH combined with cytoplasmic/nuclear fractionation experiments revealed that LINC02888 was predominantly localized in the cytoplasm (F g.  H, , S2A). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that LINC02888 is abnormally upregulated in HGSOC and correlates with poor outcomes.
 

## LINC02888 promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HGSOC cells in vitro
The functional effects of LINC02888 in HGSOC were investigated. Two specific siRNAs (Table  ) targeting LINC02888 were synthesized and transfected into SKOV3 and OVCAR3 cells. The siRNAs significantly downregulated LINC02888 (Fig.  A, ). Functionally, CCK-8 and EdU assays demonstrated that knockdown of LINC02888 impeded significantly decreased cell proliferation in SKOV3 and OVCAR3 cells (Fig.  C–H). A similar inhibitory effect of LINC02888 on cell proliferation was observed in colony formation assays (F g.  I–L). Moreover, silencing of LINC02888 significantly impaired cell migration and invasion, as verified using Transwell assays (Fig.  A–D). Wound-healing assays further demonstrated that downregulation of LINC02888 impaired cell mobility (Fig.  E–H). Additionally, knockdown of LINC02888 in flow cytometry assays showed enhanced apoptosis in SKOV3 and OVCAR3 cells (F g.  I–L). ANGLNE cells were chosen for high-expression studies because they expressed LINC02888 at relatively low levels (Fig.  H). After verifying LINC02888 overexpression via qPCR (Fig.  A), the functional effects in ANGLNE cells were assessed through in vitro experiments. CCK8, EdU, and colony formation assays demonstrated that overexpression of LINC02888 significantly increased cell proliferation (Fig.  B–F). Transwell and wound healing assays showed that transfection of ANGLNE cells with the LINC02888 vector increased cell migration and invasiveness (Fig.  G–J). Flow cytometric analysis showed that overexpression of LINC02888 inhibited the apoptotic capacity of ANGLNE cells (Fig.  K–M).
 

## LINC02888 interacts with the RBP PPIB in HGSOC
RBPs are known to be abnormally expressed in cancer and regulate the function of lncRNAs. To better understand the potential mechanism mediating Oncogenic effects of LINC02888 in HGSOC, we performed RNA pull-down assays to isolate LINC02888-associated proteins from OVCAR3 cells (Fig.  A, ). Based on the functional annotation of proteins predicted by MS analysis, we used the online database RBP Suite (http://www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/RBPsuite/) to screen for potential RBPs of LINC02888. We found that only the RBP PPIB was identified in both the MS and database screening (Fig.  C). We further validated the LINC02888–PPIB interaction by RNA pull-down followed by WB, which confirmed that the PPIB was significantly pulled down by the LINC02888 probe in SKOV3 and OVCAR3 cells (Fig.  D, ). Additionally, RIP assays performed with the antibodies against PPIB showed significant enrichment of LINC02888 (Fig.  F, ). Indicating that LINC02888 physically associates with PPIB.
 We constructed and biotinylated different deletion fragments of LINC02888 (FL: full-length, F1: 357–930 bp, F2: 931–1130 bp, F3: 1131–1395, 1–356 bp) (Fig.  H) and used them in the RNA pull-down assay with SKOV3 and OVCAR3 cells lysates. It was shown that LINC02888 F3 region truncations induced a marked reduction of LINC02888-captured PPIB compared with that of the WT, indicating that the region (1131–1395, 1–356) is the more critical binding site of LINC02888 that interacts with PPIB(F g.  I).To determine the specific binding domains of PPIB in LINC02888.According to CatRAPID (http://service.tartaglialab.com) prediction, we also constructed PPIB-FL (full-length), PPIB- F1 (47–204 bp), PPIB- F2 (1–47, 204–216 bp), and PPIB- F3 (47–141, 192–204 bp) (Fig.  J). Lysates of 293 T cells transfected with PPIB mutant plasmids were co-incubated with a biotin-labeled LINC02888 probe to conduct the pull-down assay, WB detected that in addition to the full-length (FL) LINC02888, LINC02888 could combine with the PPIB-F1. However, the PPIB-F2 and PPIB-F3 did not bind to LINC02888 probe, suggesting that the domain (141–192) of PPIB may serve as the key binding site for LINC02888 (Fig.  K). This prediction was confirmed by the experimental results. Finally, the qPCR and WB revealed that overexpression or knockdown of LINC02888 did not affect the expression of PPIB when bound to it (Fig.  L–N). Thus, the 1131–1395 and 1–356 nucleotide regions of LINC02888 interact with PPIB by binding to the 141–192 amino acid region.

## LINC02888 knockdown activates the RIG-I-like receptor pathway by downregulating LAPTM5 via PPIB Interaction
To investigate the downstream regulatory mechanisms of LINC02888 and the RBP PPIB in mediating oncogenic effects in HGSOC cells, RNA transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on OVCAR3 cells following the knockdown of LINC02888 and PPIB (Fig.  A, ). From the RNA-seq results, LAPTM5 emerged as the only overlapping gene, underscoring its potential significance in the regulatory network (Fig.  C). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment and Gene Ontology (GO) analysis showed that the RIG-I-like receptor pathway were significantly enriched (Fig.  D–F). The three candidate genes (RIG-I, IRF7, ISG15) were selected as RIG-I-like pathway-involved genes for further verification. WB confirmed that LINC02888 overexpression markedly reduced the expression of RIG-I, IRF7, and ISG15 in ANGLNE cells, whereas LINC02888 silencing increased the expression of RIG-I, IRF7, and ISG15 in SKOV3, OVCAR3, and ANGLNE cells (Fig.  G–J, S2B-C). These findings indicate that all three genes are significantly negatively correlated with LINC02888, consistent with the RNA-seq results. In addition, we examined the effect of LINC02888 overexpression or knockdown on LAPTM5 and PPIB protein levels in various HGSOC cell lines. WB assays showed that in SKOV3, OVCAR3, and ANGLNE cells, knockdown of LINC02888 reduced LAPTM5 expression, whereas in ANGLNE cells, overexpression of LINC02888 increased LAPTM5 expression. Notably, PPIB expression remained unchanged under these conditions (Fig.  K, , S2B-C). Moreover, the knockdown of PPIB reversed the promoting effect of LINC02888 overexpression on LAPTM5 protein expression (Fig.  M, , S2D-E). Abnormal elevation of LAPTM5 can induce lysosomal cell death, highlighting its significance in clinical immune system disorders and cancer [ , ]. Therefore, we hypothesize that LAPTM5 plays an integral role in LINC02888-mediated activation of the RIG-I-like receptor pathway. The activated or inactivated effect on the RIG-I-like receptor pathway caused by LINC02888 could be rescued by knockdown or overexpression of LAPTM5, respectively, indicating that LAPTM5 is the downstream target of LINC02888 (Fig.  O, , S2F-I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that LINC02888 knockdown activates the RIG-I-like receptor pathway by downregulating LAPTM5 through its interaction with PPIB.
 

## Cytoplasmic LINC02888 interacts with PPIB to stabilize LAPTM5 mRNA
The PPIB was significantly pulled down by the LAPTM5 mRNA probe in SKOV3 and OVCAR3 cells (Fig.  A, ), and the LAPTM5 mRNA was also precipitated using an anti- PPIB antibody in the RIP assay, demonstrating that LAPTM5 mRNA can be enriched by PPIB (Fig.  C). Based on the NCBI RefSeq record for LAPTM5 mRNA (NM_006762.3), the mature transcript is 2177 bp, with the coding sequence (CDS) spanning nucleotides 30 to 818. Accordingly, the 5′UTR comprises nucleotides 1–29, and the 3′UTR spans nucleotides 819–2177 (Fig.  D), defined on the spliced, mature mRNA. The untranslated regions are critical for regulating mRNA stability, translation efficiency, and localization. To determine whether PPIB interacts with the 3′UTR of LAPTM5, we performed an RNA pull-down assay using biotin-labeled probes corresponding to the truncated fragments (3′UTR, CDS, and 5′UTR) as well as the full-length LAPTM5 transcript. As shown in Fig.  E, PPIB was specifically pulled down by the 3′UTR probe, indicating a direct interaction between PPIB and the 3′UTR of LAPTM5 mRNA. This finding suggests that PPIB may regulate LAPTM5 expression through binding to its 3′ UTR. We also found that PPIB knockdown led to a decrease in LAPTM5 mRNA and protein levels in SKOV3, OVCAR3, and ANGLNE cells (Fig.  F–H, S2D-E, S2J-M), whereas PPIB overexpression increased LAPTM5 expression in ANGLNE cells (F g.  I–K). Furthermore, ActD assays showed that PPIB knockdown decreased LAPTM5 mRNA levels and shortened its half-life in SKOV3, OVCAR3, and ANGLNE cells (Fig.  L, S2N-O), while PPIB overexpression had the opposite effect in ANGLNE cells (Fig.  M). To determine whether PPIB is required for LINC02888 overexpression-induced LAPTM5 upregulation, we co-transfected LINC02888 plasmids with si-PPIB into SKOV3 cells. The qPCR analysis revealed that PPIB knockdown reversed the increase in LAPTM5 expression triggered by LINC02888 overexpression (Fig.  N).
 

## PPIB mediates LINC02888-induced HGSOC progression by regulating LAPTM5 in vitro and in vivo
First, we assessed PPIB expression and function. Analysis of the TCGA and GTEx datasets revealed that PPIB was significantly upregulated in HGSOC (Figure S1A). We further measured the relative expression levels of PPIB in ovarian carcinoma cell lines (SKOV3, A2780, OVCAR3, and ANGLNE) compared to normal epithelial cell lines (HOSEpiC) (Figure S1B), leading to its selection for further studies. Additionally, we examined PPIB expression in 30 pairs of matched normal (N) and cancerous (T) HGSOC tissues using IHC, which revealed that PPIB was predominantly localized in the cytoplasm of HGSOC cells, with some detection in the nucleus, and the IHC assay consistently confirmed that PPIB was upregulated (Figure S1C).
second, to investigate the role of PPIB in HGSOC cells, the si-PPIB significantly reduced both PPIB protein and mRNA expression levels in SKOV3 and OVCAR3 cells (Figure S1D-H), whereas transfection with PPIB plasmids markedly increased both in ANGLNE cells (Figure S1I-K). In EdU and colony formation assays, PPIB downregulation significantly inhibited cell proliferation (Fig.  A–H), whereas PPIB overexpression led to faster proliferation than controls (Fig.  M–P). Moreover, Transwell assays showed that PPIB knockdown reduced cell migration and invasion (F g.  I–L), whereas PPIB overexpression enhanced these abilities (Fig.  Q, ).
 Third, we conducted rescue experiments to further clarify the role of PPIB in the tumor-promoting effect of LINC02888. Functionally, colony formation, EdU, and Transwell assays demonstrated that PPIB overexpression reversed the reduced proliferation and the diminished migration and invasion in SKOV3 and OVCAR3 cells induced by LINC02888 knockdown, whereas PPIB knockdown counteracted the enhanced proliferation in SKOV3 and OVCAR3 cells resulting from LINC02888 overexpression (Fig.  A–J, S3K-L). Consistent with these findings, In vivo studies revealed that knockdown of LINC02888 (Lv-sh-LINC02888) significantly inhibited tumor growth in OVCAR3 cells compared with the control group (Lv-sh-NC), underscoring a potential tumor-promoting role for LINC02888. Conversely, overexpression of PPIB (Lv-PPIB) resulted in a markedly increased tumor growth rate relative to its corresponding control (Lv-vector). Notably, rescue experiments demonstrated that PPIB overexpression effectively reversed the tumor-inhibitory effects induced by LINC02888 knockdown, including the associated deletion of LAPTM5 and the upregulation of RIG-I. (Fig.  A–G).
 Finally, we examined the role of LAPTM5 in LINC02888-driven HGSOC progression through rescue experiments. IHC analysis revealed that LAPTM5 was significantly upregulated in HGSOC tissues, with predominant cytoplasmic localization and some nuclear staining (Figure S1L). Furthermore, we successfully achieved LAPTM5 knockdown in SKOV3 cells and overexpression in ANGLNE cells (Figure S1M-R), thereby confirming a functional interplay between LAPTM5 and LINC02888. EdU and colony formation assays showed that LAPTM5 overexpression mitigated the proliferation impairment caused by LINC02888 knockdown, whereas LAPTM5 depletion nullified the proliferation enhancement driven by LINC02888 overexpression in OVCAR3 and SKOV3 cells (Fig.  A–G, S3A-H). Similarly, Transwell assays confirmed that LAPTM5 overexpression counteracted the inhibitory effects of LINC02888 silencing on migration and invasion in SKOV3 and OVCAR3 cells (Fig.  H–J, S3I-J). In summary, these findings suggest that LINC02888 plays an oncogenic role in HGSOC by targeting LAPTM5 via PPIB (Fig.  ).
 

## Discussion
HGSOC remains the most lethal ovarian cancer subtype due to late diagnosis, frequent recurrences, and the development of chemoresistance [ ]. Although numerous genomic and epigenomic alterations have been implicated in HGSOC pathogenesis [ ], emerging evidence underscores the pivotal roles of noncoding RNAs—particularly lncRNAs—in driving tumorigenesis and immune evasion [ , ]. In this study, we identify LINC02888 as a novel cytoplasmic lncRNA that is robustly upregulated in HGSOC tissues and cell lines and is clinically correlated with poor prognosis. Through an integrative approach employing whole-transcriptome sequencing, bioinformatics, and extensive experimental validation, we demonstrate that LINC02888 interacts with the RBP PPIB to stabilize LAPTM5 mRNA, thereby suppressing RIG-I-like receptor pathway signaling.
Similar to other cytoplasmic lncRNAs that regulate cellular proliferation and metastasis via direct interactions with RBPs [ ] or through microRNA sponging [ ], our database screening using RBP Suite coupled with unbiased MS following RNA pull-down pinpointed PPIB as a key binding partner of LINC02888. Unlike many nuclear lncRNAs (e.g., HOTAIR, PVT1) that influence chromatin states and transcriptional programs [ , ], cytoplasmic lncRNAs often form functional ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes by interacting with various proteins, thereby modulate post-transcriptional gene expression and mRNA stability. For example, lncRNA LINC01419 binds to Y-box binding protein 1 (YBX1) in the cytoplasm to increase the mRNA stability of the metabolic enzyme pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1), promoting reprogramming of the glycolytic pathway [ ]. Similarly, Shang et al. recently reported that lncRNA GDIL has been shown to interact with XRN2 and promote its relocalization from the nucleus to the cytoplasm to degrade CHAC1 mRNA [ ]. In our study, although LINC02888 does not alter PPIB expression, the LINC02888-PPIB complex is critical for stabilizing LAPTM5 mRNA. This stabilization likely promotes elevated LAPTM5 protein levels, which may enable HGSOC cells to circumvent apoptotic signals and facilitate immune evasion.
LAPTM5 is known to play a central role in lysosomal function and has been implicated in immune regulation. Prior reports have shown that overexpression of LAPTM5 can negatively regulate T and B cell receptor levels at the plasma membrane [ , ], thereby contribute to increased cellular survival and invasive potential [ ]. In contrast, LAPTM5 exerts a positive regulatory effect on inflammatory signaling in macrophages [ ]. Our transcriptomic analyses further reveal that silencing LINC02888 leads to the upregulation of key RIG-I-like receptor pathway effectors—including RIG-I, IRF7, and ISG15—which are critical mediators of antiviral and antitumor responses [ ]. These observations suggest that the LINC02888-PPIB-LAPTM5 axis not only supports tumor cell proliferation and metastasis but also impairs innate immune signaling. In doing so, this pathway may contribute to a broader mechanism of immune evasion in HGSOC, paralleling recent evidence that tumors manipulate pattern recognition receptor pathways to escape immunosurveillance [ ]. The partial rescue of RIG-I-like receptor components upon LINC02888 knockdown further underscores the importance of lncRNA-mediated post-transcriptional regulation at the tumor-immune interface, which could have implications for improving the efficacy of immunotherapies such as checkpoint inhibitors [ ].
Interestingly, PPIB is predominantly localized to the endoplasmic reticulum and functions as a molecular chaperone with peptidyl-prolyl cis–trans isomerase activity. Recent studies have suggested that PPIB can independently induce pyroptosis in macrophages, a form of inflammatory programmed cell death characterized by cytokine release and membrane rupture [ ]. If PPIB-mediated pyroptosis alters macrophage abundance or skews their polarization in the tumor microenvironment, this mechanism might further contribute to immune evasion by HGSOC cells. This duality—where PPIB both stabilizes oncogenic transcripts and potentially modulates immune cell behavior—merits further investigation. Therapeutically, our findings position LINC02888 as a promising target in HGSOC. Given that disrupting the LINC02888-PPIB interaction effectively destabilizes LAPTM5 mRNA and reactivates RIG-I-like receptor pathway components, strategies aimed at inhibiting this lncRNA-protein interface—using antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs), small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), or small molecule inhibitors—may offer substantial benefit, particularly if LINC02888 expression is tumor-specific[ , ].
Despite the strength of our findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, validation of the prognostic significance of LINC02888 in HGSOC requires larger patient cohorts to assess its utility in predicting treatment outcomes. Second, detailed structural analyses are required to precisely define the molecular interface between PPIB and LAPTM5 mRNA, which could significantly facilitate the development of targeted inhibitors while also warranting further investigation into the role of LAPTM5 in RIG-I-like receptor signaling. Third, while our in vivo experiments using xenograft models support the oncogenic role of the LINC02888-PPIB-LAPTM5 axis, studies employing immunocompetent animal models or patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) would provide a more comprehensive understanding of its impact on the tumor-immune microenvironment.
In summary, our study reveals a novel mechanism by which the cytoplasmic lncRNA LINC02888 drives HGSOC progression and immune evasion. By binding to PPIB, LINC02888 stabilizes LAPTM5 mRNA, thereby suppressing RIG-I-like receptor pathway signaling and promoting an environment conducive to tumor growth, metastasis, and reduced immunosurveillance. These findings not only advance our understanding of lncRNA function in ovarian cancer but also highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting the LINC02888-PPIB-LAPTM5 axis to improve outcomes in patients with this aggressive malignancy.

## Conclusions
Within this investigation, our study demonstrates that LINC02888 is markedly upregulated in HGSOC and correlates with poor clinical outcomes. Functionally, LINC02888 promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion in ovarian cancer cells. Mechanistically, LINC02888 interacts with PPIB to stabilize LAPTM5 mRNA, leading to suppression of key innate immune signaling components. Although we did not directly assess immune evasion, the observed inhibition of the RIG-I-like receptor pathway suggests that the LINC02888-PPIB-LAPTM5 axis may contribute to immune escape by impairing innate immune responses. These findings not only enhance our understanding of lncRNA function in ovarian cancer pathogenesis but also highlight the potential therapeutic benefit of targeting the LINC02888-PPIB-LAPTM5 axis. Future studies should directly evaluate immune evasion mechanisms and explore the translational impact of disrupting this regulatory network in HGSOC.
