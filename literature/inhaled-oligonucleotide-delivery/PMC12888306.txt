## Abstract
Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is a type of progressive interstitial lung disease characterized by varying degrees of lung interstitial fibrosis. Lung alveolar regeneration failure in PF is critical for mediating this incurable disease. Here, we report that chronic lung injury induces the release of extracellular vesicles (EVs) from alveolar macrophages (AMs) to promote PF development by inhibiting alveolar regeneration. Mechanistically, the acetylation of mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL), which is mediated by the upregulation of acetylase lysine acetyltransferase 5 (KAT5) following chronic lung injury, enhances its capacity to facilitate endosomal trafficking and the production of EVs from AMs. Those EVs, which are taken up by type 2 alveolar epithelial cells (AEC2s), release miR-148a-3p to repress the endogenous β-catenin agonist Wnt10b and exert an inhibitory effect on AEC2 renewal. Disruption of the KAT5-MLKL interaction or pharmacological inhibition of miR-148a-3p expression restores the regenerative capacity of AEC2s, with potent therapeutic efficacy in PF. Our study suggests a potential therapeutic option for PF.

## Introduction
Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is a type of progressive interstitial lung disease (ILD) characterized by progressive stiffening of the lung parenchyma [ ]. Idiopathic PF (IPF) is the most aggressive form of PF, with a 3–5-year survival rate following diagnosis [ ]. To date, only two drugs, pirfenidone and nintedanib, have been approved by the FDA for IPF treatment. However, clinical results show that these two drugs can delay disease progression but cannot reverse the pathological changes of established PF [ , ]. The incomplete understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying PF results in poor prognosis and limited treatment options for PF patients [ ]. Thus, a comprehensive and in-depth elucidation of PF pathogenesis is urgently needed.
Progressive PF is often attributed to impaired regenerative ability of the alveolar epithelium in response to chronic lung injury [ , ]. Modulation of lung alveolar regeneration (LAR) has substantial value in PF treatment. Under normal conditions, type 2 alveolar epithelial cells (AEC2s), which produce surfactant in the lungs, function as stem cells by self-renewal and differentiation into type 1 alveolar epithelial cells (AEC1s) [ ]. Recent studies have established that failure to maintain AEC2 stemness is involved in PF pathogenesis [ , ]. However, the mechanisms regulating AEC2 renewal are poorly understood. Alveolar macrophages (AMs) are important players in host defense against microbial infections [ ]. Numerous studies, including ours, have demonstrated that AMs contribute to PF development by interacting with fibroblasts (effector cells in PF) and releasing mediators such as chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 1 (CCL1), transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1) and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) to induce the differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts [ – ]. However, whether AMs regulate the proliferation and differentiation of AEC2s by communicating with neighboring AEC2s during PF remains largely undefined.
Extracellular vesicles (EVs), including exosomes, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies, function as mediators of cell-to-cell communication through the intercellular delivery of diverse functional cargo molecules, such as proteins, lipids and nucleic acids [ , ]. During the last two decades, numerous studies have shown that EVs play vital roles in multiple aspects of the pathogenesis of various human diseases [ ]. The role of EVs in PF has recently received increasing attention. For example, EV production in the lung epithelium, which is controlled by syndecan-1, promotes PF by regulating epithelial plasticity [ ]. Furthermore, the increased amount of EVs in bronchiolar lavage fluid (BALF) from PF mice as well as from PF patients mediates WNT5A signaling in fibroblasts and thus contributes to PF pathogenesis [ ]. Additionally, exosomes released from cells in lung spheroids attenuate experimental PF by promoting lung repair [ ]. EVs derived from fibrocytes exert profibrotic effects on fibroblasts and thus promote PF [ ]. Activated fibroblast-derived EVs exert significant antifibrotic effects by suppressing myofibroblast differentiation [ ]. These studies indicate that the function of EVs in PF pathogenesis varies based on their origin cells and target cells. However, to our knowledge, the impact of EVs derived from profibrotic AMs on PF pathogenesis has not yet been addressed.
Herein, we investigated the function of EVs derived from profibrotic AMs in the pathogenesis of PF induced by repeated bleomycin (BLM) injury. AMs were the major secretors of EVs in BALF, and AMs from fibrotic mouse lungs released higher numbers of EVs than did control AMs. AM-derived EVs in PF mice played a profibrotic role in PF progression. In the current study, we analyzed the upstream signaling mediating the increased release of EVs from AMs during PF progression. Moreover, we sought to determine the mechanism by which AM-derived EVs promotes PF. Notably, we also aimed to elucidate the functional implications of these findings.

## Materials and methods

## Human subjects
Human pulmonary fibrosis and normal tissue sections were purchased from OriGene and TissueArray. Com LLC. The characteristics of the pulmonary fibrosis samples from OriGene were as follows. Patient 1 (Cat. No. CS701886): H&E staining revealed lung tissue with diffuse fibrosis and chronic inflammation. Patient 2 (Cat. No. CS806816): H&E staining revealed lesions associated with interstitial inflammation, 3 + MIX and granulomata. Patient 3 (Cat. No. CS800022): H&E staining revealed 90% alveoli, 5% bronchioles, and 5% fibrovascular septae, with interstitial fibrosis and inflammation present. Three individual normal lung tissues from OriGene (Cat. Nos. CS803096, CS800312, and CS701295) that exhibited a regular alveolar architecture and morphology according to H&E staining were similarly analyzed and compared with the patient samples. A human pulmonary interstitial fibrosis tissue array (LC561) was purchased from TissueArray. The Com LLC contained 24 cases of pulmonary fibrosis tissue and 2 cases each of normal lung tissue. A human normal lung tissue microarray (LCN241) was purchased from TissueArray. The Com LLC contained 24 cases of lung tissue.
Fresh human lung explants and bronchoalveolar lavage fluids (BALF) from PF patients were obtained from the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University. Primary AMs were obtained from the BALF of human subjects with PF. The lavage fluid was centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 min. The pellet was subsequently resuspended and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. After 1 h, the cells suspended in the medium were discarded, and the adherent macrophages were used for further study. The clinical features of the patients are listed in Table S3. All protocols involving human specimens were approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Clinical Research Center of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (approved no. 2022–052). Informed consent was obtained from all the subjects. The study conformed to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

## Animal studies
Mlkl−/− mice were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences, Inc. Male mice between 6 and 8 weeks of age were used in all studies. Mlklfl/fl mice were generously provided by Professor Jingwei Ma from Huazhong University of Science and Technology. To generate mice with Mlkl conditional knockout in macrophages, eight-week-old Mlklfl/fl mice were intratracheally administered the AAV-Lyz2-Cre virus (1.5 × 1010 vg/mouse, GeneChem Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) 14 days before the induction of the PF model. To generate mice with Wnt10b conditional knockout in AEC2s, eight-week-old C57BL/6J mice were intratracheally administered the AAV-Sftpc-shWnt10b virus (1.8 × 1011 vg/mouse, HanBio Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) 14 days before the induction of the PF model. The mice were bred and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Animal Care Center of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University. All experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (approved no. 2022 − 0586).

## Animal models
To establish the BLM model, a total of 6 doses of BLM (1 U/kg) were administered to mice via intratracheal injection at intervals of 14 days. Mice were sacrificed by an overdose of anesthesia on day 40 after the 6th BLM administration.
To establish the HCl model, 20 µL of an iso-osmolar solution of 0.1 M HCl was administered to mice via intratracheal injection for a total of 6 treatments at intervals of 14 days. Mice were sacrificed by an overdose of anesthesia on day 40 after the 6th HCl administration.
The silicosis model was established by a single intratracheal instillation of a silica particle suspension (125 mg/kg; Sigma–Aldrich), and mice were sacrificed on day 80 after SiO2 challenge.

## Cell lines and primary cultures
RAW264.7 mouse macrophages and A549 human alveolar epithelial cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)−1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Mouse MLg2908 lung fibroblasts were cultured in Eagle’s minimum essential medium (EMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM glutamine, and 1% nonessential amino acids (NEAAs). HEK293T cells were cultured in Iscove’s modified Dulbecco’s medium (IMDM) supplemented with 10% FBS. MLE-12 mouse lung epithelial cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/nutrient mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12) medium containing 5% FBS. All cells were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

## Peptide, lentivirus, and miR-148a-3p antagomir treatment in vivo.
Peptides were synthesized by a solid phase method and purified (> 95% purity) by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Guoping Pharmaceutical). Peptides were injected i.p. into mice every 3 days for 30 days beginning on day 10 after the last BLM administration. Mice were sacrificed by an overdose of anesthesia on day 40 after the last BLM administration.
For lentivirus administration, lentiviruses (5 × 107 I.U.) expressing shRNA sequences targeting Kat5 in 50 µL of PBS were administered to mice via intratracheal instillation for a total of two treatments at 2-week intervals beginning on day 10 after the last BLM administration.
For miR-148a-3p antagomir (RiboBio) treatment, mice were intravenously injected with the miR-148a-3p antagomir (10 mg/kg in 50 µL of PBS; RiboBio) once weekly for 6 weeks beginning on day 10 after the last BLM administration.

## Isolation of primary mouse AMs
Mouse AMs were isolated as previously described [ ]. In brief, cells were collected from BALF by centrifugation (500 × g for 10 min). After red blood cell (RBC) lysis, BAL cells were plated, incubated for 1 h and then washed extensively to remove unattached cells. The attached cells were used as AMs.

## 3D organoid culture of AECs
The organoid assay was performed as previously described with minor modifications [ ]. In brief, freshly isolated epithelial cells (5 × 103) were mixed with 5 × 104 MLg ([MLg2908, CCL206], ATCC) cells and then suspended in a 1:1 mixture of DMEM/F12 medium and growth factor-reduced Matrigel (Corning, 354263). The cell–medium–Matrigel mixture was then placed in a 24-well plate with 0.4-µm Transwell inserts (Falcon 353095). Cultures were maintained in 500 µL of DMEM/F12 medium containing 10% FBS. The medium was changed every other day. After 14 days of culture, colonies were counted with a Zeiss Axiovert. A1 microscope (Carl Zeiss AG), and the CFE was determined by calculating the ratio of the number of colonies to the number of input AEC2s.
For paraffin embedding, organoids were sequentially fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde overnight, embedded in Histogel (Richard-Allan), dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sliced for staining.

## Isolation of AEC2s
AEC2s were isolated as described previously [ ]. In brief, lungs were perfused sequentially with 10 mL of PBS through the right ventricle, followed by perfusion with 1 mL of dispase (1 mg/mL; Roche) and 0.5 mL of warm 1% low melting point agarose (Thermo Fisher). To obtain a single-cell suspension, the lung lobes were separated, minced, and digested with dispase (1 mg/mL) and DNase I (50 U/mL) in DMEM for 40 min at 37 °C with frequent agitation before being filtered through 100- and 40-µm strainers. Cells were then suspended in ACK lysis buffer to lyse RBCs. Primary biotinylated antibodies, including anti-CD45, anti-CD16/32, anti-CD34, and anti-CD31 antibodies, were added to cells suspended in 500 µL of DMEM containing 50 U/mL DNase I. After incubation on ice for 60 min, cells were washed and reincubated with streptavidin-labeled magnetic beads (2.5 µL of SA MyOne T1 beads/1 × 106 cells) for 30 min on a rotator, and labeled cells were then removed with a magnetic separator. Cells were further stained with antibodies, including anti-CD24-PE, anti-Sca-1-APC, and anti-EpCAM-PE-Cy7. AEC2s (PE-Cy7+APC−PE−) were sorted using a BD FACSAria III cell sorter. Data were analyzed using FCS Express 6 software.

## Isolation of EVs from murine BALF
After mice were euthanized with an overdose of Avertin, a 20-gauge catheter was inserted into the trachea. Murine lungs were lavaged 3 times with 0.8 mL of cold PBS (a total of 2.4 mL), and BALF was collected. BALF was first centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min to eliminate dead cells. The supernatant was then collected and centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 10 min to pellet cell debris. Finally, the resulting supernatant was ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 1 h to pellet total EVs. Isolated EVs were resuspended in 200 µL of PBS, quantified by NTA, and further characterized by TEM, flow cytometry, and immunoblotting.

## Isolation of AM-derived EVs
Primary AMs were isolated from BAL fluid harvested from PF mice. After incubation at 37 °C in EV-free medium for 48 h, supernatants were collected, and AM-derived EVs were separated by ultracentrifugation. In brief, EVs were isolated from the supernatants by serial centrifugation (500 × g × 5 min then 2,500 × g × 12 min at 4 °C), followed by pelleting with ultracentrifugation (17,000 × g × 30 min at 4 °C). EV pellets were subsequently washed with PBS between additional ultracentrifugation washes and ultimately resuspended in PBS.

## Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)
The particle sizes and concentrations of the EVs were determined using a ZetaView NTA instrument (Particle Metrix) based on their movement.

## Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
Freshly isolated EVs were loaded onto a 200-mesh copper grid, allowed to stand for 5 min and then stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid for 2 min. After drying for 15 min at room temperature, samples were visualized using a transmission electron microscope (FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit) operated at 80 kV.

## EVs treatment in vivo
For all EV treatment experiments, 1 × 109 or 1 × 1010 EV particles diluted in 50 µL of sterile PBS were administered intratracheally every other day for a total of 6 doses beginning on day 10 after the last BLM administration.

## EVs incubation assay
AM-derived EVs (30 mg) were incubated with primary AEC2s. After 24 h, AEC2s were harvested for RNA isolation to detect miR-148a-3p or Wnt10b expression.

## EVs uptake assay
AM-derived EVs were fluorescently labeled with the cell membrane marker PKH26 (Sigma–Aldrich) and were then cocultured with AEC2s for 24 h. Cells were subsequently washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with DAPI to label nuclei. Confocal images were acquired under a fluorescence microscope (TCS SP8X, Leica).

## Dual-luciferase reporter assay
Cells were transfected with luciferase reporter plasmids encoding the WT 3’-UTR of Wnt10b (Wnt10b-3’-UTR-WT) or a mutated Wnt10b-3’-UTR (Wnt10b-3’-UTR-MUT) together with the miR-148a-3p mimic (50 nM) or NC mimic (RiboBio) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Approximately 48 h later, the firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## In vitro acetylation assay
For the in vitro acetylation assay, recombinant MLKL protein (10 µg) and several amounts of functionally active KAT5 protein (1 µg, 2 µg, or 3 µg) were added to 1 × acetylation buffer containing 15 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 0.25 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 0.05% Tween 20, 2.5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 5% glycerol, and 500 mM acetyl-CoA. Reactions were carried out at 30 °C for 2 h. MLKL acetylation was analyzed by western blotting using a pan-acetyl–lysine-specific antibody.

## MiRNA sequencing
EVs were isolated from cultures of AMs from PBS- or BLM-treated mice. Total RNA was extracted using a QIAGEN miRNeasy Micro Kit (QIAGEN) per the manufacturer’s protocols. Approximately 20 µg of total RNA was submitted to Kangcheng Biotechnology for miRNA sequencing using the Illumina NextSeq 500 platform (San Diego) according to Illumina’s protocol. Raw reads were obtained using Illumina analysis software.

## Bioinformatics prediction
Two target prediction databases, TargetScan 7.1 (http://www.targetscan.org/vert_71/) and miRDB (http://mirdb.org/), were used to predict the target genes of miR-148a-3p.

## EVs biodistribution
AM-derived EVs from BLM-challenged mice were labeled with PKH67, suspended in 50 µL of PBS (1 × 1010 particles), and injected intravenously into C57BL/6J mice. Twenty-four hours post-injection, mice were euthanized, and lungs were harvested and cryosectioned for further immunofluorescence analysis of EV biodistribution in vivo.

## Biotin MiRNA pull-down assay
3’-Biotinylated miR-148a-3p and biotinylated scrambled oligonucleotides were synthesized by Ruibo Bio and transfected into mouse MLE-12 cells. After 24 h, cells were lysed and incubated with streptavidin magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4 °C for 4 h. The beads were washed and collected, and mRNAs were analyzed.

## 5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) labeling
EdU (5 mg/kg, RiboBio) was administered to mice via intraperitoneal injection. Twenty-four hours after EdU injection, mice were euthanized, and primary AEC2s were isolated. EdU incorporation was detected by flow cytometry using a Cell-LightTM Apollo488 Stain Kit (Code No. C10371-3).

## Quantification of miR-148a-3p expression by quantitative PCR (qPCR)
Total RNA was extracted from the EVs via TRIzol (TransGen Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The RNA quantity and quality were measured with a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer. cDNA synthesis and qPCR were performed with a LineGene9600 system using a miDETECT A Track miRNA qRT–PCR Starter Kit (RiboBio). miRNA primers were synthesized by RiboBio. U6 small nucleolar RNA was used as the internal control.

## Histology
Lungs fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde were paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and subjected to Masson’s trichrome staining. Images were acquired under a microscope.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Comparisons between two groups were performed with the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test. One-way ANOVA was used for comparisons of more than two groups. Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

## Results

## EVs from AMs promote the development of pulmonary fibrosis
EVs were isolated from the BALF of mice with experimental PF and control mice via ultracentrifugation as previously described [ ]. The characteristic sizes and shapes of EVs were determined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and the stable expression of typical exosomal marker proteins in these EVs was confirmed by western blot analysis (Figs. S1A and B). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) revealed that although the average particle size of EVs in BALF from PF mice and control mice was similar (Fig. S1C), a significantly higher level of EVs was detected in BALF from PF mice than in BALF from control mice (Fig. A and Fig. S1D), which is consistent with the findings of a published study [ ]. We then isolated EVs from cultured primary AMs (Fig. B). TEM analysis of these EVs revealed a typical size of 30–150 nm with a characteristic cup-shaped morphology (Fig. C). These EVs were positive for characteristic markers of exosomes, including Tsg101, CD63, Alix and CD9, and did not express the Golgi marker GM130 (Fig. D). NTA further confirmed that although the mean particle sizes of AM-derived EVs were similar, AMs from fibrotic mouse lungs secreted more EVs than AMs from control mice did (Figs. E and F and Fig. S1E). Consistently, a higher level of EVs was detected in culture media of AMs from PF patients than in those from controls (Fig. G). To determine the potential function of AM-derived EVs in vivo, clodronate liposomes were first used to deplete AMs in mice, and AM-derived EVs were then injected intratracheally into these mice (Fig. H). AM depletion reduced the EV content in BALF from BLM-challenged mice (Fig. S1F). Clodronate liposome administration significantly increased mouse body weight, but this effect was reversed by the addition of AM-derived EVs (Fig. S1G). Although depletion of AMs failed to improve the survival rate of BLM-treated mice (Fig. S1H), it attenuated BLM-induced PF in mice, as indicated by decreased collagen deposition, reduced hydroxyproline content and improved lung function; however, all of these changes were exacerbated in BLM-challenged mice treated with AM-derived EVs (Figs. I–K). Taken together, these data suggest that the increased secretion of EVs from AMs contributes to PF progression. 

## Acetylation of MLKL by KAT5 in AMs enhances the production of EVs
We then sought to identify the protein that mediates the increased production and secretion of EVs from AMs during PF. Analysis of a public microarray dataset revealed that the expression of 2,040 genes was altered in AMs during PF (Fig.  A). Among these genes, 7 have been reported to be involved in EV production and secretion (Figs.  A and B and Table ). However, the PCR results revealed that the expression of only Mlkl was significantly increased in AMs from PF mice compared to AMs from control mice (Fig.  C). We performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of AMs from BLM- and PBS-challenged mice. Subsequent t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) analysis revealed distinct transcriptional signatures of AMs from PF mice compared with AMs from control mice (Fig. S2A). The scRNA-seq results demonstrated the increased expression of Mlkl in AMs from BLM-challenged mice (Fig. S2B). Additionally, the MLKL protein abundance was increased in AMs from PF mice compared to AMs from control mice (Fig. S2C). Immunofluorescence staining revealed that MLKL levels were higher in macrophages from PF patients than in those from control subjects (Fig.  D). Similarly, MLKL expression was increased in AMs from PF patients compared with AMs from control individuals (Figs.  E and F). Compared with AMs from wild-type (WT) mice, AMs from Mlkl knockout (Mlkl−/−) mice presented decreased production of EVs (Fig. S2D). The severity of PF was subsequently assessed in Mlkl−/− mice following BLM challenge. Lung fibrosis was significantly attenuated in Mlkl−/− mice, as shown by decreased Masson’s trichrome staining, improved lung function, and reduced levels of hydroxyproline (Figs. S2E–G). To further confirm that MLKL mediates the increased production of EVs in macrophages during PF development, we generated macrophage-specific MLKL knockout mice by intratracheally injecting AAV-Lyz2-Cre virus into Mlklfl/fl mice (Fig.  G). Western blot results revealed deficient MLKL expression in the AMs of the AAV-Lyz2-Cre virus-treated mice (Fig.  H). We found that BLM-induced lung fibrosis was markedly suppressed in macrophage-specific MLKL knockout mice, as revealed by decreased collagen deposition and reduced hydroxyproline levels (Figs.  I and J). Furthermore, the NTA results revealed that the production of EVs was markedly reduced in AMs from macrophage-specific MLKL knockout mice (Fig.  K). These data indicate that MLKL in AMs is responsible for increased EV production during PF.

 

Several studies have reported that the phosphorylation of MLKL contributes to EV production [ , ]. However, we found no observable difference in the phosphorylated MLKL level in AMs from fibrotic lungs compared with AMs from control lungs (Fig. S2H). To identify the regulatory signals that modulate the role of MLKL in mediating EV production, immunoprecipitation of MLKL followed by mass spectrometry (coIP/MS) was performed to identify MLKL-interacting proteins. We analyzed this list of MLKL-binding partners to identify common molecular functions using the DAVID functional annotation tool (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). Acetylation was the most statistically significant function associated with MLKL binding partners (Fig. L and Fig. S2I). Moreover, a significant increase in the level of acetylated MLKL was observed in AMs from PF patients or PF mice compared with AMs from control individuals (Fig. M and Fig. S2J). To identify the acetylase responsible for MLKL acetylation in AMs, we reanalyzed the MS results and found that three acetylases can interact with MLKL: KAT5, ACAT1 and HAT1 (Fig. S2K and Table S2). Among these acetylases, only Kat5 was found to exhibit increased expression in AMs from PF mice compared with AMs from control mice (Fig. N). An in vitro acetylation assay revealed that KAT5 increased MLKL acetylation in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. O). Higher KAT5 expression was observed in macrophages of PF patients than in those of control individuals (Fig. P). RT‒PCR and immunostaining revealed greater expression of KAT5 in AMs from PF patients than in those from controls (Figs. Q and R). Furthermore, coimmunostaining of KAT5 with CD206 (a marker of M2 macrophages) or CD86 (a marker of M1 macrophages) suggested that the increased KAT5 protein abundance in AMs from PF mice was predominantly localized in M2 macrophages (Fig. S2L). We next examined whether KAT5-mediated MLKL acetylation accounts for EV production. Silencing of Kat5 expression in AMs from fibrotic mice decreased the association of MLKL with late endosomes and reduced the production of EVs (Figs. S and T). Moreover, EV production was enhanced in Kat5-overexpressing RAW264.7 cells compared to mock-transduced cells (Figs. S2M and N). However, overexpression of Kat5 in Mlkl-silenced cells failed to increase EV production, indicating that MLKL is responsible for KAT5-supported EV production (Figs. S2O and P). Silencing Kat5 expression increased body weight and improved the survival rate of BLM-treated mice (Figs. S2Q and R) and blocked BLM-induced lung pathology, as indicated by reduced collagen deposition (Fig. U) and improved lung function (Fig. V) and decreased the expression of genes associated with PF, such as Col1a1, Timp1, and Fibronectin (Fig. W). To further explore how KAT5 regulates EV production, we conducted a coIP assay to examine the interaction between MLKL and four components of the endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT) system—Vps28, Vps37A, Vps25, and Vps24—as well as proteins involved in the autophagy-related secretion pathway (LC3) and two other membrane proteins (Flotillin 1 and 2). All of these proteins have been reported to play roles in MLKL-mediated EV production. Importantly, overexpressing Kat5 enhanced the interaction between MLKL and Vps28 and Vps25 but not with the other proteins (Fig. S2S). These findings indicate that elevated KAT5 expression in AMs promotes EV production by inducing MLKL acetylation and increasing its interaction with Vps28 and Vps25.
To determine the sites in MLKL acetylated by KAT5, the acetylation set enrichment-based (ASEB) server was used to predict the potential sites of KAT5-mediated acetylation in the MLKL protein [ ]. Lysine (K) residues 252, 331, 95, 372, 422, 57, and 173 in MLKL were identified as potential targets of KAT5-mediated acetylation (Fig. S2T). Thus, each of these K residues was replaced with arginine (R), and acetylation assays were conducted. The K95R mutant but not the other mutants could no longer be acetylated by KAT5 (Fig. S2U), indicating that K95 in MLKL is a site of KAT5-mediated acetylation. MLKL K95 is a highly conserved site across species (Fig. S2V). Next, we investigated whether acetylation of MLKL at K95 is required for its enhanced endosomal function. RAW264.7 cells with endogenous Mlkl depletion were transduced with lentiviruses expressing MlklWT, MlklK95R (an acetylation-resistant mutant), or MlklK95Q (an acetylation mimetic mutant). As expected, compared with MlklWT cells, MlklK95Q mutant cells presented increased EV production, whereas MlklK95R mutant cells presented reduced EV production (Fig. S2W). In addition, overexpression of Kat5 failed to increase EV secretion from cells expressing MlklK95R (Fig. S2X). Collectively, these data reveal that acetylation of MLKL at K95 by KAT5 in AMs facilitates the EV production function of MLKL during PF.

## AM-derived EVs exert an inhibitory effect on AEC2 renewal
We subsequently aimed to identify which type of cell takes up AM-derived EVs in the alveolar microenvironment. AM-derived EVs were labeled with the fluorescent dye PKH67 and injected intratracheally into PF mice (Fig.  A). Confocal microscopy revealed the uptake of labeled AM-derived EVs by AEC2s but not by fibroblasts, endothelial cells, bronchiolar epithelium or associated stroma (Fig.  B). An in vitro study also confirmed the internalization of PKH26-labeled EVs by isolated primary AEC2s (Fig.  C). We further explored whether AM-derived EVs affect the regenerative capacity of AEC2s. Compared with untreated AEC2s, AEC2s treated with EVs secreted by AMs isolated from BLM-challenged mice presented a lower colony-forming efficiency (CFE) and smaller colony size (Figs.  D–F). Confocal microscopy revealed that the epithelial cells at the periphery of the organoids were SFTPC+ (indicating AEC2s) and that those in the interior region were Podoplanin+ (indicating differentiating AEC1s). In organoids treated with EVs secreted by AMs isolated from BLM-challenged mice, the number of Podoplanin+ differentiating AEC1s inside the lumen was reduced (Figs.  G and H). Moreover, fewer SFTPC+ Ki67+ cells were found in the lungs of mice treated with AM-derived EVs than in the lungs of BLM-challenged mice without AM-derived EVs treatment (Fig.  I). AM-derived EV treatment resulted in reduced restoration of Aquaporin 5 (Aqp5)+ Podoplanin+ AEC1s in the lungs (Fig.  J). The CFE of AEC2s from mice with AM depletion was higher than that of AEC2s from control mice but was lower than that of AEC2s from mice treated with AM-derived EVs (Fig.  K). In addition, the expression of the AEC1-specific gene Aqp5 was significantly decreased in organoids from mice treated with AM-derived EVs (Fig.  L). Taken together, these data indicate that AM-derived EVs from PF mice exert an inhibitory effect on AEC2 renewal.

 

## miR-148a-3p impairs the renewal capacity of AEC2s
Then, we explored the molecular mechanism by which AM-derived EVs suppress AEC2 proliferation and differentiation. We proposed that EVs may mediate the transfer of information between AMs and AEC2s. As miRNAs are important mediators that are abundantly encapsulated in EVs and function well in EV-targeted cells, we assessed the expression profiles of miRNAs in AM-derived EVs. Differential miRNA profiles were found in AM-derived EVs from control and PF mice (Figs.  A and B). Among the top ten upregulated miRNAs in AM-derived EVs from PF mice, miR-148a-3p was the most highly expressed miRNA (Fig.  C). RT–PCR analysis revealed elevated expression of miR-148a-3p in AM-derived EVs from PF mice (Fig.  D) and in AEC2s from PF mice (Fig.  E). We then reanalyzed miR-148a-3p expression in several published GEO datasets and found higher expression of miR-148a-3p or miR-148a not only in lung tissues of PF mice but also in lung tissues of patients with ILD or IPF (Figs.  F–I). 
We then assessed the effects of miR-148a-3p on AEC2 renewal. AEC2s transfected with the miR-148a-3p mimic displayed a markedly lower CFE and smaller colony size than those treated with micrON mimic negative control (miR-NC) (Figs.  J–L). miR-148a-3p inhibitor treatment reversed the decreases in the CFE and colony size of AEC2s induced by EVs derived from profibrotic AMs (Figs.  M–O), indicating that AM-derived EVs suppress the renewal of AEC2s by delivering miR-148a-3p. To further validate the suppressive effects of miR-148a-3p on AEC2s renewal in vivo, mice were treated with the miR-148a-3p antagomir on day 10 after BLM challenge (Fig.  P). The severity of pulmonary fibrosis was much lower in mice treated with the miR-148a-3p antagomir, as demonstrated by the improved lung function (Fig.  Q) and reduced collagen deposition (Fig.  R). Consistent with this finding, treatment with the miR-148a-3p antagomir increased the proliferation of AEC2s (Fig.  S) and enhanced the CFE of AEC2s from BLM-challenged mice (Fig.  T). Collectively, our data underscore the potential role of miR-148a-3p inhibition in promoting LAR and reducing fibrosis.

## EV-derived miR-148a-3p suppresses the renewal of AEC2s by targeting Wnt10b
To further evaluate the molecular mechanism by which miR-148a-3p suppresses the renewal of AEC2s, we sought to identify genes targeted by miR-148a-3p via two miRNA target prediction databases (TargetScan and miRDB). We identified 184 potential target genes overlapping in the predictions of these databases (Fig. A). We then reanalyzed the expression of these potential target genes in the published GSE109913 dataset and found that the expression of 7 genes was significantly different between AECs from BLM-challenged mice and AECs from control mice (Fig. A). Among these 7 genes, only Wnt10b exhibited markedly downregulated expression in primary AEC2s transfected with the miR-148a-3p mimic (Fig. B). Consistent with this result, miR-148a-3p mimic treatment reduced the Wnt10b mRNA level in two lung epithelial cell lines (A549 and MLE-12) (Fig. C). In addition, downregulation of several WNT/b-catenin target genes—Axin2, Nkd1, and Lgr5—was observed in miR-148a-3p mimic-treated AEC2s (Fig. S3A). Wnt10b contains a miR-148a-3p seed sequence in its 3’-untranslated region (3’-UTR). Luciferase activity in cells transfected with the Wnt10b-3’-UTR-WT luciferase reporter was suppressed by transfection with the miR-148a-3p mimic (Fig. D). However, mutation of the 3’-UTR seed sequence abolished the inhibitory effects of miR-148a-3p on luciferase activity (Fig. D). Moreover, the association of miR-148a-3p with Wnt10b mRNA was observed in the complexes precipitated from AEC2s transfected with biotin-labeled miR-148a-3p (Fig. E). Consistent with previous reports [ , ], these data verified that Wnt10b is the direct target of miR-148a-3p in AEC2s.

 

We next examined WNT10B expression in AEC2s from mice and patients with PF. Analysis of our RT–PCR results combined with the published GSE109913 dataset revealed that Wnt10b expression was reduced in AECs from BLM-challenged mice (Figs.  F and G). Additionally, compared to that in AEC2s from control mice, the WNT10B protein abundance was markedly reduced in AEC2s from PF mice (Fig.  H). In addition, the mRNA levels of Wnt10b and miR-148a-3p were negatively correlated in primary AEC2s (Fig.  I). Consistent with this finding, down-regulation of WNT10B expression in AEC2s was observed in the lungs of PF patients (Fig.  J). To determine the role of WNT10B downregulation in promoting PF in AEC2s, AEC2-specific WNT10B knockout mice were generated via intratracheal injection of AAV-Sftpc-shWnt10b into mice (Fig.  K). Immunofluorescence results revealed that the expression of WNT10B in AEC2s was significantly reduced (Fig.  L). The severity of PF was assessed in these mice following BLM challenge. WNT10B-specific knockout in AEC2s had no significant effects on the mortality rates or body weights of PF mice (Figs. S3B and C). However, we found that BLM-induced lung fibrosis was markedly aggravated in AEC2-specific WNT10B knockout mice, as revealed by increased collagen deposition and elevated hydroxyproline levels (Figs.  M and N). Since the mRNA levels of Wnt10b and miR-148a-3p were negatively correlated in primary AEC2s, we further explored whether miR-148a-3p suppresses the renewal of AEC2s via WNT10B. Notably, Wnt10b treatment reversed the miR-148a-3p transfection-induced decreases in the CFE and colony size of AEC2s (Figs.  O–Q). In addition, the expression of Wnt10b and the Wnt/β-catenin target genes Axin2, Nkd1, and Lgr5 was specifically reduced in AEC2s after AM-derived EV exposure and was effectively restored by miR-148a-3p inhibitor treatment (Figs. S3D and E). Moreover, miR-148a-3p inhibitor treatment reversed the decreased nuclear localization of b-catenin in AEC2s exposed to EVs from profibrotic AMs (Fig. S3F). These data indicate that miR-148a-3p in EVs from profibrotic AMs suppresses the renewal of AEC2s via downregulation of Wnt10b expression.
To test the hypothesis that miR-148a-3p in EVs from profibrotic AMs suppresses LAR during PF via Wnt10b/b-catenin signaling in vivo, mice were treated with (1) the miR-148a-3p antagomir or (2) both the miR-148a-3p antagomir and the b-catenin/CBP inhibitor PRI-724. Treatment with PRI-724 reversed the miR-148a-3p antagomir-induced antifibrotic changes in the lungs (Figs. S3G and H). And the CFE of primary AEC2s were functionally enhanced by miR-148a-3p antagomir treatment (Fig. S3I). However, these phenotypes were impaired by the inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin signaling with PRI-724. RT‒PCR analysis of the mRNA expression of the Wnt/β-catenin target genes Axin2, Nkd1, and Lgr5 demonstrated the activation of Wnt/β-catenin signaling in AEC2s from miR-148a-3p antagomir-treated mice, and this activation was suppressed in AEC2s from mice treated with PRI-724 (Fig. S3J). Collectively, these results indicate that AM-derived exosomal miR-148a-3p suppresses AEC2 renewal and LAR by repressing Wnt10b expression.

## Disruption of the MLKL-KAT5 interaction restores the renewal capacity of AEC2s
Given that the KAT5/MLKL interaction results in MLKL acetylation to increase EV production in AMs, which further suppresses the renewal of AEC2s in PF, disrupting this interaction might be a promising therapeutic strategy for PF via the restoration of LAR. CoIP, glutathione-S-transferase (GST) pulldown, and immunofluorescence staining assays verified that KAT5 physically interacted with MLKL (Figs. S4A–C). To map the interaction regions in KAT5 and MLKL, serial deletion mutants of either DDK-tagged KAT5 or GFP-tagged MLKL were constructed. CoIP assays revealed that KAT5 interacted with the M2 domain of MLKL (Fig. S4D). Conversely, MLKL interacted with the Zn finger domain of KAT5 (Fig. S4E). Collectively, these findings indicate that the M2 domain of MLKL and the Zn finger domain of KAT5 are required for the MLKL-KAT5 interaction.
We analyzed the M2 domain of MLKL with the I-TASSER server to predict its secondary structure. Four α-helix structures were found in the KAT5-binding domain (Fig. A). These 4 α-helices were synthesized with N-terminal and C-terminal protective amino acids, and their ability to bind to KAT5 was measured. Peptide M2 displayed a high affinity for KAT5 (Kd = 6.45 nM) (Fig. B), but no interactions occurred between the other α-helices and KAT5. To explore whether this peptide can disrupt the MLKL-KAT5 interaction, M2 and the corresponding control peptide were fused to Pep2, a cell-penetrating peptide [ ], to generate the chimeric peptides PM2 and Pcon. Treatment of AMs from PF patients with PM2 reduced the interaction between MLKL and KAT5 (Fig. C). Additionally, PM2 dose-dependently inhibited the MLKL-KAT5 interaction in HEK293T cells transfected with MLKL and KAT5 plasmids (Fig. D). In addition, acetylation of MLKL in AMs from PF patients was reduced in AMs treated with PM2 (Fig. E). Furthermore, the association of MLKL with CD63 in AMs from PF patients decreased shortly after PM2 treatment (Fig. F). Notably, PM2 reduced EV production in AMs from PF patients (Fig. G). Consistent with the data from PF patients, PM2 disrupted the MLKL-KAT5 interaction (Fig. S5A), suppressed MLKL acetylation (Fig. S5B), decreased the association of MLKL with CD63 (Fig. S5C), and reduced the EVs production of AMs from PF mice (Fig. S5D). Primary AEC2s were treated with culture medium from profibrotic AMs pretreated with Pcon or PM2 (Fig. H). Compared to AEC2s exposed to culture medium from Pcon-pretreated AMs, AEC2s exposed to culture medium from PM2-pretreated AMs exhibited substantial increases in the expression of Wnt10b and its downstream target genes, including Axin2, Nkd1, and Lgr5 (Figs. I and J). The CFE and colony size of AEC2s were reduced when they were treated with culture medium from profibrotic AMs but increased when AMs were pretreated with PM2 (Figs. K–M). In organoids treated with culture medium from PM2-exposed AMs, the number of Podoplanin+ differentiating AEC1s within the lumen was increased (Fig. N). Analysis of the relative mRNA levels of Wnt10b and Axin2 revealed that PM2 treatment significantly decreased Wnt10b signaling in those colonies (Fig. O). Thus, these data confirm that disrupting the MLKL-KAT5 interaction in AMs decreases EV production, restoring the self-renewal and differentiation of AEC2s.

 

## Disruption of the MLKL-KAT5 interaction restores LAR and reduces experimental lung fibrosis
Correspondingly, we further explored the effect of PM2 on PF in vivo. After BLM induction, mice were injected intraperitoneally (i.p.) with PM2 (4 mg/kg) or Pcon every three days for one month and then euthanized for analysis of fibrosis (Fig.  A). Although administration of PM2 did not affect mortality rates, it significantly increased body weight in mice (Figs. S6A and B). As shown in Fig.  B and C, PM2 treatment significantly reduced collagen deposition and improved lung function. Consistent with this finding, mice administered PM2 presented much lower levels of hydroxyproline in the lung (Fig.  D). Next, to confirm that the mechanism of action of PM2 involves the disruption of MLKL acetylation, lungs were collected from mice after PM2 treatment and then analyzed by coIP. As expected, PM2 treatment markedly reduced MLKL acetylation (Fig.  E), which was accompanied by a significant reduction in the EV content in BALF (Fig.  F), a significant decrease in miR-148a-3p expression in AEC2s (Fig.  G) and a significant increase in the expression of WNT10B in AEC2s and AXIN2 in lung tissues (Figs.  H and I). Additionally, PM2 promoted AEC2/AEC1 differentiation in lung tissues from BLM-challenged mice (Fig.  J). Importantly, the CFE of AEC2s from PM2-treated mice was higher than that of AEC2s from Pcon-treated mice (Fig.  K).

 

Next, the therapeutic effect of PM2 on PF was explored in a mouse model of hydrochloric acid (HCl)-induced PF (Fig.  L). PM2 treatment improved the body weight and overall survival of HCl-treated mice (Fig. S6C and Fig.  M). Consistent with this finding, administration of PM2 significantly alleviated HCl-induced PF, as shown by the reduced collagen deposition and improved lung function (Figs.  N and O). Moreover, the HCl-induced suppression of AEC2 proliferation and differentiation was fully reversed by PM2 treatment (Figs. S6D and E).
We further tested the therapeutic potential of PM2 in PF induced by SiO2 (Fig. S6F). Consistent with the findings in BLM- and HCl-induced PF, PM2 protected against the SiO2-induced increase in collagen deposition, decrease in lung function, and increase in lung hydroxyproline content (Figs. S6G–I). We also observed an increased number of proliferating AEC2s and an elevated proportion of AEC2/AEC1 differentiation in SiO2-exposed mice treated with PM2 (Figs. S6J and K). Furthermore, AEC2s in SiO2-exposed mice showed an increased CFE after PM2 treatment (Fig. S6L), indicating enhancement of AEC2 stemness.
Considering the potential off-target effects, we focused on two well-characterized protein interactions reported in earlier studies: the binding of MLKL to RIPK3 or TRIM21 and the binding of KAT5 to STUB1 or cGAS [ – ]. CoIP assays revealed that PM2 did not interfere with the binding of MLKL or KAT5 to their known interacting proteins, suggesting that PM2 does not have obvious off-target effects (Figs. S7A and B). Additionally, to assess whether PM2 influences immune balance in lung tissue, flow cytometry was used to quantify CD4+ T cells, Th1 cells, Th2 cells, Th17 cells, Treg cells, B cells, and plasma cells in the lungs of PM2-treated mice compared with those in the lungs of control mice. No notable differences were observed in the numbers of these immune cell types (Figs. S7C and D), suggesting that PM2 does not disturb immune homeostasis in the lungs.
Collectively, these data demonstrate that PM2 exerts antifibrotic effects in human and experimental models of lung fibrosis by restoring LAR, highlighting the potential for its use in treating PF patients.

## Discussion
EVs are reported to mediate cell communication by delivering bioactive molecules, including proteins, DNA, mRNA, and noncoding RNA, from the donor cell to the recipient cell [ ]. miRNAs, a class of small noncoding RNAs, regulate gene expression by binding to the 3’-UTRs of target genes, resulting in their translational repression and/or mRNA degradation [ ]. miRNAs are reported to be abnormally expressed in PF, suggesting that they play a role in the pathogenesis of PF [ , ]. For example, Guiot et al. reported that exosomal miR-142-3p levels are increased in the sputum and plasma of patients with IPF compared with those of normal control subjects [ ]. Another study, by Sato et al., reported increased EV-coupled miR-21-5p expression in fibrocytes isolated from the BALF of IPF patients [ ]. The identification of miRNAs with aberrant expression in PF and determination of their roles in the pathogenesis of PF may provide therapeutic approaches for PF and other ILDs. Here, we found that AM-derived miR-148a-3p was upregulated in mouse models of PF. miR-148a-3p contained in AM-derived EVs reduced the regenerative capacity of AEC2s by directly targeting Wnt10b and contributing to fibrogenesis. Notably, delivery of the miR-148a-3p antagomir into mice significantly ameliorated BLM-induced PF. Thus, our study demonstrates a pivotal role for miR-148a-3p in lung fibrogenesis and may reveal a potential approach using miRNA therapeutics in fibrotic lung diseases. Future studies are needed to investigate other miRNAs with aberrant expression in AM-derived EVs during PF, as these miRNAs are also likely to be involved in PF pathogenesis.
Our experimental results, along with data obtained from microarray analysis, identified MLKL as a potential mediator of EV production in AMs during PF. MLKL is a pseudokinase originally described as the final effector of necroptosis, a proinflammatory form of programmed cell death linked to diverse inflammatory disorders [ , ]. Recently, accumulating evidence has shown that in addition to its role in necroptosis, MLKL is extensively involved in various human diseases via its nonnecroptotic functions, such as promoting nerve regeneration [ ]; NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and interleukin 1β release [ , ]; regulating gene expression [ ]; and facilitating endosomal trafficking and EV production [ ]. These existing studies therefore shed light on the pleiotropic functions of this protein. As widely reported, the activity of MLKL is controlled mainly by phosphorylation. For necroptosis induction, MLKL is activated by RIPK3-mediated phosphorylation at serine (S) 345 (in mice) or threonine (T) 357/S358 (in humans), followed by phosphorylation at tyrosine (Y) 376, which is mediated by Tyro3, Axl, and Mer (TAM) kinases [ , ]. Tanzer et al. reported that the phosphorylation of S158 in MLKL mediated by unknown kinases plays a role in suppressing MLKL activation [ ]. Moreover, following nerve injury, MLKL phosphorylated at S441 by unknown kinases promotes axon regeneration by inducing myelin breakdown [ ]. Additionally, other posttranslational modifications modulate the function of MLKL. Garcia et al. reported that K63-linked ubiquitination of MLKL at K219 allows MLKL activity to facilitate necroptosis [ ]. Here, we found that increased KAT5 in AMs interacts with and acetylates MLKL to increase its capacity to facilitate endosomal trafficking and the production of EVs. These EVs are taken up by AEC2s to suppress their regenerative capacity and further contribute to fibrogenesis. Moreover, by using glutamine (Q; acetylation mimetic) or arginine (R; acetylation-deficient) mutants of MLKL, we identified K95 in MLKL, a site previously not reported to be modified, as the site of KAT5-mediated acetylation. Notably, KAT5-mediated acetylation of MLKL neither affected its phosphorylation nor altered its induction of necroptosis because the phosphorylation level of MLKL in AMs was unchanged, and no necroptosis was observed in AMs during PF progression. These studies, along with our findings, highlight that alternative modifications control MLKL activity and are likely dependent on the cell type, timing, and extracellular stimulus. The proteins that regulate the posttranslational modifications of MLKL are incompletely identified and need to be further clarified to allow the targeting of MLKL in anti-PF therapies.
As MLKL is a widely recognized protein involved in numerous human diseases, some researchers have attempted to develop drugs to block its function. To date, GW806742X, necroptosis-blocking compound 1 (NBC1), necrosulfonamide (NSA), and TC13172 have been identified as MLKL inhibitors [ – ]. However, these agents have been verified to inhibit only necroptosis mediated by MLKL, and whether they can inhibit nonnecroptotic functions of MLKL remains to be explored. Considering the specific role of MLKL in certain diseases, identifying MLKL inhibitors that efficiently block a specific function of MLKL may be helpful for developing more potent targeted therapies. Here, we found that KAT5-mediated MLKL acetylation facilitated the production of EVs, which played a profibrotic role in PF development. Thus, we generated an α-helical peptide that disrupted the KAT5-MLKL interaction and exhibited a potent therapeutic effect on PF through the suppression of MLKL activity and the subsequent reduction in EV production. This specific MLKL-derived protein–protein interaction (PPI) inhibitor effectively abolished the capacity of MLKL to contribute to EV production. Because KAT5-mediated MLKL acetylation does not affect the necroptotic function of MLKL, it is speculated that this PPI inhibitor cannot be used to treat diseases associated with MLKL-induced necroptosis. Owing to the pleiotropic nature of MLKL, follow-up studies are needed to clarify whether this peptide regulates other functions of MLKL, which may result in severe side effects.
Aberrant expression of lysine acetyltransferases, which catalyze lysine acetylation of histone or nonhistone proteins, has been closely linked to PF [ ]. Here, the expression of KAT5, which belongs to the MYST (named after the abbreviations of the founding members MOZ, Ybf2/Sas3, Sas2, and KAT5) family of acetyltransferases [ ], was increased in AMs from PF mice compared with AMs from control mice. Depletion of KAT5 effectively alleviated BLM-induced PF progression. Mechanistically, KAT5, which was upregulated in AMs from PF mice, interacted with and acetylated MLKL. Acetylation of nonhistone proteins has been reported to affect diverse aspects of protein function, such as subcellular localization, stability, and enzymatic activity. We found that increased KAT5-mediated MLKL acetylation increased the capacity of MLKL to contribute to EV production. Collectively, our results suggest a novel function of KAT5 in AMs in enhancing EV production to promote PF development by suppressing stemness traits in AEC2s, indicating that approaches aimed at silencing KAT5 in AMs could be viable clinical therapeutic strategies for PF.

## Conclusion
In summary, our study revealed that the upregulated KAT5 in AMs during PF progression interacts with and acetylates MLKL and enhances its capacity to promote the production of EVs, which are taken up by AEC2s, thereby reducing the regenerative capacity of AEC2s and contributing to fibrogenesis. Our work provides compelling evidence implicating EVs as key mediators of communication between AMs and AEC2s through the transfer of functional miRNAs, especially in the context of PF, thus elucidating a mechanism underlying PF development and identifying potential therapeutic targets for PF.
