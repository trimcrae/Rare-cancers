A comprehensive understanding of this matter will guide clinicians to optimize acupuncture manipulations to improve acupuncture effectiveness. Recently, keratinocyte Piezo1 channels and cascade ATP release are independently unveiled to be essential for the skin tactile sensation. Thus, we aimed to investigate whether keratinocyte Piezo1-mediated ATP release, in orchestration with CD39, contributed to this initiation mechanism via producing adenosine. 20-min needling was administered at the unilateral ST36 acupoint on the acute ankle arthritis rats. The pain thresholds in the affected hindpaws were determined. Interstitial ATP and adenosine were extracted with microdialysis and assessed by luciferase-luciferin and HPLC, respectively. To simulate needling stimulation, keratinocyte HaCaT cells were subjected to hypotonic shock. ATP release and live calcium imaging were conducted. Immunofluorescence labeling showed the presence of Piezo1 and CD39 on keratinocytes. Acupuncture led to a prompt analgesic effect, as well as a temporary accumulation of ATP and adenosine. Activating adenosine A1 receptors, promoting ATP release by activating Piezo1, or facilitating ATP hydrolysis by potentiating CD39 achieved anti-nociceptive effects. Conversely, altering these modulations in the opposite direction reversed the subsequent effects of acupuncture. Needling-induced accumulation of interstitial ATP parallelly changed with these modulations. Hypotonic shock led to ATP release and [Ca2+]i rise in HaCaT, which were impeded by introducing siRNA-Piezo1, or replicated by agonism at Piezo1. These findings suggest that keratinocyte Piezo1 and CD39 co-mediated ATP mobilization at acupoints contributes to the initiation signals of acupuncture analgesia via triggering adenosine signaling.

## Introduction
Acupoints are the initiation sites for acupuncture (AP) signals, where AP generated-mechanical stimulation, 240–280 mN in force and 10–15 mN/mm in torque, are transduced into the biochemical or bioelectrical signals by touch-receptors or mechanosensitive channels. , , The contribution of mechanosensitive channels at acupoints, such as TRPV1 studied by Y. W. Lin, as well as TRPV2 , and TRPV4 identified by our previous research, to the AP analgesic effect has been confirmed.
Epidermal keratinocytes are essential for the sensations of touch and heat. , However, despite being the predominant cell type in the skin, the superficial layer of the acupoint structure, the role of keratinocyte in the AP effect has not received much attention yet. Certain experts have started to be aware of the irrationality of taking shallow AP as a sham intervention. Piezo1, identified in 2010 as a low-threshold mechanoreceptor, has been newly verified to be the key mechanotransducer for cutaneous mechanosensation. We wonder whether keratinocyte Piezo1 is activated by needling manipulation and contributes to the AP effect.
It's hypothesized by Burnstock G. that the beneficial effects of AP may involve triggering the purinergic signaling cascade via lysis ATP release in acupoints. Subsequently, the role of adenosine (Ado), the product of ATP, and its A1 receptors at acupoints have been well documented in AP analgesic mechanism. Our recent studies in series verified that rather than lysis release, non-lysis ATP secretion was potentiated by needling, which might be prior to Ado signaling. , , Piezo1-mediated ATP release is involved in various physiological and pathological processes, for example, controlling blood pressure and bile secretion. Tactile-induced cascaded ATP release and Piezo1 activation are independently reported on epidermal keratinocytes to be the foundation for skin mechanosensation. , 
Taken together, we hypothesized that keratinocyte Piezo1 channels at acupoints are activated by needling to release ATP, which contributes to AP analgesia as the precursor of Ado. In order to improve this hypothesis, CD39 was consequently studied. CD39 is the prototype NTPDase that represents the major nucleotide-hydrolyzing enzymes, degrading ATP to AMP with intermediate formation of ADP. Our results suggest that keratinocyte Piezo1 and CD39 co-mediate ATP mobilization at acupoints, which contributes to the initiation signals of acupuncture analgesia. This study sheds light on how a fine needle triggers the AP signals and will guide clinicians to optimize the AP manipulations and improve its efficacy.

## Materials and methods
2.1

## Animals
Adult male SD rats weighing 220 ± 20 g were randomly grouped and treated as shown in . All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care Committee of Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Shanghai, China) (protocol code: PZSHUTCM2212270003). 

2.2

## Acute adjuvant arthritis (AA) model
The classic acute AA model was built on the rats, , except for the blank group. In brief, 50 μL complete Freund's adjuvant (CFA) was injected into the left ankle joint cavity after inhalation anesthesia with 1.5 % isoflurane. Inflammatory syndrome appeared within 48 h.

2.3

## Adeno-associated virus (AAV) injection
AAV-rCD39-shRNA (AAV-rat-CD39 shRNA-EGFP) and AAV-NC (AAV-EGFP-NC) were constructed and packaged by HanBio, China. The AAV-rCD39-shRNA (1.3 × 1012 vg/mL) or AAV-NC (1.6 × 1012 vg/mL) were injected into the left ST36 of rats weighing 45 ± 10 g. 3 weeks later, quantitative real time-PCR (qRt‐PCR) was performed to validate the knockdown efficacy and then the AP experiment was started.

2.4

## Behavioral tests
Before the tests, rats were allowed to acclimate to the housing facilities daily for 3 days. Mechanical allodynia (paw withdrawal threshold, PWT) and thermal hyperalgesia (paw withdrawal latency, PWL) were assessed as we usually did. , In brief, PWT and PWL were evaluated by radiant heat (336G, IITC Life Science, USA) and electronic Von Frey anesthesiometer (2450, IITC Life Science, USA), respectively. Time points for their measurements are illustrated in . At each time point, PWT and PWL were measured 3 times at 10 min intervals and the averages were taken as the thresholds.

2.5

## AP treatment and acupoint pre-injection
AP intervention was performed at left ST36 on the conscious and unrestrained rats, as established in our previous work. A stainless-steel needle (0.18 mm × 13 mm, Cloud Dragon Medical Equipment Company, China) was gently inserted at an approximate depth of 7 mm. The manipulation consisting of lifting-thrusting and twisting, lasted for 20 min.
In order to interfere with certain signals at acupoints, antagonists were pre-injected 30 min prior to AP and agonists were introduced 20 min before the behavioral tests.

2.6

## Microdialysis
The linear microdialysis probe (CMA 31, Sweden) was implanted at a distance of 4–6 mm from ST36 of the rat anesthetized with isoflurane, then it was restrained with the fixator to allow wake. After continuous perfusion with Hank's balanced salt solution for a 2 h-equilibration, a 30 min-microdialysalt for Ado assessment or a 5 min-outflow for ATP assay was collected. The detailed process referred to our published work. 

2.7

## Determination of Ado and ATP in the microdialysis
Ado level was performed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) referred to the publication of our group. ATP was determined by luciferin-luciferase (L-L) assay (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). 5 μL L-L mixture was added to each sample, and the luminescence was measured by the microplate reader (Synergy Mx, BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA). Calibrations of Ado and ATP were executed before and after each determination.

2.8

## qRt-PCR
After the rats were euthanized with CO2, the treated ST36 tissues were immediately dissected to separate the skin and the muscle layers. Total RNA was extracted with RNAiso Plus (Takara, Japan). The quantity and quality of RNA were measured using a spectrophotometer (DeNovix, USA). Reverse transcription of the mRNA was performed using the Prime Seipt®Reverse Transcription Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). The resulting cDNA was proceeded to real‐time PCR using TBGreen®Premix Ex Taq™ (Takara, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The primer sequences are listed below. Piezo1(human):Forward:5′-TGAAGCGGGAGCTCTACAAC-3′,Reverse:5′-TCTCGTTGGCATACTCCACA-3’; Piezo1(rat):Forward:5′-GACGCCTCACAAGGAAAGC-3′,Reverse:5′-GGGCAGCATCTATGTCATCC-3’; CD39(rat):Forward:5′-ATGCCAAGTGAAAGGTCCCG-3′,Reverse:5′-TGCTGTTTGGACGCTGGTAT-3’; β‐actin(human): Forward: 5′-GGACTTCGAGCAAGAGATGG-3′, Reverse: 5′-AGCACTGTGTTGGCGTACAG-3’; β‐actin(rat):Forward:5′-TTGCTGACAGGATGCAGAAGGAGA-3′,Reverse:5′-ACTCCTGCTGCTGATCCACATCT-3’; GAPDH(rat):Forward:5′-AAGGTCGGTGTGAACGGATTT-3′, Reverse: 5′-AACTTGCCGTGGGTAGAGTC-3’. Real‐time PCR was performed by real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument (Bioer, China). The mRNA expression level was normalized to that of β‐actin or GAPDH using the 2‐ΔΔCt method.

2.9

## Immunofluorescence staining
The euthanized rats were perfused with 4 % Paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution transcardially (pH 7.4, 4 °C). The dissected ST36 tissues were soaked in the PFA solution and 30 % sucrose solution. Frozen sections (30 μm) were prepared by cryosection (SLEE Medical GMBH, Germany) and processed with 10 % goat blocking serum (Solarbio Life Sciences, China). HaCaT cells were fixed by 4 % PFA (pH 7.4, 37 °C). Then incubated with 0.1 % Triton X-100 and 2 % fetal bovine serum (FBS). The samples were incubated with Piezo1 primary antibody (1:200) (cat#PA5-106296, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) or CD39 primary antibody (1:200) (cat#PA5120870, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) overnight (4 °C). Subsequently, they were incubated in the corresponding secondary antibodies (Alexa Flour 488, 1:200) (cat#34206ES60, Yeasen, China) and counterstained with DAPI reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Finally, they were examined under confocal microscope (Axio Observer Z1, Germany).

2.10

## Cell culture
Human epidermal keratinocytes, HaCaT line (iCell Bioscience Inc, China) were cultured in DMEM (iCell Bioscience Inc, China) which was supplemented with 10 % FBS (Gibco, CA) and 1 % penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, CA). The cells were cultured in a humidified atmosphere of 5 % CO2 at 37 °C and passaged every 3 days by using 0.25 % trypsin (Sigma, USA) containing 0.53 mM EDTA.

2.11

## Small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection
After being seeded in 12‐well plate (3 × 105 cells/well), cells were transfected with 100 nM Piezo1‐specific siRNA (si-Piezo1) or negative control siRNA (si-NC) by using Transheep™siRNA transfection reagent (TranSheepBio, China). 48 h post-transfection, cells were ready for testing. qRt‐PCR was performed to validate the knockdown efficacy.

2.12

## Solutions and reagents
Isotonic solution (IS) consisted of (in mM) 59 NaCl, 5 KCl, 1.8 CaCl2, 1.2 MgCl2, 10 D-glucose, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 25 HEPES and 128 D-mannitol. The pH was adjusted to 7.4 with NaOH. 40 % hypotonic solution (HS) was prepared by removing D-mannitol from IS.
ARL67156 (6-N,N-Diethyl-β-γ-dibromomethylene-D-adenosine-5′-triphosphate trisodium salt hydrate), Yoda1, CCPA (2-Chloro-N6-cyclopentyladenosine), Ticlopidine, Apyrase, AMP-CP (α,β-Methylene adenosine 5′-diphosphate sodium salt) and L-L assay mixture were procured from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). GsMTx4 was purchased from Tocris (Britain). For preparing the stock solutions, Yoda1 was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide, CCPA and Ticlopidine were in methanol, and the rest were in distilled water. All stocks were stored at −20 °C and diluted into a working solution with 0.9 % NaCl (for in vivo tests) or IS (for in vitro tests) when used.

2.13

## Mechanical stimulation on HaCaT
Hypotonic shock is commonly applied to cells in vitro to stretch the membrane. We gently introduced HS to HaCaT, which was used to attach to the bottom of the well-plate or the perfusion chamber, mimicking needling stimulation as we did previously. , 

2.14

## Extracellular ATP (eATP) assessment on living cells
eATP of the living cells was quantified by L-L assay. HaCaT were plated in a 96-well plate (1 × 105 cells/well) overnight and washed with PBS. For kinetic recording, 100 μL HS was gently added to the cells, 100 μL isotonic L-L mixture was immediately added, and luminescence was monitored at a 30 s interval for 25–35 min. For non-kinetic measurement, assessment was carried out after cells were incubated in HS for 10 min. The cells exposed to IS were taken as control. Calibration of luminescence vs. ATP standards was performed with corresponding solutions. Finally, the eATP level was expressed in molar concentration per 106 cells.

2.15

## Assessment of intracellular calcium intensity ([Ca2+]i) in living cells
[Ca2+]i changes in HaCaT were presented as fluorescence F340/F380 ratio of Fura-2 AM captured by MetaFluor software, as described in our previous work. In brief, after being plated on 25 mm-round cover-slip for 24 h, the cells were loaded with 4 μM Fura-2 AM in serum-free DMEM containing 250 μM probenecid for 30 min at room temperature. Then the slide was fixed into an imaging/perfusion chamber (Warner Instruments Co.), and perfused continuously with IS at 1.5 mL/min. HS or IS containing Yoda1 (10 μM) was used to activate the cells. Fluorescence images were recorded at 2 s intervals.

2.16

## Statistical analysis
The data were expressed as mean ± SE when following a normal distribution, and Median (Q1, Q3) otherwise. The figures were prepared by GraphPad Prism 8.0 and analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0. Paired or unpaired t-test was chosen to compare variables between the two groups. One-way ANOVA and LSD test, or Kruskal-Wallis test and Dunn's test was performed for comparison among multiple groups. When data were affected by two factors and the interaction effects were concerned, differences among multiple groups were tested with Two-way ANOVA and LSD test. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results
3.1

## Needling mediated the accumulation of interstitial Ado and ATP at acupoints during AP analgesia
Determination of the microdialysate uncovered that both Ado and ATP transiently accumulated at ST36 during AP manipulation ( a). Interstitial Ado was significantly increased by 30 min-needling and recovered when needling terminated for 30 min. Similarly, in response to a 20 min-intervention, eATP dramatically increased peaking at 5 min and gradually declined towards the baseline value at the termination of needling. Their corresponding total amounts during AP were 2.2 ± 0.3 pmol (n = 8) and 2.8 ± 0.6 pmol (n = 7), respectively ( b). 
Behavioral tests revealed that AA rats severely suffered from pain hypersensitivity, exhibiting tactile allodynia and thermal hyperalgesia, but clearly benefited from 20 min-AP at ST36 ( c and d). Preventing eATP hydrolysis by pre-injection of ARL67156 (ARL, 100 μM, 50 μL), a non-specific inhibitor of ecto-ATPase, impaired the AP analgesic effect. Specific activation of local Ado A1 receptors by CCPA (0.16 mg/mL, 50 μL) duplicated the AP effect.
These results indicate that the involvement of Ado signaling at the treated acupoint in the initiation mechanism of AP analgesia is closely related to eATP accumulation and hydrolysis.

3.2

## Piezo1 channels-mediated eATP accumulation at acupoints contributed to AP analgesia
Behavioral tests ( a and b) demonstrated that the AP analgesic effect was reversed by antagonizing Piezo1 with GsMTx4 (50 μM, 50 μL), and duplicated by Yoda1 (100 μM, 50 μL), a specific agonist of Piezo1 channels. The microdialysate analysis showed that AP-induced ATP mobilization was partially suppressed in the presence of GsMTx4 ( c). Although the suppression of the total amount in ATP did not show significance, the concentration at peak was dramatically decreased from 413.9 ± 112.1 nM to 84.91 ± 28.03 nM (n = 5–7, p < 0.05) ( d and e). qRt-PCR determination showed the presence of Piezo1 mRNA in the skin tissue rather than in skeletal muscles ( f). Further immunofluorescence staining illustrated that keratinocytes, particularly the suprabasal layer, have high Piezo1 expression ( g), which aligns with the report. 
These results imply that Piezo1 channels at acupoints contribute to the analgesic mechanism of AP intervention. And the role of the skin epidermis might not be lightly dismissed.

3.3

## Activation of keratinocyte Piezo1 channels contributed to mechanosensitive ATP release
Since force and torque were generated by AP manipulation, we continued to take cultured human keratinocytes, HaCaT line, to verify whether Piezo1 channels mediated ATP release in response to mechanical stimulation. Immunofluorescent labeling showed that HaCaT cells expressed Piezo1 ( a). Kinetic recordings of ATP-related luminescence showed that cell suspension in HS was brighter than that in IS ( b). Difference calculation revealed that HS induced more ATP release in an analogous parabolic profile ( b inserted), which was similar to the interstitial ATP in vivo ( a). Newly generated eATP reached the peak of 146.0 ± 9.3 nM at 10.4 ± 1.0 min and gradually declined towards the baseline level (data not shown). In the entire HS process, HS promoted extra 173.1 ± 18.9 pmol ATP to release from HaCaT cells (data not shown). Referring to the peak time, 10-min hypotonic bath was performed to efficiently stimulate cells. The results showed that Yoda1 (25 μM) could also trigger ATP release as 10 min-HS did ( c). Moreover, HS had a null effect on Piezo1-siRNA (si-Piezo1) cells, compared with negative control-siRNA (si-NC) cells (n = 11, p > 0.05 vs. HS in si-NC) ( d). 
Since Piezo1 is a non-specific Ca2+ channel, cytoplasm Ca2+ activation in HaCaT cells was monitored. Recordings of [Ca2+]i, indicated by the F340/F380 ratio, captured a transient rise in response to HS (0.46 ± 0.09 in amplitude, n = 6), which was copied by Yoda1 (10 μM) (0.56 ± 0.10 in amplitude, n = 5). While si-Piezo1 cells had a smaller response to HS (0.26 ± 0.01, n = 3) compared with NC cells ( e and f).
These results, together with the above findings obtained from rats, indicate that keratinocyte Piezo1-mediated ATP release participates in AP analgesia.

3.4

## Regulating CD39 activities at acupoints parallelly influenced the AP analgesic effect
Our previous work illustrated the presence of CD39 (NTPDase1) mRNA in ST36 skin. Our immunofluorescence staining in the glabrous skin of the hind plantar showed that keratinocytes displayed high CD39 expression, particularly in the suprabasal layer ( a). Behavioral tests ( b and c) revealed that down-regulating CD39 activities by pre-injection of ticlopidine (100 μM, 50 μL) prevented the subsequent effect of AP. Conversely, exogenous CD39, Ayprase (50 U/mL, 50 μL), relieved hypersensitivity in AA rats. However, Ayprase plus AP didn't show a further potentiating effect. Assessment of the microdialysate uncovered that ticlopidine did not potentiate needling-induced ATP accumulation, and even dramatically suppressed it ( ). While, our previous work had shown that non-specific inhibition of CD39 by ARL had an amplifying effect. 
Ticlopidine was reported to prevent ATP release from human platelets. In order to figure out the truth, AAV-rCD39-shRNA rats were tested. The corresponding results showed that the AP analgesic effect was maintained in AAV-NC rats, but was abolished in AAV-rCD39-shRNA ones ( d and e). Meanwhile, AP-induced ATP accumulation was partially potentiated in AAV-rCD39-shRNA rats, compared to AAV-NC ones ( f).
Immunofluorescence staining illustrated that HaCaT cells highly expressed CD39 ( a). The presence of ticlopidine significantly increased eATP in IS but failed to further potentiate HS-mobilized eATP ( ). ARL increased eATP of cells in IS and dramatically potentiated eATP of HS-treated cells ( b). 
CD39, in orchestration with CD73, achieves the conversion from ATP to Ado. , Thus, we finally tested CD73. Expectedly, specific inhibition of CD73 activities with AMP-CP (300 μM, 50 μL) impaired the AP analgesic effect ( ). 
These findings imply that AP analgesia is partially dependent on the activation of CD39, together with CD73, via hydrolyzing ATP.

## Discussion
4.1

## The treated acupoints were the initial sites for AP coding anti-nociceptive signals
Acupoints dynamically reflect the body's condition. They become sensitized when their corresponding organs or tissues become disordered, and have superiority over non-sensitized points, especially for treating pain syndromes. Similar to the current findings, some related studies have revealed that modulating the signaling at acupoints, for example, TRPV1, influenced the subsequent AP effect. These indicate that sensitized acupoints are the original sites for AP signals.
The skin, as the superficial layer of acupoints, serves numerous functions, particularly sensation and immunological responses. Compared to non-acupoints, acupoints exhibit a higher abundance of mast cells, nerve endings, microvessels, and lymphatic vessels, which are mainly distributed in cutaneous and subcutaneous layers. The current studies showed the presence of functional Piezo1 and CD39 in keratinocytes, where CD73 also expresses. These findings confirmed the crucial role of the skin layer in the AP effect. Moreover, these outcomes partially evidenced the potential effect of shallow AP, a kind of AP manipulation commonly utilized in Japan. In comparison to the deeper AP (30–70 mm) utilized in China, Japanese-based needling is relatively shallow and light (2–4 mm). Shallow AP was once used as a sham control. But recently this adoption is aware of being suppositional. In addition, wrist-ankle AP, developed in China in the 1970s, is also a superficial needling therapy, which is primarily used for acute pain. 

4.2

## Needling-mediated ATP accumulation triggered the AP analgesic signals, potentially serving as the precursor to Ado or as the basis for calcium wave propagation (CWP)
Our series of in vitro and in vivo studies have confirmed the physiological secretion of ATP at acupoints in response to needling stimulation. , Building upon the valuable discovery that Ado signaling at acupoints contributes to the AP analgesic effect, we proposed that needling-mobilized ATP might serve as the precursor to Ado. This guess was confirmed by the current findings that eATP hydrolysis at acupoints was necessary for AP to exert an anti-nociceptive effect. The immunomodulatory effects of Ado have been demonstrated to involve the suppression of the production of inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-12 and TNF-α, but the promotion of producing anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. These may all be the beneficial effects of Ado, recruited at acupoints, contributing to AP analgesia.
Besides, eATP at acupoints may serve as the foundation for CWP to spread the AP signals. In keratinocytes, mechanosensitive ATP release triggers CWP by activating P2Y2 receptors. Our prior study discovered a cascade ATP release from mast cells in response to mechanical irritation, which was mediated by adjacent P2X7 receptors and distant P2Y13 receptors. In animal tests, AP-generated acoustic shear waves can cause CWP at treated acupoints. Mice ST36s present the usually necessary elements for CWP, including P2Y2 receptors, connexin, and pannexin-1 channels. Additionally, our previous work had observed that antagonism at P2Y receptors at acupoints impaired the AP analgesic effect. 

4.3

## Keratinocyte Piezo1 channels at acupoints contribute to AP analgesia via mediating ATP release
In the present work, modulation of Piezo1 channels at acupoints parallelly influenced AP analgesia, which was tightly related to mechanosensitive ATP release from keratinocytes. Partial suppression of GsMTx4 on the interstitial ATP implies the presence of other mechanically gated proteins. For example, TRPV4-mediated eATP accumulation at acupoints was identified in our previous work. Regarding keratinocytes, the involvement of mechanosensitive ATP release , , and Piezo1 channels in cutaneous tactile sensation have been independently verified. The current study provides initial evidence for Piezo1 channels-mediated ATP release in keratinocyte function. This pathway was found to control blood pressure and bile secretion. 

4.4

## CD39, in orchestration with CD73, converted eATP into Ado to participate in AP analgesic mechanism
Exto-nucleotidases are supposed to have an anti-nociceptive effect because they convert pro-nociceptive ATP to anti-nociceptive adenosine. Among them, CD73 , and prostatic acid phosphatase (PAP) have been well documented. They hydrolyze AMP and ATP into Ado, respectively, and provoke a long-lasting anti-nociceptive effect through activating A1 receptors. A study reported that PAP injection at the BL40 acupoint alleviates neuropathological pain. CD39 is the major physiological E-NTPDase member showing a nearly ubiquitous tissue distribution. The hypothesis that E-NTPDases possess an anti-nociceptive effect was based on their co-location with nociceptive receptors in primary sensory neurons. , , 
The present work firstly provided solid behavioral evidence to confirm this conjecture, in which modulating CD39 parallelly influenced the AP analgesic effect and ATP hydrolysis. By the way, Apyrase plus needling did not show synergism, which might be due to the ceiling effect generated by either of them. Surprisingly, ticlopidine exerted a contrary effect on ATP accumulation. CD39 is the dominant ecto-nucleotidase in skin Langerhans cells. Our work demonstrated the presence of CD39 in keratinocytes too, especially in the suprabasal layer. Ticlopidine is reported to inhibit ATP release during platelet aggregation. If it was true in our experiments, ticlopidine would have the potential to decrease interstitial ATP and hinder AP analgesia as well. Our tests in vitro observed that ticlopidine remarkably increased eATP at baseline. As a result, its potentiating effect on mechanosensitive-ATP levels could not be highlighted, possibly due to insufficient chemical potential energy to drive more ATP release. The precise mechanistic reasons are still unclear.
With the assistance of ecto-AMPase, especially CD73, CD39 converts ATP into Ado. The CD39/CD73 pathway has been extensively studied in immunity and inflammation, which exerts a reversal effect by driving a shift from a pro-inflammatory environment to an anti-inflammatory milieu. , In the present work, the antagonism at CD73 impaired the AP analgesic effect, indicating the involvement of the CD39/CD73 pathway in the initiation mechanism of AP analgesia.

5

## Conclusions
We elucidate the keratinocyte Piezo1-mediated ATP release, which contributes to the initiation mechanism of AP analgesia via producing Ado with the assistance of the CD39/CD73 pathway or triggering CWP to locally spread AP signals, as unveiled in our prior research ( ).
