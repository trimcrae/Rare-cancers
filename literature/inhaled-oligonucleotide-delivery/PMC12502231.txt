## Abstract
Glioblastoma (GBM), as one of the most common and aggressive primary brain tumors, pose significant challenges in diagnosis and treatment, highlighting the urgent need for the identification of novel biomarkers, NFKBIE, which may play a critical role in tumor progression, immune modulation, and therapeutic response. We investigated the role of NFKBIE in GBM through both bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation. Initially, bioinformatics analysis based on public databases revealed significant differential expression of NFKBIE across various cancer types, particularly in GBM, where elevated expression was correlated with poor patient prognosis, including overall survival, disease-specific survival, and progression-free survival. Furthermore, genetic alterations in NFKBIE, such as copy number variations (CNVs) and tumor mutational burden (TMB), were significantly associated with tumor progression. In the experimental validation phase, we utilized small interfering RNA (siRNA) technology to silence NFKBIE expression in GBM cell lines, including U87 and T98G. Functional assays were then performed to assess the impact of NFKBIE knockdown. Cell proliferation assays, including CCK-8 and clonogenic assays, demonstrated that silencing NFKBIE significantly inhibited the proliferation of GBM cells. Migration and invasion assays, including wound healing and Transwell assays, further confirmed that NFKBIE knockdown markedly suppressed the migratory and invasive abilities of GBM cells. Additionally, apoptosis assays, such as TUNEL staining, revealed that NFKBIE silencing significantly promoted apoptosis in GBM cells. Western blot analysis confirmed the changes in the expression of key markers associated with proliferation, stemness, migration, invasion, and apoptosis. Furthermore, in a mouse xenograft model, NFKBIE knockdown significantly slowed tumor growth. Histological analysis of the excised tumors confirmed that reduced NFKBIE expression was closely associated with the inhibition of tumor growth. Notably, NFKBIE silencing also led to the downregulation of components of the Hedgehog signaling pathway, suggesting that NFKBIE may regulate tumor progression through modulation of this critical pathway. In conclusion, high NFKBIE expression is strongly associated with GBM progression and poor prognosis, and targeting its expression may offer a promising therapeutic strategy for GBM treatment.

## Introduction
Gliomas are a group of brain tumors that originate from glial cells and are among the most common primary malignant brain tumors [ – ]. According to the Global Agency for Research on Cancer (GLOBOCAN), approximately 260,000 people are diagnosed with gliomas annually worldwide, with incidence rates varying across different regions [ ]. Advances in biomarker research have provided valuable insights for the early detection and targeted treatment of gliomas [ ]. A key marker for gliomas is the mutation of the IDH gene (isocitrate dehydrogenase) [ , ]. Around 70–80% of patients with low-grade gliomas carry the IDH1 mutation, and these patients generally have a better prognosis [ ]. In contrast, gliomas with wild-type IDH, particularly glioblastomas (GBM), tend to be more aggressive and associated with poorer outcomes [ ]. Another important prognostic factor for low-grade gliomas is the 1p/19q co-deletion, present in 50–60% of patients with oligodendrogliomas, who typically experience longer survival [ , ]. Despite these advancements, the mortality rate for gliomas remains high, especially for GBM, where the five-year survival rate is often below 5%, signaling a generally poor prognosis for this tumor type [ , ]. Consequently, the identification and development of novel biomarkers for diagnosing, prognosticating, and treating GBM are crucial.
NFKBIE (NF-κB inhibitor ε) is a key negative regulator in the NF-κB signaling pathway, influencing cell proliferation, survival, and immune responses by binding to the NF-κB complex and inhibiting its activity [ – ]. Recently, the role of NFKBIE in cancer research has gained significant attention, particularly regarding its potential effects on tumor stemness, migration, and invasion. Studies suggest that NFKBIE may play a crucial role in promoting the self-renewal and proliferation of tumor stem cells through modulation of NF-κB activity [ , ]. Aberrant activation of NF-κB signaling is linked to increased survival, drug resistance, and invasiveness in tumor stem cells, while elevated expression of NFKBIE may help mitigate these traits [ ].
The expression of NFKBIE is linked to tumor aggressiveness and prognosis. In certain tumor types, elevated NFKBIE levels are associated with increased invasiveness and poorer outcomes [ ]. For instance, in hepatocellular carcinoma, high NFKBIE expression often correlates with enhanced cell migration and invasiveness [ ]. Moreover, NFKBIE may contribute to tumor immune escape by inhibiting NF-κB activation, which can alter immune responses in the tumor microenvironment [ ]. While existing research highlights the role of NFKBIE in tumor progression, its specific function in GBM remains unclear. Further studies are needed to explore whether NFKBIE could serve as a novel biomarker for early diagnosis, prognostic assessment, and targeted therapy in GBM treatment.
This study aims to investigate the role and underlying mechanisms of NFKBIE in GBM. Specifically, we will examine the differential expression of NFKBIE in GBM and its clinical relevance using bioinformatics. Additionally, we will assess the impact of NFKBIE on GBM growth through various biological function experiments, including tests on proliferation, stemness, migration, invasion, and in vivo tumor transplantation models. We will also explore biomarkers linked to these tumor characteristics to better understand the potential mechanisms by which NFKBIE influences GBM progression. Ultimately, this research will provide both theoretical and practical insights into the role of NFKBIE in GBM and support its potential as a therapeutic target.

## Materials and methods

## Pan-cancer bioinformatics analysis of NFKBIE
The expression profile and potential roles of NFKBIE across multiple cancers were analyzed using an online bioinformatics tool (http://biowinford.site:3838/trial/). This analysis focused on NFKBIE mRNA levels and their correlation with clinical outcomes, including overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI). Additionally, the association between NFKBIE expression and genomic alterations, such as copy number variations (CNV) and tumor mutational burden (TMB), was examined. The tool also investigated the functional implications of NFKBIE in various tumor types. Data from the TCGA and GTEx projects were integrated, facilitating a comprehensive and multidimensional analysis.

## Expression and clinical correlation analysis of NFKBIE in gliomas
The Xiantao Academic Online tool was used to examine variations in NFKBIE expression across different glioma subtypes, including histological type, WHO grade, and IDH mutation status, based on data from the TCGA projects. This approach provided a comprehensive analysis of NFKBIE expression patterns in gliomas.
For the specific analysis of GBM, the mRNA expression data of NFKBIE were obtained from the TCGA database. We compared the expression differences between GBM and normal brain tissues using the XiantaoAcademic Online tool (https://www.xiantaozi.com/). Protein expression data were sourced from the immunohistochemical analysis results in the HPA database, representing protein expression levels in GBM samples.

## Prognostic analysis and modeling of NFKBIE in GBM
In this study, the Sangerbox 3.0 Online Tool was used to examine the correlation between NFKBIE expression in GBM and patient outcomes, including OS, DSS, and PFI, based on TCGA and GTEx data. A prognostic model was then constructed using NFKBIE expression as a Cox factor, utilizing GBM data from TCGA. The model’s accuracy was validated through a calibration curve to evaluate its predictive reliability.

## Correlation analysis of NFKBIE with key genes in the Hedgehog signaling pathway in GBM
In a bioinformatics analysis of clinical samples from GBM patients, we utilized the GlioVis online tool to examine the correlation between NFKBIE and key genes of the Hedgehog signaling pathway [ ]. The results were visualized through a scatter plot, where each point represents an individual GBM sample. The plot includes annotations for the correlation coefficient and p-value.

## Cell culture
The human astrocyte cell line (HA) and GBM cell lines U87, U251, and T98G were purchased from Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd. (HA, catalog number G150) and Beijing Zhongtai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (U87, catalog number ZTB-001; U251, catalog number ZTB-002; T98G, catalog number ZTB-003), all sourced from China. Cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, catalog number 11965-092) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, catalog number 10099141) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, catalog number 15140122) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The medium was changed every 2–3 days, and cells were passaged once they reached 80–90% confluence using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA solution (Gibco, catalog number 25200056). For experimental purposes, cells were harvested during the logarithmic growth phase to ensure consistency in the results.

## Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis of gene expression
Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, catalog number 15596026, USA), and cDNA was synthesized using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, catalog number RR047A, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (TaKaRa, catalog number RR420A, Japan) on a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). The expression levels of NFKBIE, CD133, CD44, SOX2, OCT4, and NANOG were quantified with specific primers designed by Primer 5.0 and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (China). The primers for each gene were as follows:
NFKBIE: Forward 5’-GAGGATGTAGCGGCGTGAAG-3’, Reverse 5’-CTGCAGAGTGGGGTCACAG-3’;
CD133: Forward 5’-TGGAAGGCAAGGAGAAAGGA-3’, Reverse 5’-GAGCAGGGAGGAGGAAGGAG-3’;
CD44: Forward 5’-AGGACACCTGTTGGTGACCT-3’, Reverse 5’-GAGGACAGCCTCGTTGAGTG-3’;
SOX2: Forward 5’-CTGGGAGGTTGTGAGAGGA-3’, Reverse 5’-GTCACAGAGTGCACACAGG-3’;
OCT4: Forward 5’-TGAGGAGGAGAAAGGAAGGA-3’, Reverse 5’-AGAGGAGCAGGAGGAGGAAG-3’;
NANOG: Forward 5’-GAGGAAGGAAGAGGGAAGA-3’, Reverse 5’-AGGAGGAGGAAGAGGAAGGA-3’.
Each 20 µL reaction contained 10 µL of SYBR Green Master Mix, 1 µL of cDNA, 0.5 µL of each primer (10 µM), and 8 µL of nuclease-free water. The qPCR cycling conditions were 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 sec and 60°C for 30 sec, with a final melt curve analysis. Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH (forward: 5’-TGGAGCCACCCAGCAAGG-3’, reverse: 5’-GGAGGAGTGGGAGTTGCTG-3’) using the 2 − ΔΔCt method. All reactions were performed in triplicate, and results were analyzed using QuantStudio™ 5 Software.

## Western blot (WB) analysis of protein expression
Protein was extracted from cells using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, catalog number P0013C, China) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, catalog number 04693159001, Switzerland). The protein concentration was determined by the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog number 23227, USA). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE on 10% polyacrylamide gels (Bio-Rad, catalog number 456–1033, USA) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, catalog number IPVH00010, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against the following proteins: NFKBIE (Abcam, catalog number ab204163, UK), CD133 (Abcam, catalog number ab19898, UK), CD44 (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog number 3560 S, USA), SOX2 (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog number 3579 S, USA), OCT4 (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog number 2840 S, USA), NANOG (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog number 3580 S, USA), Ki67 (Abcam, catalog number ab16667, UK), Caspase3 (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog number 9662 S, USA), Caspase9 (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog number 9502 S, USA), BAX (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog number 5023 S, USA), Bcl2 (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog number 2870 S, USA), SHH (Abcam, catalog number ab53281, UK), GLI1 (Abcam, catalog number ab121195, UK), PTCH (Abcam, catalog number ab53715, UK), PCNA (Abcam, catalog number ab18197, UK), E-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog number 14472 S, USA), N-cadherin (Cell Signaling Technology, catalog number 13116 S, USA), MMP2 (Abcam, catalog number ab37150, UK), MMP9 (Abcam, catalog number ab38898, UK), Vimentin (Abcam, catalog number ab92547, UK), and GAPDH (Abcam, catalog number ab9485, UK). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog number 31460, USA) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by washing and detection using the ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog number 32209, USA). Blots were visualized using a ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA) and protein expression was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA), with GAPDH serving as the loading control.

## Construction of si-NFKBIE cell model
To construct the si-NFKBIE cell model, two siRNA sequences targeting NFKBIE were designed and synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China): si-NFKBIE-1 (5’-GAGGAAACAGGAGAGGGA-3’), si-NFKBIE-2 (5’-GGAAGAGAGAGGAAGGGA-3’). Cells (U87 and T98G) were seeded in 6-well plates (Corning, catalog number 3516, USA) and transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, catalog number L3000001, USA) with 50 nM siRNA. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with complete medium, and the cells were incubated for 48 h. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by RT-qPCR.

## CCK-8 assay
Cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 kit (Dojindo, catalog number CK04, Japan). After 48 h of NFKBIE knockdown, cells were seeded in 96-well plates (Corning, catalog number 3596, USA) at 1 × 104. After 48 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a Multiskan Sky Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The relative cell viability was calculated based on the OD values, and all experiments were performed in triplicate.

## Tumorsphere formation assay
NFKBIE knockdown cells were seeded in 6-well ultralow attachment plates (Corning, catalog number 3471, USA) at 1 × 104 cells per well and cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, catalog number 11330-032, USA) supplemented with 20 ng/mL EGF (PeproTech, catalog number 100 − 15, USA), 20 ng/mL bFGF (PeproTech, catalog number 100-18B, USA), and B27 supplement (Gibco, catalog number 17504-044, USA) without FBS. The medium was refreshed every 2–3 days. After 7–10 days, tumorspheres with a diameter ≥ 50 μm were counted and visualized under a microscope (Olympus, Japan). The number of tumorspheres in triplicate wells was averaged to assess the tumorigenic potential of the NFKBIE knockdown cells.

## Wound healing assay
NFKBIE knockdown cells were seeded in 6-well plates (Corning, catalog number 3516, USA) at 2 × 105 cells per well and cultured to 90% confluence in DMEM (Gibco, catalog number 11965-092, China) with 10% FBS. A linear scratch was created using a p200 pipette tip (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog number 94013, USA), and the cells were washed with PBS (Gibco, catalog number 10010023, China) to remove debris. Cells were then cultured in serum-free DMEM for 24 h, and migration was observed at 0, and 24 h under an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan). The wound area was measured using ImageJ software (NIH, USA), and the percentage of wound closure was calculated to assess cell migration ability.

## Transwell invasion assay
Cell invasion was evaluated using a Transwell assay. NFKBIE knockdown cells were resuspended in serum-free DMEM (Gibco, catalog number 11966025, China) and seeded at 1 × 105 cells per well in the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (Corning, catalog number 3422, USA) pre-coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, catalog number 356234, USA). The lower chamber was filled with DMEM containing 10% FBS. After 24 h of incubation, non-invading cells on the upper surface were removed, and the invading cells on the lower surface were fixed with methanol (Sinopharm, catalog number 100056, China) and stained with crystal violet (Beyotime, catalog number C0121, China). The number of invaded cells was counted under an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan) and quantified from five random fields. This assay assessed the invasive capability of NFKBIE knockdown cells, reflecting their potential for metastasis.

## TUNEL assay
Apoptosis was measured using the TUNEL assay. NFKBIE knockdown cells were seeded in 6-well plates (Corning, catalog number 3516, USA) and cultured to 80% confluence. After 48 h, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, catalog number P0099, China) and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma, catalog number T9284, USA). The assay was performed using the TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime, catalog number C1088, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After incubating with the TUNEL reaction mixture for 1 h, cells were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, catalog number C1005, China) to visualize the nuclei. Apoptotic cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan), and the percentage of apoptotic cells was determined by counting the TUNEL-positive cells.

## CDX mouse model for tumor formation
To ensure the persistence of NFKBIE knockdown in vivo, we used a lentivirus-mediated shRNA system rather than transient siRNA transfection to establish stable cell lines. First, shRNA sequences targeting specific regions of NFKBIE mRNA were designed, with a non-targeting scrambled sequence (sh-NC) serving as the negative control. All sequences were validated for specificity through BLAST alignment. The synthesized shRNA oligonucleotides were annealed to form double-stranded fragments and then cloned into the linearized pLKO.1 lentiviral vector (HonorGene, HG-VRT0356). This construct was subsequently transformed into Stbl3 competent E. coli, and positive clones were identified via ampicillin selection and colony PCR.
The detailed sequences of the shRNA constructs are as follows:
sh-NFKBIE Forward: 5’-CCGG-AGAACCAACCACTCATGGAAT-CTCGAG-ATTCCATGAGTGGTTGGTTCT-TTTTTG-3’.
sh-NFKBIE Reverse: 5’-AATT-CAAAAA-AGAACCAACCACTCATGGAAT-CTCGAG-ATTCCATGAGTGGTTGGTTCT-3’.
sh-NC Forward: 5’-AATTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-CTCGAG-ACGTGACACGTTCGGAGAATT-TTTTTG-3’.
sh-NC Reverse: 5’-CAAAAA-AATTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-CTCGAG-ACGTGACACGTTCGGAGAATT-3’.
The recombinant plasmid was co-transfected with packaging plasmids (pVSV-G and pHR) into HEK293T cells, and the supernatant containing lentiviral particles was collected, filtered, and stored at -80 °C for future use. U87 cells were subsequently infected with the viral supernatant, followed by selection with 2 µg/mL puromycin for 72 h. This process resulted in the establishment of stable NFKBIE knockdown and control cell lines.
The tumorigenicity of NFKBIE knockdown cells was assessed in a CDX model using BALB/c nude mice (Vital River, China). Six-week-old male mice were housed under SPF conditions and received subcutaneous injections of 1 × 106 NFKBIE knockdown cells suspended in PBS (Gibco, catalog number 10010023, China) into the right flank. Tumor growth was monitored twice a week by caliper measurement, and tumor volume was calculated as V = (L × W²) / 2. Mice were euthanized after 4–6 weeks, and tumors were excised, weighed, and analyzed histologically. Ethical approval for animal experiments was granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hebei General Hospital (approval number: 2024-WD-96), and 6 mice were used per group.

## Immunohistochemistry
Tumor tissues from NFKBIE knockdown xenograft mice were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, catalog number P0099, China), and embedded in paraffin. Section (5 μm) were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (Beyotime, catalog number P0085, China). After blocking endogenous peroxidase activity, slides were incubated overnight with a rabbit anti-NFKBIE antibody (Proteintech, catalog number 10271-1-AP, China) at 1:200 dilution, then with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Beyotime, catalog number A0208, China) for 1 h. NFKBIE expression was visualized using DAB substrate (Beyotime, catalog number P0202, China) and counterstained with Hematoxylin (Beyotime, catalog number C0107, China). The slides were observed under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan), and NFKBIE expression was assessed based on staining intensity and distribution.

## Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining
Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and then washed with running water. The samples were subsequently dehydrated in a series of ethanol solutions (50%, 70%, 85%, 95%, 100%), followed by clearing in a mixture of absolute ethanol and xylene, and then in pure xylene. The tissues were then embedded in paraffin after being infiltrated with paraffin wax. For sectioning, slides were coated with poly-L-lysine, and 5 μm thick sections were prepared. After mounting the sections, they were subjected to H&E staining. The procedure involved dewaxing the slides with xylene, rehydrating through graded ethanol, and staining with hematoxylin. Differentiation was achieved using hydrochloric acid alcohol, followed by eosin counterstaining. The sections were then dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. Finally, tissue sections were examined under a microscope at 200× magnification, and three random fields were selected for imaging and analysis.

## Data analysis
Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8 and R 4.2.0. For cellular assays, comparisons between two groups were made with the Student’s t-test and for multiple groups, one-way ANOVA. IHC staining was scored 0–3, and differences were tested with the Mann-Whitney U test. Tumor volume was calculated as V = (L × W²) / 2, and tumor growth was analyzed with two-way ANOVA. Immune infiltration was assessed with the ssGSEA algorithm and Spearman’s correlation. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Data are presented as means ± SD or images.

## Results

## Differential expression of NFKBIE in Pan-Cancer affects tumor prognosis and exhibits multiple tumor biological functions
To investigate the differential expression of NFKBIE across various tumors and its potential clinical significance, we conducted a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis. Our results revealed significant differential expression of NFKBIE in several cancer types, including adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), bladder cancer (BLCA), breast cancer (BRCA), cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CESC), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBC), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC), kidney chromophobe (KICH), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP), acute myeloid leukemia (LAML), low-grade glioma (LGG), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), ovarian serous cystadenocarcinoma (OV), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma (PCPG), prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), rectum adenocarcinoma (READ), skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM), stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), testicular germ cell tumors (TGCT), thyroid carcinoma (THCA), thymoma (THYM), uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), and uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS) (Fig.  A). Survival analysis further showed that NFKBIE expression in BLCA, BRCA, GBM, KICH, LIHC, SKCM, and THYM was significantly associated with overall survival (OS) (Fig.  B); its expression in BLCA, BRCA, GBM, kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC), LIHC, SKCM, and THYM correlated with disease-specific survival (DSS) (Fig.  C); and its expression in BLCA, GBM, KIRC, kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIPP), LUSC, PAAD, LIHC, SKCM, and THYM was linked to progression-free interval (PFI) survival (Fig.  D). It is noteworthy that the correlation between NFKBIE expression and glioma prognosis varies by pathological grade. In GBM, high NFKBIE expression was significantly associated with worse OS, DSS, and PFI. However, no significant association was observed in LGG (P > 0.05), suggesting that the prognostic value of NFKBIE may be limited to GBM.
 Additionally, genetic mutations can influence tumor progression, and our analysis identified copy number variations (CMV) and tumor mutational burden (TMB) mutations in NFKBIE, which showed significant positive and negative correlations across various cancers (Fig.  E and F). Lastly, single-gene Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) functional enrichment analysis indicated that NFKBIE was associated with several key biological pathways, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) signaling, interferon gamma response, and interferon alpha response, across pan-cancer (Fig.  G).

## High expression of NFKBIE in GBM correlates with poor prognosis
Previous bioinformatics analyses have demonstrated that NFKBIE is differentially expressed in gliomas and significantly affects the prognosis of GBM. To further investigate this relationship, we observed substantial variations in NFKBIE expression across different histological type (Fig.  A). As the World Health Organization (WHO) grade of gliomas increased, NFKBIE expression also rose, showing statistically significant differences (Fig.  B). Furthermore, NFKBIE expression was notably higher in wild-type IDH gliomas compared to those with mutant IDH (Fig.  C).
 GBM, as the most aggressive form of glioma, is characterized by high malignancy and poor prognosis, making it a critical subtype for studying the oncogenic mechanisms of NFKBIE. Therefore, we conducted an in-depth analysis of NFKBIE expression in GBM. Our differential expression analysis revealed that NFKBIE expression was significantly elevated in GBM tissues compared to normal brain tissues (Fig.  D). Consistent with these findings, the protein expression of NFKBIE was also substantially higher in GBM than in normal brain tissues (Fig.  E).
Subsequently, we found that increased NFKBIE expression was strongly associated with poorer OS, DSS, and PFI in GBM patients (Fig.  A and C). We developed a prognostic model incorporating WHO grade, IDH status, and NFKBIE expression, which successfully predicted patient survival over 1.3 and 5 years (Fig.  D). Validation of the prognostic model using a calibration curve confirmed its accuracy, though with some discrepancies (Fig.  E).
 

## Expression of NFKBIE in GBM cell lines
To validate our bioinformatics findings, a series of experiments were conducted. First, we compared NFKBIE mRNA expression in GBM cell lines (U87, U251, and T98G) to normal astrocyte cells (HA). Significantly elevated mRNA levels of NFKBIE were observed in the GBM cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 1A). WB analysis further confirmed that NFKBIE protein expression was markedly higher in U87, U251, and T98G cells compared to HA cells (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Based on the high expression of NFKBIE mRNA and protein in U87 and T98G cells, we established si-NFKBIE knockdown models in these cell lines for further experimentation (Supplementary Fig. 1C-D).

## NFKBIE knockdown inhibits cell proliferation and stemness
Subsequent experiments revealed that NFKBIE knockdown significantly inhibited cell proliferation in U87 and T98G cells (Fig.  A-B). WB analysis of proliferation markers indicated a marked reduction in the levels of Ki67 and PCNA proteins following NFKBIE knockdown, with statistically significant differences (Fig.  C). In T98G cells, similar reductions in Ki67 and PCNA protein levels were observed following NFKBIE knockdown (Fig.  D). As tumor cell proliferation is closely linked to stemness, we observed a significant decrease in tumor spheroid formation in NFKBIE-deficient cells (Fig.  E-F). Additionally, bioinformatics analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between NFKBIE expression and tumor stemness in glioma (Fig.  A). Further evaluation of key stemness markers in U87 and T98G cells revealed a significant downregulation of CD133, CD44, SOX2, OCT4, and NANOG mRNA levels following NFKBIE inhibition (Fig.  B-C). Corresponding protein expression analysis confirmed that NFKBIE knockdown resulted in a significant reduction in the protein levels of CD133, CD44, SOX2, OCT4, and NANOG (Fig.  D-E).
 

## NFKBIE knockdown suppresses tumor cell migration and invasion
Cell migration and invasion are critical processes in tumor progression and metastasis. In this study, we assessed the effect of NFKBIE knockdown on the migration and invasion abilities of GBM cells. Cell scratch assays showed that NFKBIE inhibition significantly impaired the migratory capacity of U87 cells (Fig.  A-B). Similarly, NFKBIE knockdown also suppressed migration in T98G cells (Fig.  C-D). Transwell invasion assays further demonstrated that NFKBIE knockdown significantly reduced the invasive potential of both U87 and T98G cells (Fig.  E-H). To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying these observations, we examined the expression of key migration and invasion-related markers. NFKBIE knockdown led to a significant increase in the expression of E-cadherin, while the expression of matrix metalloproteinases 2 and 9 (MMP2, MMP9), vimentin and N-cadherin was significantly decreased (Fig.  ).
 

## NFKBIE knockdown promotes tumor cell apoptosis
In this study, we also investigated the impact of NFKBIE knockdown on apoptosis in tumor cells. TUNEL assay results showed a significant increase in apoptosis in U87 cells following NFKBIE inhibition (Fig.  A-B). This finding was further confirmed in T98G cells (Fig.  C-D). To better understand the molecular mechanisms behind the enhanced apoptosis, we analyzed the expression of key apoptosis-related markers in U87 and T98G cells. The results showed that NFKBIE knockdown led to a significant upregulation of pro-apoptotic proteins Caspase-3, Caspase-9, and BAX, while the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2 was downregulated (Fig.  ).
 

## Knockdown of NFKBIE inhibits U87 cell growth in vivo
To assess the impact of NFKBIE knockdown on tumor growth in vivo, we established a cell-derived xenograft (CDX) model using U87 cells. Tumor volumes were measured at regular intervals (days 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25), and we observed that NFKBIE knockdown significantly inhibited tumor growth over time (Fig.  A). Upon euthanizing the nude mice, tumors were excised and weighed, with the NFKBIE knockdown group showing significantly smaller tumor weights compared to the control group, and the differences were statistically significant (Fig.  B).
 Additionally, histological analysis revealed a reduction in NFKBIE expression in the tumors from the knockdown group (Fig.  C and D). Further examination through H&E staining showed notable structural changes in the tumor tissue of the NFKBIE silenced group (Fig.  E).

## NFKBIE Silencing suppresses tumor growth via Hedgehog signaling pathway
To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the inhibitory effect of NFKBIE silencing on tumor growth, we first conducted a bioinformatics analysis to examine the clinical correlation between NFKBIE and key components of the Hedgehog signaling pathway. In clinical samples from GBM patients, we found a significant positive correlation between NFKBIE expression and the core Hedgehog signaling molecules, GLI1 and PTCH1 (r = 0.5, P < 0.001). However, no significant correlation was observed between NFKBIE and SHH expression (Fig.  A and C).
 To further validate the effect of NFKBIE silencing on the Hedgehog pathway, we performed in vitro experiments using U87 cells. Western blot analysis revealed that NFKBIE silencing significantly downregulated the protein levels of key Hedgehog pathway molecules, including SHH, GLI1, and PTCH1 (Figs.  D). Additionally, molecular analysis of tumors in vivo showed a marked reduction in SHH, GLI1, and PTCH1 expression in the NFKBIE silencing group (Figs.  E). These findings are consistent with the in vitro data, suggesting that NFKBIE silencing suppresses tumor growth by inhibiting the Hedgehog signaling pathway.

## Discussion
Gliomas are among the most aggressive and treatment-resistant tumors, characterized by a high degree of malignancy and poor prognosis, especially in higher-grade cases [ , ]. Recent bioinformatics analyses and experimental studies have highlighted the significant role of NFKBIE (Nuclear Factor Kappa B Inhibitor Epsilon) in various cancers. This discussion synthesizes the findings from our current study on the differential expression of NFKBIE in GBM, its potential as a prognostic biomarker, and its involvement in key biological functions such as cell proliferation, migration, invasion, stemness, and apoptosis. Additionally, we explored the therapeutic potential of targeting NFKBIE in GBM treatment.
NFKBIE expression was notably higher in higher-grade gliomas and in gliomas with wild-type IDH. These results suggest that NFKBIE could be a useful prognostic marker for gliomas, as its expression correlates with tumor grade and the aggressive nature of the disease. In line with this, our survival analysis revealed that high NFKBIE expression is significantly associated with poor OS, DSS, and PFI in GBM patients. These findings suggest that NFKBIE could serve as a valuable biomarker to predict patient outcomes, providing clinicians with better tools to assess prognosis and potentially tailor therapeutic strategies. Moreover, genetic mutations and alterations in CNV and TMB have been shown to influence tumor progression. In gliomas, genetic mutations in NFKBIE were found to be associated with both positive and negative correlations with CNV and TMB. These correlations suggest that NFKBIE expression could serve not only as a prognostic marker but also as an indicator of tumor genetic landscape, providing insight into the molecular mechanisms driving tumor progression.
Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) functional enrichment analysis reveals that NFKBIE is associated with several key biological pathways across various cancers, including the Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α) signaling pathway, interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) response, and interferon-alpha (IFN-α) response. These pathways play crucial roles in tumor immune responses and the tumor microenvironment (TME), suggesting that NFKBIE may influence tumor immune evasion, inflammation, and progression by modulating these pathways.
The TNF-α signaling pathway is a critical component of the tumor microenvironment, known for its dual role in promoting tumor cell apoptosis, migration, and immune evasion [ ]. TNF-α not only activates pro-inflammatory responses but also suppresses tumor growth by inducing anti-tumor immune reactions. However, tumor cells often manipulate the TNF-α pathway to escape host immune surveillance. For example, studies have shown that both the tumor stroma and TME continuously produce endogenous TNF-α, which induces angiogenesis and promotes tumor progression [ ]. It is likely that NFKBIE influences the TNF-α signaling pathway to modulate the tumor immune microenvironment and facilitate tumor progression.
The IFN-γ response pathway also plays a critical role in anti-tumor immunity, exhibiting a dual function in immune regulation and anti-tumor responses [ ]. On one hand, IFN-γ acts as a key immune cytokine, activating T cells and macrophages to enhance anti-tumor immune responses [ ]. Studies have demonstrated that IFN-γ can improve the efficacy of immune therapies. On the other hand, IFN-γ can promote immune evasion in the tumor microenvironment by inducing the expression of PD-L1 and IDO, and stimulating immune editing [ , ]. Recent research indicates that tumor cells alter the expression patterns of the IFN-γ response pathway to escape immune surveillance, supporting the potential role of NFKBIE in tumor immune evasion. NFKBIE may modulate IFN-γ signaling to either promote immune evasion or accelerate tumor progression by suppressing immune responses. The enrichment of these pathways is closely associated with NFKBIE expression, suggesting that NFKBIE plays multiple roles in tumor immune responses. By regulating these immune-related pathways, NFKBIE may promote immune evasion mechanisms in tumor cells, thus influencing tumor growth and patient prognosis. Notably, as a potential immune modulator, NFKBIE could serve as an important target for the development of novel immunotherapeutic strategies.
These findings further highlight the role of NFKBIE in the cancer immune microenvironment, suggesting its potential impact on tumor immune evasion, inflammatory responses, and immune system regulation. Future research should explore the specific mechanisms by which NFKBIE affects these immune pathways, particularly how it modulates the TNF-α and IFN-α/γ response pathways, offering new therapeutic targets for cancer immunotherapy.
The regulation of tumor cell growth and stemness is a critical factor in the aggressiveness of GBM [ , ]. Cancer stem cells (CSCs) are thought to contribute to tumor recurrence, metastasis, and resistance to treatment [ , ]. Our study provides compelling evidence that NFKBIE plays a key role in regulating GBM cell growth and stemness. Inhibition of NFKBIE in U87 cells led to a significant reduction in cell proliferation, as demonstrated by decreased levels of Ki67 and PCNA, markers commonly used to assess cell proliferation. Moreover, tumor sphere formation assays revealed that NFKBIE inhibition markedly reduced the ability of GBM cells to form spheres, a hallmark of stemness. These findings suggest that NFKBIE plays a critical role in maintaining the proliferative capacity and stem-like properties of GBM cells. Furthermore, our analysis of key stemness markers showed that the expression of CD133, CD44, SOX2, OCT4, and NANOG was significantly reduced following NFKBIE inhibition. These markers are well-established indicators of cancer stem cells and are crucial for maintaining the stem-like properties of tumors [ , ]. The reduction in their expression following NFKBIE knockdown suggests that NFKBIE is essential for maintaining GBM stemness, further supporting its role in tumor progression and resistance to therapy. Inhibition of NFKBIE, therefore, may not only suppress tumor cell proliferation but also target GBM stem cells, which are often resistant to conventional therapies. This dual effect makes NFKBIE a promising therapeutic target in GBM, as its inhibition could reduce tumor growth and prevent recurrence by targeting the cancer stem cell population.
The ability of tumor cells to migrate and invade surrounding tissues is a critical step in metastasis, and it is often associated with poor prognosis in cancer patients [ ]. In GBM, tumor cell migration and invasion contribute to the invasive nature of the disease, making it difficult to treat effectively [ , ]. Our study demonstrated that NFKBIE inhibition significantly impaired the migration and invasion of GBM cells. This was confirmed by both scratch wound healing and Transwell assays, which showed reduced migratory and invasive potential in NFKBIE-depleted U87 cells. To further investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying these observations, we examined the expression of key migration- and invasion-related biomarkers. We found that NFKBIE inhibition led to a significant reduction in the expression of epithelial cadherin (E-cadherin), matrix metalloproteinases 2 and 9 (MMP2, MMP9), vimentin, and N-cadherin. These molecules are critical mediators of cell adhesion, motility, and tissue remodeling, all of which are involved in tumor invasion [ , ]. The reduction in their expression following NFKBIE knockdown suggests that NFKBIE is involved in regulating the molecular pathways that govern tumor cell migration and invasion. Given that GBM are highly invasive tumors, the ability to suppress migration and invasion is an important therapeutic goal. Targeting NFKBIE may provide a novel approach to limit GBM metastasis and prevent the spread of tumor cells to surrounding tissues. This could improve patient outcomes by reducing the likelihood of tumor recurrence and resistance to treatment.
Apoptosis, or programmed cell death, is a crucial mechanism for maintaining cellular homeostasis and eliminating damaged or aberrant cells [ ]. In cancer, the evasion of apoptosis allows tumor cells to survive and proliferate uncontrollably [ ]. In our study, we observed that inhibition of NFKBIE significantly promoted apoptosis in GBM cells, as evidenced by increased TUNEL staining and upregulation of pro-apoptotic proteins Caspase-3, Caspase-9, and BAX. Conversely, the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2 was downregulated following NFKBIE knockdown, indicating a shift toward apoptosis. The ability of NFKBIE inhibition to induce apoptosis in GBM cells suggests that it may play a protective role in tumor cells by preventing programmed cell death. This finding highlights the potential of NFKBIE as a therapeutic target, as its inhibition could enhance the sensitivity of GBM cells to apoptosis, thereby improving the efficacy of existing therapies. By promoting cell death in tumor cells, NFKBIE inhibition may offer a novel strategy to combat GBM resistance to conventional treatments such as chemotherapy and radiation.
To evaluate the in vivo effects of NFKBIE inhibition, we established a CDX model using U87 cells. Tumor growth was significantly inhibited in NFKBIE knockdown mice, with tumor volumes and weights reduced compared to controls. Histological analysis confirmed reduced NFKBIE expression in the tumors, along with downregulation of key tumor-associated proteins, such as SHH, GLI1, and PTCH, which are involved in the Sonic Hedgehog signaling pathway. This pathway plays a critical role in regulating stem cell fate and tumor growth in GBM [ ], and its modulation by NFKBIE highlights a potential mechanism through which NFKBIE drives tumor progression. These in vivo results further support the therapeutic potential of targeting NFKBIE in GBM. By inhibiting NFKBIE expression, it may be possible to suppress tumor growth and overcome the resistance mechanisms that contribute to GBM recurrence and metastasis.

## Conclusion
This study demonstrates that NFKBIE plays a critical role in the development of GBM through multiple mechanisms, including modulation of immune cell infiltration, promotion of tumour cell growth and stemness, enhancement of migration and invasion, and prevention of apoptosis. Therefore, targeting NFKBIE could be a novel therapeutic approach to reduce tumour growth in GBM patients.
