## Objective
This study aims to elucidate the mechanisms by which nanovesicles (NVs) transport curcumin(CUR) across the blood–brain barrier to treat hypothalamic neural damage induced by heat stroke by regulating the expression of poly(c)-binding protein 2 (PCBP2).

## Methods
Initially, NVs were prepared from macrophages using a continuous extrusion method. Subsequently, CUR was loaded into NVs using sonication, yielding engineered cell membrane Nanovesicles loaded with curcumin (NVs-CUR), which were characterized and subjected to in vitro and in vivo tracking analysis. Evaluations included assessing the toxicity of NVs-CUR using the MTT assay, evaluating neuroprotection of NVs-CUR against H2O2-induced oxidative stress damage in PC12 cells, examining effects on cell morphology and quantity, and detecting ferroptosis-related markers through Western blot and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Proteomic analysis was conducted on PC12 cells treated with NVs (n = 3) and NVs-CUR (n = 3) to identify downstream key factors. Subsequently, the expression of key factors was modulated, and rescue experiments were performed to validate the impact of NVs-CUR through the regulation of key factor expression. Furthermore, a mouse model of hypothalamic neural damage induced by heat stroke was established, where CUR, NVs-CUR, and ferroptosis inducer Erastin were administered to observe mouse survival rates, conduct nerve function deficit scoring, perform histological staining, and measure levels of inflammation and oxidative stress factors in hypothalamic tissue.

## Results
NVs-CUR was successfully prepared with excellent stability, serving as an advantageous drug delivery system that effectively targets brain injury sites or neurons both in vitro and in vivo. Subsequent in vitro cell experiments demonstrated the biocompatibility of NVs-CUR, showing superior protective effects against H2O2-induced PC12 cell damage and reduced ferroptosis compared to CUR. Moreover, in the mouse model of hypothalamic neural damage induced by heat stroke, NVs-CUR exhibited enhanced therapeutic effects. Proteomic analysis revealed that NVs-CUR exerted its effects through the regulation of key protein PCBP2; silencing PCBP2 reversed the protective effect of NVs-CUR on neural damage and its inhibition of ferroptosis. Additionally, NVs-CUR regulated solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) expression by PCBP2; overexpression of SLC7A11 reversed the promotion of neural damage and ferroptosis by silencing PCBP2. Animal experiments indicated that ferroptosis inducers reversed the improved survival and nerve function observed with NVs-CUR, silencing PCBP2 reversed the ameliorative effects of NVs-CUR on hypothalamic neural injury induced by heat stroke, and overexpression of SLC7A11 further reversed the adverse effects of silencing PCBP2 on hypothalamic neural injury induced by heat stroke. This suggests that NVs-CUR alleviates hypothalamic neural damage induced by heat stroke by targeting the PCBP2/SLC7A11 axis to reduce neuronal ferroptosis.

## Conclusion
This study successfully developed engineered cell membrane NVs-CUR with neuron-targeting properties. NVs-CUR increased the expression of PCBP2, maintained the stability of SLC7A11 mRNA, reduced ferroptosis, and ultimately alleviated hypothalamic neuroinflammation induced by heatstroke.

## Introduction
Heat stroke is a severe heat stress-related illness that commonly occurs in high-temperature environments and is characterized by rapid elevation of body temperature, central nervous system dysfunction, and multi-organ damage [ – ]. Early symptoms such as dizziness, headache, nausea, and vomiting are often overlooked, but if not promptly intervened, they can progress to neurological damage, acute brain disease, and even death [ , ]. Notably, hypothalamic neural damage plays a crucial role in the pathophysiological process of heat stroke due to its critical role in temperature regulation and neuroendocrine function. Hence, investigating the pathogenic mechanisms of neural damage in heat stroke and exploring effective treatment strategies are of paramount importance for reducing patient suffering and improving treatment outcomes [ , ].
As a multifunctional RNA-binding protein, PCBP2 plays a pivotal role in cellular growth, proliferation, and apoptosis [ , ]. Recent studies have demonstrated the significant role of PCBP2 in regulating intracellular iron homeostasis and the ferroptosis pathway [ , ]. Iron is an essential element in cellular activities, and ferroptosis, as a critical pathway for cell death, is vital for maintaining intracellular iron balance [ , ]. However, when ferroptosis is dysregulated, it can lead to iron overload within cells, exacerbating neuronal damage, especially in cases of neural system impairment in heat stroke. Therefore, the regulatory function of PCBP2 not only pertains to the balance of fundamental cellular activities and directly impacts neural health and disease development [ ]. This discovery offers important insights for further exploring the association between iron metabolism and neurological diseases, providing new avenues for exploring novel therapeutic strategies and interventions in the future.
Nanovesicles (NVs) as an innovative drug delivery system show immense potential in the field of drug delivery [ ]. By adjusting their surface membrane proteins, NVs can achieve targeted drug delivery, enhancing drug specificity and bioavailability, thereby boosting therapeutic efficacy [ ]. Curcumin (CUR), a natural antioxidant and anti-inflammatory substance, has been extensively studied and confirmed to possess the potential to protect against neuronal damage [ ]. Therefore, investigating the utilization of NVs for CUR delivery to effectively protect and repair neurons is of significant importance in treating hypothalamic neural damage in heat stroke [ , ]. This novel drug delivery system not only shields the drug from degradation in the body but also enhances its accumulation in target tissues, maximizing the therapeutic effects of CUR [ , ]. Thus, exploring the application prospects of the CUR delivery system utilizing NVs as carriers in treating neural damage holds promise for clinical advancements [ – ].
This study aims to investigate the efficacy and mechanism of action of NVs-CUR in treating hypothalamic neural damage induced by heat stroke. Through a combination of in vitro and in vivo experiments, we will comprehensively analyze the protective effects of NVs-CUR on PC12 neuronal cells, focusing on its role in modulating the PCBP2 and ferroptosis pathways. By establishing animal models to validate the effectiveness of NVs-CUR in treating neural damage caused by heat stroke, we will further explore its potential clinical applications. This research not only enables a thorough evaluation of the therapeutic efficacy of NVs-CUR as a drug delivery system but also promises to reveal its profound impact on neuronal protection mechanisms [ ]. By elucidating the role of NVs-CUR in regulating the PCBP2 and ferroptosis pathways, we aim to provide new therapeutic strategies for neuroprotective treatments. Therefore, this study holds significant clinical significance for the treatment and prevention of neural damage associated with heat stroke, providing robust support for future translational medical research.
In summary, this study aims to investigate the mechanistic role of NVs-CUR in heatstroke-induced neural damage and their therapeutic potential. The findings reveal that NVs-CUR regulates PCBP2 and its downstream factor SLC7A11 to mitigate neuronal ferroptosis, offering a novel perspective on the pathological mechanisms of heatstroke-induced neural injury and providing a scientific basis for developing clinical treatment strategies. This research holds promise for bringing breakthroughs and hope to the treatment of heatstroke patients.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture and cell membrane separation
Mouse RAW264.7 cells (SNL-112) were purchased from the Shanghai Institute of Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (D5030, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (10100147, Invitrogen, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (10378016, Invitrogen, USA) under conditions of 37 °C and 5% CO2. Upon reaching confluence, adherent cells were detached using a cell scraper. Approximately 1 × 107 cells were collected and centrifuged, and the pellet was resuspended in PBS buffer to obtain a single-cell suspension for subsequent preparation of NVs.
Macrophages were exposed to a hypotonic solution to disrupt the cell membrane, and morphological changes before and after rupture were observed under bright-field microscopy. Subsequently, a Dounce homogenizer was used to disrupt cell structures fully. The membranes were collected by density gradient centrifugation and stored at – 80 °C for further experiments [ ].

## Isolation of NVs using continuous extrusion method
NVs were isolated from macrophages (RAW264.7) using a continuous extrusion method. In brief, after 3 days of cell culture, the cell suspension was subjected to extrusion using a small extruder machine (Avanti Polar Lipids, USA) equipped with a 100 nm pore size polycarbonate (PC) membrane filter (Whatman, UK). The suspension was extruded 5 times successively through PC membranes with pore sizes of 10 μm, 5 μm, and 1 μm. Subsequently, centrifugation was carried out at 2000 g and 20,000 g for 15 min each. Following a wash in PBS solution (centrifuged at 200,000 g for 2 h), the NVs were resuspended in a 5% trehalose PBS buffer solution (C1049, Beijing Baoosen Biological Technology Co., Ltd).

## Preparation of macrophage membrane NVs-CUR
CUR loading into NVs was achieved using sonication as follows: Initially, a stock solution of 30 mg/mL CUR (C7727, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Various concentrations of CUR (0.14, 0.42, 0.56, and 0.69 mg/mL) were added to 0.28 μg/μL NVs, with a PBS to DMSO mixture ratio of 2:1 (v/v). Three methods were evaluated for encapsulating CUR into NVs: freeze–thaw cycles, sonication, and room-temperature incubation. In the freeze–thaw method, the CUR solution was mixed with NVs, incubated for 30 min, rapidly frozen at – 80 °C for 8 min, and then thawed at room temperature; this freeze–thaw cycle was repeated three times. For sonication, the drug-NV mixture was sonicated for 15 min (at a frequency of 2 kHz, power of 70%) (KQ-600 KDE, Kunshan Ultrasonic Instruments Co., China), cooled at − 20 °C for 5 min, followed by another round of sonication. In the case of room temperature incubation, the drug-NV mixture was incubated at room temperature for 2 h. The purification of NVs-CUR was carried out using a two-step gradient centrifugation process. Initially, centrifugation at 8000 g for 15 min at 4 °C was performed to separate unloaded CUR, followed by centrifugation at 20,000 g for 15 min to obtain a brownish precipitate, identified as NVs-CUR, which was then resuspended in PBS.

## Characterization and stability analysis of NVs-CUR
Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM): 20 µl of NVs were dropped onto a copper grid and allowed to stand for 3 min. The liquid was then gently absorbed from the side using filter paper, followed by the addition of 30 μL of phosphotungstic acid solution (pH 6.8) (79690, Merck, USA). After incubating at room temperature for 5 min, the sample was air-dried under incandescent light. Subsequently, observation was conducted using a transmission electron microscope (JEM-1011, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) under an accelerating voltage of 80 kV, and images were captured utilizing the side-mounted Camera-Megaview III (Soft Imaging System, Münster, Germany).
Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA): NVs samples were resuspended in PBS and diluted 500 times with Milli-Q water. The diluted NVs were then injected into the sample chamber of the NanoSight LM10 (Malvern, UK) instrument using a sterile syringe to ensure the absence of air bubbles and to fill the chamber completely. The videos were analyzed using NanoSight version 2.3 (Malvern, UK) software with a gain of 6.0 and a threshold of 11. The software tracked the motion of particles, outputting concentration and size distribution plots for the diluted sample, from which the original concentration of NVs was calculated based on the dilution factor.
Identification of NVs surface markers with Western blot: NVs were resuspended in RIPA lysis buffer (R0010, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and subjected to Western blot analysis to detect surface markers including CD9, CD81, CD63, and Alix, as well as the endoplasmic reticulum marker Calnexin. Antibody information for each marker can be found in the subsequent Western blot section. Each experiment was repeated three times [ ].

## Determination of drug loading efficiency
To quantitatively determine the amount of CUR incorporated, purified NVs-CUR were collected, and the absorbance of CUR at 436 nm was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (U-3900, Hitachi, Japan). Subsequently, the respective CUR concentration in each NVs-CUR sample was calculated based on CUR’s calibration curve. Next, the drug encapsulation efficiency (EE) and loading capacity (LC) of NVs were calculated according to the following equations: EE = CNVs-CUR/Cinitial; LC = CNVs-CUR/(Cinitial + NNVs). Here, CNVs-CUR represents the amount of CUR in NVs-CUR, Cinitial is the initial amount of CUR added to the formulation, and NNVs is the amount of NVs.

## Drug release kinetics determination
A specific amount of FITC NVs-CUR was added to a release medium of physiological saline, and the temperature was set to 37 °C to mimic human body conditions. At predetermined time points (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48 h), samples of a certain volume were withdrawn from the release medium, and the concentration of CUR in the release medium was measured using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (ND-1000, Nanodrop, Thermo Fisher, USA). The fluorescence intensity of FITC is correlated with the concentration of CUR, allowing the determination of the release rate through colorimetric analysis. By comparing the released concentrations at different time points with the initial loading amount, the proportion of CUR released within 48 h was calculated [ ].

## Cellular uptake of drug-loaded NVs
In order to investigate the internalization and intracellular distribution of NVs-CUR, Dil-labeled NVs-CUR were utilized to examine the uptake by PC12 cells (SNL-124, obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank). 1,10-Dioctadecyl-3,3,30,30-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate (Dil) (465498, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was employed as a fluorescent dye to label NVs-CUR. Dil red fluorescent cell membrane probe (10 mM) was added to the suspension of NVs-CUR and incubated for 20 min to prepare Dil-labeled NVs-CUR. PC12 cells were cultured overnight in a confocal laser dish. When the cell density reached 70–80%, Dil-labeled NVs-CUR was introduced into the culture medium and co-incubated with PC12 cells. DAPI was used to stain the cell nuclei. At 1, 3, and 6 h post-incubation, the distribution of Dil-labeled NVs-CUR in PC12 cells was observed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Synergy-2, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, Vermont, USA).

## Cell viability and toxicity assays of nucleic vesicles (NVs)
PC12 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with heat-inactivated 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin and then incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Initially, the cytotoxicity of blank NVs at concentrations of 5–25 mg/mL on PC12 cells was evaluated using the MTT assay. PC12 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 104 cells per well. Upon cell adhesion, NVs-CUR were diluted in the culture medium to concentrations of 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 mg/mL. The culture medium served as the blank control. After 48 h of cell treatment, 20 µl MTT solution (5 mg/mL, 0.5% MTT) from Sigma-Aldrich was added to each well and incubated for 4 h. Subsequently, the liquid in the wells was aspirated and replaced with 150 µl DMSO. After shaking at low speed for 10 min, the optical density was measured at a wavelength of 490 nm using a microplate reader to calculate cell survival rates.
To assess the protective effect of NVs-CUR on PC12 cells, the influence of H2O2 (HX0640, Supelco, USA) on PC12 cell viability was determined using the MTT assay. Four hours after the H2O2 addition, PC12 cells were treated with PBS, CUR, and NVs-CUR. Subsequently, PC12 cell viability and proliferation were assessed using the MTT assay, with CUR and NVs-CUR both applied at a concentration of 100 ng/mL. Axonal growth of each well was observed and captured under a Nikon ECLIPSE80i (Nikon, Japan) microscope after 5 days.

## Construction of lentivirus vectors for silence and overexpression
The potential short hairpin RNA (shRNA) target sequences for mouse cDNA were analyzed based on GenBank. Three sequences targeting PCBP2 and SLC7A11 were designed, along with a negative control lacking interfering sequences (sh-NC), with primer sequences listed in Table S1. The oligonucleotides were synthesized by Genewiz and used to construct a lentivirus packaging system via lentivirus interference vector LV-1 (pGLVU6/GFP) (C06001, Genewiz, China).
Human embryonic kidney 293t cells (HEK293T) cell line was obtained from ATCC (CRL-3216). The packaging virus and the target vector were co-transfected into human embryonic kidney cells HEK293T using lipofectamine 2000 (cell confluency 80–90%). After 48 h of cell culture, the supernatant was collected, containing viral particles after filtration and centrifugation. Virus titers were determined by collecting the virus in the logarithmic growth phase. The lentivirus vector overexpressing PCBP2 was constructed and packaged by Genewiz [ ].

## Cell transfection and grouping
During the logarithmic growth phase of cells, digestion with trypsin and trituration were performed to prepare a cell suspension at a concentration of 5 × 104 cells/mL, which was then seeded into 6-well plates at 2 mL per well. Prior to cell grouping, lentivirus particles (multiplicity of infection, MOI = 10, viral titer of 1 × 108 TU/mL) were added to the cell culture medium containing the surfactant polybrene (TR-1003, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and incubated for 48 h. Stable cell lines were selected with 2 µg/mL puromycin (HY-K1057, Med Chem Express, USA) over a period of 2 weeks. RNA and protein levels were assessed 48 h post-transfection to confirm silencing or overexpression efficiency. The treatment concentrations for CUR and NVs-CUR were both 100 ng/mL.
PC12 cell grouping was conducted as follows: Control group (blank control), H2O2 group (treated with H2O2 for 4 h), CUR group (treated with CUR after H2O2 treatment for 4 h), NVs-CUR group (treated with NVs-CUR after H2O2 treatment for 4 h).
Within the H2O2 group of PC12 cells, further subgroups were designated as follows: (1) sh-NC group (transfected with lentivirus sh-NC), sh-PCBP2 group (transfected with lentivirus sh-PCBP2), oe-NC group (transfected with lentivirus oe-NC), or-PCBP2 group (transfected with lentivirus oe-PCBP2); (2) NVs-CUR + sh-NC group (treated with NVs-CUR and transfected with lentivirus sh-NC simultaneously), NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 group (treated with NVs-CUR and transfected with lentivirus sh-PCBP2 simultaneously); (3) sh-NC group (transfected with lentivirus sh-NC), sh-SLC7A11 group (transfected with lentivirus sh-SLC7A11). Among these, the NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 group of PC12 cells was further subdivided into: oe-NC group (transfected with lentivirus oe-NC), oe-SLC7A11 group (transfected with lentivirus oe-SLC7A11) [ ].

## Determination of reduced glutathione (GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG)
Cell samples were treated with trypsin, and cell suspensions were prepared by centrifugation to remove debris. The samples were then lysed using a cold freeze–thaw solution. The levels of intracellular GSH and GSSG were measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the commercially available GSH and GSSG assay kit (S0053, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). The absorbance of the samples was measured at 450 nm using a plate reader (Infinite200, Tecan, Beijing, China), and quantification was done using a standard curve. Additionally, hypothalamic tissue samples from each group of mice were collected. The tissues were homogenized in lysis buffer, rapidly homogenized using a tissue homogenizer under frozen conditions until fully disrupted, centrifuged at high speed to separate cell debris and undissolved tissue, and the supernatant was collected for GSH content determination using the assay kit [ ].

## Determination of malondialdehyde (MDA)
The levels of MDA in various cell groups and hypothalamic tissues of mice were quantified using the MDA assay kit (S0131S, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) [ ].

## Determination of Fe2+ content
The levels of Fe2+ in cells were assessed using the FerroOrange probe (F374, Dojindo, Japan). Pre-treated PC12 cells were seeded on confocal culture dishes, washed with Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS, 13150016, Gibco, USA), and then incubated with 1 μM FerroOrange for 30 min. The fluorescence intensity was observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM780, Zeiss), and the average fluorescence intensity within the cells was measured to evaluate the Fe2+ content. Simultaneously, the iron levels in the cells were determined using an iron assay kit (ab83366, Abcam, UK), following the manufacturer’s instructions [ ].

## TEM observation of mitochondrial damage
Mitochondrial damage in PC12 cells was observed using TEM. The samples were fixed overnight at 4 °C in a 25% glutaraldehyde solution (G5882, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), followed by fixation in a 1% osmium tetroxide solution (209104, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 1–2 h at room temperature. Dehydration was carried out in a series of graded ethanols (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 95%) at room temperature, followed by treatment with pure acetone (ST1663, BiyunTian Biotechnology, China) and overnight embedding in a pure embedding agent. The embedded samples were then heated at 70 °C overnight to obtain well-embedded specimens. Subsequently, the specimens were sliced to a thickness of 70–90 nm using the UM10 ultramicrotome (Jiangsu Leibo Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., China). These slices were stained with lead citrate solution (M67147, Shanghai Mayer Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.) and 2% uranyl acetate saturated aqueous solution (541-09-3, Guangdong Yunxing Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) for 15 min each, enabling observation under the transmission electron microscope [ ].

## RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)
The EZ-Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation kit (Merck Millipore, USA) was utilized to investigate the interaction between PCBP2 and SLC7A11 mRNA in PC12 cells. RIP was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions. The general procedure was as follows: initially, the fusion of PCBP2 with a Flag-tag sequence at the DNA level was constructed to generate an expression vector. The constructed Flag-PCBP2 expression vector, along with an empty plasmid vector, was transfected into PC12 cells via lentivirus transfection to induce overexpression within the cells. The cells were harvested and lysed in RIPA lysis buffer. Subsequently, the cell lysates were incubated overnight with IP buffer coated with anti-mouse IgG (ab205718, Abcam, UK) or anti-Flag-PCBP2 (ab251324, Abcam, UK) magnetic beads. Following this, the RNA–protein complexes were washed and incubated with 50 µl of proteinase K (107393, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent for PCR-qPCR analysis [ ].

## RNA pull-down
Biotinylated RNA probes targeting a specific region of SLC7A11 mRNA were prepared using in vitro transcription. Cells were collected and lysed using an RNase inhibitor (HY-K1033, Med Chem Express, USA) and RIPA lysis buffer to isolate RNA and proteins. The biotinylated RNA probes were incubated with the cell lysate, followed by the addition of streptavidin-coated magnetic beads (HY-K0208, Med Chem Express, USA) to capture the biotinylated RNA and its associated proteins. The mixture was incubated at 4 °C for 3 h, then washed twice with cold lysis buffer, three times with low-salt buffer (150 mM NaCl), and once with high-salt buffer (500 mM NaCl). Protein extraction was performed for Western blot analysis to detect the expression of PCBP2 [ ].

## Analysis of RNA stability
To assess the impact of PCBP2 on the stability of SLC7A11 mRNA, we treated cells with Actinomycin D (SBR00013, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at a concentration of 5 μg/mL. After incubation for designated periods, cell samples were collected at 2, 4, 6, and 8 h. Subsequently, RNA was extracted from each sample, and the expression levels of SLC7A11 were analyzed using RT-qPCR. For detailed methodology, please refer to the following RT-qPCR section [ ].

## Ethical statement
This study strictly adheres to the relevant ethical principles and regulations regarding animal experiments. All experimental procedures have been approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of General Hospital of Xinjiang Military Command (No. DWLL20200111). All animals are housed and cared for in conditions that meet humane principles and are subjected to experiments with the utmost effort to minimize pain. At the conclusion of the experiments, all mice are euthanized humanely under ether anesthesia.

## Establishment and grouping of the mouse model
Healthy male C57BL/6 mice aged 8–12 weeks weighing 20–25 g were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., China. The mice were individually housed in cages under controlled conditions of 60–65% humidity and temperatures ranging from 22 to 25 ℃, with a 12-h light/dark cycle in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal facility. After an acclimatization period of 1 week with standard feeding, the mice were observed for their health status prior to the experiment. The C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to the Control group (n = 8) and the Model group (n = 80). The procedure for model establishment involved placing the mice in a specialized constant temperature chamber with a temperature set at 41–43 °C and relative humidity of approximately 40–60% for a duration of 10–15 min, during which the mice had no access to water or food. Following the heat stress, the mice were swiftly transferred to room temperature for rewarming until their body temperature returned to normal (approximately 36.5–37.5 °C). Subsequent to the heat stress and rewarming, the mice underwent close observation to monitor changes in behavior and physiological status, with a record of the survival rate. Two weeks after the heat stress treatment, the degree of damage to the hypothalamic neuroaxis was assessed through neurological deficit scoring and histological examination (H&E staining and Nissl staining) to confirm the successful establishment of the model [ ].
The Model group mice were further randomly divided into the following subgroups: (1) CUR group, NVs-CUR group; (2) NVs + DMSO group, NVs-CUR + DMSO group, and NVs-CUR + Erastin group; (3) NVs + sh-NC + oe-NC group, NVs-CUR + sh-NC + oe-NC group, NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 + oe-NC group, and NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 + oe-SLC7A11 group, each consisting of 8 mice. Prior to modeling, each mouse received a single tail vein injection of lentivirus at a dose of 6.4 × 106 TU in a volume of 20 μL. After 12 h of heat stress, CUR and NVs-CUR (2 × 109) were administered intravenously, with a dose of 30 mg/kg/day for CUR, both given continuously for two weeks [ ]. After heat stress treatment, the ferroptosis activator Erastin (HY-15763, Med Chem Express, USA) was administered via intravenous injection at a dose of 30 mg/kg once daily for two weeks [ ].

## In vivo fluorescence imaging of NVs-CUR
To assess the biodistribution of CUR and NVs-CUR in the mouse brain, FITC-labeled CUR (Q-0127482, QIYUE Biology, China) and NVs-CUR were administered 2 h prior to injection. Subsequently, at 6 and 24 h post-injection, the in vivo fluorescence signals of the mice were detected using the IVIS Lumina III live imaging system (PerkinElmer, CLS136334). Prior to imaging, the mice were briefly anesthetized to ensure their immobility throughout the procedure [ ].

## Detection of neuronal uptake of NVs-CUR by flow cytometry
Following in vivo fluorescent imaging, neuronal distribution of NVs-CUR in the hypothalamic tissue of Model mice was analyzed using FITC-labeled anti-NeuN antibody (ab177487, 1:1000, Abcam, UK) as a surface marker, with neuronal sorting conducted via the Cytek Aurora Flow cytometer. Subsequently, the percentage of cells uptaking DiR-labeled NVs and NVs-CUR was determined [ ].

## Neurological function deficit scoring
Based on a previously reported scoring system [ ], we assessed the neural damage in each group of mice before and after modeling at 1, 3, 7, and 14 days, which involved motor, sensory, and balance tests. The scoring ranged from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating more severe neural damage. Exclusion criteria were: (a) death within the specified time frame; (b) obtaining a score of 0 at any given time.
The wire-hanging test was conducted to evaluate the mice’s motor coordination ability and muscle strength. The procedure involved preparing a wire with a diameter of approximately 2–3 mm. Prior to the formal experiment, the mice were familiarized with the wire through brief training without scoring. They were then gently hung by their front paws on the center of the wire, and the timing started from when they grasped the wire until they dropped. Each mouse underwent the test three times with intervals of no less than 15 min. The scoring was based on the duration the mice hung, with shorter durations indicating more severe motor impairment [ ].
The bilateral tactile stimulation test was performed to evaluate the mice’s responsiveness to tactile stimuli. The mice were placed in the experimental cage and allowed to acclimate for approximately 5 min before lightly touching their flanks alternately with a brush or cotton swab to avoid eliciting a stress response. Responses such as turning, licking, or evading touch were recorded and graded based on the intensity of the mouse’s reaction to the tactile stimuli, with weaker responses indicating more severe sensory impairment [ ].
The balance beam walking test was conducted to assess the mice’s balance and coordination. Prior to the formal experiment, the mice were allowed to walk freely on the balance beam to adapt to the environment. Subsequently, each mouse was placed at one end of the beam, and the time taken to walk to the other end was recorded. The stability of the mice during walking, including swaying, pausing, or falling, was observed. Scores were assigned based on the completion speed of the test and the stability during walking, where slower speed and poorer stability indicated a more severe impairment in balance ability [ ].

## H&E staining
At the conclusion of the experiment, all mice were euthanized by inhalation of isoflurane (1% O2). The hypothalamic tissues were prepared for fixation by perfusion and then washed three times for 12 h each with PBS, followed by immersion in a 30% sucrose solution at room temperature for 24 h. The tissues were embedded in a chilled embedding medium and immediately frozen. The hypothalamic tissues were then sliced into 7 µm sections using a cryostat (Leica Microsystems) for further analysis. These prepared sections were stained in hematoxylin–eosin solution (H8070, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for typically 5 to 10 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the sections were rinsed with distilled water, dehydrated in 95% ethanol, and placed in an eosin staining solution (G1100, Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 5 to 10 min. Standard dehydration, clearing, and mounting procedures were followed [ ].

## Nissl staining
The frozen sections, as described previously, were rinsed in distilled water for 2 min to a thickness of 7 μm. Subsequently, the sections were stained with Nissl staining solution (C0117, Biyun Technologies, China) at 37 °C for 10 min, followed by replacement with fresh distilled water and a 2-min wash. Next, they were briefly immersed in 95% ethanol for 5 s, followed by two washes in 70% ethanol every 5 s. The stained sections were then visualized and scanned using the Pannoramic MIDI CaseViewer 2.0 system (3DHISTECH Ltd.) [ ].

## Immunofluorescence staining
For the immunofluorescence staining of the hypothalamic tissue, we conducted DAPI and NeuN staining to acquire representative confocal immunofluorescence images. The procedure involved the following steps: tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval treatment, delineated with a hydrophobic barrier using a histology pen to outline the tissue surrounding the region of interest, and then immersed the slices in a 3% H2O2 solution to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Subsequently, the sections were blocked with serum or BSA and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit monoclonal anti-NeuN antibody (ab190565, 1:50, Abcam, UK). Following this, incubation with DAPI (ab104139, 1: 1000, Abcam, UK) was performed [ ].
For the cellular immunofluorescence staining, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P885233, Macklin, Shanghai, China) for 15–30 min. Subsequently, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (L885651, Macklin, Shanghai, China) for 15 min to penetrate the cell membrane. After two washes with PBS, cells were incubated in PBS with 15% FBS at room temperature for 1 h. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with rabbit polyclonal anti-PCBP2 antibody (ab184962, 1:250, Abcam, UK). On the following day, sections were washed with PBS, incubated with goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (ab150115, 1:200, Abcam, UK) at room temperature for 1 h, rinsed with PBS, and stained with DAPI (ab104139, 1:1000, Abcam, UK) for 5 min to label the nuclei. Excess DAPI was removed by washing the slides three times with PBS for 5 min each. Subsequently, fluorescence imaging was performed using a Zeiss Observer Z1 microscope (Germany). Five random fields of view were chosen, the proportion of PCBP2-positive cells was quantified in each field, and the mean value was calculated [ ].

## ELISA assay
The hypothalamic tissue was washed in PBS and homogenized using a homogenizer in a centrifuge tube containing an appropriate amount of protein extraction buffer, followed by centrifugation of the homogenate and collection of the supernatant. Mouse ELISA kits for IL-1β (PI301), IL-6 (PI326), and TNF-α (PT512) from Biotech Inc., China were selected for analysis and performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions [ ].

## RT-qPCR
Total RNA was extracted using the Trizol reagent kit (T9424, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The quality and concentration of RNA were assessed using UV–visible spectrophotometry (ND-1000, Nanodrop, Thermo Fisher, USA). Reverse transcription was performed following the PrimeScript™ RT-qPCR kit (RR086A, TaKaRa, Mountain View, USA). Real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was carried out on the LightCycler 480 system (Roche Diagnostics, Pleasanton, USA) using SYBR Premix Ex Taq™ (DRR820A, TaKaRa, Japan). The housekeeping gene GAPDH was used as a reference for mRNA. The primer sequences were designed and provided by Shanghai General Biotech Co., Ltd. Refer to Table S2 for primer sequences. The fold change in target gene expression between the experimental and control groups was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, where ΔΔCT = ΔCt experimental group—ΔCt control group, and ΔCt = Ct target gene—Ct housekeeping gene [ ].

## Western blot
Cellular or tissue total protein was extracted using the efficient RIPA lysis buffer following the manufacturer’s instructions. After 15 min of lysis at 4 °C and centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min, the supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration of each sample was determined using the BCA assay kit (20201ES76, Yisheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Quantification was performed based on different concentrations. Proteins were separated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to a PVDF membrane using the wet transfer method. The membrane was blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 1 h. Primary antibodies were then incubated overnight at 4 °C (antibody information in Table S3). Subsequently, the membrane was washed with TBST for 5 min × 3 times, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ab205718, 1:20,000, Abcam, UK) dilution at room temperature for 1 h. After washing the membrane with TBST for 5 min × 3 times, the membrane was developed using a chromogenic substrate. Protein quantification analysis was performed using ImageJ software (v1.48, National Institutes of Health) by evaluating the ratio of the grayscale values of each protein to the internal control GAPDH [ ]. The experiment was repeated three times.

## Statistical analysis
All experiments were independently carried out at least three times, and the data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between groups were assessed using independent samples t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). If the ANOVA results indicated significant differences, further Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc tests were conducted to compare differences among groups. For non-normally distributed or inhomogeneous variance data, the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis H test was utilized. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant [ ].

## Results

## Preparation of macrophage-derived membrane NVs loaded with drugs and their neuronal targeting and therapeutic potential
Artificial NVs mainly consist of NVs and cell membrane NVs. NVs are obtained by directly squeezing cells (enriched with the contents of the parent cells and possessing physiological functions similar to those of the parent cells), while cell membrane NVs are formed by extracting the cell membrane structure and then squeezing out (without cell contents), during which various functional modifications such as targeted peptide conjugation and drug loading can be carried out [ ]. Macrophage membrane-derived NVs have been reported to hold therapeutic potential for nerve damage [ ], exhibiting neuronal targeting capabilities as a nanocarrier [ ]. Additionally, CUR has been extensively studied in the context of nerve damage [ – ]. Thus, in order to develop a novel drug delivery system with neuronal targeting capabilities, this study prepared macrophage membrane CUR-loaded NVs, NVs-CUR.
We utilized a continuous extrusion method to prepare NVs from macrophages (RAW264.7) and employed ultrasonication to load CUR into NVs (Fig.  A). First, membrane biomimetic nano-drug characterization was performed on NVs and NVs-CUR. Western blot results revealed the positive expression of extracellular vesicle markers CD9, CD81, CD63, and Alix in both NVs and NVs-CUR, while the endoplasmic reticulum membrane marker Calnexin showed negative expression (Fig.  B). Under TEM, NVs and NVs-CUR appeared spherical with a lipid bilayer membrane structure (Fig.  C). NTA indicated that the average particle sizes of NVs and NVs-CUR were 93.15 ± 4.36 nm and 105.39 ± 2.05 nm, respectively (Fig.  D), with corresponding Zeta potentials of − 25.23 ± 1.16 and − 17.82 ± 2.14 mV (Fig.  E). Therefore, both NVs and NVs-CUR exhibited classical features of extracellular vesicles. Furthermore, after 30 days, there were no significant changes in the concentration and size of NVs-CUR, indicating the sustained stability required for therapeutic applications (Fig.  F, ). 
It is worth noting that initially, three different methods for loading CUR into NVs were explored in this study. As shown in Figure S1A-C, the UV–Vis spectra displayed a main peak centered at 436 nm without interference, confirming no disruption of CUR in the UV–Vis range. The loading efficiency (LE) of NVs-CUR was determined through UV–Vis absorption spectra, resulting in drug loading amounts of 125.45, 41.33, and 3.78 μg/mg for ultrasonication, incubation, and freeze–thaw methods, respectively. Correspondingly, the encapsulation efficiency (EE) values of CUR in NVs were 9.82, 2.75, and 0.28 (Figure S1D). Hence, UV–Vis spectroscopy of NVs-CUR complexes suggested that ultrasonication was the optimal drug delivery method. The loading capacity (LC) of NVs-CUR formulations was closely associated with the preparation method. It was speculated that the cavitation or the sonochemical effect generated by ultrasonication led to transiently repairable membrane gaps in NVs, significantly enhancing drug entry into NVs. Optimization of the initial CUR loading amount was conducted by detecting 0.5, 1.5, 2.0, and 2.5 mg (Figure S1E), revealing that the absorption peak of NVs-CUR was maximal at 1.5 mg CUR input. These results further substantiated the rationale for using ultrasonication to load CUR into NVs and determined the optimal drug loading of CUR.
Moreover, FITC-labeled NVs-CUR released approximately 78–85% of the encapsulated CUR within 48 h. This indicated a stable and slow release of CUR from NVs-CUR, reflecting the advantage of NVs as a drug delivery system (Fig.  H). Plasma concentration curves and pharmacokinetic parameters (F g.  I) showed that the area under the curve from 0 to the last time point (AUC0-t) of the CUR group was approximately double that of the NVs-CUR group (851.55 ± 32.71 ng/mL h and 1792.77 ± 28.34 ng/mL h, respectively). These pharmacokinetic test results indicated that CUR in NVs-CUR exhibited sustained release and prolonged action.
Furthermore, Western blot results (Figure S2A) demonstrated that the macrophage characteristic marker proteins F4/80 and CD11b were expressed at lower levels in the RAW264.7 cell group compared to the RAW264.7 cell membrane, NVs, and NVs-CUR groups, suggesting the successful extraction of the RAW264.7 cell membrane and no alteration in the surface protein properties during the preparation process. The feasibility of the membrane extraction process was indirectly validated by images showing changes in cell morphology before and after bursting in a low osmotic solution, where cells enlarged and ruptured significantly compared to normal cells (Figure S2B). Due to the abundance of surface proteins and ribonucleic acid on macrophage membrane NVs, their sourced NVs play a critical role in targeted inflammation therapy [ ]. Hence, due to the homology of macrophages at inflammatory sites, they are deemed suitable as a drug carrier for targeted inflammation.
In conclusion, the successful preparation of macrophage membrane NVs-CUR was achieved, showing excellent stability and advantages as a drug delivery system for targeted inflammation.

## Novel macrophage-derived NVs-CUR demonstrate enhanced neuronal targeting
To further investigate the neuronal targeting capacity of NVs-CUR, we utilized Dil-labeled NVs-CUR to observe their uptake by PC12 cells. As depicted in Fig.  A, the red fluorescence signals within PC12 cells increased over time, indicating a time-dependent internalization of NVs-CUR by PC12 cells. 
In in vivo animal studies, fluorescently labeled CUR or NVs-CUR was initially intravenously injected into mice with heat stroke-induced hypothalamic neuro injury, and their in vivo biodistribution and neuronal targeting were assessed through live fluorescence imaging. Fluorescence imaging of major organs (brain, liver, heart, spleen, lungs, kidneys) at 6 h post intravenous injection in both mouse groups is illustrated in Fig.  B. Compared to the CUR group, higher fluorescence signals were detected in the brain and liver tissues of mice in the NVs-CUR group.
Imaging of brain tissues demonstrated that NVs-CUR effectively targeted the injured hypothalamic tissues. According to the IVIS images, the distribution of FITC-labeled CUR and FITC-labeled NVs-CUR in the brain is shown in Fig.  C. At 6 h, both the CUR and NVs-CUR groups exhibited distribution at the injury site, with fluorescence intensity gradually increasing over time. At 6 h and 24 h, the fluorescence intensity of NVs-CUR at the injury site was significantly higher than that of the CUR group. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the CUR group only targeted 22% of neurons, while the engineered NVs-CUR targeted 52.1% of neurons, showing higher fluorescence intensity in neuronal cells in the NVs-CUR group (Fig.  D). Combined with existing literature, macrophages possess inherent attraction to inflammation and can be recruited to inflammatory sites within the body. NVs inherit these macrophage properties, allowing them to transport CUR towards the injured sites. Moreover, CUR encapsulated within the macrophage membrane system evades clearance by the reticuloendothelial system, prolonging its circulation time for sustained effects [ ].
These findings collectively indicate that the novel macrophage-derived NVs-CUR exhibits superior neuronal targeting, potentially offering therapeutic benefits for heat stroke-induced hypothalamic neuron injury.

## NVs-CUR exhibits enhanced protective effect against H2O2-induced PC12 cell damage while reducing ferroptosis
To assess the toxicity of NVs-CUR, we performed an MTT assay 24 h post treatment with NVs-CUR and found that over 90% of PC12 cells remained viable, indicating the biological safety of NVs-CUR (Fig.  A). Subsequently, we induced oxidative stress damage in PC12 cells using H2O2. The MTT results showed a decrease in cell survival with increasing H2O2 concentration, with a calculated IC50 of 45.3 mM. Therefore, we chose a 50 mM H2O2 concentration to establish the cell model (Fig.  B). 
Next, we treated the H2O2-induced cell model with CUR and NVs-CUR to evaluate their protective effects on neurons. The cell survival rate of PC12 cells in the H2O2 group was 35.38%, while in the CUR and NVs-CUR groups, the cell survival rates were 53.47% and 68.54%, respectively, with NVs-CUR significantly higher than CUR (Fig.  C). Quantitative analysis of PC12 cells with axon lengths exceeding 50 μm revealed that the numbers of live cells in the Control, H2O2, CUR, and NVs-CUR groups were 41.23 ± 1.35, 1.15 ± 0.51, 30.36 ± 1.37, and 38.67 ± 1.25, respectively. This indicates that both CUR and NVs-CUR could inhibit neuronal death and protect neuronal axons, with NVs-CUR showing a more pronounced effect (Fig.  D). Compared to the Control group, most PC12 cells in the H2O2 group exhibited spherical morphology, a drastic decrease in cell number, and nearly disappearing synapses; in contrast, the CUR and NVs-CUR groups had higher cell numbers and longer synapses. Among them, NVs-CUR demonstrated the most significant therapeutic effect (Fig.  E). These results demonstrate that NVs-CUR has a better protective effect against H2O2-induced damage in PC12 cells and promotes the recovery of neuronal synapses post-injury. Although both CUR and NVs-CUR can enter cells, the introduction of NVs enhances the bioavailability, sustained release, cellular uptake efficiency, and targeting of CUR, leading to significant differences in their cellular protective effects. Specifically, NVs-CUR enhances drug stability and solubility through a nano-carrier, thereby increasing its intracellular accumulation [ ]. NVs may better protect CUR from degradation, thus increasing its therapeutic efficacy. When using NVs, CUR may be released into cells in a more controlled manner. This slower and sustained release helps maintain higher drug concentrations, providing better neuroprotective effects. While both can enter cells, nanocarriers typically utilize cellular uptake mechanisms like endocytosis [ ], thereby improving drug absorption by cells. NVs-CUR may be more efficiently taken up by cells due to their particle size, surface modifications, and other characteristics.
Ferroptosis plays a significant role in heat stroke and neural damage [ , ]. Studies indicate that glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and acyl-coa synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) are marker genes for ferroptosis, with decreased GPX4 expression and increased ACSL4 expression indicating elevated levels of ferroptosis [ , ]. Thus, we assessed the protein expression of GPX4 and ACSL4 in each group using Western blot analysis. As shown in Fig.  F, compared to the H2O2 group, both the CUR and NVs-CUR groups exhibited significantly increased GPX4 expression and decreased ACSL4 expression. Using FerroOrange probe analysis to study the regulation of iron balance by CUR and NVs-CUR, as shown in Fig.  G, , the CUR and NVs-CUR groups showed significantly reduced iron levels compared to the H2O2 group.
To investigate the effects of CUR and NVs-CUR on redox balance, we compared oxidative stress indicators and peroxidation products. The results indicated that ROS in the cytoplasm can oxidize membrane polyunsaturated lipids, leading to the production of end products such as MDA [ ]. We observed a significant decrease in MDA levels in the CUR and NVs-CUR groups compared to the H2O2 group (F g.  I). Furthermore, the GSH/GSSG ratio in the CUR and NVs-CUR groups was significantly higher compared to the H2O2 group (Fig.  J). Additionally, iron-induced cell death was associated with distinctive mitochondrial morphological changes [ , ]. Transmission electron microscope observations revealed that neurons in the CUR and NVs-CUR groups exhibited reduced mitochondrial shrinkage and decreased membrane density compared to the H2O2 group (Fig.  K). These findings suggest that CUR and NVs-CUR can alleviate H2O2-induced iron-mediated cell death in PC12 cells.
Further comparison revealed that compared to the CUR group, the NVs-CUR group exhibited significantly increased GPX4 expression, decreased ACSL4 expression, decreased iron and MDA levels, further reduced mitochondrial shrinkage, and decreased membrane density (Fig.  F–K). This indicates that NVs-CUR has a more pronounced inhibitory effect on ferroptosis in H2O2-induced PC12 cells than CUR.

## Efficacy and mechanism study of NVs-CUR in the treatment of hypothalamic nerve damage induced by heat stroke
To further evaluate the therapeutic potential of NVs-CUR in treating hypothalamic nerve damage induced by heat stroke, we established a mouse model of heat stroke-induced hypothalamic nerve injury and treated the model mice with CUR and NVs-CUR, respectively (Fig.  A). We observed that the survival rates of mice in the Control group, Model group, CUR group, and NVs-CUR group were 100%, 62.5%, 75%, and 87.5%, respectively (Fig.  B). Neurological deficit assessment results indicated a significant decrease in scores in the CUR and NVs-CUR groups compared to the Model group, with a notable increase in scores in the NVs-CUR group compared to the CUR group (Fig.  C). This suggests that, compared to CUR, NVs-CUR can improve the survival rate of mice with heat stroke-induced hypothalamic nerve injury and enhance neurological function. 
Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining revealed that compared to the Control group, the Model group exhibited neuronal and glial cell swelling, vacuolation, nuclear condensation, and loose cell arrangement in the hypothalamic tissues, which were ameliorated in the CUR and NVs-CUR groups. Furthermore, the tissue structures in the NVs-CUR group were more intact and clearer than those in the CUR group, with more significant reductions in neuronal damage and evident neurogenesis (Fig.  D). NeuN staining for neurons and Nissl staining for observing neuronal damage repair in the hypothalamus showed that compared to the Control group, the Model group had a decreased neuronal count, leading to disintegration or even disappearance by 7 days post-injury, while the CUR and NVs-CUR groups showed increased neuronal presence, with the NVs-CUR group demonstrating superior improvement compared to the CUR group. These results demonstrate that NVs-CUR provides better protection against heat stroke-induced hypothalamic nerve damage, with superior efficacy over CUR (Fig.  E, ).
Additionally, ELISA was employed to measure the levels of inflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) in the hypothalamic tissues of the mice in each group to evaluate the impact of NVs-CUR on the inflammatory response induced by heat stroke. Simultaneously, MDA and GSH levels were determined to assess the antioxidant effect of NVs-CUR. The results showed that compared to the Model group, the CUR and NVs-CUR groups exhibited significant reductions in IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, MDA, and GSH levels. Moreover, the NVs-CUR group displayed significantly lower levels of inflammatory factors and oxidative stress compared to the CUR group (Fig.  G–K). This indicates that NVs-CUR can decrease the levels of inflammation and oxidative stress in mice with heat stroke-induced hypothalamic nerve damage.
Western blot analysis was conducted to evaluate the protein expression of GPX4 and ACSL4 in the hypothalamic tissues of the mice in each group to assess the influence of NVs-CUR on ferroptosis. The results revealed that compared to the Model group, the CUR and NVs-CUR groups had significantly increased GPX4 expression and decreased ACSL4 expression. Furthermore, the NVs-CUR group exhibited significantly reduced GPX4 expression and increased ACSL4 expression compared to the CUR group (Fig.  L). This indicates that NVs-CUR can notably decrease the levels of ferroptosis in the hypothalamic tissues of the model mice.
In conclusion, the novel macrophage nanovesicle NVs-CUR provides superior therapeutic effects in heat stroke-induced hypothalamic nerve damage compared to CUR.

## Mechanisms of NVs-CUR-mediated inhibition of neuronal ferroptosis via PCBP2 regulation
To investigate the specific mechanisms by which novel macrophage-derived NVs-CUR regulate neuronal ferroptosis, we collected samples of PC12 cells treated with NVs (n = 3) and NVs-CUR (n = 3) for proteomic analysis (Fig.  A). Using a threshold of |log2FC|> 1 and P < 0.05, we identified a total of 23 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) shared between the NVs and NVs-CUR groups, with 19 upregulated and 4 downregulated proteins (Fig.  B, ). 
The GO and KEGG, enrichment analysis results, revealed that 23 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were predominantly enriched in biological processes such as regulation of mRNA metabolic processes and mRNA splicing, as well as in cellular components, including ribonucleoprotein granule, cytoplasmic stress granule, and cytoplasmic ribonucleoprotein granule. They were also enriched in molecular functions like double-stranded RNA binding, mRNA 3’-UTR binding, and RNA stem-loop binding, as well as in signaling pathways such as Spliceosome and iron death (Fig.  D–G). These findings illustrated that NVs-CUR significantly impacted the RNA degradation metabolism and iron death pathway in PC12 cells. Notably, the enrichment of DEPs in these processes suggests that NVs-CUR may regulate gene expression and function in neurons by influencing mRNA metabolism and splicing. Subsequent results in this study mention that PCBP2 enhances the stability of SLC7A11 mRNA to inhibit neuronal iron death, aligning the proteomic analysis results with the follow-up cellular experimental findings.
Previous studies have demonstrated the critical involvement of PCBP2 in ferroptosis [ ] and its relevance in neurotrauma [ ]. Subsequently, we validated the differential expression of PCBP2 in the cell model using Western blot and immunofluorescence staining. The results showed a significant downregulation of PCBP2 expression in the H2O2 group compared to the Control group, whereas the CUR and NVs-CUR groups exhibited a substantial upregulation, with NVs-CUR demonstrating a more pronounced effect on PCBP2 expression (F g.  H, ). Furthermore, Western blot analysis of PCBP2 expression in the hypothalamic tissues of mice revealed a significant downregulation in the Model group compared to the Control group, while the CUR and NVs-CUR groups exhibited notable upregulation. Moreover, the NVs-CUR group showed a further increase in PCBP2 expression compared to the CUR group (Fig.  J), consistent with the cell experiments. Therefore, we selected PCBP2 as the focus of our study. In conclusion, NVs-CUR may potentially ameliorate neurotrauma in the hypothalamus by suppressing neuronal ferroptosis through the upregulation of PCBP2 expression.

## Reversal of neuroprotective effects of NVs-CUR by silencing PCBP2 and its inhibitory role on ferroptosis
Our bioinformatics analysis has shown that NVs-CUR may regulate the expression of PCBP2, which affects neuron ferroptosis. Subsequently, we overexpressed/silenced PCBP2 in neurons to observe the impact of PCBP2 interference on H2O2-induced neuron ferroptosis. Initially, we designed three shRNA sequences targeting PCBP2, validated the silencing effect of sh-PCBP2, and selected the most efficient silencing sequence, sh-PCBP2-2, for further experiments (Figure S3A-B). The overexpression of PCBP2 is depicted in Figure S3C-D.
Results revealed that compared to the oe-NC group, the oe-PCBP2 group exhibited increased cell quantity, longer synapses (Figure S4A), a higher number of axons longer than 50 μm (Figure S4B), and improved cell survival rate (Figure S4C). This indicates that overexpressing PCBP2 provides better protection against H2O2-induced PC12 cell damage and promotes synaptic recovery post-injury. Compared to the oe-NC group, the oe-PCBP2 group showed downregulation of GPX4 expression, simultaneous upregulation of ACSL4 expression (Figure S4D), a significant increase in iron levels (Figure S4E-F), a notable decrease in GSH/GSSG ratio (Figure S4G), a significant rise in MDA levels (Figure S4H), and mitochondrial shrinkage, reduced cristae, and increased membrane density (Figure S4I), suggesting that PCBP2 overexpression can reduce neuron ferroptosis. Conversely, silencing PCBP2 had the opposite effect, promoting PC12 cell damage, inhibiting synaptic recovery, and facilitating ferroptosis (Figure S4A-I).
Subsequently, we treated PC12 cells with NVs-CUR while silencing PCBP2, which resulted in poorer cell morphology, decreased quantity, shorter synapses, fewer axons longer than 50 μm, and reduced cell survival rate in the NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 group compared to the NVs-CUR + sh-NC group (Fig.  A–C). This indicates that silencing PCBP2 can reverse the beneficial effects of NVs-CUR on neuron damage and synaptic recovery. Furthermore, in comparison to the NVs-CUR + sh-NC group, the NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 group exhibited downregulation of GPX4 expression, upregulation of ACSL4 expression (Fig.  D), a significant increase in iron levels (Fig.  E, ), a notable decrease in GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig.  G), a significant rise in MDA levels (Fig.  H), and mitochondrial shrinkage, reduced cristae, and increased membrane density (F g.  I), indicating that silencing PCBP2 can reverse the inhibitory effects of NVs-CUR on neuron ferroptosis. 

## PCBP2 suppresses neuronal ferroptosis by enhancing SLC7A11 mRNA stability
This study delves further into the specific mechanism by which PCBP2 influences ferroptosis. Research indicates that PCBP2 maintains the stability of SLC7A11 mRNA, thereby promoting its expressionH2O2 [ ]. As SLC7A11 acts as a cystine-glutamate antiporter protein, it can inhibit neuronal ferroptosis [ ]. Consequently, we speculate that PCBP2 may inhibit induced neuronal damage and ferroptosis by enhancing the stability of SLC7A11 mRNA.
Firstly, we assessed the regulatory effect of PCBP2 on SLC7A11 expression using RT-qPCR. The results demonstrate that silencing PCBP2 suppresses SLC7A11 mRNA expression while overexpressing PCBP2 has the opposite effect (Fig.  A). PCBP2, an RNA-binding protein, was validated to bind with SLC7A11 mRNA through RIP assays (Fig.  B). CLIP data further predicted the binding interaction between PCBP2 and SLC7A11 mRNA (Figure S5A). Additionally, RNA pull-down assays confirmed the strong interaction between SLC7A11 and PCBP2 (Fig.  C). Moreover, using Actinomycin D assay to assess SLC7A11 mRNA stability, we observed a significant decrease in stability upon PCBP2 silencing, whereas stability was increased with PCBP2 overexpression (Fig.  D). These findings indicate that PCBP2 enhances the stability of SLC7A11 mRNA and promotes its expression. 
Next, does NVs-CUR influence the expression level of SLC7A11 by promoting PCBP2 expression? Results from RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis revealed that compared to the Control group, SLC7A11 expression is significantly reduced in the H2O2 group. Conversely, SLC7A11 expression is significantly upregulated in the NVs-CUR group compared to the H2O2 group. Furthermore, SLC7A11 expression is markedly decreased in the NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 group compared to the NVs-CUR group (Fig.  E, ). These results suggest that NVs-CUR is involved in the regulation of SLC7A11 expression by PCBP2.
To explore the impact of SLC7A11 on PC12 cell damage and ferroptosis, three shRNA sequences targeting SLC7A11 were designed, with sh-SLC7A11-3 (hereinafter referred to as sh-SLC7A11) showing the highest silencing efficiency for subsequent experiments (Figure S5B-C). It was observed that compared to the sh-NC group, the sh-SLC7A11 group exhibited reduced cell numbers, shorter synapses, decreased cell viability, downregulated GPX4 expression, upregulated ACSL4 expression, significantly increased iron levels, decreased GSH/GSSG ratio, elevated MDA levels, mitochondrial shrinkage, reduced dendritic spines, and increased membrane density (Fig.  G–O). This indicates that silencing SLC7A11 promotes PC12 cell damage, inhibits neuro synaptic recovery, and enhances ferroptosis.
Furthermore, we verified whether NVs-CUR acts through the regulation of the PCBP2/SLC7A11 axis. The NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 group was further divided into oe-NC and oe-SLC7A11 groups for rescue experiments. The overexpression effect of SLC7A11 is depicted in Figure S5D-E. Results show that compared to the NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 + oe-NC group; the NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 + oe-SLC7A11 group had more cells, longer synapses, increased axon lengths exceeding 50 μm, and higher cell viability (F g.  G–I). This indicates that overexpressing SLC7A11 can further reverse the effects of silencing PCBP2 on PC12 cell damage and neurosynaptic recovery. Compared to the NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 + oe-NC group, the NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 + oe-SLC7A11 group exhibited upregulated GPX4 expression, downregulated ACSL4 expression, significantly reduced iron levels, increased GSH/GSSG ratio, decreased MDA levels, reduced mitochondrial shrinkage, and lower membrane density (Fig.  J–O). These results suggest that overexpressing SLC7A11 can reverse the promoting effects of silencing PCBP2 on neuronal damage and ferroptosis.

## NVs-CUR alleviates hypothalamic neuronal damage induced by heat stroke by targeting the PCBP2/SLC7A11 axis
The preceding cellular experiments demonstrate that NVs-CUR promotes the expression of PCBP2 and maintains the stability of SLC7A11 mRNA, subsequently inhibiting H2O2-induced neuronal damage and ferroptosis. Thus, we proceeded with in vivo animal experiments for validation.
Initially, we treated model mice with the ferroptosis inducer Erastin, dividing them into the NVs + DMSO group, NVs-CUR + DMSO group, and NVs-CUR + Erastin group to verify the impact of NVs-CUR through ferroptosis inhibition (Figure S6A). The survival rates for the three groups were 62.5%, 87.5%, and 62.5%, respectively (Figure S6B). Compared to the NVs + DMSO group, the NVs-CUR + DMSO group exhibited significantly reduced neurofunctional deficit scores, more intact and clearer tissue structure, reduced neuronal damage, and increased neuronal count. Meanwhile, compared to the NVs-CUR + DMSO group, neuronal damage was exacerbated in the NVs-CUR + Erastin group of mice (Figure S6C-F), indicating that the ferroptosis inducer could reverse the beneficial effects of NVs-CUR on mouse survival and neurofunction. Additionally, in comparison to the NVs-CUR + DMSO group, the levels of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, MDA, and GSH significantly increased in the NVs-CUR + Erastin group (Figure S6G-K), suggesting that the ferroptosis inducer could counteract the improvement of inflammation and oxidative stress by NVs-CUR.
Finally, to confirm whether NVs-CUR acts through targeting the PCBP2/SLC7A11 axis to alleviate hypothalamic neuronal damage induced by heat stroke, we further divided the model mice into the NVs + sh-NC + oe-NC group, NVs-CUR + sh-NC + oe-NC group, NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 + oe-NC group, and NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 + oe-SLC7A11 group (Fig.  A) for subsequent index measurements. In comparison to the NVs + sh-NC + oe-NC group, the NVs-CUR + sh-NC + oe-NC group showed improvements in all indices, consistent with the previous results. Contrary to the NVs-CUR + sh-NC + oe-NC group, the NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 + oe-NC group of mice exhibited decreased survival rates, increased neurofunctional deficit scores, more neuronal swelling vacuoles in the hypothalamic tissue, loose cell arrangement, reduced neuronal count, significantly elevated levels of inflammation, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis. This indicates that silencing PCBP2 can reverse the beneficial effects of NVs-CUR on hypothalamic neuronal damage induced by heat stroke. In comparison to the NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 + oe-NC group, the NVs-CUR + sh-PCBP2 + oe-SLC7A11 group of mice showed increased survival rates, reduced neurofunctional deficit scores, more noticeable alleviation of neuronal damage, clearer neurogenesis, and significant reductions in inflammation, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis levels, demonstrating that overexpressing SLC7A11 further counteracts the adverse effects of silencing PCBP2 on hypothalamic neuronal damage induced by heat stroke (Fig.  B–M). 
These results indicate that NVs-CUR targets the PCBP2/SLC7A11 axis to modulate neuronal ferroptosis, thereby mitigating hypothalamic neuronal damage caused by heat stroke.

## Discussion
In this study, NVs were chosen as the drug delivery system due to their many advantages [ , ]. Compared to traditional systems, engineered NVs can effectively traverse the blood–brain barrier, facilitating precise drug targeting to brain tissues or neurons. Additionally, these NVs demonstrate superior stability and biocompatibility, enhancing drug bioavailability and reducing side effects [ , , ]. This innovative drug delivery system holds significant promise for the treatment of neurological disorders.
Mechanisms of NVs-CUR in Neuronal Protection and Neuroferroptosis Our research findings suggest that NVs-CUR provides better neuronal protection than traditional CUR and can reduce neuroferroptosis. This indicates a potential mechanism for NVs-CUR in treating hypothalamic neuronal damage induced by heat stroke. By regulating the expression of PCBP2 and SLC7A11, NVs-CUR exerts neuroprotective and ferroptosis-inhibiting effects within cells, thereby positively impacting neurological diseases.
Role of PCBP2 in Hypothalamic Neuronal Damage Induced by Heat Stroke Studies reveals that PCBP2 plays a crucial role in heat stroke-induced hypothalamic neuronal damage. Proteomic analysis shows a close correlation between the neuroprotective effects of NVs-CUR and the regulation of PCBP2. The expression levels of PCBP2 may influence the therapeutic efficacy of NVs-CUR in neuronal damage, offering new leads for exploring PCBP2 functions in neurological disorders.
Comparison of NVs-CUR with Studies in Other Disease Models Compared with research in other disease models, this study further elucidates the mechanisms and therapeutic effects of NVs-CUR in neurological diseases. Comparisons with traditional drug delivery systems indicate that NVs-CUR has superior neuroprotective effects and the ability to reduce neuroferroptosis, providing valuable insights for potential applications in treating various neurological disorders.
Breakthrough and Innovation of the Study: A key breakthrough of this study lies in the successful formulation of neuron-targeting NVs-CUR, which reduces neuronal ferroptosis by regulating the expression of PCBP2 and SLC7A11. This innovative approach offers a pathway for developing more effective strategies for neuroprotection, with the potential to significantly impact the treatment of neurological diseases.
Conclusion and Scientific/Clinical Value From the comprehensive research results, we can conclude that the NVs drug delivery system NVs-CUR, by modulating PCBP2 and SLC7A11, reduces neuroferroptosis, thereby alleviating hypothalamic neuronal damage due to heat stroke. This finding holds not only significant scientific value but also provides new avenues for treating heat stroke-related nerve injuries, potentially yielding positive clinical outcomes.
Limitations of the Study and Future Perspectives However, the study does have limitations, such as a small sample size and incomplete representation of certain clinical features in animal models. Future research endeavors could focus on expanding the sample size, delving deeper into the mechanisms of PCBP2 and SLC7A11 in neurological disorders, and exploring their potential applications in treating other diseases. Looking forward, this research outcome has the potential to open up new therapeutic pathways in treating neurological diseases, offering additional choices for clinical treatment.
Through the analysis and discussion of this study, we have delved into the mechanisms of the NVs drug delivery system in neuroprotection, underscoring the importance of PCBP2 and SLC7A11 in neuroferroptosis. This study extends the research perspective in the field of neurological disease treatment, with the potential to enhance patient treatment outcomes and quality of life.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, this study successfully prepared neuron-targeted NVs-CUR. The NVs-CUR demonstrated the ability to promote the expression of PCBP2 and maintain the stability of SLC7A11 mRNA, thereby reducing ferroptosis and alleviating hypothalamic neuronal damage caused by heat stroke. This research elucidates the mechanism by which novel macrophage NVs-CUR treats hypothalamic neuronal injury in heat stroke, providing a new basis for the clinical application of novel drug delivery systems. However, due to time and budget constraints, we were unable to conduct crucial gene detection through clinical samples. Additionally, factors such as the size, morphology, and composition of NVs used in the study, as well as their distribution and metabolism in vivo, may impact the safety and efficacy of clinical application. Further validation is needed to determine the potential clinical value of NVs-CUR.
