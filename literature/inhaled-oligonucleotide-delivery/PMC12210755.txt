## Abstract

## Background
Ferroptosis, a form of cell death reliant on iron metabolism dysregulation and lipid peroxidation, has emerged as a promising target for improving tumor drug resistance. This study aims to unveil the underlying molecular mechanisms of Kruppel-like factor 13 (KLF13) in ferroptosis and chemotherapy sensitivity in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD).

## Results
We conducted RNA sequencing on lung adenocarcinoma cells treated with ferroptosis inducers and found that the expression level of KLF13 changed during ferroptosis, suggesting its potential involvement in this process. Subsequently, we generated stable cell lines overexpressing and knocking down KLF13. Cytotoxicity assays and reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection demonstrated that overexpression of KLF13 promoted ferroptosis induced by IKE and RSL3 in LUAD cells, whereas silencing KLF13 inhibited ferroptosis. Furthermore, overexpression of KLF13 enhanced the chemotherapeutic efficacy of cisplatin and pemetrexed. RNA-seq, qPCR, and western blot experiments revealed that KLF13 downregulated the RNA and protein expression of GPX4. ChIP assays and dual-luciferase reporter gene assays indicated that KLF13 directly bound to the promoter region of GPX4, inhibiting transcriptional activity of GPX4. Additionally, overexpression and knockdown of GPX4 could reverse the effects of KLF13 on ferroptosis and chemosensitivity. These findings were further confirmed through immunohistochemical staining and animal experiments.

## Conclusions
Our study reveals that KLF13 promotes ferroptosis in LUAD by inhibiting GPX4, thereby enhancing sensitivity to chemotherapy drugs. Overall, targeting KLF13 may contribute to developing new therapeutic strategies for LUAD.

## Background
Currently, lung cancer stands as one of the most prevalent malignancies globally, accounting for a substantial proportion of tumor-related mortality. Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) constitutes 80%, with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) being the predominant histological subtype [ , ]. Advanced LUAD is characterized by aggressive progression, frequent distant metastasis, and poor prognosis [ , ]. Platinum-based chemotherapy has long been the cornerstone of LUAD treatment. Recent advances integrating immunotherapy with chemotherapy have significantly improved therapeutic outcomes for LUAD patients, thereby broadening the scope of application for chemotherapy [ ]. However, drug resistance remains a significant limitation. Moreover, the intricate mechanisms underlying tumor development and drug resistance remain elusive, emphasizing the urgent need to uncover novel targets or mechanisms that transcend traditional transcriptional regulation.

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death, arises from an overabundance of iron ions, the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and subsequent lipid peroxidation [ – ]. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a key inhibitor of ferroptosis, mitigates potentially harmful lipid hydroperoxides by converting them into less toxic, thereby safeguarding cells against ferroptosis [ – ]. Recent evidence underscores that chemotherapy, at least partially, operates through the induction of ferroptosis. Furthermore, numerous preclinical investigations have showed robust antitumor efficacy by targeting a key regulator of lipid peroxide metabolism to elicit ferroptosis [ – ]. As a member of the Kruppel-like transcription factor family, KLF13 plays a critical role in the development of the cardiovascular and nervous systems. Its tumor-suppressive function has been increasingly recognized across multiple cancers. KLF13 overexpression in colorectal cancer (CRC) enhances cisplatin resistance [ ]. KLF13 also suppresses tumorigenesis in thyroid cancer (THCA) by inhibiting IFIT1 [ ]. Wang et al. reported that KLF13 inhibits prostate cancer cell proliferation through AKT inactivation [ ]. Conversely, KLF13 promotes progression in hepatocellular carcinoma and oral cancer. Prognostically, KLF13 expression is significantly downregulated in both glioma and gastric cancer, correlating with improved clinical outcomes [ , ]. However, its role in LUAD prognosis and progression remains unexplored.
Through the integration of transcriptional sequencing and experimental validation, we have uncovered a significant alteration in the expression of KLF13 within LUAD cells after exposure to ferroptosis-inducing agents (FINs), indicating its potential role as a key mediator in ferroptosis. Subsequent investigations have revealed that KLF13 fosters ferroptosis in LUAD both in vitro and in vivo, therefore augmenting the efficacy of chemotherapy agents. Mechanistically, ChIP-seq, transcriptome analysis, and rescue experiments demonstrated that KLF13 exerts these effects by suppressing GPX4. Collectively, these discoveries point to the targeting of the KLF13-GPX4 axis as a promising and innovative therapeutic approach for the management of LUAD.

## Results

## KLF13 was involved in ferroptosis in LUAD
To investigate ferroptosis, A549 cells were treated with DMSO (control) or RSL3 (FIN) for 48 h, followed by RNA sequencing after TRIzol-mediated RNA extraction to identify differentially expressed genes (Fig. A) [ ]. Subsequent analysis revealed a significant decrease in KLF13 mRNA expression following FIN treatment (Fig. B). Further validation through PCR and western blot assays on A549 and PC-9 cells subjected to RSL3 and IKE concentration gradients unveiled a dose-dependent reduction in both KLF13 mRNA and protein levels after the induction of ferroptosis (Fig. C–E). Upon cotreatment with ferroptosis inhibitors, we observed a restoration of KLF13 expression levels (Fig. F–I). These findings suggested that KLF13 might be a critical regulator of ferroptosis.
 

## KLF13 promoted ferroptosis and chemotherapy sensitivity in LUAD
To further corroborate the role of KLF13 in ferroptosis, we transfected A549 and PC-9 cells with lentivirus and validated the transfection efficacy. PCR and western blot analyses conclusively demonstrated the successful establishment of both KLF13-overexpressing and knockdown cell lines (Fig. A, B). As illustrated in Fig. C and D, KLF13 overexpression prominently potentiated ferroptosis elicited by RSL3 and IKE, whereas downregulation of KLF13 markedly dampened ferroptosis. Subsequent experiments underscored the efficacy of ferroptosis inhibitors, ferrostatin-1 and liproxstatin-1, in reversing the ferroptosis-inducing effects orchestrated by KLF13, whereas necroptosis inhibitor (necrosulfonamide) and apoptosis inhibitor (Z-VAD-FMK) remained ineffectual, reinforcing KLF13 promoted ferroptosis rather than other forms of cell death (Fig. E–H). These observations were further substantiated through the lipid peroxidation assay (Fig. A, B). Moreover, we delved into the influence of KLF13 on chemotherapeutic responsiveness, revealing that KLF13 overexpression significantly bolstered cellular sensitivity to cisplatin (CDDP) and pemetrexed (PEM), whereas silencing KLF13 conferred drug resistance (Fig. C, D). When chemotherapy drugs were combined with ferroptosis inhibitors, the efficacy of chemotherapeutic drugs could be partially reversed (Fig. E, F). Collectively, these findings underscored the role of KLF13 enhanced chemotherapeutic efficacy by augmenting ferroptosis, thereby presenting a promising therapeutic target in cancer management.
 

## KLF13 was involved in promoting ferroptosis by suppressing GPX4
To delve into the precise mechanism underlying KLF13’s role in ferroptosis and chemosensitivity, we initiated a comprehensive RNA sequencing analysis of KLF13-CTRL, KLF13-SH1, and KLF13-SH2 samples (Fig. A). Drawing upon prior research, we postulated that KLF13 operates as a repressive transcriptional regulator. Consequently, we refined our target gene identification strategy by intersecting the upregulated genes following KLF13 knockdown from RNA-seq data with downstream genes of KLF13 curated in GTRD and gene sets associated with ferroptosis (Fig. B, C). After rigorous screening, GPX4, MAPK8, and STAT3 emerged as candidate genes, which we subsequently validated in A549 and PC-9 cell lines. Our findings revealed that the mRNA levels of MAPK8 and STAT3 remained unaffected by KLF13 modulation (Fig. D, E). In contrast, overexpression of KLF13 significantly diminished GPX4 mRNA expression, while its knockdown elicited an increase in GPX4 expression. This regulatory pattern was consistent at the protein level as well (Fig. F, G). Simultaneously, we examined the expression of pivotal genes of ferroptosis in each group. The results indicated that the expression of SLC7A11, ACSL4, and FSP1 was not regulated by KLF13 (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A–D). Collectively, these results strongly suggested that KLF13 exerted a regulatory function in ferroptosis by modulating the expression of GPX4.
 

## KLF13 bound to the GPX4 promoter to inhibit expression
To further elucidate KLF13’s regulatory mechanism over GPX4 expression, we leveraged the Encode database, which revealed a pronounced enrichment of KLF13 within the promoter region proximal to the transcription start site (TSS) of GPX4 (Fig. A). Following the 5′ to 3′ direction convention, we designated the transcription start site (TSS) of GPX4 as position 0. Genomic bases upstream of the TSS are assigned negative values ( −), while downstream bases are assigned positive values ( +). Our study identified three regions where KLF13 bound to the GPX4 promoter, located at − 2000 to − 1714, − 1324 to − 674, and − 464 to + 200. Subsequently, we harnessed the JASPAR database to pinpoint presumed transcription factor binding sites (BS) for KLF13 and designed three pairs of primers. The relative positions of the three pairs of primers we designed are as follows: primer 1: − 1954 to − 1559, primer 2: − 1127 to − 766, and primer 3: − 542 to − 148 (Fig. B, C). The results of ChIP-PCR revealed a substantial occupancy of KLF13 at the predicted BS within the GPX4 promoter, as evidenced by a marked enrichment of DNA fragments with anti-KLF13 antibodies compared to the control IgG (Fig. D). To decipher the functional implications of this interaction, we devised a reporter assay employing firefly luciferase plasmids that incorporated either wild-type (WT) or mutant-type (MT) putative BS of GPX4 promoter and transfected them into 293T cells following negative control or overexpression of KLF13. Our findings indicated a specific decrease in firefly luciferase activity solely in cells harboring the WT-GPX4 promoter plasmid, whereas no such attenuation was observed in cells transfected with the MT-promoter variant (Fig. E). These outcomes conclusively demonstrated that KLF13 directly bound to the GPX4 promoter, thereby inhibiting its transcriptional activity.
 

## GPX4 reversed the effect of KLF13 on ferroptosis and chemosensitivity in LUAD
To demonstrate that KLF13 modulates ferroptosis and chemotherapy resistance in LUAD via GPX4, we overexpressed GPX4 in A549 and PC-9 cells with KLF13 overexpression and conversely knocked down GPX4 in tumor cells with KLF13 silencing. The successful establishment of these stable cell lines was confirmed through PCR and WB analysis (Fig. A–C, Additional file 1: Fig. S2A–C). Upon exposure to ferroptosis inducers RSL3 and IKE, we observed that GPX4 overexpression effectively counteracted the stimulatory effect of KLF13 overexpression on ferroptosis, whereas GPX4 knockdown intensified the sensitivity of KLF13-deficient cells to these inducers (Fig. D, E, Additional file 1: Fig. S2D, E). Furthermore, ROS detection assays revealed that GPX4 overexpression mitigated the ROS surge in KLF13-overexpressing cells, whereas GPX4 knockdown exacerbated it (Fig. G, H, Additional file 1: Fig. S2G, H). Notably, in response to chemotherapy agents cisplatin and pemetrexed, cells co-overexpressing GPX4 and KLF13 (GPX4-OE + KLF13-OE) exhibited heightened resistance, whereas GPX4 suppression in KLF13-knockdown cells restored their sensitivity to these drugs (Fig. F, Additional file 1: Fig. S2F, Additional file 1: Fig. S3A, B). The ferroptosis inhibitor, when co-treated with CDDP and PEM, completely reverses this effect of GPX4 (Additional file 1: Fig. S3C–F). These results suggested that KLF13 promoted ferroptosis and chemotherapy sensitivity through GPX4.
 

## KLF13 enhanced ferroptosis and chemotherapy sensitivity in vivo and was inversely associated with tumor proliferation and progression
To validate these findings in vivo, we embarked on animal tumor models. Upon administering IKE and CDDP/PEM via the intraperitoneal route, we discerned a pronounced attenuation in tumor dimensions and mass in KLF13-overexpressing mice compared to the control cohort, with GPX4 expression conspicuously reversing this KLF13-mediated effect (Fig. A, B). Bioinformatic interrogation revealed that diminished KLF13 levels in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues and elevated KLF13 expression portended improved clinical outcomes (Fig. C, F). To further explore the clinical implications of KLF13 in tumor patients, we compiled clinicopathological data from 110 LUAD patients, with baseline patient characteristics outlined in Additional file 1: Table S1. Utilizing both univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models, we determined that heightened KLF13 expression emerged as an independent and significant predictor of overall survival (OS) (Fig. D). IHC staining on clinical patient samples and mice tumors underscored the relatively lower KLF13 expression in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues, accompanied by a negative relation between GPX4 and KLF13 expression level (Fig. E, Additional file 1: Fig. S4A). Moreover, Kaplan–Meier analysis encompassing the cohort of 110 LUAD patients illuminated that patients exhibiting high KLF13 expression manifested enhanced OS and RFS (Fig. G). We analyzed data collected from 53 of 110 LUAD patients, who underwent postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy (ACT). To categorize patients, we defined patients who relapsed before the end of follow-up as chemotherapy-resistant and those who did not relapse as chemotherapy-sensitive. Analysis showed that elevated KLF13 expression levels and diminished GPX4 expression were significantly associated with better chemotherapy effects (Additional file 1: Table S2). In conclusion, these findings underscored the compelling potential of KLF13 as a therapeutic target in the management of LUAD.
 

## Discussion
The latest global cancer statistics for 2024 highlight the dominant role of lung cancer, representing 12.4% of all cancer cases and causing 18.7% of cancer-related deaths worldwide [ ]. In NSCLC management, platinum-based combination therapies remain the standard of care; however, intrinsic or acquired chemoresistance persists in a significant patient subset [ ]. The molecular mechanisms underlying NSCLC chemoresistance involve intricate crosstalk between intracellular signaling pathways and extracellular microenvironmental factors, posing a major clinical challenge [ ]. Amidst this backdrop, ferroptosis has emerged as a promising avenue in the quest to overcome chemotherapy resistance, fostering numerous investigations that herald its potential significance.
Ferroptosis is a novel form of iron-dependent regulatory cell death resulting from the accumulation of ROS and lipid peroxidation [ , ]. Accumulating evidence underscores the potential of harnessing natural compounds to elicit ferroptosis in chemotherapy-refractory cancers, significantly augmenting the efficacy of chemotherapeutic interventions [ – ]. In our prior investigation [ ], we performed transcriptome sequencing on A549 cells after DMSO and RSL3 treatment for 48 h. Intriguingly, we observed a downregulation of KLF13 expression. To further explore this phenomenon, we subjected A549 and PC-9 cells to RSL3 and IKE treatments, respectively, and analyzed the ensuing alterations in mRNA and protein expression levels via qPCR and western blot assays. Our findings revealed a concentration-dependent decrease in KLF13 expression upon ferroptosis induction, and ferroptosis inhibitors restored KLF13 expression levels, prompting us to hypothesize a potential involvement of KLF13 in ferroptosis process.
KLF13, a constituent of the Kruppel-like family of transcription factors, is endowed with three C2H2 zinc fingers positioned at its C-terminal end, imparting structural specificity and functionality [ ]. The KLF1-17 spectrum can be segregated into two discernible classes based on their transcriptional proclivities: activators and repressors, thus forming an intricate and interconnected regulatory network [ ]. Studies have reported that KLF11, a member of the KLF transcription factor family, enhances radiotherapy and thermotherapy efficacy in lung adenocarcinoma and KLF11 regulates ferroptosis through targets including GPX4, NCOA4, and FSP1 [ ]. Intriguingly, Wang et al. have reported a decrement in KLF13 expression within prostate cancer cells, where it exerts an antiproliferative effect by attenuating AKT signaling [ ]. In colorectal tumors, Yao et al. have elucidated the tumor-suppressive nature of KLF13, which negatively modulates HMGCS1-mediated cholesterol biosynthesis [ ]. One investigation underscores KLF13’s tumor-suppressive role by orchestrating the autophagy-mediated degradation of β-catenin, leading to the downregulation of CCND1 and MYC in gastric cancer [ , ]. However, there is currently no research regarding the impact of KLF13 on prognosis and tumor progression in LUAD. For the first time, our study demonstrated KLF13’s positive impact on LUAD prognosis, its role in improving chemotherapy resistance, and its promotion of ferroptosis by regulating GPX4, thereby first identifying the significant potential of KLF13 as a therapeutic target for LUAD. Although KLF13 is not the most differentially expressed gene in RNA-seq data, considering its innovation, we believe it remains valuable for further investigation, and thus we continue to study KLF13 in depth.
In this research, we transfected A549 and PC-9 cells with lentiviruses to establish KLF13 overexpression and knockdown cell lines. Subsequently, each group of cells was exposed to ferroptosis inducers, revealing that a pivotal role for KLF13 in augmenting ferroptosis upon overexpression and conferring resistance against ferroptosis inducers upon downregulation in tumor cells. The cells were then treated with ferroptosis inhibitors, necroptosis inhibitors, and apoptosis inhibitors, demonstrating that ferroptosis distinct from apoptosis and necroptosis occurred in tumor cells. Additionally, ROS detection demonstrated that KLF13 promoted the accumulation of lipid peroxidation.
The relationship between ferroptosis and chemotherapy has been documented in previous studies. CDDP and PEM induce cellular damage by disrupting DNA structure and promoting mitochondrial ROS accumulation, further accelerating lipid peroxidation and triggering ferroptosis [ , ]. In ferroptosis, GPX4 protects cells from oxidative damage by eliminating lipid peroxides. Inhibition of ferroptosis via GPX4 upregulation contributes to chemotherapy resistance [ ]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that CDDP/PEM-treated LUAD cell lines exhibit distinct ferroptosis characteristics, which can be partially reversed by ferroptosis inhibitors [ ]. Therefore, we proposed that KLF13 enhances ferroptosis by promoting chemotherapy efficacy. Thus, when tumor cells were treated with CDDP/PEM, a notable enhancement in drug sensitivity was observed in the presence of KLF13 overexpression. And when chemotherapeutic drugs and ferroptosis inhibitors were co-administered to cells, the efficacy of the chemotherapeutic drugs was partially reversed. Collectively, these findings underscored KLF13’s function in promoting chemotherapy sensitivity by enhancing iron death within tumor cells in tumor cells.
Among the intricate regulatory networks governing ferroptosis, the GSH-GPX4 system emerges as a critical antioxidant machinery, mitigating iron-catalyzed cell death [ , ]. GPX4, a selenocysteine-harboring enzyme, is localized in both mitochondria and cytoplasm, where it orchestrates the conversion of toxic lipid hydroperoxides (PUFAs-OOH) into innocuous lipid alcohols (PUFAs-OH), thereby thwarting the deleterious accumulation of lipid peroxides and safeguarding cells from oxidative insult [ – ]. In this study, we screened three candidate genes, GPX4, MAPK8, and STAT3, leveraging the ChIP data of KLF13 curated in GTRD, in conjunction with the ferroptosis-centric gene set and A549 RNA-seq outcomes. Employing PCR and western blot assays, we unveiled that the expression of GPX4 was suppressed by KLF13. After identifying a significant enrichment of KLF13 in the region of the transcription start site (TSS) of GPX4, as evidenced by the Encode database, we delved deeper into the JASPAR database to pinpoint the putative transcription factor binding site (BS) of KLF13 and designed 3 pairs of primers. Subsequently, we crafted three pairs of primers and executed a ChIP assay, which corroborated the robust enrichment of KLF13 within the predicted GPX4 promoter sequence. This direct interaction was further validated through a dual-luciferase reporter assay, conclusively demonstrating that KLF13 could bind directly to the promoter of GPX4, thereby repressing GPX4 transcription. Meanwhile, we treated both wild-type and mutant GPX4-expressing HEK-293T cells with DMSO or ferroptosis inducers (RSL3 and IKE) and measured fluorescence intensity changes. The results showed that compared to the control group, RSL3 and IKE treatment led to a slight increase in fluorescence intensity in wild-type GPX4 cells; however, no significant difference was observed between the treated wild-type cells and the control group or among the wild-type cell groups. This result ruled out the effect of ferroptosis inducers on the fluorescence intensity of cells of GPX4 wild-type or mutant-type promoter groups.
To substantiate these findings, we conducted rescue experiments, which not only corroborated the ability of KLF13 to promote ferroptosis and chemotherapy sensitivity in LUAD cells but also underscored GPX4’s capacity to reverse these effects, demonstrating the critical role of KLF13-GPX4 axis in modulating ferroptosis. Ye et al. demonstrated that KLF14 promotes ferroptosis by inhibiting GPX4 in cervical cancer [ ]. The question arises whether KLF13 and KLF14—both members of the KLF family—competitively inhibit GPX4. We argue that they do not. First, KLF13 and KLF14 exhibit distinct expression patterns in LUAD cells. Our transcriptome sequencing data from LUAD cells, alongside expression datasets from multiple LUAD cell lines in the DepMap database and TCGA LUAD cohort, revealed significantly lower KLF14 expression compared to KLF13 in LUAD. Second, while sharing conserved DNA-binding domains, KLF family members display distinct binding site preferences within target gene promoters. Existing studies show KLF14 binds a specific region (− 350 to + 50 bp) of the GPX4 promoter, whereas our study identified KLF13 binding sites at − 2000 to − 1714, − 1324 to − 674, and − 464 to + 200 bp. These spatial disparities in binding loci indicated that KLF13 and KLF14 occupied non-overlapping regions of the GPX4 promoter, precluding direct competition for binding to GPX4.
Our IHC analysis of clinical patient samples unveiled a pronounced downregulation of KLF13 expression in tumorous tissues, compared with normal tissues, manifesting a negative correlation with GPX4 expression and a positive association with 4-HNE expression. Integrating survival follow-up data from clinical patients with bioinformatics interrogation, our findings underscored the association between elevated KLF13 levels and favorable prognosis. Furthermore, our preclinical animal studies, utilizing IKE and CDDP/PEM treatments, demonstrated that KLF13 potently impeded tumor progression, underscoring its tumor-suppressive capabilities. Notably, GPX4 emerged as a pivotal mediator, capable of reversing the antitumorigenic effects elicited by KLF13. Collectively, this investigation underscores the pivotal role of the KLF13-GPX4 axis in fostering ferroptosis and enhancing sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents in LUAD. These findings underscore the profound therapeutic potential of targeting this axis as a novel strategy to combat LUAD, offering a promising avenue for precision medicine.
However, this study has some limitations. First, further molecular experiments and more clinical samples could be utilized to prove that the KLF13-GPX4 axis regulates ferroptosis and chemotherapy effects. Second, no specific drug has been developed for KLF13. Third, though we demonstrated that FIN treatment of LUAD cells reduced KLF13 expression levels, the molecular mechanisms underlying this effect have not been thoroughly investigated. Therefore, further exploration of the upstream regulatory molecular mechanisms of KLF13 and the development of effective and safe KLF13-specific drugs are important directions for future research.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, our study demonstrated that KLF13 enhances ferroptosis by suppressing the transcription GPX4, thereby enhancing chemotherapy efficacy in LUAD. Elevated KLF13 expression correlates with improved prognosis of LUAD patients, suggesting its potential as a diagnostic biomarker. Therefore, our study identifies KLF13 as a promising therapeutic target for LUAD, providing a novel strategy to overcome chemotherapy resistance.

## Methods

## Patient selection
With the acquisition of informed consent, paraffin-embedded specimens of tumors and adjacent normal tissue counterparts were collected from 110 patients with LUAD who underwent thoracic surgeries at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University, spanning the period from 2018 to 2020. All patients were selected based on pathological diagnosis of lung adenocarcinoma. All diagnostic confirmations were performed through computed tomography (CT) scans for disease recurrence, PET-CT imaging, or pathological biopsy examinations. The pathological classification adhered to the guidelines stipulated by the World Health Organization (WHO). Furthermore, tumor staging was determined based on the TNM classification system, as outlined in the eighth edition of the AJCC (American Joint Committee on Cancer) Manual of Cancer Staging. All patients have provided written informed consent, and this research was carried out in adherence to the ethical principles enshrined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was granted ethical clearance by the Research Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (B2022-180R).

## Cell culture and compounds
The human LUAD cell lines, A549, PC-9, and HEK-293T, were purchased from the Chinese Academy of Science Cell Bank and cultured in a nutrient-rich environment comprising high-glucose Dulbecco Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), fortified with 10% fetal bovine serum sourced from EveryGreen (Zhejiang, China), and supplemented with 100 U/mL of penicillin/streptomycin obtained from Hyclone (Logan, UT, USA). All experiments were conducted using low passage cells (< 30 passages). In 2024, these cell lines were identified through short tandem repeat (STR) analysis and were passaged every 3 to 5 days based on their proliferation rates. This cultivation was conducted in a 95% air and 5% CO2 humidified incubator, meticulously maintained at 37 °C to ensure optimal growth conditions. To safeguard against contamination, all cell lines underwent rigorous and periodic mycoplasma screening, with the experimental cells confirming a negative status for mycoplasma. The reagents employed in our study, namely RSL3 (T3646), IKE (T1765), ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1; T6500), liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1; T2376), Z-VAD-FMK (Z-VAD; T7020), necrosulfonamide (Necro; T6904), cis-diammineplatinum(II) dichloride (CDDP, T1564), and pemetrexed (PEM, T6226), were provided by Topscience (Shanghai, China). Taking into account their distinct solubility profiles, the former six compounds were meticulously dissolved in DMSO, whereas the latter two were dissolved in PBS also sourced from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). These prepared solutions were then stored at − 80 °C for future utilization, ensuring their stability and readiness for experimentation.

## Transfection of siRNA, lentivirus, and gene overexpression
We employed two KLF13-specific siRNAs (designated as siRNA-1 and siRNA-2), alongside two Ribobio-designed negative control siRNAs (Ribobio, Guangzhou, China), which were efficiently delivered into cells utilizing Lipo8000 transfection reagent (Beyotime) in conjunction with Opti-MEM medium (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The siRNA sequences were provided in Additional file 1: Table S3. Guided by their proven efficacy in silencing the KLF13 gene, we subsequently crafted two shRNAs from the siRNA sequences and devised an overexpression construct, which was subsequently subcloned into a puromycin-selectable lentiviral vector (Genechem, Shanghai, China). Following lentiviral transduction, cells underwent a 48-h puromycin selection process.

## RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
Following the manufacturer’s protocol, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol® reagent (Tiangen, Beijing, China). Subsequently, the total RNA was reverse-transcribed into single-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) using the HiScript III first-strand cDNA synthesis kit (YEASEN, Shanghai, China). The qRT-PCR analysis was conducted on the ABI QuantStudio 5 real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher, USA) with Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (YEASEN), and GAPDH was utilized as a reference gene for normalizing relative expression levels. Gene expression levels were determined by the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers for each gene were custom-synthesized by Sangon Biotech and are detailed in Additional file 1: Table S4.

## RNA-seq
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Germany), and then the sequencing was conducted by OE Biotech (Shanghai, China). In accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines, the library was constructed utilizing the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Preparation Kit. Subsequently, the library was sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq™ 6000 platform, generating paired-end reads of 150 base pairs in length. Utilizing the fastp tool, the raw reads in fastq format were processed to meticulously filter out low-quality sequences, ensuring an optimal reading quality suitable for project validation. Clean reads were then mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2. Following this, the Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) for each gene were computed, while the read counts for each gene were obtained through HTseq counting. Subsequently, the limma and ggplot 2 software packages in R software were used to analyze and visualize differentially expressed genes (DEGs). We established standards for p values < 0.05 and | log twofold changes |> 0.5.

## Western blot (WB) experiments
The cells were lysed on ice with a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Topscience, Shanghai, China) along with RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 15 min. Following centrifugation at 12,000 × g at 4 °C for 15 min, the supernatant was collected and quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (YEASEN). Twenty micrograms of protein was resolved on a 12.5% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel (Epizyme Biotech, Shanghai, China), then transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). After blocking with TBST containing 5% skim milk powder for 1 h, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with various primary antibodies. Subsequently, a secondary antibody conjugated with peroxidase (anti-mouse second antibody: dilution: 1:3000; catalog number M21001S; Abmart; anti-rabbit IgG second antibody: dilution: 1:3000; catalog number M21002S; Abmart) was applied for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected by BeyoECL Moon Chemiluminescence Reagent (Beyotime). The primary antibodies used in this study are shown in Additional file 1: Table S5.

## Detection of lipid peroxidation (LPO)
As previously reported [ , ], C11-BODIPYTM 581/59 (D3861; Thermo Fisher Technologies) was employed for the quantification of lipid peroxidation. Upon oxidation, C11-BODIPY exhibited a fluorescence emission peak shift from 590 to 510 nm, directly correlating with the production of lipid ROS. Following incubation with C11-BODIPY at a final concentration of 5 μM at 37 °C for 30 min, cells underwent three washes with PBS. Analysis was performed using the BD Accuri C6 Plus flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), with a minimum of 10,000 cells being evaluated under each condition.

## Cell viability assays
In the cytotoxicity assay, 3000 cells were seeded per well in 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, various concentrations of ferroptosis inducers or chemotherapy drugs were added to treat the cells for 48 h. Cell viability was assessed using CCK-8 (Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocol, and absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader. The experiment was repeated three times.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Tissue specimens, originating from NSCLC patients and preserved through formalin fixation and paraffin embedding, were resected to yield 4 μm thick sections. Following the manufacturer’s instructions (GeneTech, Shanghai, China), the samples were processed using the GTVisionTM + assay system/Mo&Rb immunohistochemical kit. This protocol entailed a series of steps, including dewaxing and rehydration facilitated by xylene and ethanol solvents, antigenic retrieval achieved through citric acid solution treatment, and subsequent blocking of endogenous peroxidase activity. Following a 1-h incubation period at room temperature, the slides were subjected to an incubation with the primary antibodies—anti-KLF13 (1:100, A17158, ABclonal), anti-GPX4 (1:100, CY6959; Abways), and anti-4HNE (1:50, ab48506, Abcam)—at 4 °C overnight for optimal results. Post-primary antibody incubation, the slides underwent meticulous washing with PBS and were then further incubated with a secondary antibody at 37 °C for 30 min, prior to another thorough PBS wash. Finally after DAB reagent incubation, the tissue staining intensity was observed under a bright field microscope. Immunoreactivity was evaluated in a blinded manner using ImageJ software based on staining intensity and staining extent. The staining intensity of pigmented cells was scored as follows: 0 (colorless/negative), 1 (light yellow/low), 2 (dark yellow/moderate), and 3 (tan/high).

## Dual-luciferase reporter assays
As Zhao et al. reported [ ], the wild-type or mutant plasmid containing the GPX4 promoter region was transfected into 293T cells, with either KLF13-OE and KLF13-CTRL plasmids utilizing Lipo8000 (Beyotime) as the transfection vehicle. Post 48 h of incubation, the cells were subsequently harvested for the purpose of conducting a dual-luciferase reporter assay, facilitated by the Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Beyotime). Ultimately, the assessment of luciferase activity was achieved through the utilization of a microplate spectrophotometer manufactured (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analysis
As documented by Bi et al. [ ], we utilized SimpleChIP® Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) to conduct our experiments. To initiate the cross-linking process of proteins and DNA, cells were initially subjected to a 10-min treatment with 1% formaldehyde. Subsequently, a micrococcal ribonuclease-mediated fragmentation of the cellular chromatin ensued, yielding DNA/protein fragments with sizes ranging from 150 to 900 base pairs. Subsequently, an overnight incubation was performed at 4 °C, where these chromatin fragments were immersed with either IgG or an anti-Flag antibody (diluted 1:50, catalog number 14793S, Cell Signaling Technology). Protein-DNA complexes were sequestered utilizing protein G magnetic beads, followed by the elution of chromatin that underwent a process of crosslink reversal. DNA fragments were purified through the employment of spin columns and subsequently quantified using qRT-PCR. The primer sequences utilized for ChIP-qPCR are detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

## Tumor xenograft model in nude mice
Animal experimentation was carried out in strict adherence to the regulations stipulated by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, as well as the prevailing statutory frameworks and institutional protocols governing animal welfare and ethical usage. The animals were housed in the animal laboratory of our institute. The animal laboratory maintains a standard 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, with a temperature of 22 ± 1 °C and humidity of 45–55%. All mice have unrestricted access to food and water. Each experimental cohort encompassed six BALB/c male mice, aged 4 weeks, into which a total of 2 × 106 cells were subcutaneously inoculated into the left side. At designated experimental intervals, precise measurements of tumor width (W) and length (L) were recorded using calibrated calipers. Two weeks after transplantation, mice were randomly assigned to three groups and received normal saline, IKE, or CDDP/PEM treatments. Four weeks post-transplantation, all mice were deeply anesthetized using inhaled isoflurane and subsequently euthanized via cervical dislocation, followed by the excision of their tumors, which were preserved for in-depth analysis.

## Statistics and reproducibility
All statistics were conducted utilizing R software (version 4.1.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and GraphPad Prism 7.0 software. Unpaired Student’s t-test and two-sided one-way ANOVA were used to determine differences in continuous variables when appropriate. For survival analysis, Kaplan–Meier analysis was employed, complemented by a log-rank test for statistical significance. Furthermore, Cox proportional hazards regression models were leveraged to identify independent prognostic indicators. All tests were two-tailed and a significance threshold of p < 0.05 was adhered to throughout all analyses. Unless otherwise specified in the figure legends, data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and the experimental protocol was replicated three times to ensure reproducibility and robustness.
