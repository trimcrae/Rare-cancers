Excessive alcohol use causes AM oxidative stress, which impairs AM phagocytosis and pathogen clearance from the alveolar space. Alcohol induces expression of NADPH oxidases (Noxes), primary sources of oxidative stress in AM. In contrast, alcohol decreases AM peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), a critical regulator of AM immune function. To explore the underlying molecular mechanisms for these effects of alcohol, we hypothesized that ethanol promotes CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein beta (C/EBPβ)-mediated suppression of Nox-related microRNAs (miRs), in turn enhancing AM Nox expression, oxidative stress, and phagocytic dysfunction. We also hypothesized that PPARγ activation with pioglitazone (PIO) would reverse alcohol-induced C/EBPβ expression and attenuate AM oxidative stress and phagocytic dysfunction. Cells from the mouse AM cell line (MH-S) were exposed to ethanol in vitro or primary AM were isolated from mice fed ethanol in vivo. Ethanol enhanced C/EBPβ expression, decreased Nox 1-related miR-1264 and Nox 2-related miR-107 levels, and increased Nox1, Nox2, and Nox 4 expression in MH-S cells in vitro and mouse AM in vivo. These alcohol-induced AM derangements were abrogated by loss of C/EBPβ, overexpression of miRs-1264 or −107, or PIO treatment. These findings identify C/EBPβ and Nox-related miRs as novel therapeutic targets for PPARγ ligands, which could provide a translatable strategy to mitigate susceptibility to lung infections in people with a history of AUD. These studies further clarify the molecular underpinnings for a previous clinical trial using short-term PIO treatment to improve AM immunity in AUD individuals.

## Introduction
Over 100 million people worldwide had been diagnosed with an alcohol use disorder (AUD) in 2016 ( ), and physicians have recognized that alcohol use is associated with lung infections for more than a century ( ). Compared to non-AUD individuals, people with AUD have higher rates of hospitalizations and longer hospital stays ( ; ), in part due to increased risk of major complications like pneumonia ( ). AUD increases susceptibility to respiratory infections through impairment of the innate immune system ( ).
Excessive alcohol use impairs mucociliary clearance from the upper airway ( ; ), barrier integrity of the alveolar epithelium ( ), and alveolar macrophage (AM) phagocytic capacity ( ; , ). Alcohol-induced oxidative stress mediates lung endothelial barrier dysfunction ( ) and AM phagocytic dysfunction ( , ). Prior studies found that NADPH oxidases (Nox) constitute interrelated sources of alcohol-induced AM oxidative stress in AM, the first line of cellular immune defense within the alveolar space. Activation of either Nox 1 or Nox 2 led to increases in Nox 4, whereas blocking either Nox1 or Nox2 partially decreased alcohol-induced levels of Nox4. Additionally, blocking both Nox1 and Nox2 exhibited a more robust decrease in alcohol-induced Nox4 expression and activity and partially restored phagocytic function ( ). We recently extended these observations to human AM (hAM) isolated from subjects with AUD. We demonstrated that ethanol activates the transcription factor CCAAT/enhancer binding protein beta (C/EBPβ) which suppresses miR-1264 and miR-107 and upregulates hAM Nox1 and Nox2 and oxidative stress. Further, ex vivo treatment of hAM with the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) ligand, pioglitazone (PIO), reversed ethanol-induced hAM oxidative stress and phagocytic dysfunction ( ).
The current study extends this report in several important ways. First, our findings illustrate that commonly employed experimental models of alcohol-induced pathobiology accurately replicate the molecular dysregulation observed in human AM. We exposed the mouse AM cell line, MH-S, to alcohol in vitro and mice to chronic alcohol ingestion in vivo ( , ; ) to reproduce critical features of AUD-induced AM dysfunction ( ). Second, the mouse model permits in vivo exploration of novel pharmacological therapies in the setting of chronic alcohol exposure. Based on our data in hAM ( ), the current study focuses on microRNAs (miRs), a common mechanism of post-transcriptional destabilization of the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of target mRNAs to reduce target protein levels. We focused on the transcription factor C/EBPβ, a redox-sensitive transcription factor up-regulated in the liver following chronic ethanol ingestion ( ; ). C/EBPβ suppressed the promoter activity of Nox-related miRs to increase AM Nox levels ( ). The current studies thereby define novel mechanisms whereby PIO attenuates alcohol-mediated increases in C/EBPβ and reductions in Nox-related miRs to reduce AM oxidative stress and dysfunction. Elucidating the molecular underpinnings of PIO as an intervention to restore AM function in experimental models of human AUD provides important validation for future clinical studies.

## Materials and methods

## MH-S cells
The mouse alveolar macrophage cell line, MH-S (ATCC; Manassas, Virginia, United States), was utilized to study the effects of ethanol in vitro. MH-S cells were cultured for 24 h in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS plus 1% penicillin/streptomycin, followed by treatment ±0.08% (17 mM) ethanol for 3 days ± 10-μM pioglitazone (PIO) during the last 24 h of ethanol exposure ( ). Ethanol-stimulated cells were incubated in a modular chamber (Billups-Rothenberg, Inc.; Del Mar, California, United States) to prevent alcohol evaporation. MH-S cells were collected for analysis 24 h following the last control or ethanol treatment. Exposure of MH-S cells to 0.08% or 17-mM ethanol for 3 days results in similar experimental findings as AM from our in vivo mouse model of chronic ethanol ingestion and AM isolated from human subjects with a history of AUD ( ; , ; ).

## Mouse model of chronic ethanol consumption, alveolar macrophage isolation, and intranasal administration
The Atlanta Veterans Affairs Health Care System Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee reviewed and approved all animal protocols, and studies were performed in accordance with NIH guidelines in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Primary mouse alveolar macrophages (mAM) were isolated from 20-week-old mice, where 8–10-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice (Jackson Laboratory; Bar Harbor, Maine, United States) were given either no ethanol or ethanol (20% w/v) in their drinking water for 12 weeks ( ; ), resulting in clinically relevant blood alcohol levels (0.12%) ( ). Since studies of chronic alcohol ingestion have shown similar phagocytic derangements in AM isolated from male and female mice ( ; ), only male mice were used for these studies. Control (Con) and ethanol-fed mice were gavaged once daily with PIO (10 mg/kg in 100 μL methylcellulose vehicle) or with vehicle alone for the last 7 days of ethanol feeding ( ; ). Primary mAM were isolated 24 h following the last control, ethanol, and/or PIO treatment. Following sacrifice, mouse tracheas were cannulated via tracheotomy, and BAL fluid was collected with three 1-mL saline lavages. The BAL fluid was centrifuged to isolate mAM. The cell pellet population was determined to be ~95% mAM after differential staining with Diff-Quik (Dade Behring; Newark, Delaware, United States) and counting with a hemocytometer. mAM were cultured for 24 h in RPMI-1640 medium containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin ( ).
In separate studies, primary mAM were isolated from mice receiving control drinking water or ethanol for 12-weeks, and during the final 3 days, daily intranasal administration of 50-μL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing plasmid vectors for: a) 500-pM scrambled microRNA (miR)-1264 mimic (miR-1264-Scr); b) 500-pM miR-1264 mimic (miR-1264-M); c) 500-pM scrambled miR-107 mimic (miR-107-Scr); d) 500-pM miR-107 mimic (miR107-M); e) 5-nM scrambled CCAAT-enhancer-binding protein (C/EBP)β silencing RNA (siRNA) (C/EBPβ-Scr); or f) 5-nM C/EBPβ siRNA (siC/EBPβ). Primary mAM were isolated 24 h following the last intranasal treatment.

## Transient transfections
MH-S cells (1 × 106 cells) or primary isolated (1 × 106 cells) mAM were resuspended in 100 μL of Amaxa Mouse Macrophage Nucleofector Kit solution (Lonza) containing 50 nM of miR-1264-Scr, miR-107-Scr, combined miR-1264-Scr + miR-107-Scr, miR-1264-M, miR-107-M, or combined miR-1264-M + miR-107-M, or 20 nM of C/EBPβ-Scr or siC/EBPβ, followed by nucleofection according to the manufacturer’s protocol using program Y-001. Transfected MH-S cells were then cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS plus 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and transfected mAM were then cultured in RPMI-1640 medium containing 2% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin for 24 h.

## microRNA and RNA isolation and quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR)
The species-conserved database, TargetScan 5.1 and in silico analysis of the 3′ untranslated regions (UTR) of NADPH oxidase (Nox)1 and Nox 2 were used to identify binding sites for miRs-1264 and −1982 on the Nox 1 3′UTR and miRs-107 and −103 on the Nox2 3′UTR. mirVana miRNA isolation kits (Ambion-Applied Biosystems; Carlsbad, California) were used to isolate miRs from MH-S cells or mAM (1 × 106 cells each). miR levels were measured and quantified using methods previously described ( ) and specific miR primers for miR-1264–5p, miR-1982–5p, miR-103–5p, and miR-107–5p as described ( ). Values for each target are expressed relative to miRNA levels of ribosomal 5U in the same sample.
TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen; Carlsbad, California) was used to extract total RNA from MH-S cells or mAM (1 × 6 106 cells each). mRNA levels of Noxes were measured and quantified using specific mRNA primers described previously ( ; ). mRNA levels of C/EBPβ were determined and quantified by qRT-PCR and using specific mRNA primers for mouse C/EBPβ (F) 5′-CAAGCTGAGCGACGAGTACA-3′ and (R) 5′-AGCTGCTCCACCTTCTTCTG-3′. Values for each target are expressed relative to mRNA levels of the 9s housekeeping gene in the same sample ( ; ; ; ; , ).

## Confocal and fluorescent immunomicroscopy
For localization of C/EBPβ, fluorescence of tetramethyl rhodamine (FITC)-labeled C/EBPβ was confirmed with confocal microscopy at 50% of the cell depth in cells co-stained with the nuclear indicator, 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 300-nM for 10 min), as previously described ( ). Protein levels of Noxes were assessed by fluorescent immunomicroscopy ( ), and levels of C/EBPβ were assessed by confocal immunomicroscopy ( ). Quantitating protein targets with these immunostaining methods correlated well with western blotting of the same targets in MH-S cells, as previously reported ( ). In brief, cultured MH-S cells and primary mAM were fixed to chamber slides with 4% paraformaldehyde. Cells (1.2 × 105) were then incubated with primary antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for Nox 1 (1:100), Nox 2 (1:100), Nox 4 (1:100), and C/EBPβ (1:50), followed by incubation with fluorescent FITC-labeled secondary antibodies. Confocal and fluorescent microscopy images were obtained with FluoView analysis (Olympus; Melville, New York, United States) following staining. Fluorescence units of other FITC-target-stained cells were normalized to DAPI nuclear stain in the same cells and expressed as fold-change of mean relative fluorescence units (RFU) per cell ± SEM, relative to untreated control samples. Experimental treatments did not significantly alter DAPI staining, and small within-group differences in DAPI staining were normalized as reported ( ). RFU were measured in at least 10 cells per field for which there were 10 fields per experimental condition. Microscope gain and gamma settings were consistent for each field and experimental condition.

## Cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production
Intracellular ROS production was assessed in MH-S cells or mAM (1.2 × 105 cells each) by incubating cultured cells with 5-μM 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, Invitrogen) in RPMI at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark ( , ). Fluorescence was measured using FluoView (Olympus; Melville, New York) via quantitative digital analysis as previously reported ( ). ROS production values are expressed as mean ± SEM, relative to average control values.
Amplex Red assay (Invitrogen) was used to measure hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) released into media collected from MH-S cells or mAM (1.2 × 105 cells each). Briefly, cells were incubated with 500 μL Amplex Red (20 μM) and horseradish peroxidase (0.1 U/mL) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark ( , ). Fluorescence of reaction mixtures was measured in duplicate (excitation 540 nm, emission 590 nm), and H2O2 concentrations were calculated utilizing standard curves as previously described ( ). H2O2 concentrations were normalized to cellular protein concentrations (determined using bicinchoninic acid [BCA] assay) and are expressed as mean ± SEM, relative to average control values.

## Phagocytosis
pHrodo Staphylococcus aureus BioParticles conjugate (Invitrogen) was used to determine in vitro phagocytic capacity ( ) of MH-S cells or mAM (1.2 × 105 cells each). As reported ( ), cells were incubated with 1 × 106 particles of pH-sensitive pHrodo S. aureus BioParticles conjugate for 2 h, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde. Phagocytosis of bacteria was analyzed using an Olympus confocal microscope, and cells from 10 fields per experimental condition were assessed using quantitative digital fluorescence imaging software (Olympus FluoView 300, Version 4.3). To measure S. aureus internalization, argon/krypton laser confocal microscopy was performed at 50% of cell depth using identical background and gain settings. All cells with internalized bacteria were considered positive for phagocytosis. Phagocytic index (calculated as the percentage of cells positive for phagocytosis multiplied by the RFU of S. aureus per cell) was used to quantify phagocytosis and is expressed as mean ± SEM, relative to average control values.

## Statistics
Data are presented as means ± SEM. Data were found to be normally distributed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. In studies with more than two groups from combining factors (for example, we performed one-way ANOVA by combining two factors [ethanol and miR-M] to one variable with four groups [control + miR-Scr, control + miR-M, ethanol + miR-Scr, ethanol + miR-M]), statistical significance was calculated using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey–Kramer post hoc tests to detect specific pair-wise differences between individual experimental groups (GraphPad Prism version 5; San Diego, California). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Nox 1-related miR-1264 mimic reverses ethanol-induced increases in Nox1 and Nox 4 expression, oxidative stress, and phagocytic dysfunction in MH-S cells
To investigate the molecular mechanisms responsible for alcohol-induced increases in AM Nox 1 ( ), we used in silico analysis to identify Nox1-related miR-1264 expression for further examination. Since transfecting human AM with miR-1264 mimic-restored miR-1264 expression ( ), we transfected MH-S cells with miR-1264 mimic. Compared to controls, ethanol decreased miR-1264 levels, which were restored by transfecting MH-S cells with miR-1264 mimic ( ). miR-1264 mimic downregulated basal and ethanol-induced Nox1 and Nox 4 mRNA levels ( ) and protein levels ( and ). miR-1264 mimic did not alter basal or ethanol-induced Nox 2 expression levels ( and ). In addition to regulating Nox expression, the roles of miR-1264 in regulating ethanol-induced AM oxidative stress and phagocytic dysfunction were examined. Compared to MH-S cells transfected with scrambled mimics, miR-1264 mimic decreased basal and partially reversed ethanol-mediated alterations in ROS production ( ), H2O2 levels ( ), and phagocytic index ( ).

## Nox 2-related miR-107 mimic reverses ethanol-induced alterations in Nox2 and Nox 4 expression, oxidative stress, and phagocytic dysfunction in MH-S cells
To investigate the molecular mechanisms responsible for alcohol-induced increases in AM Nox 2 ( ), we used in silico analysis to identify Nox2-related miR-107 expression for further examination. Since transfecting human AM with miR-107 mimic restored miR-107 expression ( ), we transfected MH-S cells with miR-107 mimic. Compared to controls, ethanol decreased miR-107 levels, which were restored by transfecting MH-S cells with miR-107 mimic ( ). miR-107 mimic downregulated basal and ethanolinduced Nox2 and Nox 4 mRNA levels ( ) and protein levels ( and ). miR-107 mimic did not alter basal or ethanol-induced Nox 1 expression levels ( and ). In addition to regulating Nox expression, the roles of miR-107 in regulating ethanol-induced AM oxidative stress and phagocytic dysfunction were examined. Compared to MH-S cells transfected with scrambled mimics, miR-107 mimic decreased basal and partially reversed ethanol-mediated alterations in ROS production ( ), H2O2 levels ( ), and phagocytic index ( ).

## miR mimics reverse ethanol-induced alterations in Nox expression, oxidative stress, and phagocytic function in primary mAM in vivo
To further explore the role of miRs in alcohol-mediated alterations in AM Nox expression, mAM were isolated from control or ethanol-fed mice treated intranasally with miR-1264 or miR-107 mimics or scrambled controls. Mimics of miR-1264 or miR-107 induced time-dependent increases in basal miR-1264 ( ) or miR-107 ( ) levels that peaked following three days of treatment. This treatment reversed ethanol-mediated decreases in miR-1264 and miR-107 levels. Previous studies confirmed that miR-1264 or miR-107 mimics directly regulate Nox 1 and Nox 2 3′UTR luciferase activity, respectively ( ). Intranasal miR-1264 mimic administration in vivo reduced mAM basal and ethanol-induced alterations in Nox1 and Nox 4 mRNA ( ) and protein ( and ) levels, ROS production ( ), H2O2 levels ( ), and phagocytic index ( ). Similarly, miR-107 mimics also attenuated alcohol-induced reductions in miR-107 levels and increases in Nox2 and Nox4, ROS, and phagocytic dysfunction in mAM ( – and ). Taken together, these data demonstrate that ethanolinduced alterations in Nox expression, oxidative stress, and phagocytic function in mAM in vivo can be diminished by increasing Nox-related miRs, subsequently downregulating Nox1 and Nox2 and preventing downstream upregulation of Nox4.

## Loss of C/EBPβ reverses ethanol-induced alterations in AM miRs, Nox expression, oxidative stress, and phagocytic function in vitro and in vivo
CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein beta (C/EBPβ) is a transcription factor that regulates miR expression and innate immune responses to microbial infections ( ). Since in silico promoter analysis suggested that C/EBPβ might also regulate miRs-1264 and −107 in primary human AM from subjects with a history of alcohol use disorders ( ), we determined whether ethanol altered C/EBPβ expression and whether silencing C/EBPβ might similarly reverse ethanol-induced AM derangements in these models. Ethanol increased mRNA and protein levels of C/EBPβ in MH-S cells more than 2-fold ( and ). MH-S cells were transiently transfected with siC/EBPβ or scrambled controls followed by treatment ± ethanol. siC/EBPβ attenuated alterations in ethanol-induced C/EBPβ mRNA ( ) and cytosolic and nuclear protein levels ( ); Nox 1-related miR-1264 and Nox 2-related miR-107 levels ( ); Nox1, Nox2, and Nox 4 mRNA ( ) and protein levels ( and ); ROS production ( ); H2O2 levels ( ); and phagocytic index ( ). Additionally, mAM were isolated from control and ethanol-fed mice that were intranasally treated with siC/EBPβ. Likewise, siC/EBPβ attenuated alcohol-induced alterations in miRNAs, Noxes, oxidative stress, and phagocytic function in mAM in vivo ( – and ). Collectively, these findings provide novel and compelling evidence that strategies to directly mitigate ethanol-induced C/EBPβ activation in AM can reverse Nox expression, downstream increases in oxidative stress, and subsequent phagocytic dysfunction.

## Pioglitazone treatment in vitro and in vivo reverses ethanol-mediated alterations in AM C/EBPβ, miR-1264 and miR-107 expression, Nox expression, oxidative stress, and phagocytic function
Pharmacological activation of the PPARγ receptor can mitigate oxidative stress in the lung ( ) and reverse alcohol-induced derangements in human AM ( ). We used models of alcohol exposure to further examine whether PPARγ activation with its ligand pioglitazone (PIO) could reverse ethanol-induced derangements in MH-S cells in vitro and in primary mAM from ethanol-fed mice in vivo. C/EBPβ mRNA and protein (cytosolic and nuclear) were upregulated in ethanol-exposed MH-S cells ( and ) and mAM from ethanol-fed mice ( and ). Ethanol increased C/EBPβ in cytosolic fractions, but more robust increases in nuclear C/EBPβ protein ( & ) indicate C/EBPβ transcriptional upregulation, nuclear translocation, and activation. PIO partially attenuated ethanol-induced increases in C/EBPβ and decreases in miR-1264 and −107 levels in MH-S cells in vitro ( – ) and mAM in vivo ( – ). The effects of ethanol, pioglitazone, or ethanol and pioglitazone were specific to Nox 1-related miR1264 and Nox 2-related miR-107, as there were no changes in other Nox-related miRs such as miRs-1982 and −103 (data not shown). To determine whether the therapeutic effect of PIO on miR-1264 and −107 levels occurs through C/EBPβ, MH-S cells were transiently transfected with C/EBPβ-Scr or siC/EBPβ and treated with either control or ethanol, followed by treatment with PIO in vitro for 24 h. Ethanol-mediated decreases in levels of Nox1-related miR-1264 and Nox2-related miR-107 in MH-S cells were unchanged with combined siC/EBPβ and PIO treatments, suggesting that PIO modulates miR-1264 and miR-107 levels via C/EBPβ ( ). Treatment with PIO in vitro for 24 h effectively attenuated ethanol-induced alterations in Nox1, Nox2, and Nox 4 mRNA and protein levels ( and and ), ROS production ( ), H2O2 levels ( ), and phagocytic index ( ) in MH-S cells. Similar observations in a complementary model of chronic ethanol exposure including mAM isolated from ethanol-fed animals ( – and ) support the fundamental nature of this pathophysiologic cascade and the therapeutic potential of PIO in ethanol-induced AM dysfunction. These results provide novel evidence that short-term treatment with PIO can provide a pharmacological strategy to target ethanol-induced derangements in AM gene expression, oxidative stress, and phagocytic function.

## Discussion
Functional immunosuppression of the AM contributes to enhanced risk of respiratory infections in individuals with AUD. A more complete understanding of the pathobiology underlying AM dysregulation can reveal novel approaches for therapeutic intervention in AUD to mitigate lung infections and related complications. The current study seeks to validate common in vitro and in vivo alcohol models that are relevant to human AUD pathobiology and to clarify how alcohol perturbs AM function in vitro and in vivo. While Nox 1, Nox 2, and Nox 4 upregulation increases oxidative stress and causes alcohol-induced AM phagocytic dysfunction ( ), the molecular mechanisms underlying these events are unknown. The current study focuses on C/EBPβ and lung-targeted, Nox-related miR-1264 and miR-107 as novel mechanisms of AM immune regulation. We hypothesized that alcohol-mediated reductions in AM PPARγ contribute to increased C/EBPβ, suppression of Nox-related miR-1264 and miR-107, upregulation of Nox1, Nox2, and Nox4, excessive oxidative stress, and AM phagocytic dysfunction. Our findings further reveal that PPARγ activation with PIO reversed these alcohol-induced AM derangements in vitro and in vivo, highlighting novel approaches to address and reverse AM dysregulation in AUD.
Although alcohol can be metabolized by alcohol dehydrogenase to acetaldehyde in the liver ( ), lung cells including bronchial epithelium, club cells, type II pneumocytes, and AM express cytochrome enzymes ( ), where microsomal cytochrome P450 2E1 and peroxisomes metabolize alcohol to reactive aldehyde adducts ( ; ) and ROS ( ). We have demonstrated that treatment with the antioxidant glutathione reverses ethanol-induced expression of Nox 1, Nox 2, and Nox 4 ( ). Further, we have recently shown that alcohol misuse increases lung malondialdehyde-acetaldehyde protein adducts, stimulating transforming growth factor-β ( ). These studies suggest that the detrimental effects of chronic alcohol ingestion on AM may be due to either ethanol itself or to the ethanol-derived metabolites, reactive aldehyde adducts, and ROS.
MiRs control multiple mRNA targets. For example, in addition to Nox 1, in silico analysis of miR-1264 using TargetScan shows that miR-1264 has a putative binding site on the 3′UTR of arginase 1 (Arg 1). Previous studies have shown that chronic ethanol ingestion increases Arg1 expression in AM, leading to an altered activation state in which phagocytic function is impaired ( ). Further, miR-107 has a putative binding site on the 3′UTR of mitochondrial fission regulator 1. We recently demonstrated that chronic ethanol ingestion augments mitochondrial fission and mitochondrial derangements in AM ( ), leading to phagocytic dysfunction ( ). Our findings that miR-1264 and miR-107 may be modulated by treatment with siC/EBPβ or PIO suggest that these therapeutic strategies may mitigate alcohol-induced AM immune dysfunction through multiple pathways.
C/EBPβ expression in the lung epithelium is necessary for the inflammatory response to cigarette smoke ( ) and, in part, mediates bronchial epithelial–mesenchymal transition in the chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)-induced mouse cigarette exposure model ( ). Recent studies have shown that PIO is associated with a lower risk of COPD in type 2 diabetics ( ), PIO treatment is protective against endothelial dysfunction in a cigarette smoke-exposed mouse model of COPD ( ), and PPARγ ligands attenuate inflammation and promote AM efferocytosis of apoptotic neutrophils in experimental models of COPD ( ). Therefore, the airway use of siC/EBPβ or PIO as treatments to restore AM function may provide benefit to people with histories of both alcohol use disorders and cigarette smoking.
The current study validates common experimental models of human AUD that support the continued study of molecular mechanisms and pathways underlying alcohol-induced AM immune dysfunction. For example, the recapitulation of derangements in gene expression and function in multiple models including MH-S cells treated with alcohol in vitro, mAM from a mouse model of chronic alcohol ingestion, and hAM from AUD subjects ( , ; ; ) provides compelling evidence that alcohol is the proximal stimulus for these alterations. Consistent alcohol-induced changes in C/EBPβ, downstream Nox 1-and Nox 2-related miRs, and AM derangements among these models highlight their fundamental importance as molecular targets in alcohol’s effects on the AM. In addition, we previously reported that ex vivo treatment with the PPARγ thiazolidinedione ligand, rosiglitazone, for 24 h increased hAM PPARγ levels that had been depleted by chronic alcohol consumption ( ), and that ex vivo treatment with PIO for 24 h reversed alcohol-induced hAM phagocytic dysfunction ( ). Despite variations in the duration of alcohol exposure across models and species, consistency in alcohol-induced AM derangements provides reassurance that the pathways being explored are of primary importance. While thiazolidinediones exert systemic effects in humans and mice, the recapitulation of the alcoholic AM phenotype in MH-S cells exposed to alcohol in vitro and their reversal with PIO suggests that the systemic effects of thiazolidinediones are not required for therapeutic impact on the AM. The reversal of the alcohol AM phenotype in mice treated with oral PIO in vivo further suggests that clinically achievable PIO concentrations in the airway are sufficient for therapeutic purposes.
The mouse model permits in vivo exploration of innovative pharmacological therapies in the setting of chronic alcohol exposure. The demonstration that in vivo intranasal administration of Nox-related miR mimics ( and ) and C/EBPβ siRNA ( and ) reversed alcohol-induced AM derangements indicates that PIO-induced alterations in these pulmonary targets are sufficient to reverse the alcohol-associated AM phenotype. These studies further suggest that intranasal or nebulized treatment with PIO could represent a novel therapeutic strategy for AUD individuals in the future.
In summary, the current findings provide compelling evidence that common experimental models, including MH-S cells treated with alcohol in vitro and a mouse model of chronic alcohol ingestion, accurately recapitulate previously reported derangements in C/EBPβ, Nox-related miRs, and phagocytic dysfunction in hAM from individuals with AUD ( ). The importance of these novel alcohol-induced pathways is supported by evidence that reducing alcohol-mediated increases in C/EBPβ levels with intranasal siRNA or restoring miRs-1264 and −107 with intranasal miR mimics effectively reverses AM dysfunction. Further, the reversal of alcohol-induced derangements in hAM with PIO ex vivo ( ) coupled with the current findings that oral PIO in vivo reverses AM derangements in mice provided the foundation for a previous clinical trial investigating the impact of a 2-week course of oral PIO on AM function in AUD individuals (A Study of Pleiotropic Pioglitazone Effects on the Alcoholic Lung: APPEAL Study, ClinicalTrials.gov
NCT03060772). Unfortunately, this trial could not be completed during the COVID-19 pandemic and was closed. Nonetheless, our data indicate that future studies should be considered to investigate acute pharmacological interventions that reverse alcohol-induced pathobiological signaling to mitigate the risk of infection and lung injury in people with AUD who present for medical care ( ). Collectively, this work advances our understanding of pathobiological pathways involved in alcohol-induced AM dysregulation and reveals novel therapeutic targets that can be further explored to mitigate adverse effects of alcohol on the lung.
