## Significance
Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related death in the United States, and KRAS is the most frequently mutated oncogene in lung cancer. However, KRAS mutant lung tumors acquire resistance to standard-of-care therapies, and improved understanding of the underlying tumor biology is warranted in order to improve therapeutic options for this patient population. We found that HDAC3 restrains a gene expression program in lung cancer cells that is functionally linked to T cell recruitment. Preclinical studies revealed that therapeutically impinging upon this pathway enhances T cell recruitment into Kras mutant lung tumors, which contribute to tumor growth control. Together, our study highlights a key role for tumor cell–intrinsic HDAC3 in regulation of the tumor immune microenvironment.

Histone Deacetylase 3 (HDAC3) function in vivo is nuanced and directed in a tissue-specific fashion. The importance of HDAC3 in Kras mutant lung tumors has recently been identified, but HDAC3 function in this context remains to be fully elucidated. Here, we identified HDAC3 as a lung tumor cell–intrinsic transcriptional regulator of the tumor immune microenvironment. In Kras mutant lung cancer cells, we found that HDAC3 is a direct transcriptional repressor of a cassette of secreted chemokines, including Cxcl10. Genetic and pharmacological inhibition of HDAC3 robustly up-regulated this gene set in human and mouse Kras, LKB1 (KL) and Kras, p53 (KP) mutant lung cancer cells through an NF-κB/p65-dependent mechanism. Using genetically engineered mouse models, we found that HDAC3 inactivation in vivo induced expression of this gene set selectively in lung tumors and resulted in enhanced T cell recruitment at least in part via Cxcl10. Furthermore, we found that inhibition of HDAC3 in the presence of Kras pathway inhibitors dissociated Cxcl10 expression from that of immunosuppressive chemokines and that combination treatment of entinostat with trametinib enhanced T cell recruitment into lung tumors in vivo. Finally, we showed that T cells contribute to in vivo tumor growth control in the presence of entinostat and trametinib combination treatment. Together, our findings reveal that HDAC3 is a druggable endogenous repressor of T cell recruitment into Kras mutant lung tumors.

Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) is the most frequently mutated oncogene in NSCLC, where it is mutated or amplified in ~25% to 30% of cases. In the lung adenocarcinoma subset of NSCLC, KRAS mutation frequently co-occurs with inactivating mutation in either the tumor suppressor p53/TP53 or LKB1/STK11 in a mutually exclusive manner, and KRAS, p53 (KP) or KRAS, LKB1 (KL) mutant NSCLC represent two major molecular subtypes of KRAS mutant NSCLC ( ). Although much progress has been made since the molecular characterization of these NSCLC subtypes, current overall survival rates of less than two years for KRAS mutant NSCLC patients highlight the necessity to better understand the tumor biology of this devastating disease ( , ).
Alterations in histone acetylation levels have long been associated with cancer, and histone acetyltransferases are established regulators of this process ( ). However, the in vivo function of many of the individual Histone Deacetylases (HDACs) in solid tumors remains incompletely understood. HDAC3 is a member of the Class I family of HDACs, yet HDAC3 mechanism of action is unique from that of other HDACs. HDAC3 is a core component of the nuclear receptor corepressor (NcoR) complex, and HDAC3 is the only endogenous HDAC found within this complex ( , ). HDAC3 has been shown to regulate gene expression through deacetylation of histones, deacetylation of nonhistone proteins, and deacetylase-independent mechanisms ( ). In contrast with other HDACs, HDAC3 has been shown to control tissue-specific gene expression and to mediate tissue-specific biological processes in vivo ( ). However, a critical role for HDAC3 in lung tumors in vivo was only recently reported ( ), and many questions about HDAC3 function in this context remain to be elucidated. In a previous study, we identified that HDAC3 is essential in Kras mutant NSCLC in vivo, which led to the discovery of a LKB1-specific drug resistance mechanism ( ). However, we found that HDAC3 was required for lung tumor growth in both the KL and KP genetically engineered mouse models (GEMM) of NSCLC. We hypothesized that HDAC3 may regulate a common set of target genes in both KL and KP lung cancer cells, which we define here.

## Results

## HDAC3 Target Genes Common to KL and KP Lung Cancer Cells are Associated with the NF-κB and SASP Pathways.
To assess whether HDAC3 regulates a common set of target genes in both KL and KP lung cancer cells, we inactivated HDAC3 using CRISPR/Cas9 in NSCLC cell lines derived from the GEMMs. We used two cell lines from the KP GEMM, KP T3 cells ( ) and KP 634T cells ( ). We also used two cell lines from the KL GEMM, KL LJE1 cells ( ) and KL LJE7 cells, which we established from individual primary tumors ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–C). HDAC3 inactivation reduced cell growth rates across the four cell lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D), consistent with the tumor-supporting role of HDAC3 in KP and KL NSCLC that we reported previously ( ). We profiled these cell lines by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to identify how HDAC3 inactivation impacted gene expression in NSCLC cells. Results from the four cell lines were compared in order to identify genes consistently altered upon HDAC3 loss (>0.5-fold change, adj. P-value < 0.05). We found that a core set of 26 genes were up-regulated ( ) and 12 genes were down-regulated (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E) upon loss of HDAC3 across KP and KL lung cancer cells. Pathway analysis did not identify a specific pathway associated with the genes down-regulated upon HDAC3 loss. In contrast, the genes up-regulated upon HDAC3 loss were significantly enriched for the senescence pathway and the NF-κB/p65 transcription factor ( ). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirmed that the expression of “SASP Fridman Senescence” and “TNFα Signaling via NF-κB” gene sets were enriched upon HDAC3 inactivation ( ).
 Senescence is a cellular process of largely irreversible cell cycle arrest driven by the concerted action of the RB, p53, and NF-κB/p65 pathways. RB and p53 coordinate to arrest cell cycling, and NF-κB/p65 drives the gene expression of a well-defined set of chemokines known as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) to direct immune-mediated senescent cell clearance ( ). The SASP is a bioactive transcriptional program that impacts the microenvironment from senescence onset until senescent cell clearance. Our findings raised the hypothesis that HDAC3 could be an endogenous upstream repressor of genes associated with the SASP gene signature in Kras mutant lung cancer cells.
To explore this, we assessed whether the impact on SASP gene expression was through direct versus indirect regulation by HDAC3. We integrated our previously published HDAC3 ChIP sequencing (ChIP-seq) data (SI Appendix, Fig. S1F) with RNA-seq data from HDAC3 knockout (KO) cells ( ). This analysis identified 468 genes that were associated with direct HDAC3 genome binding and HDAC3-dependent gene expression in KL lung cancer cells ( ). Motif enrichment analysis revealed that the HDAC3 ChIP-seq peaks associated with genes up-regulated upon HDAC3 loss (red box, ) were enriched for the NF-κB/p65 DNA binding motif ( ). This analysis suggested that NF-κB/p65 was the most enriched transcription factor (TF) at target genes that were directly repressed by HDAC3 in lung cancer cells. HDAC3 has been shown to mediate its function by deacetylating histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27) and lysine 9 (H3K9), or via mechanisms independent of histone deacetylation ( ). To query which mechanism was involved in lung cancer cells, we performed H3K27ac and H3K9ac ChIP-seq on KL LJE1 cells. At the HDAC3-bound sites associated with up-regulated gene expression, we did not detect changes in H3K27ac or H3K9ac (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 G–H). Specifically, we observed HDAC3 genome binding near the transcription start site (TSS) of SASP genes, but no major changes in H3K27ac or H3K9ac were observed at these genomic regions ( ). Therefore, the HDAC3 mechanism of action at these target genes appears to be independent of H3K27 or H3K9 deacetylation.

## HDAC3 Represses Chemokine Gene Expression in Mouse and Human NSCLC Cells.
We next queried how HDAC3 impacted the expression of genes associated with the SASP program in lung cancer cells. NF-κB/p65-dependent SASP genes have been previously defined ( ). Of these, we found that the expression of Ccl20, Cxcl5, Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Cxcl3, and IL-1a was consistently induced upon HDAC3 inactivation in Kras mutant lung cancer cells. Expression of these genes was increased (>0.5-fold change, adj. P-value < 0.05) in response to HDAC3 KO in two different lung cancer cell lines derived from the KL GEMM ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A), and lung cancer cells derived from the KP GEMM (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Furthermore, in A549 cells, which are a widely used human KRAS mutant lung cancer cell line, these genes were also up-regulated in response to HDAC3 KO (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C).
 We next queried the impact of HDAC3-targeting inhibitors on the expression of this group of chemokine genes. Entinostat is a clinically well-tolerated HDAC1- and HDAC3-selective inhibitor ( , ), and RGFP966 is an HDAC3-selective tool compound ( ). We found that treatment of mouse KL cells with entinostat or RGFP966 resulted in robust induction of gene expression ( ), consistent with results obtained from HDAC3 genetic deletion experiments ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–C). In A549 and H460 cells, which are human KRAS mutant lung cancer cells, treatment with entinostat (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 D–E) and the HDAC3-selective inhibitor BRD3308 ( ) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 F–G) induced the expression of these genes as well. Together, our data indicated that in mouse and human KRAS mutant lung cancer cells, endogenous HDAC3 restrained the expression of a set of chemokine genes. Consequently, both genetic deletion and pharmacological inhibition of HDAC3 increased the expression of this chemokine gene set.
The SASP genes, including those identified here as HDAC3-dependent target genes, encode for proteins which function as secreted chemokines. Therefore, we next assessed how HDAC3 impacts the chemokines secreted from lung cancer cells. Using a cytokine array ELISA, we profiled the protein expression of 111 cytokines in the cell culture media from NT and HDAC3 KO cells. Of the 32 cytokines that were detected in the media, 11 were differentially expressed (fold change > ±0.5, P-value < 0.05) between NT and HDAC3 KO cells ( ). Next, we compared results from the ELISA to the RNA-seq to identify which transcriptional changes corresponded to functional differences in secreted proteins ( ). Of the HDAC3-dependent chemokine genes identified by RNA-seq, the ELISA confirmed that Ccl20, Cxcl5, and Cxcl2 protein secretion was HDAC3 dependent. An additional 7 secreted proteins were also identified by the ELISA as HDAC3-dependent. Of these, Ccl5, Cxcl10, G-Csf, and Gdf-15 were readily detectable at the mRNA level and were up-regulated upon HDAC3 deletion (SI Appendix, Fig. S2H). Taken together, we identified and defined a group of HDAC3-dependent secreted chemokines that were transcriptionally repressed by HDAC3 in Kras mutant lung cancer cells: Ccl20, Cxcl5, Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Cxcl3, IL-1a, Cxcl10, Ccl5, G-csf, and Gdf-15.

## NF-κB/p65 Is Required for Chemokine Gene Induction Following HDAC3 Inactivation in Lung Cancer Cells.
Since the SASP transcriptional program is driven by the NF-κB/p65 transcription factor ( ), we queried the role of NF-κB/p65 in the expression of these genes. We used CRISPR/Cas9 to inactivate p65, and confirmed that p65 supports basal expression of Ccl20, Cxcl5, Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Cxcl3, and IL-1a (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). Cxcl10, Ccl5, G-csf, and Gdf-15 expression were not significantly altered by p65 inactivation in basal conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A).
To determine whether HDAC3 impacts chemokine gene expression via p65, we used CRISPR/Cas9 to inactivate p65 in HDAC3 KO cells ( ). Gene expression analysis confirmed that HDAC3 KO induced the expression of the genes identified in , and revealed that p65 was required for the HDAC3-dependent effects on Ccl20, Cxcl5, Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Cxcl3, IL-1a, Cxcl10, and Ccl5 ( ). Notably, across our cell systems, IL-1a mRNA was expressed at extremely low levels, often barely above the threshold of detection. Taken together with the observation that IL-1a protein is not detected in the media ( ), we conclude that IL-1a is unlikely to be a functionally relevant target in Kras mutant lung cancer cells and, therefore, it was omitted from further analysis. HDAC3-dependent induction of G-csf and Gdf-15 expression did not require p65 ( ) and, therefore, these genes were excluded from subsequent analysis of the HDAC3-p65 pathway. Next, we used RNA-seq to identify all genes that were transcriptionally repressed by HDAC3 in a p65-dependent manner ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). 23 genes were entirely dependent on p65 for their HDAC3-responsive expression pattern ( ). This suggested a model where the impact of HDAC3 on p65 function is limited with regard to the number of genes impacted, but potent with regard to the magnitude of the gene expression changes. Furthermore, testing the role of p65 for the gene expression response to HDAC inhibitors, we found that p65 is required for maximal gene expression induction in response to both entinostat (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C) and BRD3308 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3D).
 Together, we identified that in Kras mutant lung cancer cells HDAC3 robustly repressed a set of p65-dependent genes, Ccl20, Cxcl5, Cxcl1, Cxcl2, Cxcl3, Cxcl10, and Ccl5, which we define as HDAC3-Repressed Chemokine Genes (“HRCG”). These genes are the focus of subsequent analysis of the HDAC3-p65 pathway.
Next, we queried the impact of HDAC3 on NF-κB/p65 protein. HDAC3 has been reported to directly deacetylate p65 (Rela), resulting in its nuclear exclusion ( ). Consistent with this mechanism, we found that HDAC inhibition with entinostat, RGFP966, or the pan-HDAC inhibitor trichostatin a (TSA) increased the quantity of p65 protein in the nuclear fraction ( ). Using a commercially available antibody developed to recognize acetylation of transfected p65 on lysine 310 (K310) and two different published p65 cDNA constructs ( , ), we found that entinostat treatment increased K310 acetylation on p65 ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). To understand the impact of p65 acetylation at K310, we mutated K310 to alanine (K310A), confirmed that entinostat and BRD3308 induce acetylation of wildtype p65 at K310, and validated that the p65 K310A mutant ablated all acetylation at K310 of p65 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3F). Analysis in basal conditions identified that K310 of p65 impacted HRCG expression, although in most cases it elicited only a modest impact on net gene expression compared to wildtype p65 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3G). In the presence of entinostat, K310 only significantly altered the gene expression response of Ccl20, Cxcl5, and Ccl5 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3H). We conclude that K310 of p65 is involved in HRCG expression control but it does not fully explain how HDAC3 regulates p65 function; we expect that additional mechanisms are also involved.

## HDAC3 Regulates HRCG Expression in Lung Tumors In Vivo.
Next, we queried whether HDAC3 impacts HRCG expression in lung tumors in vivo. The KL and KP GEMMs recapitulate two major molecular subtypes of KRAS mutant NSCLC ( ). We had previously crossed these GEMMs to the HDAC3 floxed mouse model ( ) to generate the KL-HDAC3 and KP-HDAC3 GEMMs. When lentivirus expressing Cre recombinase was delivered by intratracheal intubation to the lungs of these mice, tumorigenesis was initiated in infected lung cells, and in KL-HDAC3 and KP-HDAC3 mice, HDAC3 was genetically deleted selectively within lung tumor cells at the time of tumor initiation. These GEMMs facilitated genetic assessment of HDAC3 function in vivo within autochthonous lung tumor cells in the context of an otherwise HDAC3-wildtype and immune-competent animal. Here, to assess HRCG expression in lung tumors in vivo, we applied immunohistochemistry (IHC) to lung sections from tumor-bearing mice from the KL-HDAC3 and KP-HDAC3 GEMMs. A commercially available antibody directed against Cxcl5 had been used for IHC on GEMM lung tumors ( ) and, therefore, we selected this antibody to query HRCG expression. IHC on lung tissue from the KL-HDAC3 and KP-HDAC3 GEMMs revealed that Cxcl5 protein expression was elevated in HDAC3-deleted lung tumors as compared to HDAC3-wildtype lung tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A−D). These data confirmed that genetic inactivation of HDAC3 resulted in increased Cxcl5 protein expression in Kras mutant lung tumors in vivo in both the KL and KP GEMMs, consistent with our findings from cell culture experiments where HDAC3 inactivation induced Cxcl5 expression in KL and KP lung cancer cell lines ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A−C).
Next, we assessed whether pharmacological inhibition of HDAC3 impacted HRCG expression in vivo as we had observed in cell culture experiments ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 D−G). To this end, we tested the impact of entinostat treatment on HRCG expression in vivo. We initiated lung tumorigenesis in KL GEMM mice and monitored disease growth over time using bioluminescence imaging (BLI) potentiated by the Rosa26LSL-luciferase genetic allele. When tumor burden was established in all mice (BLI > 5 × 107 photons/sec), mice were randomized and treatment was initiated. Mice were treated daily for 5 d with vehicle or 10 mg/kg/d entinostat administered by oral gavage, after which lung tumors and liver were collected (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). Using qRT-PCR, we assessed gene expression of HDAC3 target genes in lung tumors and liver. In lung tumors, entinostat treatment potently induced HRCG expression ( ). However, in livers from the same mice, entinostat treatment did not induce HRCG expression ( ). To confirm that entinostat treatment was engaging on-target effects in the liver samples, we assessed the gene expression of canonical HDAC3 liver target genes Nr1d1 and Arntl ( ). Indeed, entinostat treatment impacted Nr1d1 and Arntl gene expression in the liver in a manner consistent with the published effects of HDAC3 inactivation on these target genes ( , ). This suggested that, despite systemic administration, entinostat treatment induced HRCG expression selectively in lung tumors but not in livers from the same mice.
 We hypothesized that the difference between lung tumor and liver response could be due to either tissue specificity or tumor specificity. Tumors in the KL and KP GEMMs are driven by oncogenic KrasG12D. Since HDAC3 regulates HRCG expression in both KL and KP lung cancer cells in vivo and in cell culture, we reasoned that a tumor-specific effect would be driven by the oncogenic commonality, KrasG12D. We used the recently developed KrasG12D-selective inhibitor MRTX1133 as a tool to inactivate KrasG12D in lung cancer cells derived from the KL GEMM. First, we confirmed that MRTX1133 treatment resulted in on-target Kras inhibition by assessing ERK phosphorylation at Thr202/Tyr204, an established downstream read-out of Kras pathway signaling. Indeed, established dosing ( ) resulted in blunted ERK phosphorylation in KL LJE1 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4F). Next, we used qRT-PCR to assess HRCG expression in the presence of MRTX1133 treatment. Remarkably, MRTX1133 treatment reversed the HDAC3-dependent induction of Cxcl5, Cxcl1, Cxcl2, and Cxcl3 expression ( ). This suggested that oncogenic KrasG12D was required for HDAC3 to engage these genes and, by extension, that HDAC3 repression of HRCGs may be a tumor-specific molecular event. In contrast, the expression of Cxcl10 and Ccl5 did not follow this pattern, as their expression was maintained in the presence of MRTX1133 ( ). Cxcl5, Cxcl1, Cxcl2, and Cxcl3 are considered immunosuppressive chemokines in the lung, whereas Cxcl10 and Ccl5 are known to mediate antitumor immunity ( ). Thus, our findings revealed a divergence in response between tumorigenic chemokines and immunosurveillance-related chemokines, respectively.

## HDAC3 Genetic Deletion Results in T Cell Recruitment into GEMM Lung Tumors.
We next queried the biological impact of HRCG expression control. HRCGs are established effectors of immune cell recruitment. Therefore, we reasoned that the phenotype of HDAC3 deletion in lung tumors related to HRCG expression control would correspond to changes in immune cell recruitment. Indeed, assessment of H&E-stained lung sections from KL-HDAC3 mice revealed a striking influx of immune cells in HDAC3-deleted lung tumors ( ). The SASP has been shown to recruit immune cells including NK cells and T cells to tumors ( , ). Therefore, we used IHC to visualize macrophages, NK cells, neutrophils, and T cells in KL and KL-HDAC3 lung tumors ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). We did not observe differences in macrophage, NK cell, or neutrophil influx in response to HDAC3 deletion (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). However, the quantity of CD3+ T cells was markedly increased in HDAC3-deleted lung tumors ( ). We observed a similar phenotype in the KP-HDAC3 GEMM, where HDAC3-deleted tumors exhibited a clear influx of immune cells ( ). HDAC3 deletion did not impact the quantity of macrophages, NK cells, or neutrophils (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B), but the quantity of T cells was significantly higher in KP lung tumors deleted for HDAC3 ( ). We conclude that deletion of HDAC3 selectively in lung tumors in vivo resulted in increased T cell recruitment and infiltration into KL and KP lung tumors. Importantly, these findings revealed that in Kras mutant lung tumors endogenous HDAC3 restrains T cell infiltration.
 We next considered how tumor cell–intrinsic HDAC3 mediated T cell recruitment. We hypothesized that the observed HDAC3-dependent T cell recruitment into lung tumors was through transcriptional regulation of HRCGs. We had identified that Ccl20, Cxcl5, Cxcl2, Ccl5, and Cxcl10 corresponded to functional differences in proteins secreted from Kras mutant lung cancer cells ( ). Cxcl9, Cxcl10, and Cxcl11 are known to recruit T cells ( , , ). In our lung cancer cells, neither Cxcl9 or Cxcl11 were detected at the mRNA or protein level, but Cxcl10 mRNA was readily detectable. Therefore, we hypothesized that Cxcl10 was likely involved in mediating HDAC3 control of T cell recruitment. To query the molecular link between HDAC3 and Cxcl10, we assessed Cxcl10 response to HDAC3 modulation. We had identified Cxcl10 as a p65-dependent HRCG ( ) and an HDAC3-dependent secreted protein ( ). In vivo, Cxcl10 expression was induced upon entinostat treatment selectively in lung tumor cells ( ). Examination of ChIP-seq data at the Cxcl10 genomic locus identified HDAC3 binding near the Cxcl10 TSS in lung cancer cells ( ). Consistent with findings at other HRCGs ( ), we did not observe changes in H3K27 or H3K9 acetylation at the HDAC3-bound region of the Cxcl10 genomic locus ( ). Finally, we confirmed that HDAC3 inactivation via both genetic deletion and pharmacological inhibition induced the expression of Cxcl10 in mouse ( ) and human ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C and D) Kras mutant lung cancer cells. Together, these data identified Cxcl10 as a direct target of HDAC3 transcriptional repression in Kras mutant lung cancer cells.
Next, we tested whether Cxcl10 is required for T cell recruitment into the KL-HDAC3 and KP-HDAC3 GEMMs. C-X-C Motif Chemokine Receptor 3 (CXCR3) is a receptor with selectivity for Cxcl9, Cxcl10, and Cxcl11 and, therefore, we used antibody directed against CXCR3 to test the requirement for the Cxcl10–CXCR3 axis in vivo. We initiated tumors in KL-HDAC3 and KP-HDAC3 mice, tracked tumor growth over time using bioluminescence imaging, randomized mice into two treatment groups, treated mice with either anti-CXCR3 blocking antibody or IgG control, and tumor-bearing lungs were collected at endpoint (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E). Using CD3 IHC, we found that CXCR3 blocking antibody treatment significantly reduced the quantity of T cells within HDAC3-deleted tumors in the KL-HDAC3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 F and G) and KP-HDAC3 (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 H and I) GEMMs. Taken together with our prior observation that these tumor cells do not express Cxcl9 or Cxcl11, these findings suggest that Cxcl10 contributes to the T cell recruitment elicited by HDAC3 inactivation in vivo. Consistent with our findings, a recent study using MCA205 mouse fibrosarcoma cells reported HDAC3 binding and repression of Cxcl10 expression and used CXCR3 antibody blocking to rescue the effect of HDAC3 inhibition on tumor cell growth ( ).

## Entinostat Plus Trametinib Combination Therapy Enhances T Cell Infiltration into GEMM Lung Tumors.
Taken together, our findings indicate that HDAC3 endogenously restrains T cell infiltration into Kras mutant lung tumors and that expression of the HRCGs associated with this phenotype is induced by treatment with HDAC3-targeting inhibitors including entinostat. The implication is that pharmacological agents which target HDAC3 could provide a therapeutic means for recruiting T cells into Kras mutant lung tumors, which would be clinically advantageous. However, if T cell recruitment occurred concurrently with the induction of an immunosuppressive chemokine program, therapeutic benefit could be occluded. Our findings with MRTX1133 treatment suggested that inhibition of the Kras pathway may dissociate immunosuppressive and immunosurveillance-related HRCG expression ( ). Indeed, in Kras mutant lung cancer cells, combination treatment of trametinib with entinostat blunted the induction of Cxcl5, Cxcl1, and Cxcl3 in response to entinostat, whereas trametinib and entinostat cotreatment enhanced the expression of Ccl5 and Cxcl10 ( ). This was consistent with a recent report that trametinib treatment increased Ccl5 and Cxcl10 expression in Kras mutant lung tumors ( ), and suggested that cotreatment of entinostat with trametinib may maximize potential T cell recruitment.
 In a previous study, we reported the finding that trametinib and entinostat combination treatment elicited therapeutic benefit in the KL GEMM of NSCLC ( ), but we did not assess the impact of this treatment on immune cells in the tumor microenvironment. Therefore, here we tested whether pharmacological inhibition of HDAC3 in the context of trametinib treatment impacted T cell recruitment into lung tumors. To this end, lung tumorigenesis was initiated in the KL GEMM, and disease growth was monitored over time by bioluminescence imaging. When tumor burden was established in all mice, mice were randomized and treatment was initiated. Mice were treated daily by oral gavage with vehicle, 5 mg/kg entinostat, 1 mg/kg trametinib, or entinostat+trametinib (Ent + Tram) for 3 wk, after which lungs were collected. H&E-stained lung sections were prepared from Formalin-Fixed, Paraffin-Embedded (FFPE) tissue and analyzed. Interestingly, Ent + Tram-treated tumors exhibited an increased quantity of intratumoral immune cells ( and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). RNA-seq performed on treated tumors revealed that the genes selectively up-regulated in Ent + Tram-treated tumors compared to all other treatment conditions were most associated with T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). Next, we used IHC to visualize T cells in the FFPE lung sections. This revealed that Ent + Tram-treated lung tumors exhibited an increased quantity of CD3+ T cells compared to all other treatment conditions ( ). The important implication is that pharmacological inhibition of HDAC3 in combination with trametinib may provide a therapeutic approach for recruiting T cells into KL lung tumors.
Since systemic drug administration can impact immune cells directly, we used flow cytometry to assess how trametinib and entinostat treatment affected the tumor immune infiltrate and spleen from treated mice. Ent + Tram combination treatment did not strongly impact Treg frequency (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C) or CD4+ or CD8+ T cell proliferation rates (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). The percentages of naïve, effector, and memory CD8+ T cells were also not potently altered upon Ent + Tram treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S6E). However, the percentage of transitory (TrnsEx) and terminally exhausted (TerEx) CD8+PD-1+ T cells was reduced to similar degrees in both trametinib- and Ent + Tram-treated tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S6F). Furthermore, CD8+ T cells exhibited increased cytokine production capacity specifically in lung tumors from trametinib and Ent + Tram conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S6G). Recent studies have identified that entinostat impacts the quantity of immunosuppressive myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in bladder ( ), lung and renal ( ), and breast ( ) cancer models and that trametinib treatment can cause granulocytic MDSCs (gMDSCs) to block the function of T cells corecruited into Kras mutant lung tumors ( ). While we did not observe changes in gMDSCs in response to entinostat treatment alone, we did observe increased gMDSCs in lung tumors treated with trametinib alone (SI Appendix, Fig. S6H). In contrast, Ent + Tram treatment reversed the effect of trametinib on gMDSC influx (SI Appendix, Fig. S6H), suggesting that gMDSC recruitment was unlikely to significantly counteract the immunosurveillance function of T cells recruited into lung tumors in response to Ent +Tram treatment. Together, these data suggested that systemic Ent + Tram treatment did not reduce the functionality of T cells recruited into KL lung tumors.
Finally, we queried whether T cells recruited by Ent + Tram treatment contribute to tumor growth control. KP T3 cells were injected into the flanks of syngeneic mice, and tumor size was measured by caliper three times per week. 10 d postinjection, mice were randomized into two treatment groups, and T cell depleting anti-CD4/8a antibodies or IgG2b control antibody was administered weekly by i.p. injection. 13 d postinjection, treatment with Ent + Tram was initiated. Daily oral gavage delivery of Ent + Tram potently reduced tumor growth in the IgG2b control antibody group, but tumor size continued to increase in mice treated with anti-CD4/8a antibody ( ). We also confirmed that anti-CD4/8a treatment resulted in reduced T cells within flank tumors from treated mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 I and J). To test the role of T cells in tumor growth control in LKB1-null lung cancer cells, we inactivated LKB1 in KP T3 cells using CRISPR/Cas9 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6K). Following a similar experimental design as in , we injected these cells into the flanks of syngeneic mice, treated mice weekly with anti-CD4/8a antibody or IgG2b control, and subsequently administered Ent + Tram combination treatment by daily oral gavage. This experiment confirmed that in Kras mutant lung cancer cells lacking LKB1, Ent + Tram treatment reduced tumor growth in the IgG2b control antibody group, whereas tumor size increased in mice treated with anti-CD4/8a antibody ( ). Taken together, this suggested that T cells contribute to Kras mutant lung tumor growth control in Ent + Tram-treated mice.

## Discussion
HDAC inhibitors were FDA approved for cancer treatment as early as 2006 in the case of the pan-HDAC inhibitor vorinostat for CTCL. Multiple clinical trials since have tested different HDAC inhibitors in a wide range of cancer indications, yet clinical response has frequently proven disappointing. However, few clinical trials were performed with selective HDAC inhibitors and considering the context-specific functions of individual HDACs and their diverse mechanisms of action, toxicity from inhibition of many HDACs simultaneously would be expected to limit therapeutic potential. It is now clear that predicting the impact of systemically administered HDAC-targeting therapeutics on tumor growth control will require an understanding of in vivo function of each inhibited HDAC, including the impact on both tumor cells and the tumor immune microenvironment. Indeed, recent studies have identified that systemic HDAC inhibitors including Class I HDAC-selective and HDAC3-selective inhibitors can impact tumor immune surveillance in vivo ( , , ). These findings highlight the importance of understanding how HDAC inhibition impacts immune response to cancer therapy.
On-target function of pan-HDAC and Class I HDAC inhibitors includes inhibition of HDAC3, yet the continued emergence of new discoveries suggests that understanding of HDAC3 function in cancer remains incomplete. Since HDAC3 lacks a functionally redundant homologue, the fact that HDAC3 is druggable positions it as a potentially promising molecular target for cancer indications where HDAC3 is essential for tumor growth. It is becoming apparent that there is no single unifying mechanism driving HDAC3 function in cancer but instead, consistent with its tissue-specific function in normal tissues, HDAC3 supports tumor growth and survival through a diverse set of molecular mechanisms in cancer including lymphomas ( , , ), rhabdomyosarcoma ( ), melanomas ( ), breast cancer ( ), pancreatic cancer ( ), and lung cancer ( ), and prevents tumor growth through other mechanisms in liver ( ) and prostate cancer ( ). These findings highlight the necessity to understand HDAC3 function in specific cancer indications in order to accurately predict the mechanisms and biology on which HDAC3-targeting therapeutic strategies would impinge. Here, we report a mechanism of action through which tumor cell–intrinsic HDAC3 impacts the immune microenvironment in lung tumors, revealing that the tumor microenvironment is directly controlled by an HDAC3-dependent tumor-specific molecular mechanism.
We found that HDAC3 actively restrains T cell infiltration into lung tumors via a tumor cell–intrinsic mechanism and that disruption of HDAC3-mediated repression, by genetic deletion or pharmacological approaches, resulted in T cell influx into lung tumors. Our study highlights how defining the tumor cell–intrinsic functions of HDAC3 in vivo can lead to insight into how systemic HDAC inhibitors may impact both the primary tumor and the immune microenvironment. We show that this finding can be leveraged to enhance recruitment of functional T cells into GEMM lung tumors in vivo via systemic administration of clinically tolerated therapeutics. An important implication is that existing HDAC3-targeting therapeutics may have the capacity to elicit T cell infiltration into NSCLC tumors independently of mechanisms employed by immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). Clinically, KRAS, LKB1 mutant lung cancer is resistant to ICIs, and these tumors are characterized by low PD-L1 expression and a paucity of tumor-infiltrating T cells ( ). Since ICIs are standard-of-care treatment for KRAS mutant lung cancer, this finding has spurred much interest in identifying methods for overcoming ICI resistance. Therefore, the ability to pharmacologically increase T cell recruitment into these tumors is clinically desirable, and our findings suggest that HDAC3 inhibition may provide a method for accomplishing this. Whether an HDAC3-directed therapeutic approach in NSCLC can complement ICIs or provide an alternative therapeutic option for patients at risk for ICI toxicity remains to be determined, but our data suggest that these are potentially tractable avenues to explore.

## Materials and Methods

## Cell Culture and Cell Lines.
All cell lines were incubated at 37 °C and were maintained in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Drug treatments were carried out using the following doses: entinostat 1 μM or 2 μM, RGFP966 10 μM, TSA 0.5 μM, BRD3308 5 μM, trametinib 10 nM, and MRTX1133 10 nM, 25 nM, and 50 nM for the indicated treatment lengths.

## CRISPR/Cas9 Studies.
Oligonucleotides for sgRNAs were annealed in vitro and subcloned into BsmbI-digested lentiCRISPRv.2-puro (Addgene 52961) or lentiCRISPRv.2-hygro (Addgene 98291). For SI Appendix, Figs. S4F and S6K, the RNP system was used to deliver CRISPR/Cas9 via electroporation.

## Lentiviral Production and Titering.
Lentiviruses made from pLentiCRISPRv.2 were produced by cotransfection of the lentiviral backbone constructs and packaging plasmids into 293T cells using Lipofectamine 2000. Viral supernatant was used to infect destination cells. Large-scale viral preps of Lenti Pgk-Cre were made by the University of Iowa Viral Vector Core.

## Western Blot.
Protein lysates in CST buffer with protease inhibitors were equilibrated using a BCA protein assay kit, resolved on SDS-PAGE gels, and transferred to the membrane. Membranes were blocked in milk, incubated overnight in primary antibody, washed, incubated in secondary antibody, and developed using SuperSignal ECL.

## mRNA Preparation, qRT-PCR, and mRNA Sequencing.
mRNA was prepared using the Quick-RNA Miniprep kit (Zymo Research), including DNase treatment. cDNA was synthesized using SuperScript III. mRNA-seq libraries were prepared using the TruSeq RNA library preparation kit and sequenced by single-end 50 base pairs using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. Raw sequencing data were demultiplexed and converted into FASTQ files using CASAVA.

## Bioinformatic Analysis of RNA-seq Data.
Sequenced reads were quality tested, aligned to the mouse mm10 genome, and analyses were carried out using HOMER ( ).

## ChIP Sequencing.
For HDAC3 ChIP-seq, cells were DSG+formaldehyde cross-linked. For H3K27ac and H3K9ac ChIP-seq, cells were formaldehyde cross-linked. Nuclei were isolated, washed, diluted, and sonicated. Lysate from 5 million cells per ChIP was incubated with antibody, incubated with Dynabeads, washed, eluted, and de-cross-linked, and DNA was isolated and sequenced.

## Bioinformatic Analysis of ChIP-seq Data.
Sequenced reads were aligned to the mouse mm10 genome using the STAR aligner. HOMER was used for data processing.

## Cytokine Array ELISA.
The Cytokine Array ELISA was carried out on cell culture supernatants using the Proteome Profiler Mouse XL Cytokine Array.

## Mouse Studies.
Lentivirus expressing Cre recombinase was delivered by intratracheal intubation to each mouse to initiate lung tumorigenesis. Bioluminescent (BLI) imaging was performed biweekly or more frequently as indicated using an IVIS Spectrum and Living Image software. Mice were injected intraperitoneally with 150 mg/kg D-luciferin (Caliper Life Sciences), anesthetized with isoflurane, and imaged both ventrally and dorsally 10 min after luciferin injection.

## Immunohistochemistry and Image Analysis.
Lungs from mice were collected at each experimental endpoint as noted in the Figures and were fixed in formalin for 18 to 22 h, transferred to 70% ethanol, and paraffin-embedded (FFPE). Five-micrometer sections from FFPE tissues were prepared and stained. Stained slides were scanned using a Perkin Elmer Slide Scanner for downstream analysis using Panoramic Viewer software, Inform v2.1 image analysis software, or QuPath software.

## Short-term In Vivo Entinostat Treatment.
KL mice were intratracheally intubated with lentivirus expressing Cre recombinase to initiate tumorigenesis and imaged biweekly by BLI starting 4 wk after Cre administration. When tumor burden in all mice was greater than 5 × 107 photons/sec as determined by BLI, treatment was initiated. Mice were randomized and treated daily by oral gavage for 5 d with vehicle or 10 mg/kg entinostat. The experiment was terminated after 5 d of treatment, at which time primary lung tumors and liver were isolated by dissection and immediately flash frozen.

## CXCR3 Antibody Depletion.
KL-HDAC3 and KP-HDAC3 mice were intratracheally intubated with lentivirus expressing Cre recombinase to initiate tumorigenesis and imaged biweekly by BLI starting 4 wk after Cre administration. One hundred one days post-Cre, mice were randomized, and antibody treatment was initiated. Mice were treated twice weekly i.p. with 200ug anti-CXCR3 antibody or IgG control. The experiment was terminated 116 d post-Cre, at which time lungs were isolated and FFPE.

## In Vivo Entinostat and Trametinib Combination Treatment.
KL mice were intratracheally intubated with lentivirus expressing Cre recombinase to initiate tumorigenesis and imaged weekly starting 4 wk post-Cre. The 3-wk duration study: When tumor burden was detectable in all mice by BLI, mice were randomized, and treatment was initiated. Entinostat was administered at 5 mg/kg. Trametinib was administered at 1 mg/kg. Drug(s) were administered daily by oral gavage. The 6-wk duration study was carried out according to the same experimental design except for the following: To ensure an extended treatment duration, treatment was initiated after tumor burden was detected by BLI in >30% of mice in the study. Entinostat was administered at a dose of 10 mg/kg. Drug(s) were administered daily by oral gavage.

## Flank Tumor Treatment Experiment.
KP T3 cells with ( ) or without ( ) LKB1 expression were injected into the flanks of female FVB mice. Tumors were measured by caliper three times per week. When tumor size exceeded an average of 100 mm3, mice were randomized and antibody treatment was initiated. A quantity of 200 ug antibody in PBS per mouse was administered i.p. weekly. When tumor size exceeded an average of 250 mm3, mice were randomized and combination treatment was initiated. Drugs were administered by daily oral gavage: 5 mg/kg entinostat and 1 mg/kg trametinib.

## Flow Cytometry Immune Profiling.
Tumor digestion and cell isolation: Tumors were excised, minced, collagen digested, filtered, and resuspended to single-cell suspension. Flow Cytometry, cell sorting, and antibodies: Single-cell suspensions were incubated with Fc receptor–blocking anti-CD16/32 and stained with the LIVE/DEAD® Fixable Red Dead Cell Stain Kit. Surface proteins were stained for 30 min in FACS buffer. For ex vivo cytokine production, cell suspensions were resuspended in RPMI with PMA and ionomycin in the presence Brefeldin A and processed as described above. Analysis was carried out with the LSR-II flow cytometer and quantitated with FlowJo.

## Statistics.
Statistical analyses described in each figure were all performed using Graph Pad Prism 9. Results are expressed as mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated.
