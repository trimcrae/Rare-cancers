## ABSTRACT
Therapeutics for eradicating hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection are still limited and current nucleos(t)ide analogs (NAs) and interferon are effective in controlling viral replication and improving liver health, but they cannot completely eradicate the hepatitis B virus and only a very small number of patients are cured of it. The TCR-like antibodies recognizing viral peptides presented on human leukocyte antigens (HLA) provide possible tools for targeting and eliminating HBV-infected hepatocytes. Here, we generated three TCR-like antibodies targeting three different HLA-A2.1-presented peptides derived from HBV core and surface proteins. Bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) were developed by fuzing variable fragments of these TCR-like mAbs with an anti-CD3ϵ antibody. Our data demonstrate that the BsAbs could act as T cell engagers, effectively redirecting and activating T cells to target HBV-infected hepatocytes in vitro and in vivo. In HBV-persistent mice expressing human HLA-A2.1, two infusions of BsAbs induced marked and sustained suppression in serum HBsAg levels and also reduced the numbers of HBV-positive hepatocytes. These findings highlighted the therapeutic potential of TCR-like BsAbs as a new strategy to cure hepatitis B.
KEYWORDS: Hepatitis B virus, TCR-like antibody, bispecific antibody, therapeutic antibody, HBsAg suppression

## Introduction
Chronic Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infections can cause severe liver diseases, including hepatitis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that there were 296 million people chronically infected with HBV worldwide, and approximately 820,000 died from HBV-related disease in 2019 [ ]. New HBV infections can be effectively blocked via vaccine-based immunoprophylaxis and prevention of perinatal virus transmission from mother to child. Functional cure of chronic hepatitis B (CHB) is defined as the loss of circulating Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and undetectable serum HBV DNA, which can lead to significant improvements in clinical outcomes, such as improved quality of life for patients, reduced inflammatory damage and reduced risk of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [ ]. However, approved anti-HBV drugs, either peginterferon (PEG-IFN) or nucleos(t)ide analogs (NA), can only achieve the functional cure goal in limited CHB patients [ ]. Among them, NAs requires continuous medication and rarely causes a functional cure, while the overall rate of PEG-IFN is about 7% to 8% [ ]. Long-term HBV persistence is attributed to the intrahepatic HBV covalently-closed circular DNA (cccDNA), which current drugs cannot directly affect and efficiently inhibit [ ]. Current CHB drug development mainly includes two classes: direct antivirals that block the life cycle of the virus, such as small interfering RNA (siRNAs), capsid assembly modulator (CAMs) and entry inhibitors, and indirect antivirals that clear the virus through regulation or activation of the immune system, such as therapeutic vaccines, antibodies and TLR agonists [ , ]. Specifical targeting and eliminating HBV-infected hepatocytes may be a possible strategy to improve therapeutic effectiveness towards curing HBV infections [ ].
The successful progress of anti-tumor immunotherapy based on CD8+ T-cell enhancing strategy in certain cancers provides a possible route to develop new anti-HBV treatments [ , ]. However, the HBV-specific CD8+ T cells in people with chronic HBV infections are usually exhausted [ ]. In clinical trials, the immune checkpoint blockade using anti-PD1 or anti-PDL1 antibodies exhibited some anti-HBV suppression effects but is still not promising in achieving CHB cure [ , ]. Peptide-HLA (pHLA) complexes on the surface of target cells can be recognized by CD8+ T lymphocytes through their T-cell receptors (TCRs), which can firmly bond and activate intracellular signalling pathways and cause the release of cytokines and cytolytic molecules that kill the target cells [ ]. For HBV-infected hepatocytes, proteasomal degradations of intracellular viral proteins into short peptides can be presented by HLA-I molecules, thereby generating pHLA complexes for TCR recognition [ , ]. Besides TCRs, the TCR-like or TCR-mimic antibodies can also specifically recognize the pHLA complex [ ]. The first TCR-like antibody was identified in the 1980s, showing the capability of targeting influenza virus-infected cells [ , ]. High-affinity TCR-like antibodies can play a therapeutic role in various forms, such as full-length antibodies, bispecific antibodies (BsAbs), antibody-drug conjugates (ADC), and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell approaches [ ]. BsAbs, particularly the T-cell engagers, that recruit T cells to bind with antigen-presenting target cells, thereby activating cytotoxic effects, represent a hopeful strategy for the specific eradication of virus-harbored cells. Currently, there is successful strategy for developing T-cell engager TCR drugs. Tebentafusp, developed by Immunocore, constructed from affinity-matured soluble TCR protein in fusion with anti-CD3, was approved to treat HLA-A*02:01 positive patients with metastatic uveal melanoma [ ]. Recent studies have developed TCR-like BsAbs with dual binding capabilities to CD3 and HIV-1 pHLA complexes, engaging cytotoxic T cells to eliminate the HIV-1 reservoir [ ]. Moreover, there are number of other TCR or TCR mimic bispecific T cell engagers constructed with anti-CD3 antibody to single-chain variable fragment (scFv), that have achieved outstanding preclinical results targeting infected cells [ , , ]. These studies demonstrate the therapeutic potential of such approaches for the treatment of CHB and other viral infections.
Here, we investigate the immunotherapeutic approach toward HBV clearance using TCR-like bispecific antibodies. We developed three TCR-like mouse monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) with high affinities to bind HLA-A∗02:01 (HLA-A2.1) displayed HBV peptides, including aa 18–27 (C18) of HBcAg protein, aa 20–28 (S20) and aa 197–205 (S197) of HBsAg protein. The variable fragments of these TCR-like mAbs were converted to recombinant BsAbs by fuzing with anti-CD3ϵ scFv. We systematically evaluated the in vitro activities of these antibodies using biochemical and cell-based analyses. Moreover, the in vivo anti-HBV effects were also assessed in HLA-A2.1 transgenic and humanized HBV carrier mice.

## Results

## Generation and characterization of TCR-like mAbs targeting pHLA complexes presenting HBV peptide
According to previous studies regarding T cell epitopes of HBV antigens, we selected three epitopes capable of being presented by HLA-A*02:01 and activating CD8+ T cells from HBV core protein (C18) and surface proteins (S20 and S197) as targets for developing TCR-like mAbs [ ] (Figure S1A). The pHLA complexes of these epitopes were produced as single-chain trimer (peptide-HLA-B2M, SCT) fusion proteins using CHO cells (Figure S1B). We used recombinant SCT proteins as immunogens to elicit TCR-like antibodies targeting the pHLA complex in mice. Given the requirement for binding to both HLA and peptide components, screening TCR-like antibodies is more challenging than conventional antibodies. Consequently, we developed a high-throughput flow cytometry-based antibody method using peptide-pulsed T2 cells, which enables direct screening of TCR-like mAbs with binding capabilities to pHLA on the cell surface. Finally, we successfully obtained three mAbs of C18-75, S20-5G1, and S197-3G11, specifically recognizing C18, S20, and S197 peptides-pulsed HLA complexes, respectively ( A). The three mAbs exhibited nanomolar affinities to the corresponding pHLA proteins( B) [ ]. The binding signals (MFI) of the three mAbs on T2 cells exhibited a peptide dose-dependent manner ( C). Notably, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) suggested that the C18-75 showed no binding to HBcAg, but showed weak binding activity at relatively high concentrations to HLA-A2.1 presenting a control irrelevant prptide( A, S1C). In contrast to S20-5G1, the S197-3G11 showed weak binding activity to both pHLA and intact HBsAg protein, indicating the antibody can also recognize the HBsAg epitope in the absence of HLA-A2 (Figure S1D-E).

 

## The capability of TCR-like mAbs to recognize HBV-infected hepatocytes
To test the potential of the 3 mAb in recognize HBV-infected hepatocytes, we first evaluated their binding capacity to HepG2-N10 cells, which is stably transfected with 1.3-fold HBV genome of genotype Ae [ ]. In contrast to HBV-free HepG2 cells, all three mAbs showed significantly (p < 0.01) higher MFI (mean fluorescent intensity) signals in binding with the HepG2-N10 cells ( A). Specifically, S20-5G1 and S197-3G11 exhibited approximately a 4-fold higher MFI, whereas C18-75 showed a 1.9-fold MFI increase ( A). Moreover, we infected HepG2-NTCP (HepG2-2B1) cells with cell culture-derived HBV (ccHBV) of different multiplicities of infection (MOI, genome equivalent per cell) ranging from 10 to 1000. As expected, simultaneous increases in the levels of HBsAg and HBeAg in the cell supernatants with the increased MOI were observed ( B). Of note, the S20-5G1 and S197-3G11 mAbs presented significant binding signals with HepG2-NTCP cells infected with over 100 MOI of ccHBV ( C). However, C18-75 did not show specific binding with ccHBV-infected cells, potentially reflecting limited infection efficiency and/or low antigen expression that may affect the sensitivity of the assay. Moreover, we also tested the reactivities of these mAb to recognize liver tissues from CHB patients. As shown in D, immunohistochemistry stains suggested the three TCR-like mAbs could bind with HBV-positive hepatocytes from patients with HLA-A2.1 allele. Particularly in the C18-75 groups, a stronger staining signal was exhibited, suggesting that C18-75 is capable of target recognition in natural specimens( C).

 

## Anti-HBV TCR-like bispecific antibodies can effectively recognize cells bearing relevant pHLA and activate immune cells in vitro
TCR-like antibodies can be converted to bispecific antibodies (BsAbs) with dual binding activities to targets and effector immune cells, like T cells. The single-chain diabody (scDb), which consists of a single-chain protein consisting of variable regions from two antibodies separated by flexible linkers, is a commony used bispecific antibody format as described previously [ , ]. In this study, we generated BsAb by fusion of the individual TCR-like mAbs (C18-75, S20-5G1, and S197-3G11) with an anti-human (UCHT1) or mouse (145-2C11) CD3ϵ scFv following the scDb format ( A). As expected, all six recombinant BsAbs preserved binding capabilities to the corresponding pHLA complexes (Figure S2A-C) and the relevant peptide-pulsed T2 cells ( B). These TCR-like BsAbs fuzed with UCHT1 or 145-2C11 could also specifically recognize human or mouse primary T cells ( C-D). To test whether the BsAbs can bridge the CD3ϵ and the relevant pHLA complexes, we established a double-sandwich ELISA, as illustrated in Figure S2D. The results demonstrated all BsAbs could simultaneously bind with the relevant pHLA complexes and human or mouse CD3ϵ (Figure S2E and S2F). Moreover, the dual binding activities of these BsAbs were also evidenced by surface plasmon resonance analysis, and their affinity decreased by less than one log compared to monoclonal antibodies ( E-G and S2G-H). These BsAbs have a Tm value of approximately 51∼54°C (Figure S3A-B), and they still possess the same reactivity after two weeks of storage at 37°C (Figure S3C-D), indicating that these BsAbs have good thermal stability. To further test the function of BsAbs, we performed co-cultures of human PBMCs and T2 cells pulsed with cognate or irrelevant peptides in the presence of UCHT1-based BsAbs and assessed effector cell activation via measurements of IFN-γ release in supernatants. H to J showed that the UCHT1-chimeric C18-75, S20-5G1, and S197-3G11 BsAbs could induce significantly elevated IFN-γ secretions, which were cognate peptide-dependent. Of note, among the three BsAbs, the S20-5G1-UCHT1 showed the most potent activities, which could activate significant IFN-γ release at a concentration as low as 0.0002 nM. Overall, these data demonstrated the bispecific binding potencies and specificities of these BsAbs, suggesting they may engage and activate T cells to kill target cells bearing cognate pHLA complexes.

 

## Anti-HBV therapeutic effects of TCR-like BsAbs in HLA-A2.1/HBV double transgenic mice
Next, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of 145-2C11-fuzed C18-75, S20-5G1, and S197-3G11 BsAbs in HLA-A2.1/HBV-dTg mice. In the experiment, HLA-A2.1/HBV-dTg mice were grouped and received infusions of the three TCR-like BsAbs via intravenous injection at 5, 10, and 15 mg/kg dosages, respectively. During one-week daily monitoring for serum HBsAg levels and animal body-weight changes, compared with the control group (received PBS), mice that received BsAbs treatments exhibited body-weight loss and HBsAg decline, particularly in the C18-75 and S20-5G1 groups, after a transient elevation of HBsAg in some groups. ( A and B). During the monitoring period, changes in the body weight and serum HBsAg levels of the animals showed a dose-dependent effect of the BsAbs, with higher doses of the BsAbs showing a greater reduction in both body weight and HBsAg. For the high-dose (15 mg/kg) groups, the C18-75, S20-5G1, and S197-3G11 BsAbs caused a maximum body-weight loss of 10.7%, 5.5%, and 11.9%, accompanied by a percentage of HBsAg reduction of 48%, 46%, and 80%, respectively. Notably, the effects of BsAbs in HLA-A2.1/HBV-dTg mice, either in causing body-weight loss or serum HBsAg decline, were transient and almost recovered to similar levels to the control groups on day 7 post-infusion. To investigate the mechanism of action of the TCR-like BsAbs, we performed immunohistochemical (IHC) analyses using liver tissues collected from mice treated with C18-75 BsAb at different time points (0, 6, 12, 24, and 72 hours) after antibody infusion. As shown in C, livers of C18-75 BsAbs-treated mice showed rapid (since 6 hours post-infusion) upregulation of myeloperoxidase (MPO), followed by a gradual decrease over time. The intrahepatic MPO appearance suggested neutrophil infiltration and transient inflammation. At 24 hours post-injection, staining signals of mouse CD3 emerged weakly and experienced upregulation at 72 hours, signifying the recruitment of T cells in the liver. Concurrently, intrahepatic Cathepsin K (CatK) elevated since 12 hours post-injection and progressively intensified over time. In addition to mediating bone resorption, CatK is also produced in response to cytokine stimulation following tissue injury [ ], implying the potential elimination of HBV-infected liver cells after BsAb infusion. The IHC analyses of mouse liver tissues from S20-5G1 and S197-3G11 treated groups also presented similar results (Figure S4). These data evidenced that these BsAbs activated intrahepatic immune cell infiltration, ultimately resulting in the apoptosis of target cells. To test whether the role of BsAbs is dependent on the cognate pHLA, we further performed BsAb infusion at 10 mg/kg in different mouse strains, including HBV-Tg, HLA-A2.1, and C57BL/6. Compared with the data of HLA-A2.1/HBV-dTg mice that received BsAb treatments at the same dose, the three BsAb-induced body-weight declines were abolished ( D). In addition, the C18-75 and S20-5G1 infusion-associated decreasing effects on HBsAg were also weakened. Interestingly, in HBV-Tg mice that do not bear HLA-A2.1, the S197-3G11-mediated HBsAg suppression could also be noted, possibly attributed to the cross-reactivity of this mAb with circulating HBsAg ( D).

 

## Anti-HBV therapeutic effects of TCR-like BsAbs in HLA-A2.1 humanized mice with HBV persistence
The abovementioned evaluations in HLA-A2.1/HBV-dTg mice preliminarily suggested the anti-HBV therapeutic effects of the BsAbs developed in our study. However, as the HBV genome is integrated into mouse chromosomes in this mouse model, all hepatocytes may theoretically express HBV antigens and pHLA complexes and thereby act as the targets of BsAbs. Therefore, the HLA-A2.1/HBV-dTg mice may not be suitable for assessing the long-term effects of BsAbs. To further test the long-term HBsAg suppression effects of BsAbs, we generated HBV-persistent mice via AAV-mediated HBV genome in vivo transduction in both HLA-A*02:01 humanized mice (B-HLA-A2.1) and C57BL/6 mice (HLA-A2.1 absent) [ ]. B-HLA-A2.1 and C57BL/6-based HBV persistent mice were divided into four groups (n = 5 per group) and administrated with C18-75, S20-5G1, S197-3G11, and PBS (phosphate buffer saline) infusion at weeks 0 and 2, respectively. All groups presented extremely high baseline serum HBsAg levels, averaging over 20,000 IU/mL. For B-HLA-A2.1-based HBV carrier mice, at two weeks (days 14) after the first dose administration, two (2/5) mice treated with C18-75 BsAb and one (1/5) mice treated with S20-5G1 BsAb showed marked HBsAg decline (dropped to a level <5,000 IU/mL) ( A-D). When assessing at six weeks (days 56) after the second dose administration, 4, 3, 1, and 0 mice infused with C18-75, S20-5G1, S197-3G11, and PBS control showed an endpoint HBsAg <5,000 IU/mL, respectively. In contrast, when the same BsAb treatments were performed in wildtype C57BL/6-based HBV persistent mice, most animals treated with BsAbs showed a gradual and slight decline in HBsAg levels over time, which resembled the decline seen in the phosphate buffer saline (PBS)-treated group ( A-D). Marked serum HBsAg decline (endpoint level <5,000 IU/mL) was only observed in one mouse of the S197-3G11 group, whereas none in other groups. These results further demonstrated that the HBsAg suppression effects of C18-75 and S20-5G1 highly depended on the HLA-A2.1 expression. Similar to that observed on serum HBsAg, marked and transient mouse body-weight decreases were also noted in B-HLA-A2.1/HBV mice treated with the three BsAb. For the first dose, the body weights of B-HLA-A2.1/HBV mice decreased immediately after BsAb infusions but gradually returned to baseline within 7 days for the S20-5G1 and S197-3G11 groups and within 14 days for those in the C18-75 group (Figure S5A). Of note, the BsAb-associated body-weight loss appeared to be more pronounced during the post-infusion period of the first dose than with the second dose (Figure S5A). On the other hand, although the BsAb-associated mouse body-weight reduction was also seen in C57BL/6-based HBV mice, it was much milder than that in B-HLA-A2.1/HBV mice (Figure S5B). When examining serum levels of liver enzymes, the BsAb injections of C18-75 and S20-5G1 led to marked ALT and AST flares after either the first or second dose in B-HLA-A2.1/HBV mice but not in C57BL/6-based HBV mice ( E-F). Furthermore, we collected mouse liver tissues at the experimental endpoint (week 8) to assess the intrahepatic HBV antigens. As shown in G, immunohistochemical analysis revealed a reduction in the HBcAg-positive hepatocytes and the HBsAg-positive liver area in B-HLA-A2.1 mice treated with BsAbs compared to the C57BL/6 mice or the PBS group, the positive area of tan signal markers in the control group was larger than that in the experimental group ( G). BsAbs-treated B-HLA-A2.1 mice also showed a significant decline in intrahepatic HBV DNA (Figure S5C). Moreover, we also confirmed that non-HBV-associated ScDb do not lead to sustained reduction or even clearance of serum HBsAg in mice ( A). These results suggested that TCR-like BsAbs could eliminate HLA-A2.1 and HBV double-positive hepatocytes in mice.

 

## Cross-reactivities of TCR-like mAbs against pHLA from different HBV genotypes and HLA-A2 alleles
To assess the accessibility of these three TCR-like antibodies within different populations, we conducted additional investigations on their diverse range of recognition targets, including various HBV genotypes and HLA-A2 alleles. According to viral genetic divergence, HBV can be classified into ten genotypes (A to J). We analyzed the cross-genotype amino-acid variations of the three epitopes involved in our study. For the C18 epitope, the variations include V27I in genotype B/C and P25Y in genotype H. The cell binding tests using the relevant peptides pulsed T2 cells suggested the C18-75 mAbs could efficiently bind with the pHLA complexes carrying mutated peptides with P25Y or V27I ( B). The R24 K variation of the S20 epitope presented in HBV genotypes B, E, F, and J dramatically abolished the binding activities of S20-5G1. For the S197 epitope, the M198I, Y200F, S204N, and M198I/S204N are presented in genotypes J, B, G, and F ( A), respectively. As shown in B, the M198I substitution eliminated the activity of S197-3G11, and the Y200F mutation in genotype B also markedly diminished its binding capability, whereas the S204N exerted a negligible impact on the mAb. The decreased binding capacity caused by these variants is consistent with the results of alanine scanning.

 Although the HLA-A*02:01 allele is relatively common in the population, it encompasses only a limited portion of the population [ ]. Recent studies have revealed that the same HBV epitope can be presented by HLA molecules of various subtypes or even types [ ]. Besides HLA-A*02:01, the HLA subtypes of A*02:03, A*02:06, or A*02:07 could also present the C18 epitope [ ]. Notably, the C18-75 mAb could bind with pHLA complexes consisting of HLA-A*02:03, HLA-A*02:06, and HLA-A*02:07 subtypes ( C-D), showing comparable affinities to that of HLA-A*02:01. The S20 epitope was also predicted to be presented by HLA-A*02:06 subtype [ ]. The S20-5G1 could also bind to the HLA-A*02:06 presented S20 peptide complex with a similar affinity to that presented by the HLA-A*02:01 ( E-F). These results suggested these TCR-like mAbs may target the cognate peptide of certain HBV genotypes presented by various HLA-A2 subtypes.

## Discussion
Recent progress of adaptive T-cell-based immunotherapy against cancers in clinical applications highlighted its therapeutic potential to treat persistent viral infections. Several studies have explored the possibilities of engineered CAR-T and TCR-T-based strategies in treating chronic HBV infection and HBV-related HCC [ , , ]. Many CARs or TCRs with high affinities against HBV antigens or pHLA complexes have been developed and shown inspiring in vitro and in vivo efficacy [ , , ]. However, the CAR-T or TCR-T strategies require adoptive cell transfer and ex vivo modifications of the patient's effector cells, which hinder the clinical translations and final applications due to the high costs and limited accessibility. T-cell engager antibodies provide a robust and convenient tool for achieving effects similar to TCR-T or CAR-T therapies but do not require personalized cell manipulations. For HBV, a previous study described T-cell engager BsAbs based on HBsAg-specific antibodies fuzed with CD3 or CD28 antibodies. They showed excellent inhibition of HBV expressing liver cancer in mice by the BsAbs and elucidate their mechanism of antion, which may have important implications for the future treatment of liver cancer [ ]. However, the BsAbs only recognize HBsAg epitopes, do not have a clear relationship with HLA, and tend to favour the treatment of HBV-induced tumors rather than chronic hepatitis B. Moreover, the high levels of circulating HBsAg in patients or animals with HBV persistence may act as a competitive inhibitor to reduce the cell-targeting effects of the anti-HBs-based T-cell-engaging antibodies. In contrast, soluble TCR or TCR-like antibody-based BsAbs could provide more specific targeting against virus-infected cells, bypassing the interference of circulating virus antigens such as those encountered by CAR T cells. ImmTAV-Env is a bispecific soluble TCR protein that can redirect endogenous polyclonal T cells to target HBV-infected cells and eliminate them without potentially being affected by high levels of free antigens in the environment, while demonstrating a good safety profile against primary human hepatocytes [ ].
Currently, more and more TCR-like antibodies are generated and used extensively in basic research, and there are over 40 TCR-like antibodies in pre-clinical development for cancer or infectious diseases [ , , , ]. Two T-cell therapies, ET140203 and ECT204, from Eureka Therapeutics for the treatment of HCC or hepatoblastoma have been granted Orphan Drug Designation (ODD) by the FDA. These therapies engineer T cells with TCR-like antibodies to target HLA complexes presenting AFP or GPC3 antigenic peptides on the surface of tumor cells [ ]. For HBV, A. Bertoletti's team has previously generated TCR-like antibodies that recognize HBV pHLA complexes specific to HBcAg C18 and HBsAg Env183 (same as S20 in our study) epitopes [ , ]. These antibodies have been harnessed to direct the delivery of interferon to HBV-infected hepatocytes [ ]. Compared to soluble TCR proteins, TCR-like antibodies targeting pHLA complexes can combine the specificity of the target of TCR capture with the stable biological and pharmacological properties of the antibody, and at the same time it can shift from a T cell therapy mode to an antibody-mediated immune response, triggering a broader pathway of action, with broader application prospects. In addition, TCR-like antibodies can also avoid the possible cytokine storm caused by cell therapy [ , , ]. However, T-cell engager BsAbs based on TCR-like antibodies that recognize HBV pHLA complexes still lack conceptual study to explore the anti-HBV therapeutic potential, particularly for the efficacy of eliminating HBV-infected hepatocytes in animal models.
In this study, we developed three TCR-like mAbs with high affinity to three HBV epitopes presented on HLA-A*02:01, providing new tools to recognize HBV-harboured cells. Through antibody engineering, we generated novel T-cell engagers with bispecific binding activities against relevant HBV pHLA and CD3, which showed potent in vitro capabilities to induce activation of polyclonal T cells at subnanomolar dosage ( ). In HBV mouse models, for both transgenic and AAV-HBV transduced, two BsAb (C18-75 and S20-5G1) exhibited marked effects in suppressing serum HBsAg levels and eliminating HBV-infected hepatocytes ( and ). In addition, BsAbs, especially 75-1452C11 and 5G1-1452C11, did not cause significant reactions in single HLA or HBV transgenic mice, which also reflected their specificity. While the antibody C18-75 maintains high binding activity to target cells, some non-specific reactivity was observed ( and A, H and S1C). However, this non-specific reaction usually only occurred at high antibody concentrations, high than those typically involved in clinical applications.
Interestingly, the S197-3G11 mAb recognizing pHLA complex of S197 epitope, showing binding activities against both pHLA and intact HBsAg protein, exhibited less in vivo anti-HBV effects than the other two mAbs. Weight loss indirectly reflecting the liver injury caused by the antibodies, affecting their normal diet and activities, provided a potential indication of drug activity in vivo. Through this phenomenon, we can preliminarily judge whether the antibody has played a corresponding effect, and at the same time evaluate the acceptable degree of safety, so weight loss is the expected outcome of BsAbs. Given that the liver enzyme elevations and weight loss associated with S197-3G11 BsAb infusion in B-HLA-A2.1/HBV mice were also obviously less pronounced than those observed with the other two antibodies, we speculated that this antibody's in vivo capacity to activate T cells to kill HBV-expressing liver cells is inferior to the others, and although it can also reduce HBV DNA levels in the liver of mice, there may still be many remaining HBV-carrying hepatocytes in mice preventing HBsAg clearance. This may be due to its cross-reactivity with free HBsAg, which competitively inhibits the T cell redirection mediated by the BsAb to some extent. In addition, the difference in the number and distribution of pHLA complexes on the cell surface may also be the cause of this phenomenon. In in vivo models, different peptides may be displayed at different frequencies, and the display frequency of the same peptide on different cells is not necessarily uniform, which are important factors to be considered and investigated in future TCR-related studies [ ].
The TCR-like BsAbs acted as T cell engagers to recruit and activate T cells, triggering the release of cytokines such as IFN-γ. In animals with HBV persistence and HLA-A2.1 expression, our data showed liver inflammation occurred rapidly after BsAb injection and resolved within a few days ( C and Figure S4) [ ]. During this process, the intrahepatic CD3 signal was observed at 24 hours and gradually increased, accompanied by serum liver enzyme fluctuations and the presence of CatK ( C and Figure S4). This phenomenon suggested that the initial liver inflammation possibly resulted from target cell killing mediated by the BsAb-redirected T cells. Meanwhile, intracellular viral antigen releases from damaged hepatocytes may cause a transient increase followed by a subsequent decrease in serum antigen levels, as observed in some of the mouse models in the study, such as 75-1452C11 and 3G11-1452C11 treated HLA/HB-dTg mice ( and ). In addition, viral transcriptional silencing may also be a way to influence the efficacy of therapy, but this usually occurs with the action of siRNA [ ]. The relative quantification of HBV DNA in mouse liver suggests that cell damage and clearance is still the main mechanism of action in this study (Figure S5C), but the possibility of transcriptional silencing cannot be ruled out.
Against CHB, inducing immune control to eliminate HBV-infected cells safely is proposed as one of the essential approaches toward curing hepatitis B [ ]. Clinically, patients who achieved functional cure (HBsAg loss) may experience ALT flares [ ]. Among patients receiving peginterferon alpha-2a combined with NA treatments, on-treatment ALT elevations were significantly associated with a higher probability of HBsAg loss [ ], which highlighted the importance of immune-mediated elimination of HBV-harbouring hepatocytes towards achieving CHB functional cure. Our study provided preclinical evidence for the therapeutic potential of TCR-like BsAb to eradicate hepatocytes expressing HBV antigens, including HBcAg and HBsAg, which may be derived from cccDNA or viral genome integrated into the host chromosome. Several challenges should be noted for further development of TCR-like BsAbs in treating chronic HBV infection. Firstly, the HLA restriction of antigen presentation and the high HLA polymorphism in human populations imply that a single TCR-like antibody may not be able to cover the majority of HBV patients. Further development of a set of TCR-like antibodies targeting high-frequency HLA subtypes (presenting different viral epitopes) may be a way to address this issue. Secondly, the HBV suppression effects of TCR-like BsAbs are associated with the direct killing of hepatocytes. Consequently, for safety concerns, this strategy may be more suitable for patients with a low proportion of hepatocytes expressing HBV antigens. In patients with a high proportion of infected hepatocytes, using an TCR-like antibody to clear part of the hepatocytes might pose a risk, making it necessary to adopt a cautious dosing strategy. A recent study has revealed that NA treatment could significantly reduce intrahepatic frequencies of HBV antigen-positive hepatocytes in either HBeAg-positive or HBeAg-negative CHB patients [ ]. Antiviral pretreatments with NA drugs may improve the safety of TCR-like BsAb-mediated anti-HBV regimens. Additionally, any treatment based on infected liver cell clearance, such as TCR-like antibodies, must also be extensively evaluated for specificity before clinical use.
In summary, we developed TCR-like bispecific antibodies that could redirect T cells to target and eliminate HBV-infected cells bearing pHLA complexes. Our data demonstrated the anti-HBV therapeutic potential of TCR-like BsAb in both cell and animal models, laying the groundwork for future development of such revolutionary strategies to achieve hepatitis B cure.

## Methods and materials

## Viruses, cells, and experimental animals
Viral particles of recombinant AAV (rAAV)-HBV1.3 (ayw) were purchased from Beijing FivePlus Molecular Medicine Institute. HBV virus for cell infection experiments was produced by HepAD38 cells following previously described [ ]. HBV sequences of different genomes were obtained from HBVdb Index (The Hepatitis B Virus database). The 174×CEM.T2 (T2) cells were purchased from Shanghai Fuheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The HepG2 cells were purchased from ATCC.
C57BL/6 and BALB/c mice were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal. HLA-A*02:01 humanized mice (B-HLA-A2.1) mice were purchased from Biocytogen Pharmaceuticals (Beijing). The HBV-Tg mice were provided by Pei-Jer Chen (Taiwan University) and bred at the Laboratory Animal Centre of Xiamen University, an animal model that persistently carries high levels of HBV DNA and expresses HBsAg and HBcAg [ , ]. HLA-A2.1 transgenic mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Strain #:003475). The HLA/HBV-dTg mice were generated via cross-breding of the HLA-A2.1 and HBV-Tg strains in our laboratory.

## Constructs, protein expressions, and purifications
Three pHLA fusion proteins (C18hA2, S20hA2, S197hA2) were constructed by linking epitope, human β2M, and HLA-A2 extracellular domain (ECD) using a (GGGGS)2 linker, and an amino acid substitution (Y84C) was introduced in the HLA-A2 [ ]. A polyhistidine tag (his) motif or human Fc tag (hFc) motif was appended to the protein expression sequence.
The bispecific antibodies were constructed by connecting the variable regions of the antibodies in the following order: TCR-like antibody light chain, anti-mouse CD3 antibody heavy chain, anti-mouse CD3 antibody light chain, and TCR-like antibody heavy chain [ ]. Subsequently, they were incorporated into the expression vector, along with a polyhistidine tag [ ].
Transient protein expressions and the establishment of stable cell lines expressing the protein were conducted using the ExpiCHO expression system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The culture supernatants were collected and purified using Ni Sepharose 6FF or Protein A (Cytiva).

## Screening of TCR-like antibody
BALB/c mice were immunized with multiple doses of recombinant pHLA protein mixed freund's adjuvant and a splenic immunization three days before the sacrifice, their spleens were isolated for hybridoma cell fusion. The cultural supernatant from the cells was incubated with 1 × 106 T2 cells pulsed with targeted peptides or control AFP158-166 peptide after blocking with irrelevant pHLA complex, the blocked cell supernatant avoids screening for HLA-only antibodies. The T2 cells incubated with the cell supernatant were washed with DPBS and centrifuged, then stained with fluorescently labelled goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L), F(ab’)2 Fragment antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, 4410S) and subsequently detected. Flow cytometry was used to measure the fluorescence intensity,. The MFI of T2 cells corresponding to the incubation target peptide and the control peptide of the same clone was compared, and the clone with a larger ratio was selected for the next round of screening until the monoclonal antibody cell line was obtained.

## ELISA assay
100μL of 1μg/mL protein or 4μg/mL polypeptide in carbonate buffer (pH9.6) were coatedin 96-well microporous plates, incubated at 37℃ for 2 h, washed and blocked, and incubated at 37℃ for 2 h for use.
The TCR-like antibody or BsAbs were gradiently diluted at the initial concentration of 10μg/mL, then added into the well at a volume of 100μL, incubated at 37℃ for 1 h and washed the plate, and added HRP conjugated Goat anti-mouse IgG (for mAbs) or anti-his tag antibody (for BsAbs) (Proteintech, HRP-66005), incubated at 37℃ for 30 min and washed the plate. and the value was read after colour development was terminated. The control pHLA complexes in the experiment were HLA-A2 and AFP158-166 epitope (KWVESIFLIF) complexes.
In multiple ELISA assays, recombinant CD3ϵ & CD3γ heterodimer protein hFc Tag (Sino Biological inc, CT061-M02H) was coated, and then BsAbs, recombinant pHLA complexes, and HRP conjugated anti-β2 m antibody are subsequently added. The diluent and blocking liquid used in the tests are from Beijing Wantai Biological.

## Flow cytometry
1 × 106 T2 cells were exposed to 10 μg of peptide and pulsed for 2-6 hours at 37℃. After that, the cells were washed twice with PBS. The antibodies were incubated with the T2 cells for 1 hour and stained with fluorescent conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L), F(ab’)2 Fragment antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, 4410S) for 30 minutes at 4℃. After thorough washing, the samples were analyzed by flow cytometer (BD, LSRFortessaX-20). The control peptide in the experiment were AFP158-166 epitope.
HepG2 cells and HepG2-N10 cells were pre-stimulated with 200 units (U) of IFN-γ for 48 hours and then blocked with unrelated mouse IgG1 antibodies. The cells were then stained sequentially using TCR-like antibodies and subsequently measured. The mAbs were labelled with a fluorescent dye such as Alexa Fluor® 647 using a conjugation kit (Abcam, ab169823), which added the Modifier reagent after mixing antibody and fluorescent dry powder, followed by Quencher reagent at room temperature for 15 min to complete the rapid fluorescence labelling. When the concentration of antibody labelled fluorescence is 1 mg/mL, the dilution of C18-75 is 1:500, and the dilution of S197-3G11 and S20-5G1 is 1:100.
HepG2-NTCP (2B1) cells were treated with doxycycline (DOX) for 4–5 days to induce NTCP expression and were subsequently infected with HBV at the corresponding MOI using PEG8000 [ ]. On day 5 after post-virus infection, the cells were collected and then subjected to the blocking and staining procedures as described above. The dilution of all three antibodies is 1:200. The culture supernatants were collected to measure the concentrations of HBsAg and HBeAg.
Through negative selection, human T cells were isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) using magnetic beads (Stemcell). Murine T cells were obtained from mouse spleens and collected using magnetic bead sorting (Stemcell) after tissue grinding. The BsAbs were labelled with a fluorescent dye such as Alexa Fluor® 647 using a conjugation kit (Abcam, ab169823) as described above, and then used for staining 1 × 106 T cells or T2 cells that had been pulsed with peptides at 4℃. The dilution of all three antibodies is 1:200.

## Surface plasmon resonance assay (SPR)
TCR-like antibodies were immobilized onto a protein G sensor chip (Cytiva) at approximately 100–200 response units (RUs) using Biacore 8 K (Cytiva). The sensor chip was then injected with serial dilutions of recombinant pHLA. The Biacore 8 K was used to measure the change in response units. The RU data were fitted to a 1:1 binding model using the Biacore Insight evaluation software. The association rates (ka, 1/Ms), dissociation rates (kd, 1/s), and affinity constants (KD, M) were calculated using the evaluation software of the Biacore 8 K system. For the identification of affinity of bispecific antibodies, we used the above method to replace the chip with protein A, the first injected protein was replaced with the pHLA complex carrying hFc tag instead of his tag, and then injected the bispecific antibodies with ScDb structure.A CM5 sensor chip was coated with recombinant CD3ϵ & CD3γ heterodimer protein hFc Tag (Sino Biological inc, CT061-M02H & CT089-H46H). The surface of the sensor chip was activated using an Amine coupling kit and Acetate pH4.5 (Cytiva). BsAbs and recombinant pHLA were sequentially diluted with PBS-P + buffer and injected. T2he change in response units (RUs) was recorded in real-time to determine if the injected sample could elicit a response.

## Thermal stability by differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF)
The DSF was detected using a real-time PCR instrument, Bio-Rad T100™Thermal Cycler. The purified BsAbs was diluted to the corresponding concentration using PBS, and the fluorescent dye SYPRO Orange (Sigma-Aldrich) was diluted 50 times from the concentration of the original solution with deionized water, and mixed with BsAbs at the ratio of 1:9. Subsequently, 50 μL of the mixture was added to each well. The real-time PCR machine is heated from 10°C to 90°C at intervals of about 0.5°C. Fluorescence emission was collected using HEX filters (560 nm-580 nm). The melting curve of fluorescence intensity at 570 nm was obtained by Bio-Rad CFX Manager.

## T cells redirection assay
After the removal of endotoxin using Detoxi-Gel Endotoxin Removing Columns (Thermo Scientific), gradient dilutions of BsAbs were added to a 96-well microplate. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) (4 × 104 cells/well) were isolated and co-incubated with T2 cells (2 × 104 cells/well) pulsed with the corresponding peptide (10μmol) at a ratio of 2:1 in microwells containing antibodies. Additionally, 200U of IL-2 was added to the system, and after 20 hours of incubation, the supernatant was collected for cytokine detection. IFN-γ was detected using the Human IFN-γ ELISA kit (Dakewe Biotech, 1110003).

## Animals study
HLA/HBV-dTg, HBV-Tg, HLA-Tg, and C57BL/6 mice were received intravenous antibody injections on Day 0 ( , Figure S4 and Figure S5). Body weight was measured daily and peripheral blood was collected daily to obtain serum samples for seven consecutive days. B-HLA-A2.1 and C57BL/6 mice received tail vein injections of rAAV8-HBV1.3 (ayw) and were subsequently grouped based on their serum HBsAg levels after 3–4 weeks. Antibodies were diluted in PBS and administered at Week 0 and Week 2. Body weight was measured weekly, and peripheral blood was collected to obtain serum samples until Week 8 or 12. In addition, body weight was also weighed and blood was collected on day 1 and day 3 after each dosing. Subsequently, mice were euthanized, and their livers were isolated for further investigation. The animal studies were approved by the Laboratory Animal Centre of Xiamen University. (XMULAC20220054).

## Relative quantification of intrahepatic HBV DNA
After paraffin-embedded liver tissue was cut into 5-10μm slices, total DNA was extracted using Tianquick FFPE DNA kit (TIANGEN, DP330), and primers and probes corresponding to HBV or mouse GADPH (NM_008084.3) were designed for real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR(Bio-Rad, CFX96 Touch). Primers of HBV: F (5’-TTTCACCTCTGCCTAATCAT-3’); R (5’-TCAGAAGGCAAAAAAGAGAGTAACTC-3’). Primers of GADPH: F (5’-AGCTCACTGGCATGGCCTTC-3’); R (5’-CGCCTGCTTCACCACCTTCT-3’). Calculate the relative HBV DNA levels normalized to GAPDH.

## Quantitative immunoassays and immunohistochemical staining
HBsAg levels were measured using a chemiluminescence enzyme immunoassay (Beijing Wantai Biological, HBV-1396). ALT and AST levels were determined using a fully automatic biochemical analyzer equipped with the corresponding reagents (Mindray Bio-Medical). Mouse liver tissues were fixed in 10% formalin for a duration of over 72 hours, subsequently embedded in paraffin, and sectioned following dehydration. Human liver biopsy samples were obtained from the Department of Infectious Diseases at Xiang'an Hospital of Xiamen University (XAHLL2023007). Written informed consent was obtained. All three antibodies work at a concentration of 2μg/mL. The mAbs 83H12 (1μg/mL) and 11H10 (1μg/mL) were used to detect HBsAg and HBcAg, allowing for the assessment of the extent of infected hepatocytes [ ]. Staining of mouse CD3 (1:200 diluted), myeloperoxidase (MPO) (1:500 diluted), and Cathepsin K (CatK) (1:1000 diluted) was performed using distinct rabbit polyclonal antibodies (Servicebio, Wuhan). The results were captured using a fully automatic digital slide scanner, specifically the AxioScan7 model (Zeiss).

## Statistical analyses
The unpaired t-test, or Kruskal-Wallis test, was used to compare continuous variables between groups. Statistical significance was determined for P-values less than 0.05, using a two-tailed approach. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo V10 software. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software.
