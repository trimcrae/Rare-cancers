ZhouLing ZhangFengqin WangLingling ZhengPengdou MaoZhenyu ZhuXiaoyan ZiGuisha ChenLixiang CaiXiaojing LiuHuiguo LiuWei , ] Obstructive sleep apnoea–hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS) is a sleep-related respiratory disorder in which partial or complete airway obstruction occurs repeatedly during sleep, leading to sleep deprivation and intermittent hypoxia.[ ] The reported prevalence of OSAHS in different regions is approximately 2% to 4%.[ ] Research has suggested a close mutual relationship between these two diseases.[ ] Many asthma patients exhibit OSAHS symptoms or are diagnosed with OSAHS through polysomnography (PSG), and OSAHS may exacerbate asthma and its presence is associated with poor asthma control.[ ] According to PSG results, 38.9% of severe asthma patients have OSAHS.[ ] Currently, up to 75% of asthma patients have not achieved disease control, and research suggests that OSAHS and related intermittent hypoxia (IH) may impact the onset and progression of asthma.[ ] However, the mechanisms underlying the interaction between OSAHS and bronchial asthma are not yet fully understood.
IH is a primary pathophysiological feature of OSAHS.[ ] Although the role of IH in airway inflammation in asthma remains poorly understood, inflammation has been suggested to be a major contributing factor in studies that have explored how OSAHS worsens asthma.[ ] Among patients with persistent asthma symptoms, up to 60% have airway inflammation primarily driven by neutrophils rather than eosinophils, and these patients exhibit poor responses to standardized asthma treatment.[ – ] A study investigating the sleep quality of severe asthma patients showed that an increase of one standard deviation in the Sleep Apnea Scale of Sleep Disorders Questionnaire (SA-SDQ) score was associated with a 7.78% increase in the percentage of neutrophils in sputum.[ ] Therefore, in asthma patients, there is a close association between OSAHS and neutrophilic airway inflammation, suggesting that OSAHS may be a significant contributing factor to neutrophilic asthma.[ ]
Some research has revealed that activation of the NOD-like receptor pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome plays a role in the pathogenesis of neutrophilic asthma.[ , ] NLRP3 levels are elevated in the airways of neutrophilic asthma patients.[ – ] The NLRP3 inflammasome can be activated in IH-treated RAW264.7 cells, promoting the progression of lung inflammation.[ ] A study showed that bronchial epithelial pyroptosis exacerbates airway inflammation and hyper-responsiveness in toluene diisocyanate (TDI)-induced asthma via NLRP3 inflammasome activation and gasdermin D (GSDMD) cleavage.[ ] Chronic IH induces TGF-β-mediated epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition and aggravates airway collagen deposition.[ ] In the context of asthma, the NLRP3 inflammasome complex plays a critical role in bronchial epithelial pyroptosis, particularly as observed in severe asthma. The activation of this inflammasome complex is an early response to hypoxia and is regulated by hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α).[ ] HIF-1α is a key factor in the regulation of oxygen metabolism and is highly expressed in OSAHS patients.[ ] HIF-1α is a crucial transcription factor that activates numerous genes, including NLRP3.[ ] However, the role of IH-mediated inflammation in the development of neutrophilic asthma is still unclear.
This study aimed to investigate whether OSAHS exacerbates airway inflammation in patients with asthma at the clinical level and to explore the correlations among HIF-1α, NLRP3 and various clinical indicators. To determine whether IH-induced airway inflammation is initiated by pyroptosis, we examined the potential effects of IH on interleukin (IL)-13/OVA-induced inflammation at the cellular and animal levels and the underlying mechanisms involved. Furthermore, we investigated whether IH affects the occurrence and progression of asthma-associated inflammation by promoting the HIF-1α-regulated NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis pathway in asthmatic airways.

## Methods

## Study subjects
After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, patients who were diagnosed with severe bronchial asthma at Tongji Hospital Affiliated with Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology between January 2022 and December 2022 were enrolled, and the patients were diagnosed by polysomnography.
Inclusion criteria were as follows[ , ]: (1) All patients included in this study were assessed as having severe asthma, defined as grades 4 and 5 with incomplete or uncontrolled asthma despite optimized high-intensity treatment. (2) All participants underwent sleep monitoring by PSG (SOMNO screen plus PSG+, Wurzburg, Germany). Diagnostic criteria for OSAHS were as follows: sleep monitoring no less than 7 hours per night, more than 30 recurrent sleep events or an apnea hypopnea index (AHI) 5 times/hour. The severity was classified as mild (AHI 5–14 events/hour), moderate (AHI 15–29 events/hour), or severe (AHI ≥30 events/hour). (3) Patients who were willing to participate in the study and signed an informed consent form.
The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe lung disease that could affect lung function or other examination results. (2) Patients who were not suitable for induced sputum were excluded for the following criteria: ① pulmonary function tests indicating an forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) <1 L; ② recent episodes of significant haemoptysis, acute asthma exacerbation, respiratory failure, pneumothorax, mediastinal emphysema, or any condition leading to substantial pleural or pericardial effusion or severe heart failure due to underlying heart disease; and ③ active pulmonary tuberculosis. (3) Patients with central or mixed sleep apnoea, narcolepsy, or other sleep-related disorders were excluded.
All participants provided informed consent. The Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology approved this study (No. TJ-IRB20220706).

## General data
General data, including sex, waist circumference, body mass index (BMI), neck circumference, age, hypertension status, diabetes status, coronary heart disease status, smoking history, gastroesophageal reflux status, age of asthma onset, FEV1% pred, FEV1/FVC (%), and polysomnography monitoring results, including the AHI, oxygen desaturation index (ODI), lowest oxygen saturation (LSpO2%), time when blood oxygen saturation was less than 90% (T90%), percentage of time when blood oxygen saturation was less than 90% (TS90%), average blood oxygen saturation, and the snoring index, were collected from the enrolled patients.

## Induced sputum and Liu’s staining
For patients who inhaled 2 puffs of albuterol aerosol, 4.5% saline was added to the nebulizer. After the inhalation, they thoroughly rinsed their mouths with water. During nebulization, the patients pinched their noses and tightly sealed their lips around the nebulizer mouthpiece. Once the nebulization was complete, the patients expectorated, and the sputum was carefully collected. The induction of sputum collection involves nebulization carried out in intervals of 1 minute, 2 minutes, 4 minutes, and 8 minutes successively until a sputum specimen is obtained. If the subject experienced any discomfort during nebulization, the process was stopped immediately, and the patient inhaled 4 puffs of albuterol aerosol. The collected sputum plug was placed in a labelled centrifuge tube. After the sputum plug was weighed, an appropriate amount of 0.1% dithiothreitol (DTT) solution was added. The sample was shaken gently at room temperature for 5 minutes on a shaker, followed by 5 minutes of incubation in a water bath. This process was repeated 2–3 times until the sputum plug was dissolved. The dissolved sputum plug was filtered through a 40 μm pore sieve and centrifuged at 1000 revolutions per minute (rpm) for 5 minutes with the centrifugal radius of 20 cm. The supernatant was aliquoted for testing, and cell counting was performed on the sediment. The cell concentration was adjusted to 5 × 105 cells/mL for cytospin analysis.
Cells in induced sputum and mouse bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were detected using Liu’s staining. Liu A solution was prepared by dissolving 0.15 g of azure B and 0.3 g of eosin Y in 100 mL of methanol, while Liu B solution consisted of 0.6 g of methylene blue and 0.6 g of potassium hydroxide dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water. The solutions were filtered through 0.22-μm membrane filters to remove any particulate matter before use.​
Four drops of Liu A solution were evenly added to the air-dried smears using a sterile pipette. The smears were then incubated at room temperature (22 ± 2°C) for 3–5 seconds to allow the initial dye uptake by the cells. Subsequently, an equal volume of Liu B solution was added dropwise, covering the entire smear surface. After a 15–30-second incubation period, the smears were gently rinsed with distilled water at a flow rate of approximately 50 mL/min for 10 seconds to remove excess stain. The rinsed smears were air-dried in a dust-free environment and examined under microscope.​

## Cell culture
Human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B, ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco, USA). Cells were treated with IL-13[ , ] or IH[ , ] to simulate asthma or OSAHS, respectively. BEAS-2B cells were grown to the appropriate confluence level and treated with IL-13 (4 ng/mL) and/or IH (21% O2 for 25 minutes, 5% O2 for 35 minutes; 1 cycle/1 hour) for 24 hours, and the NLRP3 activator group was administered nigericin to enhance the expression of NLRP3 (10 μmol/L).

## Small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection
The transfection reagent Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to transfect BEAS-2B cells with human HIF-1α-siRNAs or NC-siRNAs (Ribobio, Guangzhou, China). The transfection mixture was added to the cells for 24 h. The HIF-1α-siRNA sequence used was 5′GGAACAUGAUGGUUCACTT3′.

## Animal model and treatments
Female C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from Gempharmatech (Jiangsu, China; Laboratory Animal Production Certificate No: SCXK (Jing) 2021–0106). The animal protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji Hospital (No. TJH-202101003).
The mice were acclimated to the environment for 1 week. C57BL/6J mice were exposed to chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH) or ambient air for 1–28 days. The mice were placed in the IH chamber from 9:00 AM to 5:00 PM daily. The IH protocol was as follows: O2 was reduced from 21% to 5% and maintained for 150 seconds, followed by an increase from 5% to 21%, which was maintained for 90 seconds; one cycle took 4 minutes, and 15 cycles were completed in one hour. The IH chamber was operated for 8 hours per day.[ , ] The asthma model groups were sensitized by intraperitoneal injections of a mixture of OVA and Al(OH)3 as an adjuvant on the 3rd, 10th and 17th day. The mice were given the OVA solution via nasal drops on the 24th, 25th, 26th, 27th and 28th days. The presence of positive reactions such as restlessness, rapid breathing, and abdominal muscle spasms in the mice served as the criteria for the successful modelling of asthma.[ ] In the treatment group, the HIF-1α inhibitor LW6 (10 mg/kg) was intraperitoneally injected three times per week, and the control group was treated with the same volume of normal saline. On the 29th day, the mice were euthanized and tissue samples and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were collected [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C428].

## ELISA
Commercial kits for the detection of IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), were obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Commercial kits for the detection of HIF-1α and NLRP3 were obtained from HYcell Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). The levels of IL-6, IL-8, HIF-1α, NLRP3, TNF-α, MMP-9, and LDH were monitored by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm.

## Reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT–qPCR)
TRIzol reagent (Takara Co., Dalian, China) was used to extract total RNA from induced sputum cells and BEAS-2B cells and the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara Co., Dalian, China) was used for reverse transcription in the first-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis reaction. SYBR Premix Ex Taq (Takara Co., Dalian, China) was used for qRT–PCR reactions on a Bio-Rad C1000 Thermal Cycler. The relative mRNA expression was analysed using the 2-∆∆Ct method, and the expression level is presented as the fold change relative to the control. The primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and are shown in Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C428.

## Western blot
A protein extraction kit (HYcell Biotechnology) was used to extract protein from cells and tissues according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total protein was extracted from cultured cells and lungs with radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA) (HYcell Biotechnology). Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) (HYcell Biotechnology) was used to measure the protein concentration. The quantified protein extracts were separated on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dried milk in Tris-buffered saline with tween 20 (TBST) for 90 min. Then, the membranes were incubated with the appropriate primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The primary antibodies were provided as follows: anti-NLRP3, anti-HIF-1α, anti-GSDMD, anti-IL-1β, anti-cleaved-caspase-1, and anti-IL-18. Subsequently, the membranes were washed and treated with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labelled secondary antibody for 90 min at room temperature on a shaker. Afterwards, the membranes were again washed with 1 × TBST 3 times and assayed using a ChemiDoc XRS gel imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The protein expression levels were quantified by ImageJ (NIH Image, Bethesda, MD).

## Cell apoptosis
After the cells were treated, the cells were digested with trypsin to obtain a suspension, transferred to a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 980 rpm with the centrifugal radius of 20 cm and the supernatant was discarded. After washing the cell pellet, 500 μL of binding buffer was added, followed by 5 μL of Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC). The mixture was gently shaken, 5 μL of propidium iodide (KeyGen, Nanjing, China) was added, and the tube was gently shaken to ensure thorough mixing of the reagents. After 15 minutes, flow cytometry (Meirui, Shenzhen, China) was used for detection. Cells that were double-positive for Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (upper right quadrant) were considered apoptotic cells.

## Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining
The lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Then, the sections were embedded in paraffin, cut into 4-μm-thick sections, and subjected to H&E staining. The scoring criteria for peribronchial inflammation were as follows: 0, no inflammatory cells; 1, a small number of cells; 2, one layer of cells; 3, 2–4 layers of cells; and 4, >4 layers of cells around the bronchi. The lung tissue sections were subjected to PAS staining to determine the abundance of PAS-positive cells in the airway and scored. Lung tissue sections from the mice were stained with PAS and examined under a microscope. Intact airways were selected, and the number of PAS-positive cells in airways with different circumferences was counted. This count was then divided by the number of airways counted to obtain the average number of PAS-positive cells in airways with different circumferences.

## Immunofluorescence staining of tissues
The tissue sections were sequentially immersed in xylene twice for 15 minutes each, followed by immersion in anhydrous ethanol for 5 minutes. This process was repeated once. The sections were then immersed once in 85% ethanol and once in 75% ethanol for 5 minutes each. The sections were washed with distilled water for 5 minutes, and this step was repeated three times. Citrate repair solution was added to a pressure cooker and placed on an induction cooker, and the tissue sections were placed in the pressure cooker. The pressure cooker was heated for 2 minutes, after which the device was turned off to allow the liquid to cool to room temperature. The sections were then placed in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and soaked for 5 minutes, and this step was repeated three times. The sections were immersed in hydrogen peroxide and incubated in the dark for 25 minutes. The slides were then placed in PBS (pH 7.4) for three rounds of decolorization, each lasting 5 minutes. After the sections were air-dried, 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) was added, and the sections were incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes. The blocking solution was gently removed, and the primary antibody was added and incubated at 4°C overnight. The sections were decolorized three times for 5 minutes each. The secondary antibody was added and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 50 minutes. Rhodamine-CY3 was added to the tissue sections, followed by three washes with Phosphate Buffered Saline Tween 20 (PBST) for 5 minutes each. The slides were placed in citrate repair solution, heated in a microwave oven at high power for 6 minutes, removed and cooled to room temperature. The sections were immersed in PBS for 5 minutes of decolorization, and this decolorization step was repeated three times. 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining solution was added to the sections, and the sections were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 10 minutes. The sections were sealed with anti-fade mounting medium. The stained sections were observed under a fluorescence microscope. DAPI-stained cell nuclei appeared blue, HIF-1α-positive staining appeared green, and NLRP3-positive staining appeared red.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was performed, and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). Categorical variables are presented as n (%) and were analysed using the chi-squared test. Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and were analysed using independent samples t tests. Skewed continuous variables are presented as medians (Q1, Q3) and were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Correlation analysis was conducted using Pearson analysis, and the correlation coefficient was denoted as r. P <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. The figures were constructed with Figdraw (Duotai, Hangzhou, China).

## Results

## Analysis of the general demographic data
A total of 49 severe asthma patients were included in this study: 16 asthma patients without OSAHS, 17 asthma patients with mild or moderate OSAHS, and 16 asthma patients with severe OSAHS. Compared with asthma without OSAHS group and asthma with mild and moderate OSAHS group respectively, the waist circumference and neck circumference in the asthma with severe OSAHS group increased significantly (P <0.05). Compared with asthma with mild and moderate OSAHS group and the asthma with severe OSAHS group, the age of asthma onset in the asthma without OSAHS group was younger (P <0.05). With the worsening of OSAHS, the proportion of patients with partially controlled and uncontrolled asthma also increased (P <0.05). There was no significant difference in age, sex, comorbidities, FEV1% pred or FEV1/FVC among the three groups (P >0.05) [Table ].
 

## Classification of induced sputum cells and patient cytokine analysis
The induced sputum cells were classified and counted, and the proportions of cell types among the total cells were calculated. The results showed that the proportion of macrophages [Figure A] and eosinophils [Figure B] were decreased with the exacerbation of OSAHS, the proprotion of leukomonocyte had no significant difference among the three groups [Figure C], and the proportion of neutrophils [Figure D] increased with the exacerbation of OSAHS. With increasing OSAHS severity, the expression of IL-6 [Figure E], IL-8 [Figure F], HIF-1α [Figure G], NLRP3 [Figure H] and CRP [Figure I] in induced sputum increased.
 

## Correlation analysis of HIF-1α and NLRP3 protein expression in induced sputum cells and clinical indicators
Correlation analysis revealed that HIF-1α was positively correlated with NLRP3 (r = 0.7179) [Supplementary Figure 1A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C551], the AHI (r = 0.5471) [Supplementary Figure 1B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C551], proportion of neutrophils (r = 0.5087) [Supplementary Figure 1C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C551], and IL-6 (r = 0.4248) [Supplementary Figure 1D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C551]. HIF-1α was negatively correlated with mean blood oxygen saturation (r = –0.5747) [Supplementary Figure 1E, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C551] and the number of eosinophils (r = –0.4624) [Supplementary Figure 1F, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C551]. NLRP3 was positively correlated with the neutrophil count (r = 0.5599) [Supplementary Figure 1G, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C551] and AHI (r = 0.6797) [Supplementary Figure 1H, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C551]. NLRP3 was negatively correlated with mean blood oxygen saturation (r = –0.4036) [Supplementary Figure 1I, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C551] and the number of eosinophils (r = –0.4265) [Supplementary Figure 1J, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C551].

## IH activates the HIF-1α/NLRP3/GSDMD pathway to promote IL-13-induced pyroptosis and inflammation in vitro
Studies have proposed that IH can mediate cell damage through the HIF-1α signalling pathway. To investigate whether IH exacerbates IL-13-induced damage in BEAS-2B cells and the underlying mechanisms involved, we examined the expression of pyroptosis-related molecules (NLRP3, cleaved caspase-1, GSDMD, IL-1β, and IL-18), LDH and inflammatory factors (TNF-α, MMP-9, IL-6, and IL-8) and apoptosis rate. The results suggested that compared with the control group, IL-13 did not increase HIF-1α expression in BEAS-2B cells. However, IH or IH plus IL-13 activated HIF-1α expression, and there was higher HIF-1α expression in the IH+IL-13 group [Figure A]. Compared to the IH or IL-13 group, the IH+IL-13 group exhibited significantly increased expression of NLRP3, GSDMD, IL-1β, cleaved caspase-1 and IL-18, which indicated that IH+IL-13-induced pyroptosis in BEAS-2B cells [Figure B]. Flow cytometry showed an increase in the number of apoptotic cells to some extent in the presence of IL-13 or IH compared to that in the blank control group. However, IH plus IL-13 markedly increased the number of apoptotic BEAS-2B cells [Figure C]. IH plus IL-13 also significantly increased LDH, indicating the exacerbation of damage or inflammation to human bronchial epithelial cells [Figure D]. Following the combined intervention, the IH+IL-13 group exhibited further increases in the levels of the inflammatory factors TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8, which were significantly greater than those in the IL-13 group [Figure E]. The IL-13 group exhibited greater increases in inflammatory factor levels than did the IH group. qRT–PCR confirmed a significant increase in the mRNA expression of NLRP3, GSDMD, IL-1β, cleaved caspase-1, and IL-18 in the IH+IL-13 group [Figure F]. In the presence of IL-13, IH exacerbated the injury to BEAS-2B cells. It increased the expression levels of key molecules in pyroptosis, including NLRP3, GSDMD, IL-1β, cleaved caspase-1, and IL-18. Furthermore, IH promoted the production of inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, and MMP-9.
 

## HIF-1α downregulation alleviates IL-13-induced pyroptosis and inflammation under IH by inhibiting the NLRP3/GSDMD pathway in vitro
To investigate the relationship between HIF-1α and NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis, RNA interference was used to downregulate HIF-1α expression, and the expression of relevant molecules in the NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis pathway and the extent of cell damage were assessed.
Under normoxic conditions, BEAS-2B cells have low baseline expression of HIF-1α. Therefore, this study validated the knockdown efficiency of HIF-1α-siRNA under intermittent hypoxic conditions. Compared to that in the NC+IH group, HIF-1α expression in the HIF-1α-siRNA+IH group was significantly reduced, confirming the successful knockdown of Hif-1α in BEAS-2B cells [Figure A]. Compared to that in the NC+IH+IL-13 group, HIF-1α expression was significantly reduced in the HIF-1α-siRNA+IH+IL-13 group [Figure B]. Compared to the NC+IH+IL-13 group, the HIF-1α-siRNA+IH+IL-13 group exhibited significantly reduced protein expression of NLRP3, GSDMD, IL-1β, cleaved caspase-1, and IL-18, resulting in decreased pyroptosis [Figure C]. Flow cytometry indicated that knocking down HIF-1α reversed the cell damage induced by IH+IL-13 [Figure D–E]. Additionally, transfection with HIF-1α-siRNA reduced LDH levels in the HIF-1α-siRNA+IH+IL-13 group [Figure F]. IL-13 and/or IH increased the expression of inflammatory factors, including TNF-α, IL-8, IL-6, and MMP-9. Compared to the NC+IH+IL-13 group, the HIF-1α-siRNA+IH+IL-13 group exhibited significantly reduced expression of TNF-α, IL-8, IL-6, and MMP-9. However, compared to the NC+IL-13 group, the HIF-1α-siRNA+IL-13 group did not show significant differences in the expression of TNF-α, IL-8, IL-6, or MMP-9 [Figure G]. This finding suggested that HIF-1α mediates the inflammatory response in IH+IL-13-induced BEAS-2B cells but is not involved in the inflammatory response in NC+IL-13-induced BEAS-2B cells, highlighting the essential role of intermittent hypoxia in HIF-1α activation.
 

## Activation of NLRP3 reverses IL-13 plus IH-induced pyroptosis and inflammation that are alleviated by downregulation of HIF-1α in vitro
Studies have shown that nigericin upregulates NLRP3 expression, thereby promoting pyroptosis.[ ] To reverse-validate the relationship between HIF-1α and pyroptosis induced by IH plus IL-13 in BEAS-2B cells, BEAS-2B cells were treated with nigericin, and the expression of pyroptosis-related pathway proteins and inflammatory factors was subsequently assessed. After silencing HIF-1α, the expression of NLRP3 was upregulated by nigericin, the expression of the pyroptosis-related pathway proteins GSDMD, IL-1β, cleaved caspase-1 and IL-18 increased [Figure A], and the expression of the inflammatory indicators TNF-α, IL-6, MMP-9 and IL-8 increased [Figure B]; however, the expression of HIF-1α did not significantly change, confirming that NLRP3 is a downstream molecule of HIF-1α.
 

## CIH promotes pyroptosis and inflammation by activating HIF-1α/NLRP3/GSDMD signalling in a mouse model of asthma
To further validate the impact of CIH on lung inflammation in asthmatic mice, a mouse asthma model was constructed, and CIH treatment was performed. Histopathological analysis via H&E staining [Figure A], differential cell counting in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) [Figure B], and PAS staining [Figure C] were performed. H&E staining revealed that CIH led to structural damage in lung tissues, but there was no significant increase in the number of peribronchial inflammatory cells. In contrast, OVA exposure resulted in a significant increase in peribronchial inflammatory cell counts. Differential cell counts in the BALF indicated that the airway inflammation induced by OVA alone was mainly eosinophilic inflammation, and the increase in neutrophils was not significant. The airway inflammation induced by CIH and OVA was mainly caused by an increase in neutrophils. Although eosinophils were increased, the increase in neutrophils was significant [Figure D]. The airway inflammation score was significantly increased in mice in the OVA group and CIH+OVA group [Figure E]. PAS staining revealed that the influence on the number of PAS-positive goblet cells in the airway epithelium varied depending on airway size. The OVA group was compared to the vehicle (VEH) group, and except for the largest airway (perimeter 901–1200 μm), there were significant differences in the number of PAS-positive cells in the other airways. This indicates that OVA exposure led to an increase in the number of PAS-positive cells, resulting in increased mucus secretion in airways. Asthma primarily causes lung inflammation through small airway obstruction. However, regardless of airway size, CIH did not affect the number of PAS-positive cells in the airway epithelium. This finding suggested that CIH did not increase mucus secretion in the airways [Figure F]. In the context of lung inflammation induced by CIH and OVA, the levels of the inflammatory indicators TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β, and MMP-9 were significantly greater than those induced by OVA alone [Figure G]. The combination of CIH and OVA promoted neutrophilic inflammation in asthmatic mice. In the lung tissue in the CIH+OVA group, the expression of HIF-1α and pyroptosis-related signalling molecules (NLRP3, GSDMD, cleaved caspase-1 and IL-18) increased significantly [Figure H]. Fluorescence double staining of HIF-1α and NLRP3 confirmed that CIH plus OVA promoted HIF-1α and NLRP3 expression in lung tissue [Figure I]. Fluorescence double staining of GSDMD and IL-18 confirmed that CIH plus OVA promoted GSDMD and IL-18 expression in lung tissue [Figure J].
 

## LW6 alleviates CIH plus OVA-induced inflammation and pyroptosis by inhibiting the HIF-1α/NLRP3/GSDMD pathway in mice
Mice were treated with the HIF-1α inhibitor LW6, and changes in lung inflammation were subsequently assessed. The results revealed that compared to that in the OVA+Air group, the reduction in the inflammation score was not significant in the OVA+Air+LW6 group. However, compared to those in the OVA+CIH group, the inflammation scores in the OVA+CIH+LW6 group were significantly lower. Classification counting of BALF cells in mice treated with LW6 revealed that the proportions of various cell types were not significantly altered in the OVA+Air+LW6 group compared with the OVA+Air group. However, compared to the OVA+CIH group, the OVA+CIH+LW6 group exhibited a significant reduction in neutrophils [Figure A–B]. The inflammatory factors in BALF were examined, and the results confirmed that LW6 did not reverse lung inflammation induced solely by OVA. However, compared to those in the OVA+CIH group, the TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8 levels in the OVA+CIH+LW6 group were significantly lower [Figure C]. Compared to those in the OVA+CIH group, the expression of HIF-1α, NLRP3, GSDMD and IL-18 in the OVA+CIH+LW6 group was significantly lower [Figure D]. Similarly, there were no significant changes in the expression of HIF-1α or NLRP3 pathway-related molecules in the OVA+air+LW6 group compared to the OVA+air group. The expression of HIF-1α in the OVA+air group was close to the control group, and immunofluorescence double staining of HIF-1α and NLRP3 confirmed these results [Figure E]. These results indicate that HIF-1α/NLRP3 mediates neutrophil-based lung inflammation caused by OVA+CIH but does not participate in eosinophil-based lung inflammation caused by OVA alone.
 

## Discussion
This study analysed the clinical characteristics of severe asthma patients with and without OSAHS and revealed that the waist and neck circumference of the patients are related to the severity of OSAHS. Obesity is a risk factor for both asthma and OSAHS[ ] and it plays a central role in the development of OSAHS. The prevalence of obesity in nonsevere asthmatic patients is 18%, whereas in severe asthma patients, it is 39%.[ ] Specific obesity phenotypes for severe asthma have been identified in regions with high obesity rates in Europe and the United States.[ , ] The effect of obesity on severe asthma is complex.[ – ] Inflammation is a key mechanism through which obesity influences asthma, and studies have shown an increase in neutrophils in the sputum of obese patients with asthma.[ , ] Patients with OSAHS experience intermittent hypoxia during sleep, which leads to increased vagal nerve tone and can be detrimental to asthma control.[ ]
Several studies have suggested that OSAHS is a significant risk factor for severe asthma.[ – ] Epidemiological data suggest that approximately 50% of adult asthma patients have OSAHS, and the odds of having OSAHS are 2.64 times greater in asthmatic patients than in the general population.[ ] Among severe asthma patients, the prevalence of OSAHS increases with the severity of asthma.[ ] From a physiological perspective, studies have reported that asthma can impair lung function.[ , ] However, this study showed that the severity of OSAHS was not significantly associated with FEV1% pred or FEV1/FVC. While some studies have proposed that intermittent hypoxia can impair lung function and exacerbate preexisting airway hyperresponsiveness,[ ] others have indicated that intermittent hypoxia has no impact on lung function, as shown by multivariable logistic regression analysis.[ ] A meta-analysis revealed no significant difference in FEV1 decline in asthma patients with or without OSAHS.[ ] Therefore, further research with larger sample sizes is needed to determine the effects of intermittent hypoxia on lung function.
Research has shown that OSAHS can increase the levels of inflammatory cytokines and lead to an increase in neutrophils in sputum, worsening the condition of asthma patients.[ ] The results of this study align with this conclusion and showed that OSAHS affected the proportion of neutrophils in the induced sputum of severe asthma patients, while eosinophil proportions decreased.
Inflammation appears to be a major mechanism underlying the interaction between OSAHS and asthma.[ , ] The results of this study revealed that IH exacerbated the inflammatory response induced by IL-13 in BEAS-2B cells, leading to increased expression levels of inflammatory factors, including IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, and MMP-9. IL-6 is associated with neutrophilic asthma.[ ] IL-6 may lead to a poor prognosis in adult asthma patients and might stimulate the progression of severe asthma, particularly when neutrophils are the predominant cell type.[ , ] IL-8 plays a crucial role in the adhesion of neutrophils and monocytes to the vascular endothelium.[ – ] Several studies have linked elevated serum TNF-α levels to OSAHS.[ ] However, after controlling for obesity as a confounding factor, OSAHS did not lead to increased TNF-α or IL-6 levels. This finding is consistent with the findings of the present study, and isolated IH intervention did not increase the expression of TNF-α or IL-6. However, IH significantly increased the expression of TNF-α in IL-13-treated BEAS-2B cells. Elevated TNF-α levels are common in most cases of asthma.[ ]
MMPs have been widely studied in respiratory diseases,[ ] can induce neutrophil chemotaxis, and neutrophil aggregation is an essential factor in assessing the condition of critically ill patients with asthma. Stimulation by cytokines such as IL-8 and MMP-9 can induce neutrophil migration and increase the release of inflammatory factors. Elevated levels of MMP-9 can be detected in the BALF, induced sputum, and serum of asthma patients, and MMP-9 levels are greater in severe asthma patients than in mild asthma patients.[ ] It is well known that IL-13 can induce eosinophilic asthma, and IL-13 combined with IH significantly increases the levels of neutrophil-driven factors, such as IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, and MMP-9.
HIF-1α is a key mediator of hypoxia signalling, and intermittent hypoxia is the main mechanism that activates HIF-1α. Notably, OSAHS patients, especially those with severe OSAHS, exhibit a significant increase in plasma HIF-1α. Severely ill OSAHS patients receiving continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) treatment experience a reduction in plasma HIF-1α.[ ] HIF-1α not only plays a central role in hypoxia and inflammatory responses; in an asthma model, the activation of HIF-1α signalling reversed the protective effect of Runx3 on BEAS-2B cells.[ ] In a model of allergic airway inflammation, hypoxia increased airway inflammation, and mice with HIF-1α knockout exhibited reduced airway inflammation. HIF-1α likely plays a role in the pathogenesis of neutrophilic asthma.[ ]
Recent research has indicated that HIF-1α can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome under hypoxic conditions, promoting pyroptosis.[ ] The NLRP3 inflammasome primarily consists of NLRP3, caspase-1, and apoptosis-associated speck-like protein (ASC). An increase in NLRP3 may be involved in the development and progression of asthma. Airway stimuli and allergens activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, resulting in significant pulmonary inflammation and the exacerbation of asthma symptoms.[ ] Related in vitro research has validated the correlation between HIF-1α and NLRP3. Hif-1α knockdown may lead to a significant decrease in the mRNA and protein expression of pyroptosis-related factors, including caspase-1, NLRP3, GSDMD, IL-1β, and IL-18, following LPS treatment.[ ] The results of this study align with this conclusion. This study aimed to clarify the predominance of pyroptosis and its mechanisms in asthma associated with OSAHS. We recognize that detecting other forms of cell death, such as apoptosis, is essential for a comprehensive understanding of asthma-induced cell death. The observed increase in the apoptosis rate can be attributed to two factors: first, airway inflammatory mediators may activate both pyroptosis and apoptosis pathways; second, the release of pyroptotic factors can trigger apoptosis in neighboring cells. Importantly, the detection of apoptosis provides a holistic view without detracting from the focus on pyroptosis. These findings enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying asthma in the context of OSAHS, paving the way for future research into targeted therapies.
Among the various proinflammatory cytokines released during pyroptosis, IL-1β has been widely studied in neutrophilic asthma.[ ] NLRP3 may regulate the inflammatory response by modulating the recruitment of neutrophils. Studies have reported that NLRP3-mediated IL-1β release is associated with the progression of severe neutrophilic asthma, and the levels of IL-1β in sputum increase with disease severity.[ ] The production of TNF-α, IL-1β and other cytokines can induce neutrophil migration and infiltration. Moreover, the significant influx of neutrophils induced by Fas ligand is also dependent on IL-1β. The impact of IL-1β on the inflammatory response depends on the number of recruited neutrophils. Therefore, the expression level of IL-1β reflects the extent of cellular damage to some extent. The HIF-1α signalling pathway has a significant influence in IH-mediated cell damage and plays a crucial role as a key transcription factor in regulating genes, including NLRP3, in the context of the inflammatory response during asthma. These findings confirm the connection between HIF-1α/NLRP3/IL-1β and neutrophilic inflammation, which affects the proportion of neutrophils in BALF, as well as the expression of IL-8, MMP-9, and related factors [Figure ].
 However, this study has several limitations. It has not been clinically validated whether OSAHS treatment results in improvements in asthma-related inflammation. Future research will explore the impact of CIH on neutrophilic asthma/hormone-resistant asthma and its potential molecular mechanisms to identify new therapeutic targets for related diseases.
This study revealed that as the severity of OSAHS increased, patients tended to have a greater degree of obesity, the proportion of patients with partially controlled and uncontrolled asthma also increased, and there was an increase in the proportion of induced sputum neutrophils and a decrease in the proportion of eosinophils. OSAHS plus asthma leads to increased expression of NLRP3 and HIF-1α, which are correlated with the severity of OSAHS and inflammatory indicators. In vitro experiments demonstrated that IH exacerbated IL-13-induced damage in BEAS-2B cells and increased the expression of pyroptosis-related molecules through the activation of HIF-1α, resulting in an increase in the expression of inflammatory factors. In vivo, the airway inflammation induced by CIH plus OVA was primarily characterized by an increase in neutrophils.
