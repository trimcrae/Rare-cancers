## Abstract
Biliverdin IXβ reductase (BLVRB) is an NADPH-dependent enzyme previously implicated in a redox-regulated mechanism of thrombopoiesis distinct from the thrombopoietin (TPO)/c-MPL axis. Here, we apply computational modeling to inform molecule design, followed by de novo syntheses and screening of unique small molecules retaining the capacity for selective BLVRB inhibition as a novel platelet-enhancing strategy. Two distinct classes of molecules are identified, and NMR spectroscopy and co-crystallization studies confirm binding modes within the BLVRB active site and ring stacking between the nicotinamide moiety of the NADP+ cofactor. A diazabicyclo derivative displaying minimal off-target promiscuity and excellent bioavailability characteristics promotes megakaryocyte speciation in biphenotypic (erythro/megakaryocyte) cellular models and synergizes with TPO-dependent megakaryocyte formation in hematopoietic stem cells. Upon oral delivery into mice, this inhibitor expands platelet recovery in stress thrombopoietic models with no adverse effects. In this work, we identify and validate a cellular redox inhibitor retaining the potential to selectively promote megakaryocytopoiesis and enhance stress-associated platelet formation in vivo distinct from TPO receptor agonists.
Subject terms: Target validation, Medicinal chemistry, X-ray crystallography, Computational biophysics

The heme degradation enzyme biliverdin IXβ reductase (BLVRB) is implicated in a redox-regulated thrombopoietic pathway. Here, the authors validate BLVRB as a cellular target by generating an orally-available small molecule inhibitor that retains platelet-enhancing functions that are mechanistically distinct from thrombopoietin receptor-targeted mimetics.

## Introduction
Megakaryocytopoiesis maintains the normal circulating pool of platelets, cells critical to normal hemostasis, pathological thrombosis, and adaptive immunological responses , . Platelet generation regulated by the thrombopoietin (TPO)/c-MPL axis drives megakaryocyte (MK) commitment from common bipotent megakaryocyte-erythrocyte progenitors , although divergent MK-biased genetic pathways differentially regulate thrombopoietic responses in diverse settings including basal, age- or stress-regulated stimuli , . Similarly, whereas MKs are reduced in mpl-deficient mice, animals still produce MKs and platelets, implying that hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) maintain the capacity for lineage fate in the absence of c-MPL .
Previous studies in human thrombocytosis cohorts have identified biliverdin IXβ reductase (BLVRB) redox function in a physiologically relevant thrombopoietic pathway of hematopoietic lineage fate , presumably in a ROS (reactive oxygen species)-regulated mechanism of erythroid/megakaryocyte (E/Meg) lineage commitment . Erythrocytes also express BLVRB as a non-physiological methemoglobin reductase , although it functions as the target redox coupler in cytochrome b5 reductase-deficient patients with methemoglobinemia. While murine Blvrb is dispensable for organ and cellular development, Blvrb-deficient mice display exaggerated thrombopoietic responses with megakaryocytic hyperplasia in stress hematopoiesis , . BLVRB and its non-redundant homolog BLVRA (biliverdin IXα reductase) function downstream of heme oxygenases in NAD(P)H dependent catabolic processes coupled to biliverdin (BV) reduction and cellular antioxidant functions (Fig.  ) , . BLVRA retains specificity for the predominant BV IXα in adults , while BLVRB is promiscuous, catalyzing the reduction of non-IXα BVs (IXβ, IXγ, IXδ) – , flavins , pyrroloquinoline quinones , and ferric ion , along with putative function(s) as a nitric oxide intermediary to downstream effector targets . BLVRB catalysis likely occurs via a compulsory two-step mechanism initiated by NAD(P)H binding, followed by protonation of the pyrrolic nitrogen and hydride transfer from the nicotinamide for bilirubin formation; bulk solvent is the presumed proton source , .
 Here, we apply computational and medicinal chemistry to de novo synthesize and characterize novel small molecules targeting BLVRB redox activity. A selective diazabicyclic inhibitor retaining excellent oral bioavailability and pharmacokinetic parameters displays megakaryocyte-promoting activities in vitro and exaggerated platelet recovery in murine models of stress thrombocytosis. BLVRB’s dispensable function, coupled with its unique mechanism of action, suggested that BLVRB redox inhibitors could be developed as novel cellular targets to promote thrombopoiesis. Previous studies have identified acridine/xanthene-based , or FDA-repositioned compounds that retain BLVRB inhibitory functions. Cell-based assays confirm dose-dependent redox inhibition in BLVRB-expressing cell lines for a subset of compounds , although none have been evaluated for lineage-promoting megakaryocytic potential. In principle, lead-optimized BLVRB inhibitor(s) provide alternative platelet-enhancing strategies in thrombocytopenic cohorts distinct from current TPO receptor agonist (TPO-RA) mimetics .

## Results

## Molecular modeling
BLVRB contains a single dinucleotide-binding domain (Rossman fold) that accommodates both NAD(P)H (or NADH) and substrate(s) within the verdin/flavin binding pocket , with no evidence to date for structural or preferred partitioning for substrate(s) or inhibitors , , . We reviewed 12 BLVRB PDB (Protein Data Bank) entries, each complexed with NAD(P)H and a specific substrate/inhibitor (1HE4, 1HE5, 5OOG, 5OOH, 7ER7, 7ER8, 7ER9, 7ERA, 7ERB, 7ERC, 7ERD, 7ERE), and applied Glide XP precision to replicate the binding modes of the co-crystallized ligands retaining an RMSD (root-mean-squared) of 2 Å or less (Fig.  ). After preparing the protein targets , we established BLVRB/NADPH/Olsalazine (7ERA, 1.35 Ǻ), and BLVRB/NADPH/Deferasirox (7ERC 1.5 Å) trimolecular complexes as optimal PDB structures for our docking investigations. Both structures demonstrated superior performance in accommodating additional ligands, including febuxostat, ataluren, and deferasirox . Notably, 7ERA and 7ERC displayed diverse side chain orientations within the binding site, particularly at Arg170 and Arg174, which are crucial for proper substrate binding. To account for the distinct conformations of Arg170 and Arg174, we employed separate docking grids for 7ERA and 7ERC, allowing for accurate accommodation of the para- and meta-substituted benzoic rings in the ligands. Each compound was evaluated in both grids, and we selected the configuration that maximized protein-ligand interactions and Glide score, retaining the most favorable pose for further analysis.
A combination of virtual screening analyses was applied to compare ligand similarity to a high-affinity reference molecule (Ataluren ), and protein-ligand interactions with the 7ERA complex are displayed (Fig.  ). Notably, Ataluren forms hydrogen bonds with His153 and Arg174, a salt bridge with Arg174, and a π-stacking interaction with Phe113; previous characterization of an Arg174Ala mutant demonstrated no effect on cofactor KMNADPH or kcat activities, although the KMFMN could not be reliably characterized , . Following the same methodology, we also applied scaffold hopping and in silico analyses using PharmScreen to reinforce the structural analysis. PharmScreen allows for comparative hydrophobic profiles of molecular pairs, facilitating the comparison of molecules in different chemical series. We investigated enzyme-ligand interactions for derivatized chemical series distinguished by binding interactions to Arg174 (6-thia-1,3-diazabicyclo analogs modeled on Ataluren, Series 1), or to Arg170 (pyrazolopyrimidinones modeled on NSC379651, Series 2); NSC379651 displays a comparable BLVRB dissociation constant (KD~2.49 μM) as Ataluren (KD~3.85 μM), with differences in IC50 using distinct substrates (Table  , Fig.  , e and Supplementary Fig.  ). The Arg170 preference presumably is attributed to the carboxylic para orientation compared to the Ataluren meta orientation, and docking in two modes allowed for accurate sampling of group positioning in the context of different polar group orientations.
 

## Medicinal chemistry and structure-activity relationships
De novo syntheses of small molecule (Series 1 and Series 2) compounds were followed by an orthogonal screen that incorporated two distinct BLVRB substrates (FMN and DCPIP) , followed by 8-point IC50 curves for compounds displaying at least 50% BLVRB inhibitory activity at <250 nM (Fig.  ). Structure-activity relationships (SARS) of N = 58 de novo Series 1 derivatives focused on both phenyl rings (Fig.  ), and we determined that the para position is preferred for the carboxylic acid on the left ring (BCT1020 vs BCT1030, Table  ). Selection of this structural isomer, followed by SAR on the right phenyl ring, established that the best activity was obtained when substituents were introduced in the ortho position, with Cl being the best substituent (BCT1028). A simple phenyl ring resulted in a completely inactive compound (BCT1038), and introducing substituents in meta or para positions markedly decreased activity (BCT1035). Finally, replacement of the 2-chloro benzene with heterocyclic rings like thiophene, furane (BCT1046), or imidazole resulted in the loss of activity. We also analyzed the bicyclic ring by introducing small substituents and replacing the sulfur with oxygen (i.e., BCT4003) or CH2CH2, resulting in activity loss for all cases.
 Starting from the original “hit” NSC379651, we synthesized pyrazolopyrimidinone compounds (see Table  for critical derivatives). The introduction of a carboxylic group on the left side of the molecule significantly improves activity (BCT2009), while SAR focused on the right side of the bicyclic group established that a heterocyclic furan ring can be replaced with a phenyl ring (Fig.  , BCT2029 vs. BCT2054). For the furan series, moving the carboxylic acid from para to meta enhances BLVRB inhibitory activity (BCT2051 vs. BCT2054). Similarly, introducing a methyl group into the bicyclic ring significantly improves the potency of the class. The introduction of a small alkyl group in the pyrazolopyrimidinone ring seems to be of general usefulness. Surprisingly, replacement of the methyl group with ethyl yielded one of the most potent compounds in this series (BCT2029), and replacement of the methyl group with a cyclopropyl also led to a very potent derivative (BCT2066). Notably, the position of the carboxylic acid on the left aromatic ring seems to be dependent on the overall structure of the molecule, and no general rule can be applied; the presence of this group, however, is essential as its removal delivered an inactive compound. Replacement of the carboxylic group with a bioisostere, like the tetrazole group, led to a significant decrease in potency (BCT2045). Introducing small substituents in the ortho position of the right phenyl ring did not have a major effect on activity. Importantly, two Series 1 and four Series 2 inhibitors assayed for BLVRA/BV IXα inhibition (at 10 μM compound) failed to affect BLVRA activity, confirming selectivity for BLVRB inhibition (Table  ).

## BLVRB/small molecule crystallization studies
We validated critical binding interactions of de novo compounds, focusing on BCT1028 and BCT2029. Ternary crystal structures of BLVRB/NADP+/BCT1028 and BLVRB/NADP+/BCT2029 were determined at 1.64 Ǻ and 1.58 Ǻ, respectively. Overall structures are almost identical to ligand-free BLVRB (PDB entry 1HDO) , with an RMSD of 0.66 Ǻ and 0.67 Ǻ (204 aligned C-alpha atoms). Electron densities were clear in the molecular replacement maps, and both ligands localized unambiguously to the BLVRB active site, displaying comparable conformations of the BLVRB side chains (Fig.  d, e). Ring stacking between the nicotinamide moiety of the NADP+ cofactor and both small molecules was evident (comparable to FMN) , and hydrophobic interactions between BLVRB and both compounds were observed for residues Leu81, Phe113, and Leu125. Although both compounds are contiguous to the redox-substituted Ser111 residue , direct interactions were not seen and are consistent with a thermodynamically stabilizing Ser111 role for maintaining the BLVRB conformation . Unlike FMN and lumichrome , BCT1028 and BCT2029 establish several water-mediated polar interactions. In each structure, the benzoic acid carboxylate of both compounds formed a water-mediated H-bond with the N1 nitrogen of the Trp116 indole. Both compounds displayed H-bonding and salt-bridging with Arg174, although BCT1028 displayed an additional interaction with one of the two Arg170 side chain conformers that were computationally modeled in the BLVRB/NADP+/BCT1028 structure. A primary difference between the compounds is that the para-substituted benzoic acid on the BCT1028 thiazole scaffold extends further than its meta-substituted analog in the BCT2029 pyrimidinone scaffold to coordinate the participating Arg170 side chain conformer via a water molecule (Fig.  ). On the other side of the bicyclic ring, the chloro group in the thiazole scaffold is bulky and forces the benzyl ring out of the pocket.
We excluded other conformational changes using Glide XP docking , which successfully reproduced the crystallized poses of BCT1028 and BCT2029, confirming a precise recognition mode for both compounds (Supplementary Fig.  ). To evaluate the binding site dynamics, we performed MD simulations of BCT2029 in complex with cofactor/BLVRB, demonstrating low RMSD values (approximating 2.2 Å) for the binding site residues, in support of its structural stability. The RMSF (root-mean-squared fluctuation) analysis confirmed the largest fluctuations at Trp116, Arg124, and Arg170 (RMSF > 1.5 Å), which are solvent-exposed residues located in the loop regions. Interestingly, although Arg174 adopted multiple conformations in the crystallographic structures, its RMSF value remains relatively low due to the stabilizing interaction with the carboxyl group of the ligand (Supplementary Fig.  ).

## Characterization of binding modes
NMR titrations using apo and holo BLVRB confirmed active site engagement in solution, consistent with crystallographic structural features. All CSPs (chemical shift perturbations) for BCT1028, BCT2029 (and BCT2066) mapped onto the BLVRB active site for both apo- and holo BLVRB (Fig.  for BCT1028, and Supplementary Fig.  for all inhibitors). Upper limits of the binding isotherms with estimated dissociation constants could only be fit for residues undergoing fast exchange (i.e., apo BLVRB), which were extracted and determined to be 0.5 ± 0.4 µM for BCT1028, consistent with the IC50 determinations (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Since all CSPs for holo BLVRB were on the slow NMR timescale, it was not possible to obtain experimental binding isotherms to estimate binding affinities. Instead, affinity was probed by a displacement experiment using the natural bilirubin (BR) isomer, which binds holo BLVRB in the high nanomolar range . For BCT2066, complete displacement of BR from the substrate binding site was observed, further establishing that inhibitors target the identical substrate binding site (Fig.  ). Since both FAD and FMN bind BLVRB with considerably weaker binding affinities than the BR byproduct , , this further indicates that novel inhibitors will displace these substrates at considerably lower concentrations.

## Metabolic characteristics of a prototype lead BLVRB small molecule inhibitor
The diazabicyclo class displayed generally more favorable ADME (absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion) profiles than pyrazolopyrimidinones (Supplementary Table  ). Cell permeability assays of four Series 1 compounds (including BCT1028) in MDCK-MDR1 cells demonstrated high permeability and low efflux (indicating a poor P-glycoprotein substrate) and predicted to have high oral bioavailability. These characteristics contrasted with those of pyrazolopyrimidinone derivatives (BCT2029, BCT2066, BCT2045), which demonstrated poor permeability properties predicted to result in generally limited oral bioavailability. Metabolic stability using murine and human microsomes further highlighted differences, demonstrating stability and moderate clearance of diazabicyclo derivatives (including BCT1028). Favorable in vitro metabolic profiles of BCT1028 were confirmed by pharmacokinetic (PK) studies and sharply contrasted with PK studies of Series 2 compounds (BCT2029 and BCT2045), which demonstrated poor oral availability and rapid IV clearance (T1/2 < 1 h) (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Finally, we excluded BCT1028 off-target effects using a 44-member pharmacologic in vitro target subset (Fig.  and Supplementary Table  ) . A single-point (10 μM) screen demonstrated <5% promiscuity (off-target inhibition >50%), restricted to the single pharmacologic target (δ opioid receptor), which displayed ~82% inhibition. Two additional targets demonstrated modest 47% (cholecystokinin A receptor) and 38% (5-hydroxytryptamine receptor 1A) inhibition. Subsequent dose-response study against the δ opioid receptor revealed an IC50 of ~5 μM, data collectively establishing BCT1028 as a low-promiscuity, highly-selective small molecule inhibitor not predicted to have off-target side effects .
 

## Megakaryocyte-promoting effects of BLVRB inhibition in E/Meg lineage cell models
Transcriptomic profiles of heme degradation genes from the NCI60 gene set , identified K562 (BCR-ABL1+ chronic myelogenous leukemia) cells among the four most abundant BLVRB-expressing lines (Fig.  ). Within the 6-member hematopoietic subset (inset, Fig.  ), BLVRB expression was greater than that of BLVRA and displayed minimal to absent expression in lymphoid (CCRF and MOLT4)-derived leukemias (log2 RPKM < 0.1). K562 (and JAK2V617F human erythroleukemia cells, HEL ) display bilineage potential in models of E/Meg differentiation , ; consistent with RNA expression, BLVRB was readily immunodetected in K562 and HEL, but not MOLT4 cells. Similarly, K562 and HEL cells displayed dose-dependent DCPIP reductive (DR) capacity compared to BLVRB-negative MOLT4 cells, with the greatest BLVRB expression and activity observed in K562 cells (Fig.  , b).
 We compared DCPIP activity of BCT1028 (Series 1), BCT2066 (Series 2) and ataluren (as reference compound) using K562 lysates, and confirmed near equipotent inhibition at high (40 μM) concentrations (Supplementary Fig.  ). At a dose-attenuated concentration of 10 μM, the most potent inhibition was evident using BCT1028 (p-value 3 × 10−4), followed by BCT2066 (p-value 9 × 10−4, Fig.  ); inhibition by ataluren was modest and approached statistical significance (p-value 0.052). BCT1028 activity in complex lysates was recapitulated in intact cells and resulted in statistically significant BLVRB inhibition at concentrations ≥20 μM (Fig.  ), establishing that the small molecule retained the capacity for cellular uptake and enzymatic inhibition in vitro. Hematopoietic effects of BLVRB inhibition (also refer to CD34+ experiments, vide infra) were initially studied by assessing BCT1028-induced morphological changes in K562 cells, which demonstrated features typical of megakaryocytic differentiation, i.e., increased size and cytoplasmic content (Fig.  , f). These characteristics are comparable to those using phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA), which is known to induce maximal megakaryocyte features – . K562 and HEL cells exhibit an erythroid-biased phenotype with >90% cell-surface positivity for CD71+ (transferrin receptor) and CD235+ (glycophorin A, GlyA), although they display dichotomous expression of megakaryocyte-specific CD61 (β3 integrin). Baseline CD61+ is <5% in K562 cells, and flow cytometry (and immunoblots using an orthogonal marker CD41, αIIB) confirmed that the BCT1028-inhibited phenotype is megakaryocyte-selective, displaying both time- and dose-dependent appearance of CD61 (Fig.  , h). CD61 acquisition was near-maximal by 48 h, a time point when >90% of cells were CD61+. CD61-positivity developed concomitantly with a progressive ~6-fold increase in cell-surface CD61 abundance, evident at 48 and 72 h and asymptotically exceeding the MK-promoting effect of PMA. The BCT1028 EC50 (for CD61-positivity) variably ranged (by time point) from 13.4 to 25.9 μM (Fig.  ). MK-promoting BCT1028 effects contrasted to those on erythroid speciation, which demonstrated variable loss of both CD71 and CD235a, most evident at time points beyond 24 h and at BCT1028 concentrations ~50 μM (Supplementary Fig.  ). These results paralleled those of PMA which interferes with terminal erythroid differentiation and hemoglobinization , . Nearly identical patterns of E/Meg lineage speciation were evident using HEL cells as a confirmatory biphenotypic model system. Despite higher (~60%) basal expression of MK markers CD61 and CD41 (αIIB), BCT1028-induced comparable dose-dependent increases in both CD61 and CD41 (% positivity and cell-surface abundance, Supplementary Fig.  ), and concomitant ~5-fold CD61 induction by immunoblot (maximal at 50 μM); DNA cell cycle analyses using both HEL and K562 cells did not identify an increase in polyploid (>4 N) cells, in contrast to PMA (Supplementary Fig.  ). In both E/Meg models, immunoblots confirmed that BLVRB inhibition did not cause reciprocal BLVRA induction, and generally stable BLVRB expression (modest BLVRB loss was evident in HEL cells at concentrations ~20 μM, Supplementary Fig.  ).

## BLVRB-inhibited redox activity provides the ROS signal for megakaryocyte differentiation
We exploited the intrinsic fluorescent properties of BCT1028 (λEx/Em 350/465) to precisely correlate intracellular compound accumulation with ROS accumulation as a signal for megakaryocyte differentiation and CD61 acquisition , , . BCT1028 displayed time- and dose-dependent uptake in K562 (and HEL, Supplementary Fig.  ) cells, and its intracellular accumulation phenotypically co-segregated with CD61+ expression at 24, 48, and 72 h (Fig.  , j). Similarly, intracellular BCT1028 accumulation caused dose-dependent ROS accumulation in K562 cells, with no comparable increase in MOLT4 cells (Fig.  , l). ROS accumulation was near-maximal at 20 μM BCT1028, a concentration that caused statistically significant DCPIP inhibition (Fig.  ).

## Comparative and thrombopoietin-synergizing effects in hematopoietic stem cells in vitro
We compared megakaryocyte-promoting effects of BCT1028, BCT2066, and ataluren in HSCs (CD34+) differentiated in TPO-directed suspension cultures, and demonstrated statistically-enhanced expansion of CD41a+ and CD61+ megakaryocytes when supplemented with 10 μM BCT1028 at all differentiation time points (Days 3, 7, 10, Fig.  ). BCT2066 displayed less consistent MK-promoting effects, and ataluren had no effect. BCT1028 in TPO-deficient media insufficiently promoted megakaryocytopoiesis (Supplementary Fig.  ), data contextually placing BLVRB-dependent synergisms downstream of the c-MPL/TPO axis. Flow cytometry designed to phenotypically delineate BCT1028+ and BCT1028− MK developmental characteristics demonstrated statistically-enhanced CD41a and CD61 cell-surface intensity and cellular complexity (side scatter) in BCT1028+ populations (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ), consistent with a developmentally advanced stage of megakaryocyte differentiation . In contrast to TPO-directed cultures, we saw no differences in multipotential progenitor cultures supplemented with BCT1028, establishing that single-dose priming insufficiently promoted colony expansion or lineage commitment (Supplementary Fig.  ). These collective results highlighted the unique characteristics of BCT1028 as a lead inhibitory compound, and established a requisite and cooperative BLVRB effect with the c-MPL/TPO axis to promote megakaryocyte development in vitro.
 

## Bioavailable BCT1028 enhances platelet recovery during stress thrombopoiesis in vivo
Effects of BCT1028-dependent Blvrb inhibition were subsequently established in murine models of quiescent and stress hematopoietic recovery (Fig.  ). Cohorts were either pre-treated (or not) with 5-fluorouracil (5FU, 150 mg/kg) as stressor or sterile saline (control) by intraperitoneal injection , , followed by a 21-day course of BCT1028 (100 mg/kg in polyethylene glycol (PEG) 400) or control (PBS/PEG 400) by oral gavage. Serial hemograms identified no statistically significant differences in blood counts in non-5FU treated mice (Cohort 2) irrespective of BCT1028 exposure (Supplementary Fig.  ), although BCT1028-treated mice post-5FU displayed statistically significant increases in peak (Day 10/11) platelet recovery (Fig.  ). Plasma TPO levels corresponding to peak platelet recovery (Day 10) were minimally increased in BCT1028-treated cohorts compared to PBS/PEG controls (1.4 fold, p-value not statistically significant), excluding hepatocyte-derived TPO as a predominant driver in the thrombocytotic response (Supplementary Fig.  ). This pattern of exaggerated thrombocytosis at peak platelet recovery is nearly identical to that of Blvrb-deficient mice , establishing phenotypic recapitulation using a small molecule Blvrb inhibitor. Persistently exaggerated differences on Day 17 were evident, although not statistically significant (p = 0.1). Exaggerated platelet recovery in BCT1028-treated cohorts was associated with statistically greater reticulated (newly formed) platelets at all time points post-platelet nadir (Day 3), most consistent with enhanced megakaryocytopoiesis or proplatelet formation as the platelet-enhancing mechanism. Indeed, the platelet mass index (PMI, as an optimal parameter of hemostatic coverage during thrombopoietic recovery) was considerably enhanced in BCT1028-treated mice compared to PBS/PEG control cohorts (time-calculated PMI AUC 214.8 ± 35.4 vs. 137.1 ± 38.0, p < 0.0001), further highlighting the predicted improvement in hemostasis post-chemotherapeutic stress (Fig.  ). Results evident in platelet recovery were distinct from those affecting erythropoiesis (Fig.  ). Although red blood count (RBC) nadirs (Day 10) were exaggerated in BCT1028-treated mice, differences were not significant, and RBC counts were fully recovered by Day 20; the exaggerated anemia was associated with rebound reticulocytosis at Day 17, approaching statistical significance (p = 0.08). No differences were seen in post-5FU leukocyte recovery (Supplementary Fig.  ). Finally, hematopoietic (platelet) differences were not explained by differential clearance/accumulation of BCT1028 in stress recovery models since nadir plasma concentrations were comparable to those of non-5FU exposed controls (Fig.  ).
Cytotoxic recovery of 5FU-induced recycling stem cells results in sequential megakaryocyte to erythroid repopulation . We examined hematopoietic organs (bone marrow, spleen) to better dissect temporal E/Meg lineage reconstitution at peak platelet recovery (Day 10/11). Bone marrow cellularity in BCT1028 and control mice was normal and fully reconstituted (>90% cellularity) at Day 10 (and Day 11) with no clear differences in trilinear hematopoietic repopulation (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Lineage-specific Q-PCR of erythroid (HBB), myeloid (CD45/PTPRC), or MK (Gp1ba, CD61 (Itgb3), Pf4) genetic markers demonstrated no differences in Hbb expression, although BCT1028-treated mice displayed evidence for enhanced myeloid (p < 0.05)- and megakaryocyte-biased repopulation (Fig.  ). All MK markers were greater in BCT1028-treated mice, statistically significant for Pf4. Comparative splenic analyses demonstrated transient Day 10 hyposplenic repopulation (based on normalized organ weight) in BCT1028-treated mice (Fig.  ), although fully reversible by Day 11 and consistent with the rebound reticulocytosis (Fig.  , supra vide). Transient hyposplenia was explained by relative loss of red pulp constituents (erythrocytes, macrophages) with general preservation of the lymphoid-predominant white pulp (Fig.  ). Similar to the results in bone marrow, repopulating BCT1028-treated spleens (Day 10) demonstrated selectively upregulated expression of megakaryocyte markers CD61 and PF4 (Fig.  ). A concomitant increase in splenic megakaryocytes was demonstrated by immunohistochemical stain using α-granule von Willebrand factor (vWF) as the MK-specific lineage marker (vWF+ MK, p-value 0.0005; Supplementary Fig.  ), excluding the possibility that the thrombocytotic response induced by BCT1028 was due to platelet accumulation and destruction in the spleen. These collective data confirm selective and divergent effects on E/Meg lineage speciation in Blvrb-inhibited mice, with megakaryocyte-biased expansion during stress hematopoietic recovery .

## Discussion
We applied computational modeling and de novo chemical syntheses to generate novel classes of small molecules with drug-like properties that selectively inhibit BLVRB redox activity. A lead diazabicyclo derivative compound (BCT1028) retaining excellent metabolic and oral bioavailability profiles displayed megakaryocyte-promoting characteristics in vitro and phenocopied the exaggerated stress thrombopoietic response previously identified in Blvrb−/− mice , . Similar to studies in Blvrb−/− mice, prolonged (21-day) exposure of BCT1028 displayed no effect on peripheral blood counts during basal hematopoiesis; indeed, Blvrb−/− mice followed for nearly two years displayed no organ pathology, confirming a dispensable role in physiological development. The complementary and consistent phenotypes evident in Blvrb−/− and Blvrb-inhibited mice highlight the rigor and reproducibility of our conclusions, while also providing target validation and proof-of-principle studies for a lead optimization program focusing on platelet recovery. Our data also establish the selectivity of BLVRB inhibitors, which displayed no BLVRA cross-inhibitory or off-target effects using a targeted pharmacologic subset .
Both classes of BLVRB inhibitors identified in our study bind the BLVRB active site. Compound classes display comparable conformations of the BLVRB side chains with ring stacking between the nicotinamide moiety of the NADP+ cofactor, hydrophobic interactions with His153, and H-bonding and salt-bridging with Arg174, although BCT1028 displayed an additional interaction with Arg170. The critical roles of Arg174 and Arg170 were predicted by computational modeling, and the novel scaffolds with structurally-confirmed binding modes validate key structural determinants of ligand binding. Interestingly, Series 1 compound BCT1028 displayed consistently enhanced MK-promoting effects compared to BCT2066 in CD34+ differentiation studies, with no MK-promoting effects of ataluren. Ataluren was originally developed as a ribosomal “read-through” drug to treat muscular dystrophy, with no clear identification of its biological target . Structural and functional data confirm ataluren binding and inhibition of BLVRB, although hematopoietic effects are distinct from those of BCT1028 using CD34+ HSCs in vitro; a priori, we would not anticipate ataluren-mediated platelet-enhancing effects comparable to those seen in vivo using BCT1028. Finally, whether or not subsequent BCT1028 modifications predicated on our initial structural studies can enhance inhibitor potency (or phenotypic responses) remains an ongoing development area.
Delayed stress erythropoietic recovery was evident in BCT1028-treated mice, which displayed exaggerated (not statistically significant) red cell nadirs and delayed splenic erythroid repopulation; both these phenotypes were transient and nearly identical to the erythroid stress responses in Blvrb−/− mice , . The erythroid effect(s) may not be a direct consequence of MEP (megakaryocyte/erythroid progenitor) partitioning since Blvrb-null RBCs display a discrete stress-associated lipid peroxidation defect that may explain BLVRB-associated cytoprotective loss . Importantly, no long-term hematopoietic effects were seen in BCT1028-treated mice, which maintained normal hemograms beyond three months of follow-up. While we continue to investigate the mechanism(s) of divergent E/Meg speciation, our current data do not suggest limiting effects on erythropoiesis and/or recovery. Finally, comparable reciprocal bilineage phenotypes (progressive anemia with exaggerated platelet counts) have previously been identified in human thrombocytosis cohorts sub-stratified by a redox-defective BLVRB mutation , further validating the relevance of our studies to human hematopoietic responses .
Divergent effects on megakaryocytopoiesis and erythropoiesis position BLVRB at the interface of a bifurcating genetic network regulating the megakaryocyte/erythroid transition, apparently restricted to hematopoietic stress responses. Indeed, Blvrb is a dominant erythroid transition gene in murine single-cell RNA hematopoietic studies , , and displays reciprocal expression with megakaryocyte platelet factor 4 (Pf4). In two distinct biphenotypic model systems of erythro/megakaryocyte fate (HEL and K562 cells), BLVRB inhibition resulted in MK-selective differentiation with variable effects on erythroid features. BCT1028 cellular uptake and inhibition in K562 and HEL cells resulted in the acquisition of morphological and cell-surface megakaryocyte features, and (in K562 cells) directly correlated with enhanced CD61+ expression and ROS accumulation as the putative signal for MK-enhancing effects . Molecular mechanism(s) regulating PMA-induced MK differentiation in K562 cells have identified both ERK/MAPK activation , and ROS accumulation as MK-inducing signals . Interestingly, the MAPK pathway may be restricted to the endomitotic repertoire , and provides a putative explanation for the divergent cell cycle differences between BCT1028 and PMA in both E/Meg cellular models.
Previous data have suggested that developmentally-regulated ROS signal(s) serve to prime hematopoietic progenitors for differentiation , and that p22hox-dependent NADPH oxidase activity is required for megakaryocyte differentiation via ROS accumulation . We have not specifically studied BLVRB inhibitory effects on MEPs, although E/Meg bilineage CD34+ cultures demonstrated no differences in MK partitioning , most consistent with a model whereby temporally-restricted BLVRB redox activity (and ROS accumulation) provide the developmentally-regulated signal for exaggerated post-commitment megakaryocytopoiesis during stress hematopoiesis ; a synergistic ROS-promoting effect on proplatelet formation during late-stage megakaryocyte development cannot be excluded . Interestingly, recent data have implicated BLVRB as an S-nitrosylated intermediary critical for insulin receptor signaling . Whether a comparable mechanism of BLVRB-dependent c-MPL modulating activity exists in the regulation of thrombopoiesis (stress or basal) remains to be established; based on current redox inhibitory characteristics and binding modes, our a priori ligand-binding model would not predict any effects on BLVRB S-nitrosylation.
Both TPO and TPO-RA have been developed as interventions for thrombocytopenia, although ongoing concerns remain for enhanced thromboembolic risk and moderate/severe bone marrow reticulin fibrosis in select cohorts . Our in vitro and in vivo data suggest that BLVRB inhibition synergizes with the TPO/c-MPL cell signaling cascade; (1) BCT1028 enhances MK development using CD34+ TPO-dependent models, and (2) enhances thrombopoiesis in a stress model of hematopoietic platelet recovery; indeed, the in vivo platelet mass index which incorporates the mean platelet volume as an ancillary parameter of platelet function predicts optimal hemostatic coverage using a selective BLVRB inhibitor. The TPO-RAs are currently approved for various thrombocytopenic disorders, with the most extensive experience in immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) cohorts, where suboptimal responses or adverse events may lead to drug discontinuation in select patient cohorts (including those previously splenectomized) , . Previous studies using immune-mediated platelet recovery models in Blvrb−/− mice failed to recapitulate the exaggerated post-stress thrombocytotic response . Whether concomitant BLVRB inhibition with its unique mechanism of action would enhance responses and/or provide TPO-RA dose-sparing efficacy in ITP remains to be established.
In contrast to extensive experience in ITP, chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia (CIT) remains an area of critical need for oncologic development since treatment-related thrombocytopenia results in dose-modifications or treatment discontinuation . TPO-RAs generally increase the pre-treatment and nadir platelet counts in patients with CIT (reviewed ), although routine clinical adoption awaits evidence establishing relative dose intensity maintenance, reduction of platelet transfusions and bleeding, and possibly improved survival , . Our initial in vivo model focused on 5FU as chemotherapeutic stressor to comprehensively define Blvrb-regulated effects on recycling stem cells and lineage recovery during development; although this model provides well-established insights into repopulation dynamics of megakaryocyte and erythroid precursors , it insufficiently recapitulates hemostatically-significant thrombocytopenia predicted to cause bleeding (i.e. platelet counts <50,000/μL). Predicated on our promising platelet recovery data, expanded efficacy studies using lead-optimized BLVRB inhibitors in thrombocytopenic models may provide (TPO-RA) alternative or complementary strategies to ameliorate CIT (or congenital thrombocytopenias ) in appropriately selected cohorts.

## Computational modeling
An integrated computational pipeline for in silico design of BLVRB inhibitors was implemented for (1) structure-based screen to assess compound compatibility within the binding pocket, followed by (2) ligand-based screening for comparative analysis of hydrophobicity profiles.

## Receptor-based virtual screening (Glide)
BLVRB (Uniprot ID P30043) PDB trimolecular structures (N = 12) each complexed with NAD(P)H and a specific inhibitor (1HE4, 1HE5, 5OOG, 5OOH, 7ER7, 7ER8, 7ER9, 7ERA, 7ERB, 7ERC, 7ERD, and 7ERE) were evaluated by Glide XP precision to identify structures that accurately replicated the binding modes of the co-crystallized ligands with an RMSD of 2 Å or less. From this analysis, two protein structures were selected for docking predicated on their differences in the side chain positions of Arg170 and Arg174. Both protein targets were prepared with the Protein Preparation Wizard in Maestro . Conserved water molecules were retained, and the cofactor NAD(P)H was left intact. The docking grid was defined by placing the box at the ligands’ original locations (Olsalazine for 7ERA and Deferasirox for 7ERC) using default Glide grid preparation parameters. LigPrep (Schrodinger) was used to generate a single, low-energy pose for each ligand, optimized using the OPLS_2005 force field by Epik . During docking, Glide automatically handled the conformational sampling, generating multiple conformers for each ligand to ensure accurate sampling of possible binding modes. The final docking results were evaluated based on the Glide score and protein-ligand interactions for both protein grids.

## Ligand-based virtual screening (Pharmscreen)
We employed PharmScreen , a tool designed to compare ligands based on their 3D hydrophobicity similarities. PharmScreen utilizes MST (Miertus-Scrocco-Tomasi)-derived hydrophobicity descriptors (Hyphar) to assess the similarity between a ligand and a given compound , . These descriptors have been shown to be effective for hit identification & hit-to-lead strategies . Each ligand is scored with a value ranging from 0 to 1, where a score of 1 indicates a perfect overlap in hydrophobic similarity with the reference ligand, while 0 denotes no similarity. To account for the flexibility of the ligands, up to 100 conformers were generated for each compound using the PharmScreen internal ligand preparation protocol . Lastly, each conformer is aligned to the reference structure using the bioactive conformation , generating a sorted list of compounds based on their hydrophobicity profiles.

## Molecular dynamics simulation
To extend beyond the static nature of the docking calculations, we performed all-atom Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations of a select inhibitor (BCT2029) in complex with the cofactor-bound BLVRB protein using GROMACS (version 2022.5) . The system was constructed with CHARMM-GUI starting from the crystal structure, with the protonation states of both the protein and the ligand assigned at pH 7.0 using PROPKA 3 and LigPrep, respectively. The protein was capped with a neutral acetyl group at the N-terminus and a neutral methylamide group at the C-terminus. The system was then solvated with water molecules and neutralized by adding counterions up to a concentration of 0.15 M NaCl. The CHARMM36m force field was used to parametrize the system . The final protein-ligand system was energy minimized and equilibrated at 300 K under an NVT (canonical) ensemble. A cutoff of 1.2 nm was applied for van der Waals and Coulomb interactions. To avoid artefactual rearrangement of the ligand during thermalization, equilibration was performed imposing positional restraints on the protein backbone and the ligand. They were slowly eliminated during the first 80 ns of MD production to allow a progressive relaxation of the protein-ligand complex. Then, a single distance restraint was applied from the ethyl chain of the ligand to the Cα to maintain the hydrophobic cluster formed with Phe113, Leu125, and Val128 in a restrained MD simulation under the NPT ensemble (400 ns). This was motivated by the aim to select representative frames at 200, 250, 300, 350, and 400 ns, which were finally taken as starting points to run five independent unrestrained MD replicates (150 ns each). These timescales are sufficient to capture the dynamics of the binding site residues' side chains. Root-mean-square deviation and root-mean-square fluctuation values were computed using MDAnalysis to assess the stability of the binding site residues over the course of the simulations and visualized using VMD. All input files, along with the scripts used for these analyses, are provided (Supplementary Materials and Supplementary Table  for simulation setup parameters).

## Biochemical assays
Recombinant BLVRB used for enzymatic studies was expressed and purified as a glutathione S-transferase (GST) fusion protein in BL21(DE3) cells as previously described , , . Briefly, protein expression was induced using 0.1 mm isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside and allowed to proceed for 3 h at 37 °C. GST-tagged BLVRB was purified by affinity chromatography using glutathione-Sepharose 4B, and the fusion protein was cleaved overnight at 4 °C with 1 nM α-thrombin, resulting in >95% separation from the GST carrier. The cleaved protein was re-purified by a final gel filtration step on Sephacryl S-200, and was >95% pure as established by SDS-PAGE and densitography; BLVRB concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid assay.
Inhibition assays were performed using a Cary 60-UV visible spectrophotometer, using DCPIP and FMN as substrates. DCPIP reduction performed superiorly to FMN and was applied as the more sensitive single-point screen (assay sensitivity ~100 nM BLVRB). Assays lacking inhibitor (solvent only) and/or enzyme served as positive and negative controls, respectively. Flavin reductase (FR) activity was determined by monitoring the rate of oxidation of NAD(P)H to NADP+ at 340 nm (ε = 6200 M−1 cm−1). Enzymatic activity was measured at 37 °C in 100 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, containing 200 μM FMN, 100 μM NAD(P)H, and varying concentrations of inhibitor after addition of 200 nM BLVRB. DCPIP reduction (DR) proceeded in a reaction containing 100 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 100 μM NADPH, inhibitor, and 50 μM DCPIP at 25 °C. Reactions were initiated by the addition of 100 nM BLVRB, and reductase activity was measured spectrophotometrically by following the decrease in absorbance at 600 nm corresponding to the reduction of DCPIP (ε = 20.7 mM−1 cm−1) . Potent inhibitors from the single-point assay were characterized to determine the concentration of inhibitor required to inhibit enzymatic activity by 50% (IC50) using 8-point screens (concentrations (0–5000 nM)) in reaction mixtures at fixed concentrations of substrate (200 μM FMN or 50 μM DCPIP) and NAD(P)H (100 μM). The total amount of organic solvent added was kept constant at a final concentration of 2% (v/v). IC50 values were determined by nonlinear regression fits using GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.0). BLVRA activity was measured in a reaction mixture containing 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.7, 100 μM NADPH, and 25 μM biliverdin IXα (with our without inhibitor) at 25 °C, initiated by the addition of 1 μM BLVRA (Fitzgerald, 30R-137). Enzymatic activity was monitored following the conversion of biliverdin (ε650nm = 14.3 mM−1 cm−1) to bilirubin (ε450nm = 53 mM−1 cm−1) .

## Surface plasmon resonance (SPR)
Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) inhibitor binding kinetics were determined using BLVRB (22 kDa, >90% pure based on SDS-PAGE) immobilized to a Series S Sensor Chip CM5 through amine-coupling chemistry on a Biacore T200 instrument at 25 °C. The surfaces of flow cells one and two were activated for 7 min with a 1:1 mixture of 0.1 M NHS (N-hydroxysuccinimide) and 0.4 M EDC (3-(N,N-dimethylamino) propyl-N-ethylcarbodiimide) at a flow rate of 5 μL/min. The ligand at a concentration of 50 μg/mL in 10 mM sodium acetate (pH 5.0) was immobilized at a density of 2000–4000 resonance units (RU) on flow cells 2–4, and flow cell 1 was left blank to serve as a reference surface. Both surfaces were blocked with a 7-min injection of 1 M ethanolamine (pH 8.0). NAD(P)H (1 mM) was included in the running buffer (10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM EDTA, 0.05% Surfactant P20, 2% DMSO) for immobilization and all runs. To collect kinetic binding data, the analytes in running buffer were injected at concentrations from 0.08 μM to 50 μM (varied by inhibitor) at a flow rate of 30 µL/min and at a temperature of 25 °C. The complex was allowed to associate and dissociate for 60 and 90 s, respectively. Solvent correction was included as a recurrence, and a buffer blank was flowed over the two surfaces. Each sample was analyzed in duplicate. The data were fit to a simple 1:1 interaction model using the global data analysis option available within Biacore Evaluation software.

## NMR spectroscopy
NMR spectroscopy was completed using recombinant His6-tagged BLVRB as previously described , . Briefly, 15N-labeled protein was expressed in BL21/DE3 cells at 37 °C, and induced at OD~0.6 using 600 nM isopropyl b-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) in M9 minimal media with 15N ammonium chloride for 5 h. The cell pellet was denatured with Ni denaturation buffer (5 M guanidine, 50 mM Na2HPO4, pH 7.0, 500 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole), followed by sonication and affinity purification using a Ni-affinity column (Sigma). Protein was eluted in the same buffer supplemented with 400 mM imidazole and dialyzed extensively at 25 °C using a Ni denaturation buffer to remove any residual coenzyme nonspecifically bound to the Ni column (apo BLVRB). BLVRB refolding was then completed at 4 °C using refold buffer (100 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT) supplemented with 1 M arginine for 24 h, followed by two additional changes in the same refold buffer for at least 12 h. After thrombin cleavage (1 nM α-thrombin overnight at 4 °C) to remove the His6 tag, the sample was applied to a Superdex-75 (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) equilibrated in NMR buffer (50 mM bis-tris, pH 6.5, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT). NMR experiments were conducted on a Varian 900 MHz spectrometer equipped with a cryo-probe at 20 °C. Data were processed using nmrPipe and analyzed using ccpNMR . For the investigation of apo and holo (with NADP+) human BLVRB, 15N-HSQC spectra were recorded at a protein concentration of 200 μM. Inhibitor screening was performed at ratios of 1:0.5, 1:1, 1:1.5, and 1:2 (BLVRB:inhibitor) in the absence and presence of NADP+ coenzyme. Inhibitors were dissolved in DMSO, while BLVRB was in a solution containing 50 mM Bis-Tris, 50 mM NaCl, and 1 mM DTT at pH 6.5. For NMR assignments within the titrated 15N-HSQC spectra, BMRB accession 27462 was used for apo BLVRB, and BMRB accession 27463 was used for holo BLVRB. Binding affinities for titrations were all fit to the following equation: Y = Fmax*(A −sqrt(A^2-B))/(2*C), where X is the concentration of inhibitor, Fmax is the maximum chemical shift change, A = ([BLVRB] + X + KD), B = 4*[BLVRB]*X, and C = [BLVRB]. These are estimates of the true binding affinities, as only resonances under fast exchange were used for binding isotherms and their CSPs were therefore small.

## BLVRB co-crystallization
BLVRB/NADP+ crystals were grown at 18 °C with sitting drop vapor diffusion. BLVRB (17.8 mg/mL) was incubated with 1 mM NADP+ on ice prior to crystallization. Drops comprised 0.5 μL BLVRB/NADP+ and 0.5 μL well solution [0.1 M MES monohydrate (pH 6.5), 12% w/v PEG 20,000, and 25% (v/v) MPD (2-methyl-2,4-pentanediol)]. Ligand co-crystals were prepared by soaking BLVRB/NADP+ for 20 h in a drop containing 2 mM BCT1028 or BCT2029. Crystals were cryoprotected with 20% glycerol before plunge freezing.

## Data collection and processing
X-ray diffraction data were collected for BLVRB/NADP+, BLVRB/NADP+/BCT1028, and BLVRB/NADP+/BCT2029 at the AMX beamline (17-ID-1) at the National Synchrotron Light Source II at Brookhaven National Laboratory (Upton, NY). In each case, complete datasets were collected from single crystals. Each dataset comprised a 200 degree rotation with 0.1 degrees per frame and an exposure time of 0.005 s using radiation at a wavelength of 0.9201 Å. Diffraction data were indexed, integrated, and scaled with XDS , followed by merging with AIMLESS , iteratively performed with the autoPROC processing pipeline . The data collection and processing statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table  ; raw diffraction data were deposited with Zenodo.

## Structure solution and refinement
BLVRB/NADP+/BCT2029 phases were determined via molecular replacement with Phaser using the coordinates of BLVRB (PDB entry 5OOH ) stripped from ligands and solvent as a search model. The BLVRB/NADP+/BCT2029 structure was in turn used as a search model to determine phases of the BLVRB/NADP+ and BLVRB/NADP+/BCT1028 structures with Phaser; the resulting models were subjected to alternating cycles of manual building with COOT and refinement with PHENIX until model completion. Ligand restraints for BCT1028 and BCT2029 were generated with PHENIX eLBOW using AM1 optimization and SMILES input. BLVRB/NADP+, BLVRB/NADP+/BCT1028, and BLVRB/NADP+/BCT2029 were refined to final Rwork/Rfree values of 20.6/22.9%, 17.8/20.0%, and 20.6/22.6%, respectively. The refined three-dimensional coordinates and structure factors were deposited at the Protein Data Bank with accession codes 9C16 (BCT2029) and 9C17 (BCT1028), and 9C18 (NADP in space group P1). Molecular structures shown in Fig.  were depicted with PyMOL, version 1.6.0 (Schrödinger).

## Inhibitor metabolic profiles and pharmacokinetics

## Metabolic stability
Compound metabolic stability mediated by the cytochrome P450 system was determined using mouse and human liver microsomes (0.5 mg/mL) in 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer, pH 7.4. Inhibitors (1 μM final concentration in 0.1% DMSO) were added in duplicate, and aliquots were removed and incubated with 10 mM NADPH so that the final compound concentration was 1 μM. Reactions were quenched with acetonitrile 0, 5, 15 and 30 min, centrifuged at 3220 × g for 20 min, and supernatants were analyzed by LC-MS/MS in columns equilibrated in 90% solvent A (0.2% formic acid)-10% solvent (acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 0.9 mL/min, after which the concentration of solvent A was decreased to 10% and maintained for 1.4 min. Loperamide was used as the internal standard, and verapamil (P-glycoprotein inhibitor, 1 μM) was used as a control standard for rapid clearance.

## Cellular permeability assays
Compound absorption/permeability assays were completed using MDR1 (P-glycoprotein)-transfected MDCK (Madin Darby Canine Kidney) monolayers propagated in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium containing 4,500 mg/L D-glucose, 110 mg/L sodium pyruvate, and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Transport studies were completed in duplicate using cells seeded onto Transwell filter membranes (5 × 105 cells/well), incubated with compounds (5 μM) diluted in transport medium HBSS buffer with 10 mM HEPES, pH 7.4; receiver buffer was the same as transport buffer. Bioanalysis was completed by LC-MS/MS using a column equilibrated in 95% solvent A (0.2% formic acid)-5% solvent (acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 0.9 mL/min. After 1.4 min, the concentration of solvent B was increased to 95% and maintained for 1.4 min. Loperamide was used as the internal standard for HPLC. Compound transport was determined in both apical to basolateral (A → B) and basolateral to apical (B → A) directions, using Eq.  .
1Papp(cm/sec)=(Vr/C0)(1/S)(dC/dt)where Papp is the apparent permeability, Vr is the volume of medium in the receiver chamber, C0 is the initial drug concentration in the receiver chamber, S is the surface area of the monolayer, and dC/dt is the drug flux (10−6 cm s−1) in the receiver chamber with time. Permeability categories are low (<2), medium (2.0–10.0), and high (>10) × [10−6 cm s−1]. The efflux ratio is defined as Papp B-A/Papp A-B, using digoxin (P-glycoprotein substrate) and propranolol (low passive permeability) as controls. An efflux ratio ≥2 is indicative of active compound efflux.

## In vitro pharmacologic screen
The Safety Screen 44-target panel was applied to identify potential adverse drug reactions of BCT1028 (Eurofins Discovery). Well-characterized ligand/receptor binding pairs included G-protein coupled receptors (N = 24), ion channels (N = 8), transporters (N = 3), nuclear receptors (N = 2), kinases (N = 1), and non-kinase enzymes (N = 6), details provided in Supplementary Table  . All assays were completed in duplicate as previously described , and the single target demonstrating >80% inhibition (δ opioid receptor) was assayed using an 8-point screen for IC50 determination.

## Pharmacokinetics and plasma measurements
Pharmacokinetic studies of select inhibitors were completed in male C57BL/6J mice, age 9–11 weeks (weight 25–28 g). Mice were fasted for four hours, then treated with 2 mg/kg (intravenously via tail vein) or 10 mg/kg (orally) compounds solubilized in PEG 400. For the intravenous route 100 µL of blood was collected from N = 3 mice at 0.083, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 10 and 24 h, while time points for the oral route were 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 24 h (N = 3 mice/time point). Blood samples were collected into tubes containing K2 EDTA as an anticoagulant, and centrifuged at 350 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to harvest plasma samples. All plasma samples were stored at −80 ± 10 °C until bioanalysis by LC/MS/MS using conditions as outlined above. Time vs. plasma concentration profiles and pharmacokinetic parameters maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), peak plasma time (Tmax), area under the curve (AUC), half-life (T1/2), volume of distribution (Vd). volume of distribution at steady state (Vss), and bioavailability (% F) were calculated using Phoenix WinNonlin (version 8.2) software.

## Cellular assays

## CD34± hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs)
Immunoselected CD34+ cells isolated from human umbilical cord blood (independent of sex) served as the source for cell differentiation or multipotential progenitor (MPP) colony assays, and were >95% pure prior to differentiation , . Suspension cultures were expanded overnight in StemSpan™ SFEM II medium containing StemSpan™ CC100 expansion supplement, and megakaryocyte differentiation was completed in serum-free StemSpan™ SFEM II medium plus StemSpan™ CC220 (containing stem cell factor (SCF), IL-6, IL-9 and TPO cytokines; concentrations proprietary), without (v/v DMSO as control) or with 10 μM BCT1028 at 72-h intervals. TPO-deficient cultures were maintained in StemSpan™ SFEM II medium supplemented with IL-6, IL-9, and SCF (10 ng/mL each), supplemented without or with TPO 10 ng/mL, and 10 μM BCT1028 (or v/v DMSO as control) every 24 h. ## Cell lines
Human erythroleukemia (HEL), K562, or MOLT4 cells were obtained from ATCC and grown in RPMI supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum; all studies were completed on cells passaged <5 times. Cellular DCPIP reduction (DR) assays were completed as outlined above using RIPA-solubilized lysates ; inhibitor effects were assayed using either K562 lysates (6 μg/reaction) or intact cells. DR assays using lysates were completed after 60 min incubation with inhibitors (or DMSO v/v control), whereas direct cell-based inhibition was quantified after 24 h incubation, followed by washes ×3, and DR activity assays using solubilized lysates (6 μg/sample). Differentiation studies were completed using serum-free media supplemented with PMA (phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate), various concentrations of inhibitors (supplemented daily) or DMSO-alone (v/v) controls; volume-adjusted DMSO was used for all cellular experiments. K562 cells were affixed to slides using a cytocentrifuge at 500 × g for 5 min, fixed in methanol for 10 min, and stained with Giemsa (Sigma-Aldrich; St. Louis, MO cat#R03055-76) for 20 min. Examination and image capture were performed on the Zeiss Primovert Microscope (Zeiss; Jena, Germany), and cell size was quantified using ImageJ software.

## Flow cytometry
Cell-surface immunophenotyping was completed as previously described , using lineage-restricted monoclonal antibodies (MAb) (BD Biosciences; Franklin Lakes, NJ): FITC (fluorescein isothiocyanate)-conjugated anti-CD41 [integrin αIIb, megakaryocyte, 1.25 μg/mL], FITC-conjugated anti-CD71 [Transferrin receptor protein 1, 0.65 μg/mL], PE (phycoerythrin)-conjugated anti-CD61 [integrin β3, megakaryocyte, 2.5 μg/mL], 7-AAD (7-amino-actinomycin D) (Biolegend, San Diego, CA) was used for dead cell exclusion. Intracellular ROS accumulation was quantified using the cell-permeant fluorogenic probe CellROX Red [500 nM, 30 min at 37 °C] (Life Technologies; Carlsbad, CA). Flow cytometric quantification was completed by data acquisition of 10,000 events using logarithmic gain settings for light scatter and fluorescence detection, applying isotype-matched IgG controls for gate delineation (CytoFLEX; Beckman Coulter; Brea, CA); data were analyzed using Kaluza Flow Cytometry Analysis Software (Beckman Coulter; Brea, CA).
To assess cellular DNA content, cells were incubated for 72 h with BCT1028 (40 μM), PMA (1 × 106 M), or v/v DMSO (control), fixed in 70% ethanol overnight at 4 °C, washed with D-PBS (Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline), and incubated in 500 μl Propidium Iodide (PI)/RNAse Staining Buffer (BD Pharmingen; San Jose, CA cat#550825) for 4 h at 4 °C for flow cytometric analyses using standardized gates for cell cycle and ploidy analyses .

## Immunoblotting analysis
Cells and tissues were lysed in 1× RIPA buffer (Rockland Immunochemical; Pottstown, PA) containing protease inhibitor cocktail (Millipore Sigma cat#P8340) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Millipore Sigma cat#524625). Tissues were homogenized on ice using a Dounce Homogenizer. Cellular debris was removed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and protein concentrations were determined by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as standard. Protein lysates were size-fractionated by SDS-PAGE , transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, and immunodetection was completed using sheep anti-BLVRB antibody (1:1000; R&D Systems; Minneapolis, MN cat#AF6568) followed by donkey anti-sheep antibody (1:2000; Jackson ImmunoResearch; West Grove, PA; cat#713-035-147); mouse anti-BLVRA (1:1000; Santa Cruz; Dallas, TX cat#sc-393385) followed by goat anti-mouse antibody (1:2000; Jackson ImmunoResearch; West Grove, PA cat#115-585-003); goat anti-Transferrin Receptor (CD71; R & D Systems, Minneapolis, MN cat#AF2474) followed by mouse anti-goat antibody (1:2000; Jackson ImmunoResearch; West Grove, PA cat#205-032-176); mouse anti-BlvrB (Santa Cruz; Dallas, TX cat#sc-373692) followed by goat anti-mouse (1:5000; Jackson ImmunoResearch; West Grove, PA cat#115-036-072); rabbit anti-PF4 (1:500; Abcam; Cambridge, UK cat#ab129183) followed by donkey anti-rabbit (1:5000; Millipore Sigma; Burlington, MA cat#MANA9340); rabbit anti-CD61 (1:1000; Proteintech; Rosement, IL cat#18309-1-AP) followed by donkey anti-rabbit (1:5000, Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA cat#NA9340); mouse anti-actin antibody (1:1000; Millipore Sigma; Burlington, MA cat#MAB1501R) followed by goat anti-mouse antibody (1:2000; Jackson ImmunoResearch; West Grove, PA cat#115-036-072); mouse anti-GAPDH antibody (1:1000; Invitrogen; Carlsbad, CA cat#MA5-15738) followed by goat anti-mouse antibody (1:5000; Jackson ImmunoResearch; West Grove, PA cat#115-036-072). Immunodetection was completed by enhanced chemiluminescence using Luminata™ Forte Western HRP substrate (Millipore Sigma; Burlington, MA) and visualized using a C-DiGit® blot scanner (LI-COR; Lincoln, NE). ImageJ software was used for all densitometry calculations.

## Biochemical activity assays
Cell lysates (K562, HEL, MOLT4) were prepared in lysis buffer by sonication (on ice) in 4-s bursts repeated 5 times with intermittent cooling at an amplitude of 50%. Cellular debris was removed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. DCPIP reduction was determined as outlined above using varying lysate concentrations at 25 °C and fixed (100 μL) reaction volumes containing 100 μM NADPH and 50 μM DCPIP in HEPES buffer (100 mM, pH 7.0).

## Q-PCR
Lineage-specific RNA quantification was completed using fluorescence-based real-time Q-PCR (polymerase chain reaction) technology (Opticon 2 System, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) as previously described , , , . RNA abundance was determined from triplicate assays performed in parallel (2 ng input RNA), calculated in arbitrary units as 2[−ΔCt] , and normalized to β-actin (oligonucleotide primers are provided in Supplementary Table  ).

## Murine in vivo studies
All murine studies were approved by the Stony Brook University IACUC (Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee), and judicious guidelines were followed to minimize mouse utilization and to ensure humane animal care. In vivo studies were completed using sex- matched (male) C57Bl/6 mice aged 12–16 weeks, weight 25–28 g. For terminal experiments, mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation, followed by cervical dislocation.

## Cytopenia induction experiments
The chemotherapeutic agent 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) (Fresenius Kabi, USA) was diluted to 50 mg mL−1 with sterile saline, and mice were injected with a single intraperitoneal dose of 5-FU (or sterile saline as control) at a final concentration of 150 mg kg−1 (Day −1). CBCs were measured blinded to cohort/study arm at 72 h before 5-FU injection to determine baseline cell counts, and weekly until experimental completion; cohorts were staggered to ensure blood sampling at 3–4 day intervals. Mouse weights and phenotypic observations were recorded at baseline, and every two to three days until the experiment was terminated. Anticoagulated blood isolated by submandibular puncture served as the source for automated complete blood counts (CBCs) on a multi-species Element HT5 Veterinary Hematology Analyzer (Heska; Loveland, CO). Quantification of reticulated platelets (RP) and reticulocytes was completed on whole blood using thiazole orange (TO) (Sigma-Aldrich; St. Louis, MO). A 1 mg mL−1 stock solution of TO (in methanol) was diluted 1:20,000 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2 mM EDTA and 0.2% sodium azide. Whole blood (5 μL) was added to 1 mL of dilute TO dye, and samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min for flow cytometric quantification of nucleic acid-containing platelet and erythrocyte subpopulations.

## Murine phenotypic and histologic studies
Freshly isolated mouse organs were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen (for RNA or protein isolation), or paraffin embedded in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h at 4 °C prior to histological stains using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E); mouse femurs were decalcified in 10% EDTA, pH 7.4 for 72 h . Bone marrow flushes were completed in 100 μL of D-PBS, centrifuged (2500 × g for 5 min), washed, and immediately solubilized in Trizol for RNA isolation. Immunohistochemical detection of megakaryocyte von Willebrand factor (vWF) was completed in fixed (4% formaldehyde) and deparaffinized sections (5 μm) using rabbit polyclonal vWF primary antibody (1:200; Proteintech, Rosemont, IL), anti-rabbit biotinylated IgG (1:100), and peroxidase-based signal detection using Vectastain Antibody ABC-HRP (Novus Biologicals; Minneapolis, MN); slides were counterstained with hematoxylin. All microscopic analyses were performed blinded to the treatment cohort. Plasma thrombopoietin (TPO) levels were quantified using platelet-poor plasma (PPP) stored at −80 °C. Samples were thawed on ice, diluted 5-fold with diluent, and 50 μL aliquots were assayed in duplicate using the solid phase Mouse Thrombopoietin Quantikine ELISA Kit (Biotechne, Minneapolis, MN catalog #MTP00), quantified to a standard control curve displaying an R2 = 0.999.

## Statistical analyses
Statistical comparisons were completed using ANOVA or Student’s t-tests (or their non-parametric counterparts if the normality assumptions were not met) at the significance level of p < 0.05. Simple linear regression was performed in GraphPad Prism (version 9.3.1) for correlation analyses.
