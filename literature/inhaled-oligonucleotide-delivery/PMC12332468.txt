## Abstract

## Objective
The lungs of patients with systemic sclerosis-associated interstitial lung disease (SSc-ILD) contain inflammatory myofibroblasts that arise in association with fibrotic stimuli and perturbed innate immunity. The cytosolic DNA-binding receptor cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) is implicated in inflammation and fibrosis, but its involvement in SSc-ILD remains unknown. We examined cGAS expression, activity and therapeutic potential in SSc-ILD using human biospecimens, cultured fibroblasts, precision-cut lung slices and a well-accepted animal model.

## Methods
Expression and localisation of cGAS, cytokines and type 1 interferons were evaluated in SSc‑ILD lung tissues, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and isolated lung fibroblasts. CGAS activation was assessed in a publicly available SSc-ILD single-cell RNA-sequencing dataset. Production of cytokines, type 1 interferons and α-smooth muscle actin elicited by transforming growth factor-β1 or local substrate stiffness was measured in normal human lung fibroblasts via quantitative reverse transcription PCR, ELISA and immunofluorescence. Small molecule cGAS inhibition was tested in cultured fibroblasts, human precision-cut lung slices and the bleomycin pulmonary fibrosis model.

## Results
SSc-ILD lung tissue and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid were enriched for cGAS, cytokines and type 1 interferons. The cGAS pathway showed constitutive activation in SSc-ILD fibroblasts and was inducible in normal human lung fibroblasts by transforming growth factor-β1 or mechanical stimuli. In these settings, and in precision-cut lung slices, cGAS expression was paralleled by the production of cytokines, type 1 interferons and α-smooth muscle actin, which was mitigated by a small molecule cGAS inhibitor. These findings were recapitulated in the bleomycin mouse model.

## Conclusion
cGAS signalling contributes to pathogenic inflammatory myofibroblast phenotypes in SSc‑ILD. Inhibiting cGAS or its downstream effectors represents a novel therapeutic approach.

## Introduction
Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is an autoimmune condition characterised by progressive fibrosis of the skin and visceral organs [ ]. Patients with SSc frequently develop interstitial lung disease (SSc-ILD), a pulmonary condition characterised by inflammatory cell infiltration and fibrotic remodelling [ ]. SSc-ILD underlies the considerable morbidity and mortality in patients with SSc [ ], and current consensus treatment guidelines recommend immunosuppressive agents and antifibrotic drugs that are not universally effective and impose significant burden in terms of side-effects and costs [ – ]. Thus, understanding the mechanisms contributing to both inflammation and fibrosis is imperative for the development of effective treatments to improve patient care.
The lungs of patients with SSc-ILD exhibit varying degrees of immune activation and extracellular matrix accumulation [ ]. Additionally, these lungs contain heterogeneous fibroblast populations that produce inflammatory mediators, secrete collagens and other extracellular matrix molecules, and express myofibroblast markers such as α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) [ – ]. These properties are inducible in normal human lung fibroblasts (NHLFs) by fibrotic stimuli, including transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) [ ] and interactions with stiffened tissue substrates [ ]. The presence of inflammatory myofibroblasts in SSc-ILD may account for heterogeneous treatment responses because currently used first-line treatments are designed to suppress either the inflammatory role of immune cells or the fibrogenic function of fibroblasts. Missing from current therapeutic strategies are agents that can simultaneously mitigate inflammation and fibrogenesis in a single population of effector cells. In view of these limitations, identifying druggable pathways that simultaneously contribute to inflammatory responses and fibrotic functions in fibroblasts remains a critical unmet need.
Innate immune drivers of fibroblast activation have emerged as important pathogenic pathways in SSc-ILD. Of particular interest are nucleic acid receptors that recognise and respond to DNA and RNA derived from invading pathogens and injured cells. We and others have demonstrated vital roles for Toll-like receptor 9 in fibroblast activation and disease progression in SSc-ILD and associated conditions [ – ]. In contrast, the pathogenic contribution of alternate nucleic acid receptors, such as cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), remains less well understood. The broadly expressed intracellular receptor cGAS is a nucleotidyltransferase that, upon binding to cytosolic DNA derived from invading pathogens or endogenous sources (nuclei, mitochondria), catalyses the production of 2,3-cGAMP [ ]. This substrate then activates the cytosolic kinase stimulator of interferon genes (STING) to initiate production of interferon regulatory factor-3 (IRF3) and NF-κB-dependent proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines and type I interferons (IFNs) in immune cells [ ]. Reflecting the emerging importance of cGAS in disease development, numerous approaches to cGAS–STING modulation exist in the therapeutic pipeline for conditions of chronic inflammation and autoimmunity [ , ]. Intriguingly, the cGAS–STING pathway has also been implicated as a fibroblast activator in diverse conditions such as cancer [ ], ageing [ ], pulmonary fibrosis [ ] and SSc [ ]. Endogenous cGAS ligands such as DNA from mitochondria (mtDNA) and nuclear sources are elevated in patients with SSc [ , , ]. However, the mechanistic and therapeutic potential of cGAS in SSc-ILD is currently unknown.
On the basis of this biology, we surmised that engagement of cGAS drives inflammatory myofibroblast accumulation in SSc-ILD. To test this hypothesis, we evaluated the expression and function of cGAS in patient samples, ex vivo modelling systems and a well-accepted mouse model. Our results show that expression of cGAS and its associated inflammatory mediators is increased in lung tissue and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) from SSc-ILD patients, where it is constitutively expressed and activated in SSc-ILD fibroblasts. We also show that cGAS expression is inducible in NHLFs following stimulation with either TGF-β1 or mechanical stimuli. In these settings, and in human precision-cut lung slices (PCLS), cGAS expression is paralleled by the production of inflammatory mediators and α-SMA expression, which is mitigated by a small molecule cGAS inhibitor. Similar antifibrotic effects of cGAS inhibition are seen in an experimental model of mouse lung fibrosis. Altogether, these findings implicate cGAS in inflammatory fibroblast activation and fibrosis, putting forth cGAS inhibition as a novel therapeutic for SSc-ILD and related disorders.

## Materials and methods
Detailed methods are provided in the supplementary methods.

## Human subjects
De-identified lung tissues and primary lung fibroblasts were a kind gift from Dr. Carol Feghali-Bostwick. De-identified BALF specimens were previously collected from healthy donors and patients with SSc using informed consent on protocols approved by the institutional review board at Yale School of Medicine (HIC 2000024862).

## Single-cell RNA-sequencing analysis
Analyses of single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) data (GSE128169) were performed with Seurat version 5.0.318 [ ] as described in the supplementary methods. The AddModuleScore, a Seurat function that calculates the gene expression of a cluster of genes on a single-cell level and then subtracts the aggregated expression of a randomly assigned control set, was used to identify cell types expressing genes associated with cGAS.

## Fibroblast cultures
Fibroblasts harvested from the lungs of three individual patients with SSc-ILD were cultured in the absence or presence of the small molecule cGAS inhibitor G140 (10 µM, Invivogen). NHLFs harvested from four separate healthy donors were cultured in the absence or presence of recombinant human TGF-β1 (5 ng·mL−1) and CpG ODN2006 (50 µmol) and in the absence or presence of G140. Additionally, NHLFs were transfected with small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting CGAS or scramble sequence (Dharmacon) and were cultured in the absence or presence of TGF-β1 and CpG ODN2006. In parallel, NHLFs were cultured on collagen-coated hydrogels (Matrigen) of 1 kPA and 25 kPA stiffness in the absence or presence of G140.

## Human PCLS
PCLS from normal human donors (Institute for In Vitro Sciences) were incubated in culture media supplemented with or without an inflammatory fibrotic cocktail (IFC) comprising the following: TGF-β1 (5 ng·mL−1), platelet-derived growth factor-AB (PDGF-AB) (5 μM), tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) (10 ng·mL−1), lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) (5 μM) and CpG ODN2006 (50 µmol). PCLS subjected to this cocktail were then treated in the absence or presence of G140 (10 nM).

## Mouse experiments
Animal procedures were approved by the Yale Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. C57Bl/6 mice received a single dose of inhaled bleomycin (0.8 units·kg−1) or normal saline. Vehicle control or G140 (30 mg·kg−1) was administered via intraperitoneal injection on days 3, 7 and 10 post-bleomycin. Mice were euthanised on day 14, and biospecimens were collected for analysis.

## RNA isolation and quantitative reverse transcription PCR
Total cellular RNA was isolated and reverse transcribed and underwent polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) for glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), actin α2, smooth muscle (ACTA2) and CGAS from biospecimens as described in the supplementary methods.

## Cell lysis
Cells were washed and lysed in a solution containing Pierce RIPA buffer and a Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## Quantification of cytokines
Multiplex quantification of interleukin (IL)-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), IFN-γ inducible protein-10 (IP-10), IFN-α and IFN-β was performed using U-PLEX cytokine panel human or mouse kits (Meso Scale Discovery).

## ELISA
Cell lysates were assayed for α-SMA (Abcam) or cGAS (Cell Signaling Technology) via commercially available ELISA kits as described in the supplementary methods.

## MTT assay
Cell viability was determined using a 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (Abcam) as described in the supplementary methods.

## cGAS immunohistochemistry
Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded slides of human lung tissue from healthy or SSc-ILD donors were stained with a human cGAS antibody (1:100, Invitrogen) as described in the supplementary methods. cGAS quantification was performed by manually counting total nuclei and nuclei from cGAS-expressing cells; results are reported as the percentage of positive cGAS cells per high-power field.

## Immunofluorescence
Immunofluorescence detection of α-SMA and cGAS in fibroblasts was performed with mouse anti-α-SMA (1:250, Abcam) and rabbit monoclonal cGAS (1:100, Invitrogen) antibodies as described in the supplementary methods.

## Lung histology and collagen quantification
Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded lung sections from PCLS or mouse lungs were stained with Masson's trichrome [ ]. Collagen was quantified with ImageJ software as described in the supplementary methods. Soluble collagen was quantified using a Sircol Collagen Assay kit (Biocolor) [ ].

## Bulk RNA-seq
RNA isolated from PCLS was sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform as described in the supplementary methods. Differential gene expression was identified with the DESeq2 package on R statistical software (version 4.3.2; www.r-project.org). Heatmaps were generated in R with the gplots package. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted via MetaCore; a false discovery rate (FDR) <0.10 was considered significant.

## Statistical analysis
Data distribution was assessed using a Shapiro–Wilk test, and categorical data were analysed with Fisher's exact test using GraphPad Prism 10.4.1. Multiple hypothesis testing was conducted with one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction with GraphPad Prism. Pairwise comparisons were conducted with an unpaired t-test or Mann–Whitney test based on data distribution with GraphPad Prism. p-values <0.05 were considered significant.

## Results

## Expression of cGAS and its associated soluble mediators is enriched in the lungs of patients with SSc-ILD
To assess a potential relationship between cGAS and SSc-ILD, immunohistochemistry was performed on lung tissues obtained from healthy control (unsuitable organ donors) or SSc-ILD patients undergoing orthotopic lung transplantation. In contrast to the low level of cGAS detected in control samples, SSc-ILD lung tissues displayed abundant cGAS expression in cells exhibiting the morphology of immune populations, epithelium and fibroblasts ( , b). cGAS-associated soluble mediators were then measured in BALF specimens from the control and SSc donors shown in . Compared to controls, SSc-ILD BALF showed high concentrations of cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α; –e), chemokines (MCP-1, IP-10; , g) and type 1 IFNs (IFN-α, IFN-β; , i), all of which are known to be downstream of cGAS activation. These data show that the lungs of patients with SSc-ILD are enriched for expression of cGAS and its associated soluble mediators.
 

## Enhanced expression and activation of cGAS in SSc-ILD fibroblasts
Having identified enhanced cGAS expression in SSc-ILD lung tissues, we then investigated this biology in disease-relevant cell types. We began by analysing a publicly available scRNA-seq dataset (GSE128169) generated from end-stage SSc-ILD lung explants. Consistent with our immunohistochemistry findings above, multiple cell populations showed transcriptional evidence of cGAS activation (supplementary figure S1 and ). When this analysis was limited to stromal cells, fibroblasts exhibited high expression of cGAS pathway transcripts ( ). These in silico studies were confirmed in vitro with primary lung fibroblasts, where evaluation of cGAS expression via qRT-PCR and ELISA showed increased expression at the mRNA and protein levels ( , d). Moreover, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that the baseline nuclear cGAS localisation seen in NHLFs was accompanied by the detection of cytoplasmic aggregates in SSc-ILD fibroblasts, indicating that these cells may exist in a state of heightened cGAS activation (supplementary figure S2 and ). Importantly, this phenotype was accompanied by increased production of cGAS-associated inflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, MCP-1, IP-10, IFN-α, IFN-β) along with elevated mRNA and protein levels of the myofibroblast marker α-SMA ( ). These results implicate cGAS as a potential driver of inflammatory myofibroblast activation in SSc-ILD.
 

## A small molecule cGAS inhibitor mitigates inflammatory mediator production and fibrotic activation of SSc-ILD fibroblasts
To link cGAS more firmly with inflammatory myofibroblast activation in SSc-ILD, we employed the small molecule cGAS inhibitor G140, a commercially available, highly specific small molecule that blocks DNA entry into the human cGAS catalytic pocket to impede enzymatic activation [ , ]. In our study, treatment of SSc-ILD fibroblasts with G140 reduced cytoplasmic cGAS aggregates ( ), suggesting successful inhibition. This observation was paralleled by decreased production of inflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, MCP-1, IP-10, IFN-α and IFN-β; –h). G140 treatment also reduced α-SMA mRNA measured by qRT-PCR ( ) and protein measured by immunofluorescence and ELISA ( , j). Importantly, these findings were not due to alterations in cellular viability given that the results of an MTT assay were similar between untreated and treated cells ( ). These studies show that cGAS inhibition reduces production of cytokines, chemokines, type 1 IFNs and α-SMA in SSc-ILD fibroblasts, providing proof-of-concept evidence for targeting cGAS in this disease.
 

## Small molecule cGAS inhibition suppresses TGF-β1 and CpG-induced fibroblast activation
Studies of diseased lung fibroblasts are useful for the development of mechanistic-based SSc-ILD therapies. However, while providing evidence of target engagement, they are limited by the end-stage nature of the disease. Because the therapeutic goal is to avert clinical progression, studies aimed at preventing cellular activation have the potential to halt or even reverse disease. Reasoning that fibroblasts adopt their ultimate phenotype in response to microenvironmental cues, we developed a model of cGAS activation in which NHLFs were co-stimulated with TGF-β1 and CpG ODN2006 to simulate an endogenous cGAS ligand (supplementary figure S3). Following co-stimulation, NHLFs phenocopied many aspects of SSc-ILD fibroblasts, including increased expression and cytoplasmic aggregation of cGAS (supplementary figure S4a–c), production of inflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, MCP-1, IP-10, IFN-α, IFN-β; supplementary figure S4d–j), and α-SMA mRNA and protein (supplementary figure S4k, l).
Next, to determine if cGAS is required for these findings, we conducted siRNA-mediated loss of function studies with these cells. As compared to NHLFs transfected with a scramble sequence, cells subjected to siRNA targeting CGAS displayed a concentration-dependent reduction in CGAS transcription (supplementary figure S5a). Subsequently, transfected cells were co-stimulated with TGF-β1 and CpG ODN2006; relative to scramble sequence, specific siRNA transfection resulted in decreased transcription of CGAS and ACTA2 (supplementary figure S5b, c). These findings demonstrate proof of concept of a cGAS-dependent mechanism of fibroblast activation, further supporting our therapeutic strategy of pharmacological inhibition with G140. Thus, we treated co-stimulated NHLFs with this small molecule inhibitor, which resulted in diminished cytoplasmic cGAS aggregation ( ) and suppressed production of all inflammatory mediators except for IL-6 ( –h). Production of α-SMA mRNA and protein were also reduced ( , j). Lastly, an MTT assay revealed these findings to be unrelated to changes in cell viability ( ). Taken together, these data provide evidence of cGAS antagonism as a novel strategy for mitigating fibroblast activation that arises in response to TGF-β1 and endogenous cGAS ligands.
 

## A small molecule cGAS inhibitor opposes stiffness-induced fibroblast activation
Cells encounter and respond to physical properties, such as stiffness, in their microenvironment. While cGAS has been previously linked to fibrotic mechanosensing [ ], its involvement in SSc-ILD and therapeutic targeting in lung fibroblasts remains underexplored. Thus, we investigated this area using tunable hydrogels constructed to simulate the stiffness of normal (1 kPA) or SSc-ILD (25 kPA) lungs [ ]. Compared to cells cultured under physiological conditions, NHLFs grown on 25 kPA gels developed increased cGAS mRNA transcription and protein levels (supplementary figure S6a, b); increased secretion of IL-1β, MCP-1, IP-10, IFN-α and IFN-β, but not IL-6 or TNF-α (supplementary figure S6c–i); and induction of α-SMA mRNA and protein expression (supplementary figure S6j, k). Treatment with G140 reduced IL-1β, IP-10, IFN-α and IFN-β, but not MCP-1 ( –e), and attenuated α-SMA mRNA and protein ( , g) without affecting cellular viability ( ). Overall, these data frame cGAS as a druggable driver of mechanosensitive fibroblast responses that are pathogenic in SSc-ILD.
 

## A small molecule cGAS inhibitor suppresses inflammatory mediator production and fibrotic end-points in human PCLS
Studies of isolated lung fibroblasts provide direct evidence of cell autonomous responses in these fibrotic cells. However, they are limited by the lack of information regarding the complex multicellularity of the adult lung, which can activate fibroblasts via non-cell autonomous processes. To overcome these issues, we extended our studies to include PCLS, an advanced in vitro modelling system that allows previously impossible tissue-level insights into human lung injury, repair and remodelling. This technology improves upon standard primary monoculture methods by replicating the complex 3D topography and multicellularity of the lung [ ]. Our studies used a model of normal PCLS cultured in media containing an IFC of TGF-β, PDGF-AB, TNF-α and LPA, and supplemented with the mtDNA surrogate CpG ODN2006. As compared to naïve PCLS, those cultured in this IFC displayed enhanced collagen content (supplementary figure S7a, b), cGAS transcripts (supplementary figure S7c) and protein levels of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, MCP-1, IP-10 and IFN-α, but not IFN-β (supplementary figure S7d–j). The IFC also induced α-SMA transcripts (supplementary figure S7k). Treatment with the cGAS inhibitor G140 reduced all these end-points ( –i).
 Lastly, to identify parallel transcriptional changes that occur in the absence or presence of G140, we conducted exploratory bulk RNA-seq and identified 126 differentially expressed genes ( ). Unbiased pathway enrichment analysis revealed a multitude of transcriptional changes involving stromal and immune cells, such as airway epithelium cells, alveolar macrophages, antigen-presenting cells and T-cells. Altered genes were associated with inflammatory and immune functions, such as cytoskeletal remodelling, IL-17, cell adhesion, antigen presentation, NF-kB activation and mesenchymal cell activation ( ). The finding that cGAS inhibition suppresses production of cytokines, chemokines, type 1 IFNs, α-SMA expression and collagen production during human lung fibrogenesis suggests that targeting cGAS and/or its associated pathways holds therapeutic potential in SSc-ILD.

## A small molecule cGAS inhibitor mitigates bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis
Finally, having demonstrated the benefit of a cGAS inhibitor in orthogonal ex vivo modelling experiments, we sought to determine its efficacy in vivo. For this purpose, we used the inhaled bleomycin mouse model, which replicates many features of pulmonary fibrosis and is commonly used to model the inflammatory fibrogenesis seen in SSc-ILD [ ]. Mice were subjected to orotracheal instillation of either normal saline or bleomycin in the presence of vehicle or G140. As shown in supplementary figure S8a–c, bleomycin-challenged mice exhibited greater lung collagen accumulation than those that received normal saline or G140 alone. Evaluation of BALF revealed elevations of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, MCP-1, IP-10, IFN-α, IFN-β and white blood cell (WBC) counts (supplementary figure S8d–k). When bleomycin-challenged mice were treated with G140, we observed marked reductions in lung collagen content ( –c) and BALF concentrations of IL-6, TNF-α, IP-10, IFN-α and IFN-β, but not IL-1β, MCP-1 or WBC counts ( –k). When viewed in combination, our results identify cGAS as a modulator of inflammatory fibroblast activation in mammalian lung fibrosis and supports the clinical development of cGAS inhibitors for treatment of SSc-ILD.
 

## Discussion
Innate immunity and inflammatory myofibroblast activation are increasingly linked to SSc-ILD; however, their mechanistic and therapeutic implications are not well understood [ ]. Here we identified the cytosolic DNA sensor cGAS as a mechanistic driver of and therapeutic target in SSc-ILD. Our results revealed that expression of cGAS and its downstream mediators are enhanced in SSc-ILD lung tissues, BALF and lesional fibroblasts. These findings are recapitulated in SSc-ILD modelling systems, including NHLFs exposed to biochemical and biophysical fibrotic stimuli, human PCLS exposed to fibrogenic mediators, and the well-accepted bleomycin mouse model. A small molecule cGAS inhibitor exerted an anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effect in all these settings. Targeting aberrant cGAS activation holds promise as an innovative strategy in SSc-ILD as well as for a myriad of other inflammatory fibrosing conditions characterised by autoimmunity [ ], neurodegeneration [ ], cancer [ ] and ageing [ ].
Upon its 2013 discovery, cGAS was described as a nucleotidyltransferase that recognises pathogen-derived DNA and activates type 1 IFN production in a STING-dependent, IRF3-dependent manner [ ]. Since then, cGAS has been shown to be activated by endogenous DNA, including that derived from nuclei and mitochondria [ ]. cGAS can recognise self-ligands that leak into the cytoplasm following DNA damage responses or mitochondrial stress, or that have been internalised as damage-associated molecular patterns present in the extracellular milieu. Our work advances this notion because we were able to induce fibroblast activation and cGAS signalling with the administration of exogenous CpG, a surrogate for cell-free mtDNA [ , , ]. While our current findings complement a prior report linking cGAS to fibroblast senescence in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [ ], our prior studies implicate mtDNA as a putative cGAS activator in SSc-ILD but not idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis [ , ], highlighting the need for further investigation in this area. Further work characterising the nature of mtDNA and its interactions with cGAS [ ] has the potential to elucidate intervenable innate immune mechanisms of inflammatory fibroblast activation in SSc-ILD and related conditions.
The results reported herein are predicated on the ability of cGAS to activate STING and induce NF-κB and IRF3 signalling [ ]. In our work, cGAS inhibition suggested context-dependent aspects of the various modelling systems. For example, while SSc-ILD BALF and fibroblasts are enriched for NF-κB-dependent and IRF3-dependent cytokines and type 1 IFNs, we have not characterised the contribution of STING and its associated transcription factors. The expression of NF-κB-dependent and IRF3-dependent inflammatory mediators supports a pleiotropic effect, and the effect on α-SMA expression likely reflects the recently described role of cGAS–STING in canonical TGF-β1 activation and Yes1-associated transcriptional regulator (YAP)/transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ)-mediated mechanosensing. It is equally possible, however, that our observations stem from noncanonical cGAS pathways, such as protein kinase R-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), that are independent of STING. Further study is needed to decipher the context-dependent paradigm of cGAS activation and its downstream signalling pathways in SSc-ILD and related conditions.
In terms of therapeutic potential, cGAS inhibition remains an active area of investigation for clinical benefit. Currently available antagonists include the anti-malarial agents hydroxychloroquine and quinacrine, which bind to the minor groove of the DNA–cGAS interface to disrupt ligand–receptor interactions [ ]. Suramin, a drug indicated for the treatment of river blindness and African sleeping sickness, acts as a DNA mimic that competitively binds to cGAS and inhibits 2,3-cGAMP synthesis [ ]. While repurposing these agents for SSc-ILD is a consideration, the potential for nonspecific, off-target liabilities limits their widespread use [ , ]. Thus, the identification of compounds that directly inhibit cGAS has been actively pursued. One promising compound was RU.521, which occupies the catalytic site of cGAS to inhibit generation of 2,3-cGAMP [ ]. However, this effect was only seen in mouse studies, propelling the development of G140, a human-specific small molecule inhibitor of cGAS with a mechanism of action similar to RU.521 [ , ]. The pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of G140 have been evaluated across various biochemical and cellular assays and assessed for off-target effects [ , ]. Most notably, these works showed that this agent inhibits cGAS in a concentration-dependent manner in vitro [ ] and reduces the production of cGAS-associated cytokines in a lung injury model in vivo [ ]. Our study is the first to evaluate G140 in an inflammatory fibrotic setting, and further work is needed for testing this agent in SSc-ILD human cohorts.
Though novel and provocative, our findings raise additional questions for future research. While our work was focused on the SSc-ILD lung, similarly rigorous studies in commonly afflicted organs such as the skin, kidneys and heart have the potential to yield novel insights into the convergent and divergent mechanisms of inflammatory fibrosis present in this multi-organ disease. Moreover, although we were able to recapitulate mechanisms of active inflammatory fibrosis with our cellular, PCLS and mouse models, the relevance of our findings would be enhanced with the inclusion of human SSc-ILD tissue from patients with early-stage disease to elucidate mechanisms of active tissue remodelling specific to SSc. Although we have identified fibroblasts as one site of activated cGAS function, additional cell types are likely involved. Mechanistically, we have not determined if cGAS activation mediates canonical versus noncanonical TGF-β activation, nor explored its interactions with mechanosensitive signalling, such as those involving Hippo [ ], YAP [ ], focal adhesion kinase [ ] and Rho-associated kinase [ ]. In terms of the cGAS ligand, we have not determined the contribution of DNA derived from mitochondrial, nuclear or even microbial sources. Moreover, we have yet to determine the impact of canonical cGAS signalling components, such as the second messenger protein 2,3-cGAMP and STING. Lastly, we have not evaluated how cGAS inhibition compares to immunosuppressive strategies [ ] and antifibrotic agents [ ] that are currently in use. Despite these relatively minor limitations, in this first of its kind study, we found that cGAS is expressed in SSc-ILD lung tissues and that its inhibition mitigates the development of inflammatory fibrosis in tissue-based, cellular and animal models. Further investigation may yield the development of cGAS-targeted therapies for SSc-ILD and related conditions.
