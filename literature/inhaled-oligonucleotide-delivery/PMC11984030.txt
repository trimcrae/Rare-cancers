## Abstract

## Background
Cellular senescence is one of the key steps in the progression of pulmonary fibrosis, and the senescence of type II alveolar epithelial cells (AEC IIs) may potentially accelerate the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying cellular senescence in pulmonary fibrosis remain unclear.

## Methods
The researchers first conducted in vitro experiments to investigate whether AEC IIs cultured on high matrix stiffness would lead to cellular senescence. Next, samples from mouse pulmonary fibrosis models and clinical idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) patients were tested to observe extracellular matrix deposition, lamin A/C levels, and cellular senescence status in lung tissue. Construct lamin A/C knockdown and overexpression systems separately in AEC IIs, and observe whether changes in lamin A/C levels lead to cellular senescence. Further explore the degradation mechanism of lamin A/C using protein degradation inhibitors.

## Results
In vitro experiments have found that high matrix stiffness promotes senescence of AEC IIs. In a mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis, AEC IIs were found to exhibit significant cellular senescence on day 21. In clinical IPF samples, it was found that senescent cells expressed low levels of lamin A/C. In the lamin A/C SiRNA knockdown system, it was further confirmed that AEC IIs with low levels of lamin A/C are more prone to cellular senescence. Under high matrix stiffness, lamin A/C in AEC IIs is degraded through the autophagy lysosome pathway. The use of chloroquine can effectively alleviate cellular senescence.

## Conclusions
High matrix stiffness degrades lamin A/C in pulmonary fibrosis through lysosomal degradation pathways, promoting AEC II senescence. Inhibition the degradation of lamin A/C could alleviate AEC II senescence.

## Introduction
In many cases, pulmonary fibrosis is the consequence of chronic inflammatory and autoimmune pulmonary diseases. Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), the most common type of pulmonary fibrosis, is characterized by damage to alveolar epithelial cells, proliferation of fibroblasts, differentiation into myofibroblasts, and progressive collagen deposition. These processes ultimately lead to organ dysfunction and potentially, death [ – ]. Unfortunately, there are currently limited effective treatments for IPF, partly due to unclear underlying pathogenic mechanisms [ , ]. Therefore, we urgently need to study the molecular mechanisms underlying the progress of IPF.
Type II alveolar epithelial cells (AEC IIs) possess stem cell-like properties and can differentiate into Type I alveolar epithelial cells (AEC Is) in response to lung tissue damage. AEC IIs secrete alveolar surfactant to maintain the alveolar microenvironment. In patients with IPF, areas of fibrotic tissue are characterized by a depletion of AEC IIs and abnormal activation of cellular senescence-related signaling pathways, such as p53, indicating that AEC IIs undergo senescence in IPF [ , ]. The damage and senescence of AEC IIs may serve as initiating factors for the occurrence of fibrosis. Therefore, there is an urgent need to further explore the molecular mechanism of cellular senescence in alveolar epithelial cells.
The extracellular matrix (ECM) serves as a fundamental framework for maintaining the homeostasis of tissues and organs [ , ]. It undergoes dynamic remodeling through the synthesis and degradation of ECM proteins, resulting in a complex and intricate structure [ , ]. Recent studies have found that senescent cells have the ability to trigger remodeling and stiffening of the ECM, thereby influencing the recruitment of immune cells during the senescence process [ ]. Although it is recognized that senescent cells interact specifically with the ECM, research on the effect of matrix stiffness on the AEC II senescence is limited.
Nuclear lamins, also known as type V intermediate filaments, are fibrous proteins found in the cell nucleus that provide structural support and regulate transcriptional activities [ – ]. Lamins are involved in the disassembly and reassembly of the nuclear membrane during mitosis, as well as in the positioning of nuclear pores [ , ]. Evidence has demonstrated that lamin A is positively correlated with tissue stiffness and promotes matrix-directed differentiation [ , ]. In addition, the phosphorylation and turnover of lamin A/C are regulated by matrix elasticity, providing feedback to actomyosin dynamics [ ]. Lamin A/C is considered to be important regulator of gene expression and key mediator in determining cell fate. Therefore, lamin A/C has been extensively studied in chromatin organization, DNA replication, gene transcriptional regulation, cell differentiation, and tissue specific functions [ ]. However, there is limited research on the role of lamin A/C in IPF.
In this study, our aim is to investigate the molecular mechanisms of AEC II senescence in IPF. Our investigation found that AEC IIs cultured on high matrix stiffness exhibited features of cellular senescence with reduced levels of lamin A/C. After the application of autophagy inhibitors, the senescence of AEC IIs was attenuated. Therefore, we speculate that high matrix stiffness promotes a decrease in lamin A/C, which is a key factor leading to cellular senescence.

## Methods and materials

## Cell culture
Polyacrylamide hydrogel were obtained with 40% w/v acrylamide (Diamond, A100341-0500) + 2% w/v bis-acrylamide (Yuanye, S14002-100 g), adding the coagulant 10% ammonium persulfate (APS; Sangon Biotech, A100486-0100) and TEMED (PHYGENE, PH0341). Different stiffness were obtained by changing the final concentration of acrylamide/bis-acrylamide, with expected stiffness of 5 and 20 kPa according to previous article [ ].
The mouse AEC IIs were purchased from Procell and cultured in ATII cell special medium (Procell, CM-M003). AEC IIs are pluripotent stem cells that could differentiate into AEC Is in vitro without proliferating, therefore, in vitro experiments should be completed within 4 days to avoid AEC IIs differentiation. To investigate whether the effects of matrix stiffness on lamin A/C degradation and AEC II senescence are reversible or persistent. We cultured AEC IIs on high matrix stiffness for two days, and then transferred AEC IIs to soft matrix stiffness for another two days before conducting protein detection.
The inhibitors were purchased from Med Chem Express (MCE, USA) and the corresponding concentrations of compounds used are as follows: chloroquine (10 µM); bafilomycin A1 (10 µM), and MG-132 (20 µM) respectively.

## Animal study and clinical samples
The animal procedures adhered to ethical standards and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University (Accreditation number: SYSU-IACUC-2023-000786). The mice (C57BL/6, 8 weeks) were provided by the Animal Center of Sun Yat-sen University. Male mice were selected for the experiments and accommodated at 22–24 °C. The mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis was constructed and induced using intratracheal injection of bleomycin (3 U/kg). Slices from patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis were obtained from The First Affiliated Hospital of Gannan Medical University (Accreditation number: LLSC-2025-015).

## Western blot
Cells were washed twice with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Servicebio, G4207). Then, the cells on matrix were directly lysed in 1X RIPA buffer (Solarbio, R0020) with 30 min ice-bath and centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The total cell protein was located in the supernatant lysate. The total protein concentration was assessed by BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, 23227). Equal amount of protein was resolved by SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (EpiZyme, PG212). Proteins were electrotransferred to a 0.45 μm pore-sized polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (PVDF; Millipore, IPVH00010). The membranes were incubated with blocking buffer (5% skimmed milk in TBS-T buffer) for 1 h. After blocking, the target proteins were immunoblotted with specific antibodies. The primary and secondary antibodies used and their concentrations are shown in Table  . The protein signals were detected by the ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad). Bands from at least three independent experiments were quantified using ImageJ software.
 

## Senescence-associated beta-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining
According to the manufacturer’s protocol of senescence associated β-galactosidase kit (Beyotime, C0602), cells seeded on matrix in 24-well plates were washed twice with PBS and fixed for 15 min. Then cells were incubated with working solution of β-galactosidase plus X-Gal, and placed under the conditions of protection from light at 37 °C for 2 days. The senescent cells were observed via an optical microscope (CKX53) from 5 random fields of vision in each group and received means by statistical calculation. Each set of experiments was repeated three times.

## Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, 15596018) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantification was performed on a NanoDrop 8000 spectrophotometer (Thermo scientific), and 500 ng of total RNA per sample was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara, RR037A). cDNA was amplified using SYBR kits (Takara, DRR820A) on CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad). All samples were run in duplicate, and the results were normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as a relative mRNA level. The primer sequences are provided in Table  .
 

## Reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection
CellROX (Thermo, C10491) was used to determine ROS combining with flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman). Performed according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, a final concentration of 1 µM of CellROX was added to each sample and incubated for 60 min at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in the dark. Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CellROX was measured from three independent experiments.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde fix solution (PFA; biosharp, BL539A) for 15 min, and incubated with PBS containing 0.25% Triton X-100 (Diamond, A110694-0100) for 10 min. Then the cells were blocked with 10% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Beyotime, ST023-200 g) for 30 min. Following blocking, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies listed in Table  overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the cells were incubated with secondary antibodies for 60 min at 37 °C and visualized under Zeiss 880 Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope with Airyscan (ZEISS, Germany).

## Atomic force microscopy
The specimens were embedded with optimal cutting temperature (OCT) and made into frozen sections. The height and phase signals of frozen tissue slice were recorded by atomic force microscopy (AFM, USA) which was used to measure the Young’s modulus. AFM software NanoScope version 1.4 was then used to analyze optical, structural and electrical characterization of the acquired images.

## Statistical analysis
All data were obtained from at least three independent experiments and expressed as mean ± SD. Sample sizes were indicated in the figure legend. Statistical analysis between two groups was performed using an unpaired t-test. Multiple-group statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the Tukey multiple comparison test. Prism software (GraphPad) was used for data analysis. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels were defined as p < 0.05 (*), p < 0.01 (**), p < 0.001 (***), p < 0.0001 (****).

## Results

## High matrix stiffness promotes AEC II senescence
To mimic the pathological phenomenon of increased stiffness of the extracellular matrix in pulmonary fibrosis, we cultured AEC IIs on polyacrylamide hydrogel. Senescence-related β-Gal (SA-β-Gal) staining and dual fluorescence staining of surfactant protein C (SPC) and P16 showed that high matrix stiffness promotes AEC II senescence (Fig.  A). Furthermore, a serial of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)-related genes IL-6, IL-8, Tgf-β, and Mmp1 were all significantly increased in AEC IIs cultured on high matrix stiffness (Fig.  B). Cell senescence is usually characterized by cell cycle arrest, and therefore, the cycle arrest proteins P16 and P21 are highly expressed. Western blot analysis revealed that the protein levels of P16 and P21 were significantly increased in AEC IIs cultured on stiff matrix compared to those cultured on soft matrix (Fig.  C). Additionally, we assessed reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels in AEC IIs cultured on different matrix stiffness conditions by flow cytometry. The results showed that a significant increase in ROS levels in AEC IIs cultured on a stiff matrix compared to those on a soft matrix (Fig.  D). Taken together, the results indicated that cellular senescence is related to the stiffness of extracellular matrix, and an increase in mechanical signals promotes cellular senescence.
 

## Pulmonary fibrosis promotes AEC II senescence
Therefore, we constructed a mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis to verify that extracellular matrix stiffness promotes cellular senescence. We established an experimental pulmonary fibrosis model by using intratracheally administered bleomycin to male C57BL/6J mice for three weeks. Histology assessment revealed matrix deposition can be observed on the 7th day and progressed forming extensive interstitial fibrosis in the following two weeks. An elevated signal of α-SMA immunoreactivity was observed in the fibrotic region (Fig.  A). Through dual immunofluorescence staining of SPC and P16 in the control and BLM group, it was found that the number of P16 positive cells in the AEC IIs population of the BLM group significantly increased on day 21, and P16 was co-localized with cells expressing SPC (Fig.  B). Next, we compared the elastic modulus between control and BLM groups, and observed a significant increased Young’s modulus in BLM groups (Fig.  C). In addition, we observed the accumulation of collagen and α-SMA, an increase in senescent cells, and decrease in lamin A/C levels in clinical IPF patients samples (Fig.  D). Cell senescence was significantly increased with increased matrix stiffness, indicating that there was a strong correlation between cellular senescence and matrix stiffness in pulmonary fibrosis.
 

## High matrix stiffness reduces lamin A/C protein levels in AEC IIs
The nucleus is a crucial mechanical transduction element, and its stiffness plays an important role in cellular stress. As is well known, lamin A/C contributes to nuclear stiffness, but lamin B has little or no effect on the nuclear stiffness [ ]. Here, we measured the mRNA and protein levels of lamin A/C and lamin B1. Compared with soft matrix, the mRNA levels of Lmna and Lmnb1 were not significantly decreased in AEC IIs cultured on stiff matrix (Fig.  A). Then, western blot analysis showed that compared to AEC IIs cultured on soft matrix, the protein levels of lamin A/C in AEC IIs were significantly reduced, while lamin B1 was not significantly reduced (Fig.  B). Additionally, we also detected the lamin A/C protein using immunofluorescence analysis, and results showed that the protein levels of lamin A/C were decreased on high matrix stiffness (Fig.  C). We speculated that the mechanical signal might reduce the levels of lamin A/C. Therefore, we transferred the AEC IIs that had been cultured on high matrix stiffness for two days to soft matrix stiffness, and found that the protein levels of lamin A/C were partially restored. This further indicates that matrix stiffness could regulate the levels of lamin A/C (Fig.  D). We further validated the role of lamin A/C in cellular senescence by constructing lamin A/C knockdown and overexpression systems, and found that the levels of lamin A/C were negatively correlated with cellular senescence (Fig.  E-F).
 

## High matrix stiffness reduces lamin A/C protein levels in AEC IIs by the autophagy-lysosome pathway
The intracellular protein degradation system is mainly responsible for the degradation and clearance of abnormal proteins, including the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and the autophagy-lysosome pathway (ALP) [ ]. Among them, UPS has been reported to the main pathway for the intracellular protein degradation. Thus, we treated AEC IIs with different inhibitors, including MG-132 (a proteasome inhibitor), bafilomycin A1 (Baf, a autophagy inhibitor), and chloroquine (CQ, a autophagy inhibitor), and measured the levels of lamin A/C by western blot. The results showed that there was no significant difference in lamin A/C protein levels in AEC IIs cultured on the soft matrix, while in higher matrix stiffness, the addition of autophagy inhibitors (Baf or CQ), restored the levels of lamin A/C (Fig.  A). The results indicated that on high matrix stiffness, AEC IIs significantly reduced the levels of lamin A/C through autophagy lysosome pathway. Furthermore, immunofluorescence assay was used to observe the co-localization of lysosome-associated membrane protein-1 and − 2 (LAMP-1/LAMP-2) with lamin A/C on high matrix stiffness (Fig.  B). After CQ treatment, the MFI of lamin A/C was significantly restored in ACE IIs cultured on high matrix stiffness, indicating that high matrix stiffness could degrade lamin A/C through the autophagy-lysosome pathway (Fig.  C).
 

## CQ attenuated the effect of high matrix stiffness on AEC II senescence
As we have known in previous experiments, high matrix stiffness activated the autophagy-lysosome pathway. We further investigate whether inhibiting the autophagy-lysosome pathway can alleviated cellular senescence. On high matrix stiffness, the number of SA-β-Gal positive cells significantly increased, while after treatment with CQ, the number of SA-β-Gal positive cells significantly decreased, indicating that CQ could delay senescence of AEC IIs caused by high matrix stiffness (Fig.  A). Next, we using immunofluorescence analysis to detect the the cycle arrest proteins P16 and P21. We observed that the high expression of P16 and P21 was significantly reduced on high matrix stiffness (Fig.  B-C). Additionally, the results of flow cytometry showed that CQ significantly reduced ROS levels of AEC IIs cultured on high matrix stiffness (Fig.  D), indicating that CQ could delay senescence of AEC IIs caused by high matrix stiffness. In summary, during the process of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, the extracellular matrix stiffness significantly increases. High matrix stiffness could accelerate the senescence of AEC lls by degrading lamin A/C protein through the autophagic lysosome pathway. By adding autophagy inhibitors, the homeostasis of AEC lls could be restored, thereby delaying cellular senescence (Fig.  ).
 

## Discussion
IPF is a chronic progressive lung disease. It is characterized by progressive scarring of lung tissues, destruction of alveolar structures, reduced lung compliance, impaired gas exchange, and ultimately respiratory failure or even death [ ]. Currently, the specific pathogenesis of IPF remains unclear, and most researchers believe it is associated with an interaction of factors, including environmental exposure, infections, genetics, aging, and others [ – ]. Although animal experiments cannot cover all the features of human IPF, they can still partially reproduce the disease’s pathological manifestations, which is essential for studying the underlying pathological mechanisms. Among all kinds of animal models of pulmonary fibrosis, the BLM model is the most widely used mouse model due to its favorable characteristics. BLM is a chemotherapeutic agent used to treat different types of tumors [ ]. Its most severe side effect is pulmonary toxicity, which causes lung remodeling and loss of lung function, rapidly leading to death. The mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis induced by BLM closely resembles the human IPF condition, which can lead to senescence of AEC IIs with extracellular matrix deposition [ ]. To elucidate the mechanisms underlying IPF and provide a foundation for clinical treatment, this study aims to employ a mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis induced by directly intratracheal injection of BLM. And the study focuses on investigating the mechanisms by which AEC II senescence promotes the progression of pulmonary fibrosis.
The incidence of IPF increases with age, and senescence is an independent risk factor for IPF progression [ ]. Cellular senescence and stem cell depletion are hallmarks of all age-related diseases [ ]. AEC IIs act as stem cells in the lungs playing a crucial role in maintaining alveolar homeostasis, immunity, and regeneration [ , ]. Senescent AEC IIs promote IPF by secreting elevated levels of a variety of proteins such as inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors and matrix metalloproteinases, known as SASP [ , ]. In our study, we observed that AEC IIs undergo deformation, flattening (differentiation towards AEC Is), and cellular senescence under high matrix stiffness conditions. Cellular senescence is manifested in cell cycle arrest, loss of cell replication, SA-β-gal accumulation, increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and oxidative damage (elevated ROS levels). In mouse models of pulmonary fibrosis and clinical samples of IPF patients, it was observed that the senescence of lung epithelial cells increased significantly with the severity of fibrosis, indicating that cellular senescence was positively correlated with pulmonary fibrosis. When the alveolar area is damaged, leading to the loss of a large number of alveolar epithelial cells, AEC IIs will accelerate their proliferation and differentiate into AEC Is, in an attempt to restore the integrity of the alveolar epithelium and reestablish the normal structure and function of the alveoli. When the proliferation or differentiation ability of AEC IIs is impaired due to AEC II senescence, their stem cell potential is gradually depleted, which is detrimental to maintaining lung homeostasis and repairing lung damage [ – ]. This impairment not only affects self-proliferation, but also hinders the differentiation of AEC IIs into AEC Is, resulting in senescence-associated differentiation disorder (SADD). In addition, highly purified AEC IIs have been reported to be incapable of proliferating [ , ] in vitro and spontaneously differentiating into AEC Is [ , – ]. The similar phenomenon was observed when we cultured primary AEC IIs.
Lamin A/C is a nuclear protein with a variety of functions that help maintain cellular structural stability [ ], cell motility [ ], mechanical sensing [ ], chromosomal organization [ ], gene expression [ ], cell differentiation [ ], DNA damage repair [ ], and telomere protection [ ]. Mutations in the lamin A/C gene, misprocessing of proteins, and lamin A/C dysregulation can lead to a variety of diseases [ ]. The nucleus is able to sense the surroundings environment and adjust the ratio of lamin A/C in the nucleoplasm, as well as modify the composition of the extracellular matrix, to either soften or stiffen the nucleus in response to external cues [ ]. And this ability to sense the cellular environment plays an important role in cell differentiation [ ]. Lamin A/C is involved in cellular senescence, thereby preventing cell division and growth [ ]. Increased expression of lamin A/C splice variants has been reported in pulmonary fibrosis [ , ]. Moreover, lamin A can be reduced by mutation of LNMA, substrate constraint, decrease of fluid shear stress and negative regulation by miR-124-3p [ – ]. In this study, we found that the levels of lamin A/C were reduced by the autophagy-lysosomal degradation pathway in senescent AEC IIs. Autophagy, a highly selective cellular clearance pathway associated with the maintenance of cellular tissue homeostasis, is a conserved degradation system in which cellular components such as proteins, organelles, and lipids are broken down in a lysosome-dependent manner [ ]. The alteration of autophagy within AEC IIs may also play an important role in IPF. It has been reported that AEC IIs in the lung tissue of IPF patients exhibit excessive endoplasmic reticulum stress, which promotes autophagy [ ]. Buemo et al. found that AEC IIs in IPF patients displayed obvious mitochondrial dysfunction, with reduced mitophagy triggering cellular senescence [ ]. In our study, we can alleviate AEC II senescence and inhibit the progression of pulmonary fibrosis through the use of autophagy inhibitors.
However, there are some limitations in our current study. First, the pathological mechanisms of lamin A/C in IPF is not well understood, and second, it remains unclear whether the progression of IPF can be blocked by targeting AEC II senescence. This may be the direction we need to study in the future.

## Conclusions
The main purpose of the article is to investigate the effect of alveolar microenvironment-extracellular matrix stiffness on the senescence of AEC IIs. It has found that high matrix stiffness promotes senescence of AEC IIs by degrading lamin A/C through the autophagy-lysosome pathway. Our research findings highlight the importance of lamin A/C in cellular senescence, while also indicating that mechanical factors play a crucial role in the regulation of dynamic changes in nuclear structure and function. The nucleus not only serves as an important genetic and regulatory center within the cell, but also regulates cellular senescence by altering the content of lamin A/C in response to mechanical stimuli.
