Among microRNA inhibitors antimiRs have been proven successful in lowering aberrant microRNA levels in the clinic. The tool compounds were taken up by a bronchial epithelial cell line and primary human bronchial epithelial cells, followed by efficient knockdown of miR-34a. ## Graphical abstract
 
Rydzik and colleagues investigated microRNAs, which are emerging therapeutic targets in chronic respiratory disease. They demonstrated differences between uptake, localization, and target engagement of chemically modified antimiRs in 2D and 3D lung epithelial cell models. In particular, apical administration of antimiRs in air-liquid interface culture paves the way toward development of inhaled microRNA therapeutics.

## Introduction
MicroRNAs (miRNA) are a class of small, non-coding RNAs involved in regulation of various cellular processes. , These endogenous molecules can downregulate target mRNA levels via RNA interference. In this process, mature miRNA is loaded into the RNA-induced silencing complex, where, in the final stages, it is recognized by Argonaut protein (Ago2). Specifically, Ago2 loaded with one of the miRNA strands (guide/passenger) can scan mRNA and recognize complementary sequences, usually present in the 3′ UTR of mRNA. This leads to binding and translational repression of target mRNA. A single miRNA is usually not specific for one target mRNA and usually regulates many targets at the same time, hence contributing to the regulation of whole cellular pathways leading to profound effects on cellular homeostasis. Therefore, miRNAs represent attractive, but complex, therapeutic targets. , , 
AntimiRs (AMO) are short synthetic oligonucleotides complementary or partially complementary to the guide strand of a target miRNA. They act as miRNA inhibitors and are designed to inhibit upregulated endogenous miRNA levels. This is a relatively unexplored class of oligonucleotide therapeutics compared with antisense oligonucleotides (ASO) or small interfering RNAs; however, seven AMO drug candidates are currently investigated in clinical trials. , , 
Chemical modifications are essential for oligonucleotide therapeutics and have rendered oligonucleotide therapies of clinical use, as they can alter potency, stability, and pharmacokinetic profile of the oligonucleotide in vivo. , , , Several chemical modification patterns have been applied in AMO design. , , , In particular, chemical modifications that enhance binding affinity to the target miRNA have been explored, allowing for the design of shorter AMOs. Moreover, modifications that increase nucleolytic stability are of high interest.
Targeting miRNAs may provide new therapeutic opportunities to treating chronic respiratory diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, or idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). , , miR-34a has been reported to be dysregulated in both COPD and IPF, , and it is functionally implicated in the regulation of cell senescence and lung fibrosis, which are key biological processes associated with both diseases. , , , In this study, we investigate six different synthetic miR-34a AMOs as tool compounds for miR-34a knockdown (KD) in different lung-relevant cell models. The immortalized human bronchial epithelial cell line Beas2B has been widely used as a lung epithelium in vitro cell model in a large number of studies associated with respiratory diseases. However, immortalized cell lines tend to show phenotypic instability during passaging and may not be biologically representative of the corresponding primary cells. For the purpose of functional target validation, it is imperative to perform experiments in human primary model systems. For this reason, we tested our chemically synthesized AMOs in primary human bronchial epithelial cells (HBECs), which enabled assessment of uptake and targeting in multiple donors under different growth conditions. Air-liquid interface (ALI) culturing of HBECs closely mimics airway physiology and, unlike conventional 2D model systems, ALI culturing allows for the differentiation and polarization of basal cells to form a stratified bronchial epithelial layer in vitro. Moreover, the apical side of these cultures are exposed to air, enabling administration of compounds in a similar manner to inhalation.

## Results

## AMO design and synthesis
In this work we utilized hsa-miR-34a-5p (miR-34a) as our target miRNA. For the AMO design, we have considered an approach with sequences that are complementary full length or part of the miR-34a guide strand ( A). All designs contained sequences fully complementary to the seed region of miR-34a (nucleotides 2–7). The seed region is an essential element of miRNAs, being responsible for the recognition of target mRNA. Many miRNAs retain complementarity to their mRNA targets in the seed region, while the rest of the sequence can be loosely or not complementary to the mRNA. To date, there are several reports published on the design of AMOs for both in vitro and in vivo use. , , , , , , These reports utilize several chemical modifications to ensure good potency and stability of the AMOs. In this work we chose to employ some of the most commonly used sugar and backbone oligonucleotide modifications, namely locked nucleic acids (LNAs), deoxyribose (DNA), 2′-methoxy modified ribose (2′OMe)`, and phosphorothioates (PS) in our designs ( ). Sugar modifications such as LNA and 2′OMe are associated with duplex stabilization, and LNAs, in particular, are associated with a strong increase in binding properties. Sugar-modified oligonucleotides are also more resistant to nucleolytic cleavage, although this protective effect is only partial. Moreover, replacing the phosphodiester (PO) linkage with PS leads to increased nucleolytic stability. However, this increase is at the expense of slightly lower binding affinity of PS-containing oligonucleotides compared with their PO versions. , 
In this study we have compared six different AMO designs ( B). Shorter AMOs (AMR_1F to AMR_4F) were extensively modified with LNAs to increase target binding affinity. AMR_1F was a fully LNA modified 15-mer with flanking regions containing PS modifications. AMR_2F also contained fully LNA-modified sugars and, additionally, the entire sequence was PS modified. The AMR-3F-adapted modification pattern from the miR-122 AMO, which was proven effective in a non-human primate study and consisted of alternating LNA and DNA modifications and a fully PS-modified backbone. AMR_4F represented a typical “LNA gapmer” design, often used in ASO. , In this design, three flanking nucleotides on the 5′ and 3′ ends are modified with LNA sugars and a PS backbone, while the middle region (“gap”) consists of DNA residues that can engage in RNAseH-mediated cleavage of ASO target duplex. Longer AMOs employed designs previously reported for miR-21 AMOs containing LNA and 2′OMe residues (AMR_5F). In addition, the design of AMR_6F included conjugation of cholesterol for improved cellular uptake. As control molecules, we used non-targeting sequences , based on AMR_1F and AMR_4F ( B). In all AMOs, we also introduced a fluorescent label (Cy5), allowing cellular uptake of AMOs to be monitored. Cy5 labels were introduced via amide linker to introduce as little structure bias on the AMO properties as possible. This was achieved employing N-hydroxysuccinimidyl ester of Cy5, which was coupled to amino-modified AMO under basic aqueous conditions ( C). We also compared the use of a bulkier linker (AMR_1DF) for the fluorophore conjugation ( ), and even though it had a very minimal effect of the final miR-34a KD ( ), uptake of more bulky conjugates was higher than the simple amide one ( ). This was proven to be the case for both Beas2Bs and in HBECs ( and ). Exact structures of chemical modifications, fluorophores, cholesterol, and linkers are shown in C and 1D.

## Evaluation of AMOs in 2D cell culture
Given the potential role of miR-34a in lung disease, we investigated the uptake of AMOs in three relevant respiratory in vitro models. We started our evaluation with the immortalized bronchial epithelial cell line, Beas2B. As this is a non-tumorigenic cell line, Beas2Bs are commonly used for in vitro studies of the human airway epithelium. Moreover, we continued to test the AMOs in primary human bronchial epithelial cells derived from four healthy donors. Cells were treated with 1 μM final concentration of each AMO for 6 and 48 h, upon which miR-34a KD was assessed by qRT-PCR. Maximal KD was reached already after 6 h with only minor changes in values after 48 h ( ). The AMO KD profile was similar between Beas2Bs and HBECs, with most AMOs showing efficient KD of more than 50%. Only the ASO-like design AMR_4F was not effective under these conditions. Four different HBEC donors were examined, with all showing very similar miR-34a KD values ( ). In the next step, uptake of AMOs was assessed. In general, all AMOs were taken up gymnotically by both Beas2Bs and HBECs and displayed very similar uptake patterns ( ). Only minor differences in uptake between the 6 and 48 h time points were observed. Cholesterol-modified AMR_6F had by far the highest uptake intensity, with almost 10-fold difference in fluorescence intensity compared with the other AMOs. For this AMO, 100% of both Beas2Bs and HBECs were AMO positive by 6 h. AMR_5F, which has same modification pattern as AMR_6F, although without cholesterol conjugation, displayed a much weaker uptake. Uptake of AMR_5F was more efficient in HBECs than in Beas2Bs, with 58% and 99% AMO positive cells at 6 and 48 h, respectively (29% and 57% AMO positive cells in Beas2Bs at 6 and 48 h, respectively). AMR_1F was taken up slower than other designs, with considerable differences in fluorescence intensity between the 6 and 48 h time points. In addition, not all cells were taking up AMR_1F (33% and 57% AMO-positive cells in Beas2Bs at 6 and 48 h, respectively, and 41% and 63% AMO-positive cells in HBECs at 6 and 48 h, respectively). Designs AMR_2F, AMR_3F, and AMR_4F displayed very similar uptake patterns with good intensity in both Beas2Bs and HBECs with 100% AMO-positive cells already at the 6 h time point. 
Finally, cellular distribution of the AMOs was assessed by confocal microscopy in Beas2Bs and HBECs ( ). AMOs were characterized by different cellular distribution patterns that did not change significantly over time. There was also no significant difference in distribution pattern of each AMO between Beas2Bs and HBECs. To enable comparison between AMOs, fluorescence intensity was adjusted individually for each image. Images adjusted with the same threshold and brightness are presented in . We then assessed the subcellular localization of AMOs by co-staining for lysosomal and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) markers and using Hoechst DNA stain to identify the nuclear compartment ( ). The cholesterol derivative AMR_6F showed very high intensity and was widely distributed in the cytoplasm including occasional foci of concentrated AMO staining, some colocalizing with the lysosom ( ). AMR_6F was also associated with various membranes, whereas no nuclear localization was observed. In striking contrast, all other AMOs showed varying degrees of nuclear distribution. Designs AMR_2F, 3F, and 4F expressed strong nuclear presence, along with punctate structures in the cytoplasm, suggesting partial colocalization with the lysosomal marker ( ). The AMOs AMR_1F and 5F showed weaker nuclear signals, suggesting differential subcellular distribution. The AMOs did not appear to localize within the ER. 

## Evaluation of AMOs in 3D cell culture
ALI culture of primary HBECs is increasingly being recognized as an important in vitro cell model that enables physiologically relevant respiratory research. HBECs cultured at the ALI undergo extensive mucociliary differentiation, resulting in a model system that closely resembles the in vivo airway. In this work, we evaluated three AMOs in HBECs grown as ALI cultures. AMR1_F, 4F, and 6F were chosen as they represented different patterns in the 2D cell culture models. In 2D models, AMR_1F was the most active AMO, with lower uptake and mainly cytoplasmic distribution. AMR_4F represented the common ASO design, but was inactive in 2D culture demonstrating strong nuclear localization. Finally, AMR_6F had moderate to high activity and increased uptake with wide cytoplasmic distribution.
To mimic dosing by inhalation, the AMOs were administered to the apical side of the ALI cultures and incubated for 48 h ( A) followed by determination of miR-34a KD or imaging to assess AMO distribution (the additional 6 h time point is depicted in ). As ALI cultures, like the in vivo bronchial epithelium, show a pseudostratified morphology with multiple layers of cells growing over each other, it was not possible to properly quantify AMO uptake. When assessing the activity of the AMOs in the ALI cultures, AMR_6F was the most active and almost completely abolished miR-34a levels. AMR_1F, which was the most active in the 2D cell models, displayed only moderate (50% KD) activity in the ALI culture. As observed in the 2D models, AMR_4F was inactive ( B). The cholesterol derivative AMR_6F again showed cytoplasmic distribution, with a portion of the compound bound to cellular membranes. A slightly different distribution was observed for the other two AMOs compared with the 2D system. AMR_1F exhibited increased nuclear localization, while AMR_4F was mainly cytoplasmic. 

## Discussion
miRNAs are endogenous molecules regulating around 60% of the whole genome and have recently been gaining attention as therapeutic targets. , , , To inhibit miRNA levels, oligonucleotide-based miRNA inhibitors (antimiRs/AMOs) can be employed. Currently, seven AMO molecules are under investigation in clinical trials for various indications. Still, compared with other therapeutic oligonucleotide modalities, AMOs are less-prominent agents with fewer published studies with regard to their design and characterization. We investigated the properties of six different AMO designs targeting miR-34a and validated them in three different in vitro cell models widely employed in the field of respiratory research: the bronchial epithelial cell line Beas2B, submerged HBEC 2D cultures, and 3D HBEC ALI cultures. This is, to our knowledge, the first publication dedicated to profile comprehensively the potency, the uptake, and subcellular localization of AMOs with varying chemistry in lung epithelial cell models. Unmodified nucleic acids, including short oligonucleotides, usually demonstrate low to moderate permeability through cellular membranes due to their highly anionic character. Cellular uptake of oligonucleotides can be achieved through various strategies including external carriers or transfection reagents. Oligonucleotide therapeutics can be also chemically modified within the sequence itself or by conjugation to appropriate molecules to enhance their gymnotic uptake by the cell. In this work, we explored common chemical modifications used in RNA therapeutics, namely sugar and PO backbone modifications compared side-by-side with cholesterol conjugation, to modulate cellular uptake. This was then correlated with miR-34a KD efficiency, thereby providing insight into key features of successful AMO development, namely compound exposure and target engagement. In addition, cholesterol conjugation was investigated. Uptake of AMOs was then correlated with miR-34a KD efficiency.
AMOs were designed based on known modifications and previously published , , , , data employing similar modification patterns. Internal modifications were LNA, 2′OMe ribose, and PS, all of which have been associated with increased stability of oligonucleotide therapeutics to nucleolytic cleavage resulting in longer half-life, especially in the in vivo setting. Chemically modified oligonucleotides show different binding to the complementary oligonucleotide strand. Here, LNA modification is of particular interest, as it is able to significantly stabilize duplex interactions and is therefore commonly used to improve the binding affinity of oligonucleotide therapeutics. , We utilized a varied number of LNA modifications in all our designs, creating AMOs that bound very strongly to the target miR-34a. Use of fully or highly LNA-modified sequences allowed for very efficient inhibition even with designs shorter than the target miRNA (AMR_1F, 2F, and 3F). In contrast, introducing a weaker binding DNA region in design AMR_4F resulted in no activity against the target, despite effective cellular uptake to different cellular localizations (mainly nuclear in 2D and cytoplasmic in 3D cell systems). This highlights the need for multiparameter optimization of AMOs, considering both physicochemical properties and potency arising from the specific chemistries used. Phosphorothioate backbone modification is a common approach to altering the overall chemical properties of therapeutic oligonucleotides providing good nuclease stability as well as increased hydrophobicity and protein binding. Importantly, we observed that phosphorothioate content was key for gymnotic cellular uptake and determined cellular localization and trafficking to the nucleus. Phosphorothioate-driven internalization is a known phenomenon believed to occur via binding to cell surface proteins, possibly driven by disulfide exchange. , Fully phosphorothiolate-modified designs AMR_2F, 3F, and 4F showed similar cellular distribution patterns and were not affected much by other modifications such as LNAs. AMR_1F, which has a lower phosphorothioate content was not internalized as efficiently as fully phosphorothiolate-modified designs; however, it displayed very good activity in the 2D model systems, which might be due to its more cytoplasmic localization. AMOs are known to act mainly in the cytoplasm; however, there is some evidence that they could bind to pre- and pri-miRNA. The functional effects arising from miRNA inhibition at these stages have not been quantified and their exact contribution toward overall miRNA inhibition remains unknown. Therefore, it could be that increased nuclear localization may result in reduced miRNA KD efficiency.
Consistent with this interpretation, in the ALI system, where AMR_1F localized more to the nucleus, its activity was also diminished. High cellular uptake might, however, not be directly correlated with activity of the AMO. The cholesterol-modified design AMR_6F was taken up over 10 times more efficiently than other AMOs, displaying a very distributed cytoplasmic localization; however, it had equal or lower activity compared with other designs in 2D culture systems. It has been previously observed that cholesterol-conjugated oligonucleotides have higher uptake efficiency than their unconjugated counterparts, , and this may be due to tight association with various membrane proteins and/or proteins involved in endosomal trafficking. Tight association with membranes may facilitate release of conjugates from endosomal compartments. On the other hand, organelle and cellular membrane association of cholesterol conjugates might also contribute to its lower availability for target inhibition.
Our observations confirm and extend reported properties of chemical modifications of therapeutic oligonucleotides and prove their translatability to the AMO system. However, the effects of the different modifications are highly dependent on the cellular system used. In this work we investigated the same parental cells (derived from the same donors) grown in submerged 2D or 3D ALI cell culture. In the latter system, cells are exposed to air on the apical side, which allows them to differentiate and form a stratified epithelial cell layer, similar to that seen in vivo. In this system, we employed a method for dosing AMOs to the air-exposed apical side, thereby mimicking in vitro an inhaled therapeutic administration. Future studies will benefit from the possibility to compare the apical delivery of an AMO with basolateral treatment of epithelial cells grown in the ALI culture model and then use these findings for building a rationale for selecting a compound administration route. We then related AMO KD activity to subcellular distribution as assessed by imaging. In this model, the cholesterol modified AMR_6F was a very efficient inhibitor, outperforming AMR_1F, which was the strongest inhibitor in the 2D models. The subcellular distribution of AMR_1F and AMR_4F also appeared different in ALI cultures compared with the 2D model system. This might be related to the presence of specialized differentiated cells in the ALI culture, with the apical administration mainly targeting ciliated and goblet cells, whereas the 2D system mainly consists of airway basal cells, and these cell types commonly have different cellular uptake mechanisms and transport machineries. , , Furthermore, the differentiated and polarized cells have specialized lipids that might affect the cytosolic localization and activity of the cholesterol modified AMR_6F. , , A possible drawback for the work presented here is the use of primary cells from healthy individuals and without any treatment that could mimic disease, leading to low levels of the target miRNA. Nevertheless, the possibility of studying the effects of miRNA inhibition through gymnotic uptake of AMOs removes the risk of unwanted effects related to a transfection reagent. Despite the more commonly used basolateral treatment of ALI cells, our findings highlight the ability of these cells to efficiently take up AMOs through apical administration and emphasize the need to understand subcellular localization and uptake of AMOs, or other therapeutic oligonucleotides, when aiming to target specific cell types through specific routes of administration.
More studies correlating or comparing observations related to AMO uptake and effect in vitro and in vivo are needed to guide design, formulation, administration, and dosing of these compounds. For AMOs developed for respiratory diseases, the use of primary cells, and epithelial cells grown at ALI, needs to be expanded to better reflect the human airways. However, epithelial ALI cultures are still lacking in the complexity and cell-cell contact and communication that may be altered by compound administration in the airways. To date, the translatability of AMOs administrated in ALI culture to dosing in murine or human models is relatively unknown. Recent emerging data in cystic fibrosis models aiming for utilization of inhaled ASOs as therapeutics may give early indications of methodology and translatability in in vitro and in vivo models (E.O.-G., unpublished data). However, the field is still young and translatability from in vitro to in vivo models remains a challenge.
In this study, we employed different strategies of AMO design such as variation of sequence length, backbone chemistry, and conjugation (cholesterol) to investigate AMO uptake and potency in respiratory disease-relevant 2D and 3D in vitro model systems. The emergence of high-throughput workflow solutions for fluorescence imaging and gene expression analysis enables screening of large numbers of AMO molecules, and our work shows the importance of having information for target KD, compound uptake, and subcellular distribution to guide AMO design. 2D cell models may have a feasibility advantage, but our data clearly demonstrate the importance of including physiologically more relevant 3D models in the AMO development process. For instance, the employment of lung epithelium 3D ALI cultures in screen cascades for the development of inhaled AMOs holds the promise of becoming a very informative step before introducing even more complex screening systems such as precision cut lung slices or lung explants and in vivo models.

## Materials and methods

## 2D cell culture methods
Beas2B cells (ATCC, no. 75007) were grown in DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Gothenburg, Sweden, no. 11039047) supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and penicillin (100 U/mL) and streptomycin (100 U/mL). Primary normal HBECs (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland, no. CC-2541) were grow in BEGM Bronchial Epithelial Cell Growth Medium (Lonza, no. CC-3170) in submerged 2D culture.
Cells were plated at a density of 10,000 cells/well in 96-well plates for imaging (PerkinElmer, no. 6055300) or RNA isolation (Corning, no. 3595, Darmstadt, Germany). AM_1 and AM_1F were used as a model AMOs in Beas2B cells. The AMO was added directly to cell culturing medium and uptake and miR-34a expression levels were examined at different concentrations and time points ( and ). A final concentration of 1 μM for 6 or 48 h at 37°C with 5% CO2 was chosen for further experimentation. After the stated time points, the cell culture medium was removed, and cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (VWR, no. 9713.1000) for imaging or 100 μL TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) was added for RNA extraction.

## ALI epithelial cell culture and stimulation
Primary normal HBECs were expanded to passage 3, seeded onto 0.4 μm Corning HTS Transwell 24-well permeable supports with open culture reservoirs underneath (Corning, nos. CLS3378 and CLS3395) and differentiated at ALI using the PneumaCult media system (STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.
Once fully differentiated (after 4 weeks at ALI) cells were washed by incubation with DPBS with Ca2+ and Mg2+ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, no. 14040091) apically for 2 h followed by aspiration of the wash buffer. Immediately after washing, 25 μL of AMO diluted to 1 μM in DPBS was added apically and incubated for 6–48 h before cells were fixed or lysed for RNA isolation. As negative control, DPBS only was used.

## RNA extraction and qPCR analysis
Total RNA was extracted from the cells with TRIzol Reagent followed by the Zymo Direct-zol 96 kit (Zymo Research, Freiburg im Breisgau, Germany, no. R2057) following the manufacturer’s protocols. The yield of extracted RNA was determined by NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Sweden). RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, no. 4366596) according to the supplier’s instructions. Real-time PCR (using TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix and MicroRNA Assays, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Applied Biosystems, nos. 4444557 and 4440887) was performed for miR-34a (hsa-miR-34a, Assay ID: 000426) and RNU48 (RNU48, Assay ID: 001006; reference) on a QuantStudio 7 Flex (Thermo Fisher Scientific) qPCR machine. The comparative 2−ΔΔCt method was used to analyze fold changes between control and AMO treatment, which was calculated ratio = 2−(ΔCt treatment − ΔCt control), where Ct is the cycle threshold and ΔCt (Ct target − Ct reference) is the Ct value normalized to the reference gene (RNU48) obtained for the same cDNA samples. Each experiment was performed in at least biological triplicate with technical replicates.

## Staining of 2D cell culture models (Beas2B and HBECs)
After AMO treatment, cells were washed briefly twice with PBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, no. 10010015) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. The cells were permeabilized with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, no. T8787) and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, no. A7030) for 1 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies (LAMP1, dilution 1:400 [Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, no. 9091] and PDI, dilution 1:600 [Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, no. MA3-019]) were incubated for 1 h at room temperature, followed by three washes with PBS. The secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594, diluted 1:500 [Invitrogen, cat. no. A-11037] and goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488, diluted 1:500 [Invitrogen, cat. no. A-32723]) were incubated for 1 h in room temperature, followed by three washes with PBS. The plates were sealed with black plate sealers (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MAcat., no. 6050173) and stored at 4°C until analysis. The imagining was done using a Yokogawa CV7000 confocal microscope and image and data analysis were carried out using Columbus (PerkinElmer) and R. 

## Staining and mounting of ALI epithelial cell cultures
After treatment as described above, ALI cultures were fixed by treatment with 4% paraformaldehyde on both apical and basolateral sides for 15 min followed by washing with PBS. Staining of ALI cultures was performed as described for HBECs with solutions added apically. The permeable supports with fixed and stained cells were then cut out using a scalpel blade and mounted onto microscope slides using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific, no. P36930) and covered with cover slips. Slides were cured at room temperature for 24 h and then stored at 4°C until analysis. Imaging was performed using a Zeiss 880 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) in Airyscan mode and analysis was performed with same tools as for 2D cell culture models.

## Oligonucleotide synthesis and purification
Oligonucleotides were synthesized on solid phase using a K&A Laborgeräte H8 SE oligonucleotide synthesizer. The synthesis was performed according to standard procedures on a 1 μmol scale using 0.1 M in acetonitrile phosphoramidites (Sigma-Aldrich) and CUTAG CPG support (Sigma-Aldrich). Detritylation was achieved by using 3% dichloroacetic acid in dichloromethane (Sigma-Aldrich). Activator 42 (Sigma-Aldrich) was used for coupling steps. Oxidation conditions were pyridine/water/iodine 9/1/12.7 (v/v/w). Thiolation was performed using xanthane hydride (0.2 M) (TCI Chemicals). Sequences were synthesized with removal of the final dimethoxytrityl group. At the end of the synthesis, cyanoethyl deprotection was performed on resin with diethylamine/acetonitrile (20% v/v) (Sigma-Aldrich). Variations from standard procedure were observed when amino-modified oligonucleotide or 5′-cholesterol were present. 5′-Amino-modified oligonucleotides were obtained using 5′-Amino-Modifier C6 (Glen Research) at the final coupling step following the standard procedure. The monomethoxytrityl (MMT) protecting group was not removed prior to cyanoethyl deprotection, cleavage, and purification, but later in solution once the oligonucleotide was purified. This avoids formation of the cyanoethyl-amino adduct and simplifies purification with improved separation. The incorporation of the 5′-cholesterol modification was done using 5′-cholesteryl-TEG phosphoramidite (Glen Research) at the final coupling step following the standard procedure. Structures of non-standard phosphoramidites are summarized in . After synthesis, the oligonucleotides were cleaved from the resin and deprotected for 15 h at 55°C using aqueous ammonia (35%) (1 mL/1 μmol). The crude oligonucleotide was then evaporated to dryness in a Speedvac vacuum concentrator. Purification of the crude oligonucleotides was performed by RP HPLC and pure fractions were freeze dried. The column was an XBridge C18, 5 μm 19 × 150 mm with mobile phase A: 60 mM DBuAA (H2O/ACN 95/5) [pH 7]; and phase B: 60 mM DBuAA in ACN. The gradient was adapted to each sequence. Precipitation was achieved by addition of a 3 M NaOAc solution at pH 5.2 (10% of volume) to an aqueous solution of the oligonucleotides. EtOH was then added (80% of total volume) and the mixture placed at −20°C. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation, dried in a Speedvac vacuum concentrator, and dissolved in water for subsequent use.
Oligonucleotides containing MMT protected 5′-amino-modifier C6 were taken up in aqueous solution to a final concentration of 0.5 mM and pH adjusted to 4.5 with acetic acid. Solution was then incubated for 1 h at 40°C and then pH was adjusted to 7 with 3 M NaOH. Solution of 3 M sodium acetate was added to the mixture (10% of volume), followed by ethanol (4× of the mixture volume). The mixture was cooled to −80°C for 1 h and then centrifuged. Precipitate was collected by centrifugation and dried over reduced pressure.

## Oligonucleotide labeling
Amino-modified oligonucleotide (300 μL of 1 mM solution in water) was taken up in 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (600 μL [pH 7.4]). Sulfo-cyanine 5 N-hydroxysuccinimide ester (Glen Research, structure in ) was dissolved in DMSO to final concentration of 50 mM and 30 μL was added to the solution of oligonucleotide. The mixture was mixed for 1 h at 30°C and then treated with 100 μL of 3 M sodium acetate solution, followed by 5 mL of ethanol. The mixture was cooled to −80°C for 1 h and then centrifuged. Precipitate was collected by centrifugation and dried over reduced pressure. Conjugate was purified by RP HPLC. The column was XBridge C18, 5 μm 10 × 100 mm with mobile phase A: 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate in water (pH 8.0); and phase B: methanol or acetonitrile. Preparation of AMR_1DF is described in the supplemental information and depicted in .

## Characterization of oligonucleotides
The concentration was determined by absorbance measurements using a NanoDrop. Purity determination and high-resolution mass analysis were performed on a Waters BioAccord LC-MS equipped with TUV and ACQUITY RDa Detectors. The column was an XBridge BEH C18, 3.5 μm 2.1 × 150 mm with mobile phase A: 10 mM TBuAA in MeCN/water 1:9; and phase B: MeCN. The gradient was 20%–70% B over 8 min. The purity was estimated at 260 nm. The spectra and mass data are included in the supplemental information ( ).
