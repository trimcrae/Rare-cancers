## Abstract

## Background
Neuropathic pain involves neuroinflammation and upregulation of glycolysis in the central nervous system. Unfortunately, few effective treatments are available for managing this type of pain. The overactivation of lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), an essential enzyme in glycolysis, can cause neuroinflammation and nociception. This study investigated the spinal role of LDHA in neuropathic pain.

## Method
Using immunohistochemical analysis, nociceptive behavior, and western blotting, we evaluated the cellular mechanisms of intrathecal administration of LDHA inhibitors, including FX11 and oxamate, in chronic constriction injury (CCI)-induced neuropathic rats.

## Result
FX11 and oxamate attenuated CCI-induced neuronal LDHA upregulation and nociceptive sensitization. Moreover, CCI-induced neuroinflammation, microglial polarization, and angiogenesis were attenuated by LDHA inhibitors. These inhibitors regulate the TANK binding kinase-1 (TBK1)/hypoxia-inducible factor 1 subunit alpha (HIF-1α) axis, crucial for controlling inflammation and new blood vessel growth. Additionally, CCI-induced nuclear LDHA translocation, as associated with oxidative stress resistance, was attenuated by LDHA inhibitors.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, LDHA may be a potential therapeutic target for treating neuropathic pain by regulating neuroinflammation and angiogenesis.

## Background
Neuropathic pain affects over 7–10% of the global population, with its incidence growing because of an aging society [ , ]. Besides physical syndromes, patients with neuropathic pain often experience psychological distress, leading to a substantial burden on society via decreasing performance and productivity at work [ , ]. Current drugs provide some relief, with only a modest 20–40% clinical analgesic effect [ ] and have dose-limiting adverse effects on neuropathic pain [ , ]. This underscores the urgent need for novel, efficacious, and safe treatments for neuropathic pain.
Neuropathic or neuroinflammatory insults alter cellular metabolic pathways, specifically glycolysis [ – ]. Nociceptive sensitization is linked to upregulated glycolysis and mitochondrial dysfunction in the central nervous system (CNS) [ – ]. Moreover, neuropathic pain can reprogram the metabolic network from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis [ ]. Reducing glycolysis by silencing pyruvate kinase M2 expression [ ] or enhancing mitochondrial function by inhibiting pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase [ ] can alleviate neuropathic pain. Our previous study first found that ipsilateral spinal lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) is upregulated in CCI-induced neuropathy [ ]. Accompanied by the upregulation of glycolysis, the byproduct lactate generated by LDHA accumulates and causes acidification, neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, and nociception [ – ]. Miyamoto et al. indicated that intrathecal lactate injection into the spinal cord induces nociception [ ]. Neuropathic pain also increases spinal lactate levels [ , ]. Notably, LDHA plays both canonical and non-canonical enzymatic roles, maintaining glycolysis in the cytoplasm [ ] and regulating oxidative stress resistance in the nucleus [ ].
Moreover, LDHA mediates TANK binding kinase-1 (TBK1) phosphorylation by lactate [ , ]. TBK1 plays a vital role in angiogenesis [ ] and neuroinflammation-related diseases, including nociceptive sensitization [ , ]. Neuropathy-induced nociception is attenuated by TBK1 inhibition or gene knockout [ , ]. Furthermore, TBK1 is responsible for proinflammatory cytokine synthesis via the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinases pathways [ , ]. Neuroinflammation sustains chronic pain [ , ] by activating microglia and astrocytes, which releases ATP, chemokines, and neuropeptides to trigger nociceptive transmission and central sensitization [ ]. In addition, lactate induces the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor 1 subunit alpha (HIF-1α) [ ], which regulates neuroinflammation [ ] and angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelium growth factor (VEGF) [ ] and angiopoietin 2 (Ang2) [ ]. Angiogenesis regulates nutrient supply and oxygen-increasing processes [ , ]. Angiogenesis is upregulated in neuropathic states and neuroinflammatory disease [ – ]. Angiogenesis inhibition ameliorates neuropathic pain [ , ]. Neuroinflammation and angiogenesis are involved in the development and maintenance of neuropathic pain. However, the mechanisms linking LDHA, neuroinflammation, and angiogenesis in neuropathic pain remain unclear.
This study investigated the spinal role of LDHA in neuropathic pain by using LDHA inhibitors and detecting potential signaling pathways related to neuroinflammation, angiogenesis, and nociception in the ipsilateral spinal cord dorsal horn (SCDH).

## Materials and methods

## Animal
Adult male Wistar rats (BioLASCO Taiwan Co., Ltd., Taipei, Taiwan) weighing 250–285 g were subjected to a light/dark cycle (lights on at 8:00 AM). This study took place in a temperature-controlled environment maintained at 22 ± 1 °C and 70% humidity. Rats had free access to food and water and were housed in Plexiglas cages. For surgical and drug injection procedures, all rats were anesthetized using 2.5% isoflurane inhalation and a sterile formulation. Postoperative care included intraperitoneal administration of cefazolin (170 mg/kg) to prevent infection. All experimental protocols involving animals were approved by the National Sun Yat-sen University Institutional Committee for the Care and Use of Animals (Approval Nos. IACUC-10830 & 11,137) and complied with the guidelines set by the American Physiological Society. In addition, after completing the nociceptive behavior experiments, the rats were humanely euthanized, and their spinal cords were analyzed. Every effort was made to minimize both the number of animals used and their suffering. Only rats showing no signs of hematomas or paralysis were included in the study. This ensured that the observed biological and biochemical effects were not influenced by intrathecal catheterization or manipulation.

## Intrathecal catheter implantation and induction of neuropathic pain
We used the catheter implantation technique described by Yaksh and Rudy [ ] and previous work [ ]. Briefly, a catheter (PE5 tubing: 9 cm; inner diameter: 0.008 inches; outer diameter: 0.014 in) was inserted into the intervertebral space at the lumbar extension, drawn out, and affixed to the cranial surface of the head. Post-surgery, rats were allowed a 5-day recovery period in their home cages. Rats exhibiting severe nerve damage or cerebrospinal fluid hematomas were excluded from the study. After catheterization, rats underwent immediate control or CCI surgery on the right sciatic nerve. All surgeries were performed under anesthesia with 2.5% isoflurane inhalation. CCI was performed on the right sciatic nerve (middle part of the thigh), as described by Bennett and Xie [ ] and our previous study [ ]. For CCI, a 5 mm segment of the sciatic nerve was dissected and placed into four loosely ligated colons (chronic colon 4–0). A skin incision (at intervals of 1 mm) was made around the sciatic nerve, and the skin incision in each layer was sutured.

## Drug administration
LDHA inhibitors, namely FX11 (427,218, EMD Millipore, Temecula, CA, USA) and oxamate (A16532.06, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Heysham, UK), were administered intrathecally using a PE5 catheter. FX11 and oxamate were dissolved in a solution containing 30% (v/v) dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) (D2650, SIGMA-ALDRICH, St. Louis, MO, USA) and artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF), which includes 2.6 mM K+, 21.0 mM HCO3−, 151.1 mM Na+, 1.3 mM Ca2+, 3.5 mM glucose, 0.9 mM Mg2+, 2, 5 mM HPO42−, and 122.7 mM Cl−, respectively. DMSO was diluted with aCSF.

## Experimental Grouping
To investigate the patterns of CCI-induced nociception, we observed several time points during the experiment, including 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days (d) post-CCI. For testing of the acute analgesic effects of LDHA inhibitors, rats received intrathecal doses of 0.05, 0.1, 0.25, and 1 µg of FX11 or 1, 5, 10, 50, and 100 µg of oxamate in 10 µl of 30% DMSO post-CCI. Each dosage group included at least six rats.
FX11, a small-molecule LDHA inhibitor, downregulates aerobic glycolysis and lactate production [ ] and has shown therapeutic potential in cancer treatment [ – ]. Moreover, FX11 attenuates proinflammatory immune cell proliferation in rheumatoid arthritis management [ ]. Oxamate, the earliest documented small-molecule LDHA inhibitor discovered in 1959 [ ], can induce apoptosis during cancer treatment [ , ] and reduce serum lactate concentrations while increasing insulin secretion in diabetic mice [ ]. However, no recent clinical trials on FX11 or oxamate have been conducted [ , ]. The maximum daily dose of FX11 is approximately 2 mg/kg in mice with cancer [ ] and tuberculosis [ ]. In addition, the maximum daily dose of oxamate is approximately 750 mg/kg in mice with cancer [ ] and diabetes [ ]. Based on the equilibrium dosing conversion, the dosage used in rats is approximately two times less than that in the mice [ ]. The systemic-to-intrathecal administration conversion ratio is 100:1 [ ]. Collectively, the suggested maximum daily intrathecal doses of FX11 and oxamate in rats are 10 and 3750 µg/kg, respectively. Converting this to hourly intrathecal administration for rats with a body weight of 360 g in the present study, the dosages of FX11 and oxamate are 0.15 and 56.25 µg/h/rat, respectively. In addition, the 1-h 50% effective dose (ED50) for intrathecal FX11 and oxamate in rats with neuropathy were 0.14 and 6.47 µg/rat, respectively. Based on the suggested maximum intrathecal dosage, 1-h ED50, and the dose–response analgesic curve, 0.1 µg/h of FX11 and 5 µg/h of oxamate were selected for intrathecal administration in rats with CCI-induced neuropathy using an osmotic pump. Consequently, the LDHA inhibitors FX11 and oxamate have competitive and therapeutic potential for neuropathic pain management.
For the preventive analgesic effects test, FX11 (0.1 µg/µl/h), oxamate (5 µg/µl/h), or the vehicle (1 µl/h of) were chosen to be continuously infused intrathecally after CCI surgery with an osmotic pump (2001 and 2002, DURECT Corporation, Cupertino, CA, USA) immediately after CCI surgery. Rats were randomly divided into several groups: (I) the control group (rats without CCI surgery except intrathecal vehicle infusion), (II) the 7d group (rats received CCI and intrathecal vehicle infusion for 7 days), (III) the 14d group (rats received CCI and intrathecal vehicle infusion for 14 days), (IV) the 7d + FX11 group (rats received CCI and intrathecal 0.1 µg/µl/h of FX11 infusion for 7 days), (V) the 14d + FX11 group (rats received CCI and 0.1 µg/µl/h of FX11 infusion for 14 days), (VI) the 7d + oxamate group (rats received CCI and 5 µg/µl/h of oxamate infusion for 7 days), and (VII) the 14d + oxamate group (rats received CCI and 5 μg/μl/h of oxamate for 14 days).

## Thermal hyperalgesia assessment
The rats were placed in clear plastic cages on elevated glass platforms. We assessed thermal hyperalgesia using an IITC analgesiometer (IITC Inc., Woodland Hills, CA, USA), following the method described by Hargreaves et al. [ ]. The medial plantar surface of the right hind paw of rats was exposed to a low-intensity (active intensity = 25) through the glass platform. The mean paw withdrawal latency (PWL; in seconds) was calculated by averaging three positive test responses. Behaviors such as licking or rapid paw withdrawal were considered indicative of nociception. The maximum duration for PWL was set at 30 s.

## Mechanical allodynia assessment
The rats were placed in clear plastic cages with raised metal mesh floors to facilitate access to the hind paws. Mechanical allodynia was evaluated using von Frey filaments (Stoelting, Wood Dale, IL, USA), as described by Chaplan et al. [ ]. The center of the right hind paw was subjected to a vertical array of von Frey filaments with logarithmically increasing stiffness (0.2–10 g), applied until the filament bent slightly. The average paw withdrawal threshold (PWT; in grams) was determined using the "up-down" method described by Chaplan and was calculated from three positive test thresholds. Quick paw withdrawal or licking was considered a sign of nociception. The maximum threshold for PWT was set at 10 g.

## Spinal tissue preparation for western blotting
Rat ipsilateral lumbar spinal cords were collected from the control, 7d, 14d, 7d + FX11, 14d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, and 14d + oxamate groups through exsanguination under isoflurane anesthesia 7 or 14 d post-surgery. Immediately upon collection, the spinal samples were frozen and stored at -80 °C until analysis. The samples were homogenized in ice-cold isotonic homogenizer buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 0.32 M sucrose, 0.3 mM fresh phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and 10 mg/mL fresh leupeptin), then centrifuged at 1,000 × g at 4 °C for 30 min to separate supernatant and pellet. The supernatant was mixed with 1% Triton X-100 (v/v), stirred for 30 min at 4 °C, and then centrifuged at 20,000 g (4 °C) for 60 min, yielding the cytosolic and solubilized membrane protein fraction. The pellet was resuspended in hypotonic homogenizer buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.4), 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 0.3 mM fresh phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and 10 mg/mL of fresh leupeptin) to extract nuclear protein fractions [ , ]. Subsequently, lysates from the ipsilateral lumbar spinal cord were subjected to electrophoresis and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane, following established protocols [ ]. The PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. We used mouse monoclonal anti-CD86 (Sc-28347, Santa Cruz, Dallas, Texas, USA), rabbit monoclonal anti-LDHA (MBP2-67,483, Novus Biologicals, LLC, Centennial, CO, USA), rabbit polyclonal anti-Malondialdehyde (MDA, ab6463, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), rabbit polyclonal anti-SOD1 (ab13498, Abcam), and rabbit monoclonal anti-pTBK1 (5483, Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA). The membranes were then incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for one hour at 37˚C, and detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (Trident femto Western HRP substrate, GTX14698, GeneTex). Densitometry was utilized to analyze the density of bands relative to the background. HRP-conjugated mouse monoclonal anti-β-actin (12,262, Cell Signaling) and mouse monoclonal anti-PCNA (GB12010, Servicebio, Wuhan, China) served as internal controls. Images were captured using the ChemiDocTM Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), and relative densitometric quantification was performed using Image Lab 6.1 (Bio-Rad).

## Immunofluorescence assay
The immunohistochemistry protocol and image quantification were carried out as described in our previous studies [ , , ]. Briefly, 7 and 14 d post-CCI injury, rats were terminally anesthetized with isoflurane and sacrificed via transcardial perfusion with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) containing heparin (200 U/mL), followed by 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS. To minimize variations in immunohistochemical procedures, lumbar spinal tissues from various groups were embedded in the same OCT compound (Sakura Finetek, Torrance, CA, USA), sectioned into 20 µm-thick sections using cryostat Microm HM550 (Microm International GmbH, Waldorf, Germany), mounted serially on microscope slides, and processed for immunofluorescence studies. Sections were blocked with a buffer containing 0.2% BSA, 0.3% Triton X-100, 0.05% Tween 20, and 1X PBS for an hour, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: mouse monoclonal anti-GFAP (MAB3402, Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), goat polyclonal anti-Iba1 (Ab5076, Abcam), mouse monoclonal anti-CD86 antibody (Sc-28347, Santa Cruz), rabbit monoclonal anti-LDHA (MBP2-67,483, Novus Biologicals, LLC), rabbit polyclonal anti-Ang2 (ab125692, Abcam), rabbit polyclonal anti-CD31 (ab25490, Abcam), rabbit polyclonal anti-HIF-1α (GTX127309, GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA), rabbit polyclonal anti-SOD1 (ab13498, Abcam), and rabbit monoclonal anti-pTBK1 (5483, Cell Signaling). After incubating in the blocking buffer, the sections were incubated with a mixture of Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated mouse monoclonal anti-NeuN (MAB377X, Millipore), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibody (715–545-151, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, USA), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG antibody (711–546-152, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories), Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-goat IgG antibody (705–546-147, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories), Cy™ 3-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibody (715–165-151, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories), Cy™ 3-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG antibody (711–166-152, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories), DAPI (D21490, Invitrogen), or DRAQ5 (ab108410, Abcam) for 40 min at room temperature. Imaging was performed using a Leica DM-6000B fluorescence microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) equipped with a K5 CMOS camera and Leica Application Suite X 3.8.1.26810 software (Leica Microsystems BV, Rijswijk, The Netherlands). The same exposure times and acquisition parameters were used for all sections on a slide. Fiji 2.14.0, an ImageJ program with a plugin (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA), was utilized for pixel counting and analysis [ ]. Immunofluorescence quantification spanned laminae I to III of the ipsilateral spinal cord dorsal horn (SCDH), with data averaged from three rats per group. Confocal images were captured using a Leica TCS SP5-II confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) equipped with a Leica HyD (hybrid detector). Representative images were taken at 100X, and 400X, and co-localization images at 400X or 630X magnification.

## Co-localization of two immunofluorescence signals
Co-localization analysis was performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (Francois-Moutal et al., 2015), with quantification based on an intensity correlation coefficient method utilizing Fiji. Pearson’s coefficient values < 0.1 were interpreted as indicating no co-localization, while values > 0.5 suggested near maximal co-localization [ ]. To quantify LDHA expression in the nucleus, ImageJ software incorporating the JACoP plugin was used to calculate the Manders coefficient. This coefficient measures the proportion of the fluorescent signal in one channel that overlaps with the signal in another channel and is widely used to assess co-localization between different channels [ ]. The total intensity of DAPI in each image was normalized to 100%, and the proportion of DAPI overlapping with the LDHA signal was calculated for each group [ ].

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEMs). Changes in protein levels and immunofluorescence reactivity are reported relative to the control, 7d, and 14d groups. Differences between groups were analyzed using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a Bonferroni post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SigmaPlot Version 15.0 (Systat Software, Inc., San Jose, CA, USA).

## Results

## CCI induces LDHA expression in the ipsilateral spinal cord dorsal horn
Using immunofluorescence, we examined changes in LDHA immunoreactivity in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH following CCI (Fig.  a). Quantification at various time points— 3, 7, 14, 21, and 28 d—revealed significant upregulation of LDHA protein expression by CCI at 7 and 14 d, peaking at 14 d (Fig.  b). However, LDHA expression began to decline at 21 d. Collectively, CCI induced a significant increase in LDHA expression from 7 to 14 d post-surgery.
 

## The analgesic effect of LDHA inhibitors, FX11, and oxamate, on CCI-induced nociceptive sensitizations
The acute analgesic effects of LDHA inhibitors on CCI-induced nociception were evaluated in rats. They were randomly allocated into several groups and intrathecally injected with varying doses of FX11 (0.05, 0.1, 0.25, or 1 µg) or oxamate (1, 5, 10, 50, or 100 µg). Antithermal hyperalgesia behavioral tests were conducted to assess analgesic efficacy. Paw withdrawal latency was converted to the percentage of maximum possible effect (MPE%). Figure  a shows that FX11 exhibited an effective acute analgesic effect, indicating therapeutic potential for CCI-induced thermal hyperalgesia, with the pharmacodynamic (PD) effect of FX11 lasting for 24 h. The dose–response curves for FX11 were then converted into the 24-h area under the curve (AUC), demonstrating a dose-dependent analgesic effect (Fig. S1a). Similarly, Fig.  b illustrates that oxamate also displayed an effective acute analgesic effect, suggesting therapeutic potential for CCI-induced thermal hyperalgesia, with the PD persisting for 24 h. The dose–response curves of oxamate were subsequently converted into the 24-h AUC, showing a dose-dependent analgesic effect (Fig. S1b). Consequently, the analgesic effects of LDHA inhibitors on CCI-induced neuropathic pain showed a dose-dependent pattern.
 In assessing the chronic analgesic effects of LDHA inhibitors in CCI-rats, 0.1 μg of FX11 and 5 μg of oxamate were administered intrathecally and hourly using an osmotic pump. Nociceptive behavior, mechanical allodynia (paw withdrawal threshold, PWT), and thermal hyperalgesia (paw withdrawal latency, PWL) were assessed every 2 d starting on day 1 post-CCI surgery. As shown in Fig.  c, the PWT in the CCI group started to decrease on day 1 and gradually decreased to a minimum on day 13. After treatment with LDHA inhibitors, the PWT of the CCI + FX11 and CCI + oxamate groups showed significant differences from that of the CCI group from days 1 to 13. The PWL in the CCI group decreased on day 1 and gradually decreased to a minimum on day 13 (Fig.  d). Furthermore, the PWL of the CCI + FX11 and CCI + oxamate groups also showed significant differences from that of the CCI group from days 1 to 13. Consequently, both LDHA inhibitors significantly improved CCI-induced nociception, indicating that FX11 and oxamate exert robust analgesic effects on CCI-induced neuropathic pain through consistent intrathecal injections.

## LDHA inhibitors inhibit CCI-induced neuronal LDHA upregulation in the ipsilateral SCDH
To determine whether LDHA inhibitors regulate CCI-induced LDHA expression in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH, immunofluorescence staining was performed to examine LDHA expression. Immunofluorescence showed upregulated LDHA immunoreactivity in the 7d and 14d groups (Fig.  a). Intrathecal administration of LDHA inhibitors for 7 and 14 d reduced CCI-induced LDHA upregulation in the 7d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, 14d + FX11, and 14d + oxamate groups. Quantification of the results indicated that CCI-induced LDHA upregulation from lamina I to lamina III was significantly mitigated by LDHA inhibitors for 7 and 14 days in the 7d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, 14d + FX11, and 14d + oxamate groups (Fig.  b). Furthermore, western blot analysis and quantification of the relative cytosolic LDHA expression indicated that CCI-induced cytosolic LDHA upregulation in the ipsilateral lumbar spinal cord was significantly attenuated by LDHA inhibitor for 7 and 14 d in the 7d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, 14d + FX11, and 14d + oxamate groups (Fig.  c). To identify which cell types participate in LDHA expression regulation, double immunofluorescence staining revealed co-staining patterns among astrocytes-specific marker glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, green), microglia-specific marker ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba1, green), neurons-specific marker neuronal nuclei (NeuN, green), and LDHA (red) in lamina I to lamina III in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH in the control, 7d, and 14d groups (Fig.  d). NeuN-positive cells co-stained with LDHA (yellow) indicated neuronal localization, whereas GFAP- and Iba1-positive cells showed less co-localization with LDHA, indicating lower expression in non-neuronal cells. The Pearson’s correlation coefficient demonstrated that LDHA co-staining with GFAP or Iba1 was negative, whereas co-staining with NeuN was positive (Fig.  e). Consequently, LDHA was primarily expressed in neurons rather than glial cells in the SCDH. Consequently, LDHA inhibitors effectively inhibited CCI-induced neuronal LDHA upregulation in the ipsilateral SCDH.
 

## LDHA inhibitors alleviate CCI-induced neuroinflammation
To verify whether LDHA inhibitors can attenuate CCI-induced neuroinflammation, immunofluorescence staining was used to examine microglial and astrocyte markers, Iba1 and GFAP, respectively, in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH. As shown in Fig.  a, CCI enhanced Iba1 and GFAP immunoreactivity in the 7d and 14d groups. Intrathecal administration of LDHA inhibitors for 7 and 14 d reduced CCI-induced Iba1 and GFAP upregulation in the 7d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, 14d + FX11, and 14d + oxamate groups. Quantification results (Fig.  b) indicated that Iba1 immunoreactivity from lamina I to lamina III in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH (Fig.  a) was significantly upregulated by CCI in the 7d and 14d groups compared to that in the control group. This upregulation was significantly attenuated by LDHA inhibitors in the 7d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, 14d + FX11, and 14d + oxamate groups.
 CCI significantly upregulated GFAP immunoreactivity in the 7d and 14d groups compared to that in the control group (Fig.  c). The CCI-induced GFAP upregulation was significantly inhibited by oxamate for 7 and 14 d of administration in the 7d + oxamate and 14d + oxamate groups. GFAP expression in the 7d + FX11 and 14d + FX11 groups showed a declining pattern; however, it did not achieve statistical significance compared to the 7d and 14d groups, respectively. Consequently, LDHA inhibitors attenuated CCI-induced glial cell activation and neuroinflammation in the ipsilateral SCDH.

## LDHA inhibitors mitigate CCI-induced M1 microglia upregulation
The classical microglial activation pathway mainly expresses the M1 phenotype [ ]. M1 microglia are considered proinflammatory because they release proinflammatory mediators. To evaluate whether LDHA inhibitors modulate CCI-induced M1 microglial expression in the ipsilateral SCDH, immunofluorescence staining was performed to label the M1-specific marker CD86. As shown in Fig.  a, CD86 immunoreactivity was enhanced by CCI in the 7d and 14d groups. Intrathecal administration of LDHA inhibitors inhibited CCI-induced CD86 upregulation. Moreover, immunofluorescence results indicated that CD86 (red) colocalized with Iba1 (green) in the ipsilateral SCDH (inset, yellow). As shown in Fig.  b, the quantification of CD86 from lamina I to lamina III in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH indicated that CCI significantly upregulated CD86 expression in the 7d and 14d groups, compared to that in the control group. LDHA inhibitors significantly attenuated CCI-induced CD86 upregulation in the 7d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, 14d + FX11, 14d + oxamate groups. As shown in Fig.  c, western blotting and quantification results indicated that CCI significantly upregulated the relative CD86 protein expression in the 14d group compared to that in the control group. LDHA inhibitors significantly downregulate CCI-induced CD86 upregulation in the 7d + oxamate, 14d + FX11, and 14d + oxamate groups. In the 7d + FX11 group, CCI-induced CD86 upregulation showed a decreasing trend; however, it was insignificant compared to the 7d group. Consequently, intrathecal administration of LDHA inhibitors for 7 and 14 d attenuated CCI-induced M1 microglial upregulation.
 

## LDHA inhibitors alleviate CCI-induced neuronal phosphor-TBK1 upregulation
To verify whether LDHA inhibitors can attenuate CCI-induced phosphor-TBK1 (pTBK1) upregulation, immunofluorescence staining was used to detect pTBK1 expression in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH. In Fig.  a, pTBK1 immunoreactivity was upregulated by CCI in the 7d and 14d groups. Intrathecal administration of LDHA inhibitors for 7 and 14 days reduced the CCI-induced upregulation of pTBK1 in the 7d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, 14d + FX11, and 14d + oxamate groups. As shown in Fig.  b, quantification revealed pTBK1 immunoreactivity from lamina I to lamina III in ipsilateral lumbar SCDH (Fig.  a). CCI significantly upregulated pTBK1 in the 7d and 14d groups compared with the control group. CCI-induced pTBK1 upregulation was significantly attenuated by FX11 and oxamate in the 7d + FX11 and 7d + oxamate groups. Furthermore, compared with the 14d group, CCI-induced pTBK1 upregulation was significantly alleviated by LDHA inhibitors in the 14d + FX11 and 14d + oxamate groups. As shown in Fig.  c, the western blot analysis and quantification results of the relative expression of pTBK1 were enhanced by CCI in the 7d (P = 0.114) and 14d groups; however, they were not statistically significant compared to the control group. LDHA inhibitors significantly mitigated CCI-induced pTBK1 upregulation in the 7d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, and 14d + FX11 groups. pTBK1 expression in the 14d + oxamate group showed a declining trend; however, it was not statistically significant compared to the 14d group. Moreover, to elucidate which cell types would participate in pTBK1 regulation, double immunofluorescence staining was used to reveal the co-staining pattern among astrocytes (GFAP, green), microglia (Iba1, green), neurons (NeuN, green), and pTBK1 (red) in lamina I to lamina III in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH in the control, 7d, and 14d groups (Fig.  d). NeuN-positive cells co-stained with LDHA (yellow), whereas GFAP- and Iba1-positive cells showed less co-staining with pTBK1, indicating less co-localization with non-neuronal cells. Furthermore, the Pearson’s correlation coefficient results showed that only pTBK1 and NeuN co-staining had a positive value, whereas double staining with pTBK1, GFAP, and Iba1 was negative (Fig.  e). Collectively, LDHA inhibitors mitigated CCI-induced neuronal pTBK1 upregulation in the ipsilateral SCDH.
 

## LDHA inhibitors attenuate CCI-induced neuronal HIF-1α upregulation
To verify whether LDHA inhibitors can attenuate CCI-induced HIF-1αupregulation, an immunofluorescence staining was used to detect HIF-1α expression in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH. CCI enhanced HIF-1α immunoreactivity in the 7d and 14d groups (Fig.  a). Intrathecal administration of LDHA inhibitors for 7 and 14 days reduced CCI-induced HIF-1α upregulations in the 7d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, 14d + FX11, and 14d + oxamate groups. Quantification of HIF-1α immunoreactivity from lamina I to lamina III in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH indicated that CCI significantly upregulated HIF-1α expression in the 7d and 14d groups compared to that in the control group (Fig.  b). CCI-induced HIF-1α upregulation was significantly inhibited by FX11 and oxamate for 7 d in the 7d + FX11 and 7d + oxamate groups compared to the 7d group. Furthermore, compared with the 14d group, CCI-induced HIF-1α upregulation was significantly mitigated by LDHA inhibitors in the 14d + FX11 and 14d + oxamate groups. Double immunofluorescence staining revealed the co-staining pattern among astrocytes (GFAP, green), microglia (Iba1, green), and neurons (NeuN, green), and HIF-1α (red) in lamina I to lamina III in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH in the control, 7d, and 14d groups (Fig.  c). NeuN-positive neurons was co-stained with HIF-1α (yellow). GFAP- and Iba1-positive cells, showed less co-staining with HIF-1α, indicating less co-localization with non-neuronal cells. As shown in Fig.  d, Pearson’s correlation coefficient results illustrated that HIF-1α co-staining with NeuN presented a positive value, whereas double fluorescence staining of HIF-1α with GFAP or Iba1 showed negative values. Consequently, LDHA inhibitors can attenuate neuronal CCI-induced HIF-1α upregulation in the ipsilateral SCDH.
 

## LDHA inhibitors alleviate CCI-induced angiogenesis
To verify whether LDHA inhibitors could attenuate CCI-induced Ang2 and cluster of differentiation 31 (CD31), which represent neo-angiogenesis [ ], immunofluorescence staining was used to detect Ang2 and CD31 expression in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH. As shown in Fig.  a, CCI enhanced Ang2 and CD3 immunoreactivity in the 7d and 14d groups. Intrathecal administration of LDHA inhibitors for 7 and 14 d reduced CCI-induced Ang2 and CD31 upregulation in the 7d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, 14d + FX11, and 14d + oxamate groups. As shown in Fig.  b, quantification results elaborated on Ang2 immunoreactivity from lamina I to lamina III in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH (Fig.  a). CCI significantly upregulated Ang2 in the 7d and 14d groups compared to that in the control group. This upregulation was significantly attenuated by LDHA inhibitors in the 7d + FX11 and the 7d + oxamate groups compared to the 7d group. Moreover, compared to the 14d group, LDHA inhibitors significantly downregulated CCI-induced Ang2 upregulation in the 14d + FX11 and 14d + oxamate groups. Additionally, Ang2 was primarily expressed in neurons rather than glial cells in the ipsilateral SCDH under neuropathic pain (Fig. S2a), consistent with our previous publication [ ]. Pearson’s correlation coefficient showed that Ang2 co-stained with NeuN had a positive value (Fig. S2b).
 In addition, CD31 immunoreactivity was significantly upregulated by CCI in the 7d and 14d groups compared to that in the control group (Fig.  c). This upregulation was significantly inhibited by FX11 and oxamate for 7 and 14 days of administration in the 7d + oxamate and 14d + FX11 groups compared to that in the 7d and 14d groups, respectively. CCI-induced CD31 upregulation was alleviated by FX11 and oxamate in the 7d + FX11 and 14d + oxamate groups; however, they were not statistically significant compared to the 7d and 14d groups, respectively. Herein, we demonstrated that LDHA inhibitors mitigate CCI-induced angiogenesis by downregulating Ang2 and upregulating CD31 in the ipsilateral SCDH.

## LDHA inhibitors mitigate CCI-induced neuronal nuclear LDHA upregulation
LDHA can translocate to the nucleus both in vitro [ ] and in vivo [ ]. To verify whether FX11 or oxamate could attenuate CCI-induced neuronal nuclear LDHA upregulation in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH, we utilized double immunofluorescent staining, labeling LDHA (red) and nucleus (DAPI, blue) (Fig.  a). Mander’s coefficient analysis indicated that CCI significantly enhanced neuronal nuclear LDHA expression in the 7d and 14d groups. Moreover, intrathecal administration of LDHA inhibitors significantly alleviated CCI-induced nuclear LDHA expression in the 7d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, 14d + FX11, and 14d + oxamate groups. Confocal microscopy of spinal sections labeled with NeuN (green), LDHA (red), and nucleus (DRAQ5, blue) confirmed that neuronal LDHA is expressed in the neuronal nucleus (Fig.  b). Western blot analysis and quantification of nuclear LDHA expression (Fig.  c) indicated that LDHA inhibitors significantly attenuated CCI-induced LDHA nuclear expression in the 7d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, 14d + FX11, and 14d + oxamate groups. Consequently, CCI-induced neuronal LDHA upregulation was significantly mitigated by the intrathecal administration of LDHA inhibitors, which also attenuated CCI-induced neuronal nuclear LDHA expression.
 

## LDHA inhibitors can ameliorate CCI-induced oxidative stress
To verify whether LDHA inhibitors attenuate CCI-induced oxidative stress, superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) expression in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH was examined using immunofluorescent staining. CCI increased SOD1 immunoreactivity in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH in the 7d the 14d groups (Fig.  a). Intrathecal treatment with LDHA inhibitors for 7 and 14 d reduced this CCI-induced SOD1 upregulation in the 7d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, 14d + FX11, and 14d + oxamate groups. As shown in Fig.  b, quantification of SOD1 immunoreactivity from the lamina I to the lamina III in the ipsilateral lumbar SCDH revealed significant upregulation of SOD1 in the 7d and 14d groups compared with the control group. This CCI-induced SOD1 upregulation was significantly attenuated by LDHA inhibitors in the 7d + FX11 and 7d + oxamate groups compared to that in the 7d group. Furthermore, compared to the 14d group, CCI-induced SOD1 upregulation was significantly downregulated by LDHA inhibitors in the 14d + FX11 and 14d + oxamate groups. Western blotting and quantification results (Fig.  c) showed that relative SOD1 expression was significantly enhanced by CCI in the 7d and 14d groups in the ipsilateral spinal cord compared to that in the control group. Moreover, LDHA inhibitors significantly mitigated CCI-induced SOD1 upregulation in the 7d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, and 14d + FX11 groups. Although CCI-induced SOD1 upregulation in the 14d + oxamate group showed a decreasing trend, it was not statistically significant compared to the 14d group. Intrathecal LDHA inhibitors administration significantly alleviated CCI-induced malondialdehyde (MDA) expression in the 7d + FX11, 7d + oxamate, 14d + FX11, and 14d + oxamate groups compared to the 7d and 14d groups (Fig. S3). Consequently, LDHA inhibitors effectively reduced CCI-induced oxidative stress accumulation in the ipsilateral SCDH.
 

## Discussion
The major finding of this study was that CCI-induced neuropathic pain increased LDHA expression in ipsilateral SCDH. Intrathecal administration of LDHA inhibitors FX11 and oxamate reduced CCI-induced nociception. Although LDHA inhibitors typically reduce LDHA activity and lactate production rather than LDHA expression [ , , , ], we found that both FX11 and oxamate reduced CCI-induced LDHA upregulation in lumbar ipsilateral SCDH. Additionally, FX11 and oxamate decreased CCI-induced glial activation and M1 microglial expression. They also alleviated CCI-induced TBK1 phosphorylation and HIF-1α upregulation in neurons. LDHA inhibitors reduced CD31, a new vessel formation marker, and neuronal Ang2 upregulation in the ipsilateral SCDH of CCI rats. The CCI-induced neuronal nuclear LDHA upregulation was also mitigated by LDHA inhibitors. Moreover, LDHA inhibitors mitigated CCI-induced SOD1 upregulation in the ipsilateral SCDH.
Cellular metabolic pathways such as glycolysis change under pain or inflammatory conditions [ – ]. Glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation are key energy pathways that generate 2 and 36 units of ATP, respectively. Moreover, Zhu et al. demonstrated that the neuron-consumed energy escalates [ ] owing to abnormally increased action potential frequency increases nociceptive transmission [ , ]. However, it is paradoxical to enhance glycolysis and decrease oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to maintain neuropathy-induced nociception, as nociceptive sensitization has a high energy demand [ , – ]. Glycolysis may meet this energy consumption in the neuropathic pain state, attributed to the metabolic rate being 10–100 times faster than OXPHOS [ , , ]. Notably, neuropathy-induced nociception can be attenuated by inhibiting glycolysis or activating mitochondrial OXPHOS [ , ]. Thus, reprogramming energy metabolism is a potential approach for managing neuropathic pain.
LDHA exists in tetramer and dimer isoforms that are enzymatically active [ ] and transcription factors [ ], respectively. The canonical enzymatic form primarily expresses in the cytoplasm and converts pyruvate into lactate to regenerate NAD+ from NADH to sustain glycolysis without OXPHOS to maintain energy production [ , ]. LDHA’s role in lactate production is well-established. LDHA inhibition by siRNA or the inhibitors FX11 and oxamate attenuates lactate production both in vitro and in vivo [ , , , ]. LDHA upregulation leads to lactate accumulation in the extracellular space, causing acidification [ ] and inflammation [ , ]. Lactate is crucial for developing and maintaining neuropathic pain [ , ]. This study is the first to show that intrathecal LDHA inhibitors can ameliorate CCI-induced neuroinflammation and nociception by reducing the function and protein expression of spinal neuronal LDHA.
In addition, dimeric LDHA, primarily expressed in the nucleus, has non-classical enzymatic functions such as regulating epigenetic modification [ ] and oxidative stress resistance [ ]. Nuclear LDHA mediates interferon-gamma expression via histone acetylation [ ], a vital regulator of inflammatory and chronic pain [ ]. Furthermore, Liu et al. showed that nuclear LDHA mediates SOD1 expression during oxidative stress resistance [ ]. Notably, we found that CCI-induced SOD1 upregulation and MDA, markers of cellular oxidative stress [ ], were significantly mitigated by LDHA inhibitors in the ipsilateral spinal cord. Collectively, we hypothesized that nuclear LDHA expression is a compensatory mechanism that protects cells under neuropathic conditions and participates in the restoration of CCI-induced oxidative stress. However, the exact mechanism underlying nuclear LDHA expression in neuropathic pain remains unclear.
TBK1 modulates microglial polarization, neuroinflammation, and nociceptive sensitization [ , , ]. Möser and colleagues indicated that TBK1 is primarily expressed in neurons and that TBK1 knockout has an antinociceptive effect in mice by mediating the NF-κB signaling pathway [ ]. Notably, pTBK1 was primarily expressed in neurons rather than glial cells. However, the mechanism by which neuronal pTBK1 regulates neuroinflammation remains unknown and requires further investigation. Koh et al. demonstrated that HIF-1α escalates proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, leading to neuroinflammation and neural injury [ ]. Moreover, persistent activation of HIF-1α promotes neuropathic pain [ ], and LPS-induced HIF-1α upregulation causes M1 microglia polarization [ ]. In this study, we identified that HIF-1α is expressed in neurons rather than microglia or astrocytes in the ipsilateral SCDH (Fig.  ). A recent study demonstrated that TBK1 might be upstream of HIF-1α, as HIF-1α expression is mitigated after treatment with a specific TBK1 inhibitor, amlexanox, both in vitro and in vivo [ ]. Moreover, lactate enhances TBK1 phosphorylation [ , ] and induces HIF-1α gene and protein expression in the CNS [ ], suggesting a positive feedback loop between LDHA and the TBK1/HIF-1α signaling pathway. Thus, we suggest that neuronal LDHA regulates the TBK1/HIF-1α signaling pathway to enhance neuroinflammation.
Neuropathic pain upregulates angiogenesis [ – ] and increases regional blood flow and metabolism in the peripheral nervous system and CNS in rats [ – ]. A strong correlation between inflammation and angiogenesis exists [ ]. VEGF and Ang2 are two essential angiogenic regulators [ , ]. CCI upregulates VEGF expression [ ], primarily in astrocytes [ ], and induces neuronal Ang2 upregulation [ ]. Ang2 participates in the synthesis of proinflammatory cytokines [ ] and the regulation of neuroinflammation [ ]. However, the mechanism linking Ang2 and nociception in neuropathic pain remains unclear. Evidence suggests that HIF-1α may serve as a link between LDHA and Ang2. Previous research and our preliminary data indicated that CCI-induced neuropathic pain upregulates HIF-1α expression in the CNS [ ], promoting angiogenesis [ , ]. Furthermore, LDHA expression is regulated by HIF-1α [ ], inducing Ang2 expression [ ]. Interestingly, gene and protein expression of HIF-1α is regulated by lactate [ ]. Collectively, LDHA produces lactate to mediate HIF-1α expression, subsequently regulating Ang2 expression. The relationship between LDHA and HIF-1α-induced Ang2 upregulation forms a positive feedback loop. Hence, we suggest that LDHA uses lactate to mediate Ang2 and angiogenesis via HIF-1α in neuropathic pain. However, the exact mechanisms remain unclear and require further investigation.
Herein, we propose a role for LDHA and its inhibitors in neuropathy-induced nociceptive sensitization of the CNS (Fig.  ). CCI activates TBK1 and upregulates HIF-1α expression in the ipsilateral SCDH. TBK1 plays a pivotal role in promoting the synthesis of pro-inflammatory cytokines and has been identified as a crucial regulator of neuroinflammation. HIF-1α, a downstream target of TBK1, modulates neuroinflammation, angiogenesis, and nociception. Upregulation of the TBK1/HIF-1α axis enhances neuronal LDHA expression after CCI. LDHA upregulation enhances lactate production, activating astrocytes and microglia, leading to M1 microglial upregulation, neuroinflammation, and nociceptive sensitization. Notably, previous studies have demonstrated an increase in lactate levels in the ipsilateral SCDH in CCI-induced neuropathic pain [ , ]. Additionally, the upregulation of the neuronal TBK1/HIF-1α signaling pathway enhances Ang2 expression, leading to new vessel formation and CD31 upregulation. Besides the mediating role of angiogenesis, Ang2 also regulates neuroinflammation [ ]. Angiogenesis participates in the development and maintenance of neuroinflammation and nociception. The upregulation of LDHA escalates lactate production and accumulation, leading to TBK1 and HIF-1α upregulation. LDHA creates a positive feedback loop via lactate generation along the TBK1/HIF-1α axis. Intrathecal administration of LDHA inhibitors FX11 and oxamate has shown the potential to relieve CCI-induced nociceptive sensitization. LDHA inhibitors attenuate the CCI-induced upregulation of the TBK1/HIF-1α axis and LDHA expression, thereby reducing glial cell activation, M1 microglial upregulation, and Ang2 expression. Thus, LDHA inhibitors alleviate CCI-induced neuroinflammation and angiogenesis. In contrast, CCI-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation leads to neuroinflammation and HIF-1α upregulation. HIF-1α induces the translocation of cytoplasmic LDHA into the nucleus. Nuclear LDHA confers resistance to oxidative stress and may serve as a compensatory mechanism to protect cells under neuropathic conditions. Following LDHA inhibitor administration, nociception relief is accompanied by a decrease in ROS accumulation, reducing the need for an endogenous oxidative stress resistance system and restoring CCI-enhanced SOD1 and nuclear LDHA expression by mitigating CCI-induced TBK1/HIF-1α axis expression and LDHA upregulation.
 LDHA inhibitors ameliorate CCI-induced neuroinflammation, angiogenesis, and nociception by regulating the neuronal TBK1/HIF-1α signaling pathway. Furthermore, nuclear LDHA can resist CCI-induced oxidative stress accumulation, which is mitigated by LDHA inhibitors. Collectively, LDHA inhibitors demonstrate analgesic potential for treating neuropathic pain.

## Conclusion
This study is the first to demonstrate that intrathecal LDHA inhibitors have analgesic effects on neuropathic pain. Moreover, spinal neuronal LDHA mediates neuroinflammation and angiogenesis via the TBK1/HIF-1α signaling pathway under persistent peripheral neuropathic stimulation. Thus, LDHA is a promising therapeutic target for the management of neuropathic pain. However, the exact regulating neuronal signaling pathways and neuropathic pain need further elucidation.
