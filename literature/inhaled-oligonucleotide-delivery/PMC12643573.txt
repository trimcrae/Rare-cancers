## BACKGROUND:
Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA), a pathological dilation of the abdominal aorta, is primarily driven by chronic aortic wall inflammation. The well-established anti-inflammatory microRNA 146a (miR-146a) has been implicated as a key regulator in various chronic inflammatory pathologies. However, its potential functional role in the pathogenesis of AAA remains to be elucidated.

## METHODS:
We constructed Ang II (angiotensin II)–induced and PPE (porcine pancreatic elastase)–induced models in global miR-146a knockout mice, vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC)–specific miR-146a knockout mice, and macrophage-specific miR-146a knockout mice, respectively, to explore the role of miR-146a in AAA. Western blot, quantitative polymerase chain reaction, and immunohistochemistry were used to detect the levels of aortic proinflammatory markers and VSMC contractile proteins, whereas flow cytometry was used to assess M1/M2–like macrophage polarization. To validate the downstream mechanism, dibenzazepine was intraperitoneally injected to inhibit the Notch1 pathway in rescue experiments.

## RESULTS:
In the Ang II–induced and PPE-induced model, global knockout of miR-146a promoted AAA development, increased maximal aortic diameter, exacerbated medial elastin degradation, and upregulated aortic proinflammatory markers (COX2 [cyclooxygenase 2], MMP [matrix metalloproteinase] 2, MMP9, and CCL2 [chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 2]). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that global miR-146a deficiency also induced macrophage polarization toward the inflammatory M1 phenotype. Conditional deletion of miR-146a in VSMCs and macrophages largely replicated AAA formation and proinflammatory effects. Furthermore, AAV9 (adeno-associated virus)-mediated miR-146a knockdown significantly reduced VSMC contractile proteins CNN1 (calponin 1), SM22α (smooth muscle 22α), and α-SMA (α-smooth muscle actin) in mouse aortas at 7 days post–Ang II perfusion. Mechanistically, Notch1 antagonist dibenzazepine effectively rescued AAA characteristics and M1 biomarkers while enhancing M2 biomarkers in global miR-146a knockout mice.

## CONCLUSIONS:
The absence of miR-146a potentiates AAA formation by promoting VSMC phenotypic switching, Notch1 signaling–mediated aortic inflammation, and macrophage M1 polarization. Thus, miR-146a plays a critical role in maintaining aortic structural integrity to prevent aneurysmal pathogenesis.

## Highlights
Genetic ablation of microRNA 146a promotes abdominal aortic aneurysm formation in Ang II (angiotensin II)–induced and PPE (porcine pancreatic elastase)–induced mice by enhancing M1-like macrophage polarization, inflammatory responses, and vascular smooth muscle cell phenotypic transition.
Selective ablation of microRNA 146a in either macrophages or vascular smooth muscle cells was sufficient to replicate proinflammatory M1-like macrophage activation and drive pathological abdominal aortic aneurysm formation.
MicroRNA 146a deficiency promotes abdominal aortic aneurysm formation through activation of the Notch1 signaling pathway.
Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA), characterized by local weakening and enlargement of the abdominal aorta, is a major and intractable contributor to morbidity and mortality in adults. Currently, surgical repair is reserved for symptomatic, large, rapidly enlarging, or ruptured AAAs, whereas effective therapies remain lacking for the predominant small and asymptomatic AAAs that, if left untreated, will progress to large and potentially ruptured aneurysms. – Identifying the key factors in AAA pathogenesis is essential for advancing mechanistic understanding and guiding the development of therapeutic targets for AAA prevention and management.
Despite the involvement of complex mechanisms in AAA development, a consensus has been reached regarding the major pathogenic role of chronic vascular inflammation. Circulating inflammatory cells massively infiltrate the aorta and continuously secrete excessive cytokines, chemokines, free radicals, and proteases, which incite multiple other determinants of aortic remodeling—including vascular smooth muscle cell (VSMC) dysfunction and apoptosis, and medial destruction—thereby weakening the aortic wall. – Macrophages are the predominant and critical inflammatory infiltrates throughout AAA development. – The initially recruited macrophages promptly differentiate into the proinflammatory M1-like macrophage phenotype to promote and sustain aortic inflammation via constant release of proteolytic enzymes and inflammatory cytokines. Physiologically, in injured tissues, M1 later converts to the anti-inflammatory M2-like macrophage phenotype to foster inflammation resolution and tissue repair by producing anti-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors. – However, during AAA development, the recruited M1-like macrophages are not adequately balanced by M2-like macrophages. This skewed M1/M2 ratio is critical in sustaining chronic inflammation that accelerates AAA development and drives ongoing aortic expansion. The phenotypic switching (PS) of VSMCs, which comprise the vast majority of aortic cells, represents another early and key mechanism of aortic remodeling. , On initial inflammatory signals, VSMCs undergo prompt dedifferentiation from the contractile phenotype to a proliferative and synthetic phenotype, namely PS, where they lose their matrix-producing and tissue-repair capacity while secreting increased MMPs (matrix metalloproteinases) and reactive oxygen species, aggravating matrix degradation, and local inflammation to boost aortic degeneration. – Therefore, it has been believed that VSMC PS and aortic inflammation reciprocally fuel AAA formation.
MicroRNA 146a (miR-146a) is a highly conserved microRNA initially proposed as a canonical biomarker for age-related inflammatory diseases. – Emerging studies have also demonstrated its involvement in multiple chronic inflammatory conditions, including inflammatory neurodegeneration, periodontitis, psoriasis, and atherosclerosis. – On exposure to various inflammatory mediators, miR-146a is induced and directly regulated by NF-κB (nuclear factor-κB), a master transcription factor governing inflammatory responses. , , Intriguingly, stimulated miR-146a reciprocally functions as an endogenous negative feedback inhibitor of NF-κB activation by targeting multiple components within the NF-κB signaling pathway across diverse disease contexts, highlighting its potential as an effective anti-inflammatory regulator. , Accumulating evidence has shown that miR-146a is significantly upregulated in macrophages on cellular activation and regulates macrophage activation, function, and phenotypic transition. , In a murine model of acute lung injury, miR-146a deficiency exacerbated macrophage-derived proinflammatory cytokine production, whereas miR-146a overexpression promoted alternative M2-like macrophage polarization and attenuated lung inflammatory injury. Recent clinical investigations have revealed elevated miR-146a expression in both aortic tissues and peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with AAA, suggesting its regulatory role in macrophage dysfunction and AAA pathogenesis. Furthermore, emerging evidence indicates that miR-146a is associated with VSMC migration and differentiation, processes integral to VSMC PS. – 
Based on these findings, we hypothesized that miR-146a dysregulation contributes to AAA pathogenesis by promoting M1-like macrophage-mediated vascular inflammation and VSMC PS. To test this hypothesis, we used a loss-of-function approach using both global miR-146a knockout mice and miR-146a conditional knockout mice in Ang II (angiotensin II)–induced and PPE (porcine pancreatic elastase)–induced AAA formation. Our results demonstrated that miR-146a deficiency exacerbates aortic M1/M2 macrophage polarization imbalance, accelerates VSMC PS, increases aortic inflammatory infiltration, and promotes AAA development through activation of the Notch1 signaling pathway. These findings establish a critical role for miR-146a dysregulation in AAA pathogenesis and identify miR-146a as a potential therapeutic target for AAA prevention and management.

## Materials and Methods
The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

## Animals
All animal care and experiments followed the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Research Committee of Southern Medical University. Ten- to 12-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were supplied by Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center. All animals had free access to a normal mouse diet and water, and were housed under pathogen-free conditions at a temperature of 22 °C and humidity 60% to 65%, 12-hour dark/light cycle.

## Generate Global miR-146a Knockout Mice
The miR-146a knockout mice were generated using CRISPR-Cas9 (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat–associated 9) technique (Cyagen Biosciences Inc, Suzhou, China). Cas9 protein was microinjected into fertilized mouse eggs along with 2 gRNAs (guided RNAs; gRNA-A1 [matching reverse strand of gene]: TGCACCACCCATGATAGGGC-AGG, gRNA-A2 [matching reverse strand of gene]: GCTTCGCTTTTCCCAACCGG-AGG). Resulting embryos were transferred to recipient female mice to obtain F0 mice. The genotype of knockout mice was confirmed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using 2 pairs of primers (F1: 5′-AAGGGAAGGATTGAACATGACACA-3′, R1: 5′-TTATTGCCTCTCTACAAGGACCTG-3′; F2: 5′-AAGGGAAGGATTGAACATGACACA-3′, R2: 5′-ACCATCAATAGCAGAGATGACTGG-3′; internal control-F: 5′-GCAGAAGAGGACAGATACATTCAT-3′, internal control-R: 5′-CCTACTGAAGAATCTATCCCACAG-3′) and sequencing.

## Generate VSMC-Specific miR-146a Knockout Mice
The gRNA to the mouse miR-146a gene, the donor vector containing loxP (locus of X-over P1) sites, and Cas9 mRNA were coinjected into fertilized mouse eggs to generate targeted conditional knockout offspring. F0 founder animals were identified by PCR followed by sequence analysis, which were bred to wild-type (WT) mice to test germline transmission and F1 animal generation. Breed a F1 targeted mouse with a tissue-specific Tagln (transgelin)-Cre delete mouse to generate F2. Breed heterozygous, Cre+ (Cre recombinase positive) mice with homozygous mice. gRNA-A1 (matching reverse strand of gene): TGCACCACCCATGATAGGGCAGG; gRNA-A2 (matching reverse strand of gene): GCTTCGCTTTTCCCAACCGGAGG; gRNA-B1 (matching forward strand of gene): GCCCTATCATGGGTGGTGCATGG; gRNA-B2 (matching reverse strand of gene): TCGCTTTTCCCAACCGGAGGAGG. The genotype of knockout mice was confirmed by PCR using primers: primers1: F1: 5′-GCTCTTGCTGACGTGAAGAAATAC-3′, R1: 5′-TTCCTAGAGTGACCCAGTTCTACA-3′; primers2: Tagln-M-F: 5′-CAGACACCGAAGCTACTCTCCTTCC-3′, Tagln-M-R: 5′-CGCATAACCAGTGAAACAGCATTGC-3′. The tissue-specific gene deletion can be confirmed by adding 1 additional primer to the PCR assay: primers3: F2: 5′-AAGGGAAGGATTGAACATGACACA-3′, R2: 5′-GTGGATTCGGACCAGTCTGA-3′.

## Generate Macrophage Conditional miR-146a Knockout Mice
The gRNA targeting the mouse miR-146a gene, the donor vector containing loxP sites, and Cas9 mRNA were coinjected into fertilized mouse eggs to generate conditional miR-146a knockout offspring. F0 founder mice were identified by PCR followed by sequence analysis and subsequently bred to WT mice to test germline transmission and F1 animal generation. An F1 targeted mouse was crossed with a tissue-specific Cx3cr1 (C-X3-C motif chemokine receptor 1-Cre)-Cre mouse to generate F2. Heterozygous Cre+ F2 mice were then bred with homozygous mice to generate F3. gRNA1 (matching reverse strand of gene): 5′-TGCACCACCCATGATAGGGCAGG-3′; gRNA2 (matching reverse strand of gene): 5′-GCTTCGCTTTTCCCAACCGGAGG-3′; gRNA3 (matching forward strand of gene): 5′-GCCCTATCATGGGTGGTGCATGG-3′; gRNA4 (matching reverse strand of gene): 5′-TCGCTTTTCCCAACCGGAGGAGG-3′. The genotype of knockout mice was confirmed by PCR using primers: primers1: F1: 5′-GCTCTTGCTGACGTGAAGAAATAC-3′, R1: 5′-TTCCTAGAGTGACCCAGTTCTACA-3′. primers2: Cx3cr1-F: 5′-GACATTTGCCTTGCTGGAC-3′, Cx3cr1-R: 5′-GCAGGGAAATCTGATGCAAG-3′; The tissue-specific gene deletion can be confirmed by adding 1 additional primer to the PCR assay: primers3: F2: 5′-AAGGGAAGGATTGAACATGACACA-3′, R2: 5′-GTGGATTCGGACCAGTCTGA-3′.

## Ang II Infusion and Dibenzazepine Administration
Ang II dissolved in pure water (1000 ng/kg per min, A9525; Sigma) and normal saline were infused for 28 days via separate mini-osmotic pumps (ALZET, Model 2004; DURECT Corporation, Cupertino, CA), each of which was subcutaneously implanted on the back of anesthetized mice through a small incision, as previously described. For Notch1 rescue experiments, mice were intraperitoneally injected daily with either dibenzazepine (20 μmol/kg per day), a γ-secretase inhibitor, or vehicle (0.1% Tween-80, 0.5% hydroxypropylmethylcellulose, and 0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide) 1 day before Ang II perfusion and continued for 4 weeks.

## PPE-Induced Mouse AAA Model
After the mice were fully anesthetized by inhalation of a mixture of 1% to 2% isoflurane and air, they were placed on the surgical table. The abdomen was incised at the midline to fully expose the infrarenal abdominal aorta. The aorta was then covered with a gelatin sponge soaked in PPE (0.2 U) or heat-inactivated PPE (preheated at 100 °C for 30 minutes) for 5 minutes. After the removal of the gelatin sponge, the abdominal cavity was irrigated with physiological saline and subsequently sutured. The mice were euthanized 14 days after feeding for further experiments.

## Systolic Blood Pressure Measurements
Systolic blood pressure monitoring was performed in conscious mice from 9 to 11 am using a noninvasive tail-cuff system (BP-2010 series, Softron, Tokyo). Measurements were taken a week before Ang II infusion (deemed as baseline systolic blood pressure) and then once a week.

## AAA Quantification
Quantification for aneurysm formation was performed at the end of the experimental protocols, before the final utilization of the aorta for subsequent analysis. Briefly, mice were euthanized via carbon dioxide inhalation. After confirming that the mice had died, the aorta was horizontally dissected, exsanguinated, and rinsed by PBS perfusion through the left cardiac ventricle, with effluent exiting through the severed right atrium. Under dissecting microscopy, periadventitial tissues were carefully removed from the exposed aorta. The suprarenal abdominal aorta segment was defined as the region between the last intercostal artery pair and the right renal artery bifurcation. Maximum aortic diameter was determined as the external diameter of the most dilated abdominal aortic segment. The measurement was conducted ex vivo using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health) after adjusting the scale based on a ruler in the aorta pictures. All measurements were conducted by 2 coworkers who were blinded to the group allocation, and the average value was determined subsequently to ensure the repeatability and integrity of the experiment. AAA formation was defined as over 50% increase in external aortic diameter relative to control mice.

## Elastin Staining
Suprarenal aortic elastin integrity was evaluated by Verhoeff-Van Gieson staining in ×40 magnification images. The criteria for elastin lamina degradation score were as follows , : grade 1, no degradation with well-organized elastic lamina; grade 2, interruptions and breaks; grade 3, multiple interruptions and breaks; grade 4, severe fragmentation or loss, or aortic rupture. Specifically, after scaling adjustment, a coworker who was blind to the grouping used Image Pro Plus software (Media Cybernetics) to quantify the elastic fiber content in 5 representative images of each section. The areas of the aortic media and elastic fibers were measured, respectively, and averaged. Then, the ratio of elastic fiber area to aortic media area was calculated for grading purpose.

## Histology, Immunohistochemistry, and Immunofluorescence
Paraffin sections of the suprarenal aorta samples were utilized. After deparaffinization, the sections were blocked by 3% hydrogen peroxide and preincubated with 10% normal serum (in 1% BSA) for 1 hour. For immunohistochemistry staining, sections were incubated with primary antibodies (in 1% BSA) at 4 °C overnight, followed by secondary antibodies (in 1% BSA) for 1 hour. Subsequently, sections were incubated with an HRP (horseradish peroxidase)–labeled streptavidin solution. Then, the slides were stained with diaminobenzidine and counterstained with hematoxylin. The primary antibodies included rabbit anti-MMP2 (MMP2, ab37150; Abcam), rabbit anti-MMP9 (ab228402; Abcam), rabbit anti-CCL2 (chemokine [C-C motif] ligand 2, ab25124; Abcam), rabbit anti-IL6 (interleukin 6, ab7737; Abcam), and rabbit anti-CD68 (ab283654; Abcam).

## Western Blot
Total aortic extracts were obtained using ice-cold RIPA (radio immunoprecipitation assay) lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Beyotime, Beijing, China), and quantified using the BCA (bicinchoninic acid) Protein Array Kit (Solabio, Beijing, China). After denaturation, equivalent amounts of protein samples were separated by gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. After blocking with 5% BSA in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with tween) at room temperature, samples were incubated with primary antibodies (in 5% BSA) at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies were rabbit anti-COX2 (cyclooxygenase 2, ab179800; Abcam), anti-MMP2 (1:1000 ab37150; Abcam), anti-MMP9 (ab58803; Abcam), anti-CCL2 (ab25124; Abcam), anti-CNN1 (calponin 1, ab46794; Abcam), anti-SM22α (smooth muscle 22α, ab14106; Abcam), anti-αSMA (α-smooth muscle actin, ab124964; Abcam), anti-Notch1 (3608S; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-INOS (inducible NO synthase, 18985-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-Arg1 (arginase 1, 1:1000 16001-1-AP; Proteintech), and anti-CD206 (60143-1-Ig; Proteintech). Anti-β-actin (ab8226; Abcam) or anti-eIF5 (eukaryotic translation initiation factor 5, ab170915; Abcam) were used as loading controls. Then the membrane was washed 5× with TBST and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology) for 1 hour at room temperature. The probed proteins were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence (Advance, No. RPN2235; GE Healthcare Life Sciences). Relative protein density was measured using ImageJ software.

## Gelatin Zymography Analysis
Protein extracts were prepared using nonreducing sample buffer under nondenaturing conditions (without heat treatment). Electrophoretic separation was performed using 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gels copolymerized with 0.1% gelatin as substrate, with the electrophoresis process maintained at 4 °C to preserve enzyme activity. After electrophoresis, gels underwent 2 washes with 2.5% Triton X-100 solution to facilitate sodium dodecyl sulfate removal and protein renaturation. For enzymatic digestion, gels were incubated for 16 hours at 37 °C in reaction buffer (50 mmol/L Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, supplemented with 5 mmol/L CaCl₂ and 0.02% NaN₃ as preservative). Subsequent visualization involved Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 staining followed by appropriate destaining. Proteolytic activities of MMP9 and MMP2 were identified as distinct transparent zones within the stained gelatin matrix and quantified using digital densitometric analysis.

## Real-Time Quantitative PCR
Total RNA was isolated from mouse abdominal aortas using a total RNA extraction reagent (15596018CN; Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by reverse transcription into cDNA. Real-time quantitative PCR was then performed on a Light Cycler 80 II instrument (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland). Employing a SYBR Green One-Step qRT-PCR Kit (D7268S; Beyotime, China) and gene-specific primers, the relative expression levels of target genes were quantified, with normalization against the GAPDH mRNA transcript serving as an internal control. Finally, the acquired experimental data were analyzed via the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers used in this experiment are as follows: mouse GAPDH forward, 5′-TGTCCGTCGTGGATCTGAC-3′, and reverse, 5′-CCTGCTTCACCACCTTCTTG-3′; mouse α-SMA forward, 5′-TCCTGACGCTGAAGTATCCGAT-3′, and reverse, 5′-GGCCACACGAAGCTCGTTATAG-3′; mouse CNN1 forward, 5′-GCGTCACCTCTATGATCCCAA-3′, and reverse, 5′-CCCAGACCTGGCTCAAAGAT-3′; mouse SM22α forward, 5′-GATATGGCAGCAGTGCAGAG-3′, and reverse, 5′- AGTTGGCTGTCTGTGAAGTC-3′; mouse MMP9 forward, 5′-GCGTGTCTGGAGATTCGACTTG-3′, and reverse, 5′-ACTGCAGGAGGTCGTAGGTCAC-3′; mouse MMP2 forward, 5′-ACCAACACTGGGACCTGTCAC-3′, and reverse, 5′-CGAAGAACACAGCCTTCTCCT-3′; mouse CCL2 forward, 5′-ACCTGCTGCTACTCATTCAC3′, and reverse, 5′-CATTCAAAGGTGCTGAAGAC-3′; mouse COX2 forward, 5′-CACAGTGCACTACATCCTG-3′, and reverse, 5′-ATGATCTGGATGTCAGCAC-3′.

## Flow Cytometry
Analysis of the M1/M2 ratio in AAA lesions was performed by flow cytometry. The aortas were removed and digested in 1× aorta dissociation enzyme stock solution (125 U/mL collagenase type XI, 60 U/mL hyaluronidase type 1-s, 60 U/mL DNase I, and 450 U/mL collagenase type I in 2.5 mL PBS; Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 1 hour. Single-cell suspensions were then obtained by shearing the aortas apart and passing them through a 70-μm cell strainer. Cells were collected, centrifuged, and resuspended. For flow cytometry analysis, cells were incubated with fluorochrome-labeled anti-F4/80-phycoerythrin (1:50, 123109; BioLegend) and anti-CD86-–fluorescein isothiocyanate (1:50, 105005; BioLegend) for detecting surface markers. For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized using fixation and permeabilization solution (BD Biosciences) for 20 minutes followed by incubation with anti–CD206-APC (allophycocyanin) antibody (1:50, 141707; BioLegend). Flow cytometry analysis was then conducted within 1 hour. Data was analyzed with FlowJo software.

## Statistics
Quantitative data were expressed as the means±SD. Statistical analysis was conducted with SPSS 20.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL). Normality was assessed in all continuous variables using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Unpaired Student t test was performed to compare differences in 2 independent groups. Two-way mixed-effects ANOVA was performed to assess comparisons among multiple groups at different time points, and 1-way or 2-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post hoc test was used for comparing data of >2 groups. If data did not exhibit normal distribution, Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparisons between 2 groups, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for comparisons among multiple groups. Data on aneurysm incidence were analyzed using Fisher exact test. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Genetic miR-146a Ablation Induces Mouse AAA Formation
We first investigated the role of miR-146 in AAA formation. After mice with global ablation of miR-146 were successfully generated (Figure S1), adult WT mice and adult miR-146 global ablation (miR146a-knockout [KO]) mice were randomly assigned to 2 groups and administered Ang II or saline infusion. We found Ang II infusion substantially elevated systolic blood pressure in both WT and global miR-146 ablation mice, but global miR-146 ablation did not significantly alter systolic blood pressure in C57BL/6J mice treated with saline or Ang II (Figure A). Mice were euthanized, and aortas were collected at 28 days postinfusion. The fates of the experimental mice are detailed in Figure S2. The incidence of AAA was significantly higher in the miR-146 ablation group compared with the WT group under Ang II infusion (4% versus 50%), but this difference disappeared in saline-treated mice (Figure B and C). The maximal abdominal aortic diameter was also substantially increased in the miR-146 ablation group (Figure D). Histological analysis revealed that elastin degradation, as quantified by Verhoeff-Van Gieson staining and elastin degradation score, was significantly exacerbated in miR-146 ablation mice compared with WT controls (Figure E and F). These findings were corroborated in a PPE-induced AAA model, where global miR-146 ablation led to increased maximal aortic diameters (Figure S3A and S3B) and enhanced elastic fiber degradation (Figure S3C). Collectively, our results demonstrate that miR-146 deficiency promotes AAA pathogenesis in mice.
 

## Genetic miR-146a Ablation Exacerbates Aortic Inflammation in Mice
Chronic tissue inflammation plays a major role in AAA pathogenesis. Next, we investigated whether ablation of the classical anti-inflammatory miR-146a would affect the aortic inflammatory state. After 28 days of Ang II infusion, immunohistochemical staining demonstrated that proinflammatory markers CD68, MMP9, CCL2, and IL6 were significantly elevated in the aortas of global miR-146 knockout mice compared with those in WT mice (Figure A through D). Concurrently, western blot and real-time quantitative PCR analyses revealed that aortic expression levels of COX2, MMP2, MMP9, and CCL2 were upregulated after Ang II treatment, with further enhancement observed in miR-146-deficient mice compared with WT controls (Figure E through J). In addition, in PPE-induced AAA tissues, miR-146 ablation potentiated the expression of COX2, MMP9, MMP2, and CCL2 (Figure S3D). Gelatin zymography further showed increased MMP9 and MMP2 enzymatic activities in PPE-treated miR-146 knockout mice (Figure S3E). Collectively, these findings indicate that miR-146 deficiency predisposes the aorta to inflammatory activation.
 

## Genetic miR-146a Ablation Promotes Aortic Macrophage Polarization Toward a Higher M1/M2 Ratio In Vivo
Macrophages are prominent and critical inflammatory cells recruited to the aorta during AAA formation, and an elevated in situ M1/M2 ratio drives ongoing aortic inflammation. To investigate the role of miR-146a in aortic macrophages, we collected aortic cells and performed flow cytometry analysis at 28 days post Ang II or saline infusion. Our results confirmed an increase in CD86⁺CD206⁻ M1-like macrophages and a decrease in CD86⁻CD206⁺ M2-like macrophages in the miR-146a ablation group compared with the WT group under both saline and Ang II treatment conditions (Figure ), demonstrating that miR-146a deficiency significantly promotes aortic M1/M2 polarization toward a proinflammatory state.
 To establish clinical relevance, human THP-1 (Tohoku Hospital Pediatrics) cells were separately transfected with either miR-146a–specific siRNAs (small interfering RNA) or control siRNAs, and with either miR-146a overexpression plasmids or control plasmids. After transfection, cells were stimulated with LPS (lipopolysaccharide; 100 ng/mL) plus IFN (interferon)-γ (50 ng/mL) for 24 hours. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that miR-146a knockdown enhanced LPS/IFN-γ–induced polarization toward M1-like macrophages (Figure S4A). Conversely, miR-146a overexpression attenuated this polarization process into M1-like macrophages (Figure S4B).

## Genetic miR-146a Ablation Promotes Aortic VSMC PS at Early Stages of Ang II Infusion in Mice
PS in VSMCs is considered an early and critical mechanism in AAA pathogenesis. To investigate the regulatory role of miR-146a in VSMC fate determination, we harvested aortic tissues from mice treated with Ang II for 7 days—representing the early AAA development phase characterized by VSMC PS—to analyze contractile marker expression. Our results demonstrated that both protein and mRNA levels of CNN1, SM22α, and α-SMA were significantly decreased after Ang II treatment compared with saline controls, with further downregulation observed in the miR-146a knockout group versus WT controls (Figure A through D). These findings indicate that genetic ablation of miR-146a accelerates VSMC PS during early AAA formation.
 To further validate the role of miR-146a in VSMCs during AAA formation, we generated VSMC-specific miR-146 knockout mice and their WT counterparts (Figures S5 and S6), and subsequently induced AAA via PPE perfusion. We observed that 7 days after PPE stimulation, VSMC-specific miR-146 knockout promoted PS of VSMCs (Figure S7A and S7B). Furthermore, at 28 days poststimulation, this knockout significantly accelerated AAA progression, evidenced by increased abdominal aortic diameter and marked elastic fiber degradation (Figure S8A through S8C). Consistent with these findings, PPE-treated VSMC-specific miR-146 knockout mice exhibited marked upregulation of inflammatory markers, including COX2, MMP9, and MMP2 (Figure S8D). These results demonstrate that VSMC-derived miR-146a not only regulates VSMC phenotypic modulation but also affects the occurrence and progression of AAA through inflammatory response mediation.

## Conditional miR-146a Deletion in Macrophages Induces AAA Formation in Ang II–Infused Mice
Given the pivotal role of macrophage polarization in AAA formation and the abundant expression of miR-146a in macrophages, we next investigated the contribution of macrophage-specific miR-146a to AAA pathogenesis. Conditional macrophage-specific miR-146a knockout mice were generated as previously described (Figures S9 and S10). Adult WT mice and adult macrophage-specific miR-146a knockout mice were randomly assigned to receive either Ang II or saline infusion. The fates of the experimental mice are detailed in Figure S11. Consistent with our hypothesis, macrophage-specific miR-146a ablation did not significantly alter systolic blood pressure in either saline-infused or Ang II–infused mice (Figure A). However, this genetic deletion substantially increased AAA incidence, maximal abdominal aortic diameter, and elastin degradation specifically in Ang II–infused mice compared with saline-infused controls (Figure B through F). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that macrophage-specific miR-146a deficiency promoted proinflammatory mediator expression, including MMP9, CCL2, IL6, and CD68 (Figure A through D). Furthermore, this ablation upregulated protein levels of inflammatory markers COX2, MMP2, MMP9, and CCL2 in the aortic tissue after 28 days of Ang II infusion (Figure E through I).
 To confirm the effect of macrophage-specific miR-146a ablation on AAA, we further established a PPE-induced model. We found that macrophage-specific miR-146a ablation resulted in an increased aortic diameter and exacerbated elastic fiber degradation (Figure S12A through S12C). Gelatin zymography revealed elevated MMP9 and MMP2 activities in the PPE-induced macrophage-specific miR-146a knockout mice (Figure S12D). These results demonstrated that selective depletion of macrophage miR-146a largely recapitulates both AAA formation and the proinflammatory effect previously observed in global miR-146a knockout mice. To investigate the impact of macrophage-specific miR-146a ablation on VSMC PS, we harvested aortic tissues 7 days post-PPE stimulation and analyzed VSMC contractile markers by Western blot. The results showed that macrophage-specific miR-146a ablation also promoted VSMC PS during the early AAA formation phase (Figure S13A and S13B). This finding indicates a close link between VSMC PS and macrophage-driven inflammation in AAA formation. Collectively, our data suggest that macrophage-specific miR-146a plays a critical anti-inflammatory role during AAA development.

## Notch1 Inhibitor Dibenzazepine Rescues AAA Formation and Aortic M1-Like Macrophage Transition Induced by Global miR-146a Ablation
Notch1 is a crucial proinflammatory signaling pathway contributing to AAA formation, and its inhibitory regulation by miR-146a, particularly in macrophages, has been increasingly documented. To elucidate the mechanism by which miR-146a deficiency induces AAA formation, we administered the Notch1 antagonist dibenzazepine or an identical volume of vehicle to global miR-146a knockout mice subjected to Ang II infusion. Ang II infusion significantly induced aortic Notch1 expression, which was further augmented after miR-146a ablation (Figure A). Notably, dibenzazepine treatment markedly reversed the exacerbation of AAA incidence, maximal aortic diameter, elastin degradation, and expression of the macrophage infiltration marker CD68 that were induced by miR-146a deficiency. Furthermore, Notch1 inhibition substantially reduced the protein levels of M1 markers iNOS and MMP9 while increasing M2 markers CD206 and Arg1. These findings demonstrate that miR-146a deficiency promotes AAA formation through activation of the Notch1 signaling pathway.
 

## Discussion
The current study established an obligatory role of miR-146a–mediated regulation in protecting the abdominal aorta against aneurysm formation. Genetic silencing of miR-146a induced AAA formation in Ang II–induced and PPE-induced C57BL/6J mice by enhancing M1-like macrophage polarization, inflammatory response, and VSMC phenotypic transition. Notably, selective ablation of miR-146a in macrophages or VSMCs was sufficient to recapitulate proinflammatory M1 activation and pathological AAA formation. Mechanistic investigations revealed that miR-146a deficiency disrupted its inhibitory constraint on Notch1 signaling, thereby promoting macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype and accelerating aneurysm formation. These findings validate miR-146a as an essential safeguard for preserving aortic structural integrity under pathological conditions characterized by Ang II or PPE exposure.
Our study aimed to explore the role of miR-146a in AAA formation based on the following compelling evidence: (1) AAA pathogenesis is characterized by persistent chronic inflammation, and miR-146a has consistently been shown to function as a paradigmatic anti-inflammatory miRNA in various chronic inflammatory conditions. (2) Clinical analyses demonstrate that miR-146a is significantly upregulated in human AAA tissues compared with thoracic aortic diseases, suggesting its pathological relevance and potential therapeutic targeting value in AAA pathogenesis. (3) Emerging evidence reveals an interplay between miR-146a expression patterns and AAA formation. First, there are confirmed alterations in tissue and plasma miR-146a levels in healthy individuals, observed in an age-related manner and in a male sex–specific pattern. These 2 factors affecting miR-146a expression overlap with the major risk factors for AAA. , Second, experimental models confirm that miR-146a dysregulation contributes to the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis, which is the most prevalent risk factor for AAA. , (4) Inverse associations have been identified between miR-146a levels and proinflammatory factors of AAA including MCP-1 (monocyte chemoattractant protein 1), IL6, and TNF (tumor necrosis factor)-β. , – (5) Recent investigations highlight involvement of miR-146a in critical AAA-related pathways, particularly macrophage polarization and VSMC PS.
In contrast to the typical decline of miR-146a expression observed in age-related diseases where miR-146a dysregulation drives chronic tissue inflammation, miR-146a exhibits aberrant hyperexpression in the aortic tissue of both patients with AAA and mouse models, as demonstrated by previous studies. This expression pattern aligns with the upregulated miR-146a profiles in most miR-146a–involved chronic inflammatory disorders. The pathological elevation of miR-146a may result from NF-κB signaling hyperactivation during AAA formation, as emerging evidence confirms miR-146a functions as a dual-component regulatory node within the NF-κB/miR-146a feedback loop, acting as both a downstream effector and a negative feedback regulator in various cellular contexts. The divergent expression profiles of miR-146a in age-related versus inflammatory pathologies suggest context-dependent functional specialization of this microRNA in disease mechanisms. To clarify whether miR-146a dysregulation represents an adaptive compensatory response to vascular inflammation or constitutes a primary pathogenic factor of AAA, we established 2 classical AAA models, the Ang II model and the PPE model, in miR-146a knockout mice. This intervention resulted in significantly increased AAA incidence, enhanced elastin degradation, and elevated proinflammatory biomarkers (IL6, MMP2, and MCP-1), indicating a pathogenic role of miR-146a dysfunction in AAA formation. Our study provides in vivo evidence establishing miR-146a as a primary indispensable maintenance factor for aortic homeostasis. Deregulation of this microRNA predisposes the abdominal aorta to a proinflammatory state, which progresses to aneurysm formation.
We further demonstrated that global miR-146a deregulation promotes AAA formation by potentiating tissue inflammation and directing macrophage differentiation toward the inflammatory M1 phenotype, as evidenced by increased immunostaining signals for CD68, CCL2, and IL6 along with flow cytometry results. Macrophages constitute rapid and integral inflammatory infiltrates during AAA formation, where an imbalanced ratio of proinflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 plays a critical role in sustaining persistent inflammation. Previous studies have reported that circCdyl (circular RNA chromodomain Y-like) overexpression enhances M1 polarization to increase the M1/M2 ratio, thereby promoting vascular inflammation and inducing AAA formation. Given that miR-146a is broadly expressed in multiple immune cells including macrophages, and considering the majority of aortic macrophage infiltrates originate from circulating macrophages, , we performed conditional genetic ablation of miR-146a in macrophages to investigate its intrinsic role in macrophage biology and AAA pathogenesis. miR-146a deficiency in macrophages recapitulated the proinflammatory and proaneurysmal effects observed in Ang II–induced AAA formation, as demonstrated by elevated inflammatory immunostaining signals. , Consistent with its role as a dominant negative regulator of innate immunity, miR-146a was shown to be autonomously induced in monocytes on inflammatory stimulation, thereby suppressing circulating monocyte infiltration into inflamed tissues while inhibiting their differentiation into macrophages and subsequent inflammatory molecule release. 
In patients with AAA, miR-146a was reported to be overexpressed in peripheral blood mononuclear cells and to suppress TNF-α release from cultured peripheral blood mononuclear cells by inhibiting p65, a key factor of the NF-κB pathway. In a mouse model of acute lung injury, miR-146a induction facilitated macrophage activation toward a lower M1/M2 ratio and alleviated lung inflammatory injury. Taken together, our results demonstrated that miR-146a is an innate regulator of aortic infiltrating macrophages and inflammation during AAA formation. On the other hand, we observed that genetic miR-146a absence promoted VSMC PS, as shown by decreased expression of VSMC contractile biomarkers SM22, CNN1, and α-SMA. As VSMC PS is considered to be an early and key process in aortic remodeling, the enhanced VSMC PS should be partly responsible for the exacerbated AAA development in response to miR-146a dysregulation. Taken together, our findings reveal an obligatory role of miR-146a in counteracting macrophage M1-mediated inflammation and VSMC PS during AAA formation.
Through rescue experiments, we corroborated that miR-146a ablation promotes AAA formation by abolishing its suppression of Notch1 activation, thereby releasing the downstream promoting effects of Notch1 on M1 polarization. Notch1 is the canonical receptor of the evolutionarily conserved Notch signaling pathway. – After activation on ligand binding, the intracellular domain of the Notch1 receptor is released and translocates to the nucleus, where it serves as a transcriptional activator. Notch1 signaling has been identified as a critical proinflammatory signaling in diverse diseases including AAA. , – Our data showed that aortic Notch1 signaling was activated when miR-146a was ablated, and that Notch1 signaling inhibition by dibenzazepine largely abrogated the inflammatory and degradative features of aneurysmal aorta once elicited by miR-146a deletion, suggesting the participation of Notch1 signaling in miR-146a ablation–induced AAA formation. Moreover, Notch1 inhibition enhanced the expression of M2 markers in the aortas of miR-146a–ablated mice, suggesting its promotion toward M2 polarization. Our results demonstrated the indispensability of Notch1 in miR-146a absence–induced AAA formation and echoed previous reports of the critical role of Notch1 signaling in AAA progression through direct regulation of macrophage polarization. Furthermore, the inhibitory effect of miR-146a on Notch1 signaling, particularly in macrophages, has been increasingly documented in vitro and in various disease contexts. For instance, miR-146a prevents lipopolysaccharide-induced organ damage by inhibiting Notch1 in macrophages, , while long noncoding RNA NIFK-AS1 inhibits M2 polarization by targeting the miR-146a/Notch1 axis in endometrial cancer65. Taken together, our findings indicate that miR-146a dysregulation promotes AAA formation at least partially through Notch1-mediated macrophage polarization, suggesting that targeting macrophage phenotypes via the miR-146a signaling axis may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for AAA treatment.
Some limitations exist in the current study. First, although we have revealed that the absence of miR-146a facilitates AAA development, whether exogenous miR-146a supplementation can conversely prevent AAA formation remains unclear. Second, because Notch1 is widely expressed in various vascular cells, we cannot exclude the possibility that miR-146a deficiency may mediate AAA formation through Notch1 signaling activation in other cell types, such as aortic SMCs and endothelial cells. Third, future investigations should be conducted to evaluate the role of miR-146a in AAA formation using different animal models, which would help determine its clinical applicability as an interventional target.
In conclusion, genetic ablation of miR-146a promotes AAA formation through enhancing VSMC PS and Notch1 signaling–mediated polarization of inflammatory M1-like macrophages. miR-146a represents a promising therapeutic target for preventing AAA formation and progression.
