## Background
M2 macrophages are known to enhance the malignant phenotype of breast cancer (BC) cells, yet the underlying mechanisms remain unclear.

## Methods
scRNA sequencing analysis, chromatin immunoprecipitation, immunoprecipitation, exosome isolation, and biological experiments were used to analyze this crosstalk.

## Results
Maestro (MRO) was involved in the crosstalk between M2 macrophages and BC cells. MRO expression was elevated in BC tissues, particularly in those with high CD68 and CD206 expression, and correlated with poor prognosis. MRO was highly expressed in M2 macrophages, and its mRNA was transferred from M2 macrophages to BC cells via exosomes. Engineering exosomes with MRO knockdown demonstrated reduced tumor-promoting effects compared to controls. Mechanistically, MRO in BC cells functioned as a nuclear import protein, facilitating the translocation of HNRNPU into the nucleus. HNRNPU then enhanced CCL5 transcription by binding to its promoter. Elevated CCL5 not only promoted BC cell malignancy, but also recruited more macrophages and induced M2 polarization.

## Conclusions
These findings reveal a novel MRO/HNRNPU/CCL5 feedback loop in the interaction between M2 macrophages and BC cells, driving BC progression and reshaping the tumor microenvironment. Targeting this feedback loop offers a potential therapeutic strategy for BC.

## Introduction
Despite the introduction of targeted therapies, such as anti-HER2 agents, immunotherapy, and endocrine therapy, providing an expanding array of treatment options, the incidence and mortality rates of breast cancer (BC) continue to rise [ ]. The primary cause of death among BC patients remains tumor metastasis [ ]. Consequently, investigating the underlying mechanisms driving BC initiation and progression remains critically important.
Recent research has revealed that tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which are prevalent in the tumor microenvironment, play a critical role in driving the malignant progression of BC [ ]. TAMs can generally be categorized into two types: classically activated M1 macrophages and alternatively activated M2 macrophages [ ]. M1 macrophages possess pro-inflammatory and anti-tumor characteristics, while M2 macrophages exhibit pro-tumor and anti-inflammatory properties [ ]. Notably, elevated levels of M2 macrophages have been observed in BC tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues [ ]. These M2 macrophages have been implicated in promoting BC cell proliferation, metastasis, and drug resistance [ , ]. However, the precise mechanisms by which M2 macrophages contribute to BC cell proliferation and metastasis remain unclear.
Exosomes are vesicles secreted by cells into the extracellular environment [ ], with diameters ranging from 30 to 150 nanometers [ ]. These vesicles can be released by nearly all cell types and are widely present in various biological fluids [ ]. Exosomes are rich in diverse cellular components, including proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, sugars, and even organelles [ , ]. They act as molecular shuttles, transporting cargo molecules between cells to facilitate intercellular communication and signaling [ ]. Notably, tumor-associated macrophages have been shown to transfer exosomes to BC cells, playing a crucial role in breast cancer progression, immune evasion, and presenting potential avenues for therapeutic interventions [ ]. However, the mechanisms through which exosomes influence breast cancer development and progression remain to be fully elucidated.
In the current study, we revealed that Maestro (MRO) was transferred from M2 macrophages to BC cells via exosomes. Exosomes engineered with MRO knockdown exhibited diminished tumor-promoting effects compared to controls. Mechanistically, MRO in BC cells acts as a nuclear import protein, facilitating HNRNPU translocation into the nucleus, where it binds to the CCL5 promoter, enhancing its transcription. Elevated CCL5 drives BC malignancy, recruits additional macrophages, and promotes M2 polarization. Targeting MRO signaling presents a promising therapeutic strategy for BC.

## Materials and methods

## Single-cell RNA (scRNA) sequencing analysis of BC tissues
Single-cell sequencing analysis was conducted using the GSE148673 dataset obtained from GEO. The reliability of the data was initially evaluated by assessing the correlation between gene features and sequencing depth. The percentages of red blood cell genes, mitochondrial genes, and ribosomal genes within each cell were calculated using the “Percentage Feature” package (Version 5.0.3). Cells were filtered based on the following criteria: a minimum mitochondrial gene percentage of 20%, a minimum red blood cell gene percentage of 20%, gene expression counts ≥ 1000, and gene expression features ranging from 200 to 10,000.
Subsequently, 2000 highly variable genes were selected for further analysis, and the number of principal components (PCs) was set to 20 to facilitate cell cluster identification. The clusters were visualized using a tSNE plot generated with the “Etsne” R package (Version 3.1). Cell annotation was performed using the “singleR” R package (Version 2.6.0). The expression of biomarker genes within the clusters was examined, and the expression levels of MRO in each cell type were illustrated using the “VlnPlot” package (Version 5.0.3). Finally, cell-cell communication was analyzed using the “CellChat” package (Version 1.0.0). GO analysis was performed using the “clusterProfiler” R package (Version 4.12.2) after extracting the top 100 genes expressed in TAMs. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was applied.

## Clinical samples
A cohort of 56 paired human BC tissues and adjacent normal tissue samples was obtained from the Pathology Department of the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University in Guiyang, China. The diagnosis of all cancer tissue specimens was confirmed through postoperative pathological examination, and clinicopathological data were collected at the time of sample acquisition. Ethical approval for all experiments involving human specimens was granted by the Human Research Ethics Review Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Approved number: 2024-187).

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and multiplex immunohistochemical (mIHC) experiments
For IHC, the tissue sections were subjected to dewaxing, rehydration, and incubation in a repair solution at 96°C. After cooling, endogenous enzymes were inactivated, and the sections were blocked with 10% goat serum. Primary antibodies, including anti-CD68 (1:100, Cat. no. A23534, ABclonal Technology Inc., Wuhan, China), anti-CD163 (1:100, Cat. no. A8383, ABclonal Technology Inc., Wuhan, China), anti-MRO (1:50, Cat. no. PA5-54550, Sigma, USA), and anti-KI67 (1:500, Cat. no. A20018, ABclonal Technology Inc., Wuhan, China), were then applied and incubated overnight at 4°C. This was followed by the addition of enzyme-labeled goat anti-mouse/rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:4000; cat no. PV-6000, ZSBIO, Beijing, China). The sections were stained with 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) and hematoxylin, dehydrated, sealed with mounting medium, and subsequently examined under a microscope. IHC scoring was determined by integrating the proportion of positive cells and the intensity of staining. The proportion of positive cells was scored as follows: 0 (0%), 1 (< 25%), 2 (25-50%), 3 (51-75%), and 4 (> 75%). Staining intensity was graded as: 0 (no staining), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), and 3 (strong). The final IHC score was calculated by multiplying these two scores (ranging from 0 to 12). For each sample, three random field were evaluated, and scoring was performed independently by two blinded observers, with the average score used for analysis.
For mIHC, the steps for hydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking of BC tissues were consistent with those used in IHC. After these steps, a mixture of primary antibodies, rabbit-derived anti-MRO (1:50; cat. no. PA5-54550, Sigma, USA) and mouse-derived anti-CD163 (1:800, cat no. 68218-1-Ig, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), was added for overnight at 4 °C. After washing by PBS for three times, FITC–conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200; cat no. SA00003-2, Proteintech) and CY3–conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:200; cat no. SA00009-1, Proteintech) were added in dark for 1 h. Following by washing by PBS three times, BC tissues were then stained with DAPI, and observed in fluorescence microscope.

## Cell culture and transfection
Human monocyte THP-1 cells, normal breast epithelial cells MCF-10 A and BC cell lines (MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7) were sourced from ATCC (USA). BC cell lines were cultured in DMEM (Invitrogen, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Invitrogen, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ environment, while THP-1 and MCF-10 A cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Invitrogen, USA) with 10% FBS under identical conditions. Small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting MRO and the negative control (NC) were procured from iGeneBio (Beijing, China). The siRNA sequence targeting MRO was CTCCATATCTGAAACTAAA. Lentiviruses overexpressing MRO were generated by inserting PCR-amplified full-length human MRO cDNA into the pMSCV retroviral plasmid. Lentiviral infections were carried out using Polybrene (iGeneBio, Beijing, China). To establish BC cell lines stably expressing MRO, cells were treated with 1.0 µg/mL puromycin for 12 days post-infection.

## Macrophage induction
Monocyte-like THP-1 cells were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells and cultured in serum-free high-glucose medium with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; MCE, Wuhan, China) and 0.3% BSA for 48 h to induce differentiation into M0 macrophages. Subsequently, M2 macrophages were generated by adding 20 ng/mL IL-4 (MCE, Wuhan, China) for an additional 48 h in the presence of PMA.

## Flow cytometry
Cells were collected in 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Both macrophages and THP-1 cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated on ice for 30 min in a blocking buffer containing 50 µL PBS, 2% FBS, and 0.02% NaN3₃. The cells were then stained with fluorescently conjugated anti-human CD68 (1:50; cat no. #24850, CST, USA), CD11B (1:100; cat no. #24442, CST, USA), CD163 (1:100; cat no. FITC-65169, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), and CD206 antibodies (1:100; cat no. FITC-65155, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) for 20 min on ice, followed by rinsing with washing buffer. Flow cytometry was performed using a BD FACS Calibur (BD, USA), and data were analyzed with FlowJo software (version: 7.1.0).

## qRT-PCR assays
Total RNA in BC cells was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA was performed with the Quantscript RT Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Gene expression was quantified using a SYBR RT-PCR kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and specific primers, with relative expression levels analyzed by the 2^-ΔΔCT method using β-actin as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table .

## Western blotting
Protein concentrations from all cell lysates were determined using the BCA method after extraction with RIPA buffer (Boster, Wuhan, China) containing 5% PMSF protease inhibitor (Boster, Wuhan, China). For each sample, 30 µg of protein was loaded onto a 10% SDS-PAGE gel (Meilune, Dalian, China), separated over 120 min, and transferred onto 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including anti-β-actin (1:1,000, AC006, ABclonal), anti-CD68 (1:1,000, A23534, ABclonal), anti-CD11B (1:1,000, A24120, ABclonal), anti-CD163 (1:1,000, A8383, ABclonal), anti-CD206 (1:1,000, A8301, ABclonal), anti-MRO (1:1,000, ab181048, Abcam), anti-HSP70 (1:5,000, 10995-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-calnexin (1:20,000, 10427-2-AP, Proteintech), anti-CD9 (1:1,000, 20597-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-TSG101 (1:2,000, 28283-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-CCL5 (1:1,000, A14192, ABclonal), anti-HNRNPU (1:1,000, A3917, ABclonal), anti-α-tubulin (1:6,000, 11224-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-H3 (1:8,000, 17168-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-ARG1 (1:5,000, 16001-1-AP, Proteintech), and anti-CCL17 (1:6,000, A2854, ABclonal). Membranes were then incubated for 2 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (1:4000; cat no. AS014, ABconal), followed by three washes with TBST. Protein bands were visualized using high-sensitivity ECL reagent (Boster, Wuhan, China) and analyzed for relative expression using ImageJ software.

## Exosome extraction and engineered exosome construction
Culture dishes containing RPMI1640-supplemented medium were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for M2 macrophages culture. The medium was then replaced, and the M2 macrophages were incubated for an additional 3 days. The supernatant was collected and processed through sequential low- and high-speed centrifugation, followed by filtration and ultracentrifugation for 90 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the exosome pellet was resuspended in PBS and subjected to a second round of ultracentrifugation to obtain the purified exosomes. Engineered exosome was constructed by embedding MRO siRNA and NC siRNA using ExoLoad® Exosome Nucleic Acid Loading Kit (Biomart, Beijing, China). In brief, before opening the centrifuge tube, the exosome transport peptide (ETP) provided in the kit was centrifuged at 3000–4000 rpm for 1 min to prevent the dry powder from dispersing upon opening. Then, a total of 1 mL of ETP dissolution buffer was added to fully dissolve the contents, and stored at 4 °C for later use. Sequentially, a total of 50 µL (1000 pmol) of siRNAs targeting MRO (or NC siRNAs), 2260 µL (1 mg/mL) of purified M2 macrophage-derived exosomes, 200 µL of ETP, and 226 µL of reaction buffer, were added and then mix thoroughly. The complex was incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 2 h with continuous shaking at 150 rpm. The incubated sample was transferred to a 100 kDa ultrafiltration tube (Millipore, UFC8100), and wash buffer was added to a total volume of 4 mL. Then, incubated sample was centrifuged at 4000 × g for 30 min until the volume is reduced to approximately 100 µL. Wash buffer was added again to 4 mL for a second wash, and incubated sample was centrifuged at 4000 × g. The exosomes retained on the filter membrane was gently pipetted, then transferred to a clean EP tube to obtain the siRNA-loaded exosomes. The siRNA-loaded exosomes were used for downstream experiments within 12 h.

## Cell proliferation detection
The in vitro proliferation ability of BC cells was detected using cell count kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EDU) assay, and colony formation assay. For CCK-8 assay, BC cells were plated at a density of 3,000 per well in 96-well plates and incubated with indicated treatment (2 µg/ml corresponding exosomes, addition of 10 µM GW4869 or 0.2 µM maraviroc) for the CCK-8 assay. Cell growth was assessed at designated time points using the CCK-8 kit (C6005M, UElandy, China). After a 2-hour incubation, absorbance at 450 nm was measured with an iMark microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories).
The EDU assay was conducted using the BeyoClick™ EdU-488 Proliferation Detection Kit (Beyotime, Suzhou, China). MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 BC cell lines were cultured in 6-well plates, allowed to adhere, and treated with 10 µM EDU combination with indicate treatment (2 µg/ml corresponding exosomes, addition of 0.2 µM maraviroc) in fresh medium. After a 2-hour incubation at 37 °C, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Boster, Wuhan, China) for 15 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Boster, Wuhan, China) for 8 min. Cells were then incubated with 500 µL Apollo reaction buffer for 40 min in the dark, followed by DAPI staining for 10 min. The assay was repeated at least three times, and EDU staining was visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan). Three fields of view were selected for statistical analysis in each group. The EDU-positive rate was calculated by dividing the number of EDU-positive stained cells by the number of DAPI-stained nuclei.
The colony formation ability of MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells was assessed after treatment (2 µg/ml corresponding exosomes, addition of 10 µM GW4869 or 0.2 µM maraviroc). Colonies larger than 75 μm in diameter or containing over 50 cells were considered positive. A total of 1,000 cells per well were seeded in six-well plates, with three replicates per sample. After a 2-week incubation, cells were washed twice with PBS and stained with crystal violet. Each experiment was performed at least three times.

## Transwell assay
A total of 2,000 MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells were resuspended in 300 µL of serum-free DMEM and seeded into Matrigel-coated transwell chambers (Corning, USA). The lower chambers were filled with DMEM containing 10% FBS and indicated treatment (2 µg/ml corresponding exosomes, addition of 10 µM GW4869 or 0.2 µM maraviroc). After 24 h, invading cells were fixed and stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 20 min. Each experiment was conducted at least three times. A microscope was used to visualize fields of transwell and invasive cells in five randomly selected fields (top, bottom, left, right, and center) of each chamber were counted, and the counts were manually performed by two researchers, with the average taken.

## In vivo experiment
The in vivo model was established using female BALB/c nude mice (n = 30) obtained from the Animal Center of Guizhou Medical University. All procedures were performed in compliance with the Animal Ethics Committee guidelines of Guizhou Medical University (Approved number: 2400449).
For tumor xenograft experiments, 2 × 10⁶ MCF-7 cells were subcutaneously injected into the upper-right flank of the mice. Starting on the 9th day post-injection, mice harboring 40mm3-50mm3 tumor tissues were used for further study. Treated exosomes (2 µg) were administered intratumorally every 3 days according to the experimental groups, while mice in control group were treated with PBS in same volume (n = 5 mice in per group). Tumor size was measured every 3 days using the formula: (length × width²)/2. When the tumor diameter reached 12 mm, mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were collected for further analysis.
For lung metastasis experiments, 2 × 10⁶ MCF-7 cells in 100 µL PBS were injected into the tail vein of the nude mice. Treated exosomes (2 µg) were injected into tail vein every 3 days according to the experimental groups, while mice in control group were treated with PBS in same volume (n = 5 mice in per group). After 7 weeks, the mice were sacrificed, and lung tissues were harvested. Metastatic foci in the lungs were counted following hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining.

## RNA-sequencing and analysis
Total RNA was extracted from MCF7 cells treated with either NC exosomes or exosomes with MRO knockdown for 24 h using TRIzol™ Reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). RNA quantity and purity were assessed using a Bioanalyzer 2100 and RNA 6000 Nano LabChip Kit (Agilent, CA, USA). High-quality RNA samples (RIN > 7.0) were used for sequencing. After mRNA purification, fragments were reverse transcribed using SuperScript™ II Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, USA). A cDNA library with 300 ± 50 bp inserts was prepared and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 (LC-Bio Technology, Hangzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol.
Raw count data were processed using the Limma package in R software after probe annotation and batch normalization. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with a|LogFC| ≥ 1 and P < 0.05. Changes in gene expression were visualized in a volcano plot, where upregulated genes were marked in red and downregulated genes in blue. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using the DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/), with significant terms as P < 0.05.

## Nuclear and cytoplasmic separation
Nuclear and cytoplasmic RNAs were isolated using the PARIS™ Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After cell lysis, centrifugation at 4 °C for 5 min was performed to separate nuclear and cytoplasmic RNA fractions. The supernatant, containing cytoplasmic RNA, was transferred to an RNase-free tube, while the nuclear RNA pellet was lysed in 500 µL of cell disruption buffer. RNA fractions were then filtered, washed, eluted, and stored at − 80 °C.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (CHIP)
Cells were treated with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min to crosslink DNA and proteins, followed by two washes with ice-cold PBS. The cells were then harvested, centrifuged, and resuspended in a lysis buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail. Sonication was performed to shear chromatin into fragments. Anti-MRO (1:50, cat no. ab181048, Abcam, USA) or control IgG antibodies were added to the chromatin solution, and the mixture was incubated overnight at 4 °C to allow antibody-DNA binding. The antibody-DNA complexes were then precipitated, and the crosslinks were reversed. Purified DNA fragments were obtained and analyzed using sequencing method and PCR analysis to identify specific sequences associated with the bound protein.

## Immunoprecipitation
After lysis and centrifugation, 2.5% of the crude lysate was collected as input. Equal amounts of the target protein were precleared and immunoprecipitated by incubating overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies anti-MRO (1:50, cat no. ab181048, Abcam, USA) and Protein A/G (MCE, Wuhan, China). Rabbit IgG (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) served as a control. The immunoprecipitates were washed four times with lysis buffer and once with PBS containing inhibitors. The pellets were resuspended in 2× loading buffer. Binding proteins with MRO were analyzed by LC/MS analysis and western blotting.

## Confocal microscopy
Cells at 70% confluence were washed twice with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 0.5 h at 4 °C, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min. Cells were then incubated with the primary antibody including anti-MRO (1:100; cat no. PA5-98922, Sigma, USA), anti-HNRNPU (1:100, cat no. MA1-24632, Sigma), anti-CD68 (1:200, cat no. A24386PM, Abconal) and anti-CD206 (1:100; cat no. A21014, Abconal) at 4 °C for 12 h. After two PBS washes for 15 min each, the secondary antibodies including FITC–conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200; cat no. SA00003-2, Proteintech), CY3–conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (1:200; cat no. SA00009-1, Proteintech) and CY3–conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200; cat no. SA00009-2, Proteintech) were added and incubated at 37 °C for identifying MRO-, HNRNPU- and CD molecule-antibody complex respectively, followed by three 15-minute PBS washes. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China) and washed twice for 5 min. Images were captured using an Olympus confocal microscope. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (version: 1.8.0). Colocalization of the two proteins was assessed by measuring the degree of overlap in their spatial fluorescence intensity. The fluorescence strength was determined by calculating the mean fluorescence intensity, defined as the total fluorescence intensity divided by the fluorescent area.

## Dual-luciferase reporter assay
A dual-luciferase reporter assay was performed to confirm the binding of HNRNPU to the CCL5 promoter. Wild-type (WT) and mutated (MUT) CCL5 promoter sequences were cloned into the psi-basic luciferase reporter vector (Promega, USA). MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate and incubated overnight. The luciferase reporter vectors, containing either the WT or MUT CCL5 promoter sequence, were co-transfected with NC or HNRNPU plasmids into MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells using Lipo2000 (Invitrogen, USA). After 24 h, luciferase activity was measured to evaluate promoter binding activity.

## Statistics analysis
Statistical analysis was performed using the Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons and one-way ANOVA for multiple groups. All experiments were repeated three times, and results are expressed as mean ± SD. Survival rates were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, with comparisons made via log-rank tests. Statistical significance was assessed using GraphPad Prism 8, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

## Results

## MRO plays a critical role in the interaction between TAMs and BC cells
Using the scRNA sequencing cohort of BC tissues (accession number: GSE148673) from GEO, we conducted scRNA-seq analysis. Cells with low expression levels were filtered out based on predefined criteria (Fig.  A). The 2000 most variable genes were selected for further analysis (Fig.  B). PCA was performed using 20 principal components (Fig.  C), enabling the identification of 24 distinct cell clusters (Fig.  D-E). These clusters were annotated into seven cell types: malignant cells, B cells, endothelial cells, TAMs, fibroblasts, epithelial cells, and CD8 T cells (Fig.  F), with representative biomarkers displayed (Fig.  G).

 

Cellular communication network analysis revealed TAMs as the most closely associated with BC cells (Fig.  H-I). A Gene Ontology (GO) analysis of the top 100 TAM genes showed enrichment in processes such as cell activation, defense response, immune regulation, immune effector processes, and leukocyte-mediated immunity (Fig.  J). Using the BC transcriptome dataset from TCGA cohort, we found six TAM-associated genes correlated with BC prognosis, with MRO exhibiting the highest hazard ratio (Fig.  K). It is interesting that TAMs are the primary source of MRO expression, with BC cells as a secondary source, while other cell types showed minimal expression (Fig.  L). Moreover, we found that MRO expression was unrelated to BC molecular subtypes (Fig.  M). However, through performing Cibersort algorithm in BC tissues from TCGA cohort, we found that MRO was highly expressed in BC tissues with high M2 TAM levels (Fig.  N), as well as correlating with total TAM levels and M2 TAM levels (Fig.  O). These results indicated that MRO plays a critical role in the interaction between TAMs and BC cells.

## MRO expression is elevated in BC tissues and is associated with poor prognosis
A total of 56 pair BC tissues were collected in research cohort, it was revealed that protein levels of MRO were significantly elevated in BC tissues (Fig.  A-B). Pair analysis further demonstrated that a total of 66.01% BC patients with elevated MRO expression (Fig.  C). Moreover, ROC analysis indicated that MRO protein had distinguish diagnostic value to predict BC tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues with AUC as 0.747 (Fig.  D). Survival analysis (Fig.  E-F) demonstrated that high MRO expression correlates with lower overall survival and disease-free survival days. Elevated MRO levels were also linked to high lymph node invasion cases (Fig.  G). Furthermore, we detected the biomarkers of M2 macrophages in BC tissues, and found that MRO proteins had positive relationship with CD68 and CD163 in BC tissues (Fig.  H-I). Moreover, the mIHC results were consistent with IHC evidences, indicating that MRO protein levels were positively associated with CD163 in BC tissues (Fig.  J).

 

## M2 macrophages transfer MRO mRNA to BC cells via the exosome pathway
To investigate the role of MRO in the crosstalk between M2 TAMs and BC cells, a strategy was developed to induce THP-1 cells into M2 macrophages (Fig.  A). Observations of cell morphology revealed a transition from a suspension state to an adherent state after PMA treatment (Fig.  B). Western blot (Fig.  C) and flow cytometry (Fig.  D) analyses showed that cells treated with PMA and IL-4 expressed higher levels of M2 markers, including CD11B, CD68, CD163, and CD206. These results confirm the successful differentiation of THP-1 cells into M2 macrophages.

 

The protein and mRNA expression levels of MRO were analyzed in THP-1, M0 and M2 macrophages, revealing significantly higher MRO levels in M2 cells compared to THP-1 and M0 cells (Fig.  E-F). Additionally, we found that both MRO mRNA and protein levels in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells were elevated after co-culture with M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (Fig.  G-H).
Previous studies have shown that exosomes containing proteins, mRNAs, and non-coding RNAs play a critical role in cell communication [ ]. To investigate this, the exosome formation inhibitor GW4869 was utilized. Treatment with GW4869 under co-culture conditions reversed the increase in MRO expression in BC cells co-cultured with M2 macrophages (Supplement Fig.  A-B). Furthermore, GW4869 treatment significantly reduced the pro-tumorigenic effects of M2 macrophages, including enhanced BC cell proliferation (Supplement Fig.  C), colony formation (Supplement Fig.  D), and invasion (Supplement Fig.  E).
Moreover, exosomes from THP-1, M0, and M2 macrophages were extracted and validated using electron microscopy and western blot analysis (Fig.  I-J). Notably, no MRO protein was detected in the exosomes from these cells (Fig.  J). However, MRO mRNA levels were significantly higher in exosomes from M2 macrophages compared to those from THP-1 and M0 macrophages (Fig.  K). Furthermore, treatment of BC cells with exosomes from THP-1, M0, and M2 macrophages revealed that MRO mRNA and protein levels were significantly increased in BC cells treated with exosomes from M2 macrophages, but not in those treated with exosomes from THP-1 or M0 macrophages (Fig.  L-M). Taken together, these evidences indicated that M2 macrophages transfer MRO mRNA to BC cells via the exosome pathway.

## M2 macrophage-derived Exosomal MRO promotes the malignant behavior of BC cells
To evaluate the impact of exosomal MRO from M2 macrophages on BC cells, engineered exosomes with MRO knockdown or NC were generated and used to treat BC cells. CCK-8 (Fig.  A) and EDU assays (Fig.  B) demonstrated that NC exosomes from M2 macrophages significantly enhanced BC cell proliferation, whereas exosomes with MRO knockdown showed reduced effects. Colony formation assays further revealed that MRO knockdown in exosomes from M2 macrophages markedly diminished their ability to promote BC cell colony formation (Fig.  C). Similarly, transwell assays showed that, compared to NC exosomes, exosomes with MRO knockdown exhibited significantly lower pro-invasion effects on BC cells (Fig.  D). These findings suggest that M2 macrophage-derived exosomal MRO promotes BC cell proliferation and invasion in vitro.

 

In vivo experiments were conducted to evaluate the effects of M2 macrophage-derived exosomal MRO. MCF-7 cells were subcutaneously injected into the upper-right flank of mice and treated with engineered exosomes containing either MRO knockdown or NC (2 µg; Fig.  E). Compared to NC exosomes from M2 macrophages, exosomes with MRO knockdown exhibited reduced pro-proliferation effects on MCF-7 cells in vivo (Fig.  F-G). Similarly, compared with mice treated with NC exosomes, mice treated with exosomes with MRO-knockdown exhibited lower weight loss (Fig.  H). IHC analysis further revealed that tumor tissues treated with exosomes containing MRO knockdown displayed significantly lower KI67 expression compared to those treated with NC exosomes (Fig.  I-J). Additionally, in a lung metastasis model, mice treated with si-MRO exosomes exhibited significantly fewer metastatic foci in lung tissues compared to those treated with NC exosomes (Fig.  K-M). These results indicate that M2 macrophage-derived exosomal MRO enhances BC cell proliferation and metastasis in vivo.
Furthermore, we used NC exosomes and exosomes with MRO knockdown to treat normal breast epithelial cells MCF-10 A. Results indicated that, compared with NC exosomes, exosomes with MRO knockdown exhibited less ability to promote MCF-10 A proliferation (Supplemental Fig.  A) and migration (Supplemental Fig.  B). These results may also indicate that MRO derived from M2 macrophage exosomes has the potential to promote MCF10A malignant transformation.

## M2 macrophage-derived exosomal MRO promotes BC cell proliferation and invasion via transcriptional activation of CCL5 in BC cells
To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which M2 macrophage-derived exosomal MRO affects BC cells, RNA sequencing was conducted in MCF-7 cells treated with engineered exosomes with MRO knockdown (si-MRO) or negative control (si-NC). Principal component analysis revealed distinct clustering between MCF-7 cells treated with si-NC exosomes and those treated with si-MRO exosomes (Fig.  A). DEGs analysis identified 20 upregulated and 129 downregulated genes in MCF-7 cells treated with si-MRO exosomes compared to si-NC exosomes (Fig.  B). Functional enrichment analysis of the 129 downregulated genes demonstrated significant involvement in biological processes such as cell migration, cellular component movement, cytokine response, lipid response, and cytokine-mediated signaling (Fig.  C). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that these downregulated genes were significantly enriched in pathways including cytokine signaling in the immune system, extracellular matrix interactions, FOXO-mediated transcription, syndecan interactions, and interferon alpha/beta signaling (Fig.  D). Notably, cytokine signaling exhibited the most significant enrichment (Fig.  D). Given the critical role of cytokine dysregulation in promoting BC metastasis, subsequent analysis was focused on this pathway.

 

We identified a total of 14 downregulated genes induced by MRO knockdown in exosomes that were enriched in the cytokine signaling pathway. These genes included ISG15, IL20RB, CCL5, IRAK2, OASL, DUSP4, FN1, SOCS2, IFIT2, IFIT3, SOD2, TRIM34, CXCL8, and LCN2 (Fig.  E). To validate these findings, qRT-PCR was performed to assess the expression changes of these genes in BC cells treated with engineered exosomes containing either MRO knockdown or NC. The qRT-PCR results revealed that among the 14 genes, CCL5 consistently exhibited the most significant increase in expression in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells treated with NC exosomes (Fig.  F). Conversely, CCL5 showed the greatest reduction in expression in cells treated with MRO-knockdown exosomes compared to those treated with NC exosomes (Fig.  F). Based on these findings, we proposed that CCL5 may serve as a key downstream gene of MRO.
To further investigate how M2 macrophage-derived exosomal MRO regulates CCL5 mRNA levels in BC cells, we examined the pre-mRNA levels of CCL5. Our results showed that CCL5 pre-mRNA levels were significantly elevated in BC cells treated with NC exosomes, whereas exosomes with MRO knockdown exhibited a markedly reduced effect on CCL5 pre-mRNA levels (Fig.  G). Additionally, western blot analysis revealed that MRO protein levels followed a similar trend to its mRNA and pre-mRNA levels in BC cells treated with NC exosomes or MRO-knockdown exosomes (Fig.  H). To assess whether MRO influences mRNA stability, we conducted actinomycin D-induced degradation experiments. These experiments demonstrated no significant differences in the stability of CCL5 mRNA (Fig.  I) or pre-mRNA (Fig.  J) between treatments with NC exosomes and MRO-knockdown exosomes. Similarly, treatment with either NC exosomes or MRO-knockdown exosomes did not significantly alter the nucleoplasmic distribution of CCL5 mRNA (Fig.  K). Furthermore, analysis of the TCGA SpliceSeq database revealed the presence of two CCL5 transcript variants in BC tissues, primarily mediated by alternative splicing events at variable donor sites (Fig.  L). Treatment with NC exosomes or MRO-knockdown exosomes did not affect the ratio of these CCL5 transcripts, suggesting that MRO does not influence the alternative splicing of CCL5 (Fig.  M). Taken together, these findings indicate that M2 macrophage-derived exosomal MRO increases the transcription of CCL5 in BC cells without affecting its mRNA stability, nuclear export, or alternative splicing.
To investigate whether CCL5 was involved in MRO-induced proliferation and metastasis of BC cells, we generated MRO-overexpressing BC cells and conducted rescue experiments using the CCL5 inhibitor maraviroc, which blocks CCL5 from binding to its receptor CCR5. Our results demonstrated that MRO overexpression significantly enhanced BC cell proliferation (Supplemental Fig.  A-B), while treatment with maraviroc effectively reversed these effects (Supplemental Fig.  A-B). Similarly, colony formation assays revealed that maraviroc attenuated the enhanced colony-forming ability induced by MRO overexpression in BC cells (Supplemental Fig.  C). Additionally, transwell assays showed that maraviroc reduced the MRO-induced increase in invasion capacity of BC cells (Supplemental Fig.  D). These findings suggest that the pro-proliferation and pro-invasion effects of MRO in BC cells were CCL5-dependent.

## MRO, as a nuclear import protein, facilitates the translocation of the transcription factor HNRNPU from the cytoplasm into the nucleus
Given that MRO enhances CCL5 transcription in BC cells, ChIP-seq experiments were conducted to determine whether MRO directly acts as a transcription factor for CCL5. However, the results indicated that MRO is not a transcription factor. Instead, it binds randomly to non-specific distal gene sequences, similar to the negative control IgG (Fig.  A-B). Since the 3D structure and specific functions of the MRO protein remain unknown, we utilized the AlphaFold platform (https://deepmind.google/technologies/alphafold/) to predict its structure and function. The analysis identified five proteins with structures most similar to MRO, most of which function as nuclear import proteins, with the exception of CLASP2 (Fig.  C-D). Similarly, predictions from I-TASSER platform (https://zhanggroup.org/I-TASSER/) indicated that MRO’s primary functions involved protein binding and transport (Fig.  E-F). These findings suggested that MRO may function as a nuclear import protein, facilitating the entry of transcription factors into the nucleus, thus increasing CCL5 transcription.

 

To further investigate the proteins that interact with MRO in BC cells, we performed a combination of IP and LC/MS spectrometry in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells overexpressing MRO (Fig.  G). A total of 81 overlapping binding proteins were identified in both cell lines (Fig.  H). Interestingly, these 81 MRO-binding proteins were significantly associated with mRNA metabolic processes and mRNA transcription (Fig.  I). Further analysis revealed that 41 of these binding proteins were specifically enriched in mRNA metabolic processes (Fig.  J). Notably, among these proteins, HNRNPU consistently emerged as a highly abundant binding partner of MRO in both MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells (Fig.  K). Based on these findings, we focused our subsequent analysis on the HNRNPU protein.
Using IP-western blotting, we confirmed the interaction between HNRNPU and MRO in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells overexpressing MRO (Fig.  L). Western blotting analysis further revealed that the expression levels of HNRNPU remained unchanged in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells treated with either NC exosomes or MRO-knockdown exosomes (Fig.  M). Similarly, HNRNPU expression showed no significant difference between NC BC cells and BC cells overexpressing MRO (Fig.  N). However, nucleocytoplasmic fractionation followed by western blotting demonstrated that MRO overexpression reduced the cytoplasmic levels of HNRNPU while increasing its nuclear levels in BC cells (Fig.  O). Additionally, co-localization analysis showed that MRO and HNRNPU were predominantly co-localized in the nucleus of MCF-7 cells overexpressing MRO (Fig.  P). Taken together, these findings indicate that MRO functions as a nuclear import protein, facilitating the translocation of the transcription factor HNRNPU from the cytoplasm into the nucleus.

## MRO promotes the transcription of CCL5 in an HNRNPU-dependent manner
Previous studies indicated that HNRNPU can function as a splicing factor, regulating alternative splicing of genes [ ]. Additionally, it can bind to RNA polymerase II, acting as a transcription factor to promote the transcription of target genes [ ]. When functioning as a transcription factor, the transcriptional pattern of HNRNPU is consistent with that of the transcription factor CTCF [ ]. Therefore, to determine whether HNRNPU directly acts as a transcription factor for CCL5, we first retrieved the HNRNPU motif from the JASPAR database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/) (Fig.  A). Based on this motif, four potential HNRNPU binding sites were predicted in the CCL5 promoter region (Fig.  B). ChIP-qPCR assays revealed significant enrichment of HNRNPU at site 2 of the CCL5 promoter (Fig.  C).To further confirm this binding, dual-luciferase reporter plasmids containing either the WT CCL5 promoter or MUT CCL5 promoter were constructed (Fig.  D). The results demonstrated that mutation at site 2 significantly reduced luciferase activity in BC cells overexpressing HNRNPU (Fig.  E). Moreover, overexpression of HNRNPU increased both the mRNA and protein levels of CCL5, while HNRNPU knockdown led to the opposite effects (Fig.  F-G). Importantly, high levels of CCL5 protein were observed in BC tissues with elevated nuclear HNRNPU expression (Fig.  H). These findings suggested that HNRNPU directly binds to site 2 of the CCL5 promoter, driving its transcription.

 

To assess the role of HNRNPU in the pro-transcriptional effects of MRO on CCL5, we analyzed CCL5 transcription in BC cells with HNRNPU knockdown and in NC BC cells. qRT-PCR and western blotting showed that MRO significantly promoted CCL5 transcription in NC BC cells, but not in HNRNPU-knockdown cells (Fig.  I-J). These results collectively demonstrate that MRO promotes CCL5 transcription in an HNRNPU-dependent manner.

## BC cells with high MRO expression recruit more tumor-associated macrophages and promote M2 polarization
Previous studies indicated that CCL5 is a cytokine that can be autocrinally secreted by tumor cells, binding to their own CCR5 receptors to promote malignant behaviors [ ]. It can also be secreted in a paracrine manner, binding to CCR5 receptors on macrophages in the microenvironment, thereby inducing macrophage infiltration and M2 polarization [ ]. Therefore, to investigate the effects of MRO in BC cells on macrophages, we collected the respective conditioned medium (CM) and performed a transwell migration assay (Fig.  A). The results showed that CM from MRO-overexpressing BC cells exhibited a significantly stronger ability to induce M0 cell migration compared to CM from NC BC cells (Fig.  B). However, when a CCL5-neutralizing antibody was added to the CM, the migration-promoting effect of CM from MRO-overexpressing cells was significantly diminished (Fig.  B). These findings suggested that BC cells with high MRO expression recruit more tumor-associated macrophages.

 

Interesting, we found that migrated cells in CM treatment group from MRO-overexpressing BC cell exhibited more ARG1 and CCL17 mRNA levels compared with those in CM treatment group from NC cell (Fig.  C). However, addition of CCL5 antibody in CM can block these effects (Fig.  C). Furthermore, we observed that M0 cells treated with CM from MRO-overexpressing BC cells exhibited significantly higher expression levels of the M2 markers CD68 and CD206 compared to those treated with CM from NC BC cells (Fig.  D). However, these promotive effects were markedly reduced after CCL5 antibody neutralization (Fig.  D). Similarly, the expression patterns of M2-secreted proteins ARG1 and CCL17 were consistent with the expression trends of M2 surface markers CD68 and CD206 (Fig.  E-F). These findings indicate that BC cells with high MRO expression promote M2 macrophage polarization.

## Discussion
The pathogenesis of BC is influenced by a variety of factors, including genetic predisposition, hormonal effects, metabolic changes, and the impact of the tumor microenvironment [ ]. Among these, the components of the tumor microenvironment play a pivotal role in every stage of tumor initiation and progression [ ]. Substantial evidence highlights the role of TAMs, particularly the M2 sub-type, in promoting breast cancer growth and metastasis through immunosuppressive mechanisms [ ]. Investigating the pathways by which M2-like TAMs facilitate breast cancer proliferation and metastasis could provide valuable insights for developing novel therapeutic strategies.
MRO is a member of the newly identified maestro thermo-repeat family [ ] containing classic HEAT repeat domain [ ], firstly characterized by tissue-specific expression patterns [ ]. It is primarily expressed in human luteinizing granulosa cells, cumulus cells, and testicular germ cells [ ]. Notably, MRO expression is upregulated in diseased sexual organs, such as ovarian tissues from patients with thin polycystic ovary syndrome [ ]. Despite its specific expression in sex organ cells, male and female mice homozygous for MRO deletion display normal survival and fertility [ ], suggesting that MRO does not regulate reproduction but may serve other functions. Interestingly, analyses of the TCGA cohort revealed that MRO expression is not strictly confined to sex organ specificity. For instance, it is highly expressed in papillary thyroid cancer, highlighting its broader biological relevance. Consequently, the distribution, function, and molecular mechanisms of MRO have garnered significant attention. In this study, we present the first evidence that MRO plays a pivotal role in the interplay between M2-like TAMs and BC cells. M2-like TAMs secrete exosomes containing MRO mRNA, which are taken up by BC cells. Interesting, we found that M2-like TAMs cannot secrete MRO protein via exosomes. We speculate that this may be because the MRO-encoded protein has nuclear localization properties, making it less likely to be secreted extracellularly. Furthermore, once inside the breast cancer cells, MRO promotes tumor cell proliferation and metastasis, underscoring its potential as a key factor in cancer progression.
The cytokine microenvironment refers to the localized environment where cytokines facilitate intercellular communication and regulate various physiological processes [ ]. Cytokines are small peptides or glycoproteins synthesized and secreted by various tissue cells, primarily immune cells [ ]. They play a pivotal role in cell-to-cell interactions and exhibit a wide range of biological functions, including regulating cell growth, differentiation, maturation, maintaining cellular function, modulating immune responses, participating in inflammatory reactions, wound healing, and tumor growth [ ]. CCL5 (C-C motif chemokine ligand 5) is a chemokine belonging to the CC subfamily [ ]. It is a small secreted protein with a significant regulatory role in the immune response, capable of recruiting various blood cells, such as T cells, macrophages, eosinophils, and basophils, to sites of inflammation [ ]. Studies [ ] have shown that CCL5 positively correlates with the expression of M2-like TAM markers by binding to its receptor CCR5. Subsequently, CCL5 activates the AKT signaling pathway, leading to the recruitment and repolarization of TAMs. These TAMs then secrete CCL18, which further enhances the aggressive phenotype of tumors. Moreover, elevated CCL5 expression in various tumors has been linked to disease progression [ ], making it a potential molecular biomarker for tumor prognosis and a specific target for therapeutic intervention [ ]. In BC, CCL5 plays a crucial role in promoting cell invasion, metastasis, and regulating cellular metabolism [ ]. Through its interaction with CCR5, CCL5 significantly contributes to BC progression and represents a critical factor in tumor biology [ ].
In this study, we identified a novel regulatory mechanism of CCL5 in BC. We found that MRO derived from M2 macrophage exosomes can promote the transcriptional regulation of CCL5, thereby enhancing the malignant behavior of BC cells. Additionally, the MRO-mediated increase in CCL5 expression in BC cells can feedback to recruit more TAMs and induce M2 macrophage polarization, ultimately forming a feedback loop. Targeting for this feedback loop maybe a strategy for BC therapy.
The HEAT repeat domain consists of multiple repeat sequences, each comprising two antiparallel α-helices and two turns arranged around a common axis [ ]. Previous studies have shown that the HEAT repeat domain has potential to mediate protein-DNA interactions [ ]. Herein, we found that MRO regulates the transcription of CCL5, leading us to initially hypothesize that MRO, which contains a HEAT repeat domain, might be a novel transcription factor. However, contrary to our initial assumption, CHIP-seq experiments indicated that MRO does not function as a transcription factor. Instead, using artificial intelligence-based analysis, we predicted that MRO is a novel nuclear import protein.
In eukaryotes, the nuclear envelope separates the nucleus from the cytoplasm, allowing the nucleus and cytoplasm to perform specific functions independently [ ]. However, studies have shown that this nuclear-cytoplasmic balance is disrupted in tumor cells [ ]. In cancer cells, the expression of nuclear import and export proteins is significantly upregulated [ ]. Nuclear import proteins facilitate the entry of large numbers of transcription factors and DNA repair factors into the nucleus, leading to increased transcriptional activity and enhanced DNA repair in tumor cells [ ]. On the other hand, nuclear export proteins transport tumor suppressor factors out of the nucleus, reducing cell cycle arrest and transcriptional inhibition [ ]. As a result, various inhibitors targeting nuclear import/export, such as Leptomycin B, CBS9106, and KPT-330 [ ], have been developed and demonstrated antitumor effects in preclinical and clinical studies. Consistent with previous studies, we found that, as a nuclear import protein, MRO promoted the nuclear localization of HNRNPU, thus inducing increase of CCL5 transcribe.

## Conclusion
Taken together, we demonstrated that M2 macrophages transferred MRO mRNA into BC cells via exosome pathway. MRO in BC cells acts as a nuclear import protein, facilitating HNRNPU translocation into the nucleus, where it binds to the CCL5 promoter, enhancing its transcription. Elevated CCL5 drives BC malignancy, recruits additional macrophages, and promotes M2 polarization. MRO/HNRNPU/CCL5 feedback loop was existed in the crosstalk between M2 macrophages and BC cells, and targeting MRO signaling presents a promising therapeutic strategy for BC (Fig.  ).
