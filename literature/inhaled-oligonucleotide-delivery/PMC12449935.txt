## Background
Pulmonary hypertension is a pathophysiological condition characterized by multiple forms of regulated cell death. PANoptosis, which is an inflammation‐driven mode of regulated cell death, is regulated by the PANoptosome. Super‐enhancers (SEs) have been implicated in the pathology of pulmonary hypertension by modulating the transcriptional regulation of target genes. However, it remains unclear whether SEs influence the onset of PANoptosis in pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) via the regulation of specific target genes.

## Methods
Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)‐sequencing was integrated with RNA sequencing to identify SE‐regulated target genes in hypoxic PASMCs. Bioinformatics analysis was used to predict transcription factors with potential binding sites in the promoter or SE regions of these target genes. Coimmunoprecipitation and ChIP‐polymerase chain reaction were conducted to validate the epigenetic regulatory mechanisms governing SE‐regulated target genes. Immunoblotting was performed to assess the expression of PANoptosis proteins, whereas YP1 (YO‐PRO‐1; Oxazole yellow) and PI (propidium iodide) fluorescence staining and immunofluorescence were used to evaluate the effects of transcription factors and SE on PANoptosis in PASMCs. Additionally, reverse transcriptase–quantitative polymerase chain reaction, immunoblotting, and ChIP‐polymerase chain reaction assays were used to investigate the impact of the SE region of the target gene on PANoptosis in PASMCs following CRISPR‐Cas9 (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats‐associated protein 9)‐mediated knockdown of the SE regions of the target genes.

## Results
A combination of ChIP sequencing and RNA sequencing analyses confirmed that FOSL2 (Fos‐like antigen 2) is a target gene regulated by SEs. Bioinformatics predictions revealed that the transcription factor MAZ (myc‐associated zinc finger protein) has binding sites within both the SE and promoter regions of FOSL2. Coimmunoprecipitation and ChIP‐polymerase chain reaction experiments demonstrated that KAT7 (lysine acetyltransferase 7) interacts with H3K27ac (acetylation of lysine 27 of histone H3) and that circ_chr11_67292179‐67294612 (circ‐myh8) and KAT7 enhances H3K27ac enrichment in the SE region of FOSL2. CRISPR‐Cas9‐mediated knockdown of the FOSL2 SE resulted in a reduction in PANoptosis in PASMCs. Additionally, ChIP‐polymerase chain reaction assays revealed that FOSL2 functions as a transcription factor that binds to the promoters of PANoptosis‐related genes.

## Conclusions
Circ‐myh8 RNA, which functions jointly with KAT7, enhances H3K27ac enrichment, thereby promoting SE activity for FOSL2 transcription. This process ultimately contributes to the induction of PANoptosis in PASMCs. These findings elucidate the role of the circ‐myh8/KAT7/SE/FOSL2 axis in the regulation of PANoptosis in PASMCs, thus offering new insights into the pathophysiology of PANoptosis in pulmonary hypertension. These findings may lead to novel therapeutic strategies for the treatment of pulmonary hypertension.

## Hypoxia Increases FOSL2 Expression
To further verify the characterization of FOSL2, we initially performed immunofluorescence staining on mouse lung tissue and tissue sections from patients with Idiopathic Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension (iPAH). The results revealed that FOSL2 expression was upregulated in the lung tissues of PH model mice after 21 days of hypoxia and in tissues from patients with iPAH (Figure  , Figure  ). Additionally, RT‐qPCR and Western blot results revealed that the mRNA and protein expression levels of FOSL2 were increased in the lung tissues of hypoxic mice (Figure  through ). The Western blot results revealed that the expression of FOSL2 was elevated in patients with PAH compared with that in normal human smooth muscle cells (Figure  ). We subsequently examined the expression levels of FOSL2 in mPASMCs subjected to gradient hypoxia for 0 hours, 6 hours, 12 hours, 24 hours, and 48 hours. The results (Figure  and ) confirmed that the protein and mRNA expression levels of FOSL2 were increased under hypoxia at 12 hours, 24 hours, and 48 hours, with the most significant increase observed at 24 hours. Thus, 24 hours was selected as the hypoxia time point for subsequent experiments. Afterward, immunofluorescence staining revealed increased FOSL2 expression after 24 hours of hypoxia compared with the normoxic control (Figure  ). These in vivo and in vitro results demonstrate that FOSL2 expression is upregulated under hypoxic conditions.
 

## 
FOSL2 Affects PANoptosis in PASMCs

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed that the role of SEs in PH is associated with signaling pathways involving apoptosis and inflammasomes (Figure  ). We subsequently explored the role of FOSL2 in the expression of PANoptosis‐related markers by knocking down FOSL2 via siRNA. We first verified the efficiency of the 3 siRNA fragments, and the results (Figure  ) revealed that fragment 1 displayed the highest interference efficiency among the 3 siRNA fragments; thus, fragment 1 was used for further experiments. Afterward, RT‐qPCR and Western blot results revealed that the mRNA and protein expression levels of ZBP1, CASP8, NLRP3, and MLKL were decreased after FOSL2 knockdown in hypoxia‐treated PASMCs (Figure  and ). YP1/PI experiments also revealed a decrease in the number of positive PASMCs after both hypoxia and interference with FOSL2 (Figure  ). Additionally, LDH release was significantly reduced after FOSL2 knockdown (Figure  ). To further demonstrate the effect of FOSL2 on PANoptosis in PASMCs, immunofluorescence experiments for the PANoptosis markers ASC, RIPK3, and CASP8 were performed. The expression levels of these genes were increased due to hypoxia but were significantly reduced after FOSL2 interference (Figure  ). These results indicate that FOSL2 knockdown inhibits the occurrence of PANoptosis in PASMCs caused by hypoxia.
 

## 
SEs Regulate FOSL2 Expression and PANoptosis

Having previously demonstrated that FOSL2 is a target gene for SEs and has an effect on PANoptosis in PASMCs, we further explored the impact of FOSL2 SEs on PANoptosis in PASMCs. Previous experiments have indicated that the second SE region of FOSL2 exhibits the most significant regulatory effect; thus, we performed CRISPR‐Cas9‐mediated knockdown of this region (Figure  ). RT‐qPCR was used to assess the knockdown efficiency, and the agarose gel results confirmed successful knockdown of the second SE region of FOSL2 (Figure  ). Both RT‐qPCR and Western blot analyses revealed decreased mRNA and protein levels of FOSL2 after FOSL2‐SE knockdown (Figure  and ). Despite previous findings suggesting that FOSL2 participates in the PANoptosis of PASMCs, the underlying mechanism remains unclear. To further elucidate the regulatory network of FOSL2 in PANoptosis, we identified binding sites for the FOSL2 transcription factor on the PANoptosis‐related indicator promoters by using the database predictions of the Global Transcriptional Regulatory Database (GTRD), Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE), Human Transcription Factor Targets (hTFtarget), and JASPAR databases (Figure  ). ChIP assays using the FOSL2 antibody, followed by qPCR with primers for PANoptosis indicators (ZBP1, CASP8, NLRP3, and MLKL), demonstrated the binding of FOSL2 to the promoter regions of PANoptosis indicators. This binding was reduced after FOSL2‐SE knockdown via CRISPR‐Cas9 (Figure  ). The RT‐qPCR and Western blot results further revealed decreased expression levels of the PANoptosis‐related indicators ZBP1, CASP8, NLRP3, and MLKL after FOSL2‐SE knockdown (Figure  and ). Additionally, FOSL2‐SE knockdown significantly decreased LDH release (Figure  ). YP1/PI assays revealed reduced numbers of YP1‐positive cells (indicating apoptosis or necroptosis) and PI‐positive cells (indicating necroptosis and pyroptosis) in PASMCs after FOSL2‐SE knockdown (Figure  ). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CRISPR‐Cas9‐mediated knockdown of FOSL2‐SE reduces FOSL2 expression, thereby attenuating PANoptosis.
 To further investigate the effects of FOSL2‐SE on FOSL2 and PANoptosis, we treated mPASMCs with the SE inhibitors JQ1 and iBET. Western blot analysis revealed that H3K27ac and FOSL2 protein expression levels were lower in the JQ1‐ and iBET‐treated groups than in the control group (Figure  and ). Similarly, RT‐qPCR experiments revealed decreased FOSL2 mRNA levels (Figure  ). Moreover, after mECs were treated with the SE inhibitors JQ1 and iBET, the mRNA and protein levels of FOSL2 did not change (Figure  through ). Additionally, the ELISA, RT‐qPCR, and Western blot results demonstrated the downregulation of PANoptosis indicators (ZBP1, CASP8, NLRP3, and MLKL) following JQ1 and iBET treatment (Figure  through ). Furthermore, immunofluorescence of the PANoptosis indicators known as RIPK3, ASC, and CASP8 demonstrated reduced fluorescence intensity (Figure  ). These results demonstrate the effects of SEs on FOSL2 expression and PANoptosis.
 

## 
MAZ Regulates FOSL2 Transcription by Binding to the FOSL2 Promoter and SE Sequence
Database prediction (JASPAR database and others) revealed that the FOSL2 SE sequence in the enriched H3K27ac region highly aligned with transcription factor MAZ motifs. Bioinformatics analyses also revealed FOSL2 as a target gene of MAZ in lung tissues (Figure  and ). Additionally, the performance of ChIP with the MAZ antibody, followed by qPCR with primers specific for the promoter and SE regions of FOSL2, revealed increased MAZ binding in these regions (Figure  ). We subsequently examined the effects of MAZ and FOSL2‐SE on FOSL2 expression by constructing luciferase reporter plasmids for the 3 SE segments and their mutants. Dual‐luciferase assays revealed that cotransfection with MAZ and SE plasmids resulted in increased luciferase activity, whereas the SE mutation led to significantly decreased activity (Figure  through ). Western blot analyses revealed increasing MAZ expression levels due to gradient hypoxia levels at 0 hours, 6 hours, 12 hours, 24 hours, and 48 hours (Figure  ). Additionally, the siRNA interference efficiency of MAZ was determined, and the results indicated that fragment 1 and fragment 2 of the 3 MAZ siRNA fragments exhibited increased interference efficiency (Figure  ). Thus, siRNA fragment 2 of MAZ was used in subsequent experiments. RT‐qPCR and Western blot analyses revealed that the mRNA and protein levels of FOSL2 were reduced after MAZ knockdown in hypoxia‐treated PASMCs (Figure  and ). These results demonstrate that the transcription factor MAZ could affect FOSL2 expression by binding to FOSL2‐SE.
 

## Circ‐myh8 Recruits Lysine Acetyltransferase 7 to Affect the H3K27ac of FOSL2

Previous research in our laboratory demonstrated that circ‐myh8 can bind to KAT7 (lysine acetyltransferase 7) and recruit it as a modular scaffold, thereby increasing the acetylation level of H4K5 in the HIF1α (Hypoxia‐inducible factor 1 alpha) promoter region. Coimmunoprecipitation experiments revealed that KAT7 can interact with H3K27ac, with the interaction increasing under hypoxic conditions but decreasing after circ‐myh8 knockdown (Figure  ). We subsequently assessed the siRNA interference efficiency for KAT7, identifying fragment 1 as the most effective among the 3 siRNA fragments (Figure  ). Knockdown experiments using siRNA were performed to further investigate the effects of circ‐myh8 and KAT7 on H3K27ac enrichment. Western blot analysis revealed that H3K27ac expression was increased in hypoxic mPASMCs but was significantly decreased following circ‐myh8 and KAT7 knockdown (Figure  ). Consequently, siRNA fragment 1 was selected for subsequent experiments. After circ‐myh8 and KAT7 were silenced in mPASMCs, ChIP was performed with an H3K27ac antibody, followed by qPCR with primers targeting the promoter and 3 SE regions of FOSL2. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in H3K27ac signal in both the promoter and SE regions of FOSL2 following interference with circ‐myh8 and KAT7 (Figure  and ). These findings suggest that circ‐myh8 influences H3K27ac enrichment by recruiting KAT7.
 

## Circ‐myh8 Affects Pulmonary Artery PANoptosis via FOSL2

To further explore the impact of circ‐myh8 on FOSL2 and its role in a hypoxia‐induced PH mouse model, we used lentiviral short hairpin RNA to elicit circ‐myh8 knockdown (Figure  ). For the hypoxia‐induced PH group, the mice were maintained under normoxic conditions for 2 weeks, followed by 3 weeks of hypoxia. RT‐qPCR analysis confirmed the successful knockdown of circ‐myh8 (Figure  ). Right heart catheterization further revealed a significant reduction in RVSP in the circ‐myh8 knockdown group compared with the hypoxia group (Figure  ). Additionally, the right ventricular hypertrophy index (RV/LV + S, weight of Right Ventricular/ weight of Left Ventricular + Septum) was notably reduced upon circ‐myh8 knockdown (Figure  ). Hematoxylin and eosin and Masson staining revealed that the knockdown of circ‐myh8 significantly inhibited hypoxia‐induced fibrosis and thickening of the vascular wall (Figure  and ). These findings collectively suggest that the knockdown of circ‐myh8 alleviated hypoxia‐induced PH. However, the impact of circ‐myh8 knockdown on FOSL2 expression was still unknown. To address this issue, we performed immunofluorescence, RT‐qPCR, and Western blot analyses. The results demonstrated that circ‐myh8 was upregulated in patients with iPAH (idiopathic pulmonary arterial hypertension) and that the knockdown of circ‐myh8 in a hypoxia‐induced PH mouse model led to a reduction in both the mRNA and protein expression levels of FOSL2 (Figure  ; Figure  and ). Moreover, statistical analysis of the SMA (smooth muscle actin) immunofluorescence results revealed that the immunofluorescence area and integrated fluorescence intensity of the circ‐myh8 knockdown model mice were reduced; however, the mean fluorescence intensity was unchanged. Therefore, the results demonstrated that α‐SMA was not regulated by circ‐myh8 (Figure  through ). The Western blot results demonstrated the downregulation of PANoptosis indicators (ZBP1, CASP8, MLKL, NLRP3, and CASP1) in the lung tissue of PH model mice following circ‐myh8 knockdown (Figure  ). Additionally, immunofluorescence revealed that ZBP1 and CASP8 were upregulated in patients with iPAH and that the expression levels of ZBP1 and CASP8 were also diminished in the lung tissue of PH mice following circ‐myh8 knockdown (Figure  and ; Figure  and ). In conclusion, these results suggest that circ‐myh8 modulates PANoptosis pathways via the regulation of FOSL2.
 

## DISCUSSION
This study demonstrated 3 essential discoveries. First, we observed that FOSL2 (a gene regulated by SEs) participated in the PANoptosis of PASMCs, thus affecting PH. Second, circ‐myh8 was shown to recruit KAT7, thereby leading to increased H3K27ac modification of FOSL2. This modification increased SE activity, thus facilitating the binding of the transcription factor MAZ to both the promoter and SE regions of FOSL2, which increased FOSL2 expression. Third, the knockdown of circ‐myh8 decreased FOSL2 expression and reduced PANoptosis in PASMCs, thereby reversing pulmonary vascular remodeling in PH (Figure  ). These results indicate that the circ‐myh8/KAT7 axis regulates FOSL2 expression via SEs and emphasize the crucial role of FOSL2 in PH pathogenesis via its impact on PANoptosis, thus suggesting that it is a potential therapeutic target for PH.
 PANoptosis, which is a newly identified form of regulated cell death, integrates 3 distinct cell death pathways: pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis. This interaction can integrate various components of other cell death modes to form multiple PANoptosome complexes, such as the ZBP1‐, Absent in Melanoma 2 (AIM2)‐, Receptor‐Interacting Serine/Threonine Kinase 1 (RIPK1)‐, and NLR Family Pyrin Domain Containing 12 (NLRP12)‐PANoptosome. Both pyroptosis and necroptosis are inflammatory types of regulated cell death that involve the release of intracellular components, which act as damage‐associated molecular patterns to trigger a robust inflammatory response. Although apoptosis has been traditionally considered to be a noninflammatory type of regulated cell death, recent studies have shown that the activation of the apoptosis marker CASP3 in cells can promote the expression of GSDME (gasdermin E), which induces secondary necrosis/pyroptosis. These findings suggest that the pathways governing apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis are not independent but exhibit significant crosstalk with interconnected signaling pathways. In rectal cancer, the activation of ZBP1‐NLRP3 inflammasome complexes can induce PANoptosis, thereby inhibiting cancer suppression ; this highlights the significance of PANoptosis in disease pathology and addresses a gap in the current understanding of PANoptosis in PH.
Moreover, the role of PANoptosis‐associated inflammasomes in autoinflammation, disease development, and cancer has provided valuable insights into the function of PANoptosis in innate immunity and inflammatory cell death, thus allowing for the identification of new therapeutic targets for inflammasome‐mediated diseases. , , Additionally, TNF‐α (Tumor necrosis factor)/IL (interleukin)‐1β‐regulated PANoptosis in SMCs (smooth muscle cells) has emerged as a novel target for mitigating the progression of abdominal aortic aneurysms. Preclinical studies have highlighted the involvement of cellular pyroptosis in PH development, and reports in the literature have linked necroptosis‐induced cell loss in PH to focal cell death during organ damage. This finding implies that necroptosis, which is an upstream event of focal cell death, plays a critical role in right ventricular injury in PH. Furthermore, CASP8 has been shown to participate in the production of inflammasome‐derived IL‐1β by macrophages, which promotes smooth muscle cell proliferation and perivascular inflammatory cell infiltration in the lungs of patients with PH. Moreover, the inhibition of CASP8 has been demonstrated to delay PH progression. 

Pronecrotic proteins, such as RIPK3 and MLKL (particularly their nonphosphorylated forms), have been shown to regulate the formation of the NLRP3 inflammasome complex, thereby promoting PANoptosis. In the present study, we primarily focused on the expression of their nonphosphorylated forms. Our findings demonstrated that the expression levels of PANoptosis‐related markers, including ZBP1, CASP8, NLRP3, and MLKL, were significantly upregulated under hypoxic conditions. The inhibition of SE activity has been proposed as a promising strategy for cancer treatment. In this context, we used CRISPR‐Cas9 to elicit the knockdown of FOSL2‐SE and used SE inhibitors (JQ1 and iBET) to reduce FOSL2 expression, which ultimately revealed decreased expression levels of PANoptosis‐related markers. These experimental results further elucidate the connection between SEs and PANoptosis in PH and suggest a novel approach for regulating PANoptosis in PH via the modulation of SEs activity.
FOSL2, which is a member of the transcription factor AP1 (activator protein 1) family, is extensively expressed in various tissues and contextually regulates gene transcription. It influences cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, and inflammation. FOSL2 has been linked to several diseases, including lung fibrosis, lung carcinogenesis, and breast cancer. Additionally, previous studies have shown that the EphA2 SE contributes to tumor progression and angiogenesis, as well as RV dysfunction in PH, by recruiting FOSL2 and TCF7L2 (Transcription factor 7 like 2). , Nevertheless, the exact mechanism by which FOSL2 impacts PH remains unclear. In our study, we investigated the role of FOSL2 in PANoptosis by disrupting its expression, and the results showed that inhibiting FOSL2 significantly reduced the expression levels of PANoptosis‐related markers. Bioinformatics analysis and ChIP‐qPCR experiments confirmed that FOSL2 binds to the promoters of PANoptosis‐related genes, such as Zbp1, Casp8, Nlrp3, and Mlkl, thereby regulating their transcription. These results suggest that FOSL2 contributes to the pathogenesis of PH by modulating PANoptosis.
In this study, the bioinformation database prediction and corresponding sequencing results of SEs were conducted exclusively in PASMCs. The promoter and 3 SE regions of FOSL2 were also specific to PASMCs of mice. ChIP‐qPCR results performed in endothelial cells demonstrated that SE did not influence FOSL2 expression. Moreover, literature reported that FOSL2 expression was upregulated in the endothelial cells of PH mice, and our results were consistent with this finding. The FOSL2 expression increased in pulmonary artery endothelial cells (PAECs) under hypoxic conditions but did not change after the addition of SE inhibitors JQ1 and iBET. Therefore, we believed that there were mechanisms other than SE regulation contributing to the increased FOSL2 expression in PAECs under hypoxic conditions; however, specific mechanisms require further exploration. Additionally, our research group is investigating PANoptosis in PAECs and the potential involvement of FOSL2 in this process.
The MAZ gene is located on human chromosome 16p11.2 and is expressed in various tissues. MAZ proteins are essential for the initiation and termination of the transcription of multiple genes. Moreover, increased MAZ expression is linked to several diseases. For example, in hepatocellular carcinoma, increased MAZ expression is closely associated with tumor development and poor prognosis. MAZ also contributes to prostate cancer progression by driving transcription and promotes thyroid cancer progression by enhancing Extracellular Signal‐Regulated Kinase 1 and 2 (ERK1/2) activation. MAZ is also highly upregulated in chronic inflammatory diseases. Although these studies have highlighted the involvement of MAZ as a transcription factor in cancer and inflammatory diseases, its role in PH has not been reported. In this study, we identified MAZ binding sites in both the promoter and SEs regions of FOSL2 via database predictions (eg, via the JASPAR database). This finding was confirmed via ChIP‐qPCR and dual luciferase reporter assays, which revealed that MAZ regulates FOSL2 transcriptional activity. Furthermore, the interference of MAZ expression was demonstrated to affect FOSL2 levels. However, whether MAZ influences the PANoptosis mechanism in PH via FOSL2 remains to be determined.
Our previous study demonstrated that circ‐myh8 recruits KAT7 as a modular scaffold to promote proliferation and cell cycle progression in PASMCs. This mechanism does not contradict the PANoptosis effect of circ‐myh8/KAT7 observed in the current study, whereby it influenced PASMCs via SE‐mediated regulation of FOSL2 expression. Under hypoxic conditions, PASMC proliferation contributes to the thickening of the pulmonary vascular wall and exacerbates the inflammatory response. This can trigger focal and necroptosis, as well as protective apoptosis mechanisms, thus leading to increased pulmonary vascular resistance. We further demonstrated that circ‐myh8/KAT7 promotes PASMC proliferation by enhancing H4K5ac at the HIF1α promoter region. Additionally, circ‐myh8/KAT7 was observed to influence PANoptosis in PASMCs by facilitating H3K27ac enrichment, thereby upregulating FOSL2 expression via SEs regulation. Our findings revealed that circ‐myh8/KAT7 regulates both PASMC proliferation and PANoptosis via distinct signaling pathways, thus impacting pulmonary vascular resistance. This study is the first to demonstrate the role of SEs and PANoptosis in the pathogenesis of PH, thus providing a novel target for therapeutic intervention.

## CONCLUSIONS
Our research demonstrated that SEs play crucial roles in PH by modulating FOSL2 expression, thus influencing PANoptosis in PASMCs. Moreover, we provide novel evidence that circ‐myh8/KAT7 plays a key role in regulating PANoptosis both in vitro and in vivo via the SE‐driven modulation of FOSL2 expression.
