## ABSTRACT
Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is a biliary tract cancer with a poor prognosis. Consistent evidence suggests that fasting has extensive antitumor effects in various cancers and influences levels of poly (rC)-binding protein 2 (PCBP2). However, whether fasting and PCBP2 are involved in GBC remains unknown. We assessed the expression of PCBP2 in GBC tumor tissues and cells. Knockdown and overexpression of PCBP2, combined with in vitro and in vivo assays using fasting mimic medium or diets, were conducted to provide functional significance. The effect of PCBP2 on glycolysis was assessed by glucose uptake, lactate production, oxygen consumption rate, and limiting glycolytic-associated enzymes (PDK1, PKM2, and HK-2). We found that fasting could inhibit glycolysis and cell migration/invasion in GBC cells and that fasting mimic diets could significantly inhibit GBC cell proliferation in a mouse xenograft model. PBCP2 was upregulated in GBC tumor tissues and cells. Moreover, PCBP2 is a key downstream target of fasting, and fasting decreases PCBP2 expression in GBC cells. PCBP2 knockdown inhibits GBC cell proliferation, migration/invasion, and glycolysis, whereas PCBP2 overexpression has the opposite effect. Through co-immunoprecipitation, we identified a physical connection between PCBP2 and the angiopoietin-like protein ANGPTL4. PCBP2 can negatively regulate the expression of ANGPTL4. Hence, fasting inhibits cell proliferation, migration/invasion, and glycolysis through PCBP2/ANGPTL4 signaling. We conclude that PCBP2 is a target of fasting and is involved in cell migration/invasion and glycolysis through the negative regulation of ANGPTL4 in GBC. PCBP2 represents a potential therapeutic target for GBC.

## Introduction
Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is a malignancy of the biliary tract, endemic in specific regions, and often associated with poor prognosis because of late diagnosis [ , ]. GBC is believed to be associated with cholelithiasis because it frequently occurs in patients with a high incidence of gallstones. However, genetic factors relating to an anomalous pancreaticobiliary junction may also be involved in its development [ , ]. Moreover, an increasing number of studies implicate dietary factors, such as high-fat diets and advanced glycation end-products, in the carcinogenesis of GBC [ ].
Several cancers have been linked to metabolic diseases such as diabetes, obesity, and fatty liver disease [ , ]. Glycolysis is known to be enhanced in cancer cells in a process known as the Warburg effect [ ]. An increase in glucose consumption through glycolysis in cancer cells can lead to elevated levels of ATP and metabolic enzymes, such as phosphoinositide-dependent kinase-1 (PDK1), pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), and hexokinase 2 (HK-2), to sustain the biosynthesis of tumor cells [ ]. Fasting or fasting mimic diets (FMDs) are being adopted as strategies in the management of these cancers [ ]. Nutrient deprivation leads to alterations in growth factors and metabolites and reduces the proliferation of cancer cells [ ].
The expression of poly(rC)-binding protein 2 (PCBP2) is known to be influenced by fasting [ ]. Moreover, PCBP2 is becoming recognized as a gene associated with the promotion of tumorigenesis, enhancing cell proliferation, and facilitating metastasis [ ]. PCBP2 is differentially regulated in several cancers and is associated with a poor prognosis in cancers related to metabolic diseases, including hepatocellular carcinoma [ ], gastric carcinoma [ ], and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [ ]. However, whether PCBP2 has a role in GBC is unclear.
Proteins in the angiopoietin-like protein (ANGPTL) family are known to suppress angiogenesis, cancer invasion, metastasis, and cancer progression [ , ]. Angiopoietin-like protein 4 (ANGPTL4) is upregulated during fasting and is known to promote glucose uptake and glycolysis activity in cancer cells [ ]. Moreover, variation in the levels of ANGPTL4 can lead to a poor prognosis in patients with GBC [ ]. In this study, we demonstrate that PCBP2 can modify the expression of ANGPTL4 in GBC. In addition, we investigate the influence of fasting on glycolysis in GBC cell lines and assess the effects of FMD in a xenograft mouse model of GBC. Our results indicate that PCBP2 is a target of fasting and is involved in cell migration, invasion, and glycolysis through the regulation of ANGPTL4. Our study indicates that PCBP2 could be a therapeutic target in GBC.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture
The four human gallbladder cancer cell lines (GBC-SD, SGC-996, NOZ, and EH-GB1) used in this study were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in a Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2. The fasting mimic medium comprised glucose-free DMEM supplemented with 0.5 g/L glucose and 1% FBS. Fasting was mimicked by incubating cells in this medium for 24 or 48 h.

## Clinical samples
Tumors and adjacent tissues were from 36 patients with GBC who attended Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University. The patients had provided written consent, and the study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Shanghai, China) (Ethics No. Y-2021–467).

## Cell transfection
The vectors used in this study were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Vectors were transfected into GBC and control cells using Lipofectamine 2000 Transfection Agent (#11668019; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. In siRNA-mediated silencing, the target RNA was transfected into cells using Lipofectamine RNAiMax (#13778100; Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequences for PCBP2 knockdown are shown in .
 

## Cell counting assay
Cells from different growth conditions were plated in 96-well plates (1 × 104 cells/well). Cell proliferation was determined using cell counting kit‐8 (CCK‐8; #SKU: CK04, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) and measured using a microplate reader according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Colony formation
Colony formation was measured in transfected GBC and control cells. Cells were plated onto six-well plates (500 cells/well) and cultured under experimental conditions. After incubation, cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde for 20 min and then stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 30 min. Colonies were then counted.

## Quantitative RT-PCR
Total RNA was extracted from cells using Trizol reagent (#15596026, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the concentration of DNA was determined using a NanoDrop 8000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA was reverse-transcribed using a First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (#AT341–01, TransGen, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative PCR was conducted using a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The details of the primers used in the study can be found in . Sequence data were analyzed relative to β-actin or GAPDH using the comparative cycle threshold (2–∆∆Ct) method.

## Western blot analysis
Total protein was extracted from cells using cell lysis buffer (#FNN0011, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and quantified using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (#23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were separated with sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to Immobilon PVDF membranes (#IPVH00010, Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk and then incubated with the following antibodies overnight at 4°C: PCBP2 (ab200835, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), ANGPTL4 (ab196746, Abcam), PDK1 (ab202468, Abcam);# PKM2, HK2 (ab227198, Abcam); GAPDH (ab8245, Abcam); and β-actin (ab8227, Abcam). After incubation, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488, ab150077, Abcam) for an hour at room temperature, and then proteins were detected using Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate (#32109; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Band density was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

## Glycolysis analysis
Glycolysis was detected in GBC and control cells by using Glucose Uptake Fluorometric Assay Kits (#K666–100, Biovision, Milpitas, CA, USA) and Lactate Colorimetric Assay Kits (#K627–100, Biovision) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

## Oxygen consumption rate (OCR)
Cellular oxygen consumption in the GBC-SD and SGC-996 cell lines was determined using a Seahorse Bioscience XF96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience, North Billerica, MA, USA). Briefly, 2 × 104 cells were seeded into 96-well cell culture XF microplates and incubated overnight. After OCR measurements, cells in each well were lysed, and protein quantification was conducted using a BCA protein assay kit (Pierce). The OCR was calculated after normalization to total protein content and is plotted as the mean ± SD. The results were expressed as OCR (μmol/min/mg protein).

## Animal experiments
All the animal experiments performed in this study were in accordance with the procedures approved by the institutional review board of Zhongshan Hospital. In this study, we used BALB/c mice (aged 4–6 weeks) obtained from Shanghai Zhongshan Hospital. All the animal experiments were conducted in the animal lab in Zhongshan Hospital and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee. The mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12/12 h light/dark cycle. GBC-SD or SGC-996 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of the mice. When the tumors were palpable, the mice were randomly assigned to the control group and the FMD group by drawing numbers. In the control group, the average daily consumption was 14.9 kJ. The FMD consisted of three components, designated as the day 1 diet, day 2–3 diet, and day 4–7 diet, fed in this order. The day 1 diet contained only 50% of the calories of the normal intake. The day 2–3 diet contained only 10% of the calories of the normal intake. During the day 4–7 phase of the diet, the mice were fed their normal intake. This progression was followed by another cycle of the FMD. The animals had free access to water. The mice were further monitored, and the subcutaneous tumors were measured throughout the 5 weeks. At the end of the experiment, the mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation. The tumors were dissected and weighed, and images were obtained.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Immunohistochemical analyses were conducted using the paraffin sections of the tumor and adjacent tissue from 36 patients with GBC. The sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with antibodies toward PCBP2 and ANGPTL4 and then stained with a Diaminobenzidine Staining Kit (#34002, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to instructions.

## Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP)
Cells were lysed with cell lysis buffer as mentioned in Section 2.7 and centrifuged at 4,000×g for 20 min. The supernatant and target antibody (PCBP2, #ab200835, Abcam) were incubated overnight at 4°C. Protein A/G Sepharose (3.0 mg, #P2012; Beyotime Biosciences, Jiangsu, China) was added to the lysate and incubated for 2 hours. The A-Sepharose beads with bound immune complexes were washed in co-IP buffer and then boiled in SDS-PAGE sample buffer for 5 min. The samples were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 1 min, and then proteins were identified by western blot analysis.

## Wound healing and cell invasion assay
To determine the migration and invasion of cells, wound healing and Transwell assays were conducted. For the wound healing assay, cells (5 × 106) were seeded onto six‐well plates and grown under experimental conditions. On reaching 80% confluence, a scratch was created on the surface of the cells with a 200‐μL pipette tip. Images were obtained at 0 and 48 hours to determine the level of wound closure. For the invasion assay, cells (1 × 105) were seeded onto an upper Transwell pre-coated with Matrigel in serum‐free DMEM (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The lower chamber contained DMEM with 20% FBS. After 24 hours, cells that invaded the lower chamber were stained with 0.5% crystal violet and counted.

## Statistical analysis
All data shown in this study were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.0. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SD). All experiments were replicated at least three times. For comparison between two groups, normal distribution was first verified using Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and then paired Student’s t-test was performed to assess statistical significance. One-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was performed to assess significance when comparing multiple groups. p < 0.05 is considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Fasting inhibits cell proliferation, migration/invasion, and glycolysis in GBC cells
We first determined the influence of fasting on GBC in vitro by measuring proliferation, invasion, and glycolysis in two GBC cell lines (GBC-SD and SGC-996) cultured in fasting mimic media. CCK-8 proliferation and colony formation assays indicated that fasting for 24 and 48 hours significantly lowered cell viability and reduced cell proliferation ( ). Invasion and migration ability were also impeded in GBC-SD and SGC-996 cells cultured in fasting mimic medium ( ). In addition, levels of glucose uptake and lactate production were lower in GBC-SD and SGC-996 cells after fasting ( ), whereas OCR, ATP, and maximal respiration were increased ( ). This indicated that fasting decreased glycolysis. This was confirmed by the downregulation of rate-limiting glycolytic enzymes (PDK1, PKM2, and HK-2) in GBC-SD and SGC-996 cells cultured in fasting mimic media ( ). Our results imply that fasting can prevent the glucose uptake and glycolysis that are conducive to the proliferation of cancer cells.

 

## PCBP2 is upregulated in GBC tumor tissues and cells
As PCBP2 is associated with fasting, we next determined the expression levels of PCBP2 in GBC. Using TCGA data and IHC, we confirmed that the expression of PCBP2 is elevated in cholangiocarcinoma and GBC tissue compared to normal controls. However, the association between PCBP2 and prognosis in cholangiocarcinoma is weak in the TCGA database because of the limited sample size ( ). The expression of PCBP2 was also elevated in GBC cell lines ( ). Moreover, CCK-8 and colony formation assays revealed that cell proliferation was inhibited by PCBP2 knockdown but enhanced by PCBP2 overexpression in both SGC-996 and GBC-SD cells ( ). A nuclear mass separation assay indicated that PCBP2 was expressed both in the cytoplasm and nucleus of GBC-SD and SGC-996 cells ( ).

 

## Expression of PCBP2 is targeted and decreased during fasting
Having established that fasting could prevent cell proliferation and glycolysis in GBC cells, we next determined the association between PCBP2 expression and fasting. We discovered that fasting could lower the expression of PCBP2 in GBC cells ( ). We then assessed the influence of fasting on the level of PCBP2 expression when it was inhibited or overexpressed. Fasting further inhibited the expression of PCBP2 after knockdown or overexpression ( ). Moreover, the knockdown of PCBP2 could reduce the viability and proliferation of GBC cells more than fasting alone ( ). In contrast, the overexpression of PCBP2 could increase the level of cell viability and proliferation, but fasting reduced the impact of PCBP2 overexpression on viability and proliferation in GBC cells ( ). Similar findings were obtained in a xenograft mouse model of GBC. The knockdown of PCBP2 combined with an FMD resulted in a smaller tumor size and volume in mice ( ). Although fasting could reduce tumor growth, inhibiting the expression of PCBP2 could further reduce tumorigenesis in vivo.

 

## Fasting suppresses GBC cell metastasis and glycolysis via PCBP2 regulation
In agreement with our findings on cell viability and proliferation, the overexpression of PCBP2 significantly increases cell migration and invasion in GBC-SD cells. However, fasting reduces the level of cell migration and invasion ( ). In contrast to PCBP2 overexpression, the knockdown of PCBP2 inhibits SGC-966 cell migration and invasion, and these are further reduced by fasting ( ). A wound healing assay confirmed these results ( ). To determine if these characteristics were influenced by glycolysis, we measured glucose uptake and lactate production in GBC cells with PCBP2 inhibited or overexpressed. Fasting consistently reduced glucose uptake and lactate production in GBC cells, whereas the overexpression of PCBP2 significantly increased both glucose uptake and lactate production ( ). Inhibiting the expression of PCBP2 significantly reduced the level of glucose uptake and lactate production, suggesting that PCBP2 is involved in the glycolysis of GBC cells. OCR and the expression of rate-limiting glycolytic enzymes in glucose appeared to substantiate the involvement of PCBP2 in glycolysis ( ). Levels of PDK1, PKM2, and HK-2 were higher when PCBP2 was overexpressed, but fasting reduced levels of glycolysis under all conditions assessed.

 

## PCBP2 interacts with ANGPTL and modulates its expression
ANGPTL4 was found to interact with PCBP2 in co-IP in both GBC-SD and SGC-996 cells ( , B). Further characterization indicated that when the expression of PCBP2 was inhibited, the expression of ANGPTL4 was increased, whereas the opposite occurred when PCBP2 was overexpressed. The overexpression of PCBP2 decreased the expression of ANGPTL4 ( ). In addition, the expression of ANGPTL4 was assessed in response to fasting. Fasting for 48 h significantly increased the expression of ANGPTL4 ( ). However, the overexpression of PCBP2 could lower the overall expression of ANGPTL4 during fasting, whereas inhibiting PCBP2 could enhance the expression of ANGPTL4. TCGA data indicated that the expression of ANGPTL4 is lower in cholangiocarcinoma ( ). Similarly, ANGPTL4 is downregulated in GBC tissue (n = 36) compared to non-cancerous adjacent tissue, and the IHC results of tissue sections confirm these results ( ). To provide further evidence, ANGPTL4 was downregulated in GBC cells compared to normal EH-GB1 cells ( ). These results suggest that PCBP2 binds to ANGPTL4 and negatively regulates ANGPTL4 expression to promote tumorigenesis in GBC.

 

## PCBP2 regulates cell migration/invasion and glycolysis through ANGPTL4
To examine the interaction between PCBP2 and ANGPTL4 in greater detail, GBC-SD cells were co-transfected with pcDNA/pcDNA-PCBP2 and vector/ANGPTL4, and SGC-996 cells were co-transfected with sh-NC/sh-PCBP2 and shCtrl/shANGPTL4. Migration and invasion were at the highest levels when PCBP2 was overexpressed or ANGPTL4 expression was inhibited ( ). The lowest migration and invasion occurred when ANGPTL4 was overexpressed or PCBP2 was inhibited. When both PCBP2 and ANGPTL4 were either overexpressed or inhibited in the same cells, migration and invasion resembled control levels ( ). Wound healing assays confirmed the migration ability of GBC-SD and SGC-996 cells with PCBP2 and ANGPTL4 either overexpressed or inhibited ( ). Relative glucose uptake and lactate production were highest when PCBP2 was overexpressed and lowest when ANGPTL4 was overexpressed ( ). When PCBP2 and ANGPTL4 are either overexpressed or inhibited simultaneously, glucose uptake and lactate production are comparable to the control. OCR analysis and the expression of rate-limiting glycolytic enzymes in glucose confirm that glycolysis increases with PCBP2 overexpression and decreases with ANGPTL4 overexpression. The simultaneous overexpression or inhibition of both PCBP2 and ANGPTL4 results in glycolytic activity comparable to the control ( ). These results indicate that PCBP2 regulates GBC cell migration/invasion and glycolysis through targeting ANGPTL4.

 

## Discussion
An increasing number of cancers are associated with dietary-related factors [ ]. For instance, metabolic syndrome is related to several cancers, including cholangiocarcinoma [ ]. Moreover, the deprivation of nutrients and metabolites is known to reduce the survival and proliferation of cancer cells [ ]. In particular, a reduction in hyperactive glucose uptake and aerobic glycolysis is believed to suppress tumorigenesis [ ]. In this study, the role of fasting is examined relative to the expression of PCBP2 and ANGPTL4 in GBC.
We found that PCBP2 is upregulated in GBC tumor tissues and cells. PCBP2 knockdown inhibits cell proliferation, whereas its overexpression promotes cell proliferation. Several other studies have found that PCBP2 is overexpressed in cancer and that it contributes to the proliferation of cancer cells [ , , ]. For instance, in hepatocellular carcinoma, PCBP2 is not only upregulated and leads to poor prognosis but is also associated with drug resistance [ ]. Moreover, silencing PCBP2 was found to reduce extracellular matrix production and levels of drug resistance in pancreatic cancer [ ]. A recent review proposed that PCBP2 is involved in the silencing or enhancement of translation in several cancers and is predominantly located in the nucleus because it contains nuclear localization signals [ ]. However, it exists in splice variants that can be localized to both the nucleus and the cytoplasm [ ]. In our study, we confirmed that PCBP2 was located in the nucleus, but also found in the cytoplasm, suggesting an alternative isoform may occur in GBC. The localization and function of PCBP2 isoforms in different cancers require further investigation.
In the present study, we found that fasting decreased PCBP2 expression and that PCBP2 is a downstream target of fasting. Moreover, PCBP2 knockdown combined with FMD decreases tumor growth in vivo. PCBP2 knockdown significantly inhibits cell migration/invasion and glycolysis in GBC cells cultured in fasting media. Although a direct link between the Warburg effect and PCBP2 has not been established, there is an association between the Warburg effect and GBC [ ]. In particular, an elevated level of PKM2, a key enzyme in the Warburg effect, is an unfavorable prognostic factor in GBC [ ]. We also found that levels of PKM2 were higher when PCBP2 was overexpressed but were lowered under fasting conditions or when the expression of PCBP2 was suppressed. Our findings imply that glycolysis promotes proliferation and invasiveness in GBC, whereas fasting inhibits the consequences of glycolysis and downregulates the expression of PCBP2.
We found that ANGPTL4 is downregulated in GBC tumor tissue and cells. In addition, PCBP2 knockdown promotes ANGPTL4 expression, whereas PCBP2 overexpression inhibits the expression of ANGPTL4. A co-IP assay indicated that PCBP2 interacts with ANGPTL4. These results imply that PCBP2 regulates cell migration/invasion and glycolysis through ANGPTL4. ANGPTL4 is induced under hypoxic conditions and is involved in regulating lipid metabolism [ ]. The downregulation of ANGPTL4 is associated with several cancers, including colorectal cancer [ ] and osteosarcoma [ ]. However, in humans, fasting induces the expression of ANGPTL4, and this is believed to be caused by elevated levels of free fatty acids [ , ]. Moreover, the induction of ANGPTL4 is known to suppress tumor growth and angiogenesis in mice [ ]. High-fat diets may contribute to a chronic inflammatory state that gives rise to an elevated production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species [ ]. Therefore, the induction of ANGPTL4 through fasting could assist in the management of GBC.
Numerous studies have reported the effects of PCBP2 and fasting/FMD on migration/invasion in malignant tumors, including triple-negative breast cancer [ ] and colorectal cancer [ , ]. However, these findings have not extended to GBC. In this manuscript, we have explored the effects of PCBP2 on GBC tumor progression under intermittent fasting conditions, including the effects of cell proliferation, invasive migration, and glycolysis in vitro and tumor growth in a mouse xenograft model in vivo. Our findings provide a theoretical basis for in-depth explorations and clinical studies. Although our study provides a preliminary view of the roles of fasting on GBC cell lines and a mouse xenograft model, further clinical studies would assist in establishing the link between the modulation of ANGPTL4 by PCBP2 and whether this improves the outcome of patients. Clinical trials to assess fasting/FMD on other malignancies have been conducted, including gynecological cancer [ , ], glioma [ ], breast cancer [ ], lung cancer, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma [ ]. However, no clinical trials have been undertaken in GBC. Limitations in our study include the low number of samples in the online GBC tissue profiles, which may influence the accuracy of the data. Larger sample sizes are needed to validate the results. Additionally, our animal studies were limited to a single fasting protocol in xenograft models, and exploring different fasting regimens or combining fasting with conventional treatments might provide more therapeutic insights. Moreover, further in-depth clinical studies are needed to investigate the underlying mechanisms.

## Conclusion
Fasting regulates cell migration/invasion and glycolysis in GBC through PCBP2/ANGPTL4 signaling. Therefore, inducing the expression of ANGPTL4 through dietary changes or targeting the expression of PCBP2 could be beneficial to patients with GBC.
