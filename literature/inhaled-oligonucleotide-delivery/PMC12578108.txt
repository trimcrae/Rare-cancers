## Abstract
Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD) is a genetic disorder caused by mutations in the SERPINA1 gene, resulting in reduced or dysfunctional alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT) protein. This deficiency leads to progressive lung and liver diseases, including emphysema, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and cirrhosis. Despite its clinical significance, AATD remains underdiagnosed, delaying treatment. This review explores the molecular mechanisms of AATD, emphasizing Z-variant AAT protein accumulation in hepatocytes and excessive protease activity in the lungs. Advancements in imaging modalities – such as computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), dark-field radiography, and hyperpolarized MRI – enhance early diagnosis and disease monitoring. Novel therapies are reshaping AATD management, including small interfering RNA therapies (fazirsiran, belcesiran), gene-editing techniques (CRISPR-Cas9), regenerative approaches, autophagy-enhancing drugs, proteostasis regulators, aerosolized AAT, and artificial intelligence (AI) for real-time disease tracking via wearable devices. The integration of AI, advanced imaging, and emerging therapies represents a paradigm shift in AATD diagnosis and treatment. This review highlights the need for a multidisciplinary approach, early intervention, and personalized medicine to improve outcomes in both pulmonary and hepatic complications.
Keywords: alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, artificial intelligence, COPD, emerging therapies, imaging technologies, liver disease, SERPINA1, siRNA therapy

## Introduction
Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD) is an autosomal codominant genetic disorder caused by low levels of alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT), a protease inhibitor. AAT typically protects lung tissue by counteracting serine proteases, such as neutrophil elastase. In AATD, this protective function is impaired, resulting in alveolar damage and, ultimately, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). This deficiency disrupts the balance between protease and antiprotease activity, leading to unchecked proteolytic damage. AATD increases the risk of emphysema or COPD, liver disease, panniculitis, and vasculitis. More than 100 genetic variants of the AAT gene have been correlated to previous disease progression.
AATD affects approximately 1 in 2000 individuals worldwide[ ]. The prevalence of AATD is difficult to determine, given the slow disease progression and diagnostic delays and complex interplay of genetic, molecular, and clinical features. However, it has been estimated to be around 100 000 cases in the United States[ ]. Between 1994 and 2005, the diagnostic delay decreased from 7.2 to 5.6 years[ ]. However, an important gap persists between the onset of symptoms and the administration of therapy [ ].
The correlation between AATD and COPD has been investigated in a recent global analysis. A comprehensive systematic review estimated roughly 235 000 individuals with severe AATD worldwide, with variable prevalence among patients with COPD depending on geographic region, e.g., approximately 1 in 995 among patients with COPD in Europe[ ]. AATD is a significant genetic risk factor for COPD, and understanding this connection is crucial for both diagnosis and treatment. It was first identified as a genetic risk factor for COPD over 50 years ago.
Since then, the understanding of AATD’s role in COPD has expanded, revealing clinical manifestations including pan-acinar emphysema, airway hyperresponsiveness, and bronchiectasis[ ]. AATD-associated liver disease arises from the accumulation of misfolded Z-variant AAT (Z-AAT) protein in hepatocytes, leading to fibrosis and cirrhosis.
Efforts to enhance early diagnosis and treatment strategies have gained attention, especially in the respiratory and hepatology fields. The 2017 European Respiratory Society working statement, building on previous international guidelines, serves as a foundation for diagnosing and managing AATD. Imaging plays a crucial role in diagnosing AATD-related complications, particularly emphysema.
Traditional imaging techniques, such as chest X-rays, are limited in their ability to detect early-stage emphysema or quantify the extent and distribution of lung tissue damage. Additional diagnostic methods are being developed for early disease detection. The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in data interpretation holds the promise to improve efficient analysis of complex patient data in support of personalized treatment plans. The treatment of AATD has focused on lung health, aiming to delay disease progression; however, emerging therapies are also targeting liver complications frequent in AATD, providing a more comprehensive approach to disease management. This dual focus on lung and liver therapies addresses the multifaceted challenges of AATD[ ].
The aim of this review is to provide a comprehensive overview of the molecular mechanisms underlying AATD, and to critically examine recent advances in diagnostic imaging and emerging therapeutic approaches – including gene-editing, small interfering RNA (siRNA)-based treatments, regenerative strategies, and AI-guided disease monitoring.

## Molecular and pathophysiological considerations

## Structure and Function of AAT
The AAT molecule is a glycoprotein predominantly produced by hepatocytes. AAT is also synthesized by other cell types, such as macrophages, intestinal, and bronchial epithelial cells[ ]. AAT plays a crucial role in inhibiting various proteinases, including pancreatic trypsin, neutrophil elastase, cathepsin G, and proteinase-3[ ]. The pathophysiology of AATD involves a complex interplay between genetic, molecular, and cellular mechanisms. Neutrophil elastase, proteinase 3, and cathepsin G are hematopoietic serine proteases stored in the azurophilic granules of neutrophil cytoplasm. These proteases, in combination with reactive oxygen species, contribute to the degradation of microorganisms inside phagolysosomes. Additionally, during neutrophil activation, these proteases are externalized in active forms at inflammatory sites, where they regulate inflammatory and immune responses[ ]. Structurally, AAT comprises three β-sheets and a reactive center loop that acts as a pseudo-substrate for target proteinases[ ]. Specific residues within this loop are critical for binding neutrophil elastase[ ]. Upon binding, AAT undergoes a conformational change, leading to elastase inactivation and subsequent clearance of the complex by hepatic receptors[ ]. Once the enzyme has been inactivated, it is cleared from circulation by hepatic receptors[ ]. In AATD, however, elastase and other proteinase activities are unchecked, causing tissue damage that can progress to clinical manifestations of disease[ ]. At the molecular level, the pathophysiology of AATD involves misfolded AAT proteins forming aggregates within hepatocytes, leading to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR) pathway[ ]. This response can result in hepatocyte injury, apoptosis, and eventually liver fibrosis or cirrhosis over time[ ].

## HIGHLIGHTS
Genetic Basis and Pathophysiology: Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD) is a genetic disorder caused by mutations in the SERPINA1 gene, leading to deficient or dysfunctional alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT) protein. This results in lung damage (emphysema, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) and liver disease (cirrhosis, fibrosis) due to toxic Z-variant AAT accumulation in hepatocytes.
Underdiagnosis and Diagnostic Advances: AATD remains underdiagnosed, often delaying treatment. Advances in imaging techniques such as computed tomography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), dark-field radiography, and hyperpolarized MRI enable early detection and improved monitoring.
Emerging Therapies: Novel treatment approaches include small interfering RNA-based therapies (e.g., Fazirsiran, Belcesiran), CRISPR gene editing, autophagy-enhancing drugs, and proteostasis regulators to target both pulmonary and hepatic complications.
Artificial Intelligence in AATD: Artificial intelligence (AI) integration in imaging and personalized medicine aids in early detection, disease progression tracking, and optimized therapeutic strategies using predictive modeling.
Regenerative Medicine: Induced pluripotent stem cells and aerosolized AAT delivery are being explored for liver regeneration and direct lung-targeted therapy, offering potential long-term solutions.
Future Directions: Combining advanced imaging, AI, genetic therapies, and regenerative medicine represents a paradigm shift in early diagnosis, treatment, and personalized care for AATD.

## Mechanism of liver disease
AATD significantly impacts liver morphology and function, with the extent and type of damage influenced by the genetic variant involved. The most prominent liver complication is caused by the Z-AAT, which results in a toxic gain – of- function mechanism rather than a simple deficiency of AAT in the bloodstream[ ]. The Z mutation alters the protein’s conformation, making it unstable and prone to polymerization. These polymers aggregate within the ER of hepatocytes, causing ER stress, triggering the UPR, and leading to hepatocyte injury, apoptosis, and subsequent liver dysfunction[ ]. Unlike null alleles, which do not produce AAT and rarely cause cirrhosis, the pathological accumulation of Z-AAT polymers in the liver is the primary driver of hepatic damage in AATD[ ]. While some patients can degrade abnormal Z-AAT proteins, approximately 10–20% exhibit significant liver damage[ ]. The progression in affected individuals includes chronic hepatitis, cirrhosis, and, in some cases, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)[ ].
Clinically, liver disease is characterized by diverse presentations, ranging from mild liver abnormalities to severe conditions such as cirrhosis or HCC. Liver biopsies often show lobular inflammation, steatosis, varying degrees of fibrosis or cirrhosis, and diastase-resistant, periodic acid-Schiff-positive globules in hepatocytes[ ]. These globules result from Z-AAT polymer accumulation, reflecting the pathological retention of misfolded AAT in hepatocytes[ , ]. Cirrhosis, a key consequence of AATD, is marked by the replacement of functional liver tissue with scar tissue. This fibrosis arises from chronic hepatocyte injury, immune system activation, and inflammation[ ]. Over time, scar formation compromises blood flow and liver metabolism, impairs protein synthesis (including AAT), and ultimately leads to liver failure[ ].
Furthermore, HCC, the most common form of primary liver cancer, is frequently associated with cirrhosis in patients with AATD. Studies show a 23.4 fold higher risk of developing HCC in patients with AATD[ ], with an annual HCC incidence of 0.88% in patients with end-stage liver disease[ ]. The risk of HCC increases in individuals with prolonged liver injury and chronic inflammation[ ]. These hepatic complications highlight the intricate interplay between the genetic basis of AATD, molecular mechanisms of Z-AAT polymerization, and immune-mediated tissue damage[ ]. Understanding the pathophysiology of AATD-associated liver disease is essential for developing targeted interventions that address both its hepatic and pulmonary manifestations[ , ] (Fig. ).

 The impact of AATD on the morphology and function of the liver and the lungs changes depending on the genetic variant. Current evidence suggests that the liver condition linked to Z-AAT deficiency is a result of an excess buildup of abnormal AAT protein in the liver, rather than a shortage of AAT in the bloodstream. The Z mutation causes most unstable protein molecules to form polymers[ ]. Moreover, null alleles which do not produce any AAT, do not result in cirrhosis[ ]. Animal models have shown that the overexpression of Z-AAT can result in liver damage[ ]. The Z mutation causes a change in the conformation of the AAT molecule, leaving it vulnerable to interaction with other AAT molecules to form polymers that are trapped in the ER[ ].

## Mechanism of lung disease
The development of lung disease associated with AATD is primarily caused by cigarette smoking[ ]. This is due to an imbalance in the protease-antiprotease activity in the lungs, particularly in individuals with Z-variant AATD who have a less effective enzyme[ ]. The residual AAT is vulnerable to oxidation by free radicals from leukocytes or cigarette smoke, leading to the formation of polymers in the lung[ ]. This results in excess neutrophils and inflammation, ultimately causing destruction and scarring of the lung parenchyma, leading to emphysema[ ]. In individuals with AATD, inflammation in the lung parenchyma can be exacerbated through the increased recruitment of neutrophils, often caused by cytokines or AAT polymers[ ]. Furthermore, alveolar macrophages release leukotriene B4 (LTB4) at higher levels in patients with AATD, which has been linked to an increased frequency of exacerbations[ , ]. The supplementation of ATT protein or augmentation therapy has been shown to reduce LTB4 levels[ ]. Neutrophil elastase binding to alveolar macrophages results in the release of LTB4, which contributes significantly to neutrophil chemotactic activity in sputum from patients with COPD[ ]. Elevated bronchoalveolar concentrations of human neutrophil peptide in patients with AATD are proinflammatory and can synergistically contribute to the release of macrophage LTB4, neutrophil elastase, and IL-8[ ]. Unopposed neutrophil elastase activity is one of the main contributors to the destruction of lung parenchyma. Meanwhile, IL-8 attracts more neutrophils through its interaction with CXCR1 receptors expressed on the surface of these cells, which maintains a high level of elastase[ ]. Z-type AAT polymers are associated with increased alveolar neutrophils, which is thought to stimulate myeloperoxidase release and neutrophil adhesion[ ]. This explains the interstitial neutrophilia observed in the lungs of patients with Z-type AATD as well as the sustained disease progression even during augmentation therapy. Moreover, the administration of AAT reduces sputum elastase and LTB4 but does not significantly reduce myeloperoxidase, a marker of neutrophil activity[ ]. The cyclic strain on alveolar wall tissue can lead to the development and progression of emphysema. Cyclic strain refers to the repetitive stretching and relaxing of alveolar walls during normal breathing. In the context of emphysema, this cyclic strain can contribute to the destruction of alveolar septa, a hallmark of the disease, leading to irreversible airflow limitation[ ]. Lung volume reduction surgery (LVRS) may accelerate the loss of the forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) due to additional biomechanical stresses on the remaining lung tissue[ , ]. While the main target of AAT is believed to be neutrophil elastase, it also binds to human neutrophil peptide and IL-8. Reduction or absence of AAT decreases these bindings, leading to unchecked proteolytic breakdown that causes further lung injury and seems to be accelerated by mechanical tissue strain. Mechanical injury, usually associated with polymerization in AATD, does not respond to augmentation therapy[ ]. It is believed that the emphysema of AATD is mainly basal because it follows the gravitational distribution of the pulmonary blood flow[ ] (Fig. ).

## Lung disease and mechanical changes with AATD
Pulmonary emphysema was first identified by Laennec in 1819 [ ]. Since then, emphysema has been described as an abnormal and permanent dilatation of the distal air spaces of terminal bronchioles[ ]. The pathogenesis of emphysema involves the destruction of alveolar walls and fibrosis[ ]. The correlation between pathological abnormalities and clinical features has been studied by Joseph F Tomashefski Jr, who reported the presence of severe panacinar emphysema, primarily affecting the lower lobes, with minimal centriacinar distribution, in addition to mild bronchial inflammation and goblet cell hyperplasia. However, minimal changes were observed in the small airways. Dilatation of the membranous bronchioles was a frequent finding, but bronchiectasis of larger airways was only observed in a minor feature[ ]. Yamashiro et al conducted a study on COPD patients with AATD and found a significant correlation between FEV1 and computed tomography (CT) lung imaging. COPD patients with AATD had airway wall thickening and airway narrowing in the upper lobes, while patients with emphysema had a greater impact on the lower lobes[ ]. This suggests that airway obstruction plays an important role in airflow limitation in patients with AATD, which worsens as more distal airways are affected[ ] (Fig. ).

## Clinical suspicion and early detection
Patients with COPD, unexplained liver disease, unresponsive asthma, c-ANCA vasculitis, necrotizing panniculitis, granulomatosis with polyangiitis, or bronchiectasis, as well as first-degree relatives of those diagnosed with AATD, should be assessed for AAT levels[ ]. The American Thoracic Society published guidelines for early disease detection and care of individuals with COPD, bronchiectasis, partially reversible adult asthma, consanguineous relatives of patients with AATD, family members with chronic cough and dyspnea, individuals with absence of the alpha-1 peak on a protein gram, and patients with unexplained hepatopathy, panniculitis or vasculitis of unknown origin[ ]. AATD should be considered in children with persistent unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia, elevated transaminases, neonatal hepatitis syndrome, or signs of liver dysfunction. A thorough assessment is essential for older children presenting symptoms of chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, or portal hypertension[ ]. It is crucial to consider the medical background of children whose parents have been diagnosed with AATD.

## Advances in imaging technology
One of the methods for diagnosing AATD is imaging with its various types. Chest X-ray images are not often used for screening of emphysema since they only show indirect signs of the disease, which are typically not present until later stages. Conventional X-rays also cannot detect emphysema in its early stages, assess its regional distribution, or quantify its severity[ ]. As a result, CT has become the preferred imaging method for diagnosing and studying emphysema. CT scans can visually assess the distribution of emphysema and measure its density using densitometry[ ]. AATD emphysema can be identified on chest X-ray by several distinct features. First, there is hyperlucency, which indicates the destruction of healthy lung tissue. Additionally, oligemic areas are observed due to the absence of the normal rich pattern of branching blood vessels. It is worth noting that around 60% of individuals with the PiZZ genotype present with emphysema limited to the basilar portion of the lung. On the other hand, smoking-related emphysema tends to have a more severe distribution in the apical region[ ]. The use of CT scans results in significantly higher radiation exposure for patients compared to chest X-rays, with diagnostic chest CT scans typically delivering a dose of 2–7 mSv, while X-rays average below 0.05 mSv. As a result, CT scans are currently only recommended for specific cases based on risk assessment and are not suitable as a general screening method for the public. Consequently, there is a pressing need for an imaging technology that is sensitive enough to detect emphysema at an early stage and enable long-term monitoring without subjecting patients to the high radiation doses associated with CT scans[ ]. A new type of X-ray imaging called dark-field radiography has been developed. This technology provides information about the microstructural integrity of the lungs[ ]. The underlying principle of dark-field radiography is based on a three-grating interferometer for X-rays, which was introduced by Pfeiffer et al. This allows conventional X-ray sources to be used[ ]. In traditional X-ray or CT imaging, image contrast is determined solely by the attenuation of the X-ray beam as it passes through an object. However, dark-field X-ray imaging takes advantage of the wave properties of X-rays to create contrast. It does this by visualizing small-angle scattering that occurs at interfaces, such as air–tissue interfaces in pulmonary alveoli or bone–bone marrow interfaces in trabecular bone. The more interfaces present, the greater the small-angle scattering, resulting in a higher dark-field signal[ ].

## CT imaging: applications in AATD
CT scanners use focused X-rays that are rotated around the patient to create a three-dimensional image of the scanned region. These images consist of stacked “slices” made up of volume elements called voxels. The density of each voxel is represented by its attenuation, which is measured in Hounsfield Units (HU)[ ]. Different tissue densities are assigned different gray color tones for visualization. For example, water has an HU of 0, while air has an HU of −1000[ , ]. Healthy lungs typically have attenuation values between −750 and −850 HU, while emphysematous lungs have values below −910 to −960 HU or lower[ ]. The burden of emphysema can be quantified by measuring the area of lung tissue below the cutoff attenuation value as a percentage of the total lung area[ ]. Another measure, called the percentile density (PD15), represents the HU value at which 15% of voxels are below the cutoff and is often used to assess AATD-related emphysema[ ]. PD15 has been found to be a more consistent measure of lung density change across different impairments compared to other quantitative methods like voxel index (VI-910 HU)[ ]. In the field of AATD, it is recommended that newly diagnosed patients showing symptoms of COPD and/or impaired lung function undergo a baseline CT scan[ ]. Clinical practice typically involves using high-resolution CT (HRCT) scanning to evaluate the presentation of the disease. This type of scanning is particularly useful in characterizing the extent and distribution of emphysema and bullae, as well as assessing the presence and severity of bronchiectasis. HRCT is more effective than conventional CT scanning in visually identifying small areas of emphysema due to its improved resolution and reduced volume averaging. As a result, HRCT can accurately detect emphysema at an early stage compared to conventional CT scanning[ ]. HRCT of patients with AATD shows pan-lobular emphysema, which is characterized by the uniform expansion of air spaces from the bronchioles to the alveoli and is predominant at the lung bases. Other features might appear, including bronchiectasis, bulla formation, and bronchial wall thickening[ ] (Fig. ).

 A higher degree of emphysema on CT scans is linked to worse disease progression and reduced exercise ability. The presence of emphysema on CT scans has been linked to a higher likelihood of death, especially when there is evidence of worsening density in the lower areas of the lungs in patients with AATD and significant lung function impairment. Measurements of emphysema on CT scans (such as VI-910 HU) were found to be more closely related to mortality than FEV1[ ]. Patients with AATD undergo thorough assessment using CT scans, which provide crucial information about the presence, distribution, and appearance of emphysema. They also allow for quantification of the extent of emphysema, which can guide treatment decisions such as starting AAT therapy or determining suitability for surgical or endoscopic interventions to reduce lung volume. Additionally, CT scans have helped researchers understand how emphysema progresses in patients with AATD and have supported studies on the effectiveness of AAT therapy[ ].

## Magnetic Resonance Imaging: functional insights
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a different technology from CT scans, as it uses magnetic fields instead of ionizing radiation[ ]. In the past, MRI scans of the lungs were limited due to the large amount of air contained in the lungs, which contains low levels of hydrogen. In MRI, the signal is generated from hydrogen atoms, and their abundance is a key factor in obtaining a clear image. The lungs, being primarily filled with air, contain significantly lower levels of hydrogen compared to other tissues. This low hydrogen content results in a weak signal and poor image quality when using conventional MRI techniques. However, the introduction of inhaled hyperpolarized contrast agents like helium (He) and xenon (Xe) has allowed for direct visualization of lung function without any background signal[ ]. MRI is more expensive than CT[ ] but has the advantage of not using radiation, making it suitable for repeated scanning in research studies. However, compared to HRCT, the spatial resolution of lung MRI is currently limited, and HRCT is better at visualizing small structural changes[ ]. Nonetheless, lung MRI can directly assess function by using hyperpolarized gas to measure ventilation and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC). ADC is a marker for alveolar structure and emphysema progression that correlates with traditional measures of gas transfer[ , ]. The ability of lung MRI to provide regional functional information is advantageous over standard pulmonary function tests (PFTs)[ ]. Recently, 129Xe has become a popular alternative to 3He MRI for imaging the lungs.[ – ] It is able to detect ventilation defects more easily than 3He MRI because of its higher density and lower diffusivity in the smaller airways[ ]. Unlike 3He, 129Xe can dissolve in tissues and move freely through various lung compartments, including alveoli, interstitium, capillaries, and red blood cells. Each compartment has unique resonance frequencies that allow for separate detection of 129Xe in each area[ ]. This enables 129Xe MRI to accurately measure ventilation, membrane diffusing capacity, and regional capillary blood volume with high resolution and sensitivity[ , ]. Additionally, specific signatures on 129Xe MRI have been identified for emphysema and chronic bronchitis, which can be useful for clinical classification[ ]. While traditional PFTs will still be important, lung MRI using xenon gas is likely to complement these tests, especially when assessing regional gas exchange function. The technology is currently undergoing Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval and is expected to be approved for clinical use in the near future. Although the costs may be higher than standard MRI, a 129Xe MRI is much quicker to perform (around 10 minutes instead of 60 minutes), allowing for scanning of a larger number of patients[ ]. Currently, there is limited literature on the use of lung MRI to characterize the progression of emphysema in individuals with AATD. However, initial studies have shown promising results. For example, a Danish pilot study involving nine patients with AATD found a strong correlation between changes in diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLco) and MRI ADC over a 2-year period[ ]. Another study from Canada compared various MRI parameters, CT lung area, and DLco between patients with AATD, ex-smokers with non-AATD COPD, and a healthy control group[ ]. The results revealed that only the MRI parameters were significantly different between the AATD and non-AATD COPD patients, indicating that lung MRI may be able to detect lung disease at an earlier stage than CT imaging[ ].

## PET-CT (FDG PET-CT): biomarkers of inflammation
Another imaging technique used in studies of AATD is positron emission tomography (PET), which creates an image by measuring gamma rays emitted from radiolabeled markers. Specifically,18F-fluorodeoxyglucose PET-CT (FDG PET-CT) can provide quantitative and spatial data on pulmonary glucose uptake by pulmonary neutrophils, serving as a noninvasive biomarker for pulmonary neutrophilic inflammation. This type of inflammation is believed to play a significant role in COPD, particularly in cases associated with AATD. However, the use of FDG PET-CT has shown that patients with non-AATD COPD have higher FDG uptake by pulmonary neutrophils compared to patients with severe AATD (PI*Z phenotype). Additionally, there was a correlation between FDG uptake and clinical measures of disease severity in non-AATD COPD patients[ ]. While patients with AATD did not exhibit noticeable FDG uptake, the utilization of PET holds promise for quantitative imaging in AATD. However, for this imaging method to truly contribute to AATD research, an alternative radiolabeled marker capable of monitoring a vital biochemical process unique to AATD must be identified[ ].

## Emerging technologies: dark-field radiography and hyperpolarized MRI
Emerging imaging technologies, including dark-field radiography and hyperpolarized MRI, are transforming the diagnosis and management of AATD. These advanced methods overcome the limitations of traditional imaging modalities, providing enhanced sensitivity, specificity, and the ability to detect early pathological changes that are crucial for timely intervention and disease monitoring[ ].
Dark-field radiography utilizes the wave-like properties of X-rays to capture small-angle scattering signals, offering detailed insights into lung microstructure. Unlike conventional X-rays, which primarily rely on attenuation contrast, dark-field radiography focuses on the interaction between X-rays and air-tissue interfaces, such as those in pulmonary alveoli [ ]. This unique capability allows it to detect early-stage emphysema, a key complication of AATD, before significant structural changes become evident on standard imaging. Studies have shown that in healthy individuals, dark-field images display a uniform, high scattering signal across the lung fields, reflecting intact alveolar structures. In contrast, patients with AATD exhibit markedly reduced scattering, particularly in the lower lung zones, where emphysema caused by protease-antiprotease imbalance is most pronounced. Dark-field radiography can distinguish between AATD-related emphysema and smoking-induced emphysema, which tends to affect the upper lobes. This differentiation is clinically valuable for tailoring treatment strategies. Furthermore, its low radiation exposure compared to CT scans makes it an attractive option for repeated use in longitudinal disease monitoring, especially in pediatric or young adult populations. Despite its promise, the widespread application of dark-field radiography is currently limited by the need for specialized equipment and a lack of large-scale validation studies to confirm its diagnostic and prognostic capabilities[ ].
Hyperpolarized MRI is another innovative technology offering both structural and functional imaging of the lungs. By employing hyperpolarized noble gases, such as helium-3 (^3He) or xenon-129 (^129Xe), as inhalable contrast agents, hyperpolarized MRI enables visualization of ventilation, gas exchange, and alveolar integrity with unparalleled precision[ , , , ]. Upon inhalation, the hyperpolarized gas distributes throughout the airways and alveoli, allowing for real-time mapping of ventilation and diffusion abnormalities. This is particularly advantageous in AATD, where early functional changes, such as ventilation-perfusion mismatch, can precede overt structural damage. Xenon-129, due to its ability to dissolve in tissue, further enhances the imaging capability by assessing gas transfer across the alveolar-capillary membrane, providing insights into regional diffusing capacity[ ]. This has significant implications for understanding the extent of emphysema and the progression of lung damage in patients with AATD. Moreover, hyperpolarized MRI avoids the use of ionizing radiation, making it a safer option for repeated imaging, particularly in long-term monitoring or research settings[ ]. Despite its many advantages, hyperpolarized MRI faces challenges in widespread implementation, including high operational costs, limited availability of hyperpolarized gases, and the need for advanced technical expertise.
Both dark-field radiography and hyperpolarized MRI represent significant advancements in pulmonary imaging, offering new opportunities to improve the care of patients with AATD. These technologies enable earlier detection of disease, precise monitoring of progression, and comprehensive assessment of therapeutic responses. Future research should focus on integrating these modalities into routine clinical practice, improving accessibility, and reducing costs. Furthermore, the combination of these imaging techniques with AI could enhance their diagnostic accuracy and predictive capabilities, paving the way for personalized medicine approaches in AATD management. By addressing current challenges, these emerging technologies hold the potential to redefine the diagnostic standards for AATD and significantly improve patient outcomes.

## AI in AATD

## AI-driven imaging: enhancing early detection of AATD
Traditional diagnostic approaches have historically left many patients undiagnosed for years. While AATD affects approximately 1 in 2500–5000 individuals worldwide, studies suggest that, on average, only 5–10% of patients with AATD are included in AATD registries in their respective countries[ ]. This has prompted researchers to explore the use of AI, machine learning, and related applications to improve early and accurate identification of patients with AATD.
For example, Hinson et al developed a machine learning model using claims data from the Komodo US database that achieved 81% accuracy in identifying symptomatic patients who should be tested for AATD[ ]. The model was also able, with high accuracy, to distinguish patients with AATD from patients with COPD but no AATD and patients with unspecified chronic liver disease[ ]. Another machine learning model by Pfeffer et al accurately distinguished AATD-positive patients with 90% accuracy[ ].
Imaging has also greatly benefited from advances in AI. An AI-based, fully automated CT chest model was developed by Fischer et al to quantify the severity of emphysema and evaluate how accurately these measurements reflect pulmonary function testing results[ ]. The AI model showed a strong negative correlation with pulmonary function testing (r = − 0.86) and accurately predicted emphysema severity[ ].
Another AI-based, CT chest model was developed by Nagaraj et al based on minimum intensity projection techniques[ ]. It achieved 88% sensitivity for emphysema detection in low-dose CT scans, with the optimal performance observed using 11-mm slab thickness[ ]. This approach will be particularly valuable for patients with AATD who require long-term monitoring, as it enables early detection while minimizing radiation exposure.

## Personalizing therapy using AI models
Machine learning can be used to study the various genetic variants of AATD and how they affect the structure of the AAT protein; this could help us understand which variants respond best to an intervention and which patients would benefit the most from such an intervention. A similar analysis by Wang and Balch on cystic fibrosis (CF) using variation spatial profiling revealed 63 variants and showed that ivacaftor, the first FDA-approved personalized therapy for CF, had the most impact on patients with the G551D variant, while it had no effect on those with the F508del variant[ ]. Moreover, a machine learning model applied to about 50 Niemann-Pick C1 (NPC1) disease variants revealed that many variants could be corrected by targeting proteostasis mechanisms (which improve protein folding and trafficking) or by epigenetic therapies (such as histone deacetylase inhibitors).[ , ] However, some variants did not respond to these approaches. A similar machine learning study would be highly beneficial and is needed in AATD to analyze how different AAT variants affect disease and treatment response, and, therefore, personalize AATD treatment, improve outcomes, and reduce costs.
Personalizing therapy in AATD can be achieved by applying population pharmacokinetic modeling. Machine learning is increasingly being applied to optimize the dosing of therapeutic drugs in various diseases and predict individual clearance rates through population pharmacokinetic modeling.[ – ] For AATD, these approaches could integrate patient-specific factors to accurately predict and tailor optimal dosing regimens, which are conflicting in the literature[ ].

## Future directions: integrating AI into holistic AATD management
Managing AATD remains challenging due to its complex impact on both the lungs and liver, and the relative rarity of the disease limits widespread clinical experience. AI holds promise to overcome these challenges by making care more personalized, accessible, and proactive.
Combining AI with telemedicine is particularly promising because many patients with AATD live far from specialized centers, resulting in delayed diagnosis and suboptimal management. AI-enhanced telehealth platforms can remotely monitor patients by analyzing data from wearable devices and patient reports, enabling early detection of worsening respiratory or liver function to guide timely clinical intervention without frequent clinic visits[ ].
Wearable devices integrated with AI can continuously track vital signs such as respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and physical activity, which are crucial for patients prone to lung exacerbations or hepatic deterioration. These AI algorithms effectively filter noise from biometric data and identify meaningful changes before clinical symptoms appear, allowing for earlier intervention. Advancements in sensor design, device comfort, and data privacy are rapidly improving the feasibility of long-term home monitoring for patients with AATD[ ].
Federated learning, a method that trains AI models on decentralized datasets while preserving patient privacy, could enhance collaboration between institutions. This approach would enable the development of more accurate and generalizable models without compromising data security[ ].
Emerging technologies like explainable AI (XAI) are critical for fostering trust in AI-driven decisions. By making predictions more transparent and interpretable, XAI ensures clinicians can confidently integrate AI into their workflows[ ]. Incorporating AI into multidisciplinary care teams could further enhance decision-making. By synthesizing insights from geneticists, hepatologists, pulmonologists, and other specialists, AI enables a unified approach to managing AATD, addressing both pulmonary and hepatic complications simultaneously.
While the integration of AI into AATD diagnosis and management is promising, it also introduces limitations and challenges that warrant serious discussion. One major challenge is algorithmic bias, which may result in health inequity and discrimination based on race, sex, or other features[ , ]. Inadequate representation of particular demographics and subgroups in the training dataset may yield inaccurate predictions for these groups[ ]. A notable example is the study by Seyyed-Kalantari et al, which demonstrated consistent underdiagnosis of underrepresented groups (e.g., female patients, Black patients, patients of low socioeconomic status) across three large public datasets[ ]. It hence follows that inadequate representation of AATD patients with rare genotypes or specific demographics may introduce bias and result in inaccurate recommendations. The STANDING Together consensus recommendations have provided multiple recommendations to reduce algorithmic bias[ ]. Additionally, various techniques such as data preprocessing, in-processing, and post-processing have been proposed to mitigate bias during different stages of the algorithm’s development[ ].
The validation of the algorithms before their implementation in clinical practice represents another major challenge. Many algorithms are trained on limited or single-center datasets, which raises concerns about their generalizability across broader patient settings[ ]. A notable example is the infamous, IBM’s Watson Health Cancer AI algorithm, which was designed to provide treatment guidance for oncologists[ ]. The algorithm was trained on a limited, small dataset of hypothetical, “synthetic” cases and was later shut down due to numerous instances of unsafe and incorrect treatment recommendations[ ]. Datasets should ideally be of sufficient size and involve multiple institutions and patient populations, and robust validation and evidence are needed before approval and implementation of the algorithm[ ].
Another challenge is integrating AI into current clinical workflows. AATD is a rare genetic disease, for which data are expected to be scarce and rare; this limits AI training datasets and potentially introduces reproducibility bias[ ]. Moreover, data should be standardized into a common format so that it can be integrated seamlessly with electronic health records (EHRs), imaging platforms, and genetic testing tools that are already in use[ , ]. This integration is often difficult due to variations in EHR formats and terminology and requires substantial resources, technical adjustments, and workflow redesign[ ]. Lack of standardization can cause significant delays, and it also increases the risk of overfitting and hinders generalizability[ ]. Additionally, there is often scepticism and hesitancy among clinicians if the algorithm recommendations cannot be easily interpreted, i.e., the “black box” of the algorithm, which has caused a lot of controversy and has called for demands to decipher the algorithm’s function before its implementation in healthcare[ ].

## Treatment

## General therapy for AATD
Treatment aims to slow lung deterioration, alleviate symptoms, and prevent complications such as exacerbations or infections. Long-acting bronchodilators like beta-agonists and muscarinic antagonists relieve airway obstruction and dyspnea by relaxing bronchial smooth muscles and keeping airways open. Inhaled corticosteroids, which are typically combined with bronchodilators, manage airway inflammation and prevent acute exacerbations, especially in patients with frequent episodes or asthma-like symptoms. For acute exacerbations, short-acting bronchodilators and oral corticosteroids reduce inflammation, while antibiotics combat bacterial infections that can worsen respiratory function[ , ].
Preventive strategies are vital for minimizing complications and improving outcomes in AATD. Vaccinations against influenza and pneumococcal infections reduce severe respiratory infections and their exacerbations[ ]. Pulmonary rehabilitation – including exercise training, nutritional counselling, and psychological support – enhances endurance, alleviates breathlessness, and improves quality of life, particularly for patients with limited activity[ ]. Additionally, oxygen therapy is essential for patients with chronic hypoxemia to relieve symptoms, improve sleep, reduce cardiac workload, and prevent complications like pulmonary hypertension[ ].
Lifestyle modifications are key in AATD management, with smoking cessation being the most critical intervention. Smoking exacerbates the protease-antiprotease imbalance by lowering AAT levels and increasing neutrophil elastase activity, thereby accelerating lung tissue destruction[ , ]. Minimizing exposure to environmental irritants such as pollutants, occupational chemicals, and indoor allergens is also important[ , ]. Maintaining a healthy weight reduces the respiratory workload associated with obesity[ ], and aerobic exercise improves lung efficiency, muscle strength, and cardiovascular health[ ]. For advanced AATD-related emphysema unresponsive to medication or lifestyle changes, surgical options include LVRS to remove severely damaged lung tissue and improve mechanics, especially in patients with upper-lobe predominant emphysema[ ], and lung transplantation, which can reverse respiratory limitations and dramatically enhance quality of life[ ].
Surgical interventions require careful patient selection and postoperative care. Holistic management of AATD should also address psychological and emotional challenges, as the chronic decline in lung function can lead to anxiety, depression, and social isolation[ ]. Counselling, support groups, and patient education empower individuals to take an active role in their care, enhancing treatment adherence and satisfaction. Overall, AATD therapy, which encompasses pharmacology, prevention, lifestyle modifications, and surgery, addresses both physical and psychological needs, thereby optimizing outcomes and quality of life.

## Augmentation therapy for AATD
Augmentation therapy, which involves intravenous infusion of purified AAT, is the only specific treatment for AATD. It targets symptom control, survival, and slowing emphysema progression, and is also indicated for necrotizing panniculitis. Its safety and efficacy have been validated in various observational and randomized clinical trials, and it is recommended for nonsmokers or ex-smokers over 18 with severe AATD, low serum AAT levels, and confirmed airflow limitation to restore physiological AAT levels[ ]. Management of AATD requires specialized teams with expertise in genetic interpretation, patient screening, and disease treatment, so augmentation therapy should be administered in specialized rare disease centers equipped with multidisciplinary expertise and emergency response tools[ ].
Because regular intravenous infusions of plasma-purified AAT are costly and depend on protein availability, researchers are exploring alternatives such as new delivery methods (including aerosol delivery), gene therapy, induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) approaches, and non-augmentation strategies to prevent AAT polymerization in hepatocytes. Additional avenues include autophagy-enhancing drugs and silencing RNA strategies. Early human studies on aerosol delivery have shown biochemical efficacy and safety, but larger trials are needed[ ].

## Emerging therapies for AATD-associated liver disease
Emerging therapies target the accumulation of misfolded Z-AAT proteins in hepatocytes, aiming to mitigate liver damage[ ] (Fig. ).

 

## Fazirsiran: siRNA therapy for AAT-liver disease
Fazirsiran is an siRNA drug under clinical trials for treating AAT-LD by harnessing RNA interference (RNAi), a natural defence mechanism that silences genes by degrading mRNA via the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC)[ ]. Designed to mimic natural RNAi molecules, siRNA can theoretically target any gene, offering treatment options for diseases that conventional drugs cannot address. Administered subcutaneously, Fazirsiran (also known as AROAAT) has demonstrated promising results by reducing Z-AAT protein levels in hepatocytes, decreasing inflammation, and normalizing disease-associated gene expression[ , ] (Fig. ). Similarly, siRNA therapies like Belcesiran utilize GalXC technology, employing N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) to bind the asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR) on hepatocytes and thereby enhancing endocytosis; however, clinical data are still pending[ , ] (Table ).

 

## Belcesiran: advancing siRNA with GalXC technology
Belcesiran represents a significant advance in treating AATD-associated liver disease using GalXC technology. It employs GalNAc, which binds with high affinity to the ASGPR on hepatocytes[ ]. Administered subcutaneously, the GalNAc moiety enables selective delivery of siRNA to liver cells, concentrating the therapeutic effect and reducing systemic side effects[ ].
Inside hepatocytes, Belcesiran activates the RNAi pathway to silence the SERPINA1 gene, lowering production of the misfolded Z-AAT protein that causes cellular stress, inflammation, fibrosis, and cirrhosis in AATD. Preclinical and early-phase trials have shown significant reductions in hepatic Z-AAT levels and inflammatory markers, supporting its potential to slow or reverse disease progression[ ].
GalXC technology offers advantages over traditional siRNAs by precisely targeting hepatocytes, minimizing off-target effects and immune activation risks. Its efficient delivery mechanism may allow less frequent dosing, thereby improving patient compliance and treatment outcomes[ ].
Ongoing clinical trials are evaluating its efficacy, safety, and long-term benefits. Early data indicate that Belcesiran not only reduces misfolded protein production but also blunts downstream inflammatory processes contributing to liver injury. This positions Belcesiran as a promising addition to the evolving treatment paradigm for AATD, with further studies expected to refine its use and expand its therapeutic scope[ ]. The combination of advanced targeting technology with robust gene silencing underlines its potential to change the standard of care in liver-related complications for AATD[ ] (Table ).

## Gene therapy: correcting the genetic defect
Gene therapy for AATD targets the disorder’s genetic root. Using tools like CRISPR-Cas9 to repair SERPINA1 mutations responsible for AAT[ ], it aims to restore functional AAT production and may offer a one-time, long-term, or permanent solution[ ].
Advances in delivery systems have enhanced the safety and precision of gene editing. Common methods use viral vectors, such as adeno-associated viruses engineered for low immunogenicity, to deliver CRISPR-Cas9 components to hepatocytes where SERPINA1 is expressed[ ]. Lipid nanoparticles have also emerged as a safer, scalable non-viral alternative that encapsulates gene-editing machinery for liver cell uptake[ ].
Preclinical studies show that CRISPR-Cas9 can correct Z-AAT in laboratory models, potentially halting misfolded protein production and preventing hepatocyte damage. Improved CRISPR components and dual-editing techniques are being developed to increase specificity and repair efficiency, moving gene therapy for AATD closer to clinical application[ ].
As this research is ongoing, it might provide a game-changing approach that will redefine the standard of care for AATD and provide the patients with a durable, effective solution for both pulmonary and hepatic complications (Table ).

## IPSCs: regenerating liver function
iPSCs offer a novel treatment for AAT-LD by addressing both protein misfolding and liver regeneration. Reprogrammed from patient-specific somatic cells using skin or blood[ ], iPSCs can differentiate into functional hepatocytes that produce normal AAT, avoiding immune rejection associated with donor cells[ ].
This strategy provides a sustainable source of functional AAT while reducing toxic Z-AAT accumulation and may reverse liver fibrosis and cirrhosis by repopulating damaged tissue[ ]. Advances in reprogramming and differentiation have improved safety by minimizing genetic instability and tumorigenicity[ ], and integrating gene-editing tools such as CRISPR-Cas9 can correct SERPINA1 mutations before differentiation, ensuring only functional AAT is produced[ ] (Table ).
iPSCs enable personalized medicine by generating patient-specific hepatocytes, and iPSC-derived liver organoids can model AATD for drug discovery[ ]. Despite challenges in large-scale hepatocyte production and long-term liver integration, overcoming these hurdles could establish iPSC technology as a comprehensive treatment for AAT-LD that combines disease correction with tissue regeneration[ ].

## Autophagy-enhancing drugs: clearing misfolded proteins
Autophagy-enhancing drugs offer a new approach to treat AATD-related liver disease by clearing toxic, misfolded Z-AAT proteins from hepatocytes. Autophagy degrades damaged organelles and misfolded proteins via lysosomal pathways; in AATD, impaired autophagy leads to ER stress, inflammation, and progressive liver damage. These drugs aim to restore cellular homeostasis and prevent fibrosis/cirrhosis[ ].
Preclinical studies show that compounds like carbamazepine clear Z-AAT aggregates in hepatocytes[ ], reducing injury and inflammation. Similarly, rapamycin analogs inhibit mTOR to enhance autophagic flux, accelerating aggregate clearance and reducing cellular stress[ ]. Combined with treatments such as siRNA, like Fazirsiran, which lowers misfolded protein production, autophagy enhancers provide a dual mechanism for reducing hepatocyte damage[ ]. This synergy is especially promising for advanced liver disease patients, where both preventing further accumulation and clearing existing aggregates are crucial[ ] (Table ).
However, challenges remain for clinical application. Ensuring hepatocyte specificity is essential to avoid unwanted systemic autophagy activation, and autophagic activity must be balanced to prevent excessive degradation of vital cellular structures[ ]. Further research may establish autophagy enhancers as a key component of the therapeutic arsenal for AATD, offering complementary and transformative options, especially in combination regimens.

## Proteostasis regulators: improving protein folding
Proteostasis regulators target the protein homeostasis imbalance in AATD by enhancing the folding, trafficking, and secretion of AAT, thereby reducing toxic misfolded Z-AAT accumulation in hepatocytes and attenuating liver injury[ , ].
These small molecules stabilize Z-AAT’s native structure to promote correct folding and secretion. Preclinical studies have identified candidates that improve protein folding and degradation – often by targeting chaperones and enhancing ER-associated degradation – to reduce ER stress, inflammation, and hepatocyte injury[ , ].
A promising class of these compounds, small-molecule correctors, prevents aberrant interactions among mutant Z-AAT proteins. In preclinical models, they increase functional secretion while reducing hepatic aggregation, protecting the liver from fibrosis and ensuring enough functional AAT reaches the lungs to prevent emphysema[ ]. When combined with siRNA therapies that reduce Z-AAT production and autophagy enhancers that clear existing aggregates, proteostasis regulators offer a comprehensive strategy for managing AATD at various disease stages[ ] (Table ).
Challenges for clinical translation include ensuring specificity and efficacy in human hepatocytes, optimizing pharmacokinetics, and minimizing side effects to avoid unwanted impacts on other cellular processes[ ]. Nonetheless, proteostasis regulators hold promise as a targeted treatment for AATD by directly addressing its cellular consequences[ ].

## Aerosolized Delivery of AAT
Aerosolized administration of AAT offers a promising alternative for treating AAT deficiency by delivering the protein directly into the lungs, thereby targeting both pulmonary and hepatic complications while reducing systemic side effects[ , ].
In pulmonary AATD, aerosolized AAT restores deficient protein levels and protects lung tissue from neutrophil elastase damage[ ]. Inhalation achieves high local concentrations that inhibit elastase and limit emphysema development, reducing the need for intravenous infusions and improving patient compliance[ ] (Table ).
Beyond lung protection, some aerosolized AAT enters systemic circulation, potentially reaching hepatocytes and mitigating liver inflammation[ ]. Advances in aerosol technology, such as optimized nebulizers and inhalers for deep lung penetration, precise dosing, and improved formulation stability, enhance the feasibility and therapeutic appeal of this approach[ ].
Ensuring consistent delivery across patients with varying degrees of lung function remains a key focus, as airway obstruction in advanced emphysema might impact the distribution of inhaled particles[ ]. In addition to this, long-term studies have to be carried out in order to test the safety and efficacy of aerosolized AAT for extended use, especially the risk of local irritation or inflammation in the airways.
While it remains under investigation, aerosolized delivery of AAT is an especially promising approach that could offer an equally effective but far more patient-friendly alternative to intravenous therapy[ ]. Targeted protection to the lungs and even systemic inflammation may significantly enhance the quality of life in individuals living with AATD and provide a more convenient and efficient option for disease management.

## Conclusions and future directions
The evolving landscape of AATD research underscores the necessity for a holistic, multidisciplinary approach to its management. AATD, with its dual impact on pulmonary and hepatic health, demands advancements in diagnostic, therapeutic, and preventive strategies. The future of AATD research lies in addressing gaps in early detection, enhancing therapeutic efficacy, and exploring innovative technologies to combat this complex disorder. Early diagnosis remains critical in stemming disease progression, and the emergence of new diagnostic methods is likely to redefine the practice. Besides conventional CT imaging, there are new advancements such as dark-field radiography and hyperpolarized MRI, which provide unparalleled insights into early lung and liver damage. These technologies, combined with AI-enhanced imaging analytics, will enable the precise staging of diseases, early intervention, and longitudinal monitoring of therapeutic efficacy. Future studies should be targeted toward guaranteeing better access to this kind of advanced equipment, particularly in the most deprived parts of the world, to equitably provide healthcare.
At the heart of this next chapter in AATD management is therapeutic innovation. The current augmentation therapies provide the foundation, while in the emerging pipeline, there is siRNA treatment represented by Fazirsiran and Belcesiran, regulators of proteostasis, enhancing autophagy drugs, and gene-editing technologies using CRISPR-Cas9-all targeted and disease-modifying. These approaches have the potential to target the root cause of AATD at a molecular level and offer potential curative solutions not only for pulmonary but also for hepatic manifestations. Other avenues toward liver repair and functional restoration with regenerative strategies using iPSCs may offer personalized and durable treatments. AI has turned the tide in all aspects of AATD management, from diagnostic imaging to personalized therapy optimization. Risk stratification is enhanced through AI-driven predictive modelling, as disease progression is followed in real-time by means of wearable devices. The integration of AI into clinical workflows may further ensure streamlined decision-making and improve patient outcomes while reducing healthcare costs. With federated learning and XAI models, data is guaranteed to be secure and transparent, while cross-institutional collaboration will make strides toward better AATD care. Directions for future research will need to focus on the causes of divergent inflammatory mechanisms, particularly those underlying the differences in FDG-PET neutrophil uptake between AATD and non-AATD COPD. The information obtained may yield new targets and biomarkers for a more personalized treatment. There will be further challenges, though: the equitable dispensing of these new therapeutic opportunities globally and economic and logistics obstacles to the wider availability of therapy. Therefore, the future of AATD research and management lies in the use of advanced technologies, collaboration across disciplines, and overcoming systems challenges. Early diagnosis, new treatments, AI, and regenerative medicine are set to change the outlook in patients with AATD and offer a better quality of life and prolonged survival.
