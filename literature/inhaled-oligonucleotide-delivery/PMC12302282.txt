## Abstract
Inflammatory lung diseases, particularly those associated with acute pulmonary inflammation, represent a major clinical challenge due to their high global morbidity and mortality rates, as well as the absence of effective and safe therapies. While traditional pharmacological interventions have been frequently used for managing acute inflammatory lung diseases, novel therapeutic strategies are urgently needed to improve clinical outcomes. Recently, advanced nanotherapies have emerged as a promising paradigm for targeted treatment of inflammatory lung diseases, leveraging the unique pathophysiological features of acute pulmonary inflammation, characterized by excessive inflammatory mediator production and robust immune cell infiltration. This review first outlines the pathogenic mechanisms and pathological microenvironment linked with acute lung inflammation. Then different bioresponsive nanocarriers are comprehensively summarized and discussed, with a particular emphasis on inflammation-responsive nanotherapies engineered for modulating acute pulmonary inflammation. Furthermore, innovative biomimetic nanotherapies, such as those based on cell hitchhiking, cell membrane coating, and extracellular vesicles, are critically examined. Finally, we highlight the key challenges impeding the clinical translation of these next-generation nanotherapies for acute lung inflammation and propose potential avenues for future research.
Keywords: Lung inflammation, Nanoparticle, Drug delivery, Bioresponsive nanotherapy, Biomimetic nanotherapy

## Introduction
Inflammation is pivotal in initiating both innate and adaptive immune responses, serving as a key defense mechanism for the host against invading pathogens such as viruses and bacteria [ , ]. However, following pathogen clearance, it is imperative that inflammatory responses should be meticulously regulated and promptly resolved to prevent subsequent tissue damage and organ failure [ ]. In pathological states like trauma and sepsis, the lung serves as a major site for the secretion and release of pro-inflammatory mediators, making it highly susceptible to acute inflammation [ , ]. Acute pulmonary inflammation is fundamentally characterized by impaired barrier functions of the pulmonary epithelium and endothelium, resulting from both direct pulmonary insults and indirect systemic inflammatory responses [ ]. Uncontrolled acute inflammatory responses can lead to acute lung injury (ALI) and its more severe manifestation, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [ , ]. As the disease progresses, acute lung inflammation may also trigger systemic inflammatory response syndrome, sepsis, and even septic shock [ ]. The crosstalk between pulmonary and systemic inflammation further exacerbates disease progression, thereby substantially increasing therapeutic complexity and clinical challenges [ ]. Acute pulmonary inflammation arises from diverse etiologies, with systemic and pulmonary infections representing major causative factors [ ]. In addition to infectious origins, non-infectious factors such as traumatic injury [ ], aspiration [ , ], and pancreatitis [ ] constitute other clinically significant contributors to this condition. Since 2000, the etiological landscape of acute pulmonary inflammation has notably evolved, with emerging evidence implicating drug use [ ], cigarette smoking [ , ], and environmental air pollutant exposure as new risk factors [ , ]. Particularly, the SARS-CoV-2 virus (2019), responsible for the COVID-19 pandemic, has resulted in over 777 million confirmed cases and 7.1 million deaths worldwide [ ], with acute pulmonary inflammation representing the primary pathological manifestation [ ].
The current standard therapeutic approaches for acute pulmonary inflammation, particularly ALI/ARDS, primarily involve mechanical ventilation, fluid management, and supportive care. However, inappropriate ventilator use can paradoxically worsen lung injury through secondary damage [ ]. Furthermore, the optimal method for lung-protective ventilation remains unclear [ ]. To evaluate therapeutic efficacy of pharmacological interventions for the treatment of acute pulmonary injury/inflammation, over 20 multicenter clinical trials spanning several decades have assessed various anti-inflammatory drugs including glucocorticoids [ ], nitric oxide [ ], and β2 agonists [ ]. Nevertheless, despite these efforts, no examined pharmacotherapy has shown definitive therapeutic benefits for acute pulmonary inflammation in clinical practice [ ]. Other therapeutic interventions, such as surfactant replacement [ , ], neutrophil elastase (NE) inhibition [ ], anticoagulant agents [ ], statins [ ], and antioxidants [ ], have demonstrated only marginal clinical benefits [ ]. Consequently, there is an urgent unmet need for developing more efficient and safer therapeutic modalities against acute pulmonary inflammation.
To circumvent limitations of existing pharmacotherapies, numerous nanocarriers, such as polymeric nanoparticles (NPs), lipid NPs, and inorganic NPs, have been investigated as delivery vehicles for the treatment of acute pulmonary inflammation [ ]. NPs exhibit powerful capabilities in enhancing the stability and solubility of encapsulated payloads, prolonging drug half-life, facilitating transport across different biological barriers, and realizing site-specific drug delivery and controlled release, thus potentiating therapeutic effects and reducing side effects [ , ]. In particular, physicochemical properties of NPs (e.g., the shape, size, surface chemistry, types of carrier materials, and targeting moieties) can be precisely tailored by rational design to address challenges associated with treating complex diseases [ ]. While NPs can passively accumulate in the interstitial space in the inflamed lungs via the enhanced permeability and retention effect (EPR) [ ], their targeting efficiency is generally inadequate. In this aspect, active targeting nanocarriers have been developed by incorporating targeting moieties onto their surfaces, including monoclonal antibodies, functional peptides, and specific small molecules [ , , ]. These moieties can effectively bind to target tissues and/or cells through antibody-antigen, ligand-receptor, or enzyme-substrate interactions [ ]. Despite their potential, these NPs face several critical concerns including undesirable interactions with the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS), protein corona formation, rapid blood clearance, insufficient cell-specific targeting ability, and uncontrolled payload release at disease sites.
Recent advances have highlighted therapeutic advantages of nanotherapies derived from bioresponsive and biomimetic vehicles for targeted lung disease treatment. Bioresponsive NPs can undergo structural or surface modifications in response to external stimuli, enabling site-specific nanotherapy accumulation and controlled release at pathological sites [ ]. This strategy leverages key pathological features of inflamed lungs as endogenous biochemical signals to trigger responsive mechanisms [ , ]. For instance, the inflammatory microenvironment, characterized by elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS), decreased pH, and upregulated enzyme activity, acts as an endogenous trigger for site-specific nanotherapy accumulation and on-demand release of anti-inflammatory agents, which can be achieved by rationally incorporating ROS-responsive linkers, pH-sensitive bonds, or enzyme-cleavable peptides into nanocarriers. On the other hand, biomimetic NPs synergistically integrate the beneficial properties of natural and synthetic materials by harnessing intrinsic biological interactions and emulating native cellular functions [ ]. These systems encompass diverse biological components, including whole cells, subcellular organelles, extracellular vesicles (EVs), and cell membrane proteins, which collectively enhance immune compatibility and therapeutic efficacy [ , , ]. They enable efficient delivery primarily by leveraging inflammatory cell recruitment to injury sites, mimicking physiological processes such as prolonged circulation of red blood cells, and harnessing endogenous EVs transport dynamics for biocompatible transit. By exploiting these disease-specific triggers and barrier disruptions, these innovative bioresponsive and biomimetic strategies demonstrate great potential to overcome biological barriers, achieve precise spatiotemporal control of drug bioavailability, and address premature systemic clearance before reaching target sites.
This review presents a comprehensive overview of recent advances in bioresponsive and biomimetic nanotherapies for acute pulmonary inflammation, with particular focus on ALI/ARDS ( ). We first elucidate the pathophysiological mechanisms of acute lung inflammation, specifically emphasizing biological barriers and pathological microenvironments relevant to drug delivery. Then we critically analyze various bioresponsive and biomimetic drug delivery systems, highlighting their applications in enhancing lung targeting specificity, overcoming biological barriers, and addressing interpatient heterogeneity. Notably, this work provides the first systematic discussion that integrates pathophysiological barrier analysis with engineering strategies for bioresponsive and biomimetic nanocarriers. Furthermore, we especially emphasize clinical translation challenges and offer novel insights into the practical implementation of these nanotherapies for acute pulmonary inflammation.

## The pathophysiology of acute pulmonary inflammation
Acute pulmonary inflammation is a complex clinical condition characterized by a multifaceted pathogenesis and ongoing pathological changes in lung tissues [ ]. This pathophysiological complexity frequently limits therapeutic efficacy of conventional pharmacological interventions in achieving substantial clinical resolution [ ]. The variability in injury patterns and clinical symptoms among acute pulmonary inflammation patients depends strongly on disease severity and progression stage. The pathological course of acute pulmonary inflammation is generally classified into three phases: the exudative phase, proliferative phase, and fibrotic phase [ , ]. The exudative phase, also known as the acute phase, typically begins within 24 h following initial injury ( ). During this stage, a cascade of inflammatory responses is activated, leading to marked neutrophil infiltration [ ]. This excessive inflammation may damage the alveolar epithelial and endothelial barriers, causing the accumulation of protein-rich alveolar edema fluid [ ]. The accumulation of edema fluid in the pulmonary interstitium and airspace results in heightened respiratory effort, compromised gas exchange, hypoxemia, reduced carbon dioxide excretion, and ultimately leading to acute respiratory failure [ ]. Additionally, the presence of transparent membranes consisting of injured cells, surfactants, and plasma proteins is a hallmark feature of diffuse alveolar damage [ ]. As the disease progresses to the proliferative phase, essential tissue repair and remodeling processes commence, which are crucial for patient survival. During the late proliferative phase, most of the transparent membranes are reabsorbed, although some residual materials may persist along the alveolar septa [ ]. Early fibrotic changes and alveolar-capillary thickening can also be observed in this phase. The final or fibrotic phase of acute pulmonary inflammation is distinguished by increased collagen deposition and airspace enlargement. It should be noted that not all patients progress to this stage. However, established fibrosis may lead to various negative outcomes, including reduced lung compliance, extended reliance on mechanical ventilation, impaired lung function, and heightened mortality risk [ , ]. In the subsequent section, we provide a concise overview of typical pathophysiological characteristics of acute pulmonary inflammation, which are essential for rational design of bioresponsive and biomimetic nanotherapies.
 2.1.

## Alveolar-capillary barrier injury
The pathogenesis of acute pulmonary inflammation is intricately linked to dysfunction of epithelial and endothelial cells, which significantly contributes to disease progression [ ]. Damage to lung epithelial and endothelial barriers directly impacts the physiological integrity of gas exchange, respiratory effort, and observable radiological features of acute pulmonary inflammation [ ]. The alveolar-capillary barrier is composed of thin layers of alveolar epithelial cells (AECs) and capillary endothelial cells [ ]. AECs can be further classified into two subtypes: type I (AEC I) and type II (AEC II) cells. While AEC I cells facilitate gas exchange, AEC II cells synthesize and secrete surfactant proteins to maintain alveolar tension [ ]. AEC I cells exhibit an elongated and flat morphology, encompassing 95 % of the alveolar surface area, despite comprising ∼8 % of the total lung cells in healthy adults. Unfortunately, AEC I cells are particularly susceptible to substantial harm induced by inflammatory stimuli and other insults, lacking the capacity for self-proliferation and renewal [ ]. Conversely, AEC II cells, functioning as a stem cell population, possess a diverse array of progenitors or facultative progenitors essential for maintenance and regeneration of the alveolar niche [ ]. AEC II cells serve as pivotal regulators of the immune response, mitigating lung injury by bolstering host immune competency and serving as a pivotal resource for lung regeneration and repair processes [ ]. Following the death of AEC I cells, AEC II cells proliferate and differentiate into AEC I cells. The activation and proliferation of AEC II cells may be associated with the injury type, inflammatory environment, hyperoxia, and oxidative stress [ , , ]. During the initial stage, a diffuse and overwhelming inflammatory reaction occurs in the lungs, profoundly disrupting the homeostatic balance in the pulmonary environment. AEC II cells are vulnerable to multiple modes of cellular damage such as necrosis, apoptosis, pyroptosis, autophagy, and ferroptosis, resulting in destruction of DNA and mitochondria [ , ]. In cases where the body's self-repair mechanisms are insufficient to counteract the continuous excessive damage, this can lead to severe limitations in gas exchange and alveolar fluid clearance, ultimately developing into ARDS characterized by extreme respiratory distress [ ].
Vascular endothelial injury, a crucial feature of acute pulmonary inflammation, is characterized by the emergence of intercellular gaps among endothelial cells and the upregulation of adhesion molecules (such as P-selectin and E-selectin) and endothelial injury mediators (like angiopoietins) [ ]. Various mediators, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin (IL)-6, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), ROS, and histamine, as well as mechanical stretch via activating the endothelial actin-myosin contractile apparatus, are significant contributors to endothelial activation, adhesion molecule expression, microvascular injury, and barrier disruption [ ]. Notably, angiopoietin-2 (Ang-2) produced by activated endothelial cells, which competes with Ang-1 for Tie2 binding, can also disrupt vascular stability and connections [ ].
The elevated permeability of both pulmonary endothelial and epithelial barriers results in alveolar flooding, as protein-rich pulmonary edema leaks from the vasculature into the airspaces [ ]. Furthermore, the dysfunction of alveolar ion channels decreases the osmotic force that is necessary for alveolar fluid clearance, thus impairing the normal transport of alveolar epithelial fluid [ ]. Consequently, this exacerbates alveolar flooding, ultimately leading to additional complications. Activated and injured endothelial and epithelial cells initiate a cascade of events involving TNF-mediated tissue factor expression, fostering intravascular and intra-alveolar coagulopathy, platelet aggregation, microthrombus formation, and the formation of hyaline membranes along exposed basement membranes [ ]. Furthermore, the loss of alveolar surfactant function occurs, causing severe impairment of gas exchange and hypoxemia.

2.2.

## Inflammatory cell infiltration and mediator overproduction
In acute pulmonary inflammation, persistent lung injury triggers the release of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), subsequently activating innate immune cells and initiating inflammatory responses [ , ]. Serving as the primary defense mechanism of the pulmonary innate immune system, alveolar macrophages (AMs) rapidly identify and phagocytose pathogens [ ]. Through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), AMs recognize PAMPs and DAMPs, thereby activating the inflammatory cascade [ ]. During the initial inflammatory phase, AMs secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-1β, recruiting additional immune cells to the affected site [ ]. As inflammation progresses, AMs transition to an anti-inflammatory phenotype, releasing cytokines like IL-10 to promote resolution and tissue repair [ ].
Neutrophils, pivotal effector cells in the initial phase, are rapidly recruited to the inflammatory site [ ]. They combat pathogens through phagocytosis and secretion of antimicrobial agents, including ROS and proteases. In severe cases, neutrophils generate extracellular traps (NETs) that effectively capture pathogens but can also damage surrounding tissues [ ]. Other immune cells, including monocytes/macrophages [ ], dendritic cells [ ], T cells [ , ], and natural killer (NK) cells [ ], contribute to both inflammatory responses and tissue repair, collectively maintaining pulmonary homeostasis.
Inflammation regulation constitutes a highly complex process involving cytokine networks, signaling pathways, and immune cell interactions [ ]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10 and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)) form a dynamic regulatory network [ ]. These cytokines interact through autocrine, paracrine, and endocrine signaling to modulate the intensity and duration of inflammatory responses [ ].

2.3.

## The inflammatory microenvironment
Both local and systemic acute inflammatory responses are key characteristics of acute pulmonary inflammation. In the inflamed lung, a complex network of cells and mediators establishes a microenvironment characterized by low pH, elevated ROS levels, and increased enzyme expression [ , ]. This pathophysiological setting, marked by hypoxia alongside the downregulation of the pentose phosphate and tricarboxylic acid cycles and the upregulation of glycolysis, contributes to lactate accumulation and tissue acidosis [ ]. Extracellular acidosis functions as an endogenous danger signal, influencing the synthesis and release of inflammatory mediators [ , ]. It activates neutrophils and dendritic cells while modulating NK and T cell activities [ ]. However, monocytes and macrophages exhibit differential responses. Low pH suppresses several pro-inflammatory mediators in monocytes, suggesting an anti-inflammatory effect [ ]. Conversely, macrophages under acidic conditions tend to shift from the anti-inflammatory M2 towards the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype [ ]. Consistently, acidosis upregulates cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and IL-6, while enhancing phagocytic capacity of macrophages. Paradoxically, it simultaneously downregulates monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) and TNF-α, indicating an intrinsic regulatory mechanism that mitigates excessive inflammatory responses [ ].
Moreover, acute pulmonary inflammation is marked by an imbalance between ROS generation and antioxidant defenses. This oxidative stress aggravates lung injury and hinders tissue repair. Various lung cells, including endothelial cells, neutrophils, eosinophils, AMs, epithelial cells, fibroblasts, perivascular adipocytes, and smooth muscle cells, generate ROS [ , ]. While ROS support normal cellular functions at physiological levels, their overproduction during inflammation damages lipids, proteins, and DNA [ ], inducing cell necrosis and apoptosis in lung tissues. Multiple factors account for the elevated production and accumulation of ROS in acute pulmonary inflammation, including leukocyte infiltration, increased levels of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidases (NOXs) and xanthine oxidase (XO), and the administration of high-concentration oxygen during mechanical ventilation [ ]. ROS serve as key pro-inflammatory mediators in this condition by activating diverse signaling pathways and transcription factors critical to the inflammatory cascade. Beyond their well-documented toxic effects on pulmonary tissues, ROS can function as potent regulators of immune cell plasticity. Traditionally, ROS have been viewed as definitive activators of M1 macrophage polarization via redox-sensitive pathways [ , ]. However, emerging evidence indicates ROS play a more nuanced, context-dependent role in M2 polarization [ , ]. Current understanding recognizes macrophage activation as existing along a spectrum of functional states rather than a binary switch [ ]. M1-like and M2-like phenotypes can coexist and dynamically interconvert under spatiotemporal regulation, highlighting the complexity of ROS-mediated macrophage dynamics in pulmonary inflammation.
The pathogenesis of acute pulmonary inflammation also involves several enzymes that contribute to tissue injury and inflammation, including NE, myeloperoxidase (MPO), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [ , ]. NE, a major protease in the primary granules of neutrophils, is involved in microbicidal activity [ ]. However, overproduction of NE not only promotes inflammation but also causes substantial lung tissue damage and remodeling [ ]. MMPs are a family of proteolytic enzymes that perform critical physiological functions such as remodeling the extracellular matrix (ECM), facilitating cell migration, cleaving cytokines, and activating defensins [ ]. Nevertheless, the excessive MMP activity can degrade all components of the ECM, including the basement membrane and crucial non-matrix mediators involved in lung injury [ ]. Meanwhile, cathepsin S (Cat S) retains its proteolytic activity at neutral pH and can promote remodeling of the ECM through its potent elastolytic activity [ ]. These enzymes collectively emphasize the complex mechanisms involved in the pathogenesis of acute pulmonary inflammation, elucidating the delicate balance between inflammation and tissue remodeling in this severe respiratory condition.
Collectively, ROS, extracellular pH, and enzyme activity interact dynamically across spatial and temporal scales to influence the progression of acute pulmonary inflammation. During the early acute phase, infiltrating neutrophils and macrophages generate localized bursts of ROS, triggering a rapid decrease in alveolar pH and the formation of acidified microdomains surrounding activated cells. This acidification alters the optimal catalytic pH of Cat S to more acidic levels, initiating a feed-forward mechanism that enhances NADPH oxidase-mediated ROS production [ ]. Spatially, significant acidification occurs at hypoxic lesion sites, correlating with neutrophil accumulation and increased ROS generation. Subsequently, lactate diffusion into adjacent tissues induces localized acidosis through metabolic acid accumulation, promoting macrophage polarization towards an M1 phenotype and increasing pro-inflammatory cytokine release. Temporally, the regulatory network evolves in two distinct phases: an initial pro-inflammatory phase dominated by M1-like macrophage activation and robust ROS/protease activity, followed by a reparative phase distinguished by attenuated ROS production, gradual pH normalization, and predominant M2-like macrophage functions and matrix remodeling. Although this spatiotemporal crosstalk complicates precision intervention strategies, it critically defines therapeutic windows for region- and stage-specific drug delivery.

## Bioresponsive nanotherapies
Bioresponsive drug delivery systems, capable of responding to biological signals or pathological alterations through specific activation mechanisms, provide an innovative paradigm for precision medicine development [ ]. By selecting various functional motifs sensitive to distinct biological signals, bioresponsive delivery systems can be tailored to achieve specific drug release profiles [ ]. For acute pulmonary inflammation therapy, bioresponsive drug delivery systems are designed to achieve targeted pulmonary drug deposition, thus enhancing therapeutic efficacy while reducing off-target adverse effects.
Bioresponsive drug delivery systems are promising for targeted pulmonary therapy by leveraging their dynamic interactions with inflammatory mediators and pathological abnormalities characteristic of acute lung inflammation [ ]. Recent studies employing pathophysiology-informed design strategies have consistently demonstrated therapeutic advantages of these bioresponsive therapies in terms of enhancing therapeutic precision and minimizing systemic toxicity, thereby offering a new avenue for managing this challenging respiratory disorder. In this section, we focus on the design of ROS-, enzyme-, and pH- responsive biomaterials and their derived delivery systems ( ).
 3.1.

## ROS-responsive nanotherapies
Due to the significant role of ROS, targeting ROS production or enhancing antioxidant defenses has been a key research focus for developing therapies to mitigate lung injury in acute pulmonary inflammation. Substantial advances have been achieved in antioxidant nanotherapeutics, encompassing ROS-scavenging inorganic NPs, organic NPs with intrinsic antioxidant activity, and drug-loaded antioxidant NPs [ ]. In these systems, drug release primarily occurs through ROS-triggered changes in carrier solubility or ROS-induced hydrolysis of the delivery vehicle, enabled by the design of ROS-responsive materials ( A) [ ]. ROS-responsive delivery platforms enhance therapeutic precision through several key advantages. First, their dynamic adaptability allows ROS-sensitive linkers, such as thioketals and boronate esters, to align drug release with pathological ROS fluctuations, making them particularly suitable for acute pulmonary inflammation. Second, integrating ROS-scavenging modules with anti-inflammatory agents achieves synergistic therapeutic effects, enabling simultaneous neutralization of oxidative stress and modulation of inflammatory cascades. Third, these systems offer enhanced targeting efficiency by specifically activating in inflamed lung tissues with excessive ROS, minimizing off-target effects compared to passive targeting. Lastly, safety is improved by using biodegradable ROS-cleavable polymers that fully degrade after drug release, reducing systemic toxicity. Collectively, these properties position ROS-responsive nanotherapies as promising candidates for acute inflammation control, effectively balancing therapeutic efficacy and biosafety.
 3.1.1.

## Nanotherapies based on boronate ester bond-containing materials
The facile cleavage of linkages between boronic esters and conjugated molecules under oxidative conditions has led to their widespread use in ROS-responsive delivery systems [ ]. In particular, phenylboronic acid pinacol ester (PBAP) derivatives have emerged as promising candidates, showcasing their responsiveness to ROS by oxidation of the arylboronic acid moiety [ ]. In the presence of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), they undergo rapid oxidation to produce phenol and boronic acid. For example, a type of high ROS-responsive materials, known as OxbCD, was successfully synthesized, by incorporating PBAP into β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) [ , ]. OxbCD NPs were engineered to specifically respond to elevated ROS levels at inflammatory sites, triggering their hydrolytic decomposition and enabling targeted drug delivery in the affected tissues. Based on in vitro cell culture experiments under conditions simulating acute inflammation, it was found that OxbCD NPs exhibited superior antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Further, to evidence the hypothesis that agents with broad-spectrum ROS-scavenging capabilities can serve as more effective anti-inflammatory therapies, a superoxide dismutase (SOD)/catalase (CAT)-mimetic material, termed as TPCD, was synthesized by simultaneously conjugating a small-molecule antioxidant Tempol (capable of scavenging superoxide anions and free radicals) and PBAP onto β-CD ( A) [ ]. Subsequent tests revealed efficient and dose-dependent elimination of various types of ROS by TPCD. Moreover, TPCD can be easily manufactured into stable and controllable NPs. In a mouse model of ALI, TPCD NPs showed considerable accumulation in the lungs of ALI mice compared to healthy controls. Notably, treatment with TPCD NPs at either 0.1 or 1 mg/kg significantly reduced neutrophil counts in the lungs, with efficacy enhancement in a dose-dependent manner. Likewise, TPCD NPs at 1 mg/kg significantly decreased the levels of MPO, H2O2, TNF-α, and IL-1β in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, effectively attenuated the inflammatory cell infiltration and hemorrhagic damage to the alveoli, indicating strong anti-inflammatory and lung-protective properties. Further, He et al. expanded the application of this methodology by employing silk fibroin (SF) as a carrier material [ ]. Tempol and PBAP were sequentially conjugated to the SF scaffold to afford a bioactive macromolecule defined as TPSF, which exhibits antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and macrophage reprogramming abilities. In mice with LPS-induced ALI, TPSF NPs administered at 2 mg/kg showed superior tissue protection compared to N-acetylcysteine therapy. This therapeutic advantage was quantitatively evidenced by significantly lower lung wet/dry weight ratios, diminished pulmonary infiltration of inflammatory cells, reduced secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, suppressed formation of oxidation products, and attenuated cellular apoptosis in lung tissues.
 Based on the above mentioned promising results, our group designed and developed a series of inflammation-resolving conjugates by strategically integrating diverse functional modules into a hydrolyzable scaffold, to develop novel anti-inflammatory therapies with significantly improved potency, well-defined hydrolytic/metabolic profiles, and outstanding safety performance [ , , ]. In these multifunctional conjugates, polyethylene glycol (PEG) serves as a hydrophilic module that provides colloidal stability and biocompatibility to the resultant materials. PBAP was used as a H2O2-responsive/eliminating unit, which enables ROS-triggered hydrolysis and H2O2 scavenging. Importantly, ROS-induced hydrolysis of PBAP can generate a pharmacologically active phenolic compound. The third module is Tempol, i.e., a SOD mimic. These three modules are covalently conjugated onto hexachlorocyclotriphosphazene or cyanuric chloride to afford anti-inflammatory conjugates. Through a facile synthesis procedure, the molecular structures of final conjugates can be precisely tailored, yielding well-defined macromolecular therapies with fully predictable and safe hydrolytic products, along with multifaceted anti-inflammatory effects. Due to their amphiphilic nature, these anti-inflammatory conjugates can assemble into small-size nanomicelles. At the cellular level, the assembled micelles effectively suppressed the migration and activation of neutrophils and macrophages as well as inhibited their pro-inflammatory mediator production. In different animal models of acute inflammation, the micelles efficaciously alleviated oxidative damage, inhibited inflammatory cell infiltration and activation, and promoted inflammation resolution. Consequently, these anti-inflammatory materials and their derived NPs represent effective, safe, scalable, and cost-effective therapies for acute pulmonary inflammation.

3.1.2.

## Nanotherapies derived from thioketal-containing materials
Thioketals are commonly utilized as protecting groups for carbonyl in organic synthesis due to their notable stability under acidic and basic conditions, and can be effectively cleaved by ROS. Exposure to ROS leads to hydrolysis of thioketal bonds, resulting in the production of acetone and thiols [ ]. Taking advantage of the unique ROS-labile property of thioketals, Gao et al. encapsulated dexamethasone acetate (Dex) within NPs based on polythioketal polymers [ , ]. The massive thioketal bonds in the polymer main chains endow the NPs with fast ROS-responsive degradation and elimination. The obtained ROS-responsive Dex nanotherapy (PTKNPs@Dex) accumulated in inflamed lungs and rapidly released the encapsulated payload, resulting in reduced ROS levels, decreased pro-inflammatory cytokine production, alleviated lung injury, and reduced mortality in mice. Extending this approach, the same team developed a ROS-responsive polymer (PFTU)-based co-delivery system where azithromycin and ibuprofen were co-encapsulated within PFTU NPs. This platform enables co-delivery of antioxidant, antibacterial, and anti-inflammatory agents for synergistic therapy of ALI [ ]. In other studies, the thioketal-containing linker was used to prepare NPs coated with bovine serum albumin (BSA). At inflammatory sites, the linker can be cleaved by excessive ROS, leading to the detachment of BSA from the NP surface and subsequent release of curcumin (Cur) payloads [ ]. Intriguingly, it was found that thioketal-based polyurethane NPs (PUSB NPs) exhibit ROS type-selective degradation behaviors [ ]. These NPs preferentially cleave in response to superoxide anion (O2•-) and hydroxyl radical (•OH) even at relatively low concentrations, while showing significantly slower responsiveness to H2O2 in vitro, even at higher concentrations. Notably, mechanistic studies in ALI mice revealed that Pazopanib-loaded PUSB NPs do not exert therapeutic effects through indiscriminate ROS scavenging. Instead, they operate via a dual mechanism: paradoxically enhancing H2O2 secretion while concurrently suppressing cytotoxic ROS subtypes such as O2•- and •OH. This ROS-selective modulation represents a paradigm shift in the design of ROS-responsive materials. The focus is transitioning from achieving universally high ROS sensitivity towards the microenvironment-specific customization of thioketal moieties to better align with pathological ROS profiles.
In addition to conventional thioacetal/thioketal ROS-responsive motifs, other chalcogen-based systems exhibit unique stimulus-responsive behaviors [ ]. For instance, thioether-containing polymers undergo ROS-triggered hydrophobic-to-hydrophilic transitions along their polymer backbones, thereby facilitating controlled drug release [ ]. Similarly, selenium- and tellurium-containing materials can modulate payload delivery through redox-mediated interconversion. Specifically, selenides and tellurides are oxidized by ROS into hydrophilic selenoxide/selenone or telluroxide derivatives, destabilizing the carrier structure and promoting cargo release [ ]. Diselenide bonds introduce an additional dimension of dual redox responsiveness, i.e., they undergo cleavage in oxidative environments to form seleninic acid and release therapeutics, while reductive conditions promote reformation of selenol motifs [ ]. This mechanistic diversity provides a versatile molecular toolkit for the rational engineering of intelligent, stimulus-responsive delivery platforms.

3.1.3.

## Peroxalate ester bond-based nanotherapies
It is widely recognized that aryloxalates readily undergo reaction with H2O2 to generate 1,2-dioxetanedione, a compound that subsequently decomposes into carbon dioxide. Consequently, incorporating aryloxalate ester bonds within carrier materials can facilitate their degradation and release of cargoes upon exposure to H2O2 [ ]. As a typical example, Wang et al. engineered an aryloxalate-based nanoplatform for ALI diagnosis and treatment [ ]. Specifically, prednisolone (Pre) was conjugated to a two-photon fluorophore via an oxalyl chloride (OC) bridge, giving rise to a ROS-sensitive compound termed TPP. TPP was loaded into core-shell micelles self-assembled from poly(2-methacryloyloxyethyl phosphorylcholine)-b-poly(2-methylthioethyl methacrylate) (PMPC-b-PMEMA, abbreviated as PMM), affording TPP@PMM. Upon exposure to ROS, the hydrophobic-to-hydrophilic transition of PMEMA initially disrupts the micelle structure, releasing TPP. Subsequently, the ROS-responsive bond in TPP breaks, enabling precise delivery of Pre for inflammation treatment. TPP@PMM showed considerable accumulation in the lungs of ALI mice, significantly mitigating inflammatory responses and reducing the expression levels of inflammatory cytokines. The OC-based strategy has also been extended to prepare porous carriers [ ]. The cyclodextrin-metal-organic framework (CD-MOF), with its cubical structure and uniform morphology, was chosen as a template for synthesizing OC-COF, owing to its straightforward synthesis, excellent biocompatibility, and tunable size [ , ]. This innovative system efficiently delivers ligustrazine, an active alkaloid, to the lungs in a ROS-responsive manner, thereby affording enhanced therapeutic effects on ALI.
Recently, our group has designed and synthesized a biocompatible vitamin E peroxalate compound (OVE) through a simple reaction between OC and vitamin E ( B) [ ]. After incorporating various fluorescent molecules, OVE NPs exhibited prolonged ROS-responsive luminescence, spanning wavelengths from blue to near-infrared. These luminescent NPs are capable of illuminating activated inflammatory cells, with the luminescence intensity positively correlating with cell counts. In a mouse model of ALI, these luminescent NPs demonstrated efficient accumulation in the injured lung tissue and enabled real-time imaging of disease progression. Concomitantly, H2O2-mediated hydrolysis of the oxalate bond releases vitamin E, delivering localized antioxidant therapy. Accordingly, these pivotal characteristics underscore the potential of developing OVE-based NPs for simultaneous luminescent imaging and therapeutic intervention of ALI.

3.1.4.

## Nanozyme-derived nanotherapies
The integration of nanotechnology, synthetic biology, and computational chemistry enables unprecedented precision in designing antioxidant therapeutics, as evidenced by recent advances in nanozyme engineering. As a typical example, cerium oxide has been employed as a responsive motif to engineer redox-sensitive NPs that particularly serve as prominent antioxidant inorganic nanozymes [ ]. Cerium oxide NPs (CNPs) exhibit versatile properties as rare earth nanomaterials, demonstrating exceptional capability of mimicking CAT and SOD [ ]. CNPs exhibit chemical reactivity by efficiently scavenging superoxide anion radicals and H2O2 through reversible oxygen atom binding and transitioning between Ce3+ (reduced) and Ce4+ (oxidized) states, thereby showing great promise in various antioxidative therapeutic applications. This redox-adaptive mechanism has been strategically exploited in ALI therapeutics through microRNA (miRNA) conjugation strategies. In mice with methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus Aureus (MRSA)-induced ALI, intratracheal administration of CNPs conjugated with anti-inflammatory miRNA-146a (CNP-miR146a) at 4 h post-injury significantly reduced bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) total protein, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and CD45+ leukocyte infiltration [ ]. The therapeutic efficacy was further validated in mice with bleomycin-induced fibrotic lung injury, showing protective effects in both prophylactic and therapeutic regimens by reducing acute inflammation and fibrotic progression [ , ]. Specifically, early-phase intervention significantly downregulated IL-6 and TGF-β1 expression. Furthermore, sustained treatment reduced late-phase fibrosis, concurrently improving pulmonary mechanics, as well as enhancing compliance, inspiratory capacity, and tissue elasticity. Leveraging ROS-responsiveness of CNPs and pH-induced charge reversal of amino groups in chitosan (CS), Shi et al. developed a pH/ROS dual-responsive nanomedicine by loading Cur and CNPs into vehicles based on octenyl succinic anhydride-functionalized CS, termed Cur@Ce/OCS, for sepsis treatment [ ]. In septic mice, intravenous (i.v.) administration of Cur@Ce/OCS demonstrated multimodal therapeutic efficacy by synergistically achieving a 97 % reduction in bacterial load, mitigating the systemic cytokine storm evidenced by significant reductions in IL-6 (81.5 %), IL-1β (45.9 %), and TNF-α (66.7 %), and enhancing the survival rate to 85 % versus 20 % in the control group. To elucidate the effects of CNPs size on lung targeted delivery efficiency, Yang et al. introduced a novel thermal decomposition approach, enabling synthesis of cerium nanowires with precisely tailored diameters and lengths for in vivo applications [ ]. By carefully tuning reaction conditions, they successfully synthesized nanowires with a diameter of approximately 1.5 nm and lengths ranging from 14 to 161 nm. To enhance the stability of CNPs in aqueous or physiological solutions and prevent nanowire collapse, a PEG surface modification strategy was applied. The PEGylated cerium nanowires displayed significantly enhanced ROS scavenging capabilities compared to the control NPs. Notably, nanowires exceeding 100 nm in length showed pronounced pulmonary targeting and accumulation, along with remarkable ROS clearance capability. These custom-designed cerium nanowires, with exceptional ROS scavenging and pulmonary targeting properties, effectively mitigated excessive ROS in lung tissues, thus inhibiting edema and inflammation that are pivotal to ALI progression.
By strategically modulating active-site architecture, surface functionalization, and microenvironment responsiveness, next-generation nanozymes can perform multienzymatic functions that mimic SOD, CAT, and glutathione peroxidase (GPx). To validate this design principle, Qu et al. developed a biomimetic cobalt-based nanozyme (Co/PMCS) featuring atomically dispersed, unsaturated cobalt-porphyrin centers [ ]. This unique configuration drives coordinated catalytic cycles mimicking SOD, CAT, and GPx, enabling efficient ROS elimination. Furthermore, the nanozyme scavenges •OH through repeated redox cycling and removes nitric oxide by forming nitrosyl-metal complexes, outperforming conventional nanozymes. In parallel, Zhang et al. engineered a copper single-atom nanozyme (Cu-SAzyme) using carbonization and metal coordination, affording uniform active sites and solving the challenge of uneven active-site distribution [ ]. By precisely mimicking SOD activity, Cu-SAzyme mitigated oxidative stress-induced multi-organ failure in septic shock models, significantly enhancing in vivo survival rates. Collectively, these studies emphasize the therapeutic potential of rationally engineered nanozymes for redox-related diseases, highlighting how systematic structural and functional engineering translates to clinically relevant benefits.

3.1.5.

## Other ROS-responsive nanotherapies
Natural polyphenols represent a critical class of endogenous antioxidants with an inherent capacity to modulate pathological host responses. However, their therapeutic application has been constrained by limited aqueous solubility and rapid systemic clearance. Recent advances in molecular engineering and nanoformulation have transformed these compounds into clinically viable nanotherapeutics. A representative example is the temperature-controlled hydrothermal synthesis of catechin-derived condensation nanomaterials (CCN150), as reported by Kim et al. [ ]. This engineered nanosystem exhibited substantially enhanced aqueous dispersibility compared to its precursor, catechin. In experimental models of endotoxin-induced systemic inflammation, CCN150 demonstrated potent therapeutic efficacy by effectively suppressing ROS-mediated inflammatory cascades. Comparative analysis with the LPS-challenged control group revealed substantial cytokine attenuation, with IL-6 decreasing by 69.2 %, IL-1β by 36.7 %, and TNF-α by 41.2 %. These findings underscore the translational potential of engineered polyphenol nanotherapies in overcoming intrinsic pharmacokinetic barriers while enhancing their biological activity.
In summary, ROS-responsive delivery systems offer a novel approach for developing effective therapies for acute pulmonary inflammation. These advanced vehicles are engineered to respond to elevated ROS levels commonly detected in inflamed lung tissues, enabling potentially precise and regulatable therapeutic regimens. Preclinical studies across different animal models have demonstrated encouraging therapeutic benefits of ROS-responsive nanotherapies in mitigating inflammatory responses and reducing tissue injury associated with acute pulmonary inflammation.

3.2.

## Enzyme-responsive nanotherapies
Extracellular enzymes, such as NE, MMPs, cathepsins, and MPO, are frequently studied as inflammation-associated signals for designing drug delivery systems. Typically, enzyme-responsive systems are activated by the hydrolase-mediated cleavage of specific chemical linkers, including peptide substrates, esters, and glycosidic bonds. Additionally, other enzyme classes such as oxidoreductases and transferases have been explored for prodrug activation ( B). By leveraging enzyme-specific triggering mechanisms, therapeutic release or drug activation can be precisely regulated through various physicochemical changes, such as disassembly/reassembly, morphological changes, size transformation, and charge conversion [ ]. Moreover, enzyme-responsive NPs can be integrated with active targeting by leveraging the enzyme-rich microenvironment characteristic of inflammatory sites. This dual-targeting strategy increases drug accumulation at target sites while reducing off-target deposition. Compared to other responsive systems, enzyme-activated platforms offer superior specificity due to the unique overexpression patterns of inflammation-relevant enzymes. Unlike dynamically fluctuating ROS, inflammatory enzymes maintain persistently elevated expression in specific pathological states, ensuring reliable activation. As biological catalysts, enzymes can efficiently cleave substrates at nanomolar concentrations, enabling sensitive responses even at low abundance. Additionally, localized enzymatic activation minimizes off-target effects, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy and safety.
3.2.1.

## NE-responsive nanotherapies
NE can degrade proteoglycans in the glycocalyx and components of the endothelial basement membrane, including elastin and collagen types I to IV. It has been demonstrated that NE can compromise the integrity of the endothelial vascular barrier and exacerbate microvascular damage [ ]. The NE-mediated damage may result in enhanced permeability and interstitial edema, which further lead to tissue injury and remodeling in acute pulmonary inflammation [ ]. According to these pathological roles of NE, Roy et al. developed a NE-sensitive, self-regulated NP-in-microgel system [ ]. This carrier system was constructed by the Michael addition reaction in a water-in-oil emulsion formulation [ ]. Specifically, maleimide moieties in 4-arm PEG were crosslinked with the thiol groups on the cysteine residues of either a trypsin-responsive peptide backbone (CGRGGC) or a NE-responsive peptide backbone (CGAAPVRGGGGC) [ ]. This strategy enables controlled degradation of the engineered microgels by proteases, facilitating responsive release of the loaded NPs. Poly(lactide-co-glycolide) (PLGA) NPs encapsulated in NE-responsive microgels can be activated by NE to release the encapsulated model drug Nexinhib20, a neutrophil degranulation inhibitor. The successful in vivo delivery of Nexinhib20 to the airways and neutrophils significantly alleviated the inflammatory response, primarily by reducing neutrophil recruitment and degranulation, inhibiting the production of proinflammatory cytokines in both airway and systemic compartments, and decreasing neutrophil-derived pathological extracellular vesicles in the lung fluid. Building on protease-triggered release strategies, Stavrou et al. developed an innovative neutrophil-targeting approach by repurposing NE as a molecular docking motif [ ]. They engineered NPs functionalized with a short NE-binding peptide derived from α1-antitrypsin. This modification conferred high-affinity binding to active NE expressed on primed neutrophils and platelet-neutrophil aggregates, enabling selective carrier adhesion to activated neutrophils both in vitro and in vivo. This strategy provides a versatile platform for targeted delivery across neutrophil-driven pathologies. Together, these approaches underscore the dual utility of NE in nanomedicine: serving as both a drug release trigger and a biomarker directing NPs to relevant neutrophil populations. Harnessing NE in either or both capacities could yield highly specific interventions to modulate excessive neutrophil activity underpinning acute pulmonary inflammation.

3.2.2.

## MMP-responsive nanotherapies
MMPs play important roles in numerous physiological and pathological processes, including inflammatory responses [ ], innate immune functions [ ], tissue remodeling [ ], and the ECM biology [ ]. MMPs function as both mediators and effectors of alveolar capillary membrane injury and repair, generally showing elevated activity in acute pulmonary inflammation [ ]. Taking advantage of this pathological feature, Zheng et al. developed a responsive delivery system by coupling DNase I as an externally cleavable arm to an exosome/liposome hybrid nanocarrier (termed SEL) through an MMP-9-cleavable peptide [ ]. In response to MMP-9, DNase I was released from the nanocarrier, leading to the exposure of the SEL core and precise delivery of methylprednisolone sodium succinate to macrophages, thereby promoting M2 macrophage polarization. Simultaneously, the release of DNase I degraded NETs, inhibited neutrophil activation, and alleviated mucus obstruction in the microenvironment, thus further enhancing the M2 polarization efficiency. Consequently, inhalation of this MMP-9-responsive nanotherapy significantly increased M2 macrophages in lung tissues, with a 1.46-fold increase compared to the unmodified group. During the exudative phase of ARDS, the MMP-2 regulatory pathway in AMs becomes activated, exacerbating lung inflammation [ ]. To intercept this mechanism, Ma et al. designed a ferritin-based co-delivery system (GF9-HFn/Str) incorporating an MMP-2-cleavable peptide (GF9) and streptomycin [ ]. In a cecal ligation and puncture sepsis mouse model, GF9-HFn/Str treatment significantly prolonged survival, reduced Escherichia coli burden, suppressed TNF-α and IL-6 expression, and attenuated histological lung injury. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that MMP-responsive nanotherapies can exploit elevated protease activity to achieve spatiotemporal control over drug release and synergistically reprogram inflammatory microenvironments in acute pulmonary disease.

3.2.3.

## MPO-responsive nanotherapies
MPO, a heme-containing enzyme, is secreted by neutrophils and specific subsets of monocytes and macrophages. It represents the most abundant protein within the azurophilic granules of neutrophils [ ]. MPO plays a pivotal role in the innate immune response, being intricately involved in the generation of ROS (particularly hypochlorite) and diffusible free radicals [ ]. Extensive research has substantiated that oxidative substances derived from MPO exert deleterious effects, which can cause tissue damage in a spectrum of acute and chronic inflammatory conditions [ , ]. Our group previously synthesized a MPO-responsive material LCD by covalently conjugating luminol onto a cyclic oligosaccharide β-CD, for imaging and treating ALI ( A) [ , ]. Subsequent studies showed that LCD NPs effectively responded to increased ROS and MPO levels under inflammatory conditions. Specifically, this type of NPs significantly inhibited the migration of neutrophils and macrophages, attenuated the inflammatory response, and reduced oxidative stress in ALI mice ( B).
 Additionally, it has been well-documented that phenolic moieties will be oxidized in the presence of MPO and H2O2, leading to the generation of free radicals that subsequently undergo dimerization or oligomerization [ ]. Inspired by these findings, our group engineered PLGA NPs peripherally decorated with different phenolic groups [ ]. Notably, tyramine (Tyr)-coated PLGA NPs exhibited a significantly high accumulation at the inflammatory site in mice with LPS-induced ALI, mainly resulting from in situ cross-linking and tissue anchoring of NPs triggered by local MPO and ROS. To augment the therapeutic efficacy, we further developed a multifunctional nanotherapy (TTN) by surface engineering of TPCD NPs with Tyr. This nanotherapy demonstrated enhanced cellular uptake, intensified accumulation in inflammatory tissues, and pronounced anti-inflammatory properties, ultimately affording amplified therapeutic effects in multiple murine models of acute inflammation. Overall, current studies are concentrated on enhancing the precision, effectiveness, and safety of enzyme-responsive delivery platforms in the treatment of acute pulmonary inflammation. These endeavors involve incorporating enzyme-sensitive components into nanocarriers to facilitate enhanced accumulation and controlled drug release in response to elevated enzyme levels associated with acute pulmonary inflammation. Furthermore, the integration of enzyme-responsive elements enables customized therapeutic interventions, such as anti-inflammation, anti-oxidation, and immune regulation, thereby enhancing therapeutic benefits of these nanotherapies in mitigating acute pulmonary inflammation.

3.3.

## pH-responsive nanotherapies
Capitalizing on acidosis in inflamed tissues and acidic conditions within cellular endosomes/lysosomes and Golgi bodies, sophisticated pH-responsive NPs have been engineered for therapeutic delivery [ ]. At the tissue level, these systems exploit the pH gradient between inflamed microenvironments (pH 6.5–6.8) and physiological tissues (pH 7.4) to target diverse inflammatory diseases including ALI, rheumatoid arthritis, and atherosclerosis [ ]. At the subcellular level, they leverage the pH differential between endosomes/lysosomes (pH 4.5–5.5) and the cytoplasm (pH 7.2) to orchestrate multistage cascade release mechanisms, enabling sequential cargo delivery from endosomal escape to cytoplasmic targeting [ ]. The pH-responsive NPs can be created by pH-degradable linkages, pH-cleavable cross-linkers, or charge-shifting polymers ( c) [ ]. The pH-sensitive nature of these NPs stems from either the hydrolysis of acid-labile bonds or the protonation of ionizable groups. Acid-labile bonds, encompassing vicinal esters, vinyl/silyl ethers, β-thiopropionates, acetals/ketals, and hydrazones/oximes, are frequently utilized to construct delivery systems capable of selectively releasing loaded therapeutic molecules in acidic environments [ ].
3.3.1.

## Nanotherapies derived from poly(β-amino esters)
Poly(β-amino esters) (PBAEs) have been widely used in the development of stimuli-responsive drug delivery systems, owing to their favorable pH sensitivity, excellent biocompatibility, and straightforward synthetic accessibility. Their pH-responsive properties arise from the ester bonds in the polymer backbone, which undergo hydrolysis under acidic conditions. The resulting water-soluble degradation byproducts, including bis(β-amino acids) and diols, exhibit negligible cytotoxicity under physiological conditions [ ]. Furthermore, the tertiary amine groups along the backbone enhance pH responsiveness. In slightly acidic environments, these amines undergo protonation, endowing the polymer with cationic and hydrophilic characters that promote efficient lysosomal escape through the proton sponge effect [ ]. While PBAEs display hydrophobic characteristics at physiological pH, they rapidly solubilize in aqueous media under acidic conditions, thereby enabling pH-triggered drug release. Du et al. utilized electrostatic interactions to coat liposomes with alternating layers of PBAEs and sodium alginate (NaAlg), creating hybrid NPs (termed as Spe@HNPs) with uniform size (198 nm) and negative ζ-potential (−55.0 mV at pH 7.4) [ ]. These NPs showed pH-sensitive drug release kinetics, liberating 90 % of spectinomycin (Spe) in 10 h at pH 6.0 versus 33 % at pH 7.4. In an ALI model, Spe@HNPs reduced pulmonary bacterial load by 99.9 % and significantly attenuated inflammation, correlating with their prolonged systemic circulation ( A). Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed a blood half-life of 5 h for Spe@HNPs, compared to less than 0.5 h for free Spe, highlighting the nanocarrier's critical role in enhancing drug bioavailability and therapeutic outcomes. In another study, Wang et al. explored PBAE as a core element for drug encapsulation and controlled release, while simultaneously incorporating a PEG-biotin layer on the particle surface to enable bioconjugation, facilitating prolonging circulation and targeted delivery to the lungs [ ]. In this case, acrylate-terminated PBAE was synthesized and conjugated with PEG-biotin to form block copolymers that self-assembled into micelles (82.3 nm). This nanovehicle encapsulated an anti-inflammatory drug [5-(p-fluorophenyl)-2-ureido]thiophene-3-carboxamide (TPCA-1) with 20.01 ± 0.21 % (w/w) loading capacity. The pH-sensitive release profile was demonstrated: <20 % TPCA-1 released at physiological pH over 24 h versus 90 % within 15 h at endosomal pH. Surface modification with anti-ICAM-1 antibody doubled endothelial cell targeting efficiency compared to non-targeted micelles. In a mouse model of acute lung inflammation, the TPCA-1-NPs-anti-ICAM-1 formulation showed preferential accumulation in inflamed lungs, achieving twice the drug concentration of non-targeted NPs. This study demonstrates that pH-responsive NPs conjugated with targeting antibodies can selectively home to inflamed lungs following i. v. Injection, where acidic microenvironments trigger drug release to effectively mitigate inflammation and tissue injury.
 
3.3.2.

## Acetal/ketal-containing nanotherapies
For drug delivery, acetals/ketals are generally employed as acid-labile moieties to construct pH-responsive materials and prodrugs derived from small-molecule drugs or proteins [ ]. In particular, acetals have been utilized as pH-sensitive linkages to fabricate various drug delivery systems, such as polymer-drug conjugates, micelles, NPs, and microgels [ , ]. Our group has synthesized a diverse library of biodegradable and acid-sensitive acetalated cyclodextrin (CD) materials by kinetically controlled acetalation [ ]. The pH-labile hydrolysis profiles of these CD materials can be precisely tailored by adjusting the acetalation time. Notably, complete hydrolysis of acetalated β-CD-derived NPs results in the production of free β-CD and other water-soluble molecules. Furthermore, both in vitro and in vivo safety assessments have confirmed the excellent biocompatibility of acetalated CD materials. By encapsulating diverse therapeutic agents, the obtained drug-containing acetalated CD NPs offered beneficial therapeutic outcomes in treating inflammatory diseases [ , , , , , ]. A typical example involves acetalated α-CD (ACD) NPs containing anti-miR33, an antisense oligonucleotide, which enabled site-specific delivery and triggerable release of therapeutic cargoes [ ]. In LPS and IFN-γ-activated macrophages, treatment with anti-miR33/ACD NPs reprogrammed macrophages toward the M2 phenotype while promoting naïve T-cell differentiation into regulatory T (Treg) cells. In view of anti-inflammatory effects of both M2 macrophages and Treg cells, these findings underscore the therapeutic potential of this pH-responsive nucleic acid nanotherapy in addressing acute pulmonary inflammation.
To provide more precise delivery in response to inflammatory conditions, dual-responsive biomaterials and delivery systems sensitive to both pH and ROS have been developed. As a decent example, Almutairi et al. fabricated dual-responsive NPs by combining ROS-responsive phenylboronic acid-modified dextran with acid-sensitive acetal dextran [ ]. The dual-responsive NPs exhibited markedly enhanced efficacy in mitigating the levels of IL-6 by 120-fold and TNF-α by 6-fold compared to free naproxen in LPS-activated macrophages. Our team engineered pH/ROS-responsive NPs using two CD-derived materials, i.e., ACD and PBAP-modified CD (OCD), with tunable responsiveness governed by the ACD/OCD weight ratio [ ]. The optimized pH/ROS-dual responsive AOCD NPs loaded with rapamycin (RAP) rapidly released RAP under acidic conditions or elevated H2O2. Conjugation of a specific peptide further enhanced targeting efficiency. In a rat model of vascular inflammation, the dual-responsive RAP nanotherapy demonstrated beneficial efficacy, compared to control nanotherapies. The superior anti-inflammatory activities of these pH/ROS-responsive delivery systems provide valuable insights into the rational design of advanced nanotherapies for inflammatory lung diseases.

3.3.3.

## Hydrazone-based nanotherapies
Hydrazone linkages have been widely used in pharmaceutical sciences due to their excellent pH-sensitivity as well as synthetic flexibility and diversity. In a study by Wang et al. a pH-sensitive doxorubicin (DOX) prodrug was synthesized by conjugating DOX to BSA via a hydrazone bond, giving rise to DOX-hyd-BSA ( B) that can form NPs by self-assembly [ ]. In a LPS-induced ALI mouse model, treatment with DOX-hyd-BSA NPs significantly reduced the neutrophil infiltration in the lung tissue and lowered cytokine levels, in comparison to free DOX or PBS treatment. Following i. v. administration, DOX-hyd-BSA NPs exhibited specific targeting toward activated neutrophils in circulation, leading to their high internalization in neutrophils responding to infections or tissue injury. In the mildly acidic organelles of neutrophils, cleavage of hydrazone bonds released DOX molecules, inducing neutrophil apoptosis and thereby alleviating transmigration. By contrast, a pH-insensitive analogue showed no apoptosis-inducing activity in neutrophil-like HL-60 cells, underscoring the indispensable role of the hydrazone trigger.
Adopting a similar strategy, Chang et al. established a pH-responsive nanotherapy, i.e., Omi-hyd-Dex@HA NPs, which comprises a conjugate (Omi-hyd-Dex) derived from an antimicrobial peptide omiganan and Dex linked via the hydrazone bond [ ]. Of note, this system harnesses HA coating to facilitate targeted delivery to inflamed endothelial cells via interaction with ICAM-1, thereby enhancing drug accumulation at primary infection sites. Additionally, the pH-sensitive cleavage of hydrazone bonds promotes dual drug release, simultaneously enabling eradication of pathogens and suppression of pulmonary inflammation. Compared to other pH-sensitive linkers such as orthoesters, acetals, or ketals, hydrazone bonds are more conducive to the development of acid-triggered prodrug systems. On the other hand, hydrazone bonds have been utilized for synthesizing diverse carrier materials to create various smart nanotherapies. For instance, Kandasamy et al. incorporated hydrazone bonds in a copolymer of chitosan and mPEG to develop prednisolone-loaded polymeric NPs that exhibit pH-responsive drug release behaviors [ ]. Notably, PEG conjugates with hydrazone linkers are frequently employed in fabricating long-circulating nanocarriers with acid-responsive detachable coatings, which are particularly intriguing for intracellular delivery of peptides, proteins, and nucleic acids [ ]. Collectively, these studies highlight hydrazone bonds as a versatile and clinically translatable triggering mechanism for spatiotemporally controlled therapy in acute pulmonary inflammation and related pathologies.

3.3.4.

## Nanotherapies based on pH-responsive peptides
The inherent self-assembly properties of pH-responsive peptides, integrating with pH-responsive peptide-based biomaterials, offer a powerful approach for precise drug delivery and enhanced tissue regeneration [ ]. These systems leverage pH-driven ionization states of side chains to control hydrophilic-hydrophobic balance [ ]. Under acidic conditions, protonation of carboxyl-containing amino residues reduces net charge, triggering hydrophobic collapse into well-defined nanostructures. Conversely, at elevated pH, deprotonation increases negative charge density, promoting electrostatic repulsion and hydration-induced disassembly into soluble monomers. These assemblies undergo reversible structural interconversion in response to ambient pH fluctuations, showing dynamic material adaptability.
For example, a pH-responsive cationic amphipathic peptide LAH4-L1 demonstrated effectiveness as a delivery vector, facilitating the release of nucleic acids into the cytoplasm through endosomal escape mechanisms [ ]. In efforts to enhance the DNA transfection efficiency of LAH4-L1, Chen et al. strategically incorporated a nuclear localizing signal to facilitate nuclear import. Concurrently, they introduced a N-terminus PEGylation strategy utilizing a 12-monomer linear monodisperse PEG, which significantly improved the mRNA transfection efficiency and biocompatibility of the original non-PEGylated cationic peptide carrier [ , ]. The transfection efficiency of the LAH4-L1 peptide-based mRNA delivery system was evaluated by non-invasive intranasal administration of luciferase mRNA into mice. Notably, the PEGylated LAH4-L1 peptide formulation, defined as PEG12LA/mRNA, successfully targeted the deep lung regions, in contrast to free mRNA that showed negligible lung expression. The successful deep lung targeting and notable mRNA expression by PEG12LA/mRNA suggest that this pH-responsive platform is promising for developing targeted nucleic acid delivery systems for effective treatment of pulmonary inflammation.

3.3.5.

## Nanotherapies derived from pH-responsive metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)
MOFs, due to their three-dimensional porous architecture, offer a multitude of benefits for drug delivery, such as high porosity, substantial specific surface area, robust stability, and tunable structures. Among diverse MOFs, zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) distinguishes itself as an exceptionally biocompatible delivery vehicle, which is well-suitable for drug encapsulation and controlled release, resulting from its unique pore geometry. Notably, the ZIF-8 framework is characterized by metal-imidazole coordination bonds and zinc ions. Its pH-responsive behavior is primarily attributed to the protonation of imidazole moieties under acidic conditions, a process that induces disassembly of ZIF-8 structure, making it an attractive candidate for constructing responsive drug delivery systems.
Dong et al. utilized a pH-responsive nanospray based on ZIF-8, loaded with pentoxifylline and edaravone, for targeted treatment of ARDS [ ]. In both in vitro and in vivo models, this nanospray significantly inhibited the inflammatory response, mitigated oxidative stress, and efficiently mitigated lung tissue damage associated with ARDS. In a separate study, Wang et al. innovatively formulated a composite ZIF-8 system, which effectively encapsulated Ag NPs and integrated a phytochemical (Phy) [ ]. Its surface was further modified with HA to increase the affinity to bacterial cells, thereby amplifying its antibacterial effects. The resultant multifaceted system, termed as Ag-Phy@ZIF-8@HA, was rationally engineered to address bacterial drug resistance, enhance safety, and overcome the issues associated with unregulated silver release and accumulation. On the one hand, bacterial metabolism produces hyaluronidase, leading to enrichment of Ag-Phy@ZIF-8@HA around the bacteria and hydrolysis of HA coating. On the other hand, ZIF-8 will be disintegrated in the acidic microenvironment around the strain, thus enhancing specific release of Ag and Phy to realize simultaneous antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects.
Taken together, pH-responsive delivery systems hold great promise for the treatment of pulmonary inflammation. The previously developed advanced drug delivery systems are specifically engineered to exploit the unique acidic microenvironment in the lungs caused by acute inflammation. By utilizing pH-responsive mechanisms, these systems provide tailored drug retention and release profiles to improve efficacy of existing drugs for the potential management of pulmonary inflammation.

3.4.

## Limitations of bioresponsive nanotherapies
Whereas preclinical studies in animal models have demonstrated therapeutic advantages of different bioresponsive nanotherapies for the treatment of acute pulmonary inflammation, their clinical translation faces considerable hurdles. After i. v. delivery, a large proportion of nanotherapies will be accumulated in the MPS, such as the liver and spleen. Accordingly, off target-associated side effects are critical concerns to be addressed for most nanotherapies developed for acute pulmonary inflammation. Particularly, in vivo hydrolysis, metabolism, and excretion profiles of nearly all bioresponsive nanotherapies investigated thus far have not been thoroughly evaluated. Consequently, their safety performance remains to be confirmed by intensive studies, taking into account a variety of factors, such as the material structures, formulation compositions, physicochemical properties, route of administration, and the dosage regimen [ ]. For enzyme-responsive nanotherapies, their stability in the physiological environment is also an important issue. Moreover, the fabrication of bioresponsive nanotherapies generally requires intricate design strategies as well as involves complicated procedures and the use of new carrier materials [ ]. These aspects cause additional challenges in the development pipeline, particularly regarding quality control, repeatable mass production, and cost-effectiveness.
Furthermore, while the high sensitivity to the pathological extracellular and intracellular microenvironment is a very important parameter for responsive nanotherapies to achieve well controlled drug release and afford high bioavailability, the development of carrier materials capable of responding to biochemical signals at pathologically relevant levels (such as within the nanomolar concentration range) is also highly challenging [ ]. Last but not the least, despite the established methods for quantifying the levels of biochemical molecules in vitro and ex vivo, there have been few techniques that enable real-time and in situ precise measurements of their dynamic changes in lung tissues during the progression of acute inflammation, thus limiting rational engineering of precision bioresponsive nanotherapies.

4.

## Biomimetic nanotherapies for acute pulmonary inflammation treatment
To address the limitations of purely synthetic nanocarriers, researchers are increasingly turning to natural materials for the development of advanced nanotherapies. A particularly promising approach involves the creation of biomimetic nanotherapies that exploit the unique recognition mechanisms and biological interactions inherent in living systems. Drawing inspiration from nature, recent studies have focused on engineering vehicles that emulate the properties and functions of biological components, including cells, cell membranes, and extracellular vesicles (EVs), to enhance targeted drug delivery to damaged pulmonary tissues. These biomimetic platforms synergize the favorable attributes of both synthetic and natural materials, offering significant advantages such as improved biocompatibility, prolonged systemic circulation, and precise targeting capabilities in the complex microenvironment of acute lung inflammation ( ).
 4.1.

## Cell hitchhiking-based biomimetic therapies
Hitchhiking, an intriguing approach in drug delivery, involves the strategic utilization of endogenous molecular or cellular carriers to transport therapeutic molecules to their designated location [ , ]. The adaptability of NPs enables them to engage with circulatory cells or proteins, thereby facilitating the hitchhiking process [ ]. By leveraging the inherent capabilities of circulatory cells or proteins, these hitchhiking NPs navigate the vasculature while avoiding immune system clearance. This allows them to perform specialized functions, ranging from delivering drugs/nutrients to tissues/cells, eliminating pathogens, to even monitoring the immune system's activities [ ]. The general strategies for NPs to hitchhike on circulatory cells or proteins include internalization-mediated hitchhiking and adsorption-mediated hitchhiking ( A) [ ]. The phagocytic ability of cells is crucial for successful hitchhiking in internalization-mediated hitchhiking, wherein NPs are typically incubated with phagocytic cells in a culture medium, facilitating their adsorption onto the cell surface and subsequent internalization by the cells [ ]. To address the challenges associated with cell extraction and purification, innovative direct in vivo hitchhiking strategies have been explored, predominantly leveraging the scavenging capabilities of neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, and other cells [ , ]. According to the different interaction modes between NPs and cells, adsorption-mediated hitchhiking can be roughly divided into two categories: non-specific adsorption driven by electrostatic interactions, van der Waals forces, and hydrogen bonding; and site-specific adsorption mediated by specific ligand-receptor recognition. As a strategy for cell-mediated drug delivery, the use of carefully designed and well-defined synthetic molecules to modify cell surfaces has been shown to be an effective way to introduce innovative functionalities or regulating cellular behaviors [ ].
 The process of cell-hitchhiking enables the precise delivery of therapeutic molecules by leveraging the inherent homing capabilities of select cell populations to target sites of inflammation in the lungs affected by acute inflammation. Through emulation of the interactions between circulating cells and the pulmonary endothelium, biomimetic nanotherapies can afford improved selectivity and prolonged retention within the afflicted tissue. Various cell types, such as red blood cells (RBCs), mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), neutrophils, and macrophages, exhibit the capacity for prolonged circulation or migration to lung tissues ( ). Taking advantage of these properties, numerous innovative nanotherapies have been developed, demonstrating promising opportunities for addressing the multifaceted nature of acute pulmonary inflammation.
 4.1.1.

## Hitchhiking on RBCs
RBCs represent the most abundant cell type in the human body. The potential of RBCs as delivery vehicles has been evidenced by their low immunogenicity and ultralong circulation time as well as capacity to efficiently load various types of therapeutics [ ]. Recently, a RBC-hitchhiking strategy has been established for the targeted delivery of ivermectin (IVM)-loaded NPs specifically to the lung [ ]. IVM-loaded PLGA NPs (IVM-PNPs) and chitosan-coated IVM-PNPs (IVM-CSPNPs) were prepared and adsorbed onto RBCs in a non-covalent manner. The nanotherapies hitchhiked on RBCs until they disassociated under physiological shear stress. Both RBC-hitchhiked IVM-PNPs and IVM-CSPNPs significantly enhanced IVM delivery to lungs, inhibited the inflammatory responses, and finally alleviated the progression of ALI. Notably, cationic IVM-CSPNPs hitchhiked on RBCs exhibited prolonged circulation time, slower accumulation and elimination rates, and higher anti-inflammatory activities compared to anionic IVM-PNPs. The distinctive redistribution and circulation effects are related to the inherent properties of nanotherapies. The same group further demonstrated that cationic CSPNPs, prepared by an ionic gel method, can rapidly adsorb on RBCs via electrostatic interactions and facilitate targeted delivery of methylprednisolone sodium succinate to the lungs, thereby enhancing drug accumulation in lung tissues while prolonging circulation times [ ].
As well demonstrated, β-CD-based supramolecular host-guest assemblies possess great potential for drug delivery, due to their superior biocompatibility and adaptable guest-binding properties [ , ]. Of note, these assemblies can be easily functionalized to engineer stimuli-responsive nanotherapies [ ]. In particular, the binding affinity between β-CD and ferrocene (Fc) exhibits a transition from robust under normal conditions to comparatively weak under oxidative conditions. This unique property makes β-CD-Fc pairs effective as non-covalent redox-sensitive linkages for constructing responsive nanotherapies. Taking advantage of this property, Wang et al. developed an RBC-NP conjugate mediated by redox-sensitive β-CD-Fc host-guest pairs ( A) [ ]. The RBC-NP conjugate exhibited remarkable stability in vitro, maintaining its integrity for at least 12 h. Upon oxidation of Fc by ROS, the host-guest pairs were dissociated, and the NPs efficiently detached and released from RBCs. In vivo experiments demonstrated that this ROS-responsive supramolecular RBC-hitchhiking drug delivery system can transport the attached NPs to the inflamed sites in the lungs, enabling specific and on-demand drug release at the diseased site. After loading Cur into the β-CD-Fc NPs, the resulting RBC-Cur-NP nanotherapy can release Cur in response to high levels of ROS at the site of lung injury. Administration of RBC-Cur-NP via i. v. Injection significantly alleviated pneumonia by downregulating inflammatory cytokines, ameliorating pulmonary edema, and repolarizing proinflammatory macrophages.
 The cell surface of RBCs harbors abundant receptors, providing excellent opportunities for NPs to hitch a ride through ligand-receptor interactions [ ]. Brenner et al. introduced a concept of dual affinity to RBCs and target cells (DART), which involves the conjugation of nanocarriers to two affinity ligands. These ligands consist of antibody-protein fusions that bind to specific proteins on RBC surfaces via the Fab region of the antibody. One ligand binds to RBCs while the other one specifically targets pulmonary endothelial cells [ ]. In contrast to passive RBC hitchhiking, DART significantly enhanced the adsorbing efficiency of nanocarriers on the surface of RBCs, facilitating the delivery of over 65 % of the injected nanocarrier dose to the lungs. This method surpassed single antibody targeting by 2.5 folds even when compared to well-studied lung-targeting antibodies like anti-CAMs. Flow cytometry analysis confirmed that in the lung, DART-loaded nanocarriers exhibited a 5.7-fold higher distribution in the target endothelial cells compared to identical nanocarriers not loaded on RBCs. Overall, the cell-hitchhiking strategy utilizing RBCs demonstrates targeted lung delivery capability, providing a foundational basis to engineer precision nanotherapies for treating acute pulmonary inflammation.

4.1.2.

## Hitchhiking on MSCs
MSCs exhibit a wide range of characteristics, such as anti-inflammatory activity, immunomodulatory effects, antimicrobial actions, enhanced alveolar fluid clearance, and restoration of alveolar function and pulmonary capillary permeability barrier function [ , ]. Of particular interest is their capacity for homing, allowing them to navigate to sites of injury and inflammation [ ]. Yang et al. employed the click chemical reaction to bind a nanotherapy based on antioxidant liposomes (i.e., Lipo-OPC) containing oligomeric proanthocyanidin (OPC) to the surface of MSCs. This approach aims to combat the detrimental effects of high levels of ROS in inflammatory environments which can compromise the viability and pluripotency of MSCs, thereby realizing specific accumulation to inflamed regions ( B) [ ]. The finally obtained therapy MSC-Lipo-OPC showed significant accumulation in the X-ray-exposed lung tissue, demonstrating a superior targeting effect for inflamed lungs over healthy lungs. In various stages of lung injury, MSC-Lipo-OPC could modulate the function of innate immune cells, thereby mitigating the inflammatory response and delaying the progression of pneumonia.
Multiple studies have confirmed that MSCs can serve as vectors for gene therapy to alleviate ARDS [ ]. Nuclear factor-erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a key transcription factor for antioxidant response element (ARE)-mediated expression of antioxidant proteins. This pathway functions as a major cell defense mechanism against oxidative damages caused by injury and inflammation [ ]. It was found that Nrf2 transfection can enhance the efficacy of human amniotic MSCs (hAMSCs) in repairing lung injury induced by LPS [ ]. Nrf2-overexpressing hAMSCs also exhibited increased lung retention, improved differentiation into AECs, significantly higher expressions of Nrf2 mRNA and protein, and stronger DNA-binding activity, compared to the control hAMSCs without Nrf2 transfection. Shen et al. employed lentiviral-mediated CXCR7 overexpression in rat bone marrow-derived MSCs to enhances their paracrine activity and homing capacity [ ]. This genetic modification significantly increased MSC recruitment to phosgene-injured lungs and attenuated lung injury. Collectively, surface engineering and genetic functionalization exemplify precision-engineering strategies for optimizing MSC-based therapies against inflammatory lung diseases.

4.1.3.

## Hitchhiking on neutrophils
Neutrophils, as crucial components of the human immune system, serve as the initial line of defense by rapidly responding to inflammatory stimuli. Upon activation, these cells enhance their phagocytic capabilities by upregulating surface receptors [ ]. Furthermore, neutrophils demonstrate a proclivity towards the inflammatory site, adeptly navigating through various biological barriers by shape alterations and other intricate mechanisms. Once they arrive at the inflamed site, neutrophils release NETs, facilitating the release of loaded nanotherapies [ ]. In this context, the Malik's team has capitalized on the neutrophil hitchhiking strategy to achieve targeted delivery of albumin NPs to vascular inflammatory sites [ ]. Serum-derived albumin NPs were chosen due to their biocompatibility, non-immunogenicity, and preferential uptake by activated neutrophils on inflamed endothelium. These NPs enable specific targeting of inflammatory neutrophils while maintaining essential host-defense functions in circulating neutrophils, thereby avoiding adverse effects associated with broad inhibition of neutrophil activity. Intravital microscopy observation of mouse cremaster venules showed that denatured albumin NPs specifically target activated neutrophils adherent to vessel walls, with subsequent transport across endothelial barriers to infection sites.
To further enhance the precision and effectiveness of neutrophil hitchhiking approaches, recent studies reveal that specific pathogens utilize the complement system to facilitate cellular hitchhiking. This occurs through “voluntary opsonization” involving complement fragment iC3b, followed by complement receptor 3-mediated phagocytosis [ ]. These iC3b-opsonized liposomes subsequently hitchhike into inflamed lungs, where neutrophils release therapeutic cargo either through NETosis or by serving as intracellular microcontainers that co-sequester bacteria and antibiotics for targeted microbial killing [ ].
Moreover, the integration of ROS-responsive NPs with the neutrophil hitchhiking strategy to create a multistage targeting system is another innovative approach to enhance targeting and therapeutic effects of available drugs. You et al. designed a nanomicelle utilizing HA and α-tocopherol succinate (α-TOS) as the main components for loading an endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress inhibitor KIRA6 and an anti-inflammatory drug Dex [ ]. By linking through redox-responsive disulfide bond, a copolymer HA-SS-α-TOS (named HSST) was obtained, which could be enriched into inflamed lungs, enabling efficient lung targeting via hitchhiking neutrophils. In this delivery system, HA targets the CD44 receptor upregulated on activated macrophages and neutrophils during inflammation [ ], while α-TOS dual-functionally forms the hydrophobic core for drug encapsulation and enhances therapeutic efficacy through potent antioxidant activity. HSST undergoes CD44-mediated phagocytosis by these immune cells, leveraging enhanced phagocytic capacity during inflammation. The internalized nanomicelles are subsequently transported to inflamed pulmonary tissues through neutrophil migratory pathways, where ROS-triggered drug release selectively targets damaged epithelial cells. This strategy achieves fivefold greater pulmonary accumulation compared to non-targeted NPs. By incorporating a targeting molecule Pardaxin, KIRA6 can be delivered specifically to the ER. Meanwhile, redox-responsive release of Dex synergized with KIRA6 to effectively reduce oxidative stress, mitigate cytokine storms, restore ER homeostasis, and reverse imbalanced macrophage polarization. The findings of precise drug delivery, coupled with the exceptional anti-inflammatory properties and robust infection control, collectively indicate that the neutrophil hitchhiking strategy represents a promising therapeutic modality for treating acute lung inflammation. This approach, by capitalizing on the natural trafficking patterns of neutrophils, emerges as an innovative and potent strategy for addressing the intricate pathophysiological mechanisms underlying pulmonary inflammation.

4.1.4.

## Hitchhiking on macrophages
Similar to neutrophils, macrophages also own a unique capacity for chemotactic migration and accumulation at diseased sites, highlighting their importance as sophisticated delivery vehicles [ ]. Their remarkable heterogeneity and plasticity empower them to polarize into either classically activated macrophages (M1) or selectively activated macrophages (M2), which are precisely tailored to meet the demands of the local microenvironment. Based on the high binding affinity between cucurbit [ ]uril (CB [ ]) and adamantanamine (ADA) [ ], Wang et al. constructed “supramolecular macrophages”, by modifying the macrophage surface with CB [ ] via simple lipid ligand membrane-insertion. This study presents a novel and cell-friendly approach to form stable macrophage-liposome complexes [ ]. To confirm rapid targeted delivery and immune regulation in a mouse model of ALI, quercetin (Que) was loaded into liposomes to construct therapeutic macrophage-liposome (M-Que-L) hybrids. Compared to the mice treated with Que-containing liposomes (Que-L) and macrophages combined with Que-L (M + Que-L), M-Que-L exhibited superior lung targeting ability. It significantly improved heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) expression and mitigated severe lung injuries induced by LPS, with respect to inhibiting inflammation in the lung interstitium and alveolar spaces as well as reducing thickening of the alveolar wall. In another study by the same group, the authors demonstrated that the high binding affinity between β-CD and ADA can be utilized to prepare supramolecular cell-liposome and cell-cell delivery systems [ ]. These systems were obtained via host-guest interactions between β-CD-modified macrophages and ADA-modified liposomes, as well as between β-CD-modified macrophages and ADA-modified hitchhiking cells. In ALI mice, supramolecular conjugation of peritoneal macrophages and Que-loaded liposomes significantly improved the targeting efficiency of liposomes and effectively alleviated lung inflammation by anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. In addition to the inherent homing capability of cell carriers, the homing or targeting capability can be further enhanced by NPs. For example, Wang et al. developed manganese dioxide NPs separately modified with β-CD and ADA, leading to self-assembly of large intracellular aggregates through host-guest interactions [ ]. This system offers several advantages, such as inhibition of NPs efflux, catalytic depletion of H2O2 to mitigate inflammation, and generation of O2 to facilitate macrophage migration for deep tissue penetration in the inflammatory lungs.

4.1.5.

## Limitations of cell hitchhiking-based therapies
The process of hitchhiking or the uptake of nanotherapies by carrier cells has the possibility to disrupt normal cellular functions, impact their intrinsic capabilities, and potentially result in immune-related toxicities. If nanotherapies are retained in the hitchhiking cells for a prolonged period, there is a potential risk of bioaccumulation, leading to the gradual accumulation of drug molecules and potential unpredictable consequences. Meanwhile, both endocytosis and adsorption-mediated hitchhiking strategies have limitations. Endocytosis-mediated hitchhiking has inherent disadvantages in terms of therapeutic potential, as nanotherapies are contained within the cells and thus susceptible to degradation and deactivation [ ]. A critical limitation in the application of internalized nanotherapies lies in their inefficient release from carrier cells, particularly when they need to modulate neighboring cells or the extracellular microenvironment. Additionally, drug loading capacity must be carefully constrained to avoid cytotoxic effects on the carrier cells themselves.
To be emphasized, when nanotherapies are tethered on the cell surface, it is paramount to ensure stability and controlled dissociation from the vehicle cells. Since circulatory cells transport nanotherapies through the bloodstream, they encounter significant shear forces [ ]. Appropriate stability of the NP-cell complexes is crucial for preventing premature release of nanotherapies during the hitchhiking process. Therefore, the following two aspects should be considered for rational design of ideal nanotherapies that rely on adsorption for in vivo hitchhiking. First, a strong binding affinity between nanotherapies and cells should be ensured to withstand shear forces in blood circulation. Second, controlled release from hitchhiked cells upon reaching targets is necessary to optimize therapeutic effects and simultaneously minimize toxic effects. In addition, long-term interactions with the cell surface may promote the cellular internalization of nanotherapies, potentially altering their intended functionalities. Therefore, resistance to cellular endocytosis should also be an important property of the employed nanotherapies.

4.2.

## Cell membrane-based biomimetic nanotherapies
The cell membrane serves as the outermost protective layer of the cell, primarily composed of lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates. It interacts with the surrounding environment to perform complex biological functions [ , ]. In 2011, Zhang et al. pioneered a top-down biomimetic strategy, harnessing natural red blood cell membranes to cloak polymeric NPs, with the objective of diminishing macrophage uptake and systemic clearance [ ]. Subsequently, an increasing variety of cellular membranes have been investigated for surface modification of NPs, thereby expanding the scope of biomimetic nanotherapies ( ). Cell membrane-coated NPs seamlessly integrate biological properties of the cell membrane with the functional versatility of template nanocarriers, offering an efficient strategy for engineering advanced nanotherapies [ ]. This approach holds the promise of significantly simplifying the development of nanotherapies with advanced functions, enabling them to effectively navigate complex biological environments by evading immune clearance and specifically targeting disease sites for accumulation [ ].
 Irrespective of the cell source, the preparation process of cell membrane-based biomimetic nanoplatforms comprises three principal stages: cell membrane extraction, selection/preparation of a template based on expected properties for drug release, and assembly of cell membrane-coated NPs ( B). The assembly of extracted cell membranes from target cells with the selected NPs, capable of incorporating the required biophysiochemical properties, is the most crucial step [ ]. A variety of techniques are commonly employed to coat cell membranes onto NP cores, including physical extrusion, sonication, and microfluidic electroporation [ , ]. Furthermore, advanced strategies such as lipid insertion, membrane hybridization, metabolic engineering, and genetic modification can be employed to non-destructively enhance the multifunctionality and multitasking capability of cell membrane-coated NPs. These approaches not only preserve the natural biological functions of the membrane components but also significantly improve nanocarrier adaptability in complex biological systems [ , ]. Cell membrane-coated nanotherapies represent a highly promising strategy for mitigating the cytokine storm characteristic of ARDS, demonstrating superior biocompatibility, precise targeting capabilities, and immunomodulatory potential compared to conventional therapies.
4.2.1.

## Macrophage membrane-coated nanotherapies
Inspired by the inherent affinity of macrophages to inflammatory sites, Chen et al. constructed a targeted nanotherapy for pneumonia using macrophage-mimicking NPs [ ]. This nanotherapy can effectively accumulate in inflamed lung tissues and exhibit precise release of a model drug Cur at the site of inflammation. In an ALI mouse model, the Cur-containing nanotherapy effectively suppressed the production of pro-inflammatory factors and the infiltration of inflammatory cells, thereby reducing inflammation and cytokine storm. Moreover, macrophage membrane-cloaked NPs can prevent the exposure of exogenous nanotherapies and inhibit their ingestion by the MPS, ensuring long-term circulation in the body [ ]. Taking advantage of surface receptors and signaling molecules similar to those of macrophages, NPs coated with macrophage membranes and loaded with ceftiofur enabled targeted therapy of pulmonary inflammation [ ].
Macrophage membrane-cloaked NPs have also been examined as new therapies for COVID-19. As a typical example, Huang et al. introduced multifunctional alveolar macrophage-like NPs, which were engineered by enveloping polymeric cores with alveolar macrophage membranes [ ]. To a certain degree, these NPs mimic the surface receptors of AMs, including the receptors for coronavirus and a spectrum of cytokine receptors. By functioning as nanodecoys, the macrophage membrane-cloaked NPs effectively prevented the coronavirus from entering host cells and simultaneously sequestered various proinflammatory cytokines. This dual action provides a synergistic therapeutic effect, offering both antiviral and anti-inflammatory benefits in the treatment of COVID-19.
By genetic modification, the applications of cell membrane-based biomimetic therapies can be further expanded. For example, Cui et al. developed inhalable methacrylated HA hydrogel microspheres, which were coated with genetically engineered membranes derived from HEK293 cells overexpressing angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptors and proinflammatory macrophages ( A) [ ]. The inhalable microspheres can compete with SARS-CoV-2 for ACE2 binding, thus significantly reducing the virus infectivity throughout the respiratory system. Whereas the monotypic cell membrane inadequately fulfills the diverse demands of biomedical applications, the integration of various types of cell membranes, referred to as biomimetic hybrid membrane-based nanoplatforms, offers a promising approach for targeted therapy of ARDS [ , ]. In another study, cell nanosponges were engineered using the plasma membranes of human lung epithelial type II cells or macrophages displaying the receptors required for the entry of SARS-CoV-2 into cells [ ]. Upon incubation with the nanosponges, SARS-CoV-2 was neutralized and unable to infect cells. More interestingly, the nanosponge platform is agnostic to viral mutations and potentially viral species. As long as the target of the virus remains the identified host cells, the nanosponges will be able to neutralize the virus.
 
4.2.2.

## Neutrophil membrane-derived nanotherapies
Neutrophils, comprising 40–75 % of the total white blood cell population in humans, are the most prevalent leukocytes [ ]. During inflammatory processes, neutrophils are the initial responders to migrate towards the injury site, a behavior closely linked to the receptors expressed on their cell membranes [ ]. Leveraging this distinctive tropism, neutrophil membrane-coated NPs offer a promising approach for achieving efficient and targeted drug delivery to the lungs. In a previous study, our group extracted neutrophil membrane without compromising its functional integrity and coated it onto NPs derived from luminol-conjugated α-CD (LaCD) to create a biomimetic nanotherapy NM-LaCD NP, aiming to enhance targeting and therapeutic effects for inflammatory pulmonary diseases ( B and C) [ ]. It is worth noting that NPs derived from luminol-conjugated CD materials have demonstrated intrinsically anti-inflammatory activity in mice with LPS-induced ALI [ ]. Leveraging the tropism of neutrophil-derived cell membrane towards inflammatory sites, the lung targeting efficiency of NM-LaCD NP was significantly improved ( D). In comparison to the control NPs without cell membrane coating, treatment with NM-LaCD NP via i. v. Injection at 5 mg/kg more effectively reduced neutrophil infiltration and neutrophil-mediated inflammatory responses in the lung tissues of mice.
Membrane vesicles resemble naturally secreted extracellular vesicles, but with a reduced number of organelles and nucleic acids [ ]. Utilizing the presence of chemokine and cytokine receptors, a neutrophil membrane vesicle-based delivery system has exhibited superior targeting capabilities towards macrophages [ ]. This extracellular nanovesicle-based delivery system effectively neutralized a diverse array of cytokines, and it was also harnessed to deliver Dex, thus providing a new biomimetic nanotherapeutic approach for ARDS. Gao and his colleagues employed a nitrogen cavitation strategy to disrupt neutrophils and generate nanoscale membrane vesicles [ ]. By loading an anti-inflammatory drug andrographolide into the neutrophil-derived membrane vesicles through a pH gradient, the obtained nanotherapy was able to significantly mitigate acute lung inflammation/injury and sepsis triggered by LPS in mice.
Activation of β2-integrin conformations can enhance the targeted adhesion of neutrophils, and the use of lung-specific lipids in combination with β2-integrins represents a useful strategy to improve pulmonary targeting capabilities [ ]. Zhang et al. first developed the biomimetic nanovesicle (ANeu-DDAB) by incorporating lung-targeting functional lipids, specifically a cationic lipid DDAB, together with neutrophil membranes enriched with activated β2 integrins. Subsequently, a therapeutic lipid docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) was loaded into ANeu-DDAB using the thin film hydration method, resulting in a biomimetic nanotherapy DHA@ANeu-DDAB [ ]. After i. v. administration, it initially targeted the lung tissue, followed by secondary targeting of inflammatory vascular endothelial cells. Additionally, the β2 integrins present in DHA@ANeu-DDAB could bind to the ICAM-1/2 ligands expressed on the inflamed vascular endothelium, thereby inhibiting the infiltration of neutrophils into the bloodstream during inflammation. DHA@ANeu-DDAB effectively modulated the macrophage phenotype in inflamed lungs and enhanced their anti-inflammatory activity by increasing the biosynthesis of specific pro-resolving mediators. In a mouse model of ARDS induced by LPS, DHA@ANeu-DDAB demonstrated remarkable capability to markedly suppress pulmonary inflammation and promote the repair of injured lungs.

4.2.3.

## Platelet membrane-derived nanotherapies
Platelets, serving as multifunctional effectors in inflammatory responses, are intricately intertwined with the onset and progression of diverse inflammatory diseases [ ]. The platelet membrane (PM) surface exhibits a robust affinity to immune regulatory and adhesion-related antigens, thus precisely targeting focal sites of inflammatory diseases. Platelets display a circulatory half-life of approximately 30 h, which is partly attributed to their surface expression of CD47, a “self-marker” that interacts with signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα) on immune cells, thereby inhibiting immune clearance [ ]. This prolonged circulation ensures the ability of PM-coated NPs to achieve passive targeted delivery of different drugs with high efficiency. Furthermore, platelets express a unique array of surface receptors that dynamically adhere to damaged vascular systems and pathogenic microorganisms [ , ]. PM-coated NPs inherit these dynamic binding properties from platelets, which are harnessed for targeted drug delivery. This distinctive attribute allows PM-coated NPs to actively pinpoint lesions and significantly enhance therapeutic efficacy of drugs administered for treating inflammatory conditions [ ]. Likewise, PM vesicles were extracted and coated onto the surface of tea polyphenol (TP)-loaded PLGA NPs (TP-NPs), which were prepared using the emulsification and evaporation method [ ]. This coating process resulted in a biomimetic nanotherapy PM@TP-NPs. Following i. v. delivery, PM@TP-NPs effectively suppressed lung vascular injury, as manifested by reduced lung vascular permeability, alleviated proinflammatory cytokine burden, decreased infiltration of inflammatory cells in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and lung tissue, inhibited secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, and blocked NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

4.2.4.

## Red blood cell membrane-coated nanotherapies
Also, RBCs have garnered extensive studies in drug delivery systems, owing to their remarkable properties such as biocompatibility, biodegradability, and ultralong circulation half-life [ ]. Consequently, the utilization of RBC membrane camouflaging transforms NPs into a platform that owns the advantages of native RBC membrane with the unique characteristics of nanovehicles [ ]. For RBC membrane-coated NPs (RCNPs), the RBC membrane serves as an exterior coating for synthetic NPs laden with therapeutic agents. As compared to bare NPs, the presence of “self-markers” on the RBC membrane imparts lower immunogenicity and prolonged blood circulation time to the engineered nanotherapies [ ]. The RBC membrane coating extends the half-life of NPs in systemic circulation, circumventing the drawbacks associated with PEGylation, which can diminish receptor-mediated cellular uptake of NPs and hinder endosomal escape efficiency of payloads, thereby attenuating adverse effects delivery efficiency [ ]. Furthermore, RBC membrane encapsulation serves as an effective strategy to prevent drug leakage from porous NPs and mitigate agglomeration of bare NPs. On the other hand, the synthetic NPs employed as therapeutic vehicles can endow RBCs with additional properties such as stimuli-sensitivity, controlled drug release performance, and efficient intracellular delivery capacities. These features can be leveraged to direct the particles towards targeted sites for on-demand release of their loaded therapeutic cargoes, facilitating the development of highly specific nanotherapies for acute pulmonary inflammation.
Zhang et al. first invented human red blood cell nanosponges (hRBC-NS), created by enclosing PLGA cores with natural cell membranes derived from human RBCs [ ]. As these NPs retain the identical repertoire of cell membrane receptors, they present a multifaceted toxin decoy strategy with a wide-ranging ability to sequester and neutralize various pore-forming toxins, endotoxins, and proinflammatory cytokines, irrespective of their molecular structure and origin. The inner polymeric core plays a crucial role in stabilizing the RBC membrane, allowing for a prolonged half-life in the bloodstream and maximizing toxin absorption. Based on this promising result, the same research group found that hRBC-NS can attenuate β-H/C-mediated hemolysis, lung epithelial cell death, macrophage apoptosis, suppression of neutrophil bactericidal properties, and inflammasome activity [ ].

4.2.5.

## Other cell membrane-coated nanotherapies
Besides the above mentioned cell membranes, other cell membranes have also been investigated to construct biomimetic nanotherapies for acute pulmonary inflammation. Hu et al. successfully encapsulated Cur within polymeric NPs that were further coated with a layer of lung epithelial cell membrane containing an Nrf2 activator, leading to the preparation of PC@MBs. PC@MBs exhibited preferential accumulation in the lung due to their autologous cell membrane coating, enabling them to effectively repair pneumonic damage by restoring redox homeostasis through the activation of Nrf2 and HO-1 [ ]. Dex-conjugated polyethylenimine (PEI), an efficient vector, can effectively deliver plasmid DNA (pDNA) to the lungs [ , ]. By coating Dex-PEI/pDNA NPs with cell membrane derived from LA-4 lung epithelial cells, the obtained nanotherapy demonstrated significantly enhanced transfection efficiency of pDNA in the pulmonary tissue [ ]. In addition, cell membrane coating of hybrid NPs prolonged the circulation time, enhanced their interactions with the plasma membranes of target cells, and promoted the intracellular intake of pDNA, thus potentiating the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. Luo et al. exploited the affinity and adhesiveness between activated endothelial cells and innate immune cells during inflammatory responses to engineer hybrid polyester NPs coated with endothelial cell membranes (EM-Ps) containing adhesion molecules [ ]. In vivo studies in lung inflammation mouse models indicated that EM-Ps can preferentially interact with neutrophils and monocytes in circulation. The presence of cellular membrane-based biosurface enhanced the transportation of NPs to inflamed lungs, which was potentially facilitated by the motility of neutrophils. By employing a transgenic zebrafish model, the leukocyte-mediated transportation and biodistribution of EM-Ps were visualized in real time at the whole-organism level.
Stem cells have sparked interest in the development of targeted nanodevices for tissue repair and inflammation regulation, due to their superior tissue-specific homing capability. This capability can be further bolstered by a distinctive surface array of stem cell membrane-associated biomolecules, which enables the derived nanovehicles to evade the immune system and precisely navigate to sites of inflammation [ ]. The findings by Huang et al. have convincingly shown that biomimetic NPs, cloaked with MSC membranes and loaded with naringin, exhibit remarkable targeting capabilities towards lung inflammation [ ]. After intratracheal instillation, the released payload naringin effectively inhibited the production of inflammatory cytokines and ROS, indicating the potential of nanotherapies derived from MSC membrane-functionalized NPs in treating ALI.
It is worth noting that gene-editing techniques can effectively achieve “cell editing” or “cell renovation”, thereby enhancing biological performances of the modified cells at the target site, and surpassing the capabilities of their original, unedited counterparts [ ]. In a study by Zhang et al., wild-type C1498 mouse leukemia cells were genetically engineered to express VLA-4, a heterodimer composed of integrin α4 and β1. Subsequently, the isolated cell membranes were harvested from these genetically modified C1498 cells and coated onto Dex-loaded NP cores (Dex-NP). The resulting VLA-4-expressing cell membrane-cloaked NPs can specifically target VCAM-1 on inflamed lung endothelial cells, thus enhancing site-specific drug delivery [ ].

4.2.6.

## Limitations of cell membrane-based biomimetic nanotherapies
The current extraction efficiency of cell membranes is relatively low, often necessitating the cultivation of a large number of cells to obtain only a limited yield of cell membranes. This may lead to high cost for the engineered biomimetic nanotherapies. In addition, current cell membrane isolation techniques, such as mechanical co-extrusion and sonication, present significant challenges for scalable industrial production due to limitations in reproducibility and processing capacity. Therefore, further improvements and optimizations of the techniques for cell membrane isolation and purification are urgently needed. Moreover, comprehensive studies have yet to be conducted to examine the effects of the sources of membranes or membrane components, the long-term stability and safety of membrane-decorated nanotherapies, as well as their potential side effects or off-target effects. For different types or batches of cell membranes, there is currently a lack of uniform evaluation criteria for functional tests prior to production, which significantly limits their translational potential for clinical applications.

4.3.

## EVs-based biomimetic nanotherapies
EVs are transport vesicles secreted and released by living cells into the extracellular environment [ , ]. They are composed of lipid bilayers and contain nucleic acids, proteins, and cell organelles [ ]. EVs can activate and regulate the functions and phenotypes of target cells by delivering biological signals or components [ ]. In this way, EVs may serve as mediators of intercellular communications ( ). As natural nanocarriers, EVs can harness their endogenous transport properties to deliver various bioactive substances for therapeutic applications ( C) [ ]. Currently, there exist two primary approaches for EVs to deliver therapeutics. One involves the endogenous loading mechanism, wherein the source cells are co-cultured with therapeutic drugs to induce the secretion of drug containing EVs [ ]. The other approach employs the exogenous loading mechanism, which entails directly combining drugs with EVs [ ]. Hydrophobic drugs are combined with EVs by co-incubation, while drug-loaded liposomes are prepared through co-incubation, coextrusion, or freeze-thaw hybridization with EVs. Additionally, hydrophilic drugs can be loaded into EVs via electroporation [ ]. Further research and development in this field may lead to innovative and targeted therapeutic strategies for managing acute pulmonary inflammation and related pulmonary complications.
 4.3.1.

## MSC-derived EVs
Whereas MSCs have been the most extensively investigated stem cells for treating various diseases, their clinical applications face several significant challenges, including the high cost associated with maintenance and storage, the complexity of their long-term homing capability, and the transient nature of their survival after transplantation [ ]. Furthermore, the differentiation potential of MSCs may become compromised, particularly following extended cryopreservation and subsequent thawing for reuse. Serious safety concerns also persist regarding risks of malignant transformation and vascular occlusion associated with MSC administration. Consequently, the MSC secretome, rich in cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and EVs, has emerged as a promising alternative, accounting for the majority of therapeutic efficacy of MSCs [ , ]. MSC-derived EVs (MSC-EVs) have received considerable attention as alternative therapeutic agents for ALI [ ]. With their anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties, MSC-EVs can effectively suppress cytokine storms and mitigate tissue damage [ ]. MSC-EVs exert therapeutic effects in ARDS by anti-inflammatory mechanisms, repair of epithelial and endothelial cell injuries, alveolar fluid clearance, and antimicrobial properties ( ) [ , ]. The promising clinical application prospects of these EVs in ARDS can be attributed to their low tumorigenicity, simple storage requirements, off-the-shelf availability, safety, and the ability to down-regulate cytokine storms [ ].
 Therapeutic activities of MSC-EVs are mainly mediated via the horizontal transfer of their cargo components, which then regulate and modulate the behaviors of recipient cells by an array of mechanisms. Extensive studies have implicated epigenetic regulatory small RNAs, particularly miRNAs (miRs), as crucial regulators and mediators responsible for therapeutic effects of MSC-EVs [ ]. Chen et al. demonstrated that MSC-EVs alleviated ischemia-reperfusion injury in the lung in an exosomal miR-21-5p-dependent manner. MSC-EVs ameliorated IRI in vivo and hypoxia/reoxygenation-induced apoptosis in vitro by suppressing both the intrinsic and extrinsic apoptosis pathways through miR-21-5p targeting phosphatase and tensin homolog deleted on chromosome ten and programmed cell death 4 [ ]. Furthermore, MSC-EVs demonstrate a robust capacity for macrophage reprogramming under inflammatory conditions. This effect is primarily attributed to miR-181a in EVs [ ]. By targeting and downregulating phosphatase and tensin homolog expression, miR-181a activates the suppressor of cytokine signaling proteins, thereby markedly reducing the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-8. The ability to transfer mRNA and miRNA not only allows EVs to reshape the transcriptional profiles of target cells but also enables the production of functional proteins. Additionally, they can precisely target specific pathways in the cells by delivering proteins. Moreover, their potential to modify the epigenetic environment in cells offers the intriguing possibility of regulating certain genes. Beyond miRNAs, MSC-EV-mediated mitochondrial transfer represents one of the most promising therapeutic strategies. Shi et al. have demonstrated that adipose MSC-EV-mediated mitochondrial transfer restores cellular oxidative phosphorylation and metabolic homeostasis in airway macrophages, markedly reducing pulmonary inflammation [ , ]. Other mechanisms encompass inhibition of NF-κB and Hedgehog signaling pathways, suppression of macrophage glycolysis, and attenuation of pyroptosis in alveolar macrophages [ , , ].
Whereas drug-loading capabilities of native MSC-derived EVs are not satisfactory, their therapeutic activity can be enhanced by alternative approaches [ ]. Preconditioning stimulation of MSCs can influence the efficacy of MSC-EVs [ ]. Exposure to proinflammatory agents, pharmacological agents, and hypoxia has been employed to prime MSCs, resulting in enhanced MSC survival and augmented production of anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective mediators [ , , ]. Aricha et al. isolated EVs from MSCs induced to secrete increased levels of neurotrophic and immunomodulatory factors, termed as Exo MSC-NTF. Compared to EVs derived from untreated MSCs (Exo MSC), Exo MSC-NTF was superior in reducing LPS-induced ARDS markers, including physiological lung damage such as alveolar wall thickness, fibrin production, and neutrophil accumulation, as well as increasing oxygenation levels. Furthermore, Exo MSC-NTF reversed the imbalance in the host immune response, as implicated by decreased levels of IFN-γ, IL-6, TNF-α, and RANTES (a chemokine capable of regulating T cell migration and function during acute and chronic infections) in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid [ ]. A study by Laffey et al. showed that EVs derived from IFN-γ-primed human umbilical cord MSCs were more effective in attenuating E. coli-induced lung injury than vesicles from untreated stem cells. This superior protection may be mediated by enhanced macrophage phagocytosis and killing of E. coli [ ]. Additionally, hypoxia-preconditioned olfactory mucosa MSC-derived EVs transfer miR-612 to stimulate angiogenesis via TP53 targeting, thereby improving ARDS outcomes [ ].
In addition, MSCs can be genetically engineered to overexpress different genes, such as angiopoietin 1, HO-1, SOD, IL-10, IL-1RL1, ACE2, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), keratinocyte growth factors (KGF), and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) [ , , , , , , , ]. This approach has also been used to engineer and enrich EVs with specific properties [ ]. Genetic modification of MSCs can greatly impact miR compositions in EVs [ ]. Xu et al. indicated that EVs derived from genetically modified MSCs with GATA binding protein 4 (GATA-4) overexpression had increased pro-angiogenic capacity due to the delivery of let-7 miRs that target thrombospondin 1 in endothelial cells [ ]. Vieira and colleagues modified MSCs to overexpress miR-135b or miR-210 using a lentiviral vector and found that EVs enriched with miR-210 demonstrated an improved angiogenesis potential, as indicated by tube formation assays, as well as increased angiogenesis-related proteins (e.g., activin A and endothelin-1) in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), compared to control EVs [ ]. Other stem cell-derived EVs also showed desirable therapeutic benefits in the treatment of ALI/ARDS, including those derived from endothelial progenitor cells, pluripotent stem cells, and endometrial stem cells [ , ].

4.3.2.

## Platelet-derived EVs
Among the diverse spectrum of EVs, platelet-derived EVs (PEVs) constitute one of the most abundant classes, accounting for over 50 % of all EVs present in the bloodstream, mainly originating from platelets and megakaryocytes [ ]. PEVs are released by activated platelets and are encapsulated within platelet-derived membranes that contain CD41, P-selectin, and other platelet-specific proteins. The presence of these distinctive proteins determines immunomodulatory functions of PEVs [ ]. Moreover, they facilitate intricate interactions with a multitude of cellular entities, encompassing both immune and non-immune cells. It is noteworthy that PEVs are capable of both passive and active targeting of injured vasculature and activated endothelial cells, thereby assuming a significant role in the treatment of acute pulmonary inflammation [ ].
A significant quantity of PEVs can be easily generated by activating platelets in vitro [ ]. Wang et al. demonstrated that PEVs have high accumulation efficiency at sites of pneumonia. Compared to platelets, PEVs release a significantly reduced amount of inflammatory factors in inflammatory environments. This characteristic renders them ideal candidate carriers for anti-inflammatory drugs [ ]. Accordingly, PEVs were isolated from thrombin-activated platelets by ultracentrifugation based on a standard protocol ( A) [ ]. In contrast to platelets, PEVs exhibited negligible release of cytokines upon thrombin treatment, indicating that they are less likely to exacerbate the inflammatory response upon delivery to the inflamed site. Moreover, PEVs demonstrated a remarkable ability to adhere to activated/inflamed vascular walls via a diverse array of receptor patterns, enabling their high accumulation in injured lung tissues. Following loading with TPCA-1, which possesses anti-inflammatory properties, PEVs effectively suppressed the production of inflammatory factors, such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, thereby significantly enhancing therapeutic benefits in ALI ( B). This was achieved by inhibiting the infiltration of inflammatory cells and mitigating local cytokine storms in pulmonary tissues. Conversely, treatment with free TPCA-1 only modestly reduced cytokine levels, while PEVs alone did not reduce immune cell infiltration or cytokine levels. Furthermore, the same group has demonstrated that PEVs-based delivery can remarkably reduce the side effects and enhance the therapeutic efficacy of DEX in the treatment of acute pneumonia [ ].
 
4.3.3.

## Neutrophil-derived EVs
The excessive activation of neutrophils has been identified as a contributing factor in the pathogenesis of acute pulmonary inflammation. This process involves the release of NETs as well as neutrophil-mediated oxidative stress and degranulation [ ]. Furthermore, neutrophils are highly dynamic cells that can be recruited to sites of inflammation, where they interact intricately with both immune and non-immune compartments [ ]. Neutrophil-derived EVs (NDEVs) are subsequently engulfed by macrophages. These NDEVs function as pivotal mediators of neutrophil-macrophage crosstalk and itaconate metabolism in macrophages, thereby mitigating excessive inflammation [ ]. NDEVs can be categorized into two subtypes according to their generation mechanism: neutrophil-derived microvesicles (NDMVs) and neutrophil-derived trails (NDTRs) [ ]. NDMVs are produced by neutrophils following their arrival at inflammatory sites [ ]. NDMVs exert anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing the expression of inflammatory genes in natural killer cells [ ], monocyte-derived dendritic cells [ ], macrophages [ ], and endothelial cells [ , ]. NDTRs are a subtype of neutrophil-derived EVs that are produced during the migration of neutrophils from blood vessels to inflammatory tissues [ ]. NDTRs are proinflammatory extracellular vesicles. NDTRs generally contain proinflammatory miRNAs and can induce proinflammatory phenotype polarization of macrophages. Their primary function is to navigate immune cells towards areas of inflammation, thereby modulating the immune response at the site of injury or infection [ ]. Study by Nagaoka et al. demonstrated that LL-37, a human cathelicidin antimicrobial peptide, can stimulate mouse bone-marrow neutrophils to release ectosomes ex vivo. These LL-37-induced ectosomes exhibited antibacterial activity, by effectively reducing the bacterial load and safeguarding mice from fatal septic conditions [ ]. Furthermore, the miRNA cargo of neutrophil-derived EVs, particularly miR-223, critically regulates immune cell polarization, proliferation, and differentiation during pulmonary inflammation. This mechanistic insight highlights the dual regulatory capacity of neutrophil-derived vesicles in balancing pro- and anti-inflammatory responses, suggesting novel therapeutic strategies for inflammatory lung pathologies [ ].

4.3.4.

## Epithelial cell-derived EVs
Epithelial cells primarily function as the initial protective barrier, transducing mechanical signals into pathological ones when the organism encounters external environmental stimuli [ ]. In response to damage, these cells produce numerous growth factors, subsequently releasing cytokines and chemokines into the ECM [ ]. Notably, EVs secreted by epithelial cells play a crucial role in facilitating intercellular communications with a spectrum of target cells [ ]. Due to the abundance of bioactive molecules, epithelium-derived EVs present a promising avenue for the diagnosis and treatment of acute pulmonary inflammation.
Wang et al. showed that AEC II cell-derived EVs containing miR-371b-5p may serve as niche signaling to augment survival and proliferation of AEC II cells, as well as promote re-epithelialization of injured alveoli, thus providing promising candidate therapies for currently incurable lung diseases [ ]. Study by Xia et al. indicated that EVs derived from AEC II cells, which contain miR-27b-3p can specifically target and regulate the expression of regulator of G protein signaling-1 (RGS1) in recipient cells [ ]. RGS1 is able to modulate the immune phenotype of AM subsets [ ], by PLC-IP3R signaling-dependent intracellular Ca2+ responses, which affects both proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory activities, as well as profibrotic and antifibrotic phenotypes. The depletion of EVs from AEC II cells resulted in the immune imbalance among AMs. Notably, treatment with AEC II cell-derived EVs significantly ameliorated LPS-induced ARDS and bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in mouse models. The A549 cell line, originating from the human lung adenocarcinoma tissue, has been extensively employed for in vitro assays, due to its capability of producing surfactant lipids and multilamellar body biogenesis, akin to the surfactant secretion function of AEC II cells [ , ]. Huang et al. successfully extracted ACE2-overexpressing microparticles derived from A549 cells, defined as AO-MPs, which can be potentially used as a therapeutic agent against SARS-CoV-2 infection [ ]. These AO-MPs were able to adsorb SARS-CoV-2 viral particles in the alveoli and facilitate their delivery to AMs, thereby enabling viral clearance mediated by macrophages. Other studies demonstrated that the concurrent administration of amnion epithelium-derived EVs at a dosage of 25 μg per mouse, along with an anti-fibrotic agent serelaxin at 0.5 mg/kg/day, for a period of 7 days, provided more comprehensive protective effects against airway and lung inflammation, remodeling, fibrosis, and associated dysfunction, compared to either treatment modality administered alone [ ]. This combination therapy was also more effective than serelaxin plus 1 × 106 epithelial cells or a positive control drug pirfenidone. Of note, serelaxin significantly enhanced the ability of EVs to inhibit basement membrane fibrosis. These findings provide promising new prospects for the treatment of acute pulmonary inflammation.

4.3.5.

## Endothelial cell-derived EVs
Endothelial cells can produce a large number of vesicles, facilitating the exchange of macromolecules between the blood and parenchymal cells. This feature, along with their polarized secretory machinery, promotes the regulated transport of hormones, growth factors, nutrients, and pathogens. Consequently, endothelial cells emerge as the second-largest contributor to the particle secretory pool in circulation, following platelets [ ]. Endothelial cell-derived EVs are a heterogeneous group of vesicles produced by activated endothelial cells through the process of shedding, which include apoptotic bodies, exosomes, and microvesicles [ ]. Notably, endothelial cell-derived exosomes are abundant in miRNAs, which not only suppress the expression of genes associated with acute inflammation but also exert regulatory effects on the functionality of various immune cells, showing important roles in maintaining immune homeostasis [ , , ].
miR-126, highly expressed in endothelial cell-derived EVs, can regulate vascular integrity and angiogenesis [ , , ]. Specifically, miR-126-3p preserves endothelial barrier function by targeting PIK3R2 and Spred-1, thereby stabilizing vascular endothelial-cadherin junctions [ , ]. Meanwhile, miR-126-5p attenuates inflammatory signaling triggered by high mobility group box-1 and VEGFα, reducing endothelial barrier disruption and vascular hyperpermeability [ , ]. Conversely, inhibition of miR-126 may enhance vascular permeability [ ]. Chichger et al. further demonstrated that the endosomal protein p18 promotes release of protective EVs from endothelial cells, which mitigate LPS-induced pulmonary endothelial leakage. Notably, p18 regulated miRNA processing via a mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)-dependent pathway, enabling modulation of the miRNA composition in these EVs. This specifically affected let-7i-5p, miR-96-5p, and miR-137-3p [ ].

4.3.6.

## Limitations of EVs-based biomimetic nanotherapies
Depending on their origin and cargo, EVs can either serve as valuable therapeutic agents or contribute to the progression of diseases. In particular, EVs are regarded as markers and mediators of acute pulmonary inflammation. Therefore, the purification and isolation of EVs are paramount. However, high-yield purification of EVs without compromising their integrity has long been a challenge in this field. Moreover, there is a paucity of knowledge regarding the biosynthesis, cellular origin, composition of EVs, and their influence on drug pharmacokinetics. This limited understanding might cause inefficiencies and potential safety issues in their clinical use. Additionally, the low production yield of EVs and their high cost resulting from the complicated preparation procedures and bioreactor usage further constrain their widespread clinical applications.

5.

## Challenges and prospects
Significant progress has been made in the development of advanced nanotherapies for the treatment of acute pulmonary inflammation. In particular, a large number of materials and delivery systems responsive to different inflammatory microenvironments have been synthesized and evaluated in various cellular and animal models. By triggering the release of therapeutic molecules from nanotherapies in response to pathologically relevant biochemical signals, such as low pH, elevated ROS, and enzyme overexpression, their targeting and therapeutic effects can be significantly enhanced. For biomimetic nanotherapies based on endogenous materials, they not only possess good biocompatibility and high safety, but also inherit specific biological functions from their source cells, facilitating targeted drug delivery and improved therapeutic outcomes. Despite considerable advances in preclinical studies on bioresponsive and biomimetic nanotherapies, it must be recognized that many challenges need to be addressed for these innovative therapies to translate from the laboratory to clinical applications.
5.1.

## Signaling selection strategies
Our understanding of multiple key cellular and molecular mediators in the pathogenesis of acute pulmonary inflammation remains limited. Selection of the optimal biochemical signals for bioresponsive nanotherapies is highly challenging. Also, we lack sophisticated and efficient strategies in the field of biomimetic therapeutic delivery. Different stages of acute pulmonary inflammation may be accompanied by varying degrees of acidosis, significant changes in the production and types of ROS, as well as differences in enzyme expression levels and enzyme spectra. Complicating the matter further, some normal tissues and subcellular organelles such as the stomach and endolysosomes, physiologically exhibit acidic environments. In other non-target organs, such as the heart, kidneys, and liver, which undergo functional impairment due to acute pulmonary inflammation, there are also abnormal elevations in enzyme expression and ROS levels. These factors can lead to nonspecific drug release from responsive nanotherapies, thereby affecting in vivo drug efficacy and potentially causing unexpected side effects [ ]. In addition, the composition of biomimetic particles is complex and diverse, yet our current understanding of the functions of cells, cell membranes, and EVs remains relatively limited. How various biomimetic particles change and function in the pathogenesis of acute pulmonary inflammation is still a largely unresolved mystery. Therefore, it is of paramount importance to delve deeper into the pathogenesis of acute pulmonary inflammation, understand the relationships between specific membrane molecules and the pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic behaviors of biomimetic therapies, explore novel functional molecules, and investigate the changes and mechanisms of action of various bioresponsive/biomimetic NPs in acute pulmonary inflammation.
To address these challenges, we propose hierarchical signaling selection strategies that include: 1) prioritizing disease-specific microenvironmental cues and pathological changes; 2) employing a multiplexed sensing system capable of distinguishing inflammatory acidosis (alveolar pH 6.2–6.8) from physiological acidic compartments (e.g., stomach pH < 3, lysosome pH 4.5–5.0); and 3) establishing dynamic thresholds for activation triggers that account for temporal variations in the inflammatory microenvironment. Regarding nanocarrier design optimization, we advocate the comprehensive integration of bioresponsive and biomimetic delivery systems, the incorporation of responsive architectures, and the inclusion of spatiotemporal control modules aligned with disease progression to enhance specificity. Additionally, we recommend conducting rigorous structure-function analyses to understand the compositional complexity of biomimetic nanocarriers. Future research should focus on: investigating spectral signature changes in the inflammatory microenvironment across different inflammation stages; mapping nanocarrier protein corona formation under inflammatory conditions; and elucidating the pharmacokinetic interactions between nanocarrier surface ligands and target tissues or cells.

5.2.

## Balance and synergy of multi-target strategies
Effective nanotherapies must uniformly coordinate multi-target interventions while maintaining optimal targeting efficiency. Acute pulmonary inflammation often progresses to acute systemic inflammation, culminating in multi-organ failure, underscoring the limitations of lung-specific targeting. To address this, delivery systems must be optimized to balance therapeutic effects across diverse target tissues, cell types, and signaling pathways. A critical approach involves multimodal anti-inflammatory mechanisms that minimize off-target effects. Advanced delivery platforms should employ combinatorial targeting to account for spatial and temporal heterogeneity of inflammation. This can be achieved through surface functionalization with rationally designed ligand arrays, where controlled valency and spacing enable affinity-based multi-target recognition. Such systems promote synergistic interactions among inflammatory mediators while reducing nonspecific binding.
Equally important is spatiotemporal control over drug release kinetics. Hierarchically stimulus-responsive structures are essential for sequential drug activation. Initially, rapid release of short-acting anti-inflammatory agents (e.g., corticosteroids) is needed to mitigate acute cytokine storms and stabilize critical symptoms. Subsequently, sustained release of immune modulators (e.g., JAK inhibitors) ensures long-term reprogramming of dysfunctional immune cells. Finally, adaptive-phase pulsatile delivery, guided by feedback mechanisms (e.g., pH/ROS sensing), dynamically adjusts systemic anti-inflammatory dosing based on real-time biomarker fluctuations. This three-tiered approach confines high-dose therapy to inflamed tissues, minimizing systemic immunosuppression while continuously modulating both localized and disseminated inflammatory pathways.
Furthermore, synergistic multi-organ biodistribution is vital. Balancing organ accumulation requires strategic coordination of passive and active targeting. For instance, PEG surface modifications can fine-tune renal clearance and hepatic uptake, while particle size optimization alters biodistribution patterns. By evaluating organ-specific roles in the inflammatory cascade, nanotherapies can be engineered for coordinated efficacy across affected tissues, thereby preventing progression to multi-organ failure. By integrating these design principles, ideal nanotherapies can synergistically modulate pro-inflammatory signaling, immune-cell trafficking, endothelial barrier integrity, and coagulation pathways to enhance therapeutic efficacy.

5.3.

## Preclinical experimental models
Currently available animal models cannot fully replicate the pathological and clinical characteristics of patients with acute pulmonary inflammation. Most studies utilize murine models, yet the results obtained cannot be directly extrapolated to humans. Despite numerous positive preclinical findings in animal models over the past fifty years, none have been validated in clinical trials or translated into clinical practice [ ]. Therefore, it is crucial to select and optimize experimental models that can more accurately predict actual therapeutic effects. To address the translational gap in acute pulmonary inflammation research, we propose three complementary evaluation models to enhance the predictive validity of therapeutic interventions.
First, humanized murine models, established by transplanting human hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) into immunodeficient strains, serve as indispensable platforms for investigating human immune system development and function in vivo [ , ]. Notably, Flavell et al. developed a COVID-19 humanized mouse model through lung-targeted delivery of human ACE2 via gene therapy [ ]. This approach enabled SARS-CoV-2 infection in mice engrafted with human HSPCs. These models recapitulate key architectural features of human innate and adaptive immune systems, providing unique advantages for mechanistic studies of acute pneumonitis pathogenesis and relevant drug screening.
Second, microphysiological organ-on-chip (OOC) platforms accurately recapitulate organ-specific cellular microenvironments and tissue-level functionalities, enabling comparative analysis with clinical data [ ]. Advanced lung-on-chip models facilitate complex multicellular in vitro investigations with measurable phenotypic endpoints [ , ]. For instance, Guenat et al. developed an inflammation-on-chip system featuring real-time imaging of immune cell extravasation and migration during pulmonary inflammatory responses [ ]. This platform integrates pulmonary endothelial barriers, ECM microenvironments, and inflamed alveolar epithelial interfaces, using chemotactic gradients to drive immune cell migration. Such bioinspired engineering advances establish robust foundations for improving drug discovery pipelines by more faithfully reflecting human pulmonary pathophysiology.
Finally, multi-organ microphysiological platforms model systemic physiology through engineered tissue communication networks, providing powerful tools for multi-organ pathophysiological reconstruction [ ]. These systems typically integrate interconnected microfluidic circuits that simulate vascular networks, recapitulating inter-organ transport dynamics and crosstalk while maintaining physiological relevance [ ]. Key design principles include: 1) vascular perfusion linking pulmonary and extra-pulmonary compartments, 2) selective endothelial barriers isolating organ modules from systemic flow, and 3) hematogenous immune microenvironments incorporating peripheral immune cell populations.
Notably, large animal models with pulmonary physiology closely analogous to humans (e.g., porcine or non-human primate systems) remain indispensable for validating inhalational delivery routes and assessing immunotoxicity profiles. However, given ethical and cost constraints, a tiered testing strategy is recommended: initial screening using humanized murine models, followed by mechanistic dissection via OOC systems, culminating in late-stage validation through multi-organ microphysiological platforms and large animal models, augmented by integration of emerging technologies such as 3D-bioprinted pulmonary constructs.

5.4.

## Translation challenges and future directions
The clinical translation of nanotherapies presents complexities that surpass those of conventional pharmaceuticals. Quality assessment must extend beyond traditional standards, requiring comprehensive characterization, rigorous process control, and evaluation of stability during clinical use, storage, and transportation. Three interconnected challenges are pivotal to the clinical translation of nanotherapeutics, including immunotoxicity assessment, scalable manufacturing, and regulatory framework adaptation.
5.4.1.

## Immunotoxicity and biocompatibility: a shift from passive response to proactive design
Immunotoxicity and biocompatibility of NPs continue to pose significant challenges in nanotherapy development. NPs can interfere with cellular integrity by disrupting membrane structures and organelles, while also influencing signaling pathways and cell viability. NP-mediated cytogenetic toxicity manifests through multiple mechanisms: direct physical damage to cellular membranes and organelles; leaching of toxic components that interact with genetic materials; ROS-mediated oxidative stress leading to enzymatic and DNA damage; and surface charge-dependent formation of protein coronas that modify biodistribution patterns and provoke immune reactions. The degree of cytotoxicity is influenced by a complex interplay of physicochemical parameters including NP dimensions, morphology, structural characteristics, aggregation tendency, surface chemistry, administered dose, material composition, target cell type, and exposure time. To advance the clinical translation of nanotherapeutics, proactive consideration of immunotoxicity and biocompatibility must be integrated into the design phase. A promising approach involves multi-omics profiling that synergizes transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic analyses to identify characteristic biomarker signatures following NP exposure. Furthermore, the implementation of advanced preclinical models, such as 3D vascular-immune cell co-culture platforms, enables systematic safety assessment across acute, subchronic, and chronic exposure scenarios. This paradigm shift from reactive testing to predictive design represents a transformative advancement in nanomedicine development strategies.

5.4.2.

## Scalable manufacturing: bridging laboratory synthesis and industrial production
Scalable manufacturing is another key challenge in nanotherapy translation. The absence of standardized protocols in formulation processes presents significant obstacles for industrial-scale production. For bioresponsive nanotherapies in particular, advancements are required in several areas: optimization of formulation methodologies, precise characterization of material structural properties, development of customizable biophysicochemical attributes, and consistent reproducibility of manufacturing processes along with guaranteed product quality. During biomimetic nanocarrier modification, drug loading, and storage, it is essential to avoid inactivation of biomolecules, since this can compromise their intended targeting and immune evasion capabilities. Furthermore, the issues of yield, purity, and preservation of biomimetic nanotherapies (e.g., EVs) in clinical applications deserve comprehensive and in-depth studies. Currently, there are significant differences among different research teams in terms of EV separation and purification methods as well as recovery efficiency. These discrepancies directly impact the practical applications of EVs. Additionally, the need for large-scale cell culture to obtain sufficient cell membranes or EVs may significantly increase production costs of corresponding biomimetic nanotherapies. During the preparation process, it is crucial to strictly monitor various process parameters to ensure consistent product quality across batches. These parameters include formulation variables (like cell types, component ratios, and solvents used), manufacturing processes (e.g., time, speed, flow conditions, and power), and environmental conditions (such as temperature, pH, and pressure). To address the limitations of current preparation technologies, such as time-consuming procedures, high energy consumption, and elevated costs, streamlining production processes and improving efficiency are essential. Modular nanocarrier designs, like “plug-and-play” ligand exchange systems, can expedite clinical translation by rapidly adapting new targets without modifying core manufacturing protocols. Collaborative efforts like the National Cancer Institute's Nanocarrier Alliance further facilitate progress by promoting shared access to good manufacturing practice (GMP) facilities and preclinical toxicology data, thereby minimizing redundant development efforts.

5.4.3.

## Regulatory science and standardization: building a global collaborative framework
The lack of standardized GMP protocols poses a notable barrier to industrial-scale translation of nanotherapies, compounded by the currently fragmented regulatory landscape for multifunctional nanosystems. Nevertheless, coordinated global initiatives are making progress in addressing these issues. Notably, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued a guidance for industry titled “Drug Products, Including Biological Products, that Contain Nanomaterials” in April 2022. This guidance delineates appropriate regulatory pathways for the approval of drug products containing nanomaterials. Furthermore, it elucidates the principles for identifying and characterizing the critical quality attributes (CQAs) of these nanomaterials. Similarly, the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles has proposed minimal information standards for EV clinical trials [ ], recognizing EVs' unique position between conventional drugs and biologics. In China, the National Medical Products Administration's Center for Drug Evaluation issued guidance in August 2021 concerning the quality and nonclinical research of nanomedicine. These global endeavors, alongside landmark cases like the FDA-approved amikacin liposome inhalation suspension (Arikayce®) [ ] and the phase III clinical trial of MSC-derived EVs (DirectBiologics) for ARDS treatment, demonstrate the potential to overcome these challenges. Strategic integration of regulatory considerations and manufacturing innovations into early-stage research will be crucial for transforming laboratory breakthroughs into clinically viable products, ultimately paving the way for a harmonized global regulatory framework that accelerates and strengthens nanomedicine translation.
In summary, through sustained optimization and innovative approaches, advanced nanotherapies are poised to achieve enhanced efficacy and safety in treating acute lung inflammation. Future advancements in bioresponsive and biomimetic nanotherapeutic platforms are anticipated to significantly improve treatment outcomes, facilitating the development of personalized and precision-targeted interventions in acute pulmonary inflammation management.
