## Abstract
Colorectal cancer progression involves dysregulated signaling pathways such as Hippo-YAP, but upstream regulators remain poorly defined. Here we demonstrate that oxysterol-binding protein-like 3 (OSBPL3) modulates Hippo-YAP signaling to drive tumor aggressiveness. Analysis of clinical specimens and experimental models shows that elevated OSBPL3 levels in colorectal cancer tissues correlate with shortened patient survival. Depleting OSBPL3 impairs cancer cell proliferation and invasion and induces cell cycle arrest, while its overexpression accelerates tumor growth. Mechanistic studies reveal that OSBPL3 binds 14-3-3 proteins to promote YAP1 nuclear translocation, activating downstream oncogenic pathways. Notably, tumors with high OSBPL3 expression exhibit resistance to MEK inhibitors, but this resistance is overcome by YAP1 suppression or combined YAP/MEK inhibition in patient-derived organoids. These results establish OSBPL3 as a critical Hippo-YAP pathway regulator and propose targeting OSBPL3-mediated signaling as a therapeutic strategy for colorectal cancers with Hippo pathway alterations.
Subject terms: Gastrointestinal cancer, Cell signalling

## Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a prevalent malignancy of the digestive tract. As of 2023, statistics indicate that CRC is the second most common cause of cancer-related deaths in the United States, with approximately 153,020 individuals diagnosed and 52,550 succumbing to the disease annually . The Yes1-Associated Protein (YAP1), a critical effector of the Hippo pathway, plays a significant role in various cancers, including CRC. Its translocation into the nucleus and subsequent association with the TEAD family promote transcription, thereby enhancing its oncogenic potential. Prior studies demonstrate that both overexpression and knockdown of YAP1 can impact CRC proliferation, invasion, and metastasis phenotypes – . In CRC, YAP1 hyperactivation promotes cancer stemness, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and chemoresistance. Beyond CRC, YAP1 is widely regarded as an oncogenic factor in many other tumor types , . However, the upstream mechanisms driving YAP1 dysregulation in CRC are incompletely understood, particularly those involving lipid-metabolism-related proteins.
Oxysterol-binding protein-like 3 (OSBPL3), a member of the oxysterol-binding protein family, has recently gained attention for its dual role in lipid homeostasis and cancer progression. Previous research identifies OSBPL3 as an intracellular lipid receptor involved in the regulation of cell adhesion and organization of the actin cytoskeleton . Studies indicate a trend of overexpression of OSBPL3 in CRC and establish it as an independent prognostic risk factor through immunohistochemistry . Similar findings in liver, lung, and pancreatic cancers suggest a pro-oncogenic role for OSBPL3 – . Another study in gastric cancer explores the molecular biology of OSBPL3, revealing that its knockdown leads to reduced proliferation and impacts the cell cycle in gastric cancer . While OSBPL3 overexpression has been associated with poor prognosis in other cancers, its functional significance in CRC remains unexplored. The specific function and role of OSBPL3 in CRC remain elusive, yet our study uncovers its regulatory effect on the expression and subcellular localization of YAP1 in CRC.
MEK inhibitors, targeting the MAPK (Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase) pathway, play a crucial role in numerous biological processes, including cell growth, proliferation, and differentiation , . Trametinib (TRA), a commonly used MEK inhibitor targeting MEK1 and MEK2, serves as a primary treatment modality in melanoma . In CRC, TRA functions as a second-line treatment for certain patients with the BRAF V600E mutation, achieving notable efficacy . Research indicates that the key Hippo pathway effector, YAP1, is a pivotal determinant of TRA resistance and sensitivity. Overexpression of YAP1 induces TRA resistance, an effect observable in both BRAF-mutant and wild-type cells. However, the alteration in TRA drug sensitivity is more pronounced in mutant CRC upon modulation of YAP1 expression . Our research delves into the function of OSBPL3 in CRC, uncovering its impact on TRA drug sensitivity. The TRA sensitivity reduction caused by OSBPL3 can be reversed by YAP1 inhibitors.
This study systematically investigates the clinical significance, biological functions, and molecular mechanisms of OSBPL3 in CRC progression. Using multi-omics approaches combined with functional genomics and pharmacological interventions, we demonstrate that OSBPL3 drives tumor aggressiveness through 14-3-3-dependent regulation of YAP1 nuclear translocation. Furthermore, we reveal a previously unrecognized role for OSBPL3 in mediating resistance to trametinib, an FDA-approved MEK inhibitor, and identify combinatorial targeting strategies to overcome this resistance. These findings establish OSBPL3 as a critical node linking lipid metabolism to Hippo-YAP signaling in CRC, providing fresh insights into tumor progression mechanisms and therapeutic development.

## Results

## OSBPL3 overexpression and YAP1 association in CRC
The results demonstrate that OSBPL3 expression is significantly associated with poor outcomes in colorectal cancer. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (Fig.  ) using TCGA-COAD showed that patients with high OSBPL3 expression had significantly reduced overall survival compared to those with low expression levels (p = 0.03). Analysis of GEO datasets revealed consistent upregulation of OSBPL3 in colorectal cancer tissues compared to normal or adjacent tissues (Fig.  ). Specifically, OSBPL3 expression was elevated in primary colorectal cancer relative to normal epithelium (GSE50760), in adenomas compared to rectal mucosa (GSE77635), and in metastatic tissues compared to primary colorectal cancer (GSE41258) (p < 0.0001 for all comparisons) – . Consistent with these findings, a previous TCGA analysis showed higher OSBPL3 expression in colon adenocarcinoma and rectal adenocarcinoma than in matched normal tissues, reinforcing the link between OSBPL3 upregulation and adverse prognosis .
 In colorectal cancer, the molecule YAP1, integral to the Hippo pathway, is known for its role in promoting tumor proliferation, migration, and invasion. However, the specifics of YAP1 regulation in CRC are not fully understood. Correlation analysis demonstrated a strong positive association between OSBPL3 and YAP1 expression in both independent datasets and colorectal cancer cells, as well as a correlation with Hippo signaling pathway activity (Fig.  ). Immunohistochemical staining further showed increased OSBPL3 protein expression in the cytoplasm of colorectal cancer tissues compared to normal colon and rectum tissues (Fig.  ). OSBPL3 protein staining was more frequently detected in CRC tissues than in normal colon based on HPA annotations. Among 11 CRC cases, 9 showed detectable staining: 8 Medium, 1 Low, and 2 were Not detected. In contrast, all 6 normal samples were Not detected (p = 0.0045). Consistently, Western blot analysis revealed a significant increase in OSBPL3 protein levels in colorectal cancer tissues relative to paired normal tissues (p < 0.001, Fig.  ). Taken together, these findings indicate that OSBPL3 is highly expressed in colorectal cancer, correlates with poor prognosis, and is associated with oncogenic signaling pathways, supporting its potential as a biomarker and therapeutic target.

## OSBPL3 knockdown attenuates CRC malignancy
In our exploration of OSBPL3’s role in colorectal cancer, we postulated that its knockdown might impede CRC progression, given its high expression levels and known involvement in gastric cancer. Our study focused on key cancer-related phenotypes: proliferation, migration, invasion, and cell-cycle progression.
We commenced by analyzing OSBPL3 expression across various CRC cell lines. Western blot analysis confirmed that OSBPL3 is highly expressed at the protein level in colorectal cancer cell lines, with particularly high expression observed in SW620 and SW480 cells (Fig.  ). Relative quantification of protein levels (Fig.  ) and data from the CCLE database (Fig.  ) corroborated this finding. This heterogeneity is expected and likely reflects differences in genetic backgrounds that shape lipid metabolism and Hippo/YAP signaling. Distinct tumor origins and phenotypes, such as primary versus metastatic disease, may also contribute. The SW480 and SW620 pair derived from a primary tumor and a lymph node metastasis from the same patient shows higher OSBPL3 than HCT8 and RKO, consistent with an association between OSBPL3 and aggressive features. To enhance generalizability, we profiled OSBPL3 at both mRNA and protein levels across multiple lines and validated these patterns with CCLE, and we provide a summary of key genomic characteristics of the cell lines together with their OSBPL3 levels in Supplementary Table  .
 Knockdown of OSBPL3 using siRNA significantly reduced its mRNA levels in SW620 and SW480 cells (Fig.  ) and was further validated at the protein level through Western blot analysis (Fig.  ) and its relative quantification (Fig.  ). Following successful siRNA implementation, complemented by shRNA design, we conducted cell proliferation assays. Functional assays revealed that OSBPL3 knockdown suppressed cell proliferation, as evidenced by reduced confluence over time using the Incucyte live-cell imaging system (Fig.  ) and diminished colony formation in a clonogenic assay (Fig.  ). Additionally, transwell migration assays demonstrated that OSBPL3 knockdown significantly inhibited cell migration (Fig.  ), while scratch wound-healing assays confirmed a reduced wound closure rate in OSBPL3-depleted cells (Fig.  ). Flow cytometric analysis of the cell cycle indicated that OSBPL3 knockdown led to a significant accumulation of cells in the G1 phase and a reduction in the S phase in both SW620 and SW480 cells (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). To verify the specificity of these knockdown phenotypes, we performed rescue experiments by re‑expressing OSBPL3 in the shRNA background. Immunoblotting confirmed robust re‑expression of OSBPL3 in SW620 and SW480 cells harboring sh#2 relative to shNC and sh#2 controls (Supplementary Fig.  ). Re‑expression restored proliferative capacity over 72 h compared with sh#2 alone, as measured by relative OD growth curves (Supplementary Fig.  ). Consistently, OSBPL3 re‑expression attenuated the knockdown‑induced defects in invasion and migration: transwell invasion counts increased toward control levels and wound‑closure rates improved relative to sh#2 (Supplementary Fig.  ). Together, these rescue data substantiate that the observed reductions in proliferation, migration, and invasion upon OSBPL3 depletion are on‑target consequences of OSBPL3 loss rather than off‑target effects of RNA interference. Collectively, these results support a model in which OSBPL3 promotes colorectal cancer cell proliferation, motility, and cell‑cycle progression, underscoring its potential role in CRC progression.
Our study thus underscores the significance of OSBPL3 in CRC, demonstrating that its knockdown markedly diminishes cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. The altered cell-cycle dynamics further elucidate the mechanisms behind these effects. These insights into OSBPL3’s function in CRC not only enrich our understanding of the disease’s molecular underpinnings but also open avenues for potential therapeutic interventions targeting OSBPL3.

## OSBPL3 overexpression enhances tumor aggressiveness
After establishing that knocking down OSBPL3 hampers cell proliferation and malignancy, we hypothesized that enhancing its expression could potentially accelerate these cellular behaviors. Overexpression of OSBPL3 was validated in HCT8 and RKO cells at both protein and mRNA levels. Western blot analysis (Fig.  ) confirmed increased OSBPL3 protein levels in OSBPL3-overexpressing (OE) cells compared to negative control (NC) cells, which was further supported by mRNA quantification showing significant upregulation of OSBPL3 expression (p < 0.0001, Fig.  ).
 Functional assays indicated that OSBPL3 overexpression significantly enhanced cell proliferation, as demonstrated by increased cell confluence over time using the Incucyte live-cell monitoring system (Fig.  ) and by augmented colony formation capacity in clonogenic assays (Fig.  ). Migration assays revealed that OSBPL3-overexpressing cells exhibited significantly enhanced migratory ability, as shown by both wound-healing assays (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ) and transwell migration assays (Fig.  ).
In vivo, subcutaneous tumor xenograft experiments using nude mice demonstrated that tumors derived from HCT8-OSBPL3 cells were significantly larger and heavier compared to those from control cells (p < 0.0001, Fig.  ). The average tumor volume was 106.9 mm³ for the negative control group and 296.0 mm³ for the OSBPL3 overexpression group. The average tumor weight was 182.7 mg for the NC group and 500.6 mg for the OE group.
We established 12 independent organoid lines derived from clinical patient specimens. The organoids were stratified into high and low OSBPL3 expression groups based on quantitative RT-PCR analysis of endogenous OSBPL3 mRNA levels. Cell viability and growth kinetics were assessed with all values normalized to baseline measurements for each line. Organoid culture experiments showed that under the same conditions, organoids with high endogenous OSBPL3 expression grew faster than those with low OSBPL3 expression (Fig.  ). A positive correlation was also observed between OSBPL3 expression levels and organoid viability (R = 0.67, p = 0.018, Fig.  ). Collectively, these findings suggest that OSBPL3 overexpression promotes colorectal cancer cell proliferation, migration, and tumorigenic potential, supporting its role as a key driver of tumor progression. Both the enhancement and reduction of OSBPL3 expression in CRC cells lead to notable phenotypic changes, highlighting OSBPL3’s influence and potential as a focus for future therapeutic interventions in CRC.

## OSBPL3 modulates the Hippo pathway via multi-omics profiling
Having established the marked effects of both overexpression and knockdown of OSBPL3 on various CRC cell lines, we turned our attention to unraveling the underlying mechanisms. We conducted transcriptome sequencing of SW620-siNC compared to SW620-siOSBPL3#1 RNA to discern the broader transcriptional impacts exerted by OSBPL3. Figure  illustrates the PCA dimensional reduction results between the control group and the OSBPL3 knockdown group, highlighting significant differences post-OSBPL3 knockdown. Figure  presents a comparative analysis of FPKM expression levels between the two groups, ensuring comparability.
 In Fig.  , a volcano plot of the genes altered in the siOSBPL3 group relative to the control group is depicted. Applying criteria of fold change and q-value for selection, we identified 1120 downregulated and 282 upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Subsequent enrichment analysis of the downregulated DEGs, as shown in Fig.  D, E, revealed significant enrichment in key pathways. Notably, the Hippo pathway, MAPK pathway, and PI3K-Akt pathway were significantly represented. Additionally, mechanisms related to metastasis, including extracellular machineries and extracellular vesicles, were also significantly enriched in the results. In Supplementary Fig.  , B, we focused on the enrichment analysis of upregulated DEGs. The KEGG analysis revealed enrichment in several critical pathways and processes, including cellular senescence, platinum drug resistance, and the p53 signaling pathway. In the Gene Ontology analysis, the enriched categories encompassed aspects like regulation of the MAPK pathway and cytokine.
Building upon our RNA-Seq findings, we also investigated the proteomic alterations in HCT8 cells overexpressing OSBPL3 compared to the control group. Figure  displays a PCA graph from TMT proteomics, depicting three replicates each from both groups, where significant differences were observed. Supplementary Fig.  , B reveal the identification of peptides and proteins, with peptides ranging from 7 to 9 amino acids in length being the most abundant. The majority of proteins fell within the molecular weight range of 20–60 kDa. The heatmap illustrates the distinct expression patterns of differentially expressed proteins between the two groups (Supplementary Fig.  ). In the GLAD4U enrichment analysis targeting upregulated DEPs (Differentially Expressed Proteins), enrichment was observed across various cancer diseases with statistical significance (Supplementary Fig.  ).
Further enrichment analysis of the differential proteins identified in the TMT study was conducted. As shown in Fig.  , the KEGG enrichment analysis for upregulated DEPs revealed significant enrichment in several key pathways. These included the Hippo pathway, colorectal cancer, bladder cancer, liver cancer, and various metabolic pathways. Additionally, GSEA (Gene Set Enrichment Analysis) highlighted several important signaling pathways significantly enriched with overexpressed OSBPL3. Figure  illustrates the top GSEA results, encompassing the Hippo pathway, WNT pathway, and bladder cancer, among others.
This comprehensive approach, integrating proteomics and transcriptome sequencing, sheds light on the intricate ways OSBPL3 influences vital cellular pathways, particularly highlighting its role in modulating the Hippo pathway. This deeper understanding of OSBPL3’s role at the molecular level highlights its potential relevance in the pathogenesis and treatment of colorectal and other cancers.

## OSBPL3 regulates Hippo-YAP signaling in CRC
Knockdown of OSBPL3 in SW480 and SW620 cells resulted in increased LATS1 expression and reduced YAP1 and phosphorylated YAP1 (p-YAP) levels, accompanied by a significant downregulation of the Hippo pathway downstream targets CYR61 and AREG. In contrast, OSBPL3 overexpression in HCT8 and RKO cells showed the opposite effects, with decreased LATS1 and increased YAP1, p-YAP, CYR61, and AREG expression levels (Fig.  A, B). Immunofluorescence and subcellular fractionation analyses (Fig.  C, D) revealed that OSBPL3 knockdown induced YAP1 translocation from the nucleus to the cytoplasm in SW620, a finding further validated in SW480 cells (Supplementary Fig.  , B).
 Fig.  illustrates the effects of YAP1 knockdown on OSBPL3-overexpressing RKO cells. Rescue experiments demonstrated that YAP1 knockdown reversed the pro-proliferative effects of OSBPL3 overexpression in RKO cells, as evidenced by reduced cell confluence over time (Fig.  ) and impaired wound-healing ability in scratch assays (Fig.  ). Conversely, YAP1 overexpression partially rescued the proliferation and migration deficits caused by OSBPL3 knockdown in SW620 cells, as shown by Western blot validation (Fig.  ), increased cell confluence (Fig.  ), and enhanced wound-healing ability (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ).
In vivo, subcutaneous tumor xenografts in nude mice demonstrated that YAP1 overexpression partially rescued the tumorigenic defects caused by OSBPL3 knockdown, as evidenced by increased tumor volume and weight compared to OSBPL3 knockdown alone (Fig.  K–M). In vivo subcutaneous xenograft experiments in nude mice showed that average tumor volumes for the shNC, shOSBPL3, shOSBPL3-YAP1, and shNC-YAP1 groups were 172.2 mm³, 68.8 mm³, 205.3 mm³, and 480.2 mm³, respectively, while the corresponding average tumor weights were 390.8 mg, 164.6 mg, 384.0 mg, and 996.6 mg, indicating that tumors in the shNC-YAP1 group were the largest and heaviest, whereas those in the shOSBPL3 group were the smallest and lightest. Taken together, these findings indicate that OSBPL3 exerts its oncogenic effects in colorectal cancer by modulating the Hippo-YAP pathway, highlighting its critical role in tumor proliferation, migration, and progression.

## OSBPL3-14-3-3 interaction governs YAP1 localization
Based on the preceding experimental and research findings, we can ascertain that OSBPL3 plays a significant role in modulating YAP1 expression and subcellular localization within CRC. However, the precise mechanistic underpinnings remain undisclosed. We hypothesize that OSBPL3 may interact with other proteins to collectively influence the subcellular localization and expression status of YAP1. Consequently, we employed IP-mass spectrometry techniques to identify proteins that interact with OSBPL3. Figure  presents a multifaceted analysis of the interaction between oxysterol-binding protein-like 3 and 14-3-3 proteins, highlighting their potential role in cancer biology. Figure  features mass spectrometry data contrasting immunoprecipitation results from OSBPL3-IP and IgG control groups, indicating a distinct protein profile associated with OSBPL3. Figure  depicts the total ion chromatogram (TIC) of pulled-down proteins in both the IgG and OSBPL3 groups. Through IP-mass spectrometry analysis, it was revealed that 706 proteins were pulled down by OSBPL3. After excluding proteins that could also bind to IgG, 469 proteins were identified as specifically interacting with OSBPL3. HitPredict is a database that investigates protein-protein interactions through published studies and high-throughput mass spectrometry results . We further utilized this resource to query key proteins binding to OSBPL3. As depicted in Fig.  ’s Upset intersection diagram, the 14-3-3 proteins were found to interact with OSBPL3. We prioritized 14-3-3 for further study because clinical colorectal cancer datasets show coordinated expression of OSBPL3 with a 14-3-3 transcript signature, both markers associated with adverse clinicopathologic features and poorer survival , and 14-3-3 directly regulates YAP1 cytoplasmic retention and nuclear entry. In patient tissues, OSBPL3 is upregulated with cytoplasmic and perinuclear enrichment, and 14-3-3 is abundant in both compartments, supporting a plausible functional axis.
 Consequently, we postulate that the formation of a complex between OSBPL3 and 14-3-3 influences the subcellular localization of YAP1. Subsequently, we validated the interaction between endogenous OSBPL3 and 14-3-3. In SW620 cells, we performed co-immunoprecipitation assays using antibodies against OSBPL3 and pan 14-3-3 to pull down interacting proteins, thereby confirming their mutual association (Fig.  ). Positive results were obtained regardless of whether OSBPL3 pulled down 14-3-3 after agarose bead conjugation or vice versa. Furthermore, we conducted immunofluorescence multicolor staining to label and visualize 14-3-3 and OSBPL3, confirming their co-localization (Fig.  ). OSBPL3 fluorescence is represented in green, while 14-3-3 fluorescence appears in pink, and a significant overlap between the two fluorescence signals is observed. To establish the co-localization relationship between these two proteins, we quantified the fluorescence using ImageJ (Fig.  ). Notably, a clear concordance trend between the two fluorescence signals is observed. Subsequently, we subjected the fluorescence trend to statistical analysis (Fig.  ), which revealed a highly significant positive correlation between the two fluorescence signals, demonstrating statistical significance.
To further ascertain the interaction sites, we constructed truncated mutants and wild-type plasmids for OSBPL3 and 14-3-3 and transfected them accordingly. Figure  illustrates the schematic representation of the truncated mutants, with the upper half depicting the OSBPL3 truncation and the lower half depicting the 14-3-3 truncation. Initially, by co-immunoprecipitating FLAG-tagged and HA-tagged proteins, we confirmed the interaction between wild-type OSBPL3 and wild-type 14-3-3 in 293T cells, thereby substantiating their interaction (Fig.  ). In the truncation interaction experiments, we observed that all truncated versions of OSBPL3, following the 1-167aa sequence truncation, were able to pull down the target protein using the 14-3-3 tag. However, mutants lacking the 1-326aa and 1-454aa segments of OSBPL3 failed to pull down 14-3-3, indicating an interaction between the 326-454aa segment of OSBPL3 and 14-3-3-wt (Fig.  ). Finally, we identified the structural region of 14-3-3 responsible for interaction with OSBPL3-wt as the 1-106aa sequence. Truncation mutants lacking the 167aa sequence did not affect the interaction between OSBPL3 and 14-3-3 (Fig.  ). We next examined whether phosphorylation within the OSBPL3 segment that engages 14‑3‑3 contributes to the interaction and whether the C‑terminal oxysterol‑binding domain is required. Supplementary Fig.  summarizes phosphorylation mapping and mutational testing of the OSBPL3-14‑3‑3 interface. In Supplementary Fig.  , a site map compiled from PhosphoSitePlus highlights dense phosphorylation across OSBPL3, including multiple residues within the N‑terminal region and the C‑terminal oxysterol‑binding domain, with annotated sites in the mapped 167–326aa segment that engages 14‑3‑3. Co‑immunoprecipitation assays in 293T cells are shown in Supplementary Fig.  . Single‑site alanine substitutions at Ser251 and Ser304 preserved 14‑3‑3 binding at levels similar to wild type, indicating that phosphorylation at either site alone is not required for association under these conditions. Point mutations designed to disrupt oxysterol engagement, K603E and H631A, H632A, also retained 14‑3‑3 binding, whereas deletion of the entire C terminus, Δ522–885, markedly reduced recovery of 14‑3‑3 in the IP. Together, these results indicate that OSBPL3-14‑3‑3 recognition does not depend on Ser251 or Ser304 individually and does not require ligand binding by the oxysterol‑binding pocket, while the structural integrity of the C terminus contributes to maintaining a competent interaction platform. Further functional assessment of compartmental YAP1 distribution with these mutants will be necessary to define the consequences for YAP regulation in colorectal cancer cells.

## OSBPL3 modulates trametinib sensitivity via YAP1
Prior studies have established YAP1 as a pivotal gene for MEK inhibitor trametinib (TRA) drug sensitivity in cancer. Our research has demonstrated a close correlation between YAP1 expression and OSBPL3 regulation in CRC. In the RNA-seq results of siOSBPL3, both MAPK and the Hippo signaling pathway were significantly enriched. These findings suggest a potential association between OSBPL3 and TRA. Knockdown of OSBPL3 increased sensitivity to TRA in SW620 and SW480 cells, as reflected by a significant reduction in IC50 values. In SW620 cells, the IC50 decreased from 4.6 μM in the shNC group to 0.49 μM and 1.40 μM in the sh1 and sh2 groups, representing 9.38-fold and 3.28-fold changes, respectively. Similarly, in SW480 cells, the IC50 decreased from 916 nM in the shNC group to 188 nM and 243 nM in the sh1 and sh2 groups, representing 4.85-fold and 3.77-fold changes, respectively (Fig.  ). As depicted in Supplementary Fig.  , under identical TRA concentrations, abundant colonies were observed in shNC cells, whereas sh1 and sh2 cells exhibited minimal colony formation, both in SW620 and SW480 cells.
 As illustrated in Fig.  , following the overexpression of OSBPL3, the TRA IC50 increased in both cell types. Specifically, RKO-NC exhibited an IC50 of 149 nM, while RKO-OE showed an IC50 of 710 nM. Similarly, HCT8-NC displayed an IC50 of 9 nM, which increased to 86 nM upon overexpression of OSBPL3. The IC50 values for these two cell lines increased by 4.77-fold and 9.56-fold, respectively. Although they did not meet the resistance criteria, they did indeed confer enhanced tolerance to TRA. In colony formation assays, we used lower IC50 drug concentrations. As shown in Supplementary Fig.  , the number of cell colonies in both overexpressing cell lines was significantly higher. These experiments collectively demonstrate that elevated levels of OSBPL3 expression indeed led to increased tolerance to TRA.
The primary mechanism of action for TRA involves inhibiting the downstream MAPK pathway, particularly MEK1/2, thereby suppressing cell proliferation and promoting apoptosis. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that OSBPL3 knockdown enhanced trametinib-induced apoptosis in SW620 and SW480 cells, while OSBPL3 overexpression reduced apoptosis in RKO and HCT8 cells under the same conditions (Fig.  C, D). Similarly, organoid culture experiments showed that organoids with high OSBPL3 expression exhibited reduced apoptosis compared to those with low OSBPL3 expression under identical trametinib treatment conditions (Fig.  ). A positive correlation was observed between trametinib IC50 values and organoid OSBPL3 expression levels (R = 0.6, p = 0.038, Fig.  ).
Rescue experiments demonstrated that YAP1 knockdown reversed the trametinib resistance conferred by OSBPL3 overexpression in HCT8 and RKO cells, as evidenced by reduced IC50 values (Fig.  G, H) and decreased colony formation under trametinib treatment (Fig.  ). Furthermore, combined treatment with the YAP1 inhibitor verteporfin (VP) and trametinib showed a synergistic effect on reducing cell viability in OSBPL3-overexpressing RKO and HCT8 cells, as shown by response surface modeling (Fig.  J, K). We observed that both HCT8-OSBPL3 and RKO-OSBPL3 cells were insensitive to VP alone, but their viability significantly decreased after combination treatment with TRA, suggesting a synergistic effect of VP and TRA on cells overexpressing OSBPL3. We validated the trend of apoptosis in cells after drug combination therapy. As shown in Supplementary Fig.  , the flow cytometry results of apoptosis for the DMSO group, VP-alone group, TRA-alone group, and VP-TRA combination group are depicted. Regardless of whether it was RKO-OSBPL3 or HCT8-OSBPL3, the cytotoxic effect of VP alone on cells was limited. However, after combining VP with TRA, the proportion of apoptotic cells significantly increased, especially in the late apoptosis phase.
These results indicate that OSBPL3 modulates trametinib sensitivity through the YAP1 pathway and highlight the therapeutic potential of combining trametinib with YAP1 inhibition in OSBPL3-overexpressing colorectal cancer.

## Discussion
YAP1, as a key effector of the Hippo pathway, is closely associated with the occurrence and development of CRC, with YAP1 being one of the important molecules maintaining intestinal stem cell proliferation , . Despite its established role in driving tumorigenesis, the intricate mechanisms governing YAP1 in CRC remained elusive. Taking this as our starting point, we identified the target gene OSBPL3 through co-expression analysis. OSBPL3, as a lipid receptor, has yet to be revealed and discovered in its connection with the Hippo pathway and YAP1. To address the interplay between lipid metabolism and Hippo-YAP signaling, OSBPL3’s function as a lipid transfer protein likely involves modulating specific lipids that regulate pathway activity. For instance, OSBPL3 binds oxysterols and cholesterol, potentially influencing the mevalonate pathway to enhance RhoA activity and inhibit LATS-mediated YAP phosphorylation. Lipids like phosphatidic acid could further disrupt LATS-MOB1 complexes, promoting YAP nuclear entry. This lipid-dependent mechanism may explain OSBPL3’s role in CRC tumorigenesis, warranting future studies on specific lipid species , . We found that OSBPL3 not only has a significant co-expression relationship with YAP1 but also is highly expressed in CRC. Its expression level in adenomas is higher than that in adjacent normal tissues, and in adenocarcinomas, the expression of OSBPL3 is higher than in adenomas. Furthermore, in CRC lesions with liver metastases, the expression level of OSBPL3 is even higher. If OSBPL3 is used as a grouping criterion, CRC patients with high OSBPL3 expression have a shorter survival period. These results all suggest that OSBPL3 may play a pro-cancer role in CRC. Based on this, we have reasons to conduct in-depth research and exploration on OSBPL3.
Considering the co-expression relationship with YAP1, we conducted transcriptome sequencing and proteomic high-throughput analysis after knocking down and overexpressing OSBPL3, aiming to determine whether interference with OSBPL3 could affect the Hippo pathway. Our RNA‑seq indicates that OSBPL3 enhances Hippo output in CRC by YAP1 nuclear presence. The “bladder cancer” enrichment observed with OSBPL3 overexpression likely reflects shared pathway activity rather than tumor specificity. Notably, Wang et al. reported a tumor‑suppressive role for OSBPL3 in bladder cancer . We posit that this divergence reflects lineage and context: urothelial versus intestinal epithelium differ in genetic landscapes, baseline transcriptional states, and lipid milieus. Thus, OSBPL3 can act as a suppressor in bladder cancer yet as a promoter in CRC.YAP1 exerts powerful functions, primarily degraded in the cytoplasm while exerting its function in the nucleus. Central to this work is the discovery that OSBPL3 directly regulates YAP1 activity through interaction with 14-3-3 proteins. While 14-3-3 isoforms are known to sequester phosphorylated YAP1 in the cytoplasm . To avoid degradation, 14-3-3 is required to bind to the phosphorylated YAP1, retaining it in the cytoplasm to prevent ubiquitination degradation . Our co-IP and immunofluorescence data demonstrate that OSBPL3 competitively disrupts this interaction, promoting YAP1 nuclear translocation. This mechanism explains the observed inverse relationship between OSBPL3 expression and LATS1-mediated YAP1 phosphorylation, as well as the downstream upregulation of YAP1 targets CYR61 and AREG. Importantly, rescue experiments confirmed the functional dependency of OSBPL3-driven malignancy on YAP1, as both genetic and pharmacological YAP1 inhibition reversed OSBPL3’s pro-proliferative and migratory effects. These findings position OSBPL3 as a lipid-metabolism-related regulator of Hippo-YAP signaling, bridging a critical gap in understanding how microenvironmental cues modulate this pathway in CRC.
Through high-throughput experimental techniques and traditional WB assays, we confirmed the influence of OSBPL3 on YAP1. Therefore, we also conducted rescue experiments targeting YAP1 after overexpressing OSBPL3, using siRNA and the YAP1 inhibitor verteporfin. In cell experiments, various phenotypes were significantly reversed, indicating that the oncogenic function exerted by OSBPL3 largely depends on YAP1. Furthermore, in the reverse experiments of overexpressing YAP1 after knocking down OSBPL3, both in vivo and in vitro experiments showed rescued malignant phenotypes of tumors.
YAP1, a pivotal component of the Hippo pathway, also serves as a crucial mediator in the MAPK pathway. Given YAP1’s influence on trametinib sensitivity and the enrichment findings , , we hypothesize that OSBPL3 may modulate downstream effectors of the MAPK pathway, such as MEK inhibitors like trametinib. YAP1, acting as a bypass in the MAPK pathway, becomes hyperactivated in response to MEK inhibition, contributing to trametinib resistance , . This study further uncovers OSBPL3’s role in modulating sensitivity to MEK inhibitors. The 4.8–9.6-fold increase in trametinib IC50 upon OSBPL3 overexpression highlights its potential as a predictive biomarker for MEK inhibitor resistance. This resistance appears to stem from OSBPL3’s dual activation of YAP1 and MAPK pathways, as evidenced by transcriptomic enrichment of both Hippo and MAPK signaling components. The synergistic effect of combining trametinib with the YAP1 inhibitor verteporfin provides a compelling rationale for dual-pathway targeting in OSBPL3-high tumors—a strategy that could circumvent adaptive resistance mechanisms commonly observed in single-agent therapies. Together, these data suggest that OSBPL3 constrains YAP1‑mediated adaptive escape to MEK blockade and can thereby enhance trametinib response. Given trametinib’s current third‑line use in metastatic CRC, reducing YAP1 output via OSBPL3 targeting offers a mechanistic path to deepen and prolong benefit in BRAF V600E disease, where MEK inhibitors are already deployed, and potentially extend efficacy to additional CRC subsets in which YAP1 undermines MAPK‑directed therapy.
Despite our study’s inclusion of multiple samples for organoid culture and drug sensitivity validation, the absence of patient mutation data may introduce biases and inaccuracies in correlating organoid trametinib sensitivity with OSBPL3 expression.
In drug combination assays, we observed a synergistic cytotoxic effect of verteporfin and trametinib in OSBPL3-overexpressing cells, underscoring its potential in CRC treatment. Trametinib, currently employed as a second-line therapy for CRC, faces limited adoption due to its potent antitumor effects often accompanied by severe adverse reactions , . Our findings suggest that CRC patients with heightened OSBPL3 expression may exhibit increased trametinib tolerance. While monotherapy with trametinib may yield suboptimal outcomes, combination regimens incorporating verteporfin or other YAP1 inhibitors hold promise for enhanced therapeutic efficacy. Nonetheless, further research and experimentation are warranted to substantiate these findings.
Certain limitations of this study must be acknowledged. Firstly, while evaluating the modulation of Trametinib sensitivity in vitro, we did not corroborate our findings with in vivo xenograft experiments, leaving unresolved whether the observed shifts in IC50 values could translate into clinically meaningful antitumor effects. Second, the precise lipid species mediated by OSBPL3 in CRC progression remain unidentified, leaving open questions about its metabolic functions. Third, we did not directly compare the effects of targeted YAP inhibition, MEK knockdown, or their combination on trametinib sensitivity, and thus cannot fully delineate the therapeutic interplay between Hippo-YAP and MAPK signaling in our system. Lastly, clinical validation of OSBPL3 as a biomarker requires prospective trials correlating its expression with trametinib response in CRC patients. Despite these caveats, our multidimensional investigation has systematically delineated the oncogenic roles of OSBPL3 in colorectal cancer and its intricate interplay with YAP1, providing novel insights into the molecular underpinnings of CRC pathogenesis.
This study establishes OSBPL3 as a critical oncogenic driver in colorectal cancer, orchestrating tumor progression through direct regulation of the Hippo-YAP pathway. Our findings demonstrate that OSBPL3 overexpression disrupts 14-3-3-mediated cytoplasmic retention of YAP1, promoting its nuclear translocation and subsequent activation of downstream oncogenic targets. Clinically, OSBPL3 serves as a prognostic marker, with its overexpression correlating significantly with reduced survival and enhanced therapeutic resistance to MEK inhibitors such as trametinib. The observed synergy between YAP1 inhibition and trametinib in OSBPL3-high tumors provides an actionable strategy to circumvent drug resistance, highlighting the therapeutic potential of dual-pathway targeting. In summary, these results redefine OSBPL3 as a multifunctional scaffold protein that bridges 14-3-3-mediated YAP1 regulation and CRC progression. By delineating its role in Hippo signaling and therapeutic resistance, this work provides a foundation for targeting OSBPL3 or its interactors in precision oncology approaches, while underscoring the need to explore its lipid-related biology in subsequent investigations.

## Methods

## Cell culture
The cell lines employed in this study include 293T, SW480, SW620, RKO, and HCT8. All cell lines were obtained from Meisen Chinese Tissue Culture Collections (https://www.ctcc.online/). These cell lines were authenticated by Short Tandem Repeat profiling and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% carbon dioxide. 293T, SW480, SW620, and RKO cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, while HCT8 cells were propagated in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with an equivalent FBS concentration. Moreover, all culture media were fortified with 1% penicillin-streptomycin to prevent microbial contamination.

## Cell transfection
Cellular transfections were primarily conducted using small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs). For siRNA transfections, the selected cell lines were cultured in an incubator at 37 °C until reaching the appropriate confluence during the logarithmic growth phase. The siRNAs targeting the gene of interest were diluted in nuclease-free water, followed by complexation with Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent. The siRNA-lipid complexes were incubated at room temperature for 15 min and subsequently added dropwise to the culture vessels containing cells. The vessels were gently swirled to ensure uniform distribution of the complexes over the cellular monolayer before returning them to the incubator for 24 h under normal culture conditions. Thereafter, the medium was replenished to support further cellular proliferation for downstream experiments.
For shRNA delivery, lentiviral transduction particles encoding the shRNA constructs were employed. Cells were dissociated, counted, and seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well 24 h prior to transduction. Lentiviral particles were added to complete medium devoid of antibiotics at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) optimized for each cell line, with Polybrene supplementation to enhance transduction efficiency. Twenty-four hours post-transduction, the medium was replaced with fresh puromycin-containing medium to initiate selection of stably transduced cells. The initial puromycin concentration of 1 μg/mL was gradually escalated until all non-transduced parental cells underwent puromycin-mediated death. The shRNA sequences targeting OSBPL3 were: sh1: GGAACATCCTGAGGAACAA; sh2: GCGTGTCATGCTGAGTCTA. The siRNA sequences targeting OSBPL3 were: si1 (5′-3′) sense: GGAACAUCCUGAGGAACAATT, si1 (5′-3′) antisense: UAGCAUCUUUGCCAAUAGCTT; si2 (5′-3′) sense: GCGUGUCAUGCUGAGUCUATT, si2 (5′-3′) antisense: UAGACUCAGCAUGACACGCTT. The siRNA sequence targeting YAP1 was: siYAP1 (5′-3′) sense: AUGUGAUUUAAGAAGUAUCUC, siYAP1 (5′-3′) antisense: GAUACUUCUUAAAUCACAUCG.

## Cell proliferation assays
Cellular proliferation kinetics were monitored and quantified using the IncuCyte S3 Live-Cell Analysis System. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2000–4000 cells per well. Following an initial attachment period of 12–24 h in a standard humidified incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2, the plates were transferred to the IncuCyte system maintained under identical environmental conditions. Phase-contrast images were acquired in quadruplicate per well at 4–8 h intervals over a continuous 96-h period to longitudinally track cell proliferation. The IncuCyte software suite leveraged an automated confluence algorithm based on machine learning (“AI Confluence”) to quantify the percentage of the well surface area occupied by cells at each time point. Proliferation curves were plotted, and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v8.4.2).

## Cell-cycle and apoptosis analysis
For cell-cycle analysis, cells were stained with propidium iodide (PI) and subjected to flow cytometry. Upon reaching the desired confluence, cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), dissociated using trypsin, and collected by centrifugation. After removing residual media through an additional PBS wash and centrifugation step, cells were fixed by dropwise addition of ice-cold 70% ethanol and incubated at 4 °C for 60 min. Staining was performed using a solution containing PI stain, RNase A, and provided buffers according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Post-staining, cells were washed, resuspended in staining buffer, and incubated light-protected at 37 °C for 30 min. Flow cytometric acquisition was performed using 488 nm excitation to detect PI fluorescence, with subsequent cell-cycle modeling conducted using FlowJo software (v10).
Apoptotic cells were identified via Annexin V-FITC and PI co-staining. Conditioned media containing detached cells were collected and combined with adherent cells harvested by trypsin treatment without EDTA. After centrifugation in spent media to ensure maximal cell recovery, cells were washed twice with PBS to remove residual serum proteins. Staining was achieved by resuspending the cell pellet in binding buffer supplemented with Annexin V-FITC and PI staining solutions, followed by light-protected incubation for 20 min. Prior to flow analysis, cells were diluted in additional binding buffer. Samples were excited at 488 nm. The flow cytometry gating strategy is shown in Supplementary Fig.  , where 9A represents cell-cycle analysis and 9B represents apoptosis analysis.

## Cell migration and invasion assays
Transwell and wound-healing assays were employed to evaluate the migratory and invasive capabilities of cells upon experimental perturbations. For Transwell migration assays, cells were trypsinized, counted, and 2 × 104 cells were seeded into the upper chambers of Transwell inserts in serum-free medium. The lower chambers were supplemented with medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum to serve as a chemoattractant. After 24–48 h of incubation, non-migratory cells were carefully removed from the upper chambers using sterile PBS washes and cotton swabs. Migratory cells adherent to the lower surface of the membrane were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and subsequently stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min. Excess stain was removed by PBS washes, and membranes were visualized and imaged under a light microscope.
For wound-healing assays, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured to near confluence. Homogenous cell monolayers were scratched using a 200 μL pipette tip to generate linear wounds, followed by PBS washes to remove dislodged cells and debris. Serum-free medium was then replenished, and closure of the wounds was monitored over 24–48 h. Phase-contrast images were acquired immediately post-wounding and at the endpoint to quantify the migratory capacity by measuring the remaining unrecovered areas.

## Evaluation of drug sensitivity
Drug sensitivity was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) colorimetric assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates and exposed to serial dilutions of the compound of interest, in this case Trametinib, prepared as 4-fold concentration gradients. Vehicle-only wells served as negative controls. Upon attaining 70% or greater confluence in the untreated control wells after 4–5 days of culture, the CCK8 assay was initiated by carefully aspirating spent media and replacing it with fresh media supplemented with 10% CCK8 reagent. Following a 1-h incubation under standard culture conditions, absorbance was quantified at 450 nm using a multimode plate reader. Dose-response curves were generated, and IC50 values were calculated using non-linear regression analysis in GraphPad Prism software.

## Protein truncation mutagenesis
To delineate the interacting domains between OSBPL3 and 14-3-3 proteins, we employed a truncation mutagenesis approach using 293T cells as an overexpression system. Full-length human OSBPL3 and 14-3-3 cDNA templates were amplified using Q5 High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs). For OSBPL3, a series of six truncation mutants were engineered based on the predicted structural domains: wild-type (wt), Δ1-51aa, Δ1-146aa, Δ1-167aa, Δ1-326aa, and Δ1-454aa. Similarly, six truncated variants of 14-3-3 were generated: wt, Δ1-106aa, Δ1-137aa, Δ1-167aa, Δ167-246aa, and Δ216-246aa. The PCR products were purified and subcloned into the BamHI and XhoI sites of the pcDNA3.1+ vector using Exonuclease III (Exo III)-mediated ligation-independent cloning. To facilitate downstream immunoprecipitation assays, OSBPL3 constructs were fused with a C-terminal 3×FLAG epitope, while 14-3-3 variants harbored a C-terminal HA epitope tag.
In addition to the truncations described above, site-directed mutagenesis was performed to generate OSBPL3 phospho-site mutants S251A and S304A, oxysterol-binding pocket mutants K603E and H631A+H632A, and a C‑terminal deletion removing the oxysterol-binding domain (Δ522–885 aa) . All constructed plasmids were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. For these experiments, OSBPL3 variants were expressed as FLAG-tagged constructs, and 14‑3‑3 was expressed as HA‑tagged wild type to allow reciprocal detection in co‑immunoprecipitation assays exactly as performed for the truncation series.

## Ethical statement and animal care
We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. All animal procedures and study protocols were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University.

## Xenograft tumor models
To evaluate the in vivo tumorigenic potential, we employed a subcutaneous xenograft comparing control versus genetically manipulated CRC cells (OSBPL3 knockdown or overexpression and YAP1 overexpression), with the individual mouse as the experimental unit. Female athymic nude mice, 4–5 weeks old, were sourced from an accredited vendor, acclimatized for ≥7 days under SPF conditions, and randomly assigned (computer-generated sequence, weight-balanced) to groups of 5 mice each (total n per experiment indicated in figure legends). Cells were implanted subdermally into the anterior flank or posterior thigh region. Prior to implantation, cells underwent medium replenishment and were washed twice with PBS, followed by standard trypsinization and resuspension in complete growth medium for quantification. An additional wash in serum-free medium was performed to minimize potential xenogenic serum reactions. For xenografting, 5 × 106 cells were resuspended in 100 μL of sterile PBS and injected subcutaneously into each mouse.
Animals were housed in the facility with a standard acclimatisation period before experiments. Acclimatization allowed animals to adjust to their environment, reducing stress-related variability in experimental outcomes. Investigators performing caliper measurements and endpoint assessments were blinded to group allocation; allocation was concealed from data analysts until primary analyses were completed. Tumor dimensions were measured using digital calipers at regular intervals, with volumes calculated using the formula (length × width2)/2. According to institutional animal care guidelines, the maximal allowed tumor diameter was 1.5 cm (or maximal volume 1500 mm³). Mice were closely monitored and euthanized by carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation if tumor burdens approached these humane endpoints or if ulceration occurred. At the experimental endpoint (4–6 weeks), all remaining mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation, and tumors were resected for further analyses. Statistical analyses were two-sided using GraphPad Prism or R: endpoint tumor weights and volume were compared between groups by an independent-samples t-test; exact n for each analysis is provided in figure legends. All procedures were approved by the institutional animal care and use committee and complied with national guidelines.

## Protein and RNA extraction
For the extraction of proteins from cells and tissues, the RIPA method is employed. Cells are cultured to an appropriate confluence, washed twice with PBS, and prepared for protein extraction on ice. To the RIPA lysis buffer, PM‍SF, and phosphatase inhibitors are added to prevent protein degradation and denaturation. After adding RIPA, a cell scraper is used to scrape off all cells, which are then transferred to an EP tube. Cell disruption is ensured using a vortex oscillator or an ultrasonic crusher. The mixture is then centrifuged in a pre-cooled centrifuge at 4 °C, at a speed of 15,000 × g for 15 min. The supernatant obtained is the total cellular protein. Prior to experimentation, it is necessary to perform a BCA protein concentration assay and balance the protein concentrations. Standards of varying concentrations are prepared in a 96-well plate, along with different protein samples. BCA working solution is then added, and the mixture is incubated at 37 °C for 20 min before absorbance is measured. The protein concentration of the lowest sample is used as the standard for balancing.
The Beyotime cell nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction kit is used for the extraction of nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins. Simply, 200 μL of the protein lysis buffer from the kit is used to lyse the proteins, after which the cytoplasmic protein extraction reagent is added. The mixture is then centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min at 15,000 × g. The supernatant, which contains the cytoplasmic proteins. After disposing of the residual liquid, 50 μL of nuclear protein extraction reagent is added, followed by vigorous vortex oscillation. The mixture is centrifuged again under the same conditions to obtain nuclear proteins.
For RNA extraction, the Trizol method is utilized. Cells are first washed twice with PBS. 1 ml of Trizol reagent is added to the cell culture dish, and cells are thoroughly dissolved by repetitive pipetting or scraping. The Trizol-cell mixture is transferred to an EP tube, to which 200 μL of chloroform is added and mixed thoroughly. It is then centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min at 12,000 × g. The supernatant is carefully transferred to a new RNase-free centrifuge tube, and an equal volume of RNase-free alcohol is added and mixed. The RNA is then precipitated in a −20 °C freezer. After another round of centrifugation, the supernatant is discarded, and the residue is washed with 70% ethanol. Finally, the precipitate is dissolved in DEPC water to obtain the final RNA, which is stored at −80 °C.

## Western blot (WB)
Following the equalization of protein concentration, proteins are mixed with loading buffer and then denatured in a 95 °C metal bath for 10 min. The SDS-PAGE gel used is 10% concentration with a thickness of 1 mm. Specifically, after securing the gel in the electrophoresis clamp, it is placed into the electrophoresis tank and filled with running buffer. Protein samples are then loaded into the wells of the gel. Electrophoresis is initially conducted at 80 V. Once the protein migrates to the lower layer of the gel, the voltage is increased to 150 V. After electrophoresis, a transfer ‘sandwich’ is prepared, stacking the PVDF membrane, the gel, and the transfer clamp in sequence. This assembly is then placed into the transfer tank, and the transfer buffer is added, with the setup maintained on ice to keep it cool. Transfer is carried out at 200 mA for 100 min. Following this, the membrane is washed three times with TBST, each wash lasting 5 min on a shaker. The membrane is then blocked with TBST containing 5% skim milk, shaken at room temperature for 2 h. After another three washes with TBST, the PVDF membrane is cut according to the protein molecular weight marker, and the corresponding bands are placed in an antibody incubation box with the primary antibody (1:1000 dilution) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the primary antibody is recovered, and the membrane is washed three times with TBST. The secondary antibody specific to the species is incubated at room temperature for 2 h (1:2000 dilution). Finally, the membrane is developed using the ECL developing solution and exposed to capture the results. For densitometry in ImageJ (v1.54f), we delineated tight regions of interest around each band, measured background from an adjacent blank area within the same lane, and subtracted this background from the band’s integrated density. The background-corrected OSBPL3 intensity in each lane was then divided by the background-corrected GAPDH intensity from the same lane to obtain the OSBPL3/GAPDH ratio. No further normalization or scaling across lanes or blots was performed beyond this within-lane ratio. Details for the antibodies are provided in Supplementary Table  .

## Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
Co-IP was conducted following standard procedures. Agarose beads were prepared by washing with TBS and then incubating with antibodies to achieve a final concentration of 50 µg/mL. After incubation, the beads were washed and set aside. Cell protein extraction was performed when cell confluency reached over 90%. Cells were lysed with gentle lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, followed by centrifugation to collect the protein sample. The IP procedure involved incubating the protein sample with antibody-coated agarose beads overnight at 4 °C. After incubation, the beads were washed with lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. Elution of the target protein was achieved using either an SDS-PAGE sample loading buffer for Western blot analysis or an acidic elution buffer to preserve biological activity. The elution process was conducted according to the intended purpose of the experiment.

## Tandem Mass Tag proteomics
In this study, Tandem Mass Tag (TMT) proteomics was employed to analyze the differential protein expression in cells. Samples were lysed in SDS lysis buffer containing 1 mM PMSF and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay. For digestion, 50 μg of protein from each sample was reduced with 5 mM dithiothreitol at 55 °C for 30 min, alkylated with 10 mM iodoacetamide in the dark at room temperature for 15 min, and precipitated with cold acetone. The pellets were resuspended in 200 mM TEAB and digested with trypsin (1:50 w/w) overnight at 37 °C. Peptides were labeled using the TMT10-plex Isobaric Label Reagent Set (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and quenched with 5% hydroxylamine. The labeled peptides were fractionated using high-pH reverse-phase HPLC on an Agilent 1100 system equipped with an Agilent Zorbax Extend-C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 5 μm). Peptides were eluted with a gradient of acetonitrile in 10 mM ammonium hydroxide (pH 10) and consolidated into 15 fractions. Peptide fractions were separated using an EASY-nLC 1000 system coupled to an Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid mass spectrometer. Separation was performed on an Acclaim PepMap RSLC column (75 μm × 50 cm) using a gradient of 2–90% solvent B (80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) over 75 min at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. Proteome Discoverer 2.3 software was employed to analyze the mass spectrometry data. The protein database used for the identification of peptides and proteins was the ‘uniprot-Homo sapiens-9606-2023.2.1.fasta’, selected for its specificity to the organism under study. For mass spectrometry analysis, the mass tolerance for precursor ions (MS1) was set to 10 ppm. The MS1 full scan was acquired in the Orbitrap with a resolution of 60,000, an AGC target of 1e6, a maximum injection time of 50 ms, and a scan range of 350–1500 m/z. This stringent tolerance level was chosen to ensure high accuracy in the measurement of the mass-to-charge ratio of the precursor ions, thereby enhancing the specificity of the peptide identification. For fragment ions (MS2), the mass tolerance was set to 0.02 Da, and precursor ions were selected for MS/MS using high-energy collision dissociation (HCD) with a normalized collision energy of 36%. MS/MS spectra were acquired in the Orbitrap with a resolution of 30,000, an AGC target of 1e5, a maximum injection time of 80 ms, and a dynamic exclusion duration of 30 s, allowing for precise fragment identification crucial for accurate peptide sequencing. Normalization and statistical analysis were carried out to identify proteins that were significantly differentially expressed between different cell lines.

## RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)
RNA was extracted using standard protocols using TRIzol reagent. RNA purity and concentration were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, and RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies), followed by library construction for transcriptomic analysis. The VAHTS Universal V5 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit was employed for library preparation. Specifically, poly(A) mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) beads and fragmented. Double-stranded cDNA was synthesized, followed by end-repair, A-tailing, and ligation of adapters. Finally, the libraries were enriched by PCR amplification. This kit is designed for high-efficiency library construction, crucial for accurate transcriptomic profiling. The prepared libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Sequencing was performed to obtain 150 bp paired-end reads, allowing for enhanced coverage and more accurate read alignment. Raw sequencing data in FASTQ format were processed using the Fastp software (v0.20.1, with parameter --length_required 50). Fastp was selected for its efficiency in trimming adapters and filtering low-quality reads. Quality control checks were further performed using FastQC (v0.11.9). This preprocessing step is essential to ensure that subsequent analyses are based on high-quality data, thus improving the reliability of the results. The high-quality reads, termed ‘clean data’, were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 software (v2.1.0) with strand-specific parameters set to --rna-strandness rf --fr. Samtools (v1.9) was used to process BAM files. Post-alignment, the expression levels of genes were quantified in terms of Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM), offering a normalized measure of gene expression. Finally, the HTSeq-count tool (v0.11.2) was used for counting reads aligned to each gene.

## Immunofluorescence staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Cells were seeded into confocal-specific culture dishes and grown until reaching 50–70% confluency. Prior to experimentation, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min to 1 h. After fixation, cells were washed with pre-cooled PBS and underwent permeabilization using a blocking solution containing Triton X-100, followed by incubation at room temperature for 1 h on a shaker. Following removal of the blocking solution, cells were incubated with a primary antibody (diluted between 1:200 and 1:500) overnight at 4 °C or for 1–2 h at room temperature on a shaker. After incubation, cells were washed three times with TBSTx at room temperature on a shaker. Subsequently, cells were incubated with a species-specific secondary immunofluorescent antibody for 1 h at room temperature on a shaker, protected from light. After secondary antibody incubation, cells were washed three times with TBSTx. Cells were then stained with diluted DAPI for 15 min at room temperature to visualize the nucleus, followed by three washes with TBSTx. For Actin-Tracker Red-555 staining, incubation was performed at 37 °C. Finally, a fluorescence quenching agent was added to ensure even coverage at the bottom of the dish, with precautions taken to avoid light exposure throughout the process. For double-staining immunofluorescence, different species of primary antibodies were co-incubated, followed by incubation with corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies using different excitation wavelengths, specifically 488 nm (green light) and 647 nm (red light) in this study.
For IHC, Representative OSBPL3 IHC images and staining annotations were obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA; https://www.proteinatlas.org/). We extracted colorectal cancer tissue entries and normal colon and rectum tissue entries with available categorical staining calls for OSBPL3. The resulting sample counts were CRC: n = 11 and Normal: n = 6. HPA reports categorical IHC staining as Not detected, Low, Medium, or High. For the included entries, the observed categories were Not detected, Low, and Medium. For statistical comparison between CRC and normal, the three original categories (Medium, Low, Not detected) were compared using Fisher’s exact test. P-values are reported as two-sided. All analyses were performed on the HPA-reported calls; no additional in-house staining or re-scoring was performed.

## Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)
The process involved reverse transcription of RNA into cDNA using the Promega GoScript Reverse Transcription Kit, followed by qPCR experiments utilizing SYBR Green and specific primers. In the reverse transcription step, an RT-mix reagent was prepared using components provided in the kit, including nuclease-free water, reaction buffer, MgCl2, Oligo primer, random primer, PCR nucleotide mix, RNase inhibitor, and reverse transcriptase. This reagent was mixed with extracted RNA and incubated in a PCR instrument at 42 °C for 15 min, followed by 70 °C for 15 min. For the qPCR stage, the Thermo PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix was utilized for relative quantification. Each sample underwent four replicates with a 10 μL reaction volume containing SYBR green, cDNA template, forward and reverse primers, and nuclease-free water. The mix was vortexed and centrifuged before fluorescence quantification using Roche’s LightCycler96 PCR instrument. Cycling parameters included initial denaturation, followed by denaturation and annealing/extension cycles. Melting curve analysis was performed post-cycling.
Primer information: GAPDH-F: CAGGAGGCATTGCTGATGAT, GAPDH-R: GAAGGCTGGGGCTCATTT; OSBPL3-F: GTGGCCCTTAAAAGGCTGGC, OSBPL3-R: GAGCCCGACATCAATGCAGC. All primers are synthesized by Shanghai Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd.

## Organoid culture
Clinical tissue samples were stored and transported in Wash Buffer at 4 °C after collection. Upon arrival, tumor tissue fragments, approximately the size of a soybean grain, were placed in a 6 cm culture dish containing 4 ml of Wash Buffer. The tissue was washed three times by rotation, with the Wash Buffer collected after each wash. Subsequently, the washed cancer tissue was transferred to an Eppendorf tube, minced with scissors, and then transferred to a 15 ml centrifuge tube. Tissue digestion solution was added, and the cells were disaggregated by shaking and then digested in a 37 °C water bath. After digestion, the Wash Buffer was added to halt the process. The tissue suspension was filtered through a 70 μm mesh to obtain tumor cells, which were then transferred to a 15 ml centrifuge tube and centrifuged at a low temperature of 1800 rpm for 12 min. Following centrifugation, the cells were resuspended, and lysed red blood cells were added. A solution of red blood cell lysis was added to the tube, followed by incubation at 37 °C for one minute. Wash Buffer was added, and the mixture was centrifuged to obtain a cell pellet. The cell pellet was then transferred to a 1.5 ml Eppendorf tube, where 100 μL of matrix gel was added. The cells were gently blown into the matrix gel and seeded in a 48-well plate. After seeding, the matrix gel was solidified in a 37 °C environment, and 200 μL of organoid culture medium was added. All patient samples utilized in this study were obtained with the appropriate approval and consent from our university’s ethics center.

## Organoid passaging
To pass the organoids, the culture medium is first discarded, and the organoids are washed twice with Wash Buffer. Subsequently, the organoids are digested using treble digestive fluid, with 200 μL of the digestive fluid added, and digestion is carried out at 37 °C for five minutes. After digestion, the cells and matrix gel are mixed and centrifuged at 1800 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant is then discarded, and an appropriate amount of matrix gel is added before re-seeding the organoids in a new well plate.

## Organoid proliferation experiment
In our study, we employed the Cell Titer method, widely used in organoid research, to evaluate organoid proliferation. This method relies on detecting ATP content within cells, which can penetrate the matrix gel. The process involves preparing the Cell Titer reagent by mixing the substrate with the buffer and adding it to a 96-well plate. After shaking for 2 min and incubating for 10 min at room temperature, the luminescence of each well is measured using an ELISA reader.

For protein interaction predictions, the HitPredict database is utilized for queries . Statistical analyses used two-sided tests unless noted. Kaplan–Meier survival differences were assessed by two-sided log-rank tests. For two-group comparisons of continuous variables, independent-samples t-tests were applied when normality and homoscedasticity held; otherwise, Wilcoxon rank-sum (Mann–Whitney U) tests were used. Categorical IHC data (HPA calls: Not detected/Low/Medium) were compared between tumor and normal by two-sided Fisher’s exact test using HPA-reported categories without re-scoring. Correlations between expression measures, pathway activity, and IC50 values were evaluated by Pearson correlation with two-sided p-values. Cell-cycle distributions were analyzed by two-way ANOVA with appropriate post hoc comparisons. Sample sizes are given in figure legends, and significance is reported as **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, and n.s. as not significant.
