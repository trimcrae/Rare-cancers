## ABSTRACT
Intracellular persistence caused by Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is among the primary reasons for recurrence and difficulty in eradicating S. aureus infections. In this study, we identify the secreted protein Hla (α-hemolysin) by S. aureus as a key factor enabling its intracellular retention. We demonstrate that intracellular Hla secreted by S. aureus inhibits lysosome degradation via disrupting lysosomal function, which sustains the survival and proliferation of S. aureus within autophagosomes. Furthermore, we identify the interaction between Hla and intracellular LGALS3 (galectin 3) as crucial for sustaining intracellular survival of S. aureus, resolve the structure of the Hla-LGALS3 complex, and identify the Loop 68–75 region of Hla as the key binding domain with LGALS3. Moreover, the interaction between Hla and LGALS3 influences the recruitment of PDCD6IP/ALIX (programmed cell death 6 interacting protein) to the damaged lysosomal surface, resulting in disruption of lysosomal degradative function. Our results highlight an unknown role of Hla in the intracellular survival of S. aureus and suggest that interrupting the interaction between Hla and LGALS3 May be a potential therapeutic strategy for treating S. aureus infections.Abbreviations: 3 MA: 3-methyladenine; AECII: alveolar epithelial cells II; Agr: accessory gene regulator; ATG13: autophagy related 13; Baf A1: bafilomycin A1; BLI: biolayer interferometry; CFU: colony-forming units; ClfA: clumping factor A; Co-IP: co-immunoprecipitation; CRD: carbohydrate recognition domain; ER: endoplasmic reticulum; ESCRT: endosomal sorting complex required for transport; FnbA: fibronectin-binding protein A; FnbB: fibronectin-binding protein B; Hla: α-hemolysin; IP-MS: immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry; LGALS3: galectin 3; LLoMe: L-leucyl-L-leucine methyl ester hydrobromide; LMP: lysosomal-membrane permeabilization; MOI: multiplicity of infection; PDCD6IP/ALIX: programmed cell death 6 interacting protein; S. aureus: Staphylococcus aureus; SPA: staphylococcal protein A; SSPA: staphylococcal surface protein A; TEM: transmission electron microscopy; TRAF3: TNF receptor associated factor 3; ULK1: unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1.

## Introduction
S. aureus is one of the most infamous and widespread bacterial pathogens leading to fatal pneumonia, and infections of surgical wounds, prosthetic joints, and of the cardiovascular system [ , ]. Previously regarded solely as an extracellular pathogen, S. aureus has been reclassified as a non-traditional facultative intracellular pathogen capable of surviving in both professional and nonprofessional phagocytic cells [ ]. Failure to treat and control S. aureus infection is closely related to the intracellular persistence of this pathogen, which leads to low response rates to antibiotics, immune evasion, and the formation of a reservoir of pathogens for recurrent infections [ ].
The secreted proteins of S. aureus play a crucial role in its pathogenesis against its infected host. Ess-dependent secreted proteins plays a role in either promoting or inhibiting the production of certain cytokines during host infection [ ], and secretion of Efb acts to hinder the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and dampen host immune responses through interaction with TRAF3 (TNF receptor associated factor 3) [ ]. However, whether the secreted protein can modulate the intracellular persistence of S. aureus is unclear.
Macroautophagy/autophagy is recognized as a pathway of the innate immune response, serving to target intracellular bacteria located in the cytosol, damaged vacuoles, or
phagosomes, thereby mediating the clearance of intracellular bacteria by transporting them to lysosomes for degradation [ ]. Although host autophagy serves as an intracellular degradation pathway that targets S. aureus, the accessory gene regulatory system in S. aureus, which governs its virulence factors, can counteract host immune mechanisms by avoiding and potentially exploiting autophagy for its replication [ , ]. It has been demonstrated that factors regulated by the Agr (accessory gene regulator) system, which activates autophagy, can inhibit the maturation of phagosomes containing S. aureus in human epithelial cells [ ]. In line with this, in the absence of Agr there is a marked decrease in phagosomal escape and autophagosome accumulation, leading to a significant reduction in the intracellular bacterial load [ , ]. Although evidence suggests that S. aureus can modulate host cell autophagy, the precise molecular mechanisms are still incompletely understood.
Lysosomes are important organelles that enable host cells to resist pathogenic invasion. Diverse pathogens inflict lysosomal-membrane permeabilization (LMP) via multiple, well-defined mechanisms during intracellular infection. Listeria secrete the pore-forming toxin listeriolysin O to damage the lysosomal membrane and escape into the cytosol for propagation [ ], Mycobacterium and Toxoplasma block the fusion of lysosomes with phagosomes or parasitophorous vacuoles, thereby avoiding degradation [ ]. Damaged lysosomes expose lumenal glycans that are rapidly sensed by galectins, which mark injury sites and scaffold endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) components to reseal lesions [ ]. However, whether cellular invasion by S. aureus induces lysosomal damage and affects the lysosomal-repair process remains to be fully elucidated.
In the present study, we identify that α-hemolysin (Hla), a well-known pore-forming cytotoxin of S. aureus, promotes the intracellular survival of S. aureus through an inhibitory effect on autophagic degradation, and define its underlying molecular mechanisms. Our findings demonstrate that S. aureus secretes Hla intracellularly to disrupt lysosomal membrane stability, thereby impairing lysosomal degradation.
LGALS3 (galectin 3) acts as a sentinel of lysosomal damage by recognizing exposed lumenal glycans on ruptured phagosomal, autophagosomal and lysosomal membranes and rapidly accumulate at injury sites [ ]. On recruitment, LGALS3 recurrently recruits ESCRT components to promote membrane sealing and restore compartmental integrity. When damage exceeds immediate repair, LGALS3 helps couple the repair response to autophagy initiation and lysophagic clearance of irreparably damaged organelles [ , ]. Our mechanistic studies revealed direct interaction between Hla and LGALS3. Through cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) analysis, we elucidated the structure of the Hla-LGALS3 interaction, demonstrating its impact on the recruitment of membrane repair protein PDCD6IP to lysosomes by LGALS3. Consequently, damaged lysosomes within the cell remained unrepaired, leading to an inability for autophagosomes to undergo degradation, and thus facilitating the intracellular survival of S. aureus.
Overall, our study demonstrates for the first time the mechanism by which Hla promotes intracellular survival of S. aureus, and indicates that targeting the interaction of Hla and LGALS3 May be a potential therapeutic strategy for the treatment of persistent S. aureus infections.

## Results

## S. aureus preferentially resides within lung epithelial cells and prolongs the duration of antibiotic use in pneumonia patients
To elucidate the relationship between the intracellular persistence of S. aureus and the progression to staphylococcal pneumonia, we collected lesion tissues from 35 patients clinically diagnosed with S. aureus pneumonia. The process of case collection and classification is illustrated in . Interestingly, patients with intracellular S. aureus had a significantly prolonged duration of antibiotic use compared to those without intracellular S. aureus (median, 35 vs. 10 days; Table S1, ) and slow absorption of inflammatory lesions as measured by CT) scans ( ). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis of patient samples revealed the presence of S. aureus within epithelial cells and macrophages ( ). Noteworthily, intracellular bacterial burdens within lung epithelial cells were significantly higher than within alveolar macrophages ( ). These findings suggest that in S. aureus-induced pneumonia, the bacteria tend to proliferate within epithelial cells rather than alveolar macrophages, indicating lung epithelial cells are the preferential niche for bacterial replication.

 

## Hla promots intracellular survival of S. aureus in lung epithelial cells
In vitro bacterial-infection experiments demonstrated that the number of bacteria within lung epithelial cells gradually increased with prolonged infection time but significantly decreased within macrophages 4 h post-infection ( ), prompting us to establish subsequent infection models in lung epithelium. S. aureus virulence factors play a critical role in resisting host defenses and promoting bacterial infection [ ]. To investigate the impact of S. aureus virulence factors on its intracellular survival, 38 eukaryotic-expression plasmids encoding various S. aureus virulence factors were constructed and transfected into A549 human lung epithelial cells. A pre‑screening LDH (lactate dehydrogenase) release assay showed that A549 viability was unaffected at a 0.5-µg plasmid dose, whereas 1–2 µg of pore‑forming toxin plasmids (Hla, Hlb, HlgA, or HlgB) reduced viability (Figure S1A). Therefore, we used the 0.5-µg dose for the experiments. The infection process is shown in Figure S1B. Analysis of the effects of these virulence factors on the intracellular survival of two S. aureus strains (NCTC8325, a standard strain; MRSA252, a clinically isolated methicillin-resistant S. aureus strain) demonstrated that cellular expression of Hla led to the highest intracellular bacterial load (Figure S1C, D), suggesting that Hla plays
a predominant role in promoting intracellular survival amongst critical S. aureus virulence factors.

 To validate whether Hla facilitates intracellular survival of S. aureus in both primary lung epithelial cells and cell lines, murine alveolar epithelial cells II (AECII) were isolated and authenticated (Figure S1E). Subsequently, three kinds of S. aureus strains-wild type (WT), Hla deletion (Δhla), and Hla complementation (Δhla:hla)-were constructed (Figure S1F, G) and used to infect A549 or AECII cells, followed by quantification of intracellular bacteria. Immunoblot analysis showed that Hla could be secreted within the cells (Figure S1H). The in vitro growth rates of WT, Δhla, and Δhla:hla
were not significantly different (Figure S1I). As shown in , the intracellular bacterial burden increased in WT- and Δhla:hla-infected cells over time while decreasing in the Δhla-infected group. TEM analysis revealed that the intracellular bacterial burden in WT- and Δhla:hla-infected cells were significantly higher than in the Δhla group 4 h post-infection ( ). Given that intracellular bacterial numbers depend on both the efficiency of bacterial internalization and the ability to clear pathogenic bacteria, we next examined the expression of critical proteins involved in the internalization process of S. aureus, such as cell wall-anchored proteins (FnBA, FnBB, and ClfA), staphylococcal protein A (Spa), and staphylococcal surface protein A (SspA). Deletion and complementation of the Hla gene did not affect the expression of internalization-related genes (Figure S2A-E). Concurrently, leupeptin, a naturally occurring protease
inhibitor that broadly inhibits cellular degradation functions, was used to analyze the pathogen-clearing ability of cells against different S. aureus strains. As shown in Figure S2F-G, cells pretreated with leupeptin and then infected with the three strains showed gradually increasing intracellular bacterial numbers over time, with no significant difference between WT, Δhla:hla, and Δhla strains. These results suggest that there is no difference in the internalization efficiency of pulmonary epithelial cells toward different S. aureus strains and that Hla may instead affect the degradative ability of cells to promote intracellular survival.
We next examined the functional role of Hla in the intracellular survival of S. aureus in a mouse model by administering bacterial antibiotic pressure to allow entry into lung cells. Epcam was used as an epithelial cell marker to quantify bacterial burden specifically in lung epithelial cells ( ). Notably, Hla depletion in S. aureus resulted in a reduced intracellular number of bacteria in mouse lung epithelial cells at 3 weeks post-infection ( ). Histopathological analysis of lung sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin revealed that infection with either the WT or Δhla:hla strain produced markedly more-severe pulmonary injury than infection with the Δhla strain (Figure S2H, I). Consistently, enumeration of lung homogenates demonstrated significantly higher bacterial burdens in mice infected with the WT or Δhla:hla strain than in those infected with the Δhla strain (Figure S2J). Taken together, these data indicate that Hla promotes the intracellular survival of S. aureus in vitro and in vivo.

## Hla inhibits autophagic degradation in lung epithelial cells
To further investigate the mechanism by which Hla promotes the intracellular survival of S. aureus, mass spectrometry was used to identify the alterations in protein expression between WT- and Δhla-infected cells ( ). ATG4B (autophagy related 4B cysteine peptidase), a MAP1LC3/LC3 (microtubule associated protein 1 light chain 3)-processing protease and an autophagosome marker, SQSTM1/p62 (sequestosome 1), an autophagy receptor and UBQLN1 (ubiquilin 1), a ubiquitin-proteostasis/autophagy receptor were upregulated, while CTSD (cathepsin D), a lysosomal aspartyl protease and RAB7A (RAB7A, member RAS oncogene family), which is involved in late endosome/lysosome trafficking, were downregulated. KEGG pathway enrichment showed that the differentially expressed proteins were mainly enriched within the autophagy pathway ( ).

 We first examined whether Hla blocks the initiation of autophagy by assessing key components of the autophagy initiation complex, ATG13 (autophagy related 13) and ULK1 (unc-51 like autophagy activating kinase 1). As infection progressed, phosphorylated ATG13 (p-ATG13) decreased while total ATG13 increased; meanwhile, p-ULK1 (S555) increased, p-ULK1 (S757) decreased, and total ULK1 increased in both the WT and Δhla infection groups (Figure S3A, B), indicating that Hla did not impair autophagy initiation. Building on prior evidence that S. aureus can subvert host autophagy to support intracellular proliferation and caspase-independent cell death [ ], we examined the influence of Hla on autophagic flux during S. aureus infection using the mCherry-GFP-LC3B reporter plasmid to monitor LC3 aggregation in autophagosomes and autolysosomes. The mCherry-GFP-LC3B puncta exhibit yellow fluorescence in pH-neutral autophagosomes and red fluorescence in low pH autolysosomes (loss of pH-sensitive GFP signal; ). In WT-infected cells, LC3-positive bacteria-containing compartments remained non-acidified (appearing yellow in the tandem mRFP-GFP-LC3 reporter), indicating stalled autophagosomes that had not entered the degradative phase. By contrast, in Δhla-infected cells, LC3+ bacteria-containing compartments were acidified (appearing red), consistent with productive fusion and degradation ( ). TEM detection revealed a significant increase in the number of autophagosomes in cells transfected with Hla expression plasmids compared to both control groups and cells transfected with vector plasmids ( ). Analysis of autophagy-related genes revealed that compared to the group infected with Δhla, the WT strain exhibited a significant increase in the autophagy markers LC3-II and the autophagic substrate protein SQSTM1 with extended infection time ( ), which suggests that autophagosomes in the WT group were not efficiently degraded, leading to the accumulation of autophagic substrate proteins. Pretreatment with rapamycin (initiates autophagosome formation) and bafilomycin A1 (Baf A1, inhibits autophagosome degradation) in Hla-transfected cells also led to increased levels of LC3-II and SQSTM1, while treatment with 3-methyladenine (3-MA, inhibits autophagosome formation) decreased the levels of these two proteins (Figure S3C, D). Taken together, these data indicate that Hla may suppress the autophagic flux during S. aureus infection.
To determine the impact of cellular autophagy on the intracellular survival of S. aureus, experiments using inhibitors targeting different stages of autophagy were performed. Use of the autophagy initiation inhibitor 3-MA significantly reduced the intracellular bacterial load compared to the untreated group (Figure S3E-G). Conversely, inhibition of autophagosome degradation with Baf A1 resulted in a markedly higher intracellular bacterial count compared to the control group (Figure S3H-J). These findings suggest a dual role of cellular autophagy in bacterial intracellular survival, wherein inhibiting autophagosome formation is detrimental to bacterial intracellular survival, while inhibiting autophagosome degradation favors bacterial intracellular survival. Taken together, Hla could inhibit autophagy degradation in lung epithelial cells, therefore facilitating intracellular survival of S. aureus.

## Hla disrupts lysosomal acidity and degradative function
The factors influencing the degradation process of autophagosomes primarily fall into two categories: those affecting the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes and those impacting lysosomal functionality [ ]. First, the fusion of lysosomes and autophagosomes was detected by immunofluorescence, which showed no significant difference in the colocalization of autophagosomes and lysosomes between the WT- and Δhla-infected cells (Figure S4A, B).
However, the lysosome-specific tracer LysoTracker labeling revealed distinct patterns between the WT- and Δhla-infected groups. In the WT-infected group, Lysotracker fluorescence
was dispersed throughout the cells, whereas in the Δhla-infected group, the fluorescence was more concentrated (Figure S4C). Further analysis showed significantly lower LysoTracker fluorescence intensity in the WT-infected group compared to the Δhla-infected group (Figure S4D, E). Given that Lysotracker is pH-sensitive, we hypothesized that during S. aureus infection, Hla may alter lysosomal acidity, thereby affecting lysosomal functionality.
We next assessed lysosomal degradation activity using the self-quenched fluorophore DQ-BSA. Results revealed that, similar to the results of bafilomycin A1 treatment, cells infected with WT exhibited significantly lower red fluorescence intensity compared to cells infected with Δhla ( , B), indicating decreased BSA hydrolysis in the WT-infected group, suggesting that Hla impairs lysosomal degradative activity.

 The integrity of lysosomal membranes is crucial for maintaining lysosomal function [ ]. We assessed the impact of Hla on lysosomal membrane integrity using acridine orange staining (measured as red fluorescence intensity). As shown in Figure S4F, the red fluorescence inside the cells was significantly lower in WT-infected cells than in Δhla-infected cells. As infection time increased, red fluorescence intensity inside WT-infected cells gradually decreased, while it increased in Δhla-infected cells (Figure S4G, H). Measurements of lysosomal diameter showed that WT-infected cells had a significantly larger lysosomal diameter than in the control and Δhla-infected groups ( , D). These results suggest that Hla may increase permeability of lysosomal membranes, leading to lysosomal swelling and disrupted lysosomal function.
To further assess lysosomal function, lysosomal pH was evaluated using the fluorescent LysoSensor. Strikingly, lysosomal pH of cells infected with the WT strain (pH = 5.48 ± 0.02) was increased compared to Δhla-infected cells (pH = 4.56 ± 0.05) ( ). Cumulatively, the findings suggest that Hla destabilizes lysosomal membranes and increases membrane permeability, leading to comprised lysosomal acidification and destroyed lysosomal degradative ability.

## Hla accumulates at lysosomes and interacts with LGALS3 carbohydrate recognition domain
Intracellular bacteria typically secrete proteins into cytoplasm, where these proteins can translocate to specific organelles and interact with intracellular target proteins, affecting the physiological functions of the host cell [ ]. Using TEM and immunofluorescence detection, we confirmed that phagosomes containing S. aureus had gaps, allowing Hla to be secreted outside these phagosomes/autophagosomes (Figure S5A, B). In Hla eukaryotic-expression plasmid-transfected cells, Hla was mainly detected on lysosomes, with minimal levels detected on mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and Golgi complex (Figure S5C). Native gel immunoblotting analysis showed that Hla formed heptamers (pore-forming structures) on lysosomes, suggesting that after breaching phagosomes (autophagosomes), Hla could further damage the lysosomal membrane (Figure S5 D).
To identify intracellular targets of Hla, immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) analyses were utilized ( ). SiRNA-mediated knockdown was used to selectively screen impacting the intracellular survival of S. aureus among the top 10 target proteins identified and found; the results showed that LGALS3 knockdown markedly increased the number of intracellular bacteria (Figure S5E, F).

 When lysosomal-membrane damage occurs, cytosolic LGALS3 translocates to the injured lysosomal surface, where it coordinates ESCRT-mediated repair [ ]. We hypothesized that LGALS3 May be a key target of Hla in promotion of intracellular survival of S. aureus ( ). Indeed, this interaction was confirmed using co-immunoprecipitation and confocal analyses in both A549 cells and mouse primary AECII cells ( ). Further, we found that Hla could co-localize with LGALS3 in cells infected with Hla complemented strains (Figure S6G). Direct interaction between Hla and LGALS3 was demonstrated by purifying the proteins and testing their interaction in vitro (Figure S5G). Additionally, biolayer interferometry (BLI) analysis revealed a high affinity between the two proteins, with an affinity of 1.98 nM ( ).
To confirm whether Hla promotes intracellular survival of S. aureus through interaction with LGALS3, we generated LGALS3 knockout A549 cells and conditionally knocked out LGALS3 in murine AECII (Figure S5H). In vitro infection experiments revealed that the difference in intracellular survival between WT and Δhla strains in lgals3 KO A549 cells was abolished. It was observed that the intracellular bacterial load was significantly higher in cells infected with S. aureus after LGALS3 knockout compared to wild-type cells (Figure S5I). In vivo experiments also demonstrated that the difference in bacterial quantities between the WT and Δhla strains in mouse lung epithelial cells was reduced upon LGALS3 knockout (Figure S5J). Collectively, these data indicated that Hla directly interacted with LGALS3 and therefore promoted intracellular survival of S. aureus.
Structural analysis of LGALS3 revealed an unstructured region spanning amino acids 1–117 [ ]. Based on prior structural analyses of LGALS3 that excluded this region, a truncated variant comprising amino acids 118–250 was constructed. Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) confirmed that Hla bound to the LGALS3 carbohydrate recognition domain (CRD) rather than the N terminus 1–117 region ( , K). In vitro interaction assays corroborated the CRD of LGALS3 as the pivotal domain mediating interaction with Hla, with BLI data supporting the high affinity between
LGALS3 (CRD) and Hla (Figure S5K, L). The sugar-binding site within the LGALS3 CRD is essential for glycan recognition [ , ]. We therefore tested whether five key glycan-binding residues (His158, Arg162, Asn174, Trp181, and Arg186) of LGALS3 are critical for its interaction with Hla. Mutating these residues had minimal impact on Hla-LGALS3 binding (Figure S5M), indicating that the determinants mediating Hla-LGALS3 interaction were distinct from those required for LGALS3-glycan recognition.

## Hla binds to the carbohydrate recognition domain of LGALS3 via Loop 68–75 at the RIM region
To elucidate the interaction mechanisms between Hla and LGALS3, we employed cryo-EM to resolve the structural complex formed by these proteins. Initially, we analyzed the structural forms of Hla and LGALS3 in solution, noting that Hla exists as both monomers and heptamers, whereas LGALS3 is solely monomeric [ ]. BLI results indicated a higher affinity of LGALS3 for the Hla heptamer, with negligible binding to the Hla monomer (Figure S6A, B).
The Hla heptamer was mixed with LGALS3 (CRD) and allowed to interact overnight at 4°C. Hla-LGALS3 complexes were isolated using size-exclusion chromatography, confirmed via SDS-PAGE analysis, and prepared for cryo-EM (Figure S6C-E). After 2D classification and 3D refinement, a 2.46 Å resolution density map was determined (Figure S7). Previously published atomic models of Hla (PDB_ID: 4M1G) and LGALS3 (PDB_ID: 1A3K) were fitted into the map, revealing the overall conformations ( ). The elucidated structure demonstrated that the RIM domain of the Hla heptamer interacts with LGALS3. Due to the flexibility of the interaction, high-resolution maps could not be obtained. However, the fitted crystal structures of Hla and LGALS3 showed a high degree of matching. Using cryo-EM, we discovered that Hla bound to LGALS3 (CRD) via its loop region composed of amino acids 68–75 in the RIM region ( ).

 To assess the importance of Loop 68–75 in the Hla-LGALS3 interaction, we examined their affinity using BLI upon mutation of Loop 68–75, in which all amino acid residues within this loop were substituted with alanine. The results demonstrated a rapid loss of affinity between Hla and LGALS3 upon mutation of Loop 68–75 ( ). This was corroborated by co-IP and immunofluorescence assays ( ), confirming a significant decrease in the interaction following Loop 68–75 mutation. Next, we found that infection with S. aureus mutated at Hla Loop 68–75 did not affect the alter of ULK1 or ATG13 during the autophagy initiation stage (Figure S6F-G), while levels of LC3-II and SQSTM1 in cells were lower compared with those of cells infected with the WT Hla strain, indicating that the Loop 68–75 mutation impaired Hla’s ability to inhibit autophagic degradation ( ). Moreover, this mutation restored the acidity (Figure S6I-J) and function of the lysosome (Figure S6K-N). Despite no significant differences in the in vitro growth curves of the four bacterial strains ( ), we found that the Hla Loop 68–75 mutation could not promote intracellular survival of S. aureus (Figure S6J-M). Taken together, our data showed that Loop 68–75 was important for binding with LGALS3 and sustaining the intracellular survival promotion function of Hla.

## The interaction between Hla and LGALS3 influences the recruitment of LGALS3 to PDCD6IP
As repair of damaged lysosomal membranes requires LGALS3 translocation to the lysosomal surface, we investigated the impact of Hla on LGALS3 translocation using immunofluorescence and lysosome-immunoprecipitation assays. Transfection of cells with the Hla plasmid did not affect the colocalization of LGALS3 with lysosomes induced by L-leucyl-L-leucine methyl ester hydrobromide (LLoMe) (Figure S8A-C). Similarly, infection with WT and Δhla strains did not affect the colocalization of LGALS3 with lysosomes (Figure S8D-F). These results suggest that Hla does not influence LGALS3 translocation to lysosomes during lysosomal membrane damage. Given its impact on lysosomal function and interaction with LGALS3, we investigated Hla binding to LGALS3 on lysosomes. After transfection with the Hla plasmid, intracellular Hla co-localized with lysosomes (Figure S8G, H). Co-immunoprecipitation demonstrated a direct interaction between Hla and LGALS3 on lysosomes (Figure S8I). Cumulatively, these results suggest that the Hla-LGALS3 interaction on the lysosomal surface influences LGALS3-mediated repair during lysosomal membrane damage.
The functional role of LGALS3 in mediating lysosomal membrane repair relies on its recruitment of ESCRT molecules, such as PDCD6IP [ ]. We investigated the impact of Hla on LGALS3’s recruitment of PDCD6IP. Confocal microscopy revealed a significant reduction in the co-localization of PDCD6IP and LGALS3 on lysosomes in cells infected with the WT strain compared to the LLoMe-treated and Δhla-infected groups ( ). Co-immunoprecipitation showed that, with prolonged infection, the binding between PDCD6IP and LGALS3 in the WT infection group was significantly reduced, whereas it persisted in the Δhla infection group ( ). Further, Hla significantly inhibited LGALS3-PDCD6IP binding induced by LLoMe treatment or in cells transfected with an Hla plasmid ( ).

 To directly demonstrate the impact of Hla on LGALS3-PDCD6IP binding, we purified PDCD6IP ( ) and conducted a competitive inhibition experiment. BLI results revealed that after saturation binding of LGALS3 and Hla, subsequent binding with PDCD6IP was inhibited
( , red line). However, LGALS3 could successfully bind to PDCD6IP when not pre-bound to Hla ( , blue line).
Because the Loop 68–75 mutation of Hla abrogated the interaction with LGALS3, we next employed Hla Loop 68–75 mutation complement strain or plasmid to detect interaction between LGALS3 and PDCD6IP. CO-IP analysis revealed that disruption of the Hla-LGALS3 interaction restored the binding ability of LGALS3 and PDCD6IP ( ).
In summary, the interaction between Hla and LGALS3 influences LGALS3 recruitment of PDCD6IP, subsequently inhibiting lysosomal repair by the ESCRT system and disrupting lysosomal function and therefore promoting intracellular survival of S. aureus.

## Discussion
As an invasive facultative intracellular pathogen, S. aureus can occupy diverse niches within host organisms, which renders the host immune system to fully eradicate the pathogen challenging [ , ]. Herein, we demonstrated for the first time that Hla, a well-known secreted protein of S. aureus, inhibited degradation of autophagosomes by impairing lysosomal function, thereby promoting intracellular survival of S. aureus. Mechanistically, the binding of Hla to intracellular LGALS3 on lysosomal membranes impedes recruitment of the lysosomal-membrane repair protein PDCD6IP to LGALS3, thus affecting repair of lysosomal damage induced by S. aureus invasion. Consequently, preventing the clearance of intracellular S. aureus within autophagosomes leads to persistent infection ( ).

 Host cell autophagy plays a crucial role in clearing intracellular S. aureus [ , ]. In the present study, we observed a larger intracellular bacterial load in lung epithelial cells than in alveolar macrophages ( ). This apparent paradox likely reflects distinct post‑engulfment fates. Alveolar macrophages avidly phagocytose S. aureus yet activate potent phagolysosome-killing mechanisms that eliminate most bacteria within hours, reducing the net intracellular pool [ , ]. By contrast, epithelial cells are nonprofessional phagocytes with weak antimicrobial response, leading S. aureus to preferentially conceal itself within lung epithelial cells rather than alveolar macrophages.
During the interaction with host cells, the secreted proteins of S. aureus are involved in hijacking host immunity and mediating its escape from autophagic degradation [ ]. Previous studies have reported that Hla activates autophagy in a PI3K/Becline1-independent manner [ , ]. However, the autophagy process comprises both autophagy induction and autophagosome degradation [ ]. Whether Hla affects autophagosome degradation and its underlying molecular mechanisms remain to be investigated. In the present study, we revealed that Hla could inhibit autophagosomal degradation via compromised lysosomal function.
Lysosomes are cellular organelles primarily responsible for degradation, receiving substances from endocytosis, phagocytosis, and autophagy pathways, and are responsible for nutrient sensing and cholesterol homeostasis [ ]. Additionally, lysosomes serve as a key component of the intracellular defense system against invading pathogens via accelerated lysosomal biogenesis and acidification to better
degrade foreign material. Prior studies have reported that pathogenic invasion can activate TFEB (transcription factor EB), a key regulator of lysosomal biogenesis [ , ]. For example, TFEB is rapidly activated in murine macrophages following S. aureus infection and contributes to the induction of innate immune response genes [ ], Salmonella infection has been shown to induce IFNG/IFN-γ-dependent lysosomal acidification [ ]. Specifically, lysosomes have emerged as a primary target for pathogen destruction including induction of various alterations in the lysosomal landscape, LMP or depletion, and redistribution of lysosomes [ ]. The listeriolysin O/LLO of Listeria monocytogenes disrupts lysosomal calcium ion concentrations, thereby enabling the pathogen to escape from the host cell before degradation occurs [ ] and the VacA toxin from intracellular Helicobacter pylori disrupts endolysosomal trafficking, creating a protective intracellular niche [ ]. Herein, we report that Hla increases lysosomal membrane permeability, leading to elevated lysosomal pH levels, thereby affecting the degradative function of lysosomes.
Lysosomal membranes can be damaged in a variety of ways including pathogen (bacteria, virus and fungi) invasion, protein aggregates, and chemical exposure [ , ]. Pores formed by Hla heptamer are sufficient to induce LMP (selectively permeable to small ions and protons), leading to lumenal pH neutralization and ionic redistribution. This ion leak at endolysosomal membranes triggers lysosomal swelling while cells activate repair ( ). After lysosomal membrane damage, cells recruit ESCRT machinery to the injured lysosomes, mediating lysosomal membrane repair [ , ]. Galectins, a family of sugar-binding lectins, play an important role in detecting lysosomal rupture by sensing abnormal glycan exposure to the cytoplasm upon membrane damage [ , , , ]. Meanwhile, as the first protein discovered to bind to bacterial lipopolysaccharides and the sole member of the chimeric subfamily of galectins, LGALS3 exhibits high affinity for recognizing intracellular pathogens [ ]. In this study, we demonstrated that Hla interacts with host LGALS3 on the lysosomal membrane. Deletion of LGALS3 eliminated the survival difference between WT and Δhla within lung epithelial cells. Additionally, mutating the binding domain of Hla, which impedes this interaction, blocks the function of Hla in promoting intracellular survival of S. aureus. These data indicated that the interaction between Hla and LGALS3 affects certain functions of LGALS3 as an intracellular antimicrobial protein, thereby promoting the intracellular survival of S. aureus, extending our understanding of the functional role of S. aureus-secreted protein in pathogen-host interaction. However, because Hla expression is strongly context dependent (e.g., media, oxygen, Agr activity), obtaining an accurate quantitative readout in clinical isolates is challenging, which limited our ability to directly link our clinical observations to the proposed mechanism.
LGALS3 is recognized as a general and sensitive reporter for lysosomal damage that is recruited to the pathogen-induced damaged lysosome and recruits the ESCRT component PDCD6IP to promote lysosomal repair [ ]. Our results demonstrate that Hla forms a complex with LGALS3 on the lysosomal membrane. Through structural biology approaches, we discovered that Hla interacts with the carbohydrate recognition domain (CRD) of LGALS3, thus affecting the ability of LGALS3 to recognize glycans exposed on damaged lysosomes. The binding of LGALS3 to surface glycans of damaged lysosomes is essential for recruiting the ESCRT molecule PDCD6IP [ , ]. Therefore, the interaction between Hla and LGALS3 inhibits the recruitment of PDCD6IP to the surface of damaged lysosomes, consequently impeding the subsequent function of PDCD6IP in lysosomal repair, which provides a novel understanding of the function of Hla apart from disrupting host cell membranes and causing cell lysis.
It is well established that Hla assembles on sphingomyelin-rich membranes via the host receptor ADAM10, and ADAM10-deficient epithelia are refractory to Hla-mediated lung injury [ ]. Our cryo-EM structure was obtained by treating purified Hla with deoxycholate, which drives receptor-free heptamerization and thus captures a fully open pore ready to engage cytosolic partners such as LGALS3. While this detergent-based model enabled us to visualize Hla-LGALS3 interaction in the absence of any host receptors, we acknowledge that membrane lipids or ADAM10 might have imposed additional conformational constraints. Moreover, recent work shows that the deubiquitinates OTULIN restricts cellular sensitivity to Hla [ ], demonstrating that multiple host factors can influence pore formation or downstream signaling. Future nanodisc-based reconstructions and ADAM10- and OTULIN-deficient cell systems will be instrumental to reconciling these receptor-dependent and -independent states.
Collectively, our study revealed the functional role of Hla in promoting the intracellular survival of S. aureus. Mechanistic investigations have provided evidence that the interaction between Hla and LGALS3 influences the ability of LGALS3 to recruit PDCD6IP and repair the lysosomal membrane. This interaction, which disrupts the degradation capability of the lysosome, is essential for promotion of the intracellular survival of S. aureus ( ). Our findings provide insights into the function of Hla during S. aureus infection, and suggest that interruption of the interaction between Hla and LGALS3 May be a potential therapeutic strategy for combatting S. aureus infections.

## Ethics statement
All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Experiment Administration Committee of Army Medical University. Patients enrolled in the present study, which was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xinqiao Hospital (No. YL202-CN-201–01), were recruited from Xinqiao Hospital.

## Cell lines
A549 cells (human lung epithelial cells) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection/ATCC (027). lgals3−/− A549 cells were created by targeting the LGALS3 gene using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing. All cells were cultured in Ham’s F-12K (Kaighn’s) medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 21,127,030) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1,009,941).

## Mice
All mice used in this study were on the C57BL/6 background and purchased from Charles River Laboratories (CCL-185). Mice were raised in specific pathogen free conditions. To generate lung epithelial-cell-specific LGALS3-deficient mice, we hybridized mice that contained loxP sequence flanking the third and fourth exon of LGALS3 (lgals3fl/fl) with Sftpc-CreERT2 mice from Cyagen Biosciences (C001359). Mouse genotypes were identified by PCR following the extraction of genomic DNA from tails, with generated lgals3fl/fl;Stpfc-Cre mice carrying a tamoxifen-induced lgals3 conditional knockout.

## Bacterial strains
S. aureus strain NCTC 8325 was purchased from NCTC and used in this study as the wild-type strain. The Hla-depleted strain (Δhla) was constructed using temperature-sensitive shuttle vector pBT2 (provide by X. Rao, department of microbiology, Army Medical University) with an allelic replacement strategy, and the pBUS-P1-HC vector (provide by Q. Liu, West China Biopharmaceutical Research Institute) was used to complement the Δhla strain with wild-type Hla or Hla (Loop 68–75 mutant; all complement plasmid compromise with a Flag tag in the C terminus of the target gene). Confirmation of the Δhla and Hla complement strains were examined by immunoblot assays. All S. aureus strains were cultivated in trypticase soy broth/agars (BD Biosciences, 257,161). For the purpose of selecting S. aureus strains, chloramphenicol (25 μg/mL; Sangon Biotech, A373735) was utilized. Briefly, bacteria were cultured at a temperature of 37°C in a shaking incubator set to 200 rpm. The bacteria used for cell or animal infections were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 × g, for 10 min, washed twice, and the suspension initially diluted to an absorbance of OD600 nm = 1.0 (109 colony-forming units [CFU]/mL) using a UV/Vis spectrophotometer. Bacterial cultures were then further adjusted to the indicated multiplicity of infection (MOI) for infection experiments.

## Clinical-enrollment criteria

## Inclusion criteria:

Adults age 18–80 years, any sex.
Pneumonia diagnosed according to the Clinical Practice Guideline for Adult Community-Acquired Pneumonia (Emergency Department), 2024 edition, with S. aureus infection confirmed by airway secretion culture or molecular pathogen testing.
Able to provide written informed consent and to understand and comply with study requirements and assessment schedules.

## Exclusion criteria:

Cases with missing key clinical data, including absence of pathological or microbiological evidence, or incomplete imaging data.
Patients unable to complete the clinical study procedures.
Presence of contraindications to bronchoscopy, including but not limited to: active massive hemoptysis; severe hypertension or significant arrhythmias; recent myocardial infarction or unstable angina; severe cardiac or pulmonary dysfunction (except patients already on cardiopulmonary support); uncorrectable bleeding tendency (e.g., severe coagulopathy), uremia, or severe pulmonary hypertension; severe superior vena cava syndrome; conditions in which bronchoscopy was likely to cause laryngeal edema or severe bleeding; suspected aortic aneurysm; multiple pulmonary bullae; or extreme debilitation.
Violation of study requirements or protocol procedures.
Any other condition that, in the investigator’s judgment, made the patient unsuitable for enrollment.

## Clinical case collection and classification
We initially treated patients diagnosed with S. aureus pneumonia with conventional antibiotics for 7 days, including second-generation cephalosporins and linezolid. Subsequently, we performed transbronchial lung biopsies via bronchoscopy to obtain lung tissue from the primary lesion site, and conducted transmission electron microscopy to determine the presence of intracellular S. aureus. Patients were then categorized into two groups: those with intracellular S. aureus and those without. We continued to monitor patients for the duration of antibiotic use until complete resolution of the infection.

## Intracellular bacteria count
Cells were infected with S. aureus at a MOI of 10 for 1 h, followed by removal of extracellular bacteria with lysostaphin (Solarbio Life Sciences, L9070) for 10 min. Cells were then washed three times with PBS (Sangon Biotech, B040100) containing 300 μg/mL gentamicin (Sangon Biotech, A620217). Next, cells were incubated for indicated times and lysed in 200 μl PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sangon Biotech, A110694). The number of viable intracellular bacteria (CFU) was determined by serial dilutions and plating out.

## Lysosomal-pH measurement (LysoSensor yellow/Blue dextran)
Lysosomal pH was measured using LysoSensor Yellow/Blue DND-160 dextran conjugate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, L7545). Briefly, cells were trypsinized, resuspended at 1 × 106 cells/mL, and loaded with 1 mg/mL LysoSensor for 0.5 h at 37°C and 5% CO2. Cells were then washed with PBS, distributed into black 96-well plates, and processed for ratiometric readings on a microplate fluorimeter (Ex, 405 nm; Em, 450 ± 10 nm and 510 ± 10 nm). For each well, the I450:I510 emission ratio was calculated after background subtraction.
For confocal imaging, A549 cells were seeded on glass-bottom dishes 24 h in advance, loaded with LysoSensor-dextran (1 mg/mL, 0.5 h at 37°C and 5% CO2), washed in PBS, and maintained in phenol-red-free HBSS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 14,025–092) in a live-cell chamber. Images were acquired on a laser-scanning confocal microscope using a 405-nm laser; emission was collected in two channels (430–470 nm and 500–550 nm) under identical detector gain/laser settings across conditions.
For pH calibration, parallel wells were incubated for 10 min at 37°C in 2-ethanesulfonic acid (MES) clamp buffer (115 mM KCl, 5 mM NaCl, 1.3 mM MgSO4, 25 mM MES, pH set as indicated) supplemented with monensin (10 µM; Sigma-Aldrich, M5273) and nigericin (30 µM; Sigma-Aldrich, N7143) to equilibrate intra-lysosomal and extracellular pH. Calibration buffers were prepared at pH 4.0, 4.5, 5.0, 5.5, 6.0, and 6.5. The corresponding I450:I510 ratios were used to generate a standard curve (linear fit within this range), which was then applied to convert sample ratios to absolute lysosomal pH.

## Quantification of intracellular bacteria in fluorescence image
Confocal images (GFP-S. aureus) were opened in Fiji, converted to 8-bit, and calibrated. Z-stacks were max-projected. Channels were split; each channel underwent background subtraction and light Gaussian blur. A cell mask was generated from the red channel by fixed global thresholding, binarization, hole filling, and closing (or single-cell regions of interest [ROIs]). Bacteria were segmented in the GFP channel using a fixed threshold and Watershed. Intracellular bacteria were obtained by logical and counted with Analyze Particles (size, 0.05–1.5 μm2; circularity, 0.20–1.00; edges excluded). Counts were reported per cell. All images used identical parameters; analysis was blinded.

## Expression and immunoprecipitation mass spectrometry (MS)

IP-MS: A549 cells were transfected with vector or Hla-Flag plasmids and lysed after 48 h. Lysates were incubated with anti-Flag magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A36797) for 3 h, beads were immobilized, and supernatants were discarded. After five PBST (Sangon Biotech, B640016) washes, on-bead trypsin digestion was performed.
Expression MS: Equal amounts of protein from cells infected with WT or Δhla S. aureus were processed via acetone precipitation. Briefly, lysates were mixed with acetone (1:1), supplemented with four-fold acetone, cooled at 0°C for 2 h, and centrifuged at 4500 g for 5 min. Pellets were washed twice with acetone, air dried, resuspended in 200 mM TEAB (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 90,114), and sonicated. Proteins were digested overnight with trypsin (1:50, enzyme: protein; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 90,058). Reduction was performed with 5 mM DTT (Sangon Biotech, A620058) at 56°C for 30 min, followed by alkylation with 11 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma-Aldrich, I6125) at room temperature for 15 min in the dark.
LC-MS/MS Analysis: Peptides dissolved in 0.1% formic acid/2% acetonitrile were separated on an EASY-nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using a gradient of 4–23% buffer B (0.1% formic acid, 90% acetonitrile) over 62 min, 23–35% B over 20 min, 35–80% B over 4 min, and 80% B over 90 min at 500 nL/min. Ionization was performed in a nano-electrospray ionization (nano-ESI) source and analyzed on a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer. The spray voltage was 2.1 kV. MS1 and MS2 spectra were acquired in the Orbitrap at resolutions of 120,000 and 15,000, respectively. Data-dependent acquisition selected the top 10 precursors for HCD fragmentation at 28% collision energy. AGC was set at 5 × 104, signal threshold at 2.5 × 105 ions/s, maximum injection time at 40 ms, and dynamic exclusion at 30 s.

## In vivo and in vitro infection experiments

In vivo infection experiment: Cells were seeded in dishes one day before infection. Cells were infected (OD600 = 1.0) with bacterial suspensions adjusted to a MOI = 10 for 1 h, and extracellular bacteria removed using lysostaphin.
In vitro infection experiment: Female C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were challenged with indicated S. aureus strains intra-tracheally (5 × 107 CFU per mouse) and then administrated vancomycin (intranasal, 50 mg/kg; Selleck Chemicals, S2575) and gentamycin (intraperitoneal, 100 mg/kg) once a day for three days to clear extracellular bacteria. After three rounds of infection and antibiotic treatment, mice were euthanized and lung tissues collected for downstream immunofluorescence experiments.

## Protein expression and purification
E. coli B21 (DE3) cells harboring plasmids that encode the GST-tagged Hla, His-tagged LGALS3, and LGALS3 (CRD) constructs were cultivated in LB medium (Sangon Biotech, A507005) until they reached an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.8. The expression of each protein was triggered by the addition of 0.4 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG; Solarbio Life Sciences, I8070), and the cultures were subsequently incubated overnight at 16°C. The bacterial cells were harvested through centrifugation, and the resulting pellets were resuspended in PBS. Subsequently, the cells were homogenized using a high-pressure homogenizer, followed by another round of centrifugation at 10,000 × g to collect the supernatants. These supernatants were then subjected to glutathione Sepharose 4B (Cytiva, 17,075,605) for the purification of GST-Hla (which necessitates digestion with PreScission protease (Cytiva, 45,001,319) after binding with the affinity resin) and Ni-Sepharose 6FF (Cytiva, 17,531,804) for the purification of His-LGALS3/LGALS3(CRD) (which require elution using 100 mM imidazole-PBS after resin washing with 20 mM imidazole). The proteins were purified further using HiTrap Q HP ion-exchange and Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL (GE Healthcare Life Sciences) chromatography.

## Statistical analyses
Data from separate experiments were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). After the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test, all data were subjected to parametric or non-parametric testing. An unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test was used for two-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA for multiple group comparisons, and two-way ANOVA for comparing different groups. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. All experimental results were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software.
