## Abstract

## Background
Perioperative neurocognitive disorder (PND), a prevalent neurological complication in elderly patients following anesthesia and surgery, has recently been linked to ferroptosis as a central pathogenic mechanism. Sirtuin-3 (SIRT3), a NAD+-dependent deacetylase, exhibits neuroprotective properties in neurodegenerative disorders, including PND. However, its role in neuronal ferroptosis during PND remains unclear. This study investigates the impact of SIRT3 on ferroptosis modulation in PND and its underlying mechanism.

## Methods
A murine model of PND was established using tibial fracture surgery under isoflurane anesthesia to assess SIRT3 expression and cognitive function. Mice were treated with Honokiol (HKL) or erastin to evaluate hippocampal ferroptosis. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed to identify the underlying neuroprotective mechanisms. In vitro, PC12 and HT22 cells were treated with erastin or HKL to analyze ferroptosis markers. GPX4 silencing in HT22 cells was used to validate the effect of HKL in modulating ferroptosis. Adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated overexpression of SIRT3 and Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), were employed to further elucidate its mechanism in suppressing ferroptosis.

## Results
SIRT3 expression was found to be reduced in the hippocampal CA1 and CA3 regions post-surgery. HKL alleviated cognitive decline by inhibiting ferroptosis, evidenced by suppression of iron accumulation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction. In erastin-treated PC12 and HT22 cells, HKL effectively counteracted ferroptosis, which was abolished by GPX4 silencing. SIRT3 overexpression in the mouse hippocampus suppressed anesthesia/surgery-induced neuronal ferroptosis. Mechanistically, HKL-activated SIRT3 upregulated mitochondrial GPX4 expression and reduced its acetylation, thereby inhibiting neuronal ferroptosis.

## Conclusions
SIRT3 activation by HKL alleviates hippocampal neuronal ferroptosis in PND by suppressing mitochondrial GPX4 acetylation, providing a novel therapeutic strategy for the management of PND.

## Introduction
Perioperative neurocognitive disorder (PND), encompassing preoperative and postoperative cognitive dysfunction, is a common complication in older patients following anesthesia and surgery [ ]. Characterized by deficits in memory, attention, and executive function [ ], PND is associated with prolonged hospitalization and increased mortality risk [ ]. Neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, synaptic plasticity impairment, blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption, and neuronal damage are widely recognized as the primary pathogenic mechanisms of PND [ , ]. Specially, anesthesia and surgery in mice activate microglia, triggering the release of inflammatory factors, including interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α. This leads to altered BBB permeability, promoting oxidative stress and neuronal apoptosis [ ]. Recently studies have highlighted the association between ferroptosis and PND [ , ]. Ferroptosis, a form of programmed cell death driven by iron-catalyzed lipid peroxidation [ ], has been shown to increase iron content and alter iron metabolism proteins in the hippocampus following abdominal surgery [ ]. Sevoflurane has also been reported to induce neurotoxicity and cognitive dysfunction by triggering ferroptosis in hippocampal neurons [ , ]. Therefore, targeting ferroptosis may present a promising therapeutic approach for PND [ ]. However, the mechanisms of underlying ferroptosis in hippocampal neurons during PND remain poorly understood.
Sirtuin-3 (SIRT3), a mitochondrial NAD+-dependent deacetylase associated with longevity [ ], mitigates oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in neurodegenerative diseases [ , ]. In PND, SIRT3 has been shown to attenuate hippocampal neuroinflammation and cognitive decline [ ], and ameliorates sevoflurane-induced postoperative cognitive impairment through mitochondrial redox regulation [ ]. Notably, SIRT3 inhibits ferroptosis by deacetylating and stabilizing glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a critical enzyme that prevents lipid peroxidation [ ]. However, the role of SIRT3 in neuronal ferroptosis during PND remains unexplored. Honokiol (HKL), a bioactive compound derived from Magnolia species, exerts neuroprotective effects in neurodegenerative diseases primarily through SIRT3 activation [ , ]. In Alzheimer’s disease (AD), HKL improves synaptic plasticity and reduces amyloid β-protein (Aβ) toxicity via SIRT3-dependent mitochondrial biogenesis [ ]. Additionally, HKL attenuates high glucose-induced peripheral neuropathy by inhibiting ferroptosis [ ], providing a therapeutic target for ferroptosis. However, the effects of HKL on ferroptosis in PND remains unelucidated.
In this study, we demonstrated that hippocampal SIRT3 expression was downregulated post-surgery and identified HKL, a SIRT3 activator, as a protector against anesthesia/surgery-induced cognitive impairment through the inhibition of ferroptosis. HKL reduced iron accumulation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction in PND mice, while erastin (a ferroptosis inducer) reversed these effects. In vitro, HKL counteracted erastin-induced ferroptosis in PC12 and HT22 cells, an effect dependent on GPX4 expression. Furthermore, SIRT3 overexpression alleviated cognitive impairment by suppressing neuronal ferroptosis and mitochondrial GPX4 acetylation. These findings establish SIRT3 as a therapeutic target for PND by inhibiting ferroptosis.

## Methods

## Animals and ethics approval
C57BL/6J mice (10–12 months old, weighing 25–35 g) were supplied by Hunan Slack Jingda Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. They were housed under standard conditions with 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness, 45-65% humidity, 22–25 °C temperatures, and free access to water and food. The experimental procedures adhered to the “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals” published by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Xiangyang No. 1 People’s Hospital (Ethics Approval No. XYYYE20220047).

## Drug treatment
According to the reference [ ], to assess HKL’s prophylactic potential against PND, mice were administered 10 mg/kg/day of HKL (MCE, HY-N0003) intraperitoneally for 7 days. Additionally, to elucidate SIRT3’s role in ferroptosis regulation during PND, mice received HKL (10 mg/kg/day) or erastin (20 mg/kg/day, MCE, HY-15763) either alone or in combination intraperitoneally for 7 days [ ].

## Animal model
The tibial fracture fixation surgery, classified as a major non-cardiac procedure, is strongly associated with PND due to its prolonged operative duration, significant anesthetic demands, and intense surgical trauma [ ]. The epidemiological evidence confirms that these factors synergistically elevate neuroinflammatory responses and oxidative stress, thereby increasing the susceptibility. Consequently, this surgical model has been widely adopted in preclinical research of PND [ – ]. Based on our previous study [ ], an animal model was developed, and tibial fracture surgery was performed under isoflurane anesthesia (3% isoflurane by inhalation for anesthesia and maintained with 1.5% isoflurane by inhalation, and the average duration of the surgical procedure was 2 h). After a week of acclimatization, mice underwent tibial fracture surgery under isoflurane anesthesia. During surgery, 0.5% bupivacaine (1 mg/kg) was applied for pain relief, and the left tibia was exposed and stabilized with a 0.3 mm pin.

## Open field test (OFT)
Mice were positioned in the neutral zone of a 50 cm cubic white chamber and permitted to explore for 5 min following the reference [ ]. To eliminate odor interference, the chamber was sprayed with alcohol between trials. The total distance traveled (in centimeters) and the time spent in each quadrant of the target area were recorded.

## Novel object recognition test (NORT)
The NORT was performed as in a previous study [ ], two identical objects were positioned at two corners, each 5 cm from the respective borders, within an open field measuring 40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm. During the initial phase, mice were permitted to adapt to the environment freely for 5 min, and the time spent exploring each object was documented. The procedure was repeated the next day, substituting one object with a new one of the same dimensions but with a different appearance. The exploration times for both objects were recorded. To eliminate any residual odors, the apparatus was cleansed with 75% alcohol following each experimental session.

## Y-maze test (YMT)
According to the reference [ ], there are three identical arms in the Y-maze apparatus, each spaced 120° apart, measuring 30 cm × 15 cm × 6 cm. In spatial cognition experiments, the two stages were conducted with a 2-hour interval between them. In the initial stage, 3 min were given for the subject to explore the start arm with the novel arm closed. The second stage mirrored the first, except that the novel arm was opened. The number of entries and the time spent in the novel arm were recorded. During free alternation experiments, the animal was positioned at the far end of an arm, and entries into arms were recorded over 8 min. The percentage of spontaneous alternation (SA) was calculated.

## Morris water maze test (MWMT)
According to the reference [ ], a circular pool with a 120-centimeter diameter and 50-centimeter height was filled with water to a 40-centimeter depth, and white tempera paint was uniformly mixed in. A white platform measuring 10 centimeters in diameter was submerged 1 centimeter below the water’s surface. The testing protocol included three daily trials for four consecutive days, followed by a probe test. During each trial, the mouse was gently placed into the water and allowed to search for the hidden platform over one minute. Mice that found the platform stayed on it for 15 s, while those that did not were placed on it for the same duration. The time required to reach the hidden platform, known as escape latency, was recorded. On the fifth day, a 60-second probe trial was performed, during which the platform was removed. The number of times mice crossed the former platform location was measured.

## Histology staining
According to the reference [ ], day 14 after anesthesia and surgery, the brain tissues were fixed in 10% formalin, dehydrated, embedded, and sectioned into 4 μm slices. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining assessed histological morphology, while Nissl staining evaluated neuronal damage. After dewaxing and rehydration, brain tissues were incubated in Nissl staining solution (G1434, Solarbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China) for 30 min at 37 °C, then immersed in PBS. Optical microscopy (Olympus IX73P2F, Japan) was used for visualization, and cell counting was performed with ImageJ (v1.51, NIH, USA).

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and Immunofluorescence (IF)
According to the reference [ ], mice underwent transcranial perfusion with PBS and 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brain tissue sections were subsequently dewaxed and dehydrated. The tissue slices were incubated with primary antibodies of anti-SIRT3 (1:200, 82210-1-RR, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and anti-GPX4 (1:200, 67763-1-Ig, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), followed by co-incubation with secondary antibodies. For immunofluorescence analysis, brain tissue sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, and immersed in 0.01 M sodium citrate buffer, then heated in a microwave oven for antigen epitope recovery. After permeabilization and blocking, the tissue slices were incubated with primary antibodies (anti-SIRT3, 1:200, 82210-1-RR, Proteintech; anti-GPX4, 1:2001, 67763-1-Ig, Proteintech; anti-NeuN, 1:200, 26975-1-AP, Proteintech), followed by co-incubation with secondary antibodies and DAPI. The quantification of IHC was performed using standardized optical density measurement with rigorous normalization by ImageJ, and the aggregates of IF were quantified using ImageJ by measuring the mean gray value and integrated density.

## RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)
Total RNA was isolated from the mouse hippocampus and assessed by the NanoDrop 2000 (ThermoFisher, USA). In this study, mRNA with poly (A) tails was enriched and fragmented into approximately 300 bp segments. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was amplified via PCR and screened for 450 bp fragments. Following library construction, the enriched fragments underwent additional amplification. Finally, the libraries underwent paired-end sequencing using next-generation sequencing (NGS).

## Iron ion detection
Iron ions in mouse hippocampal tissue were detected by an Iron Assay Kit (I291, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) following the manufacturer’s protocol [ ]. 100 mg of fresh mouse hippocampal tissues were homogenized and then ultrasonically crushed at low temperatures for 5 min to collect the supernatant. Fe2+ levels were quantified by adding assay buffer to the supernatant, and total iron levels were measured by adding reducer solution. After a one-hour incubation at 37 °C, the absorbance at 593 nm was detected using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA). Subsequently, the contents of Fe2+, Fe3+, and total iron were determined.

## Measurement of glutathione (GSH) and glutathione disulfide (GSSG)
According to the manufacturer’s instructions [ ], a volume of 200 µL protein removal reagent was added to 20 mg of freshly harvested mouse hippocampal tissues. The tissues were then homogenized and incubated for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was collected for analysis after centrifuging. Total GSH and GSSG contents were quantified using the GSH and GSSG Assay Kit (S0053, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The absorbance of the wells was then measured at a wavelength of 412 nm using a microplate reader.

## Tissular glutathione peroxidase activity assay (GSH-Px)
According to the reference [ ], the activity of glutathione peroxidase was assessed by an assay kit (S0056, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). 20 mg of fresh mouse hippocampal tissues were homogenized and centrifuged to collect the lysates. The absorbance of the wells at a wavelength of 340 nm was recorded at 5-minute intervals maintained at 25 °C. The results were subsequently calculated based on the guidelines provided by the manufacturer.

## Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
Mouse hippocampal tissues were fixed, dehydrated, and embedded in resin. Ultrathin 60 nm sections were cut, coated with epoxy resin, and polymerized at 60 °C. They were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and then imaged using a Hitachi electron microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

## Stereotaxic injection of adeno-associated virus (AAV)
AAV2 vectors for SIRT3 overexpression (pHBAAV-CMV-MCS-3flag-T2A-SIRT3) and empty virus vectors were obtained from HANBIO (Hanbio Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China).The virus titers were 1 1012 vg/mL, after anesthesia, mice were restrained in stereotaxic apparatuses (RWD Life Science, China). A burr hole was used to perforate the skull, and a stepper motor-driven microinjector (Hamilton, Reno, NV, USA) was used to inject 500 nl of virus solution at each injection site into the hippocampus. The stereotaxic coordinate of hippocampus was as follows (AP: −2.0 mm, ML: ±1.5 mm, DV: −1.7 mm) and the rate was 50 nL/min. The needles remained in place for 10 min post-injection before being slowly withdrawn. Finally, mice were placed on a heating pad until fully recovered.

## Cell culture and RNA interference
PC12 rat pheochromocytoma and HT22 mouse hippocampal neuronal cells were sourced from the Shanghai Institute’s Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences. They were grown in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The cell group was divided into a blank control group (Con), a model group (10µM Era), and a treatment group (10µM Era + 10µM HKL; 10µM Era + 20µM HKL). HKL, an efficient SIRT3 activator purchased from Selleckchem (S2310, Houston, TX, USA), was added to the cells 30 min beforehand. A negative control (Hanbio Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) or GPX4 siRNA (forward: 5′-CUGACGUAAACUACACCATT-3′; reverse: 5′-UGAGUGUAGUUUACGUCAGTT-3′) was transfected into HT22 cells with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). Cells were collected for analysis 48 h after transfection.

## Cell viability
Cell viability was assessed using the cell counting kit-8 assay (CCK-8, HY-K0301, MCE, Shanghai, China). The cytotoxicity of HKL was evaluated by exposing PC12 and HT22 cells to different HKL concentrations (0, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, or 40 µM), with or without 10 µM erastin. After 48 h, cell viability was assessed using CCK-8, and absorbance was read at 450 nm with a microplate reader.

## Oxidative stress assays
H2DCFH-DA staining (R252, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) and C11-BODIPY 581/591 (D3861, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA) were employed independently to quantify the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxidation [ ]. Cells were incubated with 5 mM H2DCFH-DA and 2 mM C11-BODIPY 581/591 for 30 min. Fluorescence images were captured, and fluorescence intensity was quantified with ImageJ software. Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were quantified using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) assay kit (S0131M, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), where MDA forms adduct with TBA at 100 °C, detectable at 532 nm with a microplate reader. Oxidative stress was also evaluated by measuring total superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, GSH, and GSH-Px activity following the manufacturer’s instructions (S0101, S0053, S0056, Beyotime, Shanghai, China).

## Mitochondrial staining and mitochondrial membrane potential testing
Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos (C1035, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and the JC-1 detection kit (KGA1904, KeyGEN BioTECH, Jiangsu, China) were utilized [ ]. Incubation with MitoTracker Red CMXRos (50 nM) or JC-1 was carried out for 20 min. Images were taken through an Olympus IX73P2F microscope, and intensity was evaluated using ImageJ software.

## FerroOrange staining
The free Fe2+ in the cytoplasm was identified with the fluorescent probe FerroOrange (F374, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) [ ]. Incubations were conducted at 37 °C for 30 min in serum-free, phenol-red-free DMEM with 1 mM FerroOrange. Images were taken through an Olympus IX73P2F microscope, and intensity was evaluated using ImageJ software.

## RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis
The RNAiso Plus (9108, Takara, Japan) was used to extract total RNA, the RNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop 2000, and the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (RR036A, Takara, Japan) was used to synthesize cDNA. Finally, qRT-PCR was conducted using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II FAST qPCR (CN830A, Takara, Japan). The primers are listed in (Table  ).
 

## Extraction of mitochondrial and cytoplasmic proteins
Approximately 50 mg of fresh mouse hippocampal tissues were homogenized and incubated at 4 °C for 10 min to collect the supernatant after centrifuging at 10,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The precipitates were washed with PBS, centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Buffer was then added to the mitochondrial pellet for further experiments.

## Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
According to the reference [ ], approximately 500 µg of mitochondrial proteins were mixed with 25 µL A/G Magnetic beads (HY-K0202, MCE, shanghai, China), and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h. Then, 1 µg of primary antibody was added and incubated at 4 °C for 3 h. After removing the supernatant, the beads were collected, mixed with 30 µL of 1× buffer, boiled for 10 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 60 s at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was used for Western blotting analysis.

## Western blotting
Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (P0009, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). 40 µg of total proteins in each group were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat skim milk for 1 h, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Antibodies agains SIRT3 (1:1000, A5419), GPX4 (1:5000, A1933), SLC7A11 (1:1000, A13685), NCOA4 (1:1000, A25307), VDAC1(1: 1000, A19707), TfR1(1: 2000, A5865), FTH1(1:1000, A19544) were from ABclonal Technology (Wuhan, China), and anti-acetylated-lysine (1: 1000, 9441) and Tom20 (1:2000, 42406) were from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). The HRP-linked secondary antibody was incubated for 1 h, and protein bands were quantified using a chemiluminescence assay and Image Lab software (Bio-Rad, USA).

## Statistical analysis
In our study, we used GraphPad Prism 10 for data analysis. Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). For comparisons between two groups with normally distributed data, an unpaired Student’s t-test was employed. For multiple group comparisons, the escape latency was assessed using a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Additional analyses of Western blotting and behavioral tests were performed using a one-way ANOVA with subsequent Tukey’s post hoc test. For non-normally distributed data, the Kruskal-Wallis test was utilized. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05.

## Result

## SIRT3 was downregulated in the mouse hippocampus following anesthesia and surgery
The flowchart displays all experimental procedures (Supplementary Fig.  A). Firstly, the open field test (OFT) was used to assess the locomotor activity of each group. No significant differences were observed in total distance traveled or time spent in the central area (Supplementary Fig.  B-D). Similarly, the novel object recognition test (NORT), used to evaluate spatial learning and memory, showed no significant differences in total exploration time between the groups. However, mice in the surgery group exhibited a notable reduction in the discrimination index compared to the control group (Supplementary Fig.  G-J). In the Y-maze test (YMT), spontaneous alternation and the percentage of novel entries were significantly decreased post-surgery (Supplementary Fig.  E, F, J, K). Finally, the Morris water maze test (MWMT) results revealed that mice in the Sur group took longer to escape, spent less time in the target area, and crossed the platform fewer numbers compared to the control group (Supplementary Fig.  L-P). These findings suggest that mice exhibited remarkable learning and memory impairment following anesthesia and surgery.
To explore the role of SIRT3 in anesthesia/surgery induced cognitive impairment, we measured its mRNA and protein expression. We found a significant decrease in the surgery group compared to the control group (Fig.  A and C). RNA sequencing analysis revealed that SIRT2 and SIRT3 levels were more highly expressed than other SIRT family members, with SIRT3 significantly reduced in the surgery group relative to the control group (Fig.  D). The results of H&E staining showed no significant structural abnormalities in the hippocampal architecture between groups; however, the surgery group exhibited notably disorganized neuronal arrangement and increased vacuolation compared to the control group (Fig.  E). Dual immunofluorescence staining revealed that decreased SIRT3 was primarily localized to the CA1 and CA3 regions of the hippocampus (Fig.  F and I). These findings suggest that the reduction of SIRT3 in hippocampal neurons may play a crucial in the development of PND.
 

## SIRT3 activation by HKL attenuated cognitive impairment following anesthesia and surgery
To assess the effect of SIRT3 activation on PND, HKL, a SIRT3 activator, was administered for one week prior to surgery, as illustrated in the flowcharts (Fig.  A). The mRNA and protein levels of SIRT3 in the hippocampus were measured using qRT-PCR and Western blotting, revealing increased SIRT3 expression in both HKL and HKL + Sur groups (Fig.  B and D). Dual immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that HKL enhanced SIRT3 expression in the CA1, CA3, and dentate gyrus (DG) regions of the hippocampus in the HKL group, and the decrease in SIRT3 expression in the CA1 and CA3 regions post-surgery (Fig.  E and H). Furthermore, Nissl staining results showed a reduction in the percentage of Nissl-positive cells in all neurons of the PND mice. However, this effect was diminished after HKL pretreatment, suggesting a neuroprotective effect of HKL (Fig.  I and L).
 To examined HKL’s impact on PND. The NORT revealed consistent total exploration time across all four group. However, HKL pretreatment counteracted the decrease in the discrimination index (Fig.  M and O). Additionally, the YMT results showed that HKL pretreatment significantly increased the percentages of enriches into the novel arm in the HKL + Sur group compared to the Sur group, as well as spontaneous alternation (Fig.  P and Q). In the MWMT, the HKL + Sur group demonstrated reduced escape latency, increased time spent in the target quadrant, and more platform crossings compared to the Sur group, while mean speed remained consistent across all four groups (Fig.  R and U). Altogether, these results suggest that HKL attenuates cognitive decline following anesthesia and surgery.

## SIRT3 activation by HKL attenuated neuronal ferroptosis following anesthesia and surgery
Transcriptomic analyses were performed on mouse hippocampus tissues from the Con, Sur, and HKL + Sur groups to investigate the neuroprotective mechanisms of HKL in PND. Supplementary Fig.  B illustrated significant genome-wide expression changes in the Sur group relative to the Con group, with 3,391 DEGs down-regulated and 229 DEGs up-regulated. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis identified pathways related to glutamatergic synapses, neuronal cell bodies, cognition, and brain development (Supplementary Fig.  C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses revealed that the DEGs were enriched in pathways such as calcium signaling, neurodegeneration, and endocytosis (Supplementary Fig.  D). When comparing gene expression between the HKL + Sur and Sur groups, 399 DEGs were downregulated and 4,404 DEGs were upregulated (Supplementary Fig.  E). GO analysis identified pathways related to glutamatergic synapses, synaptic signaling, and neuron projection development (Supplementary Fig.  F). KEGG analysis revealed enrichment in pathways such as glutamatergic synapses, MAPK signaling, and endocytosis (Supplementary Fig.  G). Moreover, 1,579 DEGs were common across all three groups, exhibiting similar expression levels in the Con and HKL + Sur groups, but differing in the Sur group (Supplementary Fig.  I). GO analysis identified dendritic trees, presynapse, and synaptic signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig.  J), while KEGG analysis indicated enrichment in glutamatergic synapse and endocytosis pathways (Supplementary Fig.  K). Recent studies indicate that SIRT3 inhibits mitochondrial GPX4 acetylation and renal cell ferroptosis induced by cadmium [ ], highlighting its significant role in ferroptosis. The relationships among 11 significant proteins associated with SIRT3 and ferroptosis-related proteins are shown in the STRING interaction analysis (Supplementary Fig.  H), confirming a direct or indirect connection between SIRT3 and GPX4.
To further verify SIRT3’s impact on PND-induced neuronal ferroptosis. We measured the mRNA expression of ferroptosis-associated genes in the hippocampus. As shown in Fig.  A, PND mice had upregulated pro-ferroptosis genes and downregulated anti-ferroptosis genes compared to the control group. HKL pretreatment inhibited ferroptosis by suppressing the expression of pro-ferroptosis genes. Additionally, we examined the levels of protein related to ferroptosis in the mouse hippocampus across the four groups. As shown in Fig.  B and G, the expression of pro-ferroptosis proteins, including TFR1 and NCOA4, were upregulated, while anti-ferroptosis proteins, including FTH1, GPX4, and SLC7A11, were downregulated in the Sur group compared to the Con group. These changes were reversed by HKL pretreatment. Dual immunofluorescence staining of GPX4 and NeuN confirmed that HKL inhibited neuronal ferroptosis in the CA1 and CA3 regions of the mouse hippocampus following anesthesia and surgery, with no significant difference in NeuN fluorescence intensity among the four groups (Fig.  H and M).
 To examine the effects of HKL on ferroptosis, ultrastructural analysis revealed ferroptosis-related morphological changes in the mitochondria of the Sur group, such as shrunken mitochondria and reduced or absent mitochondrial cristae. After HKL pretreatment, these changes were mitigated. Furthermore, various ferroptosis markers, including the content of Fe2+, Fe3+, GSH, GSSG, MDA, SOD, and GSH-Px, were measured in the mouse hippocampus across the four groups. As expected, the Sur group exhibited elevated levels of Fe2+, Fe3+, total iron, and MDA, along with reduced levels of SOD, GSH-Px, GSH, and the GSH/GSSG ratio compared to the Con group. These alterations were ameliorated by HKL pretreatment (Fig.  O and V). Overall, the data suggest that SIRT3 activation by HKL attenuates anesthesia/surgery-induced neuronal ferroptosis.

## Erastin activating ferroptosis mitigated the neuroprotective effect of HKL in PND
To further verify that HKL improved anesthesia/surgery-induced cognitive impairment by inhibiting ferroptosis, erastin, a classical ferroptosis inducer, was intraperitoneally injected combined with HKL in PND mice. Neurobehavioral tests were then performed to assess cognitive function. In the MWMT, HKL reduced escape latency, increased time in the target quadrant, and increased platform crossings in PND mice. However, these effects were reversed by erastin (Fig.  A and D). Similarly, results of YMT and NORT showed that HKL improved the discrimination index, the percentage of enriches into the novel arm, and spontaneous alternation in PND mice, but erastin diminished these effects of HKL (Fig.  E and G). Additionally, immunohistochemistry staining of hippocampus tissue was performed to detect GPX4 levels in all four groups. As shown in Fig.  H, GPX4 expression decreased in PND mice, while HKL increased GPX4 levels in the CA1, CA3, and DG regions of the hippocampus in the HKL + Sur group, which was reversed by erastin. Nissl staining confirmed that HKL increased the percentage of Nissl-positive cells in these regions, but this effect was nullified by erastin (Fig.  I). A significant reduction in Nissl-positive cell counts within hippocampal subregions was observed in the surgery group compared to control group, but this protective effect was abolished by co-administration of erastin (Fig.  M and O). We also examined the mRNA and protein expression of ferroptosis-associated genes. HKL was found to inhibit neuronal ferroptosis by downregulating pro-ferroptosis genes and upregulating anti-ferroptosis genes, but this effect was reversed by erastin (Fig.  A). Consistently, compared to the HKL + Sur group, the HKL + Sur + Era group exhibited increased levels of NCOA4 and decreased levels of GPX4 and SLC7A11 (Fig.  B). As expected, erastin upregulated pro-ferroptosis markers, such as Fe2+, Fe3+, total iron, and MDA, while downregulating GSH-Px, GSH, and the ratio of GSH/GSSG compared to the HKL + Sur group (Fig.  F and M). Finally, TEM revealed that significant mitochondrial ultrastructural alterations in mice hippocampus of the HKL + Sur + Era group compared to the HKL + Sur group, including a decrease in mitochondrial numbers, increased matrix vacuolization, and disorganization of cristae (Fig.  N). These findings suggest that erastin attenuates the mitochondrial protective effect of HKL, promoting mitochondrial ultrastructure changes toward ferroptosis. Together, these results indicate that erastin counteracts the anti-ferroptosis effects of HKL.
 

## SIRT3 activation by HKL attenuated ferroptosis in erastin-exposed PC12 and HT22 cells
Erastin was used to establish a ferroptosis model on PC12 and HT22 cells in vitro. Supplementary Fig.  A-D shows that varying HKL concentrations did not significantly affect cell viability. However, pretreatment with HKL notably enhanced cell viability in erastin-exposed PC12 and HT22 cells. We evaluated oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in PC12 and HT22 cells before and after treatment with HKL or erastin. Oxidative stress levels were measured using H2DCFH-DA and BODIPY 581/591 C11. Following erastin stimulation, ROS and lipid peroxidation products (green fluorescence) accumulated in the cells, but pretreatment with HKL significantly reduced these effects (Supplementary Fig.  E-M). Similarly, HKL inhibited the decrease in SOD levels in erastin-exposed PC12 and HT22 cells (Supplementary Fig.  K, N). We also examined mRNA and protein expression of ferroptosis-related genes. Figure  A and J showed that erastin upregulated mRNA expression of pro-ferroptosis genes and downregulated the levels of anti-ferroptosis genes. HKL counteracted the effect of erastin and exhibited anti-ferroptosis properties in PC12 and HT22 cells. Western blotting analysis revealed that NCOA4 was upregulated, while GPX4 and SLC7A11 were downregulated in the erastin-treated group. HKL treatment increased GPX4 and SLC7A11 levels while decreasing NCOA4 expression. FerroOrange staining confirmed that HKL significantly reduced intracellular Fe2+ accumulation (Fig.  K and N). Additionally, we used JC-1 staining and the MitoTracker Red CMXRos probe to evaluate mitochondrial function. These analyses indicated that HKL mitigates erastin-induced mitochondrial dysfunction by enhancing mitochondrial membrane potential. Erastin specifically reduced mitochondrial membrane potential (red fluorescence) in cells, an effect that was effectively reversed by HKL (Fig.  O and V). Together, these results suggest HKL attenuates erastin-induced neuronal ferroptosis by inhibiting lipid ROS accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction.
 

## GPX4 deficiency abolished the beneficial effect of HKL in erastin-induced ferroptosis
To further determine the mechanism by which HKL alleviates erastin-induced neuronal ferroptosis, GPX4 knockdown was performed in HT22 cells using siRNA. GPX4 mRNA and protein levels were analyzed. Compared to the control group, both GPX4 protein and mRNA levels were reduced after erastin treatment in HT22 cells, and this effect was further enhanced in the si-GPX4 + Era group. HKL mitigated the erastin-induced reduction in GPX4 expression, an effect nullified by si-GPX4 (Fig.  A and C). Furthermore, GPX4 silencing significantly enhanced the erastin-induced decline in cell viability and abolished the beneficial effects of HKL (Fig.  D). As shown in Fig. 7E and G, GPX4 silencing in HT22 cells promoted the accumulation of ROS (green fluorescence) and intracellular Fe2+ (red fluorescence) compared to erastin treatment alone. This silencing also reversed the inhibitory effects of HKL toward ROS and intracellular Fe2+ accumulation. MitoTracker Red CMXRos probe and C11-BODIPY staining confirmed that GPX4 silencing negated the inhibitory effect of HKL on erastin-induced ferroptosis (Fig.  H and K).
 

## SIRT3 overexpression in the mouse hippocampus attenuated cognitive decline by inhibiting PND-induced neuronal ferroptosis
We constructed an AAV2-SIRT3 vector to overexpress SIRT3 in the mouse hippocampus (Fig.  A). The experimental design is illustrated in the flowcharts (Fig.  B). Neurobehavioral tests were used to assess cognitive function. Compared to Sur + AAV2-empty group, SIRT3 overexpression in the hippocampus of PND mice resulted in reduced escape latency, increased time in target quadrants, and more platform crossings (Fig.  C and G). Consistently, the results of YMT and NORT demonstrated that SIRT3 overexpression improved the discrimination index and spontaneous alternation in PND mice (Fig.  H and I). Immunohistochemistry revealed that SIRT3 overexpression elevated SIRT3 levels in the CA1, CA3, and DG regions of the PND mouse hippocampus compared to the Sur + AAV2-empty group (Fig.  J). Nissl staining showed a higher percentage of Nissl-positive cells in these regions with SIRT3 overexpression (Fig.  K). Overall, these findings suggest that SIRT3 overexpression in the mouse hippocampus reduces cognitive decline in PND.
 Figure  A and D showed that AAV-associated overexpression of SIRT3 significantly increased the mRNA and protein levels of SIRT3 in the mouse hippocampus. Western blotting and immunohistochemistry further confirmed that SIRT3 overexpression also elevated GPX4 protein expression in mouse hippocampal regions (Fig.  F and I). Additionally, the detection of ferroptosis markers revealed that SIRT3 overexpression downregulated the levels of pro-ferroptosis markers, including Fe2+, total iron, and MDA, while upregulating GSH-Px, GSH, and the GSH/GSSG ratio compared to the Sur + AAV2-empty group (Fig.  J and N). Ultrastructural analysis showed that SIRT3 overexpression significantly attenuated anesthesia/surgery-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, as evidenced by preserved mitochondrial density, reduced matrix vacuolization and restored cristae architecture (Fig.  O). In summary, SIRT3 overexpression protects against cognitive decline by suppressing PND-induced ferroptosis.
 

## SIRT3 activation by HKL inhibited mitochondrial GPX4 acetylation
It has been reported that SIRT3 inhibits the acetylation of mitochondrial GPX4, thereby increasing GPX4 levels. This study assessed the effects of anesthesia and surgery on mitochondrial GPX4 and SIRT3 proteins expression. As shown in Fig.  A and D, cytoplasmic GPX4 levels remained unchanged, but mitochondrial GPX4 levels were lower in the Sur group compared to Con group. Additionally, mitochondrial SIRT3 protein expression was also decreased in the Sur group (Fig.  E and F). Next, the expression of mitochondrial GPX4 acetylation was analyzed by Co-IP, revealing a significant increase in acetylation and a decrease in mitochondrial GPX4 protein in the Sur group compared to the Con group (Fig.  G and J). Interestingly, we also found that mitochondrial SIRT3 levels were downregulated in the Sur group following GPX4 immunoprecipitation. To further investigate how SIRT3 activation affects PND-induced mitochondrial GPX4 acetylation, we measured GPX4 protein and its acetylation levels before and after HKL treatment. As shown in Fig.  K and N, HKL decreased GPX4 acetylation in the HKL + Sur group compared to the Sur group. As anticipated, HKL treatment led to upregulated mitochondrial SIRT3 and GPX4 expression after GPX4 immunoprecipitation. These data suggest that SIRT3 activation by HKL inhibits mitochondrial GPX4 acetylation.
 

## Discussion
Perioperative neurocognitive disorder (PND) is a common neurological issue in elderly patients following anesthesia and surgery [ , ]. Previous studies have reported that patients of all ages may be at risk of PND, with an overall incidence is approximately 2–3%. However, the incidence of elderly individuals undergoing cardiac and orthopedic surgeries can reach 50% [ , ]. Despite numerous proposed mechanisms, the underlying causes of PND remains unclear. Various pathological processes are implicated in PND, including neuroinflammation, blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeability disruption, alterations in synaptic plasticity, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and neuronal apoptosis [ , – ]. Neuronal ferroptosis is a critical factor in the pathogenesis of PND [ , ]. We found that HKL, an activator of SIRT3, mitigated neuronal ferroptosis and alleviated cognitive decline in PND. Additionally, we also demonstrated that AAV-associated overexpression of SIRT3 in the mouse hippocampus exhibited anti-ferroptosis effects in PND.
Substantial evidence has shown that neuronal ferroptosis contributes to cognitive dysfunction in PND mice [ ]. Ferroptosis is characterized by intracellular iron overload and membrane lipid peroxidation [ ]. Ge et al. found that sevoflurane-induced postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) involved alterations in iron and glucose metabolism [ ]. And anesthetic exposure also disrupted neuronal iron homeostasis, leading to cognitive deficits in the brain [ , ]. Additionally, surgical stimulation increased iron levels in the hippocampus and the release of ROS, promoting neuronal ferroptosis [ – ]. Our findings align with previous studies, showing that PND mice experienced hippocampal neuronal ferroptosis marked by lipid peroxidation, iron overload, and mitochondrial dysfunction. HKL pretreatment notably inhibited ferroptosis. In PND mice, hippocampal iron uptake increased, converting Fe3+ in ferritin to Fe2+, resulting in unstable iron accumulation. Furthermore, HKL reversed the elevations in MDA and ROS induced by PND or erastin, as well as the decrease in GSH, the GSH/GSSG ratio and GSH-Px, and mitigated mitochondrial dysfunction. The Xc−/GSH/GPX4 system plays a crucial in inhibiting ferroptosis, with SLC7A11 as a major component of Xc− and GPX4 as a key ferroptosis regulator [ , ]. We found that HKL reversed the mRNA and protein expression related to ferroptosis, such as NCOA4, SLC7A11, and GPX4, under PND or erastin exposure. Pro-ferroptosis markers like ACSL4, VDAC1, and TfR1 increased in the PND mouse hippocampus, while the anti-ferroptosis marker FTH1 decreased. HKL pretreatment or SIRT3 overexpression reversed these changes, which were exacerbated by erastin. These results suggest that targeting ferroptosis maybe a promising strategy to alleviate cognitive impairment in PND. As PND induces iron metabolism disorders, iron chelators like deferoxamine (DFO) and deferiprone (DFP) have been shown to attenuate cognitive decline by inhibiting neuronal ferroptosis or neuroinflammation [ , ].
In addition, the mechanisms underlying iron homeostasis dysfunction due to surgery or anesthetics remain poorly understood. Zhao et al. found that sevoflurane significantly increased the levels of mind bomb-2 (MIB2), which in turn enhanced GPX4 ubiquitination and reduced its stability, thereby inducing ferroptosis and cognitive impairment [ ]. Liu et al. reported that isoflurane exposure elevated Beclin1 phosphorylation, which formed a complex with SLC7A11 to downregulate its expression, further promoting neuronal ferroptosis [ ]. Additionally, another study demonstrated that sevoflurane downregulated the expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4 through ASCL4, mediating sevoflurane-induced ferroptosis via AMPK/mTOR signaling [ ]. Our findings revealed that in PND, mitochondrial SIRT3 and GPX4 were downregulated, while mitochondrial GPX4 acetylation was elevated, subsequently inducing neuronal ferroptosis. Interestingly, we found that HKL inhibited ferroptosis by mitigating mitochondrial GPX4 acetylation.
The SIRT3 enzyme reduces mitochondrial protein acetylation to maintain mitochondrial protein stability [ ]. It has been shown that downregulation of SIRT3 promotes mitochondrial GPX4 acetylation, resulting in ferroptosis of renal cells induced by cadmium [ ]. In our study, we found that SIRT3 was downregulated in the hippocampus of PND mice. Notably, pretreatment with HKL or overexpression of SIRT3 significantly ameliorated cognitive impairment and neuronal ferroptosis in PND. Although numerous studies have reported that SIRT3 ameliorates cognitive impairment, the specific impact of SIRT3 activation on ferroptosis in PND remains unclear. SIRT3 has been shown to suppress hippocampal neuroinflammation in aged mice, which contributes to the development of POCD [ ]. Additionally, the overexpression of SIRT3 in mice reduced the oxidative stress response and inflammation induced by anesthesia [ ]. Consistently, our findings also indicated that SIRT3 prevents oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in vivo and in vitro experiments. However, this neuroprotective effect of SIRT3 was attenuated by ferroptosis activation or si-GPX4, suggesting that the attenuation of cognitive impairment in PND by SIRT3 is dependent on the inhibition of ferroptosis. Despite these valuable findings, there are some limitations in this research. The neuroprotective effects of SIRT3 could be influenced by other factors like neuroinflammation, synaptic plasticity, or BBB disruption, which are not fully explored in our study. Additionally, while HKL is used as an activator of SIRT3 and a modulator of ferroptosis, we have not compared it to other known ferroptosis inhibitors or explored their relative effectiveness. Although we emphasized the role of GPX4 acetylation as a key mechanism underlying the neuroprotective effects of HKL, the role of other ferroptosis-related enzymes or signaling pathways could be further explored. Moreover, there are limitations related to the research methods, such as the absence of immunofluorescence assays to detect the protein expression of SIRT3 and GPX4 in the mitochondria of mouse hippocampal neurons after SIRT3 overexpression.

## Conclusion
In this study, the activation of SIRT3 significantly mitigated cognitive impairment and neuronal damage in PND by inhibiting neuronal ferroptosis. Mechanistically, PND led to downregulation of mitochondrial SIRT3 and elevation of GPX4 acetylation. SIRT3 activation by HKL reduced mitochondrial GPX4 acetylation and neuronal ferroptosis following anesthesia and surgery. These novel findings suggest that SIRT3 holds potential as a clinical therapeutic agent for treating PND.
