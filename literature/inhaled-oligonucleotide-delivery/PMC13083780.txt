## Summary
Preterm white matter injury (PWMI) causes lasting neurological deficits by blocking oligodendrocyte precursor cell (OPC) differentiation. Perinatal hypoxia suppresses mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), essential for OPC maturation. We identify Lipin1 nuclear translocation as a key downstream event of mTORC1 suppression. In PWMI mice, Lipin1 was enriched in OPC nuclei, while primary OPCs and OLN-93 cells showed that mTORC1 inhibition induces Lipin1 dephosphorylation, nuclear accumulation, and impaired differentiation. Mechanistically, nuclear Lipin1 recruits histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC2) to sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP1) target promoters, repressing sphingolipid biosynthesis required for myelination. Lipin1 or HDAC2 knockdown restored SREBP1 targets and OPC differentiation under rapamycin. In vivo, Lipin1 knockdown or HDAC2 inhibition enhanced myelin gene expression, promoted oligodendrocyte maturation, improved myelin ultrastructure, and alleviated motor deficits. Thus, a Lipin1-HDAC2 epigenetic axis mediates hypoxia-induced myelin gene repression, offering targets for white matter repair in preterm infants.
Keywords: hypoxia-ischemia, oligodendrocyte precursor cells, mTORC1 signaling, Lipin1, HDAC2, SREBP1, sphingolipid biosynthesis, myelination, preterm white matter injury

## Introduction
Preterm white matter injury (PWMI) is a major cause of long-term neurological deficits in preterm infants, including cerebral palsy and cognitive impairment ( ). A hallmark of PWMI is impaired differentiation of oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) into mature, myelinating oligodendrocytes, leading to insufficient myelination, particularly in the corpus callosum ( ; ). Perinatal hypoxia, a key driver of PWMI, disrupts developmental signaling and lipid metabolic pathways required for OPC maturation ( ; ). Because efficient myelination depends on timely OPC differentiation, defining the molecular mechanisms underlying this process is critical for therapeutic development.
The mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) is a central regulator of oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelination ( ; ). Although perinatal hypoxia suppresses mTORC1 activity, how this leads to OPC differentiation failure remains unclear. Lipin1, a downstream effector of mTORC1, functions as both a phosphatidic acid phosphatase and a transcriptional coregulator ( ). mTORC1-dependent phosphorylation retains Lipin1 in the cytoplasm, whereas dephosphorylation under hypoxic conditions promotes its nuclear translocation ( ). Beyond its role in glycerophospholipid biosynthesis, nuclear Lipin1 directly modulates gene expression via interactions with transcription factors and chromatin regulators ( ; ). Consistently, we observed increased nuclear accumulation of Lipin1 in OPCs within the corpus callosum in a hypoxia-ischemia (HI)-induced PWMI mouse model, linking hypoxia-mediated mTORC1 inhibition to altered nuclear Lipin1 function.
Nuclear Lipin1 has been shown to repress sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP1), a master regulator of lipid biosynthesis, including sphingolipid pathways essential for myelin membrane expansion ( ). Transcriptional repression frequently involves recruitment of histone deacetylases (HDACs), leading to chromatin condensation ( ). Notably, HDAC2, but not its homolog HDAC1, has been implicated in inhibiting OPC differentiation when aberrantly activated ( ). These findings suggest that, under metabolic stress, nuclear Lipin1 may suppress lipid metabolic programs in OPCs by recruiting HDAC2 to SREBP1 target gene promoters.
Here, we show that hypoxia induces nuclear translocation of Lipin1 in OPCs both in vivo and in vitro. Using primary OPC cultures, we demonstrate that mTORC1 inhibition promotes Lipin1 dephosphorylation and nuclear accumulation, leading to impaired OPC differentiation. Mechanistically, nuclear Lipin1 recruits HDAC2 to SREBP1 target promoters, repressing sphingolipid biosynthesis required for myelination. Knockdown of Lipin1 or HDAC2 restores SREBP1 activity and rescues OPC maturation under mTORC1 suppression. These findings are validated in a PWMI model, where targeting Lipin1 or HDAC2 enhances OPC differentiation, improves myelin integrity, and ameliorates motor deficits. Together, this work identifies a mechanistic link between mTORC1 signaling, lipid metabolism, and epigenetic regulation in white matter development and highlights potential therapeutic targets for preterm brain injury.

## Results

## Nuclear translocation of Lipin1 is triggered by HI and observed in an OGD/R model
Lipin1 is known to localize in both the cytoplasm and nucleus, with its distribution tightly regulated by upstream signaling. Under normal conditions, Lipin1 was predominantly cytoplasmic in OPCs within the corpus callosum. However, in the PWMI model, Lipin1 was redistributed to the nucleus in a majority of OPCs at postnatal day 3 + 7 days after injury (P3+7) and P3+21. Although nuclear Lipin1 partially declined by P3+28, oligodendrocyte maturation remained impaired, suggesting that early hypoxia-induced nuclear accumulation exerts lasting effects on white matter development ( A, A, and S1B). These findings indicate a critical post-injury window during which Lipin1 nuclear translocation disproportionately impacts oligodendrocyte lineage progression, and that delayed normalization of localization alone is insufficient to reverse developmental deficits.
 This transient nuclear translocation suggests that Lipin1 responds dynamically to HI injury, particularly during the subacute phase. To model this phenomenon in vitro, we applied an oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) protocol to OLN-93 cells. Given that the in vivo model involves dynamic post-injury recovery, we first focused on the effect of different reperfusion durations after a fixed 3-h OGD period. Immunofluorescence and nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation revealed that nuclear Lipin1 levels were significantly increased after 12 and 24 h reperfusion, but not at 6 h ( B–1E and C). Total cellular Lipin1 protein levels were decreased after OGD/R across all reperfusion time points ( C and 1E).
Similarly, increasing OGD duration (3–12 h) under constant 24 h reperfusion consistently enhanced nuclear Lipin1 while reducing total cellular levels ( D–S1G). These data indicate that OGD/R induces Lipin1 nuclear translocation independent of increased protein abundance and support the use of 3 h OGD followed by 24 h reperfusion for subsequent studies.

## Lipin1 nuclear translocation is phosphorylation-dependent and regulated by mTORC1
Given that mTORC1 signaling governs Lipin1 phosphorylation and subcellular distribution ( A), we first examined whether its activity is altered during OGD/R. The phosphorylated mTOR (p-mTOR)/mTOR ratio was significantly reduced at 12 and 24 h reperfusion, as well as across all OGD durations under 24 h reperfusion ( B, 2C, H, and S1I), coinciding with Lipin1 nuclear accumulation.
 Pharmacological inhibition of mTORC1 with rapamycin mimicked OGD-induced Lipin1 nuclear translocation, whereas activation with MHY1485 suppressed nuclear accumulation under OGD but not under basal conditions ( D–2G). Immunoprecipitation further revealed that rapamycin and OGD reduced the phospho-Lipin1/total Lipin1 ratio, while MHY1485 preserved Lipin1 phosphorylation under OGD conditions ( E and 2F). Importantly, early mTORC1 suppression-induced Lipin1 dephosphorylation likely represents a key molecular event linking hypoxic stress to OPC differentiation defects, providing a mechanistic basis for persistent white matter impairment in vivo ( A and S1B).

## Inhibition of Lipin1 nuclear translocation restores OPC differentiation
To determine whether Lipin1 nuclear accumulation causally mediates the differentiation defects induced by mTORC1 inhibition, we examined oligodendrocyte lineage progression under conditions that modulate mTORC1-Lipin1 signaling. Primary mouse OPCs were treated with rapamycin or the mTORC1 activator MHY1485 under basal or OGD conditions. Western blot analysis confirmed that rapamycin and OGD markedly reduced the p-mTOR/mTOR ratio, whereas MHY1485 restored mTORC1 activity ( A and S2B). Given that platelet-derived growth factor receptor alpha (PDGFR-α) is a canonical marker of undifferentiated OPCs, while MBP (myelin basic protein) marks mature, myelinating oligodendrocytes, we assessed OPC differentiation status by evaluating the expression of both markers. Consistent with impaired differentiation, rapamycin-treated and OGD-exposed OPCs exhibited elevated PDGFR-α expression and a reduced MBP+/PDGFR-α+ ratio. Activation of mTORC1 by MHY1485 significantly rescued these defects in OGD-injured OPCs, restoring MBP expression and increasing the proportion of differentiated cells ( A and S2C–S2E), indicating that mTORC1 activity is required for OPC differentiation.
To directly assess the contribution of nuclear Lipin1 to the differentiation block induced by mTORC1 inhibition, we knocked down Lipin1 in primary OPCs treated with rapamycin under both basal and OGD conditions, thereby reducing its nuclear pool. Lipin1 silencing markedly alleviated the differentiation defects, as evidenced by an increased MBP+/PDGFR-α+ ratio under both basal and OGD conditions ( A and 3B). Western blot analysis further confirmed these effects, showing reduced PDGFR-α and increased expression of both MBP and CC1 (known as adenomatous polyposis coli, APC), a marker of differentiated oligodendrocytes, in Lipin1-silenced cells compared with rapamycin-treated controls ( C–3G). These results indicate that nuclear Lipin1 is a critical downstream effector mediating OPC differentiation arrest upon mTORC1 suppression.
 Finally, under OGD conditions, nuclear-cytoplasmic fractionation revealed robust nuclear accumulation of Lipin1, which was effectively reduced by siLipin1. Correspondingly, Lipin1 knockdown partially restored OPC differentiation after hypoxic injury, as indicated by increased MBP and decreased PDGFR-α expression ( F–S2H). Together, these findings identify nuclear Lipin1 as a convergent mediator of OPC differentiation failure downstream of hypoxia-induced mTORC1 inhibition.

## Nuclear Lipin1 suppresses SREBP1 transcriptional output
Given that mTORC1 inhibition drives Lipin1 nuclear translocation in OPCs, we next asked whether nuclear Lipin1 affects SREBP1-mediated transcription, a key regulator of lipid and sphingolipid biosynthesis essential for myelination ( ). A working model summarizing this proposed mechanism is shown in A.
 Integrative analysis of ChIP-Atlas and CistromeDB identified 53 overlapping SREBP1 target genes enriched in sphingolipid metabolism pathways ( B and 4C). Among these, we selected three representative genes for validation: Sgms1 and B4galt6 (linked to sphingolipid metabolism), and Syk, a canonical SREBP1 target ( D). Neither total nor mature nuclear SREBP1 protein levels were altered by rapamycin, OGD, or Lipin1 knockdown ( E–4G). ChIP analysis revealed comparable SREBP1 occupancy at the Sgms1 and B4galt6 promoters across treatment groups ( A and S3B), indicating that Lipin1 does not affect SREBP1 abundance or DNA binding. Notably, the near-background ChIP signals observed after knockdown likely reflect threshold-dependent loss of chromatin enrichment rather than complete absence of the protein, a common feature of ChIP-based assays when occupancy falls below detection limits.
In contrast, rapamycin and OGD significantly suppressed Sgms1 and B4galt6 transcription, which was partially restored by Lipin1 knockdown, whereas Syk expression remained unchanged ( H–4J). These data indicate that nuclear Lipin1 selectively represses sphingolipid-related SREBP1 target genes at the transcriptional level.

## HDAC2 mediates Lipin1-dependent transcriptional repression and OPC differentiation arrest
We next investigated whether nuclear Lipin1 represses transcription by recruiting histone deacetylases. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) revealed that Lipin1 interacts with both SREBP1 and HDAC2 in rapamycin-treated OPCs ( A and 5B). To validate the physiological relevance of this interaction in vivo, we examined PWMI mouse brains at P3+21, a time point characterized by prominent Lipin1 nuclear accumulation. Endogenous Co-IP assays from corpus callosum nuclear extracts demonstrated a robust Lipin1-HDAC2 interaction in PWMI mice, which was markedly reduced in sham controls ( C), indicating disease-relevant association of Lipin1 with HDAC2 in vivo.
 ChIP analysis demonstrated that rapamycin increased Lipin1 and HDAC2 enrichment at the Sgms1 and B4galt6 promoters, whereas Lipin1 knockdown markedly reduced HDAC2 recruitment ( D–5G). To determine whether this recruitment results in epigenetic repression, we examined histone acetylation at these loci. ChIP-qPCR analysis revealed that acetylation of histone H3 at lysine 9 (H3K9ac) was significantly reduced at the Sgms1 and B4galt6 promoters following rapamycin treatment, whereas Lipin1 knockdown restored H3K9ac levels ( H and 5I), consistent with HDAC-mediated chromatin condensation. Functionally, HDAC2 knockdown in rapamycin-treated OPCs significantly restored Sgms1 and B4galt6 expression despite sustained mTORC1 inhibition and persistent nuclear Lipin1 accumulation ( J and 5K). Consistently, depletion of HDAC2 increased the MBP+/PDGFR-α+ ratio, indicating rescue of OPC differentiation ( L and 5M). Finally, to assess specificity among class I HDACs, we compared the effects of HDAC1 and HDAC2 depletion. In contrast to HDAC2 knockdown, silencing HDAC1 failed to restore SREBP1 target gene expression or OPC differentiation, despite comparable knockdown efficiencies ( J–5M, C, and S3D). Together, these results demonstrate that Lipin1-dependent transcriptional repression and OPC differentiation arrest are selectively mediated by HDAC2 rather than generalized class I HDAC activity.

## Targeting the Lipin1-HDAC2 axis restores myelination and motor function in vivo
To assess in vivo relevance, we targeted Lipin1 or HDAC2 in a neonatal PWMI model using shLipin1 or the HDAC2 inhibitor Santacruzamate A (SCA) ( A). PWMI-induced robust nuclear Lipin1 accumulation in OPCs at P3+21, which was reduced by shLipin1 but not by SCA ( A–S4J). Both interventions restored Sgms1 and B4galt6 expression ( K and S4L). Furthermore, at a later stage (P3+28), these interventions significantly increased MBP expression and elevated the CC1+/PDGFR-α+ ratio in the corpus callosum, indicating enhanced oligodendrocyte differentiation ( B–6E). Consistent with these molecular and cellular improvements, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) performed at P3+28 revealed thicker myelin sheaths and reduced G-ratios in both shLipin1 and SCA groups compared to vehicle-treated PWMI mice ( F–6H).
 To determine whether enhanced myelination translates into functional improvement, we conducted behavioral assessments. In the open field test, PWMI mice displayed reduced total distance and average speed, which were alleviated by both shLipin1 and SCA treatment ( A–S5C). In the rotarod test, PWMI mice exhibited impaired motor coordination, as evidenced by shortened latency to fall, whereas both interventions significantly improved performance ( D). Together, these findings demonstrate that targeting the Lipin1-HDAC2 axis restores SREBP1-mediated transcriptional activity, promotes OPC differentiation and myelination, and rescues neurobehavioral deficits in white matter injury in premature infants.

## Discussion
This study identifies a previously unrecognized mechanism by which mTORC1 signaling regulates OPC differentiation through Lipin1-dependent transcriptional repression. We demonstrate that HI injury and OGD/R conditions induce dephosphorylation and nuclear translocation of Lipin1 in OPCs, leading to HDAC2-dependent repression of SREBP1 target genes and impaired oligodendrocyte maturation.
A notable feature of this process is the dynamic and transient nature of Lipin1 nuclear accumulation. In the PWMI model, Lipin1 is predominantly cytoplasmic under basal conditions but transiently accumulates in the nucleus during the critical window of OPC differentiation, before declining at later stages. Consistently, in vitro OGD/R experiments revealed delayed but robust nuclear Lipin1 accumulation during the subacute recovery phase rather than immediately after insult. These findings suggest that transient metabolic stress during early post-injury periods can exert long-lasting effects on white matter development, consistent with prior observations in neonatal brain injury models ( ).
Interestingly, nuclear accumulation of Lipin1 occurred despite an overall reduction in total cellular Lipin1 levels. This dissociation suggests that Lipin1 function may be governed by subcellular localization rather than protein abundance. Similar phenomena have been reported for other metabolic regulators under hypoxic stress, where nuclear localization confers functional persistence despite reduced total protein levels ( ; ). Whether selective nuclear import, altered degradation, or nuclear retention contributes to Lipin1 regulation in OPCs warrants further investigation.
Our data further establish mTORC1 signaling as a key upstream regulator of Lipin1 localization in OPCs. Pharmacological inhibition of mTORC1 enhanced Lipin1 nuclear accumulation and impaired OPC differentiation, whereas mTORC1 activation partially suppressed Lipin1 nuclear translocation under metabolic stress. While mTORC1 has been extensively linked to oligodendrocyte differentiation ( ; ), our findings extend this paradigm by identifying Lipin1 nuclear function as a critical downstream effector that mediates transcriptional repression in OPCs.
A central mechanistic insight of this study is that nuclear Lipin1 represses SREBP1 transcriptional activity without altering SREBP1 abundance, cleavage, or promoter occupancy. Instead, Lipin1 functions as a transcriptional corepressor by recruiting HDAC2 to SREBP1-bound promoters, including key sphingolipid biosynthetic genes such as Sgms1 and B4galt6. The apparent loss of ChIP signal upon knockdown likely reflects disruption of cooperative corepressor complexes rather than complete chromatin dissociation. Although repression of SREBP1 target genes was modest at the mRNA level, myelin biogenesis is highly sensitive to sustained perturbations in lipid metabolic flux. Oligodendrocyte differentiation and myelin membrane expansion require coordinated upregulation of lipid biosynthetic pathways over extended periods ( ). Even partial suppression of key sphingolipid enzymes can cumulatively constrain membrane production, ultimately resulting in pronounced defects in myelination. Thus, the observed discrepancy between mRNA fold changes and phenotypic severity likely reflects the non-linear and threshold-dependent nature of myelin formation rather than a lack of functional relevance. We acknowledge that lipid synthesis was not directly quantified in this study, and future work incorporating metabolic flux analysis or lipidomic profiling will be important to further refine the contribution of the Lipin1-HDAC2-SREBP1 axis to myelin lipid production.
HDAC2 is a well-established transcriptional repressor that limits oligodendrocyte differentiation by silencing pro-myelinating gene programs ( ; ). Our findings reveal that HDAC2 is co-opted by nuclear Lipin1 to repress lipid biosynthetic pathways essential for myelin formation. Genetic or pharmacological targeting of Lipin1 or HDAC2 restored SREBP1 target gene expression, rescued OPC differentiation, improved myelination, and ameliorated motor deficits in PWMI mice, highlighting the Lipin1-HDAC2 interaction as a key regulatory node.
Although this study was conducted in mouse models and primary OPC cultures, the core components of the mTORC1-Lipin1-HDAC2-SREBP1 axis are highly conserved across species. Analysis of publicly available human brain transcriptomic datasets indicates that LPIN1, HDAC2, and SREBF1 are robustly expressed during developmental windows corresponding to peak vulnerability to PWMI. Future studies using human single-cell and spatial transcriptomic approaches will be essential to further define the clinical relevance of this pathway.
In summary, our findings uncover a phosphorylation-dependent mechanism linking metabolic stress to epigenetic repression of lipid biosynthesis in OPCs. By connecting mTORC1 suppression, Lipin1 nuclear translocation, HDAC2-mediated chromatin remodeling, and impaired sphingolipid production, this study identifies a regulatory axis that controls oligodendrocyte maturation and white matter development. Targeting this pathway may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for promoting white matter repair in preterm brain injury.

## Methods

## Animals and ethics statement
Specific pathogen-free (SPF) C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Xuzhou Medical University. After acclimation, mice were housed under standard conditions (12 h light/dark cycle, 20°C–22°C, 50%–60% humidity) with ad libitum access to food and water. Breeding was performed at a ratio of one male (8 weeks) to two females (6 weeks). The day of birth was designated postnatal day 0 (P0). All animal procedures complied with NIH guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Medical University (approval no. SYXK2005-0018).

## PWMI model
Neonatal HI-induced PWMI was established in unsexed P3 pups. Under isoflurane anesthesia (3% induction, 1.5% maintenance), the right common carotid artery was isolated and ligated. Surgery was completed within 5 min. After recovery, pups were exposed to hypoxia (8% O2 and 92% N2) for 90 min at 37°C, then returned to the dam. Gas concentrations were continuously monitored using a real-time gas analyzer (BioSpherix, ProOx 110, USA). Sham controls underwent identical procedures without ligation or hypoxia.

## In vivo HDAC2 inhibition
Santacruzamate A (SCA, MedChemExpress, CAY-10683, USA), an HDAC2-selective inhibitor, was administered intraperitoneally (5 mg/kg/day) for 15 consecutive days starting at P6. Control animals received vehicle (DMSO diluted in 20% PEG300/PBS).

## Immunofluorescence
Frozen brain sections (20 μm) or cultured cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA. Samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti-MBP (Santa cruz, sc-376995, USA; 1:200 dilution), anti-PDGFR-α (Abcam, ab203491, UK; 1:300 dilution), anti-Olig2 (Millipore, MABN50, USA; 1:500 dilution), and anti-Lipin1 (Abcam, ab181389, USA; 1:200 dilution), followed by Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (488 or 594, Abcam; 1:500). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Solarbio, C0060, China) for 5 min. Fluorescent images were captured using either an Olympus BX53 fluorescence microscope (equipped with a DP80 camera and CellSens software) or a Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany), depending on the experiment.

## Cell culture and treatment
OLN-93 cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, 11965092, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, 10099141C, USA) and antibiotics at 37°C with 5% CO2. Medium was replaced every 2–3 days, and cells were passaged at 70%–80% confluency using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, 25200056, USA). All experiments were conducted with cells at passages below 20. Differentiation was induced by culturing cells in low-serum medium (0.5%–1% FBS) for 48–72 h.
For oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) experiments, OLN-93 cells were incubated in glucose-free DMEM under hypoxia (1% O2) for 3 h, followed by reperfusion with complete medium under normoxia for the indicated times. Rapamycin (10 nM, MedChemExpress, HY-10219, USA) or MHY1485 (5 μM, MedChemExpress, HY-B0795, USA) was applied 2 h before OGD and maintained throughout reperfusion. Controls received DMSO.

## Nuclear and cytoplasmic extraction
Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted from adherent cells using a Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, P0027, China) with minor modifications. Briefly, approximately 2 × 106 cells were washed with cold PBS and harvested by EDTA treatment or cell scraping. Cell pellets were resuspended in 200 μL Cytoplasmic extraction reagent A supplemented with 1 mM PMSF (Beyotime, ST506), incubated on ice for 10 min, followed by addition of 10 μL reagent B. After centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 5 min at 4°C, the supernatant was collected as the cytoplasmic fraction. The remaining pellets were resuspended in 50 μL nuclear extraction reagent containing 1 mM PMSF, incubated on ice for 30 min with intermittent mixing, and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was collected as the nuclear fraction. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0012, China), and all samples were stored at −80°C until analysis.

## Western blot analysis
Cells were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay (Beyotime, P0012). Equal amounts of protein (20–30 μg) were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to 0.22 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in PBST for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against Lipin1, mTOR, phospho-mTOR (Ser2448), MBP, PDGFR-α, SREBP1, HDAC2, β-actin, β-tubulin, or histone H3 (dilutions listed above). After washing, membranes were incubated with IRDye 680RD or 800CW secondary antibodies (LI-COR) for 1–2 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using an Odyssey infrared imaging system and quantified with ImageJ. All experiments were repeated at least three times.

## Primary OPC culture
Primary OPCs were isolated from neonatal mice (P0-P2). Cerebral cortices were dissected, meninges removed, and tissues mechanically dissociated in DMEM/F-12. After filtration through a 40 μm mesh, cells were plated onto poly-D-lysine-coated T25 flasks at 1–2 × 106 cells/flask and maintained in DMEM/F-12 containing 10% FBS. Mixed glial cultures were shaken after 7 days to enrich OPCs. Detached cells were collected, dissociated, and replated at 2 × 104 cells/mL. For proliferation, cultures were supplemented with bFGF (10 ng/mL). To induce differentiation, bFGF and PDGF were withdrawn and triiodothyronine (T3, 15 nM) was added. Media were changed every 3 days, and cells were differentiated for 5–10 days.

## siRNA transfection
OPCs were transfected at 60%–70% confluency using SilentFect Lipid Reagent (Bio-Rad, 1703361). siRNAs targeting Lipin1 or HDAC2 (sequences in ) were diluted in serum-free DMEM/F-12 and incubated with lipid reagent for 20 min before addition to cells. After 6–8 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Cells were harvested 24–96 h post-transfection for downstream analyses.

## Bioinformatic analysis
ChIP-seq datasets for SREBP1 were obtained from ChIP-Atlas (https://chip-atlas.org/) and Cistrome Data Browser (http://cistrome.org/db/). Genes with high-confidence binding peaks (MACS2 score ≥100) within ±1 kb of transcription start sites were identified and overlapped using Venn analysis. Enriched pathways were analyzed using DAVID (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) and Metascape (https://metascape.org/) based on KEGG annotations.

## Reverse transcription and quantitative PCR
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018, USA) and reverse transcribed with the HiScript III cDNA synthesis kit (Vazyme, R312-01, China). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Q711-02, China) on a LightCycler R480 System (Roche, Switzerland). Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2ˆ−ΔΔCt method with Gapdh as the internal control. Primer sequences are listed in .

## Co-IP
Cells were dissociated with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA, collected by centrifugation, and lysed in IP lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013, China) with protease inhibitor cocktail (1:100; Beyotime, P1005, China). Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min with intermittent vortexing and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. An 80 μL aliquot was reserved as input, mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer, denatured at 100°C for 10 min, and stored at −80°C. For immunoprecipitation, 20 μL anti-Flag magnetic beads (Sigma-Aldrich, M8823, USA) were washed with PBS and incubated with cleared lysates overnight at 4°C. Beads were washed three times with IP lysis buffer, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in SDS loading buffer at 100°C for 10 min, followed by centrifugation. Supernatants were analyzed by SDS-PAGE and western blot or stored at −80°C.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation and qPCR analysis
Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were performed using a ChIP Assay Kit (Beyotime, P2078) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde, lysed, and sonicated to generate 200–500 bp chromatin fragments. Immunoprecipitation was performed using antibodies against HDAC2 (Proteintech, 12922-3-AP, USA) or H3K9ac (Abcam, ab177177, USA), with rabbit IgG as control. DNA was purified and analyzed by qPCR using primers targeting the Sgms1 and B4galt6 promoters. Enrichment was calculated as percentage of input. Primer sequences are listed in and .

## Stereotaxic injection
Lentiviruses expressing shLipin1 or control shRNA (2 × 109 TU/mL; GeneChem) were stereotaxically injected into the corpus callosum of neonatal mice. Coordinates relative to bregma were AP: −1.60 mm, ML: 1.40 mm, DV: −1.30 mm. A total volume of 2 μL was delivered at 0.2 μL/min.

## TEM
Corpus callosum tissues were fixed with glutaraldehyde and paraformaldehyde, post-fixed with osmium tetroxide, dehydrated, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (∼50 nm) were imaged by TEM. G-ratios were calculated from axon and fiber diameters using ImageJ.

## Behavioral assessments
Open Field Test: general locomotor activity was assessed using an open field apparatus (50 × 50 × 30 cm) and recorded with the ANY-maze tracking system. Mice were acclimated to the behavioral testing room for at least 2 h before testing. Each mouse was placed in the center of the arena and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. Total distance traveled, mean velocity, and time spent in the central and peripheral zones were automatically quantified. The apparatus was cleaned with 75% ethanol between trials to eliminate olfactory cues. Rotarod Test: motor coordination and balance were evaluated using an accelerating rotarod apparatus. Mice underwent a habituation session consisting of sequential rotations at 4 rpm for 1 min, 10 rpm for 2 min, and 30 rpm for 3 min, with 15-min intervals between stages. After a 24-h recovery period, formal testing was performed. The rotarod was set to accelerate to a constant speed of 20 rpm, and latency to fall was recorded with a maximum cutoff time of 300 s. Each mouse completed three trials, and the mean latency was used for statistical analysis.

## Statistics
Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software; RRID:SCR_002798) and SPSS 24 (IBM Corp., USA). Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the Levene’s test was applied to verify the equality of variances. Two-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t test or Mann-Whitney U test. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. If a significant main effect was observed (p < 0.05), post hoc tests were conducted using Dunnett’s or least significant difference (LSD) methods as appropriate. No data were excluded from analysis, and no outlier removal was performed. Results with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
