## Abstract
The transcriptional repressor forkhead box N3 (FOXN3) has been reported to suppress pulmonary fibrosis by inhibiting Smad transcriptional activity. However, FOXN3 becomes unstable in response to profibrotic stimuli. This study identifies poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase–1 (PARP1) as a stabilizing partner of FOXN3, preventing its degradation by blocking p38-mediated phosphorylation. Lung-specific knockout (KO) of PARP1 promotes the development of pulmonary fibrosis by reducing the abundance of FOXN3. Conditional overexpression of FOXN3 notably mitigates pulmonary fibrosis resulting from PARP1 KO by impeding Smad signaling, underscoring the critical role of the PARP1-FOXN3 axis in pulmonary fibrosis. Mechanistically, p38 is a Smad response gene that is transcriptionally repressed by the PARP1/FOXN3 complex. The disruption of PARP1 or FOXN3 increases p38 expression, which in turn facilitates FOXN3 degradation through a feedback mechanism. This cascade activates Smad signaling, leading to a profibrotic response and myofibroblast activation. Notably, levels of PARP1 and FOXN3 are significantly reduced in patients with pulmonary fibrosis, highlighting PARP1’s crucial role in suppressing the disease by regulating FOXN3-mediated Smad signaling.

PARP1 interacts with and stabilizes FOXN3, thereby impeding the activation of Smad signaling and restraining pulmonary fibrosis.

## INTRODUCTION
Pulmonary fibrosis is characterized by chronic and progressive scar tissue formation and can occur in various diseases, such as systemic sclerosis, sarcoidosis, infections, and environmental factors ( – ). However, in most patients, the underlying cause of pulmonary fibrosis remains unknown, leading to its classification as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) ( , ). IPF is associated with a poor prognosis and unpredictable progression, often resulting in irreversible decline, respiratory failure, and ultimately, death ( – ). The pathophysiology of IPF involves abnormal alveolar repair processes characterized by excessive proliferation of activated fibroblasts, known as myofibroblasts. This abnormal cellular response leads to excessive deposition of extracellular matrix components, resulting in further lung tissue remodeling and impaired lung function ( , , , – ). Understanding the underlying mechanisms of IPF is critical for developing therapeutic strategies to address this challenging and debilitating condition.
Transforming growth factor–β (TGF-β) and its associated polypeptides form the largest cytokine family and are essential for regulating a variety of cellular processes, including proliferation, adhesion, differentiation, and apoptosis ( – ). These TGF-β–like factors exert their effects through two types of transmembrane receptors: the TGF-β type I receptor and the type II receptor. Intracellular signaling is mediated by Smad proteins, which serve as substrates for these receptors. When ligands bind to receptors, they become activated and phosphorylate regulatory Smad proteins, specifically Smad2 or Smad3. The phosphorylated regulatory Smad proteins then associate with Smad4 and translocate to the nucleus, where they modulate the transcription of a wide array of genes ( – ). Mounting evidence suggests that the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. Aberrant activation of this pathway can result in the overproduction of profibrotic factors, including collagen proteins and fibronectin [FN1 (fibronectin 1)] ( – ). Furthermore, this activation enhances the expression of mesenchymal marker proteins such as N-cadherin and vimentin, thereby facilitating the process of epithelial-mesenchymal transition ( – ).
Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerases (PARPs) are enzymes that add ADP-ribose groups to various substrate proteins, either as single units or as oligomers, and can create linear or branched poly-ADP-ribose chains ( – ). This modification, referred to as poly(ADP-ribosyl)ation (PARylation), affects protein stability and subcellular localization, playing a vital role in the regulation of numerous biological processes ( ). Among the 18 members of the human PARP family, PARP1 is the most thoroughly researched. It participates in several essential cellular functions, including transcriptional regulation, chromatin remodeling, apoptosis, and DNA repair ( , ). As a vital transcriptional repressor, forkhead box N3 (FOXN3), a member of the forkhead domain–containing protein family, has been recognized as a key regulator in the suppression of pulmonary fibrosis, achieved by inhibiting the transcriptional activity of Smad signaling through a mechanism involving ubiquitination ( ). In this study, we identified PARP1 as a close interacting partner of FOXN3. Functionally, PARP1 interacts directly with FOXN3 to increase its stability, playing a crucial role in the FOXN3-mediated suppression of pulmonary fibrosis. Notably, FOXN3 is an unstable protein when exposed to profibrotic stimuli, highlighting the importance of understanding how its stability is regulated in the context of Smad signaling–mediated pulmonary fibrosis. Our results show that lung-specific knockout (KO) of PARP1 leads to a substantial decrease in FOXN3 levels and accelerates the pathological progression of pulmonary fibrosis. This effect is significantly reversed upon the reintroduction of FOXN3, indicating that FOXN3 acts as an effector protein in PARP1-mediated pulmonary fibrosis. Thus, understanding the molecular mechanisms by which PARP1 regulates FOXN3 stability could reveal potential therapeutic targets for the clinical treatment of pulmonary fibrosis.

## RESULTS

## FOXN3 and PARP1 demonstrate notable colocalization at gene promoters
Previous studies have demonstrated that FOXN3 is an unstable protein in response to profibrotic stimuli and that reduced levels of FOXN3 promote pulmonary fibrosis through the transcriptional activation of Smad signaling ( ). Therefore, we investigated whether any interacting partners of FOXN3 could stabilize its regulatory role in Smad signaling–mediated pulmonary fibrosis. To this end, we reanalyzed our prior mass spectrometric data (ProteomeXchange Consortium, PXD032864) to identify potential partners of FOXN3 ( ). Notably, we found that poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase–1 (PARP1) strongly interacts with FOXN3 ( ), a finding that was further validated through endogenous and exogenous coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays ( and fig. S1A). This interaction was significantly diminished following TGF-β treatment ( ), suggesting a specific association between the two proteins. We additionally conducted immunofluorescence assays to examine the subcellular localization of FOXN3 and PARP1 in A549 and NIH3T3 cells. Our results revealed significant colocalization of FOXN3 and PARP1 in the nucleus ( and fig. S1B). To further characterize the specific region of FOXN3 required for its interaction with PARP1, we performed deletion-mapping experiments. Our data clearly indicate that the forkhead DNA-binding domain of FOXN3 is responsible for its association with PARP1 ( ), which aligns with our previous finding that the biochemical function of FOXN3 depends on its interaction with chromatin ( ). To determine whether FOXN3 directly interacts with PARP1, we purified bacterially expressed FOXN3 and PARP1 purified from Sf9 insect cells for an in vitro glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assay. The results indicated that FOXN3 does interact directly with PARP1 ( ).
 Given that FOXN3 is a transcription factor, we hypothesized that FOXN3 and PARP1 may cotarget specific gene promoters to regulate transcription. To investigate this, we performed cleavage under target and tagmentation (CUT&Tag) analysis to examine the genomic binding profiles of both FOXN3 and PARP1. Our analysis revealed 42,434 peaks from the anti-FOXN3 CUT&Tag experiment and 15,848 peaks from the anti-PARP1 CUT&Tag experiment. Notably, we identified 8536 genes that were cotargeted by FOXN3 and PARP1 at their promoters ( ), which presented similar genomic landscapes and peak distributions ( and fig. S1C). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analysis revealed that several signaling pathways, including the TGF-β signaling pathway, were coenriched (fig. S1D), suggesting that FOXN3 and PARP1 have closely related biochemical functions. Further analysis revealed that FOXN3 and PARP1 presented substantially similar peak locations within the promoter regions of representative Smad response genes ( ), confirming the physical interaction and potential functional connection between FOXN3 and PARP1 in the regulation of Smad signaling.

## PARP1 stabilizes FOXN3 to impede Smad signaling
In light of previous reports indicating that FOXN3 becomes unstable in response to profibrotic stimuli and that its down-regulation activates the Smad transcriptional response ( ), we investigated whether PARP1 contributes to the stability of FOXN3 through direct interaction, thereby modulating Smad transcriptional activity. We initially monitored the expression levels of both FOXN3 and PARP1 under profibrotic conditions. Our results indicated that both PARP1 and FOXN3 were significantly decreased following treatment with either TGF-β or bleomycin (BLM) ( , and fig. S2A). To further evaluate the impact of PARP1 on FOXN3 protein stability, we used the CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing system to knock out PARP1 in A549 cells. We then assessed the changes in the expression of FOXN3 following PARP1 disruption. Our results indicated that PARP1 KO significantly reduced FOXN3 protein levels ( ). This finding is consistent with the small interfering RNA (siRNA)–mediated PARP1 knockdown results, which revealed that PARP1 depletion accelerated the degradation of FOXN3 (fig. S2B). The instability of FOXN3 resulting from PARP1 deficiency was further corroborated by an immunofluorescence assay (fig. S2C). To validate these results in vivo, we established a lung-specific floxed KO mouse model via intratracheal delivery of an adenovirus encoding Cre recombinase. We then measured FOXN3 levels in the lungs of mice following PARP1 disruption. Immunoblot analysis consistently demonstrated that PARP1 KO led to the instability of FOXN3 ( ). Furthermore, the reintroduction of wild-type (WT) PARP1, but not the catalytically inactive mutant E988A, into Parp1−/− A549 cells significantly enhanced protein PARylation ( ), as determined by an antibody specific to ADP-ribosylated proteins. Notably, this increased PARylation resulted in a substantial elevation of FOXN3 protein levels ( ).
 As a poly(ADP-ribosyl) transferase, we sought to determine whether PARP1 stabilizes FOXN3 through PARylation. To test this hypothesis, we affinity purified Flag-tagged PARP1 and FOXN3 from human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells for subsequent in vitro reactions. Western blot analysis revealed that PARP1 is unable to catalyze the PARylation of FOXN3; however, it does undergo auto-PARylation upon the addition of NAD+ [nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (oxidized form)] in vitro (fig. S2D). As is well established, PARP1 auto-PARylation can modulate its interactions with other components ( , ). Coupled with the observation that the catalytically inactive mutant E988A lost its protective effect on FOXN3 stability, we propose that PARP1 stabilizes FOXN3 through its auto-PARylation mechanism. To test this hypothesis, we assessed the impact of two specific inhibitors of PARP1 catalytic activity, BYK204165 and PJ34, on the protein levels of FOXN3. Our findings consistently demonstrated that both inhibitors markedly suppressed protein PARylation, consequently promoting the degradation of FOXN3 ( ). This degradation hindered the occupancy of FOXN3 at Smad response gene elements, as shown by CUT&Tag assays and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) analyses ( ), ultimately leading to the transcriptional activation of Smad signaling (fig. S3, A to C). The inhibitory role of PARP1 in the suppression of the Smad transcriptional response was further confirmed in cells with PARP1 KO ( ). Furthermore, the reintroduction of exogenous WT PARP1—unlike its inactive E988A mutant—significantly blocked the transcriptional activity of Smad signaling (fig. S3D). These observations reveal a potential molecular mechanism by which FOXN3 is stabilized through the auto-PARylation of PARP1, thereby inhibiting Smad transcriptional activation.

## Lung-specific PARP1 KO promotes pulmonary fibrosis
The critical role of PARP1 in the regulation of the Smad signaling cascade prompted us to explore its potential involvement in pulmonary fibrosis. To investigate this, we isolated mouse lungs with or without conditional KO of PARP1 via intratracheal delivery of an adenovirus encoding Cre recombinase or vehicle control [phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)] before BLM treatment. Following treatment, we isolated the lungs to establish RNA expression profiles after PARP1 KO ( ). RNA sequencing analysis revealed that 755 genes were down-regulated, whereas 948 genes were up-regulated following the disruption of PARP1 ( ). Further analysis revealed that a substantial number of profibrotic regulators downstream of Smad signaling were significantly increased upon PARP1 KO ( ), suggesting a potential role for PARP1 in modulating Smad signaling–mediated pulmonary fibrosis. To confirm the functional implications of PARP1 in pulmonary fibrosis, we subsequently performed trichrome and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) histological staining analyses. Our findings revealed that PARP1 KO enhanced both profibrotic and pro-inflammatory responses in the lungs of mice ( ).
 In addition, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) revealed a significant increase in pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid collected from the lungs of PARP1-KO mice compared to those from control mice ( ). To further validate the inhibitory role of PARP1 in lung fibrosis, we evaluated the levels of profibrotic factors in mouse lungs. Our findings demonstrated that the disruption of PARP1 led to a significant up-regulation of these profibrotic factors ( ). Furthermore, the loss of PARP1 was associated with a substantial increase in total collagen content, as indicated by hydroxyproline measurements ( ).
It has been reported that the pathological process of pulmonary fibrosis involves repeated epithelial injury, which results in the dysfunction of alveolar epithelial cells and trigger the development of pulmonary fibrosis ( – ). Therefore, we examined the impact of PARP1 deficiency on epithelial cell apoptosis. The loss of PARP1 significantly facilitated apoptosis in primary type II alveolar epithelial (ATII) cells and epithelial-like A549 cells. This was evidenced by flow cytometry analysis and Western blot assays, which revealed that PARP1 disruption led to an increased proportion of apoptotic cells and elevated levels of cleaved caspase–3 (fig. S4, A to D). Furthermore, in vivo studies confirmed epithelial injury associated with PARP1 deficiency, as indicated by a marked reduction in the proliferation of surfactant protein C (ProSPC)–positive ATII cells ( ). We subsequently investigated the influence of PARP1 on myofibroblast differentiation, another contributing factor to pulmonary fibrosis ( , ). Consequently, PARP1 KO led to a significant increase in the number of α–smooth muscle actin (α-SMA)–positive cells ( ), a marker indicative of myofibroblast activation, suggesting that myofibroblast differentiation is triggered by PARP1 disruption. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PARP1 deficiency accelerates the pathological development of pulmonary fibrosis by impairing alveolar epithelial cell function and promoting myofibroblast differentiation.

## FOXN3 is essential for PARP1-mediated pulmonary fibrosis and the transcriptional activity of Smad signaling
To determine whether PARP1-mediated pulmonary fibrosis is highly dependent on FOXN3, we crossed floxed PARP1 mice with a loxP-stop-loxP (LSL) knockin (KI) FOXN3 mouse model (Foxn3LSL). We then assessed the impact of conditional FOXN3 overexpression on the pulmonary fibrotic response induced by PARP1 deficiency. Notably, the reintroduction of FOXN3 prevented the exacerbation of pulmonary fibrosis caused by PARP1 disruption, as demonstrated by Masson’s trichrome staining analysis ( ). In addition, H&E staining revealed a reduced pulmonary inflammatory phenotype following FOXN3 overexpression in the PARP1-floxed (Parp1fl/fl) mice that received Cre recombinase administration ( ). Correspondingly, ELISA analyses of BAL fluid indicated that the production of inflammatory cytokines resulting from PARP1-KO mice was also inhibited upon FOXN3 reintroduction ( ). We further examined the levels of profibrotic regulators in the lungs of PARP1-KO mice with or without FOXN3 conditional overexpression. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analyses revealed that FOXN3 overexpression significantly inhibited the production of key profibrotic factors ( ). To validate the inhibitory role of FOXN3 in the enhanced pulmonary fibrosis induced by PARP1 KO, we evaluated the collagen content in the lungs of these mice. As expected, the hydroxyproline assay clearly revealed that high levels of FOXN3 strongly impeded the increase in collagen content in the lungs of PARP1-KO mice ( ). We subsequently assessed whether FOXN3 is required for PARP1-mediated alveolar epithelial injury and myofibroblast activation. Intriguingly, enforced overexpression of FOXN3 appeared to have no significant effect on primary ATII cell apoptosis induced by PARP1 disruption (fig. S5, A and B). However, the high levels of FOXN3 significantly impeded the production of profibrotic factors (fig. S5, C and D). These in vitro observations were consistent with in vivo data showing that Cre recombinase–induced FOXN3 overexpression substantially restrained myofibroblast differentiation induced by PARP1 KO without restoring alveolar epithelial injury ( ). Collectively, these findings suggest that FOXN3 is essential for PARP1-mediated pulmonary fibrosis, primarily by modulating myofibroblast activation.
 Given that the activation of Smad signaling can trigger a profibrotic response and myofibroblast activation ( ), we further investigated the influence of FOXN3 on activated Smad signaling resulting from PARP1 disruption. To this end, we reintroduced WT FOXN3 and its forkhead DNA-binding domain–deficient mutant (ΔF), which has been reported to lose its repressive effect on Smad signaling ( ), into PARP1-KO A549 cells. We then conducted anti-Smad ChIP analyses. Our results demonstrated that the reexpression of WT FOXN3, but not the ΔF mutant, suppressed the binding capacity of the Smad2/3/4 complex to its representative target gene elements (fig. S6, A and B), leading to transcriptional inactivation of Smad signaling (fig. S6C). On the basis of these findings, we conclude that FOXN3 is crucial for PARP1-mediated pulmonary fibrosis and the transcriptional activity of Smad signaling.

## p38 is a Smad response gene that is transcriptionally repressed by the PARP1/FOXN3 repressor complex
In light of our previous report demonstrating that phosphorylation of FOXN3 at the S83 and S85 sites regulates its degradation, we hypothesized that the stabilization of FOXN3 by PARP1 is associated with this phosphorylation event. To test this hypothesis, we first monitored the levels of FOXN3 phosphorylation at S83 and S85 in response to profibrotic stimuli. Our data revealed that the phosphorylation levels of FOXN3 at S83 and S85, as well as the direct kinase p38, which is responsible for phosphorylating these sites, were significantly elevated in a time-dependent manner following stimulation with either TGF-β or BLM ( , and fig. S7A). This increase was negatively correlated with the expression levels of PARP1 and FOXN3 ( , and fig. S7A). Next, we assessed the potential role of PARP1 in regulating p38 kinase–mediated phosphorylation of FOXN3 at S83 and S85. Western blot analyses indicated that inhibiting PARP1, either through specific inhibitors or siRNA-mediated knockdown, resulted in significantly elevated protein levels of p38 ( , and fig. S7, B and C). This increase, in turn, promoted the phosphorylation of FOXN3 at serine residues S83 and S85 ( , and fig. S7, B and C). Similarly, PARP1 KO in A549 cells also resulted in a significant increase in this phosphorylation status ( ). In addition, quantitative PCR analyses showed that inhibiting PARP1 enzymatic activity or knocking out PARP1 greatly increased the RNA levels of p38 ( ), suggesting a crucial role for PARP1 in the transcriptional regulation of p38.
 To clarify the molecular basis of how PARP1 modulates p38 expression, we first assessed the distribution of both PARP1 and FOXN3 at the p38 locus via CUT&Tag analyses. Our findings revealed a strong colocalization of PARP1 and FOXN3 at the promoter region of p38 ( ). Furthermore, the inhibition of PARP1 hindered the enrichment of FOXN3 at the p38 promoter ( ). We further reanalyzed our previously published CUT&Tag data ( ) to investigate whether p38 is also a Smad response gene. We found a significant enrichment of Smad4 at the p38 promoter region, where it colocalized with the PARP1/FOXN3 complex ( ). This finding suggests the potential involvement of PARP1/FOXN3 in the transcriptional regulation of p38 via Smad signaling. To support this concept, we first assessed the chromatin loading of PARP1, FOXN3, and Smad2/3/4 in response to TGF-β treatment. ChIP assays revealed a marked decrease in the enrichment of both PARP1 and FOXN3, accompanied by an obvious increase in the Smad2/3/4 complex at the p38 promoter region following TGF-β stimulation (fig. S7D). We then measured the binding affinity of these regulators in cells with or without PARP1 inhibition. Intriguingly, the inhibition of both PARP1 enzymatic activity and PARP1 KO significantly reduced the recruitment of FOXN3 to the p38 promoter region while simultaneously increasing the levels of Smad proteins at that site ( ). Consistent with this finding, disruption of FOXN3 also led to increased occupancy of Smad2/3/4 ( ), resulting in a significant elevation of both protein and RNA levels of p38 (fig. S7, E and F).
In addition, we constructed an exogenous luciferase reporter driven by the p38 promoter to evaluate the transcriptional regulation of p38 by PARP1 and FOXN3. As expected, we observed a marked increase in luciferase activity upon inhibition of either PARP1 or FOXN3 (fig. S7G). On the basis of these observations, we conclude that p38 is a Smad response gene that is transcriptionally suppressed by the PARP1/FOXN3 repressor complex.

## p38 transcriptionally repressed by PARP1/FOXN3 complex feedback enhances the stability of FOXN3 and modulates Smad signaling
Our previous studies have shown that p38 directly phosphorylates FOXN3 at the S83 and S85 sites in response to pulmonary inflammation, leading to its instability ( ). Furthermore, this phosphorylation status was found to be significantly elevated in the presence of profibrotic stimuli, as demonstrated in . We thus hypothesized that this phosphorylation-dependent degradation of FOXN3 contributes to the activation of Smad signaling. As the direct kinase responsible for phosphorylating FOXN3 at S83 and S85, p38 is transcriptionally repressed by the PARP1/FOXN3 complex, as demonstrated above. Therefore, we investigated whether the negative regulation of p38 expression by this FOXN3-involved repressor complex could provide feedback that stabilizes FOXN3 by preventing its phosphorylation at S83 and S85, ultimately suppressing Smad transcriptional activity. To explore this hypothesis, we silenced p38 via targeted siRNAs and subsequently assessed changes in FOXN3 expression. Western blot analyses revealed that the knockdown of p38 increased FOXN3 protein levels in both A549 and NIH3T3 cells by inhibiting its phosphorylation at S83 and S85 ( ). Consistent with these results, treatment with the specific p38 inhibitor SB 203580 also increased FOXN3 protein levels while abolishing its phosphorylation at S83 and S85 ( ). To determine whether p38-mediated FOXN3 instability contributes to the activation of Smad signaling, we conducted ChIP assays with and without p38 depletion. We found that p38 knockdown significantly reduced the binding affinity of the Smad2/3/4 complex to its target gene elements ( ) while simultaneously enhancing FOXN3 enrichment at these sites ( ). This ultimately led to the transcriptional suppression of Smad signaling ( ). Similarly, cells treated with SB 203580 presented a reduced transcriptional response of Smad proteins, as evidenced by the reduced recruitment of the Smad2/3/4 complex to its target gene elements (fig. S8A), along with decreased RNA expression levels of these genes (fig. S8B). This decrease in the enrichment of Smad proteins was accompanied by an increase in the binding of FOXN3 to the Smad response elements (fig. S8C). On the basis of these findings, we conclude that the reduced abundance of p38, modulated by the PARP1/FOXN3 repressor complex, provides positive feedback to stabilize FOXN3, ultimately hindering Smad transcriptional activation.
 

## Disruption of FOXN3 phosphorylation at S83 and S85 suppresses pulmonary fibrosis induced by PARP1 deficiency
Given the critical inhibitory role of PARP1 in regulating Smad signaling by preventing p38-mediated phosphorylation and degradation of FOXN3, we aimed to investigate whether the pulmonary fibrosis induced by PARP1 deficiency is significantly dependent on this phosphorylation event. To investigate this, we crossed floxed PARP1 mice with the KI mice in which the S83 and S85 residues of FOXN3 were replaced with alanine (Foxn3KI/KI) to create a double-mutant strain. Following Cre recombinase administration, we assessed whether the abrogation of FOXN3 S83 and S85 phosphorylation could prevent PARP1 KO–induced pulmonary fibrosis. As expected, Western blot analysis revealed that conditional KO of PARP1 markedly down-regulated FOXN3 protein levels by facilitating phosphorylation at S83 and S85 ( ). However, disrupting these two phosphorylation sites resulted in a marked increase in total FOXN3 protein levels ( ). Functional studies demonstrated that the inhibition of S83 and S85 phosphorylation strongly impeded both the profibrotic and pro-inflammatory responses induced by the genetic disruption of PARP1, as evidenced by trichrome and H&E histological staining assays ( ). We further evaluated the expression levels of profibrotic factors in the lungs of these mice and found that blocking S83 and S85 phosphorylation conferred strong resistance to the increased RNA levels of these profibrotic regulators as a result of PARP1 deficiency ( ). Consistent with these findings, hydroxyproline measurements indicated that the lungs from Parp1fl/flFoxn3KI/KI mice exhibited significantly reduced collagen content compared to those from Parp1fl/flFoxn3+/+ mice following Cre recombinase administration ( ). In addition, we isolated BAL fluid from these mice and assessed cytokine production. BAL fluid analyses suggested that the inhibition of FOXN3 S83 and S85 phosphorylation critically suppressed the production of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor–α (TNFα) triggered by PARP1 KO ( ). These collective observations provide compelling evidence that PARP1 deficiency–induced pulmonary fibrosis heavily relies on the phosphorylation of FOXN3 at S83 and S85.
 

## Both PARP1 and FOXN3 expression levels are negatively correlated with clinical pulmonary fibrosis
Given the significant role of PARP1 in maintaining FOXN3 stability and its suppressive effects on pulmonary fibrosis in mice, we investigated the clinical relevance of PARP1 expression in pulmonary fibrosis. To test this hypothesis, we recruited 10 patients diagnosed with pulmonary fibrosis and evaluated the expression levels of both PARP1 and FOXN3. Consistent with previous findings showing significantly down-regulated levels of PARP1 and FOXN3 in response to profibrotic stimuli ( and fig. S7), we observed a marked decrease in the expression of both proteins in the fibrotic lesion areas compared to adjacent normal tissue samples ( ). These observations not only highlight a potentially crucial relationship between PARP1 and FOXN3 in the progression of pulmonary fibrosis but also underscore the importance of the steady-state balance of PARP1 and FOXN3 in suppressing the development of this disease.
 

## DISCUSSION
As a key suppressor of pulmonary fibrosis, we previously identified FOXN3 as an unstable protein in response to profibrotic stimuli, such as TGF-β and BLM ( ). We therefore propose that specific interaction patterns may modulate the stability of FOXN3. In our reanalysis of previous mass spectrometry data, we identified PARP1 as a close partner of FOXN3. Unexpectedly, our CUT&Tag analyses revealed that PARP1 and FOXN3 cotarget a substantial number of genes at their promoters, potentially co-regulating gene transcription and highlighting the biochemical and functional connections between these two protein regulators. Notably, the levels of PARP1 decreased alongside FOXN3 in response to profibrotic stimuli, suggesting that PARP1 may play a role in maintaining the abundance of FOXN3. Subsequent functional studies demonstrated that lung-specific KO of PARP1 significantly promotes the development of pulmonary fibrosis by regulating FOXN3 levels. Specifically, PARP1 deficiency in the lungs of mice leads to a marked down-regulation of FOXN3 protein, which serves as a central suppressor in BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis ( ). In addition, we conducted conditional overexpression of FOXN3 in the lungs of PARP1-deficient mice to validate whether FOXN3 is a key effector protein involved in the progression of PARP1-mediated lung fibrosis. Our findings indicate that reintroducing FOXN3 impedes the pulmonary fibrosis triggered by PARP1 deficiency, underscoring the critical role of FOXN3 in PARP1-modulated pulmonary fibrosis. We specifically investigated the effects of the PARP1-FOXN3 axis on myofibroblast activation and alveolar epithelial injury, two critical biological processes involved in the progression of pulmonary fibrosis ( – , ). Notably, PARP1 deficiency led to increased myofibroblast differentiation and heightened apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells, thereby facilitating the development of pulmonary fibrosis. While the reintroduction of FOXN3 significantly inhibited Smad signaling–mediated myofibroblast activation resulting from PARP1 disruption, it did not appear to have a notable impact on PARP1-mediated alveolar epithelial injury. Therefore, the intricate molecular mechanisms by which PARP1 regulates apoptosis in alveolar epithelial cells represent a compelling area for future research.
Our previous studies demonstrated that FOXN3 negatively regulates Smad transcriptional activity by ubiquitinating Smad4, which triggers the dissociation of the Smad2/3/4 complex from its target gene elements, thereby suppressing Smad signaling–mediated pulmonary fibrosis ( ). We therefore investigated the potential role of PARP1 in the FOXN3-mediated Smad signaling cascade. Our data strongly indicate that PARP1 deletion enhances Smad transcriptional activity. Furthermore, the reintroduction of FOXN3 effectively restrains the elevated Smad transcriptional activity resulting from PARP1 disruption, indicating that FOXN3 acts as the central regulator of Smad signaling activation modulated by PARP1. Notably, lung-specific overexpression of FOXN3 significantly mitigated the aggravated pulmonary fibrosis induced by PARP1 KO, underscoring the central role of FOXN3 in PARP1-mediated lung fibrosis. Although a previous study revealed that PARP1 can directly PARylate both Smad4 and Smad3 in vitro, leading to their release from Smad response elements ( ), it remains unclear whether this PARylation event contributes to the dissociation of the Smad complex in vivo. It would be intriguing for future studies to explore whether the PARylation of Smad proteins interacts with FOXN3-mediated ubiquitination of Smad4 to modulate the transcriptional activity of Smad signaling, potentially contributing to the progression of pulmonary fibrosis.
The phosphorylation of FOXN3 at S83 and S85 by p38 leads to its degradation ( ). We therefore investigated the potential involvement of this phosphorylation event in PARP1-mediated FOXN3 instability. Through CUT&Tag analyses, we observed a substantial colocalization of PARP1 and FOXN3 at the p38 promoter region, suggesting a likely role for the PARP1/FOXN3 complex in regulating p38 transcription. Reanalysis of our previously published anti-Smad4 CUT&Tag data revealed that p38 may be a potential Smad response gene, as we observed abundant binding levels of Smad4 that colocalize with the PARP1/FOXN3 complex at the p38 promoter. The loss of PARP1 or FOXN3 enhances the recruitment of the Smad complex to the p38 promoter, leading to an increase in p38 expression. Intriguingly, the elevated protein levels of p38 can provide positive feedback to promote the degradation of FOXN3 through direct phosphorylation, ultimately resulting in the overactivation of Smad signaling and the acceleration of pulmonary fibrosis ( ). In addition, while our results show that PARP1 does not catalyze the PARylation of FOXN3, its auto-PARylation may introduce a negative charge that repels the negatively charged phosphate group. This repulsion could block the phosphorylation of FOXN3 at S83 and S85, thereby contributing to its stability to some extent. In this context, PARP1 functions as a molecular switch that controls this positive feedback loop, preventing the phosphorylation-dependent degradation of FOXN3 and therefore impeding the transcriptional activation of Smad signaling. This underscores the crucial role of PARP1 in FOXN3-mediated pulmonary fibrosis through Smad signaling. Moreover, we assessed the potential clinical relevance of the PARP1-FOXN3 axis in patients with pulmonary fibrosis. Our analysis revealed that both PARP1 and FOXN3 expression levels exhibit a significant decrease in fibrotic lesion samples compared to adjacent normal areas, underscoring the strong functional relationship between these two proteins and the development of pulmonary fibrosis. On the basis of these findings, we conclude that targeting the PARP1-FOXN3 axis may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for treating pulmonary fibrosis.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Cell culture, isolation, and TGF-β treatment
HEK293T cells, NIH3T3 cells, and mouse pulmonary fibroblasts were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium, while A549 cells were grown in the F-12K medium. Both types of culture media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Millipore). Human primary ATII cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/F12 (DME/F12). All the cultures were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The HEK293T, NIH3T3, and A549 cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. For the isolation of pulmonary fibroblasts, we adhered to the detailed protocol previously described ( , ). The human primary ATII cells were purchased from iCell Bioscience (Shanghai, China). The PARP1 KO A549 cells (#YKO-H954) were purchased from Ubigene Biosciences (Guangzhou, China). For TGF-β treatment, cells were initially cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 0.05% fetal bovine serum for 8 hours before the administration of TGF-β.

## Plasmid constructions
Flag-tagged FOXN3 was constructed by cloning human complementary DNA into a pLV-EF1α lentiviral vector. HA-tagged PARP1 was created by inserting human complementary DNAs into a pCMV6 vector. All the constructs produced in this study were verified via DNA sequencing.

## Lentivirus and adenovirus
Lentivirus infection was performed using a pLV-EF1α–based core vector that expresses the target protein or short hairpin RNA (shRNA), along with psPAX2 packaging and pMD2.G envelope plasmids. These plasmids were cotransfected into HEK293T cells to generate lentiviral particles. The lentiviral infection procedure was conducted in accordance with previously established protocols ( ). In addition, an adenovirus encoding Cre recombinase was obtained from OBIO Company (Shanghai, China).

## Immunoprecipitation and GST pull-down assays
For immunoprecipitation, the cells were lysed in a buffer consisting of 50 mM tris-Cl (pH 7.4), 250 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA, and a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The resulting cell lysates were then incubated with the specified antibodies for immunoprecipitation, followed by immunoblot analysis. For GST pull-down assay, bacterially expressed GST-tagged FOXN3, purified from Escherichia coli, was incubated with PARP1 purified from Sf9 insect cells. The incubation was carried out using glutathione Sepharose beads in a buffer containing 50 mM tris-Cl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, and 1 mM EDTA.

## Apoptosis assay
Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate/propidium iodide staining was performed using a commercial apoptosis detection kit (C6031L, Amresco) to quantify apoptosis. Briefly, cells were harvested at 80 to 90% confluency, washed with PBS, and resuspended in 1× binding buffer at a density of 1 × 106 cells/ml. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate and propidium iodide in the dark for 30 min at room temperature. Analysis was immediately carried out on a BD FACS LSRFortessa flow cytometer following the manufacturer’s protocol.

## Immunofluorescence assay
Cells cultured on glass coverslips were washed three times with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100. Subsequently, they were incubated with the appropriate primary antibodies, followed by staining with fluorescently conjugated secondary antibodies. Images were acquired using a confocal microscope.

## Hydroxyproline assay
Lung hydroxyproline levels were measured via a hydroxyproline colorimetric assay kit from Sigma-Aldrich in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. In summary, the lungs of both the control and experimental mice were desiccated until a stable weight was reached. They were then hydrolyzed in 12 M HCl for 3 hours at 120°C. The resulting digests were treated with Chloramine T reagent and visualized with the p-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde reagent. The absorbance was recorded at 560 nm via a microplate reader.

## BAL fluid acquisition and analysis
Mouse lungs were lavaged three times with 0.8 ml of ice-cold PBS to collect the BAL fluid. The BAL fluid was subsequently centrifuged at 4°C to obtain the cell-free supernatant. This supernatant was then used directly to measure the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. The pro-inflammatory cytokines TNFα and IL-6 were quantified using two ELISA assay kits from Invitrogen.

## BLM-induced fibrosis model and adenovirus delivery
BLM with a dosage of 1.5 mg/kg was prepared in 50 μl of saline and administered intratracheally to the lungs of mice. Three days after BLM treatment, each mouse received an intratracheal delivery of 5 × 108 plaque-forming units of adenovirus encoding Cre recombinase. The mice were euthanized 3 weeks after adenoviral infection to allow for lung isolation for further studies.

## Animal studies
This study used 8- to 10-week-old male C57BL/6 mice from Cyagen (Suzhou, China). The Foxn3LSL KI mice, which carry a floxed stop cassette at the Hipp11 locus, were provided by Y. Liang at Xinxiang Medical University. FOXN3 KI mice with S83 and S85 mutations, as well as Parp1fl/fl mice, were generated via the CRISPR-Cas9 gene-editing system, as previously detailed ( ). The genotyping was verified by PCR analysis. All the mice were housed under specific pathogen–free conditions with a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle at 21° to 24°C and 40 to 60% humidity and fed a standard chow diet. At the conclusion of the animal studies, all the mice were euthanized using carbon dioxide inhalation. All animal studies were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Bengbu Medical University (approval number 2024-141). The specific guide RNA (gRNA) sequences for the Parp1fl/fl mice are as follows: gRNA-A1: GGCCACTTTTTTCGCCTATGAGG; gRNA-A2: CTAGGTTCTATCAGACGCACTGG. All mice in different groups were analyzed randomly to eliminate bias from drug or virus injection order. Experimental data were collected and analyzed in a blinded manner, with the operator and designer unaware of each other’s identities.

## Histological assays
For the histological staining assay, lungs of mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and then embedded in paraffin. The embedded tissue was sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. These sections were subsequently subjected to H&E staining or Masson trichrome staining analysis. The detailed procedures for staining were carried out as previously described ( , ).

## High-throughput mRNA sequencing
The mRNA sequencing experiments were carried out by GENEWIZ in Suzhou, China. In summary, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and then subjected to library construction following standard Illumina protocols. The libraries were sequenced using the paired-end RNA sequencing method on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. For data analysis, the raw reads were aligned to the reference genome using HTSeq-count and then processed with Cufflinks. Cufflinks uses normalized RNA sequencing fragment counts to evaluate the relative abundances of transcripts. DESeq2, designed for differential gene expression analysis, uses a negative binomial distribution for count values. Unlike its predecessor DESeq, DESeq2 estimates both sample depth and gene-specific parameters, applying linear regression for dispersion to accommodate shared expression variations among similarly expressed genes. It calculates the expression level of each gene in each sample using linear regression, determines P values through the Wald test, and adjusts them using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Genes with a q value of ≤0.05 and a |log2_ratio| of ≥1 are classified as differentially expressed genes. The raw data have been deposited in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under the accession numbers SRR32235374, SRR32235375, SRR32235376, SRR32235377, SRR32235378, and SRR32235379.

## Quantitative real-time PCR analysis
Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent, following the manufacturer’s instructions. For reverse transcription, 1 μg of RNA was used, and the reaction was carried out according to the guidelines provided with the 5× All-In-One RT Kit (G486, Applied Biological Materials). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using EvaGreen qPCR Master Mix (G891, Applied Biological Materials). The relative gene expression levels were calculated via the 2−ΔΔCT method. The sequences of primers used for gene amplification in this study are listed in tables S1 and S2.

## Luciferase assay
A firefly luciferase reporter plasmid featuring a promoter with a Smad response element was combined with a Renilla luciferase reporter plasmid and the specified plasmids. These constructs were cotransfected into cells for subsequent analysis. Luminescence was measured, and the analysis of Smad transcriptional activity was conducted according to previously established methods ( ).

## ChIP assay
Briefly, 1 × 106 A549 cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature and subsequently quenched with 125 mM glycine. The cells were lysed using a lysis buffer composed of 50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, and 0.5 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride. The genomic DNA was then sonicated to generate fragments ranging from 100 to 500 base pairs. After centrifugation, the cleared cell lysates were subjected to immunoprecipitation with the indicated antibodies, followed by quantitative PCR analysis. The detailed procedures were performed as previously described ( ). The sequences of primers used in the quantitative PCR analysis are shown in table S3.

## CUT&Tag assay
CUT&Tag was performed using the NovoNGS CUT&Tag High-Sensitivity Kit. A549 cells were incubated at either 37° or 4°C, harvested, and centrifuged at 500g for 4 min. Following gentle lysis and a 10-min incubation on ice, intact nuclei were collected via centrifugation. The nuclei were resuspended in PBS and cross-linked with formaldehyde for 2 min, after which the reaction was quenched with glycine. Concanavalin A–coated magnetic beads were added, and the mixture was incubated for 15 min. The unbound supernatant was discarded, and the beads were resuspended in a 1:50 dilution of the specified primary antibody, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C. After a 1-hour incubation with a secondary antibody, the nuclei were washed and incubated with the pA-Tn5 adapter complex for 1 hour. The nuclei were then resuspended in a tagmentation buffer containing Mg2+ and incubated at 37°C for 1 hour before being treated with SDS overnight at the same temperature. DNA was extracted using tagment DNA extract beads, followed by PCR amplification and cleanup using solid-phase reversible immobilization magnetic beads. The purified DNA was used for library preparation, quality assessed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100, and sequenced on an Illumina platform. The raw data have been submitted to the SRA database under the following accession numbers: SRR32226467 and SRR32226466 (for ) and SRR32235370, SRR32235371, SRR32235372, and SRR32235373 (for ).

## Patient samples
Lung samples from patients with pulmonary fibrosis were collected in accordance with research ethics board approval from Bengbu Medical University and its affiliated hospitals (approval number 2024-090). Informed consent was obtained from all participants in the study. The collected samples included both lesioned areas and corresponding adjacent normal tissues. ## Statistical analysis
All the statistical analyses were conducted via GraphPad Prism software. The data are expressed as the means ± SD and were analyzed via GraphPad. Two-tailed Student’s t test was used to compare two groups, whereas one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for comparisons involving three or more groups. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the data represent at least three independent experiments, unless otherwise noted.
