## Introduction
Osteosarcoma is frequently occurring malignant bone tumor in children and adolescents, with a high incidence occurring between 10 and 20 years of age [ ]. It predominantly affects the long bones of the extremities, such as the femur, tibia, and humerus. The etiology of osteosarcoma is multifactorial, involving genetic mutations, malignant transformation of benign bone tumors, and exposure to ionizing radiation [ ]. Clinically, osteosarcoma can be classified into localized, metastatic, and recurrent forms based on the extent of tumor spread. The current standard treatments include surgical resection, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy [ ]. To minimize tumor dissemination, neoadjuvant chemotherapy is often administered prior to surgery, followed by adjuvant chemotherapy and sometimes radiotherapy to reduce the risk of recurrence. Despite these multimodal approaches, the prognosis remains poor for many patients, highlighting the instant need for more effective and simplified therapeutic strategies [ ].
In recent years, nanoparticles have acted as highly promising tools for targeted osteosarcoma therapy. Nanoparticles are demarcated as particles with diameters in the nanometer range (1–100 nm), possessing unique physicochemical properties [ ]. Nanomedicines refer to drug delivery systems that utilize nanoparticles as carriers to encapsulate, load, or modify therapeutic agents. Typically, nanomedicines are based on engineered nanoparticles, facilitating targeted delivery, controlled release, and multifunctional therapy [ ]. Nanoparticles can be fabricated from a diverse array of materials, including organic, inorganic, and hybrid sources. Their precise engineering and functionalization allow for specific administration of treatment agents directly to osteosarcoma tumor sites, while also enabling multifunctional modalities such as imaging and hyperthermia [ ]. This targeted strategy not only improves treatment effectiveness but also minimizes systemic toxicity and the adverse side effects typically linked to traditional chemotherapy [ ]. Nanoparticle-based therapies offer several specific advantages over conventional therapies, including enhanced targeting of tumor tissues, reduced systemic toxicity, improved drug delivery efficiency, and the potential for multifunctional treatment modalities.
Moreover, nanoparticles can be developed to be sensitive to various endogenous or exogenous stimuli present in the tumor microenvironment. Traditional stimulus-responsive mechanisms include changes in pH, enzymatic activity, redox gradients, temperature, or extrinsic factors such as light energy and magnetic influences [ ]. These stimulus-responsive nanoparticles enable controlled and on-demand drug release, guaranteeing that treatment compounds are delivered at the optimal time and location to maximize their antitumor effects. Emerging mechanisms, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) responsiveness and mechantransduction, have further expanded the potential for multimodal synergistic therapies, metabolic regulation, and reversal of immune suppression in osteosarcoma. Intelligent nanoparticle-mediated drug delivery platforms can bypass physiological obstacles, enhance cellular uptake, and improve the overall pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of anticancer drugs [ ].
However, the clinical translation of nanoparticles in osteosarcoma therapy faces several challenges. The biological toxicity of nanoparticles can vary significantly depending on their composition and organ specificity, and concerns persist regarding their long-term bioaccumulation and potential chronic effects [ ]. Issues such as structural stability, reproducibility, biodegradability, and clearance rates in vivo are also under active investigation. Future development of nanomedicines for osteosarcoma should prioritize safety and scalability, incorporate artificial intelligence-driven design, and focus on multifunctional platforms responsive to the tumor microenvironment [ ].
This review summarizes the current classifications of nanoparticles utilized in osteosarcoma therapy, analyzes their stimulus-responsive mechanisms, discusses the limitations associated with nanoparticle applications, and explores future directions for the design and development of nanomedicines. These future directions include the development of biodegradable, personalized, and multifunctional nanoparticles, as well as the integration of artificial intelligence to optimize nanoparticle design. Collectively, these advancements aim to accelerate the clinical translation of nanoparticle-driven therapies for osteosarcoma.

## Nanoparticle classification and features in osteosarcoma treatment
Nanoparticles have garnered significant interest as innovative platforms for osteosarcoma therapy, primarily owing to their distinctive physicochemical properties and multifunctional capabilities [ ]. According to their composition, they can generally be classified into organic, inorganic, and hybrid types, each offering dis tinct advantages for drug delivery and therapeutic applications (Table  ). 

## Organic, inorganic, and hybrid nanoparticles
Lipid-based nanoparticles such as liposomes, solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), and nanoemulsions are widely used in biomedical applications as carriers for chemotherapeutic agents like doxorubicin and cisplatin in osteosarcoma therapy [ ]. These systems improve drug bioavailability, prolong circulation time, and enhance tumor targeting through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, while reducing systemic toxicity. Polymeric nanoparticles, including Poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA), Poly (lactic acid) (PLA), and polycaprolactone (PCL), are biodegradable and biocompatible, offering controlled and sustained drug release [ , ]. By adjusting polymer composition and particle size, drug release profiles can be optimized, improving therapeutic efficacy and patient compliance while minimizing side effects [ ]. Protein-based and peptide-based nanoparticles, such as albumin nanoparticles, are appreciated for their biocompatibility and ability to encapsulate poorly soluble drugs [ ]. They utilize natural transport mechanisms to enhance drug accumulation at tumor sites and can be further modified for targeted delivery, reducing off-target toxicity [ ]. Organic nanoparticles provide versatile and effective platforms for delivering chemotherapeutic drugs in osteosarcoma, improving drug targeting and safety.
The use of metal nanoparticles, such as gold, silver, and ferrite, confers multiple advantages in osteosarcoma management, including their application in drug delivery systems, imaging modalities, and thermal therapies. [ ]. The large surface area and straightforward functionalization of these nanoparticles enable efficient drug loading and targeted delivery, while also serving as contrast agents for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT), aiding diagnosis and treatment monitoring [ ]. Ferrite and gold nanoparticles can generate localized heat for selective tumor ablation via magnetic or photothermal therapy. Silica-based nanoparticles, especially mesoporous silica, provide excellent drug loading and surface modification capabilities [ ]. Their tunable pore structure allows efficient encapsulation and targeted delivery of chemotherapeutic agents, with surface modifications enabling stimulus-responsive controlled drug release specifically in the tumor microenvironment [ ]. Carbon nanotubes and graphene oxide, representative carbon-based nanomaterials, are characterized by high surface area and stability, making them suitable for effective drug delivery and regulated release. They also enable photothermal therapy by converting near-infrared light into heat, enhancing localized tumor treatment. Inorganic nanoparticles combine diagnostic and therapeutic functions, offering controlled and targeted drug delivery to improve osteosarcoma management and patient outcomes (Fig.  ). 
Organic–inorganic hybrid nanoparticles combine the biocompatibility and versatility of organic polymers with the unique physicochemical properties of inorganic components [ ]. This allows them to harness the advantages of both organic and inorganic materials [ ]. For example, hybrid nanoparticles can be composed of a polymer matrix loaded with chemotherapeutic drugs combined with inorganic materials, including iron oxide and gold nanoparticles [ ]. These core–shell structures can be engineered for improved stability, controlled drug release, and enhanced therapeutic efficacy. They also enable simultaneous delivery of imaging agents for real-time monitoring and can be functionalized for targeted and stimulus-responsive therapy [ ].

## Functionalized and stimulus-responsive nanoparticles
Functionalized nanoparticles are engineered with targeting molecules, such as antibodies, peptides, or carbohydrates [ ]. These surface modifications enable active drug delivery to osteosarcoma cells, thereby enhancing treatment specificity and minimizing off-target effects. Stimulus-responsive nanoparticles are designed to release their therapeutic payload in response to specific cues [ ]. These cues include changes in pH, enzyme activity, or temperature within the tumor microenvironment [ ]. By exploiting these tumor-specific conditions, stimulus-responsive nanoparticles can achieve precise drug release at the desired site and time, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy [ ]. For instance, temperature-responsive systems facilitate drug release through localized heating during hyperthermia treatments [ ]. Generally, applications of functionalized nanoparticles in osteosarcoma treatment enable intelligent focused drug delivery, limiting side effects and optimizing therapeutic results [ ].

## Other novel nanoparticles
In recent years, exosome nanoparticles have attracted considerable attention as natural nanocarriers for the treatment of osteosarcoma. Exosomes are small extracellular vesicles secreted by various cell types, playing a crucial role in intercellular communication [ ]. Due to their inherent biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and ability to cross biological barriers, they are commonly used as natural nanocarriers designed to deliver drugs or gene therapies for osteosarcoma [ , ]. One of the main advantages of exosomes as drug or gene delivery systems is their natural origin, which allows them to escape the immune system and achieve efficient cellular uptake [ , ]. Exosomes can be engineered to encapsulate chemotherapeutic drugs, including small interfering RNA (siRNA), microRNA (miRNA), or other genetic material, thereby promoting targeted therapy and gene regulation in osteosarcoma [ ]. Furthermore, their surface can be functionalized with targeting ligands to further enhance specificity for osteosarcoma cells. Similarly, in the delivery of gene therapy drugs, nucleic acid nanoparticles, with special design, can also encapsulate and deliver genetic material such as siRNA and miRNA to osteosarcoma cells. By facilitating the targeted delivery of these gene therapy drugs, nucleic acid nanoparticles can effectively regulate gene expression and interfere with molecular pathways driving osteosarcoma growth and progression [ ]. Nucleic acid nanoparticles can protect fragile genetic material from degradation in the bloodstream and ensure efficient cellular uptake [ ].

## Response mechanisms and multifunctional therapeutic platforms of nanoparticles in osteosarcoma treatment
Traditional response mechanisms in osteosarcoma treatment, such as pH-responsiveness and enzyme reactivity, have played a crucial role in developing intelligent therapeutic delivery systems [ ]. These strategies leverage the unique characteristics of the tumor microenvironment, which often differs significantly from healthy tissue [ ]. For example, many osteosarcoma tumors exhibit a weakly acidic microenvironment compared to normal tissue. pH-responsive nanoparticles maintain stability in the blood but rapidly release drugs when encountering the lower pH within osteosarcoma cells. Enzyme-responsive nanoparticles degrade overexpressed enzymes in osteosarcoma cells, releasing their drug load [ ].
Traditional response mechanisms, such as pH-responsiveness and enzyme reactivity, have greatly facilitated the development of osteosarcoma treatment by enabling more precise and efficient drug delivery. These approaches lay the groundwork for the advancement of next-generation intelligent nanocarriers (Fig.  ). 

## Emerging response mechanisms

## ROS response mechanisms
ROS response mechanisms represent an emerging innovative strategy for designing intelligent drug delivery systems for osteosarcoma treatment. Osteosarcoma cells typically exhibit higher ROS levels compared to normal tissue, creating a unique microenvironment suitable for targeted therapy [ , ]. By designing nanoparticles that specifically respond to high concentrations of ROS selective and controlled release of therapeutic drugs at tumor sites can be achieved [ ]. ROS-responsive nanoparticles are typically constructed from materials or binders sensitive to oxidative stress. When these nanoparticles encounter high concentrations of ROS in osteosarcoma tissue, they undergo structural changes or degradation, triggering the release of their encapsulated drugs [ , ]. This targeted release not only improves treatment effectiveness but also minimizes damage to healthy tissues, thereby reducing systemic side effects [ ].

## Mechanical force response mechanisms
Compared to ROS response mechanisms, mechanobiological response mechanisms involve nanoparticles sensing mechanical stimuli in the tumor microenvironment. This allows for precise drug release and improved therapeutic efficacy in osteosarcoma [ ]. As a malignant tumor originating in bone tissue, osteosarcoma possesses a unique microenvironment characterized by high rigidity, stress concentration, and abnormal extracellular matrix structure [ ]. In recent years, researchers have developed various mechanically responsive nanoparticles that can undergo structural changes in response to external or internal mechanical stimuli, such as pressure, stretching, shear force, or ultrasound, thereby triggering drug release [ ]. For instance, certain polymeric or lipid nanoparticles can rupture or open pores under ultrasound or mechanical pressure, allowing the encapsulated chemotherapeutic drugs to be rapidly released at the tumor site, increasing local drug concentration and reducing damage to normal tissues [ ]. In addition, some nanoparticles can be surface-modified with mechanosensitive molecules or peptides, enabling them to undergo conformational changes in the high-stress or high-rigidity environment of osteosarcoma tissue, further enhancing targeting and therapeutic effects [ ].
Mechanobiological response mechanisms not only improve the intelligence of nanomedicines but also provide new strategies for the precise treatment of solid tumors such as osteosarcoma [ ]. In the future, nanoparticles designed to combine the characteristics of the osteosarcoma microenvironment with multimodal mechanical stimuli are expected to achieve more efficient and safer tumor therapies.

## Multimodal synergistic therapy
Nanoparticle-based multimodal synergistic therapy is a research hotspot in osteosarcoma treatment. It differs from ROS and mechanobiological response mechanisms. This approach integrates two or more therapeutic modalities—such as chemotherapy, photothermal therapy, photodynamic therapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy—into a single nanoparticle platform to achieve enhanced therapeutic efficacy through synergistic effects [ , ]. Nanoparticles, with their excellent drug-loading capacity, surface modifiability, and targeting ability, can simultaneously carry multiple therapeutic agents or functional molecules for combination therapy [ ]. In particular, nanoparticles can co-deliver chemotherapeutic drugs and photosensitizers, enabling combined chemotherapy and photothermal or photodynamic therapy upon activation by external laser irradiation at the tumor site, thereby improving treatment efficiency and reducing drug resistance [ ]. Additionally, nanoparticles can be combined with immunomodulators to activate the immune system and enhance anti-tumor immune responses [ ].
By leveraging multimodal synergistic therapy, nanoparticles can enhance the therapeutic efficacy against osteosarcoma while simultaneously minimizing the side effects and drug resistance often seen with single-mode treatments. With ongoing progress in nanotechnology and biomedical engineering, multimodal nanoparticle platforms are anticipated to play a pivotal role in precision osteosarcoma therapy, paving the way for more effective and safer personalized treatment approaches. Preclinical studies suggest that combining photothermal therapy with chemotherapy can enhance tumor cell apoptosis and reduce tumor growth more effectively than either modality alone. Similarly, integrating immunotherapy with chemotherapy or photothermal therapy has shown synergistic effects in preclinical models, leading to improved immune activation and tumor suppression. However, clinical evidence directly comparing these combinations is still scarce, and further studies are needed to determine the most effective therapeutic strategies.

## Regulatory mechanisms

## Reversal of immunosuppression
Reversal of immunosuppression by nanoparticles in osteosarcoma represents an important treatment in tumor immunotherapy research [ ]. The osteosarcoma microenvironment is typically characterized by pronounced immunosuppression, including impaired immune cell function, high expression of immune checkpoint molecules, and increased secretion of immunosuppressive factors, all of which contribute to tumor cells evading immune surveillance and elimination. Nanoparticles can help reverse this immunosuppressive state through multiple mechanisms [ ]. Firstly, they can serve as delivery vehicles to precisely transport immune activators—such as immune checkpoint inhibitors, cytokines, or peptide vaccines—into the tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing local immune responses. Secondly, nanoparticles can modulate the function of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), dendritic cells, and T cells, promoting their polarization toward anti-tumor phenotypes and improving tumor cell killing capacity [ ]. Additionally, during photothermal or chemotherapeutic treatments, nanoparticles such as gold nanoparticles can induce the release of tumor-associated antigens from dying tumor cells. This process further activates the host immune system and enhances immune recognition and clearance of tumor cells.
By modulating these mechanisms, nanoparticles can optimize the immunological microenvironment of osteosarcoma and boost the effectiveness of immunotherapy, providing novel strategies and renewed hope for comprehensive osteosarcoma treatment (Fig.  ). 

## Osteosarcoma cells metabolic regulation
Osteosarcoma cells typically exhibit abnormal metabolic characteristics, such as enhanced glycolysis, disrupted lipid metabolism, and elevated oxidative stress, which contribute to tumor proliferation, invasion, drug resistance, and immune evasion [ ]. Nanoparticles can intervene in osteosarcoma cell metabolism through various mechanisms. Specifically, certain nanoparticles can deliver metabolic inhibitors or small-molecule drugs directly to tumor cells, suppressing glycolysis or lipid synthesis and thereby blocking energy supply to inhibit tumor growth [ ]. Additionally, nanoparticles can modulate the redox status of tumor cells by inducing or scavenging ROS, affecting cell apoptosis and proliferation [ ]. Certain smart nanoparticles, such as metal–organic framework mineralized nanoparticles, are engineered to release drugs in response to the metabolic characteristics of the tumor microenvironment. This strategy enhances the precision and efficiency of treatment. By regulating osteosarcoma cell metabolism, nanoparticles not only directly inhibit tumor progression but also enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and other treatment modalities, providing new strategies for comprehensive osteosarcoma therapy [ , ] (Fig.  ). 

## The current limitations of nanoparticle applications in osteosarcoma treatment
The issue of biological toxicity of nanomedicines in the treatment of osteosarcoma is one of the important focuses of current nanomedicine research [ ]. Although nanomedicines, such as nanoparticles, nanocarriers, have shown great potential in targeted therapy, drug delivery and imaging of osteosarcoma, their biological toxicity remains a key factor limiting their clinical application [ ] (Fig.  ). 

## Biological toxicity of nanomedicine in the treatment of osteosarcoma
The toxicity manifestations of different types of nanoparticles in the treatment of osteosarcoma vary significantly, mainly influenced by factors such as material composition, physicochemical properties, and surface modification [ ](Fig.  ). 

## Toxicity differences among different types of nanoparticles
Different types of nanoparticles exhibit significant differences in toxicity. Generally, organic nanoparticles have good biocompatibility and degradability, low toxicity, and their metabolites are mostly harmless small molecules, rarely triggering immune responses or organ damage [ ]. In contrast, inorganic nanoparticles are non-degradable or degrade slowly, potentially accumulating in vivo and leading to chronic or organ-specific toxicity. To illustrate, silver nanoparticles may induce oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, while high doses of ferrite nanoparticles may affect liver and kidney function [ ]. Carbon-based nanomaterials, due to their unique structure and surface activity, may cause cell membrane damage, inflammatory responses, and genotoxicity, posing a risk of chronic toxicity with long-term or high-dose exposure. Hybrid nanoparticles combine the properties of organic and inorganic materials; their toxicity depends on each component and surface modification [ , ].
Proper design can reduce toxicity, but inappropriate combinations may increase immunogenicity or the risk of bioaccumulation [ ]. Therefore, in the design and application of nanoparticles, the material type, dosage, route of administration, and long-term biosafety must be fully considered to ensure their safety and effectiveness in the treatment of diseases such as osteosarcoma (Fig.  ). 

## Organ-specific toxicity
Nanoparticles have broad application prospects in the medical field, but their distribution and metabolism in the body may produce toxic reactions in specific organs, i.e., organ-specific toxicity [ ]. Different types and surface-modified nanoparticles exhibit different homing characteristics in vivo, with common target organs including the liver, kidneys, lungs, spleen, and central nervous system. As an illustration, the liver is the primary organ for nanoparticle accumulation and metabolism [ ]. Long-term accumulation of some inorganic nanoparticles in the liver may cause hepatocellular damage, inflammatory responses, and even abnormal liver function. The kidneys are an important route for nanoparticle excretion; smaller nanoparticles can be filtered through the glomeruli, but high doses or non-degradable nanoparticles may lead to renal tubular damage and renal dysfunction [ ]. The lungs are susceptible to the effects of inhaled nanoparticles; materials such as carbon nanotubes can cause lung inflammation, fibrosis, and even impaired lung function. Furthermore, some nanoparticles have the capacity to target the central nervous system, potentially causing toxic effects on nerve cells. The spleen, as an immune organ, also easily accumulates nanoparticles, which may affect immune function [ ].
Thus, the organ-specific toxicity of nanoparticles is closely related to their physicochemical properties, dosage, route of administration, and in vivo distribution. Therefore, in the development and application of nanomedicines, it is necessary to fully assess their potential toxicity to major organs to ensure their biosafety (Fig.  ). 

## Long-term bioaccumulation
Long-term bioaccumulation of nanoparticles is a significant issue in nanomedicine, particularly for inorganic and carbon-based nanoparticles used in osteosarcoma treatment [ ]. These particles are typically poorly biodegradable and slowly cleared from the body, resulting in extended accumulation within organs like the liver, spleen, and kidneys, and sometimes even the lungs or brain. The reticuloendothelial system (RES) tends to take up these nanoparticles, resulting in their accumulation in the body, which can last for weeks or even months. Long-term presence of nanoparticles in tissues can trigger persistent immune responses, oxidative stress, and inflammation, ultimately impairing organ function [ ]. In some cases, accumulated nanoparticles have been shown to interact with cellular DNA, raising concerns about their genotoxicity and potential carcinogenicity. Factors such as particle size, shape, surface chemistry, and material type all influence the degree of bioaccumulation and its associated toxicity, with smaller, non-biodegradable particles posing a higher risk [ ].
To minimize long-term bioaccumulation and toxicity, researchers are working to develop biodegradable nanoparticles and surface modification methods to enhance clearance, such as polyethylene glycation [ ]. Comprehensive long-term toxicology studies must be conducted to assess the safety of these materials before they can be used clinically. Although polymer and lipid-based nanoparticles generally exhibit low bioaccumulation, continued research and standardized evaluation protocols are still needed to ensure their long-term safety in the treatment of osteosarcoma.

## Stability, biodegradability, and clearance rate in current nanoparticle applications
The stability, biodegradability, and clearance of nanoparticles are critical factors influencing their safety and therapeutic efficacy in osteosarcoma treatment.
Stable nanoparticles maintain their structural integrity, prevent aggregation, and retain drug payloads under physiological conditions, ensuring consistent drug release, prolonged circulation, and effective tumor targeting [ ]. Achieving optimal stability remains challenging because interactions with biomolecules in the bloodstream can cause aggregation or rapid clearance of nanoparticles. To address this, surface modifications such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) coating (PEGylation) and the use of stabilizing agents are commonly employed to enhance colloidal stability and prolong circulation time.
Biodegradability is equally important, as nanoparticles made from materials such as PLGA, PCL, chitosan, or albumin are designed to break down into non-toxic byproducts [ ]. This allows for safe elimination from the body, minimizing long-term accumulation and toxicity [ ]. Surface engineering strategies, such as applying biodegradable coatings or incorporating cleavable linkers, can further enhance the safe metabolism and excretion of nanoparticles. These approaches help address concerns about the persistence and potential toxicity of inorganic or carbon-based materials [ ].
Clearance rate, determined by factors such as particle size, surface charge, and hydrophilicity, dictates how efficiently nanoparticles are removed from the body via renal or hepatic pathways [ ]. Nanoparticles designed for optimal size and surface properties can achieve favorable clearance profiles, reducing the risk of bioaccumulation and chronic toxicity [ ]. Overall, ongoing research aims to develop nanoparticle formulations that balance robust stability, efficient biodegradability, and predictable clearance, thereby maximizing therapeutic benefit while ensuring long-term safety in clinical applications.

## Reproducibility in current nanoparticle applications
Reproducibility is a fundamental requirement for the successful translation of nanoparticle-based therapies from laboratory research to clinical practice [ ]. In current applications, reproducibility refers to the ability to consistently synthesize nanoparticles with uniform size, shape, surface properties, and drug loading across different batches [ ]. High reproducibility ensures predictable pharmacokinetics, therapeutic efficacy, and safety profiles, which are essential for regulatory approval and large-scale manufacturing [ ]. Nevertheless, achieving reproducibility in nanoparticle synthesis remains challenging due to the sensitivity of production processes to variations in raw materials, reaction conditions, and scaling methods. Minor changes in temperature, pH, mixing speed, or precursor concentrations can lead to significant differences in nanoparticle characteristics. Additionally, complex surface modifications and drug encapsulation techniques further complicate standardization [ ]. As a result, batch-to-batch variability can affect the stability, clearance rate, and biological performance of nanoparticles.
To address these challenges, researchers are developing standardized protocols, advanced characterization techniques, and automated manufacturing systems to improve reproducibility. Regulatory agencies also emphasize the importance of quality control and validation in nanoparticle production (Table  ). 

## Clinical translation in current nanoparticle applications
Clinical translation is a pivotal step in the development of nanoparticle-based therapies, facilitating the translation of laboratory research into clinical applications. In current applications, the clinical translation review process involves rigorous evaluation of nanoparticle formulations for safety, efficacy, reproducibility, and scalability [ ]. Regulatory agencies such as the US food and drug administration (FDA), and european medicines agency (EMA) require comprehensive preclinical studies, including toxicology, pharmacokinetics, and biodistribution assessments, before approving clinical trials [ ]. These reviews also emphasize the importance of standardized manufacturing processes and quality control to ensure batch-to-batch consistency [ ].
Despite significant advances in nanoparticle technology, clinical translation remains challenging due to issues such as long-term toxicity, bioaccumulation, and variability in biological responses [ ]. Organic nanoparticles often face challenges related to stability and rapid clearance in vivo, while inorganic nanoparticles are limited by potential long-term toxicity and poor biodegradability. Hybrid nanoparticles aim to combine the advantages of both types, but their complex structures can complicate large-scale manufacturing and regulatory approval. Many promising nanoparticle systems demonstrate excellent results in vitro and in animal models but face obstacles in human trials, including unpredictable immune reactions and difficulties in large-scale production [ ]. Furthermore, the complexity of nanoparticle formulations often necessitates specialized analytical methods and regulatory frameworks, which can prolong the approval process [ ].
To facilitate successful clinical translation, ongoing research focuses on improving the safety profiles, reproducibility, and scalability of nanoparticle formulations [ ]. Effective cooperation among researchers, clinicians, and regulatory authorities is crucial for addressing translational barriers and accelerating the adoption of nanomedicine in clinical settings [ ]. Ultimately, thorough clinical translation review ensures that nanoparticle-based therapies for osteosarcoma and other diseases are both effective and safe for patient use (Fig.  ). 

## Medical ethical considerations in current nanoparticle applications
Medical ethical considerations are increasingly important in the development and clinical application of nanoparticle-based therapies [ , ]. As these technologies advance, issues such as patient safety, informed consent, risk–benefit assessment, and equitable access must be carefully addressed [ , ]. The potential for long-term toxicity, bioaccumulation, and unforeseen side effects raises ethical concerns regarding the responsible use of nanoparticles in humans, especially in vulnerable populations such as children or patients with compromised health [ ]. Informed consent forms the foundation of ethical medical practice, ensuring that patients are thoroughly informed about the potential risks and benefits of nanoparticle-based therapies, such as the use of metal nanoparticles in combination with chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Researchers and clinicians must provide transparent information regarding the experimental nature of these therapies, possible adverse effects, and uncertainties related to long-term outcomes. Additionally, ethical review boards play a crucial role in assessing study protocols to ensure that patient welfare is prioritized and that research adheres to established ethical standards.
Equitable access to advanced nanoparticle therapies is another key ethical consideration, as disparities in healthcare resources may limit availability for certain populations. Efforts should be made to ensure that the benefits of nanomedicine are distributed fairly and that vulnerable groups are not disproportionately exposed to risks. Overall, integrating medical ethics into the development and application of nanoparticle technologies is essential for safeguarding patient rights, promoting public trust, and guiding responsible innovation in osteosarcoma and other disease treatments (Table  ). 

## Future directions in the design and development of nanoparticles for osteosarcoma treatment
The future development of nanomedicines for osteosarcoma is expected to emphasize novel design strategies to improve treatment effectiveness, safety, and the potential for clinical application.

## Security
Safety remains a primary consideration in the design and development of nanomedicines for future osteosarcoma treatment [ ]. Since nanocarriers are designed to deliver therapeutic drugs directly to the tumor site, it is crucial to ensure that these systems do not induce significant toxicity or adverse immune responses in patients [ ]. Future research will focus on utilizing biocompatible and biodegradable materials that can be safely metabolized and eliminated from the body, thereby minimizing the risk of long-term accumulation and chronic toxicity [ ]. Comprehensive preclinical and clinical safety assessments are essential, including studies on biodistribution, immunogenicity, genotoxicity, and potential off-target effects [ ]. Developing standardized testing protocols and long-term monitoring strategies is also important to help identify and mitigate any unforeseen risks that may exist in novel nanomedicine formulations [ ]. Ultimately, prioritizing safety in the design of future nanomedicines is crucial for obtaining regulatory approval and ensuring patient confidence in these advanced osteosarcoma treatments.

## Artificial intelligence-driven nanoparticle design
Artificial intelligence (AI) holds promise for transforming the design and development of nanoparticles for osteosarcoma treatment [ ]. Through the application of machine learning and big data analytics, researchers are able to rapidly screen and optimize nanoparticle formulations based on a variety of physicochemical properties, biological interactions, and therapeutic effects [ , ]. AI-driven approaches can predict the behavior of nanoparticles in complex biological environments, helping to determine optimal materials, sizes, shapes, and surface modifications to enhance targeting, stability, and safety [ , ]. Furthermore, AI can assist in analyzing patient-specific data, such as genomic profiles and tumor microenvironment characteristics, guiding the development of personalized nanomedicine strategies [ , ]. This data-driven design process can accelerate the discovery of novel nanocarriers and reduce reliance on traditional trial-and-error methods, thereby improving efficiency and reproducibility [ , ]. With the continuous advancement of AI technology, its application in nanoparticle research promises to bring smarter, more effective, and safer treatment options for osteosarcoma and other cancers (Fig.  ). 
The orally administered novel drug ISM8969, discovered and designed using an AI platform, has been officially approved by the US FDA to enter human clinical trials. The upcoming Phase I clinical trial will assess the drug’s safety, tolerability, and pharmacokinetics in healthy volunteers, thereby laying the foundation for subsequent efficacy studies in patients.

## Tumor microenvironment response platform
The development of tumor microenvironment-responsive nanoparticle platforms represents a promising direction for the future treatment of osteosarcoma [ ]. These advanced nanocarriers are meticulously designed to recognize and respond to distinct physiological features of the tumor microenvironment, supporting controlled and localized drug release, which improves therapeutic results and decreases systemic toxicity [ ]. Responsive drug delivery platforms typically incorporate smart materials or cleavable linkers that trigger drug release only in the presence of tumor—associated stimuli. Tumor microenvironment-responsive nanoparticle platforms offer significant advantages in precision medicine, providing more effective and safer treatment options for osteosarcoma. Current research aims to further refine these systems by integrating multiple response mechanisms and combining therapeutic drugs with diagnostic functions, thus paving the way for next-generation nanomedicine solutions [ ].
Tumor microenvironment-responsive platforms are designed to exploit specific stimuli within the tumor milieu, such as acidic pH, elevated levels of ROS, and overexpressed enzymes, such as matrix metalloproteinases. Among these, pH, ROS, and enzymatic activity are considered the most clinically relevant triggers for controlled drug release. However, translating these lab-based designs to clinical applications remains challenging due to the heterogeneity of tumor microenvironments in patients, variability in stimulus intensity, and difficulties in achieving precise responsiveness and safety in vivo.

## Commercial-scale production
Commercial-scale manufacturing is a crucial step in the future development and clinical translation of nanomedicines for osteosarcoma treatment. Among nanomedicines either approved for clinical use or undergoing various stages of clinical translation, liposomes and lipid-based nanoparticles constitute the most prevalent category, accounting for approximately 33% of all formulations; Vyxeos serves as a representative example. Antibody–drug conjugates comprise around 15%, while polymer-drug and polymer-protein conjugates represent approximately 10%, exemplified by Abraxane. Additionally, polymer-based nanoparticles account for another 10% of nanomedicine platforms [ , ].
The shift from laboratory-scale synthesis to mass production encounters several significant challenges, including maintaining batch-to-batch consistency, ensuring product quality, and meeting regulatory standards [ ]. Scalable and reproducible manufacturing processes are essential for producing nanoparticles with uniform size, shape, surface properties, and drug loading, all of which directly impact therapeutic efficacy and safety. To address these challenges, advanced manufacturing technologies such as microfluidics, continuous flow synthesis, and automated process control can be explored [ ]. These methods enable precise control of reaction parameters and facilitate the production of high-quality nanoparticles at industrial scale [ ]. Furthermore, implementing Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) guidelines and rigorous quality assurance programs is critical to ensuring regulatory compliance and supporting clinical and commercial applications [ ]. Cost-effectiveness and scalability must also be considered to ensure nanomedicines reach a wider patient population. Collaboration among academic researchers, industry partners, and regulatory agencies is essential to overcoming manufacturing hurdles and accelerating the commercialization of nanoparticle-based osteosarcoma therapies [ ]. Ultimately, the successful development of robust commercial-scale manufacturing platforms will play a key role in bringing innovative nanomedicines from the laboratory to the clinic.

## Conclusion
Nanoparticles offer significant advantages in osteosarcoma treatment, including enhanced drug targeting, efficacy, and reduced systemic toxicity, with various types showing promising preclinical results. Despite these advances, challenges remain in ensuring long-term safety, scalable manufacturing, and regulatory approval. Future research will focus on developing biodegradable, personalized, and multifunctional nanoparticles, as well as integrating artificial intelligence to optimize design and accelerate clinical translation. To summarize, nanomedicine holds great promise for more effective, safe, and individualized osteosarcoma therapies.
