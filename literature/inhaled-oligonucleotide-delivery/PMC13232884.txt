In the United States alone, sepsis incurs healthcare costs exceeding $24 billion per year and remains without any FDA-approved targeted therapies [ ]. Although advances in antimicrobial treatment, organ support strategies, and immunomodulatory interventions have modestly reduced in-hospital mortality [ ], long-term outcomes remain poor, with persistently high post-discharge mortality rates [ , ]. This therapeutic stagnation highlights the pressing need for deeper mechanistic insights into sepsis pathophysiology to identify novel therapeutic targets.
The traditional paradigm of sepsis pathogenesis centers on early-stage hyperinflammation and cytokine storm as key drivers of mortality [ ]. However, clinical trials targeting principal inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and Toll-like receptors have failed to improve survival [ ], indicating the involvement of previously unrecognized pathogenic pathways in sepsis progression.
Interleukin-24 (IL-24), a member of the IL-10 cytokine family, is broadly expressed in innate and adaptive immune cells [ , ], including monocytes, macrophages, T cells, B cells, and NK cells, and modulates their functional responses. Recent evidence indicates that IL-24 promotes inflammatory responses and exacerbates susceptibility to autoimmune disorders such as psoriasis, rheumatoid arthritis, and atopic dermatitis [ ]. However, the role of IL-24 in sepsis, a condition defined by the host’s inflammatory response to infection, remains poorly understood.
In this study, we observed a marked increase in serum IL-24 levels in septic mice, prompting a systematic investigation of its functional role in sepsis pathogenesis. Using IL-24−/− knockout mice in an experimental Escherichia coli(E. coli)-induced sepsis model, we found significantly improved survival compared to wild-type controls. Mechanistic analyses revealed that IL-24 exerts dual pathogenic effects by promoting both bacterial accumulation and leukocyte recruitment to sites of infection. Importantly, therapeutic inhibition of IL-24 expression significantly reduced sepsis-related mortality in mice. These findings reveal a previously unappreciated role for IL-24 in sepsis immunopathology and support its potential as a promising target for precise therapeutic intervention.

## Materials and methods

## Differential gene expression and correlation analyses
RNA sequencing data were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE90727 & GSE137340). The R package featureCounts was used for data preprocessing. Differential expression of IL-6, IL-1β, IL-17, IL-24, and IL-1α was analyzed using DESeq2, and visualized with pheatmap. Pearson correlation coefficients between IL-24 and IL-6, IL-1β, IL-17, or IL-1α were calculated using the stats package in R.

## Animals
All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Animal Experiments at Guangzhou Lingfutop Bio Co., Ltd. (Approval No. LFTOP-IACUC-2024-0058) and were adhered to ARRIVE guidelines. Experiments were conducted in the Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF) Experimental Animal Center of Guangzhou Lingfutop Bio Co., Ltd. C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Zhuhai BesTest Bio-Tech Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). C57BL/6 IL-24−/− knockout (KO) mice (#S-KO-15440) were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system and obtained from Cyagen Biosciences (China). Cas9 mRNA and single-guide RNA (sgRNA) were introduced via high-throughput zygote electroporation to generate IL-24 heterozygous (IL-24+/−) mice. Homozygous IL-24−/− mice were identified by PCR genotyping of IL-24+/− offspring (#D7283S, Beyotime, China). All animals were acclimated for one week prior to model establishment and were euthanized by dislocation of the cervical vertebra after intraperitoneally (i.p.) injection of 20 μL tribromoethanol per gram of mouse weight [ ] (#M2910, Aibei Biotechnology, Nanjing, China). Besides, the details of the key reagents were shown in Supplementary Table S3.

## E.
coli culture
The Escherichia coli strain ATCC 25922 was kindly provided by Prof. Menghua Xiong (South China University of Technology, Guangzhou, China). Bacteria were cultured in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth (#HB8297-5, Hopebio, China) at 37°C. After 12 h of incubation, cultures were centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min at 25°C, and the pellet was resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; #P1020, Solarbio, China) for downstream applications.

## Mouse model of sepsis induced by E. coli
Wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 male mice (6–8 weeks old; n = 4) were ii.p. injected with 5 × 107
E. coli in 0.2 mL PBS. Control mice (n = 4) received 0.2 mL PBS via i.p. injection. Four hours post-injection, blood was collected for serum IL-24 analysis.
To evaluate survival and downstream effects, WT (n = 13) and IL-24−/− (n = 12) mice were infected i.p. with 5 × 107
E. coli in 0.2 mL PBS. After 4 h, 8 mice per group were euthanized for further analysis. The remaining mice were monitored for survival at 6, 12, 18, 24, 48, and 72 h.
In order to detect the effect of recombinant IL-24 protein (rIL-24) on sepsis, C57BL/6 mice were assigned to control (PBS, i.p.) and rIL-24 treatment (1 μg, i.p.) groups (n = 6/group). One hour after injection, all mice were infected i.p. with 5 × 107
E. coli. Mice were euthanized 4 h later for subsequent analyses.
For gene knockdown, mice were divided into two groups: small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting IL-24 (forward: 5′-CCGCAGAGCAUUCAAACAGUUTT-3′; reverse: 5′-AACUGUUUGAAUGCUCUGCGGTT-3′; n = 14) and negative control (NC; forward: 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCAC-3′; reverse: 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′; n = 13). Complexes were prepared using transfection reagent (#V20207005, Golden Transfer, China) and injected i.p. (3.3 nmol per mouse). After 12 h, all mice were infected i.p. with 5 × 107
E. coli and observed for survival at 6, 12, 18, 24, 48, and 72 h.
All CFU back-titer verification of animal experiments was shown in Supplementary Figure S2(D, E). The blinding process of animal experiments was shown in Supplementary Table S4.

## Peritoneal lavage and flow cytometry
Mice were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane. A total of 5 mL sterile PBS was injected into the peritoneal cavity, and peritoneal lavage fluid (per-lav) was collected via abdominal incision. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation, resuspended in FACS buffer, and stained for 20 min at 4°C with APC-conjugated anti-mouse Gr-1 antibody (#108411, BioLegend, USA) and FITC-conjugated anti-mouse CD3 antibody (#100203, BioLegend, USA). A total of 10,000 events per sample were acquired using a DxFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA) and analyzed with FlowJo software (v10.8.1, BD Biosciences, USA).

## Capillary assay for bacterial chemotaxis
Bacterial chemotaxis was assessed using a modified capillary assay as described by Jose et al. [ ]. One end of a capillary tube (#5-000-2005, Drummond Scientific, USA) was filled with either PBS or 10 μg/mL recombinant IL-24 (rIL-24; #HY-P700200AF, MCE, USA)/recombinant IL-19 (rIL-24; #HY-P7213, MCE) diluted in PBS, and the other end was sealed with paraffin. The open end was immersed in 200 μL of 1 × 105
E. coli for 1 h. After incubation, capillaries were washed, and their contents were expelled into microcentrifuge tubes containing 50 μL PBS. Samples were plated on BHI agar using an L-shaped spreader and incubated at 37°C for 12 h. Colony counts were photographed and quantified using a Scan® 500 colony counter (Interscience, France).

## Blood collection
Mice were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane, and blood was collected via periorbital puncture using EDTA-coated tubes (#CSBHC-CXG-KN-5ML, Prosperich). Blood samples from experimental groups were analyzed using the BC-5000Vet hematology analyzer (Mindray Animal Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) to determine white blood cell (WBC), neutrophil (NEU), and lymphocyte (LYM) counts. Blood samples from healthy male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) were used for the isolation of neutrophils and CD3+ T cells.

## Bacterial burden determination
Spleens and livers were harvested, weighed, and homogenized in 1 mL PBS. Serial dilutions were plated on BHI agar, and colony-forming units (CFUs) were enumerated using the Scan® 500 automatic colony counter (Interscience, France) following 12 h of incubation at 37°C.

## Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining
Spleens and livers were harvested and were immediately fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, dehydrated by an automatic dehydrator (Leica ASP300S, Germany), and fully embedded in paraffin. Finally, the tissues were carefully dissected into 4 μm thick slices. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the slices were subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval, blocked with 3% H2O2 and 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and then sequentially co-incubated with IL-24 antibody (1:400; #6772-1-AP, Proteintech, USA) for 12 h at 4°C. DAB horseradish peroxidase color development kit (#G1212-200T, Servicebio, China) was used for signal detection. Finally, all the slices were stained with hematoxylin for 5 s. The slices were scanned by Aperio VERSA 8(Lecia, Germany) and analyzed by IHC Profiler of Image J.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
Within 30 min of collection, blood was centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 10 min at 25°C, the serum was harvested and diluted 10 times by PBS. IL-24 levels (1:10 dilution) were quantified using a sandwich ELISA kit (#YNL-20180, Chengdu yuannuo tiancheng technology Co., Ltd, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a BioTek Synergy H1 multi-mode microplate reader (Agilent Technologies, USA), with standard concentrations ranging from 6.25 to 200 pg/mL.

## Isolation of neutrophils and CD3+ T cells
Neutrophils were isolated from whole blood using a density gradient centrifugation method (#P9201, Solarbio, China). Whole blood was diluted 1:1 with PBS, layered onto the gradient, and centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 30 min at 25°C. Neutrophils and lymphocytes were aspirated from their respective interfaces. Red blood cells in the neutrophil fraction were lysed osmotically. Neutrophils were washed three times in PBS (500 × g, 5 min) and resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium (#11875093, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% Fetal bovine serum (FBS, #FB25015, Clark, USA) for immediate use or stored in liquid nitrogen.
Lymphocyte viability (>95%) was confirmed by trypan blue exclusion (#3006793, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). CD3+ T cells were isolated using the MojoSort™ Mouse CD3 T Cell Isolation Kit (#480023, BioLegend, USA). Briefly, 100 μL of a 107 cells/mL suspension was incubated with 10 μL of antibody cocktail on ice for 15 min, followed by 10 μL of streptavidin nanobeads for another 15 min. After adding 2.5 mL buffer, cells were magnetically separated twice. Isolated CD3+ T cells were centrifuged and resuspended in RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS for immediate experiments or cryopreservation.

## Transwell migration assay
To evaluate the chemotactic effect of IL-24, cells including neutrophils, CD3+ T cells, HL60 (#CL-0110, Procell, China) and Jurkat cells (#GC40108, Ginkgo Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China), were seeded in the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (#TCS003024, Biofil, China). HL60 and were Jurkat cells, respectively, pretreated with 20 μM Reparixin [ ] or 10 μM Elubrixin [ ] for 30 min before seeding. Control wells contained standard medium in the lower chamber, while experimental wells contained medium supplemented with 1 μg/mL recombinant IL-24. After 12 h of incubation, migrated cells including HL60 and Jurkat cells in the lower chamber were visualized under a microscope and quantified. After 24 h of incubation, migrated cells including neutrophils and CD3+ T cells in the lower chamber were visualized under a microscope and quantified.

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v9.0.0, GraphPad Software, USA). Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and between-group differences were analyzed via the Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon test, which assigns greater weight to events occurring earlier in the time course. The hazard ratio (HR) and its 95% confidence interval were calculated using the Mantel–Haenszel method. The significance threshold was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed). Quantitative data were assessed for normality using Shapiro–Wilk tests with p value >0.05. Between-group comparisons employed unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests following confirmation of variance homogeneity (F-test, p value >0.05). The significance threshold was defined as α = 0.05. If p value was less than 0.05 in F-test, the data were used two-tailed Student’s t-test with Welch’s correction. The significance threshold was defined as α = 0.05. If data was not Gaussian distribution, the data were used two-tailed Mann–Whitney test. The significance threshold was defined as α = 0.05.

## Results

## Bioinformatic analysis revealed a pro-inflammatory role for IL-24 in sepsis
Analysis of the GEO datasets (accession: GSE90727 & GSE137340) revealed significant upregulation of inflammatory mediators, including i(IL)-24, IL-6, IL-1β, IL-17, and IL-1α, in the blood of mice ( ) and patients ( ) with peritonitis-induced sepsis. Correlation analysis in mouse and human showed similar results that IL-24 expression was no significantly correlated with IL-1β ( ), and positively correlated with IL-6 (Pearson’s r = 0.4612, p < 0.0079: ; Pearson’s r = 0.4888, p = 0.0007: ), IL-1α (r = 0.8239, p < 0.0001: ; Pearson’s r = 0.5598, p < 0.0001: ), and IL-17 (r = 0.6165, p = 0.002; ; Pearson’s r = 0.3151, p = 0.0350: ), suggesting that IL-24 may function as a pro-inflammatory mediator in the cytokine network during sepsis pathogenesis.
 

## IL-24 is highly expressed in septic mice, and its deficiency improves survival
Peritonitis-induced sepsis was established via intraperitoneal injection of E. coli ( ). ELISA revealed significantly elevated serum IL-24 levels in septic mice compared with healthy controls (p = 0.0013; ). IL-24−/− KO mice were generated ( ) and validated via genotyping ( ). IL-24 protein was virtually undetectable by ELISA in plasma samples from IL-24−/− (KO) mice, in contrast to WT controls (Supplementary Figure S2(C)). In sepsis models using WT and IL-24−/− mice ( ), the survival rate of IL-24−/− KO mice without IL-24 expression (Supplementary Figure S2(A,B)) was significantly higher than that of WT mice (p = 0.0046; & Supplementary Table S1), indicating that genetic ablation of IL-24 attenuates sepsis-associated mortality.
 

## IL-24 deficiency reduces bacterial burden and leukocyte infiltration
At 4 h post-sepsis induction, bacterial load and immune cell profiles were assessed in IL-24−/− and WT mice ( ). CFU assays showed a 24% and 44% reduction in splenic and hepatic bacterial loads, respectively, in IL-24−/− mice compared with WT mice (p < 0.0001; ), suggesting that IL-24 promotes bacterial dissemination. Flow cytometry of peritoneal lavage fluid revealed significant reductions in Gr1+ neutrophils (p = 0.0031) and CD3+ T cells (p = 0.0016) in IL-24−/− mice ( ), indicating attenuated local immune cell recruitment. Furthermore, systemic analyses showed decreased total white blood cells (p = 0.0209), neutrophils (p = 0.0412), and lymphocytes (p = 0.0017) in IL-24−/− mice ( ), suggesting a dampened systemic inflammatory response.
 

## IL-24 enhances bacterial accumulation and immunocyte chemotaxis in vitro
Based on the in vivo findings, we hypothesized that IL-24 may aggravate hyperinflammation by promoting bacterial accumulation and immune cell chemotaxis. Capillary assays ( ) demonstrated that rIL-24 significantly enhanced E. coli accumulation under protein-gradient assay conditions compared with controls (p = 0.0079; ). Transwell assays ( ) further confirmed that rIL-24 significantly increased the migration of neutrophils (p = 0.0248; ) and T cells (p = 0.0015; ) to the lower chamber. These findings indicate that IL-24 augments early inflammation by promoting the recruitment of both bacteria and immune cells.
 

## In vivo validation of IL-24’s pathogenic role and therapeutic targeting potential
To confirm the pathogenic role of IL-24, rIL-24 was administered intravenously to C57BL/6 mice 1 h before E. coli-induced sepsis ( ). At 4 h post-injection, rIL-24-treated mice exhibited significantly higher splenic (p = 0.0124) and hepatic (p = 0.0002) bacterial loads than controls ( ). Complete blood counts ( ) showed marked increases in white blood cells (p = 0.0009), neutrophils (p = 0.0022), and T cells (p = 0.0136) in the rIL-24 group.
 
To assess the therapeutic potential of IL-24 inhibition, we designed IL-24 siRNA modified with a 3′ methoxy group for increased stability and a 5′ cholesterol moiety for enhanced cellular uptake. IL-24 siRNA or negative control was administered intraperitoneally 12 h before E. coli-induced sepsis ( ). Mice receiving IL-24 siRNA exhibited a 72-h survival rate of 77%, compared with 50% in controls (log-rank p = 0.0136; & Supplementary Table S2), indicating that targeted silencing of IL-24 confers significant survival benefit.

## Discussion
This study provides novel insight into the pathogenic role of IL-24 in an E. coli-induced peritonitis model of sepsis. Our data demonstrated a significant elevation of IL-24 expression in septic mice. Notably, IL-24-deficient mice exhibited markedly improved survival, reduced bacterial colonization in major organs, and decreased leukocyte infiltration in both peritoneal lavage fluid and peripheral blood. In contrast, administration of rIL-24 exacerbated sepsis pathology, leading to accelerated mortality, increased bacterial burden, and enhanced leukocyte infiltration. Furthermore, IL-24-targeted siRNA significantly reduced mortality in septic mice, supporting its potential as a therapeutic target in precision medicine approaches for sepsis management.
Sepsis is characterized by the rapid release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, resulting in a “cytokine storm” and subsequent multi-organ dysfunction and failure [ ]. While historical trials targeting late-stage, pleiotropic mediators like IL-6 or the early alarmin TNF-α have largely failed—often due to redundant pathways or narrow therapeutic windows [ , ]—IL-24 occupies a critical niche: it is a potent inducer of multiple key cytokines (including IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ) and its inhibition offers a broader dampening of the inflammatory cascade [ , ]. Our bioinformatics analyses preliminarily verified that elevated IL-24 expression that positively correlated with high levels of canonical pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β [ ], IL-6 [ ], and IL-17 [ ] in septic mouse and human blood, and in patients with severe sepsis, the expression of IL-24 significantly enhanced (Supplementary Figure S1). Previous studies have also proved that IL-24 is not only on the rise in septic infants, compared with healthy infants, but also provides as one of the predictive inflammatory biomarkers of necrotizing enterocolitis in premature infants [ ]. Furthermore, IL-24’s unique position in the IL-10 family, where it paradoxically signals through a shared receptor with anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 [ , ] to exert pro-inflammatory effects by different signal pathways [ ]. The most compelling evidence comes from genetic models, where IL-24−/− mice exhibit significantly improved survival, demonstrating that its function is non-redundant and critically pathogenic, thereby validating IL-24 as a promising node for intervention that addresses the complexity of sepsis more effectively than targeting individual downstream effectors.
The well-established Cecal Ligation and Puncture (CLP) model is considered the gold standard for its ability to recapitulate the polymicrobial nature of human sepsis [ ]. The lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced sepsis model is widely used due to its ability to rapidly induce high cytokine levels, but given its rapid clearance and lack of microbial replication, it fails to mimic the prolonged inflammatory response observed in clinical settings [ , ]. The use of a monomicrobial E. coli sepsis model was a deliberate choice to meet the specific aims that precisely dissect the role of IL-24 in the host response to a defined Gram-negative challenge. The E. coli model provides superior controllability and reproducibility, enabling a clear mechanistic interpretation of IL-24’s effects by eliminating the confounding variables inherent in polymicrobial infections. Furthermore, as Gram-negative bacteremia represents a major clinical entity [ ], this model offers direct relevance and long-lasting inflammatory reaction [ ]. We propose that the core immune functions of IL-24 identified here likely constitute fundamental host defense mechanisms with broader implications for septic shock pathogenesis.
Sepsis involves a dysregulated immune response in which pathogens evade host defenses, replicate, and induce sustained tissue damage, particularly via overactivation of leukocytes, ultimately disrupting immune homeostasis [ ]. To understand how IL-24 contributes to this process, we investigated its impact on bacterial pathogens and leukocyte behavior. IL-24−/− mice showed significantly reduced bacterial loads during early sepsis, and in vitro assays demonstrated that rIL-24 promotes E. coli accumulation, and rIL-19 had similar molecular weight and isoelectric point with rIL-24, show no E. coli accumulation ability (Supplementary Figure S3(A, B)). This suggests that IL-24 facilitates bacterial proliferation in target organs by enhancing local pathogen aggregation, similar to previous reports that IL-24 promotes infection by Staphylococcus aureus [ ] and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [ ]. Additionally, our in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that IL-24 enhances sepsis severity by promoting neutrophil and T-cell chemotaxis. IL-24 has been shown to drive leukocyte recruitment and activation across various inflammatory contexts. In epidermal inflammation, IL-24 stimulates epithelial cells to secrete cytokines and chemokines, promoting leukocyte infiltration [ ]. In a murine typhoid model, IL-24 induced IFN-γ, IL-12, and nitric oxide production by neutrophils, which in turn activated CD8+ T cells and contributed to resistance against Salmonella infection [ ]. These observations underscore IL-24’s context-dependent immunomodulatory role and highlight its capacity to exacerbate sepsis through coordinated recruitment of both pathogens and immune cells.
In our study, HL60 and Jurkat cells were used as models for neutrophils and T cells, respectively, and were blocked the shared receptors of the interleukin-10 family, CXCR1 and CXCR2. Our results suggest that both CXCR1 and CXCR2 may be involved in the chemotactic effect of IL-24 on cells (Supplementary Figure S3(C-F)). A limitation of this study is that the precise molecular mechanisms by which IL-24 mediates its effects in sepsis need in-depth investigated, as IL-24 is expressed across various cell types and sepsis is a complex, systemic disorder involving multiple organs. On the other hand, while our capillary assay demonstrates specific bacterial accumulation in an IL-24 gradient, this assay alone cannot distinguish between active chemotaxis and other mechanisms such as enhanced adhesion or trapping. Future studies employing real-time tracking of bacterial motility in defined gradients will be necessary to conclusively establish IL-24 as a true chemoattractant. Moreover, although our findings support the feasibility of targeting IL-24 in animal models, further investigation is required to translate this strategy into clinical practice.
