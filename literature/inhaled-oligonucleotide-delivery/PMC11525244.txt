## Abstract
Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), a leading cause of mortality and morbidity, is associated with a substantial healthcare and economic burden. Reduction of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) to guideline-recommended goals is crucial in the prevention or management of ASCVD, particularly in those at high risk. Despite the availability of several effective lipid-lowering therapies (LLTs), up to 80% of patients with ASCVD do not reach evidence-based LDL-C goals. This nonattainment may be due to poor adherence to, and lack of timely utilization of, LLTs driven by a range of variables, including polypharmacy, side effects, clinical inertia, costs, and access issues. Inclisiran was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2021 as a novel, twice-yearly, healthcare provider (HCP)-administered LLT. In-office administration allows HCPs more control of drug acquisition, administration, and reimbursement, and may allow for more timely care and increased patient monitoring. In the USA, in-office administered drugs are considered a Medical Benefit and can be acquired and reimbursed using the “buy-and-bill” process. Buy-and-bill is a standard system for medication administration already established in multiple therapeutic areas, including oncology, vaccines, and allergy/immunology. Initiating in-office administration will involve new considerations for clinicians in the cardiovascular specialty, such as the implementation of new infrastructure and processes; however, it could ultimately increase treatment adherence and improve cardiovascular outcomes for patients with ASCVD. This article discusses the potential implications of buy-and-bill for the cardiology specialty and provides a practical guide to implementing HCP-administered specialty drugs in US clinical practice.

## Key Points

 

## Introduction
Cardiovascular (CV) disease is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally [ ]. CV disease poses a substantial healthcare and economic burden, with total annual average direct and indirect costs estimated at $407.3 billion in the USA between 2018 and 2019 [ ]. Persistently elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is a major causal factor in the development of atherosclerotic CV disease (ASCVD), and cumulative exposure over time is associated with increased atherosclerotic plaque burden and CV risk [ ]. Consequently, alongside lifestyle changes, pharmacological LDL-C reduction has become a key strategy in primary and secondary prevention of ASCVD events [ – ]. Lowering LDL-C to goal levels recommended by evidence-based lipid guidelines in patients at high risk of ASCVD can prevent CV events, reduce mortality, increase quality of life, and reduce healthcare costs [ ].
There are several lipid-lowering therapies (LLTs) available for ASCVD prevention, the majority of which are self-administered [ – ]. However, in real-world clinical practice, most patients do not achieve LDL-C goals [ – ]. In December 2021, inclisiran was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration as the first available healthcare provider (HCP)-administered non-statin LLT [ , ]. Inclisiran is a small interfering RNA (siRNA) that lowers LDL-C by inhibiting hepatic proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) production, and is administered twice yearly (after doses at initiation and 3 months) by an HCP [ , ]. HCP-administered medications such as inclisiran have the potential to overcome barriers to LDL-C goal threshold attainment via increased adherence and persistence compared with self-administered products. Unlike self-administered specialty drugs, HCP-administered specialty drugs are typically acquired and reimbursed under the “buy-and-bill” system in US clinical practice [ ]. This mode of acquisition has implications for both patients and healthcare systems. As further novel CV risk-modifying medications with longer durations of action are developed as specialty drugs, HCP administration will likely become more common in CV care. This article explores the potential implications of implementing HCP-administered CV specialty drugs to treat patients at high risk of ASCVD in US clinical practice and provides a practical guide to establishing the buy-and-bill process.

## The Current Lipid Management Landscape
For the past 4 decades, statins have been the cornerstone of lipid management for primary and secondary ASCVD prevention. While high-intensity statins have been shown to reduce LDL-C by at least 50% [ ] and to significantly reduce the risk of major adverse CV events [ ], many patients do not or cannot take them. A US national survey found that of 1677 statin-eligible patients (ASCVD or high-risk primary prevention), only 44% were receiving statin therapy, and only 20% were at LDL-C goal. Moreover, adherence to recommended low-fat diet and lifestyle measures was low [ ]. Much of the lack of statin use is attributed to a perceived or real “statin intolerance,” defined in a National Lipid Association (NLA) statement as at least one adverse effect associated with statin therapy that resolves or improves with dose reduction or discontinuation [ ]. The reported prevalence of statin intolerance is 5–30% but varies between studies [ ]. Statin-associated muscle symptoms (SAMS) are the most common form of statin intolerance, and pharmacological SAMS are estimated to occur in approximately 1–2% of patients [ ]. For patients with known or suspected statin intolerance who are at high or very high ASCVD risk, the NLA recommends that non-statin therapy is considered to avoid delay while additional attempts are made to identify a tolerable statin regimen [ ].
There are now several non-statin LLTs available for clinical use, with more on the near horizon. In pivotal clinical trials utilizing these agents, ezetimibe reduced LDL-C levels by ~ 24% [ ], bempedoic acid by 13–17% [ , ], anti-PCSK9 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) by ~ 60% [ , ], and inclisiran by ~ 50% [ , ] compared with statins alone. Presently, the addition of ezetimibe and anti-PCSK9 mAbs to statins, and the use of bempedoic acid in statin-intolerant patients, have been shown to improve CV outcomes [ , , , ]. CV outcomes trials involving inclisiran are underway [ , ]. While non-statin LLTs are well tolerated, they are also associated with side effects that have the potential to impact adherence, including upper respiratory tract infections, diarrhea, fatigue, arthralgia, and myalgia [ – ].
Lipid management guidelines recommend the addition of non-statin LLT to maximally tolerated statins for ASCVD prevention when LDL-C goals are not met [ , ]. The 2018 American Heart Association (AHA)/American College of Cardiology (ACC)/Multisociety lipid management guidelines recommend the addition of non-statin LLTs to maximally tolerated statins to lower LDL-C by ≥ 50% to a threshold of < 70 mg/dL for patients with ASCVD at high risk of ASCVD events, or < 100 mg/dL for high-risk primary prevention [ ]. Notably, a 2022 ACC expert consensus decision pathway recommended more stringent LDL-C goals of < 70 mg/dL for patients with clinical ASCVD and < 55 mg/dL for those with clinical ASCVD at very high risk of recurrent ASCVD events [ ].
Despite the availability of these non-statin LLTs [ , – , ], registry studies suggest that up to 80% of patients in the USA are still not at guideline-recommended LDL-C goals for primary or secondary ASCVD prevention [ – ]. Poor adherence to, and uptake of, self-administered LLTs are major contributors to the lack of achievement of LDL-C goals and subsequent increase in ASCVD risk and mortality [ – ]. Oral LLT adherence rates reported in primary and secondary ASCVD prevention settings vary widely depending on the study design, location, and duration [ – ]. In addition, while long-term data on adherence to anti-PCSK9 mAbs are limited, initiating an anti-PCSK9 mAb has been associated with decreased statin adherence compared with statin monotherapy [ , ]. Factors that increase nonadherence to prescribed LLT include the need for regular self-administration (i.e., daily oral administration of statins, ezetimibe, and bempedoic acid), side effects or fear of side effects (particularly for statin therapy), clinical inertia and unfamiliarity with non-statin LLTs or with guideline recommendations, and polypharmacy due to comorbidities [ , , – ]. Furthermore, there are well-established disparities and inequalities in CV care that may also impact patient uptake and adherence to LLT, including age, sex, race/ethnicity, socioeconomic status, geographic factors, and other social determinants of health [ ]. Nonadherence to medication has financial implications; patients with good adherence to LLTs have been shown to have substantially lower total healthcare costs despite higher medication costs [ , ].
Another challenge to optimal LDL-C lowering is access to medication. In the USA, specialty drugs such as anti-PCSK9 mAbs [ ] are often associated with high out-of-pocket costs or complex prior authorization criteria, with increased likelihood of prescription rejection by payers [ , , – ]. In the years immediately following the approval of anti-PCSK9 mAbs, there were low levels of uptake due to complex prior authorization criteria, prescription rejection by payers, and high out-of-pocket costs [ , , ]. A more recent analysis compared the uptake of anti-PCSK9 mAbs before and after price reductions, label expansions, and guideline updates (2015–2018 versus 2019–2021) and found continuing barriers to use despite an increase in new prescriptions [ ].
Inclisiran is an siRNA conjugated to triantennary N-acetylgalactosamine carbohydrates, which facilitates rapid uptake to hepatocytes, reducing the risk of off-target effects [ – ]. The recommended dose of inclisiran is 284 mg (equivalent to inclisiran sodium 300 mg) administered by an HCP as a single subcutaneous injection at treatment initiation, again at 3 months, and then every 6 months [ ]. HCP-administered medications have the potential to improve medication adherence and persistence compared with self-administered drugs, as demonstrated for patients with psoriasis [ , ]. Reduced dosing frequency has also been proposed as a potential strategy to increase medication adherence and persistence [ , , ]. Additionally, due to the reported favorable safety profile of inclisiran, it has been proposed as a suitable treatment for patients with liver or kidney impairment [ ].
A retrospective analysis of US claims databases reported a higher mean 6-month medication adherence rate in patients receiving inclisiran compared with anti-PCSK9 mAbs [ ], while VICTORION-INITIATE, a phase 3b trial, demonstrated non-inferior statin discontinuation rates with an “inclisiran first” implementation strategy (adding inclisiran immediately upon failure to reach LDL-C < 70 mg/dL despite receiving maximally tolerated statins; 6.0%) compared with usual care (16.7%) [ ].

## The Potential Impact of Healthcare Provider-Administered Drugs on Cardiovascular Care
In the USA, specialty drugs are classified according to how they are administered; they can be covered by medical insurance plans as either a Medical Benefit (also referred to as "Major Medical Coverage") or Pharmacy Benefit (also referred to as "Drug Coverage"). With Traditional Medicare, HCP-administered drugs are typically reimbursed as a Medical Benefit under Medicare Part B. In contrast, self-administered drugs are reimbursed as a pharmacy benefit under Part D [ ]; however, this could differ for other payers.
HCP-administered drugs are provided in a physician’s office, a hospital outpatient facility, or at an alternative site of care [ , ]. The process typically used to purchase, administer, and claim reimbursement for in-office administration is known as buy-and-bill [ , ]. As part of buy-and-bill, the physician or their healthcare system is required to purchase/procure the medication and either store it on-site (referred to as inventory) or order it, as needed, to arrive shortly before patient visits. The HCP will schedule the patient’s visit, administer the medication, and subsequently submit a claim to the patient’s health insurance provider for reimbursement of the cost of the medication and administration [ , ]. An alternative site of care can be used if providers do not wish to undertake the buy-and-bill approach or are part of an integrated delivery network that restricts where drugs can be administered. The acquisition and reimbursement process for buy-and-bill differs from self-administered drugs that are most frequently acquired via specialty or retail pharmacies. For the latter, the physician will typically send the prescription to the specialty pharmacy, who will fulfill the order and ship the drug to the patient, the physician’s office, or it can be collected by the patient from a retail pharmacy [ , , ]. Anti-PSCK9 mAbs were initially only available from specialty pharmacies, but this is no longer the case [ ].
In-office administration through buy-and-bill has been widely used for cancer drugs [ – ] and vaccines [ ] as well as immunology, asthma, and dermatology specialty drugs [ , ]. It has several potential benefits in clinical practice (Fig. ), most importantly, treatment adherence and delivery. The process allows for more timely and convenient care, patient preference (e.g., among patients who are unwilling or unable to self-administer), and greater control of inventory management and the administration process. In conditions such as psoriasis, treatment adherence is higher in patients receiving in-office administered biologics compared with those self-administered at home [ , , ].
 Evidence from real-world studies of non-statin LLT suggests that out-of-pocket costs and complex prior authorization procedures results in poor uptake of and adherence to these novel therapies, with the additional challenge of eligible patients not receiving approval from their insurance provider [ , , , , , – ]. One US healthcare claims study revealed prescription rejection rates of up to 61% for anti-PCSK9 mAbs, resulting in an increased risk of CV events compared with patients with approved and paid prescriptions. Further, patients who abandoned prescriptions (15%) had less favorable CV outcomes [ ].
For patients with Traditional Medicare, the prior authorization process can be circumvented for in-office administered medications as prior authorization is rarely required for medical benefits reimbursed under Medicare Part B [ ]. This reduces the administrative burden for these patients [ ]. In a single US center, 100% of patients with Traditional Medicare were approved for inclisiran treatment without prior authorization [ ]. Conversely, for patients with commercial insurance or Medicare Advantage, for which prior authorization is usually required, there was a 25% approval rate [ ]. Still, additional requirements, such as previous anti-PCSK9 mAb use and site-of-care restrictions, could potentially limit access for patients with other insurance types [ , – ].
In addition, out-of-pocket costs for in-office administered drugs can be more predictable for patients with Traditional Medicare, as 80% of the costs are reimbursed under Part B. The remaining 20% of the cost may be covered by a Medicare Supplement (including Medigap, Medicaid, or employer-provided insurance) [ , ]. These policies are held by approximately 80% of the Traditional Medicare beneficiary population [ ]. Importantly, patients with Traditional Medicare and a co-pay supplement plan would have zero out-of-pocket costs for these medications [ ]; furthermore, these medications would not contribute to Part D costs, which can push patients toward the coverage gap (also known as the “donut hole”). The coverage gap begins after a certain amount has been spent on drugs covered under Part D and means that patients would have to pay up to 25% of the drug cost over that limit. This would not only potentially impact the cost of LLT but all other medications the patient is taking [ ].
Most patients with commercial or private insurance are subject to a co-insurance (% cost of medication) or co-payment (fixed cost for medication) on the basis of their elected healthcare plan. Patients required to pay a co-insurance may be subject to significant out-of-pocket costs when prescribed inclisiran or anti-PCSK9 mAbs, regardless of where these are administered [ – ]. In addition, regardless of insurance type, disparities in access based on race/ethnicity and other social determinants of health may still be apparent; White patients are more likely to have supplemental Medicare insurance than Black patients and are therefore more likely to use medical services [ ]. Additionally, as people under 65 years of age are not eligible for Medicare (except those with long-term disability), access gaps may remain for younger patients [ , , ].
While inclisiran is the first US Food and Drug Administration-approved HCP-administered drug in the CV space, there are several other medications with the potential to be HCP-administered in the near future. Thus, mechanisms to provide these drugs need to be considered.

## Practical Guide for Providing In-Office-Administered Medications in Cardiovascular Clinical Practice
Buy-and-bill is the acquisition process required for specialty drugs reimbursed under Medicare Part B. One key aspect of in-office administration for HCPs is setting up the buy-and-bill process for drug acquisition and reimbursement. ## Initial Setup: Expertise and Infrastructure Required
Establishing a buy-and-bill process requires collaboration between multiple functions within an HCP’s office or system and may require hiring new staff, providing staff training, and developing new infrastructure. Implementing buy-and-bill requires the provider to identify and work with an accredited distributor, which could be a manufacturer if direct purchase options are offered. The HCP must also ensure they have sufficient staff skilled in the buy-and-bill process.
Administrative steps include benefits investigations, review of provider–payer plans, patient co-payment/co-insurance collection processes, contracting, billing, and coding. Staff will also be needed to assist with patient education and the scheduling of drug administration and follow-up assessments, as required. The HCP’s office should plan for stock management, product storage (e.g., provision for storage at specific temperatures or other conditions) and maintain awareness of lead times and inventory (as needed). Electronic health records (EHR) software and other information technology (IT) systems can help ensure seamless scheduling, ordering, and billing when operated by appropriately trained staff [ , ]. Regular cross-functional meetings will be required when preparing for a buy-and-bill process, including representatives from population health, finance, billing, scheduling, pharmacy, and IT [ ].
Utilizing a cardiac risk management team within the office is helpful for managing the entire buy-and-bill system. Care managers and lead nurses within these teams play a crucial role in planning and coordinating patient care and helping patients adhere to medications. Intensified patient care has been shown to significantly improve LLT adherence rates compared with usual care [ ].
However, these administrative requirements have the potential to have a negative impact on care by reducing the number of HCPs who can undertake this enhanced administrative burden. We anticipate that the introduction of additional HCP-administered CV medications over the next few years will increase the workload for clinics. Systemic changes in the US healthcare system will be needed to accommodate the introduction of novel HCP-administered CV medications to ensure they are accessible to patients in need.

## Step-By-Step Implementation of Buy-and-Bill

## Step 1: Benefits Investigation and Prior Authorization
Once the HCP has determined and prescribed the appropriate treatment, they/their office needs to assess the patient’s health plan to determine what benefits they have in place and the methods of acquisition allowed by the plan.
It is important to obtain patient consent before performing benefits investigations, which include checking whether the health plan requires precertification or prior authorization (not usually required for patients with Traditional Medicare) and whether there are any network restrictions, step therapy requirements, or site-of-care restrictions. Network restrictions can take the form of health plans mandating that patients seek care from specific providers, and out-of-network provision may incur higher costs for the patient. Some health plans also implement step therapy requirements, which require a patient to try and fail another medication before the payer covers an alternative medication for the same condition. The payer often requests information about medications tried previously as part of the precertification/prior authorization. Some commercial payers limit the site of care or restrict where a patient can receive a drug, and patients could have to pay the full cost if it is administered at a non-approved site.
If prior authorization requests are denied, these can be appealed. Denials can occur due to a clerical error (which can be rectified by resubmission with correct information), for clinical reasons (further information can be provided to demonstrate medical necessity), or can be benefit driven, in which case a call to the payer can determine whether an exception to the benefit is allowed. The appeals process can be very time consuming, and together with obtaining prior authorizations, requires a team of experienced personnel to work with the provider.
Becoming familiar with the medical policy for each treatment in a patient’s health plan is important. These policies are used to support coverage decisions for specific procedures and address medical necessity or investigational status on the basis of the contract definitions.
The availability of an insurance supplement and/or co-payment for the patient is also determined at this stage, and patient out-of-pocket costs are estimated.

## Step 2: Payment Plans and Scheduling
Once the benefits investigation is complete and any prior authorization has been requested, the HCP’s office needs to determine the fee schedules that define the payment plan for the service to be provided. Generally, there are three levels of fee schedules: Medicare, commercial, and Medicaid [ ]. Medicare fee schedules are defined by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS); these are available on their website (https://www.cms.gov/medicare/physician-fee-schedule/search/overview) [ ]. Commercial payers reimburse on a contracted basis, negotiated between the office and the patient’s health plan. Medicaid fee schedules are determined individually by state, vary across the USA, and are available on state Medicaid websites. At this stage, the HCP should discuss any co-payment or other out-of-pocket costs with the patient and determine affordability. Cost is one of the most important factors influencing medication adherence for patients with ASCVD [ , ], so this is a crucial step. Should patients with Medicare Advantage or Medicare without supplemental insurance require assistance with co-payments, the HCP should discuss the availability of patient-assistance programs, including funding and grants. These are often provided directly by pharmaceutical companies or through charitable organizations, such as the HealthWell Foundation® [ ]. Navigating these cost-mitigation strategies is part of the HCP’s responsibility and adds to the overall administration burden associated with in-office administered medications [ ].
Drug administration can be arranged once approval is obtained and the patient has agreed to costs and a payment schedule. If the HCP has the drug in inventory, administration can occur on the same day. The provider should ensure they/their office have the infrastructure to schedule patients’ appointments and follow-ups to ensure the patient receives their medication per the recommended frequency and dosing intervals.

## Step 3: Acquisition
At this stage, the HCP’s office contacts the supplier/distributor (a specialty distributor or a group purchasing organization) to order (procure) the medication, negotiate costs, and/or apply discounts. The medication is delivered to the office and handled and stored on-site (inventory), or delivery is scheduled for arrival just before the patient’s visit. It is important to separate any buy-and-bill inventory from specialty pharmacy inventory or free drug samples. The HCP must take financial responsibility for any stock kept in inventory. Inclisiran is stored at room temperature, thereby simplifying storage and handling [ ].

## Step 4: Administration
The patient visits the HCP’s office to receive the medication via injection or infusion. It is recommended that the patient’s insurance information be checked before each subsequent visit/administration, as any changes may require re-verification of their benefits. This is especially relevant if a temporary Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System (HCPCS) code exists for the therapy. Co-payment or co-insurance may also be collected from the patient at this visit.
Following drug administration, the HCP can monitor the patient for any immediate adverse events and schedule the next drug administration and/or follow-up laboratory assessments, as necessary. It is good practice to provide a callback number for patients if they become concerned about adverse events, as these may not occur immediately after administration. To lessen the administrative burden on the provider and prevent patients or charges from “falling through the cracks,” developing a therapy plan within the EHR will not only help document the need for the agent but also assist in charge capture and set appointment dates for the following appropriate dosing intervals.

## Step 5: Charge Capture
Documenting the procedure in the patient’s EHR at the time of the patient’s visit or shortly after is important. Timely documentation ensures that codes used in the claims form for reimbursement are supported by EHR documentation. Failure to promptly document the procedure can result in payers recouping payments, denials, penalties, fines, and potentially other legal actions.

## Step 6: Developing a Reimbursement Claim
Under buy-and-bill, HCPs are responsible for billing and coding to receive reimbursement.

## Coding
It is extremely important that coding in claims forms is accurate. Inaccurate or incomplete coding will impact provider reimbursement by delaying claims processing times due to requests for additional information or correcting claims and increasing claim denial rates. The types of codes that are used in reimbursement claims forms are presented in Table [ – ].
 

## Claims Forms
Physicians and other non-facility-based HCPs use the CMS-1500 claim form (or its electronic equivalent), while hospitals and facility-based HCPs most commonly use the CMS-1450 claim form. Details such as drug name, total dosage administered, drug concentration, route of administration, National Drug Code (NDC) number, Current Procedural Terminology and HCPCS codes, modifiers, and units must be entered in the form.
Common billing mistakes occur when claims forms are submitted with invalid codes, incorrect units, or missing information, such as NDC numbers or modifiers.

## Steps 7–8: Reimbursement Claim Submission, Processing, and Tracking
Reimbursement claim forms are submitted into the billing system, and a third-party medical billing clearinghouse can act as an intermediary between the HCP and the payer.
The payer processes the reimbursement claim and either approves or denies the claim (the latter is usually due to administrative or coverage/policy issues). Reimbursement by the payer is dependent on the following three components: coverage (products and services that are eligible for payment), coding (classification of patient conditions, services, and supplies), and payment (payment processes and amounts).
Payers use various payment methodologies. Examples for Medicare include average sales price (ASP) plus 6% (for Medicare Part B drugs) and wholesale acquisition cost (WAC) plus 3% (for newly approved drugs) [﻿ ]. For patients with commercial insurance, reimbursement methodologies vary by payer and contract. Example models include ASP plus a percentage, WAC plus a percentage, average wholesale price plus a percentage, percentage of billed charges, contracting pricing or reimbursement structure, and flexibility in payment policies. There is substantial variation in payment methods used for Medicaid on the basis of state policy decisions, practice patterns, and geographic differences in markets and costs [﻿ ].
Claims can be tracked by contacting the insurance company directly.

## Steps 9–11: Payment, Review, and Reporting
Once the payer has approved the reimbursement claim, payment is sent to the HCP. The HCP/their office posts payment, reviews it for accuracy, and generates accounts receivable reports.

## Conclusions
Recent guidelines recommend the use of non-statin LLT in patients at high risk of ASCVD events who do not meet LDL-C-lowering goals on statins alone. Despite the availability and proven efficacy of self-administered non-statin LLT, uptake of and adherence to these therapies has been suboptimal due to factors including complex drug acquisition and reimbursement processes, the need for frequent self-administration, clinical inertia, and polypharmacy. This may increase overall ASCVD-related healthcare costs and the risk of CV events.
In-office administration of novel CV risk-modifying medications with longer durations of action will likely increase in number in the next decade. These agents will provide HCPs more control of drug administration, which, in our opinion, would enhance medication persistence and provide patients with more timely access to care on the basis of need and not on artificial barriers designed by payers.
As in-office drugs are considered a Medical Benefit, they must be acquired and reimbursed using the buy-and-bill process, which is a standard process for medication administration in multiple specialties (including rheumatology, allergy, and oncology) and may improve treatment access and reduce out-of-pocket costs for patients with Traditional Medicare.
Initiating in-office administration will involve new considerations for clinicians in the CV specialty, such as new infrastructure and processes. The potential eligible patient population for novel provider-administered CV medications is likely to be large. As such, systemic changes to the US healthcare system are required to ensure the availability of providers who are able to cope with the increased workload associated with the introduction of these agents. However, in our opinion, in-office administration could ultimately increase treatment adherence and could improve CV outcomes for people with ASCVD.
