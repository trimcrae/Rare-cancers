Viruses have evolved
multiple mechanisms to counteract the stimulator
of the interferon genes (STING) pathway, resulting in the suppression
of antiviral responses. Accordingly, in addition to developing STING
agonist analogs with enhanced stability and deliverability, overcoming
the defective STING function in virus-infected cells is essential
for defense against viral infections. In this study, we developed
STING pathway-activating complexes (SPAC) based on bioinspired vesicles
that display and multimerize STING molecules with a specific affinity
for agonist binding. As a broad-spectrum antiviral agent, this universal
STING mimic triggers IFN-I signaling independently of endogenous STING.
In infectious models, including CMV and SARS-CoV-2 infection, both
prophylactic and therapeutic regimens of SPAC can reduce viral load
and disease severity. These results indicate that SPAC, functioning
as a host-targeted immune modulator, provides the distinct advantage
of broad-spectrum therapy against infectious diseases caused by both
DNA and RNA viruses, particularly those with strong STING antagonistic
functions. The endogenous STING-independent activation mechanisms
of SPAC may provide a universal therapy for infectious diseases, potentially
serving as a candidate option to defend against future pandemics of
“Disease X”.

## Introduction
The innate immune system plays a crucial
role in recognizing pathogen
infections and establishing effective cellular host defense mechanisms. , Upon virus exposure, pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)
are recognized by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), initiating
signaling cascades that lead to the production of type I interferon
(IFN-I) and upregulation of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs). As an essential cytosolic PRR, stimulator of
interferon genes (STING) is activated by cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP), a
cyclic dinucleotide synthesized by cGAMP synthase (cGAS) in response
to cytosolic DNA as a danger signal. , Activation
of STING induces IFN-I production and subsequent initiation of viral
resistance and clearance through expression of several functional
ISGs, such as Mx1, Isg15, and Cxcl10, which has been demonstrated
as an important approach for antiviral immunotherapy. , The critical role of STING signaling in antiviral immune surveillance
has driven recent investigations into the use of cGAMP as a therapy
to stimulate antiviral immunity. − However, therapeutic applications of cGAMP have been
hindered by inefficient bioavailability due to its short half-life
and rapid clearance in vivo, poor cellular targeting,
and inadequate delivery to the cytoplasm where STING is localized. Consequently, current efforts are primarily
focused on the development of STING agonist analogs with enhanced
stability and improved pharmacokinetics, as well as innovative delivery
systems to improve in vivo agonist delivery and innate
immune cell activation. 
Notably,
the activation of STING signaling by agonists or analogs
requires the presence of a functional STING protein in the cell. The
growing understanding of virus evolution and pathology reveals surveillance
mechanisms to combat innate immune response and establish robust infection.
Indeed, our transcriptome data demonstrated that human cytomegalovirus
(HCMV) infection inhibits the STING-mediated antiviral IFN-I response
( ). Both RNA
and DNA viruses have evolved multiple mechanisms to antagonize the
STING-mediated antiviral IFN-I response in direct and indirect manners.
As a representative of acute virus infections, SARS-CoV-2 papain-like
protease can suppresse IFN-I responses by deubiquitinating STING. For the example of chronic and latent virus
infections, several cytomegalovirus proteins such as UL82, UL31, IE86, and pp71 have been
reported able to downregulate IFN-I response by inhibiting the cascade
member proteins of cGAS-STING signaling. These studies suggest that
viral proteins impair STING activity or signaling to antagonize innate
antiviral immunity. These findings imply that STING agonist therapy
is inadequate to activate the suppressed antiviral response in cases
of defective STING function, particularly when viral infections directly
inhibit or degrade the STING protein, thus preventing the downstream
signaling necessary for immune activation.
 As an important cytosolic pattern recognition receptor,
the endoplasmic
reticulum (ER)-resident STING can be activated by cGAMP, a cyclic
dinucleotide that is synthesized by cGAS in response to cytosolic
DNA. In response to cGAMP, STING dimerizes and translocates to a perinuclear
region, which is key for activation of downstream signaling pathway
members. Following STING relocalization,
TBK-1 is recruited to STING, leading to the subsequent phosphorylation
of STING and IRF-3 by TBK1, and ultimately resulted in production
of instinct IFN I. During STING-mediated
IFN-I production, STING polymerization and trafficking after binding
to cGAMP are critical for downstream immunological outcomes. Inspired by the biological transformation of
the Golgi apparatus and TBK1 recruitment during STING activation,
we hypothesized that delivering functional STING complexes with activating
effects could address the limited efficacy of cyclic dinucleotide
(CDN) or small molecule-based STING agonists in virus-infected cells
with defective STING function. In this work, to bypass the absolute
dependence on cellular endogenous STING, we designed STING pathway-activating
complexes (SPAC) based on membrane vesicles that display STING (STING-MVs).
These bioinspired vesicles, which are derived from the natural cell
membrane and feature a two-dimensional lipid bilayer environment,
could serve as a bioinspired scaffold to ensure STING-specific molecular
conformation and multimerize STING molecules with binding affinity
of STING agonists. Taking advantage of its nanoscale vesicular structure,
SPAC efficiently enters phagocytic cells, such as macrophages, making
it an ideal platform to effectively regulate the innate immune response.
SPAC could directly recruit and activate TBK1 as well as IRF3 to promote
the expression of IFN-I and ISGs. Importantly, robust activation of
STING signaling was also observed in STING-knockdown macrophages,
suggesting that SPAC functions independently of endogenous STING expression.
Accordingly, SPAC induced spontaneous innate antiviral defenses and
interfered with viral infection in animals infected with mouse cytomegalovirus
(MCMV) or SARS-CoV-2, which have been shown to antagonize the STING
signaling. More importantly, the universal antiviral mechanism of
SPAC, which induces an endogenous antiviral immune response, is not
attenuated by the elevated pathogenicity of different viruses and
their variants.

## Results and Discussion

## Cytomegalovirus Infection
Suppresses Activation of cGAS-STING
Signaling
To characterize the innate immune responses to
virus, HCMV-infected human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs) were harvested at 0, 3, 5, and 7 days postinfection
(dpi) for transcriptome analysis. The results of the principal component
analysis (PCA) demonstrated that the transcriptomic profiles of the
healthy group were clearly distinct from those of the HCMV-infected
group ( A).
The distances between the two groups were found to be relatively close
at 3 and 5 dpi, but became more pronounced at 7 dpi, which is largely
due to the progress of virus-induced cytopathic effect. Subsequent
differential analysis of transcriptomics before and after infection
revealed that CMV infection on days 3, 5, and 7 resulted in the upregulation
(downregulation) of 6270 (3,549), 5466 (3,025), and 6,131 (3,824)
genes, respectively ( B). In order to gain insight into the characteristics of transcriptomic
changes in cells following HCMV infection, all differential genes
were clustered, and the gene expression trend of different clusters
was analyzed by Mfuzz via the ‘ClusterGVis’ R package
(v0.1.1; https://github.com/junjunlab/ClusterGVis). The results identified 10 clusters with distinct expression patterns
during HCMV infection (Figure S1, Supporting
Information).
Further enrichment analyses demonstrated that
the genes of cluster 1 were significantly enriched in immune-related
pathways ( C).
The expression levels increased 3 days postinfection, followed by
a downregulation. An enrichment analysis based on the biological process
of GO (gene ontology) data set revealed that the genes of cluster1
were significantly enriched in innate immune pathways represented
by IFN-I production, response to IFN-I, regulation of IFN-I mediated
signaling pathway, and so forth ( D). Meanwhile, the enrichment analysis of
cluster 1 genes based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes
(KEGG) database revealed that they were primarily enriched in the
biological processes including interactions of viral proteins and
cytokines, cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway, complement and coagulation
cascades, necroptosis, and apoptosis, which present a typical cellular
manifestation of infectious disease ( D). Notably, the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway
was also enriched in cluster 2, where genes exhibited a rapid decline
from the beginning of the infection to the third day, followed by
a stabilization. The cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway is a pivotal pathway
for cells to recognize exogenous DNA and initiate the innate immune
response. As a result, suppression of innate immunity may be associated
with the inhibition of this pathway. Nevertheless, both cluster 1
and cluster 2 were found to be enriched within the cytosolic DNA-sensing
pathway, indicating that the pathway contains genes with both expression
patterns.
Consequently, a further pathway was mapped based on
the KEGG database
in order to explore the changes in gene expression within the cytosolic
DNA-sensing pathway and the interactions between genes ( E). In comparison to the healthy
group, cytokine-related genes, such as Il6 and Il1b, exhibited notable
upregulation following infection. In contrast, IFN-I exhibited no
discernible alterations, which is consistent with the inhibition outcomes
of the IFN-I related pathway, as revealed in the preceding enrichment
analysis. It is noteworthy that the genes associated with the core
protein of the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, STING, were found to
be significantly repressed. The cGAS-STING signaling pathway is the
upstream pathway for interferon. The findings indicate that the CMV
infection has the potential to suppress the cGAS-STING signaling pathway
via STING, thereby limiting the production of IFN-I.
In order
to investigate the overall changes in the IFN-I related
pathway after infection, the pathway was scored in each sample using
GSVA, and the resulting pathway scores were analyzed for differences
between the infected pathway scores and the healthy group ( F). The results demonstrated
a significant inhibition of the IFN-I related pathway on the fifth
day after infection in comparison to the noninfected state, which
is consistent with the previous findings. Of interest is the observation
that the cGAS-STING signaling pathway demonstrated significant and
sustained inhibition immediately after infection. The viral proteins
have been shown to interact with STING, antagonizing various vertebrate
STING functions, including attenuating STING-TBK1 association, impairing
STING oligomerization and aggregation, and preventing STING trafficking. − Therefore, for infectious diseases caused by such viruses with STING
inhibitory function, development of an agonist or delivery system
alone appears to be difficult to efficiently induce a potent STING-mediated
antiviral immune response. Timely and appropriate activation of the
STING pathway and its mediated antiviral immune response is critical
to suppressing viral replication. However, current medical countermeasures
can rarely meet this urgent demand. Taken together, these results
prompted us to design a new immunomodulatory agent to address the
antagonism of the STING pathway.

## Vesicular STING Forms Complexes
with STING Agonists and Effectively
Targets the Phagocytes
Inspired by the biological transformation
of the Golgi apparatus following STING activation, we hypothesized
that the delivery of functional STING-agonist complexes could overcome
the challenges of limited therapeutic efficacy and defective STING
signaling in virus-infected cells ( A). To obtain a universal STING pathway-activating
complex (SPAC), we sought to design a STING mimic based on bioinspired
vesicles that displayed and multimerized STING molecules with a specific
affinity of the agonists binding, which is supposed to make them independent
of cGAS activation and cGAMP production in the infected cells. As
a vesicle donor, the cell line overexpressing STING on the cell membrane
was engineered by transfection of STING carrier plasmids containing
a plasma membrane targeting signal peptide and analyzed for STING
expression by immunostaining and immunoblotting ( B, C). With the cytomembrane
of STING-overexpressing, STING-MVs were fabricated based on the biomimetic
vesicle system that we previously developed. Vesicles derived from HEK-293T cells (Mock-MVs) were set as a control.
Transmission electron microscope (TEM) images revealed the typical
vesicular morphology of MVs ( D). Further dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis
showed that the average size of STING-MVs was 199.6 ± 3.4 nm
with a polydispersity index (PDI) at 0.104 ± 0.023 ( E). The zeta potential
measurement illustrated that MVs had a negative charge of nearly −30
mV ( F). The
STING-MVs maintained a stable size intensity and potential after cryopreservation
at −80 °C (Figure S2, Supporting
Information). Immunoblotting analysis confirmed the preservation of
STING on the surface of the MVs, which provided a basis for loading
of STING agonists on the MVs ( G).
 To characterize the binding between STING agonists and STING-MVs,
cGAMP and DMXAA (a small molecule agonist for mouse STING) were coincubated with the MVs, respectively
( H). High performance
liquid chromatography (HPLC) assays were performed to assess binding
efficiency after the removal of free agonist. Compared with Mock-MVs,
the binding affinity between STING-MVs and STING agonists was significantly
higher. There was a difference of approximately 1.5-fold when bound
to cGAMP and 2.4-fold when bound to DMXAA ( I), indicating that STING-MVs can complex
with STING agonists in a STING-dependent manner. To promote the polymerization
of STING, heparin (0.1 mg/mL) was added during the preparation of
SPAC. The complex formed by STING-MVs
binding the specific agonists is named SPAC. To evaluate the cell-penetrating
potential of SPAC, we used STING knockdown 4T1 cells coincubated with
STING-MVs, followed by Western blotting analysis of intracellular
STING proteins. As shown in J, incubation with STING-MVs significantly increased
the STING levels in 4T1 cells. In addition to the monomers (35 kDa),
STING-MVs predominantly existed as dimers (estimated molecular weights
of 70 kDa) and oligomers under nondenaturing conditions. Collectively,
the above results demonstrated that STING-MVs could serve as a bioinspired
scaffold and multimerize STING molecule with high affinity binding
of STING agonists.
To determine the distribution characteristics
and potential target
cells of STING-MVs, we intravenously administered mice with fluorescence
dye DiD-labeled STING-MVs (100 μg). In vivo fluorescence imaging results showed that the signal of STING-MVs
was enriched in the abdomen 2 h after injection and peaked at 24
h ( A). We identified
that STING-MVs were extensively distributed in the liver and spleen
( B). Further
flow cytometry analysis showed that STING-MVs, either in peripheral
blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) or in the spleen, were mainly present
in macrophages and dendritic cells, which play an essential role in
virus sensing and initiation of potent antiviral immunity ( C). In an in vitro coincubation assay, DiD-labeled STING-MVs were
coincubated with macrophages (RAW 264.7) and dendritic cells (DC2.4).
Within 6 h, the increase in DiD-positive cells indicated that STING-MVs
could be effectively internalized by phagocytes ( D, E). Further protein detection
revealed increased levels of STING, indicating the preservation of
the target protein ( F). The safety of STING-MVs as assessed by the hemolysis test
and CCK8 assay demonstrated that these biomimic vesicles could be
administered systemically with no apparent cytotoxicity (Figures S3, S4, Supporting Information). Collectively,
these data indicate that phagocytes, including macrophages and dendritic
cells, are the primary immune cellular targets of STING-MVs. These
designed nanoscale vesicles inherently interact with phagocytes and
are ideal platforms to effectively regulate the innate immune response
by delivering drugs.
 

## SPAC Elicits IFN-I Signaling
Independent of Endogenous STING
Levels and Induces Antiviral Activity In Vitro
Given the critical role of IFN-I in antiviral immunity, we evaluated
the ability of SPAC to stimulate the IFN-I responses in innate immune
cells. Macrophages were treated with PBS, free DMXAA, Mock-MV, STING-MV,
Mock-MV+DMXAA, and SPAC, respectively. Overall, we observed that the
SPAC treatment elicited higher Ifnb expression than the groups with
DMXAA alone or DMXAA codelivery with Mock-MV ( A). IFN-I and downstream ISGs play important
roles in the host antiviral defense. SPAC treatment elevated the mRNA
levels of antiviral genes Cxcl10, Mx1, and Isg15 ( B). To confirm whether Ifnb
induction depends on the STING-TBK1-IRF3 signaling axis, Western blotting
was performed to detect the phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3 (p-TBK1
and p-IRF3) after 6 h of treatment with PBS, STING agonist, or SPAC.
As shown in Figure S5 (Supporting Information),
treatment with SPAC increased the expressions of p-TBK1 and p-IRF3.
Furthermore, confocal imaging verified the colocalization of p-TBK1
with STING-MVs in the cytosol of macrophages ( C), suggesting that the direct recruitment
and phosphorylation of TKB1 by SPAC. IRF3 was additionally observed
in the nucleus, indicating its activated state ( D). Furthermore, we employed siRNA to transiently
knockdown STING and processed the cells with SPAC 48 h after siRNA
treatment. As a result of E, DMXAA failed to activate Ifnb expression after transient
knockdown of STING. However, macrophages with attenuated STING expression
exhibited comparable levels of Ifnb transcription compared to the
control siRNA, suggesting that SPAC functions independently of endogenous
STING expression.
 It has been reported that both HCMV and MCMV interrupted
the cGAS-STING
signaling pathway by inhibiting of STING translocation from endoplasmic
reticulum (ER) to Golgi and impairing the recruitment of TBK1 and
IRF3 to the STING complex, leading to inhibition of innate antiviral
response. To determine the antiviral
effect of SPAC in vitro, GFP-expressing reporter
MCMV (MCMV-GFP) was employed. We collected the supernatants from macrophages
that previously incubated with PBS, DMXAA, or SPAC, and then transferred
them to NIH 3T3 cells together with MCMV-GFP at different multiplicity
of infection (MOI) ( F and Figure S6). Measurement of
Ifnb concentration ( G) and ISGs levels (Cxcl10 and Mx1) ( H) in macrophages showed that SPAC significantly
improved IFN-I and ISGs response. At 3 dpi, the supernatant of macrophages
stimulated by DMXAA could not inhibit MCMV infection in NIH 3T3 cells
(MOI = 0.1). As the virus replicated, the host cells shrank and became
round in morphology with GFP expression. However, cells incubated
with supernatant from SPAC-treated macrophages remained normal and
grew densely with low GFP expression (Figure S6, Supporting Information). In infections at an MOI of 10, SPAC similarly
inhibited viral replication ( I). Collectively, these data indicated that SPAC could
independently recruit and activate TBK1 as well as IRF3 to promote
the expression of Ifnb and downstream ISGs, which play important roles
in host antiviral defense and protect NIH 3T3 cells from MCMV infection.

## Prophylactic SPAC Administration Protects against MCMV Infection
in Mice by Promoting IFN-I Signaling
MCMV is able to establish
a robust infection and cause pneumonia in mice. − To evaluate
the protective potency of SPAC in vivo, luciferase-expressing
reporter MCMV (MCMV-Luc) was used to visualize viral infection in vivo by bioluminescence imaging. CMV has been shown to
have a wide range of cellular tropisms in multiple organs. The 8-week-old
female BALB/c mice, which are susceptible animal models for MCMV,
were first treated intravenously with PBS, DMXAA, or SPAC once a day
for 2 days, and then inoculated with 1 × 105 PFU of
MCMV-Luc ( A).
Analysis of the levels of STING pathway-related genes in mouse spleen
showed that SPAC upregulated the levels of Ifnb, Ifna, and ISGs (Mx1,
Mx2, and Isg15) ( B). With the activation of IFN-I signaling, viral replication was
significantly inhibited in SPAC-treated mice compared to that in other
control groups ( C). In addition, bioluminescence imaging of major organs collected
from the mice further confirmed the distribution of the viral signal
in the organs, indicating that MCMV tended to invade the liver and
spleen ( D),
where nanoscale MVs were most abundant ( B). To determine the viral load in the liver,
we homogenized tissues and performed tissue culture infectious dose
(TCID50) assays. Viral titers in the livers of mice in both the SPAC
and DMXAA groups were reduced compared with those in the PBS group,
but the reduction was more significant in mice treated with SPAC ( E). Simultaneously,
as evidenced by the control of elevated ALT levels ( F), SPAC suppressed inflammation
and injury caused by MCMV infection of the liver. Furthermore, the results of the pathological analysis confirmed
that SPAC therapy was adequate to alleviate the typical disease manifestations
of acute virus infections such as inflammatory cell infiltration in
liver tissues and diffuse lung injury in lung tissues ( G). Collectively, these results
demonstrated that SPAC could enhance IFN-I and ISGs production by
activating the STING pathway in mouse spleen and effectively inhibit
viral replication in vivo, thereby establishing a
line of defense for mice to escape tissue damage caused by viral infection.
 

## Therapeutic Administration of SPAC Inhibits
MCMV Replication
To investigate whether SPAC could induce
antiviral effects in the
MCMV-infected mouse model, female BALB/c mice were infected with 1
× 105 PFU MCMV-Luc followed by treatment with intravenous
administration of SPAC ( A). In vivo monitoring showed that DMXAA
moderately inhibited viral replication. In contrast, SPAC therapy
provided significantly better efficacy, with the viral signal being
virtually undetectable ( B, C). Major organs were harvested from euthanized mice at
5 dpi for pathological analysis. H&E staining showed, in agreement
with previous histological observations, that MCMV infection induced
pronounced inflammatory symptoms or lesions in the liver and lungs.
Significant inflammatory damage was observed in the livers of PBS-
and DMXAA-treated groups, but not in SPAC-treated mice. The alveolar
architecture in lungs from PBS- and DMXAA-treated mice became unclear
accompanied by a thick alveolar wall and the collapsed alveolar cavity,
which was significantly ameliorated in SPAC-treated mice ( D). Overall, SPAC
showed an excellent therapeutic effect on MCMV-infected mice, alleviating
virus-induced organ lesions.
 

## SPAC Effectively Protects
Mice from SARS-CoV-2 Infection and
Pneumonia
SARS-CoV-2 induced an unbalanced innate immune
response characterized by suppression of IFN-I production and signaling,
resulting in excessive release of proinflammatory cytokines, an indicator
of poor disease outcome. − SARS-CoV-2 variants with higher transmissibility
and pathogenicity pose a great challenge to the effectiveness of the
therapy. The B.1.351 strain has evolved to achieve higher infectivity
and lethality than the prototype SARS-CoV-2, , crossing the species barrier to establish infection and pneumonia
in mice. , Thus, we asked whether SPAC therapy could
efficiently rescue mice after SARS-CoV-2 variant infection at a lethal
dose. Next, we investigated the immunomodulatory effects of SPAC in
a mouse model of SARS-CoV-2 infection. In a previous study, we have
identified that inhaled nanoscale vesicles derived from cytomembrane
were extensively distributed in the lungs and taken up by alveolar
macrophages, which are the primary cells
that play an important sentinel role in the lungs by sensing and triggering
potent antiviral immunity. 80-week-old male BALB/c mice were intranasally
inoculated with 1 × 105 PFU of prototype SARS-CoV-2,
followed by treatment with intranasal SPAC ( A). Body weight loss, a typical disease manifestation
of COVID-19 in animal models, was recorded from 0 to 6 dpi. As the
results showed, inhaled SPAC therapy can significantly reduce body
weight loss ( B). Lung tissues were collected from euthanized mice at 6 days postinfection
for analysis of the innate immune response. Consistent with previous
reports, SARS-CoV-2 induced an imbalanced
innate immune response that characterized by transcriptional suppression
of antiviral genes ( C) and high mRNA levels of proinflammatory cytokines ( E). One of the mechanisms
of antiviral response inhibition is disruption of the STING pathway
by the papain-like protease of SARS-CoV. In contrast to the PBS-treated mice, inhaled SPAC therapy caused
significant increases of Ifnb, Mx1 and Isg15 mRNA levels, respectively,
which were even higher than those of DMXAA-treated controls. Thus,
we speculated that the activation of antiviral responses by SPAC might
effectively ameliorate lung pathology and control the virus load.
Indeed, SPAC therapy resulted in approximately 10- to 100-fold decrease
of viral RNA in the turbinate, trachea, and lung, respectively ( D). Notably, the
mRNA levels of inflammatory factors, including Il6, TNF-α, Ifng,
Mipa, and MCP1, were effectively suppressed along with inhibition
of viral replication ( E). In addition, pathological analysis of lung tissue confirmed
that inhaled SAPC therapy can greatly alleviate lung injury caused
by SARS-CoV-2 infection ( F, G, Figure S7 and Table S2, Supporting Information). Collectively,
these results confirmed that SPAC ameliorated the disease outcome
of COVID-19 in mice after a lethal dose of SARS-CoV-2 variants. More
importantly, the antiviral mechanism of SPAC, which induces an endogenous
antiviral immune response, would not be attenuated by the elevated
pathogenicity in different SARS-CoV-2 variants.
 An important immune escape
approach and immunopathogenic manifestations
of virus infection are inhibition of PRRs and endogenous IFN-I production,
which usually resulted in a decline of viral clearance and enhancement
of disease severity. For example, HSV-1 infection is lethal in mice
with deficient STING and IFN-I signaling. Fu et al. found that HCMV protein UL82 inhibits STING-mediated signaling
by preventing the trafficking of STING and impairing the STING-TBK1-IRF3
complex formation, leading to inhibition of innate antiviral response
and immune evasion by HCMV. The MCMV
protein m152 specifically targets the IFN-I response by binding to
STING, thereby delaying its trafficking to the Golgi compartment from
where STING initiates IFN-I signaling. In addition to DNA viruses, RNA viruses also have evolved multifaceted
mechanisms to specifically antagonize the STING-mediated antiviral
IFN response. The main protease 3CL and accessory protein ORF3a of
SARS-CoV-2 can antagonize various vertebrate STING functions, thereby
playing an important role in the suppression of host innate immune
responses triggered by the cGAS-STING pathways. SARS-CoV-2 open reading frame 10 (ORF10) interacts with
STING, attenuates the STING-TBK1 association, and impairs STING oligomerization
and aggregation. Furthermore, ORF10 also prevents the ER-to-Golgi
trafficking of STING by anchoring STING in the ER. Beside cytomegalovirus and coronavirus, other important
viruses such as herpesviruses, flaviviruseses, hepatitis B virus (HBV),
poxvirus, and foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV) were able to impair
STING-meidated instinct IFN-I production by multiple mechanisms include
evasion, disruption, and direct and indirect antagonisim. − A virus may antagonize the STING pathway by different mechanisms.
FMDV Lpro and 3Cpro target cGAS for cleavage to dampen the cGAS/STING-dependent
antiviral response. On another hand,
FMDV 2B protein suppressed activation of STING signaling by recruiting
YTH m6A RNA-binding protein 2 to bind to STING mRNA. In both fundamental studies and clinical translations,
the species specificity of interaction between STING signaling members
and viral proteins is an indispensable factor. For instance, in humans,
but not in mice, STING is subject to cleavage by proteases encoded
by Zika virus, Dengue virus, West Nile virus, and Japanese encephalitis
virus, but not that of yellow fever virus. Taken together, these findings highlighted that STING signaling
is a critical frontline of virus–host interplay and immunotherapy
of infectious diseases. It is reported that pharmacological activation
of STING signaling can restrict infections of influenza virus, hCoV-OC43, and
SARS-CoV-2, and inhibit HBV replication,
liver injury, and fibrosis. Therefore,
directly or indirectly relieving the virus-caused antagonism of instinct
innate immune response and improving production of IFN-I serves as
a priority option of broad-spectrum antiviral immune-therapy.
The STING signaling pathway is of profound importance in the elimination
of numerous viruses due to its critical role in the initiation of
the endogenous IFN-dependent innate immune response. Consequently,
administration of STING agonists such as cGAMP has been identified
as a promising approach to tackling infectious disease. Drug candidates
that targeting STING signaling are of great significance in clinical
applications due to their targeted delivery, controlled drug release,
improved solubility, multifunctionality, and enhanced stability. , Of note, as mentioned above, a number of viruses are equipped to
counteract the STING pathway. By interacting with STING, viral proteins
interfere with STING oligomerization and aggregation or prevent STING
trafficking and interfere with the formation of the STING-TBK1-IRF3
complex. In addition to the development of STING agonist analogs with
improved stability and deliverability, overcoming the inhibition of
endogenous STING function by viruses is a key concern in defending
against viral infections. Because virus infection might strongly inhibit
or destroy the STING pathway, it is difficult to achieve ideal antiviral
treatment effects solely by activating STING, which requires research
for approaches independent of endogenous STING.
To address the
challenges of limited therapeutic efficacy of cyclic
CDN or small molecule-based STING agonists and defective STING function
in virus-infected cells, we developed SPAC based on membrane vesicles
anchoring STING complexes as a universal STING mimic to activate STING
signaling pathways in a manner independent of cGAS activation, cGAMP
production, and endogenous STING levels. By exploiting the natural
lipid environment of human-derived cells, the conformation and polymerization
of the membrane-anchored STING could be well controlled. Our animal
experiments demonstrated that both the prophylactic and therapeutic
regimens of SPAC could both rescue the poor disease outcome of MCMV
infection in mice, confirming that supplementation of the exogenous
STING mimic complex is an efficient approach to combat virus infection
and associated diseases. In the aged mice with SARS-CoV-2 infection,
inhaled SPAC therapy can largely inhibit viral replication in respiratory
tract organs, alleviate body weight loss and lung injury, downregulate
inflammatory cytokine genes, and upregulate genes of IFN-I and ISGs.
These data demonstrated that a host–target immune modulator
such as SPAC has the unique advantage of broad-spectrum therapy encompassing
infectious diseases caused by infections of both DNA and RNA viruses.
Of note, SPAC displayed a significant increase of antiviral, anti-inflammation,
and immune regulatory effects compared to DMXAA. First, based on the
inherent interaction with innate immune cells, which play an important
sentinel role by sensing and triggering potent antiviral immunity,
SPAC promotes the delivery efficiency of STING agonists to target
cells. Second, the dual delivery of STING and STING agonist avoids
the defective function of endogenous STING and boosts activation of
the IFN-I pathway. Third, the vesicular structure design of SPAC ensures
its in vivo stability and allows its multiroutes
administration. Taken together, these characteristics of SPAC suggest
that a functional complex mimic may be an ideal alternative to conventional
small molecule STING agonists, especially in treatment of infectious
diseases caused by viruses with strong STING antagonistic functions.

## Conclusions
Decreasing the medical burden of virus infections
and subsequent
infectious diseases remains a major challenge for our current and
future biomedical research. The virology diversity demands huge investments
in the development of virus-specific vaccines and antiviral drugs.
The high mutation rate and immune escape capacity of a respiratory
tract virus such as SARS-CoV-2 attenuate the efficiency of virus-target
countermeasures. Therefore, the development of a broad-spectrum host–target
immune therapy strategy will be an important supplement to address
these issues. This investigation revealed the multidimensional effects
of SPAC, including counteracting viral immune evasion and reconstituting
the antiviral immune response, providing new insights for the design
of biomimic biologics. In conclusion, our findings highlight that
the endogenous STING-independent activation mechanisms of SPAC is
a universal therapy of infectious diseases, which might be a candidate
option to defend the pandemic of “X” disease in the
future.

## Materials and Methods

## Cell Culture
293T
cells, 293T-STING cells, RAW 264.7
macrophages, and NIH 3T3 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s
modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM). DC2.4 and 4T1 cells were cultured
in RPMI 1640 medium. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine
serum (Biochannel), penicillin (100 U/ml), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL).
The culture medium for 293T-STING cells was supplemented with puromycin
(2 μg/mL) to ensure the stable expression of transfected constructs.
All cells were grown at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in a humidified
incubator.

## Fabrication and Characterization of Cell
Membrane Vesicles (MVs)
Cells were harvested to suspend in
phosphate-buffered saline (PBS),
frozen at −80 °C, and thawed at room temperature to rupture
the cells. Following three repeated washes with cold PBS mixed with
protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Scientific), the cells were then
sonicated at 20 MHz for 20 s × 3 on ice, followed by multistep
differential centrifugation at 4 °C to isolate vesicles. To promote
the polymerization of STING, heparin (0.1 mg/mL) was added. The vesicles
(STING-MVs) were resuspended in cold PBS, and further uniform MVs
were generated through a 200 nm pore filter on the Mini-Extruder (Avanti
Polar Lipids). The membrane protein concentrations were determined
by using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific). The STING proteins
anchored on STING-MVs and retention of STING-MVs in cells were verified
by Western blotting with STING antibody (Cell Signaling Technology).
The size, surface zeta potential, and stability of MVs were measured
by Zetasizer (Malvern Nano-ZS90). The morphology was characterized
with a transmission electron microscope (FEI Tecnai G2 Spirit).

## STING Pathway-Activating Complex Preparation
The STING
pathway-activating complex (SPAC) was prepared via physical mixing
of STING-MVs and STING agonists (MedChemExpress), either cGAMP or
DMXAA in sterile EP tubes. The binding ability of MVs to agonists
was tested by HPLC. Briefly, the mixture of STING-MVs and STING agonists
was gently mixed to thoroughly mix and then incubated at 37 °C
for 1 h. After centrifugation at 15,000 rpm under 4 °C for 1
h, the supernatant was collected to measure the concentration of
agonists through a HPLC system (Waters Alliance e2695 HPLC).

## In Vitro Antiviral Effect
RAW 264.7
cells were incubated with PBS, DMXAA, or SPAC for 24 h. The culture
medium of RAW 264.7 was transferred to NIH 3T3 cells with a 70% confluent
rate, and MCMV-GFP was added simultaneously. Monitoring of viral infection
in NIH 3T3 cells was carried out by observing cells morphology and
detecting GFP signals under a fluorescent microscope (Nikon Eclipse
Ti-U).

## Animal Studies
All work conducted on animals was approved
by the Animal Care and Use Committee (CC/ACUCC) of Xiamen University.
All the mice employed in the MCMV infection experiments were six-
to eight-week female BALB/c mice. For MCMV infection, mice were intraperitoneally
injected with MCMV-Luc. All the mice employed in the SARS-CoV-2 infection
experiments were 80-week-old male BALB/c mice. For bioluminescence
imaging, mice were intraperitoneally injected with D-Luciferin potassium
salt (150 mg Luciferin/kg mouse body weight, Gold Biotechnology).

## In Vivo Retention and Mononuclear Cells Uptake
of MVs
BALB/c mice were tail-based injected with STING-MV
labeled by DiD fluorescent dyes (Beyotime). We monitored the fluorescence in vivo at setting time points on the In Vivo Imaging System
(PerkinElmer IVIS Lumina II) and sacrificed the mice at 24 and 168
h to assess the fluorescence intensities in the main organs. The peripheral
blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) and spleen mononuclear cells were acquired
from the mice using isolation kits (Solarbio) according to the manufacturer’s
instructions, and the cellular uptake of MVs was studied through flow
cytometry (Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX) with FITC antimouse CD19 antibody,
PE antimouse CD11c antibody, and FITC antimouse F4/80 antibody (BioLegend).

## Animal Protection Experiments against MCMV
Mice were
first injected intravenously with PBS, DMXAA, or SPAC for 2 days and
then infected with MCMV-Luc. The viral signals in mice were tested
through bioluminescence imaging on the In Vivo Imaging System (PerkinElmer
IVIS Lumina II). Bioluminescence monitoring began on the third day
after virus infection, and the mice were sacrificed on the fifth day.
The spleens from mice were ground to extract RNA for gene expression
analysis, including Ifna, Cxcl10, Mx1, Isg15, Tnf, and Il6. Briefly
speaking, 20 mg of spleen was put into a 2 mL RNase-free EP tube containing
600 μL Buffer RLT from a RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen) and 2 magnetic
beads. The samples were ground in an automated tissue homogenizer
(Servicebio), followed by high speed centrifugation under low temperature.
We collected the supernatant to extract RNA according to the manufacture’s
instruction and carried out qRT-PCR. To evaluate liver virus titer,
50 mg of liver in a sterile EP tube with 1 mL PBS and 2 magnetic beads
were ground to prepare tissue lapping fluid. Various liver lapping
fluid dilutions were added to NIH 3T3 cells at 70% confluency in a
96-well plate and incubated until a virus-induced cytopathic effect
could be seen. We counted the number of affected wells and determined
the TCID50 value. For alanine aminotransferase (ALT) analysis, sera
were collected at the end of monitoring and tested in the automatic
biochemical analyzer (Mindray BS-240 Vet). The H&E staining images
of liver and lung tissues were obtained through the EVOS M7000 imaging
system (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## Animal Therapy Experiments
against MCMV
Mice infected
with MCMV were given two consecutive injections with PBS, DMXAA, or
SPAC, respectively. The viral signals in mice were monitored lasting
from the third day to the fifth day after virus infection through
bioluminescence imaging (PerkinElmer IVIS Lumina II). H&E staining
images of the main organs were obtained through the EVOS M7000 imaging
system (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## Transcriptome Analysis
of HCMV-Infected Cells
Gene
expression was quantified using the featureCounts (v1.6.4) software,
while gene annotations were retrieved with the R package “biomaRt”
(v3.6.0). PCA was conducted using the “prcomp” function,
following the standardization of expression values with the “ScaleData”
function. The “edgeR” package (v3.28.1) was employed
to assess transcriptome alterations resulting from HCMV infection.
For the purpose of transcript change detection, significance was defined
as “FDR < 0.01 and |FC| > 2”. The false discovery
rate was computed with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (as
implemented in the R function p.adjust) in order to control for type
I error resulting from multiple testing. Further, the DEGs were clustered
based on their expression with the mfuzz cluster method in the R package
Cluster GVis (v0.1.1; https://github.com/junjunlab/ClusterGVis). Subsequently, enrichment analysis was conducted based on GO (https://geneontology.org/)
and KEGG (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/) databases for each cluster with the “cluster Profiler”
package (v4.6.2). The Path view library (https://pathview.uncc.edu/home) was used to display gene changes within the pathway. To investigate
the variation in GO and KEGG pathway activity among the samples, the
“GSVA” package (v1.46.0) was used to convert the gene
expression matrix to an expression matrix of gene set GSVA scores.
Differential analysis of GSVA scores between HCMV-infected groups
and healthy controls was performed with the “limma”
package (v3.54.2), with the difference filter condition for p-value
< 0.05. Heatmaps were created with the “pheatmap”
package (v1.0.12), while PCA plot and bubble plots were implemented
with the “ggplot2″ package (v3.3.6) in R language (https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/ggplot2/index.html).

## Statistical Analysis
Data are presented as mean ±
SEM. Statistical significance was analyzed by one-way ANOVA, two-way
ANOVA, or unpaired two-tailed t test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant: n.s., not significant,
*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001.
