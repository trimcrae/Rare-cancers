## Abstract

## Introduction
Intestinal ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI) is a critical condition often requiring emergency intervention. Ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death driven by phospholipid peroxidation, plays a central role in its pathogenesis.

## Objective
This study aimed to explore whether CoQ10 could mitigate intestinal IRI by suppressing ferroptosis.

## Methods
We analyzed serum CoQ10 levels and inflammatory cytokines in patients with mesenteric artery embolism. In mice, intestinal IRI was induced by transient superior mesenteric artery ligation following two weeks of CoQ10 pretreatment. Histology, ELISA, immunoblotting, and RNA sequencing were used to assess therapeutic effects. To explore mechanisms, we used CRISPR/Cas9 to generate FSP1 and COQ2 knockouts in enterocytes, along with targeted metabolomics and co-autoxidation assays. In vivo loss of FSP1 function was induced by AAV9 to evaluate its role in CoQ10-mediated protection.

## Results
Utilizing both our own and publicly available intestinal IRI cohorts, we identified a correlation between elevated CoQ10 levels and reduced systemic inflammation, along with decreased oxidized lipid accumulation in ischemia–reperfusion-affected small intestines. Transcriptomic enrichment analyses and biochemical assays demonstrated that CoQ10 supplementation effectively mitigates IRI by modulating lipid metabolism and inhibiting lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. To elucidate the mechanism of action of CoQ10 against lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis, we established an in vitro ferroptosis-associated intestinal IRI model using enterocytes, which revealed that the CoQ10-mediated suppression of ferroptosis is dependent on FSP1. Targeted metabolomics analyses and co-autoxidation assays indicated that FSP1 suppresses ferroptosis by reducing CoQ10, thereby preventing phospholipid peroxidation. Loss of function FSP1 generated by genetic and pharmacological mechanisms in enterocytes or mouse intestines led to a decrease in the levels of reduced CoQ10, negating the therapeutic effects of CoQ10 on intestinal IRI.

## Conclusion
Our study reveals a crucial role of CoQ10 in ferroptosis and highlights the potential of CoQ10 as a promising target for intestinal IRI treatment.

## Introduction
Intestinal ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI) is a common pathological process that inevitably occurs in various clinical settings, including after intestinal tumor excision, small intestine transplantation, volvulus and superior mesenteric arterial embolism [ , ]. IRI disrupts epithelial barrier function, leading to bacterial translocation, endotoxin release, and systemic inflammation [ , , ]. Furthermore, upon restoration of the local blood flow, instead of recovering their physiological functions, epithelial cells undergo a variety of pathological cell death mechanisms, such as apoptosis [ ], pyroptosis [ ], necroptosis [ ] and ferroptosis [ , ].
Coenzyme Q (CoQ), also known as ubiquinone, is a redox-active lipid molecule that is located in cell membranes, particularly in the inner mitochondrial membrane. CoQ exhibits structural diversity with a benzoquinone head and a polyisoprenoid tail of variable length. Notably, while coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) is the form synthesized by Homo sapiens, CoQ9 is primarily produced by Mus musculus, with only a small amount of CoQ10 synthesized. Endogenous de novo synthesis of CoQ10 is sufficient to meet daily biological needs. However, congenital genetic mutations, including those in COQ2, COQ4, COQ6 and PDSS2, result in primary CoQ10 deficiency. In addition, underproduction or overconsumption of CoQ10 can be caused by the progression of several diseases, which thereby exacerbates disease development. Previous studies have demonstrated that mitochondrial CoQ10 can transport electrons from complex I/II to complex III to regulate energy metabolism, whereas membrane CoQ10 acts as an antioxidant to suppress lipid peroxidation. James et al. reported that mitochondrial CoQ deficiency increased superoxide/hydrogen peroxide production and induced insulin resistance. Furthermore, Juriscova et al. reported that CoQ10 treatment restored oocyte mitochondrial function and enhanced female fertility in mice.
Ferroptosis is a distinct form of programmed cell death characterized by the accumulation of peroxidized phospholipids [ ]. First reported by Stockwell et al., ferroptosis is characterized by distinct morphological, biochemical and genetic features that distinguish it from apoptosis, pyroptosis, necroptosis and autophagy [ ]. Other scientists have subsequently investigated the canonical ferroptosis-suppressing pathway known as the System Xc--GSH-GPX4 pathway. This pathway regulates the synthesis of oxidized glutathione (GSSG) while simultaneously reducing phospholipid hydroperoxides (PLOOHs) into their corresponding nontoxic alcohols (PLOHs) [ ]. The activity of this ferroptosis pathway has been associated with injury to multiple organs and therapy-resistant cancers [ , , , ]. In intestinal IRI, ACSL4 activation promotes ferroptosis-mediated tissue damage, and inhibition of ACSL4 can attenuate injury [ ]. However, no ferroptosis-targeting therapy has gained FDA approval, largely due to concerns regarding toxicity and unclear mechanisms.
In this study, we tested the hypothesis that CoQ10 protects against intestinal IRI by inhibiting ferroptosis. Specifically, we proposed that (1) CoQ10 levels are inversely correlated with intestinal IRI severity and inflammatory burden; (2) CoQ10 supplementation mitigates IRI through ferroptosis suppression; and (3) the protective effect of CoQ10 is mediated by FSP1-dependent reduction of CoQ10, which prevents phospholipid peroxidation.

## Results

## CoQ10 may protect against intestinal IRI
To explore the potential relationship between CoQ10 and intestinal IRI, 97 patients with intestinal IRI were divided into a non-intestinal necrosis (non-IN) group and an intestinal necrosis (IN) group. The clinicopathological characteristics of the patients are shown in . Logistic multivariate regression analysis revealed that body mass index (BMI) may be a risk factor for intestinal necrosis after intestinal IRI, whereas the plasma CoQ10 concentration is a protective factor ( ). Iron overload is a potential indicator of ferroptosis associated with IRI [ ]. To explore the correlation between CoQ10 and ferroptosis, we analyzed the relationship between the serum ferritin level, a marker of iron overload, and the CoQ10 concentration. The results revealed that the serum ferritin level was negatively correlated with the CoQ10 concentration ( A). Restoration of blood flow triggers a systemic inflammatory response; thus, the levels of inflammatory cytokines in serum are often used to evaluate the severity of intestinal IRI [ ]. Our analysis revealed a significant inverse relationship between the serum CoQ10 concentration and the levels of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α and IL-1β, suggesting that elevated CoQ10 levels may mitigate the severity of intestinal IRI ( B–E). C11-BODIPY 581/591-stained intraoperative frozen sections from patients who underwent small bowel resection, a procedure associated with intestinal IRI, revealed increased lipid peroxidation in the intestinal mucosa of the low CoQ10 group ( F), as well as more pronounced mucosal layer damage ( G and I). Collectively, these findings suggest that CoQ10 supplementation may be applicable for the treatment of intestinal IRI.
 Additionally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of the intestinal IRI cohort in the GEO database (GSE37013) revealed that the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were predominantly enriched in ubiquinone and other terpenoid–quinone biosynthesis pathways, terpenoid backbone biosynthesis, ferroptosis, and various inflammation-related pathways ( H). Assessment of CoQ10 levels and COQ2 expression in fresh IRI versus non-IRI small bowel resection intestinal samples revealed a significant reduction in CoQ10 levels and COQ2 protein expression in IRI tissues ( J–L). These results suggest that CoQ10 may be a protective factor against intestinal IRI.

## Ferroptosis suppression pathways are impaired in intestinal IRI
To elucidate the potential involvement of ferroptosis inhibition pathways in intestinal IRI, we developed an IRI model in mice by inducing ischemia for various durations (15, 30, 60, and 120 min) via temporary occlusion of the superior mesenteric artery (SMA). Following a uniform reperfusion duration of 120 min, we harvested both serum and intestinal tissues for analysis. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining indicated that ischemia exceeding 30 min irreversibly compromised the intestinal villi, with the extent of barrier damage increasing with increasing ischemia duration ( A–B).
 To obtain a complete understanding of the relative contributions of different types of cell death in intestinal IRI, we conducted experiments to assess the dose-dependent effects of emricasan, necrostatin-1, disulfiram, and ferrostatin-1 on intestinal epithelial cell viability and mice were intraperitoneally injected with various inhibitors of cell death before undergoing ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) surgery with different durations of ischemia ( A). After the same reperfusion duration, the serum intestinal fatty acid-binding protein (I-FABP) levels were measured. The results revealed that emricasan (an inhibitor of apoptosis) and necrosatin-1 (an inhibitor of receptor-interacting protein kinase 1-mediated necroptosis) significantly suppressed I-FABP release after 15 min of ischemia and 120 min of reperfusion only. Additionally, disulfiram (an inhibitor of pyroptosis) suppressed I-FABP release after 30 min of ischemia and 120 min of reperfusion. Moreover, the classic ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 suppressed I-FABP release after 60 or 120 min of ischemia followed by 120 min of reperfusion ( C). In addition, immunofluorescence analysis of the intestines subjected to different durations of ischemia was performed to detect markers of apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis and ferroptosis. The results revealed that the intestinal epithelial cells of the I15R120 group expressed mainly cleaved caspase-3 and MLKL, which are markers of apoptosis and necroptosis, respectively. Additionally, the epithelial cells of the I30R120 group mainly expressed Gasdermin-D, a marker of pyroptosis. Furthermore, the intestinal epithelial cells of both the I60R120 and I120R120 groups expressed TFRC, a common marker of ferroptosis ( B–C). These results suggested that ferroptosis was the predominant form of programmed cell death in intestinal IRI when the duration of ischemia reached 60 min.
To investigate the potential perturbations in ferroptosis inhibition pathways during intestinal IRI, we assessed the expression of ferroptosis-related proteins by immunoblotting. The analysis revealed the ACSL4 was upregulated and ZO-1, SLC7A11, GPX4, and FTH were downregulated in intestinal tissues, indicating that IRI is associated with disruption of ferroptosis suppression pathways ( D and A). Interestingly, we did not detect significant changes in FSP1 levels in intestinal tissues subjected to I/R ( B). The unchanged expression may reflect tissue-specific regulatory mechanisms or post-translational modifications that modulate FSP1 activity independently of its expression level. The serum lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), which was quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs), confirmed that a 60-min ischemic interval induced the most significant systemic inflammatory response ( E).
Given that redox-active iron plays a pivotal role in ferroptosis [ ], we measured the intestinal iron concentrations. The analysis revealed elevated levels of total and ferrous iron in the IRI groups compared with those in the sham control group, but there were no notable differences in ferric iron levels ( F–G and C). Additionally, the System Xc--GSH-GPX4 pathway relies on the cystine–glutamate antiporter and GPX4 activity to function [ ]. To investigate the integrity of the System Xc--GSH-GPX4 pathway, which is critical for ferroptosis regulation, we measured malondialdehyde (MDA), reduced glutathione (GSH), and GSSG levels and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) activity. The findings revealed increased MDA and GSSG levels and decreased GSH levels and GPx activity in the IRI group compared with the sham control group, indicating impairment of the canonical ferroptosis suppression pathway ( H–J and D–S2E).
Additionally, by assessing the FSP1/ubiquinone pathway, a known alternate route of ferroptosis suppression independent of the System Xc--GSH-GPX4 axis, we observed decreased CoQ10 and CoQ9 levels in IRI tissues, which is consistent with our clinical small bowel resection samples ( K–L and I). Collectively, these findings imply a multifaceted disruption of ferroptosis suppression pathways in intestinal IRI.

## The levels of CoQ10 and CoQ9 increase in major organs following CoQ10 supplementation
CoQ10 was discovered in the bovine heart by Crane et al. in 1957 and has been recognized for its roles in the electron transport chain (ETC), antioxidation, and lipid metabolism regulation [ , , ]. Recently, the ability of CoQ10 to inhibit ferroptosis via the FSP1 pathway by scavenging radicals and thwarting lipid peroxidation has been demonstrated. On the basis of these insights, we propose that CoQ10 supplementation might mitigate intestinal IRI by preventing lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. To explore this possibility, we administered 15 mg/kg (low dose) or 30 mg/kg (high dose) CoQ10 to mice subcutaneously three times a week for two weeks to evaluate its impact on CoQ10 and CoQ9 levels in various organs and assess its potential toxicity.
Quantitative analysis revealed elevated CoQ10 and CoQ9 levels in heart, kidney, liver, and intestinal tissues, but no discernible increase in the lung or brain ( A–F). Notably, CoQ10 levels increased more significantly than did the CoQ9 levels, especially in the heart and liver, where they nearly doubled compared with those in the controls ( A and C). Furthermore, the concentrations of CoQ10 and CoQ9 did not significantly differ between the low- and high-dose CoQ10 groups. Given that CoQ10 is available over-the-counter as a health supplement but lacks FDA approval for treating medical conditions, we examined its safety profile through histological and biochemical assessments.
After two weeks of CoQ10 administration, histological examination using H&E staining revealed no appreciable alterations in the cellular structure or tissue architecture across the tested organs ( G). Serum analyses of aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), total bilirubin, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, and cardiac troponin-I (cTnI) revealed no significant differences among the groups, suggesting the absence of adverse effects on liver, kidney, or heart function ( H–M). Thus, our findings affirm both the safety of CoQ10 supplementation and its efficacy in increasing the CoQ10 concentration in the heart, liver, kidney, and intestine, supporting its potential therapeutic application in mitigating intestinal IRI.

## CoQ10 supplementation confers protection against intestinal IRI in mice
Given the pivotal role of CoQ10 in mitigating intestinal IRI in both humans and murine models, we posited that CoQ10 supplementation could offer therapeutic benefits for IRI. To test this hypothesis, mice were subcutaneously pretreated with either low (15 mg/kg) or high (30 mg/kg) doses of CoQ10 or a vehicle control (coconut oil) for two weeks. The mice were subsequently divided into IRI and sham cohorts, with the former undergoing 60 min of ischemia followed by 120 min of reperfusion and the latter subjected only to laparotomy.
Histopathological analysis of the intestinal tissues using H&E staining revealed that CoQ10 supplementation significantly preserved the mucosal architecture, indicating a protective effect against IRI-induced damage ( A–B). CoQ10 supplementation rescued the expression of ZO-1, a tight junction protein essential for maintaining epithelial cell barrier integrity [ ] ( C and A). This observation was further corroborated by measuring the serum levels of I-FABP and fluorescein isothiocyanate–dextran (FD-4), as increased CoQ10 supplementation was associated with decreased I-FABP and FD-4 leakage, indicating improved intestinal barrier function ( D and B). Additionally, CoQ10 supplementation led to reductions in serum LDH activity and the levels of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α, underscoring its potential to mitigate systemic inflammatory responses associated with IRI ( E–G). Taken together, these results robustly demonstrate the efficacy of CoQ10 in alleviating intestinal IRI, emphasizing its therapeutic potential for enhancing mucosal integrity and reducing inflammation.
 

## CoQ10 mitigates ferroptosis in intestinal IRI
To investigate the mechanisms by which CoQ10 ameliorates intestinal IRI, we performed transcriptomics analyses of intestinal tissues from mice subjected to either sham surgery or IRI with or without CoQ10 supplementation. Pathway analysis revealed that the pathways enriched in the upregulated genes in the intestines of mice with IRI were predominantly associated with inflammation, cell death, and the lipid response. In contrast, processes related to lipid metabolism, steroid biosynthesis, hydrogen peroxide catabolism, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism were significantly enriched in the genes downregulated in the intestines of mice with IRI ( H). Furthermore, a comparison between the vehicle and CoQ10-supplemented groups highlighted the ability of CoQ10 to increase lipid and fatty acid metabolism, including unsaturated and long-chain fatty acid metabolism, as well as other small molecule metabolic pathways ( I). These modifications likely contributed to the reduction in inflammation and cell death observed after CoQ10 supplementation ( A and D–G).
Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) suggested that CoQ10 supplementation mitigated lipid peroxidation and ROS accumulation by bolstering lipid, fatty acid, and isoprenoid metabolism ( J–K). As lipid peroxidation and metabolic dysregulation are key indicators of ferroptosis, we directly assessed the impact of CoQ10 on this cell death process by analyzing the expression of ferroptosis markers. CoQ10 supplementation restored the expression levels of SLC7A11, GPX4, and FTH and regulated ACSL4 expression ( L and C–F), underscoring its inhibitory effect on ferroptosis. However, CoQ10 supplementation did not affect FSP1 expression in intestinal tissues after IRI ( G). Notably, CoQ10 also prevented mitochondrial outer membrane rupture, a characteristic alteration observed in ferroptosis ( H)[ ].
With respect to ferroptosis, which is a hallmark of iron homeostasis imbalance, CoQ10 supplementation significantly reduced the total and ferrous iron levels without affecting ferric iron levels ( M–N and I–J). Furthermore, CoQ10 decreased MDA levels, which is indicative of reduced lipid peroxidation ( O), and increased the total glutathione and GSH levels while decreasing GSSG concentrations ( P–Q and K). Consequently, the GSH/GSSG ratio was greater in the CoQ10-supplemented IRI group than in the control group ( K), confirming that CoQ10 supplementation effectively counteracts ferroptosis in intestinal IRI.

## CoQ10 mitigates ferroptosis via FSP1 in intestinal epithelial cells
To elucidate the mechanism by which CoQ10 inhibits ferroptosis in vivo, we simulated intestinal IRI in vitro using Caco-2 cells, which serve as a model for primary enterocytes because of their ability to differentiate into monolayers that feature functional tight junctions and distinct apical and basolateral domains [ ]. Contrary to our expectations, a single hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) event did not trigger ferroptosis in Caco-2 cells (data not shown). Previous research has shown that 15 nM RSL3 successfully induces ferroptosis in this cell line [ ], and our experiments further revealed that concentrations of RSL3 as low as 3 nM significantly increased C11 oxidation after H/R, suggesting the induction of H/R-associated ferroptosis ( A). In addition, our Western blot results demonstrated that H/R-associated ferroptosis did not affect the expression level of FSP1 in Caco-2 cells ( A–B). Live-dead staining and cell viability assays demonstrated that H/R sensitized Caco-2 cells to RSL3-induced ferroptosis ( B–C). Treatment with the radical-trapping antioxidant (RTA) liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1), the iron chelator deferoxamine (DFO), and CoQ10 notably decreased lipid peroxidation in cells subjected to H/R followed by RSL3 treatment, as evidenced by C11-BODIPY 581/591 staining ( D). Furthermore, the viability of H/R- and RSL3-treated Caco-2 cells was significantly restored by Lip-1, DFO, and CoQ10 ( E–F), confirming the efficacy of CoQ10 as an inhibitor of ferroptosis and lipid peroxidation. To determine the long-term protective effect of CoQ10 on RSL3-induced ferroptosis, we generated curves of cell viability over time after CoQ10 treatment. Compared with vehicle treatment, CoQ10 treatment significantly increased the cell survival duration ( C). In addition, similar findings were observed in primary human intestinal epithelial cells ( D–E).
 Building on the previously reported finding that FSP1 performs an antiferroptotic function via a quinone-mediated mechanism in osteosarcoma fibrosarcoma cells [ ], we investigated the roles of FSP1 and CoQ10 in H/R-associated ferroptosis. By using the CRISPR/Cas9 system, we generated two FSP1-knockout Caco-2 cell clones (FSP1KOno.1 and FSP1KOno.2) ( F). Viability assays revealed that FSP1 knockout increased cell susceptibility to H/R-induced ferroptosis ( G). Treatment with 4-chlorobenzoic acid (4-CBA), a potent inhibitor of CoQ10 biosynthesis, increased ferroptosis sensitivity in Cas9 control cells but not in FSP1 knockout cells after H/R and RSL3 treatments ( H–I). Similarly, CoQ10 supplementation promoted ferroptosis resistance in Cas9 control cells, but this effect was not observed in FSP1 knockout cells ( H–I). Additionally, knockout of both FSP1 and COQ2 (FSP1KOCOQ2KO) confirmed the role of CoQ10 in ferroptosis suppression via the FSP1 pathway in this model ( J–K and G). These findings collectively underscore the role of CoQ10 in mitigating ferroptosis through FSP1 in intestinal epithelial cells, offering mechanistic insights into the therapeutic potential of this axis for intestinal IRI.

## FSP1 is involved in the CoQ10-mediated suppression of ferroptosis by inhibiting phospholipid peroxidation in intestinal epithelial cells
Previous findings highlighted that N-myristoylation of FSP1 is crucial for its ferroptosis resistance function [ ], as lipid peroxidation, particularly phospholipid peroxidation, drives ferroptosis [ ]. We therefore investigated the relationship between FSP1 and phospholipid peroxidation in Caco-2 cells, a model of primary enterocytes, by treating Cas9Ctrl and FSP1KO cells with RSL3 in the presence of CoQ10. By performing targeted metabolomics analysis, we observed that FSP1 knockout did not significantly alter the levels of most lipids, including diacylglycerol (DAG), free fatty acids (FFAs), ceramides (Cers), and triacylglycerol (TAG) ( A–B and ). However, we identified some notable changes in lipid levels, including decreased levels of polyunsaturated fatty acid-containing phospholipids, increased levels of the corresponding lysophospholipids, and elevated levels of ether-linked phospholipids, which serve as key indicators of ferroptosis due to lipid peroxidation ( C–Q) [ , ]. These results suggested that FSP1 suppressed ferroptosis by preventing phospholipid peroxidation in intestinal epithelial cells and that FSP1 plays a pivotal role in mitigating ferroptosis by impeding phospholipid peroxidation in intestinal epithelial cells.
 Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis was further conducted to evaluate the influence of FSP1 on the CoQ10 redox state by measuring the levels of reduced and oxidized CoQ10 in FSP1KO cells. FSP1 knockout led to a decrease in reduced CoQ10 levels and an increase in oxidized CoQ10 levels without altering the total CoQ10 concentration ( A–D). To further explore the potential interaction between FSP1 and CoQ10, we performed molecular docking analysis using AutoDock Vina. The results showed a strong binding energy of –8.8 kcal/mol, with CoQ10 fitting into a hydrophobic pocket of FSP1 and forming hydrogen bonds with residues Lys322 and Ser342. This computational evidence supports a direct interaction model between FSP1 and CoQ10, complementing our cellular and in vivo data demonstrating FSP1-dependent reduction of CoQ10 ( A). Treatment with an FSP1 inhibitor phenocopied these effects, decreasing the concentration of the reduced form of CoQ10 ( E–H). Given the demonstrated role of CoQ10 in ferroptosis and phospholipid peroxidation suppression in Caco-2 cells ( D and F), we next investigated whether CoQ10 supplementation enhances the intracellular levels of reduced CoQ10. After treating cells with CoQ10, we observed increases in the levels of both total and reduced CoQ10, but their ratio was unaffected ( I–L). Overexpression of FSP1 in Caco-2 cells mediated by transfection with pCMV-FSP1 did not significantly reduce the CoQ10 levels ( M and P). The specificity of FSP1 for CoQ10 reduction was investigated through NADH consumption assays with various oxidizing agents, and we found that FSP1 could reduce the levels of CoQ10, idebenone, and resazurin but not GSSG, highlighting that its ferroptosis suppression mechanism is independent of GPX4 activity ( Q). To further evaluate whether FSP1 prevents phospholipid peroxidation by reducing CoQ10 to generate RTAs, we performed co-autoxidation assays with egg phosphatidylcholine (egg PC) liposomes and STY-BODIPY, a styrene-conjugated fluorogenic probe that indicates RTA activity. The results showed that FSP1, in combination with its reducing substrate NADH, was unable to prevent phospholipid peroxidation ( R). However, the addition of idebenone or CoQ10 efficiently suppressed phospholipid peroxidation ( R). These outcomes highlight the critical role of FSP1 in the reduction of CoQ10 to prevent phospholipid peroxidation, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis in intestinal epithelial cells.
 

## CoQ10 mitigates intestinal IRI via the FSP1-mediated reduction of CoQ10
To investigate the interplay between FSP1 and CoQ10 in intestinal IRI, we used adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV-9) to specifically downregulate FSP1 in mouse intestinal tissues. Mice were intraperitoneally injected with either AAV9-shCtrl or AAV9-shFSP1, and in situ fluorescence imaging, quantitative PCR, and immunoblotting were conducted after four weeks to evaluate the efficiency of AAV-9 transduction and FSP1 knockdown. The results confirmed successful infection of the intestinal structures and a significant reduction in the mRNA and protein levels of FSP1 in the AAV9-shFSP1 group compared with those in the control group ( A and A–B).
 The mice were subsequently treated with either CoQ10 or vehicle for two weeks, followed by the establishment of the standardized IRI model ( B). Histological analysis and immunofluorescence staining were used to assess mucosal integrity and tight junction protein expression, and the results revealed that CoQ10 effectively ameliorated intestinal villi damage and restored the levels of the tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin in the AAV9-shCtrl group; however, these effects were not detected in the AAV9-shFSP1 group ( C–E). To confirm that CoQ10 supplementation restores intestinal function after intestinal IRI, we recorded the daily food intake and time of defecation of the mice after surgery. The results revealed that the mice in the AAV9-shFSP1 group ate significantly more food on the 2nd, 3rd and 4th days after intestinal IRI than the mice in the AAV9-shFSP1 group, and their first bowel movement occurred earlier ( C–D). Additionally, the decrease in the serum I-FABP level corroborated the observed improvements in intestinal permeability ( F). Finally, CoQ10 decreased the levels of the systemic inflammatory markers LDH, TNF-α, and IL-6 in the AAV9-shCtrl group ( E–G).
The impact of CoQ10 on iron metabolism was assessed and reductions in the total, ferrous, and ferric iron levels were observed in the AAV9-shCtrl group, trends that were not observed in the AAV9-shFSP1 group ( G and H–I). The MDA levels in intestinal tissues mirrored this pattern, suggesting that the ability of CoQ10 to suppress lipid peroxidation is dependent on FSP1 ( H). LC–MS/MS analysis of the levels of oxidized and reduced CoQ10 revealed that, in the AAV9-shCtrl group, CoQ10 effectively increased the content of reduced CoQ10, thereby alleviating IRI by inhibiting ferroptosis ( I). These findings underscore the role of CoQ10 in attenuating intestinal IRI through the FSP1-mediated reduction of CoQ10, highlighting the critical function of FSP1 in the protection offered by CoQ10 against ferroptosis in intestinal epithelial cells.

## Discussion
Intestinal IRI is a multifaceted and sustained process initiated by obstruction and the subsequent restoration of local blood flow in the intestines [ ]. The ischemic phase induces a rapid decrease in the oxygen concentration, promoting cytosolic anaerobic glycolysis and oxidative stress in enterocytes. This metabolic shift results in lactate accumulation and an increase in the intracellular concentration of hydrogen ions. While reperfusion restores the oxygen and blood supply to previously ischemic tissues, it paradoxically triggers ETC damage and disrupts cellular metabolic homeostasis, generating an abundance of ROS. ROS, which carry unpaired electrons, exacerbate mitochondrial and membranous organelle damage, break DNA double-stranded structures, promote lipid peroxidation, and contribute to diverse forms of enterocyte death, including necrosis [ ], apoptosis [ ], pyroptosis [ ], autophagy [ ], and the recently elucidated phenomenon of ferroptosis [ ].
Previous studies have revealed that reperfusion induces ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes [ , ], renal proximal tubular cells [ ], and enterocytes. Li et al. contributed significantly to our understanding of this process by identifying ferroptosis in the early stages of reperfusion. They specifically highlighted the role of ACSL4, a pivotal enzyme in lipid synthesis regulation, in ferroptosis [ ]. However, these studies failed to elucidate specific alterations in lipid metabolism or the extent of lipid peroxidation in enterocytes during I/R-associated ferroptosis.
Li et al. reported that ferroptosis occurs during the early stage of intestinal reperfusion and that ACSL4, a key lipid synthesis regulatory enzyme, contributes to ferroptosis. However, these studies failed to elucidate the specific changes in lipid metabolism and the degree of lipid peroxidation in intestinal epithelial cells during I/R-associated ferroptosis. In this study, we observed disturbances in multiple metabolic pathways, including macromolecular biosynthesis, ubiquinone and other terpenoid − quinone biosynthesis, hydrogen peroxide metabolism, and lipid metabolism. While the canonical ferroptosis defense mechanism via the System Xc–GSH–GPX4 axis is known to be compromised during IRI—as demonstrated by decreased GPX4 activity, GSH depletion, and elevated MDA levels in our study—the FSP1–CoQ10 axis remained functionally relevant. Our results show that FSP1 expression remains stable during IRI and plays a critical role in reducing CoQ10 to counteract phospholipid peroxidation, even when GPX4 is downregulated.
Unlike the classical GPX4-dependent pathway, which relies on the cystine-glutamate antiporter system (System Xc−) and glutathione metabolism, our study highlights a distinctive FSP1-CoQ10 axis in ferroptosis suppression. Specifically, GPX4 inhibits ferroptosis by directly reducing phospholipid hydroperoxides using glutathione as a substrate, whereas FSP1 suppresses ferroptosis via reducing CoQ10 into its antioxidant form (ubiquinol), which in turn inhibits lipid peroxidation independently of glutathione. Importantly, we found that CoQ10 supplementation significantly rescued ferroptosis independently of GPX4 activity, emphasizing the unique therapeutic potential of targeting the FSP1-CoQ10 axis.
To further confirm the distinct protective effects between the FSP1-CoQ10 and GPX4 pathways, we compared the effects of GPX4 inhibitors (e.g., RSL3) and classical ferroptosis inhibitors (e.g., liproxstatin-1). Our results demonstrated that while GPX4 inhibition strongly induced ferroptosis, CoQ10-mediated ferroptosis protection persisted even under conditions of impaired GPX4 function. Thus, the FSP1-CoQ10 pathway represents a complementary and potentially more resilient therapeutic mechanism against ferroptosis compared to classical GPX4-dependent approaches.
In previous studies, epithelial cells were subjected to H/R to investigate IRI-associated ferroptosis. However, we found that simple H/R failed to strongly induce ferroptosis in epithelial cells. To address this limitation, we established an innovative in vitro model that specifically mimics ferroptosis-related I/R and precisely reproduces the lipid peroxidation observed during IRI, providing insights into the mechanistic intricacies of ferroptosis in intestinal epithelial cells and enhancing our understanding of this phenomenon in the context of patients experiencing IRI.
Although ferroptosis is the central focus of our study, intestinal IRI is a complex pathological process involving multiple regulated cell death pathways. Our temporal analysis using pharmacological inhibitors and immunofluorescence staining revealed that apoptosis and necroptosis were predominant after short ischemia (15 min), pyroptosis emerged at intermediate durations (30 min), and ferroptosis became dominant after prolonged ischemia (≥60 min). These findings suggest a sequential activation pattern of cell death pathways, possibly reflecting the escalating metabolic and inflammatory stress during IRI. Notably, these death mechanisms may not act in isolation—oxidative stress, iron overload, and lipid peroxidation likely serve as common upstream triggers that influence multiple pathways in parallel [ , ]. For instance, ferroptosis-induced mitochondrial dysfunction could enhance inflammasome activation, while iron-mediated ROS might amplify both apoptotic and pyroptotic cascades. Our results support ferroptosis as a key contributor to severe IRI, yet the interplay with other forms of cell death warrants further investigation, as targeting multiple pathways may offer synergistic therapeutic potential.
Previous studies on ferroptosis in IRI have focused on the System Xc--GSH-GPX4 pathway and the squalene-mediated or di/tetrahydrobiopterin-mediated pathways. In 2019, by using CRISPR/Cas9 and global cDNA overexpression screening techniques, two independent teams reported that the novel FSP1/ubiquinone pathway acted in parallel with the canonical System Xc--GSH-GPX4 ferroptosis pathway to inhibit ferroptosis [ , ]. Both groups demonstrated that FSP1 functions as an oxidoreductase, catalyzing the conversion of ubiquinol from ubiquinone, which acts as a potent RTA to prevent lipid peroxidation in cancer cells. However, studies elucidating the role of the FSP1/ubiquinone pathway in IRI and determining whether CoQ10 could be a viable therapeutic target for intestinal IRI are lacking. CoQ10 is biosynthesized in all healthy human cells, and its concentration initially increases but then decreases with age, possibly due to decreased levels of CoQ10 synthase. By integrating bioinformatics analyses with data from our intestinal IRI cohort, our work revealed, for the first time, a significant impairment in CoQ10 biosynthesis during intestinal IRI. Our speculation is that the observed reduction of CoQ10 may be linked to mitochondrial damage in intestinal epithelial cells caused by IRI. Additionally, some congenital mutations in the COQ family and prolonged use of statin drugs were also identified as risk factors for decreased CoQ10 levels in plasma and tissues.
Previous studies have described two important functions of CoQ10: an electron acceptor from the mitochondrial complex I and complex III and an antioxidant. Indeed, previous prospective and retrospective clinical trials reported that CoQ10 supplementation could reduce serum MDA levels and increase superoxide dismutase activity in patients with multiple sclerosis and rheumatoid arthritis [ , , ]. In addition, Mohr et al. suggested that reduced CoQ10 could inhibit the peroxidation of cell membrane lipids and lipoproteins in circulation and increase the resistance of low-density lipoproteins to lipid peroxidation initiation [ ]. However, these studies did not provide direct evidence or experimental results revealing the mechanism by which CoQ10 inhibits lipid peroxidation in vitro. In the present study, we observed that in epithelial cells, CoQ10 can reduce lipid peroxidation and sensitivity to RSL3 and enhance resistance to H/R-associated ferroptosis. The cell viability curves also confirmed that the ability of CoQ10 to suppress ferroptosis was not less than those of Lip-1 or DFO. In contrast, the sharp decrease in intracellular CoQ10 levels mediated by 4-CBA or COQ2 knockout directly led to the loss of resistance to H/R-associated ferroptosis. We further demonstrated that alterations in CoQ10 levels did not affect the sensitivity of FSP1-knockout epithelial cells to ferroptosis, suggesting that the CoQ10-mediated suppression of ferroptosis is dependent on FSP1 function.
Our in vitro and in vivo models consistently showed that CoQ10′s ability to suppress ferroptosis was significantly diminished in the absence of FSP1, suggesting that its protective role is largely FSP1-dependent. However, we cannot fully exclude the possibility of minor FSP1-independent effects—such as its general antioxidant properties—contributing to cellular protection under certain conditions [ ]. Further studies are warranted to determine whether such mechanisms play a role in other cell types or disease contexts.
FSP1, formerly known as apoptosis-inducing factor mitochondria-associated 2 (AIFM2), was initially characterized as a mitochondrion-associated apoptosis-inducing gene because of its sequence similarity to apoptosis-inducing factor mitochondria-associated 1 (AIFM1). However, Bersuker et al. used fluorescence labelling and total internal reflection fluorescence microscopy to directly show that FSP1 is located on the plasma membrane and revealed that it functions as a nonmitochondrial antioxidant system to suppress lipid peroxidation. Our work further revealed that FSP1 functions as an antioxidant mainly by increasing the levels of phospholipids containing polyunsaturated fatty acids and decreasing the levels of lysophospholipids and ether-linked phospholipids but does not affect the levels of most neutral lipids. Creating loss of function of FSP1 via genetic manipulation or administering a small molecule inhibitor resulted in lower levels of reduced CoQ10 and a corresponding increase in the levels of oxidized CoQ10 in epithelial cells. Adding CoQ10 to the culture medium effectively elevated the intracellular levels of reduced CoQ10, whereas plasmid-mediated FSP1 overexpression did not. Plasmid-mediated overexpression of FSP1 in intestinal epithelial cells does not affect the level of reduced CoQ10 because FSP1 is endogenously expressed at high levels in normal intestinal epithelial cells and intestinal tissues, and this method of overexpression cannot effectively increase the protein and mRNA levels of FSP1. FSP1, an NADH-dependent oxidoreductase, can catalyse the conversion of oxidized CoQ10 into reduced CoQ10, thereby increasing the proportion of reduced CoQ10 in intestinal epithelial cells. Furthermore, there were no significant differences in the total CoQ10 levels in FSP1-KO cells and control cells, but the proportion of reduced CoQ10 was significantly decreased in the former. Reduced CoQ10 can act as a RTA and is sufficient to suppress lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis [ , , ].
To directly assess the enzymatic role of FSP1 in reducing CoQ10, we performed NADH consumption assays using recombinant FSP1 protein and oxidized CoQ10 as a substrate. The results confirmed that FSP1 exhibited NADH-dependent oxidoreductase activity toward CoQ10, idebenone, and resazurin, but not GSSG, indicating substrate specificity. These findings support the notion that FSP1 actively catalyzes the reduction of CoQ10, rather than serving merely as a CoQ10 carrier protein. Although plasmid-mediated overexpression of FSP1 in Caco-2 cells did not significantly increase reduced CoQ10 levels—likely due to high endogenous expression in this cell type—the in vitro enzymatic activity assay provides strong mechanistic evidence of its functional capacity.
CoQ10 has received widespread attention as a health supplement for the prevention and amelioration of a range of medical conditions, including insulin resistance [ ], cardiovascular diseases [ ], neurodegenerative diseases [ ] and primary CoQ10 deficiency-related diseases [ ]. While the mitochondrial energy coupling function of total CoQ10 extensively investigated in these studies, a detailed examination of the individual roles of oxidized and reduced CoQ10 is lacking. Our findings indicated that subcutaneously administered CoQ10 was well tolerated and effectively increased the total CoQ10 levels in multiple types of mouse tissues, including the intestines, heart, liver and kidneys. Although two doses of CoQ10 were administered (15 mg/kg and 30 mg/kg), the tissue CoQ10 concentrations did not differ significantly between groups. This may be explained by metabolic saturation of CoQ10 uptake pathways, limited absorption capacity in certain tissues, or pharmacokinetic limitations of the subcutaneous delivery route. These factors should be further investigated in future studies. In vitro, reduced CoQ10 accounted for approximately 33 % of the total CoQ10 in human intestinal epithelial cells. Pharmacological inhibition or genetic knockout of FSP1 significantly lowered the absolute and relative levels of reduced CoQ10. While a previous report highlighted the capacity of CoQ10 supplementation to mitigate hepatic injury and lipid peroxidation [ ], our study revealed a novel role of CoQ10 in attenuating intestinal IRI, which is contingent on its reduction by FSP1. This study provides the first evidence suggesting that an increase in reduced CoQ10 levels in intestinal tissues is crucial for alleviating IRI.
An intriguing question remains regarding the mechanism underlying the increase in the levels of reduced CoQ10 in local tissues. Compared with control cells, FSP1 overexpressing epithelial cells did not exhibit greater levels of reduced CoQ10. This observation may be attributed to the fact that FSP1 overexpression does not increase its catalytic activity toward oxidized CoQ10.
Several limitations exist in the human component of our study. First, the clinical sample size was relatively small, and all subjects were recruited from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, we employed a convenience sampling method rather than a randomized or consecutive enrollment approach, potentially introducing selection bias. Furthermore, the absence of a statistical power calculation prior to the study means we cannot definitively confirm the adequacy of our sample size to detect all relevant clinical associations. Lastly, the lack of a clearly defined healthy control group constrains our ability to compare our findings with baseline physiological states.
In conclusion, our study revealed that IRI is characterized by ferroptosis concurrent with impaired CoQ10 production in intestinal epithelial cells. The reduced CoQ10 produced by FSP1 robustly inhibitied ferroptosis by directly impeding phospholipid peroxidation. Consequently, increasing the absolute content of reduced CoQ10 in the intestines via CoQ10 supplementation has emerged as a promising strategy for the treatment of intestinal IRI.

## Materials and methods

## Cell lines and drug treatments
Caco-2 cells, HEK-293 T cells, and MC38 cells were obtained from Procell, FHs74 Int primary small intestinal cells were obtained from ATCC and maintained in a Thermo Scientific incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. Prior to experimentation, all the cell lines were rigorously tested for mycoplasma contamination and confirmed to be negative. HEK-293 T cells and MC38 cells were cultured and passaged in Dulbecco's modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, 8122471) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin solution (P/S). Caco-2 cells were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM; Gibco, 8122489) supplemented with 20 % FBS (Gibco, 10100147) and 1 % P/S (Solarbio, P1400). DH5α and Stbl3 were obtained from Tsingke Biotech.
RSL3 (Selleckchem, S8155) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Solarbio, D8371). Various concentrations of RSL3 (ranging from 1 nM to 10000 nM) were prepared for the treatment of Caco2 cells. Idebenone (Cayman Chemical, 15475), Lip-1 (Selleckchem, S7699), DFO (Cayman Chemical, 14595) and iFSP (Selleckchem, S9663) were dissolved in DMSO at a stock concentration of 10 mM. 4-CBA (Sigma–Aldrich, 135585) and CoQ10 (Sigma–Aldrich, C9538) were dissolved in ethanol (EtOH) to generate stock solutions with concentrations of 300 mM and 3 mM, respectively. A 3 mM solution of 4-CBA was utilized to inhibit CoQ10 synthesis.

## Cell viability assays
Cells were seeded in 48-well plates at a density of 15,000 cells per well and treated with a panel of small molecule compounds (RSL3, CoQ10, Lip-1, DFO, and 4-CBA). To evaluate cell viability in response to varying RSL3 concentrations, 20 µL of CCK-8 solution (TargetMol, C0005) was added to each well after treatment. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Viability was assessed by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm, with normalization to a reference wavelength (630 nm). Dose–response curves were constructed from the plots and fitted using GraphPad Prism (version 8 for macOS).

## BODIPY 581/591 analysis
Caco-2 cells were cultured in 48-well plates one day prior to the experiments and then treated with ethanol (control), RSL3, or RSL3 in combination with idebenone, Lip-1, DFO, or 4-CBA for 48–72 h. After treatment, the cells were washed with HBSS buffer. Lipid peroxidation was then assessed by incubating the cells with 10 μM BODIPY 581/591 C11 (Invitrogen) for 30 min at 37 °C. After a subsequent wash, the cells were incubated with 10 μM Hoechst (Sigma–Aldrich, 94403) for 5 min to stain the nuclei. Fluorescence microscopy was employed to visualize the cells. Quantitative analysis of the mean fluorescence intensity was conducted using ImageJ software, with red fluorescence indicative of reduced BODIPY 581/591 C11 (nonoxidized lipids) and green fluorescence reflecting oxidized BODIPY 581/591 C11 (lipid peroxidation).
For BODIPY 581/591 staining of human intestinal tissues, the samples were first subjected to dehydration in a 25 % sucrose solution at room temperature for 2 h before being embedded in OCT compound. Tissue sections (5 μm thick) were meticulously cut and thoroughly rinsed with PBS three times. The sections were then stained with a BODIPY 581/591 working solution for 20 min in a 37 °C incubator. After BODIPY staining, the sections were further incubated with DAPI solution to stain the nuclei in the dark to prevent photobleaching. The fluorescence signals were captured using a fluorescence microscope, and quantification of the fluorescence intensity was performed with ImageJ software.

## Cell death analysis
Live/dead dye (Invitrogen, L3224) was used to distinguish dead cells from live cells on the basis of their emission of red or green fluorescence. Briefly, after the specified treatments, the medium was replaced with prewarmed DPBS containing 4 μM calcein-AM and 2 μM ethidium homodimer-1. The plates were then incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. Cells exhibiting red or green fluorescence were quantified using a fluorescence microscopy and Fiji 2.0 software. Calcein-AM emits green fluorescence in the presence of intracellular esterase activity, which is ubiquitous in live cells, whereas ethidium homodimer-1 can enter dead cells through damaged membranes and bind to nucleic acids, resulting in red fluorescence emission.

## CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout
LentiCRISPRv2 (Addgene, 52961) vector plasmids were digested with BsmB I (NEB, R0136), followed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and the target band was separated using a gel extraction kit (Omega, D2500). Single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) were designed, cloned and inserted into the BsmB I-digested lentiCRISPRv2 vector. The successfully constructed lentiCRISPRv2 plasmids and the packaging plasmids pVSVg and psPAX2 were added to Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, 11668019), mixed well and transfected into HEK293T cells for 48 h. The virus supernatant was collected and concentrated using PEG6000 (Merck, 807491). Caco-2 cells were infected with high-titer lentivirus for 48 h and selected with puromycin. Single-cell clones were picked and sequenced to ensure that out-of-frame mutation was successful. Western blotting was used to confirm successful knockout of the target gene. For the double-knockout cell lines, two lentiviral particles were produced using the lentiCRISPRv2 and lentiCRISPRv2-Blast plasmids targeting FSP1 and COQ2, respectively, and used to coinfect Caco-2 cells. The cells were subsequently subjected to dual selection with both puromycin and blasticidin to generate FSP1 and COQ2 double-knockout cells.

## Generation of FSP1 overexpressing cells
For FSP1 overexpression, Caco-2 cells were prepared at 70 %–80 % confluence in 6-well plates. Two micrograms of pCMV3-FSP1 (Sino Biological, MG52065-UT) or pCMV3-untagged negative control vector plasmid (Sino Biological, CV011) diluted in Opti-MEM was transfected into Caco-2 cells using transfection reagent. After 48 h, FSP1 overexpression efficiency was determined using PCR and Western blot analysis. Then, the cells were continuously selected with hygromycin to generate stable FSP1 overexpressing Caco-2 cells.

## AAV-9-mediated FSP1 knockdown in the intestine
An AAV-9 system was used specifically knock down FSP1 expression in the intestine. To determine the most effective siRNA sequence targeting FSP1, we employed an online tool (https://sirna.wi.mit.edu/siRNA_search.cgi?tasto=2024336241) and designed multiple siRNA sequences. A third-generation lentiviral system comprising the transfer plasmid pLko.1 (Addgene, 8453), along with the helper plasmids pGag/pol (Addgene, 14887), pVsvg (Addgene, 8454) and pRev (Addgene, 12253), was used to verify the efficiency of each siRNA in MC38 cells. The sequence 5′-GATGGCAGCAGAGATTAAA-3 demonstrated the highest knockdown efficiency against FSP1, and AAV9-shFSP1 containing this sequence was synthesized by Hanbio Tech (Shanghai, China). Eight-week-old C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and 1.7 × 1012 vg/mL HBAAV2/9-m-FSP1 shRNA1-EGFP (AAV9-shFSP1) or HBAAV2/9-EGFP NC (AAV9-shCtrl) was injected carefully into different sites of the mouse peritoneal cavity (30 μL per site).

## Mice
Eight- to ten-week-old C57BL/6 mice were acquired from the Experimental Animal Center of Xi'an Jiaotong University. The animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility, with the temperature maintained at 24–25 °C on a consistent 12-h dark/light cycle. Sterilized food and water were provided to the mice ad libitum. The Institutional Ethics Committee of Xi'an Jiaotong University granted ethical approval for all animal-related experimental protocols (approval no. XJTUAE2023-564). The procedures were performed in strict compliance, and all the experiments were conducted in accordance with the established Guidelines for the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xi'an Jiaotong University.

## Intestinal IRI models
To induce intestinal IRI, male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg). The animals were placed on a sterile operating platform equipped with a heating pad to maintain body temperature at 37 ± 0.5 °C throughout the surgical procedure. A 3–5 mm midline abdominal incision was made using ophthalmic scissors to expose the abdominal cavity. The ileum was gently exteriorized, and the SMA was carefully isolated using moistened cotton swabs. Ischemia was induced by clamping the SMA with microvascular clips for predetermined durations (15, 30, 60, or 120 min). Reperfusion was initiated by removing the clips, and allowed to proceed for 120 min.
At the end of the reperfusion period, mice were humanely euthanized via carbon dioxide inhalation. Blood samples were collected by cardiac puncture, and ischemic intestinal tissues were immediately harvested for histological, biochemical, and molecular analyses.
To establish an IRI model in vitro, Caco-2 cells were subjected to H/R to induce enterocyte IRI. Briefly, Caco-2 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were transferred to a hypoxic incubator with 5 % carbon dioxide and 95 % nitrogen for 12 h. After the hypoxia phase, the cells were subjected to 95 % oxygen and 5 % carbon dioxide conditions for 2 h to simulate the reperfusion phase.
In cell experiments involving Caco-2 cells, hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) conditions were precisely controlled. Cells were subjected to hypoxia by culturing them in an anaerobic chamber with 5 % CO2, and 95 % N2 for 12 h. Subsequently, reoxygenation was performed by transferring cells to an environment with 95 % O2 and 5 % CO2 for another 12 h. For ferroptosis induction, Caco-2 cells were treated with RSL3 at concentrations ranging from 1 nM to 1000 nM. Specifically, optimal ferroptosis induction was achieved with 15 nM RSL3, determined by preliminary dose–response assays. For rescue experiments, cells were pre-treated with CoQ10 (5 μM), liproxstatin-1 (1 μM), or deferoxamine (DFO, 100 μM) for 2 h prior to RSL3 treatment. All experimental concentrations were selected based on previous literature and preliminary optimization experiments.

## Antibodies
We used antibodies against β-actin (CST, 4970, 1:5000), ZO-1 (CST, 13663, 1:200), Occludin (CST, 91131, 1:200), GPX4 (CST, 59735, 1:1000), FTH1 (CST, 4393, 1:1000), ACSL4 (Abcam, 155282, 1:1000), FSP1 (CST, 24972, 1:1000), SLC7A11 (CST, 12691, 1:1000), and COQ2 (Abcam, 220914, 1:1000) along with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Proteintech, SA00001-2, 1:5000), HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Proteintech, SA00001-1, 1:5000), CoraLite488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Proteintech, SA00013-2, 1:500), and CoraLite594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Proteintech, SA00013-4, 1:500).

## Quantification of intestinal iron concentrations
The iron levels in intestinal tissues exposed to IRI were quantitatively assessed utilizing an iron assay kit (Abcam, 83366) according to the manufacturer's guidelines. Intestinal segments from the IRI models were first rinsed with chilled PBS and then cut into small pieces using scissors. Approximately 10–15 mg of intestinal tissue was quickly homogenized on ice in iron assay buffer, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to eliminate insoluble material. Total and ferrous iron levels were quantified by measuring the absorbance at 593 nm. The concentration of ferric iron was determined by subtracting the amount of ferrous iron from the total iron content to provide a comprehensive analysis of the iron status within intestinal tissues subjected to IRI.

## Measurement of intestinal GPx activity
The enzymatic activity of GPx in intestinal tissues affected by IRI was assessed using a glutathione peroxidase assay kit (Cayman Chemical, 703102) following the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, intestines subjected to IRI were washed to eliminate the intestinal contents and then cut into small pieces. Approximately 5 mg of each tissue sample was homogenized in 30 mL of a specifically formulated cold lysis buffer composed of 100 mM Tris-HCl, 10 mM EDTA, and 2 mM DTT. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, allowing for the separation and removal of insoluble materials, after which the clear supernatant was carefully extracted for analysis. GPx activity was indirectly determined through a coupled reaction in which GPx reduced hydroperoxide to produce GSSG and GSSG was subsequently reduced to produce GSH and NADP+.

## Serum collection and inflammatory marker analysis
Blood samples were collected from the retroorbital sinuses of anesthetized mice, followed by coagulation at room temperature for 30 min to facilitate serum separation. The clotted blood was then centrifuged to obtain serum for biochemical analyses. The levels of IL-6 and TNF-α, as well as the activity of LDH, were determined using the mouse IL-6 ELISA kit (Abcam, 100712), mouse TNF-α ELISA kit (Abcam, 208348), and LDH assay kit (Abcam, 102526), respectively, following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer.

## Serum collection and biochemical analysis
Whole blood was collected from deeply anesthetized mice via cardiac puncture. The blood was allowed to stand for 30 min at room temperature to clot, and the serum was separated by centrifugation at 1500 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The activities of AST and ALT in the serum were measured using aspartate aminotransferase (Abcam, 105135) and alanine transaminase activity assay kits (Abcam, 105135), respectively. In addition, total bilirubin, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen and cTnI levels were measured using a bilirubin ELISA kit (Abbexa, 156748), creatinine assay kit (Abcam, 65340), urea nitrogen colorimetric detection kit (Invitrogen, EIABUN) and mouse cardiac troponin-I ELISA kit (Life Diagnostics, CTNI-1-HS).

## Measurement of intestinal GSH, GSSG and MDA levels
Small intestines subjected to IRI were rinsed to remove the intestinal contents and then cut into small pieces. The levels of GSH and GSSG and the ratio of GSH/GSSG were measured using a GSH + GSSG/GSH assay kit (Abcam, 239709) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Additionally, the level of MDA was determined using a lipid peroxidation assay kit (Abcam, 118970) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

## Western blotting
Tissues or cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 250 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 50 mM NaF, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1 % Triton X-100, 0.1 % SDS, and 0.02 % NaN3) supplemented with 1 % protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Merck, PPC1010). The protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay (Beyotime, P0012). The proteins were subsequently separated by SDS–PAGE on the basis of their molecular weight and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Merck, IPVH00010). Following transfer, the membrane was blocked with 5 % nonfat milk for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was then washed three times with TBST buffer and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized via electrochemiluminescence (NCMBiotech, P10300), and gray value analysis of the images was conducted using Fiji software.

## Measurement of CoQ levels in tissues and cells
The serum CoQ10 concentrations were determined using a Coenzyme Q10 ELISA kit (Abbexa, 159767). Additionally, we assessed the levels of total, reduced and oxidized CoQ10 in Caco-2 cells; mouse intestines, hearts, kidneys, livers, lungs and brains; and small bowels from patients via LC–MS/MS. Briefly, cells or tissues were washed with prechilled PBS and resuspended in lysis buffer. To determine the ratio of reduced/oxidized CoQ10, 100 μL of CoQ10-d9 (Sigma–Aldrich, 802891) was added to the cell homogenate as an internal standard, and butylhydroxytoluene (Merck, B1215000) was included to prevent the autooxidation of reduced CoQ10. The separation of reduced and oxidized CoQ10 was achieved using a Nexera X2 UHPLC system coupled with an LCMS-8060 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Shimadzu Corporation) and a Kinete Core-Shell C18 HPLC column (Phenomenex, 00D-4462-AN), employing a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min, injection volume of 2 μL, and a run time of 6 min. Shimadzu LabSolutions analytical data management software was used to quantify the levels of reduced CoQ10 and oxidized CoQ10 in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode.
The total CoQ9 levels in the intestines, hearts, kidneys, livers, lungs and brains of the mice were quantified using a similar LC–MS/MS method.

## Targeted metabolomics
For targeted metabolomics analysis, 1 × 108 Cas9Ctrl and FSP1KO Caco-2 cells were treated with RSL3 under culture conditions, including supplementation with CoQ10, for 24 h. After treatment, the cells were collected by gentle scraping and lysed in 400 μL of ultrapure water, followed by homogenization at 45 Hz three times and sonication for 1 min in an ice water bath. An extraction solution, which included internal standards, was then added to each sample. The homogenates were subsequently centrifuged at 3000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were gently collected and transferred to a new tube. The pellet was further processed with an equal volume of methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) and vortexed, sonicated and centrifuged again. The resulting supernatants were pooled, and the liquid was evaporated in a vacuum concentrator at 37 °C until the samples were dry. For LC–MS/MS analysis, the dried samples were resuspended in 100 μL of resuspension buffer consisting of dichloromethane (DCM), methanol (MeOH), and water (H2O) at a 60:30:4.5 ratio, vortexed for 30 s, and sonicated for 10 min in an ice water bath. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min, 40 μL of the clear supernatant was transferred to a glass vial for analysis. A total of 800 lipids were quantified by Shanghai Biotree Biotech Co., Ltd., using the SCIEX ExionLC and SCIEX QTrap 6500 + LC–MS/MS systems.

## Quantitative RT–PCR
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Invitrogen, 15596018) and then reverse transcribed into DNA using the iScript™ Reverse Transcription SuperMix (Bio-Rad, 1708840). Quantitative PCR was conducted using SYBR Green reagent. The sequences of the primers used were as follows: FSP1 (forward: 5′-AGTAGTGGGGATAGACCTGAAGA-3′ and reverse: 5′-CCACCACGATGAACCGTGA-3′) and β-actin (forward: 5′-AGGATTCCTATGTGGGCGAC-3′ and reverse: 5′-ATAGCACAGCCTGGATAGCAA-3′).

## Transmission electron microscopy
Tissues were fixed with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde fixation buffer (Biosharp, BL911B), dehydrated, embedded, sectioned, and stained. The mitochondria were subsequently imaged using a transmission electron microscope (HT7800, Hitachi, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV.

## Immunofluorescence and H&E staining
For immunofluorescence staining of the intestinal tissues, the slides were initially fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min. Then, the slides were permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton-X-100 in PBS and subsequently blocked with 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA). The slides were then incubated with the corresponding primary antibodies at 4 °C for 24 h. Following incubation, the slides were washed with PBS and then incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies for 1 h. After repeated washes, the cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI staining solution (Abcam, 228549).
For H&E staining, the slides were incubated with hematoxylin (Servicebio, G1004) and eosin (Servicebio, G1001) solutions. The images were scanned using a ZEISS Axioscan 7 microscope slide scanner, and quantification was performed using ImageJ software.

## FSP1 reduction assay
To determine the kinetic parameters of the FSP1 reduction reaction, 100 nM recombinant human FSP1 (Cayman, 29611) was incubated with 100 μM NADH (Solarbio, N8120) and different electron acceptor substrates, including CoQ10, idebenone, resazurin and GSSG, in PBS at 37 °C. The rates of reduction of different substrates by FSP1 were determined by monitoring the consumption of NADH, which absorbs light at 340 nm. The absorbance at 340 nm was recorded every two min using a BioTek 800 TS absorbance reader (Agilent).

## Inhibition of egg PC liposome autoxidation
To determine the role of FSP1 in the inhibition of the autoxidation of egg PC liposomes, we established the following experimental conditions. Initially, 1 mM egg PC liposomes (Avanti Polar Lipids, 840051), 1 μM STY-BODIPY, and 25 μM NADH were added to a black polypropylene plate as a blank control (uninhibited). To study the effects of FSP1 and CoQ10/idebenone on autoxidation inhibition, additional plates were prepared with 100 nM recombinant human FSP1 and 10 μM CoQ10, 10 μM idebenone or DMSO. The autoxidation of egg PC was initiated by the addition of 0.2 mM DTUN (Cayman, 32742) and incubation at 37 °C. We measured the relative fluorescence unit (RFU) values using a BioTek Synergy H1 plate reader (Agilent) at five minute intervals with excitation at 488 nm and emission at 518 nm. The levels of oxidized STY-BODIPY were then calculated by dividing the RFU values by 7.49 × 104 RFU/μM.

## Patient samples
The human serum samples in this study were collected from patients who were diagnosed with mesenteric artery embolism and before they received interventional thrombectomy therapy. The serum CoQ10 concentration was quantified using a Coenzyme Q10 ELISA kit (Abbexa, 159767). The levels of IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, IL-4, IL-17 and INF-γ in the patients’ serum were measured by the Clinical Laboratory of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University. Human IRI and non-IRI intestinal samples were collected from individuals who had undergone intestinal ostomy or intestinal stoma closure. All samples were utilized exclusively for scientific research purposes and were not used for clinical diagnosis or treatment. All patients who participated in this study signed an informed consent form in accordance with the regulations governing the collection of informed consent documents in China. ## RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)
RNA was isolated from mouse intestinal tissues with TRIzol reagent, and the quantity and integrity of the RNA were assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer and NanoDrop spectrophotometer, respectively. For library preparation, 1.5 μg of total RNA per sample was processed using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. First, mRNA was purified with poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads followed by first-strand cDNA synthesis using random hexamer primers and M−MuLV reverse transcriptase. Second-strand cDNA synthesis was then performed utilizing DNA polymerase I and RNase H. The AMPure XP system was used to purify the library fragments, and cDNA fragments 200 to 250 bp in length were selected. Prior to PCR amplification, the library fragments were subjected to treatment with 3 μL of USER Enzyme at 37 °C for 15 min and subsequently denatured at 95 °C for 5 min. PCR was then conducted using Phusion High-Fidelity DNA polymerase, universal PCR primers, and the Index (X) primer. The PCR products were subsequently purified using the AMPure XP system, and library quality was evaluated using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 platform by Beijing Allwegene Technology Company Limited (Beijing, China), and 150 bp paired-end reads were produced.

## KEGG pathway enrichment analysis
The RNA-seq expression profiles of 28 human jejunum samples, comprising 7 control samples and 21 IRI samples, were obtained from the GEO database. Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package in R, adopting a stringent false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of less than 0.05 to identify DEGs. For pathway enrichment analysis, the DEGs were subjected to KEGG pathway analysis using the hypergeometric test with the clusterProfiler package in R. This approach facilitated the identification of pathways significantly enriched in the DEGs, providing insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying IRI in the human jejunum.

## Gene ontology (GO) pathway enrichment and GSEA
RNA-seq analysis was conducted to evaluate the gene expression profiles of intestinal samples from mice in different experimental groups, including 4 sham tissue samples, 4 IRI tissue samples, 4 IRI tissue samples treated with vehicle, and 4 IRI tissue samples treated with CoQ10. To gain insight into the biological pathways associated with the DEGs, GO biological process term enrichment analysis was performed using Cytoscape (version 3.10.1) and the clusterProfiler package in R. Each GO term was considered a gene set and was imported into the Java gene set enrichment analysis platform for further analysis. Gene sets exhibiting a P value less than 0.05 and an FDR less than 0.25 were considered statistically significant, indicating the enrichment of the specific biological processes associated with the identified DEGs.

## Molecular docking
Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina (version 1.2.3) following established protocols [ ]. The crystal structure of FSP1 (PDB ID: 8wik) was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. All water molecules and non-essential ligands were removed from the protein structure using AutoDock Tools (version 1.5.6). All hydrogen atoms were added, and charges were assigned to the protein. The 2D structure (sdf format) of CoQ-10 was obtained from PubChem (CID: 5281915) and converted to 3D structure (mol2 format). Torsion angles of the ligand were defined using the AutoDock Tools rotatable bonds algorithm. A grid box encompassing the entire binding pocket was generated with the following dimensions: center coordinates (x = 16.442, y = −9.336, z = −18.572) and size (69.65 × 69.65 × 69.65). Then, output the docking parameter to the vina config.txt file and run autodock vina. The docking poses were ranked by binding affinity (kcal/mol). The conformation with the lowest binding energy was selected as the most probable binding mode. Structural visualization and ligand–protein interaction analysis were performed using PyMOL (version 2.5.4).

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism, and the normality of the quantitative data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two groups were conducted using t tests. One-way ANOVA was used for comparisons of multiple groups with a single factor, and two-way ANOVA was used for comparisons of multiple groups with two factors. For nonnormally distributed data, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare multiple groups. The chi-square test was used for rank data. Univariate logistic regression was first performed to identify potential predictors of intestinal necrosis following IRI. Variables with a P-value <0.10 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors. Plasma coenzyme Q10 levels were treated as a categorical variable based on the overall distribution. Normality was confirmed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (P > 0.05). The mean coenzyme Q10 level in the cohort (735 ng/mL) was used as the cutoff to classify patients into high (≥735 ng/mL) and low (<735 ng/mL) groups. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. A two-tailed P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. No correction for multiple comparisons (e.g., Bonferroni adjustment) was applied to the multivariate regression results, as each variable's effect was evaluated in the context of a single comprehensive model. This approach aligns with established statistical practice, where multivariable models aim to assess adjusted associations rather than perform multiple independent tests. The in vitro data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) of three independent experiments conducted in triplicate. All animal experimental data are expressed as the means ± SDs. All P values are shown in the corresponding figures, and P values less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.
