## Abstract
The peritoneum is a frequent site of metastasis in ovarian cancer (OVCA), often accompanied by the accumulation of ascites in the peritoneal cavity. Despite its prevalence, ascites and its role in the peritoneal growth of OVCA remain poorly understood. OVCA cells are vulnerable to ferroptosis, a type of cell death caused by lipid hydroperoxides, raising the question of how these ferroptosis-sensitive cells survive during metastasis. Here, we show that ascites from female donors protects OVCA cell lines, patient-derived tumor cells, and organoids against ferroptosis and enhances peritoneal tumor growth in female mice. Mechanistically, ascites downregulates 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 2 (HMGCS2), contributing to increased lipid droplets. Additionally, upon ferroptosis induction, ascites represses upregulation of the transferrin receptor TFRC, thereby decreasing labile iron levels. Furthermore, lipid-lowering fibrates reverse ascites-induced changes and attenuate peritoneal growth in female mice. These findings identify ascites-mediated ferroptosis protection as a key mechanism in OVCA metastasis and a potential therapeutic vulnerability.
Subject terms: Ovarian cancer, Metastasis, Cell death

Peritoneal metastasis of ovarian cancer is often accompanied by the accumulation of ascitic fluid. Here, the authors find that ascites protects ovarian cancer cells against ferroptosis, thus enabling their spread in the peritoneum.

## Introduction
Metastasis significantly contributes to the fatal progression of high-grade ovarian cancer (OVCA) , . OVCA metastasis typically involves the spread of cancer cells from the primary tumors, either in the ovaries or the fallopian tubes, to other parts of the abdominal cavity, such as the omentum, the peritoneum, and lymph nodes . Advanced high-grade OVCA is often accompanied by increased vascular permeability and fluid leakage , leading to an abnormal accumulation of the ascites fluid in the peritoneal cavity , . Although ascites is observed in other diseases, such as liver cirrhosis, it is most frequently observed with metastatic OVCA , . Due to the enrichment of cellular and acellular factors, the ascitic fluid is reported to harbor a growth-promoting and immune-evading environment for cancer cells and is thought to serve as a medium for cancer cell dissemination and tumor progression and metastasis . However, much remains unknown about how ascites affects the process of peritoneal spread of OVCA.
The biological processes in the metastatic cascade for OVCA cells are harsh, often rendering them sensitive to various stressors , – . Specifically, we and other groups have reported that detached and metastasizing OVCA cells are especially vulnerable to ferroptosis – , a form of cell death characterized by iron dependency and an irreversible accumulation of lipid hydroperoxides . Lipid peroxidation can occur due to ROS (reactive oxygen species) generation, which are natural byproducts of several cellular metabolism pathways and are often catalyzed by labile iron . Usually, cellular defense mechanisms are in place to prevent or repair lipid hydroperoxides, thereby preventing ferroptosis . For example, the xCT transporter imports cystine, which leads to the generation of the antioxidant glutathione (GSH) as a cofactor of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), an enzyme responsible for the repair of lipid hydroperoxides . Concurrently, GPX4-independent ferroptosis protection mechanisms include the ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1) and dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH), which generate ubiquinol to protect against ferroptosis on the plasma membrane and inner mitochondrial membrane, respectively . Finally, lipid droplets can protect against ferroptosis by serving as protective sinks – . Nevertheless, ferroptosis ensues if lipid hydroperoxide accumulation outweighs the cellular neutralizing mechanisms or when these mechanisms are inhibited by various ferroptosis inducers . Previously, we have found that OVCA is particularly sensitive to ferroptosis . In addition, several metastasis-associated processes promote ferroptosis, such as epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), loss of cell-cell contact, the YAP1/TAZ activation, and the detachment of tumor cells from adjacent cells and the ECM .
This vulnerability of metastasizing OVCA cells to ferroptosis is intriguing, as it confounds how these ferroptosis-sensitive OVCA cells overcome ferroptosis-promoting processes to establish metastasis. To investigate this intriguing question, we wanted to examine the potential role of ascites in ferroptosis sensitivity and the peritoneal spread of OVCA. Nothing is currently known about how ascites may influence OVCA cells’ ferroptosis. For metastasizing melanoma cells, the lymph significantly impacts ferroptosis sensitivity, which may contribute to preferential lymphatic metastasis . Given the common occurrence of ascites with peritoneal metastasis, ascites may be crucial for the peritoneal spread of OVCA.
Here, we report that OVCA ascites robustly protects established OVCA cells, and OVCA patient-derived tumor cells and organoids against ferroptosis, and it enhances the peritoneal growth in murine models. Mechanistically, ascites protects against ferroptosis by increasing lipid droplets via repression of the mitochondrial enzyme, 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 2 (HMGCS2) and a subsequent decrease in fatty acid oxidation. Furthermore, ascites represses the ferroptosis-induced membrane upregulation of the transferrin receptor (TFRC) and labile iron. We show that these effects of ascites can be attenuated using lipid-lowering fibrates, namely bezafibrate, and we validate our findings in OVCA patient-derived tumor cells, organoids, and murine models of OVCA peritoneal metastasis.

## Results

## Ascites protects ovarian cancer cells from ferroptosis
To investigate whether ascites from OVCA patients influences ferroptosis, we added ascites collected from three different patients with metastatic OVCA to different OVCA cell lines, including CAOV3 and TYKNU (high-grade serous), TOV21G (clear), and TOV112D (endometroid) cells. These cells were incubated in regular media with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and ferroptosis was triggered by ferroptosis-inducing agents in the presence or absence of ascites. Then, the effects of ascites on ferroptosis were determined via cell viability. As little as 2% of ascites robustly protected all four OVCA cell lines against ferroptosis induced both by the xCT inhibitor, erastin (Fig.  ), and RSL3, a GPX4 inhibitor (Fig.  ) . This ferroptosis protection by ascites was reproduced with additional ferroptosis inducers that have distinct chemical structures, including Imidazole Ketone Erastin (IKE) and JKE-1674 (Supplementary Fig.  ). We also genetically triggered ferroptosis by CRISPR-mediated knockout of GPX4 in CAOV3 and TYKNU and found that all three tested ascites samples were able to rescue both cell lines from ferroptosis, comparable to the protection conferred by ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) and liproxstatin-1 (Lipro-1) (Supplementary Fig.  ). Importantly, increasing the concentration of FBS in the culture media did not replicate the ferroptosis-protective effect, demonstrating that the rescue is not attributable to general nutrient supplementation (Supplementary Fig.  ). Thus, ferroptosis resistance is a specific property of ascites rather than a nonspecific consequence of increased media nutrients or content. To test whether the protective effect of ascites was specific to ferroptosis, we tested ascites’ impact on the cell viability of CAOV3 cells when treated with non-ferroptosis cell death inducers (e.g., staurosporine, actinomycin D, and rapamycin). In contrast to its robust protection against ferroptosis, ascites did not protect cells from these alternative cell death reagents (Supplementary Fig.  ). We also tested the effects of ascites on cisplatin, a platinum-based drug commonly used for treating OVCA patients . Although cisplatin has been reported to induce ferroptosis , neither ascites nor the ferroptosis inhibitor, Lipro-1 provided protective effects in our experiments (Supplementary Fig.  ). This lack of protection suggests that cisplatin predominantly induces cell death through ferroptosis-independent mechanisms, such as apoptosis, which it has been shown to induce . These findings further support the specificity of ascites-mediated protection of ferroptosis instead of a general resistance to all forms of drug-induced cell death. Additionally, we measured the effects of ascites on various ferroptosis biomarkers, such as lipid peroxidation. While RSL3 and erastin significantly increased lipid peroxidation, this increase was abolished by ascites (Fig.  ). Furthermore, we assessed CHAC1 induction, an established biomarker of ferroptosis, and observed that its expression significantly decreased with ascites co-treatment (Supplementary Fig.  ).
 We then investigated whether ferroptosis protection required the continuous presence of ascites. To test this, we added ascites to CAOV3 cells for 16 hours, after which the ascites was removed, washed with PBS, and the cells were immediately treated with erastin and monitored for cell death for 72 hours (Fig.  ). Notably, while CAOV3 cells not previously exposed to ascites died within 24 hours after erastin treatment, CAOV3 cells that had a short-term exposure to ascites, or had continuous ascites exposure, were still viable after 72 hours post erastin treatment (Fig.  ). These results indicate that short-term exposure to ascites induces a ferroptosis-resistant state that persists even after its removal. The results, together with the ability of ascites to protect against ferroptosis triggered by genetic means (GPX4 knockout in Supplementary Fig.  ), also further rule out that ascites protects against ferroptosis by inactivating various ferroptosis-inducing agents.
We then wanted to further test the reproducibility of this ferroptosis protection phenotype in additional ascites samples and its potential relationship to disease settings. Other than advanced OVCA, many patients with liver cirrhosis (LC) also develop ascites . Therefore, we compared the ferroptosis protection effects of a dose-titration of 16 OVCA ascites samples and eight LC ascites samples against ferroptosis induced by erastin in CAOV3. To retain the individual variability between ascites samples, we used the same dilution of ascites from each sample and observed that LC ascites samples, in contrast to OVCA ascites, had less ferroptosis protection as they required a significantly higher volume and exhibited less consistency in their protective effects (Supplementary Fig.  ). To further establish the clinical relevance of our findings, we cultured three validated tumor cells (Supplementary Fig.  ) collected from OVCA patients with high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) and observed that addition of ascites robustly rescued them from ferroptosis (Fig.  ). We also confirmed the ability of ascites to rescue against ferroptosis in organoids that had been derived from two OVCA patients with different cancer types – serous ovarian carcinoma (MOS 01), and adenocarcinoma with squamous differentiation of the ovary (MOS 02). From these patient-derived organoids (PDOs), we generated micro-organospheres (MOS), a high-throughput, miniaturized 3D culture platform that enables functional drug testing in a scalable and uniform format . These MOS were then treated with ascites and IKE , which is a more bioavailable analog of erastin, to evaluate the ferroptosis-protective effect of ascites in a physiologically relevant model. Our results show that ascites protected both MOS lines against IKE-induced ferroptosis (Fig.  ), confirming that ferroptosis protection by ascites can be extended to patient-derived organoids.
Lastly, ferroptosis has been proposed as a potential tumor suppression mechanism, and detached tumor cells have been shown to be particularly vulnerable to this form of cell death . Given this, we wished to determine whether the ferroptosis protection provided by ascites may promote the survival and dissemination of detached ovarian tumor cells during peritoneal spread. To model this process, CAOV3 cells transduced with a luciferase reporter and resuspended in either PBS or human ascites were injected intra-peritoneally (IP) into immunodeficient female mice. The mice then received cell-free PBS or ascites IP injections 5 and 10 days after tumor cell injections and were subsequently monitored for peritoneal tumor cell growth via IVIS imaging on day 10. We observed that ascites injections significantly increased tumor growth of CAOV3 in the peritoneal cavity (Supplementary Fig.  ). Additionally, we quantified lipid peroxidation in tumors from each group using an antibody specific for malondialdehyde (MDA)-modified lipoproteins and proteins as a proxy for lipid hydroperoxide accumulation. Compared with PBS-injected controls, tumors from the ascites-injection group exhibited lower levels of lipid hydroperoxides, consistent with reduced basal ferroptosis (Supplementary Fig.  ). Concordantly, expression of ferroptosis marker CHAC1 was lower in the ascites-injection group (Supplementary Fig.  ), whereas SLC7A11, a key anti-ferroptosis regulator, was significantly upregulated (Supplementary Fig.  ). Moderate upregulation of SLC7A11 has previously been linked to ferroptosis resistance and increased metastasis potential – . Importantly, to rule out possibility that increased tumor burden simply reflects enhanced proliferation, we quantified Ki-67 and PCNA and observed no differences between PBS- and ascites-injected groups (Supplementary Fig.  ). Together, these data indicate that ascites-driven tumor growth is attributable primarily to reduced ferroptotic cell death rather than increased proliferation.
To more closely model the dissemination of detached OVCA cells within the peritoneal cavity, we repeated our mouse experiment using a substantially lower intraperitoneal injection dose (1.25 × 10⁵ cells). Immunodeficient female mice again received cell-free PBS or ascites every five days, and peritoneal tumor growth was monitored for 20 days. Consistent with our earlier findings, ascites-treated mice exhibited significantly greater tumor burden (Fig.  ) and reduced survival (Fig.  ). Finally, to directly assess whether ascites protects against ferroptosis in vivo, we generated GPX4-knockout, luciferase-expressing CAOV3 cells and injected them intraperitoneally with or without ascites into immunodeficient female mice. GPX4KO CAOV3 cells resuspended in PBS exhibited markedly impaired intraperitoneal growth compared with WT cells, whereas GPX4KO cells resuspended in ascites displayed tumor growth comparable to WT cells, demonstrating that ascites directly suppresses ferroptosis in vivo (Fig.  ). Together, these data establish that OVCA ascites provides robust and sustained protection against ferroptosis, thereby promoting the survival and subsequent growth of detached tumor cells in the peritoneal cavity.

## Ascites lipids dictate ferroptosis protection
Next, we wanted to investigate which components in ascites are responsible for the ferroptosis protection. The acellular components of ascites consist of at least lipids, proteins, and small metabolites . To remove different components, we used dialysis to remove small metabolites from ascites, including vitamin E and other antioxidants, heat to inactivate proteins like albumin, and the biphasic separation method to remove the lipids. Triglycerides and cholesterol measuring kits were used to verify the removal of lipids (Supplementary Fig.  ), and protein levels were measured by BCA assay to normalize different samples (Supplementary Fig.  ). When comparing the effects of removing various components from ascites on its ability to protect against ferroptosis, we found that delipidation, but not dialysis or heat inactivation, significantly diminished ascites-mediated protection against erastin-induced ferroptosis in CAOV3 cells (Fig.  ). The importance of lipids in ferroptosis protection was repeated using additional OVCA cell lines (Supplementary Fig.  ), as well as with an additional ferroptosis-inducer, RSL3 (Supplementary Fig.  ). Together, these results show that the lipid components in ascites are the essential components for protecting against ferroptosis.
 We then conducted RNA-seq to identify transcriptional changes of CAOV3 and TOV21G cells exposed to ascites (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Volcano plots of differentially expressed genes (DEG) (Fig.  ) and Gene Set Enrichment Analyses (GSEA) (Fig.  ) showed that ascites downregulated genes involved in lipid metabolism pathways. Given these transcriptional changes, we next explored the potential impact on lipid composition through lipidomic profiling of erastin-treated CAOV3 cells upon exposure to ascites. Pairwise comparison of structural lipid abundance revealed that ascites treatment led to a significant accumulation of intracellular lipids (Fig.  ). Notably, the four lipid subclasses with the greatest increase in species number following ascites exposure were triglycerides (TG), phosphatidylcholines (PC), phosphatidylethanolamines (PE), and cholesteryl esters (CE) (Fig.  ). These subclasses likewise accounted for the most substantial increases in total intracellular lipid abundance (Fig.  ). To determine the source of these elevated lipids, we performed lipidomic profiling of the ascites sample used for treatment. Of the 293 lipid species that were elevated in ascites-treated CAOV3 cells, 248 shared identical fatty acid compositions with species in the ascites sample (Supplementary Fig.  ), indicating substantial overlap in lipid species between the treated cells and ascites.
Given that ascites increases intracellular lipid levels and that mono-unsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) protect against ferroptosis, whereas poly-unsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) promote it , we sought to determine whether ascites alters the MUFA-PUFA composition of the plasma membrane. To investigate this, we extracted membrane lipids of CAOV3 cells cultured with or without ascites exposure and performed LC-MS/MS analysis of the MUFA, oleic acid (18:1) and the PUFA, linoleic acid (18:2). Interestingly, while membrane oleic acid levels remained unchanged, linoleic acid levels significantly increased following ascites exposure (Supplementary Fig.  ). Given that ascites protects against ferroptosis despite this PUFA enrichment in the membrane, we considered whether other subcellular lipid compartments may contribute to ferroptosis protection. Notably, the four lipid subclasses most elevated in ascites-treated cells are known components of cellular lipid droplets . TGs comprise a glycerol backbone esterified with three fatty acids, which may be either saturated or unsaturated. CEs are composed of cholesterol conjugated to fatty acids. Both TGs and CEs are classified as ‘neutral’ lipids that are stored within lipid droplets . Lipid droplets are thought to protect against ferroptosis by sequestering ferroptosis-enhancing PUFAs . PCs and PEs, meanwhile, are structural phospholipids forming the lipid droplets’ monolayer surface and may facilitate their stability and organization . Given the marked increase in these lipid species following ascites exposure, we next investigated whether there was a corresponding increase in the levels of cellular lipid droplets. Indeed, using BODIPY 493/503 staining to detect neutral lipids, we observed that ascites treatments increased lipid droplet contents as observed using confocal microscopy (Fig.  ) and flow cytometry in CAOV3 cells (Fig.  ). Additionally, we further analyzed the fatty acid composition of the ascites-induced TGs. Notably, oleic acid was the most enriched fatty acid within this lipid pool (Supplementary Fig.  ). Together, these results identify lipids, specifically neutral lipids associated with lipid droplets, as the key components in ascites-mediated protection against ferroptosis.
Consequently, we performed a time-course lipidomic analysis to investigate how these lipids, which are too large to pass through the membrane, enter the cell. CAOV3 cells were harvested at 0-, 2-, 8-, and 16-hours following ascites exposure (Supplementary Fig.  ). A marked increase in intracellular lipid abundance was observed only at 16 hours, suggesting that ascites-derived lipids undergo a time-dependent internalization and processing step. A similar temporal pattern was observed for lipid droplet accumulation following ascites treatment (Supplementary Fig.  ). Notably, post 16 hours of treatment, lipid droplets remained elevated for up to 24 hours after ascites removal (Supplementary Fig.  ). To determine whether these lipid changes are functionally relevant for ferroptosis protection, we induced ferroptosis at defined time points (0, 2, 8, 16, 24, and 48 hours) after ascites removal. As shown in Supplementary Fig.  , the progressive decline in lipid droplet abundance closely paralleled the loss of protection from ferroptosis, supporting a functional role for lipid droplet accumulation in ferroptosis resistance.
Given the delayed uptake of ascites lipids and their importance for ferroptosis protection, we next investigated the different potential pathways of lipid entry. Ovarian cancer cells utilize multiple pathways for lipid uptake. Free fatty acids can enter cells through passive diffusion across the plasma membrane or via specific transporters such as the long-chain fatty acid transporter CD36 . In contrast, complex structural lipids such as TGs and CEs are typically delivered in LDL and HDL particles. These lipoproteins are internalized through receptor-mediated mechanisms involving the LDL receptor (LDLR) for LDL particles and scavenger receptor class B type 1 (SR-B1) for HDL , . We first assessed whether CD36-mediated fatty acid transport contributes to the ferroptosis-protective effect of ascites. Knockdown of CD36 led to only a modest reduction in the protective effect (Supplementary Fig.  ), suggesting a limited role. Given that both LDL and HDL have been reported to be present in ascites , , we next investigated whether these lipoproteins alone could replicate the ferroptosis-protective effect observed with whole ascites. Indeed, treating CAOV3 cells with either LDL or HDL conferred protection against ferroptosis, with HDL showing a markedly stronger effect (Fig.  ). These results are particularly notable because HDL has been reported at higher concentrations in malignant ascites and harbors more CEs compared to LDL , , .
To investigate whether HDL-mediated transport contributes to the protective effects of ascites, we silenced SCARB1, which encodes the HDL receptor SR-B1. Knockdown of SR-B1 significantly diminished the protective effects of ascites. Moreover, simultaneous knockdown of CD36 and SR-B1 resulted in the most pronounced reduction in ferroptosis resistance (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ), indicating that both transporters contribute to lipid uptake from ascites. A recent study supports the protective effect of HDL against ferroptosis but identifies α-tocopherol within lipoproteins as the primary driver of protection . However, we were unable to detect α-tocopherol in ascites-exposed cells by LC-MS/MS analysis. Moreover, dialyzing ascites to remove small metabolites did not diminish its ability to protect OVCA cells from erastin-induced ferroptosis (Fig.  ), arguing against a mechanism mediated by a diffusible small-molecule antioxidant. These findings suggest that ascites-mediated ferroptosis resistance occurs through a mechanism distinct from α-tocopherol transfer and establishes a unique microenvironmental context in which lipid remodeling is the dominant protective pathway. Finally, to test whether the accumulated lipids must be sequestered into lipid droplets to exert their protective effect, we disrupted lipid droplet formation by knocking down Perilipin-2 (PLIN2), a key structural protein involved in lipid droplet maintenance . This intervention similarly reduced the protective effects of ascites (Supplementary Fig.  ), reinforcing the notion that lipid droplet accumulation is required for ferroptosis resistance conferred by ascites.
To further probe the lipid composition underlying differences in ferroptosis protection capacity, we conducted a lipidomic comparison profiling between eight LC ascites and 17 OVCA ascites samples, normalized to protein content. OVCA ascites not only contained a higher total lipid content but also showed a striking enrichment in cholesteryl esters (CEs) relative to LC ascites (Fig.  ). While this does not establish a direct causal role, the elevated CE levels are consistent with the observed superiority of HDL – known to be CE-rich – as a mediator of ferroptosis protection. This aligns with previous reports of CE accumulation correlating with both ferroptosis resistance and cancer aggressiveness , . Overall, these results show that structural lipids, primarily TGs and CEs, are delivered via HDL particles in ascites, leading to the accumulation of lipid droplets that mediate resistance to ferroptosis.

## Lipid-lowering fibrates mitigate ascites protection
Our results indicate that the ascites exposure of OVCA cells is associated with an increase in protective lipids and the resulting ferroptosis protection. We were then interested in how this protective phenotype can be mitigated in ascites-exposed cells. Therefore, we tested a small panel of lipid-lowering drugs for their potential to affect ascites-mediated ferroptosis protection (Supplementary Fig.  ). Among the tested drugs, bezafibrate and C-75 Trans mitigated the ascites’ protection against ferroptosis. While the mitigating effects of C-75 Trans were not reproduced in subsequent experiments, bezafibrate consistently diminished the ascites-mediated protection against ferroptosis triggered by erastin (Fig.  ) or RSL3 (Fig.  ). Bezafibrate also attenuated the ascites-mediated protection against ferroptosis across multiple OVCA cell lines, including TYKNU, TOV21G, and TOV112D (Supplementary Fig.  ). This effect was observed not only with erastin and RSL3 but also with other ferroptosis inducers, such as IKE and JKE-1674 (Supplementary Fig.  ). Similar effects were seen using other fibrates, including fenofibrate and ciprofibrate, which are more selective against PPARα – , and a non-fibrate PPARα agonist, GW7647 (Supplementary Fig.  ). Conversely, the knockdown of PPARα abrogated the mitigating effects of bezafibrate (Supplementary Fig.  ), supporting a PPARα-dependent mechanism. Fibrate co-treatment also reduced the protective effect of HDL and liver cirrhosis ascites against ferroptosis (Supplementary Fig.  ). Importantly, these fibrates did not show notable toxicity on their own but specifically counteracted ascites-mediated ferroptosis protection. Among the tested fibrates, bezafibrate demonstrated the best solubility and lowest baseline toxicity (Supplementary Fig.  ). Hence, we focused on bezafibrate in our subsequent experiments.
 We then employed lipidomic profiling to determine how bezafibrate affected the ascites exposure-mediated changes in intracellular lipids. Pairwise comparisons revealed that bezafibrate significantly attenuated the lipid increases observed with ascites exposure alone (Fig.  ). A comparison analysis of all increased lipids in cells treated with ascites and bezafibrate showed many ascites-increased lipids were unaltered or significantly lowered by simultaneous treatment with bezafibrate (Fig.  ). Consistently, bezafibrate significantly reduced lipid droplet accumulation in the presence of ascites using confocal microscopy (Fig.  ) and flow cytometry (Fig.  ). Having established the lipid-lowering effect of bezafibrate, we then validated that ascites reduced the activity of the PPAR Response Element (PPRE)-driven luciferase reporter, which was increased by combining bezafibrate with ascites (Supplementary Fig.  ). Among all three PPARs, PPARα appears to play the most prominent role, as other PPARα agonists also reversed ascites-mediated ferroptosis resistance while PPARα knockdown abrogated bezafibrate’s effects (Supplementary Fig.  ). Supporting this, our RNA-seq showed that ascites exposure downregulated numerous PPARα target genes (Supplementary Fig.  ) . Taken together, these results suggest that bezafibrate restores ferroptosis sensitivity by reversing ascites-induced lipid metabolic changes, primarily through the PPARα pathway and reduction of intracellular lipid accumulation, including lipid droplets.
We then wanted to confirm the ferroptosis-resensitizing effect of fibrates in a patient-derived OVCA MOS line, in which both bezafibrate and ciprofibrate reversed the ascites-mediated protection against IKE-mediated ferroptosis (Fig.  ). Bezafibrate treatment also attenuated the ascites-mediated protection against ferroptosis in patient-derived OVCA tumor cells (Fig.  ). Subsequently, we wanted to determine whether bezafibrate affected the tumor growth of detached tumor cells in the peritoneal cavity. We injected immunodeficient female mice with CAOV3 cells treated with DMSO vehicle, the ferroptosis-inducer JKE-1674, bezafibrate, or combined JKE-1674 and bezafibrate. We chose JKE-1674 because it is available as an oral administration and requires a lower dosage (Supplementary Fig.  ). Tumor burden and dissemination within the peritoneal cavity were tracked using IVIS imaging. As expected, cells treated only with JKE-1674 showed similar tumor growth as the control, likely due to the protective factors of the peritoneal cavity and mouse ascites (Supplementary Fig.  ). In contrast, combining JKE-1674 and bezafibrate treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth within the peritoneal cavity (Supplementary Fig.  ). To extend this experiment and assess effects on survival, we injected immunodeficient female mice with a much lower number of cells (1.25 × 105) and monitored peritoneal tumor cell growth for 20 days. As expected, combining JKE-1674 and bezafibrate treatment suppressed tumor growth (Fig.  ) and promoted longer survival (Fig.  ). Taken together, our findings show that using fibrates may mitigate the ferroptosis protection of OVCA cells and PDOs within the peritoneal cavity and reduce the peritoneal growth in OVCA murine models of peritoneal metastasis.

## HMGCS2 and TFRC direct ascites-mediated protection
To elucidate the underlying mechanisms involved in the ferroptosis protection by ascites that were reversed by bezafibrate, we conducted RNA-seq of CAOV3 cells treated with bezafibrate. As expected, bezafibrate treatment upregulated multiple downstream targets of PPARα (Fig.  ). We compiled a list of ascites-regulated genes from RNA-seq results collected from cells exposed to ascites from two different OVCA patients and compared the ascites-affected gene list to bezafibrate-affected genes. Such comparison analysis identified 13 genes that were downregulated by ascites and reversed by bezafibrate (Fig.  ). To prioritize candidate genes, we conducted a focused screening and found that ferroptosis was robustly protected by the knockdowns of 3-Hydroxy-3-Methylglutaryl-CoA Synthase 2 (HMGCS2) and the transferrin receptor gene (TFRC) (Supplementary Fig.  ). Although the knockdowns of fatty acid desaturase 1 (FADS1) and squalene epoxidase (SQLE) also conferred ferroptosis resistance, they significantly impaired baseline cell viability (Supplementary Fig.  ). Based on these results, HMGCS2 and TFRC were selected for further validation (Supplementary Fig.  ). In addition, we observed that ascites exposure did not affect the expression of canonical ferroptosis regulators, such as GPX4, FSP1, and ACSL4 (Supplementary Fig.  ). This emphasizes the importance of HMGCS2 and TFRC for further validating the ascites protection mechanism.
 HMGCS2 encodes the mitochondrial, rate-limiting ketogenesis enzyme and is a canonical target of the PPARα . We found that ascites markedly suppressed HMGCS2 expression, an effect that was reversed by bezafibrate treatment (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Furthermore, CRISPR-mediated removal of HMGCS2 also robustly protected against ferroptosis induced by erastin and RSL3 (Fig.  ), HMGCS2 knockout was validated at the protein level (Supplementary Fig.  ), and ferroptosis sensitivity was restored by the restored expression of an sgRNA-resistant mouse hmgcs2 (Fig.  ), confirming the specificity of the effect. Moreover, erastin-induced lipid peroxidation was significantly reduced in the HMGCS2KO CAOV3 when compared with control CAOV3 (Fig.  ). In contrast, overexpression of HMGCS2 enhanced lipid peroxidation levels when compared with empty vector (EV) control in CAOV3 cells (Supplementary Fig.  ).
These observations led us to hypothesize that changes in HMGCS2 levels enable the ascites protection against ferroptosis and that this effect can be reversed by bezafibrate. Previous studies have shown that dysregulation of HMGCS2 influences lipid accumulation and lipid droplet content , . Consistent with this, the overexpression of HMGCS2 in CAOV3 cells mitigated the ascites-induced increase in lipid droplets similar to bezafibrate treatment (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ).
We next sought to determine how HMGCS2 modulates lipid droplet content. Given the function of this enzyme in the ketogenesis pathway , we initially examined whether ascites exposure or HMGCS2 expression altered the levels of 3-hydroxybutyric acid (BOH) and acetoacetic acid (AcAc). However, we were unable to detect changes in ketone levels. HMGCS2 is also recognized for its regulation of fatty acid oxidation (FAO) – , with prior studies showing that its knockdown abolishes FAO . FAO involves the breakdown of fatty acids into acetyl-CoA, leading to cellular energy production and reduced lipid droplet content . To test whether FAO influences lipid droplet accumulation and ferroptosis sensitivity, we examined the effects of baicalin, a known activator of FAO through carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 (CPT1) , the first rate-limiting enzyme of FAO . Baicalin treatment significantly reduced ascites-mediated ferroptosis protection (Fig.  ) and mitigated the ascites-mediated increase in lipid droplets (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Conversely, inhibiting CPT1 with malonyl CoA lithium protected against ferroptosis (Fig.  ) and increased lipid droplet content (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ), indicating that fatty acid oxidation is a critical factor influencing lipid droplet content and ferroptosis susceptibility.
Collectively, these results suggest that ascites-mediated protection against ferroptosis is driven by decreased HMGCS2, leading to reduced fatty acid oxidation, increased lipid droplet accumulation, and enhanced resistance to ferroptosis. Analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) pathology data reveals that low HMGCS2 expression correlates with poor patient survival, not only in ovarian and liver cancer but across all cancers (Fig.  ). In addition, knockdown of HMGCS2 protected OVCA patient-derived tumor cells against ferroptosis (Supplementary Fig.  ), and tumors harvested from immunodeficient female mice injected with ascites exhibited lower HMGCS2 levels compared with the PBS group (Supplementary Fig.  ). These findings align with the hypothesis that HMGCS2 downregulation contributes to ferroptosis resistance, tumor progression, and poor clinical outcomes.
 

## Oleic acid TFRC regulation influences ascites-mediated protection
Another gene downregulated by ascites and reversed by bezafibrate was TFRC, encoding transferrin receptor protein 1 (TfR1) that mediates cellular iron uptake via transferrin . Previous literature shows that TFRC is upregulated during ferroptosis , and its expression is associated with sensitivity to ferroptosis-inducing agents , . Consistently, we observed that knockdown of TFRC robustly protected against ferroptosis (Supplementary Fig.  ) and confirmed that ascites significantly decreased TFRC mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig.  ). Importantly, ascites significantly reduced TFRC membrane expression and attenuated the RSL3-induced increase in labile iron, effects that were reversed by bezafibrate treatment (Fig.  ). Ascites exposure also impaired transferrin uptake, consistent with the functional effects of reduced TFRC expression (Fig.  ). These results suggest that ascites protects against ferroptosis not only by altering lipid metabolism but also by inhibiting the functional expression of TFRC, a critical mediator of ferroptosis, an effect that can be counteracted by bezafibrate.
We next investigated how ascites regulates TFRC expression. Previous literature indicates that cis-MUFAs, namely oleic acid, can regulate TFRC expression . Our structural lipidomic analysis showed oleic acid as the predominant fatty acid in ascites-mediated cellular TG increases (Supplementary Fig.  ), indicating a predominant presence of oleic acid in ascites and ascites-treated cells. To validate this, we conducted a free fatty acid analysis of eight OVCA ascites samples and found that oleic acid was indeed the most abundant free fatty acid across all eight OVCA ascites in comparison to eight LC ascites samples (Fig.  ). Since we also observed a considerable amount of the PUFA linoleic acid, we added both oleic acid and linoleic acid together in a ratio equal to what was found in OVCA ascites (1.5:1.0). This combination similarly protected cells against ferroptosis (Fig.  ). Furthermore, bezafibrate reversed the oleic acid-mediated protection, mirroring the effects of ascites (Fig.  ). We also confirmed that oleic acid mimicked ascites’ effects on transferrin uptake, TFRC membrane expression, and labile iron levels, with these changes reversed by bezafibrate treatment (Fig.  ). Concurrently, these data suggest that, in addition to regulating HMGCS2 and intracellular lipids, the decrease in iron via TFRC regulation may contribute to ferroptosis protection by further preventing increased labile iron, which propagates lipid peroxidation.

## Discussion
Our findings identify ascites as a key determinant of ferroptosis resistance in metastatic OVCA and highlight its role in promoting tumor survival and dissemination within the peritoneal cavity. Ascites from OVCA patients robustly protects tumor cells and organoids from ferroptosis, an effect reversed by the PPARα agonist bezafibrate. Given the intrinsic vulnerability of metastatic cancer cells to ferroptosis , these data suggest that ascites-mediated protection represents a critical mechanism supporting peritoneal survival. Importantly, re-sensitization to ferroptosis by bezafibrate raises the possibility that therapeutic targeting of this pathway may limit peritoneal dissemination.
Transcriptomic analyses further indicate that ascites-driven ferroptosis resistance is mediated in part through regulation of HMGCS2 and TFRC. The role of TFRC in ferroptosis is well established through its function in iron import, which drives lipid peroxidation – . Although TFRC’s involvement in ferroptosis is consistent with prior studies, its regulation by ascites and potential contribution to peritoneal metastasis represent previously uncharacterized aspects of this pathway. By contrast, the regulatory role of HMGCS2 in ferroptosis reveals a connection that has not been previously described. Previous studies associate HMGCS2 overexpression with reduced lipid droplet abundance , and increased oxidative stress and cell death , yet HMGCS2 has not previously been linked to ferroptosis sensitivity. Our patient-derived cell models and in vivo ascites-conditioned tumors support a role for HMGCS2 suppression in promoting ferroptosis resistance during peritoneal tumor growth. However, we have not yet directly tested HMGCS2 gain-of-function in vivo; future studies will be needed to determine whether restoring HMGCS2 expression is sufficient to reverse ascites-mediated ferroptosis resistance. Together, these findings define a mechanism by which the ascitic microenvironment modulates ferroptosis susceptibility and support a model in which metabolic remodeling promotes metastatic progression.
Interestingly, prior work has implicated PPARα activation as protective against ferroptosis, largely through lipid metabolic pathways and fatty acid-dependent signaling . However, PPARα can be activated by both ferroptosis-protective MUFAs and ferroptosis-promoting PUFAs , , and oleic acid has been reported to bind but not activate PPARα , underscoring the complexity of its regulation. Within our experimental framework, PPARα activation only resensitized cells to ferroptosis in the context of ascites exposure, where it reversed ascites-induced lipid remodeling. Neither bezafibrate treatment alone nor PPARα activation in the absence of ascites was sufficient to promote ferroptosis, and bezafibrate-treated cells did not show reduced tumor growth in vivo. These observations emphasize that PPARα manipulation exerts context-dependent effects on ferroptosis and underscore the importance of the lipid microenvironment in shaping ferroptotic sensitivity.
In addition to triglyceride accumulation, our data indicate that cholesterol metabolism may also contribute to ferroptosis resistance in ascites-exposed OVCA cells. Cholesterol and its biosynthetic intermediates, including 7-dehydrocholesterol, have been implicated in ferroptosis suppression in multiple studies through their ability to limit membrane lipid peroxidation and act as radical-trapping antioxidants , , – . Consistent with this, we observed transcriptional alterations in cholesterol-associated genes and enrichment of cholesteryl esters in OVCA ascites, supporting a role for sterol metabolism in the ascites response.
Although cholesterol was not directly manipulated in this study, our findings are consistent with a model in which cholesterol delivery via HDL and sequestration into lipid droplets act in parallel with triglyceride storage to suppress peroxidative stress and promote ferroptosis resistance. We did not focus on cholesterol as an individual species because ascites simultaneously alters multiple lipid classes, including triglycerides, fatty acids, and cholesteryl esters, making it difficult to attribute ferroptosis resistance to any single lipid component. Instead, our data support lipid droplet accumulation as an integrative readout of ascites-driven lipid remodeling and a unifying mechanism by which diverse lipid inputs converge to promote ferroptosis resistance.
Our study carries important therapeutic implications. Ascites is present in over 90% of patients with metastatic OVCA , , yet its role in regulating ferroptosis has not previously been examined, despite ferroptosis representing a key vulnerability of metastatic cells , . The identification of intracellular lipid remodeling as a determinant of ferroptosis resistance and the potential to reverse this phenotype using fibrates – a widely prescribed drug class – offers a promising avenue for translational investigation. These findings may also extend beyond ovarian cancer to other metastatic settings, including peritoneal colorectal cancer and pleural, brain, or spinal metastases. More broadly, our work highlights the route of tumor dissemination as a previously underappreciated determinant of metastatic behavior and therapeutic response.

## Ovarian patient-derived cell line establishment, maintenance, and treatment

Cells received via Yale University were cultured with RPMI-1640 (Gibco, 11875093) that was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Cytiva, SH30071.03HI-LSF), 1% streptomycin-penicillin (Gibco, 15140122), and 0.3% amphotericin B (Gibco, 15290018). All cells were trypsinized using trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, 25200072). The pathology information of each tumor cell sample is provided in Supplementary Data  .

## Ovarian patient-derived organoid establishment, maintenance, and treatment
OVCA tissue samples were collected at the Duke University Hospital through the Duke BioRepository and Precision Pathology Center (BRPC), which is part of the National Cancer Institute’s Cooperative Human Tissue Network. Samples were collected with written informed consent under a Duke Institutional Review Board approved protocol (Pro000089222). Tissue samples were cut into pieces ~2 mm3 with a sterile scalpel and enzymatically digested in 5 mL tubes with 4.7 mL DMEM F12 media (Gibco, 11320033) and manufacturer-recommended amounts of H, R, and A enzymes in the Tumor Dissociation Kit, human (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-095-929). Samples were digested for 60 min at 37 °C in the Roto-Therm Plus (Ward’s Science). Cells and tissue fragments were then filtered through a 70 µm filter and centrifuged at 500 × g for five min. The supernatant was aspirated, and 1.25 × 105 cells were plated per 50 µL dome of Matrigel (Corning, 356234).
Ovarian patient-derived organoids (PDO) were maintained in ovarian media, which consisted of DMEM F12 media supplemented with the following components: 10 mM HEPES, 1X GlutaMax, 100 U/mL Penicillin/Streptomycin, 10 nM 17-B-Estradiol, 500 nM A83-01, 1X B27 without Vitamin A, 50 ng/mL EGF, 25 ng/mL FGF7, 100 ng/mL FGF10, 10 nM [Leu15]-Gastrin I, 10 ng/mL HGF, 20 ng/mL IGF, 1X N2, 1 mM N-Acetylcysteine, 10 ng/mL Neuregulin I, 10 mM Nicotinamide, 100 ng/mL Noggin, 100 µg/mL Primocin, 10 nM Prostaglandin E2, 100 ng/mL R-Spondin 1, 3 µM SB202190, and 10 µM SB203580 (p38i). All PDOs were maintained in a 37 °C humidified incubator at 5% CO2.
Once the PDOs were confluent, media was aspirated, and 1 mL of PBS was added to each well to detach the Matrigel domes. The solution was centrifuged at 500 × g for five min. 1 mL of TrypLE Express (Gibco, 12604013) was used to dissolve Matrigel and dissociate organoids. These mixtures were incubated for five min at 37 °C, and TrypLE was neutralized by adding 5 mL of DMEM F12 media with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. After centrifuging at 500 × g for five min, supernatants were aspirated. PDO cell suspensions were used to make micro-organospheres (MOS) at a concentration of 50 cells per MOS. MOS were then plated at a concentration of 100 MOS in 50 µL of media per well in a 96-well plate and treated with DMSO control, IKE (50 µM), and 10, 20, and 40% ascites with or without IKE (50 µM). Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Titer-Glo® luminescent cell viability assay kit (Promega, G7570) after 72 h. Samples that were the most responsive to ascites treatment were subsequently made into MOS and treated with the following conditions: DMSO control, 20% ascites, IKE (50 µM), IKE (50 µM) and 20% ascites, bezafibrate (1 mM), bezafibrate (1 mM) and 20% ascites, bezafibrate (1 mM) and IKE (50 µM), and bezafibrate (1 mM) and IKE (50 µM) and 20% ascites. The same experimental design was repeated with ciprofibrate (1 mM) in place of bezafibrate. MOS plates were imaged daily for three days using an ImageXpress Pico (Molecular Devices) cell imaging system, and cell viability was measured as described above after 72 h. The pathology information of each organoid line is provided in Supplementary Data  .

## Mouse studies
CAOV3 cells were transduced with a GFP-luciferase reporter (System Biosciences, pGreenFire1-CMV Positive Control, TR011PA-1), and GFP+ cells were isolated by flow cytometry. For the first set of experiments, 0.5 × 10⁶ or 1.25 × 10⁵ sorted cells were resuspended in 100 µL PBS or human ovarian cancer (OVCA) ascites and injected intraperitoneally (IP) into 10-12 six-week-old female immunodeficient mice (Mus musculus; Taconic Biosciences, CBSCBG-F). Beginning five days after tumor cell injection, 100 µL of cell-free PBS or human OVCA ascites was administered IP every five days for up to 20 days.
For the second set of experiments, sorted CAOV3 cells were transduced with a GPX4 knockout construct and selected with puromycin (1 µg/mL; Sigma, P8833) prior to IP injection into 20 eight-week-old female immunodeficient mice (Mus musculus; Duke DLAR Rodent Breeding Core, NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG)). Cells were counted using a hemocytometer and stained with trypan blue to exclude dead cells, and 1.0 × 10⁵ live cells from each treatment group were resuspended in 100 µL PBS or human ascites before injection. Cell-free PBS or ascites (100 µL) was administered IP every five days for up to 20 days.
For the third set of experiments, sorted CAOV3 cells were treated with bezafibrate (800 µM), JKE-1674 (10 µM), or both for 5 h prior to injection. After exclusion of dead cells by trypan blue staining, 2.0 × 10⁶ or 1.25 × 10⁵ live cells were resuspended in 100 µL PBS and injected IP into 20–28 six-week-old female immunodeficient mice (Taconic Biosciences, CBSCBG-F for high-density injections; Duke DLAR Rodent Breeding Core, NSG for low-density injections). Injections were performed randomly, and mice were subsequently marked on the tail to track treatment groups. Peritoneal tumor growth was monitored by bioluminescent imaging at 7 and 10 days or at 10, 15, and 20 days after injection.
All mice were maintained on a standard normal chow diet (PicoLab® Rodent Diet 20, 5R53; LabDiet) and provided food and water ad libitum. Environmental conditions were maintained in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Mice were housed under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle (lights on from 7:00 AM to 7:00 PM), at an ambient temperature of 21–23 °C and relative humidity maintained between 30–70%. Mice were euthanized either 7 or 10 days after tumor injection or monitored until reaching predefined humane endpoints for survival analyses. Euthanasia was performed by carbon dioxide inhalation, and tumors and ascites were collected on the same day for molecular analyses. Mice were euthanized if they became moribund or met other IACUC-defined criteria indicating pain or distress (including primary tumor size reaching 2000 mm3, >15% weight loss, ruffled fur, visible ascites accumulation, or ascites-associated weight gain >2.5 g), in accordance with approved Duke IACUC protocols (Registry Numbers A118-21-06, A115-22-06, A88-24-04, and A115-22-06-25).

## Bioluminescent imaging
Peritoneal tumor growth was assessed via bioluminescent imaging. 10 minutes before the imaging, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (Covetrus, 029405) and injected IP with D-luciferin potassium (MedChemExpress, HY-12591B) dissolved in PBS (150 mg/kg). The mice were then imaged using an IVIS Kinetic Imaging System (Caliper Life Sciences). The exposure time was set at 1 s. Tumor growth was quantified as the bioluminescence signal (total photon flux) calculated with ‘region of interest’ (ROI) measurement tools in the Living Image software (4.8, Revvity).

## Ascites delipidation, dialysis, and heat inactivation
The ascites delipidation protocol was adapted from a lipid-stripped serum protocol . Briefly, 3 mL of ascites was stirred with 0.6 mL of n-butanol (Sigma, 34867) and 2.4 mL of di-isopropyl ether (Sigma, 296856) for 30 min followed by centrifugation at 4,000 × g at 4 °C for 15 min. The lower phase was separated using a glass Pasteur pipette (VWR) and stirred with 3 mL of di-isopropyl ether for 30 min, followed again by centrifugation at 4,000 × g at 4 °C for 15 min. The lower phase was separated and put in a SpeedVac Concentrator (Savant) and spun at top speed for 1 h. Then, the sample was dialyzed in a 10,000 MWCO Slide-A-LyzerTM Dialysis cassette (ThermoFisher Scientific, 66380) in a beaker containing 2 L saline solution (9 g/L NaCl) for 3 days at 4 °C. A control ascites sample of the same volume was added to the beaker, and the saline solution was changed daily. After the dialysis process, triglyceride (Promega, J3160), cholesterol (ThermoFisher Scientific, A12216), and protein (ThermoFisher Scientific, 23225) levels were measured using a FLUOstar Optima plate reader (BMG Labtech). Protein concentrations of dialyzed and delipidated ascites samples were determined using a bovine serum albumin (BSA) standard curve and normalized to the corresponding unprocessed ascites prior to use in subsequent experiments. To equalize total protein input across conditions, sample volumes were adjusted based on measured protein concentrations. For example, if a dialyzed ascites sample contained half the protein concentration of its matched untreated sample, twice the volume of the dialyzed ascites was used in parallel experiments. For heat inactivation of ascites albumin, samples were incubated at 56 °C for 30 min, followed by incubation at 70 °C for 10 min.

## Cell viability and cytotoxic assays
Cell viability assays were done with the CellTiter-Glo® luminescent cell viability assay (Promega, G7570) following manufacturer’s instructions. 2.5–3.0 × 103 cells were seeded in white 96-well plates (Corning, 3903) for the assay. After treatment, 10 μl of CellTiter-Glo® reagent was added to each well (containing 100 µl of media), and plates were covered and shaken for 2 min and then incubated for 10 min. The resulting luminescent signal was then quantified using a FLUOstar Optima plate reader. The CellTox™ Green cytotoxicity assay (Promega, G8741) was used for the visualization and measurement of cell death at multiple timepoints. The CellTox™ reagent was added to the media in a 1:1,000 dilution, and the fluorescent signal was read using a FLUOstar Optima plate reader.

Tumor tissues harvested from mice were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and homogenized using a mortar and pestle in RIPA buffer (300 µL per 5 mg tissue), followed by mixing as described above. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were diluted in 4× Laemmli sample buffer (Bio-Rad, 1610747) and denatured at 95 °C for 5 min. Equal amounts of protein (15–20 µg) were resolved on 10–12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to 0.45 µm PVDF membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88518) using a semi-dry transfer system. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in 5% BSA. After three washes in 1× TBST (5 min each), membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, 7074 and 7076) for 1-1.5 h at room temperature, followed by three washes in 1× TBST (15 min each). Signals were developed using SuperSignal™ West Pico (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34577) or West Femto (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34096) chemiluminescent substrates and imaged on a ChemiDoc™ Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Primary antibodies used were GPX4 (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology, 52455), CHAC1 (1:1,000; ThermoFisher Scientific, MA5-26311; clone OTI1E2), malondialdehyde (1:1,000; Abcam, ab27642), HMGCS2 (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology, 20940), ACSL4 (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology, 38493; clone F6T3Z), FSP1 (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology, 24972), EpCAM (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology, 2929; clone VU1D9), β-tubulin (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology, 2128; clone 9F3), GAPDH (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology, 97166; clone D4C6R), and PE-conjugated TFRC (1:100; Cell Signaling Technology, 82582; clone D7G9X).

## RNA sequencing and analysis
Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen), and samples (n = 3 per condition) were submitted to the Duke Sequencing and Genomic Technologies (SGT) Facility for RNA sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Data processing and analysis were performed independently by the Duke Analytics Core and Solvuu Inc.

## Duke Analytics Core pipeline
Raw reads were processed using Trim Galore, which employs Cutadapt to remove low-quality bases and Illumina adapter sequences, retaining reads ≥20 nucleotides in length. Reads were aligned to the human reference genome and transcriptome (GRCh38, Ensembl v93) using STAR, and only uniquely mapped reads were retained. Gene-level counts were generated using HTSeq, and genes with fewer than 10 reads in any library were excluded from downstream analysis. Normalization and differential expression analysis were performed using DESeq2 in R, with false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied to control for multiple hypothesis testing. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to identify gene ontology terms and pathways associated with differential gene expression. This pipeline was used for the analyses presented in Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  .

## Solvuu Inc. pipeline
Raw reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.39) and aligned to the human genome and transcriptome (GRCh38, GENCODE v39) using STAR (v2.7.9a). Gene-level counts, transcripts per million (TPM), and fragments per kilobase per million (FPKM) values were generated using RSEM (v1.3.3). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.28.1), and pathway enrichment analysis was conducted using GSEA (v4.2.3) with MSigDB (v7.4). This pipeline was used for the analyses presented in Fig.  . Venn diagram comparison in Fig.  was performed using differential expression results obtained from both analysis pipelines. Raw and processed sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession numbers GSE281415 and GSE281433.

## Lipidomic profiling

## Structural lipidomic
In all, 0.5 × 106 or 1.0 × 106 CAOV3 cells were treated with the following conditions for 0, 2, 8, and 16 h (n = 3) with DMSO control, ascites (10%), or 16 h (n = 5) with DMSO control, erastin (5 µM), ascites (10%), erastin and ascites, bezafibrate (200 µM), bezafibrate and ascites. After treatment, cells were washed 3× with PBS, trypsinized, and resuspended in 200 µL of PBS. A set of T0 samples was also collected before treatment. Cell samples and the corresponding ascites sample used for their treatment were sent to BPGbio for lipidomic profiling. Semiquantitative concentration (nmol lipid/mg protein) of lipids was measured via UHPLC-MS/MS, provided in Supplementary Data  . In brief, lipids were extracted using the Bligh-Dyer method, in the presence of known amounts of isotopically-labeled internal standards (Equisplash Lipidomix, Avanti Polar Lipids). Extracts in chloroform were dried fully under nitrogen gas, reconstituted in 100 µL isopropanol, and stored in an autosampler-compatible 96-well plate at 4 °C until analysis. Nontargeted lipidomic analysis was completed using an Agilent 1290 LC pump equipped with an Acquity Premier CSH C18 column (2.1 mm × 150 mm, 1.7 µM; Waters), coupled to a Thermo Q-Exactive Plus mass spectrometer. The mobile phase consisted of A: 60:40 acetonitrile:water (v/v) and B: 90:10 isoproanol:acetonitrile (v/v), both with 10 mM ammonium formate, 0.1% formic acid and 0.1 µM reserpine (lock mass). The flow rate was 230 µL/min and the column compartment temperature was 55 °C. The gradient elution program was as follows: starting at 32% B, linear increase to 55% B from 0-5 min, followed by linear increase to 65% from 5-8 min, hold at 65% B from 8-10 min, linear increase to 80% B from 10-14 min, linear increase to 90% B from 14-25 min, linear increase to 100% B from 25-25.5 min, hold at 100% B until 27 min, drop to 32% B at 27.1 min and equilibration until the 30 min mark. The samples were analyzed using a polarity-switching full-scan mass spectrometry method at 17,500 resolution with spray voltages +3.5 kV and -2.5 kV in the positive and negative ion modes, respectively, with scan range m/z 120 - 1600. Centroided spectra were automatically lock-mass corrected during acquisition (reserpine [M + H]+ m/z 609.28121 and [M+formate]- m/z 653.27104). Representative pools were used as targets in pseudo top-40 data-dependent tandem mass spectrometry (DDA) experiments with iterative exclusion lists using IE-Omics . Lipids were putatively identified using LipidMatch software using the following search settings: retention time window 0.25 min; precursor ion mass tolerance 0.01 Da; product ion mass tolerance 10ppm; MS/MS intensity threshold 1,000. Peak areas were normalized using appropriate isotopically labeled internal standards (matched by lipid subclass and/or chromatographic retention time) and by protein content measurement using a Pierce™ BCA assay. In total, these analyses comprised 94 biological or technical replicates including control and treated samples, 6 extraction blank injections used for background subtraction, 7 injections of a quality control plasma sample, 42 injections of representative pools used for the generation of peak lists (targets) and lipid annotations based on MS/MS. A study report was generated following recommendations from the Lipidomics Standards Initiative and can be downloaded from https://sftp.bpgbio.com/public/folder/t7lzkto88uoie9wigadyng/NatureComm_AscitesNontargetedLipidomics_RawLCMSData.

## Long-chain free fatty acid lipidomic
Long-chain fatty acids from eight OVCA and eight LC ascites samples were quantified by a custom ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC–MS/MS) at the Duke Proteomics and Metabolomics Core Facility. Ascites samples (20 µL) were thawed on ice and extracted with 60 µL methanol, incubated at −20 °C for 20 min, and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 5 min at 10 °C. Supernatants (40 µL) were combined with 10 µL of an internal standard mixture containing isotopically labeled fatty acids and transferred to glass vials for analysis. Calibration standards and quality control samples were prepared in parallel by serial dilution in 80% methanol and processed identically. Samples were analyzed on a Sciex QTrap 6500+ mass spectrometer coupled to a Waters Acquity I-Class Plus UPLC system using negative electrospray ionization and scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters Acquity BEH column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 µm) using a linear gradient of 0.1% formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (mobile phase B) at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min. The injection volume was 10 µL, the autosampler temperature was maintained at 10 °C, and the column temperature at 45 °C. Ion source parameters included an ion spray voltage of -4.5 kV, source temperature of 500 °C, and curtain and source gases set to 32, 60, and 60 psi, respectively. Quantification of 22 long-chain fatty acids (C12-C24) was performed using calibration curves generated for each analyte in accordance with FDA bioanalytical validation guidelines. Data processing, peak integration, and linear regression (1/x weighting) were performed using Skyline (daily version 22.2.1.278). Final concentrations were reported from validated calibration ranges with internal standard normalization. Skyline output files and calibration range tables are provided in the Source Data file.

## Structural lipidomic analysis
Pairwise differences in lipid abundance between treatment groups were determined using two-tailed Student’s t-tests with calculation of log₂ fold change. P values were adjusted for multiple hypothesis testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method (n = 5, adjusted P < 0.01). For pie chart analyses, based on pairwise comparison groups, significantly increased lipid species were grouped by lipid class, and the total abundance and percentage composition of each class were calculated and visualized using Prism 10 (GraphPad). To calculate semiquantitative concentration changes of lipid species or lipid classes after ascites exposure, semiquantitative values of each significantly increased lipid were subtracted from the corresponding value in untreated control samples. Heat maps were generated using Prism. To determine the total number of unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs) detected in increased triglycerides after ascites exposure, significantly increased triglyceride species (relative to untreated control) were isolated, and individual UFAs were manually counted and summed. For comparison of lipid species between ascites samples and ascites-treated cells, undetected lipids were filtered out and overlapping lipid species were identified between ascites samples and significantly increased lipids (relative to untreated control) using Microsoft Excel. Additional Venn diagram analyses were performed using the same approach by comparing common lipid species between groups.

## Membrane lipid extraction and LC-MS/MS analysis

## Membrane lipid extraction
The protocol was adopted from Bezrukov et al. (2009) . Briefly, Pyrex® glass petri dishes (Corning, CLS3160102BO) were soaked in 1:1 hydrochloric acid:methanol for 30 min at 25 °C, washed five times with sterile water and once with ethanol, and coated with 0.25 µg/mL poly-L-lysine (Sigma, P2636) dissolved in 0.15 M borate buffer (pH 8.3). CAOV3 cells (1.0 × 10⁶) were seeded in DMEM containing 4% FBS and incubated overnight, followed by treatment with 10% ascites for 16 h at 37 °C. Cells were washed once with TBS and incubated with cold sterile water for 10 min to induce hypotonic lysis. Intracellular debris was removed by washing with TBS, and the procedure was repeated twice. The remaining plasma membrane material was extracted with chloroform:methanol (2:1, v/v) for 1 h at 25 °C with gentle shaking. Extracts were collected in glass vials, vortexed, and centrifuged for 15 min at 400 × g, and the lower organic phase was transferred to fresh glass vials for lipidomic analysis.

## LC-MS/MS analysis
Membrane lipid extracts of six samples were dried under nitrogen and resuspended in 50 µL of 90% methanol supplemented with 10 µL of an internal standard mixture containing d17-oleic acid (50 µg/mL) and d4-linoleic acid (1.25 µg/mL). Samples were vortexed for 10 min at 700 rpm (ThermoMixer) and centrifuged for 2 min at 150 × g before transfer to the LC autosampler. Samples were analyzed on a Sciex QTrap 6500+ mass spectrometer coupled to a Waters Acquity I-Class Plus UPLC system. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters Acquity BEH column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 µm) using mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in water) and mobile phase B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile) at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min with a linear gradient (0-2 min, 70% A; 16-18.3 min, 0% A; 18.4-19.9 min, 70% A). The injection volume was 5 µL, the autosampler temperature was maintained at 10 °C, and the column temperature at 45 °C. Mass spectra were acquired in negative electrospray ionization mode with an ion spray voltage of −4.5 kV, a source temperature of 500 °C, and curtain and source gases set to 32, 60, and 60 psi, respectively. Oleic acid and linoleic acid were quantified using scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) with isotopically labeled internal standards. Data acquisition was performed using Analyst 1.7.1, and peak integration and quantification were performed using Skyline (daily version 23.1.0.455). Skyline output files are provided in the Source Data file.

## Statistics
Unless otherwise indicated in the figure legends, n represents the number of biologically independent replicates, which are reported in each figure legend and illustrated by individual data points in each panel. Biologically independent replicates were defined as samples derived from (a) different ascites samples or cells treated with different ascites, (b) different cell models (established cell lines or primary patient-derived cultures), or (c) different animals (mice). In addition, biologically independent replicates also include independently performed experiments using the same biological source (e.g., the same ascites sample or cell model processed in separate experimental runs). Sample sizes were chosen based on previous experience and published literature. Specific statistical tests are described in the corresponding figure legends, and analyses were performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad). Except for box-and-whisker plots, all data are presented as mean ± s.d. For box-and-whisker plots, boxes indicate the 25th and 75th percentiles, center lines indicate the median, and whiskers indicate minimum and maximum values. Statistical significance for experiments containing more than two groups was assessed using repeated-measures one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and experiments containing more than two groups across multiple time points were analyzed using repeated-measures two-way ANOVA. Multiple comparisons were adjusted using either Dunnett’s test or Holm-Šídák’s method, as appropriate for the comparison design. Statistical significance for experiments containing two groups was assessed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests. For pairwise comparisons of RNA-seq data, differential expression was assessed using DESeq2 with Wald tests and false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. For pairwise comparisons of lipidomic data, P values were adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction method. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were analyzed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. All statistical tests were two-tailed where applicable. All other relevant statistical information, including exact P values where possible and replicate numbers, is provided in the Source Data file.
