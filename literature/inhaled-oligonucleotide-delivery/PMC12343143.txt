## Abstract
Neutrophils, the most abundant leukocytes in human blood, play a critical role in the initial response to acute infection and injury. Activated neutrophils exert three primary effector functions: phagocytosis, degranulation of proteolytic enzymes into pericellular spaces, and generation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs). However, dysregulated neutrophil function can lead to tissue damage and inflammation, resulting in organ dysfunction that ultimately contributes to the progression of various diseases. Given the implication of neutrophils in the pathogenesis of diseases arising from chronic inflammation, exploring emerging therapies targeting these cells is critical for developing more effective treatment options. This review highlights nanotechnology‐based therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating neutrophil activity and NET formation, with a focus on nanoparticles (NPs) and hydrogels. NPs‐based delivery systems can regulate excessive neutrophil activity through targeted delivery of anti‐inflammatory drugs, alteration of gene expression, induction of cell death, or inhibition of neutrophil recruitment. Additionally, various nanotechnology‐based therapeutics can inhibit NET formation or degrade NETs following neutrophil activation. NPs can also be internalized by neutrophils and utilized as carriers, facilitating localized therapeutic delivery as neutrophils are recruited to inflammatory sites. The importance of targeting or harnessing neutrophils are explained and we discuss therapeutic strategies to control their activity, which may aid in designing future treatments for neutrophil‐mediated inflammatory diseases.
Keywords: inflammation, nanoparticles, nanotechnology, neutrophil extracellular traps, neutrophils

This review highlights recent advances in nanoparticle‐based strategies to modulate neutrophil activity in inflammatory diseases. By targeting inflammatory neutrophils, NET formation, and neutrophil apoptosis or recruitment, these approaches aim to improve therapeutic precision. These strategies are designed to selectively modulate neutrophil dysregulation associated with disease while preserving their immune functions, either by directly targeting neutrophils or by employing them as drug delivery vehicles at disease sites.

## Introduction
Neutrophils are the primary mediators of the innate host defense against invading pathogens. As first responders, they rapidly recognize inflammation or infection and are recruited to sites of inflammation.[
 
] Upon reaching these sites, neutrophils execute various effector functions to eliminate pathogens.[
 
] Neutrophils are highly specialized immune cells that exhibit unique structural and functional characteristics, which enable them to play a critical role in the innate immune response.[
 
] With their multi‐nuclei and numerous cytoplasmic granules, neutrophils are equipped with a range of antimicrobial mechanisms, including phagocytosis, degranulation, and the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs).[
 , 
]

Neutrophils are among the first immune cells recruited to sites of infection or tissue injury. This process is initiated by the production of chemokines and cytokines, which guide neutrophils along a chemoattractant gradient.[
 
] The recruitment process involves a cascade of interactions between neutrophils and the activated endothelium, including tethering, rolling, adhesion, and transmigration through the vascular wall, leading to the release of additional inflammatory mediators that amplify the immune response.[
 
]

Despite their importance in antimicrobial defense, dysregulated neutrophil activation can result in chronic inflammation and tissue damage.[
 
] Excessive NET formation and prolonged neutrophil survival contribute to the development of persistent inflammatory conditions and autoimmune diseases.[
 , 
] These pathological processes are often fueled by the sustained release of pro‐inflammatory mediators, which amplify tissue damage and perpetuate the inflammatory response.[
 , 
] Furthermore, the failure to maintain neutrophil homeostasis can further exacerbate local inflammation and hinder tissue repair.[
 
] Given the central contribution of neutrophils to dysregulated inflammation, targeting neutrophils represents a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of various human diseases.
Targeting neutrophils requires a detailed understanding of neutrophil biology and the development of therapeutic strategies that can precisely modulate their activity. Recently, nanotechnology has emerged as a promising field for developing therapeutics to combat infectious and inflammatory diseases.[
 
] Nanotechnology offers significant advantages, including enhanced drug stability, precise delivery, and the ability to modulate specific cellular processes. Leveraging these properties, researchers have developed neutrophil‐directed therapeutic approaches aimed at regulating cell recruitment, activation, and apoptosis.[
 
] Such advancements provide innovative strategies to mitigate excessive neutrophil activation while preserving their essential roles in host defense.
In this review, we provide a concise overview of neutrophil properties and functional roles that facilitate the development of neutrophil‐based nanotechnology therapies. It provides an overview of neutrophil properties and their roles in immunity, with a focus on their implications for nanotechnology‐based therapies. We summarize the key characteristics of nanotechnology, including nanoparticles (NPs) and hydrogels, which can serve as drug delivery systems (DDS) targeting neutrophils and NETs. Furthermore, we discuss various therapeutic strategies for NPs targeting neutrophils, such as delivering anti‐inflammatory agents, inhibiting neutrophil recruitment, inducing neutrophil apoptosis, and modulating gene expression. Additionally, we provide an overview of NPs and hydrogel‐based delivery systems designed to degrade excessive levels of NETs. Moreover, leveraging the intrinsic properties of neutrophils, nanotechnology can exploit the inflammation‐targeting capabilities of these cells. Thus, we also examine neutrophil‐mediated delivery systems aimed at targeting inflammatory sites and overcoming biological barriers.

2.

## Neutrophil Biology
2.1.

## Neutrophil Homeostasis
Neutrophils (also known as polymorphonuclear leukocytes [PMNs]) are the most abundant circulating leukocytes in humans. In circulation, neutrophils have a short lifespan with a half‐life of ≈13–19 h.[
 
] Neutrophil homeostasis is tightly regulated by balancing neutrophil production, margination, and clearance. Neutrophils are continuously produced at rates of 109 cells kg−1 body weight per day under normal physiologic conditions.[
 
] Granulopoiesis, the process of granulocyte production, including neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils, occurs in the bone marrow. Hematopoietic stem cells undergo sequential differentiation, first into multipotent progenitors, then lymphoid‐primed multipotent progenitors, followed by granulocyte‐monocyte progenitors. Granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (G‐CSF) commits granulocyte‐monocyte progenitors to give rise to neutrophils through sequential differentiation,[
 
] with myeloblasts maturing into promyelocytes, myelocytes, metamyelocytes, then finally bands and mature segmented neutrophils.[
 
]

Mature neutrophils continuously egress out of the bone marrow and into circulation under homeostatic conditions, existing in two compartments in the vasculature: the circulating intravascular blood pool and the marginated pools. The marginated pools refer to neutrophils that reside in specific tissues.[
 
] Neutrophils account for ≈50–70% of the total leukocyte count in peripheral blood, representing the most abundant subtype of circulating white blood cells.[
 
] Neutrophils residing within the marginated pools are transiently arrested, adhering to the endothelial lining of blood vessels in the lung, liver, and spleen.[
 , 
] Marginated neutrophils serve to replenish circulating neutrophils, as well as a reservoir that allows for rapid mobilization of neutrophils in response to infection and tissue injury.[
 
] The processes of neutrophil homeostasis, encompassing production, circulation, and mobilization, are closely aligned with the structural characteristics of neutrophils, which are essential for their roles in host defense and immune responses.

2.2.

## Neutrophil Structure
Neutrophils have multi‐lobed nuclei and express various cell surface receptors specialized in recognizing pathogens and tissue injury. Circulating neutrophils can be mobilized to sites of infection and inflammation along chemoattractant gradients.[
 
] Neutrophils also produce and contain numerous cytoplasmic granules, which are essential for their role in innate immunity.[
 , , 
] These granules have phospholipid bilayer membranes and contain antimicrobial and inflammatory substances that can fight pathogens through direct killing, recruitment of other immune cells, and facilitation of immune cell interactions.[
 , , , 
] Chemokine and cytokine receptors in cell surface membranes detect inflammatory cues, guiding neutrophils from vascular beds to local tissues, while specific granules harbor the antimicrobial proteins required for pathogen killing.[
 , 
] Neutrophils contain at least four types of granules: primary or azurophilic granules; secondary or specific granules; tertiary or gelatinase granules; and secretory granules. Primary granules contain potent antimicrobial proteins and reactive oxygen species (ROS)‐producing enzymes, including myeloperoxidase (MPO), neutrophil elastase (NE), proteinase 3, and cathepsin G.[
 , , , 
] Secondary granules contain lactoferrin, while tertiary granules contain matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP‐9), both of which facilitate neutrophil migration and promote an antimicrobial environment.[
 , 
] Secretory granules, similar to other granule types, can rapidly transport their transmembrane proteins to the cell surface and are necessary for neutrophil recruitment, extravasation, and adhesion.[
 , , 
] A detailed discussion of the microbicidal mechanisms of these granules is provided in Section  , “Normal Neutrophil Function”. The structural features of neutrophils, including their granules and surface receptors, are essential not only for executing antimicrobial functions but also for enabling their recruitment and activation during immune responses.

2.3.

## Neutrophil Recruitment and Activation
Neutrophils are the first leukocytes recruited to sites of infection or tissue injury.[
 
] This process requires the production of chemokines and cytokines to attract circulating neutrophils, a transient phenotypic change from non‐adherent to adherent, and tethering and rolling along the endothelium to begin the extravasation cascade. When tissue‐resident sentinel leukocytes, commonly macrophages, dendritic cells,[
 , , 
] and endothelial cells interact with pathogens, these cells produce interleukin‐8 (IL‐8) and other chemokines and cytokines to recruit neutrophils[
 , 
] and activate the endothelium. Endothelial activation leads to upregulation of P‐selectin and E‐selectin, which bind to neutrophil P‐Selectin Glycoprotein Ligand‐1 (PSGL‐1) and other glycoproteins, enabling non‐adherent circulating neutrophils to tether to the endothelium[
 
] and engage in slow rolling. The rolling motion of neutrophils promotes their contact with the endothelium, enabling firm adhesion through binding of neutrophil β2 integrins (CD11a, CD11b, CD11c/ CD18) to Intercellular Adhesion Molecule‐1 (ICAM‐1).[
 , , , 
] Firm adhesion to the inflamed endothelium leads to crawling and transmigration from the vascular lumen into sites of tissue injury or infection.[
 , , 
]

Transmigration through venular walls, comprised of endothelial cells, pericytes, and the venular basement membrane,[
 , 
] is a critical step in neutrophil extravasation. To penetrate the endothelium, neutrophils migrate via transcellular or paracellular pathways regulated by various signaling molecules, including β2 integrins and ICAM‐1.[
 , , 
] After crossing the endothelium, neutrophils degrade the basement membrane with granule‐derived enzymes, including NE and MMP‐9, and then migrate through gaps between adjacent pericytes.[
 , , 
] As neutrophils migrate to sites of infection and inflammation, they activate effector functions through pattern‐recognition receptors.[
 , , 
] Neutrophils then release numerous chemokines and cytokines into the extracellular space[
 , , 
] to recruit other effector immune cells and orchestrate immune responses, thereby creating amplification loops to enhance host defense.[
 
] Once recruited and activated, neutrophils execute their functional roles in pathogen clearance and the regulation of immune responses.

2.4.

## Normal Neutrophil Function
Neutrophils have three primary effector functions: phagocytosis, degranulation, and formation of NETs.[
 , , 
] Phagocytosis, the primary antimicrobial function of neutrophils, is initiated by the engulfment of pathogens through an activated transmembrane Fcγ receptor‐mediated process. The plasma membrane of neutrophils forms a membrane‐bound vacuole, called a phagosome, through cytoskeletal rearrangement. The early phagosome lacks antimicrobial properties and must undergo a series of maturation steps to degrade internalized microorganisms,[
 , 
] including fusion with granules to enable destruction of ingested pathogens.[
 , , 
] Intraphagosomal killing of microorganisms results from both the respiratory burst and the activity of proteolytic enzymes. The respiratory burst is initiated upon neutrophil stimulation, with superoxide produced through activation of the NADPH oxidase complex, NOX2, and while other toxic ROS are produced through the action of MPO.[
 , 
]

Another microbicidal mechanism of neutrophils is degranulation, involving the release of granules, a specialized neutrophil structure discussed earlier, which contain ROS and proteins with antimicrobial properties.[
 , 
] Upon activation, granules are mobilized and fuse with the plasma membrane to release their contents.[
 , 
] The released granule contents induce pathogen killing, recruit other immune cells, and promote inflammation to bolster the immune defense.[
 
]

Neutrophils can also exert another extracellular defense by undergoing a form of cell death in which decondensed chromatin[
 
] and enzymes typically contained within granules[
 , 
] are expelled extracellularly to form net‐like structures called NETs. The process by which this occurs is termed NETosis, and resulting NETs trap and kill extracellular pathogens.[
 , , , 
] NETosis can be induced by various stimuli, including oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐8, or components of microorganisms, like lipopolysaccharide (LPS).[
 
]

The key effector functions of neutrophils, including phagocytosis, degranulation, and NETosis, play critical roles in combating infections and regulating immune responses. However, when these tightly regulated processes become dysregulated, they can result in significant pathological consequences.

3.

## Nanotechnology and Neutrophil
3.1.

## Dysregulated Neutrophil Phenotype and Function Leads to Chronic Inflammation
After completion of the acute inflammatory phase, neutrophils undergo constitutive or spontaneous apoptosis and are cleared by efferocytosis to contribute to normal resolution of infection and inflammation.[
 , , 
] While neutrophils are crucial for the initial response to infection and tissue injury, their dysregulation and excessive activation lead to the development of chronic inflammatory conditions.[
 , 
] Inflammatory mediators present in tissues and circulation, along with hypoxic conditions, may prolong neutrophil survival.[
 , 
] Numerous cytokines, such as IL‐1β, TNFα, GM‐CSF, G‐CSF, and IFNγ, inhibit neutrophil apoptosis by preventing the engagement of death receptors.[
 , , 
] This prolonged neutrophil survival leads to persistent effector functions, such as degranulation, the release of ROS, and hyperactivity, ultimately contributing to tissue damage and persistent inflammation,[
 , , 
] leading to the development of numerous inflammatory disorders and autoimmune diseases.[
 , , 
]

In chronic inflammation, high levels of pro‐inflammatory chemokines and cytokines (such as IL‐1, TNF‐α, and IL‐8) lead to the recruitment of neutrophils and other immune cells.[
 
] Furthermore, neutrophils can either directly interact with other effector cells or recruit and modulate the activity of other effector cells through the production of cytokines and chemokines.[
 , 
] The latter results in the recruitment and activation of innate immune cells, such as dendritic cells and macrophages, as well as adaptive immune cells, including T and B lymphocytes, to inflammatory sites.[
 , , , 
] A key requirement for resolving inflammation is the decline in the number of immune cells recruited to inflammatory sites.[
 
] Persistent neutrophil‐driven activation of other immune cells can exacerbate tissue damage and promote sustained inflammatory environments.[
 
]

ROS production and exposure to NET components (granular enzymes and extracellular DNA) can also induce epithelial and endothelial damage and degrade extracellular matrix, exacerbating tissue injury. Furthermore, NETosis can result in increased autoantigen exposure,[
 , 
] which can lead to the production of autoantibodies that underly the development of autoimmune diseases. Indeed, excessive NET formation has been observed to contribute to the pathogenesis of chronic inflammatory diseases,[
 , , , , , , 
] including atopy, autoimmune diseases, chronic respiratory diseases, and metabolic syndrome.[
 , , , 
]

3.2.

## Necessity to Target Dysregulated Neutrophil Function
Dysregulated neutrophil function significantly contributes to the development of various inflammatory disorders.[
 
] For instance, conditions such as sepsis, diabetes, acute respiratory distress syndrome, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cystic fibrosis, tissue damage, and autoimmune diseases are often associated with neutrophil dysfunction.[
 , , , , 
] Additionally, excessive NETosis driven by dysregulated neutrophils has been implicated in the progression of respiratory‐related diseasesas well as tumor growth and metastasis.[
 , 
] Consequently, neutrophils have become critical therapeutic targets for addressing inflammatory disorders, enhancing tissue repair, and combating cancer.[
 , 
] There is a growing demand for strategies to modulate neutrophil function and restore immune balance using targeted DDS.[
 
] Precise control of DDS is crucial to regulate neutrophil activity while preserving their essential roles in immune defense. In this context, NPs represent a promising approach for neutrophil‐targeting DDS, as they can be readily engineered to incorporate pharmaceutical agents, biological drugs, or adjuvants.[
 
]

3.3.

## Interaction Between NPs and Neutrophils
As discussed earlier, excessive neutrophil effector functions can result in host tissue damage. Consequently, significant efforts have been made to develop strategies targeting neutrophils to modulate their phenotypes and functions.[
 , 
] Given their specialization as phagocytic cells, NPs have been investigated as effective targeting materials due to their ability to be readily engulfed and retained by neutrophils. Notably, since neutrophils play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases by interacting with inflammatory and autoreactive cells, considerable research has focused on NPs‐based DDS as a therapeutic strategy for autoimmune diseases.[
 , 
] Various types of NPs, including polymeric, metallic, lipid‐based, and silica NPs, can be internalized by neutrophils via endocytosis.[
 
]

Several parameters of NPs, such as size, surface modifications, and material composition, influence their cellular uptake by neutrophils. Bisso et al. demonstrated that neutrophils preferentially internalize larger NPs up to 200 nm, with gold NPs (AuNPs), liposomes, polystyrene, and polylactide‐co‐glycolide (PLGA) NPs.[
 
] Additionally, NPs size impacts toxicity; for instance, 10 nm silver NPs (AgNPs) exhibit higher toxicity than 50 nm AgNPs, causing membrane damage.[
 
] Similarly, NPs sized between 10 and 40 nm have been shown to accelerate plasma membrane damage and induce NETosis.[
 , 
]

Surface modification of NPs, such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) coating (PEGylation), is commonly employed to prevent immune clearance. Kelley et al. demonstrated that PEGylation enhances NPs uptake by neutrophils.[
 
] However, the degree of PEGylation must be carefully optimized, as lower PEGylation levels have been associated with increased NET formation.[
 
]

The material composition of NPs also impacts neutrophil function. For example, AuNPs have been shown to be promising local drug carriers due to their optical, photothermal, and anti‐inflammatory properties. Interestingly, it has been observed that naked AuNPs can induce human neutrophil apoptosis through activation of caspase‐4.[
 
] Similarly, internalization of AgNPs can induce an atypical form of neutrophil cell death through activation of caspase‐1 and caspase‐4, induction of ROS production, and NETosis[
 
] (Figure  ). Furthermore, AgNPs have been shown to enhance neutrophil heterogeneity, affecting specific subsets of neutrophils.[
 
] On the other hand, AgNPs impair respiratory burst and neutrophil migration, limiting their antimicrobial functions. This highlights the need for further modification of AgNPs to limit antimicrobial properties while promoting the anti‐inflammatory effects of these molecules.[
 
] By carefully considering these parameters, effective strategies can be designed to target neutrophils for specific therapeutic purposes.
 

4.

## Targeting Neutrophils with NPs‐Based DDS
Given neutrophils’ detrimental roles in various human diseases, the development of therapeutic strategies for targeting neutrophils, including dampening neutrophil activation, inducing apoptosis, and promoting clearance, is crucial in controlling the resolution of inflammation.[
 , , 
] Since neutrophils play an important role in host defense and the resolution of acute inflammation, it is crucial to consider developing therapeutics that target the harmful neutrophil subset selectively. Non‐selective targeting of neutrophils could result in severe systemic side effects, including uncontrolled infection.[
 , 
] Furthermore, unmodified forms of anti‐inflammatory therapeutics and nucleotide‐based therapies are often unstable, leading to very short half‐lives in circulation and poor delivery to tissues of interest.[
 , 
] Consequently, many efforts have been focused on developing NPs to precisely target neutrophils and promote intracellular drug delivery, which can prolong drug stability and enhance therapeutic efficacy to effectively suppress inflammatory responses and restore immune homeostasis.[
 , , , 
] In particular, during acute inflammation, NPs‐based DDS have been designed for both tissue‐specific targeting and systemic inflammatory treatment. By optimizing NPs composition, size, and morphology, these systems can regulate inflammatory responses while maintaining biocompatibility and versatility.[
 
] Furthermore, incorporating various ligands, such as antibodies, peptides, and small molecules, into NPs systems can reduce off‐target effects. Additionally, incorporating stimulus sensitivity, such as pH, redox, and enzyme responsiveness, into NPs systems allows for more regulated drug release.[
 , 
] Several strategies employed to modulate neutrophils include directing anti‐inflammatory therapies specifically to neutrophils, inhibiting neutrophil‐endothelial interactions, inducing neutrophil apoptosis, and altering neutrophil gene expression (Scheme  and Table  ).
 4.1.

## NPs Targeting Neutrophils with Anti‐Inflammatory Therapies
Anti‐inflammatory medications have been used to manage inflammatory conditions, the most common of which include glucocorticoids and non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), which generally reduce pain, fever, and inflammation. While these medications are effective at globally reducing inflammation, high doses and/or chronic use can be associated with unwanted side effects. For example, NSAIDs such as ibuprofen, cause various undesired on‐target effects, including thinning of the gastric lining leading to gastritis, ulcers and risk of gastrointestinal bleeding; reducing renal flow; increasing risk for acute and chronic kidney disease; cardiovascular disease resulting from altered prostaglandin‐dependent vascular function; and impaired platelet function leading to increased risk of bleeding.[
 
]

Using NP technologies, anti‐inflammatory medications can be efficiently delivered to neutrophils to inhibit their activation. One example of such a strategy has been employed for the treatment of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), a heterogeneous group of lung diseases characterized by persistent small airway inflammation, leading to progressive airflow obstruction. Neutrophilic inflammation is a prominent feature of COPD, and their activation is induced by chronic exposure to toxins, leading to release of granular proteins, including NE, proteinase‐3, and cathepsin G. Persistent neutrophil activation promotes COPD progression, facilitating alveolar destruction and tissue damage,[
 
] as well as inducing mucus gland hyperplasia and hypersecretion.[
 , 
] A study showed ibuprofen encapsulated in PEG‐PLGA NPs conjugated with NIMP‐R14, an anti‐neutrophil antibody, was successfully delivered to neutrophils via intranasal administration. The NIMP‐R14 conjugation facilitated selective targeting of neutrophils, enabling efficient delivery of ibuprofen. The encapsulated ibuprofen attenuated NF‐κB‐mediated pro‐inflammatory responses, leading to a significant reduction in lung inflammation and injury in mouse models of obstructive lung diseases (Figure  ).[
 
]

It has also been demonstrated that targeting pro‐inflammatory neutrophils can be an effective strategy to reduce inflammation. One such example of this is the use of MPO‐directed therapy for the management of secondary brain injury following an ischemic event or traumatic brain injury. Secondary brain injury arises in part from chronic neutrophil activation and subsequent MPO production, leading to increased immune cell recruitment and induction of oxidative stress. Neuronal damage, potentiation of neutrophil and microglia (central nervous system‐resident macrophages) activation, blood‐brain barrier dysfunction, and modulation of cytokine/chemokine production are also observed in secondary brain injury.[
 
] In one example, hesperetin, a natural flavanone known for its anti‐inflammatory properties, can effectively accumulate at brain injury sites. By utilizing a ligand with two 5‐hydroxytryptamine (5HT) ends, which are substrates for MPO, coupled with PLGA‐PEG NPs loaded with hesperetin (PLGA‐PEG‐bis‐5HT NPs), the system could effectively target neutrophils producing MPO (Figure  ). This system enabled selective targeting of neutrophils producing MPO, facilitating precise drug delivery to inflammatory sites. Following intravenous administration, PLGA‐PEG‐bis‐5HT NPs successfully identified inflammatory neutrophils via MPO catalysis. The sustained release of hesperetin at the injury site promoted microglial polarization, reduced activation of inflammatory cells, and lowered levels of inflammatory mediators. These effects collectively alleviated inflammation and improved neurological function after traumatic brain injury.[
 
] In another study, NE was chosen as a therapeutic target to address neutrophil activity at inflammatory sites. By decorating the surface of NP with an α1‐antitrypsin‐derived peptide, binding specificity to activated neutrophils was enhanced. Using this approach, Cruz et al. encapsulated hydroxychloroquine into peptide‐functionalized NPs. Following intravenous administration in a deep venous thrombosis model, the NPs successfully targeted thrombo‐inflammatory sites and delivered the therapeutic cargo.[
 
]

4.2.

## Inhibition of Neutrophil Recruitment by Disrupting Endothelial‐Neutrophil Interactions
Dysregulated endothelial activation induces expression of proinflammatory cytokines and adhesion molecules that lead to the recruitment and activation of neutrophils. Prevention of circulating neutrophil adhesion to the endothelium has been proposed as a potential therapeutic strategy to reduce neutrophil recruitment. One such strategy is to disrupt integrin signaling to prevent firm adhesion, resulting in decreased neutrophil infiltration into the inflamed tissues, thereby halting the extravasation cascade. For example, in a model of neuroinflammation, neutrophil migration was reduced by rod‐shaped PLGA NPs loaded with piceatannol, a spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK)‐selective tyrosine kinase inhibitor. As neutrophils selectively phagocytosed NPs with a higher aspect ratio (ARs; length‐to‐width ratio),[
 
] the elongated shape of the NPs facilitated their endocytosis (Figure  ). These NPs resulted in the intracellular release of piceatannol to reduce neutrophil adhesion to endothelial cells efficiently by inhibiting β2 integrin‐mediated neutrophil migration by blocking SYK signaling.[
 
] Similarly, piceatannol encapsulated into albumin NPs was also able to prevent β2 integrin‐mediated neutrophil adhesion to the endothelium mediated by Fcγ receptor‐mediated adhesion, facilitating their release into circulation.[
 
] Interestingly, even larger particles, such as a 1 µm diameter, can be internalized by neutrophils and modulate the surface expression of adhesion molecules. L‐selectin shedding is a hallmark of inflammation, and treatment with microparticles composed of salicylate‐based poly(anhydride‐ester) polymer effectively prevented this shedding. This intervention resulted in a reduction in neutrophil influx and lung injury in an acute lung injury model. While soluble aspirin alone did not affect neutrophil localization within the lungs, the microparticle formulation demonstrated therapeutic efficacy via neutrophil internalization.[
 
]

Another strategy to prevent neutrophil recruitment is to enhance the preferential interaction of NPs with neutrophils. A study showed that drug‐free polymeric NPs made from poly(D,L‐lactide‐co‐glycolide) (PLG) with two molecular weights (low [PLG‐L] and high [PLG‐H]) or poly(D,L‐lactide) (PLA [PDLA]), but similar in size and charge, influenced immune cell internalization and inflammatory responses. Particle association with blood monocytes and/or neutrophils was related to the composition of the NPs, with neutrophils preferentially associating with PLG‐H. PLG‐H particles effectively inhibited neutrophil migration into the inflamed central nervous system (CNS) and alleviated inflammation compared to PLG‐L and PDLA particles. This approach, which efficiently reduces the initial inflammatory response by modulating neutrophil trafficking and phenotype, promoted the alleviation of inflammatory symptoms in the CNS even without the use of therapeutic agents (Figure  ).[
 
]

Beyond neutrophil‐endothelium interactions, the interplay between neutrophil‐platelet aggregates and the endothelium also plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of inflammation. Banka et al. observed that cargo‐free micron‐sized (≈2 µm) polymeric particles exhibited a greater ability to prevent neutrophil‐platelet aggregatation and interaction with inflamed endothelium compared to NPs. In a mouse model of systemic inflammation, treatment with microparticles significantly reduced neutrophil‐mediated platelet adhesion to the mesentery, highlighting their potential as a promising therapeutic strategy for thrombo‐inflammation.[
 
]

4.3.

## Induction of Apoptosis and Macrophage‐Mediated Clearance of Neutrophils
The tight regulation of neutrophil death is essential to maintain homeostasis under physiological conditions. However, perturbation of neutrophil death, such as delayed neutrophil apoptosis and impaired clearance, directly contributes to the pathogenesis of many infectious and inflammatory disease states.[
 
] Thus, the induction of neutrophil death and turnover to limit inflammation and tissue damage has been proposed as a potential therapeutic strategy for patients with inflammatory diseases. Recently developed strategies are aimed at i) inducing neutrophil apoptosis through the activation of apoptotic pathways by cytotoxic agents and ii) promoting macrophage‐dependent neutrophil clearance.
Doxorubicin (DOX), well known as an antineoplastic drug, has also been shown to induce neutrophil apoptosis. Building on this property, Zang et al. developed a pH‐sensitive DOX conjugate with bovine serum albumin (BSA) using a hydrazone bond. In mouse models of sepsis and cerebral ischemia/reperfusion, neutrophil activation resulted in the upregulation of Fcγ receptors, which facilitated the uptake of BSA NPs by neutrophils. Following intravenous administration, DOX‐BSA NPs significantly reduced neutrophil numbers in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid compared to free DOX, as the BSA NPs enabled targeted delivery of DOX to neutrophils. In the acidic microenvironment of neutrophils, the hydrazone bond was cleaved, releasing DOX and inducing neutrophil apoptosis.[
 
]

Venetoclax, a small‐molecule inhibitor of the anti‐apoptotic protein BCL2, has been proven to induce neutrophil apoptosis. However, its bioavailability and therapeutic effect on inflamed lung tissues are hindered by its poor solubility. To overcome this limitation, venetoclax was encapsulated with PEG‐modified poly α‐lipoic acid NPs, resulting in prolonged retention of venetoclax within the inflamed lung tissue. These NPs exhibited high mucosal penetration efficiency, thereby enhancing the drug's accumulation in mucus layers. Compared to free venetoclax, these NPs prolonged the lung tissue residence time and more effectively alleviated acute lung injury by increasing neutrophil apoptosis (Figure  ).[
 
]

 Engineered neutrophil apoptotic bodies (eNABs) were generated by combining natural neutrophil apoptotic body membranes with mesoporous silica NPs loaded with hexyl 5‐aminolevulinate hydrochloride (HAL). These eNABs are designed to enhance efferocytosis, the process by which macrophages and other phagocytes recognize and clear apoptotic cells. By incorporating the natural membrane of neutrophil apoptotic bodies onto the surface of mesoporous silica NPs, eNABs acquire inflammation‐targeting capabilities and enable selective uptake by macrophages. In a rat model of myocardial infarction, eNABs demonstrated efficient accumulation at local inflammatory sites. Additionally, the controlled release of HAL from eNABs reprogrammed macrophages, resulting in tissue repair and regeneration, thereby facilitating inflammation resolution.[
 
] As another strategy for regulating neutrophil apoptosis and macrophage activity, dexamethasone was loaded into natural glycyrrhiza protein NPs (GNPs). These GNPs selectively targeted neutrophils in inflamed lungs and promoted neutrophil apoptosis. The subsequent phagocytosis of GNPs‐loaded apoptotic neutrophils by macrophages induced their polarization into an anti‐inflammatory phenotype. In a mouse model of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), intravenous injection of dexamethasone‐GNPs suppressed neutrophil infiltration and enhanced macrophage efferocytosis, ultimately reducing organ damage.[
 
]

4.4.

## Modulating Inflammation by Altering the Expression of Pro‐Inflammatory Molecules
In recent years, modulation of gene expression by genetic materials has become a powerful tool for the treatment of chronic inflammatory diseases, particularly inherited diseases. These technologies include gene‐editing nucleases, like the CRISPR/Cas9 system, RNA interference, and antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs). More recently, these tools have also shown promise in regulating inflammation.
NE, one of the enzymes contained within primary granules, plays a crucial role in regulating inflammatory response, and inhibition of NE activity has been considered a promising therapeutic target.[
 
] Indeed, encapsulating a CRISPR‐Cas9 vector expressing a guide RNA targeting NE in cationic lipid‐assisted NPs (CLANs) based on PEG‐b‐PLGA (CLANpCas9/gNE) (Figure  ) resulted in efficient internalization of CLANs by neutrophils, leading to efficient knockdown of NE expression in neutrophils. By optimizing the PEG density and surface charge of CLANs, these NPs demonstrated superior targeting efficiency toward neutrophils in liver tissue compared to free genetic materials. This ultimately reduced insulin resistance in a mouse model of a high‐fat diet‐induced type 2 diabetes mellitus.[
 
]

 siRNA and ASOs have also been used to inhibit neutrophil activation. For example, Miettinen et al. identified a specific dodecapeptide lipid that binds to the neutrophil‐specific surface molecule CD177 using a random dodecapeptide library. The peptide was subsequently functionalized onto NPs to enhance binding to neutrophils and enable the targeted delivery of complement C5a receptor (C5aR)‐specific siRNA and ASOs. (Figure  ).[
 
] This strategy enabled the targeted knockdown of the C5aR on neutrophils to disrupt neutrophil‐mediated complement activation and reduce inflammation.[
 
]

## Strategies for Therapeutic Targeting to Inhibit NETosis
Despite the pathogen‐killing activity of NETs, excessive formation of NETs has been identified as a major contributor to autoimmune diseases, chronic inflammation, and tissue damage.[
 
] Consequently, numerous efforts have been made to inhibit NETosis through DNase I treatment, PAD4 inhibition, and prevention of NETosis that depends on ROS production and the inflammasome activation.[
 , 
] However, naive forms of therapeutic agents face challenges in efficient delivery due to their short half‐life and instability. To address this, nanocarriers and hydrogels have been employed to deliver drugs with spatial and temporal control over their release.[
 , , 
] (Scheme  and Table  ).
 5.1.

## NPs‐Based Delivery System for NET Degradation
To degrade excessive NETs, therapeutic agents specifically targeting NET components can be encapsulated within NPs to enhance their therapeutic efficacy and minimize potential side effects. The use of NPs‐based delivery systems improves the stability, bioavailability, and targeting efficiency of these agents, addressing the inherent instability of their native forms. Notably, the size and charge of NPs play a crucial role in determining their interaction with NETs. Raghavan et al. demonstrated that positively charged, small‐sized NPs (≈200 nm) exhibited enhanced interactions with NETs. Moreover, these positively charged NPs demonstrated the ability to adhere to NET structures even under high shear forces.[
 
]

Although targeting NE demonstrated therapeutic efficacy in preclinical rodent models of inflammation, early human clinical studies with NE inhibitors were largely unsuccessful.[
 , 
] Subsequently, evidence has shown that NE is difficult to target, as NE bound to NET‐derived extracellular DNAs[
 , , 
] and NE bound to neutrophil membranes[
 
] are generally resistant to proteolytic degradation, and are inefficiently deactivated by endogenous proteinases. To address this, Okeke et al. generated lipid‐based NPs encapsulating the NE inhibitor sivelestat, which resulted in reduced neutrophil infiltration, lowered inflammatory cytokines levels, and improved overall survival, an effect not observed with administration of unmodified sivelestat (Figure  ).[
 
]

 Similarly, delivering nucleases capable of degrading NET‐derived extracellular DNA is challenging due to their enzymatic destruction of protein‐based therapeutics in the digestive tract and poor bioavailability in vivo.[
 
] Dong et al. encapsulated staphylococcal nuclease in calcium alginate‐based NPs to facilitate the degradation of intestinal NETs as a potential treatment for ulcerative colitis.[
 
] Administration of these NPs to a dextran sodium sulfate‐induced mouse model of colitis showed significant improvements in morbidity due to colitis as determined by reductions in weight loss, colon shortening, and mortality.[
 
] It also significantly reduced proinflammatory cytokine production, intestinal permeability, and inhibited NET formation.[
 
]

Interestingly, in addition to NPs, larger polymeric particles have also been explored as modulators of NETosis. Specifically, micron‐sized (1 µm) particles composed of poly‐salicylic acid have been shown to inhibit NETosis both in vitro and in vivo. In primary neutrophils, phagocytosed microparticles released salicylic acid, leading to NET degradation. Furthermore, in a mouse model of deep vein thrombosis, intravenous injection of these microparticles resulted in a reduction in NETosis‐related protein markers in plasma, demonstrating their potential therapeutic efficacy.[
 
]

5.2.

## Hydrogel‐Based Delivery of Therapeutics
Hydrogels are increasingly utilized as DDS due to their high‐water content, biocompatibility, tunable properties, and ability to achieve controlled and targeted release of encapsulated therapeutic agents.[
 
] They serve as effective platforms for protecting and delivering therapeutic agents aimed at degrading NETs while optimizing their physicochemical properties for enhanced stability and efficacy. Furthermore, hydrogels enable controlled delivery of dual drugs and can be integrated with other DDS strategies to further enhance therapeutic outcomes. For example, inhaled topical administration of DNase I has been shown to reduce sputum viscosity and improve pulmonary function in patients with cystic fibrosis.[
 , 
] However, the poor water solubility of DNase I causes rapid adsorption to the surface, limiting bioavailability.[
 
] Additionally, DNase I exhibits high instability in circulation due to its short half‐life and rapid clearance rate.[
 , , , , 
] These limiting factors present ongoing challenges for its clinical applications. To address this, hydrogel‐based therapeutics have been employed in a variety of clinical settings to improve the delivery and therapeutic efficacy of DNase I. Hosseinnejad et al. conjugated DNase I to highly hydrophilic microgels synthesized from two polymers, N‐(2‐hydroxypropyl) methacrylamide and zwitterionic carboxybetaine methacrylamide. Administration of this microgel increased drug hydrophilicity while conserving protein structural stability, enzymatic activity, and antifouling properties of microgels.[
 
] in vitro microgel‐conjugated DNase I exhibited more rapid and efficient digestion of NETs released from activated human neutrophils treated with phorbol‐12‐myristate‐13‐acetate (PMA) compared to free DNase I (Figure  ).[
 , 
] Similarly, DNase I‐conjugated mesoporous bioactive glass NPs were incorporated into silk methacrylate‐based hydrogels for ocular surface repair. This hydrogel system enabled sustained delivery of DNase I, resulting in long‐term therapeutic effects on the ocular surface, including inhibition of NET‐induced corneal neovascularization and accelerated corneal repair.[
 
] Similar strategies have been employed for other NET‐targeting therapeutics, including a threonine‐aspartic acid‐phenylalanine‐amidine tripeptide, a highly potent peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4) inhibitor,[
 
] and metformin, a glucose‐lowering medication with anti‐inflammatory effects (Figure  ).[
 , 
]

Hydrogels have also been used for local drug delivery. By incorporating stimuli‐responsive properties, hydrogels optimize drug delivery, thereby improving the efficacy of therapeutic agents. For example, temperature‐sensitive hydrogels exist in a liquid state at room temperature but polymerize to form a matrix at body temperature. This feature enables targeted drug delivery to the site of action, where hydrogels selectively sustain drug release and reduce systemic side effects and toxicities.[
 
] Sun et al. employed this strategy, utilizing hydroxyprolyl chitin to generate hydrogels that were temperature‐sensitive, highly biocompatible, exhibited minimal systemic toxicity, and were readily enzymatically degradable. They found that DNase I encapsulated in these hydroxyprolyl chitin hydrogels showed prolonged DNase I release in vitro. Furthermore, administration of this hydrogel‐encapsulated DNase I to a murine laminectomy model of post‐operative inflammation improved in vivo delivery and release of DNase I, thereby significantly reducing NET formation and epidural fibrosis at 4 weeks post‐surgery.[
 
] Thermosensitive hydrogels composed of PLGA‐PEG‐PLGA have been utilized to achieve dual delivery of DNase I and vancomycin, an antibiotic, while preserving the enzymatic activity of DNase I. The dual release of DNase I and vancomycin from the hydrogel effectively alleviated inflammatory responses and eliminated methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) infections.[
 
]

Hydrogels can be further modified to increase enzymatic half‐life as well as delivery of cytotoxic therapeutics. Wang et al. developed a DNase I functionalized hydrogel by conjugating DNase I with activated hyaluronic acid through an oxidizing Schiff base reaction and subsequently cross‐linked the DNase I‐activated hyaluronic acid to a carboxymethyl chitosan‐based hydrogel. They found that this DNase I‐functionalized hydrogel can prolong its half‐life, enhancing nuclease adherence to articular surfaces without affecting the enzyme activity, as confirmed by an in vitro NET assay.[
 
] This hydrogel platform was then utilized to encapsulate methotrexate, a cytotoxic immunosuppressive therapy for rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and[
 
] intra‐articular of injectable hydrogels significantly reduced the expression of inflammatory cytokines and alleviated RA symptoms.[
 
] In another study, Pan et al. developed a semi‐interpenetrating network hydrogel system based on ε‐polylysine and hetastarch for the co‐delivery of DNase I and oridonin to treat oral mucositis. This hydrogel adhered effectively to oral mucosal membranes, prolonging drug activity at the wound site. The controlled release of encapsulated DNase I and oridonin facilitated NET degradation and exhibited antibacterial activity, promoting the healing of oral mucositis.[
 
]

Hydrogels can also be combined with other DDS, such as microneedles, to enhance localized delivery and sustain drug release. Microneedles are physical structures typically fabricated from materials like silicon, metals, or biodegradable polymers specifically designed to puncture the outermost layer of the skin to create micropores that allow for therapeutic delivery into the skin.[
 
] Coating or embedding microneedles within hydrogel matrices allows for the simultaneous puncturing of skin and deposition of a drug‐loaded hydrogel.[
 , 
]

For example, Cao et al. generated a methacrylate‐modified hyaluronic acid‐coated ethylcellulose‐based microneedle loaded with a chemically modified aptamer, DTA6, targeting the chromatin‐remodeling protein DEK (Figure  ),[
 
] which is essential for NET formation.[
 
] Formulation in this manner enhanced aptamer stability and provided drug delivery in a less invasive manner.[
 
] Furthermore, blocking DEK with the DTA6‐loaded hydrogel microneedle significantly reduced foot edema, clinical arthritis scores, and systemic inflammatory markers in a murine model of collagen‐induced arthritis.[
 
]

## Neutrophil‐Mediated Drug Delivery
Although nanotechnology‐based DDS can be utilized to improve therapeutic efficacy, bioavailability, and specific delivery, biological barriers can impede efficient drug delivery to sites of inflammation. The biological properties of neutrophils, particularly their ability to traffic and transmigrate through membranes, make them an attractive candidate for drug carriers.[
 , , 
] Considerable research has focused on designing NPs that can specifically target and be internalized by neutrophils to harness their biological properties.[
 
] This has been achieved through the development of neutrophil‐specific material‐based NPs or by functionalizing NPs surfaces with neutrophil‐targeting ligands to improve internalization efficiency. For instance, Vols et al. identified neutrophil‐specific short peptide sequences that selectively target CD177, a neutrophil surface marker. Conjugation of a CD177‐targeting peptide to PLGA NPs successfully enhanced neutrophil specificity and facilitated inflammation‐targeted trafficking.[
 
] Indeed, neutrophils that have internalized NPs have been demonstrated to overcome these impermeable barriers and transport NPs into inflamed tissues[
 , 
] and sites of pathogen invasion,[
 , , 
] thus providing drug delivery to these protected sites, such blood‐brain barrier (BBB)  (Scheme  and Table  ).[
 , 
]

 6.1.

## Neutrophils as a DDS for the Sites of Inflammation
NPs‐laden neutrophils can serve as effective carriers for targeted delivery of therapeutic agents to sites of inflammation, leveraging the intrinsic properties of neutrophils to home to and accumulate in inflamed tissues.[
 , , , 
] This section provides an overview of various strategies utilized to deliver both anti‐inflammatory and disease‐modifying therapeutics through neutrophil‐mediated drug delivery systems.
BSA is a highly water‐soluble protein that can bind non‐covalently to therapeutics, and is selectively internalized by activated neutrophils, making it a promising strategy for the development of NPs that leverage neutrophils as a cellular delivery system (Figure  ). Indeed, BSA NPs could be loaded with immunomodulatory agents, such as TPCA‐1, the NF‐κB inhibitor, or the broad‐spectrum antibiotic cefoperazone acid, efficiently reducing lung inflammation and bacterial proliferation, respectively.[
 
]

 Neutrophil‐mediated delivery of liposome‐encapsulated therapeutics has also been explored in various chronic inflammatory diseases and infections. For example, to specifically deliver steroids to the affected joints in a rat model of rheumatoid arthritis, liposomes were loaded with the steroid dexamethasone palmitate and decorated with sialic acid, which selectively binds to L‐selectin. Administration of these liposomes resulted in successful delivery of the steroid into affected joints, improvement in joint inflammation, and reduction in systemic inflammation markers.[
 
] As another example, Gao et al. aimed to address multidrug resistance and disrupt biofilm production by the bacterium Pseudomonas aeruginosa by leveraging neutrophils to both deliver combinatorial antibiotic therapy and promote neutrophil‐mediated clearance of infections. While the antibiotic azithromycin can be freely taken up by neutrophils, the antibiotic colistin was packaged into fusogenic liposomes to facilitate neutrophil uptake. Neutrophils laden with azithromycin and colistin in this manner demonstrated effective anti‐bactericidal and anti‐inflammatory activities both in vitro and in vivo (Figure  ).[
 
]

NPs enhance the internalization of therapeutic agents by neutrophils, and neutrophil‐mediated delivery systems have demonstrated exceptional efficacy in targeting inflammatory sites and alleviating inflammation. Beyond this, their unique ability to traverse strict biological barriers, such as the blood‐brain barrier (BBB), extends their therapeutic potential to otherwise inaccessible regions, enabling effective drug delivery to the CNS under inflammatory conditions.

6.2.

## Neutrophils as a DDS to Overcome Biological Barriers
The CNS is an immunologically privileged site, in which the BBB tightly regulates the passage of therapeutic agents and immune cells between the bloodstream and the brain parenchyma to protect the CNS against pathogens and immune‐mediated damage.[
 
] In a steady state, the BBB limits neutrophil trafficking. However, during CNS injury and infection,[
 
] neutrophils can cross the BBB, providing an opportunity to utilize them as a cellular transport system to deliver therapeutic agents into the CNS, an otherwise inaccessible site.
One strategy that has been employed is to load neutrophils with NPs containing therapeutic agents to load neutrophils using receptor‐mediated endocytosis. Neutrophils cross the BBB following infection or injury, where drugs are then released through various mechanisms, including NETosis and exosome transfer. For example, Liu et al. generated liposomes containing the isoflavone puerarin, a neuroprotective substance with anti‐inflammatory and anti‐oxidant properties, and loaded them into neutrophils. Following the induction of brain ischemia‐reperfusion injury in mice, NPs‐laden neutrophils were able to transit across the BBB, where they underwent NETosis to release puerarin to provide its neuroprotective effects.[
 
] Zhang et al. employed a similar method to deliver catalase, a highly unstable ROS scavenger and neuroprotectant, to areas of ischemia/reperfusion injury. In this case, catalase was encapsulated by polymeric NPs based on dendrigraft poly‐L‐lysine studded with PGP, an endogenous tripeptide with high affinity for the neutrophil CXCR2 receptor to promote endocytosis of NPs. Trafficking of neutrophils across the BBB led to exosome‐mediated NPs transfer to neuronal cells and accumulation of catalase in the brain and reduced neuronal apoptosis following brain ischemia/reperfusion injury.[
 
] Building upon this approach, Dang et al. developed PGP‐polyamidoamine dendrimer‐based NPs encapsulating scutellarin, a flavonoid with antioxidant, anti‐apoptotic, and anti‐inflammatory properties, functionalized with angiopep‐2 to enhance BBB transcytosis. This dual‐targeting strategy significantly improved NPs uptake by neutrophils, facilitated scutellarin delivery to ischemic regions, reduced infarct size, and suppressed inflammatory cytokine production (Figure  ).[
 
] In another example, Hou et al. generated liposomes modified with a cyclic peptide RGD (Arg‐Gly‐Asp‐d‐Tyr‐Lys), which is recognized by integrin αvβ1 expressed on monocytes and neutrophils to efficiently trigger internalization of their receptors to deliver encapsulated edaravone, a hydroxyl radical scavenger, to areas of cerebral ischemia.[
 
] Edaravone delivered in this way improved functional motor and cognitive performance in rat models of ischemia/reperfusion injury as assessed by the balance beam test and Morris water maze test.[
 
]

Another strategy for delivering anti‐inflammatory agents to areas of CNS ischemia/reperfusion injury is to use liposomes to facilitate endocytosis by neutrophils and macrophages. For example, negatively‐charged magnetic liposomes coated with RGD and loaded with the NSAID diclofenac facilitated selective endocytosis of liposomes by monocytes and neutrophils, and enhanced delivery of the drug to sites of inflammation in the brain.[
 
]

7.

## Conclusion and Perspectives
Despite neutrophils’ essential roles in regulating both innate and adaptive immunity, they are important players in the pathogenesis of several inflammatory diseases. Neutrophils are recruited to sites of tissue injury and infection to clear pathogens and promote wound healing. However, failure to resolve inflammation promotes chronic inflammation, leading to dysregulated neutrophil function, immune activation, prolonged inflammation, and ultimately tissue damage. Leveraging neutrophil effector functions is emerging as an exciting new therapeutic strategy for treating various diseases associated with chronic inflammation. Advanced NP technology provides a unique opportunity to develop therapeutic drugs to overcome the limitations of free therapeutics, including i) drug stability, ii) bioavailability, iii) solubility, and iv) biological barriers. Furthermore, novel strategies employing NPs in preclinical models have shown that neutrophils can be exploited to treat infection and inflammatory conditions by i) neutrophil‐targeting therapeutics, ii) disrupting neutrophil effector function, such as NETosis, iii) activating neutrophils death or promoting neutrophil clearance, iv) leveraging the ability of neutrophils to migrate to impermeable areas, and v) leveraging neutrophils as a DDS.
Despite the promising advances made in preclinical animal models, there are still many limitations to the translation of NPs‐based neutrophil therapeutics into clinical practice. One of the major challenges is that modulation of neutrophil effector functions must be tightly controlled so that treatment efficacy does not interfere with normal neutrophil function, including clearance of infections, peripheral tolerance, and adaptive immune responses. When designing neutrophils as drug carriers, the timing and extent of neutrophil migration and infiltration are dependent on the disease state and the host's immunity. Thus, delivery and therapeutic efficacy with neutrophil‐based delivery systems need to be assessed. Furthermore, the current knowledge for understanding neutrophil function is limited to specific therapeutic targets and may not be broadly generalizable across disease states.[
 
] As we gain greater insight into the mechanisms underlying neutrophil dysregulation, our ability to identify novel approaches to suppressing neutrophil activation will be greatly expanded. In conclusion, by overcoming these limitations and further understanding neutrophils’ pathologic roles in diseases, NP technologies have the potential to become highly efficient platforms for drug delivery.
