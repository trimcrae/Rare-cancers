## ABSTRACT
Members of the ankyrin repeat domain (ANKRD) family are involved in multiple cellular functions, including cell cycle regulation, cell adhesion, and signaling, and have been implicated in the pathogenesis of various cancers. However, the function of ANKRD62, a recently identified member of this family, remains largely unexplored. This study investigated the clinical significance and biological function of ANKRD62 in uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC). Through gene expression profiling, we identified ANKRD62 upregulation in UCEC tissues compared to paired adjacent normal tissues. Using overexpression and shRNA‐mediated knockdown in cell lines and mouse models, we demonstrated that ANKRD62 is significantly upregulated in UCEC tissues, with expression levels positively correlated with tumor grade and inversely associated with patient survival. In vitro functional assays revealed that ANKRD62 overexpression markedly enhanced UCEC cell proliferation and migration, whereas ANKRD62 knockdown suppressed these malignant phenotypes without significantly affecting apoptosis. Consistent with these findings, in vivo experiments showed that ANKRD62‐overexpressing cells exhibited accelerated tumor growth and increased metastatic potential. Mechanistically, ANKRD62 was found to be associated with NF‐κB pathway modulation, promoting p65 nuclear translocation and phosphorylation, thereby affecting cellular function. In summary, this study provides the first evidence that ANKRD62 acts as a critical oncogenic driver in UCEC progression and unveils its functional association with NF‐κB signaling. These findings highlight the potential of ANKRD62 as a novel therapeutic target and offer new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying UCEC pathogenesis.
Keywords: ANKRD62, cell migration, cell proliferation, NF‐κB, uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma

## Introduction
Uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC) ranks among the most prevalent gynecological malignancies and the sixth most common cancer in women globally [ ]. Despite advancements in diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, patients with advanced‐stage UCEC continue to face poor prognosis and low 5 year survival rates [ ]. This unmet clinical need underscores the urgency to elucidate molecular drivers of UCEC progression and identify novel therapeutic targets.
The ankyrin repeat domain (ANKRD) protein family, characterized by conserved structural motifs mediating protein–protein interactions, has emerged as critical regulators of tumorigenesis [ , ]. ANKRD members modulate diverse oncogenic processes, including cell proliferation, migration, and apoptosis [ , ]. Additionally, ANKRD family proteins may interact with multiple signaling pathways and participate in the regulation of the tumor microenvironment, further impacting tumor progression and drug resistance [ , , , , ]. As a newly identified member of the ANKRD family, ANKRD62 is likely to play an important role in tumor progression, similar to other ANKRD proteins. Previous studies have reported that ANKRD62 is frequently mutated in patients with refractory and recurrent tumors [ , , ], suggesting its potential role as a cancer driver gene. However, the specific functions and molecular mechanisms of ANKRD62 in UCEC have not been reported.
As a family of proteins containing ankyrin repeat domains, ANKRD proteins are known to play significant roles in modulating the NF‐κB signaling pathway [ ]. Several studies have shown that ANKRD family proteins can act as regulators of NF‐κB, influencing its nuclear translocation, DNA binding, and transcriptional activity, thereby affecting NF‐κB‐mediated inflammatory responses, apoptosis, and immune regulation [ , , ]. Notably, ANKRD proteins are increasingly recognized as key modulators of this pathway through diverse mechanisms. For instance, Protein Phosphatase 6 (PP6) holoenzyme directly dephosphorylates and regulates components of the NF‐κB pathway [ ]. Other members, such as ANKRD2, can act as a transcriptional co‐repressor by directly interacting with the NF‐κB subunit p50 to modulate inflammatory responses [ ]. Conversely, ANKRD1 has been shown to promote cancer metastasis by activating NF‐κB signaling [ ], while ANKRD49 can inhibit apoptosis by modulating the same pathway [ ]. This regulatory interplay can even extend to non‐canonical interactions, as demonstrated by the mechanosensitive channel TRPV4, which uses its N‐terminal ANKRD domain to sequester p65 and limit NF‐κB activation [ ]. This growing body of evidence underscores that ANKRD proteins are not merely passive structural components but are active, context‐specific regulators of NF‐κB signaling, with significant implications in cancer biology.
This study addresses these knowledge gaps by systematically evaluating ANKRD62s clinical relevance, biological functions, and molecular mechanisms in UCEC. We demonstrate that ANKRD62 overexpression correlates with aggressive clinicopathological features and poor survival outcomes. Through integrated in vitro and in vivo models, we establish ANKRD62s role in driving UCEC proliferation and metastasis, mechanistically linking these effects to NF‐κB pathway activation. These findings position ANKRD62 as both a prognostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target in UCEC.

## Methods
2.1.

## Human Studies
This research adhered to the ethical guidelines established by the Declaration of Helsinki. All human specimens were obtained and processed under protocols authorized by the Institutional Review Board of Maternal and Child Health Hospital of PanYu. Endometrial carcinoma tissues and paired adjacent non‐tumor samples were acquired from patients undergoing specific procedures, e. g., hysterectomy at Maternal and Child Health Hospital of PanYu District, following approval from its Ethics Committee (Approval No. 22025020702). Prior to enrollment, written informed consent was secured from all participants. Clinicopathological data were extracted from anonymized medical records (Table  ), with stringent measures implemented to safeguard patient confidentiality, including data encryption and restricted access to identifiers.

2.2.

## Animals
Six‐week‐old BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China) and acclimatized for 5 days in a specific‐pathogen‐free (SPF) facility prior to experimentation (n = 5 mice per group). Throughout the study, animals had ad libitum access to autoclaved standard feed and sterile purified water. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a random number table method, with the principal investigator blinded to group allocation. Investigator were also blinded to group allocation during data acquisition and imaging analysis. In vivo imaging and procedures, anesthesia was induced via inhalation of a 2%–3% isoflurane‐oxygen mixture delivered through a precision vaporizer. Deep anesthesia was achieved by increasing isoflurane concentration to 3%–5% for rapid unconsciousness, followed by euthanasia via cervical dislocation. This study was reviewed and approved by the institutional animal care and use committee of Maternal and Child Health Hospital of PanYu (Approval No. IACUC‐2025‐0611), with full compliance to the European Parliament Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes.

2.3.

## Cell Lines, Culture Conditions, and Transfection
These experimental approaches were performed as our previously described [ ]. HEC‐1A, KLE and HEK‐293 T cell lines were acquired from the Wuhan Servicebio Technology CO. LTD. These cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM) or DMEM/F12, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Vazyme, F101‐01). All cells were incubated in a 5% CO2 atmosphere at 37°C. Upon reaching 70%–80% confluence, transfection was performed by introducing 15 μg of plasmid DNA per 10 cm dish. All cell lines used in this study were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling prior to experimentation. Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using a PCR‐based detection method and confirmed to be mycoplasma‐free throughout the study.

2.4.

## Stable Transfection Cell Lines
Stable ANKRD62‐modified cell lines were established following a previously optimized lentiviral transduction protocol [ ]. The experimental workflow comprised the following steps: The pLVX‐Puro vector was engineered to generate two recombinant constructs, one for ANKRD62 overexpression and another for shRNA‐mediated ANKRD62 knockdown. HEK‐293 T cells were transfected with a lentiviral packaging system comprising psPAX2 (Addgene #12260) and pMD2. G (Addgene #12259), combined with either the ANKRD62 overexpression construct or its shRNA counterpart. Viral supernatants were harvested 48 h post‐transfection, concentrated by ultracentrifugation, and used to infect target cells in the presence of polybrene (8 μg/mL, Beyotime ST1380). At 24 h post‐infection, transduced HEC‐1A and KLE cells underwent selection with 300 μg/mL G418 (MedChemExpress, HY‐32772) or 3 μg/mL puromycin (Sigma, P8833) for 10 days to establish stable polyclonal populations. Antibiotic‐resistant colonies were expanded and validated through qPCR and immunoblotting.

2.5.

## Quantitative Real‐Time Polymerase Chain Reaction
Total RNA isolation was performed with RNAiso Plus reagent (Takara Bio, Cat#9108, Japan). RNA purity and integrity were assessed via absorbance measurements at 260/280 nm using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). First‐strand cDNA synthesis was carried out using 4 × EZscript Reverse Transcription Master Mix (EZBioscience, Cat#EZB‐RT2GQ) with integrated gDNA removal, following the manufacturer's thermal cycling parameters. Quantitative PCR amplifications were performed in triplicate using Hieff UNICON Blue SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen Biotech, Cat#11184ES08, China) on a QuantStudio 3 Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Reaction conditions included an initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 60°C for 30 s. Cycle threshold (Ct) values were normalized to β‐actin reference gene expression, with primer sequences provided in Table  .

2.6.

## Immunostaining
The immunofluorescence protocol was adapted from previously published method [ ]. Briefly, tissue sections or cultured cells were subjected to the following steps: samples were treated with ANKRD62 and Ki67 primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer and incubated at room temperature for 1 h, cell nuclei were labeled with DAPI (0.1 μg/mL) for 5 min at ambient temperature, initial fluorescence screening was conducted using a Pascal fluorescence microscope (model M205FA, Germany). High‐resolution images were captured with a Leica TCS SP8 laser‐scanning confocal system (Germany), with optical sections collected at 0.5 μm intervals. Acquired images were processed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

2.7.

## Immunoprecipitation and Western Blotting
The experimental workflow was adapted from established methodologies, with modifications as follows: Samples were homogenized in ice‐cold RIPA buffer (Servicebio, Cat#G2038) supplemented with phosphatase inhibitors (Servicebio, Cat#G2006) at a 1:100 dilution. Protein A/G magnetic beads (Yeasen, Cat#36417ES08) were conjugated with primary antibodies through gentle rotation (25 rpm) for 45 min at 24°C. Post‐conjugation, beads underwent three washes with lysis buffer (centrifugation at 3000 × g, 5 min). Antibody‐bound beads were incubated with clarified lysates under constant rotation (10–12 h, 4°C). Post‐incubation complexes were washed thrice with ice‐cold lysis buffer, followed by two additional washes with TBST buffer. Captured proteins were eluted in 5 × SDS‐PAGE loading buffer and denatured at 95°C for 8 min. Proteins were resolved on discontinuous SDS‐PAGE gels. Separated proteins were electroblotted onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 3% BSA in TBST for 1–2 h, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After three TBST washes (10 min each), HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 2 h at 25°C. Chemiluminescent signals were captured using ECL Prime substrate and quantified via Image Lab software.
The antibodies used in the experiment included: anti‐GAPDH (10494–1‐AP, Proteintech), anti‐Histone H3 (17168–1‐AP, Proteintech), anti‐Beta Actin (66009–1‐Ig, Proteintech), anti‐ANKRD62 Antibody (NBP2‐82624, Novus Biologicals), anti‐Phospho‐NF‐κB p65 (Ser536) Antibody (TA2006, Abmart), anti‐NF‐κB p65 Antibody (TA5006, Abmart), anti‐IκB alpha Antibody (T55026, Abmart).

2.8.

## Bioinformatics Analysis
The Cancer Genome Atlas UCEC (TCGA‐UCEC) data is publicly available from the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) Genome Browser (https://xenabrowser.net). We performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on differences in KEGG pathway enrichment between low ANKRD62 and high ANKRD62 UCEC samples from the TCGA.

2.9.

## Cell Migration, Invasion, Wound Healing and Colony Formation Assay
Transwell assays were conducted using 8 μm pore inserts (Corning, USA). For invasion assays, inserts were pre‐coated with 50 μL Matrigel (1:10 dilution, BD Biosciences). Cells were resuspended in serum‐free DMEM at 1 × 104 cells/mL, with 500 μL aliquots added to upper chambers. Complete medium (DMEM + 10% FBS) served as chemoattractant in lower chambers. After 24 h incubation, migrated/invaded cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), stained with 0.1% crystal violet (30 min), and quantified across five random 20 × fields per insert using an Olympus microscope.
Confluent monolayers of transfected UCEC cells in 6‐well plates were scratched using 200 μL pipette tips. Debris removal was achieved by PBS washing, followed by culture in DMEM containing 1% FBS. Wound closure was monitored at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h intervals under phase‐contrast microscopy. Migration rates were calculated.
Single‐cell suspensions (500 cells/well) were plated in 6‐well plates with complete medium. After 14‐day incubation (media refreshed every 3 days), colonies were methanol‐fixed (15 min) and crystal violet (0.1%, 30 min). Clusters containing ≥ 50 cells were counted as viable colonies using Colony Counter software (v2.0.3.0).

2.10.

## Statistical Analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. Continuous data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent biological replicates, and categorical data as counts (%). Prior to analysis, normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed via Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's tests, respectively, with no outliers excluded. In vitro experiments included three replicates, while animal studies comprised five mice per group. Two‐group comparisons were analyzed using two‐tailed unpaired Student's t‐test. For three or more groups, one‐way ANOVA with Bonferroni's post hoc test was applied; non‐parametric data were analyzed by Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn's test. Categorical variables were compared using χ
2 or Fisher's exact t‐test (expected count < 5). Survival was evaluated by Kaplan–Meier with log‐rank test. Significance was set at * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01.

## Results
3.1.

## 
ANKRD62 Is Upregulated in UCEC Tissues and Cell Lines
Gene microarray analysis of clinical UCEC specimens and matched adjacent non‐cancerous tissue demonstrated a marked differential expression pattern of ANKRD62, with substantially elevated levels in tumor tissues compared to their normal counterparts (Figure  ). This observation suggests a potential oncogenic role of ANKRD62 in UCEC pathogenesis. To validate these findings, we quantified ANKRD62 expression in 61 paired tumor and adjacent non‐cancerous tissue samples from our laboratory‐preserved cancer tissue (from an initial cohort of 73 pairs, 61 met the quality control criteria of CT values < 35 in qPCR analysis). The quantitative analysis confirmed the significant overexpression of ANKRD62 in tumor tissues (Figure  ). Consistent with our experimental data, bioinformatic analysis of the TCGA dataset revealed a similar pattern of ANKRD62 upregulation in UCEC tissues (Figure  ). Furthermore, endogenous expression profiling across multiple UCEC cell lines identified substantial ANKRD62 expression variability, with particularly high levels detected in HEC‐1A cells and relatively low expression in KLE cells (Figure  ). These distinct endogenous expression patterns guided our selection of cell lines: HEC‐1A cells with high ANKRD62 expression were used for loss‐of‐function (knockdown) studies, while KLE cells with low endogenous expression served as the model for gain‐of‐function (overexpression) experiments.
 To extend our investigation to the protein level, we performed quantitative protein expression analysis in 16 paired tumor/adjacent tissue samples stratified by disease stage (8 early‐stage and 8 advanced‐stage). The protein expression data revealed a consistent pattern of ANKRD62 upregulation in tumor tissues, with a notable increase in expression levels correlating with disease progression (Figure  ). This expression pattern was further corroborated by immunofluorescence analysis, which demonstrated enhanced ANKRD62 signal intensity in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Figure  ). Collectively, these results establish ANKRD62 as a consistently overexpressed molecular feature in UCEC, with expression levels potentially associated with disease aggressiveness and progression.

3.2.

## Clinical Associations of ANKRD62 Expression
Initial examination of TCGA data demonstrated marked upregulation of ANKRD62 in UCEC tissues compared to matched adjacent non‐cancerous tissues (Figure  ), with elevated expression correlating with increased cumulative patient risk (Figure  ). These findings were further substantiated through analysis of our laboratory cancer tissue repository, where evaluation of 63 UCEC specimens revealed significant associations between ANKRD62 expression levels and key clinicopathological parameters, including cervical invasion depth and lymph node metastasis status (Figure  ). Prognostic analysis identified ANKRD62 as a significant predictor of disease‐free survival (DFS) in our patient cohort (Figure  ). TCGA data showed that the expression of ANKRD62 had significant correlations with both overall survival (OS) and DFS (Figure  ). Multivariate Cox regression analysis of our local cohort and the TCGA cohort further confirmed ANKRD62 as an independent prognostic factor for overall survival (Tables  and ).
 The clinical relevance of ANKRD62 was further underscored by its strong association with advanced tumor grade and histological features indicative of aggressive disease (Figure  ). Immunofluorescence analysis of clinical tumor specimens revealed a significant co‐expression pattern, with tissues exhibiting elevated ANKRD62 expression demonstrating markedly increased Ki67 proliferative index (Figure  ), suggesting a potential role in tumor cell proliferation. These multi‐dimensional findings collectively establish ANKRD62 as not only an overexpressed molecular marker in UCEC but also as a prognostic indicator associated with advanced disease characteristics and unfavorable clinical outcomes.

3.3.

## 
ANKRD62 Modulates Cellular Proliferation
Building upon the established correlation between ANKRD62 overexpression and adverse clinical outcomes in UCEC, we systematically investigated its functional contribution to endometrial cancer pathogenesis. To delineate the oncogenic properties of ANKRD62, we developed a dual‐model experimental system utilizing HEC‐1A cells (endogenously high ANKRD62 expression) and KLE cells (endogenously low ANKRD62 expression). We established ANKRD62 knockdown in HEC‐1A cells, complemented by generating stable ANKRD62‐overexpressing KLE cell lines. The efficiency of genetic manipulation was rigorously validated through parallel assessment of transcriptional (qPCR) and translational (Western blot) expression profiles (Figure  ), ensuring the reliability of our experimental models.
 Comprehensive functional analysis revealed a relationship between ANKRD62 expression levels and malignant phenotypes. Quantitative assessment of cellular proliferation through CCK‐8 assays and colony formation experiments demonstrated that ANKRD62 overexpression significantly enhanced both cellular viability and clonogenic potential in cancer cells (Figure  ). Flow cytometric cell cycle analysis revealed that ANKRD62 overexpression accelerated cell cycle progression, whereas its genetic ablation induced G1/S phase arrest (Figure  ), suggesting its regulatory role in cell proliferation.
To establish the physiological relevance of these in vitro observations, we employed a luciferase‐based xenograft model system. Subcutaneous implantation of genetically engineered HEC‐1A‐luciferase cells into immunocompromised mice, followed by bioluminescence imaging over 28 days, revealed a dramatic reduction in tumor growth kinetics following ANKRD62 knockdown (Figure  ). Quantitative analysis of tumor volumes and bioluminescence intensities confirmed the significant growth‐suppressive effects of ANKRD62 ablation in vivo (Figure  ). These multi‐dimensional results provide compelling evidence for ANKRD62 as a critical regulator of UCEC cell proliferation.

3.4.

## 
ANKRD62 Drives Metastatic Capacity
To comprehensively characterize the metastatic functions of ANKRD62 in UCEC pathogenesis, we performed a series of in vitro and in vivo experiments to assess its role in cellular invasion and migration. Using HEC‐1A and KLE cellular models, we first evaluated the impact of ANKRD62 modulation on metastatic potential. transwell migration and matrigel invasion assays demonstrated that ANKRD62 overexpression significantly enhanced both migratory capacity and invasive potential, while ANKRD62 knockdown resulted in marked suppression of these metastatic properties (Figure  ).
 To establish the physiological relevance of these findings, we employed in vivo models; luciferase‐labeled HEC‐1A cells were intravenously injected via tail vein to simulate hematogenous dissemination. Bioluminescence imaging over 28 days demonstrated a significant reduction in pulmonary metastatic burden in mice receiving ANKRD62‐knockdown cells compared to control groups (Figure  ). Mechanistic investigation through apoptosis analysis revealed that ANKRD62 modulation did not significantly alter cellular apoptotic rates (Figure  ). Interestingly, in the subcutaneous tumor model, while robust luciferase activity was observed at primary injection sites, no detectable signals were observed in distant organs including the liver and lungs (Figure  ). These comprehensive findings suggest that ANKRD62 may facilitate later stages of the metastatic cascade, particularly through enhancing tumor cell survival during circulation, promoting invasive capabilities at secondary sites, and supporting metastatic colonization, rather than mediating initial detachment from primary tumors.

3.5.

## 
ANKRD62‐NF‐κB Pathway Interaction
To delineate the molecular mechanisms underlying ANKRD62‐mediated oncogenesis in UCEC, we employed an integrated bioinformatics approach to identify potential signaling pathways. Initial pathway mapping using the UCSC Genome Browser (http://genome.ucsc.edu/) identified significant network connectivity between ANKRD62 and core components of the NF‐κB signaling cascade (Figure  ). We performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on RNA‐seq data from UCEC tumor specimens in the TCGA database. The GSEA revealed significant enrichment of NF‐κB‐related gene signatures in tumors with high ANKRD62 expression (Figure  ). These findings demonstrate that ANKRD62 promotes UCEC progression may through NF‐κB pathway activation.
 We further elucidated the molecular mechanisms underlying ANKRD62s regulation of NF‐κB signaling in UCEC. Western blot analysis demonstrated that ANKRD62 expression significantly enhanced NF‐κB signaling, as indicated by an increase in p65 phosphorylation (Figure  ). Given the critical role of p65 nuclear translocation in NF‐κB signaling transduction, we performed nuclear‐cytoplasmic fractionation assay coupled with immunoblotting. The investigation demonstrated that elevated ANKRD62 expression significantly enhanced p65 nuclear translocation (Figure  ). To investigate potential direct molecular interactions, we conducted endogenous co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) and cellular immunofluorescence (IF) assays. IF observation with confocal microscopy showed that ANKRD62 and p65 were obviously co‐localized in the nucleus and surrounding area (Figure  ), but ANKRD62 did not form a firm complex with p65 to influence its activity (Figure  ). To establish the functional significance of NF‐κB signaling in mediating ANKRD62s oncogenic effects, we employed pharmacological modulation using specific pathway modulators. Treatment with the NF‐κB inhibitor PDTC significantly attenuated ANKRD62‐induced cellular proliferation (Figure  ). Conversely, activation of NF‐κB signaling using NF‐κB activator 2 in ANKRD62‐knockdown cells greatly restored migratory capacity (Figure  ). To functionally validate the significance of ANKRD62s nuclear co‐localization, we constructed a nuclear localization signal‐deficient mutant (NLS‐Mut). In KLE cells, the NLS mutant exhibited impaired nuclear accumulation with unaltered protein stability, and unlike wild‐type ANKRD62, failed to promote cell migration, enhance p65 phosphorylation, or upregulate downstream NF‐κB target genes CCND1 and MMP9 (Figure  ). These results indicate that the nuclear localization of ANKRD62 is crucial for its ability to drive cell migration and activate the NF‐κB pathway. Thus, ANKRD62 promotes UCEC progression through NF‐κB pathway activation, revealing potential therapeutic targets.
 

4.

## Discussion
UCEC progression, marked by enhanced proliferation, invasion, and metastasis, poses significant clinical challenges due to poor prognosis. The ANKRD protein family—characterized by ankyrin repeat domains—regulates oncogenesis through cell proliferation, migration, apoptosis, and signaling pathway interactions [ , , , , ]. Their functional significance extends to tumor microenvironment modulation, influencing stromal interactions and metastatic potential [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ]. The clinical relevance of ANKRD proteins is further underscored by recent multi‐omics investigations, including a comprehensive study integrating high‐throughput HPV capture sequencing, whole‐genome sequencing, transcriptome profiling, and OncoScan microarray analysis [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ], which identified ANKRD family genes as preferential sites for HPV integration in small cell cervical carcinoma (SCCC) [ ]. This discovery positions ANKRD members as promising diagnostic markers and therapeutic targets in gynecological malignancies.
Our study identifies ANKRD62 as a novel oncogenic driver in UCEC progression, demonstrating its dual role as a prognostic biomarker and functional regulator of NF‐κB signaling. Clinical analyses revealed strong correlations between ANKRD62 overexpression and aggressive clinicopathological features—including advanced tumor stage, lymph node metastasis, and reduced survival—positioning it as an independent predictor of poor outcomes. These findings align with emerging evidence implicating ANKRD family members in gynecological malignancies [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ], yet distinguish ANKRD62 through its unique association with proliferative and metastatic phenotypes rather than apoptotic regulation.
Functional experiments in cellular and animal models mechanistically linked ANKRD62 to UCEC pathogenesis. ANKRD62‐knockdown suppressed proliferation and metastasis by inducing G1/S arrest and impairing invasive capacity, while overexpression recapitulated aggressive phenotypes. These effects mirror observations for ANKRD1 in squamous cell carcinomas but contrast with ANKRD13Ds immunomodulatory roles in renal cell carcinoma [ , ], suggesting tissue‐specific functional diversification within the ANKRD family. Notably, our in vivo models revealed ANKRD62s specific involvement in metastatic colonization rather than primary tumor detachment.
Our study positions ANKRD62 as a novel oncogenic driver within the functionally diverse ANKRD family. To contextualize its role, it is informative to compare its oncogenic mechanism with those of other members. Similar to ANKRD2 or ANKRD37, which modulate NF‐κB activity in inflammatory or trophoblast contexts [ , ], ANKRD62 acts as a specific promoter of canonical NF‐κB signaling in UCEC. This contrasts sharply with the canonical repressor IκB, another ankyrin‐repeat protein. Furthermore, while several ANKRD members drive tumor progression through disparate pathways—such as ANKRD29 with MAPK signaling, ANKRD22 with metabolic reprogramming, or ANKRD35 within an E3 ligase axis [ , , , , , , , , , , , , , , , ] our work definitively links ANKRD62 to NF‐κB activation. Functionally, ANKRD62 shares the common property of enhancing proliferation and migration with members like ANKRD1 [ ] but is distinguished by its lack of impact on apoptosis and its specific association with advanced UCEC. This comparative analysis underscores that beyond shared structural motifs, ANKRD proteins exhibit remarkable mechanistic divergence and tissue‐specificity. ANKRD62 thereby defines a unique pathogenic role by hijacking the NF‐κB pathway to fuel endometrial carcinoma progression.
Mechanistically, we identified NF‐κB signaling as the critical pathway mediating ANKRD62s oncogenic effects, as evidenced by GSEA and enhanced p65 phosphorylation, nuclear translocation, and the rescue of migratory capacity upon NF‐κB pathway modulation. However, it is important to distinguish these findings from the mechanistic hypotheses they generate. Co‐immunoprecipitation assays did not detect a stable direct interaction between ANKRD62 and p65. Therefore, we propose potential models for future investigation: ANKRD62 may regulate NF‐κB indirectly, possibly by (1) modulating upstream kinases of the IKK complex, (2) serving as a scaffold for an as‐yet‐unidentified bridging adaptor protein, or (3) influencing the activity or stability of regulatory components such as IκBα. These hypotheses, while grounded in our observations, represent a crucial direction for subsequent research to precisely map the signaling node at which ANKRD62 acts.
Intriguingly, although ANKRD62 is required for NF‐κB activation and displays nuclear co‐localization with p65, our data argue against a stable, direct interaction. A primary limitation is the mechanistic ambiguity—whether ANKRD62 functions through weak, transient binding or via an unidentified bridging adaptor. Despite this gap, the translational implications of our findings are clear: the strong link between ANKRD62 overexpression and disease aggressiveness nominates it as a promising stratification biomarker for selecting high‐risk patients for NF‐κB pathway inhibition. Consequently, preclinical evaluation of ANKRD62 blockade, employing siRNA, CRISPRi, or small molecules, represents a logical next step toward new therapies for advanced UCEC, promising both biological insight and clinical progress.
