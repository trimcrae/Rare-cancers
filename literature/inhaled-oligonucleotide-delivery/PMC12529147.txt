## Confirm the Synthesis of Radioactive Probes
The synthesis
of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C, [68Ga]­Ga-DTN, and [68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C were analyzed through a Radio-High-Performance
Liquid Chromatography (HPLC, Agilent) system with a size exclusive
chromatography column (BioCore SEC-150, 5 μm, 7.8 × 300
mm2, China). The mobile phase is 10× PBS with a flow
rate of 1.0 mL/min. The concentration was measured by their absorbance
at 260 nm with a UV spectrophotometer (HITACHI U-3010) and radioactivity
detectors (CRC-25R, CAPINTEC).

## In Vitro Stability
A radioactive activity of 7.4 MBq
(200 μCi) for [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C was mixed with equal
volumes of 100% FBS and incubated at room temperature. RCP was assessed
using the instant thin-layer chromatography (iTLC)-SG method at 1,
5, 10, 15, 30, 45, 60, and 120 min to evaluate the in vitro stability,
with [68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C as the control.

## Preliminary Assessment
of the Plasma Half-Life in Healthy Mice
Healthy female BALB/c
mice (n = 5) received a tail vein injection
of 3.7 MBq (100 μCi) of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C. Blood
samples were collected at various time points (1, 5, 10, 15, 30, 45,
60, 120, and 240 min) via the retro-orbital vein, with [68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C as a control. The samples were weighed, and the radioactivity
was measured using a γ-counter. Plasma concentrations were expressed
in MBq/L and analyzed with a two-compartment model after the attenuation
correction. Plasma concentration versus time curves were plotted using
GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0) to determine the plasma half-life
(T
1/2) and the area under the concentration–time
curve (AUC0–∞) from baseline to infinity.

## Dynamic PET Imaging of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-Syl3C in HT-29
Tumor-Bearing Balb/c Nude Mice
HT-29 tumor-bearing BALB/c
nude mice received a tail vein injection of 1.85 MBq (50 μCi)
of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C. Anesthetized with an isoflurane–air
mixture (1–2%), the mice underwent 60 min of dynamic PET imaging
to assess the in vivo distribution of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C,
with [68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C and [68Ga]­Ga-DTN as controls.
Scans were performed using a micro-PET/CT scanner (Inveon MM, Siemens
Ltd., Munich, Germany) and divided into 20 frames: 60 s × 10
frames and 300 s × 10 frames. PET data were reconstructed using
Ordered subsets expectation maximization 3D or maximum a posteriori
(OSEM3D/MAP) algorithm.

## Construction of the Pharmacokinetic Model
To establish
the time–activity curve (TAC) for regions of interest (ROI),
uptake values were expressed as a percentage of the injected dose
per gram of tissue (%ID/g). The circulation half-life was determined
by fitting the blood pool TAC with a monoexponential model. The distribution
and clearance of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C in major organs were
analyzed using ROI data, generating equation curves for the heart,
kidneys, bladder, and liver with the GraphPad curve fitting tool.
The half-life of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C in each organ was derived
from these curves, and the accuracy of these rate constants is crucial
to understanding DTN-SYL3C metabolism. Corresponding metabolic information
can also be obtained for [68Ga]­Ga-DTN and [68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C as the controls.

## Histological Analysis of
Major Organs
Following the
plasma half-life assessment, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, kidneys,
and lungs) from healthy mice injected with [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C
were collected, washed with PBS, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde.
The samples were stored at 4 °C for paraffin sectioning and H&E
staining, then examined under a light microscope, showing blue nuclei
and pink cytoplasm. A control group received an equal volume of PBS.

## In Vivo Biodistribution Study
After PET/CT imaging,
HT-29 tumor-bearing mice (n = 3) were euthanized
by cervical dislocation and dissected. The tissues of interest (blood,
brain, heart, lungs, liver, spleen, kidneys, stomach, small intestine,
large intestine, muscle, bone, and tumor) were removed and recounted,
and the relevant radioactivity (attenuation correction) was measured
using the γ-Counter.

## Statistical Analysis
Data were processed
and analyzed
using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software, and quantitative data were expressed
as the mean and standard deviation. A t test or one-way
ANOVA was used to compare the statistical differences between the
groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically
significant.

## In Vitro Stability
After purification with PD-10 columns,
the radiochemical purity (RCP) of the [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C
and [68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C by iTLC, both of them above 90%. Their

The results allow us to evaluate the pharmacokinetics and tumor uptake
of DTN-SYL3C more accurately in vivo by nuclear medicine techniques.

## Preliminary Assessment of the Plasma Half-Life in Healthy Mice
Healthy female BALB/c mice (n = 5) are injected with [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C at 3.7 MBq (100 μCi) via the tail vein. Blood
was collected from the retro-orbital plexus at 1, 5, 10, 15, 30, 45,
60, 120, and 240 min postinjection, and its radioactivity was measured.
[68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C was used as a control ( ), and plasma half-life T
1/2 and area under the concentration–time
curve from zero (predose) to infinity (AUC0‑∞) were plotted and calculated using GraphPad 9.0. As can be seen
from A, both
reached a peak of radioactivity in the blood at 5 min, and the blood
clearance of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C was slower than that of
[68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C. The AUC0‑∞ of
[68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C was 421.4 ± 40.69%ID·g–1·h, and the AUC0–∞ of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C was 755.2 ± 68.21%ID·g–1·h. Its in vivo bioavailability was 1.8 times higher than that
of SYL3C alone. The plasma half-life of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C
(11.39 ± 2.66 min) was 1.6 times longer than that of [68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C (7.26 ± 0.17 min) as shown in B. The results demonstrate that the conjugation
strategy of DTN in healthy mice can improve the in vivo circulation
of SYL3C aptamers to a certain extent.
 

## Dynamic PET Imaging and Pharmacokinetic Model
Construction
HT-29 BABL/C tumor-bearing nude mice (n = 3)
were anesthetized
with continuous isoflurane (1–2%) gas, and 3.7 MBq (100 μCi)
of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C and the same dose of [68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C and [68Ga]­Ga-DTN were injected via the tail
vein catheter during a prescan PET acquisition protocol, and the metabolic
distribution map ( ) and tumor-level distribution map ( A) of tumor-bearing mice were obtained by
60 min dynamic PET imaging. ROIs were drawn, and the activity concentration
was expressed as %ID/g, the corresponding uptake curves were plotted
using GraphPad ( B), and the corresponding drug half-lives were calculated using a
single-compartment model.
 The metabolic results showed that [68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C
was
mainly metabolized by the kidney, and the half-lives in the heart
and kidney were 5.13 ± 1.59 and 6.62 ± 2.13 min, respectively.
The clearance of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN and [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C
involved both renal and hepatic pathways, and the half-lives of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN and [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C were 9.19 ±
2.88 and 16.00 ± 2.04 min in the heart and 9.45 ± 4.14 and
21.65 ± 6.77 min in the kidney, respectively, indicating that
the in vivo circulation time of SYL3C was extended by 2.5 times on
average after DTN coupling. As a single-stranded DNA, the molecular
weight of SYL3C is significantly smaller than that of DTN, so the
peak concentration of SYL3C reaches the kidney within 5 min, and DTN
is excreted into the kidney within about 10 min. In the tumor uptake
curve, it can be seen that the tumor uptake of both the aptamer and
the DTN peaked at early time points and both decreased intratumoral
uptake due to in vivo metabolism, but the uptake of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN was lower than that of [68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C after
30 min and lasted for nearly 20 min. Combined with the immunofluorescence
results, it is considered that the aptamer has a high affinity for
the EpCAM target, leading to higher retention in the tumor compared
to the nontargeted DTN, which is passively retained and therefore
cleared from the tumor area with metabolism. The metabolic curves
of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C in tumors indicated that the conjugate
not only retained the targeting of SYL3C aptamers but also delayed
the decline rate in tumors due to the good in vivo stability of DTN.
Compared with aptamers alone, DTN-SYL3C conjugates have a certain
degree of increased uptake and retention time in tumors, indicating
the effectiveness of DTN-conjugation strategies (see ).
 

## Biodistribution Studies
The biodistribution results
were consistent with the dynamic PET results, and the uptake values
of [68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C, [68Ga]­Ga-DTN, and [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C at 1 h time point were 0.27 ± 0.22% ID/g, 0.32
± 0.16% ID/g, and 0.89 ± 0.30% ID/g, respectively ( ). This resulted
in tumor-to-muscle (T/M) ratios of 1.49, 0.92, and 1.85; tumor-to-blood
(T/B) ratios of 0.84, 0.53, and 1.43; tumor-to-liver (T/L) ratios
of 0.02, 0.03, and 0.06; and tumor-to-kidney (T/K)
ratios of 0.03, 0.05, and 0.07 for [68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C, [68Ga]­Ga-DTN, and [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C, respectively.
 

## Toxicological Study
After studying the hemodynamics
of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C in HT-29 tumor-bearing mice, major
organs were taken for pathological analysis. The results of H&E
staining showed that compared with the PBS group, there were no significant
pathological changes in the heart, liver, spleen, kidney, and lung
tissues of healthy mice injected with [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C
(Figure S4).

## Discussion
Aptamers
are widely used for molecular imaging due to their specificity
and designability. They can be used alone or with various agents for
diagnosing and treating diseases, including tumors. Evaluating in
vivo biosafety and metabolism is vital for the clinical use of aptamers
alongside optimizing aptamer-drug combinations. Thus, this study aims
to expand the application of the aptamer SYL3C, which has shown strong
cellular affinity but lacks in vivo imaging studies, by evaluating
its metabolism and biosafety in vivo. On the other hand, DTNs have
been used to improve the in vivo pharmacokinetics of aptamers due
to their superior drug-loading properties. Recent studies on DTN-modified
aptamers show the strategy’s effectiveness, but few studies
have quantitatively compared the effects of DTN conjugation, a gap
that our study addresses using dynamic PET imaging. The results reveal
that unconjugated SYL3C undergoes predominant renal elimination (plasma T
1/2 = 7.26 ± 0.17 min), resulting in suboptimal
tumor uptake (0.27%ID/g at 1 h) despite its target affinitya
limitation consistent with other small aptamer probes like [68Ga]­Ga-SGC8 (T
1/2 = 8.1 ± 0.9 min),
which limits its utility for imaging.
The conjugation of SYL3C
to DTN represents a strategic advance
in probe engineering. DTN’s rigid architecture confers nuclease
resistance and extends systemic circulation (T
1/2 = 11.39 ± 2.66 min, 1.6-fold increase), while its
size exploits the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect.
This dual-targeting mechanism combining passive EPR-driven accumulation
and active EpCAM binding synergistically increased tumor uptake to
0.89%ID/g, which is 3.3-fold higher than that of free [68Ga]­Ga-SYL3C. Notably, this enhancement surpasses PEGylation strategies
and approaches multivalent systems like gold nanoparticle-aptamer
conjugate.
Despite these gains, clinical translation requires
addressing key
limitations. Although the significantly higher tumor uptake of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C compared to the nontargeted [68Ga]­Ga-DTN control strongly suggests EpCAM-mediated targeting, we
acknowledge that a formal in vivo blocking study with excess unlabeled
DTN-SYL3C would provide the most direct evidence for specificity.
Additionally, while the conjugation between DTN and SYL3C was confirmed
by gel electrophoresis and HPLC, characterization via spectroscopic
methods (e.g., Raman or FTIR) could have provided more detailed molecular-level
insights into the chemical binding.
−

 Similarly, confocal
microscopy imaging to visually confirm cellular uptake and internalization
of the conjugate in EpCAM-positive cells would have strengthened the
in vitro validation. These experiments will be a critical focus of
our future research.
The quantitative tumor-to-background ratios
revealed a significant
challenge for clinical translation: high off-target accumulation in
the liver and kidneys (T/L = 0.06, T/K = 0.07), which is a common
characteristic of nucleic acid–based therapeutics and nanomaterials
cleared by the hepatorenal system. This currently limits its utility
for imaging primary abdominal and pelvic lesions,
−

 as background
activity in these organs could obscure nearby tumors. However, the
favorable T/M (1.85) and T/B (1.43) ratios demonstrate that [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C can still delineate EpCAM-positive tumors
from surrounding soft tissue and blood pool, and the tumor uptake
of [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C (0.89%ID/g at 1 h) remains lower
than antibody-based tracers, partly due to EpCAM heterogeneity in
metastatic lesions. Future designs could therefore focus on modifying
surface chemistry (e.g., with polyethylene glycol (PEG) chains) to
improve pharmacokinetics as well as incorporating dual-targeting ligands
(e.g., CD133 aptamer) to broaden applicability and overcome heterogeneity.
Most importantly, this study positions [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C
within the landscape of existing CRC imaging strategies. Compared
to the widely used but nonspecific metabolic tracer [18F]­FDG, our aptamer-based probe offers the potential for higher specificity
by directly targeting EpCAM, which could lead to improved differentiation
of CRC from inflammation and reduced false positives. Furthermore,
when compared to EpCAM-targeted antibody-based imaging agents, [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C boasts a significantly shorter plasma half-life,
enabling same-day imaging and potentially lower background signal
due to faster clearance from nontarget tissues. This combination of
rapid kinetics and specific targeting could position [68Ga]­Ga-DTN-SYL3C as a valuable complementary tool.
The dynamic
PET methodology employed here provides a robust framework
for quantifying nanocarrier–aptamer interactions. Our pharmacokinetic
model precisely delineates how DTN decelerates clearance of SYL3C
from tumors, offering a reference for optimizing oligonucleotide-based
theranostics.

## Conclusions
In summary, we developed
a [68Ga]­Ga-labeled DTN-SYL3C
aptamer conjugate as a proof-of-concept PET probe for EpCAM-positive
colorectal cancer. Quantitative dynamic imaging revealed that DTN
conjugation extends the plasma half-life of SYL3C and amplifies tumor
uptake. This study primarily establishes a robust methodological framework
for quantitatively evaluating the in vivo pharmacokinetic impact of
nanocarrier–aptamer conjugation. While the current probe’s
high hepatorenal clearance presents a challenge for abdominal imaging,
the strategy provides a valuable template for future optimization
and design of oligonucleotide-based theranostics.
