## Abstract

## Background:
Antenatal glucocorticoid administration represents a standard therapeutic intervention for preterm birth; however, its long-term consequences on vascular endothelial function in offspring remain poorly understood. Nitric oxide (NO), the principal vasodilator secreted by vascular endothelial cells (VECs), plays a pivotal role in vascular pathophysiology when its biosynthesis is dysregulated. This investigation elucidates the effects and underlying mechanisms of gestational exposure to clinically relevant glucocorticoid doses on endothelial NO synthesis in offspring.

## Methods and results:
Pregnant Sprague–Dawley rats were administered dexamethasone (DEX), a synthetic glucocorticoid, during the final gestational week. Thoracic aorta specimens from both fetal and adult offspring were isolated for experimentation. Gestational DEX exposure markedly attenuated NO-dependent acetylcholine-induced vasodilation in thoracic aorta rings from both fetal and adult male offspring. Furthermore, acetylcholine-stimulated NO synthesis was significantly impaired in VECs derived from DEX-exposed offspring thoracic aorta. Mechanistic investigations revealed that gestational DEX exposure diminished endothelial NO synthesis capacity in offspring, primarily through downregulation of inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor type 3 (IP3R3) expression. Notably, gestational DEX-induced epigenetic reprogramming of IP3R3 gene expression appears to be mediated by alterations in DNA methylation status, ultimately disrupting the IP3R3/Ca2+/NO synthase signaling cascade in offspring VECs and predisposing to long-term vascular dysfunction through impaired NO biosynthesis.

## Conclusion:
This study provides the first experimental evidence that gestational glucocorticoid exposure compromises endothelial NO synthesis in offspring vasculature through epigenetic reprogramming of IP3R3 gene expression. These novel findings demonstrate that the detrimental effects of antenatal glucocorticoid exposure on fetal vascular function persist into adulthood, offering critical insights into the developmental origins of vascular disease pathogenesis.

Keywords: DNA methylation, gestational glucocorticoid exposure, IP3R3, NO synthesis, offspring

## INTRODUCTION
It is now widely acknowledged that developing embryos or fetuses are highly sensitive to insults from the intrauterine environment, which can have varying degrees of impact on fetal growth and development. These impacts can lay the groundwork for diseases later in life, such as hypertension, renal diseases, and type 2 diabetes [ – ]. Glucocorticoids are crucial stress hormones that play a role in fundamental processes, such as metabolic homeostasis, cognition, and development. In clinical practice, synthetic glucocorticoids serve dual roles as therapeutic agents for diverse diseases and as critical regulators of normal physiological functions, particularly in fetal growth and development. The administration of glucocorticoids during pregnancy, specifically dexamethasone (DEX), has become a standard therapeutic intervention to accelerate preterm fetal lung maturation and reduce the incidence of respiratory distress syndrome, owing to its ability to effectively cross the placental barrier. However, emerging evidence has raised concerns regarding the potential long-term consequences of gestational glucocorticoid exposure. Accumulating data from both animal studies and clinical observations have demonstrated that gestational glucocorticoid exposure not only influences intrauterine growth but also predisposes offspring to cardiovascular complications [ – ]. Notably, offspring exposed to glucocorticoids in utero frequently exhibit hypertension and cardiovascular dysfunction [ , ].
The vascular endothelium, comprising vascular endothelial cells (VECs) that line the inner vascular surface, plays a crucial role in vascular homeostasis through the secretion of various regulatory factors. Among these, nitric oxide (NO) stands as the principal vasodilator, orchestrating the regulation of vascular tone, blood pressure, and blood flow [ , ]. Dysregulation of NO synthesis and bioavailability has been implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous vascular disorders [ , ]. The synthesis of endothelial NO is primarily mediated through the Ca2+-dependent activation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS). Acetylcholine (Ach), a prototypical vasodilator, serves as a key activator of eNOS, thereby inducing NO-dependent vasodilation and frequently being employed to assess endothelial NO synthesis capacity. The molecular mechanisms underlying Ach-induced NO synthesis involve a complex cascade of events. Upon binding to muscarinic receptors on the cell membrane, Ach activates adjacent Ca2+ channels, predominantly transient receptor potential (TRP) channels, facilitating extracellular Ca2+ influx. Simultaneously, Ach stimulates phospholipase C (PLC), which catalyzes the hydrolysis of phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) to generate the second messenger inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3). IP3 subsequently binds to its specific receptors (IP3Rs) on the endoplasmic reticulum, triggering Ca2+ release from intracellular stores [ , , ]. The synergistic action of extracellular Ca2+ influx and endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ release significantly elevates intracellular Ca2+ concentrations. This Ca2+ surge binds to calmodulin (CaM), activating Ca2+/CaM-dependent kinase II, which phosphorylates eNOS (p-eNOS), thereby enhancing eNOS activity and catalyzing NO synthesis. Additionally, endothelial NO synthesis and bioavailability are modulated by key components of this signaling pathway and the intracellular redox state, particularly through the action of NADPH oxidases (NOXs) and superoxide dismutase (SODs) [ , ]. The synthesized NO diffuses to adjacent vascular smooth muscle cells, where it activates the guanylate cyclase/cGMP pathway, ultimately mediating vasodilation [ ].
Despite growing evidence suggesting that gestational glucocorticoid exposure induces hypertension and vascular dysfunction in offspring, the precise mechanisms underlying impaired endothelial NO synthesis remain poorly understood. Given the widespread clinical use of glucocorticoids during mid to late pregnancy, it is crucial to investigate their potential long-term effects on endothelial NO synthesis in fetal and offspring vasculature. The present study aims to elucidate the persistent effects and underlying pathological mechanisms of mid to late pregnancy glucocorticoid exposure on endothelial NO synthesis. Our findings may provide valuable insights into the developmental origins of vascular diseases, contribute to the theoretical framework for early prevention and intervention strategies, and offer novel perspectives for mitigating the adverse effects of gestational glucocorticoid exposure in clinical practice.

## METHODS

## Experimental animals
Sprague–Dawley rats were obtained from the Animal Center of Soochow University and maintained under standard laboratory conditions, including controlled temperature (20–25 °C), relative humidity (40–60%), and a 12-h light/dark cycle. The animals were provided with standard rodent chow and tap water ad libitum. All experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Soochow University (Approval No. 20222476). Following a 1-week acclimatization period, breeding pairs were established by co-housing male and female rats at a 2 : 1 ratio. Successful mating was confirmed through vaginal smear examination for the presence of sperm, with the day of positive confirmation designated as gestational day (GD) 1. Pregnant rats were randomly allocated to either the dexamethasone (DEX) treatment group or vehicle control (0.9% saline, VEX) group (N = 40 per group) beginning on GD 14. The gestational DEX exposure model was established in accordance with our previous research [ , ]. Specifically, pregnant rats in the DEX group received intraperitoneal injections of DEX (200 μg/kg), while those in the VEX group were administered equivalent volumes of saline on GD 14, 16, 18, and 20. On GD 21, a subset of pregnant rats (N = 20 per group) was humanely euthanized via isoflurane (3%) inhalation. Only pregnant rats with litter sizes between 10–14 were included in subsequent analyses. The fetal thoracic aorta was immediately dissected and processed for further examination. The remaining pregnant rats were allowed to deliver naturally for subsequent offspring-related studies. Following a 1-month lactation period, male offspring were weaned and maintained on standard diet and water. These offspring were subsequently evaluated at 8 and 16 weeks of age.

## Blood pressure measurement
Blood pressure monitoring was performed on adult male offspring, as previously described [ ]. Briefly, animals were anesthetized with sodium phenobarbital (50 mg/kg, i.p.), and a silicone catheter was surgically implanted into the femoral artery. After a 2-day recovery period, baseline blood pressure was recorded for 60 min in conscious rats using the PowerLab system and associated software (AD Instruments, Oxford, UK). Continuous measurements were obtained through the arterial catheter, ensuring accurate and reliable blood pressure data collection.

## Hematoxylin and eosin staining
Histological evaluation of thoracic aorta specimens was performed using standard Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining protocols. thoracic aorta samples were collected from both fetal and adult offspring and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.4). Following fixation, specimens were decalcified in 5% hydrochloric acid and subsequently embedded in paraffin blocks. Tissue sections of 2–4 μm thickness were obtained using a Leica CM1850 microtome (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and stained with H&E (#C0105S, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Digital images were acquired using a Nikon E600 microscope equipped with a DMX1200 digital camera (Nikon Instruments, Tokyo, Japan). For quantitative analysis, 10 thoracic aorta sections per group were imaged, capturing both outer and luminal diameters, and wall thickness measurements were performed using Image-Pro 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, Maryland, USA).

## Measurement of vascular relaxation
Vascular relaxation studies were conducted following established protocols. Pregnant dams and adult offspring were euthanized via inhalation of 3% isoflurane, followed by immediate collection of thoracic aorta specimens. The excised vessels were maintained in ice-cold physiological saline solution (pH 7.4) containing (in mmol/l): NaCl 142.0, KH2PO4 1.2, MgSO4 1.7, CaCl2 • 2H2O 2.8, KCl 4.7, NaHCO3 14.9, EDTA 0.02, glucose 5.0, and HEPES 10.0, continuously aerated with 95% O2/5% CO2 mixture. thoracic aorta rings (2–3 mm length) were carefully prepared and mounted on stainless steel wires in a multichannel myograph (620M, DMT, Denmark) for isometric force recording. To minimize individual variation, two thoracic aorta rings from each male fetus/offspring were randomly selected, with their mean values used for statistical analysis. Equilibration and normalization procedures were conducted in accordance with the methods outlined in our previous research [ , ]. Before the administration of pharmacological agents, the viability and structural integrity of the thoracic aorta rings were evaluated by subjecting them to a high potassium solution (120 mmol/l KCl). After this, the thoracic aorta rings were thoroughly rinsed three times with the physiological saline solution and allowed to stabilize for 1 h. Once stabilization was achieved, serotonin hydrochloride (5-HT, at a concentration of 10−4 mol/l) was introduced to elicit a stable contraction, providing a baseline for testing vascular relaxation. Upon reaching a stable contraction with 5-HT, cumulative concentrations of acetylcholine (Ach, ranging from 10−11 to 10−4 mol/l) or sodium nitroprusside (SNP, ranging from 10−11 to 10−4 mol/l) were added to the system. The millinewton (mN) values of the stable contractions induced by 5-HT were recorded, and the change in force relative to the baseline (ΔmN) was calculated. The tension changes induced by Ach or SNP were expressed as a percentage of the ΔmN value. Dose–response curves for relaxation were constructed by cumulatively adding Ach or SNP. To investigate the underlying mechanisms of Ach-mediated relaxation, thoracic aorta rings were preincubated for 30 min with specific pharmacological inhibitors: L-Name (nonselective NO synthase inhibitor, 10–5 mol/l), 2APB (IP3R channel inhibitor, 10–6 mol/l), D-inositol-1,3,5-triphosphate (IP3, 10–6 mol/l), or 5-Aza (DNA methyltransferase inhibitor, 10–6 mol/l). Following inhibitor pretreatment, thoracic aorta rings were contracted with 5-HT (10–4 mol/l) and subsequently exposed to cumulative concentrations of Ach (10–11 to 10–4 mol/l) to assess relaxation responses. All pharmacological agents were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (USA).

## Endothelial cell isolation and treatment
Primary VECs were isolated from thoracic aortas of 16-week-old male offspring using an established protocol [ ]. Briefly, thoracic aortas were immediately excised and immersed in PBS. Following longitudinal dissection, the vessels were flattened in a petri dish, and the intimal surface was enzymatically treated with 0.2% collagenase type II solution. After 10 min of digestion, endothelial cells were gently harvested from the luminal surface using sterilized tweezers. The collected cells were centrifuged, resuspended, and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, penicillin (100 U/ml), and streptomycin (100 mg/ml) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. VEC identity was confirmed through CD31 immunostaining (#131R-2, Sigma-Aldrich). Cultures were maintained in 10 cm2 dishes with passaging every 2–3 days, and cells from passages 3 to 5 were utilized for experiments. For IP3R3 knockdown, VECs were transfected with specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's protocol. siRNA sequences (synthesized by Genepharma, Shanghai, China) are detailed in Table . Following 48 h of posttransfection culture, cells were harvested for subsequent analyses.
 

## Measurement of nitric oxide release and intracellular Ca2+ dynamics
NO and Ca2+ dynamics in VECs were assessed using confocal microscopy as previously described [ , ]. VECs were loaded with either 3-amino,4-aminomethyl-2′,7′-difluorescein diacetate (DAF-FM, 10–6 mol/l; #S0019, Beyotime) or Fluo-3 AM (10–6 mol/l; Invitrogen) in Tyrode's buffer (composition: 135.0 mmol/l NaCl, 4.7 mmol/l KCl, 1.0 mmol/l MgCl2, 2.0 mmol/l CaCl2, 10.0 mmol/l HEPES, 1.2 mmol/l NaH2PO4 2H2O, and 10.0 mmol/l glucose, pH 7.4) for 20 min at room temperature. After three washes with Tyrode's buffer, cells were transferred to a recording chamber. Prior to Ach stimulation, VECs were pretreated for 30 min with either 2APB (10–6 mol/l), D-inositol-1,3,5-triphosphate (IP3, 10–6 mol/l), or 5-Aza (10–6 mol/l). NO and Ca2+ responses to Ach (10–4 mol/l) were captured on a per-cell basis using total internal reflection fluorescence microscopy with an EMCCD imaging system. Representative single-cell images and traces were documented. Fluorescence intensity was quantified as F/F0, where F0 represents baseline fluorescence before Ach stimulation and F denotes the fluorescence intensity within the region of interest after Ach stimulation. All images were processed and analyzed using Fiji software (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

## DNA extraction and methylation analysis
Genomic DNA was extracted from VEC lysates [lysis buffer: 10 mmol/l NaCl, 10.0 mmol/l EDTA, 10.0 mmol/l Tris–Cl (pH 7.5), 0.5% sarcosyl, and 1 mg/ml proteinase K] after overnight incubation at 60 °C using phenol/chloroform extraction. Bisulfite conversion was performed using the EZ DNA Methylation-Direct Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, California, USA) following manufacturer's instructions. CpG islands in the proximal IP3R3 gene promoter were selected based on the following criteria: at least 200 bp length; at least 50% guanine–cytosine content; at least 60% observed/expected CpG ratio. For bisulfite sequencing, bisulfite-treated DNA was amplified by PCR; amplified regions were cloned into vector pMD19-T and sequenced by standard Sanger sequencing (TSINGKE). Data were presented as the percentage of methylation at regions of interest [methylated CpG/total CpG) × 100]. Data were analyzed by the online tool QUMA (http://quma.cdb.riken.jp/). Primers for bisulfite sequencing were listed in Table .

## Quantitative real-time PCR
Total RNA was extracted from VECs or thoracic aortas using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) and reverse transcribed using the first-strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara, Dalian, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on the iQ5 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad) with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, California, USA). mRNA levels were normalized to GAPDH and calculated using the ΔΔCt method. For miRNA analysis, RNA was isolated using the miRcute miRNA Isolation Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China), reverse transcribed with miRcute Plus miRNA First-Strand cDNA Kit (Tiangen), and quantified using miRcute Plus miRNA qPCR Kit (SYBR Green) on the iQ5 system. U6 served as the normalization control for miRNA expression. All primer sequences are detailed in Table . Experiments were performed in triplicate with independent biological replicates (Table ) [ , ].
 

## Western blotting
Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer (50 mmol/l Tris, 150 mmol/l NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 10 mmol/l EDTA) supplemented with Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (#sc-29130, Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford Protein Assay Kit (#P0006, Beyotime). Subsequently, 20 μg of protein extract from each sample was separated on 10% SDS–PAGE gels and electrotransferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against IP3R3 (YT8058, 1 : 1000, Immunoway, Jiangsu, China), eNOS (AF6762, 1 : 2000, Beyotime), p-eNOS (S1177, 1 : 800, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, Massachusetts, USA), and β-actin (A5441, 1 : 3000, Sigma). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (#7074/6, Cell Signaling Technology) were used for detection. Protein bands were visualized using the EC3 Imaging System and quantified using ImageJ software. Experiments were performed in triplicate with independent samples [ ].

## Statistical analysis
Data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Concentration–response curves were analyzed by two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni post hoc test using GraphPad Prism version 7 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). Statistical significance was evaluated for other data using one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test or Student's t test. Results with a P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

## RESULTS

## Nitric oxide-dependent vasodilation in fetal thoracic aorta rings
The experimental model of gestational DEX exposure in rats is illustrated in Fig. a. Histological analysis of fetal thoracic aorta sections revealed comparable vascular wall thickness between VEX and DEX groups (56.55 ± 1.115 vs. 57.04 ± 1.324 μm, respectively; Fig. b). To evaluate endothelial function, we employed Ach as an endothelial-dependent vasodilator that stimulates NO synthesis, and SNP as an endothelial-independent NO donor to assess vascular smooth muscle responsiveness. Comparative analysis of Ach-induced and SNP-induced vasodilation responses provides an indirect assessment of endothelial NO synthesis capacity. There was no significant difference in the maximal contraction induced by 5-HT (10–4 mol/l) (Figure S1A), so the Ach/SNP-induced vasodilation was expressed as a percentage of this maximal contraction. Preliminary data indicated that gestational DEX exposure exerted more pronounced detrimental effects on Ach-induced relaxation in male fetuses compared to female counterparts (Fig. c and Figure S1B), prompting our focus on male offspring in subsequent investigations.
 In male fetal thoracic aorta rings, gestational DEX exposure significantly attenuated Ach-induced vasodilation (Fig. d, 10−7∼10−4 mol/l), with maximal dilation percentages reduced from 64.37% in VEX group to 37.97% in DEX group (Fig. c and d). This impairment was further evidenced by a significant reduction in pD2 (−LogEC50) values (Fig. e). In contrast, SNP-mediated vasodilation remained unaltered between groups (Fig. f). Notably, pretreatment with L-Name, a specific eNOS inhibitor, abolished the differential Ach-induced vasodilation between groups (Fig. g). These collective findings demonstrate that gestational DEX exposure specifically impairs endothelial NO-dependent vasodilation in fetal vasculature.

## NO-dependent vasodilation in adult offspring thoracic aorta rings
Blood pressure assessment revealed significant elevations in systolic pressure (116.6 ± 1.32 vs. 123.3 ± 1.38 mmHg, P = 0.0026), diastolic pressure (67.25 ± 1.48 vs. 76.01 ± 1.27 mmHg; P = 0.0003), and mean arterial pressure (82.59 ± 1.32 vs. 90.08 ± 1.67 mmHg; P = 0.0025) in DEX offspring compared to VEX controls (Fig. a and b). Despite comparable vascular morphometric parameters (external diameter, internal diameter, and wall thickness) in adult offspring thoracic aorta (Figure S2), DEX offspring exhibited persistent impairment in Ach-mediated vasodilation (10−7∼10−4 mol/l), characterized by reduced maximal dilation percentages and pD2 values (Fig. c and d, Figure S3A). Consistent with fetal observations, SNP-induced vasodilation remained unaffected (Fig. e), and these patterns persisted in 8-week-old offspring (Figure S3B–D). The differential Ach response was again abolished by L-Name pretreatment (Fig. f), confirming that gestational DEX exposure induces long-lasting impairment in endothelial NO-dependent vasodilation that persists into adulthood.
 

## IP3R3 expression and Ach-induced nitric oxide releases in the offspring's TA-VECs
To further explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the impaired endothelial NO-dependent vasodilation in gestational DEX offspring, we isolated and cultured VECs from offspring thoracic aorta in vitro (Figure S4A). Upon evaluating the expression of key genes involved in NO synthesis, we found no significant differences in the expression of Ach receptors (M1–5), eNOS, TRP channel components, SODs, and NOXs in the TA-VECs between the two groups (Figure S4B–F). We examined the expression of key IP3Rs and CaMs in the offspring's TA-VECs (Fig. a–c) and observed a significant decrease in the expression levels of IP3R3, both at the protein and mRNA level, in the DEX group (Fig. a–c, Figure S5). Concurrently, the p-eNOS level was also markedly reduced in the DEX group (Fig. d–e, Figure S5). These findings suggest that gestational DEX exposure impairs NO-dependent vasodilation in offspring, potentially due to a decrease in p-eNOS mediated by IP3R3.
 Subsequently, we performed dynamic evaluations of Ach-induced NO synthesis and intracellular Ca2+ levels in individual VECs. We determined the maximum fluorescence intensity as F/F0 (max), where F denotes the peak fluorescence value induced by Ach, and F0 represents the Ca2+ or NO fluorescence value prior to Ach stimulation. Figure f and g depicts the alterations in fluorescence intensity and traces of Ach-induced Ca2+ levels and NO synthesis. The F/F0(max) of Ach-induced Ca2+ levels and NO synthesis were diminished in the DEX group, suggesting that the impaired NO synthesis in gestational DEX offspring VECs is associated with the Ca2+ pathway.

## IP3R3 deficiency underlies impaired nitric oxide synthesis
Pharmacological modulation of IP3R3 signaling was employed to establish causality. In adult VEX offspring thoracic aorta rings, 2APB (IP3R inhibitor) significantly attenuated Ach-induced vasodilation, while IP3 supplementation restored vasodilatory capacity in DEX offspring thoracic aorta rings (Fig. a–d). Parallel experiments in cultured VECs demonstrated that 2APB impaired Ach-induced NO release in VEX offspring TA-VECs, while IP3 rescued NO synthesis in DEX offspring TA-VECs (Fig. e and f). These findings collectively demonstrate that gestational DEX-induced downregulation of IP3R3 is the primary mechanism underlying impaired endothelial NO synthesis in offspring vasculature.
 

## DNA methylation within IP3R3 gene promoter
To elucidate the mechanistic role of IP3R3 in NO synthesis regulation within TA-VECs, we designed and validated specific siRNAs targeting IP3R3 (Fig, 5a and b). Following transfection of TA-VECs with either negative control siRNA (NC) or siRNA-2077, we performed quantitative analyses of IP3R3 and p-eNOS expression levels. As demonstrated in Fig. a–c and Figure S6, IP3R3 knockdown resulted in significant attenuation of p-eNOS levels and Ach-induced NO release in offspring TA-VECs. These findings strongly suggest that IP3R3 modulates NO synthesis in VECs through regulation of p-eNOS activation. Importantly, our data indicate that IP3R3 downregulation represents a critical molecular mechanism underlying impaired NO synthesis in VECs of gestational DEX-exposed offspring.
 Considering the well established regulatory role of miRNAs in gene expression, we employed bioinformatics tools to predict potential miRNA targets of IP3R3. Subsequent expression profiling revealed significant upregulation of several candidate miRNAs (miR-122b, miR-124-3p, and miR-153-3p) in VECs from gestational DEX offspring (Figure S7A). To establish a potential causal relationship between miRNA overexpression and IP3R3 suppression, we performed gain-of-function experiments using miRNA mimics. However, transfection with these miRNA mimics failed to significantly reduce IP3R3 expression compared to negative control miRNA (Figure S7B), suggesting that the downregulation of IP3R3 expression in gestational DEX offspring VECs is not influenced by miRNA regulation. Sequence analysis revealed two CpG islands within the promoter of the IP3R3 gene, encompassing a total of 58 CpG sites (Fig. d). To investigate the epigenetic regulation of IP3R3 expression, we performed demethylation experiments using 5-Aza in offspring TA-VECs. Remarkably, 5-Aza treatment significantly enhanced IP3R3 expression (Fig. e). Targeted bisulfite sequencing analysis revealed a substantial increase in mean methylation percentage within CpG island 2 (VEX vs. DEX; 1.43 vs. 6.67%; Fig. f). Collectively, these findings indicate that IP3R3 downregulation is associated with hypermethylation of its promoter region, leading to impaired endothelial NO synthesis in gestational DEX offspring.
To further validate the functional consequences of IP3R3 promoter methylation, we examined the effects of DNA methylation inhibition on NO-dependent vasodilation in thoracic aorta rings. In adult DEX offspring thoracic aorta, 5-Aza treatment significantly restored Ach-induced vasodilation, enhanced NO synthesis, and upregulated IP3R3 expression (Fig. a–c). Similarly, in adult DEX offspring TA-VECs, 5-Aza treatment markedly improved Ach-induced NO release (Fig. d). In summary, our study provides compelling evidence that gestational DEX exposure induces IP3R3 promoter hypermethylation, leading to its downregulation in offspring TA-VECs, ultimately resulting in impaired NO synthesis and endothelial dysfunction. The comprehensive mechanistic model of our findings is presented in Fig. e.
 

## DISCUSSION
Our study employed a rat model to investigate the effects of gestational DEX exposure during mid-to-late gestation, and demonstrated that gestational DEX exposure adversely affects vascular endothelial NO synthesis in adult offspring. Notably, male offspring exposed to gestational DEX exhibited a significant impairment in vascular NO synthesis capacity. This impairment is primarily due to a decrease in IP3R3 expression in fetal vasculature induced by gestational DEX stress, which persists from birth through adulthood. Furthermore, our findings provide robust evidence that gestational DEX-induced reprogramming of IP3R3 gene expression patterns is associated with alterations in DNA methylation status. This epigenetic modification leads to disruption of the IP3R3/Ca2+/eNOS signaling pathway, ultimately resulting in long-term impairment of endothelial NO synthesis in adult offspring. Collectively, these novel findings highlight that the detrimental effects of gestational glucocorticoid exposure on fetal vascular endothelial function persist into adulthood, offering crucial insights into the pathogenesis of vascular diseases.
During normal pregnancy, the placental glucocorticoid barrier serves to protect the developing fetus from excessive maternal glucocorticoid exposure. However, under certain pathological conditions, such as compromised placental barrier function or excessive maternal glucocorticoid exposure, these hormones can adversely affect fetal development. Clinical studies have demonstrated that prenatal administration of multiple courses of glucocorticoids in preterm infants is associated with significantly elevated blood pressure 1 week postnatally [ – ]. A longitudinal study involving nearly 300 preterm infants revealed a positive correlation between SBP at age 3 and umbilical cord blood glucocorticoid levels at birth [ ]. Animal studies have consistently shown that prenatal glucocorticoid exposure significantly increases the incidence of hypertension in rat offspring [ , ]. Our current findings extend these observations by demonstrating that adult offspring exposed to gestational glucocorticoids during mid-to-late pregnancy develop elevated SBP, DBP, and mean arterial blood pressure. Given that blood pressure regulation is fundamentally dependent on vascular tone and endothelial function [ , ], these findings have significant implications. While previous research has predominantly focused on the effects of gestational glucocorticoid exposure on vascular contractile function [ , ], our study provides novel insights into its impact on vasodilation mechanisms, particularly through the NO synthesis pathway.
NO, synthesized by eNOS, serves as a critical vasodilator secreted by VECs, playing an indispensable role in regulating vascular tone, blood pressure, and blood flow [ , ]. Within the cardiovascular system, eNOS expression demonstrates marked developmental stage-specific variations. While fetal eNOS expression remains relatively low, it undergoes progressive upregulation postnatally, reaching significantly higher levels in adult. Importantly, during fetal and early postnatal development, eNOS expression exhibits particular susceptibility to various environmental factors [ ]. This developmental plasticity suggests that gestational glucocorticoids exposure during late pregnancy may profoundly impact eNOS expression and NO synthesis in the fetal vascular system. The transplacental passage of excessive glucocorticoids during pregnancy enables their direct entry into fetal circulation, potentially compromising endothelial function through direct interaction with the vascular endothelium. However, the current understanding of the relationship between gestational glucocorticoid exposure and offspring endothelial NO synthesis, particularly regarding NO-dependent vasodilation, remains limited. The thoracic aorta, a principal conduit for systemic blood distribution, represents an ideal model for vascular studies due to its accessibility in fetal pups and its clinical relevance, as impaired thoracic aorta elasticity constitutes a significant risk factor for cardiovascular diseases. In this study, we established a gestational DEX-exposed rat model, which accurately reflects clinical scenarios of mid-to-late pregnancy DEX exposure in humans, enabling comprehensive investigation of fetal and offspring thoracic aorta dilation function.
Previous research has demonstrated sex-specific effects of gestational DEX exposure on offspring vascular systems, with male offspring exhibiting greater susceptibility to vascular dysfunction compared to female offsprings [ ]. Our preliminary investigations into the dose-dependent effects of gestational DEX exposure on NO-dependent vasodilation in fetal thoracic aortas revealed more pronounced adverse effects in male fetuses, thus justifying our focus on male offspring in this study. The observed sex-specific effects may be attributed to complex interactions between glucocorticoids and sex steroids. Existing evidence suggests that androgens can modulate glucocorticoid receptor expression and activity, while glucocorticoids interact with sex steroids through multiple mechanisms across various tissues, potentially influencing sex steroid-dependent developmental processes [ – ]. Consequently, glucocorticoids–sex steroid crosstalk is considered a crucial factor underlying the sex-dependent differences observed in offspring exposed to glucocorticoids during pregnancy [ , ]. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that sex-specific placental development trajectories and adaptive responses to stressful environments also contribute significantly to these differences. Rodent models, extensively utilized in placental development research, have demonstrated distinct growth and developmental patterns between male and female placentas, along with differential adaptive responses to environmental stressors [ , ]. These placental adaptations appear to be influenced by the nature, duration, and intensity of the stressor, as well as the specific developmental window of exposure. Notably, Kalisch-Smith et al. have proposed that perturbations occurring in the latter half of gestation may confer greater viability and growth advantages to male fetuses compared to those occurring during early placental development [ , ]. The precise mechanisms underlying these sex-dependent differences in offspring exposed to gestational glucocorticoids warrant further comprehensive investigation in future studies.
IP3R channels are intracellular Ca2+ release channels that respond to the second messenger IP3, facilitating precise modulation of cytoplasmic Ca2+ levels to maintain vascular tone and hemodynamic stability. The vascular system expresses three distinct IP3R subtypes (IP3R1, IP3R2, and IP3R3), each contributing to the complex regulation of vascular tone through diverse mechanisms [ ]. Our previous investigation demonstrated that gestational DEX exposure impairs mesenteric arterial function in offspring, a phenomenon associated with aberrant IP3R1 expression mediated by epigenetic modifications [ ]. In the present study, we extend these findings by demonstrating that gestational DEX exposure specifically modulates IP3R3 expression in the thoracic aorta endothelium of both fetal and offspring stages, without affecting IP3R1/2 expression patterns. The differential impact of gestational DEX exposure on vascular beds warrants particular attention. While mesenteric arteries, as prototypical peripheral resistance vessels, primarily regulate peripheral blood pressure and blood flow distribution, the thoracic aorta, being a major conduit artery directly connected to the heart, exerts significant influence on central blood pressure and pulse pressure characteristics. Our findings collectively suggest that gestational glucocorticoid exposure exerts tissue-specific effects on the expression of critical vascular genes in offspring, though the precise mechanisms underlying this vascular bed specificity remain to be fully elucidated.
The central finding of this study reveals that IP3R3 deficiency underlies impaired NO synthesis in DEX offspring. The observed reduction in both IP3R3 mRNA and protein levels suggests transcriptional inactivation as the underlying mechanism. Considering that epigenetic modifications can induce heritable changes in gene expression, we explored two principal epigenetic regulators: miRNAs and DNA methylation. While previous studies have established that miRNAs can suppress IP3R3 transcription and expression through mRNA binding [ ], our investigation revealed no significant involvement of miRNA-mediated regulation in this context. DNA methylation, a well characterized epigenetic mechanism governing gene transcription [ ], emerged as the principal regulatory pathway in our study. Clinical sample experiments have indicated that gestational DEX exposure can alter DNA methylation profiles in both placental and umbilical cord tissues [ , ]. Furthermore, animal model studies have consistently shown that gestational DEX exposure induces persistent DNA methylation imprints in offspring [ , ]. Our current investigation provides novel mechanistic insights by demonstrating a significant increase in the average methylation percentage within the IP3R3 gene promoter in VECs of offspring exposed to gestational DEX. Importantly, we established that DNA methylation inhibitors can effectively reverse both the suppressed IP3R3 expression and the compromised acetylcholine-induced vasodilation and NO synthesis in these offspring. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that gestational glucocorticoid exposure induces IP3R3 downregulation through DNA methylation of the gene promoter, ultimately leading to reduced NO synthesis and endothelial dysfunction in offspring. These results not only highlight the remarkable susceptibility of the fetal vascular system to gestational glucocorticoid exposure but also underscore the potential for long-term endothelial dysfunction in both local and systemic vascular systems persisting into adulthood, mediated by altered epigenetic patterns established during fetal development.
In conclusion, previous studies have predominantly focused on enhanced vascular contractile performance as a mechanism underlying elevated blood pressure in gestational glucocorticoid-exposed offspring [ , ]. Our findings highlight the pivotal role of vascular endothelial dysfunction, characterized by impaired NO synthesis in this pathological process. Through longitudinal investigation spanning from fetal development to adulthood, this study elucidates the persistent impact of gestational glucocorticoid exposure on endothelial NO synthesis in offspring vasculature. These findings not only advance our understanding of the mechanisms driving hypertension in gestational glucocorticoid-exposed offspring but also identify potential therapeutic targets for vascular disorders, particularly hypertension.
Despite these significant findings, several limitations of this study warrant consideration. Firstly, the exclusive focus on male offspring limits the generalizability of our findings to clinical populations. Future investigations should systematically examine sex-specific effects to provide a more comprehensive understanding of its long-term consequences. Secondly, while this study primarily investigated endothelial NO synthesis in the thoracic aorta, it did not explore other aspects of vascular endothelial function or different vascular beds. Thirdly, the mechanisms underlying gestational glucocorticoids-induced reprogramming of IP3R3 expression through DNA methylation require further elucidation. Furthermore, establishing a premature model of glucocorticoid exposure would be more clinically relevant, given the widespread use of glucocorticoids for promoting lung maturation in preterm infants. However, technical challenges in manipulating premature delivery have led most gestational glucocorticoids studies, including ours, to utilize full-term rat models. This represents a notable limitation of the current study. Nevertheless, our animal model successfully replicates the clinical scenario of four-course glucocorticoid treatment and demonstrates that the adverse effects of gestational glucocorticoid exposure on fetal vascular endothelial function persist into adulthood. To validate these findings, future clinical studies should investigate individuals exposed to multiple courses of gestational glucocorticoids during development.
In conclusion, this study contributes to the growing body of evidence regarding the developmental origins of vascular dysfunction and underscores the need for careful consideration of glucocorticoid administration protocols during pregnancy. The identification of IP3R3 as a key molecular target provides new insights into the mechanisms underlying gestational programming of vascular function and opens avenues for potential therapeutic interventions.
