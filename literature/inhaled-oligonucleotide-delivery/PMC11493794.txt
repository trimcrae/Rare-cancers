## Abstract
As an indispensable inflammatory mediator during sepsis, granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) facilitates neutrophil production by activating G-CSFR. However, little is known about the role of intracellular downstream signalling pathways in the induction of inflammation. To explore the functions of molecules in regulating G-CSFR signalling, RNA sequencing and integrated proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses were conducted to predict the differentially expressed molecules in modulating the inflammatory response after G-CSFR expression was either up- or downregulated, in addition to the confirmation of their biological function by diverse experimental methods. In the integrated bioinformatic analysis, 3190 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and 1559 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified in multiple-group comparisons (p < 0.05, FC >  ± 1.5) using enrichment analyses, as well as those classic pathways such as the TNF, NFkappaB, IL-17, and TLR signalling pathways. Among them, 201 proteins, especillay intercellular cell adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM1) and PKCa, were identified as potential molecules involved in inflammation according to the protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis, and the leukocyte transendothelial migration (TEM) pathway was attributed to the intervention of G-CSFR. Compared with the control and TNF-a treatment, the G-CSFR (G-CSFROE)-overexpressing led to an obvious increase in the number of leukocytes with the TEM phenotype. Mechanically, the expression of ICAM1 and PKCa was significantly up- and downregulated by G-CSFROE, which directly led to increased TEM; moreover, PKCa expression was negatively regulated by ICAM1 expression, leading to aberrant leukocyte TEM. Altogether, the ICAM1‒PKCa axis was found a meaningful target in the leukocyte TEM induced by G-CSFR upregulation.

## Introduction
G-CSF is an upstream regulator of neutrophil production that is in charge of the maturation and release of neutrophils to facilitate the induction of the inflammatory response. In acute lung injury (ALI), a variety of cell types, such as macrophages and endothelial cells, have the capacity to secrete G-CSF, and the levels of G-CSF in the plasma and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of patients with ALI are markedly elevated in response to various proinflammatory mediators, such as LPS, IL-6 and TNF-a, that are prominent in ALI. Emergency granulopoiesis is then induced through the activation of myeloid progenitors in the bone marrow to activate innate immunity and induce tissue repair (Kreisel et al. ). G-CSF can be recognized as an orchestrator of immune homeostasis and inflammation and a potential target in many clinical conditions involving infections (Yerushalmi et al. ; Rabaan et al. ; Moss and Rappaport ) such as COVID-19. Moreover, the mortality of sepsis-associated ALI in clinical settings is closely related to high levels of G-CSF (Rojahn et al. ), which directly leads to the hypothesis by some investigators that G-CSF might be an upstream cytokine in acute inflammation. However, some contrary studies reported that (Adage et al. ; Collins and Troeberg ) the knockout of G-CSF could inhibit vascular endothelial injury in septic mice, leading to favourable outcomes instead of further inflammatory damage (Suzuki et al. ). Therefore, it is important to clarify the underlying mechanism by which G-CSF regulates inflammatory signalling.
The inflammatory G-CSF signalling cascade is initiated by the binding of G-CSF to its transmembrane receptor (G-CSFR), but the levels of G-CSF does not parallel the level of G-CSFR expression under all conditions. Some reports have shown that exogenous G-CSF can induce the internalization of the ligand‒receptor complex, which directly results in the downregulation of the receptor (Alessandris et al. ); this partially indicates that G-CSF is not just a reactive cytokine that can respond to immune factors to induce the production of neutrophils, and that the G-CSFR is more important than G-CSF to be involved in the induction of inflammation. Therefore, increasing research attention has been given to G-CSFR. In this study, the downstream target of G-CSFR under inflammatory conditions are investigated.
Immune homeostasis depends heavily on a low baseline G-CSF concentration; if G-CSF concentrations were continuously high, the inflammatory response would be exacerbated and difficult to control. G-CSFRs are normally activated in conditions such as cancer and granulocytopenia, and the signalling pathways involved are JAK1/2-Tyk-STAT (Shimoda et al. ; Nicolson et al. ; Nicholson et al. ) and MAPK-PI3K (Zhu et al. ; Kamata et al. ). However, information on the signalling cascade downstream of G-CSFR activation and subsequent internalization in inflammatory conditions is limited. Although the mechanism by which G-CSF regulates neutrophil biology in noninflammatory contexts has been researched, the advantages or disadvantages of exogenous G-CSF application in ALI remain to be elucidated, as do the downstream targets of the G-CSFR.
In the present study, integrated bioinformatics analysis was used to clarify the mRNA, proteomic and phosphoproteomic changes in factors downstream of G-CSFR after G-CSFR was up- or downregulated in vitro. Through integrated analysis, PKCa was found to be the most promising downstream molecule regulated by G-CSFR under TNF-α stimulation. Furthermore, G-CSFR-ICAM1 signalling could strongly influence the expression level of PKCa. Moreover, the leukocyte transendothelial migration (TEM) phenotype is markedly altered by the ICAM1-PKCa axis under G-CSFR inflammatory signalling.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture and reagents
HULEC-5a cells (human lung microvascular endothelial cell line-5a, cat. no. CL-0565, Wuhan, China), primary cells (PRI-H-00006) and HEK-293 T cells (cat. no. CL-0005) were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., and cultured with the corresponding medium and in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. HL-60 cells (cat.no. ZQ0433) were purchased from ScienCell Company. All experimental cells used were passaged fewer than 5 times. anti-PKCa (cat. no. ab32376, rabbit monoclonal), anti-ICAM1 (cat. no. ab282575, rabbit monoclonal) and anti-G-CSFR (cat. no. ab156878, rabbit monoclonal) antibodies were purchased from Abcam and used for western blotting. An ICAM-1 inhibitor (Selleck, 10 μM A-205804) was purchased from Selleck. Go 6976, a PKCa inhibitor, was purchased from Abcam (cat. no. ab141413).

## RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis
After the total RNA was extracted from the three groups (control vs. TNF-a vs. TNF-a + GCFSR, triplicate samples), the quality of the RNA was evaluated using a NanoDrop ND-1000 instrument. A total of 1 ~ 2 μg of total RNA was selected for each sample to construct the RNA sequencing library according to specific protocols. The quality of the constructed library was evaluated using an Agilent2100 Bioanalyzer and quantified via qPCR. The mixed sample libraries were sequenced via the DNBSEO-T7 platform, and the examined reads were retrieved. The sequencing quality of the reads was evaluated via FastQC software, and the reference genome was compared via HISAT2 software. Transcript-level expression analysis was performed using StringTie software with reference to official database annotation information. The fragments per kilobase of transcript per million (FPKM) values of each gene were calculated via the R software Ballgown, and the expression differences at the transcript level were calculated. Finally, the genes that were differentially expressed between the screened samples were identified. In terms of data mining, principal component analysis (PCA), correlation analysis, clustering analysis, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis were performed in RStudio.

## Protein extraction and digestion
For omics samples, cells were randomly divided into the TNF-α and KD groups and treated accordingly. Commercial SDT buffer was used to lyse the cells after they were washed with ice-cold PBS, and the total protein content was evaluated after centrifugation. After the protein content was quantified via the BCA method, the protein was further digested with trypsin in accordance with the specific protocols of Matthias Mann. Consequently, the samples were desalted using desalting cartridges (Empore™ SPE Cartridges, volume 3 ml, C18, bed I.D. 7 mm, Sigma), concentrated by vacuum centrifugation and reconstituted in formic acid (0.1% (v/v), 40 µl).

## SDS‒PAGE
For protein quantification, an appropriate number of samples from each group were dissolved in SDT lysis buffer, and the samples were boiled in 5X loading buffer for 10 min. Afterwards, 10 µg of protein was added to each lane of a 4‒12% SDS‒PAGE gel (150 V, 90 min; FutureGEL, cat. no. F0001gel). After the proteins were transferred to a membrane, the protein bands were verified by Coomassie blue staining (R-250).

## Filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) digeston procedure
Proteins samples (200 µg) in SDT buffer were ultrafiltered several times to remove DTT and detergent using UA buffer (150 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 8 M urea). Iodoacetamide (100 μl, 100 mM IAA) was added and incubated in the dark for 30 min to block the reduced cysteine residues, and then the suspensions were collected after washing (three times with 100 μl of UA buffer and two times with 100 μl of 25 mM NH4HCO3 buffer). Finally, 4 μg of trypsin was used to digest the samples overnight at 37 °C with NH4HCO3 buffer (40 μl, 25 mM), and the filtrates full of peptides were retained. The filtrates were transferred to cartridges as previously described, concentrated via centrifugation, and reconstituted in formic acid (40 µl of 0.1% (v/v)). To quantify the peptide content, UV light (280 nm, spectral density) with a 0.1% (g/l) extinction coefficient solution was used to calculate the content of tryptophan and tyrosine in the protein filtrates.

## Immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC) for phosphopeptide enrichment
In addition to the enrichment of ordinary peptides, a HighSelect™ Fe-NTA Phosphopeptide Enrichment Kit was used to evaluate the enrichment of phosphopeptides in each sample according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After lyophilization, the phosphopeptides were redissolved in 20 µL of formic acid for mass spectrometry (MS) analysis.

## Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC‒MS/MS) analysis
A Nanoelute (Bruker Daltonics) liquid chromatography system and a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer were used to conduct the LC‒MS/MS analysis for 60 min. Briefly, the dissolved peptides and phosphopeptides were loaded onto a 1.9 μm C18 reversed-phase analytical column (C18, 75 μm inner diameter, 25 cm long) in buffer A, which was filled 0.1% formic acid, and separated by buffer B, which was composed of 0.1% formic acid and 84% acetonitrile, at a flow rate of 300 nl/min with a linear gradient. For the MS procedure, positive ion mode was selected, ion mobility MS spectra over a mass range of m/z 100–1700 and 1/k0 of 0.6 to 1.6 were pooled, and additionally, thresholds of 2500 and 10 cycles of PASEF MS/MS with a target intensity of 1.5 k were used. Finally, the release time of 0.4 min was activated as needed.

## Identification and quantitation of overlapping proteins
For each omics analysis, the raw data of each sample were gathered and imported into MaxQuant software (version 1.6.14) to analyse the protein information with the help of the UniProt database (the item of database: uniprot_mouse_76417 represents, species: mouse, http://www.uniprot.org/). The false discovery rate (FDR) threshold in terms of protein and peptide content was P ≤ 0.01, and the software settings and database parameters can be found in supplementary file 1.

## Cluster analysis of phosphorylated peptides
On the basis of the previously quantified results, the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) and peptides were screened accordingly with the criteria of P < 0.05 and a fold change > 1.5. The clustering analysis of those targets was subsequently conducted via Cluster 3.0 software (http://bonsai.hgc.jp/~mdehoon/software/cluster/software.htm) and Java Treeview (http://jtreeview.sourceforge.net). During hierarchical clustering, the Euclidean distance algorithm and average linkage clustering algorithm were used, and a heatmap was constructed to visualize the data.

## Motif, subcellular localization and domain analysis
With the use of MeMe software (http://meme-suite.org/index.htm), the underlying amino acid sequences were undermined from the 13 amino acid sites, which were composed of six upstream/downstream amino acids and the modified site. On the basis of these sequences, the target motif was predicted according to parameters such as width, occurrence, and background (species). The CELLO system, a multiclass support vector machine (SVM) classification platform (http://cello.life.nctu.edu.tw/), was applied to explore the subcellular localization of all DEPs. For protein domain prediction, protein sequence annotation was conducted on the InterPro member database (Pfam) of the InterProScan software.

## GO enrichment analysis
NCBI BLAST + client (ncbi-blast-2.2.28 + -win32.exe) and InterProScan software were used to retrieve homologous sequences of the DEPs, and the sequences were annotated via Blast2GO software. GO (http://geneontology.org/, annotation 773,921)enrichment analysis of the DEPs was performed, and Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the target proteins to the all-quantified proteins in the database. In addition, subsequent Benjamini‒Hochberg correction was conducted for adjusted p values. Finally, only the statistical results with p values less than 0.05 were considered significant. The GO enrichment results were obtained and visualized via RStudio software.

## KEGG enrichment analysis
The annotated proteins were uploaded to the online KEGG database (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/), to investigate the involved pathways. The same analysis method was applied as above.

## Protein‒protein interaction (PPI) analysis
To analyse the interactions among of the identified proteins, the protein data was uploaded onto the STRING website (http://string-db.org/) or the IntAct molecular interaction database (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/intact/). The retrieved results were subsequently imported into Cytoscape software (http://www.cytoscape.org/, version 3.2.1) to construct a PPI network via a specific algorithm. Eventually, hub proteins were chosen for further analysis because of their importance in the interaction network.

## Inflammatory model
Primary endothelial cells were pretreated with 100 ng/ml TNF-α for 6 h to induce a cellular inflammatory response on the basis of methods reported in a previous study (Kamianowska et al. ), and the inflammatory status was verified by the expression of cellular inflammatory mediators. As in vitro intervention agents, Go 6976 and A-205804 were added to the medium for 24 h before sample collection. The animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of JXMC (PUMC2023-012). The in vivo sepsis model was established by the intraperitoneal injection of LPS (20 μg/g) into male C57BL/6 mice for 24 h after the corresponding AVV2- virus (negative control or G-CSFR specific virus) was administered for 4 weeks. The virus, an endothelial-specific G-CSFR-overexpressing or knockdown AVV, used in in vivo mouse model, was constructed by Hanbio Biotechnology through the intratracheal injection of 75 µl.

## TEM assay
The TEM assay was performed in a 24-well plate to evaluate endothelial cell infiltration. Briefly, diverse single-cell suspensions of endothelial cells (2.5*105 cells/ml)were generated via different experimental protocols. Next, 200 µl of the suspensions were seeded into the upper chamber of a Transwell plate with an 8 µm pore size PET membrane inserted (BD Biosciences). The medium used contained 1% antibiotics and no serum. After they reached confluence, endothelial cells were stimulated with TNF-α (10 ng/ml) for 6 h, after which differentiated HL-60 cells were added to the monolayer. Next, 500 µl of high-serum medium (20% FBS) containing the chemoattractant fMLP was added to the lower chamber. At the end of the procedure, the number of cells that migrated to the lower chamber was calculated accordingly.

## Lentivirus transduction
The lentivirus, which included ZsGreen-PURO and G-CSFR-ZsGreen-PURO, was packaged by Hanbio and had a virus titre not less than 1.0*108 TU/ml. To overexpress the G-CSFR protein, HULEC cells were incubated in 6-well plates overnight to reach above 60% confluence at 24 h, after which the virus was added the control, NC, and G-CSFR groups at the optimal MOI value (MOI = 2 from the pilot experiment). Polybrene was also added, the cells were incubated for at least 24 h, and then the medium was changed to fresh standard medium. After the cells were incubated for 48 h, the effectively infected cells were selected by adding 2 μg/ml puromycin to the medium. Finally, the selected G-CSFR-overexpressing cells were used for the next experiment.

## Transient overexpression and siRNA procedure
For the overexpression plasmid, the cDNA of the human gene PKCa was subcloned and inserted into the pcDNA3.1 vector to construct the PKCa‐overexpressing plasmid (pcDNA3.1‐PKCa) by the Qingke Company. HULEC cells at 70% confluency seeded in 6-well plates were transfected with the pcDNA3.1 vector or pcDNA3.1‐PKCa in media containing 2% FBS and 1% antibiotics. Lipofectamine® 3000, P3000 (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) and the plasmid were diluted with OptiMEM according to the manufacturers’ protocols, and the ratio of plasmid to Lipofectamine® 3000 was 1:2. After 6 h, the medium was changed to standard DMEM, and the cells were incubated for another 48 h until the expression level was checked. For the siRNA experiment, a specific sequence targeting ICAM1 (CCGGTATGAGATTGTCATCAT) was used to construct siICAM1, which was transfected into endothelial cells according to the manufacturer's protocol. Successful transfection was confirmed after 48 h by western blotting.

## Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT‒qPCR)
The cells were washed with PBS, and total RNA was subsequently extracted via the TRIzol method. After the total RNA concentration was determined using a Nanodrop instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), reverse transcription was conducted. Next, the qPCR experiments were performed with a commercial kit (PrimeScriptRT Master Mix [Real Time], RR036A and TB Green Premix DimerEraser [Perfect Real Time], RR091A), following the manufacturer’s instructions (TaKaRa Biomedical Technology [Beijing] Co., Ltd.). In brief, up to 0.5 μg of RNA was used to produce cDNA. Reactions were conducted in a 10 μl volume containing Master Mix and 2 μl of cDNA. The reaction parameters were 40 cycles of (1) 95 °C for 1 min; (2) 95 °C for 5 s; and (3) 60 °C for 1 min. The relative expression levels of each gene were adjusted to those of the housekeeping gene GAPDH. The primers used in this study were as follows: G-CSFR(F): 5’-CAGCTTGACTCAAAATTCCTGGA-3’, G-CSFR(F):5’-GGTACAAGGGAGTCACGATGATC-3’; ICAM1 (F): 5’- ATGCCCAGACATCTGTGTCC-3’, ICAM1 (F):5’- GGGGTCTCTATGCCCAACAA-3’; and GAPDH(F): 5’-AGGTCGGTGTGAACGGATTTG-3’, GAPDH(F): 5’- GGGGTCGTTGATGGCAACA-3’.

## Western blotting
To determine the relative protein expression levels in vitro, whole-cell lysates were generated by incubating the cells in strong RIPA lysis buffer combined with a protease cocktail. Afterwards, the total protein concentrations were determined, and approximately 20 μg of lysate sample was loaded per lane and separated on a 12.5% SDS–PAGE gel. Proteins were subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes and incubated in milk plus 5% bovine serum albumin for 1 h. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight in a 4 °C rotator. The primary antibodies used were diluted 1:1000. After the samples were washed 3 times for 10 min each, a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody was added, and the samples were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Finally, specific protein bands were visualized via an enhanced bioluminescence instrument (Bio-Rad, USA) with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate.

## Statistical analyses
All the data are presented as the means ± standard errors of the means (SEMs) if they were normally distributed. GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA) was used to analyse the results. The analysis was conducted with a two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t test or ANOVA based on the involved groups, followed by Tukey comparisons if appropriate. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## G-CSFR was found to regulate the leukocyte TEM signalling under TNF-a stimulation
The RNA-seq results revealed that the average distribution of the data matrix was comparable across the three groups, and PCA and correlation analysis revealed consistent sample profiles within the groups and different sample profiles among the groups (Fig.  a-c). A total of 498 and 367 DEGs were found to be upregulated and downregulated in the TNF-a vs. control group comparison, respectively, on the basis of the criteria p value < 0.05, FPKM >  = 0.5 and FC >  ± 1.5. Additionally, 1528 and 797 DEGs were found to be upregulated and downregulated in the TNF-a_G-CSFR vs. TNF-a group comparison, (Fig.  d-h). To clarify the functions and signalling pathways associated with these DEGs, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the DEGs identified in both group comparisons were conducted according to gene number and -log10 values (p value). As indicated in Fig.  a-b, the GO enrichment analysis revealed that the DEGs in the control vs. TNF group comparison were associated primarily with the inflammatory response, defence response to virus and protein binding; the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that the TNF and NFkappaB signalling pathways were enriched in these DEGs. For the DEGs in the TNF vs. TNF-G-CSFR group comparison, a different picture appeared (Fig.  c-d), and the enriched signalling pathways were mainly the PI3K-Akt and MAPK pathways rather than the TNF and cytokine‒cytokine receptor pathways, and the GO analysis results revealed similar enriched terms. In addition, compared with the KEGG gene set, which refers to the inflammatory response in both comparisons, the leukocyte TEM pathway was strongly downregulated in the TNF group compared with the TNF-G-CSFR group, whereas it was upregulated in the control group compared with the TNF group (Fig.  e-f). Therefore, this bioinformatics analysis revealed that G-CSFR could be an effective player in the inflammatory response and especially in the leukocyte TEM pathway.
 

## Proteomic analysis results
After the LC‒MS/MS procedure and quantification of DEPs, all 5410 DEPs were retrieved accordingly, including 5357 DEPs in the TNF-α group and 5332 DEPs in the KD group. Among them, 5279 proteins were common to both groups (Fig.  a). The PCA results comparing the similarities and differences of samples among the groups clearly illustrated in Fig.  (Fig.  b). Among the quantified results, 5354 proteins and 32,530 peptides were found among all the groups, as visualized by the flow chart (Fig.  c, supplementary file 1). On the basis of the criteria of a FC above 2 and a p value less than 0.05, 322 upregulated proteins and 372 downregulated proteins were screened, and the statistical results and volcano plot are shown in Fig.  (Fig.  d-e, supplementary files 2–3). The results of the GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of all the DEPs revealed that the DEPs were enriched in the molecular function term “binding” and the biological process term “cellular, metabolic processes or biological regulation”. Additionally, the ECM‒receptor interaction was most enriched signalling pathway (Fig.  f‒g).
 

## Phosphoproteomic analysis results
The phosphoproteomic analysis (supplementary file 1) revealed 4330 phosphorylated proteins, 11,054 phosphorylated peptides, and 17,915 phosphorylated sites, in this study. In particular, there were 6574 phosphorylated peptides and 8139 phosphorylated peptides on 3113 modified proteins. For the KD and TNF group comparison, the shared phosphorylated proteins and phosphorylated peptides identified across all the samples are shown in Fig.  (Fig.  a-b). The raw data matrix of the screening results can be found in supplementary file 4. With the abovementioned screening criteria, 437 upregulated phosphoproteins and 328 downregulated phosphoproteins were found for further enrichment analysis (Fig.  c). Briefly, the enriched KEGG pathways were mainly the “tight junction pathway, adherens junction pathway and regulation of the actin cytoskeleton pathway” among the 20 selected signalling pathways. The results of the GO enrichment analysis revealed that the DEPs associated with “protein binding” were involved in many biological processes, such as “positive regulation of multiorganism process, cell junction assembly” and associated with the cellular component “adherens junction” (Fig.  d-e). Additionally, the cluster analysis in Fig.  f revealed obvious differences in expression among the groups and was highly consistent across the samples. Finally, the 20 most abundant domains of the DEPs were identified, and the “SAM domain” was found to be highly enriched in this analysis (Fig.  g). For the analysed peptides, the key motif was located on serine residues, and this motif accounted for 83.8% of all amino acid modifications (Fig.  h-i). Details about the phosphorylation site properties and motif analysis can be found in supplementary file 5.
 

## The protein correlation analysis revealed PKCa as a target protein
A protein correlation analysis was conducted to explore the expression patterns of proteins, modified proteins and kinases, with the aim of identifying target molecules of G-CSFR under inflammatory conditions. Through correlation analysis, 793 DEPs and 1717 differentially expressed phosphoproteins were screened on the basis of their individual datasets; however, only 201 associated proteins were found to be correlated among the 2 omics analyses (Fig.  a). Among these 201 correlated proteins, 41 proteins were stably upregulated in both analyses and 42 proteins were downregulated in both analyses (Fig.  b, supplementary file 6). All the changes in the expression of these overlapping DEPs are shown in Fig.  c. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses (Fig.  d-e) revealed that the 201 correlated proteins were enriched mainly in the functions of “phospholipid binding” and “regulation of cell junction” and participated in the “tight junction” and “leukocyte transendothelial migration” signalling pathways. This result is consistent with that of the previous RNA-seq analysis, which revealed that G-CSFR might function in the leukocyte TEM process. Finally, PPI analyses were applied to identify the hub proteins among the 201 proteins involved in the downstream signalling cascade of G-CSFR. As shown in Fig.  f, TP53, EGFR, CAV1, PRKCA, ANXA2, and ITGB4 were the top hub proteins; however, considering the cell properties of HULEC cells, which are typically used in combination with other cell lines, PRKCA was chosen as a target protein in addition to cancer-related molecules such as TP53 and EGFR.
 

## Overexpression of G-CSFR increase the inflammatory response and leukocyte TEM
The lentivirus-transfected endothelial cells were lysed and subjected to mRNA and protein expression analyses to ensure successful overexpression of G-CSFR (G-CSFROE). The results can be found in supplementary file 7. First, to further elucidate the process involved in the progression of inflammation, leukocyte TEM was examined via Transwell migration experiments in which the leukocyte cells were fully differentiated with 1.3% DMSO for 5 days. Trypan blue staining was used to confirm leukocyte viability, and the number of migrated cells in each group was also determined (Fig.  a). The results showed that TNF-α stimulation can promote the TEM of leukocyte cells; moreover, there was a 1.6-fold increase in the number of migrated cells in the TNF-a + G-CSFR group compared with the TNF-a group (Fig.  b,  < 0.05). Additionally, compared with that in the control group, TNF-α stimulation in the TNF group greatly increased the inflammatory response by increasing the mRNA expression of IL-6, IL1B, and NFkappaB. Furthermore, the overexpression of G-CSFR in the TNF + G-CSFR group resulted in a far greater elevation of these inflammatory mediators than that in the TNF group (Fig.  c,  < 0.05). The number of neutrophils in the also clearly increased in inflamed lung tissue of sepsis model mice when the specific AVV-G-CSFR was administered intratracheally (Fig.  d,  < 0.05). As a result, these results suggest that G-CSFR leads to the overexpression of NFkappaB, IL-6, IL1B and promote leukocyte TEM.
 

## Overexpression of G-CSFR leads to an increase in ICAM1 levels
The adhesion of neutrophils to endothelial cells is a vital step in the TEM process; thus, VCAM1 and ICAM1 are indispensable participants in mediating the inflammatory response. Therefore, the expression of ICAM1 from the adhesion molecule family was investigated in the subsequent experiments. The mRNA, protein expression and fluorescence staining results revealed that ICAM1 was strongly increased by G-CSFROE after TNF-α administration in vitro (Fig.  a-e). The expression of the hub protein PKCa was significantly greater in the TNF-α stimulation group than in the control group; however, a marked decrease was detected in the TNF + G-CSFR group when endothelial cells were stimulated with TNF-α after G-CSFROE transfection (Fig.  f-g).
 

## ICAM1 negatively modulates PKCa expression in G-CSFR signalling
To further clarify whether ICAM1 or PKCa is the upstream mediator of G-CSFR signalling, a cDNA3.1 PKCa plasmid and ICAM1 siRNA were constructed, respectively. As shown in Fig.  a, the mRNA expression of ICAM1 did not change in stably transfected endothelial cells under TNF-α stimulation when PKCa was overexpressed, and the immunofluorescence staining results were similar (Fig.  d). However, PKCa expression increased when ICAM1 siRNA was transiently transfected into endothelial cells 6 h after TNF-α was administered (Fig.  b). Moreover, in the TEM experiment, the inhibition of ICAM1 by siICAM1 decreased the number of leukocytes that migrated, which was induced by TNF-a + G-CSFR treatment in the lower chamber of the plate, whereas this effect was reestablished by the addition of PMA, a PKCa activator, for 3 h (Fig.  c). The protein expression of PKCa was also negatively regulated by an ICAM-1 inhibitor (Selleck, 10 μM A-205804) under similar inflammatory conditions as previously described (Fig.  d). Taken together, these findings suggest that PKCa is negatively regulated by ICAM1 in G-CSFR signalling induced by TNF-α stimulation in vitro.
 

## Thein vivoknockdown of G-CSFR on endothelial cells in lung of mice regulate the inflammation and expression of ICAM1-PKCa
To fulfil the function of G-CSFR, the rescue experiment was conducted in in vivo animal model with the downregulated G-CSFR expression by AVV2-TIE-shG-CSFR (Fig.  a). Apart from the significant injury of mouse lung in LPS group, the knockdown of G-CSFR on endothelial markedly decrease the injury of lung tissue compared to the LPS group (Fig.  b). Meanwhile, the level of inflammatory mediator in blood, such as IL-6, was decreased sharply by the interreference with G-CSFRkd (Fig.  c). As for neutrophil’s expression profile on this context, it's obvious that G-CSFRkd led to lesser infiltration of neutrophil in lungs compared to LPS treatment, which might be attributed to a lesser migration of neutrophil across the vessel (Fig.  d). The downstream molecular expressions of ICAM1 and PKCa in septic lungs was also verified by western blot, it turned out the knockdown of G-CSFR induced the down-and up expression of ICAM1 and PKCa, respectively (Fig.  e-f).
 

## Discussion
For many years, G-CSF has been a promising therapy for curing agranulocytosis through interactions with myeloid cells, but its exact impact on nonhaematopoietic cell types is still unclear. In particular, G-CSF has a variety of functions in immunity and host defence during infection; however, the downstream cytoplasmic signalling pathway that determines the development of disease following the activation of G-CSFR is unknown, as controversy about the advantages or disadvantages of exogenous G-CSF application still exists. Therefore, some researchers strongly suggest that efforts should be made to regulate the expression of G-CSFR on different immune cell types without changing the expression of G-CSF to mimic immune responses in the body. In the present study, G-CSFR on the endothelial cell surface was found to be involved in TEM, an initial stage of the immune response that mediates the interactions between neutrophils and endothelial cells, in an inflammatory cell model induced by TNF-α or LPS stimulation. The downstream molecule ICAM1 also plays an important mechanical role in modulating PKCa expression during cytoplasmic signalling.
In the initial phase of the inflammatory response, G-CSF functions as a marker of inflammation and is overexpressed systemically, which directly results in the secretion of neutrophils in peripheral blood. However, how do neutrophils arrive at the infected site? This process depends on the interaction between neutrophils and endothelial cells. Adhesion molecules, chemokine molecules and tight junction proteins are all important factors for facilitating leukocyte migration into the circulation (Grönloh et al. ; Filippi ). Rapid adhesion and migration are the first steps, and with the assistance of chemotactic agents, such as CXCL1, CXCL8, LTB4, and C5a, more neutrophils can be recruited to amplify their response (Wang et al. ). Moreover, the route for leukocyte migration to the endothelium surface is not random but rather specifically recognized (Hyun et al. ). Among them, adhesion molecules, such as ICAM1 and VCAM1, are the most studied factors in this process (Singh et al. ). These proteins are normally expressed at very low levels in noninflamed tissues. However, their expression is markedly increased upon inflammation, which may influence the capture and crawling of immune cells and thereby modulate diapedesis. Therefore, many studies have focused on changing the expression of adhesion molecules to study the effects of such interventions on leukocyte TEM (Wu et al. ; Wang et al. ). In the context of ALI, Nurlu et al. (Nurlu Temel et al. ) reported that leukocyte TEM biomarkers such as MMP-2, TIMP-1, and ICAM-1 were robustly increased in an in vivo ALI model induced by LPS. Filippi and Singh (Filippi ; Singh et al. ) also reported that some classic adhesion molecules, including ICAM-1/2, VCAM-1, JAM-A/C and PECAM-1, are expressed by endothelial cells and sequentially participate in the TEM process to promote leukocyte migration; additionally, VCAM1 and ICAM1 are major modulators in inflammatory diseases. Tao Fu (Fu et al. ) reported that VEGFR2 is an indispensable agent in diapedesis when the PECAM/VE-cadherin/VEGFR2 complex formed via its phosphorylation site at the Y1175 residue. Moreover, some studies have shown that G-CSF has a limited effect on the TEM of inflamed endothelial cells or that the magnitude of this effect is much smaller than that of classic neutrophil chemoattractant, such as TNF-a and IL-8 (Eissner et al. ; Smith et al. ; Li et al. ). However, G-CSF is still a powerful stimulator of transmigration and is able to influence neutrophil recruitment. Therefore, we hypothesize that G-CSFR plays an unknown role in this process by binding to G-CSF.
In the bioinformatics analysis of our study, changes in the inflammatory response were extensively investigated by means of G-CSFR was upregulated or downregulated. For the DEGs in the TNF vs. TNF-G-CSFR group comparison, some genes function crucially in inflammation and immunology were retrieved including IFNL2, ZBP1, IFNL3, CCL3L1, et al. Through investigating their biological properties, it’s easy to find out that they can directly or indirectly influence the interaction between immune cells and endothelial cells. This result was consistent with another bioinformatics analysis using the GEO database by Schneider (Schneider et al. ), which revealed that CSF3R was enriched in the immune response, leukocyte recruitment and migration processes in a mastitis model. Through further enrichment analysis, some enriched signalling pathways, such as the PI3K-Akt pathway, MAPK pathway and leukocyte TEM signalling pathway, were found to confer an extraordinary function compared to those conventional pathways in control vs TNF-a comparison. It’s well known that PI3K-Akt pathway and MAPK pathway have diverse functions in transmitting intracellular signalling, influencing the development of inflammatory response. Popielarczyk et al. (Popielarczyk et al. ) discovered that the activation of PI3K/Akt and MAPK signalling have an important influence on the mobilization and migration of endogenous MSCs to injury sites through the endothelial lining. Cooperated with present results, it might be the fact that the upregulation of G-CSFR activated the inflammatory signalling, PI3K/Akt and MAPK signalling to improve the leukocyte TEM.
In the integrated proteomic analysis, the most abundant proteins among the 201 integrated family are AMOT, DAXX, IRS4 et al. For them, tight junction and the MAPK signalling pathway are primarily involved pathways. Through participating these signalling, they can regulate diverse cellular processes. Tight junction is one of the proprietary features in the cellular barrier that has the ability to block the transfer channels between endothelial cells, thereby impediments intercellular molecular movement. This result was consistent with that of Wang et al.’s study (Wang et al. ) which found MAPK/PI3K signalling pathway was altered to influence the tight junction of intestine in septic rats, and eventually the gut microbiota. So, all in all, the leukocyte TEM signalling pathway was identified as the major cause of differential expressed protein, and the hub molecule was identified as PKCa by this study. Therefore, to our knowledge, this is the first study investigating the role of G-CSFR signaling in the inflammatory response and leukocyte TEM, nevertheless, inherent molecules such as ICAM1, VCAM1 and PCKa, and the interactions among them, have yet to be studied in this context.
PKCa, also known as PRKCA, is a well-known inflammatory mediator that plays a protective role in various types of inflammatory diseases (Zhu et al. ; El-Banna et al. ; Wang et al. ; Roos and Berkholz ). A limited number of studies have reported that the overexpression of PKCa decreases inflammation in macrophage modelsand in vivo ALI models, and the pathways through which the inflammatory response is attenuated are the MAPK or ERK1/2 signalling pathways (Zhu et al. ; Wang et al. ). However, there is another scenario in which inflammation is promoted by endothelial cells. Kerrin et al. (Roos and Berkholz ) reported that the upregulation of PKC activity in endothelial cells is necessary to increase the production and secretion of IL-6 and IL-8. Zuraidah et al. (Abdullah and Bayraktutan ) also demonstrated that the suppression of PKCα could be highly valuable for relieving the injury of brain microvascular endothelial cells stimulated by TNF-a. In our present study, a Transwell experiment was used, and TEM was confirmed to be closely associated with the overexpression of G-CSFR, which directly led to the secretion of proinflammatory mediators such as IL6 and IL1B from endothelial cells. For example, the percentage of transmigrated cells (differentiated HL-60) in the G-CSFR + TNF-a group increased 1.6-fold compared with that in the TNF-a group, and ICAM1 was significantly upregulated, accompanied by a decrease in PKCa levels. In contrast, in the rescue experiment, the abovementioned results changed in a PKCa-dependent manner. These results are consistent with those of Lin’s report on the in vivo leukocyte migration process, which involved the modulation of PKCa in lung inflammation (Lin et al. ). In addition, other studies (Mathisen et al. ; Huang et al. ; Chen et al. ) have indicated that NFkappaB and ICAM1 are sequentially modulated by intracellular PKCa under TNF-α stimulation, which coincides with our results. Taken together, the results of these functional experiments demonstrated that the ICAM1‒PKCa axis potentially regulates the TEM process and inflammation following stimulation with TNF-a + G-CSFR.
Some limitations should also be addressed in this study. First, the recombinant G-CSF was not used to overexpress G-CSFR in this study because exogenous G-CSF might not induce the upregulation of the receptor, but sometimes can induce the downregulation of the receptor through internalization. Second, the phosphorylation of PKCa was not examined in our study, and further mechanistic studies may be needed; however, this topic is not included in the present research scope. Finally, the findings of the present study will inspire future research on this topic.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, PKCa was found to be a promising mediator of the leukocyte TEM process and the inflammatory response following changes in G-CSFR and ICAM1 expression when endothelial cells are activated by TNF-α.
