## Abstract
This study aims to investigate the role of Interferon Regulatory Factor-8 (IRF8) in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and its underlying mechanisms. A COPD mouse model was established by cigarette smoke exposure, which revealed the aberrant upregulation of IRF8 in lung tissues, with IRF8 expression being associated with genes related to inflammation and cellular senescence. In a COPD mouse model, knockdown of IRF8 significantly alleviated lung injury. This was demonstrated by a reduced lung index, improved emphysema, decreased inflammatory cell infiltration, and lower levels of inflammatory factors. Furthermore, IRF8 knockdown inhibited cell senescence in lung tissue, as characterized by downregulated expression of markers (p16, p53, p21), reduced SA-β-gal activity, and modulation of the Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. In vitro experiments demonstrated that the expression of IRF8 was increased in human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) treated with cigarette smoke extract, while knockdown of IRF8 could suppress cigarette smoke extract-induced inflammatory responses and cellular senescence, and the protective effects of IRF8 knockdown could be reversed by Akt activation. Further studies showed that IRF8 was transcriptionally regulated by FOXM1, which could bind to the IRF8 promoter, and FOXM1 overexpression increased IRF8 expression, while FOXM1 knockdown decreased it, and FOXM1 regulated cigarette smoke extract-induced inflammation and senescence by modulating IRF8. In summary, IRF8 promotes the progression of COPD through the transcriptional regulation by FOXM1, which subsequently affects inflammatory responses and cellular senescence, providing a potential therapeutic target for COPD.

## Introduction
Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) is a chronic respiratory disease closely associated with cell senescence. Its main pathological features include chronic airway inflammation, airway remodeling, emphysema, and progressive decline in lung function [ ]. Cigarette smoke is the most common environmental risk factor for COPD. Long-term exposure to cigarette smoke can induce chronic pulmonary inflammation and oxidative stress, which in turn lead to DNA damage in bronchial epithelial cells and accelerated cellular senescence [ – ]. Cellular senescence, an irreversible state of cell cycle arrest, plays an important role in the development of COPD [ , ], but its specific regulatory mechanisms have not been fully elucidated.
Interferon Regulatory Factor-8 (IRF8), a key member of the IRF family, plays pivotal roles in immune regulation, cell growth, and apoptosis. Accumulating evidence implicates IRF8 in the regulation of inflammatory responses and cellular senescence across various disease contexts. Overexpression of IRF8 can exacerbate liver inflammatory injury, while its knockdown can alleviate cisplatin-induced renal tubular cell apoptosis [ , ]. Additionally, IRF8 has been shown to promote senescence in fibroblasts and lung cancer cells, suggesting its potential regulatory function in aging-related diseases [ , ]. However, whether IRF8 is involved in the pathogenesis of COPD, especially in CS-induced pulmonary inflammation and cellular senescence, remains unreported.
The transcription factor FOXM1 has been widely implicated in regulating cell proliferation and genomic integrity [ , ]. Cigarette smoke exposure upregulates the FOXM1 expression in the lung tissues, and FOXM1 deficiency can alleviate airway inflammation and goblet cell metaplasia [ , ]. Notably, FOXM1 has also been found to regulate the expression of senescence-related genes such as p21 and promotes chondrocyte senescence [ , ]. Through bioinformatics analysis (JASPAR database), we predicted that FOXM1 may directly bind to the promoter region of IRF8, suggesting a potential regulatory relationship between the two factors. However, it remains unclear whether FOXM1 mediates COPD inflammation and cellular senescence through IRF8.
Based on the above research background, we hypothesize that IRF8 may be a key downstream factor of FOXM1 in regulating COPD inflammation and cellular senescence. This study aims to investigate the role of IRF8 in a COPD model induced by cigarette smoke and further elucidate the molecular mechanisms in pulmonary inflammation and cellular senescence, with the expectation of providing a new theoretical basis for targeted therapy of COPD.

## Materials and Methods

## Vector Construction
Recombinant adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors were constructed and produced using the shuttle plasmid pAV-U6-shRNA-CMV-RFP (Vigene Biosciences, pAV100004-KD). AAV5-shNC was used as the negative control. A short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting mouse IRF8 was cloned into the BamHI/HindIII restriction sites. The shRNA sequence was: 5’-ccgGCCATATAAAGTTTACCGAATttcaagagaATTCGGTAAACTTTATATGGCttttt-3’. The The packaging system included pHelper (Takara, 6230) and the AAV5 serotype plasmid pRC5 (Fenghui Biotechnology, ZT336). Transfection was carried out using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000015). AAV-293 cells (Saibakang Biotechnology) were cultured in DMEM (Servicebio) supplemented with 10% FBS and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Transfection was performed when cells reached approximately 50% confluence in 10 cm culture dishes. Viruses were harvested 72 h post-transfection by lysing cells through freeze-thaw cycles followed by filtration through a 0.45 μm filter.

## Animal Model
The COPD model was established as previously described with modifications [ , ]. Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into control and cigarette smoke (CS)-exposed groups (6 replicates/group). The COPD model was established through 16-week cigarette smoke exposure (5 days/week) as previously described with modifications. Briefly, mice were placed in a 63 × 55 × 45 cm exposure chamber and subjected to mainstream smoke from commercially available filtered Hongta cigarettes (10 mg tar, 10 mg CO, and 0.9 mg nicotine per cigarette). Each daily exposure session consisted of two 90-minute periods (9:30 AM and 2:00 PM, separated by a 3-hour smoke-free interval) with 18 cigarettes burned per session, delivered via a smoking machine connected to the chamber. Control mice received filtered air under identical conditions. The mice in the model group were exposed for 5 days a week for 16 weeks. Viral delivery was performed two weeks prior to modeling via intratracheal administration of adeno-associated virus (5 × 1010 particles in 25 µL 0.9% NaCl). After 16 weeks of exposure, mice were weighed and sacrificed 24 h after the final cigarette smoke exposure for subsequent lung tissue collection and analysis, with the experimenters blinded to group assignment during tissue collection, histological assessment, and quantitative analyses. Animals were excluded from the final analysis based on predefined criteria, including unexpected death unrelated to experimental procedures, or the development of severe infection/complications that could confound experimental outcomes.

## mRNA Sequencing
The mRNA sequencing of lung tissues was performed by Novogene. Initially, the quality of the mRNA samples was assessed using Agilent 2100 to precisely determine RNA integrity. Subsequently, library construction was initiated. After the RNA samples passed the quality control, poly(A) mRNA was enriched using Oligo(dT)-conjugated magnetic beads. The mRNA was then fragmented using a fragmentation buffer. Utilizing the fragmented mRNA as a template, first-strand cDNA was synthesized with random hexamers. Next, second-strand cDNA was generated by adding buffer, dNTPs, DNA polymerase I, and RNase H. The double-stranded cDNA was purified using AMPure XP beads. The purified cDNA was subjected to end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation. Subsequently, AMPure XP beads were used for size selection of the fragments. The selected fragments were then amplified via PCR, and the PCR products were purified using AMPure XP beads to obtain the final library. After library construction, the library concentration was initially measured using Qubit 2.0. The library was diluted, and the insert size was assessed using Agilent 2100. Once the insert size met the expected range, the effective concentration of the library was accurately determined using Q-PCR to ensure library quality. Upon passing the quality control, the libraries were pooled according to the effective concentration and the desired sequencing output. The pooled libraries were clustered on a flowcell using cBOT and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

## Bioinformatics Analysis
Raw sequencing data (FASTQ format) were generated from high-throughput sequencing and demultiplexed using bcl2fastq2. Initial quality assessment was performed using FastQC. Next, raw reads were filtered to obtain high-quality clean reads, which were then aligned to the Mus_musculus.GRCm39 reference genome using HISAT2. Alignment quality was assessed using Qualimap. Gene-level quantification was performed using HTseq. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 with standard parameters, applying negative binomial generalized linear models and Wald tests for significance. Genes with p-value < 0.05 and |log2(fold change) | > 1 were considered differentially expressed. All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using R packages.

## HE Staining
Fixed tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and subsequently infiltrated with paraffin by incubation in a xylene-paraffin mixture at 60 °C for 2 h. The tissues were then embedded in molten paraffin within a metal mold and allowed to solidify into blocks. Serial Sect. (5 μm thick) were obtained using a microtome, mounted onto slides, and dried. For H&E staining, the sections were first deparaffinized and rehydrated through xylene and a descending ethanol series. The slides were then stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, rinsed in distilled water, and briefly differentiated in 1% acid alcohol. After counterstaining with eosin for 3 min, the sections were dehydrated through an ascending ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with a resinous medium. Finally, stained tissue sections were examined under a light microscope for histological evaluation.

## Preparation of Cigarette Smoke Extract
Cigarette smoke extract was prepared as follows: 10 mL of cell culture medium was placed in a 50 mL centrifuge tube. Two glass tubes were attached to the tube lid, one connected to a 50 mL syringe and the other to a cigarette. The cigarette was lit, and smoke was drawn into the tube by pulling the syringe plunger to aspirate 40 mL of smoke. One cigarette was used per 10 mL of medium. After the entire 40 mL of smoke from one cigarette was introduced, the original lid was replaced with a new airtight cap, and the tube was vigorously vortexed for at least 30 s. This resulting solution was considered 100% cigarette smoke extract. The cigarette smoke extract was then filtered through a 0.22 μm filter and used within 30 min of preparation.

## Cell Experiments
Human bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells, obtained from iCell Bioscience, were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. For in vitro modeling, BEAS-2B cells were treated with 8% cigarette smoke extract (CSE) for 6 h. siRNAs targeting IRF8 or FOXM1, along with their negative control (siNC), were designed and synthesized. An IRF8 overexpression plasmid (oeIRF8) and its corresponding empty vector (Vector) were also constructed. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. At 24 h post-transfection, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 8% cigarette smoke extract for 6 h. Cells and supernatants were then collected for subsequent analysis. In some experiments, transfected cells were pretreated with 10 µM SC79 for 2 h after transfection, followed by exposure to 8% cigarette smoke extract for 6 h before sample collection.

## Real-Time PCR
Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using TRIpure reagent, and RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer. Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized from the extracted RNA via reverse transcription. For quantitative real-time PCR, reactions were performed in a 20 µL system containing 1 µL cDNA template, 0.5 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 0.5 µL SYBR Green, 10 µL 2× Fast Taq Plus PCR Master Mix, and ddH₂O to adjust the final volume. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 10 s, and extension at 72 °C for 15 s. A melting curve analysis was conducted from 60 °C to 94 °C (increment of 1 °C per second) to verify amplification specificity. Gene expression levels were normalized to the reference gene GAPDH, and relative quantification was performed using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. The primer used in this study: homo-IRF8-F 5’-GAGCCATACAAAGTTTACCGA-3’, homo-IRF8-R 5’-AGCGACCGCACTCCATC-3’; homo-CDKN2A-F 5’-TTCCTGGACACGCTGGTGGT-3’, homo- CDKN2A -R 5’-GCATCTATGCGGGCATGGTTA-3’; homo-CDKN1A-F 5’-CGGGATGAGTTGGGAGGAG-3’, homo-CDKN1A-R 5’-TGAGCGAGGCACAAGGGTA-3’; homo-TP53-F 5’-ACCACCATCCACTACAACTACAT-3’, homo-TP53-R 5’-CACAAACACGCACCTCAAA-3’; mouse-IRF8-F 5’-AACGTGGCGTGCTACTGC-3’, mouse-IRF8-R 5’-CTCGGATGAACTGGTTGGTG-3’; mouse-CDKN2A-F 5’-ATGGGTCGCAGGTTCTT-3’, mouse-CDKN2A-R 5’-GCACCGTAGTTGAGCAGA-3’; mouse-CDKN1A-F 5’-TTCCGCACAGGAGCAAAG-3’, mouse-CDKN1A-R 5’-AAGTCAAAGTTCCACCGTTCT-3’; mouse-TP53-F 5’-ATGACGGAGGTCGTGAGA-3’, mouse-TP53-R 5’-GGATAGGTCGGCGGTTC-3’.

## Western Blot
Total proteins were extracted from tissues or cells using RIPA lysis buffer, and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit. The protein samples were then separated by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto a PVDF membrane. After blocking with 5% skim milk, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies (IRF8 antibody, dilution 1:300; p-AktSer473 antibody, dilution 1:500; Akt antibody, dilution 1:1000; p-mTORSer2448 antibody, dilution 1:1000; mTOR antibody, dilution 1:500; p-4EBP1Thr37/Thr46 antibody, dilution 1:3000; 4EBP1 antibody, dilution 1:3000; p-S6Ser235/236 antibody, dilution 1:3000; S6 antibody, dilution 1:3000. FOXM1 antibody, dilution 1:1000; GAPDH antibody, dilution 1:20000) overnight at 4 °C, followed by thorough washing and incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies (goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP, dilution 1:3000; goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP, dilution 1:3000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL substrate, and the optical density of the target bands was quantitatively analyzed with a gel imaging system (Gel-Pro-Analyzer software) to assess protein expression levels.

## Immunofluorescence
For tissue sections, 5 μm paraffin-embedded slices were dewaxed in xylene (I and II) and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in antigen retrieval buffer for 10 min using a low-temperature microwave method. After blocking with 1% BSA, the sections were incubated with primary antibody (IRF8 antibody, dilution 1:50; KRT5 antibody, dilution 1:100) in a humidified chamber at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the slides were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibody (FITC-Goat Anti-Mouse IgG, dilution 1:200; Cy3-Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG, dilution 1:200) for 60 min at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and the sections were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium after PBS washes. Immunofluorescence signals were visualized under a fluorescence microscope. For cell immunofluorescence, cultured cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 30 min at room temperature, and blocked with 1% BSA. Primary and secondary antibody incubations (p21 antibody, dilution 1:100; FITC-Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG, dilution 1:200) were performed as described above, followed by DAPI nuclear staining and mounting for fluorescence imaging.

## Immunohistochemistry
For immunohistochemical analysis, 5 μm paraffin-embedded tissue sections were first dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in antigen retrieval buffer at low power for 10 min, followed by cooling to room temperature. After washing with PBS, endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubating the sections with 3% H2O2 for 15 min at room temperature. Non-specific binding was blocked with 1% BSA, after which the sections were incubated with primary antibody (p21 antibody, dilution 1:100) in a humidified chamber at 4 °C overnight. Next, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (HRP-Goat Anti-rabbit IgG, dilution 1:100) for 45 min at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using DAB chromogen, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. Finally, sections were dehydrated through an alcohol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with resinous medium for microscopic examination.

## Giemsa Staining
Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid was centrifuged, resuspended in PBS, and adjusted to an appropriate cell density for counting. Smears were prepared on glass slides, fixed in methanol, and stained sequentially with Giemsa A (1 min) and Giemsa B (7 min) solutions. After brief washing and differentiation in 80% ethanol, slides were air-dried and examined under light microscopy. Differential cell counts were performed by evaluating 200–300 nucleated cells per sample based on standard morphological criteria.

## β-galactosidase Staining
Tissue samples were embedded in OCT compound and frozen for cry sectioning. 10-µm sections were cut, transferred to slides, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. For β-galactosidase staining, sections were fixed, washed, and incubated with staining solution at 37 °C overnight in a humidified chamber. After washing, slides were cover slipped and stored at 4 °C for microscopic analysis. The staining clearly visualized β-galactosidase activity while preserving tissue morphology.

## ELISA
Mouse TNF-α ELISA Kit (EM0183), Mouse IL-1β ELISA Kit (EM0109), Mouse IL-1α ELISA Kit (EM0108), Human TNF-α ELISA Kit (EH0302), Human IL-1β ELISA Kit (EH0185), and Human IL-1α ELISA Kit (EH0184) were used in this study. All these kits were purchased from FineTest (Wuhan, China).

## Dual-Luciferase Assay
An IRF8 promoter luciferase reporter plasmid (pGL3-basic) containing different promoter fragments and a FOXM1 overexpression plasmid (oeFOXM1) were constructed. BEAS-2B cells were transfected with these plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000. Four experimental groups were designed: cells co-transfected with either the short IRF8 promoter fragment (−980 to + 35) or the long promoter fragment (−1860 to + 35), along with either the empty vector control (Vector) or oeFOXM1. At 48 h post-transfection, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Firefly luciferase signals were normalized to Renilla luciferase activity for each sample.

## ChIP-PCR
Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were performed following standard protocols with minor modifications. Briefly, protein-DNA complexes were cross-linked using 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min, followed by quenching with glycine. Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed in SDS Lysis Buffer supplemented with 1 mM PMSF protease inhibitor. Chromatin was fragmented to 200–500 bp fragments using a sonicator with three 10-second pulses at 50 W (30% maximum power) with 30-second cooling intervals between pulses. After pre-clearing with Protein A/G Agarose/Salmon Sperm DNA for 30 min at 4 °C, chromatin aliquots were immunoprecipitated overnight at 4 °C with 1 µg of specific antibody or appropriate negative control IgG. Immunocomplexes were washed sequentially with low-salt, high-salt, and LiCl wash buffers, followed by TE buffer. Cross-links were reversed by incubation with 200 mM NaCl at 65 °C for 4 h, followed by proteinase K digestion at 42 °C overnight. DNA was purified and quantified by real-time PCR using specific primers. The ChIP-PCR primers are listed below: chip-1-F, 5’-GCTCCTGTTCTTGGCACTA-3’, chip-1-R, 5’-CGTGTCATTGGCAGTAGGT-3’; chip-2-F, 5’-CCAGTGTCGTTCTCCCTC − 3’, chip-2-R, 5’-ACTGAAACAGGAGCATCG-3’.

## Statistical Analysis
Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality of data distribution within each group was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test was used if data passed both normality and equal variance assumptions; otherwise, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test was applied. For comparisons among more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed data, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For non-normally distributed multi-group data, the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test was used. Statistical significance was defined as *p* < 0.05. In figures, p-values are denoted by asterisks: *p* < 0.05, **p* < 0.01, ***p* < 0.001.

## Results

## Cigarette Smoke Exposure Induces Aberrant Upregulation of IRF8 Expression in Lung Tissue
A mouse model of COPD was established through cigarette smoke (CS) exposure. Representative lung images from control and COPD groups are shown in Fig.  A. Compared with the control group, CS-exposed lungs exhibited darker coloration and an increased lung index (Fig.  B). H&E staining revealed emphysematous changes and an increased mean linear intercept (MLI) in the model group, indicating typical pathological features of COPD (Fig.  C-D). Subsequently, mRNA sequencing was performed to analyze gene expression profiles in lung tissues. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed clear separation between the two groups (supplementary Fig.  A). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the thresholds |log₂FC| ≥ 1 and p < 0.05, and visualized by volcano plot and heatmap (supplementary Fig.  B-C). A total of 1383 upregulated and 494 downregulated genes were identified. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of these DEGs revealed several significantly enriched gene sets, including GO:0032609 (interferon-gamma production), GO:0002263 (cell activation involved in immune response), and GO:0002285 (lymphocyte activation involved in immune response) (Fig.  E). Using “cell senescence” as a keyword, related genes were retrieved from the GeneCards database and intersected with the GSEA-derived gene sets. As shown in Fig.  F, five overlapping genes were identified, among which IRF8 was significantly upregulated in the COPD model (Fig.  G). Given the important role of IRF8 in immune regulation and cellular senescence, we focused on IRF8 to investigate its function and underlying mechanisms in COPD. The increased expression of IRF8 was further confirmed by real-time qPCR and Western blot (Fig.  H-I). Moreover, we validated this upregulation and observed its co-localization with the epithelial cell marker KRT5 through immunofluorescence double staining (Fig.  J), pointing to its potential role in regulating epithelial cell functions in disease.
 

## Knockdown of IRF8 Alleviates Lung Injury in COPD Mice
To further elucidate the role of IRF8 in COPD, AAV carrying IRF8-specific shRNA was intratracheally administered to knock down IRF8 expression in mice. Pathological changes in lung tissue were subsequently evaluated. IRF8 knockdown alleviated cigarette smoke-induced pulmonary damage. Specifically, the elevated lung index induced by cigarette smoke exposure was significantly reduced upon IRF8 knockdown (Fig.  A). Histological examination via H&E staining revealed that IRF8 knockdown attenuated emphysema and inflammatory infiltration in COPD mice (Fig.  B). IRF8 knockdown conferred a protective effect on alveolar architecture. This was evidenced by a significant increase in the mean alveolar number (Fig.  C) and the preservation of finer, more densely packed alveolar structures, in contrast to the pathologic alveolar enlargement seen in the COPD group (Fig.  D). Additionally, IRF8 knockdown attenuated lung inflammation in COPD mice, as reflected by lower histopathological scores (Fig.  E). The efficiency of IRF8 knockdown in lung tissues was confirmed by real-time qPCR and Western blot (Fig.  F-G). We next investigated inflammatory changes in lung tissues of the COPD model. As shown in Fig.  H-L, cigarette smoke exposure led to significant accumulation of inflammatory cells-including neutrophils, eosinophils, macrophages, and lymphocytes-in the alveoli. When IRF8 was knocked down, the number of inflammatory cells decreased markedly, suggesting that IRF8 promotes inflammatory cell infiltration. Similarly, levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-1α were elevated in cigarette smoke-exposed lung tissues, whereas IRF8 knockdown suppressed the production of these pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig.  M-O).
 

## Knockdown of IRF8 Attenuates Cigarette smoke-induced Lung Senescence
Cellular senescence is a key driver of COPD progression. We found that cigarette smoke exposure upregulated the expression of senescence-related markers CDKN2A (p16), TP53 (p53), and CDKN1A (p21) in lung tissues (Fig.  A-C). However, absence of IRF8 significantly reduced their expression. A consistent trend was observed in p21 immunohistochemical staining (Fig.  D). Furthermore, senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity, a specific marker of cellular senescence, was elevated in the lungs of COPD mice, indicating substantial cell senescence in COPD lung tissues (Fig.  E). IRF8 knockdown effectively suppressed cellular senescence, as evidenced by reduced SA-β-gal-positive area. In addition, Western blot analysis revealed increased levels of phosphorylated Akt (p-AktSer473) and its downstream signaling molecules, including p-mTORSer2448, p-S6Ser235/236, and p-4EBP1Thr37/46, in COPD mouse lungs, with no significant changes in their total protein levels (Fig.  F). This suggests a cigarette smoke-induced activation of the Akt/mTOR pathway. IRF8 knockdown attenuated the phosphorylation of these key nodes, thereby inhibiting the activation of this entire signaling axis.
 

## Knockdown of IRF8 Suppresses Cigarette Smoke-Induced Inflammatory Response and Senescence in Human Bronchial Epithelial Cells
An in vitro model was established by treating human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) with 8% cigarette smoke extract. Following cigarette smoke extract treatment, IRF8 expression was significantly upregulated in BEAS-2B cells (Fig.  A-B). To investigate the functional role of IRF8, its expression was knocked down using siRNA. The efficiency of IRF8 knockdown was confirmed by Western blot analysis compared to the siNC (Fig.  C). Also, in contrast to control (siNC) cells, IRF8 knockdown cells failed to upregulate IRF8 protein expression following 8% CS exposure (Fig.  D). We observed that cigarette smoke extract increased the secretion of inflammatory factors, while IRF8 knockdown suppressed this effect (Fig.  E-G). Additionally, exposure to cigarette smoke extract elevated the expression of senescence-related markers (CDKN2A, TP53, and CDKN1A), and IRF8 knockdown attenuated this upregulation (Fig.  A-C). Immunofluorescence staining revealed a higher number of p21-positive cells in the exposed group, whereas IRF8 knockdown reduced p21 positivity (Fig.  D). Furthermore, cigarette smoke extract exposure enhanced SA-β-gal activity, indicating induced cellular senescence (Fig.  E). In contrast, IRF8 knockdown decreased SA-β-gal activity, suggesting a suppression of cell senescence. Similarly, cigarette smoke extract exposure activated the Akt/mTOR pathway, and IRF8 knockdown inhibited this activation (Fig.  F).
 To investigate whether the function of IRF8 is mediated through Akt activation, a rescue experiment was performed using SC79, a specific Akt pathway activator, in BEAS-2B cells. As expected, reversed the suppressive effect of IRF8 knockdown on the secretion of both TNF-α (Fig.  A) and IL-1β (Fig.  B). In terms of cellular senescence, SC79 treatment counteracted the effects of IRF8 knockdown, reducing the mRNA expression of CDKN2A and CDKN1A (Fig.  C-D), which coincided with a recovery in SA-β-gal activity (Fig.  E). This confirms that the Akt pathway acts as a critical downstream mediator of IRF8 in regulating cellular senescence.
 

## FOXM1 Modulates Cigarette Smoke-Induced Inflammation and Senescence Via IRF8
Furthermore, we identified FOXM1 as a potential upstream regulator of IRF8. FOXM1 was enriched in the KEGG pathway “Cellular senescence” and the GO term “DNA damage response, signal transduction by p53 class mediator resulting in transcription of p21 class mediator” (Fig.  A-B). FOXM1 expression was significantly upregulated in cigarette smoke-induced COPD models in vivo (Fig.  C). This upregulation was recapitulated in vitro, where a similar increasing trend was observed in CS-treated BEAS-2B cells (Fig.  D). Based on the JASPAR prediction that the transcription factor FOXM1 binds to the IRF8 promoter, we hypothesized that FOXM1 regulates IRF8 transcription. To test this hypothesis, we first constructed a FOXM1 overexpression plasmid and confirmed its transfection efficiency (Fig.  E). Subsequently, dual-luciferase reporter assays were conducted by co-transfecting BEAS-2B cells with this plasmid and IRF8 promoter constructs of varying lengths. The results showed that FOXM1 overexpression enhanced IRF8 promoter activity (Fig.  F), suggesting that FOXM1 may transcriptionally regulate IRF8 expression. Moreover, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-PCR experiments confirmed the direct binding of FOXM1 to the IRF8 promoter region (Fig.  G).
 Functional rescue experiments were further conducted to validate the FOXM1–IRF8 regulatory axis. siRNA targeting FOXM1 and an IRF8 overexpression plasmid were designed and transfected into BEAS-2B cells, with knockdown and overexpression efficiencies confirmed by Western blot (Fig.  A-B). Cells were then transfected with siFOXM1 alone or in combination with oeIRF8, followed by exposure to 8% CS (Fig.  C). It was found that FOXM1 knockdown reduced the secretion of inflammatory cytokines, while IRF8 overexpression restored the inflammatory response induced by cigarette smoke extract (Fig.  D-E). Additionally, FOXM1 knockdown suppressed the mRNA expression of CDKN1A and CDKN2A (Fig.  F-G). SA-β-gal staining indicated that FOXM1 knockdown attenuated cigarette smoke extract-induced cellular senescence, whereas IRF8 overexpression increased SA-β-gal activity (Fig.  H). The role of the FOXM1–IRF8 axis in regulating Akt/mTOR pathway activation was also confirmed (Fig.  I).
 

## Discussion
COPD is a major global health concern with significant morbidity and mortality, and cigarette smoke is widely recognized as its primary etiological factor [ , ]. Understanding its underlying mechanisms is crucial for developing effective diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. In this study, we identified the IRF8 as a novel and pivotal mediator of cigarette smoke-induced lung injury. We further elucidated a previously unrecognized signaling axis wherein FOXM1 transcriptionally upregulates IRF8, which in turn exacerbates inflammation and senescence. Mechanistically, we identified that IRF8 exerts these effects primarily by activating the Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, a finding that reveals a novel and context-dependent role for IRF8 in chronic lung disease.
Persistent pulmonary inflammation and accelerated cellular senescence are key drivers of COPD pathogenesis [ ]. Cigarette smoke activates immune cells, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory mediators that directly damage airway and alveolar cells, perpetuating a cycle of tissue injury [ – ]. Concurrently, smoke induces cellular senescence, a state of cell-cycle arrest in which cells remain metabolically active and secrete inflammatory and matrix-degrading factors, further amplifying damage and impairing repair [ , ]. This vicious cycle of inflammation and senescence underlies the characteristic airway remodeling and emphysema seen in COPD.
Within this context, our study establishes a crucial role for IRF8 in the COPD pathogenic network. This extends prior evidence implicating IRF8 in inflammatory diseases, such as its role as a hub gene in psoriasis and rheumatoid arthritis [ , ]. Our results confirm that IRF8 acts as a central upstream regulator orchestrating the chronic inflammatory responses in COPD. We observed that IRF8 expression is significantly elevated following cigarette smoke exposure in both in vivo and in vitro models. Functional experiments demonstrated that knockdown of IRF8 markedly attenuates cigarette smoke-induced pathology, reflected by improved lung index, reduced alveolar destruction, decreased inflammatory cell infiltration, and lower levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This positions IRF8 as a functionally significant driver of COPD pathology, consistent with its pro-inflammatory roles in other chronic diseases. Akt/mTOR pathway is a key signaling node known to integrate inflammatory and survival signals. We further discovered that IRF8 downregulation inhibited the p-Akt, p-mTOR, p-S6, and p-4EBP1, suggesting that the pro-inflammatory action of IRF8 is mechanistically linked to its ability to activate the Akt/mTOR pathway.
Beyond inflammation, a key and novel finding of our study is the role of IRF8 in promoting cellular senescence, and this effect was similarly dependent on Akt activation. Cigarette smoke exposure led to a significant increase in the expression of senescence markers and SA-β-gal activity, all of which were suppressed upon IRF8 knockdown. Importantly, the protective effect of IRF8 knockdown on senescence was largely reversed by the Akt activator. This suggests that IRF8 is an active contributor to the senescence program in lung parenchyma and airway epithelium. This finding is supported by previous studies indicating that IRF8 mediates the senescence-associated secretory phenotype and that its upregulation can drive cellular senescence [ , ]. Thereby, IRF8 facilitates the persistent tissue remodeling and functional decline observed in COPD.
The role of IRF8 as an upstream Akt activator in our model contrasts with its reported function as an Akt suppressor in certain cancers [ ], highlighting the context-dependency of transcriptional regulators in disease. In the setting of chronic CS-induced inflammation, IRF8 appears to be reprogrammed to sustain pathogenic signaling. This aligns with its role as a necessary Akt activator in other processes, such as immune cell chemotaxis and atheroprotective macrophage polarization [ , ]. The plasticity of this axis is further evidenced by the opposite relationship in multiple myeloma, where Akt activation silences IRF8 [ ]. This contextual view reconciles disparate findings and positions the IRF8-Akt node as a malleable circuit shaped by the disease microenvironment.
Furthermore, we sought to identify the upstream regulator responsible for the cigarette smoke-induced overexpression of IRF8. Through bioinformatic analysis and experimental validation, we discovered that the transcription factor FOXM1 is directly involved in controlling IRF8 expression. FOXM1 was itself upregulated by cigarette smoke, consistent with reports that implicate FOXM1 in driving inflammatory responses and cellular senescence in other pathological contexts [ , , ]. Our mechanistic studies confirmed that FOXM1 binds to the IRF8 promoter and enhances its transcriptional activity. The functional relevance of this regulatory relationship was solidified by rescue experiments, wherein the anti-inflammatory and anti-senescence effects achieved by FOXM1 knockdown were nullified by concurrent overexpression of IRF8. This series of experiments defines a coherent FOXM1-IRF8-Akt/mTOR axis in the pathogenesis of COPD.
Notably, our finding that FOXM1 drives CS-induced senescence appears to contrast with its reported anti-senescent role in naturally aged human dermal fibroblasts [ ]. This discrepancy highlights a central concept in biology that the function of key regulatory molecules is context-dependent. Emerging evidence supports that in pathological stress-induced senescence associated with chronic diseases, FOXM1 is often reprogrammed to promote cell survival and pathological phenotypes. Mikawa et al. [ ] reported that FOXM1 accumulation in senescent cells driven by oncogenic or metabolic stress suppresses the pro-apoptotic protein BIM and enhances DNA repair, thereby strengthening senescent cell survival and pathological injury. Similarly, in osteoarthritis, upregulation of FOXM1 by HNRNPD directly promotes chondrocyte senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction [ ]. Thus, our work extends this paradigm to COPD, revealing how CS hijacks FOXM1 to activate a unique pro-inflammatory senescence program via IRF8.
The current study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the reliance on mouse models and an immortalized human bronchial epithelial cell line (BEAS-2B), although well-established in COPD research, may not fully capture the complexity of human disease. Future validation using primary human airway epithelial cells and clinical specimens from COPD patients would strengthen the clinical relevance of our findings. Second, while we identified Akt/mTOR activation as a key downstream event in IRF8-mediated inflammation and senescence, the exact mechanism by which IRF8 regulates Akt phosphorylation remains an open question. Whether this interaction is direct or involves additional intermediaries offers an interesting direction for further mechanistic investigation. Finally, although rescue experiments in vitro support our conclusions, future studies employing tissue-specific IRF8 knockout mice or in vivo Akt/mTOR modulation could provide more comprehensive physiological insights into this pathway.

## Conclusion
Collectively, the present work demonstrated a novel signaling axis-FOXM1-IRF8-Akt/mTOR as a critical mechanism in COPD. Cigarette smoke exposure significantly upregulates the expression of FOXM1, which in turn transcriptionally activates IRF8 by directly binding to its promoter region. Elevated IRF8 promotes pulmonary inflammation and cellular senescence, both hallmarks of COPD pathogenesis, primarily through activating the Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. These findings not only deepen the understanding of COPD progression but also highlight IRF8 as a potential diagnostic biomarker and promising therapeutic target for COPD intervention.
