## Abstract
Although significant progressions in antiviral studies of IFNβ have been demonstrated, the role of the proteasome in modulating cross-talk between TBK1-IFNβ signaling and viral replication during viral infection is not fully elucidated. Here, we discover that deficiency of REGγ, a proteasome activator, significantly reduces IFNβ production and increases viral replications in mice, leading to increased mortality in virus infection models. Our mechanistic study indicates that REGγ interacts with and degrades the protein phosphatase subunit Protein Phosphatase 2 Catalytic Subunit Beta (PPP2CB). This degradation disrupts the dephosphorylation of TBK1 and its interaction with IRF3, resulting in the activation of IFNβ-mediated antiviral signaling. In response to viral infection, up-regulation of REGγ in macrophages accelerates the degradation of PPP2CB, which increases the activation of TBK1-IRF3-IFNβ axis and thereby restricts viral replications and pathology. Interestingly, IFNβ enhances REGγ expression in viral infection, forming a positive feedback regulatory loop. In conclusion, our work demonstrates that REGγ is a positive modulator of IFNβ signaling during antiviral response, highlighting that this procedure is regulated via REGγ degradation of PPP2CB and provides a new insight into the coordination between antiviral response and proteasome activity. Thus, REGγ-proteasome activity and phosphatase PPP2CB may be potential targets in host defense against viruses.

## Introduction
Infectious disease pandemics have remained predominantly viral since the Spanish flu of 1918. In 2019, Coronavirus disease (COVID-19) ravaging worldwide, causing more than 775 million confirmed cases and more than 7 million deaths as to March 24, 2024 [ ]. With the emergence of different variants, the increasing rate of virus infection remains a threat to human health. Although vaccination is the most effective method of controlling viruses, the prediction of constantly mutating immunogenic epitopes and challenges in vaccine production limits the efficacy and availability of vaccination. In addition, increasing mutations in antiviral-resistant strain, especially in RNA viruses, restrict the development of universal and effective interventions against viral infections, therefore there remains an urgent public health need. The innate immunity is the first line of host resistance to encounter invaded pathogens. Type I interferon (IFN-I) is a central player in antiviral innate immune response to both DNA and RNA viruses. Targeting fine modulation of IFN-I signaling may be a potential but crucial antiviral strategy in clinical practice.
IFN-Is are induced mainly by three families of innate immune systems, including the Toll-like receptor (TLR) family, the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), and the intracellular retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I)-like receptor (RLR) family [ , ]. Insufficient IFN-I production leads to chronic infection, whereas excessive IFN-I causes unwanted tissue damage [ , ]. Therefore, IFN-I signaling must be temporally and spatially coordinated to activate an effective antiviral response so that infection is resolved without host pathology to avoid immune dysfunction. TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), as the downstream of TLRs, RLRs and DNA receptors [ ], activates the NF-kB signaling and interferon regulatory factors (IRFs), especially IRF3, consequently triggering the production of IFN-I [ ]. TBK1 is tightly controlled by post-translational modification, including protein phosphorylation or ubiquitination. For instance, TBK1 undergoes self-association and autophosphorylation at Ser172 under the catalysis of glycogen synthase kinase-3 (GSK3) during virus infection [ ]. The activation of TBK1 can also be triggered via K63-linked ubiquitination mediated by mind bomb E3 ligase 1 (MIB1), MIB2, and ring finger protein 128 (RNF128) [ ]. However, few mechanisms are known for the regulation of TBK1 by intracellular proteasome activity and phosphatases.
The REG/11S proteasome activator family consists of REGα, REGβ and REGγ [ ]. Among them, REGα and REGβ can recognize antigens and directly participate in immune regulation, but not REGγ (also known as PSME3, PA28γ and Ki) [ ]. REGγ, which is a proteasome activator by binding to and activating the 20S proteasome, promotes the degradation of a variety of target proteins in a ubiquitin- and ATP-independent manner, with functions in multiple cellular pathological processes [ ]. Our previous study found that REGγ also participates in the regulation of dendritic cell antigen presentation through degradation of the target protein and plays an important role in autoimmune diseases [ ]. As a ubiquitin- and ATP-independent manner proteasome activator, REGγ is not only a key regulator of tumor growth and metastasis [ ], but also controls premature aging and neurodegenerative diseases [ , ]. REGγ regulates different signaling pathways by degrading key substrate proteins, including the mTORC1-HIF1α, NF-κB, YAP and STAT3 pathways [ , – ]. Meanwhile, several studies have suggested that REGγ is involved in immune regulation in response to lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced immune stress [ ] or antimicrobial effects [ ]. However, the role of REGγ in innate immunity and host defense against infection by viral pathogens remains largely unknown.
Here, we have discovered that REGγ plays a role in regulating IFN-I production and host antiviral responses, at least to some extent, through the TBK1-IRF3 axis. PPP2CB is found as a bridge molecule between REGγ and IFN-I signaling, retaining TBK1 in an inactive state by dephosphorylation. Mechanistically, ablation of REGγ inhibits TBK1-IRF3 pathway activation by increasing the stability of PPP2CB in vitro and in vivo. Interestingly, a high level of REGγ and a low level of PPP2CB are observed upon viral infection or stimulation of IFNβ, reflecting a specific positive feedback loop in viral infection, which mediates posttranslational modification of TBK1.

## Results

## REGγ enhances IFNβ expression for antiviral responses
To investigate the role of REGγ-proteasome activity in innate antiviral immunity, we generated REGγ knockout (REGγ−/−) mice (Fig. a). Firstly, bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) from WT and REGγ−/− mice were infected with the RNA virus vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) to detect the expression of IFN-I. We discovered that the expression of IFN-I (IFNβ and IFNα4) mRNA and protein (IFNβ) were significantly lower in REGγ-deficient BMDMs than in WT (Fig.  a and b). Also, VSV induced IFNβ expression in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. b). It has been shown that intracellular RNA virus is mainly recognized by RIG-I to produce IFN-I, so we stimulated BMDMs with the polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid [poly(I:C)], a mimic of RNA viral nucleic acid, to detect the influence of REGγ on RIG-I-associated signaling. The results displayed that intracellular poly(I:C)-induced IFNβ production was significantly decreased in REGγ knockout BMDMs (Fig.  c).
 Subsequently, we sought to investigate whether REGγ is specific for RNA viruses or affects the immune response to different kinds of viruses. WT and REGγ-deficient BMDMs were infected with the DNA virus herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1). In line with the above results, the expression of IFNβ mRNA and protein in infected-REGγ−/− BMDMs were also significantly reduced compared with WT BMDMs (Fig.  d and e), as well as in REGγ-deficient peritoneal macrophages (PEMs) (Fig.  f and g). We also obtained similar results by stimulating BMDMs with the lipopolysaccharides (LPS) (Fig. c).
To further verify the effect of REGγ on IFNβ production, human embryonic kidney (HEK)−293 T cells were transiently transfected Flag-REGγ (Fig. d and e). We found that VSV-induced expression of IFNβ (mRNA and protein) was dramatically enhanced in REGγ-transfected HEK-293 T cells (Fig. f and g). Consistent with the cellular level, REGγ deletion mice produced much lower IFNβ in serum (Fig.  h and i), liver, lung and spleen (Fig.  j, k) than the littermate of WT mice after VSV and HSV-1 infection. Therefore, REGγ is able to positively regulate IFN-I production in innate antiviral immune responses both in vitro and in vivo.

## REGγ positively modulates host defense against viral infection
Since REGγ is involved in virus-stimulated IFN-I generation, we investigated its roles in the antiviral immune response. BMDMs and PEMs were infected with the RNA viruses, VSV or VSV-GFP. We found that VSV replication (Fig.  a) and VSV-GFP infection (Fig.  b) were also apparently increased in REGγ depletion of BMDMs. In addition, HSV-1 and VSV replication were also dramatically increased in REGγ ablation of PEMs (Fig.  c and d). Consistently, increased expression of REGγ by exogenously transfected in HEK-293 T cells limited VSV and HSV-1 replication in a dose-dependent manner (Fig.  e and ). VSV and HSV-1 replications were also dramatically increased in various organs (Fig.  f and ), and REGγ−/− mice showed aggravated lung injury after infection than controlled mice (Fig.  g). Consequently, REGγ−/− mice with increased VSV replication displayed a much lower survival rate than that WT mice (Fig.  h). Similar results were observed when we challenged REGγ−/− mice with HSV-1 (Fig.  g and h). Thus, REGγ deletion reduced IFN-I production and aggravated virus-induced tissue damage and mortality.
 

## REGγ expression is associated with the TBK1-IFNβ axis activity and antiviral responses
To investigate the contribution of REGγ to IFN-I signaling, we detected the key components of the intrinsic innate immunity signaling pathways, including the NF-κB (p65) [ ], MARK(p38, ERK) [ ], and TBK1-IRF3 [ ] pathways in infected WT and REGγ−/− BMDMs. Interestingly, the VSV-activated phosphorylation of TBK1 (Ser172) and the transcription factor IFN regulatory IRF3 (Ser396), which are responsible for initiating the production of IFN-I, was markedly inhibited in REGγ deficiency BMDMs, whereas there was no significant difference in the phosphorylation of P65, ERK and P38 (Fig.  a). Similarly, HSV-1, LPS, poly(I:C) and Pasm3-induced phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3 was significantly reduced in REGγ deficiency BMDMs (Fig.  b-d and ). These data suggest that REGγ is involved in activating the TBK1-IRF3 axis in response to various stimulations. Moreover, immunofluorescence results indicated that REGγ deficiency reduced the level of p-TBK1 in the cytoplasm and p-IRF3 in the nucleus in both VSV and HSV-1 infected BMDMs (Fig.  e and f). Consistent with these results, exogenous overexpression of REGγ in REGγ knockout 293 T cells infected with HSV-1 rescued the activity of IFNβ luciferase (Fig.  g). Also, VSV-induced IFNβ luciferase was inhibited in a dominant-negative mutation (N151Y) REGγ 293 T cells (Fig. ). Additionally, we transiently transfected HEK-293 T cells with indicated dosage of Flag-REGγ resulted in dramatically enhanced VSV-stimulated IFNβ luciferase in a dose-dependent manner (Fig.  h). Taken together, these results indicate that REGγ promotes the production of IFNβ via activation of TBK1-IRF3 axis in antiviral responses.
 

## REGγ regulates TBK1-IRF3-IFNβ axis activity depending on PPP2CB for antiviral responses.
To further clarify how REGγ affects the activity of TBK1-IRF3-IFNβ pathway by analyzing both RIG-I and cGAS signaling, which are involved in regulating TBK1 activation. We found the expression of MDA5, MAVS and RIG-I were dramatically enhanced in both WT and REGγ−/− BMDMs after VSV infection, but not STING and cGAS in both WT and REGγ−/− BMDMs. Accordingly, these indicated that both RIG-I and cGAS signaling have no significant changes between WT and REGγ−/− BMDMs (Fig.  a). Since REGγ deficiency had no effect on pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) of the host recognize viral RNA/DNA, we hypothesized that REGγ may regulate TBK1 phosphorylation by phosphatases. Screening a phosphatase library [ , ], we identified several potential candidate phosphatases, including the PP2A catalytic subunits (PPP2CA and PPP2CB) and PPM1A (Fig ), among which PP2Ac has been reported to be recognized as a candidate substrate in liver cancer cells for the REGγ proteasome [ ]. Subsequently, we pretreated BMDMs with Okadaic acid (OA), an inhibitor of PP2A. Immunofluorescence results showed that phosphorylated TBK1 was lower in REGγ deficiency BMDMs than in controls, but this difference could be minimized or even increased similar levels in both WT and REGγ−/− infected BMDMs after OA treatment (Fig.  b and c). Consistent with the results, immunoblot and immunofluorescence analysis showed that the phosphorylation of TBK1 and the nuclear accumulation of IRF3 were rescued in REGγ−/− BMDMs after OA treatment (Fig.  d, f and ). Similarly, when PPP2CB activity was blocked by transiently transfected short interfering RNA (siRNA) in WT and REGγ−/− BMDMs (Fig. ), there was a significant up-regulation of TBK1 and IRF3 phosphorylation, as well as IFNβ production (Fig.  e and ). Moreover, there was no difference in IFNβ expression (protein or mRNA) after OA-treated in WT and REGγ−/− BMDMs (Fig.  g and ). Additionally, when transiently transfected PPP2CB over-expression in WT and REGγ knockout HEK-293 T cells, VSV-stimulated IFNβ luciferase were dramatically decreased to similar levels in both WT and REGγ knockout infected cells (Fig.  h). Collectively, all these findings elucidate that REGγ promotes the innate antiviral immune response mainly through blocking PPP2CB activity.
 

## PPP2CB abolishes TBK1 activity by facilitating its dephosphorylation
As REGγ affects the antiviral immune response through degrading PPP2CB, we evaluated the effect of PPP2CB on TBK1 phosphorylation. The dephosphorylation of TBK1 by PPP2CB was initiated in a dose-dependent manner by co-expressing TBK1 and Flag-PPP2CB in HEK-293 T cells (Fig.  a). Additionally, the inhibition of PPP2CB activity, either by OA treatment or siRNA, also increased the phosphorylation of TBK1 and IRF3 (Fig. and b). Contrarily, the catalytically defective PPP2CB (H118N) did not remove TBK1 phosphorylation (Fig.  b). Next, we further confirmed the endogenous TBK1 could interact with PPP2CB, and the interaction was substantially enhanced in REGγ deficiency BMDMs, indicating that PPP2CB recruited TBK1 for subsequent dephosphorylation, which was blocked by REGγ (Fig.  c). Consistently, the results of reciprocal co-immunoprecipitation assays demonstrated that exogenously expressed TBK1 and PPP2CB also have interaction with each other (Fig.  d). These data indicate that PPP2CB could dynamically control TBK1 activity by interaction.
 To confirm whether PPP2CB dynamically binds to TBK1 to modulate host defense against viral infection. Confocal microscopy showed that PPP2CB and TBK1 were colocalized in the cytoplasm of BMDMs, and this colocalization was constantly attenuated during either VSV or HSV-1 infection (Fig.  e). Consistently, endogenous immunoprecipitation also found a weakening of interaction between PPP2CB and TBK1 during VSV or HSV-1 infection (Fig.  f). Furthermore, the interaction between TBK1 and PPP2CB was even more significantly attenuated in the presence of OA (Fig. ), suggesting that PPP2CB activity plays a curial role in modulating the antiviral immune response by dynamically interaction with TBK1. Subsequently, we observed that the interaction of the catalytically defective PPP2CB (H118N) with TBK1 was markedly attenuated (Fig.  g), thereby indicating that PPP2CB activity is required for dephosphorylation in the protein–protein interactions. Additionally, TBK1 could only be dephosphorylated by WT PPP2CB in a time dependent manner, but not by the catalytically defective PPP2CB (H118N) in vitro dephosphorylation assay (Fig.  h), which further confirms that TBK1 is a substrate of PPP2CB.

## Increased expression of REGγ via viral infection accelerates degradation of PPP2CB
To elucidate how REGγ regulates PPP2CB during viral infection, we analyzed PPP2CB and REGγ protein expression profiles and found that PPP2CB expression was markedly reduced, whereas REGγ expression was enhanced in BMDMs with the prolongation of viral infection (Fig.  a), suggesting that REGγ is negatively correlated with PPP2CB expression. When we stimulated BMDMs with indicated IFNβ mimic viral infection, we observed similar results that REGγ expression was increased in IFNβ dose-dependent manner, while PPP2CB expression was markedly inhibited (Fig.  b). Subsequently, quantitative PCR with reverse transcription (RT-qPCR) analysis showed that PPP2CB mRNA level was not changed, but REGγ mRNA level was elevated in infected BMDMs (Fig.  c). Therefore, we blocked REGγ to further analyze PPP2CB expression. The results showed markedly increased PPP2CB in REGγ deficiency BMDMs (Fig.  d). Nevertheless, there was no much difference of PPP2CB mRNA level in WT and REGγ−/− BMDMs (Fig.  e). These results indicate that REGγ mediates antiviral immune response by modulating the stability of PPP2CB.
 To validate the role of REGγ in dynamically regulating PPP2CB stability, we performed immunofluorescence staining. The results displayed the nuclear accumulation of PPP2CB in REGγ deficiency BMDMs, and that PPP2CB protein was much more stable in REGγ-deficient BMDMs with the prolongation of viral infection (Fig.  f). Similar dynamic changes were observed after CHX treatment in viral-infected BMDMs, PPP2CB degraded much faster in infected than control (Fig. ). Moreover, the protein stabilization assay showed that REGγ deficiency apparently prolonged the half-life of PPP2CB compared to WT BMDMs with viral infection or not (Fig.  g and h). To further explore the influence of PPP2CB on REGγ-proteasome associated degradation, we inhibited the activity of proteasome with MG132 treatment, and found that PPP2CB increased to the same level in both WT and REGγ-deficient BMDMs (Fig. ). Reciprocal immunoprecipitation assay revealed a physical interaction between REGγ and PPP2CB (Fig.  i). Additionally, immunofluorescence analysis showed that REGγ deficiency significantly increased the interaction between PPP2CB and TBK1 compared to WT BMDMs with viral infection or not (Fig.  j and ). Finally, in vitro cell-free proteolytic assay showed REGγ and 20S proteasome promoted degradation of PPP2CB (Fig.  k). Collectively, these findings suggest that PPP2CB is a direct target of REGγ, and that this effect is enhanced to activate antiviral response during viral infection.

## Discussions
Here, we uncover a novel positive feedback regulatory loop of REGγ that functions to limit the strength of the antiviral immune response. We propose a working model, with viral infection initial activation of IFNβ signaling promotes REGγ expression, which in turn accelerates the degradation of PPP2CB in an ATP and ubiquitin-independent manner, as well as the accumulation of phosphorylated TBK1, and leads to increased IRF3 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation to induce the transcription of IFNβ, which limits viral replications and ameliorates the survival rate of REGγ+/+ mice. Conversely, deficiency of REGγ increases the stability of PPP2CB protein, so that TBK1 is dephosphorylated and inhibits the nuclear transcriptional activity of IRF3, thereby limiting the production of IFNβ, which exacerbates viral immune evasion in REGγ−/− mice during infection (Fig.  ). Therefore, REGγ signaling forms a positive feedback loop that activates TBK1 signaling and IFN-I expression, which may be a limiting pathway utilized by the host during viral infection.
 IFN-I can restrict viral replication by directly inducing antiviral activity and activating innate immune cells. Although almost all cells are capable of producing IFN-I, macrophages are the major producers during acute viral infection of the host. IFN-I is usually expressed in the early stages of infection and decreases after clearance of antigen. However, IFN-I remains elevated during chronic infections and certain cancers [ , ], which may have deleterious effects on the host. Therefore, the IFN-I immune response must be under strict regulation of the host, pathogen, and environmental factors to maintain immune homeostasis. As the most critical kinase in IFN-I signaling, TBK1 not only phosphorylates the downstream transcription factors IRF3 to induce the production of IFN-I and other inflammatory cytokines, but also activates a series of adaptor proteins such as STING and MAVS, both of which play a crucial role in the antiviral innate immune response [ ]. Here, we found that the REGγ-PPP2CB axis targets and phosphorylates TBK1, thereby enhancing TBK1-mediated IFN-I production and antiviral immune regulation.
An increasing number of studies have shown that the proteasome system is associated with viral immune responses. The proteasome activator REGγ has been reported to promote the degradation of viral proteins, such as HCV core proteins and SARS-CoV-2 N proteins, suggesting its involvement in the viral immune responses [ , ]. This is also different from the study of Wang et al. [ ], who have reported that REGγ was involved in anti-bacterial innate immunity through degradation of the NF-KB pathway negative regulator KLF2. Additionally, REGγ has been reported to be associated with autoimmune diseases and inflammatory bowel disease [ , , ]. This indicates that REGγ plays an important role in innate immunity, but its involvement in the regulation of multiple pathogens suggests that it may have more universal and precise regulatory mechanisms. Thus, whether REGγ is involved in viral immune responses through TBK1-IFN-I signaling remains to be further investigated in macrophages.
The phosphorylation of TBK1 can be influenced by many factors. It is reported that TRIM29 target STING to regulate type I interferon production in response to DNA viruses [ ] and TRIM29 could also regulate reactive oxygen species to oxidize TBK1 to control cardiotropic virus induced myocarditis [ ]. Additionally, TRIM18 is reported to recruit PPM1A to dephosphorylate TBK1 and dampen TBK1-mediated type I interferon response [ ]. It has been reported that several phosphatases have been identified, including PPM1A [ ], PPM1B [ ], and PP4 [ ], that bind to and dephosphorylate TBK1, thus participate in TBK1-mediated IFN-I production. Among these phosphatases, PPP2CB, a catalytic subunit of PP2A, is unique in that it is dependent on REGγ for specific regulation of its protein stability by IFNβ signaling during viral infection. Many viruses target PP2A to block the IFN-I response in favor of increasing viral replication. Viral antigens can displace the regulatory subunit of PP2A, which in turn prevents PP2A from dephosphorylating many substrates and increases viral replication [ ]. Similarly, PP2A inhibitors reduce the dephosphorylation of viral core proteins and promote the formation of viral DNA [ , ], suggesting a protective effect of PP2A against viral infection. Conversely, it has also been reported that inhibition of PP2A activity dramatically reduces cellular susceptibility to respiratory syndrome virus (PRRSV) infection by eliminating activated phosphorylation of IRF3 and nuclear localization of IRF3 [ – ]. This suggests that the role of PP2A in the antiviral innate immune response is complex. Previously, we showed that PPP2CB stability is modulated by a ubiquitin-independent mechanism via the proteasome subunit REGγ [ ], and then hypothesize that this conflicting role may be due to proteasome activity effects. In our present infection model, depletion of REGγ increased nuclear stability of PPP2CB and inhibited TBK1-mediated IFN-I production. In addition, inhibition of PP2CB activity by H118N point mutation inactivator or okadaic acid (OA) treatment activates the TBK1-IRF3-IFN-I axis and promotes antiviral responses. In any case, our data provide new evidence about how the proteasome system dynamically regulates PPP2CB protein stabilization and plays a switching role in the regulation of antiviral innate immune responses.
Taken together, our results emphasize that REGγ-proteasome mediates the antiviral innate immune response via a ubiquitin- and ATP-independent manner, which activates TBK1 and enhances the nuclear transcriptional activity of IRF3, thereby positively regulating the production of IFN-I and controlling the intensity of antiviral innate immune response. This study highlights that REGγ dynamically regulates PPP2CB stability in the viral immune response, and expands our understanding of the function of the innate immune system in intracellular virus replication, which could contribute to the development of new strategies to regulate the antiviral responses.

## Materials and methods

## Mice
C57BL/6 wild-type mice were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). REGγ−/− mice were kindly provided by Prof. Xiaotao Li (East China Normal University). All the mice were bred and maintained under specific-pathogen-free conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle and were fed standard chow. Animal studies were conducted under the protocols approved by Shanghai General Hospital Clinical Center Laboratory Animal Welfare & Ethics Committee Approval Letter (IACUC No. 2023AW062). All the procedures were performed in accordance with Guides for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Academy of Sciences, China).

## Cell culture and treatments
Raw264.7, Vero and HEK-293 T were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection and incubated in DMEM supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin and 10% FBS. 293WT and 293-REGγ (N151Y) cells were generated as described previously [ ]; Bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDM) were isolated from the femurs and tibias of REGγ+/+ and REGγ−/− mice and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS, penicillin–streptomycin (50 mg/ml) and M-CSF (10 ng/ml). Peritoneal macrophages (PEM) were generated from the peritoneal wash fluid of mice intraperitoneally injected with 4% thioglycolate broth and cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS and penicillin–streptomycin (50 mg/ml). Cells were stimulated with 10 mM OA, 100 μg/mL CHX, or 10 μM MG132 at indicated time.

## Viral propagation and infection model
HSV-1 (Herpes simplex virus 1), VSV (Vesicular stomatitis virus) and VSV-GFP were propagated in Vero cells, and the virus titer was measured by plaque assay in Vero cells. BMDMs and PEMs (1 × 106 per ml) were infected with HSV-1 or VSV, then cells were harvested at the indicated time. For intravenous injection, mice were fully anesthetized by isoflurane (5% induction, 1.5% maintenance) inhalation and then infected by intravenous application of HSV-1 (3 × 107 PFU per mouse) or VSV (5 × 107 PFU per mouse). After indicated time, serum and tissues were collected and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis.

## RNA interference and plasmid transfection
The siRNAs against PPP2CB and negative control siRNA were synthesized by GenePharma. The target sequence of PPP2CB siRNAs were provided in Supplementary Table S2. IFNβ reporter and Flag-TBK1 were kindly provided by Prof. Bing Du (East China Normal University). HA-TBK1, Flag-PPP2CB, Flag-PPP2CB-H118N, HA-PPP2CB, HA-PPP2CB-H118N, His-PPP2CB, His-PPP2CB-H118N, Flag-REGγ, HA-REGγ, Flag-REGγ-N151Y plasmids were constructed following standard PCR techniques and all constructed were sequenced. Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) were used in the transfection, according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

## Luciferase reporter assay
HEK-293 T cells were seeded on 24-well plate and co-transfected with IFN-β reporter plasmid and TBK1. Transfected cells were infected HSV-1 or VSV, then lysed and centrifuged for 10 min at 12000 rpm. Luciferase activity of the supernatant was measured with a dual-luciferase assay system, according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega).

## Real-time PCR analysis
Total RNAs were extracted from the indicated treatment BMDMs or PEMs using TRIzol reagent (Takara) as recommended by the manufacturer. Isolated RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using 5 × HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme). qPCR was carried out on 5 ng of cDNA with primer pairs and 2 × SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme). Primer pairs were provided in Supplementary Table S3. Results were analyzed by the comparative analysis of relative expression based on ΔΔCT.

## Immunoblot analysis
BMDMs were collected and lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (containing protease inhibitors and phosphatase inhibitors). Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were quantified using BCA protein assay and added 2 × loading buffer. Protein samples were separated by electrophoresis through 10% polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Milli-pore), membranes were blocked by incubation with NcmBlot blocking buffer (P30500, NCM biotech) for 30 min at room temperature, then membranes were washed and incubated with the specific primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Next day membranes were washed and incubated for 1 h with appropriate secondary antibodies, then washed 3 more times and visualized by using a BIO-RAD ChemiDoc XRS system.

## Immunoprecipitation
Cells were harvested and dissolved in IP lysis buffer with protease inhibitor cocktail and phosphatase inhibitor for 30 min on ice. 10% cell lysate was separated and added with 2 × loading buffer as the input group, and the remaining cell lysate was incubated with specific primary antibodies, or anti-FLAG-agarose beads at 4 °C overnight as the IP group. Next day, the beads were washed five times with the wash buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol) and added with 1 × loading buffer. Then the eluted immunoprecipitants were separated by SDS-PAGE, followed by immunoblotting with indicated antibodies.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and then permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min at room temperature. For block step, cells were treated with 1% BSA in PBS for 1–2 h. Then, incubated the cells with primary antibodies p-TBK1 (dilution 1: 200), TBK1 (dilution 1: 500), IRF3 (dilution 1: 400) or PPP2CB (dilution 1: 400) at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibody for 1–2 h. The nucleus was stained with DAPI mounting medium. Images were captured using confocal laser scanning microscopy (Lecia, SP8).

## HE staining
Lung tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and then embedded in paraffin. Tissues were cut into 5-μm slices and stained with hematoxylin and eosin according to standard protocols. Images were acquired using the microscope (Lecia, DMi8). The quantitative method of acute lung tissue damage was performed according to previous report [ ].

## ELISA
The concentration of IFNβ in virus-infected mice serum and cell culture medium were measured with Elisa kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Mouse IFNβ ELISA Kits were purchased from YOBIBIO (Shanghai, China).

## In vitro phosphatase assay
Flag-PPP2CB and Flag-PPP2CB (H118N) proteins were immunoprecipitated using anti-Flag beads from HEK-293 T cell lysates and eluted with 3 × Flag peptide. His-TBK1 protein was purified from E. coli. The in vitro phosphatase assay was performed in phosphatase buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM dithiothreitol, 0.01% Brij 35,1 mM MnCl2). After TBK1 incubation with PPP2CB (WT or mutation) at 30 °C for indicated time with gentle shaking, the reactions were stopped by 2 × SDS loading buffer and boiled for 5 min, followed by immunoblotting analysis.

## In vitro proteolytic assay
Recombinant REGγ protein was purified by Profinity eXact affinity chromatography. The substrate of PPP2CB was generated by a cell-free protein translation system (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The proteolytic assay was performed by incubating substrate of PPP2CB, 20S proteasome (Boston Biochem) and REGγ heptamers for 1.5 h in a 50 μl reaction system at 30 °C with proper controls, followed by immunoblotting analysis.

## Statistical analysis
Results were shown as the mean ± S.E.M. GraphPad 8 Software was used for statistical analysis. Statistical significance was determined by unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test or two-way ANOVA. Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed to compare differences in survival rates. Differences were considered significance when the P value was less than 0.05. *P values < 0.05; **P values < 0.01; ***P values < 0.001.
