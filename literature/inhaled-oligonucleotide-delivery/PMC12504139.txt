## ABSTRACT
Mechanical ventilation (MV), though life‐saving in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), can cause ventilator‐induced lung injury (VILI). MicroRNA‐24 (miR‐24) has been implicated in regulating inflammation and apoptosis, but its role in VILI remains unexplored. Therefore, our study aimed to explore the role of mechanism of miR‐24 in VILI. MiR‐24 expression was analyzed in MV‐induced ARDS rat models (GSE57223), plasma from ARDS patients, and cyclic stretch (CS)‐treated alveolar epithelial cells. Functional studies included intratracheal delivery of miR‐24‐agomir in rats with VILI and transfection of miR‐24 mimic in CS‐exposed cells. Inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress markers, apoptosis, and mitochondrial dysfunction were assessed using ELISA, RT‐qPCR, TUNEL, JC‐1 staining, and ATP assays. BOK was identified as a target of miR‐24 via bioinformatics, luciferase reporter, and RNA pull‐down assays. Rescue experiments using BOK overexpression vectors (pcDNA3.1/BOK) were conducted in both models to confirm functional interaction. MiR‐24 was significantly downregulated in ARDS patients and VILI models and positively correlated with oxygenation index. Overexpression of miR‐24 attenuated MV‐ and CS‐induced inflammation, oxidative damage, and mitochondrial apoptosis dysfunction. BOK was confirmed as a direct target of miR‐24; its expression was upregulated in ARDS and VILI and inversely correlated with miR‐24 levels. Silencing of BOK attenuated MV‐induced inflammation, oxidative damage, and apoptosis in rats. Importantly, BOK overexpression reversed the protective effects of miR‐24 both in vivo and in vitro, confirming its role as a key downstream effector. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis showed that miR‐24 had good diagnostic potential (AUC = 0.834). Overall, MiR‐24 protects against MV‐induced lung injury by targeting BOK and modulating key injury pathways. The miR‐24/BOK axis offers a promising therapeutic avenue for ARDS‐associated VILI.
Keywords: acute respiratory distress syndrome, BOK, mechanical ventilation, miR‐24

## Introduction
Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a distressing form of acute respiratory failure [ ]. Trauma, pneumonia, sepsis, and viral infections may predispose individuals to ARDS [ ]. In clinical practice, mechanical ventilation (MV) is employed as a life‐saving intervention for ARDS management. Although MV is indispensable for sustaining gas exchange, it can also lead to ventilator‐induced lung injury (VILI), characterized by alveolar edema, barrier dysfunction, and infiltration of inflammatory cells [ ]. Despite the adoption of lung‐protective MV strategies, VILI remains a persistent challenge and negatively affects clinical outcomes [ ]. Thus, understanding the molecular basis of VILI may contribute to the development of targeted therapies for ARDS.
MicroRNAs (miRNAs) regulate posttranslational gene expression [ , ]. MiRNAs mediate physiological processes and induce diseases such as ARDS [ ]. For instance, miR‐214 exacerbates VILI in mice by targeting FGFR1 and regulating the PI3K/AKT pathway [ ], whereas miR‐9a‐5p overexpression alleviates VILI by modulating CXCR4 and suppressing the MAPK/ERK pathway [ ]. Additionally, miR‐150 has been shown to reduce inflammation, lung edema, and microvascular endothelial cell injury by modulating apoptotic signaling and the MAPK pathway [ ].
MiR‐24 has been implicated in several pulmonary diseases. For instance, miR‐24‐3p promotes the metastasis of pulmonary adenocarcinoma by targeting KLF8 [ ] and has been reported to suppress LPS‐stimulated neonatal lung injury by modulating NLRP3 [ ]. Additionally, bioinformatics analysis of the GSE57223 data set revealed lower miR‐24 expression in pulmonary tissues of MV‐induced ARDS rat models [ ]. However, the precise role and regulatory mechanisms of miR‐24 in MV‐induced lung injury remain largely unknown.
Bcl‐2‐related ovarian killer (BOK), a versatile member of the Bcl‐2 family, is a key mediator of intrinsic apoptotic stress, primarily localized in cellular membranes such as the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [ ]. BOK promotes apoptosis by inducing mitochondrial membrane permeabilization [ , ]. It is considered a tumor prognostic marker and is inversely correlated with the progression of various cancers [ ]. BOK has also been implicated in the exacerbation of SARS‐CoV‐2‐induced lung injury and edema through M‐protein signaling [ ]. Additionally, BOK enhances apoptosis in response to ER stress [ ]. In breast cancer, miR‐296‐5p‐mediated suppression of BOK regulates cell proliferation via the BOK/Mcl‐1 axis [ ]. However, its specific role in ARDS‐related oxidative stress and inflammation remains unclear.
This study aimed to elucidate the role of miR‐24 in mitigating mechanical ventilation‐induced lung injury through directly targeting the proapoptotic effector BOK. By leveraging both in vivo and in vitro models of VILI, we sought to define how miR‐24 modulates oxidative stress, inflammation, and mitochondrial apoptosis, thereby contributing to the identification of miR‐24/BOK as a novel regulatory axis and potential therapeutic goal in ARDS.

## Materials and Methods
2.1.

## Human Subjects
This study was authorized by the Ethics Committee of Changzhou Jintan First People's Hospital (#CJ2025_FPHC442) and followed the Helsinki Declaration. Informed consent was secured from 20 ARDS‐diagnosed patients and 20 age and gender‐matched healthy individuals before enrollment. The inclusion criteria for ARDS patients were as follows: (1) fulfillment of the diagnostic criteria for ARDS; (2) age greater than 18 years; and (3) admission to the ICU with mechanical ventilation support. The exclusion criteria included: (1) the presence of malignant tumors; (2) coexisting functional disorders of major organs such as the heart, liver, or kidneys; and (3) severe impairment of consciousness. For the control group, healthy individuals were selected based on medical history and physical examination. None of them exhibited symptoms or signs of pulmonary discomfort, had a history of underlying diseases, or showed abnormalities on routine chest X‐ray examination. As presented in Table  , there were no significant differences in age or gender distribution between ARDS patients and healthy controls (p > 0.05). Peripheral blood was sampled in EDTA tubes within 24 h of ARDS diagnosis. Samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min to isolate plasma and stored at − 80°C until further analysis.
 
2.2.

## Animal Study
Male Sprague‐Dawley rats were procured from Vital River Laboratory (Beijing, China). The animal procedures were accredited by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Changzhou Jintan First People's Hospital (#CJ2025‐020A). Ensuing a week of habituation, rats were allocated to control, VILI, VILI+agomir‐NC, VILI+miR‐24‐agomir, VILI+sh‐NC, VILI+sh‐BOK, and VILI+miR‐24‐agomir+oe‐BOK groups. Control rats were anesthetized with 3% pentobarbital sodium (30 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) and underwent tracheostomy with spontaneous breathing maintained. Rats in VILI groups were ventilated under anesthesia using a 14‐G catheter connected to an Inspira ventilator (Harvard Apparatus) as per a previously described protocol [ ]. The ventilation specifications were set: tidal volume (TV) of 40 ml/kg, respiratory rate (RR) of 60 breaths/minute, and positive end expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 0 cmH2O.
Before MV, rats in the VILI+agomir‐NC, VILI+miR‐24‐agomir, and VILI+miR‐24‐agomir+oe‐BOK groups received intratracheal administration of agomir‐NC, miR‐24‐agomir (Ribobio, China), sh‐NC, sh‐BOK (Ribobio, China) or miR‐24‐agomir combined with oe‐BOK vector (pcDNA3.1/BOK), respectively [ ]. To deliver shRNA to the lungs of rats, we used the intratracheal instillation method. Briefly, rats were anesthetized with 3% propofol sodium (30 mg/kg, intraperitoneally) and placed on the operating table with the neck fully extended to expose the trachea. A micropipette was used to draw the prepared solution containing sh‐BOK or sh‐NC (the synthesized shRNA powder was dissolved in nuclease‐free sterile water to the desired concentration). The solution was carefully introduced into the trachea through the gap between the tracheal rings and slowly injected. Immediately after injection, the rats were placed upright and gently rotated to allow the solution to distribute evenly throughout the lungs. shRNA was administered 4 h before mechanical ventilation to ensure adequate uptake and effective gene knockdown. 4 h after MV, rats were euthanized with phenobarbital overdose (> 150 mg/kg, IP) and ventilator disconnection. A secondary thoracotomy confirmed death. Lung tissues, peripheral blood, and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were collected for analysis. After washing the lung tissue with pre‐cooled phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), it was rinsed thoroughly, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored at − 80°C for subsequent RT‐qPCR analysis. Successful establishment of the model was confirmed using both pathological and molecular criteria. Pathologically, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed to assess lung tissue changes and verify that mechanical ventilation induced VILI. At the molecular level, confirmation of BOK gene knockdown at the mRNA level served as a key indicator of successful model establishment.

2.3.

## Measurement of MPO Activity
Myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity in lung tissues was assessed using an MPO assay kit (Cat# SEKR‐0073, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Homogenized tissue supernatants were reacted with a chromogenic substrate, and absorbance was checked at 460 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA).

2.4.

## MDA and SOD Measurement
The level of malondialdehyde (MDA) was determined using a thiobarbituric acid (TBA)‐based MDA assay kit (Cat# A003‐1‐2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). Briefly, the homogenized lung tissue samples were reacted with TBA reagent under high‐temperature conditions (95°C) for 40 min in a water bath. After cooling and centrifugation, the absorbance was calculated at 532 nm by a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA), and MDA levels were calculated using a standard curve.
Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was evaluated using a SOD assay kit (Cat# CS0009‐1KT, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA), based on the inhibition of superoxide anion generation. In brief, lung tissues were homogenized and centrifuged, and the supernatant was mixed with a working reagent containing xanthine and xanthine oxidase. The reduction of the reaction mixture was monitored spectrophotometrically at 450 nm. SOD activity was estimated using the standard provided with the kit and expressed as U/mg protein.

2.5.

## Analysis of Lung Wet/Dry Weight Ratio and BALF
After perfusion, the left lung was excised, blotted, and weighed (wet weight), then dried at 60°C for 72 h to obtain dry weight. The W/D ratio was calculated as (wet/dry weight) × 100%. BALF was centrifuged at 1000g for 10 min, and TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6 levels in the supernatant were measured using ELISA kits (Cat# ab236712, ab100767, ab234570; Abcam).

2.6.

## Histopathological Analysis
Rat lung tissues were fixed in formaldehyde, paraffin‐embedded, sectioned, and then deparaffinized by xylene, followed by alcohol rehydration. Nuclear and cytoplasmic staining was performed by immersing the hydrated slides in H&E dyes. The morphological alterations were detected and photographed using a light microscope (Olympus, Japan). A pathologist, blinded to the experimental groups, scored lung injury based on the severity of alveolar edema, hemorrhage, and inflammatory cell infiltration.

2.7.

## TUNEL Assay
Apoptosis activity in lung tissue sections was gauged using a TUNEL (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling) assay kit (Cat# ab206386, Abcam, UK). Precisely, the paraffin‐embedded tissue sections were cured with xylene for 5 min, followed by ethanol rehydration. Then the enzyme‐digested sections were rinsed with TBS and treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 5 min. After incubation with 100 µL of TdT Equilibration Buffer for 30 min, 40 µL of TdT Labeling Reaction Mix was added and incubated for 90 min. Subsequent steps included treatment with Stop Buffer and blocking buffer, followed by DAB chromogen development for 15 min. Sections were counterstained with DAPI, dehydrated, mounted, and number of TUNEL‐positive cells was quantified under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).

2.8.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
After dewaxing and rehydration, the lung tissue was incubated in 0.3% hydrogen peroxide for 0.5 h, followed by antigen retrieval using citrate buffer. Sections were kept overnight with primary antibodies: anti‐cleaved caspase‐3 (Cat# 9664, 1:2000 dilution, Cell Signaling Technology) and BOK (Cat# PA5‐120442, Thermo Fisher). The next day, the PBS rinsed slides were incubated with HRP anti‐Rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Cat# G‐21234, 1:2000 dilution, Thermo Fisher) at room temperature for 30 min. DAB substrate was used for color development, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. Stained sections were visualized and imaged using a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).

2.9.

## RNA Isolation and RT‐qPCR
RNA was acquired from rat lung tissues using an RNA Isolation Kit (Cat# AM1912, Thermo Fisher). For mRNA detection, cDNA synthesis was carried out using SuperScript II Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Cat# 18064014, Thermo Fisher Scientific). For miR‐24 detection, reverse transcription was performed using the TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Cat# 4366596, Applied Biosystems, USA). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was conducted using a SYBR Green PCR kit or a miRNA Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Cat# MQ101‐02, Vazyme). The relative expression levels of miR‐24 and mRNAs were calculated using 2ΔΔCt method, with U6 and β‐actin serving as miRNA and mRNA controls. The primer details for qPCR are mentioned in Table  .
 
2.10.

## Cell Culture and Treatment
Primary human alveolar epithelial cells (Passage 2) were sourced from Procell Life Science & Technology Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Cells (5 × 10⁵ per well) were seeded in 8% DMEM at 37°C. To simulate VILI in vitro, cells were subjected to cyclic stretch (CS) using an FX‐5000 T Flexercell Tension Plus system (USA). The CS regimen consisted of 30 cycles/min for 12 h with 20% elongation, as described previously [ ].

2.11.

## Intracellular ROS Levels
Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were estimated by the fluorescent probe 2’,7’‐dichlorofluorescin diacetate (Cat# 35845‐5 G, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA). Alveolar epithelial cells were treated with 10 µM DCFH‐DA for 20 min at 37°C in the dark, rinsed three times with PBS, and fluorescence intensity was evaluated at 535 nm by a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA). ROS levels were quantified relative to the control.

2.12.

## Apoptosis Analysis
Apoptotic cells were assessed by an Annexin V‐FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Cat# 40302ES20, Yeasen, China). Cells were harvested, rinsed with PBS, and resuspended in 1× binding buffer. Then, 5 µL Annexin V‐FITC and 10 µL propidium iodide were added and incubated for 15 min at 37°C in the dark. Apoptotic cell populations were analyzed by a flow cytometer (BECKMAN COULTER, USA).

2.13.

## Mitochondrial Membrane Potential (MMP) Assay
The MMP was evaluated using JC‐1 staining (Cat# HY‐K0601, MCE, USA). Cells were incubated with JC‐1 working solution for 30 min. Following incubation, cells were washed and resuspended, and fluorescence was examined using a fluorescence microscope. Red fluorescence (JC‐1 aggregates) indicated intact MMP, while green fluorescence (JC‐1 monomers) reflected mitochondrial depolarization. JC‐1 red/green fluorescence ratios were quantified using ImageJ software.

2.14.

## ATP Assays
Cellular ATP levels were quantified using a luciferase‐based luminescence assay kit (Cat# G9241, CellTiter‐Glo, USA). Alveolar epithelial cells were put in 96‐well plates (1 × 10⁴ cells/well), followed by adding 100 µL CellTiter‐Glo reagent to dissolve the cells and stabilize the luminescence signal. After 10 min of incubation at room temperature, luminescence was measured by a microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA).

2.15.

## Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay
To assess the direct relation between miR‐24 and BOK, the 3’ untranslated region (UTR) of BOK, consisting of either the wild‐type (WT) or mutated (MUT) miR‐24 binding site, was introduced into the pmirGLO Dual‐Luciferase miRNA Target Expression Vector (Cat# E1330, Promega, USA). Alveolar epithelial cells were plated in 24‐well plates and cotransfected with WT or MUT reporter vectors and either miR‐24 mimic or negative control (NC) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). 48 h later, the luciferase activity was gauged by the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega).

2.16.

## RNA‐Pulldown Assay
A biotin‐labeled miR‐24 probe and an NC probe were synthesized (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 20164) and incubated with streptavidin‐coated magnetic beads in RIP buffer for 4 h, followed by the addition of cell lysates and overnight incubation at 4°C. The magnetic beads were rinsed with cold PBS, and bound RNAs were extracted and subjected to RT‐qPCR analysis to detect BOK mRNA enrichment.

2.17.

## Western Blot Analysis
Total protein was extracted from rat lung tissues or cultured alveolar epithelial cells through RIPA lysis buffer (Cat# 89900, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the protein concentrations were estimated using BCA Assay Kit (Cat# 23225, Pierce, USA). Proteins were isolated by SDS‐PAGE and shifted to nitrocellulose membranes. Following occlusion with 5% skimmed milk, membranes were kept overnight with primary antibodies: anti‐Bcl‐2 (Cat# ab196495, 1:2000, Abcam), anti‐Bax (Cat# 664, 1:200, CST), anti‐β‐actin (Cat# ab205587, 1:1200, Abcam), anti‐IL‐6 (Cat# ab259341, 1:1500, Abcam), anti‐IL‐1β (Cat# ab254360, 1:1500, Abcam), Cleaved caspase‐3 (R & D, AF835, 1:450) and anti‐BOK (Cat# ab233072, 1:50, Abcam). After TBST rinse, membranes were incubated for 1 h at RT with anti‐rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Cat# G‐21234, 1:20000, Thermo Fisher). Enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Elabscience, China) was employed, and band intensities were checked using ImageJ software, normalized to β‐actin. The original uncropped blots are provided as Supporting Information File  .

2.18.

## Statistical Analysis
GraphPad Prism 8.0. was employed for statistical analysis and data was presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Two‐group comparisons were conducted using Student's t‐test, and multiple group comparisons were performed with one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Correlation between miR‐24 expression and either the oxygenation index or BOK expression levels in ARDS patients was evaluated using Pearson correlation analysis. p < 0.05 was statistically significant.

## Results
3.1.

## MiR‐24 Is Downregulated in ARDS With Diagnostic Value
To probe the involvement of miR‐24 in ARDS, we analyzed its expression profile in a publicly available microarray data set (GSE57223) from MV‐induced ARDS rat models. Bioinformatic analysis revealed a significant downregulation of miR‐24 in pulmonary tissues of ARDS rats compared to controls (Figure  ). To validate these findings in clinical samples, we quantified miR‐24 expression using RT‐qPCR in plasma samples from ARDS patients and age‐ and gender‐matched healthy individuals. Consistent with the animal model results, miR‐24 expression was markedly reduced in ARDS patients relative to healthy controls (Figure  ). Pearson correlation analysis indicated a strong positive association between miR‐24 expression levels and the oxygenation index (PaO₂/FiO₂) in ARDS patients (R² = 0.769, p < 0.001), suggesting that lower miR‐24 levels may correlate with worse pulmonary function (Figure  ).
 To check the diagnostic potential of miR‐24, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted, and the area under the curve (AUC) was 0.834, indicating high diagnostic accuracy of miR‐24 in distinguishing ARDS patients from healthy subjects (p < 0.001) (Figure  ).

3.2.

## MiR‐24 Overexpression Mitigated the MV‐Induced Lung Injury in Rats
To evaluate the functional role of miR‐24 in vivo, a mechanical ventilation‐induced VILI model was established in rats. For miR‐24 overexpression, miR‐24‐agomir was administered via intratracheal injection before mechanical ventilation. As shown in Figure  , RT‐qPCR analysis confirmed significantly reduced levels of miR‐24 in the lung tissues of the VILI group, which were effectively restored in the VILI+miR‐24‐agomir group.
 The lung W/D weight ratio, an indicator of pulmonary edema, was increased in the VILI group. However, miR‐24 overexpression significantly reduced this ratio, indicating an attenuation of edema (Figure  ). Histopathological examination via H&E staining revealed characteristic lung injury features in the VILI group, including alveolar hemorrhage, edema, neutrophilic infiltration, and mild thickening of the alveolar septa. Treatment with miR‐24‐agomir substantially alleviated these pathological changes, as reflected by significantly reduced lung injury scores (Figure  ). In addition, the total protein content in BALF, a marker of alveolar‐capillary barrier disruption was markedly elevated in the VILI group but significantly decreased following miR‐24 overexpression (Figure  ). Furthermore, both ELISA and RT‐qPCR analyzes demonstrated significantly upregulated levels of the proinflammatory cytokines in BALF and lung tissues of VILI rats. These elevations were markedly suppressed in rats treated with miR‐24 agomir, at both protein and mRNA levels (Figure  ).

3.3.

## MiR‐24 Overexpression Attenuated Oxidative Damage and Apoptosis in VILI‐Induced Rats
Oxidative stress is a critical contributor to mechanical ventilation‐induced lung injury [ ]. To assess whether miR‐24 mitigates oxidative damage, we evaluated oxidative stress markers in lung tissues of VILI rats. MPO and MDA levels were considerably elevated in the VILI group, whereas SOD activity was notably decreased, indicating the presence of severe oxidative stress during lung injury. Notably, treatment with miR‐24 agomir significantly reversed these alterations, demonstrating that miR‐24 overexpression reduced MV‐induced oxidative stress in the lungs (Figure  ). To examine apoptosis, TUNEL staining was conducted on lung sections. An increase in TUNEL‐positive cells was observed in VILI group, which was significantly reduced in the miR‐24‐agomir‐treated rats, indicating lower apoptotic activity (Figure  ). Similarly, IHC staining revealed elevated expression of cleaved caspase‐3 in the lungs following MV, which was markedly suppressed by miR‐24 overexpression (Figure  ). Western blot analysis further confirmed these findings: MV led to decreased expression of Bcl‐2 and increased levels of Bax and cleaved caspase‐3. These molecular alterations were effectively reversed upon miR‐24 overexpression (Figure  ), suggesting that miR‐24 shields against MV‐induced lung injury by alleviating both oxidative stress and apoptosis.
 
3.4.

## MiR‐24 Overexpression Alleviated the Cyclic Stretch‐Induced Injury in Alveolar Epithelial Cells
To further elucidate the role of miR‐24 in VILI, human alveolar epithelial cells were subjected to CS stimulation in vitro. RT‐qPCR analysis revealed that miR‐24 expression was downregulated in CS‐exposed cells, but was markedly restored upon transfection with a miR‐24 mimic (Figure  ). Cell viability assays indicated that CS treatment reduced the viability of alveolar epithelial cells, whereas miR‐24 overexpression partially reversed this decline (Figure  ). The expression of inflammatory cytokines was subsequently evaluated. Both RT‐qPCR and western blot analyzes showed that CS exposure led to a notable elevation of TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β, while miR‐24 mimic transfection significantly suppressed these inflammatory responses (Figure  ). Oxidative stress markers were also assessed. CS treatment substantially increased intracellular ROS levels and MDA content, and concurrently declined SOD activity. These oxidative alterations were considerably overturned following miR‐24 overexpression (Figure  ). To explore the apoptotic response, western blot analysis was performed. CS exposure resulted in Bcl‐2 attenuation and Bax and cleaved caspase‐3 augmentation. These changes were effectively counteracted by miR‐24 mimic transfection (Figure  ). Consistent with these findings, flow cytometry revealed that CS significantly elevated the apoptotic rate of alveolar epithelial cells, which was markedly attenuated by miR‐24 overexpression (Figure  ). MMP was evaluated using JC‐1 staining. Following CS treatment, the J‐aggregate/monomer fluorescence ratio was significantly reduced, indicating mitochondrial depolarization. This loss of MMP was effectively rescued by miR‐24 mimic (Figure  ). Moreover, ATP levels were significantly decreased in CS‐treated cells but were restored upon miR‐24 overexpression (Figure  ). Collectively, miR‐24 mitigated CS‐induced inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial apoptosis in alveolar epithelial cells.
 
3.5.

## MiR‐24 Directly Targeted BOK in Alveolar Epithelial Cells
To identify potential targets of miR‐24, the miRDB database was utilized. To evaluate the expression levels of the top five predicted target genes in alveolar epithelial cells exposed to CS, RT‐qPCR was performed. Among these, only BOK was upregulated in CS‐treated cells (Figure  ) and was therefore selected for further validation. To confirm direct binding, a dual‐luciferase reporter assay was conducted using constructs containing either the WT or MUT 3’ UTR of BOK. Luciferase activity significantly decreased when miR‐24 was cotransfected with the WT BOK reporter construct, whereas the MUT group showed no discernible change. This suggested that miR‐24 interacts with the 3'UTR of BOK. (Figure  ). RNA pull‐down assays further validated this interaction, showing significant enrichment of BOK mRNA in the miR‐24‐associated complex in alveolar epithelial cells (Figure  ). Western blot analysis and IHC showed that BOK protein expression was considerably elevated in the lung tissues of VILI rats but significantly downregulated upon treatment with miR‐24 agomir (Figure  ). Similarly, RT‐qPCR and western blot assays demonstrated that overexpressing miR‐24 reversed the CS‐induced upregulation of BOK in alveolar epithelial cells, suggesting that miR‐24 negatively regulates BOK expression in vitro (Figure  ). Consistent with these findings, RT‐qPCR analysis of peripheral blood samples revealed significantly higher BOK expression in ARDS patients compared to healthy controls (Figure  ). Pearson correlation analysis indicated a negatively correlated expression of miR‐24 and BOK in ARDS patient samples (Figure  ). Together, miR‐24 directly targeted and negatively regulated BOK, implicating the miR‐24/BOK axis as a critical regulatory pathway in pathogenesis of VILI and ARDS.
 
3.6.

## BOK Silencing Mitigated the MV‐Induced Lung Injury in Rats
To evaluate the functional role of BOK in vivo, sh‐BOK was administered in rats via intratracheal injection before mechanical ventilation to silence BOK expression. RT‐qPCR confirmed a significant increase in BOK expression in the VILI group, which was effectively suppressed in the VILI + sh‐BOK group (Figure  ). BOK silencing significantly reduced the elevated lung wet/dry weight ratio observed in VILI rats, indicating reduced pulmonary edema (Figure  ). Histological analysis showed that typical features of lung injury, alveolar hemorrhage, edema, inflammatory infiltration, and septal thickening were evident in the VILI group but markedly alleviated in the sh‐BOK group, as reflected by reduced injury scores (Figure  ). Similarly, the increased total protein concentration in BALF was significantly diminished following BOK knockdown (Figure  ). Moreover, ELISA and RT‐qPCR revealed that sh‐BOK treatment significantly suppressed the elevated levels of TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6 in BALF and lung tissues (Figure  ), confirming that BOK silencing mitigated MV‐induced lung inflammation and injury.
 
3.7.

## BOK Silencing Attenuated Oxidative Damage and Apoptosis in VILI‐Induced Rats
To assess whether BOK silencing mitigated oxidative damage, we evaluated oxidative stress markers in lung tissues of VILI rats. MPO and MDA levels were significantly elevated in the VILI group, while SOD activity was reduced, indicating oxidative injury. These changes were effectively reversed by sh‐BOK treatment, suggesting reduced oxidative stress following BOK knockdown (Figure  ). TUNEL staining showed increased apoptosis in the VILI group, which was significantly diminished in sh‐BOK‐treated rats (Figure  ). Likewise, IHC analysis revealed that elevated cleaved caspase‐3 expression in VILI lungs was suppressed by BOK silencing (Figure  ). Western blot analysis confirmed that BOK knockdown restored Bcl‐2 levels and decreased Bax and cleaved caspase‐3 expression (Figure  ). Collectively, these results indicated that BOK silencing attenuated both oxidative stress and apoptosis in MV‐induced lung injury.
 
3.8.

## MiR‐24 Alleviated Mechanical Ventilation‐Induced Lung Injury in Rats by Targeting BOK
To investigate whether BOK is involved in the protective role of miR‐24 against VILI in vivo, the overexpression efficiency of BOK via pcDNA3.1/BOK vectors was confirmed by RT‐qPCR (Figure  ). VILI rats were then co‐administered miR‐24‐agomir and pcDNA3.1/BOK vectors. RT‐qPCR analysis showed that miR‐24‐agomir downregulated BOK expression in VILI rat lung tissues, and BOK overexpression reversed this change (Figure  ). The W/D weight ratio, which was reduced by miR‐24‐agomir treatment, was significantly elevated upon BOK overexpression (Figure  ). H&E staining revealed that the attenuation of pathological changes such as alveolar hemorrhage and edema by miR‐24‐agomir was reversed by BOK overexpression (Figure  ). Similarly, BOK upregulation negated the miR‐24‐agomir‐induced reduction in total protein concentration in BALF, indicating restored barrier disruption (Figure  ). ELISA results further showed that BOK overexpression enhanced inflammation in miR‐24‐agomir‐treated VILI rats, as indicated by elevated TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6 levels in BALF (Figure  ). Western blot analysis confirmed that BOK overexpression counteracted miR‐24‐agomir‐mediated suppression of these inflammatory cytokines in lung tissues (Figure  ). Overall, miR‐24 alleviated MV‐induced lung injury and inflammation in rats by targeting and suppressing BOK.
 
3.9.

## MiR‐24 Alleviated Mechanical Ventilation‐Induced Oxidative Damage in Rat Lungs via BOK
To further evaluate the role of BOK in miR‐24‐mediated protection against MV‐induced oxidative damage, oxidative stress markers were assessed in the lungs of VILI rats. MPO activity, which was significantly reduced by miR‐24‐agomir treatment, was markedly elevated in the VILI+miR‐24‐agomir+oe‐BOK group compared to the VILI + miR‐24‐agomir group (Figure  ). Similarly, the reduction in MDA levels and the increase in SOD activity observed in miR‐24‐agomir‐treated lungs were reversed by BOK overexpression (Figure  ). TUNEL assays further demonstrated that the decrease in apoptotic cell count induced by miR‐24‐agomir in VILI rat lungs was counteracted by BOK overexpression (Figure  ). Correspondingly, Western blot analysis showed that the miR‐24 agomir–mediated augmentation of Bcl‐2 and attenuation of Bax and cleaved caspase‐3 were reversed by BOK overexpression (Figure  ). These findings suggested that BOK functions as a key mediator of oxidative stress and apoptosis in the setting of MV‐induced lung injury via miR‐24.
 
3.10.

## MiR‐24 Protected the Alveolar Epithelial Cells From CS‐Induced Injury by Regulating BOK
To investigate the role of BOK in miR‐24‐mediated protection against CS‐induced injury in alveolar epithelial cells, we examined the effects of BOK overexpression in this context. RT‐qPCR analysis revealed that the downregulation of BOK by miR‐24 overexpression was reversed upon cotransfection with oe‐BOK vectors in CS‐treated alveolar epithelial cells (Figure  ). CCK‐8 assays showed that the miR‐24‐induced increase in cell viability was significantly reduced by oe‐BOK cotransfection (Figure  ). Additionally, RT‐qPCR and western blot analyzes demonstrated that the miR‐24‐mediated suppression of inflammatory cytokines in CS‐exposed cells was reversed by BOK overexpression (Figure  ). Consistent with these findings, the miR‐24‐induced reduction in ROS and MDA levels, along with elevated SOD activity, was negated by oe‐BOK transfection (Figure  ). Furthermore, both western blot and flow cytometry analyzes confirmed that the antiapoptotic effect of miR‐24 overexpression in CS‐treated alveolar epithelial cells was offset by cotransfection with oe‐BOK (Figure  ). JC‐1 staining showed that the increased J‐aggregate/monomer fluorescence ratio, indicative of improved mitochondrial membrane potential following miR‐24 mimic treatment, was significantly reversed by BOK overexpression (Figure  ). Likewise, the elevated ATP levels observed with miR‐24 overexpression were markedly diminished upon cotransfection with oe‐BOK in CS‐exposed cells (Figure  ). Overall, miR‐24 protected alveolar epithelial cells from CS‐induced inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis by targeting the BOK signaling pathway.

## Discussion
MV is a vital supportive intervention for ARDS patients, however, excessive mechanical stress can lead to VILI, thereby aggravating clinical outcomes [ ]. Despite the application of lung‐protective ventilation strategies, the adverse effects of MV remain a major clinical challenge [ ]. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying VILI is therefore critical for identifying potential therapeutic targets.
MicroRNAs have emerged as promising biomarkers and therapeutic modulators in ARDS [ ]. MiR‐24 dysregulation has been implicated in multiple pathological contexts, including lung, gastric, and colorectal cancers [ , ]. miR‐24 also regulates inflammation and cell death through specific signaling pathways. For instance, it targets Chi3l1 to suppress vascular inflammation in abdominal aortic aneurysm [ ] and downregulates TRAF6 to inhibit LPS‐induced endometrial inflammation by repressing the NF‐κB/MAPK pathways [ ]. Lin et al. reported that miR‐24 mitigates LPS‐induced injury by targeting NLRP3 [ ] in lung tissue. Additionally, its role in angiogenesis, inflammation, and tissue recovery has been noted in myocardial infarction and stroke models [ ].
This study is the first to demonstrated that miR‐24 expression was significantly reduced in ARDS patients compared to healthy controls. Pearson correlation analysis revealed a strong positive relationship between miR‐24 expression levels and the oxygenation index in ARDS patients, suggesting clinical relevance. Importantly, ROC curve analysis yielded an AUC of 0.834, indicating that miR‐24 has high diagnostic accuracy in distinguishing ARDS patients from healthy individuals. This finding aligned with previous reports, who reported an AUC of 0.87 for plasma miR‐155 in diagnosing sepsis‐associated ARDS [ ], and Yu et al., found that serum miR‐146a levels correlated with ARDS severity and prognosis [ ]. Additionally, our study demonstrated that miR‐24 alleviated mechanical ventilation‐induced lung injury in rats by significantly reducing the lung wet/dry weight ratio, lowering the expression of inflammatory cytokines (TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6), decreasing intracellular ROS levels and MDA content, enhancing SOD activity, upregulating Bcl‐2 protein expression, and downregulating Bax and cleaved caspase‐3 levels. For in vitro validation, alveolar epithelial cells were subjected to CS to simulate MV‐induced injury [ , ]. CS exposure led to reduced miR‐24 expression and cell viability, along with elevated proinflammatory cytokines, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis. Notably, miR‐24 overexpression partially restored cell viability and mitigated these damaging effects.
BOK is a proapoptotic member of BCL‐2 family, is distinct from other family members due to its predominant localization in the ER and mitochondria [ ]. It functions as a critical regulator of stress‐induced apoptosis, primarily via mitochondrial pathways [ ]. BOK is known to be upregulated under ER‐stressed conditions and sensitizes cells to apoptotic stimuli [ , ]. Under normal physiological conditions, BOK is degraded via the endoplasmic reticulum‐associated degradation (ERAD) pathway, which involves key components such as AMFR/gp78, VCP/p97, and the proteasome complex. Inhibition of the PERK‐eIF2α signaling pathway or treatment with proteasome inhibitors (e.g., MG132) can stabilize BOK, leading to mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) and the initiation of apoptosis [ ]. BOK facilitates calcium (Ca²⁺) transfer from the ER to mitochondria via mitochondria‐associated membranes (MAMs), activates calcium‐dependent proteases like calpain, and cleaves BID into tBID, which in turn activates BAX/BAK pores, resulting in the release of cytochrome c from mitochondria and promotion of apoptosis [ ]. GRP78, an ER chaperone, supports protein folding under mild stress but dissociates from PERK, ATF6, and IRE1 during sustained ER stress, thereby activating the unfolded protein response [ ]. Overexpression of BOK may impair GRP78's protective role by competitively binding to it, indirectly leading to CHOP upregulation [ ]. CHOP serves as a marker of prolonged ER stress, and induces GADD34, which dephosphorylates eIF2α to restore protein synthesis. This can exacerbate the accumulation of misfolded proteins and simultaneously upregulate proapoptotic genes such as BIM and PUMA [ ]. Through this mechanism, BOK may amplify CHOP signaling and accelerate mitochondrial apoptosis [ ]. Prior research also suggests that miRNAs such as miR‐296‐5p can suppress BOK to modulate oxidative and apoptotic responses in cancer progression. Our data showed that BOK expression was elevated in both VILI rat lungs and CS‐treated epithelial cells, and that miR‐24 directly targeted and downregulated BOK, thereby mitigating inflammation and mitochondrial apoptosis. The ER and mitochondria form dynamic contact sites known as mitochondria‐associated ER membranes (MAMs), which play critical roles in calcium signaling, lipid metabolism, and protein quality control [ ]. A potential mechanism for the coordinated regulation of cellular stress responses by miR‐24 and BOK may involve competitive interactions with ER chaperones, modulation of calcium signaling pathways, and the regulation of the balance between apoptosis and autophagy. This supports the role of the miR‐24/BOK axis as a key mediator in the pathogenesis of VILI.
Despite these findings, our study has several limitations. First, we did not include in vivo experiments specifically assessing the effects of BOK inhibition, which could further validate its role as a therapeutic target. Second, the study lacks data on animal survival rates or long‐term outcomes, which are important for assessing clinical relevance. Third, while antisense oligonucleotides and mimics were used effectively, there is a possibility of off‐target effects that may influence the observed outcomes. Additionally, our clinical sample size (20 ARDS patients and 20 healthy controls) may be insufficient to support robust diagnostic conclusions based on the ROC analysis (AUC = 0.834). Larger patient cohorts or external validation data sets will be necessary to confirm the diagnostic potential of miR‐24. Moreover, the low RNA pull‐down enrichment observed in Figure  may be due to multiple factors. On the experimental side, the probe design may not have fully encompassed key binding regions (e.g., the miR‐24 seed sequence), thereby reducing specificity for target capture. From a technical standpoint, the detection sensitivity of traditional Northern blot methods is limited (> 10 pg), and the cellular abundance of miR‐24 may fall below this threshold. These limitations underscored the need for further mechanistic and technical optimization in future studies.
In summary, our findings revealed a significant downregulation of miR‐24 in ARDS. MiR‐24 overexpression provided protective effects against MV‐induced lung injury in rats and CS‐induced injury in alveolar epithelial cells by direct BOK targeting. These findings showed that miR‐24/BOK axis may represent a potential therapeutic target for mitigating VILI in patients with ARDS.
