In recent years, a growing body of work has explored the roles of endogenous gasotransmitters—namely nitric oxide (NO), carbon monoxide (CO), and hydrogen sulfide (H2S)—in pathways relevant to PD pathology. Though traditionally viewed as toxic, these molecules may, under regulated conditions, influence oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and mitochondrial function in ways that could be therapeutically significant. NO illustrates this complexity. At physiological concentrations, it supports synaptic function and cerebral blood flow. Yet, when overproduced, it promotes oxidative damage and disrupts mitochondrial processes. Therapeutic strategies include NOS enzyme inhibition and controlled-release NO donors. Nanoparticle-based delivery systems have also been proposed to fine-tune local NO availability, though their clinical relevance remains to be fully validated. CO, produced via heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), has shown cytoprotective and anti-inflammatory effects. While promising in modulating redox signaling and apoptosis, any therapeutic use of CO requires careful dose regulation to avoid toxicity. H2S, whose levels appear diminished in PD models, has demonstrated antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and mitochondrial-supportive effects in preclinical studies. As with NO and CO, maintaining balanced levels of H2S is critical—both too little and too much can cause harm, making precise delivery essential. This review examines the involvement of NO, CO, and H2S in PD-related stress responses, emphasizing how their effects vary across experimental models and remain only partially understood.

## Introduction
Parkinson's disease (PD) primarily affects elder adults and is marked by the gradual deterioration of motor function and diverse non-motor impairments [ ]. As the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder, PD prevalence and incidence is increasing, with projections suggesting it could double within the next 30 years as it has doubled already within the last 25 years [ , ]. First described by James Parkinson in An Essay on the Shaking Palsy in 1817 [ ], PD is primarily characterized by the degeneration of dopaminergic (DA) neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc), leading to dopamine depletion in the striatum [ ]. Dopamine is synthesized from the amino acid tyrosine by the enzyme tyrosine hydroxylase (TH). Nissl staining and loss of TH-IR (TH-immunoreactivity) is considered a proxy for dopaminergic degeneration, since TH expression is localized to and regulated by dopaminergic and noradrenergic neurons [ , ]. Downregulated TH expression is positively correlated with motor control, motivation, and reward circuits affected in PD [ ]. Degeneration of the nigrostriatal pathway, which connects the SNc to the striatum is a major pathology of PD, severely affects motor inhibition and output within basal ganglia circuitry [ ]. The colloquial neuropathological hallmark of PD is the presence of Lewy bodies (LBs) and Lewy neurites (LNs) [ ], which are intracellular aggregates rich in misfolded α-synuclein (α-syn) [ , ]. α-Synuclein is a 140-amino-acid presynaptic protein implicated in synaptic function, axonal transport, and autophagy [ , ]. Its progressive accumulation correlates with disease severity. The Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) and Hoehn and Yahr staging system are commonly used to assess symptom severity, with stage 1 representing mild, unilateral involvement and stage 5 indicating severe disability requiring full-time care [ ]. Clinically, PD is characterized by motor symptoms such as bradykinesia, resting tremor, muscle rigidity, and postural instability, as well as a range of non-motor features, including cognitive decline, mood disorders, sleep disturbances, anosmia, and autonomic dysfunction [ , , , , , , , ]. Notably, non-motor symptoms can precede motor manifestations by years, during what is termed the “prodromal” phase [ ].
The precise mechanisms underlying the selective loss of DA neurons remain poorly understood, and current treatments primarily provide symptomatic relief without stopping disease progression. It has been well-documented that the loss of DA neurons arises from several avenues of effect. Defects in protein clearance pathways, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuro-inflammation, oxidative stress, calcium dysregulation, and genetic mutations are all implicated in PD pathology [ , , , , ]. Among the multifarious risk factors influencing the pathogenesis of PD, the interplay of mitochondrial impairment and oxidative stress is increasingly recognized as a core pathogenic axis in PD, primarily because dopaminergic neurons are intrinsically prone to reactive oxygen species (ROS)-induced damage [ ]. This interaction is a major determinant of their degeneration and contributes to the clinical manifestation of PD symptoms [ , , ].
Emerging evidence proposes that targeting mitochondria represents a promising new therapeutic strategy for reducing neurodegeneration in PD [ ]. Even with the outstanding progress made within the last 2 decades toward unraveling the complex pathophysiology of PD, this multifactorial disease remains incurable. However, research focuses on exploring potential treatments using various in vitro and in vivo models of PD. Among multiple attempts in this area, "gasotransmitters" or "gaseous mediators" like nitric oxide (NO), carbon monoxide (CO), and hydrogen sulfide (H2S) are garnering increasing attention for their therapeutic potential roles, such as anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects at low concentrations [ ]. Although these colorless gases have traditionally been classified as industrial toxins for humankind, it is now well recognized that all are synthesized endogenously in mammalian tissues, are biologically active, and play significant roles in both physiological and pathological contexts through their distinctive signaling pathways [ , , , , , ]. Several lines of study have revealed the protective effects of these gaseous mediators; for example, H2S exhibits antioxidant [ ], anti-inflammatory [ , ], and neuroprotective activity [ ], particularly in neurodegenerative disorders such as PD [ ]. Both in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated H2S and/or H2S donors exert protection against the pathophysiological onset and progression of apoptosis, oxidative stress, and inflammation in several PD models. H2S also prevented motor impairments and TH-positive neuron death [ , , ]. PD patients with elevated NO levels tended to exhibit more advanced clinical symptoms and longer disease courses, as reflected in UPDRS metrics [ ]. Interestingly, patients that have experienced CO intoxication (COI) exhibited almost a 10-fold higher risk of PD in an observational study [ ].
Normally, NO and CO contribute to vasodilation, inflammation, and neuroprotection [ , , , , ]. Recently, an in vivo study using rodent models of PD demonstrated that low-dose CO reduces DA cell death and enhances the heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) pathway, a mechanism involved in cell protection [ ]. Moreover, it is worth noting that these gaseous mediators specifically target mitochondria and impact their metabolic function [ ]. Continued elucidation of gasotransmitter pathways may revolutionize how we conceptualize and approach neuroprotection in the clinical setting. This review summarizes the main features of PD, covering the synthesis, signaling pathways, and physiological effects of H2S, NO, and CO, with a particular focus on their relevance to PD and potential innovative therapeutic implications.

## PD and mitochondrial dysfunction
While the etiology of PD remains unclear, it is widely accepted that both genetic predisposition and environmental factors contribute to the onset and progression of the disease. The majority of cases are sporadic, although numerous genetic mutations have been associated with familial forms [ , ]. Key converging mechanisms in PD pathology include impaired protein clearance and mitochondrial dysfunction [ , , ]. Deficiencies in the ubiquitin-proteasome and autophagy-lysosomal pathways exacerbate α-syn accumulation and toxicity [ ], while mitochondrial dysfunction leads to increased oxidative stress.
A well-cited older study by Song et al. (2004) demonstrated the overexpression of α-syn in an 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)-induced mouse model of PD impairs mitochondrial activity and increases oxidative damage, thereby highlighting the link between α-syn and mitochondrial dysfunction [ ]. Mitochondria are essential for ATP production, calcium homeostasis, and apoptosis regulation [ , , ]. In PD, complex I of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) is often deficient [ , ], leading to ROS overproduction and neuronal injury [ , ]. This has been supported by in vitro and in vivo studies implicating a complex I defect in DA neuron loss [ ].
Another seminal 2015 study by Pacelli et al. compared DA neurons in the SN and the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and found that SN neurons exhibit greater mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, more extensive axonal arborization, and higher oxidative stress than VTA neurons—factors contributing to heightened vulnerability in PD [ ]. Similarly, Geibl et al. demonstrated that α-syn pathology suppresses mitochondrial gene expression and ATP production in DA neurons, reinforcing the link between α-syn aggregation and mitochondrial impairment [ ].
Genetic knock-in and knock-out studies in animal models as well as human epidemiological studies further support this relationship [ ]. Mutations in genes such as Parkin, PINK1, DJ-1, and LRRK2 disrupt mitochondrial quality control, dynamics, and biogenesis, worsening PD development and facilitating continued disease progression [ , , ]. Environmental mitochondrial toxins such as MPTP and rotenone are widely used in preclinical PD models due to their selective toxicity toward DA neurons [ ].
Substantial evidence in recent years has indicated roles of NO, CO, and H2S in modulating mitochondrial function implicated in PD pathogenesis [ , ]. These gaseous molecules localize to mitochondria and can inhibit ETC activity in a concentration-dependent manner, particularly through suppression of cytochrome c oxidase [ ]. At physiological levels these molecules act as antioxidants and signaling cues, yet at pathologically elevated concentrations they contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction further discussed below. NO, synthesized by nitric oxide synthases (NOS), interacts with different components of the mitochondrial respiratory chain. High concentrations of NO irreversibly inhibit complex I, promoting oxidative stress and worsening mitochondrial injury [ ]. In contrast, low NO levels confer protection by scavenging ROS and maintaining redox balance [ , ]. The dual role of NO is influenced by factors such as its site of generation, local concentration, and molecular interactions [ ].
Endogenously synthesized H2S, also exhibits biphasic effects. At low concentrations, it preserves mitochondrial membrane potential and reduces oxidative stress, while higher concentrations impair mitochondrial respiration. In a myocardial ischemia model, Elrod et al. showed that H2S preserved mitochondrial function and reduced inflammation [ ].
Similarly, CO, generated by heme oxygenase (HO) activity, demonstrates cytoprotective actions at low concentrations by attenuating ROS production and stabilizing mitochondrial respiration [ ]. In a hemorrhagic shock model, CO administration protected mitochondrial integrity and function [ ].
Collectively, these findings highlight the complex and dose-dependent interactions between gasotransmitters and mitochondria, suggesting that therapeutic modulation of NO, CO, or H2S could improve mitochondrial function and protect DA neurons in PD.

## Experimental models of PD
Although no model perfectly mirrors the human disease conditions, animal models have been critical toward fostering our current understanding of how PD develops. A valid model for PD should mimic the neuropathological and clinical features of the disease as closely as possible. The heterogeneity of PD (both in symptomatology and molecular pathology) is reflected in animal models that employ either neurotoxin-based approaches or transgenic manipulations to emulate the characteristic pathological features of PD. Several vertebrate and invertebrate models of PD have been established to date, including Caenorhabditis elegans (C. elegans), Drosophila, rodents, and non-human primates. Rodent (mouse, rat) models are of the most commonly used experimental representations of PD progression and development [ ].
Pioneering PD research relied heavily on neurotoxins like MPTP and 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA) to create neurotoxin-based models. However, the identification of PD-related genes and mutations has also led to the development of valuable genetic models [ ]. In this section, we aim to provide a brief overview of the most extensively studied and available PD models. It is important to note that the models summarized here do not represent a comprehensive list. For a detailed description of PD models, please see this book [ ].

## Neurotoxin-based models
Neurotoxins such as MPTP, 6-OHDA, and pesticides (rotenone and paraquat) are capable to induce oxidative stress, leading to DA neuronal damage, thus replicating a core pathology observed in human PD. MPTP, a lipophilic toxin, easily crosses the blood brain barrier (BBB) and recapitulates the cardinal symptoms of PD following systemic injection. Initially, astrocytes take up MPTP, where it undergoes oxidation by monoamine oxidase-B (MAO-B) and is finally converted into the toxic 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium (MPP+). This compound enters DA neurons through the dopamine transporter (DAT) and induces the production of ROS [ , , ]. In addition, MPTP inhibits complex I of the mitochondrial ETC, reduces ATP synthesis, and enhances oxidative stress [ ]. However, models using acute doses of MPTP lack the formation of LB, a key neuropathological hallmark of PD in humans [ ]. 6-OHDA, a structural analogue of dopamine and norepinephrine, is extensively used to induce degeneration of DA neurons in animal models and to explore motor and biochemical impairments in PD. Unlike MPTP, 6-OHDA cannot cross the BBB [ ], thus it needs to be delivered directly into the brain using stereotaxic surgery in specific regions such as the SNc, striatum, or medial forebrain bundle (MFB; a tract containing DA axons projecting from the SNc to the striatum) [ ]. 6-OHDA can enter both DA and noradrenergic neurons via monoamine transporters. To avoid noradrenergic neuron damage, a selective noradrenergic reuptake inhibitor (e.g., desipramine) must be administered during surgery [ ]. Neurotoxin 6-OHDA exerts its detrimental effects by producing free radicals, increasing oxidative stress, and disrupting mitochondrial function in DA neurons, leading to rapid neuronal death that can range from moderate to massive [ ]. It should be noted that 6-OHDA can be utilized to model non-motor symptoms of PD as well [ ].
Pesticides, herbicides, fungicides, and insecticides have been used to recapitulate PD features including those relevant to oxidative stress [ , , , ].
Rotenone, like MPTP, is lipophilic and readily crosses the BBB. Following systemic injection, the pesticide causes mitochondrial dysfunction, increased ROS production, and LB formation, resulting in significant loss of DA neurons and motor abnormalities [ ]. Paraquat is structurally similar to MPP+, and its systemic injection is used to create a PD model. This herbicide generates free radicals, resulting in oxidative stress-mediated neurotoxicity and eventually DA neuron death [ ]. It also exhibits a low affinity for complex I of mitochondria [ ]. However, it is thought that its primary mechanism of toxicity is the generation of ROS. The presence of LB-like structures has been reported in rodent studies in both models [ ].

## Genetic models
A positive family history of PD is associated with an increased risk of developing the condition. Furthermore, genetic mutations linked to PD are found in about 10 ​% of individuals displaying clinical signs of the disease. To date, multiple genes and mutations related to PD pathology have been identified. Pathogenic and mutagenic variants in genes including SNCA, DJ-1, LRRK2, Parkin, and PINK1 alter the expression and activity of proteins governing kinase-driven signal transduction such as Phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/Protein Kinase B (AKT) and Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK). These signaling pathways regulate mitochondrial biogenesis and function, ROS production, autophagy, and cell survival/apoptosis [ , ].
The SNCA gene encodes the protein α-syn and was the first genetic link found for familial PD [ ]. This gene can induce PD when it undergoes duplication, triplication, or point mutations [ ]. The duplication or triplication of SNCA results in elevated levels of α-syn. This excess α-syn is prone to misfolding and aggregation, which is a key factor in PD progression [ ]. As previously noted, misfolded α-syn accumulates to form the major constituent of LB. To investigate α-syn pathology, researchers have developed several animal models, including transgenic mice that overexpress α-syn and virally induced expression of α-syn. Notably, while many of these mouse models do not exhibit substantial loss of DA neurons, they do demonstrate impaired dopamine release, altered TH expression in the striatum, and certain behavioral abnormalities [ ].
Mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired protein clearance pathways and age-related neurodegeneration have been reported in a mouse model containing α-syn overexpression [ , ]. Mutations in the DJ-1 gene, also known as PARK7, are associated with autosomal recessive and early-onset PD. However, mutations in DJ-I are rare. DJ-1 knockout (KO) mice revealed no degeneration of DA neurons; however, some of them showed mitochondrial malfunction, altered dopamine transmission, and decreased locomotor activity [ , ]. Mutations in the LRRK2 gene are the most common cause of late-onset autosomal dominant PD [ ]. In general, most rodent models carrying LRRK2 mutations do not display significant loss of DA neurons or alterations in striatal DA levels. Nonetheless, some models demonstrate motor impairments and DA pathology in the striatum [ ]. Mutations in the Parkin and PINK1genes are associated with autosomal recessive familial PD. Parkin and PINK1 KO mice show no significant deficits in dopamine release, DA neuron degeneration, or LB formation. However, they do exhibit mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative damage, reduced locomotor activity and a moderate decrease in striatal DA levels [ , , ]. In addition, Parkin overexpression in rats resulted in progressive and age-dependent DA cell death [ ].

## Gasotransmitter synthesis and signaling

## NO synthesis and signaling
NO is a gaseous signaling molecule with diverse functions in the brain and other organs, including the regulation of neurotransmission, cerebral blood flow, and immune responses. In humans, NO is produced through two main pathways. The first is the l-arginine–NO oxidative pathway, in which nitric oxide synthase (NOS) enzymes convert l-arginine and molecular oxygen into NO and citrulline [ , ]. The second is the nitrate–nitrite–NO reductive pathway, which becomes more prominent under hypoxic or acidic conditions and involves the enzymatic or non-enzymatic reduction of nitrate and nitrite to NO [ ].
Three principal NOS isoforms are expressed in the nervous system: neuronal NOS (nNOS or NOS1), inducible NOS (iNOS or NOS2), and endothelial NOS (eNOS or NOS3) ( ) [ ]. In neurons, NO is synthesized on demand by neuronal NOS (nNOS), which is activated by calcium influx following glutamate-mediated N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptor stimulation. NO acts as a retrograde messenger to modulate synaptic plasticity and neurotransmitter release [ ]. nNOS is predominantly localized in neurons of the cortex, hippocampus, cerebellum, and spinal cord [ , ]. 
Endothelial NOS (eNOS) is expressed in endothelial cells of the cerebral vasculature and choroid plexus [ ]. Inducible NOS (iNOS) is typically upregulated in glial cells such as microglia and astrocytes during neuroinflammation, although low-level constitutive expression has been reported in some brain regions and vascular smooth muscle cells [ ].
NOS enzymes function as homodimers and require several cofactors for activity, including NADPH, tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4), flavin mononucleotide (FMN), and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) [ ]. Both nNOS and eNOS are calcium/calmodulin-dependent enzymes: elevated intracellular Ca2+ promotes calmodulin binding, which displaces an autoinhibitory loop and enables electron transfer from NADPH in the reductase domain to the heme group in the oxygenase domain [ ]. In contrast, iNOS binds calmodulin with high affinity even at low Ca2+ concentrations and is constitutively active once expressed, producing sustained, high levels of NO—up to 100–1000 times more than nNOS or eNOS—until it is degraded [ , ].
Oral bacteria utilize nitrate as an alternative electron acceptor during respiration, reducing the anion to nitrite. Nitrite-rich saliva then enters the acidic environment of the stomach, where it is chemically reduced to NO, which subsequently diffuses into the circulation. The contribution of the nitrate–nitrite–NO pathway to total NO production is estimated at approximately 100 ​mmol/day, compared to ∼1000 ​mmol/day produced by the NOS-dependent l-arginine–NO pathway [ ]. Nitrate reduction to nitrite and then to NO also occurs in blood and peripheral tissues. Mammalian tissues express nitrate reductase activity and can convert nitrate to nitrite under normoxic conditions [ ]. Nitrite reduction to NO proceeds via both enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms, including acid-catalyzed reactions and disproportionation [ , ]. Enzymes involved in the enzymatic reduction of nitrite to NO include xanthine oxidoreductase, aldehyde oxidase, deoxygenated hemoglobin and myoglobin, cytochrome P450, cytochrome c, and some components of the mitochondrial respiratory chain [ , ].
NO signals through both cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP)-dependent and cGMP-independent pathways ( ) [ ], with the former being the most prominent in the central nervous system [ , , , , ]. NO activates guanylyl cyclase (sGC) [ , , ], which converts guanosine triphosphate (GTP) to cGMP [ ]. Elevated cGMP levels activate protein kinase G (PKG), modulate ion channels, and influence cAMP signaling via the regulation of cGMP-sensitive phosphodiesterases [ , , ]. These cascades affect intracellular calcium dynamics and contribute to neuronal excitability, plasticity, and survival. NO also modulates MAPK pathways in a concentration- and context-dependent manner. Under conditions of oxidative stress or high NO levels, it can activate p38 ​MAPK and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), contributing to inflammation and apoptosis [ , ]. While at lower concentrations, or under physiological conditions, NO may promote cell survival through ERK1/2 activation [ , ]. 
NO mediates post-translational protein modifications. S-nitrosylation—the reversible covalent attachment of an NO group to cysteine thiols—can alter protein function, subcellular localization, and interactions [ , , ]. This modification is typically mediated by NO itself or nitrosating species such as dinitrogen trioxide (N2O3), rather than by peroxynitrite (ONOO−). In contrast, ONOO− formed by the reaction of NO with superoxide, mediates nitration reactions, especially of tyrosine residues [ , ]. In the nervous system, NO influences the release and activity of neurotransmitters, including acetylcholine, noradrenaline, dopamine, glutamate, GABA, serotonin, ATP, CO, opioids, and endothelin [ ]. These effects involve a cascade of mechanistic components, such as cGMP signaling, receptor S-nitrosylation, modulation of ion transport, and regulation of mitochondrial energy metabolism [ , , , ]. Furthermore, NO can regulate gene expression in neurons and glia by modulating transcription factors like NF-κB and CREB, thereby influencing both neuroinflammatory and neuroprotective responses [ , ].

## CO synthesis and signaling
CO is produced endogenously in virtually all cell types through heme degradation by HO enzymes, which cleave heme into biliverdin, ferrous iron (Fe2+), and CO ( ) [ ]. HO enzymes—primarily HO-1 (inducible) and HO-2 (constitutive)—are endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-associated and function alongside NADPH-cytochrome P450 reductase [ ]. HO-1 is upregulated under cellular stress, including within the CNS [ , , ], whereas HO-2 maintains basal expression in neurons, glia, and cerebral vasculature [ , , ]. A third isoform, HO-3, has been identified in rats, though its physiological role remains unclear [ ].
Endogenous CO acts via two primary mechanisms: activation of the sGC/cGMP/PKG pathway [ ] and carbonylation of specific amino acid residues such as lysine, arginine, and threonine [ ]. CO binds to the Fe2+ moiety of heme within sGC, though its efficacy is approximately 1/80 to that of NO [ , ].
Protein carbonylation, once considered merely a marker of oxidative protein damage and degradation, is now recognized as a reversible regulatory modification. This reversibility enables dynamic modulation of protein activity by CO [ ]. In biological systems, CO exhibits selective binding to transition metals, with iron-containing heme proteins being the most extensively studied. Unlike NO, which can bind both oxidized (Fe3+) and reduced (Fe2+) iron, CO binds exclusively to the reduced Fe2+ form of heme [ ].
CO has also been shown to modulate MAPK signaling pathways ( ). For example, it can inhibit pro-inflammatory p38 ​MAPK signaling or activate ERK1/2 pathways, contributing to cell survival, anti-inflammatory responses, or cytoprotection depending on the context [ , , ]. Additionally, CO may also influence JNK signaling in oxidative or inflammatory conditions, highlighting its context-sensitive modulation of the MAPK cascade [ , , ].

## H2S synthesis and signaling
H2S is synthesized in mammalian tissues through four main enzymatic pathways ( ) involving tissue-specific enzymes: cystathionine β-synthase (CBS), cystathionine γ-lyase (CSE, also known as CGL or CTH), the tandem system of cysteine aminotransferase (CAT) and 3-mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase (3-MST), and the d-amino acid oxidase (DAO)/3-MST pathway, which is especially relevant in the cerebellum and kidney ( ) [ , ]. CBS and CSE are pyridoxal-5′-phosphate-dependent enzymes that act on cysteine and homocysteine, while 3-MST catalyzes the conversion of 3-mercaptopyruvate, derived from cysteine via CAT [ ]. DAO generates 3-mercaptopyruvate from d-cysteine, adding to the functional complexity of the H2S system [ ].
CBS and CSE are primarily localized to the cytosol [ , ], whereas 3-MST and CAT are found in both the cytosol and mitochondria [ , ]. Mitochondrial localization of 3-MST and CAT enables compartment-specific H2S production, which plays a critical role in regulating mitochondrial bioenergetics and redox balance [ , ]. Given the significantly higher concentration of cysteine in mitochondria compared to the cytoplasm, it is likely that most of H2S produced by 3-MST occurs within the mitochondria [ , ]. All four enzymes are expressed in the CNS [ , , , ] with CBS and 3-MST serving as the primary contributors in the brain [ , , , , ]. CBS is enriched in the hippocampus and cerebellum and is found in astrocytes and microglial cells [ , ], whereas 3-MST is primarily found in neurons and astrocytes [ , ]. Astrocytes have been reported to generate relatively high levels of H2S in vitro compared to microglia or neuron-like cells [ , ]; however, whether they represent the dominant in vivo source of H2S in the brain remains unclear and requires validation through cell-specific knockout studies and in vivo quantification.
In the CNS, H2S plays a multifaceted role. It acts as a neuromodulator, redox regulator, and anti-inflammatory mediator, with functions highly dependent on concentration and cellular context [ , ]. One major mechanism of H2S signaling is the post-translational modification of cysteine residues in proteins, known as S-sulfhydration (or persulfidation), which alters protein conformation and function [ ] ( ).
H2S protects cells from oxidative damage by directly scavenging ROS and by upregulating antioxidant defenses. It activates the Nrf2 signaling pathway [ ], a transcription factor that promotes the expression of numerous antioxidant enzymes. H2S also modulates the MAPK cascade—enhancing ERK1/2 signaling under protective conditions while activating p38 and JNK during stress or injury [ , ]. S-sulfhydration of phosphodiesterase 5 (PDE5) inhibits its dimerization and enzymatic activity, increasing intracellular cGMP levels and further contributing to vascular relaxation and signal transduction [ ].
Impaired protein persulfidation has been reported in experimental models and patient samples of PD and Huntington's disease, linking deficient H2S signaling to key pathogenic processes such as mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and loss of protein activity; for example, decreased parkin persulfidation in PD impairs its E3 ligase function (see details below), and widespread persulfidation deficits have been documented in HD models [ ].

## The role of NO, CO, and H2S in PD

## NO in PD
NO plays diverse roles in the brain, ranging from neurotransmission to vascular regulation. In dopaminergic circuits, physiologically low levels of NO—primarily generated by nNOS and eNOS—facilitate synaptic signaling and help maintain blood flow in the substantia nigra and related regions. These actions are generally protective and support normal neuronal function. These beneficial effects are primarily mediated by nNOS and eNOS, with eNOS-derived NO contributing to neurovascular coupling and vascular homeostasis [ ]. Contrarily, elevated nNOS expression in the midbrain has been identified in PD patients upon postmortem tissue analyses [ ]. However, in pathological contexts such as neuroinflammation, NO production is markedly increased due to the upregulation of iNOS, resulting in nitrosative stress and neurotoxicity [ ]. Excess NO reacts rapidly with O2− to form ONOO−, a highly reactive oxidant that damages lipids, DNA, and proteins [ ]. One critical downstream effect of ONOO− is the nitration of tyrosine residues ( ) on cytoskeletal and synaptic proteins, including α-syn and tubulin [ , ]. Tyrosine nitration alters protein structure, promotes aggregation, and impairs intracellular transport. Nitrated α-syn, in particular, has been shown to enhance fibril formation and contribute to LB development [ ] ( ). 
Postmortem analyses of PD brains have commonly revealed elevated NO and iNOS expression in the SNpc, the region most severely affected in PD [ ]. In experimental models, including MPTP- and rotenone-induced neurotoxicity, increased NO levels correlate with the loss of dopaminergic neurons. Genetic studies have clarified the contributions of different NOS isoforms to PD pathology. Mice lacking iNOS are significantly protected against MPTP-induced dopaminergic neuron loss, consistent with the role of iNOS in inflammation-driven NO overproduction. nNOS knockout mice also show partial protection, implicating neuronally derived NO in excitotoxic injury. These results point to distinct but overlapping mechanisms by which excessive NO from glial and neuronal sources contributes to neurodegeneration in PD [ ].
Despite its pathological potential, NO is essential for neuronal viability. Systemic NOS inhibition is associated with impaired neurotransmission, reduced cerebral blood flow, and cognitive dysfunction [ ]. Thus, therapeutic strategies that seek to modulate rather than abolish NO signaling are gaining attention (discussed in section 5).
Glial cells also contribute to NO dysregulation in PD. During reactive gliosis, activated microglia and astrocytes become significant sources of iNOS-derived NO in the SNpc [ ]. This excessive NO not only damages neurons but also impairs the viability of glial cells, thereby amplifying neuroinflammation. A pathogenic feedback loop may ensue: neuronal injury triggers glial activation, which in turn sustains NO overproduction, exacerbating dopaminergic neurodegeneration. NO also mediates neurotoxicity through post-translational modification of neuroprotective proteins. One example is the S-nitrosylation of parkin discussed in section 4.4.1.

## CO in PD
CO was first recognized as a neurotransmitter in 1993, following the discovery of its production via HO enzymes and its ability to activate sGC in neurons [ ]. Since then, accumulating evidence suggests that low or near-physiological concentrations of CO are essential for maintaining CNS homeostasis [ ]. While the precise molecular mechanisms of CO signaling remain incompletely understood, physiological CO levels are associated with cytoprotective effects, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic actions [ , ], ( ). The HO-1/CO pathway, in particular, has been studied as a therapeutic target in PD and other neurodegenerative disorders [ ]. For instance, simvastatin has been shown to induce HO-1 and upregulate antioxidant defense proteins, including Nrf2-responsive genes, leading to neuroprotection in PD models [ ]. While the original study did not directly test CO's role in this effect, CO is known to contribute to redox balance via sGC-PKG signaling and Nrf2 activation, raising the possibility that some antioxidant effects of HO-1 may be mediated through CO [ , , ]. This remains speculative and warrants direct testing. 
Activation of Nrf2 by HO-1-derived CO orchestrates the expression of antioxidant genes, including SOD and HO-1 itself, forming a positive feedback loop that reinforces cellular redox defense [ ]. These cytoprotective effects are also associated with the preservation of mitochondrial membrane potential, inhibition of caspase activation, and suppression of inflammatory cytokine release. For example, treatment with 2′,3′-dihydroxy-4′,6′-dimethoxychalcone (DDC) was shown to increase HO-1 expression and confer neuroprotection via GC/PKG signaling in both in vitro and in vivo PD models [ ].
In addition, CO has been implicated in promoting neurotrophic signaling and neuronal differentiation. Exogenous CO exposure enhances the expression of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and glial-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) in both glia and neurons [ , ]. One study showed short-term low-dose CO exposure improved the survival and dopaminergic differentiation of human neural stem cells in vitro [ ]. Complementing these findings, recent investigations have extended COs therapeutic promise to in vivo contexts. For instance, inhaled CO therapy in rodent models of PD demonstrated protective effects on dopaminergic neurons and reduced microglial activation, potentiating its anti-inflammatory and neurorestorative effects [ ]. Similarly, therapeutic administration of CO via CO-releasing molecules (CORMs) showed beneficial outcomes in attenuating neuroinflammation and preserving neuronal viability in neurodegenerative settings [ , ]. These studies moreover suggest controlled CO delivery may support neurogenesis and promote neural repair, dopaminergic resilience, and anti-inflammatory signaling. While low-dose CO exerts neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects, at high concentrations or under pathological conditions, CO can suppress mitochondrial respiration, reduce ATP production, and enhance oxidative injury. Clinical evidence has linked carbon monoxide intoxication (COI) to a significantly increased risk of developing Parkinsonian syndromes [ ]. While hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) is often administered following CO poisoning, one retrospective cohort study found that patients receiving HBOT after COI had higher rates of subsequent PD diagnosis than those who did not [ ]. This likely reflects underlying injury severity and is not evidence of HBOT-induced harm. COI has also been associated with depressed endogenous antioxidant defenses [ ]. These findings highlight the narrow therapeutic window of CO in the CNS and the need for precise dosing.

## H2S in PD
H2S has many diverse biological functions in the CNS, including cytoprotection, modulation of redox status, and regulation of inflammatory signaling. Alterations in H2S metabolism have been implicated in the pathogenesis of PD. In preclinical models, significantly reduced levels of endogenous H2S were observed in the striatum, correlating with enhanced oxidative stress and neuronal damage [ ]. Previous research has also shown H2S prevents mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) collapse, and cytochrome c release, caspase-9/3 activation and poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase cleavage, suggesting a potential therapeutic avenue involving its antioxidant capacity [ ].
H2S exerts antioxidant effects through multiple up- and downstream mechanisms ( ). It scavenges ROS, enhances glutathione (GSH) synthesis, and inhibits pro-oxidant signaling cascades [ , , ]. Importantly, H2S has been shown to preserve the activity of glutamate transporters under oxidative stress, thereby preventing glutamate accumulation and excitotoxicity—a mechanism relevant to PD pathophysiology [ , ]. In this context, H2S mitigates the inhibitory effects of hydrogen peroxide on glutamate uptake, stimulates ATP production, and suppresses the ERK1/2 ​MAPK pathway, culminating in reduced excitotoxic injury [ ]. These antioxidants and anti-excitotoxic actions are closely linked, as oxidative stress and glutamate dysregulation often co-occur in neurodegenerative conditions, including PD [ , ]. 
H2S also exhibits anti-inflammatory properties by modulating microglial activation and cytokine production. Microglia, the brain's resident immune cells, contribute to PD progression by releasing pro-inflammatory mediators, including NO and TNF-α. Neuroinflammation is not a passive consequence, but a driver of degeneration, supported by longitudinal imaging studies (e.g., PET with [11C]-PK11195) showing that microglial activation precedes overt neuronal loss in PD patients [ ]. Misfolded α-synuclein released from dying neurons binds to pattern recognition receptors on microglia including TLR2, TLR4, and CD36, triggering a pro-inflammatory cascade, supporting NF-κB activation and NLRP3 inflammasome assembly [ ]. In PD models, exogenous H2S donors suppress the expression of these cytokines in the striatum, suggesting a role for restraining neuroinflammation [ , , ].
Building on these mechanisms, several H2S-releasing compounds have demonstrated neuroprotective effects in experimental models of PD. In a 6-OHDA-lesioned rat model, the H2S donor sodium hydrosulfide (NaHS) restored autophagic flux via the leptin signaling pathway, attenuating dopaminergic neuronal loss [ ]. Additionally, a novel nanocarrier platform combining H2S release with siRNA delivery was shown to modulate inflammatory gene expression and reduce oxidative damage in MPTP-induced PD mice [ ]. In that study, the siRNA targeted TNF-α, supporting a dual approach to modulating inflammation and redox imbalance. While these strategies remain experimental, they highlight the need for integrated delivery platforms that can address multiple pathogenic mechanisms in PD.
H2S modulates complex biochemical pathways integral to mitochondrial bioenergetics. At low concentrations, H2S enhances mitochondrial respiration and supports ATP production, whereas high concentrations inhibit cytochrome c oxidase and impair oxidative phosphorylation [ , ]. In PD models H2S administration reversed deficits in ATP levels and normalized oxygen consumption [ , ]. These effects highlight the concentration-dependent nature of H2S and its relevance to mitochondrial resilience under neurotoxic stress. While 3-MST is considered the primary H2S-producing enzyme within the mitochondria, some findings suggest that under certain stress conditions, such as hypoxia, the normally cytosolic enzyme CSE can translocate to the mitochondria to augment local H2S production [ ]. The specific cell types in which this occurs and the direct relevance of this mechanism to the chronic progression of PD are not clear. This shift promotes ATP generation while reducing oxygen consumption, a process referred to as metabolic inhibition, which enhances resistance to hypoxia [ ]. Additional findings suggest that 6-OHDA induces PD-like pathology by reducing ATP production and increasing oxygen consumption—effects that are reversed by H2S [ , ].

## Interplay between NO, CO, and H2S
NO, CO, and H2S exhibit complex, bidirectional interactions that modulate each other's synthesis, degradation, and signaling pathways. These interactions contribute to integrated regulation of vascular tone, inflammation, mitochondrial function, redox homeostasis, and neuronal survival [ ], and . While each gas has distinct biosynthetic routes and primary effectors, they frequently converge on shared molecular targets [ , , ]. 

## NO–H2S crosstalk
H2S enhances eNOS activity and expression through Akt phosphorylation and intracellular Ca2+ mobilization, leading to increase NO production and vasodilation [ , ]. H2S also stabilizes eNOS dimerization and preserves the essential cofactor BH4, thereby improving NO bioavailability [ ]. In addition, H2S inhibits PDE5, resulting in increased intracellular cGMP levels and potentiation of NO-cGMP signaling, a critical mechanistic interaction that extends beyond NO generation alone [ ]. Conversely, NO can regulate H2S production by modulating CBS and CSE activity ( ). For example, NO binds to the heme moiety of CBS, inhibiting its enzymatic activity [ , ], while NO may also upregulate CSE expression under specific physiological or pathological conditions [ ]. Together, NO and H2S form hybrid species such as thionitrous acid (HSNO), which may act as reservoirs for bioactive sulfur and nitrogen species, modulating downstream protein function through S-nitrosylation and S-sulfhydration [ , , ].
One of the most well-defined molecular intersections between NO and H2S signaling in PD involves their opposing effects on parkin, a key E3 ubiquitin ligase whose dysfunction contributes to dopaminergic neuronal loss in PD [ ]. NO inactivates parkin via S-nitrosylation [ ], whereas H2S restores its function through S-persulfidation, thereby regulating mitophagy and dopaminergic neuron survival [ , ]. This reciprocal regulation has also been demonstrated in vivo: postmortem brain tissue from individuals with PD exhibits reduced persulfidated parkin and elevated nitrosylated parkin, reinforcing the functional antagonism between these two redox modifications [ ].
Relatively recent work from a large consortium has provided crucial insights into the reciprocal regulation of NO and H2S in mitochondrial signaling. In a 2019 study, Zivanovic et al. demonstrated that endogenous H2S prevented S-nitrosylation-induced dysfunction of mitochondrial proteins, in part by preserving thiol redox homeostasis under conditions of oxidative and inflammatory stress [ ]. Another study identified a layer of crosstalk at the level of post-translational modification between NO and H2S involving S-nitrosation and S-sulfhydration competing at the same cysteine residues. H2S can persulfidate enzymes such as cysteine desulfurase NFS1, enhancing Fe–S cluster biogenesis and mitigating mitochondrial vulnerability to NO–driven nitrative stress [ ]. On the other hand, elevated nitric oxide can S-nitrosate CBS and CSE, suppressing endogenous hydrogen sulfide production and tipping the balance toward oxidative damage. Some groups have demonstrated co-application of NO donors and H2S donors results in the formation of nitrosopersulfide species and polysulfides, signaling molecules with unique biological properties that cannot be recapitulated by either gasotransmitter alone [ , , ]. These findings elucidate a broader protective role for H2S in maintaining mitochondrial function and in limiting aberrant NO signaling during PD pathogenesis.

## NO–CO crosstalk
Both NO and CO activate sGC, although CO is a significantly weaker agonist. CO can attenuate NO-mediated responses by occupying the heme moiety of sGC or by stabilizing its oxidized, inactive form [ , ]. Conversely, NO can induce HO-1 expression via Nrf2-and ERK1/2-dependent signaling pathways, thereby increasing endogenous CO production under inflammatory conditions [ , , , , , ]. CO also regulates NOS isoform expression: it inhibits iNOS by suppressing NF-κB signaling [ , ], and modulates eNOS activity through activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway [ ]. These reciprocal regulatory effects may contribute to the fine-tuning of neurovascular homeostasis and glial activation during the progression of PD.
While direct evidence of NO–CO crosstalk in PD is limited, CO exerts neuroprotective effects through HO-1 induction and modulation of dopaminergic and glutamatergic signaling. For instance, CO inhibits dopamine and glutamate uptake in the striatum independently of NOS activity [ , ]. Dysregulation of the HO-1/CO system has also been observed in PD models and patients, suggesting that CO may influence neurovascular and glial pathways relevant to PD pathogenesis, even in the absence of direct NO interaction.

## CO–H2S crosstalk
H2S can upregulate HO-1 expression by inducing mild oxidative stress and activating the Nrf2 pathway, thereby increasing endogenous CO production [ , , ]. Conversely, CO can inhibit H2S biosynthesis by binding to the heme moiety of CBS, suppressing its activity in a redox- and context-dependent manner [ , , , ]. These reciprocal interactions modulate both antioxidant defenses and mitochondrial respiration. Notably, both CO and H2S inhibit cytochrome c oxidase, but with distinct physiological outcomes: CO induces a reversible hypometabolic state with cytoprotective effects, while H2S exhibits biphasic, concentration-dependent effects on mitochondrial respiration and ATP production [ , ].
Dysregulation of the HO-1/CO system has been observed in both experimental models and patient samples of PD, suggesting that CO may influence neurovascular and glial pathways relevant to PD pathogenesis, even in the absence of direct interaction with H2S. In a transgenic mouse model, astrocyte-specific overexpression of HO-1 (GFAP–HMOX1) led to progressive dopaminergic neurodegeneration, α-syn accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and motor deficits—recapitulating key features of PD pathology [ ]. Complementing these findings, elevated serum HO-1 levels have been detected in PD patients, which correlate with substantia nigra iron deposition, anemia, and hippocampal atrophy, suggesting that HO-1 dysregulation may contribute to systemic and neurovascular impairments [ ]. Together, these findings suggest that the HO-1/CO axis is involved in glial and vascular dysfunction in PD.

## Shared convergence on PDE and MAPK pathways
A central signaling axis where all three gasotransmitters converge is the cGMP–PDE5 pathway. NO and CO stimulate sGC to produce cGMP, while H2S prolongs cGMP signaling by inhibiting PDE5 [ ]. This convergence is particularly relevant in neurovascular regulation, synaptic plasticity, and mitochondrial biogenesis—processes disrupted in PD. For example, inhaled H2S administration has been shown to protect against MPTP-induced dopaminergic neuron loss in mice via antioxidant and anti-inflammatory mechanisms [ ], while PDE5 inhibitors such as sildenafil have demonstrated neuroprotective and cognition-enhancing effects in models of memory impairment, including improved object recognition and hippocampal cGMP signaling in mice [ ] their impact on mitochondrial function and dopaminergic survival in PD models has not been directly established. NO–cGMP signaling is also known to support synaptic transmission and plasticity, but direct experimental evidence in PD-specific contexts remains limited. [ ]. All three gases also modulate MAPKs. NO and H2S activate ERK1/2 and p38 ​MAPK in a redox-sensitive manner [ , , ], while CO generally inhibits JNK and p38 but activates cytoprotective ERK1/2 signaling [ , , , ]. These effects influence glial reactivity, apoptosis, and cytokine production—critical components of PD pathophysiology.

## Therapeutic targeting of gasotransmitters in PD: Current progress and future directions
NO, CO, and H2S are increasingly recognized as modulators of pathological features of PD. Their pleiotropic signaling capabilities make them attractive therapeutic targets; however, the development of clinically viable delivery strategies remains a significant challenge. This section highlights current approaches and future directions in gasotransmitter-based therapies for PD.

## NO-based therapeutics
In the CNS, NO exerts concentration-dependent effects. At physiological levels, it supports neurotransmission, vasodilation, and synaptic plasticity [ , , ]. However, excessive NO—particularly derived from iNOS—contributes to nitrosative stress, tyrosine nitration of α-syn, and mitochondrial dysfunction in PD [ , , ]. Pharmacological inhibition of iNOS using selective inhibitors such as 1400W and L-NIL has demonstrated neuroprotective effects in toxin-induced PD models [ , ].However, systemic NO donors have shown limited clinical success due to poor brain-targeting and potential for exacerbating oxidative stress [ , ].
Low-dose NO donors have demonstrated neuroprotective effects in specific models by enhancing blood flow, supporting mitochondrial function, and promoting dopaminergic signaling. For example, molsidomine, a NO donor, has been shown to reduce behavioral deficits, increase striatal dopamine content, and provide neuroprotection in 6-OHDA-lesioned rats [ ], while S-nitrosoglutathione (GSNO) has been shown to protect dopaminergic neurons and improve motor function in 6-OHDA-lesioned rats through antioxidant and anti-apoptotic mechanisms [ ]. NO-releasing NSAIDs such as HCT1026 (a flurbiprofen derivative) have demonstrated efficacy in MPTP-treated mice, preserving dopaminergic fibers, improving motor performance, and reducing neuroinflammation compared to the parent NSAID [ ]. However, the therapeutic duality of NO underscores the importance of spatial and temporal control over its bioavailability. Compounds like HCT1026 have demonstrated CNS efficacy in preclinical PD models—indicating sufficient brain penetrance and controlled NO release. Their success highlights the importance of chemical design in ensuring therapeutic NO delivery without triggering harmful nitrosative effects.
Several studies have explored nanotechnology-based approaches to achieve spatial and temporal control over NO bioavailability. For example, carbon dot (CD)-based nanocarriers have been developed to deliver NO in a controlled manner, often employing diazeniumdiolate or NO-releasing functional groups to enable time- or stimulus-responsive release. Such platforms have shown in vitro antibacterial and anticancer efficacy—sometimes paired with fluorescent imaging—but have not been validated in neurodegenerative or PD-specific models [ , ]. However, few studies include rigorous mechanistic controls (such as NO scavengers or PD-relevant endpoints), underscoring the need for disease-focused and functionally validated evaluations before clinical translation.
Although NO-releasing nano formulations have been evaluated in cardiovascular and oncology models—e.g., PLGA-based nanoparticles designed for vascular restenosis and dendrimer-conjugated NO donors [ , , ]—few validated studies have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy of NO-releasing nanocarriers in PD, although recent work using PLGA-based systems has begun to explore their application in neurodegenerative contexts [ ]. Consequently, claims regarding NO nanomedicine for PD remain largely speculative.

## CO-based therapeutics
CORMs, such as CORM-2 and CORM-3, have demonstrated vasodilatory, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective effects in various preclinical models [ , ]. However, the field faces significant methodological challenges that limit the interpretation and translation of these findings. For example, CORMs contain transition metals (e.g., ruthenium in CORM-2 and CORM-3, but also iron, manganese, cobalt, and molybdenum in other CORMs) that can elicit biological effects independent of CO. These metals may influence cellular redox state, enzyme activity, or signaling pathways [ ] and have raised concerns for long-term toxicity in a clinical setting [ , , ]. Thus, while CORMs remain valuable tools, establishing CO-specific mechanisms requires carefully controlled studies with appropriate comparators, including CO-inert analogs and metal-only vehicles.
Therapeutic targeting of CO has also progressed via alternative strategies using inhaled CO and PEGylated hemoglobin-based CO carriers. Phase II clinical trials for indications such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (NCT00122694; 2006), Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (NCT01214187; 2015) [ ], vaso-occlusive crises (NCT02672540, NCT02411708; 2017) and ulcerations (NCT02600390; 2016) of sickle cell disease, subarachnoid hemorrhage (NCT02323685; 2016), and kidney transplant (NCT02490202; 2016) [ ]. In addition, there is an active phase II clinical trial (August 2024) observing the safety and efficacy of inhaled CO for the treatment of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) (NCT03799874). These trials suggest that low-dose CO administration is generally safe and well tolerated.
Another therapeutic approach involves pharmacologic induction of HO-1 to elevate endogenous CO levels. Several small molecules with anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects act partly through HO-1 induction [ , , ], although none have entered clinical trials. Furthermore, the association between these therapeutic properties and endogenous production of CO is yet to be established. On the other hand, HO-1 inhibition is being explored in oncology, where excess CO may promote tumor progression and immune evasion. For example, inhibition of HO-1 using zinc protoporphyrin IX (ZnPPIX) has demonstrated potent antitumor activity in vivo, highlighting the potential of targeting CO signaling in cancer contexts [ ].
While none of these CO-based interventions have yet been tested in PD, they provide valuable insights into safety, dosing, and feasibility. Translating CO therapy to PD will require rigorous preclinical validation of CO-specific mechanisms, including appropriate delivery systems and brain-targeted release kinetics.

## H2S-based therapeutics
Among gasotransmitters, H2S has shown the most robust neuroprotective profile in PD models. It exerts antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, anti-apoptotic, and mitochondrial-supportive effects [ ]. Exogenous donors such as NaHS, GYY4137 (a slow-releasing donor), and AP39 (mitochondria-targeted) have demonstrated efficacy in both 6-OHDA and MPTP models by preserving dopaminergic neurons, reducing oxidative stress, and suppressing glial activation [ , ].
A notable innovation is the development of H2S-releasing nanocarriers. Zhao et al. (2024) engineered a nanomotor-based system that crosses the blood–brain barrier, accumulates in dopaminergic neurons, and delivers H2S in response to pathological signals [ ]. This system attenuated α-syn aggregation, reduced inflammatory and oxidative markers, and improved motor and cognitive deficits in MPTP-lesioned mice.
H2S-releasing nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (HS-NSAIDs), such as S-aspirin and S-diclofenac, have also demonstrated neuroprotective effects. In glial–neuron co-culture models, these compounds reduced microglial and astrocyte-mediated neurotoxicity more effectively than their parent NSAIDs or inorganic sulfide salts. Mechanistically, they attenuate the release of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α and IL-6) and downregulate MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways, highlighting their potential for treating inflammatory neurodegenerative disorders [ ].

## Hybrid gasotransmitter strategies
A growing area of interest involves hybrid molecules that release multiple gasotransmitters. NOSH-NSAIDs [ ] (compounds releasing both NO and H2S) exhibit enhanced anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties compared to parent drugs [ ]. Recent preclinical work shows that NOSH-aspirin (NBS-1120) protects dopaminergic neurons and improves motor function in a 6-OHDA rat model of PD [ ], and suppresses neuroinflammation in glial–neuron co-cultures by attenuating microglial and astrocytic activation, reducing TNF-α and IL-6 production, and promoting neuronal survival [ ] demonstrating that dual-donor compounds may offer synergistic benefits in neurodegenerative diseases. Importantly, these effects were observed despite prior concerns that NO at high concentrations—such as that produced by iNOS—can exacerbate PD pathology. The neuroprotection seen with NOSH-aspirin suggests that the NO released is likely within a physiological range, sufficient to support vasodilation, mitochondrial function, and antioxidant defenses without tipping into nitrosative stress.
The collective evidence presented in this review illustrates the complex, context-dependent roles of the endogenous gasotransmitters NO, CO, and H2S in both the pathogenesis and potential treatment of PD. Traditionally viewed as mere toxicants, these small gaseous molecules are now appreciated for their broad regulatory functions within the CNS. Their influence spans oxidative stress modulation, mitochondrial dynamics, synaptic plasticity, neuroinflammation, and cell survival—processes intimately tied to the pathogenesis and progression of PD.
Physiologically, NO, CO, and H2S play protective roles in maintaining neuronal homeostasis. At low concentrations, NO supports dopaminergic neurotransmission, vasodilation, and neurovascular coupling. CO contributes to cytoprotection by activating antioxidant signaling pathways downstream of HO-1, including sGC/PKG and Nrf2, and modulates neurotransmitter release through its interactions with ion channels and sGC. H2S exhibits potent antioxidant activity, enhances glutamate uptake, suppresses microglial activation, regulates autophagy, and supports mitochondrial ATP production, particularly under hypoxic stress. Each gasotransmitter, therefore, possesses intrinsic neuroprotective potential when maintained within physiological limits.
However, under conditions of dysregulation, these same molecules can exacerbate the pathology of PD. Excessive NO production promotes peroxynitrite formation, leading to the nitration of key proteins, such as α-syn, and mitochondrial dysfunction—all of which contribute to dopaminergic neurodegeneration. CO, although cytoprotective at low levels, can inhibit mitochondrial respiration and antioxidant defenses at high concentrations, particularly in the context of environmental exposure or CO poisoning. Similarly, H2S, while protective in moderation, becomes detrimental at high concentrations by impairing mitochondrial function and increasing oxidative stress. These findings underscore the importance of concentration-dependent effects and the necessity for tightly controlled regulation of gasotransmitter levels in therapeutic contexts.
Effective therapeutic strategies must account for the dual, concentration-dependent roles of gasotransmitters in neuroprotection and neurotoxicity. Advances in delivery systems have begun to address this challenge. NO-releasing nanoparticles, CORMs, H2S-donating compounds, and the dual NO–H2S-releasing aspirin (NOSH-aspirin) have shown promise in preclinical models of PD by enabling the controlled, sustained, and localized release of these gases. Furthermore, cell-based therapies, such as MSC-EVs, engineered to deliver H2S or modulate NO/CO signaling, offer additional specificity and translational potential. The inclusion of biosensor technologies capable of monitoring real-time fluctuations in gasotransmitter levels may also pave the way for personalized medicine approaches in PD management.
Despite these advancements, several challenges remain. The therapeutic window for each gasotransmitter must be precisely defined, taking into account variability in individual redox states, disease stage, and regional brain vulnerability. Long-term safety and off-target effects of gasotransmitter-releasing systems are not yet fully understood and require comprehensive investigation in both animal models and clinical trials. The crosstalk between NO, CO, and H2S signaling pathways introduces further complexity that must be addressed through systems-level approaches and integrated network modeling.
In conclusion, NO, CO, and H2S constitute a dynamic trio of gasotransmitters with profound influence over neurodegenerative processes in PD. Their roles are not inherently beneficial or harmful, but are defined by dose, timing, cellular context, and/or interactions/coordinations with other up- or down- stream molecular pathways. Translating these insights into safe and effective therapetic interventions will require a deeper understanding of gasotransmitter biology, precise delivery platforms, and real-time monitoring technologies.
