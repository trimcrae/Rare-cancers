## Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a leading cause of dementia that represents a formidable challenge in neurodegenerative disease research and healthcare, with the accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tangles as its hallmark features (Busche and Hyman, 2020). According to the latest report, the number of individuals living with AD worldwide surpassed 35 million in 2010, marking an increase of 10% over the estimate published in 2005 by Alzheimer Disease International. Furthermore, the number of patients with dementia is projected to almost double every 20 years, reaching 65.7 million in 2030 and 115.4 million in 2050 (Dartigues, 2009). The surge in the prevalence of AD is predominantly fuelled by new data from low- and middle-income countries. September 21, 2006 is World Alzheimer’s Day, which commemorates the 100th anniversary of identifying and naming the clinicopathological entity that is now recognized as the primary cause of dementia syndrome and represents one of the most challenging conditions of old age (Ferri et al., 2005). Nevertheless, AD has historically been devoid of effective treatment strategies. Particularly, drugs targeting the hallmark features are of limited efficacy. Moreover, the current clinical efficacy of Lecanemab and Donanemab (Aβ protein-targeting drugs) reflects the presence of additional significant pathogenic processes in AD (Boxer and Sperling, 2023). Therefore, it is of significance to deeply investigate the pathogenesis of AD, to discover the new therapeutic targets for AD and develop therapeutic regimens with high clinical advantages and few adverse effects.
Neuroinflammation is a major contributing mechanism to the development of AD (Mangalmurti and Lukens, 2022). Specifically, resident microglia experience pro-inflammatory activation during the progression of AD, leading to increased conversion of resting astrocytes into reactive astrocytes. Additionally, inflammatory cytokines are released from the peripheral system due to the leakage of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), thus facilitating the progression of AD (Yue and Hoi, 2023). Therefore, blocking the activation of microglia-astrocytes can restrain neurodegeneration in AD, highlighting microglia and astrocytes as potentially vital therapeutic targets for AD (Park et al., 2021).
As a subtype of purinergic receptors, the P2Y1 receptor (P2Y1R) specifically responds to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and, to a lesser extent, adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and assumes an important role in various physiological processes, including platelet aggregation, smooth muscle contraction, and neurotransmission (Burnstock, 2018). Because of its involvement in the central nervous system (CNS), P2Y1R emerges as a promising therapeutic target for a spectrum of neurological diseases and disorders (Weisman et al., 2012). Notably, P2Y1R is involved in neuroinflammatory pathways, which offers novel insights into potential therapeutic interventions for AD. To be specific, targeting P2Y1R can provide a novel treatment approach that slows down or alters the course of AD by repressing brain inflammation. In this context, this review analyzed emerging evidence on the relationship between P2Y1R and AD (Luo et al., 2023), with a focus on the potential neuroprotective effects of P2Y1R in AD, which is not only academic but also opens up new avenues for drug development and holds promise for developing more effective treatments for AD. As a result, this review can help researchers and pharmaceutical developers develop therapies that more accurately target the underlying mechanisms of AD, potentially improving the outcomes of individuals affected by this devastating condition.

## Search Strategy
Articles cited in this narrative review published from 1996 to 2023 were searched on PubMed and Google Scholar with the following keywords: P2Y1 receptor, purinergic receptors, neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation, reactive astrocyte, AD, and gene therapy. A total of 123 articles were cited in this review.

## Current Status of Research on Alzheimer’s Disease
AD, which presents as a gradual decline in memory, thinking, behavior, and social skills, is a growing threat to the global economy and health as the population in the world is aging. The World Alzheimer Report 2022 showed that over 55 million individuals suffered from AD or related conditions across the world, with this number expected to reach 82 million by 2030 and 138 million by 2050 (Gauthier et al., 2022). AD exerts a profound impact on the physical and mental well-being of individuals while imposing a substantial economic burden on society. As a burgeoning public health challenge, AD requires concerted global efforts to decelerate its progression and increase the quality of life of affected individuals and their caregivers. Nonetheless, there is no effective treatment for AD. As such, there is an urgent need for intensive research on unknown cellular mechanisms underlying AD to develop effective and safe therapeutic options.
It has been broadly accepted that AD is characterized by the deposition of Aβ plaques and neurofibrillary tangles consisting of hyperphosphorylated tau (Scheltens et al., 2021). Treatment strategies for AD are varied, including targeting of Aβ protein, modulation of neuroinflammation and immune responses, inhibition of tau protein phosphorylation, repression of oxidative stress, enhancement of neuroprotection, mediation of neurotransmitter receptors, and interventions for vascular factors (Lucke-Wold, 2022). Additionally, these strategies may involve the use of biological therapies such as monoclonal antibodies, small molecule inhibitors, and repurposed agents from other diseases. Emerging research on AD also focuses on the role of factors including lipids, lipoprotein receptors, neurogenesis, bioenergetics, metabolism, cell death, growth factors, hormones, circadian rhythms, and epigenetic regulators in the progression of AD (Cummings et al., 2023b). As of early 2023, a total of 187 clinical trials in various phases have been conducted to assess 141 unique treatments for AD, with a large number of trials in phases 2 and 3 (Cummings et al., 2023a). Among these tested treatments, there are a few overlapping therapeutic targets. According to these trials, the development of anti-Aβ protein drugs has become mainstream, although many drugs targeting this protein have failed in clinical trials (Asher and Priefer, 2022) and only Lecanemab, a drug targeting Aβ plaques in the brain of AD patients, was fully approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in July 2023, becoming the first fully FDA-approved drug for AD in 20 years (Sara Reardon, 2023). However, the clinical use of drugs clearing Aβ protein cannot decrease the global burden of AD (Karran and De Strooper, 2022). summarizes the development state of novel drugs for AD in clinical settings. Apparently, research on most of the recently developed drugs for AD, including those targeting Aβ, is proceeding slowly, underlining the pressing need to identify novel therapeutic targets for AD. Despite some advancements in these pipeline drugs, challenges persist, such as the limited clinical efficacy of new therapies and demand for more effective and safer treatment options. ## Role of Purinergic Receptors in the Central Nervous System
P2Y1R is a subtype of purinergic receptors responding specifically to ADP and ATP, which is responsible for diverse physiological processes, including platelet aggregation, smooth muscle contraction, and neurotransmission (Burnstock, 2018). P2Y1R has a broad regional, cellular, and subcellular distribution in the brain, which emerges as a potential therapeutic target for numerous neurological diseases and disorders because of its involvement in the CNS (Nobili et al., 2022). Of note, there exists a therapeutic hypothesis that P2Y1R inhibition has neuroprotective effects in pathological conditions such as brain injury (Sun et al., 2008; Kuboyama et al., 2011).
Since P2Y1R is abundant in astrocytes (Shigetomi et al., 2018), astrocytic P2Y1R regulates synaptic function (Kruyer et al., 2023) and neuronal activity (Lines et al., 2020) and is required for the control of astrocyte activity and astrocyte-neuron communication. In addition, P2Y1R also synchronizes neuronal activity by mediating neuron-glia interactions and calcium waves (Shigetomi et al., 2018). Microglial P2Y1R can regulate microglial activity, which is critical for neuroprotection. A prior study demonstrated that microglia responded to extracellular signals, such as ATP, by upregulating P2Y1R, orchestrating inflammation (Kita et al., 2019). Another study revealed the P2Y1 agonist MRS2365 diminished tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α production induced by lipopolysaccharide stimulation, therefore protecting neurons from excessive inflammatory damage (Kita et al., 2019). In neurons, P2Y1R is distributed in dendrites, cell bodies, and axons non-synaptically, pre-synaptically, and post-synaptically (Kolen and Slegers, 2006; Amadio et al., 2007). Pre-synaptic P2Y1R controls the release of neurotransmitters including glutamate (Simões et al., 2018) and gamma-aminobutyric acid by modulating N-type Ca2+ channels in neurons (Donato et al., 2008). Post-synaptic P2Y1R downregulates N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor, which affects synaptic plasticity (Martorell et al., 2020). In addition, neuronal P2Y1R is involved in regulating neuronal differentiation (Yano et al., 2012). A prior study displayed that P2Y1R activation in neurons can induce neuronal excitotoxicity (Guzman and Gerevich, 2016). Therefore, it can be hypothesized that P2Y1R inhibition can exhibit neuroprotective effects. Interestingly, the activation of specific P2YR subtypes, particularly P2Y1R has been unveiled to facilitate axonal elongation and boost Na+- and Cl–-dependent glycine transport in the synaptic cleft, thereby contributing to neuroprotection (Erb et al., 2015; Alves et al., 2019). In conclusion, the impacts of P2Y1R on AD may vary across cell types.
The widespread presence of P2Y1R in the CNS contributes to its complex effect on brain function and disease regulation. As a member of the P2Y receptor family, P2Y1R affects the activity of other receptors and interacts with a range of brain receptors. For instance, the activation of P2Y1 receptors results in the inhibition of P2X receptors, while P2Y1R activates Gβγ subunits to suppress post-synaptic NMDA receptors (NMDAR) in layer 5 pyramidal cells of the prefrontal cortex (Luthardt et al., 2003; Kolen and Slegers, 2006; Simões et al., 2018). These complex interactions of P2Y1 further underline the intricate role of P2Y1R in the development of diseases. Given the multifaceted functions of P2Y1R, it is crucial to investigate the function of P2Y1R in individual cell types and it is promising to study the specific roles of P2Y1R at the single-cell level and to analyze its potential as a therapeutic target for AD.
Several ionophilic and metabolic receptors are distributed in astrocytes, which trigger the entry or release of Ca2+ from inositol 1,4,5-triphosphate (IP3), including purinergic receptors and G protein-coupled metabolizing adenosine (A1, A2A, A2B, and A3) receptors (Sherwood et al., 2017; Lopes et al., 2021). As exhibited in a former study, ATP-induced Ca2+ responses in Aβ1–42-exposed rat astrocytes were reduced in amplitude but prolonged in duration compared to those in control astrocytes, which was mediated by P2X7R and P2Y1R. Furthermore, Aβ1–42 exposure elevated P2Y1R protein levels in astrocytes, and the A2AR antagonist SCH58261 governed both P2X7R- and P2Y1R-mediated Ca2+ responses in astrocytes in a protein kinase A–dependent manner (Dias et al., 2022).

## Astrocytic P2Y1R regulates glial activation and thereby influences neuroinflammation in AD
The involvement of glia in neuroinflammation has become a hotspot for research on AD. Accumulating clinical and molecular data highlight the involvement of neuroinflammation mediated by astrocytes and microglia in the pathogenesis of AD (Sofroniew, 2015; Stephenson et al., 2018; Ge et al., 2022). A previous study has demonstrated the role of neuroinflammatory markers in the development, diagnosis, and treatment of neurodegenerative diseases. Immune cells, such as microglia or astrocytes, orchestrate the release of inflammatory molecules, including TNF-α, interleukin (IL)-1β, nitric oxide, and cyclooxygenase-2 (Rauf et al., 2022). Upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines and downregulation of anti-inflammatory cytokines result in neuroinflammation, respectively, and thus elicit the onset and/or progression of diseases.
Astrocytes, a subtype of glia, play numerous essential roles in the normal functioning of the brain, such as synaptogenesis, BBB permeability regulation, and extracellular homeostasis maintenance. In addition to the function of favoring signaling between astrocytes and other cells, astrocytes also regulate extracellular pH and glutamate uptake in the brain (Siracusa et al., 2019). As reported, astrocytes are in a reactive state in numerous neurodegenerative conditions (Makarava et al., 2021). Reactive astrocytes are a cellular component of gliosis in neurodegenerative diseases such as AD, which are detected in the early stages of AD, even before neuronal death, and are ubiquitous throughout disease progression (Kumar et al., 2023). It has been widely recognized that classical markers such as glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) immunoreactivity can be used for determining the extent of astrocyte reactivity. In general, reactive astrocytes can suppress or exacerbate neuropathology (Pekny et al., 2016). However, the mechanisms underlying this effect are incompletely understood.
As resident macrophages in the CNS, microglia are activated in response to systemic inflammation or neurodegeneration (Tang and Le, 2016; Leng and Edison, 2021; Tiwari et al., 2021). Chronic microglial activation is neurotoxic, which may foster the progression of neurodegenerative diseases. It has been recently revealed that microglial activation provokes the transformation of resting astrocytes into reactive astrocytes in a variety of neurodegenerative diseases including AD and Parkinson’s disease by secreting IL-1α, TNF-α, and C1q (Liddelow et al., 2017; Li et al., 2022). In other words, resident microglia experience pro-inflammatory activation in AD due to the existence of factors including Aβ, tau, and neuronal tangles, encouraging the transformation of resting astrocytes into reactive astrocytes.
Reactive astrocytes, as well as neuroinflammation modulated by both microglia and astrocytes, facilitate the progression and severity of AD. Specifically, highly reactive astrocytes produce excess hydrogen peroxide due to the presence of monoamine oxidase B, inducing glial activation, tauopathy, and neuronal death and leading to brain atrophy, cognitive impairment, and even death (Chun and Lee, 2018). Meanwhile, altered reactive astrocyte function in AD can impact neurotransmitters, which is crucial for maintaining synaptic balance (Verkhratsky and Nedergaard, 2018). It has been reported that cleavage of amyloid precursor protein (APP)-cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE1), the beta-site of APP, initiates Aβ production (Zhao et al., 2011; Haim et al., 2015). Zhou et al. (2023) observed that BACE1 deficiency in astrocytes increased the proportion of reactive astrocytes and that Aβ clearance–related genes, such as Clusterin (Clu), were markedly upregulated in BACE1-deficient astrocytes, illustrating that inhibiting BACE1 in astrocytes could potentially facilitate amyloid removal, which offers an alternative therapeutic option to attenuate off-target effects correlated with substantial BACE1 inhibition in neurons. In summary, reactive astrocytes may enhance amyloid production. A former study revealed that in AD mice, impaired spike probability, synaptic plasticity, and learning and memory abilities were completely restored by suppressing the production and release of gamma-aminobutyric acid from reactive astrocytes (Jo et al., 2014). Moreover, a clinical trial showed that Aβ was associated with the increased plasma level of phosphorylated tau protein only in individuals with positive astrocyte reactivity (Bellaver et al., 2023). Conclusively, reactive astrocytes are not only a pre-diagnostic sign of AD but also an important pathogenic factor of AD, highlighting that targeting reactive astrocytes is an important treatment strategy for AD.
 P2Y1R can regulate astrocyte and microglia activity, thus protecting CNS function. In AD, P2Y1R is overexpressed in reactive astrocytes and is immunohistochemically colocalized with neurofibrillary tangles and Aβ plaques, indicating its significant role in mediating the progression of AD in the brain (Moore et al., 2000; Delekate et al., 2014). P2Y1R overexpression in reactive astrocytes implicates that modulating P2Y1R activity can be a key strategy for controlling neuroinflammation that contributes to AD progression. Chin et al. (2013) found that P2Y1R inhibition improved post-stroke cognitive deficits in a model of middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO), which may be achieved by reducing inflammation and GFAP-marked astrocytes in the brain. A recent study, which used P2Y1R-short hairpin RNA (shRNA) adeno-associated virus vectors (AAVs) to silence astrocytic P2Y1R in AD mice, elucidated that after 2 months of P2Y1R-shRNA AAV injection (Luo et al., 2023), the number of overactivated astrocytes and microglia decreased in the brain, which maintained the homeostasis of the glial-neuronal network in the brain, accompanied by downregulation of the pro-inflammatory factors IL-6 and IL-1β. This result illustrates that P2Y1R inhibition in astrocytes is associated with neuroinflammation in AD mice and that the reduction of reactive astrocytes and microglia after P2Y1R knockdown in astrocytes may lower proinflammatory factor levels to a certain extent, thereby protecting against neuroinflammation in AD mice.
Because of its importance in modulating reactive astrocyte activity and neuroinflammation in AD, P2Y1R has been increasingly recognized to be crucial for neuroprotection and manipulating P2Y1R may be a potential strategy for enhancing neuroprotection, particularly in conditions including AD. Specifically, targeting P2Y1R possibly reduces astrocyte reactivity, thereby mediating neuroinflammation and offering neuroprotection in AD. As a result, P2Y1R is a potential target for the therapeutic interventions of AD, particularly for neuroinflammation and reactive astrocyte activity in AD, and the therapeutic strategy of targeting P2Y1R can delay the progression of AD and mitigate brain dysfunction.

## Astrocytic P2Y1R affects neuroinflammation in AD possibly by regulating BBB function
As is well known, glia are key to maintaining the tight junctions that constitute the neurovascular unit (NVU) and BBB ( ). Vascular pathology is a well-established feature of AD (Zlokovic, 2005), manifesting as reduced vascular density, decreased cerebral blood flow, and BBB breakdown (Halliday et al., 2016). In the context of AD, dysfunction of glia or BBB assumes a significant role in brain inflammation. Glia are not only responsible for maintaining BBB and NVU integrity but also for strengthening the BBB because of their response to inflammatory signals in pathological states, constraining brain penetration by peripheral immune cells (Sofroniew, 2009). Moreover, this response of glia, in different disease stages and contexts, may aggravate or ameliorate inflammation and tissue damage (Burda and Sofroniew, 2014). During the development of AD, astrocytes, as a key component of the NVU responsible for the structure of the BBB, remain a pivotal cell type associated with the clearance of many proteins in the brain, and their dysregulation fosters Aβ accumulation and AD progression (Zhao et al., 2015). In addition, cerebrovascular dysregulation is also an essential pathophysiological feature of AD, which is attributed to altered NVU kinetics and impaired BBB integrity (Kalaria, 1996, 2000). Moreover, elevated BBB permeability results in the accumulation of various peripheral immune cells and neurotoxic compounds in the brain (Zlokovic, 2008), thus exposing the CNS to the inflammatory factor fibrinogen produced in the peripheral circulation, which has been reported to exert deleterious effects on neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and cognitive function in aged syngeneic mouse models (Davalos et al., 2012; Sweeney et al., 2018; McLarnon, 2022). Intriguingly, fibrinogen is formed in the periphery and not detected in the healthy brain (Wolberg, 2023). Petersen et al. discovered that in the brain of AD mice, the entry of fibrinogen into the brain from the periphery further aggravated inflammation in the brain due to BBB leakage, reflecting that fibrinogen can be used as an evaluation indicator for BBB leakage (Petersen et al., 2018). They also observed that the disruption of the BBB also led to increased inflammation in the brain, which further promoted the progression of AD in mice.
 In addition, pericytes are a component of the BBB, which can maintain the stability of the BBB. Aβ40 and Aβ42 in the brain are deposited in the blood vessels and pericytes of the brain, causing brain damage (Gireud-Goss et al., 2021). Aβ protein accumulation in the pericytes causes pericyte death, which in turn culminates in BBB dysfunction in the brain. The clearance of Aβ protein through the BBB to the peripheral system has been revealed to be mediated by the scavenger receptor LDL receptor-associated protein-1 in pericytes, underscoring that the absence of pericytes in the early stages of AD is a key factor for inducing BBB leakage and failure of Aβ protein transport to the periphery in AD (Ma et al., 2018). Indeed, pericyte depletion, which can be measured with the pericyte marker platelet-derived growth factor receptor-β (PDGFRβ) in the BBB, is strongly linked to the functional collapse of BBB (Nikolakopoulou et al., 2017). Hence, BBB integrity can be affected by various factors that further contribute to the development of AD. Elucidating the role of the BBB in AD is important, and targeting the restoration of BBB integrity is a promising therapeutic strategy for AD.
Considering the complex interactions between cells in the brain, it is plausible to speculate that P2Y1R affects the function of the BBB by modulating astrocyte activity. A recent study exhibited that in AD mice, PDGFRβ expression was substantially elevated and fibrinogen expression was obviously diminished 3 months after specific silencing of P2Y1R in astrocytes. Additionally, this study also unraveled that IL-6 downregulation after astrocytes were isolated from the brains of AD mice further substantiated the decrease in neuroinflammation caused by astrocytic P2Y1R silencing, which might be correlated with improvements in the BBB and cognitive functions (Luo et al., 2023). The BBB transport of amyloid to the periphery is a major form of amyloid clearance from the brain. A previous study reported that reductions in BBB leakage and correction of PDGFRBB–PDGFRβ signaling improved BBB function for amyloid protein transport (Shen et al., 2019).

## Astrocytic P2Y1R serves as a regulator of glial-neuronal network homeostasis in AD
Excitotoxicity occurs in both acute and chronic brain diseases and is a common event in the development of neurodegenerative diseases (Salińska et al., 2005). Excitotoxicity is characterized by neuronal hyperactivation due to overstimulation of excitatory neurotransmitters. According to the study by Yu et al. (2023), excitotoxicity involves abnormal Ca2+ influx toward intracellular spaces and is primarily mediated by highly Ca2+-permeable NMDARs, which can activate proteases such as calpain and then the cell death pathway. Excitotoxicity has also been associated with a broad range of acute and chronic neurodegenerative disorders, oxidative stress, cytoskeletal damage, and mitochondrial dysfunction (Yu et al., 2023). A prior study elaborated that the noxious gain of function of P2Y1R facilitated astrocyte hyperactivity, entrained Ca2+ waves, and induced the release of glutamate by either directly or indirectly attracting microglia and/or predisposing neurons to injury, favoring neurodegeneration in multiple brain pathologies (Rodrigues et al., 2022). To sum up, the neuroprotective role of P2Y1R may be related to calcium activity in the brain.
In AD, Aβ plaques disrupt brain activity without directly influencing calcium in neurons (Magi et al., 2016). Conversely, they significantly impact astrocytes near neurons, eliciting brain damage (Brandebura et al., 2023). Therefore, the neuroprotective effect of P2Y1R in AD may stem from its ability to regulate the function of the neuronal-glial network by maintaining calcium homeostasis in astrocytes. An earlier study reported that increased astrocytic calcium activity was correlated with P2Y1R upregulation and that in AD mice, astrocytic hyperactivity, including calcium waves and transients, was pronounced around Aβ plaques and could be normalized by blocking P2 purinoreceptors or releasing nucleotide via connexin hemichannels (Delekate et al., 2014). Strikingly, this study also displayed that astrocytic hyperactivity could not be affected by P2X receptor blockade but was completely normalized by inhibiting P2Y1R, which is highly expressed in reactive astrocytes around plaques (Delekate et al., 2014). This study suggests that P2Y1R-mediated purinergic signaling in reactive astrocytes may be a therapeutic target for AD since it can ameliorate network dysfunction. Moreover, a study involving the APP/PS1 mouse model of AD revealed that chronic intracerebroventricular infusion of P2Y1R inhibitors not only normalized astrocytic and neuronal network dysfunction but also retained long-term potentiation in the hippocampus and increased the morphological complexity of peri-plaque reactive astrocytes. As a principal factor for neuronal-glial network dysfunction in AD, astrocytic hyperactivity is mediated by the P2Y1R pathway, which is integral to sustaining cellular calcium ion dynamics. Reichenbach et al. (2018) found that P2Y1R repression successfully normalized neuronal-glial network dysfunction in AD, illustrating that the modulation of calcium pathways through P2Y1R blockade holds therapeutic potential for AD. All in all, astrocytic P2Y1R assumes a crucial function in maintaining calcium homeostasis and controlling neuronal-glial interactions, as evidenced by the correlation of its overexpression with enhanced calcium activity in astrocytes. P2Y1R normalizes hyperactivity in reactive astrocytes around Aβ plaques, indicating P2Y1R-mediated purinergic signaling as a prospective therapeutic target.
 A prior study involving IP3-knockdown AD mice has partially demonstrated the importance of astrocytic P2Y1R in calcium ossification in AD but also has highlighted that P2Y1R in other cells may also be involved in this process (Reichenbach et al., 2018). Neuronal P2Y1R results in the loss of axonal integrity, which is caused by upregulated NMDARs and occurs before dendritic injury and after neuronal death, and thus may also play a role in the first synaptic dysfunction/loss (Simões et al., 2018). For example, P2Y1R in calretinin+ interneurons may be implicated in neuronal functioning, which has been extensively accepted to participate in cognitive processes and be affected in neurodegenerative diseases. P2Y1R upregulation may lead to abnormal calcium signaling in calretinin+ interneurons in the hippocampal region because of the association of P2Y1R with intracellular calcium ion flow and regulation (Shi et al., 2020). In AD, aberrant P2Y1R expression may enhance the in-cell flow of calcium ions, which increases neuronal excitotoxicity, a common pathological process in neurodegenerative diseases (Shi et al., 2020). Accordingly, the role of P2Y1R in AD may indeed be related to the regulation of calcium ion activity in neurons and astrocytes, which affects neuronal function and survival. Nevertheless, further research is warranted to confirm the exact effects of P2Y1R in each cell type on AD.

## Controversy exists as to whether neuroinflammation repression by P2Y1R is related to ADP/ATP
Extracellular ATP levels are elevated in different brain diseases. A prior study showed that damaged cells emitted chemicals including ATP when brain inflammation or damage occurred (Cauwels et al., 2014). This study unveiled that in both in vitro and in vivo models, P2Y1R-induced ATP/ADP activation promoted glutamate-induced neuronal death in the rat hippocampus and that glutamate exposure sustainably increased extracellular ATP levels, which resulted in NMDAR-mediated neuronal death through P2Y1R activation. Interestingly, the role of P2Y1R in ATP upregulation acts as a catalyst to lower the threshold for glutamate-induced neurotoxicity, rather than as a direct contributor to neurodegeneration (Simões et al., 2018). A prior study exhibited that P2Y1R regulated astrocyte migration, particularly in spinal cord injury, which was mediated by macrophages via the ADP-P2Y1R axis. To be specific, astrocytes are attracted to P2Y1R via ATP-derived ADP released from macrophages, ultimately causing brain damage in neurological disease (Ono et al., 2023). Another study elucidated that binding of extracellular ATP to P2Y1R conferred protection against glutamate-induced excitotoxicity via Erk1/2 phosphorylation-mediated autophagy inhibition, underscoring the therapeutic potential of ATP for neurodegenerative disorders (Xiong et al., 2022). Therefore, we can hypothesize that ATP activates P2Y1R in microglia and astrocytes, which stimulates microglial and astrocyte activation and results in inflammation, calcium shock, and glutamate release, causing neuronal excitotoxicity and death and further promoting ADP production ( ).
 On the contrary, ATP-induced P2Y1R activation also has been validated to exert neuroprotective effects. Specifically, microglia activate P2Y1R when responding to ATP as a “danger signal,” thus migrating to the injury site and producing inflammatory mediators that contribute to the removal of pathogens and damaged tissues (Fukumoto et al., 2019). In research involving an in vitro model of cerebral ischemia, ATP-induced neuroprotection in the in vitro cocultures of astrocytes and neurons was completely depressed after astrocytic P2Y1R silencing, suggesting that ATP acts on astrocytes and enhances the neuroprotective functions of these cells by activating P2Y1R (Fujita et al., 2009). Moreover, it has also been revealed that P2Y1R, although an adenylate receptor, is not involved in the neuroprotective effect of ATP, implying that the neuroprotective effect of P2Y1R as currently discovered may be independent of ATP. According to studies on the mechanism of P2Y1R in AD, P2Y1R can alleviate neuroinflammation to exhibit neuroprotective functions in AD. Nonetheless, there is still debate as to whether the role of P2Y1R in neuroinflammation is ATP-dependent.
A prior study involving MCAO mice, a model for cerebral stroke and vascular dementia, analyzed the role of P2Y1R, in which the hippocampus-dependent cognitive function was assessed with a contextual fear conditioning test. The results manifested that P2Y1R-knockout MCAO mice showed a decline in sensory–motor function but not in cognitive function (Yano et al., 2012; Chin et al., 2013). In recent research, P2Y1R silencing has garnered attention as a strategy for improving memory ability in AD animals. For instance, Koch et al. found that P2Y1R activation in the medial prefrontal cortex impaired the cognitive function of rats and that the selective P2Y1R agonist MRS2365 decreased the performance accuracy in tasks requiring working memory and impulse control and compromised the performance in reversal learning tasks (Koch et al., 2015). The research by Reichenbach et al. (2018), which involved the APP/PS1 mouse model of AD, displayed that chronic intracerebroventricular infusion of P2Y1R inhibitors not only normalized astroglial-neuronal network dysfunction but also preserved long-term potentiation in the hippocampus to prevent the decline in spatial learning and memory. Another study focusing on astrocytic P2Y1R in APP/PS1 mice with AD unraveled that astrocytic hyperactivity contributed to neuroinflammation and cognitive impairment in AD mice with improved spatial and fear memory (Luo et al., 2023). All these observations illustrate that P2Y1R is related to the cognitive ability of AD animals. Therefore, it can be proposed that astrocytic P2Y1R is a potential target for improving cognitive function in AD.

## Gene therapy is promising in the treatment of AD
Gene therapy is a broad issue, which is now used to describe any procedure that modifies genes in target cells. Currently, gene therapy, especially adenoviral vector- and AAV-mediated gene therapy, has achieved great strides in the development of vaccines and the clinical treatment of cancers. Since adenovirus vector- and AAV-mediated gene therapy does not require the integration of DNA into host cells, the use of AAVs as an intermediary is preferred in clinical practice for the introduction of gene therapy agents into the body. Of note, AAV-mediated gene therapy drugs including Zolgensma® and Hemgenix® were approved by the FDA (Keeler and Flotte, 2019; Navale et al., 2022). The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 enabled the rapid creation and application of an mRNA vaccine, which opened the eyes of experts to the enormous possibilities of gene therapy. Despite the immense promise of gene therapy, no gene therapy drugs are currently available in clinical practice for the treatment of AD.
Gene therapy has emerged as a primary focus for the treatment of AD because of its two significant advantages. First, gene therapy uses methods such as adenovirus vectors, enabling brain cells to autonomously produce drugs and circumventing the issue of BBB constraints on macromolecular drug delivery. Second, gene therapy requires only a single administration because of its long-lasting effects and has the potential to cure diseases if proven safe and effective. Additionally, gene therapy precisely targets diseased cells or tissues, with minimal impact on adjacent healthy tissues. It also allows for the development of personalized treatment based on individual genetic variations. lists gene therapy drugs intended for the treatment of AD. Evidently, the application of gene therapy to AD treatment has opened up the possibility of drugs targeting molecules other than amyloid protein. Accordingly, the incorporation of gene therapy into AD treatment is anticipated to invigorate the development of AD drugs and overcome the current stagnation in their advancement.
 Clinical trials on AAV-based gene therapy for AD are ongoing. In the treatment of AD, AAV-based gene therapy drugs such as AAV2-brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and AAV2-nerve growth factor (NGF) are also currently in clinical trials (Tuszynski et al., 2005; Gao et al., 2022). In a phase 2 clinical trial of AAV2-NGF, 49 mild-to-moderate AD patients aged 55–80 years were treated with a total dose of 2 × 1011 GC of AAV2-NGF through one single localized intracerebroventricular injection targeting the Nucleus basalis of Meynert or underwent sham operation. The results demonstrated that AAV2-NGF treatment was highly safe and improved some symptoms of AD within 2 years (Rafii et al., 2018). AAV2-BDNF is currently in a phase 1 clinical trial with data being collected. Lentiviral vectors harboring BDNF have been confirmed to reverse neuronal atrophy and alleviate age-related cognitive deficits with favorable therapeutic effects in rodents and primates (Nagahara et al., 2009). Although AAV2-NGF and AAV2-BDNF have displayed significant efficacy in studies, AAV2 cannot effectively cross the BBB (Merkel et al., 2017). Hence, direct brain-localized injections are typically used in pre-clinical trials. However, this approach involves higher technical requirements and confronts potential risks although it enhances the therapeutic effect by delivering drugs directly to the specific area of the brain. Specifically, brain-localized injections may increase the complexity of the procedure and may cause patient discomfort with such invasive treatments. Meanwhile, the postmortem analysis in a clinical trial of AAV2-NGF showed that although AAV2-NGF expression in the brain persisted for seven years, its therapeutic effect was not as good as expected, which may be related to the inability of AAV2 to penetrate the brain effectively (Castle et al., 2020). Therefore, future research should focus on developing carriers that can more effectively cross the BBB or on improving existing brain-localized injection techniques, which can not only increase the safety and efficacy of treatment but also potentially reduce the invasiveness of treatment, thereby improving the experience and outcome of patients.
To increase the efficacy of the medicines discovered in gene therapy for AD, a drug delivery system is needed to effectively transport the genome into the brain, thereby reducing the limitation of the BBB on drug penetration. In addition to viral vectors with stronger BBB penetration capabilities, materials such as liposomal or polymeric nanoparticles, novel carbon-based nanomaterials, and magnetic nanoparticles (Seven et al., 2021), as well as specific biocompatible vectors including endogenous proteins or peptides, hold the promise of facilitating the transit of gene therapy vectors for AD treatment through the BBB for effective drug delivery into the brain. Additionally, biomass-derived materials like exosomes, cells, and bacteria can also be considered. For AD treatment, improving drug delivery systems is crucial, but equally important is exploring non-invasive delivery methods. Prospective non-invasive techniques may include focused ultrasound technology and nasal delivery via aerosol inhalation. Fortunately, the aerosolized Ad5-nCoV vaccine has yielded impressive results in phase 1 clinical trials (Wu et al., 2021), indicating that such non-invasive approaches, particularly nasal mucosal aerosol delivery, may eventually replace invasive approaches in current clinical practices. ## Prospect of gene therapy involving P2Y1R in AD treatment
Given the involvement of P2Y1R in neuroinflammation and cognitive function, research on the role of P2Y1R in AD treatment is becoming increasingly important. P2Y1R, especially its interactions with astrocytes, is implicated in the pathophysiology of AD. Recent research has demonstrated that P2Y1R inhibition can suppress calcium oscillation, Aβ accumulation, glia-related neuroinflammation, BBB dysfunction, and cognitive impairment in models of AD. displays the timeline for research on P2Y1R, which may help understand and contextualize the research status of P2Y1R, particularly in quickly changing fields.
 Emerging research on the use of AAVs, especially P2Y1R-shRNA, for the treatment of AD opens up novel frontiers in gene therapy. A recent study demonstrated that astrocytic P2Y1R silencing via AAVs markedly improved the cognitive ability of AD mice (Luo et al., 2023), implicating the potential of AAV-mediated astrocytic P2Y1R silencing as a promising therapeutic strategy for AD. However, the journey from preclinical success to clinical application of this treatment option is fraught with challenges and considerations, among which safety is one of the foremost concerns. Although AAVs harboring P2Y1R-shRNA have no side effects in mouse models, their safety in humans remains under investigation. Future studies are required to rigorously evaluate the safety and potential toxicity of astrocytic P2Y1R silencing in humans.
Moreover, available studies focus on the transcript of mouse P2Y1. Accordingly, future endeavors should focus on the development of a silencing vector for human P2Y1 and the analysis of its knockdown effect and safety in animal models, laying the groundwork for human trials.
Additionally, it is imperative to modify the drug delivery method for effective and safe transport of P2Y1R-shRNA. AAV-PHP.eB, a specially engineered variant of AAV9, is the AAV serotype previously used in the construction of P2Y1R-shRNA AAVs, which has advantages in BBB crossing and transduction efficiency in the CNS (Pietersz et al., 2021). Hence, the use of suitable AAV serotypes, which are more likely to penetrate the BBB, provides the possibility of using a non-invasive approach to deliver AAV drugs for gene therapy into the brain of AD patients to achieve therapeutic benefits.
Understanding the exact mechanisms by which P2Y1R silencing improves cognitive function in AD can help identify new therapeutic targets and pathways. New technologies such as RNA sequencing may offer more details about the mechanism of astrocytic P2Y1R silencing in AD.
When designing clinical trials, researchers need to consider complex ethical factors and rigorous testing standards. For example, long-term efficacy and outcome studies should be used to evaluate the impact of drugs on cognitive function, disease progression, and quality of life in AD patients.
Future research is promising, albeit challenging. With advancements in gene therapy for AD treatment and deeper understanding of AD pathophysiology, therapies targeting P2Y1R are a novel and promising approach to combating this debilitating disease. Though we are eager to utilize P2Y1R silencing vectors constructed based on astrocytic P2Y1R in the clinical treatment of AD, many efforts are needed to utilize AAV-mediated gene therapy for AD treatment.

## Conclusion
AD is the most prevalent form of dementia, which imposes a huge burden on individuals, families, and society due to the lack of effective treatment. Currently, the development of new drugs for AD treatment is progressing slowly. Accordingly, it is imminent to develop novel drugs for AD treatment based on the newly discovered targets. In this context, the purinergic receptor, P2Y1R, is a potential therapeutic target for AD, because it participates in the progression of AD through a variety of mechanisms. For instance, P2Y1R regulates the activity of the glia to stabilizing the homeostasis of the glial-neuronal network, influencing neuroinflammation in the context of AD. In addition, it increases PDGFRβ signaling in pericytes to maintain the integrity of the BBB. The role of P2Y1R in many aspects of AD underlines its importance in AD treatment. In vivo evidence has demonstrated P2Y1R silencing as an effective treatment for AD, with the ability to improve spatial and fear memory, decrease Aβ protein accumulation, repress neuroinflammation, reduce glial-neuronal network dysfunction, and enhance BBB function. Many efforts are required although P2Y1R silencing-related gene therapy is hoped to be utilized in the clinical treatment of AD.
