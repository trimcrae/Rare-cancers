## Background
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is the third leading cause of death worldwide and is characterized by persistent inflammation, microbiota dysbiosis, and excessive neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation. Rosavin, a natural phenylpropanoid glycoside, exhibits anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory activities, but its therapeutic potential in COPD remains unclear.

## Methods
A COPD rat model was induced by intratracheal lipopolysaccharide instillation combined with chronic passive cigarette smoke exposure. Rosavin (50 or 100 mg/kg) or the NF-κB inhibitor BAY 11-7082 (3 mg/kg) was administered for treatment. Pulmonary function tests (FEV1/FVC, PEF, and airway resistance), histopathological evaluation (HE and PAS staining), bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) inflammatory cell counts, ELISA-based cytokine assays, and oxidative stress markers (MDA, MPO, and SOD) were systematically assessed. NET formation was evaluated using MPO-DNA ELISA, Western blotting, and immunofluorescence for CitH3 and MPO/IL-17 colocalization. In addition, lung microbiota composition was analyzed by 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Cigarette smoke extract (CSE)–stimulated BEAS-2B cells were used to assess the direct effects of Rosavin on NF-κB activation in vitro.

## Results
Rosavin significantly inhibited NF-κB activation, improved lung function, and reduced structural damage, oxidative stress, and inflammatory cytokines in COPD rats. It also suppressed NET formation, including IL-17-enriched NETs, by downregulating MPO, NE, and CitH3. In BEAS-2B cells, Rosavin similarly reduced CSE-induced NF-κB activation and cytokine release. Microbiota profiling showed decreased diversity and enrichment of Fusobacterium nucleatum in COPD rats, whereas Rosavin restored beneficial taxa such as Lactobacillus spp. BAY 11-7082 produced comparable effects, supporting NF-κB inhibition as a key mechanism.

## Conclusion
Rosavin ameliorates COPD-associated pathology through integrated mechanisms involving NF-κB inhibition, reduction of IL-17-enriched NET formation, and modulation of lung microbiota composition. These findings identify Rosavin as a promising multi-target therapeutic candidate for COPD.

## Introduction
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive and debilitating respiratory disorder characterized by persistent airflow limitation and chronic airway inflammation. It ranks as the third leading cause of mortality worldwide, contributing significantly to global morbidity and healthcare burden [ ]. Clinically, COPD manifests as chronic cough, sputum production, and progressive dyspnea, which can culminate in respiratory failure in advanced stages [ ]. According to the World Health Organization, over 384 million individuals globally are affected by COPD, with more than 3 million deaths annually. The majority of cases occur in low- and middle-income countries, largely attributable to high smoking prevalence, environmental pollution, and limited access to healthcare resources [ ]. Despite current pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions aimed at alleviating symptoms and slowing disease progression, no effective cure exists, underscoring the urgent need for novel therapies that target the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms [ ]. Among these mechanisms, the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway is recognized as a pivotal regulator of airway inflammation and tissue damage in COPD. Persistent NF-κB activation enhances the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin (IL)-6, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and IL-8, and promotes oxidative stress, perpetuating chronic airway injury [ , ]. Environmental triggers, including cigarette smoke and bacterial infections, induce NF-κB activation by degrading its inhibitor IκBα via Toll-like receptor signaling and oxidative stress pathways [ ]. Notably, suppression of NF-κB signaling has been shown to alleviate COPD symptoms in experimental models, and certain herbal formulations with antioxidant properties demonstrate promising NF-κB inhibitory effects [ ]. Collectively, these observations highlight NF-κB modulation as a promising therapeutic target for interrupting chronic airway inflammation in COPD.
In addition to NF-κB-driven inflammation, excessive formation of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) has emerged as a critical contributor to COPD pathogenesis. NETs, consisting of DNA, histones, and granule proteins such as myeloperoxidase (MPO) and neutrophil elastase (NE), play a role in trapping pathogens. However, excessive NET formation amplifies epithelial injury, promotes airway remodeling, and drives mucus hypersecretion [ , ]. Interleukin-17 (IL-17) has been implicated in neutrophil recruitment and NET formation. In other inflammatory diseases such as periodontitis, NETs have been shown to upregulate IL-17/Th17 responses and exacerbate tissue damage [ ]. These insights highlight IL-17-enriched NETs as a potential therapeutic target for mitigating disease progression.
Moreover, growing evidence indicates that the lung microbiota exerts profound effects on respiratory health and disease. In COPD, microbial dysbiosis characterized by reduced diversity and increased pathogenic bacteria contributes to heightened inflammation, exacerbations, and rapid lung function decline [ , ]. The interplay between the microbiota and innate immune responses, including macrophage and neutrophil activation, underscores the potential of microbiota modulation as a therapeutic avenue [ ]. Notably, probiotic interventions have shown preliminary benefits in restoring microbial balance and dampening airway inflammation in COPD [ ].
Rosavin demonstrates broad pharmacological activities, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory properties, making it a biologically plausible candidate for COPD intervention [ ]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that Rosavin can suppress NF-κB activation, alleviate lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced acute lung injury, and improve microbial dysbiosis [ , ]. However, its therapeutic potential in COPD, particularly its effects on NF-κB signaling, IL-17-enriched NET formation, and lung microbiota composition, remains unexplored. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the multifaceted effects of Rosavin in a COPD model to provide novel insights into its mechanisms and therapeutic value.

## Materials and methods

## Rosavin preparation
Rosavin (≥ 98% purity, HPLC; Cat.No. SML0336) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). For in vivo experiments, Rosavin was dissolved in sterile saline and freshly prepared prior to administration. For in vitro experiments, Rosavin was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a stock solution and subsequently diluted with culture medium, with the final DMSO concentration maintained below 0.1%.

## Measurement of rosavin concentration in lung tissue by LC–MS/MS
Lung Rosavin levels were determined using LC–MS/MS-based quantitative analysis performed by an external certified technical service provider. Lung tissue samples used for this analysis were obtained from rats in the original experimental cohorts and had been collected and stored at − 80 °C following euthanasia, as described above. Briefly, lung tissue samples (~ 50 mg) were processed according to the provider’s standard protocol, including organic solvent extraction, centrifugation, and reconstitution prior to analysis. Quantification was conducted based on external standard curve calibration, and Rosavin concentrations were expressed as ng/g of lung tissue. Only the validated quantitative results were returned for subsequent analysis.

## Establishment of a COPD rat model
The Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee provided approval for the study protocol prior to the rat experiments (Approval No.D202412-9). The selected male Sprague-Dawley rats (6–8 weeks old, 200 ± 20 g) underwent a one-week acclimation period in an environment with humidity and controlled temperature.
The COPD model was developed by placing the rats in a smoke exposure chamber equipped with a negative pressure system. This exposure paradigm represents a passive (sidestream) cigarette smoke exposure model, in which animals inhale smoke indirectly from the chamber environment. Then, cigarettes were ignited, and the smoke was evenly delivered into the exposure chamber, exposing the rats to cigarette smoke for 30 min. After each exposure, the rats were allowed to freely move and recover normal breathing. This process was repeated daily for 8 consecutive weeks. This passive smoke exposure approach more closely mimics environmental tobacco smoke exposure in humans and is widely used in experimental COPD models.
To support the modeling method, the cigarette-smoke–plus–LPS protocol used in this study follows previously reported and widely accepted procedures in COPD animal research [ , ]. In addition, the intratracheal LPS instillation dose (1 mg/kg on days 1 and 28) is consistent with established COPD modeling approaches [ ]. Additionally, on days 1 and 28, the rats underwent anesthesia using isoflurane (4–5% for induction, 1.5–2.5% for maintenance) until loss of the pedal withdrawal reflex, followed by intratracheal instillation of 0.2 mL LPS (1 mg/kg).
Importantly, the treatments were initiated at week 8 (day 56), after the successful establishment of the COPD model, to evaluate the therapeutic effect of Rosavin on established COPD pathology rather than its preventive effect. The dosing regimen of Rosavin (50 mg/kg and 100 mg/kg by gavage) was selected based on previously published studies demonstrating its anti-inflammatory and lung-protective effects in rodent models [ , ]. Treatments for COPD rats were initiated in the eighth week (day 56). For 4 consecutive weeks, the rats were administered equivalent volumes of saline (Control), Rosavin (100 mg/kg, COPD + R-H), the NF-κB inhibitor BAY 11-7082 (3 mg/kg, COPD + BAY), or Rosavin (50 mg/kg, COPD + R-L) once daily via gavage. Rats were housed under standard atmospheric conditions and administered equivalent volumes of saline via intratracheal instillation and gavage as the Control group.
This study ensured that animal welfare requirements were strictly adhered to throughout the experimental procedures. At the end of the experimental period, rats were deeply anesthetized with 5% isoflurane and humanely euthanized prior to tissue collection. Lung tissues were harvested post-mortem for subsequent analyses. Isoflurane is a short-acting inhalational anesthetic with rapid elimination and minimal residual effects on pulmonary inflammation and structural parameters, and therefore is unlikely to interfere with the respiratory outcomes evaluated in this study.

## BEAS-2B cell treatment
BEAS-2B human bronchial epithelial cells were pretreated with Rosavin (20 µM or 60 µM) for 3 h or BAY 11-7082 (2 µM) for 1 h, followed by exposure to 10% cigarette smoke extract (CSE) for 24 h. CSE was freshly prepared as previously described by bubbling the smoke of three commercially available Daqianmen cigarettes (2.5 mg nicotine and 12 mg tar per cigarette) into 10 mL of PBS [ ]. The pH of the resulting 100% CSE solution was adjusted to 7.4 and filtered through a 0.22 μm sterile membrane to remove particulate matter. The 100% CSE was then diluted with serum-free RPMI 1640 medium to the indicated concentrations immediately before use. Untreated cells served as the Control group. After treatment, cells were harvested for Western blot analysis of IκBα and p-IκBα, and culture supernatants were collected for ELISA measurement of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β.

## Differentiation of HL-60 cells and IL-17A knockdown
HL-60 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and differentiated into neutrophil-like cells (dHL-60) by incubation with 1.3% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for 5 days [ ]. Differentiated HL-60 cells were then allocated into five groups: Control (untreated dHL-60 cells), siNC (cells transfected with negative control siRNA), siNC + LPS (siNC-transfected cells stimulated with lipopolysaccharide, LPS, 1 µg/mL for 3 h), si-IL-17A + LPS (cells transfected with IL-17A siRNA and subsequently stimulated with LPS, 1 µg/mL for 3 h), and si-IL-17A + LPS + rIL-17A (IL-17A siRNA-transfected cells treated with LPS, 1 µg/mL, together with recombinant IL-17A, 100 ng/mL), according to published protocols [ , ]. siRNA transfection was performed using a liposome-based reagent following the manufacturer’s instructions. The siRNA sequences were as follows: si-IL17A: 5’-CGGACTGTGATGGTCAACCTGAACA-3’; si-NC: 5’-CGGGTGTTAGGCTAACCGTACAACA-3’.

## Pulmonary function test
For assessing lung function in rats, the Buxco® pulmonary function testing system (DSI, USA) was adopted. Following complete anesthesia with isoflurane (4–5% for induction, 1.5–2.5% for maintenance), the insertion of a silicone catheter into the trachea was conducted, followed by its connection to the respiratory circuit of the testing system. The system was activated to record the rats’ spontaneous breathing parameters. The maximum expiratory flow rate [peak expiratory flow (PEF)] was observed by measuring the tidal volume (TV), the air exhaled or inhaled volume in a single respiratory cycle through airflow. Using the system’s built-in control functions, transient airflow pressure was applied to the rats to induce forced inspiration and expiration. Measurements were taken for the total forced vital capacity (FVC) and forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), followed by calculating the FEV1/FVC ratio. Airway resistance (Raw) was determined by real-time recording the relationship between airway pressure and airflow velocity.

## Western blot
Approximately 50–100 mg of frozen lung tissue was weighed and obtained. After adding 1 mL of radioimmunoprecipitation lysis buffer, the tissues were ultrasonically disrupted on ice (3 s each time with 10-second intervals, repeated 5–8 times). A supernatant was yielded from the lysates after centrifuging for 10 min (12,000 × g, 4 °C) and was collected as the total protein solution. Using a Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (P0028, Beyotime, China), the nuclear protein supernatant was obtained. For detecting the protein concentrations, the Bicinchoninic Acid Protein Assay Kit (P0009, Beyotime, China) was adopted, and all samples were standardized to the same concentration. Then, a protein sample (40 µg) was loaded after preparing a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel. Electrophoresis at 80 V was employed to concentrate the proteins, followed by 120 V for separation. Under wet transfer conditions, polyvinylidene fluoride membranes were applied for protein transfer for 1.5 h at 200 mA. Tris-buffered saline with Tween buffer with 5% skimmed milk was applied for blocking the membranes at ambient temperature for 1 h. As instructed by the manufacturer, the primary antibodies underwent dilution and subsequent overnight incubation at 4 °C. The primary antibodies employed included histone H3 (1:1000, A17562, ABclonal, China), IκBα (1:1000, A19714, ABclonal, China), citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3, 1:1000, 97272, CST, USA), MPO (1:1000, A22900, ABclonal, China), NE (1:1000, A8953, ABclonal, China), IL-17 (1:1000, A21266, ABclonal, China), β-actin (1:80000, AC026, ABclonal, China), NF-κB p65 (1:1000, 8242, CST, USA), and phosphorylated-IκBα (p-IκBα, 1:1000, AP0707, ABclonal, China). Afterward, a 1-hour incubation of the membranes was performed at ambient temperature through horseradish peroxidase-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibodies (1:50000, AS014, ABclonal, China). The ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio-Rad, USA) was utilized for capturing the chemiluminescent band after adding the enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (RM00020, ABclonal, China). The bands’ grayscale values were subjected to quantitative analysis via ImageJ software, and the target proteins were normalized to β-actin or histone H3 (nuclear protein reference).

## Immunohistochemistry
Immunohistochemical staining was performed to evaluate the expression of IκBα, p-IκBα and NF-κB p65 in lung tissues. Briefly, paraffin-embedded lung Sects.  (4–5 μm) were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was conducted using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 15 min. After cooling to room temperature, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, followed by incubation with 5% bovine serum albumin for 30 min to block nonspecific binding. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against p-IκBα (1:200, ABclonal, China) or NF-κB p65 (1:200, CST, USA). After washing, the sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signal was developed using DAB substrate, and counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin. Images were captured using a Leica DM750 microscope, and the integrated optical density (IOD) of positive staining was quantified using ImageJ software.

## Hematoxylin and eosin staining
Following excision, the left lung of the rat was gently rinsed with saline to remove blood. Next, 4% paraformaldehyde was adopted for fixing the lung tissue for 24–48 h. Afterward, the tissue underwent three stages of processing: dehydration (graded ethanol), clearing (xylene), and infiltration (paraffin). Using a Leica CM3050 S microtome (Leica Biosystems, Germany), the lung tissue was sliced into consecutive 4–5 μm sections following paraffin embedding. A graded ethanol series (100%–70%) to distilled water was utilized to rehydrate the sections upon deparaffinization with xylene. Following a 10-minute hematoxylin staining, the sections were rinsed under running water. They were then treated with 1% hydrochloric acid in ethanol and blued in soda water. The section underwent a 30-second staining in eosin solution and a wash with running water. Then, through a graded ethanol series, the section was processed by dehydration, xylene clearing, and mounting in neutral resin. A light microscope (DM750, Leica, Germany) was applied for observing and photographing the stained sections. Mean linear intercept (MLI) was adopted for evaluating the alveolar size, and airway remodeling was assessed via bronchial wall thickness (BWT). Additionally, structural damage of lung tissues was scored based on pathological changes, and inflammation severity was subjectively graded. ImageJ software was employed to quantify the MLI and BWT data. Histological and inflammation scoring were independently evaluated by three pathologists.

## Periodic acid-schiff staining
After sectioning, the rat’s left lung tissue was deparaffinized using xylene and rehydration via a graded ethanol series (100%–70%) to distilled water. Following a 5–10 min oxidation in 0.5%–1% periodic acid solution, the sections were rinsed with running water. Subsequently, Schiff’s reagent was used to immerse the sections at ambient temperature for 10 min in the dark, and they underwent a rinse with running water again. After 1 min of hematoxylin staining, the sections underwent dehydration through a graded ethanol series, xylene clearing, and mounting in neutral resin. Using a light microscope (DM750, Leica, Germany), observation of the stained sections took place, where mucus and nuclei appeared magenta and blue-purple, respectively. Five random fields were selected to record mucus secretion and the goblet cell counts, and the scoring was independently evaluated by three pathologists.

## Inflammatory cell count in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid
Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (4–5% for induction, 1.5–2.5% for maintenance) prior to BALF collection. The trachea was then incised, and a silicone catheter was inserted to ensure connection to the lung. A syringe was adopted for injecting ice-cold sterile saline into the lung via the silicone catheter (2–3 mL per infusion, totaling 8 mL). After remaining in the alveoli for approximately 10 s, the perfusate was slowly withdrawn. The cell pellet was obtained by 10-minute centrifugation (500 × g, 4 °C), while collection and storage of the supernatant were conducted. Then, phosphate-buffered saline (1 mL) was utilized to resuspend the cell pellet. The cell suspension (10 µL) was dropped on the counting plate, and under a microscope, the total cell number was counted. Subsequently, through the centrifugal smear method, the cell suspension (100–200 µL) was used for depositing cells onto slides. A Wright-Giemsa Stain kit (BL1574A, Biosharp, China) was applied for staining the cells, followed by air-drying for further analysis. The different cell type numbers, such as neutrophils and macrophages, were counted by selecting five random fields under a light microscope (DM750, Leica, Germany).

## Cytokine assays
Assessments were taken for the monocyte chemotactic protein 1 (CSB-E07429r, Cusabio, China), TNF-α (CSB-E11987r-IS, Cusabio, China), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CXCL5, CSB-E08179r, Cusabio, China), IL-17 (CSB-E08058Rb, Cusabio, China), IL-6 (CSB-E04640r, Cusabio, China), IL-1β (CSB-E08055r-IS, Cusabio, China), and MPO-DNA (HB1200, hnybio, China) levels in BALF via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits. For ELISA detection, the collected BALF was utilized directly. As per the manufacturer’s guidelines, BALF samples were introduced into the assay wells, followed by capture and washing. Upon color development, measurements of optical density values at 450 nm were conducted via a microplate reader (Synergy HTX, BioTek, USA). Based on the optical density values, a standard curve was plotted, and a calculation of the relative cytokine concentration in the samples was conducted.
Commercial kits were adopted for determining the superoxide dismutase (SOD, S0101S, Beyotime, China), MPO (BC5715, Solarbio, China), and malondialdehyde (MDA, S0131S, Beyotime, China) levels in rat lung tissues. After adding cold lysis buffer (1 mL) to the lung tissue (100 mg), a homogenizer was employed for thorough homogenization. Collection of the supernatant was conducted upon 10-minute centrifugation (12,000 × g, 4 °C). The reaction solution was added as per the kit instructions, and via a microplate reader (Synergy HTX, BioTek, USA), the absorbance of MPO and SOD at 450 nm and MDA at 532 nm was assessed. The standard curve served as the basis for calculating the SOD, MPO, and MDA concentrations.
The concentration of human IL-17A in the culture supernatants of differentiated HL-60 cells was determined using a human IL-17A ELISA kit (Sangon Biotech, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cell culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The clarified supernatants were used directly for analysis. Standard IL-17A solutions were prepared by serial dilution to generate a standard curve. Aliquots of 100 µL standards or samples were added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. After washing, biotin-conjugated anti-IL-17A antibody was added and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated streptavidin at 37 °C for 30 min. After extensive washing, color development was achieved by adding substrate solution in the dark for approximately 15 min, and the reaction was terminated with stop solution. Optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy HTX, BioTek, USA). IL-17A concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve.

## RT-qPCR
Total RNA was extracted from differentiated HL-60 cells using the FastPure Cell Total RNA Isolation Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were collected by centrifugation and lysed in Buffer RL1 supplemented with β-mercaptoethanol. Genomic DNA was removed using gDNA-Filter Columns, followed by RNA binding to RNAPure Columns, sequential washing with Buffer RW1 and RW2, and elution with RNase-free water. RNA concentration and purity were assessed by measuring the absorbance at 260 and 280 nm using a microvolume UV spectrophotometer.
Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the HisyGo All-in-One RT Red SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA Wiper) in a 20 µL reaction system containing 5 µL 4× SuperMix, total RNA (1 pg–1 µg), and RNase-free water. Reverse transcription was performed at 50 °C for 15 min, followed by enzyme inactivation at 85 °C for 5 s.
Quantitative polymerase chain reaction was carried out using SupRealQ Ultra Hunter SYBR qPCR Master Mix (U+) in a 20 µL reaction mixture containing 10 µL 2× SYBR Master Mix, 0.4 µL forward primer (10 µM), 0.4 µL reverse primer (10 µM), template cDNA, and nuclease-free water. The amplification conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. Melt-curve analysis was performed to confirm amplification specificity. The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are listed in Table  .

 

## Immunofluorescence
Following deparaffinization in xylene, the left lung tissue sections were hydrated via a graded ethanol series (100%–70%) to distilled water. Citrate buffer (pH 6.0) was adopted for heating the sections for 10 min. The sections, upon cooling, received three rinses with phosphate-buffered saline, followed by a 1-hour incubation with 5% goat serum at ambient temperature. Afterward, two separate sets of lung tissue sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C: one set with MPO (1:200, ab90810, Abcam, UK) and CitH3 (1:2000, 97272, CST, USA) primary antibodies for co-localization analysis, and the other set with IL-17 (1:100, ab189377, Abcam, UK) and CitH3 (1:200, 97272, CST, USA) primary antibodies under the same conditions. After that, the sections underwent 1-hour incubation at 37 °C via ABflo® 488-conjugated Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) (1:200, ABclonal, green fluorescence, China) as well as ABflo® 594-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:200, ABclonal, red fluorescence, China). DAPI staining solution (C1005, Beyotime, China) was added for a 5-minute incubation in the dark at ambient temperature. Through a confocal laser scanning microscope (DM2500, Leica, Germany), images were captured, with excitation wavelengths of 488 nm for MPO and IL-17, 594 nm for CitH3, and 405 nm for DAPI. Multiple fields of view were captured to record colocalization signals. The confocal microscopy images were analyzed using ImageJ software. Total cell numbers were counted in the blue channel (DAPI), while the cell numbers formed by NETs were calculated in the red and green channels (MPO and CitH3 colocalization). For IL-17–enriched NETs, cells showing overlapping IL-17 and CitH3 signals were defined as IL-17–associated NETs, and the percentage of double-positive cells relative to total nuclei was calculated. Similarly, the fluorescence intensity of IL-17 was analyzed using the same method.

## Metagenomic sequencing of BALF
Using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (QIAGEN), collected BALF samples were adopted for bacterial DNA extraction. DNA purification was conducted by removing proteins and RNA, as instructed by the manufacturer. DNA concentration and purity were evaluated via the Qubit and NanoDrop, respectively. The target 16 S rRNA region (V3–V4) was amplified through the Nextera XT DNA Library Prep Kit. Following DNA fragmentation, end-repair, and adapter ligation, AMPure XP beads were adopted for purifying the PCR amplification products. The Illumina platform was employed for paired-end sequencing after thorough denaturation of the DNA. The original sequencing data were subjected to quality assessment with FastQC, and the low-quality sequences were removed and adaptors were trimmed using Trimmomatic. Sequence clustering was conducted with DADA2 to generate operational taxonomic units or amplicon sequence variants. The bacterial classification was annotated through comparison with the Greengenes database, accompanied by α and β diversity analyses.

## Data analysis
Original data were imported into Excel and subjected to normality tests using SPSS statistics. For normally distributed data, an independent samples t-test was adopted for analyzing differences between groups; differences among multiple groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance. The Kruskal-Wallis test was utilized for assessing differences among groups when data were not normally distributed, GraphPad Prism was employed for generating standard statistical graphs. P < 0.05 represented the statistical significance threshold.

## Results

## Rosavin reaches lung tissue after oral administration
To assess whether orally administered Rosavin is detectable in lung tissue, LC–MS/MS-based quantitative analysis was performed prior to mechanistic investigations. Rosavin was not detected in the Control and COPD groups, whereas measurable levels of Rosavin were detected in lung tissues from the COPD + R-H and COPD + R-L groups (Fig.  ). Moreover, the lung Rosavin level in the high-dose group was higher than that in the low-dose group, indicating a dose-dependent distribution trend.

 

## Rosavin suppresses NF-κB activation in the lung tissue of COPD rats
The NF-κB activation represents a fundamental mechanism in COPD development and progression. This study aimed to investigate whether Rosavin affects NF-κB activation in COPD rats. Rosavin was given to COPD rats via oral gavage for 4 consecutive weeks. The COPD group presented a notable reduction in IκBα protein levels as well as an obvious rise in p-IκBα protein levels in comparison with the Control group, as demonstrated by Western blot analysis (Fig.  A–B). Additionally, in the lung tissues of COPD rats, the nuclear NF-κB p65 protein level was evidently increased (Fig.  C–D). As opposed to the COPD group, Rosavin treatment significantly reduced nuclear NF-κB p65 levels and decreased p-IκBα expression in COPD rats compared with the untreated COPD group. However, no significant differences were observed between the low-dose (COPD + R-L) and high-dose (COPD + R-H) groups, suggesting that the suppressive effect of Rosavin on NF-κB activation was not dose-dependent under the current experimental conditions.

 

## Rosavin and BAY 11-7082 suppress NF-κB signaling in lung tissues of COPD rats
To further validate the inhibitory effect of Rosavin and BAY 11-7082 on the NF-κB signaling pathway at the tissue level, immunohistochemistry was performed to examine the expression of IκBα, p-IκBα and NF-κB p65 in lung sections. Compared with the Control group, the COPD group exhibited a marked reduction in IκBα expression, accompanied by a pronounced increase in p-IκBα staining and enhanced nuclear accumulation of NF-κB p65, indicating robust activation of the NF-κB pathway. In contrast, both Rosavin (high and low dose) and BAY 11-7082 treatment substantially restored IκBα expression, reduced p-IκBα expression, and attenuated the nuclear localization of NF-κB p65. Quantitative analysis of IOD values further confirmed the significant suppression of NF-κB pathway activation by Rosavin and BAY 11-7082 (Fig.  A–D).

 

## Rosavin suppresses NF-κB activation and inflammatory cytokine release in CSE-stimulated BEAS-2B cells
To further validate the inhibitory effect of Rosavin on the NF-κB signaling pathway at the cellular level, an in vitro model was established using cigarette smoke extract (CSE) to stimulate BEAS-2B cells. Western blot results showed that CSE markedly decreased IκBα and increased p-IκBα levels, whereas Rosavin treatment effectively reversed these changes (Fig.  A). Consistent with these findings, ELISA assays demonstrated that Rosavin significantly reduced CSE-induced secretion of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β (Fig.  B). Moreover, CSE stimulation markedly increased the nuclear accumulation of NF-κB p65, while pretreatment with Rosavin or BAY 11-7082 significantly reduced p65 nuclear translocation, as shown by nuclear protein Western blot analysis (Fig.  C).These findings indicate that Rosavin directly inhibits CSE-induced NF-κB activation and downstream cytokine production in BEAS-2B epithelial cells, thereby reinforcing the in vivo observations obtained from COPD rats.

 

## Rosavin enhances lung function in COPD rats by modulating the NF-κB signaling pathway
Rosavin’s ability to suppress NF-κB nuclear translocation has been demonstrated. Further investigations were conducted to determine whether Rosavin could treat COPD rats by suppressing NF-κB activity. As illustrated in the pulmonary function test results (Fig.  A–D), the COPD group exhibited a remarkable drop in TV, FEV1/FVC, and PEF in the fourth week as opposed to the Control group, with these levels remaining lower throughout the experimental period. Additionally, airway resistance in COPD rats presented an upward trend after the fourth week and remained at a high level in the subsequent experimental period. Further analysis revealed that the FEV1/FVC, PEF, and TV were elevated in the COPD + R-H, and COPD + R-L groups starting relative to the COPD group from the eighth week, while the Raw was decreased. Furthermore, BAY 11-7082 treatment exhibited similar effects to those observed with Rosavin. The above-mentioned findings suggested that in COPD rats, lung function was improved by Rosavin via modulating the NF-κB signaling pathway.

 

## Rosavin alleviates pulmonary pathological damage in COPD rats
Further, Rosavin’s effects on pulmonary pathological damage in COPD rats were evaluated using hematoxylin and eosin staining. The Control group demonstrated basically intact lung tissue structure and alveolar walls, with normal alveolar morphology and no inflammatory infiltration. Conversely, disorganized and damaged alveolar structure was found in the COPD group, with bullae formation as well as alveolar rupture and fusion. Additionally, some bronchial shapes were altered, accompanied by increased BWT, enlarged alveolar diameters, and substantial inflammatory cell infiltration. However, the COPD + R-H and COPD + R-L groups presented varying degrees of improvement in pulmonary damage, including alleviated inflammatory cell infiltration. Among them, the COPD + R-H group exhibited the most significant improvement in pulmonary damage (Fig.  A). Further analysis revealed that the COPD group showed significantly elevated MLI, BWT, histological scores, and inflammation scores relative to the Control group. However, compared to the COPD group, the MLI, BWT, histological scores, and inflammation scores were considerably reduced in the COPD + R-L and COPD + R-H groups (Fig.  B–E). Furthermore, according to the PAS staining results, the COPD group presented an elevated number of goblet cells relative to the Control group, along with an obvious elevation in PAS staining score. The COPD + R-H, COPD + R-L, and COPD + BAY groups displayed a decreased goblet cell number as well as markedly lower PAS scores as opposed to the COPD group (Fig.  F–G). These outcomes suggested that Rosavin alleviated pulmonary pathological damage in COPD rats.

 

## Rosavin inhibits oxidative stress in the lung tissues of COPD rats
COPD development is strongly influenced by oxidative stress [ ]. Measurements were performed on the MDA levels, as well as the MPO and SOD activities, in the lung tissues across all groups to explore Rosavin’s effect on oxidative stress levels in COPD rats. The analysis revealed that compared to the Control group, the COPD group presented a significant rise in MDA levels and MPO activity in the lung tissues, alongside a remarkable decline in SOD activity. Importantly, as opposed to the COPD group, the COPD + R-H, COPD + R-L, and COPD + BAY groups displayed a considerable drop in the MDA levels and the MPO activity, along with a notable elevation in the SOD activity (Fig. A–C). The aforementioned findings indicated that in the lung tissues of COPD rats, oxidative stress was suppressed by Rosavin via modulating the NF-κB signaling pathway.

 

## Rosavin improves the number of inflammatory cells and the levels of chemokines and inflammatory cytokines in the BALF of COPD rats
Excessive inflammatory responses are a key factor contributing to COPD progression [ ]. To investigate Rosavin’s effects on inflammation in the lung tissues of COPD rats, the cell number in BALF was measured. In contrast to the Control group, the total cells were evidently increased in the BALF of the COPD group. However, the total cells in treated rats were notably declined in the treatment groups than those of the COPD group (Fig. A). The COPD group presented a markedly higher number of macrophages and neutrophils in BALF compared with the Control group, according to inflammatory cell statistics. Conversely, relative to the COPD group, these numbers were remarkably dropped in the treatment groups (Fig. B–C). Subsequently, ELISA kits were adopted for assessing chemokine and inflammatory cytokine levels in BALF. The findings demonstrated a marked elevation in the TNF-α, IL-6, monocyte chemotactic protein 1, IL-17, IL-1β, and CXCL5 levels in the BALF of the COPD group relative to the Control group. Conversely, in the treatment groups, these levels exhibited a remarkable downward trend compared with the COPD group (Fig. D–I). The above-mentioned outcomes indicated that Rosavin improved the levels of chemokines and inflammatory cytokines and inflammatory cell number in the BALF of COPD rats.

 

## Rosavin inhibits NET formation in the lung tissues of COPD rats
As web-like structures capable of eliminating foreign pathogens, NETs resulted in tissue damage and immune-mediated thrombosis during excessive inflammation, thereby exacerbating lung diseases. This study demonstrated that Rosavin could notably lower the elevated MPO-DNA levels caused by COPD (Fig.  A). According to the Western blot analysis, the COPD group presented an evident elevation in the CitH3, MPO, and NE protein levels in the lung tissues in comparison with the Control group. However, relative to the COPD group, these levels were obviously dropped in the treatment groups (Fig.  B–C). Furthermore, immunofluorescence was employed to observe the colocalization of CitH3 and MPO. The results presented that the COPD group revealed evidently increased MPO and CitH3 colocalization in the lung tissues, along with a marked elevation in the rate of NET formation, as opposed to the Control group. However, an evident reduction was observed in the rate of NET formation and the CitH3 and MPO colocalization in the lung tissues of COPD rats after treatment (Fig.  D–E). These outcomes demonstrated that in the lung tissues of COPD rats, NET formation could be inhibited by Rosavin.

 

## Rosavin inhibits the formation of IL-17-enriched NETs in the lung tissues of COPD rats
The IL-17 family is involved in the body’s anti-infection immunity and pathological inflammation associated with autoimmune diseases. IL-17-positive cell numbers are significantly elevated in COPD patients. Therefore, this study investigated whether IL-17 is present within NETs. According to the immunofluorescence analysis, the COPD group presented a remarkable elevation in the CitH3 and IL-17 colocalization, as well as the IL-17 fluorescence intensity, in the lung tissues compared with the Control group. The treatment groups displayed significantly lower IL-17 and CitH3 colocalization relative to the COPD group, alongside a notable decline in the IL-17 fluorescence intensity (Fig.  A–B). Colocalization was quantified as the percentage of IL-17–CitH3 double-positive cells, which revealed a marked reduction in IL-17–associated NETs in Rosavin-treated rats (Fig.  B). Furthermore, as opposed to the Control group, the IL-17 protein expression in the lung tissues was markedly higher in the COPD group, as evidenced by Western blot. However, the IL-17 protein expression was considerably lower in the treatment groups than that of the COPD group (Fig.  C–D). These findings suggested that IL-17 is enriched within NETs in COPD lungs and that Rosavin reduces IL-17-enriched NET formation. To further confirm the causal role of IL-17 in NET formation, we next examined the effect of IL-17A knockdown on NET markers in vitro.

 

## IL-17A knockdown attenuates NET formation in vitro
To verify the efficiency of IL-17A silencing, differentiated HL-60 cells were transfected with IL-17A-specific siRNA. RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that si-IL-17A significantly reduced IL-17A mRNA expression under both basal and LPS-stimulated conditions compared with siNC-transfected cells (Figure A). Consistently, ELISA analysis showed a marked decrease in IL-17A protein levels in the culture supernatants following IL-17A knockdown (Figure B), confirming the effective silencing of IL-17A in differentiated HL-60 cells.
Functionally, in LPS-stimulated HL-60 cells, IL-17A silencing markedly reduced MPO–DNA complex levels compared with the siNC + LPS group, whereas recombinant IL-17A partially restored these changes (Fig.  A). Consistently, Western blot analysis showed that IL-17A knockdown decreased the expression of NET-related proteins, including CitH3, MPO, and NE, while IL-17A add-back reversed this effect (Fig.  B). These findings demonstrate that IL-17A is a critical mediator of NET formation in vitro.

 

## Effects of rosavin on microbiota in the lung tissues of COPD rats
Further taxonomic analysis at different levels revealed more specific microbial alterations. At the phylum level, 12 taxa were identified. COPD rats showed a reduction in Bacteroidetes and an increase in Fusobacteria compared to controls, although the increase in Fusobacteria was primarily driven by a single sample (COPD 2). In contrast, Rosavin treatment was associated with an increase in Bacteroidetes and a decrease in Proteobacteria abundance (Fig.  A). At the family level, Streptococcaceae and Veillonellaceae were elevated in COPD rats, while Bacteroidaceae was reduced. Rosavin treatment increased Bacteroidaceae and Lactobacillaceae levels, but considerable variability was observed across individual samples (Fig.  B). At the genus level, Veillonella and Lachnoclostridium showed higher abundance in the COPD group, whereas Bacteroides was reduced. In the Rosavin-treated group, Lactobacillus and Bacteroides exhibited increased abundance, though these changes lacked uniformity across all samples (Fig.  C). At the species level, Roseburia inulinivorans and Ruminococcus gnavus were detected in only a single COPD sample (COPD 1), highlighting the intra-group heterogeneity. Rosavin treatment increased the abundance of Lactobacillus gasseri, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, and Lactobacillus fermentum, while reducing Escherichia coli levels (Fig.  D). Notably, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron was not present in all samples of the COPD + R-H group, suggesting variability in microbial response to treatment. Collectively, these findings indicate that Rosavin may influence the lung microbiota composition by promoting beneficial taxa and reducing potential pathogens.
To determine whether Rosavin alleviates COPD symptoms by modulating lung microbiota, BALF samples from each group were collected for 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. First, α- and β-diversity analyses were conducted to evaluate the overall microbial community characteristics. Compared to the Control group, the COPD group showed a clear reduction in microbial richness and evenness, as indicated by decreased Observed, Shannon, Simpson, Chao1, and Pielou indices (Fig.  A–E). Although Rosavin treatment appeared to increase certain diversity indices, such as Observed and Simpson, the differences were not statistically significant, particularly for Chao1 and Shannon indices. These findings suggest that Rosavin had only a modest effect on restoring alpha diversity under the current experimental conditions. The Goods Coverage index indicated sufficient sequencing depth for community profiling (Fig.  F). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on unweighted UniFrac distances revealed group-level separation in microbial communities among COPD, COPD + R-H, COPD + BAY, and Control groups, although considerable intra-group variation was observed (Fig.  G).

 

 

## Differential analysis of lung microbiota species
To accurately and clearly observe the differences in microbiota among different groups of rats, differential statistics were carried out at the species, genus, phylum, and family levels. At the phylum level, compared to the Control group, the COPD group exhibited increased relative abundances of Fusobacteria and Actinobacteria, along with a reduction in Bacteroidetes and Verrucomicrobia. In contrast, no significant difference in Firmicutes abundance was observed among the groups. In contrast to the COPD group, Rosavin treatment could notably decrease Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria abundances in the BALF of rats, coupled with a considerable elevation increase in Bacteroidetes abundance (Fig.  A). At the family level, the Veillonellaceae, Streptococcaceae, and Fusobacteriaceae abundances were evidently higher in the COPD group in contrast to the Control group, along with remarkably lower Ruminococcaceae and Bacteroidaceae abundances. However, as opposed to the COPD group, Rosavin treatment caused a notable rise in Bacteroidaceae and Lactobacillaceae abundances and an obvious drop in Enterobacteriaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Veillonellaceae, Streptococcaceae, and Fusobacteriaceae abundances (Fig.  B). At the genus level, the COPD group displayed a significantly increased abundance of Klebsiella, Streptococcus, Veillonella, and Fusobacterium relative to the Control group, accompanied by a considerably reduced abundance of Bacteroides and Subdoligranulum. Conversely, Rosavin treatment significantly elevated the Bacteroides and Lactobacillus abundances relative to the COPD group, while remarkably reducing Escherichia-Shigella, Klebsiella, Streptococcus, Veillonella, and Fusobacterium abundances (Fig.  C). At the species level, in the BALF of the COPD group, the Fusobacterium nucleatum abundance was markedly elevated compared with the Control group, alongside a notable reduction in Phascolarctobacterium faecium and Acinetobacter johnsonii. Compared to the COPD group, Rosavin treatment caused an evident elevation in the Lactobacillus gasseri, Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, Lactobacillus fermentum, Bacteroides fragilis, and Lactobacillus salivarius abundances (Fig.  D). The above-mentioned outcomes suggested that the beneficial bacteria abundance could be enhanced by Rosavin in the lung tissues of COPD rats.

 

## Discussion
COPD remains a global health burden characterized by chronic airway inflammation, oxidative stress, and progressive lung damage. In this study, we demonstrated that Rosavin effectively ameliorated COPD-associated pathological changes in a rat model, acting through multiple mechanisms, including suppression of NF-κB signaling, inhibition of IL-17-enriched NET formation, and modulation of lung microbiota composition. These findings provide novel insights into the therapeutic potential of Rosavin for COPD.
We established a COPD model using cigarette smoke exposure combined with LPS instillation, which exhibited typical features such as airflow limitation, inflammatory cell infiltration, and alveolar structural damage. These characteristics are consistent with modeling approaches reported by Wang et al. [ ]. and Song et al. [ ]. Treatment with Rosavin significantly improved lung function (FEV1/FVC and PEF) and reduced airway resistance. Notably, the therapeutic effects were similar between the low- and high-dose Rosavin groups, suggesting that the lower dose may have reached a therapeutic threshold. This observation is consistent with the dose-independent effects reported by Liu et al. [ ] in an LPS-induced lung injury model.
The NF-κB signaling pathway is a key regulator of chronic airway inflammation in COPD [ , ]. In line with previous studies showing that NF-κB inhibition can alleviate airway inflammation and tissue damage [ ], our study found that Rosavin significantly downregulated phosphorylated IκBα and nuclear NF-κB p65 expression, along with reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. These anti-inflammatory effects are consistent with the immunomodulatory properties of Rhodiola-derived compounds reported by Wang et al. [ ] and Xin et al. [ ].
Oxidative stress is another critical factor in COPD pathogenesis. In our model, we observed elevated levels of MDA and reduced activity of the antioxidant enzyme SOD, consistent with the findings of Wiegman et al. [ ]. Rosavin treatment significantly reduced oxidative stress markers, potentially disrupting the positive feedback loop between reactive oxygen species (ROS) and NF-κB activation [ ]. This dual action of anti-inflammation and antioxidation enhances Rosavin’s potential as a multi-target therapeutic agent, offering advantages over traditional single-target treatments.
More importantly, our study provides mechanistic evidence that Rosavin inhibits NET formation in the lung tissue of COPD rats. NETs—composed of MPO, neutrophil elastase (NE), and citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3)—contribute to lung tissue damage and immune dysfunction when excessively formed [ , ]. Although NETs are recognized as contributors to COPD progression, few natural compounds have been shown to directly regulate their formation. In our study, Rosavin significantly reduced NET biomarkers and MPO-DNA complex levels. Notably, we observed for the first time that Rosavin inhibited IL-17-enriched NET formation—a mechanism increasingly implicated in chronic inflammation [ ]. These findings indicate that Rosavin regulates IL-17–associated NET formation predominantly through NF-κB inhibition, consistent with prior evidence that Rosavin suppresses NET-related pathways in inflammatory lung injury [ ].
From a translational perspective, IL-17–associated NET formation and NF-κB activation in human COPD could be evaluated using established clinical sample–based approaches. In lung tissue obtained from COPD patients, NETs may be assessed by immunohistochemistry or immunofluorescence for CitH3, MPO, and NE in combination with IL-17 staining, allowing quantitative colocalization analysis. In sputum samples, NET formation can be quantified by measuring MPO–DNA complexes using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, together with immunocytochemical detection of extracellular CitH3-positive DNA structures. NF-κB pathway activation in human samples could be examined by assessing p-IκBα expression and NF-κB p65 nuclear localization using immunohistochemistry or immunofluorescence, or by Western blot analysis of nuclear extracts from sputum-derived cells.
Lung microbiota dysbiosis is also an important factor in the pathophysiology of COPD, although it remains relatively underexplored. In our study, COPD rats showed decreased microbial diversity and enrichment of the pathogenic bacterium Fusobacterium nucleatum, consistent with previous findings linking dysbiosis to disease exacerbation [ , ]. Rosavin was able to regulate microbiota composition by increasing beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Bacteroides, aligning with recent studies using probiotics or herbal medicines to restore pulmonary microbiota balance [ ]. Although the mechanistic linkage between microbiota restoration and downstream NET or NF-κB regulation remains uncertain, the concurrent improvement in microbial structure and inflammatory indices suggests a potential interplay worthy of future investigation.
Despite these promising findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted in a rat model, and the translation of these results to the complex human pulmonary environment remains uncertain. Second, while Rosavin improved COPD pathology, the lack of clear dose-dependency in some outcomes suggests the need for further dose-optimization studies. Third, the analysis of lung microbiota was based on a limited number of samples, which may not fully capture the microbial diversity and variability within groups. Finally, although the present study supports an indirect NF-κB–dependent mechanism, future ex vivo studies using primary cells isolated from COPD patients, such as sputum-derived neutrophils or bronchial epithelial cells, will be required to determine whether Rosavin exerts comparable modulatory effects on NF-κB activation, IL-17 expression, and NET formation in a human context.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, this study demonstrates that Rosavin ameliorates COPD-related pathology through a multifaceted mechanism. Specifically, Rosavin suppresses inflammation and oxidative stress, inhibits NF-κB signaling, and disrupts IL-17-enriched NET formation, while simultaneously modulating the lung microbiota composition. These combined effects lead to improved pulmonary function and reduced tissue injury. Collectively, the findings highlight Rosavin as a promising multi-target therapeutic candidate for COPD, warranting further investigation in clinical settings.
