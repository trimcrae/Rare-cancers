## Background
Many studies have reported the important role of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) in regulating glucose homeostasis. However, in addition to the pancreas, GLP-1R is distributed in organs such as the lungs. A few researches have reported the mechanism of action of GLP-1R in acute and chronic lung diseases. Nevertheless, its effect on lung development remains unclear. In this research, we aimed to explore the role of GLP-1R in regulating lung development and its potential mechanisms in in vivo and in vitro bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) models.

## Methods
Neonatal Sprague-Dawley rats were divided into hyperoxia (FIO2 = 0.85) and control (FIO2 = 0.21) groups. Lung tissues were extracted at 3, 7, 10, and 14 postnatal days and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin staining for histopathological and morphological observation. Single-cell RNA sequencing was performed to explore the role of GLP-1R in lung development. Western blotting was conducted to assess the expression of GLP-1R, dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1), and glycolysis-associated enzymes, including phosphofructokinase (PFKM), hexokinase 2 (HK2), and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), in the lung tissues, primary alveolar type II (ATII) cells, and RLE-6TN cells. Double immunofluorescence staining was performed to confirm the co-localization of GLP-1R, DRP1, and ATII cells. A Seahorse XF96 metabolic extracellular flux analyzer was used to perform real-time analyses of extracellular acidification rate and oxygen consumption rate in ATII cells isolated from lung tissues and RLE-6TN cells. The adenosine triphosphate (ATP) concentrations in ATII and RLE-6TN cells were measured using an ATP kit. Mitochondria were stained with MitoTracker and observed using HiS-SIM. GLP-1R gene levels in lung tissues, primary ATII cells, and RLE-6TN cells were tested using RT-qPCR. We used MeRIP-qPCR to determine the m6A modification level of GLP-1R mRNA in RLE-6TN cells. A reporter gene was used to verify the modification site and key methyltransferases.

## Results
We observed that GLP-1R signaling regulates lung development and plays a key role in ATII cells, particularly after birth. Hyperoxia inhibits GLP-1R protein and gene expression in ATII cells and accelerates BPD development. ATP production decreased and glycolysis levels increased in ATII cells under hyperoxia. Activation of GLP-1R signaling promotes ATP production and downregulates glycolysis by regulating DRP1 induced mitochondria fission. In RLE-6TN cells, we verified that the m6A modification level of GLP-1R mRNA decreased; the modification site was tested by MeRIP-qPCR and was primarily induced by the methyltransferase-like 14 (METTL14).

## Conclusion
GLP-1R is primarily expressed in ATII cells of neonatal rats and can promote lung development during the early postnatal period. The GLP-1R signaling pathway modulates mitochondrial fission and glucose metabolism in ATII cells under hyperoxia. Hyperoxia can inhibit the activation of GLP-1R by inhibiting m6A methylation during BPD pathogenesis.

## Introduction
The glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R) belongs to the G-protein-coupled membrane receptor, and is best known for binding with its ligand GLP-1 to promote pancreatic β cell secreting insulin to regulate blood glucose [ ]. Furthermore, GLP-1R takes part in the regulation of lipid metabolism, T cell-induced inflammation, and cell apoptosis [ , ]. In addition to the pancreas, GLP-1R is distributed in many other tissues, including the central nervous system, heart, liver, and blood vessels, and is highly expressed in the lungs [ ]. Studies have also reported its role in acute and chronic lung diseases, such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Xu et al. found that activated GLP-1R maintains endothelial barrier function and relieves lipopolysaccharide-induced acute lung injury in mice [ ]. Wu et al. reviewed the anti-inflammatory effects of GLP-1R signaling in asthma through the modulation of airway inflammation [ ]. In vitro studies show that allergen-induced type 2 inflammation could be inhibited after the administration of exogenous GLP-1R agonists (GLP-1RAs) [ , ]. Clinical studies have also shown that patients with diabetes combined with asthma have fewer asthma exacerbations after treatment with GLP-1RAs [ ]. In patients with COPD, GLP-1R is significantly decreased in peripheral blood mononuclear cells and the airway smooth muscle [ ]. Furthermore, GLP-1R activation by GLP-1RAs can improve surfactant production [ , ]. In an experimental model of obstructive lung disease, Viby et al. showed that although GLP-1RAs have potential therapeutic effects in chronic obstructive pulmonary diseases, the mechanism of action does not seem to involve the modulation of inflammation and surfactants [ ]. Therefore, there must be many other regulatory roles for GLP-1R signaling in chronic lung disease.
GLP-1R expression is significantly upregulated after birth in neonatal rats. In addition, the administration of GLP-1RAs to the mother from 14 days after gestation until birth increases the expression level of GLP-1R and improves nitrofen-induced lung hypoplasia, demonstrating the vital role of GLP-1R signaling in lung development [ ]. Lung development comprises five stages: embryonic, pseudoglandular, canalicular, saccular, and alveolar. Restriction in the canalicular and saccular stages causes bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), one of the most common respiratory complications among premature infants, resulting in simplified alveolar structure and pulmonary vessel hypoplasia [ ]. Infants with BPD have high mortality, long hospital stays, risen economic and social burdens, and long-term adverse outcomes in many systems, including the respiratory and nervous systems [ , ]. To lower the BPD incidence of premature births and improve the dismal prognosis, it is crucial to identify efficient preventive, therapeutic, and predictive markers for BPD. The pathogenesis of BPD is not fully understood, oxidative stress, mechanical ventilation, inflammation, vascular malformation, malnourishment, and metabolic dysregulation are all part of the complicated pathophysiology of BPD [ , ]. The pathological basis of BPD is the injury of alveolar type II (ATII) cells, that have a significant function in the secretion, regeneration, and differentiation of alveoli and in maintaining lung homeostasis [ , ]. Our previous transcriptomic results showed that the expression level of GLP-1R gene was markedly decreased in the lung tissues of animal models of BPD, although the role of GLP-1R signaling has not been reported in the pathogenesis of BPD. Therefore, in this research, we aimed to explore how GLP-1R affects lung development, its role in BPD pathogenesis, and its upstream regulatory mechanisms.

## Materials and methods
2.1

## Antibodies and reagents
The antibodies that we utilized in this research contained anti-GLP-1R mouse antibody (D-6) (sc-390774, Santa Cruz), anti-DRP1(D6C7) rabbit monoclonal antibody (8570, Cell Signalling Technology), anti-PFKM polyclonal antibody (55028-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-Hexokinase 2 polyclonal antibody (22029-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-Beta Actin Monoclonal antibody (66009-1-lg, Proteintech), anti-LDHA-Specific polyclonal antibody (19987-1-AP, Proteintech) and anti-SFTPC polyclonal antibody (10774-1-AP, Proteintech). Transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1), vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGF-A), and bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4) ELISA kits were purchased from RUIXIN BIOTECH.

2.2

## Hyperoxia exposure animal model
According to our previously reported methods, in the hyperoxia group (FiO2 = 0.85), newborn Sprague-Dawley rats were exposed to a hyperoxic environment no more than 12 h after birth, whereas the control group (FiO2 = 0.21) were exposed to a normoxic environment. Soda lime was used to maintain a CO2 content of <0.5 %, and the oxygen tank's water vapor was eliminated using silica gel. To minimize the influence of variations in nursing ability, newborn rats were fed by their mothers, who were shifted between cages every 24 h. Clean drinking water and food were supplied, and the cage was opened for 30 min each day. A 12 h day/night cycle was in place in the room. They have unlimited access to food and drink.
At 3, 7, 10, and 14 days after exposure to hyperoxia or normoxia, the neonatal rats were anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation and euthanized. Whole lungs were aseptically collected by opening the chest. The right lower lungs were preserved in paraformaldehyde for subsequent hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining, whereas the other lung lobes were used for ATII cell isolation. Mature Sprague-Dawley rats weighing 220–250 g were bought from BEIJING HFK BIOSCIENCE CO., Ltd. A sperm-positive vaginal smear verified mating and was regarded as gestational day one after proestrous females were kept with males for 24 h. Fetal lungs were collected at different developmental stages (E15, E17, E19, and E21) under normoxic conditions. The Ethics Committee of Animals at China Medical University approved and supervised all animal experiments (2023PS932K) [ ].

2.3

## Lung histology
Right lower lung tissues were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde last for at least 24 h, dehydrated with an ethanol gradient, vitrified with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were cut into 4 μm, and then stained with HE. The morphological alterations were observed under an optical microscope. Three fields were randomly chosen for analysis in each section. Using the ImageJ software, the alveolar wall thickness and radial alveolar count (RAC) were calculated to assess alveolar development.

2.4

## ATII cells isolation and purification
ATII cells were isolated from neonatal rats at 3, 7, 10, and 14 days postnatally. Neonatal rat lung tissues were perfused with normal saline, sectioned with scissors, and subjected to digestion with 0.25 % trypsin-EDTA (cat. no. 25200056; Gibco) at 37 °C. Digestion was stopped after 25 min, and samples were filtered via 200-μm filters. Then the cell pellet was resuspended in 0.1 % collagenase I (C8140, Solarbio). The cells were harvested after centrifugating at 800 rpm for 5 min, resuspended in DMEM/F12 containing penicillin and streptomycin (KGM12500-500, KeyGEN BioTECH) and 10 % fetal bovine serum (FSP500, Excell), and incubated. In order to dislodge fibroblasts and unattached cells, the culture plate was totally changed four times every 45–60 min. Lastly, an IgG-coated plate for differential attachment was used to exclude foreign cells such macrophages and fibroblasts. The cells were harvested after 24 h for subsequent experiments [ ].

2.5

## Hyperoxia exposure cell model
Normoxia group RLE-6TN rat type II lung epithelial cells (BNCC337708, BeNa Culture Collection) were incubated in RPMI 1640 medium with penicillin and streptomycin (KGM31800-500, KeyGEN BioTECH), supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (164210–50, Procell) at 37 °C with 21 % O2 and 5 % CO2 for 24, 48, and 72 h. They were subjected to hyperoxia (85 % O2 and 5 % CO2) for 24, 48, and 72 h as the hyperoxia group once the normoxic cell density reached 70–80 % [ ].

2.6

## Cell viability analysis
We evaluated the viability of cells using the cell counting kit-8 (HY–K0301, MCE). ATII and RLE-6TN cells were seeded in two 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 μL enhanced CCK-8 reagent was added, which was diluted in 100 μL of serum-free medium. Finally, the 96-well plate was cultured for 1–2 h at 37 °C. The optical density (OD) was ascertained at 450 nm with a microplate reader. The formula for cell viability (%) was as follows: (treatment cell OD-blank OD)/(control cell OD-blank OD) × 100.

2.7

## Detection of apoptosis
ATII (14 days) or RLE-6TN cells were seeded at 100,000 cells/well in six-well plates. Apoptosis level was detected by an Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection reagent (C1062 M, Beyotime). After trypsin digestion, cells were resuspended in PBS, and the supernatant was discarded after centrifuging. Subsequently, 195 μL Annexin V-FITC binding solution, 10 μL propidium iodide staining solution, and 5 μL Annexin V-FITC were gently mixed. The mixture was added and the cells were incubated at 26 °C in the dark for approximately 20 min. Then, a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX, BECKMAN COULTER) was performed to analyze.

2.8

## Western blot analysis
We extracted the total protein from ATII and RLE-6TN cells with a mixture of RIPA (P0013B, Beyotime), PMSF (ST507-10 ml, Beyotime), Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (K1007, Apebio), and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (K1015, Apebio). Following sodium dodecyl sulphate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, isolated proteins were put onto a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. After blocking with 5 % skim milk, different antibodies were added for 18 h at 4 °C: Anti-GLP-1R mouse antibody (D-6) (1:500); Anti-DRP1(D6C7) rabbit monoclonal antibody (1:1000); anti-PFKM polyclonal antibody (1:1000); anti-Hexokinase 2 polyclonal antibody (1:3000); anti-LDHA-Specific polyclonal antibody (1:3000); and anti-Beta Actin Monoclonal antibody (1:40000). The membrane was subsequently incubated with a secondary antibody for 2 h (SA00001-1, SA00001-2, Proteintech). Ultimately, protein bands were identified utilizing enhanced chemiluminescence (180–501, Tanon). The band intensities were normalized to the Beta Actin signal utilizing Image J software.

2.9

## Quantitative real-time PCR
To quantify gene expression, we used TRIzol reagent (T9108, Takara) to extract total RNA from tissues and cells. We used AG kits (AG11702, AG11728, Accurate Biology) to reverse transcribe total RNA to cDNA and for quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analyses. The protocol for two-step qPCR was 95 °C for 30 s, succeeded by 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s of 40 cycles. Relative mRNA levels were computed using the 2−ΔΔCT method based on the acquired Ct values. β-actin gene expression was assessed for normalization purposes. The primers were synthesized by Shenggong (Shanghai, China). The primer sequences utilized in this study are presented in . 

2.10

## Metabolic flux analysis
We tested the metabolic flux and oxidative phosphorylation levels by utillizing Seahorse XF96 analyzer (Agilent Technologies) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The cell culture plate was opened on a super-clean table, 80 μL of cells were added to each well (for ATII cells: inoculation density of 10,000 cells/well; for RLE-6TN cells, inoculation density of 5000 cells/well), and the medium was added to the background hole, which was placed on a super-clean workbench for 1 h and then cultured in a 5 % CO2 cell incubator at 37 °C overnight to make the cells adhere to the wall. The cell density in each well was approximately 80 % of the density before the machine was installed. Simultaneously, the probe plates were hydrated and placed in a CO2-free cell incubator for 20 h at 37 °C. A Seahorse XF96 analyzer was turned on for at least 5 h in advance.
The next day, the fluid of the cell plate was changed before testing, the culture medium was replaced with the detection liquid, and the final volume of each hole was 180 μL. This was placed into the CO2-free cell incubator at 37 °C for warming. Next, the probe plate was removed, the drug was added to the dosing hole, placed into the Seahorse XF96 analyzer for calibration, and the cell plate was used for detection after calibration.
With regard to the glycolysis stress test, the detection liquid was mixed with Seahorse XF DEME medium and glutamine. Drugs of 10 mM glucose, 1 μM oligomycin, and 50 mM 2-DG in the XF Glycolysis Stress Test Kit were used following the manufacturers' guidelines. For the mitochondrial stress test, the detection liquid was mixed with Seahorse XF DEME medium, glutamine, glucose, and pyruvate. Drugs of 1.5 μM oligomycin, 0.5 μM FCCP, and 0.5 μM Rot/AA in the XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit were used following manufacturer's instructions.

2.11

## Mitochondrial measurement
The density of 50,000 cells/well were seeded in six-well plates. MitoTracker Red CMXRos (M9940, Solarbio) was used to label mitochondria following the instructions. MitoTracker was diluted by DMEM/F12 medium with serum to 200 nM and added to the ATII cells, incubated for 30 min at 37 °C, and then observed by HiS-SIM (LSM880; Zeiss AG).

2.12

## Double immunofluorescence staining
For tissue immunofluorescence, after dehydration, microwave repair was conducted using citric acid for 7 min, followed by cooling to 26 °C. The lung tissues were washed thrice again with PBS for 5 min. Then, 5 % BSA (ST025-20g, Beyotime) was used to block the membrane at 26 °C for 1 h. After drying, the membrane was incubated with anti-GLP-1R mouse antibody (D-6) (1:100, sc-390774, Santa Cruz) and anti-SFTPC polyclonal antibody (1:100, 10774-1-AP, Proteintech) at 4 °C for 18 h followed by incubation with Cy3 Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Antibody (1:100, K1209, ApeBio) and Alexa 488 Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Antibody (1:100, K1204, ApeBio), incubated for 2 h at 26 °C. Finally, DAPI (AR1176, Boster) was utilized to stain the nuclei, and fluorescence pictures were acquired with a confocal microscope (LSM880; Zeiss AG).
For cellular immunofluorescence, ATII cells at 7, 10, and 14 days were seeded at 500,000 cells/mL in six-well plates for detection. The cells were then washed with PBS and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 30 min. The cells were permeabilized with 0.5 % Triton (9002-93-1, Solarbio) for 10 min and blocked with serum at 26 °C for 1 h. The cells were then incubated with anti-GLP-1R antibody and anti-SFTPC polyclonal antibody at 4 °C for 16 h, followed with fluorescent secondary antibody, and incubated for 2 h at 26 °C. DAPI was used to stain the nuclei. The fluorescent images were captured by a confocal microscope.

2.13

## ATP test kit
ATII (14 days) or RLE-6TN cells were seeded at 100,000 cells/well in six-well plates. An ATP test kit (S0027, Beyotime) was used to measure ATP production. The cells were lysed with 200 μL of ATP detected lysate in each well of the six-well plate, centrifuged at 4 °C at 12,000 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was removed for subsequent determination. The ATP standard solution was diluted with the ATP detected lysate to the appropriate concentration gradients: 0.01, 0.03, 0.1, 0.3, 1, 3, and 10 μM. Configuration of the ATP test liquid involved dilution of the ATP test reagent with the diluent at a ratio of 1:4. The test was conducted in a 96-well plate, with 100 μL ATP detection working liquid added to each hole, placed at 26 °C for 3–5 min, and then added 20 μL of samples or standard products to each hole, and finally tested with a multimode reader (Cytation 5, Biotek).

2.14

## Single-cell RNA sequencing
After harvesting, tissues were washed in ice-cold RPMI1640 and dissociated with Miltenyi Biotec's Multi-tissue dissociation kit 2 following the manufacturer's instructions. DNase treatment was not necessary, depending on the homogenate's viscosity. Following the removal of erythrocytes (Miltenyi 130-094-183), the fluorescent Cell Analyzer (Countstar® Rigel S2) with AO/PI reagent was added to estimate the cell count and viability. Debris and dead cells were removed to determine whether to proceed (Miltenyi 130-109-398/130-090-101). A SeekOne Digital Droplet Single Cell 3 Library Preparation Kit (SeekGene Catalog No.) was used to create single-cell RNA-Seq libraries. In short, the sample well in SeekOne® DD Chip S3 was filled with the appropriate number of cells combined with reverse transcription reagent. Then, in chip S3, the partitioning oil and barcoded hydrogel beads were individually poured into appropriate wells. Following the creation of emulsion droplets, reverse transcription was executed for 90 min at 42 °C and inactivated for 15 min at 80 °C. The fragmented droplets were then used to extract cDNA, which was then amplified using PCR. The amplified cDNA product was obtained after cleaning, fragmentation, end repair, A-tailing, and ligation to a sequencing adaptor. Cell Barcode and Unique Molecular Index were also included in the DNA that represented the 3′ polyA portion of the expressing genes, which was amplified using indexed PCR. Following the cleaning of the indexed sequencing libraries with SPRI beads and quantitative PCR (KAPA Biosystems KK4824), sequencing was carried out on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with a PE150 read length or the DNBSEQ-T7 platform with a PE100 read length.

2.15

## Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing
We used TRIzol reagent (cat. NO 15596026) to extract total RNA from RLE-6TN cells in accordance with the procedure outlined by Chomczynski et al. [ ]. Following extraction, DNase I was used to digest the DNA. A NanoDropTM OneC spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) was utilized to measure the A260/A280 ratio in order to assess the quality of the RNA. Then, we used 1.5 % agarose gel electrophoresis to verify the integrity of the RNA. Using a Qubit3.0 and the Qubit RNA Broad Range Assay kit (Life Technologies, Q10210), the amount of qualified RNAs was measured. VAHTS mRNA Capture Beads (VAHTS, cat. NO. N401-01/02) and ZnCl2 was added to the mRNA and incubated until the RNA fragments were mostly dispersed in 100–200 nt.
The remaining RNA fragments were then used for m6A immunoprecipitation (IP), with 10 % being kept as “Input”. An anti-m6A antibody (Synaptic Systems, 202203) was utilized for m6A IP. Then, we used TRIzol (Invitrogen, cat. NO 15596026) to produce both input and IP RNA samples. The KC-DigitalTM Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Catalog No. DR08502, Wuhan Seqhealth Co., Ltd.) was utilized to produce a stranded RNA sequencing library in accordance with instructions. The kit eliminated duplication bias in the PCR and sequencing procedures by assigning a distinct molecular identifier made up of eight random bases to the pre-amplified cDNA molecules. Using the PE150 model, library products were enriched, quantified, and sequenced with DNBSEQ-T7 sequencer (MGI Tech Co., Ltd.).

2.16

## m6A mutation and luciferase reporter assays
After being directly synthesized by HANBIO, GLP-1R-3ʹUTR segments with wild-type and mutant m6A motifs were added to the luciferase reporter vector, psiCHECK2. LipoFiter 3.0 (HANBIO) was used to co-transfect pCDNA3.1-METTL14 and wild-type or mutant GLP-1R segments into HEK-293T cells (BOSTER, CX0001) in six-well plates for dual-luciferase reporter experiments. The cells were harvested 40 h after transfection and lysed using a passive lysis buffer. A dual-luciferase reporter gene assay (Yeasen Biotechnology) was utilized to test the amount of luciferase, following the instructions. The ratio of relative luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity.

2.17

## Statistical analysis
GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software) was utilizing for data analyzation. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to check the normality of the data sets. Correlation analysis was conducted using Pearson's tests. We used two-way analysis of variance to compare multiple groups. P < 0.05 was deemed significant for all comparison.

## Results
3.1

## Expression of GLP-1R in hyperoxia-exposed lung tissues
We conducted RNA sequencing of lung tissues from normal controls and BPD rat models at 1, 3, 7, 10, and 14 days after birth. The data were uploaded to the Gene Expression Omnibus database (accession number: GSE 212098). The Venn diagram showed five differentially expressed genes at 1, 3, 7, 10, and 14 days after birth, including GLP-1R, NTRK2, GSTA3, OLR59, and WFDC10A ( A). Among these, the log2FC of GLP-1R in BPD models declined gradually, alveolar dysplasia was also aggravated gradually in BPD model, the inhibition of GLP-1R after exposed to hyperoxia may play a key role in the mechanism of BPD ( B). We verified in lung tissues of neonatal rats, in contrast to that in the control group, that the RNA and protein levels of GLP-1R in the hyperoxia group of neonatal rats were significantly downregulated at 3, 7, 10, and 14 days after birth (P < 0.05, C–E). Histological changes in the lungs at 3, 7, 10, and 14 days after birth showed that, in the hyperoxia group, the number of alveoli decreased, the alveolar cavity diameter increased, and the alveolar structure was simplified. The alveolar septum thickened gradually and the alveolar RAC decreased (P < 0.05, F–H). Inhibition of the GLP-1R signaling pathway may be associated with lung development, resulting in lung histological changes and delayed alveolar growth after exposure to hyperoxia. 

3.2

## GLP-1R expression over time in lung development
The expression level of GLP-1R in lung tissues was significantly downregulated from 5 to 11 days after birth compared to day 1 and after 11 days, the protein level increased gradually until day 14, but not significant ( A–B). At 4 days after birth, neonatal rats were infected with 2.5∗108 PFU/g/d adenovirus knockdown of the GLP-1R gene (AD24050812, HANBIO) for 2 days and administered dropwise into one nostril until the virus was completely inhaled [ ]. Histological changes in the lungs 14 days after birth showed that the alveolar structure was simplified in the knocked-down group of GLP-1R. The alveolar septum was thickened, and alveolar RAC were reduced (P < 0.05, C–E). 
Many molecules and signaling pathways regulated the lung development, including TGF-β1, BMP 4, and VEGF-A [ ]. ELISA results also exhibited that the level of TGF-β1, VEGF-A, and BMP4 were reduced in the group of GLP-1R knocked-down compare with control group at 14 days after birth ( F), indicating the GLP-1R signaling could promote lung development.
In addition, we tested the expression level of GLP-1R in fetal lung tissues, including the pseudoglandular (E15), canalicular (E17), saccular (E19, E21), and alveolar stages. The results showed that GLP-1R level was upregulated obviously after birth ( G–H), similar to a previous report [ ].

3.3

## Effect of GLP-1R in lung development by single-cell sequencing
To elucidate the effect of GLP-1R in different cell clusters of lung tissues, we conducted single-cell sequencing of the control and GLP-1R knockdown groups. The final dataset consisted of 10,813 cells from the lungs of the control group and 7849 cells from the lungs of the GLP-1R knockdown group, with 15 distinct cell types identified in both groups based on representative marker genes. In the KD-GLP-1R group, the proportion of different cell types changed; among them, the proportion of fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and epithelial cells decreased, especially that of epithelial cells decreased from to 14.8 %–9.7 %. The proportion of B cells, dendritic cells, macrophage, neutrophil, and monocyte increased, but not very obvious ( A–B). We then conducted a subgroup analysis of epithelial cells, and eight distinct cell types were identified in both groups, predominantly ATI and ATII cells. The proportion of ATI cells were almost the same between groups, suggesting that GLP-1R was not involved in the regulation of transdifferentiation from ATII to ATI cells. The proportion of ATII cells decreased most from 25.8 % to 19.2 %. After GLP-1R knockdown in lung tissues, GLP-1R gene expression levels changed obvious in ATI and ATII cells of different cell types, especially in ATII cells, suggesting that the regulation of GLP-1R signaling on lung development primarily targets ATII cells ( C–E). Some key genes of lung development like BMP-4, WNT4, VEGF-A, TGF-β1, and NOTCH2 were decreased in ATII cells of the GLP-1R knocked-down group ( F–J). Flow cytometry results exhibited that the ratio of cell apoptosis increased and the capacity for proliferation decreased as tested by CCK8 (HY–K0301, MCE) in KD-GLP-1R group in contrasted to the control group at 14 days after birth ( K–M). These results indicated that GLP-1R signaling had a vital role in regulating lung development and mainly serves a function in ATII cells, which may be important in the pathogenesis of BPD. 

3.4

## Inhibition of GLP-1R signaling in ATII cells under hyperoxia
In primary ATII cells, the protein and mRNA level of GLP-1R were markedly lower in the hyperoxia group ( A–C). Double staining of GLP-1R and surfactant protein C (SPC) was performed on lung tissue sections and primary ATII cells to evaluate the expression of GLP-1R. SPC is a biomarker for the identification of ATII cells [ ]. These two proteins are primarily distributed in the cytoplasm. GLP-1R and SPC co-localized, and the fluorescence of GLP-1R decreased gradually over time in the hyperoxia group in contrasted to the control group ( D–E). Four days after birth, neonatal rats were infected with 2.5∗108 PFU/g adenovirus overexpression of the GLP-1R gene (AD23032206, HANBIO), administered dropwise into one nostril until the virus was completely inhaled. Histological changes in the lungs 14 days after birth showed that the alveolar structure was simplified in the hyperoxia group, and this was alleviated in the GLP-1R overexpression group. The thickened alveolar septum and decreased alveolar RAC were improved (P < 0.05, A–C). The ELISA results exhibited that the level of TGF-β1, VEGF-A, and BMP4 were reduced in the hyperoxia group and alleviated after GLP-1R overexpression ( D–F). The results of flow cytometry revealed that the ratio of cell apoptosis upregulated and the capacity of proliferation decreased as tested by CCK8 (HY–K0301, MCE) in the hyperoxia group than that in the control group at 14 d after birth, and was relieved by overexpression of GLP-1R ( G–I). 

3.5

## GLP-1R signaling can regulate glucose metabolism in ATII cells after exposed to hyperoxia
We also observed that the ATP level decreased in the hyperoxia group in contrasted to the control group, and after the GLP-1R overexpression, the decline improved (P < 0.05, A). This suggests that hyperoxia inhibits ATP levels and that GLP-1R signaling is associated with ATP production. In cells, ATP is primarily produced by mitochondria, and mitochondrial dysfunction might occur under hyperoxia; therefore, we suspected that GLP-1R signaling could regulate mitochondrial metabolism reprogramming by facilitating glycolysis and inhibiting oxidative phosphorylation. 
Real-time analyses of the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) were performed using ATII cells isolated from lung tissues 14 d after birth utilizing a Seahorse XF96 metabolic extracellular flux analyzer. The ECAR value of the hyperoxia group was risen than the control group and was reduced after GLP-1R overexpression. Furthermore, the mitochondrial OCR of maximal respiration was reduced in the hyperoxia group in contrast to the control group and was improved in GLP-1R overexpression group (P < 0.05, B–C). We then stained the mitochondria of primary ATII cells using MitoTracker and captured images by HiS-SIM, which revealed a mitochondrial transformation from long and tubular to short and fragmented in the hyperoxia group. This alleviated GLP-1R overexpression, indicating that mitochondrial fission was increased under hyperoxia and that GLP-1R inhibited mitochondrial fission ( D). DRP1 was the key protein in regulating mitochondrial fission, and its expression level was upregulated in ATII cells of the hyperoxia group. The expression levels of glycolysis-associated enzymes, including PFKM, HK2, and LDHA, also upregulated compared to those in the control group, and downregulated by GLP-1R overexpression ( E–G).

3.6

## Inhibition of GLP-1R mRNA level by m6A methylated modification
In the hyperoxia-exposed cell model, GLP-1R mRNA level in RLE-6TN cells was obviously decreased in the hyperoxia group in contrasted to the control group at 24, 48, and 72 h, especially at 48 h after exposure to hyperoxia ( A). The decreased mRNA levels may also be associated with epigenetic changes. Epigenetic alterations include DNA/RNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNA. RNA methylation constitutes over 60 % of all RNA modifications, with m6A being the predominant alteration in higher biological mRNA and lncRNAs. Our MeRIP-qPCR exhibited that m6A abundance was markedly reduced in GLP-1R mRNA after exposure to hyperoxia, and 10 sequences of m6A modification sites in both the control and hyperoxia groups were shown at 3′UTR ( B). mRNA transcriptome sequencing results of RLE-6TN cells from the hyperoxia and control groups showed that mRNA m6A modification methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3), METTL14, wilms' tumor 1-associating protein (WTAP), and vir-like m6A methyltransferase (VIRMA) decreased, which often occurred as a complex; among them, METTL14 decreased significantly ( C). After knockdown of METTL3, METTL14, WTAP, and VIRMA, GLP-1R mRNA levels were significantly decreased ( D–H). 
We performed luciferase reporter assays utilizing constructs with either wild-type or mutant GLP-1R m6A sites to evaluate the influence of target mRNA m6A modifications on gene regulation. This allowed us to determine how the m6A modification affected GLP-1R expression. The m6A modification was eliminated in the mutated GLP-1R constructs by replacing all bases in the m6A consensus sequence (RRACH) with other bases. The m6A modification level of wild-type, but not mutated, GLP-1R was reduced after overexpressing of METTL14, as predicted by luciferase reporter experiments ( I).

## Discussion
The function of GLP-1R signaling has mainly been studied in metabolic diseases such as diabetes mellitus and obesity [ , ]. In this study, we first discuss the vital role of GLP-1R signaling in the pathogenesis of BPD and regulation of lung development. GLP-1R is classified as a G protein-coupled receptor, functions mainly through the Gαs/cAMP pathway, specifically binding with its ligand GLP-1 [ , ]. GLP-1, an insulin-stimulating hormone, is secreted primarily by intestinal L cells and a subset of neurons in the nucleus tractus solitaris of the brain stem. Researches on the function of GLP-1R signaling in the lungs have primarily focused on subcutaneous administration of GLP-1RAs. In our study, adenovirus was administered to the lungs directly to knock down or overexpress GLP-1R, and the results showed that GLP-1R signaling could promote lung development, particularly after birth, and alleviate BPD under hyperoxia.
In the present research, we observed that GLP-1R level in BPD was associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, especially mitochondrial fission. The regulation of GLP-1R in mitochondrial function in lung diseases has not been reported, but in other systems such as the brain, Timper et al. reported that the basal and maximum OCR of mitochondria and mitochondrial ATP production were impaired in hypothalamic astrocytes after GLP-1R knockdown, suggesting that this was associated with reduced expression of the complex I subunit NDUFB8 regulated by GLP-1R [ ]. In hepatic fibrosis, Boland et al. reported that GLP-1RAs could induce mitochondrial turnover and enhance mitochondrial function by upregulating the expression of mitochondrial biogenesis regulator PGC-1α mRNA in primary mouse and human hepatocytes [ ]. Naruse et al. have shown that GLP-1R promotes mitochondrial fusion in heart failure by inhibiting the DRP1 pathway in cardiomyocytes [ ]. These results support the fact that GLP-1R has a vital effect in maintaining cell homeostasis by regulating mitochondria, and that GLP-1R could accelerate mitochondrial fission and inhibit ATP production in ATII cells.
Single-cell sequencing showed that in the GLP-1R knockdown group, the number of epithelial cells, including ATII cells, decreased. In addition to epithelial cells, the proportion of some immune cells changes, including increased numbers of neutrophils, monocytes, B cells, and macrophages. Studies had reported that GLP-1R could also regulate inflammation [ , ]. Wong et al. reported that GLP-1R activation inhibits toll-like receptors and exerts anti-inflammatory effects [ ]. Our results also exhibited that the inhibition of GLP-1R elevated the number of immune cells, indicating its anti-inflammatory effect. Inflammation is also a key risk factor for BPD, and the inhibition of GLP-1R after hyperoxia-induced pro-inflammation may accelerate the development of BPD, which will be explored in further studies.
Declining mRNA levels of GLP-1R may be associated with epigenetic regulation. In this study, MeRIP-qPCR results showed that m6A modification levels of GLP-1R mRNA decreased in the hyperoxia group. Currently, m6A modifications are found in microRNAs, circRNAs, rRNA, tRNA, and snoRNAs. m6A modification primarily occurs on adenine in the RRACH sequence, whose function is determined by the “Reader”, “Writer”, and “Eraser”. m6A is recognized by the “Reader,” m6A binding protein, which has been found to have YTH domain proteins (including YTHDF1, YTHDF2, YTHDC1, and YTHDC2) and the HNRNP family of nuclear heterogeneous proteins (HNRNPA2B1 and HNRNPC). The “Writer” is methyltransferase, and the components of this complex include METTL3, METTL14, WTAP, and VIRMA. The “Eraser” is demethylases, including ALKBH5 and FTO, which can reverse methylation [ , , ]. m6A modification is a dynamic and reversible process that regulates key biological processes such as splicing, translation, transport, and RNA stability. This dynamic and reversible m6A modification determines the fate of the modified mRNA at the post-transcriptional level. The m6A modification not only inhibits mRNA levels, but also promotes mRNA stability. The interaction of m6A modifications with the chromatin state and transcriptional regulation adds another layer of regulation to the dynamic epigenome and increases the functional complexity of m6A modifications [ ]. Our MeRIP-qPCR results indicated that m6a modification can promote the stability of GLP-1R mRNA levels.
Our study had some limitations. First, lung development is a complex process involving multiple mechanisms, we mainly explored from the perspectives of mitochondrial damage and metabolism, but there are still many potential mechanisms and pathways remain to be found in the future. Second, we showed that GLP-1R could regulate mitochondria fission protein DRP1, but we did not explore the specific mechanism between GLP-1R and mitochondria fission, and there might be upstream pathways. Third, the levels of the GLP-1R ligand GLP1 may also influence GLP-1R function, which remains to be explored. Forth, our study primarily discussed the epigenetics of m6A, other epigenetic modifications, such as histone modifications and DNA methylation, may also be associated with decreased levels of GLP-1R mRNA. Future researches should focus on the effects of the GLP1/GLP-1R pathway on the pathogenesis of BPD and the specific pathways regulating mitochondrial homeostasis.

5

## Conclusion
We demonstrated the role of GLP-1R signaling in lung development and its key role in BPD pathogenesis by regulating mitochondrial homeostasis and glucose metabolism in ATII cells. Hyperoxia made the GLP-1R m6A modification level decrease, downregulated GLP-1R mRNA levels, upregulated DRP1-induced mitochondria fission, promoted glycolysis, and inhibited ATP production. Determining the molecular mechanisms underlying hyperoxia-associated lung injury could aid in the development of strategies to target and protect ATII cells from injury in BPD.
