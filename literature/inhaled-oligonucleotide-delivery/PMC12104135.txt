## Abstract
Sweeping contact with cancer continues to rise globally, which has led to advanced research on new treatment approaches; nanotechnology has become crucial to targeted cancer therapy. Within the intimate of nanomaterials, Au/Ag nanostructures have emerged as highly attractive because of their distinctive desirable characteristics and their prospective roles in diagnosis as well as cancer therapy. The nanostructures developed revealed remarkable biocompatibility, optically recursive alteration, and magnificently improved therapeutic effects of gold and silver in conjunction with each other. This review addresses the molecular and systemic aspects of Au/Ag nanostructures in cancer research, including the impact of nanostructures on the molecular genetic pathways and their use of systemic administration in the human organism. We explain some of the related mechanisms of action, such as photothermal therapy (PTT), and photodynamic therapy (PDT), as well as the drug delivery systems where they display potential benefits towards offering a more targeted treatment approach with fewer side effects. The latest development has shown that they have the prospect of real-time imaging and biomarker identification, and owing to this they are being viewed as a tool for individualized treatment. However, there are still some limitations: challenges of scaling up, biological safety, and bringing it to the clinic. It is therefore incumbent upon these managements to overcome these hurdles to optimize for their impact. As a result, the current findings are briefly reviewed, and the development directions are discussed to support the revolutionary role of Au/Ag nanostructures in cancer research and therapy.
Keywords: Tumor, Nanotechnology, Gold/silver nanostructures, Detection, Therapy, Gold nanoparticles, PTT, PDT, Personalized medicine

## Introduction
Cancer is one of the leading causes of global mortality due to the limitations of conventional therapies like chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and surgery, which are associated with systemic toxicity, poor specificity, high cost, and resistance development [ , ]. Nanotechnology has revolutionized the biomedical field by enabling precise diagnosis, targeted drug delivery, and minimally invasive therapies at the molecular and cellular levels [ – ]. As such, these challenges have inspired the shift in nanotechnology, particularly in the particular framework of personalized and focused cancer treatment [ ]. Bimetallic gold-silver (Au/Ag) nanostructures have emerged among various nanomaterials as potent multifunctional agents; they possess biocompatibility and stability due to gold combined with strong optical and antimicrobial activity from silver [ – ]. 
There is great promise for enabling cancer theranostics based on Au/Ag nanostructures due to their unique physicochemical properties and the ability to functionalize Au/Ag nanostructures with biomolecules, that is, DNA, RNA, antibodies, and aptamers. Therefore, they are able to selectively recognize the cancer-specific genetic signature and can be used in gene silencing, gene editing using CRISPR, and molecular diagnostics by SERS [ , ]. Wang, Russo [ ] highlight their utility to early diagnosis and personal therapy by detecting specific molecular biomarkers.
Photothermal therapy (PTT) and photodynamic therapy (PDT) are two of the many modalities of combination therapy where Au/Ag nanostructures excel. These LSPR have a strong ability to turn near-infrared (NIR) light into heat or reactive oxygen species (ROS) to target cancer cells while causing minimal damage to nearby healthy tissue. In addition, PDT and real-time monitoring are also possible due to their optical properties acting as effective photosensitizer carriers [ , ]. 
Au/Ag nanostructures also excel in controllable and targeted delivery of drugs beyond phototherapies. Modifying the hybrid structures with targeting ligands (e.g., antibodies, peptides) through the modifiable surfaces enables high drug loading and targeted drug delivery with low off-target toxicity [ ]. In addition, it has a dual function, which can serve as a basis for a combinatorial approach [ ] for simultaneous chemotherapy and PDT to combat multidrug resistance and diminish systemic side effects. 
They also are in the process of developing these nanostructures for the advancement of gene and immunotherapies. They are expected to effectively deliver nucleic acids for gene silencing (siRNA, miRNA), controlling immune checkpoints, and delivering vaccines specifically for tumors. Nevertheless, although the preclinical attainments are broadly successful, their apparent challenges in large-scale synthesis, biosafety, reproducibility, and clinical translation [ ] have hindered the widespread clinical use of AW mAb therapeutics.
Future research should address important issues of long-term biocompatibility, biodegradability, immune response, and pharmacokinetic profiling to unlock their potential in the clinic. Further safety and functionality can be achieved with such things as green synthesis [ – ], stimuli-responsive design, and real-time imaging integration. The essential first step in translating the extraordinary in vitro and in vivo performance of Au/Ag nanostructures to actual oncology models revolves around interdisciplinary collaboration between chemists, biologists, and clinicians [ ]. 
We summarize and critically discuss the role of Au/Ag bimetallic nanostructures in gene-level intervention to system-level therapeutics from bottom to top. We provide mechanisms, synthesis methods, biomedical potentials, and limitations of gene delivery, phototherapies, and multifunctional integration. To decode how Au/Ag nanostructures transform cancer diagnostics and therapy, we provide recent pictures, accumulation, and translational challenges and compare them with more related studies.

Our research starts by examining cancer biology before moving on to create and change Au/Ag NPs through chemical and light-based reduction steps. Nanoparticle delivery systems receive therapeutic agents and targeting elements where they prepare to connect with specific DNA and RNA elements. The special coating on NPs helps them reach specific cancer cells better while staying biocompatible and having adjustable surface features. The system can perform SERS and CT imaging alongside helping patients receive better treatments that are easier to eliminate.
 

## Synthesis of bimetallic Au/Ag nanostructures
Au/Ag nanostructures are produced through the deposition of gold and silver on a substrate or in a solution in a predetermined manner. The synthetic approach, like chemical reduction, photochemical, or electrochemical approaches, dominates the size, shape, as well as dispersion of the NPs, which act as a major factor of biological interactivity [ , ].
The procedures as part of the synthesis of bimetallic Au/Ag nanoparticles shown in Fig.  , are multifaceted techniques that afford higher-order dimensionality to the control of these particles’ size, shape, and composition to serve various biomedical applications [ ]. Chemical reduction is one of the most popular methods in nanoscale synthesis that exploits reducing agents capable of producing a desirable particle size. Electrochemical synthesis utilizes electrical currents to achieve shape-controlled deposition. Photocatalytic synthesis incorporates the use of light energy to reduce metal salts, and it hence produces optical and plasmonic characteristics. The primary advantage of seed-induced growth is the possibility to manipulate nanoparticle shapes through the regulation of nucleation and growth kinetics. Galvanic replacement is another technique employed based on the redox reaction between the metals; assists the formation of hollow or porous structure. The last method is Solvothermal /hydrothermal processes in which crystal uniform nanoparticles are synthesized with various structures by applying high temperature and pressure. These synthesis methods combined enable researchers to tailor the nanoparticle's properties in imaging, drug delivery, and cancer therapy applications. Some of them are detailed explained well below [ ].
 

## Chemical reduction
The chemical reduction method is one of the oldest and widely established methods for synthesizing nanoparticles and this approach has been widely used because of its effectiveness, applicability of the technique, and the potential of achieving nanoparticles of specific properties. In this method, the use of reducing agents to reduce ions of specific metals into their nanoparticles is used. It allows the formation of bimetallic nanoalloys with different ratios, which can be useful for the development of nanocomposites with desired optical, chemical, and biological characteristics [ , ]. Another advantage of chemical reduction is that by changing the type and concentration of reductants, reaction temperature, and time one can maintain the exact control over the size, shape, and distribution of nanoparticles [ , ]. This level of customization is very important for applications that need nanoparticles with deterministic and reproducible responses. In addition, the chemical reduction method is flexible, enabling the growth of nanostructures from the solution to the substrates. Many of these substrates are essential in biomedicine since nanoparticles are widely used in drug delivery applications, bioimaging, and diagnostics [ ]. The importance of this method stems from its basic concept integrated with the capability of fabricating Nanostructures in high quality; making it one of the reference techniques used in Nanotechnology and Biomedical fields.

In this research, the chemical reduction technique for preparing the Au/Ag nanoparticles involved the following: A metal salt solution was prepared by dissolving the metal precursors, specifically HAuCl₄ and AgNO₃ in distilled water. An ideal reducing agent is sodium borohydride, citrate, or hydrazines is then added to reduce the metal ions Au3⁺ and Ag⁺ to their metallic state. To enhance the process of achieving proper reduction and dispersion of the nanoparticles the solution is stirred during the process. Among the reaction parameters, Temp, pH, and time act as parameters that determine the size, shape, and uniformity of the formed NPs. Cucumbers or capping agents such as PVP or CTAB are added, to the hind formation of pellets and thus enhance stability. The nanoparticles are washed with acetone and then with distilled water and then use centrifugal forces to separate the remaining unreacted precursors and byproducts. After that, the nature and size of the synthesized nanoparticles are confirmed by UV–VIS spectra, TEM, and or XRD.
 

## Electrochemical synthesis
Electrochemical synthesis is a simple and efficient method for designing and synthesizing shape-controlled Au/Ag nanostructures. This method uses an electric current or potential to promote the deposition of gold and silver on the electrode surface or in solution, providing nanoparticle growth with a technically controlled pathway [ – ]. One of its main benefits is the possibility of optimizing the current density, the chemical composition of the used electrolytes, and the deposition time. Such adjustable factors allow researchers to obtain repeatable results and have exact control of the size, form, and morphological characteristics of the nanostructure. This high level of customization is especially useful for enhancing the functional properties of the functional nanoparticles, which are reliant on their morphology since it affects their optical, chemical, and biological characterizations [ ]. Such customized nanostructures are even more needed for high-level applications, for example, molecular biosensors, and photothermal therapy of cancer in which the structural characteristics are significant [ , ]. It is for the reason of simplicity, flexibility, and accuracy that the electrochemical synthesis method using electrochemical synthesis units is popular for preparing nanomaterials tailored to their functions to particular applications in the advanced biomedical and technology industries [ , , ].
Electrochemical synthesis refers to the utilization of an electric current to cause a series of chemical reactions in the creation of a material shown in Fig.  . The main techniques are applied electrode, where the reactions take place at the electrodes; electrochemical redox reactions to change the nature of a molecule; and electrochemical plating for the formation of layers. For the synthesis of complicated molecules, methods such as electrochemical coupling can be used while for surface functionalization through electrochemical grafting, the surface will be made suitable for certain purposes. Anodization allows for the synthesis of protective oxide layers of metals while flow systems improve the synthesis rate and capability for large-scale synthesis. These methods are especially crucial in material chemistry, energy, and catalysis contexts.
 

## Photochemical methods
Photochemical synthesis is a unique and efficient method for converting metal salts to gold and silver (Au/Ag) nanostructures by harnessing light energy. This method involves the use of reducible compounds that undergo reaction upon exposure to light and cause the formation of nanoparticles. The primary benefit of this approach is the highly flexible regulation of the process and, therefore, the possibility to control the intensity and the time of the irradiation [ , ]. These parameters affect such characteristics of the obtained nanostructures as size, shape, optical properties, and surface properties. The photochemical tuning is more suitable for the synthesis of nanostructures with prescribed plasmonic characteristics, which are critical for such applications as PTT and SERS [ , ]. This method also provides a green and cost-effective synthetic approach that will minimize the risk of side reactions and also provides a high degree of control over the fabrication of nanoparticles for desired functionality [ – ].
Further, different photochemical syntheses used to control the size, surface charge, and biocompatibility of Au/Ag nanostructures. These attributes are especially important in biomedical applications of nanoparticles with a major emphasis on targeted cancer therapy [ ]. For example, in controlling the surface charge and biocompatibility there is a proper interaction with biomolecules that makes the nanoparticles to be effective carriers for drugs for cancerous cells [ , ]. One advantage of using nanostructures is that any nanostructure can tailored to function in a specific biological environment for improved logistic therapeutic capacity and fewer side effects. This Link sought to demonstrate that due to the new opportunities in the organizational control provided by photochemical synthesis, new nanostructures can be developed from the ground up for application in state-of-the-art fields such as nanomedicine [ ].
Photosynthetic methods utilize light energy to instigate conversions and are powerful strategies for synthesis detailed shown in Fig.  . This involves choosing the correct form of light, Ultra Violet, Visible, or Infrared depending on the energy requirement of the reaction. The reactants are made ready and introduced into a clear photoreaction with the intention of accessing light to interact with molecules. These molecules become excited by photons, moving to states with higher energy where either bond scission can occur radicals can form or molecules are transferred from one to another. The reaction occurs under moderated radiation exposure, and it facilitates direct control of chemical transformations. After that, the products are precipitated, purified, and identified. This technique can facilitate green and economical synthesis applications from organic pharmaceutical chemistry to advanced materials and environmental technology.

## Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR)
Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR) is one of the chief characteristics of bimetallic Au/Ag nanocomposites and refers to the collective oscillation of free conduction electrons in response to light at certain wavelengths [ ]. These improvements in SPR properties allow Au/Ag nanostructures to form effective light absorption and scattering, which is critical across a variety of biomedical applications [ , ]. Spearmint implementations are most prevalently found principally in imaging methods, for instance, surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy systems (SERS), here the electromagnetic field induced in SPR boosts Raman signals in molecules relative to the nanostructure [ , ]. This enables an ultra-sensitive determination of biomolecules and resolution of molecular fingerprinting, which makes SERS a versatile tool in cancer, pathogen, and environmental detection [ , ]. Such tunability in the SPR characteristics of the Au/Ag nanostructures with size, shape, and composition controllable in this study extends the versatility of these nanostructures in imaging and therapeutic applications [ , ].
550 nm photothermal analysis of synthesized gold (Au) and silver (Ag) nanoparticles (NPs) function based on the polarization of free electrons on the metal surface on adopt light, creating surface plasmon wave. Complete mechanism is shown in Fig.  . These free electrons thus vibrate at a characteristic frequency, also referred to as localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), and are influenced by the size, shape, and surrounding matrix of the particles. When the surface plasmon are excited, the nanoparticles, in turn, absorb and scatter light at certain wavelengths, and how they do so depend on the size, shape, and material of the particle. The oscillating plasmon field moves energy to other molecules or ions in the vicinity which in turn influences the optical behavior of the system. When the local environment alters, for instance through a molecular binding or a refractive index solicitation, the LSPR peak shifts which means intensity or wavelength variations of the scattered light can be measured. This shift is monitored by sensors a feature indicating the accurate detection of the analyte, making SPR a versatile method in biosensing, material, and environmental analysis. The label-free, real-time, and non-destructive character of SPR enables the investigation of molecular interactions such as antigen–antibody or DNA-DNA or DNA-RNA interactions and effectively utilizes the distinct optical characteristic of the Au/Ag NPs for more sophisticated sensing (Table  ).
 

## Thermal ablation
In thermal ablation, photothermal effects of Au/Ag nanostructures are key to improving cancer therapy using NIR light by converting it to heat through the conversion of NIR light to heat. Under NIR exposure, these nanostructures show the strong surface plasmon resonance, which in turn produces the rapid heat at the tumor site [ ]. The localized hyperthermia raises tumor temperature and thereby damages cancer cells through activity of proteins, membrane disruption, and apoptosis. One advantage is the surface functionalization of ligands or antibodies to achieve targeted accumulation of these nanostructures into tumor tissues [ ]. Also, the deeper tissue penetration of NIR light is not detrimental to the surrounding cells, which increases both therapeutic efficacy and safety [ ]. For Au/Ag-based thermal ablation therapy, a minimally invasive strategy, it can also be combined with chemotherapy or immunotherapy to enhance the treatment outcome [ ].

## Drug delivery and release
Nanostructures of Au/Ag can be applied as potent drug delivery and systems for drug release due to their peculiar surface affinity and controllable physical and chemical characteristics. These nanostructures can also be further conjugated with or coated with chemotherapeutic agents, targeting ligands, or stabilizing polymers, which allows targeted delivery of drugs to the tumor site [ ]. Larger surface area and flexibility in conjugation with a wide range of biomolecules make them great vectors for transiting therapeutics [ , , ]. Based on linkers, functionalization may incorporate pH-sensitive or temperature-sensitive links to make sure the drug will only be released in some conditions, such as the environment of a tumor or upon application of a stimulus, light, or heat, respectively [ , ].
At the tumor site, the responses from nanostructures can be more controlled drug release, showing minimum side effects on other internal organs and better therapeutic benefits [ ]. The passive targeting of the Au/Ag nanostructures, as well as active targeting using ligands or antibodies, affords even higher concentrations within cancer cells compared to healthy cells. Moreover, they demonstrate plasmonic effects that enable stimuli-sensitive release of the encapsulated drug by near-infrared light, enabling an additional level of control in treatment [ , ]. Both features convey the high potential of Au/Ag nanostructures as an advanced tool in targeted therapies for cancer, as it combines both diagnostics and treatment procedures in one system [ , ].
The drug delivery and release mechanisms of gold (Au) or silver (Ag) nanoparticles (NPs) are first to synthesize nanoparticles with control over size, shape, and surface to achieve optimal delivery of the drug (Fig.  ). Innovative coatings like the PEG, antibodies, or peptides coatings make the nanoparticles stable, targetable, and biocompatible which consecutively means proper drug attachment. The drugs, chemotherapeutic or proteins, are incorporated into the nanoparticles through adsorption, chemical conjugation, or encapsulation. The functionalized NPs are planned to interact with receptors on the target cells or tissues, and thus facilitate the process of endocytosis in order to enter cells. Once the nanoparticles have entered the cells the drug is released by alterations in pH, temperature, or by other stimuli such as light or magnetism. The release is moderated by the surface characteristics of the nanoparticles that can provide the sustained or triggered release of the drug. The drug acts at the target site only and remains relatively less toxic to normal tissues unlike in radiation therapy where general body cells are affected. Lastly, after the drug has been released and the desired effect produced, the nanoparticles disintegrate or are washed out of the body through normal elimination processes, this makes drugs have a very selective and effective delivery that enhances their performance in the body [ ].
 

## Gene and immunotherapy
Au/Ag nanostructures are useful tools in gene and immunotherapy in cancer treatment owing to the capability of the versatile nano platform to attach DNA or RNA molecules. This unique capability makes them a useful vehicle for the delivery of gene therapies that deliver the necessary genetic material into cancer cells [ – ]. When properly synthesized, these nanostructures can have therapeutic DNA or RNA, such as siRNA, mRNA, or plasmid DNA, conjugated to their surface, hence allowing it to avoid constraints such as the cell membrane layer by specifically delivering packaged genetic payloads. It can also see that the size and charge of the developed nanostructures can regulated to enhance cellular endocytic uptake, thus affecting the targeted introduction of gene therapies into cancerous cells. This approach may help change the tumor cells’ genes, thus allowing the deceased or overexpression of proteins that are therapeutic or suppression of those that are deadly to tumor cells [ – ].
Besides applications in gene delivery, the developed Au/Ag nanostructures may also use in improving immunotherapy by provoking the immune response to identify and destroy cancer cells. When certain molecules like cytokines, antibodies, or immune checkpoint inhibitors grafted on the cancer cell, an immune signal against tumors launched or amplified. The nanostructures also serve as vaccines or adjuvants for enhancing the immune response towards the desired tumor antigens [ , ]. The targeted accumulation and immunomodulatory effects of synthesized Au/Ag nanostructures make them well suited for cancer therapies like gene and immunotherapy in which the nanostructures site at tumor areas to activate both inherent and adaptive immunity. The Au/Ag nanostructures can also couple with DNA or RNA molecules to serve as gene delivery vectors for cancer cells or to trigger immune system responses against the cancer cells [ , ].

## DNA damage and repair
Au/Ag nanostructures are excellent agents for damaging cancer cell DNA, changing its primary structure and the cancer cells’ DNA repair mechanism. Such nanostructures are capable of generating ROS after exposure to light or light-triggered catalytic activity and lead to cell damage in cancer cells by disrupting their DNA. These damages include strand breaks, base alterations, and cross-linking, which overburden the repair mechanisms that cancer cells depend on for their growth and survival [ – ]. By regulation specific to p53, homologous recombination, and non-homologous end joining homologous recombination, the Au/Ag nanostructures raise the genotoxic stress on the tumor cell, compelling it to either apoptosis or mitosis [ – ].
At the same time, these nanostructures work as improved forms of traditional treatment strategies, including chemotherapy and radiation therapy. Both therapies rely on the creation of DNA damage to carry out the cancer cells, but due to the increased DNA repair ability within the region of the tumor, the former resists the therapies. Through this, Au/Ag nanostructures strengthen the effects of the cancer treatments, thus enabling the adoption of small amounts that hurt the healthy cells but also bringing a halt to the repair mechanisms in the cancer cells. To the extent that nanostructure-triggered injuries overlap with traditional cures, this cone blurb will show an improvement over repetition/multidrug resistance and direly needed therapeutic improvements that would be a boon in cancer therapy [ , ].

Following the performance of their task, the biocompatible nanoparticles are either metabolized or expelled from the body with little or no ill effects.
 

## Biological mechanisms of Au/Ag nanostructures in cancer diagnostics

## Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) and enhanced signal detection
The plasmonic characteristics of bimetallic Au/Ag nanostructures enhance the detection signal mechanisms that doctors utilize to diagnose cancer. Gold and silver nanoparticles together generate localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), which improves surface electromagnetic fields while intensifying diagnostic assay signals. The SERS method depends on this effect to deliver ultra-sensitive detection of cancer biomarkers [ ]. Kalashgrani and colleagues produced gold-fluorescence hybrid nanoparticles with high signal enhancement that served as biomedical SERS sensors, enabling fast detection of femtomolar concentrations of oncogenic DNA and proteins.
Guerrero-Florez, Mendez-Sanchez [ ] demonstrated that they could make Au/Ag nanoparticles with varying shapes and sizes suitable for targeted SERS detection methods. The study compared different shapes of nanoparticles and found that nanourchins and nanostars had the best SERS enhancement and surface activities due to their unique shapes. These nanosensor systems possess the ability to detect premalignant cancer cells through their identification of distinctive cancer markers and malignant developmental changes on cell surfaces.

## Biomolecular recognition and targeting pathways
Bimetallic Au/Ag nanostructures maintain two distinct functions that include signal detection enhancement together with targeted biomolecular platforms. The nanostructures receive surface modifications through aptamers and antibodies and DNA probes that provide precise cancer target recognition, including HER2, EGFR, PSA, and ctDNA. Different research studies have demonstrated the potential of functionalized nanostructures to provide both diagnostic and therapeutic ("theranostic") capabilities [ ]. The incorporation of site-specific ligand conjugation into their design led to significantly improved detection of both cancer cell oncogenes and protein overexpression, hence raising biosensing precision and sensitivity.
Esmailzadeh, Taheri-Ledari [ ] identified the fundamental importance of sulfur precursors when bonding gold-silver pairs with biological ingredients. Biomarkers in bodily fluids are efficiently detected and captured through the stable bioactive interface that develops from thiol group interactions between peptides or proteins and Au/Ag nanoparticle metallic surfaces. The functional groups enable liquid biopsy systems, which might 1 day replace traditional invasive detection methods.

## Molecular pathways in cellular uptake and intracellular diagnostics
The ability of Au/Ag nanostructures to be taken up by cells represents a vital component for their diagnostic applications. In their research, Yu, Xia [ ] established that noble metal nanoparticles use photothermal properties for controlled ablation and tumor cells intracellular diagnostic imaging methods simultaneously. The authors detailed in their work that Au/Ag nanoparticles specifically accumulated in nuclei to generate localized heating effects and increased signals through near-infrared (NIR) illumination. Such a mechanism works best for detecting malignancies that do not show clear imaging contrast during traditional radiology studies.
The structural modification of plasmonic Au/Ag nanoparticles received attention from Hang, Wang and Wu [ ] for maximizing their cellular targeting properties and biological compatibility levels. Tuning both particle shape and surface charge improved the selective uptake, while clathrin-mediated endocytosis led to cytoplasmic retention, according to their research. Relocation of nanoparticles within cells creates optimal conditions for direct identification of tumorigenic genetic mutations and mRNA expression and oxidative stress signals.

## Real-time imaging and multiplexed sensing platforms
Scientists have dedicated recent research to creating Au/Ag nanostructure systems that work with real-time imaging systems. Delgado-Corrales, Chopra and Chauhan [ ] created gold nanostars and nanourchins that exhibit improved NIR absorption ability for combined photothermal therapy and bioimaging functions. The technology allowed researchers to see tumors along with their metastatic nodes with high precision during imaging sessions. Kalashgrani, Mousavi [ ] created SERS-active nanoparticles that can identify multiple biomarkers at once, as this capability is vital for making personalized diagnostic panels. A single diagnostic session utilizing Au/Ag hybrids on multiplexed SERS platforms has been proven to determine cancer subtypes through gene mutations and protein expressions and microRNA detection, according to Rabeea, Naeem [ ] and Guerrero-Florez, Mendez-Sanchez [ ]. Research points toward future possibilities of direct-time cancer detection systems built with minimal invasiveness and high-precision measuring capabilities.

## In vivo applications
The ability to move Au/Ag nanostructures from the lab environment into actual clinical practice depends on an understanding of how these structures interact when in the human body. Important aspects are the compatibility of the vector with the body and the routes of elimination and distribution in the body to avoid adverse effects in the body [ , ]. There is strong evidence to support the notion that the size, shape, and surface chemistry of nanostructures affect cellular uptake, immune response, and circulation time. The need for stability in biological niches and aggregation to prevent also must overcome. Furthermore, chemical modifications with targeting ligands improve the selectivity and thereby decrease the side effects. Compliance with regulatory requirements, syntheses at a large scale and replicability are important for clinical practice applications. Knowledge of these factors will enable safe and efficient use in diagnostics, imaging, and therapy for adherence to clinical standards [ , ].

## Biocompatibility
The hematological compatibility of the developed Au/Ag nanostructures is desirable for their use in biological systems as it allows for increased selectivity towards diseased cells without preferential toxicity towards normal cells, for example in cancer treatment. Ranges of mathematical models that describe the physical features, size, shape, and surface chemistry of these nanostructures have postulated to define and explain their interactions with biological systems. Specifically, surface modification approaches have created a great place to improve their biocompatible property and minimize cytotoxicity. Functionalization of nanostructures with biocompatible polymers like PEG can enhance the stability of nanostructures in physiological environments, reduce protein interaction with the nanostructure surface, and increase the circulation time of the nanostructures in biological systems. The sensitivity of these nanostructures increased functionalization with targeting peptides or antibodies to bind to cancer cells while leaving normal tissues intact[ , ].
Furthermore, these changes minimize inflammatory reactions that may be caused when the immune system is stimulated, thus decreasing its possibility. Other techniques, such as stimuli-responsive coatings, also help the drug to release at the localized area and increase the treatment efficacy [ , ]. However, many fundamental questions, such as those regarding the biodistribution of the vectors, metabolism, and clearance, remain too answered due to the limited preclinical trials involving long-term assessment of the vector. It is from these aspects that strategies will developed to optimize the use of Au/Ag nanostructures in clinical practice, affording optimal therapeutic impacts while minimizing toxic effects on the health of tissues [ – ].
The biocompatibility of prepared bimetallic Au/Ag nanoparticles involves their synthesis through the chemical reduction of gold and silver salts where the size, shape, surface charges, and stability can affect their toxicity, shown in Fig.  . The ligands, which can be polyethylene glycol (PEG), help to avoid aggregation and reduce the influence of immune systems by stabilizing particles. After the introduction of these nanoparticles into the biological context they immediately interact with cells, proteins, and lipids that change the cellular uptake and form protein corona possessing the ability to change the toxicity or otherwise. Silver ions (Ag +) can bring about oxidation stress, inflammation, or apoptosis, and size, surface charge, and functionalization also influence the immune response and de-chronic inflammation. Dose-dependent biodistribution and clearance of nanoparticles are dependent on the size and surface coating of nanoparticles; functionalization promotes clearance of nanoparticles and prevents accumulation in the body. For evaluating chronic toxicity, long-term exposure studies are necessary, while biocompatibility is defined using in vitro and in vivo models with no immunogenicity and unsafe biodistribution.
 

## Targeted delivery
Specific targeting is a fundamental concept practiced in clinical therapeutics of Au/Ag nanostructures, which increases therapeutic efficiency and decreases side effects. Functionalization with targeting moieties, including antibodies, peptides, or low molecular weight compounds, means these nanostructures can selectively interact with particular receptors elevated within a tumor cell of interest, including EGFR or folate receptors [ – ]. This selective binding helps in the enhanced target enrichment of the nanostructures at the tumor site rather than the healthy tissues. Since aptamers or cell-penetrating peptides can fused to the formulation, the therapeutic payload can easily get into the required cell or tissue type and inflict its action. It is now possible to conjugate these ligands selectively and covalently to the carriers through activation of the appropriate functional groups on the ligands; further, these ligands can remain functional in physiological environments [ – ].
Besides improving the exclusivity for binding, targeted functionalization could also allow for active cellular uptake by endocytosis, guaranteeing that encapsulated drugs or therapeutic agents can directly transferred into cancer cells. Such approaches are useful in applications such as photothermal therapy and drug delivery, where it is important to have minimal effect on the rest of the system [ , ]. In addition, nanostructures carrying targeting ligands and imaging agents provide theranostic applications with tracking treatment effectiveness in real time. However, there are still some issues: receptor heterogeneity, off-target effects, and the immunogenicity of the ligands used for targeted delivery systems. In other words, designing the nanostructures with requisite efficacy, testing them under standardized conditions, and optimizing the choice of ligands based on tumor characteristics are critical to optimizing the effectiveness of gold-silver nanostructures as carriers for targeted cancer therapy [ , , ].

This is known as surface functionalization where targeting ligands such as antibodies or peptides are conjugated by things such as thiol or amine for example. The therapeutic agents (drugs, genes, or proteins) are then incorporated into the NPs based on the particular ratio of drug to nanoparticle and drug release characteristics. The targeting mechanism involves targeting the targeting moiety for direct interaction with Target cell receptors, which are overexpressed on the target cells, to facilitate receptor-mediated endocytosis. These NPs are distributed via systemic circulation or localized injection, the distribution of which is demonstrated through imaging. The therapeutic effect concerns involving the agents in such a way that they are released at the intended site to minimize side effects. In vitro, in vivo, and in vivo all assess the targeting potential, compatibility, and treatment efficiency and its final goal is the functional uses in cancer treatment, gene therapy, or chronic disease management.
 

## Biodistribution and clearance
Biodistribution and clearance of Au/Ag nanostructures are critical determinants of their toxicity and effectiveness in clinical practice. These parameters include size, shape, and surface, which macroscopically define circulation, tissue distribution, and clearance from the body of these nanostructures. Nanoparticles less than 100 nm are preferentially home to the tumor site based on the EPR effect, where the nanoparticles permeate through the blood vessels of the tumor, which are porous compared to normal blood vessels but have limited access to the lymphatic system [ – ]. This targeting passivation raises the therapeutic efficiency and decreases the harm to healthy tissue proportions. While spherically shaped nanoparticles typically display increased circulation times than rod-shaped or irregular forms, specific structures well designed to interact with cellular membranes as per certain intended applications [ ].
Clearance mechanisms, mainly residing in the RES, serve as an effector to reduce the toxic impact of accumulated nanoparticles in non-target organs. Some strategies, such as the substitution of PEG chains on nanocarrier surfaces, used to avoid fast recognition and uptake by macrophages, thus increasing circulation time [ , ]. However, long-term circulation in the body has some issues regarding toxicity and biocompatibility. Engineering the external layer of nanostructures to be biodegradable or fixing their size below 5–8 nm for renal clearance is a sound approach to safe removal. Real-time biodistribution assessment through imaging techniques offers an added advantage of assessing the nanoparticle behavior in vivo to improve on NAN design and apply the same depending on the treatment required in specific clinical contexts [ – ]. The fine-tuning of the desired biodistribution, clearance, and minimal systemic toxicity is an essential issue for clinical applications of Au/Ag nanostructures [ , ].
Circulation and elimination of all bimetallic Au/Ag NPs start from the use of their dose through a certain route, such as intravenous, oral, or inhalation, shown in Fig.  . The NPs when in contact with the bloodstream are incorporated and then transported through the system to organs like the liver, spleen, kidney, lung, and even target specific organs if the nanoparticles are functionalized. The NPs still come into contact with biological systems such as endocytosis to enter cells and through the proteins, lipids, and immune cells. Molecules’ biophysical properties, for instance, through PEGylation of the surface, can also affect their stability and metabolism in the primary metabolic organ, the liver. The clearance of the NPs happens through urine, feces, or bile, with some of them, maybe retained in organs like the spleen or liver for considerable periods. The clearance of NPs depends on particle size, shape, charge, dose, frequency of exposure, and the biological status of the organism such as disease states.

Further, patient characteristics, including immune profile and tumor heterogeneity, have to consider. These are the existing challenges in the use of nanostructures, and overcoming these challenges is the key to bringing nanostructures safely into clinical applications [ , ].

## Scalability of synthesis
Au/Ag nanostructures synthesized at a large scale; thus, the systematic problem of Au/Ag nanostructures is the limitation of the application of such nanostructures in clinical practice [ , ]. Methods commonly used at the laboratory scale include chemical reduction, seed-mediated growth, or templating techniques that may not easily extended to large-scale production. The consistency of size, shape, and surface characteristics of the nanoscale structures in a batch from one another is crucial, as tiny changes in size, shape, or surface features could mean large differences in the biological response, effectiveness, and toxicity of the structures [ , ]. Another issue relevant to some of the synthesis processes is the employment of toxic chemicals, increasing the concern about environmental and workplace safety and therefore the need to employ more environmentally friendly processes [ ].
However, increasing the scale brings several issues, including rigid control of reaction conditions such as temperature, pH, and concentrations of the reagents, which is quite challenging and may prompt a change in reaction conditions. The utilization of sophisticated micro technologies such as microfluidic systems and automated reactors may provide solutions since they give better control and reproducibility[ , ]. However, these technologies could call for high initial investments and knowledge in the usage of the technology and materials. The challenge lies with the cost of the raw material, which may be gold or silver, and the addition of functionalization steps when preparing materials that can used clinically amplifies the complexity of the entire process. Sustaining these challenges can only be addressed through an intensive scientific collaboration where engineering, material science, and process technology work in conjunction to develop Au/Ag nanostructures for clinical application at a one-time cost [ , ].

## Regulatory hurdles
The acceptance of nanomedicine, the Au/Ag nanostructures included, undergoes a highly rigorous procedure since these materials are relatively complex and new. Bodied like the FDA and EMA, extensive information on their safety, toxicity, efficacy, and other long-term impacts is needed before approval of clinical use [ – ]. Compared to conventional drug molecules, nanostructures possess different physicochemical characteristics that define their behaviors in biological environments, which require unique assessment techniques. Subgroup analysis needs to done to evaluate the distribution in body areas, its biotransformation, excretion, and its capability to accumulate in body tissues, its immunogenicity, and any pathological effect on other target sites [ , , ]. Another major issue is the absence of a unified methodology for describing nanomedicine, thus making it difficult to compare conclusions made by authors of different works. Moreover, the functionalized nanostructures with targeting ligands or coatings simply add further unknowns, for example, the nanostructures’ stability in a biological environment as well as degradation products. Chronic toxicity and environmental assessment to support long-term evaluation for the approval of new substances increase the duration and costs of approval. The formation of a multi-disciplinary and combative organizational structure between the researchers, the industrial makers, and the regulatory bodies is crucial to enhance the approval cycle. The above-mentioned challenges can only overcome if stringent guidelines defined for nanomedicine separately from traditional drugs, accompanied by strong frameworks of preclinical and clinical testing methods [ – ].

## Complex integrated functions
The design and fabrication of a multifunctional single Au/Ag nanostructure that combines imaging, therapy, and drug delivery functions presents a critical problem. The challenge is closer to the variability and heterogeneous problem, which consists of the fulfillment of a wide range of forces regarding different functional elements, without damaging one or the others and without slowing down an overall global functionality [ – ]. Additional functionalities include targeting ligands, imaging agents, and therapeutic payloads for which appropriate coupling to the nanostructure surface demands elaborate chemical modifications. Such changes must preserve the integrity of the nanostructure in biological environments, ensure the system’s accumulation at the site of interest, and provide the controlled release of therapeutic agents [ – ].
Obtaining multi-functionality also raises the likelihood of aggregation, changes in biodistribution, and unintended reactions in the body. Additionally, the combination and integration of all such functions without one affecting or conflicting with the other poses a great optimization and characterization challenge. The general challenges in scaling up such devices with equally enhanced reproducibility add to the ambitious clinical application of such designed nanostructures. Current overall challenges in the designing of nanoscale systems towards meeting its fundamental goals form significant challenges, which include modular designs, and stimuli-responsive systems can see as solutions to these challenges in nanotechnology [ – ]. However, there is a strong and increasing need for additional research and development work to create efficient and versatile nanostructures that can provide selectable multi-functions that are necessary in clinical environments [ – ].

## Immunogenicity
This brings about an immune response that another component that needs to well control to avoid the negative effects but enhance the clinical effectiveness of Au/Ag nanostructures. Macroscale nanostructures are a threat and inappropriate foreign matter; they cause inflammation, and activation of macrophages, and the complement system. These immune reactions are not only detrimental to the safety profile of the nanostructures, but they can also have an impact on the therapeutic effect of the nanostructures by providing a shorter circulatory half-life as well as changing the biodistribution pattern [ , , ]. Characteristic features such as charge, size, and functionalization of the surface significantly influence immunogenicity. Surface modification in terms of functionalization, or, for example, passivating the nanostructures with biocompatible polymers like polyethylene glycol (PEG) or zwitterionic polymers, minimizes protein adsorption and immunogenicity. However, its repeated administration may lead to PEG-related antibodies; hence, a need for other approaches. Making structures smaller, invisible and using immune-evasive coatings or ligands will help to avoid the immune response [ , – ]. More extensive preclinical testing is necessary to examine short-term and chronic immune reactions to the treatment, which is required safely translate the therapy into clinical use. The basic strategies clearly defined, although it is a balance between immune evasion and proper therapeutic function to maximize Au/Ag nanostructures for clinical applications [ – ].

## Hybrid nanomaterials
This is because hybrid nanomaterials that comprise Au/Ag nanostructures conjugated with other nanomaterials such as liposomes, dendrimers, or carbon-based nanomaterials present one of the most successful approaches to increasing the therapeutic efficacy of such nanostructures in cancer therapy. This approach of incorporating Au/Ag nanoparticles with liposomes enhances the physical stability and biomedical compatibility of these nanostructures and allows the targeted release of therapeutic agents at pre-designated sites [ ]. Enhanced payload and maneuverability due to targeted dendrimers, along with the fact that the dendrimers’ highly functionalized branched structure provides an ability to enhance the payload of Au/Ag nanostructures while decreasing side effects. Moreover, further investigation of Au/Ag nanostructures through the functionalization of carbon-based materials such as graphene oxide or carbon nanotubes may improve their photothermal and photodynamic performance towards cancer cells using light-triggered therapies [ , , ]. These hybrid nanomaterials can therefore enhance the multipart therapy that includes the delivery of drugs, imaging, and the execution of therapy to the tumor-bearing regions. This composite idea holds the promise to avoid the shortcomings of individual nanomaterials and usher in cancer treatments that are more efficient, targeted, and toxic-free [ , ].

## Personalized medicine
The development of Au/Ag nanostructures for targeted cancer therapy based on the inherent genetic characteristics of each patient point a large number of prospects for the development of innovative approaches to cancer treatment. Personalized medicine is a treatment strategy directed to the particular genetic alterations, biomarkers and traits of the tumor tissue in a given patient [ , , ]. When using this approach, with the Au/Ag nanostructures incorporated, the treatment can carried out directly and efficiently. For example, these nanostructures can be surface-modified with specific functional groups or antibodies that recognize unique receptors overexpressed by cancer cells or proteins mutated in tumor cells to ensure that drug molecules achieve selective release in the tumor site. This targeted delivery reduces side effects and improves the specific treatments of drugs and therapy, like photothermal and photodynamic, by using the Au/Ag nanostructures [ , , , ].
In addition, multi-component nanostructured Au/Ag films could be employed to implement monitoring of the treatment outcome through employing various imaging methods, including CT or MRI, and make necessary changes according to the particular reaction of a patient. The integration of genetic data with nanotechnology can help to enhance certain individual treatment plans, minimize side effects, and create an effective pathway to less aggressive and more personalized treatment of cancer based on tumor-specific biology [ , – ].

## Combination therapies
The synergistic approach using Au/Ag nanostructures together with conventional methods of chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and radiation therapy can also be an effective approach that can overcome the drawbacks of a given type of treatment, where the focus is on a combination of treatments [ ]. Auxiliary to these traditional therapeutic approaches, the present study estimated that Au/Ag nanostructures have the potential to increase the efficiency of these therapies by combining mechanisms of cancer cell attacks. The incorporation of Au/Ag nanostructures into chemotherapy can also provide another functional value; they can use as a delivery system for the chemotherapeutic agents to the tumor site [ – ]. This localized delivery raises the concentration of the drug in cancer cells while minimizing side effects that are associated with generalized use on the entire body. Moreover, the effectiveness of Au/Ag nanoparticles in generating hyperthermia through photothermal therapy makes the tumor cells sensitive to chemotherapeutic drugs [ – ].
In immunotherapy, Au/Ag nanostructures can increase the immune response through the stimulation of dendritic cells or macrophages, which results in improving the antigen presentation as well as increasing a powerful immune reaction against tumors. They can also function as a cancer vaccine or enhance the results of an immune checkpoint inhibitor. In addition, the surface of these nanostructures can coated to adhere to particular immune molecules that can help regulate the localization of immune cells to tumor regions [ , ]. Radiation therapy, in conjunction with Au/Ag nanostructures, improved the higher deposition of radiation energy at the local site. Gold and silver nanoparticles can increase the sensitivity of radiation by enhancing the ability of tumors to absorb X-ray frequencies. This result in enhanced DNA damage within the cancer cells because of is less hazardous to other normal tissues [ ]. In general, Au/Ag nanostructures, therefore, may become synergistic with conventional cancer treatments because of improved drug targeting, better-known immune system responses, and optimized radiation therapy [ ].

## Conclusion
Au/Ag bimetallic nanostructures are prospective multifunctional agents in nanomedicine, considered to provide novel and multiple approaches to cancer theranostics. These nanostructures have unique characteristics that qualify them to address the cancer cells effectively. Due to their capability to directly engage with genetic material, they can release therapeutic payloads directly to the tumor site with reduced downstream off-target effects. Consequently, the Au/Ag nanoparticles with protuberances functionalized by targeting molecules like antibodies or peptides can effectively recognize the surface of cancer cells and enhance the targeting capability for delivering the drug with negligible damage to the healthy cells in the body.
Another strength of bimetallic Au/Ag nanostructures focuses on the improvement of therapeutic efficacies using heat and light triggers. Au/Ag nanostructures, when placed under light, bend the energy into heat, thus providing a means for photothermal therapy in the eradication of cancer cells. This process can further improved by the presence of surface plasmon resonance in the Au/Ag alloy nanocomposites, cutting down the energy required for light absorption. Furthermore, there may be an enhancement effect when both gold and silver are incorporated into these nanostructures for therapeutic uses. It is therefore evident that overcoming toxicity, stability, and efficient synthesis will be central to achieving the future incorporation of these researched compounds in clinical practice. Once these challenges surmounted bimetallic Au/Ag nanostructures will common in cancer therapy providing targeted and efficient treatment procedures with small or no toxic consequences at all. The prospect of further development of these nanomaterials will give new opportunities to cancer patients and increase the rate of treatment all over the world.
