## Abstract
Transmembrane B cell lymphoma 2-associated X protein inhibitor motif-containing (TMBIM) 6, also known as Bax Inhibitor-1 (BI-1), has been heavily researched for its cytoprotective functions. TMBIM6 functional diversity includes modulating cell survival, stress, metabolism, cytoskeletal dynamics, organelle function, regulating cytosolic acidification, calcium, and reactive oxygen species (ROS). Clinical research shows TMBIM6 plays a key role in many of the world’s top diseases/injuries (i.e., Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, diabetes, obesity, brain injury, liver disease, heart disease, aging, etc.), including cancer, where TMBIM6 expression impacts patient survival, chemoresistance, cancer progression, and metastasis. We show TMBIM6 is activated by, and undergoes, different conformational changes that dictate its function following a significant change in the cell’s IntraCellular Environment (ICE). TMBIM6 agonism, following ICE change, can help the cell overcome multiple stresses including toxin exposure, viral infection, wound healing, and excitotoxicity. However, in cancer cells TMBIM6 agonism results in rapid paraptotic induction irrespective of the cancer type, sub-type, genotype or phenotype. Furthermore, the level of TMBIM6 expression in cancer did not dictate the level of paraptotic induction; however, it did dictate the rate at which paraptosis occurred. TMBIM6 agonism did not induce paraptosis in cancer via canonical routes involving p38 MAPK, JNK, ERK, UPR, autophagy, proteasomes, or Caspase-9. Instead, TMBIM6 agonism in cancer upregulates cytosolic Ca2+ and ROS, activates lysosome biogenesis, and induces paraptosis via ERAD II mechanisms. In xenograft models, we show TMBIM6 agonism induces rapid cancer cell death with no toxicity, even at high doses of TMBIM6 agonist (>450 mg/kg). In summary, this study shows TMBIM6’s functional diversity is only activated by severe ICE change in diseased/injured cells, highlighting its transformative potential as a therapeutic target across various diseases and injuries, including cancer.

## Introduction
Transmembrane B cell lymphoma 2-associated X protein (BAX) inhibitor motif-containing (TMBIM) 6, also known as Bax inhibitor 1 (BI-1), was identified as an anti-apoptotic protein that predominantly localizes to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [ ]. TMBIM6 structure remains unknown but is predicted to be a 6 or 7-transmembrane protein who’s N terminus (TMBIM6 Ntrm) interacts with pro-survival binding partners including cardiolipin (CP), phosphatidylserine (PS), Bcl-2, Bcl-xL and anti-apoptotic bacterial effector proteins [ – ]; but seemingly lacks direct functionality with relation to TMBIM6 activity. TMBIM6 C-terminus (TMBIM6 Ctrm) is directly involved in TMBIM6 function and regulates ER stress [ – ], reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [ , ], actin cytoskeletal dynamics [ , – ], metabolism [ ], cell survival [ ], and cytosolic Ca2+ concentration [ – ] through interaction with numerous binding partners. TMBIM6 has environmental detection sites that detect variations in pH and Ca2+ [ – ]. TMBIM6’s functional diversity is key in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative disease (Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease), ischemic-reperfusion injury, viral and bacterial infection, heart disease, liver disease, wound healing, aging, traumatic brain injury, and cancer [ , , – ].
Within cancer, TMBIM6 expression varies greatly, with breast, glioma, prostate, uterine, and ovarian cancers over-expressing TMBIM6 two-fold or more whereas stomach, colon, kidney, lung, and rectal cancers downregulate TMBIM6 expression two-fold or more [ , , ]. Within breast, ovarian and uterine cancers TMBIM6 knockdown, using siRNA (TMBIM6kd), either induced spontaneous apoptosis [ , , ] or, where spontaneous apoptosis did not occur, increased cellular stress and heightened sensitivity to pro-apoptotic stimulants [ , , ]. Recently, a TMBIM6 antagonist, BIA, was shown to inhibit tumor growth and, for some cancers, reduced tumor size and volume [ ]. BIA acted by inhibiting TMBIM6 interaction with mTORC2, thereby blocking mTORC2 complex formation with RICTOR and RPL19, resulting in the inhibition of Akt phosphorylation; cellular metabolism; cancer cell migration; and tumor invasion [ ]. The TMBIM6 interaction site for BIA was not elucidated nor was the type of cell death (i.e., apoptosis, anoikis, etc.) or cellular changes (i.e., nuclear condensation, caspase activation, etc.).
Our results show TMBIM6 activates specific functions in response to distinct IntraCellular Environmental (ICE) changes, with TMBIM6Ntrm modulating TMBIM6 response. ICE changes are fundamental to disease/injury progression and regulation dictates disease outcome. We show, following disease/injury-induced ICE change, TMBIM6Ntrm modulation can overcome the effects of toxins, viral infection, excitotoxicity observed in brain injury, and promote wound healing. Yet in cancer, we reveal TMBIM6 has pro-death capabilities, inducing paraptotic cell death. However, TMBIM6Ntrm modulation had no effect on non-cancerous/non-stressed cells even at high doses. We observed that TMIBM6 induces paraptosis in non-canonical manner and did not involve ER stress signaling, p38 MAPK pathways, proteasomal or autophagosomal pathways, and protein synthesis is not required. TMBIM6 paraptotic induction occurred due to elevated cytosolic ROS and Calcium which activated lysosomal-mediated ER degradation. This was universal across all cancers tested and was not dictated by the level of TMBIM6 expression or variations in the genotype/phenotype of the cancer. In mice models, TMBIM6 Ntrm modulation had no toxic effects, even at doses >400 mg/kg, and induced significant reduction ( > 95%) in tumor size and volume in breast and ovarian models. In summary, we highlight the therapeutic potential of ICE regulation through TMBIM6 modulation across multiple diseases, injuries, and cancers.

## Results

## TMBIM6 modulating peptides act via TMBIM6 N terminus to promote cell survival to a broad range of cell stresses, including injury/disease
TMBIM6 modulating therapeutics were identified via high-throughput screening (HTS) using materials from ChEMBL, proprietary peptide/chemical libraries, and known TMBIM6 binding partners described in [ , ]. Small molecules and peptides that bind to TMBIM6 and modulate its activity were identified. This paper focuses on MQT, a chemically synthesized peptide, that binds to TMBIM6Ntrm modulating its function (Supplementary Fig ) and is described in patent PCT/US2019/048414.
MQT had no effect on cell survival in normal cells (HMEC, HOSEpC, HUF, and MRC-5) (Supplementary Fig ), however, in cells challenged with apoptosis-inducing toxins, MQT significantly promoted cell survival (Fig. ). MQT blocked apoptotic characteristics including caspase-3 activation (Fig. ), nuclear condensation (Fig. ), mitochondrial hyper-permeabilization (Fig. ) and activation of the ER stress-associated transcription factor CREB3 (Fig. ). Interestingly, MQT pre-treatment, prior to toxin challenge, had no effect on cell survival (Supplementary Fig ) and did not inhibit apoptotic characteristics when challenged with toxins. Knockdown of TMBIM6 negated MQT effect indicating TMBIM6 is essential for MQT activity (Fig. ). The anti-apoptotic effects of TMBIM6 modulation by MQT were blocked by ABT-737 (Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, and Bcl-w inhibitor) for ER stress-inducing toxins, tunicamycin (TUN) and brefeldin A (BFA), but only marginally impacted FasL and had no impact on staurosporine (StS) (Fig. ); indicating anti-apoptotic BCL-2 members are required for TMBIM6 to inhibit ER stress induced cell death.
 A key step in the Fas-mediated death pathway, is Akt phosphorylation [ , ] which TMBIM6 regulates [ ]. Following FasL challenge, MQT greatly increased Akt phosphorylation in the first hour (Fig. ) but had no effect prior to Fas challenge (Fig. , time 0). The data indicates MQT modulates TMBIM6 post stress to significantly enhance its activity.
The only measured, pre-stress effect of MQT on healthy cells was increased ERK phosphorylation (Fig. ). TMBIM6 induces ERK phosphorylation [ , ] to promote cell survival, indicating MQT likely activates this TMBIM6 function. However, as pre-treatment of cells with MQT did not negate toxin effects, then pre-activation of the ERK pathway was not key in promoting cell survival. Therefore, non-ERK activating TMBIM6 activities are more important in promoting TMBIM6-mediated cell survival to different stresses.
 TMBIM6 is important in real-world stresses such as brain injury [ – ], viral infection [ , ], and wound healing [ ]. To assess the therapeutic potential of TMBIM6 modulation on these diseases/injuries, we used MQT or the TMBIM6 antagonist BIA [ ] on translational models; specifically excitotoxicity challenge of neuronal cells; viral challenge assays with Influenza A (Panama/2007/99 (H3N2) and A/X-31 (H3N2)), RSV (strain A2), Adenovirus (type 5), and the beta-coronavirus (OC43); and wound healing as described in [ ]. TMBIM6 modulation significantly; overcame excitotoxicity (p < 0.001) (Fig. ); protected cells to viral challenge against all viruses tested (Fig. , with Influenza A infection responding most profoundly (p < 0.001) (Fig. ); and promoted wound healing (p < 0.05) (Fig. ). BIA mildly inhibited wound healing (p < 0.05) (Fig. ) but no impact on viral assay (Fig. ) or excitotoxicity (Fig. ). Again, pre-treatment of cells with MQT or BIA, prior to challenge with excitotoxicity, infection or injury had no effect. Toxin challenge, brain injury, viral infection, and wound healing impose unique stresses, yet TMBIM6Ntrm modulation could overcome them.

## TMBIM6 detects changes in the cells intracellular environment and reacts with a conformational change
TMBIM6 has diverse pro-survival activity [ – ] and our data shows it can respond to unique stresses. We hypothesized TMBIM6 must be capable of detecting unique IntraCellular Environmental (ICE) change that induce conformational change to activate specific TMBIM6 functions.
Using circular dichroism (CD), we estimated TMBIM6 structure to be 45% helix, 18% sheet, 11% turn and 27% disordered at pH7.4 (Fig. ). MQT induced a 91% decrease in TMBIM6 sheets, 67% increase in turns and 24% increase in disorder (Fig. ). This conformational change could be the reason for MQT induced ERK phosphorylation (Fig. ). BIA had no impact on TMBIM6 conformation. To date, there is no data showing BIA directly interacts with TMBIM6. Therefore, we assessed BIA-TMBIM6 interaction via BLI and SPR. Neither approach confirmed direct BIA-TMBIM6 interaction. We hypothesize BIA might only interact with TMBIM6 when in the mTORC2 complex, as described in [ ]. As there is no data supporting direct TMBIM6 interaction we did not use BIA as a future control.
Next, we assessed if environmental stimuli (pH, Ca2+, and ROS) impact TMBIM6 conformation. At low pH (≤6.5) TMBIM6 was 55% helix, 3.3% sheet, 13% turn and 29% disordered, showing a shift to a more helical state relative to TMBIM6(pH 7.4) (Fig. ). MQT addition to TMBIM6(pH 6.5) reduced helices by 37% and increased sheets >400%, indicating a more alpha-beta formation (Fig. ). MQT pre-treatment of TMBIM6(pH 7.4), then lowering pH to ≤6.5, had no impact, with TMBIM6 adopting its pH6.5 conformation (Fig. ).
To assess the effect of intracellular Ca2+ changes we used 500 nM Ca2+. At this concentration, mitochondria actively uptake Ca2+ and several physiological changes occur [ , ]. TMBIM6(500 nM Ca2+) was 74% helix, 3.9% sheet, 9.3% turn and 13.3% unstructured, indicating a highly helical structured state that bears a strong resemblance to the TMBIM6 bacterial homolog BsYetJ [ , , , ]. Adding MQT to TMBIM6(500 nM Ca2+) reduced helices by 24%, increased sheets by 123%, increased turns 48%, and increased disorder by 64% (Fig. ). MQT pre-treatment of TMBIM6 followed by 500 nM Ca2+ decreased the degree of TMBIM6 conformational change, relative to 500 nM Ca2+ in the absence of MQT (Fig. ).
ROS (H2O2) had little impact on TMBIM6 conformation, only slightly reducing helical and sheet structures whilst increasing disorder (Fig. ). Neither treatment nor pre-treatment, with MQT impacted TMBIM6 conformation.
CD data shows a complex picture with at least five TMBIM6 conformations (normal, ERK activating, H+ regulating, Ca2+ regulating and ROS regulating). However, detailed structural analysis is required to understand these distinct changes and assess the impact on binding partner interaction.
TMBIM6 structure has not been experimentally determined, therefore, to assess the conformational changes we used the AlphaFold2 model. AlphaFold2 (AF2) predicts TMBIM6 is a seven-transmembrane protein composed largely of helical structures, with the C-terminus residing in the ER intraluminal space. To validate this model, we carried out immunofluorescence analysis in cells overexpressing N or C terminal HA-tagged TMBIM6. Both N-HA-TMBIM6 and TMBIM6-HA-C were detected by immunofluorescence indicating both the N and C termini project into the cytosol. This aligns to the results in [ , ], which also contradict the AlphaFold2 model. However, the tags themselves may have affected TMBIM6 folding. The AlphaFold2 model is seemingly greatly influenced by BsYetJ, to which TMBIM6 identity is 23.77% (BLASTp). BsYetJ acts primarily, or exclusively, as a Ca2+ channel and reacts to H+ to create open and closed forms that regulate Ca2+ efflux [ , , , ]. Our CD data shows TMBIM6(500 nM Ca2+) adopts a high helical form, which fully aligns to the AlphaFold2 model (Fig. ). We hypothesized the AlphaFold2 model represents TMBIM6 in a high Ca2+ environment.
For this reason, we assessed if TMBIM6(500 nM Ca2+)/AlphaFold2 model reacts to acidification in a manner like that of BsYetJ. To achieve this, we used the TMBIM6(500 nM Ca2+)/AlphaFold2 model and titrated three amino acids (Asp, Glu and His) to perform 100-ns constant pH molecular dynamic simulations (CpHMD) at pH6.5 and pH7.5 (Fig. ). CpHMD allows the protonated state of the titrated residues to change during the simulation considering the interaction with surrounding residues. This enables exploration of where the protein has pH-dependent structural features. At pH6.5, the radius of gyration indicated TMBIM6(500 nM Ca2+) becomes more compact relative to pH7.5 (Fig. ). To further evaluate this, we selected Transmembrane (TM) helices 1, 4 and intra-membrane (TM7) as reference structures for alignment of CpHMD trajectories, to calculate the root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the non-reference TMBIM6 structures. RMSD results show significant variations for TM2, 3, 5 and 6 at pH6.5 versus 7.5 (Fig. ) with DSSP analysis showing TMs 2, 3 and 5 have increased helical or turn structures (Fig. ). Our data shows TMBIM6(500 nM Ca2+)/AlphaFold2 model behaves like BsYetJ as described in [ , ], with acidification (pH6.5) inducing TM5 into an ‘open’ conformation with amino acids 151–159 being exposed to the solvent region (Fig. ); this is closed at pH7.5. In support, we identified a hydrophobic pocket (Fig. ) which becomes larger at low pH, suggesting a potential site for Ca2+ efflux/influx and showing how TMIBM6 could regulate Ca2+ levels. This data further supports the idea that TMBIM6 can detect ICE change, which induces structural conformational change. Several publications indicate TMBIM6 aspartic acid-213 (D213) is key for ICE detection [ , , – ]. To assess this, we created TMBIM6KD cells using CRISPR, and ectopically expressed TMBIM6D213A or wild-type TMBIM6. MQT had no effect on TMBIM6KD or TMBIM6D213A cells (Fig. ) but promoted cell survival in cells reverted to wild type, indicating the anti-apoptotic effect was both due to TMBIM6 ICE detection and MQT modulation of TMBIM6Ntrm. Altogether, this data shows TMBIM6’s ability to detect ICE change is key in dictating the function it needs to perform. This explains TMBIM6’s high importance in response to different injuries and diseases, such as cancer, where significant ICE change occurs and TMBIM6 relevance has been well researched.
 

## TMBIM6 induces rapid cancer cell death irrespective of its level of expression or the cancer type/subtype
TMBIM6 expression has been extensively linked to cancer survival, metastasis, chemoresistance and growth [ , , , , , , , , ], with most research focusing on breast and lung cancers [ , , , , ]. TMBIM6 mRNA expression analysis in cancer biopsies from the NCBI/GEO database (Human Protein Atlas; www.proteinatlas.org) indicate higher TMIBM6 expression significantly correlates (p < 0.05) with reduced patient survival in breast (Fig. ), head and neck, melanoma, glioma, and lung cancers (Supplemental Table ). However, for colorectal, urothelial, and renal cancers higher TMBIM6 expression correlated with higher patient survival (p < 0.05) (Table ). Three cancers (breast, head and neck, and renal) indicated high significance (p < 0.001) between patient survival and TMBIM6 expression. In breast cancer patients (n = 1,075) the median patient survival was strikingly different for cancers with high (7.85 yrs) versus low (12.21 yrs) TMBIM6 expression (Fig. ). This was also true for head and neck cancers (n = 499) where the median survival was 2.96 years for high and 5.49 years for low TMBIM6 expression (Fig ). However, renal cancers (n = 877) inverted this trend as median survival was >15 yrs for high TMBIM6 expression, whereas low TMBIM6 expression resulted in median survival of 5.4 yrs (Fig ).
 We postulated MQT modulation of TMBIM6Ntrm, where pro-survival partners bind, offers insight into key pathways that promote cancer cell survival and tumorigenesis. We focused on breast cancers, where patient data indicated the strongest relationship between TMBIM6 expression and patient survival. Initially, we assessed TMBIM6 expression in three highly characterized breast cancer cell lines; luminal A (MCF-7 and T47D) and triple negative (MDA-MB-231). We observed significant differences in the level of TMBIM6 expression (Fig. ), with MDA-MB-231 expressing significantly lower TMBIM6. TMBIM6 knockdown (Fig. ) resulted in significant cell death of MDA-MB-231, which had the lowest levels of TMBIM6 expression, but not that of MCF-7 or T47D cell lines (Fig. ); however, we did observe MCF-7 and T47D became sensitive to stress. Based on this data, TMBIM6 expression alone does not dictate patient survivability, as TNBCs have lower survivability yet the representative TNBC cell line, MDA-MB-231, had the lowest level of TMBIM6 expression.
To assess the importance of TMBIM6Ntrm we treated 8 breast cancer cell lines (ATCC 30–4500 K) with MQT; each cell line represents a breast cancer sub-type with key genetic variations/mutations (Fig ). Non-cancerous breast cell line HMEC was used as a control. MQT (5 μM) reduced cancer cell viability ≥50% in 24 h across all cancerous cell lines tested, this continued to >97% over 96 h (Fig. ). No change in cell viability of control cell lines relative to untreated (Fig. ; HMEC). This suggests the genotype/phenotype of the cancerous cell did not affect how TMBIM6Ntrm modulation decreased cell viability (Table ). Cell detachment assays corroborated cell viability data, with MQT (5 μM) inducing ≥45% loss in cancer cell attachment in 24 h rising to >90% over 96 h across all cancer cell lines (Fig. ). There was no change in the attachment of control cells relative to untreated (Fig. ; HMEC). FACS analysis, gated on cell size and shape, showed MQT (5 μM) induced a forward and side scatter pattern indicative of dying cells (Fig. ), this increased over 96 h. This was not observed in non-cancerous cells (MCF-10F). To ensure these effects were due to TMBIM6, TMBIM6 siRNA (TMBIM6kd) was used on MCF-7 (a breast cancer cell line that does not apoptose following TMBIM6kd) and MCF-10F cells before exposure to MQT. MQT had no impact on cell viability in MCF-7 TMBIM6kd cells indicating TMBIM6 is essential (Fig. ). Dose-response analysis (1–25 μM MQT) showed cancer cell viability decreased in a concentration-dependent manner, with effects plateauing at 12–15 μM MQT inducing >95% cell death in 24 h (Fig ); no effect was observed in healthy cells at any MQT concentration.
To determine if this was unique to breast cancers, we assessed MQT impact on cell viability in ovarian, uterine, lung, and colorectal cancers (respective ATCC panels TCP-1021, TCP-1023, TCP-1016, and individual colorectal cell lines HCT-114 and HCT-116). For all ovarian and uterine cancers, the data mirrored that of breast cancer cell lines (≥45% loss in cell viability or attachment in 24 h, >90% in 96 h) (Fig. Ovarian, S2e Uterine). In lung and colorectal cancers MQT induced ≥30% loss in cell viability/attachment in 24 h, rising to >90% in 96 h (Fig. lung, S2f colorectal). No impact was observed in healthy ovarian/uterine/lung cells. We postulate the same effect was observed across different cancer types and sub-types because they share common intracellular environmental changes i.e., high intracellular ROS [ – ]; high intracellular calcium [ – ]; changes to intracellular acidification due to increased expression of proton and lactate transporters [ – ], oxygen deficiency (hypoxia) [ – ] etc., However, the degree of ICE change between cancers does differ, which could explain the difference in the level and rate of the MQT effect. For example, breast cancer has up to 5-fold increase in ROS [ , ] whereas lung cancer has up to a 3-fold increase [ ].
To assess if TMBIM6Ntrm modulation impacts cancer survival in vivo, we used 8-week-old balb/c female mice which were either orthotopically injected with MCF-7 (breast) or subcutaneously injected with SK-OV-3 cells. Following a period to enable primary tumor growth, we initiated the study with a loading dose (100 mg/kg), followed by 14 days QoD treatment with MQT (20 mg/kg), placebo (PBS), or tamoxifen (5 mg/kg) for breast / Gedatlosib (15 mg/kg) for ovarian. Modulating TMBIM6Ntrm with MQT showed a significant (p < 0.001) reduction (>95%) in breast and ovarian tumor size, volume, and weight within 20 days (Fig. for breast, 4 m, n for ovarian) relative to the control. There were no differences in the body weight of control and MQT-treated mice (Fig ). In vivo data indicates TMBIM6Ntrm modulation can induce rapid tumor death, which is in alignment with the in vitro data.

## TMBIM6 induces paraptosis in cancerous cells dependent upon intracellular environmental detection
Cancer cell death induced by TMBIM6 modulation was unexpected, so we investigated the mechanism by which this occurs. TMBIM6 is a regulator of apoptosis, therefore we assessed early, mid, and late-stage apoptotic markers. Externalization of annexin-V (early apoptotic marker) did occur but only late-stage in rounded cells, many of which were floating (Fig. , ). Adherent cells did not exhibit Annexin-V externalization but had cytosolic vacuolation (Fig. , pre-rounding), indicating vacuolation occurred prior to cell rounding and Annexin-V externalization. Caspase-3 activation (early apoptotic marker) also did not occur when assessed via IF (Fig. ). This was validated using Caspase-Glo 3/7 (Promega) which showed no caspase 3/7 activation in MQT-treated cells, confirming caspase-independent cell death. We assessed if any caspase activation was necessary for cancer cell death using pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-fmk prior to the addition of 5 μM MQT. Z-VAD-fmk did not block cancer cell death (Fig. ) or the observed cytosolic vacuolation (Fig. ). Mitochondrial permeabilization (early apoptotic marker) was observed in cancer cells treated with MQT, but not in control cells (MCF-10A) (Fig. ), indicating loss of mitochondrial viability. Late-stage characteristics of apoptosis; nuclear condensation, DNA fragmentation, formation of apoptotic bodies, and cell membrane blebbing were not observed. However, MQT did induce a form of ‘nuclear condensation’ in >50% of cancer cells (24 h) (Fig ), rising to >97% (96 h). (Fig ). No apoptotic markers, early or late, were observed in non-cancerous cell lines. Taken together, TMBIM6Ntrm modulation did not promote cancer survival but had the opposing effect irrespective of cancer’s genotype or phenotype. Furthermore, cancer cells were not dying via apoptotic-related pathways.
 The absence of apoptotic characteristics and cytosolic vacuolation were unexpected. To determine where MQT localizes, we used Myc-tagged MQT in MCF-7 cells and immunostained with calnexin (ER), DAPI (nuclei), anti-Myc (MQT), and actin. The observed vacuoles were in the ER with MQT localizing around the vacuoles (Fig. ). ER vacuolation was also observed in ovarian (Fig. ), uterine (Fig. ), lung (Fig. ), and colorectal (Fig. ) cancers. To determine the importance of TMBIM6 for ER vacuolation, TMBIM6 knockdown (TMBIM6kd) was done on MCF-7 and MCF-10F cells. In addition, to determine the importance of TMBIM6 environmental detection site (TMBIM6 D213) for ER vacuolation TMBIM6 antisense (TMBIM6kd) was used on MCF-7 and MCF-10F cells, before ectopically expressing TMBIM6D213A. In both MCF-7 TMBIM6kd and TMBIM6D213A cells TMBIM6 modulation by MQT had no effect, confirming the importance of both TMBIM6 and its environmental detection capabilities for ER vacuolation and cell viability (Fig. ).
ER vacuolation is not an apoptotic characteristic. To assess its formation, we performed live-cell time series analysis. Small vacuoles were observed as early as 1 hr post TMBIM6 modulation in breast, ovarian and uterine cancers; however, in lung and colorectal cancer small vacuoles did not occur until ~3 h. Smaller vacuoles were observed to combine into larger vacuoles between 6–12 h (Fig. ). 8–12 h after TMBIM6 modulation we observed cell rounding, but the plasma membrane was maintained. Cell detachment occurred after 18–24 h (Fig. ) with no loss in cell membrane integrity. The data suggests a similarity between different cancers as the genotypes and phenotypes varied significantly between the cancers tested, yet all underwent the same form of cell death. Prior data (Figs. , , ) shows MQT does not act prophylactically and requires TMBIM6 and its environmental detection capabilities. Structural data (Figs. , ) also indicated TMBIM6’s ability to detect ICE change is key in dictating the function it needs to perform. This supports our prior hypothesis that cancers share similar intracellular environment characteristics i.e., ER burden, elevated cytosolic ROS, elevated cytosolic H+, increased metabolism, etc. These common changes would explain why MQT affected diseased/injured cells, where ICE change occurred. However, there was no effect on healthy cells where no ICE change was present and TMBIM6 was ‘inactive’.
Owing to the absence of apoptotic characteristics yet extensive ER vacuolation, which progressed over time, we believe TMBIM6 modulation induces paraptosis. [ ] reviews the features of paraptosis relative to other forms of cell death. The morphological features of paraptosis are all observed in MQT treated cancer cells and include occurrence in a single cell (Fig. , ), intact cytomembrane (Fig. – actin, ), presence of cytoplasmic vacuoles (Fig. ), dilatation of mitochondria (indirectly shown – Fig show dilated mitochondria using MitoTracker) and endoplasmic reticulum (Fig. ), and irregular chromatin aggregation (Fig. , ) [ ]. We observed vacuolation intensity increases with smaller vacuoles fusing to form larger ones (Fig. ), ruling out methuosis. Larger vacuolation formation progressed to chromatin condensation and finally intact cell rounding/detachment.
Paraptotic induction occurs via several distinct mechanisms summarized in [ ] the most widely reported being via the MAPK/ERK/JNK pathway. To identify the paraptotic mechanism involved we conducted time-course analysis on SK-OV-3 (Fig. ) and MCF-7 cells treated with MQT, with pathway-specific activation assessed via western blot. No activation or downstream consequences of the p38 MAPK pathway were detected (Fig. ). Alix, a known inhibitor of p38 MAPK-mediated paraptosis, was not elevated and showed a reduction in expression.
 JNK and ERK pathway analysis showed decreased expression and phosphorylation of Ask1 and cRAF (Fig. ). However, their downstream targets, MKK4 and MEK1/2 respectively, showed slightly increased phosphorylation at 2 h, with significant phosphorylation occurring after 3.5 h (Fig. ); this was also the case for ERK and p90RSK phosphorylation. However, significant JNK phosphorylation occurred within the first 2 h (Fig. ), indicating it is activated via non-MKK4 routes. Unusually, the downstream effects of JNK phosphorylation, i.e., c-Jun phosphorylation, followed the same pattern as that of MKK4. Morphologically, paraptotic characteristics (i.e., ER vacuolation) occurred within 45 min. These findings imply the MAPK/ERK/JNK pathways are not directly associated with paraptotic features, like ER vacuolation, induced by TMBIM6 within the first hour of MQT treatment.
As both JNK and ERK were activated, to determine if they contribute to TMBIM6-induced paraptosis we employed various inhibitors (SP600125 – JNK inhibitor, SCH772984 – ERK1/2 inhibitor, LY294002 – PI3K inhibitor and StS – Global kinase inhibitor). None of the inhibitors affected the formation of paraptotic features or altered the level of cell death observed (Fig. ), leading us to investigate non-kinase mechanisms, such as proteasomal, autophagocytic, and ER stress pathways.

## TMBIM6 induces paraptosis via ROS and Calcium-dependent mechanisms to activate ERAD II pathways via lysosomal activation
Autophagy and the proteasomal pathways can induce paraptosis, with TMBIM6 known to regulate autophagy [ , ]. However, no change in localization of LC3, a marker of autophagy induction [ ], or LC3 recruitment to autophagosomes was observed (Fig. ). To confirm autophagy is not involved, cells were pre-treated with 3-MA, an autophagy inhibitor, and then treated with MQT. 3-MA reduced MQT induced cancer cell death by ~25%, relative to 3-MA untreated cells, following MQT (5 μM) treatment within 24 h (Fig ). 3-MA did not affect MQT induced paraptotic characteristics i.e., ER vacuolation (Fig ), membrane integrity (Fig ). Despite a reduction in the level of cell viability within 24 h, at 28 h 100% cell death occurred, indicating autophagy was not essential but contributed to the rate at which death occurred. Proteasomal pathways were not involved as cells pre-treated with MG132, a proteasome inhibitor, had no impact on MQT reduction in MCF-7 cell viability (Fig ). Furthermore, MG132 did not inhibit the formation of paraptotic characteristics i.e., cytosolic vacuolation. This data indicates TMBIM6-induced paraptosis is not activated via non-autophagocytic/proteasomal mechanisms. To ensure these effects were due to TMBIM6, TMBIM6 siRNA (TMBIM6kd) was used on MCF-7 (a breast cancer cell line that does not apoptose following TMBIM6kd). MQT had no impact on cell viability in MCF-7 TMBIM6kd cells indicating TMBIM6 is essential (Fig ). To assess if TMBIM6-induced paraptosis was due to detection/response to ICE change, TMBIM6kd cells ectopically expressing TMBIM6D213A, were treated with MQT. MQT had no impact on cell viability in MCF-7 TMBIM6kd cells ectopically expressing TMBIM6D213A indicating TMBIM6 ICE detection/response is essential (Fig ).
ER stress assessment showed minor reduction in UPR activators (IRE1, PERK, ATF6) without changes in calnexin or Bip/Grp78 expression, suggesting minimal/no UPR involvement (Fig. ). Downstream analysis of UPR pathways confirmed the absence of increased expression of key UPR proteins [ – ] such as CHOP and BCL-2 family members (Fig. ). In support, ABT-737 pre-treatment, a Bcl-2/xL inhibitor, potentiated MQT-induced cell death (Fig. ) indicating TMBIM6-induced paraptosis does not involve UPR or BCL-2 proteins. Recently, caspase-9 cleavage was reported as an activator of paraptosis [ ]. However, no caspase-9 cleavage was observed until 24 h when all cells were floating (Fig. ). This aligns to Z-VAD-fmk data (Fig. ), which showed caspases were not involved.
Taken together, TMBIM6 does not induce paraptosis via MAPK, JNK, ERK, proteasomal, autophagocytic, caspase-9 or UPR pathways. This indicates TMBIM6-induced paraptosis does not occur via ER associated degradation (ERAD) pathway I [ ]. Despite the lack of ER stress, there was an increase in expression of ERO1α and PDI (Fig. ), indicating a role for oxidative protein folding and ROS generation in TMBIM6 paraptotic processes. This expression change was coupled with enhanced phosphorylation of eIF2α (Fig. ), indicating the shutting down of protein synthesis.
Cytosolic Ca2+ and ROS are significantly elevated in cancerous cells and have a critical role in paraptotic induction. Therefore, we assessed if Ca2+ and ROS were important for TMBIM6-induced paraptosis. TMBIM6Ntrm modulation elevated both [Ca2+]cytosolic (Fig. ) and [ROS]cytosolic (Fig. ) in breast, colorectal, lung, uterine (Fig ) and ovarian (Fig ) cancer cells; healthy cells showed no increase in [Ca2+]cytosolic or [ROS]cytosolic following TMBIM6 modulation (Fig. ; Control). Both [Ca2+]cytosolic and [ROS]cytosolic levels peaked ~12 h post MQT-treatment (Fig. ), and increased proportionally to MQT dosage, plateauing at 12–15 μM. High [Ca2+]cytosolic leads to mitochondrial membrane hyper-permeability (MOMP), which was observed (Fig ). MOMP promotes the production and release of mitochondrial ROS, pro-apoptotic protein release, and disruption of the electron transfer chain affecting ATP production [ , , ]. To determine if high [Ca2+]cytosolic caused elevated [ROS]cytosolic by promoting Mitochondrial ROS production and release, we treated MCF-7 with MQT and obtained spectrophotometric readout using a highly specific mitochondrial ROS dye (MitoSOX Red (ThermoFisher)). MQT significantly decreased mitochondrial ROS (Fig. ), indicating disruption of mitochondrial membrane integrity, lack of production of MitoROS, and release of existing Mitochondrial ROS into the cytosol. None of these effects were observed in control cells (MCF-10F, MCF-10A or HMEC) (Fig ). MitoROS decreased in all other cancer cells tested including lung, colorectal, uterine (Fig , ) and ovarian (Fig ). The data shows elevated cytosolic ROS (Fig. ) was not due to TMBIM6 increasing production of MitoROS, therefore elevated ROS occurred via other mechanisms.
 To determine if elevated [Ca2+]cytosolic and [ROS]cytosolic was due to TMBIM6 and its environmental detection site, we performed a TMBIM6kd of MCF-7 cells and treated them with MQT. We saw no elevation in [Ca2+]cytosolic or [ROS]cytosolic in TMBIM6kd cells (Fig ) or TMBIM6kd cells ectopically expressing TMBIM6D213A following TMBIM6 activation by MQT. Implying both TMBIM6 and its ability to detect the environment was necessary for TMBIM6 elevation of [Ca2+]cytosolic and [ROS]cytosolic. Both elevated [Ca2+]cytosolic and [ROS]cytosolic have been directly implicated in causing paraptosis. This aligns to previous data (Fig. ) indicating no ER vacuolation or cell death occurred following TMBIM6KD or loss of the TMBIM6 environmental detection site.
To determine whether [Ca2+]cytosolic or [ROS]cytosolic are key in TMBIM6-induced paraptosis, we used Ca2+/ROS scavengers. Pre-treatment with ROS scavengers NAC or Trolox completely inhibited cell death within 24 h post-MQT treatment (Fig. ), however, only NAC prevented paraptotic characteristics like ER vacuolation (Fig. ). Calcium dependence was confirmed using BAPTA-AM, which inhibited cell death (Fig. ) and paraptotic characteristics (Fig. ). However, BAPTA-AM treated cancer cells exhibited a high number of small vesicles (Fig. ), that did not develop beyond their small size. Those vesicles were identified as lysosomal in origin, indicating ROS induce lysosomal recruitment but not their expansion/biogenesis. This data shows both [Ca2+]cytosolic and [ROS]cytosolic are essential for TMBIM6-induced paraptosis. Moreover, MQT treatment resulted in co-localized actin recruitment (Fig. ). This is consistent with TMBIM6’s role in regulating [Ca2+]cytosolic as reported in [ ].
To confirm the involvement of TMBIM6’s environmental detection site, we created MCF-7 TMBIM6KD cells using CRISPR. Neither elevated cytosolic Ca2+ nor ROS was observed in TMBIM6KD cells or TMBIM6D213A mutants following MQT treatment, indicating both TMBIM6 and its environmental detection capabilities are necessary for these effects (Fig ). Lysosomal activation, assessed via LysoTracker Green, confirmed significant upregulation in cancer cells but not in control cells, further supporting the critical role of TMBIM6 in lysosomal activation (Fig. ).
ER vacuolation and degradation can also occur via ERAD II mechanisms, one of which involves lysosome. TMBIM6 is reported to activate lysosomal V-ATPase [ ] and promote lysosomal biogenesis via calcium regulation [ ]. To assess lysosomal recruitment and formation, we treated MCF-7 cells with MQT and used LysoTracker Green (ThermoFisher). There was significant recruitment to the ER and up-regulation of lysosomes in all cancers tested (Fig. , ), but not in control cell lines (Fig. ). To determine if elevated lysosomal activity was due to TMBIM6 and its environmental detection capabilities, we performed a TMBIM6kd of MCF-7 cells and treated with MQT. We saw no elevation in lysosome formation in TMBIM6KD cells or TMBIM6KD cells ectopically expressing TMBIM6D213A (Fig. ), indicating TMBIM6 and its environmental capabilities were necessary for lysosomal activation. Lysosome can be formed via the endosomal pathway. To assess if lysosomal activation and biogenesis was via the endosomal pathway we assessed the formation of early and late endosomes. We observed lysosomes formed within the first 1 h following MQT treatment (Fig ). No early endosome formation was ever observed (Fig ), however, considerable late endosome formation occurred at ~12 h (Fig ), indicating lysosomes were not formed via the endosome pathway.

## Discussion
Each disease/injury induces a unique stress and change in the IntraCellular Environment (ICE) which is essential for disease/injury pathogenesis. For example, excitotoxicity induces an intracellular surge of cytosolic Ca2+ and promotes cell death [ – ]. In contrast, influenza infection induces ROS to favor viral gene replication [ , ]. TMBIM6 can detect unique ICE changes to activate specific functions that overcome the imposed disease/injury stress. In the aforementioned examples, TMBIM6 alone could overcome excitotoxicity, and secondary TBI events [ , , , ], as well as stop influenza infection [ ]. This study shows TMBIM6Ntrm can be modulated to overcome toxin exposure, excitotoxicity in neurodegenerative disease, viral infection, and promote wound healing. Current TMBIM6 understanding could explain these results. For example, TMBIM6 is localized to the ER (cells’ main store of Ca2+) and functions, in part, as a Ca2 + /H+ channel to regulate cytosolic Ca2+ and acidification; both of which are key in regulating mitochondrial function [ , , , , , ]. During excitotoxicity, TMBIM6 directly blocks Ca2+ channels, such as the IP3 receptor (IP3R). IP3R is a major ER Ca2+ efflux pump and is activated by IP3. IP3 is released by MetabR upon glutamate binding [ , ] triggering a significant increase in cytosolic Ca2+, in turn contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and cell death. To block this, TMBIM6 directly inhibits IPR3 [ ] but also interacts with anti-apoptotic BCL-2 members (i.e., Bcl-2/Bcl-xL) localizing them to the ER/mitochondria. Doing so allows Bcl-2/xL to bind IP3R and inhibit IP3R Ca2+ efflux [ ]. Localized Bcl-2/xL could also interact with any pro-death proteins i.e., BAX/BID that were released from MOMP to promote cell survival. TMBIM6’s ability to localize Bcl-2/xL could explain how MQT overcame ER stress-inducing toxins TUN and BFA. By inhibiting Ca2+ efflux pumps, via different mechanisms, TMBIM6 asserts itself as the major [Ca2+]cytosolic controller in the cell. TMBIM6 can now directly regulate cytosolic Ca2+ thereby controlling [Ca2+]cytosolic levels. As such TMBIM6 can block/minimize downstream effects of high [Ca2+]cytosolic, stopping excitotoxicity-induced cell death. In the viral challenge, TMBIM6 inhibits influenza gene replication by blocking and reverting virally elevated ROS via the mechanisms described in [ ]. Our data aligns to publications that show TMBIM6 overcomes viral infection [ , , , ], reverts/stops brain injuries [ – , – ], promotes wound healing, and can slow/stop aging [ , ]. However, our data shows modulation of TMBIM6Ntrm alone was insufficient to treat disease/injury, without TMBIM6 first sensing and reacting to ICE change. Our data indicates TMBIM6 is only activated in a cell where disease/injury ICE change occurs. Structural assessment supports this, showing TMBIM6 has distinctly different conformations dictated by the environment i.e., low pH form, high Ca2+ form, etc. It is highly likely that each TMBIM6 conformation enables unique partner interaction to regulate and overcome the ICE change i.e., NPR interaction to regulate ROS, IRE1 interaction to regulate ER stress, mTOR interaction for pro-survival, etc.
In cancer, ICE changes are a byproduct of the disease and not necessary for its pathogenesis.
For example, cancers have increased glucose uptake, preferential use of lactate, and constitutive aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect). These produce detrimental byproducts such as increased Ca2+, ROS and acidification; which confer cancer benefits by promoting metastasis and cell motility [ ], activating pro-survival pathways (i.e., Akt, NFκB), enhancing chemoresistance, and promoting gene expression [ – ]. However, if ICE byproducts are unregulated, by regulators such as TMBIM6, they will quickly result in cell death. Interestingly, there is significant variation in TMBIM6 expression between cancers, which impacts patient survivability and portrays a complicated picture. However, this picture must be contextualized by the large variations in ICE severity between different cancers. Those ICE variations occur due to differences in cancer growth rates, metabolic burdens, and expression of other proteins i.e., XIAP, Bcl-2, etc. These variations in ICE severity create different degrees of TMBIM6 dependency i.e., cancers with severe ICE change/cellular burden impose more dependence on TMBIM6.
Consistent with published data, we found the TMBIM6Ntrm to be pro-survival, enabling the cell to overcome stresses imposed by toxins, excitotoxicity, etc. Surprisingly in cancer, the opposite was observed, with TMBIM6Ntrm modulation inducing a unique form of cell death; paraptosis. Paraptotic induction did not occur via common pathways involving MAPK, JNK, ERK, proteasomal, autophagocytic, caspase-9, or UPR. Instead, TMBIM6-paraptotic induction occurred by inducing ICE change, elevating both cytosolic Ca2+ and ROS.
TMBIM6 is a well-researched regulator of [Ca2+]cytosolic that can seize [Ca2+]cytosolic control by blocking major ER Ca2+ efflux channels i.e., IP3R [ , , ]. TMBIM6 directly regulates [Ca2+]cytosolic via oligomerization, to form a Ca2+ pore, or via its C-terminal ER-membrane-dipping domain to release ER Ca2+ [ , , ]. In high [Ca2+] our structural data show TMBIM6 adopts a conformation akin to BsYetJ, the bacterial TMBIM6 homolog that acts as a Ca2+ channel. Our model shows TMBIM6-Ca2+ release is regulated by acidification, which induces an ‘open’ conformation to increase cytosolic Ca2+. TMBIM6Ntrm modulation in cancer increased [Ca2+]cytosolic 3-fold and was required for paraptotic induction. Elevated [Ca2+]cytosolic affects cationic balance through continuous leakage of Ca2+ from the ER [ ]. This would activate H+-associated transporter systems [ , ], in turn, increasing cytoplasmic H+. We show scavenging [Ca2+]cytosolic with BAPTA-AM stopped TMBIM6-induced paraptosis and blocked paraptotic characteristics (i.e., ER vacuolation). However, it did not prevent lysosomal recruitment to the ER, indicating a second player is involved.
Elevated [ROS]cytosolic occurred in tandem with elevated [Ca2+]cytosolic. Signaling analysis showed increased expression of ERO1α and PDI, which are heavily involved in ROS formation at the ER [ ]. However, increased ERO1α and PDI does not occur until ≥3.5 h following TMBIM6Ntrm modulation, yet paraptotic characteristics and lysosomal recruitment occur within the first 1–2 h. Assessing other mechanisms for ROS formation, TMBIM6 negatively regulates ROS production at the ER by disrupting the microsomal monooxygenase (MMO) system or increasing expression of ROS scavengers i.e., heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1)[ , ]. The MMO is known to increase tumor progression, tumorigenesis, metastasis, and cancer risk [ , ]. TMBIM6Ntrm modulation increased [ROS]cytosolic~2-fold within the first 2 h, indicating loss of TMBIM6 inhibition of the MMO. Increased ROS was required for TMBIM6 paraptotic induction, as ROS scavengers i.e., NAC and Trolox blocked/inhibited paraptotic characteristics (i.e., ER vacuolation) and cell death. Interestingly, blocking increased [ROS]cytosolic stopped ER lysosomal recruitment, implying elevating [ROS]cytosolic is necessary to recruit lysosome to the ER.
Our data shows that TMBIM6Ntrm modulation in cancer induces significant ICE change and cellular stress within 2 h. Therefore, the observed signaling changes that occur from 3.5 h onwards are not the causative factors in elevating cytosolic ROS and Ca2+. In alignment, we showed eIF2 phosphorylation, which occurs in response to environmental stress (i.e., ICE change) occurred within the first 2 h. p-eIF2 globally inhibits translation and transcription factor expression (i.e., CHOP) to halt protein synthesis in response to ICE change. This acts to conserve energy and enable reprogramming of gene expression/signaling pathways to restore environmental balance. This supports our data showing ICE change i.e., increased [ROS]cytosolic and [Ca2+]cytosolic, and paraptotic characteristics (i.e., ER vacuolation) occur in the first 2 h and do not depend on ‘canonical’ paraptotic routes involving signaling cascades
We observed lysosomal recruitment to the ER also occurred within the first 1-2 h, with lysosome staining showing an increase in number and intensity; indicating lysosomal biogenesis is occurring. Studies show TMBIM6 increase lysosomal activity [ ] and induce lysosome biogenesis, either via local release of Ca2+ [ ] or by stimulating the lysosomal V-ATPase by elevating cytosolic H+ and Ca2+ [ ]. We showed lysosome recruitment, but not biogenesis, occurred in cells where [Ca2+]cytosolic was scavenged by BAPTA-AM. Indicating ROS to be key in recruiting lysosome, however, despite lysosomal recruitment their intensity did not increase. In contrast, loss of ROS using scavengers showed no lysosomal recruitment or biogenesis. Taken together, the data shows ROS must be involved in TMBIM6 recruitment of lysosome to the ER, but not in lysosomal biogenesis. Indicating elevation in [Ca2+]cytosolic must be required for lysosomal biogenesis. Furthermore, ER vacuolation and death did not occur in Ca2+/ROS scavenger experiments indicating both are essential for TMBIM6-induced paraptosis. Our data shows TMBIM6-induced paraptosis occurs via elevation of [ROS]cytosolic and [Ca2+]cytosolic to induce ERAD II lysosome-mediated pathways.
These effects can be explained with the current TMBIM6 understanding. Modulation of TMBIM6Ntrm could relinquish TMBIM6 control of the MMO, which would elevate [ROS]cytosolic and have a dual effect. Firstly, it would activate H+-associated transporter systems [ , ], increasing cytoplasmic H+ and lowering pH. Lowering pH would activate lysosome by stimulating their V-ATPase, which TMBIM6 can also activate [ ]. Secondly, acidification promotes the ‘open’ TMBIM6 Ca2+ channel conformation [ ] and enables its Ca2 + /H+ anti-porter activity [ ]. Both lysosomal activation and TMBIM6-Ca2+ regulation response is to lower intracellular pH. However, this acts to create a perfect storm as activated lysosomes would be forced into biogenesis as TMBIM6 would increase [Ca2+]cytosolic. TMBIM6-Ca2+ release would also have an additive effect, as elevated [Ca2+]cytosolic affects cationic balance through continuous leakage of Ca2+ from the ER [ ]. This further activates H+-associated transporter systems [ , ]. This TMBIM6-induced ICE change would recruit and activate nearby lysosome, promote their biogenesis via Ca2+ release, and initiate ER vacuolation and paraptosis via the ERAD II lysosome-mediated pathway.
An artefact of TMBIM6Ntrm modulation, actin recruitment, supports this conclusion. TMBIM6 has an actin-binding LxxL motif on its C-terminus [ , ], actin is recruited to TMBIM6 in the presence of increased, localized [Ca2+]cytosolic [ ]. Actin was recruited to the site of the ER vacuolation, indicating TMBIM6Ntrm modulation was indeed promoting localized Ca2+ release. This artefact also indicates the C-terminus of TMBIM6 must be cytosolic facing as it would not be able to recruit actin if it was within the ER transmembrane space. TMBIM6 actin polymerization is a function that is manipulated by cancers to promote metastasis by increasing cell adhesion [ ], as exemplified in non-small lung cell carcinomas [ ].
The induction of paraptosis is poorly characterized but is gaining considerable attention within cancer research [ ]. Taken together, we believe TMBIM6 exhibits novel cancer-killing qualities by mis-regulation of cytosolic Ca2+, H+, and ROS to promote lysosome formation, culminating in paraptosis. We showcased how TMBIM6 modulation can treat breast, ovarian, lung, colorectal, and uterine cancerous cells. Irrespective of their different genotypes and phenotypes, we saw the same morphological changes and loss of cell viability. However, the timeframe this occurred changed depending on the cancer, which we hypothesize is due to the differences in severity of ICE change. The absence of toxicity and broad therapeutic potential is centralized around the activation of TMBIM6 in response to severe ICE change. Severe ICE change only occurs in diseased/injured cells. Beyond cancer, TMBIM6 modulation can overcome toxin challenge, excitotoxicity, viral challenge and promote wound healing. To date, TMBIM6 has been implicated in some of the world’s top diseases including Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, type II diabetes, Liver disease, aging, stroke, traumatic brain injury, and many other disease areas. This showcases the unprecedented potential of TMBIM6 in transforming patient outcomes across different disease/injury areas.

Specifically, the following cell panels were used: breast cancer (ATCC 30-4500 K), ovarian (ATCC TCP-1021), uterine (ATCC TCP-1023), and lung (ATCC TCP-1016). ## Construction of TMBIM6 expression vectors
The plasmids and primers used in this study are listed in Method Tables and , respectively. The TMBIM6 gene was amplified from human genomic DNA (Promega) by PCR using the primer pairs TMBIM6-EcoR1-Fw/TMBIM6-Hind3-Rv or TMBIM61-40 Fw/ TMBIM61-40 Rv and ligated into EcoRI/HindIII digested pGAD424, pGEX-6p2, or pRK5 to generate N-terminal fusions to TMBIM6 (respectively, GST-TMBIM6, GAL4 activation domain-TMBIM6 or Myc-TMBIM6).

## Purification of GST-tagged TMBIM6 constructs in batch
PCR amplification of TMBIM6 constructs using primers stated in Table , was cloned downstream of a GST fusion tag into pGEX-KG (Novagen). GST-TMBIM6 was expressed in 400 ml E. coli BL21 (DE3) grown at 37 C for ~3 h until the OD600nm readout was between 0.4–0.6, when IPTG was added at 1 mM to induce expression. Following induction, the cells were incubated for a further 3 h then pelleted by centrifugation (10,000RPM, 20 min, 4 °C). The supernatant was decanted, and the pellet resuspended in 10 ml cold lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, 50 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT, 50 mg Lysozyme, 1 mM PMSF, pH7.4) with 5 ml Popculture (Novagen) and incubated on ice for 30 min. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation (15,000 rpm, 30 min, 4 °C) and the supernatant decanted into washed and equilibrated Glutathione Sepharose 4B (GE Healthcare) beads from 400μl slurry. The tagged recombinant protein was batch purified by incubating the lysed and centrifuged supernatant with the equilibrated glutathione Sepharose beads for 1 h at 4 °C. The sample was centrifuged (1,500 rpm, 10 min, 4 C) and the supernatant collected, representing the non-specifically bound (NSB) fraction. The beads were resuspended in 1 ml lysis buffer and incubated and pelleted as previous, the supernatant collected represented a washed fraction. Elution of the recombinant protein occurred stepwise using 1 ml elution buffer (50 mM Tris, 10 mM Reduced Glutathione, pH8.0) per fraction with the sample being incubated, centrifuged, and the supernatant collected as previous, resulting in 3 final eluted fractions. Each fraction was run on a 10–12% Tris-Glycine gel to ensure collection of the sample and highlight points of sample loss or impurities.

## Purification of His-tagged TMBIM6 constructs on the AKTA prime
PCR amplification of TMBIM6 constructs using primers stated in Table cloned downstream of a His fusion tag into pET28a-TEV (Merck). His-TMBIM6 constructs were expressed in 400 ml E. coli BL21 (DE3) grown at 37 °C for ~3 h until the OD600nm readout was between 0.4–0.6, when IPTG was added at 1 mM to induce expression. Following induction, the cells were incubated for a further 3 h and then pelleted by centrifugation (10,000RPM, 20 min, 4 °C). The supernatant was decanted, and the pellet was resuspended in 10 ml cold lysis buffer (50 mM TRIS, pH7.5 m 150 mM NaCl, 10 mM β-mercaptoethanol and protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma)) with 5 ml Popculture (Novagen) and incubated on ice for 30 min. The suspension was continually sonicated at 40% amplitude for 1 min. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation (15,000 rpm, 30 min, 4 °C) and the supernatant was passed down a 1 ml HiTrap FF NiNTA column (GE Healthcare) on an AKTA prime (GE Healthcare), loaded at 1 ml/min. An isocratic wash step was done over 5 column volumes (CV) at a flow rate of 1 ml/min using 40 mM Imidazole (Lysis buffer base) followed by a 300 mM Imidazole (Lysis buffer base) elution for 15 CV at 1 ml/min collecting 1 ml fractions. Fractions indicating the presence of proteins, observed by an UV280nm read-out, were analyzed by SDS-PAGE for confirmation and those of interest were pooled.

## Cell viability assay (MTT)
Cells were untreated or treated in 24-well tissue culture plates for 24 h before being assessed. The cells were washed once with PBS and replaced with DMEM containing 0.1 mg/m1 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5- diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) (Sigma) for 1 h, after which 75μl of the medium was removed and 50μl of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Sigma) was added to each well. After thorough mixing on an orbital shaker for 1 min the cells were incubated at 37 C,5% CO2 for 15 min before reading the absorbance at 540 nm for each well was obtained using a FLUOstar Omega microplate reader (BMG Labtech). Results were obtained from at least three biological replicates performed in triplicate.

## Measurement of cytosolic Ca2+ levels
Cytosolic Ca2+ levels were measured using the commercially available fluorescent indicator Fluo-4 Direct (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
Cells were grown in a 96 well microplate, treated for 12 h with MQT, Control (CPP-GFP), or positive control (Thapsigargin – ~100 min after adding Fluo-4) incubated with Fluo-4 Direct for 1 h at 37 °C. Fluorescent intensities were determined using a fluorometer set for excitation at 494 nm and emission at 516 nm.

## ROS assessment in MQT-treated cells
The reduction of nitro-blue tetrazolium salt (NBT) into a turquoise-colored product was used to indirectly estimate the intracellular ROS levels generated in treated MCF-7 and control cells. NBT (100 μl of 1 mg/ml) was added to treated cells, and then incubated for 1 h in a CO2 chamber at 37 °C. The formed crystals were solubilized by the consecutive addition of KOH (120 μl) and DMSO (140 μl). The intensity of the developed color was measured using an ELISA reader at 645 nm. The percentage of reduction of NBT, which is inversely proportional to the ROS generated, was calculated relative to a control treated with ethanol. The results shown are representative of ROS relative to the untreated control for each cell’s respective cell line.

## Tissue culture, MQT dosage and transfection
Cell lines were grown in DMEM containing 1000 mg/L glucose and supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal calf serum, non-essential amino acids, and glutamax in a humidified atmosphere at 5% (v/v) CO2 at 37 °C. Cells were treated with MQT at a final concentration of 0.4 mg/ml, alternatively, cells were transfected with either pHM6-TMBIM6 or pEGFP-N1 (Clontech) (control plasmid) using lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) in accordance with manufacturers protocol and incubated in a humidified atmosphere for 24 h before adding MQT at a final concentration of 0.4 mg/ml. Cells were then incubated for an additional 24 h. The transfection efficiency for pHM6-TMBIM6 was ~30–40% whilst control plasmids were transfected at a higher efficiency ~70% - this was controlled for during counting, with 100 transfected cells counted in a field of view. siRNA transfections were performed using Hyperfect (Qiagen) in accordance with manufacturers protocol using 20 μM TMBIM6 or control siRNA. After 72 h, knockdown of TMBIM6 expression was tested by RNA isolation using the QIAGEN RNAeasy kit according to the manufacturer’s recommendation and semi-quantitative RT-PCR using TMBIM6 (h)-PR (Santa Cruz, sc-37298-PR) or GADPH primers.

## Immunofluorescence staining and Co-localization
Semi-confluent cell monolayers were grown on coverslips and fixed with 3% paraformaldehyde (Sigma) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) pH 7.4 for 15 min at RTP. The cells were washed 3 times with PBS and the paraformaldehyde neutralized with ammonium chloride (10 mM) for 10 min. Cells were permeabilized with PBS containing 0.2% Triton-X (Sigma) for 4 min and washed twice more in PBS, then incubated for 10 min in PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma). Cells were then incubated with primary antibodies for 1 h at RTP, or overnight at 4 °C if recommended by manufacturer’s guidelines. Coverslips were washed twice in PBS and once more with PBS containing 1% BSA, they were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody for 45 min. In the instance of co-localization, the primary antibody and secondary corresponding to that primary were put on first. The second primary was added, and it was ensured the secondary antibody was from a different source (i.e. if first primary antibody was raised in mice, the second primary antibody would be raised in rabbit). Dye such as MitoTracker or DAPI was used in accordance with manufacturer’s guidance. Coverslips were finally washed three times in PBS and once more in autoclaved distilled water and mounted on slides using ProLong Gold antifade (Invitrogen). Coverslips were visualized on a Zeiss Axioimager immunofluorescence microscope at 32- or 100-times magnification and analyzed using Axiovision Rel 4.5 software. Cells were counted within numerous fields of vision, counting at least 600–9000 cells from any one coverslip; all experiments were repeated three to five times. All counts were performed in a double-blind manner.

## Flow cytometry and cell sorting
Cells were grown in a T75 flask until ~70% confluence (1.6 e106 cells) and treated at a concentration of 0.2 mg/ml of MQTS or control for a 96-hour period. Every 24 h a sample was taken, and all cells were assessed based on their forward and side scatter patterns. The forward scatter correlates with the cells volume, whilst the side scatter correlates with the inner complexity of the cell (i.e. shape of nucleus, number of cytoplasmic granules, or membrane roughness). Adherent cells on the flask were washed in PBS and trypsinized then resuspended in DMEM to generate a single-cell suspension. The cell suspension was immediately assessed on the BD LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences). MQTS and the control both had a GFP tag, in the MQTS sample to show uptake and treatment of cells with MQTS internalized these are highlighted in black.

## Wound healing assay
Cells were cultured in a 6-well plate until they reached 70–80% confluence. A scratch was made through the cell monolayer using a sterile 200 μl pipette tip, and floating cells were removed by washing with a culture medium. Fresh medium with or without MQT was added and the cells were allowed to migrate into the wound area for 24 h. Photomicrographs were acquired with an Axiovert 200 M fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss) immediately after wounding and after 24 h of incubation. The percent change in migration was determined by evaluating the change in wound width using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

## Annexin V and condensed nuclei counts
Cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium low glucose (1 g/liter; Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, non-essential amino acids (Sigma) to 70% confluence in a T25 flask. Cells were then treated with MQT, Control, or untreated 12 h to induce apoptosis. Both floating and attached cells were then collected and labeled using the annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate apoptosis detection kit (catalog number K101-100; BioVision), following a protocol provided by the manufacturer. 104 cells from each condition were analyzed by FACS to identify cells into that were Annexin V-positive. Aliquots of the collected cells were also suspended at 106 cells/ml in PBS with 2 g/ml Hoechst dye, and the percentage of cells with apoptotic nuclear morphology was determined by UV microscopy. This data was confirmed by individual counts and repeats of the experiment using immunofluorescence as described above.

## RT-PCR
Verso one-step RT-PCR (Thermo Scientific) was performed in accordance with manufacturer’s protocol. Primers were purchased from Sigma as part of an Apoptosis multiplex kit.

## Immunoprecipitation/Co-Immunoprecipitation
Cells were grown as stated in T75cm2 flasks and were transfected with pHM6-MQTS or pHM6- for 24 h prior to lysis in IP buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mM EDTA, 2 mM Sodium Orthovanadate, 10 mM Sodium fluoride, 1 mM PMSF, EDTA-free Protease cocktail inhibitor). Endogenous TMBIM6 was immunoprecipitated, using Anti-HA Magnetic Beads (Pierce) to capture the HA-tagged MQT. Beads were washed three times with IP buffer and finally resuspended in 200μl of IP buffer. 20μl of samples was loaded with 5μl SDS loading buffer. All samples were boiled for 5 min, subjected to SDS–PAGE, and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were probed with the following primary antibodies as necessary: Anti-TMBIM6 (Calbiochem), anti-tubulin (Cell Signaling), and anti-HA (Sigma) antibodies were used for immunoblotting. All antibodies were diluted in accordance with manufacturer’s instructions and left overnight TBS-Tween (0.1%) with 5% BSA. Images were visualized using an MFChemiBis imaging station (DNR).

## Microsomal stability
The viability of the microsomal reaction system was confirmed by loss of 7-ethoxycoumarin (m/z 191) and the formation of 7-hydroxycoumarin (7-OHC, m/z 163) and 7-OHC glucuronide (m/z 339). Microsomes were incubated with uridine 5’-diphospho-glurcuronic acid (UDPGA) cofactor solution A (BD Gentest) at a reaction concentration of 2 μM along with solution B (BD Gentest); 50 mM Tris–HCl, 8 mM MgCl2, and 25 μg alamethicin in deionized water. MQTS was added to individual reaction mixtures to yield a final concentration of 10 μM and reaction mixtures (0.5 ml) were incubated in triplicate at 37 C for the defined time and quenched with 0.1 ml 7% perchloric acid, centrifuged at 12,500 rpm (11,0009 g) for 5 min. Supernatants were transferred to autosampler vials for analysis. The reaction system was validated using a substrate/metabolite-positive control (7-ethoxycoumarin/7-hydroxycoumarin; 7-ethoxycoumarin/7-hydroxycoumarin glucuronide) and four negative control reactions conducted in parallel with each set of substrate reactions.

## Animal experiments
All mice were handled in accordance with the 1986 Animal Scientific Procedures Act and experimentation was carried out under a United Kingdom Government Home Office–approved project license 70/8586. On day 0 all animals received MCF-7 human breast cancer cells or SK-OV-3 human ovarian cancer cells (ATCC) were cultured in αMEM containing 5% FBS (Life Technologies). Both cell lines were grown in T-150 flasks and yielded 5–10 × 106 cells/flask depending on confluence. For inoculation into balb/c mice, cells were washed with PBS, trypsinized, centrifuged, and resuspended in 0.3 ml Matrigel-αMEM.
Female BALB/c mice (n = 30 per breast/ovarian cancer type; 8 weeks old; 18-20 g) were randomly assigned to each group (n = 10); the three groups were PBS, control, MQTS. Animals were housed at 22 ± 5 °C in a 12 h light/dark cycle and fed rodent chow and water freely. Orthotopic mammary fat pad implantation was performed with MCF-7 cells with subcutaneous injection performed with Sk-OV-3 cells. For orthotopic injection, Female BALB/c mice were inoculated with the cell resuspension in the mammary fat pads under anesthesia via Matrix VMS anesthesia machine (Midmark Corporation) by continuous inhalation of 2% isoflurane gas for 5–10 min. Sterile tweezers were used to lift the fourth nipple and a syringe needle (BD Biosciences) was used to implant cell or tissue suspensions directly into the mammary fat pad. In all studies, mice were implanted sc with 17β-estradiol-sustained release pellets (Innovative Research). The tumor take rate ranged from 95–100%.
Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured twice weekly using calipers, and tumor volume (V) was calculated as [V = (L x W2)/2]. After 3 weeks, mice bearing tumors with volumes averaging approximately 200 mm3 were utilized for treatment. In the studies, tumor-bearing mice were treated daily for 5 days with PBS, Control, or MQT at a dose of 10 mg/kg (3.4 μMol/kg). Tumor volume was determined three times a week. Body weights were measured twice weekly. Mice were sacrificed on day 20 due to the significant reduction in tumor size and volume of MQTS-treated mice. Significance (P < 0.001) between control groups and MQTS-treated groups was determined using a series of mixed-model analyses as described in Statistical Methods. A log-quadratic mixed-model fit the data and identified 10 mg/kg MQT as significantly different than control or PBS.

## In vitro stability in plasma
The in vitro stability of the test compounds was studied in human, mouse, and non-human primate (NHP). The plasma from the different species was diluted to 80% with 0.05 M PBS (pH 7.4) at 37 °C. The reactions were initiated by the addition of the test compounds to 1 ml of preheated plasma solution to yield a final concentration of 200 µM. The assays were performed in a shaking water bath at 37 °C and conducted in triplicate. Samples (50 µl) were taken every 5 h over a 50-hour period and added to 200 µl acetonitrile to deproteinize the plasma. The samples were subjected to vortex mixing for 1 min and then centrifugation at 4 °C for 15 min at 14,000 rpm. The clear supernatants were analyzed by HPLC. The values represent the mean of three independent experiments. Samples were tested at 10μg/ml.

## HPLC analysis
The samples were analyzed by HPLC. LC was performed using a Shimadzu LC-20AD HPLC system consisting of a UV/VIS detector (SDP-20AV), degasser (DGU-20A), and an autosampler (SIL-HTA). LC was carried out on an analytical column (Phenomenex Luna C18 (2); 3.0 µm, 100 by 4.6 mm). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile-water with 0.1% acetic acid (40:60, v/v). The injection volume was 20 µl and the flow rate was 1 ml/min. Standard curves were constructed by plotting the peak areas vs. diluted concentrations (in a range of 0.01–1000 µg/ml) of stock solutions of MQ therapeutics.

## Histopathology
Segments of the lung, heart, brain, kidney, spleen, and liver of each mouse were collected at the final endpoint (20 days after treatment) rinsed of their content, and fixed in 10% buffered formalin for microscopic examination. Formalin-fixed tissues were then processed, paraffin-embedded, sectioned at 5 μM, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) according to standard techniques.

## Statistical methods
The optimal number of mice per group was calculated as follows: alpha error = 5% (Z = 1.96) and beta error = 1% (Z = 2.32), the estimated standard deviation for time to evaluation size of 5 days, and a minimum significant difference = 10 days: N = (5/10)2 *(1.96 + 2.32)2 + 0.25*(1.96)2 = 7.91 or 8 animals per group for statistical significance. In vitro and in vivo data was largely analyzed via ANOVA with a 95% confidence interval. However, data distribution dictated if non-parametric tests are needed. Bonferroni or Dunnett’s corrections were used where multiple comparisons were done to ensure the robustness of the assessment. Data represented a mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and only p values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical tests were done using commercially available GraphPad InStat Version 3.06 software on experiments tested in at least triplicate and repeated a minimum of three times. Counts of cells in experiments using transfected or treated cells were carried out as previously described with 500 cells counted in each repeat, with a minimum of three repeats per experiment. Blots were analyzed using commercially available antibodies and validated using ImageJ software to assess any potential differences. All statistical analysis is done with reference to the control unless otherwise stated or shown. In animal studies, both univariate and multivariate methods were used. The univariate method consisted of computing the summary statistics for the tumor volume for each group (treated with MQTS, PBS, and control) for each of the days on which measurements were taken. The nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test was used to examine the differences between the groups at each time point, and P-values were computed to determine the significance of the treatment.
