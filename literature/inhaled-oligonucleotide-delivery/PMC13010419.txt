## Summary
We present a high-sensitivity fluorescence anisotropy-based protocol (fluorescence anisotropy single-stranded targeting [FASST]) to monitor in vitro ligand-induced conformational switching of riboswitches. We first describe the sequence design of single-stranded fluorescent DNA probes that selectively bind to specific riboswitch conformational states. Next, we describe DNA probe binding affinity and conformational switching measurements using a 96-well plate reader. We then detail the procedures for data analysis. This protocol has the potential to screen novel riboswitch ligands based on their capacity to target functional conformational changes.
For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Rivera et al.

## Before you begin
Riboswitches are structured RNA elements that can regulate gene expression in response to ligands. Ligand binding induces conformational changes to the riboswitch’s secondary and tertiary structures that shift the riboswitch between functional states ( ). , 
Given the abundance of riboswitches in pathogenic bacteria, considerable efforts have been directed for the development of riboswitch-targeting antimicrobials. , Currently, most in vitro ligand-screening approaches require high RNA concentrations, fluorescently labeled RNA, or are unable to distinguish ligand binding from ligand-induced conformational changes that are critical for bacterial gene regulation. , , , 
This protocol describes a fluorescence anisotropy-based approach called FASST (Fluorescence Anisotropy Single-Stranded Targeting) for in vitro riboswitch conformational switching quantification. A single-stranded fluorescent DNA probe is designed to selectively bind either the apo (ligand-free) or holo (ligand-bound) riboswitch conformational state ( ). When the DNA probe binds to the riboswitch, fluorescence anisotropy increases, allowing for the detection of riboswitch structural changes. 
The FASST assay yields a switching constant (S50FASST), which is the concentration of ligand required to shift 50 percent of the riboswitch population to the alternate conformational state. This protocol is optimized for evaluating one riboswitch-ligand pair and can be adapted for high-throughput screening or for evaluating structure-switching aptamers from capture-SELEX. We recommend validating the method using a native ligand known to induce conformational switching before testing additional compounds.

## Innovation
Rivera et al. (2024) present a sensitive in vitro fluorescence anisotropy-based assay that directly reports on riboswitch conformational switching equilibria. Unlike traditional reporter-based or structure-probing approaches, the assay does not require transcriptional readouts, covalent modification of the RNA or extensive downstream processing. The assay is more cost-effective than NMR or sequencing-based approaches, requiring only standard fluorescence instrumentation and low concentrations of single-stranded fluorescent DNA probes. The FASST assay is amenable to high-throughput small molecule ligand screening and, by isolating riboswitch-ligand interactions from confounding cellular variables inherent to in vivo assays, provides a suitable platform for early-stage riboswitch-targeted drug discovery.

## Design of single-stranded fluorescent DNA probes

Timing: 1 h

1.Gather available structural data for the riboswitch of interest in its apo (ligand-free) conformational states and its holo (ligand-bound) conformational states, ideally including its expression platform, to inform DNA probe design (for example: Rfam, SHAPE-MaP, NMR, PDB or x-ray crystal structures ).
2.Identify regions in the riboswitch RNA sequence that are single-stranded in one conformational state, and double-stranded in the other conformational state.

Note: These are called switching regions, which are most commonly found within the riboswitch’s expression platform. The switching region can be mostly single-stranded in the apo conformational state and mostly double-stranded in the holo conformational state, or vice versa. .

3.Design a single-stranded complementary DNA probe to hybridize to a switching region, such that the following criteria are satisfied:a.Melting temperatures (Tm) are between 35°C to 40°C (approximately 11–16 nucleotides in length).
b.Intramolecular secondary structures and self-dimerization are avoided.
c.Off-target hybridization within the riboswitch RNA sequence is avoided.

Note: You can predict the Tm of the probe designs using Integrated DNA Technologies’ OligoAnalyzer, Sigma-Aldrich’s OligoEvaluator or any other Tm prediction calculator. We used Integrated DNA Technologies’ OligoAnalyzer to evaluate intramolecular secondary structures, self-dimerization and off-target hybridization.

4.Include a fluorophore modification at the 5′ or 3′ end of the DNA probe sequence (we used the 6-fluorescein amidite (6-FAM) on the 5′ end). .
5.Order or synthesize the DNA probe with HPLC purification.

Note: The DNA probes used in the original research publication were ordered at the 100 nanomole scale with HPLC purification from Integrated DNA Technologies and were shipped dry.

6.Reconstitute the DNA probe at 100 μM in nuclease-free water.
7.As the fluorescently-labelled DNA probe is light sensitive, store the stock DNA probe solution at −20°C in a nuclease-free tube wrapped with aluminum foil. Alternatively, use brown/opaque microcentrifuge tubes to store light-sensitive solutions.

## Scrambled RNA sequence design for control experiments

Timing: 1 h

A scrambled RNA is used as a negative control to confirm that changes in anisotropy are specific to riboswitch conformational switching. The scrambled RNA is always DNA probe accessible; it is designed to keep the original switching region single-stranded in the absence or presence of ligand ( ).8.Use the riboswitch of interest’s RNA sequence as a design template to keep nucleotide composition consistent.
9.Keep the original switching region (where the DNA probe binds) intact.
10.Shuffle the remaining RNA using a sequence randomization tool (e.g., Shuffle DNA in the Sequence Manipulation Suite ), ensuring that the internal complementary sequence of the switching region is fully shuffled.
11.Use a computational tool (e.g., RNAfold ) to ensure low predicted base-pairing probabilities within the switching region.

## Expected outcomes
In both the KDprobe determination step and the FASST assay, the blank and background controls should possess minimal fluorescence anisotropy values. .
In a successful KDprobe determination, fluorescence anisotropy values should increase as riboswitch RNA concentration increases, but only in the conformational state that permits DNA probe binding. . Different fluorescence anisotropy profiles may arise during KDprobe determination, depending on the DNA probe’s selectivity and the riboswitch’s conformational response:Ideal outcome: Anisotropy increases in the riboswitch’s probe-binding competent state but remains unchanged in the non-binding state. If the KDprobe falls in the nanomolar to low micromolar range, this indicates strong and selective binding, making the DNA probe suitable for detecting conformational switching ( A). 
Partially selective outcome: Anisotropy increases in both conformational states but with a higher signal in one. In this case, the DNA probe binds both conformational states but with different affinities ( B). This can still enable detection of conformational switching, although assay sensitivity may be reduced. .

Identical fluorescence anisotropy profiles from the scrambled RNA in the absence and presence of ligand should confirm that differences in KDprobe result specifically from ligand-induced conformational changes. Otherwise, DNA probe and scrambled RNA redesign is required.
After a successful KDprobe determination, the FASST assay can be used to find the riboswitch’s switching constant (S50FASST). In the FASST assay, changes in fluorescence anisotropy reflect the shift in riboswitch conformational equilibrium induced by ligand binding. For an apo-specific DNA probe, increasing ligand concentrations should decrease anisotropy down to the blank control anisotropy values ( A). For a holo-specific DNA probe, anisotropy should initially resemble blank control anisotropy values and increasing ligand concentrations should increase anisotropy ( B). If anisotropy never reaches the blank control values, the DNA probe maintains some affinity for the opposite conformational state. Finally, the scrambled RNA should possess a uniformly high fluorescence anisotropy since there is no ligand-induced conformational shift. 
An S50FASST value is obtained by fitting anisotropy data to a single-site binding model. S50FASST values are typically equal to or greater than ligand binding affinities via orthogonal methods such as SPR, , ITC, , or microarray assays. 

## Limitations
The FASST assay has several practical and conceptual limitations related to detection range and riboswitch structure. Some ligands have limited solubility in aqueous buffers used for the FASST assay. Up to 8% DMSO can be tolerated in the fluorescence polarization buffer without significantly affecting anisotropy measurements. Picomolar switching constants are not quantifiable using the FASST assay because of limitations in DNA probe concentration (5 nM) for signal detection. Because the riboswitches are fully transcribed and then refolded, the assay is insensitive to transient conformational states that arise during co-transcriptional folding. In addition, the FASST assay requires riboswitches to undergo secondary structure changes that produce distinct single-stranded regions. It is not suitable for riboswitches lacking conformation-specific single-stranded sites (switching regions). Finally, at pH 7, extended high-temperature incubation accelerates 2′-OH-mediated RNA backbone cleavage and chemical degradation, and improper cooling after denaturation can trap misfolded states; each of which can impair riboswitch function.

## Troubleshooting

## Problem 1
When designing single-stranded DNA probes, there is no known switching region within the riboswitch RNA sequence.

## Potential solution
Riboswitch RNA sequences can sometimes be modified or truncated to create suitable switching regions. Refer to the original research publication for more details. 
A panel of DNA probes targeting the riboswitch’s 5′ end and expression platform can also be designed then screened to identify a switching-sensitive region and an appropriate DNA probe.

## Problem 2
The chosen small molecule ligand has absorption values close to those of the chosen fluorophore on the single-stranded DNA probe.

## Potential solution
The fluorophore on the DNA probe can be changed for greater sensitivity, provided that it is compatible with the optical filter cube.
If the fluorophore cannot be changed, all fluorescent readings should be performed with a gain fixed to 50. Within the FASST assay, include DNA probes with increasing ligand concentrations (without RNA) as an additional control.

## Problem 3
When preparing the small molecule ligand stock, the ligand has limited solubility in nuclease-free water.

## Potential solution
In this case, dissolve the ligand to 100 mM in anhydrous DMSO and filter using a 0.22 μm syringe filter rated for organic solvents. Determine the minimum DMSO percentage required to maintain the ligand in solution by preparing a series of 5 ligand dilutions (12.5X, 25X, 50X, 100X, 200X) in nuclease-free water.
The fluorescence polarization buffer can include up to 8% DMSO without significantly affecting anisotropy measurements. Use the previously determined minimum DMSO percentage within the 1X fluorescence polarization buffer. Prepare 2X FP buffer with twice this percentage. Prepare small volumes of DMSO-free 1X and 2X FP buffers to ensure that diluting the 100 mM ligand stock does not lead to any buffer mismatch.

## Problem 4
During KDprobe determination or in the FASST assay, fluorescence intensities are too low for accurate anisotropy calculations.

## Potential solution
Always protect solutions/wells containing fluorescent DNA probes from light to prevent any photobleaching.
Ensure that the gain is set correctly and that the DNA probe concentration is above the detection threshold on your plate reader. If needed, increase the probe concentration slightly. Refer to the original research publication for more details. 

## Problem 5
Unexpected high or low anisotropy values in all conditions, including controls, during KDprobe determination or in the FASST assay.

## Potential solution
Contamination with other nucleic acids can cause high anisotropy values. Ensure that pipette tips and tubes are clean and never reuse 96-well plates.
Nuclease contamination may degrade RNA, leading to unexpectedly low anisotropy values that resemble the background control. Ensure that the bench space and all reagents and lab consumables are nuclease-free.

## Problem 6
During KDprobe determination or in the FASST assay, anisotropy reads appear noisy or highly variable between replicates.

## Potential solution
Inadequate mixing of components or pipetting errors can lead to poor reproducibility. Use a multichannel pipette and mix thoroughly. Avoid air bubbles and check that all wells are properly filled and aligned in the plate reader.

## Problem 7
High anisotropy signals in wells without RNA (background control) during KDprobe determination ( ).

## Potential solution
This may suggest non-specific DNA probe interactions. Redesign the DNA probe to reduce self-association.

## Problem 8
No fluorescence anisotropy increase is observed for both riboswitch conformational states during KDprobe determination ( ).

## Potential solution
The riboswitch RNA may not be correctly folded. Some riboswitches require greater salt concentrations to fold into their native structures. Try optimizing the MgCl2 concentration present in the buffer or testing alternative buffer conditions.
Probe binding may also be too weak to generate a measurable signal. Redesign the DNA probe to raise its melting temperature (e.g., lengthen the probe, increase GC content, or target a less structured site). Alternatively, redesign the riboswitch to enlarge the switching region and expose a longer single-stranded probe-binding site.

## Problem 9
Anisotropy shows the same increase for both conformational states during KDprobe determination ( ).

## Potential solution
The ligand may not induce conformational change. Orthogonal methods such as SPR may help confirm whether the ligand effectively binds to the riboswitch. If no binding occurs, the ligand compound may be degraded. If binding occurs without conformational switching, the ligand may not be functionally relevant.
The DNA probe may bind to both conformational states in a non-discriminatory manner. In this case, DNA probe redesign is necessary.
