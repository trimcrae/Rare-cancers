## Abstract
Peripheral nerve invasion (PNI) is an early and decisive step in gallbladder cancer progression that strongly predicts poor postsurgical outcome. The tumor–neuron interactions that drive PNI could represent potential targets and biomarkers to improve treatment of gallbladder cancer. In this study, we demonstrated that gallbladder cancer provoked necroptosis of neurons to enable PNI. Gallbladder cancer cells transferred extracellular vesicles (EV) containing O-GlcNAcase (OGA) to neurons, which activated RIPK1-dependent necroptosis. Mechanistically, EV-derived OGA suppressed RIPK1 glycosylation while enhancing its phosphorylation, thereby activating the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL axis to trigger neuronal necroptosis. Subsequent neuronal release of HMGB1 engaged RAGE on gallbladder cancer cells, establishing a loop that accelerated PNI. Moreover, the RAGE antagonist FPS-ZM1 synergized with gemcitabine to suppress tumor progression. Collectively, these findings uncover an EV-mediated cross-talk between gallbladder cancer cells and neurons in which RIPK1-dependent necroptosis and its effector HMGB1 drive PNI, positioning the HMGB1–RAGE axis as a tractable therapeutic target.

## Significance:
Tumor-derived extracellular vesicles trigger neuronal necroptosis that fuels peripheral nerve invasion, creating a tumor-neuron signaling loop that could be leveraged for liquid biopsy and personalized therapy strategies in neurotropic cancers.

## Introduction
Gallbladder cancer is a highly aggressive malignancy of the digestive system, characterized by its propensity early metastasis and high recurrence rates, which contributes to its poor prognosis with a 5-year survival rate ranging from 5% to 10% ( – ). Perineural invasion (PNI), a critical factor affecting surgical outcomes, is observed in up to 71% of gallbladder cancer cases, with its incidence escalating to 96% when the tumor extends into the extrahepatic bile duct ( – ). PNI is a significant adverse prognostic factor in advanced stages of gallbladder cancer, yet the precise mechanisms underlying the PNI by gallbladder cancer remain elusive ( – ).
The concept that PNI involves “active invasion by cancer cells rather than passive diffusion” has garnered considerable attention ( – ). Tumor–neuron interactions, particularly those mediated by extracellular vesicles (EV), are highlighted as one of the central aspects to understand tumor development and potential therapeutic intervention ( – ). PNI is regulated by dynamic signaling between tumor cells and nerve fibers, encompassing molecular, cellular, and metabolic mechanisms ( – ). However, the cellular and molecular dynamics involved in tumor–neuron interactions, especially those mediated by EVs, are still not well understood.
In the present study, molecular maps of the neural microenvironment in patients with PNI-positive and PNI-negative gallbladder cancer were constructed using laser microdissection and high-sensitivity mass spectrometry. By analyzing protein changes in neurons within PNI regions, key alterations in nerves were identified after invasion. Notably, we found a significant increase in O-GlcNAcase (OGA) expression within invaded nerves. Clinical cohort analysis confirmed that elevated OGA expression in neurons is associated with both PNI intensity and overall survival (OS). To further investigate the role of EVs in tumor–neuron interactions, we established a noncontact coculture system of gallbladder cancer cells and neurons using standardized EV extraction and characterization techniques, as well as untargeted metabolomics. Our findings suggested that gallbladder cancer cells transfer OGA to neurons via the EV pathway. Subsequent experiments, including metabolic drug screening, real-time cell death kinetic assays, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assays, demonstrated that EV-derived OGA induces RIPK1-dependent necroptosis in neurons. Coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP), point mutation, and glycosylation substrate complementation experiments revealed that OGA promotes RIPK1 phosphorylation while inhibiting its glycosylation, thereby activating the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL (complex IIb) pathway. The function of RIPK1-dependent necroptosis in the remodeling of the neural microenvironment was confirmed using dorsal root ganglion (DRG) three-dimensional (3D) coculture models, nude mouse sciatic nerve invasion models, and adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated neuronal RIPK1 knockdown models. Finally, we revealed the molecular pathway by which HMGB1, released from necroptotic neurons, enhances gallbladder cancer invasion via the RAGE receptor. In summary, our study demonstrates that gallbladder cancer cells transfer OGA to neurons through the exosomal pathway. By inhibiting RIPK1 glycosylation and promoting its phosphorylation, OGA induces necroptosis in neurons, causing neuronal rupture and subsequent HMGB1 release. HMGB1 activates RAGE receptors on gallbladder cancer cells, thereby promoting neurotropic invasion. Additionally, FPS-ZM1, a high-affinity RAGE receptor inhibitor, exhibited synergistic effect with gemcitabine, enhancing anticancer efficacy against gallbladder cancer.

## Materials and Methods

## Patient enrollment and sample collection
This study retrospectively analyzed 66 patients with gallbladder cancer undergoing surgical resection at Xinhua Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, from January 2013 to December 2021. Studies were conducted in accordance with International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects (Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences). Patients who received preoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy were excluded from the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee of Xinhua Hospital (No. XHEC-C-2021-095-2). All collected tumor specimens underwent fixation in 10% neutral buffered formalin and were then embedded in paraffin using established laboratory methods. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were prepared and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining by Servicebio Inc. The H&E staining procedure was performed as follows: sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 to 8 minutes, rinsed in tap water, differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid ethanol, and returned to blue. Subsequently, eosin was then applied as a counterstain for 1 to 2 minutes, with the procedure concluding through dehydration in graded ethanol, xylene clearing, and mounting in neutral resin. Microscopic examination was performed using a Leica DMi8 upright optical microscope. Images were captured under 10×, 20×, and 40× objectives using a camera integrated with the microscope system. All images were saved in Tagged Image File Format for subsequent analysis. The criterion for PNI was tumor cell involvement of ≥33% of the nerve perimeter or confirmed invasion into the nerve sheath layers (epineurium, perineurium, and endoneurium). PNI severity was assessed based on two parameters: the extent and frequency of neural invasion. The invasion extent was scored as follows: 0 (none), 1 (perineural type, tumor encircling ≥33% of the nerve), and 2 (intraneural type, tumor infiltrating the nerve sheath). Frequency was scored as follows: 0 (none), 1 (occasional), 2 (frequent), and 3 (highly frequent). A composite PNI score was derived from the product of invasion extent and frequency. Disease severity was then graded as mild (score of 2), moderate (score of 3), or severe (score of 4 or higher) based on this calculation. For subsequent analysis, PNI-positive cases were defined as those with a score of 2 to 5, and PNI-negative cases as those with a score of 0 to 1. Two independent, experienced pathologists, blinded to clinical data, performed all histologic evaluations. Any discrepant assessments were adjudicated by a third senior pathologist to establish a final consensus.

## Clinical feature analysis
Based on postoperative H&E staining, PNI status was assessed, and patients were categorized into PNI-positive (n = 39) and PNI-negative (n = 27) groups. Subsequent to this analysis, which compared survival distributions via the Mantel–Cox test between PNI-positive and PNI-negative groups, immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining was performed to further evaluate the prognostic significance of OGA expression in the former. Patients were stratified into low-, medium-, and high-expression subgroups (OGA-low, OGA-medium, and OGA-high groups) based on quantitative scoring metrics such as integrated optical density. OS was assessed as the duration from surgery until death or the last documented follow-up (in months). Kaplan–Meier curves were first plotted to compare OS between patients with and without PNI. Subsequently, a separate analysis was conducted within the PNI-positive cohort to compare OS between the OGA-medium and OGA-high expression groups. In addition to evaluating the proportional hazards assumption via Schoenfeld residual plots, the log-rank test was used to determine the statistical significance of survival differences. HRs and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were computed to estimate relative risk. A multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression model was constructed to determine whether PNI held autonomous prognostic value, when considered alongside other clinical variables, including sex, age, tumor size, histologic grade, tumor–node–metastasis stage, and OGA expression level. HRs, 95% CIs, and P values were reported for each variable. Significant associations were annotated by corresponding P values, and GraphPad Prism (RRID: SCR_002798) was used for analysis and visualization.

## Single-cell transcriptomic analysis
From Xinhua Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, six patients diagnosed with advanced gallbladder cancer were included in the study between July 2023 and January 2024. All six patients underwent neoadjuvant therapy (NAT), leading to significant tumor regression, followed by radical resection. Tissue samples were collected during the surgical resection, encompassing tumors (n = 6). Pathologic examinations were conducted to ascertain the tissue origin. To augment our analysis, we incorporated an additional 11 patients with gallbladder cancer from a previous study, for whom paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues had undergone single-cell RNA sequencing [scRNA-seq, National Omics Data Encyclopedia (NODE) ID: OEP001237]. These patients did not undergo NAT and were assigned into the control group in this study. Marker genes for each cell cluster were identified via the R package COSG (https://github.com/genecell/COSGR/), which applies a cosine similarity–driven algorithm to enable a more precise and scalable comparison of transcriptional profiles between clusters. A default two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for the differential expression analysis, performing a comprehensive comparison of all gene expression profiles across the two datasets. Genes were defined as significantly differentially expressed if they met the following criteria: a Bonferroni-adjusted P value of less than 0.05 and an absolute log-scaled fold change (FC) greater than 0.5. Cells from each cluster were annotated into various cell types according to the reference datasets of SingleR, ScType, and Celltypist. Further refinement of the cell cluster annotations was informed by the panel of cluster-specific, upregulated marker genes identified through differential expression analysis.

## H&E, immunofluorescence, and IHC staining
The tissue samples used in this study included human gallbladder cancer tissues and sciatic nerves from nude mice invaded by tumor cells. All specimens were processed and paraffin-embedded according to the following protocol. Upon harvest, tissues were immediately placed in neutral formalin for fixation at room temperature for a minimum of 24 hours and were subsequently maintained in the same fixative for storage and transport. After fixation, target tissue areas were trimmed with a scalpel in a fume hood to ensure flat surfaces and then placed in dehydration cassettes with corresponding labels for embedding preparation. The dehydration protocol consisted of a graded ethanol series (75% for 4 hours, 85% for 2 hours, and 90% for 2 hours, 95% for 1 hour) followed by two 30-minute incubations in absolute ethanol. Subsequent transitions included a 5- to 10-minute alcohol–xylene step and two final 5- to 10-minute xylene washes. Samples were then transferred to 65°C paraffin wax for three rounds of infiltration, each lasting 1 hour. Following dehydration, tissues were embedded in paraffin using an automated embedding station. The process involved orienting the tissues in molds prefilled with molten paraffin, into which they were swiftly transferred from the dehydration cassettes. Following labeling before paraffin hardening, the embedded blocks were serially cooled at −20°C, demolded, and trimmed. The ribboned sections were processed by floating on a 40°C water bath, mounting onto slides, and baking at 60°C to ensure adhesion and complete wax removal. Completed sections were stored at room temperature for future experiments. H&E staining was performed through a series of steps: deparaffinization, rehydration, and H&E staining according to the specified durations, with intermediary rinsing, differentiation, and bluing, concluding with dehydration, clearing, and final mounting.
To assess the localization of tumor and neuronal markers, CK19 (Abcam, ab76539, RRID: AB_1523469) and TUJ1 (Abcam, ab18207, RRID: AB_444319) were used for dual immunofluorescence (IF) staining, with DAPI applied for nuclear counterstaining. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections from patients with gallbladder cancer or sciatic nerves from tumor-infiltrated nude mice were processed for double staining using primary antibodies derived from different host species. Tissue sections underwent deparaffinization through sequential xylene immersions (three changes, 10 minutes each) and were rehydrated in a graded ethanol series, concluding with a thorough distilled water rinse. Antigen retrieval was then conducted following the primary antibody–specific protocol, with meticulous care to prevent buffer evaporation and tissue desiccation. After cooling to room temperature, the sections were subjected to three 5-minute PBS (pH 7.4) washes on a shaker. Prior to blocking, a hydrophobic barrier was created around the tissue with a  hydrophobic barrier PAP pen. The sections were then incubated in 3% BSA for 30 minutes at room temperature to prevent nonspecific binding. For goat primary antibodies, 10% donkey serum was substituted for blocking. For primary antibody incubation, a mixture of the two antibodies (raised in different species) was applied at optimal dilutions, and slides were kept at 4°C overnight in a humidified box. Subsequently, after thorough washing in PBS (3 × 5 minutes), a mixture of fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies specific to the primary antibody hosts was introduced. After three additional PBS washes after secondary antibody incubation, nuclear staining was performed using DAPI (10 minutes, dark). Autofluorescence was suppressed by a 5-minute treatment with quenching reagent B, followed by a 10-minute running water rinse. Coverslips were applied with antifade mounting medium to complete the preparation, with antibody details cataloged in Supplementary Table S1.
Paraffin-embedded gallbladder cancer tissues and sciatic nerve tissues invaded by gallbladder cancer cells in nude mice were subjected to IHC staining for OGA. Following a standard protocol of dewaxing and ethanol gradient rehydration, heat-induced antigen retrieval was carried out in suitable buffers. Subsequent to cooling and PBS washes, endogenous peroxidases were blocked by the application of 3% hydrogen peroxide for 25 minutes under dark conditions. Nonspecific binding was blocked with 3% BSA for 30 minutes at room temperature. Primary immunolabeling was performed using anti-OGA antibody (1:200, Abcam, ab197389, RRID: AB_3711368) during an overnight incubation at 4°C. Secondary detection was carried out after PBS washes by applying the matching horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody for 50 minutes at room temperature. Signals were developed with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine substrate, monitored under a microscope, and terminated with tap water. After hematoxylin counterstaining with subsequent differentiation and bluing, the sections underwent sequential processing through graded ethanol dehydration, n-butanol and xylene clearing, and mounting in neutral resin. IHC images were acquired under a bright-field microscope with consistent imaging settings across samples. OGA expression levels were quantified using ImageJ (RRID: SCR_003070) by assessing staining intensity and positive staining area. Quartile-based classification was utilized to categorize the samples into three tiers: low, intermediate, and high expression. A dual assessment of all scores was conducted independently by two blinded investigators, with the mean value calculated for subsequent analysis. All statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism software (RRID: SCR_002798).

## Laser-assisted proteomics
A proteomic analysis was conducted on gallbladder cancer tissues resected at Xinhua Hospital during the 2023 to 2025 timeframe to characterize the protein expression signatures correlated with PNI. All enrolled cases were histopathologically confirmed as gallbladder cancer. Furthermore, no patient had a history of neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy before the surgical procedure. In each sample, both PNI-positive and PNI-negative regions were identified for analysis. All tissues were snap-frozen and sectioned into consecutive 10-μm-thick cryosections. H&E staining, IF, and IHC were performed on adjacent sections to facilitate spatial localization and morphologic confirmation. PNI-positive and PNI-negative regions were manually annotated based on the stained images. Laser microdissection was used to precisely isolate the targeted regions of interest. The microdissected tissues were lysed, and proteins were extracted and digested with trypsin. Prior to LC/MS-MS analysis, the resulting peptides were concentrated via nanofiltration. Protein identification and quantification were achieved by processing the raw spectral data through MaxQuant (RRID: SCR_014485) and searching against the UniProt human protein database. Statistical comparisons of normalized protein expression data between PNI-positive and PNI-negative groups were performed in GraphPad Prism using unpaired two-tailed t tests (for two groups) or one-way ANOVA with the Tukey post hoc test (for multiple groups). Values are shown as the mean ± SD, and a P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For each protein, FC values were calculated, and statistical significance was determined. A volcano plot was constructed in GraphPad Prism to visualize differentially expressed proteins, with the vertical axis (y-axis) indicating the log2 FC and the horizontal axis (x-axis) showing the protein ranking. Applying the significance thresholds of P < 0.05 and an absolute FC greater than 2, we identified differentially expressed proteins, among which, OGA was selected for subsequent analysis.

## Noncontact coculture system of neuron and gallbladder cancer cells
All cell-based assays were conducted using an EV-free FBS culture system, specifically designed to avoid confounding effects from serum-derived vesicles. The EV-free FBS used (MeilunBio, PWL218) was prepared by ultracentrifugation (>100,000 × g, 4°C, 10 hours) and 0.22 μm filtration to remove EV contamination. Before any treatment or experimental manipulation, all cell lines were cultured in EV-free medium for at least 24 hours to eliminate any residual vesicles that may have been adsorbed or internalized under conventional culture conditions, thereby ensuring the reliability and reproducibility of EV extraction and subsequent functional assays. Human neurons were obtained by a two-step division method using the SH-SY5Y differentiation method of Shipley and colleagues ( ). This study utilized two human gallbladder cancer cell lines: NOZ from the Health Sciences Research Resources Bank, Japan, and GBC-SD from the Shanghai Institute for Biological Sciences, China. All cells were cultured under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2) in medium containing 10% EV-free FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Neurons were cultured according to the conditioned medium (CM) used in the culture phase. To decipher how gallbladder cancer–derived EVs in neuron–tumor interactions, a series of in vitro coculture models were established. In the basic coculture model, gallbladder cancer cells and primary neurons were seeded at a 1:1 ratio in a Transwell apparatus configured with 0.4-μm pores, placing tumor cells on the insert membrane and neurons in the lower chamber, respectively. This setup ensured that there was no direct cell–cell contact, allowing only indirect interaction via soluble factors or vesicles. After 48 hours of coculture, downstream functional analyses were conducted.
An EV-supplemented coculture model was implemented by introducing exogenous gallbladder cancer cell–derived EVs into the neuronal chamber of the established coculture system. Without explicit labeling, the EVs added into the cells were 1 × 106 particles/mL, and the treatment time was 48 hours. In the EV direct treatment model, neurons were cultured alone and treated directly with gallbladder cancer–derived EVs for 48 hours to evaluate the direct impact of tumor-derived EVs on neuronal function. To establish the gallbladder cancer–CM treatment model, culture supernatant was harvested from gallbladder cancer cells following a 48-hour incubation. The supernatant was then subjected to sequential centrifugation at 300 × g (10 minutes) and 2,000 × g (20 minutes) to eliminate cellular debris. Subsequently, the resulting CM, enriched in tumor-secreted factors, was then applied to neurons to assess the effect of tumor secretome on neuronal status. In the neuron-CM (CM-N) feedback model, neurons were first treated with gallbladder cancer–derived EVs for 48 hours. Following collection, the CM-N was repurposed to culture gallbladder cancer cells, thereby assessing the feedback regulatory influence of EV-modulated neuronal secretions on tumor behavior. All incubations were performed under standard conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). The endogenous protein synthesis in neurons was inhibited by pretreatment with cycloheximide at 20 μg/mL for 2 hours. To ensure comparability, consistent EV dosages and incubation times were applied consistently to every group.

## EV isolation and characterization
EVs were isolated from cell culture supernatants using a sequential centrifugation protocol designed to eliminate cells, debris, and large particles ( ). To isolate EVs, cell culture medium from exogenous vesicle-free systems was processed through a sequential centrifugation protocol. An initial step at 3,000 × g for 10 minutes (repeated once) removed cells and debris. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 30 minutes to pellet large vesicles and apoptotic bodies. The resulting supernatant was used for EV enrichment via two cycles of high-speed centrifugation (12,000 × g, 60 minutes each) in PBS. All steps were conducted at 4°C.
To visualize the ultrastructure of gallbladder cancer–derived EVs, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed. The EV suspension (10 μL) was deposited onto carbon-coated copper grids and left undisturbed for 1 minute to facilitate particle adhesion. The grids were then gently blotted with filter paper to remove unbound solution. Next, 10 μL of 2% uranyl acetate was added to the grids for negative staining for 1 minute, followed by gentle drying with filter paper. To visualize typical EV morphology, the air-dried grids were subjected to TEM analysis at an acceleration voltage of 100 kV, which enabled the identification of the characteristic round or cup-shaped structures.
EV size distribution and concentration were quantified using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). A 10 μL sample was appropriately diluted in sterile PBS (Sangon Biotech, E607008), ensuring that the final concentration fell within the instrument’s linear detection range and prevented needle clogging. The instrument was calibrated with standard particles prior to use to ensure performance consistency. After sample measurement, the system automatically generated output including average particle diameter, particle concentration (particles/mL), and size distribution histograms. All NTA procedures were conducted by EObiotech.
To validate the identity of isolated EVs, Western blot (WB) analysis was performed to detect classic EV marker proteins. The protein concentration of EV lysates (with protease inhibitors added to RIPA buffer) was determined by bicinchoninic acid (BCA). After SDS-PAGE separation and polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane transfer, blocking was done with 5% nonfat milk. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against EV markers, including CD63 (Abcam, ab134045, RRID: AB_2800495), TSG101 (Abcam, ab133586, RRID: AB_2943043), OGA (Abcam, ab197389, RRID: AB_3711368), GM130 (Abcam, ab169276, RRID: ab169276), and calnexin (Abcam, ab92573, RRID: AB_10563673). The next day, membranes were washed and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents and imaged using a chemiluminescence detection system.
To track neuronal uptake of gallbladder cancer–derived EVs, the EVs were labeled with the green lipophilic dye DiO (Invitrogen, V22886). A 1 mmol/L DiO stock solution was diluted to 5 μmol/L and incubated with EVs in the dark at room temperature or 37°C for 10 to 20 minutes. Unincorporated dye was removed through either ultrafiltration and ultracentrifugation (100,000 × g, 70 minutes), followed by multiple PBS washes and final resuspension to obtain purified DiO EVs. Primary neurons were seeded in six-well plates and cultured to 70% to 80% confluence. For uptake experiments, DiO-labeled EVs were incubated with cells for 4 hours at 37°C. Blank controls and unlabeled EV controls were included in parallel. After incubation, red fluorescence signals within neurons were observed under a fluorescence microscope to assess EV uptake efficiency.

## WB analysis
Total protein was extracted from cells or EVs using RIPA lysis buffer (Solarbio, R0010) containing phenylmethylsulfonylfluoride (1:100, Sangon Biotech, A610425) and Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Sangon Biotech, C600386), with concentrations determined by BCA assay (Beyotime, P0011). For immunoblotting, equal protein loads (20–40 μg) were resolved by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, ISEQ00010), and blocked with 5% nonfat milk. Membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies (listed in supplemental information) at 4°C, followed by TBST washes and 1-hour incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000, Beyotime A0208 and A0216) at room temperature. Blots were developed with enhanced chemiluminescence reagent and visualized using a GE600 imaging system. For EV protein detection, vesicle samples were isolated by ultracentrifugation, and equal amounts of protein were used for blotting. The expression of specific proteins in EVs was compared with their parent cells to validate enrichment or differential expression. Each WB procedure was carried out in at least three independent replicates. The corresponding antibody specifications are compiled in Supplementary Table S1.

## 
In vitro modeling of tumor invasion toward neurons
Prior to the assay, Transwell insert membranes were coated with a uniform layer of Matrigel matrix (Corning, 354348) and incubated at 37°C for 1 hour to facilitate polymerization. Following experimental treatment, gallbladder cancer cells were harvested, resuspended in serum-free medium, and seeded into the inserts at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well.
To create a chemotactic gradient, cells were seeded in the upper chamber in serum-free medium, whereas the lower chamber contained complete medium supplemented with 10% EV-free FBS. Depending on the experimental design, various treatments were applied to either the upper or lower chamber. These included small-molecule interventions (e.g., specific inhibitors or agonists), EV treatment (e.g., EVs derived from NOZ, GBC-SD, or L-2F7 cells added to the lower chamber), or CM (CM-N or CM-T). After a cell line–optimized invasion period (≥24 hours), the membranes were processed by removing noninvading cells from the upper side with a swab. Cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and quantified by counting five random microscopic fields per membrane. Invasion was calculated as the mean cell count per field multiplied by 100, with triplicates per group. Drug information is listed in Supplementary Table S2.

## DRG and tumor coculture assay
DRGs were isolated from neonatal BALB/c nude mice after euthanasia by CO2 inhalation. To prevent damage to nerve roots, cervical dislocation was not sed. All procedures from this point onward were conducted under sterile conditions. After thorough disinfection of the skin with 70% ethanol and air-drying, mice were placed in the prone position on a sterile dissection board. Using sterile scissors, a midline incision was created along the spine to reveal the underlying vertebral column. Additionally, the ribs on both sides were removed, and the entire spine was excised and rinsed twice in cold PBS. Under a stereomicroscope, the spine was placed on sterile Telfa pads or gauze, and surrounding muscle and connective tissue were removed. The vertebral lamina was cut along the spinal axis and spread open to expose the spinal cord and nerve roots. DRGs were identified as pale yellow, spherical structures. The distal intercostal nerves were cut, leaving 2 to 3 mm of the nerve stump to facilitate handling. The dorsal and ventral roots were then cut sequentially to isolate intact DRGs. Isolated ganglia were transferred into prechilled DMEM containing 10% EV-free FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Corning, 30-002-Cl), 1% sodium pyruvate (Gibco, 11360070), and 1% nonessential amino acids (Gibco, 11140050). For DRG embedding and culture, all procedures were conducted on ice using precooled pipette tips. To create a defined substrate, a 20 μL aliquot of growth factor–reduced Matrigel was deposited onto the center of a 35-mm glass-bottom dish, forming a gel spot of approximately 1 mm. A single DRG was carefully placed at the center of the extracellular matrix (ECM) using a stereomicroscope, ensuring close contact with the dish surface. After gelling at 37°C for 10 to 15 minutes in a sterile laminar flow hood, 2 mL of prewarmed neuronal culture medium (Neurobasal-A + B27 + L-glutamine + NGF at 50 ng/mL) was gently added. Under standard incubation conditions (37°C, 5% CO2), medium changes followed a regimen of replacement on day 1 and every second day afterward. A dense neurite network was established after a 48-hour culture period prior to coculture initiation. Tumor cells (NOZ and GBC-SD cells) were first maintained in EV-free FBS medium for at least 24 hours to clear residual exogenous EVs. Subsequently, 20,000 pretreated tumor cells were washed with PBS and prepared in 40 μL of ECM suspension for coculture. Using microscopic guidance, aliquots (1–2 μL) of the cell–ECM suspension were deposited at several sites surrounding the DRG within a 500 μm radius, thereby establishing a spatially defined, noncontact coculture system. After gel solidification, prewarmed medium (neuronal medium or mixed neuro-tumor medium) was added, and the coculture was maintained for 24 to 72 hours to observe neurite guidance, chemotactic responses, and neuroplasticity. To assess migratory potential along nerve fibers, a nerve invasion index was derived from the ratio of outer to inner region tumor cell counts, indicative of distal migration tendency. Quantification involved independent evaluation by two blinded observers, with mean values calculated for subsequent analysis.

## Establishment of the perineural invasion mouse model by sciatic nerve injection
To recapitulate gallbladder cancer PNI in an in vivo setting, we established a mouse model via perisciatic injection of tumor cells into immunodeficient mice. Female BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks old) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. The study was conducted in compliance with ethical guidelines for animal research under the approval of Xinhua Hospital’s Laboratory Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee (XHEC-F-2025-054). Nude mice were acclimatized for 7 days before random assignment to experimental groups, with sample sizes determined based on prior research and established laboratory protocols. NOZ cells were enzymatically detached and suspended in an ice-cold 1:1 solution of serum-free PBS and Matrigel, achieving a final concentration of 1 × 105 cells per 10 μL. Surgical procedures were performed under isoflurane anesthesia, with mice positioned in lateral recumbency. The right hind limb was prepared by shaving and aseptic preparation, followed by a 1.5- to 2-cm incision along the lateral hip. Blunt dissection through muscular layers revealed the sciatic nerve. Under microscopic visualization, 25 μL of tumor cell suspension was precisely administered into the perineural space adjacent to the sciatic nerve using a Hamilton microsyringe (10 μL capacity, model 80330), with careful attention to preserving nerve integrity. Wound closure was achieved through layered suturing of muscle and skin tissues. Postoperative management consisted of antimicrobial prophylaxis with ceftezole (20 mg/kg, intramuscular) and analgesic support using buprenorphine as clinically indicated. Mice were monitored daily for general condition, motor function, and hind limb paralysis score. Neurologic deficits associated with PNI typically appeared within 14 days after injection. At the endpoint, mice were euthanized, and the injected sciatic nerve and surrounding tissues were collected for histologic and molecular analysis. Histologic analysis was performed on 4% PFA-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections stained with H&E to examine neural architecture disruption and tumor infiltration.

## siRNA-mediated gene silencing
To transiently knock down the expression of OGA or RIPK1 in cells, siRNA transfection was performed to induce gene-specific silencing. Cells were plated in either six-well (Corning, 3516) or 12-well (Corning, 3513) culture plates, and transfection procedures were commenced upon reaching 50% to 60% confluence. All transfection experiments were performed using antibiotic-free medium containing 10% EV-depleted FBS to eliminate potential confounding effects from external nucleic acids or vesicular contaminants. Human OGA and RIPK1 siRNAs were synthesized by OBiO Technology Corp., Ltd. Multiple siRNA sequences targeting each gene were screened to identify effective silencing candidates. For control purposes, a scrambled siRNA sequence was used as the negative control.
Gene silencing transfections were performed with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent according to the manufacturer’s specifications. siRNA oligonucleotides were prepared at 30 nmol/L in Opti-MEM medium and complexed with the transfection reagent through a 15-minute incubation at ambient temperature. These complexes were then applied directly to cultured cells. Following 6 hours of exposure, the transfection medium was replaced with complete medium supplemented with 10% EV-free FBS, and cells were cultured until the predetermined endpoints. Cellular samples were collected 48 to 72 hours after transfection for subsequent examination. Knockdown efficiency was verified through immunoblotting assessment of OGA and RIPK1 protein expression. All transfection assays included a minimum of three independent biological replicates to ensure experimental reproducibility and statistical robustness.

## Untargeted metabolomic profiling of neurons after EV treatment
To investigate metabolic alterations induced by EVs, neurons were treated with approximately 3 × 106 particles/mL of EVs for 24 hours. For intracellular metabolite extraction, cells were washed with warm PBS after treatment, followed by the addition of 800 μL ice-cold methanol/acetonitrile (1:1) for protein precipitation. After vortexing and centrifugation (14,000 × g, 20 minutes), the supernatant was vacuum-dried, reconstituted in acetonitrile/water (1:1), and centrifuged again. The final extract was analyzed by LC/MS. For quality control, a pooled sample from each experimental aliquot was injected after every five runs to ensure system stability. Metabolomic analysis was carried out by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. using ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (Vanquish UHPLC system, Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with high-resolution mass spectrometry (Orbitrap Exploris 480). Separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm; Waters) with a binary mobile phase consisting of (A) 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and ammonium hydroxide in water and (B) acetonitrile. The gradient program was as follows: 95% B (0.5 minutes), 95%→65% B (6.5 minutes), 65%→40% B (1 minute), and 40% B (1 minute), followed by rapid re-equilibration. MS detection was performed in both positive and negative electrospray ionization modes under the following source conditions: sheath gas 50, auxiliary gas 2, capillary temperature 350°C, and spray voltages of +3.5 kV (positive) and –2.8 kV (negative). Full scan spectra (m/z 70–1,200) were acquired at 60,000 resolution, with data-dependent MS-MS acquisition incorporating a 4-second dynamic exclusion window. Raw data were processed and normalized to the total ion current. Multivariate statistical approaches were used to analyze the metabolomic data, incorporating both principal component analysis and orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis with Pareto scaling. Model validation was conducted through 7-fold cross-validation and permutation tests. Metabolites meeting the criteria of variable importance in projection scores exceeding 1.0 and statistical significance (P < 0.05, determined by Student t test) were identified as differentially abundant.

## Cell Counting Kit-8 assay 
Neuronal viability was determined by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay (MedChemExpress, HY-K0301) after NOZ-EV treatment. Following exposure, 10 μL CCK8 was added to 100 μL medium in 96-well plates. After 2-hour incubation in the dark at 37°C, absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Triplicate data were normalized to control and analyzed by one-way ANOVA (significant at P < 0.05).

## Compound library screening for cancer metabolic modulators
To identify potential small-molecule compounds that could reverse EV-induced neuronal metabolic alterations, a functional rescue screening was performed using a cancer metabolism–focused compound library (L5700, TargetMol). Neurons were cultured under standard conditions, and once 70% to 80% confluence was reached, they were treated with NOZ-derived EVs for 24 hours to mimic the tumor-derived EV intervention. The L5700 compound library comprises 339 small molecules targeting key metabolic pathways, including glycolysis, hexosamine biosynthetic pathway, oxidative phosphorylation, and amino acid metabolism. All test compounds were initially aliquoted into 96-well plates in DMSO solution and administered at 10 μmol/L final concentration. Following EV pretreatment, neuronal cells were plated into the compound-containing wells (100 μL medium/well) and cocultured for 24 hours. Two control groups were included: untreated neurons and EV-treated neurons with DMSO only. Cell viability was used as the screening readout.

## Primary bone marrow–derived macrophage isolation
Primary bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDM) were harvested from 6- to 8-week-old healthy male C57BL/6J mice (RRID: IMSR_JAX:000664). After CO2 anesthesia and cervical dislocation, femurs and tibiae were aseptically dissected for subsequent cell isolation. The bone marrow cavities were opened at both ends, and BMDMs were flushed out using PBS supplemented with 2% FBS through a syringe. Following collection, the flushed suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 minutes. The resulting pellet was resuspended, filtered to eliminate tissue debris, and counted. Cells were then seeded in nonadherent dishes at 1 × 106 cells/mL in complete DMEM with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL recombinant mouse macrophage colony–stimulating factor (M-CSF) to drive macrophage differentiation. Cells were cultured at 37°C under 5% CO2 with periodic medium replacement (50% volume every 48 hours). Following 7 days of M-CSF induction, the adherent population exhibited characteristic macrophage morphology, including polygonal cellular architecture. Differentiated macrophages were collected on day 7 for downstream applications.

## Real-time imaging of neuronal and BMDM cell death using SYTOX Green
Neurons were cultured following standard protocols, including untreated and genetically modified (e.g., site-mutated or siRNA-transfected) groups. Cells were plated in 96-well plates at densities ranging from 6,000 to 10,000 cells per well (Corning, 3596). After stabilization, cells were subjected to various treatments, such as EV exposure (e.g., from gallbladder cancer cells), pharmacologic intervention [e.g., necrostatin-1 (Nec-1), glucosamine, or OGA inhibitor thiamet G (TMG)], and appropriate negative (PBS or DMSO) and positive controls (Triton X-100). SYTOX Green dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific, S7020), which penetrates only membrane-compromised cells and fluoresces upon nucleic acid binding, was added at 1:10 dilution. Readings were acquired with a BioTek Synergy H1 instrument housed within an environmentally controlled chamber providing precise regulation of temperature and CO2 levels. Time-lapse images were acquired every 30 minutes for 24 hours. Cell death was quantified based on SYTOX-positive nuclei counts or fluorescence intensity, expressed as either “number of dead cells per field” or “percentage of dead cells.” Six biological replicates were used per group. The results were presented as the mean ± SD and used for statistical comparisons. For BMDM cell death monitoring, the same protocol was applied. To construct RIPK1-dependent and -independent cell death models, the following stimuli were used: TNFα (10 ng/mL) + Smac mimetic BV6 (2 μmol/L; ref. ); TNFα + 5Z-7-oxozeaenol (200 nmol/L; ref. ); TNFα + BV6 + z-VAD-FMK Z-VAD-FMK (ZVAD; 20 μmol/L; ref. ); TNFα + 5Z-7-oxozeaenol + z-VAD-FMK; lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 200 ng/mL) + 5Z-7-oxozeaenol ( ); Yersinia [multiplicity of infection (MOI = 40; ref. )]; Salmonella (MOI = 100; ref. ); and LPS + nigericin (10 μmol/L; ref. ). For metabolic starvation models, glucose-free media were used 5 hours prior to stimulation. Pretreatments (e.g., TNFα, LPS, and TMG) were administered 1 hour before costimulation. Information on drugs was provided in Supplementary Table S3.

## LDH release assay
To assess membrane integrity and cell damage in neurons and BMDMs, LDH release was quantified using a commercial LDH Assay Kit (MedChemExpress, HY-K1090). For experimental analysis, cells were plated in 96-well plates at densities of 6,000 to 10,000 cells per well and exposed to different treatment conditions. These interventions included EVs, pharmaceutical compounds (such as Nec-1, TMG, and GlcN), siRNA-mediated gene silencing, or coculture with tumor cells. Controls included untreated wells and a positive lysis control (Triton X-100) representing maximal LDH release. Following treatment, plates were centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 minutes, and 50 μL aliquots of supernatant were transferred to fresh plates. An equal volume of LDH reaction solution was added, mixed, and incubated protected from light for 30 minutes at room temperature. The reaction was terminated with stop solution (50 μL/well), and absorbance was read at 490 nm. Background absorbance (media only) was subtracted. LDH release was calculated using the following formula: LDH release (%) = [(OD490 sample–OD490 background)/(OD490 max–OD490 background)] × 100. All experimental data represent three or more independent replicates and are presented as the mean ± SD.

## Structural modeling and docking analysis
To explore potential interactions between OGA and RIPK1, protein–protein docking and structural modeling were performed. The 3D structures of OGA and RIPK1 were sourced primarily from the RCSB Protein Data Bank, supplemented by predictions from the AlphaFold Database in the absence of high-resolution experimental structures. Structures were preprocessed using PyMOL (RRID: SCR_000305) or UCSF ChimeraX (RRID: SCR_015872), including chain renaming, residue renumbering, and removal of nonprotein molecules (e.g., ligands, ions, and water). Proteins were color-coded, and preliminary interface mapping was performed. Interface analysis was conducted using PDBePISA and PDBsum to identify interface area, hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic contacts. Protein–protein docking was performed using HADDOCK 2.4 (RRID: SCR_019091) and ClusPro online servers (RRID: SCR_018248), with active residues defined based on interface prediction. The best-scoring complexes (based on lowest binding energy and best clustering score) were visualized in PyMOL. These in silico findings provided a theoretical basis for subsequent experimental validation.

## Construction of RIPK1 point mutants
RIPK1 point mutants at serine residues S166A, S331A, S440A, and S669A were generated by overlapping PCR mutagenesis. The human RIPK1 open reading frame was used as the template. Mutation-specific primers containing site-directed base substitutions with 15 to 20 bp complementary overlaps were designed for each site. Site-directed mutagenesis of RIPK1 was performed by two-step overlapping PCR. The fused product was digested and inserted into pcDNA3.1-FLAG, followed by DH5α transformation and Sanger sequencing confirmation of successful mutation. The validated constructs (S166A, S331A, S440A, and S669A) were subsequently used for transfection and functional assays.

## Co-IP assay
To empirically validate the physical association between OGA and RIPK1 (including its mutant variants), co-IP was conducted. ## Results

## Elevated neuronal OGA expression in gallbladder cancer PNI
Initially, a cohort of 39 PNI-positive patients and 27 PNI-negative patients with gallbladder cancer was categorized using H&E staining. In PNI-positive patients, tumor cells were observed to invade the perineural space or nerve sheath ( ). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that PNI-positive patients had a significantly lower 5-year survival rate compared with PNI-negative patients (OR: 2.36–7.03, P < 0.001; ). Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified tumor size ≥3 cm, high/moderate grade, stages III to IV, and PNI positivity as independent factors for unfavorable prognosis (P < 0.05; ). Notably, single-cell sequencing of patients with gallbladder cancer showed more robust communication between tumor cells and neurons than with Schwann cells ( ; Supplementary Fig. S1A). Subsequently, tumor tissues from 11 PNI-positive and 11 PNI-negative patients were selected to construct molecular maps of the neural microenvironment via laser microdissection and high-sensitivity mass spectrometry ( ; Supplementary Fig. S1B). Proteomic analysis revealed a significant increase in OGA expression in PNI regions ( and ). The tumor-surrounding PNI region was confirmed by staining for the gallbladder cancer marker CK19 and the neural marker TUJ1. IF analysis showed CK19-positive tumor cells were clustered around TUJ1-positive neural markers ( ). IHC combined with IF demonstrated a marked increase in neuronal OGA expression within PNI regions across 11 patients (P < 0.001, ). Patients were stratified based on OGA expression in PNI-positive cases. Among them, 34 patients (87.18%) exhibited medium-to-high OGA expression in neural regions, whereas 5 (12.82%) patients displayed low expression. All PNI-positive patients showed elevated OGA expression in neural areas ( ). These results indicated that PNI-positive patients with high OGA expression had a poorer prognosis ( ). Additionally, the extent of neural invasion was graded, revealing a direct correlation between the degree of PNI and the proportion of neurons with high OGA expression ( ).
 

## Gallbladder cancer–derived EVs mediate neuronal OGA upregulation
To investigate the mechanisms underlying OGA overexpression in neurons, gallbladder cancer cells were cocultured with neurons ( ). In a noncontact coculture system, neuronal OGA levels were elevated following coculture or treatment with CM from NOZ cells (CM-T). This increase was attenuated when gallbladder cancer cells were pretreated with the EV inhibitor GW4869 ( ). Coculture also enhanced the neurotropic invasion of gallbladder cancer cells toward neurons, whereas neuronal cell supernatant alone had no effect on gallbladder cancer cell invasion. Inhibition of gallbladder cancer–derived EVs significantly reduced the neural invasion capacity of gallbladder cancer cells ( ). Given the fact that OGA is an intracellular protein rather than a secreted one, these findings suggested that the coculture medium might facilitate these changes through the EV pathway. To explore this hypothesis, EVs and other supernatants containing cytokines and metabolites were isolated from the culture media of two gallbladder cancer cell lines (NOZ and GBC-SD) using ultracentrifugation ( ). NTA and TEM confirmed successful isolation and characterization of gallbladder cancer–derived EVs. TEM images revealed disc-shaped or cup-shaped EVs, and NTA quantification indicated that the size of gallbladder cancer–derived EVs were primarily ranged from 50 to 65 nm ( ). WB analysis confirmed OGA cargo within EVs ( ). To further test the hypothesis that neuronal OGA upregulation during PNI was mediated by gallbladder cancer–derived EVs, DiO-labeled EVs were coincubated with neurons to assess the delivery of tumor-derived EV delivery ( ). After supplementation of neurons with gallbladder cancer–derived EVs, the increase in neuronal OGA was found to correlate with the quantity of EVs ( ). Additionally, adding varying concentrations of EVs to the coculture system enhanced the invasive potential of gallbladder cancer cells ( ). To validate that the increase in neuronal OGA was mediated by gallbladder cancer–derived EVs and assess changes in tumor invasiveness following coculture, OGA was knocked down in NOZ and GBC-SD cell lines, and EVs were extracted for OGA content analysis ( and ). The siRNA targeting OGA (siOGA-2) demonstrated the highest knockdown efficiency. Despite the knockdown, tumor cell invasiveness remained unaffected ( ). WB analysis revealed that CM from siOGA-transfected tumor cells did not deliver OGA to neurons ( ). Although EVs from control gallbladder cancer cells increased neuronal OGA, EVs derived from siOGA-transfected cells did not ( ). The neurotropic invasion of tumor cells was reduced in the absence of OGA in EVs ( ). These results collectively demonstrate that gallbladder cancer–derived EVs carrying OGA promote neurotropic invasion by transferring OGA to neurons.
 

## Gallbladder cancer EVs package OGA to promote PNI
As in vitro coculture experiments have shown that tumor-derived EVs carrying OGA enhance the neurotropism of gallbladder cancer, we hypothesized that gallbladder cancer cells elevated neuronal OGA levels via EVs, thereby facilitating neurotropic invasion. This hypothesis was further validated using both in vitro and in vivo models. A 3D coculture model was used to evaluate how OGA within gallbladder cancer–derived EVs affects tumor cell invasion of DRG ( ). Addition of EVs significantly enhanced the invasion of gallbladder cancer cell into DRG, although this effect was abolished when EVs lacked OGA. Reintroducing OGA via EVs rescued the invasive capacity of OGA-deficient tumor cells ( and ). Given the high protein homology of OGA between humans and mice (Supplementary Fig. S2), nude mouse sciatic nerve invasion models were utilized to further substantiate these findings. NOZ cells and OGA-deficient NOZ cells were injected into the sciatic nerve, followed by EV intervention ( ). Sciatic nerve function index (SFI) revealed that supplementation with intact, OGA-containing EVs exacerbated tumor invasion of the sciatic nerve, whereas EVs lacking OGA failed to do so. Knockdown of OGA in the tumor cells attenuated the decline in the SFI, and this decline was restored by reintroducing functional OGA via EVs ( ). Supplementation with either of the two OGA-deficient EV populations, derived from OGA-knockdown tumor cells or from normal gallbladder epithelial cells, elicited no statistically significant change in the SFI (P > 0.85; ). The length of gallbladder cancer invasion along the sciatic nerve increased upon supplementation with intact tumor-derived EVs, whereas OGA-deficient EVs failed to elicit any such augmentation (P > 0.99; ). Mice treated with intact tumor-derived EVs exhibited more severe hind limb paralysis and markedly reduced paw width, whereas OGA-deficient tumor cells and supplementation with OGA-depleted EVs resulted in only moderate paralysis and intermediate paw width. IF staining showed that, compared with the OGA-deficient tumor group or the OGA-deficient EV group, mice receiving intact tumor-derived EVs displayed more extensive PNI, decreased neuronal marker TUJ1, and increased tumor marker CK19. Importantly, OGA-deficient tumor cells regained their capacity to invade nerves after receiving EV-delivered OGA ( ). Collectively, these in vitro and in vivo data demonstrated that tumor-derived EVs carrying OGA markedly enhance PNI in gallbladder cancer.
 

## Exosomal OGA induces RIPK1-dependent necroptosis in neurons
Protein glycosylation modification is a dynamic and reversible process regulated by OGT (O-GlcNAc transferase) and OGA (O-GlcNAc hydrolase; refs. , ). The hexosamine biosynthesis pathway (HBP) is a critical metabolic pathway responsible for generating UDP-GlcNAc, the core substrate for various glycosylation modifications, including O-GlcNAc modification ( ; ref. ). To confirm that the increase in OGA originated from tumor cell EVs, metabolomics sequencing was performed on neurons treated with EVs (Supplementary Fig. S3A). After treatment with tumor-derived EVs, the overall metabolic flux in the HBP pathway of neurons was reduced (Supplementary Fig. S3B and S3C). Key metabolites, including glucose-6-P, fructose-6-P, glutamine, N-acetylglucosamine, glucosamine-6-P, and UDP-GlcNAc, were significantly decreased, indicating that exogenous OGA inhibits glycosylation modification in neurons ( ). Neuronal viability also decreased following treatment with tumor-derived EVs, an effect that could be reversed by TMG ( ). This suggests that the disruption of metabolic balance in neurons by exogenous OGA may contribute to reduced neuronal viability. Following EV-mediated transfer, OGA reduced neuronal O-GlcNAcylation, whereas OGT levels remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. S3D). To further explore the mechanism underlying reduced neuronal viability, a metabolism-related drug library was used to screen for potential treatments following EV treatment ( ). Nec-1, a RIPK1-targeting inhibitor, demonstrated the most significant neutralizing and rescuing effects against the EV-induced reduction in neuronal viability ( ). Given that Nec-1 is an apoptosis-related inhibitor, neuronal cell death following EV treatment was further investigated. Tumor-derived EVs were found to induce neuronal cell death, as confirmed by real-time cell death kinetics assay and LDH release experiments (Supplementary Fig. S4A and S4B). LDH release indicated neuronal membrane damage after exposure to tumor-derived EVs, which decreased after treatment with Nec-1 (Supplementary Fig. S4C–S4F). EV-induced neuronal cell death was observed exclusively in the presence of OGA ( and ). EV-derived OGA induces neuronal cell death primarily through RIPK1 activation, whereas RIPK1 activation lead to the formation of protein complexes IIa and IIb, which mediated apoptosis and necroptosis, respectively ( ). To distinguish between these pathways, cells were treated with the pan-caspase inhibitor ZVAD. ZVAD failed to neutralize or rescue EV-induced neuronal death, and no activation of complex IIa–associated proteins (caspase-8, cleaved caspase-8 p43, and cleaved caspase-8 p18) was observed after EV treatment (Supplementary Fig. S4G and S4H). This suggests that neuronal cell death was primarily mediated through the complex IIb pathway. We next examined the relationship between the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL pathway and complex IIb. After EV treatment, significant activation of this pathway was observed in neurons, and targeted inhibition of RIPK1 in neurons rescued EV-induced neuronal death ( ). EVs activated the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL pathway in a dose-dependent manner, and Nec-1 suppressed this activation ( ). EV-induced phosphorylation of RIPK1 in neurons occurred exclusively in the presence of OGA ( ). Selected inhibition of neuronal OGA neutralized and rescued EV-induced neuronal death, also preventing the activation of the necroptotic complex IIb pathway in neurons ( ; Supplementary Fig. S5A–S5C). Furthermore, supplementing GlcN to neurons directly increased glycosylation levels, decreased RIPK1 phosphorylation, and inhibited complex IIb formation, thereby rescuing neurons from EV-induced death (Supplementary Fig. S5D–S5H). To validate the general role of OGA in regulating RIPK1 phosphorylation, we constructed eight distinct cell death models. TMG was used to directly inhibit intracellular OGA, whereas GlcN was used to modulate intracellular glycosylation levels, thereby indirectly suppress OGA function. This finding confirmed the critical role of OGA in regulating cell survival and death. In cell death models dependent on RIPK1 activation (T/O, T/S, T/S/Z, T/O/Z, LPS/O, and Yersinia infection), treatment with TMG significantly rescued cells from death (Supplementary Figs. S6A–S6F and S7A–S7F). Similarly, supplementation with GlcN or inhibition with Nec-1 also effectively mitigated cell death (Supplementary Figs. S7G–S7L and S8A–S8F). Notably, direct inhibition of intracellular OGA produced the most pronounced rescue effects. Conversely, in cell death models independent of RIPK1 (LPS/nigericin and Salmonella infection), neither direct inhibition of OGA nor GlcN supplementation significantly mitigate cell death (Supplementary Figs. S6G and S6H, S7M–S7P, S8G and S8H). These findings indicated that OGA-mediated deglycosylation of RIPK1 enhanced its phosphorylation, thereby triggering necroptosis via the protein complex IIb pathway ( ).
 

## OGA inhibits glycosylation and promotes phosphorylation of RIPK1
To investigate whether OGA induces necroptosis by inhibiting RIPK1 glycosylation, we utilized the HDOCK software to predict the 3D interaction model of OGA and RIPK1 ( ). The most probable docking regions and relevant amino acid residues were identified ( ) and further visualized in a 2D model ( ). In HEK293T cells, cotransfection with HA-tagged OGA and FLAG-tagged RIPK1 and subsequent IP experiment was performed to confirm their direct interaction ( and ). Cotransfection with FLAG-RIPK1 and HA-OGT demonstrated that OGA significantly reduced RIPK1 O-GlcNAcylation ( ). Moreover, endogenous co-IP analysis in neurons revealed that OGA derived from EVs inhibited RIPK1 glycosylation while promoting its phosphorylation ( ). These findings suggested that EVs derived from gallbladder cancer deliver OGA to neurons, thereby inhibiting the glycosylation of RIPK1. This inhibition subsequently enhanced the phosphorylation and activation of RIPK1, ultimately triggering neuronal necroptosis. In subsequent experiments, knockdown in neurons ( ) effectively abrogated the enhanced invasion of neurons by EVs ( ). Moreover, the induction of neuronal necroptosis by EVs was also prevented ( and ), thereby confirming that OGA-mediated inhibition of RIPK1 glycosylation promoted its phosphorylation and subsequently drives necroptosis in neurons. To establish that downstream RIPK1 signaling depends on phosphorylation following deglycosylation, we introduced the phospho-null S166A mutation into neuronal RIPK1 (Supplementary Fig. S9). Despite gallbladder cancer–derived EVs markedly lowering global neuronal O-GlcNAc levels, mutation of the critical phospho-acceptor site S166 blocked RIPK1 phosphorylation and consequently prevented activation of the RIPK3/MLKL cascade ( ). Considering the high degree of conservation of RIPK1 between humans and mice (Supplementary Fig. S10), AAV9 was utilized to specifically target mouse sciatic nerves, resulting in reduce RIPK1 expression and a subsequent decrease in neuronal necroptosis ( ). This reduction in necroptosis significantly impeded the enhanced neural invasion mediated by EVs ( ). These results demonstrated that targeting RIPK1 to inhibit neuronal necroptosis effectively mitigates PNI.
 

## Ser440 is a critical site of OGA-dependent O-GlcNAc site on RIPK1
To verify that OGA promoted RIPK1 phosphorylation by inhibiting its glycosylation, we first evaluated the glycosylation levels in cells treated with EVs. Neurons treated with EVs containing OGA exhibited reduced O-GlcNAc levels ( ). We then utilized the YinOYang online database to predict potential O-GlcNAc glycosylation sites on RIPK1, which identified Ser331, Ser440, and Ser669 as possible modification sites ( ). Plasmids harboring mutations at these sites were generated, comprising WT RIPK1 (RIPK1WT) and single-point mutants (RIPK1S331A, RIPK1S440A, and RIPK1S669A), with their sequences confirmed via Sanger sequencing ( ). The spatial locations of these glycosylation sites on RIPK1 are depicted in . Notably, compared with RIPK1WT and other mutants, the interaction between RIPK1 and OGA was significantly weakened in the 440A mutant, although it was markedly enhanced in the 331A mutant ( ). Following the 440A mutation, RIPK1 bound OGA less avidly and displayed a corresponding increase in O-GlcNAc signal. However, the 331A mutation had the opposite effect ( ). These findings indicate that glycosylation at Ser 440 was a crucial for the interaction between RIPK1 and OGA. In the RIPK1 structure, the phosphorylation site S166 was positioned almost equidistant to the O-GlcNAcylation sites S331, S440, and S669 ( ). To further elucidate the role of Ser440 in mediating OGA’s inhibition of RIPK1 glycosylation and its subsequent phosphorylation activation, cell death and the complex IIb pathway were examined. In cell death assays, the RIPK1S331A mutation significantly enhanced EV-induced necroptosis ( and ), whereas the 440A mutation attenuated necroptosis ( and ). The 331A mutation enhanced RIPK1 phosphorylation and activated the complex IIb pathway, with the inhibitory effect of Nec-1 on necroptosis being significantly weaker in RIPK1S331A than in RIPK1WT. This effect was not observed with the S440A mutation ( and ). These observations were consistent with the differential effects of OGA on glycosylation following point mutations. Coculture experiments revealed that the RIPK1-S440A mutation counteracted neurotropic invasion ( ). Collectively, these findings underscored the critical role of Ser440 in OGA-mediated inhibition of RIPK1 glycosylation and subsequent promotion of its phosphorylation ( ).
 

## DAMP HMGB1/RAGE axis facilitates PNI in gallbladder cancer
Necroptosis was characterized by plasma membrane rupture and the extracellular release of DAMPs. ELISA quantification of canonical DAMPs revealed that HMGB1 was the most abundant and persistently released factor after EV-induced neuronal necroptosis ( ). Neuronal HMGB1 release exhibited both time- and dose-dependent correlations with EVs. Exposure to EVs evoked a time- and dose-dependent increase in HMGB1 levels in neuronal supernatants ( ). Inhibition of EVs release effectively suppressed HMGB1 secretion, indicating that EVs regulated its export (Supplementary Fig. S11A). To establish HMGB1 as the DAMP mediating neurotropism in gallbladder cancer, we treated cocultured system with the HMGB1 inhibitor NecroX-7. NecroX-7 significantly attenuated the invasion effect of EVs ( ), demonstrating that HMGB1 release promoted neurotropism through receptor engagement on tumor cells.
 To identify the downstream ligands of HMGB1, various inhibitors were tested. The results showed that the RAGE inhibitor FPS-ZM1 significantly reduced the invasion of gallbladder cancer cells, whereas inhibitors of TLR2 and TLR4 had no significant effect ( ). Further validation confirmed RAGE activation in tumor cells ( ). The RAGE–HMGB1 interaction, a well-established receptor–ligand pair, was widely implicated in tumorigenesis. Molecular docking further verified the high affinity between RAGE and HMGB1 ( ). Following EV treatment, RIPK1 phosphorylation increased in neurons, which resulted in the release of HMGB1 as DAMPs and subsequent RAGE activation in tumor cells. Inhibition of necroptosis with the S440A mutation and Nec-1 reduced the release of HMGB1 ( ). RAGE, a surface receptor on tumor cells, was targeted by the high-affinity inhibitor FPS-ZM1 (Supplementary Fig. S11B and S11C). In dual cell line experiments, FPS-ZM1 demonstrated strong synergy with gemcitabine, with HSA values exceeding 10 ( and ; Supplementary Fig. S11D–S11G). The combination of FPS-ZM1 and gemcitabine more effectively inhibited invasion than either agent alone ( ). In a nude mouse model of neural invasion, combination therapy with FPS-ZM1 and gemcitabine provided superior inhibition of invasion and reduced hind limb motor deficits ( ). Serum and pathologic analyses showed no significant organ toxicity from FPS-ZM1 (Supplementary Fig. S11H–S11K).

## Discussion
The concept of tumor as a systemic disease has gained wide acceptance, drawing considerable attention to the specific role of the nervous system in tumor progression. PNI is a common pathologic hallmark in gallbladder cancer, and it is significantly correlated with unfavorable outcomes, such as poor prognosis, high recurrence rates, and low survival rates ( – , , ). The interaction between cancer cells and nerves is crucial to the adverse biological behavior of PNI, which involves the activation or inhibition of various signaling pathways, including chemokines, interleukins, and nerve growth factors ( , , ). These interactions trigger specific alterations within the perineural niche, thereby disrupting normal nerve function and facilitating cancer cell migration, invasion, and adhesion ( ). However, the precise mechanisms underlying PNI in gallbladder cancer are still not completely elucidated. In this study, we utilized laser-capture microdissection coupled with high-sensitivity mass spectrometry to investigate the changes in the perineural niche induced by PNI. OGA was identified as being aberrantly upregulated in neurons following tumor invasion. Using a noncontact coculture system, we showed that tumor-derived EVs deliver OGA to neurons. This EV-delivered OGA deglycosylated RIPK1, thereby promoting its phosphorylation and triggering complex IIb signaling, ultimately leading to neuronal necroptosis. Intriguingly, the necroptotic DAMP HMGB1 fed back on tumoral RAGE receptors to complete a tumor-driven neural invasion loop. Our findings clarify how tumor EVs induce neuronal programmed death and identify a targetable mechanism for new gallbladder cancer therapies.
EVs are nanosized vesicles released by cells that transport various biomolecules and regulate the behavior of recipient cells ( , ). As key mediators of intercellular communication, EVs have emerged as promising candidates in liquid biopsies. Recent research has highlighted the role of EVs in facilitating communication between tumor cells and their surroundings ( – ). The high metabolic activity and signaling requirements of neurons necessitate dynamic posttranslational modifications, with O-GlcNAc modifications, regulated by OGT and OGA, playing a central role in neuronal energy sensing and stress responses ( – ). Neurons maintain high levels of O-GlcNAc, which help inhibit abnormal phosphorylation and maintain cellular homeostasis ( ). In Alzheimer’s disease, reduced O-GlcNAc is associated with tau protein hyperphosphorylation ( ). OGT expression is notably elevated in neurons, particularly in synapses and nuclei, where it contributes to synaptic plasticity and transcriptional regulation ( ).
In this study, tumor cells were found to transfer OGA to neurons via EVs, inhibiting RIPK1 glycosylation and promoting its phosphorylation, which in turn triggers neuronal necroptosis. RIPK1 was activated via the complex IIb pathway upon exogenous OGA regulation. Another key finding of this study was the identification of Ser440 in RIPK1 as crucial for O-GlcNAcylation. Mutations at this site disrupted the glycosylation–phosphorylation balance, altering RIPK1’s interaction with OGA and its downstream signaling. Nevertheless, several limitations remained. Future work should test the generality of this mechanism across additional cancer types and pursue rigorous biochemical validation of the OGA–RIPK1 interaction. Recombinant enzyme substrate binding assays should serve as the primary approach for confirming the direct interaction between OGA and RIPK1. An intriguing finding of this study was that the RIPK1 331A mutation enhanced its interaction with OGA, whereas the 440A mutation weakened it. This opposite behavior at distinct glycosylation sites calls for further structural investigation of the protein. Meanwhile, the antagonism between glycosylation and phosphorylation modifications warrants further investigation.
HMGB1-driven RAGE signaling in tumor cells fuels PNI, and the RAGE inhibitor FPS-ZM1 synergistically augments gemcitabine efficacy in preclinical models, underscoring clinical potential. Although HMGB1–RAGE has been implicated in multiple malignancies, its mechanistic role in neurotropic tumors such as cholangiocarcinoma and pancreatic cancer remains to be fully elucidated ( – ). This study established the HMGB1–RAGE loop as a driver of PNI. However, the intracellular signaling cascades triggered by this axis remain elusive, and the canonical nodes of RAGE activation merit comprehensive elucidation. In subsequent work, we will verify this pathway in at least two neurotropic tumor types and will focus particularly on the intracellular signaling alterations elicited by HMGB1–RAGE activation. Although FPS-ZM1 effectively disrupted the HMGB1–RAGE loop in models of PNI, we acknowledge several inherent limitations of this strategy. Systemic RAGE inhibition may compromise RAGE-dependent immune surveillance, and off-target liabilities could undermine therapeutic benefit. Future work should therefore prioritize antibody–drug conjugates or nanoparticle-encapsulated delivery to confine drug exposure to the tumor microenvironment and deploy biomarker-enriched trials that select patients with high HMGB1/RAGE expression to maximize the therapeutic index.
In conclusion, gallbladder cancer transfers OGA to neurons via EVs, inhibiting RIPK1 glycosylation and promoting its phosphorylation. This activates the complex IIb pathway, inducing neuronal necroptosis. Neuronal rupture releases HMGB1, which activates the RAGE receptor on gallbladder cancer cells, enhancing PNI and establishing a feedback loop.
