## Abstract
Lung bacterial infections, including hospital‐acquired pneumonia, remain a serious problem for public health. Endothelial cell (EC) exposure to heat‐killed Staphylococcus aureus (HKSA) represents a clinical scenario of high titers of killed bacterial particles present in the host after antibiotic therapy, which triggers inflammatory cascades, cytokine storms, and EC dysfunction leading to acute lung injury (ALI). GPR68 is a member of the proton‐sensing G protein‐coupled receptor family. Acting as a pH sensor, GPR68 becomes activated upon pH reduction and contributes to pathologic cell responses by activating ER stress and unfolded protein response. This study investigated the role of GPR68 in HKSA‐induced EC dysfunction and HKSA‐induced ALI. HKSA robustly increased GPR68 mRNA levels in human pulmonary EC and directly stimulated GPR68 activity. A selective GPR68 small molecule inhibitor, OGM‐8345, attenuated HKSA‐induced EC permeability and protected cell junction integrity. OGM‐8345 inhibited HKSA‐induced activation of inflammatory genes TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐1β, and CXCL5 and decreased cytokine secretion by HKSA‐challenged EC. Co‐treatment with the GPR68 activator Ogerin or medium acidification to pH 6.5 augmented HKSA‐induced EC dysfunction, which was rescued by OGM‐8345. Intratracheal HKSA injection increased vascular leak and lung inflammation in mice which were monitored by lung Evans blue extravasation, increased cell and protein count in bronchoalveolar lavage, and mRNA expression of inflammatory genes. ALI and barrier dysfunction was attenuated by OGM‐8345. We show for the first time the role of GPR68 in mediating HKSA‐induced lung injury and the strong potential for OGM‐8345 as a therapeutic treatment of bacterial pathogen‐induced ALI associated with tissue acidification.

Staphylococcus aureus infections increase the expression and activity of GPR68, which leads to endothelial dysfunction, ultimately causing acute lung injury. Acidosis further enhances S. aureus‐mediated increase in the expression and activity of GPR68. Inhibition of GPR68 with OGM protects against S. aureus‐induced and acidosis‐exacerbated endothelial dysfunction and thus rescues lung injury.

3.2

## 
GPR68 inhibition rescues HKSA‐induced endothelial permeability and inflammation
Since the results showed the activation of GPR68 by HKSA, we next evaluated potential EC‐protective properties of a small molecule GPR68 inhibitor, OGM‐8345, recently discovered by our group. , Measurements of GPR68 activity by PRESTO‐Tango assay showed that OGM‐8345 strongly inhibited HKSA‐induced GPR68 activation observed at 6 h and 24 h after HKSA challenge (Figure  ). Interestingly, HKSA‐induced selective transcriptional activation of GPR68 shown in Figure  was significantly attenuated by OGM‐8345 (Figure  ), while mRNA expression of GPR4 and GPR65 was not affected by HKSA or OGM‐8345. Direct visualization of EC monolayer permeability for FITC‐labeled avidin used as a tracer showed that incubation of human pulmonary EC with OGM‐8345 attenuated HKSA‐induced endothelial barrier disruption reflected by the permeation of FITC‐labeled tracer through disrupted cell–cell junctions in the XPerT assay (Figure  ). In agreement with these results, TER measurements showed that HKSA‐induced HPAEC barrier disruption was dose‐dependently attenuated by OGM‐8345 (Figure  ). In contrast, NE52‐QQ57, a pharmacological inhibitor specific to GPR4, failed to show such barrier protective effects.
 Likewise, OGM‐8345 reversed HKSA‐induced breakdown of EC adherens junction reflected by disruption of the continuous pattern of junctional VE‐cadherin staining (Figure  , upper panel). OGM‐8345 also suppressed HKSA‐induced actin cytoskeletal remodeling and formation of paracellular gaps (Figure  , lower panel). Surface protein biotinylation assay demonstrated that HKSA challenge decreased presentation of adherens junction protein VE‐cadherin and tight junction protein JAMA‐1 at the cell surface, reflecting disassembly of cell junction complexes. This effect was strongly attenuated by OGM‐8345 (Figure  ).
 Effects of GPR68 inhibition on HKSA‐induced endothelial inflammatory response were evaluated in the next experiments. HKSA stimulation increased mRNA levels of TNF‐α, VCAM‐1, IL‐6, IL‐8, and IL‐1β in human pulmonary EC. This effect was strongly attenuated by OGM‐8345 with an optimal dose range of 0.5–1 μM (Figure  ). The anti‐inflammatory effects of single OGM‐8345 treatment were sustained, as strong inhibition of HKSA‐induced mRNA expression of TNF‐α, VCAM‐1, IL‐6, and IL‐8 was observed up to 24 h after HKSA challenge (Figure  ). In consistency with mRNA data, HKSA‐induced increases in protein expression of phospho‐NF‐κB, ICAM‐1, and VCAM‐1 were dose‐dependently inhibited by OGM‐8345 in the 0.5–3 μM concentration range (Figure  ). O33, a structural analogue of OGM‐8345 but without GPR68 inhibitory activity, had no protective effects against HKSA‐induced inflammation in EC. Taken together, these data strongly indicate that GPR68 inhibitor OGM‐8345 successfully blocks HKSA‐induced endothelial dysfunction.

3.3

## Post‐treatment with OGM still inhibits HKSA‐induced endothelial dysfunction
OGM‐8345 protective effects against HKSA‐induced endothelial dysfunction were next tested in a more clinically relevant post‐treatment model. In these experiments, OGM‐8345 was added to HPAEC monolayers 6 h after the initial HKSA challenge, and endothelial barrier function was monitored by measurements of TER over a 25‐h period. The results showed that 6 h post‐treatment with OGM‐8345 was able to attenuate the HKSA‐induced endothelial permeability (Figure  , left panel). In contrast, O33 failed to rescue HKSA‐caused drop in TER (Figure  , right panel). These results provide further confirmation that only OGM compounds with selective GPR68 inhibitory effects can interfere with bacterial endothelial dysfunction. Barrier protective effects of OGM‐8345 post‐treatment were further examined by XPerT assay and visualizing EC adherens junctions with VE‐cadherin immunostaining. Imaging analysis showed that post‐treatment with OGM‐8345 for up to 4 h after the initial HKSA challenge efficiently attenuated endothelial macromolecular permeability, reflected by a decreased permeation of FITC‐labeled tracer through EC monolayers (Figure  ). In support of these observations, VE‐cadherin immunofluorescence staining images revealed that OGM‐8345 post‐treatment at 0.5, 1, and 4 h reversed HKSA‐induced breakdown of EC junctions reflected by reappearance of continuous VE‐cadherin staining (Figure  ). Anti‐inflammatory effects of OGM‐8345 post‐treatment were evaluated by western blot analysis of HKSA‐induced protein expression of phospho‐NFκB, ICAM‐1, and VCAM‐1. These effects were strongly suppressed if OGM‐8345 was administered 0.5, 1, or 2 h after HKSA challenge, while 4‐h posttreatment caused minimal protective effect (Figure  ). Altogether, these results mimicking clinical scenarios indicate a potential therapeutic potential for OGM‐8345.
 
3.4

## Acidosis exacerbates HKSA‐induced endothelial injury
GPR68, along with other proton‐sensing GPCRs, responds by gradual activation to reduction of pH to 6.8–6.5. Thus, we tested the effect of medium acidification on HKSA‐induced GPCR expression in lung EC. Our results demonstrated a profound 6‐fold increase in GPR68 mRNA expression in response to HKSA stimulation that was further augmented to about a 9‐fold increase by medium acidification to pH 6.5 (Figure  ). Remarkably, neither HKSA nor acidic pH alone, nor their combination, had any effects on transcriptional activation of GPR4 and GPR65. HKSA treatment induced breakdown of EC adherens junctions leading to a discontinuous pattern of immunofluorescent staining of VE‐cadherin at cell–cell junctions (Figure  , upper panel). F‐actin staining showed the appearance of actin stress fibers and formation of paracellular gaps (Figure  , lower panel). These EC barrier disruptive effects of HKSA were further augmented by lowering pH in the medium to 6.5. In consistency with these results, transcriptional activation of endothelial inflammatory marker genes TNF‐α, VCAM‐1, ICAM‐1, and IL‐1β caused by HKSA was significantly enhanced by culture medium acidification (Figure  ). Likewise, HKSA‐induced protein expression of ICAM observed at normal pH 7.4 medium was further augmented by medium acidification to pH 6.5 (Figure  ).
 
3.5

## Acidosis causes endothelial dysfunction in lung microvascular EC

ECs from microvascular and macrovascular beds exhibit variability in their responses to agonists. We next examined the effects of medium acidification on lung microvascular EC barrier disruption and inflammation. Measurements of TER showed that acidic pH and HKSA alone triggered barrier dysfunction in HLMVEC monolayers, and this effect was augmented when both stimuli were combined (Figure  ). Acidic pH and HKSA exhibited similar to HPAEC results in additive effects on transcriptional activation of TNF‐α, VCAM‐1, ICAM‐1, and IL‐8 (Figure  ). Furthermore, HKSA‐induced increases in ICAM‐1 protein levels were further augmented in HLMVEC challenged with HKSA in acidic pH medium (Figure  ). Altogether, these observations suggest that acidosis‐induced endothelial dysfunction is not limited to macrovascular EC but also exists in EC from microvascular origin.
 
3.6

## 
OGM‐8345 attenuates HKSA and acidosis‐induced endothelial dysfunction
Protective effects of OGM‐8345 against combined detrimental effects by HKSA and medium acidification on pulmonary EC function were investigated in more detail. HKSA‐induced endothelial permeability reflected by increased FITC fluorescence signal in the XPerT assay was markedly increased upon medium acidification to pH 6.5 from that of physiologic pH 7.4 (Figure  ). OGM‐8345 strongly attenuated HKSA‐induced EC permeability at both normal and acidic pH. Furthermore, HKSA‐induced mRNA expression of TNF‐α, VCAM‐1, ICAM‐1, IL‐8, and IL‐1β was further augmented in cells cultured at pH 6.5 compared to those at pH 7.4 (Figure  ). The augmenting effect of acidic pH on HKSA‐induced inflammatory gene expression was markedly attenuated by single treatment with OGM‐8345. In contrast, the inhibitor of the GPR4 pH sensor, NE52‐QQ57, failed to show protective effects against endothelial inflammation caused by pH 6.5 and HKSA (Figure  ). These findings were further confirmed by analysis of HKSA‐induced protein expression of ICAM‐1, which was enhanced at acidic pH 6.5 and strongly inhibited by OGM‐8345 at both pH ranges (Figure  ). To test the broader efficacy of OGM‐8345 towards EC sub‐phenotypes, its protective effects were further examined in cultured primary human lung microvascular EC. The results showed that HKSA‐induced breakdown of HLMVEC junctions was exacerbated at acidic pH and reversed by OGM‐8345 at both normal and acidic pH conditions (Figure  , upper panel). OGM‐8345 also prevented HKSA‐induced F‐actin remodeling and paracellular gap formation, which was more pronounced at acidic pH (Figure  , lower panel). In addition, HKSA‐induced protein expression of ICAM‐1 was augmented by acidic pH and efficiently blocked by OGM‐8345 (Figure  ). These data clearly establish the role of GPR68 in mediating HKSA or acidic pH‐induced endothelial dysfunction and rule out the involvement of GPR4 in this process.
 
3.7

## 
OGM‐8345 rescues HKSA‐induced ALI in mice
The promising vascular barrier protective and anti‐inflammatory effects of the GPR68 inhibitor were further tested in the murine model of HKSA‐induced lung injury. Initial analysis of GPR68 and GPR4 mRNA expression showed abundant presence of both proton‐sensing GPCRs at comparable levels in mouse lungs, heart, brain, kidney, and spleen (Figure  ). Intratracheal administration of HKSA caused a profound and selective increase in GPR68 mRNA levels in the lungs, heart, and, to a lesser extent, in brain tissues, while the kidney, liver, and spleen were not affected (Figure  ). Analysis of BAL from mice receiving simultaneous injection of vehicle or OGM‐8345 (i.p.) and HKSA (i.t.) and collected 24 h after treatments showed that HKSA caused robust, 15‐fold neutrophil accumulation (Figure  ) and a 5‐fold increase in protein content (Figure  ). These increases were strongly attenuated by co‐administration of OGM‐8345. Of note, different routes of HKSA and OGM‐8345 administration suggest predominant primary targeting of lung vasculature by GPR68 inhibitor. HKSA‐induced lung inflammation was also evaluated by analysis of mRNA expression of selected endothelial inflammatory marker genes in lung samples. The results showed that HKSA‐induced elevation of mRNA transcripts of TNF‐α, IL‐6, KC, IL‐1β, and CXCL2 was significantly attenuated by OGM‐8345 (Figure  ). Evans blue extravasation assay further confirmed HKSA‐induced vascular leak in mouse lungs, reflected by the accumulation of the dye tracer in lung parenchyma, which was strongly attenuated by OGM‐8345 (Figure  ). Taken together, these data show that in vitro beneficial effects of the GPR68 inhibitor OGM‐8345 can be expanded to in vivo lung injury models.

## DISCUSSION
Pathologic effects of Staphylococcus aureus‐derived bacterial products on endothelial function are well recognized, and the present study revealed a novel mechanism of GPR68 involvement in this process. The major findings of this study show that: (i) HKSA directly stimulates GPR68 mRNA expression and activity; (ii) Ogerin, a synthetic GPR68 activator, exacerbated, while siRNA‐mediated knockdown of GPR68 attenuated HKSA‐induced endothelial damage; (iii) acidic pH known to activate GPR68 augmented HKSA‐induced endothelial dysfunction; and (iv) the GPR68‐specific small molecule inhibitor OGM‐8345 rescued HKSA‐induced vascular leak and inflammation both in vitro and in vivo. Collectively, these results support the role of GPR68 as an essential mediator of HKSA‐induced vascular endothelial dysfunction and lung injury. The key role of GPR68 during HKSA‐induced endothelial damage augmented by acidosis was further supported by the findings that acidic pH did not activate other proton‐sensing GPCRs in EC, and pharmacological inhibitors of GPR4 failed to block the detrimental effects of HKSA on lung endothelium.
The precise mechanism of HKSA‐induced GPR68 activation in endothelial cells warrants further investigation. It is now recognized that inflammatory processes frequently cause extracellular acidification in the inflamed regions through increased glycolysis and lactate secretion. The glycolytic metabolism rate in inflamed cells is enhanced with the increased demand for energy and oxygen. Consequently, the secreted lactic acid accumulates in the extracellular space in affected tissues. For example, while the physiological lactate concentration in blood and tissues is approximately 1.5–3 mM, the blood lactate concentration in sepsis patients can reach more than 20 mM, and the lactate concentration in inflammatory tissues can even reach 40 mM (see Ref. [ ] for review). In addition, inflammatory conditions and associated hypoxia may also enhance GPR68 expression by a mechanism involving NF‐κB transcriptional activity that can be further exacerbated at low pH. Although it is likely that these mechanisms may be common for infections triggered by both gram‐negative and gram‐positive bacteria, further studies are needed to clarify this question.
This study evaluated the protective effects of a first‐in‐class GPR68 inhibitor, OGM‐8345, recently developed by our group, , against HKSA‐induced ALI. A pioneering work by Xu et al. has demonstrated the presence of GPR68 in EC from small‐diameter arteries with an essential role of GPR68 as a mechanosensing receptor in flow‐mediated dilation of arterioles. To further elucidate GPR68's role in endothelial pathophysiology, we carried out a comprehensive analysis of the GPR68 involvement in the modulation of EC response by bacterial pathogens. The results showed selective GPR68 upregulation by HKSA among other GPR family members. In addition to gene expression results, functional GPR68 activity PRESTO‐Tango assay showed that HKSA caused activation of GPR68 at a comparable level to its known agonist Ogerin. The definitive role of GPR68 in HKSA‐induced endothelial dysfunction was further illustrated by the finding that HKSA‐induced endothelial barrier disruption and inflammatory response were augmented by Ogerin and acidic pH, which are both well‐known GPR68 activators. In agreement with an essential role of GPR68 in HKSA‐induced EC dysfunction, OGM‐8345 potently attenuated HKSA‐induced endothelial barrier disruption and inflammatory response. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration of the bacterial pathogen‐induced upregulation of GPR68 augmenting EC dysfunction and lung injury.
Acidosis and inflammation are closely related pathological events in many diseased states, including those from bacterial infections. Cooperative action of inflammatory agonists and tissue acidification has been reported to worsen patient outcomes. , On the other hand, proton‐sensing GPCRs, including GPR68, remain inactive at physiological pH but become activated upon acidification of cell the microenvironment and stimulate signaling pathways regulating various cellular responses. Taking reference to our results, at least in pulmonary vascular EC, acidic pH selectively upregulated GPR68 expression and activity and augmented HKSA‐induced GPR68 mRNA expression, but neither acidic pH nor HKSA altered the expression of other GPCRs, including GPR4 and GPR65. In addition, a robust increase in GPR68 gene expression in lung, heart, and, to a lesser extent in, brain tissues of HKSA‐challenged mice places GPR68 as a crucial mediator of bacterial pathogen‐derived disorders. These data also suggest the potential of the GPR68 inhibitor OGM‐8345 and its other analogs for targeted therapy of cardiopulmonary dysfunction in sepsis and ARDS.
Earlier studies have reported a role of GPR4 in acidosis‐induced endothelial permeability. , Interestingly, our results show a minimal, if any, role of GPR4 in human pulmonary EC permeability caused by culture medium pH reduction to 6.5. The reasons behind these discrepancies are not clear at the moment but may be due to inherent experimental limitations such as potential differences in endothelial and lung organ response to heat‐inactivated and live bacteria or model limitations of cell culture. For example, host response to live bacteria may be more complex and involve activation of additional pH sensors (i.e., GPR4 or GPR65) that may require additional strategies to mitigate the severity of tissue damage in vivo. Pulmonary EC culture in vitro is an important model that allows the dissection of signaling mechanisms triggered by injurious stimuli and GPR68 activation. However, we cannot exclude the involvement of other GPRs by other cell types (i.e., lung epithelial or immune cells) under inflammatory conditions that may further contribute to the severity of ALI.
Nonetheless, a series of analyses using GPR68 activator, GPR68 and GPR4 specific inhibitors, siRNA‐induced GPR68 protein depletion, and verified by multiple complementary assays of measuring endothelial function in both macro‐ and microvascular lung EC suggest that GPR68 is the major contributor to human pulmonary endothelial dysfunction caused by HKSA and/or acidic pH. However, possible roles of other GPRs (GPR4, GPR65, GPR132) in vascular regulation under other physiologic circumstances cannot be completely ruled out. Moreover, contrasting effects of various GPCRs in the same disease model are not unprecedented, as illustrated by GPR68 acting as pro‐inflammatory but GPR65 exerting anti‐inflammatory effects during colon inflammation. In line with our findings, recent studies have shown the protective effects of GPR68 inhibitors against intestinal and cardiac inflammation. , 

Precise mechanisms behind the protective effects of the GPR68‐selective inhibitor OGM‐8345 against pH‐ and HKSA‐induced EC dysfunction warrant further investigation. Our data showed that OGM‐8345, when added alone, enhanced EC barrier reflected by elevation of TER and strengthened EC adherens junctions, thus preventing the agonist‐induced paracellular gap formation and stabilization of the actin cytoskeleton, the features essential for EC barrier integrity. , In addition, OGM‐8345 demonstrated a broad spectrum of anti‐inflammatory activities by interfering with the NF‐κB signaling pathway, inhibiting production of multiple pro‐inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and EC adhesion molecules. Furthermore, equally effective protection by OGM‐8345 in microvascular EC highlights its versatility, and successful inhibition of HKSA or acidosis‐induced endothelial dysfunction in clinically relevant post‐treatment schemes underscores the potential of this compound as a therapeutic for restoring vascular function. The vascular barrier protective and anti‐inflammatory activities of OGM‐8345 were also evident in the mouse model of ALI. Increased lung vascular permeability, infiltration of immune cells in mouse lungs, an increase in BAL total protein content, and upregulation of endothelial‐specific inflammatory marker genes caused by intranasal HKSA administration were all attenuated by OGM‐8345 treatment. Altogether, the findings from this study uncovered a crucial role of GPR68 in mediating HKSA‐induced lung injury, and inhibition of the receptor appears to be a plausible therapeutic approach to treat such disorders.
In summary, the urgency in the development of new therapeutics against severe respiratory illnesses caused by bacterial or viral pathogens is emphasized by the devastating current global pandemic, COVID‐19, that has already taken more than 6 million human lives, as well as by challenges of future pandemics. The findings from the present study demonstrate the amelioration of HKSA‐induced ALI by OGM‐8345 in cell and animal models. Our results strongly suggest this small molecule GPR68 inhibitor as a promising therapeutic candidate to alleviate disease states associated with bacteremia‐associated endothelial dysfunction.
