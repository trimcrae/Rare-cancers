However, the underlying causes of intestinal dysfunction and its impact on disease progression remain to be fully elucidated. Using Caenorhabditis elegans as a model, we found that loss-of-function mutations in pink-1 delayed defecation rhythm, resulting in aberrant intestinal colonization upon the pathogenic bacterium Pseudomonas aeruginosa PA14 exposure, which subsequently accelerates dopaminergic neurodegeneration. Mechanistically, PINK-1 in neurons regulated dgk-1 expression, a key positive regulator of defecation, through direct binding of the downstream transcription factor CEH-22 in the dgk-1 promoter region. Concurrently, pink-1 mutation in the intestine suppresses the glutathione metabolic pathway and impairs the clearance of reactive oxygen species (ROS) induced by pathogenic accumulation. Notably, the regulatory role of PINK-1 is evolutionarily conserved in mammalian cells. Our findings demonstrate that pink-1 mutation in the C. elegans model exhibits the constipation phenotype similar to that observed in early-stage PD patients and weakens resistance to microbial infections via inactivating glutathione metabolic pathway. These results clarify a causal link between intestinal dysfunction and neurodegeneration, providing novel insights into the role of PINK-1 in PD pathogenesis.

## Introduction
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a common and incurable neurodegenerative disorder, with a prevalence of 2 to 3% among individuals over 65 years of age . The pathological hallmarks of PD include the loss of dopaminergic (DA) neurons in the substantia nigra, accumulation of the misfolded α-synuclein (α-syn) protein, mitochondrial dysfunction, and elevated oxidative stress , . As PD progresses, patients develop a range of motor and non-motor symptoms , . Gastrointestinal dysfunction-particularly constipation, one of the most prevalent non-motor symptoms-often manifests years before the onset of motor symptoms, with a reported prevalence ranging from 25% to 60% among PD patients – . Although quite a few previous reports have shown that constipation could increase the risk of developing PD, the detailed molecular mechanisms between constipation and neurodegeneration still remain poorly understood .
Besides aging and environmental factors, several genes have been implicated in PD pathogenesis, among which PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1), a serine/threonine kinase localized to the mitochondrial outer membrane, plays a key role in the clearance of damaged mitochondria by recruiting the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin (PDR-1) . Moreover, various mutations in pink-1 gene have been identified in both recessively inherited familial and sporadic forms of PD , . Although the association between pink-1 mutations and PD is well established in epidemiological studies, it is noteworthy that rodent models harboring pink-1 mutations often fail to recapitulate the core pathological hallmarks of PD, such as DA neuron loss in the substantia nigra and the formation of Lewy bodies – . Similarly, pink-1 mutants of Caenorhabditis elegans model do not exhibit accelerated degeneration of dopaminergic neurons the normal feeding conditions, despite displaying deficits in dopamine-dependent behaviors . Consequently, research has increasingly focused on potential functions of PINK1 beyond mitochondrial quality control in the context of PD pathogenesis. Experimental evidence from Matheoud et al. demonstrated that PINK1 acts as a suppressor of adaptive immunity by inhibiting mitochondrial antigen presentation (MitAP) and mitochondrial-derived vesicles (MDVs) formation . The same group further showed that intestinal colonization with gram-negative bacteria or exposure to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) can convert asymptomatic pink1-/- mice into a fully penetrant model exhibiting acute motor symptoms, suggesting a novel role for PINK1 in triggering autoimmune mechanisms leading to dopaminergic dysfunction . Additionally, a recent study revealed that, similar to early non-motor symptoms in PD patients, pink1-/- mice exhibit delayed intestinal peristalsis and constipation-like phenotypes . However, whether and how PINK1 participates in the regulation of intestinal function remains largely unknown. To address this gap, we employed C. elegans model, which allows for more direct observation of intestinal function and in vivo degenerative changes in DA neurons, to uncover a novel mechanism by which tissue-specific PINK1 contributes to PD pathophysiology through the coordinated regulation of defecation motor programs (DMP) and glutathione metabolism in the intestine, thereby elucidating a causal link between intestinal dysfunction and neurodegeneration.

## Results

## The pink-1 mutation exacerbates DA neurodegeneration upon pathogenic infection in a C. elegans model of PD
Since degeneration of DA neurons is a hallmark feature of PD, we first examined neurodegenerative changes in dat-1p::gfp;pink-1(ok3538) worms, which expressed a GFP reporter in DA neurons and carried the pink-1 loss-of-function mutation. Consistent with previous findings showing the absence of overt PD-like phenotypes in pink1-/- mice under the normal feeding conditions , minimal neurodegeneration was observed in DA neurons of both wild-type and pink-1(ok3538) mutants when maintained on standard E. coli OP50 for 48 h. In both strains, the structural integrity of somata and axons remained intact (left panels of Fig. ). However, upon exposure to the pathogenic bacterium P. aeruginosa PA14, dat-1p::gfp;pink-1(ok3538) worms exhibited markedly reduced GFP fluorescence in neuronal cell bodies, along with axonal blebbing and rupturing (right panel of Fig. ). As damage to DA neurons is commonly associated with impaired motor function, we assessed locomotor behavior by measuring body bend frequency and basal slowing response . It was found that pink-1(ok3538) mutants exposed to P. aeruginosa PA14 for 48 h displayed a significant reduction in body bends compared to wild-type controls under the same conditions (Fig. ). Furthermore, infected pink-1(ok3538) worms showed increased locomotion on food-containing plates and a marked decrease in basal deceleration relative to wild-type animals (Fig. ). Given that aging is considered a major risk factor for neurodegenerative disorders , , we investigated whether the observed neurodegenerative effects could be attributed to accelerated development or aging in pink-1 mutants. Our results indicate that, under both standard and pathogenic feeding conditions, pink-1(ok3538) worms did not exhibit accelerated developmental progression or premature aging compared to wild-type controls (Fig. ). 
Overall, the above results have indicated that the pink-1 mutant, when exposed to the pathogenic bacterium P. aeruginosa PA14, exhibits enhanced degeneration of dopaminergic neurons and locomotor deficits in C. elegans models. These effects do not result from either pathogenic infection or genetic mutation alone, but rather from their combined effects.

## The pink-1 mutation induces the neurodegeneration via impairing intestinal dynamics
A significant disruption of the intestinal epithelial barrier has been reported in C. elegans following intestinal infection with P. aeruginosa PA14 . To investigate whether DA neuron loss in pink-1(ok3538) mutants under such infection is associated with altered intestinal barrier permeability, we assessed intestinal integrity using the food dye FD&C Blue No. 1. The intestinal epithelial barrier in pink-1(ok3538) mutants remained intact and comparable to that of wild-type controls during the 48-hour exposure period (Fig. ). Notably, intestinal morphology was visualized by staining, revealing a marked bloating of the intestinal lumen in pink-1(ok3538) mutants, irrespective of whether the animals were fed E. coli OP50 or P. aeruginosa PA14 (Fig. ). Previous studies have shown that intestinal lumen distension promotes bacterial colonization in the gut , . Therefore, we quantified bacterial colonization in wild-type and pink-1(ok3538) mutants grown on lawns of DsRed-labeled P. aeruginosa PA14, E. coli OP50, or E. coli DH5α. We found that pink-1(ok3538) mutants exhibited significantly higher levels of bacterial colonization compared to wild-type animals at the same time point (Fig. ). This increased colonization was not attributable to differences in bacterial toxicity or quality, as both pathogenic (P. aeruginosa PA14) and non-pathogenic strains (E. coli OP50 and DH5α) displayed similar fluorescence intensities in the intestinal lumen (Figs. – ). Given that defects in DMP have been linked to enhanced bacterial retention , we analyzed DMP cycles in both strains. While wild-type animals exhibited an average defecation cycle of approximately 45 s per consecutive cycle, the cycle duration in pink-1(ok3538) mutants was significantly prolonged (Fig. ). This alteration in defecation timing was largely independent of feeding condition (Fig. ). To rule out the possibility that increased bacterial load resulted from changes in pharyngeal pumping rate—a key determinant of food intake—we compared pharyngeal pumping frequencies between wild-type and mutant worms. No significant difference was observed in the contraction frequency of the terminal pharyngeal bulb in pink-1(ok3538) mutants, regardless of whether they were fed P. aeruginosa PA14 or E. coli OP50 (Fig. – ). 
To validate the correlation between defective DMP induced by pink-1 mutation and DA neurodegeneration, we knocked down flr-4, a gene encoding a protein with serine/threonine kinase activity known to negatively regulate defecation cycle time , in the pink-1(ok3538) mutant background (Fig. ). Knockdown of flr-4 accelerated DMP in pink-1(ok3538) mutants and effectively suppressed intestinal colonization by P. aeruginosa PA14 (Fig. ). Neuronal degeneration was then assessed upon flr-4 knockdown in the worms of dat-1p::gfp;pink-1(ok3538), and the data showed that flr-4 knockdown rescued the degenerative phenotypes in DA neurons during intestinal infection (Fig. ). Moreover, to further examine bacterial colonization and DA neuron degeneration, we also performed RNAi targeting dsc-1, a gene previously reported to inhibit enteric muscle contractions and produce a constipated phenotype when mutated in C. elegans . We found that dsc-1 knockdown indeed enhanced intestinal colonization by P. aeruginosa PA14 (Fig. ) and exacerbated DA neuron loss (Fig. ). Taken together, these results suggest that impaired intestinal dynamics resulting from pink-1 mutation contribute to DA neurodegeneration under P. aeruginosa PA14 exposure.
Finally, we compared the chronological sequence of defecation cycle abnormalities, intestinal lumen bloating, and degeneration of DA neurons. We observed that prolonged defecation cycles and significant intestinal lumen distension were evident at 24 h after exposure to P. aeruginosa PA14 (Fig. – ), whereas degenerative changes of DA neuron became apparent only after 48 h (Fig. ). This temporal progression is also consistent with the clinical presentation of PD patients, in whom constipation typically precedes motor symptoms during disease progression .

## Function-loss of pink-1 in neurons contributes to defective DMP and DA degeneration
To further investigate the regulatory role of pink-1 in DMP and DA neurodegeneration, a rescue construct pink-1p::pink-1::mCherry was generated by fusing the endogenous pink-1 promoter with its full-length coding sequence in frame with mCherry, which was then introduced into dat-1p::gfp;pink-1(ok3538) mutants to restore pink-1 expression. Notably, under standard culture conditions with E. coli OP50, intestinal expression of the transgene was observed in pink-1(ok3538) animals (upper panel, Fig. ). However, when the non-pathogenic bacteria was replaced by P. aeruginsa PA14, pink-1 expression was significantly enhanced in neurons, including DA neurons (lower panel of Fig. ). Subsequent analysis revealed that restored pink-1 expression effectively rescued the DMP defect in pink-1(ok3538) mutants (Fig. ). Moreover, re-expression of pink-1 markedly suppressed DA neuronal degeneration induced by P. aeruginsa PA14 exposure (Fig. ). 
Because of the localization of pink-1 in both intestine and neurons, we sought to determine whether its regulation of DMP exhibits tissue specificity. To address this issue, we crossed the neuron-specific RNAi strain TU3401 (uIs69 [pCFJ90 (myo-2p::mCherry)+unc-119p::sid-1]) and the intestinal-specific RNAi strain VP303(kbIs7 [nhx-2p::rde-1+rol-6(su1006)] with the dat-1p::GFP reporter line, respectively. The results showed that the defecation cycle time was significantly increased in the worms of neuron-specific RNAi pink-1, while the intestinal-specific RNAi pink-1 had minimal effect compared to the negative control (Fig. ). We further detected the bloating of the intestinal lumen and the intestinal colonization of bacteria. Neuron-specific knockdown of pink-1 markedly increased intestinal lumen distension and promoted the intestinal colonization of P. aeruginsa PA14, while no significant changes were observed in animals subjected to intestinal-specific RNAi (Fig. ). Additionally, neuron-specific RNAi of pink-1 led to DA neuron loss under intestinal infection conditions, whereas animals with intestinal-specific RNAi exhibited no notable neurodegeneration relative to the control group (Fig. ). To further validate these findings from tissue-specific RNAi experiments, we performed rescue experiments by driving pink-1 expression specifically in neurons or intestine using the unc-119 promoter and vha-6 promoter, respectively, in the dat-1p::gfp;pink-1(ok3538) background (Fig. ). Consistent with previous observations, pan-neuronal expression of pink-1 under the unc-119 promoter rescued DA neuronal degeneration, whereas intestinal expression driven by the vha-6 promoter failed to confer protection (Fig. ).

## A positive regulator of DMP, dgk-1, acts downstream of pink-1
Previous studies have indicated that 12 genes (egl-36, dgk-1, tax-6, unc-16, lim-6, shn-1, exp-2, unc-44, fat-3, crt-1, hlh-8, rho-1) positively regulate DMP in C. elegans , , the qPCR analysis revealed that the mRNA levels of 5 out of these 12 genes (egl-36, dgk-1, exp-2, fat-3, crt-1) were significantly decreased in pink-1(ok3538) mutant (Fig. ). To determine whether the downregulation of these genes contributes to impaired DMP, RNAi-mediated knockdown of egl-36, dgk-1, exp-2, fat-3 and crt-1 was performed. The results showed that dgk-1 knockdown significantly prolonged the defecation cycle time (Fig. ). 
To comprehensively assess the downstream effects of pink-1 mutation under feeding either E. coli OP50 or P. aeruginosa PA14 at a whole-genome level, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis using wild-type and pink-1(ok3538) mutant animals exposed to these bacterial diets. Compared to wild-type animals fed E. coli OP50, pink-1(ok3538) mutants exhibited down-regulation of 213 genes and up-regulation of 1293 genes under this standard culture conditions (upper panel, Fig. ). In contrast, when exposed to P. aeruginosa PA14, pink-1(ok3538) mutants showed down-regulation of 848 genes and up-regulation of 2449 genes relative to wild-type controls (lower panel, Fig. ). To minimize confounding effects due to differences in bacterial diet, we identified overlapping sets of up-regulated and down-regulated genes in pink-1(ok3538) mutants compared to wild-type animals across both feeding conditions (Fig. ). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of commonly down-regulated genes, performed using DAVID, revealed significant overrepresentation of terms including protein quality control (GO:0006515), ubiquitin-dependent protein catabolism (GO:0032436), protein deglycosylation (GO:0006517), glycoprotein catabolism (GO:0006516), and cell signaling (GO:0035556)—the top five enriched categories (Fig. ). Similarly, GO analysis of overlapping up-regulated genes identified enrichment in cell differentiation (GO:0030154), protein oligomerization (GO:0051260), stratum corneum development (GO:0042338), innate immune response (GO:0006955), and defense response to Gram-negative bacterium (GO:0050829) as the most significantly represented biological processes (Fig. ). Notably, dgk-1 was identified within the cell signaling (GO:0035556) category among the down-regulated genes (Fig. ), and thus it was selected for further functional investigation.
To determine the functional expression pattern of dgk-1, mCherry fluorescence in transgenic worms expressing dgk-1p::dgk-1::mCherry was observed in multiple amphid neurons, phasmid neurons, and the ventral nerve cord, with the highest signal intensity detected in head neurons (Fig. ). Next, we crossed the transgenic worms dgk-1p::dgk-1::mCherry with pink-1(ok3538) mutant and evaluated the expression change of dgk-1 by measuring mCherry fluorescence intensity. The results showed that, compared with the transgenic worms dgk-1p::dgk-1::mCherry, the fluorescent intensity was much weaker once pink-1 was mutated (Fig. ). Finally, Western blot analysis confirmed a marked reduction in dgk-1p::dgk-1::mCherry protein levels in pink-1(ok3538) animals (Fig. ).

## DGK-1 in GABAergic neurons is involved in DA neurodegeneration via regulating DMP
To further evaluate the function of dgk-1, we examined the DMP in dgk-1(ok1462) mutant animals and observed a significant increase in defecation cycle duration compared to wild-type animals (Fig. ). Moreover, the rescued expression of dgk-1 in dgk-1(ok1462) mutant animals substantially restored the prolonged DMP (Fig. ). To determine whether the effect of dgk-1 on DMP is controlled by pink-1, we overexpressed dgk-1 in pink-1(ok3538) mutants and found that both DA neuron degeneration (Fig. ) and DMP abnormalities (Fig. ) were largely rescued relative to pink-1(ok3538) controls. Given that dgk-1p::dgk-1::mCherry was not co-localized with dat-1p::GFP in DA neurons (Fig. ) and considering that the DMP in C. elegans has been reported as a highly coordinated rhythmic behavior governed by GABAergic neurons , we hypothesized that dgk-1 might also function in GABAergic neurons. As expected, DGK-1 was detected to colocalize with UNC-25, a maker specifically expresses in GABAergic neurons, in the transgenic worms harboring dgk-1p::GFP; unc-25p::mCherry (Fig. ). Furthermore, three-dimensional confocal imaging of unc-25p::mCherry; dgk-1p::GFP transgenic animals confirmed the overlapping localization of DGK-1 and UNC-25 (Fig. ). 

## The transcription factor CEH-22 regulates dgk-1 expression downstream of pink-1
To further investigate the regulatory mechanism of dgk-1 expression, potential transcription factors capable of binding to the dgk-1 promoter region were predicted using the JASPAR database (http://jaspar.genereg.net/), and eight candidates with the highest scores were selected: ceh-22, lin-14, mab-3, che-1, daf-12, dyp-27, efl-1, and blmp-1. RNAi-mediated knockdown of each transcription factor was performed in dgk-1p::dgk-1::mCherry transgenic worms, followed by quantification of mCherry fluorescence intensity. Silencing of either ceh-22 or lin-14 significantly reduced mCherry fluorescence intensity (Fig. ). The levels of dgk-1 mRNA were subsequently analyzed upon RNAi knockdown of the eight transcription factors, revealing that ceh-22 depletion markedly suppressed dgk-1 transcription (Fig. ). CEH-22 has been previously reported to be predominantly expressed in the pharyngeal muscle, head neurons, and ventral nerve cord . To determine whether CEH-22 and DGK-1 are co-localized in the same tissues, the transgenic worms expressing ceh-22p::mCherry;dgk-1p::GFP were examined, showing partial co-localization in amphid neurons and the ventral nerve cord (Fig. ). Additionally, based on JASPAR-predicted binding sites for CEH-22 within the dgk-1 promoter, a conserved CCACTTGAAA motifs were identified in this promoter region (Fig. ). ChIP-qPCR analysis demonstrated that the enrichment of the DNA fragment containing these two motifs was significantly reduced following ceh-22 RNAi (Fig. ). 
Although our above data confirmed that dgk-1 expression was regulated by the transcription factor CEH-22, the relationship between pink-1 and ceh-22 remained to be further investigated. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that the mRNA level of ceh-22 was significantly downregulated in pink-1(ok3538) mutant animals (Fig. ). When GFP fluorescence was assessed in transgenic strains carrying ceh-22p::GFP and ceh-22p::GFP;pink-1(ok3538), a marked reduction in fluorescence intensity was observed in the presence of the pink-1 mutation (Fig. ). Furthermore, overexpression of ceh-22 in the pink-1(ok3538) background resored DMP function (6H), rescued DA neuron degeneration (Fig. ), and reduced intestinal colonization by P. aeruginosa PA14 (Fig. )—phenotypes that were initially caused by pink-1 deficiency.

## Intestine-expressed pink-1 functions in activation of the glutathione metabolism pathway during P. aeruginosa PA14 infection
Since feeding on pathogenic bacteria is an essential condition for DA neurodegeneration in pink-1 mutant worms, we analyzed the RNA sequencing data comparing differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in pink-1(ok3538) mutants fed with P. aeruginosa PA14 versus E. coli OP50 to those in wild-type animals. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis was performed on gene sets affected by exposure to the two bacterial diets. The results showed that DEGs from wild-type worms were significantly enriched in the pathway related to glutathione metabolism, a major antioxidant defense mechanisms against pathogens and other stresses (Fig. ). In contrast, this enrichment was absent in DEGs from pink-1(ok3538) mutants under the same dietary comparison (Fig. ), suggesting that PINK-1 plays a role in activating the glutathione metabolism pathway. Next, we directly analyzed downregulated DEGs in pink-1(ok3538) mutants relative to wild-type animals following exposure to P. aeruginosa PA14. This analysis also revealed that genes associated with glutathione metabolism were significantly overrepresented among downregulated transcripts in the mutant (Fig. ). Consistently, Gene Ontology (GO) analysis indicated that DEGs in wild-type worms exposed to different bacteria were enriched in the “glutathione metabolic process”, whereas such enrichment was absent in pink-1(ok3538) animals (Fig. – ). By comparing upregulated glutathione metabolism-related genes in wild-type worms fed P. aeruginosa PA14 versus E. coli OP50 with downregulated counterparts in pink-1(ok3538) mutants, nine overlapping genes were identified: gst-4, gst-10, gst-24, gst-26, gst-28, gst-36, gst-38, gpx-5, F56A4.3 (Fig. ). qPCR analysis confirmed that mRNA levels of eight of these genes were significantly downregulated in pink-1(ok3538) animals following P. aeruginosa PA14 exposure (Fig. ). Using reporter transgenic lines, we similarly found that pink-1 knockdown markedly reduced fluorescence intensity in gst-4p::gst-4::EGFP worms, while exerting only a minor effect on gst-28 expression (Fig. – ). 
One of the key consequences of antioxidant pathway inactivation is the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which damage mitochondria and promote degeneration of DA neurons . To determine whether pink-1 mutation enhanced ROS production, DHE staining was performed in wild-type and pink-1(ok3538) mutant worms fed with P. aeruginosa PA14. Compared to wild-type animals, ROS accumulation was significantly increased in pink-1(ok3538) mutants (Fig. ). To further evaluate whether elevated ROS levels contribute to DA neuron degeneration in C. elegans, N-acetylcysteine (NAC), a potent antioxidant, was applied to scavenge ROS, followed by assessment of DA neuron changes. We found that elimination of ROS accumulation partially rescued DA neuron degeneration in pink-1(ok3538) animals exposed to P. aeruginosa PA14 (Fig. ), but did not accelerate defecation cycle time (Fig. ). Given that neuronal expression of pink-1 confered neuroprotective effect, whereas intestinal expression had no a similar protection in our above result (Fig. ), we hypothesized that pink-1 may exert tissue-specific roles. To test this hypothesis, neuron-specific and intestine-specific RNAi-mediated knockdown of pink-1 were performed. Consistent with our hypothesis, ROS accumulation was significantly increased only in animals subjected to intestine-specific pink-1 RNAi (Fig. ), but not in those with neuron-specific knockdown (Fig. ). Furthermore, we examined the mRNA levels of the nine glutathione metabolism-related genes in animals undergoing intestine-specific or neuron-specific pink-1 RNAi. Our results showed that most of these genes were significantly downregulated in the intestine-specific knockdown group, but not in the neuron-specific group (Fig. – ).

## A conserved regulatory mechanism of pink-1 in mammalian cells
To determine whether the regulatory mechanism of pink-1 is evolutionarily conserved in mammals, we enzymatically isolated individual enteric neurons from C57BL/6 J mice and performed RNAi-mediated knockdown of pink-1 in the cultured enteric neurons, with knockdown efficiency confirmed by immunofluorescence and Western blotting (Fig. ). Our data further showed that upon pink-1 knockdown in enteric neurons, NK2 homeobox 2 (NKX2-2), the ortholog of C. elegans CEH-22, and diacylglycerol kinase theta (DGKQ), the ortholog of C. elegans DGK-1, were significantly reduced, as demonstrated by both Western blotting (Fig. ) and immunofluorescence assay (Figure ). Consistent with findings in C. elegans, NCM460 human colonic epithelial cells treated with 1.5 mM MPP+ for 24 h exhibited increased DHE fluorescence intensity in the pink-1 knockdown group compared to the wild-type control (Fig. ). We also examined the mRNA levels of GSTP-1, GPX-3, GPX-5, and HPGDS, the mammalian homologs of gst-10, gst-28, gst-38, gpx-5, and F56A4.3, in NCM460 cells and found that the transcriptional expression of GSTP-1, GPX-3, GPX-5, and HPGDS was significantly suppressed following pink-1 knockdown (Fig. ). Collectively, these results indicate that the regulatory mechanism of pink-1 is evolutionarily conserved across species. 

## Discussion
PD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by complex interactions among multiple biological systems . According to Braak’s hypothesis, PD may originate from intestinal colonization caused by unidentified pathogens . Supporting this hypothesis, gastrointestinal (GI) deficits, such as constipation, is highly prevalent among individuals with PD . However, due to limited research on prodromal and early-stage PD, the mechanistic link between GI dysfunction and disease progression remains poorly understood. Moreover, accumulating evidence indicates that, in addition to its critical role in mitophagy regulation, PINK-1 also participates in modulating adaptive immunity, endoplasmic reticulum clearance, and α-synuclein degradation , , . Although delayed intestinal peristalsis and constipation-like symptom had been observed in pink1 − /− mice , the molecular mechanisms underlying PINK1-mediated regulation of intestinal function remain unclear. Our findings in the C. elegans model reveal that tissue-specific pink-1 exerts distinct roles in PD pathogenesis: In neurons, pink-1 regulates intestinal motility non-cell-autonomously via the ceh-22/dgk-1 signaling axis, thereby mitigating pathogenic bacterial colonization and subsequent excessive accumulation of ROS; on the other hand, intestinal pink-1 activates glutathione metabolism to facilitate ROS clearance during infection. Loss of pink-1 function disrupts both processes, establishing a self-amplifying pathological loop (Fig. ). 
Despite the strong association between pink-1 mutations and clinical PD patients, numerous studies have shown that animal models harboring pink-1 mutations often fail to recapitulate the core neuropathological hallmarks of PD . Krasko et al. reported that pink-1-/- mice do not exhibit motor symptoms under normal feeding conditions; however, motility deficits were observed in the cecum and colon of the lower gastrointestinal tract. Notably, upon infection with Gram-negative bacteria, pink-1-/- mice exhibited overactivation of cytotoxic mitochondria-specific CD8+ T cells, which accelerated degeneration of DA neurons . Impaired gastric emptying and prolonged small intestinal and cecal transit times have been consistently documented in PD patients compared to healthy controls – . Similar to GI dysfunction observed in PD patients, pink-1(ok3538) mutants in our study exhibited a range of intestinal motility disorders, including intestinal lumen distension, prolonged defecation cycles, and increased bacterial colonization, suggesting a regulatory role of pink-1 in intestinal dynamics. Notably, the extent of lumen distension seemed greater in the worms fed P. aeruginosa PA14 compared with those fed E. coli OP50. This exacerbation is mechanistically consistent with established evidence linking diet-derived bacterial toxins to intestinal bloating in C. elegans . As a well-characterized opportunistic pathogen of C. elegans, P. aeruginosa PA14 exhibits substantially higher virulence than the non-pathogenic laboratory food source E. coli OP50, thereby potentiating toxin-mediated intestinal pathology. Furthermore, exposure to the PA14 led to DA neuron degeneration in pink-1(ok3538) mutants, whereas feeding with E. coli OP50 did not induce such effects. Dysmotility of the gastrointestinal tract promotes pathogenic bacterial colonization, while enhanced motility may confer resistance to infection. By shortening the defecation cycle in pink-1(ok3538) mutants through RNAi-mediated knockdown of flr-4, we observed reduced intestinal colonization by P. aeruginosa PA14 and partial rescue of DA neuronal degeneration.
ROS generated during infection are essential for host defense , yet their sustained production can induce oxidative stress. Oxidative stress has been implicated in aging and neurodegenerative processes. Dimovasili et al. demonstrated that ROS accumulation triggers oligodendrocyte senescence and suppresses expression of the NKX2-2 transcription factor . Our investigation has similarly revealed that pink-1(ok3538) mutants exhibit accelerated ROS production, and the premature ROS accumulation in pink-1(ok3538) animals may partially account for the downregulation of ceh-22, although the underlying regulatory mechanism requires further investigation. To elucidate the basis of increased ROS accumulation in pink-1(ok3538) animals, we conducted transcriptome analyses comparing wild-type and pink-1(ok3538) animals under different feeding conditions. We found that glutathione metabolism pathways were upregulated in wild-type animals fed P. aeruginosa PA14, but not in pink-1(ok3538) animals under the same condition. Glutamine has been shown to alleviate oxidative stress in humans and enhance resistance to microbial infections . During infection, levels of reduced glutathione surge to counteract oxidative damage. These findings suggest that the pink-1 mutation may suppress the expression of genes encoding glutathione reductase, thereby impairing activation of glutathione metabolic pathways. Moreover, pyocyanin, a metabolite of P. aeruginosa, has been reported to deplete glutathione disulfide, disrupt its antioxidant capacity, and promote intracellular oxidative stress – . These results further clarify a candidate mechanism underlying excessive ROS accumulation in pink-1(ok3538) animals: the pink-1 mutation appears to inhibit glutathione metabolic pathways, while pyocyanin facilitates the conversion of reduced glutathione to its oxidized form, contributing to ROS accumulation and oxidative stress. Additionally, we found that pink-1 primarily contributes to the elimination of ROS accumulation in the intestine of C. elegans. However, intestinal-specific knockdown of pink-1 by RNAi did not lead to neurodegeneration in dopaminergic neurons. We hypothesize that this is because intestinal-specific knockdown of pink-1 does not alter intestinal dynamics in C. elegans, thereby preventing colonization by P. aeruginosa PA14 and averting continuous production of massive ROS. In contrast, although neuronal-specific knockdown of pink-1 does not impair the ability to clear ROS per se, an increased P. aeruginosa PA14 colonization leads to a rate of ROS production that exceeds the worms’ clearance capacity, ultimately resulting in DA neuron loss.
In summary, pink-1 plays distinct roles across different tissues in C. elegans, and its mutation in neurons and the intestine exhibits the constipation phenotype similar to that observed in early-stage PD patients and reduces resistance to microbial infection through suppression of the glutathione metabolic pathway, respectively. These findings clarify a causal link between intestinal dysfunction and neurodegeneration, providing novel insights into the role of PINK-1 in PD pathogenesis.

## Methods

## Animal models
The C. elegans strains used in this study were obtained from the Caenorhabditis Genetics Center (CGC) and included the following: wild-type N2 worms; the loss-of-function mutant pink-1(ok3538), which carries a 500 bp deletion in the pink-1 gene; BZ555 [dat-1p::GFP], expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) under the dopamine transporter promoter as a marker for DA neurons; OK29 [ceh-22p::ceh-22::GFP+rol-6 (su1006)], expressing CEH-22::GFP fusion protein under its endogenous promoter; TU3401 [pCFJ90 (myo-2p::mCherry) + unc-119p::sid-1], a neuron-specific RNAi strain; VP303 [nhx-2p::rde-1+rol-6 (su1006)]], an intestine-specific RNAi strain. All experiments were conducted using C. elegans hermaphrodites. When required, males were generated by heat shock treatment at 31 °C for a specified duration . All strains were maintained on nematode growth medium (NGM) agar plates seeded with Escherichia coli OP50 as a food source. Culturing and experimental analyses were performed at a constant temperature of 20 °C.
Fifteen male C57BL/6 wild type (sourced from Yunnan University Laboratory Animal Center, Kunming, China) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at the Kunming Medical University Laboratory Animal Center. Animals were maintained in standard ventilated cages (five per cage) under controlled environmental conditions: temperature 22 ± 1 °C, relative humidity 40–60%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle. Food and UV-sterilized water were provided ad libitum. After the mice reached adulthood (8-week-old), they were anesthetized via inhalation using a calibrated small-animal anesthesia system with 4-5% isoflurane in oxygen at a flow rate of 1 L/min. Following confirmation of deep anesthesia, all mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation to harvest the colorectal tissue. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines established by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Kunming Medical University.

## Synchronization of C. elegans
Gravid adult worms were treated with a hypochlorite solution containing 12% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite and 10% (v/v) 1 M sodium hydroxide to isolate large numbers of C. elegans eggs. The collected eggs were washed with M9 buffer and centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 1 min repeatedly, followed by overnight incubation in fresh M9 buffer at 20 °C. Newly hatched L1 larvae were then transferred to NGM agar plates seeded with E. coli OP50 as a food source and allowed to develop to the young adult stage for subsequent experiments.

## Mammalian cell culture
For experimental studies, the undifferentiated NCM460 human colonic epithelial cell line was cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 IU/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2.

## Assay for DA neurodegeneration
DA neurodegeneration in wild-type and pink-1(ok3538) worms was assessed after 48 h of exposure to either the standard food bacterium E. coli OP50 or the pathogenic bacterium Pseudomonas aeruginosa PA14. Adult worms were collected from culture plates using M9 buffer and transferred into 1.5 ml tubes, followed by three washes with M9 buffer. Subsequently, the worms were placed on glass slides and anesthetized with 10 μL of 10 mM levamisole (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Fluorescence signals from GFP-labeled DA neurons, including CEP, ADE and PDE neurons, were observed and captured under blue fluorescence filters (excitation wavelengths: 455–490 nm) using a Zeiss Axio Imager M2 microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany). Worms were classified as exhibiting neurodegenerative changes if GFP fluorescence showed a marked reduction in the soma of DA neurons or displayed morphological abnormalities such as blebbing and rupture in neuronal processes.

## Body bend assay
To assess locomotion capacity, young adult pink-1(ok3538) and wild-type worms were exposed to P. aeruginosa PA14 plates for 48 h. The number of body bends was recorded over a 30-s period by counting only spontaneous forward undulations. Thirty animals were analyzed per treatment group, with three independent replicates performed.

## Basal slowing response
Young adult pink-1(ok3538) and wild-type animals were exposed to P. aeruginosa PA14 for 48 h, followed by three washes with M9 buffer to remove residual pathogenic bacteria. Subsequently, the two strains were transferred to plates containing either food bacteria or no food. After a 5-min recovery period, the number of body bends per unit time was recorded both in the presence and absence of food. Data were collected from 30 animals per condition. Percent slowing was calculated as the difference between locomotion rates on and off food, divided by the locomotion rate off food.

## Analysis of intestinal function
To analyze the defecation motor program in worms, the defecation cycle, defined as the time interval between two consecutive posterior body wall contractions (pBoc), was recorded in a temperature-controlled room using a dissecting microscope (Nikon SMZ800, Tokyo, Japan) as previously described . The defecation cycle duration of 25-30 worms was measured, and experiments were performed in triplicate.
Pharyngeal activity in worms was assessed by measuring pharyngeal pumping frequency . Briefly, the number of contractions in the terminal pharyngeal bulb was counted over a 30-second period using a manual counter under the dissecting microscope (Nikon SMZ800, Tokyo, Japan). At least 20 worms were analyzed per group.

## Assay of intestinal barrier integrity
To assess the intestinal integrity, synchronized young adult pink-1(ok3538) mutants and wild-type N2 controls were exposed to P. aeruginosa PA14 or E. coli OP50 for 48 h. Following exposure, worms were harvested from plates, washed three times with M9 buffer to remove surface-associated bacteria, and then incubated for 2.5 h in liquid M9 medium supplemented with E. coli OP50 with OD600 0.5–0.6 and 0.5 mg/mL FD&C Blue No. 1 (Bis[4-(N-ethyl-N-3-sulfophenylmethyl) aminophenyl]-2-sulfophenylmethylium disodium salt) (AccuStandard, New Haven, CT). After incubation, animals were collected by centrifugation, washed three times with M9 buffer to eliminate residual dye and bacteria, and mounted onto glass microscope slides. Intestinal barrier integrity was determined by visualizing dye leakage into the pseudocoelomic cavity using brightfield microscopy on a Zeiss Axio Imager M2 system.

## RNA Interference (RNAi) Treatment
RNAi was administered by feeding C. elegans with bacteria harboring RNAi clones from the RNAi library (Source BioScience, Nottingham, UK) . The designated RNAi bacterial strains and the control strain HT115 were seeded onto NGM plates supplemented with 1 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside to induce dsRNA expression and incubated overnight at 25 °C. Next, synchronized L1-stage animals were transferred to RNAi-containing plates and maintained until they reached the L4 stage for use in subsequent experiments.
To specifically interfere with the mRNA level of pink-1 in NCM460 cell lines, cells were seeded onto 6-well culture plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells cells per well and grown overnight. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used to transfect NCM460 cells with 80 nM pink-1 siRNAs for 24 h. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with fresh medium, and the interference efficiency was evaluated by Western blotting after an additional 24-h incubation.

## RNA-sequencing sample preparation
A total of 12 RNA samples were prepared and sent to Biomarker Technologies (Beijing, China) for sequencing, comprising wild-type N2 and pink-1(ok3538) mutants, each maintained under two dietary conditions of E. coli OP50 or P. aeruginosa PA14. Synchronized L1-stage larvae were initially cultured on E. coli OP50-seeded NGM plates until reaching the young adult stage. Subsequently, worms were transferred to fresh plates seeded with either E. coli OP50 or P. aeruginosa PA14 and incubated for 48 h prior to harvest.

## RNA extraction and qPCR quantification
Different worm samples were collected from culture plates using M9 buffer and transferred into 1.5 ml tubes. The animals were washed three times with M9 buffer to remove residual bacteria. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the worm pellets were subjected to three freeze-thaw cycles using liquid nitrogen. Total RNA was extracted using the Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and stored at −80 °C. The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa Bio, Beijing, China). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was then performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (YEASEN, Shanghai, China) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System. Gene expression data were analyzed using the 2–△△Ct method and normalized to the reference gene act-1.

## Genetic manipulation
For gene localization or rescue, pink-1 and dgk-1 cDNA sequences, along with their respective native promoters, were cloned into the vector pPD95.75 in frame with GFP or mCherry using the In-Fusion cloning method. For tissue-specific rescue experiments, the vha-6 or unc-119 promoter was used to drive pink-1 expression specifically in the intestine or neurons, respectively. Germline transformation was performed by microinjecting the prepared DNA constructs (30 ng/μL) into the germlines of N2 and pink-1(ok3538) animals.

## Western blot
For Western blot analysis, worm samples subjected to different treatments were collected in M9 buffer and immediately homogenized on ice to a final volume of 500 μL in M9 buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The homogenates were then centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min to collect the supernatant. Total protein concentration in the supernatant was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes, which were incubated with the primary anti-mCherry antibody at a dilution of 1:10,000 (ABclonal Biotech, Wuhan, China). Anti-actin antibody (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was used as a loading control.

## ChIP–quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ChIP-qPCR)
ChIP-qPCR were performed using a Simple ChIP Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To assess CEH-22 occupancy at the dgk-1 promoter, chromatin was isolated from transgenic animals expressing ceh-22p::ceh-22::GFP and subjected to immunoprecipitation with anti-GFP antibody (Proteintech, Wuhan, China). For ChIP reaction, the lysate was incubated with anti-GFP (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and shaken at 4 °C overnight, while the same sample was incubated beads with rabbit IgG as a negative control for nonspecific genomic DNA binding. Immune complexes were captured using protein G agarose beads, then sequentially washed with low-salt, high-salt, LiCl, and twice washes in TE buffer. Chromatin–protein crosslinks were reversed by incubation at 65 °C for 2 h, followed by proteinase K treatment. Purified DNA was recovered using spin-column-based purification and quantified by qPCR with primers flanking the predicted CEH-22 binding motif within the dgk-1 promoter region.

## Determination of reactive oxygen species (ROS) level
Young adult pink-1(ok3538) and wild-type animals that had been exposed to P. aeruginsa PA14 for 48 h were stained with 3 µM dihydroethidium (DHE) (Beyotime, Wuhan, China) at room temperature for 3 h. The animals were washed three times with M9 buffer, and ROS generation was assessed using a fluorescence microscope (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany). N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC), an antioxidant known to suppress ROS production, was used as a control treatment to mitigate intracellular ROS levels.
To assess ROS generation in NCM460 cell lines following pink-1 knockdown, NCM460 cells were seeded onto 24-well culture plates containing glass coverslips at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and incubated overnight. DHE was used to stain intracellular ROS, and the level of ROS was evaluated using a fluorescence microscope.

## Enteric neuron progenitor cell isolation
Colon tissues from 6–8-week-old female and male wild-type C57BL/6 J mice were excised and washed with cold Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The stool in the intestinal lumen was flushed out 3-5 times using a 2 mL syringe filled with cold HBSS. Mesenteries, fat tissue, and gut-associated organs were carefully removed from the gut wall. The colon tissues were transferred into a new sterile Petri dish containing HBSS, and the luminal opening was made using scissors. Smooth Muscle/Myenteric Plexus (SM/MP) strips were separated from the mucosal and submucosal layers using forceps. The SM/MP strips were cut into 2–4 cm segments with fine scissors and transferred into a 15 mL tube. One milliliter of prewarmed 1% collagenase digestion medium was added to the tube containing the minced SM/MP strips, followed by incubation in a 37 °C water bath for 15 min. Digestion was terminated by adding an equal volume of HBSS. The tissue suspension was centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was discarded. Subsequently, 1 mL of prewarmed 0.05% Trypsin-EDTA (diluted in HBSS) was added to the tube and incubated for 10 min with gentle shaking in the 37 °C water bath. Two milliliters of digestion neutralizing medium (DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% FBS) were added to the tube, and the solution was pipetted up and down thoroughly for 1 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in 5 mL HBSS, filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer into a new 50 mL tube, and centrifuged again at 200 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, followed by removal of the supernatant. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in 1 mL proliferation medium consisting of Neurocult Basal Medium (StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada), Neurocult Proliferation Supplement (StemCell Technologies), 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific), antibiotic/antimycotic (500 μL), 100 μL EGF (10 μg/mL; StemCell Technologies), and 100 μL bFGF (10 mg/mL; StemCell Technologies). The cells were then equally distributed into gelatin-coated 24-well plates (pre-coated with a thin film of mechanically prepared gelatin) in 500 μL of proliferation medium per well and cultured to allow neurosphere formation.

## Statistical analysis
All data obtained from at least three independent biological replicates were calculated and expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD). Prism v.9.0 software (GraphPad, La Jolla, CA, USA) was used for the statistical analysis and to generate graphs. Statistical comparisons between groups were analyzed with the Student’s t test or by one-way analysis of variance, followed by Dunnett’s test.
