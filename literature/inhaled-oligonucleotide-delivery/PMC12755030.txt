However, the upstream regulators that initiate these morphological and functional changes in microglia remain unclear. In this study, we observed marked cytoskeletal reorganization in the hippocampal microglia of 2VO rats at 8 weeks, indicative of a shift from a homeostatic to a pro-inflammatory state. Notably, Tincr expression was significantly downregulated in both the microglia of 2VO rats and the hippocampi of AD patients. Tincr knockdown promoted microglial deformation and migration, accompanied by enhanced cytokines release and phagocytic capacity. These morphological changes correlated with redistribution of non-muscle myosin IIA ( NM IIA) and reduced expression of MYPT1, both in vitro and in vivo, effects that were reversed by Tincr overexpression. Genetic rescue of Mypt1 restored MYPT1 levels and attenuated Tincr-deficiency-induced microglial deformation in the hippocampi of 5xFAD mice. Mechanistically, Tincr enhanced MYPT1 protein expression through dual: functioning as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) that sponged miR-153-3p, and serving as a direct protein-binding scaffold for MYPT1, thereby suppressing NM IIA phosphorylation and stabilizing microglial structure. These findings identify the Tincr-MYPT1-NM IIA axis as a critical regulatory pathway underlying chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH)-induced microglial deformation and dysfunction, offering a novel mechanistic insight into the pathogenesis of neuroinflammation in AD.

## Introduction
Sustained release of inflammatory cytokines due to the activation of microglial cells is an early event in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathogenesis, playing a critical role in promoting Aβ deposition, Tau propagation, and subsequent neurodegeneration [ , ]. Targeting on microglial priming in the pre-disease period and preventing the cell damage of post-inflammatory are showing promise as part of a disease-modifying strategy. For example, triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) and C-X3-C motif chemokine receptor 1 (CX3CR1) [ , ].
Chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH), defined as a persistent reduction in cerebral blood flow that fails to meet the neurometabolic demands of brain tissue, is considered an early, preclinical marker of both Alzheimer;s disease (AD) and vascular dementia (VaD) [ – ], and could be the driver of MCI transitioning to AD [ ]. Previous studies have shown that CCH leads to disrupted axonal transport, hyperphosphorylation of Tau (p-Tau), and the formation of neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), all of which are neuropathological hallmarks of AD [ – ] and VaD [ – ]. These findings suggest that preventing CCH-induced brain damage may be a promising strategy to mitigate or delay the onset of AD. Interestingly, microglial activation is also considered an early event in AD pathogenesis, playing a critical role in promoting Aβ deposition, Tau propagation, and subsequent neurodegeneration [ , ]. This phenomenon suggests that CCH may interact with microglial activation to contribute to the early pathogenesis of AD. Previous animal studies have demonstrated that, regardless of the underlying cause, CCH alone can trigger microglia activation and the release of inflammatory cytokines through various mechanisms. These include activation of the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling pathway [ ], inflammasome activation [ ], and upregulation of CD73 expression and activity [ ]. These findings suggest that CCH can drive the transition of microglia toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype via multiple, distinct molecular pathways. This raises an important question: would targeting any single mechanism be sufficient to achieve an effective therapeutic outcome? Identifying a common upstream or downstream regulator, a convergent pathway essential for all CCH-induced pro-inflammatory microglial responses, may represent a more promising and valuable therapeutic strategy.
As the primary immune defense of the central nervous system (CNS) [ , ], microglia are characterized by their ability to migrate to lesion sites and release inflammatory cytokines in response to CNS invasion. This process requires ameboid morphological transformation and motility, driven by dynamic cytoskeletal rearrangements [ ]. This cytoskeletal deformation affects multiple microglial functions, including vesicle transport, phagocytosis, migration, adhesion, morphology, secretion, transcription, cytokinesis, and is plays essential roles in brain development, inflammation, repair, and myelination processes [ ]. These observations suggest that targeting microglial deformable motility may represent a promising therapeutic strategy. It is well established that the extensive cytoskeletal reorganization in microglia upon stress stimulation is heavily dependent on actomyosin dynamics [ ]. In particular, non-muscle myosin IIA (NM IIA) plays a pivotal role in microglial shape changes and motility, regulated through the phosphorylation of its regulatory light chain (RLC) [ , , ]. Furthermore, the phosphorylation status of NM IIA RLC is primarily governed by myosin phosphatase, a trimeric enzyme composed of myosin phosphatase-targeting subunit 1 (MYPT1; also known as PPP1R12A), protein phosphatase 1 C (PP1c), and an adaptor module (AM) [ ]. However, whether and how CCH influences this regulatory mechanism remains largely unknown.
Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs, > 200 nt in length), although transcribed from the genome, are typically not translated into proteins. They play essential roles in regulating gene expression, chromatin dynamics, differentiation, growth, and development [ – ]. Dysregulated lncRNAs, such as NEAT1 [ ], BACE1-AS and BC200 and BC200 [ ], MALAT1 [ ], UCHL1-AS1 [ ], and BDNF-AS [ ], have been identified as biomarkers for diagnosis and potential therapeutic targets in neurodegenerative diseases, including AD, Parkinson’s disease (PD), and Huntington’s disease (HD), multiple sclerosis, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Furthermore, several lncRNAs, such as SNHG14 [ ], GAS5 [ ], and fantom3-F730004F19 [ ] have been reported to regulate microglial function in the context of cerebral infarction, neurotoxicity and subarachnoid haemorrhage, respectively. However, whether lncRNAs are involved in CCH-induced cognitive decline remains unknown.
In this study, we investigated how CCH affects the morphological response of hippocampal microglia and found that reduced Tincr expression regulates NM IIA dephosphorylation by downregulating MYPT1 through both RNA–RNA and RNA–protein interactions, thereby potentially contributing to memory decline in AD.

## Materials and methods

## Human brain and blood samples
The brain tissue were acquired from postmortem AD patients and age-matched control people. The tissues were supported by the National Human Brain Bank for Development and Function, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College. The blood samples of AD were collected from community health examination (Approval Numbers: IRB1008621).

## Animals
A total of 85 male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (5−6 months old, 280 g−300 g) were obtained from Changsheng Biotechnology (Shenyang, Liaoning Province, China), including 20 sham rat and 65 model rats. Thirty six male mice (5–6 months old, 30 g−35 g) were purchased from Model Organisms Biotechnology (Sahnghai, China). The mouse cohort included 6 wild-type C57BL/6 mice, 6 5xFAD mice, 6 TMEM119CreERT2 mice, 6 5FT mice (TMEM119CreERT2 mice on a 5xFAD background), 6 5FTT (5xFAD; TMEM119CreERT2; injected with pAAV-CMV-DIO-Tincr-WPRE), 6 5FTTShM mice (5xFAD; TMEM119CreERT2; co-injected with pAAV-CMV-DIO-Tincr-WPRE and pAAV-CBG-DIO-miR30shRNA(Mypt1)-WPRE). All animals were housed under standard conditions (temperature 23 ± 1 ℃; humidity 55 ± 5%) with a 12-h dark–light cycle and ad libitum access to food and water. All procedures involving animals, including handling and surgery, were conducted in strict accordance with the U.K. Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act,1986, and the National Institutes of Health Guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. These procedures received approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Harbin Medical University of China (Approval Numbers: IRB1008621). To eliminate the effects of estrogen fluctuations, only male rats were used in the experiments to ensure result consistency.

## Permanent bilateral common carotid artery occlusion (2VO) in the rat
2VO models were established as previously described [ ]. Briefly, 65 rats were anesthetized with 0.5–1.0% isoflurane delivered via inhalation to maintain a stable anesthetic state. Under anesthesia, both common carotid arteries were permanently occluded using 3–0 silk sutures, taking care to avoid damaging the vagus nerve. The arteries were then transected between the two ligation sites. Next, 20 rats of sham group underwent the same surgical procedure, except for the artery ligation. Following surgery, animals were allowed to recover fully from anesthesia before being returned to their cages. After surgery, 7 rats died and 13 rats were excluded due to impaired or lost vision as assessed by pupillary light reflex testing. A total of 45 rats that underwent 2VO surgery were included in the subsequent experiments. The 2VO rats cohort was divided into the following groups: 2VO alone, 2VO with AAV-CMV-Tincr-WPRE viruses injection, and 2VO with AAV-CMV-Mypt1-tWPA viruses injection. Brain tissues from the 2VO rats were harvested 8 weeks after surgery for subsequent analyses.

## Laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI)
To assess the cerebral blood flow (CBF) changes at 8 weeks after CCH, we used a LSCI system (PeriCam PSI System, Perimed AB, Sweden). Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (0.5–1.0%) and maintained under 0.5–1.0% isoflurane following standard protocols, then positioned prone in a stereotaxic head frame. A midline incision was made over the skull to expose the calvaria, and a 1 cm x 1 cm skull cranial window was carefully drilled to expose the leptomeninges for CBF monitoring. During image acquisition, the exposed area was kept clean and dry using a tampon. The CBF was continuously recorded for 25 s under the following parameters: observation height, 10 cm, laser irradiation area, 6 × 5 cm, image matrix, 1388 × 1038. The entire cerebral cortex (100 mm2) was defined as the region of interest (ROI). CBF changes were evaluated using the vendor-supplied PIMsoft software (version 1.4; Perimed). All image acquisition and analysis of were performed by investigators blinded to experimental groups.

## Stereotaxic injection of the viral vectors
Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (0.5–1.0%), and mice were anesthetized with Tribromoethanol anesthesia (240 mg/kg) and subsequently maintained on 0.5–1.0% isoflurane, following standard protocols. For hippocampal injection, 2 μL of viral solution (1012GC/mL) were delivered at a rate of 0.5 μL/minute using a 5μL Hamilton syringe fitted with a 33-gauge needle (Hamilton, Bonaduz, Switzerland). Stereotaxic coordinates relative to the bregma, based on the Paxinos and Watson atlas, were as follows: For rats, point 1: anteroposterior at −4.2 mm, mediolateral at ± 3.1 mm, and dorsoventral at −4.0 mm; point 2:anteroposterior at −3.6 mm, mediolateral at ± 2.3 mm, and dorsoventral at −3.1 mm. For mice, the coordinates were anteroposterior at −2.0 mm, mediolateral at −1.5 mm, and dorsoventral at −2.0 mm. After injection, the needle was left in place for 5 min to minimize reflux and the puncture site was sealed with bone wax. All subsequent experiments were conducted 8 weeks following the virus injection.

## Isolation of microglia, astrocyte, and neuron from adult mouse brain
Adult mice of the specified age were deeply anesthetized with Tribromoethanol and transcardially perfused with ice-cold saline. Whole brain (excluding the cerebellum) were rapidly dissected and rinsed in pre-chilled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Brain tissues were then mechanically and enzymatically dissociated using the Adult Brain Dissociation Kit (130–107-677, Miltenyi Biotec) following the manufacturer's instructions. Cell debris was removed with Cell Debris Removal Buffers and the resulting cell pellet was resuspended in PBS conteining 0.5% BSA for downstream cell type isolation. Microglia were isolated using anti-CD11b-coated MicroBeads (130–093-634, Miltenyi Biotec); astrocytes with anti-ACSA-2 MicroBeads (130–097-678, Miltenyi Biotec); and neurons with the Adult Neuron Isolation Kit (130–126-603, Miltenyi Biotec), all using a MACS multistand separator according to the manufacturer's guidelines. Freshly isolated live microglia were used immediately for the CUT&Tag assay. For protein or RNA extraction, isolated cells were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 ℃ until required. For histone extraction and analysis, cells isolated from three mice were pooled for each sample.

## Cell culture of BV2 cells
BV2 microglial cells, obtained from the National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resource, were seeded at a density of 1–2 × 10^5 cells per well in a 6-well plate format. Cells were cultured in DMEM (Invitrogen, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) and 0.1% penicillin–streptomycin (Solarbio, China). Cultures were maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 for 3 days before being harvested for subsequent experiments.

## Primary culture of neonatal rat microglial cells
Hippocampi were dissected from postnatal days 1–3 (P1-3) Sprague–Dawley rat pups and immediately placed in ice-cold PBS (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Following careful removal of meninges and mechanical dissociation, the tissue were digested with 0.125% trypsin (Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 37 ℃ for 15 min. The resulting cell suspension was resuspended in DMEM (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and seeded into 6-well plate pre-coated with 10 μg/mL poly-D-lysine (Sigma, Saint Louis, CA, USA). After 12–14 days of culture, the medium was replaced with DMEM containing 15% fetal bovine serum and cells were incubated for an additional 24 h. To harvest microglia, culture flasks were sealed with Parafilm and shaken at shaker and 200 rpm for 2 h at 37 ℃ to detach the floating microglia float. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 190 X g for 8 min to obtain microglial cells. The cultures were maintained a 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Microglia were used for in vitro experiments after 5–7 days of culture.

## Plasmids construction and transfection
Cells were seeded into 6-well one day prior to transfection to achieve 60–70% confluence for plasmid transfection or 40–50% for miRNA or siRNA transfection. Transfections were carried out using X-treme GENE siRNA transfection reagent (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, the cells were harvested for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis. The sequences of the siRNAs used are listed in Table S1.

## Western blot
Total protein was extracted from the hippocampi and cortex of rats, as well as from cultured BV2 cells, using Radio Immunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Protein concentrations of both total and nuclear proteins were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Nanjing, China), with bovine serum albumin as the standard. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% or 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. These membranes were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against NM IIA (ab75590, Abcam), phosphothereonine (PTM0705RM, PTMBio), MYPT1 (2634, Cell Signaling Technology), PP1c (GTX105618, Genetex), iNOS (GTX130246, Genetex), Iba-1 (019–19741, Wako), CD68 (MCA1957, AbDserotec), CD206 (MCA2235, AbDserotec), and β-actin (AC038, ABclonal). Protein bands were visualized using the Odyssey Infrared Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA) and analyzed using Odyssey image studio software. Band intensities were normalized to β-actin as the internal loading control.

## Quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was extracted from cells and tissue samples using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to manufacturer’s protocols. cDNA was synthesized from 0.5 μg of total RNA using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, USA). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix Kit (Applied Biosystems, USA) on a 7500 FAST Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). GAPDH or U6 served as internal controls for normalization The primer was listed in Table S2.

## Microglial migration assay
Microglial migratio was assessed using a 48-Well Micro Chemotaxis Chamber (Neuro Probe, Gaithersburg, USA) as previously described by Janssen et al. [ ]. The lower wells were filled with MGP+ cell culture medium containing 100 ng/ml of the chemokine CCL-2 (Peprotech, Rocky Hill, USA) as a chemoattractant. A polyvinylpyrrolidone-free polycarbonate membrane (8 μm pore size,Neuro Probe) was used to separate the upper and lower chambers.. Microglia (7 × 10^4), suspended in MGP + medium with or without various concentrations of blebbistatin, were seeded into the upper chamber compartments. Cells were allowed to migrate for 5–6 h at 37 ℃. Non-migrated cells on the upper membrane surface were removed, and migrated cells on the lower surface were fixed and stained using the Diff-Quik Set (Dade Behring, Deerfield, USA). Each condition was tested in triplicate. Migrated cells were quantified by counting cell bodies in three randomly selected fields per well under a light microscope (Olympus BX61, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at 400 × magnification. The data represent the combined results from three independent experiments.

## Phagocytosis assay
Microglial phagocytic activity was evaluated using a latex bead uptake assay previously described by Pul et al [ , ]. Briefly, microglia isolated from mixed glial cultures were seeded into 24-well plates (Nunc, Life Technologies, Paisley, UK) at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well. After 48 h, the cells were incubated at 37 ℃ for 2 h with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled latex beads (1 μm diameter, Fluoresbrite™ YG carboxylate microspheres; Polysciences, Warrington, USA) at a ratio of approximately 30 beads per cell. As a negative control, a parallel set of cells was incubated with beads at 4 ℃ (on ice). Following incubation, cells were detached using 0.1% Trypsin/EDTA, transferred into polystyrene tubes, and washed three times with ice-cold PBS to remove non-phagocytosed and surface-bound beads. The cells were then resuspended in PBS for cytometric analysis using a FACSCalibur system (Becton–Dickinson, San Jose, CA, USA). Unbound beads, identified by low forward scatter and high fluorescence signal, were excluded from analysis by gating. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) shift resulting from bead uptake was quantified as a measure of phagocytic activity. Active phagocytosis was calculated by subtracting the MFI of 4 ℃ controls from that of the 37 ℃ samples. Results were expressed as the phagocytosis rate relative to untreated (medium control) cells. In selected experiments, siRNA and LPS were added to the cell culture medium at specified concentrations 4 h prior to bead exposure.

## Co‑immunoprecipitation (CO-IP) assay
Cells were lysed using a lysis buffer compatible with both Western blot and immunoprecipitation (IP) analyses (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Protein complexes were immunoprecipitated using antibodies against phosphothereonine (PTM0705RM, PTMBio), MYPT1 (2634, Cell Signaling Technology), PP1c (GTX105618, Genetex), or normal IgG antibody, followed by incubation with Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, California, USA) to capture antibody-protein complexes.The precipitated samples were then subjected to SDS-PAGE and analyzed by Western blotting using the appropriate target antibodies.

## Dual-luciferase reporter assay
The full-length binding site of Tincr and the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) of Mypt1 were amplified by PCR and individually cloned into the multiple cloning sites of the psi-CHECK-2 luciferase reporter vector (Promega, USA). HEK293T cells were seeded at approximately 40%–50% confluency and transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) with 20 μmol/L miR-153-3p mimic or negative control (NC) mimic, along with 0.5 μg of recombinant plasmid DNA. After 48 h of transfection, luciferase activity was measured using a dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (cat. no. E1910, Promega, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Luminometer was quantified with a GloMax® 20/20 luminometer (Promega, USA). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity, and results were presented as relative luciferase activity.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Rats and mice were anesthetized and transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS (0.01 M, pH 7.40, 4 ℃) followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution (pH 7.40, 4 ℃). Brains were dissected and post-fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h, then dehydrated in 30% sucrose solution at 4 °C for 72 h.The samples were subsequently frozen at − 80 ℃. Coronal brain Sects. (20-μm thick) were cut using a cryostat microtome (Leica Microsystems, Germany), mounted onto APS (Amino Silane)-coated glass slides (Solarbio, China), and stored at −80 ℃ until further use.
For immunostaining, sections were permeabilized and blocked in PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 and 10% goat serum at room temperature for 2 h. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against Iba-1, NM IIA, MYPT1, CD68, and CD206. The following day, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 and 594 -conjugated secondary antibodies and counterstained with DAPI. The cultured cells were fixed in 4% PFA for 30 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization and blocking in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 and 10% goat serum for 1 h. Cells were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies against GFAP, NEUN, Iba-1, CD68, and CD206 to label astroglia, neurons, and microglial cells, respectively. The next day, cells were incubated with corresponding Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI.
Fluorescence images were captured using a Zeiss Axio Scope A1 fluorescence microscope at 20 × magnification, under identical exposure conditions, with a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels (24 bit). The field of view is 232.45 μm × 232.45 μm. Quantification of Iba-1+ cells, CD68+/Iba-1+, and CD206+/Iba-1+ cells in the dentate gyrus (DG) or CA1 region of the hippocampi was performed in a blinded manner using ImageJ software (NIH, MD, USA). For each animal, the average cell count was derived from 3 randomly selected fields per section. A total of 3 animals per group were analyzed. Results are expressed as the mean number of positive cells per square millimeter. Images post-processing of was limited to contrast adjustment without color adjustment.

## Morris water maze (MWM) test
The Morris Water Maze (MWM) test wasperformed as previously described [ ]. The apparatus consisted of a black circular pool (diameter: 2.0 m) filled with opaque water maintained at 25 ± 1 ℃ using black food dye. The pool was divided into four equal quadrants, with with a circular escape platform (20 cm in diameter) submerged 2.0 cm below the water surface and positioned in the center of the first quadrant. Prior to training, all rats subjected to pupillary light reflex testing, and those exhibiting impaired reflexes were excluded to minimize potential confounding effects of visual impairment as described before [ ]. During the cued training, rats were gently placed into the pool facing the wall and given three trials per day for five consecutive days. Each trial lasted a maximum of 120 s. If the rat failed to locate the platform within this period, it was manually guided to the platform and allowed to stay for 20 s. After the final cued trial on day 5, the platform was removed from the pool and a 120-s probe trial was conducted on day 6 to assess spatial memory. Behavioral parameters, including escape latency (seconds), swim speed (cm/s), the percentage of swimming distance traveled in the target quadrant, and the number of platform crossings were recorded and analyzed using the DigBehav-Morris Water Maze Video Analysis System (Mobile Datum Software Technology). To ensure unbiased results, rats were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Animals exhibiting immobility in water or obvious motor deficits were excluded from both MWM testing and subsequent behavioral analyses.

## Barnes maze test
The Barnes maze consists of an elevated, brightly lit circular platform (91 cm in diameter) with 20 evenly spaced holes (5 cm diameter) around its perimeter, only one of which leads to an escape tunnel. The platform is covered in white vinyl to contrast with the animal's black fur, enabling automated tracking using the SuperMaze video system. Spatial cues include distinctive wall symbols and room layout. The test comprises three phases:

Habituation: Each mouse is first placed in the escape tunnel for 1 min, then positioned at the maze's center and allowed to explore for up to 5 min until it enters the tunnel. The maze and tunnel are sanitized with 70% ethanol between animals.
Acquisition Training (Day 1-Day 5): Mice undergo two daily trials (2 min each), separated by ~ 1 h. Starting positions are pseudorandomized across quadrants. Each trial begins with a 15-s confinement in a central start chamber. Once released, mice navigate under aversive stimuli (bright light and wind). Trials end when the escape tunnel is found or after 3 min. Mice failing to locate the tunnel are gently guided into it and allowed to remain for 20 s.
Probe Trial (Day 9): Three days post-training, a 2-min probe trial is conducted with the escape tunnel removed. The start chamber is placed at the center of the maze.

Performance is evaluated by recording the number of incorrect holes visits (error times) and total path length before locating the escape tunnel.

## RNA–protein docking
RNA–Protein Interaction Prediction (RPISeq) (http://pridb.gdcb.iastate.edu/RPISeq/#) was used to predict the interaction probability between Tincr and MYPT1. RPISeq assigns a probability score from 0 to 1, with scores > 0.5 suggesting a likely interaction. Benchmark cross-validation experiments report classifier accuracies of 87%- 90%, while independent test sets yield 57%−99% accuracy.
Homology models of MYPT1 were generated using the SWISS-MODEL server, while Tincr RNA 3D structures were built via the 3dRNA (http://biophy.hust.edu.cn/3dRNA). Structural optimization was performed for both MYPT1 proteins and Tincr. Protonation states under physiological pH (PH = 7), were assigned using the H + + 3 server to simulate native conditions, followed by Amber14SB assignment via UCSF Chimera. Charge distribution was based on atom types and local chemical environments.
Molecular docking was carried out using the HDOCK tool, a dedicated protein–RNA docking platform. The empirical, scoring function ITScorePP was employed to evaluate binding affinity based on the interaction forces and geometric complementarity up to 100 docking conformations were generated per run, and the top 10 were ranked by docking score and Confidence score. Conformations with a Confidence score > 0.7 were considered reliable; the top-scoring model was selected for downstream analysis. Three-dimensional visualization and structural analysis were performed using PyMOL 2.0.

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as mean ± standard error of mean (SD). For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student’s t-test was employed. One-way ANOVA was utilized for comparisons involving more than two groups, with Tukey post hoc tests conducted for post hoc analyses when significant main effects were detected. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All graphs were produced using GraphPad Prism version 10.4 software.

## Results

## Tincr is downregulation in disease-associated microglia (DAM) and regulates microglial deformation and activation

## CCH induces pro-inflammatory response with microglial deformation and activation
To investigate the impact of CCH on microglial deformation and associated function, we established a rat model of CCH using bilateral common carotid artery occlusion (2VO) surgery, as described in previous studies [ ]. CBF was monitored by laser speckle contrast imaging (LSCI), which revealed a significant reduction in CBF at 8 weeks post-surgery in 2VO rats (Fig. A). Consistent with earlier reports, the Morris Water Maze (MWM) test demonstrated that CCH for 8 weeks significantly prolonged the escape latency to target all quadrants during the training phase compared to Sham-operated controls (Fig. S1A-C). In the probe trial, 2VO rats exhibited significantly fewer platform crossings, shorter swimming distance, and reduced swimming time spend in target quadrant (Fig. B-D), while swimming speed remained comparable between the groups (Fig. S1D). These findings confirm that 8-week CCH via 2VO surgery successfully induces CBF reduction and impairs spatial learning and memory in rats. 
Immunofluorescence staining revealed that a marked increase in ameboid-like microglia in the hippocampi of 2VO rats than that in sham-operated control rats, characterized by enlarged and swollen cell bodies (Fig. E), morphological features indicative of microglial activation [ ]. The phenomenon reoccurred in 5-month old 5xFAD mice (Fig. S1E) that was also previously identified [ ]. In parallel, the number of CD68 positive fluorescence cells, a marker of activated microglia, were significantly elevated in Iba-1+ microglia in the hippocampi of both 2VO rats and 5xFAD mice (Fig. E; Fig. S1E).
Moreover, qRT-PCR analysis showed significantly elevated mRNA expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β in the hippocampi of 2VO rats compared to the sham group (Fig. F). Similarly, iNOS expression was upregulated, whereas the anti-inflammatory marker Arg-1 was significantly downregulated in the hippocampi of 2VO rats (Fig. G). Collectively, these results indicate that 8-week CCH induces morphological transformation of microglia in the hippocampi and promotes a pro-inflammatory microglial phenotype.

## Tincr is downregulation in hippocampi of AD patients, CCH rats and 5xFAD mice
We next investigated the potential triggers underlying CCH-induced morphological remodeling of microglia. Given the translational relevance to human disease, we first analyzed RNA-seq data from the brains of AD patients available in the Allen Brain Atlas. Using LIMMA differential expression analysis, we identified 78 differentially expressed lncRNAs in the hippocampi of AD autopsy samples compared to age-matched controls, including 23 downregulated and 55 upregulated lncRNAs(Fig. S2A, B; Table S3). To further screen for microglia-specific lncRNAs, we analyzed RNA-seq data from oxyhemoglobin-stimulated BV2 microglial cells obtained from GEO database (GSE200224). This analysis revealed 3007 differentially expressed lncRNAs (1449 upregulated, 1558 downregulated) (Fig. S2C, D; Table S4). Notably, Tincr was the only lncRNA found to be consistently downregulated in both datasets, with no overlapping upregulated lncRNAs between the two comparisons (Fig. H; Fig. S2E). qRT-PCR analysis revealed that Tincr expression was higher in the hippocampi of SD rats compared to other brain regions (Fig. S2F). Notably, Tincr expression was significantly decreased in the hippocampi of both 2VO rats (Fig. I) and 5xFAD mice (Fig. S2G). This downregulation was also observed in autopsy hippocampal samples from AD patients (Fig. J; Table S5).
Moreover, to clarify whether Tincr involves in CCH-induced microglia deformation and activation, we need to know its level in microglia. Using a single cell extraction and separation kit, we isolated microglia, neurons, and astrocytes from the hippocampi of 2VO rats (Fig. K), and found that Tincr expression significantly decreased in the microglia and neurons, but not changed in astrocytes (Fig. L). The Tincr reduced ratio were 71% in microglia and 24% in neurons, respectively. These data suggest that downregulation of Tincr in microglia in hippocampi of 2VO rats might impact on microglia deformation and activation.

## Tincr contributes microglial deformation and activation
Although neuroinflammatory response can be triggered by various pathological insults, including infection, trauma, ischaemia, CCH and toxins, the downstream cascade typically converges on the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, TNF-ɑ, and IL-6 [ , ]. LPS is a well-established inflammatory stimulus and is widely used to activate microglia both in vitro and in vivo, as it robustly induces the release of TNF-ɑ, IL-1β, NO, and ROS in microglial cells [ , ]. Importantly, amoeboid morphological transformation and motility represent the common prerequisite for microglial inflammatory responses. Therefore, we selected LPS as an in vitro inflammatory model to investigate whether and how Tincr is involved in microglial deformation and activation. Consistent with previous studies [ , ], we observed that LPS stimulation markedly activated BV2 microglia cells, as evidenced by increased iNOS expression, decreased Arg-1 levels, and elevated production of pro-inflammatory cytokines MCP-1, IL-1β, TNFα, and IL-6 in BV2 cells (Fig. S3A, B). Importantly, LPS intervention significantly downregulated Tincr expression in BV2 cells (Fig. A), suggesting the possibility of the interaction between microglial activation and Tincr. 
To further clarify the relationship, we designed two siRNA sequences targeting Tincr at position 1435−1455 bp (si-Tincr #1) and 1633–1653 bp (si-Tincr #2). Transfection of these siRNAs effectively reduced Tincr expression in BV2 cells (Fig. S3C). Remarkably, Tincr knockdown significantly increased the expression of pro-inflammatory markers such as iNOS (Fig. B), MCP-1, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Fig. C), while simultaneously decreasing anti-inflammatory factors such as Arg-1 (Fig. B), TGF-β (Fig. C), reinforcing its anti-inflammatory role.
As microglial migration to damaged sites is essential for initiating phagocytosis and debris clearance [ ], we next examined the effect of Tincr on microglial motility using a boyden chamber assay with CCL-2 (MCP-1) as the chemoattractant. Silencing Tincr enhancing the migratory ability of microglia (Fig. D), and this effect persisted across conditions with or without blebbistatin, indicating a regulatory role in cytoskeletal dynamics.
Pro-inflammatory microglia are often characterized by an amoeboid morphology, reflecting enhanced phagocytic capacity [ ]. Using fluorescent latex beads (green color) to assess phagocytic ability, we found that LPS treatment increased bead uptake in BV2 cells, and Tincr knockdown resulted in even greater uptake (yellow signal overlap), suggesting a more pronounced enhancement of phagocytosis (Fig. E). This was corroborated by increased CD68 positive signals (pro-phagocytic) and decreased CD206 (anti-inflammatory) fluorescence intensity following si-Tincr transfection, mimicking the changes induced by LPS (Fig. F). Similar results were reproduced in primary cultured microglia (Fig. S3D-F).
Importantly, overexpression of Tincr via transfection with a Tincr- overexpressing plasmids reversed LPS-induced increase in phagocytic activity and cell bodies swelling (Fig. G, H). Furthermore, Tincr overexpression attenuated the LPS-induced increase in CD68 and reduction in CD206 fluorescence intensity (Fig. I, J). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Tincr downregulation contributes to microglial morphological remodeling and activation, highlighting its potential role as a negative regulator of neuroinflammation.

## Tincr regulates microglial deformation dependent on MYPT1-mediated phosphorylation of NM IIA

## NM IIA phosphorylation is involved in Tincr-regulated microglial deformation
To elucidate how Tincr influences morphological changes in microglia, we turned our attention to the cytoskeletal regulator non-muscle myosin IIA (NM IIA). Previous studies have shown that dynamic phosphorylation of NM IIA is essential for cytoskeletal rearrangement and cellular deformation [ , ]. Interestingly, although the total protein levels of NM IIA remained unchanged in the hippocampi of 2VO rats evaluated by western blot, co-IP revealed significantly increased phosphorylation of NM IIA (Fig. A). A similar pattern was observed in BV2 microglial cells following LPS stimulation (Fig. B) and Tincr knockdown (Fig. C). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed that NM IIA protein redistributed across the cytoplasm, accompanied by marked morphological changes and enlargement of cell body in BV2 cells and primary cultured microglia after either LPS or si-Tincr treatment (Fig. D, E; Fig. S4A, B). As predicted, overexpression of Tincr restored LPS-induced increased phosphorylation of NM IIA without affecting it’s protein levels (Fig. F), which was confirmed by immunofluorescence evaluation (Fig. G). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that NM IIA phosphorylation is involved in Tincr-induced microglial morphological remodeling and activation. And CCH-induced microglial deformation might be associated with enhanced phosphorylation rather than increased expression of NM IIA. 

## Tincr controls NM IIA phosphorylation by regulating MYPT1 expression
Phosphorylation of NM IIA protein is primarily mediated through its regulatory light chain (RLC), controlled by myosin phosphatase, a trimeric complex consisting of myosin phosphatase-targeting subunit 1 (MYPT1; also known as PPP1R12A), protein phosphatase 1c (PP1c), and an adaptor module (AM). Among these, MYPT1 plays a pivotal role in determining myosin phosphatase activity [ , ].
To investigate why NM IIA phosphorylation was elevated upon Tincr loss-of-function, we examined MYPT1 and PP1c protein levels by western blot. Compared with NC group, we found MYPT1 protein was significantly reduced in si-Tincr-treated BV2 cells, while PP1c remained unchanged (Fig. H). Co-IP assays further revealed decreased MYPT1-PP1c binding in the si-Tincr group compared to NC group, indicating impaired myosin phosphatase activity. Immunofluorescence showed diminished MYPT1 signal intensity and altered peripheral distribution in BV2 cells after Tincr knockdown (Fig. I), resembling the distribution of NM IIA (Fig. E). Furthermore, we found that Mypt1 mRNA levels were significantly reduced following LPS stimulation and restored by Tincr overexpression (Fig. J). Moreover, in LPS-treated BV2 cells, decreased MYPT1 protein, unchanged PP1c protein expression, and reduced MYPT1-PP1c binding, were all restored by co-transfection of Tincr plasmids (Fig. K). These findings were further validated by immunofluorescence (Fig. L), supporting the notion that Tincr regulates NM IIA phosphorylation through MYPT1-dependent myosin phosphatase activity.
These findings indicate that MYPT1 loss-of-function may critically contribute to Tincr-mediated inflammatory responses associated with CCH, likely through its effects on microglial morphological remodeling and activation.

## Downregulation of MYPT1 triggers microglial deformation and activation
To further determine whether MYPT1 is a key molecular mediator of Tincr's effects on microglial deformation, we designed two siRNA sequences targeting Mypt1 at position 599 −617 bp (si-Mypt1 #1) and 2444–2462 bp (si-Mypt1 #2) (Fig. S5A). Transfection of these siRNAs significantly reduced MYPT1 protein expression without affecting of NM IIA or PP1c protein levels, as assessed by western blotting (Fig. A). Co-IP experiments revealed that loss-of-founction of MYPT1 led to increased phosphorylation of NM IIA and diminished MYPT1-PP1c bindings (Fig. A). Strikingly, immunofluorescence further demonstrated that MYPT1 knockdown not only decreased MYPT1 signal intensity (Fig. B), but also caused NM IIA to redistribute diffusely throughout the cytoplasm (Fig. C), accompanied by enlarged BV2 cell bodies and evident morphological deformation (Fig. B, C). 
To confirm the protective role of MYPT1 on LPS-stimulated inflammatory responses and microglial morphological deformation, we overexpressed MYPT1 in LPS-treated BV2 cells (Fig. S5B, C). We observed that MYPT1 overexpression not only restored its own expression levels by western blotting, but also reversed LPS-induced phosphorylation of NM IIA and restored MYPT1-PP1c interaction, as shown by Co-IP (Fig. D). Immunofluorescence analysis indicated that MYPT1 overexpression not only promoted the central aggregation of NM IIA protein, but also rescued the LPS-induced microglial morphological deformation (Fig. E). These results suggest that loss-function-of MYPT1 results microglial deformation, while MYPT1 upregulation prevents LPS-induced microglial remodeling.
We next examined whether MYPT1-mediated morphological changes are associated with microglial functional alterations. Following MYPT1 knockdown in BV2 cells, western blotting showed increased iNOS and p-PKC levels (markers of post-inflammatory microglial activationt) (Fig. F). ELISA revealed elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (MCP-1, IL-1β, IL-6) and a reduction in the anti-inflammatory cytokine TGF-β (Fig. G). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed that MYPT1 knockdown increased CD68 and decreased CD206 fluorescence intensity in both BV2 cells (Fig. H) and primary cultured microlia (Fig. S5D, E). Conversely, MYPT1 overexpression effectively blocked LPS-induced upregulation of CD68 and downregulation of CD206, and cell swelling in BV2 cells (Fig. I). Moreover, MYPT1 knockdown led to an increase in microglial phagocytic activity (Fig. J), while MYPT1 overexpression attenuated the LPS-induced enhancement of microglial phagocytosis (Fig. K).

## Tincr-induced microglial deformation and activation dependent on MYPT1 protein expression
To further determine whether Tincr downregulation-induced microglial deformation and activation are dependent on MYPT1 expression, we transfected Mypt1 gene into si-Tincr-treated BV2 cells. Tincr knockdown significantly suppressed Mypt1 gene levels, whereas overexpression of MYPT1 did not alter the Tincr expression reduced by si-Tincr (Fig. S6A). As expected, co-transfection of Mypt1 gene with si-Tincr plasmids markedly rescued the si-Tincr-induced reduction in MYPT1 protein levels, restored MYPT1-PP1c binding, and reversed the increase in NM IIA phosphorylation, without affecting total NM IIA protein level (Fig. A). Immunofluorescence analysis further demonstrated that, MYPT1 overexpression in si-Tincr-treated BV2 cells significantly attenuated lower expression of Tincr knockdown-induced morphological enlargement of microglia and restored the subcellular distribution of MYPT1 and NM IIA in both BV2 and primary cultured microglial cells (Fig. B, C; Fig. S6B, C). Notably, MYPT1 and NM IIA proteins were redistributed toward the nucleus following MYPT1 overexpression (Fig. B; Fig. S6C). 
Importantly, upregulation of MYPT1 in si-Tincr-treated BV2 significantly reduced the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines MCP-1, IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and while increasing the anti-inflammatory cytokine TGF-β, as detected by ELISA (Fig. D). Moreover, MYPT1 overexpression decreased CD68 and increased CD206 immunoreactivity (Fig. E, F; Fig. S6D) and suppressed microglial motility and phagocytic activity (Fig. G, H).
Collectively, these findings indicate that Tincr regulates microglial deformation and activation through a mechanism dependent on MYPT1-mediated phosphorylation of NM IIA.

## Upregulating Tincr and MYPT1 reverse CCH-induced cognitive decline by blocking microglial deformation and activation

## Upregulating Tincr and MYPT1 alleviate CCH-induced cognitive decline and inflammatory responses
We next sought to investigate whether the Tincr-MYPT1 axis serves as a potential therapeutic target for CCH-induced cognitive decline and neuroinflammation. Consistent with the downregulation of Tincr (Fig. I; Fig. A; Fig. S2G), we found that, both mRNA and protein levels of MYPT1 were significantly decreased in hippocampal microglia of 2VO rats and 5xFAD (Fig. A-D, Fig. S7A), and also suppressed by LPS treatment (Fig. S7B-D). Perticularly, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that in the hippocampi of 2VO rats, reduced MYPT1 fluorescence intensity was accompanied by a redistribution of MYPT1 protein toward the cell periphery (Fig. D), a pattern that was similarly observed in LPS-treated microglia in vitro(Fig. S7B). 
More importantly, analysis of human hippocampal tissue from AD autopsy samples and Allen Brain Atlas revealed that both MYPT1 mRNA and protein levels were significantly reduced in AD patients compared with age-matched healthy controls (Fig. E, F; Table S5; Fig. S7E; Table S6). Similarly, MYPT1 mRNA expression was markedly downregulated in the peripheral blood of AD patients relative to age-matched healthy controls (Fig. G). These results highlight the clinical relevance of MYPT1 downregulation, paralleling that of Tincr, and support the therapeutic potential of targeting the Tincr-MYPT1 pathway in AD and CCH-related pathologies.
We next investigated the action of Tincr and MYPT1 on CCH-induced cognitive decline and microglial inflammatory responses by directly injecting AAV-CMV-Tincr-WPRE or AAV-CMV-Mypt1-tWPA viruses into the bilateral hippocampal CA1 region of 2VO rats, separately (Fig. H). Injection of neither AAV-CMV-Tincr-WPRE or AAV-CMV-Mypt1-tWPA viruses into hippocampi affected CCH-induced CBF decline (Fig. S7F), however, in MWM test, they both notably reduced swimming latency to the target platform in all quadrants during the cued learning trial (Fig. S7G), and significantly increased platform crossings, swimming distance and time in target quadrant relative to untreated 2VO counterparts (Fig. I-K) with no significant differences in swimming speed among groups (Fig. S7H).
qRT-PCR analysis showed that AAV-CMV-Tincr-WPRE injection upregulated not only its own expression but also improved 2VO-induced reduction of Mypt1 mRNA level, however, AAV-CMV-Mypt1-tWPA injection did not affect 2VO-induced low expression of Tincr although it reversed its own reduction (Fig. L-M). As predicted, both AAV-CMV-Tincr-WPRE and AAV-CMV-Mypt1-tWPA injection reversed 2VO-induced reduction of MYPT1 without affection on NMIIA and PP1c (Fig. N; Fig. S7I). Moreover, both injections reversed 2VO-induced phosphorylation of NM IIA and improved the weakened MYPT1-PP1c binding (Fig. O). Importantly, upregulation of both Tincr and MYPT1 alleviated 2VO-induced high CD68 signal intensity and enlarged cell bodies (Fig. P). These findings suggest that the functional enhancement of Tincr and MYPT1 can not only improves CCH-induced cognitive decline but also alleviate microglial deformation and activation.

## Upregulating Tincr and MYPT1 in microglia of hippocampi alleviate cognitive decline of 5xFAD mice
To experimentally validate the biological functions of the Tincr-MYPT1 axis in microglia, we selectively increased Tincr expression and silenced MYPT1 in mouse microglia by stereotactically delivering pAAV-CMV-DIO-Tincr-WPRE and pAAV-CBG-DIO-miR30shRNA(Mypt1)-WPRE viral vectors into the bilateral hippocampi of TMEM119CreERT2 mice on a 5xFAD background. To activate Cre recombination, 5-month-old mice received tamoxifen (75 mg/kg, intragastrically) one day prior to and for five consecutive days following viral injection. Behavioral assessments and pathological analyses were subsequently conducted. Robust conditional expression was observed, with significantly elevated levels of Tincr and MYPT1 specifically in hippocampal microglia, but not neurons, of 5FTT (5xFAD; TMEM119CreERT2; injected with pAAV-CMV-DIO-Tincr-WPRE) compared to the 5FT (5xFAD; TMEM119CreERT2) (Fig. A, B; Fig. S8A-C). Conversely, MYPT1 levels were markedly reduced in hippocampal microglia, remained unchanged in neurons, in the 5FTTShM (5xFAD; TMEM119CreERT2; co-injected with pAAV-CMV-DIO-Tincr-WPRE and pAAV-CBG-DIO-miR30shRNA(Mypt1)-WPRE), compared to the 5FTT group, without affecting Tincr expression (Fig. A, B; Fig. S8A-C). 
Barnes maze testing revealed that Tincr overexpression in microglial significantly reduced the number of errors (Fig. C-E). However, co-expression with sh-Mypt1 abolished this improvement, resulting in increased error times compared to the 5FTT group, suggesting that MYPT1 knockdown in microglia impairs spatial memory, a deficit that is rescued by Tincr upregulation (Fig. D-E). Immunofluorescence further confirmed that a marked increase in MYPT1 fluorescence intensity in Iba-1+ microglia in 5FTT mice (Fig. S8B), accompanied by decreased CD68 expression and smaller microglial soma diameter (Fig. F). These changes were reversed following sh-Mypt1 injection (Fig. S8B; Fig. F). Co-IP analyses further showed that MYPT1 knockdown in Tincr-overexpressing microglia significantly reduced both MYPT1 expression and its interaction with PP1c, while concurrently increasing NM IIA phosphorylation (Fig. B). These findings suggest that the functional enhancement of Tincr in microglial cells can alleviate cognitive impairment, improved morphological remodeling and functionality of microglial cells in vivo, which may attributed to the direct targeting of MYPT1 by Tincr.
In summary, upregulating Tincr and MYPT1 in microglia plays a pivotal role in reversing CCH-induced cognitive impairment by promoting beneficial microglial remodeling and reducing neuroinflammation.

## Tincr regulates MYPT1 expression by dual-mechanism way

## Tincr upregulates the expression of MYPT1 via the ceRNA model by sponging miR-153-3p
To investigate the mechanism by which Tincr regulates MYPT1 expression, we first examined the subcellular localization of Tincr using the lncLocator database (Fig. A) and validated the results in BV2 cells (Fig. B). Both analyses indicated that Tincr is predominantly localized in the cytoplasm. Knockdown of Tincr in BV2 cells via transfection with si-Tincr plasmids significantly reduced both Mypt1 mRNA (Fig. S9A) and protein levels compared to negative control (Fig. H), suggesting that Tincr may regulate MYPT1 through a competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) way [ ]. 
Using the TargetScan and StarBase databases, we initially identified 39 miRNAs predicted to bind to the 3'-UTR of Mypt1 mRNA (Fig. C; Table S7). We then cross-referenced these 39 miRNAs with 207 miRNAs upregulated in the hippocampi of 2VO rats in the GEO database GSE142087 (Table S7), resulting in 12 overlapping candidate miRNAs (Fig. C; Table S8). To further narrow down potential Tincr–miRNA interactions, we utilized the RNAhybrid database to evaluate both the number of binding sites and binding free energy (ΔG) between Tincr and the 12 miRNAs (Table S9). The four miRNAs with the highest absolute ΔG values, miR-153-3p, miR-186-5p, miR-23b-3p and miR-23a-3p were selected for further analysis (Table S9). Among them, knockdown of Tincr resulted in a most significant upregulation of miR-153-3p, compared with the others three miRNAs, supporting the hypothesis that Tincr may act as a molecular sponge for miR-153-3p
(Fig. D). Ultimately, miR-153-3p was selected for the following experiments.
Our previous research demonstrated that CCH increases hippocampal levels of miR-153-3p, leading to impaired presynaptic neurotransmitter release [ ]. This finding is reaffirmed in the current study (Fig. E). Notably, we found that Tincr overexpression further decreased miR-153-3p expression in the hippocampi of 2VO rats (Fig. E). Moreover, transfection of miR-153-3p mimic significantly upregulated miR-153-3p levels while simultaneously downregulating Tincr expression, whereas this effect was reversed upon transfection with miR-153-3p inhibitors (Fig. F, G), supporting the hypothesis that Tincr may function as a molecular sponge for miR-153-3p.
To further explore this interaction, we employed the RegRNA2.0 database and identified three putative binding sites for miR-153-3p within the Tincr sequence 974 bp-982 bp (site 1: Tincr: ACUAUGCA; miR-153-3p: UGAUACGU), 1150 bp-1156 bp (site 2: Tincr: UAUGCA; miR-153-3p sequence: AUACGUU) and 792 bp-799 bp (site 3: Tincr: CUAUGUGA; miR-153-3p sequence: GAUACGUU) (Fig. H; Fig. S9B). Dual-luciferase reporter assays revealed that miR-153-3p mimics significantly reduced luciferase activity when co-transfected with wild-type Tincr constructs. This suppressive effect was abolished upon mutation of binding site 1, but not sites 2 or 3, indicating that site 1 is the primary interaction site (Fig. H; Fig. S9B).
Functionally, miR-153-3p mimics significantly suppressed both Mypt1 mRNA and MYPT1 protein levels, effects that were reversed by co-transfection with miR-153-3p antisense oligonucleotides (Fig. I, J). Furthermore, combining Tincr knockdown with miR-153-3p inhibition partially restored MYPT1 protein levels (Fig. J). TargetScan analysis revealed that miR-153-3p (sequence: AUACGU) has a conserved binding site not only on Tincr but also within the 3’-UTR of Mypt1 mRNA (sequence: UAUGCA)(Fig. K). Consistently, dual-luciferase assays confirmed that miR-153-3p mimics significantly suppressed luciferase activity of the wild-type Mypt1 3′-UTR reporter, while this effect was abolished upon mutation of the binding site (Fig. L). Co-IP experiments showed that inhibition of miR-153-3p effectively prevented the si-Tincr-induced reduction in MYPT1 protein levels, rescued the disrupted MYPT1–PP1c interaction, and normalized the elevated phosphorylation of NM IIA(Fig. M).
Collectively, these findings support a ceRNA-based regulatory mechanism in which Tincr modulates MYPT1 expression by competitively binding to miR-153-3p, thereby preventing its suppressive effects on Mypt1 translation.

## Tincr regulates MYPT1 expression through RNA–protein interaction patterns
Utilizing predictions from HEX 8.0, we identified a direct binding interaction between Tincr and the MYPT1 protein (Fig. S9C). To further validate this interaction, molecular docking was performed using the HDOCK plateform, yielding 100 potential binding conformations. Among these, we prioritized the top 10 ranked conformations for detailed evaluation of binding quality (Table S10). Following comprehensive statistical analyses, the highest-ranked docking conformation between Tincr and MYPT1 proteins was selected for further investigation, with a docking score of −294.89 kcal/mol and a confidence score of 0.9477 (Table S10). Based on computational modelling analyses, we further explored the molecular basis of this interaction (Fig. N). The results demonstrated clear evidence of MYPT1 and Tincr binding mediated by multiple amino acids—nucleotide contacts primarily through Van der Waalsand hydrogen bonds contacts. At the S1 site (Fig. N), amino acid residue Asp165 and Arg175 of MYPT1 formed a hydrogen bond with RNA base U1192 and G1050 of Tincr, respectivily; and amino acid residue Arg182 of MYPT1 simultaneously bond with RNA base A1051 and C1191 of Tincr; in addition, amino acid residue Leu168 engaged in Van der Waals contacts with RNA base U1188. At the S2 site, amino acid residue Lys192 of MYPT1 formed a hydrogen bond with RNA base A221 of Tincr while Gln915 and Glu911 of MYPT1 interacted with C1272 of Tincr (Fig. N). At the S3 site, the amino acid residue Gln884 of MYPT1 formed a hydrogen bond with the RNA base C74 of Tincr (Fig. N). These in silico results indicate that Tincr—MYPT1 binding is stabilized by a multifactorial network of non-covalent interactions.
Consistent with these computational findings, RNA binding protein immunoprecipitation assays confirmed significant enrichment of Tincr in the MYPT1 group compared with the IgG control group (Fig. O). Consistently, in Tincr-depleted cells treated with cycloheximide (CHX), the half-life of MYPT1 protein was markedly reduced (Fig. P), further validating Tincr’s role in stabilizing MYPT1.

## Discussion
Sustained release of inflammatory cytokines by activated microglia at lesion sites is recognized as a hallmark of AD. However, previous studies have demonstrated that CCH induces microglial release of pro-inflammatory cytokines through multiple distinct mechanisms [ – , ], suggesting that targeting a single pathway may be insufficient to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes. In the present study, we provide the first evidence that microglial cells in the hippocampi of 2VO rats undergo marked cellular reorganization. Mechanistically, reduced Tincr expression emerged as a key regulator of CCH-induced morphological development of DAMs, acting through MYPT1 downregulation to promote NM IIA the dephosphorylation via both miR-153-3p sponging and RNA–protein interactions.Given that this pathway critically governs microglial cytokine release, our findings identify dysfunction of the Tincr-MYPT1-NM IIA axis axis as a novel mechanism underlying microglial deformation and dysfunction in CCH, thereby highlighting it as a potential therapeutic target for AD.
In the adult CNS, microglia maintain homeostasis through immunosurveillance, displaying a ramified morphology with long protrusions that scan the microenvironment while showing minimal phagocytic activity. In response to injury or disease, they become activated, adopt an amoeboid form, migrate to the lesion site, and releases pro-inflammatory cytokines [ ]. Notebly, although the stimuli that activate microglia may differ, the release of pro-inflammatory factors consistently depends on cytoskeletal reorganization, with NM IIA playing a pivotal role in this process [ ], as well as amoeboid-like morphological transformation and migration [ , ], highlighting the indispensable role of structural proteins in microglial biology. Previous studies have reported that the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines from microglia through various mechanisms, including activation of the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling pathway, inflammasome activation, and elevated CD73 expression [ – , ].In the present study, we provide new evidence that CCH directly contribute to microglial morphological transformation by regulating the phosphorylation of the structural protein NM IIA which was controlled by protein levels of MYPT1. We found that 2VO-induced CCH induced downregulation of MYPT1 protein that promoted NM IIA phosphorylation and cytoplasmic redistribution leading to amoeboid-like morphological changes, whereas dephosphorylation of NM IIA through upregulating MYPT1 not only prevented CCH-induced microglial remodelling but also suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines release and ameliorated cognitive decline in 2VO rats. More importantly, this phonomenon was recapitulate in 5xFAD mice and LPS-induced microglial cells. These results suggest that targeting structural proteins may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for developing drugs against neuroinflammatory mechanisms.
LncRNAs are increasingly recognized as key regulators in various neurodegenerative diseases [ ], with studies showing their crucial role in modulating the pro-inflammatory phenotype of microglia [ , ]. Our previous work revealed that the lncRNA Tincr regulates proliferation and migration in breast cancer by modulating PD-L1 expression [ ]. Intriguingly, Tincr colocalizes with multiple immune response genes, including CCL2, CCL4, CCL7, CSF3, CXCL5, IL-33, S100A8, and S100A9 [ ], all of which are implicated in neuroimmunogenesis in AD. However, whether Tincr is involved in CCH-induced microglial dysfunction has remained largely unexplored. In this study, we found that 2VO-induced CCH downregulated Tincr expression, a finding recapitulated in 5xFAD mice, LPS-stimulated microglial cells, and the hippocampi of AD patients. Notably, Tincr knockdown not only enhanced pro-inflammatory cytokine release but also induced microglial deformation accompanied by increased phosphorylation of NM IIA, whereas Tincr upregulation reversed these pathological changes in the hippocampi of 2VO rats, 5xFAD mice, and LPS-treated BV2 cells, while also ameliorating cognitive decline in both 2VO and 5xFAD models. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Tincr contributes to CCH-induced cognitive decline by driving microglial remodeling.
Typically, lncRNAs modulate biological processes by interacting with chromatin DNA, RNA, or proteins, thereby influencing chromatin accessibility, RNA stability, and protein functioneither a cis- or trans-manner [ , ]. In the present study, using a battery of approaches, including bioinformatics, dual-luciferase reporter assays, gene manipulation, co-immunoprecipitation and immunofluorescence, we identified that Tincr regulates microglial deformation by inhibiting NM IIA phosphorylation through both ceRNA activity and RNA–protein interactions..Cytoplasmic lncRNAs often act as ceRNAs by competing for miRNA response elements (MREs), thereby attenuating miRNA-mediated suppression of target genes [ ]. Consistently, we found that cytoplasmic Tincr acts as a ceRNA to upregulate MYPT1 expression by sponging miR-153-3p, preventing its post-transcriptional inhibition of MYPT1 and thus suppressing NM IIA phosphorylation. In parallel, Tincr directly binds to MYPT1 protein through a multifactorial network of non-covalent interactions,stabilizing its expression (Fig. S10). Taken together, our study provides the first evidence that Tincr enhances MYPT1 expression via dual mechanisms: RNA-RNA and RNA–protein interactions, thereby filling a conceptual gap in lncRNA regulation and offering new insight into microglial dysfunction in dementia.
Recently, increasing evidence suggests that microglial states should be viewed as a dynamic concept, constantly adapting to instantaneous signals from the CNS microenvironment [ ]. This continuous sensory responsiveness results in diverse transcriptional signatures across different stages of AD progression [ ]. While the present study primarily focuses on morphological alterations of microglia in CCH, the dynamics of their interactions with other cell types remain to be clarified. A pressing challenge for future research is distinguishing microglia from other brain macrophages in terms of identity and function. Although recent studies have revealed a unique intrinsic core signature of microglia, their functional overlap and divergence with other brain macrophages are not yet fully understood. Moreover, questions persist regarding the origins of distinct microglial states-specifically, how environmental signals in the CNS are integrated to drive these state transitions. Addressing these questions will require the development of advanced imaging tools and molecular reporters capable of tracking and manipulating microglial states in vivo. Such technological progress will be crucial for deepening our understanding of microglial biology and their roles in CNS health and disease.In addition, this study focuses on elucidating the role of lncRNAs in CCH-induced microglial deformation and cognitive decline. However, whether screening target molecules from mRNA or proteomic data could reveal novel candidates remains an open question and warrants further investigation.
