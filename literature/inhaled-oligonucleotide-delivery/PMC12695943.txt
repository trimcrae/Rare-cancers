Although molecular targeted therapies and immunotherapies have advanced the treatment landscape , , patient responses remain highly variable, largely due to tumor microenvironment (TME) heterogeneity , . The TME, comprising immune cells, stromal cells, extracellular matrix, and soluble factors, critically shapes disease progression and therapeutic response , . In particular, immune infiltration within the TME strongly influences prognosis and underpins the success of immunotherapeutic approaches such as immune checkpoint blockade . However, BC has exhibited comparatively limited responsiveness to immunotherapy relative to other solid tumors, which has been partly ascribed to immunosuppressive cell populations and metabolic adaptations within the TME , . Therefore, elucidating the crosstalk between tumor metabolism and immune regulation is crucial for enhancing clinical outcomes and paving the way for more effective immunotherapeutic approaches in BC.
The importance of glycolytic metabolism in regulating immune landscape and therapeutic response has been systematically reviewed in recent reports , . A hallmark of cancer cell metabolism is aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect), wherein tumor cells preferentially convert glucose to lactate even under normoxic conditions . This metabolic reprogramming not only fuels rapid proliferation but also profoundly remodels the TME through nutrient depletion and accumulation of metabolites such as lactate, which can suppress immune cell activation, proliferation, and cytotoxic function . Across multiple cancer types, studies have consistently linked heightened glycolytic activity with the establishment of an immunosuppressive TME that promotes immune evasion . For instance, elevated glycolysis is associated with reduced infiltration of cytotoxic T cells and expansion of immunosuppressive populations, including regulatory T cells and M2-polarized macrophages , . Lactate, as a major glycolytic byproduct, has been shown to impede dendritic cell maturation, natural killer cell cytotoxicity, and effector T cell activity – . Moreover, metabolic competition for glucose between tumor and immune cells represents a critical mechanism that constrains antitumor immunity .
BNIP3 (BCL2/adenovirus E1B 19 kDa interacting protein 3) is a mitochondrial pro-apoptotic protein belonging to the Bcl-2 family, known to regulate apoptosis and autophagy under hypoxic and metabolic stress. Recent evidence has highlighted its potential as a prognostic and diagnostic biomarker in several solid tumors . Our analysis of the DISEASES database also revealed a strong association between BNIP3 and cancer, including breast disease (Supplementary Fig. ), prompting us to investigate its role in BC. Although BNIP3 has been implicated in BC and other malignancies, its function appears context-dependent , with reports suggesting both tumor-suppressive and tumor-promoting roles. For example, low BNIP3 expression correlates with enhanced tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis in certain cohorts , , while its overexpression has been linked to increased recurrence and therapy resistance in others , . Interestingly, emerging studies in other cancers suggest that BNIP3 may participate in metabolic reprogramming and immune regulation . However, most research to date has centered on its canonical roles in cell death and autophagy, leaving its influence on tumor glycolysis and the immune microenvironment poorly understood.
In summary, the crosstalk between glycolytic metabolism and immune landscape remodeling remains an under-explored area in BC biology. This study aims to comprehensively characterize the role of BNIP3 in BC, specifically evaluating its impact on glucose metabolism, immune infiltration, and clinical outcomes. Elucidating BNIP3-driven mechanisms may provide new biomarkers and therapeutic strategies to improve immunotherapy efficacy in BC.

## Materials and methods

## Data acquisition
Clinical and patient data from BC were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). The TCGA-BRCA dataset included 1207 BC samples and 113 normal tissues (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). The associated clinical markers included pathologic stage, TNM stage, patient age, and gender. The gene expression data used in this study were specifically sourced from the Breast Invasive Carcinoma (BRCA) project within the TCGA database, accessed and downloaded in December 2023.

## Survival analysis and diagnostic assessment
Utilizing data from TCGA, patients with BC were categorized into high and low BNIP3 expression groups based on the median BNIP3 expression level. The relationship between BNIP3 expression and survival outcomes in BC was assessed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and log-rank tests. The Kaplan‐Meier (KM) plot for survival curve analysis was performed using TCGA datasets from GEPIA . Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were generated using the “pROC” and “timeROC” R packages. A Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was conducted, incorporating variables such as TNM stage, patient age, gender, and BNIP3 expression levels. All analyses were performed in R, with the package pROC used to generate ROC curves and calculate AUC values. ROC curves plot sensitivity versus 1 − specificity across probability thresholds ranging from 0.0 to 1.0. The AUC represents the area under the ROC curve, where a value of 1.0 indicates perfect prognostic accuracy of BNIP3, and a value of 0.5 indicates performance no better than random. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated for each group, and P-values for the log-rank test are indicated.

## Enrichment analysis
The package “ClusterProfiler” was used for gene ontology (GO) enrichment and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis of BNIP3-related genes. We used GSEA software and analyzed the differences in signaling pathways between the high BNIP3 and low BNIP3 groups in BC. P < 0.05 was considered significant.

## Analysis of immune cell infiltration
The estimation of stromal and immune cell infiltration in malignant tumor tissues was performed using the STromal and Immune cells Estimation (ESTIMATE) . Wilcoxon’s test was utilized to compare differences between BNIP3 expression levels and tumor microenvironment (TME) groups. The Cell-type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets Of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) R script quantified the relative proportions of infiltrating immune cells. We subsequently plotted box plots for 22 immune cell types and created correlation lollipop plots using the Spearman method.

## Tumor mutation profiles and drug sensitivity screening
The TCGA-BRCA Somatic mutation datawere collected from the UCSC Xena (https://gdc.xenahubs.net), and the expression information of BNIP3 was extracted, and the samples were divided into high-expression and low-expression groups according to the median value. The R package maftools was used to determine the significantly mutated genes and build a somatic mutation landscape between the low- and high-risk groups. The Tumor mutation burden (TMB) was calculated using “maftools”.
We first downloaded the IC50 values for a number of drugs from Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC; https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) . The R package “pRRophetic” was utilized to predict the drug IC50 in the different BNIP3 risk groups. This algorithm can calculate the IC50 of chemotherapeutic drugs according to the gene expression matrix information and then accurately predict drug sensitivity . The mean IC50 values for each chemotherapeutic drug were calculated in both BNIP3 expression groups. The IC50 values between the two groups were compared using the Wilcoxon Rank Sum test, with a significance threshold set at 0.05.

## Collection of patient samples
Tumor specimens (10 breast cancer tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues) were collected from patients at Changzhou Cancer Hospital. The specific case information can be found in Supplementary Table . The research protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Changzhou Cancer Hospital (Approval No. 2024(SR)004). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional committee and with the Declaration of Helsinki.​Written informed consent was obtained from all participating patients prior to specimen collection.

## Immunohistochemistry staining
Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to evaluate BNIP3 expression in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections from BC patients and matched normal controls. Tissue sections (4–5 μm thick) were prepared and baked at 60 °C for adhesion. Antigen retrieval was conducted using citrate buffer (pH 6.0), followed by blocking of endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against human BNIP3 (1:200 dilution; Cat# BA0897, Boster Biological Technology). After washing, sections were incubated with a secondary HRP-conjugated antibody (Cat# SA0001, Boster Biological Technology) for 1 h at room temperature, visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB; Cat# D001, Solarbio Life Sciences), and counterstained with hematoxylin (Cat# G1010, Solarbio Life Sciences). The staining intensity and percentage of positive cells were scored independently by two pathologists blinded to the clinical data. A semiquantitative scoring system was applied based on two parameters: the proportion of positive cells (scored as 1: 0–24%; 2: 25–49%; 3: 50–74%; 4: 75–100%) and the staining intensity (scored as 0: no staining; 1: weak; 2: moderate; 3: strong) . The final immunohistochemical score was calculated by combining these two scores; specifically, a combined score of ≥ 4 was defined as positive staining, while scores below this threshold were considered negative . Negative controls, in which the primary antibody was omitted, were included to confirm staining specificity.

## Cell culture and plasmid construction
BC cell lines MCF-7 (#YC-D006, Ubigene, China) and MDA-MB-231 (#YC-D005, Ubigene) were received for the study. L-15 media with 10% FBS (Catalog Number: UBG-12345) was used to sustain the cells. The cultures were incubated in a humidified environment with 5% CO2 at 37 °C.
To construct cell lines that stably knockdown BNIP3 expression, we selected shRNA lentiviral particle (item No.: GKLV05947) for human BNIP3 gene and corresponding non-targeted control shRNA lentiviral particle (item No.: GKLV00001) provided by Shanghai Chemical Technology Co., LTD. Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfecting 10 µg of either shBNIP3 or shCtrl plasmid together with the packaging plasmids psPAX2 (7.5 µg) and pMD2.G (2.5 µg) into HEK293T cells using the calcium phosphate precipitation method. The viral supernatant was collected 48 h post-transfection, filtered through a 0.45 µm PVDF filter, and used immediately for infection or stored at − 80 °C. For infection, MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well. Upon reaching 60–70% confluence, the culture medium was replaced with 1 mL of fresh viral supernatant containing 8 µg/mL Polybrene (Sigma-Aldrich). After 24 h of incubation, the viral supernatant was removed and replaced with complete growth medium. After 72 h of post-infection, the transduced cells were subjected to selection using 2 µg/mL puromycin (InvivoGen) for MCF-7 cells and 1.5 µg/mL puromycin for MDA-MB-231 cells. The selection medium was changed every 2 days until all cells in the non-infected control plate had died (approximately 7–10 days). The resulting polyclonal populations of puromycin-resistant cells were then used for subsequent experiments. The knockdown efficiency at both the mRNA and protein levels was confirmed in the cell linesby qRT-PCR and Western blot.

## qRT-PCR
Total RNA was isolated from BC cell lines (MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231) or BC tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, #15596026). Briefly, cells or homogenized tissues were lysed with 1 mL of TRIzol. After adding 200 µL of chloroform and phase separation by centrifugation, the aqueous RNA-containing phase was transferred to a new tube. RNA was precipitated with an equal volume of isopropyl alcohol, washed with 75% ethanol, and finally dissolved in nuclease-free water. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.9 and 2.1 were used for subsequent analysis. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the PrimeScript™ RT kit and gDNA Eraser (Takara Bio Inc., Kusatsu, Japan), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. This two-step process included a 2-min incubation at 42 °C with gDNA Eraser to remove genomic DNA contamination, followed by reverse transcription. qRT-PCR was performed using the SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ kit (Takara Bio Inc.) on a ABI7500 PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each 20 µL reaction mixture contained 10 µL of SYBR Premix Ex Taq, 0.8 µL of each forward and reverse primer (10 µM), 2 µL of cDNA template, and 6.4 µL of nuclease-free water. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s; 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 34 s; followed by a melt curve analysis step (95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and 95 °C for 15 s) to confirm amplification specificity. The housekeeping gene ACTB was used as an endogenous control for normalization. The relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the comparative 2−ΔΔCt method. All primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table .

## Western blot
Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The protein concentration was quantified with a BCA assay kit (Pierce, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-BNIP3 (Proteintech, 68091–1-Ig, 1:5000) and anti-Tubulin (Abcam, EP1332Y, 1:5000). Following incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies, immunoreactive bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Tanon, China). Tubulin was used as a loading control.

## BNIP3 knockdown by siRNA
To achieve BNIP3 knockdown, three different siRNAs (Ubigene, China) were initially tested in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells. After evaluating their effectiveness, the siRNA with the highest knockdown efficiency was selected for further experiments. MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells per well 1 day prior to transfection to ensure 70–80% confluence at the time of transfection. Cells were transfected with 50 nM of siRNAs using Lipofectamine 2000 (#11668019, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, siRNA and Lipofectamine 2000 were diluted separately in Opti-MEM® I Reduced Serum Medium (Gibco). These solutions were mixed gently and incubated for 20 min at room temperature to allow complex formation. The mixture was then added dropwise to the cells, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C in a CO2 incubator. After 48 h, the efficiency of gene knockdown was verified by qRT-PCR. A scrambled RNA was used as negative control. siBNIP3 sequence was as follows:siBNIP3-1: GGAAGATGATATTGAAAGA
siBNIP3-2: GAACTGCACTTCAGCAATA
siBNIP3-3: GGAACACGAGCGTCATGAA

## CCK-8 experiment
Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and cultured overnight to allow attachment. After treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Japan) was added to each well followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, USA). All experiments were performed in at least three independent replicates. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2, and cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to the control group.

## Animal experiment
Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Shanghai Tenth People’s Hospital Animal Experiment Ethics Committee (Approval Number: SHDSYY-2024-3031-1). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines, and were adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines to ensure ethical and methodological rigor. Ten 6-week-old female nude mice (14–16 g) were obtained from Shanghai Sipple-Bikai Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. The mice were housed under pathogen-free conditions at 22 ± 2 °C and 50% humidity. For the MDA-MB-231 xenograft tumor model, mice were randomly assigned to two groups prior to the experiment: the shNC group and the shBNIP3 group. MDA-MB-231 cells with BNIP3 knockdown (shBNIP3 cells) or negative control cells (shNC cells) (4 × 106 cells per mouse suspended in PBS) were injected subcutaneously into the right axilla of the mice. Mice were maintained for 30 days post-injection, with data collection commencing 5 days after tumor implantation. Tumor volume was estimated based on the length and width measurements. The survival of mice in each group was monitored for 20 days, and survival curves were plotted to evaluate the effect of BNIP3 knockdown on mouse survival. Anesthesia was inhaled preoperatively with 1% isoflurane. Mice were euthanized with excessive isoflurane after operation and tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed. Any deceased mice were appropriately disposed of by incineration.

## Glucose uptake assay
MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 96-well black-walled plates at a density of 8 × 103 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. To assess glucose uptake, the culture medium was replaced with glucose-free DMEM and the cells were serum-starved for 4 h. Subsequently, the cells were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with serum-free medium containing 100 µM of the fluorescent glucose analog 2-NBDG (Cayman Chemical, #11046). Following incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS to remove excess 2-NBDG. The fluorescence intensity was immediately measured using a Thermo Scientific Varioskan LUX Multimode Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with excitation and emission wavelengths set at 485 nm and 535 nm, respectively. The relative fluorescence units (RFU) were normalized to the total protein content per well, quantified by a BCA assay, and the results are expressed as a percentage of the control group.

## Lactate production measurement
Lactate levels in the cell culture supernatant were determined as an indicator of glycolytic flux. Briefly, MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at 2 × 105 cells per well. After 24 h of attachment, the cells were treated according to the experimental design for a duration of 48 h. At the end of the treatment period, the culture media were collected. The lactate concentration was quantified using a Lactate Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, MAK064) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm using the microplate reader mentioned above. The lactate concentration (nmol/µL) in each sample was calculated from a standard curve and normalized to the total cellular protein content.

## Extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) analysis
To evaluate glycolytic function, the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) was measured using a Seahorse XF96 Analyzer (Seahorse XFe96, Agilent). Cells were seeded in XF96 cell culture microplates (Agilent, #101085-004) at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and incubated overnight at 37 °C to allow for complete adhesion. Prior to the assay, the growth medium was replaced with Seahorse XF DMEM medium (pH 7.4, Agilent, #103575-100) supplemented with 10 mM glucose (Agilent, #103577-100), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Agilent, #103578-100), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Agilent, #103579-100). The cells were then incubated for 60 min in a non-CO2 incubator at 37 °C to allow for temperature and pH equilibration. The Glycolysis Stress Test was performed by sequentially injecting the following compounds through the instrument’s injection ports to achieve the indicated final concentrations: 10 mM glucose (to assess glycolytic response), 1 µM oligomycin (Agilent, #103015-100, to inhibit mitochondrial ATP production and reveal glycolytic capacity), and 50 mM 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG, Sigma-Aldrich, #D8375, to inhibit glycolysis). The ECAR (mpH/min) was measured in real-time under standard conditions (37 °C). Key glycolytic parameters, including basal glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve, were calculated from the resultant kinetic data using the Agilent Wave Desktop software (version 2.6.0). An increase in ECAR is indicative of an elevated glycolytic rate.

## Statistical analysis
Correlation coefficients were calculated using either Spearman or Pearson analysis, as appropriate. The chi-square test and Wilcoxon test were employed to evaluate differences between groups, and P-values were compared and adjusted multiple times using the Benjamini Hochberg method to control for false discovery rate (FDR). All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.0.3).

## Results

## BNIP3 expression as an independent prognostic biomarker in BC
By integrating data from the TCGA breast cancer cohort and clinically obtained BC tissue samples, we systematically evaluated the expression patterns and prognostic significance of BNIP3 in BC. Analysis of the TCGA dataset indicated that BNIP3 expression increases with patient age (Fig.  a) and is elevated in basal-like and HER2-positive subtypes compared to other BC subtypes (Fig.  b). Immunohistochemistry revealed significantly higher BNIP3 protein levels in tumor tissues than in adjacent normal breast tissues (Fig.  c). Moreover, BNIP3 expression was positively associated with advanced TNM stage, indicating higher levels in more aggressive tumors (Fig.  d). This upregulation was further confirmed at the transcriptional level by RT-qPCR, which showed increased BNIP3 mRNA expression in tumor samples relative to normal adjacent tissues (Fig.  e). Consistent with these findings, multiple BC cell lines also exhibited high BNIP3 expression (Fig.  f). 
Kaplan–Meier survival analysis indicated that high BNIP3 expression was significantly associated with reduced overall survival (OS) in BC patients (Fig.  g; P = 0.019). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis further supported the potential of BNIP3 as a prognostic biomarker (Fig.  h). Univariate Cox regression yielded a hazard ratio (HR) of 1.480 (95% CI 1.073–2.041). After multivariate adjustment for age, TNM stage, and pathological M stage, BNIP3 remained an independent prognostic factor (HR = 1.448, 95% CI 1.014–2.069), underscoring its clinical relevance (Table ). 

## BNIP3 mediates glycolysis in BC
To investigate the functional role of BNIP3 in BC and its associated signaling pathways, we performed a systematic bioinformatic analysis to identify enriched biological processes and pathways. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that BNIP3 is significantly involved in the regulation of glycoprotein biosynthesis and metabolic processes (Fig.  a). KEGG pathway analysis further revealed its strong association with key metabolic pathways, including glycolysis/gluconeogenesis and cGMP-PKG signaling (Fig.  b). Notably, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) suggested that BNIP3 may exert an inhibitory effect on signaling related to cell adhesion molecules, receptor-mediated transmembrane signal transduction, and immune response networks (Fig.  c). 
To functionally characterize BNIP3 in BC pathogenesis, we established a BNIP3 knockdown model (Fig.  a–d). A marked reduction in cell viability following BNIP3 silencing underscored its critical role in sustaining BC cell growth (Fig.  e). We next explored the functional link between BNIP3 and glycolytic metabolism by assessing its impact on lactate production and glucose uptake. BNIP3 knockdown led to significant decreases in both parameters (Fig.  f, g). Consistent with these findings, measurement of the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) revealed that glycolytic activity was substantially suppressed upon BNIP3 depletion (Fig.  h). Moreover, RT‑qPCR analysis demonstrated that BNIP3 knockdown significantly downregulated the expression of key glycolytic genes (PKM2, HK2, GLUT1, G6PD) in BC cells (Fig.  i, j), highlighting its pivotal role in regulating glycolytic gene expression and metabolic flux. 

## Relationship between BNIP3 expression and the tumor immune microenvironment
Beyond its role in glycolytic regulation, BNIP3 was found to be significantly enriched in immune-related pathways. To assess its impact on the BC immune microenvironment, we applied the ESTIMATE algorithm to characterize the TME. Results showed that StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and ESTIMATEScore were all significantly lower in the high-BNIP3 group (Fig.  a). Using CIBERSORT, we quantified the abundances of 22 immune cell types within tumor samples. This revealed that high BNIP3 expression was associated with decreased infiltration of CD8+T cells, naïve B cells, and resting mast cells, but increased proportions of M2 macrophages, plasma cells, and neutrophils (Fig.  b). Correlation analysis further supported these trends, showing a notable negative association between BNIP3 and CD8+ T cells, and a strong positive correlation with M2 macrophages (Fig.  c). These findings suggest that BNIP3 may facilitate tumor progression by fostering an immunosuppressive TME phenotype. 
Given that high tumor mutational burden (TMB) is linked to improved response to immunotherapy , we evaluated the relationship between BNIP3 and TMB in BC. TMB was significantly elevated in the low-BNIP3 group (Fig.  a). Moreover, BNIP3 expression was inversely correlated with the expression of most immune checkpoint molecules (Fig.  b). Based on the immune subtype classification proposed by Thorsson et al. , which includes wound healing (C1), IFN-γ overexpression (C2), inflammatory (C3), lymphocyte exhaustion (C4), immune quiescence (C5), and TGF-β dominant (C6) subtypes, we observed that TCGA-BRCA patients were distributed across C1–C4 and C6. The high-BNIP3 group showed a higher proportion of the immunosuppressive C4 subtype and a lower proportion of the C6 subtype (Fig.  c). In addition, immunophenotype score (IPS) analysis indicated significantly elevated scores in the low-BNIP3 group for IPS_CTLA4_neg_PD-1_pos, IPS_CTLA4_pos_PD-1_neg, and IPS_CTLA4_pos_PD-1_pos (Fig.  d–f), suggesting a more favorable immune context for checkpoint inhibitor response. Together, these results demonstrate substantial differences in immune infiltration and functional immune profiles between BNIP3-high and BNIP3-low patients, which may underlie differential outcomes following immunotherapy. 

## Tumor mutation and drug sensitivity analysis of BNIP3
We further explored the potential of BNIP3 expression as a stratification biomarker to inform chemotherapy or targeted therapy selection, particularly in patients with specific gene mutations. Among the 20 most frequently mutated genes in the high-BNIP3 group, TP53 mutations were significantly enriched (Fig.  a). Drug sensitivity analysis indicated that BNIP3 expression may serve as a predictive marker for treatment response: the high-BNIP3 group exhibited increased sensitivity to the IGF-1R inhibitor BMS-754807, the p53 activator Nutlin-3a, and the GSK3β inhibitor SB216763 (Fig.  b-d), whereas the low-BNIP3 group showed better response to the mTOR inhibitor OSI-027 (Fig.  e). These findings provide a rationale for BNIP3-based stratification in personalized targeted therapy. However, the underlying regulatory mechanisms and clinical applicability of this approach await further validation through in vivo experiments and prospective clinical studies. 

## BNIP3 regulates BC progression by modulating glycolysis
To further validate the role of BNIP3 in BC in vivo, we subcutaneously inoculated nude mice with MDA-MB-231 cells stably transduced with either control shRNA (shCtrl) or BNIP3-targeting shRNA (shBNIP3). Starting on day 6 post-inoculation, tumor weight, maximum diameter, and minimum diameter were measured every 2 days using a vernier caliper. The results demonstrated that BNIP3 knockdown significantly suppressed tumor progression in the mouse xenograft model (Fig.  a–c). Furthermore, analysis of tumor tissues revealed that BNIP3 silencing led to reduced glucose uptake and decreased lactate production (Fig.  d, e), corroborating the metabolic alterations associated with BNIP3 inhibition observed in vitro. 

## Discussion
This study found that BNIP3 is highly expressed in BC and is significantly associated with a poor prognosis. And our preclinical study demonstrated that BNIP3 promotes tumor progression by regulating glycolytic metabolic reprogramming. This discovery provides a potential molecular target for the prognostic assessment and personalized treatment of BC.
Through integrated analysis of TCGA datasets and clinical specimens, we confirmed the elevated expression of BNIP3 in BC tissues and observed correlations with clinicopathological variables such as age, molecular subtypes and TNM stage. Survival analysis further confirmed that the overall survival of patients with high BNIP3 expression was significantly shortened. The independent prognostic value of BNIP3 revealed in the multivariate analysis suggests its potential application in clinical stratification. This result is consistent with previous studies. For instance, in melanoma and glioma , , high BNIP3 expression is also associated with tumor invasiveness and chemoresistance. In addition, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve demonstrates the prognostic predictive efficiency of BNIP3, indicating that it can serve as a supplementary marker to traditional clinical parameters.
BNIP3, an atypical member of the Bcl-2 protein family, exhibits context-dependent functionality and can play dual roles in tumorigenesis . As a classical hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) target gene, its functional outcome is strongly influenced by the intensity and duration of hypoxic stress . Moderate or chronic hypoxia may mainly upregulate BNIP3 to mediate adaptive mitochondrial autophagy, helping cells remove damaged mitochondria, maintain energy homeostasis, and resist apoptosis , . In severe or acute hypoxia/hypercapnia environments, BNIP3 may be more inclined to form homodimers, disrupting mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby triggering caspase-independent apoptosis , . Additionally, the tumor microenvironment of BC also affects the functional tendency of BNIP3. Through functional experiments and bioinformatics analysis, our study revealed that BNIP3 promotes the metabolic reprogramming of BC cells by activating key genes involved in glycolysis, such as pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), hexokinase 2 (HK2), and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1). Knocking down BNIP3 significantly inhibited lactate production, glucose uptake, and the extracellular acidification rate, which is consistent with the mechanism reported in the literature that BNIP3 regulates energy metabolism by maintaining mitochondrial function , . Furthermore, elevated BNIP3 expression has been observed in both ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) and invasive breast carcinoma compared to normal breast tissues, suggesting that its role may also vary with molecular subtype .
A limitation of this study is that the precise mechanism by which BNIP3 regulates the expression of the aforementioned glycolysis-related genes remains unexplored. Given the established role of BNIP3 as a classic pro-apoptotic mitochondrial protein, we postulate that its regulatory effect is likely indirect. One plausible scenario is that BNIP3 influences mitochondrial function, thereby altering the intracellular metabolic state . Such metabolic shifts could modulate the activity of epigenetic modifiers, ultimately leading to changes in chromatin architecture and gene transcription . Furthermore, BNIP3 is a well-characterified transcriptional target of Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) . Within the hypoxic tumor microenvironment, elevated BNIP3 may engage in a positive feedback loop with HIF-1α . Specifically, BNIP3-mediated mitophagy promotes the clearance of dysfunctional mitochondria, which in turn contributes to the stabilization of HIF-1α activity . As HIF-1α is a master transcriptional regulator of key glycolytic genes (e.g., GLUT1, HK2, PKM2), BNIP3 could potentially amplify the HIF-1α signaling axis to indirectly drive the transcription of glycolytic genes . To elucidate the underlying mechanism, future work will include RNA-seq analysis of BNIP3-knockdown cells to comprehensively identify affected signaling pathways. Additionally, luciferase reporter gene assays will be employed to definitively determine whether BNIP3 directly influences the transcriptional activity of the promoters of these identified glycolysis-related genes.
Notably, GSEA indicated that BNIP3 is associated with inhibition of signaling pathways related to cell adhesion molecules and immune responses, suggesting its potential involvement in tumor progression through metabolism-immunity crosstalk. Currently, the role of BNIP3 in tumor immunomodulation remains unexplored, with its potential regulatory mechanisms in immune system interactions still uncharacterized. This study for the first time found that high expression of BNIP3 is closely associated with an immunosuppressive microenvironment, manifested as a decrease in CD8+ T cells, an increase in the infiltration of M2 macrophages and neutrophils, and a downregulation of the expression of immune checkpoint molecules (such as PD-1 and CTLA-4). This is consistent with the characteristics of the immunosuppressive phenotype in BC . The negative correlation between BNIP3 and TMB further implies that it may weaken the response to immunotherapy by reducing tumor antigenicity. In addition, immune subtype analysis showed that the proportion of the C4 (lymphocyte-depleted subtype) increased among high-risk patients, suggesting that BNIP3 may promote immune escape by regulating the TME. However, it is not clear whether the correlation between BNIP3 and the immunosuppressive microenvironment of BC is mediated by the glycolysis driven by BNIP3. The current data cannot fully establish the causal relationship between glycolysis and immunosuppression of BC. Therefore, more relevant validations are needed in the future.
Pharmacological sensitivity analysis revealed that the group with high BNIP3 expression was more sensitive to insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF-1R) inhibitor (BMS-754807) and p53 activator (Nutlin-3a), while the group with low BNIP3 expression responded better to mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitor (OSI-027). BMS-754807 is an effective and reversible dual inhibitor of IGF-1R and insulin receptor (InsR) . It has been reported that it can be combined with endocrine therapy drugs (such as letrozole or tamoxifen) and shows a powerful synergistic anti-tumor effect in estrogen-dependent BC models . Moreover, BMS-754807 in combination with metformin also exhibits significant synergy in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), and in the tested 13 TNBC cell lines, a synergistic effect was observed in 11 of them (85%), effectively inhibiting cell proliferation . Nutlin-3a is a small molecule MDM2 antagonist that stabilizes p53 protein and activates its transcriptional activity by blocking the interaction between MDM2 and p53 . Nutlin-3a in combination with carboplatin has a strong synergistic effect in mutant p53 TMD231 TNBC cells, promoting cell death . This provides a basis for precision BC treatment based on the expression level of BNIP3. For example, TP53 mutations were enriched in the group with high BNIP3 expression, suggesting that combined targeting of the p53 pathway may enhance the therapeutic efficacy. However, the current conclusions need to be further verified through animal experiments and prospective clinical trials.
Although the present study has confirmed through in vivo experiments that the knockdown of BNIP3 inhibits tumor growth, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying its regulation of glycolysis and the tumor immune microenvironment remain to be fully elucidated. For instance, whether BNIP3 can coordinate the interaction between metabolism and immunity simultaneously is worthy of further investigation. In addition, it is necessary to expand the sample sizes of animal models and clinical specimens and incorporate multicenter cohorts to verify the effectiveness of the target and the universality of the prognostic model.
