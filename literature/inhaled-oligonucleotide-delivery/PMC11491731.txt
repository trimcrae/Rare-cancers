## Background
Pyroptosis is an inflammatory form of regulated necrosis that has been implicated in the pathogenesis of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). However, the role of lipid peroxidation in pyroptosis and its underlying mechanisms in COPD remain unclear.

## Methods
In vitro, human bronchial epithelial cells (Beas-2b cells) were exposed to cigarette smoke extract (CSE) for 24 h. In vivo, mice were exposed to cigarette smoke (CS) for 4 weeks. To investigate the role of xCT, we used siRNA and AAV6 to conditionally knock down xCT in vitro and in vivo, respectively.

## Results
The administration of ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), a ferroptosis inhibitor that inhibits lipid peroxidation, significantly reduced the cytotoxicity of CSE to Beas-2b cells and mitigated inflammatory exudation, lung injury and mucus hypersecretion in mice with CS-induced COPD. Fer-1 suppressed gasdermin D (GSDMD)-mediated pyroptosis caused by CS in vitro and in vivo. However, in Beas-2b cells and the lung epithelial cells of mice, conditional knockdown of xCT (a negative regulatory factor of lipid peroxidation) inhibited the xCT/GPx4 axis, leading to more severe lipid peroxidation and GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis during cigarette smoke exposure. Moreover, we found that CS promoted the degradation of xCT through the ubiquitin proteasome system (UPS) and that treatment with MG132 significantly inhibited the degradation of xCT and downregulated the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins.

## Conclusion
The results of this study suggested that the ubiquitination-mediated degradation of xCT drives GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis in COPD and is a potential therapeutic target for COPD.

## Highlights
•In addition to ferroptosis, lipid peroxidation could trigger pyroptosis in COPD.
•xCT deficiency exacerbated pyroptosis in cigarette smoke-exposed airway epithelial cells.
•Cigarette smoke could promote the degradation of xCT through the ubiquitin proteasome system.
•Inhibiting xCT degradation might be an effective treatment strategy for COPD.

## Introduction
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive lung condition characterized by persistent airflow obstruction and is the third leading cause of global morbidity and mortality [ ]. Cigarette smoking, the primary environmental risk factor for COPD, involves detrimental agents such as free radicals, which trigger oxidative pathways that lead to lung cell damage and chronic airway inflammation. Notably, epithelial cell damage in the airways and alveoli is thought to play a significant role in the pathogenesis of COPD [ , ]. However, the etiology of COPD remains unclear (see , ). 
Lipid peroxidation is one of the important forms of oxidative damage, and is initiated by free radical interacting with cell membrane lipids, contributing to the progression of various types of regulated cell death [ ]. Notably, CS contains numerous free radicals that oxidize polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) [ , ], on the other hand, CS can trigger ferroptosis, a novel form of programmed cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [ , ]. Besides, elevated lipid peroxidation and levels of its byproduct, 4-hydroxynonenal, are observed in COPD patients [ ]. Hence, lipid peroxidation plays an important role in the pathogenesis of COPD. In addition to being a mainspring of ferroptosis, lipid peroxidation can trigger pyroptosis, an inflammatory form of regulated necrosis caused by the cleavage of GSDMD into its N-terminal fragment (GSDMD-N), which can bind to inner membrane lipids, forming pores in the plasma membrane and ultimately resulting in cellular lysis [ , , , ]. Lipid peroxidation can trigger GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis in fatal polymicrobial sepsis [ ]. Moreover, Zhang et al. reported that Fer-1 mitigated neonicotinoid imidacloprid-induced pyroptosis in nephrotoxicity [ ]. Additionally, liproxstatin-1 (LPT1), another ferroptosis inhibitor that inhibits lipid peroxidation, effectively suppressed caspase-1 and GSDMD cleavage in metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease in murine models [ ]. Although overactivated inflammasomes, increased caspase-4 expression, and GSDMD cleavage contribute to cell death, airway inflammation and remodeling in COPD [ , , ], whether pyroptosis can be triggered by lipid peroxidation in COPD has not yet been reported.
When conditions impede the xCT-glutathione (GSH)-GSH peroxidase 4 (GPx4) axis, cells are prone to lipid peroxidation; in short, the GSH depletion and consequent decrease in GPX4 activity caused by system Xc− injury blocks the reduction of peroxide or radical intermediates [ , ]. Notably, experimental evidence has shown that CS can induce a decrease in xCT mRNA and subsequently affect the translation of xCT, resulting in reduced protein expression [ , ]. To date, however, the direct effect of CS on xCT at the protein level has not been studied. Recent research has indicated that in certain malignancies, xCT can be degraded by the ubiquitin‒proteasome system (UPS) [ , ], orchestrating the degradation of cellular proteins through an enzymatic cascade involving an E1-activating enzyme, an E2-conjugating enzyme, and an E3-ligating enzyme, ultimately tagging substrates with ubiquitin (Ub) chains for directed degradation by the 26S proteasome complex [ ]. Therefore, we sought to investigate whether the downregulation of xCT induced by CS is related to ubiquitination-mediated degradation.
In this study, we found that lipid peroxidation can trigger GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis in COPD through the ubiquitination and degradation of xCT. Our research revealed that xCT not only acts as a negative regulator of ferroptosis but also has inhibitory effects on pyroptosis and is a promising therapeutic target for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).

## Materials and methods
2.1

## Materials and reagents
Cigarettes were purchased from Hongta Tobacco Group Co., Ltd. (Yunnan, China). Fer-1 and disulfiram (DSF) were obtained from MedChemExpress (New Jersey, USA). xCT siRNA was obtained from HanBio Technology (Shanghai, China). Primary antibodies against β-actin, NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1, GSDMD, and IL-1β were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Primary antibodies against caspase-4/11, FSP1, GCH1 and GPx4 were obtained from Zenbio (Chengdu, China). Primary antibodies against xCT were obtained from Abcam (MA, USA). A Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) kit was obtained from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). An LDH assay kit was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). ELISA kits for IL-1α and IL-1β were purchased from Biolegend (California, USA), and IL-18 was purchased from Boster (Wuhan, China). The MDA and GSH/GSSG assay kits were obtained from Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing China). Dojindo (Kyushu, Japan) provided the Liperfluo assay kit. An ROS assay kit was obtained from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). The RNA CELL Kit was provided from Baidai Biotechnology Co., LTD (Changzhou, China). The All-in-one RT EasyMix for qPCR and SYBR Mix were purchased from TOLOBIO Biotech. (Shanghai, China). The reagents used for cell culture were DMEM, purchased from Gibco (Thermo Fisher Science, China), and fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin–streptomycin (100 × ), trypsin-EDTA and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), purchased from Solarbio, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and methacholine were obtained from Sigma (USA), the 8-epi-PGF2α ELISA Kit (FineTest) was provided by Wuhan Fine Biotech Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). The whole body plethysmography system was provided by Shanghai Tawang Intelligent Technology Co., LTD (Shanghai, China).

2.2

## Cigarette smoke extract (CSE) preparation
The cigarette smoke from one cigarette was dissolved in 10 ml of DMEM without FBS to make a 10 % CSE solution.

2.3

## Cell culture and model establishment
Human bronchial epithelial (Beas-2b) cells were purchased from the Cell Line Bank (Beijing, China), cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % penicillin–streptomycin and incubated in a 5 % CO2 atmosphere at 37 °C. The cells were exposed to CSE (0, 1.25 %, 2.5 %, or 5 %) for 24 h for subsequent experiments.

2.4

## Cell viability and LDH assay
Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate (1.5 × 104 cells per well) and treated with Fer-1 or DSF and CSE for 24 h. The CCK-8 kit was added and incubated for 1.5h to measure the absorbance at 450 nm. The LDH release reagent was added to the untreated cells 1 h earlier as a positive control group. Then the 96-well plate was centrifuged at 400×g for 5 min, 50 μl of the supernatant was added to a new 96-well plate, and then incubated with LDH test working fluid for 30min. Finally, the absorbance was measured at 490 nm.

2.5

## Lipid peroxidation measurements and ROS detection
BEAS-2b cells were seed into a 20-mm laser confocal cell culture dish (1.5 × 105 cells/well) and treated with Fer-1 or DSF and CSE for 24h. Then cells were washed with PBS 3 times and incubated in the dark with 1 μM Liperfluo at 37 °C for 30 min. After washing three times, lipid peroxide was finally measured and evaluated by confocal microscopy (Olympus FV3000) with the wavelengths of excitation and emission are 524 nm and 535 nm, respectively. BEAS-2b cells were seed into a 12-well plate (1 × 105 cells/well) and incubated with DCFH-DA in the dark for 20 min and then observed by fluorescence microscopy. According to manufacturer's instructions, the collected cell samples and lung tissues were detected by the 8-epi-PGF2α ELISA Kit to analysis the level of lipid peroxidation.

2.6

## GSH/GSSG ratio and MDA detection
According to the manufacturers’ instructions, the fresh lung tissues and cells were collected to prepare the supernatants for detection, which were sequentially added the assay reagents from the kit as instructed, and incubated at room temperature for 5 min and 30 min respectively, then measured the absorbance at 405 nm to determine the levels of GSH and GSSG. For the determination of MDA, after adding the reagents to the supernatant, the mixture was incubated in a 95 °C water bath for 80 min, then measure the absorbance at 532 nm.

2.7

## Calcein/PI cell viability/cytotoxicity assay
BEAS-2b cells were seed into a 12-well plate (1 × 105 cells/well). After treated with 24h, cells were washed with PBS, then incubated with 1 ml Calcein/PI dye liquor in the dark for 30 min, and finally observed by fluorescence microscopy.

2.8

## Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction
Total RNA was extracted from Beas-2b cells by using the BIOG RNA CELL Kit and then reverse transcribed into cDNA. The content of cDNA was measured by real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to determine the gene transcription levels.

2.9

## LC-MS analysis
LC-MS/MS analyses were performed using an ExionLC™ AD system (SCIEX) coupled with a QTRAP® 6500+ mass spectrometer (SCIEX). Samples were injected onto a C18 Column (10cm × 2.1 mm) using a 14-min linear gradient at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min with a column temperature of 500 °C, Ion Source Gas of 1:55, Ion Source Gas of 2:55.

2.10

## siRNA transfection
Beas-2B cells were transfected with small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting xCT (si1, si2 and si3), designed by HanBio Technology (Shanghai, China), by using the RNA-FIT RNA transfection reagent (HanBio Technology, Shanghai, China). The efficiency of the siRNA treatments was assessed by western blotting.

2.11

## Ubiquitylation and coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP)
The cells were treated with 10 μM MG132 (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) and then exposed to CSE for 24 h. After the cells were lysed, lysates were incubated with 1 μg of anti-xCT antibody, followed by western blot analysis.

2.12

## Animal experiments
Male BALB/c mice aged 6–8 weeks were obtained from Jilin Qianhe Technology Industrial Co., Ltd., and housed under standard conditions. Two protocols were followed. In protocol 1, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group): control, CS, CS + DSF (50 mg/kg), and CS + Fer-1 (5 mg/kg). In protocol 2, mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 per group): negative control (EV), EV + CS, KD, and KD + CS. The COPD model was induced by the intranasal instillation of LPS (10 μg/mouse) on days 1 and 14, along with exposure to cigarette smoke (CS) for 4 weeks using a passive smoking exposure system. DSF (50 mg/kg) or Fer-1 (10 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally before CS exposure. The negative control group received an equivalent amount of saline.
All animal procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Jilin University.

2.13

## AAV6-mediated xCT knockdown in lung epithelial cells of mice
The adeno-associated viruses were developed and obtained from GeneChem Company (Shanghai, China). Generally, a siRNA oligo was subcloned into a GV711 AAV serotype 6 vector (SP-Cp-EGFP-MIR155-SV40 PolyA) to develop the siRNA adeno-associated virus. To induce CKD-xCT in lung epithelial cells of mice, adeno-associated virus-6 (AAV6)- packaged xCT knockdown (KD) plasmid were intranasally instilled into each nasal cavity with 40 μl AAV6-packaged xCT knockdown plasmid with a concentration of 1 × 1012 vg/ml. The mice in negative control group were intranasally instilled with AAV6-packaged empty vector (EV). Three siRNA sequences of targeting xCT were listed as follows: iRNA1: AAGAAGTAGACAACCCTGAAA; iRNA2: TGTTCGCTGTCTCCAGGTTAT; iRNA3: AGATATGCATCGTCCTTTCAA.

2.14

## Western blot
Cells or lung tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride and then centrifuged to collect protein samples. After determining the protein concentration with a BCA kit, protein samples were separated by 12.5 % SDS‒PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking in 5 % BSA for 1 h, the membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies and then with secondary antibodies for 1 h, followed by visualization using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL). The protein bands were quantified using ImageJ.

2.15

## ELISA
The levels of the cytokines IL-1α, IL-1β and IL-18 in the cell culture supernatant or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were measured by ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions.

2.16

## Giemsa staining
BALF was centrifuged to separate the supernatant from the cells. The cells were spun onto slides using a cytospin and fixed with methanol. Subsequently, the fixed cells were stained sequentially with Giemsa stain, followed by a rinse with running water and morphological identification under a microscope.

2.17

## Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), periodic acid-schiff (PAS), and immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining
Lung tissues were collected, fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, embedded, sliced, dehydrated and stained with H&E and PAS. For immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining, sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h.

2.18

## Pulmonary function test
The mice were placed in a sealed tracing chamber connected with the inductor and given different concentrations (0, 12.5, 25, 50 mg/ml) of methacholine (Ach) by aerosol inhalation. After analyzed by a computer, the WBP system could detect and record the airway hyperresponsiveness during the excitation process.

2.19

## Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
Cells were fixed with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde, followed by sectioning into ultrathin slices and subsequent staining. Subsequently, the morphology of the cell membranes and mitochondria was examined using a transmission electron microscope.

2.20

## Statistical analysis
All experimental data were analyzed by GraphPad Prism 9.0, and one-way t tests or ANOVA were used to analyze the means ± standard deviations. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 or 0.01. NS represents >0.05, ∗P represents P < 0.05, and ∗∗P represents P < 0.01. All experiments were independently repeated 3 times.

## Results
3.1

## Ferroptosis inducers erastin and RSL3 induced pyroptosis in Beas-2b cells
Erastin and RSL3 can trigger ferroptosis by inhibiting system xc- or GPx4 activity, respectively, which can induce lipid peroxidation. Ferroptosis inhibitors (Fer-1, 2 μM), a pyroptosis inhibitor (DSF, 1 μM), the apoptosis inhibitor z-VAD-FMK (ZVF, 20 μM), the necrosis inhibitor necrostatin-1 (Nec-1, 10 μM) and an autophagy inhibitor (3-MA, 50 μM) were used to prevent cell death. Fer-1 and DSF, but not ZVF, Nec-1 or 3-MA, increased the viability of erastin- or RSL3-treated cells ( A and B). In addition, erastin and RSL3 significantly increased the levels of IL-1α, IL-1β and IL-18 in the cell culture supernatant, while Fer-1 treatment decreased the secretion of these pyroptosis-related inflammatory factors ( C–E). Moreover, we found that erastin and RSL3 increased the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins, as indicated by increases in the levels of NLRP3, ASC, cleaved caspase-1, GSDMD and IL-1β in Beas-2b cells ( F); these increases were significantly suppressed by Fer-1 ( G). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining showed that the fluorescence intensity of N-GSDMD was greatly enhanced when Beas-2b cells were exposed to erastin and RSL3 and that this effect was weakened by Fer-1 ( H). Our data demonstrated that lipid peroxidation contributed to pyroptosis in human bronchial epithelial cells. 

3.2

## CSE induced lipid peroxidation and pyroptosis in Beas-2b cells
To verify that CSE induced cytotoxicity of Beas-2b cells, LDH release was measured. As shown in A, the LDH release rate increased with the increase of CSE concentration, and RSL3 treatment acted as a positive group. Additionally, CSE induced lower GSH/GSSG ratio and MDA production in a dose-dependent manner ( B and C). The Liperfluo assay kit and DCFH-DA were used to measure lipid peroxidation and ROS levels. As shown in G, the fluorescence intensities of Liperfluo and DCFH-DA were enhanced by CSE in a dose-dependent manner. Moreover, CSE downregulated the expression of xCT and GPx4, but not FSP1 and GCH1( H and I). To verify that CSE triggered pyroptosis in Beas-2b cells, we measured the levels of pyroptosis-related inflammatory factors in the cell culture supernatant by ELISA. D–F have shown that CSE mildly increased the level of IL-1α but markedly increased the levels of IL-1β and IL-18 in culture supernatants in a dose-dependent manner. Moreover, CSE upregulated the expression of NLRP3 and ASC and promoted the cleavage of caspase-1/4, GSDMD, and IL-1β ( H). Additionally, Calcein/PI staining showed that when exposed to CSE, the number of PI-positive cells increased significantly ( J). Moreover, through TEM, we found that cells exposed to CSE contained pyroptotic cells and pyroptotic bodies, damaged cell membranes with a substantial release of cellular contents, mitochondrial swelling, loss of cristae, and a significant increase in vacuolization ( K). In summary, these data indicated that CSE induced lipid peroxidation and pyroptosis in Beas-2b cells. 

3.3

## Fer-1 alleviated lipid peroxidation induced by CSE in Beas-2b cells
Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) is a synthetic antioxidant that prevents damage to membrane lipids, consequently inhibiting cell death. We found that Fer-1 effectively reversed the decrease in cell viability and the increase in LDH release induced by CSE ( A and B). In addition, Fer-1 significantly increased the GSH/GSSG ratio and MDA production in CSE-exposed cells, as shown in C and D. Moreover, through the use of a DCFH-DA probe and a Liperfluo assay kit, we found that lipid peroxidation and ROS production induced by CSE were inhibited by Fer-1, as depicted in E and F. Oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) is the main driver of lipid peroxidation, we used LC-MS to detect the levels of PUFAs in CSE and Fer-1-treated Beas-2b cells. We found that many metabolites of polyunsaturated fatty acids levels, including linoleic acid (LA), α-linolenic acid (ALA), and arachidonic acid (AA), were significantly decreased in Fer-1-treated cells compared with CSE-exposed cells. In addition, the relative quantitative values of differential metabolites were normalized transformed and clustered. As shown in H, compared with CSE group, Fer-1 treatment significantly reduced the contents of 5-HETE, 10-HDoHE, 17-HDoHE, 9-HETE, 13-HODE, 8-HETrE, 5-HEPE, DHA, 11-HETE, 11-HDoHE and 16-HDoHE. 
Meanwhile, Fer-1 could significantly suppress the production of 8-epi-PGF2α, a prostaglandin derivative formed by free radical catalyzed lysis of esterified arachidonic acid on cell membrane, which was induced by CSE ( ). Moreover, western blot results showed that Fer-1 significantly increased the expression of xCT and GPx4 in CSE-exposed cells ( I). Then we investigated how Fer-1 can rescue this downregulation of xCT or GPx4 at the RNA and protein levels. Our results showed that CSE exposure caused the increased mRNA levels of xCT and GPx4 ( J), which were inconsistent with the protein levels. Perhaps this is a protective compensatory mechanism of cells to counter the disruption of Xc-system by lipid peroxidation induced by CSE, while Fer-1 reduced the increased expression of GPx4 mRNA, but not xCT mRNA in CSE-exposed cells. Additionally, Fer-1 couldn't prevent the degradation of xCT protein ( K). Therefore, the effect of Fer-1 on upregulating expression of xCT in CSE-exposed cells was due to its role in lipid peroxide clearance. Our data indicated that inhibiting lipid peroxidation protected against CSE-induced bronchial epithelial cell injury.

3.4

## Fer-1 relieved CSE-induced pyroptosis in Beas-2b cells
To verify the role of pyroptosis in CSE-induced cell injury, we treated cells with DSF, which can inhibit the formation of GSDMD pores to prevent inflammatory caspase-mediated cell pyroptosis, to serve as the positive control group. We found that DSF effectively reversed cell death and decreased LDH release in CSE-exposed cells ( A and B). Moreover, our ELISA results showed that Fer-1 effectively reduced the secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 in cell culture supernatants after exposure to CSE ( C–D). Additionally, western blot results showed that Fer-1 significantly decreased the expression of NLRP3 and ASC and inhibited the cleavage of caspase-1/4, GSDMD and IL-1β in CSE-treated cells ( E and F). The immunofluorescence results showed that Fer-1 treatment inhibited the CSE-induced cleavage of GSDMD into N-GSDMD ( G). Moreover, TEM revealed that Fer-1 treatment alleviated mitochondrial swelling, loss of cristae, vacuolization, pyrogenic body formation and cell membrane damage ( H). Furthermore, Calcein/PI staining showed that in the Fer-1 or DSF treatment significantly reduced the number of PI positive cells exposed to CSE ( I). Our data demonstrated that inhibiting lipid peroxidation alleviated pyroptosis in CSE-exposed Beas-2b cells. 

3.5

## Fer-1 suppressed CS-induced lipid peroxidation in the lungs of COPD mice
Next, we investigated the protective effect of Fer-1 on CS-exposed COPD mice. Giemsa staining revealed that CS exposure induced inflammatory cell exudation in BALF and that DSF and Fer-1 treatment significantly decreased the number of inflammatory cells ( A). In addition, H&E and PAS staining indicated that inflammatory infiltration, alveolar fusion, interstitial thickening and mucus hypersecretion occurred in the lungs of COPD mice and that after treatment with DSF and Fer-1, these pathological changes were obviously relieved, as shown in B and C. Moreover, CS exposure triggered ROS overproduction in BALF cells, which was effectively alleviated by treatment with Fer-1 but not DSF ( D). Additionally, GSH depletion and MDA production occurred in the lung tissues of COPD mice, while Fer-1 treatment markedly alleviated MDA production and GSH depletion ( E and F). Additionally, Fer-1 could inhibit 8-epi-PGF2α production in lung tissues of COPD mice, but not DSF ( . C). In addition, immunohistochemical staining revealed increased 4-HNE production in the lungs of COPD mice, but both DSF and Fer-1 decreased 4-HNE production ( G). Furthermore, as shown in H, CS exposure greatly downregulated the expression of xCT and GPx4 and upregulated the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins, including NLRP3, N-GSDMD, cleaved caspase-1/11 ASC and cleaved IL-1β, in COPD mice; however, Fer-1, but not DSF, significantly upregulated the expression of xCT and GPx4 and inhibited the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins. Moreover, the COPD mice showed the elevation of Penh values, while Fer-1 treatment significantly reduced Penh values in COPD mice ( . F). Our data revealed that Fer-1 protected against lipid peroxidation and pyroptosis in COPD mice. 

3.6

## Fer-1 protected against pyroptosis in COPD mice
We measured the levels of pyroptosis-related inflammatory factors, including IL-1α, IL-1β and IL-18, in BALF by ELISAs. CSF exposure increased the levels of IL-1α, IL-1β and IL-18 in the BALF of mice in the model group compared with those in the BALF of mice in the control group, while Fer-1 effectively normalized the levels of these pyroptosis-related inflammatory factors in the treatment group, similar to DSF ( A–C). In addition, as shown in D and E, CS exposure greatly upregulated the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins, including NLRP3, N-GSDMD, cleaved caspase-1/11, ASC and cleaved IL-1β, in COPD mice, while Fer-1 or DSF treatment significantly inhibited the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining showed that CS exposure increased the expression of N-GSDMD, an effect that was greatly suppressed by Fer-1 and DSF treatment ( F). Our results showed that Fer-1 inhibited not only ferroptosis but also pyroptosis in CS-induced COPD mice. 

3.7

## xCT downregulation aggravated lipid peroxidation and pyroptosis induced by CSE in Beas-2b cells
xCT, a multipass transmembrane protein, mediates cystine/glutamate antiporter activity in system xc− (Sato et al., 1999; Koppula et al., 2018), which prevents lipid peroxidation. To determine the role of xCT in CSE-induced peroxidation and pyroptosis, three different siRNA-xCT sequences were used for the specific knockdown of xCT, and the resulting siRNAs were named si1, si2 and si3. Western blot analysis indicated that si3-xCT was more effective than si1-and si2-xCT; therefore, si3-xCT was used for subsequent experiments. Moreover, the expression of GPx4 was inhibited after xCT knockdown ( A). As shown in B, the viability of xCT-knockdown cells was lower than that of negative control (NC) cells during CSE exposure. In addition, the levels of IL-1β and IL-18 in the medium supernatant of xCT-knockdown cells were greater than those in the medium of NC cells when the cells were treated with CSE ( C and D). Moreover, compared with those in the NC groups, GSH depletion and MDA production caused by CSE exposure were more significant in the xCT-knockdown groups, as shown in E and F. Besides, knockdown of xCT further increased the production of 8-epi-PGF2α in CSE-treated cells ( . D). Western blot results showed that xCT knockdown markedly decreased the expression of components of the xCT/GPx4 axis and increased the expression of NLRP3 and ASC, as well as the cleavage of caspase4, GSDMD, caspase-1 and IL-1β, which was induced by CSE treatment ( G and H). Moreover, the immunofluorescence staining results showed that the expression of N-GSDMD was greater in the siRNA-xCT group than in the negative control group (NC) during CSE exposure ( I). Furthermore, knockdown of xCT significantly aggravated airway hyperreactivity in COPD mice ( . G). 
Therefore, our results indicated that the downregulation of xCT expression played a crucial role in CSE-induced lipid peroxidation and pyroptosis in Beas-2b cells.

3.8

## Conditional specific knockdown of xCT in lung epithelial cells aggravated lipid peroxidation and pyroptosis in COPD mice
To further investigate the role of xCT in COPD, we used AAV6-SPC conditional specific knockdown of xCT (KD) in lung epithelial cells in mice. First, we verified the knockdown efficiency of 3 AAV6-SPC vectors (named iRNA1, iRNA2, and iRNA3) by western blot and immunofluorescence. As shown in A and B, iRNA3 had the best knockdown efficiency and was used for subsequent studies. The expression of xCT in KD COPD model mice was inhibited more significantly than that in EV COPD mice, as shown in C. Next, we measured the protective effect of xCT in COPD mice. We found that the number of inflammatory cells in the BALF of CS-exposed KD mice was much greater than that in the BALF of CS-exposed EV mice ( D). HE staining indicated that compared with the control, CS exposure caused significant pulmonary interstitial thickening, congestion and destruction of the alveolar structure and more severe lung damage in CS-exposed KD mice than in CS-exposed EV mice ( E). Additionally, the levels of the inflammatory factors IL-18, IL-1α and IL-1β in the BALF of KD COPD mice were greater than those in the BALF of EV COPD mice ( G). In addition, compared with those in EV COPD mice, 4-HNE expression, GSH depletion and MDA production were significantly greater in KD COPD mice ( F and H). Besides, xCT deficiency led to more 8-epi-PGF2α production in lung tissues of COPD mice ( . F). Moreover, western blot analysis revealed greater expression levels of pyroptosis-related proteins in KD-COPD mice than in EV mice ( I). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining revealed that xCT knockdown increased the expression of N-GSDMD in COPD mice ( J). In brief, xCT deficiency exacerbated lipid peroxidation and pyroptosis in CS/LPS-induced COPD mice. 

3.9

## CSE induced the degradation of xCT through UPS
We further investigated whether CS could directly facilitate the protein degradation process of xCT. The UPS and autophagy-lysosomal pathway play crucial roles in the degradation of most cellular proteins and provide both selectivity and specificity. First, we found that the autophagy inhibitors CQ and 3-MA slightly normalized the downregulation of xCT induced by CSE ( A). Immunofluorescence colocalization indicated that CQ treatment did not significantly enhance the colocalization of xCT and LC3 during CSE ( B). Additionally, cycloheximide (CHX) treatment accelerated the CSE-induced decrease in xCT protein expression ( C). However, MG132 significantly reversed the decrease in xCT expression induced by CSE ( D). To verify the role of the UPS in CSE-induced xCT degradation, we further performed coimmunoprecipitation assays. As shown in E, CSE induced the binding of xCT to ubiquitin, which contributed to xCT degradation through the UPS. In addition, MG132 treatment greatly decreased the expression of NLRP3 and ASC and the cleavage of caspase4, GSDMD, caspase-1 and IL-1β, which was induced by CSE treatment ( F). These data demonstrated that CSE contributed to xCT degradation mainly through the UPS. 

4

## Discussion
Cigarette smoking is a primary environmental risk factor for COPD. Prolonged exposure to CS triggers oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, leading to airway epithelial cell damage and dysfunction, which contributes to the development of COPD [ , ]. Herein, we demonstrated that lipid peroxidation due to the ubiquitination and degradation of xCT contributes to pyroptosis in the pathogenesis of COPD. Therefore, our research demonstrated that xCT can serve as a promising target for COPD treatment.
Cytoplasmic membrane lipid peroxidation can be induced by free radical assault via enzymes or nonenzymatic processes to preferentially oxidize polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) to generate lipid peroxides, leading to various pathological conditions, such as inflammation and cancer [ ]. Recent studies have shown that although lipid peroxidation is the key downstream feature of ferroptosis, it can also trigger pyroptosis, a type of programmed necrosis mediated by gasdermin. Lipid peroxidation drives gasdermin D-mediated pyroptosis in pulmonary polymicrobial sepsis. In addition, inhibiting lipid peroxidation can prevent pyroptosis [ ]. Zhang et al. revealed that Fer-1 alleviated neonicotinoid imidacloprid-evoked pyroptosis in nephrotoxicity [ ]. Additionally, liproxstatin-1 (LPT1) has been shown to potently inhibit the cleavage of caspase-1 and GSDMD in metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease in mice [ ]. Interestingly, we found that erastin and RSL3, both of which induce lipid peroxidation, induced pyroptosis in Beas-2b cells, an effect that was suppressed by Fer-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor, to prevent erastin-induced lipid peroxidation ( ). Therefore, we learned that lipid peroxidation can trigger pyroptosis in lung epithelial cells.
Notably, lipid peroxidation is an important mechanism by which CS causes lung injury. As a primary risk factor, CS contains many free radicals capable of inducing lipid peroxidation, which entails the oxidative breakdown of PUFAs, ultimately leading to cellular damage [ ]. Besides, CS can cause the accumulation of the labile iron pool, leading to ferroptosis, featured by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [ ]. Moreover, elevated levels of lipid peroxidation and its resultant byproduct, 4-hydroxynonenal, have been identified in individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [ ]. In the present study, we found that CSE could trigger lipid peroxidation and ROS overproduction accompanied by the downregulation of xCT and GPx4 expression in Beas-2b cells ( A–C and 2G-2I). Pyroptosis can accelerate the progression of COPD and is also an important mechanism of airway epithelial damage caused by cigarette smoke. Inflammasome overactivation, increased caspase-4 expression, and GSDMD cleavage occur in COPD patients and contribute to airway inflammation and remodeling in COPD patients [ , , ]. Consistent with previous research, we found that CSE induced the secretion of pyroptosis-associated inflammatory factors, including IL-1α, IL-1β and IL-18, as well as the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins in Beas-2b cells ( H). Hence, we speculated that the effect of CS was similar to that of erastin and RSL3. Surprisingly, Fer-1 treatment significantly suppressed not only lipid peroxidation but also pyroptosis in CSE-exposed Beas-2b cells and CS-induced COPD mice ( – ). And Fer-1 could inhibit the damage of lipid peroxidation induced by CSE to Xc-system by removing lipid peroxides ( ). Therefore, we revealed that inhibiting lipid peroxidation could prevent pyroptosis in CS-induced COPD.
When the xCT/GSH/GPx4 axis is disrupted, cells are vulnerable to lipid peroxidation [ , ]. Smoking can decrease the expression of xCT and inhibit the ability of xCT to take in cystine, leading to a decrease in the intracellular GSH content and the inactivation of GPx4, which causes cells to undergo lipid peroxidation [ , ]. Since CSE can cause a decreased protein level of xCT and GPx4, while Fer-1 can rescue the decrease in xCT and GPx4 by clearing lipid peroxides, we believed that xCT/GPx4 axis played an important role in CSE-induced COPD ( ). In this study, xCT knockdown in Beas-2b cells and the lung epithelial cells of mice led to more severe lipid peroxidation and pyroptosis ( , ). CS can decrease the expression of xCT, but there have been no reports demonstrating whether CS affects the protein degradation of xCT. According to results from recent studies, xCT can be degraded by the proteasome pathway. Chen et al. reported that the ubiquitination of SLC7A11 promoted ferroptosis and radiosensitization in hepatocellular carcinoma [ ]. In addition, Wang et al. demonstrated that SLC7A11/xCT K11-linked ubiquitination and degradation facilitated ferroptosis in non-small cell lung cancer [ ]. Interestingly, our results showed that CSE increased the expressions of xCT and GPx4 at mRNA level, but decreased at protein level ( , J).
Therefore, we speculated that the protein degradation of xCT might be involved in CS-induced COPD. As expected, we found that CHX treatment accelerated the degradation of xCT in CSE-exposed Beas-2b cells. Compared with autophagy inhibitors, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 more significantly suppressed the degradation of xCT. Moreover, the co-IP assay confirmed the interaction of xCT with ubiquitin. Furthermore, MG132 inhibited pyroptosis-related protein expression in CSE-treated Beas-2b cells. Therefore, we illustrated that the UPS is involved in CS-induced xCT degradation ( ). As a result, CS could cause lipid peroxidation by promoting the ubiquitination-mediated degradation of xCT in Beas-2b cells.

5

## Conclusion
We revealed that lipid peroxidation triggered by the ubiquitination-mediated degradation of xCT could promote pyroptosis in COPD patients and that inhibiting the ubiquitination-mediated degradation of xCT could alleviate lipid peroxidation-mediated pyroptosis in COPD patients ( ). Therefore, our research revealed the inhibitory role of xCT in pyroptosis and suggested that blocking the protein degradation of xCT could be an effective strategy for the treatment of COPD.
