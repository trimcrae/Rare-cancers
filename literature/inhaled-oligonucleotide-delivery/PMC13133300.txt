## Introduction
Stroke is a leading cause of mortality worldwide, with incidence rising significantly in individuals aged 55 and older. Ischemic stroke occurs when a thrombus or embolus obstructs cerebral blood flow, resulting in neurological dysfunction. A striking 87% of global stroke incidents result from cerebral infarction, itself stemming from cerebral thrombosis (Rosamond et al. ). Vascular dementia, the second most prevalent form of dementia in the elderly, accounts for nearly 20% of dementia cases worldwide. Both ischemic cerebral injury and chronic ischemic brain disease can culminate in vascular dementia, affecting 25% of stroke survivors (Kalaria et al. ).
People who experience a stroke are at increased risk of later cognitive decline, a relationship supported by evidence showing that cerebral small vessel disease (cSVD) significantly contributes to both stroke and dementia, particularly vascular dementia (VaD) (Bos et al. ; Mok et al. ). cSVD is a major pathological substrate for lacunar infarction and is strongly associated with cognitive impairment through mechanisms including white-matter damage, chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, endothelial dysfunction, and blood–brain barrier disruption (Elahi et al. ; Rundek et al. ). These vascular abnormalities play a central role in the progression from stroke to vascular cognitive impairment by promoting neuronal vulnerability and structural brain changes (Elahi et al. ; Rundek et al. ; Skukan et al. ).
Gene-based therapeutic strategies have shown promise in preclinical ischemic stroke models. Studies using adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors have demonstrated enhanced neuroprotection, angiogenesis, and improved functional recovery following ischemic injury (Wang et al. ; Yu et al. ). Additionally, advances in genomic research have identified molecular pathways involved in stroke and dementia, highlighting potential targets for future gene-based interventions (Jagodic et al. ). Collectively, this evidence supports a consistent link between stroke and VaD, while suggesting that gene therapy may represent a potential future strategy for cerebrovascular repair (Bos et al. ; Jagodic et al. ).
The therapeutic strategies for acute stroke remain limited, with thrombolysis as the only available intervention to restore cerebral blood flow. However, thrombolytic drugs are associated with significant risks and are limited by the narrow time window after the onset of symptoms for optimal therapeutic effects (Tsivgoulis et al. ). Gene therapy has emerged as a revolutionary approach, with the potential for sustained therapeutic effects, for example, through actively expressed transgenes in the artery during therapeutic angiogenesis (Lewis ; Xing et al. ). Unlike conventional therapies, gene-based interventions utilize transgene expression to maintain therapeutic concentration over time, thereby minimizing the need for repeated dosing (Ay and Reinisch ).
Recent experimental studies have highlighted the promise of gene therapies targeting chronic cerebral hypoperfusion and ischemic brain injury (Seo et al. ; Matsui et al. ). Novel therapeutic avenues involve the preservation of penumbral regions through angiogenesis or neuroprotection using genes encoding brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2), and anti-apoptotic proteins (Zhao et al. ; Shirakura et al. ; Matsuoka et al. ). Chronic hypoperfusion induced by atherosclerosis is also targeted by gene therapy, as this condition, apart from causing cerebral ischemia episodes, leads to neuropathological alterations such as dementia (Morishita ; Yoshimura et al. ). While prolonged hypoperfusion is known to induce active angiogenesis, the natural course of angiogenesis proves insufficient to counteract the hypoperfusion scenario (Zhu et al. ).
Numerous researchers have endeavored to utilize genes encoding growth factors to induce angiogenesis and facilitate the restoration of cerebral blood flow (Gaál et al. ). This review examines the latest developments in gene therapy for stroke patients, with a particular focus on its relevance to vascular dementia.

## Search strategy and study selection
We defined the temporal limits of the search (January 2000–December 2024) to ensure adequate coverage of landmark studies including translational work on neuroprotection and angiogenesis (Ayaz-Shah et al. ). The literature search was conducted across the PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases. Inclusion criteria comprised studies involving preclinical or clinical models of ischemic stroke, chronic hypoperfusion, vascular cognitive impairment, cerebrovascular gene therapy, and neurovascular alterations contributing to dementia. Exclusion criteria were conference abstracts, non–peer-reviewed sources, and studies unrelated to neurovascular pathology. This addition ensures the review adheres to scholarly expectations for reproducibility and systematic rigor as recommended in contemporary academic guidelines for narrative reviews (Miranda et al. ).

## Stroke injury mechanisms driving vascular dementia (VaD)
Current evidence indicates that, after stroke, several injury mechanisms operate in synergy to promote vascular dementia (VaD), in line with current models of vascular cognitive impairment (VCI) (Rundek et al. ; Yang ). Residual white-matter degeneration often persists after stroke; degeneration of oligodendrocytes, axonal injury, and the accumulation of small-vessel–related lesions all contribute to this chronic white-matter damage, which provides a structural substrate for long-term executive and attentional deficits (Markus and Joutel ; He et al. ). Chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH) is a frequent sequel of stroke, driven by impaired cerebral autoregulation and microvascular damage, and it further accelerates white-matter degeneration and cognitive decline (3,4). Prolonged neuroinflammation following ischemia and hypoperfusion disrupts intercellular communication, contributes to demyelination and neuronal injury, and is strongly implicated in the progression of vascular cognitive impairment and dementia (Tian et al. ; Ben-Ari et al. ). In parallel, blood–brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction allows blood-derived components and inflammatory mediators to enter the brain parenchyma, linking vascular risk factors, CCH, and chronic neuroinflammation to white-matter damage and neuronal vulnerability (Rajeev et al. ; Xu et al. ). Taken together, these processes—white-matter degeneration, persistent hypoperfusion, neuroinflammation, and BBB breakdown—act in concert to form a pathological cycle that connects the initial ischemic event to progressive cognitive decline and vascular brain injury, thereby explaining the increased risk of VaD after stroke (Rundek et al. ; Yang ; Rajeev et al. ).

## Stroke and its challenges
Stroke is a disruption of brain blood flow from a clot or vessel damage, historically termed CVA, cerebral infarction, or cerebral haemorrhage by the late twentieth century. Globally it is the top cause of lasting disability and the second leading cause of death (Rosamond et al. ; Sato et al. ), rising in low-income countries (Kalaria et al. ), especially in adults < 45 with serious complications (Satapathy et al. ). It occurs every 40 s, with ~ one-third of ischemic cases in high-income countries being cryptogenic (Tsao et al. ; Karceski ; Acampa et al. ), while higher fatality rates worsen the burden in low-income settings (Feigin et al. ). Care is limited by scarce training and stroke units, so management often relies on internal medicine services using MRI, angiography, ECG, and CT (Prust et al. ; Gadama et al. ). Stroke is a severe hypertension-related cerebrovascular disease affecting all ages. Key risks include smoking, diabetes, older age, alcohol use, heart failure, long working hours, alpha-blockers, and stress (Boehme et al. ). Care is hindered by poor symptom recognition, high treatment costs, mistrust in healthcare, and ignoring transient ischemic attacks that may later become stroke(Kayola et al. ; Magwood et al. ). Delayed diagnosis also postpones rt-PA treatment, and in developing countries the cost of t-PA and modern diagnostics leads to misdiagnosis and suboptimal care (Kayola et al. ; Wiyarta et al. ).
Stroke management is limited by few specialized stroke units and trained staff, especially in rural areas, and weak emergency response systems that delay triage/transport and reduce timely thrombolysis. Public awareness campaigns on stroke symptoms and urgent care remain insufficient, sustaining misinformation and delayed help-seeking (Wiyarta et al. ). Post-stroke rehabilitation (physio, occupational, speech therapy) is often inaccessible or underused, leading to prolonged disability and poorer quality of life, worsening health inequalities and community reintegration (Kayola et al. ). ## Treatment limitations and arduous recovery
There are three types of hemorrhagic stroke: subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), and subdural hematomas (Powers et al. ), each requiring distinct therapeutic approaches. Intravenous thrombolysis with tissue plasminogen activator (tPA, Alteplase) and mechanical thrombectomy are the standard of care, but their utility is severely constrained by a narrow therapeutic window (typically ≤ 4.5 h for tPA) and strict eligibility criteria (Hacke et al. ; Powers et al. ; Saver et al. ). Recombinant tissue-type plasminogen activator (r-tPA) is also used to restore blood flow in patients, thereby reducing morbidity rates in stroke patients. Studies have also shown that aspirin, when initiated between 24–48 h post-stroke onset, followed by Alteplase, significantly reduces disability and mortality (Powers et al. ; Herpich and Rincon ).
However, the efficacy of these treatments is critically time-sensitive, which highlights the “time is brain” concept in stroke care. The FDA approved some non-pharmacological treatments, like corkscrew-like devices, for patients who are not eligible to take t-PA. The device is delivered through a catheter to remove the clot mechanically (Lutsep ). The Penumbra System is another device designed to remove thrombus caused by large vessel thromboembolism (Bose et al. ), Nevertheless, these devices are not available in all hospitals, and their use is restricted by limited clinician expertise in healthcare settings. Secondary prevention strategies in non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke primarily involve antiplatelet therapy, which is the cornerstone of antithrombotic therapy. Commonly used agents include aspirin, clopidogrel, and a combination of extended-release dipyridamole with aspirin. Cilostazol is another option but is less commonly used (Kleindorfer et al. ; Gladstone et al. ). In atrial fibrillation and cardiac embolism, warfarin remains the first-line antithrombotic therapy. Non-pharmacological interventions, including carotid resection and angioplasty stents, are also recommended as secondary prevention strategies. These procedures play a critical role in reducing the risk of recurrent stroke, but their accessibility varies across different healthcare system (Cohen et al. ; Dawwas et al. ; Ferdinand and Nakhle ). For haemorrhagic strokes (including intracerebral and subarachnoid haemorrhage), management is primarily supportive and surgical, focused on hematoma evacuation and preventing re-bleeding, with no specific neuroprotective agents available (Hoh et al. ; Thilak et al. ; Veltkamp and Purrucker ; Christensen et al. xxxx; Van Essen et al. ; Nouri et al. ).

## Current methods of gene therapy
Gene therapy introduces genetic material into targeted cells to produce a therapeutic effect, typically by correcting or compensating for molecular abnormalities. Gene therapy techniques can be categorized into two main types: germline gene therapy and somatic gene therapy. The germline technique involves direct manipulation of germline cells to heal the subject, whereas somatic gene therapy entails the incorporation of genes into the individual's diploid cells, which is deemed safer as it impacts just the target cells. Somatic gene therapy, albeit safer yet transient, is deemed appropriate for numerous illnesses, in contrast to germline therapy, which has never been performed on people (Nayerossadat et al. ).

## Delivery systems
Gene therapy is considered successful only if the selected gene is delivered successfully. A delivery vehicle known as a vector can facilitate this process by transporting the therapeutic gene into the target cell, where it integrates with the DNA, leading to the rectification of the abnormality (Ramamoorth ). Gene delivery systems can be divided into germline and somatic delivery methods. The former can correct hereditary issues by directly influencing germline cells, whereas in the latter, genetic information cannot be transferred to offspring since the method entails inserting genes into diploid cells. The initial human gene transfer occurred in 1989 with lymphocytes that invaded tumors (Rosenberg et al. ; Culver et al. ). In 1990, the major quality therapy focused on ADA quality for the treatment of individuals with Severe Combined Immunodeficiency Defect (SCID) (Blaese et al. ). Firstly, in vivo methods involve administering a vector carrying cDNA systemically or locally to the organ, which may lead to an enzyme expression of the transduced cells. The discussed method has been investigated in different animal models of mucopolysaccharidoses, mainly utilizing viral vectors in most research (Aronovich and Hackett ). Viral vectors such as AAV and lentivirus delivered via intracerebral, intrathecal (IT), or intracerebroventricular (ICV) routes were effective in providing a therapeutic impact in brain disorders linked to MPS I, IIIIb, and IIIb, and VII animal models, which could produce a stable vector expression (Ponder et al. ).
The Second type is the ex vivo method that emphasizes in vitro genetic modification of cells, genetically leading to the generation of therapeutic proteins after the transfer to the patient’s body (Behrstock et al. ; Naldini ). Employment of stem cell therapy for stroke management has shown efficacy in preclinical studies as well as phase I & phase II clinical trials (Kondziolka et al. ; Andres et al. ). Dementia, which may result from the neurodegenerative disease known as Alzheimer’s disease, has also been tested using genetically altered fibroblasts as a therapeutic approach. Benefits of MSC transplantation in AD mouse models exhibited regulatory effects on microglial cell activity as well as neuroinflammation (Tanna and Sachan ; Naaldijk et al. ).
The translation of such preclinical findings, however, must be contextualized within the limitations of animal models. As highlighted in gene therapy development for other complex diseases, reliance solely on murine models is insufficient for robust translational prediction (Gruntman and Flotte ). Indeed, no animal model can fully recapitulate the complexity and heterogeneity of human disease, leading to frequent discrepancies between promising preclinical outcomes and negative clinical trial results (Soufizadeh et al. ). Moreover, short lifespans in rodents restrict longitudinal studies to under 1.5 years, impeding assessment of long-term gene expression, immune responses, or disease progression. Pre-existing neutralizing antibodies in non-human primates block viral vectors like AAV9, reducing transduction efficiency in brain and organs, while small animal sizes complicate dosage scaling and tissue penetration akin to human scales (Gopinath et al. ).

## Non-viral gene transfer
Non-viral vectors are increasingly used due to their safety and effective delivery into target organs. They may be categorized into physical approaches and chemical methods. Physical techniques encompass direct DNA injection by electroporation or the bombardment of gold particles to penetrate the cell membrane (Nishikawa and Huang ). The inefficiency, harmful characteristics, and absence of diversity in applications have failed gene delivery in humans, particularly human brain. Chemical techniques involve introducing gene molecules using chemical vectors, which are then delivered into the CNS. The integration of chemical vectors with gene molecules is done by various methods. The latter involves the amalgamation of positively charged vectors with anionic gene molecules, as well as the integration of hydrophobic materials with gene molecules. A pattern of establishing hydrogen bonds between the vector and the gene molecules helps in improving their interaction. The emergence of biodegradable polymers that envelop molecules is classified as non-viral chemical vectors (Ibraheem et al. ; Nie et al. ). Non-viral vectors have significantly evolved into cationic dendrimers, niosomes, gold nanoparticles, carbon-based carriers, liposomes, PLGA nanoparticles, and nano biomaterials that facilitate gene transport to the central nervous system (Ibraheem et al. ; Harder et al. ). Chronic brain hypoperfusion, resulting from cerebral occlusive disorders, can lead to neuropathological alterations, potentially including dementia (Kurumatani et al. ; Sekhon et al. ). An earlier investigation focused on the nonviral vector system, HVJ envelope vector (HVJ-E), and its effectiveness for transfecting the central nervous system. This nonviral vector system was examined both in vitro and in vivo, showing no evident toxicity. Artery blockage succeeded by successful gene transfection via intra-arterial injection may serve as a promising strategy for treating cerebral ischemia (Okada ). Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), with its anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic properties, may serve as a therapeutic option for ischemic stroke. The ultimate destination of both viral and non-viral vectors within a living organism is contingent upon selecting an adequate delivery pathway, as it significantly influences the vectors' access to specific target areas. Each distribution route may differ in several characteristics, including aspects influencing transgene expression, such as the response to blood components and retention time. ## Direct injections
In addition to the vascular route, the non-vascular method entails the direct injection of naked plasmid DNA into the target organ, even in the absence of a delivery vehicle for effective gene expression. When comparing the intravenous and intraportal delivery methods, the rAAV vector administered directly to the liver exhibited quicker and stronger therapeutic gene expression (Berraondo et al. ). Both the reduction of inflammatory reactions and the enhancement of therapeutic gene expression occurred when the adenovirus vector was delivered intrahepatically instead of by intravenous injection (Crettaz et al. ). Transfection limited to the administration site can be resolved through a plasmid DNA injection coupled with an electroporation technique, allowing greater transfection of muscle fibres (Mathiesen ). Lentivirus vector delivered intracerebroventricularly was employed in transporting therapeutic genes into the brain (Deroose et al. ). Moreover, transgenic expression in the lung was elicited by chitosan/plasmid DNA nanoparticles via the inhalation delivery route (Mohri et al. ).

## Cerebrospinal fluid delivery
There is a brisk flow of CSF from the skull into the bloodstream; however, the spread of medication into brain tissue from CSF is limited as it relies on diffusion, which diminishes with the square of the distance. This leads to the delivery of the drug to the ependymal surface of the cerebrum instead of the brain parenchyma (Pardridge ). While intrathecal administration of the medication for conditions impacting the brain, such as carcinomatosis, is necessary, it requires exposing the cerebrum to relatively high drug concentrations to effectively deliver the drug into the brain parenchyma (Yamada et al. ; Day-Lollini et al. ). Intraparenchymal infusion of rAAVs can bring about restricted quality delivery (Snyder et al. ) to address CNS disorders affecting a specific region of the brain. Treating widespread CNS pathology would need many injections for the human brain, rendering it potentially impractical. Given that the central nervous system (CNS) is bathed in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), direct infusion of recombinant adeno-associated viruses (rAAVs) into the CSF (Elliger et al. ; Watson et al. ) Recently, several BBB-crossing AAV serotypes, such as AAV9 and AAV.rh10, have been explored for wider CNS gene delivery after CSF infusion. In general, these studies have shown that a direct introduction of rAAV9 or rAAV.rh10 into the CSF space can trigger robust transduction of neurons and glial cells across various species, such as the mouse (Bey et al. ), rat (Hordeaux et al. ), cat (Bucher et al. ), dog (Hinderer et al. ), pig (Federici et al. ; Passini et al. ), and NHPs (Hinderer et al. ; Passini et al. ). Transduction in peripheral tissues may be lower compared to the systemic distribution of the same dose, although it is typically not diminished in most instances (Hordeaux et al. ; Schuster et al. ). In NHP research, CSF conveyance has appeared to oppose NAbs to some extent (Gray et al. ). Toward interpretation to clinical use, a few examinations have shown the helpful viability of CSF rAAV to treat diverse CNS infections in creature models, including SMA (Passini et al. ), amyotrophic horizontal sclerosis (ALS) (Wang et al. ; Dirren et al. ), and lysosomal illnesses with CNS involvement (Hinderer et al. ; Hordeaux et al. ).
Methods such as intracerebral ventricular (ICV), intracisternal magna (CM), or intrathecal (IT) routes are used for delivering drugs into CSF. Of these, the IT method is relatively simple since it can be performed in an outpatient setting, whereas the first two methods require specific equipment for implementation (Federici et al. ). Moreover, the speed of injection also influences gene delivery patterns in the neurotoxins, as an injection given at a slow speed affects more of the spinal cord (Li et al. ). Furthermore, rAAV9 administration to the subarachnoid space was facilitated with an osmotic pump, at a slow rate over 24 h, resulting in an omnipresent expression of gene (Murlidharan et al. ).

AAV capsids have been utilized to enhance gene delivery beyond the CNS. Among AAV9 variations, AAV-PHP.B (Deverman et al. ) and its advanced counterpart, the more proficient variation, AAV-PHP.eB (Chan et al. ) has been shown to the adult BBB and empowers effective quality exchange with the CNS. Subsequently, extensive neurological tests were done in mice utilizing AAV-PHP.B and AAV-PHP.eB (Chan et al. ; Zelikowsky et al. ), which encompassed hereditary shortage rectification (Using and PHP.B:NPC1 Ameliorates Disease Phenotype in Mouse Model of Niemann-Pick C1 Disease ) and neurological sickness displaying (Morabito et al. ).
CNS tropism of AAV-PHP.B or related capsids in rats (Jackson et al. ; Dayton et al. ), nonhuman primates (NHPs), and BALB/cJ mice (Zelikowsky et al. ; Morabito et al. ) has yielded contrasting results (Hordeaux et al. ). Receptors for AAV9 transduced by LY6A may serve as a unique pathway for AAV-PHP.B capsids crossing the mouse BBB. Research has demonstrated that Ly6a expression on three AAV-PHP.B vectors with unique peptide insertions (TLAVPFK, SVSKPFL, and FTLTTPK) has improved their binding capability, confirming that the Ly6a receptor can be selected as the preferred option for crossing the C57BL/6 J BBB.

## Gene Therapy for Cerebral Ischemia
Ischemic cerebrovascular disease has been considered a key focus for gene therapy in the realm of cerebrovascular diseases (Sato et al. ). Gene therapy collaboration in cerebrovascular injury treatment may be due to its effective localized transgene expression at a penumbral focal region after ischemia when mediated via viral vector (Gunnett and Heistad ).
A stepwise overview of gene-therapy development after stroke. Post-stroke pathology (neuronal loss, white-matter injury, BBB disruption, neuroinflammation) guides therapeutic transgene targets aimed at neuroprotection (e.g., Bcl-2, NeuroD1, GDNF, NGF, BDNF), angiogenesis/BBB repair (VEGF, Ang-1, HIF-1α, CXCL12), and anti-inflammatory/antioxidant effects (IL-10, HO-1, ECSOD/SOD1). Targets are delivered via viral platforms (AAV, adenovirus, HSV-1, lentivirus) or non-viral systems (nanoparticles, liposomes, dendrimers), administered through intracerebral/intra-arterial routes, CSF, or intravenous infusion, leading to improved vascular repair, reduced infarct volume, and better cognitive recovery as illustrated in Fig. . 
Over a decade ago, a study observed a significant reduction in infarct size due to the expression of interleukin-1 antagonist mediated by adenoviral vectors before ischemia. Subsequently, it was discovered that neuronal protection can be offered by the gene transfer of glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (Kitagawa et al. ) and atrial natriuretic peptide (Lin et al. ) to mitigate ischemic infarction.
Research on advancement revealed that, in a post-acute phase of ischemia, SDF-1α gene transfer using adeno-associated virus to a middle cerebral artery occlusion resulted in reduced brain atrophy followed by the high-level expression of SDF-1αin an extensive area surrounding the site of administration (Li et al. ).
A study that was conducted presented its results on significant enhancement of white matter damage caused by brain ischemia and promoted the proliferation of Oligodendrocyte Progenitor Cells in the subventricular zone and their migration to the perifocal region using CXCL12 gene therapy. This research is significant as it highlights that CXCL12 gene therapy significantly prolonged the therapeutic time window after brain ischemia (Li et al. ).
A recent study employing NeuroD1-adeno-associated virus-based gene therapy in situ astrocyte-to-upon conversion after an ischemic injury demonstrated regeneration of immense new neurons that could arrest the consequent tissue loss at the site of the injury. This study establishes a novel approach for the regeneration of new neurons utilizing endogenous glial cells, thereby facilitating brain repair (Chen et al. ).
Exceptional reduction in ischemic infarct volume and number of terminal deoxynucleoferase-mediated dUTP-biotin in situ nick end label (TUNEL)-positive cells was observed in a previous study that focused on the GDNF gene with a carrier vector as Sendai virus. In addition to this, preventing the impeded death of neurons in hippocampal CA1 pyramids was observed in SeV-mediated NGF gene therapy delivered 30 min post-ischemia (Shirakura et al. ). The above findings emphasized that SeV/neurotrophic factors contributed therapeutically to addressing the effects following ischemia (Jin et al. ).
Due to limited regenerative capacity and slow turnover of neural cells, endogenous cell replacement is insufficient to repair the affected zones within the central nervous system of stroke patients. The potential approach of stem cell therapy for neurorepair and neurodegeneration surfaces through various neural trophic factors and the replacement of damaged neurons (Hao et al. ). Reduction of infarct volume and attenuated neural death, depletion of astrocytes and microglial cells, and increased expression of oligodendroglia cells and synaptic proteins have confirmed remodelling of the brain cortex within the stroke region. These findings indicate that protection and recovery of the brain in rats following a significant injury with a triple gene therapy done with adenoviral vectors carrying vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), glial cell-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), and neu(GDNF), ll adhesion molecule (NCAM) or gene-engineered gene-licial cord blood mononuclear cells (UCB-MC) overexpressing recombinant VEGF, GDNF, and NCAM (Markosyan et al. ).

## Gene Therapy and Cognitive Improvement Beyond Infarct Reduction
Accumulating evidence demonstrates that gene therapy can enhance cognitive performance and improve neural network function following stroke, extending its benefits beyond the traditional focus on infarct reduction and neuroprotection. Historically, most gene therapy studies in stroke have emphasized limiting infarct expansion and preserving neuronal viability. However, plasmid-mediated delivery of the hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) gene has now been shown to yield significant improvements in learning and memory, with enhanced Morris water maze performance persisting for several weeks after experimental stroke (Frederickson ; Winovich et al. ). In addition, viral vector–mediated suppression of the transcriptional regulator REST/NRSF selectively improves hippocampal-dependent memory while sparing non-target genes, providing direct evidence that gene therapy can modulate higher-order neural circuits rather than solely protecting tissue (Xu et al. ). Beyond stroke, several AAV-based gene therapies developed for Alzheimer’s disease, particularly those targeting synaptic function, neurotrophic support such as BDNF, and vascular integrity, have consistently reversed learning and memory deficits in preclinical models, demonstrating the capacity of gene transfer to restore memory-relevant neural networks (Nagahara et al. ; Wada et al. ). Early-phase human trials of AAV2-BDNF have similarly demonstrated increased metabolic activity in hippocampal and entorhinal regions of individuals with mild cognitive impairment, supporting the potential for cognitive benefit in clinical settings (Meglio xxxx). Despite these encouraging findings, gene therapy remains underexplored in the context of post-stroke cognitive impairment and vascular dementia (VaD), underscoring the urgent need for therapeutic strategies that integrate cognitive endpoints with mechanisms central to VaD, including white-matter repair, neuroinflammation resolution, and restoration of the blood–brain barrier.

## Reasons for Poor Translation of Preclinical Success to Clinical Trials
Despite numerous preclinical demonstrations of neurovascular protection and cognitive rescue in rodent models, translation of gene therapy for post-stroke vascular dementia to human clinical trials has been markedly limited. A major contributor is the poor fidelity of rodent stroke models, which typically employ young, healthy animals that do not reflect the advanced age, vascular comorbidities, and chronic small-vessel disease seen in human patients with vascular dementia (Kleinschnitz et al. ). Human pathology involves progressive white-matter loss, microvascular rarefaction, chronic inflammation, and mixed etiologies, none of which are adequately replicated by common rodent middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) models (Sri et al. ). Another barrier is the difference in treatment timing: preclinical studies almost always administer therapies in the acute window (minutes to hours after ischemia), whereas real patients present in subacute or chronic phases, long after neuronal death, gliosis, and vascular remodelling have occurred (Xu and Pan ). Additionally, many successful animal studies rely on invasive intracerebral injections that are impractical or unethical in aging human populations. Immunological differences further complicate translation, as humans exhibit strong pre-existing immunity to AAV serotypes, leading to vector neutralization and reduced efficacy, an issue not typically encountered in laboratory animals (Martino and Markusic ). Finally, behavioral outcome measures in rodents (e.g., Morris water maze, foot-fault tests) often fail to map onto human cognitive domains such as executive dysfunction and processing speed, leading to discrepancies in measured therapeutic benefit (Sri et al. ).

## Safety Issues in CNS Gene Therapy
Safety remains a central challenge in translating gene therapy to clinical use, particularly in vulnerable populations such as elderly patients with post-stroke vascular dementia. Immunogenicity is one of the most significant concerns, as AAV vectors can trigger both innate and adaptive immune responses, including the activation of neutralizing antibodies and cytotoxic T cells that can eliminate transduced cells or induce systemic inflammation (Ertl ). High-dose AAV administration- which is often required for adequate CNS penetration-has resulted in severe toxicities in nonhuman primates and pigs, including hepatotoxicity, dorsal root ganglion degeneration, and complement activation (Baldrick et al. ). Another safety concern is insertional mutagenesis, particularly with integrating vectors such as lentivirus, which carry a theoretical and historically documented risk of activating oncogenes, as observed in early SCID-X1 trials (Howe et al. ). Although AAV is generally considered non-integrating, rare insertion events have been reported, especially at high vector doses (Sabatino et al. ). Off-target gene modification poses additional risk in genome-editing approaches such as CRISPR-Cas9. Unintended double-strand breaks, chromosomal rearrangements, and p53 pathway activation have been documented in preclinical models, raising concerns about long-term oncogenic potential (Karimi et al. ). Collectively, these safety issues underscore the need for careful vector engineering, optimized dosing, and long-term monitoring in clinical applications targeting the CNS.

## Recent Advances in CNS Gene Therapy
Recent technological advances have significantly improved the feasibility of gene therapy for neurological disorders and may eventually benefit vascular dementia after stroke. Engineered AAV capsids such as AAV.CAP-B10 and AAV.CAP-Mac exhibit far superior neuronal and glial tropism, enabling more efficient CNS transduction in nonhuman primates compared with conventional AAV9 (Chuapoco et al. ). These vectors were developed through directed evolution and deep mutational scanning, producing capsids capable of crossing the BBB with higher fidelity (Campos et al. ).
Parallel advances in CRISPR gene-editing systems, including base editors and compact nucleases such as SaCas9 and Cas12f, have expanded the therapeutic toolkit, enabling precise gene correction or epigenetic modulation within the size constraints of AAV vectors (Hino et al. ). Another major development is mRNA-LNP therapeutics, which provide transient, non-integrating expression with reduced immunogenic risks. The success of LNP vaccines has accelerated interest in their use for CNS applications, particularly when combined with targeting ligands to facilitate BBB penetration (Hou et al. ; Cao et al. ; Moosavi et al. ). Clinically, gene therapy has shown strong success in CNS diseases such as spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), where AAV9-delivered SMN1 (Zolgensma) demonstrated life-saving benefits (Blair ), and in Batten disease and ALS, where AAV-mediated gene supplementation or silencing approaches are progressing through human trials (Jih et al. ; Worgall et al. ). Additionally, astrocyte-to-neuron reprogramming strategies using transcription factors like NeuroD1 have shown promise in restoring neuronal populations and improving functional recovery after stroke in rodent models, representing a potentially transformative approach for chronic neurovascular injury (Chen et al. ; Livingston et al. ; Clark et al. ) as summarized in Fig. .

## Gene Therapy for Subarachnoid Haemorrhage
A ruptured cerebral artery aneurysm is considered the primary cause of subarachnoid hemorrhage(SAH) (Sato et al. ). High rate of mortality and morbidity recorded can attributed to the cerebral vasospasm that follows SAH, occurring in 30–70% of individuals (Beck et al. ; Sharma and Sawarkar ). This prolonged spasm applied to the cerebral blood vessels leading to hemorrhagic stroke. The subsequent occurrence of the vasospasm succeeding SAH makes the gene product delivery and the transgene expression possible. At the same time, SAH-associated hematoma does not offer protection to the entovirus-mediated gene product administered intracisternally, indicating that gene therapy is an emerging focus for managing SAH-related vasospasm (Chu et al. ). Initial studies involving gene therapy focused on the prospect of vasospasm associated with SAH (Sato et al. ) as shown in Fig. . A study demonstrated the arrest of vasospasm following SAH, linked to excessive expression of the endothelial nitric oxide synthase gene incorporated in an adenoviral vector (Chen et al. ; Onoue et al. ).
 
A potent vasodilator, calcitonin gene-related peptide, being the first successfully used vasoactive peptide by gene transfer, could significantly reduce the vasoconstriction followed by SAH in rabbits (Toyoda et al. ). One of the strategies to avoid vasospasm was to alter the metabolism of hemoglobin and heme, since hemoglobin plays a key role in the pathogenesis of SAH that precedes vasospasm. Previous research emphasized the neuroprotective benefits resulting from the induction of HO-1 following SAH. Increased HO activity induced by the adenovirus expressed HO-1, lessened vasospasm after SAH by reducing the arterial contractions, and countered the diminished blood flow on exposure to hemoglobin (Ono et al. ).
Derangements in the cerebral vessels observed during subarachnoid hemorrhage are ascribed to elevated amounts of superoxide anion. It was proved by the successful gene transfer of CU/Zn SOD-1 that led to the significant alleviation of the impairment of cerebral blood flow that occurs in the acute stage of SAH (Shin et al. ). Ongoing efforts to alleviate vasospasm linked to SAH, interestingly, gene therapy using extracellular superoxide dismutase (ECSOD) delivered with adenovirus also suggested improved vasodilator responses after experimental SAH (Watanabe et al. ). Later, a research team presented findings that the adrenomedullin (ADM) gene delivered via the plasmid vector pVAX1 alleviated the vasospasm on the cerebral blood vessels, followed by SAH in a rat model of CVS (Li et al. ).
Apoptosis is the crucial mechanism for the early brain damage that generally occurs three days post-AH (Cahill, 2006) and is also considered to be involved in the pathogenic process of cerebral vasospasm (Li et al. ). Research conducted in experimental subarachnoid haemorrhage in rats demonstrated that inhibition of Sentrin/SUMO–specific protease (SENP3) mediated by lentivirus produced a suppression of apoptosis, attributed to a rise in SENP, which is a direct impact of high oxidative stress level often observed after SAH (Yang et al. ).

## Key Gene Targets in Vascular Dementia
Key gene targets for neurovascular repair primarily include BDNF, VEGF, FGF-2, HO-1, and CXCL12, which act on core mechanisms implicated in vascular dementia pathogenesis. BDNF signaling improves cerebrovascular dysfunction by modulating astrocytes, microglia, BBB permeability, revascularization, and glia–neuron coupling under chronic cerebral hypoperfusion. The same pathways enhance synaptic plasticity, dendritic spine maturation, NMDA receptor signaling, and cholinergic function, supporting memory in vascular dementia and subcortical ischemic vascular dementia (Song ). On the other hand, VEGF in vascular dementia is a key mediator of neuroprotection, angiogenesis, and regulation of cerebral blood flow, but its impact depends on aging‑related endothelial dysfunction and inflammatory milieu (Agrawal et al. ).
FGF‑2 is part of a broader fibroblast growth factor (FGF) system implicated in cognitive disorders, where it promotes hippocampal neurogenesis, protects astrocytes from ischemia–reperfusion, and induces angiogenesis. It also supports oligodendrocyte proliferation, maturation, and survival, helping maintain white‑matter integrity, and modulates microglial phenotype and Aβ clearance in dementia models (Zhai et al. ). HO‑1 is induced by oxidative and nitrosative stress and can be neuroprotective through anti‑inflammatory and antioxidant effects (via CO, biliverdin/bilirubin), which is relevant to small‑vessel disease–related oxidative injury in vascular dementia (Barone et al. ).
Finally, CXCL12 signaling has context‑dependent roles in cognition. It can support neuroprotection and neurogenesis but also drive monocyte recruitment, perivascular macrophage activation, and neuroinflammation leading to cognitive impairment. Experimental work in neuropathic pain models shows that CXCL12‑driven monocyte transmigration into hippocampal perivascular spaces induces gliosis and memory decline, whereas blocking CXCL12–CXCR4 signaling prevents these changes, a mechanism implicated across vascular cognitive impairment (Barsegyan and Maas ; Mai et al. ).

## Conclusion
In conclusion, the emerging field of neurovascular gene therapy offers a scientifically grounded and potentially transformative strategy for preventing and mitigating vascular dementia (VaD) following ischemic stroke. Vascular dementia arises from a complex cascade involving chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, white-matter injury, and progressive neurodegeneration. Current therapeutic options remain limited, particularly in addressing long-term neurovascular damage after stroke. Gene therapies directly targets these pathological mechanisms by enabling sustained expression of neuroprotective, angiogenic, and anti-inflammatory molecules within the injured brain effects that cannot be achieved with conventional pharmacotherapy.
Among the most promising targets, BDNF supports synaptic plasticity, neuronal survival, and cognitive resilience, counteracting the long-term synaptic dysfunction characteristic of VaD. VEGF and FGF-2 promote angiogenesis and vascular remodeling, thereby improving chronic hypoperfusion and restoring microvascular integrity key contributors to white-matter degeneration in VaD. HO-1, a potent anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective enzyme, reduces oxidative stress, attenuates endothelial injury, and stabilizes the neurovascular unit following ischemia. Likewise, CXCL12 (SDF-1α) enhances oligodendrogenesis, white-matter repair, and progenitor cell migration, extending the therapeutic window for repairing post-stroke tissue loss. Collectively, these gene targets intervene across multiple levels of the ischemia → hypoperfusion → neurodegeneration pathway, providing a mechanistically coherent strategy for slowing or preventing the development of vascular dementia after stroke.
Despite these advances, several challenges remain before gene therapy can be reliably translated into clinical practice. Future research should prioritize:Optimization of delivery systems, including BBB-penetrant AAV serotypes and non-viral nanocarriers tailored for deep-white-matter targets relevant to VaD.
Long-term safety evaluation, with particular emphasis on immune responses, off-target expression, and sustained transgene regulation in aging cerebrovascular tissue.
Combination gene strategies, leveraging synergistic interactions for example, pairing angiogenic factors (VEGF, FGF-2) with neuroprotective or anti-inflammatory genes (BDNF, HO-1).
Mechanistically informed therapeutic timing, especially the development of interventions tailored to the subacute and chronic phases of hypoperfusion where vascular dementia pathology evolves.
Biomarker development for monitoring vascular integrity, white-matter repair, and treatment response in patients at high risk for post-stroke dementia.
Clinical trial frameworks designed for long-term cognitive outcomes rather than short-term infarct reduction.

As our understanding of neurovascular biology and vector engineering continues to expand, gene therapy is poised to shift the paradigm of stroke recovery from one of damage limitation to one of structural and functional restoration. Targeting key molecular pathways such as BDNF, VEGF, FGF-2, HO-1, and CXCL12 represents a promising and mechanistically robust approach to altering the natural history of vascular dementia. With continued refinement and rigorous translational research, gene-based interventions have the potential to deliver durable, disease-modifying effects and redefine therapeutic possibilities for patients with cerebrovascular disease.
