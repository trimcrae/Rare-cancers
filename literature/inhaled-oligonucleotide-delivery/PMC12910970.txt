## Abstract

## Background
Diabetic wounds are a debilitating complication of diabetes mellitus with a high rate of amputation and mortality. 6-Hydroxyhyoscyamine hydrobromide (6-HH) is a belladonna alkaloid with smooth-muscle-relaxing and microcirculation-enhancing properties, yet its role in diabetic wounds remains unknown.

## Methods
In vivo, diabetic wounds were established in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced type 1 diabetic mice, high fat diet (HFD)/STZ-induced type 2 diabetic mice, and db/db diabetic mice to investigate the therapeutic effects of 6-HH, and skin samples were collected for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining, immunofluorescence, RNA-seq analysis, and western blotting. In vitro, the mechanism of action of 6-HH on cytokines, nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) signals, and the interaction between sirtuin 6 (SIRT6) and p65 in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced macrophages were detected by using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), western blotting, reverse-transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), immunofluorescence, and immunoprecipitation, while molecular docking was used to evaluate the binding energy between 6-HH and SIRT6 protein. Coculture systems of THP-1 cells–human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs)/immortalized human keratinocytes (HaCaTs) were established to investigate the effects of 6-HH on cross-talk between macrophages and endothelial cells/keratinocytes. SIRT6-specific inhibitor Oss_128167 was applied in vitro and in vivo to verify the mechanism of 6-HH in diabetic wound healing.

## Results
6-HH exhibited excellent pro-healing effect in the three types of diabetic mouse model. RNA-seq analysis found that 6-HH recovered diabetic-induced aberrant expression changes of genes in the local wounds, especially those related to M1 macrophage polarization with downregulation of Toll-like receptor (TLR) signals and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP+) nucleosidase activity. Molecular docking analysis found that 6-HH could effectively bind to the active site of the SIRT6 protein. Remarkably, decline of SIRT6 in M1 macrophages resulted in lysine hyperacetylation, while activation and stabilization of SIRT6 by 6-HH suppressed M1 macrophage polarization and hyperacetylation through inhibiting p65 transcription with deacetylation of p65Lys310 and H3K9, contributing to improve angiogenesis and re-epithelization through interaction between macrophages and endothelial cells/keratinocytes. However, pharmacological inhibition of SIRT6 reversed the action of 6-HH in macrophages and diabetic wounds.

## Conclusions
Collectively, deacetylase SIRT6 might be a direct pharmacological target of 6-HH that downregulates the hyperacetylated state of macrophages, thus contributing to diabetic wound healing.

## Introduction
Diabetic foot (DF) serves as a major cause of the high rate of amputation among patients with diabetes that seriously increases the economic burden and social costs [ , ]. Epidemiological studies reveal that up to 25% of case of diabetes will develop to DF, and these chronic wound ulcers are associated with adverse clinical outcomes [ ], leading to a 5-year mortality rate of 50% in patients with DF [ ]. Although a variety of treatments for DF have been developed [ , ], clinical management of DF remains challenging owing to its multifactorial pathophysiology, especially the chronic inflammatory responses [ ]. In this respect, the development of novel agents that promote diabetic wound healing via targeting dysregulated inflammatory responses is crucial for treatment of DF and prevention of amputation.
Macrophages are central regulators of immune responses and tissue homeostasis that exhibit dynamic phenotypic plasticity and play a pivotal part in the physiopathology of diabetic wound healing [ ]. Hyperacetylation has been recognized as the key contributor to the pathological pro-inflammatory macrophage activation and the progression of diabetic diseases [ ]. In diabetic conditions, persistent stimulation with Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) ligands leads to aberrant protein hyperacetylation in macrophages, which disrupts normal epigenetic regulation and reinforces a persistent pro-inflammatory phenotype [ ]. M1 macrophages generally exhibit high levels of acetylation [ ], which generally modulates the activity of key transcription factors relevant to inflammation, especially p65, whose hyperacetylation enhances its DNA-binding ability, prolongs nuclear retention, and amplifies transcriptional activity of pro-inflammatory genes [ ]. On the contrary, deacetylation restores the positive charge and strengthens DNA–histone interactions that prevents the access of transcription factors to promoters in the chromatin [ ]. Deacetylases, such as HDAC3 and HDAC6, suppress inflammatory responses and facilitate macrophage polarization to M2 phenotype via recruiting to the promoter of interleukin (IL)-10 [ ]. Remarkably, elevating the activity of class III histone deacetylases improves wound healing in diabetic mice through reducing prolonged M1 macrophage polarization [ , ]. Therefore, driving deacetylation of M1 macrophages might be an imperative way to promote wound healing in diabetes.
6-Hydroxyhyoscyamine hydrobromide (6-HH), a belladonna alkaloid, exhibits outstanding performance in relaxing smooth muscles, relieving vasospasm, and enhancing microcirculation [ ]. Besides, 6-HH could alleviate inflammatory response in LPS-treated macrophages, and enhance M2 polarization via inhibiting G9a-mediated methylation and interferon regulatory factor 4 [ , ]. However, there are currently no research studies on its use for treatment of DF. Herein, diabetic wounds were established in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced type 1 diabetes, high-fat diet (HFD)/STZ-induced type 2 diabetes, and db/db diabetic mice to explore the role of acetylation and the action of 6-HH in diabetic wounds.

## Materials and methods

## Establishment of diabetic mice
For the HFD/STZ-induced type 2 diabetic mice model, male C57BL/6 J mice aged 6–8 weeks were divided into a regular chow diet or a HFD (Trophic Animal Feed High-Tech Co., China) for 8 weeks. STZ was freshly prepared in citrate buffer (pH 4.5). Subsequently, the HFD-fed mice were intraperitoneally injected daily with 40 mg/kg STZ for continuous 10 days. After STZ injection finished, mice with random blood glucose above 11.1 mmol/L and with polydipsia, polyuria, and polyphagia were regarded as diabetic mice and were subjected to the skin wound model [ ]. The mice fed with regular chow diet served as NC group, and the diabetic mice were randomly divided (n = 6) into the HFD/STZ group, HFD/STZ-6-HH group (200 mg/kg), and HFD/STZ-6-HH (200 mg/kg) + Oss_128167 (Selleck, USA) group. After establishment of the skin wound model, the mice in HFD/STZ-6-HH groups were intragastrically treated with 200 mg/kg of 6-HH, and mice in NC group and HFD/STZ group were intragastrically administrated with the same volume of saline once a day for 8 consecutive days from the day of surgery. The mice in HFD/STZ-6-HH + Oss_128167 group were intragastrically administrated with Oss_128167 (50 mg/kg) every other day and 6-HH (200 mg/kg) once a day for 8 days. Samples were collected at 1, 3, 5, 7, and 8 days after surgery, respectively.
For the model of db/db diabetic mice, 10-week-old C57BL/6 mice, db/m control mice and db/db diabetic mice were randomly divided (n = 6) into NC group, db/m group, db/db group, db/db + 6-HH (200 mg/kg) group, and db/db + 6-HH (200 mg/kg) + Oss_128167 (50 mg/kg) group. After establishment of the skin wound model, mice in the db/db + 6-HH group were intragastrically administrated with 6-HH, and mice in NC, db/m, and db/db groups were intragastrically administrated with the same volume of saline once a day for 12 consecutive days from the day of surgery. Mice in the db/db-6-HH + Oss_128167 group were intragastrically administrated with Oss_128167 every other day and 6-HH once a day for 12 days. Samples were collected at 12 days after surgery.

## Skin wound model
At first, 3% isoflurane was inhaled to anesthetize the mice. Subsequently, two full-length wounds through the panniculus carnosus were induced dorsally with a 6-mm biopsy puncture needle. The wound images were captured once a day after surgery, and ImageJ was used to quantitate the wound area. The representative wound photographs truly reflected the group average. The rates of wound healing were calculated according to the following equation: (wound area on day 0 − wound area on day X)/wound area on day 0 × 100%.

## Transcriptome analysis
Total RNA was extracted from granulation tissues of mice using TRIzol reagent (Magen). Agilent Bioanalyzer 4150 (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) was used to detect the RNA integrity number (RIN) to detect the quality, concentration, and purity. Paired-end (PE) libraries were prepared with ABclonal mRNA-seq Lib Prep Kit (ABclonal, China). Sequencing was performed by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). HISAT2 software was used to compare the reference genome and the clean reads, for subsequent analysis of the mapped reads. FeatureCounts was then used to calculate the number of reads to each gene, according to the length of the gene, that is, the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped fragments (FPKM) value of each gene. Differential expression analysis of genes between groups was performed using DESeq2 with default screening thresholds for differentially expressed genes of fold change ≥ 2 and adjusted P value < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed by using Enrichr (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/). Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were performed using the OmicStudio tools at https://www.omicstudio.cn.

## Cell culture
HUVECs (cat. no. #8000), purchased from ScienCell Research Laboratories (USA), were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (Gibco, USA). Human immortalized keratinocyte (HaCaT) cells (cat. no. TCHu 57) and human monocytic cell line (THP-1 cells, cat. no. SCSP-5091) from Stem Cell Bank (Chinese Academy of Sciences) were cultured according to the instructions. Specially, THP-1 cells were cultured in 200 nM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; Sigma, USA) for 24 h to differentiate into macrophages for the following stimulation with LPS (Sigma) or IL-4 (Sino Biological Inc.), respectively.

## Bone-marrow-derived macrophage (BMDM) differentiation
Male C57BL/6 J mice aged 6 weeks were utilized for isolation of BMDMs as in previous reports [ ]. The BMDMs were pretreated with 6-HH for 4 h and exposed to LPS for the corresponding time.

## RT-qPCR
The relative level of mRNAs was standardized with Gapdh or 18s and was evaluated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer information for THP-1 cells, BMDMs, and granulation tissues is available in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, respectively [ ].

## Western blot analysis
Western blot analysis was performed as described previously [ ]. Enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaging system (Tanon, China) were used to analyze the immunoblots. The antibody information is provided in Supplementary Table S3.

## Molecular docking
Docking simulations were conducted using Discovery Studio 2019 Client (Biovia Co., San Diego, CA, USA) as previously reported [ ]. Initially, the target protein SIRT6 (PDB code 3K35) was obtained by removing any solvent molecules, adding hydrogen atoms, and subjecting the structure to minimization using the “Clean protein” protocol within Discovery Studio. Subsequently, the 6-HH ligands were prepared using the “Ligand Preparation” modules embedded in the software. Docking simulations employing the CDOCKER algorithm integrated into Discovery Studio were performed for the investigated compounds at the active site of the SIRT6 homology model.

## Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
The CESTA was carried out as previously reported [ ]. After 12 h of incubation of THP-1 cells with or without 200 μM 6-HH, cell lysates were collected and the concentration was adjusted. The respective lysates were separated and heated at different temperatures (45–65 °C) for 5 min, after which the samples were collected by centrifugation for western blot analysis.

## Immunoprecipitation
THP-1 cells were pretreated with Oss_128167 for 30 min and co-cultured with 6-HH for 4 h, and then stimulated with LPS. Subsequently, the protein lysates were incubated with p65 or IgG antibody and precipitated with Protein A/G magnetic beads (Selleck, USA). Then, the beads were collected and washed for detection of the level of SIRT6 and of p65 acetylation by western blotting.

## Histochemical analysis
H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining were used for histological evaluation. For immunohistochemical staining of CD11c, the sections were stained with CD11c antibody (1:200; ABclonal, China), and then immersed in the secondary antibody (ABclonal, China) after washing in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Then, nuclei were restained using hematoxylin (Beyotime, China) after visualization by 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB; Beyotime, China). Finally, the images of slides were captured with microscope (Nikon, Japan).

## Immunofluorescence
Immunohistofluorescence analysis of tissue sections and cells was performed as described previously [ ]. Confocal fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan) was used to analyze slides. The information of antibodies is presented in Supplementary Table S3.

## Isolation of nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins
Nuclear Separation kit (Beyotime) was used for isolation of nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins of THP-1 cells. Subsequently, the expression of p65 protein was detected by immunoblotting.

## Co-culture of macrophages and HUVECs/HaCaT cells
Differentiated THP-1 cells were pretreated with Oss_128167 for 30 min, and co-treated with 6-HH for 4 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with LPS and the supernatants were collected at different time points for HUVEC and HaCaT cell culture. HUVEC and HaCaT cell samples were collected at the corresponding time for subsequent western blot analysis and immunofluorescence.

## Cell scratch assay
Culture inserts (Ibidi, 80209) were placed into 24-well plates, and 70 μL of cell suspension was seeded into each well of the inserts and cultured until the cells adhered. After the cells adhered to the wall, the inserts were carefully removed with forceps and washed with PBS. THP-1 supernatant (200 μL) was added to 24-well plates, 0 h scratch and 24 h scratch were observed and photographed, and the area of cell migration was analyzed by ImageJ software.

## Tube formation assay
Matrigel@ (356234, Corning, USA) was thawed at 4 °C and added to 96-well plates, subsequently polymerized for 0.5 h at 37 °C. After coagulation, HUVECs were resuspended with THP-1 cell supernatant and seeded into 96-well plates. Tube formation was observed with an inverted microscope after 4 h. The number of tubes and branch points were analyzed by ImageJ software.

## Transwell assay
Transwell chambers with 6.5-mm-diameter polycarbonate membrane (pore size 8 μm) were used for cell invasion assay. Supernatant (500 μL) from different groups of THP-1 cells was added to the lower chamber, and HUVEC and HaCaT cells were added to the upper chamber. After 18 h of incubation, migrated cells were fixed and stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Cell migration was measured by calculating cells on the lower surface using fluorescence inverted microscopy.

## Explant migration assays
For ex vivo explant migration assays, dorsal skin tissues were obtained from 6-week-old C57BL/6 J mice and washed with 75% ethanol followed by PBS containing 1% penicillin–streptomycin (PS). Subcutaneous fat was gently removed from skin tissues, then 1.5-mm circular punch biopsies were taken and placed to the bottom of culture dishes with 2 μL Growth Factor Reduced Matrigel (BD Biosciences) [ ]. Skin biopsies were treated with CM from BMDMs exposed to LPS and 6-HH, and explant outgrowths were imaged and analyzed using ImageJ software after 4 days.

## Statistical analysis
Data are shown as mean ± standard error on the mean (SEM) and analyzed using SPSS software. Differences between mean values of normally distributed data were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## 6-HH accelerates wound healing in both type 1 and type 2 diabetic mice
To study the effect of 6-HH (Supplementary Fig. S1A) on diabetic wound healing, skin wound model was established in STZ-induced type 1 diabetic mice and HFD/STZ-induced type 2 diabetic mice (Supplementary Figs. S1B and S2A). 6-HH treatment obviously accelerated the wound healing course of these two models (Supplementary Figs. S1E and S3A, B) without influencing body weight and random blood glucose (RBG) (Supplementary Figs. S1C, D and S2B, C). The rate of wound closure in 6HH-treated STZ mice was 79% after 8 days post-injury (cf. 56% in untreated STZ mice) (Supplementary Fig. S1E), and the rate of wound closure in 6HH-treated HFD/STZ mice was 82% after 8 days post-injury (cf. 41% in untreated HFD/STZ mice) (Supplementary Fig. S3A, B).
Besides, 6-HH shortened the width of wound, increased the epithelial thickness, enhanced the thickness of granulation tissue (Supplementary Material Fig. S3C), and reduced the number of CD86+ macrophages and elevated that of CD206+ macrophages and CD31+ cells with upregulation of hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α), keratin 10, and alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) in diabetic wound (Supplementary Fig. S3D–F). Thus, 6-HH could accelerate diabetic wound healing via modulating macrophage polarization and promoting angiogenesis and re-epithelialization processes.

## 6-HH recovers aberrant gene changes related to M1 macrophages in diabetic wounds
Next, RNA sequencing was conducted on granulation tissues to determine the molecular signals by which 6-HH promotes diabetic wound healing (Fig.  A). Principal component analysis (PCA) exhibited a strong clustering of samples from NC group, HFD/STZ group, and HFD/STZ-6-HH group (Fig.  B). K-means cluster analysis revealed the entire genes from three groups could be divided into six clusters with different patterns of variability (Supplementary Fig. S4A, B). The heatmap illustrated that most of genes from diabetic mice showed a tendency to be close to NC mice after 6-HH administration (Fig.  C). Then, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was performed on entire genes from NC and HFD/STZ groups on the basis of Gene Oncology Molecular Function (GOMF) gene sets. As shown in Fig.  D, E, the genes related to Toll-like receptor (TLR) binding and NADP+ nucleosidase activity were significantly upregulated in HFD/STZ-induced diabetic mice. Besides, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) based on gthe Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database revealed a significant upregulation of TLR signaling pathway in HFD/STZ group, whereas 6-HH treatment induced the distribution of core genes in TLR signaling pathway approach to that of NC group (Fig.  F). Likewise, GSEA based on GOMF database showed that NADP+ nucleosidase activity was elevated in HFD/STZ group, while the expression of core genes associated with NADP+ nucleosidase activity in 6-HH-treated HFD/STZ-induced diabetic mice was close to that of NC group (Fig.  G). Correlation network analysis exhibited a strong positive relationship between the key genes from these two gene sets (Fig.  H). Thus, 6-HH might accelerate diabetic wound healing through downregulating TLR signals and NADP+ nucleosidase activity.
 Furthermore, analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) showed that 522 genes were upregulated and 457 genes were downregulated from HFD/STZ group compared with NC group, while HFD/STZ-6-HH induced 163 upregulated genes and 69 downregulated genes compared with HFD/STZ group (Fig.  A). Among them, 76 DEGs were found to overlap between the HFD/STZ versus NC and HFD/STZ-6-HH versus HFD/STZ comparisons (Fig.  B). On the basis of these 1135 DEGs, PCA (Fig.  C) and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) plots (Fig.  D) revealed that samples from NC group, HFD/STZ group, and HFD/STZ-6-HH group were separated from each other. Subsequently, cell type profiling based on immune cell (LM22) reference datasets was performed on 1135 DEGs, inferring strong association with monocytes, neutrophils, M1 macrophages, M0 macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells, M2 macrophages, and mast cells (Supplementary Fig. S4C). Notably, the relative abundance of monocytes, M1 macrophages, and M0 macrophages was higher in granulation tissues from three groups, while that of M2 macrophages was higher except in HFD/STZ group (Fig.  E). Next, 1135 DEGs were divided into four clusters using K-means cluster analysis (Supplementary Fig. S4D), among which the expression of DEGs in cluster 1 was upregulated in HFD/STZ group but downregulated post 6-HH administration (Fig.  F). Further cell-type enrichment analysis on DEGs in cluster 1 revealed that these DEGs were significantly enriched in macrophages under inflammatory conditions, particularly in peritoneal macrophages treated with LPS and thioglycolate and bone-marrow-derived macrophages treated with LPS (Fig.  G) that usually serve as M1 phenotype. Besides, pathway enrichment analysis based on the MSigDB Hallmark gene set collection demonstrated significant enrichment of inflammatory-related pathways, especially tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) signaling via NF-κB (Fig.  H). The expression of DEGs associated with M1 macrophages and NF-κB-mediated inflammatory response mentioned above was significantly elevated in granulation tissues from HFD/STZ group while decreased after 6-HH treatment (Fig.  I), and protein–protein interaction (PPI) enrichment analysis of these DEGs revealed significant associations with NFKB1 as the top-ranked transcription factor (Fig.  J). Moreover, the key target genes were further validated by RT-qPCR, and the data showed that 6-HH significantly decreased inflammatory gene expression, including Tnf-α, Il-6, Mcp-1, inos, and Il-1β (Supplementary Fig. S4E). Taken together, 6-HH might improve diabetic wound healing through inhibiting M1 macrophage-driven inflammatory response with blocking of NF-κB signaling pathway.
 

## 6-HH activates SIRT6 to ameliorate inflammatory responses in macrophages
Inspired by the results of transcriptome analysis, the effects of 6-HH on inflammatory responses in macrophages were investigated (Supplementary Fig. S5A, B). 6-HH downregulated the levels of IL-6 and TNF-α and mRNA expression levels of Il-6, Il-8, Cox-2, and Tnf-α in LPS-treated THP-1 cells (Supplementary Fig. S6A, B). Besides, 6-HH decreased the expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and the phosphorylation of inhibitor of NF-κB alpha (IκBα), IκB kinase alpha/beta (IKKα/β), p65 in LPS-induced THP-1 cells and BMDMs (Supplementary Fig. S6C, D). Remarkably, 6-HH significantly counteracted LPS induced downregulation of SIRT6 in both THP-1 cells and BMDMs (Fig.  A). Then, the SIRT6-specific inhibitor Oss_128167 was used to confirm the action of 6-HH. As expected, Oss_128167 partially offset the anti-inflammatory effects of 6-HH in macrophages, as revealed by the decrease of SIRT6 and IκBα, and the increase of iNOS and p-p65 (Fig.  B). Consistently, the downregulated mRNA expression levels of Cd86, Cox-2, Il-1β, Il-6, inos, and Tnf-α caused by 6-HH were obviously attenuated by Oss_128167 in LPS-treated BMDMs (Fig.  C). Besides, 6-HH obviously decreased the nuclear translocation of p65 in LPS-treated macrophages, while Oss_128167 diminished this change (Fig.  D, E). Taken together, these phenomena suggested that 6-HH mitigates inflammatory response by activating SIRT6 in macrophages.
 CETSA revealed that 6-HH improved the stabilization of SIRT6 protein (Fig.  F). Furthermore, molecular docking was employed to assess the binding mode and energies of 6-HH to SIRT6 (PDB code 3K35). As exhibited in Supplementary Fig. S7A–C, 6-HH could effectively bind to the active site of the target protein, with the highest Cdocker interaction energy of −55.6691 kcal/mol. Further comparison of energy between MDL 800 (the specific activator of SIRT6) and 6-HH at this site showed that MDL 800 had higher binding energy (−45.9734 kcal/mol), suggesting a better combining capacity of 6-HH with SIRT6. These findings indicated that 6-HH may fit into the hydrophobic pocket and form tight bonds with the catalytic amino acid residues (D61, F62, A51, N238, S214, R63, and Q216) through various interactions. Besides, 6-HH reduced the acetylation level of lysine of macrophage both in the absence and in the presence of LPS (Fig.  G, H). Immunoprecipitated endogenous SIRT6 and immunoblot analysis demonstrated a specific interaction with p65, while 6-HH counteracted the endogenous SIRT6 binding to p65 in LPS-treated macrophage (Fig.  I). Likewise, the confocal microscopic analysis revealed that 6-HH decreased LPS-induced co-localization of SIRT6 and p65 in macrophage (Fig.  J). And 6-HH decreased the hyperacetylation of p65 (Fig.  I), p65Lys310, and H3K9 (Fig.  K) in LPS-treated macrophages, while Oss_128167 obviously mitigated these effects (Fig.  J, K). Thus, deacetylase SIRT6 activated by 6-HH might inhibit the polarization of macrophages towards M1 phenotype through suppressing hyperacetylation of p65.

## 6-HH promotes SIRT6 to accelerate wound healing in type 2 diabetic mice
To further investigate the effect of SIRT6 in the 6-HH-mediated diabetic wound healing, HFD/STZ-induced type 2 diabetic mice were co-treated with Oss_128167 (Fig.  A). As expected, 6-HH accelerated the wound healing rate and increased blood flow of wound in diabetic mice; the rate of wound closure in 6-HH-treated HFD/STZ mice was 86% after 8 days post-injury (cf. 43% in untreated HFD/STZ mice) (Fig.  B), while Oss_128167 significantly reversed the therapeutic effect of 6-HH (Fig.  B, C). Besides, 6-HH shortened the width of wound, elevated the thickness of skin and granulation, and improved collagen deposition in wound areas (Fig.  D, E). In addition, 6-HH significantly decreased the number of CD11c+ cells in the wound sites of diabetic mice (Supplementary Fig. S8), reduced the number of CD86+ macrophages, downregulated the expression levels of HIF-1α (Fig.  F), and increased the number of CD206+ macrophages and CD31+ cells, accompanied by elevation of K10 expression (Fig.  G, H). Meanwhile, Oss_128167 treatment diminished the effect of 6-HH at the wound sites, resulting in a trend close to that of HFD/STZ group (Fig.  C–H).
 Additionally, the expression of SIRT6 and p-p65 was significantly elevated at the trauma sites of mice in HFD/STZ-6-HH group at day 1 and day 3 after surgery, followed by a gradual decrease at postoperative days 5 and 7, whereas Oss_128167 consistently suppressed SIRT6 expression throughout the wound healing process and upregulated p-p65 at postoperative days 5 and 7. Besides, 6-HH obviously decreased the acetylation of p65Lys310 at diabetic wounds on days 5 and 7 after surgery, while Oss_128167 partially reversed the changes induced by 6-HH. Intriguingly, 6-HH also decreased lysine acetylation of diabetic wounds at postoperative days 1, 5, and 7, while the inhibitory effect of Oss_128167 on 6-HH was most apparent on postoperative day 5 and 7 (Fig.  A, B). Subsequently, the relationships among SIRT6, p-p65, and Ace-p65Lys310 and the core genes in TLR signaling pathway and NADP+ nucleosidase activity were evaluated. As shown in Fig.  C, there are 40 key genes belonging to TLR signaling pathway and 12 key genes associated with NADP+ nucleosidase activity, among which 5 genes are shared by the two gene sets. The results of protein–gene correlations showed that the correlations between SIRT6, p-p65, and Ace-p65lys310 and genes relevant to TLR signaling pathway and NADP+ nucleosidase activity in HFD/STZ-6-HH group revealed a tendency to be close to that of NC group (Fig.  D), suggesting that 6-HH might alter the protein–gene correlation profiles in HFD/STZ-induced diabetic mice and thus accelerate diabetic wound healing. Altogether, 6-HH might inhibit NF-κB-mediated inflammatory responses and suppress the hyperacetylation status of diabetic wounds via activating SIRT6, which thereby promoted angiogenesis and re-epithelialization and ultimately facilitated diabetic wound healing.
 

## SIRT6 activation by 6-HH accelerates wound healing in db/db diabetic mice
Furthermore, db/db diabetic mice were utilized to validate the action of 6-HH in diabetic wound healing (Fig.  A). The db/db mice exhibited slower healing rate than HFD/STZ-induced mice, so the endpoint was longer (12 days) in db/db mice. As shown in Fig.  B, 6-HH treatment significantly improved wound healing of db/db diabetic mice: the rate of wound closure in 6-HH-treated db/db mice was 85% after 12 days post-injury (cf. 61% in untreated db/db mice) (Fig.  B). Also, 6-HH treatment reduced the width of wound and elevated the collagen deposition (Fig.  C, D). Besides, 6-HH treatment downregulated the amount of CD86+ macrophages at wound site, with elevated numbers of CD206+ macrophages and CD31+ cells and increased expression levels of K10 and α-SMA (Fig.  E–G and Supplementary Fig. S9). Furthermore, 6-HH decreased p-p65, iNOS, and Ace-lysine expression and upregulated SIRT6 expression in granulation tissue from db/db diabetic mice (Fig.  H). Notably, Oss_128167 reversed these therapeutic effects of 6-HH in db/db diabetic mice (Fig.  B–H and Supplementary Fig. S9). Altogether, 6-HH inhibited wound hyperacetylation through activation of SIRT6, which suppressed inflammation and promoted angiogenesis and re-epithelialization, thereby accelerating wound healing in db/db diabetic mice.
 

## 6-HH improves angiogenesis and re-epithelization via activating SIRT6 in macrophages
Co-culture systems were established to validate whether 6-HH improves angiogenesis and re-epithelization by targeting macrophages. As shown in Fig.  A, culture medium (CM) of THP-1 cells (CM-THP-1) exposed to different conditions was collected for HUVEC and HaCaT cell culture. 6-HH-intervened CM-THP-1 (CM-LPS + 6-HH) significantly improved tube formation of HUVECs compared with LPS-treated CM-THP-1 (CM-LPS) (Fig.  B). Besides, CM from 6-HH-treated BMDMs obviously promoted the migration of keratinocytes in mouse skin explant (Fig.  C). Additionally, CM-LPS + 6-HH enhanced the wound healing rate and migration of HUVEC and HaCaT cells (Fig.  D, E) and suppressed the nuclear translocation of Forkhead box O1 (FOXO-1) in HUVEC and HaCaT cells (Fig.  F). In addition, CM-LPS + 6-HH remarkably upregulated the expression of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in HUVECs (Fig.  G) and elevated the expression of tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-1 (TIMP-1) and downregulated the expression of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) and c-Myc in HaCaT cells (Fig.  H), while these effects of 6-HH were partially abrogated by Oss_128167 (Fig.  C–F). Collectively, these data indicated that 6-HH might promote angiogenesis and re-epithelization by activating SIRT6 in macrophages.
 

## Discussion
Macrophages, the major contributor to the persistent and excessive inflammatory response at wound sites in DF, have been considered as a therapeutic target for improved diabetic wound healing. Macrophages in diabetic wounds are dominated by the M1 phenotype that release excessive pro-inflammatory cytokines, resulting in a decrease of growth factors, increase of oxidative damage, and impaired local neovascularization [ , ]. Consistently, we found that 6-HH significantly decreased the TNF-α and IL-6 levels in LPS-treated macrophages, with inhibition of the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators. Macrophage phenotypic shift from M1 to M2 is a typical feature of the transition from inflammatory to proliferative phase during wound healing, as M2 macrophages secret platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), VEGF, and various anti-inflammatory cytokines and recruit stem cells for tissue repair [ ]. In vivo, 6-HH treatment could reduce the number of CD86+ and CD11c+ cells in wound site of diabetic mice, while increasing the number of CD206+ macrophages. Besides, 6-HH enhanced the M2 macrophage polarization through activating the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ)/phosphorylated signal transducer and activator of transcription 6 (p-STAT6) signaling pathway in THP-1 cells (Supplementary Fig. S10). Taken together, these results indicated that 6-HH suppressed the abnormal M1 macrophage polarization with promotion of M2 transformation to mitigate inflammatory response in diabetic wound.
NADP+ nucleosidase activity contributes to the degradation of NADP+ that influences the balance of NAD+/NADP+, so its elevation reduces the production of NAD⁺ that decreases the activity of sirtuins, leading to aging and metabolic dysfunction [ ]. SIRT6 is an NAD+-dependent deacetylase that takes charge of DNA repair, gene expression, and telomeric maintenance, and plays an essential role in the progression of multiple life-threatening human disorders such as DF [ ]. The current study found that genes related to NADP+ nucleosidase activity were significantly upregulated in diabetic wounds while decreased in 6-HH treated mice, suggesting that 6-HH might influence the activity of sirtuins such as SIRT6 to support wound healing in diabetic mice. Previous research has reported that the gene expression of SIRT6 in peripheral blood mononuclear cells is significantly decreased in subjects with DF compared with those with diabetes or healthy controls, accompanied by increased expression of IL-6 and TNF-α [ ]. SIRT6 knockout in myeloid cells could cause delayed wound closure in mice, and SIRT6 small interfering RNA (siRNA) aggravates the proinflammatory response and decreases angiogenesis at the wound site of diabetic mice [ , ]. Our study firstly found that SIRT6 was downregulated at wound site of diabetic mice during inflammatory phase, while upregulated in subsequent proliferative phase, suggesting that the diabetic environment might delay the expression of SIRT6 at the wound site, thereby slowing wound closure. Furthermore, inhibition of SIRT6 in macrophages decreased the capacity of efferocytosis and resulted in an imbalance between M1 and M2 polarization [ ]. Besides, SIRT6 was found to be reduced in M1 polarization and increased in M2 polarization of macrophages [ ], and myeloid-specific SIRT6 knockout gives rise to serious inflammatory response in mice [ ]. Encouragingly, 6-HH treatment significantly reversed the decline of SIRT6 in M1 macrophages with reduced hyperacetylated lysine levels, probably owing to its ability for stabilization of SIRT6. Lysine acetylation on both histone and non-histone proteins induced by acetyltransferases and deacetylases affects various cellular and physiological processes. Remarkably, altered lysine acetylation governs macrophage activity, polarization, and inflammatory responses. Elevated acetylation level of lysine could be observed in LPS-treated peritoneal macrophages that amplified the activation of NOD-, LRR- and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome [ ]. Herein, the lysine acetylation level in LPS-treated macrophages was significantly decreased by 6-HH treatment. Interestingly, hyperacetylation in the hippocampus or aorta of diabetic animals was proved to be associated with severe cognitive impairment and vascular dysfunction, respectively [ , ]. Likewise, increased acetylation could be observed in the heart and kidneys of diabetic animals, contributing to exacerbate the progression of complications of diabetes [ , ]. Notably, we found for the first time that the level of acetylated lysine was increased at the diabetic wound site during inflammatory phase, indicating that hyperacetylated protein levels at the wound site might be correlated with poor wound healing, while 6-HH might promote diabetic wound healing by stabilizing the SIRT6 protein to mitigate the increase of acetylated lysine.
NF-κB p65 is a target of SIRT6 that interacts with the DNA binding domain of p65 and occupies the promoter of NF-κB target genes to repress the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby inhibiting the polarization of M1 macrophages [ ]. SIRT6 overexpression promotes p65 distributed in the cytoplasm that contributes to the retardation of NF-κB signal and relief of inflammation, while SIRT6 knockdown brought about inverse outcomes [ – ]. Herein, 6-HH inhibited the phosphorylation of p65 and decreased the co-location of SIRT6 with p65 in nucleus, which blocked the downstream cascade signals, and therefore decreased polarization of M1 macrophages. However, it is unknown whether nuclear co-localization of p65 with SIRT6 affects the function of SIRT6, which may be the focus of future studies. Moreover, the effect of SIRT6 in regulating macrophages polarization is tightly ascribed to its deacetylation modification capacity on histone and p65/RelA [ , ]. Acetylated Lys310 constitutes a platform for binding to bromodomain-containing proteins that is required for full transcriptional activity of p65 [ ]. SIRT6 was found to deacetylate the Lys310 site of the p65 subunit and repress the transcriptional activity of p65, which subsequently decreased the release of IL-1β [ ]. On the other hand, H3K9 is reported to be a site for NF-κB p65 binding, and diabetic condition contributed to increased binding of acetylated H3K9 and p65 [ ], while SIRT6 recruited to the chromatin at the promoters of p65 inhibits the expression of NF-κB target gene via deacetylating histone H3K9, which subsequently reduces occupancy of the p65 subunit of NF-κB promoter [ ]. As expected, 6-HH reduced the acetylation of p65Lys310 and H3K9 in M1 macrophages to overcome inflammatory response, and the protein–gene correlations further proved that 6-HH altered the correlation profiles between proteins including p65, SIRT6, and Ace-p65Lys310 and genes involved in inflammation and NADP+ nucleosidase activity in diabetic mice, demonstrating that 6-HH might influence the interaction of NF-κB p65 and SIRT6 to facilitate diabetic wound healing. Notably, SIRT6-specific inhibitor Oss_128167 abrogated the therapeutic effects of 6-HH in vitro and in vivo. Altogether, this evidence strongly suggest that SIRT6 facilitates wound healing in diabetes, partially through deacetylating p65 and H3K9 in macrophages, and 6-HH might function as a SIRT6 activator to improve diabetic wound healing.
In the context of diabetic wounds, chronic inflammation driven by M1 macrophage phenotype disrupts the coordinated communication required for efficient tissue repair, particularly angiogenesis and re-epithelialization, and these processes are relevant to cross-talks between macrophages and endothelial cells/keratinocytes [ – ]. Large number of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages recruited to the granulation tissue caused by diabetes could produce excessive pro-inflammatory cytokines and suppress alternatively activated macrophages responsible for VEGF secretion that hinders functions of endothelial cells and keratinocytes, contributing to poor angiogenesis and defective epidermal barrier formation [ , ]. Nuclear translocation of FOXO-1 driven by diabetes irritates a thin and over-pruned vascular network with less endothelial cells, which ultimately causes impaired neovascularization [ ]. In addition, activation of FOXO-1 in diabetes also induces excessive MMP expression and reduced levels of TIMP in keratinocytes, limiting the ability of keratinocytes to migrate over the wound bed [ ]. In our study, SIRT6 activated by 6-HH in M1 macrophages improved the functions of endothelial cells and keratinocytes, while Oss_128167 reversed the effects of 6-HH, which indicated that 6-HH modulated the cross-talk between macrophages and endothelial cells/keratinocytes to improve diabetic wound healing. In vivo, 6-HH enhanced the numbers of CD31+ cells in wound site of diabetic mice and increased the expression of K10, an early marker of differentiating keratinocytes [ ], in wound edge, while this effect was attenuated by SIRT6 inhibition. Taken together, these findings strongly demonstrated that 6-HH improves angiogenesis and re-epithelization in diabetic wound through activating SIRT6 in macrophages.
Indeed, 6-HH is an anticholinergic drug with smooth-muscle-relaxing and microcirculation-enhancing properties in clinic since 1965, being included in China’s National Essential Medicine list in 2012. For the treatment of smooth muscle spasms, vertigo, microcirculation disorders, and organophosphorus poisoning, the adult dosage of 6-HH is 5–10 mg per administration, taken three times daily. Meanwhile, in the management of septic shock, the dosage of 6-HH can be increased to 40 mg per dose, repeated every 10–30 min, as the patient’s condition improves, the interval between doses may be extended until the medication is discontinued. Besides, as a muscarinic antagonist, 6-HH exerts similar pharmacological effects to atropine with less toxicity [ , ]. Therefore, considering the long history of 6-HH’s clinical application and its low toxicity together with our current findings, there is great potential for 6-HH to be used in the treatment of DF in the clinic in the future.
In spite of some novel insights gained from the current study, several limitations remain. First, there was a lack of verification at the genetic level. Subsequent research could construct macrophage cell models with SIRT6 knockdown or overexpression in vitro, and build mouse models with SIRT6 gene knockout in macrophages in vivo to further verify whether 6-HH acts as a specific SIRT6 activator. Second, further research is required to investigate whether 6-HH mitigates macrophage inflammation by regulating the acetylation level at other sites of p65, and explore whether 6-HH directly affects deacetylase activity and NAD+ binding. Third, the treatment of diabetic wounds mostly adopts local administration to achieve the purpose of directly regulating the microenvironment of the wound. However, this study only used the oral administration method, and further research is required to change the administration method to further explore the action of 6-HH. Fourth, off-target toxicities of 6-HH have not been reported in nondiabetic mice and will be investigated in our future work. In addition, compared with diabetic mice, 6-HH did not show an effect in lowering blood glucose (Supplementary Figs. S1D and S2C), so the correlation between blood glucose levels and wound healing rates was not analyzed in the current study.

## Conclusions
Our data firstly reveal that the acetylation status takes charge of the process of diabetic wound healing, and SIRT6 might be a direct pharmacological target of 6-HH to accelerate diabetic wound healing. The promotion and stabilization of SIRT6 by 6-HH reduce acetylated lysine levels of macrophages that inhibits the polarization of M1 macrophages via deacetylating p65Lys310 and H3K9, thus improving the dysfunction of endothelial cells and keratinocytes to promote diabetic wound healing (Fig.  ). These findings reveal that targeting the SIRT6-associated deacetylation function might be an effective strategy for the treatment of DF, and 6-HH might be a potential candidate for diabetic wound healing therapy.
