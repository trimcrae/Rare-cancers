## ABSTRACT

## Background
Lactate, traditionally viewed as a glycolysis byproduct, has emerged as an important mediator influencing immunity, inflammation, and tissue damage. While PM2.5 exposure is known to cause various metabolic disturbances, the role of lactate metabolism in PM2.5‐induced lung injury remains unclear. This study aims to elucidate the mechanisms underlying PM2.5‐induced lung injury from a metabolic perspective.

## Methods
Lactate and pyruvate assays were performed to assess metabolic changes following PM2.5 exposure. Protein expression and tissue damage were assessed using Western blot, IHC, ELISA, and TUNEL staining. The biological role of USP38 in PM2.5‐induced injury was identified using gain‐ and loss‐of‐function experiments. Co‐immunoprecipitation and ubiquitination assays were conducted to analyze the interaction between USP38 and MCT1, as well as the regulation of MCT1 deubiquitination. The role of MCT1 in lactate metabolism and PM2.5‐induced apoptosis was validated through gene editing. Proteomics revealed the potential mechanisms involved in USP38 regulation of apoptosis.

## Results
Our results demonstrated that PM2.5 exposure induced lactate accumulation, leading to cell apoptosis and lung injury. USP38 stabilized MCT1 expression by deubiquitination, facilitating lactate export and reducing apoptosis and lung injury caused by lactate accumulation. Mechanistically, PM2.5 increased lactate production, suppressed AKT/mTOR pathway activation, and promoted apoptosis and lung injury. USP38 promoted lactate export through MCT1, activated the AKT/mTOR pathway, and mitigated PM2.5‐induced lung injury.

## Conclusion
USP38 reduces lactate accumulation by promoting AKT/mTOR pathway activation through MCT1‐mediated lactate export, thereby alleviating PM2.5‐induced lung injury. These findings reveal a novel mechanism of PM2.5‐related lung injury and highlight potential therapeutic targets.

PM2.5 modulates the metabolic pathways of pulmonary epithelial cells, reprogramming their metabolic profile to favor glycolysis and consequently leading to an increase in lactate production. Excess lactate induces apoptosis by inhibiting the AKT/mTOR pathway, offering a novel metabolic perspective on PM2.5‐induced lung injury. The deubiquitinase USP38 fortifies MCT1 expression by deubiquitination, thereby enhancing lactate efflux and significantly alleviating lung damage induced by PM2.5 exposure.

## USP38 Reduced PM2.5‐Induced Lactate Accumulation and Cell Apoptosis In Vitro
The role of USP38 in apoptosis and metabolism is becoming clearer, but its involvement in lactate metabolism under PM2.5 exposure remains unexplored. We found that USP38 protein expression increased in a dose‐dependent manner with PM2.5 exposure. Similarly, the addition of exogenous lactate also dose‐dependently upregulated USP38 (Figure  ). Overexpression of USP38 reduces PM2.5‐induced cell damage (Figure  ), while knockdown of USP38 enhances PM2.5‐induced cell damage (Figure  ). Knockdown of USP38 enhanced PM2.5‐induced cell apoptosis (Figure  and Figure  ) and led to increased lactate accumulation (Figure  ). Conversely, overexpression of USP38 inhibited PM2.5‐induced cell apoptosis (Figure  and Figure  ) and reduced PM2.5‐induced lactate production (Figure  ). Additionally, excess lactate supplementation diminished the protective effect of USP38 (Figure  ).
 
3.3

## USP38 Alleviated Lung Injury by Reducing PM2.5‐Induced Lactate Accumulation In Vivo
Additionally, a significant increase in USP38 expression was observed in mice exposed to PM2.5 (Figure  ). To investigate the role of USP38 in PM2.5‐induced lung injury, we utilized USP38 knockout mice. USP38 knockout mice exposed to PM2.5 exhibited significantly greater lung damage compared to their wild‐type counterparts (Figure  ). Moreover, USP38 knockout mice exhibited the highest levels of lactate and inflammatory cytokines in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid following PM2.5 exposure (Figure  ). The administration of lactate to mice exacerbated pulmonary injury and promoted the release of inflammatory cytokines in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid following exposure to PM2.5 (Figure  ). TUNEL staining provided further evidence that USP38 knockout exacerbated apoptosis in the lung tissue of mice (Figure  ).
 
3.4

## USP38 Deubiquitinated the Lactate Transporter Protein MCT1
MCT1 (also known as SLC16A1) is part of the SLC16A gene family and is responsible for the transport of monocarboxylates such as lactate and pyruvate across cell membranes. Proteomic analysis further revealed a significant decrease in MCT1 expression following USP38 knockdown (Table  ). Given the critical role of MCT1 in lactate metabolism under stress conditions, we investigated the relationship between USP38 and MCT1 through protein–protein docking. The docking model indicated that MCT1 and USP38 form stable interactions via hydrogen bonds at amino acid residues such as CYS159‐PRO353 and ARG326‐TYR774 (Figure  and Table  ). To further validate this interaction, we performed a Co‐IP assay, which confirmed the binding between USP38 and MCT1 (Figure  ). Both knockdown and overexpression of USP38 influenced MCT1 protein expression levels (Figure  and Figure  ). In cells overexpressing USP38 and treated with Cycloheximide (CHX), the degradation rate of MCT1 was significantly slower compared to cells transfected with an empty vector (Figure  ). In contrast, the knockdown of USP38 enhanced the degradation rate of MCT1 (Figure  ). Additionally, the knockdown of USP38 increased the ubiquitination level of MCT1 (Figure  ), while overexpression of USP38 reduced the ubiquitination level of MCT1 (Figure  ). In vivo experiments further demonstrate that exposure to PM2.5 increased USP38 expression and stabilized MCT1 protein level expression, exhibiting a synchronized trend (Figure  ). These findings indicate that USP38 stabilizes the MCT1 protein by preventing ubiquitination‐dependent degradation through the deubiquitination process, effectively removing ubiquitin tags.
 
3.5

## MCT1 Alleviated PM2.5‐Induced Cell Apoptosis by Reducing Lactate Accumulation
Given that MCT1 is a substrate of USP38, we further investigated the role of MCT1 in regulating PM2.5‐induced lactate accumulation and subsequent cell apoptosis. Cell viability assays revealed that overexpression of MCT1 inhibited PM2.5‐induced cytotoxicity (Figure  ), while knockdown of MCT1 intensified PM2.5‐induced toxicity (Figure  ). Additionally, the overexpression of MCT1 suppressed the expression of apoptosis‐related markers, including Bax and Cleaved‐CASP3 (Figure  ). Moreover, the extra supplementation of lactate partially inhibited the protective role of MCT1 (Figure  ). In contrast, MCT1 knockdown increased the expression of these pro‐apoptotic markers (Figure  ). The overexpression of MCT1 reduced intracellular lactate accumulation following PM2.5 exposure, whereas MCT1 knockdown exacerbated lactate accumulation induced by PM2.5 (Figure  ). These results suggest that MCT1 alleviates PM2.5‐induced cell apoptosis by facilitating the export of excess lactate, thereby maintaining intracellular metabolic balance and reducing cytotoxicity.
 
3.6

## USP38 Stabilized MCT1 to Inhibit Lactate Accumulation and Alleviated PM2.5‐Induced Cell Apoptosis
We have demonstrated that both USP38 and MCT1 reduce PM2.5‐induced lactate accumulation. Furthermore, MCT1 has been identified as a deubiquitination substrate of USP38. To determine whether the protective effect of USP38 is dependent on MCT1 or functions independently, we conducted further investigation. MCT1 knockdown impaired the ability of USP38 to reduce PM2.5‐induced lactate accumulation (Figure  ) and abolished the protective effect of USP38 on cell viability (Figure  ). Western blot analysis confirmed that MCT1 knockdown completely eliminated the ability of USP38 to inhibit PM2.5‐induced apoptosis (Figure  ). TUNEL staining provides additional evidence that the suppression of MCT1 markedly diminishes the protective role of USP38 in counteracting PM2.5‐induced apoptotic cell death (Figure  ). These findings indicate that USP38 exerts its protective effect against PM2.5‐induced cell damage through an MCT1‐dependent mechanism. By stabilizing MCT1, USP38 facilitates lactate export, thereby preventing intracellular lactate accumulation and reducing apoptosis.
 
3.7

## USP38 Activated the AKT/mTOR Pathway to Inhibit PM2.5‐Induced Apoptosis
To investigate the molecular mechanism by which USP38 regulates MCT1 in PM2.5‐induced apoptosis, we performed proteomic sequencing on cells with USP38 knockdown to identify potential protein changes (Figure  ). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis of the differential proteins revealed significant enrichment in pathways such as the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, Glycolysis, HIF‐1 signaling pathway, and Ubiquitin and Proteasome pathways (Figure  ). As shown in the heatmap in Figure  and Figure  , related proteins in these pathways, including CASP3 and SLC16A1 (MCT1), underwent significant changes. Previous studies have shown that lactate can inhibit the AKT/mTOR pathway and promote cell apoptosis [ ]. Our earlier research also demonstrated that PM2.5 suppresses AKT/mTOR activation, leading to apoptosis in lung epithelial cells [ ]. Interestingly, we observed that USP38 activated the AKT/mTOR pathway and counteracted the inhibitory effects of PM2.5 (Figure  ). After administering the AKT inhibitor MK2206, the USP38‐mediated suppression of PM2.5‐induced cellular apoptosis and the subsequent activation of the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway were reduced (Figure  ). Subsequent research indicated that the downregulation of MCT1 inhibited the ability of USP38 to activate the AKT/mTOR pathway (Figure  ). In conclusion, these findings demonstrated that USP38 mitigates PM2.5‐induced lung epithelial cell damage by activating the AKT/mTOR pathway via MCT1, reducing lactate accumulation and apoptosis.

## Discussion
This study demonstrated that USP38 interacts with the monocarboxylate transporter MCT1 (SLC16A1), deubiquitinates MCT1 to enhance its expression, and thereby promotes lactate efflux under PM2.5 exposure. PM2.5 stimulation induces glycolysis, generating lactate as the primary metabolite, which triggers cell apoptosis and mediates lung damage. USP38 plays a protective role by reducing lactate accumulation via MCT1.
Exposure to PM2.5 is intricately linked to a wide range of health issues, particularly metabolic disturbances. Research has demonstrated that prolonged exposure to PM2.5 can induce metabolic disorders, which may subsequently contribute to the development of various diseases. Exposure to PM2.5 can result in the dysregulation of the intestinal microbiota, thereby impacting glucose homeostasis [ ]. Exposure to PM2.5 is associated with the development of glucose and lipid metabolic disorders in the liver, which may contribute to the pathogenesis of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and consequently elevate the risk of liver‐related complications [ ]. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms by which PM2.5 affects metabolism is crucial for developing effective public health strategies.
Lactate accumulation can directly cause tissue damage by depleting mitochondrial ATP, increasing mitochondrial ROS, and promoting mitochondrial apoptosis. This process is characterized by increased ROS, downregulation of BCL‐2, and upregulation of BAK, ultimately leading to apoptosis [ ]. Furthermore, investigations have found elevated levels of lactate in the cerebrospinal fluid of Parkinson's disease patients, contributing to the apoptosis of dopaminergic neurons. Higher levels of lactate boost the activity of AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) and suppress the phosphorylation of serine/threonine kinase 1 (AKT) and the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR). This disruption in signaling ultimately leads to dopaminergic neuronal apoptosis [ ]. Normally, low ROS levels stimulate several transcription factors to increase MCT1 expression, promoting lactate transport into the mitochondria for oxidative metabolism [ ]. Nonetheless, in pathological states, the levels of lactate inside cells rise considerably, leading to excessive ROS production. Asiatic acid boosts MCT1 expression while decreasing the levels of cytc, CASP9, and CASP3 in cardiomyocytes stimulated by lactate [ ]. According to Li, Qinglin, et al. lactate in the microenvironment of acute liver injury triggered by acetaminophen promotes macrophage pyroptosis, which intensifies liver damage [ ]. Lactate induces oxidative stress and cell apoptosis through BNIP3‐mediated mitochondrial autophagy defects, accelerating the osteoblastic phenotype transition and calcium deposition in smooth muscle cells [ ]. L‐lactate treatment in mice resulted in impaired learning and memory, diminished hippocampal ATP and NADH, increased oxidative stress, mitochondrial issues, and heightened neuronal apoptosis [ ]. The excessive accumulation of lactate can cause significant damage to cells and tissues.
MCT1, a key protein involved in lactate transmembrane transport, directly removes excess lactate from cells, thereby maintaining intracellular metabolic balance. MCT1 plays a crucial role in various diseases by regulating lactate levels. MCT1 transports lactate based on what the cell functionally requires. During conditions of excess lactate production due to external stimuli, MCT1 facilitates lactate efflux to maintain metabolic balance. Conversely, in cells with an increased lactate demand, such as tumor cells, the use of MCT1 enables lactate uptake to meet energy needs. In hepatocellular carcinoma cells, MCT1 facilitates lactate absorption, boosting ATP generation and turning off AMPK, which helps resist ferroptosis [ ]. In a similar fashion, MCT1 is significantly increased in many tumor tissues, where it is essential for exporting lactate salts to the tumor microenvironment [ ]. Conversely, during adipocyte differentiation, the loss of MCT1 function leads to intracellular lactate accumulation, promoting adipocyte apoptosis [ ]. In this study, PM2.5 exposure causes lung epithelial cells to produce excessive lactate. MCT1 facilitates lactate export, preventing its accumulation and thereby protecting against epithelial cell apoptosis. Given the close association between MCT1 and energy metabolism, its expression can be upregulated via inducible mechanisms. For instance, acute exercise activates AMPK in fast‐twitch muscles, which increases MCT1 mRNA expression [ ]. However, the post‐transcriptional regulation of MCT1 is primarily mediated by miRNA, and its post‐translational modifications (PTMs) remain incompletely understood [ ]. Recent studies highlight the crucial role of PTM in regulating protein stability, with deubiquitination shown to influence the stability of various proteins. In this study, we found that under PM2.5 exposure, MCT1 undergoes deubiquitination, which regulates lactate metabolic flux.
USP38, a member of the ubiquitin‐specific protease (USP) family, is increasingly recognized for its role in regulating various biological processes. For instance, USP38 stabilizes HIF1α through deubiquitination under hypoxic conditions, thereby reducing cellular ROS and inhibiting apoptosis [ ]. In this study, we demonstrated that PM2.5 enhances glycolysis in epithelial cells, and USP38 provides a protective effect by mitigating lactate accumulation under these stress conditions. Our study suggests that the upregulation of USP38 in response to PM2.5 stimulation may represent a cellular feedback mechanism for self‐protection. This conclusion is supported by experiments involving both USP38 knockdown cells and Usp38 knockout mice, which consistently demonstrate the protective role of USP38 against PM2.5‐induced damage. Activating USP38 can reduce LPS‐induced apoptosis in alveolar type II epithelial cells [ ]. Studies on Usp38−/− mice exhibit increased susceptibility to inflammation‐induced cell death, whereas the presence of USP38 mitigates lung inflammation caused by inflammatory factors and bleomycin‐induced pulmonary fibrosis [ ]. These findings suggest that USP38 plays a protective role in both inflammation‐induced cell death and lung injury. This study demonstrates that USP38 protects against PM2.5‐induced lung damage by reducing lactate accumulation. We have preliminarily demonstrated that USP38 regulates lactate levels through the modulation of MCT1 expression. However, direct evidence supporting an interaction between USP38 and MCT1 remains to be established. Future studies will focus on investigating this potential interaction in greater depth.
In summary, our findings demonstrate that USP38 modulates MCT1 expression through deubiquitination, promoting the export of lactate accumulated in lung epithelial cells due to PM2.5 exposure. This process mitigates lactate accumulation, suppresses cell apoptosis, and prevents lung injury. Our research provides novel metabolic targets and insights for therapeutic strategies against PM2.5‐associated lung injury.
