Chemical composition analysis using inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry identified iron (Fe) as the predominant heavy metal (101,067.31 ± 8246.12 mg/kg). Proteomic analysis revealed three inflammation-associated pathways, including leukocyte transendothelial migration, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, and phagosome activity. The increase in pulmonary inflammation may contribute to the pathogenesis of various chronic diseases and promotion of lung cancer. These findings provide valuable insights for academic researchers, electricity enterprises, and policymakers in developing effective strategies to manage air pollution worldwide.

## Introduction
Particulate matter (PM) is recognized as a major environmental pollutant contributing to air pollution worldwide , . PM originates from both natural sources and human activities. These include volcanic eruptions, wildfires, biomass burning, power plant emissions, industry combustions, and vehicle exhaust, with varying prominence across different geographical areas , . Coal-fired power plants have been one of the primary sources of global electricity production in the past century . However, besides electricity production, these power plants generate carbon dioxide (enhancing the greenhouse effect) and release small PM .
The Mae Moh Power Plant is the oldest and largest coal-fired power plant in Thailand, located in the central region of the Indochina Peninsula. Pulverized lignite from the coal mine in Mae Moh District (Lampang, Thailand) is used as fossil fuel in thermal power stations to generate electricity . Fly ash from the coal-fired power plants is a key target for air pollution control by the Pollution Control Department (Thailand), a government regulatory agency. It is trapped using an electrostatic precipitator (ESP) to improve air quality before release into the atmosphere . This system helps meet OECD regulatory standards for air emissions .
Small PM, particularly in the 0.01–10-micron size range, has recently emerged as a major health concern , . PM is a heterogenous mixture comprising varying particle sizes and chemical compositions. Its major chemical components include inorganic elements, water-soluble ions, elemental carbon, organic carbon, and organic compounds . PM derived from coal-fired power plants, especially those using lignite coal, has become a critical public concern owing to its significant contribution to air pollution and associated health risks from the by-products of electricity generation.
Lignite is a brownish-black coal, with high moisture (30–50 % w/w) and low carbon content (approximately 25–35 % w/w) . The chemical components of lignite fly ash are mainly silicon oxide (15–45 %), calcium oxide (15–40 %), aluminum oxide (20–25 %), and iron oxide (4–15 %) . Several studies have reported the hazardous toxins in fly ash PM, with heavy metals and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) being among the most concerning pollutants. These substances can cause both short-term and long-term toxicological effects . The PM toxicity levels depend on particle size, chemical composition, exposure duration, and host demographic factors such as age, gender, and hormonal status , , .
Acute pulmonary toxicities of fly ash PM were reported mainly in rodent models with different doses and routes . Among these, intratracheal instillation is a commonly used method for delivering PM directly into the trachea, bypassing the upper airway sedimentation . This technique offers the advantage of precise dose delivery to the lower respiratory tract with fewer technical demands compared to inhalation exposure . Although natural inhalation more closely mimics real-world exposure to PM, it is subject to several experimental limitations. Variability in breathing patterns, mucosal defense mechanisms, and physical activity levels can lead to inconsistent pulmonary dosing, potentially affecting the toxicological outcomes. In contrast, intratracheal instillation minimizes these inconsistencies and reduces the risk of laboratory personnel being exposed to highly toxic or carcinogenic substances, which is a major concern during aerosol generation in inhalation chambers.
Fly ash PM has been shown to induce several features of acute pulmonary injury, including interstitial edema, alveolar macrophage accumulation, and neutrophilic infiltration in the peribronchial zones. Short-term exposure to fly ash PM can provoke neutrophilic inflammation and elevate inflammatory markers in lung tissues of rat models . However, this histopathology method cannot provide mechanistic insights into cellular biochemistry, especially regarding protein functions.
Proteomics enables a better understanding of biological structure and function by identifying the types and expression levels of proteins affected by various stimuli, using unbiased selection . Global protein expression and pathway mapping can provide deeper insights into disease states and offer greater predictive power for toxicity assessment than conventional toxicological evaluations . Proteomic analysis, combined with bioinformatic pathway identification, can help identify potential disease markers and their mechanisms. These factors are valuable for disease screening and the development of preventive interventions against PM-induced health effects.
The primary objective of this study was to determine acute pulmonary toxicity of fly ash PM from a coal-fired power plant, using Balb/c mice with vehicle-treated mice serving as a negative control. The secondary objective was to explore the distinct molecular pathways involved associated with the toxicological effects of coal fly PM, using standard PM2.5 as positive control. For this purpose, we used Standard Reference Materials® (SRM) 2786 (fine PM < 4 μm), developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST, United States). This reference material is characterized by a wide range of organic and inorganic constituents. SRM 2786 was collected from atmospheric PM via an air intake filtration system at a major exhibition center in Prague, Czech Republic, reflecting typical urban air pollution. The comprehensive characterization of this material was conducted using validated analytical methods. By providing a standardized benchmark, SRM 2786 enables researchers to assess the composition and potential health impacts of ambient PM.
PM exposure doses were selected based on the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) air quality guidelines , and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration's (USFDA) allometric scaling principles for xenobiotic exposure across mammal species , especially humans and mice. Our study findings may lead to a better understanding of the toxicological profiles and mechanistic pathways of fly ash PM from coal-fired power plants, providing relevant insights for researchers and policymakers worldwide.

## Material and methods
2.1

## Chemicals
Standard PM2.5 (SRM 2786, Fine Atmospheric Particulate Matter < 4 µm) was purchased from NIST (United States). The standard PAH mix (PAH Mix 642,000 µg/mL in benzene/dichloromethane) was purchased from Dr. Ehrenstorfer™ (Germany). It included naphthalene, acenaphthylene, acenaphthene, fluorene, phenanthrene, anthracene, fluoranthene, pyrene, benz[a]anthracene, chrysene, benzo[b]fluoranthene, benzo[k]fluoranthene, benzo[a]pyrene, indeno[1,2,3-c,d]pyrene, dibenz[a,h]anthracene, and benzo[g,h,i]perylene. The internal standard mixture (EPA 8270 Internal Standard Mix 4000 µg/mL) was sourced from LGC Standards (England). It comprised 1,4-dichlorobenzene-d4, naphthalene-d8, acenaphthene-d10, phenanthrene-d10, chrysene-d12, and perylene-d12. Hexane (HPLC grade) was obtained from RCI Labscan (Thailand), while HNO3 (65 %) and HCl (trace analysis grade, Suprapur®) were sourced from Merck (Germany). Fly ash PM was generously provided by the Mae Moh Power Plant (Lampang, Thailand), with the collection date recorded as November 29, 2021 via ESP.

2.2

## Heavy metal analysis by inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS)
Heavy metals (arsenic, cadmium, chromium, cobalt, copper, iron, lead, manganese, nickel, zinc, and mercury) were measured using the ICP-MS-2030 system (Shimadzu, Japan). The fly ash PM sample was prepared via microwave digestion (Ultrawave, Milestone, Italy). In brief, 100 mg of the fly ash PM was digested with 1.25 mL of HNO3 and 3.75 mL of HCl, then diluted with distilled water to a final volume of 25 mL. The digestion process involved two steps: (i) 1500 W, with pressure 110 bar, at 250 °C (temperature 1) and 60 °C (temperature 2) for 15 min (step 1); (ii) 1500 W, with pressure 110 bar, at 250 °C (temperature 1) and 60 °C (temperature 2) for 30 min (step 2). ICP-MS analysis was performed under the following conditions: radio frequency power of 1.20 kW, sampling depth of 5.00 mm, plasma gas flow rate of 9.00 L/min, auxiliary gas flow rate of 1.10 L/min, and carrier gas (Ar, purity 99.995 %) flow rate of 0.70 L/min. The analytical performance was validated through quality control measures, including assessment of linearity and limit of quantification ( ).

2.3

## PAH analysis by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS)
Fly ash PM sample (30 mg) was extracted thrice with hexane (100 µL) for 30 min using a mixing shaker, then centrifuged at 11,180 g for 5 min to collect the supernatant. The extracted sample was filtered through a 0.45 µm nylon syringe filter (Shimadzu, Japan). PAHs were analyzed using a gas chromatograph and an electron impact mass spectrometer (GC-MS QP-2010 ultra, Shimadzu, Japan). Separation was performed using an Rxi-5Sil MS GC capillary column (0.25 mm internal diameter × 30 m length × 0.25 µm film thickness; crossbond 1,4-bis(dimethylsiloxy)phenylene dimethyl polysiloxane stationary phase, Restek, United States). The carrier gas was Helium, maintained at a flow rate at 1.00 mL/min, and the inlet temperature was set at 280 °C. The injection volume was 1.0 µL in splitless mode.
The oven temperature program was as follows: 40 °C for 1 min, ramped to 300 °C (at 8 °C/min) over 20 min. The MS interface and ion source temperatures were set at 280 °C and 200 °C, respectively. Solvent elution time was 4 min and data sampling time was 4.5–53.5 min. Selected Ion Monitoring of MS parameters was used for PAH detection, with quantitative and confirmation ions for each PAH and internal standard listed in . Quality control parameters, including the limit of detection (LOD), limit of quantification (LOQ), and linearity, were validated to ensure of analytical performance ( ).

2.4

## Animal procedures and PM treatment
All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Protocol, Faculty of Pharmacy, Mahidol University-Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Ethic approval number: PYR 001/2024). Eight to twelve-week-old male Balb/c mice were purchased from Nomura Siam International (Bangkok, Thailand) and housed at the Central Animal Facility, Faculty of Science, Mahidol University (Bangkok, Thailand). The acclimatization period was 1 week. All mice were maintained at 22 ± 1 °C, on a 12-h light-dark cycle, with 30–70 % relative humidity and provided reverse osmosis water ad libitum. All animal procedures complied with the Thailand National Guidelines for Animal Use, the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and the ARRIVE guidelines , .
Mice were separated into five groups according to their sources and PM dose (n = 6 per group): (1) Control (normal saline), (2) Low-dose fly ash PM (1 mg/kg/day), (3) High-dose fly ash PM (10 mg/kg/day), (4) Low-dose standard PM2.5 (1 mg/kg/day), and (5) High-dose standard PM2.5 (10 mg/kg/day). The standard PM2.5 and fly ash PM were dissolved in normal saline and directly delivered to mouse lungs via intratracheal instillation once daily for 7 days. Body weight was measured on day 1 and day 7 to compare the differences in weight changes across experimental groups.
On day 7, mice were sacrificed by thiopental overdose (150 mg/kg IP). Following dissection, the whole left lung was used for histopathological analysis, while the whole right lung was used for proteomic analysis, due to differing sample preparation requirements. The left lung was perfused with 10 % neutral buffered formalin (Leica, United States) for histological preservation, while the right lung was snap-frozen at −20 °C without prior fixation to preserve protein composition. All other organs (heart, spleen, liver, kidney, brain, and intestines) were immediately dissected and fixed in the same formalin solution.
For intratracheal instillation, anesthesia was induced by placing the mouse in the induction chamber and administering 5 % isoflurane in oxygen. After loss of consciousness and absence of response to toe pinch, isoflurane concentration was reduced to 1–2 % for maintenance via a nose cone. The anesthetized mouse was placed supine on an intubation platform, with the upper incisors secured to an incisor hook to stabilize the head and maintain an open airway. Tracheal intubation was performed by gently opening the mouth and inserting an endotracheal optic fiber light into the oral cavity to illuminate the tracheal opening. An endotracheal tube was carefully inserted into the trachea under direct vision. Once the tube was properly positioned, the optic fiber light was removed. The PM2.5 suspension was administered through the tube using a syringe in a slow, controlled manner according to the predetermined dose and volume. After administration, the syringe was disconnected, and the endotracheal tube was gently removed. Subsequently, the animal was transferred to a recovery box with a warm environment and monitored until full recovery of consciousness and normal behavior. It was then returned to its home cage. The total handling time for all intratracheal procedures was approximately 3–5 min per animal.

2.5

## Histopathological analysis
The lung, heart, spleen, liver, kidney, brain, and intestines were dissected, rinsed with 1 × phosphate-buffered saline, and fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde. Slides were prepared using the paraffin section technique and microsected at thickness of 5 µm. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and evaluated blindly by a pathologist. Lung histopathology was assessed based on the following criteria: (i) perivascular inflammation (graded as absent, rare = fewer than three foci; few = 3–6 foci); (ii) area of involvement by significant alveolar edema (defined by fluid-filled alveolar sacs without appreciable air space); (iii) percentage of bronchiole showing airway inflammation (including the presence of intra-alveolar macrophages and intra/peribronchiolar inflammatory cell infiltration; ).

2.6

## Proteomic analysis of mouse lungs
After sacrificing the mice using the method described earlier, the lung tissues were collected, pooled, and homogenized via sonication using a 2 mm stepped microtip at 30 % amplitude. Protein lysates were prepared for proteomics analysis using the EasyPep™ Mini MS Sample Prep Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Protein concentration in the lysates was determined using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States) and adjusted to 1 µg/µL in a final volume of 100 µL. Reduction and alkylation solutions were then added, followed by incubation at 95 °C for 10 min. After cooling to 25 °C, trypsin/Lys-C protease mixture was added and the mixture was shaken at 37 °C for 12 h to achieve complete protein digestion. The digested protein samples were purified via peptide clean-up columns, and the purified samples were collected and dried under vacuum centrifugation at 45 °C . The samples were reconstituted with 100 µL of 0.1 % formic acid (Fisher Scientific, United States) in water for liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis.
LC-MS analysis was performed using a rapid separation liquid chromatography (RSLC) nano UltiMate™ 3000 system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris™ 480 Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States), equipped with a PepMap™ RSLC C18 column (2 µm, 100 Å, 75 µm × 25 cm; Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States). Each peptide sample was analyzed in triplicate injections. A previously described protocol was used with modifications .
Chromatographic separation was achieved with the following elution gradient: starting with 3 % mobile phase B (0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile; Fisher Scientific, United States) maintained for 7 min, increasing to 6 % B over 1 min, then to 40 % B over 35 min. The gradient was then rapidly increased to 80 % B over 1 min and held for an additional 4 min. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1 % formic acid in water. The flow rate was maintained at 0.4 µL/min.
Mass spectrometry was performed in positive ion mode using a nanospray ion source with a spray voltage of 1.2 kV and an ion transfer tube temperature of 280 °C. An Orbitrap resolution of 120,000 was used for MS1 data acquisition with a scan range of 525–1200 m/z, employing a high-field asymmetric waveform ion mobility spectrometry Pro interface at a compensation voltage of -60V and radio frequency lens fixed at 50 %. MS2 scans were conducted at an Orbitrap resolution of 15,000, with the higher-energy collisional dissociation at 27 % and an isolation window of 1.2 m/z.
Data analysis was performed using Proteome Discoverer software (version 2.5.0.400), applying a contaminants exclusion list provided by the Max Planck Institute of Biochemistry (Martinsried). The strict target false discovery rate (FDR) was set at 0.01, and the relaxed target FDR was set to 0.05. The SEQUEST-HT and Minora (Minora Feature Detector) algorithms were used to match the tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) spectra to the UniProt Mus musculus databases (taxon ID 10090) for protein identification and label free protein quantitation, respectively.
Enrichment analysis was performed on Enrichr , , (accessed at: https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/ on December 15, 2023) using the KEGGPathway_2021_Human database (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.html) . Biological pathways were visualized using Appyters (accessed at: https://appyters.maayanlab.cloud/#/ on December 15, 2023) , . Protein-protein interaction networks were constructed using STRING (accessed at: https://string-db.org/ on December 15, 2023) .

2.7

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using STATA® Statistical Software (version 15.0). The Kruskal-Wallis test was applied because of the non-parametric distribution of outcome variables, followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

## Results
3.1

## Heavy metal analysis of fly ash PM
All major heavy metals in the fly ash PM were quantitated using ICP-MS analysis, with a detection range of 1–100 µg/L. The concentrations of heavy metals were recorded in mg/kg and as relative values to the standard PM2.5. Arsenic in the fly ash PM exhibited a markedly high relative value, approximately 1000 % higher than that in the standard PM. Iron was the second highest, with relative values of 200–300 %. Other heavy metals, such as cobalt and manganese, showed relative values of approximately 100 %–130 % compared to the standard PM. In contrast, lead, zinc, copper, and cadmium exerted low relative values, ranging approximately 1–10 % ( ). 

3.2

## PAH analysis of fly ash PM
The PAHs in fly ash PM from Mae Moh Power Plant were quantitated using a validated GC-MS method. Analytical performance of the GC-MS method was confirmed via estimation of the LOD, LOQ, and linearity. The LODs and LOQs of 16 analyzed PAHs ranged 0.20–4.9 µg/L and 0.99–16.2 µg/L, respectively. The regression coefficient of the standard curves was > 0.995, which was considered acceptable linearity. PAH concentrations in fly ash PM were recorded in mg/kg and as relative values to the standard PM2.5 ( ). Notably, Fly ash PM showed high relative concentrations of dibenz[a,h]anthracene and acenaphthene, approximately 300–600 %, in comparison with standard PM. Some PAHs, including acenaphthylene, anthracene, and fluorene, were also elevated, approximately 150 %–300 % higher than standard PM. In contrast, benzo[b]fluoranthene, fluoranthene, and pyrene levels were substantially lower, approximately 1–10 % of those in standard PM. 

3.3

## Toxicological effects of fly ash PM and standard PM2.5 in Balb/c mice
All animals could tolerate the test substances, including vehicle, fly ash PM, and standard PM2.5. No mortality or any abnormal physical appearance were observed in the animals after administration. Mice in the vehicle control group exhibited a slight increase in body weight from day 0 to day 7. In contrast, a significant reduction (p < 0.05) in body weight over the same period was observed in both fly ash PM and standard PM2.5 groups compared to that in the vehicle control group ( ). The inflammatory responses were blindly scored according to the percentage of area affected by parenchymal edema, peri/intrabronchiolar infiltration, perivascular infiltration, and intra-alveolar macrophages ( ). The infiltrating inflammatory cells included mixed populations of neutrophils, lymphocytes, and plasma cells. Microscopic examination demonstrated acute and chronic inflammation in both standard PM2.5 and fly ash PM groups (p < 0.05). Acute inflammatory responses included intra-alveolar macrophages and neutrophilic infiltration in the peribronchial and perivascular zones. Lymphocytes and plasma cells, typically recruited in the later stages of inflammation, were also detected admixed with neutrophils in these areas. 
Notably, intra-alveolar macrophages were prominent in the High-dose standard PM2.5 group. Compared to the control group, the severity of lung tissue damage in the standard PM2.5 groups was moderate to severe and dose dependent. In contrast, the Low-dose fly ash PM group showed only mild inflammation. Interestingly, perivascular infiltration was detected in all mice in the High-dose standard PM2.5 group, and in 40–80 % of mice in the fly ash PM and Low-dose standard PM2.5 groups. In contrast, perivascular infiltration was completely absent in the control group. Histopathology data of individual animals are provided in . Additional histologic examination of the heart, spleen, liver, kidney, brain, and intestines showed no notable pathological alterations ( ).

3.4

## Proteomics analysis of mouse lung tissue after exposure to fly ash PM and standard PM2.5 in Balb/c mice
Proteomic analysis of mouse lung tissue with LC-MS/MS identified a total of 2246 proteins (1460 master proteins) after excluding all contaminants. All raw data have been deposited and can be accessed through the accession ID: PXD058082. The complete list of identified master proteins and their abundance in each treatment group is available in supplementary file 2. Sample preparation and analytical consistency were validated by similar protein abundance distributions across all groups ( A). Principal component analysis showed distinct groupings based on exposure source and dose: blue (control), green (Low-dose fly ash PM), orange (High-dose fly ash PM), purple (Low-dose standard PM2.5), and red (High-dose standard PM2.5). PC1 accounted for 44.1 % of the total variation, and PC2 accounted for 23.3 % ( B). The proteins in the High-dose fly ash PM group exceeded the threshold of ±2log2 fold change and p < 0.05 when compared to those in the High-dose standard PM2.5 group. They were considered differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) and were subjected to pathway analysis (abundance of these proteins across all groups are provided in supplementary file 3). Pathway enrichment analysis (PEA) of DEPs was conducted through Enrichr using the KEGG pathway 2021 Human database (gene symbols were used instead of protein names). 
To investigate inflammation-related mechanisms in mice lungs, three pathways of interest were selected from PEA of DEPs between the High-dose fly ash PM and High-dose standard PM2.5 groups: leukocyte transendothelial migration (hsa04670), neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation (hsa04613), and phagosome activity (hsa04145). A volcano plot summarizing pathway comparisons is presented in . In addition, hierarchical cluster heatmaps visualizing protein expression across all treatment groups are shown in . Protein–protein interaction networks generated via STRING revealed a major network of experimentally verified interactions ( ). Hierarchical clustering grouped the High-dose standard PM2.5 samples distinctly from all the other treatment groups, including the control. A heatmap was generated using all proteins associated with inflammation ( ). Additionally, the network analysis of protein–protein interactions revealed overlapping pathways between the High-dose fly ash PM and High-dose standard PM2.5 groups, both of which induced inflammation and immune responses ( ). This approach facilitates the identification of potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets by highlighting key nodes within the inflammatory response network. 
Key proteins from the leukocyte transendothelial migration pathway included Ezrin (EZR), catenin alpha 1 (CTNNA1), catenin delta 1 (CTNND1), and actinin alpha 1 (ACTN1). The essential proteins involved in the formation of NETs were macroH2A.1 histone (MACROH2A1), H4 clustered histone 1 (H4C1), histone deacetylase 1 (HDAC1), and histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC2). Essential proteins involved in the phagosome pathway included ATPase H+ transporting lysosomal V1 subunits A, B2, and E1 (ATP6V1A, ATP6V1B2, and ATP6V1E1), as well as tubulin beta-2A class IIA (TUBB2A) and tubulin beta-4A class IVA (TUBB4A).

## Discussion
Coal-fired power plants are major contributors of the global electricity supply, accounting for approximately 30 % of total production share in the past decade . Pulverized coal is burned to generate heat, which is then used to convert boiler water into steam for electricity production. However, pollution from by-products of coal-fired power plants remains a significant environmental concern. Lignite coal, the most commonly used type in such plants, is considered a low-quality hydrocarbon that contains nitrogen and sulfur in its molecular structure. As a result, the combustion of lignite releases common pollutants such as carbon dioxide, nitrogen oxide, and sulfur dioxide. Several modern technologies have been developed to reduce air pollution from these emissions. These include carbon dioxide removal technologies, flue-gas desulfurization, and ESPs. ESPs are widely used to remove PM from fly ash in coal-fired power plants . The chemical composition of fly ash PM collected from ESPs has been reported by several researchers . Evaluating the fly ash PM from ESPs is therefore important to identify its major toxin components and assess its toxicological effects in animal models.
Intratracheal instillation was selected as the delivery method to determine the respiratory toxicity of fly ash PM in rodents. Dose selection was based on the criteria of the WHO air quality guidelines (2021), which recommend PM2.5 exposure levels of 5–50 µg/m3. In addition, allometric scaling of xenobiotic exposure among mammal species was used to calculate the appropriate, non-lethal PM2.5 doses for mice. The low dose of fly ash PM (1 mg/kg/day) corresponds to the maximal safety limit of PM2.5 exposure in ambient air, while the high dose (10 mg/kg/day) reflects concentrations associated with adverse health effects. After intratracheal instillation for seven consecutive days, no mortality was observed in any of the groups (vehicle control, fly ash PM, or standard PM2.5). All animals maintained normal physical activity throughout the experiment, with limited signs of toxicity. Consistent with previous findings, we observed a reduction in body weight following PM2.5 exposure .
To minimize potential inconsistencies in PM2.5 distribution between the lungs, we employed a standardized intratracheal instillation protocol, carefully controlling the volume and rate to ensure consistent delivery. For comparability and reproducibility, we consistently used the same lung side for each specific analysis across all experimental animals. These measures allow us to attribute our findings to true biological effects rather than technical variations in PM2.5 deposition. Our primary objective in separating lung tissues was to optimize sample integrity and data quality for each analytical method. Histology requires fixation to preserve morphology, which degrades the proteins essential for proteomic analysis. Conversely, proteomics requires fresh-frozen tissue to maintain protein integrity, which is incompatible with fixation. By dedicating one lung to each technique, we achieved better data quality for both morphological and molecular assessments. Previous studies have used separate lungs for histology and omics analyses, with one side of the lung fixed for histological evaluation and the other side snap-frozen for metabolomic or proteomic profiling , , . This method helps minimize inter-animal variability and ensures that both types of analyses are performed under consistent biological conditions. Under normal conditions, mice exhibit symmetrical distribution of major immune cell types, including alveolar macrophages, interstitial macrophages, dendritic cells, innate lymphoid cells, and various lymphocytes (T and B cells), in both lungs . However, in certain disease models, such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection, immune responses and pathological changes may preferentially affect specific lung lobes . These cases represent exceptions where asymmetrical distribution of immune cells or inflammatory mediators may occur.
The primary target organ of toxicity in this experiment is likely to be the respiratory system. Acute inflammation of pulmonary tissue was observed in both the fly ash PM and standard PM2.5 compared with that in the vehicle control. In the standard PM2.5 groups, a dose-dependent relationship was observed after increasing the dose from 1.0 to 10 mg/kg/day. Consistent with a previous report by Smith et al., neutrophil infiltration and increase in pulmonary inflammation were observed. Smith et al. reported that coal fly ash exposure can increase neutrophil counts in both blood circulation and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid in SD rats . The proteins involved in lung inflammation were investigated via proteomics to gain mechanistic insights into lung histopathology. Although inflammation is a natural host response to infection and injury, excessive or unresolved inflammatory processes can contribute to cell injury and tissue remodeling, resulting in severe pathological consequences .
Leukocyte migration from the bloodstream into tissues is crucial for inflammation and immune surveillance. Pulmonary inflammation is mediated by adhesion molecules expressed on both leukocytes and endothelial cells of the pulmonary vasculature . In our study, EZR expression was increased specifically in the High-dose fly ash PM group ( A). EZR is considered as an important modulator of airway cells, regulating membrane receptor complexes and signal transduction pathways . In addition, high EZR expression can increase the progression of non–small-cell lung cancer . Among the downregulated proteins of the leukocyte transendothelial migration pathway in the High-dose standard PM2.5 group (as shown in the heatmap), CTNNA1 and CTNND1 emerged as hub genes in the STRING analysis ( A). CTNNA1 is widely recognized as a crucial protein that maintains the connection between E-Cadherin and β-catenin. Insufficiency or non-expression of CTNNA1 may result in the malfunction of the E-cadherin/catenin complex. This promotes tumor cell proliferation and invasion, and disrupts intercellular contact inhibition . CTNND1, also known as p120-catenin, is a critical component of adherens junctions in epithelial cells. A recent study indicates that p120 is essential for regulating cadherin stability and turnover, as well as modulating innate immunity. Severity of inflammation has also been associated with decrease in p120 levels. Therefore, the severe lung inflammation observed in p120EKO animals can be attributed to the disruption of E-cadherin facilitated by p120 ablation. This destabilization leads to a breakdown in the alveolar epithelial barrier, allowing antigens to enter the alveoli and trigger lung inflammation . Other studies demonstrated that siRNA-mediated p120 knockdown in lung endothelial cells enhances the lipopolysaccharide-induced lung inflammatory response . Our findings suggest that changes in CTNNA1 and CTNND1 expression may influence ACTN1 expression ( ). Heatmap analysis showed the highest expression of ACTN1 in the High-dose standard PM2.5 group, which also exhibited the highest inflammation score among all treated groups. As expected, CTNNA1 and CTNND1 expression levels were the lowest in the High-dose standard PM2.5 group. ACTN1 is known to participate in the signaling pathway chain events initiated by TNF-α and IL-1 . Collectively, both High-dose standard PM2.5 and High-dose fly ash PM groups showed pro-inflammatory properties. However, the High-dose standard PM2.5 group showed the most pronounced inflammation, likely due to altered protein expression within the leukocyte transendothelial migration pathway.
Neutrophils have a crucial function in the innate immune defense system, and the formation of NETs is one of their key mechanisms of action. In our study, the High-dose standard PM2.5 group showed elevated expression of MACROH2A1 and H4C1, which are potentially associated with NET formation. This suggests that standard PM2.5 can induce NETs in a dose-dependent manner. Moreover, MACROH2A1 expression is typically elevated in slowly proliferating lung cancers and is linked to a cancer stem cell phenotype , . Significantly higher levels of macroH2A1 transcripts have also been observed in hepatoblastoma tumor tissues compared to those in the adjacent normal tissues . Similarly, H4C1 is significantly increased in lung cystic fibrosis , and highly expressed in breast cancer, particularly during metastatic relapse . These findings indicate that prolonged exposure to particulate matter could potentially promote conditions favorable for cancer progression, warranting further investigation. Additionally, fly ash PM exhibits a distinct expression pattern from standard PM2.5, which may result from differences in their chemical and metal composition, specifically its high iron content. Iron overload is known to inhibit the release of NETs . Differences in PM composition based on their source are well documented and are known to influence toxicological outcomes . Within the NET pathway, we observed high expression of HDAC1 and low expression of HDAC2 in the High-dose standard PM2.5 group, suggesting potential epigenetic alterations associated with pulmonary diseases and tumorigenesis , . HDAC1 is highly expressed in conditions such as allergic rhinitis and severe asthma. A previous study indicates that HDAC1 is present in most airway cells and inflammatory cells that infiltrate asthmatic lung tissues . Histone deacetylases are crucial epigenetic regulators that have been shown to have vital functions in cancer stem cells . According to Dunaway et al., 2022, HDAC1 is the most abundant member of the class I HDACs in pulmonary endothelial cells . Meanwhile, HDAC2 plays a crucial role in regulating cancer stem cell characteristics and promoting tumor formation, particularly in brain tumor stem cells with diverse genetic backgrounds .
Furthermore, particulate matter exposure leads to macrophage phagosome dysfunction by interfering phagosome activity. Phagocytosis is a cellular process in which large particles are engulfed, playing a crucial role in tissue remodeling, inflammation, and defense against infectious agents. A phagosome is formed when the phagocyte surface receptors identify specific ligands on target particles. In the High-dose standard PM2.5 group, TUBB2A and TUBB4A were downregulated, while ATP6V1A, ATP6V1B2, and ATP6V1E1 were upregulated. Previous studies have demonstrated the significant involvement of ATP6V1 in disease processes, including cancer. Decreased expression of ATP6V1A, ATP6V1B2, and ATP6V1E1 has been linked with reduced overall survival in renal clear cell carcinoma . TUBB2A is associated with cellular proliferation, movement, and adhesion, while LTF is involved in cell death, the immune response, and metabolism. TUBB2A is upregulated in distant metastatic breast cancer compared to its levels in non-distant metastatic cases. It is also overexpressed in highly invasive cancer cell lines compared to that in cells with low invasiveness . TUBB4A, also known as human tubulin beta class IVa, belongs to the β-tubulin family. TUBB4A expression is negligible or absent in normal tissues, but is downregulated in hypoxic conditions in prostate cancer, a state known to increase cancer aggressiveness .
Overall, the findings of this study indicate that short-term exposure to PM activates defense mechanisms in lung tissue. To gain a deeper understanding of the potential link with cancer development, further studies investigating the effects of long-term exposure are warranted. The limitations of this study include the use of fly ash PM collected specifically from the Mae Moh Power Plant. As a result, certain chemical components may differ from those reported in other studies owing to variations in lignite origin. Additionally, certain heat labile PAHs may have degraded in the coal furnace at high temperatures, potentially reducing their toxicity. Furthermore, the animal exposure model involved sub-acute toxicity testing via intratracheal instillation. This method may not fully replicate chronic respiratory exposure experienced by humans through ambient air inhalation.

5

## Conclusion
The major toxicants with high relative abundance in fly ash PM from the Mae Moh power plant were arsenic, iron, dibenz[a,h]anthracene, and acenaphthene. This coal-derived PM significantly induced airway inflammation in Balb/c mice. Proteomic analysis revealed three key pathways, including leukocyte transendothelial migration, NET formation, and phagosome activity, which may contribute to increased physiological inflammatory levels in vivo. Persistent inflammation may lead to the development of various chronic diseases and promote the progression of certain cancers, particularly lung cancer. These findings may be useful in developing appropriate strategies for pollution management targeting fly ash PM from coal-fired power plants.
