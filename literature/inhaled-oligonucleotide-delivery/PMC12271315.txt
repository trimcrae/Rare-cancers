## Abstract
Immature neutrophils (imNeu) are a minor population of circulating neutrophils that migrate from the bone marrow (BM) into the circulation and inflamed tissues during infection, injury, physical stress, and cancer. However, the underlying mechanism of their mobilization from BM and its pathophysiological significance remains incompletely understood. Here, we show that interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) derived from type 1 innate lymphoid cells (ILC1) enhances the migration of imNeu, but not mature neutrophils, from the BM into inflamed liver tissue with ischemia–reperfusion injury and the blood circulation during polymicrobial sepsis in mice. Mechanistically, the scaffold protein Ahnak, which is specifically expressed in imNeu, underpins Smad7 nuclear translocation in response to IFN-γ, thus downregulating C-X-C chemokine receptor 4 expression critical for neutrophil retention in the BM. Furthermore, imNeu produce interleukin-10 to ameliorate tissue inflammation. Our findings thus reveal the ILC1–imNeu axis that protects tissues from acute inflammation due to injury or microbial infection.
Subject terms: Experimental models of disease, Acute inflammation, Neutrophils, Innate lymphoid cells

## Introduction
Neutrophils are the predominant immune cell type in the blood circulation, where they play key roles in host defense against pathogens through degranulation, phagocytosis, and the release of nuclear material in the form of neutrophil extracellular traps . Recent studies have revealed various neutrophil subpopulations with phenotypically and functionally heterogeneous characteristics in healthy and pathological conditions . Two major subpopulations are present in the circulation: proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory neutrophils. Proinflammatory neutrophils have the multilobular segmented nucleus associated with mature neutrophils (mNeu), show the surface markers of CD11b–CD49d+, and produce interleukin (IL)−12 in mice with methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infections . In contrast, anti-inflammatory neutrophils contain an immature type of ring-form nucleus (imNeu), are CD11b+CD49d–, and produce IL-10 . It is increasingly evident that imNeu have superior anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and tissue remodeling capabilities in response to acute infection with Japanese encephalitis , methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus , and optic nerve crush injury .
Because neutrophils are short-lived, they must be replenished constantly through their migration from the bone marrow (BM). Rapid mobilization of neutrophils from the BM requires the coordinated yet distinct actions of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, which augments neutrophil mobilization by disrupting the interaction between C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) and stromal cell-derived factor 1, and C-X-C chemokines that stimulate neutrophil chemotaxis across the BM endothelium via interaction with CXCR2 . Although imNeu are a minor population of circulating neutrophils, they actively migrate from the BM into the circulation and inflamed tissues during infection, injury, physical stress, and cancer. The mechanism underlying the mobilization of imNeu from the BM and the pathophysiological significance of this process remains incompletely understood.
Type 1 innate lymphoid cells (ILC1) are tissue-resident immune cells that are particularly enriched in the liver and involved in the pathogenesis of liver damage , . ILC1 sense pathogen-associated molecular patterns and damage-associated molecular patterns in inflamed tissues and promptly initiates local immune responses by producing cytokines, including interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) – . Recent evidence suggests that hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) shapes the ILC1 phenotype through upregulation of the transcriptional factor T-bet, and oxidative insults induce IFN-γ secretion from ILC1 subsets , . Yet, although tissue-resident ILC1 subsets have unique roles as the “first responders” in maintaining tissue homeostasis against inflammation, little is known about the pathophysiological role of ILC1 in acute inflammation.
Here, we investigate the pathophysiological role of imNeu and ILC1 in acute inflammation. We show that IFN-γ secreted from ILC1 in response to ischemic liver damage or systemic polymicrobial sepsis enhances the mobilization of imNeu from the BM into the circulation and inflamed tissues via downregulating CXCR4 expression on imNeu. Moreover, imNeu produces an anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in the inflamed tissue, reducing inflammation. Our results unveil the role of the ILC1-imNeu axis in inflammation.

## Results

## ILC1-derived IFN-γ ameliorates liver IRI
To investigate the role of ILC1 in inflammatory responses, we used a mouse model of ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) in the liver, the organ with the greatest number of ILC1. We found that plasma concentrations of IFN-γ were increased at 6 h after ischemia-reperfusion (IR) (Fig.  ). To analyze the role of IFN-γ in the pathogenesis of liver IRI, we compared liver damage between wild-type (WT) and IFN-γ-deficient (Ifng–/–) mice. At 6 h after IR, Ifng–/– mice had higher plasma concentrations of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and more severe hepatic necrosis and hemorrhage than did WT mice (Fig.  ). Moreover, mice subjected to IR and then injected intravenously with a neutralizing monoclonal antibody (mAb) against IFN-γ had higher plasma concentrations of ALT and larger areas of hepatic necrosis and hemorrhage than did mice injected with an isotype control mAb (Fig.  ). Taken together, these results indicate that IFN-γ has a protective effect against liver IRI.
 To determine whether ILC1-derived IFN-γ ameliorates IRI, we used flow cytometry to analyze the composition of liver lymphocytes during the 24 h after IR (Supplementary Fig.  ). Although the frequencies of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) T cells at 6 h after IR were comparable to those before IR, the populations of ILC1 and NK cells were increased as early as 1 and 3 h, respectively, after IR (Fig.  ). The absolute numbers of total liver lymphocytes, ILC1 and NK cells were comparable between WT and Ifng–/– mice before and 3 h after IR (Supplementary Fig.  ), suggesting that IFN-γ did not impact on the existence of liver lymphocytes including ILC1 and NK cells in homeostasis or after IRI. To examine the role of ILC1 and NK cells in the pathogenesis of IRI, we used Rag2–/– mice, which lack adaptive lymphocytes, including T, B, and NKT cells, and Rag2–/–Il2rg–/– mice, which, in addition to those adaptive lymphocytes, are devoid of NK cells and ILC1 . At 6 h after IR, Rag2–/–Il2rg–/– mice had higher plasma concentrations of ALT and more severe necrosis and hemorrhage in the liver than did Rag2–/– mice (Supplementary Fig.  ); this result suggests that NK cells or ILC1 (or both) ameliorate hepatic IRI. We then analyzed the activation of NK cells and ILC1 after IR and found that ILC1, but not NK cells, showed upregulation of the activation marker CD69 and increased Ifng mRNA expression and IFN-γ production at 3 h after IR (Fig.  ). Together, these results suggest that IFN-γ derived from ILC1, rather than NK cells, ameliorates IRI in the liver.
 To test this hypothesis, we purified ILC1 from the livers of WT and Ifng–/– mice, cultured them in the presence of IL-7, and transferred the cultured cells with IL-15 into Rag2–/–Il2rg–/– mice, resulting in the reconstitution of ILC1 in the liver at a level comparable to WT mice 7 days later (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). These mice underwent liver IR. At 6 h after IR, mice that received Ifng–/– ILC1 had higher plasma ALT concentrations and more severe hepatic necrosis and hemorrhage than did mice given WT ILC1 (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). These results indicate that ILC1-derived IFN-γ protects the liver against IRI.

## IFN-γ-dependent IL-10 production by CD49d– imNeu ameliorates liver IRI
To investigate how ILC1-derived IFN-γ ameliorates liver IRI, we analyzed plasma concentrations of proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines in WT and Ifng–/– mice. At 6 h after IRI, plasma IL-10 concentrations were elevated in WT but not Ifng–/– mice (Fig.  ). In contrast, plasma concentrations of IL-6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and IL-12p70 at 6 h after IR were comparable between WT and Ifng–/– mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). To examine whether IL-10 was produced in inflamed liver tissue, we collected injured lobes of the liver 5 h after IR. IL-10 expression in damaged liver tissue after IRI was increased in WT mice compared with in the liver tissue of naive mice (Fig.  ) but scarcely present in Ifng–/– mice (Fig.  ). These results suggest that the injured liver produces IL-10 and that this response is dependent on ILC1-derived IFN-γ.
 To examine the role of IFN-γ-dependent IL-10 production in the pathogenesis of liver IRI, we intravenously injected a neutralizing mAb against IL-10 into WT and Ifng–/– mice just after IR. Compared with mice that received a control mAb, WT mice treated with anti-IL-10 mAb after IR had increased plasma ALT concentrations and exacerbated tissue damage, including necrosis and hemorrhage in the liver parenchyma (Fig.  ). In contrast, plasma ALT concentrations and liver damage in Ifng–/– mice were comparable between those treated with control and anti-IL-10 mAbs (Fig.  ). Taken together, these results indicate that ILC1-derived IFN-γ induces IL-10 production, which plays an important role in ameliorating liver IRI.
To determine which cell type produces IL-10 in response to ILC1-derived IFN-γ after IR, we examined various myeloid cell subsets at 5 h after IR. WT mice that suffered from IRI exhibited increased numbers of both inflammatory monocytes (iMo) and neutrophils, but not dendritic cells and Kupffer cells (Supplementary Fig.  ). In comparison, Ifng–/– mice showed less increase in both iMo and neutrophils than did WT mice (Supplementary Fig.  ); this finding suggests that IFN-γ induces the recruitment of iMo and neutrophils into the liver after IR. Of note, Il10 expression was increased in neutrophils, but not in other myeloid cell subpopulations, in WT but not Ifng–/– mice after IR (Fig.  ). The depletion of neutrophils by i.p. injection of an anti-mouse Ly6G mAb did not increase the plasma IL-10 concentration in WT mice as well as in Ifng–/– mice. These data suggested that IFN-γ-dependent IL-10 production was mediated by neutrophils (Fig.  ).
During inflammation, circulating neutrophils comprise two major subpopulations: proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory. Proinflammatory neutrophils show a mature phenotype and are characterized as CD49d+IL-12+. In comparison, anti-inflammatory neutrophils have an immature phenotype and are CD49d–IL-10+ , . We found that both mNeu and imNeu containing segmented and ring-shaped forms of nuclei, respectively, infiltrated into the parenchymal region from the hepatic artery after liver IR (Supplementary Fig.  ). To examine whether CD49d expression on neutrophils is indeed associated with the maturation degree, we sorted CD45.2+Lin–CD11b+Ly6ClowLy6G+CD49d+ and CD45.2+Lin–CD11b+Ly6ClowLy6G+CD49d– neutrophils from the liver after IR. The morphological analysis demonstrated that the CD49d– subset had large, ring-shaped nuclei, while CD49d+ neutrophils had segmented nuclei with three to five lobes (Supplementary Fig.  ), indicating that neutrophils can be divided into mNeu and imNeu by the CD49d expression in the liver after IR. We then analyzed the Il10 expression of CD49d+ mNeu and CD49d– imNeu at 5 h after IR and found that only CD49d– imNeu, and not CD49d+ mNeu, expressed Il10 in the liver (Fig.  ). In addition, CD49d– imNeu in the blood expressed less Il10 than those in the liver (Fig.  ), suggesting that the amount of IL-10 produced by CD49d– imNeu depends on an environmental factor in the damaged liver. To examine this hypothesis, we isolated CD49d– imNeu from the blood and stimulated them with tissue homogenate supernatants of livers harvested from mice before and at 5 h after IR. The supernatant of liver homogenate from mice after IR, but not from naive mice, increased Il10 expression in CD49d– imNeu collected from the peripheral circulation (Fig.  ). We also found that in vitro stimulation of CD49d– imNeu with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) also induced Il10 expression, we hypothesized that a Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) ligand in the IRI liver may stimulate CD49d– imNeu to induce Il10 expression. Indeed, a TLR4 selective antagonist, TAK-242, inhibited Il10 expression induced by stimulation with the supernatant of liver homogenate from IRI mice (Fig.  ), suggesting that a TLR4 ligand such as High-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) released from the damaged cells in the IRI liver triggers CD49d– imNeu in the liver to produce IL-10 after IR.

## IFN-γ downregulates CXCR4 expression on imNeu and thus enhances its mobilization from BM
To address how ILC1-derived IFN-γ recruits imNeu into the liver after IR, we investigated the kinetics of mNeu and imNeu in WT and Ifng–/– mice after IR. In both genotypes, the number of mNeu at 5 h after IR increased in both the liver and blood and decreased in the BM. However, compared with WT mice, Ifng–/– mice had fewer imNeu in the liver and blood and more in the BM (Fig.  ). Unlike at 5 h after IRI, however, the numbers of imNeu in the blood and liver were comparable between WT and Ifng–/– mice at 12 h after IR, indicating that IFN-γ-dependent imNeu recruitment from the BM was downregulated at a later time point (Supplementary Fig.  ). These results suggest that IFN-γ is involved in imNeu mobilization from the BM only in the early phase after IRI. To confirm the effect of IFN-γ on imNeu mobilization, we i.v. injected IFN-γ into Ifng–/– mice, which resulted in a decrease in the number of imNeu in the BM and an increased circulating imNeu count (Fig.  ). In contrast, intravenous treatment with IFN-γ had no effect on mNeu mobilization (Fig.  ). We then analyzed how imNeu are recruited into the liver after IR. While the IFN-γ injection increased the number of imNeu as well as mNeu in the liver of Ifng–/– mice after liver IR, it did not show any effect on the number of both imNeu and mNeu in sham-operated mice (Fig.  ), indicating that IRI is required for the recruitment of the neutrophils into the liver. Collectively, these results suggest that IFN-γ enhances the recruitment of imNeu from the BM into the circulation but requires IRI to imNeu recruitment from the circulation into the liver.
 To address how IFN-γ mediates imNeu mobilization from the BM, we examined the expression of CXCR4, which plays an important role in the retention of neutrophils in the BM in response to CXCL12 , . In particular, we assessed CXCR4 expression on pre-neutrophils (i.e., granulocyte–macrophage progenitors) (preNeu; Lin–CD115–SiglecF–c-KitlowGr-1+CD11b+CXCR4high), imNeu (Lin–CD115–SiglecF–c-Kit–Gr-1lowCD11b+CXCR4–/lowCXCR2–), and mNeu (Lin–CD115–SiglecF–c-Kit–Gr-1+CD11b+CXCR4–/lowLy6G+CXCR2+) after treatment with IFN-γ for 20 h in vitro. IFN-γ treatment dose-dependently downregulated CXCR4 expression on imNeu but not preNeu and mNeu (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Moreover, a chemotaxis assay revealed that IFN-γ dose-dependently enhanced the mobilization of imNeu (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Together, these results suggest that IFN-γ induces the mobilization of imNeu from the BM through the downregulation of CXCR4 expression.
To further analyze the IFN-γ-mediated signaling other than the CXCR4-mediated pathway for the mobilization of imNeu from the BM, we investigated the expressions of Ifngr1, Fpr1, and Ltb4r1 that encode IFN-γ receptor 1, formyl peptide receptor 1 (Fpr1), and block lipid transport-1 (BLT1) on imNeu and mNeu. Ifngr1 was expressed in both neutrophil subsets, and IFN-γ stimulation similarly upregulated its expression. Fpr1 expression was detected in mNeu, rather than imNeu, in the BM and not altered by IFN-γ stimulation (Supplementary Fig.  ). On the other hand, Ltb4r1 was highly expressed in imNeu, but not in mNeu, but its expression was rather downregulated by IFN-γ stimulation (Supplementary Fig.  ). We also analyzed the expression of CXCR2, which is expressed on mNeu, but not imNeu, and mediates mobilizing signal from the BM. We found that CXCR2 expression was upregulated on mNeu after stimulation with IFN-γ in a dose-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig.  ). Taken together, these results suggest that Fpr1, BLT1 and CXCR2 were not involved in the imNeu migration in response to IFN-γ and supported the findings that the IFN-γ-CXCR4 axis plays a major role in imNeu mobilization from BM.

## IFN-γ induces the formation of the Ahnak–Smad7 complex in imNeu, thereby suppressing their expression of CXCR4
To elucidate the molecular mechanism as to how IFN-γ downregulates CXCR4 expression on imNeu but not preNeu and mNeu, we compared the distinct transcriptome expression profiles (i.e., more than 3000 differentially expressed genes) of preNeu, imNeu, and mNeu harvested from BM . Compared with gene expression in preNeu and mNeu, we identified one downregulated and eight upregulated genes in imNeu (Supplementary Table  ). Among those genes, we focused on Ahnak (Supplementary Fig.  ), which encodes the scaffold protein Ahnak. Ahnak is known to associate with the Smad proteins and drives their nuclear translocation and transcriptional regulation in the transforming growth factor-β signaling pathway , . We found that, regardless of IFN-γ stimulation or its absence, Ahnak was constitutively expressed in BM imNeu but not mNeu (Fig.  ). In addition, Smad7 expression was dose-dependently induced after IFN-γ stimulation in both imNeu and mNeu (Fig.  ), consistent with a previous report that IFN-γ induces Smad7 expression via STAT1 signaling . Given that CXCR4 expression is downregulated in Smad7-overexpressing tumor cells , we hypothesized that Ahnak supports the translocation of IFN-γ-induced Smad7 into the nucleus of imNeu in the BM, where it downregulates CXCR4 expression.
 To test this hypothesis, we used the cytoplasmic fraction of BM imNeu in a far-western blot analysis, where IFN-γ stimulation induced the physical association of Smad7 with Ahnak in the cytoplasm (Fig.  ). Moreover, the structural rendering from 3-dimensional images demonstrated that IFN-γ stimulation induces the translocation of the Ahnak–Smad7 complex into the nucleus (Fig.  , Supplementary Movies  , ). However, Smad7 remained in the cytoplasm in Ahnak-deficient BM imNeu, even after IFN-γ stimulation (Fig.  , Supplementary Movies  , ). These results suggest that Ahnak supports the nuclear translocation of Smad7.
To examine whether Smad7 downregulates CXCR4 expression on imNeu, we used short interference (si) RNA to knock down Smad7 in BM imNeu (Supplementary Fig.  ). The expression of CXCR4 after IFN-γ stimulation was higher in imNeu treated with Smad7 siRNA than in those transduced with scramble siRNA (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). In comparison, Smad7 knockdown had no effect on CXCR4 expression in mNeu (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Furthermore, IFN-γ stimulation suppressed CXCR4 expression in WT imNeu but not Ahnak–/– imNeu (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ). Although Ahnak regulates actin organization , the chemotaxis assay revealed that Ahnak–/– imNeu mobilized at a comparable level to WT imNeu after IFN-γ stimulation (Supplementary Fig.  ). In addition, WT and Ahnak–/– imNeu showed a comparable level of Il10 expression after stimulation with supernatants of IRI liver (Supplementary Fig.  ). These results suggest that Ahnak does not directly promote imNeu migration and IL-10 expression. Consistent with these results, after hepatic IRI, the pools of imNeu were larger in the BM and smaller in the circulation and liver of Ahnak–/– mice than WT mice (Fig.  ). Moreover, Ahnak–/– mice had higher plasma ALT concentrations than WT mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). Together, these results suggest that IFN-γ stimulation induces the formation of the Ahnak–Smad7 complex in imNeu, which downregulates their CXCR4 expression and consequently promotes their mobilization from the BM.

## ILC1-derived IFN-γ ameliorates inflammation induced by cecal ligation and puncture in mice
We next investigated whether ILC1 have the same role in microbial infections as in liver IRI. For this purpose, we used a polymicrobial sepsis model by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) in mice . At 24 h after CLP, peritoneal counts of NK cells were unchanged, but ILC1 were markedly increased (Supplementary Fig.  ), and both Ifng–/– and Ahnak–/– mice showed more severe loss of body weight (BW) and greater mortality after CLP than did WT mice (Fig.  ). In addition, compared with the injection of vehicle only, intravenous injection of IFN-γ ameliorated the post-CLP weight loss of Ifng–/– mice (Fig.  ), indicating that IFN-γ plays an important role in CLP-induced inflammation. To determine whether ILC1-derived IFN-γ achieves the reduction in inflammation, we purified ILC1 from the liver of WT and Ifng–/– mice and transferred them intraperitoneally into Rag2–/–Il2rg–/– mice. Mice transferred with Ifng–/– ILC1 showed more severe body weight loss than in those given WT ILC1 (Fig.  ). These results indicate that ILC1-derived IFN-γ ameliorates CLP-induced inflammation.
 We also noted that, at 48 h after CLP, both WT and Ifng–/– mice had increased numbers of circulating CD49d+ mNeu, whereas CD49d– imNeu counts increased only in WT, and not Ifng–/–, mice (Fig.  ). In addition, Il10 expression after CLP was not altered in circulating CD49d+ but upregulated in circulating and peritoneal CD49d– imNeu (Fig.  ), consistent with the results from the liver IRI model. Furthermore, Ifng–/– mice showed higher concentrations of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-12p70, and TNF, in the plasma and peritoneal fluid than WT mice after CLP (Supplementary Fig.  ). In contrast, concentrations of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 in the plasma and peritoneal fluid after CLP were lower in Ifng–/– mice than in WT mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). Finally, compared with using a control antibody, intraperitoneal injection of WT mice with a neutralizing mAb against IL-10 at 24 h after CLP exacerbated mortality (Fig.  ). Moreover, treatment with either an anti-IL-10 mAb or an anti-IFN-γ mAb before CLP exacerbated BW loss at a similar level to each other, and the combination of these two mAbs had no additive effect (Fig.  ). We also demonstrated that the transfer of IFN-γ-deficient ILC1 decreased BW compared with that of WT ILC1 after CLP (Fig.  ). Given that bacterial clearance at 48 h after CLP was comparable between WT and Ifng–/– mice (Supplementary Fig.  ), these combined results suggest that ILC1-derived IFN-γ recruits IL-10-producing CD49d– imNeu from the BM to alleviate the acute systemic inflammation caused by polymicrobial sepsis.

## AHNAK+ imNeu producing IL-10 are recruited in the blood of patients with COVID-19
To examine whether IFN-γ influences CXCR4 expression on human imNeu, we sorted immature (band form nucleus; CD11b+CD49dmidCD66b+CCR3–CD16highCD10–) and mature (segmented form nucleus; CD11b+CD49dmidCD66b+CD16highCCR3–CD16midCD10+) neutrophils from human umbilical cord blood (Supplementary Fig.  ). IFN-γ stimulation downregulated CXCR4 expression in imNeu (band nuclei) but not in mNeu (segmented nuclei) (Fig.  ). The expression of AHNAK mRNA was higher in imNeu than in mNeu (Fig.  ). SMAD7 expression in both imNeu and mNeu was upregulated after the stimulation with IFN-γ compared to naive and vehicle controls, consistent with the results from mouse neutrophils. (Fig.  ). These results suggest that IFN-γ also promotes the recruitment of human imNeu from the BM by downregulating CXCR4 expression.
 To investigate whether AHNAK contributes to the mechanism driving the efflux of BM imNeu in infectious disease, we reanalyzed single-cell RNA-seq datasets of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) from patients with COVID-19. Dimensionality reduction and clustering identified several clusters of immune cell subsets, including lymphoid cells and myeloid cells (Fig.  ). Subsequent analysis highlighted neutrophil, classical monocyte, and CD4+ T cell clusters in patients with COVID-19 (Fig.  ). In particular, patients with COVID-19, but not healthy controls, showed clustering with imNeu (Fig.  ). More importantly, the imNeu subset prominently expressed AHNAK (67.5%) in patients with COVID-19, which was lacking in the mNeu subset (AHNAK: 0.19%) (Fig.  , f). Similarly, IL10 expression was detected in imNeu, but not mNeu, and classical monocytes in patients with COVID-19 (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig  ). Furthermore, in contrast to mNeu in the patients and healthy controls, CXCR4-expressing imNeu were scarcely detected in PBMC of patients with COVID-19 (Fig.  ). These data suggest that, in patients with COVID-19, a subpopulation of imNeu that expresses IL10 is recruited from the BM by regulating CXCR4 expression in an AHNAK-dependent manner.

## Discussion
In the present study, we investigated how imNeu are recruited from the BM into the blood circulation and inflamed tissues during injury and infection and probed the pathophysiological significance of this cell subset. Using mouse models, we showed that ILC1-derived IFN-γ enhanced the migration of imNeu from the BM into inflamed tissues, such as the liver with IRI, and the blood circulation during polymicrobial sepsis. We also showed that these imNeu produce the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, thereby ameliorating tissue inflammation. Our findings have unveiled the ILC1–imNeu axis that protects tissues against acute inflammation due to injury and microbial infection.
We showed that the scaffold protein Ahnak is constitutively expressed in imNeu but not in mNeu or preNeu. The regulatory mechanism for Ahnak expression in neutrophil lineage cells remains unclear. Ahnak is involved in the regulation of cell–cell contact of epithelial , skeletal muscle , smooth muscle , and tumor cells , . In the present study, we revealed that, in response to IFN-γ, Ahnak supports the translocation of Smad7 into the nucleus of imNeu and downregulates their CXCR4 expression, which is critical for the retention of neutrophils in the BM . Although IFN-γ induces Smad7 expression in both imNeu and mNeu, Ahnak is expressed in imNeu only, such that IFN-γ downregulates CXCR4 expression specifically on imNeu, and thus their migration from the BM.
In the present study, we showed that IFN-γ ameliorated liver IRI. IFN-γ potentially can be produced by several cell types in the liver, including NK, NKT, CD4+ and γδT, and dendritic cells, as well as ILC1 . However, although ILC1 accounts for only 0.6% of the immune cells in the liver , we demonstrated that ILC1-derived IFN-γ was responsible for ameliorating hepatic IRI via the recruitment of imNeu into the liver at 5 h after IR. Although both ILC1 and NK cells were activated to produce IFN-γ after liver IR, IFN-γ levels in the ILC1 subset increased nearly 5-fold as early as 1 h after IR, whereas NK cell activation occurred later. This rapid IFN-γ-production response by ILC1 may be critical for recruiting imNeu into the liver to extinguish the as-yet mild inflammation present during the early phase of acute inflammation. Indeed, the number of imNeu in the blood and liver was comparable between WT and Ifng–/– mice at 12 h after IRI.
We also found that the CD49d– imNeu produces IL-10 after their infiltration into the inflamed tissues of liver IRI and the blood circulation during polymicrobial sepsis and COVID-19 infection. IL-10 exhibits protective effects against liver IRI through the upregulation of antioxidant heme oxygenase 1 , , B-cell lymphoma 2 , and B-cell lymphoma-extra large and the downregulation of nuclear factor (NF)-κB , IL-1β , IL-2 , and macrophage inflammatory protein-2 . In addition, with its potent anti-inflammatory functions, IL-10 is considered to be a regulatory cytokine against systemic inflammatory response syndrome and to control the inflammation, microbial infection, organ failure, and mortality associated with septic shock . We showed that neutralization of IL-10 aggravated liver injury in WT, but not Ifng–/–, mice, indicating that IFN-γ-dependent IL-10 production ameliorated liver IRI. However, because IFN-γ regulates the production of chemokines such as CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL10 in liver IRI , , these chemokines might also contribute to immune cell migration and tissue inflammation involved in the pathogenesis of liver IRI. Further studies are required to address this issue.
Given that in vitro stimulation of CD49d– imNeu with LPS amplified Il10 mRNA expression, TLR4 ligands such as damage-associated molecular patterns (e.g., HMGB-1) or pathogen-associated molecular patterns may trigger IL-10 production by CD49d– imNeu during liver IRI and infectious diseases, respectively. However, pre-treatment of a TLR4 selective antagonist TAK-242 did not completely diminish Il10 expression by CD49d– imNeu after stimulation with the supernatant of the homogenate of the liver with IRI. Since HMGB-1 stimulates not only TLR4 but also TLR2, it is likely that IL-10 production was mediated by both signals via TLR4 and TLR2. It may also be possible that stimuli other than TLR4 ligands contained in the liver with IRI might be involved in the IL-10 production from CD49d– imNeu as well.
We demonstrated that imNeu transmigrates into the parenchymal region of the liver from the hepatic artery after IR. imNeu in the BM sufficiently expressed adhesion molecule-related genes, including Itgam, Itgb2, and Itgb2l (GEO: GSE109467, Supplementary Table  ). In addition, IFN-γ treatment upregulates the expression of Selplg, Itgam, Itgal, Itgax, Itga4, Itgb7, Ceacam1, and Sell in maturing neutrophils . Together, these results suggest the possibility that Itgam and Itgal might be involved in the infiltration of imNeu into the inflamed tissue. However, we showed that IFN-γ alone was not sufficient for imNeu recruitment into the liver, but liver IRI was also required. It remains undetermined how liver IRI is involved in the imNeu recruitment. Future studies are required to clarify this issue.
In patients with sepsis, the number of circulating ILC1, but not other lymphocyte subsets, increases during the first 3 days of the disease . However, the pathophysiological role of ILC1 in sepsis has been unclear. IFN-γ restores monocyte function, resulting in bacterial clearance, in patients with sepsis . However, we found that ablating IFN-γ did not reduce the bacterial load in the peritoneal cavity at 48 h after CLP in mice, whereas IFN-γ attenuates the cytokine storm, leading to improved septic outcomes. Therefore, during CLP-induced sepsis in mice, the IFN-γ secreted from ILC1 suppresses acute inflammation specifically by recruiting the CD49d– imNeu subset rather than by enhancing bacterial clearance. However, we did not present formal evidence of the link between ILC-1-derived IFN-γ and imNeu-derived IL-10 in the pathogenesis of CLP. This was one of the limitations of the current study on CLP and should be clarified in future studies.
It has become increasingly clear that imNeu are known to have decreased phagocytic capacity due to the failure of phagosomal alkalinization, which impairs the optimal antimicrobial and digestive functions of serine proteases such as elastase, cathepsin G, and azurocidin . Therefore, the CD49d– imNeu subset could have an anti-inflammatory function but may lack bacterial-killing activity.
Despite its anti-inflammatory functions, IL-10 can exert a paradoxical effect on adaptive immunity after sepsis. In this so-called immune paralysis, immune responses are downregulated through lymphocyte apoptosis, reduced T cell proliferation, and increased Treg cell suppressor function . However, CD49d– imNeu produces IL-10 only during the acute phase after the development of sepsis, ameliorating inflammation.
Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has caused more than 6 million deaths worldwide . Although a marked increase in IL-10 concentrations in severe and critically ill patients is a unique feature of the cytokine storm during SARS-CoV-2 infection, it is controversial whether IL-10 has an anti-inflammatory or pro-inflammatory function and which cell type(s) produces IL-10 after SARS-CoV-2 infection . Although COVID-19 is well associated with systemic expansion and activation of the imNeu subset in the peripheral blood and lung , , the role of imNeu in the pathogenesis of COVID-19 has also been unclear. In the present study, we showed that imNeu were increased in the peripheral blood and a major producer of IL-10. We also showed that AHNAK was specifically expressed in imNeu, but not mNeu, among the PBMC of patients with COVID-19. This gene expression signature is consistent with that of imNeu and mNeu in the BM of mice. We revealed the high frequency of AHNAK+ imNeu in COVID-19 patients. Therefore, AHNAK may promote the efflux of IL-10-producing imNeu from the BM of patients with COVID-19. However, unlike mouse models of liver IRI and microbial sepsis, we could not demonstrate the association of ILC1 with the increased imNeu in patients with COVID-19 because the cluster of ILC1 was not detected in the single-cell RNA-seq datasets. Moreover, the pathophysiological role of IL-10-producing imNeu still remains undetermined in patients with COVID-19. These are important issues to be clarified in the future to better understand the pathology of COVID-19.

## Methods

## Ethical statement for animal experiments and mice
All animal experiments were approved by the University Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Tsukuba (approval no. 21–273, 22–167, 23–128, and 24–083) and conducted at the laboratory animal resource centers of the University of Tsukuba in accordance with the guidelines of the institutional ethics committee. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, at experimental endpoint or when they showed any signatures of the following symptoms: difficulty in feeding and drinking, signs of distress (including self-injury, abnormal posture, respiratory distress, vocalization), prolonged diarrhea, bleeding, contamination of the external genital area, and a drastic loss of body weight (more than 20% decrease).
C57BL/6J and BALB/c mice were purchased from the CLEA Japan Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). Ifng–/– mice (B6.129S7-Ifngtm1Ts/J, strain 002287) , and Rag2–/–Il2rg–/– mice (C;129S4-Rag2tm1.1FlvIl2rgtm1.1Flv/J, strain 014593) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine, USA). Rag2–/– mice (C;129S6-Rag2tm1FwaN12, strain 601) were purchased from Taconic Biosciences (Rensselear, New York, USA). Ahnak-deficient B6. Cg-AhnaktmJ1tak mice (strain RBRC01958) were obtained from RIKEN BRC Inc. (Tsukuba, Japan). Mice between 7 and 14 weeks old were used in the experiments, with a gender-matched approach. All mice were housed and maintained under specific-pathogen-free conditions at an ambient temperature of 23.5 °C ± 2.5 °C on a 12-hour light cycle. The mice were fed an MF diet (MF, Oriental Yeast, Tokyo, Japan). All strains of mice were bred separately.

## Liver ischemia-reperfusion (IR)
Mice were anesthetized by the intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of 0.3 mg/kg medetomidine, 4 mg/kg midazolam, and 5 mg/kg butorphanol in saline. After the midline supraumbilical incision, the liver can be exposed by the retractors. The connective tissue around the portal vein, hepatic artery, and common vile duct was carefully removed. The triad was then clamped, and mice were kept a body temperature at 37 °C on the heat controller. Sixty minutes after ischemia, circulation was restored into the left and median lobes for 1, 3, 5, 6, and 24 h according to each individual study .
To block cytokines signaling, 100 µg of neutralizing mAbs against mouse IFN-γ (clone R4-6A2, Bio X Cell, New Hampshire, USA) or mouse IL-10 (clone JES5-2A5, Bio X Cell, New Hampshire, USA) were injected into mice intraperitoneally before reperfusion.

## Cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)
To induce the systemic inflammatory responses, polymicrobial sepsis was induced by CLP according to the general guidelines . Briefly, female C57BL/6J mice were anesthetized by the inhalation of isoflurane, and a 1 cm incision was made in the abdomen. The cecum was exposed, ligated at the bifurcation by a 4–0 suture, and punctured once with a 22 gauge needle. The abdominal musculature and skin were closed with simple running sutures. Body weight loss and mortality were monitored for 6 days.

## Preparation of lymphocytes in the liver and peritoneal cavity
Ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was injected into the portal vein for perfusion, and the liver tissue was isolated and mechanically ground into a cell suspension by using a tissue grinder (Sanyo, Tokyo, Japan). The liver cells were washed with RPMI-1640 culture medium (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, U.S.A.), followed by PBS, then resuspended with 40% Percoll PLUS density gradient medium (GE Healthcare, Chicago, Illinois, USA), overlaid on 60% Percoll PLUS medium, and centrifuged at 800 x g for 35 min at 21 °C. Buffy coats were collected and washed with RPMI-1640 medium, followed by PBS supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA), and used as liver lymphocytes. Peritoneal lymphocytes were collected after gentle abdominal massage for 1 min with 2 ml PBS. Each lymphocyte was identified as follows; ILC1, NK1.1+TCRβ–B220–DX5–CD200R+ or NK1.1+TCRβ–B220–DX5–CD49a+ lymphocytes; NK cells, NK1.1+TCRβ–B220–DX5+CD200R– or NK1.1+TCRβ–B220–DX5+CD49a– lymphocytes; CD8+ T cells, NK1.1–TCRβ+CD8a+ lymphocytes; CD4+ T cells, NK1.1–TCRβ+CD4+ lymphocytes; NKT cells, NK1.1+TCRβ+ lymphocytes.

## Adoptive transfer of ILC1
ILC1 (NK1.1+TCRβ–B220–DX5–CD200R+) and NK cells (NK1.1+TCRβ–B220–DX5+CD200R–) were sorted from the liver of WT and Ifng–/– mice by using BD FACS Aria (BD Biosciences, San Jose, California, USA). ILC1 (1 ×106) were cultured in 96-well round-bottom plates for 24 h under 5% CO2 and 37 °C in RPMI-1640 culture medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FCS, 2 mM glutamate, 100 U penicillin, 100 µg/ml streptomycin, 10 mM HEPES, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 100 µM MEM non-essential amino acids, and 500 nM 2-mercaptoethanol in the presence of 25 ng/mL rmIL-7 for 24 h. Hundred or 50 thousand ILC1 together with 4 µg rmIL-15 were i.v. injected into male Rag2–/–Il2rg–/– mice 24 h before liver IR or CLP, respectively.

## Preparation of myeloid cells in the liver, blood circulation, and peritoneal cavity
The liver was perfused with ice-cold PBS and incubated in the RPMI-1640 medium containing 500 CDU/mL Clostridium histolyticum-derived collagenase type IV (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, U.S.A.), 150 U/mL DNase I, 10 nmol CaCl2, and 4 nmol MgCl2 at 37 °C for 30 min with gentle shaking. One ml of blood was collected from the heart and diluted in 4 ml PBS (-) in the presence of heparin. The peritoneal cavity was injected with 2 ml PBS, and peritoneal cells were collected after a gentle abdominal massage for 1 min. Neutrophils and inflammatory monocytes in the liver, blood, and peritoneal cavity were identified as CD45.2+Lin–CD11bhighF4/80–Ly6CmidLy6G+ cells and CD45.2+Lin–CD11bhighF4/80–Ly6ChighLy6G– cells, respectively. Kupffer cells and conventional dendritic cells in the liver were identified as CD45.2+Lin–CD11b–/lowF4/80+ cells and CD45.2+CD11b–CD11chigh cells, respectively. Lineage markers included CD3, B220, and NK1.1.

## Effect of IFN-γ on neutrophil influx from the BM in vivo
To investigate the effect of IFN-γ on neutrophil influx from the BM, IFN-γ or vehicle were i.v. injected into Ifng–/– mice. The dosage of IFN-γ injected was calculated to reach the plasma level of IFN-γ at 10 ng/ml using the following formula: total dosage of IFN-γ = body weight x 72 ml/kg (whole blood volume) x 10 ng/ml. BM and whole blood were collected from the femora and heart of Ifng–/– mice, respectively, 24 h after the injection of vehicle or IFN-γ. The injured lobes were collected from Ifng–/– mice at 5 h after liver IR. Red blood cells were lysed by an ammonium-chloride-potassium lysis buffer for 1 min, and imNeu and mNeu in the BM, blood and injured lobes were identified by flow cytometry as follows: imNeu, Lin–CD115–SiglecF–c-Kit–Gr-1lowCD11b+CXCR4–/lowCXCR2– cells and mNeu, Lin–CD115–SiglecF–c-Kit–Gr-1+CD11b+CXCR4–/lowLy6G+CXCR2+ cells, respectively. Lineage markers included CD90.2, B220, MHCII, and NK1.1.

## Effect of IFN-γ on CXCR4 expression on mouse BM and human umbilical cord blood neutrophils in vitro
Pre-neutrophil (preNeu), imNeu and mNeu were sorted from the BM of WT, Ifng–/–, and Ahnak–/– mice by flow cytometry according to the following gating strategies: preNeu, Lin–CD115–SiglecF–c-KitmidGr-1+CD11b+CXCR4high cells; imNeu, Lin–CD115–SiglecF–c-Kit–Gr-1lowCD11b+CXCR4–/lowCXCR2– cells; mNeu, Lin–CD115–SiglecF–c-Kit–Gr-1+CD11b+CXCR4–/lowLy6G+CXCR2+ cells. Each neutrophil subsets were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium in the presence or absence of IFN-γ for 5 or 20 h and analyzed for CXCR4 expression by flow cytometry. BM imNeu or mNeu (106 cells) were also transfected with 30 pmol Smad7 siRNA or its scrambled siRNA by using lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, U.S.A.) for 20 h according to the manufacturer’s instruction with slight modification, stimulated with IFN-γ for 5 h, and then analyzed for CXCR4 expression by flow cytometry. Lineage markers included CD90.2, B220, MHCII, and NK1.1.
Human umbilical cord blood was overlayed onto PolymorphPrep (Serumwerk bernburg, Bernburg, Germany) and was centrifuged at 450 x g for 35 min at 21 °C with a gentle break. In both mononuclear and polymorphonuclear cells were harvested and washed by RPMI-1640 medium twice. imNeu and mNeu were sorted from mononuclear and polymorphonuclear fragments by flow cytometry according to the following gating strategies: imNeu, CD11b+CCR3–CD49d+CD66b+CD16lowCD16+CD10– cells; mNeu, CD11b+CCR3–CD66b+CD16highCD10+ cells. Each neutrophil subsets were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium in the presence or absence of 100 ng/ml IFN-γ for 24 h, and CXCR4, AHNAK, and SMAD7 mRNA expression was analyzed by qPCR.

## Nuclear morphology of neutrophils
CD49d– imNeu and CD49d+ mNeu were sorted from the liver at 6 h after IRI, and fixed on the silane-coated slides (Muto Pure Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan) after cytospin at 600 rpm for 5 min by using Cytospin 3 (Shandon, Cheshire, UK). Each sample was fixed in 100% methanol for 1 min followed by HE staining. Neutrophils with a minimum diameter less than or greater than one-third of the maximum diameter of the nucleus were identified as mNeu and imNeu, respectively. After sorting, the purity of imNeu and mNeu was analyzed by counting 300 cells/area in randomized 5 images under the microscope.

## Cytokine assay
Blood was collected in the presence of heparin 6 and 48 h after IR and CLP, respectively. Plasma samples were prepared by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Peritoneal fluid was collected from the peritoneal cavity after injection with 1 ml PBS and gentle massage 30 times and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12p70, and TNF in the plasma and peritoneal fluid were quantified using BD Cytometric Bead Array Mouse Flex Sets (BD Bioscience). IFN-γ was measured using Milliplex assay kits (Sigma-Aldrich) (detection range; 1.1-10,000 pg/m) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Immunoblotting
For immunoblotting of IL-10 in the liver, the liver from naive mice or 6 h after IR was lysed with 1% nonidet P-40 supplemented with 20 U/ml aprotinin and protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). After SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), separated proteins were transferred onto the polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes by electroblotting, incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and immunoblotted with rat anti-mouse IL-10 (clone: JES052A5, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, U.S.A.) or rabbit anti-mouse GAPDH (clone: 14C10, Cell Signaling Technology, Massachusetts, U.S.A.). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rat IgG was then incubated for 20 min with gentle shaking, and HRP was detected by ECL western blotting substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, U.S.A.). For immunoblotting of Ahnak and Smad7, the cytoplasmic protein was extracted from BM neutrophil, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membrane was incubated with 5% rabbit non-immunized serum or 5% BSA, and the immunoblotted with 0.5 µg/ml rabbit anti-mouse Ahnak or 1 µg/ml anti-mouse Smad7 antibodies. The membrane was then incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit polymer IgG (Perkin Elmer, Massachusetts, U.S.A.) or HRP-conjugated sheep anti-mouse IgG (Cytiva, Amersham, U.K.) for 30 min at RT, and HRP was detected by ECL western blotting substrate. The PVDF membranes were stripped with Retore Western Blot Stripping Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, U.S.A.) for 20 min at RT, incubated with 3% BSA for 1 h, and reblotted with anti-GAPDH mAb (clone: 14C10, Cell Signaling Technology, Massachusetts, U.S.A.) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG. HRP was detected by the ECL western blotting substrate.

## Far-western blotting
To assess the direct binding of Ahnak and Smad7 proteins, 5 x 106 cells of imNeu and mNeu were sorted from the BM of naive mice and lysed in the cytoplasmic protein extraction reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, U.S.A.), followed by IFN-γ stimulation for 20 h in vitro. Separated proteins by SDS-PAGE were transferred onto the PVDF membranes by electroblotting and refolded using 6 M guanidine hydrochloride buffer supplemented with 20 mM Tris, 1 mM dithiothreitol, 1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 0.1% Tween 20, and 0.1 M sodium chloride for 30 min with gentle shaking. Endogenous Ahnak protein were then bound with refolded prey protein overnight at 4 °C, followed by the blocking of non-specific binding sites. One µg/ml anti-mouse Smad7 mAb was immunoblotted, and HRP-conjugated donkey anti-rabbit IgG was incubated for 30 min at RT. HRP was detected by ECL western blotting substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, U.S.A.). The PVDF membranes were stripped for 15 min at 57 °C and reblotted to detect Smad7 and GAPDH.

## Histology
Injured left and median lobes were collected from naive and subsequent 6 h after hepatic IR and fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde. Paraffin-embedded cross sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or eosin Y alone. To examine the necrotic area, congestion, vacuolization, and necrosis were graded as 0 (no-symptom), 1 (minimal), 2 (0–30%), 3 (31–60%), and 4 (60% over) for a sign and symptom of acute liver injury . The parenchymal area of the liver where red blood cells could be detected was defined as the hemorrhagic area. Briefly, red blood cells were specifically detected in deep red by eosin Y staining. We determined the threshold of pixel intensity that specifically detects red blood cells in each image after the gray scaling, binarization, and trimming of the parenchymal region of the liver specimens and calculated the total area of red blood cells in each liver section as hemorrhage area by image analysis using Image J software.

## cDNA synthesis and qPCR
cDNA was synthesized and total RNA was extracted by using Isogen reagent (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan). cDNA was synthesized by using a high-capacity RNA-to-cDNA kit (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA). Expression of genes was measured through quantitative real-time PCR analysis by using SYBR Green master mix (Applied Biosystems) and appropriate primers. Primer sequences of the target genes are as follows: mouse Ifng, forward, 5’- ACA GCA AGG CGA AAA AGG ATG -3’, reverse, 5’- TGG TGG ACC ACT CGG ATG A -3’; mouse Il10, forward, 5’- GCT GGA CAA CAT ACT GCT AAC C -3’, reverse, 5’- ATT TCC GAT AAG GCT TGG CAA -3’; mouse Fpr1, forward, 5’- CCA TTT GGT TGG TTC ATG TGC -3’, reverse, 5’- CTT CTT GGC TAG GCT CAC AGT -3’; mouse Ltb4r1, forward, 5’- GAT GCA GAA ACG CAC GGT C -3’, reverse, 5’- ACT GCC AGT GAT CGG TCC A -3’; mouse Ifngr1, forward, 5’- GTG GAG CTT TGA CGA GCA CT -3’, reverse, 5’- ATT CCC AGC ATA CGA CAG GGT -3’; mouse Actb, forward, 5’- ACT GTC GAG TCG CGT CCA -3’, reverse, 5’- GCA GCG ATA TCG TCA TCC AT -3’; human CXCR4, forward, 5’- GGG CAA TGG ATT GGT CAT CCT-3’, reverse, 5’- TGC AGC CTG TAC TTG TCC G -3’; human SMAD7, forward, 5’-TTC CTC CGC TGA AAC AGG G-3’, reverse, 5’-CCT CCC AGT ATG CCA CCA C-3’; human AHNAK, forward, 5’-TAC CCT TCC TAA GGC TGA CAT T-3’, reverse, 5’-TTG GAC CCT TGA GTT TTG CAT-3’; human GAPDH, forward, 5’- CTT CAC CAC CAT GGA GAA GGC -3’, reverse, 5’- GGC ATG GAC TGT GGT CAT GAG -3’. The thermal cycling conditions comprised an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 50 cycles at 95 °C for 15 sec and 60 °C for 1 min.

## Chemotaxis assay
For chemotaxis assay, we used a QCM chemotaxis 96-well cell migration assay kit (Merck, New Jersey, U.S.A.). Briefly, RPMI-1640 medium containing 100 ng/ml CXCL1 was poured into the 96-well feeder tray. Four hundred thousand imNeu sorted from the BM were resuspended in 100 µl of RPMI-1640 medium containing 1, 10, or 100 ng/ml IFN-γ in the presence of 10 ng/ml CXCL12, placed onto the migration chamber with 3 µm of microporous membrane, and incubated for 20 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2, and then removed from the plate assembly, followed by flipping out to discard cell suspension. A new feeder trey containing 150 µl of cell detachment solution was inserted into the trey, and cells were removed completely from the underside of the migration chamber by incubation for 30 min at 37 °C with gentle shaking and tilting several times and then lysed with CyQuant GR Dye-contained lysis buffer. The lysates were analyzed for fluorescence using a 480/520 nm filter set according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Bacterial colony formation unit
For the determination of bacterial burden in the peritoneal cavity, mice were euthanized at 48 h after CLP. Peritoneal fluid dilutions were performed, and the samples were seeded on tryptic soy agar plates. The colony numbers were counted 24 h after incubation at 37 °C.

## Reanalysis of single-cell RNA-Seq of PBMC in patients with COVID-19
Expression of AHNAK, SMAD7, CXCR4, IL10 and other neutrophil signature genes in the PBMC of patients with COVID-19 or healthy volunteers was investigated by re-analysis of publicly deposited data. Public single-cell RNA-seq databases of PBMC in patients with COVID-19 are available on NCBI Expression Omnibus (GEO) and European Genome-phenome Archive, including GSE150728, GEO155673, GSE149689, and EGAS00001004571 (or Schulte-Schrepping_2020_COVID19_10x_PBMC under FastGenomic) . Original and processed data can be downloaded on Mondeley Data (http://cellxgene.cziscience.com/collections/b9fc3d70-5a72-4479-a046-c2cc1ab19efc).

## Statistics
Statistical analyses were performed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD test or unpaired student t test. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All results are presented as the mean ± SD of at least two independent experiments.
