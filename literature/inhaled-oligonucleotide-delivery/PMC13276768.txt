## Summary
Sleeve gastrectomy (SG) was used to probe the gut–brain axis in Alzheimer’s disease (AD). In APP/PS1/Tau triple transgenic mice, SG improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity without lasting effects on body weight or food intake. SG significantly improved cognitive performance and reduced anxiety-like behavior, attenuated neuronal damage and pTau accumulation in the hippocampal CA3 region, and increased both peripheral levels and hippocampal immunoreactivity of GIP and GLP-1 along with ERK/CREB/BDNF pathway activity. In HT22 cells, GIP receptor knockdown exacerbated Aβ-induced pTau and suppressed this pathway, whereas GLP-1 acted synergistically. These findings indicate that SG could improve cognition in non-obese AD mice largely through GIP/GLP-1 signaling rather than metabolic changes, supporting dual incretin targeting as a therapeutic strategy for AD.

## Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by cognitive dysfunction and behavioral impairment. Its characteristic pathological changes include the deposition of β-amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles in the central nervous system. , 
Compared with the relatively less studied role of glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), the neuroprotective effects of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) signaling in Alzheimer’s disease have been more extensively investigated. , , Previous preclinical studies have shown that GLP-1 receptor agonists, including liraglutide and lixisenatide, can improve learning and memory, enhance synaptic plasticity, and reduce amyloid-related pathology and neuroinflammation in AD mouse models. , , Our group previously demonstrated that Roux-en-Y gastric bypass ameliorated cognitive impairment and neuropathological changes in APP/PS1/Tau triple transgenic mice, accompanied by activation of the peripheral GLP-1-hippocampal sodium-glucose cotransporter 1 (SGLT1) pathway. These findings suggest that GLP-1-related gut-brain signaling may be an important mediator of the neuroprotective effects of metabolic surgery and provide a rationale for examining whether another incretin, GIP, exerts similar or complementary effects in AD.
Although often overlooked in comparison to other incretins, the importance of GIP is increasingly being recognized. In various animal studies, GIP has been shown to reduce β-amyloid plaque deposition, alleviate hippocampal inflammation, and improve cognitive function. , Furthermore, GIP analogs exert neuroprotective effects by increasing central BDNF levels. 
Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which is mainly produced by neurons and glial cells in the brain, is widely expressed throughout the central nervous system and supports neuronal growth, differentiation, and synaptogenesis. , BDNF is closely associated with AD, with research indicating decreased BDNF levels in the brains and serum of AD patients, correlating with cognitive impairments. Furthermore, epidemiological evidence suggests that higher serum BDNF levels are associated with a lower risk of developing AD. 
However, the specific mechanisms by which GIP upregulates BDNF expression remain insufficiently investigated. Sleeve gastrectomy (SG), favored due to its procedural simplicity and low rate of complications, has become the predominant bariatric procedure. , Meta-analyses indicate a consistent elevation in postprandial GLP-1 levels following SG, although changes in GIP levels post-surgery remain inconsistent and are subject to ongoing debate. , Previous studies also suggest that SG may improve cognitive function in obese patients and mitigate cognitive decline in diabetic rats. , Given that SG may stimulate the secretion of various insulin-promoting hormones, including GLP-1 and GIP, we attempted to explore whether there is a synergic effect of GIP and GLP-1 on cognition and whether this effect is independent of metabolic improvement.

## Results

## Sleeve gastrectomy improves glucose homeostasis without altering long-term body weight, intake, or fasting glucose
To characterize the systemic metabolic effects of SG, we monitored body weight, food intake, and key glucoregulatory parameters. SG induced a transient reduction in body weight and food intake in both AD and WT mice. Weight loss was most pronounced at postoperative day 7 in ADSG and WTSG groups compared to their sham controls (F(3,32) = 10.04, p = 0.04, p = 0.05; F(3,40) = 5.55, p = 0.008, p = 0.0006). By postoperative day 30, however, body weight in the ADSG group had recovered to levels statistically indistinguishable from the ADSHAM group (p > 0.05) ( A and 1B). Food intake followed a similar pattern of acute reduction and subsequent recovery. This indicates that the acute hypophagic and weight-reducing effects of SG had largely subsided by the time of long-term cognitive assessment. 
Assessment of glucose homeostasis revealed substantial and lasting improvements following SG. On postoperative day 30, fasting blood glucose levels in the ADSG group showed no significant difference compared to either preoperative levels or the contemporaneous ADSHAM group (p > 0.05). In the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) performed at postoperative day 30, ADSG mice exhibited a significantly lower area under the curve (AUC) compared to ADSHAM mice (F(3,24) = 1.11, p = 0.03), indicating markedly improved glucose tolerance. A similar improvement was also observed in WTSG mice compared with WTSHAM controls (F(3,24) = 1.11, p = 0.02) ( C and 1D). Consistent with this, fasting insulin measurements and HOMA-IR calculations showed that at week 4 after surgery, insulin levels and the HOMA-IR index were significantly lower in the ADSG group than in the ADSHAM group (F(3,32) = 15.25, p = 0.002; F(3,32) = 12.46, p = 0.009) and were also lower than the preoperative baseline values (F(3,32) = 15.25, p = 0.0001; F(3,32) = 12.46, p = 0.0001) ( E and 1F). Together, these findings indicate that SG partially improved insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance, but did not change the long-term body weight, food intake, and fasting blood glucose of normal-weight mice.

## Sleeve gastrectomy improves cognitive function, motor abilities, and anxiety levels in AD mice
The Morris water maze and Y maze were employed to assess cognitive abilities and motor function in mice. In the Morris water maze experiment, the escape latency of mice in the ADSHAM group was significantly greater than that of the WTSHAM group (F(4,4) = 2.87, p = 0.03), while the escape latency of mice in the ADSG group was significantly reduced compared to the ADSHAM group (F(4,4) = 3.15, p = 0.04). Comparison of the number of platform crossings among the four groups revealed that the ADSHAM group had significantly fewer crossings than the WTSHAM group (F(5,5) = 4.81, p = 0.009), and AD mice showed a significant increase in platform crossings after SG (F(5,5) = 3.44, p = 0.04) ( A–2D). Similar beneficial results were obtained in the Y maze experiment. Compared to the WTSHAM group, the ADSHAM mice exhibited a significant decrease in the number and time spent exploring the novel arm (F(5,5) = 1.36, p = 0.001, F(5,5) = 1.70, p = 0.004), while the ADSG group showed a significant increase in these parameters compared to the ADSHAM group (F(5,5) = 1.20, p = 0.002, F(5,5) = 1.21, p = 0.001) ( E and 2F). These findings suggest that SG can improve cognitive and spatial exploration abilities in AD mice. 
The open field test was primarily used to evaluate the motor abilities and anxiety levels in AD mice. In comparison to the central zone movement time, the ADSHAM group exhibited a significant reduction in central zone movement time compared to the WTSHAM group (F(5,5) = 1.86, p = 0.0002). The time spent immobile in the open field was significantly increased in the ADSHAM group compared to the WTSHAM group (F(5,5) = 3.59, p = 0.0001). However, after SG, the ADSG group showed a significant reduction in immobility time compared to the ADSHAM group (F(5,5) = 3.02, p = 0.009) ( G and 2H). These results indicate that SG improves motor abilities and reduces anxiety levels in AD mice.

## Sleeve gastrectomy improves CA3 neuronal injury in the hippocampus of AD mice
We assessed hippocampal neuronal apoptosis and injury through Nissl staining. We evaluated the neuronal status of multiple subregions of the hippocampus, among which the neuronal loss in the CA3 region was the most significant in the AD model, and the improvement effect of SG was most obvious in this region. Results showed that, compared to the WT sham surgery group, mice in the AD sham surgery group exhibited loss of CA3 hippocampal neurons, sparse arrangement, and a reduction in Nissl bodies (F(2,2) = 2.71, p < 0.0001). The ADSG group showed an improvement in the loss of hippocampal neurons and Nissl bodies compared to the ADSHAM group (F(2,2) = 3.95, p = 0.0006) ( A–3C). 

## Sleeve gastrectomy ameliorates excessive pTau protein expression in the hippocampus of AD mice
Results from western blotting for pTau protein demonstrated that, compared to the WT sham surgery group, mice in the AD sham surgery group exhibited a significant increase in hippocampal pTau protein levels (F(2,2) = 8.34, p = 0.001). The ADSG group showed a significant reduction in pTau protein compared to the ADSHAM group (F(2,2) = 3.37, p = 0.003) ( B–3D). This suggests that SG can improve pathological changes in the hippocampus of AD mice.

## Sleeve gastrectomy increases peripheral serum and hippocampal GIP and GLP-1 levels in AD mice
To investigate the impact of SG on the secretion of GIP and GLP-1 in the mouse small intestine, we employed ELISA to assess serum GIP and GLP-1 levels in the four groups of mice. The results indicated that serum levels of GIP and GLP-1 in AD mice were lower than those in WT mice (F(4,4) = 7.18, p = 0.001, F(4,4) = 1.66, p = 0.01), and mice in the ADSG group showed an elevation in serum GIP and GLP-1 levels compared to the sham surgery group (F(4,4) = 3.20, p = 0.02, F(4,4) = 1.30, p = 0.03) ( D and 4E). Previous studies have suggested that GIP and GLP-1, secreted by the small intestine, can cross the blood-brain barrier to affect the central nervous system. Immunohistochemistry was employed to assess hippocampal GIP/GLP-1 levels in the four groups of mice, revealing a similar trend to peripheral GIP and GLP-1. Hippocampal levels of GIP and GLP-1 in AD mice were lower than those in WT mice (F(2,2) = 9.29, p = 0.006, F(2,2) = 1.47, p = 0.002), and after SG, hippocampal GIP/GLP-1 levels in AD mice increased (F(2,2) = 6.52, p = 0.02, F(2,2) = 1.33, p = 0.02) ( A–4C). These findings suggest that SG is associated with increased circulating and hippocampal GIP/GLP-1 levels in AD mice, which may contribute to central neuroprotective signaling. 

## Sleeve gastrectomy improves expression of the ERK/CREB/BDNF signaling pathway in the hippocampal tissue of AD mice
To further investigate the mechanisms of GIP/GLP-1 action in the central nervous system, we employed western blotting to assess the expression levels of the ERK/CREB/BDNF signaling pathway in the four groups of mice. This analysis aimed to determine whether these pathways are involved in the cognitive improvement and neuroprotective effects of SG in AD mice. Results indicated that, compared to the WT sham surgery group, mice in the AD sham surgery group exhibited a decrease in the expression levels of pERK/ERK, pCREB/CREB, and BDNF in the hippocampal tissue (F(2,2) = 1.24, p = 0.02, F(2,2) = 6.13, p = 0.002, F(2,2) = 6.77, p = 0.04). Following SG, the AD mice in the SG group (ADSG) demonstrated a significant increase in the expression of pERK/ERK, pCREB/CREB, and BDNF (F(2,2) = 3.37, p = 0.03, F(2,2) = 9.07, p = 0.003, F(2,2) = 1.08, p = 0.006) ( A–5D), suggesting that SG can improve cognitive abilities and neuronal damage in AD mice by activating the ERK/CREB/BDNF signaling pathway. 

## GIP and GLP-1 attenuate neurodegeneration through the ERK/CREB/BDNF signaling pathway in HT22 cells
Subsequently, an in vitro model of Alzheimer’s disease was established using the HT22 cell line. Given the prior confirmation within our research group that the central GLP-1-SGLT1 pathway can ameliorate pathological changes and glucose metabolism in Alzheimer’s disease mice, and considering the lack of relevant studies on the specific mechanism through which GIP and the combination of GIP and GLP-1 affect BDNF secretion, our focus shifted to the investigation of GIP and the combination of GIP and GLP-1. We explored whether GIP acts on the ERK/CREB/BDNF pathway. Results indicated that, compared to the NC group, HT22 cells in the Aβ group exhibited increased expression of pTau protein (F(2,2) = 1.50, p = 0.01) and decreased expression of pERK/ERK, pCREB/CREB, and pTrkB/TrkB (F(2,2) = 1.11, p = 0.04, F(2,2) = 1.42, p = 0.0004, F(2,2) = 2.83, p = 0.01). In comparison to the Aβ group, the Aβ+GIP group showed a decrease in pTau (F(2,2) = 1.26, p = 0.04) and an increase in pERK/ERK, pCREB/CREB, and pTrkB/TrkB expression (F(2,2) = 2.51, p = 0.02, F(2,2) = 2.24, p = 0.005, F(2,2) = 51.95, p = 0.02). Following the silencing of GIPR receptor expression, the Aβ+GIP+siGIPR group exhibited an increase in pTau (F(2,2) = 1.50, p = 0.01) and a decrease in pERK/ERK, pCREB/CREB, and pTrkB/TrkB expression (F(2,2) = 5.07, p = 0.03, F(2,2) = 1.49, p = 0.009, F(2,2) = 14.49, p = 0.002) ( A–6F). The siGIPR sequence used for GIPR knockdown is listed in . 
We also explored whether GIP and GLP-1 act synergistically on the ERK/CREB/BDNF pathway. Results indicated that, compared to the NC group, HT22 cells in the Aβ group exhibited increased expression of pTau protein (F(2,2) = 4.47, p = 0.01) and decreased expression of pERK/ERK, pCREB/CREB, and pTrkB/TrkB (F(2,2) = 3.17, p = 0.001, F(2,2) = 12.1, p = 0.03, F(2,2) = 1.16, p = 0.001). In comparison to the Aβ group, the Aβ+GIP group showed a decrease in pTau (F(2,2) = 1.45, p = 0.03) and an increase in pERK/ERK, pCREB/CREB, and pTrkB/TrkB expression (F(2,2) = 2.22, p = 0.001, F(2,2) = 3.76, p = 0.0003, F(2,2) = 1.32, p = 0.03). Similar to the previous group, the Aβ+GLP-1 group showed a decrease in pTau (F(2,2) = 2.06, p = 0.01) and an increase in pERK/ERK, pCREB/CREB, and pTrkB/TrkB expression (F(2,2) = 12.89, p = 0.0006, F(2,2) = 1.51, p = 0.003, F(2,2) = 14.5, p = 0.008). Following concomitant GIP and GLP-1 administration, the Aβ+GIP+ GLP-1 group exhibited a significant decrease in pTau (F(2,2) = 1.89, p = 0.01) and an obvious increase in pERK/ERK, pCREB/CREB, and pTrkB/TrkB expression (F(2,2) = 15.67, p = 0.01, F(2,2) = 3.03, p < 0.0001, F(2,2) = 1.70, p = 0.03) ( A–7G). These findings suggest that GIP and GLP-1 synergistically reduce pTau protein levels in HT22 cells by activating the ERK/CREB/BDNF signaling pathway, thereby exerting protective effects in an in vitro AD model. 

## Discussion
In this study, we demonstrated that SG improves cognitive function, reduces anxiety-like behaviors, and mitigates neuronal damage and pTau pathology in AD mice. Notably, these cognitive and neuroprotective benefits were achieved without sustained alterations in body weight, food intake, or fasting glucose, and only mild improvements in glucose homeostasis were observed in this non-obese AD model. Mechanistically, SG elevated both peripheral levels and hippocampal immunoreactivity of GIP and GLP-1, with a particular focus on the less-characterized incretin GIP. We further identified that GIP, acting synergistically with GLP-1, activates the hippocampal ERK/CREB/BDNF pathway, thereby suppressing Tau phosphorylation and supporting neuronal integrity. Crucially, in vitro knockdown of GIPR abolished these protective effects, supporting the involvement of GIP signaling in vitro. Collectively, these findings reveal a GIP-mediated neuroprotective pathway that operates independently of major metabolic changes and highlights the therapeutic potential of GIP/GLP-1 co-activation in AD.
SG, a widely adopted bariatric procedure, has been extensively studied for its metabolic benefits, including improvements in blood glucose, lipid profiles, and body weight. , In the present study, we investigated its potential effects on cognitive function in a mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease. APP/PS1/Tau transgenic mice develop beta-amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles formed by abnormal aggregation of Tau proteins between 4 and 6 months, exhibiting a decline in cognitive and memory abilities. Our results demonstrate that SG ameliorates this cognitive impairment in AD mice. Consistent with previous reports by Smith et al. and Ding et al., which documented cognitive improvement following the procedure, our findings further reveal a reduction in neuronal damage within the hippocampal CA3 region—an area critical for spatial memory—along with attenuated pTau protein overexpression. The CA3 region’s neuronal activity is associated with spatial memory. These outcomes enhance our understanding of the mechanisms through which SG improves cognitive and spatial exploration abilities in AD mice.
Weight loss surgery is known to alter endocrine and metabolic functions. Following SG, accelerated gastric emptying and enhanced intestinal transit are commonly observed. This increased stimulation of the distal gut promotes secretion of GLP-1 from L cells, and elevated postprandial GLP-1 levels have been consistently documented in clinical studies after SG. GLP-1 receptor agonists have been widely shown to exert neuroprotective effects in neurodegenerative models, potentially through pathways including the enhancement of neuronal survival, reduction of amyloid burden, and modulation of insulin signaling. Our previous studies also found that GLP-1 mediates its central effect through the GLP-1-SGLT1 pathway, thereby improving the cognitive function of AD mice. 
In contrast to GLP-1, the role of GIP in cognition remains less defined and evidence is relatively limited. Although some studies suggest that GIP may influence brain function, , the specific central signaling mechanisms are not well characterized. In our study, SG led to elevated levels of both GIP and GLP-1 in the peripheral circulation and hippocampal tissue of AD mice. Notably, we identified that GIP activates the hippocampal ERK/CREB/BDNF pathway—a key cascade involved in synaptic plasticity and neuronal survival. Using an in vitro AD model, we further confirmed that GIP suppresses phosphorylated Tau expression via GIPR and that knockdown of GIPR abolishes this protective effect. These results provide mechanistic evidence supporting a direct neuroprotective role of GIP in AD, largely mediated through the ERK/CREB/BDNF axis.
Notably, our in vitro experiments revealed that co-administration of GIP and GLP-1 produced a more pronounced activation of the ERK/CREB/BDNF pathway, indicating a synergistic interaction between these two incretins. This finding aligns with previous reports that dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists exert stronger neurotrophic effects compared to GLP-1 mono-agonists in models of Parkinson’s disease. Thus, beyond their individual effects, GIP and GLP-1 appear to cooperate to enhance neuroprotective signaling, suggesting a potential therapeutic advantage of dual-receptor targeting in AD.
In parallel with endocrine changes, we examined the systemic metabolic effects of SG. While the surgery induced transient reductions in body weight and food intake—both of which recovered by the time of cognitive testing—only mild and sustained improvements in glucose homeostasis were observed. Importantly, the cognitive and neuropathological benefits occurred despite the absence of persistent changes in body weight, food intake, or fasting glucose. This temporal and quantitative dissociation indicates that the cognitive improvements mediated by GIP/GLP-1 are largely independent of major metabolic alterations.
In summary, our study suggests that SG improves cognitive function and neuropathology in AD mice, potentially through incretin-mediated activation of the hippocampal ERK/CREB/BDNF pathway. We provide evidence for a synergistic neuroprotective role of GIP alongside GLP-1, and importantly, show that these benefits occur independently of sustained metabolic changes. These findings not only advance our understanding of the gut-brain axis in AD but also support the therapeutic potential of GIP-based or dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists as metabolic-neuroprotective strategies for Alzheimer’s disease.
In conclusion, our findings suggest that SG ameliorates cognitive impairment and neuronal pathology in AD mice, independent of sustained changes in body weight or food intake. The neuroprotective effect may be mediated through the upregulation of GIP and subsequent activation of the hippocampal ERK/CREB/BDNF signaling pathway. Furthermore, GIP and GLP-1 act synergistically to enhance neurotrophic signaling, revealing a potential neuroprotective role for GIP beyond its metabolic functions and supporting the therapeutic targeting of the GIP pathway in AD.

## Limitations of the study
This study also has certain limitations. First, our in vivo mouse data show association rather than necessity. Although the HT22 siGIPR experiments support biological plausibility for GIP involvement, in vivo receptor blockade or genetic deletion studies will be required to determine whether GIP signaling is necessary for the cognitive benefit of SG. Second, our analysis focused primarily on the hippocampus, where GIP-related effects were most pronounced. While this builds on our previous work, the prefrontal cortex was not examined. This represents an important limitation and a key direction for our ongoing investigations. Third, the immunohistochemical findings are supportive rather than definitive because peptide-blocking or knockout-based validation was not performed in hippocampal tissue. Fourth, the small sample size for some exploratory endpoints is acknowledged as a limitation of this study.

## Experimental model and study participant details

## Animals
Male APP/PS1/Tau transgenic mice and male C57BL/6J wild-type mice were used in this study. APP/PS1/Tau mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA), and wild-type C57BL/6J mice were obtained from the Animal Experimental Center of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University. Mice were 6 months old at the time of surgery and were maintained at 23 ± 2°C under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Mice were group-housed at four mice per cage, except during food intake measurements, when they were individually housed for 24 h.
At 6 months of age, male APP/PS1/Tau transgenic mice and wild-type mice were randomly assigned to four groups: AD SG (ADSG), AD sham surgery (ADSHAM), WT SG (WTSG), and WT sham surgery (WTSHAM). Following surgery, mice received water and milk powder for 7 days and were then returned to a normal diet. Only male mice were included in this study. Therefore, sex-dependent differences were not evaluated, and caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to female animals.
All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University under protocol number WDRM动(福)第 20230904E 号 and were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

## Cell line
The mouse hippocampal neuronal cell line HT22 was obtained from Wuhan University People’s Hospital. The HT22 cell line was not additionally authenticated in the current study. Cells were not tested for mycoplasma contamination in the current study.

## Method details

## Sleeve gastrectomy surgery
At the age of 6 months, mice were fasted for 12 h before surgery and randomly assigned to undergo sleeve gastrectomy (SG) or sham surgery. Anesthesia was induced and maintained by inhalation of isoflurane (induction 3–5%, maintenance 1.5–2%, in 100% oxygen) using a vaporizer and a nose cone for small rodents. During surgery, animals were placed on a heated pad, and the abdominal skin was sterilized with povidone-iodine.
For SG, a midline laparotomy was performed. Approximately 80% of the gastric lumen along the lesser curvature was occluded using medical-grade titanium clips, and approximately 80% of the stomach, including the gastric fundus, was resected. The resection line was positioned approximately 0.5–1 cm from the right side of the titanium clip. Interrupted sutures with 5-0 absorbable threads were used to secure and fix the titanium clip in place. Sham-operated mice underwent laparotomy and gentle manipulation of the stomach along the lesser curvature without gastric resection. After surgery, mice received meloxicam (2–5 mg/kg, SC, once daily for 48 h) and were monitored daily for recovery, hydration, wound healing, and food intake. Mice were maintained on a water and milk powder diet for 7 days and were then returned to a normal diet. No perioperative deaths or exclusions occurred.

## Metabolic measurements
Body weight was measured before surgery and at the indicated postoperative time points. Food intake was assessed before surgery and at postoperative weeks 1, 2, 3, and 4. During food intake measurement, mice were individually housed, and 24 h food consumption was determined from 8:00 a.m. to 8:00 a.m. the next day.
Fasting blood glucose was measured before surgery and at postoperative weeks 1, 2, and 4 after a 6–8 h fast using tail-vein blood and a portable glucometer. Oral glucose tolerance tests were performed before surgery and at postoperative weeks 1 and 4 after a 6–8 h fast. Baseline blood glucose was measured, and mice were then administered a 50% glucose solution by oral gavage at 2 mL/kg body weight. Blood glucose levels were recorded at 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min after glucose administration via tail vein sampling using a glucometer.
Serum insulin levels were measured before surgery and at postoperative weeks 1, 2, and 4. After fasting, approximately 1 mL of blood was collected from the retro-orbital venous plexus. Blood samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 2 h and centrifuged to obtain serum. Serum insulin concentration was measured using an Insulin ELISA Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. HOMA-IR index was calculated as follows: HOMA-IR = (Fasting Blood Glucose [mmol/L] × Fasting Insulin [mU/L])/22.5.

## Behavioral testing
Behavioral testing was performed 2 months after surgery. The order of testing was Y-maze, open field, and Morris water maze. Apparatuses were cleaned with 95% ethanol between animals. Investigators were blinded to group allocation during behavioral testing and data analysis.
For the Y-maze test, the apparatus consisted of three symmetrical opaque arms, each measuring 30 cm in length, 5 cm in width, and 10 cm in height. Each mouse was placed in the maze and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. ANY-maze software was used to record the movement trajectories, the number of entries into each arm, and the time spent in each arm. The novel arm entry ratio was calculated as the number of entries into the novel arm divided by the total number of entries into all three arms, and the novel arm time ratio was calculated as the time spent in the novel arm divided by the total test time.
For the open field test, mice were placed individually in the center of a square arena whose floor was divided into 16 squares. The central four squares were defined as the center zone and the remaining as the peripheral zone. ANY-maze software was used to record the locomotor trajectories of mice and the time spent in the center zone during the open field test.
For the Morris water maze, mice were tested in a circular pool filled with white opaque liquid maintained at 22°C–24°C. The pool was divided into four quadrants, and a circular hidden platform (10 cm in diameter) was placed in the center of the target quadrant, submerged 1 cm below the water surface. The experiment consisted of 5 days of place navigation training followed by a probe trial on day 6. The mice’s movement trajectories, escape latency during the training phase and the frequency of platform crossings during the probe test were recorded and analyzed using ANY-maze.

## Nissl staining
After the completion of behavioral experiments, mouse brain tissues were collected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded sections were subjected to deparaffinization and then stained with toluidine for 1–2 min. Following staining, the sections were differentiated in acetic acid to enhance contrast, dehydrated, and then cleared in xylene. Finally, the sections were mounted with neutral resin. Under a microscope, hippocampal neurons were observed, and cell counting was performed using ImageJ software.

## Immunohistochemical staining for GIP and GLP-1
Paraffin-embedded brain sections were cut at 5 μm, deparaffinized, and rehydrated through graded ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95°C–100°C for 20 min, followed by cooling to room temperature. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H2O2, and sections were blocked with 10% goat serum for 30 min at room temperature. Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against GIP and GLP-1 at the following dilutions: anti-GIP, 1:100; anti-GLP-1, 1:100. After washing, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature, developed with DAB, counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and coverslipped. Immunoreactivity was quantified as average optical density (AOD), calculated as integrated optical density (IOD) divided by the area of the region of interest (AOD = IOD/area) using ImageJ. For each animal, 3 sections were analyzed by an investigator who was blinded to group identity.

## Elisa for serum GIP and GLP-1
Approximately 1 mL of blood was collected from the orbital sinus and allowed to clot at room temperature for 2 h. Samples were then centrifuged to isolate serum. Serum GIP and GLP-1 concentrations were measured using commercial ELISA kits for GIP and GLP-1 according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Samples were assayed in triplicate, and absorbance was read at 450 nm.

## Cell culture and siRNA transfection
The mouse hippocampal neuronal cell line HT22 was cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, maintained at 37°C in humidified air containing 5% CO2. Prior to transfection, cells were seeded into six-well plates until reaching 80%–90% confluency. GIPR siRNA were purchased from RiboBio, with the nucleotide sequences listed in . Transfection was performed using a transfection reagent (RiboBio) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The final siRNA concentration was 10 nM. Subsequent interventions were performed 48 h post-transfection.

## Aβ treatment in HT22 cells
Aβ1-42 peptide was dissolved in DMSO and diluted in PBS to prepare a 400 μM stock solution. The solution was incubated at 4°C for 24 h. HT22 cells were pretreated with siGIPR (5′-CCAGATCATGACCCAATAT-3′), 10 nM GIP or 10 nM GLP-1 for 24 h and then treated with 5 μM Aβ per well for 24 h in serum-free medium. Control cells received the corresponding vehicle in serum-free medium, and cells in the siGIPR control group were transfected with a scrambled siRNA.

## Western blotting analysis
Protein extracts were obtained from hippocampal tissues and cells by homogenization in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with PMSF and protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. After homogenization, the samples were allowed to stand for 15 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant was then collected, and protein concentration was measured using the BCA method. Subsequently, loading buffer was added to denature the proteins, which were separated by SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and transferred onto a PVDF membrane. After blocking the PVDF membrane with 5% BSA, it was incubated with primary antibodies β-actin (1:2000), Tau (1:2000), pTau (1:1000), BDNF (1:5000), ERK (1:1000), pERK (1:1000), CREB (1:2000), pCREB (1:2000), TrkB (1:10000), pTrkB (1:1000), at 4°C overnight. Membranes were washed with TBST and incubated with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 2 h. After additional washing, protein bands were visualized using an ECL kit. Band intensities were analyzed using ImageJ. Protein levels were normalized to β-actin or to the corresponding total protein for phospho/total ratios, as indicated in the figure legends.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
All data are presented as mean ± SD. Exact n values are provided in the figure legends, where n represents the number of animals for in vivo experiments and the number of independent biological replicates for cell-based experiments. Sample size for the animal experiments was estimated using G∗Power (version 3.1) with the following parameters: F test (ANOVA: fixed effects, omnibus), effect size f = 0.45, α = 0.05, and power = 0.80, yielding a required sample size of n = 6 per group. Some exploratory assays were performed with n = 3 per group and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.
Longitudinal measurements, including body weight, food intake, fasting glucose, OGTT time courses, and Morris water maze acquisition curves, were analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test. Single-time-point comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. Comparisons between two groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test when appropriate. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups using simple randomization. Investigators were blinded during behavioral testing, histological analysis, immunohistochemical quantification, and western blot densitometry. Data distribution was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. Animals that died during surgery or recovery were excluded. Samples with obvious technical failure or poor quality were excluded from analysis. No additional animals or samples were excluded unless predefined criteria were met.
