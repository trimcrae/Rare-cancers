## Abstract
Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a severe cardiovascular disease with high morbidity, significant mortality, and poor prognosis. To date, the pathogenesis of PAH is still not clarified, and the effectiveness of current treatment strategies is limited. Therefore, it is essential to understand the pathophysiology of PAH and to explore optimal treatment approaches. RTKs (receptor tyrosine kinases) are a family of enzyme‐linked receptors that can be bound with specific ligands and then phosphorylate tyrosine residues of substrate targets, playing a crucial role in various biological processes. Accumulating evidence indicates that RTKs are strongly associated with the pathogenesis of PAH. Interestingly, even the same RTK may exhibit opposite effects in PAH under different circumstances. We hypothesize that this may stem from factors such as genetic background, cell line specificity, hypoxia gradient variations, and other underlying mechanisms. Although several RTK‐targeting small molecule drugs have entered clinical trials, concerns regarding their toxicity and limited efficacy have been reported. To facilitate the clinical translation of these therapeutic agents, we highlight the pressing need for developing highly selective drugs (eg, monoclonal antibodies) and more sophisticated drug delivery systems. In this review, we therefore summarized and constructed a multilevel regulatory framework of RTKs with PAH pathogenesis, aiming to guide future clinical practice.
Keywords: pulmonary arterial hypertension, receptor tyrosine kinase, tyrosine kinase inhibitor, vascular remodeling

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) refers to a disease defined by the aberrant increase of mean pulmonary arterial pressure (PAP) >20 mm Hg at rest, leading to obstructive pulmonary circulation, high right heart load, and eventually right heart failure (RHF) or even death. PH is notorious for high morbidity, high mortality, and poor prognosis. It is estimated that >1% of the population is affected by PH worldwide. The prognosis of patients is related to the disease severity and the age at diagnosis, with a mean survival of 4.4 years. PH can be divided into 5 groups according to different physiological bases, including pulmonary arterial remodeling (group 1), left‐sided heart disease (group 2), hypoxia or lung disease (group 3), chronic thrombosis (group 4), and underlying unclear or multifactorial mechanisms (group 5). Group 1 PH, also referred to as PAH, constitutes the central focus of this review. PAH is characterized by pulmonary vascular remodeling, along with increased pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR). In addition, the right ventricle (RV) compensatively increases cardiac output. However, this response transitions to a decompensated form as PVR continues to increase, leading to cardiac hypertrophy, fibrosis, and, ultimately, RV failure. 

Although the underlying mechanisms of PAH have not been elucidated, it is believed that pulmonary artery endothelial cell (PAEC) dysfunction, pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell (PASMC) hyperproliferation, and persistent chronic inflammation are critical risk factors involved (Figure  ). PAEC dysfunction includes increased vascular permeability, alterations of PAEC phenotype, and vascular inflammation. Endothelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition (EndMT) is one of the most common endothelial phenotypic alteration events and has been validated to be involved in PAH progression. In this progress, PAECs transform into a cell type that produces and remodels the extracellular matrix, contributing to vascular fibrosis. Cytokines secreted by PAECs, such as nitric oxide and ET‐1 (endothelin‐1), are crucial for vasodilation and vasoconstriction. PASMCs, the main component of the tunica media, are responsible for vasoconstriction, regulating blood pressure and flow. During the pathological progression of PAH, PASMCs hyperplasia and hypertrophy narrow the arterial lumen, increase the stiffness of the arteries, and even abolish the function of the pulmonary postcapillaries. Adventitia inflammation is closely linked to the exaggeration of PAH. Inflammatory cells like monocyte and dendritic cells are recruited to the adventitia and secret cytokines that contribute to fibroblast proliferation and increased collagen production. 

 Currently, vasodilation is the primary therapy strategy for PAH, using agents such as prostacyclin analog epoprostenol and phosphodiesterase‐5 inhibitors like sildenafil and tadalafil, which target the nitric oxide pathway (Figure  ). However, these drugs have limited therapeutic effects and cannot reverse vascular remodeling. Sotatercept is a fusion protein designed to eliminate the TGF‐β (transforming growth factor beta) superfamily and reverse vascular remodeling. Clinical studies have demonstrated its efficiency in improving exercise capacity and hemodynamics, but hematological adverse events like thrombocytopenia are also reported. , Moreover, there are still no effective biomarkers for PAH diagnosis. Invasive right heart catheterization is still the gold standard for PAH diagnosis. Although echocardiography is a noninvasive test for PAH early screening, its reliability and repeatability are questioned. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanism of PAH pathogenesis, along with the development of novel treatment and diagnostic strategies, is essential.
RTKs are a transmembrane receptor family involved in cell signaling transduction. To date, 58 different types of RTKs have been identified and classified into 20 subfamilies (Table  ). RTKs have similar structures, including a receptor domain, a single transmembrane helix structure, and an intracellular tyrosine kinase domain. These domains are highly conserved across various species, possessing similar activation processes and signaling pathways.
 The mechanism of RTK signaling includes 2 subprocesses: RTK activation and signal transduction. Typically, RTKs first interact with the corresponding ligands and then self‐dimerize (ligand‐induced dimerization). Subsequently, activated RTKs initiate downstream signal transduction. Compared with the receptor activation mechanisms, this process is usually more sophisticated. First, the intracellular segments of RTKs perform precise autophosphorylation. Then, the phosphotyrosine region recruits cytoplasmic signaling molecules containing SH2 (Src homology 2) and phosphotyrosine binding transducers to trigger downstream signaling. Researchers have found that the downstream signaling pathways of RTKs consist of a complex network. This complexity may help us to explain the versatility of RTKs involved in a wide range of cellular behaviors.
Correspondingly, mountains of evidence underscore the pivotal role of RTKs in all stages of the progression of PAH. Notably, targeting RTKs presents a promising avenue for the development of diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for PAH. This review summarizes the development and clinical studies of RTKs in PAH and aims to provide new guides for improving clinical outcomes for patients (Figure  ).
 

## ROLE OF RTK IN PAH

## The Role of EGFR in PAH

The EGFR (epidermal growth factor receptor) family consists of 4 members, including EGFR (ErbB1), HER2 (ErbB2), HER3 (ErbB3), and HER4 (ErbB4). As specific ligands, EGF, TGF‐α, amphiregulin, and epigen bind only ErbB1. In comparison, heparin‐binding EGF, epiregulin, and betacellulin can bind with ErbB1 and ErbB4. NRG (neuregulin) 1 and NRG2 are ligands for ErbB3 and ErbB4, whereas NRG3 and NRG4 bind only ErbB4. No ligands for ErbB2 have been identified so far, even though it was shown to heterodimerize with other ErbB family members, increasing tyrosine residue phosphorylation and promoting downstream signaling. 

ErbB3 could be transformed into a soluble isoform and mediates other EGFR family members signaling. As such, it presents a novel biomarker and a promising target for the treatment of PAH. ErbB3 level significantly increases in the plasma and lung tissue from patients with PAH compared with that from healthy donors. ErbB3 expression in serum exhibits a strong correlation with clinical indicators, including systolic PAP, mean PAP, PVR, and cardiac index. Importantly, the monoclonal ErbB3 antibody, seribantumab, markedly suppresses hypoxia‐induced PAH in the rat model. Additionally, ErbB3 is also associated with the drug resistance of targeted therapies for cancer. Though few clinical studies focused on the efficiency of anti‐EGFR drugs in PAH, these drugs still demonstrate the ability to overcome possible drug resistance in PAH in the future.
Furthermore, inhibition of ErbB signaling by dacomitinib suppresses PASMC proliferation and attenuates pulmonary vascular remodeling via PI3K/Akt/mTOR (phosphatidylinositol 3 phosphate/protein kinase B/mammalian target of rapamycin) pathway. EGFR could also be activated by membrane‐located protein and mediate vascular pathophysiology. Phosphorylation of EGFR by midkine‐nucleolin complex on the cell membrane enhances PASMC proliferation and migration. Pharmacological inhibition of midkine‐nucleolin interaction is effective in ameliorating PAH in Sugen5416/hypoxia rat models. Additionally, AT1R (angiotensin II type 1 receptor) activates EGFR by increasing expression and release of EGF, direction interaction with EGFR, and phosphorylation of EGFR residues by AT1R‐induced signaling, contributing to vascular remodeling, cardiac hypertrophy, endothelial dysfunction, and angiogenesis. , However, the role of such transactivation in PAH remains unknown.
These studies suggest that the ErbB family serves as a promising therapeutic target and diagnostic biomarker for PAH. Inhibition of ErbB by monoclonal antibodies or small molecules might help suppress the progression of PAH. However, more clinical trials are still required to evaluate their security and efficiency.

## The Role of VEGFR in PAH

The VEGFR (vascular endothelial growth factor receptor) family includes VEGFR1, VEGFR2 and VEGFR3. VEGF‐A, VEGF‐B, VEGF‐C, VEGF‐D, and PIGF (placenta growth factor) are ligands for VEGFR. VEGF usually exists as a homodimer and induces VEGFR to dimerize and transactivate upon binding. VEGFR1, which binds VEGF‐A, VEGF‐B, and PIGF, plays a crucial role in embryonic angiogenesis through mechanisms that are independent of its tyrosine kinase domain. VEGFR2, the receptor for VEGF‐A, the mature form of VEGF‐C and VEGF‐D after the proteolytical process, is mainly expressed on blood vascular and growing lymphatic endothelial cells. Full‐length or proteolytically processed VEGF‐C and VEGF‐D can both bind VEGFR3. Neuropilin1 and neuropilin2 serve as coreceptors for VEGF binding and enhance the signaling of VEGFR. 

VEGF‐A is a key angiogenic growth factor involved in both normal physiology and various diseases. Elevated levels of VEGF‐A and its receptors have been observed in patients with PAH and experimental PAH, leading to the frequent classification of PAH as a proangiogenic pulmonary vascular disease. However, inhibiting VEGF‐A/VEGFR signaling through the tyrosine kinase inhibitor Sugen5416 or genetic deletion of the VEGFR kinase domain gene in combination with hypoxia has been found to induce angio‐obliterative PAH. , 

Considerable effort has been devoted to reversing pulmonary vascular remodeling by regulating VEGF levels, but contrary results have been obtained. Jiang et al. emphasize the protective role of the inhibition of abnormal VEGF levels by showing that SETD3 (SET domain‐containing 3) lentiviral vector treatment ameliorates pulmonary vascular remodeling and RV hypertrophy. Mechanistically, SETD3 binds to the promoter region of VEGF and suppresses its transcription. However, the beneficial role of VEGF‐A is also documented. Transfer of smooth muscle cells (SMCs) overexpressing VEGF‐A via the internal jugular vein abolishes the development of PAH. These opposite results might be attributed to the fact that they regulate VEGF levels in different cell types, emphasizing the significance of cell specificity when applying VEGF‐related therapies.
In addition to VEGF levels, the phosphorylation of VEGFR residues is also crucial for its role in PAH. VEGFR Y949 phosphorylation increases pulmonary vascular permeability via Src/Yes1 pathways, subsequently enhancing macrophage infiltration. It also contributes to distal vascular muscularization via Rac1 signaling. Unfortunately, selective VEGFR residue inhibitors are currently unavailable, which warrants more exploration and investigation.
The aforementioned studies enrich the signaling landscape of VEGFR, revealing an increasing number of associated pathways (Figure  ). However, these findings indicate a seemingly paradoxical role of VEGFR in PAH development. More research is needed to clarify why VEGFR has shown distinct effects in PAH preclinical studies to guide clinical practice. For example, different isoforms of VEGFR/VEGF often have different functions. sVEGFR1 (soluble VEGFR1) is regarded as a trap for VEGFA and reduces the bioavailability of VEGFA, subsequently suppressing the activation of VEGFR2. In women with suspected preeclampsia, increased expression of sVEGFR1 is associated with the antiangiogenic environment. Additionally, in atherosclerosis, VEGF‐A is a proinflammatory factor and is associated with the deterioration of atherosclerosis. On the contrary, VEGF‐C exerts a protective effect on SMCs, and its low serum level indicates a better prognosis. 

 

## The Role of HGF/Met in PAH

Met, the receptor for HGF (hepatocyte growth factor) with a conserved tyrosine kinase structure, is mainly expressed in epithelial and endothelial cells. Five intracellular tyrosine residues mediate Met activation and signaling transduction. These include residues within the kinase domain, those that negatively regulate Met activation, and those that provide the docking domain for transducers. 

Current research shows that restoring HGF or exogenous HGF supplement is a promising therapy for PAH. Transfection with HGF gene dramatically inhibits the thickening of pulmonary arteries and disordered growth of PASMCs along with suppression of endothelin‐1 and TGF‐ β. Administration of recombinant HGF ameliorates PAH and improves survivability in rats. These studies validate that HGF complementation has the potential to reverse PAH and improve prognosis. However, more specific mechanisms remain elusive. For example, it is unclear whether the improvement in survivability is attributed to the amelioration of PAH or the reduction of oxidative stress. Additionally, the potential hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity associated with HGF complementation necessitate thorough evaluation.
Sox17 mutations are considered a risk factor for PAH by both genome‐wide association studies and Sugen5416/hypoxia‐treated mice models. In contrast to previous studies, in endothelial‐specific Sox17‐deficiency mice, HGF signaling in PAEC exaggerates the contribution of Sox17 deficiency to PAH progression. Met inhibitor preventive treatment improves the PAH phenotypes of these transgenic mice, including fewer muscularized pulmonary vessels and reduced right ventricular systolic pressure. In addition, HGF‐induced nascent vessel leakage has been documented, but it is still unclear whether this phenomenon contributes to the pathogenesis of PAH.
Available evidence indicates the great potential of HGF in treating PAH. However, contradictory results are obtained in Sox17 endothelial‐deficiency mice, implying that PAH patients with diverse genetic backgrounds might have different outcomes if they receive HGF treatment. Thus, greater caution should be exercised when employing therapies involving HGF.

## The Role of PDGFR in PAH

PDGFR (platelet‐derived growth factor receptor) is classified into 2 types: PDGFRα and PDGFRβ. They are located on the cell membrane as monomers but dimerize upon ligand binding. Ligands of PDGFR include PDGF‐A, PDGF‐B, PDGF‐C and PDGF‐D. All of these ligands must be modified by certain proteins and dimerized before binding to the receptor or the extracellular matrix, among which only PDGF‐BB can bind all PDGFR. 

PDGFR is indispensable for angiogenesis, as validated by the vascular defect in PDGF‐B knock‐out mice. Substantial evidence has shown that PDGF‐B and PDGFRβ are upregulated in patients with PAH and involved in endothelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition, which aggravates vascular remodeling and PAH. Additionally, PDGF serves as a messenger between PAECs and PASMCs in response to hypoxia (Figure  ). Specifically, chronic hypoxia increases the expression of PDGF‐B in PAECs, which then binds to PDGFRβ on PASMCs, thereby upregulating Beclin1. Beclin1 suppresses PASMC apoptosis and contributes to distal pulmonary vascular muscularization. From this point of view, the treatments for reactivating the apoptosis pathway that is suppressed by PDGFR are worth exploring. This study also emphasizes the significance of intercellular communication between PAECs and PASMCs in the progression of PAH, proposing the potential use of neutralizing antibodies to disrupt such interactions as a therapeutic strategy.
 Additionally, in vitro studies provide more evidence for PDGFR in the regulation of PASMC function and pulmonary vessels. In rat primary PASMCs, PDGF treatment activates STAT (signal transducers and activators of transcription) 5 and upregulates nicotinamide phosphoribosyl transferase, which is then secreted and binds to the TLR4 (toll‐like receptor 4), subsequently promoting PASMC proliferation. Moreover, PDGF‐BB induces pulmonary vascular tone through PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling. Contrarily, blockage of PI3K‐α/γ inhibits PDGF‐BB‐induced vasoconstriction. 

To sum up, PDGF/PDGFR signaling has been regarded as a significant mechanism in PAH progression as it induces EndMT, PASMCs hyperproliferation, and vasoconstriction. Consequently, various PDGFR inhibitors, including imatinib and sorafenib, have already been in clinical studies to evaluate their efficiency and safety, which will be discussed later. Nevertheless, the studies mentioned still suggest the potential of targeting PDGF‐mediated intercellular communication and its downstream pathways to reverse vascular remodeling and induce vasodilation in PAH.

## The Role of Trk in PAH

The Trk (tropomyosin receptor kinases) family comprises 3 members, TrkA, TrkB, and TrkC, which are encoded by NTRK (neurotrophic receptor kinase) 1, NTRK2, and NTRK3 genes, respectively. The primary distinction among these Trk subtypes lies in their ligand specificity: TrkA predominantly binds to NGF (nerve growth factor). TrkB interacts with BDNF (brain‐derived neurotrophic factor), and TrkC selectively binds to NT‐3 (neurotrophin‐3). NGF, BDNF, and their receptors are typically recognized for their critical roles in the nervous system, but emerging evidence also reveals their functions in PAECs and PASMCs in hypoxia.
The expression of NGF and phosphorylation of TrkA are increased in PAH, promoting phosphorylation of CX43 (connexin‐43) and enhancing pulmonary vascular contractility. Additionally, a positive feedback loop between BDNF/TrkB and HIF‐1α (hypoxia‐induced factor 1‐alpha) signaling perpetuates pulmonary vascular remodeling under hypoxic conditions. (Figure  ) Hypoxia appears to be a critical driver of excessive Trk activation in PAH. However, the effects of Trk activation in PAH contrast sharply with their protective role in the brain during hypoxic conditions. , This disparity may stem from the intrinsic differences in cellular behavior: neural cells have limited proliferative capacity and primarily rely on Trk signaling to prevent apoptosis, whereas in PASMCs, Trk activation not only inhibits apoptosis but also enhances proliferation, contributing to pathological vascular remodeling. These multifaceted roles of Trks warrant further investigation to elucidate their context‐specific effects and therapeutic potential.

## The Role of FGFR in PAH

FGFR (fibroblast growth factor receptor) consists of FGFR1, FGFR2, FGFR3, FGFR4, and FGFs include FGF1 through FGF23 (FGF1‐10 and FGF16‐23 in humans). FGFRs are shown to homodimerize or heterodimerize and be autoinhibitory in the absence of FGF due to the interaction of the extracellular domain. FGF and FGFR typically form a ternary complex upon binding, among which heparin promotes FGFR dimerization and FGF‐FGFR interaction. 

Clinically, FGF23 exhibits the capability to serve as a biomarker for PAH prognosis. Patients with PAH with high FGF23 plasma levels show poor hemodynamics including higher PVR, mean PAP, and lower cardiac index compared with those with low FGF‐23 levels. Additionally, FGF2 is upregulated in pulmonary endothelial cells and PASMCs from patients with PAH. , , It is secreted by pulmonary endothelial cells and enhances pulmonary endothelial cells and PASMC proliferation (Figure  ). , , Both genetic ablation of FGF2 by siRNA or miRNA and pharmacological inhibition of its receptor, FGFR1, reverse PAH in rats. , These studies lay a solid foundation for the effect of FGF2/FGFR1 in both PAECs and PASMCs and also imply the potential of the interruption of FGF2/FGFR1 signaling to treat PAH. However, further investigations are required to reveal the effect of transgenic mice. Interestingly, other FGF family members, such as FGF12 and FGF21, act as a protective factor in PAH. , FGF12 contributes to the maintenance of the quiescent phenotype of PASMCs. FGF21 restrains the overproliferation of PASMCs and the production of inflammatory cytokines. 

As described before, the FGF family contains plenty of members, and they have different effects on PAH. Although the role of FGFR in PAH is ambiguous, the results still emphasize the significance of FGFR in endothelial function and its mediating effect between PAECs and PASMCs. However, the underlying mechanisms that contribute to these differences are not well known, which might include different phosphorylation sites of FGFR and activation of other receptors or costimulators, as FGF‐FGFR specificity is mediated by tissue‐specific molecules or secreted protein. Consequently, in addition to focusing solely on the functions of FGF/FGFR, the transactivation and specific activation of costimulators also merit investigation.

## The Role of Eph in PAH

The Eph family is divided into 2 receptor subgroups (EphA: EphA1‐A8/EphA10 and EphB: EphB1‐B4/EphB6), comprising the largest subfamily of RTKs. The classification of Eph receptors is determined by their ligands, ephrin. Eph receptors that bind to ephrin‐A are designated as EphA, whereas those that bind to ephrin‐B are designated as EphB. Unlike other RTK ligands, ephrin is not soluble and is located on the cell membrane instead. Consequently, ephrin can also trigger signaling transduction, which is called reverse signals, and forward signals refer to signaling triggered by Eph. , 

Ephrin‐A1 is downregulated in chronic hypoxia‐induced PAH rats lung and PAECs from patients with PAH. , Ephrin‐A2 deficiency accelerates PAH development by increasing the loss of small arteries in the lung. Ephrin‐A3 serves as a “guard” in vascular inflammation as the elevated EphA3 expression is strongly related to M2 macrophage (the anti‐inflammatory phenotypes) module genes in hypoxic‐induced PAH. These pieces of evidence suggest that EphA/ephrin‐A signaling exerts a protective effect on PAH. However, therapies like exogenous supplements of ephrin‐A have not been validated in mouse models. Ephrin‐B2 mitigates PH by promoting the growth of alveoli and vessels. On the contrary, ephrin‐B2 and EphB4 on PASMC induce PASMC proliferation and vasoconstriction, respectively, along with the deterioration of PAH (Figure  ). 

Eph family signaling is more complex than expected because ephrin can bind more than 1 receptor and even all receptors in the corresponding subfamily. The studies of Eph in PAH are also not comprehensive enough to explore therapeutic targets and conduct clinical trials. Nevertheless, the Eph family deserves further studies as it is capable of triggering forward and reverse signaling simultaneously, affecting PAEC and PASMC function at the same time. In addition, from a vascular structural perspective, Eph might also serve as a bridge between PAECs and circulating immune cells, playing a critical role in vascular inflammation associated with PAH.

## POSSIBLE REASONS FOR THE DISCREPANCY OF RTKs IN PAH

Opposite results are obtained when researchers try to enhance or suppress the function of a specific RTK, implying the complicated relationship between RTKs and PAH. Here, we propose several possible explanations for these unexpected results. First, the role of RTKs might be associated with the genetic background. For instance, exogenous activation of Met signaling reverses PAH in WT rat models, and Met inhibitors also ameliorate PAH in Sox17‐deficiency mice. In this case, the distinct effect of Met signaling in PAH is due to the different genetic backgrounds, such as the compensation effects of the Sox17 abolishment. Second, RTK expression in different cell lines would have diverse contributions to PAH (Figure  ). Both injection of SMCs overexpressing VEGF‐A27 and inhibition of the VEGF‐A excessive production reduce PAH. Consequently, treatments targeting specific cell types and regulations of the interaction between cells might provide new insight into PAH studies. Finally, hypoxia gradient, epigenetic regulation of RTK, ligand accessibility, and mechanical tension might also be involved in RTK signaling, which is worth further research. For example, shear stress, which can activate VEGFR, might be responsible for its different role in PAECs and PASMCs.

## CURRENT RTK‐TARGETED THERAPIES OF PAH

Up to now, several types of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) targeting different RTKs have been developed and can be classified into 3 generations according to their efficiencies, selectivity, and abilities to overcome drug resistance. Some TKIs have been evaluated in PAH clinical trials (Table  ). Imatinib, the first Food and Drug Administration‐approved first‐generation TKI for chronic myelogenous leukemia, inhibits the activities of PDGFR, KIT (kinase insert domain receptor), and BCR‐ABL1 (a fusion protein encoded by the Philadelphia chromosome). Its efficiency in PAH is evaluated in both PAH rodent models and clinical trials. Imatinib reverses vascular remodeling in rat model, and patients with PAH treated with imatinib show increased 6‐minute walk distance and decreased pulmonary vascular resistance. , However, adverse events are also reported, including subdural hematoma and negligible improvement in PAH worsening. Inhaled imatinib (seralutinib) is developed and expected to reduce the side effect. The Phase I multiple ascending dose clinical trial in healthy adults demonstrates better tolerability. Another phase II trial verifies that seralutinib is well tolerated as the cough is the most frequent adverse event. PVR and PAP in patients with PAH who receive seraluntinib treatments decrease with statistical significance. However, 6‐minute walk distance and cardiac output have no remarkable changes. The efficiency of imatinib is well validated but changes when it comes to seralutinib. Combination therapies may help to resolve these issues but require more research.
 Second‐generation TKIs like dasatinib and nilotinib have broader target profiles compared with first‐generation TKIs, so they might lead to unprecedented adverse events. For instance, the number of tyrosine kinases that dasatinib binds to is greater than the number targeted by nilotinib. Patients with chronic myelogenous leukemia treated with dasatinib are likely to be diagnosed with PAH, but substitution with nilotinib reverses this pathological change. Thus, more attention should be paid to second‐generation TKIs when dealing with PAH. Another second‐generation TKI, sorafenib, was investigated in a Phase I trial that enrolled 12 patients with PAH. All participants experienced adverse events, and the hand/foot reaction was the most frequent case. The 6‐minute walk distance did not change significantly, but treadmill metabolic equivalents increase. This study indicates that skin events in patients treated with sorafenib are less tolerative compared with other TKI treatments. The effect of sorafenib on PAH is not efficient enough in this study, which requires more investigation.
Third‐generation TKIs, including osinertinib and rociletinib, are more sensitive to EGFR mutants and spare wild‐type EGFR simultaneously. They have been the first‐line treatment for nonsmall cell lung cancer patients with EGFR mutations. Unfortunately, few studies are focused on the relationship between third‐generation TKIs and PAH. Moreover, further investigations are warranted to determine whether patients with PAH harbor RTK mutations that reduce the efficacy of TKIs. Such research would facilitate the development of more targeted TKI therapies, ultimately improving treatment outcomes for this patient population.
To reduce side effects and improve patient outcomes, researchers are investigating more TKIs in experiment PAH models. Icotinib is one of the first‐generation EGFR TKIs and has been approved for the treatment of EGFR‐mutant advanced/metastatic nonsmall cell lung cancer with adverse events. Differentiation of PASMCs from a contractile phenotype to a synthetic phenotype, which is associated with alterations of gene expression and vascular adaptability, could be suppressed by icotinib in vitro. Pulmonary vascular remodeling is reversed by icotinib treatment in rat models, along with reduced right ventricular systolic press and alleviated RV hypertrophy. Dacomitinib, a second‐generation EGFR TKI, inhibits PASMCs proliferation and migration through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, prohibiting tunica media thickening and RV hypertrophy in experimental PAH. Infigratinib is an inhibitor of FGFR and received its Food and Drug Administration approval in May 2021. Combination treatment with infigratinib and a conventional vasodilation drug, sildenafil, improves right ventricular systolic press and vascular remodeling in monocrotaline‐treated rat models. Although numerous studies have shown the role of RTK inhibitors in experimental PAH, there is still a long way to go before clinical application.
As illustrated in Table  , all TKIs used in clinical trials for PAH are designed to target PDGFR. This focus stems from the critical role of PDGFR in driving vascular remodeling. Currently, PDGF is widely employed to induce PASMC proliferation, serving as a model system for studying cellular behavior. As a result, inhibiting PDGFR effectively suppresses smooth muscle proliferation, thereby attenuating vascular remodeling and potentially mitigating PAH progression. Nevertheless, inhibitors that target other RTKs have not advanced to clinical investigations. This is attributed to the limited understanding of the specific mechanisms through which other RTKs contribute to PAH pathogenesis, underscoring the need for further foundational research into RTKs in the context of PAH.

## CHALLENGES AND PERSPECTIVES
Dysregulation of RTKs contributes to endothelial dysfunction and SMC proliferation in PAH via regulating various signaling pathways, serving as a promising target for PAH diagnosis, prediction, and effective treatments. Great advancement has been made in this field, but many unsolved challenges remain (Figure  ).
 The beneficial effects of TKIs in PAH clinical trials are limited, especially with significant side effects. The phase III clinical trial of imatinib for the treatment of PAH performed by Novartis shows high discontinuation rates, and volunteers present with adverse reactions such as excessive subdural hematomas. One of the underlying reasons for this outcome is that imatinib is a multitarget drug, targeting not only PDGFR but also BCR‐ABL1 and KIT. More important, as RTKs are essential for growth and development in mammals, excessive inhibition of their function may lead to unimaginable harm. For instance, a study has demonstrated that deficiency of ROR2 (receptor tyrosine kinase‐like orphan receptor 2) leads to the development of tetralogy of Fallot, as demonstrated in both in vitro and in vivo investigation. PDGFRA loss‐of‐function mutation leads to metabolic disturbances in most gastrointestinal stromal tumors. Therefore, exploring more specific TKIs or novel alternative approaches for targeting RTKs in PAH treatment is essential. At present, various novel approaches and technologies have emerged for targeting RTKs in the treatment of PAH. The application of artificial intelligence in drug design offers new perspectives for identifying more specific TKIs. Zhavoronkov et al. developed a deep‐learning model for small‐molecule drug design. Using this model, they identified a potent DDR1 (discoidin domain receptor 1) inhibitor that exhibits promising results in both in vitro pharmacological activity and toxicity assessments, as well as in vivo pharmacokinetic evaluations. Furthermore, peptides and antibodies that target RTKs may be promising candidates for PAH treatment. These biopharmaceuticals have fewer side effects and better specificity compared with traditional TKIs. Our research indicates that intraperitoneal injecting MM‐121 (a human monoclonal antibody targeting the ligand‐binding domain of ErbB3) attenuates RV dysfunction and reduces vascular remodeling in PAH rats. These novel technologies and drugs will provide new insights into PAH treatment.
Accurately delivering drugs to the lungs, especially pulmonary vasculature, has always been a big challenge. Therefore, improving the delivery of TKIs to the pulmonary vasculature warrants further investigation. Airway inhalation (aerosolized delivery) may be the optimal solution for addressing the low‐targeting pulmonary vasculature of TKIs. The inhalation route delivers the drug directly to the alveolar region, resulting in milder systemic adverse reactions. The clinical research on the inhalation of imatinib (dry powder formulation) for PAH treatment has yielded promising outcomes. However, inhaled administration also has certain limitations. Inhalation administration could irritate the airways, resulting in coughing. Furthermore, controlling the concentration of the drug upon reaching the pulmonary arteries is challenging due to the complexity of the respiratory tract. Besides, a substantial part of the drug may be deposited primarily in the airways and eventually cleared. To address this issue, some novel drug delivery systems offer new perspectives for enhancing targeting. Nanoparticles, such as liposomes, polymeride, and mesoporous silica, emerge as a novel and promising way for drug delivery. They improve the solubility of the loaded cargo by taking advantage of the nanoparticle amphipathicity. The size of the nanoparticle can also be adjusted to pass through vessels with specific permeability. Modifications of specific targeting groups on nanoparticles further enhance the specificity. Numerous studies have demonstrated the high efficiency of nanoparticle delivery systems. For example, polymeric nanoparticles linked with VEGFR antibodies, or lipid‐base nanosuspensions enhance the antitumor effect of sorafenib compared with simply oral or i.v. administration in hepatoma‐22 tumor‐bearing mice. More significantly, glucuronic acid‐modified liposomes enable targeted delivery of sildenafil to PASMCs in PAH rats. Mechanically, the upregulation of GLUT1 (glucose transporter) in PASMCs of PAH rats recruits glucuronic acid‐modified liposomes. Consequently, drug‐loaded Glc‐modified liposomes could be transported to PASMCs automatically. However, more studies are required to validate the feasibility of nanoparticle delivery systems in PAH. What is more, appropriate modifications on the nanoparticle should be investigated.
RHF is a significant factor contributing to mortality in patients with PAH. The RV initially undergoes compensatory remodeling due to increased afterload, which subsequently progresses to maladaptive remodeling characterized by cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, myocardial fibrosis, and vascular rarefaction, ultimately resulting in RHF. Thus, ameliorating RHF is a promising way to improve PAH prognosis. However, the specific role of RTKs in RV cells, such as cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts involved in RHF, is still unclear. Given the heterogeneous effects of RTK between different cell lines, to focus on their role in RHF, more studies should be conducted to investigate the regulatory mechanisms of RTKs in these specific RV cells. In addition, experimental PAH models are not considered preferred models for studying RHF. For example, Sugen5416/hypoxia models cannot form pulmonary occlusive lesions and induce progressive RHF. The monocrotaline model experiences severe liver and kidney toxicity and also myocarditis, which makes it not an ideal model specifically for RHF. Nevertheless, the pulmonary artery banding model has been widely used in RHF research, which might give new insight into the RTK functions in RHF. The pulmonary artery banding model induces RHF by increasing afterload, which is similar to the induction of RHF in patients with PAH. Moreover, the pulmonary artery banding model allows researchers to control RV afterload by adjusting the degree of pulmonary artery stenosis, thereby more accurately simulating the pathological changes in RV at different stages and facilitating a better exploration of the dynamic changes in right heart function. Importantly, RTKs have been demonstrated to play a significant role in left heart failure. Activation of ERBB4 induces cardiomyocyte hypertrophy. In transverse aortic constriction‐induced heart failure mice, inhibition of VEGFR‐3 significantly reduces cardiac lymphangiogenesis, exacerbating cardiac edema, hypertrophy, and fibrosis. Another research shows that cardiac‐specific overexpression of IGF1 (insulin‐like growth factor‐1) in mice leads to significantly enhanced cardiac function. These findings also underscore the therapeutic potential of targeting RTKs in the treatment of RHF.
For reasons of brevity, many members of the RTK family involved in PAH have not been discussed in detail in this review. For example, insulin resistance induced by insulin receptors also contributes to PAH progression. Weak activation of ROR impairs pericyte recruitment and loss of distal vessels, leading to severe PAH. However, many members of the RTK family remain unexplored in PAH. In contrast, the studies of RTKs in cancer are more extensive, and several TKIs have been approved by the FDA for cancer treatment, such as gefitinib and crizotinib for nonsmall cell lung cancer and sorafenib for advanced renal cell cancer. Consequently, from both basic research and clinical translation perspectives, the potential of RTKs in PAH is boundless.

## CONCLUSIONS
In summary, the family of RTKs plays diverse roles in PAH, with their abnormal function leading to abnormal pulmonary angiogenesis, endothelial cell dysfunction, and vascular remodeling. Targeting RTKs seems a promising strategy for the treatment of PAH. However, the mechanisms by which RTKs operate in PAH require further investigation, which will help us better combat this disease. Additionally, drugs targeting RTKs need further optimization, and the roles of certain RTKs that have not been extensively studied in PAH await confirmation. We believe that RTKs will provide more options and opportunities for the treatment of PAH in the future.
