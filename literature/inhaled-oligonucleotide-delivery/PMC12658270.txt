While supportive treatments such as mechanical ventilation and fluid management have improved patient outcomes, the mortality rate in severe cases remains between 30% and 40% , . A major contributing factor to this high death rate is the absence of targeted therapies that can control inflammation without compromising immune function . Currently, no specific drugs exist to prevent alveolar damage or mitigate cytokine storms. Current treatment strategies primarily focus on respiratory support, infection control, and maintaining homeostasis. Further research is urgently needed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of ALI/ARDS and identify new therapeutic targets.
Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a critical component of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, plays a pivotal role in the acute lung inflammation seen in ALI/ARDS . Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) possesses both extracellular and intracellular receptors. The Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/myeloid differentiation factor 2 (MD-2) complex functions as the extracellular receptor, while caspase − 4/11 serves as the intracellular receptor. These receptors demonstrate the ability to directly interact with the lipid A moiety of LPS . Activation of these pathways leads to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and chemokines, which recruit immune cells like neutrophils and monocytes to the alveolar space. This exacerbates inflammation, disrupts the alveolar-capillary barrier, and results in pulmonary edema, impaired gas exchange, and hypoxemia .
The LPS model proves highly suitable for exploring the pathogenesis of ALI or ARDS triggered by local pulmonary bacterial infections and assessing new therapeutic agents’ efficacy . Administering LPS intravenously mimics sepsis - related ARDS. In this process, endothelial cells are identified as the primary cellular targets . LPS can be administered via intratracheal injection, aerosol inhalation, or systemic injection (intraperitoneal or intravenous) in animal studies. This induces hallmark signs of lung injury, including neutrophil infiltration and alveolar damage . This study leverages the LPS-induced model to explore the effects of modulating specific genes on inflammation and tissue damage.
Transcription factors are essential for regulating gene expression, cell differentiation, and cellular responses to environmental changes . One such factor is CCAAT/Enhancer Binding Protein Alpha (CEBPA), which has been extensively studied for its roles in hematopoiesis, adipogenesis, and leukemia . CEBPA governs lineage-specific gene expression and functions as a key regulator for the terminal differentiation of multiple cell types, such as granulocytes. The CEBPA gene, featuring a single exon with an internal translational start site, generates two isoforms: the full-length CEBPA and a shorter N-terminal-truncated variant , . Recent studies have highlighted CEBPA’s emerging roles in inflammation and tissue repair , where it collaborates with pathways such as NF-κB and AP-1 to regulate cytokine production and immune responses . In models of heart injury and metabolic stress, reduced CEBPA expression correlates with heightened inflammation and tissue damage. However, its specific role in the lungs, particularly in acute lung injury, remains poorly understood. Investigating how CEBPA regulates alveolar epithelial cells and macrophages could provide insights into its function in acute lung injury.
Sirtuins (SIRT) are distinctive posttranslational modification enzymes that employ NAD⁺ as a co-substrate to deacylate lysine residues . Acting on diverse substrates, they influence major metabolic processes . The seven SIRT family members each have unique functions, targeting a broad spectrum of cellular proteins in the nucleus, cytoplasm, and mitochondria for acetylation (SIRT1, 2, 3, 5) or ADP - ribosylation (SIRT4, 6) modifications . SIRT1 and SIRT3 have been well-characterized for their roles in managing oxidative stress, regulating inflammation, and maintaining genomic stability . Recently, attention has shifted toward SIRT4, which has been shown to play a vital role in mitochondrial function and metabolic homeostasis , . In inflammatory contexts, SIRT4 protects cells by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation and reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, thereby limiting cytokine release and mitochondrial damage , . In models of heart and liver injury, upregulation of SIRT4 exhibits anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective effects . However, its role in acute lung inflammation requires further investigation. Understanding SIRT4’s functions in alveolar epithelial cells and macrophages could reveal new therapeutic avenues for treating acute lung injury.
As a transcription factor, CEBPA regulates gene expression by binding to the promoters of target genes, whereas deacetylases such as SIRT4 modulate transcription factors like CEBPA through deacetylation, impacting their stability and activity . Bioinformatics analyses have identified potential CEBPA binding sites within the SIRT4 promoter, suggesting that CEBPA may directly regulate SIRT4 transcription . Preliminary evidence points to a regulatory relationship between CEBPA and SIRT4, both of which are key players in cellular networks governing inflammation and cytoprotection. Furthermore, SIRT4 may reciprocally modulate CEBPA, forming a dynamic feedback loop that enhances their regulatory effects.

## Materials and methods

## Correlation analysis of CEBPA and SIRT4 gene expression
The correlation analysis of the expression levels of CEBPA and SIRT4 genes was carried out by leveraging the online bioinformatics analysis platform available at http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/index.html. Within the “Correlation Analysis” module of this platform, CEBPA and SIRT4 were designated as the target genes. Subsequently, the correlation between their expression patterns was meticulously analyzed in the “Lung” tissue subset retrieved from the GTEx database.

## Cell culture and treatment
RAW264.7 mouse macrophages, HEK293T human embryonic kidney cells, and A549 human lung adenocarcinoma epithelial cells (a widely used model for type II alveolar epithelial cells) were sourced from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank. The RAW264.7 cell line was chosen for its relevance to macrophage-mediated inflammation in ALI. The HEK293T cell line was used for luciferase reporter assays due to its high transfection efficiency. The A549 cell line was employed for experiments requiring alveolar epithelial context, including LPS dose-response, EMSA, ChIP, and functional assays, as it retains several characteristics of type II alveolar cells. Cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, 11965092), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, 10099141) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122), and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. In the LPS dose screening experiments, LPS concentrations (0–200 µg/mL) were selected based on prior studies – . Cell viability assays confirmed that a significant proportion of cells remained viable at this concentration (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Importantly, the upregulation of CEBPA and SIRT4 was also confirmed at a lower, more pathophysiologically relevant LPS concentration (1 µg/mL) in subsequent functional experiments (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Experimental groups included: Control (no treatment), LPS stimulation (1 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, L2630) for 24 h, overexpression (cells transfected with CEBPA or SIRT4-expressing plasmids), and knockdown (cells transfected with siRNA targeting CEBPA or SIRT4). Negative controls comprised cells transfected with an empty plasmid (NC-plasmid) or non-targeting siRNA (si-NC). Transfections were conducted using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, L3000001) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After 24–48 h, cells and supernatants were harvested for analysis. The transfection efficiency was validated via qPCR Analysis and the results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1C,D.

## Measurement of reactive oxygen species (ROS) production
The quantification of intracellular ROS generation was conducted utilizing the Fluorometric Intracellular ROS Assay Kit (catalog number MAK143), a product supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, Missouri, USA). The experimental procedure strictly adhered to the standard operating instructions provided by the kit manufacturer with the methodological approach previously documented by Cataldi et al. . To track the changes in ROS production, the assay leveraged a critical biochemical property of ROS: these reactive molecules are capable of interacting with a fluorogenic probe that localizes within the cytoplasmic compartment of cells. This interaction triggers a chemical reaction that converts the non-fluorescent probe into a fluorescent product. The cumulative quantity of the resulting fluorescent product exhibits a direct proportional relationship with the concentration of ROS present in the cells.

## Predicting putative CEBPA binding sites in the SIRT4 promoter using the JASPAR database
The putative CEBPA binding sites within the SIRT4 promoter were predicted through the following methodology. First, the JASPAR database (http://jaspar.genereg.net/) was accessed, and the CEBPA transcription factor binding profile was searched for. The species (Homo sapiens) was selected to identify the relevant position weight matrix (PWM). Next, the genomic coordinates of the SIRT4 transcription start site (TSS) were retrieved using resource NCBI Gene. The promoter sequence was extracted. Subsequently, the JASPAR Scan tool (http://jaspar.genereg.net/scan/) was utilized to scan the promoter sequence. The FASTA sequence was input, the CEBPA matrix was selected, and parameters (e.g., score threshold ≥ 80%, species-specific settings) were configured. The output was analyzed for predicted binding sites.

## Mouse sepsis-induced ALI model
Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (18–22 g) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Shanghai Bikai Keyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China) and acclimatized for one week prior to experimentation. All procedures were performed in compliance with Animal Ethics Committee guidelines. The sepsis-induced acute lung injury (ALI) model was induced by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) on mice, who were anesthetized with dexmedetomidine (Sichuan Guorui, China, 0.5 mg/kg) and ketamine (Jiuxu, China, 100 mg/kg) and immobilized. After ligation of the cecum, it was punctured multiple times before being sutured. Experimental groups included: Control (instilled with an equal volume of sterile saline), ALI group (Sepsis-induced ALI only), Overexpression group (ALI + CEBPA-plasmid or ALI + SIRT4-plasmid, tail vein injection of plasmids expressing CEBPA or SIRT4), and Knockdown group (ALI + si-SIRT4, tail vein injection of siRNA targeting SIRT4, Sangon Biotech). To collect the lung tissue, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. To assess the temporal dynamics of CEBPA and SIRT4 expression, mice were subjected to CLP-induced sepsis and lung tissues were collected at 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h post-procedure. Total RNA were extracted, and the expression levels of CEBPA and SIRT4 were analyzed by qRT-PCR, respectively. The results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. All methods were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org) and were approved by Shanghai Shidong Hospital Animal Ethics Committee. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

## H&E staining and lung injury scoring
Lung tissue was collected 24 h post- sepsis-induced ALI model establishment. Lung tissue was fixed in 4% formaldehyde (Beyotime, P0099) for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4 μm, deparaffinized with xylene (Sinopharm), rehydrated using graded alcohols, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Beyotime). Tissue structure and inflammatory infiltration were examined under an optical microscope (Olympus). Lung injury severity was assessed by scoring alveolar destruction, edema, infiltration, and hemorrhage, with each criterion graded on a scale of 0–4, for a total score of 16.

## Lung edema index (W/D Ratio)
For lung edema evaluation, lung tissue was collected, with blood and trachea removed, and wet weight (W) measured. Tissue was then dried at 60 °C for 48 h (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to determine dry weight (D), and the W/D ratio was calculated.

## Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) analysis
BALF was collected 24 h post-sepsis-induced ALI model establishment. BALF was collected by instilling 1 mL of sterile PBS (Gibco, 10010023) into the lungs via tracheal cannulation. The supernatant of BALF was used for assays: LDH activity was measured using a lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay kit (Beyotime, C0016), and inflammatory cytokine levels of TNF-α and IL-1β were quantified using ELISA kits (Abcam, ab208348, ab100768). The cell pellet was used for neutrophil counting, with neutrophil numbers per mL of BALF calculated.

## RNA extraction and qPCR analysis
Total RNA was extracted from cells or lung tissue using TRIzol (Invitrogen, 15596026), and its quality was assessed by measuring the OD260/280 ratio (1.8–2.0). cDNA synthesis was carried out using the PrimeScript RT Kit (Takara, RR047A), and quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche, 04913914001). The primers used for qPCR were as follows: CEBPA: Forward: 5’-AGCTCGTAGGTGAATGCT-3’; Reverse: 5’-GCGGTCATTGTCACTGG-3’.
SIRT4: Forward: 5’-CTTCTCCTGTTGCGTGAT-3’; Reverse: 5’-AGTGGCTCAGGTCAACAT-3’. NLRP3, Forward: GACCAGCCA GAGTGGAATGA; Reverse: CTTCAAGGCTGTCCTCCTGG. β-actin served as the internal control.

## Western blot analysis
For protein extraction, cells or lung tissue were homogenized in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) containing PMSF (Beyotime, ST506). Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA kit (Thermo Fisher, 23225). Thirty micrograms of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010), and probed with primary antibodies: CEBPA (Abcam, ab15048, 1:1000), SIRT4 (Abcam, ab10140, 1:1000), and GAPDH (Abcam, ab8245, 1:5000). HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling, 7074 and 7076, 1:5000) and ECL reagent (Thermo Fisher, 32106) were used for detection. Band intensities were analyzed with ImageJ software. The original scanned gels were shown in Supplementary Fig. 3.

## Luciferase reporter assay
Plasmids containing different fragments of the SIRT4 promoter (P1–P5) and their mutant versions (Mute-P1–Mute-P5) were constructed by Sangon Biotech. These plasmids were co-transfected with CEBPA overexpression plasmids into HEK293T cells. After 48 h, dual-luciferase reporter assays (Promega, E1910) were performed, with Renilla luciferase as the internal control. To further verify whether other sites are functional, we have tested the mRNA levels of CEBPA and SIRT4 under LPS treatment using site-directed mutagenesis against these binding sites. The results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 4. The result showed that only in the Mute-P2 group, the mRNA level of SIRT4 wasn’t significantly changed under LPS treatment.

## Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)
For EMSA, wild-type and mutant biotin-labeled probes (Sangon Biotech) were used with the Thermo Fisher EMSA Kit (20148). Nuclear proteins from A549 cells were extracted and quantified using BCA. Probes were incubated with nuclear proteins in a 20 µL reaction (final concentration 20 nM). Specificity was validated by using mutant probes, cold competition probes (100-fold excess of unlabeled probes), and antibody supershift assays (anti-CEBPA antibody). Samples underwent 6% non-denaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, transferred onto nylon membranes, and detected via streptavidin-HRP chemiluminescence. Binding intensity was analyzed using Image Lab software, with three independent experiments performed.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
For ChIP, A549 cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde (Millipore, catalog number: 17–10086) to crosslink proteins to DNA, followed by neutralization with glycine. Chromatin was fragmented to 200–500 bp by sonication and immunoprecipitated with an anti-CEBPA antibody (Abcam, catalog number: ab15048) or non-specific IgG as a negative control. After washing, DNA was recovered and analyzed by qPCR for enrichment of specific SIRT4 promoter fragments. Input DNA was used as a reference standard, and enrichment was evaluated by calculating the percentage of input (% input).

## Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
Proteins were extracted from A549 cells using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were incubated at 4 °C for 2 h with anti-CEBPA (Abcam, ab15048) or anti-SIRT4 (Abcam, ab10140) antibodies. Protein A/G magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher, 88802) were added to capture the protein complexes, followed by a 1-hour incubation. Unbound proteins were removed by washing the beads, and the complexes were eluted. Protein samples were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Western blot analysis was performed with anti-CEBPA or anti-SIRT4 antibodies to confirm interactions.

## CCK-8 cell viability assay
Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 kit (Beyotime, C0038). A549 cells were seeded at 5 × 103 cells per well in 96-well plates and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 until attachment. Cells were treated for 24 to 48 h, followed by the addition of 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent per well. After 2 h of incubation at 37 °C in the dark, absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a BioTek microplate reader. Blank controls (medium + CCK-8) and negative controls (untreated or non-transfected cells) were included to normalize absorbance for relative viability.

## Flow cytometry for apoptosis detection
For apoptosis analysis, cells were washed with PBS (Gibco, 10010023) and stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI (BD Biosciences, 556547) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After a 15-minute incubation in the dark, apoptosis was assessed using flow cytometry (BD FACSCalibur). The proportions of early (Annexin V+/PI-) and late (Annexin V+/PI+) apoptotic cells were calculated to determine the overall apoptosis rate. The original flow cytometry results were shown in Supplementary Material 5.

## Data statistics and analysis
Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experiments were performed with at least n = 6 biological replicates (derived from three independent experiments) unless otherwise stated. Differences between groups were analyzed using one-way ANOVA with post-hoc tests, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. The use of one-way ANOVA with post-hoc tests in this study is justified because the experimental design consistently involves comparing multiple independent treatment groups across continuous numerical data. The post-hoc tests are essential and correctly employed to identify which specific group pairs differ significantly from each other. GraphPad Prism 9 was used for data analysis and visualization.

## Results

## CEBPA and SIRT4 are significantly expressed in LPS-treated type II alveolar cells
To explore the potential relationship between CEBPA and SIRT4, transcriptomic correlation analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between the expression of CEBPA and SIRT4 (R = 0.41, p = 5.6 × 10–13, Fig. A), indicating a possible regulatory interaction. Western blot analysis under LPS treatment showed a dose-dependent increase in both CEBPA and SIRT4 protein levels, with maximal expression at 200 µg/mL LPS (Fig. B,C). It is noted that this concentration is higher than typical physiological levels but was used here in initial dose-screening to establish a robust injury model in vitro, consistent with approaches in some previous studies – . Similarly, qPCR analysis demonstrated a dose-dependent upregulation of CEBPA and SIRT4 mRNA, suggesting that LPS stimulation enhances the expression of these genes at both the transcriptional and translational levels (Fig. D). Additionally, transcription factor binding site prediction analysis identified multiple high-confidence CEBPA binding sites within the SIRT4 genomic region, further supporting the hypothesis that CEBPA may directly regulate SIRT4 transcription (Fig. E). Collectively, these results suggest that LPS-induced upregulation of CEBPA may drive the expression of SIRT4, revealing a regulatory relationship between the two factors.

 

## Overexpression of SIRT4 and CEBPA promotes cell growth under LPS treatment
To investigate the impact of SIRT4 on cell growth under LPS treatment, changes in cell morphology and proliferation were examined. Microscopic observations (Fig.  A) showed that LPS treatment significantly reduced cell growth compared to the control. However, SIRT4 overexpression (LPS + OE-SIRT4) resulted in a higher cell count compared to the LPS + OE-NC group. Conversely, SIRT4 knockdown (LPS + si-SIRT4) markedly decreased cell growth and number. Quantitative analysis (Fig.  B) confirmed that the LPS + OE-SIRT4 group exhibited a significantly greater cell count than the LPS + OE-NC group (p < 0.01), while the LPS + si-SIRT4 group had fewer cells than the LPS + si-NC group (p < 0.05). Cell viability assays (Fig.  C) indicated that SIRT4 overexpression significantly enhanced cell viability under LPS treatment, with the effect becoming more pronounced over time, whereas SIRT4 knockdown significantly reduced viability (p < 0.01). These results suggest that SIRT4 overexpression mitigates LPS-induced growth inhibition, whereas SIRT4 knockdown exacerbates this effect. Further experiments (Fig.  D) showed that LPS treatment inhibited cell growth, but CEBPA overexpression (LPS + OE-CEBPA) reversed this effect, increasing cell numbers compared to the LPS + OE-NC group. Conversely, CEBPA knockdown (LPS + si-CEBPA) further reduced cell density, highlighting its inhibitory role. Cell count analysis (Fig.  E) confirmed that CEBPA overexpression significantly promoted cell proliferation under LPS treatment (p < 0.01), whereas CEBPA knockdown resulted in significantly reduced proliferation (p < 0.01). Cell viability assays (Fig.  F) further demonstrated that CEBPA overexpression significantly improved cell viability under LPS exposure, while CEBPA knockdown significantly decreased viability (p < 0.01). These results suggest that CEBPA protects against LPS-induced growth inhibition and promotes cell proliferation. The NLRP3 mRNA level and ROS measurement was performed to link SIRT4 activation to downstream anti-inflammatory effects. The results are added in Fig.  G–I, which demonstrate that both SIRT4 and CEBPA overexpression suppressed the activation of NLRP3 and the accumulation of ROS, while SIRT4 and CEBPA knockdown significantly promoted the activation of NLRP3 and the accumulation of ROS.

 

## Protective effects of SIRT4 and CEBPA on ALI
To determine the time-dependent changes in CEBPA and SIRT4 expression, we performed kinetic analyses in CLP-induced septic mice. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2, CEBPA mRNA levels were significantly elevated as early as 3–6 h after LPS treatment or CLP induction, with peak expression observed at 12–24 h. SIRT4 expression followed a similar pattern but with a slight delay, reaching its maximum at 24 h. Both genes showed a decline in expression by 48 h. These results indicate that CEBPA and SIRT4 are rapidly activated in response to septic injury, with CEBPA induction preceding that of SIRT4, supporting its role as an upstream regulator. The role of SIRT4 in sepsis-induced ALI was evaluated using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fig.  A). In the control group, lung tissue exhibited intact alveolar structures. In contrast, the sepsis-treated group (ALI) displayed severe damage, characterized by smaller alveolar spaces and increased inflammatory cell infiltration. Overexpression of SIRT4 (ALI + OE-SIRT4) mitigated lung injury, restoring alveolar structure and reducing inflammatory cell infiltration compared to the ALI + OE-NC group. In the SIRT4 knockdown group (ALI + si-SIRT4), lung damage was exacerbated, with more inflammatory cells present. Quantitative analysis (Fig.  B) revealed a significantly lower lung injury score in the SIRT4 overexpression group compared to the ALI + OE-NC group (p < 0.01), whereas the SIRT4 knockdown group exhibited a significantly higher injury score than the ALI + si-NC group (p < 0.01). Lung edema analysis (Fig.  C) demonstrated that SIRT4 overexpression reduced the W/D ratio (p < 0.05), while knockdown resulted in a significant increase (p < 0.05). These results emphasize the protective role of SIRT4 in mitigating sepsis-induced lung injury.
To further investigate the role of CEBPA in ALI, H&E staining was used to assess the effects of CEBPA on sepsis-induced lung injury (Fig.  D). Lung tissue in the normal control group appeared intact, while the sepsis-treated group exhibited significant damage and inflammatory infiltration. Overexpression of CEBPA (ALI + OE-CEBPA) significantly alleviated tissue damage and reduced inflammation compared to the empty vector control group (ALI + OE-NC). In contrast, CEBPA knockdown (ALI + si-CEBPA) exacerbated tissue destruction and inflammatory response. As shown in Fig.  E, CEBPA overexpression notably decreased the injury score compared to the ALI + OE-NC group (p < 0.01), while knockdown significantly increased the score relative to the ALI + si-NC group (p < 0.01). Similarly, the W/D ratio was significantly reduced by CEBPA overexpression (Fig.  F, p < 0.05), whereas knockdown led to a marked increase (p < 0.05). These results underscore the protective effects of CEBPA in sepsis-induced ALI, where its overexpression alleviates injury and knockdown exacerbates lung damage. Figure  G,H showed the mRNA levels of SIRT4 in the lung tissue, which was successfully increased by overexpression of CEBPA/SIRT4, but significantly decreased by CEBPA/SIRT4 knockdown.

 

## Regulatory role of CEBPA on SIRT4 promoter activity
To examine whether CEBPA directly binds to the SIRT4 promoter region, bioinformatics analysis predicted multiple potential CEBPA binding sites (P1–P5) within the SIRT4 promoter (Fig.  A). Dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed to validate these binding sites (Fig.  B). Under CEBPA overexpression conditions, luciferase activity at the P2 site was significantly increased (p < 0.01), while no significant changes were observed at other sites. Mutation of the P2 site abolished this activation, confirming that the P2 site is the primary CEBPA binding site. EMSA further confirmed CEBPA binding to the P2 site (Fig.  C). The P2 probe formed a distinct shifted band with nuclear proteins, which further shifted upon the addition of a CEBPA antibody, forming a super-shifted band. In contrast, the mutated P2 probe failed to bind nuclear proteins, demonstrating specific binding of CEBPA to the P2 site. Co-IP experiments (Fig.  D) showed a direct interaction between CEBPA and SIRT4. Co-immunoprecipitation of SIRT4 with a CEBPA antibody and CEBPA with a SIRT4 antibody was observed, whereas the IgG control exhibited no interaction. To investigate the impact of LPS treatment on CEBPA-SIRT4 binding, Co-IP was performed (Fig.  E), revealing that LPS treatment significantly enhanced the binding between CEBPA and SIRT4, suggesting that inflammatory conditions promote their interaction. Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays further confirmed the binding of CEBPA to the SIRT4 promoter (Fig.  F). Compared to the IgG control, CEBPA antibody enriched fragments from the P2 site (p < 0.01), validating the direct binding of CEBPA to this region. Additionally, dual-luciferase reporter assays demonstrated significantly higher luciferase activity in cells transfected with the SIRT4 promoter fragment (pGL3-SIRT4-Luc) compared to the empty vector control (pGL3-Luc, p < 0.01), indicating that CEBPA activates SIRT4 promoter activity (Fig.  G). Knockdown and overexpression experiments (Fig.  H) further confirmed that CEBPA positively regulates SIRT4 promoter activity, with overexpression increasing activity (p < 0.01) and knockdown (siRNA-1 and siRNA-2) significantly reducing it (p < 0.01).

 

## Effects of CEBPA and SIRT4 on cell viability after LPS treatment
The impact of CEBPA and SIRT4 on cell viability under LPS treatment was evaluated through morphological assessment and CCK-8 assays (Figs.  A–C). Microscopic analysis (upper panel of Fig.  A) revealed substantial reductions in cell numbers and morphological damage in the LPS-treated group (LPS + NC-plasmid). In contrast, CEBPA overexpression (LPS + CEBPA-plasmid) partially restored cell numbers and improved morphology. Both the untreated control (Control) and the CEBPA plasmid-only group (CEBPA-plasmid) displayed normal morphology, with no significant damage. The CCK-8 assay (Fig.  B) confirmed that CEBPA overexpression significantly enhanced cell viability (p < 0.01) compared to the LPS + NC-plasmid group, indicating its protective role against LPS-induced damage. Transfection with the CEBPA plasmid alone had no substantial effect on cell viability, with results comparable to the control groups (NC-plasmid and Control). Microscopic images (lower panel of Fig.  A) further confirmed a notable reduction in cell numbers and visible damage in the LPS-treated group (LPS + NC-plasmid), whereas SIRT4 overexpression (LPS + SIRT4-plasmid) significantly increased cell numbers and restored cell morphology. Both the untreated control (Control) and the SIRT4 plasmid-only group (SIRT4-plasmid) exhibited healthy, normal cells. The CCK-8 assay (Fig.  C) indicated a significant increase in cell viability in the LPS + SIRT4-plasmid group (p < 0.01) compared to the LPS + NC-plasmid group, suggesting that SIRT4 overexpression mitigates LPS-induced cellular damage. Similar to CEBPA, transfection with the SIRT4 plasmid alone had no significant impact on cell viability, with results matching those of the control groups (NC-plasmid and Control).

 

## Effects of CEBPA and SIRT4 on LPS-induced cell apoptosis
The roles of CEBPA and SIRT4 in LPS-induced apoptosis were investigated using flow cytometry (Fig.  A) and apoptosis rate analysis (Fig.  B,C). Flow cytometry (upper panel of Fig.  A) showed a marked increase in early (lower right quadrant) and late (upper right quadrant) apoptotic cells in the LPS + NC-plasmid group. CEBPA overexpression (LPS + CEBPA-plasmid) significantly reduced apoptotic cell numbers. The untreated control (Control) and CEBPA plasmid-only groups (CEBPA-plasmid) displayed low apoptosis rates, similar to the NC-plasmid group. Quantitative analysis (Fig.  B) confirmed that CEBPA overexpression substantially reduced the apoptosis rate (p < 0.01) compared to the LPS + NC-plasmid group, suggesting a protective effect against LPS-induced apoptosis. Flow cytometry results (lower panel of Fig.  A) showed a notable reduction in both early and late apoptotic cells in the SIRT4 overexpression group (LPS + SIRT4-plasmid) compared to the LPS + NC-plasmid group. The untreated control (Control) and SIRT4 plasmid-only groups (SIRT4-plasmid) exhibited minimal apoptosis, similar to the NC-plasmid group. Quantitative apoptosis analysis (Fig.  C) demonstrated that SIRT4 overexpression significantly reduced the apoptosis rate (p < 0.01) in LPS-treated cells, further supporting its role in protecting against LPS-induced apoptosis.

 

## Impact of SIRT4 knockdown on CEBPA-mediated protective effects
The mechanism by which SIRT4 modulates CEBPA’s protective effects was investigated using microscopy (Fig.  A), flow cytometry (Fig.  B), CCK-8 assays (Fig.  C), and apoptosis rate analysis (Fig.  D). Microscopic images (Fig.  A) revealed a marked reduction in cell numbers and morphological abnormalities in the LPS + NC-plasmid group. CEBPA overexpression (LPS + CEBPA-plasmid) increased cell numbers and restored morphology, while SIRT4 knockdown (LPS + CEBPA-plasmid + si-SIRT4) compromised CEBPA’s protective effect, resulting in decreased cell numbers and worsened morphological damage. SIRT4 knockdown alone (si-SIRT4 group) also led to significant cellular damage. CCK-8 assays (Fig.  C) showed that cell viability was significantly enhanced (p < 0.01) in the LPS + CEBPA-plasmid group compared to the LPS + NC-plasmid group. However, SIRT4 knockdown (LPS + CEBPA-plasmid + si-SIRT4) markedly decreased viability (p < 0.01), reducing it to levels comparable to the LPS + NC-plasmid group. Furthermore, SIRT4 knockdown alone (si-SIRT4 group) also reduced cell viability, underscoring the exacerbating effect of SIRT4 deficiency on cellular damage. Flow cytometry (Fig.  B) revealed a significant increase in apoptosis in the LPS + NC-plasmid group. CEBPA overexpression (LPS + CEBPA-plasmid) reduced apoptosis (p < 0.01), while SIRT4 knockdown (LPS + CEBPA-plasmid + si-SIRT4) significantly enhanced apoptosis, approaching the levels observed in the LPS + NC-plasmid group (p < 0.01). Knockdown of SIRT4 alone (si-SIRT4 group) also significantly increased apoptosis (p < 0.01), indicating that SIRT4 deficiency negates the anti-apoptotic effects of CEBPA, thereby exacerbating cell apoptosis.

 

## Effect of SIRT4 on the protective role of CEBPA in alleviating acute lung injury (ALI)
SIRT4’s role in modulating CEBPA’s protective effects in sepsis-induced ALI was assessed through histopathology, lung injury scoring, W/D ratio, and inflammatory marker analysis in BALF. H&E staining (Fig.  A) revealed normal lung tissue with clear alveolar spaces and minimal inflammation in the control group. The ALI + NC-plasmid group exhibited severe alveolar damage, inflammatory cell infiltration, and pulmonary edema. In contrast, CEBPA overexpression (ALI + CEBPA-plasmid) significantly improved lung structure, restoring alveolar integrity and reducing inflammation. However, SIRT4 knockdown (ALI + CEBPA-plasmid + si-SIRT4) diminished CEBPA’s protective effects, leading to more pronounced lung damage and inflammation. SIRT4 knockdown alone (si-SIRT4 group) further exacerbated lung injury. Quantitative analysis of lung injury parameters demonstrated that CEBPA overexpression markedly improved outcomes. Lung injury scores (Fig.  B) and W/D ratios (Fig.  C) were significantly reduced in the CEBPA overexpression group compared to the ALI + NC-plasmid group (p < 0.01), while SIRT4 knockdown partially reversed these protective effects, resulting in elevated injury scores and W/D ratios (p < 0.01). SIRT4 knockdown worsened these parameters even further (p < 0.01).
LDH activity (Fig.  D) and neutrophil counts (Fig.  E) in BALF were significantly lower in the CEBPA overexpression group (p < 0.01). These improvements were attenuated by SIRT4 knockdown (p < 0.01), and independent SIRT4 knockdown led to even greater increases in both LDH activity and neutrophil counts (p < 0.01). Pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β (Fig.  F) and TNF-α (Fig.  G), were also significantly reduced in the CEBPA overexpression group (p < 0.01). However, SIRT4 knockdown weakened these anti-inflammatory effects, resulting in elevated cytokine levels (p < 0.01). These results highlight that SIRT4 is critical for enhancing CEBPA’s protective effects in sepsis-induced ALI. SIRT4 depletion impairs CEBPA’s ability to protect lung tissue and reduce inflammation, emphasizing the importance of the CEBPA-SIRT4 interaction in mitigating lung injury.

 

## Discussion
This study investigates the roles of CEBPA and SIRT4 in inflammation and ALI induced by sepsis in mouse models, employing both in vitro and in vivo assays. LPS and sepsis exposure significantly upregulates the gene expression and protein levels of CEBPA and SIRT4, with a strong correlation between their expressions. Elevating the levels of either CEBPA or SIRT4 offers protective effects against LPS-induced cellular damage by enhancing cell survival, reducing apoptosis, and mitigating tissue inflammation in the mouse ALI model. Conversely, downregulating either gene exacerbates sepsis-induced injury and diminishes the protective responses. Further investigations using bioinformatics, luciferase reporter assays, and EMSA revealed that CEBPA directly binds to the promoter region of SIRT4, promoting its expression. This interaction amplifies their collective protective effects, shielding cells and lung tissue from LPS-induced damage. These findings provide new insights into the CEBPA-SIRT4 interaction and its critical role in inflammation and tissue injury, suggesting potential targets for therapeutic strategies aimed at treating ALI and similar conditions. This study is the first to establish and validate a functional link between the transcription factor CEBPA and the mitochondrial sirtuin SIRT4 in the context of sepsis-induced ALI.
CEBPA is a pivotal transcription factor regulating cell growth, differentiation, and metabolism. Initial research focused on its role in adipogenesis and leukemia differentiation , . Recent studies have highlighted its involvement in inflammation and tissue repair. For example, Tan et al. elucidated a direct mechanistic connection among CEBPA repression, compromised AT2 cell identity, disrupted tissue homeostasis, and the development of lung fibrosis. Restoration of CEBPA expression in lung organoids and in vivo during experimental lung fibrosis reversed phenotypes induced by CEBPA deficiency . Alveolar type 2 (AT2) cells employ the cell-type -specific TF CEBPA to constrain their plasticity during neonatal and mature phases. However, upon respiratory virus infection, these cells reactivate their developmental plasticity, which may be crucial for tissue repair and response to the pathological challenge . Our study extends these findings by showing that CEBPA overexpression significantly protects against ALI, enhancing cell survival, reducing cell death, and alleviating sepsis-induced inflammatory cell infiltration and alveolar damage in mouse models. These results underscore the importance of CEBPA in mitigating acute inflammatory responses and lung tissue injury.
Historically, CEBPA’s functions have been attributed to its established regulatory mechanisms, including its bZIP domain for DNA binding . However, our study uncovers a novel mechanism whereby CEBPA directly activates SIRT4 by binding to its promoter at the P2 site. Bioinformatics analysis, dual-luciferase reporter assays, and EMSA confirm that CEBPA upregulates SIRT4 expression. This discovery not only broadens the scope of CEBPA’s biological roles but also introduces new avenues for investigating its involvement in inflammation-related conditions. The observed temporal dynamics of CEBPA and SIRT4 expression in our septic mouse model (Supplementary Fig. 2), where CEBPA induction precedes that of SIRT4, provides strong in vivo correlative support for CEBPA acting as an upstream regulator of SIRT4 transcription. An intriguing finding of this study is the physical interaction between CEBPA and SIRT4 proteins, as demonstrated by Co-IP (Fig. D,E). This is somewhat unexpected given the predominant nuclear localization of CEBPA and the mitochondrial localization of SIRT4. While this interaction could suggest a novel form of cross-compartmental communication, perhaps facilitated under cellular stress conditions that alter protein trafficking or through transient contact sites between organelles, it is more likely an indirect association mediated by an adaptor protein or occurs within a larger protein complex that co-precipitates under our experimental conditions. Future work employing subcellular fractionation coupled with immunoblotting or proximity ligation assays will be essential to validate this interaction and clarify its spatial context within the cell. Regardless, its enhancement upon LPS treatment suggests a functionally relevant association worthy of further exploration.
The sirtuin family (SIRT1–SIRT7) plays a pivotal role in regulating cellular metabolism, aging, and inflammation . While the functions of SIRT1, SIRT3, and SIRT6 in oxidative stress management, mitochondrial function, and genomic stability are well-documented, the role of SIRT4 remains less understood. Emerging evidence suggests that SIRT4 modulates glutaminase activity, regulates lipid metabolism, and influences insulin secretion , . Additionally, SIRT4 possesses anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective properties, including inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome activation and reduction of ROS , . Despite these insights, SIRT4’s function in ALI has yet to be thoroughly explored. The time-course analysis revealed that CEBPA and SIRT4 are upregulated early during sepsis progression (within 3–6 h), with peak expression at 12–24 h. This rapid activation suggests that the CEBPA-SIRT4 axis may function as part of the immediate early response to inflammatory stress, potentially aimed at limiting initial damage and promoting cellular survival. The subsequent decline in their expression at later time points (48 h) may reflect the resolution of inflammation or the onset of feedback mechanisms. These temporal dynamics provide additional support for the protective role of CEBPA and SIRT4 in the early phases of sepsis-induced ALI. While our data establish a clear protective role for SIRT4 and link it to the suppression of NLRP3 and ROS (Fig. G−I), the precise molecular mechanisms downstream of SIRT4 activation in ALI warrant further investigation. SIRT4 is a multifunctional mitochondrial enzyme with reported roles in regulating glutamine metabolism, fatty acid oxidation, and mitochondrial dynamics , , . It is plausible that its protective effects are mediated through the modulation of mitochondrial function. For instance, SIRT4-dependent demalonylation or ADP-ribosylation of key metabolic enzymes could alter mitochondrial respiration, ATP production, and redox balance, thereby influencing cellular health and inflammatory responses under stress. Future studies employing techniques such as Seahorse Analyzer to assess mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR), alongside targeted metabolomics and assessment of the acetylation/malonylation status of specific SIRT4 substrates, will be crucial to delineate the functional metabolic pathways through which the CEBPA-SIRT4 axis confers protection in ALI.
This study is the first to demonstrate and validate the protective role of SIRT4 in ALI. Our results show that increasing SIRT4 levels significantly enhances cell survival, reduces apoptosis, and alleviates key features of lung injury, including alveolar exudation, neutrophil infiltration, and elevated injury scores following sepsis exposure. These findings align with previous studies that highlight the anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects of SIRT4 in other disease models, such as acute liver failure . Both siRNA knockdown and overexpression models provide compelling evidence that inhibiting SIRT4 exacerbates cellular damage, while its overexpression offers protective benefits. This study further underscores the pivotal role of SIRT4 in modulating acute inflammatory responses and highlights its potential as a therapeutic target in inflammation-related diseases.
Inflammatory injury typically arises from intricate interactions among multiple signaling networks rather than being driven by a single gene or pathway. Our findings underscore the significance of the CEBPA-SIRT4 axis, where CEBPA-mediated transcriptional activation of SIRT4 enhances cellular resistance to sepsis-induced inflammation, thereby attenuating the inflammatory response. Previous studies have suggested that CEBPA also interacts with key inflammatory mediators, including NF-κB and AP-1, while SIRT4 may engage in synergistic or competitive interactions with pathways such as SIRT3 and AMPK in regulating metabolism and inflammation . For example, during ischemia-reperfusion injury, both SIRT4 and SIRT3 collaborate to suppress mitochondrial superoxide production, thereby reducing epithelial apoptosis and mitigating tissue damage . These observations imply that the CEBPA-SIRT4 axis operates within a larger network of regulatory pathways that are essential for managing inflammation and tissue injury. In this study, SIRT4 knockdown compromised CEBPA’s protective effects in alveolar epithelial cells and macrophages, highlighting its central role. However, the contribution of SIRT3 in inflammatory processes should not be underestimated, as it has been shown to reduce inflammation and oxidative stress in sepsis models . Further investigation is required to elucidate how the CEBPA-SIRT4 axis integrates with other molecular pathways in the context of lung injury. Furthermore, we should note that the CEBPA-SIRT4 crosstalk may not be specific to alveolar epithelial cells. The interplay between CEBPA, SIRT4, and immune cells (neutrophils, macrophages) is an emerging research area critical for understanding immune responses and therapeutic development. CEBPA regulates myeloid cell differentiation/function; SIRT4 modulates metabolism and immune cell activity. A study on artificial neutrophil-mediated CEBPA-saRNA delivery shows targeting CEBPA can modulate immune responses in ALI/ARDS . It demonstrated that CEBPA activation via this delivery induced M2 macrophage polarization-linked to anti-inflammatory effects that ameliorate ALI/ARDS pathology-highlighting CEBPA’s therapeutic potential in inflammatory diseases. SIRT4’s role in immune function is revealed through its association with microvascular/immune cell infiltration in HCC . Its expression correlates with CD4+ T cells, NK cells, and EMT markers, indicating involvement in tumor microenvironment immune regulation and cancer progression, underscoring its potential as a cancer therapeutic target.
While this study provides valuable mechanistic insights, several future research directions remain. Firstly, our investigation focused on the sepsis-induced ALI model, yet the varied etiologies of clinical ALI/ARDS necessitate testing the broader applicability of the CEBPA-SIRT4 axis in models of viral pneumonia, radiation injury, or hyperoxia. Secondly, although CEBPA’s transcriptional activation of SIRT4 has been demonstrated, the precise mechanisms by which SIRT4 modulates inflammation remain unclear, though they are speculated to involve ROS inhibition and suppression of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Further studies using transcriptomics, proteomics, and molecular interaction approaches are essential to elucidate these mechanisms , . The third limitation of this study is the exclusive use of male mice, which precludes the assessment of potential sex-specific differences in the roles of CEBPA and SIRT4 in sepsis-induced acute lung injury. Given the well-documented influence of sex hormones and genetic factors on immune and inflammatory responses, future studies should include both male and female animal models to evaluate whether the protective effects of CEBPA and SIRT4 are sex-dependent. Such investigations would not only enhance the translational relevance of these findings but may also reveal important mechanistic insights into sexually dimorphic pathways in ALI pathogenesis and treatment. Fourthly, preclinical studies in large animal models are critical for assessing the therapeutic potential of targeting CEBPA and SIRT4 in diverse genetic and immune contexts. From a translational perspective, while our genetic overexpression approaches robustly establish proof-of-concept, therapeutic applications would benefit from the development of pharmacological agents, such as small-molecule SIRT4 activators or CEBPA-stabilizing compounds. The localized nature of ALI/ARDS makes inhaled delivery an attractive strategy for such future therapeutics. Lastly, although our data from cell and animal models are robust, the clinical relevance of our findings would be further strengthened by human tissue analysis-a recognized challenge in this field. Future Directions include mining publicly available transcriptomic databases (e.g., GEO Datasets) for rare cohorts containing lung tissue from early ARDS cases or transplant-associated ARDS; investigating the CEBPA-SIRT4 axis in accessible biofluids such as bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) cells, supernatants, or circulating exosomes to correlate expression levels with disease severity or outcomes; and utilizing archived post-mortem lung tissue from ARDS patients for future immunohistochemical studies.
Finally, our study has several limitations. The use of plasmid-based overexpression, while mechanistically informative, may not fully recapitulate endogenous regulation. The downstream mechanisms of SIRT4 and the nature of the CEBPA-SIRT4 protein interaction require further elucidation. Furthermore, the exclusive use of male mice limits insights into potential sex-specific differences in the CEBPA-SIRT4 axis, an important consideration for future research.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, this study, integrating existing literature and systematic experimental analysis, enhances our understanding of the pivotal protective roles of CEBPA and SIRT4 in an sepsis-induced acute lung injury model. At the molecular level, CEBPA directly binds to the SIRT4 promoter, thereby enhancing its transcription and protein expression. This mechanism promotes cell survival, inhibits excessive apoptosis, and improves lung tissue structure and function in inflammatory and injury contexts. The identified CEBPA-SIRT4 axis provides new insights into the underlying mechanisms of acute lung injury and offers potential therapeutic targets, complementing existing research on CEBPA and the SIRT protein family in inflammation-related conditions. The therapeutic potential of targeting the CEBPA-SIRT4 axis warrants exploration. Although our study utilized genetic approaches, future research should investigate pharmacological strategies. For example, the development or repurposing of small molecule SIRT4 activators could offer a translational avenue to harness the protective effects observed in our overexpression models. Similarly, identifying compounds that enhance CEBPA expression or transcriptional activity-perhaps through epigenetic modulation or stabilization of the protein-could prove beneficial. Given the localized nature of ALI/ARDS, inhaled delivery of such therapeutics could maximize efficacy while reducing systemic exposure. Testing these interventions in complementary models of lung injury will be essential to evaluate their clinical potential as novel adjunctive therapies for mitigating inflammatory lung damage.
