However, the interactions between THM-CDs and intracellular proteins have yet to be fully elucidated. Herein, inspired by the traditional vinegar-processing of Artemisia argyi (A. argyi), acetic acid-treated Artemisia argyi-derived carbon dots (AA-CDs) demonstrate excellent resistance to iron death via efficient ROS scavenging and Fe2+ chelating. Collectively, these findings establish an HSPA5-GPX4 axis-mediated mechanism through which AA-CDs mitigate OA progression, providing novel insights into the bioactivity mechanisms of herbal-derived carbon dots and supporting their development as next-generation ferroptosis inhibitors for OA therapy.

## Introduction
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent degenerative joint disorder characterized by articular cartilage deterioration, affecting approximately 7% of the global population and imposing substantial socioeconomic burdens on both families and society [ , ]. Current clinical management primarily relies on palliative approaches including oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and physical therapy, while effective disease-modifying drugs remain unavailable [ , ]. A hallmark of OA progression involves chondrocyte death and degradation of the extracellular matrix (ECM) [ ]. Recently, numerous studies have suggested that ferroptosis might contribute to the pathology of osteoarthritis (OA) [ , , – ]. During the progression of OA, iron metabolism disorders, heightened lipid peroxidation, and mitochondrial dysfunction are significantly aggravated. Clinical studies have shown that glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a key negative regulator of ferroptosis, has a significant positive correlation between its expression level and cartilage degradation [ , – ]. Consequently, targeting chondrocyte ferroptosis may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for developing novel disease-modifying OA treatments (Scheme ). 
It has been demonstrated that ferroptosis inhibitors, including deferoxamine and ferrostatin-1, can mitigate the advancement of associated diseases by preventing ferroptosis [ – ]. Nonetheless, the use of these inhibitors in clinical settings is greatly restricted because of their brief half-life and inadequate biological stability. Recent studies have shown that traditional herbal medicine-derived carbon dots (THM-CDs) hold potential for regulating ferroptosis by inhibiting oxidative stress and chelating iron ions [ , ]. THM-CDs, synthesized using traditional herbal medicines as carbon sources, have become a focal point in the biomedical field owing to their excellent biocompatibility and pharmacological benefits [ – ]. Fundamentally, bioactive components in herbal medicines, such as polysaccharides, flavonoids, and phenolic are retained on the surface of THM-CDs due to incomplete carbonization [ , , ]. Recent investigations have revealed that THM-CDs may be one of the bioactive components for the medicinal effects of traditional herbal medicine. Currently, numerous studies have reported their roles in hemostasis, anti-inflammation, antioxidant activity, and anti-tumor effects [ – ]. 
The surface of THM-CDs contains a wealth of oxygen-based functional groups, including carbonyl, carboxyl, and hydroxyl groups, which provide outstanding water solubility and also offer essential catalytic sites needed for THM-CDs, such as superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activity [ – ]. Notably, these oxygen-rich groups on the surface can attach to Fe2+/Fe³⁺, thereby effectively inhibiting intracellular iron overload, and thus exerts an effective inhibitory effect on intracellular iron overload [ ]. Furthermore, THM-CDs also possess key advantages like high biological stability and prolonged half-life, which makes them highly promising candidates for developing a new class of ferroptosis inhibitors. Artemisia argyi (A. argyi), a traditional Chinese herbal medicine, contains various bioactive components including volatile oils, flavonoids, and tannins, and possesses anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory effects [ , ]. Therefore, A. argyi-derived carbon dots synthesized using as a precursor exhibit significant antioxidant activity and iron ion chelating ability, showing great potential in inhibiting ferroptosis.
For more than 2000 years, A. argyi and its vinegar-processed derivatives have been utilized in Chinese clinical practice, offering therapeutic benefits like enhancing blood circulation to eliminate blood stasis and alleviating pain [ ]. Vinegar processing can significantly enhance the analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects of A. argyi. In the vinegar-processing procedure, acetic acid is the main component of vinegar, and studies have shown that acetic acid can regulate the pharmacological activity of Chinese herbal medicines. Recent research has demonstrated that acetic acid acts as a catalyst and reaction precursor in the synthesis of carbon dots, regulating their surface functional groups to enhance the luminescent properties of the dots [ , ]. For instance, acetyl-terminated carbon dots with tunable emission colors can be prepared via hydrothermal reaction of dithiosalicylic acid and acetic acid [ , ]. The weakly polar acetyl group exhibits cell membrane permeability, which can enhance the passive cellular transport of excessively hydrophilic small molecules. Therefore, acetic acid might boost the anti-ferroptosis capability of A. argyi-derived carbon dots (A-CDs) by altering their surface functional groups and facilitating their passive transport.
Here, inspired by vinegar-processed A. argyi, we used A. argyi as an active precursor, combined carbonization and hydrothermal methods to synthesize carbon dots that can effectively inhibit ferroptosis in chondrocytes during OA (Schematic 1). Systematic comparison between A-CDs and AA-CDs revealed that acetic acid can significantly increase the proportion of oxygen-containing functional groups on the surface carbon dots thereby enhancing their antioxidant activity and SOD-like enzyme activity. To unravel the underlying molecular mechanism, we adopted multi-omics approaches and discovered that AA-CDs can directly target and stabilize heat shock protein A5 (HSPA5). This effect further prevents the degradation of GPX4, a key regulatory factor of ferroptosis. In vitro studies revealed that AA-CDs significantly effectively inhibit the degradation of the extracellular matrix in cartilage. More significantly, AA-CDs effectively alleviated cartilage erosion, osteophyte formation, and synovitis in a Sprague Dawley (SD) rat OA model. These results uncover a new mechanism through which AA-CDs mitigate OA and offer new insights on understanding the bioactivity mechanism of TCM-CDs.

## Results

## Synthesis and characterization of A-CDs and AA-CDs
Artemisia argyi-derived carbon dots (A-CDs) and acetic acid-treated Artemisia argyi-derived carbon dots (AA-CDs) were synthesized using pyrolysis and hydrothermal methods, respectively (Fig.  a) [ ]. Both A-CDs and AA-CDs exhibited uniformly distributed spherical particles with similar particle size, with average diameters of 2.66 ± 0.61 nm and 2.69 ± 0.54 nm, respectively (Fig.  b). A-CDs and AA-CDs exhibited a broad diffraction peak at 22° and a sharp peak at 44° in their X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns, corresponding to the (002) and (101) planes of graphitic carbon, respectively [ ]. These results confirmed that both AA-CDs and A-CDs have a graphene-like structure, and their surfaces are decorated with abundant functional groups on their surface. Raman spectroscopy further revealed that the intensity ratios of D-band/G-band ratio (1380 cm⁻¹/1596 cm⁻¹) for A-CDs and AA-CDs were found to be 0.81 and 0.86, respectively. These results implies that AA-CDs exhibit reduced surface defects and lower graphitization compared to A-CDs (Fig.  d) [ ]. 

 

We further investigated the optical characteristics of A-CDs and AA-CDs via UV-vis absorption and photoluminescence (PL) spectra. A-CDs exhibited a single absorption peak at 225 nm, whereas the absorption spectrum of AA-CDs featured two distinct peaks at 234 nm and 259 nm, respectively (Figure S2). This absorption behavior can be attributed to the n–π* electronic transitions of the C = O or C = N bonds on the surface of the carbon dots [ , ]. When the excitation wavelength was increased from 320 nm to 370 nm, the emission wavelengths of A-CDs and AA-CDs moved from 416 nm to 444 nm, demonstrating excitation-dependent fluorescence emission (Figure S3). Numerous studies have shown that carbon dots can bind with Fe3⁺/Fe2⁺ to form stable non-fluorescent complexes [ , ]. Thus, we further analyzed the fluorescence intensity of A-CDs and AA-CDs across various cationic (K+, Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Fe2+, Zn2+, Cu2+, Fe3+, Cr3+, Co3+, Al3+) (Figure S4a, S4b and S4c). Both A-CDs and AA-CDs experienced a significant drop in fluorescence intensity due to Fe³⁺ and Fe²⁺, whereas no obvious impact on their fluorescence intensity was observed with other cations. The fluorescence quenching intensity ratio (F₀/F) of both A-CDs and AA-CDs exhibited a good linear relationship with the concentration of Fe²⁺ (Figure S4d). Zeta potential analysis showed that after the addition of Fe²⁺, the surface potentials of A-CDs and AA-CDs increased from − 23.9 mV and − 29.4 mV to −4.6 mV and − 6.2 mV, respectively (Figure S4e). These findings collectively suggest that stable non-fluorescent complexes are formed between Fe²⁺ and A-CDs or AA-CDs.
Next, we investigate surface functional groups of A-CDs and AA-CDs using fourier transform infrared (FTIR), raman spectroscopy and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in Fig.  c, FTIR spectroscopy showed that A-CDs and AA-CDs exhibited a strong and broad peak at 3400 cm⁻¹, representing the stretching vibrations of O-H and N-H. Additionally, peaks at ≈ 1550 cm⁻¹ (assigned to C = O vibrations) and ≈ 1380 cm⁻¹ (assigned to C-N stretching vibrations) were observed for both A-CDs and AA-CDs. Notably, FTIR spectra of AA-CDs revealed a distinct C = O stretching vibration at 1700 cm⁻¹ and AA-CDs exhibited a new absorption peak at 259 nm, suggesting a possible red shift due to unsaturated carbonyl group formation [ ]. XPS survey spectra verified that both A-CDs and AA-CDs mainly consist of carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and oxygen (O) (Figure S5). The atomic percentages of each element in the two carbon quantum dots are as follows: 75.89% and 73.69% for C, 20.39% and 22.11% for O, and 3.72% and 4.2% for N, respectively. The above results demonstrate that acetic acid treatment significantly reduces the proportion of C while notably increasing the proportion of O. High-resolution C1s spectra further revealed that the proportion of C-C/C = C of A-CDs and AA-CDs is 74.2% and 62.7%, respectively, agreement with above result (Fig.  e). The proportion of the C = O of AA-CDs increased from 3.4% to 12.6%, and the O-C = O proportion of AA-CDs increased from 10.7% to 15.5%. The above results demonstrating that the significantly increased oxygen-containing functional groups in AA-CDs were primarily carbonyl and carboxyl groups. Interestingly, the proportions of the C-O peak in A-CDs and AA-CDs were 11.7% and 9.2%, respectively. This variation indicates that hydroxyl groups on the surface of AA-CDs have reacted with the carboxyl groups in acetic acid, thereby inducing a certain degree of acetylation modification on AA-CDs.
The oxygen-containing surface functional groups on carbon dots can effectively scavenge free radicals and serve as catalytic sites for SOD-like and POD-like nanozyme activities [ , ]. We further investigated the ROS scavenging ability of A-CDs and AA-CDs, which exhibited concentration-dependent scavenging capacities against 2,2’-ABTS and DPPH free radicals (Fig.  f and g, and S6). At a concentration of 100 mg/L, the ABTS free radical scavenging rates of A-CDs and AA-CDs were 82.6% and 51.3%, while their DPPH free radical scavenging rates were 53.4% and 31.6%, respectively. Furthermore, the EC50 values of A-CDs and AA-CDs for ABTS radical scavenging were 82.4 ± 5.5 µg/mL and 55.8 ± 5.6 µg/mL, while their EC50 values for DPPH radical scavenging were 99.1 ± 7.0 µg/mL and 59.4 ± 4.1 µg/mL, respectively. The total antioxidant capacity of A-CDs and AA-CDs showed that 200 µg/mL of A-CDs or AA-CDs corresponded to 1.3 mM or 2.2 mM FeSO4, respectively (Figure S6). The aforementioned results confirmed that both A-CDs and AA-CDs exhibit ROS scavenging ability, whereas AA-CDs demonstrate better performance in this regard. Superoxide anion (O₂⁻) and hydroxyl radical (·OH) are the main harmful reactive oxygen species in cells, thus we further assessed the scavenging ability of AA-CDs against O₂⁻ and ·OH using electron spin resonance (ESR) [ , ]. The results demonstrated that 100 µg/mL AA-CDs can effectively eliminate O₂⁻ and ·OH (Fig.  i and j). These findings indicated that AA-CDs can efficiently remove ROS, including O₂⁻ and ·OH.
Our previous results confirmed that AA-CDs possess a substantially higher number of surface carbonyl groups compared to A-CDs. Therefore, we further examined the POD-like and SOD-like nanozyme activities of A-CDs and AA-CDs. The results revealed that the SOD-like activities of A-CDs and AA-CDs were equivalent to 4582 U/mg and 9848 U/mg, respectively, and AA-CDs exhibited faster catalytic oxidation of TMB (Fig.  k and S8). To further investigate the role of oxygen-containing surface functional groups of AA-CDs in ROS scavenging ability and SOD-like nanozyme activities, 1, 3-propanesultone (PS) was used to deactivate carboxyl and hydroxyl groups (Figure S9). The results demonstrated that both the DPPH radical scavenging capacity and SOD-like enzymatic activity of AA-CDs were significantly reduced when hydroxyl and carboxyl groups were deactivated by PS. Following the hydrolysis of AA-CDs in NaOH solution, both the DPPH radical scavenging capacity and SOD-like enzymatic activity of AA-CDs were significantly increased. When the carbonyl group was reduced to a hydroxyl group, the DPPH radical scavenging capacity was significantly enhanced, while the SOD-like enzymatic activity was remarkably decreased. These results demonstrate that AA-CDs exhibit stronger POD-like and SOD-like nanozyme activities than A-CDs, which could be ascribed to their higher content of carbonyl and carboxyl groups (Fig.  l).

## AA-CDs ameliorated t-BHP-induced death of chondrocyte
We further explored the cytocompatibility of A-CDs and AA-CDs, as well as the uptake of these carbon dots by chondrocytes. MTS assay results showed that neither A-CDs nor AA-CDs at a concentration of 0 to 100 µg/mL exerted significant cytotoxicity on chondrocytes (Figure S10). Therefore, 10 µg/mL A-CDs or AA-CDs was selected for further experiments. It is known that acetyl groups possess membrane penetration ability, which can enhance the passive cellular transport of hydrophilic small molecules and a higher content of acetyl groups was appeared in AA-CDs. Based on this, we Cy5 labeled A-CDs and AA-CDs was used to analyze the uptake efficiency by chondrocytes. As shown in Fig.  a, A-CDs and AA-CDs enter into chondrocytes after 1 h of co-incubation, and the accumulation of both carbon dots in chondrocytes reached the maximum level after 4 h (Fig.  a and b). Notably, the cellular uptake of AA-CDs was significantly higher than that of A-CDs, and this difference is presumably associated with the acetylation modification of AA-CDs. The above results showed that A-CDs and AA-CDs have excellent cytocompatibility and chondrocyte internalization ability. The subcellular localization of AA-CDs in chondrocytes was further investigated in Fig.  c. Colocalization analysis revealed a significant colocalization between AA-CDs and mitochondria, with a Pearson’s correlation coefficient of 0.61. Mitochondria are the primary source of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and scavenging excessive ROS in mitochondria can effectively inhibit ferroptosis. Additionally, AA-CDs also exhibited obvious colocalization with the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), with a Pearson’s correlation coefficient of 0.51.

 

Oxidative stress leading to chondrocyte death plays a significant role in ferroptosis and the development of OA [ ]. Therefore, we further investigated the chondroprotection effects of A-CDs and AA-CDs under oxidative stress. Live/dead cell staining results indicated that A-CDs and AA-CDs significantly reduced the dead/live chondrocyte ratio by 54.4% and 88.7% compared to the tert-butyl hydroperoxide (t-BHP)-treated group, respectively (Fig.  d and e). Furthermore, MTS assay confirmed that A-CDs and AA-CDs significantly enhanced chondrocyte viability (Fig.  f), increasing it by 28.3% and 67.9%, respectively. AA-CDs exhibited a more pronounced ability to suppress the dead/live chondrocyte ratio and enhance chondrocyte viability. These results indicate that A-CDs or AA-CDs can significantly improve chondrocyte survival under oxidative stress. Additionally, we utilized DCFH-DA staining and flow cytometry to evaluate the capacity of A-CDs and AA-CDs to eliminate intracellular ROS in chondrocytes. Compared to the t-BHP group, A-CDs and AA-CDs significantly reduced the proportion of DCFH-positive chondrocytes by 26.2% and 62.2%, respectively (Fig.  g, h and i). These findings collectively indicate that both A-CDs and AA-CDs can enhance chondrocyte survival by suppressing intracellular ROS generation, with AA-CDs demonstrating superior efficacy.

## AA-CDs inhibit t-BHP-induced ferroptosis of chondrocyte
Recent studies have shown that iron overload serves as a critical factor to triggering ferroptosis in chondrocyte [ , , ]. Excessive accumulation of intracellular iron ions generates hydroxyl radicals, and then through catalyzing Fenton reaction. In addition, oxidative stress can also induce intracellular iron ion overload, thereby triggering ferroptosis [ ]. Our experiments have showed that A-CDs and AA-CDs possess excellent ferrous ion-chelating ability (Figure S4b). Furthermore, we employed the FerroOrange fluorescent staining and flow cytometry to investigate the effects of A-CDs and AA-CDs on intracellular ferrous ion levels in chondrocytes (Fig.  a, b and c). The result revealed that t-HBP treatment significantly elevated intracellular ferrous ion levels in chondrocytes, and the proportion of positive chondrocytes increased by 2.66 times. In contrast, administration of A-CDs or AA-CDs led to a marked reduction in intracellular ferrous ion levels, with the number of FerroOrange-positive chondrocytes decreasing by 30.8% and 55.5%, respectively. Although A-CDs and AA-CDs did not differ significantly in their ferrous ion-chelating ability, AA-CDs showed a higher clearance rate of intracellular ferrous ions in chondrocytes, which could be attributed to their superior chondrocyte internalization efficiency.

 

Mitochondrial are the main site of ROS generation, and mitochondrial dysfunction is one of the key hallmarks of ferroptosis [ , , ]. We further evaluated changes in mitochondrial morphology and function using Transmission electron microscope (TEM) and JC-1 staining. TEM results showed that t-BHP-treated chondrocytes exhibited typical ferroptosis characteristics, including mitochondrial shrinkage, reduced cristae structure (Fig.  d). Administration of A-CDs or AA-CDs effectively restored above phenotype. Interestingly, A-CDs or AA-CDs significantly inhibit the decrease in mitochondrial length of chondrocytes induced by t-BHP (Fig.  e). We further employed JC-1 fluorescence staining to investigate the effects of A-CDs and AA-CDs on mitochondrial membrane potential. As shown in Fig.  f, g and t-BHP treatment significantly decreased mitochondrial membrane potential (reduced red/green fluorescence intensity ratio), while AA-CDs intervention effectively restored the membrane potential. The above results collectively indicate that AA-CDs can restore mitochondrial homeostasis and inhibit chondrocyte ferroptosis induced by t-BHP.
We also investigated the expression levels of proteins related to ferroptosis, including GPX4, ferritin light chain 1 (FTL1), and divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1). The fluorescence intensity of GPX4 in chondrocytes decreases significantly after treatment with t-BHP, while A-CDs and AA-CDs increased noticeably following treatment with A-CDs and AA-CDs (Fig.  h). As shown in Fig.  i, A-CDs or AA-CDs significantly upregulated GPX4 expression while significantly suppressing FTL1 and DMT1 expression. Notably, compared with A-CDs, AA-CDs demonstrated a more significant upregulation of GPX4 expression. These results indicate that AA-CDs significantly inhibit chondrocyte ferroptosis by upregulating GPX4 protein expression.

## AA-CDs inhibit t-BHP-induced catabolism in chondrocytes
Previous studies have demonstrated that inhibiting ferroptosis in chondrocytes can alleviate chondrocyte inflammation and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation [ , , ]. We subsequently evaluated the effects of A-CDs and AA-CDs on chondrocyte ECM [ ]. As shown in Fig.  a and b, the grayscale values of safranin O and toluidine blue staining significantly reduced in t-BHP groups. While, the grayscale values in the A-CDs and AA-CDs groups were significantly increased by 19.2% and 39.2% compared to t-BHP groups, respectively. Furthermore, we examined the expression of anabolic (Collagen type II (COL2) and Aggrecan (ACAN)) and catabolic proteins (Matrix Metallopeptidase 13 (MMP13)) in chondrocytes. As shown in Fig.  c and d, both A-CDs and AA-CDs significantly upregulated the expression of COL2 and ACAN and significantly downregulating the expression of MMP13 compared to t-BHP -treated groups. Immunofluorescence results also revealed that the COL2 fluorescence intensity significantly increased in the A-CDs and AA-CDs groups compared to t-BHP -treated groups (Fig.  e and f). Notably, compared with A-CDs, AA-CDs significantly increased safranin O and toluidine blue staining intensity, significantly upregulated the expression of COL2 and ACAN, and downregulated the expression of MMP13. These findings indicate that both A-CDs and AA-CDs can significantly inhibit t-BHP-induced ECM degradation in chondrocytes, with AA-CDs demonstrating a more pronounced effect.

 

Furthermore, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis on AA-CDs-treated chondrocytes. As shown in Figs.  g and 998 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 581 upregulated and 417 downregulated genes exhibited after AA-CDs treatment. KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that these DEGs were mainly concentrated in PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, IL-17 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway and protein digestion and absorption (Fig.  h). GSEA enrichment analysis also demonstrated a significant negative correlation of TNF signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Table S2). GO enrichment analysis demonstrated that DEGs enriched in cell proliferation and cell migration, which suggests that that AA-CDs also have the potential to promote the proliferation and migration of chondrocytes under oxidative stress conditions (Figure S11). Predictably, GSEA enrichment analysis also demonstrated a significant negative correlation of ferroptosis (NES = −1.35, p.adjust = 0.014) (Fig.  i).

## AA-CDs targeting HSPA5 to inhibit chondrocyte ferroptosis
GPX4 has a significant positive correlation between its expression level and balance between anabolism and catabolism, which plays a pivotal role in OA progression [ ]. Clinical studies have shown that GPX4 has a significant positive correlation between its expression level and cartilage degradation [ , – ]. Our previous results have demonstrated that AA-CDs significantly upregulated GPX4 protein expression to inhibit ferroptosis, however no statistically significant difference was observed in GPX4 mRNA expression (Figure S12). To further explore the molecular mechanism of AA-CDs inhibiting chondrocyte ferroptosis, we employed the Drug Affinity Responsive Target Stability (DARTS) assay to identify potential direct target proteins of AA-CDs [ , ]. Coomassie brilliant blue staining results indicated that AA-CDs likely protected proteins within the 65–75 kDa (Fig.  a and b, and c). Mass spectrometry analysis of proteins in the 65–75 kDa was subsequently used to identified potential target of AA-CDs. As shown in Figs.  d and e and 143 significantly differentially proteins, including 48 upregulated and 95 downregulated proteins, was identified. Among upregulated proteins, Heat shock protein 70 kDa 5 (HSPA5) was encodes an ER-localized protein, and we have revealed significant colocalization between AA-CDs and the ER with a Pearson’s correlation coefficient of 0.51. Thus, HSPA5 was selected as a potential target protein for further validation.

 

Recent studies have showed that HSPA5, induced under endoplasmic reticulum stress conditions, is a negative regulator of ferroptosis. HSPA5 can bind to GPX4 and then inhibit GPX4 degradation, thereby suppressing ferroptosis [ – ]. Moreover, HSPA5 expression was notably reduced in the cartilage tissues of individuals with OA [ , ]. Notably, studies have demonstrated that HSPA5 overexpression in chondrocytes significantly upregulates GPX4 expression, thereby preventing extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation. These discoveries point to the substantial therapeutic possibilities of targeting HSPA5 in chondrocytes for OA therapy.
Western blot analysis of HSPA5 protein demonstrated that AA-CDs significantly inhibited HSPA5 degradation (Fig.  f). Furthermore, cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) results indicated that the binding strength between AA-CDs and HSPA5 showed a significantly higher Tm50 compared to PBS treatment (Fig.  g). Collectively, these findings suggest that AA-CDs specifically target the protein HSPA5. To validate this mechanism, we performed HSPA5 knockdown in chondrocytes using siRNA. The results showed that compared to AA-CDs treatment alone, the AA-CDs + siHSPA5 group exhibited significantly reduced GPX4 expression (Fig.  h). Additionally, siHSPA5 treatment led to significant downregulation of COL2 and ACAN expression (Fig. i). These results conclusively demonstrate that AA-CDs chondrocytes ferroptosis through HSPA5-GPX4 axis.

## AA-CDs inhibit OA progression in SD rats via targeting ferroptosis
We further developed an OA model using anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) to evaluate the therapeutic effectiveness of A-CDs and AA-CDs [ , ]. Histopathological analysis exhibited typical OA characteristics, including irregular cartilage surfaces and fissuring lesions in the ACLT groups (Fig. a and b). After treatment with A-CDs or AA-CDs, these pathological changes were significantly ameliorated, and the cartilage structure tended to normalize. Compared with the ACLT group, A-CDs and AA-CDs significantly increased cartilage thickness by 133.6 μm and 177.2 μm, respectively (Fig. c). The OARSI scores of A-CDs and AA-CDs were significantly reduced by 42.7% and 76.4%, respectively (Fig. d). Additionally, the synovitis scores of A-CDs and AA-CDs were significantly decreased by 50.0% and 71.4%, respectively (Fig. e). These results indicate that A-CDs and AA-CDs can significantly inhibit cartilage degeneration in OA.

 

In the advanced stage of OA, the osteophytes that form can induce joint pain and dysfunction. Furthermore, we evaluated the impact of A-CDs and AA-CDs on osteophyte development and subchondral bone remodeling through micro-CT scanning (Fig. f). Three-dimensional reconstruction analysis of micro-CT scans showed that osteophyte was significantly appeared in the ACLT groups, and the bone volume fraction (BV/TV) was significantly decreased in the ACLT groups. Not surprisingly, A-CDs and AA-CDs significantly suppressed osteophyte formation and increased the BV/TV (Fig. g and h). Notably, AA-CDs exhibited a more pronounced inhibitory effect on osteophyte formation compared with A-CDs. In summary, A-CDs and AA-CDs inhibit OA progression by suppressing cartilage degeneration, osteophyte formation, and abnormal subchondral bone remodeling, with AA-CDs demonstrating significantly superior therapeutic efficacy.
We also examined the expression of proteins related to anabolism (COL2 and ACAN) and catabolism (MMP13 and MMP3). As shown in Fig. a and b, the proportion of MMP13-positive and MMP3-positive chondrocyte was significantly increased in the ACLT groups. The number of chondrocytes positive for MMP3 and MMP13 was notably decreased following treatment with A-CDs or AA-CDs. Furthermore, the ACLT group showed a notable reduction in the percentage of chondrocytes positive for COL2 and ACAN, whereas the A-CDs and AA-CDs groups exhibited a notable rise in the percentage of chondrocytes positive for COL2 and ACAN (Fig. c and d). Furthermore, we detected the expression of HSPA5 and GPX4 (Fig.  e and f). In the ACLT group, there was a significant decrease in the proportion of chondrocytes positive for HSPA5 and GPX4. However, this proportion was significantly elevated after treatment with A-CDs or AA-CDs. The analysis of correlation showed a strong positive relationship between HSPA5 and GPX4, with an R² value of 0.85 (Fig.  g). Additionally, HSPA5 was found to have a significant positive correlation with COL2 (R² = 0.89) (Fig.  h) and with ACAN (R² = 0.82) (Fig.  i). Collectively, these results indicate that A-CDs and AA-CDs can significantly upregulate the expression of GPX4, COL2, and ACAN by enhancing HSPA5 expression, thereby effectively inhibiting cartilage degradation. 

## Conclusion
Emerging evidence suggests that ferroptosis and degradation of ECM play pivotal roles in OA pathogenesis. Herein, inspired by vinegar-processed A. argyi, we reported a novel carbon dot derived from Artemisia argyi and then treated with acetic acid (AA-CDs) for OA treatment through a triple-action mechanism: suppressing oxidative stress, regulating iron homeostasis, and targeting the HSPA5-GPX4 pathway. The proportion of oxygen-containing functional groups on the surface of AA-CDs enhancing their antioxidant activity and SOD-like enzyme activity. Although there was no significant difference in Fe2+ chelating ability between A-CDs and AA-CDs, AA-CDs with more surface acetylation modifications exhibited higher chondrocyte internalization efficiency. Consequently, AA-CDs showed superior performance in inhibiting intracellular oxidative stress and scavenging intracellular ferrous ions in chondrocytes, thereby more significantly suppressing ferroptosis in chondrocytes under oxidative stress. More importantly, through multi-omics analysis, we found that AA-CDs can specifically target HSPA5, thereby inhibiting the degradation of GPX4. This finding not only further explains the mechanism by which AA-CDs inhibit chondrocyte ferroptosis but also provides a new direction for the research on THM-CDs. More significantly, AA-CDs effectively alleviated cartilage erosion, osteophyte formation, and synovitis in an SD rat OA model. Through correlation analysis, we further found that HSPA5 is a crucial target for AA-CDs in the treatment of OA, and its expression is significantly correlated with that of COL2 and ACAN. These results uncover a new mechanism through which AA-CDs mitigate OA and offer fresh perspectives on understanding the bioactivity mechanism of TCM-CDs.

## Materials and methods

## Materials
Qiai (Artemisia argyi) was purchased from Hubei Qiai Technology Group Co., Ltd. Acetic acid, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), tert-butyl hydroperoxide (t-BHP), paraformaldehyde (PFA), 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPD), 2,2’-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt (ABTS), and MTT were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). The dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) assay kit, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity assay kit (WST-1 method), peroxidase (POD) activity assay kit, HE staining kit and modified Safranin O-Fast Green cartilage staining kit were purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China).

## Synthesis and characterization of A-CDs and AA-CDs
A-CDs and AA-CDs were prepared using pyrolysis and hydrothermal methods [ , ]. In brief, 100 g of Qiai was calcined at 300 °C for 1 h to acquire carbonized Artemisia argyi leaves. Next, 10 g carbonized leaves were added to 500 mL of H2O or 3% acetic acid solution and then heated in a reaction vessel at 100 °C for 1 h. Then, the larger particles were removed by filtration through a 0.22 μm membrane and small molecules was removed using 3500 Da dialysis bag. After dialysis, the solution was freeze-dried.

## ROS scavenging capacity and of nanozyme activity A-CDs and AA-CDs

## ABTS assay
Equal volumes of A-CDs or AA-CDs (0, 10, 20, 40, 80, 100, 200, 400, 800, and 1000 µg/mL) were combined with ABTS stock solution and then incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance was then measured at 734 nm using SpectraMax M5.

## DPPH assay
A total of 40 µL of A-CDs or AA-CDs (10, 20, 40, 80, 100, 200, 400, 800, and 1000 µg/mL) was added to 1 mL of DPPH solution (0.1 mM) and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance at 516 nm was recorded using SpectraMax M5.

## FRAP Assay
TPTZ solution (10 mM, Sigma), FeCl₃ (20 mM), and acetate buffer was mixed in a 10:1:1 ratio. Then, 100 µL of A-CDs or AA-CDs (100 µg/mL) was reacted with 900 µL of the above solution for 10 min. The absorbance at 593 nm was measured using SpectraMax M5.

## ESR detection of O₂⁻ scavenging ability
KO₂ (35.56 µg) was dissolved in 0.2 mL of 18-crown-6 solution (0.35 mM), followed by the addition of 1 mg BMPO. The O₂⁻ signal was recorded using an electron paramagnetic resonance spectrometer (EPR200-Plus, Guoyi Quantum) with or without 100 µg/mL AA-CDs.

## ESR detection of ·OH scavenging ability
BMPO (1 mg) was added to a hydrogen peroxide solution (0.2 mL, 1 mM), and ·OH was generated by the addition of Fe²⁺. The ·OH signal was recorded using the ESR spectrometer with or without 100 µg/mL AA-CDs.

## SOD-like and POD-like activity
A-CDs and AA-CDs (1, 2, 4, 8, 10, 20, and 40 µg/mL) was determined using WST-1 method (BC5165, Solarbio) [ ]. The POD-like activity of A-CDs (100 µg/mL) was evaluated by measuring the oxidation of 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) at different time points using the POD Activity Assay Kit (BC0090, Solarbio) [ ]. 

## Isolation of primary chondrocytes from SD rats and intracellular ROS detection
Primary chondrocytes isolated from SD rats using a collagenase digestion method as previously described and cultured in DMEM-F12 medium containing 10% FBS [ ]. For the quantification of intracellular ROS levels, DCFH-DA assay kit was employed. Briefly, chondrocytes were seeded in 24-well plates at a seeding density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and treated with t-BHP for 4 h to induce ROS production. After the t-BHP treatment period, the original culture medium was carefully aspirated, and replaced with serum-free DMEM-F12 medium that had been pre-mixed with 10 µM DCFH-DA and 1 µM Hoechst 33,342 for 30 min. Finally, fluorescence microscopy (Nikon Eclipse Ti2) was used to capture green fluorescence (DCFH, indicating ROS) and blue fluorescence (Hoechst 33342, indicating nuclei).

## Western blotting analysis
Chondrocytes treated with or without A-CDs or AA-CDs were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer. To isolate total cellular proteins, cell lysates were subjected to high-speed centrifugation to pellet insoluble debris and cell debris was removed. The concentration of the extracted total protein was quantified using an BCA Protein Assay Kit. Prior to electrophoresis, protein samples were normalized to the same concentration, mixed with SDS-PAGE loading buffer, and denatured by heating at 95 °C for 5–10 min. Proteins were resolved via 10–12% SDS-PAGE and then electrotransferred onto a 0.22 μm PVDF membrane. After transfer, the PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against target proteins (see Table S1 for antibody information, including catalog numbers and dilutions). On the following day, the membrane was washed three times with TBST and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies. Finally, protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection reagent and images were acquired using a Tanon-4800 imaging system.

## Establishment of SD rat OA model and histological evaluation
Twenty male SD rats (weighing 200–250 g) were acquired from Wuhan Jianyue Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Ethics Committee of Hubei University of Chinese Medicine. An OA model was established by ACLT, as previously reported [ ]. Briefly, rat was anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation, and then the skin and subcutaneous tissue over the right knee joint were incised to expose the joint cavity. The anterior cruciate ligament and medial meniscus were surgically transected using micro-scissors to induce joint instability. The sham group underwent only joint exposure without ligament/meniscus injury and the wound was directly sutured after exploration. Two weeks after surgery, ACLT rats were divided into three groups: ACLT, A-CDs, and AA-CDs. The treatment groups received weekly intra-articular injections of A-CDs (100 mg/mL, 100 µL) or AA-CDs (100 mg/mL, 100 µL) twice weekly for 8 weeks. and sham group received an equal volume of physiological saline (vehicle control) via the same injection route to eliminate potential confounding effects of the injection procedure. After the 8-week treatment period, rats were euthanized via carbon dioxide inhalation, and knee joints were immediately harvested. The harvested joint tissues were fixed in 4% PFA solution for 48 h, decalcified in 10% EDTA (pH 7.4) for 8 weeks, and embedded in paraffin. Sections were stained with hematoxylin & eosin (H&E) and safranin O/fast Green using commercial kits and evaluated for cartilage damage using the OARSI scoring system.

## Micro-CT analysis
For the assessment of structural changes in the knee joint, harvested rat knee samples were scanned using a SkyScan 1172 micro-CT system (Bruker). The scanning parameters were set as follows: tube voltage of 50 kV, tube current of 200 µA, and isotropic voxel resolution of 8 μm. After scanning, the raw micro-CT image data were reconstructed into 3D volumetric images and subsequently quantitative analysis of bony parameters was performed using CTAn software.

## RNA sequencing
In vitro OA-like chondrocyte model was first established by stimulating primary chondrocytes with t-BHP for 24 h. After model establishment, chondrocytes were divided into two groups for subsequent analysis: t-BHP and t-BHP + AA-CDs. Chondrocytes from both groups were collected and washed twice with to remove residual medium and cytokines. Then, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and then sent to Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for RNA sequencing [ , ]. 

## siRNA knockdown of HSPA5 in chondrocytes
To validate the functional role of HSPA5 in AA-CDs-mediated regulation of OA chondrocytes, small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting HSPA5 (siHSPA5) was used to silence HSPA5 expression in primary chondrocytes. The siHSPA5 oligonucleotides (si-r/m-HSPA5, siG161118063137-1–5) was purchased from RiboBio Co., Ltd. and a non-targeting scrambled siRNA (siNC, same manufacturer) was used as a negative control to exclude non-specific effects of siRNA transfection. Prior to transfection, primary chondrocytes were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured in antibiotic-free DMEM-F12 medium. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine™ 3000 reagent following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. After the 48-hour transfection period, the transfection efficiency was evaluated by Western blotting [ ]. 

## Drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay
To verify the direct binding interaction between AA-CDs and proteins in chondrocytes, the DARTS assay was conducted as previously described [ ]. Briefly, chondrocytes were lysed with RIPA buffer without protease inhibitors centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble cell debris. The protein extract was divided into two groups: (1) AA-CDs treatment group: incubated with AA-CDs at a final concentration of 100 µg/mL; (2) DMSO control group: incubated with an equal volume of DMSO. Both groups were incubated at 4 °C for 1 h to ensure sufficient binding between AA-CDs and potential target proteins. After incubation, different mass ratios of thermolysin were added to the two groups and incubation at room temperature for 10 min. The digestion reaction was immediately terminated by adding PMSF and separated by 10% SDS-PAGE. The gel was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 and destained with 10% acetic acid/40% methanol solution until clear bands appeared. Protein bands with a molecular weight range of 65–75 kDa were excised from the gel using sterile scalpel blades then sent for LC-MS/MS analysis to identify potential target proteins bound by AA-CDs.

## Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
To further confirm the direct binding between AA-CDs and HSPA5, the CETSA assay was conducted according to the literature with adaptations to our experimental system [ ]. Chondrocytes were lysed with RIPA buffer without protease inhibitors and incubated with AA-CDs (100 µg/mL) or DMSO for 1 h to allow full binding of AA-CDs to target proteins. After incubation, each group’s protein extract was aliquoted into 6–8 tubes and each aliquot was heated at a series of specified temperatures for 4 min. HSPA5 expression levels under different treatments were detected by western blotting.

## Statistical analysis
All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with biological replicates n ≥ 3. Statistical analyses, including one-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons and Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons, were performed using SPSS 22.0 and GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. A threshold of P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
