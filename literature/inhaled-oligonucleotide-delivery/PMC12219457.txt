## Abstract

## Background
Oil spills are a major concern due to the economic impact and severe effects on the ecosystem. To mitigate oil spills, hydrocarbon dispersion through emulsification is a promising approach, as it makes oil more susceptible to degradation by microorganisms. Environmental strains of Pseudomonas aeruginosa have demonstrated significant potential for producing rhamnolipids (RMLs) and pyocyanin (PYO), secondary metabolites associated to hydrocarbon emulsification. In this study, we isolated and characterized an environmental strain from an oil-contaminated site in the Gulf of Mexico. Upon genome sequencing and taxonomic classification, we developed genetic engineering tools and assessed their capacity to produce PYO and RMLs, molecules relevant for hydrocarbon emulsification.

## Results
Using the CRISPR/Cas9-APOBEC1-UGI system, we generated a targeted cytosine to thymine transition in the rpoS gene to generate a premature STOP codon. The resulting mutant exhibited increased production of PYO and RMLs, along with enhanced gasoline emulsification in cell-free supernatants, demonstrating successful modulation of a key regulatory gene. While the strain IGLPR01 retains certain virulence-associated features, this study contributes to the exploration of environmental isolates as future candidate chassis for biosurfactant production, emphasizing the need for further safety evaluation and rational attenuation strategies.

## Conclusion
This study provides a successful example of implementing CRISPR-based editing in an environmental P. aeruginosa strain. Despite the technical challenges, a genetic editing system was established and validated through a proof of concept to increase production of relevant metabolites. Our work demonstrates the applicability of genetic engineering tools in non-model environmental isolates, facilitating further developments. Importantly, the presence of virulence-associated features highlights the need for in-depth evaluation of pathogenicity and containment strategies before considering any future biotechnological applications.

## Background
Fossil fuels remain the primary source of energy in the world, but their extraction, refinement, and handling are both risky and complex. One of the most critical collateral issues stemming from the hydrocarbon industry is water contamination, which negatively impacts the environment, society, and the economy [ , ]. Damage to biodiversity has been reported through the acute toxic effects from oil ingestion, inhalation, smothering and drowning of marine life, and seabirds [ ]. Catastrophic oil spills, such as the March 1989 Exxon-Valdez spill in Alaska, the December 2007 Hebei Spirit oil spill in the Republic of Korea, and the 2010 Deepwater Horizon in the Gulf of México, have demonstrated long persistence and recovery times in the environment [ ]. Oil spills also represent a threat to human health increasing the risk of several diseases such as mental distress, kidney and liver failure, headache, dizziness, asthma, cardiovascular disorders, and prostate, thyroid, and blood cancers [ , , ]. Moreover, economic losses of billions of US dollars for oil remediation and the amendment of ecological disasters motivate improving remediation methodologies [ ].
Plenty of environmental microorganisms have a demonstrated ability to grow in crude oil as a carbon source [ , ]. For instance, Pseudomonas aeruginosa is commonly isolated from n-alkane hydrocarbons enriched environments [ ]. P. aeruginosa species have a versatile metabolism and can grow in paraffinic [ ], naphthenic [ ], aromatic [ ] and polycyclic hydrocarbons [ ]. P. aeruginosa is a producer of rhamnolipids (RMLs), a type of glycolipid biosurfactant made of β-hydroxy fatty acid chains with 1 or 2 rhamnose substitutes [ ]. RMLs are amphipathic molecules that emulsify oil, increasing the bioavailability of hydrocarbons and facilitating their degradation [ ]. Due to their biodegradability and low toxicity, RMLs have been approved by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency for use in food products, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals [ ]. In addition, Pseudomonas strains are prolific producers of phenazines, a group of compounds with antioxidant, antimicrobial, antiparasitic, neuroprotective, insecticidal, anti-inflammatory and anticancer activity [ – ]. Pyocyanin (PYO) is a blue phenazine produced exclusively by P. aeruginosa strains [ ], and it is involved in emulsification processes due to its synergistic action with the natural produced biosurfactants [ , ].
Given the relevance of PYO, several overproducing strains have been reported. For instance, rpoS deficient Pseudomonas strains have shown increased PYO production due to regulatory effects in the expression of phz1 and phz2 operons [ , ] and the phzM protein [ ]. Indigenous bacteria have gained attention due to their metabolic robustness for producing secondary metabolites [ ]. Incorporating environmental strains into DBTL cycle is challenging as it involves isolation, domestication and the adaptation of molecular editing techniques [ – ].
Here, we report the isolation and genomic characterization of P. aeruginosa IGLPR01, an environmental strain recovered from an oil-contaminated site. Genomic sequencing confirmed its taxonomic identity, and further analysis revealed its natural capacity to produce PYO and RMLs, metabolites associated with hydrocarbon emulsification. To explore the feasibility of genetically modifying non-model environmental strains, we aimed to enhance metabolite production through genome editing. We selected rpoS as a target gene due to its reported role as a negative regulator of PYO production, and because its disruption should lead to a visible increase in pigmentation of the media. By using the CRISPR/Cas9-APOBEC-UGI system, we introduce a premature STOP codon in rpoS, which led to increased production of PYO and RMLs, along with improved emulsification of hydrocarbon fuel using cell-free supernatant. While the P. aeruginosa IGLPR01 harbors virulence-associated genes, this study provides a methodological framework for implementing genome editing in other Pseudomonas environmental isolates and contributes to the effort of evaluating their potential for future applications.

## Materials and methods

## Strains and growth conditions
Strains, plasmids, and oligonucleotides used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Cultures were grown in LB medium [ ]. E. coli and P. aeruginosa strains were routinely incubated at 37 °C. Antibiotics were used at the following concentrations: for E. coli, 100 µg/mL Streptomycin (Sm) and 20 µg/mL Gentamicin (Gm); for all the P. aeruginosa strains Gm or Apm (apramycin) at 150 µg/mL. E. coli DH5α was used for plasmid construction and maintenance. Cultures were inoculated with washed cells at an optical density (OD 600 nm) of 0.2. Flasks were incubated at 37 °C and 225 rpm with continuous shaking.

## Genomic DNA extraction, sequencing, assembly, and annotation of P. aeruginosa IGLPR01
DNA extraction was performed as previously reported [ ]. Genome sequencing was performed following a hybrid assembly strategy with short reads from MiSeq (Illumina, USA) and long reads from MinION (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK), MaSuRCA v.3.3.3 (Maryland Super Read Cabog Assembler) with default parameters was used to assemble the genome [ ].The IGLPR01 genome was annotated using the BV-BRC server version 3.93.3 [ ].

## 16S rDNA and P. aeruginosa IGLPR01 total genome analysis
The genome sequence data was uploaded to the Type Strain Genome Server (TYGS (https://tygs.dsmz.de), for a whole genome-based taxonomic analysis [ , ]. Information on nomenclature, synonymy, and related taxonomic literature was provided by TYGS's sister database, the List of Prokaryotic names with Standing in Nomenclature (LPSN, available at https://lpsn.dsmz.de) [ ]. The results were obtained by 2024-09-18. First, the complete genome sequence of IGLPR01 was used to determine the genus and species. Once identified as P. aeruginosa, 50 representing groups of P. aeruginosa strains were selected (a complete list of selected genomes is in Supplementary Table 2) based on a previous classification [ ]. 16S rDNA was used to rapidly confirm IGLPR01 taxonomy, but the complete genome was used to determine the taxonomic group. The TYGS data was visualized using iTOL (interactive tree of life: https://itol.embl.de/) [ ]. The analysis in silico of the antimicrobial resistance profile was performed using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD: https://card.mcmaster.ca/browse) [ ], which provides curated reference sequences and Single Nucleotide Polymorphism models (SNP) organized via the Antibiotic Resistance Ontology (ARO). For the analysis of strict genes related to virulence, we used IslandViewer version 4 (https://www.pathogenomics.sfu.ca/islandviewer/browse/) [ ] with Islandpath-DIMOB [ – ] and IslandPick [ ] algorithms.

## Preparation of competent cells and transformation of P. aeruginosa IGLPR01 by electroporation
The preparation of P. aeruginosa IGLPR01 was based on [ , ], with slight modifications. A colony of P. aeruginosa IGLPR01 was grown overnight at 42 °C 250 rpm using 10 mL of LB in a 50 mL flask. 0.8 mL of the overnight culture were used for the initial inoculum in a 250 mL flask with 40 mL of LB at 42 °C 250 rpm. When the culture reached OD600 = 0.5 (cell density ~ 3 × 108 cell/mL), the flask was chilled on ice for 10 min. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4 °C 2300 × g for 10 min and the pellet was resuspended in 25 mL of sterile, ice cold SMEB buffer. Cells were harvested as above and resuspended in 10 mL of SMEB buffer (1 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 1 mM MgCl2, 300 mM sucrose). Cells were harvested again and resuspended in 480 μL of sterile, ice cold SMEB buffer and transferred to a chilled 1.5 mL tube. Finally, the cells were harvested as above and resuspended in 80 μL of SMEB buffer. Cells were kept on ice and used as soon as possible for electroporation. For each electroporated sample, 0.5 μg of plasmid and the competent P. aeruginosa cells were used in a sterile 1.5 mL microfuge tube. Samples were placed on ice for 5–10 min. Plasmid/cell suspension was transferred to a chilled 0.2 cm cuvettes and it was electroporated using P. aeruginosa preset conditions (C = 25 µF; PC = 200 Ohm; V = 2.5 kV; time constant about 5 ms; Cell Pulser Xcell BIORAD). Once electroporated, the sample was incubated in a 15 mL tube with 2 mL of SOC medium (10 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MgSO4 and 20 mM glucose) at 37 °C for 1 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 4 °C 2300 × g for 10 min and the pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of SOC media. The sample was plated on LB agar with the corresponding antibiotic and incubated at 37 °C overnight.

## CRISPR/Cas base editing plasmid design
An extended and detailed version of the methodology is available (Supplementary file). CRISPY-web and CRISPRdirect were used for spacer design [ , ]. Considering the use of a base editor (Cytosine deaminase protein APOBEC-1), we tried different spacers for the sgRNA design. The spacers were synthesized as two complementary oligonucleotides with overhangs to allow cloning into the vectors and aligned in a thermal cycler. Aligned oligonucleotides were diluted to 100 µM with nuclease-free water. Plasmids pnCASPABEC [ ] and pMBEC6 [ ] were used for the CRISPR mutation strategy. 1 µg of each plasmid was digested with BsaI enzyme following the commercial protocol (NEB BsaI-HF®v2). After that, a 1% agarose gel was performed, and the linearized fragment was purified by agarose extraction as supplier recommendations (Thermo Scientific GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit #K02691). The linearized backbone obtention was confirmed by another 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. T4 DNA ligase was used for sgRNA ligation in the backbone (Promega #M1801) as the supplier recommended. The ligated reaction was used directly for the transformation of DH5α. Positive resistance colonies were grown for pDNA extraction by alkaline lysis [ ] and sequencing was performed to confirm the correct sgRNA construction. Mutant tracking was performed by PCR and Sanger sequencing using the appropriate oligonucleotides (Supplementary Table S1).

## Plasmid curing
The rpoS-STOP codon confirmed colony was used for inoculation. Having an overnight pre-culture, 100 µL were inoculated in 10 mL of LB supplemented with sucrose at 10% (w/v) and incubated at 37 °C and 225 rpm overnight [ ]. 1:1000 dilution was plated on non-selective LB plates and incubated at 37 °C overnight. Colonies were randomly inspected by growing in two new plates, one in LB and the other with Gm. Curated colonies will grow in LB plate but will present Gm sensitivity in the antibiotic plate. DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing were performed on the curated colony for tracking the rpoS-STOP codon mutation.

## Cell-free supernatant
LB culture of P. aeruginosa strains was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min. Supernatant was recovered and filtrated in sterile conditions using vacuum and a 0.22 syringe filter.

## Pyocyanin extraction and measurement
Pyocyanin was extracted following a previously described method [ , ]. Briefly, 3 mL (0.6 volume) of chloroform was added to 5 mL of cell-free supernatant and vortexed vigorously for 2 min. The mixture was centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 10 min. 1.6 mL of the chloroform layer was transferred to a fresh tube and mixed with 0.2 volumes of 0.2 M HCl. The aqueous red phase was recovered, and the absorbance was measured at 520 nm using a Quartz Glass Elisa Plate (100 mL). The concentration of pyocyanin in mg/mL was calculated as follows: Pyocyanin concentration (mg/mL) = Abs520nm x Dilution Factor × 17.072. The mg/mL were divided by the CDW (g cell dry weight) to obtain the PYO-CDW normalized titer in LB. The CDW was obtained based on the reported relationship between biomass and OD (1.0 OD refers to 2.085 mg biomass/mL) [ ].

## RMLs assay
A colorimetric orcinol-sulfuric acid assay was used to quantify RMLs as rhamnose equivalents. Briefly, 333 μL of cell-free supernatant, collected at 8 h, 26, and 50 h, was extracted twice with 1 mL of diethyl ether. The solvent was evaporated to dryness under laminar flow and then dissolved in 1 mL of deionized water. Subsequently, 100 μL of each sample was mixed with 900 μL of a solution containing 0.19% orcinol in 53% sulfuric acid. The mixture was heated at 80 °C in a water bath for 30 min, followed by cooling at room temperature for 15 min. Absorbance was measured at 421 nm, and RMLs concentration was determined by comparing the absorbance values with L-rhamnose standards curves ranging from 0 to 50 μg/mL (≥ 99%, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. R3875, CAS 10030-85-0). The orcinol method for RMLs quantification has been recently demonstrated to be reproducible and accurate, showing comparable results to UPLC/MS/MS [ ].

## Emulsification assays
Cell-free supernatant was tested for its ability to emulsify regular 87 octanes gasoline (Gulf Regular, Mexico). Initially, 4 mL of cell-free supernatant and 4 mL of gasoline were added to the tube. The initial heights of the layers were carefully measured. The tubes were closed, and vortexed vigorously for 5 min. The tubes were then incubated at room temperature on the workbench to remain undisturbed. The height of the aqueous phase was measured at 1 h after vortexing and again at 24 h. The emulsification index (%E) was calculated according to the following equation:
%E=heightofaqueousphaseinitial-heightofaqueousphaseaftermixingheightofaqueousphaseinitial×100The selected formula aimed to quantify the percentage of the aqueous phase (the cell-free supernatant or the LB medium) that was incorporated into the emulsion during the mixing process.

## Metabolite extraction and analysis
For metabolite extraction 200 mL of LB media were inoculated with 500 µL, of an overnight P. aeruginosa culture (WT and rpoS-STOP). The flasks were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h under agitation at 200 rpm, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 g for 15 min. The resulting supernatant was filtered under vacuum using a 0.22 µm membrane filter to remove residual cells. The cell-free supernatant was extracted twice with ethyl acetate (EtOAc) in a 1:1 ratio, and the extracts were concentrated using a Buchi R-215 rotavapor®, using 140 mbar and 45 °C. The extracts were analyzed by LC–MS/MS on a Vanquish Duo UHPLC binary system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) coupled to an IDX-Orbitrap Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as previously reported [ ]. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY BEH C18 (10 cm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) equipped with an ACQUITY BEH C18 guard column, maintained at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of MilliQ water with 0.1% formic acid (v/v) (A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (v/v) (B), sourced from HiPerSolv CHROMANORM®, HPLC and LC–MS grade, VWR Chemicals BDH®. The mobile phase gradient composition was as follows: 0–0.8 min 2% B, 0.8–3.3 min 2–5% B, 3.3–10 min 5–100% B, 10–11 min 100% B. The column was then re-equilibrated at 2% B for 2.7 min. Flow rate was set at 0.35 mL/min, and the injection volume was 1 µL.
The MS measurements were performed using heated electrospray ionization (HESI) in both positive and negative ion modes as previously described [ ]. The voltage was set to 3500 V in positive mode and 2500 V in negative mode, acquiring in full MS/MS spectra (Data dependent Acquisition-driven MS/MS, DDA) in the mass range of 70–1000 Da, with a resolution of 120,000 for full MS scans and 30,000 for MS/MS events. Precursor ions were fragmented by the stepped High-energy Collision Dissociation (HCD) using collision energies of 20, 40, and 55. The automatic gain control (AGC) target value set at 4E05 for the full MS and 5E04 for the MS/MS spectral acquisition.
The MS/MS data were converted to mzXML format using MS-Convert version 3.6.0 [ ] (ProteoWizard, Palo Alto, CA, USA), and the resulting features were exported to the Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking (GNPS) [ ]. Feature detection was performed using GNPS and Sirius 6.0.7 [ ] for MS/MS dereplication, feature annotation and molecular networking generation, respectively.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was performed using the Prism software from GraphPad (www.graphpad.com).

## Results

## P. aeruginosa IGLPR01 sequencing and genome analysis
P. aeruginosa IGLPR01 was grown under laboratory conditions for genomic extraction and sequencing. The genome was assembled using MaSuRCA [ , ] obtaining a single contig (Fig.  ). The genome size is 6, 682, 021 bp with a GC content of 66.19%, which is consistent with previously reported genomes having a mean size from 6.1 to 7.1 Mpb and a GC content of 66.4% for others P. aeruginosa strains [ , , ]. Taxonomic characterization using the TYGS server determined that IGLPR01 belongs to the species Pseudomonas aeruginosa, having a digital DNA-DNA hybridization score (dDDH) of 91.7% (Supplementary Table S2).
 We used BV-BRC server (version 3.93.3) for the annotation of the IGLPR01 genome [ ]. The graphical circular distribution (Fig.  a) illustrates the genomic region where some features are distributed like CDS forward and reverse, non-CDS features (tRNA, rRNA, etc.), G-C content, and subsystem and non-in-subsystem annotations. The subsystem coverage represents all the genes that could be assigned to genes related to well-identified metabolic pathways and biological functions (Fig.  b). The annotations not-in-subsystem represent other genes related to secondary metabolism and undefined functions, covering more than half of the genome (Fig.  a). As expected, the IGLPR01 genome is larger than clinically relevant P. aeruginosa strains (~ 6.48 Mb). For instance, IGLPR01 codes for almost 13% more proteins than PAO1 type strain [ ].

## P. aeruginosa IGLPR01 strain belongs to group 2
In order to determine the taxonomic group subclassification, we generated a database of 49 strains with an even representation of all 5 groups according to the classification of Pseudomonas strains [ ] (Supplementary Table S3). While 16S rRNA-based phylogenetic tree did not reconstruct all the groups (Supplementary Fig. S1); using complete genome sequences phylogenetic analysis resolved that IGLPR01 belongs to group 2, along with Pseudomonas aeruginosa PA14 (Fig.  a).
 The genome of P. aeruginosa IGLPR01 was analyzed using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) to evaluate its potential biosafety for biotechnological applications. CARD identifies resistance determinants using a curated database of characterized associated antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes. CARD defines two sets of results (Fig.  b): “perfect” and “strict” AMR genes. Perfect refers to genes with a 100% match to validated resistance mechanism, while strict indicates a close but not exact match, suggesting a possible role in AMR. It was found that a set of 15 perfect genes for IGLPR01 referred to antibiotic efflux proteins as MexI and OprM, antibiotic target alteration as BasS and antibiotic inactivation as the β-lactamase PDC-64. Additionally, IGLPR01 genome encodes 45 strict predictions (complete list Supplementary Table S4). Given the high number of strict genes, we further analyzed the genomes using IslandViewer 4, a tool that identifies Genomic Islands (GI). GI are regions that often contain genes related to AMR or virulence. The GI analysis (Supplementary Table S5 and Fig.  c) revealed predictions of additional efflux pumps, integrases, transposases, genes involved in metal and oxidative stress tolerance, and numerous hypothetical proteins. Furthermore, the annotated IGLPR01 genome (Supplementary Table S6) includes other related genes, such as MvfR/PqsR, a multiple virulence factor regulator [ ]; the transcriptional regulators LasR and RhlR [ ]; the quinolone quorum sensing cluster pqsABCDE [ ] and the proteins involved in the Type III secretion system [ ].

## Functional annotation of the P. aeruginosa IGLPR01 genome
Due to its environmental origin from a hydrocarbon-contaminated site, we mined the genome of P. aeruginosa IGLPR01 for secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) that could support its ability to thrive in such a niche. As previously mentioned, phenazines such as PYO are redox-active molecules associated to support emulsification activity when acting synergistically with RMLs, and Pseudomonas species are well-known producers of these molecules. IGLPR01 genome was analyzed using antiSMASH [ ] which revealed two clusters associated with phenazine production, located at regions 1 and 20 (Fig.  b, and Supplementary Table S7). This finding aligns with expectations, as P. aeruginosa strains typically harbor two nearly identical core phz BGCs, called phzA1-G1 and phzA2-G2. However, these clusters differ in their protomer regions and flanking genes. In particular, the phzA1-G1 region is flanked by phzM and phzS genes, which we also identified, and which play a critical role in the conversion of phenazine-1-carboxilic acid (PCA) into PYO (Fig.  a, c) [ – ]. We also searched for genes related to RMLs biosynthesis due to their principal role as natural biosurfactants that facilitate hydrocarbon dispersion and uptake.
 We identified a BGC core responsible for mono and di-RMLs production. This cluster exhibited high sequence identity to the previously annotated RMLs BGC in P. aeruginosa PA14 (Fig.  ) [ ].
 

## Genome editing of IGLPR01 by CRISPR/Cas
To assess the feasibility of genome editing in P. aeruginosa IGLPR01, we aimed to enhance the production of secondary metabolites involved in hydrocarbon emulsification. We selected rpoS as the target for genetic modification as it is known that its disruption increases PYO production [ , , , ]. This gene was selected not only for its regulatory role but also because changes in PYO levels can be easily tracked visually and quantified, making it a useful phenotypic marker to validate genome editing.
We tested the IGLPR01 strain for antibiotic resistance and found it to be susceptible to apramycin (Apm) and gentamicin (Gm). Based on previously reported techniques, we attempted genetic engineering of the rpoS gene using traditional homologous recombination-based plasmid tools, employing both electroporation and conjugation [ , ]. However, we were unable to achieve the expected genetic modification (Supplementary Fig.  ).
CRISPR/Cas base editing has emerged as a powerful tool for genetic engineering in Pseudomonas strains. We tested a variant of CRIPSR/Cas9 using a Cas9 nickase (Cas9 D10A) fused to a cytidine deaminase protein (APOBEC1) and the uracil-glycosylase-inhibitor protein (UGI) from Bacillus bacteriophage AR9. APOBEC1, when attached to Cas9, scans for cytosine residues along the protospacer within an editing window (EW), and deaminates cytosine to convert it to thymine (C-G to T-A), thus potentially creating STOP codons. In addition, the UGI from Bacillus bacteriophage AR9 has been reported to inhibit the base excision repair system (BER) (Fig.  a) widening the EW in CRISPR/Cas base editing systems [ ].
 We used the episomal pMBEC6 plasmid (Cas9-APOBEC-UGI) as a backbone, which contains a GmR selection marker, ugi for BER inhibition and sacB for plasmid curing using sucrose [ ]. We generated the plasmid pIGL_rpos1_pMBEC6 with a sgRNA targeting rpoS (Fig.  b, c), specifically at Q157 aiming for the Q157* mutation (here identified as rpoS-STOP). The correct construction was verified by sequencing the entire plasmid. pIGL_rpos1_pMBEC6 generated at least one C to T transition in all the 16 analyzed colonies. However, only 31.5% of the colonies exhibited the desired STOP codon mutation (Fig.  d). The reported EW for the IGLPR01 strain was from 3 to 8 bases on the spacer, which is shorter than other model strains reported with the same system (e.g. E. coli, P. putida KT2440, and P. aeruginosa PA14).
A resulting positive colony was selected for plasmid curation using sucrose at 10% (Supplementary Fig. S5a and S5b). Once the strain was curated, a region of the rpoS gene flanking the sgRNA target site was amplified and compared with the IGLRP01 WT strain. Sequencing confirmed the C-to-T mutation, leading to the generation of a premature STOP codon, successfully truncating the rpoS region (Supplementary Fig. S5c).

## IGLPR01 rpoS-STOP mutant increased PYO production
We compared the growth of P. aeruginosa IGLPR01 WT and its rpoS-STOP mutant. As shown in Fig.  a, the rpoS-STOP strain exhibited reduced growth, reaching a lower optical density (~ 3.0 OD600) at 26 h compared to WT (~ 4.5 OD600). To evaluate the impact of rpoS-STOP on PYO production, we quantified its levels at three different growth stages: 8 h (mid-log phase), 26 h (stationary phase), and 50 h (death phase). As shown in Fig.  b (left panel), IGLPR01 rpoS-STOP produced significantly higher levels of PYO at 8 h, 26 h and 50 h compared to WT (p < 0.0001 at all-time points). To account for differences in biomass accumulation, PYO production was normalized by cell dry weight (CDW). The results (Fig.  b, right) showed that rpoS-STOP has a higher PYO titer per g CDW compared to WT at all time points, with the most pronounced difference at 50 h (1028.66 and 2780.24 µg/gCDW mL respectively, p < 0.0001). Additionally, we performed metabolomics analyses of the culture supernatants (Supplementary Fig. S7), that allowed us to identify ions for a key intermediate in the phenazine biosynthetic pathway, phenazine-1-carboxylic acid (PCA) [M + H]+ = 225.066 Da (mass error: 0.71 ppm), confirming the production of phenazines.
 In parallel, metabolic profiling of culture supernatants was used to identify molecules associated with biosurfactant production. Consistently with the presence of the RML BGC in the genome of P. aeruginosa IGLPR01, the metabolomic analysis led to the identification of a set of molecules linked to the rhl operon, including the RML base (C10-C10) [M + H]+ = 359.28 Da (mass error: −1.65 ppm), the mono-rhamnolipid (Rha-C10-C10) [M + H]+ = 505.338 Da (mass error: 0.048 ppm), and the di-rhamnolipid (Rha-Rha-C10-C10) [M + H]+ = 651.395 Da (mass error: −1.28 ppm), (Fig.  a). Samples were collected at 8 h, 26 h, and 50 h to quantify RMLs production. As shown (Fig.  b, left), the rpoS-STOP mutant exhibited significantly higher levels of rhamnose equivalents compared to the WT strain in all time points (p < 0.01). This trend was also observed when considering the normalized RMLs titer based on CDW (Fig.  b, right).
 Based on these findings, we evaluated the emulsification capacity of the cell-free supernatant using conventional gasoline. The assay focused on measuring the percentage decrease in the height of the cell-free supernatant layer at 1 h and 24 h after mixing. This approach reflects that the aqueous phase can be incorporated into the hydrocarbon phase to form the emulsion. As shown in Fig.  c, the IGLPR01 rpoS-STOP mutant demonstrated an enhanced emulsification capacity compared with WT strain. Emulsification percentages increased over time, reaching 30.6% at 50 h, which is statistically significant relative to the WT (p < 0.01). Emulsification stability could also be appreciated due to the persistence of the corresponding layer over time, maintaining almost the same emulsification percentage at 1 h than at 24 h. When LB medium was mixed with gasoline, no emulsification was observed, confirming that the emulsifying effect was caused by the RMLs in the supernatant. These results suggest that the rpoS mutation, which enhances PYO production, may also increase RML leading to a higher emulsification ability.

## Discussion
Non-model bacteria are natural overproducers of many important compounds and may be considered promising hosts for metabolite production. Environmental isolates tend to exhibit inherent tolerance to stressful conditions such as oxidative stress, high salinity, and the presence of toxic compounds. These characteristics often correlate with strong metabolic fluxes directed toward the synthesis of useful products, including bioactive secondary metabolites, biosurfactants, and pigments with potential industrial relevance [ , ]. P. aeruginosa IGLPR01, isolated from an oil-contaminated site in the Gulf of Mexico, exemplifies such a niche-adapted bacterium with distinctive metabolic features shaped by its ecological context.
P. aeruginosa has been classified into five phylogenetic groups based on average nucleotide identity (ANI) score obtained from 1,315 isolates [ ]. Sequencing shows that IGLPR01 belongs to clade 2, together with the reference strain PA14, a pathogenic strain isolated from a burn wound [ – ]. As mentioned, IGLPR01 harbors genes typically associated with virulence in P. aeruginosa; unlike clinically relevant strains such as PA14 and PAO1, IGLPR01 was isolated from an oil-contaminated environment. This suggests that its virulence-associated genes may primarily contribute to environmental adaptation and survival in harsh conditions rather than host infection. For instance, PYO has antibacterial and antifungal properties that contribute to interspecies competition for carbon sources [ , ] Additionally, IGLPR01 encodes various stress response mechanisms (Supplementary Table S6), including heavy metal efflux pumps, catalases (KatE), superoxide dismutase proteins, and proteins to alleviate iron levels (OxyR) [ ]. For these reasons we aimed to develop genetic engineering tools to enhance its tractability and metabolic performance, as a step toward evaluating its handling and suitability for future assays.
Using genomics and metabolomics, our results show that IGLPR01 synthetizes mono and di-RMLs. Most Pseudomonas species are known producers of RMLs which facilitate uptake of hydrocarbons and its further utilization as carbon source [ – ]. Also, we identified the genes responsible for RMLs biosynthesis and annotated Rha-C10-C10 and Rha-Rha-C10-C10.
In our study, we presented a strategy to increase PYO production by inactivating rpoS expression, which also serves as an easily trackable marker for successful genetic editing. RpoS is a sigma factor that regulates the general stress response in Gram-negative bacteria and, in P. aeruginosa PAO1 regulates more than 700 genes during the stationary phase [ ]. Also, it is known that RpoS represses the expression of the phenazines operons (phz1 and phz2), pqsE and phzM, proteins involved in PYO synthesis [ , , ].
Attempts to use genetic editing tools on environmental microorganisms often face challenges because most of them are designed for well-established laboratory strains. These model strains are highly manipulable due to years of optimization, making the tools highly efficient. However, when these tools are transferred to non-model strains, numerous obstacles can hinder success. Given that IGLPR01 is an environmental isolate, we attempted to generate a mutation via HR by constructing a plasmid containing the ApmR gene flanked by corresponding homology regions [ , ] (Supplementary Figure S2a). Although we obtained IGLPR01 Amp-resistant transformants, PCR and whole-genome sequencing revealed plasmid integration after the rpoS gene rather than a double crossover event (Supplementary Figure S2b, S2c, S2d and S2e). There are reports indicating that the second recombination can be challenging and has been suggested to combine elements such as the I-SceI endonuclease from yeast [ – ], where it can induce a break in the integrated plasmid to catalyze the second recombination and the gene replacement.
Several reports have successfully achieved gene editing in strains of P. aeruginosa using CRISPR/Cas system. While some editing strategies rely on DSBs induced by both active HNH and RuvC domains, we employed the Cas9D10A nickase variant, in which have the RuvC domain deactivated, which reduces off-target effects by up to 1500-fold compared to the wild-type SpCas9 [ – ]. In addition, the use of Cas9 nickase, in contrast to catalytically dead Cas9, has been reported to increase editing efficiency by up to sixfold by directing the mismatch repair system to the nicked DNA strand [ ]. This nickase is used in conjunction with base editing proteins such as APOBEC1, a cytosine deaminase that can be engineered to introduce STOP codons because it deaminates cytosine that seems to uracil and can generate a C to T transition [ ]. Initially, we employed the plasmid pnCASPA-BEC (Supplementary Figure S3c) [ ], which previously achieved C to T transitions in PAO1 with 100% editing efficiency in an editing window spanning positions 3 to 8 of the spacer. Our first guide contained a G upstream of target C (Supplementary Figure S3a and S3b), which was problematic to edit because a G is a steric hindrance to APOBEC1 activity [ ]. A second sgRNA version, without an upstream G (Supplementary Figure S4b), successfully achieved editing at positions 4 and 5 in 87.5% of the analyzed colonies, but the target C at position 8, intended to generate the STOP codon, remained unedited (Supplementary Figure S4c, S4d and S4e). The reported EW for pnCasPABEC is for positions 4 to 8 of the protospacer [ ] which is wider than ours (positions 4–5) and insufficient to target our desired cytosine. We hypothesized that BER system, in particular uracil-DNA glycosylase (UDG), catalytically removes uracil from DNA to trigger BER [ ]. Considering this, we switched to using UGI, a protein from the B. subtilis phage PBS1, which blocks UDG activity, effectively widening the editing window [ ]. The addition of UGI to the base editor has also been shown to increase base-editing efficiency by at least threefold in mammalian cells [ ]. Using this alternative approach, we used the same designed protospacer sequence as before. Upon generation of the plasmid IGL_rpoS1_pMBEC6 and evaluating the subsequent efficacy of the CRISPR/Cas9-APOBEC1-UGI system, we confirmed the successful introduction of the desired C-to-T transition at position 8 within the protospacer. A comparison between both CRISPR/Cas-APOBEC strategies revealed that the system lacking UGI produced an EW limited to positions 4 and 5 within the protospacer. In contrast, the system including UGI expanded the editing window to positions 3 through 8. This highlights a clear effect of BER system inhibition in broadening the editable region. In CRISPR/Cas-APOBEC-UGI system, we observed 100% editing efficiency at positions 3–5. However, editing efficiency appeared to decrease toward the 3′ end of the EW. Specifically, the target cytosine at position 8, which was critical for generating the STOP codon, showed a success rate of 62.5%. These results underscore the importance of carefully selecting the target cytosine based on its position within the EW. We propose that targeting cytosines closer to the center of the EW increases the probability of efficient editing. Notably, our findings differ from those reported for P. putida KT2440 using the same system, where the EW was defined between positions 2 and 9 [ ]. Compared to that broader range, the EW in our strain appears narrower [ – ]. This reinforces the need to consider not only the presence of a cytosine but also its precise location within the protospacer. Finally, these results highlight the variability in editing efficiency across bacterial species and target contexts, emphasizing the need to adapt editing protocols to the specific genomic and physiological conditions of each strain, as further detailed in Supplementary Protocol.
In our study, IGLPR01-rpoS STOP exhibited a higher PYO production compared to the WT strain (Supplementary Fig. S6). Deletion of rpoS gene has previously been shown to improve PYO production in a model P. aeruginosa PAO1 [ ]. However, other environmental strains show higher PYO production such as P. aeruginosa TGC02 and TGC04 strains (isolated from soil at a gas station) [ ]. This highlights the importance of the application of genetic editing techniques in environmental strains to improve strain performance. In addition, we observed a reduced growth in IGLPR01-rpoS STOP which can be explained by a diminished stress tolerance in rpoS− strains. In clinical strains such as P. aeruginosa PAO1, a rpoS mutant has been shown to exhibit sensitivity to H2O2, heat shock, and high osmolarity [ ].
The supernatant of the IGLPR01 rpoS-STOP mutant exhibited increased emulsification compared to the WT strain, a phenomenon that correlates with the elevated production of both PYO and RMLs. This enhanced emulsification may be attributed to physicochemical and regulatory mechanisms resulting from the rpoS-STOP mutation. First, the mutation of rpoS has been reported to increase PYO production [ ] and this molecule possesses antioxidant properties [ ]. These characteristics may contribute to biosurfactant stabilization by mitigating oxidative stress caused by hydrocarbons, thereby prolonging the functional integrity of RMLs. This is particularly relevant given that RMLs are the surfactant molecules that interact with hydrocarbons [ ], and are synthesized by the rhlAB operon, as shown in Fig.  . Second, the regulatory role of rpoS in quorum sensing may explain the observed increase in RMLs production. RpoS has been reported to negatively regulate quorum sensing genes, particularly the Rhl system (rhlR-rhlI) [ ]. Additionally, it has been reported that RpoS represses rhlI, which acts as an autoregulator of rhlAB expression [ ]. Therefore, rpoS mutation may relieve this repression, allowing RhlI and RhlR to induce rhlAB transcription and consequently the increased RMLs production [ , , , ].
Our results showed that supernatants from IGLPR01 rpoS-STOP emulsify hydrocarbons more effectively than those from the WT strain. Independent disruption of the PYO and RML biosynthetic pathways would be necessary to determine their individual contributions to this phenotype. Nevertheless, previous studies have reported that PYO can enhance the emulsification activity of biosurfactants on hydrocarbons. In that regard, it is reported that adding PYO to a cell-free supernatant from P. aeruginosa producing only biosurfactants resulted in improved emulsification of petrol, diesel and hexadecane compared with the test without PYO [ ]. In another study it was demonstrated that combining biosurfactants with PYO significantly enhanced oil recovery in a sand pack column system, achieving 80% recovery compared to 65% with biosurfactants alone [ ]. Sand pack columns are widely used in laboratory models that simulate porous geological formations, providing a relevant approximation of oil reservoir conditions. Notably, PYO alone was not sufficient to achieve comparable oil recovery, suggesting that its synergistic effect may be related to enhancing the interfacial activity of biosurfactants under application-relevant conditions. These findings support the idea that PYO may contribute to enhanced hydrocarbon emulsification when combined with biosurfactants.
Due to their relatively high production costs, biosurfactants are not yet cost-competitive compared to chemical surfactants. One strategy to address this limitation is to increase yields through strain engineering. As microbial metabolites continue to attract interest for applications such as microbial enhanced oil recovery, optimizing strain performance has become increasingly relevant. The ex situ approach, which relies on the use of microbial products rather than live cells, represents a promising direction, as evidenced by the growing number of related patents [ ]. In this study, we established and validated a base-editing approach in an environmental Pseudomonas aeruginosa strain, demonstrating that genetic engineering is feasible even un non-model isolates. This methodology may be extended to introduce additional modifications aimed at improving metabolite production. For instance, a mutation in the lon gene has been reported to increase RMLs production directly [ ]. Having established an efficient editing strategy, it is also important to consider ways to reduce the potential biosafety risks associated with handling P. aeruginosa IGLPR01. Several studies have identified mutations that significantly reduce virulence in Pseudomonas species. For instance, mutations in genes encoding structural components of the type III secretion system (T3SS) injectosome, such as pcsC, have been shown to abolish virulence, as described in public patent MX/a/2019/006840. In addition, studies focused on transposon mutant libraries of P. aeruginosa PA14 have identified other virulence-attenuating targets, including lasR, gacA, and lemA, which led to reduced pathogenicity in C. elegans and mouse infection models [ ]. Furthermore, components of the quorum sensing regulatory cascade, particularly the RetS/LadS/GacS signaling network, are reported to influence pathogenic behavior [ , ]. Mutations in these regulatory systems may contribute to the attenuation of virulence and facilitate safer handling of Pseudomonas strains in potential future biotechnological applications.
Our primary objective was not to present IGLPR01 as an immediately deployable production strain, but rather to demonstrate the technical feasibility of applying CRISPR-based editing in environmental isolates. This serves as an example for future efforts to unlock the potential of natural overproducers. In addition to validating the base-editing strategy, our work provides practical insights and protocol optimizations that may guide others in overcoming challenges when adapting genome editing tools to non-model organisms. These include troubleshooting steps based on the obstacles we encountered during the design and implementation of multiple editing strategies. Nonetheless, the presence of virulence-associated genes in IGLPR01 underscores the need for comprehensive biosafety evaluations and rational attenuation strategies before any practical implementation. Ultimately, we hope that this example of using an environmental strain will encourage the use of natural overproducers to achieve benefits that support social development and address current challenges.
