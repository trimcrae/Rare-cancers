## Abstract
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are membrane vesicles released or secreted from almost all cell types. EVs are derived from multivesicular bodies or from the plasma membrane and contain a subset of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids (e.g., DNA, RNA, and microRNA [miRNA]) derived from the parent cell. EVs play important roles in intercellular communication by efficiently transferring the content between cells both locally and systemically. Given their natural ability to transfer cargo to cells, sometimes in a targeted manner, and their apparent lack of immunogenicity, EVs are being engineered for delivery of therapeutic RNAs, DNAs, miRNAs, viral particles, drugs, and even proteins. In addition, many of the therapeutic effects of stem cell treatments are mediated by stem cell-derived EVs, which are safer and potentially more effective than the parental stem cells. Here we provide an overview of the use of EVs for delivery of different therapeutic nucleic acids, viruses, and drugs, as well as the use of therapeutic stem cell-derived EVs.
Keywords: extracellular vesicles, stem cells, therapeutic delivery system, miRNAs

## Introduction
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are particles that are released from most, if not all cells, contain a lipid bilayer, and are unable to replicate. EVs are composed of both large and small lipid vesicles, differing mainly by their size and mechanism of generation. , , , Large vesicles, greater than 200 nm in diameter, are generated by shedding or budding from the plasma membrane. In comparison, small vesicles, including exosomes, are usually <200 nm in diameter and are mostly characterized by an endocytic origin and formed by the reverse budding of the peripheral membrane of multivesicular bodies (MVBs). However, certain small vesicles also appear to be derived from the plasma membrane, similar to large vesicles. Given the difficulty in assigning the mechanism of generation of vesicles, early classification terms for EVs, such as exosomes, ectosomes, microvesicles, and nanovesicles, based on mechanism of secretion or release are no longer recommended. In addition to EVs released from cells, there are EV mimetics, produced by direct artificial manipulation and synthetic EVs, synthesized by or generated by fusions between EVs and liposomes.
The protein content of different types of EVs largely reflect that of the parent cells and are enriched in certain molecules, including proteins (e.g., adhesion molecules, membrane trafficking molecules, cytoskeleton molecules, heat-shock proteins, cytoplasmic enzymes, signal transduction proteins, cytokines, chemokines, proteinases, cell-specific antigens, and more). Moreover, EVs also contain a broad spectrum of nucleic acids including messenger RNA (mRNA), non-coding RNA including microRNAs (miRNAs) and small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs), transposable elements (e.g., Line-1), extra-chromosomal DNA, and likely even cytoplasmic chromatin fragments (CCFs) found in senescent cells. In addition, EVs contain a variety of lipids, mostly in their membrane as well as contain and release mitochondria and metabolites to recipient cells to induce therapeutic responses. Importantly, some proteins and RNAs appear to be preferentially sorted into EVs, allowing for enrichment compared with the parent cell.
Nearly all types of human cells, including stem cells, platelets, immune cells, adipocytes, endothelial cells, cardiomyocytes, neurons, intestinal epithelial cells, and muscle cells, secrete numerous EVs. They are also found in all biological fluids, including plasma, urine, breast milk, semen, cerebrospinal fluid, and saliva. Given their presence in bodily fluids, they can be used for diagnostic purposes, especially since they contain parent cell-specific markers, including tumor antigens and inflammatory factors. More importantly, EVs from certain sources can be used therapeutically, either in their natural state or following modification to carrying nucleic acid, proteins or small molecules, since the circulating EVs have the opportunity to come in contact with many cell and tissue types. EVs can even cross the blood brain barrier to interact with and deliver their cargo to neuronal cells. This review focuses on the therapeutic applications of EVs, including the use of stem cell-derived EVs and EVs modified to carry different types of therapeutic agents (e.g., nucleic acids, drugs, and even viral particles) ( ).
 

## Stem cell-derived EVs
Stem cell-based therapies, including different types of adult stem cells, placental-derived stem cells, and even embryonic stem (ES) cells, have attracted a substantial amount of interest over the past decade. In preclinical studies, stem cells have demonstrated therapeutic effects in a variety of disease models such as neurological, , , immunological, , , cardiovascular, , , and renal diseases. , , However, despite their therapeutic capacity, the proliferation ability of stem cells is gradually weakened with aging and disease and accompanied by a certain degree of differentiation and senescence with expansion in culture, leading to loss of function. 
Adult stem cells isolated from an aged donor are less functional and the transplantation of young, healthy stem cells into an aged environment can also lead to their dysfunction. In addition, in vivo the self-renewal ability and heredity factors of stem cells cannot be fully controlled with consequent potential for tumorigenicity. Importantly, most of the intrinsic regenerative effects of stem cells are mediated not by repopulation of damaged tissue, but instead by the paracrine-like secretion of cytokines, chemokines, extracellular matrix components, growth factors, and, in particular, EVs. , , Thus, stem cell-derived EVs offer an attractive alternative to the parental stem cells for treatment of many conditions and diseases.
EVs released by stem cells interact with recipient cells and exert therapeutic effects through the effects conferred by their cargo (e.g., nucleic acids, proteins, mitochondria, and possibly lipids and metabolites). Compared with the use of stem cells, EVs offer certain therapeutic advantages, including a reduced risk of tumorigenesis and immune rejection, greater versatility in delivery routes, more concentrated cargo, prolonged period of preservation at −80°C, stability at room temperature, absence of ethical concerns, and promising drug delivery capability , (see below). In addition, unlike stem cells, the phenotype of EVs is not altered following injection, especially in older recipients with chronic inflammation.
The therapeutic potential of stem cell-derived EVs has been demonstrated in many disease models as well as extending healthspan with aging. For example, MSC-derived EVs not only increased survival but also promoted overall healing and proliferation of tubular epithelial cells in a mouse model of acute kidney disease. Multiple injections of human liver stem cell (HLSC)-derived EVs exhibited a regenerative, anti-fibrotic, and anti-inflammatory role in a mouse model of chronic kidney disease, probably due to the downregulation of pro-fibrotic genes and modulation of miRNAs that regulate fibrotic pathways. In a model of type-I-autoimmune diabetes mellitus (T1DM), adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell (ADMSC)-derived EVs upregulated the expression of regulatory T cells, interleukin (IL)-4, IL-10 and, transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, and downregulated IL-17 and interferon (IFN)-γ, thereby significantly ameliorating T1DM symptoms. Recently, EVs have been demonstrated as a therapeutic option for many neurological disorders, mostly due to their ability to cross the blood brain barrier. For example, mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived EVs exerted neuroprotective effects by promoting cell proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis through autophagy induction mediated by miR-188-3p in a rat model of Parkinson’s disease. The same source of EVs also markedly reduced amyloid β plaques to glycosphingolipids that were then subsequently taken up by microglia in a mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease. In addition, several clinical trials using stem cell-derived EVs to treat different conditions are in phase 1 or phase 2 ( ) or even completed.
 More recently, stem cell-derived EVs have shown therapeutic activity to extend healthspan. Several injections of bone marrow-derived MSC-EVs from young mice extended the healthspan and lifespan of Ercc1-deficient mice, a mouse model of accelerated aging driven by increased DNA damage. Similarly, injections of young ADMSC-derived EVs induced pro-regenerative effects decreasing oxidative stress, inflammation, and senescence in muscles and kidney of naturally aged mice, consequently improving several functions affected by aging, such as physical condition, fur regeneration, and renal function. The ability of the MSC-derived EVs to extend healthspan is, in part, due to their ability to suppress the inflammatory secretory phenotype of senescent cells that accumulate with age. In addition, this effect appears to be mediated by certain miRNAs enriched in or specific to stem cell-derived EVs. The ability of human ES cell-derived EVs to extended healthspan and lifespan in mice is partially mediated by miR-302b, enriched in the EVs from ES cells, that targets the cell cycle inhibitors Cdkn1a and Ccng2. 

## EVs for delivery of nucleic acids
The natural ability of EVs to carry nucleic acids, such as DNA fragments, mRNAs, miRNAs, and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), and the innate and unique proprieties of EVs in cell-cell communication, strongly support their usage as a therapeutic delivery system in gene therapy. , , , Unlike synthetic nanoparticles, EVs are inherently suited for transporting nucleic acids to target cells without triggering immune responses. In addition, EV-based delivery systems can be engineered to encapsulate or incorporate nucleic acids and target specific cells, offering unprecedented precision for gene therapy.
Numerous studies have highlighted the potential use of EVs for DNA delivery. For example, bone marrow MSC-derived EVs containing exogenous DNA were shown to pass their stably integrated EV-DNA to three generations of daughter cells. However, how EVs cross the cell membrane barrier and release the DNA cargo into the cytoplasm still is poorly understood. In general, exogenous DNA delivery is inefficient and prone to degradation via the DNA cytoplasmic sensing machinery, which also stimulates inflammation through activation of cGAS/Sting.
The successful development and clinical application of an mRNA-based COVID-19 vaccine has resulted in the development of numerous mRNA-based vaccine studies as well as mRNA-based therapeutic applications for genetic diseases. The use of EVs for delivering mRNA offers advantages over lipid particles when it comes to efficiency. However, incorporating exogenous mRNA into EVs has been challenging due to the current ineffective electroporation or chemical methods. To improve mRNA incorporation in EVs, new methods to improve the efficacy, stability, and safety of mRNA transfection (e.g., lipid nanoparticles, protamine nanoparticles, peptide/protein-based biological nanomaterials, and polymeric nanoparticles) are under development. , , , 
Delivery of therapeutic miRNAs through EVs has already shown efficacy in preclinical models of several diseases and disorders, e.g., cancer, cardiovascular disorders, diabetes, kidney disfunction, and neurological disorders. Given their natural ability to carry and deliver endogenous miRNAs, EVs allow for the efficient delivery and release of exogenous miRNAs to recipient cells. For example, in a mouse model of acute lung injury, intravenous injection of human umbilical cord MSC-derived EVs modified with miRNA-146a-5p significantly improved lifespan and healthspan and reduced proinflammatory markers, e.g., IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β. Another study demonstrated that intramyocardial injections of MSC-EVs carrying an miRNA-181a-overexpressing lentivirus in a mouse model of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury significantly reduced the percentage of infarct area on the myocardium and improved cardiac function parameters, such as ejection fraction. Also, cardiac progenitor cell-derived EVs with high levels of miRNA-322 reduced the infarcted area and fibrosis, and increased angiogenesis in a mouse model of myocardial infarction. 
siRNAs that silence gene expression, can be easily loaded into EVs due to their small size and multiple studies have demonstrated the therapeutic effectiveness of siRNAs delivered in EVs. An siRNA targeting the chemokine CCL2, when loaded into EVs, effectively improved the microenvironment, limited the negative effects of the inflammatory response and neuronal injury on functional recovery, and promoted the transformation of microglia/macrophages from M1 to M2 in a mouse model of spinal cord injury. Another study demonstrated the ability of human umbilical cord MSC-EVs loaded with siRNA that targets the mutant KRASG12D in pancreatic cancer cells, one of the most difficult to treat cancer targets, to reduce their proliferation, viability, and migration. 
Despite the encouraging application of EVs as a therapeutic tool for delivering nuclei acids, several challenges still remain. These challenges include identifying the right donor cells for EV production, developing a more efficient method to load therapeutic nucleic acid into EVs, and improving the targeting of the EVs. Advances in overcoming these challenges will help to facilitate safe clinical applications of nucleic acid-EVs.

## EVs and viral delivery
It is well documented that following viral infection, the infected cell produces and releases EVs that have incorporated viral components like proteins, nucleic acids, or even whole viral particles. These EVs can then transfer the viral components to other cells where they can promote viral replication and spread or alter cell function. The viral components or intact particles in EVs are protected from immune recognition by antibodies or T cells. This can allow for the virus to expand its tropism to and potentially evade the immune response. For example, HIV and hepatitis B virus (HBV) are known to be transported in EVs, contributing to chronic infection.
This ability of EVs to expand the tropism of a virus has been used by gene therapists to try to improve viral vector delivery and reduce the immunogenicity of the viral particle. Although there are examples of how this can be accomplished with viruses such as adeno-associated viruses, it appears as if the advantages of the wider tropism and reduced immunogenicity are offset by the lower transduction efficiency. However, in the context of replication-competent oncolytic viruses, the enhanced spread of viral particles in EVs might improve efficacy.

## Application of EVs for delivery of drugs
Given the ability of EVs to target and facilitate uptake into cells in vivo, EVs are being developed as a drug delivery system. , , The low immunogenicity, high biocompatibility, and stable gastrointestinal physicochemical properties of EVs make them an excellent delivery vehicle to overcome the low utilization and poor targeting of traditional drugs. EVs can effectively load therapeutic drugs and achieve targeted drug delivery to diseased tissues, reducing problems such as adverse reactions associated with conventional medications. , Commonly used methods of EV drug loading include passive and active loading. , Passive loading is mediated via co-incubation of EVs in a drug or dye solution that enters the EVs through diffusion or endocytosis. The co-incubation is a common strategy for drug loading because it is an easy operation and has low cost. However, this approach is inefficient due to the small pore size and hydrophobic properties of EV membranes. In contrast, active approaches for exogenous EV drug loading include electroporation, sonication, extrusion, freeze-thaw cycles, and incubation with saponin. 
 outlines these active approaches for loading drugs as well as nucleic acids and proteins into EVs ( ). However, even more effective approaches are under development to increase the drug-loading rate of EVs. For example, a special lipid molecule designed to efficiently fuse with the EV lipid bilayer was effective in loading different drugs into EVs. Compared with traditional drug-loading methods such as electroporation, this method has less impact on drug activity and higher drug-loading efficiency, making it potentially suitable for the clinical translation of exosome drug-loading.
 Recent studies have successfully encapsulated several poorly stable or soluble or highly cytotoxic traditional drugs in EVs to enhance their efficacy. For example, M1-type macrophage EVs, loaded with docetaxel resulted in an increase in the concentration of the drug at the tumor site and enhanced the therapeutic effect on growth of solid tumors. In addition, tanshinone IIA from herbs was encapsulated in serum-derived EVs leading to enhanced targeting to glioblastomas. Tumor cell-derived EVs, encapsulated with paclitaxel albumin and melanin, were used to treat in situ breast cancer through a combination of chemotherapy photothermal and immunotherapy. In addition, HEK293T cell-derived EVs encapsulated with doxorubicin to induce ICAM1 expression enhanced targeting of tumor cells by stimulating IFN-γ and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α secretion. 
Despite the positive results in preclinical models, long-term use of a single drug is prone to cause drug resistance problems. To combat this potential for drug resistance, especially in the context of cancer, combined drug administration enables drugs with different mechanisms of action to synergistically act on disease targets, produce synergistic effects, and improve therapeutic efficacy. Precise delivery of two drugs by co-loading in EVs can ensure their simultaneous release and action at the target site, avoiding the pharmacokinetic differences and poor synergism that may occur when the drugs are administered separately. One recent approach co-loaded MC3T3-E1 cell EVs that had a folic acid modification on the surface with an HDAC1 siRNA along with zoledronic acid that had an inhibitory effect on growth of an osteosarcoma as well as a salvage effect on normal bone cells. Tumor-derived EVs also have been fused with liposomes and co-loaded with the emitting photothermal TPE-BBT and aspirin to scavenge cancer stem cells to inhibit cancer recurrence and metastasis. 
In addition to the delivery of genotoxic drugs, EVs have been used to deliver compounds targeting specific signaling pathways. For example, EVs carrying specific traditional drugs can promote heart repair by activating intracellular immunomodulatory TRIM59/STAT3/Cyclin B2 signaling pathways. Also, the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway was activated by microneedle-mediated delivery of polydopamine containing EVs, leading to a significant efficacy in osteoarthritis. Furthermore, pluripotent stem cell-derived EVs, encapsulated with dagliflozin to activate the HIF-1α/VEGFA pathway, were used to promote angiogenesis and diabetic wound healing. 
With the development of EVs for effective drug delivery, researchers are also exploring how to optimize the characteristics, targeting specificity, administration methods, and drug-loading method of EVs to efficiently meet more practical clinical needs. In particular, EVs are being engineered to target specific cell types, the most commonly used methods include membrane fusion, chemical modifications, genetic engineering, and physical modification. These techniques helped to prolong circulation time and improve the efficacy and safety of drug delivery. For example, HSTP1 was fused to the EV-enriched surface membrane protein Lamp2b on the surface of exosomes to target the EVs to hepatic stellate cells for liver fibrosis treatment. , Engineering dopamine onto the surface of ADSC-EVs enhanced neuronal uptake by primary neurons and neuroblastoma cells. In addition, hybrid EVs composed of MSC and macrophage membranes loaded with the NAD(P)H mimetic MitoN were shown to promote the repair and regeneration of the heart. Encapsulation of RSL3 in hybrid nanovesicles of natural killer (NK) cell-derived EVs and liposomes also enhanced targeted delivery to the site of gliomas. 
Nanotechnology also has been used with EVs to improve drug delivery. For example, MSCs-EVs were modified with carboxylated Fe3O4 nanoparticles (NPs) to allow for the regulated distribution of EVs using external magnetism to improve breast cancer-targeted delivery of doxorubicin. In addition, a magnetically responsive chemotactic exosome nanomotor was developed to enhance EV delivery and precisely target abdominal aortic aneurysms, which significantly enhanced elastic matrix repair and reduced oxidative stress by activating the PI3K/Akt pathway, leading to aneurysm constriction and reversal. , 
Synthetic formulations, such as liposomes, have been widely used for clinical drug delivery. However, the interactions between synthetic carriers and the biological environment are complex and formation of a protein corona that can mask surface ligands and trigger immunological recognition often occur. Those complex designs are typically incompatible with cost-effective and time-efficient clinical-grade manufacturing processes, all of which make EVs a cheaper, more natural, and safer option for drug and nucleic acid delivery.
Although EVs are primarily administered intravenously, EVs can now also be applied nasally, orally, intraocularly, and intraperitoneally. For example, MSC-EVs were shown to be therapeutic for central nervous system disorders following intra-nasal administration. In addition, the oral administration of a drug in liposomes following fusion with milk exosomes, whose adaptive surface properties showed better membrane protein preservation and ability to cross the mucus barrier in the jejunal lumen, was significantly improved. In addition, a multifunctional hydrogel loaded with EVs was developed for the treatment of diabetic wound healing, allowing for the controlled release of exosomes in response to glucose fluctuations. 

## Summary and challenges
EVs have shown promise for delivery of therapeutic nucleic acids, small molecules, and even viral particles. In addition, embryonic and adult stem cell-derived EVs appear to be effective mediators of the therapeutic effects of the stem cells. However, several challenges still exist that need to be addressed for the widespread clinical use of EVs. The first is the rapid clearance of EVs after intravenous injection, mostly due to engulfment by macrophages and other cell types. The second is the need to limit the off-target effects of EVs. Although progress has been made at increasing the targeting EVs to certain cell types, it has been difficult to prevent the off-target uptake of EVs. Third, the composition of EVs is complex, making the production and quality control of vesicles difficult to reproduce. Reproducible, scalable engineering and production strategies are needed as well as scalable EV isolation protocols, which also include procedures for storage of EVs. Also, despite the engineering of EVs to carry a therapeutic agent(s), the many endogenous components within EVs from a specific cell type could have unwanted effects. In addition, when loaded with drugs or nucleic acids through passive methods, the molecular composition of donor cells and EVs changes. This also may be true for active loading approaches. Despite these remaining challenges, clearly EVs have the potential to be both an excellent vehicle for delivery of therapeutics as well as an effective therapeutic when derived from certain cell types such as stem cells.
