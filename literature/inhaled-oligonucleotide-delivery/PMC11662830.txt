It is currently considered a metabolic disease, and the CH25H-CYP7B1-RORα axis of cholesterol metabolism in chondrocytes plays a crucial catabolic regulatory role in its pathogenesis. Targeting of this axis in chondrocytes may provide a therapeutic approach for OA treatment. Here, in this study, we propose to use a combination of stem cell-recruiting hydrogels and lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) that modulate cholesterol metabolism to jointly promote a regenerative microenvironment. Specifically, we first developed an injectable, bioactive hydrogel composed of self-assembling peptide nanofibers that recruits endogenous synovial stem cells (SMSCs) and promotes their chondrogenic differentiation. At the same time, LNPs that regulate cholesterol metabolism are incorporated into the hydrogel and slowly released, thereby improving the inflammatory environment of OA. Enhancements were noted in the inflammatory conditions associated with OA, alongside the successful attraction of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) from the synovial membrane. These cells were then observed to differentiate into chondrocytes, contributing to effective cartilage restoration and chondrocyte regeneration, thereby offering a promising approach for OA treatment. In summary, this approach provides a feasible siRNA-based therapeutic option, offering a potential nonsurgical solution for treatment of OA.

## Introduction
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic degenerative disease predominantly characterized by progressive cartilage matrix loss, osteophyte formation, and synovial inflammation [ ]. Consequently, OA poses substantial health and economic challenges to patients and society [ , ]. Present interventions for OA focus primarily on initial pain relief, with knee replacement surgery being the likely final treatment [ , ]. There is no effective way to reverse the progressive cartilage degeneration observed in OA [ ].
Possible factors leading to OA encompass inflammation, physical damage, oxidative stress, aging, and excess weight [ ]. In recent years, the pathogenesis of OA is increasingly believed to be closely related to changes in the chondrocyte microenvironment caused by metabolic disorders [ ]. Although factors such as mechanical stress and oxidative damage play an important role in the pathological progression of OA, metabolic factors, especially abnormal cholesterol metabolism, also have a profound impact on chondrocyte health and joint homeostasis [ , ]. Chondrocytes maintain the dynamic homeostasis of cartilage through the production of the extracellular matrix (ECM), thus preserving its structural and functional integrity [ ]. Recent studies have shown that the CH25H-CYP7B1-RORα axis of cholesterol metabolism in chondrocytes plays an important role in the pathological process of OA [ ]. In OA chondrocytes, up-regulation of cholesterol hydroxylases (CH25H and CYP7B1), as well as increased production of oxysterol metabolites, leads to ECM degradation through downstream increases in retinoic acid-associated orphan receptor alpha (RORα)-mediated metalloproteinases (MMPs) and decreases in SOX9 and COL2A1. These harmful changes in the matrix also result in decreased mechanical strength and lubrication of the cartilage, hastening its wear and breakdown [ ]. Therefore, maintenance of normal cholesterol metabolism in chondrocytes is critical for joint health. Traditional early intervention treatments for OA include nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), physical therapy, and corticosteroid injections into joints, which are widely available, cost-effective, and can relieve symptoms in many patients [ ]. However, these treatments mainly focus on relieving inflammation and pain, but they have limited effects in reducing metabolic lesions and protecting ECM and cannot prevent disease progression. In addition, long-term use of NSAIDs can lead to gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, and renal side effects [ , ]. Although corticosteroid injections are effective in the short term, repeated use may accelerate cartilage degradation [ , ]. In contrast, therapies targeting cholesterol metabolism can not only slow down the degradation of ECM by restoring the metabolic balance of chondrocytes, thereby reducing mechanical damage to the joints, but also improve the inflammatory environment of OA and reduce oxidative stress caused by abnormal cholesterol metabolism, thus providing a more comprehensive treatment for OA from the perspective of metabolic regulation. In this study, we prioritized the role of cholesterol metabolism regulation because its regulatory mechanisms may be intertwined with multiple pathogenic factors of OA. Abnormal cholesterol metabolism can amplify pathological processes such as inflammation and oxidative stress, aggravate cartilage damage, and thus become a potential therapeutic target.
Delivery of nucleic acid-based therapies to cells is an effective approach for a variety of diseases [ ]. Gene silencing therapy, which uses small interfering RNA (siRNA) to block protein production by neutralizing mRNA templates, is a potent strategy for inducing therapeutic effects with high precision [ , ]. Thus, we designed a siCH25H to silence the expression of CH25H, which regulates abnormal cholesterol metabolism in chondrocytes, down-regulates MMPs, and up-regulates expression of SOX9 and COL2A1 to maintain the ECM balance and alleviate cartilage degeneration. However, treatment of OA with siRNA faces unique delivery challenges, with one major obstacle being that most drugs are rapidly cleared by the lymphatic system due to the constant exchange of synovial fluid within the joints. [ , ]. Consequently, there is a significant demand for the prolonged retention of locally administered therapeutics within the joints following treatment. Targeting with nanoparticles is a potential approach that has traditionally relied on the use of chondroitin sulfate, CII-binding peptides, and bisphosphonates [ ]. Recent research has shown that the C5-24 peptide targets areas of damaged cartilage. In OA, injured cartilage exposes specific type XII collagen α1 chains that bind to the WXPXW sequence in the C5-24 peptide [ ]. Therefore, we designed C5-24 peptide-lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) with superior siRNA encapsulation rates and targeting ability to OA regions. The LNPs were formulated using a previously reported method [ ].
In addition to regulating cholesterol metabolism in chondrocytes, it is especially crucial to revitalize the entire knee joint and foster cartilage regeneration to offset the advancing destruction typical in OA, by attracting stem cells capable of transforming into new, youthful chondrocytes [ ].
Recently, cell homing therapy by recruiting endogenous stem cells to the site of injury has shown great promise [ ]. The peptide sequence SKPPGTSS has been identified as the bone marrow homing peptide (BMHP) through the use of phage display technology [ ]. This sequence is capable of homing to bone marrow and binding to stem cells. Studies have found that this sequence can also recruit Synovium-derived mesenchymal stem cells (SMSCs) [ ]. SMSCs have been shown to promote cartilage repair [ ]. Compared to bone marrow-derived stem cells (BMSCs), SMSCs offer advantages for cartilage regeneration, including easier acquisition, higher proliferation rates, lower immunogenicity, and greater cartilage differentiation potential [ – ]. Therefore, a hydrogel containing the SKPPGTSS peptide can be designed to have the ability to recruit SMSCs. In order to make the hydrogel have the ability to promote the differentiation of SMSCs into cartilage, we discovered the transforming growth factor-β-1 (TGF- β1) mimetic peptide motif LIANAK. TGF-β1 is an important inducing factor for the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), which can promote the differentiation of MSCs into cartilage [ , ]. Studies have shown that the TGF-β1 mimetic peptide LIANAK has similar functions to TGF-β1 and can promote the cartilage differentiation of MSCs [ ]. By mixing these two peptides, a hydrogel that can recruit SMSCs and promote the cartilage differentiation of SMSCs was synthesized. Fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl (FMOC)-modified small-molecule peptides can undergo self-assembly to form hydrogels with excellent biocompatibility and enhanced mechanical properties [ ]. Additionally, these hydrogels exhibit porous structures that mimic the extracellular matrix (ECM), rendering them ideal candidates for controlled drug delivery and cellular transplantation [ ]. In this regard, we engineered a self-assembling peptide mixture composed of FMOC-modified bone marrow homing peptide motif (Fmoc-K(Fmoc)SKPPGTSS, SKP) and TGF- β1 mimetic peptide motif (Fmoc-LIANAK, TGF) that undergoes self-assembly into a peptide hydrogel that can modulate SMSCs homing and chondrogenic differentiation [ ].
Biopolymer hydrogels, including alginate and collagen, have been shown to enable localized and prolonged delivery of siRNA [ ]. However, injectable hydrogels not only exhibit sustained-release effects, but also have favorable lubricating properties for joint protection and pain relief during cartilage defect repair [ ]. Here, we present a novel strategy to establish a restorative intra-articular environment through the administration of siRNA related to cholesterol metabolism in chondrocytes using a functionalized self-assembled hydrogel that can inhibit disordered metabolism in the cartilage ECM and promote cartilage defect repair by recruiting synovial-derived stem cells and promoting their chondrogenic differentiation (Fig.  ). In this research, we aimed to clarify the therapeutic effects of this strategy for OA treatment at the cellular level and in vivo in rat joints. 

## Results

## Sustained delivery of siRNA using the LNPs@S/T system
After confirming the specific binding of the C5-24 peptide to OA tissue, we generated targeted LNPs. For this purpose, we produced uniformly sized LNPs with high siRNA encapsulation efficiency and minimal variation between batches, utilizing a NanoAssemblr microfluidic mixing device [ ]. The ionizable lipid DLin-MC3-DMA facilitates the encapsulation of siRNA, as documented in previous studies [ ], and we used an LNPs formulation that was also previously reported [ , ]. The formulations of LNPs with and without MAL are shown in Fig. S1A and B. The unconjugated LNPs had a mean diameter of ~ 137 nm, zeta potential of approximately − 11 mV (Fig. S1C, D and E). The unconjugated LNPs were conjugated to the C5-24 peptide using maleimide–thiol chemistry. The conjugated LNPs had a mean diameter of ~ 161 nm, zeta potential of approximately − 19.5 mV (Fig.  E and H), and encapsulation efficiency close to 90% (Fig.  D). Fig. F shows that when the weight ratio of lipid to siRNA exceeds 16:1(w/w), NPs can efficiently load siRNA. Fig. S1G shows that there is no significant difference in the gel electrophoresis results between washed and unwashed LNPs. The size and uniformity of the particles was confirmed using Cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) (Fig.  C). 
The delivery of siCH25H encapsulated in LNPs offers a promising strategy for treatment of OA by regulating chondrocyte cholesterol metabolism and safeguarding against reduced production of the cartilage matrix. However, the retention time of siCH25H encapsulated in LNPs in the knee joint is very short, and multiple injections are time-intensive and impractical. To extend the retention time of the plasmid in the knee joint, we engineered a self-assembled peptide hydrogel-based delivery system. It has been confirmed that polypeptides linked to an Fmoc group can self-assemble into gels. Therefore, we designed two peptide sequences connected with Fmoc: SKPPGTSS, a peptide sequence that recruits stem cells, and LIANAK, a peptide sequence that promotes cartilage differentiation of stem cells. Based on relevant literature, the peptide hydrogel was prepared using a solvent-switch technique [ ]. We mixed the two peptides together at a ratio of 1:1 and dissolved them in DMSO as an organic solvent. When the hydrogel was formed at a concentration of 3.5% w/v, it had transparency and self-supporting properties (Fig.  A and S2). And the hydrogel is injectable (Fig.  B). The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analyses revealed the peptide nanofibers (Fig. S3A and S4A). We confirmed the presence and quantification of specific biopolymers and peptides in the samples by High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) (Fig. S3B and S4B) and the chemical structures of SKP and TGF by electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI–MS) (Fig. S3C and S4C). The hydrogel was composed of entangled nanodimensional fibers, based on the TEM and SEM images (Fig.  F). The fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum (Fig.  M) of the dried SKP/TGF (S/T) peptide hydrogel showed an Amide I region at 1700–1600 cm–1, suggesting an antiparallel β-sheet-like signature. To determine the mechanism for the hydrogel formation, Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was applied. The hydrogel exhibited positive peaks at ~ 200 nm and ~ 210 nm, revealing that it adopted a secondary structure rich in β-sheets (Fig.  L). Test hydrogels were synthesized and subjected to dynamic swept-frequency rheological tests to evaluate their rheological properties. The energy storage modulus (G′) of all hydrogels exceeded the loss modulus (Gʺ) over the frequency range examined (Fig.  K). The increase in energy storage efficiency indicated successful hydrogel formation. When the shear rate was varied from 0.01 to 100 s−1, the viscosity decreased significantly, confirming that the shear-thinned hydrogels were suitable for injection (Fig.  J). As shown in F g.  I, Cy5-conjugated LNPs were evenly distributed in the hydrogels. Furthermore, the gel degradation experiment in vitro showed that the gel degraded to about 45% after 4 weeks in PBS environment, while the gel treated with proteinase K degraded to less than 20% after 4 weeks (Fig.  G). In a relatively stable in vitro environment, the release rate of LNPs from the hydrogel was relatively fast in the first 3 days, releasing about 50% of the LNPs, and then slowed down to a plateau, releasing about 75% within 40 days (Fig. S5).

## Uptake of C5-24-LNPs by inflammatory chondrocytes, regulation of cholesterol metabolism, and attenuation of OA progression via siCH25H in vitro
In order to verify whether C5-24-LNPs can be successfully taken up by inflammatory chondrocytes and play an anti-inflammatory role, we conducted relevant experiments to verify. First, we conducted a cell uptake experiment. The results were confirmed by confocal laser scanning microscopy, indicating that the LNPs efficiently delivered the siRNAs into inflammatory chondrocytes compared with naked siRNAs (Fig.  A and B). Flow cytometry further confirmed that LNPs were more efficient in delivering siRNA into inflammatory chondrocytes compared with naked siRNA (Fig. S6A and B). Flow cytometry further corroborated the efficient delivery of genes into inflammatory chondrocytes by the C5-24-LNPs, compared with ordinary lipo3000 vectors. (Fig.  C and D). As the incubation duration increased, the extensive spread of Cy5-labeled siRNA across the inflammatory chondrocyte cytoplasm indicated that the LNPs had evaded the lysosome and relocated to the cytosol (Fig.  E). These findings were substantiated through quantitative assessments of LNPs-siRNA co-localization with endosomes/lysosomes in confocal fluorescence imagery, employing Manders' coefficients M1 and M2 for analysis (Fig.  F). Manders' coefficient M1 measures the percentage of Cy5-labeled siRNA overlapping with LysoTracker Green, while M2 measures the percentage of LysoTracker Green overlapping with Cy5-labeled siRNA. Both coefficients nearing 1 would suggest significant overlap between LysoTracker Green and Cy5-labeled siRNA, indicating high co-localization. As depicted in Fig.  F, M1 remained significantly below 1 across all time intervals, particularly at 4 h. Moreover, the M2 metrics showed minimal endosomes/lysosomes containing siRNA at the 4-h mark, signifying successful endosomal/lysosomal evasion and subsequent delivery of siRNA into the cytosol. The extensive spread of Cy5-labeled siRNA within the inflammatory chondrocyte cytoplasm, which intensified over time, further confirmed the effective lysosomal escape and entry of LNPs into the cytoplasm. To elucidate the potential mechanism by which LNPs@S/T regulate cholesterol metabolism in chondrocytes with inflammation, we validated their effects on chondrocytes with inflammation in vitro. First, we verified the biocompatibility of LNPs@S/T. The experiment showed that the primary chondrocytes in LNPs@S/T demonstrated excellent viability and robust adhesion, indicating that the hydrogel was non-toxic and biocompatible (Fig. S7A and B). In addition, we studied the dose-dependent toxicity of LNPs to chondrocytes. Fig. S7E shows that when the siRNA concentration ranged from 50 nM to 3 µM, the cell viability was above 90%. We further investigated the effects of LNPs@S/T on the expression of multiple molecules related to OA, including cartilage ECM components (Col2A1), molecules that promote ECM synthesis (SOX9), three catabolic enzymes (MMP13, Adamts5, MMP9), and three cellular cholesterol metabolism markers (CH25H, CYP7B1, RORα). Western blotting (WB) and qRT-PCR analyses were conducted to assess the molecular mechanisms. The data revealed that down-regulated CH25H expression led to up-regulation of Col2A1 and SOX9 with concurrent down-regulation of Adamts5, MMP13, MM9, CYP7B1 and RORα (Fig.  H, S8, S9A and 3I) in rat chondrocytes. Meanwhile, the immunofluorescence results for the chondrocytes also demonstrated that LNPs@S/T enhanced the expression of Col2A1 and SOX9 while inhibiting the expression of MMP13 and CH25H (Fig.  G and S9B). Literature studies have shown that downregulating CH25H can effectively reduce chondrocyte apoptosis in OA [ ]. According to dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescence staining analysis (Fig. S10A, B and C), ROS in chondrocytes in the LNPs@S/T group were significantly reduced. Based on TUNEL staining (Fig. S10D and E) analyses, chondrocyte apoptosis was significantly reduced in the LNPs@S/T group. These observations suggest that down-regulation of CH25H expression may improve OA symptoms by modulating the CH25H-CYP7B1-RORα axis to reduce catabolic enzyme expression while enhancing cartilage ECM synthesis. Collectively, these findings highlight the therapeutic potential of siCH25H in mitigating OA-related pathology. 

## LNPs@S/T induces recruitment and chondrogenic differentiation of SMSCs
SKP has a positive effect on SMSCs recruitment, and TGF has a strong ability to promote SMSCs differentiation into cartilage. The combination of the two promotes the repair of damaged cartilage in OA. Therefore, we evaluated the effect of LNPs@S/T on SMSCs recruitment and differentiation. A schematic diagram of the SMSC migration experiment is shown in Fig.  A. First, we performed a Transwell assay and the results showed that a significantly higher number of cultured cells in the LNPs@S/T group moved to the underside of the porous membrane (Fig.  B C and D). Moreover, based on the live/dead cell and cytoskeleton assays, the LNPs@S/T group was verified to promote the growth, survival, and attachment of SMSCs, demonstrating a commendable capacity to sustain the quantity and health of stem cells (Fig. S7 C and D). Regarding the dose-dependent toxicity of LNPs to SMSCs, Fig. S7E shows that when the siRNA concentration ranged from 50 nM to 3 µM, the cell viability was above 90%. Based on the results of in vitro experiments, we conducted in vivo experiments to verify the recruitment ability of LNPs@S/T. CD73 and CD90 is an important marker of SMSCs [ ]. Consistently, in the stem cell recruitment experiments in vivo, it was clearly observed that SMSCs labeled by CD73 (green) and CD90 (red) in the LNPs@S/T group were densely arranged on the synovium and arranged in clusters or layers surrounding the joint area (Fig.  C). Furthermore, SMSCs were observed to infiltrate the cartilage surface, indicating a potential for their differentiation into chondrocytes. To evaluate the effect of LNPs@S/T on the differentiation of SMSCs into chondrocytes, TGF-β was added to different groups and cultured for 7 or 14 days. Alcian blue, ACAN, and COL2A1 staining was positive in all samples, while the LNPs@S/T group had the strongest staining and highest fluorescence intensity, indicating glycosaminoglycan synthesis and enhanced expression of ACAN and COL2A1 (Fig.  B, E, F). The level s of SOX9, COL2A1, and ACAN proteins in the LNPs@S/T group surpassed those in other groups at both 7 and 14 days (Fig.  G and I). The results of PCR also showed that the expression levels of Sox9 and ACAN in SMSCs in the LNPs@S/T group were significantly higher than those in other groups, consistent with the results of WB (Fig.  H). The above results indicated that LNPs@S/T has a strong ability to recruit SMSCs and excellent potential for promoting chondrogenic differentiation of SMSCs. 

## Retention and sustained release of C5-24 modified LNPs in the peptide hydrogel in vivo
Using the OA model of anterior cruciate ligament rupture, the knee joints of 10-week-old Sprague–Dawley rats were injected at 14 days after model creation (Fig.  A). Briefly, the knee joints of the rats were injected with 0.5 mg/kg siRNA preparations containing naked LNPs or C5-24-modified LNPs. All forms of siNPs were labeled with Cy5, enabling fluorescence imaging for assessment of the pharmacokinetics. To compare the in vivo retention of C5-24-LNPs between healthy and OA knees, explanted knees were imaged at 72 h after injection (Fig.  B). The findings revealed that C5-24-LNPs were preferentially retained in OA knees compared with healthy knees (Fig.  C). The C5-24-LNPs also had significantly higher retention within OA joints compared with bare-LNPs (Fig.  D and E). We further utilized Cy5 labeling of the hydrogel to assess the biodegradability of LNPs@S/T in the anterior cruciate ligament transection (ACLT) rat model  (Fig.  F). Although we observed a gradual weakening of the fluorescence signals, they were still discernible at 14 days post-injection, suggesting that LNPs-siRNA in the hydrogel were continuously released in vivo (Fig.  G and H). In addition, to explore the permeability of LNPs to cartilage, we conducted an in vitro cartilage permeability experiment. As shown in Fig. S11, LNPs can penetrate into cartilage well over time. 

## LNPs@S/T mitigates OA cartilage deterioration following ACLT surgery
The healing impact of LNPs@S/T on OA was evaluated 4 weeks after ACLT by administering it into the rat knee joint. In the LNPs@S/T group, joint swelling, inflammation, surrounding bone growth, and fibrous connections were minimally observed. The micro-CT scans showed increased osteoclastogenesis, indicating cartilage degeneration and osteomalacia. At 7 and 10 weeks post-ACLT, the knee joint osteophytes in the LNPs@S/T group were smaller, similar to those in the normal joints, while the osteophytes in the PBS, LNPs, and S/T groups were clearly visible (Fig.  A). In particular, the stability of the subchondral bone in the LNPs@S/T group closely approached that of the sham group (Fig.  B). As shown in Fig.  C and D, the LNPs@S/T group also exhibited higher tissue BMD and superior BV/TV, suggesting that LNPs@S/T can treat subchondral bone reconstruction and osteoporosis in OA. Furthermore, the knees in the LNPs@S/T group maintained their cartilage integrity and displayed only minor signs of degeneration at 7 and 10 weeks post-ACLT, based on sections stained with H&E and safranin O/fast green (Fig.  G). In addition, the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) scores [ ] were significantly decreased in the LNPs@S/T group compared with the Control group (Fig.  E). Finally, the hot plate reaction time of the treated LNPs group, S/T group, and LNPs@S/T group was significantly shorter than that of the PBS group, while there was no significant difference between the LNPs@S/T group and the Sham group, indicating that the pain caused by OA was relieved after treatment (Fig.  F). Furthermore, blood and histological evaluations were carried out on treated rats to ascertain the systemic toxicity risks linked to the degradation byproducts (Fig. S12). 
The catabolic equilibrium in the cartilage was assessed by immunohistochemical staining. At 7 weeks postoperatively, COL2A1, SOX9, and ACAN expression was up-regulated in the LNPs@S/T group, while MMP13 expression was significantly down-regulated, approaching the level in the Sham group. At 10 weeks, the findings in the LNPs@S/T group paralleled those in the Sham group (Fig.  A and S13). Quantitative analyses revealed significantly more COL2A1-, SOX9-, and ACAN-positive cells in the LNPs@S/T group compared with the PBS, LNPs, and S/T groups, and significantly fewer MMP13-positive cells in the LNPs@S/T group compared with the other groups, indicating that cartilage ECM synthesis was increased and cartilage catabolic homeostasis was regained (Fig.  B). In summary, LNPs@S/T has significant therapeutic effects on OA in vivo. 

## Intrinsic mechanisms of LNPs@S/T on SMSCs behavior
For insights into LNPs@S/T's mechanism on SMSCs, we conducted mRNA sequencing analysis on SMSCs cultured on various materials for 7 days. We divided the cells into two groups: Ctr group and LNPs@S/T group. Compared with the Ctr group, a total of 3774 DEGs were identified in the LNPs@S/T group, of which 2185 were up-regulated and 1589 were down-regulated (P < 0.05; | log2(fold change) |> 1; Fig.  A). Volcano plots of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in SMSCs with and without LNPs@S/T treatment showed a distinct segregation in gene expression (Fig.  B). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis in terms of biological process and cellular component categories indicated a robust correlation of the DEGs with cell differentiation, cell adhesion, cell proliferation, and cell migration (Fig.  D). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis in the Ctr and LNPs@S/T groups indicated that the DEGs were also enriched in functional annotations related to PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, ECM-receptor interaction, TNF signaling pathway, focal adhesion, signaling pathways regulating pluripotency of stem cells, and TGF-β signaling pathway (Fig.  E). Fig.  F illustrates the expression and crossover of each gene. These findings suggest that differences between the two groups may be primarily attributed to cell adhesion, migration, differentiation, and extracellular space/region. Next, the cell adhesion, migration, and senescence functions were analyzed, and up-regulated expression of genes related to adhesion, migration, and chondrogenic differentiation was found in the LNPs@S/T group (Fig.  C). To further validate the gene sequencing results, qRT-PCR analysis of genes associated with cell migration, cell adhesion, and cartilage development was performed. The qRT-PCR results showed that cell migration-related genes (Mmp9, Agt, Adam9), cell adhesion-related genes (Igf1r, Pik3r1, Pdgfra), and cartilage differentiation-related genes (Tgfbr2, Smad3, Bmp2) were significantly up-regulated in the LNPs@S/T group compared with the Ctr group (Fig.  G). Taken together, these results suggest that LNPs@S/T promotes the proliferation and migration of SMSCs and induces their differentiation to chondrocytes, thereby promoting the regeneration of cartilage defects caused by OA. 

## Intrinsic mechanisms of LNPs@S/T on chondrocytes behavior
To gain insight into the regulatory mechanisms of LNPs@S/T on IL-1β-induced osteoarthritis chondrocytes, we performed mRNA sequencing analysis on chondrocytes cultured on different materials for 2 days. We divided the cells into two groups: IL-1β group and LNPs@S/T group. Compared with the IL-1β group, a total of 5055 DEGs were identified in the LNPs@S/T group, of which 2735 were upregulated and 2320 were downregulated (P < 0.05; | log2(fold change) |> 1; Fig.  A). The volcano plot and heatmap of DEGs in chondrocytes treated with and without LNPs@S/T showed a clear separation of gene expression, in which the expression of genes related to cholesterol metabolism, including CH25, Cyp7b1, and RORα, was significantly downregulated, the expression of genes involved in cartilage development and homeostasis, including ACAN, Col2A1, and Sox9, was significantly upregulated, and the osteoarthritis-related genes IL-6, TNFα, and MMP3 were significantly downregulated (Fig.  B and C). GO analysis according to biological process and cellular component categories showed that DEGs were strongly correlated with ECM organization, cholesterol metabolic process, and ECM (Fig.  D). KEGG pathway analysis of the IL-1β group and LNPs@S/T group showed that DEGs were also enriched with functional annotations related to ECM-receptor interaction, TGF-β signaling pathway, Wnt signaling pathway, cholesterol metabolism, MAPK signaling pathway, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, and JAK-STAT signaling pathway (Fig.  E). The results of RNA-seq confirmed that LNPs@S/T regulated different biological processes in chondrocytes, including cartilage ECM homeostasis, cholesterol metabolism, and inflammation response. To further verify the regulation of LNPs@S/T on cartilage ECM homeostasis and inflammatory response, we performed qRT-PCR analysis on related genes. The qRT-PCR results showed that compared with the IL-1β group, the expression of ECM homeostasis-related gene Tgfβ2 in the LNPs@S/T group was upregulated, MMP3 expression was downregulated, and the expression of inflammatory response-related genes IL-6 and TNFα was downregulated (Fig.  F). The above results suggest that LNPs@S/T attenuates cartilage damage in osteoarthritis by maintaining ECM homeostasis and regulating inflammatory response. 

## Discussion
OA has become a global challenge faced by older adults. The main pathological features are cartilage matrix degradation and synovial inflammation [ ]. However, current OA treatments, including physical therapy, medication, and surgery, only relieve painful symptoms and cannot effectively slow down or stop the biological process of tissue damage, thus failing to achieve satisfactory results [ – ]. Recently, increasing numbers of studies have shown that OA is a disease associated with metabolic imbalances [ , ]. OA can be treated by regulating the abnormal metabolic features and restoring metabolic balance [ ]. Among the findings, a role of cellular cholesterol metabolism in chondrocytes has been noted for OA development. CH25H is a major effector that controls cholesterol metabolism, and its dysregulation affects a variety of diseases [ , ]. For example, CH25H hydroxylates cholesterol to 25-hydroxycholesterol, thereby promoting adipose tissue inflammation in obesity and diabetes [ ]. Furthermore, the CH25H-CYP7B1-RORα axis of cholesterol metabolism in chondrocytes has been proposed to regulate OA [ ]. Regarding the CH25H-CYP7B1-RORα axis, in OA-affected joints, the key therapeutic goal addresses the continuous deterioration of cartilage, mainly driven by the activation of enzymes that degrade the matrix and the reduced regenerative ability of chondrocytes, both related to irregular cholesterol metabolism. The cholesterol hydroxylase CH25H is a key molecule in this axis of cholesterol metabolism. Therefore, selective targeting of chondrocytes with abnormal cholesterol metabolism as an approach to address the underlying causes of OA is worthy of attention. Currently, several drugs and biologics have been validated to show excellent efficacy in altering OA pathology in animal models [ , ]. However, this approach poses significant challenges for drug delivery, off-target effects, potential infection risks with repeated injections, and economic issues.
The ability to silence any disease-related gene in a sequence-specific manner makes siRNA therapy a promising therapeutic modality [ ]. Moreover, recent advances in gene therapies have demonstrated their potential to modulate abnormal metabolism in chondrocytes for treatment of OA [ , ]. To successfully deliver siCH25H into the nucleus of chondrocytes, we chose ionizable cationic liposomes as the carrier. Owing to the high encapsulation efficiency, low batch-to-batch variation in fusion properties, and good biocompatibility, LNPs have evolved over the years to become one of the most suitable non-viral methods for in vivo delivery of nucleic acids [ ]. Although some studies have designed LNPs to deliver drugs or gene therapy for OA, they have encountered problems such as low delivery efficiency, easy clearance, and inability to deliver drugs consistently [ , ]. Meanwhile, many studies have designed functionalized LNPs to mitigate the effects of off-target cytotoxicity and drug accumulation [ , ]. Recently, the C5-24 peptide was investigated for its specific targeting of exposed collagen fibers at cartilage defects, and showed better specificity than conventional collagen fiber-binding peptides [ ]. To achieve precise cartilage targeting and efficient nuclear-targeted gene delivery, we designed and synthesized C5-24-LNPs. The potential challenges of off-target cytotoxicity and drug accumulation were overcome by attaching the collagen-binding C5-24 peptide to the surface of the LNPs. As shown in Figure S4 and Fig. 5, C5-24-LNPs had the advantages of good biocompatibility and targeting, and they existed in the joint cavity for a long time and were not easily removed. To date, there have been no studies on the application of targeted LNPs in the treatment of OA.
Nevertheless, simple application of C5-24-LNPs for treatment of OA still has the problem of inability to continuously deliver siRNA. Therefore, we have proposed a strategy to inhibit the expression of cholesterol hydroxylase CH25H and thus alleviate OA via consistent and prolonged delivery of siCH25H using a hydrogel with stem cell homing and pro-chondrogenic differentiation functions to ameliorate cartilage degeneration in OA. In this strategy, modulation of cholesterol metabolism and expansion of the chondrocyte pool are combined to achieve a synergistic effect that addresses the root cause of OA-induced cartilage destruction. By adjusting the body's natural physiological processes, the strategy effectively promoted cartilage regeneration, maintained the metabolic balance of the cartilage ECM, improved the microenvironment for the survival of chondrocytes, and simultaneously restored the vitality of chondrocytes, achieving an extended and effective treatment, which has not been reported previously. On the one hand, the sustained release of LNPs-siRNA from LNPs@S/T protected the joints by restoring normal cholesterol metabolism in cartilage, thereby attenuating the imbalance between matrix breakdown and build-up and creating a favorable environment for chondrocyte survival. On the other hand, the SKPPGTSS peptide in LNPs@S/T expanded the number of new chondrocytes by recruiting endogenous SMSCs and synergizing with the TGF-β1-mimetic peptide LIANAK to induce chondrogenic differentiation, which complemented the shrunken chondrocyte pool due to chondrocyte apoptosis and further contributed to the restoration of chondrocyte viability. After LNPs@S/T treatment, knee joints with OA from ACLT showed restoration comparable to normal joints, with fewer osteochondral and cartilage defects, preserved cartilage integrity, and minimal degeneration. Thus, the present strategy and LNPs@S/T offer a promising therapeutic approach for OA.
The present study has some limitations. First, we did not investigate the hydrogel formulation to determine the optimal hydrogel formulation, and thus different ratios of the two self-assembling peptides may have the potential to achieve better treatment outcomes. Second, there are potential limitations in recruiting SMSCs to treat OA in OA patients, especially the elderly, and the number or quality of SMSCs in elderly patients may decrease, which may affect the therapeutic effect. In future studies, we plan to explore the feasibility of combined treatment strategies with growth factors. Specifically, the ability of adding growth factors such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) or TGF-β to promote SMSCs recruitment and activity can be tested. In addition, for patients with severely reduced numbers of SMSCs, autologous or allogeneic SMSCs transplantation can be considered to maximize the therapeutic effect. Therefore, although the LNPs@S/T system has strong efficacy, a comprehensive and long-term safety evaluation must be performed before considering clinical translation.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, we have developed an LNPs@S/T system and shown that it modulates cholesterol metabolism, prevents cartilage degradation, and promotes matrix regeneration, making it an effective drug for the treatment of OA. The delivery of siCH25H in this system modulated the dysregulated balance between ECM synthesis and inflammatory phenotypes in chondrocytes with abnormal cholesterol metabolism and joints affected by OA by inhibiting CH25H expression. The hydrogel also attracted SMSCs and induced their differentiation into chondrocytes, thereby accelerating repair of cartilage defects and promoting new cartilage formation without the need for exogenous stem cells. OA-affected joints treated with LNPs@S/T exhibited improved cartilage quality comparable to that of healthy cartilage. In conclusion, the cell-free injection method has significant advantages in terms of ease of use and practicality for clinical application, and is expected to advance the development of therapeutic approaches for the benefit of OA patients.

## Materials and methods

## Material characteristics

## Preparation of the peptide hydrogel
The peptide sequences of the self-assembling peptides Fmoc-K(Fmoc)SKPPGTSS and Fmoc-LIANAK (purity > 95%) were synthesized and purified by China Peptides (Shanghai, China) via the solid phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) as previously described [ , ]. In short, 1 mmol of resin was weighed and placed in a reactor, dichloromethane (DCM) was added to swell for half an hour, then DCM was removed, the first amino acid (2 mmol) in the sequence, 2 mmol of N, N-diisopropylethylamine (DIEA), an appropriate amount of dimethylformamide (DMF) and DCM was added, and then nitrogen bubbling was reacted for 60 min. Then 5 mmol of methanol was added, reacted for half an hour, the reaction liquid was removed, and washed with DMF and methanol. Then the second amino acid in the sequence (2 mmol), 2 mmol of 1-hydroxybenzotrichloridazole tetramethyl hexafluorophosphate (HBTU) and DIEA were added to the reactor, nitrogen bubbling was reacted for half an hour, the liquid was washed away, ninhydrin was detected, and then pyridine and acetic anhydride were used for end-capping, and finally washed, an appropriate amount of decapping liquid was added to remove the Fmoc protecting group, washed, and ninhydrin was detected. Different amino acids in the sequence were added in sequence, and various modifications were performed in the same way as before. The resin was then dried with nitrogen and taken out from the reaction column, poured into a flask, and then a cutting solution (95% trifluoroacetic acid, 2% ethanedithiol, 2% triisopropylsilane, and 1% water) was added to the flask, shaken well, and the resin was filtered out. Finally, a large amount of ether was added to the filtrate to precipitate the crude product, which was then centrifuged and washed to obtain the crude product. The crude product was purified by HPLC to a purity of more than 95%, then concentrated by a freeze dryer and lyophilized into a white powder. The purity and successful synthesis of the target peptides were verified by HPLC and ESI–MS analysis. Based on the peptide hydrogel self-assembly synthesis method in previous studies, we used solvent switching technology to make the peptide gel [ , , ]. Tests found that when the peptide solution concentration reached 3.5% (w/v), the hydrogel was able to self-assemble (Fig. S2). Therefore, the final concentration of all hydrogels in this study was set to 3.5% (w/v). Briefly, Fmoc-K(Fmoc)SKPPGTSS (5 mg) and Fmoc-LIANAK (5 mg) were co-dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 160 mg/mL. Afterward, these peptide stocks were diluted with ultrapure water to a final concentration of 35 mg/mL to form a gel. For in vitro cell studies, ultrapure water sterilized by UV light was used to prepare the hydrogels. The peptide stock solution (350 µL per well in a 24-well plate) was loaded into the wells and sterilized with UV light for 1 h. Subsequently, sterile ultrapure water was added to the hydrogel to gel. The plate was then incubated (37 °C, 5% CO₂) to allow the hydrogel to equilibrate before seeding the cells.

## Preparation and characteristics of the C5-24-LNPs-siRNA
The LNPs-siRNA was synthesized by Genenc Biomedical Technology (Hangzhou) Co. Ltd. The LNPs-siRNA were fabricated via a self-assembly approach employing the ethanol dilution technique [ – ]. Dlin-MC3-DMA, DSPC, cholesterol, DMG-PEG2000, and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[(polyethylene glycol)-2000]-maleimide (DSPE-PEG2000-MAL) were purchased from AVT (Shanghai) Pharmaceutical Tech Co., Ltd. The siRNA was synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Initially, Dlin-MC3-DMA, DSPC, cholesterol, DMG-PEG2000, and 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[(polyethylene glycol)-2000]-Maleimide (DSPE-PEG2000-MAL) were mixed and dissolved in ethanol at a ratio of 50:10:38:1.5:0.5. Simultaneously, the siRNA was diluted in a citrate buffer solution, which had been prepared to a concentration of 10 mM and adjusted to a pH of 4. Following this preparation, the ethanol phase containing the lipids was rapidly mixed with the aqueous siRNA solution. The mixing was conducted at a volume ratio of 1:3 (ethanol to aqueous) and maintained at a total flow rate of 4 mL/min. This rapid mixing allowed the lipids to self-assemble into nanoparticles encapsulating the siRNA. The resulting mixture was then incubated at room temperature for a duration of 10 min to ensure complete nanoparticle formation. To purify the RNA-loaded lipid nanoparticles, the mixture was transferred to a Millipore centrifuge tube and subsequently centrifuged at 2500 rpm. After centrifugation, the nanoparticles underwent three cycles of ultrafiltration to remove any unencapsulated siRNA and residual solvents, resulting in the final purified LNPs-siRNA preparation. Then, the C5-24 peptide (CDLQYWYPIWDTH) was synthesized by ChinaPeptides (Shanghai, China) through solid phase synthesis. The crude product was refined to over 90% purity using high-performance liquid chromatography. The purified solution was then concentrated in a freeze dryer and lyophilized into a white powder. The C5-24 peptide underwent HPLC and ESI–MS analyses to confirm the target peptide's purity and validate the synthesis process. Meanwhile, the thiol group of cysteine in C5-24 peptide was conjugated to DSPE-PEG2000-Mal of LNPs surface to form C5-24-PEG2000-DSPE [ ]. Finally, C5-24-PEG2000-DSPE micelles were incorporated into the surface of LNPs. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was utilized to assess the particle size of the nanoparticles following preparation and encapsulation. The Zeta potential was measured using a specialized analyzer based on the electrostatic double-layer model at the solution interface, as described by Stern's theory. The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential were measured by laser light scattering using a Litesizer 500 (AntonPaar, Graz, Austria). Finally, the microstructure of C5-24-LNPs-siRNA was observed by cryo-TEM. The encapsulation efficiency of siRNA within the LNPs was determined using a fluorescence-based assay.

## CD analysis of the peptide
Secondary structure was analyzed by CD using a CD spectrometer J-810 spectropolarimeter (JASCO Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). To reduce scattering, dilute the sample to about 0.01% (w/v) with pure water. Approximately 400 µL of the diluted sample was transferred to a cuvette with a path length of 10 mm. CD spectra were acquired from 180 to 320 nm with a step size of 0.5 nm and the baseline was subtracted. The obtained data were averaged and smoothed using software for further analysis.

## FTIR spectroscopy
FTIR spectra of the peptide hydrogel were acquired by depositing 30 µL samples onto KBr infrared cards, drying them under vacuum conditions, and analyzing them using an FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet iS50; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). This method allowed detailed examination of the chemical structure and composition of the hydrogel.

## Rheology
The rheological properties of the peptide hydrogel were investigated using an RS75 rheometer (Haake, Germany) equipped with an 8-mm diameter parallel plate geometry. The gel was subjected to a strain of 0.5%, and stress–frequency curves were generated across frequencies ranging from 0.1 to 10 Hz. Additionally, the viscosity–shear rate relationship was assessed logarithmically over shear rates from 0.01 to 100 1/s. Storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G”) were determined and plotted for comprehensive analysis. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate to ensure accuracy and reliability of the results.

## TEM
The 10 mg/mL peptide solution was deposited onto a copper grid for TEM analysis using a JEM-2100F microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan). Images were captured to observe the nanostructure of the peptides in detail.

## Cryo-TEM
The size, morphology, and distribution of the LNPs were confirmed by cryo-TEM. For cryo-TEM examination, the temperature of the FEI Vitrobot chamber was first set to 4 °C, the relative humidity was set to 100%, a special filter paper (Whatman grade 1) was inserted, and the system was allowed to stabilize for 10 min. Then, the clamping parameters were configured with a clamping time of 3 s, a clamping force of 10 N, and a waiting time of 30 s. Liquid nitrogen was poured into the cryogen container of the Vitrobot and allowed to stabilize for 5 min to achieve a consistent low temperature. Ethane gas was then injected into the container to liquefy it to a level of approximately two-thirds of the height of the copper cup. A copper grid (Quantifoil R2/2) was hydrophilized using a glow discharge device (PELCO easiGlow) at 15 mA for 60 s. The hydrophilized copper grid was placed in the Vitrobot chamber. Here, 3 µL of the sample solution was dropped onto the grid and the sample was allowed to sit for 30 s to allow the particles to fully adsorb to the grid surface. The grids were then blotted for 2–4 s with filter paper and immediately immersed in liquid ethane after removing excess sample solution with filter paper. For subsequent examination, the samples were transferred to a TEM (FEI Talos F200C, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Naarden, The Netherlands) and kept under cryogenic conditions. Conventional TEM imaging was performed using an accelerating voltage of 300 kV. Micrographs were taken under low-dose conditions using a 4 k direct electron detection camera (Gatan K3, Pleasanton, CA, USA). Images were subsequently analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.54).

## SEM
Following gelation, the peptide hydrogel underwent a 24-h freeze-drying process in a lyophilizer. Subsequently, the lyophilized samples were meticulously examined and imaged under a SEM model S4800 by HITACHI, located in Tokyo, Japan.

## Gel retardation assay
Gel retardation assay was used to investigate the encapsulation effect of LNPs with siRNA. NPs were formulated with lipid and siRNA at the following ratios: 4:1, 8:1, 16:1, 24:1, and 32:1. 10 µL aliquots containing 0.2 µg of siRNA were mixed with RNA loading buffer and introduced into parallel wells of a 3% (w/v) agarose gel. The gel also included 1 µL/mL GelRed dye (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) in Tris–acetate-EDTA buffer. Electrophoresis was performed at 120 V for 60 min, and the gel retardation pattern was captured using an Amersham Imager 600 RGB (Amersham plc, UK).

## In vitro experiments

## Primary articular chondrocyte and SMSCs culture
Primary articular chondrocytes were extracted from the femoral condyles and tibial plateaus of 3-day-old male Sprague–Dawley rats. Articular cartilage was removed, minced, and initially digested with 0.25% trypsin–EDTA at 37 °C, shaking at 200 rpm for 1 h, then further digested overnight with 0.2% type II collagenase at 37 °C. The mixture was filtered and centrifuged to obtain the cell pellet. The cells were rinsed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then cultured in DMEM enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (KeyGen BioTECH, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (KeyGen BioTECH) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified environment. Upon reaching 70–80% confluency, the cells were exposed to murine IL-1β (Beyotime, China) to initiate inflammation. The primary articular chondrocytes were isolated from cartilage tissue using an enzymatic digestion process, as previously described. The primary SMSCs were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and cultured in our laboratory following the supplier's recommended protocols. In all cell experiments, chondrocytes were used in P1, and SMSCs were used in P3. Hydrogels were prepared as described previously. Hydrogels with a diameter of approximately 6.4 mm and a height of 2 mm were formed in standard 24-well culture plates and equilibrated with culture medium in an incubator (37 °C, 5% CO₂) for at least 1 h prior to cell seeding. Articular chondrocytes and SMSCs were harvested from their respective culture flasks using standard trypsinization followed by centrifugation. The cells were then resuspended in their respective culture media at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. The prepared hydrogels in the standard 24-well culture plates were gently aspirated to remove any excess medium. For each well, 100 µL of the cell suspension (containing 105 cells) was meticulously pipetted onto the hydrogel surface. The plates were then gently agitated to facilitate uniform cell distribution across the hydrogel. The cells were allowed to adhere to the hydrogel for 2 h in the incubator (37 °C, 5% CO₂). After the adhesion period, 500 µL of the respective culture medium was gently added to each well to cover the hydrogel and cells. The plates were then returned to the incubator for further culture.

## Live/dead cell assay
To investigate the biocompatibility of the hydrogels, we performed the live/dead cell assay. After 2 days of culture, cells were stained with a Calcein/PI Cell Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were washed three times with PBS, treated with Calcein AM/PI solution, incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, rinsed again with PBS, and then examined under a fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

## Cell proliferation assay
To investigate the biocompatibility of the hydrogels, we performed the cell proliferation assay. On days 1, 3, and 5 after seeding rat chondrocytes and SMSCs on the hydrogel, they were incubated with 200 μL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime, China) solution for 2 h at 37 °C in darkness. Then, 100-μL samples of the supernatants were moved to a 96-well plate for absorbance measurement at 450 nm using a Multimode Plate Reader. To evaluate the dose-dependent toxicity of LNPs, cell proliferation assays were conducted. Primary chondrocytes and SMSCs were cultured in suitable media under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2) and seeded in 96-well plates, ensuring uniform cell density across all wells. Serial dilutions of LNPs-siRNA were prepared, covering a dose range from 50 nM to 3 µM. The cells were then treated with the respective concentrations of LNPs-siRNA and incubated for 2 days. Following the incubation, 200 μL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (Beyotime, China) solution was added and the cells were incubated for an additional 2 h at 37 °C in the dark. Subsequently, 100 μL of the supernatant was transferred to new 96-well plates, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a multi-mode plate reader.

## Cytoskeletal staining
To study cell adhesion on the hydrogel, rat chondrocytes and SMSCs were cultured for 3 days and then subjected to cytoskeletal staining. After removing the culture medium, cells were gently washed three times with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, China) for 30 min. Following another PBS wash, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, China) for 5 min, blocked with 1% BSA for 30 min at 37 °C, and treated with rhodamine-phalloidin for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, the cells were washed, stained with DAPI, and observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (M7000).

## SMSCs migration assay
To investigate the SMSCs recruitment capacity of hydrogels, we performed SMSCs migration experiments. Migration tests for SMSCs were performed using transwell inserts with polycarbonate films (BIOFIL). Hydrogel was placed in the lower chamber, and SMSCs were seeded on the inserts. Culture medium was added above and below the inserts. After 24 h at 37 °C, non-migrated cells on the insert's top were removed. Migrated cells on the bottom were fixed with methanol and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. The cells were then photographed and counted under a light microscope.

## SMSCs differentiation assay
To investigate the cartilage differentiation-promoting function of hydrogels, we conducted SMSCs differentiation assay. Chondrocyte differentiation was induced using TGF-β in mesenchymal stem cell differentiation mediums. Briefly, 1 × 105 SMSCs were placed on the surface of different hydrogels with 500 μL of cartilage-inducing solution and incubated at 5% CO2 for 14 days, refreshing the medium every two days. On the 14th day, the cells underwent qRT-PCR for cartilage gene expression, western blot for protein levels, Alcian Blue, and immunofluorescence staining.

## Cell uptake
For the experiment to observe whether LNPs can successfully deliver siRNA to chondrocytes to avoid lysosomal degradation. First, cells were seeded in 24-well plates (3 × 104 cells/well) and induced with IL-1β for 6 h. After successful induction, the medium was aspirated and washed twice with PBS, and then an equal amount of new medium was added. The next day, cells were incubated with free Cy5-labeled siRNA or LNPs encapsulated with Cy5-labeled siRNA. The dose was 3 μg siRNA per well (30 ng/104 cells), treated at 37 °C for 1 h, then washed with PBS, observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM), stained with DAPI (blue), and then subjected to CLSM analysis (CLSM 780, Carl Zeiss Inc.). For flow cytometry experiments, cells were seeded in 24-well plates (3 × 104 cells/well) and induced with IL-1β for 6 h. After successful induction, the medium was aspirated and washed twice with PBS, and then an equal amount of new medium was added. On the next day, cells were treated with Lipo3000 (encapsulating Cy5-labeled siRNA), LNPs without targeting peptide (encapsulating Cy5-labeled siRNA), and LNPs with targeting peptide (encapsulating Cy5-labeled siRNA) at a dose of 3 μg siRNA per well (30 ng/104 cells) at 37 °C for 1 h and then washed with PBS. To remove nonspecifically bound nanoparticles, cells were washed twice with PBS at room temperature and collected in flow cytometry buffer (PBS containing 10% v/v FBS) containing DAPI viability dye. Readings were performed in live cells using a CytoFLEX S flow cytometer (Beckman, USA). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.10).

## Immunofluorescence
Chondrocytes and SMSCs attached to the gels were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, 3 μm thick sections were cut, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min, and blocked with 1% BSA for 1 h at 37 °C. They were then incubated with primary antibodies (all primary antibodies were diluted at 1:200) overnight at 4 °C, followed by 1-h incubation at 25 °C with goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 594; 1:200; AB150084; Abcam) and goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488; 1:200; AB150117; Abcam). After DAPI staining, cells were visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Celldiscoverer 7).

## qRT-PCR
Total RNA was extracted and purified from chondrocytes and SMSCs attached to the gels using the SteadyPure Universal RNA Extraction Kit II (AG21022; Accurate Biology, China). The mRNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA using the Evo M-MLV RT Kit with gDNA Clean for cPCR (AG11705; Accurate Biology). qRT-PCR was conducted using the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit IV (AG11746; Accurate Biology) on a LightCycler 480 real-time PCR system (Roche, USA). Target gene expression was normalized to β-actin gene expression. The 2-ΔΔCt method calculated relative expression levels, with all experiments conducted in triplicate. The primers used are listed in Table S4.

## WB
Cells were washed twice with chilled PBS and lysed with cold lysis solution for protein extraction. Proteins were separated using 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). The membranes were blocked with a protein-free solution (G2052; Servicebio) for 10 min, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, then washed with Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween® (25 mM Tris, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.05% Tween-20, pH 7.5; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a General ECL Chemiluminescent Detection Kit (PK10001; Proteintech, China) and a ChemiDoc™ Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad).
The following primary antibodies were used: anti-MMP13 (1:1000; Proteintech); anti-MMP9 (1:1000; Proteintech); anti-ADAMTS5 (1:1000; Abcam); anti-COL2A1 (1:1000; Proteintech); anti-CH25H (1:1000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology); anti-SOX9 (1:1000; Proteintech); anti-ACAN (1:1000; Proteintech); anti-CY7B1 (1:1000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology); anti-RORα (1:1000; Abcam); and anti-GAPDH (1:2000; Proteintech). All of the primary antibodies were diluted in a primary antibody dilution solution (P0023A; Beyotime). The following secondary antibodies were used as appropriate: goat anti-mouse or goat anti-rabbit (1:5000; Proteintech). The relevant information of the antibodies used is summarized in Table S5.

## RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis
SMSCs were categorized into two groups (Ctr and LNPs@S/T) and induced for seven days. Three biological replicates from each group were analyzed. Chondrocytes were divided into two groups: IL-1β and LNPs@S/T. They were first induced with IL-1β for 6 h, and then the chondrocytes in the LNPs@S/T group were inoculated on the surface of the hydrogel for culture. Three biological replicates were analyzed in each group. Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol and sequenced by Qingdao Oebiotech Co. Ltd. The gene expression value was transformed as log10 [TPM (Transcripts Per Million reads) + 1]. The RNA sequencing data were normalized via the fragments per kilobase per million reads method. FPKM3 of each gene was calculated and the read counts of each gene were obtained by HTSeq-count4. PCA analysis were performed using R (v 3.2.0) to evaluate the biological duplication of samples. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq25. Q value < 0.05 and foldchange > 2 or foldchange < 0.5 was set as the threshold for significantly differential expression gene (DEGs). Hierarchical cluster analysis of DEGs was performed using R (v 3.2.0) to demonstrate the expression pattern of genes in different groups and samples. The radar map of top 30 genes was drew to show the expression of up-regulated or down-regulated DEGs using R packet ggradar. Based on the hypergeometric distribution, KEGG pathway and GO analysis of DEGs were performed to screen the significant enriched term using R (v3.2.0).

## In vivo evaluation

## Rat model of OA
All animals were obtained from Jinan Pengyue Laboratory Animal Breeding Co. Ltd. (Jinan, China). In the sham group, eight female Sprague–Dawley rats (8 weeks old, average weight 150 g) underwent a mock surgery with their knee joints rinsed with saline and stitched without further treatment. An OA model was induced in another 32 rats by ACLT, which involved opening the knee joint, exposing, and cutting the ligament. Success was confirmed by significant tibial displacement when the knee was bent to 90 degrees. The joint was then cleaned with sterile saline and sewn shut. Four weeks later, these rats were divided into four treatment groups: PBS, S/T, LNPs, and LNPs@S/T. Both the sham and ACLT groups received their respective treatments:Sham group: 60 μL PBS was injected intra-articularly into the rats that underwent skin incision but not anterior cruciate ligament rupture.
PBS group: 60 μL PBS was injected intra-articularly into the rats after anterior cruciate ligament rupture.
S/T group: 60 μL of peptide hydrogel was injected intra-articularly into the rats after anterior cruciate ligament rupture.
LNPs group: 60 μL LNPs were injected into the joint cavity of rats after anterior cruciate ligament rupture.
LNPs@S/T group: 60 μL of peptide hydrogel mixed with the same dose of LNPs as in the LNPs group was injected into the joint cavity of rats after anterior cruciate ligament rupture.

## In vivo tracking of LNPs
Cy5-labeled siRNA-encased LNPs were incorporated into the hydrogel. To test the hydrogel's extended release, three Sprague–Dawley rats underwent ACLT and received intra-articular injections of 60 μL LNPs or LNPs@S/T. The cartilage targeting efficiency of C5-24-LNPs was assessed by comparing injections of targeted and non-targeted LNPs into rat knee joints. Imaging was performed on days 1, 3, 7, 10, and 14 (IVIS Lumina LT; Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA).

## Evaluation of LNPs@S/T biocompatibility
Blood samples (200 µL) were collected from the eye socket venous plexus at 1, 2, and 4 weeks post-injection for hematological analysis. Subsequently, the animals were euthanized using CO2 inhalation, and major organs were harvested and fixed for 24 h for histology.

## Micro computed tomography (micro-CT)
High-resolution micro-CT (Quantum GX2, Japan) was utilized to acquire detailed images of rat knee joints, specifically targeting the knee cartilage, distal femur, and proximal tibia. The data were reconstructed using a micro-CT workstation. Three researchers evaluated osteochondroses and measured parameters such as bone mineral density (BMD), new bone volume (BV), and bone volume fraction (BV/TV) with CT Analyser software (version 1.11, Skyscan).

## Histopathology, immunohistochemistry, and immunofluorescence
To evaluate cartilage histology, rat knee joints collected at 7 and 10 weeks were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, decalcified with EDTA (Servicebio, China), dehydrated in ethanol, and embedded in paraffin. The embedded paraffin blocks were cut into 3 μm thick slices using a microtome (RM2016, Shanghai Leica Instrument Co. Ltd.). Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and safranin-O/fast green. Immunohistochemical staining was used to evaluate inflammatory markers (MMP13) and chondrogenic markers (COL2A1, SOX9, ACAN) at 7 and 10 weeks after surgery. For immunofluorescence, sections were incubated with anti-CD90 primary antibodies (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at a dilution of 1:400 and anti-CD73 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) primary antibodies at a dilution of 1:500 overnight at 4 °C and then incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Life Technologies, Waltham, MA) at a dilution of 1:500 for 1 h at room temperature. After DAPI counterstaining, sections were imaged using an automated digital slide scanner (VS200; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as means, with error bars indicating the standard deviation (SD) from independent samples. Animal groupings were randomized prior to treatment initiation. Statistical comparisons were made using one-way analysis of variance, or two-tailed Student's t-test, as specified in the figure legends. All statistical analyses were performed using Prism software (GraphPad). A P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.
