## Aim
Periodontitis can impair the osteogenic function of periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs), thereby compromising their capacity for periodontal tissue regeneration. In this study, we explored the impact of a synthetic small molecule, DS96432529 (DS), on the osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs and its underlying mechanism.

## Methods
The viability of DS was assessed by cell proliferation assays and apoptosis analysis. Osteogenic potential was evaluated through alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity staining and Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining for mineralized nodule formation. Inflammatory injury was induced using recombinant tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). RNA sequencing analyzed signaling pathways involved in DS-enhanced osteogenic differentiation. Western blotting quantified key pathway protein expression. Specific small molecule inhibitors and agonists modulated relevant signaling pathways. Therapeutic efficacy was evaluated in a ligature-induced rat periodontitis model.

## Results
DS inhibited cell proliferation at lower concentrations but did not induce significant apoptosis at concentrations up to 250 nM. Across tested concentrations, DS significantly enhanced ALP activity and accelerated mineralized nodule formation in PDLSCs. DS upregulated mitophagy-related protein expression under both inflammatory and non-inflammatory conditions. Additionally, DS restored TNF-α-inhibited ALP activity and attenuated TNF-α-induced activation of the RIG-I-like receptor (RLR) signaling pathway. The RIG-I activator Poly(I: C) counteracted DS-mediated repair of inflammatory injury during osteogenesis. Mitophagy inhibition diminished DS’s beneficial effects on osteogenic differentiation under inflammation and reduced its suppression of RIG-I expression. DS alleviated ligation-induced alveolar bone loss in rats with periodontitis.

## Conclusions
DS enhances the osteogenic potential of PDLSCs in association with the activation of mitophagy-related processes. It mitigates inflammation-impaired osteogenesis, potentially via modulation of the RIG-I-mediated RLR signaling pathway, in association with increased mitophagy-related activity. DS represents a potent therapeutic small molecule for ameliorating periodontitis-induced bone loss.

## Introduction
Periodontitis is the primary cause of tooth loss, mainly due to the progressive destruction of periodontal tissues, particularly alveolar bone resorption [ ]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) derived from periodontal tissues—especially PDLSCs—possess tissue-specific characteristics, robust osteogenic differentiation capacity, and raise minimal ethical concerns, making them promising candidates for periodontal tissue regeneration [ ]. MSC-based therapies overcome major limitations of conventional mechanical treatments, such as insufficient formation of new tissues and inconsistent clinical outcomes, thereby offering novel therapeutic strategies for alveolar bone repair [ ]. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that transplanted PDLSCs significantly enhance periodontal tissue regeneration in experimental defect models [ ]. However, challenges remain, including the diminished differentiation potential of PDLSCs following in vitro expansion and under inflammatory conditions, which currently represent the main barriers to their clinical application [ , ].
DS96432529 (DS), a small-molecule compound first reported in 2021, was successfully synthesized through systematic piperidine ring substitution and structural optimization based on the parent compound 3-aminothieno[2,3-b]pyridine-2-carboxamide. Studies have shown that DS administration leads to significant increases in bone density in animal models of ovariectomy-induced bone loss [ ]. Research also indicates that DS enhances the biological activity of ALP, which may be associated with the inhibition of cyclin-dependent kinase 8 enzymatic activity [ , ]. However, it remains to be determined whether DS can enhance the osteogenic potential of PDLSCs and alleviate alveolar bone loss associated with periodontitis.
In this study, we reveal that DS enhances the osteogenic capacity of PDLSCs and attenuates alveolar bone loss in a periodontitis context. Furthermore, we identify the underlying molecular mechanisms mediating these effects. Our findings provide a solid theoretical basis for the application of small-molecule therapeutics in treating periodontitis-induced bone defects, positioning DS as a promising candidate for periodontitis.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture
This study protocol was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Shenzhen Longgang ENT Hospital (KY-2024-17-0). PDLSCs were isolated from healthy periodontal ligament tissue. Briefly, the middle third of the tooth root’s periodontal ligament was gently scraped and minced, then transferred into phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 3 mg/mL collagenase type I (Gibco/Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 4 mg/mL dispase (Gibco/Thermo Fisher Scientific). Following enzymatic digestion at 37°C for 1 h, the resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70-µm cell strainer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ) and evenly seeded into sterile culture flasks for primary culture. Cells were subcultured upon reaching approximately 80% confluence. The complete growth medium comprised α-MEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco/Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco/Thermo Fisher Scientific). PDLSCs from passages three to five were employed in all experimental procedures. Characterization of PDLSCs included analysis of MSC surface marker expression and assessment of their osteogenic and adipogenic differentiation potential (Supplementary Fig. 1).

## Adipogenic differentiation assay
Adipogenic differentiation was induced using complete growth medium supplemented with 1 µM dexamethasone, 100 µM indomethacin, 500 µM 3-Isobutyl-1-methylxanthine, and 50 µg/mL L-ascorbic acid. Following a 21-day induction period, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and stained with 0.3% Oil Red O (ORO; Sigma-Aldrich/Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) for 20 min.

## Osteogenic differentiation assay
Osteogenic differentiation was induced using complete growth medium supplemented with 10 mM β-Glycerophosphate, 100 nM dexamethasone, and 50 µg/mL L-ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich/Merck). Following a 7-day induction period, total protein was extracted for analysis of osteogenic differentiation-related and signaling pathway-associated proteins. ALP activity was assessed using the Alkaline Phosphatase Color Development Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After 10 or 21 days of differentiation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 2% Alizarin Red S (ARS; Sigma-Aldrich/Merck) solution for 30 min. The staining results observed under the microscope were subjected to quantitative analysis using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA). Additionally, PDLSCs were treated with recombinant TNF-α (Beyotime), polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid [Poly(I: C)], Bafilomycin A1, and Mdivi-1 (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA).

## Flow cytometry analysis
PDLSCs were harvested and washed with ice-cold PBS. The cell density was adjusted to 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. For staining, 100 µL of the cell suspension was incubated with 5 µL of each of the following antibodies in the dark at 4°C for 30 min: Allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated antibody against cluster of differentiation 105 (CD105, clone 266, Cat# 562408), Brilliant Violet 421 (BV421)-conjugated antibody against CD90 (clone 5E10, Cat# 562556), Fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated antibody against CD45 (clone HI30, Cat# 560976) and R-Phycoerythrin-Cyanine 7 (PE-Cy7)-conjugated antibody against CD73 (clone AD2, Cat# 344009). Corresponding isotype controls were also included: APC-IgG1 (clone MOPC-21, Cat# 555751), BV421-IgG1 (clone X40, Cat# 562438), FITC-IgG1 (Cat# 555748), and PE-Cy7-IgG1 (clone MOPC-21, Cat# 400125). All antibodies were from BD Pharmingen (San Diego, CA), with the exception of the PE-Cy7-conjugated antibody against CD73 and its isotype control, which were from Biolegend (San Diego, CA). Following incubation, the cells were washed twice with PBS. Cell populations were subsequently analyzed using a BD FACSAria™ II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and data quantification was performed using FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR).

## Cell proliferation assay
A total of 5 × 103 cells were seeded per well in 96-well culture plates (JET-BIOFIL, Guangzhou, China). Cells were subsequently treated with various concentrations of DS (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) for 1, 3, and 5 days. Following the treatment, 10 µl of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) solution (Beyotime) was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37°C. The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm using a SpectraMax Paradigm Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

## Apoptosis assay
The treated cells were harvested and washed with pre-cooled PBS. Subsequently, the cell density was adjusted to 1 × 106 cells/mL using the binding buffer provided in the kit. A 100 µL aliquot of the cell suspension was taken, and 5 µL of Annexin V-Fluor647 was added for staining at room temperature in the dark for 10 min. 5 min prior to flow cytometry analysis, 10 µL of propidium iodide (PI) solution was added for co-staining. The cells were then washed with pre-cooled PBS, resuspended, and loaded onto the flow cytometer for detection. Apoptotic cells were identified as Annexin V-positive, which included both early (Annexin V⁺/PI⁻, Q3 quadrant) and late apoptotic populations (Annexin V⁺/PI⁺, Q2 quadrant). Viable cells (Annexin V⁻/PI⁻, Q4) and necrotic cells (Annexin V⁻/PI⁺, Q1) were also quantified.

## Western blot assay
Total protein lysates were prepared using RIPA lysis buffer and quantified with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Proteins (40 µg per lane) were separated by BeyoGel™ Plus Precast PAGE Gel (Beyotime) and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dried milk or 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4°C with primary antibodies targeting ALP (Cat# A0514, 1:5000), RIG-I (Cat# A0550, 1:2000, ABclonal Biotechnology, Wuhan, China), PINK1 (Cat# 23274-1-AP, 1:2000), PARKIN (Cat# 14060-1-AP, 1:4000, Proteintech, Rosemont, Illinois, USA), LC3 (Cat# 12741T, 1:1000), and GAPDH (Cat# 2118 L, 1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). After TBST washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Cat# ab6721, 1:5000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

## siRNA transfection
Cells were seeded and cultured in growth medium lacking antibiotics. Subsequently, the cells were transfected with siRNA (100 nM; Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) using the Lipofectamine™ 3000 Transfection Kit (Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 6 h in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The siRNA sequences were as follows: 5′-CAGAUGAAGCCUUGGAUUA-3′ (forward) and 5′-UAAUCCAAGGCUUCAUCUG-3′ (reverse) for RIG-I. After replacing with growth medium without antibiotics, cells were cultured for 24 h before osteogenic differentiation induction.

## Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) assay
RNA was extracted from treated cells using TRIzol® Reagent (Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by reverse transcription of 1 µg RNA into cDNA with the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan). Target genes were amplified using SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara Bio) and quantified on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems/Thermo Fisher Scientific). The two-step qPCR protocol consisted of an initial activation step at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 s. A single melt curve stage (95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 60 s, 95°C for 15 s) was included to verify amplification specificity. Reactions were performed in a total volume of 20 µL. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

## Immunofluorescent staining
The cell samples were washed twice with PBS, followed by the addition of 200 nM MitoTracker® Red CMXRos (Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific) and/or Lyso-Tracker Green (1:13333) for 30 min at 37°C in the dark. Subsequently, the samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 20 min. Non-specific binding sites were then blocked with a 2% BSA solution for 2 h. The dsRNA recombinant monoclonal antibody (Cat# 85780-2-RR, 1:250, Proteintech) was added and incubated at 4°C overnight. After incubation, the samples were washed three times with PBS, followed by the addition of CoraLite® Plus 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Cat# RGAR002, 1:400, Proteintech) and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. The samples were then washed three additional times with PBS. Finally, nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime) for 5 min, and images were acquired using a confocal microscope (Leica) following completion of sample processing. Co-localization analysis was performed using ImageJ software and visualized using GraphPad Prism 10 software.

## Ligature-induced rat periodontal defect model
Male Sprague-Dawley rats (11–13 weeks old) were purchased from the Guangdong Provincial Medical Laboratory Animal Center and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Shenzhen Longgang ENT Hospital for Laboratory Animal Care, and the procedures adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines. To minimize stress and suffering, all animal surgical procedures were performed under inhalation anaesthesia induced with 3% (v/v) isoflurane (RWD, Shenzhen, China). Periodontal defects were induced by ligature placement around the maxillary molars. Briefly, rats were placed on a heated surgical platform with their mouths immobilized using elastic bands. A 5 − 0 silk suture was inserted between the interproximal spaces of the upper first molar (M1) and upper second molar (M2), looped around the buccal surface of M2, and reinserted between the interproximal area of M2 and M3. The suture was positioned below the height of contour of M2, secured with a surgeon’s knot, and placed within the gingival embrasure on the palatal side of the dentition. Rats were randomly assigned to one of three groups: (1) Control group (n = 5): treated with PBS; (2) Periodontitis group (n = 5): subjected to ligature placement only; and (3) DS treatment group (n = 5): administered DS following ligature placement. After ligation, either 100 µL of PBS or 0.1 mg/kg of DS was administered via injection into the palatal gingiva adjacent to the ligated second molar. The experimental period lasted for two months, with treatments administered two times per week. After the experimental nodes expired, humane euthanasia was performed using carbon dioxide inhalation followed by cervical dislocation.

## Histological analysis of rat periodontium
Maxilla samples were dissected from rats, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and decalcified in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid for four weeks, followed by embedding in paraffin. Paraffin-embedded Sect.  (4 μm thickness) were cut in the mesiodistal plane. After deparaffinization and rehydration, the sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining. For immunohistochemistry, the sections were incubated with primary antibody targeting RIG-I (1:500) overnight at 4°C. After washing, the sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies for 1 h, and immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB, Thermo Fisher Scientific) as the chromogen, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. For immunohistochemical quantification, five random high-power fields (×40) were selected from the periodontal ligament region of each section, avoiding areas of necrosis or vascular congestion. The percentage of positively stained area was measured using ImageJ software. All analyses were performed by two independent observers in a blinded manner.

## Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) analysis
The collected rat maxillae were scanned using a micro-CT system (Scanco Medical, Brüttisellen, Switzerland). The X-ray generator was operated at 70 kVp, 114 µA, with a fixed exposure time of 300 ms and an isometric resolution of 7.4 μm. Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction was performed, and the distance from the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ) to the alveolar bone crest (ABC) at the palatal surface of M2 was measured using ImageJ software. Bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV) was quantified within a three-dimensional region of interest (ROI) using the Scanco Medical Evaluation system. The ROI was defined as a cuboidal volume comprising the bone tissue surrounding M2.

## RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and results analysis
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent and enriched with Oligo (dT) beads. The fragmented RNA was then used for cDNA synthesis and library preparation. After terminal repair, adapter ligation, and PCR-based size selection, the cDNA libraries were pooled according to their quality concentrations and sequencing requirements before being sequenced on an Illumina platform. The resulting reads were aligned to the human reference genome. Overlapping target genes were identified and visualized using the VennDiagram R package. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened using the threshold of |log2FC| > 1 and FDR-adjusted P value < 0.05. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using the clusterProfiler package in R to investigate their biological functions and pathways.

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t-test (or Welch’s t-test if variances were unequal) was used; for comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test (or Welch’s ANOVA with Games-Howell post hoc test if variances were unequal) was applied. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

## Results

## The influence of DS on proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs
To investigate the effect of DS on the proliferation of PDLSCs, we treated PDLSCs with varying concentrations of DS and evaluated its impact on cell proliferation. The CCK-8 assay results showed that DS significantly inhibited cell proliferation at a concentration as low as 31 nM (Fig.  B-D). We next examined cell apoptosis by flow cytometry, which revealed that DS at 125 nM and 250 nM did not induce significant apoptosis in PDLSCs, whereas 500 nM DS markedly increased apoptotic cell populations (Fig.  E, F). Based on these results, DS concentrations of 125 nM and 250 nM were selected for subsequent experiments. To assess the effect of DS on osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, cells were treated with these non-apoptotic concentrations of DS and analyzed for osteogenic potential. As shown in Fig.  G-J, treatment with 125 nM and 250 nM DS significantly enhanced ALP activity and promoted mineralized nodule formation. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DS enhances the osteogenic differentiation capacity of PDLSCs.

 

## DS enhances osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs in association with mitophagy activation
To explore the mechanism by which DS promotes the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, we performed RNA-seq analysis comparing control (osteogenic induction alone) with 250 nM DS treatment. DEGs exhibiting statistical significance are visualized in a volcano plot (Fig. A, and Supplementary Table 2). Subsequent KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified several significantly enriched signaling pathways among these DEGs (Fig. B). Based on emerging evidence implicating mitophagy in the pathogenesis of periodontitis and its potential to elucidate underlying molecular mechanisms [ , ], we prioritized this pathway for further investigation. To validate our hypothesis, we examined the expression of key mitophagy-related proteins following DS treatment. Western blot analysis revealed that the levels of PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1), PARKIN and microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 beta-II (LC3B-II) were markedly increased in the DS-treated group (Fig. C, D). Immunofluorescence staining showed enhanced colocalization of mitochondria and lysosomes after 250 nM DS administration compared to the control, indicating elevated mitophagy activity (Fig. E). To further investigate the role of mitophagy in DS-induced osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, we pretreated the cells with Mdivi-1, a mitophagy inhibitor [ ], prior to 250 nM DS administration. ALP staining and mineral nodule formation assays indicated that 1 µM Mdivi-1 markedly suppressed the osteogenic effects of DS (Fig. F-I). Additionally, cells were treated with Bafilomycin A1, an agent that blocks autophagosome–lysosome fusion [ ], after DS exposure. ALP staining results indicated that 100 nM Bafilomycin A1 markedly reduced ALP activity (Supplementary Fig. 2). Collectively, these results demonstrate that DS enhances osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, and is associated with increased mitophagy-related activity.

 

## DS ameliorates the inflammatory injury in PDLSCs by inhibiting the RLR signaling pathway
To investigate the effect of DS on inflammatory damage in PDLSCs, we stimulated PDLSCs with recombinant TNF-α to induce inflammation and subsequently treated them with DS. The results showed that TNF-α did not affect the viability of PDLSCs (Supplementary Fig. 3). In addition, 100 ng/mL recombinant TNF-α significantly inhibited ALP activity, whereas DS reversed this inhibitory effect (Fig. A, B). RNA-seq analysis was then performed to compare gene expression among three conditions: osteogenic induction alone (control), TNF-α stimulation alone, and combined TNF-α + DS treatment. DEGs were identified among the three groups, and their overlapping genes were visualized using a Venn diagram (Fig. C). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of 704 DEGs exhibiting opposite expression trends in the control ∩ TNF-α versus the TNF-α ∩ TNF-α + DS group revealed multiple significantly altered signaling pathways. Among these, the RLR signaling pathway stood out due to its high level of statistical enrichment (Fig. D). Notably, accumulating evidence has linked dysregulated antiviral innate immune responses to chronic inflammatory pathologies [ ], further supporting the biological relevance of this pathway. Therefore, we prioritized the RLR signaling pathway for functional validation and mechanistic exploration. The expression patterns of key components within the RLR pathway, as identified by KEGG analysis, were displayed in a heatmap (Fig. E). Additionally, ALP was included in the heatmap as a functional marker to confirm sample integrity and treatment efficacy (Fig. E).

 

To determine whether the RLR signaling pathway participates in the therapeutic effect of DS on inflammatory injury in PDLSCs, we first examined protein levels of RIG-I, a major receptor responsible for viral RNA recognition and activation of the RLR signaling pathway [ ]. Western blot analysis demonstrated that TNF-α significantly increased RIG-I protein expression, which DS effectively suppressed (Fig. F, G). Moreover, siRNA-mediated knockdown of RIG-I markedly attenuated the inhibitory effect of TNF-α on mineral nodule formation (Supplementary Fig. 4). To further validate this finding, we used the RIG-I receptor agonist Poly(I: C). ALP staining and mineralization assays indicated that 10 µg/mL Poly(I: C) diminished the promotive effect of DS on osteogenic differentiation in inflamed PDLSCs (Fig. H-K). These findings collectively suggest that DS alleviates inflammatory injury in PDLSCs by suppressing the RIG-I-mediated RLR signaling pathway.

## DS inhibition of the RLR signaling pathway is associated with increased mitophagy-related activity
Mitophagy functions to remove damaged mitochondria and plays a critical role in regulating the RLR signaling pathway [ , ]. First, we examined mitophagy levels before and after inflammatory stimulation. Western blot analysis showed that TNF-α significantly suppressed the protein levels of PINK1 and LC3B-II, and DS pretreatment reversed this inhibition (Fig. A, B). To determine whether mitophagy is involved in the regulation of the RLR signaling pathway, we used Mdivi-1 to inhibit the mitophagy process. ALP staining and mineralization assays demonstrated that Mdivi-1 attenuated the promotive effect of DS on osteogenic differentiation in inflamed PDLSCs (Fig. C-F), and also diminished the inhibitory effect of DS on RIG-I level (Fig. G, H). Changes in components of the RLR signaling pathway were further assessed by RT-qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 5). To investigate the mechanism by which mitophagy regulates the RIG-I receptor, we assessed the release of mitochondrial RNA into the cytoplasm—which is released under inflammatory conditions due to mitochondrial damage and acts as a potent activator of RIG-I [ , ]. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that TNF-α stimulation markedly increased cytoplasmic mitochondrial RNA signals adjacent to mitochondria. DS treatment reduced this TNF-α-induced accumulation of mitochondrial RNA, whereas Mdivi-1 pretreatment attenuated the suppressive effect of DS on mitochondrial RNA elevation (Fig. I, J). These findings indicate that DS promotes the clearance of mitochondrial RNA, potentially through the restoration of mitophagy-related processes, which was associated with the inhibition of RIG-I activation.

 

## DS alleviates alveolar bone loss in experimental rat model of periodontitis
To evaluate the therapeutic effect of DS on periodontitis, we established a ligature-induced periodontitis model in rats and treated them with DS. Micro-CT analysis revealed that, compared with the control group, the distance between the CEJ and ABC was significantly increased in the periodontitis group, while the BV/TV ratio was markedly reduced. Following DS treatment, the CEJ–ABC distance decreased and the BV/TV ratio was higher than that in the periodontitis group (Fig.  A-C). Histological analysis with HE staining showed that, on the periodontitis side compared to the control side, gingival papillae were damaged, alveolar bone height was reduced, and bone resorption was enhanced. In contrast, in the DS treatment group, alveolar bone height was greater and bone resorption was attenuated relative to the periodontitis group (Fig.  D). Notably, RIG-I expression in the periodontitis group was elevated compared to the control group. After DS treatment, RIG-I expression levels significantly decreased (Fig.  E, F). These findings collectively suggest that DS can alleviate periodontitis-induced alveolar bone loss.

 

## Discussion
The osteogenic differentiation potential of PDLSCs plays a pivotal role in alveolar bone regeneration. However, this essential capacity gradually diminishes during in vitro expansion [ ]. Moreover, the inflammatory microenvironment associated with periodontitis significantly impairs the osteogenic functionality of PDLSCs [ , ]. Our experimental findings indicate that DS exerts dual therapeutic benefits by markedly enhancing PDLSC osteogenic differentiation while effectively counteracting the inhibitory effects of inflammation on this process. Importantly, DS accelerates the formation of mineralized nodules by PDLSCs, thereby augmenting their functional competence in bone regeneration—a property with substantial clinical significance. This advantage becomes particularly relevant when compared to the reduced engraftment efficiency of MSCs, which is attributed to the competitive repair mechanisms of gingival epithelial cells [ , ]. These epithelial cells typically migrate apically ahead of MSCs, leading to the formation of a hyperplastic junctional epithelium that physically hinders MSC integration [ ]. Notably, DS administration does not induce significant apoptosis. Nevertheless, our data reveal that DS inhibits PDLSC proliferation, indicating that its growth-suppressive effect at these concentrations occurs independently of apoptosis and represents non-apoptotic growth inhibition. Although current evidence suggests that proliferative activity and osteogenic commitment may be independent processes [ ], these findings underscore the necessity for precise optimization of DS concentrations and the potential use of adjunctive therapies in translational applications.
The molecular mechanism underlying DS-induced bone formation remains incompletely understood. Our KEGG pathway analysis of RNA-seq data identified mitophagy as a key regulatory process. Mitophagy maintains cellular homeostasis by selectively removing damaged or excess mitochondria [ , ]. The most well-characterized mitophagy pathway is PINK1-dependent [ ]. Under normal conditions, PINK1 is rapidly imported into mitochondria and degraded by the proteasome within the inner mitochondrial membrane, resulting in low basal expression levels [ ]. However, mitochondrial membrane depolarization stabilizes PINK1 on the outer mitochondrial membrane. PINK1 then recruits and activates the cytosolic E3 ubiquitin ligase PARKIN through its serine/threonine kinase activity. Activated PARKIN ubiquitinates outer mitochondrial membrane proteins, generating ubiquitin chains that are specifically recognized by autophagy receptors. This recognition initiates autophagosome elongation and enclosure, marked by the conversion of LC3B-I to LC3B-II. Finally, autophagosome-lysosome fusion, mediated by mitophagy-related protein complexes, leads to mitochondrial degradation [ , ]. Previous studies have shown that inflammatory mediators such as Porphyromonas gingivalis lipopolysaccharide (Pg-LPS), TNF-α, and interleukin-1beta (IL-1β) suppress PINK1-mediated mitophagy in PDLSCs [ , ]. However, this inhibition of osteogenic differentiation by inflammation can be reversed by increasing PINK1 expression, either through inhibition of ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCHL1) or exogenous spermidine administration [ , ]. Similarly, our findings demonstrated that TNF-α significantly reduced PINK1 and LC3B-II protein levels, whereas DS treatment effectively restored these levels. Notably, DS also enhanced PINK1, PARKIN and LC3B-II expression in the absence of inflammatory stimuli, and the application of Mdivi-1 (an inhibitor of mitochondrial fission and mitophagy [ ]) or Bafilomycin A1 (an agent that blocks autophagosome–lysosome fusion [ ]) attenuated the osteogenic-promoting effect of DS. These findings are consistent with previous studies, which indicate that activation of PINK1-mediated mitophagy is beneficial for the osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, although the detailed mechanism by which mitophagy promotes osteogenic differentiation remains unclear [ , ]. Collectively, these results indicate that DS enhances osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs in association with mitophagy activation and counteracts the detrimental effects of inflammation, potentially involving mitophagy-related processes.
Through KEGG enrichment analysis of RNA-seq data, we further identified the involvement of the RLR signaling pathway in the osteogenic differentiation process during DS-mediated protection against inflammatory damage. The RLR signaling pathway is a critical mechanism by which the body defends against RNA virus infection [ , ]. Upon detection of viral RNA, RIG-I undergoes conformational changes, leading to activation of the downstream adaptor protein mitochondrial antiviral-signaling protein (MAVS), which initiates a cascade of signal transductions, including recruitment of tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 3 (TRAF3) and activation of the TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1)/IKKε kinase complex [ ]. This ultimately results in IRF3/7- and NF-κB-mediated production of IFN-α/β and pro-inflammatory chemokines such as CXCL10 [ , ]. Previous studies have shown that activation of the RLR signaling pathway impairs osteogenic differentiation of MSCs, and knockdown of RIG-I effectively alleviates the inhibitory effects of aging on osteogenic differentiation in these cells [ ]. Similarly, in our study, TNF-α stimulation activated the RLR signaling pathway, whereas DS treatment suppressed its activation. Moreover, RIG-I knockdown attenuated the inhibitory effect of TNF-α on osteogenic differentiation of PDLSCs, while activation of the RIG-I receptor diminished the protective effect of DS against TNF-α-induced suppression of osteogenesis. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DS alleviates inflammation-induced impairment of osteogenic differentiation by inhibiting RIG-I-mediated activation of the RLR signaling pathway.
Mitophagy has recently been confirmed to exert a negative regulatory effect on the RLR signaling pathway [ , ]. Similarly, we found that inhibition of mitophagy led to increased levels of the RIG-I receptor. Previous studies have shown that mitophagy can alleviate RLR signaling by eliminating mitochondrial RNA, which is released from damaged mitochondria upon inflammatory stimulation and acts as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) to activate the RLR signaling pathway [ , ]. In our study, TNF-α increased the production of mitochondrial RNA, whereas DS suppressed the TNF-α-induced accumulation of mitochondrial RNA. When mitophagy was blocked, the inhibitory effect of DS on mitochondrial RNA elevation in inflamed PDLSCs was attenuated. Collectively, these findings indicate that DS facilitates mitochondrial RNA clearance, potentially via the restoration of mitophagy-related processes, which was associated with the inhibition of RIG-I activation.
We acknowledge that the observed changes in ALP activity and mineralization may have been underestimated due to DS-induced inhibition of cell proliferation. Therefore, future studies should normalize these outcomes to parameters such as cell number or total protein content to more accurately assess the osteogenic-enhancing effect of DS. Furthermore, two key mechanistic questions remain unanswered: how DS activates PINK1, and how PINK1-mediated mitophagy subsequently enhances osteogenic differentiation—both of which warrant focused investigation in future studies. Another limitation involves the specificity of the pharmacological inhibitor Mdivi-1. Although it was used to block Drp1-dependent mitochondrial fission, its potential off-target effects—including inhibition of mitochondrial complex I and ROS scavenging [ ]—could confound the interpretation of our results and require further validation. In addition, while current literature indicates that autophagy primarily suppresses the RLR pathway through degradation of MAVS [ ], our findings suggest an additional mechanism involving clearance of leaked mitochondrial RNA. This novel observation is compelling but preliminary, necessitating more rigorous mechanistic validation. Finally, although our in vivo data show a strong association between DS-mediated protection and RIG-I modulation, this correlation does not establish direct causality. Establishing a definitive causal link will be a critical goal of future research.
In conclusion, our results indicate that the small molecule compound DS enhances the osteogenic differentiation capacity of PDLSCs, which is closely linked to the modulation of mitophagy-related processes. Furthermore, in an inflammatory environment, DS suppresses RIG-I-mediated RLR signaling, potentially involving the restoration of mitophagy-related processes, which may be associated with the alleviation of inflammation-induced impairment of osteogenic differentiation (Fig.  ). The favorable outcomes observed in vivo experiments suggest that DS is a promising therapeutic candidate for the treatment of periodontitis.
