## Abstract

## Objective
Workers in the healthcare sector are exposed to a multitude of bacterial genera. The location of their work contributes significantly to shaping personal microbiomes. In this study, we investigated the role of the workspace on the nasal bacteriome of staff working in a healthcare-associated research facility.

## Methods
The anterior nares of 10 staff working in different laboratories on the ground and first floor of the research facility were aseptically swabbed. Genomic DNA from each sample was used to amplify the V3 and V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene. The amplified products were sequenced using the MiSeq sequencer (Illumina). Operational taxonomic units were filtered through MG-RAST v.3.6. Taxonomic profiling and visualizations were performed in MicrobiomeAnalyst v2.0.

## Results
The Wilcoxson Sum test at median abundances (p < 0.05) indicated that seven taxa (Micromonosporaceae, Micromonospora, Lactobacillaceae, Lactobacillus, Betaproteobacteria, Burkholderiales, Pectobacterium) were significantly diverse between ground-floor and first-floor workers. The analysis of similarity coefficient was 0.412 (p < 0.03) between the ground and the first-floor workers. Random forest analysis predicted 15 features that were significantly different (p < 0.05) in individuals working in different laboratories. Species richness and evenness also differed according to the placement of individuals in respective laboratories.

## Conclusion
These findings add to the knowledge that the healthcare support staff are at a speculated occupational risk. A slight shift in the abundances of bacterial genera and species might lead to unwanted consequences. Continual monitoring is thus warranted.

## Highlights of the Study

This study evaluated the effects of location on differences in the nasal microbiomes of healthcare staff working in a research facility.
Significantly different pathogenic genera were recorded between the occupants of the two floors of this facility.
The study stresses the importance of continual monitoring of microbiomes of health-care personnel as a preventive measure against infections.

## Introduction
Workers in the healthcare sector are exposed to microbial contaminants via several routes [ ]. Indoor air circulating within hospitals, laboratories, and ancillary areas contains a variety of microbial communities and is considered the most viable channel to transmit them to human occupants via inhalation [ – ]. The anterior nasal cavity is the primary point of entry of these bacteria into the respiratory system of humans [ ]. Thus, it is important to study the nasal microbiomes of staff working in such environments. The recent COVID-19 pandemic has increased the awareness and potential for outbreaks mediated through hospital staff [ – ]. In this regard, the International Labour Organization (ILO) has recently recommended guidelines on occupational health and safety risks under which effective monitoring through state-of-the-art research methodologies has been highlighted [ ].
Several strategies ranging from identification of bacterial strains to detection of pathogenic microbes, antibiotic resistance genes, etc. have been employed in the past with the aim of biomonitoring [ ]. Most of these approaches were syndrome-specific and event-motivated [ ]. In addition, the efforts mainly targeted selected pathogens predetermined by the World Health Organisation (WHO) [ ]. Surveillance on a community level through these traditional methods will most likely involve a range of processing and culture methods, which are time-consuming and labor-intensive. Moreover, any novel infectious pathogen will be missed by these conventional procedures. The complex and diverse nature of microbial communities residing within the human biome can be represented accurately through the adoption of more recent approaches like metagenomic sequencing [ ].
Public hospitals in Kuwait have been regarded as hot spots of commensal and opportunistic pathogenic microbes [ , , ]. Habibi and colleagues have shown that a variety of bacterial populations persist within the nasal cavities of staff associated with one of the central hospitals in the country [ ]. Although personality traits, interpersonal interactions, and seasonal disparities were hypothesized to shape the bacterial consortium residing in the human nose [ ], the influence of the location of the personnel within the laboratory on these biomes was not analyzed. Hence, the present study was aimed at extending the understanding of the effect of spatial placement on the carriage of nasal bacteria by staff working in a research facility associated with a public university/hospital in Kuwait.

## Materials and Methods

## Study Site, Subjects, and Sample Collection
Ten volunteers working as the laboratory staff of the Research Core Facility (RCF), associated with the Health Sciences Centre, Kuwait University, were inducted into this study. The RCF receives clinical specimens for analysis from different hospitals in Kuwait. Each participant worked in one of the laboratories located in the two-story building of RCF. The ground floor (GF) has six laboratories designated as L1a – Genomics Lab 1, L1b – Genomics Lab 2, L1c – Genomics Lab 3, L1d – Genomics Lab 4, L2 – Proteomics Lab 5, and L3 – Primer Synthesis Lab; the first floor (FF) has four laboratories, namely, L4 – Biochemistry Lab, L5 – Microscopy Lab, L6 – Flow-Cytometry Lab, and L7 –Tissue Culture Lab. Six staff worked on the GF (P02, P04, P05, P06, P07, P09), whereas four staff (P01, P03, P08, P10) worked on the FF. Each laboratory staff was a dedicated instrument user and spent most of the work time on the bench close to the equipment, as described in . All the workers were sampled thrice, with their due consent. The volunteers were apparently healthy with no cases of allergies. None of them were on antibiotics. All were healthy adults of both sexes. None of their legal partners worked in a similar workspace. Approval for this study was obtained from the Ethical Committee of the Health Sciences Centre, Kuwait University, Kuwait. The anterior nares of the staff were aseptically swabbed into a disposable BD CultureSwab™ (France) containing 2 mL sterile phosphate-buffered saline pH-7.0 (1X, Gibco®, Life Technologies, Auckland, NZ). Whole genomic DNA was isolated from the tip of the swabs employing the QiaAmp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) [ ]. DNA concentrations were measured using a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer and the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
 

## 16S rDNA Amplicon Sequencing
DNA isolated in the above step was amplified targeting the V3 and V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene (∼600 bp) and sequenced as described previously [ ]. In brief, PCR reaction mixtures were assembled in a volume of 25 μL by adding template DNA (2.5 μL @5 ng/μl), 2X HiFi Hot Start PCR master mix (12.5 μL) (Kapa Biosystems, Boston, MA, USA), and 10 μL primers (5 μL Forward +5 μL Reverse) [ ]. The GeneAmp® PCR System 9,700 (Applied Biosystems, Grand Island, NY, USA) was programmed for initial activation of the DNA polymerase at 95°C (3 min) and 35 cycles of denaturation (95°C for 30 s), annealing (55°C for 30 s), and extension (72°C for 30 s). The final extension was carried out at 72°C for 5 min. PCR products were purified by Agencourt AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics, Miami, FL) and tagged with Nextera indices (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The libraries were visualized on an Agilent Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Hamburg, Germany) and quantified fluorometrically on a Qubit Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). The libraries were merged in a pool of 4 nm and added to the first well of the MiSeq v2 500 cycle cartridge (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Sequencing was conducted via 2 × 250 bp paired-end chemistry. Raw sequences were processed through the automated pipeline of Metagenomic Rapid Annotations using Subsystems Technology (MG-RAST) version 3.6 [ ]. The raw data can be accessed online through the links provided in the data availability section.

## Bioinformatics Analysis and Operational Taxonomic Unit Filtering
Raw sequences were treated on the MG-RAST server, first for quality checks employing the DRISEE algorithm [ ]. After the basic sequencing statistics step, the skewer algorithm was used to trim the adapters [ ]. Denoising and normalization were performed using the fastq-mcf command line [ ]. Bowtie 2 was used to purge the host DNA contamination [ ]. The SortMeRNA algorithm was then used for gene calling at a cutoff of 70% against the M5RNA database [ ]. This filtered a total of 2,363 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) from the dataset. Additional filters (min Count >4; IQR 5 to remove 10%; prevalence in sample 20%) were employed in the MicrobiomeAnalyst v2.0 software to filter 197 OTUs for downstream statistical analysis [ ].

## Statistical Analysis and Data Visualization
The OTUs were normalized through total sum scaling and subjected to taxonomic characterization through the marker data profiling module of MicrobiomeAnalyst [ ]. Differential tree analysis (Wilcoxon Rank Sum p < 0.05) was conducted on median abundances for pairwise comparisons [ ]. Shannon’s alpha diversity indices were estimated and accorded for spatial variability through analysis of variance at p < 0.05 [ ]. The beta diversity analysis was performed on Bray-Curtis distance between the groups (FF and GF) for estimating the analysis of similarities coefficients. Results were plotted on a principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) graph. Hierarchical clustering and dendrogram analysis were performed on the Euclidean and Bray-Curtis distances, respectively, through the WARD algorithm. The LEfSe procedure was used for the linear discriminant analysis at an adjusted false discovery rate (FDR/q value <0.05) [ ]. Random forest analysis was also performed for biomarker prediction [ ].

## Results

## Taxonomic Community Patterns
The mean relative abundances (RA) of the ten common genera in the nostrils of the first-floor (FF) and ground-floor (GF) staff were different. These genera were Corynebacterium (FF-0.15; GF-0.27), Moraxella (FF-0.22; GF-0.37), Staphylococcus (FF-0.19; GF-0.07), Pseudomonas (FF-0.15; GF-0.03), Cytophaga (FF-0.05; GF-0.02), Bacillus (FF-0.04; GF-0.01), Peptonophilus (FF-0.05; GF-0.01), Myroides (FF-0.02; GF-0.03), Flavobacterium (FF-0.01; GF-0.05), Stenotrophomonas (FF-0.02; GF-0.00) ( a). Besides these genera, several other bacterial communities existed with higher abundances in the nostrils of GF workers (0.10) than the FF workers (0.07). The differential abundance testing (Wilcoxson Sum test at median abundances at p < 0.05) indicated seven taxa (Micromonosporaceae, Micromonospora, Lactobacillaceae, Lactobacillus, Betaproteobacteria, Burkholderiales, Pectobacterium) as significantly diverse between the FF and GF workers ( b).
 The RA% also varied according to the laboratory space (Lab) utilized by the staff on the GF and FF ( c). In the case of GF, P05 and P06 stationed in L1a and L1c, respectively, had very high abundances of Corynebacterium (P05-0.90; P06-0.52), whereas Moraxella (P07-0.44) was highest in personnel staying in L1b. Similarly, Flavobacterium (P05-0.24) dominated in L1d. The staff working in L2 had a greater RA% of Staphylococcus (P09-0.42), and Moraxella (P04-0.87) was overwhelmingly high in the staff present in L3. On the FF, higher dominance of Staphylococcus (P01-0.50) was recorded in the employee present in L4. The L5-occupied persona had a maximum abundance of Moraxella (P10-65). The staff stationed in L6 inhabited more Pseudomonas (P08-47), and L7 had a higher percentage of Peptonophilus (P03-0.39). However, these genera were found in all the staff, only their RA% varied, and it appears that the location of work might contribute toward these variations ( c). The machine learning algorithm in random forest analysis generated a multitude of decision trees, and each anticipated genera that were significantly different (p < 0.05) in individuals working in separate laboratories ( a). In total, 15 genera showed varying abundances according to space ( b). Many species of these genera are infectious.
 

## Community Structure and Richness
The community structure was predicted through the beta diversity analysis. A noteworthy population structure was witnessed between the FF and GF staff ( a). The analysis of similarity coefficient was 0.412 (p < 0.03). Two overlapping clusters were observed on a PCoA plot ( a). The variations across the first and second axes were 37.9% and 19.28%, respectively. These results were corroborated by our findings of Shannon’s alpha diversity index. The feature richness was slightly higher (1.3 folds) in the GF staff as compared to the FF staff ( b). The comparison of Shannon’s alpha diversity by Mann-Whitney’s Kruskal-Wallis test was significant (p < 0.05).
 We further compared Shannon’s alpha diversity at the interlaboratory scale. These results were in agreement with the PCoA and floor-wise alpha diversity analysis. Maximum species richness and evenness were recorded in P05_L1d and the lowest was in P10_L5. The pairwise comparisons by Mann-Whitney’s Kruskal-Wallis test were significant (p < 0.05) ( c).

## Discussion
Occupational risk is a common phenomenon among healthcare professionals [ ]. While most of the previous studies have concentrated on the risk assessment of doctors and nurses, who were directly involved in patient care [ ], fewer studies have focused on laboratory staff. The present study highlights the role of the workplace in defining the microbiomes of healthcare support staff.
The dominant nasal genera of the 10 staff investigated in the current study (Staphylococcus, Corynebacterium, and Moraxella) as FF or GF occupants are opportunistic pathogens. Similar results were recorded in resident scholars in Taiwan [ ], healthcare staff of the Center for Care and Discovery, University of Chicago [ ], and medical students in Colombia [ ]. In complete agreement with our results, Staphylococcus prevailed in the nostrils of clinical workers, whereas Corynebacterium and Moraxella were more common in the nasal biomes of nonclinical settings [ ]. Other studies have attributed the colonization and succession of nasal biomes as a function of interaction between the working environment as well as the other human biomes such as an individual’s skin [ ], mouth [ ], and pharynx [ – ]. This suggests to further look at bacterial communities residing within the skin, oral cavities, and throats besides other body parts of these individuals.
Microbes are ubiquitous in human biomes; however, the consortia vary according to the variations in their surroundings in which an individual spends majority of his/her time. Some previous reports have demonstrated different bacteria, fungi, viruses, and antibiotic-resistance genes within the wards, reception, pharmacy area, laboratories, and pediatric rooms of three major hospitals in Kuwait [ – , ]. Spatial diversity is, therefore, expected to shape the microbiomes. The differences observed in the microbiome of individuals working on the GF and FF of the present study are thus justified. In agreement with our findings, nasal bacterial diversity was influenced by health status and working environment in healthcare residencies in Taiwan [ ]. The nasal cavity is the most exterior part of the upper respiratory tract acting as an interface between the ambiences as well as the internal pulmonary system of human beings. The bacteria circulating within the interiors of the laboratory settings are inhaled by these professionals. This raises several concerns. Are such personnel permanent carriers? Are they at an occupational risk? Do they transmit it to their fellow colleagues and family members? Are their homes also a home to these pernicious microbial population? Extensive surveys and longitudinal scale microbiome profiling are thus warranted in this emerging field.
Higher abundances of bacterial communities were recorded on the GF as this section was near the main entrance and observed a higher human footfall. This was corroborated by the findings of Shannon’s diversity index (GF-1.70 vs. FF-1.26). A more diverse bacterial profile was also observed in P05, P06, and P09, with the highest Shannon’s diversity recorded for P05_L1d (2.10). The genomics facility (L1a–d) is extensively used by researchers and faculty members of the Health Science Center which is in close proximity to the Mubarak Al-Kabeer Hospital, Kuwait. The L2 has the next-generation sequencer and the bioinformatics workstation. This laboratory has two exits; the first one connects it with L1a–d, while the second exit is in front of the emergency exit that opens onto a short pavement connecting the core facility with the main building of the Health Science Center. P09 located near the next-generation sequencer continually receives bioaerosols from L1a–d and exterior environment, therefore comparable Shannon's index of 1.75. L3 or the Primer Synthesis Lab was a closed structure and less utilized, however, connected to L2 through a door. Because the automated DNA extractor is located here, this laboratory is also used by P06, hence the richness of Moraxella in both individuals. Outdoor aerosols are considered vectors of microbial contaminants concerning space and time [ ]. A study conducted at the Wayne County Medical Examiner’s Office (MI, USA) revealed a positive association between the beta diversity of the nose microbiome and the external environment [ ]. Shifts in microbial communities lead toward the expression of diseased states. A microbial dysbiosis index >1 is considered as undesirable [ ].
The FF is connected to the GF through an external staircase. The L4 is the largest laboratory on this floor with a single person working inside. The L5 is the microscopy unit, a heavily utilized facility. It is surprising that the least bacterial diversity (0.80) was observed in P10 working in this area. However, this is the smallest laboratory space in the entire core facility and remains dark most of the time. The L6 or the flow-cytometry room is moderately used, hence the parallel abundances as well as richness in P06. The last laboratory, i.e., the Tissue Culture Lab is the most hygienic laboratory with HEPA filters installed over the glass door beside the main entrance. Procedures in this laboratory are carried out using laminar airflow benches, and workers wear protective gear during experiments. The abundances of bacterial genera were low in P03, and the alpha diversity was similar to P10. Less diversity of bacterial, fungal, and viral communities was observed during the COVID-19 period owing to the extraordinary cleanliness regimes followed as a preventive measure [ , ].

## Conclusion
The community structure of the nasal bacteriome was defined by the spatial differences among the occupants of the research core facility. This observation suggests that the location can be considered a major contributor to the microbial diversity of nasal microbiomes of healthcare support staff. Variations were recorded in the RAs. Furthermore, it also draws attention to the regular monitoring of the microbial community composition of healthcare professionals facing a biological occupational risk, as the genera identified were pathogenic. It is also noteworthy to monitor the homes and close associates of healthcare workers for potential transfer of the nasal carriage.
