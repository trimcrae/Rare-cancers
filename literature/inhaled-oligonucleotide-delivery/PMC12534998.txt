## Abstract

## Background
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is an invasive malignant tumour for which few effective treatment options are currently available. Lenvatinib is a small-molecule inhibitor of multiple receptor tyrosine kinases used for the treatment of patients with advanced HCC. Although lenvatinib has been proven effective in treating HCC patients, clinical data show that the response rate to lenvatinib is very low and that 76% of HCC patients are insensitive to lenvatinib. Phenformin is a well-known activator of adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which has recently attracted widespread attention because of its anticancer effects. We investigated whether phenformin could enhance the efficacy of lenvatinib in treating HCC and, if so, the underlying mechanisms involved in this process.

## Methods
The anticancer effects of the combination of phenformin and lenvatinib in HCC cells were assessed in vitro and in vivo. First, colony formation, EdU and MTT assays were conducted to measure the viability of the HCC cells. Flow cytometry was used to assess the cell cycle distribution of HCC cells. Then, western blotting (WB) was performed to detect protein expression in HCC cells after various treatments. Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays were used to determine the interaction relationships of proteins. In addition, a xenograft model was used to analyze the effects of the different treatments on the proliferation of HCC cells. Immunohistochemistry and western blot assays were conducted to investigate the expression of related proteins in the tissues of the xenograft model. Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used to analyze the toxicity to the livers and kidneys of mice. Western blot assays were used to detect protein expression in human HCC samples.

## Results
High expression of platelet-derived growth factor receptor β (PDGFRβ) resulted in the insensitivity of HCC cells to lenvatinib, and PDGFRβ knockdown increased the sensitivity of HCC cells to lenvatinib. Phenformin inhibited the proliferation of HCC cells via AMPK-mediated PDGFRβ degradation. Compared with lenvatinib monotherapy, combined treatment with phenformin and lenvatinib considerably enhanced the anticancer effects both in vivo and in vitro. Mechanistic studies showed that AMPK binds PDGFRβ and promotes its degradation via the c-Cbl-mediated lysosomal pathway.

## Conclusions
Our study reports a novel combined therapy using phenformin and lenvatinib, which can increase the sensitivity of HCC cells to lenvatinib via AMPK-mediated PDGFRβ degradation. Hence, this treatment strategy may provide a personalized approach for treating HCC patients with high PDGFRβ expression and facilitate the development of basic and clinical research on the use of lenvatinib for the treatment of HCC.

## Background
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is currently the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide [ ]. The incidence rate and mortality of HCC rank fourth and second, respectively, in China, which results in severe medical burdens [ ]. HCC is highly heterogeneous, and few drugs are available for selection [ , ]. Lenvatinib is a multitarget tyrosine kinase inhibitor that targets VEGFR1-3, FGFR1-4, PDGFRα, Kit, and RET [ ]. Global multicenter phase III clinical trials have shown that, compared with that of sorafenib, the overall sustained release rate of lenvatinib in HCC has increased from 9.2% to 24.1%, making it a widely used targeted drug for the treatment of HCC in clinical practice [ ]. However, approximately 76% of HCC patients do not respond to lenvatinib, which largely limits its clinical application [ ]. Extensive research has elucidated the mechanisms underlying the insensitivity of lenvatinib, such as the activation of EGFR [ – ], methyl guanosine modification of tRNA [ ], and the RAS/MEK/ERK pathway [ ]. Different combination therapy methods, such as EGFR inhibitor gefitinib + lenvatinib [ ], lenvatinib + pembrolizumab [ ], the CDK4/6 inhibitor + lenvatinib [ ], and lenvatinib + cisplatin [ ], have been designed to improve the efficacy of lenvatinib in response to these mechanisms. However, these plans have not been clinically approved because of the safety concerns and other issues. Nonetheless, these studies provide important insight: combination therapy is a potential strategy to improve the low response rate of lenvatinib. Hence, it is worth exploring new insensitivity-related mechanisms of lenvatinib, and more effective strategies to increase the sensitivity of HCC cells to lenvatinib.
Phenformin, a well-known adenosine phosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) agonist, has better antitumour activity than metformin does [ – ]. AMPK is considered one of the key targets for treating HCC, as its activation further phosphorylates downstream substrates, coordinating a series of cellular processes, such as autophagy, apoptosis, the cell cycle, cell metabolism, and protein synthesis [ – ]. With the continuous elucidation of its mechanism of action, phenformin is not only effective when used alone, but also has a stronger inhibitory effect on tumour cells when combined with other drugs, such as chemotherapy [ ], radiotherapy [ ], targeted therapy drugs [ ], and immune checkpoint inhibitors [ ]. In particular, in clinical trials, combination therapy with dalafenib/trametinib has shown good efficacy in the treatment of melanoma [ ]. These studies suggest that phenformin has outstanding clinical translational value in various cancers [ ]. In addition, the anticancer ability of combination therapy with phenformin and lenvatinib against HCC has not yet been reported.
A recent study reported the activation of fibrosis-related pathways in the tumour tissues of HCC patients who did not improve or partially improved after treatment with lenvatinib [ ]. A key pathway in fibrosis-related signaling is platelet-derived growth factor receptor (PDGFR) activation [ – ]. PDGFR activation involves two structurally related receptor tyrosine kinases, namely PDGFRα and PDGFRβ. PDGFRα is a known target of lenvatinib, but the role of PDGFRβ in the effect of lenvatinib is still a new issue that has not been studied. PDGFRβ plays important roles in early hematopoiesis, fibrosis, and angiogenesis, which are closely related to tumour proliferation signals [ – ]. Notably, PDGFRβ is highly expressed in approximately 20% of HCC patients and is considered a potential target for HCC treatment [ , ]. The balance between the recycling and degradation of PDGFRβ is crucial to ensure the growth of cells. Ubiquitination-mediated degradation of PDGFRβ is regulated by the Cbl ubiquitin protein [ ]. A previous study reported that AMPK inhibits the expression of PDGFRβ in esophageal cancer cells [ ]. AMPK regulates the interaction between Cbl and its downstream proteins. This finding indicates that the mechanism by which AMPK regulates PDGFRβ may be related to the degradation of PDGFRβ.
In this study, we aimed to investigate the potential mechanism and effect of phenformin, which enhances the ability of lenvatinib to inhibit the growth of HCC both in vivo and in vitro. The combination of phenformin and lenvatinib provides a new therapeutic approach for lenvatinib-insensitive cells. In addition, elucidating the relationship between AMPK and PDGFRβ will provide theoretical support for combination therapies. This study provides new ideas and a basis for the treatment of HCC patients.

## Methods

## Antibodies and reagents
The antibodies and reagents used for this study are as follow. Antibodies against PDGFRβ (#3169), p-AMPK (Thr172) (#2535), AMPK (#5831), p-P70S6K (Thr389) (#9234), P70S6K (#2708), p-ERK1/2 (#4370), ERK1/2 (#9102), CyclinD1 (#55506), CDK4 (#12790), c-Cbl (#8447) and GAPDH (#2118) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (CST, Boston, USA). Antibodies against Ki-67 (#sc-23900) were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology. The secondary antibodies anti-rabbit IgG (#7074) and anti-mouse IgG (#7076) were provided by CST (Boston, USA). Commercially available siRNAs or negative control siRNAs were obtained from Ribobio Company (Shanghai, China). Phenformin was from Aladdin Chemistry Company (Shanghai, China). Lenvatinib was obtained from Shanghai Rechemscience Co. Ltd. (shanghai, China). Lipo6000™ (#C0526) was provided by Beyotime (Boston, USA). Plasmid AMPK cDNA ORF Clone vectors (#HG10394-ACG) or the pCMV3-C-GFPSpark control vectors (#CV026) were provided by Sino Biological Inc. (Beijing, China).

## Cell culture
The cells used in this study are as follow: Huh7 cell line (RCB, Japan), and HepG2 cell line were donated by the Basic Medical College of Xiangya Medical College (Changsha, Hunan, China), and SK-HEP1 cell line were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA). All cells were performed in DMEM (Gibco, Grand Island, NY) or RPMI-1640 (Hyclone Logan, UT, USA) medium added in 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Hyclone, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2).

## 3-(4, 5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2, 5 Diphenyltetrazoliumbromide (MTT) assay
Cells were plated in 96-well plates (6.0 × 103/well) overnight, and then were treated or not treated with lenvatinib or phenformin for 72 h. MTT Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO) solution (2 mg/mL, 50 µL) was subsequently transferred to wells and was incubated with cells for 5 h. 150 µL dimethylsulfoxide was finally added in plates and the absorbance was measured at 490 nm by using microplate reader (Biotek, SYNERGY HTX, VT, USA).

## Combination index (CI)
Cells were treated with different concentrations of lenvatinib or phenformin, followed by MTT assay for 72 h. CI of the two drugs were analyzed using CompuSyn software. CI < 1 indicates synergy between the two drugs; CI = 1 indicates the addition of two drugs; CI > 1 indicates antagonism between two drugs.

## Colony formation assay
Cells were seeded in wells of 24-well plates (2 × 103 cells/well) overnight and were incubated with a range of concentrations of lenvatinib or phenformin for 6–8 additional days. Next, cells were fixed with 10% formaldehyde, which were then stained for 30 min at room temperature with 0.1% crystal violet. Absorbance value at 550 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, SYNERGY HTX, VT, USA).

## Western blotting (WB)
Protein extracts were resolved through SDS-PAGE, then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Boston, MA, USA) and probed with corresponding primary antibodies according to the experimental purpose. Peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse or anti-rabbit antibody was used as secondary antibody and the antigen-antibody reaction was visualized by ChemiDoc system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). The intensities of blot bands were quantitated using Image J software. The data were normalized to load vehicle control with the antibody against GAPDH. The original images of all western blots were displayed in original data file.

## Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
1 × 107 HepG2 cells were lysed with the lysis IP buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with PMSF. After cocktail for 30 min on ice, cell lysate was centrifuged and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was blocked by protein A/G agarose beads at 4 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation again, the supernatant was incubated with Normal IgG or appropriate primary anti-body against PDGFRβ (sc-374573, Santa Cruz) overnight at 4 °C with rotation. The beads were then washed four times with IP buffer. Sample loading buffer (5×) was mixed with the beads and boiled for 10 min. Thereafter, the protein samples were determined by WB.

## RNA extraction and ‌quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction‌ (qRT-PCR)
Cells were collected at 48 h after transfection. Then, the total RNA of cells was extracted with trizol reagent. Next, 1 mg of total RNA was transcribed into cDNA using a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Thermo, Shanghai, China). After that, quantification was conducted by TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (Bio-Rad, Shanghai, China), and the thermal cycle was as follows: 95 °C for 60 s, 1 cycle; 95 °C for 15 s, 61 °C for 20 s, 40 cycles. Last, the result was calculated with the 2−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH serving as the internal reference. The primer sequences (5’- >3’) were listed as follows: GAPDH forward, CAGGAGGCATTGCTGATGAT, GAPDH reverse, GAAGGCTGGGGCTCATTT; PDGFRβ forward, TTACCACATCCGCTCCATCCTG; PDGFRβ reverse, ATTCACACTCTCCGTCACATTGC.

## SiRNA transfection
Cells were plated in wells of 6-well plates (2.0 × 105/well). After 12 h, cells were transfected with 50 nM RNAi oligonucleotides and 50 nM negative control siRNA (Ribobio, Shanghai, China) with lipofectamine 6000 (Invitrogen, Eugene, USA) in the absence of FBS for 5 h. The cells were replaced into complete culture medium for an additional 24 h after a washing with PBS. Cell protein was collected and specific silencing was confirmed by WB and the sequences (5’- >3’) of AMPK siRNAs were listed as follows: siAMPK-1, GGACAGGGAAGCCUUAAAUTT; siAMPK-2, TTATGTCTTTGATAGTTGCTC; siCbl, CUCUCUUCCAAGCACUGA; PDGFRβ siRNA were listed as follows: siPDGFRβ−1, CAACGAGTCTCCAGTGCTA; siPDGFRβ−2, GGAACGTGCTCATCTGTGA; siPDGFRβ−3, CGATGAAAGTGGCCGTCAA.

## Overexpression of AMPK (AMPK-OE)
According to the instructions of transfection reagent, HepG2 cells (2 × 105 cells/well) were transfected with plasmid AMPK cDNA ORF Clone vector or the pCMV3-C-GFPSpark control vector (Sino Biological Inc., Beijing, China) using transfection reagent Lipo6000™ for 6 h. Next, the cells were cultured in complete medium for 48 h. Finally, the samples were collected for detection of western blot.

## Flow cytometry
When the coverage of HepG2 cells exceeded 70%, cells were harvested after trypsinization. After washing once with pre-cooled PBS, cells were fixed in 70% pre-cooled ethanol at 4 °C for 1 h. After washing with PBS, the cells were re-suspended with the prepared staining solution and the cell cycle was detected by flow cytometry (Facs Cantoll 488 N, BD, USA). Preparation ratio of cell staining solution: 40 × PI solution (2 mg/ml, Sigma): 100 × RNase solution (10 mg/ml, Takara): 1 × PBS = 25: 10: 1000.

## Immunoprecipitation-Mass spectrometry (IP-MS) assay
HepG2 cells were lysed, and then total protein was extracted. The peptide samples were analyzed using Thermo Fisher ExactiveTM plus Obitrap mass spectrometry. Mass spectrometry analysis was carried out at the Bioprofile Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) in the positive-ion mode with an automated datadependent MS/MS analysis.

## Histology
Tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, then embedded in paraffin and sectioned to 7 μm. Sections were stained with H&E. For immunohistochemistry staining, sections were progressively deparaffinised and rehydrated with xylene, 100%, 95%, and 75% ethanol. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with 3% H2O2 for 20 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Then, sections were washed with PBS and boiled in tris-EDTA retrieval solution for 5 min in a pressure cooker. Next, sections were incubated with primary antibody anti-Ki-67 or anti-PDGFRβ overnight at 4 °C. Sections were washed with PBS again and incubated with Reagent 2 and Reagent 3 (Goat hypersensitivity two-step detection kit (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China)) according to manufacturers’ instructions. Sections were stained by DAB Substrate Kit (Cell Signaling, Beverly, MA, USA) and counterstained with Gill’s haematoxylin (Solarbio, Beijing, China). Last, sections were then dehydrated and mounted using neutral resins (Solarbio, China). In IHC staining analysis, data were calculated by Image J software. The data were normalized to load vehicle control.

## Mouse xenografts
Four- to six-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (Changsha, China). All mice were treated according to protocols approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan Normal University (D2022009). HepG2 or Huh7 cells (5 × 106 cells per mouse) were injected subcutaneously into the right posterior flanks of BALB/c nude mice (5 mice per group). Tumour volume based on caliper measurements was calculated using the modified ellipsoidal formula: tumour volume = ½ length × width2. After tumour establishment, mice were randomly assigned to 6 days per week treatment with vehicle, lenvatinib (2.5 mg kg−1 or 3.2 mg kg−1, oral gavage), phenformin (80 mg kg−1, oral gavage) or a drug combination in which each compound was administered at the same dose and schedule as the single agent. For histological, mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation after anesthetized with inhaled sevoflurane.

## Human hepatocellular carcinoma specimens
Informed Consent: De-identified human liver specimens obtained from The Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients and all studies using human tissue samples were approved by the Human Subjects Committee of Hunan Normal University (2020274). All patients received the informed consent forms and agreed to participate in the study. WB assays were performed to measure the protein level of PDGFRβ in HCC tissues (n = 20) and adjacent hepatocellular normal tissues (n = 20). The information on HCC patients was shown in Table S1.

## Statistical analyses
The data of three independent experiments were expressed as mean ± SEM. Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 6.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). A statistical analysis was performed by Student’s t-test, and p-values < 0.05 were thought as statistically significant. When individual data were shown, there were representatives of at least three independent experiments.

## Results

## PDGFRβ knock down enhances the inhibitory effect of lenvatinib on HCC cells
PDGFRβ expression was detected by WB in one cohort which included 20 pairs of HCC tissues and corresponding distal noncancerous liver tissues, to analyze the expression of PDGFRβ in clinical HCC. The results revealed that PDGFRβ expression was significantly increased in HCC tissues compared with that in matched noncancerous liver tissues (Fig.  A). The clinicopathological features of all these HCC patients are presented in Table . In addition, TCGA-based RNA sequencing data aggregation showed that PDGFRB was highly expressed in HCC clinical samples (Figure A). Moreover, consistent with the clinical data, the basal expression of PDGFRβ was higher in three types of HCC cells than in normal cells (Fig.  B). Among the three HCC cell lines, PDGFRβ had the highest expression in HepG2 cells and the lowest expression in Huh7 cells. These results indicate that PDGFRβ is highly expressed in HCC.
 Next, we analyzed the relationship between PDGFRβ and the sensitivity of HCC cells to lenvatinib in vitro and in vivo. The sensitivity of the three HCC cell lines to lenvatinib was explored by clonogenic (Figure S1B) and MTT assays (Fig. C). In a previous study [ ], cell lines with half-maximum inhibitory concentration (IC50) values less than 5 µM were classified as relatively sensitive cells. One lenvatinib-sensitive HCC cell line (i.e., the Huh7 cell line) and two lenvatinib-insensitive HCC cell lines (i.e., the HepG2 and SK-HEP1 cell lines) were screened out. Next, two nude mouse subcutaneous transplant tumour models were established by subcutaneously implanting Huh7 or HepG2 cells. Both groups of model mice were given solvent control or 2.5 mg/kg lenvatinib. As shown in Fig. E and G, the growth of tumours derived from the Huh7 cell line was significantly inhibited, whereas that of tumours derived from the HepG2 cell line was not significantly decreased, according to comparisons of the size (Fig. E), weight (Fig. F), volume (Fig. G), and expression of Ki67 (Figure S1D) in tumour tissues. In addition, there were no significant changes in the body weights of the tumour-bearing mice in any of the groups, suggesting that the dose of lenvatinib had no significant toxic side effects on the nude mice (Figure S1C). Mechanistically, lenvatinib downregulated PDGFRβ in lenvatinib-sensitive Huh7 cell lines but upregulated PDGFRβ in lenvatinib-insensitive cell lines (HepG2 and SK-HEP1) (Fig. D). Similar phenomena have also been observed in tumour samples (Fig. H). These results suggested that lenvatinib induced abnormal upregulation of PDGFRβ in insensitive HCC cells and corresponding transplanted tumours. To better understand the relationship between PDGFRβ and sensitivity to lenvatinib, two lenvatinib-insensitive HCC cell lines were transfected with three siRNAs targeting PDGFRβ. The transfection efficiency was determined by a WB assay (Fig. I). Changes in the ability of lenvatinib to inhibit PDGFRβ-knockdown HCC cell proliferation were subsequently analyzed. As a result, siPDGFRβ, which has a better silencing effect, significantly enhanced the inhibitory effect of lenvatinib on HCC cell proliferation (Fig. J).
Taken together, these findings suggest that higher PDGFRβ expression results in poorer sensitivity to lenvatinib in HCC cells and that PDGFRβ knockdown helps enhance the anti-proliferative ability of lenvatinib in HCC cells. Therefore, it is particularly important to develop effective drugs to decrease the level of PDGFRβ and enhance the inhibitory capacity of lenvatinib.

## Regulating AMPK influences the level of PDGFRβ and HCC cell proliferation
AMPK is a potential target in HCC, but its relationship with PDGFRβ in HCC is still unclear [ , ]. In this study, the basal expression of AMPK in three HCC cell lines was first detected to further elucidate the relationship between AMPK and PDGFRβ. As shown in Fig. A, AMPK was expressed at the highest level in Huh7 cells and at the lowest level in HepG2 cells. Subsequently, AMPK cDNA plasmids were transfected into HepG2 cells, and siRNAs targeting AMPK were transfected into Huh7 cells. The transfection efficiency of overexpressing (Fig. B) and silencing (Fig. C) AMPK was validated by WB assays. Immediately after that, we analyzed the effect of regulating AMPK on the proliferation ability of HCC cells. The results of the EdU assay revealed that the AMPK-OE significantly reduced the number of EdU-positive HepG2 cells (Fig. D). However, AMPK knockdown dramatically increased that in Huh7 cells (Fig. E). In addition, the MTT results revealed that overexpressing AMPK inhibited the viability of HCC cells (Fig. F), whereas siAMPK had the opposite effect (Fig. G). Interestingly, there is a certain relationship between the proliferation ability of HCC cells and the level of PDGFRβ in AMPK-regulated HCC cells. As shown in Fig. H, PDGFRβ was upregulated in the AMPK-OE cells, but significantly downregulated in the AMPK-knockdown cells (Fig. I). These results indicate that AMPK negatively regulates the protein level of PDGFRβ and affects HCC cell proliferation.
 

## AMPK negatively regulates the level of PDGFRβ and increases the sensitivity of HCC cells to lenvatinib
Studies have shown that regulating AMPK can influence HCC cell viability, but whether reducing PDGFRβ by activating AMPK could increase the sensitivity of HCC cells to lenvatinib has not been reported. To this end, we compared the inhibitory effects of lenvatinib and PDGFRβ on AMPK-regulated HCC cells. The results of the MTT and EdU assays revealed that AMPK-OE enhanced the inhibitory effects of lenvatinib on the viability (Fig.  A) and DNA synthesis (Fig.  E) of HCC cells, whereas AMPK knockdown weakened this inhibitory effect of lenvatinib (Fig.  B and F). Moreover, lenvatinib abnormally increased the level of PDGFRβ in HepG2 cells, whereas AMPK-OE significantly decreased this effect (Fig.  C). In contrast, lenvatinib significantly downregulated PDGFRβ in the Huh7 cell line, while siAMPK reversed this effect (Fig.  D). These results confirmed that AMPK knockdown decreased, whereas AMPK overexpression increased the downregulation of PDGFRβ and the sensitivity of HCC cells to lenvatinib.
 

## AMPK binds PDGFRβ and promotes its degradation through the c-Cbl-mediated lysosomal pathway
The molecular mechanism by which AMPK downregulates PDGFRβ deserves further analysis. Hence, to clarify this issue, co-IP assays were performed with an anti-PDGFRβ antibody or an anti-AMPK antibody, and the relevant proteins in the immunoprecipitated complex were detected by WB. The results showed that PDGFRβ bound to AMPK in both the HepG2 (Fig. A and B) and Huh7 cell lines (Figure S2A). Subsequently, an IP-MS assay further validated the interaction between AMPK and PDGFRβ, as shown in Fig. C. AMPK physically interacted with PDGFRβ in HCC cells. The question remains: after AMPK binds with PDGFRβ, how does it promote PDGFRβ decay? Studies have shown that PDGFRβ degradation is a key step in its decay [ ]. Hence, we next analyzed the effect of AMPK on PDGFRβ protein stability after blocking protein synthesis with cycloheximide (CHX). Compared with that in HepG2 cells transfected with the vector cDNA plasmid, the mRNA level of PDGFRβ did not change (Fig. D), but the half-life of PDGFRβ was dramatically shorter in the AMPK-OE HepG2 cells (Fig. F). In contrast, the mRNA level of PDGFRβ did not change (Fig. E), but the half-life of PDGFRβ was significantly prolonged in the AMPK-knockdown Huh7 cells (Fig. G). These results indicate that AMPK tightly controls the stability of the PDGFRβ protein. Moreover, the decrease in PDGFRβ in the AMPK-OE-transfected HepG2 cells was specifically reversed by the lysosome inhibitor chloroquine (CQ), suggesting that AMPK-mediated PDGFRβ destabilization was located in the lysosome (Fig. H). These results indicated that AMPK promoted PDGFRβ degradation through the lysosomal pathway. However, how AMPK promotes PDGFRβ degradation is still unclear. Previous studies have shown that PDGFRβ usually binds c-Cbl to form a complex and is degraded through lysosomal pathways [ , , ]. Moreover, AMPK knockdown reduces the formation of complexes between c-Cbl and downstream proteins [ ]. Therefore, does the c-Cbl protein participate in the process by which the AMPK promotes PDGFRβ protein degradation? To determine this, we further explored the effect of sic-Cbl on the level of PDGFRβ in HCC cells. The results showed that sic-Cbl increased the level of PDGFRβ in HepG2 cells (Figure S2B). AMPK-OE significantly decreased the level of PDGFRβ, while c-Cbl knockdown reversed this effect of AMPK (Fig. I). These results revealed a mechanism by which c-Cbl is a key molecule for AMPK-mediated PDGFRβ degradation.
 

## AMPK activation by phenformin downregulates PDGFRβ and inhibits HCC cell proliferation
In recent years, phenformin has attracted much attention because of its superior antitumour activity and clear pharmacokinetic data [ ]. Our laboratory revealed that phenformin has excellent bioactivity in HCC cells. We focused on antitumour mechanism of phenformin and observed that phenformin inhibits HCC proliferation by activating AMPK [ ]. On the basis of the above findings that AMPK promotes the degradation of PDGFRβ in this study, we speculated that phenformin, a well-known AMPK agonist, might have a similar mechanism to decrease PDGFRβ. Therefore, to verify this hypothesis, functional experiments were conducted to analyze the inhibitory effect of phenformin on HepG2 cell proliferation. The MTT results revealed that phenformin gradually inhibited the proliferation of HepG2 cells in a dose-dependent manner, and the IC50 value of phenformin in HepG2 cells was 541.7 µM (Fig. A). Similarly, phenformin decreased the number of clones formed in HepG2 cells in a dose-dependent manner (Figure S3A). In addition, phenformin significantly decreased the number of HepG2 cells in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle, but increased the number of cells in the G1 phase (Figure S3B). These results clearly demonstrated that phenformin dramatically inhibited the proliferation of HCC cells. Mechanistically, phenformin did not affect the mRNA level of PDGFRβ (Fig. B) but downregulated its protein level by activating AMPK (Fig. C). When cells were pretreated with CHX to halt intracellular protein synthesis, the level of PDGFRβ was still downregulated, and its half-life was significantly shortened by phenformin, indicating that phenformin can significantly weaken the stability of the PDGFRβ protein (Fig. D). However, CQ reversed the downregulation of PDGFRβ caused by phenformin, indicating that PDGFRβ may be degraded through lysosomal pathways (Fig. E). In addition, sic-Cbl reduced the ability of phenformin to downregulate PDGFRβ (Fig. F). Hence, these results suggested that AMPK activation by phenformin promoted PDGFRβ degradation via the c-Cbl-mediated lysosomal pathway, thus inhibiting the proliferation of HCC cells.
 

## Phenformin synergizes with lenvatinib to inhibit HCC cell proliferation by blocking the AMPK/PDGFRβ pathway
Phenformin enhances the efficacy of various clinical drugs for treating HCC, such as sorafenib [ ] and pirarubicin [ ], but whether it can promote the ability of lenvatinib to kill HCC cells has not yet been reported. On the basis of the aforementioned analysis, we proposed a novel combination therapy of phenformin and lenvatinib and conducted a series of experiments to explore the effects and mechanisms of this combination strategy. Functionally, we first tested the viability of lenvatinib-insensitive HepG2 cells treated with a combination of phenformin and lenvatinib and found that, compared with monotherapy, combination therapy further inhibited HCC cell viability (Fig. A). Surprisingly, the CI value of approximately 0.4 indicated a strong synergistic effect between phenformin and lenvatinib (Fig. B). Moreover, the synergistic effect of phenformin and lenvatinib was identified in SK-HEP1 cells, which are another type of HCC cell that is insensitive to lenvatinib and highly expresses PDGFRβ (Figure S4A–S4B). We subsequently analyzed the effects of the combination therapy on the colony-forming ability and cell cycle of HepG2 cells. As shown in Fig. C‒6D, the combination therapy group had fewer cloned cells (Fig. C) and more G1 phase cells (Fig. D) than the monotherapy group did, suggesting that phenformin enhances the inhibitory effect of lenvatinib on HCC cell proliferation. Mechanistically, AMPK activation by phenformin decreased the level of PDGFRβ, which was increased by lenvatinib (Fig. E). In addition, ERK signaling [ ] and the cell cycle [ ] are key downstream signaling pathways of AMPK activation. We observed that the combination therapy further inhibited the levels of key proteins (e.g., cyclin D1, CDK4, phosphorylated ERK, and p70s6k) of the two signaling pathways in HCC cells (Fig. F). In summary, we demonstrated that phenformin synergized with lenvatinib to inhibit HCC cell proliferation by blocking the AMPK/PDGFRβ/ERK pathway and arresting the cell cycle of HCC cells.
 

## Evaluation of the efficacy and safety of combination therapy with phenformin and lenvatinib in vivo
Next, we determined the inhibitory effect of combination therapy with phenformin and lenvatinib on the growth of subcutaneous xenograft tumours derived from HepG2 cells in mice. The effects of the drugs on the tumorigenic ability of nude mice were evaluated by comparing the size (Fig.  A), weight (Fig.  B), and volume (Fig.  C) of tumours in each group. Compared with those in the other groups, the tumours in the combination group were the smallest, indicating that the combination therapy further inhibited the tumorigenic ability of HCC cells in nude mice. The activation of AMPK by phenformin also weakened the increase in PDGFRβ expression in lenvatinib-treated xenografts, as determined by WB assays (Fig.  D) and confirmed by the IHC assay (Figure S5A and S5B). Combination therapy with lenvatinib and phenformin further suppressed the levels of key proteins in the ERK oncogenic and cell cycle signaling pathways in xenografts, which was consistent with the findings in cells (Fig.  E). In addition, the combination therapy strategy inevitably raises concerns about whether its toxic side effects on the animal’s body will double due to the involvement of multiple drugs. Therefore, toxicity assessment is also indispensable. Encouragingly, the combination strategy of lenvatinib and phenformin had no significant toxic side effects, as indicated by the changes in mouse body weight (Figure S5C). In addition, H&E staining of the livers and kidneys verified this finding (Fig.  F). These results suggest that phenformin synergizes with lenvatinib to inhibit HCC cell proliferation in vivo without significant toxic side effects, making it a promising clinical approach.
 

## Discussion
As one of the few clinical drug choices for patients with advanced HCC, lenvatinib has improved the disease release rate to some extent. However, the only 24.1% patient response rate largely limits the clinical application of lenvatinib in HCC [ ]. Therefore, it is crucial to explore the mechanism underlying the poor sensitivity of lenvatinib. This study revealed for the first time that PDGFRβ is associated with the poor sensitivity of HCC cells to lenvatinib and PDGFRβ knockdown enhances the inhibitory effect of lenvatinib on HCC cell proliferation. PDGFRβ is an important angiogenic factor, and its abnormal expression can lead to tumour proliferation, migration, and drug resistance [ ]. These results reveal a new mechanism by which high expression of PDGFRβ results in the insensitivity of HCC cells to lenvatinib. Hence, we speculated that the downregulation of PDGFRβ may be an important factor in enhancing the sensitivity of HCC cells to lenvatinib.
Clinical drugs as part of a combination therapy regimen constitute one of the future strategies for cancer treatment [ ]. Therefore, we are committed to finding ways to enhance the antitumour effect of lenvatinib by downregulating PDGFRβ. AMPK is a very important energy regulatory center, and its activation inhibits HCC cell proliferation [ ]. Early studies reported that AMPK regulates an interaction between Cbl and its downstream proteins [ ]. Importantly, ubiquitination of PDGFRβ in response to ligand stimulation is mediated by the Cbl family of ubiquitin ligases, which promote internalization and serve as a sorting signal for vesicular trafficking of receptors [ , ]. However, it is unclear whether AMPK inhibits the formation of a complex between Cbl and PDGFRβ. This study revealed that AMPK does not affect the mRNA levels of PDGFRβ, but mainly regulates the expression of PDGFRβ at the posttranslational level, and discovers a new mechanism in which there is an interaction between AMPK and PDGFRβ and AMPK promotes c-Cbl-mediated PDGFRβ degradation through the lysosomal pathway in HCC cells. Therefore, this study lays a theoretical foundation for further elucidating the relationship between AMPK and PDGFRβ. In the next step of research, efforts can be made to investigate the ubiquitination mode and site of PDGFRβ, as well as the regulation of PDGFRβ activity by AMPK.
Notably, phenformin, an AMPK activator, is a highly promising anticancer drug for clinical development [ ]. As expected, phenformin promoted the degradation of the PDGFRβ protein through the c-Cbl-mediated lysosomal pathway. These findings provide theoretical support for the combination of phenformin and lenvatinib. Furthermore, we observed a strong combination effect of phenformin and lenvatinib in vitro and in vivo, convincingly demonstrating that activating AMPK and downregulating PDGFRβ by phenformin further inhibited lenvatinib-treated tumour cells or xenograft tumour without significant toxicity. This combination provides a new opportunity for the application of lenvatinib in the treatment of lenvatinib-insensitive HCC cells with high expression of PDGFRβ.
However, there are still significant shortcomings in this study that need to be improved in the following areas. For example, clarifying the binding mode or site between AMPK, c-Cbl, and PDGFRβ will further elucidate the tumorigenic mechanism of HCC from the perspective of protein interaction, which will be beneficial for the development of small molecule inhibitors of protein-protein interactions and provide new ideas for the treatment of tumours. In addition, the effectiveness, tolerability and safety profile of the combination therapy of phenformin and lenvatinib should be further evaluated in clinical trials. Phenformin has entered clinical trials for cancer treatment and has great potential for clinical application [ ]. Since phenformin was one of clinically approved drugs to treat diabetes in 1957, it is easier to obtain the approval of ethics committee and recruit patients. Although this study demonstrated the combined efficacy and evaluated the toxicity of phenformin and lenvatinib in cells and mice, we are not sure whether the concentrations we used in cell culture model could be achievable in human plasma and tissue. To better understand the potential anti-cancer effects of the combination therapy of phenformin and lenvatinib in humans, it is crucial to determine the concentrations of phenformin achieved in human tissue compartments and determine whether those concentrations are sufficient to activate AMPK. Hence, clinical studies are necessary to understand the impact of the combination therapy of phenformin and lenvatinib on HCC patients. Finally, the phenomenon of resistance to lenvatinib is frequently discovered in clinical applications, and its resistance mechanism is a dilemma faced. Although this study demonstrated high expression of PDGFRβ results in the insensitivity of HCC cells to lenvatinib, the expression of PDGFRβ in lenvatinib-resistant cells and lenvatinib-resistant patients is still unclear. In the future, an in-depth research can be conducted on elucidating the relationship between PDGFRβ with lenvatinib-resistance mechanisms using high-throughput technology.
In any case, this study provides new theoretical support for the low clinical response rate of lenvatinib and offers a novel personalized combination therapy of phenformin and lenvatinib for HCC patients with high expression of PDGFRβ.

## Conclusions
In summary, this study revealed that high expression of PDGFRβ results in the insensitivity of HCC cells to lenvatinib, and subsequently revealed a mechanism by which phenformin activates AMPK, which binds to PDGFRβ and promotes its degradation through the c-Cbl-mediated lysosomal pathway, thus inhibiting cell proliferation and arresting the cell cycle and ultimately increasing the sensitivity of HCC cells to lenvatinib (Fig.  ). A novel combination therapy with phenformin enhances the inhibitory effect of lenvatinib on HCC cell proliferation in vivo and in vitro. In a word, this study provides a new combination therapy for the treatment of HCC patients who are insensitive to lenvatinib and present high expression of PDGFRβ.
