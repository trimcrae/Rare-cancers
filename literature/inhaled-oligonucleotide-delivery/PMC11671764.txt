These findings suggest that TXNIP is a key regulator of ASD-induced pulmonary inflammation.

## Introduction
Particulate matter (PM) is a microscopic air pollutant that has been implicated in a range of adverse health outcomes, including respiratory diseases, diabetes, cancer, and cardiovascular conditions [ , , , ]. Recently, the atmospheric concentration of PM has markedly increased due to accelerated industrialization, which has emerged as a significant global public health issue. In particular, in China, Korea, and Japan, seasonal exposure to Asian sand dust (ASD)–a specific form of PM originating from the deserts of China and Mongolia–has been associated with heightened daily mortality rates and increased hospital admissions [ ]. ASD carries microbiological and chemical materials from the atmosphere, including silica, β-glucan, and lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which have been shown to induce neutrophilic inflammation [ ]. While numerous studies have documented the exacerbating effects of ASD on respiratory conditions such as asthma, fungal pneumonia, and bacterial pneumonia [ , , ], there remains a paucity of data regarding the molecular mechanisms underlying ASD-induced pulmonary inflammation.
Thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) is a crucial protein that binds to and inhibits the function of thioredoxin (TRX), participating in multiple cellular redox signaling pathways [ ]. Under oxidative stress conditions, elevated TXNIP expression has been shown to induce cell apoptosis and inflammation, processes that play pivotal roles in the pathogenesis of several diseases, including diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer, and acute lung injury [ , , ]. Previous studies have established that TXNIP activates the NOD-like receptor pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome [ , ]. The NLRP3 inflammasome is an intracellular protein complex that activates caspase-1, which subsequently cleaves pro-interleukin (IL)-1β and pro-IL-18 into their active forms. Dysregulation of the TXNIP/NLRP3 signaling pathway has been implicated in the development of pulmonary diseases such as asthma, LPS-induced lung injury, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [ , , ]. However, the role of the TXNIP/NLRP3 pathway in ASD-induced pulmonary inflammation remains poorly understood.
In the present study, we aimed to elucidate the effects of ASD on pulmonary inflammation and to identify the underlying mechanisms, with a particular focus on the TXNIP signaling pathway. To achieve this, we employed TXNIP knock-out (KO) mice and adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated TXNIP overexpression transgenic mice. Our findings demonstrate that TXNIP plays a critical role in mediating ASD-induced pulmonary inflammation.

## Materials and methods
2.1

## Physiochemical characteristics of ASD
The ASD utilized in this study was procured from Powder Technology Inc. (Arden Hills, MN, USA). The morphological features and particle size of ASD were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100F, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Gemini 500, ZEISS, Jena, Germany). Particle size and zeta potential measurements were conducted using a dynamic light scattering instrument (ELSZneo, Otsuka Electronics, Tokyo, Japan). The elemental composition of ASD was analyzed via energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS, X-MaxN 80T, Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, UK), providing comprehensive insights into its chemical structure.

2.2

## DNA cloning, AAV preparation, and purification
To express TXNIP via AAV, the mouse Txnip gene (NM_001009935.2) was synthesized with an N-terminal FLAG tag, driven by a CAG promoter within an AAV vector, through VectorBuilder (IL, USA). AAV production was carried out as described in our previous study.1 Briefly, a 1:1:1 M ratio of the pAAV vector harboring the target gene, pAAV2/8 (#112864; Addgene, MA, USA), and the helper plasmid pAdDeltaF6 (#112867; Addgene) was co-transfected into 293 cells. Following a 72-h incubation period, the cells were harvested and lysed using AAV lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris, pH 8.0). Nucleic acids were digested by treating the lysates with benzonase (#E8263; Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA), and the mixture was subsequently centrifuged. AAV particles were purified by layering the supernatant onto an iodixanol density gradient (15 %, 25 %, 40 %, and 60 %) within a QuickSeal tube (#345320A; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan), followed by centrifugation at 280,000×g for 3 h at 14 °C using the Himac CP80WX centrifuge with a P90AT rotor (Hitachi). Viral particles were collected from the interface between the 60 % and 40 % iodixanol layers, concentrated with an Amicon Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter Unit (#UFC910096; Sigma-Aldrich), and subjected to three washes. Purified AAV-TXNIP or control AAV-GFP viruses were then intratracheally administered to mice at a dose of 4 × 1011 viral particles per animal.

2.3

## Animal experimental protocol
Male C57BL/6 mice, 6 weeks of age, were obtained from Damul Science (Daejeon, Republic of Korea) and housed under standard laboratory conditions with ad libitum access to rodent chow and tap water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chonnam National University (CNU IACUC-YB-2022-13 and CNU IACUC-YB-2023-159).
The pulmonary inflammation model induced by ASD was established based on a previously published protocol [ ]. Mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5 per group): a normal control (NC) group and three ASD-treated groups receiving 10, 20, or 40 mg/kg of ASD (ASD10, ASD20, and ASD40 groups, respectively). On days 1, 3, and 5, mice in the ASD-treated groups were administered ASD via intranasal instillation in a volume of 50 μL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) under light anesthesia with isoflurane. Mice in the NC group received an equivalent volume of PBS intranasally as a control.
For the TXNIP KO model, TXNIP KO mice on a C57BL/6 background were provided by the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology. Age-, sex-, and weight-matched wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 mice were used as controls. These mice were divided into four groups: WT NC, WT ASD, TXNIP KO NC, and TXNIP KO ASD. ASD (20 mg/kg) or PBS was instilled intranasally three times every other day, following the same method as described above.
To investigate the effects of TXNIP overexpression on ASD-induced pulmonary inflammation, mice were assigned to four groups (n = 5 per group): NC, uninfected ASD, AAV-GFP ASD, and AAV-TXNIP ASD. Overexpression of TXNIP was induced by intratracheal injection of an AAV-TXNIP vector, with AAV-GFP serving as the control. One week following the adenoviral vector injection, all groups except the NC group received ASD instillation at a dose of 20 mg/kg, administered three times every other day, as per the aforementioned method.

2.4

## Analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)
On day 7, BALF was collected from anesthetized mice via tracheostomy. Endotracheal tubes were inserted, and the lungs were lavaged twice with 0.7 mL of PBS. BALF samples were centrifuged, and the supernatants were used for the quantification of inflammatory cytokines using a commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). The cell pellets were used to quantify inflammatory cells using a Countess II Cell Counter (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Diego, CA, USA).

2.5

## Histopathology and immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Fixed, paraffin-embedded left lung lobes were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin, as well as periodic acid-Schiff. To evaluate the expression of TXNIP, NLRP3, Caspase-1, and IL-1β in ASD-exposed lung tissues, IHC analysis was performed using a commercial ABC kit (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). The primary antibodies used were as follows: TXNIP (1:100 dilution, Novus), NLRP3 (1:200 dilution, Abcam), Caspase-1 (1:200 dilution, Novus), and IL-1β (1:200 dilution, Abcam). The representative images were obtained using a slide scanner (Motic, Hong Kong), and histological quantitative analysis were conducted using an image analyzer (IMT i-Solution Inc., Vancouver, BC, Canada). For the quantitative analysis of inflammation, we evaluated the inflammatory response over the total area captured at a magnification of 200 × . To assess mucus production, we measured the bronchial area for mucus secretion. For the quantitative analysis of protein expression, we determined the area positive for the target protein relative to the total area. The quantitative values were expressed as a percentage, representing the areas of inflammation, mucus production or target protein expression relative to the total assessed area.

2.6

## Western blot analysis
A total of 30 μg of homogenized lung tissue was separated by 10 % SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5 % skim milk for 2 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies (1:1000 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were then incubated with a secondary antibody (1:10,000 dilution) for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged with a Chemi-Doc system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

2.7

## Cell culture
The NCI–H292 human pulmonary mucoepidermoid carcinoma cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (WELGENE, Republic of Korea) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum and antibiotics and maintained in a humidified chamber at 37 °C with 5 % CO2.

2.8

## Cell viability assay
NCI–H292 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and incubated for 24 h prior to treatment. Cells were then exposed to various concentrations of ASD for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed using the EZ-Cytox kit (DoGenBio, Republic of Korea). After 4 h of incubation with the kit solution, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland).

2.9

## Small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection and double immunofluorescence
NCI–H292 cells were seeded into 12-well plates containing plastic coverslips (20018, SPL Life Sciences, Pocheon, Republic of Korea). TXNIP-specific siRNA (s20880) and scrambled siRNA (4390843) were transfected into NCI–H292 cells using Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX reagent (20 nM, Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA), following the manufacturer's protocol. After achieving suppression of endogenous TXNIP expression, the cells attached to the coverslips were treated with either 50 μg/mL ASD or PBS as a control. After 24 h of incubation, the cells were subjected to double immunofluorescence staining. Primary antibody incubation was carried out overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibody incubation for 2 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies used included anti-TXNIP (1:100, Novus), and anti-NLRP3 (1:100, Abcam), while the secondary antibodies included anti-Rabbit IgG-FITC (1:100, Sigma-Aldrich) and anti-Mouse IgG-TRITC (1:100, Sigma-Aldrich). The cells were subsequently mounted on slides using ProLong Gold Antifade Reagent with DAPI (Invitrogen) and visualized using a confocal microscope (LMS900, ZEISS).

2.10

## Statistical analysis
Data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was assessed using one-way ANOVA, performed with GraphPad Prism software. A p-value <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

## Results
3.1

## Physicochemical properties of Asian sand dust
Understanding the physicochemical characteristics of ASD is crucial, as it directly influences their biological interactions within the body. The ASD particles exhibited a spherical morphology, as illustrated by TEM and SEM images ( A). In PBS, the hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of ASD were measured as 1540.4 ± 367.4 nm and −34.31 mV, respectively ( B and C). Elemental composition analysis of ASD, performed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy, revealed the following chemical constituents: O 63.02 %, Si 31.90 %, Al 3.62 %, Fe 0.90 %, Mg 0.31 %, Ca 0.15 %, and Ti 0.10 % ( D and E). 

3.2

## Effects of ASD on inflammatory cell counts and cytokines in BALF
We first induced pulmonary inflammation through intranasal exposure of ASD and then evaluated the inflammatory responses in BALF. ASD exposure led to a significant elevation in inflammatory cell counts within the BALF compared to the NC group ( A–D). Neutrophils and macrophages constituted the majority of the inflammatory cells. Specifically, neutrophil and macrophage counts in the ASD 40 group were approximately six- and five-fold higher, respectively, than those observed in the ASD 10 group. Additionally, levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α in the BALF of ASD-exposed groups were significantly elevated relative to the NC group, with these cytokine increases exhibiting a dose-dependent pattern ( E–G). 

3.3

## Effects of ASD on histological changes
To evaluate airway inflammation in the lung tissues of ASD-exposed mice, H&E staining was performed. Exposure to ASD resulted in inflammatory cell infiltration around the bronchi, which increased in a dose-dependent manner ( A and B). Subsequently, we assessed the expression of proteins associated with the TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome pathway in lung tissues of ASD-exposed mice. The activation of NLRP3 inflammasome led to increased IL-1β secretion. Compared to the NC group, the ASD 20 and ASD 40 groups demonstrated significant increases in the expression of TXNIP, NLRP3, and IL-1β ( C–E), with expression levels increasing in a dose-dependent fashion in response to ASD exposure. 

3.4

## Effects of ASD on the TXNIP/NLRP3 pathway
The expression of proteins involved in the TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome pathway was additionally analyzed via gel electrophoresis. TXNIP expression was upregulated in the lungs of ASD-exposed mice compared to the NC group ( A and B). The activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in the ASD-induced airway inflammation model was confirmed by increased expression of NLRP3 and cleavage of caspase-1 (p20) and IL-1β (p17) in ASD-treated groups, with statistically significant effects observed in the ASD 40 group ( C–G). 

3.5

## Effects of ASD on inflammatory cell counts and cytokine levels in BALF of TXNIP KO mice
To ascertain the impact of TXNIP deficiency on ASD-induced pulmonary inflammation, we compared the inflammatory responses in the BALF of WT and TXNIP KO mice following ASD exposure. The total number of inflammatory cells in the BALF of TXNIP KO ASD mice was reduced compared to WT ASD mice, with significant reductions in neutrophil and macrophage counts ( A–D). Furthermore, levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in the BALF of TXNIP KO ASD mice were markedly lower than in WT ASD mice ( E–G). 

3.6

## Effects of ASD on histological changes in TXNIP KO mice
Histopathologic examination using H&E staining demonstrated a notable reduction in peribronchial and perivascular inflammatory cell infiltration in the lungs of TXNIP KO ASD mice compared to their WT ASD counterparts ( A and B). To investigate the effect of TXNIP deficiency on NLRP3 inflammasome activation caused by ASD exposure, immunohistochemical analysis was conducted. The results indicated a significant decrease in the expression of NLRP3, caspase-1, and IL-1β in the lungs of TXNIP KO ASD mice relative to WT ASD mice ( C–E). 

3.7

## Effects of ASD on the TXNIP/NLRP3 pathway in TXNIP KO mice
NLRP3 inflammasome activates the effector caspase-1, leading to the maturation of IL-1β. To investigate the impact of TXNIP deficiency on NLRP3 inflammasome complex formation induced by ASD exposure, we assessed the expressions of NLRP3, caspase-1, and IL-1β in the lungs of TXNIP KO mice via immunoblotting. As depicted in A and B, TXNIP KO ASD mice exhibited a reduction in NLRP3 expression in the lungs when compared to WT ASD mice. Additionally, KO ASD mice displayed a concurrent decrease in the expression of caspase-1 (p20) and IL-1β (p17) relative to WT ASD mice ( C–F). 

3.8

## Effects of ASD exposure and TXNIP knockdown in NCI–H292 cells
For in vitro assays, the ASD treatment concentration was determined based on cell viability results ( A). ASD exposure induced a dose-dependent increase in IL-6 levels in NCI–H292 cells, with the highest effect observed at 50 μg/mL of ASD treatment compared to untreated controls ( B). Immunofluorescence analysis showed elevated TXNIP and NLRP3 expression in ASD-treated cells versus untreated cells ( C). Subsequently, NCI–H292 cells were transfected with TXNIP-specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) to elucidate TXNIP's role in ASD-induced inflammation. Control siRNA had no impact on the increased TXNIP and NLRP3 expression following ASD exposure, whereas TXNIP-specific siRNA significantly reduced NLRP3 expression in ASD-treated cells. 

3.9

## Effects of ASD on inflammatory cell counts and cytokine levels in the BALF of TXNIP overexpression mice
To determine the effects of TXNIP overexpression on ASD-induced pulmonary inflammation, AAV-TXNIP or AAV-GFP were intratracheally injected 7 days prior to the initiation of ASD exposure. ASD exposure led to an increase in inflammatory cell numbers and cytokine levels in the BALF, consistent with the aforementioned results. Specifically, the numbers of inflammatory cells, including neutrophils, macrophages, and total cells, were significantly elevated in the BALF of AAV-TXNIP ASD mice compared to AAV-GFP ASD mice ( A–D). Furthermore, the levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were markedly higher in the BALF of AAV-TXNIP ASD mice relative to AAV-GFP ASD mice ( E–G). 

3.10

## Effects of ASD on airway inflammation and mucus production in TXNIP overexpression mice
We also evaluated the impact of TXNIP overexpression on airway inflammation and mucus production in the lungs of ASD-exposed mice through histological examination. Exposure to ASD resulted in a pronounced accumulation of inflammatory cells surrounding the alveolar and bronchial regions ( A and B). In AAV-TXNIP ASD mice, the severity of airway inflammation was significantly exacerbated when compared to AAV-GFP ASD mice. While ASD exposure alone induced only minor changes in mucus production, TXNIP overexpression was associated with a marked elevation in mucus secretion ( A and C). 

3.11

## Effects of ASD on protein expression in the lungs of TXNIP overexpression mice
AAV-mediated TXNIP overexpression and subsequent activation of NLRP3 inflammasome were confirmed through immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical analysis. AAV-TXNIP ASD mice demonstrated a substantial upregulation of TXNIP expression compared to AAV-GFP ASD mice ( A). This heightened TXNIP expression corresponded with enhanced activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Specifically, the levels of NLRP3, caspase-1, and IL-1β were significantly elevated in AAV-TXNIP ASD mice relative to AAV-GFP ASD mice ( B–E). 

3.12

## Effects of ASD on NLRP3 inflammasome expression in TXNIP overexpression mice
The impact of TXNIP overexpression on NLRP3 inflammasome activation was assessed via Western blot analysis. In addition to ASD exposure, AAV-TXNIP administration led to a notable increase in TXNIP expression compared to AAV-GFP injection ( A and B). Correspondingly, the expression levels of NLRP3, caspase-1 (p20), and IL-1β (p17) were significantly higher in AAV-TXNIP ASD mice compared to AAV-GFP ASD mice ( C–G).

## Discussion
The increase in airborne PM concentrations resulting from desertification, driven by rapid urbanization and industrialization, has heightened concerns regarding its adverse health effects [ ]. Inhalation via the respiratory tract is the primary route of PM exposure, and the particle size plays a critical role in determining its impact on human health. Particles smaller than 10 μm in diameter can reach the lungs, while those smaller than 1 μm are capable of entering the bloodstream, eliciting immune responses [ , ]. ASD primarily consists of fine and ultrafine particles, with a significant proportion being smaller than 2.5 μm [ ]. Despite numerous studies investigating the pulmonary toxicity of ASD, the underlying pathogenesis remains inadequately understood. Here, we characterized the physicochemical properties of ASD, examined its pulmonary toxicity, and elucidated the underlying mechanisms. ASD exposure resulted in increased inflammatory cell counts and elevated cytokine levels in BALF, accompanied by the infiltration of inflammatory cells around the alveoli and bronchi. Additionally, ASD-induced airway inflammation was associated with increased TXNIP expression, leading to the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. The role of TXNIP in ASD-induced airway inflammation was further confirmed through the use of TXNIP KO mice and AAV-mediated TXNIP overexpression in transgenic mice.
ASD can transport additional chemical components alongside its original soil constituents during its journey from the arid regions of northwestern China to Korea, Japan, and the Pacific Ocean, where various biological reactions or interactions with chemical substances may occur [ ]. Therefore, the composition of ASD may vary depending on the timepoint of origin and the locations from which it is collected [ , , ]. In this study, the particle size of ASD was determined to be less than 1 μm using SEM and TEM, while X-ray spectroscopy analysis identified the primary components of ASD as oxygen and silica, comprising 63.02 % and 31.9 %, respectively. Respiratory exposure to ASD resulted in increased inflammatory cell counts, particularly neutrophils and macrophages, as well as elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, in the BALF. Additionally, ASD exposure prompted the infiltration of inflammatory cells into the pulmonary tissue. Previous studies have reported that silica dioxide nanoparticles, the most abundant component of ASD, can induce pulmonary inflammation in animal models [ , ]. Notably, Ichinose et al. [ ] and Kobayashi et al. [ ] demonstrated that ASD exposure leads to acute airway inflammation, which is mediated by elevated cytokine levels and oxidative stress. He et al. [ ] reported that exposure to ASD induced neutrophilic airway inflammation, leading to increased levels of the chemokines macrophage inflammatory protein-1α (MIP-1α/CCL3), keratinocyte-derived chemokine (KC/CXCL1), and regulated on activation, normal T-cell expressed and secreted (RANTES/CCL5), as well as the inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, and interferon-γ in BALF. Inflammatory cytokines and chemokines are key mediators in the pathogenesis of inflammation, facilitating the recruitment and migration of inflammatory cells into the affected tissues [ , ]. Collectively, this body of evidence supports the conclusion that ASD exposure induces airway inflammation.
The concentration of PM2.5 in ASD has been shown to correlate with levels of environmentally persistent free radicals and oxidative potential in the atmosphere [ ], and exposure to ASD leads to the release of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [ , ]. Under conditions of oxidative stress, TXNIP dissociates from Trx and binds to NLRP3, which is a key component of the innate immune system [ , , ]. This interaction triggers the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, resulting in the cleavage of pro-IL-1β into its mature form, IL-1β, by caspase-1. The secreted IL-1β subsequently mediates inflammatory responses in target tissues. Li et al. [ ] confirmed that lung injury caused by PM exposure is mediated through the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Furthermore, silica dioxide, the most abundant element of ASD, has been implicated in inducing pulmonary inflammation via the TXNIP/NLRP3 signaling pathway [ , ]. Exposure to aluminum, the second most abundant element in ASD, leads to the production of ROS, which activates the NLRP3 inflammasome and further induces pyroptosis in liver [ ]. The elimination of LPS through the heating of ASD suppressed the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome induced by ASD [ ]. The LPS-induced ROS/TXNIP/NLRP3 pathway has been demonstrated in macrophages, microglial cells and lung tissue [ , , ]. Therefore, it is plausible to hypothesize that the TXNIP/NLRP3 signaling pathway plays a critical role in mediating ASD-induced pulmonary inflammation. In this study, we confirmed a dose-dependent increase in TXNIP expression in the lungs of ASD-exposed groups, alongside concomitant activation of NLRP3 inflammasome proteins, including NLRP3, caspase-1, and IL-1β. These findings suggest that ASD-induced airway inflammation is mediated by the TXNIP/NLRP3 signaling pathway.
TXNIP is known to modulate cellular oxidative stress, glucose and lipid metabolism, and immune responses. In the context of the immune system, TXNIP is implicated in both innate and adaptive immunity through its regulation of NF-κB and NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathways, as well as cytokine production in various cell types [ ]. In this study, we investigated the role of TXNIP in airway inflammation by comparing WT, TXNIP KO, and AAV-mediated TXNIP overexpression transgenic mice following exposure to ASD. In TXNIP KO mice, ASD-induced inflammatory responses, including the recruitment of inflammatory cells and cytokine levels in BALF, were significantly reduced compared to WT mice. Moreover, NLRP3 inflammasome activation was attenuated in ASD-exposed KO mice compared to their WT counterparts. Consistent with these findings, knockdown of TXNIP in ASD-treated NCI–H292 cells confirmed that the ASD-induced inflammatory responses are mediated by the TXNIP/NLRP3 signaling axis in vitro. Previous studies have similarly demonstrated that TXNIP knockdown ameliorates inflammation in various tissues, including the ovaries, blood vessels, and kidneys [ , , ]. In contrast, ASD-induced airway inflammatory responses and mucus hypersecretion were exacerbated in AAV-TXNIP transgenic mice compared to AAV-GFP controls, accompanied by increased NLRP3 inflammasome protein expression in lung tissues. Exposure to asbestos generates ROS via multiple mechanisms, such as surface iron interactions or phagocytosis, leading to the release of TXNIP and subsequent activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in mesothelial cells [ ]. The role of the NLRP3 inflammasome is highlighted by its association with the development of various respiratory diseases, linked to acute or chronic inflammation triggered by multiple pathogenic particles [ ]. These findings suggest that the exacerbated inflammatory responses observed in ASD-exposed TXNIP overexpression mice may be attributed to the enhanced activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes facilitated by the TXNIP upregulation. Mucociliary clearance, a critical defense mechanism of the airway that facilitates the expulsion of inhaled particles and pathogens via coughing, is impaired by mucus hypersecretion, a hallmark of airway inflammation [ ]. Studies have shown that TXNIP upregulation contributes to mucus overproduction in murine models of asthma and exposure to titanium dioxide [ ]. Given that respiratory diseases, such as acute lung injury and asthma, are associated with the TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, numerous studies have explored TXNIP suppression as a potential therapeutic strategy for these conditions [ , ]. Collectively, these findings underscore the pivotal role of TXNIP in modulating ASD-induced pulmonary inflammation via activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome.
In conclusion, ASD exposure significantly enhances inflammatory cell infiltration, cytokine production, and mucus hypersecretion in the lungs through the TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. Our findings using TXNIP KO and AAV-mediated overexpression transgenic mice elucidate the critical role of TXNIP in ASD-induced airway inflammation. These results suggest that targeting TXNIP may represent a promising therapeutic approach for mitigating pulmonary inflammation associated with ASD exposure.
