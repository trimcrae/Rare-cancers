## Background
Lactate has a beneficial effect on adult neurogenesis and has been identified as a mediator of lactylation of proteins. However, the regulatory role and function of histone lactylation in neurogenesis remain poorly understood. This study aimed to elucidate the relationship between lactate and the fate of neural stem cells (NSCs), and explore the role of histone lactylation in neurogenesis.

## Methods
Under hypoxic conditions, NSCs were treated with lactate to investigate its effects on cell fate. Western blot and immunofluorescence staining were performed to detect histone lactylation modifications. RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) was used to characterize the transcriptome after lactate treatment, in conjunction with Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq), to identify potential target genes for histone lactylation.

## Results
Under hypoxic conditions, L-lactate promotes neurogenesis and induces H3K9 histone lactylation (H3K9la). The inhibition of L-lactate uptake or production hinders neuronal development and is accompanied by decreased H3K9la. And the inhibition of H3K9la inhibits the differentiation of NSCs into neurons. Furthermore, H3K9la was enriched in the SnoN (also known as Skil) promoter region, and siRNA targeting SnoN inhibits the generation of Doublecortin (DCX)+ neurons.

## Conclusion
L-lactate promotes neurogenesis through H3K9la/SnoN axis under hypoxic environments and SnoN potentially serves as a novel target for enhancing neurological recovery in cerebral ischemia.

## Background
Cerebral ischemia is the second leading cause of mortality worldwide and is a major contributor to severe disability [ ]. Neuronal loss in the core area due to hypoxic-ischemic injury results in neurological disability [ ] and cognitive impairment [ ]. Therefore, neuronal regeneration and protection have been the main focus to effectively ameliorate functional recovery in cerebral ischemia. Therapeutic approaches to enhance the neurogenic activity of endogenous NSCs may provide promising opportunities for cerebral ischemic recovery. Adult neurogenesis refers to the generation of new neurons from adult NSCs [ ], which are capable of sensing and adapting to extrinsic stimuli and are correlated with cognitive function. NSCs are primarily situated in the subgranular zone (SGZ) in the hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) and the subventricular zone (SVZ) of the lateral ventricles. Multiple signaling factors within the SVZ and DG niches maintain a balance between NSC self-renewal and differentiation [ – ]. In the context of cerebral ischemia, initiating a pluripotent regenerative response [ ] by NSCs of SVZ niches is vital for the plasticity and repair of brain injury. Therefore, identifying the maintenance and differentiation mechanisms of NSCs within the SVZ is of great interest for improving neural regeneration strategies after ischemic attacks.
During hypoxic-ischemic events, an inadequate oxygen supply results in elevated lactate production within cells via glucose consumption. Simultaneously, reactive astrocytes migrate and aggregate at the periphery of the injured lesion [ ], and release lactate through glycolysis, which further contributes to the accumulation of lactate in the surrounding tissues. Lactate primarily exists as two optical isomers (i.e., L-lactate and D-lactate), with L-lactate being the main biologically active form within the body (unless otherwise specified, lactate refers to L-lactate) [ ]. Studies have shown that lactate, which is typically considered a source of energy, serves as a signaling molecule involved in various pathophysiological activities [ ]. Our previous study showed that in a model of middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO), the levels of lactate in the tissue on the injured side were significantly increased, acting on astrocytes to alleviate the inflammatory response [ ]. Similarly, lactate affects niches and positively influences adult neurogenesis, thereby contributing to brain repair [ – ]. In addition to its reparative role in cerebral ischemia, the lactate released by astrocytes under normal physiological conditions is essential for long-term memory formation [ ] and induces the expression of genes associated with synaptic plasticity (e.g., Egr1, BDNF, and c-Fos), thereby regulating neuronal excitability [ ]. However, the mechanism by which lactate enhances adult neurogenesis remains unclear.
Post-translational modification of histone, an important epigenetic regulatory process that is vital for the dynamic and sustained regulation of gene expression. Various cellular metabolites, including succinylation, crotonylation, and malonylation, can acylate histones [ ]. With the advancement of mass spectrometry, Zhao et al. discovered that lactylation of lysine residues, a derivative of lactate, serves as a novel histone modification that activates gene transcription [ ]. Thereafter, other studies reported that under high lactate conditions, histone lactylation is crucial in various cells, including microglia, ocular melanoma cells, glioblastoma stem cells, and bone mesenchymal stem cells [ – ]. Additionally, modifications involving lysine lactylation occur in the brain and are influenced by neuronal activity [ ]. Regulation of histone lactylation can achieve neuroprotection; for example, H3K18la activates neuronal transcriptional programs to facilitate cellular fate changes during development [ ]. Accordingly, exploring the pivotal role of histone lactylation modifications in neurogenesis is crucial for the development of potential therapeutic approaches for repairing cerebral ischemic damage.
In this study, we report that lactate can upregulate histone lactylation levels while promoting neurogenesis. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that lactate accelerated the differentiation of NSCs into neurons and induced a shift in cellular metabolic patterns. Mechanistically, H3K9la was enriched at the promoters of genes involved in neural differentiation and promoted their transcription. Our data indicated that SnoN positively regulates neuronal differentiation. We also showed that SnoN-specific deletion in NSCs reduced the generation of DCX+ neurons. Thus, our study revealed the contribution of the lactate/H3K9la/SnoN axis to neurogenesis and provided new insights into the use of lactate for repairing cerebral ischemic injuries.

## Materials and methods

## Animals
The newborn suckling C57BL/6 (1 day old) and C57BL/6 (weighing 20–25 g) were provided by the Experimental Animal Center (Yisi Company, China). Experimental mice were randomly selected without any sex preference. Approval for all animal experiments in this study was obtained from both the Animal Care and Experimental Animal Ethical Committee at Jilin University. This work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0, and additional supporting documents are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

## Cell culture
To isolate murine NSCs, the SVZ cells were collected from 1-day-old C57BL/6 brains and cultured to grow neurospheres. Briefly, tissue from the SVZ area of the neonatal mouse brain was dissected and digested using 0.01% trypsin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 10 min. The reaction was terminated using Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium - Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12, Corning, USA), enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldrich). SVZ tissue of brain was filtered through a 40 μm filter (Greiner Bio-One, Germany) and centrifuged to obtain cell pellet. Subsequently, the cells were resuspended in the culture medium and inoculated in a petri dish. SVZ-derived primary neurospheres were cultured in DMEM/F-12 with 1% N2 (Invitrogen, USA), 1% B27 (Invitrogen), 20 ng/ml bFGF (Peprotech, USA) and 20 ng/ml EGF (Peprotech) for 7 days. The medium was renewed every 2 days during the culture period. Primary neurospheres were dispersed to obtain the corresponding scattered NSCs. Primary neurospheres and scattered NSCs were used within a single passage.
Mouse NSC NE-4 C (ATCC, USA) was cultured in Eagle’s Minimum Essential medium (EMEM, ATCC) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco, USA). Normoxic culture conditions were 21% oxygen. Cells within 10 generations were used in this study. For verifying the NE-4 C cells differentiation potential, cells were cultured in EMEM media with 1% FBS and 10− 3 mM RA Retinoic acid (RA, Selleck, China), and culture in 21% oxygen for 48 h. To investigate the effect of lactate on NSCs differentiation, cells were cultured in EMEM media with 1% FBS and containing different concentrations of L-lactate sodium (Sigma-Aldrich) or D-lactate sodium (Sigma-Aldrich), and in a humidified chamber equilibrated with 2% oxygen for 48 h. For inhibition studies, AR-C155858 (MCT1 inhibitor, MedChemExpress, China), GSK2837808A (LDHA inhibitor, MedChemExpress), and C646 (histone acetyltransferase p300 inhibitor, MedChemExpress) were added to EMEM media and cells were cultured in indicated culture conditions.

## Cell transfection
Cell transfection was performed as described previously [ ]. Since NE-4 C cells were cloned from 9-day-old p53−/− mouse embryos, we constructed NE-4 C cells overexpressing p53 (namely NE-4 C/P53). The p53 overexpression plasmid was generated by PPL Genebio Technology (Public Protein/Plasmid Library, China) and delivered to cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer’s instructions. For the siRNA-mediated gene knockdown, Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher, USA) was utilized to deliver three different siRNAs targeting SnoN. The sequences of siRNA are listed in Supplementary Table .

## Western blot analysis
The total protein was isolated from NE-4 C, NE-4 C/P53 cells, and primary neurospheres. Proteins were quantified with a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were then blocked using 5% w/v skim milk powder (Epizyme, China) for 1 h to prevent non-specific binding, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 ℃. After washing the membranes with TBST (YEASEN, China) three times, they were further incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to HRP, and visualization was performed using ECL reagents (Meilun Bio, China). The information about antibodies used is as follows: Nestin (1:1000; 19483-1-AP, Proteintech, China), NeuroD1 (1:1000; 12081-1-AP, Proteintech), ATP5B (1:2000; sc-74549, SANTA CRUZ, USA), P300 (1:1000; ab275378, Abcam, UK), Pan Kla (1:2000; PTM-1401RM, PTM Bio, China), H3K9la (1:1000; PTM-1419RM, PTM Bio), H3K14la (1:1000; PTM-1414, PTM Bio), H4K5la (1:1000; PTM-1407, PTM Bio), H3 (1:5000; PTM-3015, PTM Bio), MCT1(1:1000; 20139-1-AP, Proteintech), LDHA (1:1000; 19987-1-AP, Proteintech), SnoN (1:1000; 19218-1-AP, Proteintech), SOX9(1:1000; ab185966, Abcam), β-actin (1:2000; 81115-1-RR, Proteintech), HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:5000; SA00001-2, Proteintech), HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse (1:5000; SA00001-1, Proteintech). H3 and β-actin were used as an endogenous control. ImageJ software (Fiji, National Institutes of Health, USA) was utilized to quantify the band intensities of the target proteins. The results were obtained from three separate experiments.

## Cell immunofluorescence staining
The samples were subjected to immunofluorescence to visualize proteins. NE-4 C, NE-4 C/P53, and primary neurospheres were cultured on coverslips and fixed with paraformaldehyde (4%, Sigma-Aldrich). Subsequently, coverslips were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Dingguo, China) and incubated with 10% normal goat serum (Solaibao, China) to block nonspecific binding. The coverslips were sequentially incubated with the primary antibody, washed thrice with PBS, and then exposed to the fluorescence-labeled secondary antibody. Subsequently, nuclear labeling was performed using Hoechst33342 (YEASEN) or DAPI (YEASEN) for 15 min. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan) and analyzed using the ImageJ software. Antibodies against Nestin (1:100; 19483-1-AP, Proteintech), chicken anti-Nestin (1:100; NB100-1604, NovusBio), Sox2 (1:200; ab92494, Abcam), DCX (1:500, ab18723, Abcam), Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, 1:300; ab4674, Abcam), ATP5B (1:200; sc-74549, SANTA CRUZ), Pan Kla (1:200; PTM-1401RM, PTM Bio), H3K9la (1:200; PTM-1419RM, PTM Bio), Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-chicken immunoglobulin G (IgG) (1:200; 103545155, Jackson Healthcare, USA), Alexa Fluor 488 Goat Anti-mouse IgG (1:500; ab150077, Abcam), and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated AffiniPure Donkey anti-rabbit IgG (1:500; 711585152, Jackson Healthcare) were used.

## Tissue immunofluorescence staining
Mice underwent transcardial perfusion using neutral formalin buffer, followed by brain dissection and fixation in formalin buffer for 48 h. Tissues were then cryoprotected through 30% sucrose dehydration protocol, and embedded for cryosectioning, or processed for paraffin embedding following routine dehydration, clearing, and embedding procedures. The frozen sections were washed with PBS, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min, and blocked with 10% normal goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies, including chicken anti-Nestin (1:100) and rabbit anti-Sox2 (1:200), overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies, such as Alexa Fluor 594-labeled AffiniPure donkey anti-rabbit IgG and Alexa Fluor 488-labeled goat anti-chicken IgG, for 1 h. The nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,342 for 15 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser-scanning microscope (Olympus, Japan). Paraffin sections were dewaxed and rehydrated using graded alcohols, and antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer at high temperatures. Then, we used the UltraSensitive™ SP IHC Kit (Maixin Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Endogenous peroxidase activity was inactivated, and nonspecific binding was blocked. The sections were incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, and then washed with PBS. Biotinylated secondary antibodies were added and incubated at room temperature for 30 min, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated streptavidin for 20 min. Visualization was performed using DAB, and the nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, mounted, and analyzed using the ImageScope imaging system (Leica, Germany). The information about antibodies used is as follows: Pan Kla (1:200; PTM-1401RM, PTM Bio), H3K9la (1:200; PTM-1419RM, PTM Bio).

## Nuclear protein extraction
The nuclear protein of NE-4 C, NE-4 C/P53, and primary neurospheres was extracted using the Nuclear Protein Extraction Kit (Sangon Biotech, China) for the nuclear fractionation assay, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, a Hypotonic buffer containing phosphatase inhibitors, phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), and dithiothreitol (DTT) was used for cell lysis. Following cell pellet collection, the prepared lysis buffer was added and the mixture was incubated on ice for 20 min. Subsequently, the solution was centrifuged to collect the supernatant, which contained the nuclear protein extract.

## qRT-PCR
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), as previously described [ ]. For mRNA analysis, RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Takara, Japan) [ ]. For miRNA, reverse transcription was performed using the All-in-One miRNA First-Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (GeneCopoeia, USA) [ ]. qRT-PCR was performed using a qPCR mix (Innovagene, China) on the 7300 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). The relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. β-actin was used as the internal control for mRNA, and U6 was used for miRNA.

## MCAO
A model of middle cerebral artery occlusion/reperfusion (MCAO/R) injury was established using the Koizumi method [ ]. Mice were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital (10 mL/kg, Sigma-Aldrich) and positioned supine. Mice were skinned and maintained at a warm temperature throughout the procedure. An incision along the midline of the neck revealed the right common carotid artery (CCA), along with the external (ECA) and internal carotid arteries (ICA). A silicon–covered filament (Cinontech, China) was inserted into the right CCA, advanced through the ICA, and positioned to occlude the initial segment of the cerebral artery, resulting in local cerebral tissue ischemia.The silicon–covered filament was withdrawn after 60 min, and the wound was sutured. Following MCAO, the behavior and health of the mice were monitored daily. 48 h after surgery, the mice were euthanized, and brain tissues were collected for further analysis. For humanitarian reasons, mice that experienced severe body weight loss (> 20%) were excluded from the experiments and were euthanized promptly. Euthanasia was performed by trained personnel using carbon dioxide inhalation.

## RNA-sequencing analysis (RNA-seq)
The samples were sent to Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) for library construction, Illumina sequencing, and data preprocessing. Total RNA was isolated using TRlzol reagent (Life Technologies, USA), and subsequent RNA concentration, purity, and integrity were determined. Each sample used 1 µg of RNA to generate a library. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the Hieff NGS Ultima Dual-mode mRNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was employed to generate sequencing libraries, which were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. ClusterProfiler software was used to analyze the enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted on all significantly enriched GO terms associated with DEGs. The dataset can be accessed using the following DOI: 10.6084/m9.figshare.27195165.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP–seq)
Cells were cross-linked using 1% formaldehyde (Beyotime) for 10 min at room temperature, followed by quenching with 0.125 M glycine solution (Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min. Subsequently, the cells were washed thrice with pre-cooled PBS and collected using a solution containing 0.1% NP-40 (Beyotime) in PBS. Cell samples were submitted to Active Motif China for CHIP-seq library construction. Qubit3.0 was used for preliminary quantification, followed by insert sequence length detection using Agilent 2100. Sequencing was conducted using the Illumina platform, and the sequencing strategy was PE150. Data visualization was performed using Integrated Genomics Viewer (IGV), and visual representations were generated from converted wig files using MACS2 software. Input samples were used as background controls during peak calling.

## ChIP assay
Cells were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde (Beyotime), and ChIP was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Beyotime). Following the reversal of cross-links, ChIP DNA was purified using a DNA Purification Kit (Beyotime) and subsequently eluted for qPCR analysis. Input control samples (10% of the total chromatin) were collected prior to immunoprecipitation and processed in parallel for qPCR or sequencing, whice served as a normalization reference for target enrichment. Primer sequences are listed in the Supplementary Table .

## Lactate determination assay
NE-4 C or NE-4 C/P53 cells were treated with different concentrations of lactate under hypoxic conditions for 48 h. Subsequently, the cells were collected and fragmented by sonication on ice for lactate analysis. A portion of the cells was utilized to measure protein concentration, while another portion was combined with the lactate kit reagent (Njjcbio, China) and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. The absorbance (A) of each sample was measured at 530 nm using a microplate reader (TECAN, Switzerland). The lactate content was then determined using the following formula: [(A measurement - A blank) / (A standard - A blank)] × 3mmol/L ÷ protein concentration.

## Statistical analysis
All data were obtained from three independent experiments (n = 3) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Comparisons between the two conditions were conducted using Student’s t-tests, whereas multi-group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA. Data that did not meet the normal distribution assumption were analyzed using non-parametric tests. Data are presented as means ± SEM, with a p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

## Results

## L-lactate promotes NSCs differentiation to neurons under hypoxic conditions
In this study, we focused on primary neurospheres derived from the SVZ of the mouse brain (Fig. A, B), as well as the neural stem cell lines NE-4 C and NE-4 C/P53. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed the expression of Nestin and Sox2, both known NSCs markers, in NE-4 C, NE-4 C/P53, and primary neurospheres (Fig.  A and Fig. C). Moreover, RA, a neural differentiation agent, was used to assess the differentiation potential of NSCs. RA effectively induced the differentiation of NE-4 C cells into DCX-positive neuron-like cells (Fig. D). These results suggest that NE-4 C cells and primary neurospheres had NSCs differentiation characteristics.

 

To evaluate the role of lactate on NSCs differentiation, the NSCs were treated with lactate for 48 h under hypoxic conditions (Fig. A). We observed a decrease in Nestin protein levels as a result of L-lactate treatment, particularly after 48 h, as shown in Fig.  B and B. NeuroD1, a lineage-specific transcription factor, is known for its ability to promote the conversion of human fibroblasts [ ], astrocytes [ , ], or microglia [ ] into neurons. In contrast, SOX9 is recognized for its role in promoting the formation of astrocytes [ ]. Thus, we used Western blotting to analyze the differences in the expression of NeuroD1 and SOX9 between cells treated with and without lactate. L-lactate upregulated the expression of NeuroD1 (Fig.  C, D, and C) but decreased the expression of SOX9 (Fig. D). However, the effect of D-lactate on the expression of NeuroD1 was consistent with that observed under hypoxia in the absence of L-lactate (Fig.  C). Studies have demonstrated the dynamic expression of miRNAs during neurogenesis, including miR-124-3p (miR-124) [ , ] and miR-9-5p (miR-9) [ , ], which are associated with neural differentiation. qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that L-lactate promoted miR-124-3p and miR-9-5p expression in NE-4 C cells (Fig. E). Overall, these findings suggest that L-lactate promotes the differentiation of NSCs into neurons under hypoxic conditions.
Next, we investigated whether L-lactate affects the neuronal fate of NE-4 C cells. Cells were treated with L-lactate for 48 h under hypoxic conditions and then cultured in the neuronal culture medium (NM) for 7 days. Remarkably, the cells aggregated into clusters and formed neurospheres within 2 days (Fig. A). We found that the increased expression of NeuroD1 was sustained in NE-4 C cells treated with L-lactate (Fig. F). Furthermore, neurospheres in the L-lactate group developed more colonies than those in the other groups (Fig. A). On the 7th day, these neurospheres exhibited the growth of slender axon-like substances and became intertwined. Simultaneously, the cells were immunolabelled with the immature neuronal marker DCX. We determined the proportion of DCX+ cells and found that the 20 mM L-lactate group had a higher percentage of DCX+ neurons than that of the other groups (Fig.  E and B). This was also verified by observing primary neurospheres and NE-4 C/P53 cells with fluorescent staining (Fig.  F, C and E), which showed that DCX+ immature neurons generated more obvious in L-lactate-treated NSCs than that in control cells. These results suggest that L-lactate could potentially direct NSCs towards neuronal differentiation.

## Transcriptome analysis reveals neural differentiation and metabolism as the main affected pathways in NSCs
To investigate the mechanism underlying the involvement of L-lactate in neural differentiation, we performed transcriptome analysis on NE-4 C and NE-4 C/P53 cells treated with L-lactate under hypoxic conditions for 48 h. Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that the lactate-treated cell samples clustered away from the control group, indicating different gene transcript expression patterns (Fig.  A and A). Subsequently, a differential expression analysis was conducted to identify the specific transcriptomic changes induced by lactate, which revealed 637 DEGs in lactate-treated NE-4 C cells, comprising 516 up-regulated and 121 down-regulated (p < 0.05, |log₂FC| >1) genes. In NE-4 C/P53 cells, 1495 DEGs were found in the lactate-treated group, with 828 up-regulated and 667 down-regulated relative to controls (p < 0.05, |log₂FC| >1). Notably, L-lactate induced a large gene expression response that was strongly increased in the control group (Fig.  B and B).

 

GO analysis of NE-4 C and NE-4 C/P53 cells revealed that multiple pathways were differentially enriched in the control and lactate-treated groups. Specifically, the differentially enriched GO terms were associated with neuronal differentiation, ATP synthesis, and electron transport chain processes (Fig.  C and C). In the neural differentiation group, significant upregulation was observed in several genes (e.g., Eed, Dcn, Crbn, Oxr1, Fastkd2 and Skil) related to neural differentiation and development of neural projections (Fig.  D). To validate the findings from the RNA-seq analysis, the representative genes associated with neuronal fate were analyzed using qRT-PCR (Fig.  E), and the expression of these genes was found to be consistent with the RNA-seq results.
The metabolic pattern during NSCs differentiation into neurons involves a transition from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) [ ]. To further investigate the metabolic changes in NSCs upon L-lactate treatment, we performed KEGG enrichment analysis of the DEGs. This analysis revealed a significant enrichment in pathways related to neurogenesis and metabolism. Notably, the OXPHOS pathway, which is central to mitochondrial function and ATP production, was significantly enriched (Fig.  F and D), suggesting that L-lactate may promote a metabolic shift from glycolysis to OXPHOS. To verify the role of L-lactate on metabolism during NSCs differentiation, we analyzed the expression of glucose metabolism-related enzymes using RNA-seq. The results revealed that the expression of glycolysis-related enzymes decreased, while that of OXPHOS-related enzymes was up-regulated in the lactate group (Fig. A). OXPHOS mainly occurs in the mitochondria. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of the mitochondria showed that L-lactate altered the number and cristae of the mitochondria. Specifically, the number of mitochondria in the lactate-treated group was relatively high, with more obvious cristae (Fig.  G).
Thus, these findings show that L-lactate has an overall activating effect on transcription, triggering metabolic processes, response to stimuli, and differentiation of NSCs.

## Elevated histone lactylation levels are associated with hypoxia and high L-lactate condition
Given that L-lactate can act as a precursor metabolite that stimulates protein lactylation, we hypothesized that protein lactylation might be altered in the context of cerebral ischemia. In our study, we investigated the protein lactylation levels in mouse MCAO. In the MCAO model, the hemisphere contralateral to the lesion was indistinguishable from the sham-treated brain, making it a reliable internal morphological control. Tissue immunohistochemical staining showed that the global lactylation level of the SVZ niches on the injured side was significantly higher than that the contralateral tissue (Fig.  A). We used hypoxia and high L-lactate to mimic the SVZ niche environment during MCAO. As the concentration of exogenous L-lactate increased, the uptake of lactate by NSCs also gradually increased (Fig. A). P300 is the currently identified enzyme responsible for lactylation modifications. Western blot analysis consistently showed that L-lactate upregulated the expression of P300 (Fig.  B) and the global lactylation level of proteins in NSCs also increased, particularly at the molecular weight of 15 kDa (Fig.  C). Interestingly, the lactylated proteins were predominantly localized in the nucleus (Fig. B), prompting us to focus on histone lactylation in the subsequent experiments.

 

Recent studies have shown that L-lactate directly modifies histones and exerts various biological functions. Based on this, we examined the lactylation of different histones and found that L-lactate had a substantial effect on the lactylation modification of H3K9, H3K14, and H4K5 (Fig.  D). Given that epigenetic modifications at H3K9 are enriched in adult niches and are involved in cell fate transitions, our study specifically investigated the impact of H3K9la. As shown in Fig.  E and C, immunofluorescence staining and Western blot analysis revealed that L-lactate up-regulated the expression of H3K9la in NSCs under hypoxic conditions. Consistently, we found that H3K9la expression was markedly elevated in the SVZ region on the ipsilateral (injured) side of MCAO mice compared to that in the contralateral side (Fig.  F), further supporting the relevance of this modification in vivo.These findings demonstrate that histone lactylation modification occurs in the SVZ niche on the injured side and is potentially involved in the differentiation of NSCs in the SVZ niche.

## Lactate-derived lactation modification is involved in NSCs differentiation
Given that monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1) facilitates the transmembrane transport of short-chain monocarboxylic acids, such as L-lactate and pyruvate, through an H+-coupled mechanism [ ], we speculated that L-lactate uptake may be dependent on MCT1. Thus, we used Western blot to analyze the expression of MCT1 in NSCs treated with lactate and found that MCT1 expression was upregulated in this condition (Fig. A and B).
To further clarify the relationship between L-lactate, histone lactylation, and NSCs differentiation, L-lactate uptake by cells through MCT1 was inhibited. The NSCs were treated with AR-C155858 to inhibit MCT1 protein expression (Fig. C and D). Notably, the inhibitor decreased the uptake of exogenous L-lactate by the NSCs (Fig. E), as well as global intracellular lactylation and H3K9la levels (Fig.  A), suggesting that the increased levels of histone Pan-Kla and H3K9la in NSCs were a result of increased L-lactate concentrations. Specifically, a reduction in histone lactylation corresponded to a marked decrease in the expression of NeuroD1 (Fig.  B and Fig. F). These results suggest a potential regulatory connection between histone lactylation and the expression of genes critical to neural processes, such as NeuroD1. Subsequently, we investigated the effect of inhibiting H3K9la with AR-C155858 on the neuronal fate of NSCs. Primary neurospheres were co-treated with AR-C155858 for 48 h under hypoxic and high lactate conditions, and subsequently cultured with NM. We found that AR-C155858 decreased the proportion of DCX+ cells compared to the control group (Fig.  C). Moreover, GSK2837808A was used to inhibit the expression of lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), an enzyme that facilitates the interconversion of pyruvate and L-lactate. We observed a decrease in intracellular L-lactate production in NSCs after treatment with GSK2837808 (Fig. G). Concurrently, Western blot analysis indicated that decreased L-lactate production significantly reduced the global lactylation levels, particularly of H3K9la (Fig.  E). GSK2837808 also downregulated the expression of NeuroD1 (Fig.  F). These data suggest that L-lactate-derived lactylation modification is associated with the NSC differentiation.

 

## Genome-wide analysis of the transcriptional targets of H3K9la in NSCs
C646 is a P300 inhibitor that inhibits protein lactylation modification. The results showed that C646 significantly reduced protein lactylation in NSCs (Fig. B), particularly H3K9la (Fig.  A). Furthermore, despite the addition of exogenous lactate, the expression of NeuroD1 was reduced alongside a decrease in H3K9la levels (Fig.  B). These findings suggest that H3K9la plays a crucial role in neural differentiation.

 

To further investigate the epigenetic regulation associated with H3K9la in NSCs, ChIP-seq with anti-H3K9la antibodies was conducted to identify genes potentially regulated by H3K9la in NSCs. Genes associated with differential H3K9la binding peaks, identified by ChIP-seq analysis comparing L-lactate-treated and untreated NSCs, were subjected to GO enrichment analysis. The results revealed significant enrichment in biological processes such as regulation of cell morphogenesis and positive regulation of neuronal differentiation (Fig.  C). These findings suggest that genomic regions showing altered H3K9la occupancy may be functionally linked to neurogenic differentiation pathways. In particular, the peaks identified potential genomic loci associated with neurogenetic genes, including Nedd4l, Acsl6, and Map1b and demonstrated increase H3K9la levels in their promoters (Fig. ). Overlapping ChIP-seq and RNA-seq data, we noticed one gene, SnoN (Skil), with significant enrichment of H3K9la peaks in its promoter region (Fig.  D and E). Furthermore, ChIP-qPCR validation confirmed the enrichment of H3K9la at the SnoN promoter (Fig.  F). Consistent with the RNA-seq results, qRT-PCR revealed a significant increase in SnoN mRNA expression following L-lactate treatment (Fig.  G). In addition, SnoN protein expression also increased after L-lactate treatment (Fig.  H). These findings indicated that the upregulation of SnoN was due to the accumulation of H3K9la at the promoter, leading to increased transcription.

## H3K9la promotes differentiation of NSCs through SnoN
Since H3K9la increased the transcription and expression of SnoN, we subsequently determined whether H3K9la promoted neurogenesis through SnoN. To examine the effect of H3K9la/SnoN pathway on neurogenesis, we formulated three distinct siRNAs targeting SnoN. As shown in Fig.  A and B, the knockdown of SnoN expression by siRNAs, especially siRNA #3, had a significant knockdown effect. Therefore, we selected siRNAs #2 and #3 for subsequent experiments. NE-4 C and primary neurosphere cells were co-treated with L-lactate and siSnoN for 48 h under hypoxic conditions. Western blot analysis showed that knocking down SnoN expression with siRNA reduced NeuroD1 expression compared to that in the control group (Fig.  C). Subsequently, we examined the effects of SnoN knockdown on the neuronal fate of NSCs. Cells were co-treated with L-lactate and siSnoN under hypoxic conditions and then cultured in NM. In NE-4 C cells, the immunofluorescence results showed that the depletion of SnoN significantly inhibited the generation of DCX+ cells induced by L-lactate (Fig.  D). Interestingly, we observed consistent results in primary neurospheres (Fig.  E). Taken together, these results indicate that histone lactylation facilitates neurogenesis progression through activating SnoN.

 

## Discussion
In this study, we evaluated the involvement of histone lactylation in L-lactate-induced adult neurogenesis in the SVZ. We found that L-lactate, but not D-lactate, increased H3K9 lactylation modification in NSCs within the SVZ under hypoxic conditions, leading to their differentiation into DCX+ neurons. Importantly, this effect relies on MCT1-mediated L-lactate transport. Furthermore, H3K9la accumulates in the promoter regions of genes associated with neuronal differentiation, particularly SnoN, thereby facilitating neurogenesis. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of L-lactate in the self-repair of cerebral ischemia in rats; however, the specific mechanism by which L-lactate exerts its neuroprotective effects remains unclear. To our knowledge, this is the first report showing that the effects of L-lactate on NSCs are mediated through lactylation modification of histones, and our data contribute to a deeper mechanistic understanding of lactate-induced neurogenesis.
The importance of the SVZ vasculature in maintaining NSCs and lineage progression within the SVZ has been well-documented in previous studies [ , ]. Despite the high vascularity of the SVZ, the cellular oxygen content in this niche is typically around 2.5–3.0% under non-pathological conditions [ ]. In cases of cerebral ischemia, oxygen levels in the SVZ niche decreased, leading to a hypoxic environment where NSCs might reside. We speculated that NSCs may be exposed to a hypoxic environment; therefore, we used 2% oxygen to study the lineage changes of NSCs. Studies have found that self-renewal and differentiation of NSCs are regulated by O2 [ , ]. Huang et al. observed that hypoxic preconditioning (HPC) significantly increased the dendrites, Edu+/DCX+, and Edu+/NeuN+ cells, and ameliorated axonal and other neuronal injuries [ ]. In line with these findings, we found that exposure to 2% O2 promoted the expression of NeuroD1 in NSCs and the generation of DCX+ cells compared to the 21% O2 group (Fig. B).
Under hypoxic conditions, astrocytes generate high levels of L-lactate [ ], leading to changes in lactate levels in the brain. L-lactate is considered a key regulator of mitochondrial OXPHOS and inhibits glycolytic capacity [ ]. Interestingly, the process of NSCs differentiation into neurons is associated with metabolic reprogramming [ ], which is of great significance in meeting the energy and biosynthesis needs during neural differentiation. Our RNA-seq results demonstrated that L-lactate increased the expression of OXPHOS-related enzymes and promoted neuronal fate. In addition, the cells utilize glycolysis and OXPHOS for ATP production [ ]. OXPHOS also yields higher amounts of ATP. In our study, the levels of ATP and expression of ATP5B, an ATPase that plays a critical role in catalyzing ATP production [ ], were higher in NSCs treated with L-lactate than in the control group (Fig. B– D). OXPHOS involves coupling the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) to efficiently synthesize ATP. Mitochondrial cristae are the major sites of OXPHOS in eukaryotes [ ], and are critical for cellular energy production. Our results showed that L-lactate regulates mitochondrial crista junction formation. However, further investigation is required to fully understand the relationship between metabolism and neural differentiation.
Lactate generally mediates signaling through the lactate receptor GPR81 or the MCT. GPR81, also known as hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 1 (HCAR1), is a G protein-coupled receptor that functions as a lactate sensor in peripheral organs, including adipose tissue. Recent studies have shown that this receptor is also expressed in the adult brain [ ]. Evidence indicates that GPR81 activation mediates neuronal activity [ ]. However, its neuroprotective effects against cerebral ischemic injury in mice remain controversial, suggesting that GPR81 activation may not be involved in this neuroprotective effects [ , ]. Although GPR81 expression is low in neuronal cells, its potential contribution of GPR81 cannot be excluded. MCT1 is primarily responsible for lactate uptake because of its high lactate-binding capacity. Our previous studies have shown that extracellular lactate may be transported to astrocytes through MCT1 to attenuate inflammation in MCAO [ ]. In this study, our results indicate that L-lactate can upregulate the expression of MCT1. AR-C155858, which inhibits MCT1 expression, leads to a reduced uptake of lactate by NSCs, which in turn, causes a reduction in the generation of DCX+ neurons.
Histone modifications alter chromatin structure to affect gene accessibility and activity, and play a crucial role in maintaining cellular function and responding to environmental stimuli. A previous study indicated that the deposition of H3K9/18 acetylation in the promoter region of neural differentiation genes regulates sensory neuron differentiation [ ]. Epigenetic modifications at H3K9, especially methylation and acetylation, are enriched in adult niches and affect the early stages of neurogenesis and neuronal plasticity [ ]. During NSCs differentiation, H3K9 acetylation (H3K9ac) often serves as a signal to activate specific neural differentiation genes. Additionally, H3K9ac ameliorates neurite outgrowth deficits [ ]. H3K9me is typically associated with transcriptional repression and maintains the silenced state of certain key developmental genes, facilitating NSC self-renewal. A previous study showed that pharmacological inhibition of H3K9 methyltransferases reduced H3K9me3 and concomitantly decreased neuronal differentiation [ ]. Histone lactylation modification is currently considered a novel form of acylation modification that occurs in hypoxic or hypermetabolic states. Pan et al. found that H3K18la and H4K12la levels were elevated in the hippocampus of mice with Alzheimer’s disease [ , ]. Our data revealed that the strongest Pan-Kla signal was observed at approximately 15 kDa, corresponding to the molecular weight of histones, prompting us to focus on histone lactylation patterns. A recent study by Hagihara et al. identified 63 lysine-lactylation proteins induced by neural excitation and reported elevated levels of stress-induced histone H1 lactylation in neurons [ ]. Consistently, Mass spectrometry (MS) analysis consistently revealed H1 Kla under hypoxic and high-lactate conditions. In addition, the accumulation of extracellular lactate further induces the lactylation of H3K9, H3K14, and H4K5 in vitro, suggesting a global chromatin response to metabolic changes under hypoxia conditions. Furthermore, we observed significant upregulation of H3K9la in ischemic brain tissue in the MCAO mouse model. Given the critical regulatory role of H3K9 modification in transcriptional regulation within the nervous system, we focused on the site-specific function of H3K9la in controlling neurogenic gene expression. These findings raise the possibility that L-lactate may also promote neurogenesis through additional lactylation marks such as H1la. The relationship and potential interplay between H1la and H3K9la in the regulation of neural stem cell differentiation warrant further investigation.
P300 is an indicator of diverse histone acylation pathways such as acetylation and lactylation. In this study, we proposed a model in which p300 functions as a histone lactylation writer. Importantly, our data demonstrated that lactate isomers exert stereospecific effects on histone lactylation. Although D-lactate and L-lactate are structurally similar and generated comparable levels of the P300 protein, only L-lactate effectively promotes H3K9la in NSCs. This suggests that P300 selectively utilizes L-lactate as a functional substrate to catalyze lysine lactylation, consistent with findings from other studies [ , ]. In addition, recent research has shown that D-lactate itself is not directly used as an acyl donor for histone lactylation. Instead, lysine D-lactylation is generated through a non-enzymatic mechanism [ ]. It originates from a spontaneous acyl transfer reaction involving the intermediate S-D-lactoylglutathione (LGSH) in the glyoxalase pathway. This pathway involves two key enzymes: glyoxalase I (GLO1) and glyoxalase II (GLO2). GLO1 catalyzes the conjugation of methylglyoxal (MGO), a byproduct of glycolysis, with glutathione to form LGSH, which is subsequently hydrolyzed by GLO2 to produce D-lactate and regenerate glutathione. As a reactive intermediate, LGSH can non-enzymatically modify the ε-amino group of lysine residues, leading to the formation of lysine D-lactylation. Notably, lysine D-lactylation predominantly occurs on cytosolic proteins, particularly glycolytic enzymes, rather than nuclear histones. This mechanistic distinction may explain the lack of significant H3K9la elevation in NSCs treated with D-lactate. Some studies suggest that acetylation and lactylation can interconvert under various environmental conditions [ , ]. We found that the level of H3K9ac was significantly higher in the D-lactate treatment group than in the L-lactate treatment group, whereas H3K9la was markedly elevated following L-lactate treatment (Fig. A, B). These findings are consistent with a current report indicating that under conditions of high lactate, the acetyltransferase KAT2A exhibits higher affinity for lactyl-CoA, thereby preferentially catalyzing histone lactylation [ ]. Addtionally, we observed that L-lactate significantly upregulated the expression of NeuroD1. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that the generation of DCX+ neurons was reduced by inhibition of H3K9la. In contrast, D-lactate did not show a similar increase in neurogenesis, further supporting the hypothesis that H3K9la is critical for neuronal differentiation in our system. Although H3K9ac and H3K9la may coexist in the nucleus, and both modifications play important roles in neurogenesis, the differences in their relative proportions of H3K9 modification may affect the neural types and efficiency of neuronal differentiation of NSCs. Further research is needed to explore how the balance between these modifications influences neurogenesis. Consequently, we regarded lactate-induced histone lactylation modification as a bridge connecting cell metabolism and differentiation, thus establishing a connection between lactate and neuronal fate. Furthermore, histone lactylation modification enhances gene transcription. Epigenetically, our ChIP-Seq and ChIP-qPCR data revealed that H3K9la activates SnoN transcription, indicating that the differentiation of NSCs into neurons occurs via the H3K9la/SnoN signaling axis.
SnoN is a gene related to the oncogene c-ski, and its function extends beyond cycling cells to postmitotic neurons [ ]. SnoN-regulated genes contain binding sites for the transcription factors N-myc and pax6, which are crucial for granule neuron precursor proliferation and differentiation [ ]. SnoN modulates neuronal cell functions by acting as a potent promoter of axonal growth [ ] or by regulating neuronal migration and dendritic branching in an isoform-specific manner [ ]. However, the role of SnoN in the NSCs remains unclear. In this study, we demonstrated that L-lactate increased SnoN expression during NSCs differentiation. We speculated that a potential association exists between SnoN and lactate-induced neuronal differentiation. To test this hypothesis, we targeted SnoN using siRNA in NSCs and observed a decrease in the generation of DCX+ cells. Notably, siRNA-mediated SnoN knockdown did not alter the levels of H3K9la (Fig. C), suggesting that SnoN is a downstream effector of H3K9la rather than a regulator. This supports our model in which lactate-induced H3K9la promotes NSC differentiation by transcriptionally activating SnoN. This study expands our knowledge of downstream target genes or signaling pathways influenced by histone lactylation and provides new insights into the epigenetic mechanisms involved in neurogenesis.
However, our research has certain limitations. The transition of NSCs into neurons involves a metabolic shift from glucose metabolism to OXPHOS. Our results support the transition of metabolic patterns; however the precise role of the energy-consuming metabolism in regulation of stem cell activity remains uncertain. Western blot analysis using Pan-Kla antibodies revealed distinct bands in several high-molecular-weight regions, indicating that lactylation modification extends beyond histones to include non-histone proteins in NSCs. Zhou et al. discovered that alanyl-tRNA synthetase (AARS1) mediates p53 lactylation [ ]. To identify these proteins, we conducted MS analysis of lactate-treated NSCs. The results revealed that several non-histone proteins underwent lysine lactylation modifications, including pyruvate kinase M (PKM), alpha-enolase (ENO1), and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA). These findings suggest that lactylation modifications may extend beyond epigenetic regulation and may also play a role in cellular metabolism and signaling pathway regulation by modulating protein function, thereby influencing the differentiation of NSCs. Future studies on these modifications are expected to uncover novel regulatory mechanisms involved in the fate transition of neural cells. Finally, although previous studies have linked lactate to neurogenesis and protein lactylation, no report to date has clearly demonstrated the role of histone lactylation in regulating neurogenesis. Moreover, its potential involvement in other cellular processes such as cell survival remains to be further explored. It is undeniable that our study revealed a direct epigenetic mechanism, whereby L-lactate promotes neuronal differentiation through H3K9la-mediated SnoN activation. This provides a new perspective on how L-lactate influence NSC fate decisions under physiological stress, such as hypoxia. This study identified SnoN as a novel target of H3K9 lactylation and lays the groundwork for future research aimed at promoting neurogenesis and developing innovative therapeutic strategies for cerebral ischemia.

## Conclusion
We demonstrated that accumulation of either exogenous or endogenous lactate enhanced neurogenesis, emphasizing the beneficial function of lactate in repairing neural damage after cerebral ischemia. Therefore, we propose a model in which lactate-dependent histone lactylation is enriched in the promoter regions of genes related to neural differentiation, such as SnoN, thereby activating transcriptional pathways involved in tissue repair and supporting neuroregeneration after cerebral ischemia. This study identifies the lactate–H3K9la–SnoN axis as a key epigenetic mechanism that promotes neurogenesis and provides a foundation for future research aimed at promoting neurogenesis and developing innovative therapeutic strategies for cerebral ischemia.
