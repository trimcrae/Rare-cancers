## Abstract

## Objective
Doxorubicin (DOX) is commonly used for chemotherapy; however, its clinical value is extremely dampened because of the fatal cardiotoxicity. Leucine zipper protein 1 (LUZP1) plays critical roles in cardiovascular development, and this study is designed for determining its function and mechanism in DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.

## Methods
Cardiac-specific Luzp1 knockout (cKO) and transgenic (cTG) mice received a single or repeated DOX injections to establish acute and chronic cardiotoxicity. Biomarkers of inflammation, oxidative damage and cell apoptosis were evaluated. Transcriptome and co-immunoprecipitation analysis were used to screen the underlying molecular pathways. Meanwhile, primary cardiomyocytes were applied to confirm the beneficial effects of LUZP1 in depth.

## Results
LUZP1 was upregulated in DOX-injured hearts and cardiomyocytes. Cardiac-specific LUZP1 deficiency aggravated, while cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression attenuated DOX-associated inflammation, oxidative damage, cell apoptosis and acute cardiac injury. Mechanistic studies revealed that LUZP1 ameliorated DOX-induced cardiotoxicity through activating 5′-AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway, and AMPK deficiency abolished the cardioprotection of LUZP1. Further findings suggested that LUZP1 interacted with protein phosphatase 1 to activate AMPK pathway. Moreover, we determined that cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression could also attenuate DOX-associated chronic cardiac injury in mice.

## Conclusion
LUZP1 attenuates DOX-induced inflammation, oxidative damage, cell apoptosis and ventricular impairment through regulating AMPK pathway, and gene therapy targeting LUZP1 may provide novel therapeutic approached to treat DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.

Keywords: AMPK, Doxorubicin, Inflammation, LUZP1, Oxidative stress

## Introduction
Doxorubicin (DOX) is frequently used for cancer treatment; however, its clinical value is extremely dampened due to the fatal cardiotoxicity [ ]. A number of factors contribute to DOX-associated cardiotoxicity, including inflammatory response and oxidative damage [ , ]. After the stimulation with DOX, nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), the nodal transcription factor of inflammation, is phosphorylated and accumulated in the nucleus to provoke the expression of many pro-inflammatory genes in the myocardium [ ]. Accordingly, Hu et al. previously found that NF-κB was activated in DOX-injured hearts, accompanied with the elevations of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [ ]. Overproduction of free radicals is the other key feature and major cause of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity [ ]. DOX can be anchored in mitochondria to compromise the normal role of electron-transport chain, thereby increasing the generations of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [ ]. In addition, the reduced form of DOX, semiquinone, undertakes auto-oxidization in the presence of molecular oxygen, and then amplifies the production of superoxide anions [ ]. Uncontrolled inflammation and oxidative stress trigger massive death of cardiomyocytes, eventually leading to cardiac dysfunction. Moreover, DOX also interacted with topoisomerase-IIβ (Top2β) to form the a ternary cleavage molecule, which subsequently induced breaks of DNA double strands and facilitated death of cardiomyocytes [ ]. These findings identify inflammatory response and oxidative damage as critical targets to attenuate DOX-associated cardiac injury.
5′-AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) exerts inhibitory effects on inflammation and oxidative stress, and possesses various cardioprotective effects [ , , ]. Zhang and colleagues found that AMPK activation suppressed fibroblasts-to-myofibroblasts transformation and subsequent collagen synthesis, eventually prevented pressure overload-induced cardiac fibrosis [ ]. In addition, activating AMPK significantly increased the protein level of uncoupling protein 2, thereby blocking DOX-associated oxidative stress and cardiac dysfunction [ ]. Moreover, we also found that AMPK activation suppressed inflammation and oxidative stress in diabetic hearts [ ]. Therefore, finding novel, especially endogenous activators of AMPK pathway, is vital to treat DOX-induced cardiotoxicity.
Leucine zipper protein 1 (LUZP1), an actin-stabilizing protein, plays critical roles in brain development [ ]. Gonçalves et al. found that LUZP1 interacted with the modulators of ciliogenesis and cilia-length, and subsequently restricted primary cilia formation [ ]. Loss of LUZP1 decreased filamentous actin expression, promoted ciliogenesis and modulated the incurable Townes-Brocks Syndrome [ ]. In addition, Yano et al. recently revealed that LUZP1 localized at the tight junctions and promoted epithelial cell apical constriction through spatiotemporally inhibiting myosin phosphatase [ ]. Intriguingly, Hsu et al. found that Luzp1-deficient mice displayed severe defects of cardiovascular system, and died at the perinatal stage, indicating a pivotal role of LUZP1 in cardiovascular diseases [ ]. This study performed gain- and loss-of-function experiments to clarify the function and mechanism of LUZP1 in DOX-associated cardiotoxicity.

## Methods
2.1.

## Chemicals

Dihydroethidium (DHE, #KGAF019, Ex/Em = 518/605 nm) was purchased from Nanjing Keygen Biotech (Jiangsu, China). Calyculin A (CCA, #HY-18983) was purchased from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, #C0037) was purchased from Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Mouse IL-1β Quantikine ELISA Kit (#MLB00C), Mouse IL-6 Quantikine ELISA Kit (#M6000B) and Mouse TNF-α Quantikine ELISA Kit (#MTA00B) were purchased from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, Canada). Myeloperoxidase (MPO) ELISA Kit (#ab105136), Lipid Peroxidation (MDA) Assay Kit (#ab118970), Lipid Peroxidation (4-HNE) Assay Kit (#ab238538), Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) Activity Assay Kit (#ab65354) and Catalase (CAT) Activity Assay Kit (#ab83464) were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). TransAM® NF-κB p65 (#40096) was purchased from Active Motif (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Anti-LUZP1 (#17483-1-AP) was purchased from Proteintech (Rosemont, IL, USA). Anti-α-actinin (#69758), anti-glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH, #2118), anti-phospho AMPK (p-AMPK, #2535) and anti-total AMPK (t-AMPK, #2603) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Anti-NF-E2-related factor 2 (NRF2, #ab62352) and anti-protein phosphatase 1 catalytic subunit α/β (PPP1CA/B, #ab52619) were purchased from Abcam.

2.2.

## Animals
The experiments were supervised by the Animal Care and Use Committee of our hospital (approval No. 20200802), and the procedures were complied with the Guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the US National Institutes of Health. Luzp1 floxed (Flox) mice were constructed by inserting two loxP inserts flanking exons 3 and 4 of Luzp1 gene, and the offspring were genotyped using two pairs of flox-specific primers as following: 5′arm F1 5′-GTTGCTGTTTCCAGCACTATTCTGT-3′, R1 5′-GACAAAGCATCTGTGAGGCAAGT-3′; 3′arm F1 5′-TCTGAGGCGGAAAGAACCAG-3′, R1 5′-CATGGCAGGACTATGCTGAATTG-3′. To generate cardiac-specific Luzp1 knockout (cKO) mice, Luzp1 floxed mice mated with the α-Mhc-MerCreMer transgenic mice (C57BL/6J background; stock no. 005650, the Jackson Laboratory) as we previously described [ , ]. To induce the deletion of Luzp1 in cardiomyocytes, LUZP1 cKO mice were intraperitoneally injected with 25 mg/kg tamoxifen daily for 5 successive days. To establish cardiac-restrict Luzp1 transgenic (cTG) mice, full-length mouse Luzp1 cDNA (#MC223433; OriGene Technologies, Inc., Rockville, MD, USA) was inserted under α-Mhc promoter, which was then injected into fertilized embryos as previously described [ ]. To establish acute cardiotoxicity, male mice with 8-10-week-old were injected with DOX (15 mg/kg, i.p.) once, and then sacrificed by cervical dislocation 8 days post-DOX injury [ ]. To clarify the role of AMPK in LUZP1-mediated protections against DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, LUZP1 cTG mice were mated with Ampkα2 knockout (KO) mice to generate cardiac-specific Luzp1 transgenic mice with Ampkα2 deficiency, and global Ampkα2 KO mice has been used previously [ ]. To specifically silence PPP1CA/B in the myocardium, LUZP1 cKO mice were injected from tail vein with AAV9 (1 × 1011 VG particles/mouse) carrying pan PP1 shRNA (shPP1, #sc-43533; Santa Cruz, Dallas, Texas, USA) under a cTnT promoter 4 weeks pre-DOX injury [ , , ]. To investigate whether LUZP1 overexpression also conferred cardioprotective effects on DOX-induced chronic injury, LUZP1 cTG mice or the non-transgenic (NTG) controls were weekly injected with 5 mg/kg DOX (i.p.) for 3 weeks [ ]. All treatment schedules of mice were provided in .

2.3.

## Cardiac function tests
To perform echocardiography, mice were inhaled with 1.5% isoflurane, and then long and/or short-axis images of the heart were viewed closed to papillary muscles at the two-dimension (2D) mode [ , ]. Next, the morphological and functional parameters were recorded and calculated at the 2D-guided M mode. Meanwhile, cardiac function was also evaluated with an invasive hemodynamic monitoring system [ , ]. Briefly, a Millar catheter transducer (1.4-French, SPR-839) was put into the left ventricular cavity along the carotid artery, and hemodynamic data were captured and analyzed by a PVAN software.

2.4.

## Immunofluorescence staining
For immunofluorescence staining of the heart samples, paraffin-embedded cardiac slices underwent citric acid antigen retrieval, and then exposed to the blockade of the non-specific binding epitope. Next, cardiac slices were probed at 4 °C overnight with anti-LUZP1 (1:200 dilution) and anti-α-actinin (1:200 dilution), and the secondary antibody at 37 °C for 1 h. Finally, the nuclei was identified, and OLYMPUS DX51 fluorescence microscope was used to record the photographs.

2.5.

## Detections of superoxide anion and apoptosis
Frozen heart slices were prepared and incubated (37 °C, 30 min) with 5 μmol/L DHE to examine superoxide anion, and the fluorescent photographs were recorded by a special OLYMPUS DX51 microscope [ , ]. Apoptosis was evaluated based on TUNEL staining according to the manufacturers′ instructions, and was calculated as the ratio of TUNEL-positive nuclei.

2.6.

## Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and picrosirius red (PSR) staining
Heart samples were immersed in 10% formalin, dehydrated and paraffin-embedded, which were then cut into 5 μm cardiac slices. The size of cardiomyocytes was evaluated by HE staining [ ]. To evaluate collagen deposition, heart samples were exposed to PSR staining [ , ].

2.7.

## Western blot, co-immunoprecipitation and pull-down assay
After extracted by RIPA buffer, the protein concentration was measured by the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit. Next, 10% SDS-PAGE was used to separate total proteins (30 μg), which were transferred onto the PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the proteins were reacted (4 °C, overnight) with the indicating antibodies (1:1000 dilution), and then reacted with (37 °C, 1 h) the secondary antibody (1:10000 dilution). Eventually, proteins were probed with the ECL reagents, scanned and processed using Image Lab 6.0 [ , , ]. For co-immunoprecipitation, heart lysates were prepared and incubated with (4 °C, overnight) anti-LUZP1 (1:50 dilution) or PPP1CA/B antibodies (1:50 dilution) or isotypic IgG, and then precipitated with Protein A/G Agarose beads. Subsequently, immunoprecipitated proteins were washed for five times, and analyzed with SDS-PAGE after thermal denaturation. For the glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assay, GST-tagged PPP1CA/B or GST were incubated with Glutathione Sepharose 4B, followed by the incubation with recombinant Flag-LUZP1 protein at 4 °C for 2 h. After immunoprecipitation and elution, protein complexes were washed and analyzed by Western blot analysis.

2.8.

## Quantitative real-time PCR
Total RNA was prepared and reversely transcribed to cDNA as we previously described [ , ]. Subsequently, PCR amplification and analysis were performed using a SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche). Following primers were used: mouse Luzp1, F 5′-AGCCGAGACAATCAGGTGGA-3′, R 5′-GGGGGTCTTCTGAACATTTCAA-3′; mouse Bax, F 5′-TGAAGACAGGGGCCTTTTTG-3′, R 5′-AATTCGCCGGAGACACTCG-3′; mouse Bcl-2, F 5′-GTCGCTACCGTCGTGACTTC-3′, R 5′-CAGACATGCACCTACCCAGC-3′; mouse Col-1, F 5′-AGGCTTCAGTGGTTTGGATG-3′, R 5′-CACCAACAGCACCATCGTTA-3′; mouse Col-3, F 5′-CCCAACCCAGAGATCCCATT-3′, R 5′-GAAGCACAGGAGCAGGTGTAGA-3′; mouse Gapdh, F 5′-ACTCCACTCACGGCAAATTC-3′, R 5′-TCTCCATGGTGGTGAAGACA-3′; rat Il-1β, F 5′-GGGATGATGACGACCTGCTAG-3′, R 5′-ACCACTTGTTGGCTTATGTTCTG-3′; rat Il-6, F 5′-GTTGCCTTCTTGGGACTGATG-3′, R 5′-ATACTGGTCTGTTGTGGGTGGT-3′; rat Gapdh, F 5′-GACATGCCGCCTGGAGAAAC-3′, R 5′-AGCCCAGGATGCCCTTTAGT-3′.

2.9.

## Cell isolation and treatments
Cardiomyocytes (CMs), endothelial cells (ECs) and cardiac fibroblasts (CFs) from adult mouse hearts were isolated to measure the expression of LUZP1 in different cell populations of DOX-injured hearts as previously described [ ]. To clarify the role of LUZP1 in cardiomyocytes, neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs) were infected with adenovirus carrying shRNA against LUZP1 (shLuzp1) or rat full-length LUZP1 (AdLuzp1) at the multiplicity of infection of 100 or 30, respectively. After 6 h, the medium was changed and allowed the cells to grow for an additional 24 h, and then 1 μmol/L DOX was added and allowed stimulation for an additional 12 h. To silence Ampk, shAmpkα2 was added before LUZP1 overexpression as we previously described [ , , ]. For PPP1C1A/B inhibition, NRCMs were incubated with 100 nmol/L CCA for 12 h in combination with DOX treatment [ ].

2.10.

## Intracellular ROS and cell viability detections
The homogenates of NRCMs were mixed (37 °C, 4 h) with DCFH-DA to determine intracellular ROS level, and the fluorescence was measured by a spectrofluorometer [ , ]. To evaluate cell viability, cells were mixed with 10 μL CCK-8, and allowed for reaction (37 °C, 2 h), which were then detected at 450 nm [ ].

2.11.

## Transcriptome analysis
The Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform was used for RNA-sequencing, and the DESeq2 R package was used for differential expression analysis. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were selected with the following standards, adjusted p value < 0.05 and |fold change| ≥ 2. KEGG analysis of the DEGs was performed by the clusterProfiler R package.

2.12.

## Biochemical analysis
Serum levels of creatine kinase isoenzymes (CK-MB), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and cardiac isoform of troponin T (cTnT) were detected using an automatic ADVIA® 2400 biochemical analyzer (Siemens; Tarrytown, NY, USA). Inflammatory cytokines in the myocardium were detected using kits (R&D Systems) following the manufacturers′ instructions. To evaluate the infiltration of neutrophils, MPO activity was evaluated using fresh heart lysates. Briefly, fresh heart homogenates were prepared with MPO Assay Buffer, and the supernatants were collected and reacted with the Reaction Buffer at 25 °C for 2 h, as well as the reaction with TNB Reagent/Standard. Eventually, the optical density (OD) value was recorded at 412 nm and calculated to obtain the relative MPO activity. To assess NF-κB activity, nuclear extracts of the heart samples were prepared, added to the Oligonucleotide coated plate, and the incubated with the primary antibody and the anti-IgG HRP conjugate according to the manufacturers′ instructions. Finally, the OD value was recorded at 450/655 nm. The levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE), CAT activity, total SOD activity and caspase-3 activity were detected following our previous studies [ , , ].

2.13.

## Measurements of collagen level
Collagen level was measured through detecting the levels of hydroxyproline that is the major component of collagen. Briefly, fresh heart homogenates were prepared and incubated (120 °C, 3h) with 12 mol/L hydrochloric acid, and then dried under vacuum at 60 °C. Next, the samples were reacted with Chloramine T/Oxidation Buffer Mixture and diluted 4-(dimethylamino) benzaldehyde reagent, respectively. Eventually, the OD data were recorded at 560 nm, and the hydroxyproline content was normalized to tissue weight.

2.14.

## Statistical analysis
Results were shown as the mean ± SD and compared with SPSS 22.0. Data normality and variance homogeneity were examined by the Shapiro-Wilk normality test and Levene test, respectively. Significance between groups was compared by Student′s t-test, and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey post hoc test was conducted to compare differences of multiple groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

## Results
3.1.

## LUZP1 expression was elevated in DOX-injured hearts and cardiomyocytes
To determine the requirement of LUZP1 in DOX-associated cardiotoxicity, we measured its level in the myocardium with or without DOX treatment. According to the Human Protein Atlas data, we found that LUZP1 was mainly expressed in human hearts, and that cardiomyocytes were the main source of LUZP1 ( A–B). As depicted in C, LUZP1 expression was significantly elevated in DOX-injured mouse hearts. Consistently, immunofluorescence results also revealed that LUZP1 was primarily expressed by the cardiomyocytes, and that the expression was upregulated by DOX stimulation ( D). Meanwhile, we isolated the different cell types in mouse hearts from CON or DOX-treated mice. Furthermore, DOX injection evidently increased the expression of LUZP1 in cardiomyocytes, but not in endothelial cells or cardiac fibroblasts ( E–F). In addition, LUZP1 protein was also elevated in NRCMs after DOX stimulation in vitro ( G). These results revealed that LUZP1 expression in the heart and cardiomyocytes was elevated by DOX stimulation.
 
3.2.

## Cardiac-specific LUZP1 deficiency aggravated DOX-induced acute cardiac injury
We then established mice with cardiac-restrict deletion of LUZP1 to clarify the function of LUZP1 ( A). Interestingly, cardiac-specific LUZP1 deficiency significantly aggravated DOX-induced myocardial injury, as evidenced by the increased CK-MB, LDH and cTnT levels ( B). DOX injection caused severe loss of cardiac mass, which was further exacerbated by LUZP1 deletion ( C). Meanwhile, the increased vacuolar disorganization and cardiomyocyte atrophy in DOX-treated hearts were further aggravated by cardiac-specific LUZP1 deficiency ( D). Accordingly, DOX-elicited cardiac impairment was also deteriorated in Luzp1-ablated mice, as shown by the decreased ejection fraction (EF) and ±dP/dt in left ventricles ( E–F). However, heart rate and blood pressure were unaffected by LUZP1 deficiency ( G–H). These loss-of-function results revealed that deleting LUZP1 in the heart exacerbated DOX-associated acute myocardial injury.
 
3.3.

## Cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression attenuated DOX-induced acute cardiac injury
Meanwhile, cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpressed mice were also generated to further confirm the role of LUZP1 ( A). As expected, cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression significantly inhibited the increased CK-MB, LDH and cTnT levels in DOX-injected rodents ( B). DOX-induced cardiac mass loss was also alleviated by LUZP1 overexpression ( C). Meanwhile, the increased vacuolar disorganization and cardiomyocyte atrophy in DOX-treated hearts were dramatically prevented by cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression ( D). Consistently, DOX-induced cardiac impairment was attenuated in Luzp1-overexpressed mice, as depicted by the increased EF and ±dP/dt ( E–F). In addition, LUZP1 overexpression also made no alteration on heart rate and blood pressure ( G–H). These gain-of-function results revealed that overexpressing LUZP1 in the heart prevented DOX-associated acute myocardial injury.
 
3.4.

## Cardiac-specific LUZP1 deficiency facilitated DOX-induced inflammation, oxidative stress and apoptosis in the heart
Inflammatory response and oxidative damage contributes to DOX-associated cardiotoxicity, and we previously also identified that inhibiting inflammatory response and ROS overproduction provided cardioprotection to DOX-treated mice [ , ]. Therefore, we investigated whether LUZP1 modulated inflammation and ROS accumulation in DOX-injured hearts. Interestingly, pro-inflammatory cytokines in the myocardium were over-produced by DOX insult, which were further amplified in those with LUZP1 deletion ( A). Meanwhile, the increased MPO activity in DOX-injured hearts was also enhanced by LUZP1 deficiency, indicating enhanced neutrophils infiltration ( B). As the nodal transcription factor of inflammation, NF-κB contributes to the induction of various inflammatory mediators [ , , ]. As expected, we found that DOX-activated NF-κB was further aggravated in Luzp1-deficient mice ( C). Meanwhile, DOX injection dramatically elevated the production of superoxide anion in the heart, which was exacerbated in LUZP1 cKO mice ( D). To clarify the source of ROS in Luzp1-deficient hearts upon DOX injection, the inhibitors of NADPH oxidase (DPI and VAS2870), mitochondria (mitoTEMPO) and xanthine oxidase (allopurinol) were used. As shown in , both DPI and VAS2870 effectively inhibited superoxide anion generation in LUZP1 cKO hearts. On the other hand, neither mitoTEMPO nor allopurinol showed inhibition of ROS production in Luzp1-deficient hearts. These findings indicated that LUZP1 deficiency-mediated ROS overproduction was largely dependent on NADPH oxidase. Accordingly, lipid peroxidation in the myocardium was also increased by LUZP1 ablation in the presence of DOX insult ( E–F). NRF2 is essential for the transcription of various anti-oxidant enzymes that plays critical roles in preventing DOX-induced oxidative stress [ , , ]. Compared with the control mice, DOX-induced downregulation of NRF2 protein was more distinct in LUZP1 cKO hearts ( G). The levels of total SOD and CAT activities were also inhibited by LUZP1 deficiency in DOX-injured hearts ( H–I). Overwhelmed inflammation and ROS production lead to massive cardiomyocyte apoptosis, eventually resulting in DOX-associated cardiac impairment. As shown in J, TUNEL + cells was elevated in the myocardium by DOX injection, which was higher in those with LUZP1 deficiency. As expected, Bax mRNA was elevated, while Bcl-2 mRNA was further reduced in LUZP1 cKO hearts with DOX insult ( K). Meanwhile, cardiac-specific LUZP1 deficiency further elevated caspase-3 activity under DOX stimulation ( L). These loss-of-function results revealed that deleting LUZP1 in the heart facilitated DOX-associated inflammation, oxidative stress and apoptosis.
 
3.5.

## Cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression inhibited DOX-induced inflammation, oxidative stress and apoptosis in the heart
As expected, DOX-induced elevations of inflammatory mediators in the myocardium were reduced in LUZP1 cTG mice ( A). Meanwhile, the increased activities of MPO and NF-κB under DOX injection were also inhibited in LUZP1 cTG hearts ( B–C). Additionally, DOX-associated elevations of ROS and oxidative damage were also suppressed in LUZP1 cTG mice ( D–F). However, NRF2 protein expression, and SOD together with CAT activities were elevated in LUZP1-overexpressed hearts with DOX insult ( G–I). Consistent with the mitigatory inflammatory response and oxidative damage, DOX-related apoptosis was blocked in LUZP1 cTG mice, as indicated by the reduced TUNEL + cells, Bax mRNA level, caspase-3 activity and increased Bcl-2 mRNA level ( J-L). These gain-of-function results reveal that overexpressing LUZP1 in the heart reduced DOX-associated inflammation, oxidative stress and apoptosis.
 
3.6.

## LUZP1 modulated DOX-induced inflammation, oxidative stress and cell survival in cardiomyocytes in vitro
To validate the cell-autonomous role of LUZP1 in cardiomyocytes, we silenced and overexpressed LUZP1 in NRCMs in vitro. As depicted in , LUZP1 protein was significantly reduced in NRCMs with shLuzp1 infection. LUZP1 silence further enhanced DOX-related death of cardiomyocytes, as depicted by the reduced cell viability and elevated LDH release ( ). In accordance with the in vivo results, we demonstrated that DOX-elicited increases of Il-1β, Il-6 and Tnf-α were further enhanced in Luzp1-silenced NRCMs ( ). Meanwhile, DOX-associated oxidative damage was also exacerbated by LUZP1 knockdown ( ). Accordingly, the apoptotic rate was higher in DOX-stimulated NRCMs with LUZP1 silence, compared with those without LUZP1 knockdown ( ). As the in vivo study, we demonstrated that LUZP1 silence-induced increases of ROS in DOX-treated NRCMs were significantly inhibited by both DPI and VAS2870 ( ). In contrast with the above results, we found that LUZP1 overexpression significantly increased viability and decreased LDH releases in NRCMs with DOX treatment ( ). DOX-induced inflammation and oxidative damage were also attenuated by LUZP1 overexpression ( ). Accordingly, the apoptotic rate was lower in DOX-stimulated NRCMs with LUZP1 overexpression, compared with those without LUZP1 overexpression ( ). These loss- and gain-of-function results revealed that LUZP1 modulated inflammation, oxidative stress and cell survival in DOX-treated NRCMs.

3.7.

## LUZP1 ameliorated DOX-induced cardiotoxicity through activating AMPK pathway in vivo and in vitro
To investigate how LUZP1 ameliorates DOX-induced cardiotoxicity, transcriptome analysis was performed in DOX-treated LUZP1 cKO or Flox hearts. Principal component analysis and Volcano map of the DEGs were provided in . The results indicated that the expressions of pro-inflammatory and pro-oxidant genes were increased, whereas anti-oxidant genes was downregulated in LUZP1 cKO hearts after DOX insult ( A). KEGG analysis showed that AMPK pathway was the most significantly altered pathways by LUZP1 deficiency ( B). Consistent with the transcriptome results, DOX-elicited inhibition of AMPK pathway was alleviated in LUZP1 cTG hearts, but further exacerbated in LUZP1 cKO hearts ( C–D). Moreover, we mated LUZP1 cTG mice with Ampkα2 KO mice to generate cardiac-specific Luzp1 transgenic mice with Ampkα2 deficiency to clarify the necessity of AMPK pathway. As depicted in E–F, AMPK deficiency blocked LUZP1 overexpression-mediated inhibition on DOX-associated inflammatory response and oxidative damage. Accordingly, LUZP1 overexpression failed to suppress cell apoptosis in DOX-treated AMPK KO hearts ( G). The improved cardiac injury and dysfunction in LUZP1 cTG mice was also blunted by AMPK deficiency ( H–J). To confirm the necessity of AMPK in vitro, Luzp1-overexpressed NRCMs were also infected with shAmpkα2 to knock down AMPK as we previously described. As shown in , DOX-induced inflammation and oxidative damage were attenuated by LUZP1 overexpression, but not in those with AMPK silence. LUZP1 overexpression-mediated inhibition on DOX-induced cell apoptosis and cell loss was also prevented by AMPK knockdown ( ). These results revealed that AMPK was required for the cardioprotective effects of LUZP1 against DOX-associated myocardial injury.
 
3.8.

## LUZP1 activated AMPK pathway through inhibiting PP1
Various studies have revealed that LUZP1 could bind to PPP1CA/B and subsequently inhibited its phosphatase activity, therefore, we explored whether this contributed to AMPK activation by LUZP1 [ , ]. As shown in A, GST pull-down assay identified a direct interaction between LUZP1 and PPP1CA/B. Meanwhile, co-immunoprecipitation assays also validated an interaction between LUZP1 and PPP1CA/B in the myocardium, which was further enhanced by DOX injection ( B). To validate the involvement of PPP1CA/B, we used AAV9 carrying pan PP1 shRNA to specifically knock down cardiac PP1 ( C). As expected, PP1 silence significantly relieved the inhibition of AMPK pathway in LUZP1 cKO hearts after DOX treatment ( D). Consistently, the pro-inflammatory, pro-oxidant and pro-apoptotic effects in DOX-treated LUZP1 cKO hearts were significantly prevented in those with PP1 silence ( E–G). And LUZP1 deficiency-induced exacerbation of DOX-associated cardiac injury and dysfunction was blocked by PP1 silence ( H–I). In addition, Luzp1-silenced NRCMs were also treated with CCA to inhibit PPP1C1A/B to further determine the necessity of PP1. Consistently, we determined that the enhanced inflammation and oxidative damage in Luzp1-silenced NRCMs after DOX insult were significantly reduced by CCA incubation ( ). As expected, DOX-induced apoptosis and cell loss in Luzp1-silenced NRCMs were inhibited in those with CCA treatment ( ). These results revealed that LUZP1 activated AMPK pathway through inhibiting PP1.
 
3.9.

## Cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression attenuated DOX-induced chronic cardiac injury
In addition to acute cardiac effects, DOX therapy also caused chronic cardiotoxicity [ ]. To enhance the clinic impact and translational value of our study, we also investigated whether cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression could attenuate chronic DOX-elicited cardiotoxicity. Interestingly, EF and ±dP/dt were decreased by repeated DOX injections, which were prevented in LUZP1 cTG mice ( A–B). In addition, echocardiographic analysis also found that DOX injection caused cardiac atrophy in mice, which was significantly improved by cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression, as evidenced by the increased interventricular septal thickness at systole (IVSs) and left ventricular posterior wall thickness at end-systole (LVPWs) ( C–D). Moreover, DOX-associated loss of cardiac mass was also attenuated by LUZP1 overexpression ( E). Consistently, HE staining also revealed that cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression could prevent DOX-associated cardiomyocyte atrophy ( F). Cardiac fibrosis is the other key pathologic feature of chronic cardiotoxicity, and facilitates cardiac dysfunction. Herein, our findings indicated that cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression significantly ameliorated DOX-induced collagen synthesis and fibrotic remodeling in mice ( G–I). Moreover, the survival rate was preserved in LUZP1 cTG mice after DOX injection ( J). These results revealed that cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression attenuated DOX-induced chronic cardiac injury.
 

4.

## Discussion
DOX remains the cornerstone in cancer chemotherapy; however, the acute and chronic cardiotoxicity evidently impeded its clinic use. Herein, we defined a cardiomyocyte-enriched protein, LUZP1, as a negative molecular switch of DOX-induced cardiotoxicity in an AMPK-dependent manner. The primary findings are as following: (i) LUZP1 expression was elevated in DOX-injured hearts and cardiomyocytes; (ii) cardiac-specific LUZP1 deficiency aggravated, while cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression attenuated DOX-associated inflammatory response, oxidative damage, apoptosis and acute cardiac injury; (iii) LUZP1 ameliorated DOX-associated cardiac injury and impairment through activating AMPK pathway, and AMPK deficiency abolished the cardioprotection of LUZP1; (iv) LUZP1 interacted with PP1 to activate AMPK pathway; (v) cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression attenuated DOX-associated chronic myocardial injury. In conclusion, we demonstrated that LUZP1 attenuated DOX-elicited inflammation, oxidative damage, cell apoptosis and cardiac impairment through regulating AMPK pathway, and gene therapy targeting LUZP1 may provide novel therapeutic approaches to treat DOX-associated cardiotoxicity ( ).
 DOX shows great affinity to the myocardium, and DOX treatment causes acute cardiotoxicity in nearly 10% patients [ ]. In addition, approximately 1.7% of the patients with DOX treatment suffers chronic cardiotoxicity [ ]. Xu et al. previously found that DOX injection disturbed mitochondrial function of cardiomyocytes, and subsequently increased the generation of free radicals, and that restraining ROS production remarkably prevented DOX-induced cardiomyocyte injury and apoptosis [ ]. DOX can also be anchored in the mitochondria inner membrane, eventually disturbing electron-transport chain and facilitating the formation of superoxide anion [ ]. To scavenging the excessive free radicals, endogenous anti-oxidant enzymes exert indispensable roles; however, the heart itself possesses less active enzymatic defenses [ ]. Besides, Li et al. recently found that DOX treatment inhibited thioredoxin system, a critical anti-oxidant defense in the myocardium, and further inhibited the anti-oxidant capacity of the heart [ ]. In our study, we found that cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression elevated the expression of NRF2, enhanced the total SOD and CAT activities, thereby attenuating DOX-related oxidative stress. Nozaki et al. previously demonstrated that NF-κB activity in mouse hearts was elevated and peaked 1 h after DOX insult, accompanied with an elevated levels of inflammatory mediators [ ]. Consistently, Dhingra et al. recently found demonstrated that DOX treatment caused TNF-α toxicity [ ]. In addition, NF-κB activation also promoted the assembling and function of NLRP3 inflammasome, and subsequently triggered the release of inflammatory mediators [ ]. Treatment with MCC950, a pharmacological inhibitor of NLRP3 inflammasome, significantly attenuated DOX-induced cardiac injury [ ]. In our study, we revealed that NF-κB activity was inhibited in LUZP1 cTG hearts after DOX injury. Accordingly, cardiac-specific LUZP1 deficiency aggravated, while cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression attenuated DOX-elicited inflammatory response, oxidative stress, apoptosis and acute cardiac injury. Interestingly, LUZP1 expression was also dramatically elevated in DOX-treated hearts and cardiomyocytes. Given the beneficial effects of LUZP1, we reasonably concluded that LUZP1 was upregulated to exert the compensatory cardioprotection after DOX injury.
AMPK exhibits anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidative functions in different cardiovascular diseases. Wang and colleagues determined that AMPK was required for NF-κB inhibition, and that AMPK deficiency caused uncontrolled NF-κB activation and inflammatory response in the myocardium [ ]. AMPK activation also facilitated NRF2 expression and nuclear translocation, eventually attenuating DOX-elicited oxidative stress and cardiac impairment [ , ]. In our study, we found that AMPK pathway was the significantly altered pathways in LUZP1 cKO hearts after DOX treatment using transcriptome analysis. Further studies confirmed that cardiac-specific LUZP1 overexpression activated AMPK to block DOX-elicited inflammation, oxidative damage, cell apoptosis and cardiotoxicity, and that AMPK ablation significantly abrogated the cardioprotection of LUZP1. Yano et al. determined that LUZP1 directly interacted with PPP1C to suppress its phosphatase activity, and subsequently elevated myosin light chain phosphorylation [ ]. Meanwhile, findings from Gonçalves et al. suggested that LUZP1 interacted with PPP1C using BioID studies [ ]. In our study, we found an endogenous interaction between LUZP1 and PPP1CA/B, and this interaction was further enhanced in the myocardium by DOX stimulation. Considering the fact that LUZP1 expression was also elevated in DOX-injured hearts, we proposed that both LUZP1 expression and its affinity with PPP1CA/B were increased in the myocardium by DOX stimulation. The interaction between LUZP1 and PPP1CA/B dramatically inhibited the phosphatase activity of PPP1CA/B, thereby enhancing AMPK phosphorylation and activity. In contrast, inhibiting PPP1CA/B prevented the deleterious effects in LUZP1 cKO hearts after DOX injection. LUZP1 is composed of a LCD1 domain at the N terminus and three leucine zipper domains. Gonçalves et al. showed that the C-terminal region of LUZP1 was required for the interaction with EPLIN, and subsequently regulated actin stability and primary cilia formation [ ]. However, whether this domain also mediates the interaction between LUZP1 and PPP1CA/B deserves investigation. Intriguingly, Bi et al. found no alteration of heart rate in mice with DOX injection, and this discrepancy with our study should be validated [ ]. As we know, cardiac function is closely associated with heart rate; however, we herein found that cardiac-specific LUZP1 improved DOX-induced cardiac dysfunction without affecting heart rate. These findings suggested that the cardioprotective effects of LUZP1 against DOX-triggered cardiac impairment was at least partially secondary to the inhibition of cardiomyocyte apoptosis, independent of heart rate.
There are some limitations of this study: (i) the specific mechanism mediating LUZP1 upregulation after DOX stimulation is unclear; (ii) whether other factors contribute to the cardioprotective effects of LUZP1 should be validated in further study; (iii) the precise structural basis of the interaction between LUZP1 and PPP1CA/B is not answered in this study; (iv) whether LUZP1 overexpression post-DOX treatment can afford cardioprotection in mice is elusive; (v) whether LUZP1 intervention affect the tumor-killing effects of DOX should also be determined in further studies.

5.

## Conclusion
In this study, we reveal that LUZP1 interacts with PP1 to activate AMPK pathway, and subsequently attenuates DOX-induced inflammation, oxidative damage, cell apoptosis and ventricular impairment. In general, we define LUZP1 as a negative regulator of DOX-associated cardiotoxicity.
