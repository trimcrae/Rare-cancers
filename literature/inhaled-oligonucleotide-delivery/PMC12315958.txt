## Abstract
Lipid overload is intimately connected with the change of endothelial epigenetic status which impacts cellular signaling activities and endothelial function. Activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) is involved in the regulation of lipid metabolism and meanwhile an epigenetic modifier. However, the role of ATF4 in the angiogenesis under lipid overload is not well understood. Here, to induce lipid overload status, we employed high‐fat diet (HFD)‐induced obese mouse model in vivo and palmitic acid (PA) to stimulate endothelial cells in vitro. Compared with mice fed with normal chow diet (NCD), HFD‐induced obese mice showed angiogenic defects evidenced by decline in (1) blood flow recovery after hind limb ischemia, (2) wound healing speed after skin injury, (3) capillary density in injured tissues and matrigel plugs, and (4) endothelial sprouts of aortic ring. ATF4 deficiency aggravated above angiogenic defects in mice while ATF4 overexpression improved the blunted angiogenic response. Mechanistically, lipid overload lowered the H3K4 methylation levels at the regulatory regions of NOS3 and ERK1 genes, leading to reduced angiogenic signaling activity. Methionine adenosyltransferase 2A (MAT2A) is identified as a target of ATF4 and formed complex with ATF4 to direct lysine methyltransferase 2A (MLL1) to the regulatory regions of both genes for the maintenance of the H3K4 methylation level and angiogenic signaling activity. Here, we uncovered a novel metabolic‐epigenetic coupling orchestrated by the ATF4‐MAT2A axis for angiogenesis. The ATF4‐MAT2A axis links lipid overload milieu to altered epigenetic status of relevant angiogenic signaling in endothelial cells, suggesting a potential therapeutic target for angiogenesis impaired by lipid overload.
Keywords: activating transcription factor 4, angiogenesis, epigenetics, lipid overload, methionine adenosyltransferase 2a

## INTRODUCTION
Endothelial dysfunction and abnormal angiogenesis are hallmarks of vascular complications that cause significant morbidity and mortality in the settings of obesity and diabetes. , , , Traditionally, the abnormal angiogenesis in such settings are mostly linked to the imbalance between pro‐ and anti‐angiogenic growth factors and related signaling pathways. , , , Recently, the burgeoning notion that endothelial metabolism is another layer of regulation on angiogenesis may provide a more comprehensive mechanistic explanation for such scenarios. , 

Disturbed lipid metabolism and resultant lipotoxicity are at the center of pathology of diabetes and obesity. , Excessive lipid has been shown to blunt angiogenesis and even causes endothelial apoptosis. , Intriguingly, lipid metabolism is actually essential for angiogenesis and lymphangiogenesis. , , These seemingly paradoxical effects are possibly due to the altered endothelial epigenetic status by different cellular nutritional status. , , , For instance, obesity‐induced adverse remodeling of H3K9me2/3 and H3K9ac primes oxidative stress and dysfunction in the vasculature of obese mice. Lipid‐derived stimuli like low‐density lipoprotein alter endothelial epigenetic status such as DNA methylation and H3K4me1/H3K27me3 levels and thereby cause alteration of genes expression and endothelial response, , highlighting the importance of epigenetic players in endothelial dysfunction caused by lipid overload.
Activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), one of the master regulators of the cellular stress response, promotes adaptation of cells to various stresses via regulating the expression of a wide range of adaptive genes involved in oxidative stress, amino acid synthesis, cellular differentiation, tumor metastasis, and angiogenesis. Additionally, a line of evidence has pointed that ATF4 is involved in the regulation of lipid metabolism and one‐carbon metabolism. , Of note, ATF4 also functions as an epigenetic modifier altering chromatin structure and thus enhancing transcription. , , However, the role of ATF4 in angiogenesis under lipid overload is still elusive. On such basis, the experiments described here were designed to characterize the role of ATF4 in endothelial epigenetic alteration and angiogenic activity under lipid overload.
In the current study, we found that ATF4‐MAT2A axis maintains the epigenetic permissive state of endothelial angiogenic activity and thereby improves post‐injury angiogenesis under lipid overload. Our observations implicate the existence of a novel ATF4‐MAT2A axis operating at the interface of lipid overload milieu and histone methylation, thus shedding new light on the metabolic‐epigenetic coupling program underlying angiogenesis in the setting of lipid overload.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1.

## Animals
All the procedures on mice were performed in accordance with the approved protocol by the ethics committee of West China Hospital of Sichuan University and conformed to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The ATF4+/− mice (B6/JGpt‐Atf4em1Cd2205/Gpt, T002330) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, Jiangsu). All mice were kept on regular rodent chow and water ad libitum in a vivarium with a 12 hours light/dark cycle at 22°C until use.
Eight‐week‐old wild‐type (WT) mice or ATF4+/− mice were randomly assigned to four groups fed either normal chow diet (NCD, New Brunswick, NJ, Cat. No. D12450J, which contains 20 kcal.% protein, 70 kcal.% carbohydrates, and 10 kcal.% fat) or high‐fat diet (HFD, New Brunswick, NJ, Cat. No. D12079B, which contains 17 kcal.% protein, 43 kcal.% carbohydrates, and 40 kcal.% fat) ad libitum for 22 weeks in total. After 20 weeks, HFD fed WT or ATF4+/‐ mice with body weight gain more than 20% of the body weight means of NCD fed WT or ATF4+/‐ mice were defined as obese and were included for further experiments. At 22 weeks, all mice were sacrificed and the blood and aortic arteries were collected. Here, we used WT+NCD (n = 15), WT+HFD (n = 15), ATF4+/−+NCD (n = 15), and ATF4+/−+HFD (n = 15) to represent the four groups of mice in this study. Plasma lipid levels including total cholesterol (TC), total triglyceride (TG) and free fatty acid (FFA), and body weight were measured. The components of serum lipid including the total TG, TC, and FFA were determined using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng BioTech. Co. Ltd, Jiangsu, China).

2.2.

## Murine hind limb ischemia and laser Doppler imaging
After 20 weeks of HFD or NCD feeding, hind limb ischemia (HLI) was conducted in WT and ATF4+/− mice (n = 6 per group). The mice were anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation, the proximal femoral artery and the distal saphenous artery were ligated and removed in one hind limb. Sham surgery was performed in the contralateral hind limb of each mouse. The recovery of blood flow in the ligated limbs of mice was performed with a laser Doppler perfusion imaging (Moor Instruments) system. Perfusion was expressed as the ratio of measurements in the ligated and nonligated (sham‐operated) contralateral limb. Fourteen days after ligation, adductor and gastrocnemius muscles of both limbs of each mouse were dissected for Masson’s trichrome staining and immunohistochemistry.

2.3.

## Wound healing assay and analysis
After 20 weeks of HFD or NCD feeding, all mice were anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation. Full‐thickness skin wound was generated with a sterile 10‐mm biopsy punch on the mouse back skin without injuring the underlying muscle (n = 6 per group). The long and short diameters of the wound regions were measured and the wound areas were calculated on day 0, 2, 4, 6, 9, 11, and 13. The wound sizes at different time points were expressed as a percentage of the wound area on day 0. The state of skin was recorded by photography. Thirteen days after the wound injury, a 2‐mm wide slice of skin around the wound surface was harvested and paraffin‐embedded for immunohistochemistry.

2.4.

## Matrigel implant assay
Briefly, after 20 weeks of HFD or NCD feeding, all included mice were anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation (n = 6 per group). 500 μl of growth factor‐reduced Matrigel containing basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF, 350 ng/mL, PeproTech China, Suzhou) and recombinant human VEGF‐A165 (100 ng/mL, PeproTech China, Suzhou) plus heparin (60 U/mL), or heparin alone, was injected into the abdominal subcutaneous tissues of all mice. On day 14, mice were sacrificed, and the Matrigel plugs were removed and fixed for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry.

2.5.

## Histological and immunohistochemical staining
H&E staining of the slides of 10% neutral formaldehyde‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded plugs was performed. Masson staining was carried out for the examinations of fibrin and collagen fiber in adductor and gastrocnemius muscles of both limbs of each mouse. Image J software was used to calculate the collagen volume fraction in a blinded fashion on five sections per mouse. Immunohistochemical staining was performed on the slides of adductor and gastrocnemius muscles, Matrix plugs, skin tissues, and aortic arteries from all the mice. A panel of primary antibodies was used, including endothelial marker CD31 (Recombinant monoclonal rabbit anti‐human CD31; 1:2000; Cat. No. ab182981; Abcam), ATF4 (Monoclonal rabbit anti‐human ATF4; 1:200; Cat. No. ab216839; Abcam), MAT2A (Polyclonal rabbit anti‐human MAT2A; 1:200; Cat. No. bs‐4054R; Bioss), and NOS3 (Polyclonal rabbit anti‐human NOS3; 1:200; Cat. No. AF0096; Affinity Biosciences LTD). Capillary density was calculated by counting CD31‐expressing vascular structures in a blinded fashion on five sections per sample (two fields per section).

2.6.

## Cells and treatments
Human cardiac microvascular endothelial cells (HCMECs, ScienCell, USA) between passages 3 and 8 were cultured in endothelial cell medium (ECM, ScienCell, Cat. No. 1001, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, GE Healthcare Life Sciences), 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin at 37°C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Palmitic acid (PA, Cat. No. V900121, Sigma‐Aldrich) was dissolved in 50% ethanol‐PBS and then the solubilized PA solution was gently added dropwise into 1% essentially fatty acid‐free bovine serum albumin (BSA, Cat. No. A1595, Sigma‐Aldrich) solution at 45°C. Conjugation of the FFAs to BSA (molar ratio 2:1) was allowed to continue for at least 1 hour at 37°C. Vehicle control for fatty acid constituted equivalent amounts of ethanol and BSA without fatty acids and with final ethanol concentrations never exceeding 0.1% (v/v). HCMECs were starved for 16‐24 hours in serum‐free culture medium, and then pretreated with PA supplementation for 24 hours followed by VEGF‐A165 (100 ng/mL) supplementation for another 24 hours.

2.7.

## Isolation and culture of primary murine aortic endothelial cells (MAECs)
Aortas were harvested from 4‐week‐old male mice and immersed in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 20% FBS and 1000 U/mL of heparin after perfused with 1 mL of phosphate buffer solution (PBS) containing 1000 U/mL of heparin. Then, a 24‐gauge cannula was inserted into the proximal portion of the aorta. After ligation at the insertion site, the other side is bound and filled with collagenase type II solution (2 mg/mL, Worthington, USA), after incubation for 45 minutes at 37°C, MAECs were flushed out with DMEM containing 20% FBS and collected by centrifugation at 1200 rpm for 5 minutes. Then MAECs were re‐suspended and cultured in a 10 mm dish coated with rat tail type I collagen (Cat. No. 08‐115, Merck Millipore). To remove smooth muscle cells, the medium is removed after 2 hours incubation at 37°C. Then, MAECs were washed and cultured in ECM supplemented with 20% FBS. MACEs were used up to passage 4.

2.8.

## RNA interference and adenoviral transduction of HCMECs
MACEs and HCMECs were transfected at 60‐70% confluence with 30 nM siRNAs targeting human ATF4 (siATF4, GenePharma, Shanghai, China) or human MAT2A (siMAT2A, GenePharma, Shanghai, China) using siRNA transfection reagent (Lipofectamine 3000, Invitrogen) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Twelve hours after transfection, HCMECs were cultured in fresh complete ECM for an additional 24 hours before further experiments.
Adenovirus encoding human ATF4 (Ad‐ATF4), human MAT2A (Ad‐MAT2A), green fluorescent protein (Ad‐GFP), GFP‐labeled‐ATF4 (Ad‐ATF4‐GFP), ATF4 shRNA (Ad‐shATF4), and MAT2A shRNA (Ad‐shMAT2A) and the control adenovirus (Ad‐Scr) were purchased from Vigene Biosciences (Jinan, Shandong). MACEs and HCMECs at 80% confluence were transduced with the adenovirus and were used for further experiments for 36 hours after the transduction.

2.9.

## Aortic ring assay
Aortas were harvested from 4‐week‐old male mice and sliced into 2‐mm wide rings. In some experiments, the sliced aortic rings were cultured and transduced with Ad‐GFP or Ad‐ATF4‐GFP for 12 hours before the rings were placed into the plate well coated with collagen gel. Thirty‐six hours later, 1mL of ECM with VEGF‐A165 (100 ng/mL) was added to each well. On day 5, the tissue and the sprouting endothelial cells were fixed. Endothelial sprouts were imaged on an Olympus IX81‐ZDC inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Center Valley, PA, USA). Vessel sprouting was quantified using AngioTool software to calculate the total number of vessel junctions.

2.10.

## Flow cytometric assay
HCMECs with indicated treatment were harvested and washed twice with pre‐cooled PBS and then resuspended in 100 μL of 1× binding buffer (4A Biotech, Beijing, China). Next, 1 ×106 cells were incubated with 5 μL of FITC Annexin V and 10 μL of propidium iodide (PI) working solution avoiding light for 15 minutes at 37°C. After adding 400 μL of 1× binding buffer, the cells were tested by flow cytometry on a FACS Calibur (BD Biosciences). The data were analyzed by CytExpert 2.0.

2.11.

## In vitro tube formation analysis
HCMECs with indicated treatment were seeded into 48‐well plates (6 × 104 cells/well) that had been coated with 250 mL of Matrigel (Cat. No. 354234, Biocoat). Twelve hours later, the endothelial tubes were observed using an inverted fluorescent microscope, and tube formation was quantified as the total number of master segments in five randomly selected fields per well. Analyses were performed with Image J software, and each experiment was conducted three times.

2.12.

## EdU incorporation analysis
HCMECs with indicated treatment were starved for 16‐24 hours in serum‐free medium and then incubated with 30 μM 5‐ethynyl‐2‐deoxyuridine (EdU, RiboBio, Guangzhou) in complete growth medium for an additional 6 hours. Following EdU treatment, cells were fixed, and incubated with Apollo dye for 30 minutes. The cells were then washed and stained with Hoechst to visualize the nuclei. Images were obtained using an inverted microscope at 10× magnification.

2.13.

## Spheroid capillary sprouting assay
HCMECs with indicated treatment (800 cells/per spheroid) were incubated for 24 hours in 40 μL of ECM supplemented with 0.25% (w/v) methylcellulose (Cat. No. M0512, Sigma‐Aldrich) to form spheroids. The spheroids were harvested and embedded in 0.9 mL of collagen solution in a pre‐warmed 24‐well plate, with a final concentration of rat tail type I collagen (Cat. No. 08‐115, Merck Millipore) at 1.5 mg/mL. The spheroid‐containing gels were rapidly transferred into a humidified incubator (37°C, 5% CO2) and allowed to polymerize for 20 minutes, after which 0.1 mL of ECM basal medium supplemented with 100 ng/mL of VEGF‐A165 were pipetted on top of the gel. After 24 hours, gels were fixed with pre‐warmed 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), and images were captured using an inverted microscope. The number of sprouts per spheroid was quantified from five spheroids for each condition using Image J software.

2.14.

## Real‐time RT‐PCR
Total RNA was extracted from MACEs using the TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen). Total RNA was reverse‐transcribed into the first‐strand cDNA using Omniscript reverse synthase (Qiagen). Quantitative real‐time PCR was conducted using SYBR green (Bio‐Rad). The mRNA levels were normalized to Ribosomal protein S11 (RPS11) mRNA levels and repeated for at least three times. The sequences of the primers are as follows (Table ).

2.15.

## Western blotting
Western blot analysis was performed to detect the related protein level. Cells were lysed in cell lysis buffer in the presence of a cocktail of proteinase/phosphatase inhibitors (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. No. 5872) and were centrifuged at 12 000× g for 30 minutes at 4°C. Equal amount of proteins were separated by SDS‐PAGE electrophoresis and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membrane was blocked and incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. We used the following primary antibodies for Western blotting: ATF4 (1:500, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. No. DB48), NOS3 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. No. D9A5L), ERK1/2 (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. No. 137F5), Tubulin (1:1000, Abcam, Cat. No. Ab7291), MAT2A antibody (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat. No. 55309‐1‐AP), and beta‐actin (1:2000, Cat. No. AF7018, Affinity Biosciences LTD). After washing, the membrane was incubated with the appropriate secondary antibodies: goat anti‐mouse IgG (H+L) antibody or goat anti‐mouse IgG (H+L) antibody was applied. Immunoblot signal was detected by chemiluminescence method using an ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio‐Rad, USA), intensity of bands was densitometrically quantified using Image J, and the protein abundance was normalized to the levels of beta‐actin or tubulin.

2.16.

## Immunoprecipitation
HCMECs were collected in PBS with 0.1% Triton X‐100. After ultrasonic homogenization, the cell lysates were centrifuged and the supernatants incubated with Protein A/G agarose beads (Cat. No. P2028, Byotime Biotechnology) for 1 hour. The samples were centrifuged again and the protein concentrations in the supernatant were measured by protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The protein samples were incubated with MAT2A antibody (Proteintech, Cat. No. 55309‐1‐AP) or MLL1 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. No. 26482; Active Motif, Cat. No. 39829) or IgG (Abcam, Cat. No. ab172730; Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. No. G3A1) overnight at 4°C. The Protein A/G agarose beads were added in and incubated overnight at 4°C. After binding, the beads were washed by PBS including 0.1% Triton X‐100 for five times. Proteins were eluted by 0.1 M glycine and subjected to SDS‐PAGE and Western blotting.

2.17.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay
Approximately 1 × 107 HCMECs with indicated treatment were harvested and processed using a ChIP assay kit (SimpleChIP Plus Sonication Chromatin IP Kit, Cell signaling technology, Cat. No. 56383) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were cross‐linked by adding directly to the culture medium formaldehyde (Cat. No. F8775, Sigma‐Aldrich) to a final concentration of 1% for 10 minutes at room temperature. After quenching the cross‐linking reaction by 125 mM glycine for 5 minutes at room temperature, the cells were washed with ice cold 1× PBS. Next, cell pellets were lysed in lysis buffer containing 1× protease inhibitor cocktail, and then sonicated to yield DNA fragments of approximately 200~500 bp using a Sonics Vibra‐Cell processor (Sonics & Materials Vibra Cell VC‐750, USA) for 3 minutes (1 s on/1 s off) at 35% of maximum power. After saving a 10% of the solubilized chromatin as input, the sheared chromatin was diluted to 10‐fold and subjected to immunoprecipitation using antibodies (1 μg each) directed against ATF4, H3K4me1, H3K4me3, MLL1 along with corresponding IgG control coupled with ChIP‐Grade Protein G Magnetic Beads for overnight at 4°C. Antibody‐chromatin complexes were pulled‐down using Magnetic Separation Rack. After de‐crosslinking and proteinase K treatment the immunoprecipitated DNA was extracted and diluted to 10‐fold, quantified, and subjected to qPCR analysis as described above. The primers for ChIP assay are listed as follows (Table ).

2.18.

## LC‐mass spectrum analysis of metabolites
HCMECs with indicated treatment were harvested. After rinsed with PBS, the cells were incubated with 80% pre‐chilled methanol for 30 minutes at −80°C. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation and dried by spin vacuum and dissolved in ACN solution. Then, 200 μL of sample was analyzed through an Thermo QE Plus LC‐MS/MS system. Data were analyzed using the Progenesis QI (version 2.2) (Waters, UK) and R software suite (version 3.4.0). Univariate analysis and multivariate analysis were employed to screen the metabolites with differential expression.

2.19.

## Statistics
Student’s two‐tailed t test was used to determine statistical significance in experiments with two groups. One‐way and two‐way ANOVA with Turkey’s multiple comparisons test were used to determine statistical significance depending on experimental designs. Error bars represent the mean ± standard deviation. P value below .05 was considered as significant. Statistical tests and graphs were made using the Prism 7 software (GraphPad Software).

## RESULTS
3.1.

## ATF4 deficiency exaggerates the blunted ischemic angiogenesis in HFD‐induced obesity
We employed a diet‐induced obesity model to investigate the impact of lipid overload on angiogenesis in vivo. First, primary MAECs were isolated and cultured for western blotting assay to confirm that ATF4 expression is decreased in endothelial cells of ATF4+/− mice (Supporting information Figure S1A). Then, all mice were assigned into HFD or NCD group. After 20 weeks of HFD, significant body weight gain was observed in both WT‐HFD and ATF4+/−‐HFD mice compared with their NCD‐control groups (Supporting information Figure S1B). HFD also caused increases in plasma total TG, TC, and FFA in WT‐HFD and ATF4+/−‐HFD mice compared with WT‐NCD and ATF4+/−‐NCD (Supporting information Figure S1C‐E), respectively.
Then, all mice were subjected to unilateral femoral artery ligation to induce HLI. WT‐HFD obese mice had significantly lower ratio of perfusion measurements in the ischemic and non‐ischemic hind limbs compared with WT‐NCD mice 14 days after femoral artery ligation, indicating obesity impairs postischemic blood flow recovery. ATF4+/−‐HFD obese mice showed the poorest blood flow recovery at day 14, indicating ATF4 deficiency further inhibited the impaired perfusion in HFD‐induced obese mice (Figure ). Immunohistochemistry assay demonstrated that HFD‐induced obesity reduced the measures of capillary density of ischemic limb muscles and thus resulted in increased volume of muscular fibrosis. Moreover, ATF4+/−‐HFD obese mice exhibited further decrease in muscular capillary density and increase in volume of muscular fibrosis compared with WT‐HFD mice (Figure ).
 Our observations in the HLI model were supported by monitoring the endothelial angiogenic activity into matrigel plug. The measures of capillary density were significantly lower in plugs from WT‐HFD mice and were nearly abrogated in the plugs from ATF4+/−‐HFD mice (Figure ). Subsequently we isolated aortic rings from mice in the four groups and cultured the rings in collagen matrix to compare their vascular response to VEGF (100 ng/mL) ex vivo. In the presence of VEGF, the aortic rings of WT‐HFD mice produced significantly fewer and shorter vascular sprouts relative to WT‐NCD mice (Figure ). The number of branching sprouts was further decreased in the aortic rings of ATF4+/−‐HFD mice compared with WT‐HFD or ATF4+/−‐NCD mice (Figure ).

3.2.

## ATF4 deficiency further delays the wound closure in HFD‐induced obesity
Angiogenesis is critical, if not sufficient, for wound repair due to its role in supplying oxygen and nutrients which support the growth and function of reparative cells in damaged tissues. We next evaluated whether ATF4 deficiency exerts adverse impact on the wound healing response under lipid overload. In agreement with above results, WT‐HFD obese mice exhibited poorer skin wound healing response compared with WT‐NCD mice, as reflected by much slower closure over a period of 14 days. ATF4 deficiency further delayed the wound closure in ATF4+/−‐HFD mice, as evidenced by that ATF4+/−‐HFD mice had the largest wound sizes on 4, 6, 9, 11, and 13 days after skin injury (Figure ). Immunohistochemistry assay demonstrated that WT‐HFD obese mice had significantly less capillary density than WT‐NCD mice. Moreover, ATF4 deficiency further diminished the number of CD31‐positive microvasculature in ATF4+/−‐HFD obese mice (Figure ).
 
3.3.

## ATF4 over‐expression improves endothelial angiogenic response under lipid overload
Disturbance in lipid metabolism is very common in obese patients. Approximately 60‐70% of patients with obesity are dyslipidemic. In the present study, HFD‐induced obese mice exhibited elevated plasma total TG, TC, and FFA, suggesting lipid overload status. Thus, HCMECs were cultured with PA at varied concentrations (50~400 μM) to simulate the lipid overload status. Flow cytometry assay showed that endothelial apoptosis increased with the elevation of PA concentration. 100μM of PA induced slight increase in the apoptosis of HCMECs while more than 100 μM of PA significantly increased the apoptosis of HCMECs (Figure ). EdU incorporation assay and capillary network formation on Matrigel indicated endothelial proliferation and tube formation were significantly inhibited at more than 100 μM of PA (Figure ). Endothelial proliferation activity was retained while tube formation started to decrease at the concentration of 100 μM. Based on this, thereafter HCMECs or MACEs were pretreated for 24 hours with vehicle control or BAS‐conjugated PA (100 μM) followed by culture with VEGF (100 ng/mL) and PA supplementation for another 24 hours.
 Capillary network formation on Matrigel and spheroid sprouting was diminished in PA‐treated HCMECs (Figure ). Transfection with Ad‐shATF4 or Ad‐ATF4 markedly decreased or increased the endothelial expression of ATF4 (Supporting information Figure S3A‐D). ATF4 knockdown further decreased the number of tube formation and spheroid sprouts while ATF4 overexpression promoted tube formation on Matrigel and endothelial sprouts in PA‐treated HCMECs (Figure ). To further confirm that ATF4 overexpression restored the angiogenic response under lipid overload, Ad‐ATF4‐GFP was transfected into the aortic endothelium from ATF4+/− mice. As shown in Figure , aortic ring from WT‐HFD mice had markedly less sprouts compared with WT‐NCD mice. Furthermore, the sprouting activity of aortic ring from ATF4+/−‐HFD mice was nearly abrogated. ATF4 overexpression increased the number of aortic sprouts from WT‐HFD mice (Figure ). Of note, in the absence of lipid overload status, ATF4 overexpression exercises no impacts on the angiogenic response to VEGF while ATF4 knockdown markedly inhibited the angiogenic response in vitro or ex vivo, suggesting ATF4 is required for maintaining proper angiogenic response under lipid overload condition.

3.4.

## ATF4 maintains the expression of endothelial NOS3 and ERK1 under lipid overload
Considering VEGF signaling is of profound importance in angiogenesis, we determined whether the VEGF‐related signaling response is affected by lipid overload. As shown in Figure , PA treatment resulted in significant decline in the mRNA and protein expression of nitric oxide synthase 3 (NOS3) and extracellular signal‐regulated protein kinase 1 (ERK1). In the presence of PA, ATF4 knockdown further reduced the expression of both genes (Figure ). Immunohistochemistry staining on aortic endothelium demonstrated that ATF4 deficiency intensified the decline in the expression of NOS3 and ERK1 in mice on HFD (Figure ).
 
3.5.

## ATF4 maintains the H3K4 methylation status at the regulatory regions of NOS3 and ERK1 genes under lipid overload
Given that dietary fat or obesity could influence cellular epigenetic status, , we asked whether lipid overload affects endothelial epigenetic status. ENCODE interrogation of the regulatory regions of NOS and ERK1 genes in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (GEO dataset ID: GSE96250, GSE35583, GSE29611) revealed abundant histone 3 lysine 4 methylation (H3K4me) and histone acetylation (H3K27ac and H3K9ac) in proximity of the transcription start sites of NOS3 and ERK1 genes (Figure ). H3K27 trimethylation, H3K9, and H4K20 methylation are almost absent across the whole length of DNA sequence of these genes (Figure ). While H3K4me1 is primarily associated with enhancers, H3K4me3 engages at promoter sites, both H3K9ac and H3K27ac represent an active regulatory region of the gene, H3K27me3 denotes transcriptional repression, indicating the expression of NOS3, and ERK1 is epigenetically permissive on physiological condition. In contrast, ChIP assay detected a significant decrease in the H3K4 methylation enrichment at the regulatory regions of NOS3 and ERK1 genes in PA‐treated HCMECs relative to the control group (Figure ). ATF4 knockdown produced a further decrease in the H3K4 methylation level in HCMECs treated with PA while ATF4 overexpression markedly elevated the H3K4 methylation levels despite of the presence of PA treatment (Figure ).
 Overall, lipid overload inhibits endothelial angiogenic signaling through altering H3K4 methylation status of NOS3 and ERK1 genes that are important for angiogenesis. ATF4 is crucial for maintaining the H3K4 methylation levels of both genes in the setting of lipid overload.

3.6.

## ATF4/MAT2A axis is responsible for the recruitment of MLL1 to the regulatory regions of NOS3 and ERK1
We then asked how ATF4 modulates the H3K4 methylation levels at the regulatory regions of NOS3 and ERK1 genes. ATF4 regulates the activity of serine and one‐carbon metabolism , through which metabolic intermediates are provided to fuel methionine cycle generating S‐adenosylmethionine (SAM). Importantly, SAM is the main methyl‐group donor for methylation reactions including histone methylation modification. Thus, we proposed that ATF4 drives SAM synthesis for histone methylation reaction. In agreement with previous reports, we found that ATF4 knockdown in MACEs significantly downregulated the expression of enzymes including phosphoserine aminotransferase 1 (PSAT1), phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH), and serine hydroxymethyltransferase 2 (SHMT2), which are involved in the serine and one‐carbon metabolism (Figure ). Methionine adenosyltransferase 2A (MAT2A) for SAM synthesis was markedly downregulated by ATF4 knockdown (Figure ). In addition, ChIP assay identified MAT2A as a target of ATF4 (Figure ). Importantly, MAT2A knockdown abolished the upregulation of NOS3 and ERK1 expression by ATF4 overexpression (Figure ).
 Although MAT2A catalyzes the generation of SAM, the metabolomic data showed no significant change in the total amount of endothelial SAM (Figue ), suggesting an alternative mechanism for MAT2A to influence the H3K4 methylation reaction. Given that MAT2A interacts with transcription factors and epigenetic regulators to regulate gene expression, we tested the possibility that MAT2A recruits histone lysine methyltransferase 2A (MLL1) to the regulatory regions of NOS3 and ERK1 for histone methylation in an ATF4‐dependent manner. As shown in Figure , ATF4, MAT2A, and MLL1 formed complex. Of note, ATF4 formed complex with MLL1 is dependent on MAT2A. At the regulatory regions of NOS3 and ERK1 genes, there exist ATF4 binding sites (cAMP response element, CRE) that are in close proximity with or overlap with the H3K4 methylation enrichment areas. We next performed ChIP assay using anti‐MLL1 antibody. MLL1 was recruited to the regulatory regions of NOS3 and ERK1 genes, which could be abolished or enhanced by ATF4 knockdown or overexpression (Figure ). Interestingly, MAT2A overexpression cannot restore the abolished recruitment of MLL1 by ATF4 knockdown while MAT2A knockdown markedly abrogated the recruitment of MLL1 to the regulatory regions of NOS3 and ERK1 genes by ATF4 overexpression (Figure ), suggesting that MLL1 recruitment is dependent on both ATF4 and MAT2A.

## DISCUSSION
In this study, we described a novel metabolic‐epigenetic mechanism by which ATF4 regulates post‐injury angiogenesis in the setting of lipid overload. Specifically, MAT2A enzyme in methionine cycle is identified as a target of ATF4. ATF4 upregulates MAT2A expression, in turn MAT2A collaborates with ATF4 to direct MLL1 to the regulatory regions of NOS3 and ERK1 gene loci for the maintenance of endothelial epigenetic active state and angiogenic response to tissue injury under lipid overload condition.
In recent years endothelial metabolism has been increasingly accepted as an important regulatory mechanism on angiogenesis or lymphangiogenesis and thus a focus of intense research. , , Metabolic pathways, such as glycolysis, fatty acid oxidation, and glutamine metabolism, have distinct but essential roles during vessel formation. , , These metabolic pathways produce intermediates which provide raw materials for endothelial proliferation or attenuate oxidative stress during intense endothelial activities to maintain normal endothelial function. , , , Intriguingly, excessive lipid inhibits angiogenesis. , Consistently, our observation demonstrated that lipid overload condition caused blunted post‐injury angiogenesis in vivo and in vitro. Regarding to the impacts of lipid on endothelial function, previous studies mostly focus on the negative impacts of oxidative stress and apoptosis on endothelial cells. Our results also demonstrated that endothelial apoptosis occurred at a higher concentration of PA treatment (400 μM). But from a clinical perspective, lipid metabolism disturbance in diabetes and obesity is a dynamic and progressively deteriorating pathological process. Indeed, we found that different concentrations of PA treatment produce differential impacts on endothelial function. A lower concentration of PA treatment (100 μM) showed no influences on endothelial proliferation and apoptosis, but significantly impaired the sprouting activities of endothelial cells. These observations indicate that different degrees of lipid overload produce differential endothelial dysfunction phenotypes even cell death, indicating the involvement of different pathological mechanisms. Our findings indicate that the blunted post‐injury angiogenesis under lipid overload is associated with altered endothelial epigenetics. Under normal condition, the highly abundant H3K4 methylation level at the regulatory regions of NOS3 and ERK1 gene loci indicates an epigenetic permissive state of endothelial cells, representing a “switch on” state of angiogenic response. In contrast, under lipid overload condition, the reduced H3K4 methylation level indicates an epigenetic repressive state of endothelial cells, representing a “switch off” state of angiogenic response (Figure ). Consistently, previous work has shown that obesity‐induced adverse remodeling of H3K9me2/3 and H3K9ac primes oxidative stress and dysfunction in the vasculature of obese mice. Low‐density lipoprotein or hyperglycemia alters epigenetic status such as DNA methylation and H3K4me1/H3K27me3 levels of genes like endothelial kruppel‐like factor 2 or apoptotic‐associated genes. , These observations indicate that endothelial metabolic‐epigenetics coupling underlies endothelial function. However, current knowledge of the endothelial epigenetic alteration under lipid overload is limited. The interaction between gene and environment as a putative mechanism for blunted angiogenesis under lipid overload remains poorly understood. In addition, considering the widespread effects of the endothelial metabolic‐epigenetics alteration, characterizing a complete picture of endothelial epigenetic status under lipid overload may be helpful for developing individualized therapy strategy for vascular complications of obesity and diabetes.
 More importantly, we found that ATF4 improves post‐injury angiogenesis by targeting the adverse epigenetic remodeling under lipid overload. Previous reports pointed that ATF4 promotes angiogenesis mainly through transcriptionally upregulating the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). , Of note, lipid metabolism disturbance is commonly associated with the occurrence and development of ischemic vascular diseases. The pathological state of sustained ischemia and hypoxia stimulates the activity of hypoxia‐inducible factor 1α, which serves as a transcription factor to promote the activity of VEGF and other pro‐angiogenic proteins. Thus, due to the enhanced growth factor expression in local ischemic tissues, ATF4 probably only produces marginal impact on post‐injury angiogenesis through upregulating the expression of VEGF. Consistently, the therapeutic strategy based on delivery of pro‐angiogenic growth factors has not seen clear clinical benefit yet, demonstrating that endothelial cells have low response to pro‐angiogenic stimuli. Our findings indicate that ATF4 maintains endothelial epigenetic status and a proper angiogenic response to tissue injury under lipid overload. These findings advance our knowledge respecting the influence of ATF4 on endothelial cells, suggesting a potential therapeutic target for vascular complications of diabetes and obesity.
ATF4 regulates serine and one‐carbon metabolism through transcriptionally upregulating related metabolic enzymes. , In addition, we found that MAT2A enzyme in methionine cycle is also a target of ATF4, establishing the importance of ATF4 on cell metabolism. However, subsequent metabolomics analysis showed no change in the intracellular total SAM levels, which raise the concern whether MAT2A‐catalyzed SAM generation is responsible for the H3K4 methylation modification in endothelial cells. Nevertheless, discretion should be applied to reach the conclusion that MAT2A enzymatic activity and its product SAM are not responsible for the endothelial epigenetic maintenance and regulation of endothelial angiogenic response. This may be attributable to the compartmentalized distribution of MAT2A, which is mainly localized in nuclei and cytoplasm. And histone methylation occurs in the nuclei while cytoplasmic SAM pool undergoes constant fluctuation, so more precisely dissecting the compartmentalized nuclear SAM pool may be helpful for conclusively deciding whether MAT2A enzymatic activity is required for the regulation of endothelial angiogenic response. Importantly, this observation provide a clue that methionine metabolism may be an under‐studied player in the angiogenesis process.
In conclusion, we reported that ATF4‐MAT2A axis regulates post‐injury angiogenesis under lipid overload. The metabolic‐epigenetic coupling mediated by ATF4‐MAT2A axis may constitute a novel mechanism in modulating post‐injury angiogenesis under lipid overload and have a potential therapeutic implication in vascular complications in obesity and diabetes.
