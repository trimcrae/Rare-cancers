## Abstract
Probiotic supplementation was a novel therapeutic approach for treating metabolic related diseases via “gut-liver” axis. However, the effect of Clostridium butyricum (CB) supplementation on type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)-associated steatohepatitis was unknown. This study revealed that CB alleviated liver damage and hepatitis in 18 weeks-old db/db mice. Targeted metabolomics analysis showed that the dysregulated short chain fatty acids (SCFAs) metabolism homeostasis was regained by CB in the colon content of db/db mice, especially butyric acid. Treatment with sodium butyrate (NaB) significantly attenuated steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis of db/db mice, and high glucose (HG) and free fatty acid (FFA) co-treated HepG2 cells. In-depth mechanism research suggested that the hepatoprotective effects of CB on T2DM was associated with the suppression of IκB-α/β-arrestin2/NF-κB signaling pathway via intestinal butyrate-medicated hepatic Takeda G-protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5). Overall, our results demonstrated a potential novel mechanism for CB as effective nutritional intervention for T2DM-related steatohepatitis via “gut-liver” axis.

## Introduction
Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) was a long-term and widespread metabolic condition that affected various organs, including the pancreas, muscles and liver. Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) was a prevalent complication of T2DM, with epidemiological research indicating that the global incidence of MASLD among individuals with T2DM ranged from 25%–29% . As the progresses of MASLD, T2DM patients could further suffer from metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH). With the acquirement of hepatic inflammation, MASH would further progress to irreversible hepatic cirrhosis via extracellular matrix deposition (ECM) induced by inflammatory factors, and even hepatocellular carcinoma .
Notably, the pathogenesis of MASH differed significantly between patients with and without concomitant T2DM. MASH in T2DM patients was characterized by more profound systemic insulin resistance (IR), affecting not only liver and adipose tissue but also skeletal muscle, leading to sustained hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, and severe lipid metabolic disorders (e.g., hypertriglyceridemia) oxidation , . This systemic metabolic derangement exacerbates hepatic lipotoxicity compared to isolated MASH, where insulin resistance was primarily localized to hepatic and adipose compartments. Additionally, T2DM-driven persistent hyperglycemia intensifies hepatic inflammation and oxidative stress through mechanisms like advanced glycation end-product (AGE) formation and activation of pro-inflammatory pathways, whereas isolated MASH relies mainly on intrahepatic lipotoxicity to trigger inflammation species – . Concomitant T2DM also accelerates hepatic fibrosis progression, driven by more severe inflammation, microvascular complications, and gut microbiota dysbiosis that exacerbated liver injury via the gut-liver axis cirrhosis , . Hepatocyte injury in T2DM-associated MASH was more pronounced, with impaired insulin signaling (e.g., IRS1/AKT pathway) compromising autophagy and promoting cell death, unlike the milder hepatocyte damage seen in isolated MASH damage , . Thus, it was essential to prevent the progression of MASH in T2DM.
Currently, the treatment options for T2DM included antihyperglycemia and lowering IR. However, gastrointestinal reactions were the significant adverse reactions of new hypoglycemic drugs, for instance, GLP-1 receptor agonists . The main treatment of MASH was to change lifestyle through diet and exercise, and the ultimately goal was to lose weight. New drugs for treating MASH were under clinical development, such as PPAR agonists, FXR agonists, GLP-1, FGF-21 or SGLT2 inhibitors . Nevertheless, there was still no cure for MASH, not to speak of T2DM-induced MASH. With further research, the important role of “gut-liver” axis in T2DM or/and MASLD/MASH has received increasing attention, including intestinal specific origin of endogenous metabolites, including short chain fatty acids (SCFAs) , .
Along with the rapid development of basic and clinical research on probiotics, their clinical applications have become increasingly widespread in gastrointestinal related diseases, such as diarrhea, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), irritable bowel syndrome, and so on. Besides, there were several trials ongoing on the therapeutic efficacy of probiotic supplementation in diabetes and MASLD . The types of probiotics were varied, such as Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Streptococcus, and Clostridium. Latest studies showed that Clostridium butyricum (CB) supplementation improved blood glucose levels and alleviated vascular inflammation in T2DM , . CB ameliorated MASLD caused by high fat diet (HFD) in rats, and CB Capsules combined with rosuvastatin affected liver function and inflammation in MASLD Patient . As a new type of probiotic microorganism, CB was the preferred product for protecting intestinal health, and it exhibited beneficial effects on various disease conditions involving inflammation, especially IBD . CB could ferment sugars to form a large amount of SCFAs, which could maintain the intestinal microenvironment, and reduced the occurrence of enterotoxins. Clinical study revealed that CB protected against autoimmune diabetes by modulating intestinal immune homeostasis . Thus, we hypothesized that CB might be beneficial for improving hepatitis caused by T2DM through the gut-liver axis, especially intestine SCFAs.
In this study, we focused on the hepatoprotective effect and potential mechanisms of CB on T2DM-induced hepatitis by using diabetic mice and HepG2 cells co-treated with HG and FFA. Our findings might uncover a feasible new mechanism for CB as a substantial nutritional preventive measure for T2DM-associated hepatitis by modulating gut-liver axis.

## Results

## CB attenuated the development of MASH in db/db mice
The entire intervention schedule of the in vivo study was shown in Fig. . First, we measured body weight, liver weight, liver weight coefficient and food intake. Compared with db/db mice, CB treatment reduced db/db mice’s liver weight and liver weight coefficient. For food intake, db/db + CB mice had higher intake than db/db from 14 weeks (Fig. ). Along with the reduced FBG levels, CB treatment also improved db/db mice’s liver morphology (including attenuated steatosis and fibrosis by HE and Masson staining) and liver function (Fig. ). Subsequently, we found that CB could significantly down-regulate TC and TG levels of diabetic mice, but there was no statistical significance in the serum levels of HDL and LDL (Fig. ). At the same time, the contents of TC and TG were also detected in mice’s liver, and the trend of TC and TG were the same with these in mice serum, indicating that CB could downregulate the contents of TC and TG in diabetic mice’s liver (Fig. ). Accompanying with the alleviated hepatic lipid accumulation, CB further inhibited hepatic inflammatory course of diabetic mice, as demonstrated by the down-regulated levels of IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α in mice liver (Fig. ). Besides, the decreased protein expression of Collagen Ⅳ and Laminin corroborated the reduced fibrosis observed histologically by CB in diabetic mice (Fig. ). These results revealed that CB alleviated liver damage and hepatitis of diabetic mice.
 

## Butyrate was the functional MASH-protective metabolite produced by CB in db/db mice
Intestinal homeostasis played an important role in the pathological development of T2DM and/or MASLD through the gut-liver axis, and CB has been shown to degrade undigested dietary fiber, producing SCFAs in the colon, which helped maintain intestinal equilibrium . To explore the impact of CB on intestinal homeostasis, we examined both the colonic pathological changes and SCFA metabolism in the colon contents of db/db mice. As shown in Fig. , CB treatment alleviated some colon lesions and inflammation in diabetic mice, leading to the changes in SCFA metabolic profiles. PCA of SCFAs revealed that colon contents from db/db mice clearly formed a distinct cluster, separated from those of db/m mice, while the CB treatment group clustered closer to the db/m group (Fig. ). A permutation test confirmed that the model did not overfit the data (Fig. ). Further analysis demonstrated that the control and CB groups clustered together, whereas the db/db group formed a separate cluster (Fig. ). Additionally, OPLS-DA revealed that T2DM caused significant disturbances in the SCFA metabolic profiles in the colon contents of mice, and that CB treatment partially restored these profiles (Fig. ).
 SCFA compositions of the mice’s colon contents were displayed Fig. . Acetic acid and butyric acid were the most noticeable alterations brought on by T2DM when compared to db/m mice, with ~66.19% and 19.40%, respectively. Acetic acid was reduced to 51.67% and butyric acid was elevated to 36.65% by CB treatment in the colon contents of db/db mice. The main metabolite of CB was butyric acid, which played a very important role in intestinal homeostasis . Thus, the levels of butyric acid were further simultaneously quantified in the colon contents of mice. Compared with those in db/m mice, butyric acid levels were significantly lower in the colon contents of db/db mice, which were upregulated by CB treatment (Fig. ). We also quantified hepatic SCFA concentrations (acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid) in mice. After administration of CB to db/db mice, compared with untreated db/db group, acetic or propionic acid levels did not change significantly, but butyric acid levels increased (Fig. , Fig. ). These results indicated that SCFAs metabolized by CB in the intestine, particularly butyrate, reached pharmacologically relevant concentrations in the liver to alleviate T2DM-induced MASH.

## NaB inhibited MASH progression in vivo and in vitro
To investigate the impact of butyric acid on T2DM-associated MASH, both in vivo and in vitro studies were carried out. The complete intervention timeline for the in vivo study was outlined in Fig. . After NaB administration, FBG levels in diabetic mice were significantly reduced (Fig. ). Additionally, liver morphology (HE and Masson staining) and liver function in db/db mice showed marked improvement following NaB treatment (Fig. ). Oil Red O staining demonstrated that NaB relieved increased lipid accumulation in diabetic mice’s liver (Fig. ). Moreover, NaB treatment led to a reduction in TC and TG levels in diabetic mice’s liver (Fig. ). In terms of inflammation, NaB inhibited the increase of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in diabetic mice’s liver (Fig. ). Additionally, NaB reduced collagen deposition in the liver, evidencing by the decreased expression of Collagen Ⅳ and Laminin (Fig. ).
 To further investigate the effects of NaB, in vitro experiments were performed. The CCK-8 assay results revealed that NaB concentrations of 1.25, 2.5, 5, and 10 mM had no significant impact on the cell viability, while viability was significantly reduced at concentrations of 20 mM and 40 mM (Fig. ). In HepG2 cells co- incubated with HG and FFA, cell viability was significantly lower compared to those cultured with NG, but NaB treatment dose-dependently increased cell viability, with the most pronounced effect at 5 mM (Fig. ). Thus, NaB concentrations of 1.25, 2.5, and 5 mM were selected for the subsequent experiments. As shown in Fig. , hepatic injury induced by HG and FFA co-stimulation was dose-dependently alleviated by NaB, evidencing by the reduced levels of ALT, AST, ALP, and LDH in co-cultured HepG2 cells. Furthermore, NaB inhibited the increase of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in the culture medium of HG and FFA co-cultured HepG2 cells in a dose-dependent way (Fig. ). Meanwhile, NaB also inhibited the increased expression of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in HepG2 cells co-stimulated with HG and FFA in a dose-dependent way (Fig. ). Moreover, the elevated levels of Collagen Ⅳ and Laminin, markers of collagen deposition, were decreased by NaB in HG and FFA co-incubated HepG2 cells, indicating reduced fibrosis (Fig. ). Together, these findings confirmed that NaB protected against hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis under diabetic conditions.
 

## The protective effect of NaB in vitro was dependent on TGR5
NaB participated in inflammation via G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), and NaB upregulated Takeda G-protein-coupled receptor 5 (TGR5), a member of the GPCR super-family, in HG and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) co-cultured Caco-2 cells, resulting in the alleviation of inflammation , , . To investigate the interaction between NaB and TGR5, we performed molecular docking simulation. The docking of NaB with TGR5 yielded 9 conformations. The optimal mode had a binding energy of -4.371 kcal/mol and formed hydrogen bonds with Cys-359 and Ser-349 (Fig. ; Fig. ; Table ). In addition, the luciferase reporter assay showed that NaB dose-dependently enhanced the luminous intensities of TGR5, and the effect of high dosage was higher than INT-777 (a specific agonist of TGR5) group, indicating that NaB induced TGR5 activities in HepG2 cells (Fig. ). Thus, we speculated that NaB alleviated inflammatory injury via activating TGR5 in the liver of T2DM-associated hepatitis.
 We initially investigated the protein expression of TGR5 in vivo. Western blot analysis and IHC demonstrated that TGR5 expression was down-regulated in db/db mice’s liver, and this reduction was reversed by NaB treatment (Fig. ). Furthermore, TGR5 expression was also increased in the liver of db/db mice following treatment with CB (Fig. ). In agreement with the in vivo findings, NaB treatment was found to upregulate TGR5 expression in HepG2 cells co-cultured with HG and FFA (Fig. ). To study the function of TGR5 in NaB’s positive effects on hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis, we employed TGR5 knockdown using a lentiviral vector in vitro (Fig. ). TGR5 knockdown significantly attenuated the protective effects of NaB on various hepatic injury markers. Specifically, the down-regulation of ALT, AST, ALP, and LDH levels induced by NaB was blocked in HepG2 cells co-cultured with HG and FFA (Fig. ). Additionally, TGR5 knockdown not only prevented the down-regulation of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α by NaB in the medium of co-cultured HepG2 cells, but also increased the expression of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in NaB-treated HepG2 cells (Fig. ). Moreover, TGR5 knockdown resulted in the upregulation of Collagen Ⅳ and Laminin levels, reversing the protective effect of NaB on collagen deposition in HepG2 cells co-cultured with HG and FFA (Fig. ).
The importance of TGR5 in the alleviation impact of NaB on hepatic inflammation and fibrosis was also further confirmed by using SBI-115, a specific inhibitor of TGR5. The results of CCK-8 showed that SBI-115 at 20, 40 and 60 μM concentrations had no significant effect on HepG2 cells viabilities, which was significantly decreased at 80 μM and 160 μM (Fig. ). Thus, 60 μM of SBI-115 was used in the following experiments. Compared with the group of NaB, co-incubation with SBI-115 significantly inhibited the protein levels of TGR5 in HG and FFA co-cultured HepG2 cells by IF (Fig. ). Consistent with the results of TGR5 knockdown, SBI-115 treatment blocked the improvement effect of NaB on liver function indicators, inflammatory factors, and fibrosis in HG and FFA co-cultured HepG2 cells (Fig. ). In summary, our results demonstrated that both genetic and pharmacological inhibition of TGR5 reversed the alleviating effects of NaB on hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis in vitro.
 

## NaB through TGR5 alleviated diabetic hepatitis via dampening NF-κB signaling
NaB suppressed the activation of NF-κB, a key regulator of inflammation, resulting in decreased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines . It has been reported that upregulation of TGR5 inhibited the activation of NF-κB, indicating that NF-κB signaling might be involved in the alleviation effect of NaB on hepatitis in diabetic condition , . The results demonstrated that the liver of diabetic mice had a higher phosphorylation level of NF-κB, which was reduced by NaB and CB (Fig. , Fig. ). Moreover, NaB down-regulated the increased phosphorylation level of NF-κB in HG and FFA co-stimulated HepG2 cells (Fig. ). However, the down-regulation effect of NaB on NF-κB phosphorylation levels was blocked by TGR5 knockdown in HepG2 cells co-stimulated with HG and FFA (Fig. ). Furthermore, TGR5 knockdown eliminated the inhibitory effects of NaB on the DNA binding and transcriptional activity of NF-κB in HepG2 cells co-stimulated with HG and FFA, indicating that NaB inactivated NF-κB signaling via TGR5 in vitro (Fig. ). Consistent with the results of TGR5 knockdown, SBI-115 treatment also abolished the inhibition of phosphorylation levels, the DNA binding and transcriptional activity of NF-κB by NaB (Fig. ).
 To further investigate whether NaB alleviated hepatic inflammatory injury via TGR5-medicated NF-κB signaling, TGR5 agonist INT-777, NF-κB agonist PMA and antagonist PDTC were used in the subsequent experiments. First, activating TGR5 by INT-777 down-regulated the phosphorylation levels of NF-κB, which were further increased by PMA in HG and FFA co-stimulated HepG2 cells (Fig. ). The reduced levels of inflammatory factors, Collagen Ⅳ and Laminin, as well as liver function indicators and LDH induced by INT-777 were also restored by PMA (Fig. ). Similarly, PMA counteracted the down regulation of AST, ALT, ALP and LDH induced by NaB in HepG2 cells co-stimulated with HG and FFA, but PDTC reversed the trend (Fig. ). The down-regulated phosphorylation levels of NF-κB induced by NaB were also increased by PMA, and PDTC reversed the effect (Fig. ). The alleviation effect of NaB on hepatic levels of inflammatory factors, Collagen Ⅳ and Laminin were blocked by PMA. Meanwhile, NF-κB inactivation by PDTC could also reduce the levels of inflammatory factors and fibrosis (Fig. ). In summary, these findings indicated that NaB mediated TGR5/NF-κB signaling participated in the alleviation effect of NaB against hepatic inflammatory injury in diabetic condition.
 

## NaB enhanced the interaction between IκB-α and β-arrestin2 by activating TGR5 in vitro
As an endogenous inhibitory protein of NF-κB, p-IκB-α could trigger NF-κB activation, triggering chronic inflammatory responses in metabolic-related inflammatory diseases , . Additionally, β-arrestin2 could directly bind to IκB-α, preventing its phosphorylation and degradation, thereby mitigating inflammation . Conversely, inhibition of β-arrestin2 disrupted its interaction with IκB-α, leading to increased phosphorylation and degradation of IκB-α, which exacerbated the inflammatory response . To investigate whether NaB alleviated hepatic inflammation by regulating IκB-α and β-arrestin2 through TGR5, both in vitro and in vivo studies were performed. In vitro, activation of TGR5 using INT-777 reduced the phosphorylation levels of IκB-α and increased β-arrestin2 expression in HepG2 cells co-cultured with HG and FFA (Fig. ). In vivo, CB suppressed the upregulation of phosphorylated IκB-α and the downregulation of β-arrestin2 in db/db mice’s liver (Fig. ). Similarly, NaB treatment reversed the elevated levels of phosphorylated IκB-α and restored β-arrestin2 expression in db/db mice’s liver (Fig. ). Furthermore, in HepG2 cells co-cultured with HG and FFA, NaB dose-dependently improved the phosphorylation levels of IκB-α, confirming its regulatory effect on inflammatory signaling (Fig. ).
 NaB treatment led to a decrease in IκB-α phosphorylation levels, accompanied by an increase in downregulated β-arrestin2 expression (Fig. ). However, the reduction in IκB-α phosphorylation induced by NaB was blocked in HepG2 cells co-cultured with HG and FFA when TGR5 was knocked down or when treated with the SBI-115 (Fig. ). IF analysis revealed that TGR5 knockdown or SBI-115 treatment not only reversed the NaB-induced increase in β-arrestin2 expression in HepG2 cells but also demonstrated the cytoplasmic colocalization of β-arrestin2 and IκB-α in HepG2 cells co-cultured with HG and FFA (Fig. ). To further explore whether NaB modulates the interaction between IκB-α and β-arrestin2 via TGR5, Co-IP was performed to assess their binding. The results demonstrated that NaB enhanced the formation of the IκB-α-β-arrestin2 complex in HepG2 cells co-cultured with HG and FFA, but this effect was abolished by TGR5 knockdown or SBI-115 treatment (Fig. ). These findings suggested that NaB promoted the interaction between IκB-α and β-arrestin2 through TGR5 activation in HepG2 cells co-cultured with HG and FFA, highlighting a critical mechanism by which NaB alleviates inflammation.

## Discussion
The prevalence of late fibrosis in T2DM patients with MASH was 17.02% . The mortality rate of combined disease markedly exceeded that of other common liver and non-liver-related chronic diseases. The acquisition of MASH would increase the difficulty of glycemic control, and inflammatory response of T2DM. Therefore, reducing liver inflammatory response were particularly important to delay the progression of T2DM complicated with MASH. In this study, we found that CB through maintaining intestinal SCFAs homeostasis alleviated hepatitis in T2DM via intestinal butyric acid-mediated-TGR5/NF-κB axis, highlighting intestinal SCFAs homeostasis as a considerable target for T2DM-associated hepatitis via “gut-liver” axis.
Gastrointestinal discomfort of hypoglycemic drugs promoted intestines was attracted more attention in diabetic patients. Furthermore, mounting evidence indicated the gut-liver axis’s significant role in liver injury diseases. With the enhancement of living standards and advancements in nutrition science, dietary therapy has been receiving increasing attention in the treatment of T2DM patients. As intestinal health regulators, probiotics were widely used for the treatment of gastrointestinal diseases. CB, a probiotic commonly prescribed in Asia, was the preferred product for protecting intestinal health, and exhibited beneficial effects on various disease conditions . In clinical applications, CB preparations or combined with other probiotics, and traditional Chinese medicine extracts could effectively treat many diseases, such as diarrhea, enteritis, and IBS. Previous studies showed that supplemental CB was beneficial to multiple liver diseases, including hepatic ischemia reperfusion injury, carbon tetrachloride-induced acute liver damage, and obese-related MASLD – . Latest studies had revealed that CB could improve IR and alleviate vascular inflammation in diabetic mice , . Besides, it was reported that CB mitigated steatohepatitis induced by a high-fat diet in mice through enterohepatic immunoregulation . Thus, we speculated that CB could a considerable nutritional prevention agent for T2DM-associated hepatitis by modulating gut-liver axis. Our data revealed that CB not only alleviated symptoms of diabetes, but also ameliorated the pathological process of MASH in diabetic condition (Fig. ).
CB exhibited beneficial effects via enhancing gut immune function, regulating gut microbiota homeostasis, and generating probiotic substances , . CB, a butyrate-producting potential probiotic, affected the production of SCFAs in intestinal inflammation , . SCFAs were mainly produced in the colon, and played an important role in energy supply and storage, and endocrine regulation. Intestines SCFAs homeostasis had a closely relationship with the regulation effect of CB on multiple diseases via gut-liver axis, gut-kidney axis, and gut-brain axis – . Based on these, we further examine the metabolic profiles of SCFAs in the colon content of diabetic mice, including seven kinds of SCFAs. And our results showed that CB restored intestines SCFAs homeostasis of 18 weeks-old diabetic mice, indicating that the restored intestines SCFAs homeostasis might be involved in the hepatoprotective effect of CB on T2DM-associated hepatitis (Fig. ).
Early antibiotic treatment resulted in a marked decrease in butyric acid levels, and dietary supplementation with either experimental or clinically utilized CB restored natural killer cell maturation and function impaired by early-life antibiotics via the butyrate-IL-18 axis . CB increased butyrate acid levels and activated butyrate/GPR43 pathway by modulating the gut-liver axis during Coprophagy prevention (CP) in rabbits . In this study, our analysis of intestinal SCFA metabolic profiles revealed that butyrate exhibited the most pronounced changes. Butyrate in the intestine was primarily produced by CB. This probiotic efficiently generated butyrate (constituting over 80% of its metabolites) through specific biosynthetic pathways. CB outperformed many other butyrate-producing bacterial genera in both yield and environmental tolerance – . These findings suggested that intestinal butyrate acid mediated CB’s protective effects against T2DM-associated MASH. It was further confirmed by the selective enhancement of butyrate without altering acetic and propionic acid levels in liver (Fig. , Fig. ). However, previous studies have reported that acetic acid alleviated inflammatory responses and liver injury in septic mice , and propionic acid induced hepatic damage in rats . Considering the potential roles of acetic acid and propionic acids in the progression of MASH, we evaluated the effects of sodium acetate (NaAc), sodium propionate (NaP), and NaB on AML12 cells co-cultured with HG and FFA. NaAc showed no significant effects on cell viability, inflammatory markers, or fibrotic markers, while NaP exerted modest effects inferior to those of NaB (Fig. – ). These results further supported butyrate as the key mediator of CB’s protection in T2DM-associated MASH. As a SCFA produced during bacterial fermentation of colonic dietary fiber, NaB had a variety of pharmacological activity – . NaB reduced serum triglycerides elevation caused by high-fat diet (HFD), and ameliorated tubulointerstitial inflammation in diabetic mice . A clinical study showed that NaB supplementation helped control blood glucose and lipids, and improved islet function in T2DM patients. Our experiments further confirmed that NaB treatment ameliorated MASH in diabetic mice, and demonstrated its anti-inflammatory effects on HepG2 cells co-cultured with HG and FFA (Figs. , ). Furthermore, similar protective effects were observed in non-parenchymal cells. NaB administration attenuated macrophage inflammation by reducing IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α secretion, and ameliorated hepatic stellate cells (LX-2) fibrosis by decreasing Collagen Ⅳ and Laminin expression. This confirmed its consistent anti-inflammatory/anti-fibrotic efficacy across hepatic cell types (Figs. , ). These data highlighted butyrate as the key SCFA driving hepatoprotection and underscored the need for further investigation into propionate’s role in MASH. Notably, butyrate also modulates feeding behavior through pathways involving intestinal L cell activation to stimulate GLP-1 secretion, coupled with gut-brain neural circuit-mediated regulation of hypothalamic appetite genes , . In our previous study, CB intervention can restore glucose-stimulated GLP-1 secretion in db/db mice , which may synergistically enhance the anti-inflammatory effect of liver metabolism through appetite regulation.
Studies revealed that NaB reduced liver inflammatory response by reducing macrophage infiltration , . Furthermore, NaB effectively prevented HFD-induced obesity in mice by activating GPCRs . Notably, TGR5, a member of the GPCR superfamily, played an essential role in regulating glucose tolerance and relieving inflammation in the liver , . Activation of TGR5 regulated glucose-lipid metabolism in diabetic patients . Restoration of TGR5 alleviated liver damage caused by T2DM . Recently, it has been demonstrated that NaB bound TGR5, inducing conformational changes that ultimately upregulated TGR5 gene transcription and protein expression . Thus, after establishing the protective effects of NaB against hepatic inflammatory injury in vivo and in vitro, we further studied the crucial role of hepatic TGR5 in the alleviative effect of NaB on T2DM-associated MASH. Molecular docking results demonstrated moderate binding between butyrate and TGR5, aligning with established literature on butyrate-TGR5 interactions. Moreover, TGR5 was activated in the liver of CB and NaB treated diabetic mice, and HepG2 cells co-cultured with HG and FFA, while the alleviative effect of NaB against hepatic inflammatory injury were counteracted by knocking down TGR5 in vitro (Fig. , Fig. , ). To further elucidate the indispensable role of TGR5 on NaB’s anti-inflammatory effect on MASH in diabetic condition, TGR5 specific inhibitor was used in later experiments. Previous study had showed that SBI-115, a novel antagonist of TGR5, represented a potential therapeutic approach in polycystic liver disease . Our results of SBI-115 revealed that TGR5 could not be absent from the alleviation of NaB on T2DM induced hepatitis (Fig. ). The recent compelling evidence directly establishing TGR5 as a bona fide binding target for butyrate provides a strong foundational understanding for butyrate’s diverse biological roles . Our current study significantly expands upon this by unequivocally demonstrating that butyrate exerts its inhibitory effects on the NF-κB pathway in a TGR5-dependent manner, as evidenced by our genetic knockdown and pharmacological inhibition experiments. This robust functional observation provides critical insights into a key downstream signaling pathway mediated by the butyrate-TGR5 axis.
Following the discovery of the mitigating effect of NaB against T2DM-associated MASH via TGR5 in vivo and in vitro, we further explored the molecule mechanism of NaB on T2DM-associated MASH. The transcription factor NF-κB, a key regulator of inflammation, played a crucial role in controlling inflammatory responses through its transcriptional activity . TGR5 activation inhibited the NF-κB pathway in microglia inflammatory, macrophage inflammation, hepatic inflammatory , , , . Studies of acute pancreatitis and colon cancer revealed that CB regulated NF-κB pathway, which was also inhibited by NaB in multiple inflammatory related diseases, including colitis, inflammatory bowel disease and neuronal mitophagy – . Thus, we wondered whether the anti-inflammatory effects of CB in T2DM-associated MASH occurred through butyrate-mediated suppression of hepatic NF-κB activity via TGR5 signaling. Our data demonstrated that both CB and NaB reduced the phosphorylation of NF-κB in db/db mice’s liver. NaB also inhibited the binding and transcriptional activities of NF-κB. While, genetic or pharmacological inhibition of TGR5 abolished the effect of NaB on NF-κB, confirming that NaB suppresses NF-κB signaling activation through TGR5 activation (Fig. , Figs. , ).
Based on the above findings, we were wondering if NaB reduced hepatic inflammatory injury through TGR5-mediated NF-κB activity in T2DM-associated MASH. For this regard, TGR5 agonist INT-777 and NF-κB agonist PMA were first treated to HepG2 co-stimulated with HG and FFA. The reduced levels of inflammation and hepatic enzyme activities induced by TGR5 activation were blocked by the activated NF-κB in HepG2 cells (Fig. ). Then, inactivation of NF-κB by PDTC exerted an inhibitory effect on hepatic inflammatory injury, while PMA blocked the amelioration effect of NaB on hepatic inflammatory injury via activating NF-κB, indicating that activation of TGR5 by NaB improved hepatic inflammatory injury through suppressing NF-κB signaling in T2DM-associated hepatitis (Fig. ).
Mechanistically, IκB-α was closely associated with the inflammatory response, and its expression was related to TGR5 receptor , . Proinflammatory factors could activate the inflammatory pathway by activating IκB-α phosphorylation . Inhibition of the phosphorylation of IκB-α reduced the NF-κB levels, and exert therapeutic effects on colonic inflammation in rats . β-arrestin2, a desensitizing molecule for GPCR, was able to prevent the phosphorylation and degradation of IκB-α via acting with IκB-α directly, resulting in the maintenance of its stability , . Inhibition of β-arrestin2 was able to affect its binding to IκB-α and increase its phosphorylation and degradation, aggravating the inflammatory response . In db/db mice, β2AR agonists had been shown to reduce inflammation and extracellular matrix deposition in renal and cardiac tissues by increasing β-arrestin2 expression and promoting its interaction with IκB-α . Studies indicated that NaB increased β-arrestin2 expression in high insulin induced HepG2 cells . Thus, we speculated that the regulation effect of NaB on NF-κB activation was closely related with the interaction between IκB-α and β-arrestin2 via TGR5 in T2DM-associated MASH. The cytoplasmic co-localization of β-arrestin2 and IκB-α was observed in HepG2 cells, and CB and NaB affected the protein levels of p-IκB-α and β-arrestin2 (Fig. , Fig. ). Subsequently, Co-IP was used to confirm whether NaB could affect the binding of β-arrestin2 to IκB-α. TGR5-siRNA lentivirus, TGR5 specific agonists INT-777 and antagonist SBI-115 were further used in the in vitro experiments, and our findings indicated that NaB through TGR5 activation enhanced the interaction between IκB-α and β-arrestin2 in T2DM-associated hepatitis (Fig. ).
Our study showed CB relieved T2DM-related hepatitis by enriching intestinal butyrate, which activated TGR5 to inhibit NF-κB, reducing liver injury and suggesting a gut-liver axis therapeutic target. However, there were some limitations in our research. First, we did not investigate butyrate’s effects on bile acid metabolism via TGR5, a well-known bile acid receptor, though emerging evidence confirmed butyrate affected bile acid profiles , . Critically, precisely how butyrate modulated bile acid metabolism remained incompletely characterized, and the functional consequences of butyrate-mediated bile acid modifications on MASH progression remain undefined. The butyrate-TGR5-bile acid axis thus defined a critical direction for future research. Second, T2DM involved polygenic and environmental factors, while db/db mice reflected a monogenic defect. Several studies indicated that the db/db mouse model required supplementary stimuli to accurately recapitulate T2DM-induced MASH , . Thus, reliance on a single animal model (the db/db mouse) restricted the generalizability of our findings to the complexity of T2DM complicated MASH. Follow-up studies will use diverse models to validate butyrate’s efficacy against MASH across different pathophysiological contexts. Third, the absence of clinical samples limited direct comparisons between preclinical data and human T2DM pathology. While db/db mice partially recapitulated SCFA trends, inherent genetic and physiological differences constrained their full representation of human disease dynamics. Future work would integrate human samples (stool, serum, liver tissue) to comprehensively characterize SCFA profiles, bridging preclinical-clinical findings. Fourth, we have not yet examined the effects of CB administration in TGR5 knockdown mice or gnotobiotic models. Notably, these experimental approaches were currently integrated into our ongoing studies, which aimed to comprehensively elucidate the functional role of TGR5 signaling pathways and the mechanistic impact of gut microbiota modulation by CB. Addressing these limitations will deepen the understanding of butyrate’s therapeutic potential in T2DM-induced MASH, enhancing the rigor and impact of our research towards microbiome-based therapies. Fifth, we do not directly assess the viability of CB during and after administration, which is an important aspect for probiotic function evaluation. Future studies will incorporate targeted viability assessments through standardized experimental protocols or direct detection methods.
In summary, our results revealed that CB alleviated hepatitis in T2DM. CB was found to maintain intestinal SCFAs metabolic homeostasis, and significantly enriched butyrate acid. Supplementation with NaB could reduce MASH-related symptoms and hepatic injury in diabetic mice by activating TGR5 to enhance the interaction between IκB-α and β-arrestin2, resulting in the inhibited NF-κB (Fig. ). Our study highlighted the significance of the intestinal butyrate-mediated-TGR5/NF-κB axis in the amelioration of T2DM-associated hepatitis, and suggested butyrate as a SFCA that was produced from CB, offering potential adjuvant therapeutic strategy for hepatic inflammatory injury induced by T2DM and other metabolic associated liver diseases. Our findings also indicated that modulating intestinal SCFAs metabolic homeostasis as a potential therapeutic target in T2DM-asscioated hepatitis via “gut-liver” axis.
 

## Methods

## Materials and reagents

Sodium propionate (NaP, 99%, S817368) was purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China). PDTC (BS5039) and INT-777 (BS6067) were obtained from Annorno (Beijing, China). SBI-115 (T7428) and PMA (T3147) were supplied by Topscience (Shanghai, China). FFA (a 2:1 mixture of oleic acid and palmitic acid) was supplied by Kunchuang Technology Co., Ltd (Xi’an, China).
CB capsules (S20040054), a commercially available prescription drug (marketed in China), were supplied by Chongqing Taiping Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd (Chongqing, China). Each capsule strictly meets bacterial content standards. Endospores of CB confer resistance to heat and acid, ensuring viability during passage through the digestive tract and enabling long-term (≥3 years) storage at room temperature. These properties supported its survival within the mouse colon in our experiments.
Antibodies against TNF-α (60291-1-Ig, BS6000) were acquired from Proteintech (Rosemont, IL, USA) or Bioworld (St. Louis, MO, USA). The antibody targeting IκB-α (10268-1-AP) was obtained from Proteintech (Rosemont, IL, USA). Antibodies against p-NF-κB-p65 (AF2006) and p-IκB-α (AF2002) were sourced from Affinity Biosciences (Cincinnati, OH, USA). Antibodies against Laminin (A21479) and Collagen Ⅳ(A25131) were provided by ABclonal (Wuhan, China). TGR5 antibodies (NBP2-23669SS, ab72608) were purchased from Novus Biologicals (CO, USA) or Abcam (CA, USA). The antibody targeting β-arrestin2 (sc-365445) was obtained from Abcam (CA, USA). Antibodies against IL-1β (BS3506), β-actin (AP0060), Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) Dylight 594 (BS10027), Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) Dylight 594 or 488 (BS10029, BS10017) were supplied by Bioworld (MO, USA). IRDye goat anti-rabbit IgG (926-32211) was supplied by LI-COR Biosciences (NE, USA).

## Animals and experiments
Male mice, 8 weeks old, db/db or non-diabetic littermates db/m with C57BLKS/J background, were supplied by the Nanjing University Animal Model Research Center. Mice were placed in controlled barrier environment at 24 ± 1 °C with 12 h light/dark cycle. Food and drink were freely accessible prior to and during the studies. Animal ethics was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Medical University. Food intake was monitored daily by weighing chow, with the total consumption per cage divided by the number of mice (n = 6/cage) to obtain individual equivalent values.
To evaluate the therapeutic effects of CB, Mice were split randomly into three groups: (1) the db/m group, given normal diet for 6 weeks (n = 6); (2) the db/db group, given normal diet for 6 weeks (n = 6); and (3) the db/db group administered CB via oral gavage daily for over 6 weeks (n = 6), with the dosage determined based on earlier studies . The CB used in this study originated from a prescription drug sold in China, with the CB content in a single dose strictly adhering to quality requirements. CB produces endospores, rendering it resistant to heat and acid, and thus unaffected by gastric acid and bile acids in the human digestive tract. Consequently, it avoided inactivation during passage through the gastrointestinal tract and could be stored at room temperature for over 3 years in vitro without losing efficacy. For administration, the bacterial powder from the capsules was re-suspended in 0.9% normal saline, ensuring each mouse received an oral gavage dose of 5 × 10⁷ CFU/kg. To investigate the effects of NaB on disease improvement, db/m mice were administered 0.9% normal saline intragastrically each day (n = 6). db/db mice were placed into two groups: a control group given 0.9% normal saline (n = 6) and a NaB treatment group given NaB (500 mg/kg dissolved in 0.9% normal saline, n = 6) . After 6 weeks of treatment, all mice were euthanized through carbon dioxide inhalation method for blood, colon and liver sample collection for subsequent assays. A portion of the liver and colon tissue were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis, while the remaining tissue was snap-frozen and stored at -80 °C for subsequent molecular biological analyses.

## Cell culture and transfection
HepG2 cells were supplied by Bio-Channel (Nanjing, China), and cultured as referred previously . AML12 cells was provided by Fuheng Biotechnology (Shanghai, China) and cultivated in dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium-Ham’s F-12 medium (Bio-Channel, Nanjing, China), which was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Bio-Channel, Nanjing, China), 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Biosharp, Beijing, China), 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium (Beyotime, Nanjing, China), and 200 ng/ml dexamethasone (Sigma, Saint Louis, USA). The human monocytic leukemia cell line THP-1 (Bio-Channel, Nanjing, China) was cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Bio-Channel, Nanjing, China), and 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Merck, Buchs, Germany). The LX-2 cell line was purchased from Bio-Channel (Nanjing, China). They were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin). The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Cells used in all experiments were between passages 4 and 6. To model diabetic nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, the medium was first supplemented with 60 mM D-glucose to increase the glucose concentration (HG). Subsequently, HepG2 cells were cultured in HG medium for 24 h, followed by exposure to 1 mM FFA (a 2:1 mixture of oleic acid and palmitic acid) with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for another 24 h. Next, NaB was introduced at concentrations of 1.25 mM (NaB-L), 2.5 mM (NaB-M), or 5 mM (NaB-H) for 24 h to assess its therapeutic effects. The concentrations of sodium butyrate used in this study (1.25, 2.5, 5 mM) refer to the physiological concentration of butyrate in the liver under healthy conditions and the elevated range under pathological conditions, covering physiological and pathological relevant levels , , . In addition, THP-1 cells were differentiated into macrophages by stimulation with 100 nM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) for 48 h.
To investigate the effect of NaB on AML12 cells, LX-2 cells and macrophages, AML12 cells were divided into seven groups: (1) NG group: cells cultured with NG (5.56 mM). (2) NG+NaB-H group: cells cultured with NG (5.56 mM) and the added NaB (10 mM) for 24 h. (3) HG group: cells cultured with HG (60 mM) for 24 h. (4) HG + FFA group: cells cultured with HG (60 mM) for 24 h and then exposed to FFA (1 mM) for 24 h. (5) HG + FFA+NaB-L group: cells co-stimulated with HG (60 mM) and FFA (1 mM) and then added NaB (2.5 mM) for 24 h. (6) HG + FFA+NaB-M group: cells co-stimulated with HG (60 mM) and FFA (1 mM) and then added NaB (5 mM) for 24 h. (7) HG + FFA+NaB-H group: cells co-stimulated with HG (60 mM) and FFA (1 mM) and then added NaB (10 mM) for 24 h. LX-2 cells and macrophages were also divided into seven groups, NaB was introduced at concentrations of 1.25 mM (NaB-L), 2.5 mM (NaB-M), or 5 mM (NaB-H) for 24 h after cells co-cultured with HG and FFA to assess its therapeutic effects.
To eliminate the effects of NaAc and NaP on AML12 cells, NaAc was introduced at concentrations of 5 mM for 24 h after AML12 cells co-cultured with HG and FFA, and NaP was introduced at concentrations of 1.25 mM (NaP-L), 2.5 mM (NaP-M), 5 mM (NaP-H) or 10 mM (NaB-H) for 24 h after AML12 cells co-cultured with HG and FFA to assess its therapeutic effects.
To investigate the involvement of TGR5 in NaB-mediated treatment of hepatic inflammatory injury in vitro, the TGR5 inhibitor SBI-115 or TGR5-shRNA lentivirus was used to treat HepG2 cells that had been activated with HG and FFA. And, the following were the TGR5-shRNA sequences: sense, 5’-CGTCTACTTGGCTCCCAACTT-3’; antisense, 5’-AAGTTGGGAGCCAAGTAGACG-3’. The cells were cultured in HG medium for 24 h, then receiving 1 mM FFA treatment for an additional 24 h. HepG2 cells were subsequently exposed to NaB (5 mM) and SBI-115 (60 μM) for 24 h. For TGR5 knockdown experiments, cells were transfected for 72 h with either the TGR5-shRNA lentivirus vector or the control lentivirus vector while 8 μg/ml polybrene was present. These transfected cells were then cultured in HG and FFA conditions, followed by NaB treatment and the lentiviral vectors were supplied by Obio Technology (Shanghai, China).
To explore the role of NF-κB-p65 is in treating T2DM-associated MASH with NaB, HepG2 cells co-incubated with HG and FFA were treated with PMA, an NF-κB-p65 activator, or PDTC, an NF-κB-p65 inhibitor. Following this, HepG2 cells were treated with INT-777 (TGR5 agonist, 10 μM) and PMA (100 nM) for 24 h. For experiments involving the PDTC inhibitor, HepG2 cells were first exposed to HG and FFA, followed by treatment with PDTC (20 μM).

## Assessment of biochemical parameters, liver function, and blood glucose
HepG2 and the AML 12 cells and mice liver tissues homogenates were suspended in an appropriate volume of PBS, followed by ultrasonic disruption on ice (power 300 W, 3-5 s per time, interval 30 s, repeated three times), and subsequent centrifugation; the resultant supernatants from homogenates and mouse serum samples were analyzed for alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (C009-2-1), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) (C059-2-2), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (C010-2-1) levels, with serum additionally assayed for low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL) (A113-1-1) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL) (A112-1-1) using corresponding kits from Jiancheng Biotechnology Research Institute Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China), while blood glucose levels in mice were quantified using commercially available test strips (CA, USA).

## CCK-8 assay
HepG2 cells and AML12 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 8000 cells/well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Following randomization into experimental groups, cells received designated treatments for 24 h. Cytotoxicity was assessed using a CCK-8 kit (C0005) (Biosharp, Beijing, China) according to manufacturer’s instructions. After 2-h incubation with CCK-8 reagent at 37 °C, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, Winooski, VT, USA). The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a 96-well plate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA), optical density values OD450 were proportional to viable cell counts.

## Hepatic and cellular TG, TC and LDH detection
The level of triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) was measured by previously described . The liver tissue was homogenized in saline or extraction buffer, and the cells were homogenized by PBS and subjected to ultrasonic disruption in an ice-water bath (power 300 W, 3-5 s per time, interval 30 s, repeated three times). The supernatant was centrifuged to determine the contents of TG (A110-1-1), TC (A111-1-1) and LDH (A020-2-2) using corresponding kit (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

## Oil red O staining
Staining was performed as earlier described . In brief, liver slices were first frozen and stained with Oil Red O. Then the slices were washed with 60% isopropanol and then dyed with hematoxylin. The staining results were examined using bright-field microscopy (Olympus, Japan).

## ELISA assay
The method of ELISA assay was carried out as described in our research previously , . The content of the IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in HepG2 cells and HepG2 cell culture supernatants was detected by the corresponding human immunoassay ELISA kits following the instructions (LanpaiBio, Shanghai, China) (LP-H0721, LP-H01320 and LP-H01925). Equilibrating the kit to room temperature (20−25 °C) before testing and removing the desired wells. The standard and cell culture supernatants were added in wells and diluent was added in the blank wells. Immediately and accurately adding 100 μL of HRP reagent to wells. A sticker was covered the plate and was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After incubation, washing the plate for 1 min, discarding the washing solution and patting the plate dry on absorbent paper, and repeating this operation 5 times. Adding 50 μL of substrate A and B into the reaction plate and incubating at 37 °C for 15 min, then adding 50 μL of termination solution and reading the OD value of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the cell culture supernatants at 450 nm within 15 min, and data were obtained with a Multiskan FC microplate reader.

## Histology analysis
Livers were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h prior to paraffin embedding. Serial 4 μm sections were prepared from paraffin-embedded tissues, deparaffinized, and subsequently stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE) and Masson’s trichrome. Following thorough rinsing with distilled water, sections were examined under a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
IHC analysis was carried out as earlier described . Briefly, after paraffin-embedded liver and colon slices dewaxing and hydration. Subsequently, the slices were incubated with 3% H2O2. Afterward, antigen retrieval was performed by incubating the slices with pepsin. Following antigen retrieval, the slices were blocked with 5% goat serum, and then incubated with the primary antibody of IL-6 (1:200 dilution), IL-1β (1:200 dilution), TNF-α (1:200 dilution) and TGR5 (1:200 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. Then the slices were then subjected to color development using the appropriate HRP detection kit (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China), and stain the nucleus with hematoxylin, and the stained slices were visualized under BX43F microscope (Olympus, Japan).

## Western Blotting
Western blotting was measured as earlier described . HepG2 proteins were extracted using NP40 lysis buffer, while mouse liver samples were homogenized in RIPA buffer. Protein concentrations were quantified with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After determining the protein concentration of the samples, the total protein amount was adjusted to 100 μg per group. The proteins were resolved on 8% or/and 10% SDS-PAGE gels. After electrophoresis, the gels were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane via electroblotting at an optimal current. After electroblotting, the bands were blocked with 3% BSA. The bands were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the appropriate primary antibody, including TNF-α (1:1000), IL-6 (1:1000 dilution), IL-1β (1:1000 dilution), IκB-α (1:1000), NF-κB-p65 (1:1000 dilution), p-NF-κB-p65 (1:1000 dilution), p-IκB-α (1:1000 dilution), TGR5 (1:1000 dilution) and β-actin (1:15000 dilution). After the membranes were washed with PBST three times, they were incubated with secondary antibodies (1:15000) for 1 h at room temperature, and the bands were then scanned. Finally, protein quantification was measured by Image J software to analyze the bands.

## Quantitative Real-time PCR
Total RNA was extracted from the cells through Trizol reagent, RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA. Subsequently, gene expression of β-actin and TGR5 was quantitatively analyzed by qRT-PCR using a qRT-PCR system (Roche, Switzerland). The primers of the target gene were as follows: β-actin forward: 5’-GTGAGGATGAGGAGAGCTATGA-3’, reverse: 5’-CCTTTGGCCACTTTCCTCTTAT-3’; TGR5 forward: 5’-GCUUCUUCUAAGCCUACUTT-3’, reverse: 5’-AGUAGGCUUAGAAGAAGCTT-3’.

## Immunofluorescence (IF)
Paraffin-embedded liver slices were used for IF, following the procedure described previously . Parts of the liver samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Liver tissue sections of 4 μm thickness were deparaffinized in xylene, hydrated in graded alcohol and water, and subsequently placed in 3% H2O2 for 20 min to eliminate endogenous peroxidase activity. After 30 min of pepsin antigen retrieval, the sections were washed with PBS for 3 min three times, blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, and incubated with primary antibodies against p-NF-κB-p65 (1:200 dilution), Collagen Ⅳ (1:200 dilution), Laminin (1:200 dilution), β-arrestin2 (1:200 dilution), IκB-α (1:200 dilution) and TGR5 (1:200 dilution) at 4°C overnight. Staining tissue sections were incubated with HRP-labeled secondary antibodies (1:200 dilution) for 1 h.
For IF staining of HepG2 cells, AML12 cells and LX-2 cells grown on cell slides. First, cells were seeded onto coverslips and allowed to grow to the appropriate density. Then, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 20 min. After cells were permeabilized, cells were blocked with 5% BSA. Following blocking, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody of p-NF-κB-p65 (1:200 dilution), Collagen Ⅳ (1:200 dilution), Laminin (1:200 dilution), β-arrestin2 (1:200 dilution) and TGR5 (1:200 dilution) targeting the protein. The next day, cells were incubated with a fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI, and observed under BX43F fluorescence microscope.

## Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)
First, nuclear proteins were extracted from cells. The DNA fragment of NF-κB was labeled with biotin, with the sequence 5’-AGTTGAGGGGACTTTCCCAGG-3’ (Beyotime, Haimen, China). The nuclear proteins were then incubated with the biotin-labeled NF-κB DNA probe at room temperature for 30 min to allow protein-DNA binding. The protein-DNA complexes were then loaded onto a non-denaturing PAGE for electrophoresis, the gel was then transferred to a nylon membrane, and the membrane was blocked at room temperature for 1 h. The membrane was then incubated with HRP-conjugated streptavidin antibody for 1 h. Finally, the membrane was visualized and quantified using the Tanon 4600 system (Tanon Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

## Luciferase assay
Luciferase assay was measured as earlier described . RNA silencing was carried out as outlined earlier. HepG2 cells were transfected with the pRL-TK luciferase reporter plasmid (Beyotime, Haimen, China/Genechem, Shanghai, China) in combination with either the p-TGR5-Luc plasmid (Genechem, Shanghai, China) or the p-NF-κB-Luc plasmid (Beyotime, Haimen, China) with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo, USA). Renilla luciferase activity was a control for transfection efficiency, and Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA) was used to detect luciferase activity.

## Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
HepG2 cells were harvested and lysed on ice with lysates for 30 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected. Additionally, to reduce nonspecific binding, the lysates were pre-incubated with Protein A/G agarose beads (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA) for 1 h prior to immunoprecipitation. The supernatant was then collected and incubated overnight at 4 °C with mouse IgG, IκB-α, or β-arrestin2 antibody, along with protein A/G agarose beads, under constant rotation. The next day, the beads then were washed with buffer, and SDS loading buffer was introduced to each sample for analysis by Western blotting.

## Gas chromatogram and mass spectra (GC/MS) analysis of SCFAs
First, a gas chromatography system equipped with a capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) was set with an initial temperature of 90 °C. Subsequently, the temperature was increased at a rate of 10 °C/min to 120 °C, then further increased at a rate of 5 °C/min to 150 °C. Finally, the temperature was raised to 250 °C. Helium was used as the carrier gas.
Mass spectrometry analysis was performed using GC-MS analysis in single ion monitoring mode. Specifically, 900 μL of 0.5% phosphoric acid was combined with 30 mg of the sample, after vigorous shaking, the supernatant was collected by centrifugation. The supernatant was combined with ethyl acetate, shaken vigorously again and centrifuged. After allowing it to stand, the upper organic phase was separated and combined with 4-methylvaleric acid, adjusting the final concentration to 500 μM. The prepared sample was subsequently analyzed by GC-MS.

## Targeted metabolomics analysis
Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to capture the overall changes in metabolic profiles of SCFAs in the colonic contents of the animal models for each group using SIMCA software (Umetrics, Sweden). Additionally, orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed using SIMCA to improve the separation among the three groups.

## Molecular docking
The 3D crystal structure of the target protein (TGR5) used in this study (PDB ID: 7XTQ) was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). First, all co-crystallized water molecules and non-protein ligands were manually removed from the crystal structure using PyMOL v2.5.0. Subsequently, the protein structure was pre-processed in AutoDockTools v1.5.7, which included adding Gasteiger charges, merging non-polar hydrogen atoms, and saving the protein structure in PDBQT format, as required by AutoDock Vina. For the small molecule ligands (acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid, with PubChem CIDs 176, 1032, and 264 respectively), their 2D structures were downloaded from the PubChem database. These 2D structures were converted to 3D structures and subjected to energy minimization using Chem3D v23.1.1.3. Then, Gasteiger charges were added to the ligands, and their rotatable bonds were detected using AutoDockTools v1.5.7, ultimately saving the ligands in PDBQT format.
Molecular docking calculations were performed using AutoDock Vina v1.2.7. The center coordinates of the grid box were set to x = 98.5, y = 93.028, z = 74.639. The grid box dimensions were set to 30 Å × 30 Å × 30 Å, the exhaustiveness parameter to 15, the num modes parameter to 9, and the energy range to 5 kcal/mol. After the completion of each docking task, the conformation with the lowest binding affinity, as output by AutoDock Vina, was selected as the best docking pose. Subsequently, visual analysis of the best pose was performed using PyMOL v2.5.0, focusing on hydrogen bonds between the ligand and key residues in the active pocket. Hydrogen bonds were identified based on a donor-acceptor distance of <3.5 Å and an angle range of 120°-180°.

## Statistical analysis
Experiments were performed in either triplicate or sextuplicate. For animal study, data were obtained from each group based on six mice, and the results were expressed as means ± SEM. Experimental data was analysed by GraphPad Prism (San Diego, CA, USA). Group comparisons were conducted using either independent-samples t-test or ANOVA, with LSD tests applied for post hoc analysis, and P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
