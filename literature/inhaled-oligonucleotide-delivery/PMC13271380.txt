It primarily affects individuals who are immunocompromised, particularly those with HIV-1/AIDS [ ]. Approximately 152 000 cryptococcal meningitis cases occur annually in persons with AIDS, resulting in approximately 112 000 deaths [ ]. The infection begins in the lungs following inhalation and disseminates to the central nervous system causing meningitis [ ]. Current antifungal drugs amphotericin B and fluconazole face challenges due to toxicity and the emergence of drug-resistant strains. Therefore, alternative treatment strategies, including immunotherapy, are gaining prominence as potential solutions [ ].
When C neoformans enters the lungs, it interacts with macrophages and dendritic cells (DCs), which recognize and control infection [ ], but their role in combating C neoformans remains incompletely understood. While some studies indicate that macrophages effectively kill C neoformans [ , ], others suggest that they allow intracellular fungal growth, enabling Cryptococcus to disseminate through a “Trojan horse” mechanism [ , , ]. DCs contribute to immune responses by phagocytosing and killing C neoformans and by initiating adaptive immunity [ , ].
Macrophages and DCs are heterogeneous, with subsets defined by distinct cell surface markers. Pulmonary macrophages include Ly6c+ and Ly6c− monocyte–like macrophages, alveolar macrophages, and interstitial macrophages. Conventional pulmonary DCs include CD11b+ (cDC2) and CD103+ (cDC1) [ ]. These subsets interact differently with pathogens, and considerable research has been dedicated to understanding subset-specific responses [ ]. Studies on Mycobacterium tuberculosis demonstrate how the lineage of alveolar and interstitial macrophages influences their antimicrobial activity [ ]. Additionally, C-type lectin receptor signaling in monocytes upregulates the glycolytic enzymes hexokinase and phosphofructokinase, increasing glucose uptake and glycolytic flux. This promotes production of proinflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species (ROS), critical for restricting Candida albicans proliferation and dissemination [ ]. We demonstrated that pulmonary CD11b+ DCs were permissive to fungal growth, while Ly6c− monocyte-like macrophages showed antifungal activity [ ]. RNA sequencing showed that the MHC-I H2-k1 gene was significantly upregulated in antifungal cells, while H2-k1 and Calr were significantly downregulated in permissive cells [ ]. Genes associated with metabolic pathways and cytochrome P450 enzymes were also differentially expressed, suggesting that metabolism plays a crucial role in the functional differences [ ]. Therefore, we hypothesized that these genes are integral in determining the outcome of murine phagocyte-cryptococcal interaction.

## METHODS

## Culture of Fungal Strains

C neoformans strains H99 (serotype A, mating type α), a kind gift of Dr John Perfect, Duke University, Durham, North Carolina, and mCherry-producing strain JLCN920 (serotype A, KN99 mating type α), a kind gift of Dr Jennifer Lodge, Duke University, were recovered from 15% glycerol stocks and stored at −80 °C until use. Strains were cultured on yeast peptone dextrose (BD Difco) agar and, for experiments, were cultured in yeast peptone dextrose broth for 18 hours at 30 °C in a shaking incubator. Cells were washed 3× with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and viable cell counts were determined by a hemocytometer with trypan blue exclusion. Cryptococcal cells were diluted in the required medium before each assay.

## Cell Culture
Murine macrophage cell line J774A.1 (ATCC) was cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 10% NCTC-109, 1% nonessential amino acids, 100 U/mL of penicillin, and 100 μg/mL of streptomycin at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and sterile filtered. Cells were passaged according to the manufacturer's directions.

## Mice
Male and female BALB/c mice (5–6 weeks old; Charles River Laboratories) were housed in the Oklahoma State University Animal Resources facility. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with guidelines set by Oklahoma State University's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

## Pulmonary Infection
Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and intranasally inoculated with C neoformans strain H99 at 1 × 104 colony-forming units (CFU) in 50 µL of sterile PBS [ , ] or with PBS as a control. On day 3 postinfection, mice were euthanized and lungs were aseptically harvested. Left lobes were homogenized in sterile PBS, and serial 10-fold dilutions were plated on yeast peptone dextrose agar for CFU. Right lobes were minced and digested with collagenase IV (1 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) in RPMI media (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C for 30 minutes. The digested tissue was filtered through 70- and 40-µm nylon filters to generate single-cell suspensions. Cells were washed with sterile Hank's Balanced Salt Solution, and erythrocytes were lysed with red blood cell lysis buffer. Cells were counted by a hemocytometer with trypan blue exclusion and were used for flow cytometry and single-cell energetic metabolism by profiling translational inhibition (SCENITH).

## Differentiation and Purification of Bone Marrow–Derived DCs
Bone marrow cells from female BALB/c mice (Charles River Laboratories) were differentiated into bone marrow–derived DCs (BMDCs) via granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), as previously described [ ]. Briefly, cells were washed, counted, and plated in sterile Petri dishes (Fisher Scientific; not tissue culture treated) at 2 × 106 cells/mL in complete medium (RPMI supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL of penicillin, 100 μg/mL of streptomycin, 50μM 2-β-mercaptoethanol, and 2mM L-glutamine; sterile filtered) with 20 ng/mL of murine GM-CSF (PeproTech–Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2, with half the medium replaced and supplemented with GM-CSF on days 3 and 6. Cells were harvested on day 8. DCs (loosely adherent cells) were gently washed and centrifuged at 3500 rpm (2986g) at 4 °C for 5 minutes. DCs were enriched by magnetic separation for F4/80-negative and CD11c-positive cells (Miltenyi Biotec), as previously described [ , ]. BMDCs induced by GM-CSF were >95% pure by flow cytometry.

## Knockdown of MHC-I H2-k1 Gene With siRNA
J774A.1 cells or BMDCs induced by GM-CSF were seeded at 4 × 106 cells/mL in sterile 96-well plates (not tissue culture treated). Cells were transfected with 1-pmol H2-k1 siRNA or negative siRNA (control) by Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Reagent in Opti-MEM Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated for 24, 48, and 72 hours at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Gene knockdown efficiency was validated by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and protein levels evaluated by flow cytometry.

## Quantitative Real-time PCR
Cells for quantitative real-time PCR were centrifuged at 3000 rpm (2194g) for 3 minutes at 4 °C, resuspended in TRIzol reagent (Fisher Scientific), and stored at −20 °C. RNA was isolated via the Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized with the Verso cDNA synthesis kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). MHC-I H2-k1, Calr, ARG-1, Nos2, and GAPDH expression was determined through TaqMan assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were analyzed by QuantStudio 5 (Applied Biosystems). Data were calculated per the 2−ΔΔCt method [ , ].

## Flow Cytometry
Immune cells were adjusted to 1 × 106 cells in 100 μL of FACS buffer (2% fetal bovine serum in PBS, sterile filtered). Fc block (CD16/CD32) was added for 5 minutes at 4 °C, followed by staining with surface antibodies CD45, F4/80, and MHC-I, as well as viability dye and staining for extracellular fungal organisms with calcofluor white (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For pulmonary phagocyte subsets, cells were stained with surface antibodies CD45, CD11c, F4/80, MHC-II, Ly6c, CD103, and CD11b. Cells were incubated at 4 °C for 30 minutes, followed by washing 3× with FACS buffer and fixation with 2% formaldehyde. For intracellular staining, cells were permeabilized and stained with cathepsin B and iNOS antibody for 30 minutes at room temperature. Cells were then washed 3× with permeabilization buffer. Samples were run on a NovoCyte 3000 flow cytometer (Agilent) and analyzed by NovoExpress software (Agilent).

## ROS Assay
To assess ROS production, cells were stained with CellROX reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific), a fluorogenic probe used to detect oxidative stress. Cells were incubated with the reagent at 37 °C for 30 minutes, washed 3× with PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

## Antifungal Assay
Antifungal assays were conducted with phagocytes and C neoformans as previously described [ , ]. Cells were incubated with C neoformans strain H99 at a 20:1 ratio with 1 μg/mL of anti-GXM Ab F12D2 (a kind gift of Dr Tom Kozel, University of Nevada Reno, Reno, NV) for 24 hours at 37 °C, 5% CO2. After incubation, cells were centrifuged at 3500 rpm (2986g) for 3 minutes at 4 °C and resuspended in sterile cell culture grade water (Corning) for 15 minutes at room temperature to lyse mammalian cells. Lysates were serially diluted, plated on yeast peptone dextrose agar, and incubated for 48 hours at 30 °C, followed by CFU quantification.

## Phagocytic Uptake Assay
J774A.1 cells or BMDCs induced by GM-CSF were incubated with C neoformans strain KN99 mCherry at a 20:1 ratio with 1 μg/mL of anti-GXM antibody for 2 hours at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Postincubation, cells were centrifuged at 3500 rpm (2986g) for 3 minutes at 4 °C and resuspended in FACS buffer. Flow cytometry was performed as described previously, staining extracellular C neoformans with calcofluor white and surface staining cells with antibodies for CD45, F4/80, and MHC-I. Additional intracellular staining was performed for MHC-I to quantify all cells positive for MHC-I .

## JC-1 Assay
J774A.1 cells were incubated with C neoformans strain KN99 mCherry for 2 hours at a 20:1 ratio with 1 μg/mL of anti-GXM antibody at 37 °C, 5% CO2. J774A.1 cells alone were used as a control. Postincubation, cells were centrifuged at 3500 rpm (2986g) for 3 minutes at 4 °C and resuspended in FACS buffer. MitoProbe JC-1 Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions with CCCP as a positive control to determine mitochondrial transmembrane potential, and cells were evaluated by flow cytometry.

## SCENITH Assay
SCENITH assays were performed as described with slight modifications [ ]. Briefly, cells were incubated with C neoformans strain H99 for 2 and 6 hours at a 20:1 ratio with 1 μg/mL of anti-GXM antibody at 37 °C and 5% CO2 on a 96-well plate. H99 cells alone were also included as a control for cryptococcal metabolism. Pulmonary phagocyte subsets from mock and C neoformans–infected mice were subjected to SCENITH assay. The experimental setup included 5 groups with reagents from Sigma-Aldrich. Cells were treated with 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG; 100 mM), oligomycin (1 μM), DMSO (1%), harringtonine (an inhibitor of protein translation; 2 µg/mL), or 2-DG + oligomycin. DMSO and Harringtonine are negative and positive controls, respectively. Cells were incubated for 25 minutes at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Then, puromycin (10 µg/mL) was added and incubated for 30 minutes at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Cells were washed once with cold PBS and resuspended in FACS buffer. Flow cytometry for surface staining and viability dye were performed as described earlier. After incubation, cells were washed 3× with FACS buffer and resuspended in a True-Nuclear Transcription Factor Buffer Set (BioLegend). Intranuclear staining with antipuromycin antibody (BioLegend) was performed per the manufacturer's instructions. After intranuclear staining, cells were washed 3× with permeabilization buffer and fixed with 2% formaldehyde before flow cytometry.

## Statistical Analysis
Prism version 5.0 (GraphPad Software) was used for statistical analyses. An unpaired 2-tailed t test with a 95% CI and P < .05 was used to detect significant differences between 2 groups. One-way analysis of variance with a Tukey posttest was used to detect significant differences between treatment groups in the cryptococcal inhibition assay, with significant differences defined as P < .05.

## RESULTS

## MHC-I H2-k1 Gene Is Involved in Phagocyte-Mediated Antifungal Activity
Previous studies from our laboratory demonstrated that pulmonary phagocyte subsets exhibit different antifungal responses against C neoformans. Specifically, CD11b+ DCs allow intracellular growth, whereas Ly6c− monocyte–like macrophages exhibit antifungal activity [ ]. RNA sequencing identified differential expression of antigen presentation genes MHC-I H2-k1 and calreticulin Calr. In permissive cells, H2-k1 and Calr were significantly downregulated, whereas H2-k1 was upregulated in antifungal cells [ ]. Therefore, we evaluated the role of H2-k1 in cryptococcal control. Real-time PCR confirmed RNA sequencing results in CD11b+ DCs ( ), consistent with our previous study [ ]. We then used siRNA to knock down H2-k1 expression in J774A.1 cells. Optimal knockdown (63.5%) was achieved at 72 hours ( A) and validated by reduced total MHC-I protein via flow cytometry ( B). To assess whether H2-k1 controls cryptococcal growth, we silenced H2-k1 in J774A.1 cells, followed by 24-hour incubation with cryptococcal strain H99. We observed a significant increase in CFU in siRNA-treated cells as compared with control cells ( C).
 To confirm this in primary cells, we knocked down H2-k1 in BMDCs, achieving 75% knockdown at 24 hours ( D), which was validated by reduced MHC-I protein via flow cytometry ( E). A significant increase in cryptococcal CFU was also observed in H2-k1 knockdown BMDCs as compared with controls ( F). Although we observe statistically significant differences in CFU when H2-k1 is reduced in both cell types, the killing ability is not completely abrogated, so MHC-I is likely only 1 contributing factor to the antifungal activity of these cells.

## MHC-I H2-k1 May Exert Downstream Effects on Calr Expression
We showed significant downregulation of Calr and H2-k1 within the antigen processing and presentation pathway [ ]. To investigate if H2-k1 has downstream effects, we knocked down H2-k1 and assessed Calr expression. Knockdown of H2-k1 correlated with reduced Calr expression, while cells with high expression of H2-k1 correlated with increased Calr expression ( A and B). Flow cytometry revealed reduced Calr protein following H2-K1 knockdown ( C). This supports the hypothesis that H2-k1 influences Calr expression through possible downstream effects.
 

## Increased Cryptococcal CFU Following H2-k1 Knockdown Is Independent of Phagocytic Uptake
To explore the mechanism underlying MHC-I–mediated antifungal activity in J774A.1 cells and coregulation between H2-k1 and Calr, we hypothesized that Calr may contribute to this process, given its roles in antimicrobial activity and phagocytosis [ ]. To determine if increased CFU in H2-k1 knockdown cells were due to reduced phagocytosis, we assessed C neoformans uptake. No significant differences in phagocytosis were observed as compared with controls ( A). These results suggest that increased CFU is not due to altered phagocytic uptake.
 

## Increased Cryptococcal CFU Following H2-k1 Knockdown Correlates With Reduced ROS Levels but Not With Nos2 Expression or Cathepsin B Activity
We next investigated postphagocytic mechanisms associated with antifungal activity, such as iNOS, ROS, and cathepsin B, a lysosomal serine protease that degrades C neoformans [ ]. No significant differences in cathepsin B ( B) or iNOS ( C) were observed between H2-k1 knockdown and controls. However, ROS, another critical antimicrobial mechanism within the phagolysosome, was significantly reduced in H2-k1 knockdown J774A.1 cells, suggesting that the increased fungal burden is due to impaired ROS-mediated killing ( D).

## 
C neoformans Induces Metabolic Alterations in J774A.1 Cells
Previous studies from our laboratory demonstrated significant upregulation of metabolic genes in permissive subsets [ ]. Since immune cell metabolism is tied to pathogen defense [ ], we investigated metabolic changes in J774A.1 cells following exposure to C neoformans KN99 mCherry using the JC-1 assay. Results showed that J774A.1 cells incubated with C neoformans exhibited a significant shift from red to green fluorescence, indicating a reduced mitochondrial membrane potential as compared with controls ( ), suggesting that Cryptococcus induces metabolic alterations, potentially impairing mitochondrial function. These results were unsurprising, as mitochondrial depolarization was reported following macrophage uptake of Cryptococcus [ ]. However, this metabolic shift has not previously been associated with antifungal macrophage activity.
 

## J774A.1 Cells Utilize the Glycolytic Pathway for Energy Production During Cryptococcal Infection
To further examine metabolic changes during cryptococcal interaction, we employed SCENITH, a flow cytometry–based method that quantifies cellular reliance on glycolysis or oxidative phosphorylation and assesses fatty acid and amino acid dependency [ ]. J774A.1 cells were incubated with the KN99 mCherry strain and exposed to 2-DG to inhibit glycolysis and oligomycin to block oxidative phosphorylation. Mean fluorescence intensities were measured via flow cytometry ( ), and from these, the following were calculated: glucose dependence, mitochondrial dependence, glycolytic capacity, and fatty acid and amino acid oxidation capacity. To confirm that inhibitors selectively affected host cells, we assessed cryptococcal metabolism under identical conditions and observed no significant changes ( A). At 2 hours, Cryptococcus-treated cells had significantly increased glucose dependence and reduced capacity for fatty acid and amino acid oxidation ( B). By 6 hours, glycolytic capacity increased significantly while mitochondrial dependence decreased ( C). These results indicate that in antifungal cells, C neoformans induces metabolic reprogramming, shifting energy production from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis.
 

## Cryptococcal Proliferation Is Enhanced in Glycolysis-Inhibited Cells
To validate SCENITH data, we measured CFU following glycolysis inhibition in J774A.1 cells. 2-DG treatment significantly increased CFU as compared with controls ( ), indicating enhanced cryptococcal proliferation under glycolytic inhibition. In contrast, oligomycin treatment did not increase CFU. Treatment of Cryptococcus with inhibitors did not change CFU ( ). Also, macrophages were not polarized toward the M1 or M2 phenotype following treatment with inhibitors ( ). These findings confirm our SCENITH data ( B and C). Interestingly, these data are opposite of Histoplasma growth in macrophages during 2-DG treatment, where this significantly reduced fungal CFU due to the lack of zinc import into macrophages, killing Histoplasma due to zinc starvation [ ]. Enhanced fungal growth in the presence of 2-DG suggests that host glycolysis is a key metabolic pathway utilized for phagocyte anticryptococcal activity. To determine whether ROS enhancement mediated by MHC-I H2-k1 correlated with altered metabolism, we conducted SCENITH after H2-k1 knockdown and found no significant changes ( ), suggesting that these may act through independent mechanisms.
 

## Divergent Glucose Metabolism Defines Antifungal and Permissive Phagocytic Cell Responses to C neoformans
To understand the antifungal responses of permissive and antifungal phagocyte subsets, we performed SCENITH following pulmonary infection. Because we observed sex differences [ ], we infected male and female mice with C neoformans to analyze metabolism in pulmonary CD11b+ DCs from male mice and Ly6c− monocyte–like macrophages from female mice [ ].
As anticipated, antifungal macrophages exhibited increased glucose dependence ( B), while permissive DCs showed a trend toward decreased glucose dependence; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .0521; A). Future studies are needed to address these differences observed in ex vivo studies as compared with pulmonary infection. These findings suggest that antifungal subsets shift toward glycolysis, which is a key determinant of the antifungal activity of phagocytes against C neoformans.
 

## DISCUSSION

C neoformans remains a significant threat to public health, particularly among individuals who are immunocompromised due to the limited availability of effective treatments, toxicity, and emergence of antifungal resistance [ ]. Given these constraints, the World Health Organization classified C neoformans as a fungal priority pathogen, emphasizing the urgent need for alternative therapies [ ]. Standard treatment includes antifungals amphotericin B, fluconazole, and 5-flucytosine [ ]. But with resistance issues, development of immunotherapies is essential. Our findings contribute to this effort by identifying immune-regulatory genes with potential therapeutic relevance. In agreement with our findings, studies in M tuberculosis showed that pulmonary phagocyte subsets display distinct metabolic responses, influencing pathogen control [ ]. Alveolar macrophages exhibited a more permissive response as compared with interstitial macrophages, and antimicrobial activity was linked to fatty acid metabolism and iron sequestration [ ]. Studies in macrophages containing Cryptococcus have shown metabolic changes including increased glycolysis, increased respiration, and the Warburg effect.
Given the differential expression of H2-K1 during cryptococcal-phagocyte interaction, we sought to investigate its role in anticryptococcal activity. While MHC-I is primarily associated with antigen presentation during viral infections, phagocytes cross-present fungal antigens, facilitating processing through the MHC-I pathway [ ]. Downregulation of H2-K1 in permissive subsets suggests that C neoformans may actively suppress this pathway, potentially helping in immune evasion and persistence. We showed that H2-K1–silenced cells had significantly higher cryptococcal CFU, indicating involvement in controlling C neoformans infection. Although our data were statistically significant, differences in CFU were modest, suggesting that this is only 1 facet of phagocyte antifungal activity against C neoformans. We further investigated calreticulin (Calr) within the same pathway, which was also downregulated in permissive subsets. In addition to the role of calreticulin as a chaperone in the endoplasmic reticulum [ ], recent studies have shown that Calr modulates phagocytosis and the expression of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species [ ]. Because silencing of Calr was unsuccessful, we examined its activity in H2-K1 knockdown cells. We found significantly decreased ROS in silenced cells, suggesting that increased fungal burden is due to impaired ROS-mediated killing, which is important in plasmacytoid DC anticryptococcal responses [ ].
We explored phagocyte metabolism during C neoformans infection. We show that antifungal cells use glycolysis for anticryptococcal responses. However, glycolysis was unrelated to MHC-I and ROS production, so we are currently evaluating other potentially important metabolic pathways. Metabolic reprogramming is consistent with M tuberculosis, where immune cells increase glycolytic enzymes involved in glucose uptake and downregulate oxidative phosphorylation-related pathways in infected murine lung tissue [ ]. Similarly, during Cryptococcus infection, significant activation of glycolysis was observed in rat lung cells [ ] and human CD14+ monocytes [ ]. SCENITH data in an allergic Aspergillus model also suggest that a glycolytic shift occurs following uptake of Aspergillus spores and inhibition of glycolysis reduced spore uptake, suggesting that glycolytic responses are involved in allergen sensitization [ ]. These suggest that glycolysis drives antimicrobial immune responses to pulmonary pathogens.
This shift toward glycolysis is likely driven by increased energy demand associated with the Warburg effect [ ]. Originally described in cancer cells, this metabolic reprogramming has been observed in bacterial and fungal infections, including Cryptococcus and Candida [ , , , ]. Future studies will focus on determining whether the metabolic switch observed in our study is indeed due to the Warburg effect by assessing lactate production and other key metabolic markers.
Finally, our findings suggest that additional metabolic pathways, such as the pentose phosphate pathway, may contribute to antifungal immunity. The increase in fungal burden following dual inhibition of glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation implies a compensatory role for alternative pathways. Future work will test pentose phosphate pathway inhibitors to determine their role in controlling C neoformans and identify novel metabolic targets for therapy.
