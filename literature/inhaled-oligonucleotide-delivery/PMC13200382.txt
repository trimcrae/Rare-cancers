## Background
Sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy (SICM) is a critical cardiovascular complication characterized by cardiac dysfunction and high mortality. The molecular mechanisms that underlie SICM remain elusive, and effective therapies are limited.

## Results
Here, we report a pivotal role for adenosine deaminases acting on RNA-1 (ADAR1) in modulating macrophage polarization and exosome-mediated intercellular communication, which ameliorates myocardial damage in SICM. We determined that ADAR1 overexpression in macrophages promotes an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, reduces myocardial inflammation, and inhibits cardiomyocyte apoptosis in a murine model of sepsis. Mechanistically, ADAR1 regulates the level of microRNA-122 (miR-122) in macrophage-derived exosomes. Exosomal miR-122 targets X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein (XIAP), modulating cardiomyocyte survival.

## Conclusions
Our study reveals a novel ADAR1-miR-122-XIAP axis in macrophage exosomes that protects against sepsis-induced myocardial injury, offering a potential disease modulation strategy for SICM.

## Background
Sepsis, defined as “life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection”, is the main cause of death in the emergency room and intensive care units and has high incidence and mortality rates worldwide [ ]. In 2017, approximately 49 million sepsis cases and more than 11 million sepsis-associated deaths were reported globally [ ]. Clinically, six damaged organ systems, namely, the cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, neurological, haematological, and hepatic systems, are frequently examined in patients with sepsis [ ]. The majority of patients with sepsis display aberrant cardiac function characterized by a reduced ejection fraction (EF), usually termed sepsis-induced cardiomyopathy (SICM), which is an important contributor to organ dysfunction [ ]. Therefore, it is urgent to explore the pathological mechanism and develop new intervention measures that target specific molecules [ , ].
SICM is characterized by increased inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, and cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Immune cells play vital roles in regulating tissue homeostasis following cardiac injury [ ]. As the first responders and gatekeepers for the clearance of pathogens, macrophages are the dominant immune cells in the heart [ – ] and play a central role in SICM [ ]. Recent studies have shown that macrophages are activated and polarized (M1 type) in sepsis, whereupon they release cytokines and exosomes, which induce cardiomyocyte apoptosis [ , ]. Disease modulation using antiapoptotic strategies, such as modulating the Bcl2 family, helps to improve the prognosis and reduce mortality in patients with sepsis [ ]. However, the specific molecular regulators and mechanisms that govern the secretion of exosomes by macrophages and their cytokine content remain poorly understood. Adenosine deaminase acting on RNA-1 (ADAR1) may play a pivotal role in this process, as it not only influences the biogenesis of microRNAs (miRNAs) but also potentially modulates the cytokine profile within exosomes, thereby impacting cardiomyocyte apoptosis.
ADAR1 has been shown to regulate the biosynthesis of miRNAs [ ], which are abundantly expressed in macrophages and play important roles in mediating cardiac contraction, cell differentiation, and inflammation [ , ]. Additionally, ADAR1 can modify the precursors of miRNAs as well as the mature miRNA sequence during the production of microRNAs, which suppresses the expression of downstream target genes [ ]. Moreover, it has been reported that ADAR1 knockout leads to an embryonic lethal phenotype because of significant apoptosis [ , ]. ADAR1 deficiency in cardiomyocytes leads to autoimmune myocarditis, which further develops into dilated cardiomyopathy and heart failure.
Our previous study demonstrated that ADAR1 regulated macrophage polarization by binding to miR-21 precursors and editing miR-21 biogenesis [ ] and that it inhibited sepsis-induced inflammation and intestinal injury by editing miR-30a to further reduce organ damage [ ]. In addition, miRNAs edited by ADAR1 can be transmitted between adjacent or remote cells through extracellular vesicles, increasing the heterogeneity and diffusion of hepatocellular carcinoma [ ]. Given that patients with SICM have a high mortality rate and that targeting cardiomyocyte apoptosis would be useful for reversing SICM, we explored the underlying mechanism through which ADAR1 modulates cardiomyocyte apoptosis through clinical samples, animal models, and in vitro experiments to explore new interference targets to alleviate SICM.

## Materials and methods

## Study participants
This observational cohort study was conducted between January 2024 and June 2025. A total of 176 individuals were enrolled, all of whom were recruited from the Emergency Department of Xijing Hospital. Among them, 116 patients with sepsis were categorized according to the Third International Consensus Definitions for Sepsis and Septic Shock (Sepsis-3) [ ]. Additionally, 60 healthy individuals who underwent routine health examinations at our institution during the same period were included as a control group. The eligibility criteria for patients were as follows: (1) age 18 years or older; (2) fulfilment of the Sepsis-3 diagnostic criteria; and (3) willingness to provide informed consent. The exclusion criteria were (1) hospital discharge or mortality within the first 24 h of admission; (2) history of haematologic diseases, malignant tumours, or immunodeficiency; (3) diagnosis of autoimmune disorders; (4) severe renal or hepatic insufficiency; (5) concurrent active infectious diseases unrelated to the presenting sepsis episode; and (6) insufficient clinical data.
The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of the Fourth Military Medical University. Written informed consent was obtained from every participant or their legally authorized representative.

## Clinical data acquisition
Detailed demographic and clinical information was gathered for all participants. Baseline demographics, such as age and sex, were recorded. A standardized panel of clinical parameters was obtained upon hospital admission and before primary treatment began. This panel included vital signs (body temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, and mean arterial pressure), essential laboratory results (white blood cell count, platelet count, haemoglobin, serum creatinine, blood lactate, interleukin-6, and procalcitonin concentrations), and the identified primary focus of infection. Disease severity was assessed using the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score, where higher values indicate more critical illness and a worse prognosis. The application of mechanical ventilation and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) during the treatment course was also documented.

## Mice
Eight-week-old (18–22 g) male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from the Animal Center of Fourth Military Medical University (Xi’an). These animals were housed in groups of 5 to 6 per cage in a temperature-controlled facility at 22 °C, with a relative humidity of 45%-55% and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. They were given free access to food and water and allowed to acclimate for at least one week before being used to establish the sepsis model. At the end of the study, the mice were euthanized by the inhalation of 5% isoflurane followed by cervical dislocation. All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the experimental animal care guidelines of Fourth Military Medical University, and the experimental protocol was approved by the Welfare and Ethics Committee of Fourth Military Medical University.

## Mouse sepsis-induced myocardial injury model
The mice were randomly divided into four groups (PBS, LPS, OE-NC + LPS, and OE-ADAR1 + LPS; n = 6 per group) using a random number table method to ensure randomization, which is a key measure to reduce experimental bias. To establish the sepsis model, mice were intraperitoneally injected with 10 mg/kg LPS (DY40109, Deeyeebio), and the control group was injected with an equal volume of sterile PBS (G4202, Servicebio). For the intervention groups, mice were first administered 108~109 PFU of adenoviral vectors carrying OE-NC/OE-ADAR1 (GenePharma) via tail vein injection using a tail vein injector (GEGO-Q9G; Globalebio), followed by intraperitoneal LPS injection 48 h later. Blinding was strictly implemented throughout the experiment: the operators responsible for mouse injection, feeding and sample collection, as well as the outcome assessors who analysed the cardiac tissue samples and experimental results, were all blinded to the group assignments of the mice to prevent subjective bias. Mouse cardiac tissues were isolated 24 h after LPS stimulation for subsequent experiments.

## Echocardiography
Mouse left ventricular systolic and diastolic functions were assessed by echocardiographic examination. Six mice from each group were examined using a Vevo 3100 echocardiography instrument (Visual Sonics, Canada). Standard echocardiographic parameters, including the left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD), left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD), fractional shortening (FS), ejection fraction (EF), and mitral valve flow velocity spectrum (E/A), were recorded. EF (%) was calculated as (LVEDV-LVESV)/LVESV × 100%; FS (%) was calculated as (LVEDD-LVESD)/LVESD × 100%.

## Pathological staining
Pathological changes in the cardiac tissues of septic mice 24 h after LPS induction were observed through H&E staining. All the mice were euthanized by 5% isoflurane inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, after which the chest was opened, and the cardiac tissue was removed. The excised cardiac tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and then cut into 5 μm thick sections. The sections were subsequently deparaffinized with xylene and dehydrated via an alcohol gradient, followed by staining with haematoxylin and eosin. Finally, the sections were imaged under an optical microscope. Cardiomyocyte apoptosis was detected by TUNEL staining. After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, the cardiac tissue sections were incubated with TdT enzyme (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C for 60 min. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labelled streptavidin was subsequently added and allowed to interact for 30 min at 37 °C. Following 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and haematoxylin staining for 10 min, apoptotic cells appeared as brown granules.

## Immunofluorescence
Briefly, paraffin-embedded sample sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, subjected to antigen retrieval, and blocked before being incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C in a humid chamber. This was followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibody, HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (#GB23303; Servicebio), at room temperature for 50 min. Subsequently, Cy3 (#G1223; Servicebio) was added to the slides, which were subsequently incubated in the dark for 10 min for tyramide signal amplification (TSA). After heating, another primary antibody was added, and the corresponding secondary antibodies were Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (#GB21303, Servicebio) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (#GB23301, Servicebio). Following antibody incubation, TSA and nuclear counterstaining were performed using FITC-tyramide (#G1222, Servicebio) and DAPI, respectively. The primary antibodies used included anti-α-SMA (#ab5694; Abcam), anti-CD31 (#ab182981; Abcam), anti-WGA (#L4895; Sigma‒Aldrich), anti-F4/80 (#GB113373; Servicebio), anti-CD86 (#GB13585; Servicebio), and anti-CD206 (#GB113497; Servicebio).

## Biochemical detection
Myocardial injury in mice was assessed through plasma cardiac enzyme profile analysis. Mouse orbital blood was collected in anticoagulant-free EP tubes, left to stand at 4 °C for 20 min, and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was divided into aliquots, which were placed in EP tubes and stored at -80 °C, avoiding repeated freeze‒thaw cycles. After dilution, the serum was analysed using a veterinary biochemical analyser (BS-240VET) to detect cardiac enzyme profile markers, including lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase-MB isoenzyme (CK-MB), and α-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase (α-HBDH).

## ELISA
Cytokine levels in the myocardial tissue of septic mice were detected by ELISA. The tissues were rinsed with precooled physiological saline to remove residual blood and other substances and were then weighed and minced. The minced tissue was homogenized with a corresponding volume of homogenization medium (generally prepared as a 10% homogenate at a 1:9 weight-to-volume ratio). To further lyse tissue cells, the homogenate was subjected to ultrasonic disruption. Finally, the homogenate was centrifuged at 3000–3500 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected in EP tubes. Cardiac tissue cytokine content was measured using a Mouse IL-1β ELISA Kit (GEM0002-1; Servicebio), Mouse TNF-α ELISA Kit (GEM0004-1; Servicebio), Mouse IL-10 ELISA Kit (GEM0003-1; Servicebio), and Mouse TGF-β1 ELISA Kit (EK981-96; Multisciences) according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

## Cell culture
The mouse monocyte‒macrophage line RAW 264.7 was purchased from the Cell Culture Center of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences. The mouse cardiomyocyte cell line HL-1 was obtained from Changsha Abiowell Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (AW-CNM080, Abiowell). Both cell lines were maintained in complete culture medium supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA; Cat. No. 10099-141), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Beyotime Biotechnology, China; Cat. No. C0222) in high-glucose DMEM (Corning, USA; Cat. No. 10-013-CV), with the medium pH adjusted to 7.2 ~ 7.4. The cells were cultured at 37 °C in an environment of 95% air and 5% CO2 with saturated humidity (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; Model 3111), and cell morphology and growth status were observed daily under an inverted microscope. When the cell confluency reached 80%~90%, 0.25% trypsin-EDTA digestion solution (Invitrogen, USA; Cat. No. 25200-056) was used for digestion, and the cells were passaged at a ratio of 1:3 ~ 1:5. The medium was changed every 2 ~ 3 days, and the cells used in all the experiments were in the logarithmic growth phase.

## Cell models
To investigate the effects of macrophage (RAW 264.7) secretions on cardiomyocytes under inflammatory conditions, cardiomyocytes were cultured in conditioned medium obtained from macrophages stimulated with 1 µg/mL LPS for 12 h. To explore the role of macrophage-derived exosomes in conditioned medium, we inhibited exosome release from macrophages by adding 20 µM GW4869 (HY-19363; MedChemExpress) 2 h before LPS stimulation.
To examine the impact of ADAR1 on exosomes derived from LPS-stimulated macrophages, RAW 264.7 cells were transfected with 0.1 nM siNC/siADAR1 (RiboBio) or 5 × 106 PFU/mL OE-NC/OE-ADAR1 (GenePharma) for 48 h. The cells were then stimulated with 1 µg/mL LPS for 12 h. The exosomes secreted by the macrophages were isolated and cocultured with HL-1 cells for 12 h, after which the HL-1 cells were collected to assess apoptosis.

## Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity assay
The quantity of exosomes released into the supernatant was determined by measuring the AChE activity of the isolated exosomes. Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) is a characteristic enzyme enriched in exosomes, and its activity is widely used as a semiquantitative indicator to evaluate exosome secretion. Reagents were prepared according to the instructions of the Acetylcholinesterase Activity Assay Kit (BC2025, Solarbio), and the AChE activity in the macrophage culture supernatant was measured to evaluate exosome release.

## Exosome isolation and identification
Exosomes derived from RAW264.7 cells were extracted using an Exosome Isolation Kit (for cell culture medium) (UR52111; Umibio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The collected exosomes were stained with 1% phosphotungstic acid for 1–2 min at room temperature and observed under a transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEM-1400FLASH) to examine their morphology. Size and concentration were determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis (PMX120, Particle Metrix). CD63 and TSG101 were used as protein markers for exosomes.

## Exosome uptake
Exosomes from RAW264.7 cells were labelled using a fluorescence kit (UR52302; Umibio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, the PKH26-labelled exosomes were resuspended in complete culture medium at a concentration of 5 µg/mL and added to fluorescence microscopy culture dishes containing HL-1 cells precultured for 24 h. After 12 h of coculture, the medium was removed, and the HL-1 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100, and stained with DAPI (G1012-100ML; Servicebio). Observations were performed using a fluorescence microscope (Life Technology).

## Flow cytometry
Flow cytometry was used to detect M1- and M2-polarized macrophages. Briefly, RAW 264.7 cells were resuspended in flow buffer and incubated with antibodies against CD11c (11-0114-82, eBioscience) and CD206 (12-2061-80, eBioscience) at 4 °C in the dark for 30 min. Analysis was performed using a flow cytometer (SONY, China) and NovoExpress software. To assess cardiomyocyte apoptosis following exosome incubation, HL-1 cells were analysed using Annexin V/7-AAD (40311ES20; Yeasen). In accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, 5 µL of Annexin V and 5 µL of 7-AAD were added to 100 µL of HL-1 cell suspension and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Subsequently, 400 µL of binding buffer was added for flow cytometric analysis.

## miRNA exosome loading
NC mimic (negative control mimic with scrambled sequence), miR-122-5p mimic, NC inhibitor (negative control inhibitor with scrambled sequence and no homology to known miRNAs), and miR-122-5p inhibitor were synthesized by Beijing Qingke Biotechnology. An exosome loading kit was purchased from Echobiotech and used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. miRNAs were loaded into resting macrophage-derived exosomes under the action of exosome transporter peptide (ETP) at 37 °C in the dark with shaking at 150 rpm for 2 h. The packaged exosomes were then obtained by centrifugation. Loaded exosomes were cocultured with HL-1 cells for 12 h to assess cardiomyocyte apoptosis.

## Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
Purified exosomes were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and dropped onto a carbon-coated copper grid. After air-drying, the samples were negatively stained with 2% (w/v) uranyl acetate for 1 min and observed under a Hitachi H-7650 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) operated at 120 kV. Images were captured at magnifications of 50,000–100,000×.

## Zeta potential measurement
The surface zeta potential of the exosomes was measured using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK) or ZetaView PMX 110 (Particle Metrix, Meerbusch, Germany). The samples were diluted with particle-free PBS (pH 7.4) and equilibrated at 25 °C for 2 min before measurement. Each sample was tested at least three times.

## Particle size and concentration analysis
The size distribution and concentration of the exosomes were determined by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) using a ZetaView PMX 110 instrument (Particle Metrix, Meerbusch, Germany). Exosome samples were diluted with particle-free PBS (pH 7.4) to a concentration of 10–10⁹ particles/mL. The data were recorded and analysed using ZetaView Software 8.04.02. Each sample was measured in triplicate.

## TdT-mediated dUTP-biotin nick end labelling (TUNEL) assay
HL-1 cells were grown in 12-well plates on poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips. After stimulation as described above for 12 h, the HL-1 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (G4202; Servicebio) at room temperature for 1 h. After being washed, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Merck) and 0.1% sodium citrate on ice for 2 min. Cell labelling was performed using an in situ cell death detection kit (Roche Group). After being washed, the cells were incubated with DAPI (G1012-100ML; Servicebio) at room temperature for 10 min to stain all the HL-1 cell nuclei. Scanning was performed using a digital slide scanner (Pannoramic 250, 3DHISTECH, Hungary), and data analysis was conducted using ImageJ software.

## JC-1
To determine the mitochondrial membrane potential, a JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential detection kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was used. HL-1 cells were seeded on sterile coverslips, cultured to the logarithmic phase, washed twice with prewarmed 1× PBS (Solarbio, Cat. No. P1010), and incubated with 1 mL of freshly prepared JC-1 staining working solution at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in the dark for 20 min. After staining, the cells were washed twice with 1× JC-1 staining buffer, incubated with DAPI solution (G1012-100ML; Servicebio, China) at room temperature in the dark for 10 min for nuclear staining, mounted with anti-fluorescence quenching medium, and observed under a fluorescence microscope (red fluorescence for normal mitochondrial membrane potential, green fluorescence for decreased potential).

## RT‒qPCR analysis
To measure mRNA expression, total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent, and the RNA concentration was determined. RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using a cDNA Synthesis SuperMix kit (11141ES10; Yeasen), followed by qPCR using SYBR Green (11201ES08; Yeasen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.
For miRNA expression measurement, a miRNA 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix kit (11148ES10; Yeasen) was used for reverse transcription to generate cDNA, which was followed by qPCRs using miRNA Universal qPCR SYBR Master Mix (11171ES03; Yeasen). GAPDH was used to normalize mRNA expression, whereas U6 was used to normalize miRNA expression. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCT method. All the primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

## Western blot analysis
Total protein was extracted from RAW264.7 and HL-1 cells using RIPA lysis buffer (E121, Genestar), and the protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit (E162, Genestar). Approximately 20 µg of protein was subjected to SDS‒PAGE and then transferred to PVDF membranes (0.45 μm), which were blocked with rapid blocking solution for 15 min. Subsequently, the PVDF membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the secondary antibody goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (HRP) (1:10000; ab205719; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at room temperature for 1 h. Observations were performed using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, China). The primary antibodies used includedβ-actin antibody (#3700; Cell Signaling Technology), cleaved-caspase3 antibody (1:1000; AF7022, Affibiotech), caspase3 antibody (1:1000; #19677; Proteintech), XIAP antibody (1:1000; #2042; Cell Signaling Technology), CD63 antibody (1:1000; #AF5117; Affibiotech), TSG101 antibody (1:1000; #72312; Cell Signaling Technology), and ADAR1 antibody (#SC-73408; Santa Cruz).

## RNP-IP
To verify the regulatory effect of ADAR1 on miR-122, RNP complex immunoprecipitation (IP) was performed. For each sample, 2 × 107 logarithmic phase cells were collected, washed with precooled 1× PBS (Solarbio, Cat. No. P1010), and lysed in RNP-IP lysis buffer (Millipore, Cat. No. 17–500) supplemented with protease inhibitor and RNase inhibitor (ice incubation for 30 min). After centrifugation (4 °C, 12,000 rpm, 15 min), 10 µL of the supernatant was reserved as the input sample. The remaining supernatant was incubated with 5 µg of ADAR1 antibody (#SC-73408, Santa Cruz) or anti-IgG antibody (#2729, CST, negative control) at room temperature for 4 h, followed by the addition of prewashed protein A/G agarose beads and incubation for another 2 h. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation (4 °C, 3,000 rpm, 5 min), washed 4 times with RNP-IP washing buffer, and coprecipitated. The coprecipitated RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent. PremiR-122 expression in the extracted RNA was measured by RT‒qPCR with the input sample as an internal reference to verify the regulatory effect of ADAR1 on miR-122.

## MicroRNA sequencing
Cell supernatant from RAW264.7 cells was collected after appropriate culture, and exosomes were isolated using an exosome isolation kit specifically designed for cell culture medium (Cat. No. UR52111; Umibio, China) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions to ensure the integrity and purity of the isolated exosomes. After isolation, the exosome pellets were gently resuspended in precooled 1× PBS (pH 7.4) to avoid damaging the exosome structure, and the resuspended samples were carefully transferred to sterile tubes and sent to Guangzhou Gidio Biotechnology Co., Ltd., for basic microRNA sequencing and related bioinformatics analyses. For miRNA sequencing, the total RNA was first extracted from exosome samples, and its purity and integrity were verified. The quality of the RNA was then determined for small RNA library construction, followed by high-throughput sequencing. The basic bioinformatics analyses included the identification of known miRNAs by alignment with the miRBase database (v22.0), prediction of novel miRNAs using the miREvo and miRDeep2 tools, screening of differentially expressed miRNAs with DESeq2 software (screening criteria: |log2-fold change|≥1 and P < 0.05), prediction of target genes of differentially expressed miRNAs by integrating the TargetScan, PicTar and miRanda databases, and Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses of target genes using ClusterProfiler software to explore the potential biological roles of the identified miRNAs.

## Dual-luciferase reporter assay
To explore the regulatory relationship between miR-122 and XIAP, we constructed wild-type and mutant psiCHECK-2 dual-luciferase reporter vectors for XIAP (Abiowell, Changsha, China). 293T cells were cotransfected with Lipofectamine-3000 and the miR-122 mimic/NC mimic. Dual-luciferase reporter gene detection was performed 48 h after transfection, following the instructions of the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Gene Detection Kit (11402ES60; Yeasen).

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses of the data are presented as the mean ± SEM from three independent experiments and were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Student’s t test was used to compare pairs of groups, and analysis of variance was used to compare sets of four groups. P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Potential binding between ADAR1 and pre-miR-122 was predicted using the RPISeq database (http://pridb.gdcb.iastate.edu/RPISeq/) with the SVM algorithm.

## Results

## ADAR1 is related to septic myocardial injury
To elucidate the relationship between ADAR1 expression and septic myocardial injury, we first measured ADAR1 expression in the PBMCs of sepsis patients. As shown in Fig.  A, the expression of ADAR1 was significantly lower in PBMCs from sepsis patients than in those from healthy controls. In addition, correlation analysis revealed that ADAR1 expression is negatively correlated with SOFA and APACHE II scores, which indicated an association between low ADAR1 expression and more severe sepsis (Fig.  B). Furthermore, ADAR1 levels were significantly negatively correlated with the expression of markers of myocardial injury, such as BNP, Hs-cTnl, and Mb (Fig.  B), suggesting that more pronounced cardiac damage occurred in patients with lower ADAR1 expression. To validate this, a murine septic myocardial injury model was established via LPS, and H&E, Masson and ADAR staining was performed to observe myocardial damage. As shown in Fig.  C, septic mice displayed significant myocardial injury, including oedema and inflammatory infiltration, while immunohistochemistry revealed significant downregulation of ADAR1 expression in heart tissue (Fig.  C). Immunofluorescence double staining revealed an increase in macrophages in the septic myocardium but decreased ADAR1 expression in macrophages (Fig.  D). In vitro, LPS stimulation of macrophages significantly reduced ADAR1 expression at 24 h (Fig.  E&F). Coculture of these macrophages with cardiomyocytes induced cardiomyocyte damage (Fig.  G&H). Hence, it is reasonable to hypothesize that LPS-induced ADAR1 downregulation leads to uncontrolled macrophage inflammation and subsequent cardiac injury. The expression of ADAR1 in macrophages was subsequently regulated by adenoviral overexpression or silencing (Fig.  I). Inflammatory cytokine expression was attenuated by ADAR1 overexpression but increased by ADAR1 inhibition (Fig.  J). As shown in Fig.  K, the downregulation of ADAR1 increased the proportion of CD11c-positive M1 macrophages, whereas the upregulation of ADAR1 increased the number of CD206-positive M2 macrophages. Critically, in coculture experiments, ADAR1 overexpression markedly alleviated cardiomyocyte injury in macrophages, whereas ADAR1 knockdown exacerbated it (Fig.  L). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ADAR1 expression is closely associated with macrophage polarization, inflammatory response, and cardiomyocyte injury in sepsis. 

## ADAR1 overexpression alleviates sepsis-induced myocardial injury and apoptosis
To explore the role of ADAR1 in sepsis-induced myocardial injury, we overexpressed ADAR1 in mice by the intravenous injection of an adenoviral vector carrying OE-ADAR1, followed by the establishment of an LPS-induced sepsis mouse model (Fig.  A). As shown in Fig.  B, C, echocardiography was used to assess the systolic and diastolic functions of the mouse hearts. Septic mice exhibited significant decreases in the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), fractional shortening (LVFS), and the E/A ratio of pulsed-wave Doppler mitral and tricuspid flow spectra, indicating both systolic and diastolic dysfunction. However, mice overexpressing ADAR1 showed marked improvement in cardiac function. Further observation through pathological staining revealed that H&E staining of septic mouse hearts revealed disordered myocardial structure, interstitial oedema, and cellular vacuolization. TUNEL staining demonstrated extensive cardiomyocyte apoptosis in septic mice. However, septic mice treated with ADAR1 overexpression exhibited improved myocardial structural integrity and significantly reduced apoptosis (Fig.  D, E). By examining the serum cardiac enzyme profiles of mice in different treatment groups, we found that during sepsis, the serum levels of myocardial injury markers, including lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), and α-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase (α-HBDH), were significantly elevated. These elevated markers were alleviated in mice overexpressing ADAR1. Mouse hearts were collected and processed into 10% tissue homogenates. ELISA of the homogenates revealed that during sepsis, the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α increased significantly, whereas the anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β1 markedly decreased. Septic mice treated with ADAR1 presented a reduction in proinflammatory cytokines and an increase in anti-inflammatory cytokines (Fig.  F–H). Further RT‒qPCR analysis of cytokine expression in mouse myocardial tissue revealed that septic mice exhibited elevated levels of both proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines in the myocardium. After ADAR1 overexpression, proinflammatory cytokines were significantly reduced, while the M1 macrophage marker Nos2 was notably decreased, and the M2 macrophage marker Arg1 was significantly increased. 

## Alleviation of myocardial injury in ADAR1-overexpressing septic mice due to the regulation of macrophages
As shown in Fig. S1, immunofluorescence costaining was performed using markers for cardiomyocyte membranes (WGA), endothelial cells (CD31), myofibroblasts (α-SMA), and macrophages (F4/80), along with ADAR1. As shown in Fig. S1, there was no correlation between the expression of ADAR1 and WGA, CD31 or α-SMA, whereas ADAR1 was expressed primarily in macrophages (Fig.  A). Further immunofluorescence staining of macrophages revealed that the proportion of F4/80+ cells increased significantly after sepsis induction, whereas ADAR1 overexpression led to a decrease in the number of macrophages (Fig.  B). Moreover, in the myocardial tissue of septic mice, there was a marked increase in F4/80+CD11c+ cells (M1 macrophage markers) and a significant decrease in F4/80 + CD206+ cells (M2 macrophage markers). Conversely, septic mice overexpressing ADAR1 exhibited the opposite trend (Fig.  B, C). These findings suggest that ADAR1 overexpression inhibits the differentiation of myocardial macrophages towards the M1 phenotype, alleviates cardiac dysfunction in septic mice, and mitigates the inflammatory response, apoptosis, and myocardial injury during sepsis. 

## Regulatory role and mechanism of exosomes in LPS/sepsis-associated cellular injury
Growing evidence indicates that exosomes are critical mediators that regulate targeted cells in multiple diseases. We therefore analysed plasma exosome levels in patients with septic myocardial injury and healthy controls. The results revealed a significant increase in the plasma exosome concentration in sepsis patients, which was approximately 7-fold greater than that in healthy controls (Fig.  A). However, electron microscopy revealed no marked morphological differences between the groups (Fig.  B). Furthermore, we harvested macrophage-derived NC-EVs and LPS-EVs and analysed them using TEM, NTA and Western blotting. The particles were approximately spherical in shape, with sizes ranging from approximately 50 nm to 120 nm. NTA revealed that the average particle sizes of the NC-EVs and LPS-EVs were 78.5 nm and 75.6 nm, respectively (Fig.  C). To verify that the isolated macrophage extracellular particles were indeed exosomes, we analysed the harvested NC-EVs and LPS-EVs using TEM (Fig.  D). The expression of the exosome markers CD63 and TSG101 was specifically enriched in the cellular exosome fraction, whereas no visible β-actin band was detected in the exosome preparations, indicating minimal contamination by cellular debris or nonvesicular proteins (Fig.  E). The harvested particles exhibited typical exosome morphology and size, confirming our successful isolation of NC-EVs and LPS-EVs. We then examined the internalization of NC-EVs and LPS-EVs by HL-1 cells. In the merged images, PKH26-labelled exosomes (red fluorescence) were taken up by HL-1 cells and colocalized with DAPI-stained nuclei (blue fluorescence). More LPS-EVs than NC-EVs were taken up by cardiomyocytes (Fig.  F). As shown in Fig. S2A, to verify whether crosstalk occurred between macrophages and cardiomyocytes, we cultured cardiomyocytes in conditioned medium from cultured macrophages and examined their status. By measuring AChE activity in the culture supernatant of macrophages, we assessed the relative amounts of extracellular vesicles released by these cells. We found that GW4869, a cell-permeable noncompetitive inhibitor of neutral sphingomyelinase (nSMase), significantly suppressed extracellular vesicle release from LPS-stimulated macrophages (Fig. S2B). When cardiomyocytes (HL-1) were cultured in conditioned medium from these cells, we observed extensive cardiomyocyte apoptosis in the LPS group, whereas GW4869 + LPS inhibited cardiomyocyte apoptosis (Fig. S2C). These findings suggest that extracellular vesicles from macrophages in an inflammatory state can promote cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Exosomes isolated from macrophages under different ADAR1 expression conditions were subsequently cocultured with cardiomyocytes to assess cardiac injury (Fig.  G). Western blotting revealed that when ADAR1 was overexpressed in macrophages, the expression of apoptosis-related proteins in cardiomyocytes was significantly reduced (Fig.  H). Conversely, the inhibition of ADAR1 expression in macrophages led to increased levels of these apoptotic markers in cardiomyocytes, indicating that ADAR1 modulates exosome-mediated effects on cardiomyocyte survival. MMP detection also revealed that mitochondrial injury significantly increased when ADAR1 expression was silenced, whereas mitochondrial injury significantly decreased when ADAR1 was overexpressed (Fig.  I). Collectively, the above results indicate that the number of exosomes increases during sepsis, which leads to cardiomyocyte injury. 

## Role of macrophage-derived exosomal miR-122 mediated by ADAR1 in macrophage–cardiomyocyte communication and apoptosis regulation
MiRNAs are the most abundant and important components of exosomes and can modulate target cell function by binding to the 3’UTR of specific mRNAs. Hence, we first performed bioinformatics analysis of the public dataset GSE1166829 to investigate exosomal miRNA expression profiles in the peripheral circulation during sepsis. As shown in Fig.  A, the expression of as many as 68 miRNAs was upregulated in serum exosomes from CLP-induced septic mice compared with those from sham-operated controls. 
To subsequently explore the effect of ADAR1 overexpression on exosomal miRNA expression in inflammatory macrophages, we established an in vitro endotoxemia model using LPS-stimulated macrophages and treated them with ADAR1 overexpression intervention (OE-A + LPS group). We detected 203 known miRNAs in the LPS-stimulated and OE-A + LPS macrophage-derived exosomes, of which 105 were upregulated and 33 were downregulated (Fig. B&C). As shown in the heatmap in Fig. S3A, the top 27 miRNAs with significantly differential expression and relatively high abundance are displayed. After OE-ADAR1, the downregulated miRNAs in macrophages included miR-191-5p, miR-99a-5p, and miR-122-5p, whereas the upregulated miRNAs included miR-10b-5p, miR-143-3p, and miR-199a-3p. According to the results of the GO enrichment analysis, the miRNAs in macrophage exosomes are involved in signal transduction, cell communication, and various cellular biological processes (Fig. S3B). Furthermore, GO analysis indicated that macrophage-derived exosomes primarily regulate cellular processes, biological regulation, metabolic processes, and the response to stimuli among biological processes and mainly regulate catalytic activity, binding activity, and molecular transduction activity among molecular functions (Fig. S3C). Further KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the genes affected by these miRNAs revealed enrichment in cellular signal transduction processes, the cAMP signalling pathway, and the regulation of TRP channels by inflammatory mediators (Fig. S3D). Additionally, the main functional areas of these signalling pathways include metabolism, human diseases, organismal systems, genetic information processing, cellular processes, and environmental information processing (Fig. S3E). Next, we found that the expression of 5 miRNAs was upregulated in septic mice but downregulated in OE-A + LPS macrophage-derived exosomes. A review of the literature revealed that miR-122 promotes apoptosis, inflammation, fibrosis, pathological hypertrophy, and remodelling in the cardiovascular system. It has also been shown to reduce LVEF, LVFS, and myocardial contractility, leading to arrhythmias and cardiovascular dysfunction [ ]. We first predicted the ADAR1-pre-miR-122 interaction via RPISeq (SVM = 0.91) and further verified their regulatory relationship through qPCR and RNP-IP (Fig. E, F). We discovered that cells with suppressed ADAR1 expression exhibited significantly elevated levels of miR-122. These results suggest that ADAR1 may inhibit the formation of mature miR-122.
To investigate the effects of exosomal miR-122 on mouse cardiomyocytes, we loaded a miR-122 inhibitor/miR-122 mimic into 293T cell-derived exosome standards using exosome transport peptides (ETP) and then cocultured the loaded exosomes with HL-1 cells (Fig.  G). The loading efficiency of this method was assessed using fluorescently labelled siRNA, which yielded an approximate encapsulation rate of 56% (Fig. S4A). Total RNA was extracted from engineered exosomes, and RT‒qPCR analysis with U6 as the internal reference revealed that the relative expression of miR-122 in the Exo-miR-122 mimic group was 10-fold greater than that in the Exo-NC mimic group (P < 0.001; Fig. S4B). This target-specific quantification confirmed the reliability of ETP-mediated miR-122 loading, ruling out nonspecific encapsulation artefacts. Physicochemical characterization confirmed successful miR-122 loading, as compared with empty exosomes (Exo-empty), Exo-miR-122-loaded exosomes exhibited significantly greater negative zeta potential (Fig.  H). TEM revealed that both the NC mimic-loaded and the miR-122 mimic-loaded exosomes retained the characteristic cup-shaped morphology of exosomes (Fig.  I). Consistent with these findings, NTA demonstrated a homogeneous size distribution of approximately 50–140 nm, with no significant differences in particle concentration or diameter observed between groups (Fig.  J). Collectively, these data verified that the engineered exosomes conformed to the canonical morphological and physicochemical properties of exosomes and that miR-122 loading did not affect their structural integrity. Functional assays using Annexin V/7-AAD flow cytometry and TUNEL staining further demonstrated that cardiomyocytes treated with miR-122 mimic-enriched exosomes exhibited markedly increased apoptosis rates (Fig.  K, L). Moreover, the use of a miR-122 inhibitor also significantly protected against apoptosis (Fig. S4C–F). These findings demonstrate that exosomes overexpressing miR-122 can promote cardiomyocyte apoptosis.

## miR-122 promotes cardiomyocyte apoptosis by targeting XIAP
To explore the mechanism through which miR-122 affects cardiomyocyte apoptosis, we used software to predict the target molecules of miR-122. We discovered that miR-122 can bind to the 3’UTR of X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein (XIAP) mRNA (Fig. A). XIAP is the strongest inhibitor of apoptosis in the IAP family, directly inhibiting caspases and regulating apoptosis through multiple pathways [ , ]. The targeting relationship between miR-122 and XIAP was verified using a dual-luciferase assay (Fig. B). Further cellular experiments were conducted by transfecting macrophages with a miR-122 mimic and a miR-122 inhibitor. Changes in XIAP transcription and translation levels were measured using qPCR and Western blotting. After transfection with the miR-122 mimic, the intracellular expression of XIAP decreased significantly (Fig. C, D). In conclusion, miR-122 may promote cardiomyocyte apoptosis by targeting XIAP. To determine whether ADAR1 regulates macrophage-derived exosomes to promote apoptosis through the ability of miR-122 to target cardiomyocyte XIAP, we modulated the expression of ADAR1 and miR-122 in macrophages (RAW264.7 cells), isolated exosomes, and coincubated them with cardiomyocytes to determine their effects on cardiomyocyte protein expression. First, we assessed the transfection efficiency of ADAR1 in macrophages using Western blotting (Fig. E). The results revealed that ADAR1 expression was significantly decreased in si-ADAR1-transfected macrophages and significantly increased in OE-ADAR1-transfected macrophages (Fig. F). RT‒qPCR was used to measure miR-122 expression levels in transfected cells, revealing a negative correlation between miR-122 expression and ADAR1 expression (Fig. G). These findings confirmed our successful modulation of ADAR1/miR-122 expression in macrophages. We subsequently isolated the exosomes released by macrophages and cocultured them with cardiomyocytes. The results revealed a positive correlation between miR-122 expression and cardiomyocyte apoptosis (Fig. H, I). In conclusion, miR-122 in macrophage-derived exosomes can promote cardiomyocyte apoptosis. 

## Inhibition of miR-122 alleviates myocardial injury in septic mice
To investigate whether miR-122 accelerates disease progression in sepsis-induced myocardial injury, we inhibited miR-122 expression by injecting an exosome-packaged miR-122 inhibitor into septic mice and examining whether it could alleviate myocardial injury. First, to assess the cardiac accumulation of exosomes in vivo, mice were administered DiR-labelled exosomes at various concentrations via tail vein injection. Subsequent in vivo imaging revealed a dose-dependent increase in fluorescence intensity within the cardiac region, with the 3 × 1010 dose group exhibiting the most intense signal (Fig.  A). By monitoring the 7-day survival rate of the mice, we found that the survival rate of the LPS + NC inhibitor group was 40% after 7 days, while that of the LPS+miR122 inhibitor group was 60% (Fig.  B). Echocardiography was used to assess cardiac function in the mice. Compared with the PBS group, the LPS + NC inhibitor group had significantly lower values of LVEF, LVFS, and the E/A ratio, whereas the cardiac function of septic mice treated with the miR122 inhibitor markedly improved (Fig.  C, D). Biochemical analysis of mouse serum revealed that the levels of the myocardial injury markers LDH, CK-MB, and α-HBDH were significantly lower in the LPS+miR122 inhibitor group than in the LPS + NC inhibitor group (Fig.  E). H&E and TUNEL staining revealed that the myocardial structure, interstitial oedema, cellular vacuolization, and extensive cardiomyocyte apoptosis were disrupted in the LPS + NC inhibitor-treated mice. In contrast, septic mice in which miR-122 was inhibited had improved myocardial structural integrity and significantly reduced apoptosis rates (Fig.  F, G). These results demonstrate that the inhibition of miR-122 slows the differentiation of myocardial macrophages towards the M1 phenotype, alleviates cardiac dysfunction in septic mice, and mitigates myocardial injury and apoptosis during sepsis.

 

In conclusion, our study revealed that exosomes derived from LPS-stimulated macrophages induce myocardial cell injury. The miR-122 present in these exosomes may mediate cardiomyocyte apoptosis by targeting XIAP, leading to caspase activation. ADAR1 can modulate the miR-122 content in macrophage-derived exosomes, thereby protecting cardiomyocytes.

## Discussion
SICM is a reversible functional disorder that affects both sides of the heart and is triggered by sepsis. Like inflammation, immune dysfunction, and coagulation disorders SICM plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of sepsis [ ]. During sepsis, the infiltration of innate immune cells and the release of proinflammatory cytokines lead to myocardial lesions, with macrophages playing an important role in this process, affecting cardiomyocyte function [ ]. ADAR1 has recently been revealed to play a role in macrophage polarization [ ]. In this study, we first demonstrated that the expression of ADAR1 is negatively related to SICM, whereas ADAR1 overexpression can protect against myocardial injury in murine SICM. The results subsequently revealed that ADAR1 overexpression promotes macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype and that exosomes derived from ADAR1-overexpressing M2 macrophages can reduce cardiomyocyte apoptosis under inflammatory conditions. Further mechanistic experiments revealed that ADAR1-overexpressing macrophages act on the miR-122/XIAP axis by reducing exosomal miR-122 expression to decrease cardiomyocyte apoptosis.
Cardiac homeostasis involves maintaining a balance of cell survival, growth, and repair, in which ADAR1 plays a crucial role [ ]. One study revealed that the specific deletion of ADAR1 in cardiomyocytes leads to severe cardiac dysfunction and increased mortality [ ]. Furthermore, Garcia-Gonzalez et al. demonstrated that enzymatically active ADAR1 prevents IRF7-mediated cardiac autoinflammatory responses triggered by endogenous nonedited RNA [ ]. Another study indicated that the expression of ADAR2, another gene in the adenosine deaminases acting on RNA family, is downregulated in blood samples from congenital heart disease (CHD) patients and in DCM ventricular tissue [ ]. Additional research has confirmed that cardiac-specific ADAR2 overexpression mediates acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and MI remodelling by reducing cardiomyocyte apoptosis [ ]. In this study, we first discovered that the expression of ADAR1 is inversely related to the severity and of sepsis and sepsis–induced myocardial damage. In addition, cardiac overexpression of ADAR1 can protect mice from SICM, indicating the disease modulation potential of ADAR1 in controlling SICM and maintaining cardiac health. Notably, the cardioprotective effect of ADAR1 overexpression may involve synergistic regulation of multiple cell types, including macrophages, cardiomyocytes, and other resident or infiltrating immune cells in the heart.
To explore the potential cellular mechanisms underlying the protective effect of ADAR1, we examined its role in macrophage polarization in vitro. Macrophages are multifunctional immune cells that play crucial roles in host defence, tissue repair, and inflammation [ ]. Their functions are largely determined by their polarization into proinflammatory (M1) or anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotypes [ ]. Previous studies have shown that ADAR1 primarily influences gene expression by editing double-stranded RNA (dsRNA), thereby regulating macrophage polarization [ ]. Additionally, some studies have indicated that ADAR1 affects allograft rejection, sepsis-associated intestinal injury, and lung injury by modulating macrophage polarization. For instance, Li et al. reported that ADAR1 regulates M2 macrophage polarization through the ADAR1-miR-21-Foxo1-IL-10 axis, thereby inhibiting allograft rejection [ ]. Furthermore, Liu et al. demonstrated that ADAR1 promotes macrophage M2 polarization in sepsis and reduces macrophage apoptosis, protecting intestinal homeostasis [ ]. Similarly, another study revealed that ADAR1 inhibits the activation of sepsis-induced lung macrophage pyroptosis through the miR-21/A20/NLRP3 axis, thereby alleviating sepsis-associated lung injury [ ]. Consistent with these findings, our study revealed a marked increase in the cellular uptake of LPS-EVs by cardiomyocytes compared with that of NC-EVs. This enhanced uptake is likely attributable to the robust increase in exosome secretion from macrophages upon LPS stimulation: under inflammatory conditions, activated macrophages produce and release large quantities of exosomes, thereby expanding the pool of LPS-EVs available for cardiomyocyte internalization [ ]. Notably, these LPS-EVs derived from M1-polarized macrophages are known to induce cardiomyocyte injury, whereas ADAR1-mediated M2 polarization reshapes the exosomal cargo to exert protective effects. In line with these observations, our in vitro studies on cultured macrophages not only confirmed that ADAR1 promotes macrophage polarization towards the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype but also revealed that exosomes derived from ADAR1-overexpressing M2 macrophages exert a direct protective effect on cardiomyocytes against inflammatory injury. Whether this exosome-mediated paracrine mechanism alone fully underpins the in vivo cardioprotective effects induced by cardiac ADAR1 overexpression warrants further investigation. Notably, our study is the first to report that this protective effect of ADAR1-overexpressing macrophage exosomes is mediated by the ADAR1-dependent regulation of miR-122 expression in these exosomes.
As a key RNA-editing enzyme, ADAR1 exerts multifaceted regulatory effects on miRNAs, which supply the molecular basis for its involvement in various pathological and physiological processes, including inflammation and cardiovascular diseases [ ]. ADAR1 can regulate miRNA biogenesis and expression through both catalytic A-to-I RNA editing and noncatalytic physical interactions; on the one hand, ADAR1 can bind to the precursor sequences of miRNAs (pri-miRNA/pre-miRNA) and modify their stem‒loop structure through A-to-I editing, thereby interfering with the recognition and cleavage of the Dicer/DGCR8 complex and inhibiting the maturation of mature miRNAs; on the other hand, ADAR1 can form a stable complex with miRNA precursors through direct physical binding, which induces steric hindrance to block the processing of miRNAs, leading to the downregulation of mature miRNA expression without relying on its deaminase activity [ ]. Our previous studies confirmed that ADAR1 can target pre-miR-21 and pre-miR-30a to regulate their maturation and participate in macrophage polarization and sepsis-induced intestinal injury [ – ]. In this study, we determined that ADAR1 negatively regulates the expression of miR-122 in macrophage-derived exosomes, which is another important manifestation of ADAR1-mediated miRNA regulation in sepsis. Bioinformatics prediction (RPISeq, SVM = 0.91) and RNP-IP experiments further confirmed the direct binding between ADAR1 and pre-miR-122, suggesting that the regulatory effect of ADAR1 on miR-122 may depend on the physical interaction between them and that the potential A-to-I RNA editing site of ADAR1 on pre-miR-122 needs to be further verified. These findings further highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting ADAR1 to drive the M2 polarization of cardiac macrophages and regulate exosomal miR-122 expression, thereby protecting cardiomyocytes from sepsis-induced injury.
MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are noncoding RNA molecules approximately 22 bases long. They regulate gene expression by binding to the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) of messenger RNA (mRNA), and they influence immune responses and programmed cell death. Previous studies have confirmed that multiple miRNAs are involved in SICM. For example, miR-146a negatively regulates the Toll-like receptor (TLR)/NF-κB pathway by binding to the mRNAs of tumour necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) and interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 1 (IRAK1), suggesting that miR-146a and the TLR-4/NF-κB axis could be potential disease modulation targets for SICM [ ]. Additionally, neutrophil-derived extracellular vesicles containing miR-150-5p participate in SICM by regulating the HIF-1 signalling system and sepsis inflammation [ ]. Furthermore, miR-17-5p can reduce oxidative mtDNA damage, mitigate mitochondrial dysfunction, and inhibit cardiomyocyte apoptosis [ ]. Sun et al. reported that miR-24-3p expression is decreased while Tnfsf10 expression is increased in septic myocardial tissue [ ]. They further demonstrated that exosomes derived from M2 macrophages (M2-exos) can improve cardiac function, reduce cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and alleviate serum inflammation in the context of LPS-induced myocardial injury [ ]. Mechanistically, miR-24-3p in M2-exo exerted cardioprotective effects by inhibiting Tnfsf10. In our study, we revealed that reduced miR-122 expression in exosomes derived from ADAR1-overexpressing macrophages played a central role and that miR-122 might promote cardiomyocyte apoptosis by targeting XIAP. Our further in vivo experiments validated the cardioprotective effect of exosomes encapsulating miR-122 inhibitors, showing that these exosomes can inhibit cardiomyocyte apoptosis and promote survival in septic mice. In recent years, an increasing number of studies have confirmed the pathological role of miR-122 in various cardiomyopathies. For instance, Shi et al. reported that miR-122 overexpression of leads to cardiomyocyte apoptosis and heart failure through the inhibition of Hand2 expression, which in turn reduces Drp1-mediated mitochondrial fission [ ]. Moreover, exosomal miR-122 inhibits mitochondrial ATP production and oxygen consumption in mouse primary cardiomyocytes by directly binding to and inhibiting the mitochondrial protein ADP-ribosylation factor-like 2 (Arl-2) [ ]. In another 5-year prospective study, Pofi et al. combined cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) and molecular analysis to determine that miR-122-5p regulates MMP-2, affects the extracellular matrix, and thereby participates in the progression of diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) [ ]. In conclusion, our research reveals an association between ADAR1 expression and SICM severity, identifies a potential mechanism involving macrophage M2 polarization and exosomal miR-122 regulation, and suggests that miR-122 inhibition is a potential disease modulation strategy for SICM. However, the establishment of macrophage-specific ADAR1 function in vivo awaits further genetic studies.

## Conclusion
This study reveals for the first time that ADAR1 can regulate the apoptotic process of cardiomyocytes in sepsis by modulating the abundance of miR-122 in macrophage-derived exosomes, thereby influencing the expression of its target gene XIAP. We consistently demonstrated, across clinical samples, animal models, and in vitro experiments, that ADAR1 expression is negatively correlated with the severity of septic myocardial injury. ADAR1 overexpression promotes macrophage polarization towards an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype and, through the exosome-mediated ADAR1-miR-122-XIAP signalling, significantly alleviates myocardial inflammation, inhibits apoptosis, and improves cardiac function. These findings not only elucidate a novel regulatory mechanism underlying septic myocardial injury but also provide important experimental evidence for the development of disease modulation strategies targeting ADAR1 or exosomal miR-122, offering promising new avenues for future clinical intervention in septic cardiomyopathy.
