## Abstract
Osteoclasts are specialized cells essential for bone resorption, a crucial process in bone remodeling, and dysregulation of osteoclastogenesis can lead to pathological bone loss such as osteoporosis and rheumatoid arthritis. Therefore, understanding the precise mechanisms governing osteoclast differentiation is crucial for developing effective therapies for skeletal diseases. In osteoclastogenesis, as well as other differentiated cells, it is well understood that cell cycle arrest is essential for terminal differentiation and is tightly regulated by CDK inhibitors such as Cip/Kip family and Ink4 family protein. In this manuscript, we identified p15Ink4b, a member of the Ink4 family, as a novel regulator of osteoclastogenesis by comprehensive single-cell RNA sequence data reanalyzing. Furthermore, histological analysis and in vitro osteoclast differentiation assay revealed that p15Ink4b functionally regulates osteoclastogenesis. Our findings may not only provide insights into the molecular mechanisms of osteoclast differentiation but also underscore the potential of harnessing cell cycle mechanisms to develop novel therapeutic strategies for bone diseases.

## Introduction
Osteoclast are specialized cells essential for bone resorption, a crucial process in bone remodeling , , and its differentiation is tightly regulated by the interaction between the receptor activator of nuclear factor-kappa B (RANK) and its ligand (RANKL) under physiological conditions – . Dysregulation of osteoclastogenesis can lead to pathological bone loss, as observed in bone diseases such as osteoporosis and rheumatoid arthritis , . Therefore, understanding the precise mechanisms governing osteoclast differentiation is crucial for developing effective therapies for skeletal diseases .
RANK-RANKL engagement initiates intracellular signaling cascades, including MAPK and NF-κB pathways, leading to the activation of nuclear factor of activated T-cells 1 (NFATc1), the master regulator of osteoclast differentiation , . The NFATc1 activation induces the expression of various osteoclast-related genes, such as cathepsin K (encoded by Ctsk) and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase type 5 (TRAP, encoded by Acp5) , , , . In vitro osteoclast differentiation models have provided valuable insights into osteoclast biology , , , . In addition, comprehensive approaches such as microarray and bulk RNA-seq have advanced our understanding of this field , . However, bulk RNA-seq analyzes the average gene expression across a population of cells, masking cellular heterogeneity and potentially overlooking small and transient transcriptomic changes in specific subpopulations, such as those occurring during distinct stages of osteoclast differentiation , . This is particularly relevant in osteoclastogenesis, where multiple cell types and differentiation stages coexist , . Recently, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a powerful tool to overcome these limitations. By analyzing the transcriptome of individual cells, scRNA-seq allows for the identification of distinct cell populations, the characterization of rare cell types, and the reconstruction of differentiation trajectories – . In the context of osteoclast studies, scRNA-seq has enabled the identification of novel regulators of differentiation, such as Cited2, a previously unappreciated transcription factor . Thus, scRNA-seq has emerged as a powerful tool to unravel cellular diversity in tissues – and cultured cells – , including osteoclasts , . Conducting comprehensive scRNA-seq analysis of in vitro osteoclast formation is expected to decipher intricate cellular dynamics during osteoclastogenesis .
Cell cycle arrest is a critical step in the terminal differentiation of various cell types – , including osteoclasts – . Upon RANKL stimulation, osteoclast precursor cells undergo rapid and transient cell cycle entry , followed by G1 phase arrest , , . This process is orchestrated by cyclins and cyclin dependent kinases (CDKs), whose activity is tightly regulated by cell cycle inhibitors (CDK inhibitors [CDKIs]) of two main family proteins: the Cip/Kip family (e.g., p21Cip1/Waf1 [encoded by Cdkn1a], p27Kip1 [encoded by Cdkn1b], and p57Kip2 [encoded by Cdkn1c]) and the Ink4 family (e.g., p16Ink4a [encoded by Cdkn2a], p15Ink4b [encoded by Cdkn2b], p18Ink4c [encoded by Cdkn2c], and p19Ink4d [encoded by Cdkn2d]) – . In osteoclasts, several CDKIs, including p21Cip1/Waf1, p27Kip1, and p16Ink4a, have been shown to be upregulated during differentiation , , . Given the diverse roles of CDKIs in regulating cell differentiation across various lineages – , we hypothesized that other CDKIs except p21Cip1/Waf1, p27Kip1, and p16Ink4a may also play a pivotal role in osteoclast differentiation.
p15Ink4b (encoded by Cdkn2b) is a member of the Ink4 family of CDKIs. It functions by interacting with CDK4/6, preventing their association with cyclin D and thereby arresting the cell cycle at the G1 to S phase transition . This function as a cell cycle regulator has led to its well-established role as a tumor suppressor, with documented involvement in inhibiting cell cycle progression in various cancers, including urothelial tumors, skin tumors, and soft tissue sarcomas – . Beyond its role in tumor suppression, p15Ink4b has also been implicated in the differentiation of other cell types. For example, studies have shown that p15Ink4b is involved in the differentiation of conventional dendritic cells in the bone marrow; and in p15Ink4b-deficient mice, the differentiation of dendritic cells from bone marrow precursor cells is impaired, resulting in reduced expression of MHC II and CD80/86 . These findings suggest that p15Ink4b, in addition to its role in cell cycle control and tumor suppression, may also play a role in cell differentiation. However, its specific role in osteoclasts remained unexplored until this study.
Through a detailed reanalysis of deposited comprehensive scRNA-seq data, we identified the well-known tumor suppressor p15Ink4b as a previously unrecognized factor critical for osteoclastogenesis. Our findings not only expand our knowledge of the molecular mechanisms underlying osteoclast differentiation but also highlight a potential therapeutic target for bone diseases.

## Results

## scRNA-seq data revealed temporal dynamics of gene expression profiles of cell-cycle inhibitors during osteoclast differentiation
To elucidate the relationship between osteoclast differentiation and the regulation of cell cycle checkpoints by the Cip/Kip and Ink4 families of cell-cycle inhibitors, we reanalyzed previously published scRNA-seq data that captured cellular dynamics of osteoclastogenesis at days 0, 1, and 3 post-RANKL stimulation in vitro. A total of 7,177 cells met our quality control criteria (see the Materials and methods section for details) and were defined 20 distinct clusters(Fig.  a). Notably, elevated expression levels of osteoclast marker genes (Acp5, Ctsk, Mmp9, Nfatc1, Dcstamp, Atp6v0d2) identified cluster 9 as predominantly comprising mature osteoclasts (Fig.  a, c and Supplementary Fig. a). In contrast, cluster 2 was identified as osteoclast precursor cells based on the expression patterns of marker genes for osteoclast precursor cells (Tnfrsf11a, Csf1r, Cx3cr1) and negative regulators for osteoclastogenesis (Irf8, Bcl6, Mafb) (Fig.  a, d, e and Supplementary Fig. a). Consistent with these findings, cluster 9 (mature osteoclast cluster) primarily consisted of cells captured at day 3 post-RANKL stimulation, which includes fully differentiated osteoclasts (Fig.  b and Supplementary Fig. b). Conversely, cluster 2 (osteoclast precursor cell cluster) contained large numbers of cells harvested at day 0 (Fig.  b and Supplementary Fig. b). Furthermore, after RANKL stimulation, clusters 5 and 0 appeared on day 1 and disappeared on day 3 (Fig.  b and Supplementary Fig. b). Additionally, the expression levels of osteoclast marker genes, marker genes for osteoclast precursor cells, and negative regulators for osteoclastogenesis suggesting that cluster 2 differentiated into cluster 9, passing through clusters 5 and 0. Next, we examined whether Cip/Kip or Ink4 family factors were altered among all cell cycle-related genes during the transition to clusters 2, 5, 0, and 9. We identified each cluster marker gene (Supplementary Table. ) and listed cell-cycle related genes according to Gene Ontology Annotations profile (GO: 0007049) (Fig.  f and Supplementary Table ). Among the top 20 cell-cycle regulators whose expression was increased in clusters 2, 5, 0, and 9, only Cdkn2b (p15Ink4b) was significantly expressed in the mature osteoclast cluster (cluster 9) (Fig.  f and Supplementary Table. ). Consistent with this, Cdkn2b (p15Ink4b), a marker characteristic of cell-cycle inhibition in maturing cells, was prominently and specifically enriched in cluster 9 (Fig.  g). These observations prompted further investigations into how Cdkn2b (p15Ink4b) influences osteoclast differentiation.
 

## Elevated expression of p15Ink4b (Cdkn2b) in osteoclasts both in vitro and in vivo
To validate the scRNA-seq-based analysis, we examined gene expression dynamics during osteoclast differentiation using established protocols , , (Fig.  a); After two days of priming with M-CSF, mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages/monocytes (BMDMs) were stimulated by RANKL for three days. The resulting analysis verified the formation of TRAP-positive multinucleated cells, indicative of osteoclastogenesis (Fig.  b), and confirmed the upregulation of osteoclast marker genes (Ctsk and Nfatc1) alongside the downregulation of negative regulators for osteoclastogenesis (Irf8 and Mafb) following RANKL stimulation (Fig.  c,d). Notably, under this condition, among the genes within the Cip/Kip and Ink4 families of cell-cycle inhibitors, the expression of p21Cip1/Waf1 (Cdkn1a) and p15Ink4b (Cdkn2b) varied significantly; however, only the latter showed significant upregulation during the late phase of osteoclast differentiation (day3 post-RANKL stimulation) (Fig.  e and Supplementary Fig. ). In line with this finding, protein levels of p15Ink4b were evident even from day 1 post-RANKL stimulation and gradually increased as differentiation proceeded (Fig.  f,g and Supplementary Fig. ; uncropped images). Next, to explore whether osteoclastic p15Ink4b expression could be detected in vivo within bone tissue, we conducted histochemical analysis on mouse tibia sections. As previously reported , TRAP-positive osteoclasts were primarily positioned around the growth plate of the tibiae (Fig.  a). Immunohistochemical staining revealed that p15Ink4b signals overlapped with regions where TRAP-positive large cells were located (Fig.  a,b). However, strong p15Ink4b signals were also abundant in other areas, indicating widespread expression of p15Ink4b protein in various cell types within the bone tissue (Fig.  b). Thus, we additionally assessed the expression of CD68, a pan-macrophage lineage marker (and also being expressed by osteoclasts) in specimens and confirmed that CD68-positive large cells around the region of the growth plate indeed strongly expressed p15Ink4b (Fig.  c). These findings suggest that p15Ink4b is a newly discovered cell-cycle inhibitor that possibly operates in the differentiation and/or functional regulation of osteoclasts.
 

## Impact of targeted silencing of the p15Ink4b (Cdkn2b) gene on osteoclastogenesis
To explore the functional role of p15Ink4b in osteoclasts/osteoclast precursor cells, we conducted an siRNA-mediated gene knockdown study. Specifically, BMDMs were transfected with two distinct siRNAs targeting p15Ink4b (siCdkn2b #1 and siCdkn2b #2) alongside a negative control siRNA (siControl) to facilitate RNA interference. Both siCdkn2b #1 and siCdkn2b #2 effectively reduced p15Ink4b expression (Fig.  a) without affecting the survival of precursor cells (data not shown). Analysis at day 4 post-RANKL stimulation revealed that BMDMs transfected with siCdkn2b #1 and siCdkn2b #2 exhibited a marked suppression in the formation of TRAP-positive multinucleated cells (Fig.  b), underscoring the crucial role of p15Ink4b during the course of osteoclast formation. Further bolstering these findings, essential osteoclast marker genes such as DC-STAMP (Dcstamp) and Cathepsin K (Ctsk), which are critical for osteoclast fusion and function, demonstrated significant downregulation following p15Ink4b knockdown (Fig.  c). The decreased expression of these genes is likely to directly correlate with the reduced osteoclastogenesis. This molecular evidence clearly supports our phenotypic observations and highlights the significant regulatory function of p15Ink4b in the osteoclast differentiation pathway, likely via mechanisms involving cell cycle arrest.
 

## Discussion
Our study identifies p15Ink4b as a novel and critical regulator of osteoclast differentiation. Our multi-faced approach, encompassing scRNA-seq, in vitro differentiation assays, and immunohistochemical analysis in mouse bone tissue, reveals a striking and specific upregulation of p15Ink4b in osteoclast-lineage cells compared to other CDKIs, both in vitro and in vivo. The observed impairment of osteoclast formation and the concurrent downregulation of key osteoclast-related factors (DC-STAMP, Cathepsin K) upon p15Ink4b knockdown strongly underscore its pivotal role in osteoclastogenesis. This aligns with the established understanding of cell cycle arrest as a prerequisite for terminal differentiation in various cell types – , including osteoclasts – . As the cell cycle of mature osteoclasts is known to be arrested in the G1/S phase , , our findings suggest that p15Ink4b plays a crucial role in orchestrating this arrest, potentially by inhibiting CDK4/6 activity. Another CDKI, p27Kip1, has been reported to be expressed in osteoclast-lineage cells , , , and our analysis also revealed the upregulation of p27Kip1 mRNA expression in mature osteoclast cluster (Supplementary Fig. and Table ). These observations raise intriguing questions about the interplay between p15Ink4b and p27Kip1 in regulating the osteoclast cell cycle. While p27Kip1 is known to have a dual role, both inhibiting and activating cyclin D-CDK4/6 complexes under different conditions – , the precise nature of its crosstalk with p15Ink4b in osteoclast-lineage cells warrants further investigation. It is plausible that p15Ink4b may influence the balance between the inhibitory and activating function of p27Kip1(40), thereby fine-tuning cell cycle progression during osteoclast differentiation. While p15Ink4b-deficient and p27Kip1-deficient mouse models exist , , their bone phenotypes, particularly with respect to osteoclast differentiation, have not been fully characterized , , . Given the potential for functional redundancy or compensation between these two CDKIs, future studies using p15Ink4b and p27Kip1 double knockout mice may elucidate their distinct roles and synergistic interactions in osteoclastogenesis, providing novel insights into the precise mechanisms governing bone development and homeostasis.
Moreover, our reanalysis of deposited scRNA-seq data suggests that p15Ink4b expression in osteoclasts may be regulated by Cited2, a transcriptional regulator implicated in osteoclastogenesis (Supplementary Fig. ). Given the Cited2 has been reported to interact with SMAD proteins , and that the SMAD4-SMAD2/3 complex can bind to the promoter of the p15Ink4b gene (Cdkn2b) and regulate its expression , , it is conceivable that Cited2 modulates p15Ink4b expression through its interaction with SMAD proteins. This interaction could be a mechanism by which Cited2 influences osteoclast differentiation, as SMAD4-SMAD2/3 complex activation has been shown to be extensively increased during osteoclastogenesis , . The observed downregulation of p15Ink4b in Cited2 knockout mice (Supplementary Fig. ) supports this hypothesis and suggests a potential regulatory axis involving Cited2, SMAD, and p15Ink4b in osteoclastogenesis.
Our findings indicate that p15Ink4b plays a critical role in osteoclast differentiation, particularly in the later stages involving cell fusion. Knockdown of p15Ink4b nearly completely suppressed the formation of large, multinucleated osteoclasts (with more than 20 nuclei) (Fig.  b), suggesting that p15Ink4b is essential for this process. It is reported that cell cycle arrest is a prerequisite for osteoclast fusion . Given the well-established role of p15Ink4b as a cell cycle inhibitor, it likely contributes to the necessary G1/S arrest required for fusion. While the precise molecular mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated, we hypothesize that p15Ink4b may regulate the expression or activity of key fusion-related proteins, such as DC-STAMP or OC-STAMP. Future studies should investigate how p15Ink4b modulates these factors in osteoclastogenesis.
p15Ink4b was found to be expressed in multiple cell types within bone tissue; Specifically, it was detected in mononuclear cells on the bone surface (likely including osteoblasts), cells embedded within the bone matrix (osteocytes), and some bone marrow cells (Fig.  b). This extensive expression pattern suggests that p15Ink4b plays a complex role within the bone microenvironment. Future studies are needed to investigate the functions of p15Ink4b in other bone cell types, such as osteoblasts and osteocytes, as well as its contribution to bone homeostasis through interactions with other signaling pathways. Furthermore, similar to reports in other cell types , , p15Ink4b may be involved in maintaining quiescence or preventing excessive proliferation in specific cell types. According to the Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/), consistent with our findings, p15Ink4b is also expressed in monocyte/macrophage lineage cells in the bone marrow, further emphasizing its diverse roles within the bone environment.
This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, our analysis of p15Ink4b function in osteoclasts was conducted using in vitro cultures with transient Cdkn2b suppression via siRNA. Although we achieved effective knockdown, transient suppression does not fully mimic the effects of sustained p15Ink4b deficiency. Future experiments employing lentivirus-mediated shRNA for stable Cdkn2b knockdown could provide deeper insights, revealing potential adaptive responses or alternative signaling pathways affected by long-term p15Ink4b depletion. Second, we did not assess the role of p15Ink4b in osteoclast function in vivo. While our in vitro results suggest a significant role for p15Ink4b in osteoclast differentiation, in vivo studies are necessary to confirm these findings within a physiological context. Developing a conditional knockout mouse model with Cdkn2b specifically deleted in osteoclast precursors (e.g., using a Rank-Cre/Cdkn2bfl/fl strategy) would help explore the therapeutic potential of targeting p15Ink4b in conditions like osteoporosis or inflammatory bone diseases.
The identification of p15Ink4b, a well-established tumor suppressor pivotal in cell cycle arrest , as a novel regulator of osteoclast differentiation may open exciting therapeutic avenues for diseases characterized by excessive bone resorption. Targeting p15Ink4b could potentially alleviate bone loss by inhibiting osteoclast formation and activity, however, given its widespread expression in bone tissue (Fig.  b), a cautious approach is necessary to mitigate potential off-target effects and maintain the balance between bone resorption and formation. Our findings may not only provide insights into the molecular mechanisms of osteoclast differentiation but also underscore the potential of harnessing cell cycle mechanisms to develop novel therapeutic strategies for bone diseases.

## Materials and methods

## Reanalysis of single-cell and bulk transcriptional profiles
The deposited single-cell RNA sequencing data (GSE147174(6)) were reanalyzed by R (version 4.4.1), and Seurat package (v5) . Briefly, genes expressed in less than three cells, cells displaying fewer than 200 number of unique molecular identifiers detected (nCount_RNA) and 500 number of genes detected (nFeature_RNA) were deemed to provide insufficient information and thus were excluded from our analysis. Additionally, cells containing more than 5% mitochondrial reads were also excluded, as this was indicative of cellular damaged. The cells that passed these criteria were then merged and the read counts per cell were normalized. Top 2,000 valuable features were selected by FindVariableFeatures with “vst” method. The data was scaled and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed specifying 30 principal components. To remove batch effects and integrate the datasets, the IntegrateLayers function was applied using Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA). RNA assay layers were joined. The integrated data was used to construct a nearest-neighbor graph with FindNeighbors, and clusters were identified with FindClusters at a resolution of 1. Finally, the data was visualized using UMAP based on the first 30 dimensions of the integrated CCA reduction. The clustering analysis based on a graph-based clustering approach was performed. The marker genes for each cluster were identified by FindAllMarker function and considered with adjusted p values < 0.05 and average Log2 fold change > 1 as statistical significance. After listed all cluster marker genes, cell-cycle related genes were picked by using Gene Ontology Annotation (GO: 0007049), and visualized top 20 genes by DoHeatmap function. The deposited bulk RNA sequencing data (GSE1471746) were reanalyzed by iDEP2 software . Log2 fold change of FPKM were shown as heap map.

## Animal experiments
Specific-pathogen-free C57BL/6J mice were obtained from Japan SLC (Shizuoka, Japan). All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Yamaguchi University. The study was conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health. The study reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation, and bone or bone marrow cells were then subjected to downstream analyses.

## RNA isolation and reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)
Total RNA was extracted using the Reliaprep Cell RNA Miniprep Systems (Promega, Z6012, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was synthesized using a ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (TOYOBO, FSQ-201, Osaka, Japan). The mRNA expression levels of each gene were normalized to 18S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) expression levels using quantitative qPCR. The relative gene expression was calculated by ΔΔCt methods. The PCR primer sequences used are as follows: 18S rRNA (forward: 5′- CTCAACACGGGAAACCTCAC-3′ and reverse: 5′- CGCTCCACCAACTAAGAACG-3′); Cdkn1a (p21Cip1/Waf1) (forward: 5′-TGTCTTGCACTCTGGTGTCT-3′ and reverse: 5′-TGAGGGCTAAGGCCGAAGAT-3′); Cdkn1b (p27Kip1) (forward: 5′-TCAAACGTGAGAGTGTCTAACG-3′ and reverse: 5′-CCGGGCCGAAGAGATTTCTG-3′); Cdkn1c (p57Kip2) (forward: 5′-GGGCAGTACAGGAACCATTTC-3′ and reverse: 5′-TTAGCTTACAGTGTCCCGCA-3′); Cdkn2a (p16Ink4a) (forward: 5′-GAACTCTTTCGGTCGTACCC -3′ and reverse: 5′-CGAATCTGCACCGTAGTTGA-3′); Cdkn2b (p15Ink4b) (forward: 5′-AGATCCCAACGCCCTGAAC-3′ and reverse: 5′-CCCATCATCATGACCTGGATT-3′); Cdkn2c (p18Ink4c) (forward: 5′-CAGATTAACCATCCCAGTCCTT-3′ and reverse: 5′-CCCCTTTCCTTTGCTCCTAA-3′); Cdkn2d (p19Ink4d) (forward: 5′-AATGTGACCCAAGGCCACT-3′ and reverse: 5′-TTTCCTCTTTTGTTGACAAGTAACC-3′); Nfatc1 (forward: 5′-GGTAACTCTGTCTTTCTAACCTTAAGCTC-3′ and reverse: 5′-TGATGACCCCAGCATGCACCAGTCACAG-3′); Ctsk (forward: 5′-GCCAGGATGAAAGTTGTATG -3′ and reverse: 5′-CAGGCGTTGTTCTTATTCC-3′); Dcstamp (forward: 5′-TGTATCGGCTCATCTCCTCCAT-3′ and reverse: 5′-GACTCCTTGGGTTCCTTGCTT-3′); Irf8 (forward: 5′-GAGCCAGATCCTCCCTGACT-3′ and reverse: 5′-GGCATATCCGGTCACCAGT-3′); and Mafb (forward: 5′-GCAGGTATAAACGCGTCCAG-3′ and reverse: 5′-TGAATGAGCTGCGTCTTCTC-3′).

## In vitro osteoclast differentiation
The methods for in vitro osteoclast differentiation was previously described . Briefly, 8- to 10-week-old female C57BL/6 mice were purchased from SLC Japan (Shizuoka, Japan). All animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Care Committee of Yamaguchi University Graduate School of Medicine. Bone marrow cells were cultured with 10 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 300-25, Waltham, MA, USA) for 2 days and then with 10 ng/mL M-CSF and 20 ng/mL RANKL (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 310-01) for 3–4 days. The medium was changed every day. The following siRNAs were used for knockdown experiments: Stealth RNAi siRNA Negative Control (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 12935300) and siCdkn2b (Sigma-Aldrich, PDSIRNA2D, SASI_Mm01_00144010 and SASI_Mm01_00144011, St. Louis, MO, USA). These siRNAs were transduced 6 h daily with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 13778100). Cells were cultured in α-MEM (FUJIFILM, 135-15175, Osaka, Japan) with 10% fetal bovine serum (HyClone, SH30396.03), sodium pyruvate (FUJIFILM, 190-14881), MEM non-essential amino acids (FUJIFILM, 139-15651), penicillin–streptomycin (FUJIFILM, 168-23191), and 50 μM 2-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, M3148). Osteoclastogenesis was evaluated by tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining on a BZ-X800 microscope (Keyence, Osaka, Japan). TRAP-positive multinucleated cells (more than three nuclei) were considered as osteoclasts.

## Immunoblotting
Cells were lysed by RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), 1% Triton-X 100, and 1% sodium deoxycholate) containing protease inhibitor cocktail cOmplete (Sigma-Aldrich, 11697498001). After centrifuge at 4 °C, 13,000 × g for 15 min, the supernatant was collected and subjected to immunoblotting. Four μg of protein, as determined using DC protein assay kit II (Bio-Rad, 5000112, Hercules, CA, USA), was suspended with 4 × Laemmli sample buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 6.8, 4% SDS, 10% glycerol, 0.1% bromophenol blue, and 1 mM dithiothreitol) and boiled at 95 °C for 5 min. The primary antibodies used were for β-actin (Proteintech, HRP-66009, 1:1000, Rosemont, IL, USA), p15Ink4b (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-613, 1:200, Dallas, TX, USA), NFATc1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-7294, 1:200), and Cathepsin K (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-48353, 1:200). The membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (Promega, W401B and W402B, 1:10,000) and visualized with Chemi-Lumi One Super (Nacalai tesque, 02230-30, Kyoto, Japan), followed by detection with chemiluminescence on Amersham Imager 680 (Danaher, Washington, D.C., USA). The band intensity was quantified using Image J software (National Institute of Health, MD, USA).

## Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence
Tibiae of 14-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for over night at 4 °C. Mice were euthanized via CO2 inhalation. All animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Care Committee of Yamaguchi University Graduate School of Medicine. Then, tissues were decalcified with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (10% w/v) (MUTO PURE CHEMICALS, 20251, Tokyo, Japan) at 4 °C for 7 days, replaced into 70% ethanol, and embedded in paraffin. Deparaffinization and hydration of the 5-μm sections were performed, using PathoClean (FUJIFILM, 161-28321) and a series of graded alcohols. The following antibodies were used for immunofluorescence staining: normal rabbit IgG (Sigma-Aldrich, 12–370, 1:1000, same as the concentration of anti-p15Ink4b), rabbit anti-p15Ink4b (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc613, 1:200), and rat anti-CD68 (Bio-rad, MCA1957GA, 1:200) as primary antibodies; donkey anti-rat IgG Alexa Fluor 555 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A78945, 1:1,000) and donkey anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor Plus 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A32795, 1:1,000) as secondary antibodies. Counter staining was performed by 4′,6′-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (DOJINDO, 340-07971, 1:2,000, Kumamoto, Japan). The following reagents were used for immunohistochemistry: normal rabbit IgG (1:1,000) and rabbit anti-p15Ink4b (1:200) as primary antibodies; horse anti-rabbit IgG peroxidase (Vector Laboratories, MP-7401, Newark, CA, USA) as a secondary antibody; and a chromogenic substrate ImmPACT DAB EqV (Vector Laboratories, SK-4103). Counter staining was performed by Mayer’s hematoxylin solution (FUJIFILM, 131-09665). All images were obtained on a BZ-X800 microscope.

## Statistical analyses
All data were visualized and analyzed in Prism (v.10.2.0) (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was determined with one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test (Figs. c, d, e, g, a, b, c Supplementary Fig. ), and student’s t-test in (Fig. a,c). P values < 0.05 were considered significant. *: P valure < 0.05, **: P valure < 0.01, ***: P valure < 0.001, ****: P valure < 0.0001.
