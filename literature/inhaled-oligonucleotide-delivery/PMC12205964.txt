## Abstract
Monocytes, key mediators of innate immunity, exhibit remarkable sensitivity to mechanical cues such as extracellular matrix (ECM) stiffness, substrate rigidity, shear stress, compression, and hydrostatic pressure, which shape their activation, differentiation, and functional polarization. Monocytes develop from the bone marrow and populate the vasculature throughout the body. During inflammation, they are recruited to injured or diseased tissues by chemokines and proinflammatory cytokines, modulating local immune responses during embryonic development and adulthood via mechanosensing and mechanotransduction pathways. This review synthesizes recent advances in monocyte mechanobiology. It highlights how the bone marrow ECM mechanics orchestrates myelopoiesis, the role of endothelium and hemodynamic forces in migration, and how tissue mechanics influences monocyte fate in chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and cancer. We discuss the mechanosensitive pathways that govern monocyte behavior in health and disease and therapeutic opportunities that emerge from targeting these mechanisms via biomaterial approaches. Additionally, future directions toward developing mechanotherapy for immune modulation are discussed. By bridging mechanobiology and immunology, this review underscores the potential of mechanical cues as therapeutic targets to reprogram monocyte behavior in disease.

I.

## INTRODUCTION
Monocytes are versatile sentinels of the innate immune system, bridging surveillance, defense, and tissue homeostasis. Derived from hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), these circulating leukocytes patrol the vasculature and infiltrate tissues during infection or injury, where they differentiate into macrophages or dendritic cells (DCs) to orchestrate immune responses. While their roles in phagocytosis, antigen presentation, and tissue repair are well-established, emerging evidence highlights the profound influence of mechanical forces, such as extracellular matrix (ECM) stiffness, shear stress, and compression, on monocyte development, polarization, and function. Monocytes exhibit remarkable mechanosensitivity across biological contexts, from embryogenesis to disease. During early development, hemodynamic forces guide HSC migration and myelopoiesis, while postnatal bone marrow (BM) niches leverage stiffness gradients and integrin-mediated adhesion to regulate monocyte egress and subset specialization. These processes are governed by mechanotransduction pathways that convert physical cues into immunomodulatory signals. Importantly, the disease relevance of monocyte mechanobiology is increasingly evident, as tissue mechanics shape monocyte phenotypes, influencing therapeutic responses and clinical outcomes. 
This review synthesizes current knowledge of monocyte mechanobiology, spanning developmental origins, subset classification, and functional plasticity in health and disease. We discuss how biophysical forces regulate myelopoiesis, tissue infiltration, and effector functions, emphasizing in vitro and ex vivo models of mechanical microenvironments. Furthermore, we explore therapeutic opportunities for targeting monocyte mechanosensing in cancer, fibrosis, and regenerative medicine, advocating for interdisciplinary approaches to translate mechanistic insights into clinical applications. A deeper understanding of how these cells sense and respond to mechanical stimuli may pave the way for novel immunotherapeutic strategies. For example, modulating monocyte behavior in cancer immunotherapy, designing mechanically responsive therapies for fibrotic diseases, and improving the design of biomaterials to tune immune cell fate for therapies. By summarizing the latest advances in monocyte mechanobiology, this review highlights key discoveries, unresolved challenges, and potential applications of this research area. This may inform disease mechanisms and inspire novel biomaterial-based therapeutic strategies for chronic inflammatory diseases more broadly.

II.

## MONOCYTES: DEVELOPMENT, CLASSIFICATION, AND FUNCTIONS
Monocytes are a subset of white blood cells derived from hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) in the bone marrow (BM) and circulate throughout the bloodstream. They are critical to the immune system by defending against microbial and non-microbial threats. When inflammation occurs, monocytes migrate to the affected tissues, differentiating into macrophages or dendritic cells (DCs), which respond to infection or inflammation. Macrophages further differentiate into specialized subtypes based on their tissue environment, such as histiocytes in connective tissue, microglia in the brain, osteoclasts in bone, mesangial cells in the kidney, and alveolar macrophages in the lung. DCs are highly efficient antigen-presenting cells that activate T-cells to initiate and propagate immune responses and maintain immune tolerance under homeostatic conditions. Monocytes and their differentiated effector cells patrol the body, detect microbial invaders, and coordinate immune responses. Monocytes serve dual functions within the innate immune system, balancing surveillance and defense. As sentinels, they patrol the bloodstream and tissues, continuously scanning for disease and pathogen-associated signals, maintaining vascular integrity, and detecting early signs of tissue damage. When infection or inflammation arises, monocytes initiate a targeted immune response by recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) through pattern recognition receptors and migrating to the site of infection. They perform multiple defensive roles at the affected site as phagocytes to engulf and destroy pathogens and cellular debris and as antigen-presenting cells to activate lymphocytes and strengthen the immune response. Through this combination of proactive monitoring and reactive defense, monocytes ensure rapid and effective protection against the spread of infection. 
A.

## Myelopoiesis and monocyte development
In mammals, embryonic monocytes originate from hematopoiesis in the fetal liver. Adult hematopoiesis occurs in the BM. The embryonic lineage of monocytes is established prenatally during embryonic development in the yolk sac, following primitive hematopoiesis of progenitors in the yolk sac. Specifically, HSCs generate erythro-myeloid progenitors (EMPs) that seed the area surrounding the dorsal aorta, termed the aorta-gonad mesonephros (AGM) region. Then, EMPs give rise to fetal monocytes, which spread via circulation to the entire tissue. Later, HSCs migrate from AGM to the fetal liver to undergo expansion and maturation into different lineages. At the tissue, fetal monocytes differentiate into tissue macrophages and are maintained independently. After birth, fetal monocytes are resupplied with BM-derived monocytes, which are differentiated from hematopoietic stem cells through pathways similar to those of fetal monocytes. Interestingly, HSCs in the fetal liver are in a cycle, whereas adult BM HSCs are primarily quiescent. 

B.

## Classification of monocytes
In humans, monocytes were initially defined based on morphology and cytochemistry (monocyte-specific esterase). The nomenclature committee of the International Union of Immunological Societies classified monocytes into three subsets—classical (CD14++CD16−), intermediate (CD14++CD16+), and non-classical (CD14+CD16++)—each with distinct surface markers and immune functions. Here, + denotes an expression level ∼10-fold above the isotype control, and ++ is ∼100-fold above the isotype control. Overall, CD14 and CD16 markers have proven helpful in determining monocyte subpopulations. For human monocytes, CD14 and CD16 antibodies targeting different epitopes are available. For CD14 antibodies directed against the lipopolysaccharide-binding domain, and for CD16, those that bind to the Fc-binding domain are suggested to characterize these cells.
Classical monocytes initiate the immune system's inflammatory response and facilitate tissue repair. They possess a strong capacity for phagocytosis, enabling them to engulf and eliminate pathogens. In addition to this function, classical monocytes secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-6, which help recruit and activate additional immune cells at sites of infection or injury. These monocytes also express high levels of chemokine receptors, including CCR2, which promote their rapid migration to inflamed or damaged tissues. Upon activation, classical monocytes differentiate into macrophages or DCs, which can both initiate and resolve inflammation via cytokine signaling and antigen presentation. This dual capability—amplifying and later moderating the inflammatory response—highlights their essential role in immune activation and tissue homeostasis. Intermediate monocytes, although less understood, are believed to play a role in bridging the functions of classical and non-classical monocytes. Evidence suggests that these cells are involved in both early immune activation and later immune regulation, although the exact mechanisms still remain unelucidated. Non-classical monocytes are characterized by high expression of CD16 and low expression of CD14. These cells patrol the vascular endothelium, continuously monitoring for signs of tissue damage or infection. Their patrolling activity, regulated by receptors such as CX3CR1, allows them to detect and respond to potential threats with minimal disruption to the integrity of the vascular system. Non-classical monocytes are associated with anti-inflammatory functions, promoting vascular homeostasis by producing factors that mitigate inflammation and support tissue repair. Together, the specialized roles of these monocyte subsets highlight their complementary functions in immune surveillance, pathogen defense, and the regulation of inflammation.

C.

## Functional roles of monocytes
Monocytes play a critical role in both innate and adaptive immune responses, with their trafficking mechanisms being essential for their functions in infection and inflammation. Classical monocytes are more prominent in sustaining inflammation and mounting a more robust antimicrobial defense. Classical monocytes are typically the first responders, while non-classical monocytes increase as inflammation progresses. 
Key chemokines such as CCR2 and its ligands, including CCL2 (MCP1) and CCL7 (MCP3), are central to recruiting CD14+ monocytes. CCL2, expressed by various cells in response to inflammatory signals, creates gradients that direct monocytes to sites of infection or inflammation. CCL7 also aids in monocyte recruitment, particularly during bacterial infections, and studies suggest that CCL2 and CCL7 may act synergistically to guide monocytes to their target tissues. The CX3CL1–CX3CR1 axis is essential for the trafficking of CD14+ monocytes, which patrol blood vessels by using CX3CR1 to bind to CX3CL1, a membrane-bound chemokine expressed in tissues. Other chemokine receptors, such as CCR1 and CCR5, also contribute to monocyte recruitment during inflammation, mediating their migration through endothelial cells (ECs). These receptors are involved in diseases like atherosclerosis, multiple sclerosis, and rheumatoid arthritis, which play distinct roles in inflammatory processes. Monocyte migration follows a series of steps, including rolling, adhesion, and transmigration across the endothelial barrier, which is mediated by several adhesion molecules (P-selectin, E-selectin, and integrins) expressed by ECs. 

III.

## MECHANICAL REGULATION OF MYELOPOIESIS
A.

## Role of forces during embryogenesis and its impact on myelopoiesis
The mechanics of the embryonic environment significantly affect homeostasis of primary HSC throughout the process, where HSCs first emerge from AGM in the yolk sac, followed by migration through fetal circulation and seeding of the fetal liver [ ]. When the first wave of hematopoiesis begins in the blood islands of the yolk sac, hemodynamic forces play a role in influencing hematopoietic cell development, including shear stress (friction), circumferential strain (stretching), and hydrostatic pressure. Shear stress, specifically, plays an important role in influencing hematopoietic cell development. Flow system culture showed that shear stress increased hematopoietic colony-forming potential and expression of hematopoietic markers in the AGM region of embryos. Shear stress also regulates HSC migration from AGM to the fetal liver. Studies demonstrated that shear stress-induced junctional Src activation regulated vascular permeability in vivo through phosphorylation of VE-cadherin. Regulation of permeability would greatly influence primary HSCs' potential to seep from the intravascular space into extravascular tissue compartments, enabling HSC localization to organs for maturation.
 On the other hand, migrating HSCs sense and respond to the mechanical properties of the environment through receptors, such as β1 integrin. HSCs lacking β1 integrin failed to seed the fetal liver, indicating that β1 integrin is essential for HSC colonization of the fetal liver during embryonic development. Once HSCs exit blood vessels and colonize the fetal liver, the matrix properties of the fetal liver support the superior expansion and differentiation needs of fetal HSCs. The fetal liver is considered to have a softer matrix than the adult liver. In vitro tests confirmed that HSC expansion was positively influenced by lower hydrogel stiffness. Following rapid HSC expansion, fetal liver mechanical properties supported HSC differentiation further into myeloid and lymphoid lineages. Mutant mice embryos deficient in solute carrier family 8 member 1 protein (Slc8a1−/− mice) lack hemodynamic stress. These embryos fail to induce myeloid progenitor formation in the pSp (para-aortic splanchnopleura), the precursor to AGM in later embryogenesis. Mechanical forces crosstalk with stem cell differentiation factors, such as Myb, granting fetal myeloid cells increased mitochondrial bioenergetics and glycolytic capacity compared to BM-derived circulating monocytes, indicating their increased survival and expansion capacity. 

B.

## Role of the mechanical properties of the bone marrow niche on myelopoiesis
Unlike fetal HSCs, adult HSCs primarily reside in the BM niche, which orchestrates HSC maintenance, proliferation, self-renewal, and differentiation. The components of the BM niche are highly heterogeneous, consisting of soluble growth factors, various cell types, varying oxygen tension and ECM physical properties. Vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) and selectin mediate homing of hematopoietic progenitors to the spleen and BM to start the hematopoiesis process. ECM composition and biophysical properties are critical in regulating HSCs when they engraft into hematopoietic organs. Cell coupling with ECM proteins allows for the transmission of force and biochemical signals between the ECM and the cell through mechanotransduction, ultimately affecting numerous cellular processes, such as phenotype changes and cell differentiation. Specifically, under in vitro settings, HSPC differentiation skews toward myeloid lineage with stiffer hydrogels (35 kPa) and increased collagen and laminin content in the ECM. 
HSCs respond to ECM mechanical properties through mechanotransduction pathways, including intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), PIEZO1, and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [ ]. Integrins regulate cell–cell and cell–matrix interaction through binding with ICAM-1. Loss of ICAM-1 decreases long-term HSC quiescence and repopulation capability and increases differentiation into myeloid lineage progenitor cells. Intracellular Ca2+ levels transduce mechanical signals by mechanically gated ion channels like PIEZO1, which is transcriptionally expressed across various immune cell subsets. HSCs biased toward myeloid cells show higher intracellular Ca2+. The PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, together with MEK/ERK, also responds to mechanical signals to provide a balance between HSC dormancy and activation. 
Recent studies have developed ex vivo BM niche models by constructing three-dimensional (3D) biomaterial-based cell culture systems to mimic biochemical and biophysical components of the BM niche for studying HSC development. In 3D cultures, HSCs alone or co-cultured with supporting cells are cultured as spheroids or embedded in ECM tethered polymer scaffolds, decellularized ECM matrices, or natural and synthetic hydrogels. Biomimetic systemssuch as perfusion bioreactors mimic blood flow to provide shear stress, nutrient delivery, and waste removal. Additionally, organ-on-a-chip models integrate various ECM components, stiffness levels, cell types, soluble factors, and vascular systems into one multi-parameter model. Together, these studies show mechanical and molecular cues regulate HSC function, where these biomimetic models provide valuable mechanistic insights into HSC maintenance, differentiation, and myelopoiesis.

IV.

## MODULATION OF MONOCYTES AND MONOCYTE-DERIVED CELLS BY BIOPHYSICAL FACTORS
In peripheral tissues, monocytes and their derived cells are exposed to significant mechanical cues. Current research primarily focuses on the impact of ECM stiffness, shear stress, and stretch on monocyte behavior, including migration, adhesion, differentiation, and function. Mechanical forces are integral to regulating monocyte function, from their initial adhesion and transmigration to their differentiation and migration within tissues. Understanding these mechanoregulatory mechanisms provides valuable insights into the role of monocytes in health and disease, particularly in inflammation, cancer, and tissue remodeling. This section discusses the effects of both extrinsic and intrinsic mechanical or biophysical signals on monocytes and their derived cells, with an emphasis on in vitro studies.
A.

## Response to ECM substrate stiffness
Alterations in the stiffness of the tissue microenvironment are observed in aging and in numerous diseases and immunological conditions. These changes in stiffness substantially influence monocyte biology and functions, such as adherence, differentiation, and cell–cell interactions. The recruitment of circulating monocytes to ECs plays a critical role in the initiation and pathogenesis of atherosclerosis. The stiffness of the ECM regulates the EC monolayer integrity and permeability and significantly influences the interactions between ECs and monocytes. Generally, 20 kPa is considered the physiological stiffness level in arteries. With aging, vessel wall stiffness increases to 40 kPa, elevating endothelial monolayer permeability and promoting inflammatory extravasation. This mechanomodulation is correlated with the expression and clustering of VCAM-1 and intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), which activate downstream effectors such as GTPase Rho and Rho-associated kinase (ROCK). 
Human monocytes cultured in stiff, elastic hydrogels display proinflammatory polarization and differentiation toward DCs, in contrast to those cultured in viscoelastic matrices. This mechanically induced cell differentiation can be blocked by inhibiting a myeloid-specific isoform of phosphoinositide 3-kinase, known as PI3K-γ. When monocytes differentiate into macrophages on hydrogels with different stiffness, the macrophages become rounder, smaller, and less phagocytic on more viscous substrates. 
ECM stiffness can also impact the differentiation and cellular functions of monocyte-derived macrophages and dendritic cells. For example, higher stiffness, which mimics fibro-inflammatory environments, shows upregulated activation of major glucose metabolic pathways, enhanced expression of the proinflammatory cytokine IL-12, and downregulated expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) in DCs. 

B.

## Role of hemodynamic forces on monocyte fate
Mechanical cues play a critical role in monocyte function during circulation within blood vessels and subsequent extravasation into tissue niches. Blood flow and shear stress can directly activate monocyte mechanosensors (e.g., Piezo1), modulating adhesion, migration, and inflammatory cytokine production, thereby influencing immune surveillance and pathological vascular inflammation. Furthermore, monocytes express adhesion molecules (e.g., integrins, selectins, and chemokine receptors) that bind ligands on ECs. These interactions mediate physical adhesion and indirectly modulate monocyte behavior through EC-mediated mechanisms ( ). 
 Specifically, high shear stress in blood flow directly activates monocytes through Piezo1 mechanoreceptors, triggering pro-inflammatory responses such as enhanced adhesion to ECs, phagocytic activity, and cytokine release, which are attenuated by shear stress reduction therapies. In addition, heat shock protein 70 (HSP70), HSP90, and HSP105 are also involved in the inflammation caused by shear stress. Shear flow contributes to the progression of various pathological conditions, such as infectious diseases. Intracellular pathogens can utilize monocytes as vehicles for dissemination within the body, and mechanical factors might influence the pathophysiology of these infections. For example, THP-1 monocytes infected with Chlamydia pneumoniae produced more IL-1β under shear compared to static conditions. Furthermore, microfluidic techniques provide 3D-perfused endothelial vessels formed by human ECs embedded within a collagen-rich matrix. Higher concentrations of collagen led to increased vascular permeability and enhanced extravasation. 
Cells sense and respond to mechanical cues during extravasation. Cytoskeletal proteins, such as actin and non-muscle myosin II, orchestrate rapid remodeling in cytoskeletal architecture to promote immune cell motility. Monocytes exhibit distinct migration modes upon differentiation into macrophages, depending on the tissue environment. Mesenchymal migration relies on specific cell–ECM adhesion, whereas amoeboid migration requires minimal cell–ECM attachment and is observed in three-dimensional (3D) ECM environments. Endogenous hormones and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) can induce tissue mechanical changes that further modulate monocyte function. For instance, interstitial fluid flow and hydrostatic pressure within tissues can regulate monocyte polarization. This is particularly evident in solid tumors, where monocytes encounter elevated interstitial fluid flow due to inflammation or aberrant vascularization. Such mechanical forces are transduced via integrin/Src-mediated signaling pathways, highlighting the interplay between mechanical and biochemical cues in monocyte regulation.

C.

## Response to mechanical stimuli (stretch/compression/hydrostatic pressure)
Cyclic strain, a hemodynamic force arising from pulsatile and hydrostatic blood pressures, deforms blood vessel walls and significantly impacts the morphology of ECs, thereby influencing the adhesion of monocytes. Larger stretches on ECs resulted in increased adherence of monocytes, and this effect was still noticeable after 24 h. Cyclic strain can also modulate the phenotype and function of monocyte-derived macrophages. High strain magnitudes might discourage monocyte-derived macrophage adhesion and promote pro-inflammatory response. When cells are subjected to different levels of mechanical strain, the forces exerted on the cytoskeleton lead to the selective activation of mechanosensors(e.g., stretch-sensitive ion channels) and the unfolding of actuated proteins. 
Piezo1 ion channel functions as a mechanosensor in myeloid cells, triggering a proinflammatory response. Mechanical stimulation of monocytes and macrophages leads to the selective expression of proinflammatory and chemoattractant mediators in a PIEZO1-dependent manner. Furthermore, monocytes sense mechanical stress via PIEZO1 and upregulate their defensive functions in response to the unique mechanical environment of the breathing lungs. The PIEZO1-dependent stabilization of HIF-1α in recruited monocytes and macrophages directly activates cytokine-encoding target genes and promotes glycolysis, thereby maintaining the inflammatory potential needed during acute infection.
The responses of monocytes to mechanical cues have been summarized ( ). In addition to passively experiencing exogenous cues, monocytes and monocyte-derived cells can also apply mechanical forces to the pericellular matrix and neighboring cells. For example, mature DCs exhibit greater stiffness than immature DCs. This cellular mechanical change plays a critical role in activating T cells. During monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation and M1/M2 polarization, cells become stiffer to minimize energy dissipation during mechanosensitive activities. Therefore, mechanical signal-based crosstalk between monocyte cells and other cells warrnats further research to gain insights into mechanobiology. Additionally, spatial confinement in tissue niches after migration significantly affects monocyte differentiation and function. Confined environments impact actin polymerization, which influences monocytes' epigenetic landscape and chromatin compaction, thereby modulating their role in inflammation and immune responses. 
 

V.

## THERAPEUTIC IMPLICATIONS OF MONOCYTE MECHANOBIOLOGY IN DISEASE
Understanding the mechanobiology of monocytic cells and related cells could provide insights for the design of biomaterials. For example, increasing the stiffness of gelatin nanoparticles from 4.12 to 9.8 MPa allows for faster and greater internalization by macrophages. Furthermore, hydrogels with a stiffness of 106 kPa can maximize the phagocytic capacity of macrophages in the treatment of periodontitis. In addition to modulating the stiffness of biomaterials, there are accumulating studies discovering potential molecules to regulate the mechanobiological signaling of monocytes and related cells to intervene in specific diseases ( ).
 A.

## Cancers
1.

## Solid tumors
The impact of matrix stiffness on chemosensitivity has been previously investigated in triple-negative breast cancer cells and hepatocellular carcinoma cells using 2D hydrogel systems, demonstrating that a stiff microenvironment promotes drug resistance in tumor cells. In addition, increased ECM stiffness leads to chemoresistance in osteosarcoma. Another recent study suggested that matrix softening (i.e., mechanical rescue) could potentially halt or reverse malignant transformations, thereby enhancing drug efficacy. This process is attributed to reduced solid stress from the matrix and downregulation of cell mechano-transductors, such as YAP1. Recent studies are beginning to uncover the therapeutic implications of regulating mechanobiology in tumor-associated myeloid cells (TAMCs), as they are among the most important immune cell populations in the tumor microenvironment and significantly influence the effectiveness of immune checkpoint blockade. 
Increased stiffness of cancer provokes macrophage differentiation toward tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) phenotype. Potential therapeutic strategies are emerging to control the tumor by targeting the mechanobiology of TAMs. The first approach involves disrupting the interaction between the physical ECM and TAMs. For instance, focal adhesion kinase (FAK) inhibition has been shown to reduce the number of TAMs, thereby promoting tumor responsiveness to immunotherapy. The second approach involves directly altering the physical microenvironment of the tumor. For example, using renin–angiotensin system inhibitors represents a promising method for reducing tumor stiffness. Moreover, TGF-β plays a role in converting fibroblasts into cancer-associated fibroblasts, which upregulates ECM fibrosis and increases tumor tissue stiffness. 

2.

## Non-solid tumors
Physical cues from microenvironments have emerged as critical regulators of non-solid tumor biology, including growth kinetics and drug sensitivity to chemotherapy. A 3D niche-based biophysical screening method has been developed to assess the impact of matrix stiffness on the progression and drug sensitivity of different human myeloid leukemia subtypes. Matrix softening leads to increased resistance of myeloid leukemia cells to conventional chemotherapy, which contrasts with the behavior observed in solid tumors. 
Furthermore, chemotherapy-induced mechanical changes in leukemia cells may significantly affect overall blood rheology. Chemotherapy-induced cell death increases myeloid leukemia cells' stiffness, potentially arising from dynamic alterations in the actin cytoskeleton, which could influence vascular flow within the microcirculation. Furthermore, a decrease in leukemia cells' mechanical properties impacted chemotherapy's effectiveness. 

B.

## Wound healing
The coordinated crosstalk between MSCs and inflammatory cells plays an important role in successful tissue healing. Specifically, tissue regenerative (M2) macrophages recruit MSCs and modulate their differentiation following the removal of pathogens or debris by inflammatory (M1) macrophages. Notably, soluble factors, such as IFN γ, IL-4, and IL-13, act synergistically with substrate stiffness to regulate macrophage polarization. The macrophages exhibit predictable behaviors that can be fine-tuned by modulating their physical substrate, displaying anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes in response to stiffness conditions of 10 kPa compared to 0.1 kPa. However, when macrophages are cultured in a stiffer matrix with 50 kPa, they show M1 phenotypes. TRPV4 and Piezo1 have been characterized in macrophages, both of which can transfer physical stimuli to induce the influx of extracellular Ca2+ and activate Ca2+-dependent signaling pathways. TRPV4 is an indispensable regulatory channel in the M1 macrophage polarization spectrum in response to increased stiffness. The depletion of the potential downstream regulator of the TRPV4, YAP, inhibits macrophage inflammation. During ischemia, the stiffness of the microenvironment activates Piezo1 in macrophages, while myeloid cell-specific Piezo1 deletion promotes angiogenesis and reverses impaired revascularization. Furthermore, selectively suppressing Piezo1 expression or function in macrophages may help avoid severe foreign body responses. 
MSCs have been demonstrated to exhibit greater osteogenic differentiation when cultured on stiff substrates. However, stiff hydrogels have been observed to stimulate macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, which is unfavorable for bone regeneration. The cellular communication between MSCs and macrophages plays a pivotal role in bone regeneration processes. Therefore, it is crucial to identify the optimal biophysical properties, including stiffness and viscoelasticity, to provide the optimal microenvironment for regeneration. Pathological scar formation is one of the most common complications during wound healing. Application of controlled tension to a wound leads to pathological scar formation in mice, implying a significant role of mechanical tension in this process. This mechanical signal modulates the adhesion ability of monocytes on ECs by increasing E-selectin expression in ECs. Additionally, E-selectin-targeted siRNA liposome nanoparticles have been shown to reduce pathological scar formation. 

C.

## Atherosclerosis
Myeloid cells participate critically in all stages of atherosclerosis, from its onset to progression, and there is a positive correlation between arterial stiffness and the proliferation and migration of these cells. Among myeloid cells, monocytes arrive first at the site during atherogenesis. When monocytes and macrophages infiltrate atherosclerotic plaques, they take up oxidized low-density lipoproteins and transform into “foam cells.” These foam cells play a crucial role in the secretion of inflammatory mediators, contributing to the progression of atherosclerosis. The therapeutic strategies for atherosclerosis prevention and treatment include reducing the recruitment of myeloid cells to plaques and promoting macrophage phenotype alteration, apoptosis, and efferocytosis. β-Aminopropiontrile has shown promising efficiency in reducing the number of macrophages in the plaque by resisting arterial stiffness. Furthermore, the recruitment of circulating monocytes to ECs is pivotal for the initiation and pathogenesis of atherosclerosis, where increased arterial stiffness potentiates this pathological interaction through mechanosensitive signaling pathways. Specifically, increased matrix stiffness promotes monocyte adhesion to ECs by downregulating the microRNAs miR-126 and miR-222, which target VCAM-1 and ICAM-1, respectively. Inhibiting these miRNAs abolishes the stiffness-induced upregulation of VCAM-1/ICAM-1, highlighting their critical roles in mediating matrix stiffness-dependent endothelial adhesion. Apart from arterial stiffness, hypertension also contributes to the regulation of monocyte adhesion and migration, increasing the susceptibility of both small and large arteries to atherosclerosis. 
Piezo1 activation in monocytes and macrophages contributes to atherosclerosis by mediating the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, a crucial event during atherogenesis. Specifically, Piezo1 senses oscillatory pressure in myeloid cells and triggers pro-inflammatory responses. Mechanical activation of monocytes and macrophages stimulates the expression of pro-inflammatory and chemo-attractive mediators, all of which depend on Piezo1. Additionally, TRPC3 inactivation favors plaque regression. In contrast, TRPV1, another mechanosensitive channel, exhibits an atheroprotective effect by reducing foam cell formation. Owing to the discovery of pharmacologic activators and inhibitors of these mechanosensitive channels, they may potentially become highly promising targets for future therapies. 

D.

## Fibrotic lesions
1.

## Pulmonary fibrosis
The lung affected by pulmonary fibrosis exhibits a rigid, rough, and stretched condition due to the deposition of ECM. This stiffness impairs the debris-clearing ability of macrophages. Surface roughness promotes alveolar macrophages toward an M1 phenotype via the integrin–NF-κB pathway. Stretching activates NLRP3 inflammasomes and releases inflammatory factors in alveolar macrophages. In addition, bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis causes abnormal changes in the lung's mechanical environment, and Piezo1 activation in myeloid cells promotes the autoinflammatory disease process. Mechanistically, Piezo1 triggers endothelin 1 (EDN1) expression via Ca2+ influx, leading to hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) accumulation and inflammation in lung myeloid cells ( ). 
 
2.

## Renal and liver fibrosis
In human fibrotic kidneys, the expression level of the Piezo1 protein is significantly upregulated. Piezo1 in myeloid cells contributes to the progression of renal fibrosis by increasing macrophage infiltration via CCL2–CCR2 signaling and mediating the inflammatory response via intracellular calcium-induced calpain activation. Conversely, myeloid-specific Piezo1 deletion alleviates renal fibrosis by inhibiting macrophage infiltration and M1 polarization. Like renal fibrosis, Piezo1 is upregulated in hepatic fibrosis and plays a role in the stiffness-dependent efferocytosis of macrophages. Pharmacological activation of Piezo1 can enhance the efferocytosis of macrophages and promote the resolution of inflammation and fibrosis. 
In summary, while therapeutic strategies targeting either ECM mechanics or mechanobiological pathways show promise, their clinical translation hinges on distinct considerations. ECM-modifying interventions (e.g., stiffness-tunable biomaterials, enzymatic ECM remodeling) excel in localized applications (e.g., cardiac patches) but face limitations in systemic delivery and long-term mechanical stability. In contrast, pharmacological targeting of mechanosensitive pathways (e.g., Piezo1 inhibition with GsMTx4/Dooku1 or epigenetic modulation via HDAC inhibitors) enables systemic intervention and has demonstrated efficacy in vascular calcification and fibrotic diseases. However, these approaches must overcome signaling redundancy and off-target effects. Emerging solutions—such as smart biomaterials for localized drug release, nanoparticle-based targeted delivery, or engineered monocytes with tuned mechanosensitivity—may bridge these gaps. Prioritizing diseases where mechanical drivers dominate pathogenesis (e.g., atherosclerosis, fibrosis) will accelerate viable therapies, while advances in single-cell mechanomics and targeted delivery systems could refine precision.

VI.

## CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES
Mechanical cues are essential for modulating myeloid cells in physiologic and pathological environments. The ECM around the cell microenvironment provides structural support and 3D biochemical and biophysical cues that affect cellular behaviors. Despite this, most research still relies on 2D cell culture systems that fail to capture the physical complexity of tissues. Future research investigating cellular behavior in a 3D context will achieve a deeper understanding of the role of mechanical cues in physiological conditions. Additionally, integrating mechanical forces as a critical dimension in cell and tissue behavior will be essential for advancing the development of immune therapies, drug delivery systems, and scaffold materials.
Diseases can induce biophysical changes in myeloid cell niches, impacting the functions and activities of these cells. However, the intracellular pathways that convert these mechanical signals into cellular responses remain largely unidentified, with new pathways being discovered only sporadically. Gaining a deeper understanding of these intracellular mechanotransduction mechanisms could pave the way for developing molecular therapies targeting myeloid cells or affected tissues, offering potential strategies for treating various chronic diseases.
