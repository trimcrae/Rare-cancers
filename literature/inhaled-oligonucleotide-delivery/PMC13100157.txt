## Abstract
Streptococcus pneumoniae (the pneumococcus) is a human bacterial pathogen and the major cause of bacterial pneumonia, which can further develop into sepsis. The pneumococcus has evolved over 107 antigenically-distinct serotypes that are defined by the chemical composition of its capsule; its major virulence factor and the main protective antigen within the vaccine. Owing to its capsule diversification, certain serotypes are known for being more effective at either colonization or invasiveness. It has historically been accepted that these differences are due to the chemical properties of the capsule itself, whose biosynthetic genes are encoded in a single capsular polysaccharide (cps) operon. Here we show that infection outcomes can also be the result of a serotype’s subtle, natural variations within the regulatory region which results in unique transcriptional control of the cps locus. We propose that these data explain, in part, how this pathogen might rapidly evolve its capsule expression to accommodate evolutionary pressures introduced from pneumococcal vaccines, without having to rely on genetic recombination.
Subject terms: Bacteriology, Bacterial genetics

Here, the authors show the mechanism by which Streptococcus pneumoniae can, through natural variation of a capsule transcriptional control element, alter capsule size and the course of serotype-specific infection with implications for vaccine escape.

## Introduction
Streptococcus pneumoniae (the pneumococcus) is a Gram-positive coccus and one of the world’s most debilitating pathogens, killing over 1 million per year globally, 300,000 of which are children under the age of 5. Many of these fatalities are a result of S. pneumoniae being the leading bacterial cause of lower respiratory tract infection worldwide, resulting in more deaths than all other etiologies combined , . The general course of disease begins with the asymptomatic colonization of the nasopharynx, followed by dissemination into the lungs, resulting in pneumococcal pneumonia, the disease for which it is named. From the lungs, the pneumococcus can spread into the bloodstream, causing bacteremia, and may then accumulate in the liver, spleen, heart and brain, the latter resulting in highly fatal meningitis , . These disease states are commonly referred to as “invasive pneumococcal disease” (IPD); however, the pneumococcus is also the dominant cause of less threatening mucosal illnesses, including sinusitis and otitis media . Currently, 107 serotypes have been described, each defined by the antigenically-distinct chemical composition of its capsule, a major pneumococcal virulence factor that enables the bacterium to evade immune detection and phagocytosis. Certain serotypes have consistently been associated with a higher likelihood of causing IPD following colonization , . This phenomenon has historically been explained by the varying ability of these serotypes to be recognized by resident phagocytes in the liver and spleen, which ultimately leads to clearance of the bacteremic state and enables the categorization of serotypes into high virulence (HV) versus low virulence (LV) serotypes . However, there is now mounting evidence to suggest that control over the amount of capsular polysaccharide that is produced, or its ‘thickness’, can also regulate phagocyte recognition, and therefore IPD severity – .
The capsule is the major active component of the pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCVs), which cover subsets of the most prevalent IPD-causing (HV) serotypes (e.g., PCV-7, PCV-13, PCV-15, PCV-20, and most recently, PCV-21) – . Although PCVs have been highly effective at reducing IPD , their widespread usage imposes a strong selective pressure, driving the emergence of strains capable of evading vaccine-induced immunity. For example, vaccine introduction can cause a shift in the distribution of serotypes - a phenomenon known as ‘serotype replacement’ - where non-vaccine type pneumococci (NVTs) replace vaccine types (VTs) in the population . These novel variants are commonly referred to as ‘vaccine escape’ strains . For all but two serotypes, the capsule biosynthesis genes are located within a single large capsular polysaccharide (cps) operon (the cps locus) and controlled by a single upstream regulatory region and promoter . Vaccine escape strains usually arise following the mutation or acquisition/loss of cps operon encoded genes, which can lead to changes in capsule structure, and therefore, antigenicity, by altering either its chemical composition or linkages. This can include single cps genes , or more frequently the entire cps locus can be replaced, referred to as ‘capsule switching’ or ‘capsule replacement’ . Of note, not all pneumococcal genomes are compatible with all serotype capsules, as evidenced by the dependency of growth on capsule type and the lack of certain switches occurring in natural populations , .
Another potential mechanism of vaccine escape that has historically been overlooked is alteration of the cps promoter and its regulatory region , . In support of this hypothesis, recent publications from our lab and others have suggested that pneumococcal pathogenicity can indeed be dictated by slight changes within the upstream regulatory region of the cps promoter that controls cps transcription , , , or an RNA leader sequence that controls cps translation . In support, although changing the entire cps regulatory region can alter capsule thickness, phagocytosis and infection outcomes , , we discovered that changing just 3 nucleotides within a small, 37 base-pair cps cis-regulatory element, the 37-CE, was sufficient to drastically alter the severity of pneumococcal disease . This change in virulence occurred by modifying the interaction between the transcription factors (TFs) SpxR and CpsR with the 37-CE , previously shown to regulate pyruvate oxidase (SpxB) and glucose-mediated capsule production, respectively , . Importantly, the 37-CE is highly variable between strains belonging to different serotypes . Given this information, the natural variation within the capsule upstream regulatory region between serotypes, including the 37-CE, demands further investigation into how intra- and inter- serotype transcriptional regulation of capsule expression can influence infection outcomes, independent of the antigenicity of the capsule. The answers to these studies may increase our understanding of vaccine escape mechanisms.
Here, using the original cps sequences deposited by Bentley et al. , we sought to investigate the relative contributions of natural variations within the 37-CE between serotypes to the regulation of capsule expression and their respective propensities to cause infection. Using the classic HV strain D39 as a genetic foundation, which expresses a serotype 2 capsule , , we found that replacing the 37-CE with certain natural variants from other serotypes affected SpxR and CpsR direct binding, capsule size, phagocytosis and ultimately their ability to accumulate in specific organs during bacteremia. When the capsule was switched to a LV capsule (19C) similar variations were seen. Together, this work demonstrates that in addition to the chemical composition (antigenicity) of serotype capsules, variation within transcriptional regulatory elements such as the 37-CE can also dictate infection outcomes. By extension, these findings suggest that in addition to changing its capsule serotype-specific antigenicity, the pneumococcus can potentially escape the selective pressure imposed by vaccines by slightly altering its regulatory sequences by only a few nucleotides.

## Results

## Capsule regulatory transcription factors SpxR and CpsR bind with variable affinity to the natural serotype variants of the 37-CE promoter control element
Previously, we described a distal cis-regulatory element (the 37-CE) that controls capsule expression during infection through the activities of two TFs: SpxR and CpsR . We found that minor changes to the more variable 5’ sequence within the 37-CE (the 21-CE), which encompasses the overlapping binding sites for SpxR (SpxR1) and CpsR, can lead to either an abrogation or enhancement of SpxR and/or CpsR binding. These alterations had profound effects on infection . Examination of the 90 original deposited serotype genomes revealed 13 unique 21-CE sequences (we designated arbitrarily in this work as sequence #1-13; Supplementary Fig.  ). We hypothesized that these naturally occurring sequences may confer variation in serotype regulation of capsule expression during infection.
To begin to address this hypothesis, we first determined if SpxR and CpsR bound to these unique cis-elements with variable affinities using Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assays (EMSAs). To perform these assays, we purified recombinant DNA-binding Domain (DBD) of SpxR (SpxRDBD) or CpsR (full-length protein). For SpxR, full-length protein could not be used as most of it would not enter the 4% non-denaturing resolving gel. Results with addition of either a fixed, non-saturating concentration of protein (3 µM), or titrations ranging from 0 to 5 µM concentrations suggested a wide range of affinities, with sequences resulting in SpxR binding only, CpsR binding only, both binding, or neither binding to various degrees (Fig.  ; Supplementary Fig.  ). As these are in vitro binding assays other factors that might influence affinity in vivo are absent, such as the histone-like protein HlpA that we have found also interacts with the 37-CE sequence . Nevertheless, they provide an indication of the variable intrinsic ability of these TFs to recognize these unique sequences.
 Based on differential binding patterns and the fact that full-length SpxR had difficulty entering a non-denaturing gel matrix, a subset of 8 oligos (representing 21-CE sequences 1, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8, 10, 13; Fig.  ) were used to further interrogate binding in solution using fluorescence polarization (FP) with full-length SpxR. These 8 sequences were chosen based on their diversification in binding either SpxR or CpsR. Of note, CpsR was not used in our FP studies because the size of the CpsR dimer relative to the size of the DNA oligo containing its binding site was previously determined to be too small for this assay . Using FP, full-length SpxR binding exhibited statistically significant differing affinities between the 8 different sequences, ranging from 189.7 nM Kd to 574.8 nM Kd (Fig.  ). Results generally paralleled that of the gel shift data with Seq. #1 exhibiting the weakest binding (574.8 nM Kd) and Seq. #6 the strongest (189.7 nM Kd). Importantly, these numerical differences in affinity can translate into large differences in vivo due to occupancy and cooperative binding (see refs. , ).

## Swapping one serotype’s 37-CE with another affects capsule thickness in a growth condition-dependent manner
Considering SpxR and CpsR control capsule expression through the 37-CE sequence and that these TFs bound to serotype-associated 21-CEs (the less conserved region of the 37-CE) with variable affinities in vitro (Fig.  ), we next asked if swapping one serotype’s 37-CE for another ‘foreign’ serotype 37-CE was sufficient to affect capsule expression. For clarity, hence forth, we will use the term ‘thickness’ to describe changes in capsule expression that alter the amount of capsule on the bacterium’s surface, a feature that greatly affects the ability of the pneumococcus to colonize and cause invasive disease . To address this question, we created isogenic 37-CE variants in the serotype 2 strain, D39, where it’s 37-CE sequence (harboring it’s inherent 21-CE sequence #6; Supplementary Fig.  ; Fig. ) was replaced with another foreign serotype’s 37-CE sequence. Capsule thickness was then measured using a modified, quantifiable, FITC-dextran exclusion assay to specifically account for pneumococcal cell growth (see Methods) . Foreign introduced 37-CE sequence numbers 1, 3, 4, 8, 10, and 13 (Fig.  ) were used and the resulting isogenic mutants referred to as D39+seq1, D39+seq3, etc. These strains were sequenced and found to have the intended cps locus sequences. In addition, we found 5 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in other parts of the genome we deemed to be inconsequential to these studies. These SNPs were shared amongst all 37-CE variants, but not present in the original WT (see Methods and Supplementary Table  ). We also found an additional 2 SNPs unique to the D39 + seq13 strain, and one unique to the D39 + Seq3 and the D39 + Seq. #8 strains. Non-synonymous SNPs were deemed unlikely to result in phenotypic changes by examination of the predicted atomic structures in AlphaFold (Supplementary Table  ). Given these results we therefore used these strains in subsequent assays as the variant genomes were otherwise identical to one another.
 Because the transition from colonization to invasive disease is thought to be accompanied by a change in capsule thickness , , we used two different growth conditions to represent the variable conditions of the airways versus blood to assess this trait: 1) aerobically (achieved with 180 rpm shaking) to attempt to mimic the airways, and 2) anaerobically (achieved with static growth in an anaerobic chamber) in the presence of 5% v/v fetal bovine serum (FBS) to attempt to mimic the blood. Both the carbon source (glucose) and genetic background (serotype 2 D39) were kept constant, as altering either one of these can influence capsule thickness background autonomously , . The wild-type D39 strain (WT), D39+seq1, D39+seq4, D39+seq10, and D39+seq13 were tested.
Results indicated that under aerobic conditions, D39 WT, D39+seq1 and D39+seq13 exhibited similar capsule thickness, whereas D39+seq4 and D39+seq10 had statistically significant lower capsule thickness than D39 WT as measured by dextran exclusion (Fig.  ; See Methods). We then verified by qRT-PCR that, indeed, D39 + seq4, D39+seq10 and D39 + seq13 RNA levels were less than wild-type D39 when aerated (using the cpsA gene as template; Supplementary Fig.  ), which demonstrated that RNA correlated with capsule size. Surprisingly, capsule size differences were reversed under anaerobic conditions, with D39+seq4 and D39+seq10 being of similar thickness to WT, and D39+seq1 being of lower thickness than WT (Fig.  ). Finally, the D39+seq13 strain trended more towards WT (which harbors 21-CE Seq. #6) levels under anaerobic conditions than under aerobic conditions.
Taken together, these data demonstrate that swapping the 37-CE sequence of one serotype to another ‘foreign’ serotype’s 37-CE is sufficient to alter its capsule thickness in a manner that was dependent upon the growth condition. The conditions used here reinforce the idea that spatiotemporal regulation of capsule expression during infection through SpxR/CpsR could be influenced by oxygen availability . However, these in vitro observations might not necessarily correlate with in vivo differences due to the absence of influential, tissue-specific host factors such as nutrient changes or the presence of the immune system.

## Changing the 37-CE sequence to that of another natural serotype’s affects phagocytosis
Although not the sole factor in dictating phagocytosis, the capsule plays a major role in preventing it . It follows that a thicker capsule may inhibit phagocytosis to a greater degree than a thinner one. Since we demonstrated that natural variants of the 37-CE sequence can alter capsule size in vitro, we hypothesized that, by inference, these changes would result in differences in phagocytosis. To test this hypothesis, we monitored uptake of mKate2-expressing D39 WT, an isogenic cps deletion mutant that cannot make capsule (∆cps), and 37-CE variants by the immortalized murine macrophage cell line RAW264.7 using flow cytometry. As expected, addition of cytochalasin D blocked uptake of D39 WT cells (Fig.  ). The ∆cps strain exhibited near 90% uptake, whereas D39 WT (harboring Seq. #6) exhibited only 4.2% uptake, reestablishing the capsule as the major barrier to phagocytosis . Several 37-CE variants were statistically different from WT, with Seq. #10 exhibiting a 1.6-fold increase over WT in phagocytosed cells (Fig.  ). This result is consistent with the fact that less capsule was present in the Seq. #10 variant and paralleled our in vitro aeration data (Fig.  ). Conversely other 37-CE variants showed an increase in phagocytosis, for example the strain with the Seq. #8 exhibited a 1.5-fold decrease in phagocytosis compared to wild-type and a 4-fold decrease compared to Seq. #10. In summation, natural variations within the 37-CE sequence significantly alter phagocytic uptake, which is a capsule-dependent phenomenon, suggesting that natural 37-CE variation between serotypes can dictate phagocyte escape in vivo.
 

## Swapping one naturally occurring serotype’s 37-CE for another affects tissue-specific infection
Although we demonstrated that natural variation between serotypes in the 37-CE sequence can control phagocytosis in vitro, such observations can be poor predictors of in vivo outcomes. To investigate the influence of the same serotype-specific 37-CE sequences on in vivo outcomes, we used two murine infection models: sepsis and pneumonia. The sepsis model directly exposes the pneumococcus to the tissues that are essential for its capsule-mediated clearance: namely, (i) the blood itself, which contains high numbers of circulating phagocytes, and (ii) the liver and spleen, which previous studies have shown are important sites of capsule-mediated phagocytic clearance and contain high levels of bacteria during a sepsis model of infection , . Importantly, the liver and its resident macrophages have been deemed the primary, early bottleneck by which the pneumococcus is either cleared or escapes to become virulent in this model – so called ‘slippery strains’ (e.g. D39 serotype 2 used here is a slippery strain) . Furthermore, other work has demonstrated that the spleen acts as a major reservoir of pneumococci, ultimately prolonging bacteremia, with their accumulation peaking at 8-hours post-infection . We therefore assessed the contribution of natural variation in the 37-CE to in vivo outcomes at this 8-hour time-point, where resident liver macrophage-dependent clearance should be near completion for liver-captured ‘non-slippery’ serotypes . In contrast to the sepsis model, where capsule-dependent phagocytic clearance is critical in determining infection outcomes , previously published data suggests the capsule is not as important in lower airway colonization (pneumonia) due to the requirement for adherence in this organ – .
First, wild-type D39 37-CE was replaced with 37-CEs containing 21-CE sequences 3, 4, 8, 10, or 13 (i.e. strains D39+seq3, D39+seq4, D39+seq8, D39+seq10, and D39+seq13) and a forced inhalation intratracheal pneumonia model of infection was performed . No statistical differences between WT D39 and 37-CE variants were observed (Fig.  ), reinforcing the idea that the capsule plays a less critical role in determining outcomes in the lung – .
Next, we performed our sepsis model of infection. 5 ×106 colony-forming units (CFUs) were given via tail vein and at 8-hours post infection, blood, liver, and spleen were collected and CFUs were enumerated by plating. At 0-hours, CFU counts were similar and at 8-hours post infection blood CFU counts also remained similar between strains, with some lower CFUs in the seq-13, 10 and 8 strains (Fig.  ). Conversely, no CFUs were detectible in the liver at this time point, except for the WT (considered a liver escape serotype ) and the D39+seq13 strain (Fig.  ). As expected from previous work , CFUs of all strains were more abundant in the spleen than the liver at 8-hours. In the spleen D39+seq4 and D39+seq10 CFUs were significantly lower than WT (D39 + seq6) where only 30% of mice had countable bacteria above the limit of detection (LOD, > 125 CFU/mL) (Fig.  ). Conversely, we found that the WT D39, and D39+seq13, D39+seq3 and D39+seq8 all exhibited CFUs in over 80% of the experimental mice (Fig.  ). These results indicated that natural allelic variation the 37-CE sequence can greatly influence splenic and liver clearance, and by association the overall disease outcome of blood infection. We also note that only some of our in vitro data reflected our in vivo data here (e.g. D39+seq4 and D39+seq10 strains), but not perfectly, suggesting factors other than the SpxR-CpsR-37CE signaling axis are also contributing to cps control (e.g. nucleoid-like proteins ).

## Natural 37-CE allelic variation between serotypes can control tissue-specific expression of the cps locus during infection
To confirm that the observed effects of serotype-specific 37-CE sequences on tissue-specific infection were due to changes in cps locus expression, we monitored and quantified the cps promoter activity of 37-CE switch strains during infection using our red light-emitting click-beetle luciferase reporter system . Reporter strains were constructed by stably integrating 37-CE switch cps promoter fusions to the CBRluc gene (Pcps:CBRluc) into an autonomous region of the chromosome (outside the cps locus as per ref. ; Fig.  ). Importantly, all reporter strains were constructed in the D39 WT background; therefore, the virulence of each reporter strain is identical, because the native cps promoter and regulatory region are the same across strains. Mice were infected with reporter strains as in Fig.  , except CFU enumeration and luciferase measurements were taken at 2- and 4-hours post-infection. Earlier time points were chosen to facilitate greater detection of luminescence signal, because as previous studies and our data in Fig.  demonstrates few WT pneumococci remain in the liver 8-hours post tail vein injection .
 Mice were infected with 5 ×106 of either the D39 + WT CBRluc, D39 +seq10 CBRluc, or D39 +seq13 CBRluc reporter strains via tail vein, then the blood, lungs (i.e. bacteria that entered the lung from the blood, which should not be considered representative of pneumonia), liver and spleen were isolated for CFU enumeration and quantification of luminescence at 2- and 4-hours post-infection. At both time points, there was no statistical difference in CFUs between strains recovered from the blood (Fig.  ), nor the lungs, liver, or spleen (Fig.  ). Crucially, the consistency in virulence between strains allows for meaningful comparisons to be made between luminescence measurements. Luminescence was quantified using an IVIS imager, then normalized to the CFUs within each organ. Strikingly, the luminescence signal from D39 +seq10 CBRluc was undetectable in the liver at both 2- and 4-hours post-infection (Fig.  ), despite producing similar CFUs to the other reporter strains (Fig. ). In contrast, the D39+seq10 CBRluc strain had signal comparable to WT in the lungs and spleen at both 2- and 4-hours post-infection, suggesting that differential organ-specific regulation of cps activity is conferred by the seq10 37-CE in this strain. To eliminate the possibility that this strain had acquired mutations in the liver that prevented expression of CBRluc, the D39 +seq10 CBRluc strain was isolated from liver homogenates after 4 hours of infection, regrown in BHI medium, and then its luminescence activity compared with the original bacterial stocks (Fig.  ).
These results show that natural inter serotype variation of the 37-CE can modulate tissue-specific control of cps expression. This point is prominently exemplified by Seq. #10, which demonstrated similar cps promoter activity to WT in the lungs and spleen, but no detectable activity in the liver at 2- and 4-hours post-infection, despite having similar CFUs in comparison to the other reporter strains tested. These data correlated with our in vitro ‘aeration condition’ (Fig.  ) and phagocytosis data (Fig.  ), demonstrating that Seq. #10 (from serotype 19C) produces less capsule under specific conditions and greater susceptibility to phagocytosis, respectively.

## Construction of strains to investigate the contribution of capsule chemical composition versus its regulation in pneumococcal infection
Historically, the chemical and structural composition of serotype-specific polysaccharide, and therefore, the antigenicity of each serotype has been considered the dominant driving force behind the variation in their tendencies to cause certain types of infections (e.g., IPDs versus colonization) , – . Thus far, we have provided evidence which suggests that natural variation in the 37-CE sequence of different serotypes can influence capsule thickness and tissue-specific disease outcomes by controlling expression of the cps locus through the activities of the TFs SpxR and CpsR. Insightful investigations into the contribution of serotype antigenicity to infection outcomes have been highly valuable in identifying mechanisms that explain, in part, inter-serotype variation , , . In these studies, and others, isogenic ‘capsule switch’ strains are constructed, where the entire cps locus, including the cps promoter and its upstream regulatory region, is replaced with that of another. This means that in these strains two variables have been changed: 1) the cps locus itself and 2) the cps promoter and its regulatory region. Therefore, our findings up to this point necessitated further dissection of the relative contributions of these two distinct variables to disease outcomes.
To begin dissecting the contributions of capsule chemical and structural composition (through the cps biosynthesis genes) and the regulation of capsule expression (through the upstream, non-coding regulatory region of the cps promoter) to infection outcomes, we constructed precise capsule switches within the cps coding region that did not include the cps donor upstream regulatory elements historically included with switch mutants , , (dexB to cpsA region; Fig.  ). Then, we swapped the 37-CE in these strains with other foreign serotype’s 37-CEs. To accomplish this, we devised a multistep strain construction (Fig.  ). Step 1: We started with the serotype 2 strain, D39 (WT) as the parent strain. Step 2: Then, we created the aforementioned classical capsule switch mutants in which the entire cps locus, its promoter, and its upstream regulatory region are swapped with that of another serotype (annotated D39 + 19C etc.; Fig.  ). Step 3: Next, we ‘fixed’ these strains by swapping the promoter and its upstream regulatory region back to that of the D39 parent strain (e.g., annotated D39 + 19C-fixed (19C-f)). Step 4: Finally, we swapped the 37-CE in these strains with a foreign serotype 37-CE found in serotypes 6A (sequence 1), 6B (sequence 13), and 19C (sequence 10) (e.g. annotated as D39 + 19C-f + 6A-37-CE etc.; Fig.  ). After strain construction we again sequenced whole genome constructions from Steps 2 and 4 as described above and compared them to both the D39 parent genome and to the donor genome (i.e. serotypes 6A, 6B and 19C). Data showed that in the cps locus the intended sequences were present (Supplementary Fig.  ). In addition, outside the cps locus we found additional SNPs and regions of homologous recombination that originated from the 19C donor genome, which importantly, were >99% shared across all 19C switch isolates (Supplementary Fig.  ). Therefore, these strains could all be compared with confidence that the difference between them was the 37-CE sequence (Supplementary Table  ; Supplementary Fig.  ). These strains were therefore used in subsequent experiments.
 

## Some serotype switches exhibit capsule thickness phenotypes
Not all serotype genomes are compatible with all serotype cps loci , , however not all combinations have been elucidated. Our capsule switches were constructed using the serotype 2 strain, D39 (the same strain as in all previous experiments in this work and previous work ) as the parent (recipient), with serotypes 6A, 6B, and 19C as capsule donor strains. These serotypes were selected due to their variability in capsule chemical composition (Supplementary Fig.  ) and differences in disease outcomes. Serotypes 6A and 6B share a similar chemical composition, only differing in a single linkage in their repeat unit (Supplementary Fig.  ) and are both generally associated with higher mortality . 19C is a less common member of the serotype 19 family that is distinguished from other serotype 19 members due to an added glucose moiety in its capsule structure . Although 19C can be invasive in children, it is generally accepted as a low virulence (LV) serotype due to its low impact on global pneumococcal disease , . Incompatibility between recipient capsule switch genomes and donor cps loci could manifest as defects in capsule expression, potentially due to metabolic constraints imposed by the recipient genome. Considering this and the pivotal role of the capsule and its thickness in pneumococcal pathogenesis, we therefore assessed capsule production and capsule thickness of switch strains prior to performing infection studies.
Initially, we screened for capsule production using dot blot analysis on our wild-type ‘Step 1 strains (Supplementary Fig.  ) and then our ‘Step 2’ switch strains (Supplementary Fig.  ). WT strains showed the expected cross reactivity to serotype-specific antibodies save one of the two 6A strains tested, which was used as a negative control (strain 6A12; Supplementary Fig.  )). For the capsule switch strains, dot blots showed that the 6A and 6B switches (D39 + 6A and D39 + 6B) were essentially defective in capsule production, whereas the 19C switch produced comparable capsule amounts to the D39 serotype 2 parent recipient (D39 + 19C) (Supplementary Fig.  ). Quantification of capsule thickness using our dextran exclusion method revealed that the D39, 6A, and 19C wild-type strains and the D39 + 19C switch strain all had similar capsule thickness (Fig.  ). In contrast and nicely paralleling our dot blot data, the D39 + 6A and D39 + 6B switch strains had significantly lower capsule thickness than D39 WT and their respective 6A and 6B parent strains (Fig.  ). Finally, we verified our capsule switch strains using a classic Quellung assay (Supplementary Fig.  ). Data from Quellung experiments parallelled data from dot blots and imaging showing a lack of capsule production in 6A and 6B switches compared to the 19C capsule switch.
Key to moving forward with our infection studies, the data here show that the classical 19C capsule switch strain exhibited similar capsule thickness compared to D39 WT, suggesting that the D39 genome and metabolism is compatible with the 19C cps locus. Therefore, going forward the 19C switch strains were deemed suitable for assessing the relative contribution of capsule composition versus its regulation in pneumococcal infection.

## Both regulation of capsule expression and capsule chemical composition can dictate tissue-specific infection
To investigate the contribution of capsule chemical composition (antigenicity) versus its regulation to infection outcomes, and to test whether natural 37-CE variants could govern infection in the presence of a foreign capsule, we repeated our sepsis model of infection using the D39/19C capsule switch strains depicted in Fig.  . Importantly, the 19C serotype switch strain (i.e. D39 + 19C) was capable of making comparable amounts of capsule to the parent D39 WT strain, whereas a serotype 6A or 6B switch struggled to synthesize polysaccharide in the D39 genetic background (Fig.  ; Supplementary Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). First, we determined if our infection model could recapitulate the well-described phenomenon of serotype-specific disease outcomes by comparing the survival of wild-type strains of: serotype 2 (D39), 6A, 6B, 19C, and serotype 2 with the 19C capsule switch strain (i.e. Step 1; Fig.  ) in the blood, spleen and liver 8-hours post-infection. Reestablishing its suspected naturally mild invasiveness , , significantly fewer CFUs of 19C WT were recovered from all tissues compared to that of D39 WT, 6A WT or 6B WT. The low virulence 19C phenotype was especially notable in the blood and liver, where 4 and 3 mice did not have any detectible CFUs in these two respective organs (Fig.  - compare light blue triangles to one another). Importantly, the 19C parent (19C WT) and 19C capsule switch strain (D39 + 19C) exhibited no statistical differences between them. Consistent with prior observations with the 19C WT strains , , the low infectivity of the 19C phenotype was therefore transferred with its cps locus and regulatory region to the recipient D39 Serotype 2 strain. The fact that a serotype 2 or 19C genetic background showed no significant difference in our infections in any organ or the blood reinforced the importance of capsule antigenicity during infection (Fig.  , compare light blue triangles to brown diamonds), and also gave us a platform to re-test the importance of the 37-CE in D39 but with a different (19C) capsule.
 Given these data, we then assessed the ability of the regulatory region to affect these phenotypes using the Step 3 and Step 4 strains (Fig.  ). When the WT D39 promoter and upstream regulatory region was reintroduced into strain D39 + 19C (i.e. ‘fixed’, Step 3; Fig.  ; strain D39 + [19C-f]), no statistical difference was reached, reinforcing the data from Fig.  and suggesting that the 19 C capsule was still dominating clearance during sepsis, despite now harboring a D39 promoter sequence (Fig.  ; compare brown diamonds to red circles). However, when comparing detectible CFUs, there were differences seen in organs with these same strains, hinting that ‘fixing’ the entire promoter region back to that of serotype 2 indeed effects survival. More pronounced statistical differences were seen in the blood when we compared the classic switch strain (D39 + 19C, brown diamonds) to the ‘fixed’ strains harboring serotype 6A (Seq. #1, yellow squares), serotype 6B (Seq. #13, dark blue triangles), and serotype 19C (Seq. #10, purple triangles) natural 37-CE variants in the blood (Fig.  ), where all strains were significantly different than the parent (D39 + 19C).
Finally, to convince ourselves that just the 37-CE sequence changes were enough to vary infection outcomes in the 19C capsule switch strains, which harbored a LV capsule instead of the HV capsule of serotype 2 we only compared changes between the 37-CE variants (last four infection strains on the right in the graphs; red circles to purple triangles). With these comparisons, statistical significance was reached in the liver between the 6A 37-CE versus 6B and 19C 37-CE variants, with the percentage of organs with detectible bacteria paralleling these data (in only 20% of animals CFUs were found in 6B and 19C 37-CE variants compared with 70% in 6A; Fig.  , below graph). In the spleen these data were essentially flipped (Fig.  ) where in only one mouse no CFUs were detected with the 19C 37-CE sequence compared to almost all of them harboring no CFUs in the liver (compare the green boxes between Fig.  and Fig.  ). This result might indicate that as per ref. , in the liver the antigenicity of the capsule is more important than the promoter driving capsule size, at least in the specific case we examine here. Moreover, these data suggest that the 19C 37-CE sequence (and by inference SpxR and CpsR regulation changes) can compensate for a survival defect originally introduced in the capsule switching. More broadly, our data here show that chemical composition of the capsule is important in dictating LV versus HV infection outcomes; however, in the presence of either a LV or HV capsule the genetic makeup of the upstream capsule regulatory region and 37-CE sequence governed by SpxR/CpsR regulation is also critical to tissue-specific infection.

## Discussion
Here we show through biochemical analyses, genetic manipulations and infection studies that in addition to the precise chemical composition and structure of capsule polysaccharide (i.e. serotype-specific antigenicity), the severity of pneumococcal infection is also driven by the degree of capsule expression through SpxR and CpsR regulation. More specifically, we show that natural variations that can occur between pneumococcal serotypes within the 37-CE regulatory sequence are sufficient to alter infection outcomes during sepsis in a tissue-specific manner. Finally, our data suggest that manipulation of the 37-CE, and general regulation of the capsule expression, may be an evolutionary strategy used by the pneumococcus, and potentially other capsule-producing pathogens, to overcome vaccine induced immunity in both wild-type capsule or capsule replacement strains.
Work by An et al. demonstrated that ‘low virulence’ (LV) pneumococcal serotypes (e.g. 19C) are recognized by resident liver macrophages (Kupffer cells) through the asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGR) and therefore cleared, whereas other ‘high virulence’ (HV) or so-called ‘slippery’ serotypes, are not recognized by this receptor (e.g. serotype 2) and can therefore escape capture. In addition, others have shown that splenic macrophages and the ability of the pneumococcus to replicate within them (presumably a capsule-dependent event as well) play a pivotal role in bacteremia clearance , . Here we present the added knowledge that natural variation within the 37-CE sequences between serotypes can, in turn, dictate infection outcomes during sepsis in the spleen and liver, likely though direct interactions with resident phagocytes. For example, switching the D39 WT 37-CE (Seq. #6) to that of 19C (Seq. #10) or 7A (Seq. #4) resulted in no detectable colonies in the liver 8-hours post-infection, whereas 6B 37-CE sequence (Seq. #13) still resulted in detectible CFUs (Fig.  ). Some in vivo studies were predicted by our in vitro data, for example the result that D39 WT harboring either a Seq. #10 or Seq. #4 produced lower amounts of both cps mRNA and capsule in vitro (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ), and would indeed be predicted to be more susceptible to phagocytic clearance during sepsis. Interestingly, when we tested the ability of macrophages to phagocytose these same strains, only WT D39 harboring Seq. #10 (from serotype 19C) demonstrated increased uptake compared to WT D39 (which harbors Seq. #6), whereas WT D39 harboring Seq. #4 (from serotype 7A) exhibited a decrease in uptake (Fig.  ). Correspondingly, these findings extrapolated to our reporter studies, where WT D39 harboring Seq. #10 was the only variant that exhibited no cps promoter activity in the liver, while conversely exhibiting robust activity in the spleen and blood (Fig.  ). In contrast, WT D39 harboring either its inherent Seq. #6 or Seq. #13 from serotype 6B resulted in detectible cps operon activation in the liver (Fig.  ). These data suggest that changing the 37-CE sequence within the promoter to the 19C/Seq. #10 sequence is enough to effectively blunt the induction of capsule synthesis at 2- and 4-hour timepoints in this specific tissue (the liver), promoting the increased clearance seen in Fig.  and suggests that 19C clearance might not be entirely dependent on polysaccharide/ASGR recognition. These findings also show that 37-CE-dependent changes in capsule expression are sufficient to influence clearance during bacteremia and can essentially convert a HV strain to a LV strain, without changing the chemical composition of its capsule.
Direct evidence of this argument is also seen when we examine differences within our capsule switch strains (Fig.  ), were we saw differences in liver versus spleen clearance among the 37-CE changes. This was especially evident in the almost complete reversal of the number of liver and spleen organs with detectable CFUs, where there were only two animals that had detectible levels of 19C 37-CE (sequence 10) harboring bacteria in the liver, and conversely all but one had a bacterial burden in the spleen – an organ that is responsible for re-seeding the bacteremia state , , (Fig.  versus Fig.  ; last column with purple triangles – green boxed). A similar trend was also observed with the 6B 37-CE (sequence 13). This suggests that if a LV strain can alter its capsule thickness during infection it might be cleared from the liver though receptor mediated recognition, but can still survive in the spleen to sustain infection. When taken together our data show that despite the new LV capsule placed into the D39 serotype 2 genetic background, the exact 37-CE sequence is still critical to driving clearance in a tissue-specific manner. In this example, the fact that addition of the 19C 37 CE (sequence 10) seemed to improve splenic survival for this strain suggests that despite genomic changes that often result in fitness costs with a capsule switch, small alterations within the cps regulatory region could provide the necessary compensatory changes to maintain or even increase such as strain’s infectivity (and potentially vaccine escape). It will thus be of great future interest to switch the capsule type to a HV structure, such as that from serotype 1, and again assess the influence of 37-CE in tissue-specific survival.
One question that arises from this work are the discrepancies between the in vitro and in vivo studies. For example, if we compare Seq. #4 and Seq. #10, both exhibited less cpsA RNA and capsule thickness when aerated in vitro (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ), however they diverged in phagocytosis assays (Fig.  ) and their ability to activate the cps promoter in the liver (Fig.  ). Further, both sequences exhibited greatly enhanced clearance in the spleen Fig. . One explanation for these differences would be the stark variation in SpxR and CpsR binding between 37-CE sequences, whose upstream signals (ligands) are not currently understood, but likely are very different from one another and therefore play varying roles in influencing capsule production during tissue-specific infections. Continuing with our example above, SpxR indeed has a higher affinity for Seq. #4, and CpsR for Seq. #10 (Fig.  ), which in and of itself might account for the aforementioned tissue-specific differences observed with these sequences. However, in the absence of known ligands of these transcription factors and the lack of knowledge of other factors that could be influencing SpxR and CpsR interaction with the 37-CE (e.g. nucleoid-like proteins, see below ) with our data presented here we can only speculate as to the how the complex interplay at this one regulatory site might be fine-tuning environmentally-driven capsule expression changes. We envision such changes can ultimately result in the pneumococcus tipping the balance in favor of either CpsR or SpxR binding, who compete for the same site (Fig.  ), resulting in increased capsule production when antigenicity of the capsule is currently the dominant pneumococcal ‘Achilles heel’. Again, we can turn to Fig.  versus  and observe the reversal of detectible bacteria by simply changing the 37-CE by a few nucleotides.
In this work we have expanded on our previous findings , showing that natural pneumococcal 37-CE variation between different serotypes can result in varying infection outcomes. However, when the capsule antigenicity remains constant (i.e. there is no capsule switch, Fig.  ), changes within the 37-CE and regulatory sequences might become even more important, especially when considering reported intra-serotype variation. Indeed, a recent publication has emphasized the importance of intra- serotype variation using serotype 23F in colonization ; a serotype that is present in the current iteration of the vaccine . Moreover, evidence also exists in human studies, where another group has identified serotype 23F as having undergone global clonal expansion within the 37-CE sequence element after vaccine introduction, and importantly, in people who have already received the vaccine. Interestingly the described 23F vaccine escape strains did not switch their capsules (i.e. undergo capsule replacement), which reinforces our hypothesis here that they could be escaping immunogenetic responses by altering their capsule thickness (through 37-CE alterations) instead of undergoing capsule replacement . Another example has concluded that the highly virulent 19A serotype, also contained within the vaccine, has also become vaccine resistant without switching its capsule . When combined with our work here, these data suggest a novel way exists for the pneumococcus (and possibly other bacterial species) to subvert vaccine introduction; through simply changing a few nucleotides to alter the regulation of its capsule synthesis , rather than accommodate a new foreign, and often metabolically cumbersome, cps locus (which inadvertently comes with additional genome SNPs and mutations; Supplementary Fig.  ).
Although the capsule replacement strategy to escape vaccine immunity has proven effective for the pneumococcus, as previously mentioned it can also cause problems for the pathogen. Capsule switches have the potential to be metabolically taxing given certain unsupportive genetic backgrounds , , which might explain why many combinations do no not occur in nature . One reason that certain capsule switches exhibit incompatibilities could be that the recipient serotype genome might not possess the correct transporters or genes required for processing of the serotype-specific capsule specialized polymers, suggesting that there are differences either in the suite of transporters and/or catabolic pathways between serotypes . Furthermore, if specific sugars required for capsule synthesis are limiting in a particular host niche (e.g., the nasopharynx, where we expect natural capsule switching is most likely to take place), metabolic flux could be impacted, whereby general metabolites such as glucose or acetyl-CoA would then be rerouted for energy production at the expense of making more capsule - ultimately resulting in enhanced phagocyte exposure and pneumococcal clearance. These ideas are further illustrated in these studies by the inability of the D39 genome to harbor the almost identical 6A and 6B cps loci , as evidenced by these strains being unable to produce capsule (in vitro) of comparable thickness to serotype 2 (D39) WT (Fig.  ). Possible explanations for this could be differences in the D39 serotype 2 versus 6A/6B capsule chemical building block availability and associated enzymes to build the polymer, where 6A and 6B require galactose (galactopyranose) but D39 serotype 2 does not (Supplementary Fig.  ). In contrast, the rhamnose sugar is shared between the D39 (serotype 2) and 19C capsule structures which could lead to them being more amenable to harboring each other’s cps loci . Another explanation could be compatibility with upstream signaling of the SpxR-CpsR-37-CE axis, and importantly other regulators of the cps locus not emphasized in this report (see discussion below). Taken together, such metabolic incompatibilities might force certain serotypes to instead alter their capsule thickness simply by changing 3-4 nucleotides within the 37-CE to escape vaccine effectiveness, rather than take a ‘metabolic hit’ by incorporating an entirely new operon and metabolic burden with it.
The 37-CE is not the only regulatory region which can undergo considerable variation both between serotypes (inter-serotype), and as discussed above, within the same serotype (intra-serotype) , , , , . Moreover, and eluded to above in the discussion, several transcription factors other than SpxR/CpsR have been shown to bind and influence regulation of the cps locus by ours and other research groups, each of which has the potential to integrate different signaling pathways into its regulation , , – . For example, FabT which regulates fatty acid biosynthesis and MalR which regulates maltose responses and utilization can both directly interact with the cps promoter . In addition, we have shown previously that the histone-like protein HlpA interacts with the cps promoter and 37-CE , and therefore likely plays a role in another complex layer of cps transcriptional regulation which would be an interesting topic of future research , . Given this knowledge, it is quite plausible that there are interdependencies between these other cps regulatory factors and the 37-CE specific regulators SpxR and CpsR such that some 37-CE/regulatory region combinations are more or less compatible with alternative capsule structure needs. This point is illustrated when we changed the 19C capsule regulatory sequence in the capsule switch strain back to D39 serotype 2 and observed a reduction in infectivity of the fixed strain in the spleen (Fig.  ; compare brown diamonds to red circles), and then further changes observed when the 37-CE is subsequently altered.
In the future, a deeper examination into the 37-CE and other changes in natural vaccine escape populations of specific serotypes will be required to biologically verify that, indeed, simplistic changes in capsule regulation and resultant alterations in capsule thickness can be used as an exclusive means to avoid the immune system - without having to undergo the potential metabolic deleterious effects that come with an entire capsule switch to another serotype. It is concerning that such minute changes within the cps and especially the 37-CE regulatory regions have the potential to facilitate vaccine escape for the available S. pneumoniae vaccines, and unfortunately suggests that other capsule-harboring bacterial pathogens (e.g. oral streptococci and group B streptococci) might use a similar escape strategy with any future vaccine introductions. Our data here reinforce the present urgency to develop new therapies that cover all pneumococcal serotypes.

## Methods

## Statistics and Reproducibility
Both male and female mice were used in these studies. For general statistical analyses and reproducibility, all experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates, unless otherwise noted. Statistical analyses were conducted using Prism (GraphPad Software) when appropriate. The specific statistical tests used, the number of subjects per experiment, whether data are presented as mean or median, and whether variability is shown as SEM or SD are indicated in the figure legends or individual figure panels. A P value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. No data were excluded from the analyses. Experiments were not randomized, and investigators were not blinded to group allocation during experiments or outcome assessment. Sample sizes of mice for infections were determined based on previous studies from the Orihuela lab and/or Yesilkaya lab. Sample sizes for qRT-PCR experiments and kD determination by fluorescence polarization were determined by previous work from the Ulijasz lab . For all other samples sizes, e.g. imaging and phagocytosis assays, data were collected on different days until a satisfactory statistical significance was reached.

## Strain Construction
Bacterial strains, plasmids and DNA oligos used in this study are shown in Supplementary Dataset  .

## 37-CE variants
The 37-CE variations were introduced into the D39 genome (serotype 2, Leicester strain) using the Janus system as described previously . The generated Janus intermediate strain, 37-CE-J, selected on 500 µg/ml Kanamycin (Kan) was grown in 3 mLs of Todd-Hewitt broth with 5% Yeast extract (THY) (Oxoid) from −80 °C glycerol stocks for 2-3 hours, then incubated with 100 ng mL-1 of CSP-1 for 14 minutes before adding 250 ng of synthetic DNA containing the new 37-CE sequence (Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT), Coralville, IA). IDT gene block sequences are shown in Supplementary Dataset  . The cells were incubated with DNA for 1 hour before plating on Tryptic Soy Agar plates supplemented with 5% sheep blood (TSBA) and 200 µg/mL streptomycin. Successful 37-CE variations were selected for kanamycin sensitivity and streptomycin resistance, and further confirmed using sanger sequencing (Azenta Life Sciences, Burlington, MA). To enhance accuracy of the dextran exclusion/capsule quantification experiments and enable detection of phagocytosed bacteria in the phagocytosis assay, an hlpA-mKate2 construct was amplified from a strain harboring the insertion using primers hlpA-up-F and hlpA-down-R . The double stranded DNA PCR hlpA-mKate2 amplicon was then purified (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) followed by transformation into each 37-CE variant strain.

## CBRluc reporter strains
CBRluc reporter constructs were made using the pPP3 vector as previously described . Pcps was amplified from the 37-CE variants and ligation inserts generated using the melt and reanneal method as described previously , with primers (P17-20). The first PCR reaction was with primers P17 + P20 and the second with P18 + P19. The two PCR products were melted and reannealed to generate the 5’ KpnI and 3’ BamHI restriction sites. These products were then phosphorylated using T4 polynucleotide kinase (Promega Co., Madison, WI). Each 60 µL phosphorylation reaction was composed of 1 x T4 Kinase Buffer, 1 mM ATP, T4 Kinase, 48 µL of DNA and brought to 60 µL using molecular grade water. The phosphorylation reaction was carried out overnight in a 37 °C water bath, then further purified using a Qiagen PCR purification kit and eluted in ddH2O. The phosphorylated fragments were ligated in pPP3 vector that was previously digested with KpnI HF and BamHI HF (New England Biolabs (NEB), Ipswitch, MA) using T4 Ligase (Promega, catalog number M1804),10X ligation buffer, and incubated at room temperature for 2 hours. Ligation reactions were then transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells and plated on LB plates supplemented with carbenicillin (50 µg mL−1). Plasmids were then extracted from E. coli using a Qiagen Miniprep Kit and then sequenced for inserts using Azenta Life Sciences (Genewiz) (Burlington, MA). Plasmids that harbored the correct insert were then transformed into S. pneumoniae D39 (serotype 02) rpsL. Tetracycline (3 µg mL−1) was used for selection and integration at the bgaA locus was verified by PCR using primers pPP3-tet-FlpPP3-tet-R and pPP3-bgaFlpPP3-bga-R (see ref. ).

## Capsule switch strains
To construct the capsule switch strains, we used the Janus system to replace the cps locus in its entirety in D39 (strain D39 cps-J; from ref. ). The cps-J strain was then transformed with genomic DNA (gDNA) of other serotypes. Serotypes 6A and 6B were kindly shared by Dr. Carlos Orihuela, and serotype 19C by Dr. Moon Nahm (University of Alabama at Birmingham) (Step 1, Fig.  ). The gDNA was isolated from each serotype following the method described by Echlin and Rosch , with the exception that strains were grown in THY medium (Oxoid, UK). The intermediate strain, D39 cps-J, was grown in THY from a freezer stock and then incubated in CSP-1 for 14 minutes before adding 10 µL of highly concentrated purified gDNA ( ~ 1 µg mL−1) . The cells were incubated with the foreign serotype purified gDNA for 1.5 hours before plating on Tryptic Soy Agar plates supplemented with 5% sheep blood (TSBA) and 200 µg mL-1 streptomycin. Transformants were then screened for kanamycin sensitivity. Further confirmation of successful capsule switching was performed via whole genome sequencing (Plasmidsaurus, Eugene, OR). Successful switches (Step 2, Fig.  ) were assessed by performing western blot analysis on the product of the first gene in the cps operon; CpsA. Dot blot assays using monoclonal antibodies specific for the 6A and 6B capsules, and 19F antiserum (to detect the 19C capsule) was performed to confirm the capsule switch strains were expressing the expected capsular polysaccharide.

## Promoter ‘fixed’ variants in capsule switch strains
To change the regulatory region of the capsule switch strains, the sequence between dexB and cpsA (wzg) was first replaced with the Janus cassette (construct 1), then the Janus cassette was replaced with the desired final sequence (construct 2) to create the ‘fixed’ strains shown in Step 3 (Fig.  ). Each construct contained three PCR generated amplimers: a 5’ “upstream” amplimer of the dexB region, a “middle segment” amplified from wild-type or 37-CE mutated genomes, and 3’ “downstream” fragment that consisted of the cpsA region. The 5’ and 3’ fragments were first amplified from either 6A, 6B, or 19C capsule switch strains. For construct 1, the 3’ fragment was amplified using either primer pairs P1-P2 for 6A/6B, or P1-P11 for 19C. The 3’ fragment was amplified using primers P8-P10 for all templates. The “middle” fragment was amplified from a Janus cassette – containing strain using P4-P5 primers for 6A/6B, or P13-P5 for 19C. For construct 2 the 5’ fragment was amplified using either primer pairs P1-P3 for 6A/6B, or P1-P12 for 19C. The “middle” fragment was amplified from either D39 WT or the 37-CE variants using primers P6-P7 for 6A/6B, or P14-P7 for 19C.
Once amplified, each fragment was gel extracted using the QIAQuick Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen), then stitched together using overlap extension PCR to form the final constructs. The overlap extension PCR was carried out in two steps. First, the three fragments were combined at equal molar amounts in a PCR tube to serve as template in a 50 µL reaction with Q5 polymerase (NEB), Q5 reaction buffer, and dNTPs. This reaction was run following the Q5 protocol (NEB) and an annealing temperature of 69 °C was used for 10 cycles. Subsequently, Lift F and Lift R primers (P1-P10) were added to the reaction, the annealing temperature was lowered to 64 °C, and the reaction was run for an additional 25 cycles. The final constructs were gel extracted, then transformed in each corresponding capsule switch strain using the method described above. Successful transformants were selected for with streptomycin, then subsequently checked for kanamycin sensitivity. Finally, strains were verified by PCR before sequencing by Plasmidsaurus (Eugene, OR).

## Electrophoresis Mobility Shift Assay (EMSAs)
Initially, EMSAs were performed at a fixed protein concentration with each fluorescently labelled variant of the 37-CE and 21-CE. To obtain the fluorescently labelled, double-stranded DNA probes, the 37-CE and 21-CE oligonucleotides containing a 3’ 6-FAM modification were mixed with their corresponding unlabeled reverse complement oligonucleotide at a 1:1.1 ratio (unlabeled in excess). Each binding reaction contained 0.5 µM of 6-FAM labelled double stranded DNA probe, 3 µM of either CpsR or SpxR DNA Binding Domain (SpxRDBD) purified protein (expressed and purified as described in ref. ), 400 ng of poly dIdC (Sigma), 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.2), 5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM CaCl2, 0.1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM DTT. Molecular grade water was added for each reaction to reach a final volume of 10 µL. The binding reactions were incubated at room temperature for 20 minutes to allow for equilibration, then 9 µL of each reaction was loaded on an 8% acrylamide native gel (TGE) and run at 120 V for 40 minutes in 1 X TGE running buffer (25 mM Tris pH 8.3, 192 mM glycine, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8.3). Additionally, titrations were performed with each probe and an increasing concentration of purified protein (0 – 5 uM). EMSAs were imaged using an AZURE 400 Imager (Dublin, CA) with a Cy2 channel.

## Fluorescence Polarization (FP)
FP was performed three times, each time using the average of technical triplicates. Each binding reaction contained 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.2), 150 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM EDTA, 20 nM 6-FAM labelled double stranded 21-CE DNA probe (synthesized by IDT, Coralville, IA), and increasing concentrations of purified full-length SpxR protein (SpxR was expressed and purified according to ref. ). Each reaction was combined in a 255 µL master mix, then 240 µL of each master mix was transferred in three 80 µL of aliquots into a 384-well opaque (black) Corning NBS plate (Corning product number 3575) to obtain three technical replicates. The binding reactions were incubated at room temperature for 20 minutes to allow for equilibration. FP was measured using a Tecan Spark multimode microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland). Excitation and emission filters used were 495/10 nm and 520/10 nm, respectively. This process was repeated three times for each DNA probe to obtain three biological repeats. Kds were determined using a one linear regression, one site total model in Graphpad Prism.

## FITC-Dextran Exclusion Microscopy and Quantification
In preparation for microscopy analysis, starter cultures of the 37-CE variants with integrated hlpA-mKate2 were made. Briefly, strains were grown in 20 mL of THY medium (Todd Hewitt broth - Oxoid, 5% Yeast extract; Beckton Dickinson (BD), Franklin Lakes, NJ) statically in 5% CO2 at 37 °C until cultures reached mid-exponential phase (OD600 = 0.4 - 0.5). The cultures were then pelleted, resuspended in THY + 10 % (v/v) glycerol to an OD600 of 0.4, and aliquoted into 1 mL starter cultures. These were stored at −80 °C until needed. For each microscopy experiment, one 1 mL starter culture for each strain was thawed in a 37 °C water bath for 90 seconds. The cultures were then pelleted at 2000×g for 5 minutes, washed once with C + Y medium + 0.2% glucose (a liquid casein-based medium supplemented with yeast extract (Sigma-Aldrich) as per Aprianto et al. , then pelleted again. The pellets were then finally resuspended in 1 mL of C + Y medium +0.2% glucose with or without the addition of 5% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma) as indicated in the text, and then diluted in 9 mL of the same media. The starting OD600 of each 10 mL culture at this point was ~0.04. These cultures were then grown at 37 °C either aerobically (shaking at 180 rpm), or anaerobically (in an anaerobic chamber; Coy Lab Products Inc.; maintained at 2% H2, 20% N2, and 78% CO2) until they reached mid-exponential phase (about 3 hours to reach OD600 0.4-0.5). 1 mL of each culture was pelleted and washed twice with phosphate buffered saline (pH 7.4; PBS). Finally, the pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of PBS. 18 µL of the resuspension was mixed well with 2 µL of 10 mg mL-1 FITC dextran (Sigma; FD2000S-100MG). The microscope slides (Thermo Scientific, Fisherbrand Extra-Thick Microslides, catalog # 12-550-10) were coated with 0.01% poly-L-lysine (0.1 poly-L-lysine (Sigma) – diluted 1:10 with sterile molecular grade water). 100 µL of poly-L-lysine were added on each clean slide and allowed to sit for 10 minutes, then the excess liquid was aspirated off the slide. After the slides were allowed to air dry, 8 µL of the fluorescent mixture was transferred onto each poly-L-lysine-coated slide, then covered with a coverslip. The slides were imaged on a Nikon Ti2-E inverted microscope with X-Cite XYLIS XT720S Broad Spectrum LED Illumination System, DS-Qi2 CMOS camera, and a CFI Plan Apo DM Lambda 100X Oil NA 1.45 objective using both phase contrast and FITC channels with 100X magnification.
To quantify capsule thickness, an ImageJ macro program was used. In summary, the program measured the capsule area (FITC), then subtracted the bacterial area (phase contrast), and finally divided that number by the length of the chain analyzed to normalize the measurement to obtain capsule area(um)/length of bacterium(um). The program code can be accessed at the following URL: 10.5281/zenodo.18216823. Each day, 100 individual bacteria of each strain were quantified (50 per slide), and each condition was repeated three times on different days (three biological replicates total). For displaying images, the phase contrast channel was inverted to show bacterial bodies as white objects on the black FITC-excluded area. Statistically significant differences were determined in GraphPad Prism by comparing the means of each biological replicate using a One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s Multiple comparisons analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Quantitative real time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)
qRT-PCR was carried out as per Sheinheit et al. with minor modifications. Starter cultures were prepared and bacteria cultured as described above under “FITC Dextran Exclusion Microscopy and Quantification”. Briefly, pneumococcal strains D39 WT, D39 + seq13, D39 + seq4 and D39 + seq10 were cultured in C + Y medium +0.2% (w/v) glucose at 37 °C with 180 rpm shaking until the culture reached mid-exponential phase (OD600 = 0.4 – 0.6). An equivalent of 1 OD of bacteria was treated with RNAprotect bacteria reagent (Qiagen, Germany) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Pellets were stored at −80 °C until RNA was isolated using a RNAeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Two DNase digests were performed during the purification to remove contaminating genomic DNA: i) on-column DNase digest using the RNase-free DNase Set (Qiagen, Germany) during purification and ii) in-solution digest of 5 µg of the resulting purified RNA with TURBO DNase (Thermo Scientific catalog # AM2238), both as per the manufacturers’ instructions. Following the second DNase digest, RNA was cleaned up using the RNAeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) and eluted in DNase/RNase-free molecular-grade water. 1 µg of the purified RNA was reverse transcribed using the iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio-Rad catalog # 1708840/1) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Controls that lacked reverse transcriptase were included for each sample. Subsequently, cDNA was diluted 8-fold with DNase/RNase-free molecular-grade water and stored at −20 °C before use.
Primers used to determine relative expression levels of cps2A were designed previously by Sheinheit et al. and used in reactions with SsoAdvanced Universal Inhibitor-Tolerant SYBR Green Supermix (2x; Bio-Rad catalog 1725016). Reactions contained 500 nM of each primer and 1 µL of cDNA template and were performed in technical triplicates for each biological replicate. The housekeeping gene rplI was used as an internal control. The relative expression level of cps2A was calculated for each biological replicate using the cycle threshold (CT) value and the 2^(-Delta Delta CT) method . Three biological replicates were performed.

## Quellung assay
Quellung reactions were performed with the 19C switch strains using a modified version of Habib et al. . 3 mL of THY medium was inoculated with 19C WT and 19C capsule switch strains from freezer stocks and grown in 5% CO2 at 37 °C to mid-exponential phase (0.4-0.5 OD600). 1 µL of culture was transferred onto a microscope slide (Thermo Scientific, Fisherbrand Extra-Thick Microslides, catalog # 12-550-10), and 1 µL of Factor 19 F antiserum which cross-reacts with 19C capsule type (Cedarlane Labs / SSI Diagnostica, product # 16964(SS), Burlington, Ontario Canada) was transferred on top. The tip of the pipette was used to gently mix the culture with antiserum before adding a coverslip (Thermo Scientific, catalog # 22-170-374) on top of the sample. The samples were observed under a Leica DMIRB microscope at 100X magnification with oil immersion. Capsule swelling was observed and compared to negative control and positive control samples. 19C capsule switch strains with Type 2 antiserum (SSI), and D39 WT with Factor 19F antiserum were used as negative control reactions (i.e. no swelling observed). D39 WT with Type 2 antiserum was used as a positive control and induced expected capsule swelling.

## Dot blot analyses
Dot blots were performed using a modified version of a protocol previously published for serotyping pneumococci . 6A and 6B capsule switch strains were grown from freezer stocks as described above. 1 mL of mid-exponential phase culture was washed twice with PBS, then 5 µL of bacteria resuspended in PBS was spotted onto a strip of 0.45 µm nitrocellulose membrane (Thermo Scientific, catalog # PI88018) and let air dry for 15 minutes. The membrane was then blocked with 5% skim milk in PBS + 0.05% Tween 20 (Thermo Scientific, catalog # 50-493-594) (PBST) for 1-hour at room temperature, then blotted with either 6A or 6B monoclonal antibodies (from Dr. Moon Nahm, University of Alabama at Birmingham), or factor 19F antiserum which recognizes the 19C capsule structure (Cedarlane Labs / SSI Diagnostica, diluted 1:10,000 in PBST + milk and incubated for an additional hour. The membranes were then washed with PBST 3 times for 10 minutes each at room temperature before being incubated with secondary antibodies diluted 1:5,000 in PBST + milk and incubated for an hour. Finally, the membranes were washed again with PBST 3 times for 10 minutes each at room temperature and imaged using Azure 400 AI400-1060 imager (Azure Biosystems).

## Phagocytosis assays
Streptococcus pneumoniae D39 rpsL mKate2 variants (WT, Δcps, and 37-CE sequence variants 2, 3, 4, 8, 10, and 13) were cultured in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth supplemented with 20% calf serum to mid-log phase (OD₆₀₀ = 0.4) and stored as single-use aliquots at −80 °C. A working stock of Cytochalasin D was then prepared (PHZ1063; MW = 507.6 g/mol) by dissolving it in DMSO to a 10 mM stock and then diluted in culture medium to 10 µM for a working concentration. RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded at 1 × 10⁶ cells per well in 24-well plates with DMEM + 10% FBS and incubated (37 °C, 5% CO₂) for 12 h. Medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM prior to infection. Bacteria were added at MOI = 100 (1 × 10⁸ CFU/well). For inhibition controls, cells were pretreated with 10 µM cytochalasin D for 20 min and maintained in the medium throughout infection. After 3 h at 37 °C, cells were washed with PBS and detached by gentle flushing. Cells were stained with Live/Dead™ Fixable Aqua (1:1000, 5 min, RT) in FACS buffer (PBS + 2% calf serum + 5 mM EDTA) and analyzed using a BD FACSymphony™ A3. Live, single-cell gates were applied, and mKate2 fluorescence identified macrophages containing internalized S. pneumoniae. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo v10.10.0. Phagocytosis efficiency was defined as the percentage of mKate2-positive macrophages. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism v10. All experiments were performed in triplicate. The gating strategy is available as Supplementary Fig.  .

## Bioinformatics
37-CE sequences were downloaded from GenBank, which originally were published from Bentley et al. ref. . Extracted 37-CE sequences were arranged in FASTA format and uploaded into Clustal Omega online application to generate alignments and phylogenetic tree outputs.

## Sequencing and variant analysis of pneumococcal genomes
Beyond verifying desired mutations created in the 37-CE promoter of mutant strains, we also investigated the presence of potential spurious mutations elsewhere in the genome. To do so, we used the Breseq tool with default parameters to map Illumina reads (1.4 to 2.4 million read pairs, i.e. 2.8-4.8 million total reads, per strain) to annotated assembled genomes of their relevant WT strains and identified variants (i.e. SNPs, insertions, and/or deletions). In the case of strains with only 37-CE changes (i.e. Fig.  ), reads were mapped solely onto the assembly of our sequenced serotype 2 D39 WT reference strain. When compared to the wild-type D39 parent strain 5 SNPs were observed in all swap mutants that were consistent over all 37-CE strain constructions, and 3 SNPs which were specific to individual 37-CE variants. By assessment of gene translation consequences and atomic structure prediction (using AlphaFold ), the nature of these substitutions would not be expected to alter phenotypic outcomes, nor did they in the presented data. A summary of the sequenced genomes and SNPs are displayed in Table  .
In the case of capsule (cps) locus swapped strains (i.e. Fig.  ) we again used Breseq to identify mutations in the 37-CE switched strains relative to the parent D39 wildtype strain that was utilized. Resulting Breseq Variant Call Format (VCF) files containing all detected polymorphisms and indels, were parsed and reformatted for comparison in a Venn diagram (Supplementary Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ), generated using Interactivenn . For the 37-CE locus, locus tags of orthologs for pneumococcal dexB and cpsA were identified across all genomes using PanOCT . Using respective genomic coordinates from the start of dexB and the end of cpsA for each strain, the loci were extracted using Bedtools . These sequences were then aligned using Clustal Omega , followed by alignment plotting using Snipit (Supplementary Fig.  ). All genome assemblies generated with Prokka , and raw Breseq outputs for all comparisons are available at NCBI (Project ID PRJNA1401821 and website https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/1401821).

## In vivo studies

## Sepsis model
The significance of mutations and switches on pneumococcal capsule has been tested using mouse models of bacteremia. In vivo studies were carried out using 8–10-week-old female CD1 outbred mice, bred in-house in the Division of Biomedical Services at the University of Leicester, UK. The bacterial strains were grown in Brain Heart Infusion (BHI) broth (Oxoid, Thermo Scientific, UK) supplemented with bovine calf serum (Merck) in a 4:1 volume ratio to prepare the inoculum. For this study, a 100 μl inoculum containing 5 × 106 CFUs in PBS was administered through tail vein. To investigate the development of bacteremia in each mouse, approximately 20 μl of venous blood was collected at 8 hours post infection and viable counts were determined by plating serial dilutions on blood agar plates.

## Pneumonia model
C57BL/6 J mice, aged 6-8 weeks, were inoculated intratracheally by forced aspiration with Streptococcus pneumoniae D39 strain or isogenic cps promoter region mutants suspended in DMEM + 4.5 g/L glucose at 5 x 105 CFU. At 24 hours post-infection, mice were euthanized and blood collected following heart puncture for quantitation of bacteria. Mice were perfused with 10 mL of heparinized PBS and lungs excised and washed in PBS. Lungs were maintained in PBS on ice until their homogenization in PBS for CFU enumeration. Animal experiments were performed in accordance with UAB IACUC protocols.

## In vivo imaging
For imaging, 3 mice per strain were administered with approximately 5x106 CFU per mouse in 100 µl PBS through the tail vein. At 2- and 4- hours post-infection, anesthesia was re-administered, and luciferin (150 mg/kg – given in µl that equaled x10 of the mouse weight in Grams) was injected subcutaneously. Mice were then imaged using the IVIS® Spectrum in vivo imaging system (Perkin Elmer), capturing images at one-minute intervals over a 20-minute period. The peak bioluminescent signal was identified and analyzed by unpaired t-test. Following each imaging session, the whole bodies of the mice were scanned for 10 minutes each, after which they were euthanized, and lung, liver, and spleen tissues were collected for further imaging. Blood was collected by cardiac puncture. Immediately after imaging, the bacterial counts were quantified in the tissue homogenates to normalize the signal levels relative the colony-forming units. Organs were also scanned for 10 minutes to collect quantitative fluorescence data. To verify that the 19C 37-CE construct did not have a mutation preventing light production in the liver, 100 µl of liver homogenates from the D39 wild-type and 19C 37-CE reporter strains were grown in BHI for 24 hours, then regrown to an OD600 of 0.775 for D39 and 0.558 for 19C 37-CE (i.e. mid-log phase). Regrown cultures were then compared to the original bacterial stocks for luciferase activity and imaged in the IVIS system.
