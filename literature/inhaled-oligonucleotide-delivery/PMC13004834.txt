However, the specific role of DDX41 in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) has not yet been elucidated. The data from public databases show that DDX41 protein expression is elevated in OSCC tumor tissues and linked to poor prognosis. Loss of DDX41 in OSCC cells leads to an inhibition in the ability of tumor proliferation and invasion. Mechanistically, DDX41 undergoes liquid-liquid phase separation with STING, forming biomolecular condensates that potentiate PD-L1 upregulation through the STING-TBK1-NF-κB pathway. And, blocking DDX41 in the OSCC mouse model confirmed that inhibition of DDX41 decreased PD-L1-mediated immune escape via the STING-TBK1-NF-kB pathway. Flow cytometry analysis revealed significantly improved tumor immune infiltration upon DDX41 knockdown, as evidenced by altered immune cell populations. Finally, clinical sample analysis revealed that DDX41 expression is associated with poor prognosis in OSCC patients and correlates with downstream proteins. Our results identify a novel mechanism by which DDX41, functioning as a cytosolic DNA sensor, promotes PD-L1-mediated tumor immune escape in OSCC via sustaining the STING-TBK1-NF-κB signaling pathway, providing both a potential therapeutic target and diagnostic indicator for this malignancy.

## Introduction
Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is a prevalent form of malignancy, comprising about 90% of oral cancer cases, with its incidence and mortality rates continuing to raise significant concerns on a global scale , . The occurrence of OSCC is influenced by a mix of external elements like betel nut use, smoking practices, and inflammatory conditions, in conjunction with internal components such as aging and impairments in DNA repair . Treatment for OSCC generally involves a multifaceted approach, including a combination of surgical procedures, chemotherapeutic agents, radiation treatments, targeted therapies, and immunotherapies . Despite advancements in treatment strategies, the mortality rate of OSCC has not significantly improved over the past three decades, largely attributable to the highly invasive and recurrent nature of OSCC, delays in diagnosis, as well as social and economic factors . Thus, in addition to enhancing educational efforts targeting OSCC, it is imperative to explore more rational and effective therapeutic targets based on its underlying mechanisms of occurrence and progression, with the aim of improving the clinical cure rate of OSCC.
The DEAD-box helicase (DDX) protein family represents one of the most extensive groups of RNA helicases and functions as RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), often exhibiting pro-oncogenic phenotypes in various types of cancer – . Identified as a new DDX RNA helicase, DEAD-box helicase 41 (DDX41) is primarily resident in the nuclear region and contributes to cellular processes like resolving R-loops, the modification of small nucleolar RNAs, and the processing of ribosomal RNAs . The role of DDX41 in the context of malignant tumors has been increasingly unraveled in the last few years. Latest research findings suggest that DDX41 acts as a promotional element in renal cell carcinoma and colorectal cancer , . However, there have been no reports on whether DDX41 has a potential oncogenic role in OSCC. Moreover, DDX41 has a cytoplasmic role as a DNA sensor, regulating the cGAS-STING pathway to elicit an innate immune response against viral and other infectious agents . Additionally, DDX family members DDX1 and DDX6 can influence tumor immunity by regulating PD-L1 expression , . Therefore, we hypothesize that DDX41 may be associated with tumor immunity.
Tumor immunotherapy is an innovative approach that combats cancer by modulating the body’s immune system, and it has achieved remarkable progress in the treatment of various types of cancer . Among the various immunotherapies, the most prominent is the immune checkpoint therapy targeting Programmed Death-Ligand 1 (PD-L1). Inhibiting PD-L1 facilitates T cell activation, strengthening the immune system’s capacity to eliminate tumors . In current clinical oncology practice, anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody therapy has demonstrated promising therapeutic efficacy . However, due to factors such as PD-L1 expression levels, T cell dysfunction, and the microbiota, a portion of patients still develop resistance, leading to tumor progression . Therefore, exploring the molecular mechanisms that regulate PD-L1 could significantly address the shortcomings of current immunotherapy. Hence, investigating the potential association between DDX41 and PD-L1 will enhance our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms of PD-L1 expression and contribute to improving the efficacy of immunotherapy.
In recent years, the important regulatory mechanism of biomolecular phase separation has attracted much attention in the field of cell biology. It can precisely regulate the molecular activities within cell . The DDX protein family, as RBPs, has been proven to have the potential to undergo phase separation . In the adaptive immunity of cancer and immune checkpoint pathways, liquid-liquid phase separation condensates (LLPS) are often detected, and they play a crucial role in the regulation of tumor immune suppression . However, whether DDX41 is involved in this process has not been studied yet.
This paper is designed to assess the levels of DDX41 transcription and function within OSCC. Upregulation of DDX41 in OSCC is associated with poor prognosis, and it can promote the formation of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment through the STING-TBK1-NF-κB-PD-L1 signaling pathway in the form of phase separation. The findings of our investigation point to DDX41 as a highly promising candidate for use as a potential intervention target and a prognostic indicator in OSCC.

## Results

## Upregulation of DDX41 in OSCC is associated with poor prognosis
The current investigation focuses on characterizing the expression status of DDX41 in OSCC, confirming its downstream regulatory effects, and investigating the oncogenic impact of DDX41 in this context. As an initial step, we queried the TCGA repository to project the mRNA expression patterns of DDX41 throughout diverse cancer spectra. Statistical analysis indicated a predominant overexpression of DDX41 across numerous cancers, notably within the subset of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) (Fig. ). Following this, we carried out a correlation study to assess how DDX41 gene expression relates to overall survival (OS) for a range of cancers in the TCGA database. The study demonstrated an inverse relationship between the expression of DDX41 and the clinical outcome for patients across various cancer types, including HNSCC, as depicted in Fig. . OSCC, a distinct variant within the head and neck cancer category, shares numerous pathological, etiological, and therapeutic parallels with HNSCC. 
In a detailed examination, we scrutinized the mRNA levels of DDX41 in a set of 43 HNSCC and 32 OSCC tissue pairs, juxtaposed with their adjacent non-cancerous counterparts from the TCGA dataset. The examination showed a significant elevation of DDX41 mRNA levels in malignant compared to benign tissues for HNSCC as well as OSCC, as illustrated in Fig. C, E. Additionally, we conducted a similar analysis on tumor and normal tissues from different individuals in the TCGA database, which revealed that DDX41 mRNA expression was significantly higher in HNSCC and OSCC tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. ). These findings from the database reinforce the observation that DDX41 mRNA is upregulated in both HNSCC and OSCC patient groups. Consistent with expectations, DDX41 expression is negatively correlated with survival (Fig. ). Furthermore, the analysis of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve suggests that DDX41 has potential as a diagnostic biomarker for OSCC, as shown in Fig. .

## DDX41 enhances the proliferation, migration, and invasion abilities of OSCC cells
To determine the role of DDX41 in the pathogenesis of OSCC, we utilized four different oral squamous cell carcinoma cell lines—SCC4, SCC9, SCC15, and CAL27 and used a normal oral keratinocyte (HOK) cell line as a control. We then conducted a protein level analysis of DDX41 in these cell lines. The data uncovered a pronounced elevation of DDX41 protein in the SCC15 carcinoma cell lines when compared to the protein expression in the HOK cells (Fig. ). Therefore, we selected the SCC15 cell line for the subsequent research. To clarify how DDX41 affects the invasive and migratory processes in OSCC cells, we used siRNA and overexpression plasmid techniques to perform in vitro knockdown and overexpression experiments of DDX41. Subsequently, western blot analysis was performed to evaluate the transfection efficiency of siRNA and plasmids (Fig. ). Two days after siRNA and plasmid transfection, compared with the control group, the SCC15 cells with DDX41 gene knockdown and overexpression respectively showed abnormal morphologies (Fig. ). In addition, CCK-8 assay showed that after downregulating and overexpressing DDX41 respectively, the growth of SCC15 cells was significantly inhibited and significantly promoted (Fig. ). In subsequent analyses, both the scratch wound healing assay and the Transwell migration and invasion assays showed that after knocking down and overexpressing DDX41, the wound healing rate and migratory activity of SCC15 cells were significantly decreased and increased, respectively (Fig. ). These data suggest that DDX41 plays a role in enhancing the proliferative and migratory capabilities of OSCC cells. 

## DDX41 promotes activation of the STING-TBK1-NF-κB signaling pathway and expression of target genes
Next, we investigated the mechanisms by which DDX41 promotes OSCC progression. First, RNA-Seq was performed on control cells and DDX41 knockdown cells. Correlation analysis showed high consistency within groups (Fig. ). Volcano plots and hierarchical clustering analysis indicated that the suppression of DDX41 led to the upregulation of 202 genes and the downregulation of 211 genes (Fig. ). KEGG analysis indicates that the reduction in DDX41 expression may be associated with the NF-κB signaling pathway in OSCC cells (Fig. ). GO analysis revealed that DDX41 is functionally associated with biotic stimulus response, innate immunity, and regulation of NF-κB expression (Fig. ). Notably, after the downregulation of DDX41 expression, the expression of NF-κB pathway-related genes (such as TRAF5 and TNF) was significantly downregulated, along with the expression of NF-κB downstream genes (such as CD274 and BCL2) (Fig. ). Previous studies have demonstrated that DDX41 functions as a cytoplasmic DNA sensor, activating the cGAS-STING pathway by detecting exogenous viral DNA in the cytoplasm . Analysis of the DDX41 protein interaction network reveals its ability to activate the STING-TBK1-NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. ). As previously shown , the promoter region of PD-L1 contains an NF-κB-p65 binding site. We hypothesize that DDX41 promotes the expression of PD-L1 through the STING-TBK1-NF-κB pathway. 
WB analysis showed that knockdown of DDX41 resulted in varying degrees of downregulation of proteins involved in the STING–TBK1–NF-κB–PD-L1 signaling pathway in tumor cells (Fig. ). To determine the role of DDX41 in p65 nuclear translocation, DDX41 was overexpressed in SCC15 cells, and p65 localization was assessed by immunofluorescence. DDX41 overexpression significantly enhanced p65 nuclear translocation compared with controls (Fig. ). Next, to determine whether DDX41 regulates the transcriptional activity of the PD-L1 promoter, we performed ChIP using an anti-p65 antibody to enrich chromatin fragments bound by p65, followed by qPCR to quantify the enrichment of the PD-L1 promoter region in the immunoprecipitated DNA. The results showed that, compared with the control group, DDX41 overexpression significantly increased the enrichment of the PD-L1 promoter, suggesting that DDX41 may enhance promoter-associated transcriptional activity by modulating p65 binding to the PD-L1 promoter (Fig. ).
Next, we performed rescue experiments in which C176 and triptolide were used to inhibit the activation of STING and p65, respectively. Western blot analysis showed that C176 treatment markedly reduced p-STING levels and concomitantly decreased the expression of p-TBK1, p-p65, and PD-L1. In contrast, triptolide treatment significantly decreased p-p65 levels and suppressed PD-L1 expression (Fig. ). In addition, we performed rescue experiments in the murine tumor cell line MOC2. MOC2 cells subjected to the indicated treatments were co-cultured in vitro with T cells isolated from the lymph nodes of control mice, and flow cytometry was used to assess the activation of cytotoxic effector molecules in T cells as well as tumor cell viability. DDX41 overexpression suppressed IFN-γ activation in co-cultured T cells and promoted tumor cell survival; these effects were partially reversed upon inhibition of STING and p65 (Fig. and Fig. ). It suggests that DDX41 could play a role in the development of OSCC through the regulation of the STING-TBK1-NF-κB-PD-L1 signaling pathway.
To evaluate whether cytosolic DNA affects this signaling pathway, we transfected herring testis DNA (HT-DNA) into SCC15 cells and assessed the expression changes of proteins associated with the STING–TBK1–NF-κB–PD-L1 axis. The results showed that transfection with HT-DNA significantly upregulated the expression of DDX41, p-STING, p-TBK1/p-p65, and PD-L1 in tumor cells; however, this inducible upregulation was markedly attenuated upon DDX41 knockout (Fig. ). This suggests that DDX41, acting as a cytosolic DNA sensor, plays a critical role in the HT-DNA–triggered immune response.

## DDX41 interacts with STING in vitro to undergo liquid-liquid phase separation
To further explore whether DDX41 exhibits phase separation behavior and whether it is involved in the regulation of PD-L1 expression. We analyzed the intrinsically disordered region (IDR) structure of DDX41 using PONDR, and then found that DDX41 has an IDR sequence between residues 50 and 100 (Fig. ). The analysis results of PhaSePred indicate that the phase separation score of DDX41 is relatively high (Fig. ). Immunofluorescence experiments show that DDX41 can form micron-sized droplets in the cytoplasm of tumor cells, and these droplets will disappear after the addition of 1,6-Hex (Fig. ). In addition, we added the DDX41-EGFP fusion protein plasmid to SCC15 cells to observe the droplet characteristics in living cells. Through microscopic imaging, we found that two DDX41 droplets merged into a larger one (Fig. ). Next, we conducted a FRAP experiment on DDX41 droplets. Within 3 min after laser bleaching, the DDX41 droplets recovered most of their fluorescence intensity (Fig. ). In addition, we further assessed whether HT-DNA transfection affects the phase separation of DDX41 droplets. As shown in Fig. , HT-DNA transfection markedly increased the number of DDX41 droplets in tumor cells. 
Previous studies have shown that DDX41 can bind to STING to activate the downstream TBK1 pathway . We hypothesize that DDX41 binds to STING to form phase-separated condensates, thereby regulating the downstream TBK1–NF-κB–PD-L1 signaling pathway. We conducted a molecular simulation experiment on the physical interaction between DDX41 and STING using AlphaFold3. The simulation results show that DDX41 can physically bind to STING (Fig. ). Next, we performed co-immunoprecipitation assays to evaluate the interaction between the two proteins. The results showed that DDX41 physically associates with STING; moreover, HT-DNA transfection markedly enhanced the DDX41–STING interaction (Fig. ). Therefore, we further investigated whether DDX41 forms phase-separated droplets with STING. Confocal microscopy showed that DDX41 co-localized with STING in the cytoplasm of tumor cells and formed droplet-like structures; however, treatment with 1,6-Hex abolished these structures and markedly reduced their co-localization (Fig. ). Finally, we constructed DDX41–EGFP and STING–mCherry expression plasmids and transfected them into SCC15 cells. Laser confocal microscopy revealed that DDX41 and STING positive droplets underwent fusion, allowing visualization of both the fusion process and the resulting fused droplets (Fig. ). By altering the NaCl concentration in the culture medium, we observed that DDX41 phase separation affects PD-L1 expression (Fig. S7A-D). The above results demonstrate that DDX41 interacts with cytoplasmic STING in a phase-separated form.

## Knockout of DDX41 inhibits tumor progression in C57 mice
To investigate the live organism-based tumor-inhibitory role of DDX41 deletion, we developed a MOC2 OSCC cell line with stable silencing of DDX41 through lentiviral-mediated gene silencing and used it to establish a C57BL/6 mouse tumor graft model. We randomly divided 10 mice into two groups (control group and shDDX41 group) and continuously monitored the body weight and tumor volume of the mice every two days for 15 days. On the 15th day, the mice were euthanized, and their tumor tissues were extracted and weighed (Fig. ). The tumor images revealed a notable impediment to tumor enlargement in the DDX41 knockdown series as opposed to the control series (Fig. ). Tumor growth curves and the weights of isolated tumors further confirmed a significant inhibitory effect of DDX41 knockdown on OSCC (Fig. ). Body weight change curves and weight gain in mice showed no significant differences between control and DDX41 knockdown groups (Fig. ). We performed immunohistochemical analysis to examine the expression of these proteins within tumor tissues. The results indicated that in DDX41 knockdown samples, the expression of DDX41, p-STING, p-TBK1, p-p65, and PD-L1 was significantly decreased. Additionally, we observed a decrease in PCNA expression, suggesting that DDX41 knockdown inhibits OSCC cell proliferation (Fig. ). Next, WB analysis showed that reduced DDX41 expression led to decreased PD-L1 levels and suppressed activation of the STING-TBK1-NF-κB pathways (Fig. ). The above experimental results indicate that inhibiting DDX41 reduces PD-L1 expression by suppressing the STING-TBK1-NF-κB pathway in vivo. 

## DDX41 knockdown improves the immunosuppressive microenvironment in OSCC
Firstly, to investigate whether DDX41 knockout affects tumor immunity in mice, we performed flow cytometry analysis on the tumor-draining lymph nodes and spleens of the mice. Figure illustrates the gating strategy used to delineate MDSCs and T cells. Firstly, the inhibition of DDX41 in mice led to a significant increase in the frequencies of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells within the lymph nodes and spleens (Fig. 6B, D, E). Meanwhile, we observed a significant reduction in the proportion of MDSCs in the DDX41 knockdown group compared to the control group (Fig. ). To further investigate the immune response within the tumor microenvironment (TME), we employed immunofluorescence staining, which revealed a substantial increase in the infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the tumors of DDX41 knockdown group (Fig. ). 
To determine the contribution of CD8⁺ T cells to this process, we established stable DDX41 knockdown cell lines and subcutaneously implanted them, together with control cells, into the dorsal flanks of C57BL/6 mice. In parallel, a CD8⁺ T-cell depletion group was included, in which anti-CD8 antibody was administered intraperitoneally on days −7 and −3 before tumor inoculation and on days 3, 7, and 10 after inoculation (Fig. ). Tumor growth was then monitored longitudinally. The results showed that DDX41 knockdown significantly suppressed tumor growth; however, this inhibitory effect was markedly attenuated upon CD8⁺ T-cell depletion (Fig. ). On day 15 after tumor inoculation, mice were euthanized, and the spleen, tumor-draining lymph nodes, and tumor tissues were collected for flow cytometric analysis. The gating strategy is shown in Fig. . The results showed that CD8⁺ T cells were markedly reduced in the tumor tissues, lymph nodes, and spleens of mice (Fig. , and Fig. ). In addition, DDX41 knockdown significantly increased IFN-γ activation in CD8⁺ T cells within the tumor microenvironment (Fig. ), indicating that DDX41 knockdown enhances the cytotoxic activity of tumor-infiltrating CD8⁺ T cells. Next, we performed immunohistochemical analysis of mouse tumor tissues. As shown in Fig. , DDX41 knockout markedly increased the number of CD8⁺ T cells in the tumor tissues, whereas the CD8 T cell–deficient group exhibited a pronounced reduction in CD8⁺ T cells. In the DDX41 knockdown group, intratumoral IFN-γ and TNF-α levels were significantly elevated (Fig. ). Finally, we performed immunofluorescence staining and found that DDX41 knockdown markedly enhanced the colocalization of CD8⁺ T cells with IFN-γ and TNF-α in tumor tissues, further supporting the notion that DDX41 knockdown promotes CD8⁺ T-cell cytotoxicity (Fig. ). These data suggest that the suppression of DDX41 decreases PD-L1 expression on tumor cells, thus reducing T cell exhaustion and potentiating T cell-mediated cytotoxic responses against OSCC cells.

## Elevated expression of DDX41, p-p65 and PD-L1 in human OSCC predicts poor prognosis
To evaluate the expression of DDX41 in human OSCC, we collected clinical samples of oral cancer and adjacent non-cancerous tissues to construct a tissue microarray, followed by analysis using immunohistochemistry. The results indicated a strong expression of DDX41 in OSCC tissues, which was notably higher when compared to the expression in normal tissues (Fig. ). In addition, our study examined the association of DDX41 expression with the clinicopathological attributes of OSCC. The result demonstrated a gradual enhancement of DDX41 expression in line with the progression of tumor stages from T1 to T4 (Fig. ). Nevertheless, the expression of DDX41 did not appear to be significantly linked to the extent of lymph node metastasis, ranging from N0 to N2 (Fig. ). A predictive analysis was performed on a group of 54 individuals with OSCC. Using a histoscore of 244.1641049 as the optimal cut-off value, patients were divided into high and low DDX41 expression groups for survival assessment. The outcomes of the Kaplan–Meier plot implied that patients with high DDX41 expression fared worse in terms of clinical outcomes than their counterparts with low expression (Fig. ). 
We performed immunohistochemical staining on 54 OSCC tissue specimens to assess the expression levels of DDX41, p-p65, PD-L1, CD33, and CD8, thereby systematically evaluating the association between DDX41 and the NF-κB–PD-L1 pathway and further exploring its relationship with MDSC infiltration and the distribution of CD8⁺ T cells within the tumor microenvironment. The results showed that DDX41 expression was significantly positively correlated with p-p65, PD-L1, and CD33 expression, but significantly negatively correlated with CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration (Fig. D–G, H, I, L, M). The results showed that p-p65, PD-L1, and CD33 were highly expressed in OSCC tissues, with expression levels significantly higher than those in normal tissues, whereas CD8 showed no significant difference between the two groups (Fig. ). To more intuitively illustrate the correlations among these markers, we compiled the data from each tumor specimen and generated a heatmap. The heatmap showed that high DDX41 expression was generally accompanied by high expression of p-p65, PD-L1, and CD33, as well as low CD8 expression (Fig. ).

## Discussions
Previous studies have shown that DDX41 is frequently recognized as a tumor suppressor in myeloid cancers . However, emerging evidence indicates that DDX41 may adopt a pro-tumorigenic function in solid tumor development. This may be due to the distinct tumor characteristics and molecular mechanisms of solid tumors and myeloid malignancies . Our comprehensive pan-cancer analysis revealed that DDX41 is upregulated in the majority of cancerous tissues, implying its possible involvement as a critical element in the pathogenesis of various cancers.
The NF-κB pathway has a crucial function in various biological activities that underlie cancer initiation, involving processes like immune and inflammatory reactions, cellular growth, programmed cell mortality, and the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) , . In the classical NF-κB pathway, p65 typically associates with IκBα to create an inactive complex that is sequestered in the cytoplasmic compartment. Activation by specific stimuli leads to the phosphorylation of p65, resulting in the formation of p-p65, its migration to the nuclear compartment, and subsequent initiation of target gene expression . Research has revealed the presence of an NF-κB binding site on the upstream promoter of PD-L1 . Our research is pioneering in showing that DDX41 is upregulated in OSCC tissues and that it enhances PD-L1 expression via the activation of NF-κB signaling. This work advances our knowledge of the regulatory framework underlying PD-L1 in OSCC, providing new therapeutic targets for improving immunosuppression in OSCC.
Target the immune checkpoint PD-L1 on tumor cells to unleash T lymphocyte-mediated cytotoxicity and to bolster immune cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment . However, the response rates of immunotherapy and the spectrum of associated adverse reactions are issues of urgent concern. The existence of multiple PD-L1 variants, such as those localized to the membrane (mPD-L1), within the cytoplasm (cPD-L1), in the nucleus (nPD-L1), and in the serum (sPD-L1), indicates that the distinct localization of PD-L1 in cancer cells may have varying implications for immune surveillance against cancer . Studies are beginning to reveal that the intracellular compartment of PD-L1 might play a role in modulating the response to PD-L1-targeted immunotherapies, which could be attributed to its potential mobilization to the cell surface triggered by regulatory T cells . Additionally, intracellular PD-L1 has been implicated in advancing tumor growth through complex molecular cascades – . For example, in the SKOV3 cell line, specific knockdown of cytoplasmic PD-L1 (cPD-L1) can significantly inhibit tumor cell growth and migration . In addition, within the context of a clinical investigation into papillary thyroid carcinoma, individuals demonstrating elevated levels of cytoplasmic PD-L1 positivity were found to have an abbreviated period of disease-free survival . These studies emphasize the contribution of cPD-L1 in tumor promotion and immune suppression, whereas current anti PD-L1 antibodies are largely unable to block cPD-L1. Our findings indicate that the attenuation of DDX41 expression diminishes PD-L1 expression and counteracts the immunosuppressive phenotype observed in OSCC.
The structural characteristics of biomolecular phase separation enable cells to respond to external stimuli and play important roles in key biological processes such as gene expression regulation, signal transduction, and cell differentiation and development . Membraneless organelles formed via phase separation can enrich signaling molecules, thereby modulating signal transduction pathways and significantly influencing cellular responses . The binding of cytosolic dsDNA to cGAS induces phase-separated droplet formation, significantly elevating cytoplasmic cGAMP levels and activating innate immune signaling . Based on our experimental data, we found that DDX41 can form phase-separated droplets with STING in the cytoplasm, further highlighting the importance of phase separation in the regulation of the STING signaling pathway by DDX41.
Recent studies and our experiments have found that DDX41 can act as a cytosolic DNA sensor to detect exogenous and abnormal intracellular DNA, thereby activating the downstream cGAS-STING innate immune response. DDX41 can bind to cytosolic dsDNA fragments and subsequently interact with STING to activate downstream type I interferon and/or NF-κB pathways . Aside from viral elements, Chromosomal instability (CIN) in malignant cells can also prompt inflammatory signaling pathways by releasing double-stranded DNA into the cytoplasm, thereby activating the cGAS-STING antiviral pathway . Although the cGAS-STING pathway can elicit type I interferon responses to restrain tumor growth, heightened cGAS-STING levels in cancer cells do not always denote the commencement of type I interferon signaling. The presence of dsDNA in the cytoplasm does not always result in robust type I interferon signaling, which is often diminished in various cancer cell strains . It is intriguing that this dampening selectively impacts the tumorcidal type I interferon pathway that is triggered by cGAS-STING, with no apparent effect on other downstream signaling cascades . Illustratively, the Triple-negative breast cancer is characterized by CIN that increases cytosolic dsDNA levels, activating the cGAS-STING pathway and promoting metastasis through a non-canonical NF-κB pathway. Despite this, type I interferon signaling is not detected . Likewise, HNSCC cell lines show extensive CIN, with tumor cells displaying elevated STING levels compared to normal tissues . Therefore, based on our experimental evidence, we demonstrate that DDX41 functions as a cytosolic DNA sensor in OSCC, promoting PD-L1 upregulation through the STING-TBK1-NF-κB signaling axis.
In summary, this study began by cross-verifying through public datasets and clinical microarray studies that DDX41 is substantially upregulated in tumor versus normal tissue, with significant implications for TNM staging and prognosis based on DDX41 expression levels. Subsequently, we decreased and increased the expression level of DDX41 in OSCC cell lines, and clarified its role in regulating the growth, invasive ability, and metastatic behavior of these cells. Our study demonstrates that DDX41 can bind to STING through phase separation, thereby activating the downstream TBK1-NF-κB pathway and regulating PD-L1 expression (Fig. ). Experimental evidence from both cell culture and animal studies robustly supports the notion that DDX41 activates the STING-TBK1-NF-κB pathway, thereby facilitating immune suppression and tumor progression in OSCC. 

## Methods

## Differential expression and prognostic analysis
A comprehensive analysis of RNA sequencing data across various cancers, with a focus on OSCC, was performed using data from the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). Information regarding the expression of DDX41 and its relevance to clinical patient outcomes was extracted from the Genomic Data Commons Data Portal (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html) to delve into the potential links between DDX41 and tumorigenesis.

## Cell culture
The HOK cellular lineage, an exemplar of normal human oral epithelial tissue, and the panel of human squamous cell carcinoma lineages, including SCC4, SCC9, SCC15, CAL27, as well as the murine counterpart MOC2, were acquired from FuHeng Biology, located in Shanghai, China. These lines were propagated under controlled laboratory conditions of 37°C and 5% CO2 in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco). Experimental assays were carried out on cell cultures certified to be free of mycoplasma contamination. All cell lines have been authenticated using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling within the past 3 years.

## CCK8 Assay
SCC15 cells with diminished DDX41 levels and their matched control counterparts, at a rate of 1 × 104 cells for every 100 μL, were distributed into a 96-well plate and cultured for one day. For each of the test and control conditions, five parallel wells were set up. The CCK-8 assay kit (Dojindo, Japan, CK04) was applied to evaluate the viability of the cells, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The optical density at 450 nm (OD 450) was measured using an MD SpectraMAX reader. Statistical outliers from the data range were removed to compute the mean optical density.

## Wound healing assay
In the experimental configuration, SCC15 cells, including both with and without DDX41 knockdown, were seeded into 6-well dishes (manufactured by Corning, United States) with a density of 5 million cells per compartment. The cultures were maintained until the cells occupied 80–90% of the well’s area. Following this, the culture medium was removed, and a delicate rinsing with 100 µl of PBS was carried out to hydrate the monolayer. An artificial injury was created by meticulously scoring the cell layer with the end of a 1 mL pipette, and the boundary of the incision was delineated for subsequent observation. The healing dynamics of the wound were visually recorded at 0, 24, and 72 h using an Olympus microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

## Transwell Experiment
For the evaluation of cellular migration, we utilized the Transwell assay system. Specifically, we introduced 2 × 104 SCC15 cells suspended in 200 µL of a serum-deprived DMEM solution into the apical compartment of the Transwell insert (353097, BD Falcon, NY, USA). In the basal compartment, we added 600 µL of DMEM enriched with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco). To assess invasive capabilities, the porous membrane of the insert was coated uniformly with Matrigel (50 μL, 1 mg/mL, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co). Following a 48-h incubation period, the cells that had successfully traversed to the lower chamber were immobilized using a 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) fixative for a duration of 15 min. Subsequent to fixation, the cells were subjected to a 0.1% crystal violet staining procedure for 8 min. To quantify the results, images of five distinct areas were captured at a 20× magnification under a microscope, providing a visual representation of the migratory and invasive behavior of the cells.

## Overexpression experiment
All the Overexpression plasmid with green fluorescent fusion protein were synthesized and purchased from at Synbio Technologies (Suzhou, China). First, dilute the overexpression plasmid and transfection reagent (SYN-IS-134 SYNBIO) with DMEM medium (6124401, Gibco, USA). Add the plasmid complexes to the cells and evaluate their transfection efficiency using immunofluorescence.

## Construction of the lentiviral vector for DDX41
Researchers at Synbio Technologies (Suzhou, China) created three specific short hairpin RNA(shRNA)—DDX41-RNAi#1(GAGGATGCCAACACGTATATT),DDX41-RNAi#2(GGTACCACTGTTCTGCAAGT), and DDX41-RNAi#3 (GCATCGCTACCACCTTCATCA)—each targeting a unique sequence of the DDX41 gene to silence it. They incorporated these shRNA sequences into the GV493 vector and then purified the plasmid to transfect 293 T cells, thus producing a lentivirus for DDX41-shRNA knockdown.

## Construction of DDX41-silenced stable cell lines
Lentivirus targeting DDX41 was utilized to infect the MOC2 cells, and the infection rate was assessed by fluorescence microscopy. Cells that had effectively transduced with the lentivirus were further cultured in the presence of 2 μg/mL puromycin to establish a stable MOC2 cell line with reduced DDX41 expression. For comparison purposes, a negative control was also prepared by infecting MOC2 cells with a lentivirus containing a vector without the DDX41 knockdown sequence, termed NC-MOC2.

## RNA­sequencing

The SCC15 cell line was cultured at a rate of 5 million cells per well in 6-well dishes, and they were provided with a 12-h period to ensure proper adherence, after which they were exposed to DDX41-targeting siRNA or a scrambled control for 24 h. ## Animal experiments
The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Southern Medical University approved and monitored all animal experimentation protocols to ensure compliance with ethical standards. Initially, under the maintenance of 3% isoflurane inhalation anesthesia, 1 × 10⁶ cells of the MOC2 cell line with stable knockdown of DDX41 and the control MOC2 cell line were subcutaneously inoculated into the dorsal area of C57BL/6 mice, respectively. Throughout the study, the mice underwent consistent daily observation. After the experimental procedures were completed, C57BL/6 mice were humanely euthanized. The euthanasia procedures for mice in our experiments strictly comply with international standards or follow the latest guidelines established by the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA). Euthanasia in mice was performed via cervical dislocation under anesthesia. Our anesthesia method is inhalation anesthesia with 5% isoflurane. The judgment method for successful anesthesia is whether the toe-withdrawal reflex of the mice is lost and whether the eyelid reaction to light is dull. Our method of euthanizing mice was based on relevant scientific validation and strictly complied with relevant regulations. The tumor samples were either embedded in paraffin or preserved at a temperature of −80°C. All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Southern Medical University and were conducted in accordance with its guidelines. ## Immunohistochemistry
Thin slices, measuring 4 micrometers in depth, were extracted from paraffin-embedded human tissue microarrays and parallel mouse tumor blocks. These slices underwent a brief 90-s steam heating protocol with a sodium citrate antigen unmasking reagent from Biosharp to expose the antigens. Once the antigens were retrieved, the sections were soaked in a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution to quench endogenous peroxidase for a period of 20 min. To avoid any unwanted protein interactions, the sections were treated with a blocking solution composed of 10% normal goat serum. After blocking, the primary antibody was applied, and the sections were left to incubate overnight at a low temperature Hypothermia of 4 °C. The immuno-histochemical procedure involved the use of primary antibodies against DDX41 (1:200, Proteintech, 27500-1-AP), p-STING (1:200, Affinity, AF7416), p-TBK1 (1:200, Bioss, BC07208022), p-p65 (1:200, Affinity, AF2006), PD-L1 (1:200, CST, 13684 T), IFN-γ (1:200, Proteintech, 80979-1-RR), TNF-α (1:200, Proteintech, 19272-1-AP), anti-CD8a (1:150, CST, 98941), CD33 (Affinity, DF6789) and PCNA (1:200, CST, 13110 T). Following the preliminary antibody exposure, the tissue sections were rinsed with PBS. Subsequently, a 20-min interval was allocated for the conjugation with biotin-conjugated secondary IgG antibodies. Following this incubation period, the specimens underwent a triple wash with PBS, after which they were stained with Mayer’s hematoxylin for contrast. Subsequent to staining, the slides were captured on a Leica digital pathology scanner, and optical density measurements were recorded via ImageScope software (Aperio, Vista, CA, USA). Evaluation of the immunohistochemical staining intensity hinged on four criteria: the sum of intensities for robust positivity, the sum of intensities for moderate positivity, the sum of intensities for faint positivity, and the extent of the stained area. These criteria provided a mean optical density score for the targeted areas in each specimen, which was applied to ascertain the protein expression level.

## Immunofluorescence
The oncological tissue sections were subjected to a standardized immunohistochemical assay as previously delineated. Subsequent to this, the sections were incubated overnight at a temperature of 4 °C in the presence of the primary antibodies, specifically anti-CD4 (1:150, CST, 25229), anti-CD8a (1:150, CST, 98941), IFN-γ (1:200, Proteintech, 80979-1-RR) and TNF-α (1:200, Proteintech, 19272-1-AP). Subsequently, the secondary antibody, Alexa 594-conjugated to anti-rabbit IgG (Invitrogen), was applied to the tumor sections for 30 min. Post-incubation, the slides were fixed with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Vector Laboratories) included in the mounting process and then enclosed. To visualize fluorescence, the microscope images were obtained through a Leica laser scanning confocal microscopy apparatus.

## Western blotting
The SCC15 cells, as well as cancerous tissues obtained from C57BL/6 mice, were subjected to lysis at 0°C for a duration of 30 min with the application of RIPA buffer (35020, CWBIO). To the lysate, protease inhibitor (#F04D16, absin) and phosphatase inhibitors (#N23A02, absin) were incorporated. Subsequently, the protein specimens underwent a heating process at 95 °C for 10–15 min to achieve protein denaturation. Following this, the supernatant containing the proteins was transferred into the wells of a 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel (Beyotime) for analysis. The process of electrophoresis was initiated at a voltage of 65 V for a duration of 30 min, which was then escalated to 110 V for a period of 60 min. After the separation, the proteins were transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore Corporation, Billerica, MA) using an electrophoretic transfer method at a current of 220 mA for 60 min, with the setup immersed in an ice bath to maintain Low temperature conditions. The PVDF membrane was subsequently sealed with a 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) blocking solution for 1 h to prevent non-specific binding. The subsequent incubation with antibodies was followed by the detection of protein bands, which was achieved using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (Biosharp, Chongqing, China). The Western blot procedure utilized a panel of antibodies, each diluted to 1:1000, including those specific to DDX41 (Proteintech, 27500-1-AP), STING (CST, 13647S), p-STING (Affinity, AF7416), TBK1 (Affinity, DF7026), p-TBK1 (Bioss, BC07208022), p65 (Abmart, T55034F), phosphorylated p65 (p-p65) (Affinity, AF2006), PD-L1 (CST, 13684 T), and beta-actin (Proteintech, 66009-1-IG). The beta-actin antibody was used to ensure consistent protein loading across the samples.

## Coimmunoprecipitation assay
The physical interaction between DDX41 and STING was examined using an IP/Co-IP Kit (KTD104-CN, Abbkine, Wuhan, China). After complete cell lysis, the lysates were incubated at 4°C with A/G magnetic beads pre-coupled with the appropriate primary antibody, followed by removal of nonspecifically bound proteins. Then, 1× SDS loading buffer was added, and the mixture was heated. The eluates were subsequently subjected to Western blot analysis.

## Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP)
We conducted a FRAP experiment using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope. A 488 nm laser pulse was selected, and the laser intensity was set at 20%. The droplets were subjected to photobleaching for 1 s. Immediately after the photobleaching operation was completed, the monitoring of the fluorescence recovery situation began. The monitoring frequency was once every 3 s, and the entire monitoring process lasted for 150 s. During the experiment, the fluorescence intensities of the bleached area and the background area, along with the corresponding images, were recorded and analyzed using the software that came with the Olympus microscope.

## Flow cytometry analysis
Once the mice were humanely dispatched, the spleens and the lymph nodes that drain the tumors were isolated. These tissues were then finely minced to produce single-cell suspensions. The subsequent step involved incubating the cells with various fluorescently labeled antibodies, including V500-conjugated anti-CD45 (BD Biosciences, RRID: AB_561487), PE-conjugated anti-CD8a (BD Biosciences, RRID: AB_553032), APC-conjugated-anti-CD4 (BD Biosciences, RRID:AB_553051), APC-Cy7-conjugated anti-CD11b (BD Biosciences, RRID: AB_557657). BV605-conjugated anti-Ly-6C (BD Biosciences, RRID: AB_563299), APC-conjugated-anti-IFNγ (Elabscience, RRID:WK2042268). After the antibodies were incubated, the samples were examined on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer. Analysis of the flow cytometry data was performed using FlowJo V10, a software designed for Windows.

## Human OSCC tissue array
Ethical approval for obtaining clinical samples for our research was granted by the Medical Ethics Committees at both the Hospital of Stomatology, Southern Medical University, and the Xiangya School of Medicine, Central South University. Patients diagnosed with OSCC who were to undergo surgery gave their voluntary consent for the use of their tissue samples. Initially, hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining was performed on 64 human tissue samples to identify representative tumor areas and normal tissue areas. Next, a 1.5 mm diameter cylindrical core was extracted from the selected regions and mounted on slides to create a tissue microarray. Protein expression levels in each tissue were quantified by measuring the average optical density following immunohistochemical staining.

## Statistical analysis
GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 was utilized for the statistical analysis of our data. To assess the statistical differences between the control and experimental cohorts, a two-sample t-test was employed. Survival statistics were analyzed utilizing the Kaplan–Meier technique. Additionally, the correlation between DDX41 and p-p65 was investigated via a two-sided Pearson correlation examination. A p-value below 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. The reported quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).
