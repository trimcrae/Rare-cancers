Luteoloside, a flavonoid glycoside compound found in plants such as those from the Asteraceae and Fabaceae families, has various biological activities. This study aimed to explore the anti-senescence and anti-fibrotic effects of luteoloside in experimental pulmonary fibrosis and investigate its underlying molecular mechanisms. Our results indicated that luteoloside attenuated bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis, oxidative stress, and lung senescence in mice. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that luteoloside reduced AEC senescence, as indicated by decreased P21 expression in SPC⁺ epithelial cells. In vitro, luteoloside removed bleomycin- and oxidative stress-induced AEC senescence and mitochondrial dysfunction. Mechanistically, we proved through the inhibition effect of EX527 that luteoloside’s protective effects were mediated through SIRT1. This study provides new insights into the mechanisms through which luteoloside modulates cellular senescence and pulmonary fibrosis, offering potential pathways for the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

## Introduction
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a serious and progressive pulmonary disease marked by heightened activity of myofibroblasts and abnormal deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) in the lungs – . This condition is strongly linked to various age-associated changes and cellular malfunctions, which result in poor adaptation to stress and a heightened risk of developing lung fibrosis. Increasing evidence indicates that cellular senescence is a key factor in the onset and progression of pulmonary fibrosis – . Cellular senescence is defined by the irreversible halting of the cell cycle and loss of cellular function . Senescent cells release a range of bioactive molecules collectively referred to as the Senescence-Associated Secretory Phenotype (SASP), which modifies the surrounding cellular environment . In the case of pulmonary fibrosis, the SASP secretes pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and matrix metalloproteinases, thereby aggravating tissue damage and fibrosis . Present FDA-approved treatments for IPF, such as pirfenidone and nintedanib, have limited therapeutic benefits and raise safety concerns , , . Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop novel therapeutic strategies that target cellular senescence in lung fibrosis, ensuring both effectiveness and safety.
Alveolar epithelial cells (AECs) play a crucial role in maintaining pulmonary homeostasis and function, especially in the pathogenesis of pulmonary fibrosis and cellular senescence , . Studies have shown that in the lung tissue of patients with IPF, the degree of senescence in AEC2 cells is significantly elevated , . This process accelerates the activation of fibroblasts through multiple classical mechanisms, thereby promoting the progression of fibrosis . In the bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis model, damage to AECs triggers oxidative stress, which further leads to AEC senescence . Senescent AECs secrete a variety of bioactive molecules, including transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which exacerbate the fibrotic microenvironment in the lung and alter the cell–matrix interactions, causing abnormal matrix remodeling and accelerating fibrosis development . This process is fundamentally driven by mitochondrial dysfunction . As senescence develops, impaired electron transport chains generate excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), which in turn oxidize mitochondrial components, further compromising their function . The resulting oxidative stress triggers DNA damage responses that reinforce senescence, creating a self-amplifying loop . Therefore, maintaining mitochondrial function is considered a key strategy in inhibiting cellular senescence.
Sirtuins (SIRTs) are a family of NAD + -dependent deacetylases involved in various cellular physiological processes, including fibrosis, metabolism, and DNA repair, particularly in senescence and mitochondrial function – . Previous studies have suggested that luteoloside can significantly enhance the expression of SIRT1, a member of the SIRT family . SIRT1 regulates the function of several key senescence-related proteins, such as p53, p16INK4a, and FOXOs, through deacetylation. It also enhances the activity of antioxidant enzymes, thereby reducing oxidative damage during the senescence process – . Additionally, SIRT1 regulates PGC-1α, promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and the maintenance of mitochondrial function . By simultaneously mitigating oxidative stress and repairing mitochondrial damage, SIRT1 activation may regulate disease processes influenced by these two pathological mechanisms. Therefore, exploring drug strategies targeting SIRT1 is significant for delaying senescence and fibrosis.
Luteoloside is an active compound extracted from plants such as those in the Asteraceae and Fabaceae families – . Studies have shown that luteoloside exhibits significant biological effects, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant properties, and potential anti-fibrotic activity – . However, its effects and mechanisms in pulmonary fibrosis remain unclear. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the effects and mechanisms of luteoloside on bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis and lung senescence in mice, both in vitro and in vivo.

## Materials and methods

## Animal experiment
Six to eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were procured from the Central South University animal facility, adhering to established ethical protocols for animal welfare. Upon arrival, the mice were housed in a pathogen-free, controlled environment with unlimited food and water.
To confirm whether luteoloside is safe to mice, 15 mice were administered 30 mg/kg of luteoloside (SELLECK, China) via oral gavage from day 1 to 14. On day 15, mice were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg, i.p.). Once euthanasia was confirmed, lung tissue was carefully harvested and collected for further analysis.
To evaluate the effects of luteoloside on pulmonary fibrosis, the mice were first anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium. On day 0, they received a 2.5 mg/kg dose of bleomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) or an equal volume of saline via i.t. injection. From days 15 to 28, they were administered different concentrations of luteoloside or pirfenidone (300 mg/kg) via oral gavage. The 90 mice were randomly assigned into 6 groups: CON group (0.9% saline, i.t. injection + 0.9% saline, oral, days 15–28); Bleomycin group (Bleomycin, i.t. injection + 0.9% saline, oral, days 15–28); Bleomycin + 1 mg/kg group (Bleomycin, i.t. injection + luteoloside 1 mg/kg, oral, days 15–28); Bleomycin + 3 mg/kg group (Bleomycin, i.t. injection + luteoloside 3 mg/kg, oral, days 15–28); Bleomycin + 30 mg/kg group (Bleomycin, i.t. injection + luteoloside 30 mg/kg, oral, days 15–28); Bleomycin + pirfenidone group (Bleomycin, i.t. injection + pirfenidone 300 mg/kg, oral, days 15–28). On day 29, mice were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg, i.p.). Once euthanasia was confirmed, lung tissue was carefully harvested and collected for further analysis.
To verify that SIRT1 mediates the antifibrotic mechanism of luteoloside, the SIRT1-specific inhibitor EX527 (SELLECK, China) was used . The 60 mice were randomly divided into 4 groups: CON group (0.9% saline, i.t. injection + 0.9% saline, oral, days 15–28); Bleomycin group (Bleomycin, i.t. injection + 0.9% saline, oral, days 15–28); Bleomycin + Luteoloside group (Bleomycin, i.t. injection + luteoloside 30 mg/kg, oral, days 15–28); Bleomycin + Luteoloside + EX527 group (Bleomycin, i.t. injection + luteoloside 30 mg/kg, oral + EX527 10 mg/kg, i.p. injection, days 15–28). On day 29, mice were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg, i.p.). Once euthanasia was confirmed, lung tissue was carefully harvested and collected for further analysis.

## Histological analysis
For microscopic analysis, lung tissue samples were initially fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution. Following fixation, the tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into thin slices. These tissue sections were then subjected to staining with hematoxylin–eosin and Masson’s trichrome to enable detailed examination. Fibrosis scoring was performed blindly by six independent pathologists based on the Ashcroft scale. To ensure accurate assessment of interstitial fibrosis, scoring was strictly confined to the alveolar parenchyma, and all peribronchial and perivascular areas were deliberately excluded from the analysis .

## Hydroxyproline assays
Hydroxyproline content in lung tissue homogenates was quantified using a Hydroxyproline Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China). First, tissue samples were homogenized in a buffer solution at a tissue-to-buffer ratio of 1 mg per 9 mL. A 300 μL aliquot of the homogenate was then mixed with the digestion solution provided in the kit at a 5:1 ratio and incubated in a 37 °C water bath for 3 h. Following the incubation, 500 μL of reagent 1 was added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min. Reagent 2 (500 μL) was then introduced, and the mixture was incubated for another 5 min at room temperature. The final step involved adding 1 mL of reagent 3, followed by thorough mixing and a 15 min incubation at 60 °C in a water bath. After cooling, the sample was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min to remove any cellular debris. The supernatant was then collected and subjected to colorimetric analysis. Absorbance was measured at 550 nm using a spectrophotometer with a 1 cm path length, and double-distilled water was used for calibration before measurement.

## RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis
Total RNA was extracted from lung tissue or cells using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA synthesis was then carried out with a Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). To assess gene expression, quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green (Promega, USA) on the Bio-Rad CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). The qPCR protocol involved an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 3 s and a combined annealing and extension step at 60 °C for 30 s. A final melting curve analysis was conducted between 60 °C and 95 °C. The specific primer sequences used are listed below. 

## Protein extraction and western blotting
Protein extracts from lung tissue or cells were prepared using RIPA buffer (Biosharp, China). The concentration of proteins in the lysates was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. An aliquot corresponding to 10% of the total protein was separated by electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bio-Rad, USA). To block non-specific binding, the membranes were incubated in a blocking solution containing 5% (w/v) non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline (TBS) with 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20 (TBST).
The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: β-actin monoclonal antibody (1:5000, Proteintech, China), α-SMA monoclonal antibody (1:1000, Proteintech, China), collagen I polyclonal antibody (1:1000, Proteintech, China), SIRT1 polyclonal antibody (1:1000, Proteintech, China), P53 polyclonal antibody (1:3000, Proteintech, China), P21 polyclonal antibody (1:1000, Proteintech, China), MFN1 polyclonal antibody (1:1000, Proteintech, China), MFN2 polyclonal antibody (1:1000, Proteintech, China), DRP1 polyclonal antibody (1:1000, Proteintech, China).
After the primary antibody incubation, the membranes were washed three times with TBST. They were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies: goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, Proteintech, China) or goat anti-mouse IgG (1:5000, Proteintech, China) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using the Luminata™ Crescendo chemiluminescent substrate (Millipore, USA), and images were captured using a GeneGnome XRQ imager (Syngene, UK).

## Transfection with RNA interference
The siRNA transfections were carried out according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen, USA). Once the cells reached 60–80% confluence, they were maintained in DMEM medium devoid of serum and streptomycin/penicillin. The cells were then transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) in combination with SIRT1 siRNA (Santacruz, USA), or with control siRNA for a duration of 24 h (Santacruz, USA). Following the transfection, various cellular assays were performed as required.

## β-galactosidase staining
After the treatments, the cells were rinsed once with phosphate-buffered saline, followed by a 30 min fixation step. Next, the cells underwent a thorough washing procedure with PBS three times. The cells were then stained using the working solution and incubated overnight at 37 °C. This process was performed in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines provided in the Senescence-Associated β-Galactosidase Stain Kit (Solarbio, China). Lastly, the stained cells were observed under a light microscope (Nikon, Japan).

## ROS levels
The cultured cells were subjected to centrifugation, and the supernatant was removed to collect the cell pellet. To measure ROS content, the pellet was incubated with DCFCDA (Solarbio, China) for 30 min at room temperature, shielded from light. ROS levels were then evaluated using fluorescence microscopy (Nikon, Japan).

## Dihydroethidium staining
Frozen lung sections were defrosted and sequentially processed: (1) 5 min cleaning solution wash, (2) 30 min light-protected dye incubation, (3) PBS rinses, (4) DAPI counterstaining in dark, and final PBS wash before fluorescence microscopy.

## Cell culture
The MLE12 cells, sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA), were cultured under controlled conditions, with a stable temperature of 37 °C and a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The growth medium used was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Procell, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution (Procell, China) to prevent bacterial contamination. To start the experiment, MLE12 cells were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 10^5 cells per well. The cells were allowed to grow until they reached approximately 60% confluence, usually within 24 h. Following this, the cells were exposed to 100 U bleomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 2 h. Afterward, the cells were thoroughly washed with PBS to remove any remaining bleomycin. Finally, the cells were cultured with fresh medium, either containing myricetin or without it, for an additional 72 h. For comparison, 20 μM SRT2104(MCE, USA), a known sirtuin-activating compound, was used to treat the cells for 72 h after bleomycin was removed.

## Cell viability assay
Cells viability was determined via CCK-8 assay (Abcam, UK) under standard protocols. Cells were plated in 96-well plates at 70% confluence using complete 10% fetal bovine serum medium. Following 24 h exposure to luteoloside (0, 5, 10, 20,40, and 80 μM), CCK-8 reagent was introduced for incubation at 37 °C. Optical density measurements at 450 nm provided quantitative viability data for subsequent analysis.

## Immunofluorescence
Lung tissue was sectioned after being fixed with paraformaldehyde and embedded following dehydration. Antigen retrieval was performed, and endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked. The sections were then incubated with 5% BSA (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to block non-specific binding. For cultured cells, fixation was followed by permeabilization using Triton X-100 (Solarbio, China), and blocking was similarly performed. Primary antibodies (SIRT1, Proteintech, China, 1:200; P21, Proteintch, China, 1:100; SPC, Abclonal, China, 1:200) were applied to the sections or cells and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS three times, the sections were exposed to the secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Solarbio, China), and the sections were mounted for observation under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan).

## MDA, GSH, and SOD levels
After euthanizing the mice, the right lungs were removed and homogenized in an extraction buffer for the subsequent analysis of MDA, SOD, and GSH levels. To evaluate lipid peroxidation in the lung tissue, MDA concentration was determined using a commercially available assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Furthermore, the activity of antioxidant enzymes in the lung tissue was measured by quantifying the levels of SOD and GSH, using kits from the same manufacturer according to their protocols.

## ATP quantification
Lung tissue was added to the lysis buffer at a ratio of 20 mg/100 μL, ground into a homogenate and centrifuged. The supernatant was taken, and subsequent tests were conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, China).

## Molecular docking
The overlap between the two datasets was assessed using Venny 2.1 (https://bioinfogo.cnb.es/tools/venny/index.html). Scores were generated via the String Database (https://string-db.org/) and further analyzed using Cytoscape 3.9.1. We obtained the SIRT1 protein (4ZZJ, 2.74 Å) from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/) and the luteoloside molecule from pubchem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The luteoloside molecules were energy-minimized and then docked with dehydrated and hydrogenated SIRT1 in the SailDock Vina platform, and the results were visualized using PLIP (https://plip-tool.biotec.tu-dresden.de/plip-web/plip/index).

## Statistical analysis
All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed using GraphPad Prism v9.0 software. Normality was first assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc correction. Survival data were compared using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

## Results

## Luteoloside alleviates bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in mice
After treating mice with the maximum drug concentration alone, we confirmed that luteoloside at concentrations of 3, 10, and 30 mg/kg was safe for mice (Fig. ). During the period from day 15 to day 28 after bleomycin treatment in the lungs, severe fibrosis formation occurs . To investigate the anti-fibrotic effect of luteoloside, we administered luteoloside at doses of 1 mg/kg, 3 mg/kg, and 30 mg/kg orally from day 15 to day 28 (Fig.  A). Hydroxyproline content indicated that luteoloside alleviated the degree of pulmonary fibrosis in a dose-dependent manner (Fig.  B). The mortality rate results show that luteoloside can effectively improve the survival rate of mice with pulmonary fibrosis (Fig.  C). HE staining with the ashcroft scores and Masson staining results showed that luteoloside reduced bleomycin-induced lung structural damage and fibrosis formation, and it also decreased the mortality of mice induced by bleomycin (Fig.  D–F). Collagen I and α-SMA, important components of fibrotic foci, were reduced at both the mRNA level and protein abundance in the lung homogenates by luteoloside treatment (Fig.  G–K). Western blotting of key fibrotic markers revealed that luteoloside inhibited bleomycin-induced activation of TGF-β/Smad signaling, decreased collagen III deposition, and downregulated TGF-β1 expression (Fig. ), confirming its suppression of core fibrotic pathways in pulmonary fibrosis. To verify the effect of luteoloside, we added the clinical drug prifenidone as positive control and compare the HE staining, Masson staining, hydroxyproline content, and the mRNA levels of collagen I and α-SMA. The results showed that there was no significant difference in the effect between pirfenidone and high dose of luteoloside (Fig. ). These results suggest that luteoloside alleviates bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in a dose-dependent manner. 

## Luteoloside alleviates bleomycin-induced pulmonary oxidative stress
Oxidative stress refers to the imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) and nitrogen species and the antioxidant defense system in the body . Studies suggest that after bleomycin treatment, mice experience significant oxidative stress damage in the lungs, which mediates pulmonary fibrosis through multiple pathways. DHE staining results indicate that luteoloside reduces bleomycin-induced pulmonary oxidative stress (Fig.  A–B). Furthermore, luteoloside decreased the upregulation of MDA levels induced by bleomycin and promoted the increase of GSH content and SOD activity (Fig.  C–E). These results suggest that luteoloside alleviates bleomycin-induced pulmonary oxidative stress in mice. 

## Luteoloside blocks bleomycin-induced pulmonary senescence
Bleomycin-induced AECs senescence is a key event in the progression of pulmonary fibrosis . Immunofluorescence results indicate that the co-staining of SPC (AECs cell marker) and P16 (senescence marker) suggests that luteoloside not only restores bleomycin-induced AECs damage but also inhibits AECs senescence (Fig.  A). Furthermore, western blotting results indicate that luteoloside reduced the protein levels of P53 and P21 in lung tissue (Fig.  B–D). AEC senescence promotes EMT and EMT-derived fibroblasts further exacerbate AEC senescence, establishing a pro-fibrotic feedback loop in pulmonary fibrosis. The western blotting results showed that luteoloside reduced the protein levels of N-cadherin, β-catenin, and vimentin, while upregulating the content of E-cadherin (Fig. ). These results suggest that luteoloside alleviates bleomycin-induced pulmonary senescence and is closely associated with the reduction of AECs senescence. 

## Luteoloside attenuates bleomycin-induced AECs senescence.
The CCK8 results showed that different concentrations of luteoloside (5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 μM) were non-toxic to AECs, and the 5, 10, 20 μM were selected for the experiment (Fig.  A). β-galactosidase staining is a gold standard for assessing cellular senescence . In the in vitro experiments, the results showed that luteoloside reduced the area of β-galactosidase staining (Fig.  B–C). Furthermore, the elisa results show that luteoloside reduced the SASP levels (Fig.  D–E). And western blotting results also indicated that luteoloside reduced the protein levels of P53 and P21, the cell fluorescence images displayed the same trend (Fig.  F–J). While acute DNA damage primarily activates P53-P21 signaling, chronic damage like fibrosis triggers P16 upregulation through epigenetic reprogramming, leading to irreversible senescence. The western blotting results show that luteoloside reduces both γ-H2AX foci and P16, suggesting it mitigates DNA damage-driven senescence in AECs (Fig. ). These results suggest that luteoloside inhibits bleomycin-induced in vitro AECs senescence. 

## Luteoloside restores bleomycin-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in AECs
ROS-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction is a major promoter of senescence . The bleomycin-induced generation of ROS and mitochondrial dysfunction form a vicious cycle, jointly promoting the progression of senescence . ROS content detection indicated that luteoloside reduced ROS levels (Fig.  A–B). ATP quantification revealed that luteoloside treatment significantly attenuated bleomycin-induced mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig.  C). Next, JC-1 staining, which reflects mitochondrial membrane potential, showed that luteoloside reversed the increase in mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig.  D–E). Additionally, MFN1, MFN2, and DRP1 are key indicators of mitochondrial function and quality. Western blotting results showed that luteoloside restored the expression of MFN1, MFN2, and DRP1 (Fig.  F–I). These results suggest that luteoloside restores bleomycin-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. 

## Luteoloside promotes the expression of SIRT1 both in vitro and in vivo
The SIRT family is an important target for alleviating the aging processs , . Therefore, after luteoloside treatment, we assessed the mRNA levels of SIRT1 to SIRT7. The results indicated that although the expression of both SIRT1 and SIRT3 increased under luteoloside treatment, the increase in SIRT1 expression was more significant, suggesting that luteoloside may exert its anti-aging activity through SIRT1 (Fig.  A). Western blotting results indicated that luteoloside concentration-dependently promoted the expression of SIRT1, and there was no significant difference in effect compared with the SIRT1 activator SRT2104 (Fig.  B). Molecular docking results suggested that luteoloside binds to multiple residues of SIRT1 (Fig.  C), including ARG-466, GLU-467, ASN465, etc.(Fig. ), indicating that luteoloside may be a direct activator of SIRT1, though further verification through experiments like MST and SPR is required. Additionally, western blotting and immunofluorescence results showed that luteoloside also promoted the expression of SIRT1 in vivo (Fig.  D–F). These results suggest that luteoloside promotes the expression of SIRT1 both in vitro and in vivo. 

## SIRT1 mediates the anti-senescence effects and mechanisms of luteoloside
Given the regulation of SIRT by luteoloside, we used SIRT1 siRNA to validate the anti-senescence mechanism of luteoloside. β-galactosidase staining, western blotting and elisa results indicated that SIRT1 siRNA reversed the anti-senescence effects of luteoloside (F g.  A–D,I–J). Moreover, SIRT1 siRNA reversed luteoloside’s reduction of ROS levels, restoration of mitochondrial membrane potential, and alleviation of mitochondrial dysfunction (F g.  E–I,K). These results suggest that SIRT1 mediates the anti-senescence effects of luteoloside as well as its role in the restoration of mitochondrial function. 

## SIRT1 mediates the in vivo anti-fibrotic, antioxidant, and anti-senescence activities of luteoloside
Based on the role of SIRT1 in mediating the biological activity of luteoloside in vitro, we used EX527, a specific SIRT1 inhibitor, in vivo to validate the mechanism of luteoloside’s action. HE and Masson staining results indicated that EX527 reversed the protective effect of luteoloside on lung tissue structure and blocked luteoloside’s effects on hydroxyproline levels and protection against mouse mortality (Fig.  A–E), while also reversing luteoloside’s inhibition of collagen I and α-SMA (Fig.  K–L). Additionally, DHE staining and measurements of MDA, SOD, and GSH suggested that EX527 mediated luteoloside’s antioxidant effects (Fig.  F–J), and the levels of P53 and P21 indicated that EX527 mediated luteoloside’s anti-senescence effects (Fig.  K–L). These results suggest that SIRT1 mediates the biological effects of luteoloside in bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis in mice. 

## Discussion
Our study first reveals that luteoloside may bind to multiple residues of SIRT1 and upregulate its expression, restoring ROS-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction induced by bleomycin treatment, thereby inhibiting bleomycin-induced AECs senescence and alleviating the progression of pulmonary fibrosis.
IPF is a chronic and rapidly progressive disease with a highly malignant nature, and the median survival time after diagnosis is 2 to 3 years , . While various factors have been identified as increasing the risk of IPF, aging is considered the most significant risk factor – . Studies have shown that the prevalence and incidence of IPF increase significantly with age, with individuals over 70 having a 6.9-fold higher risk of developing IPF compared to those over 40 . Therefore, IPF can be regarded as a disease closely associated with aging. The senescence of AECs, which are essential for maintaining lung function, plays a crucial role in the development and progression of pulmonary fibrosis . Multiple genome-wide screening studies have identified AEC dysfunction, a reduction in cell number, and cellular senescence as key mechanisms in IPF pathogenesis . For example, the telomere-related gene Tert, a major genetic variant in IPF, when deleted in mice, still requires exposure to LPS or bleomycin treatment to enhance fibrosis in the third generation of mice . Additionally, the deletion of key genes such as BMP4 or microRNA-33 has been shown to either promote or alleviate the progression of pulmonary fibrosis through senescence , . This suggests that the cumulative effects of senescence play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of IPF. SASP of senescent cells significantly influences the onset and progression of pulmonary fibrosis . The release of a wide array of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and proteases from SASP lead to chronic low-grade inflammation in the local microenvironment, which disrupts the function of surrounding healthy cells and exacerbates fibrosis through cellular interactions . In the context of pulmonary fibrosis, SASP not only promotes the proliferation of fibroblasts and their differentiation into myofibroblasts, but also enhances immune responses and inflammation, further fueling the vicious cycle of fibrosis . Moreover, SASP may impair intercellular signaling pathways, leading to the loss of tissue repair capacity and ultimately accelerating structural damage and functional loss in the lungs . Therefore, the SASP phenotype of senescent cells is a key factor in promoting pulmonary fibrosis. Inhibition of senescence has been shown to effectively alleviate the progression of pulmonary fibrosis . Compounds such as spermidine, salvianolic acid B, and baicalein have been demonstrated to suppress cellular senescence and reduce experimental pulmonary fibrosis , , . Evidence reveals a pathological interplay between cellular senescence and EMT, mediated by β-catenin stabilization in senescent AECs which promotes EMT through transcriptional activation, while EMT-derived cells further propagate senescence via SASP factors . Our results demonstrate luteoloside’s concurrent suppression of β-catenin signaling and senescence markers, suggesting its unique capacity to disrupt this vicious cycle. This dual inhibition likely contributes to its superior anti-fibrotic efficacy compared to single-target approaches. This study also suggests that luteoloside can alleviate bleomycin-induced lung senescence and fibrosis in mice, indicating that luteoloside may be a promising anti-senescence agent. However, further validation using additional models is needed to confirm the anti-senescence activity of luteoloside, particularly in the context of senescence or fibrosis induced by various causes.
Mitochondrial dysfunction and cellular senescence often occur concurrently and mutually regulate each other . Research suggests that the maintenance of mitochondrial function plays a crucial role in regulating ROS during cellular senescence, thereby preserving normal cellular function . In the context of fibrosis, mitochondrial dysfunction in AECs accelerates the excessive production of mitochondrial ROS, leading to the formation of a SASP phenotype and the promotion of pro-fibrotic factors , . Treatment strategies that regulate mitochondrial function have been shown to alleviate fibrosis progression. For instance, human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells and SH2 Domain-Containing Phosphatase-SHP2 can restore mitochondrial function and inhibit pulmonary fibrosis , . However, research on antifibrotic drugs aimed at mitochondrial function restoration remains limited. Our study found that luteoloside reduces bleomycin-induced oxidative stress both in vitro and in vivo, and in AECs, it prevents the increase in mitochondrial membrane potential while upregulating the expression of MFN1, MFN2, and DRP1, indicating a restoration of mitochondrial function. Considering the established role of NF-κB in both SASP regulation and fibrotic progression, and given luteolin’s known NF-κB inhibitory capacity, it would be valuable to explore whether luteoloside’s glycosylated structure influences NF-κB signaling in the fibrotic process , . Mitochondrial-derived ROS and damage-associated molecular patterns are potent NF-κB activators, suggesting that luteoloside’s reduction effects in oxidative stress may indirectly modulate this pathway. Future investigations comparing the NF-κB inhibitory potential of luteoloside versus luteolin, while accounting for their differential effects on mitochondrial function, could reveal important structure–activity relationships for optimizing anti-fibrotic therapeutics. The exploration of luteoloside’s effect on mitochondria is still in its early stages, and further validation through experiments such as ATP production and mitophagy assessments is necessary.
The SIRT family is a group of NAD+ -dependent deacetylases involved in regulating various cellular physiological processes, including metabolism, stress responses, aging, and the cell cycle . Among the SIRT family, SIRT1, SIRT3, SIRT4, and SIRT5 play particularly important roles in regulating mitochondrial function and aging . Studies have shown that they influence mitochondrial aging by modulating protein acetylation, energy metabolism, oxidative stress, and autophagy processes within mitochondria . According to our research, the detection of SIRT family mRNA levels indicates that luteoloside significantly promotes the expression of SIRT1. As an essential component of the SIRT family, SIRT1 is a critical target for regulating mitochondrial function. Studies suggest that SIRT1 promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and energy metabolism by deacetylating key mitochondrial proteins such as PGC-1α . SIRT1 can also regulate enzymes associated with mitochondrial function, such as AMPK and FOXO3, through deacetylation, thereby enhancing cellular metabolic balance and antioxidant capacity , . By deacetylating and inhibiting the activation of aging-related genes (such as P53 and P16), SIRT1 delays the process of cellular aging . Additionally, SIRT1 reduces ROS production and lowers the risk of cellular aging by modulating mitochondrial antioxidant enzymes . Specifically, SIRT1 activates autophagy to promote the clearance of senescent cells, thus slowing the aging process . Consistent with our findings, treatment with luteoloside significantly promotes the expression of SIRT1 both in vitro and in vivo, restoring mitochondrial function. However, the underlying mediating factors still require further research for deeper exploration. Moreover, the use of EX527 at the overall level mediated luteoloside’s anti-fibrotic, anti-aging, and antioxidant effects.
The anti-senescence activity, coupled with luteoloside’s natural origin and previously demonstrated safety profile, positions it as a particularly promising candidate for clinical development targeting cellular senescence in fibrotic lung diseases. These results highlight the therapeutic potential of Luteoloside in mitigating pulmonary fibrosis; however, this study has certain limitations. While our data confirms biological efficacy, further comprehensive pharmacokinetic studies are urgently needed to optimize its drug-like properties. This includes detailed analysis of its oral bioavailability, metabolic stability, distribution, and retention efficiency within lung tissue to ensure adequate exposure at the site of fibrotic injury. To address absorption and targeting challenges, future research should strategically explore advanced delivery systems, such as nano-formulation or inhalation strategies, specifically designed to enhance pulmonary bioavailability while minimizing systemic exposure and potential off-target effects.

## Conclusions
In summary, our data suggests that luteoloside, as an effective anti-senescence and anti-fibrotic agent, may exert its effects by binding to multiple residues of SIRT1, upregulating SIRT1 expression, and restoring mitochondrial function, thereby promoting anti-senescence and anti-fibrotic effects (Fig.  ).
