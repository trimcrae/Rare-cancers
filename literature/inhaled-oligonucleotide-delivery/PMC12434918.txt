Brain aging is indisputably the most significant risk factor for AD. Given that aging is a fundamental driving force behind the onset of AD, identifying the aging - regulated genes that contribute to AD development is of utmost importance. Such genes might hold the key to preventing AD or delaying the transition from normal aging to the disease state. In the present study, a comprehensive bioinformatic analysis was conducted on brain transcriptomic datasets obtained from both aging individuals and those with Alzheimer’s disease. Among the shared differentially expressed genes, eight genes were found to be downregulated in both aging and AD datasets. Notably, reduced expression of adaptor protein complex 1 sigma 1 subunit (Ap1s1) was validated across multiple mouse models with varying degree of dementia, including aged mice, senescence-accelerated SAMP8 mice, 5xFAD amyloidosis mice, as well as cellular models, including senescent Neuro-2a (N2a) cells, and Aβ-treated or expressing N2a neurons. Functional studies revealed that Ap1s1 knockdown induced cellular senescence without directly impairing viability. However, Ap1s1 silencing exacerbated neuronal vulnerability to oxidative stress (H₂O₂) and Aβ toxicity, manifesting as Golgi-dispersion and reduced survival. Proteomic profiling following Ap1s1 depletion implicated dysregulation of rRNA modifications in the nucleus and cytosol, Golgi-associated vesicle biogenesis. These findings position Ap1s1 as a critical aging-related gene at the nexus of brain aging and AD pathogenesis, whose decline may predispose neurons to Alzheimer’s-related insults. As such, Ap1s1 may represent a potential therapeutic target for mitigating aging-related cognitive decline and delaying the onset of AD.

## Introduction
Alzheimer’s disease (AD), a progressive and irreversible neurodegenerative disorder, which poses a growing threat to public health around the globe. It is characterized by the gradual deterioration of cognitive functions, ranging from mild memory loss to severe systemic dysfunction and ultimately death. Currently, there are no effective disease-modifying treatments for AD [ ]. Epidemiological forecasts suggest that the aging population will lead to a significant increase in AD prevalence, with global cases to be tripled by 2050 [ ]. Although oxidative stress and neuroinflammation are known contributors to AD pathogenesis [ ], the precise molecular mechanisms that link brain aging to AD-related cognitive decline remain unclear [ ].
Aging is the primary risk factor for both sporadic AD and Alzheimer’s Disease Related Dementias (ADRD) [ ]. It should be pointed out that the early signs of neuropathological processes often start several decades prior to the emergence of clinical symptoms [ ]. This extended preclinical phase suggests a biological continuum between normal aging and symptomatic cognitive decline, mediated by shared mechanisms such as mitochondrial dysfunction, protein metabolism disturbance, and chronic low-grade inflammation [ – ]. It should be noted that advanced age is closely associated with prevalence of AD, but not all elderly individuals progress to dementia [ ]. This clinical heterogeneity suggests that age-related dysregulation of specific monitoring molecules may be the basis for the transition from healthy aging to neurodegeneration [ – ].
Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified overlapping genetic risk loci for aging and AD [ – ]. However, conventional approaches that analyze these conditions independently fail to distinguish causal susceptibility factors from secondary aging-associated changes. Recent research by Grodstein et al. suggests that age-related transcriptional dysregulation in specific gene networks may increase vulnerability to AD stressors such as Aβ oligomers and oxidative damage [ , ]. Identifying such molecular regulators could transform therapeutic strategies by targeting the upstream drivers of the aging-AD continuum rather than downstream pathologies.
The present study investigated key genes involved in both aging and AD through bioinformatics approaches. By combining brain transcriptomic data from human aging and AD, we identified essential target genes central to their development. We establish various models of aging and AD induced by different factors to validate these genes’ changes and functions. Eight potential critical genes were identified, with Ap1s1 showing a declining trend in both aging and AD models, impacting survival upon depletion. We further explored how Ap1s1 depletion affects neuronal response to external stressors. This study highlights the importance of Ap1s1 in the context of aging and AD. It emphasizes how aging influences neurodegeneration and the need for targeted interventions. Ultimately, this research underscores the complex relationship between aging and AD pathogenesis.

## Methods & materials

## Bioinformatic analysis
Gene expression datasets related to Alzheimer’s disease and normal aging brains were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Specifically, we analyzed the aging-associated datasets GSE53890 and GSE1572, and the Alzheimer’s disease (AD) dataset GSE5281. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the GEO2R online tool (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/geo2r), with adjusted p-values < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| >1 considered as significant thresholds.
In the aging dataset GSE53890, which includes 41 frontal cortex samples from non-demented individuals aged 19 to 106 years with a postmortem interval (PMI) of less than 20 h, we selected 15 samples from individuals younger than 45 years and 22 samples from individuals older than 60 years to represent young and aged groups, respectively.
In the aging dataset GSE1572, which consists 30 frontal cortex samples from non-demented postmortem brains with PMI < 30 h (average PMI = 10.5 h) and age ranging from 26 to 106 years, 10 samples from individuals under 45 years and 15 samples over 60 years were included for comparative analysis.
For the Alzheimer’s disease dataset GSE5281, which contains 161 brain tissue samples (87 from AD patients and 74 from non-demented controls), we focused on late-onset AD by selecting only individuals above 60 years of age, thereby reducing confounding effects from genetic or early-onset forms. We included 18 AD patients and 11 age-matched non-demented controls for differential gene expression analysis.
For visualization of the DEGs, generation of heatmaps, and conducting cross-analyses, the Sangerbox platform (accessible at http://sangerbox.com/login.html) was utilized. Additionally, the R package ‘randomForest’ was applied to perform random forest variable importance analysis (Figure ).

## In vivo experiments

## Mouse cultivation

## Naturally aging mouse model
For the naturally aging model, 40 C57BL/6J mice were initially housed under SPF conditions beginning at 4 months of age. At 4, 12, 18, and 24 months, seven mice with comparable body weight were selected at each time point to undergo Morris Water Maze (MWM) testing to evaluate spatial learning and memory. After behavioral assessment, brain tissues—including the cortex and hippocampus—were immediately dissected, transferred into RNase- and DNase-free tubes, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C. All samples were batch-processed for molecular assays (e.g., qPCR, Western blot) at the endpoint (24 months) to ensure consistency across time points.

## SAMP8/SAMR1 accelerated aging model
SPF-grade male SAMP8 and SAMR1 mice (24 weeks old) were purchased from Peking University. To account for the early-onset aging phenotype of SAMP8 mice, behavioral testing was conducted shortly after arrival, and mice were housed in groups of four per cage to minimize aggression and stress. Behavioral assays, tissue collection, and molecular analyses followed the same protocol as the natural aging group. All procedures were performed under SPF conditions, with routine health monitoring.

## Aβ1-42 stereotaxic injection model
To model Aβ-induced pathology, male SPF-grade C57BL/6J mice (aged 16 weeks; Yisi Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd., Changchun, China) were stereotactically injected with oligomerized Aβ₁–₄₂ peptides into the hippocampus. Aβ₁–₄₂ peptides (Abcam, UK) were first dissolved in DMSO (5 mM), diluted in PBS to 100 µM, and incubated at 4 °C for 24 h to form oligomers. Mice were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital and fixed in a stereotaxic apparatus. A total of 5 µL oligomeric Aβ₁–₄₂ was bilaterally injected into the hippocampus (AP: −2.0 mm; ML: ±1.3 mm; DV: −2.2 mm) at 0.5 µL/min. The needle was held in place for 5 min post-injection. Sham controls received 5 µL of sterile PBS. Mice recovered for 7 days before behavioral testing and brain tissue harvesting.

## 5xFAD transgenic model
SPF-grade male 5xFAD mice (12 weeks old) and age-matched wild-type (WT) littermates were obtained from Shanghai Southern Model Biological Technology Co., Ltd. Mice were housed in groups of four per cage. Behavioral testing (e.g., MWM) was initiated at 5 months of age, when 5xFAD mice typically display robust AD-like pathology. After behavioral evaluation, the cortex and hippocampus were dissected, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C for molecular analyses.

## MWM
One week prior to behavioral testing, mice were acclimated for seven days to the experimental environment, including handling, room conditions, and transportation, but were not exposed to the Morris water maze (MWM) apparatus, pool, or platform during this period. MWM testing then commenced, with the platform fixed in the northwest quadrant and the water maintained at 25 ± 1 °C. The pool was rendered opaque with a sterilized titanium dioxide solution (changed every two days), and external visual cues were minimized by covering the tank and providing fixed spatial references such as colored paperboards and a black cube. During the five-day training phase, mice were randomly released from any of the four quadrants and underwent four trials per day. If a mouse failed to find the hidden platform within 60 s, it was guided to it, and the latency was recorded as 60 s. Swim paths and escape latencies were tracked using a video analysis system. Twenty-four hours after the last training day, a spatial probe trial was conducted to assess memory retention: the platform was removed, and mice were released from the opposite quadrant, with their search behavior recorded for 60 s to evaluate spatial memory performance.

## Euthanasia, tissue extraction, and storage
All mice were euthanized by inhalation of an overdose of isoflurane, in strict accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. Following euthanasia, brains were rapidly extracted on ice for subsequent analyses.
For naturally aging C57BL/6J mice, brain tissues were collected at 4, 12, 18, and 24 months of age. Whole brains were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C. These samples were later used for batch processing of total protein homogenization for western blotting and RNA extraction for gene expression analysis.
In the SAMP8 and SAMR1 accelerated aging models, as well as in the Aβ₁–₄₂ hippocampal injection and 5×FAD transgenic AD models, hippocampal and cortical regions were carefully dissected under a stereomicroscope. The isolated brain regions were transferred into sterile, RNase- and DNase-free microcentrifuge tubes, immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until simultaneous downstream processing.

## In vitro experiments

## Cell culture
The Neuro-2a mouse neuroblastoma cell line, procured from Cyagen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Product Number: M8-0101). Neuro-2a cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Upon reaching 90% confluence, the cells are subjected to digestion using 0.25% trypsin with EDTA for passaging purposes. The cells are maintained in a constant temperature incubator set at 37 °C with 5% CO2.
Mouse neuroblastoma N2a cells expressing Swedish mutant APP (N2a-APPswe) was a generously gift from Professor Dr. Yang Yu (The Key Laboratory of Medical Cell Biology, Institute of Translational Medicine, China Medical University, Shenyang, China) and tested for mycoplasma contamination before use. These N2a-APPswe cells secrete human Aβ peptides Aβ40 and Aβ42, thereby serving as a cellular model for investigating AD related to Aβ pathology. N2a-APPswe cells were grown in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 200 µg/mL of G418. Upon reaching 90% confluence, the cells are treated with 0.25% trypsin for digestion to facilitate passaging. The cells are maintained at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The procedure for preparing conditioned medium from N2a-APPswe cells is as follows: When the cells in a 10 cm culture dish achieve 80% confluence, the medium is replaced with 8 mL of DMEM/F12, and the cells are cultured for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, the medium is centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min to remove cellular debris, and the resulting supernatant constitutes the conditioned medium.

## Cellular model

## Cellular model of senescence
To establish an in vitro model of oxidative stress-induced neuronal senescence, murine neuroblastoma Neuro-2a (N2a) cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well. Cells were cultured in complete Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. Once cells reached 70–80% confluence, premature senescence was induced by treatment with 60 µM hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 48 h.

## Conditioned medium treatment
N2a-APP cells were seeded in 100 petri dish and grown until 80% confluence. After washing with PBS, the cells were then incubated with serum-free DMEM/F12 for 24 h. The resultant media contains APP.
The resulting CM was centrifuged (300 × g, 5 min) to remove debris and then applied to wild-type N2a cells to induce Aβ insult.

## β-galactosidase assay
After setting up the H2O2-induced senescence model, perform SA-β-Gal staining using the Cellular Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China, C0602). For cells in a 6-well plate, aspirate the culture medium, wash with PBS, and fix with 1 mL of β-galactosidase staining fixative at room temperature for 15 min. Rinse the cells three times with PBS for 3 min each. Add 1 mL of staining working solution per well (with a solution ratio of 10:10:930:50 for A: B:C: X-Gal). Cover the plate with plastic wrap to prevent evaporation and incubate overnight at 37 °C. The cells were then imaged using inverted light microscope (IX 71, Olympus).

## Cell viability assay
Cell viability is evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) and MTT assays. N2a cells are seeded at 5000 cells per well in a 96-well plate. For the CCK-8 assay, cells are treated with N2a-APPswe supernatant or Ap1s1 siRNA for 24–48 h, followed by the addition of CCK-8 reagent and culture medium, and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h before measuring absorbance at 450 nm.
For the MTT assay, cells are treated with H2O2 for 24–48 h, then MTT solution is added and incubated for 2 h. After removing the supernatant and adding DMSO, absorbance is measured at 570 nm.

## siRNA transfection
The cells are freshly seeded in a six-well plate, so that cells reach approximately 50% confluency on the second day. Subsequently, 5 µl of siRNA and 10 µl of lipofectamine 2000 were added to 400 µl basal DMEM media at room temperature for 20 min and added to the cells. 6 h later, the medium containing transfection mixture were replaced with fresh growth medium. Cells are subsequently collected for RNA and protein extraction at 48, 72, and 96 h after transfection. The efficiency of transfection is evaluated through RT-qPCR or Western Blot analysis. The specific siRNA sequences utilized are detailed in Table .

## Immunofluorescence staining
N2a cells were seeded on poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips (0.01 mg/mL, 24 h) in 6-well plates. After Ap1s1 siRNA transfection for 72 h, cells were treated with 60 µM H₂O₂ or N2a-APPswe-conditioned medium for 24 h. Cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 8 min, and blocked with a mixture of1% goat serum and 5% BSA in PBS for 30 min.
The cells were the incubated with primary antibody against TGN46 (1:300, Abcam, ab16059) and Phalloidin-iFluor 488 (1:200, Abcam, ab176753) in blocking solution overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor® 594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1,000, Abcam, ab150080) for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (1:1,000, Beyotime, C1025). Samples were mounted with FluorSave mounting medium (Calbiochem, 345789) and imaged using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope. Image processing and quantification were performed with ImageJ software.

## RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR
Total cellular RNA is extracted using Trizol reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific, UK) and a chloroform alternative reagent (FOREGENE, China). RNA concentration was determined with NanoDrop 1000 (ThermoFisher Scientific, UK). Complementary DNA (cDNA) is obtained from total RNA using a cDNA synthesis kit (TransGen Biotech, China). Quantitative PCR is performed using FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) is used as a normalized internal control, and the 2-ΔΔCt method is employed. The qPCR primers are listed in Table .

## Protein western blotting analysis
N2a cells were seeded in 6-well plates and subjected to treatment with either H2O2, N2a-APPswe conditioned media for 24–48 h, or Ap1s1 siRNA for 48, 72, and 96 h. RIPA lysis buffer containing EDTA, protease inhibitors, and phosphatase inhibitors, was added to each well. The cells were lysed for 30 min on ice, and the resulting lysate was transferred to a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube. Subsequently, cell were sonicated and centrifuged at 13,500 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was carefully collected.
Protein concentration was quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China, P0009), with a standard curve employed to determine the protein concentration of the samples. For electrophoresis, 5×SDS-PAGE sample buffer was added to the protein samples. The samples were heated at 95 °C for 5 min.
20 µg protein were loaded onto the gel, and electrophoresis were ran at a constant voltage of 200 V for a duration of 45 min. This was followed by a transfer at a constant current of 300 mA for 30 min. Subsequently, the membrane was blocked using 5% non-fat dry milk for 1.5 h. After 3x washing with TBST, each lasting 5 min. The membrane was incubated with primary antibody at 4℃ overnight. The membrane was again washed three times with TBST, each wash lasting 5 min. On the following day, the corresponding secondary antibody was incubated for 2 h, followed by three additional washes with TBST, each for 5 min.
The ECL reagent kit’s A and B liquids are mixed with deionized water in a 1:1:2 ratio. The PVDF membrane is moistened in the developing solution, and strips are exposed in the developer to capture images. Each experiment is conducted at least three times, and band density is analyzed using ImageJ software. Antibodies against Ap1s1 (Thermofisher scientific, Cot. No. PA5-63913) were used at a dilution of 1:1000 for Western blotting. GAPDH (Affinity, Cot. No. T0004) was used as a loading control at a dilution of 1:2000. HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (Proteintech, Cot. No. SA00001-2) and HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) (Proteintech, Cot. No. SA00001-1) were used at a dilution of 1:5000 for Western blotting.

## Data-independent acquisition (DIA) proteomics
To assess the proteomic alterations induced by Ap1s1 knockdown, a data-independent acquisition (DIA)–based quantitative proteomics workflow was employed. Six neuronal cell samples (NC, n = 3; siAp1s1, n = 3) were prepared under standardized conditions. Proteins were extracted, quantified by BCA assay, verified by SDS-PAGE, and digested using the filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) protocol with trypsin.
Peptides were separated on a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system coupled to a µPAC Neo column and analyzed on an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) in DIA mode (full MS resolution 240,000; scan range 380–980 m/z; MS/MS resolution 80,000).
Raw data were processed with DIA-NN (v1.8) against the UniProt Mus musculus database (release 2024-07-25) using a 1% false discovery rate (FDR) at both peptide and protein levels. Proteins quantified in ≥ 50% of samples were retained, and missing values were imputed. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were defined as those with |fold change| ≥ 1.5 and p < 0.05.

## Statistical analysis
All cellular experiments were independently performed in triplicate unless otherwise specified. For animal studies, each group included a minimum of five mice, with exact sample sizes detailed in the figure legends. All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Prior to statistical comparisons, data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For two-group comparisons, normally distributed data were analyzed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests; for non-normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. For multiple group comparisons, one-way or two-way ANOVA was applied followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test (for parametric data) or Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test (for non-parametric data), as appropriate. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 9.5. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Specific statistical methods used for each experiment are indicated in the respective figure legends.

## Results

## Mining and validation of key genes that regulates aging
To explore for the key genes involved in the shift from aging to neurodegenerative disorders (NDDs) like Alzheimer’s disease, we first performed DEG analysis from two independent datasets GSE1572 and GSE53890. Samples from under 45 yrs old were considered young, whereas samples from older than 60 yrs were considered aged, with adjusted p-values < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| >1 considered as significant thresholds. As shown in Fig.  A and B, In dataset GSE1572, 206 genes were upregulated, whereas 281 gene were downregulated. In dataset GSE53890, 703 genes were upregulated, 487 genes were downregulated. To reduce the batch effects, we intersected DEGs, and identified 85 genes that were co-upregulated and 146 genes were co-downregulated in both datasets. We consider those genes to be truly involved in human brain aging.

 

To identify key genes involved in AD and aging, we performed DEGs analysis comparing AD- and non-AD samples from aged population (> 60, GSE5281), with adjusted p-values < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| >1 considered as significant thresholds. As shown in Figs.  C and 560 genes were upregulated, and 8001 genes were downregulated. We then intersected the DEGs of aging and AD datasets, resulted in 68 genes that are downregulated and 39 genes that are upregulated (Fig.  C-D).
To select the genes with the highest correlation with aging or AD in each dataset, we then performed random forest analysis using those 107 genes which are commonly differentially expressed cross those datasets. Genes with Gini importance value exceeding 0.2 were selected. We found 17 genes in GSE1572, 28 genes in GSE53890 (aging dataset) and 15 genes in GSE5281 (AD data set) were in high correlation with their respective disease status (Fig.  E, F). After merging those aging related genes, and intersected with AD genes, then we acquired 8 genes that might contribute to the shift from aging to AD (Fig.  G), namely Ap1s1, Htr2a, Vdac1, Wwtr1, Pon2, Nudt21, Mt2a and Ppm1e. Subsequently, the expression level of those genes in GSE5281 data set were shown in heatmap (Fig.  H). Ap1s1, Htr2a, Vdac1, Nudt21, and Ppm1e were down-regulated, while Wwtr1, Pon2, and Mt2a were up-regulated, which are consistent with those observed in the aging database (Fig.  H).

## Ap1s1 is the common gene related to cognitive decline in both aging and Alzheimer’s disease progression

## Gene expression in age-related cognitive decline
To validate the age- and AD-related changes of the eight candidate genes, we established multiple in vivo models, including natural aging and Samp8 accelerated aging mice, as well as AD models such as Aβ1−42 intracerebral injection and 5xFAD transgenic mice. To clarify the patterns of gene expression at different age stages, we first established a natural aging mouse model (Figure A-D).

 

To clarify the patterns of gene expression in at different age-stage, we established a natural aging mouse model. Spatial memory ability were examined by Morris’s water maze (MWM) test as shown in Fig.  . On the first to third days of the positioning cruise experiment, there was no significant difference in the escape latency of mice in each group. On the fourth day of the experiment, 18- and 24-month-old mice took significantly longer to escape than 4- and 12-month-olds (Fig.  A). By the fifth day, 24-month-olds showed even longer escape times compared to 4-month-olds, while no difference was observed between 12- and 4-month-olds (Fig.  B). In a subsequent spatial exploration test, where the escape platform was removed, 24-month-olds spent significantly less time in the target quadrant than 4- and 12-month-olds (Fig.  C). Additionally, 18- and 24-month-olds crossed the former platform area significantly fewer times than 12-month-olds (Fig.  D). Additionally, no significant differences were observed in the distance traveled within the target quadrant or in swimming speed among the groups (Figure E-F). Those results suggest C57bl6/J mice showed gradual cognitive decline upon 18 month and further decrease at 24 months.

 

To validate the results of bioinformatics analysis and to explore the possible association between age induced cognitive decline with the expression of aging genes, we examined the mRNA levels of eight key genes (Ap1s1, Htr2a, Vdac1, Wwtr1, Pon2, Nudt21, Mt2a, and Ppm1e) across different ages. The mRNA level of Ap1s1 decreased with age, particularly showing a significant reduction at month 24. The mRNA level of Vdac1 showed a significant decrease at 18 and 24 months of age. Additionally, there was a decreasing trend in the mRNA levels of Htr2a, Wwtr1, and Nudt21 from 12 to 18 months of age, but without significant differences (Fig.  G). The results indicate that the changes in Ap1s1 and Vdac1 mRNA levels follow the similar trend during the aging process in mice as well as in human (Fig.  G). To further validate the expression of 8 genes in different aging models, We evaluated cognitive alterations in senescence-accelerated mouse prone 8 (Samp8) mice using the MWM test. The swimming trajectories of the mice are shown in Fig.  A. The escape latency of the Samp8 mice began to increase from the third day and showed a significantly difference by the fifth day. The number of platform crossings by Samp8 mice significantly decreased. Moreover, although there was a downward trend in the time spent in the target quadrant, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig.  B). We subsequently investigated the expression levels of eight genes in the hippocampus and cortex of Samp8 mice. Only the Ap1s1 gene demonstrated a significant reduction in expression in both the hippocampus and cortex, whereas no substantial alterations were detected in other genes (Fig.  C, D).

 

In summary, the decreasing trend of Ap1s1 and Vdac1 mRNA levels during mouse aging is consistent with the downregulation trend of genes in human aging, suggesting that these two genes may play important roles in the process of aging leading to cognitive function changes and even developing into AD.

## Expression of genes associated with alzheimer’s cognitive dysfunction
Initially, we developed a murine AD model through the intracerebral microinjection of Aβ1−42. The swimming trajectories of the mice in the MWM is shown in Fig.  E. Aβ1−42 injected AD mice exhibited a persistently longer escape latency from the second day on (Fig.  E). AD induced by Aβ1−42 showed a significant reduction in the number of platform crossings (Fig.  F) and time spent the target quadrant (Fig.  F). There was a significant downregulation of both Ap1s1 and Htr2a in the hippocampus (Fig.  G). However, in the cortex, Ap1s1 demonstrated a comparable decrease as in the hippocampus, whereas Htr2a expression exhibited an upward trend, and Wwtr1 showed a pronounced increase (Fig.  H).
we assessed cognitive function in transgenic 5xFAD mice [ ]. Comparing to wild-type mice, the escape latency of 5xFAD mice was consistently higher, and a significant statistical difference was observed on day 5 (Fig.  I). Moreover, the number of platform crossings by 5xFAD mice was significantly reduced compared to wild-type mice (Fig.  J), and the time spent in the target quadrant was also significantly shorter (Fig.  J). We examined the mRNA expression levels of the eight genes in the cortex and hippocampus of 5xFAD mice. (Fig.  K, L). The mRNA levels of Ap1s1, Vdac1, and Ppm1e were significantly decreased in the hippocampus (Fig.  K). In the cortex, only Ap1s1 and Ppm1e showed a significant decrease, while Vdac1 trended downward without reaching statistical significance (Fig.  L).
In conclusion, the findings of our research indicate a significant reduction in Ap1s1 mRNA in models of cognitive dysfunction induced by aging and AD.

## Reduced Ap1s1 protein expression in aging and alzheimer’s disease models
To assess whether Ap1s1 protein expression similarly declines in disease models, we analyzed its expression levels in animal models of aging and AD. Our findings indicate a significant reduction in Ap1s1 protein expression in both naturally aged mice and Samp8 senescence-accelerated mice (Fig.  M, N). Additionally, analysis of the 5xFAD model revealed a statistically significant decrease in Ap1s1 protein expression within the hippocampus and cortex (Fig.  O).
In line with our prior bioinformatics analyses, those results indicate Ap1s1 may function a pivotal regulator in the progression of aging and AD.

## Ap1s1 decreases upon cellular senescence and Aβ insult in vitro
To further clarify the cellular basis of its downregulation, we first validated the expression profile of Ap1s1 in brain tissues at the animal level. Primary neurons, astrocytes, and microglia were isolated and cultured from rat brains, and Ap1s1 expression was assessed at both the mRNA and protein levels. The results revealed that Ap1s1 expression was significantly higher in neurons compared to glial cell types, suggesting that Ap1s1 primarily exerts its physiological functions in neurons (Figure A–B). Complementary analysis of single-cell transcriptomic data (scRNA-seq) across different ages further confirmed this cell-type specificity. In young mice, Ap1s1 was predominantly enriched in neuronal populations, with particularly high activity in the hippocampus; in contrast, aged mice exhibited a marked decline of neuronal Ap1s1 expression, whereas glial cells maintained relatively low and unchanged levels (Figure C). These findings highlight that Ap1s1 displays neuron-specific expression patterns and undergoes region- and cell type–specific downregulation during aging.
To investigate the relationship between Ap1s1 expression and cellular senescence, we employed a hydrogen peroxide-induced senescent model on N2a cells (Fig.  A). H2O2 treatment induced of cellular senescence markers, including IL-6, p21, and p53 (Figs.  B, C), as well as increased in β-galactosidase activity (Fig.  D), without compromising cell viability (Fig.  A). Notably, H2O2 treatment led to a reduction in Ap1s1 expression at both the protein and mRNA levels (Figs.  E, F). To further examine the association between Ap1s1 and Alzheimer’s disease, we utilized N2a-app. The conditioned media from N2a-app cells resulted in elevated mRNA levels of p53 and IL-6 (Figs.  H and I). Additionally, N2a-app conditioned medium significantly decreased Ap1s1 expression at both mRNA and protein levels (Figs.  J, K). Furthermore, the mRNA and protein expression levels of Ap1s1 were markedly lower in N2a-app cells compared to N2a cells (Figs.  L, M). These findings suggest that Ap1s1 may serve as a novel risk factor for cellular senescence and Aβ insult.

 

## Ap1s1 knockdown induces cellular senescence in vitro
To validate whether Ap1s1 might function as common key regulator in aging and AD, we knocked down Ap1s1 by RNA interference. We tested siRNA with two distinct target sequences, #1 and #2, the expression of Ap1s1 were both reduced at mRNA and at protein level at various time points (Fig.  A-C). As co-transfection of #1 and #2 target siRNA resulted in higher level of knockdown at 48 h, we used co-transfection those siRNAs in the following experiments (Fig.  C). Silencing Ap1s1 alone, increased senescent marker expression (Fig.  D) and increased β-galactosidase (Fig.  F) without affecting cell viability (Fig.  G). Those results suggest that suppression of Ap1s1 might accelerate the aging process without affecting cell viability. This finding led us to question whether the impact of Ap1s1 on Alzheimer’s disease is dependent on its role in enhancing neuronal vulnerability during aging and AD.

 

## Ap1s1 knockdown increases neuronal vulnerability to exogenous stimuli
To assess whether the knockdown of Ap1s1 enhances cellular sensitivity to external stimulations, we employed H2O2 and N2a-app conditioned medium following Ap1s1 knockdown.
As expected, in N2a cell are co-treated with H2O2 or N2a-app conditioned media, we observed a drastic reduction of viability (Fig.  H, I). Those results suggest Ap1s1 might be a key protein in resisting exogenous cytotoxic factors.
We postulate that the involvement of Ap1s1 in aging and Alzheimer’s disease may be attributed to its physiological function. Subsequently, we investigate the potential mechanisms by which a constituent of the AP-1 complex may modulate cellular responses to external neurotoxic stimuli. Specifically, Ap1s1 plays a role in the formation of clathrin-coated vesicles at the trans-Golgi network [ ]. Studies have indicated that the knockdown of the AP-1 complex may lead to the retention of Golgi-resident proteins and the hyperactivation of signaling pathways. To investigate the impact of Ap1s1 gene knockdown on the structure of the Golgi apparatus under conditions of aging and AD, we first silenced Ap1s1 expression via siRNA, then performed immunostaining of trans-Golgi network marker TGN46. Comparing to non-targeting siRNA transfected samples, Ap1s1 silencing exhibited larger TGN46 clusters (Fig.  J). Surprisingly, when the cells are co-treated with H2O2 or N2a-app conditioned media, Ap1s1 silencing induced dispersion of TGN46, an indication of fragmentation of the Golgi apparatus (Fig.  K). Those data suggest Ap1s1 might function as an internal defense mechanism that shield the cells to external cytotoxic effects.

## Discussion
Aging is a widely recognized critical risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease. However, not every elderly individual develops AD has spurred interest in identifying critical genetic factors that confer increased susceptibility to AD in the aging brain. Despite advances in high-throughput genomic and proteomic techniques, the susceptibility genetic that modulate vulnerability of aging neurons to AD related pathogenic factors remain elusive. The discovery of molecular changes may explain why some aging individuals become more vulnerable to AD.
In this study, we identified Ap1s1 as a gene commonly dysregulated in both aging and AD, suggesting its potential involvement in the shared pathological landscape of these conditions. Our findings indicate that Ap1s1 serves as a critical regulator of neuronal senescence, and its downregulation in aging neurons heightens their susceptibility to AD-related stressors. Mechanistically, Ap1s1 may contribute to aging and AD vulnerability by modulating senescence-associated pathways and amplifying cellular responses to various stimuli and stress signals. These effects are likely mediated through disruptions in Golgi apparatus function, which impair rRNA and protein processing and lead to broader disturbances in protein homeostasis. Our study highlights the importance of uncovering key susceptibility genes that drive the aging process toward AD. It not only enhances our understanding of the underlying mechanisms driving AD pathogenesis but also holds the promise of enabling early diagnostic biomarkers and the development of targeted therapeutic interventions to mitigate the disease’s impact in the elderly population.
Aging is a major risk factor for cognitive impairment in Alzheimer’s disease, with both processes sharing common molecular and cellular features contribute to the increased susceptibility of the aging brain to AD pathology. Gene expression changes are central to both aging and AD. Avramopoulos et al. highlight the genetic overlap between aging and AD, supporting the idea that aging serves as a major risk factor for AD onset. Bellenguez et al. build on this by identifying novel genetic factors, particularly in immune-related pathways, that contribute to AD risk. Lanke et al. focus on the hippocampus, showing that disruptions in gene networks critical for synaptic function are common to both aging and AD [ – ]. These studies underline the importance of understanding genetic factors that drive aging and AD, offering potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for early intervention and disease prevention. However, current gene discovery strategies often rely on single datasets, making them vulnerable to batch effects and technical variation. This limits their ability to reveal molecular overlaps between aging and Alzheimer’s disease [ ]. To address this, we integrated two independent aging-related datasets (GSE1572 and GSE53890), enabling cross-validation of gene expression changes and minimizing dataset-specific noise. By contrasting young and aged without AD, aged non-AD samples and aged AD, we initially identified downregulated eight genes (Ap1s1, Htr2a, Vdac1, Wwtr1, Pon2, Nudt21, Mt2a, and Ppm1e) as potential drivers of the aging-to-AD transition. Subsequent validation across multiple animal models revealed that only Ap1s1 exhibited a consistent and reproducible downregulation trend across diverse pathological contexts, whereas other genes lacked statistical significance or showed inconsistent patterns. Nevertheless, a limitation of our study lies in the relatively small sample size used for certain molecular assays, particularly protein-level validations (n = 3 per group), which may reduce statistical power. Although we increased the number of animals for mRNA analyses to six per group to enhance robustness, future work will require larger cohorts to strengthen reproducibility and firmly establish the molecular link between Ap1s1 dysregulation, aging, and AD progression. Ap1s1 is confirmed for the first time to be significantly downregulated in both aging and Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Ap1s1 expression levels were notably altered in the hippocampus and cortex, regions most affected by cognitive impairment. Ap1s1 expression in primary neurons, astrocytes, and microglia were further quantified, and observed that Ap1s1 is predominantly expressed in neurons. This finding was further supported by single-cell transcriptomic data from GSE207848, which revealed a pronounced age-related decline of Ap1s1 expression in neurons, particularly within the hippocampus, while glial populations remained relatively unaffected (GSE207848). Highlighting its potential role as a key susceptibility gene that may drive the transition from normal aging to AD.
Ap1s1 encodes the σ1A subunit of adaptor protein complex 1 (AP-1), a key mediator of intracellular protein trafficking from the trans-Golgi network (TGN) to endosomes and lysosomes [ ]. Previous studies have associated Ap1s1 loss with aberrant protein trafficking and STING pathway overactivation [ ]. Recent research has increasingly focused on the role of Ap1s1 downregulation in various pathologies. Ap1s1 downregulation has been shown to disrupt protein sorting and trafficking, leading to the accumulation of mis-localized proteins and the subsequent activation of cellular stress pathways [ – ], including neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, which are key contributors to AD progression. Studies have also suggested that reduced Ap1s1 expression impairs the function of the Golgi apparatus, further contributing to protein homeostasis imbalances in neurons [ , ]. These findings underline the importance of Ap1s1 in maintaining cellular integrity and its potential. However, to date, no studies have specifically reported or elucidated the role and underlying mechanisms of Ap1s1 downregulation in the transition from normal aging to AD.
While Ap1s1 is downregulated in both aging and AD, our findings suggest that its loss has distinct consequences in each context. In aging, reduced Ap1s1 expression appears to contribute to neuronal senescence and a general decline in cellular resilience. This decline in protein homeostasis and cellular function is a hallmark of aging, making neurons more vulnerable to stress and damage over time. In contrast, in AD, Ap1s1 downregulation exacerbates neuronal vulnerability to specific pathogenic factors such as oxidative stress and amyloid-beta toxicity. These stressors are central to AD pathology and contribute to neuronal degeneration, suggesting that Ap1s1 plays a protective role that is compromised in AD. These divergent effects may reflect the unique molecular pathways involved in aging versus AD, supporting the notion that the same mechanism can manifest differently depending on the disease context [ ]. It will highlight how these differential effects of Ap1s1 in aging and AD may provide valuable insights into how the two processes interact and contribute to the development of AD.
Furthermore, our findings highlight the potential threshold effect of Ap1s1 in mediating the transition from normal aging to AD. In the present study, bioinformatic analyses revealed a stepwise decline in Ap1s1 expression, with levels significantly lower in aged individuals compared to young controls, and further reduced in AD patients. This suggests that while moderate downregulation is part of normal aging, a more pronounced reduction beyond a critical threshold may be pathologically significant. In vivo, Ap1s1 protein levels showed a marked decline in naturally aging mice as early as 24 months, coinciding with the emergence of mild cognitive deficits. This decrease was even more pronounced in the senescence-accelerated SAMP8 model at 6 months of age, and in Aβ-driven models (Aβ1-42-injected and 5xFAD mice), where Ap1s1 suppression was further exacerbated under AD-specific pathology. These in vivo findings imply that early and sustained loss of Ap1s1 may sensitize neurons to subsequent neurodegenerative insults. Recent study also reported that gradual changes in gene expression are often tolerated, but a sharp decline beyond a certain threshold may predispose cells, especially neurons, to neurodegenerative processes [ , ]. Complementary in vitro assays demonstrated that Ap1s1 knockdown alone did not impair baseline viability, but significantly increased cellular susceptibility to oxidative stress and Aβ toxicity, underscoring its role in maintaining neuronal resilience under stress. These data collectively support the concept that residual Ap1s1 expression may suffice to maintain homeostasis under physiological conditions but fails to buffer pathological insults once it falls below a functional threshold. Thus, Ap1s1 appears to act as a susceptibility factor whose expression level may determine the tipping point between healthy aging and neurodegeneration. Studies have demonstrated that age-related changes in protein trafficking and Golgi apparatus function can contribute to the accumulation of misfolded proteins, a hallmark of neurodegenerative diseases like AD [ , ]. Similarly, our study has shown Ap1s1 crucial role and its dysfunction leads to cellular vulnerability to stress.
These findings align with emerging evidence that Ap1s1 loss enhances EGFR degradation and disrupts trans-Golgi network (TGN) organization, thereby compromising neuronal resilience [ ]. This threshold-based vulnerability may be mechanistically mediated through downstream disruptions triggered by Ap1s1 loss, such as alterations in protein trafficking, Golgi integrity, or stress signaling pathways.
To further explore the underlying molecular mechanisms by which Ap1s1 decline contributes to this increased susceptibility, we performed proteomic analysis using Ap1s1-silenced N2a cells. Emerging evidence indicates that dysregulated ribosomal RNA processing, disruptions in Golgi apparatus function, protein homeostasis disturbances and metabolism constitute a pivotal driver of cellular senescence [ ]. Dysregulation of ribosomal RNA processing has emerged as a key driver of cellular senescence, primarily through activation of p53 and pRb pathways under oncogenic or replicative stress [ – ]. DDX24 deficiency in human cancer cells leads to G1/S cell cycle arrest and ultimately triggers senescence [ ]. Notably, Dock4 KO mice displayed an age-related decline of hippocampal dependent spatial memory, showing decreased spatial memory in Barnes maze than their WT littermates at late middle age [ ]. Up-regulation of dock4 could be an internal compensation mechanism to overcome age-related cognitive decline under Ap1s1 knockdown condition.
A wealth of data supports the notion that heightened activity within the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade can lead to cellular senescence [ – ]. Interestingly, constitutive activation of AKT similarly triggers senescence in multiple cell types, highlighting a paradoxical role of the PI3K/AKT/mTORC1 pathway in promoting both growth and senescence [ , – ]. However, we demonstrate that Ap1s1 function in regulating cellular senescence. Knockdown Ap1s1 via siRNA, increased expression of protein enriched in constitutive signaling by AKT1 E17K, rapidly induces senescence in mouse neuron N2A cells. The accumulation of reactive oxygen species has been implicated as playing a role [ , ]. In line with this, in combination with H2O2 induced senescence cell model, Ap1s1 silencing potentiated neuronal cell death. A means of bypassing this senescence barrier would possibly function as a way of reducing neuronal loss in age-related cognitive decline, thus prevent is progression to AD.
Recent research has identified the Golgi apparatus as a key aging-associated biomarker, with multiple studies showing that its morphology becomes fragmented or swollen during aging, losing the typical layered structure [ – ]. These structural changes—driven by cytoskeletal alterations or dysregulated Golgi-associated proteins—disrupt vesicle trafficking and reduce secretion, leading to impaired processing and export of cellular materials. Such dysfunction can compromise neurotransmitter synthesis, protein homeostasis, and energy metabolism in neurons, ultimately contributing to their degeneration and death [ , ]. In line with this, our proteomic analysis showed, Ap1s1 silencing reduced expression of proteins involved in Golgi associated vesicle biogenesis and trans-Golgi network vesicle budding. Immunostaining of Trans-Golgi network integral membrane protein 2 (TGN46) showed no obvious alterations with Ap1s1 silencing alone. However, in N2a cells co-treated with H2O2 or Aβ-expressing N2a-app cells, showed fragmented and dispersed distribution. Our data suggested an involvement of trans-Golgi network dysfunction in the aging induced by Ap1s1 depletion neuronal cells. Steroid and cholesterol metabolism constitute a complex, multi-system axis that governs neuronal integrity across the lifespan. Ageing is accompanied by tissue-specific shifts in cholesterol biosynthesis; within the brain, and particularly in the hippocampus, de novo cholesterol synthesis declines, undermining synaptic plasticity, neurotransmission and neuroprotective signaling, thereby predisposing to neurodegeneration [ ].
Integrating these metabolic perturbations with our mechanistic findings, we propose that Ap1s1 operates at the nexus of Golgi homeostasis, RNA processing, and cholesterol-related stress pathways. Its age-dependent decline disrupts rRNA maturation, activates p53-mediated senescence, and amplifies neuronal vulnerability to oxidative and Aβ-induced toxicity. Notably, these effects become functionally consequential when Ap1s1 expression falls below a critical threshold—sufficient to sustain basal homeostasis, yet insufficient to buffer against pathological insults.
In summary, our study has identified Ap1s1 as a novel susceptibility gene in the aging-to-AD transition. Through its specific downregulation in neurons, particularly in the hippocampus, Ap1s1 dysregulation drives early subcellular disruptions such as Golgi fragmentation, which may contribute to the microenvironmental imbalance underlying AD pathogenesis. Given the role of Ap1s1 in exacerbating neuronal vulnerability to AD-specific pathologies, Ap1s1 represents a promising early intervention target. Early identification of Ap1s1 levels through biomarkers could enable clinicians to detect individuals at risk for AD, especially those in preclinical or mild cognitive impairment stages, allowing for timely intervention. To protect neurons from AD-related stressors such as amyloid-beta toxicity and oxidative stress, maintaining Ap1s1 levels above a critical threshold could be crucial. Therapeutic strategies to modulate Ap1s1 expression, particularly in vulnerable neuronal populations like those in the hippocampus, may enhance neuronal resilience and prevent neurodegeneration. Gene-editing technologies, such as CRISPR-Cas9, and pharmacological approaches to stabilize or increase Ap1s1 expression may provide effective therapeutic avenues for mitigating AD progression [ , ]. These findings underscore the translational potential of Ap1s1 as an early intervention target in the aging–AD continuum, providing new avenues for therapeutic development and clinical application.

## Conclusion
This study identifies Ap1s1 as a critical gene linking aging and Alzheimer’s disease. Reduced Ap1s1 expression not only induces neuronal senescence but also heightens neuronal susceptibility to oxidative stress and Aβ toxicity. This increased vulnerability manifests as Golgi fragmentation and decreased neuronal survival, associated with disrupted rRNA modifications and Golgi-associated vesicle biogenesis. The decline of Ap1s1 may thus drive the neuronal transition from normal aging to AD pathology. Consequently, Ap1s1 represents a promising therapeutic target for preventing aging-related cognitive decline and delaying AD onset.
