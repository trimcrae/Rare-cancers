## Introduction
Thyroid carcinoma (TC) is the most common endocrine malignancy, with its incidence and prevalence rapidly increasing. It is projected that by 2030, thyroid carcinoma will become the fourth most common cancer [ ]. Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) accounts for approximately 90% of all thyroid carcinoma cases. It is characterized by abnormal proliferation of tumor cells, invasion of the capsule, increased tendency to compress surrounding organs, and high rate of lymph node metastasis, often leading to poor prognosis [ , ]. However, the specific molecular mechanisms underlying the proliferation, invasion, and metastasis of PTC remain unclear. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms of PTC pathogenesis, exploring new therapeutic strategies, and identifying biomarkers for predicting PTC prognosis hold significant medical value.
Invasion and metastasis represent formidable challenges in the treatment landscape in PTC [ , ]. RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) are recognized contributors to carcinogenesis through their interactions with RNA. Among RBPs, heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs) play pivotal roles in the invasion and metastasis of various cancers [ ]. HNRNPC, a member of the hnRNPs family, functions as an RNA-binding protein involved in diverse aspects of nucleic acid metabolism and the regulation of gene expression, actively contributing to tumor development [ , ]. Studies have confirmed that HNRNPC is highly expressed in cancers such as lung adenocarcinoma, prostate cancer, liver cancer, oral squamous cell carcinoma, and breast cancer, promoting tumor cell invasion and metastasis. However, the role and mechanism of HNRNPC in PTC remains unclear, necessitating further research and refinement.
More and more evidence suggests that cancer is intricately linked to energy metabolism, with metabolic reprogramming emerging as a fundamental hallmark of cancer [ ]. Despite the presence of oxygen, cancer cells exhibit a preference for glucose metabolism, leading to lactate production, a phenomenon known as aerobic glycolysis [ ]. This metabolic shift provides cancer cells with selective advantages, including enhanced proliferation, survival, migration, and invasion, by augmenting the production of essential metabolic intermediates required for biosynthesis [ ]. In our study, knockdown of HNRNPC resulted in the downregulation of the pro-metastatic splicing isoform PKM2 and the upregulation of the anti-metastatic splicing isoform PKM1. These findings suggest that HNRNPC potentially regulates PKM selective splicing, influencing the progression of PTC. Pyruvate kinase M (PKM) has been established to play a crucial role in aerobic glycolysis within tumors [ ]. However, the precise mechanism of the interaction between HNRNPC and PKM, and whether it impacts selective splicing and downstream aerobic glycolysis, remains elusive. Additionally, as an m6A binding protein, HNRNPC binds to transcripts containing m6A through m6A modification, promoting tumor progression [ ]. Further research is warranted to investigate whether HNRNPC promotes the progression of PTC through m6A modification of PKM. Therefore, comprehensive exploration is necessary to unveil the specific mechanism by which HNRNPC affects the progression of PTC.
In this study, we aim to elucidate whether HNRNPC regulates the alternative splicing of PKM through m6A methylation modification and promotes PTC invasion and metastasis via the glycolytic pathway. We focus on investigating HNRNPC as the target molecule, utilizing both cellular and animal models. Initially, we assess the expression and biological functions of HNRNPC in PTC. Subsequently, we investigate HNRNPC’s role in regulating the alternative splicing of PKM and aerobic glycolysis. Finally, we explore the involvement of m6A modification in HNRNPC-mediated regulation of PKM alternative splicing.

## Materials and methods

## Human PTC samples
The primary tumor tissues, matched para-cancerous thyroid tissues, and lymph node metastatic tissues were sourced from Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital. Patients with PTC were included, all of whom were newly diagnosed and hadn’t undergone any treatment prior to surgery. Histological diagnosis and staging followed the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) guidelines. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

## Cell culture
The human PTC cell line BCPAP and normal human thyroid cell line (HTori-3) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and a penicillin-streptomycin solution (Gibco, USA). Regular testing for mycoplasma contamination and authentication via short tandem repeat (STR) profiling were conducted for all cultured cells.

## Chemicals and antibodies
Primary antibodies of β-actin (bs-0061R), HNRNPC (bs-6459R) were purchased from Bioss (Boaosen Biotechnology, Beijing, China). PKM1 (15821-1-AP) and PKM2 (15822-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech (Rosemont, IL, USA). Peroxidase-Conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (ZB-2301) was purchased from Zhongshan Jinqiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The internal m6A inhibitor STM-2457 (HY-134836) was purchased from MedChemExpress and dissolved in 5% DMSO for use. Other general agents were commercially available.

## Immunohistochemistry staining
Specimens were dehydrated with sucrose and sectioned. After attachment to the slide, tissue sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide at room temperature (RT) for 10 min. Antigen retrieval was performed using EDTA antigen retrieval solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 10 min, followed by blocking with 3% BSA for an additional 10 min at RT. Paralleled sections were then incubated with rabbit anti-HNRNPC (1:500) overnight at 4 °C. After washing in PBS, goat anti-Rabbit IgG antibody was applied for 1 h at RT. Subsequent staining was carried out using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) peroxidase substrate, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. The images of stained sections were captured using the high-powered upright Leica DM6 microscope (Germany). Additionally, the average optical density was analyzed using Image J software.

## Bioinformatics analysis
The RNAseq data and corresponding clinical information of PTC tumors were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Statistical analysis was conducted using R software v4.0.3. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Assistance was provided by the Researcher’s Home (https://www.aclbi.com/static/index.html#/tcga). The correlation between HNRNPC and clinical factors in PTC was analyzed using the Linked Omics database.

## Construction and transduction of lentiviral vectors
Lentiviral vectors for knocking down or overexpressing HNRNPC expression were synthesized by Hanheng Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The papillary thyroid carcinoma cell line BCPAP was selected to establish stable models of HNRNPC knockdown or overexpression following the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequent experiments will be conducted based on the methods provided by the reagent supplier. Transfection efficiency will be evaluated post-stable lentiviral transfection using qPCR and Western blot analysis. The targeting sequences were as follows: shHNRNPC-1, 5’-GCCTTCGTTCAGTATGTTAAT-3’; shHNRNPC-2, 5’-CTTGTGGTCAAGAAATCTGAT-3’.

## RNA sequencing
First, total RNA was extracted using the Trizol reagent following the manufacturer’s protocol, and the quantity and purity of the extracted RNA were analyzed. Subsequently, mRNA was purified from the total RNA. After purification, the mRNA was fragmented into short fragments. Then the cleaved RNA fragments were reverse-transcribed to create the cDNA. Next, the cDNA fragments were ligated to sequencing adapters to construct the library. Finally, sequencing was performed using the Illumina Novaseq™ 6000 platform (LC-Bio Technology CO., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations for paired-end sequencing (PE150). Gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 software to compare two distinct groups. Genes with a false discovery rate (FDR) parameter below 0.05 and an absolute fold change ≥ 2 were identified as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Subsequently, these DEGs underwent enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) functions. Differential alternative splicing analysis of RNA-Seq data was performed using rMATS (https://rnaseq-mats.sourceforge.io/). Significant splicing events were filtered using a FDR threshold of < 0.05.

## Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)
Total RNA from cells was extracted using TRIzol™ reagent (Invitrogen). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, mRNA was converted to cDNA through reverse transcription using the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific, K1621). The synthesized cDNA was used for real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with a Bestar® Sybr Green qPCR Master Mix (DBI-2043, DBI Bioscience, Shanghai, China). PCR amplifications of cDNA were performed on a CFX-96 PCR system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using standard methods. The primer synthesis was performed by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai). The relative quantification of mRNA levels was calculated based on the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to GAPDH on PRISM 9 (GraphPad software, La Jolla, CA, USA). All experiments were performed in triplicate. The primer sequences are as previously reported [ , ].

## Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation-sequencing (MeRIP-seq)
MeRIP analysis was performed on cells with stable knockdown of HNRNPC using a methylated RNA immunoprecipitation kit. Total RNA (200 µg) was isolated and randomly fragmented into fragments of 100 nucleotides or fewer. Subsequently, immunoprecipitation was carried out using 10 µg of m6A antibody or mouse IgG antibody conjugated to Magna ChIP Protein A/G magnetic beads. A fraction representing 1/10 of the fragmented mRNA was retained as input. The RNA was then purified for further MeRIP sequencing. In brief, CleanData was aligned to the human genome (homo sapiens, genome version: v107) using HISAT2 (http://daehwankimlab.github.io/hisat2). The resulting BAM files were utilized for Peak calling and differential Peak analysis employing the R package exomePeak (https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/3.3/bioc/html/exomePeak.html). Differential peaks were annotated using ANNOVAR (http://www.openbioinformatics.org/annovar/), followed by GO and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses. Motif analysis was performed using HOMER (http://homer.ucsd.edu/homer/motif).

## RNA immunoprecipitation-qPCR experiment
RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays were performed in the human papillary thyroid carcinoma cell lines BCPAP using a protein-immunoprecipitation kit. Magnetic beads pre-coated with 10 µg of anti-HNRNPC or normal IgG antibodies were incubated with sufficient cell lysates overnight at 4 °C. The RNA-protein complexes were washed and treated with proteinase K digestion buffer to remove proteins. RNA was then purified, and the expression levels of PKM2 were detected and analyzed by qPCR.

## SiRNA transfection
Specific siRNAs targeting PKM2 (5′-CCAUAAUCGUCCUCACCAA-3′) were synthesized by Nantong Jinxu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nantong, China).
Cells were seeded at a density of 6 × 10^3 cells per well in a 6-well plate and cultured until reaching approximately 60% confluence. Lipofectamine 3000 and siRNA were diluted separately in serum-free medium, mixed thoroughly in a 1:1 ratio, and then incubated at room temperature for 15 min to facilitate complex formation. Following incubation, the culture medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, and the transfection mixture was added to the cells. The cells were then incubated in a constant temperature incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 8 h. After transfection, cells were washed three times with PBS, and fresh complete medium was added. The cells were further incubated in a constant temperature incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. After 48 h, cells were harvested for downstream experiments.

## RNA stability assay
To assess RNA stability, 5 µg/mL of actinomycin D (HY-17559, MedChemExpress) was added to transfected PTC cells. Actinomycin D inhibits transcription by interfering with mRNA synthesis. Total RNA was extracted at specified time points, and PKM2 mRNA levels were analyzed using qPCR.

## Western blot analysis
Total protein from the cells was prepared and extracted using the Protein Extraction Kit (KeyGEN Biotechnology, Jiangsu, China). Protein concentration was measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (KGP902, KeyGEN, Jiangsu, China). Equal amounts of protein (50 µg per lane) were resolved on a 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel and then transferred onto a 0.22-µm polyvinylidene fluoride membrane (Millipore, USA). The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated with rabbit anti-HNRNPC (1:1000), anti-PKM1 (1:2000), anti-PKM2 (1:1000) and β-actin (1:2000) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with PBST (PBS containing 1‰ Tween 20), the membrane was subsequently incubated with Peroxidase-Conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:5000). Detection was performed with the automatic chemiluminescence gel imaging analysis system (Amersham Imager 600, General Electric Company, USA), and quantification was carried out via densitometry with Image J. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

## Treatment with methyltransferase inhibitor
Papillary thyroid carcinoma cells were treated with 15 µM STM-2457, while control cells were treated with same volumes of DMSO for 48 h. Then, qPCR analysis was conducted to assess the expression of both PKM1 and PKM2.

## m6A dot blot analysis
Total RNA extraction was conducted using the RN001 RNA Quick Purification kit from ESscience (RN001), followed by quantification using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. Subsequently, 2 µL of the RNA sample was spotted onto the front of a positively-charged Nylon transfer membrane (RPN303B, GE Healthcare). After air-drying, the membrane was subjected to UV crosslinking twice to ensure RNA adherence. To serve as a control, photographs were taken after incubating methylene blue with the mRNA. The membrane was then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the anti-m6A antibody. Following this, a 1-hour incubation with the corresponding secondary antibody was performed. Signal development and visualization were achieved using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) western blotting kit (Millipore).

## Measurement of lactic acid production
Knockdown the expression of HNRNPC by transfecting BCPAP cells with lentiviral vectors. Subsequently, cells were incubated in phenol red-free medium without FBS for 4 h. The accumulation of lactic acid in the culture medium was assessed using the Lactate Colorimetric Assay Kit. Optical density values were measured at OD450nm, and lactic acid concentration was calculated.

## Glucose uptake assay
HNRNPC expression was knocked down using lentiviral vectors, and BCPAP cell lines were stably transfected. Cells were then incubated in glucose- and phenol red-free medium without L-glucose for 8 h. The amount of glucose in these media was measured using the Glucose Colorimetric Assay Kit. Fresh culture medium was used as a negative control.

## Pyruvate kinase (PK) activity assay
The Abbkine CheKine™ Pyruvate Kinase (PK) Activity Assay Kit was utilized for the detection of pyruvate kinase (PK) activity.
Harvest 5 million cells and transfer them to a centrifuge tube. Wash with cold PBS and centrifuge. Add 1 mL of Extraction Buffer to the cell pellet, then sonicate for 5 min in an ice bath. Centrifuge the lysate at 8,000 g, 4 °C for 10 min, collecting the supernatant and keeping it on ice. Preheat the spectrophotometer for 30 min and set the wavelength to 340 nm. Incubate the Substrate Mix Working Reagent at 37 °C for 5 min. In a 96-well UV plate, mix 10 µL of sample, 10 µL of LDH Working Reagent, and 180 µL of Substrate Mix Working Reagent. Quickly measure absorbance at 340 nm for 20 s (A1) and after 2 min and 20 s (A2). Calculate ΔA = A1 - A2. PK activity is determined as follows: PK (U/10^4) = 6.431 × ΔA.

## Measurement of extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and oxygen consumption rate (OCR)
To monitor metabolic changes in vitro, PTC cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10^4 cells per well in an XF 24-well plate and subjected to 24 h of serum starvation. The Seahorse XF 24 extracellular flux analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience) was then used to measure both the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR). OCR was assessed using the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test kit (Agilent Technologies), while ECAR was measured with the Seahorse XF Glycolysis Stress Test kit (Agilent Technologies), following previously described methods. ECAR and OCR measurements were normalized to cell number and reported as mpH/min or pmoles/min, respectively.

## CCK-8 assay
After 24 h in the absence of FBS, cell viability was determined by a CCK-8 assay. In brief, 100 µL of medium containing 10% CCK was added to incubate the cells for 2 h at 37 °C. After removing the cell supernatant, the optical density was detected using a microplate reader at 450 nm.

## Transwell invasion and migration assay
Transwell assay was used for accessing the migration characteristic of BCPAP cells, which was performed in using 8 μm pore transwell cell culture chambers (Coster, MA). 100 µL of culture medium were seeded in the upper chamber and a 700 µL conditional medium was added in the lower chamber. The lower chambers of all transwell inserts contained 20% FBS, while the upper chambers did not contain FBS. After cultured for 48 h, the cells passed through the filter and fix with 4% of paraformaldehyde for 20 min, and subsequently stained with 1% crystal violet for 20 min. The number of invaded cells was counted [ ]. At least three independent experiments were performed.
In the Transwell invasion assay, 100 µL of Matrigel matrix was added to the membrane in the upper chamber, while in the Transwell migration assay, Matrigel was not added.

## In vivo experimentation
The animal assays conducted in this study adhered to approved procedures by the Ethics Review Committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital. Female nude mice aged 4–6 weeks, weighing between 17 and 19 g, were procured from the Beijing Laboratory Animal Research Center (Beijing, China). These mice were accommodated in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment, with a regulated 12-hour light/dark cycle, and were provided ad libitum access to food and water. The nude mice were divided into two groups: control and HNRNPC-knockdown mice. For the tumorigenesis experiments, nude mice were subcutaneously inoculated (s.c.) with 200 µL Matrigel (BD) containing 2 × 10^6 cells under ether anesthesia. Tumor nodules were measured twice every week [ , ], and was calculated using formula: tumorous volume = length × width2/2 [ , ]. Subsequently, the mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation following ether-inhalation anesthesia after 4 weeks following the cell transplantation, and the tumors were isolated. Humane endpoints were implemented throughout the animal assays, ensuring the well-being of the mice. If any mouse exhibited weight loss exceeding 25% or reached a maximum tumor diameter exceeding 20 mm, they were promptly euthanized to minimize suffering.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was conducted using PRISM 9 software. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). One-way ANOVA was employed for comparing statistical differences among multiple groups, followed by the Dunnett or Tukey’s significant difference test. To compare the differences between two groups at different time points, repeated measures ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc tests was conducted. A p-value lower than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

## Results

## HNRNPC is abnormally overexpressed in PTC and promotes the proliferation, invasion, and migration of thyroid cancer cells
Interestingly, we acquired relevant data from the TCGA database and observed a substantial number of differentially expressed genes in the PTC group compared to the normal thyroid tissue group. Notably, the abundance of HNRNPC transcripts exhibited a remarkable increase in tumor tissues compared to normal non-tumor tissues of PTC patients (P < 0.001, Fig.  A, B). Utilizing the Linked Omics database, we discovered that HNRNPC is associated with cell proliferation and migration in PTC (Fig.  C). Furthermore, there were significant differences observed in the T and M staging of HNRNPC in PTC tissue (P < 0.001, Fig.  D, E). These findings underscore the potential implications of HNRNPC in PTC, linking its expression to crucial aspects of cell behavior and highlighting its relevance in thyroid cancer progression.

 

To investigate the biological relevance of HNRNPC in PTC, we conducted an assessment of HNRNPC expression at the protein level through IHC staining in 60 pairs of PTC tissues and their matched adjacent non-cancerous tissues (Fig.  A). The results showed that the overall HNRNPC protein in PTC tissue was significantly higher than that in matched adjacent non-cancerous tissues (P < 0.01, Fig.  B).

 

Subsequently, qPCR and Western blot analyses were used to analyze HNRNPC mRNA and protein expression in lymph node metastasis, primary cancer tissues, and para-carcinoma tissues of PTC. The results showed that HNRNPC was significantly increased in lymph node metastasis and primary cancer tissues compared to para-carcinoma tissues (P < 0.01, Fig.  C-E), with the highest expression in lymph node metastasis tissues of PTC (P < 0.01, Fig.  C-E). These results are consistent with the results observed in cell lines, where HNRNPC mRNA (P < 0.001, Fig.  F) and protein expression (P < 0.001, Fig.  G, H) were significantly increased in BCPAP cell lines compared to normal thyroid cells.

## Knocking down HNRNPC inhibits the proliferation, invasion, and migration of PTC cells
To explore the biological functions of HNRNPC in PTC, we conducted knockdown experiments targeting HNRNPC in BCPAP cells. The knockdown efficiency was assessed through both qPCR and Western blot analyses. The results demonstrated the successful knockdown of HNRNPC, with a substantial decrease observed in the mRNA (P < 0.001, Fig.  A) and protein (P < 0.001, Fig.  B, C) level of HNRNPC in BCPAP cells.

 

Subsequently, we investigated the proliferation, invasion, and migration abilities of BCPAP cells using the CCK-8 assay and Transwell invasion and migration assays. The results of the CCK-8 assay demonstrated that knockdown of HNRNPC significantly inhibited the proliferation of BCPAP cells (P < 0.001; Fig.  D). Moreover, the results of subcutaneous tumorigenesis experiments revealed that knocking down the expression of HNRNPC suppressed the size of xenograft tumors in nude mice (P < 0.001; Fig.  I, J, K). Furthermore, the results of the Transwell invasion and migration assays indicated that knockdown of HNRNPC significantly suppressed the invasion (P < 0.05; Fig.  E, F) and migration abilities (P < 0.001; Fig.  G, H) of BCPAP cells. Collectively, these findings suggest that HNRNPC exerts inhibitory effects on the proliferation, invasion, and migration of BCPAP cells.

## HNRNPC regulates PKM alternative splicing in PTC cells
To elucidate the role of HNRNPC in alternative splicing, this study employed RNA-seq in cell lines with HNRNPC knockdown, coupled with m6A-MeRIP-seq and GO enrichment analysis. The results revealed that downregulation of HNRNPC led to significant alterations in downstream alternative splicing and changes in glucose metabolism signaling (Fig.  A), particularly affecting Mutually Exclusive Exons in PTC cells’ alternative splicing (Fig.  B). We identified several genes with significant differential expression, including BPTF, UBR4, PKM, U2AF1, HMGN1, ADARB1, USP16, FN1, MAPKBP1, GYS1, AHNAK, IRS1, and PRPF8 (Fig.  C). Particularly, there was a notable difference in the expression of the pro-metastasis splicing isoform PKM2 and the anti-metastasis splicing isoform PKM1 (Fig.  D-I). It is known that two isoforms of PKM are generated by alternative splicing of PKM pre-mRNA (mutually exclusive exons), with exon 9-containing isoform referred to as PKM1 and exon 10-containing isoform as PKM2 [ ] (Fig.  D). The mRNA and protein expression of PKM1 significantly decreased in BCPAP cells (P < 0.01, Fig.  E, G, H), while the expression of PKM2 increased at both mRNA and protein levels (P < 0.01, Fig.  F, G, I). Knockdown of HNRNPC resulted in opposite effects on the expression of PKM2 and PKM1 (P < 0.001, Fig.  J-N). These findings indicate that HNRNPC influences the alternative splicing of PKM in PTC cells.

 

## HNRNPC modulates PKM RNA expression through m6A modification in PTC cells
To investigate whether HNRNPC modulates PKM through m6A methylation in PTC cells, we first identified m6A single-base sites in PKM RNA using the online database SRMAP (http://www.cuilab.cn/sramp). As PKM comprises 12 exons and produces two splice variants through exon exclusion, with exon 9-containing isoform referred to as PKM1 and exon 10-containing isoform as PKM2. We discovered m6A sites near exons 9 and 10. Combining the fact that HNRNPC modifies RNA mainly through a “switch mechanism,” we found a stem-loop structure near exon 9 (Fig.  A), with the motif GGACU consistent with the m6A-MeRIP sequencing results (Fig.  B). This suggests that HNRNPC may affect PKM alternative splicing through m6A modification in this region.

 

Further, upon inhibiting m6A methylation with the methyltransferase inhibitor STM-2457 (P < 0.01, Fig.  C, D), we observed a significant increase in PKM1 expression in PTC cells (P < 0.001, Fig.  E, G, H), while PKM2 expression decreased (P < 0.001, Fig.  F, G, I). Additionally, we eliminated m6A modification in cells overexpressing HNRNPC (P < 0.001, Fig.  J, K, L) and observed that upon removal of m6A modification, the expression of PKM1 was upregulated (P < 0.001, Fig.  M, O, P), while the expression of PKM2 was downregulated (P < 0.001, Fig.  N, O, Q) in the HNRNPC overexpression group. Furthermore, STM-2457 does not affect the expression of HNRNPC in PTC cells (Fig.  O). Additionally, HNRNPC knockdown does not affect the stability of PKM2 mRNA (P > 0.05, Fig.  R). This suggests that inhibition of m6A methylation abolishes HNRNPC-mediated regulation of alternative splicing of PKM mRNA. These results indicate that HNRNPC regulates PKM RNA expression through m6A modification.

## HNRNPC promotes aerobic glycolysis in PTC cells by upregulating PKM2 expression
The formation of the PKM2 isoform is a crucial determinant of aerobic glycolysis in cancer cells. Aerobic glycolysis is characterized by increased glucose uptake and lactate production, thereby promoting the occurrence and progression of cancer tumors. Therefore, we further investigated the impact of HNRNPC on aerobic glycolysis in BCPAP cells. We found that downregulation of HNRNPC significantly reduced glucose uptake (P < 0.001, Fig.  A), lactate production (P < 0.001, Fig.  C), and pyruvate kinase (PK) activity (P < 0.001, Fig.  E) in BCPAP cells, while overexpression of HNRNPC increased glucose uptake (P < 0.001, Fig.  B), lactate production (P < 0.001, Fig.  D), and PK activity (P < 0.001, Fig.  F). Next, OCR and ECAR assays were conducted to determine whether HNRNPC directly affects glucose metabolism. The ECAR assay showed that HNRNPC knockdown significantly reduced glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve in PTC cells (P < 0.01, Fig.  G). Additionally, the OCR assay revealed that HNRNPC knockdown increased the maximal respiration of PTC cells as measured by OCR (P < 0.05, Fig.  H). To investigate whether HNRNPC promotes PTC progression through PKM2, the RIP-qPCR results revealed that HNRNPC binds to and upregulates the expression of PKM2 in BCPAP cells (P < 0.001, Fig.  I). In addition, we knocked down PKM2 expression in BCPAP cells (P < 0.01, Fig.  J) overexpressing HNRNPC. We observed that HNRNPC enhances PTC cell proliferation (P < 0.01, Fig.  M), invasion (P < 0.001, Fig.  N, O), and migration (P < 0.001, Fig.  P, Q), as well as glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve in PTC cells (P < 0.05, Fig.  R). It also decreases the maximal respiration of PTC cells (P < 0.05, Fig.  S) and promotes glucose uptake (P < 0.01, Fig.  T), lactate production (P < 0.001, Fig.  U), and PK activity (P < 0.01, Fig.  V) in BCPAP cells. All these changes were attenuated by knocking down PKM2. These findings suggest that HNRNPC promotes glycolytic metabolism, cell proliferation, invasion, and migration in PTC cells through PKM2.

 

## Discussion
In this study, we found that HNRNPC regulates the selective splicing of PKM via m6A methylation modification and promotes the proliferation, invasion and metastasis of PTC through glucose metabolism pathways mediated by PKM2. These findings introduce novel biomarkers for screening and diagnosing PTC patients, offering personalized targets to enhance the treatment strategy for PTC (Fig.  ).

 

HnRNPs are a class of RNA-binding proteins that regulate RNA splicing, processing, and maturation. Additionally, they interact with poly(U) sequences in the 3’-UTR or 5’-UTR of mRNA to modulate processes such as stability, translation, metabolism, and transport of target mRNA molecules [ ]. HNRNPC, a member of the hnRNPs family, is involved in tumor invasion and metastasis [ ]. Studies have consistently underscored the significance of HNRNPC expression in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), where its upregulation is significantly associated with malignant characteristics and poor prognosis [ ]. Notably, the knockdown of HNRNPC has been shown to substantially inhibit IL-6/STAT3-mediated HCC metastasis [ ]. Furthermore, the knockout of HNRNPC activates type I interferon responses, thereby fostering breast cancer progression [ ]. In the context of oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), elevated HNRNPC levels in tumor tissue exhibit a positive correlation with clinical staging [ ]. This upregulation of HNRNPC contributes to the carcinogenesis of OSCC [ ]. Consistent with these findings, our study reveals abnormal overexpression of HNRNPC in PTC, implicating its role in promoting thyroid cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and migration. However, the precise mechanistic details underpinning these effects remain elusive and warrant further exploration.
Studies have demonstrated that HNRNPC primarily modulates the selective splicing, processing, and maturation of target genes [ , ]. HNRNPC has been implicated in regulating cancer-specific alternative cleavage and polyadenylation profiles, thereby inducing subtype transformation of miR-21-5p and fostering cancer progression [ ]. Moreover, it serves as a pivotal regulator in modulating Mnk2 selective splicing, promoting peritoneal metastasis in gastric cancer [ ], and interacts with USP39 to influence selective splicing, thereby impacting the reprogramming of fatty acid metabolism and promoting liver cancer progression [ ]. These findings align with our study outcomes. We depleted HNRNPC expression in BCPAP cells of papillary thyroid carcinoma and conducted RNA-seq analysis. Our results revealed a significant number of splicing events following HNRNPC knockdown. Integration of GO and KEGG enrichment analyses unveiled alterations in downstream selective splicing upon HNRNPC depletion. Notably, we observed downregulation of the pro-metastatic splicing isoform PKM2 and upregulation of the anti-metastatic splicing isoform PKM1. PKM generates two isoforms via selective splicing of pre-mRNA, with PKM1 containing exon 9 and PKM2 containing exon 10 [ ]. While PKM1 is expressed in adult tissues to promote oxidative phosphorylation, PKM2 is ubiquitously expressed in embryonic and tumor tissues, promoting aerobic glycolysis in cancer cells [ ]. These findings underscore the role of HNRNPC in regulating PKM selective splicing and its potential impact on aerobic glycolysis in PTC.
Recently, there has been growing interest in cellular metabolic reprogramming in cancer, which drives malignant transformation by activating oncogenes and conferring malignant phenotypes on cancer cells [ ]. In cancer cells, the primary metabolic pathway for glucose shifts from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis, generating ample energy for cell growth [ ]. Aerobic glycolysis, a hallmark of tumor cells, is characterized by increased glucose uptake and lactate secretion. PKM is a key player in the aerobic glycolysis of tumors [ ]. PKM1 and PKM2, isoforms of pyruvate kinase encoded by the same gene PKM through selective splicing, have emerged as critical regulators [ , ]. Recent studies have revealed that PKM1, primarily expressed in adult tissues to facilitate oxidative phosphorylation, acts as a tumor suppressor, while PKM2, widely expressed in embryonic and tumor tissues, promotes aerobic glycolysis in cancer cells [ ]. PKM2 exerts a pivotal role in tumor invasion and metastasis by regulating glycolysis and other pathways. It serves as a crucial rate-limiting enzyme in cellular glycolytic activity, converting phosphoenolpyruvate into pyruvate to generate ATP, thereby fueling the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. Its high expression in various malignant cells contributes to the progression of malignant tumors [ ]. Studies have elucidated that RBMX selectively splices PKM through hnRNPA1, leading to an increase in PKM1 expression, thus inhibiting the tumorigenicity and progression of breast cancer [ ]. In colon cancer, OTUB2 interacts with PKM2, enhancing its activity and promoting glycolysis, thereby facilitating colorectal cancer progression [ ]. In gastric cancer, the long-chain non-coding RNA VAL enhances PKM2 enzymatic activity and aerobic glycolysis in gastric cancer cells by directly binding to PKM2 [ ]. In lung cancer, SAM68 promotes tumor development by enhancing the formation of PKM2 subtypes and PKM2-dependent aerobic glycolysis [ ]. Furthermore, similar reports in myeloma have revealed that NEK2 promotes aerobic glycolysis by regulating PKM splicing and increasing the ratio of PKM2/PKM1 in myeloma cells, thereby contributing to its carcinogenic activity [ ]. These findings align with our results. Following HNRNPC knockdown in BCPAP cells of papillary thyroid carcinoma, we observed downregulation of the pro-metastatic splicing isoform PKM2 and upregulation of the anti-metastatic splicing isoform PKM1. Moreover, HNRNPC knockdown significantly reduced lactate production and glucose uptake in PTC cells, while HNRNPC overexpression increased lactate production and glucose uptake. This confirms that HNRNPC regulates PKM selective splicing and promotes aerobic glycolysis in PTC cells. Additionally, we found that HNRNPC enhances the proliferation, invasion, and migration of PTC cells, and this enhancement is attenuated by PKM2 knockdown. Thus, HNRNPC promotes metabolic reprogramming, cell proliferation, and metastasis in PTC cells by upregulating PKM2 expression.
N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification entails the methylation of the N atom at the 6th position of the adenine base [ ]. M6A modification is catalyzed by m6A methyltransferase, removed by m6A demethylase, and recognized by m6A binding proteins such as HNRNPC, exerting a pivotal role in regulating RNA processing, splicing, nuclear export, translation, and stability [ ]. HNRNPC, as an m6A binding protein, predominantly employs the m6A switch mechanism to interact with transcripts containing m6A: RNA m6A modification destabilizes complementary base pairs and enhances the accessibility of single-stranded RNA motifs (which can overlap with m6A sites), thereby facilitating their recognition and binding by HNRNPC [ ]. Previous research has demonstrated that HNRNPC promotes a malignant phenotype in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells by counteracting the anti-metastatic isoform of TAF8 (TAF8L) through m6A methylation modification [ ]. Additionally, HNRNPC binds to the m6A site of DDX58 mRNA to facilitate its maturation and contribute to the progression of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, underscoring its involvement in tumor progression via m6A methylation modification [ ]. In our investigation, we observed that HNRNPC modulates the expression of PKM2/PKM1 through m6A modification in PTC cells, corroborating prior research findings. Upon abolishing m6A modification, the expression levels of PKM2 and PKM1 reverted to those prior to HNRNPC intervention. This underscores the involvement of HNRNPC in the invasion and metastasis of PTC cells by modulating the selective splicing of PKM through m6A methylation. This study confirms that HNRNPC influences the selective splicing of PKM. However, whether specific m6A methyltransferases are involved in this process alongside HNRNPC requires further investigation to elucidate the precise mechanisms. Additionally, this study demonstrates that the downregulation of PKM2 can rescue the phenotypic alterations induced by the overexpression of HNRNPC. However, whether the overexpression of PKM2 can also rescue the phenotypic changes induced by shHNRNPC remains to be elucidated in future research. Moreover, this study has only confirmed the expression and mechanistic action of HNRNPC in thyroid cancer cells and animal models. Further targeted drug development and refinement of clinical trials are necessary to advance the clinical translation of HNRNPC, thereby benefiting human health.
In conclusion, this study establishes that HNRNPC governs the selective splicing of PKM via m6A methylation modification, thereby fostering the proliferation, invasion and metastasis of PTC through glucose metabolism pathways mediated by PKM2. This investigation delves into the mechanistic underpinnings of PTC from a novel angle, offering insights into the potential molecular mechanisms driving PTC invasion and metastasis. It sheds light on the specific pathways through which HNRNPC propels PTC invasion and metastasis, presenting novel tumor markers for the screening and diagnosis of PTC patients. Moreover, it unveils new personalized treatment targets, enriching the therapeutic against PTC.
