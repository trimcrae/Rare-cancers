## Purpose
This study aimed to investigate the role and mechanism of CD40–CD154 interaction in acute ocular hypertension–induced progressive retinal inflammation and damage.

## Methods
Transmission electron microscopy was used to observe the optic nerve of C57BL/6 mice at different time points of acute ocular hypertension and the sham group, and Western blotting was used to examine the relative expression of CD40 and CD154 at different time points. Knocking down CD40 and CD154 with CD40 and CD154 small interfering RNA, respectively, glaucomatous neural damage was assessed by using axon counts and observed by transmission electron microscopy imaging of the optic nerve. Immunofluorescence was used to determine the localization of CD154 and CD40 in the retinas of mice and flow cytometry was used to detect the expression of CD154 and CD40 and the subpopulation distribution of CD4+ T cells in mice spleens.

## Results
Axon loss continued after the high IOP of modeling mice returned to the normal range. The CD40 and CD154 expressions increased in glaucomatous mice. Compared with the 7-day group, the number of axons largely increased after CD40 or CD154 was knocked down. CD40 and CD154 were coexpressed in the retina. The trend of subpopulation distribution of T cells, especially Th1 cells in the spleens, was consistent with the expression of CD40 and CD4+CD154+.

## Conclusions
CD40–CD154 interaction contributes to progressive glaucomatous neurodegeneration after the elevated IOP returns to the normal range. T helper type 1 cells play a key role in the damage of glaucomatous mice.

High IOP is the most important pathogenetic factor in glaucoma. However, the IOP-lowering therapy practiced in the clinic does not entirely prevent some glaucoma patients from continuing to suffer from visual field deterioration. , It suggests that there is still a need to investigate the pathogenesis of glaucoma beyond elevated IOP.
Neuroinflammation is a main pathological feature in glaucoma and characterized by infiltrating T cells. Several studies have identified autoimmune response mechanisms in glaucoma, especially CD4+ T cells, suggesting that the immune response in the retina is not entirely isolated from the rest of the body despite the presence of the blood–retinal barrier. – Furthermore, in murine glaucoma models, the infiltration of T helper (Th)1 cells into the retina, where they targeted RGC and integrated into the pro-inflammatory glial network, contributed to progressive RGC loss. 
CD154 (CD40L) is a transmembrane protein expressed by activated T cells. – Additionally, it has been demonstrated that CD154 accelerates senescence and apoptosis of RGCs co-cultured with Müller cells in ocular hypertensive glaucoma. CD40 is expressed predominantly on the surface of antigen-presenting cells. , In the model of ischemia/reperfusion–induced retinopathy, retinal inflammation, loss of ganglion cells, and capillary degeneration were markedly attenuated in ischemic retinas of CD40(−/−) mice. The CD40–CD154 interaction is not only a costimulatory signal for T-cell activation, but also a regulator of activation of antigen-presenting cells. , Previous study showed that the T cell CD154 primes atherosclerosis through IFN-γ activation, which initiates immune cell recruitment, necrotic core formation, and fibrous cap thinning. Besides, in the carotid plaques and plasma of patients with cerebrovascular diseases, the concentration of sCD154/sCD40 is positively correlated with the concentration of IFN-γ. In addition, this interaction promotes T cell–B cell interactions and T-cell–dependent macrophage activation. – 
However, the effect of CD40–CD154 interactions during the innate immune response in the retina of glaucomatous mice has not been reported. Herein, we aimed to investigate whether CD40–CD154 interactions could take part in acute ocular hypertension (AOH)–induced progressive damage, and to determine which T-cell subsets play the primary role at different times in acute high IOP. Clarifying this role of CD40–CD154 interactions can further elucidate the mechanism of prolonged retinal neurodegeneration after the elevated IOP returns to the normal range and lay a theoretical foundation for an effective therapeutic target for glaucoma in the future.

## Methods

## Mice
Male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) (Qingdao, China) were supplied water and food ad libitum and kept under cyclic light (12/24 hour) conditions. After a 5-day period of quarantine and acclimatization, the mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 15). Sample size estimation was predicated on previous study (resource equation). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University (No. QYFY WZLL 29671). All treatments of animals were performed in accordance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

## Induction of AOH in Mice
IOP was induced in mice (6–8 weeks old) as described previously. , A 30G needle was connected to a sterile saline bag through a disposable infusion tube, and the saline bag was elevated approximately 100 cm to complete the assembly of the modeling device. We used 1% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) to anesthetize mice by intraperitoneal injection, and ocular surface anesthesia was performed by instilling drops of proparacaine hydrochloride. After the pupil was dilated with compound tropicamide, a 30G needle was inserted into the peripheral cornea approximately 1 mm from the angle of the scleral limbus. It was done to avoid damage to the iris and lens and to avoid repenetration from the cornea. After fixation of the needle, the infuser switch was turned on to maintain a 1-hour high IOP state. The anterior chamber of the sham group only performed puncture without elevation of IOP. After the needle was removed, levofloxacin ophthalmic ointment was used on the ocular surface to prevent infection. IOP measurements were taken and recorded before, during, and after retinal AOH damage modeling using a rebound TonoLab tonometer with a handheld rebound tonometer, as previously described. In addition, the retinas and spleens were removed at 1, 3, 7, and 14 days after modeling for our subsequent experiments.

## Immunofluorescence Staining of Retinal Frozen Sections
Mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation after inhalation anesthesia, ophthalmic scissors were used to remove the attached muscles around the eyeballs, and sterile filter paper was used to absorb the excess blood. The eyeballs were placed in optimal cutting temperature gel and quickly frozen. Then, the eyeballs were cut into 8-µm frozen slices at −25°C and sealed with goat sealing serum. These slices were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-mouse CD40 (1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and CD154 antibodies (1:200; Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA) for 12 hours and incubated with secondary antibody (1:200; Elabscience, Wuhan, China) at 37° for 1 hour on the second day. After DAPI incubation, the plate was quenched with an autofluorescence quencher and sealed. Finally, the final image was obtained by fluorescence microscopy and analyzed by ImageJ.

## Quantification of Axon Loss by Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)
The eyeball was removed and placed in an ice box, and a 1- to 2-mm length of optic nerve was taken from 1 to 3 mm behind the ball and fixed in precooled 2.5% glutaraldehyde overnight at 4°C. The optic nerves were observed using TEM. Axon counting was performed using ImageJ as in a previous study. 

## Flow Cytometry
Isolated single cells from mice spleens were freshly extracted following an established protocol. , After removing the spleens of the mice, other tissues around the spleen were carefully removed with ophthalmic scissors. Then each spleen was cut into small pieces and ground with a disposable syringe. Single spleen cells were extracted with a commercial kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China), and stimulated by protein transport blocker (Biolegend) for 5 to 6 hours. After incubated with fixable viability dye (Biolegend), the cell suspension was added in reagents to block Fc receptors and then incubated with conjugated fluorescent anti-mouse antibodies at 4°C. After fixation and permeabilization, cells were resuspended in wash buffer and added fluorophore-conjugated antibody for at least 30 minutes in the dark at 4°C. Flow cytometric data were acquired immediately and analyzed by Agilent NovoCyte 2060R (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and Novoexpress software (Agilent), respectively. Samples were stained on the surface and intracellularly with appropriate antibody combinations for the detection of CD40, CD154, and CD4+ T cell subgroups, such as helper T (Th)1 (CD4+IFN-γ+), Th2 (CD4+IL-4+), Th17 (CD4+IL−17+), and regulatory T (Treg; CD4+CD25+Foxp3+) cells. – The antibodies applied are shown in  .
 

## Western Blot (WB) Analysis
The retina of the mice was extracted with RIPA protein lysate (Solarbio) containing protease inhibitors (Solarbio) and phosphatase inhibitors (Yamei, Shanghai, China) on ice. The lysate was ultrasonically cracked and centrifuged at 4°C (12,000 rpm; 5 minutes). The BCA protein concentration determination kit (Elabscience) was used to measure the protein concentration. We followed the instructions of the kit for the operation. After the operation, we measured the OD value using a microplate reader (BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA) and determined the protein concentration based on the calculation file provided by an Elabscience supplier to calculate the loading sample. Each protein sample was electroblotted onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. We then placed the membrane in a container. After washing with PBST, the membrane was covered with the protein-free rapid blocking solution (YaMei, China) to incubate at room temperature for 20 minutes. These membranes were incubated with antibodies overnight at 4°C. The antibodies applied were as follows: CD40 (1:200; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), CD154 (1:200; Abcam), and β-tubulin (1:200; Abcam). Then, the membranes were incubated at room temperature with the secondary antibodies (1:500; Boster, Hangzhou, China) for 1 hour. The signal was detected by enhanced chemiluminescence. All strips were analyzed using ImageJ software. We chose β-tubulin, a housekeeping protein with stable expression in each group, as an internal control for protein expression normalization in WB. Data were presented relative to the sham group.

## Intravitreal Injections
In this experiment, mice were divided into five groups including the sham group (sham), the high IOP group (D7), the CD40/CD154 small interfering RNA (siRNA) group (1OD diluted in 1.5 µL PBS, 22 µg/µL), the scramble siRNA group (scr siRNA, 1OD diluted in 1.5 µL PBS, 22 µg/µL), and the PBS group (Solarbio). After perfusing the anterior chamber with saline to reach high IOP, the vitreous cavity of mice received 1.5 µL of the solution using a Hamilton syringe (Bonaduz, Bonaduz, Switzerland) with a 30G disposable needle at once. The primers are shown in  . The intravitreal injections were performed as previously described. , 
 

## RT-qPCR
The retina was reserved at −80°C. Gross RNA was extracted by RNA iso reagent (TAKARA BIO, Shiga, Japan) and its concentration was confirmed by spectrophotometer. Before performing qRT-PCR, reverse transcription kits (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China) were used to reverse transcribe total RNA according to the manufacturer's instructions. The same procedure was repeated three times for each group. Samples were normalized to the β-tubulin house-keeping gene and expressed as the relative expression using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Data were presented relative to the sham group. The primers are shown in  .
 

## Statistical Analyses
All experimental items were performed using the GraphPad Software in the study. Data normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the Brown–Forsythe test was used to assess the homogeneity of variances. Our experimental data was found to follow a normal distribution and had homogeneous variances. Data were presented as mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA was used to evaluate the significance of differences among three or more groups. When the overall difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05), the Tukey method was employed for multiple comparisons. To ensure accuracy, all experiments were repeated at least three times.

## Results

## Transient IOP Elevation Leads to Sustaining Glaucomatous Neurodegeneration
Glaucoma is associated with sensitivity to IOP. However, progressive neuron loss and visual field deterioration has continued despite an IOP of less than 21 mm Hg under effective medical treatment. To identify optic nerve damage during the course of glaucoma and further investigate whether glaucoma neurodegeneration continues after IOP has returned to the normal level, we used C57BL/6 male mice as an animal model of acute high IOP and detected changes of retinas and axons.  A showed the changes in IOP in the experimental group and the sham group at different times. The ultrastructural changes in the optic nerve after AOH application were observed using TEM ( B). The neurons in the 3-day group began to swell, and, in the 3-day and 7-day groups, vacuolar degeneration of mitochondria and loosening of myelin sheaths were observed. A decrease in the number of axons is an important lesion characteristic of glaucoma. As seen in  C, the number of axons in the optic nerves of C57/BL6 mice was significantly reduced on the first day in the AOH group. Moreover, the number of axons continued to decrease, reaching a minimum on day 14. These results are consistent with previous research findings that acute retinal ischemia–reperfusion injury initiated progressive RGC loss. Therefore, glaucomatous neurodegeneration continues although IOP has returned to the normal range.
 

## CD40 Is Expressed on Glaucomatous Retina and Evokes Prolonged Neurodegeneration
CD40 could promote RGC loss in mice after retinal ischemia-reperfusion. Because previous a study had demonstrated that CD40 was expressed on neuron-like cells and neurons, we speculated that CD40 might be expressed in retina after high IOP. Herein, we first examined the relative protein expression and the change of CD40 in retina of the AOH group by WB ( A).  B presented that the expression of CD40 in retinas increased from day 3, increased to the highest on day 7, and then became relatively low on day 14. However, there was no statistically significant difference between the sham group and the 14th day of the AOH group.
 To verify the role of CD40 in the neurodegenerative changes caused by elevated IOP, we knocked down CD40 with three CD40-siRNAs by qRT-PCR. The effect of three different siRNAs knocking down CD40 are shown in  C. The results showed that the first siRNA had the best knockdown effect. Besides, the expression of CD40 in the first group was lower than the expression of CD40 in the retina of mice on day 7 in the AOH group.
To further investigate the role of CD40 in the neurodegenerative changes of AOH mice, as shown in  E, compared with the 7-day group, the scr siRNA group, and the PBS group, the number of axons was significantly increased after CD40-siRNA injection into the vitreous cavity on day 7, and little axonal degeneration was observed ( D). Therefore, CD40 was expressed in the retina of the AOH group by WB analysis. Besides, reducing CD40 expression by CD40-siRNA can reduce neurodegeneration after elevated IOP.

## CD154 Mediates Prolonged Neurodegeneration After IOP Elevation
The mutual effects between CD40 and CD154 have played a vital role in inflammation and the development of adaptive immunity. To confirm the relevance of CD154 expression to high IOP, we detected changes of retinal damage after CD154-siRNA intravitreal injection. Among the three designed siRNAs, the second siRNA showed the most potent knockdown effect by using qRT-PCR ( A) and was used in subsequent experiments.
 After intravitreal injection of CD154-siRNA, TEM results showed that the morphology of most myelin sheaths was normal, and mitochondrial vacuolization was not observed ( B).  C suggests that axonal numbers of CD154 siRNA-2 group were increased compared with the D7 group, the scr siRNA group, and the PBS group. Thus, our experimental results suggest that lowering CD154 expression can reduce damage to the elevated IOP-induced retina.
We then performed WB analysis to explore the changes in the expression of CD154 after high IOP injury ( D). There are no differences of the protein levels of CD154 in retinas between the sham and experimental groups ( E).

## CD40–CD154 Interactions Lead to Neurodegeneration in Acute Elevated IOP Mice
Because the expression of CD154 was inconsistent with our inference, we further performed flow cytometry on mice spleens to verify the expression of sCD154 ( A). Compared with the sham group, the expression of CD4+CD154+ in mice spleens was lower on the first day after AOH injury, but it began to increase steadily from the first day to the seventh day, and then sharply decreased ( B). Intriguingly, the changes in the expression of CD4+CD154+ in AOH group were similar to those of CD40 ( A,  B). We also detected the percentage of CD40 expressions by flow cytometry and found that CD40 was barely expressed in mice spleens of each group ( A). Furthermore, we selected the seventh day, with the highest expression of CD40 and CD154, and then examined the colocalization of CD40 and CD154 by immunofluorescence staining on day 7. Further analysis of the correlation of fluorescence patterns showed that the pattern in the 2D intensity histogram was distributed linearly and the Pearson's R value (no threshold) was 0.76 (>0.5), indicating that CD40 and CD154 had a colocalization relationship ( D). Thus, there are enough reasons for us to believe the interaction between CD40 and CD154 leads to glaucomatous neurodegeneration.
 In addition, our results showed that colocalized markers of CD40 and CD154 were found mainly in the GCL of glaucomatous retina ( C,  D), and previous studies have proved that CD40 was expressed on neuron-like cells and neurons. Therefore, we conducted additional immunofluorescence experiments to verify whether CD40 is expressed in RGCs in our AOH model ( ). This result indicates that the CD40 signal does not colocalize with Brn3a-positive RGCs.

## Th1 Has the Main Impact on the Neurodegeneration in Glaucomatous Mice
We previously demonstrated that CD4+CD154+ played a crucial role in glaucoma. In addition, previous studies have confirmed that CD4+ T cells were involved in the occurrence and development of glaucoma. However, there were remaining questions which subsets of CD4+ T cell primarily became involved in glaucomatous neurodegeneration. Flow cytometry was used to study the distribution of CD4+ T-cell subsets in mice spleens at different time points ( A). After an increase on the first day after modeling, the percentages of Th2 and Th17 began to drop dramatically from the first day to the seventh day ( C,  D), whereas the percentages of Treg cells remained stable. Surprisingly, the expressions of Th1 were found to increase sharply on the first day after high IOP, remained at a high level for 7 days, and then decreased compared with that of the sham group ( B). Collectively, these results presented that Th1 cells uniquely maintained elevated expression levels throughout days 1 through 7 after the IOP returned to normal (similar trend with the expression of CD40 and CD154). In contrast, this persistent expression pattern was absent in Th2, Th7, and Treg cells. Thus, we reason that Th1 cells are responsible for glaucomatous neurodegeneration
 

## Discussion
Glaucoma is the most common, irreversible blinding disease worldwide, affecting approximately 70 million people. The pathogenesis of glaucoma has been studied and discussed by many scholars, and the currently recognized theory is one of neuroinflammation. , An elevated IOP is considered a main risk factor for glaucoma. However, it is incontrovertible that the immune system plays a major factor in its development. Therefore, we need to deepen our understanding of glaucoma immune mechanisms.
Under high IOP, CD4+ T cells could infiltrate the retina and cause a prolonged phase of RGC degeneration that persists after the IOP returns to a normal range. CD40/CD154, as costimulatory molecules, play a key regulatory role in the T-cell immune response. Previous studies have identified CD40 as a regulator of retinal inflammation. In addition, an in vitro study showed that CD154 accelerated the apoptosis of RGCs that had been cocultured with Müller cells. These findings suggest that CD40 or CD154 might be involved in glaucomatous neurodegeneration through an immune response. However, the specific mechanism by CD40–CD154 interactions is involved in T-cell immune response and result in the optic nerve and RGC damage in glaucoma is unclear. Therefore, we further explored the effect of CD40 and CD154 on glaucoma and discovered the role of their mutual reaction in vivo.
We established an AOH mouse model, which was used to study the pathological development of glaucoma and whether a constant state of high IOP resulted in the optic nerve damage. We noted that the damage progressively worsened, although the IOP returns to a normal range. These results corroborate the finding of the previous work that elevation of IOP induced T-cell infiltration and T cells mediated prolonged retinal neurodegeneration in glaucoma. In our study, the axonal morphology of the AOH mice on the first day did not differ much from that of the sham group by electron microscopic microstructural maps. However, the number of axons was statistically different between the AOH group and the sham group on the first day after elevated IOP. We speculate that high IOP directly compresses the optic nerve so that the number of axons decreases, but the pressure damage is not enough to cause microstructural changes. From day 3 to day 14, the observed gradual decrease in axon number and the broken myelin sheaths of optic nerve could be attributed to an immune response. 
It has been demonstrated that CD40 can be expressed in neuronal cell. CD40 can also lead to T-cell–mediated experimental autoimmune uveitis. , It suggests that there is a high probability that CD40 is involved in the prolonged damage of glaucoma. In addition, the results of previous study showed that CD40 stimulated retinal endothelial and Müller cells to induce adhesion molecule up-regulation and chemokine production on the first or second day after AOH. Reducing the expression of CD40 in AOH mice by siRNA, our studies further identified that, consistent with the sham group, the number of axons was significantly increased, with a few axonal degeneration could be observed in  D. These results provide new evidence for the involvement of CD40 in the progression of glaucomatous disease. Furthermore, CD40 expression increased from day 3 to day 7 after modeling, a trend consistent with the number of axons lost in mice with high IOP. These results are summarized to show that new strategies to reduce CD40 may prove useful for treatment of glaucoma.
High levels of CD154 expression have been detected in T cells from patients with autoimmune diseases such as active systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis. Previous studies have identified a link between glaucoma and CD154. Using cocultured Müller cells, an in vitro study examined how CD154 accelerated the senescence and apoptosis of RGCs that had been cocultured with Müller cells. Moreover, our in vivo study examined that reducing CD154 expression in high IOP mice could attenuate glaucomatous neurodegeneration. This finding broadly supports the work of other studies in immune response linking CD154 with glaucoma. However, there was no statistically significant difference in the expression of CD154 by WB test. This result might be explained that sCD154, the soluble active form of CD154, interacts with CD40 in glaucoma. Our study further identified the veracity of this speculation by flow cytometry and discovered a statistically significant increase in sCD154, which was surprisingly in line with the trend of CD40 expression. Thus, we confirmed that both CD154 and CD40 contribute to glaucomatous retinal degeneration.
The CD40–CD154 signaling pathway is important for a number of immune functions including antigen presenting cells activation, CD4+ T-cell priming, and enhancement of inflammation. Blockade of the CD40/154 interactions have been examined in a variety of models, from graft rejection, through autoimmune glomerulonephritis and experimental lupus erythematosus. – Our results showed that the colocalization of CD40 with CD154 in retina and proved that CD40-CD154 interactions lead to secondary damage in glaucoma. Besides, we further found that CD40 is not expressed in RGCs in our AOH model ( ).
Several reports have shown that CD154 overexpression in mice, leads to T cells infiltration in multiple organs and death caused by inflammatory bowel disease. We noted that only Th1 cells maintained high expression from the first day to the seventh day after IOP has returned to a normal range, whereas a similar trend in Th2, Th7, and Treg cells was not observed, suggesting that Th1 is the main cell responsible for secondary damage to the retina and optic nerve in glaucomatous mice. This notion is supported by the observation that CD4+ T cells revealed a bias toward Th1 responses in patients with glaucoma. However, this conclusion contrasts with previous studies, which have suggested that elevated IOP induced similar frequencies of T-cell subsets in the spleens and that Treg cells displayed a trend of population decline. This difference can be explained in part by the inconsistent sampling time of the previous experiments; we first identified the percentage of T cell subsets at different time points in glaucomatous mice. CD154 expression has been documented to activated T cells. Previous study demonstrated that T-cell–specific CD154 deficiency reduces atherosclerotic plaque development through impaired Th1 polarization. Our results suggested that Th1 cells are responsible for glaucomatous neurodegeneration. Thus, it is reasonable to speculate that CD154 shows a potential association with Th1 cells. Future experiments that generating T-cell–specific CD154-deficient transgenic mice or inhibiting the differentiation of Th1 cells in mice will further help precise targeting of the desired effector pathways within the CD40–CD154 dyad.

## Conclusions
Our experiments illustrate that the CD40–CD154 interaction could lead to retinal secondary degeneration. This process is very likely to have the participation of Th1 cells, which opens up new avenues for targeted treatment of glaucoma.
