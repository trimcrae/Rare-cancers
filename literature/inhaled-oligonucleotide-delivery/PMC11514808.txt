## Abstract
Brain damage caused by acute hypoxia is associated with the physiological activities of mitochondria. Although mitochondria being dynamically regulated, our comprehensive understanding of the response of specific brain cell types to acute hypoxia remains ambiguous. Tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated protein 1 (TRAP1), a mitochondrial-based molecular chaperone, plays a role in controlling mitochondrial movements. Herein, we demonstrated that acute hypoxia significantly alters mitochondria morphology and functionality in both in vivo and in vitro brain injury experiments. Summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) analyses revealed possible causative links between mitochondria-related genes and hypoxia injury. Advancing the protein-protein interaction network and molecular docking further elucidated the associations between TRAP1 and mitochondrial dynamics. Furthermore, it was shown that TRAP1 knockdown levels variably affected the expression of key mitochondrial dynamics proteins (DRP1, FIS1, and MFN1/2) in primary hippocampal neurons, astrocytes, and BV-2 cell, leading to changes in mitochondrial structure and function. Understanding the function of TRAP1 in altering mitochondrial physiological activity during hypoxia-induced acute brain injury could help serve as a potential therapeutic target to mitigate neurological damage.

## Introduction
The evolution of almost all life forms on Earth has been accompanied by changes in ambient oxygen concentrations. Mammals swiftly adapt to changes in physiology for survival when oxygen levels drop. The investigation of cerebral hypoxia has garnered significant attention due to the brain tissue’s high metabolic processes and its vulnerability to hypoxia stress [ , ]. Various brain cell types might react distinctively to acute hypoxia, depending on their metabolic requirements and specific functional roles. Previous studies have shown that neuronal response to hypoxia stress leads to gene expression changes and promotes mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming [ – ], and the manner in which astrocytes and microglia adapt to acute hypoxia stress merits additional exploration.
Brain damage manifests through multiple pathways, encompassing oxidative stress, programmed cell death, and mitochondrial malfunction [ – ]. Mitochondria are essential organelles responsible for various cellular functions, including energy production, metabolism, and apoptosis [ , , ]. The process of mitochondrial dynamics, balancing fission, fusion, and trafficking within cells, is crucial for preserving mitochondrial shape, distribution, quantity, and maintaining cell balance [ – ]. The imbalance in mitochondrial activity is linked to the development and advancement of various neurological conditions caused by hypoxia, including asphyxia, cardiac arrest, drowning, and stroke, as well as conditions associated with high altitude exposure, such as acute mountain sickness [ – ]. Consequently, comprehending the molecular processes that control mitochondrial movement is vital for formulating possible treatment approaches for acute hypoxia relative illness.
Tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated protein 1 (TRAP1), a member of the heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) family, has emerged as a critical regulator of mitochondrial quality control and cell survival during cellular stress conditions [ , ]. TRAP1 regulates the balance between mitochondrial fusion and fission by interacting with dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1), a key regulator of mitochondrial fission [ ]. Inhibiting the overabundance of mitochondrial division curtails the generation of impaired mitochondria, easing the load on the cellular quality control systems and averting the demise of neuronal cells in severe hypoxic environments. Additionally, TRAP1 serves as a structural protein and a crucial regulator of mitochondrial autophagy, coordinating the assembly of autophagy-related proteins and aiding in the removal of malfunctioning mitochondria [ , ]. It interacts with PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1) and Parkin, two pivotal players in the mitophagy machinery, which facilitates the selective removal of damaged mitochondria through the ubiquitin-proteasome system, a process essential for mitochondrial quality control [ ]. Recent studies have demonstrated that TRAP1 exerts potent protective effects against mitochondrial damage induced by acute hypoxia [ , ]. Nonetheless, the processes through which TRAP1 controls mitochondrial movement remain elusive and necessitate additional research.
To sum up, the regulation of mitochondrial dynamics is a complex process crucial for neuronal cell survival after acute hypoxia. TRAP1, as a chaperone protein, may become an important factor involved in the regulation of mitochondrial homeostasis, and we discuss the function of TRAP1 in regulating mitochondrial fission/fusion, and morphology in neurons and glial cells, as well as its effects on cellular bioenergetics and apoptosis. Furthermore, our research offers an in-depth analysis of the signaling routes and protein interplays that play a role in the TRAP1-driven control of mitochondrial movements during acute hypoxia-triggered brain damage. Comprehending the molecular processes behind TRAP1’s control over mitochondrial movements could offer fresh perspectives on treatment approaches to maintain the survival and functionality of neuronal cells in brain injuries caused by hypoxia.

## Materials and methods

## Acute hypoxia models
Male KM mice (Spfbiotech Ltd., Beijing, China) were randomly separated into two groups: the normoxia group and the acute hypoxia group. The mice were housed in separate ventilated cages, kept at a temperature of 23 °C and a light-dark cycle of 12:12 h, with defined levels of cleanliness without pathogens, free access to water and regular chow. Mice in the normoxia group were kept in a normal physiological environment. Mice in the acute hypoxia group were positioned in the animal hypoxia chamber, with the following instrument settings: a 24-hour hypoxia treatment duration, an oxygen level of 5.6%, control humidity between 30 and 48%, and a temperature range of 21–24 °C (Figs.  A and A). Document and monitor the behavior of mice in both normoxia and hypoxia environments. This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and was performed in accordance with the ethical standards laid down in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NBCDSER-IACUC-2018-096).
 Primary hippocampal neurons, astrocytes and BV-2 cell were randomly divided into normoxia group and acute hypoxia group. The group with normal oxygen levels, consisting of primary hippocampal neuron cells, astrocytes, and BV-2 cell exhibiting robust growth, was situated in a cell culture incubator at 37 °C containing 5% CO2, in contrast to the experimental group, which received treatment in a cell hypoxia workstation with 5% CO2 and 0.5% O2 for 24 h.

## Cell culture and siRNA transfection
BV-2 cell lines was obtained from the Peking Union Medical College (Beijing, China). The astrocyte was maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). Primary hippocampal neurons were isolated and cultured in Neurobasal medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), enriched with 2% B-27 supplement and 1% GlutaMax [ ]. Both TRAP1 siRNA and negative control siRNA were purchased from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Cells were cultured in culture medium without fetal bovine serum (FBS) for 24 h prior to siRNA transfection. Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) was employed to transfect the siRNA constructs. After transfection for 4–6 h, the cells were washed with PBS to completely remove the siRNA constructs in the medium and were then cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS.

## Isolation of primary hippocampus cells
Pregnant Wistar rats underwent sterilization using 75% alcohol in a sterile environment and were then anesthetics. The hippocampus was cleaned and stripped with dissection solution. Subsequently, the hippocampi were excised in DMEM high-glucose medium, and digested with 0.25% trypsin for 10–15 min. The digestion was then halted by adding 10% fetal bovine serum. Following digestion, the hippocampal tissue was washed with DMEM high glucose solution to eliminate serum, and triturate repeatedly with a pipette to form a cell suspension. The prepared cell suspension was centrifuged at 1000 rpm and counted (5 × 105/mL), and the harvested cells were then cultured in 6-well plates and placed in a thermostat incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Following a 4-hour incubation period, the cellular medium was substituted with Neurobasal medium, comprising 2% B-27 supplement and 1% GlutaMax.

## Cell vitality assessment
Cell viability for nerve cells and glial cells were assessed under acute hypoxia using CCK8 assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates, and 10 µL CCK-8 solution was added and then incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. The optical density (OD) values were measured at a wavelength of 450 nm.

## Antioxidant enzyme activity assay
Catalase (CAT) activity and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity were determined according to the manufacturer’s protocol [ ]. Briefly, brain tissues subjected to normoxia and hypoxia interventions were lysed and the lysates were treated with hydrogen peroxide reservoir and 2-(4-iodophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenol)-5-phenyltetrazolium chloride, respectively, and then the absorbance was measured at 240 nm and 505 nm to assess CAT and SOD activities.

## Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)
Mice were anesthetized with 4% cold formaldehyde by cardiac perfusion. Fragments (∼ 1mm3) were extracted from the hippocampus region, stored in 0.1 M Na Cacodylate buffer (pH = 7.4) with 0.13 M sucrose, and subsequently encased in 1% osmium tetroxide within Cacodylate buffer. Samples were stained in 2% uranyl acetate and then dehydrated in graded ethanol. The sample was then embedded in 100% acetone for 4 h, cut into 100 nm ultrathin sections, and finally stained with 4% uranyl acetate and 0.5% lead citrate. Morphologic performance of samples observed under the transmission electron microscopy.

## Doppler flowmetry
The methodologies have been reported in our previous studies [ ]. mice were anesthetized by inhalation of isoflurane (2.0%) after 24 h hypoxia exposure. The regional cerebral blood flow (rCBF) was measured in mouse brains using a laser doppler scan (moorLDLS, Moor Instruments, UK). The ratio of blood stream in the state of normoxia and hypoxia were computed with moor FLPI-2 V50 software.

## Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP, ΔΨm)
The effects of acute hypoxia on the mitochondrial membrane potential in mice brain tissue were evaluated by immunofluorescence using the JC-1 assay kit (Do Jindo Co., Ltd., Japan). At high MMP, JC-1 aggregates in the matrix of mitochondria and forms red fluorescence; at low MMP, JC-1 is monomeric and produces green fluorescence. And mitochondrial depolarisation is determined by the green/red fluorescence ratio. The treated mouse brain tissues were prepared into cell suspensions, and 500 µL of JC-1 staining solution was added to each group of cell suspensions, incubated at 37 °C for 20 min, and then washed twice with JC-1 buffer. Finally, the injury degree of ΔΨm was immediately detected by High-content cell imaging and analysis system.

## Mitochondrial respiratory function and ATP rate assay
Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production by mitochondrial respiration under acute hypoxic conditions were detected according to the Hippocampal XFp Cellular Mitochondrial Stress Assay Kit and ATP Real-Time Assay Kit and quantified using a Seahorse XFp Analyser (Agilent Technologies). Brain tissues from normoxia and hypoxia groups were prepared into cell suspensions, which were plated overnight in 8-well plates. Mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation including basal respiratory, spare respiratory capacity, and maximal respiration were analyzed under basal conditions, in the presence of 2 µM oligomycin, 1 µM 4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP), and 0.5 mM rotenone/antimycin A. ATP production was calculated for mitochondrial and glycolytic ATP production in the presence of 2 µM oligomycin and 0.5 mM R/A.

## Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis
MR analysis relies on three fundamental assumptions: (1) The initial assumption posits a robust correlation between instrumental variables (IVs) and mitochondria (the exposure factor under investigation). (2) The assumption of independence denotes that IVs and the outcome variables, namely hypoxia injury, remain uncorrelated with any other confounding factors. (3) IVs can solely impact hypoxia by modulating mitochondrial activity without the involvement of any additional pathways. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) summary statistics were available only for hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α), which has been suggested as a surrogate biomarker for hypoxia injury. Currently, a GWAS dataset specifically containing genetic variant association with hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α) has been published [ ]. We used this dataset to explore whether mitochondrial dysfunction is a consequence of hypoxia injury by MR analyses.
Five methods were employed to determine the causal relationship between mitochondrial biological function (exposure factor) and the outcome of hypoxia injury. These methods encompassed inverse variance weighting (IVW), MR-Egger regression, simple mode, simple median, and weighted median. The “TwoSampleMR” package in the R programming language was utilized to visualize MR results by forest plots. Typically, the IVW method’s results are the primary gauge of MR analysis reliability, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05 indicating a positive outcome.

## GeneMANIA analysis
GeneMANIA (Multiple Association Network Integration Algorithm, http://www.genemania.org), as an online analysis tool, provides information for hub genes (TRAP1) and genetic interactions, biochemical and physiological reactions, pathways, co-localization, and predicts molecules potential functions. By means of it, we constructed an interaction network centred on TRAP1.

## Molecular docking
The structures of TRAP1 and mitochondrial dynamics-associated proteins including DRP1, mitofusin 1 and 2 (MFN1/2), and fission protein 1 (FIS1) were downloaded from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/), respectively, and the protein structures were modified by adding hydrogen atoms and removing water molecules using PyMOL software. Finally, the modified protein structures and the 3D structures of the homologous small molecules were downloaded from the PubChem database and submitted to Schrödinger software for molecular docking. Binding energy indicates that the ligand can spontaneously bind to the receptor, and a lower binding score indicates a higher binding capacity.

## Western blotting analysis
Primary hippocampal neurons, astrocytes cells, BV-2 cells and brain tissue samples were lysed using RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Thermofisher, Massachusetts, USA). Lysates were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk prepared in tris buffered saline with Tween (TBST) for 2 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies (TRAP1: 1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology; DRP1: 1:800, Cell Signaling Technology; MFN1: 1:1000, Abclonal; MFN2: 1:1000, Abclonal; FIS1: 1:800, Ablonal; β-actin: 1:500, Abcam) in antibody diluent solution (0.5% BSA) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with secondary horseradish peroxidase-conjugated antibodies (1:2000, Proteintech) for 1 h in 5% skim milk prepared in TBST. Finally, the membranes were washed three times with TBST, treated with ECL solution (Abkkine, WUHAN, China), and analyzed using an Image Lab system (Bio‐Rad, California, USA).

## Data selection and analysis
Mitochondrial biological function and its associated GWAS data in relation to hypoxia injury (prot-a-1337; European; 2018) were sourced from databases such as https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/ and https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/. Two-tailed unpaired t-tests, one-way ANOVA and two-way ANOVA test were performed according to the experimental designs. Data were presented as the standard error of the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The investigators are double-blinded. *P < 0.05 indicates a statistically significant difference. Statistical analysis and Graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism 6.

## Results

## Evaluation of brain damage in acute hypoxia mice
To investigate the changes of acute hypoxia on brain morphology, microstructure and brain function, transcranial Doppler flow analysis, cell morphology and molecular biology were performed. Cerebral blood flow analysis revealed a notable reduction in cerebral perfusion during acute hypoxia, in contrast to the normoxia group (P < 0.001, Fig.  B). As shown in Fig.  C and D, electron microscopy results suggested that mitochondrial ridges in the hippocampal region of brain tissue under hypoxia were disturbed and significantly reduced, and that this was accompanied by the occurrence of mitochondrial depolarization with loss of membrane potential (P < 0.001). The response to mitochondrial damage is closely related to oxidative stress. Compared to the normoxia group, examinations of the antioxidant enzymes SOD and CAT further revealed a marked decrease in their capacity to neutralize the body’s reactive oxygen species influx following a 24-hour acute hypoxia (Fig.  E and F; P < 0.05 and P < 0.01).

## Evaluation of acute hypoxia-induced apoptosis in primary hippocampal neurons and neuroglia
In addition to the above examination of brain damage in vivo, we did further studies at the cellular level in vitro. To assess the sensitivity of different types of neuronal cells in response to acute hypoxia stimuli, we examined the cell viability of primary hippocampal neuronal cells, astrocytes, and BV-2 cells after acute hypoxia 24 h, respectively. The findings indicated a notable decrease in the survival of all three cell types, with BV-2 cells showing the highest sensitivity to hypoxia (Fig.  B, P < 0.0001). Meanwhile, quantification of primary hippocampal neuron-specific labelling and astrocyte apoptosis assays further revealed significant pro-apoptotic effects of acute hypoxia on primary hippocampal neurons and astrocytes (Supplementary Fig. ). Observations through a light microscope indicated a notable decrease in primary hippocampal neurons after 24 h of hypoxia compared to those in the normoxia group, characterized by disordered arrangement, indistinct edges, and partial structure. Additionally, there was no substantial change in astrocyte count, and the alterations in protrusions and gap junctions were not readily apparent. There was a minor reduction in BV-2 cell numbers, and the polarization of rounded and spindle cells was observed, accompanied by enlarged cytosol, more protrusions, and a higher frequency of filamentary pseudopods (Fig.  C).

## Bioinformatics analysis of TRAP1 in relation to mitochondrial dynamics
To further explore the relationship between TRAP1 and mitochondria, we performed data integration analysis based on the GWAS database. Twenty-six mitochondria related datasets, including their data sample GWAS IDs and name, were shown to be strongly associated with hypoxia injury, were screened and visualized from 10,534,735 SNPs by five methods (Fig.  A). As shown in Fig.  B; Table  , MR results revealed that 19 mitochondrial associated proteins were identified as protective factors causally associated with hypoxia, primarily involved in mitochondrial fission/fusion (“prot-a-1961”, “prot-a-896”, and “prot-a-2776”), transport (“prot-a-847”), metabolism regulation (“prot-a-2025”, “prot-a-2041”, “prot-a-1572”, “prot-a-2749”, “prot-a-2737”, “prot-a-2190”, and “prot-a-1907”) and ribosomal protein synthesis (“prot-a-1943”, “prot-a-1944”, “prot-a-2526”, “prot-a-1945”, and “prot-a-1965”). Additionally, the remaining seven exposures were causally associated with hypoxia as risk factors (“prot-a-1055”, “prot-a-825”, “prot-a-818”, “prot-a-2653”, “prot-a-2022” “prot-a-1953”, and “prot-a-2657”) (Supplementary Fig. ).
 GENEmania revealed several links between TRAP1 and proteins in signaling pathways. As shown in Fig.  C, TRAP1 interacts with significantly regulated proteins, primarily involving ubiquitin-like protein ligases binding (key enzymes that regulates mitochondrial dynamics), regulation of apoptotic processes, and biogenesis and metabolism of reactive oxygen species. In particular, TRAP1, a mitochondrial chaperone protein, is closely linked to mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins.

## Effects of TRAP1 knockdown on mitochondrial respiratory function and energy metabolism
To investigate the response of TRAP1 to energy metabolism production, TRAP1 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. ) and total ATP levels in primary hippocampal neuronal cells of brain tissue exposed to acute hypoxia were assessed. As shown in Fig.  A, compared with normoxia, ATP produced by glycolysis was significantly up-regulated by stress protection under acute hypoxia (P < 0.05), whereas ATP produced by mitochondrial respiration was significantly decreased (P < 0.01). Conversely, ATP levels during glycolysis were also significantly reduced (P < 0.05) after TRAP1 knockdown (si-TRAP1) and hypoxia, with no effect on ATP levels during mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation compared to the hypoxia group. We further assessed the effect of TRAP1 on glycolysis levels and showed that hypoxia significantly induced glucose uptake and lactate production, while TRAP1 knockdown significantly suppressed glucose levels (Supplementary Fig. ). Furthermore, to further measure mitochondrial bioenergetics after cellular metabolic stress, oxygen consumption rate of neurons in hippocampus of brain tissue was measured, indirectly reflecting the effect on overall mitochondrial respiratory function (Fig.  B). The results of OCR showed a non-significant decreasing trend in mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) after 24 h of acute hypoxia. However, basal respiration, maximal respiration, and spare respiration capacity were significantly decreased after 24 h of hypoxia and TRAP1 knockdown (Fig.  C-E, P < 0.05), suggesting that there is a real possibility that TRAP1 affects mitochondrial OXPHOS under acute hypoxia. 
 

## Bio-informatics prediction of interaction between TRAP1 and mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins (DRP1、FIS1、MFN1/2)
To further explore the possibility that TRAP1 is involved in regulating the expression of mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins, we modeled the structure of TRAP1, DRP1, FIS1, MFN1/2 and found that TRAP1 has a high docking scores with DRP1, FIS1, and MFN1/2 in MOE system (Fig.  ). MOE software reveals that Arg717 of TRAP1 engages with Glu 168 of DRP1 (S: −81.6878 and Energy: −3.04), while Lys649 of TRAP1 attaches to Leu123 of FIS1 (S: −73.8169 and Energy: −10.79). The Lys16 of MFN1 exhibited a possible interaction with Cys651 of TRAP1 (S: −77.3145 and Energy: −7.00). There is a pronounced binding observed between Glu 392 of MFN2 and Lys997 of TRAP1 (S: −95.8125 and Energy: −18.15). Table  displays the hydrogen bond binding locations among TRAP1 and DRP1, FIS1, MFN1/2, warranting additional exploration.
 

## Effects of TRAP1 knockdown on mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins in primary hippocampal neurons, astrocytes and subcellular mitochondrial dynamics in BV-2 cells
To investigate the effect of TRAP1 on the process of mitochondrial division and fusion, we performed immunoblotting analysis of DRP1, FIS1, and MFN1/2 from primary hippocampal neuronal cells, astrocytes, and BV-2 cells, respectively. As shown in Fig.  , in primary hippocampal neuronal cells, the expressions of TRAP1, DRP1, and MFN1 were significantly up-regulated after 24 h of acute hypoxia, while there was no statistically significant difference in the expression of FIS1 and MFN2. TRAP1 knockdown and hypoxia resulted in significant down-regulation of DRP1 and MFN1, while FIS1 and MFN2 remained largely unchanged.
 Following a 24-hour period of acute hypoxia in astrocytes, there was a notable decrease in the expression of FIS1 and MFN2, in contrast to a substantial increase in MNF1, while DRP1 remained largely unchanged. TRAP1 knockdown and hypoxia led to a notable increase in DRP1, FIS1, and MFN2 expression, while significantly decreasing MFN1 expression.
BV-2 cells exhibited a notable decrease in TRAP1, DRP1, and MFN1 levels following 24 h of acute hypoxia. TRAP1 knockdown and hypoxia resulted in significant down-regulation of DRP1 and FIS1 expression, and no significant changes in MFN1 and MFN2.

## Discussion
Cerebral hypoxia, whether due to environmental factors (aerospace activities, deep-sea diving, mountain climbing, altitude tourism, etc.) or pathological factors (e.g. stroke, myocardial infarction, respiratory diseases, etc.), can causes a certain degree of damage to the organism [ , ]. Regulation of mitochondrial dynamics is essential for maintaining mitochondrial function and cellular homeostasis in brain. Here, we investigated changes in mitochondrial damage in brain tissue and cells under acute hypoxia. We found that TRAP1 plays a critical role in regulating mitochondrial dynamics and preventing anoxic-induced brain damage, and that it responds differently to mitochondrial dynamics in different types of brain cells.
Under acute hypoxia, both neurons and glial cells suffer irreversible damage, resulting in neurological impairments and structure alterations in neural tissue [ – ]. Neurons exhibit a heightened sensitivity to lack of oxygen, leading to disrupted energy metabolism, elevated levels of calcium within cells, and eventually, cell demise [ ]. Astrocytes, the supportive cells in the nervous system, get activated under acute hypoxia, triggering the release of inflammatory mediators and modifying the extracellular matrix, thereby affecting the neuronal milieu [ ]. The brain’s inherent immune cells, microglial cells, also respond to hypoxia by changing their morphology and releasing pro-inflammatory factors [ ]. In this study, we noted a decrease in neuronal apoptosis, activation of astrocytes, and polarization of BV2 cell morphology following 24 h of acute hypoxia. Observations of acute hypoxia in organelles, aligning with prior research [ ], revealed mitochondrial swelling and rupture, mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization, and diminished antioxidant enzyme function. Furthermore, acute hypoxia disrupts the self-regulating mechanism of cerebral blood flow, resulting in either insufficient or excessive perfusion, subsequently causing cell damage or necrosis [ ]. The current research found a notable decrease in Doppler cerebral blood circulation in mice experiencing acute hypoxia, possibly due to sympathetic nerves activating to induce cerebral vasoconstriction, resulting in insufficient cerebral perfusion.
Further confirmation of the link between hypoxia and mitochondrial activity was achieved through Mendelian randomization and interaction studies, backed by clinical public big data, which helps to understand more clearly the underlying pathology of cerebral hypoxic injury and to capture potential biomarkers. There are certainly some limitations. Our study is mainly based on Europeans and lacks ethnic generalisability. Besides, brain hypoxia is a complex physiological process involving multiple host and environmental factors. Mitochondrial functional phenotypes may influence the risk of brain injury through these factors. In hypoxia-related diseases, the imbalance between mitochondrial fission and fusion processes disrupts mitochondrial morphology and distribution [ ]. TRAP1’s role in controlling mitochondrial movements in brain damage caused by hypoxia is intricate. Recent research indicates that TRAP1 interacts with several key proteins involved in mitochondrial dynamics and oxidative stress, contributing to the control of mitochondrial fission and fusion, along with defense against oxidative stress [ , ]. Several mechanisms have been proposed regarding the role of TRAP1 in controlling mitochondrial dynamics during brain injury. A process involves TRAP1 engaging with and suppressing Drp1’s function, resulting in decreased mitochondrial fission [ ]. Another proposed mechanism for TRAP1 regulation of mitochondrial dynamics is through the regulation of OPA1 processing. TRAP1 interacts with OPA1 and prevents its proteolytic processing, leading to increased mitochondrial fusion [ ]. We performed molecular docking of mitochondrial fission/fusion proteins in terms of molecular dynamics, and confirmed TRAP1’s interaction with DRP1, FIS1, and MFN1/2. While our study provides valuable insights into the possibility of TRAP1 binding to mitochondrial dynamics-related proteins.it is important to acknowledge the limitations associated with the absence of ChIP and pull-down experiments. Future studies incorporating these methodologies would greatly enhance our understanding and provide more robust evidence to support our conclusions.
Regarding brain damage caused by acute hypoxia, our study focused on investigate the expression and regulation of TRAP1 and assessing its role in modulating mitochondrial dynamics. The research indicated an increase in TRAP1 levels in primary hippocampal neurons and astrocytes due to hypoxia, suggesting an adaptive mechanism to guard against mitochondrial dysfunction; however, a notable decrease is observed in BV-2 cells, hinting at BV-2’s heightened vulnerability to hypoxia, resulting in cellular polarization. Additionally, our findings showed that TRAP1 knockdown affects mitochondrial dysfunction as evidenced by reduced ATP production and attenuated mitochondrial respiration. Drawing from previous studies targeting the effects of TRAP1 on brain tissue damage [ ], we additionally confirmed that TRAP1 knockdown responds differently to mitochondrial dynamics-associated proteins in primary hippocampal neurons, astrocytes and BV-2 cells. The suppression of TRAP1 in primary hippocampal neurons led to a decrease in DRP1 and MFN1 expression, indicating that acute hypoxia impacts mitochondrial fission and fusion, yet has no effect on FIS1 and MFN2. Within astrocytes, reducing TRAP1 levels enhanced DRP1 and FIS1 proteins, indicating hypoxia-triggered mitochondrial division, while simultaneously boosting MFN2 expression and diminishing MFN1 protein levels. In contrast, reducing TRAP1 in BV-2 cells led to lower levels of DRP1 and FIS1, yet it did not impact MFN1/2, suggesting that TRAP1 reduction facilitates mitochondrial division in acute hypoxic conditions. In our future studies, we will continue to experimentally determine the specific mechanisms of targeted binding of TRAP1 to mitochondrial dynamics. Subsequently, we aim to corroborate these results using animal models and potential clinical trials to evaluate TRAP1’s applicability in treating acute hypoxic brain injury.

## Conclusion
Taken together, our study provides evidence for the involvement of TRAP1 in regulating mitochondrial dynamics and protecting against hypoxia-induced brain injury. The upregulation of TRAP1 in response to hypoxia suggests its potential as a therapeutic target for mitigating mitochondrial dysfunction and improving patient outcomes in acute hypoxia-related diseases. While we recognize the importance of examining differences in mitochondrial responses in neurons, astrocytes and BV-2 cells, our current studies are limited to the specific cell types and conditions studied. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the exact mechanisms by which TRAP1 modulates mitochondrial dynamics and to explore its therapeutic potential in preclinical and clinical settings.
