## Abstract
Vascular cognitive impairment (VCI), a prevalent form of dementia resulting from chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH), remains devoid of effective therapeutic strategies. While hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) has shown promise in mitigating neurological damage, its molecular mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated. Here, we demonstrate that HBOT confers robust neuroprotection against CCH-induced VCI through the microRNA-137-3p (miR-137-3p)/TNF receptor-associated factor 3 (TRAF3) signaling axis. In a mouse model of CCH, prolonged HBOT administration significantly improved cognitive performance across multiple behavioral domains—including spatial, recognition, and contextual memory—concomitant with enhanced cerebral blood flow and oxygen saturation. Mechanistically, HBOT upregulated hippocampal miR-137-3p, which directly targeted TRAF3, leading to suppression of the TAK1/NF-κB pathway and attenuation of neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis. Using neuron–astrocyte–microglia tri-culture systems, we established that miR-137-3p agonism recapitulated HBOT-mediated neuroprotection, whereas TRAF3 overexpression exacerbated inflammatory and apoptotic cascades. Our findings identify the miR-137-3p/TRAF3/NF-κB pathway as a critical effector of HBOT efficacy, offering a promising therapeutic target for vascular cognitive impairment.

## Introduction
Vascular cognitive impairment (VCI), a silent yet devastating neurological consequence of vascular risk factors and cerebrovascular pathologies [ , ], leading to a spectrum of symptoms ranging from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to vascular dementia (VaD).VCI accounts for 30% of dementia diagnoses,just behind Alzheimer’s disease, which represents 60% [ , ]. A defining feature of VCI is chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH), a pathophysiological hallmark that stands as a beacon of ongoing neurodegeneration [ , ], this persistent reduction in cerebral blood flow (20% ~ 40%) can result in neuronal damage in vulnerable brain regions, such as the hippocampus and cerebral cortex, thereby contributing to the development of VCI [ ]. And CCH is not just a primary cause of neuronal loss, it is also a significant contributor to the development of progressive brain injuries. The ensuing brain injuries are multifaceted, encompassing neuronal apoptosis and pyroptosis, synaptic plasticity impairment, neuroinflammation, neuroendocrine perturbations and metabolic dysregulation [ – ].Despite the profound impact of CCH-induced brain injury, effective therapeutic interventions for VCI remain a challenge.
Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) involves the inhalation of 97 to 100% oxygen at pressures between 1.5 and 3.0 atmospheres absolute (ATA) for durations of 60 to 120 min [ ]. HBOT is widely considered safe and well-tolerated. It has been implicated in the amelioration of various age-related conditions, such as vascular cognitive impairment, Alzheimer’s disease, and vascular dementia [ ]. Evidence indicates that HBOT may exert therapeutic benefits in vascular cognitive impairment through a variety of potential pathways: enhancing oxygen supply and tissue oxygen partial pressure (pO2), alleviating cerebral edema, reducing intracranial pressure, promoting tissue healing and angiogenesis, improving metabolism, reducing apoptosis, mitigating oxidative stress, enhancing mitochondrial function, promoting cell differentiation, and facilitating hippocampal neural regeneration [ – ], thereby augmenting learning and memory functions.Nevertheless, the exact mechanisms of action are not yet fully understood. Further investigation into the cellular and molecular mechanisms mediating HBOT’s effects on vascular cognitive dysfunction is essential to elucidate its potential therapeutic applications in the context of vascular cognitive impairment.
MicroRNAs (miRNAs), a class of non-coding small RNA molecules measuring approximately 18–22 nucleotides in length [ , ] play a pivotal role in the regulation of target gene expression [ , ]. In recent years, several studies have shown that miRNAs mediate CCH-induced cognitive deficit, including miR-132 [ ], miRNA-181c [ ], and miR-218a-5p [ ]. Previously, miR-137-3p has been reported to participate in ischemia-reperfusion-induced neuronal apoptosis [ ]. Not only that, the development, differentiation, and maturation of the nervous system are closely associated with the expression of miR-137, the miR-137 pathway is involved in the processes of neurogenesis and the onset and progression of cognitive disorders [ ]. Furthermore,tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 3 (TRAF3), a critical adaptor protein in the TNF receptor-associated factor family, is instrumental in signal transduction, immune response modulation, and the intricate balance of cell survival and apoptosis. It is emerging as a promising therapeutic target for stroke, with its expression in neurons being induced by ischemic injury, thereby affecting apoptotic signaling mechanisms [ ].TRAF3 is also a crucial signal transducer in nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and apoptotic signal pathways [ ]. However, the roles of TRAF3 on CCH-induced brain injury remain unknown. In addition, the effects of miR-137-3p on CCH-induced brain injury via targeting TRAF3 have not been reported in our best knowledge.
Thus, in the present study, we examined the effects of long-term HBOT on CCH-induced cognitive deficits and neuropathology, and explored the underlying mechanisms against VCI. The findings from this study may potentially provide novel insights into the application of HBOT therapy for VCI in clinical settings.

## Animals
Male C57BL/6 mice, 10-14 weeks old, weighing between 24-30 g, were bred and fed in the same conditions in accordance with institutional guidelines of the Animal Care and Use Committee (Wuhan University of Science and Technology, Wuhan, China) within the University’s animal care facility. Mice were housed in a group of 4 per cage at 25 °C in a light-controlled (12 h light/ dark cycle) room, providing free access to water and food. In the present study, only male mice were used to avoid behavioral variability between genders. All experiments and analyses were performed blind to the mice treatment.

## Establishment of CCH model
CCH was induced by bilateral common carotid artery occlusion (BCCAO) according to previous studies [ , ]. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, I.P.). Through a midline incision in the neck area, the left and right common carotid artery (CCA) were dissected and separated. CCAs were ligated with silk suture by double ligation for 20 mins and then ligations were cut for recanalization. Mice in the control group were sham-operated for 20 mins without occlusion of the carotid arteries. After surgery, mice were placed in the nursing box to recover from anesthesia and then returned to their cages. Neurological severity score [ ] was measured 24 h after the operation. BCCAO mice with neurological severity score over 6 were considered successful CCH model. Only mice with neurological severity score of 0 were considered qualified control.

## Hyperbaric oxygen therapy
Mice were subjected to hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) by administering 100% oxygen at a pressure of 2 ATA for 60 min daily in a single-chamber specifically designed for small animals, over a period of 90 consecutive days. Prior to pressurization, the chamber was flushed with 100% oxygen for 5 min to enrich the oxygen content. The pressurization and depressurization processes were conducted gradually over 25 min. After pressurization, the oxygen level within the chamber reached a saturation of ≥96%, as measured by an oxygen analyzer. For the sham and CCH group, animals were placed in the chamber for 60 min without additional treatment (at 1 ATA). During all treatment sessions in the chamber, the temperature was controlled at similar conditions between groups [ ].

## Gait analysis
A rodent gait fine analysis system (SansBio) was used to detect stride length and the maximum footprint area of mice before behavioral tests. Mice were positioned on the gait analysis system’s treadmill, allowing them to walk at their own pace while their movements were video recorded. We captured at least five full and smooth walking cycles, discarding any footage where the mice paused, sniffed around, lifted their front paws, or turned back. This process was continued until we had enough good-quality video clips for analysis.

## Y maze test
A Y-maze with three arms (40 cm long, 15 cm high, and 5 cm wide) at equal angles was used to detect spatial reference memory of mice. The arm where the mice were initially placed was labeled S(start), with the other two arms being labeled O(other) and N(novel). The Y-maze test consisted of two trials, separated by a 2-h interval. The first trial was the exposure phase, in which one arm was closed and the mice were allowed to freely explore the other two arms for 10 min. During the second trial (test phase) which was conducted 2 h later, all arms were opened and the mice were allowed to move freely in the three arms for 10 min. The number of novel arm entries and time of mice entering the novel arm, as well as time in the center were recorded. Noduls automatic tracking and analysis system was used.

## Novel object recognition (NOR) test
A white square plastic box (length: 50 cm; width: 50 cm; height:40 cm) was used in the test. This test included three rounds.In the first round, the mice were placed in the empty box and allowed to move freely for 5 min to adapt to the environment. After the experiment, the empty box was cleaned with 70% alcohol to remove the odor. The second round of tests was conducted 1 h later, with two identical objects fixed 5 cm away from the two box walls; the mice were allowed to move freely for 10 min. And the third round of test was carried out 1 h after the second round, similar to the familiarization phase, except that one of the same objects was replaced with another novel object; the animals were allowed to move freely in the box for 10 min. The time mice spent exploring the familiar and novel objects were recorded using Noduls automatic tracking and analysis system, and the discrimination index was calculated of both round 2 and round 3.Discrimination index was defined as the difference of the exploratory time between two objects versus the total time spent exploring both of the objects, and was considered to be an indicator of recognition memory.

## Morris water maze (MWM) test
A circular pool (diameter: 100 cm; height: 50 cm) filled with water at 25 °C was divided into four equal quadrants by four equidistant points on the wall. A transparent escape platform (diameter: 7 cm) was submerged approximately 1.5 cm below the water surface and located in the center of quadrant IV. Four different references were hung on the wall of four quadrants as visual cues for spatial orientation. In the acquisition trial phase, each mouse received four trials, with an interval of 10 min, per day for 5 days (D1-D5). During each trial, mice were gently placed in the pool towards the wall in one of four quadrants. The time mice spending on finding the hidden platform (latency) and swimming track was recorded. The maximum latency for each trial is 60 s. If the mouse failed to find the platform in 60 s, it was guided and stayed on it for 10 s. On day 6, mice were subjected to a probe test with the platform removed. Each mouse was allowed to swim in the pool for 60 s. The time spent in the target quadrant (quadrant concluding the platform) and the number of entries into the original platform area were recorded. Noduls automatic tracking and analysis system was used.

## Fear conditioning test (FCT)
In the conditioning period, each mouse was placed in a test chamber (25x35x35 cm) and allowed to explore for 2 min. A 50-dB white noise was presented for 20 s to serve as the conditioned stimulus, and then a foot shock (0.3 mA) was presented for 2 s to serve as the unconditioned stimulus. Totally, three paired conditioned-unconditioned stimulus was presented with an interval of 2 min. The contextual test was carried out 24 h after conditioning in the same chamber for 2 min without foot shock. On the third day, cued test was carried out in a triangular chamber (35x35x35cm). After each mouse was placed in the chamber and explored for 2 min, the white noise was presented without foot shock. Freezing level of the mouse was recorded for 2 min after white noise. Data acquisition, control of stimuli, and data analysis were performed automatically, using Noduls software.

## Measurement of blood oxygen saturation
Pulse oximeters (STARR Life Sciences® Corp) were used to monitor the blood oxygen saturation(SpO₂) of experimental mice in real-time while awake.The day before the experiment, we shaved the necks of all mice because dark hairs interfere with detecting light signals. At the same time, we clamped the test clips on the necks of the mice and placed them in the experimental cage to adapt to the environment. When the formal experiment began, we turned on the pulse oximeter, selected the awake mode, and set the parameters. The sensor was clipped to the neck of the experimental mouse (where the carotid artery pulsates) and the changes in blood oxygen saturation in the mice were monitored for 24 h [ ].We averaged the data every 10 min. Oxygen saturation fluctuations were expressed by the coefficient of variation (CV) (standard deviation/mean*100) [ , ].

## Laser speckle contrast imaging
RFLSI ZW Laser Speckle Blood Flow Imaging System(RWD Life Science Co., Ltd.) was used to monitor the cerebral blood flow of experimental mice.The Laser speckle flowmetry obtains high-resolution, two-dimensional imaging and has a linear relationship with absolute CBF values. An anesthesia mask for mice was used for isoflurane inhalation. The skull was placed 16 cm below the scan head and the scalp was removed by a midline incision to expose the skull throughout CBF evaluation. CBF was measured in identically-sized regions of interest (ROI) (circle 1 mm in diameter), located 1 mm posterior and 2 mm lateral from the bregma, corresponding to regions around Heubner’s anastomoses, connecting the dorsal branches of the anterior cerebral artery and the middle cerebral artery. Average CBF values in the bilateral hemispheres were recorded [ , – ].

## TUNEL staining
TUNEL assay kit (Proteintech) was used to determine apoptosis according to the manufacturer’s instruction. 30μm frozen section slices of mouse brains were fixed in 4% polyformaldehyde solution for 30 min. After PBS rinse, slices were incubated in proteinase K solution at room temperature for 20 min and then in TUNEL reaction buffer containing TdT enzyme at 37 °C for 2 h. DAPI was used to stain nuclei. The positive cells were identified with a fluorescence microscope and analyzed by Image J software.

## RNA extraction and qRT-PCR
Total RNA was collected from mouse brain tissues or N2a cells using RNA/Protein Co-extraction Kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was then reverse transcribed using PrimeScript RT kit (Takara Biotechnology). The expression level of miR-137,TNF-α,IL-1β and IL-18 were detected by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) using a 7500 Real Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). The conditions were 95 °C for 3 min, 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 45 s (40 cycles). U6 RNA served as an endogenous reference. All experiments were repeated three times. Relative expressions were calculated based on the relative quantification 2-ΔΔCt method [ ]. Primers are listed in Table .

## Western blot
Total proteins in brain tissues or N2a cells were extracted using RNA/Protein Co-extraction Kit. Protein concentration was detected using BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime). Protein samples were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, at a voltage of 100 V. After separation, the protein sample was transferred to nitrocellulose membrane (Beyotime). 5% skim milk powder solution was used to block for 1 h at the room temperature, and the primary antibody against TRAF3 (1:1000, Proteintech), Bcl-2 (1:1000, Proteintech), Bax (1:1000, Proteintech), Cleaved caspase 3 (1:1000, Proteintech), or Actin (1:1000, Proteintech) was used to incubate overnight at 4 °C. After rinse in trisbuffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) for three times, secondary antibody (LI-COR) was used to incubate for 1 h. The protein bands were visualized using the Odyssey Imaging System (LI-COR). Western blot data were quantified using Image J software, and relative expression was calculated using Actin as a loading control.

## Immunofluorescence
The coronal sections were then incubated with the primary antibody for Iba1 (rabbit anti-Iba1 antibody, 1:500; Wako Pure Chemical Industries)at 4 °C overnight.Sections were then labeled with fluorescence-labeled secondary antibody (AlexaFluor 647-labeled donkey anti-rabbit IgG, 1:300; abcam) at room temperature for 1.5 h in the dark. Stain the nucleus with DAPI. Images were captured with a confocal fluorescence microscope. Iba1-positive cells in the hippocampus were identified and analyzed by Image J software.

## Cell culture and transfection
Mouse N2a cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Invitrogen) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Solarbio) at 37 °C in 5% carbon dioxide (CO2). Transfection of 100 nM miR-137-3p mimic or NC mimic was carried out with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. miR-137-3p mimic and negative control were synthesized by RiboBio.Prepare the transfection complex with TRAF3 overexpression plasmid (GeneCopoeia) and Lipofectamine 3000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Add the complex to N2a cells and gently rock to ensure even distribution. Incubate at 37 °C with 5% CO2.
As a supplementary experiment, primary neuron-microglia-astrocyte tri-cultures were subjected to additional validation experiments involving: transfection with miR-137-3p mimic,TRAF3 overexpression plasmid and TRAF3 knockdown siRNA.TRAF3 siRNA and TRAF3 siRNA negative control were separately dissolved in DMEM solution, which were blended with Lipofectamine3000 transfection reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

## Dual-luciferase reporter assay
TRAF3 3’ UTR Wild type or mutant target fragment was inserted into firefly-tagged pGL3 promoter luciferase vector (Promega). N2a cells were co-transfected with 1 μg of the Renilla control luciferase vector (Promega) and 1 μg of the firefly-tagged pGL3 promoter luciferase vector. Additionally, each well was co-transfected with 100 nM miR-137-3p mimic or NC mimic. 48 h after transfection, cells were lysed for luciferase activity detection using a dual luciferase assay system (Promega). Results were shown as the relative value of firefly/Renilla.

## Virus injection and agomir administration
Mice were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium, and then mounted by stereotaxic apparatus. Mice were injected with virus overexpressing TRAF3 or control virus. Purified high-titer virus was bilaterally infused in hippocampus (500 nl/side) at the following coordinates: −1.82 mm anterior posterior, 1.50 mm medial lateral, and 2.00 mm dorsal ventral. The infusion rate was 50 nl/min. miR-137-3p agomir or NC agomir (1 nmol) was intraperitoneally injected in mice two times a week for 3 weeks, miR-137-3p agomir and negative control were synthesized by RiboBio.After 3 weeks, mice were subjected to behavioral tests.

## Quantification and statistical analysis
All data were shown as the mean ± SEM. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism. Comparisons of continuous variables were performed using t test, or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. p < 0.05 was taken to indicate statistical significance.

## Results

## HBOT attenuates CCH-induced cognitive deficits
To assess the potential of HBOT to mitigate cognitive impairments induced by CCH, a series of behavioral tests were conducted following the establishment of the BCCAO model and subsequent HBOT intervention (Fig. ). The CCH model (Fig. ) was validated by a significant decrease in CBF monitored by a laser speckle flowmetry(Fig. ) (Fig. ) and a significant increase in neurological severity scores (NSS) post-BCCAO (Fig. ). Gait analysis prior to behavioral testing revealed no significant inter-group differences in stride length or maximum footprint area, suggesting that motor function of mice was unaffected (Fig. ).
 In the Y-maze test (Fig. ), designed to assess spatial reference memory, CCH mice demonstrated a significant decrease in both the number of entries into the novel arm and the time spent in the novel arm and center, indicating spatial memory deficits. HBOT significantly restored performance, with HBOT-treated CCH mice showing increased entries into and time spent in both the novel arm and the center, suggesting that HBOT effectively rescues CCH-induced deficits in spatial reference memory (Fig. ).
The novel object recognition (NOR) test (Fig. ) was employed to assess recognition memory, a measure of cognitive flexibility and memory retention. During the second round, the exploratory time spent on two identical familiar objects and the discrimination index showed no significant differences across groups (Fig. ). However, in the third round, both the sham and HBOT-treated CCH groups displayed a preference for the novel object, indicated by a significantly higher discrimination index compared to untreated CCH mice, who showed no preference. These findings suggest that HBOT successfully restores recognition memory deficits induced by CCH (Fig. ). Supplementary data showed that 30-day and 60-day HBOT failed to significantly improved cognition(Y-maze test and NOR test) in CCH + HBOT mice versus CCH mice (Fig. ).
To evaluate spatial learning and memory, we conducted the Morris Water Maze (MWM) test (Fig. ). In the acquisition phase (days 1-5), CCH mice exhibited prolonged escape latencies, indicative of impaired learning compared to the sham group. HBOT-treated CCH mice, however, demonstrated significantly reduced escape latency, indicating an improvement in learning capacity (Fig. ). In the probe trial (day 6), CCH mice spent significantly less time in the target quadrant and crossed the platform area fewer times than the sham controls, reflecting spatial memory impairment. HBOT treatment restored both time spent in the target quadrant and platform crossings, suggesting that HBOT mitigates CCH-induced spatial memory deficits (Fig. ).
Contextual and cued memory were further assessed using the fear conditioning test (Fig. ). During the conditioning phase, no significant differences in freezing behavior were observed among groups, indicating similar baseline learning of the conditioned fear stimulus (Fig. ). In the contextual memory test, CCH mice displayed significantly reduced freezing behavior compared to the sham group, suggesting impaired contextual memory retention. This deficit was alleviated in HBOT-treated CCH mice, as evidenced by restored freezing responses similar to sham controls (Fig. ). No differences were found among groups in the cued memory test, indicating that cued memory was not affected by CCH (Fig. ).
In summary, HBOT treatment effectively alleviated multiple domains of cognitive impairment induced by CCH, including spatial reference memory, recognition memory, spatial learning, and contextual memory, supporting the therapeutic potential of HBOT in the context of vascular cognitive decline.

## HBOT attenuates CCH-induced cerebrovascular hypoperfusion and improves blood oxygen saturation
Impairments in cerebral circulation and vascular reactivity have substantial roles in onset and progression of VCI. We investigated resting of CBF in three groups using laser speckle blood flow imaging system.HBOT-treated CCH mice exhibited significantly increased CBF compared with untreated CCH mice (p < 0.05) (Fig. ) (Fig. ).Our study also showed that HBOT increased blood oxygen saturation level (p < 0.01) and ameliorated oxygen saturation fluctuations (p < 0.05) in CCH mice after treatment (Fig. ). Supplementary data showed that 30-day and 60-day HBOT failed to significantly improved CBF in CCH + HBOT mice versus CCH mice (Fig. ).
 

## HBOT rescues CCH-induced neuronal apoptosis and neuroinflammation
Neuronal apoptosis is a key pathological feature of CCH. To investigate the effect of HBOT on apoptosis, we performed TUNEL staining and Western blot analysis. In CCH mice, there was a marked increase in TUNEL-positive cells within the hippocampus, indicating elevated levels of apoptosis. HBOT significantly reduced the proportion of TUNEL-positive cells, suggesting an anti-apoptotic effect (Fig. ). Western blot analysis further confirmed this observation, showing upregulation of pro-apoptotic markers Bax and cleaved caspase-3, along with a downregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 in CCH mice. HBOT restored these protein levels towards baseline, with reduced expression of Bax and cleaved caspase-3 and increased Bcl-2 expression (Fig. ).
 Given the established link between CCH and neuroinflammation, we next assessed microglial activation via Iba1 immunofluorescence staining. CCH led to significant microglial activation in the hippocampus, as evidenced by increased Iba1-positive area. HBOT markedly reduced Iba1-positive cells in the hippocampus, indicating a suppression of microglial activation (Fig. ). To further evaluate neuroinflammation, we performed RT-qPCR to measure mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-18. CCH significantly upregulated these cytokines, whereas HBOT normalized their expression to levels comparable to the sham group (Fig. ).
Together, these findings demonstrate that HBOT effectively alleviates CCH-induced apoptosis, reduces microglial activation, and attenuates neuroinflammation in the hippocampus, underscoring HBOT’s potential as a therapeutic intervention in vascular cognitive impairment.

## HBOT rescues CCH-induced aberrant miR-137-3p/TRAF3 pathway
To determine whether miR-137-3p is a molecular target of HBOT in CCH, we first assessed its expression levels in the hippocampus using qRT-PCR. CCH mice showed a significant downregulation of miR-137-3p compared to sham controls, while HBOT markedly restored miR-137-3p expression levels (Fig. ). Given previous studies implicating TRAF3 in neuronal apoptosis in stroke models [ ], we investigated whether TRAF3 might be a downstream target of miR-137-3p in this context. Western blot analysis confirmed that TRAF3 protein levels were significantly elevated in the hippocampus of CCH mice, whereas HBOT normalized TRAF3 expression (Fig. ).
 To further explore the interaction between miR-137-3p and TRAF3, we performed a dual-luciferase reporter assay using constructs containing either the wild-type or mutant 3’ UTR of TRAF3. Overexpression of miR-137-3p significantly reduced luciferase activity in cells transfected with the wild-type TRAF3 3’ UTR, indicating direct targeting of TRAF3 by miR-137-3p. No significant change in luciferase activity was observed in the mutant 3’ UTR group, confirming the specificity of the interaction (Fig. ).
These results demonstrate that HBOT rescues CCH-induced dysregulation of the miR-137-3p/TRAF3 pathway, suggesting a mechanism through which HBOT mitigates neuronal apoptosis and neuroinflammation in CCH.

## miR-137-3p/TRAF3 pathway mediates expression of apoptosis-related proteins in vitro
To explore the mechanistic role of the miR-137-3p/TRAF3 pathway in neuronal apoptosis, we conducted in vitro experiments to assess how modulation of miR-137-3p and TRAF3 levels affects the expression of key apoptosis-related proteins (Fig. ). Neuronal cells were transfected with a miR-137-3p mimic for 72 h, after which we evaluated the expression levels of TRAF3, Bcl-2, Bax, and cleaved caspase-3 by Western blot analysis. Overexpression of miR-137-3p resulted in a significant reduction in TRAF3 protein levels, consistent with miR-137-3p’s predicted targeting of TRAF3. Additionally, miR-137-3p overexpression led to decreased levels of the pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved caspase-3, while the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 was upregulated (Fig. ). These findings suggest that miR-137-3p exerts an anti-apoptotic effect, potentially through downregulation of TRAF3 and modulation of downstream apoptotic mediators.
To further delineate the pro-apoptotic role of TRAF3, we transfected neuronal cells with a TRAF3 overexpression plasmid (OE-TRAF3) and evaluated apoptosis-related protein expression after 72 h. As expected, TRAF3 overexpression led to a marked decrease in Bcl-2 levels, accompanied by a significant upregulation of Bax and cleaved caspase-3(Fig. ), supporting its pro-apoptotic role. This upregulation of pro-apoptotic markers in response to TRAF3 overexpression aligns with the hypothesis that TRAF3 mediates cell death signaling pathways in neuronal cells.
These data collectively indicate that miR-137-3p can suppress neuronal apoptosis by downregulating TRAF3 and reducing the expression of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved caspase-3, while concurrently enhancing Bcl-2 expression. Conversely, TRAF3 overexpression promotes apoptosis, highlighting the miR-137-3p/TRAF3 axis as a critical regulator of apoptosis in neuronal cells. This pathway may underlie the neuroprotective effects observed with HBOT treatment, wherein upregulation of miR-137-3p could mitigate CCH-induced neuronal apoptosis by targeting and modulating TRAF3 activity.

## miR-137-3p targeting TRAF3 in Tri-culture systems mediates neuroprotection through NF-κB suppression
Considering that neuroinflammation and apoptosis in VCI involve dynamic multicellular interactions, we further established primary neuron-microglia-astrocyte tri-cultures to study the effects of intercellular interactions on neuroinflammation and apoptosis(Fig. ).
miR-137-3p mimic transfection fully reversed TRAF3 overexpression-induced NF-κB hyperactivation, decreasing phosphorylated TAK1 (p-TAK1) and phosphorylated p65 (p-p65)while increasing IκBα levels (Fig. ). This intervention concomitantly attenuated TRAF3-driven elevation of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-18 (Fig. ) and apoptotic dysregulation (decreased Bax and cleaved caspase-3 expression alongside increased Bcl-2) (Fig. ).
Conversely,TRAF3 overexpression exacerbated pathological cascades, increasing p-TAK1 and p-p65 while reducing IκBα (Fig. ). This potentiated pro-inflammatory responses with elevated TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-18 expression (Fig. ) while concurrently exacerbating apoptotic cascades through upregulated Bax and cleaved caspase-3 alongside suppressed Bcl-2 (Fig. ).
In primary neuron-microglia-astrocyte tri-cultures, TRAF3 siRNA transfection significantly suppressed NF-κB signaling activation, evidenced by reduced p-TAK1 and p-p65 with concomitant IκBα elevation (Fig. ). This intervention attenuated pro-inflammatory responses through decreased TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-18 expression(Fig. ), while concurrently inhibiting apoptotic cascades via reduced Bax and cleaved caspase-3 alongside increased Bcl-2 protein levels (Fig. ).
These bidirectional manipulations establish TRAF3 suppression as essential for HBOT-mediated neuroprotection via neuron-glia crosstalk modulation in VCI.

## miR-137-3p agomir rescues CCH-induced cognitive impairment via TRAF3
Following the establishment of the CCH model, mice received hippocampal injections of a virus overexpressing TRAF3 or a control virus, followed by intraperitoneal injections of miR-137-3p agomir or a negative control (NC) agomir(Table ). Gait analysis prior to behavioral testing revealed no significant inter-group differences in stride length or maximum footprint area, suggesting that motor function was unaffected (Fig. ). The experimental design for this part is illustrated in Fig. .
 In the Y-maze test (Fig. ), spatial reference memory was assessed by measuring entries into and time spent in the novel arm. Mice treated with miR-137-3p agomir (agomir+Control group) exhibited a significant increase in entries into the novel arm and time spent in the novel arm and center compared to the NC+Control group, indicating an improvement in spatial memory (Fig. ). Conversely, TRAF3 overexpression (NC + TRAF3 group) led to a reduction in novel arm entries and time spent in the novel arm, further impairing spatial memory relative to NC+Control (Fig. ). These findings suggest that miR-137-3p agomir reverses CCH-induced spatial memory deficits, while TRAF3 overexpression exacerbates them.
The novel object recognition (NOR) test was conducted to assess recognition memory (Fig. ). In round 2, exploration times for two identical familiar objects and the discrimination index were similar across groups (Fig. ). In round 3, however, the NC+Control, agomir+Control, and agomir+TRAF3 groups all showed a preference for the novel object, whereas the NC + TRAF3 group displayed no significant preference (Fig. ). The agomir+Control group demonstrated a higher discrimination index compared to the NC+Control group, while the NC + TRAF3 group showed a lower discrimination index (Fig. ). These results indicate that miR-137-3p agomir rescues CCH-induced recognition memory deficits, and TRAF3 overexpression aggravates these deficits.
In the Morris Water Maze (MWM) test (Fig. ), we assessed spatial learning and memory. During the acquisition phase, the agomir+Control group showed a significant decrease in latency to locate the hidden platform compared to NC+Control, suggesting improved learning ability (Fig. ). In the probe trial, the agomir+Control group spent more time in the target quadrant and made more platform crossings, indicating better memory retention (Fig. ). Conversely, the NC + TRAF3 group exhibited a prolonged latency to reach the platform and a decrease in target quadrant time and platform crossings, suggesting that TRAF3 overexpression impairs spatial cognition in CCH mice.
Finally, contextual and cued memory were evaluated using the fear conditioning test (Fig. ). During the conditioning phase, freezing levels did not differ significantly between groups (Fig. ). In the contextual memory test, agomir+Control mice exhibited increased freezing behavior, while the NC + TRAF3 group showed reduced freezing, suggesting that miR-137-3p agomir ameliorates and TRAF3 overexpression exacerbates contextual memory impairment in CCH mice (Fig. ). No significant differences were observed in freezing levels during the cued test, indicating that cued memory was unaffected across groups (Fig. ).
Overall, these findings demonstrate that miR-137-3p agomir administration effectively rescues CCH-induced impairments in spatial memory, recognition memory, spatial learning, and contextual memory, while TRAF3 overexpression exacerbates these cognitive deficits, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting the miR-137-3p/TRAF3 pathway in VCI.

## miR-137-3p agomir rescues CCH-induced apoptosis and neuroinflammation via TRAF3
To investigate the effects of miR-137-3p agomir on apoptosis and neuroinflammation in CCH-induced cognitive impairment, we assessed hippocampal neuronal apoptosis and microglial activation (Fig. ). TUNEL staining revealed a decrease in the proportion of TUNEL-positive cells in the agomir+Control group, while the NC + TRAF3 group showed an increase, indicating that miR-137-3p agomir reduces apoptosis, whereas TRAF3 overexpression promotes it (Fig. ). Western blot analysis showed that agomir+Control mice had decreased expression of pro-apoptotic proteins Bax and cleaved caspase-3, along with increased expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2. Conversely, TRAF3 overexpression increased Bax and cleaved caspase-3 levels and reduced Bcl-2 expression, further supporting the role of TRAF3 in promoting apoptosis (Fig. ).
 Microglial activation was assessed through Iba1 immunofluorescence, which revealed a reduction in Iba1-positive cells in the agomir+Control group compared to NC+Control, while the NC + TRAF3 group displayed increased microglial activation (Fig. ). Additionally, RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated that the mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-18 were significantly reduced in the agomir+Control group, whereas these inflammatory markers were upregulated in the NC + TRAF3 group (Fig. ).
Overall, these findings suggest that miR-137-3p agomir alleviates CCH-induced neuronal apoptosis and neuroinflammation by downregulating TRAF3, while TRAF3 overexpression exacerbates these pathologies. Combined with the cognitive data, these results highlight the therapeutic potential of miR-137-3p in modulating TRAF3 to address both cognitive and neuropathological impairments in CCH.

## Discussion
The present study demonstrates that hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) effectively mitigates cognitive deficits in a chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH) model through upregulation of miR-137-3p, resulting in decreased neuronal apoptosis, neuroinflammation, and increased cerebral blood perfusion as well as oxygen saturation level. Our research identified TRAF3 as a crucial downstream target of miR-137-3p. Specifically, the overexpression of miR-137-3p provides neuroprotective effects. In contrast, the overexpression of TRAF3 intensifies neuroinflammatory responses and apoptosis, ultimately leading to cognitive dysfunction. Our tri-culture validation establishes TRAF3 as a central orchestrator of neuron-glia crosstalk in VCI pathogenesis. The bidirectional manipulations—where TRAF3 knockdown phenocopied HBOT-mediated suppression of neuroinflammation and apoptosis, while its overexpression exacerbated these pathologies—demonstrate that TRAF3 signaling drives NF-κB-dependent neurotoxicity. The complete rescue of TRAF3-driven pathology by miR-137-3p mimic confirms TRAF3 as the core effector node, resolving ambiguities about cell-autonomous effects.Collectively, our findings underscore the miR-137-3p/TRAF3 axis as a key mediator of HBOT’s neuroprotective effects, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for vascular cognitive impairment (VCI) resulting from CCH.
HBOT is an intervention that delivers 100% oxygen at pressures exceeding 1 ATA [ ]. HBOT can effectively increase the dissolved oxygen in plasma and arterial oxygen partial pressure, thereby correcting or alleviating hypoxia [ ].Vascular cognitive impairment pathologically affects the structure and function of cerebral microvessels [ , ]. In addition to enhancing oxygen delivery to ischemic/hypoxic tissues, HBOT also promotes angiogenesis [ ]. In animal models of low-perfusion dementia, Zhang et al. [ ] found that HBOT can improve blood supply to the piriform cortex.Long-term (three-month) HBOT intervention has been shown to mitigate cognitive deficits in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) mice,increase their blood oxygen saturation levels, reduce fluctuations in blood oxygen saturation, and improve cerebral blood flow and vascular dysfunction [ ]. Furthermore, evidence suggests that HBOT has a positive impact on neurological outcomes following cerebral ischemia [ ] and moderate brain injury [ ]. However, there is limited research on the effect of HBOT on the blood oxygen saturation of VCI mice. Therefore, our study observed the changes in blood oxygen saturation and its fluctuations in VCI mice after HBOT and in the sham treatment groups.Consistent with previous studies, our results demonstrated that HBOT significantly improved cerebral blood flow and blood oxygen saturation in VCI mice, although it did not significantly affect blood oxygen saturation fluctuations.
VCI, a major contributor to dementia worldwide, often arises from ischemic and hypoxic brain injury due to CCH. This condition triggers neuronal damage, largely driven by apoptosis, neuroinflammation, and autophagic dysfunction, leading to cognitive impairments. Consistent with previous research, our study shows that CCH-induced VCI in mice is characterized by increased neuronal apoptosis in the hippocampus, a region vital for learning and memory. Specifically, the downregulation of Bcl-2 and upregulation of Bax and cleaved caspase-3 in our CCH model reflect enhanced apoptotic signaling, which is in line with findings from models of cerebral ischemia and hypoperfusion [ , ]. Furthermore, behavioral assessments confirm significant deficits in spatial memory, object recognition, and contextual learning, underscoring the cognitive impact of CCH.In our study, HBOT reduced pro-apoptotic markers (Bax and cleaved caspase-3),increased anti-apoptotic Bcl-2,and normalized miR-137-3p and TRAF3 expression in the hippocampus. These molecular changes corresponded with a reduction in TUNEL-positive cells and improved performance in cognitive tasks, confirming the neuroprotective role of HBOT in CCH-induced VCI.While the current study quantified general cellular apoptosis via TUNEL, future work will employ NeuN/TUNEL co-labeling to specifically identify apoptotic neurons—further strengthening cellular resolution.
Our findings extend previous research on neuroinflammation as a central contributor to VCI pathogenesis. Neuroinflammation, driven by activated microglia and elevated cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-18, and TNF-α, exacerbates neuronal loss and white matter damage, particularly in the hippocampus [ , ]. Several studies have proposed that HBOT may mitigate neuroinflammatory processes by reducing the activation of astrocytes and microglia [ , ]. In a traumatic brain injury (TBI) rat model, HBOT has been demonstrated to alleviate neuroinflammation and increase IL-10 levels, with these alterations correlating with improvements in cognitive deficits [ ]. HBOT not only reverses hypoxia but also ameliorates brain pathology and enhances behavioral performance in mice, and this enhancement is associated with a decrease in pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [ ]. Consistent with those studies, our results show that long-term HBOT or miR-137-3p agomir administration in our model significantly reduced neuroinflammation, as evidenced by decreased microglial activation and lower cytokine levels. These findings align with studies demonstrating that targeting inflammatory pathways can ameliorate CCH-induced cognitive deficits.
Previous studies showed that TRAF3 could stimulate TAK1 phosphorylation in ischemic stroke and hepatic ischemic injury [ , ]. In the current study, we demonstrated that TRAF3 suppression mitigates neuroinflammation and apoptosis through coordinated inhibition of the NF-κB signaling cascade. Downregulation of TRAF3 significantly reduced phosphorylation of TAK1 and p65 while restoring IκBα expression, thereby attenuating NF-κB nuclear translocation. This signaling modulation concomitantly suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokine production (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-18) and rectified apoptotic dysregulation, evidenced by diminished Bax/cleaved caspase-3 expression with concomitant Bcl-2 elevation,suggesting that it has a pivotal role in the regulation of TAK1 activity.TRAF3 serves dual pivotal roles in cerebral ischemic injury as both an inflammatory driver and apoptosis inducer by driving NF-κB pathway activation via ubiquitination-dependent TAK1 phosphorylation [ , ]. Blockade of this axis may represent a novel therapeutic strategy for ischemic disorders.
Notably, an apparent inconsistency emerged in our data: while HBOT partially rescued CCH-induced deficits, miR-137-3p agomir appeared to normalize select metrics to sham levels. This observation was resolved through two key insights: first, comprehensive statistical analyses confirmed that agomir-mediated effects did not surpass physiological baselines across core endpoints—including spatial memory consolidation (time in Novel Arm(%):p = 0.31,entries to novel arm(%):p = 0.39,MWM target quadrant time: p = 0.2995), recognition memory(NOR discrimination index of round 3:p = 0.08),associative fear memory (FCT freezing: p = 0.65–0.798), and microglial activation (Iba1+ area: p = 0.46). Critically, agomir even elevated hippocampal apoptosis above sham levels (TUNEL+cells: 9.33% vs. 3.83%, p = 0.008), underscoring its failure to exceed homeostatic recovery. Second, the observed superior rescue in select phenotypes (e.g., MWM platform crossings:P = 0.02) stems from pharmacological advantages of synthetic agonism: supraphysiological miR-137-3p delivery bypasses transcriptional regulation, achieving instantaneous, maximal suppression of the TRAF3/NF-κB axis—unlike HBOT’s polypharmacological, cumulative modulation. This mechanistic distinction does not invalidate HBOT’s dependency on miR-137-3p/TRAF3 (validated by TRAF3 overexpression abolishing HBOT efficacy), but rather highlights therapeutic context-dependency: agomir excels in targeted pathway rescue, while HBOT offers broader hemodynamic and metabolic stabilization critical for clinical translation.
The miR-137-3p/TRAF3 pathway has previously been implicated in the regulation of apoptosis across various neurological models. In our study, the CCH-induced decrease in miR-137-3p and subsequent upregulation of TRAF3 were associated with increased neuronal apoptosis and neuroinflammation, suggesting a detrimental role of TRAF3 in CCH. Our findings are consistent with reports of TRAF3-mediated apoptosis in retinal ganglion cells, spinal cord injury, and subarachnoid hemorrhage models, where TRAF3 was shown to promote apoptosis through MAPK and NF-κB pathways [ , ]. In our CCH model, miR-137-3p agomir application alleviated both apoptosis and neuroinflammation,while TRAF3 overexpression further worsened these effects, underscoring the regulatory role of miR-137-3p/TRAF3 in CCH pathology.

## Conclusions
Our findings suggest that long-term HBOT can alleviate CCH-induced cognitive decline, apoptosis, and neuroinflammation by modulating the miR-137-3p/TRAF3/NF-κB pathway. And HBOT can also improve cerebral blood flow and blood oxygen saturation in mice with VCI.This study highlights the therapeutic potential of the miR-137-3p/TRAF3 axis in VCI, providing a novel mechanistic insight into how HBOT exerts neuroprotective effects. By delving into these mechanisms, researchers can identify specific pathways and processes associated with the positive outcomes of HBOT. It is noteworthy that the impact of HBOT on VCI is probably not mediated by a single pathway,but rather involve multiple interconnected mechanisms.Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of these mechanisms is crucial for developing targeted and effective HBOT interventions for VCI.Further research, including clinical trials, is warranted to evaluate the efficacy and safety of targeting the miR-137-3p/TRAF3/NF-κB pathway in human VCI patients.
