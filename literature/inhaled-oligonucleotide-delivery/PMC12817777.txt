## Abstract

## Background
Loss of isozyme diversity (LID) refers to the selective dependency on a single isozyme following the functional collapse of its redundant counterparts, uncovering a metabolic vulnerability. This metabolic liability establishes LID as a novel framework for precision targeting strategy in cancer therapy.

## Methods
We integrated a large-scale breast cancer multi-omics cohort to systematically delineate isozyme expression patterns and identify therapeutically promising LID targets across intrinsic molecular subtypes. Genomic and epigenetic data were employed to decipher the underlying causes of LID. Subsequent experiments were conducted to validate the collateral metabolic vulnerabilities arising from identified LID targets and investigate the associated mechanisms.

## Results
Our analysis delineated the isozyme utilization patterns for metabolic reactions in breast cancer, which revealed widespread and significant LID phenomena. We further decoded the enrichment characteristics of these LID reactions and identified a set of potential clinically relevant LID targets across different subtypes. Further investigation revealed that copy number loss and epigenetic silencing predominantly drive these LIDs, as exemplified by luminal subtype-specific N-myristoyltransferase 2 (NMT2) deficiency caused by hypermethylation. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that NMT2 deficiency confers synthetic lethality with NMT1 inhibition, potentially through impaired myristoylation of the oncogenic effector FMR1 autosomal homolog 2 (FXR2). These findings underscored the potential of NMT inhibitors as viable therapeutic strategies for NMT2-deficient tumors.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, this study constructed a comprehensive LID landscape across all breast cancer subtypes, advancing precision oncology through innovative metabolic targeting approaches.

## Background
Metabolic reprogramming refers to the adaptation of metabolic processes to support cellular survival and proliferation, which represents a hallmark of tumors [ ]. Tumor cells critically depend on such adaptations to fuel their high-proliferative demands, rendering targeted disruption of these pathways a promising strategy to exploit metabolic vulnerabilities and suppress malignant growth [ ]. Despite this therapeutic rationale, current metabolism-targeted agents face substantial hurdles in clinical translation [ ], including on-target toxicity to normal tissues and tumor-intrinsic metabolic plasticity that rapidly bypasses therapeutic inhibition [ – ]. Consequently, advancing next-generation strategies requires prioritizing agents that selectively leverage tumor-specific metabolic dependencies while circumventing compensatory mechanisms.
Isozymes are proteins encoded by divergent genes that catalyze identical enzymatic reactions but typically exhibit heterogeneous subcellular localization and kinetic properties. Isozyme expression patterns demonstrate tissue specificity, reflecting unique metabolic demands and regulatory constraints. Crucially, isozyme diversity underpins metabolic robustness and flexibility by enabling functional redundancy [ , ]. In neoplastic contexts, however, tumorigenic genetic alterations may cause gene loss, resulting in the deficiency of specific metabolic enzymes and loss of isozyme diversity (LID), with only certain dominant isozymes expressed in tumors [ , ]. When LID occurs in essential metabolic reactions, selective targeting of the dominant isozyme could effectively block the corresponding metabolic reaction, thereby achieving tumor suppression [ , ]. For instance, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells exhibit significantly elevated expression of HK2 while suppressing GCK, suggesting that silencing HK2 may effectively inhibit HCC progression [ ]. Since normal cells have different isozyme profiles from those of tumor cells, targeting LID could minimize toxicity to normal cells. Additionally, given that LID mostly arises from irreversible genomic alterations, targeting these genetically enforced vulnerabilities may circumvent adaptive resistance mechanisms common to conventional metabolism-targeted therapies.
In this study, we aimed to investigate novel therapeutic strategies targeting tumor metabolism by thoroughly depicting the expression patterns of isozymes and unveiling the LID targets in breast cancer. Leveraging a large-scale breast cancer multi-omics cohort with curated isozyme gene sets, we delineated expression patterns of all isozymes involved in each enzymatic reaction and identified LID targets across breast cancer intrinsic subtypes (PAM50 subtypes), followed by multi-layered investigation of their upstream genetic and epigenetic origination mechanisms. Subsequently, we conducted an in-depth investigation into the luminal subtype-specific N-myristoyltransferase 2 (NMT2) deficiency. We validated that targeting NMT1 could induce synthetic lethality in NMT2-deficient tumors, likely by inhibiting the myristoylation of downstream FMR1 autosomal homolog 2 (FXR2). Furthermore, this study indicated that NMT inhibitors served as an effective therapeutic strategy for NMT2-deficient tumors.

## Methods

## Study cohorts
This study integrated three retrospective clinical cohorts: the Chinese Breast Cancer Genome Atlas (CBCGA) cohort (n = 773)[ ], the Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center (FUSCC) TNBC cohort (n = 465)[ ], and the HR+/HER2- breast cancer cohort (n = 579)[ ]. All three cohorts had complete clinical and pathological information and multi-omics data, including whole-exome sequencing (WES), copy number variation (CNV), RNA sequencing, tandem mass tag-based mass spectrometry quantified proteomics data. Comprehensive details regarding these cohorts can be found in their originally published manuscripts. For this study, patients with RNA sequencing data from all three cohorts were selected, resulting in a cohort of 1,111 patients who received treatment in the Breast Surgery Department of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center from July 2007 to December 2014, referred to as the FUSCC cohort. All patient samples used in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center, and each patient signed an informed consent.

## Multi-omics data integration for the FUSCC cohort
The multi-omics data of three cohort samples were sequenced in two batches. To enable unified analysis in this study, the omics data of these patients were integrated respectively. The RNA sequencing data of the two batches included 360 cases in the first batch and 853 cases in the second batch, among which 102 cases had sequencing data in both batches. Therefore, the RNA sequencing data of these 102 samples were used as a reference, and the “ComBat” function in the R software package “sva” was used to correct the RNA sequencing data of the first batch to be consistent with those of the second batch, removing the batch effect [ ].
The CNV data of the two batches were generated by the OncoScan CNV detection kit and processed by Chromosome Analysis Suite (ChAS) v4.1 software and allele-specific copy number analysis of tumors (ASCAT) [ ], and the log2 ratio of the copy number at the segment level and total copy number (nAraw + nBraw, zero value set to 0.05) was obtained. The corrected CNV data at the segment level of the two batches were combined for GISTIC2.0 (version 2.0.22) [ ] analysis, and the following parameters were used: -ta 0.2 -td 0.2 -genegistic 1 -smallmem 1 -broad 1 -conf 0.95 -rx 0 -brlen 0.7 -cap 3.5 -armpeel 1 -js 100. Germline variations and potential false positive variations were excluded on the basis of the CNV-corrected values of 23 patients’ normal tissues.
For the PAM50 classification and WES data, this study directly integrated the previously reported PAM50 classification results for each patient and the somatic mutation binary matrix. Due to the difficulty in integrating proteomics data, only the protein data of 308 patients from the same batch of the CBCGA cohort and HR+/HER2- cohort were selected.
Ultimately, we integrated a unified dataset of 1,111 samples with complete RNA-seq data, along with WES data for 900 patients, CNV data for 982 patients, and quantitative proteomics data for 308 patients.

## Isozyme database
The ENZYME nomenclature database provides the naming, classification and functional information of known enzymes worldwide [ ]. In this database, each Enzyme Commission (EC) number corresponds to an enzymatic reaction and may correspond to multiple enzymes. Enzymes with the same EC number are considered isozymes. In this study, all human metabolic enzymes, including 3,553 metabolic enzymes from 1,489 enzymatic reactions, were extracted from this database.
To better analyze the expression characteristics of metabolic enzymes, we further screened the metabolic enzymes covered by the RNA sequencing data of FUSCC cohort, as well as the metabolic enzymes corresponding to enzymatic reactions with Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database annotations. Finally, 1,955 metabolic enzymes catalyzing 1,142 enzymatic reactions were selected. This construction process is illustrated in Fig. B.
 

## External breast cancer cohorts and cell line database
To verify the LID targets identified from our cohort, we additionally incorporated data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohort [ ], including WES, CNV, RNA sequencing, and DNA methylation sequencing data. In addition, proteomics data from the Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) cohort [ ] and RNA sequencing data from the I-SPY2 cohort [ ] and the Sweden Cancerome Analysis Network-Breast (SCAN-B) cohort [ ] were also used for external validation. To validate the upstream mechanisms of the LID targets, we utilized RNA-seq (GSE209998) and DNA methylation (GSE212370) data from the AURORA study [ ]. We selected paired RNA and methylation data from primary tumor samples in this cohort for analysis, with RNA-seq upper quartile-normalized gene counts log2-transformed.
We also utilized data from breast cancer cell lines to verify the metabolic vulnerability of LID targets. We incorporated RNA sequencing data from 62 cell lines from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) [ ] and gene dependency scores from 47 cell lines obtained via a clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-Cas9 protein system-based genome-wide screening from the Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap) [ ]. A negative dependency score indicates that knocking out the gene inhibits cell viability, with more negative values signifying greater cellular dependence on the gene for survival.

## Definitions of LID targets and screening process
We developed three-step criteria to screen LID targets. First, we selected metabolic reactions where only one isozyme was not downregulated in tumors compared with normal tissues. Second, expression thresholds were applied to identify targets with significantly low expression of the lost isozyme and high expression of the target isozyme (Fig. b). Finally, using gene dependency score, we verified that inhibiting the target isozyme effectively suppressed cell growth in breast cancer cell lines, outperforming the inhibition of the lost isozyme. Detailed analytical methods and thresholds are described below.
Preliminary identification of LID targets.
The Wilcoxon test was used to compare the expression changes of all isozymes in one metabolic reaction between tumor and paired normal tissues. Isozymes with a log2 fold change (FC) less than 0 and an FDR less than 0.05 were considered significanlyt downregulated isozymes. A metabolic reaction in which only one isozyme was not downregulated was screened out as a potential targetable LID reaction.

cThe expression of target isozymes in tumors is not lower than that in normal tissues: Log2FC (S3) greater than − 0.5.
dThe difference in expression between the target isozyme and all the lost isozymes in tumors was greater than that in normal tissues: Log2FC (S4) greater than 0.7.

bKnocking out the target isozyme has a stronger inhibitory effect on cells than does knocking out all the lost isozymes: the Log2FC values of dependency scores are all less than 0, and the FDR is less than 0.05.

 

## Recognition of patients with tumors manifesting specific LID
For all patients in the FUSCC cohort and the TCGA cohort, we defined them whether manifesting the identified LID. We utilized the RNA sequencing data and calculated Log2(FPKM + 1) as the absolute expression level of the gene. For each identified LID target, the tumor with the expression level of all lost isozymes less than 1 and the expression difference between the target isozyme and all lost isozymes greater than 1 was considered manifesting LID.

## DNA methylation analysis
The DNA methylation data obtained from TCGA were subjected to quality control and standardization using the R package “ChAMP"[ ]. Differentially methylated sites between tumor and normal tissues were subsequently identified. Differentially methylated sites located within promoters were specifically selected. For genes harboring these differentially methylated sites, all the methylation sites within their promoters were integrated. The methylation Δβ value for each gene in tumors compared with normal tissues was defined as the mean of the Δβ values across all CpG sites. Fisher’s method was employed to combine the P values from individual site difference tests, yielding a composite statistical test P value for each gene [ ]. Genes with an absolute Δβ value exceeding 0.1 and a P value less than 0.05 were defined as significantly differentially methylated genes.

## Cell lines
The human breast cancer cell lines MCF7, T47D, Hs578T, BT-549, SUM159PT, MDA-MB-453, MDA-MB-231 and CAL-148, as well as HEK293T embryonic kidney cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). MCF7, Hs578T, MDA-MB-453, MDA-MB-231 and HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). T47D, BT-549 and SUM159PT cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS. CAL-148 cells were cultured in DMEM with 20% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine and 0.01 mg/L human EGF. All the cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling and mycoplasma contamination tests with a Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Yeason, cat. no. 40612ES). All the cells were maintained at 37℃ in a 5% CO2 incubator.

## DNA constructs, transfection and virus infection
Single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting human NMT2 (sgNMT2) were designed and inserted into the pLent-U6-spgRNA-EFFS-spCas9-P2A-Blasticidin vector. Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting human NMT1 (shNMT1), human INPP4A (shINPP4A), and human FXR2 (shFXR2) were designed and inserted into the pLent-U6-shRNA-CMV-copGFP-P2A-puro vector. Plasmids expressing Flag-tagged human NMT1 and Flag-tagged human NMT2 were constructed using the pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1-Puro vector. Plasmids expressing human wild-type or G2A-mutant FXR2 were constructed using the pLVX-IRES-Neo vector. All the above plasmids and the NMT1, ACACA, HMGCS1 small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were purchased from YiXueSheng Biosciences Inc. The specific sequences of the sgRNAs, shRNAs and siRNAs are provided in Additional file 1: Table S10.
To transiently transfect siRNAs, the cells were grown to 60% confluency and then transfected with control siRNA or with NMT1, ACACA or HMGCS1 siRNAs, utilizing Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 13778150). The evaluation of the knockdown efficiency was conducted 48–72 hours post-transfection.
HEK293T cells were transfected with each lentiviral vector and packaging plasmid mixture to generate lentiviruses using Lipofectamine 2000 DNA transfection reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. 11668019). The supernatant containing virus was collected 48 h after transfection, filtered by a 0.45 μm syringe filter, and used for infecting target cells in the presence of 10 mg/ml of polybrene (Beyotime, cat. no. C0351). Cells infected with sgNMT2 were selected with 10 µg/ml of blasticidin (Selleck, cat. no. S7419) for two weeks. Cells infected with shNMT1, shINPP4A or shFXR2 were selected with 2 µg/ml of puromycin (Selleck, cat. no. S7417) for one week. The cells infected with the overexpression plasmids were selected with 500 µg/ml of G418 (Selleck, cat. no. S3028) for two weeks.

## Cell proliferation and colony formation assays
A Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (Selleck, cat. no. B34302) was utilized to assess cell proliferation. For the cell viability assays, the cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 2000 ~ 3000 cells per well and incubated with CCK-8 reagent at the indicated time points for 1.5 ~ 2 h before detection of the absorbance at 450 nm (OD450).
For colony formation assays, 1000 cells were plated in a 12-well plate and cultured with frequently renewed culture medium for 2 weeks. The cell colonies were fixed in methanol and stained with 0.5% crystal violet. The counting of colonies was performed with the assistance of ImageJ software.

## Immunoprecipitation (IP)
For the IP assays, NP-40 buffer was used to lyse the cultured cells. Then, the protein extracts were incubated with the indicated antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with protein A/G magnetic beads (Epizyme, cat. no. YJ003) for 5 h, or the protein extracts were immunoprecipitated using anti-Flag magnetic beads (Epizyme, cat. no. YJ007) for 5 h. The immunoprecipitates were washed with NP-40 buffer for three times and then subjected to SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. SDS gels were stained by Coomassie blue, and then subjected to liquid chromatography‒mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) at Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. For co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays, immunoprecipitates were subjected to immunoblotting and specific primary antibodies were utilized to validate the presence of the indicated protein.

## Detection of FXR2 myristoylation by click chemistry
Protein myristoylation was determined by click chemistry. Initially, the cells were cultured with Click-IT® Myristic Acid Azide (50 µM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. C10268) for 5 h. After that, protein lysates were collected and employed to click reaction with Biotin-alkyne (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. B10185) using Click-iT® Protein Reaction Buffer Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat. no. C10276). Next, the biotin-labeled proteins were pulled down with streptavidin magnetic beads (Epizyme, cat. no. YJ011). The immunoprecipitated samples were subjected to immunoblotting to detect the indicated proteins.

## Drug sensitivity assays
For drug sensitivity assays, cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 8,000 cells per well. After 48 h, PCLX-001 (Selleck, cat. no. E1217), tamoxifen (Selleck, cat. no. S1238), and ribociclib (Selleck, cat. no. S7440) were introduced to the plates. After 72 h of drug treatment, cell viability was detected using the CCK-8 kit in the same way. The half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was calculated using four-parameter logistic curve fitting. The zero interaction potency (ZIP) synergy score was evaluated by SynergyFinder+ (https://synergyfinder.org)[ ].

## RNA isolation and qRT-PCR
Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and utilized to synthesize cDNA with RT SuperMix (Vazyme, cat. no. R323). The primers used were designed via the PrimerBank website (https://pga.mgh.harvard.edu/primerbank). Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis was conducted with SYBR qPCR MasterMix (Vazyme, cat. no. Q711) and primers using ABI 7900HT Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. ACTB was used as the internal control. The primer sequences are listed in Additional file 1: Table S10.

## Patient-derived organoid (PDO) studies
For experimental validation, we established PDOs from an independent set of specimens collected in 2023 from FUSCC breast cancer surgery patients. These samples were pathologically confirmed as invasive breast cancer samples, were confirmed via immunohistochemistry (IHC) to be HR positive, and had sufficient tissue for organoid generation. All the specimens were acquired after informed consent was obtained with a form approved by FUSCC’s Ethics Committee. The organoids were cultured as described previously [ ]. Briefly, fresh breast cancer tissues were minced into small fragments and digested at 37°C for 2 hours with shaking. The cell clusters were filtered through a 100 µm strainer and centrifuged. The cell clusters were resuspended in basement membrane extract and seeded in a prewarmed 24-well cell culture plate. After gelation was complete, 500 µl of breast cancer organoid medium was added to each well, and the PDOs were cultured at 37°C in 5% CO2 incubators. To assess the viability, the organoids were plated to 384-well plates. After 48 h, PCLX-001 of different concentrations was added into the plate and continued incubation for 7 days. The viability was detected with 3D Cell Viability Assay (Promega, cat. no. G9681). Representative images of the organoids were photographed.

## Histology and immunohistochemistry (IHC)
The tumor tissues were fixed with formalin and embedded in paraffin for sectioning. Histology and IHC staining were performed by Wuhan Baiqiandu Biological Technology Co., Ltd. Anti-NMT2 (1:100, Proteintech, cat. no. 15720-1-AP) was used for incubation overnight.

## Animal studies
The animal trials were carried out at the Shanghai Laboratory Animal Center, within a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment. All animal experiments were performed according to protocols approved by the Research Ethical Committee of Fudan University Shanghai Cancer Center, and the in vivo experimental protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (FUSCC-IACUC-2024586). One week before cell injection, 17β-estradiol sustained-release tablets were implanted on the backs of 5-week-old female BALB/c nude mice. Subsequently, 5 × 106 MCF7 breast cancer cells (with or without NMT2 knockout) were injected subcutaneously into the mammary fat pad region of the mice. The mice were divided into 4 groups, each containing 5 mice: (1) injected with MCF7 sgNC cells and treated with vehicle, (2) injected with MCF7 sgNC cells and treated with PCLX-001, (3) injected with MCF7 sgNMT2 cells and treated with vehicle, and (4) injected with MCF7 sgNMT2 cells and treated with PCLX-001. When the tumor volume reached 50 mm3, the mice from the treated group were treated with PCLX-001 (50 mg/kg in sodium carboxymethyl cellulose) daily for 10 days via oral gavage. Tumor volume was measured using digital calipers, and calculated according to the formula: 0.5×length×width2. The mice were humanely euthanized via CO₂ inhalation, followed by tumor excision, weighing, and photography.

## Statistical analysis
Bioinformatics and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0) or R software (version 4.4.2). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test the normality of continuous variables. If the data were normally distributed, Student’s t test and one-way ANOVA were used for statistical tests between two groups and multiple groups, respectively. If the data were not normally distributed, the Wilcoxon test and Kruskal-Wallis test were used for statistical tests between two groups and multiple groups, respectively. Unless otherwise specified, the Pearson correlation coefficient and Spearman correlation coefficient were used to test the correlation of normally distributed and non-normally distributed data, respectively. Pearson’s chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used for statistical tests of unordered categorical variables. All statistical tests were two-sided, and the Benjamini-Hochberg method was used to correct P values in multiple comparisons. Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and tested by the log-rank test. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Study cohorts and isozyme database
To investigate the isozyme expression profiles of breast cancer and identify subtype-specific targets based on the intrinsic PAM50 subtypes, we established a new FUSCC cohort by integrating all patients with RNA sequencing data from three previously reported multi-omics cohorts to expand the sample scale and balance subtype representation [ – ]. The final FUSCC cohort comprises 1,111 Chinese female breast cancer patients treated at our center. All patients had clinical and pathological information along with RNA sequencing data, 900 patients had WES data, 982 patients had CNV data, and 308 patients had quantitative proteomics data (Fig. a and Additional file 1: Table S1).
Next, we curated a human metabolic enzyme dataset from the ENZYME nomenclature database, incorporating all isozymes associated with each enzymatic reaction [ ]. Then, we selected the enzymatic reactions that were annotated in the KEGG REACTION database and ensured that RNA sequencing data were available in our cohort for all isozymes involved in these reactions. Ultimately, 1,955 enzymes catalyzing 1,142 enzymatic reactions were included in the subsequent analysis (Fig. b and Additional file 1: Table S2). Among these reactions, 705 enzymatic reactions are catalyzed by a single enzyme. Among reactions catalyzed by multiple isozymes, those catalyzed by two isozymes predominate, with 23 reactions involving over 10 isozymes (Fig. c). These metabolic enzymes can be categorized into seven classes based on their catalytic functions (Fig. d). Among these, transferase reactions are primarily mediated by multiple isozymes, whereas lyase reactions are largely mediated by a single isozyme (Additional file 2: Fig. S1a). Moreover, reactions in the fatty acid biosynthesis pathway are mostly mediated by only one or two isozymes, whereas processes such as fatty acid elongation and degradation, as well as glycerophospholipid and sphingolipid metabolism, are primarily catalyzed by multiple isozymes (Additional file 2: Fig. S1b).
Initially, we analyzed the expression of all these metabolic enzymes in breast cancer. By comparing both the RNA and protein expression levels between tumor and normal tissues, we observed that, except for the normal-like subtype, all other subtypes of breast cancer exhibited greater numbers of downregulated metabolic enzymes (Fig. e, f and Additional file 2: Fig. S1c). The t-SNE analysis also revealed that the most pronounced disparities in metabolic enzyme profiles existed between whole breast cancer and normal tissues. Additionally, each subtype displayed distinct expression patterns, with the luminal A and luminal B subtypes sharing comparable characteristics (Fig.  g and Additional file 2: Fig. S1c). The above conclusions were also validated in the TCGA cohort (Additional file 2: Fig. S1d). Metabolic enzymes exhibited concordant overall patterns at the RNA and protein levels, with high consistency in expression trajectories (Fig.  h, i). This robust coherence validates that RNA-based quantification reflects enzyme levels, thereby substantiating the use of RNA sequencing data for subsequent analyses. Given the uncharacterized widespread metabolic enzyme downregulation and subtype-specific patterns in breast cancer, we next investigated their biological significance and potential therapeutic value.

## The change patterns of isozyme expression in breast cancer
Considering that many metabolic reactions are catalyzed by multiple isozymes, the downregulation of enzymes in tumors may lead to LID, forcing tumors to rely on the dominant isozymes to sustain these reactions. We therefore systematically analyzed the changes in the expression of all isozymes involved in each metabolic reaction in breast cancers compared with normal tissues. Six distinct change patterns of isozyme expression were distinguished: all isozymes upregulated (All Up), selective partial isozymes upregulated (Partial Up), all isozymes unchanged (No Change), selective partial isozymes downregulated (Partial Down), isozymes dual-directional regulated (Bidirectional), and all isozymes downregulated (All Down) (Fig. a). Results also revealed a predominance of downregulated metabolic reactions compared with upregulated reactions in breast cancer (Fig. b and Additional file 2: Fig. S2a). Notably, reactions catalyzed by multiple isozymes were significantly enriched for partial downregulation and dual-directional regulation (Fig. c and Additional file 2: Fig. S2b). These findings suggest that tumors may sustain metabolic flux by relying on dominant isozymes that are either retained or selectively upregulated within these reactions. Therefore, we considered these reactions as LID reactions (Fig. a). In terms of the distribution patterns, LID reactions were enriched predominantly in hydrolases and lipid metabolic processes, such as glycerophospholipid metabolism (Fig. d, e and Additional file 2: Fig. S2c).
 Furthermore, we examined the distribution of LID reactions across PAM50 subtypes. The basal-like subtype showed the most LID reactions, aligning with its extensive metabolic alterations (Fig. f, g). Although the HER2-enriched subtype exhibited the next highest degree of metabolic alterations, these alterations were characterized by more upregulated metabolic reactions, resulting in fewer LID reactions than in the luminal B subtype (Fig. f, g). Most LID reactions were shared across subtypes, with few being subtype-specific (Fig.  h). Consequently, the distributions of the enzymatic categories and metabolic pathways involved in LID reactions were generally consistent across the subtypes (Fig. i and Additional file 2: Fig. S2d). However, luminal subtypes contained a higher proportion of transferases, whereas the basal-like subtype featured more hydrolases (Fig. i). Additionally, the basal-like subtype displayed more LID reactions within nucleotide metabolism pathways (Additional file 2: Fig. S2d).
These findings corroborated widespread LID affecting numerous critical metabolic processes in tumors, suggesting that its targeted inhibition could disrupt metabolic hubs, suppress proliferation, and offer new therapeutic opportunities.

## Identification of LID targets across intrinsic subtypes of breast cancer
To prioritize LID reactions with therapeutic relevance in breast cancer, we implemented a multi-step screening-validation pipeline (Fig. a). We selected metabolic reactions catalyzed by at least two isozymes for analysis. First, we used RNA sequencing data to identify metabolic reactions where tumors retained the expression of a single non-downregulated isozyme (designated the target isozyme) while other isozymes (termed lost isozymes) exhibited suppressed expression compared with normal tissues. Within these reactions, tumors became exclusively reliant on the target isozyme to sustain catalytic activity, rendering it a mechanistically rational therapeutic target. Inhibition of the target isozyme could effectively block the corresponding enzymatic reaction and disrupt tumor metabolism. In this study, we defined the target-lost isozyme pairs underlying these reactions as LID targets. To refine clinically actionable candidates, we set expression threshold criteria for the isozymes to select metabolic reactions with a significant LID phenotype, as follows: (1) the expression of lost isozymes is significantly lower in tumors than in normal tissues; (2) the expression of target isozymes is significantly higher than lost isozymes; and (3) the expression difference between the target isozyme and lost isozymes is significantly greater in tumors than in normal tissues (Fig. b, c).
We conducted the above analyses across the entire breast cancer cohort and within each PAM50 subtype, and the same independent analysis was repeated in the TCGA cohort (Additional file 2: Fig. S3a). We filtered LID targets that met the expression threshold criteria in both the FUSCC cohort and the TCGA cohort, thereby identifying subtype-shared or subtype-specific LID targets in breast cancer (Additional file 2: Fig. S3b and Additional file 1: Tables S3-S8). Next, we validated the expression of the filtered LID targets in the breast cancer cell lines and assessed the dependency of the cell lines on the targets in the corresponding subtype cell lines (Fig.  d, e). LID targets (target isozyme/lost isozyme) fulfilling the vulnerability screening criteria were prioritized as shown in Fig. S3c. We finally identified six shared LID targets across all breast cancer subtypes, exemplified by 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 1/2 (HMGCS1/HMGCS2; a key enzyme in the mevalonate pathway) and acetyl-CoA carboxylase alpha/beta (ACACA/ACACB; the rate-limiting enzyme in lipogenesis). Subtype-specific LID targets included three luminal subtype-specific LID targets (e.g., N-myristoyltransferase 1/2 [NMT1/NMT2] and fatty acid metabolism regulators), HER2-enriched subtype-specific malic enzyme 1/3 (ME1/ME3), and basal-like subtype-specific inositol polyphosphate-4-phosphatase type IA/IIB (INPP4A/INPP4B). Then, we validated the LID phenotype at the protein level and confirmed our findings in several external breast cancer cohorts that the expression of target isozymes was significantly elevated compared with lost isozymes in tumors (Additional file 2: Fig. S3d-g).
Finally, we determined whether individual tumors manifested the aforementioned LID phenotypes. The results validated that the subtype distributions of patients with specific LIDs corresponded to their respective subtype specificities. For instance, NMT2-deficient tumors were significantly enriched in the luminal subtype patients, while INPP4B-deficient tumors were significantly enriched in the basal-like subtype patients (Fig. f). Comparing metabolic differences between LID and non-LID tumors, we found that most LIDs had minimal effects on pathway activity, indicating that functional redundancy among isozymes preserves metabolic homeostasis (Additional file 2: Fig. S3h). Some LIDs were compensated by upregulation of alternative target isozymes, further reinforcing the therapeutic potential of targeting LID (Additional file 2: Fig. S3i). Only ACACB and SCD depletion resulted in additional compensatory alterations, such as enhanced citrate availability (IDH and SLC25A1) and the upregulation of fatty acid desaturase (FADS2) (Additional file 2: Fig. S3j-l). Collectively, these comprehensively validated LID targets represent promising therapeutic vulnerabilities for precision oncology.

## Genetic and epigenetic drivers of LID
To investigate how these LID targets arise in breast cancer, we conducted genomic and epigenetic analyses to elucidate drivers of the downregulation of the lost isozymes in the identified LID targets, including somatic mutations, CNVs, and DNA methylation. Somatic mutation analysis revealed no recurrent high-frequency mutations in the lost isozymes across the FUSCC and TCGA cohorts (Additional file 2: Fig. S4a). Next, we evaluated the contribution of copy number loss to LID formation. We performed genome-wide CNV analysis across three multi-omics cohorts using GISTIC 2.0. The lost isozymes of pan-breast cancer LID targets were mostly localized near significant GISTIC loss/deletion peaks, exhibiting high loss frequencies (Fig. a and Additional file 1: Table S9). Notably, HMGCS2, GYG2, KL and the basal-like subtype-specific INPP4B showed significantly higher loss frequencies compared with their paired target isozymes (Fig. b and Additional file 2: Fig. S4b). Moreover, INPP4B loss demonstrated significant CNV-mRNA and CNV-protein cis-effects in basal-like tumors, suggesting the basal-like subtype-specific CNV-driven INPP4B deficiency (Fig. c and Additional file 2: Fig. S4c, d).
 However, copy number loss alone could not fully explain all instances of LID, prompting an investigation into DNA hypermethylation as an alternative mechanism. Utilizing DNA methylation data from the TCGA cohort, we identified hypermethylated genes in breast cancer, including ALDH1L2, KL, luminal subtype-specific NMT2 and SCD5, as well as HER2-enriched subtype-specific ME3 (Fig. d). Hypermethylation of ME3, NMT2 and KL was significantly inversely correlated with mRNA suppression, implicating that these LID targets might result from epigenetic silencing (Fig. e). Additionally, NMT2 hypermethylation occurred specifically at a promoter CpG site in luminal subtypes (Fig. f and Additional file 2: Fig. S4e), with higher methylation in NMT2-deficient tumors (Fig. g) and a negative correlation with NMT2 expression (Additional file 2: Fig. S4f), supporting luminal subtype-specific NMT2 silencing. Subsequently, we observed that NMT2-deficient tumors exhibited elevated levels of estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1), and both NMT2 hypermethylation and low expression were significantly correlated with ESR1, indicating luminal lineage specificity [ , ] (Fig. h and Additional file 2: Fig. S4g, h). We then investigated the specific DNA methyltransferases or demethylases mediating NMT2 hypermethylation [ , ]. Downregulation of ten-eleven translocation 1 (TET1) and TET3 was observed in NMT2-deficient tumors (Additional file 2: Fig. S4h), with low TET1/3 expression correlating inversely with NMT2 methylation and positively with NMT2 expression (Fig. h and Additional file 2: Fig. S4g). Notably, TET1/3 suppression itself was also associated with the luminal lineage and ESR1 expression, suggesting that lineage-specific TET1/3 loss promotes NMT2 hypermethylation and silencing (Fig. h and Additional file 2: Fig. S4i, j). Data from the AURORA cohort also validated this conclusion (Additional file 2: Fig. S4k-m).
In conclusion, the occurrence of LID in tumors may be mechanistically rooted in tumorigenic genomic deletions and promoter hypermethylation events.

## LID confers synthetic lethality to inhibition of the target isozyme
To functionally assess the therapeutic value, we validated the identified LID targets through in vitro experimentation. For each LID target, we comprehensively evaluated its innovation, subtype specificity, expression profile, metabolic vulnerability, upstream mechanism, and targeting feasibility. Luminal subtype-specific NMT2 deficiency was ultimately selected as a high-value therapeutic target. Owing to the limited number of luminal cell lines, no NMT2-deficient model was conventionally available. We therefore established stable NMT2 knockout (NMT2-KO) models in MCF7 and T47D cells using the CRISPR/Cas9 system with three individual sgRNAs (Additional file 2: Fig. S5a). CCK-8 and colony formation assays confirmed that NMT2 knockout did not impair proliferation or clonogenic capacity (Fig. a, b). Given that NMT2 deficiency did not impact cell viability, we assumed the functional compensation by its corresponding isozyme NMT1. Data from the DepMap database also demonstrated that lower NMT2 expression in cell lines was significantly associated with enhanced sensitivity to NMT1 inhibition (Fig. c). To further validate this hypothesis, we examined the role of NMT1 in tumor progression. First, we employed the mixture of siRNAs to knockdown NMT1 in the NMT2-KO and control cells (Fig. d). CCK-8 assays showed that transient suppression of NMT1 exerted a more pronounced anti-proliferative effect on NMT2-KO cells than on control cells (Fig. e). To further confirm our findings, we conducted stable NMT1 knockdown models by infecting NMT2-KO and the control cell lines with lentiviral vectors expressing two distinct shRNAs (Fig. f). The results of CCK-8 and colony formation assays both substantiated that NMT1 knockdown strongly inhibited proliferation in NMT2-KO cells but only weakly suppressed proliferation in control cells without NMT2 depletion (Fig. g, h). These findings confirmed that NMT2 deficiency confers specific vulnerability to NMT1 inhibition, providing an effective synthetic lethal strategy for therapeutic intervention. Moreover, survival analyses indicated that NMT2 deficiency did not affect the prognosis of luminal breast cancer patients in both the FUSCC cohort and the TCGA cohort, while high NMT1 expression correlated with poor prognosis in patients with NMT2-deficient luminal breast cancer (Additional file 2: Fig. S5b, c).
 To further demonstrate the generalizability of our LID screening approach, we phenotypically validated three additional LID targets: two pan-breast cancer targets (HMGCS1/HMGCS2 and ACACA/ACACB) and basal-like subtype-specific INPP4A/INPP4B. We first identified cell lines with or without LID (Additional file 2: Fig. S5d, e), and then inhibited the corresponding target isozyme in both contexts. Targeting HMGCS1 significantly inhibited cell proliferation, particularly in HMGCS2-deficient Hs578T and MDA-MB-231 cells, supporting synthetic lethality (Additional file 2: Fig. S5f, g). Similarly, ACACA knockdown significantly suppressed proliferation in ACACB-deficient Hs578T and T47D cells (Additional file 2: Fig. S5h, i). Targeting INPP4A also produced an inhibitory effect in INPP4B-deficient MDA-MB-453 and CAL-148 cells (Additional file 2: Fig. S5j, k). These additional results bolster the clinical potential of LID targets.

## NMT-mediated myristoylation of FXR2 promotes tumor proliferation
Next, we aimed to elucidate the mechanism underlying NMT1-induced synthetic lethality in NMT2-deficient tumors. NMT1 and NMT2 are myristoyltransferases that catalyze the transfer of myristic acid to the N-terminal glycine residue of proteins, thereby mediating myristoylation [ ]. This is a crucial post-translational modification that regulates the membrane localization and functional stability of proteins [ ]. Therefore, we further hypothesized that targeting NMT1 in NMT2-deficient tumors might disrupt the myristoylation of essential proteins, impairing their functions, and thereby inhibiting tumor proliferation (Fig. a). Thus, we identified the proteins that interact with NMT1 and NMT2 to investigate the key myristoylated substrates. We overexpressed Flag-tagged NMT1 and NMT2 in HEK293T cells, performed immunoprecipitation (IP) assays, and analyzed the enriched proteins using LC-MS/MS (Fig. b and Additional file 2: Fig. S6a). Among the proteins identified as interacting with both NMT1 and NMT2, only FXR2 contained the consensus N-terminal motif for myristoylation (MGXXXS) [ , ] and ranked among the top ten candidates (Fig. c). Subsequent Co-IP assays verified that FXR2 interacted with both exogenously expressed NMT1 and NMT2 in HEK293T cells (Fig. d). We also confirmed the endogenous interaction between FXR2 and NMT1/2 in human luminal subtype breast cancer cell lines (Fig. e-g). Furthermore, we detected myristoylated FXR2 by click chemistry assays (Fig. h) and found that NMT1 knockdown led to a reduction in both the total and myristoylated FXR2 levels (Fig. i). We also verified the myristoylation site by creating a glycine-to-alanine (G2A) mutation and generating a myristoylation-deficient mutant (Fig. j). These results confirmed that NMT1 could mediate the myristoylation of FXR2 at the N-terminal glycine site. Chase assays using the protein synthesis inhibitor cycloheximide (CHX) further suggested that NMT1 knockdown shortened the half-life of the FXR2 protein, indicating that myristoylation enhanced the stability of the FXR2 protein (Fig. k).
 FXR2 is an RNA-binding protein as a member of the fragile X-related protein family, playing roles in stabilizing mRNA expression, regulating mRNA transport and translation, and possibly promoting cell proliferation [ ]. We first verified the oncogenic phenotype of FXR2. We constructed stable FXR2 knockdown models in NMT2-deficient cell lines and found that FXR2 knockdown significantly inhibited cell proliferation (Additional file 2: Fig. S6b-d). In the FXR2 knockdown cell lines, overexpression of wild-type FXR2 restored cell viability, whereas the myristoylation-deficient G2A mutant only partially reversed this phenotype (Fig. l and Additional file 2: Fig. S6e, f), indicating that the myristoylation of FXR2 enhanced its oncogenic function. Therefore, we further overexpressed FXR2 in previously constructed NMT1 knockdown cell lines to verify whether NMT1 knockdown inhibited cell proliferation by disrupting FXR2 myristoylation. The results demonstrated that overexpression of wild-type FXR2 fully restored and even enhanced cell proliferation, whereas the G2A mutant showed limited rescue efficacy (Fig. m and Additional file 2: Fig. S6g, h). These results conclusively validated our hypothesis that NMT1 knockdown inhibits NMT2-deficient tumor proliferation by ablating FXR2 myristoylation and thereby destabilizing its oncogenic function.

## NMT2 deficiency enhances the anti-cancer effects of the NMT inhibitor PCLX-001
Given that NMT1 inhibition induced synthetic lethality in NMT2-deficient tumors while having minimal impact on NMT2 non-deficient cells, NMT1-specific inhibitors represent a promising therapeutic strategy for NMT2-deficient tumors. However, there are currently no clinically available NMT1-specific inhibitors. PCLX-001 is a dual NMT1/2 inhibitor, which has been granted orphan drug status by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and has been verified for safety through clinical trials [ ]. Therefore, this study further explored whether NMT2-deficient tumors are more sensitive to PCLX-001 treatment to verify the clinical implications of this LID target. First, we confirmed through in vitro cell line drug sensitivity assays that NMT2 knockout cell lines are more sensitive to PCLX-001 treatment (Fig. a). To further validate this conclusion, we quantified NMT2 expression in patient tumors using RT-qPCR and IHC (Fig. b and Additional file 2: Fig. S7a), stratified and selected two breast cancer PDOs each of high- and low-NMT2 expression, and confirmed the enhanced sensitivity of NMT2-deficient PDOs to PCLX-001 treatment (Fig. c). Finally, we constructed an in-situ xenograft tumor model of human luminal breast cancer cells in nude mice and confirmed that NMT2 knockout tumors were more sensitive to PCLX-001 treatment in vivo (Fig. d-g). In summary, NMT2 deficiency nominates NMT1 as a potential therapeutic target and also increases the sensitivity to NMT inhibitor treatment (Fig. h).
 We further assessed whether NMT1 inhibition synergizes with standard therapies for luminal breast cancer, including endocrine therapy (tamoxifen) and CDK4/6 inhibition (ribociclib). Drug sensitivity assays and ZIP model evaluation demonstrated that targeting NMT1 mildly sensitizes NMT2-deficient tumors to tamoxifen treatment (Additional file 2: Fig. S7b, c). However, no synergistic effect was observed between NMT1 inhibition and ribociclib, although the combination regimens outperformed the monotherapies (Additional file 2: Fig. S7d, e). Thus, PCLX-001 may serve as a complementary standalone strategy rather than a synergistic combination agent for treating luminal NMT2-deficient tumors.

## Discussion
Investigating tumor metabolic vulnerabilities provides critical insights for developing precision therapies, with LID representing an underexplored metabolic dimension. In our study, through integrative analysis of multi-omics cohorts, we systematically characterized isozyme expression patterns and LID landscapes across breast cancer PAM50 subtypes, identifying therapeutically actionable LID targets. Taking luminal subtype-specific NMT2 deficiency as an example, we validated that NMT1 represents an effective therapeutic target. This work advances breast cancer precision medicine by establishing LID-driven therapeutic strategies.
Previous studies predominantly focused on the upregulated metabolic characteristics of tumors for therapeutic targeting, as inhibiting overactive enzymes is pharmacologically tractable, whereas reactivating downregulated enzymes remains challenging. However, this approach risks on-target toxicity to normal tissues and fails to address tumor metabolic adaptability through redundant paralogs. In contrast, our study revealed that downregulated metabolic enzymes dominated tumor metabolic landscape. This finding prompted us to further investigate potential therapeutic strategies indicated by downregulated metabolic enzymes, and LID offers a promising avenue. We systematically collected all isozymes from the ENZYME nomenclature database and comprehensively analyzed the expression changes of all isozymes catalyzing the same enzymatic reactions in tumors. Based on the change pattern of isozyme expression, metabolic reactions were categorized into six classes. Among these, metabolic reactions with selectively partially downregulated isozymes or dual-directionally regulated isozymes aligned with the LID reactions of interest, where tumors may rely exclusively on residual dominant isozymes. Targeting these non-redundant isozymes could disrupt tumor-specific catalytic activity without harming normal tissues, while circumventing adaptive resistance via paralog compensation. This LID-targeting strategy extends targeted therapy by transforming enzyme downregulation into precision vulnerability.
Guided by the LID framework, we systematically identified therapeutic targets across breast cancer PAM50 subtypes. To prioritize therapeutically actionable LID targets, we established expression threshold criteria, validated findings through multi-cohort and multi-omics analyses, and integrated breast cancer cell line expression and gene dependency profiles to confirm metabolic vulnerabilities. Ultimately, we identified six LID targets shared across all breast cancer subtypes and five subtype-specific LID targets. In breast cancer, previous studies have verified the therapeutic value of ACACA [ ] and GRK2[ ]. Additionally, we identified HMGCS1, GYG1, and ALDH1L2 as promising candidates for targeted therapy. Subtype-specific LID targets were predominant in luminal subtype breast cancer. We performed an in-depth investigation of NMT1 in subsequent analyses. Notably, SCD [ , ] and HSD17B10[ ], both key enzymes involved in lipid metabolism, also emerged as potential therapeutic targets specific to the luminal subtype. Interestingly, prior studies demonstrated that in ME2-deficient pancreatic cancers, ME3 serves as a specific therapeutic target [ ]. Similarly, our findings suggest that in HER2-enriched breast cancer, ME3 deficiency may confer ME1 as a specific therapeutic target.
Mechanistic analyses revealed two principal drivers of LID. The first is recurrent copy number loss in pan-breast cancer targets and basal-like-specific INPP4B deletion, which is a typical subtype-specific genomic feature [ , ]. Another is DNA hypermethylation, particularly luminal-specific NMT2 deficiency correlated with promoter hypermethylation and elevated ESR1 expression—a hallmark of the luminal lineage [ , ]. This epigenetic silencing was linked to coordinated suppression of TET1/3 demethylases, which were also inversely associated with ESR1 levels. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating that TET1 is upregulated in basal-like subtype breast cancer, orchestrating malignant invasive behavior [ ]. Conversely, high expression of TET2 in luminal breast cancer induces the demethylation and upregulation of ESR1, GATA3, and FOXA1, thereby promoting the luminal subtype phenotype [ ]. Therefore, we propose that luminal lineage-specific low expression of TET1 and TET3 mediates NMT2 hypermethylation and deficiency.
Based on a comprehensive evaluation of all LID targets, we ultimately focused on the luminal subtype-specific NMT2 deficiency due to its strong clinical translational potential. This target was selected based on its compelling biological and clinical value: the novelty of NMT2 deficiency in luminal breast cancer, the well-defined upstream mechanism of NMT2 deficiency and the downstream mechanism of NMT1 targeting, and the strong subtype specificity together with clinically available inhibitors enabling a novel precision therapeutic strategy. We validated that tumors could maintain enzymatic function through NMT1-mediated compensation following NMT2 deficiency, thereby not affecting cell viability. On this basis, blocking NMT1 inhibits tumor growth by disrupting enzymatic activity in NMT2-deficient tumors, whereas blocking NMT1 alone has no such effect on tumors with intact NMT2 expression. This synthetic lethal effect based on LID has been previously reported in other studies, such as silencing of ENO2 selectively inhibits glioblastoma with the deletion of enolase 1 (ENO1) at the 1p36 locus [ ]. Similarly, a phosphatidylserine synthase 1 (PTDSS1) inhibitor selectively suppresses the growth of tumors with PTDSS2 deletion [ ]. In this study, we reported for the first time that blocking NMT1 induces synthetic lethality in NMT2-deficient tumors. Although the experimental phenotype did not achieve complete synthetic lethality, potentially owing to residual functional activity after gene knockdown, the significant efficacy of NMT1 inhibition in NMT2-deficient tumors underscores the therapeutic promise of targeting this collateral vulnerability.
NMT1 and NMT2 are a pair of myristoyltransferases that mediate the transfer of myristic acid to the glycine residue at the N-terminus of proteins. This post-translational modification could stabilize protein conformation and function, direct membrane localization, and regulate signal transduction, thereby modulating tumor cell behavior [ , ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that high NMT1 expression in breast cancer is correlated with higher histological grade, increased Ki-67%, and poorer prognosis [ ]. Although protein myristoylation might play a critical role in carcinogenesis, the specific mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated in breast cancer. In this study, we identified that FXR2 interacts with both NMT1 and NMT2, suggesting that NMT1 and NMT2 mediate the myristoylation of FXR2. In NMT2-deficient tumor models, NMT1 knockdown markedly suppressed FXR2 myristoylation and concurrently reduced total FXR2 protein levels, indicating that myristoylation may play a critical role in maintaining FXR2 protein stability. FXR2 is a member of the fragile X-related family of RNA-binding proteins, which are implicated in regulating RNA translation and subcellular trafficking, and maintaining stability [ ]. Nevertheless, whether FXR2 independently contributes to oncogenic processes remains unclear. Here, we confirmed that FXR2 functions as a critical oncogenic driver in breast cancer and myristoylation enhances its tumor-promoting capacity. Knockdown of NMT1 suppresses tumor cell proliferation by disrupting FXR2 myristoylation, thereby inhibiting its oncogenic functions. Therefore, we propose that NMT1 represents a promising therapeutic target for NMT2-deficient tumors. PCLX-001, an oral small-molecule NMT inhibitor, has demonstrated potent anti-tumor effects on B-cell lymphoma, acute myeloid leukemia, and various solid tumors [ – ], with favorable safety profiles in early-phase clinical trials [ , ]. In this study, we validated that NMT2-deficient tumors exhibit greater sensitivity to PCLX-001 treatment compared with tumors with high NMT2 expression across in vitro cell models, PDO models, and animal xenograft models, supporting its potential as a targeted therapeutic strategy. Additionally, we anticipate that selective NMT1-specific inhibitors will provide more precise and effective therapeutic options for NMT2-deficient tumors.
Our study has three principal limitations requiring future investigation. First, the LID target screening was based on metabolic compensation between isozymes, while other potential metabolic alterations resulting from LID remain unknown. Therefore, the therapeutic value of these targets warrants further experimental validation in preclinical models. Second, our exploration of the upstream mechanisms driving LID phenomena remains inferential, as the proposed causal relationships between genomic/epigenetic alterations and isozyme dysregulation require functional genomics validation. Third, although we have proposed PCLX-001 as a viable therapeutic strategy for NMT2-deficient tumors, we still anticipate the further development and clinical application of NMT1-specific inhibitors, which will serve as a more ideal and precise treatment strategy.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, this study conducted multi-omics analyses to characterize isozyme expression profiles in breast cancer, systematically identified LID targets in all breast cancers or specific to each subtype, and elucidated their upstream generation mechanisms. Our findings offer insights into potential precision therapeutic strategies for breast cancer from a novel metabolic perspective. Taking the luminal subtype-specific NMT2 deficiency as an example, NMT2-deficient tumors rely on NMT1 to sustain enzymatic activity. Targeted inhibition of NMT1 induces synthetic lethality, potentially through suppressing FXR2 myristoylation and consequently attenuating its oncogenic activity. These results highlight NMT inhibitors as promising therapeutic strategies for NMT2-deficient tumors.
