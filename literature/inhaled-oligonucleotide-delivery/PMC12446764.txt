## Abstract
Cervical cancer remains a significant health burden among women worldwide, with drug resistance posing a major obstacle to effective treatment strategies. This review examines the potential of bioorthogonal chemistry-based intelligent nanocarriers in precise drug delivery for overcoming cervical cancer drug resistance. Targeted delivery of estrogen receptor antagonists enables modulation of estrogen signaling pathways, addressing key resistance pathways such as DNA damage repair pathways, cancer stem cells, autophagy, and metabolic reprogramming. Bioorthogonal chemistry reactions, including click chemistry and Staudinger reactions, optimize drug delivery strategies of intelligent nanoparticles (NPs) within the tumor microenvironment (TME), enhancing targeting efficacy and stability. Nanocarriers designed for specific recognition of ERα and ERβ also enable modulation of the tumor microenvironment (TME) and exhibit synergistic potential when combined with immunotherapy to overcome drug resistance. Overcoming translational barriers and integrating multifunctional nanocarriers with immunomodulatory approaches, metabolic regulation, and artificial intelligence–based design may facilitate the development of advanced precision therapies for cervical cancer through the combined application of bioorthogonal chemistry and nanotechnology.
Keywords: Cervical cancer, Drug resistance, Bioorthogonal chemistry, Intelligent nanoparticles, Estrogen signaling pathways

## Introduction
Cervical cancer is one of the most common malignancies among women worldwide, posing a significant threat to female health [ ]. In 2022, approximately 151,000 new cases of cervical cancer were reported in China, with an age-standardized incidence rate of 13.83 per 100,000, and approximately 56,000 deaths, corresponding to a standardized mortality rate of 4.54 per 100,000. These figures account for 22.7 % of global new cases and 16.0 % of global deaths, with incidence and mortality rates remaining among the highest worldwide [ ]. Despite declining incidence in some regions, cervical cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women, particularly in developing countries and areas with limited medical resources [ ]. Traditional treatment methods, including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, continue to face challenges in improving survival rates and reducing recurrence [ , ]. The prognosis for advanced or recurrent cervical cancer remains poor, as current therapeutic approaches often yield limited efficacy [ ]. Therefore, novel therapeutic strategies are urgently needed to enhance survival and quality of life for cervical cancer patients [ ].
Estrogen plays a crucial role in regulating various physiological processes in women through its interaction with estrogen receptors (ERs). The two main subtypes of ER, ERα and ERβ, exhibit a 56 % homology in their ligand-binding domains, leading to functional antagonism in cervical cancer [ ]. Studies have shown that ERs are key mediators in the development and progression of cervical cancer, with HPV-E6/E7 proteins promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) through the ERα signaling pathway [ , ]. Upon binding to ERs, estrogen activates downstream signaling pathways, such as PI3K/Akt/mTOR and MAPK, thereby promoting tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis [ ]. Moreover, abnormal expression or dysfunction of ERs is closely associated with cervical cancer prognosis. Nuclear ERα positivity significantly increases the risk of postoperative recurrence by 1.94 times [ ]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of estrogen signaling pathways in cervical cancer is critical for developing novel treatment strategies based on nanoparticle (NP)-mediated targeted delivery of ER antagonists.
Currently, the treatment of cervical cancer primarily relies on traditional approaches, such as surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy ( ). However, these methods have certain limitations in clinical application. Although effective in early-stage disease, surgery offers limited benefit in advanced or recurrent cases [ ]. Radiotherapy and chemotherapy induce cytotoxic effects on cancer cells but also cause significant damage to surrounding normal tissues, resulting in severe side effects. Moreover, many patients develop drug resistance following chemotherapy or radiotherapy, leading to reduced treatment efficacy [ ]. Therefore, identifying new treatment methods to overcome drug resistance and enhance therapeutic outcomes has become a significant focus of current research.
 Endocrine therapy, a strategy targeting hormone receptors, has shown significant efficacy in treating hormone-related tumors, such as breast cancer [ , ]. However, its application in cervical cancer remains limited due to the heterogeneity in ERα/ERβ expression, resulting in a response rate of only approximately 35 % to estrogen antagonists [ , ]. Agents such as Tamoxifen and Fulvestrant exert anti-tumor effects by competitively inhibiting estrogen binding to ERs, thereby suppressing tumor cell proliferation [ , ]. Clinical studies have identified drug resistance in a subset of cervical cancer patients receiving endocrine therapy. Resistance mechanisms involve mutations in ERα, such as the Y537S mutation, which alters the conformation of the ligand-binding domain and impairs drug binding [ ]. In addition, compensatory activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway contributes to reduced therapeutic efficacy [ , ]. Additionally, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in the tumor microenvironment (TME) secrete IL-6, which induces ERβ-mediated resistance via the STAT3 signaling pathway [ ]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying endocrine therapy resistance in cervical cancer is crucial for the development of new therapeutic strategies.
Chemotherapy is another crucial treatment option for cervical cancer, with platinum-based agents commonly used in clinical settings. Despite initial responsiveness, approximately 60 % of patients with advanced cervical cancer develop acquired resistance following platinum-based chemotherapy [ ]. Accumulating evidence suggests a strong association between chemotherapy resistance and aberrant activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. Key molecular events include hotspot mutations in PIK3CA, such as E545K and H1047R, which result in hyperactivation of the PI3Kα subunit [ ], as well as PTEN loss, which increases AKT phosphorylation [ ]. Preclinical experiments have confirmed that targeting PI3Kα significantly reduces the IC50 value by 4.7-fold in cisplatin-resistant cervical cancer cell lines. Therefore, targeted inhibition of this pathway may provide a promising strategy to overcome chemotherapy resistance.
Given the limitations of conventional therapies and the growing challenge of drug resistance in cervical cancer, the development of novel therapeutic strategies has become increasingly urgent [ ]. Bioorthogonal chemistry is a chemical reaction that occurs in biological systems without interfering with natural biological processes. Among these, click reactions (e.g., tetrazine-alkene (Tz-TCO) reactions) have enabled tumor-selective drug release within the TME [ ]. In recent years, bioorthogonal chemistry has demonstrated significant potential in drug delivery and bio-labeling, including the use of cyclooctyne–tetrazine systems for in situ assembly of tumor-targeted therapeutics [ ]. Intelligent nanoparticles (NPs), as a novel drug delivery system, offer advantages such as strong targeting, controlled release, and good biocompatibility. For example, pH/ROS dual-responsive NPs enhance drug accumulation in cervical cancer tissues [ ]. Combining bioorthogonal chemistry with intelligent nanocarriers enables precise drug delivery, as demonstrated by NPs loaded with PI3Kα inhibitors, which reduce resistant tumor volumes by 67 % [ , ]. This review explores how bioorthogonal chemistry can optimize the delivery capabilities of intelligent NPs, enabling precise modulation of estrogen signaling pathways to overcome drug resistance in cervical cancer. By summarizing current research advances, new perspectives and strategies are proposed for improving treatment outcomes in cervical cancer [ , ].

## Estrogen signaling pathways and drug resistance in cervical cancer
2.1.

## Overview of estrogen signaling pathways
ER, a member of the nuclear receptor superfamily, regulates various physiological processes such as cell growth, differentiation, apoptosis, and metabolism [ , ]. Two major subtypes of ER have been identified: ERα and ERβ, which exhibit distinct tissue distribution and biological functions [ ]. In cervical cancer, ERα overexpression is closely linked to tumor initiation and progression, while ERβ may exert a tumor-suppressive effect [ , ]. Studies have shown that ERα overexpression promotes cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis in cervical cancer, while reduced ERβ expression correlates with tumor progression and increased drug resistance [ ]. Structural and functional differences between ERα and ERβ include variations in gene organization, ligand-binding specificity, and interactions with coregulators [ ]. For instance, ERα primarily activates estrogen response elements (ERE) in the nucleus to initiate transcription, whereas ERβ may suppress tumor cell growth by regulating ERα activity or interacting with other transcription factors [ , ]. In terms of downstream signaling pathway activation, ERα primarily promotes an ERE-dependent transcriptional mechanism upon binding with estrogen, thereby inducing the expression of pro-proliferative and pro-angiogenic genes such as c-Myc, cyclin D1, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF). This process drives cervical cancer cell growth, metastasis, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) [ ]. In contrast, ERβ often functions as an endogenous antagonist of ERα. By competitively binding to EREs or recruiting alternative corepressors, ERβ may inhibit ERα activity and suppress oncogenic signaling. Furthermore, ERβ regulates gene expression through ERE-independent pathways by interfering with transcription factors such as NF-κB, AP-1, and STAT3, contributing to its anti-inflammatory and anti-proliferative effects [ , ].
The estrogen signaling pathway in cervical cancer operates through multiple mechanisms, with gene transcription regulation and non-genomic signal transduction being the most prominent [ ]. Gene transcription regulation primarily depends on the dimer formed by ER binding to estrogen, which then interacts with ERE to activate or suppress the expression of target genes. This process involves various co-factors, such as co-activators (e.g., SRC-1, CBP/p300) and co-repressors (e.g., NCoR, SMRT), which collectively modulate ER-mediated transcriptional activity [ ]. ER can also activate non-genomic signaling pathways that function independently of direct gene transcription. These mechanisms involve interactions with membrane-associated receptors (e.g., G-protein-coupled estrogen receptor (GPER)) or cytoplasmic signaling proteins, rapidly triggering downstream signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT, MAPK/ERK, and Wnt/β-catenin ( ) [ , ]. These non-genomic pathways enable estrogen to quickly influence cellular behaviors, promoting tumor cell proliferation and invasion [ , ].
 The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is a crucial downstream estrogen signaling pathway, playing a central role in cell growth, metabolism, and survival [ ]. Activation of ERα can directly or indirectly trigger PI3K/AKT signaling, thereby promoting the proliferation and drug resistance of cervical cancer cells [ ]. PI3K, positioned upstream, activates AKT, which subsequently regulates several downstream effectors, including mTOR, BAD, and the FOXO family of proteins [ , ]. Studies have shown that the abnormal activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is closely associated with the invasiveness of cervical cancer and its resistance to chemotherapeutic agents [ ]. Furthermore, estrogen signaling can enhance the expression of PTEN, a negative regulator of the PI3K/AKT pathway, which can enhance tumor cell sensitivity to PI3K inhibitors and potentially improve therapeutic outcomes.
The MAPK/ERK signaling pathway also contributes significantly to estrogen-mediated regulation of cervical cancer cell proliferation and survival. ERα activates the RAS-RAF-MEK-ERK cascade by interacting with membrane receptors, such as the GPER, thus promoting cell cycle progression [ , ]. The MAPK/ERK pathway responds to growth factors and induces EMT, enhancing tumor cell migration and drug resistance [ , ]. Additionally, activation of this pathway upregulates the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2, inhibiting chemotherapy-induced apoptosis and increasing chemoresistance in tumors [ ].
The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the initiation and progression of cervical cancer [ ]. ERα activation can enhance Wnt signaling transcriptional activity by non-genomic interactions with β-catenin, thereby promoting tumor cell proliferation and invasion [ , ]. Studies have shown that aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway induces the expansion of CD44+ cervical cancer stem cell (CSC) populations, thereby increasing tumor drug resistance and the risk of recurrence [ ]. Additionally, estrogen signaling can regulate the phosphorylation status of GSK-3β, affecting β-catenin stability and further promoting the invasiveness of cervical cancer cells [ ].
Cross-talk between estrogen signaling and other cellular pathways plays a central role in promoting cervical cancer cell adaptation and therapeutic resistance. For example, activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway can inhibit the nuclear translocation of FOXO3a, thereby decreasing ERβ expression and enhancing ERα-mediated proliferative signals [ ]. This mechanism is particularly pronounced in recurrent or metastatic cervical cancer, where PIK3CA mutations and PTEN loss drive sustained PI3K pathway activation and are directly associated with chemoresistance [ ]. Aberrant MAPK/ERK pathway activation further contributes to resistance by inducing phosphorylation of ERα at Ser118 and Ser167, thereby maintaining its transcriptional activity even under conditions of low estrogen availability. Preclinical data indicate that combined inhibition of PI3Kα and PD-1 significantly enhances anti-tumor effects, underscoring the therapeutic potential of targeting cross-pathway interactions [ ]. In addition, epigenetic regulation has emerged as a key factor in resistance mechanisms. RNA 5-methylcytosine (m5C) modification, catalyzed by NSUN6, enhances the stability of NDRG1 mRNA and promotes homologous recombination repair (HRR), contributing to radiotherapy resistance. Cooperative effects between m5C-mediated regulation and the PI3K/AKT pathway further complicate therapeutic strategies [ ].
In summary, estrogen signaling pathways play multiple roles in the initiation, progression, and drug resistance of cervical cancer [ , ]. Activation of ERα promotes the activation of key signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT, MAPK/ERK, and Wnt/β-catenin, thereby enhancing tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and drug resistance [ , ]. Additionally, disruptions in the ERα/ERβ expression ratio, genetic mutations, and aberrant epigenetic modifications further impair the dynamic regulation of estrogen signaling, rendering cervical cancer cells less responsive to treatment [ ]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying estrogen signaling pathways and exploring targeted therapeutic strategies for this pathway are crucial for overcoming cervical cancer drug resistance and improving treatment efficacy.

2.2.

## Estrogen signaling pathways and their role in cervical cancer drug resistance
Drug resistance remains one of the primary challenges in the clinical management of cervical cancer, with estrogen signaling pathways playing a critical role in developing resistance mechanisms [ , ]. First, mutations or epigenetic changes in ERα and ERβ genes can directly affect the activity of estrogen signaling pathways [ , ]. For instance, mutations in the ERα gene (ESR1) can induce conformational changes that allow ERα to initiate downstream signaling in the absence of estrogen, thereby promoting cervical cancer cell growth and drug resistance [ , ]. Epigenetic modifications, such as promoter methylation or histone deacetylation, can also regulate ERα expression and activity, enabling tumor cells to survive under therapeutic stress [ , ].
Activation of estrogen signaling contributes to drug resistance by upregulating ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp), which facilitate drug efflux and reduce intracellular drug accumulation [ , ]. For example, ERα activation enhances the PI3K/AKT pathway, leading to increased transcription of the ABCB1 gene, thereby elevating P-gp expression and reducing intracellular concentrations of chemotherapeutic agents such as cisplatin and doxorubicin by 40 %–60 % in cervical cancer cells [ ]. The TME also contributes to resistance by modulating estrogen signaling. Hypoxia and chronic inflammation are key factors that interact with ER pathways to promote survival and resistance [ , ]. For instance, hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) overexpression activates the PI3K/AKT axis, enhancing the proliferative effects of ERα, while inhibiting apoptosis pathways, thereby increasing tumor cell resistance to radiotherapy and chemotherapy [ ]. Additionally, HIF-1α forms a transcriptional complex with ERα, co-regulating the expression of VEGF and glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), which promotes metabolic reprogramming and chemoresistance in cervical cancer cells [ ].
Furthermore, EMT plays a significant role in estrogen signaling pathways-mediated drug resistance. Overexpression of ERα induces EMT, enhancing the migration and invasion abilities of cervical cancer cells and increasing their resistance to chemotherapy [ ]. ERα directly upregulates EMT-related transcription factors, such as Snail, Slug, and Twist, leading to decreased expression of E-cadherin, disruption of cell-cell adhesion, and promotion of invasive behavior and drug resistance [ ]. In radiation-resistant locally advanced cervical cancer (LACC), ERα activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and co-activates Snail and Twist, resulting in a 50 %–70 % reduction in E-cadherin expression [ ]. Further research reveals that ERα and HIF-1α form a complex under hypoxic conditions, enhancing the expression of EMT-related genes, such as Vimentin and N-cadherin, and promoting chemoresistance by inhibiting the pro-apoptotic protein BCL2L1 [ ].

2.3.

## Therapeutic strategies targeting estrogen signaling pathways
Several targeted intervention strategies have been developed to address the aberrant activation of estrogen signaling pathways ( ). Traditional estrogen antagonists primarily include selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) and selective estrogen receptor degraders (SERDs) [ ]. For example, Tamoxifen,a commonly used SERM, competes with estrogen for binding to ERα, thereby inhibiting its proliferative signaling [ , ]. However, prolonged use of Tamoxifen can lead to drug resistance, partly due to ESR1 gene mutations that activate ERα in the absence of a ligand [ ]. In recurrent cervical cancer, the Y537S mutation in the ESR1 gene alters the receptor conformation, enabling ligand-independent activation and evading the inhibitory effects of Tamoxifen [ ]. Fulvestrant, a SERD, promotes the degradation of ERα, effectively blocking estrogen signaling. However, its efficacy remains limited due to the emergence of resistance mechanisms, including bypass activation of alternative pathways such as PI3K/AKT/mTOR and re-expression of ERα.
 In recent years, small-molecule inhibitors and novel combination therapies have shown promising potential in overcoming drug resistance in cervical cancer. PI3K/mTOR inhibitors, such as Alpelisib, effectively suppress the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling cascade downstream of ERα, thereby inhibiting tumor cell proliferation and enhancing apoptosis [ ]. In addition, epigenetic modulators, such as histone deacetylase inhibitors, restore the expression of tumor suppressor genes and reverse resistance associated with ER-mediated signaling. Furthermore, strategies combining immune checkpoint inhibitors—such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors—with nanodrug delivery systems have also emerged as promising approaches for enhancing antitumor efficacy in cervical cancer [ , ].
Collectively, the estrogen signaling pathway plays a critical role in the initiation, progression, and drug resistance of cervical cancer. A deeper understanding of its regulatory mechanisms and the development of targeted therapeutic strategies against this pathway may improve treatment outcomes for cervical cancer patients and provide new insights into precision therapy.

## Intelligent nanocarriers in precise drug delivery
3.1.

## Advantages of nanodrugs in cervical cancer treatment
Traditional treatment methods for cervical cancer, such as surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, are often limited by drug resistance, systemic toxicity, and lack of specific targeting [ ]. Nanodrug delivery systems offer a promising solution to these challenges due to their unique physicochemical properties [ , ]. NPs can enhance the solubility and bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs [ ], increase drug accumulation at the tumor site, and release active molecules through intelligent response mechanisms ( ) [ ].
 One major advantage of nanodrugs lies in their ability to improve the solubility of hydrophobic chemotherapeutic agents, such as paclitaxel and doxorubicin, thereby avoiding the toxicity associated with conventional solvent systems. For instance, paclitaxel nanoformulations such as Abraxane have demonstrated enhanced antitumor efficacy across various cancers. Their nanoscale design prolongs plasma half-life and promotes preferential accumulation at tumor sites [ ]. In addition, NPs achieve passive tumor targeting via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, which exploits the leaky vasculature and impaired lymphatic drainage characteristic of tumor tissues [ ]. Compared with small-molecule drugs, NPs are better retained within tumors due to their optimal particle size and reduced renal clearance. This property allows NPs to maintain higher local drug concentrations, extend therapeutic duration, and minimize nonspecific toxicity to healthy tissues [ , ].
Additionally, drug resistance in cervical cancer remains one of the primary challenges in clinical treatment. Nanodrugs have demonstrated the potential to overcome resistance through the following mechanisms: (1)Nanoparticle-based formulations can modulate ABC transporters (e.g., P-gp) to reduce drug efflux and increase intracellular drug concentrations. Studies have shown that paclitaxel NPs coated with polydopamine inhibit P-gp activity, resulting in a 3.8-fold increase in drug accumulation in cisplatin-resistant cervical cancer cells [ , ]; (2) Surface modification of NPs with specific targeting ligands (e.g., folic acid, antibodies) enhances cellular uptake. Folic acid receptor-targeted poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) NPs can deliver paclitaxel to cervical cancer cells, resulting in a 60 % increase in tumor suppression compared to free drug delivery [ , ]. Studies have also demonstrated that folic acid-modified polymer NPs effectively target cervical cancer cells, significantly improving the therapeutic efficacy of paclitaxel [ ].

3.2.

## Design of intelligent NPs
Intelligent NP platforms are engineered to optimize drug delivery through tumor-specific targeting and controlled release. These systems often incorporate surface modifications with ligands such as peptides, polysaccharides, or antibodies to improve selectivity for cervical cancer cells. In addition, stimuli-responsive features are integrated to achieve environment-triggered drug release within the TME. Functionalized nanocarriers and responsive delivery systems represent two major strategies in the design of intelligent NPs.
3.2.1.

## Functionalized nanocarriers
Functionalized NPs enhance the targeting capability of cervical cancer cells through surface modification with specific ligands, such as RGD peptides, chitosan, or polypeptides. RGD peptides bind selectively to the αvβ3 integrin receptor, which is overexpressed on cervical cancer cells, thereby improving nanoparticle targeting efficiency and cellular uptake. Studies have demonstrated that PEG–polylactic acid nanoparticles modified with RGD peptides enhance drug internalization in cisplatin-resistant cervical cancer cells by 4.8-fold via αvβ3-mediated endocytosis and significantly inhibit cancer stem cell (CSC) activity [ ]. Studies have shown that RGD-modified PEG-polylactic acid NPs increase tumor accumulation by 5.2 times compared to unmodified NPs in a cervical cancer xenograft model, while also significantly inhibiting tumor angiogenesis [ , ].
Additionally, chitosan-modified NPs exhibit excellent biocompatibility, enhance the sustained release of drugs and improve stability in the acidic TME. Thiolated chitosan/hyaluronic acid (HA) dual-modified NPs show a threefold increase in drug release in the acidic microenvironment of cervical cancer (pH 6.5) compared to normal tissue (pH 7.4), resulting in a 57 % increase in tumor suppression [ ].

3.2.2.

## Stimuli-responsive NPs
Stimuli-responsive NPs are designed to release drugs in response to specific microenvironmental conditions, enhancing therapeutic efficacy and reducing systemic toxicity. The main types include:
(1)pH-Responsive NPs NPs: The TME typically exhibits a mildly acidic pH (6.5–6.8), in contrast to the near-neutral pH (∼7.4) of normal tissues. pH-sensitive NPs formulated with polymers such as PLGA or PEG–PLGA undergo rapid degradation under acidic conditions, triggering the release of encapsulated drugs. For example, thiolated chitosan/HA dual-responsive NPs increase drug release threefold in the acidic microenvironment of cervical cancer and improve tumor suppression by 57 % [ ]. pH-responsive liposomes co-delivering paclitaxel and small interfering RNA (siRNA) to cervical cancer cisplatin-resistant cells reduce tumor volume by 70 % by inhibiting P-gp expression [ ].
(2)Enzyme-Triggered Release NPs: Matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-2/9) are upregulated in cervical cancer tissue. Nanoparticles engineered to respond to these enzymes undergo selective degradation within the TME, allowing for targeted drug release. Transferrin-modified, MMP-2–responsive mesoporous silica nanoparticles delivering cetuximab achieve over 80 % inhibition of EGFR phosphorylation in a cervical cancer model [ ]. MMP-9-responsive PEG-PLLA NPs degrade the tumor extracellular matrix, significantly enhancing drug penetration depth (up to 300 μm) [ ].
(3)Light/Magnetic/Ultrasound-Triggered NPs: Light-responsive NPs can release drugs upon exposure to specific wavelengths of light, minimizing damage to normal tissues. For instance, light-activated nanocarriers based on tetrazine-norbornene click chemistry specifically release fulvestrant in the cervical cancer microenvironment, with a targeting efficiency exceeding 90 % [ ]. Magnetic NPs can be directed to tumor sites using external magnetic fields and are also employed in magnetic hyperthermia. Superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles, when used in combination with magnetic heating to raise tumor temperature to 45 °C, induce apoptosis in 75 % of tumor cells in xenograft models.

3.3.

## Precise delivery of estrogen antagonists
In response to the critical role of estrogen signaling in cervical cancer, various nanocarrier systems have been developed to deliver ER antagonists and enhance therapeutic efficacy ( ). The progression of cervical cancer is closely linked to estrogen-driven signaling, and ER antagonists such as Tamoxifen have been extensively studied for the treatment of ER-positive cervical cancer. To improve drug targeting and minimize systemic toxicity, a range of nanotechnology-based delivery platforms has been engineered to enhance the precision and bioavailability of these agents. Nanocarriers, including PEGylated liposomes and gold NPs, have been utilized to increase the accumulation and intracellular uptake of Tamoxifen-like compounds in ER-positive cervical cancer cells. These strategies significantly improve antitumor efficacy while reducing off-target toxicity, thereby offering a promising approach for targeted endocrine therapy in cervical cancer.
 Building on these strategies, bioorthogonal chemistry-based intelligent nanocarriers provide a more precise approach to estrogen antagonist therapy. These carriers commonly introduce bioorthogonal sites, such as azide–alkyne click reactions, enabling localized drug activation and release within tumor tissues. This strategy minimizes nonspecific effects on normal tissues, enhances tumor selectivity, and reduces adverse reactions. In addition, several platforms integrate stimuli-responsive release mechanisms and multimodal therapeutic modules, enabling the concurrent blockade of estrogen receptor signaling alongside radiotherapy or immunomodulatory interventions. Such multifunctional nanocarriers establish a robust foundation for the development of highly selective and synergistic therapeutic platforms, with strong translational relevance and promising clinical potential [ ].
In addition, drug-loaded liposome and nanoemulsion platforms have also been employed to deliver anti-estrogen drugs. These nanocarriers can be functionalized with targeting ligands such as hyaluronic acid, folic acid, or anti-HER2 antibodies to enhance tumor specificity. Many formulations also incorporate pH-sensitive or thermoresponsive components to enable controlled drug release within the TME (PMC: 10927891).
3.3.1.

## Types of carriers
To enhance the delivery efficiency of estrogen antagonists in cervical cancer treatment, various nanocarriers have been developed to optimize drug stability, improve tumor targeting, and reduce systemic toxicity. Based on physicochemical properties and biological functions, these carriers can be categorized as follows:
(1)Liposomes: Liposomes, known for their excellent biocompatibility and high drug-loading capacity, are widely used for anticancer drug delivery. For example, DOXIL (a liposomal formulation of doxorubicin) has been successfully applied in clinical oncology, with its bilayer phospholipid structure protecting the drug from degradation and enhancing drug accumulation in tumor tissues. Recent studies have shown that folate receptor-targeted liposomes delivering fulvestrant to cervical cancer drug-resistant cells inhibit the ERα-PI3K/AKT pathway, reducing tumor volume by 65 % [ ]. pH-sensitive liposomes co-delivering paclitaxel and siRNA have also been reported to reverse cisplatin resistance by inhibiting P-gp expression [ ].
(2)Polymeric NPs: Polymeric NPs, such as PLGA, can achieve sustained and controlled drug release. Chitosan-modified PLGA NPs loaded with Tamoxifen demonstrate a three-fold increase in drug release in the acidic microenvironment of cervical cancer, with a 57 % increase in tumor suppression [ ]. MMP-2-responsive PEG-PLGA NPs enhance drug penetration depth (up to 300 μm) by degrading the tumor matrix barrier.
(3)Metal NPs: Metallic NPs, particularly gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), exhibit strong photothermal conversion capabilities and have been applied in combination therapies. Fulvestrant-conjugated AuNP clusters release the drug upon near-infrared irradiation, specifically targeting ERα-positive cervical cancer cells and inhibiting phosphorylation within the PI3K/AKT pathway [ ]. Superparamagnetic iron oxide NPs combined with magnetic hyperthermia in a cervical cancer xenograft model elevate tumor temperature to 45 °C, inducing a 75 % apoptosis rate [ ].
(4)Cell Membrane-Coated NPs: NPs coated with red blood cell, tumor cell, or immune cell membranes can evade immune clearance, prolong systemic circulation, and enhance tumor targeting. Fulvestrant-loaded NPs coated with cervical cancer cell membranes exhibit a 4.8-fold increase in tumor accumulation via homologous targeting [ ]. Macrophage membrane-modified NPs significantly enhance drug accumulation in cervical cancer drug-resistant cells by exploiting TME chemotaxis [ ].

3.3.2.

## Intelligent delivery strategies
Achieving precise drug release at tumor sites remains a key factor influencing the therapeutic efficacy of estrogen receptor antagonists delivered via NPs. To improve targeting specificity, a variety of intelligent delivery strategies have been developed. As illustrated in , these systems utilize mechanisms such as ERα-targeted modification, TME responsiveness, and tumor-specific penetration to enhance drug localization, release precision, and chemosensitivity.
(1)ERα-Targeted Delivery: The high expression of ERα in cervical cancer cells provides a valuable target for selective drug delivery. Surface modification of NPs with ERα antibodies or small-molecule ligands can significantly enhance selective drug accumulation at tumor sites and improve therapeutic efficacy. Recent advances in bioorthogonal chemistry have provided new technological avenues for ERα-targeted delivery systems. Recent advances in bioorthogonal chemistry have enabled the design of nanocarriers incorporating tetrazine–norbornene click reactions. In these systems, DBCO-modified NP surfaces undergo in vivo ligation with prelocalized tetrazine moieties, allowing for localized activation and controlled release of estrogen antagonists [ ]. Additionally, one study reported that a dual-receptor-mediated endocytosis strategy via CD44 and ERα significantly increased the uptake efficiency of fulvestrant in drug-resistant cervical cancer cells, achieving a targeting efficiency exceeding 90 % [ ]. Another report demonstrated that folate receptor-targeted liposomes loaded with fulvestrant effectively inhibited the ERα–PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and reduced tumor volume by 65 % [ ]. These findings highlight the potential of ERα-targeted nanoplatforms combined with bioorthogonal chemistry for achieving tumor-selective and controllable therapy while minimizing off-target toxicity.
(2)TME-Responsive Release: Stimuli-responsive delivery systems are designed to exploit specific features of the TME, including acidic pH and overexpressed enzymes, enabling spatially controlled drug release. For example, thiolated chitosan/hyaluronic acid dual-responsive NPs demonstrate a threefold increase in drug release under acidic conditions (pH 6.5), resulting in a 57 % improvement in tumor inhibition [ , ]. Enzyme-triggered NPs: Transferrin-modified MMP-2-responsive mesoporous silica NPs release cetuximab in a cervical cancer model, achieving more than 80 % inhibition of EGFR phosphorylation [ ]. Redox dual-responsive systems: Glutathione (GSH)-responsive PEG-PLGA NPs degrade the tumor stromal barrier, significantly enhancing drug penetration depth (up to 300 μm) [ ].
(3)Tumor-Specific Penetration Mechanisms: To improve intratumoral distribution, researchers have developed NPs modified with cell-penetrating peptides (e.g., TAT peptide, iRGD peptide). AuNP clusters, modified with fulvestrant, enhance tumor penetration through charge inversion and trigger drug release under near-infrared light, inhibiting PI3K/AKT pathway phosphorylation [ ]. Superparamagnetic iron oxide NPs and magnetic hyperthermia allow for external magnetic field control of tumor site accumulation, inducing apoptosis in cervical cancer cells by 75 % [ ].
 Intelligent nanocarrier platforms have demonstrated strong potential in improving the delivery of estrogen antagonists for cervical cancer therapy. Folate receptor-targeted PLGA NPs loaded with Tamoxifen exhibit a threefold increase in drug release under acidic tumor conditions, resulting in a 57 % increase in tumor inhibition [ ]. ERα-targeted nanocarriers based on tetrazine-norbornene click chemistry achieve >90 % targeting efficiency through CD44 and ERα dual receptor-mediated endocytosis [ ].

## Advantages and applications of bioorthogonal chemistry reactions
4.1.

## Principles of bioorthogonal chemistry
Bioorthogonal chemistry refers to a class of chemical reactions that proceed with high selectivity in living systems without interfering with native biological processes [ ]. Owing to this unique compatibility, bioorthogonal chemistry has emerged as a powerful tool for investigating biomolecular interactions, regulating cellular functions, and facilitating therapeutic interventions. The core principle of these reactions lies in the mutual orthogonality of the reactive groups, allowing reactions to occur exclusively at designated sites without cross-reacting with endogenous biomolecules in complex biological environments ( ). For example, the Staudinger reaction enables efficient coupling of phosphine reagents with azides in aqueous environments, facilitating the metabolic engineering of cervical cancer cells to introduce azide groups on the cell surface. This modification facilitates the site-specific anchoring of fulvestrant, achieving a targeting efficiency exceeding 90 % [ , ]. The azide-alkyne cycloaddition (click chemistry), catalyzed by Cu(I), proceeds with exceptionally rapid kinetics and has been widely applied in cellular imaging and drug conjugation. Tetrazine-norbornene click chemistry NPs specifically release estrogen antagonists in the cervical cancer microenvironment, inhibiting the phosphorylation of the ERα-PI3K/AKT pathway [ ]. Studies have shown that combining bioorthogonal chemistry-mediated antigen capture with the autonomous motility of engineered bacteria significantly enhances the cross-presentation efficiency of dendritic cells (DCs), independent of adjuvant involvement [ ]. This strategy effectively overcomes the challenge of delivering antigens from the tumor stroma to immune cells. In addition, bioorthogonal chemistry provides a foundational approach for precise imaging of deep-seated tumors. Through bioorthogonal targeting strategies, dual-wavelength near-infrared II (NIR-II) fluorescence imaging has been successfully applied, enabling accurate localization and monitoring of tumor sites [ ].
 The tetrazine–trans-cyclooctene (Tz–TCO) reaction exhibits ultra-fast kinetics (rate constant >104 M−1s−1) and proceeds without a catalyst, offering distinct advantages in targeting drug-resistant cervical cancer cells. For instance, metabolic sugar engineering introduces alkene groups on the surface of cervical cancer cells, enabling covalent binding between tetrazine-modified nanoparticles and HER2-positive drug-resistant cell surface receptors. This strategy results in a fivefold increase in Fulvestrant accumulation in tumor tissue [ ], effectively reversing cisplatin resistance by blocking phosphorylation of the ERα–PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.
Recent studies have developed pH/light dual-responsive tetrazine-alkyne NPs, which trigger drug release in the acidic microenvironment of cervical cancer (pH 6.5–7.0). The release rate is further regulated by near-infrared light (650 nm). Under acidic conditions, protonation of the tetrazine ring induces nanoparticle dissociation, leading to the release of estrogen antagonists and achieving a tumor-to-normal tissue drug concentration ratio of 8:1 [ , ]. Upon light activation, dihydrotetrazine precursors accelerate drug release, resulting in a 65 % reduction in tumor volume in a cervical cancer xenograft model [ ].
The synergistic integration of bioorthogonal chemistry with immune checkpoint inhibitors has emerged as a prominent research focus. T-cell-targeted conjugation: The Tz-TCO reaction covalently couples anti-PD-L1 nanobodies to the CD3 receptor on the surface of CD8+ T cells, significantly enhancing T-cell activation efficiency in the cervical cancer microenvironment by 70 % [ ]. Precise CAR-T cell regulation: tetrazine-functionalized receptors are engineered onto the surface of CAR-T cells to mediate site-specific IL-12 release through bioorthogonal chemistry, reversing cisplatin resistance and inhibiting phosphorylation of the ERα-PI3K/AKT pathway [ , ].
Recent studies have also revealed that circSTX6 in cisplatin-resistant cervical cancer cells regulates the METTL3/YTHDC1 positive feedback loop through m6A methylation, activating the IL6/JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. Bioorthogonal NPs targeting this pathway significantly reduce the expression of resistance-related proteins (such as P-gp), enhancing chemotherapy sensitivity [ , ].

4.2.

## Applications of bioorthogonal chemistry in nanodrug delivery
Bioorthogonal chemistry has demonstrated unique advantages in nanodrug delivery systems, particularly in enhancing the binding efficiency of NPs to target cells. Studies have shown that introducing bioorthogonal reaction groups (e.g., tetrazine or azide groups) onto the surface of NPs enables covalent binding with specific receptors on the target cell surface, significantly increasing the accumulation of NPs in tumor tissues [ ]. For example, the tetrazine–cyclooctene reaction has been applied to covalently bind NPs to E6/E7 oncoproteins on HPV-positive cervical cancer cells, resulting in nearly a fivefold enhancement in drug delivery efficiency [ ]. This study demonstrates that these conjugation strategies reduce off-target distribution and facilitate selective drug accumulation at tumor sites.
In addition, bioorthogonal chemistry provides a catalyst-free and non-interfering platform for microenvironment-responsive drug release in cervical cancer. Tetrazine–alkyne NPs have been engineered to respond to the acidic conditions of the tumor microenvironment (pH 6.5–7.0), where orthogonal reactions trigger NP dissociation and fulvestrant release. This system achieves a 3.8-fold increase in intratumoral drug release while limiting leakage in normal tissues to below 5 % [ ]. Targeting LDH, which is overexpressed in cervical cancer, further enhances selective activation of drug release and inhibits phosphorylation of the ERα–PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby reversing cisplatin resistance [ ].
Researchers have also designed a bioorthogonal chemistry-driven intelligent nanocarrier that employs azide–alkyne click chemistry to target the delivery of a Pt(IV) prodrug while simultaneously depleting intracellular glutathione in cervical cancer cells, thereby overcoming cisplatin resistance (PMCID: PMC6271432). In a preclinical study, Cao et al. systematically validated the potential of bioorthogonal chemistry-based nanocarriers in enhancing drug retention and inhibiting metastasis. In this study, two types of polymeric NPs (D-NP and C-NP) were constructed, each modified with the bioorthogonal reactive pair 2-cyanobenzothiazole (CBT) and D-cysteine (D-Cys), demonstrating robust therapeutic efficacy [ ]. Clinically, the Shasqi CAPAC® platform exemplifies the significant translational potential of bioorthogonal chemistry in cancer therapy. The lead candidate SQ3370 completed a first-in-human Phase I dose-escalation trial (NCT04106492), achieving doxorubicin doses up to 12 times the standard level without increased systemic toxicity, while markedly enhancing CD8+ T cell activation. This trial represents the first clinical validation of in vivo bioorthogonal chemistry-based activation for localized drug release. In a separate Phase II clinical study, albumin-bound paclitaxel (Abraxane®) was employed as monotherapy in patients with persistent cervical cancer to evaluate safety, tolerability, and preliminary efficacy (NCT00499291). This study provided supportive clinical evidence for Abraxane®-based nanomedicine applications and offered a model for future combination nanotherapeutic strategies. In addition, a fluorescent probe was synthesized by conjugating a triphenylmethyl (Cy3) fluorophore to ethinyl estradiol via bioorthogonal click chemistry, enabling precise imaging and localization of estrogen receptors. This approach validated the feasibility of click chemistry-mediated estrogen modification for efficient and targeted receptor labeling [ ].
Bioorthogonal chemistry has significantly improved the targeting and efficacy of immunotherapy for cervical cancer. For example, anti-PD-L1 nanobodies were covalently conjugated to the CD3 receptor on the surface of cervical cancer-specific CD8+ T cells using the Tz-TCO reaction, resulting in a 70 % increase in T-cell activation and reduced systemic immune toxicity [ ]. This approach simultaneously targeted HPV E6/E7 oncoproteins and immune checkpoint molecules, leading to tumor immune microenvironment remodeling and potentiation of antitumor immune responses.

4.3.

## Successful applications in the treatment of other cancers
Bioorthogonal chemistry has significantly advanced in treating various cancers, offering new insights for precisely treating cervical cancer. For example, in HER2-positive breast cancer, researchers utilized bioorthogonal reactions to covalently conjugate HER2 antibodies with NPs, forming a targeted delivery system. This approach effectively increased drug accumulation at the tumor site (4.2-fold) while limiting drug leakage in normal tissues to below 8 % [ , ]. This strategy employed the Tz-TCO bioorthogonal reaction to combine anti-HER2 monoclonal antibodies with liposomal NPs, enabling targeted delivery of doxorubicin. It provides a technical paradigm for treating cervical cancer subtypes that overexpress HER2 or EGFR [ , ]. Bioorthogonal chemistry has also been combined with inhalation-based drug delivery to develop an EA-Pt@MDBCO nanoplatform loaded with nitric oxide (NO) for the treatment of cisplatin-resistant non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). This system employed azide pre-labeling to achieve selective tumor targeting and released EA–Pt under acidic conditions to inhibit glutathione transferase activity and deplete intracellular GSH, thereby reversing drug resistance. Simultaneously, NO was released to augment therapeutic efficacy. In orthotopic NSCLC models, this approach demonstrated significant therapeutic benefits, providing a novel strategy for overcoming drug resistance [ ].
Bioorthogonal chemistry has been applied in click chemistry-mediated drug delivery systems in glioma research. The blood–brain barrier (BBB) presents a major obstacle for traditional chemotherapeutic agents, which often fail to reach effective concentrations in the central nervous system (CNS). However, NPs modified via bioorthogonal conjugation have shown improved BBB permeability and targeted delivery capacity [ ]. In a recent study, the tetrazine–cyclooctene reaction was employed to load doxorubicin onto nanoparticles capable of traversing the BBB, enabling site-specific drug release within glioma tissue and significantly enhancing therapeutic efficacy. This strategy provides a reference model for managing brain metastases in cervical cancer, demonstrating the potential of bioorthogonal chemistry to improve drug accumulation in TME that are otherwise difficult to access [ ]. Another study further demonstrated the use of tetrazine–trans-cyclooctene click chemistry to load doxorubicin onto BBB-penetrating nanocarriers. This approach enabled precise drug release at glioma sites and expanded the scope of bioorthogonal chemistry in overcoming physiological barriers and delivering therapeutics to deep-seated tumors [ ].
Furthermore, bioorthogonal chemistry-driven theranostic strategies have also led to significant advances in the treatment of melanoma and breast cancer. For example, 177Lu-labeled HA NPs were covalently conjugated to azide groups on the tumor surface via dibenzocyclooctyne (DBCO) to enable magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-guided precise radiotherapy [ , ]. Similar technologies can be extended to cervical cancer, where LDH is overexpressed in the acidic microenvironment, optimizing the spatiotemporal release of estrogen antagonists [ ].
In addition, a strategy involving bioorthogonal chemistry-labeled tumor antigens combined with engineered Salmonella-mediated active delivery effectively enhanced dendritic cell (DC) antigen uptake and cross-presentation. This approach significantly activated CD8+ T cells and induced robust systemic antitumor immunity. In bilateral melanoma models, pronounced abscopal effects were observed, dependent on intact adaptive immunity [ ]. This strategy offers a promising vaccination pathway for poorly immunogenic solid tumors and holds potential for application in cervical cancer, particularly in recurrent or drug-resistant cases, by improving antigen presentation efficiency and promoting local immune activation to enhance immunotherapeutic efficacy and systemic tumor control.

## A study on the mechanisms overcoming drug resistance in cervical cancer
5.1.

## Mechanisms of drug resistance
Drug resistance remains a major obstacle in the clinical management of cervical cancer. A comprehensive understanding of its underlying mechanisms is essential for the development of effective therapeutic strategies. First, DNA damage repair mechanisms play a critical role in cervical cancer resistance.
The mechanisms of resistance in cervical cancer are diverse and highly heterogeneous, including drug efflux pumps (e.g., ABCB1/MDR1), CSC phenotypes (CD44+/ALDH1+), enhanced DNA repair, impaired apoptosis pathways, EMT, and immune evasion, which often coexist at varying degrees within the same patient’s tumor. Identifying the dominant resistance mechanism in each patient is critical for guiding personalized therapeutic interventions. Multi-omics approaches such as transcriptomics, proteomics, and spatial genomics can be employed to identify the activation states of resistance-associated pathways. Functional validation using patient-derived organoids or xenograft (PDX) models allows for assessment of whether inhibition of specific pathways can restore drug sensitivity. In addition, noninvasive indicators such as microRNAs, biomarkers, and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) may serve as preliminary screening tools. For instance, Chen et al. employed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) in a HeLa xenograft model and observed a marked expansion of CD44+ALDH1+ CSC subpopulations following cisplatin treatment. These cells were predominantly distributed at the tumor periphery and co-localized with regions characterized by low immune cell infiltration, suggesting that stemness and immune evasion act synergistically to promote resistance [ ]. Similarly, Li et al. performed scRNA-seq analysis on cervical cancer patient tumor tissues and identified subpopulations with markedly high ABCB1 expression. These cells were enriched in regions that continued proliferating after chemotherapy and showed co-upregulation of multiple efflux pumps (e.g., ABCC1, ABCG2), indicating that drug efflux mechanisms may represent dominant resistance pathways in certain patients. This study underscored the critical value of single-cell heterogeneity analyses in revealing patient-specific resistance mechanisms and provided molecular evidence for developing individualized strategies targeting efflux pumps [ ].
Second, CSCs are recognized as key contributors to therapeutic resistance in cervical cancer. Characterized by self-renewal and multipotent differentiation capabilities, CSCs exhibit substantial resistance to conventional therapies ( ). Studies have shown that CSCs enhance chemoradiotherapy resistance by upregulating multidrug resistance proteins (e.g., ABCB1), anti-apoptotic proteins (e.g., Bcl-2), and activating key signaling pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, and Hedgehog [ , ]. Targeting CSCs has emerged as a promising strategy for overcoming therapy resistance. A representative study involving intramedullary spinal cord tumors (IMSCTs) implemented a stem cell-based drug delivery approach. Maleimide-modified poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanoparticles (PLGA-MAL NPs) co-loaded with paclitaxel (PTX) and metformin (MET) were covalently attached to the surface of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) via thiol–maleimide chemistry. The modification achieved stable anchoring while preserving the intrinsic tumor-homing capability of MSCs. In vivo, PTX/MET-loaded PLGA-MAL NPs demonstrated effective tumor targeting, deep tissue penetration, and enhanced therapeutic efficacy in IMSCTs, with lower toxicity compared to single-agent treatments [ , ]. The MSC-based nanocarrier platform produced synergistic antitumor effects and offers a viable strategy for treating unresectable or recurrent solid tumors, with potential applications in advanced or drug-resistant cervical cancer.
 In addition, autophagy and metabolic reprogramming also play pivotal roles in cervical cancer resistance. Autophagy, as a protective cellular mechanism, enables survival under stress conditions. Studies have shown that cervical cancer cells enhance autophagy activity to remove chemotherapy-induced damage, thereby avoiding apoptosis [ ]. At the same time, metabolic reprogramming allows tumor cells to adapt to hostile microenvironments and increases drug tolerance [ ]. For instance, the upregulation of glycolysis and alterations in mitochondrial function have been closely linked to drug resistance [ ]. Therefore, regulating autophagy and metabolic pathways may provide novel strategies for overcoming cervical cancer resistance.

5.2.

## Strategies for NP-based intervention in drug resistance
The rapid advancement of nanotechnology has opened new possibilities for overcoming drug resistance in cervical cancer ( ) [ , ]. NPs, as drug carriers, possess unique physicochemical properties that enable efficient drug delivery and targeted therapy [ ]. First, NPs targeting ERα and ERβ are employed to regulate the expression of genes associated with drug resistance. Studies have shown that the abnormal expression of ERα and ERβ in cervical cancer cells is closely related to resistance. By loading siRNA or antisense oligonucleotides (ASO) onto NPs, the expression of ERα/ERβ can be specifically suppressed, thereby reversing drug resistance [ ]. For example, Zuo et al. designed a dual-functional nanocarrier that first downregulated P-glycoprotein (P-gp) expression via siRNA to reduce drug efflux, followed by precise release of paclitaxel via a bioorthogonal click reaction. This strategy effectively restored intracellular drug accumulation in multidrug-resistant (MDR) cervical cancer cells [ ]. Additionally, targeted therapy against ESR1 gene mutations has also demonstrated potential efficacy [ ].
 An innovative study introduced a supramolecular dendrimer-based nanosystem in which fluorinated amphiphilic dendrimers self-assembled into nanostructures for doxorubicin delivery. The system successfully addressed tumor heterogeneity and drug resistance in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, achieving precise antitumor effects both in vitro and in vivo [ ]. Similarly, in cervical cancer, a hyaluronic acid (HA)-modified intelligent nanocarrier combined with a bioorthogonal activation strategy has been developed to overcome multidrug resistance and CSC-mediated therapy tolerance. For instance, Kousar et al. designed HA–thiolated chitosan nanoparticles (HA-ThCs NPs) that specifically targeted CD44-overexpressing cervical cancer cells and provided sustained cisplatin release. In HeLa cells, these NPs demonstrated significantly greater cytotoxicity compared to free cisplatin and substantially reduced toxicity to normal cervical epithelial cells [ ].
Second, modulation of the TME represents a critical strategy by which NPs intervene in drug resistance. Abnormal angiogenesis and immunosuppressive conditions within the TME have been identified as critical contributors to resistance. NPs have been employed to deliver anti-angiogenic agents, such as bevacizumab, to inhibit the VEGF signaling pathway, reduce tumor vascularization, and enhance the efficacy of chemotherapeutic agents [ , ]. In addition, NPs have been utilized to deliver immunomodulators, such as PD-L1 inhibitors, to reverse immunosuppression and enhance the host’s anti-tumor immune response. These strategies demonstrated promising potential in improving the TME and overcoming drug resistance. Furthermore, exploring drug resistance from a three-dimensional genome perspective has offered new directions for target discovery. NP-based platforms that precisely modulate the TME and incorporate synergistic gene-editing tools provide diverse intervention strategies to overcome resistance [ ].
Finally, the multifunctional characteristics of NPs provide a unique advantage in combination therapies. By loading drugs with different mechanisms of action, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors and metabolic modulators, on a single NP platform, synergistic therapeutic effects can be achieved, thereby improving treatment efficacy [ ]. For example, NPs delivering indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) inhibitors can suppress tumor immune evasion and simultaneously enhance the effectiveness of co-administered therapies. Furthermore, NPs can deliver CRISPR/Cas9 gene-editing tools to directly modify resistance-related genes directly, fundamentally reversing drug resistance [ ]. These multifunctional strategies provide promising avenues for overcoming therapeutic resistance in cervical cancer.

## Clinical translation and future perspectives
6.1.

## Challenges in the clinical translation of nanodrugs
Nanodrugs have demonstrated significant potential in cancer treatment; however, their clinical translation still faces multiple challenges ( ). Major concerns include insufficient stability, limited targeting precision, and incomplete understanding of metabolic and clearance pathways in vivo. Currently, most evidence remains confined to in vitro or animal studies, with a lack of large-scale clinical data to validate efficacy and safety. Furthermore, large-scale production of nanocarriers may encounter challenges in maintaining manufacturing consistency, such as variations in particle size distribution, drug loading efficiency, and the stability of surface modifications, which limit their controllability and reproducibility in clinical applications. NPs may aggregate or dissociate in the body, impacting drug stability and efficacy. Achieving precise targeting of nanodrugs to pathological sites while minimizing off-target accumulation remains a major obstacle. The metabolic pathways and clearance mechanisms of nanodrugs have not been fully elucidated, and different nanomaterials may exhibit varying metabolic and clearance profiles in vivo. These factors necessitate more comprehensive safety assessments. To address delivery limitations, recent studies have explored metal-free click reaction system between trans-cyclooctene (TCO) and tetrazine. This bioorthogonal reaction demonstrates exceptionally fast kinetics, high specificity, and strong biocompatibility, enabling efficient conjugation even within complex physiological environments.
 Second, the safety and toxicity evaluation of nanodrugs is a pivotal step in advancing clinical translation. The unique physicochemical properties of nanomaterials may induce immune responses, cytotoxicity, or other adverse effects in the body. Traditional toxicity evaluation methods may not fully capture the potential risks associated with nanodrugs, underscoring the need for specialized assessment systems tailored to nanomedicines. Furthermore, safety profiles established in preclinical models may not accurately predict human responses, introducing uncertainty into clinical trials. Therefore, further research into the biodistribution, long-term toxicity, and immunogenicity of nanodrugs is essential to ensure both safety and translational success.
In clinical applications, ensuring the safety of bioorthogonal chemistry and intelligent nanocarriers relies on optimizing material properties and stringently controlling reaction conditions. High-biocompatibility and biodegradable nanomaterials should be prioritized to minimize in vivo residues and reduce the risk of adverse effects. Meanwhile, bioorthogonal reactions should be conducted under physiologically mild, catalyst-free, or low-toxicity conditions to achieve precise control without disrupting normal cellular functions. Comprehensive preclinical studies are essential to verify safety, including assessments of acute and chronic toxicity, immunogenicity, tissue distribution, and long-term risks of in vivo reactions, thereby providing critical data support for subsequent clinical applications.

6.2.

## Future research directions
To overcome the challenges outlined above, future research should focus on developing multifunctional nanocarriers. These carriers have the potential to simultaneously deliver genes, proteins, and small-molecule drugs, enabling multi-target and multi-pathway combination therapies. For instance, co-loading chemotherapeutic agents and gene-silencing sequences within a single nanocarrier can concurrently suppress tumor proliferation and inhibit the expression of resistance-related genes, thereby enhancing therapeutic outcomes. Surface modification with specific ligands can further improve their targeting ability to tumor cells while minimizing off-target effects on healthy tissues. Developing such multifunctional nanocarriers holds promise for improving the therapeutic index of drugs while reducing side effects.
Optimizing combination therapies is another key focus for future research. Nanodrugs can be combined with traditional immunotherapy and radiotherapy to achieve synergistic effects. For example, nanodrugs with immune checkpoint inhibitors can enhance the body’s anti-tumor immune response, improving overall treatment efficacy. Furthermore, nanocarriers can be designed to respond to external stimuli (such as light, heat, or magnetic fields), allowing for spatiotemporal control of drug release. When combined with radiotherapy and other modalities, this approach has the potential to achieve superior therapeutic outcomes. Multi-modal combination strategies represent a promising avenue for overcoming the limitations of monotherapies and enhancing the overall effectiveness of cancer treatment.
Looking forward, intelligent nanocarriers based on bioorthogonal chemistry combined with immunotherapy or radiotherapy show significant potential for synergistic treatment and clinical translation. These nanoplatforms possess several unique advantages: Passive targeting can be achieved through their nanoscale architecture, which exploits the enhanced permeability and retention effect of tumor tissues to enable selective accumulation at tumor sites. Stimuli-responsive release allows integration of tumor-associated triggers such as pH, enzymes, or redox conditions for precise and controllable drug release. Active targeting is enhanced by surface modification with ligands such as peptides, small molecules, or antibody fragments further enhances tumor selectivity. In situ activation enables therapeutic molecules to be activated at defined sites in vivo by exploiting the high specificity of bioorthogonal chemistry, effectively reducing systemic toxicity. When combined with immunotherapy, these intelligent carriers can deliver immune adjuvants or drugs that induce immunogenic cell death, thereby enhancing tumor antigen exposure and immune activation. In the context of radiotherapy, they can carry radiosensitizers or modulate oxidative stress to improve radiosensitivity and reduce damage to normal tissues. Overall, this strategy enhances therapeutic selectivity and efficacy and provides a crucial technological pathway for overcoming drug resistance, optimizing pharmacokinetics, and constructing multimodal therapeutic platforms, demonstrating promising prospects for clinical application.
Lastly, artificial intelligence (AI)-based design and optimization of nanodrugs is emerging as a promising research area. AI technologies can process and analyze large-scale biomedical datasets to predict the physicochemical properties, biocompatibility, and in vivo behavior of nanodrugs, guiding the design and optimization of nanocarriers. For example, machine learning algorithms can identify nanomaterial combinations with optimal stability and targeting ability, accelerating the development of new nanodrugs. Additionally, AI-based modeling can simulate the biodistribution and metabolic pathways of nanodrugs, enabling early prediction of efficacy and toxicity profiles and providing critical insights for preclinical evaluation. The integration of AI into nanodrug development holds great potential for improving design efficiency and increasing translational success rates.
In conclusion, although significant barriers remain in the clinical translation of nanodrugs, future efforts focusing on multifunctional nanocarrier construction, optimization of combinatorial therapeutic strategies, and incorporation of AI-driven methodologies are expected to address these challenges. Continued advancement in these areas may broaden the clinical utility of nanodrugs and contribute to the development of more effective and safer cancer therapies.

7.

## Conclusion
In recent years, significant advancements have been achieved in the application of bioorthogonal chemistry reactions, intelligent nanocarriers, and estrogen antagonists in anti-cancer therapy. These approaches have shown tremendous potential in overcoming drug resistance in cervical cancer. Bioorthogonal chemistry, characterized by high selectivity, biocompatibility, and mild reaction conditions, has introduced innovative approaches for drug delivery and tumor-targeted treatment. Specifically, bioorthogonal methods such as the Tz-TCO reaction and azide-alkyne cycloaddition enable site-specific drug activation in specific TME, significantly enhancing therapeutic efficacy. Intelligent nanocarriers, owing to their unique EPR effect, efficient, targeted delivery capabilities, and controllable release properties, have significantly optimized the therapeutic window of anti-cancer drugs. The precise delivery of estrogen antagonists can be achieved by functionalizing the NPs with ER (ERα/ERβ)-specific ligands (such as antibodies or small molecules). Targeted inhibition of estrogen signaling in cervical cancer cells reduces resistance to both endocrine and cytotoxic therapies. The present study highlights the synergistic potential of integrating bioorthogonal chemistry, intelligent nanoplatforms, and estrogen antagonists to overcome therapeutic resistance, enhance treatment efficacy, and advance precision oncology.
Despite the promising prospects of intelligent NPs and bioorthogonal chemistry in treating cervical cancer, several challenges hinder clinical translation. Optimization of NP stability in vivo remains essential to ensure sustained bioactivity in complex physiological environments and efficient accumulation at tumor sites. Systematic evaluation of immunocompatibility and long-term toxicity is required to minimize the risk of adverse immune responses and prevent harmful retention in normal tissues. Industrial-scale production also faces significant constraints, including limited reproducibility of manufacturing processes, the absence of quality control standards, and challenges in designing personalized delivery platforms. Future research should focus on optimizing multifunctional nanocarriers, incorporating strategies such as gene delivery, immunomodulation, and targeted therapy to enhance the precision and efficacy of NP-based drugs. Furthermore, applying AI and big data analytics in NP structure optimization targeted delivery path prediction and the development of personalized treatment plans is expected to accelerate translational advancement.
Intelligent nanocarriers, characterized by superior targeting ability, controlled release, and programmable responsiveness, demonstrate immense potential in combination cancer therapy. Research focus has progressively shifted from single-drug delivery toward the construction of multimodal therapeutic platforms, aiming to enhance treatment efficacy, reduce the risk of drug resistance, and bridge the gap between basic research and clinical application. Conventional chemotherapeutics, including cisplatin and paclitaxel, remain limited by systemic toxicity and poor tumor selectivity, often leading to severe side effects and resistance. Intelligent nanocarriers can achieve precise drug release within the TME through tumor-targeted delivery and pH- or enzyme-sensitive mechanisms, thereby reducing damage to normal tissues, improving the therapeutic efficacy of chemotherapy, and delaying the onset of resistance. In combination with immunotherapy, the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-1/PD-L1) in cervical cancer remains limited, partly due to the strongly immunosuppressive TME. Intelligent nanocarriers can be designed to deliver immune adjuvants (such as CpG oligonucleotides or STING agonists) or RNA interference molecules to remodel the TME, activate dendritic cells and T cells, and promote tumor antigen release. This strategy significantly enhances antitumor immune responses, thereby improving the response rates and therapeutic outcomes of immunotherapy. Furthermore, certain nanoplatforms possess integrated multifunctionality, enabling the co-delivery of chemotherapeutic agents, radiosensitizers, and immunomodulators within a single carrier to achieve “all-in-one multimodal synergistic therapy.” This approach not only simplifies treatment regimens but also improves overall therapeutic efficacy and provides an engineered foundation for personalized treatment. Favorable safety profiles and antitumor efficacy have been demonstrated in preclinical models, offering compelling support for future translational advancement.
Looking ahead, the combination of nanotechnology and bioorthogonal chemistry offers innovative strategies for overcoming drug resistance in cervical cancer. Efficient and precise drug delivery systems can be developed through the engineered design of intelligent NPs and the high selectivity of bioorthogonal coupling techniques, allowing for targeted release, reduced systemic toxicity, and enhanced therapeutic efficacy. Moreover, combining NP-based drugs with immunotherapy and gene editing technologies, such as the nano-delivery of the CRISPR/Cas9 system or the synergistic application of immune checkpoint inhibitors, holds potential for overcoming TME–mediated resistance and improving long-term survival in patients with cervical cancer. Future studies should aim to optimize the intelligent responsiveness of NPs, explore their combined use in multi-modal therapies, and promote the advancement of personalized precision treatment. Continued progress in fundamental research and clinical evaluation is expected to support the clinical translation of bioorthogonal chemistry and intelligent nanoparticle platforms, providing more effective and safer treatment options for patients.
