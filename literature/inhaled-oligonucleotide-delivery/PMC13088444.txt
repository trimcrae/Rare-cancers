## Abstract

## Background
Light-emitting diode (LED) therapy is a promising non-invasive approach for tendinopathy, yet clinical adoption is hindered by heterogeneous treatment parameters and undefined molecular mechanisms. The role of the Interleukin (IL)-6/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3(STAT3) signaling axis in LED-mediated tendon repair remains unexplored.

## Objective
This study aimed to screen effective LED parameters for mitigating tendinopathy by evaluating their effects on inflammation and extracellular matrix (ECM) synthesis in tenocytes, to preliminarily explore the role of the IL-6/STAT3 axis, and to validate the anti-inflammatory and pro-repair effects of selected parameters in a rodent model.

## Methods
Tenocytes from 8 week-old male Sprague-Dawley rat’s Achilles’s tendon were cultured and stimulated with IL-1β to model inflammatory tendinopathy. Cells underwent LED irradiation at varying wavelengths (625 nm, 810 nm, 940 nm) and energy densities. Cell viability was assessed via CCK-8 assay; expression of inflammatory markers (IL-6, Substance P (SP)), ECM components (type I collagen (COL1), type III collagen (COL3), and lubricin (PRG4) was quantified using qPCR, Western blot, and immunofluorescence to determine optimal LED parameters. STAT3 inhibition was applied to IL-1β-stimulated cells before LED treatment to probe STAT3’s role. In vivo, collagenase-I was injected into rat Achilles tendons to induce tendinopathy, followed by LED therapy. Pain thresholds were measured using von Frey filaments, tendon histopathology was evaluated via Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining and modified Bonar scoring, and expression of COL1, COL3, IL-6, SP, and PRG4 was assessed by immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry.

## Results
LED irradiation at 625 nm/52.8 J/cm2 markedly reduced IL-1β-induced IL-6 and SP expression, while 810 nm/39.6 J/cm2 and 940 nm/26.4 J/cm2 enhanced COL1 synthesis without affecting COL3 or PRG4 levels. Mechanistically, LED therapy restored IL-1β-suppressed STAT3 activation, an effect abrogated by STAT3 inhibition. In vivo, 625 nm and 940 nm LED treatments alleviated mechanical hyperalgesia in collagenase-I-induced tendinopathy, with 625 nm showing superior efficacy in reducing IL-6 and SP, enhancing COL1 deposition, and restoring tendon architecture.

## Conclusions
IL-1β disrupts tendon homeostasis by driving inflammation and ECM degradation. LED therapy at 625 nm/52.8 J/cm2 mitigates these effects, potentially by restoring STAT3 activity within the IL-6/STAT3 axis, thereby suppressing IL-6 expression.This provides a mechanistic foundation for refining LED-based tendinopathy treatments.

## Introduction
Tendinopathy, a degenerative condition affecting the tendon extracellular matrix (ECM) and resident tenocytes, represents a major musculoskeletal burden worldwide, contributing to chronic pain, functional impairment, and substantial healthcare costs. With an estimated prevalence of 30% in musculoskeletal consultations and over a million tendon-related injuries annually among athletes, tendinopathies-such as those of the Achilles, rotator cuff, and patellar tendons-impose a profound socioeconomic toll, accounting for millions of lost workdays annually [ , ]. Pathologically, tendinopathy arises from a dysregulated interplay of mechanical overload, oxidative stress, and inflammatory cascades, culminating in ECM disorganization, tenocyte apoptosis, and aberrant neovascularization [ – ]. Conventional treatments, including eccentric exercise, platelet-rich plasma injections, and corticosteroid therapies, offer symptomatic relief but often fail to address underlying molecular dysregulation, with recurrence rates as high as 49% and limited efficacy in advanced stages [ ]. These shortcomings underscore the urgent need for non-invasive, mechanism-targeted interventions that restore tendon homeostasis without exacerbating tissue damage.
Emerging as a paradigm-shifting modality, photobiomodulation (PBM) therapy—particularly via light-emitting diodes (LED)-harnesses low-level light to modulate cellular bioenergetics and signaling, offering a safe, accessible alternative to invasive procedures [ ]. By penetrating tissues at depths of millimeters to centimeters, LED irradiation stimulates mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase, elevating adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, reducing reactive oxygen species, and activating transcription factors that govern repair processes [ ]. Preclinical and clinical evidence supports PBM’s anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative effects in diverse pathologies, from wound healing to osteoarthritis, with meta-analyses indicating up to 30% improvements in pain scores and functional outcomes in musculoskeletal disorders [ , ]. In tendinopathy, pilot studies have reported enhanced collagen deposition and pain alleviation following red/near-infrared LED exposure, yet translation to routine clinical use remains stymied by heterogeneous protocols [ ]. Disparities in wavelength (typically 600–1000 nm), energy density (1–100 J/cm²), and dosing regimens yield inconsistent results, often attributable to suboptimal penetration or off-target effects [ ]. Moreover, the mechanistic underpinnings-beyond generic anti-inflammatory actions remain opaque, impeding parameter optimization and personalized application. Elucidating how specific LED configurations interface with tendon-specific pathways is thus critical to harnessing LED’s full therapeutic potential.
At the cellular level, tendinopathy is orchestrated by pro-inflammatory cytokines that perpetuate a vicious cycle of ECM degradation and sensory neuron sensitization [ , , ]. Interleukin-1β (IL-1β), a sentinel mediator released by infiltrating macrophages and stressed tendon cells, inhibits tenogenic differentiation of tendon-derived progenitor cells, upregulates matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and suppresses anabolic factors, driving collagen fibril disarray and loss of tensile strength [ – ]. Critically, IL-1β induces IL-6 secretion in tenocytes, which may dysregulate the Janus kinase (JAK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) pathway. This dysregulation often manifests as suppressed STAT3 phosphorylation, contributing to an imbalance in the type I/type III collagen (COL1/COL3) ratio—a hallmark of ECM disorganization [ , ]. Within this axis, STAT3 activation is a pivotal event, as it can translocate to the nucleus to regulate target gene expression, including those involved in inflammation and repair. Paradoxically, STAT3 also exerts context-dependent anabolic roles, including lubricin (proteoglycan 4(PRG4)) expression, a glycoprotein critical for boundary lubrication and friction reduction at the tendon enthesis [ – ]. Dysregulated IL-6/STAT3 signaling further amplifies nociception via substance P (SP), a neuropeptide that sensitizes transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1)-expressing afferents, manifesting as mechanical hyperalgesia [ ]. In human tendinopathy biopsies, elevated IL-6 levels are observed alongside dysregulated phospho-STAT3, correlating with disease severity [ ], yet the precise modulation of this axis by IL-1β in tenocytes and potential therapeutic interventions like LED remain unexplored.
To address these gaps, our study aimed to screen effective LED parameters for tendinopathy therapy and to conduct a preliminary exploration of their effects on IL-1β-induced inflammation, ECM remodeling, lubricin (PRG4) secretion, and pain modulation, with a particular focus on understanding the potential role of STAT3 within the IL-6/STAT3 signaling axis. To achieve this, we employed a multifaceted preclinical approach: in vitro, primary tenocytes isolated from rat Achilles tendons were stimulated with IL-1β and subjected to LED exposure at varied wavelengths and energy densities, followed by STAT3 inhibition experiments to dissect pathway dependency. Complementarily, an in vivo rat model of collagenase-I-induced Achilles tendinopathy was established, with LED treatments applied post-injury. Through these integrated models, we seek to define wavelength- and dose-specific LED signatures that restore tendon homeostasis via STAT3-mediated recalibration of inflammatory and regenerative processes.

## Methods

## Animals
All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Male Sprague-Dawley(SD) rats (6 weeks old, 250–300 g) were obtained from a certified vendor and housed in a controlled environment (12 h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 2 °C, 50–60% humidity) with ad libitum access to standard chow and water. Achilles tendons were harvested from rats euthanized by CO2 inhalation (displacement rate of 30–70% chamber volume per minute, followed by secondary cervical dislocation to confirm death) [ ].

## The isolation and culture of tenocytes
Primary tenocytes were isolated from rat Achilles tendons using the tissue explant method. Briefly, tendons were dissected free of paratenon and surrounding connective tissue, minced into 1–2 mm3 fragments, and placed in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco). Explants were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator, with media changes every 2–3 days. Migrating tenocytes were passaged at 80–90% confluence (passages 3–5) using 0.05% trypsin–ethylene diamine tetra-acetic acid (EDTA) (Gibco) and seeded at 5 × 104 cells/well in 12-well plates for experiments (Fig. A–C). Experiments were conducted using cells from passages 3–5.Tenocyte identity was confirmed by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) for tenomodulin (TNMD) [ ] (Fig. D).
 

## Establishment of in vitro tendinopathy model
As Fig. A showed, to establish an inflammatory tendinopathy model, confluent tenocytes were tenocytes were cultured in DMEM with 1% FBS for 48 h and stimulated with recombinant rat IL-1β (I2393, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) at concentrations of 5, 10, or 20 ng/mL for 24 h [ ]. Cytotoxicity was assessed using the cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Gene expression of inflammatory factor (IL-6), extracellular matrix components (COL1, COL3) and lubricin (PRG4) was quantified by qPCR to evaluate model efficacy.

## In vitro tendinopathy model and LED treatment
Tenocytes were cultured in DMEM with 1% FBS for 48 h, then stimulated with optimized recombinant rat IL-1β for 24 h to induce inflammatory tendinopathy. To deliver LED irradiation, the LED light source (M625L4, M810L5, M940L3; Thorlabs, Newton, NJ, USA) was coupled to an adjustable collimating optic (SM1U25-B; Thorlabs) and mounted on a stable stand. The optic was fine-tuned to uniformly illuminate a single well of a 12-well plate. Irradiance was calibrated by placing a power meter 12 cm beneath the LED, with the LED driver (DC2200; Thorlabs) adjusted to achieve 44 mW/cm2 power density [ ]. Transparent 12-well plates containing cells were positioned directly under the beam, maintaining a 12 cm separation between the LED and the plate bottom. Cells were subsequently irradiated using this LED device delivering monochromatic light at 625 nm (red), 810 nm (near-infrared), or 940 nm (infrared) wavelengths. Energy densities were titrated as 2.64, 5.28, 13.2, 26.4, 39.6, and 52.8 J/cm2, calculated from irradiance (e.g., 44 mW/cm2 for 625 nm, yielding ~ 20 min exposure for 52.8 J/cm2). The sham-irradiated controls exposed to the device without light emission. Post-irradiation, cells were incubated for 24 h before harvest and analysis. The protocol was showed in Fig. A, B.
 

## STAT3 Inhibition
To interrogate STAT3 dependency, tenocytes were stimulated with optimized IL-1β for 24 h, followed by pretreatment with Stattic (HY-13818; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA), a selective STAT3 inhibitor, for 2 h prior to LED irradiation [ ] (Fig. D). Inhibitor concentration was optimized via dose-response (1, 2.5 µM) to achieve significant reduction in phosphor-STAT3 (p-STAT3) without cytotoxicity, as assessed by CCK8 (Fig. A, B, C).
 

## Cell viability assay
Viability was quantified using the CCK-8 (HY-K0301; MedChemExpress) per manufacturer’s instructions [ ]. Post-treatment tenocytes (5 × 103 cells/well in 96-well plates) were incubated with 10 µL CCK-8 reagent in 90 µL DMEM for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Data were normalized to untreated controls and expressed as percentage viability.

## qPCR
Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (9109; Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) and reverse-transcribed with PrimeScript RT Master Mix (RR036A; Takara Bio). The reverse reactions were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min followed by inactivation at 85 °C for 5s. qPCR was performed on a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (12011319; Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ (RR420A; Takara Bio) .Cycles of 95 °C for 30s, followed by 39 cycles of 95 °C for 5s and 60 °C for 30s, were employed as the cycling conditions. Primers are showed in Table  . Relative expression was calculated via the 2^−ΔΔCt method.
 

## Western blot
Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (R0020; Solarbio, Beijing, China) with protease/phosphatase inhibitors (P1261; Solarbio). Proteins were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Merck KgaA, Darmstadt, Germany), and probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-COL1 (ab270993; Abcam, Cambridge, UK, 1:1000), anti-COL3 (ab184993; Abcam, 1:1000), anti-lubricin (bs-11175R, Bioss, Beijing, China, 1:1000), anti-p-STAT3 (9145; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA, 1:1000), anti-STAT3 (9139; Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), and anti-β-actin (AF5003; Beyotime, Shanghai, China, 1:5000). Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (A0208, A0216; Beyotime, 1:5000) were applied, followed by enhanced chemiluminescence detection (PE0010; Solarbio). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) and normalized to β-actin.

## In vivo tendinopathy model and LED treatment
A total of 24 male SD rats were randomly assigned to 6 groups (4 rats/group). Rats were then anesthetized via intramuscular injection of ketamine (75 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg; Henry Schein, Melville, NY, USA). Preoperatively, analgesia was provided with intramuscular buprenorphine (Temgesic; 0.05 mg/kg; Reckitt Benckiser, Hull, UK). Briefly, following anesthesia, fur on the right dorsal heel was shaved, and the Achilles tendon location was identified. A longitudinal incision (~ 1 cm) was made at the dorsal heel, and 30 µL of collagenase (3 mg/mL; C6885; Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in PBS was injected into the central portion of the Achilles tendon using a 27-gauge needle. The incision was closed with interrupted sutures using 4−0 Prolene. Control rats received an equivalent volume of sterile saline injection [ , ]. Postoperatively, rats were administered antibiotics daily for 3 days to prevent infection. Starting on day 3 post-injection, rats received daily LED irradiation positioned above the Achilles tendon for 13 days at optimal parameters identified in vitro. Rats were secured in a custom restraint holder to ensure consistent positioning, with the irradiation field encompassing the entire heel region. Sham LED groups underwent identical handling and probe placement without light output. The protocol of experiment timeline was showed in Fig. A, B.
 

## Mechanical pain threshold assessment
To evaluate mechanical allodynia on day 3, 10 and 17 after collagenase injection, rats were habituated to solitary acrylic enclosures (12 × 30 × 31 cm) for 30 min in a dimly lit, sound-attenuated chamber one day before behavioral assessments. On the day of testing, animals were placed in isolated compartments atop a metal mesh floor for an additional 30 min acclimation period, allowing cessation of locomotor activity before stimulus application. Paw withdrawal thresholds were quantified using an electronic von Frey aesthesiometer (38450; Ugo Basile, Gemonio, Italy), featuring a rigid nitinol-tipped probe attached to a digital force sensor. The probe was advanced orthogonally against the plantar surface of the ipsilateral hind paw with gradually escalating pressure until eliciting a withdrawal, flinch, or lick response, which was scored as the endpoint. Activation of the foot pedal by the experimenter upon response detection triggered automatic logging of the maximum force exerted (in grams). Trials were repeated three times per animal, separated by 10-min intervals, and the mean value across these measurements served as the definitive threshold for each rat [ ].

## Histological analysis
On day 18, Achilles tendons were excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099; Beyotime) for 24 h, and paraffin-embedded. Longitudinal serial Sect. (5 μm thick) were cut at a site 2 mm proximal to the tendon-bone interface, parallel to the Achilles tendon axis, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (C0105S; Beyotime). Tendon histopathology was evaluated semiquantitatively using a modified Bonar scoring scheme. For each specimen, three independent slides were prepared, from which five random microscopic fields per slide were blindly scored by a single observer to gauge overall tendon architecture. This adapted grading rubric assigned scores from 0 to 3 across six histopathological features-fiber alignment and organization, cellularity and morphology, inflammatory cell infiltration, vascular proliferation, fatty infiltration, and proteoglycan content-yielding a cumulative range of 0 (histologically normal tendon) to 18 (severely degenerated tendon) [ , ] .

## Immunofluorescence
Immunofluorescence staining was performed on cultured tenocytes and paraffin-embedded tendon tissue Sect. (5 µm thick) using primary antibodies (anti-COL1 (ab270993; Abcam), anti-COL3 (ab184993; Abcam), anti-IL-6 (SAB5700632; Sigma-Aldrich), anti-SP (PA5-106934; Thermo Fisher Scientific); diluted 1:100). For tenocytes on coverslips, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099; Beyotime) for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (ST797; Solarbio) for 10 min, and blocked with 5% goat serum (SL038; Solarbio) for 1 h. For tissue sections, samples were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, antigen-retrieved in citrate buffer (ST797; Solarbio) in a 62 °C water bath for 12 h, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10min (Solarbio), and blocked with goat serum (Solarbio). Samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, washed thrice in PBS, and treated with species-appropriate secondary antibodies (FITC-conjugated (A0562, A0568; Beyotime, 1:500) for cells and sections) for 1 h at room temperature. Following additional PBS washes, all samples were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (F6057; Sigma-Aldrich) and mounted in antifade medium. Fluorescence images (five random fields per sample) were acquired using a Nikon Ti2-E inverted microscope (Nikon Instruments Inc., Melville, NY, USA) with appropriate filters by a researcher who was blinded to the treatment group allocations. Signal quantification was performed using ImageJ software to calculate mean fluorescence intensity within regions of interest.

## Immunohistochemistry
Paraffin sections underwent deparaffinization, antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (C1032; Solarbio) in a 62 °C water bath for 12 h, endogenous peroxidase quenching with 3% H₂O₂, and blocking with 5% goat serum (SL038; Solarbio). Primary antibody of anti-lubricin (MABT401; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:100) were applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by biotinylated secondary antibodies for 1 h (A0208; Beyotime 1:500). Diaminobenzidine (DAB) (P0203; Beyotime) chromogen was used for visualization, with hematoxylin (C0105S; Beyotime) counterstain. Microscopic images from five randomly selected fields per sample were captured using a Nikon Ti2-E inverted microscope equipped with suitable filter sets. Image acquisition was performed by a researcher who was blinded to the treatment group allocations. Staining mean intensity was quantified with ImageJ software.

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent experiments (n ≥ 3 biological replicates). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For multiple-group comparisons, parametric one-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) post-hoc test was applied if data were normally distributed. For this exploratory parameter-screening study, Fisher’s LSD test was chosen following a significant ANOVA to balance the risks of Type I and Type II errors, with the principal aim of identifying candidate treatment parameters for future investigation. Otherwise, non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test (for unpaired data) or Friedman test (for paired data) was used, with Dunn’s post-hoc correction for multiple comparisons. Pain threshold data underwent repeated-measures ANOVA with Fisher’s LSD post-hoc test. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05.

## Results

## IL-1β induces an inflammatory tendinopathy model in rat tenocyte
Primary rat Achilles tenocytes, confirmed by qPCR detection of TNMD expression (Fig.  D), were exposed to IL-1β at 5, 10, or 20 ng/mL for 24 h to validate the in vitro tendinopathy model. Cell viability exceeded 90% across all doses, as assessed by CCK-8 assay (Fig.  E). qPCR analysis revealed dose-dependent upregulation of the inflammatory marker IL-6, with all concentrations inducing significant elevation relative to controls (P < 0.001); notably, 20 ng/mL elicited the most pronounced increase compared to 5 and 10 ng/mL (P < 0.01). At this highest dose, IL-6 expression was accompanied by marked suppression of both COL1 (P < 0.01) and COL3 (P < 0.05), signifying ECM dysregulation, whereas PRG4 levels remained unaffected (P > 0.05) (Fig.  F).

## Parameters-specific effects of LED on inflammation, ECM remodeling, and lubricin secretion in rat tenocytes
IL-1β-stimulated tenocytes tolerated LED exposure across all wavelengths (625 nm, 810 nm, 940 nm) and energy densities (2.64–52.8 J/cm2), with no significant changes in viability relative to sham controls (Fig.  C). Specific energy densities exerted distinct effects on inflammation and ECM remodeling. For inflammation, 52.8 J/cm2 at 625 nm significantly reduced IL-6 mRNA compared to IL-1β alone (P < 0.05). For ECM remodeling, 39.6 J/cm2 at 810 nm (P < 0.01) and 26.4 J/cm2 at 940 nm (P < 0.05) significantly elevated COL1 expression relative to IL-1β alone, without altering COL3 levels (P > 0.05 vs. IL-1β alone); similarly, no parameters affected PRG4 expression (P > 0.05 vs. IL-1β alone) (Fig.  D, E, F).
Wavelengths differentially modulated these processes. For inflammation, qPCR revealed that only 625 nm significantly reduced IL-6 mRNA levels compared to IL-1β alone (P < 0.05), with no differences among wavelengths (P > 0.05). Immunofluorescence confirmed that 625 nm significantly decreased IL-6 (P < 0.05) and SP (P < 0.01) intensity relative to IL-1β, whereas 810 nm had no effect (P > 0.05 vs. IL-1β alone or control) and 940 nm reduced SP but not IL-6 (P < 0.05 for SP; P > 0.05 for IL-6). For ECM remodeling, qPCR showed that 810 nm (P < 0.05) and 940 nm (P < 0.001) both significantly elevated COL1 expression compared to IL-1β alone, with 940 nm further exceeding 625 nm (P < 0.05); Immunofluorescence and Western blot corroborated 940 nm’s enhancement of COL1 intensity and protein (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01 vs. IL-1β alone), while 810 nm and 625 nm showed no significant changes (P > 0.05). No wavelengths altered COL3 and Lubricin expression (P > 0.05 vs. IL-1β alone; Fig.  ).
 

## LED alleviates mechanical hyperalgesia in collagenase-induced tendinopathy
Based on in vitro findings, two wavelengths (625 nm and 940 nm) and two energy densities (26.4 J/cm2 and 52.8 J/cm2) were selected for in vivo LED intervention, initiated on day 3 post-collagenase injection and administered daily for 13 days. On day 3, paw withdrawal thresholds were significantly reduced in both collagenase-only and LED-treated groups compared to saline controls (P < 0.001 or P < 0.01; Fig.  C). On day 10, all LED regimens (625 nm at 26.4 and 52.8 J/cm2; 940 nm at 26.4 and 52.8 J/cm2) significantly elevated thresholds relative to collagenase alone (P < 0.001, P < 0.01 or P < 0.05). By day 17, thresholds remained significantly higher with 625 nm (26.4 and 52.8 J/cm2) and 940 nm (52.8 J/cm2) compared to collagenase (P < 0.01), whereas 940 nm at 26.4 J/cm2 showed no difference (P > 0.05). Compared to pre-treatment baselines, 625 nm at 52.8 J/cm2 and 940 nm at 26.4 J/cm2 significantly increased thresholds at both 7 (P < 0.001 or P < 0.01) and 14 days post-treatment (P < 0.05), 940 nm at 52.8 J/cm2 did so at 14 days (P < 0.05), and 625 nm at 26.4 J/cm² had no effect (P > 0.05). Sham irradiation yielded no improvements (P > 0.05 vs. collagenase).

## LED improves tendon histopathology and molecular markers in vivo
Collagenase injection (Tendinopathy(T) groups) induced pronounced histopathological alterations, including disrupted fiber arrangement, increased cell density scores (indicative of pathological proliferation or clustering), abnormal cell appearance (e.g., rounded/chondroid morphology), heightened inflammatory infiltration, elevated vascularity, and fat deposition, yielding significantly higher Bonar subcategory and total scores compared to saline controls (P < 0.01 or P < 0.001). Among LED treatments, 625 nm at 52.8 J/cm2 significantly lowered fiber arrangement and cell density scores compared to collagenase alone (P < 0.05 each) and further outperformed 940 nm at 26.4 J/cm2 in cell density (P < 0.05); additionally, this regimen achieved the lowest total Bonar score relative to collagenase and 940 nm at 26.4 J/cm2 (P < 0.05 vs. T + Sham LED; P < 0.05 vs. 940 nm at 26.4 J/cm2), while overall 940 nm regimens exerted no significant effect on total Bonar score (P > 0.05 vs. T + Sham LED). In parallel, 940 nm at 52.8 J/cm2 significantly reduced fat deposition compared to collagenase alone (P < 0.05), with no additional inter-LED differences observed (P > 0.05). H&E staining visually corroborated these improvements, highlighting restored fiber alignment, normalized cellularity and morphology, and reduced adiposity in LED-treated tendons, particularly with 625 nm at 52.8 J/cm² (Fig.  E, arrows denote disorganized/hyperplastic cells, vascularity and fatty infiltrates in T + Sham LED vs. ameliorated architecture in LED groups).
Immunofluorescence showed IL-6 and SP intensity were significant increased (P < 0.01), COL1 intensity significantly decreased(P < 0.01) and with no change on COL3 intensity after collagenase injection. 625 nm at 52.8 J/cm² significantly decreased IL-6 and SP intensity (P < 0.05), respectively, while enhancing COL1 (P < 0.01) without COL3 changes (Fig.  D–G) compare with the other group (Fig.  A). Immunohistochemistry confirmed lubricin secretion remained unaltered in every groups (P > 0.05) (Fig.  B).

## LED restores IL-1β-suppressed STAT3 activation
Stattic dose-response assays revealed dose-dependent cytotoxicity, with both 1 µM and 2.5 µM significantly reducing tenocyte viability at 2 h relative to controls (P < 0.05 each), though 2.5 µM exerted a more pronounced effect and induced widespread cell death at 24 h (Fig.  A, B), prompting selection of 1 µM for subsequent experiments. Western blot in Fig.  C validated that both concentrations of Stattic significantly inhibited p-STAT3 (Tyr705) phosphorylation relative to controls (P < 0.01 or P < 0.001) without altering total STAT3, confirming effective pathway blockade based on the viability outcomes (Fig.  C). CCK-8 assays confirmed that Stattic significantly reduced viability regardless of LED co-treatment (P < 0.01/ P < 0.05 vs. IL-1β or IL-1β + LED; Fig.  E). qPCR analysis indicated that LED treatment significantly decreased IL-6 mRNA expression compared to both IL-1β and IL-1β + Stattic groups (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01; Fig.  F), whereas the IL-1β + Stattic + LED combination had no effect on IL-6 (P > 0.05 vs. IL-1β + Stattic), suggesting that Stattic blocks LED’s suppressive effect on IL-6; in contrast, LED with or without Stattic had no significant influence on COL1 mRNA (P > 0.05 vs. IL-1β) (Fig.  F). Western blot further demonstrated that IL-1β markedly suppressed p-STAT3 levels relative to controls (P < 0.01), while LED-treated groups showed p-STAT3 levels comparable to controls (P > 0.05 vs. control), indicating reversal by LED (625 nm/52.8 J/cm2). However, Stattic addition, with or without LED, significantly inhibited p-STAT3 relative to IL-1β or IL-1β + LED alone (P < 0.05 each). Additionally, COL1 protein expression was significantly reduced in all non-control groups compared to controls (P < 0.001 each), with no inter-group differences observed (P > 0.05) (Fig.  G). Immunofluorescence revealed that IL-1β decreased COL1 intensity while increasing IL-6 and SP relative to controls (P < 0.01 each); LED alone significantly lowered IL-6 intensity (P < 0.01 vs. IL-1β), but Stattic co-treatment had no effect on COL1 regardless of LED (P > 0.05 vs. IL-1β), and blocked its effect on IL-6 (P > 0.05 vs. IL-1β) (Figs.  H, ).
 

## Discussion
This study systematically screened wavelength- and dose-specific LED parameters for tendinopathy and explored a potential mechanistic link between the anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative benefits of LED therapy to recalibration of the IL-6/STAT3 axis, thereby addressing two major translational barriers: parameter heterogeneity and unclear molecular targets. Using complementary in vitro IL-1β-driven tenocyte inflammation and in vivo collagenase-induced Achilles tendinopathy, we recapitulated hallmark disease features-increased inflammatory factors, ECM imbalance, architectural disarray, and mechanical hyperalgesia-enhancing the external validity of our screening framework. The principal findings are: (i) Exposure to 625 nm/52.8 J/cm2 significantly suppresses IL-6 and SP in vitro and in vivo, improves pain thresholds, and lowers histopathological scores, defining a red-light anti-inflammatory/antinociceptive window; (ii) Exposure to 810 nm/39.6 J/cm2 and 940 nm/26.4 J/cm2 selectively enhance COL1 without altering COL3 or lubricin (PRG4), indicating near-infrared/infrared bias toward anabolic matrix restoration; and (iii) LED rescues IL-1β-suppressed STAT3 phosphorylation, while pharmacological STAT3 inhibition abrogates LED’s IL-6-suppressive effect, suggesting that STAT3 activation may be a critical upstream event in LED-mediated immunomodulation.
The IL-1β model faithfully emulated early tendinopathic inflammation, with dose-dependent IL-6 surges and COL1/COL3 disequilibrium, preserving viability-hallmarks of cytokine-orchestrated tenocyte dysfunction [ – ]. This threshold optimally captured IL-1β’s suppression of ECM without apoptotic artifacts at higher doses, mirroring human biopsies where IL-1β correlates with tendon degenerative progression and MMP-1/3 overexpression [ , ]. By validating TNMD expression via qPCR, we confirmed tenocyte purity, mitigating fibroblast contamination biases in explant cultures. This model not only benchmarked LED’s anti-inflammatory potency but also highlighted PRG4’s relative resilience, suggesting lubrication deficits may manifest later in fibrotic cascades [ ]. It is acknowledged that this model primarily reflects acute inflammatory signaling and differs from the mechanical overload characteristic of chronic, overuse tendinopathy [ , ].
Our parameter-screening maps offer a coherent rationale for prior variability in LED outcomes across tendinopathy trials by linking wavelength penetration, chromophore absorption, and tissue-layer targets to biological endpoints [ ]. Red light (~ 625–660 nm), with moderate tissue penetration and strong alignment to cytochrome c oxidase (CCO) absorption peaks, preferentially modulated superficial/juxtasynovial compartments and produced pronounced reductions in IL-6 and SP alongside pain relief, consistent with CCO-driven mitochondrial and redox reprogramming that blunts inflammatory signaling [ – ]. Early rodent studies are concordant: 630–640 nm LEDs at 4–20 J/cm2 reduced inflammatory infiltrates and improved fibroblast proliferation and collagen alignment over 5–10 daily sessions, with accelerated collagen deposition over 7 days in tendinopathy models [ , ]. By contrast, near-infrared wavelengths (810–940 nm) penetrate deeper and tend to favor tenocyte anabolism in the tendon core, often enhancing COL1 synthesis; their anti-IL-6 effects are parameter- and model-dependent rather than uniformly absent. Illustratively, when dosing and scheduling align with the therapeutic window, 880 nm at ~ 7.5 J/cm2 in rat tendinitis lowered IL-6 mRNA while increasing COL1/III after 4–7 sessions, and 945 nm at ~ 3.84 J/cm2 in ovariectomized models similarly reduced IL-6 over 2–7 sessions-showing that near-infrared light can deliver concurrent anti-inflammatory and pro-anabolic benefits under suitable conditions [ – ].
In our in vivo experiments, daily 625 nm/52.8 J/cm2 from day 3 post-injury produced sustained improvements in mechanical thresholds and the lowest Bonar scores with concomitant IL-6/SP reductions, defining a red-light anti-inflammatory/antinociceptive window. In parallel, 810 nm/39.6 J/cm2 and 940 nm/26.4 J/cm2 selectively increased COL1 without altering COL3 or PRG4, indicating an near-infrared bias toward anabolic matrix restoration. Finally, the observed biphasic dose response [ , ] − 26.4 and 39.6 J/cm2 optimizing COL1, versus 52.8 J/cm2 yielding more durable analgesia—underscores the need to match dose to the intended endpoint (anti-inflammatory versus pro-anabolic) within a therapeutic window.
Mechanistically, our data suggest a potential link between LED therapy and the IL-6/STAT3 axis. IL-1β stimulation elevated IL-6 while suppressing STAT3 phosphorylation, consistent with context-dependent dysregulation of JAK/STAT signaling under cytokine stress [ – ]. We found that 625 nm LED restored p-STAT3 to basal levels and reduced IL-6, and this anti-inflammatory effect was blocked by the STAT3 inhibitor Stattic, suggesting STAT3 reactivation might be necessary for LED’s IL-6 attenuation. Although STAT3 can assume dual roles in connective tissues-pro-repair vs. pro-inflammatory depending on timing and cellular context-our data support a model in which LED based PBM shifts STAT3 from a dysfunctional, inflammation-permissive state toward homeostatic activation, which may subsequently suppress IL-6 transcription and dampen its autocrine loops and secondarily alleviates nociceptive sensitization via SP [ ]. This is congruent with PBM’s established capacity to lower ROS, stabilize mitochondrial membrane potential, and modulate transcription factor activity, including JAK/STAT [ , ]. Stattic’s selective ablation of LED benefits at non-cytotoxic doses further cements causality, paralleling STAT3’s indispensable role in tendon-derived stem cell differentiation and anti-fibrotic repair [ , ]. Preclinical precedents align: 630 nm LED in fibroblast cultures boosted proliferation/migration via STAT3-linked pathways, and 640 nm in tendinopathy models curbed IL-6 while enhancing collagen organization post-7 sessions, suggesting conserved photonic-STAT3 interfaces across models [ , ].
With respect to ECM remodeling, LED showed a selective pro-anabolic profile. Near-infrared wavelengths (810/940 nm) consistently enhanced COL1, while COL3 and PRG4 (lubricin) remained unchanged across tested parameters and time windows. This pattern supports a shift toward physiologic, load-bearing collagen without inducing a scar-like COL3 predominance-an advantageous remodeling phenotype for tensile tissues. However, these histological and molecular-level improvements do not confirm the restoration of tendon tensile strength or functional load-bearing capacity. Rodent studies are directionally concordant: in tenotomy and tendinitis models, 640 nm (4 J/cm2) and 880 nm (7.5 J/cm2) increased COL1/III expression and improved fiber alignment over multi-session regimens, without evidence of disproportionate COL3 upregulation [ , ]. The lack of PRG4 modulation suggests boundary lubrication is relatively refractory to the evaluated LED protocols and may require longer treatment horizons, alternative dosing/scheduling, or synergistic mechanical loading to unmask LED-mechanotransduction crosstalk at tendon sheaths and entheses. Future work should test combinatorial approaches (e.g., LED plus graded eccentric loading) to activate PRG4 pathways and stabilize friction-reducing interfaces.

## Clinical relevance
Clinically, our findings provide parameter candidates that may help move beyond the “one-size-fits-all” paradigm in LED-based PBM for musculoskeletal disorders. By suggesting wavelength-specific mechanisms-red light (625 nm) potentially modulating IL-6/STAT3 to control inflammation and pain, and near-infrared (810–940 nm) driving COL1-mediated matrix anabolism—we demonstrate tailored LED therapy based on disease stage and goals. This enables a potential precision medicine approach: considering 625 nm at ~ 52.8 J/cm2 for pain and inflammation, and 810–940 nm at ~ 26.4–39.6 J/cm2 for matrix remodeling in reparative phases. A staged protocol-red light first, followed by near-infrared, paired with progressive loading-may optimize outcomes and reduce inter-trial variance from non-strandardized parameters. This strategy addresses parameter heterogeneity, positioning LED as a customizable modality for patient-specific tendinopathy phenotypes. Notably, 940 nm/52.8 J/cm2 reduced fat deposition in vivo, suggesting wavelength effects on adipogenesis/angiogenesis; further assays on lipid dynamics and microvascular density could refine phase-specific parameterization. The translation of the identified parameters to deeper human tendons requires careful consideration of tissue thickness and optical properties.

## Limitations
This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results and planning future research. First, the scope is best described as a parameter screening study. While we systematically evaluated wavelength and energy density, other key parameters (e.g., irradiance, treatment frequency) were held constant based on common preclinical protocols. True clinical optimization would require exploring parameter reciprocity and may benefit from advanced experimental designs. Second, and critically for a load-bearing tissue, we lack direct assessment of tendon biomechanical properties or functional recovery. The improvements in histology and molecular markers, while promising, do not confirm restored tensile strength or load-bearing capacity. Third, the mechanistic link to the IL-6/STAT3 axis, though suggestive, relies on pharmacological inhibition. Future studies employing siRNA knockdown, phospho-mutant constructs, or multi-omic approaches are warranted to establish definitive causality and expand mechanistic insight beyond a single pathway. Fourth, the clinical relevance of the experimental models is limited. The IL-1β in vitro and collagenase-induced in vivo models represent acute inflammatory and matrix-disruptive aspects, respectively, but do not fully replicate the chronic mechanical overload central to human tendinopathy. Furthermore, the translation of the identified energy densities to deeper human tendons requires consideration of differing tissue optics. Additional methodological considerations include: the use of a single, albeit blinded, observer for histopathological scoring, which introduces potential subjectivity; behavioral assessment focused on mechanical hypersensitivity, leaving thermal and spontaneous pain modalities unexplored; and the neutral findings on PRG4, which may reflect the specific sampling windows, the Achilles tendon microenvironment, or the need for load-coupled paradigms to better reveal effects on lubrication biology.

## Conclusion
In summary, this study screened LED parameters and found that 625 nm/52.8 J/cm2 exposure alleviated inflammation and pain, potentially in part through modulation of the IL-6/STAT3 pathway, while 810 nm and 940 nm at specific doses promoted COL1 synthesis. These findings provide a basis for further investigation into parameter-specific LED strategies for tendinopathy management. Critically, the effect of these LED regimens on tendon biomechanical function requires future elucidation. Further research integrating biomechanical validation, genetic mechanistic interrogation, and models that better mimic chronic overuse pathology will be essential to translate these promising findings into optimized clinical protocols.
