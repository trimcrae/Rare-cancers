## Abstract

## Background
Activation of the cannabinoid type 2 (CB2) receptor has been demonstrated to offer protective effects against various inflammatory conditions and tissue injuries across different organs. Ferroptosis, a form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, has been increasingly recognized as a contributor to the severity of acute lung injury (ALI). However, the precise role of CB2 receptor activation in hyperoxia-induced acute lung injury (HALI) remains poorly defined.

## Methods
This study investigated the effects of JWH133, a selective CB2 receptor agonist, using both in vivo animal models and in vitro cellular models exposed to hyperoxia conditions. Lung tissue and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were collected at the end of the experiments for analyses, and animal survival rates were recorded throughout the experimental period. Concurrently, mouse lung epithelial cells (MLE-12) were cultured under hyperoxia conditions. To specifically elucidate the role of GPX4, siRNA-mediated knockdown was employed in both animal and cellular models, providing a detailed assessment of its functional relevance.

## Results
Treatment with JWH133 significantly improved survival rates, reduced pulmonary edema, and markedly decreased inflammatory cytokine levels, oxidative stress markers, and indicators of ferroptosis in hyperoxia-exposed models. Furthermore, JWH133 effectively inhibited NF-κB signaling and reduced cell death in lung tissues, accompanied by enhanced expression of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4). Consistent with these in vivo findings, hyperoxia-exposed MLE-12 cells treated with JWH133 exhibited similar protective effects. Notably, the protective effects of JWH133 were negated when GPX4 expression was silenced using targeted siRNA in both animal and cellular models.

## Conclusion
Our findings suggest that JWH133 confers protection against HALI primarily by upregulating GPX4 expression, highlighting its potential as a promising therapeutic strategy for HALI.

## Introduction
Acute lung injury (ALI) and its more severe form, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), represent critical respiratory conditions with persistently high mortality rates, despite extensive research and advancements in therapeutic strategies. Current clinical approaches have not significantly improved outcomes, as ALI continues to have a mortality rate of approximately 40%, highlighting the urgent need for novel therapeutic interventions [ ]. Oxygen therapy remains indispensable in managing severe respiratory and cardiac conditions, yet prolonged exposure to high oxygen concentrations (above 50%) can result in hyperoxia-induced acute lung injury (HALI). This condition is characterized by pulmonary edema, increased alveolar-capillary permeability, and elevated alveolar protein levels. Additionally, HALI triggers an intense inflammatory response coupled with excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, leading to substantial lung tissue damage and leukocyte infiltration [ , ]. Thus, novel therapeutic approaches to mitigate oxygen toxicity and effectively target underlying ARDS mechanisms are critically needed.
Cannabinoids, the active compounds derived from the Cannabis sativa plant, have been increasingly recognized for their therapeutic potential, including analgesic, anxiolytic, and anti-inflammatory effects [ ]. Cannabinoids primarily exert their biological activities by interacting with two major receptor subtypes: CB1 receptors, predominantly expressed in the central nervous system, and CB2 receptors, predominantly found in immune and hematopoietic cells. Activation of CB2 receptors is associated with anti-inflammatory effects, while their deletion in experimental models exacerbates inflammation and tissue injury [ , ]. Indeed, CB2 receptor activation has demonstrated protective effects in various pathological conditions such as cerebral ischemia, autoimmune disorders, and ischemia-reperfusion injuries [ , ]. CB2 receptors are present in lung tissues and immune cells. Previous studies report that pharmacological activation of CB2, including with the selective agonist JWH133, reduces lung injury induced by paraquat, sulfur mustard, ischemia–reperfusion, LPS, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, in part by limiting inflammation and inhibiting NF-κB signaling [ – ]. However, whether CB2 signaling also confers protection in hyperoxia-induced acute lung injury (HALI), a sterile, oxidative stress–driven model of oxygen toxicity, remains unclear.
Ferroptosis, a recently recognized form of cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, has emerged as an important contributor to lung injury [ ]. Ferroptosis leads to extensive cellular membrane damage, exacerbating lung injury in conditions such as HALI [ , ]. Accumulation of iron disrupts normal iron metabolism, provoking oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby aggravating tissue injury [ , ]. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) is a critical enzyme responsible for detoxifying lipid peroxides and controlling ferroptosis [ , ]. Reduced glutathione availability impairs GPX4 activity, leading to increased susceptibility to ferroptosis. Importantly, ferroptosis also intensifies inflammation by promoting the release of proinflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and IL-6 [ , , ]. Moreover, the potential involvement of ferroptosis, and in particular the ferroptosis regulator GPX4, in CB2-mediated protection has not been defined.
In this context, our study aims to investigate the role of JWH133, a selective CB2 receptor agonist, in modulating the pathophysiological processes underlying HALI in a murine model. Specifically, we explore whether ferroptosis mechanisms contribute to the protective effects observed with JWH133 treatment, utilizing GPX4-targeted small interfering RNA (siRNA) to probe the molecular pathway involved. Complementary experiments employing mouse lung epithelial cells (MLE-12) subjected to hyperoxia conditions were also conducted to validate and extend our in vivo findings.

## Materials and methods

## Animals and treatments
Male C57BL/6J mice aged 8–10 weeks were housed under standard laboratory conditions following guidelines from the National Institutes of Health. All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the National Defense Medical University (approval number: IACUC-21-089). Mice were randomly assigned to one of four experimental groups: control, control plus JWH-133 (10 mg/kg; obtained from Tocris Bioscience, Minneapolis, MN, USA), hyperoxia, and hyperoxia plus JWH-133 (administered at 2.5, 5, or 10 mg/kg). The doses used in this study were selected based on previous reports [ , ]. Mice were given an intraperitoneal dose of JWH133 immediately before hyperoxia was initiated, and then received additional injections once daily at 24, 48, and 72 h of exposure. Animals in hyperoxia groups were placed in a sealed chamber delivering oxygen at levels exceeding 95% for durations of 24, 48, or 72 h, as previously described [ – ]. The control group was exposed to normal atmospheric conditions.

## Survival analysis
To assess survival, mice received an intraperitoneal dose of JWH133 (10 mg/kg) immediately before hyperoxia, followed by once-daily injections at 24, 48, and 72 h of exposure. Survival was monitored and recorded at 5-hour intervals until all animals had died. Data were presented as Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and differences between groups were analyzed using the log-rank test.

## Lung wet/dry weight ratio
To quantify pulmonary edema, the upper right lung lobes were weighed immediately after harvesting (wet weight) and then dried at 60 °C for 48 h to obtain the dry weight. The wet-to-dry (W/D) ratio was calculated from these measurements.

## Analysis of protein and cytokine levels in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)
Mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of Zoletil and Rompun. The trachea was then exposed via a midline neck incision and intubated with a 22-gauge blunt catheter. For unilateral lavage of the left lung, the right mainstem bronchus was carefully identified and ligated with a 5 − 0 silk suture to block fluid flow. The left lung was lavaged three times with 0.5 mL of ice-cold sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2 mM EDTA. Collected BALF was centrifuged at 200 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant from the lavage was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C for later cytokine and protein measurements. Protein concentrations were measured using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Pierce, Rockford, IL, USA). The levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6), keratinocyte-derived chemokine (KC), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) were determined in BALF using specific mouse ELISA kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA).

## Measurement of oxidative stress indicators and iron levels
Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined using a colorimetric assay kit (ab65354, Abcam). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and SOD activity was calculated as the percentage inhibition of xanthine oxidase–generated superoxide relative to a no-SOD control. Glutathione (GSH) levels were measured with a fluorometric Glutathione Detection Kit (ab65322, Abcam), and results were expressed as µmol GSH per g protein. Malondialdehyde (MDA), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), and total iron were measured using the corresponding assay kits (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturers’ protocols.

## Myeloperoxidase immunohistochemical staining
Lung tissues underwent immunohistochemical staining for myeloperoxidase (MPO) using an anti-MPO antibody (1:100; Cell Signaling Technology), a rat-specific HRP polymer-conjugated secondary antibody (Nichirei Biosciences, Tokyo, Japan), and HRP substrate. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin [ ].

## Western blot analysis
Lung tissues and protein lysates from cultured cells (30 µg/lane) were electrophoresed via 8–12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. The membranes were blocked with 5% milk overnight to prevent non-specific binding. The blots were probed with one of the following primary antibodies: NF-κB p65, phospho-NF-κB p65, IκB-α, cleaved caspase-3, Bcl-2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), CB2, SOD1, SOD2, heme oxygenase 1 (HO‑1), GPX4, Acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), Lamin B (Abcam), and β-actin (Sigma-Aldrich). The blots were then washed with Phosphate-Buffered Saline with Tween® 20 (PBST) for 10 min and incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:20,000) or goat anti-mouse IgG (1:20,000) secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. After three washes with PBST for 10 min, the immunoreactive bands were visualized with an enhanced chemiluminescence kit and exposed using a UVP ChemiDoc-It Imaging System. The density of the immunoblots was calculated using image analysis software. The level of each protein was measured by repeating the Western blot analysis at least three times. The data are presented as the relative ratio of the target protein to the reference protein. The relative ratio of the target protein in the control group was arbitrarily set to 1 [ , ].

## Histological analysis
Lung tissues were fixed in 10% formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). For each sample, ten high-power fields (400×) were evaluated to count polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs) and determine a lung injury score. Morphological changes were examined using a light microscope (Olympus CKX41, Japan). At least 10 randomly selected fields were assessed for neutrophil infiltration in the airspaces or vessel walls and for alveolar wall thickening. Lung injury was graded on a four-point scale as 0 (none), 1 (mild), 2 (moderate), or 3 (severe). Two pathologists, blinded to group allocation, independently scored each section, and their scores were summed to yield a total lung injury score ranging from 0 to 6 [ ].

## Hyperoxia injury in MLE-12 cells
MLE-12 cells were cultured in DMEM/F-12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics under standard conditions. Cells were exposed to hyperoxia conditions (95% O₂, 5% CO₂) for 72 h, with treatments including vehicle control, JWH133 (4 µM), or GPX4-targeting siRNA (25 nM) [ , ]. Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; ALX-850-039, Enzo Life Sciences, Farmingdale, NY, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. MLE-12 cells (3 × 10³ cells per well) were seeded into 96-well plates and exposed to various concentrations of JWH133 (0, 1, 2, 4, or 10 µM). Each condition was tested in triplicate and cells were incubated for 72 h. Then, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and after a further 4 h incubation, absorbance at 450 nm was measured with a microplate reader. GPX4 knockdown efficacy was confirmed by Western blot analysis (Fig. A). Supernatants were analyzed for KC levels using ELISA kits (R&D Systems).
 

## Quantitative real-time PCR
Total RNA was extracted from cells using the RNA Miniprep Plus kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). cDNA was synthesized from 20 to 100 ng/µL RNA using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) using TaqMan probes for cb2 and TaqMan® Universal Master Mix II (Applied Biosystems). Relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with gapdh (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase) as the internal control.

## siRNA transfection in vivo
For in vivo GPX4 silencing, 100 µg GPX4 siRNA was administered intratracheally to mice using a MicroSprayer aerosolizer (Penn-Century) [ ]. Knockdown efficiency was validated by Western blot (Fig. A). After 48 h, mice received intraperitoneal injections of JWH133 or saline followed by 72-hour hyperoxia exposure before lung tissue collection.
 

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 6.0). Differences among groups were evaluated by one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test. A P-value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

## Results

## Pretreatment with JWH133 alleviates the severity of HALI
Survival analysis showed that all mice exposed to hyperoxia without JWH133 died within 96 h, whereas JWH133 treatment significantly extended survival under the same conditions (Fig.  A). Seventy-two hours of hyperoxia led to marked increases in protein concentration (Fig.  B) and lung wet-to-dry weight ratio (Fig.  C), consistent with pulmonary edema. Histological examination after 72 h of exposure revealed pronounced alveolar wall thickening and dense neutrophil infiltration in both the interstitium and alveolar spaces (Fig.  D). Pretreatment with JWH133 substantially attenuated these hyperoxia-induced alterations and lung injury scores (Fig.  B–D).
 

## JWH133 reduces inflammatory responses in HALI
Hyperoxia exposure for 72 h markedly elevated inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and KC, in the BALF (Fig.  A-C). Treatment with JWH133 significantly decreased the levels of these inflammatory mediators, suggesting that the protective effects of JWH133 against hyperoxia-induced lung damage might involve modulation of inflammatory responses.
 

## JWH133 suppresses NF-κB pathway activation and apoptosis in hyperoxia-exposed lungs
Western blot analysis revealed significant activation of the NF-κB pathway, characterized by decreased cytoplasmic IκB-α and increased nuclear p65 protein levels following 72 h of hyperoxia exposure (Fig.  A-C). This hyperoxia-induced activation was associated with reduced expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and increased levels of the pro-apoptotic marker cleaved caspase-3 (Fig.  A, D and E). Treatment with JWH133 notably reversed these effects, restoring IκB-α and Bcl-2 expression while reducing nuclear p65 and cleaved caspase-3 levels, indicating suppression of apoptosis and NF-κB signaling (Fig.  A-E).
 

## JWH133 mitigates hyperoxia-induced ferroptosis and lipid peroxidation in lung tissues
To assess the role of JWH133 in regulating ferroptosis during HALI, we examined key markers GPX4 and ACSL4. Hyperoxia significantly downregulated GPX4 and upregulated ACSL4 expressions (Fig.  A-B). Additionally, hyperoxia exposure resulted in increased total iron content, enhanced infiltration of MPO-positive inflammatory cells in lung tissues, elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) levels in BALF, increased H₂O₂ production, and decreased glutathione (GSH) levels (Fig.  C-G). Notably, JWH133 administration effectively reversed these indicators of ferroptosis and oxidative stress (Fig.  A-G), emphasizing its protective role against ferroptosis-driven lung damage.
 

## Hyperoxia reduces SOD activity without altering SOD1 or SOD2 protein expression
Hyperoxia significantly decreased total SOD activity compared with the control group (Fig.  A). However, the protein levels of both SOD1 and SOD2 remained unchanged across experimental groups (Fig.  B and C). JWH133 treatment significantly restored total SOD activity in hyperoxia-exposed lungs but did not significantly affect SOD1 or SOD2 protein expression. These findings indicate that the decline in SOD activity induced by hyperoxia was not attributable to reduced SOD protein abundance. 

## Protective role of JWH133 in hyperoxia-exposed MLE-12 cells through GPX4 enhancement
CCK-8 assay confirmed that 4 µM JWH133 did not reduce cell viability compared with vehicle-treated controls (Supplementary Fig. 1). To verify CB2 receptor expression in MLE-12 cells, Western blotting was performed using whole-cell lysates. A specific band corresponding to CB2 (~ 40 kDa) was detected, confirming endogenous CB2 protein expression. Moreover, hyperoxia exposure increased CB2 protein abundance in MLE-12 cells. Densitometric quantification is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2A, and full uncropped blots are provided in original western blot supplementary file. These findings were further supported by qPCR, which confirmed CB2 mRNA expression in MLE-12 cells (Supplementary Fig. 2B). To investigate the role of GPX4 in the protective mechanism of JWH133, MLE-12 cells were transfected with GPX4-targeting siRNA (Fig.  A). Consistent with the findings from the in vivo experiments, western blot analysis indicated that JWH133 treatment significantly reversed the hyperoxia-induced reduction in GPX4 protein levels (Fig.  D and F). Additionally, treatment with JWH133 markedly suppressed the hyperoxia-induced increase in ACSL4 protein expression, restored SOD activity, and elevated GSH levels (Fig.  B-E). Furthermore, JWH133 significantly reduced the hyperoxia-induced elevations in KC and NF-κB protein expression while simultaneously increasing IκB-α protein expression (Fig.  A-D). However, these protective effects of JWH133 were completely negated upon GPX4 knockdown using siRNA, underscoring GPX4’s essential role in mediating the beneficial effects of JWH133.
 

## GPX4 knockdown eliminates the protective effects of JWH133 in hyperoxia-exposed mice
The survival advantage of JWH133 in hyperoxia-exposed mice was completely lost following GPX4 siRNA treatment (Fig.  B). GPX4 knockdown also abolished the protective actions of JWH133 on lung histopathology and injury scores and blocked the JWH133-mediated reductions in BALF MDA, total protein, and TNF-α, IL-6, and KC levels (Fig.  C–G). In addition, the JWH133-induced suppression of NF-κB, the increase in IκB-α and Bcl-2, and the reduction in cleaved caspase-3 were no longer evident when GPX4 was silenced (Fig.  A–E). HO-1 protein expression in lung tissue was markedly higher in hyperoxia-exposed mice than in controls, and JWH133 significantly decreased HO-1 levels; this effect, too, was abolished by GPX4 knockdown (Fig.  ). Together, these results indicate that GPX4-dependent inhibition of ferroptosis is a key mechanism mediating the protective actions of JWH133 in HALI.
 

## Discussion
Several studies have shown that CB2 receptor activation, including with JWH133, protects against acute lung injury driven by endotoxin or bacterial infection, such as LPS- and Pseudomonas aeruginosa–induced models, primarily by dampening inflammatory responses and limiting leukocyte recruitment [ , ]. In this study, we extend these observations to HALI, a sterile, oxidative stress–dominated form of lung damage that differs mechanistically from pathogen- or LPS-driven models. JWH133 administration markedly reduced hyperoxia-induced cytokine production, inflammatory cell infiltration, reactive oxidant generation, cell death, and lung edema, and improved the associated histopathological changes. A key mechanistic finding is the identification of a CB2-GPX4-ferroptosis axis in HALI: hyperoxia was associated with reduced GPX4 expression and increased oxidative stress, whereas JWH133 restored GPX4 levels, decreased ferroptosis-associated markers, and limited injury in both mouse lungs and MLE-12 epithelial cells. Critically, GPX4 knockdown abolished the protective effects of JWH133, indicating that GPX4-dependent inhibition of ferroptosis is central to CB2-mediated protection in this setting.
HALI is characterized by the activation of inflammatory cells that release reactive oxygen species and cytokines, initiating a cascade involving lipid peroxidation, altered gene expression, and chemokine secretion, which collectively drive lung injury [ , , ]. In line with this, hyperoxia in our model led to elevated inflammatory cytokine levels and prominent neutrophil infiltration. Treatment with JWH133 significantly reduced these responses, suggesting that its protective effects in HALI are mediated, at least in part, by limiting inflammatory cell recruitment and cytokine production. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that CB2 receptor activation exerts anti-inflammatory effects across diverse disease models [ ] .
Research suggests apoptosis plays a significant role in HALI by causing cell death in lung tissues due to oxidative stress from high oxygen levels. An uncontrolled form of cell death also contributes to the severity and progression of lung damage [ , ]. Prior studies have shown that CB2 receptor activation by JWH reduces DNA fragmentation in neonatal hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy and suppresses caspase-3 activation in sepsis models, implicating CB2 signaling in the limitation of oxidative stress–induced apoptosis [ , ]. Consistent with these observations, we found that JWH133 attenuated apoptosis in hyperoxia-exposed lung tissue, as evidenced by reduced cleaved caspase-3 levels and increased expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2. These results support a dual role for CB2 activation in HALI, modulating both inflammatory and apoptotic pathways.
NF-κB is a master transcription factor that orchestrates inflammatory and immune responses by inducing a broad array of cytokines and chemokines. Under resting conditions, NF-κB is sequestered in the cytoplasm by inhibitor proteins (IκBs). Upon stimulation, IκBα is phosphorylated and degraded, allowing the p65 subunit to translocate to the nucleus and activate transcription of proinflammatory genes [ , ]. In HALI, excessive oxygen leads to the production of reactive oxygen species, which in turn activate NF-κB and enhance the pulmonary expression of inflammatory mediators like TNF-α and IL-6—a well-recognized step in the disease’s pathogenesis [ – ]. In the present work, JWH133 significantly suppressed IκB degradation and NF-κB activation, leading to reduced production of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including TNF-α, IL-6, and KC, and decreased leukocyte infiltration in lung tissue. Parallel experiments in MLE-12 epithelial cells confirmed that JWH133 blocks IκBα degradation, prevents p65 nuclear translocation, and limits chemokine release. Together, these findings indicate that JWH133 mitigates inflammation in HALI by stabilizing IκBα and inhibiting NF-κB signaling. The concordance of in vivo and in vitro results underscores the therapeutic potential of CB2 receptor activation, although further work is needed to define the specific cellular targets and upstream regulators involved.
To further interpret the reduction in SOD activity, we assessed SOD1 and SOD2 protein expression in lung tissue. Although hyperoxia significantly decreased total SOD activity, SOD1 and SOD2 protein levels remained unchanged. This discordance indicates that the loss of antioxidant capacity in our model is more consistent with impaired enzymatic function than with reduced protein abundance. Previous studies support this interpretation. Ho et al. reported that MnSOD regulation during hyperoxia may show a mismatch between enzyme activity and protein expression [ ]. Chang et al. further demonstrated that hyperoxia can result in accumulation of enzymatically inactive MnSOD despite preserved or increased protein levels [ ]. Similarly, Clerch et al. showed that hyperoxia may reduce MnSOD activity through decreased specific activity and post-transcriptional or post-translational mechanisms rather than simple loss of protein [ ]. In addition, oxidative and nitrosative stress can directly inactivate SOD; for example, MacMillan-Crow et al. demonstrated that peroxynitrite-mediated nitration impairs MnSOD activity [ ]. Taken together, these findings suggest that hyperoxia in our model compromises pulmonary antioxidant defense primarily through functional inactivation of SOD, rather than downregulation of SOD1 or SOD2 expression. Post-translational modifications and isoform-specific enzymatic activity were not directly evaluated in the present study, and their contribution should be examined in future work.
Ferroptosis is a distinct form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, glutathione depletion, and impaired GPX4 activity, and it can amplify inflammation through the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 [ , ]. Central to this process are GPX4 and ACSL4, which exert opposing influences on ferroptotic susceptibility. GPX4 protects cells by reducing lipid hydroperoxides, thereby preventing their accumulation and preserving redox balance [ ], whereas ACSL4 heightens vulnerability by enriching membranes with oxidizable polyunsaturated fatty acids and promoting lipid peroxidation [ , ]. The balance between GPX4-mediated protection and ACSL4-driven sensitization is therefore critical in determining ferroptosis sensitivity, and disruption of this balance—such as through loss of GPX4—results in uncontrolled lipid peroxide buildup and ferroptotic cell death [ ].
Our findings indicate that hyperoxia promotes ferroptosis in lung tissue, as evidenced by increased ACSL4 expression, elevated lipid peroxidation products, and intracellular iron accumulation, along with reduced GPX4 and GSH levels. Loss of GSH severely impairs GPX4 activity, reducing its ability to detoxify lipid peroxides [ ] and thereby facilitating Fe²⁺-driven lipid oxidation and additional reactive oxygen species generation [ ]. These changes are consistent with canonical features of ferroptosis. Importantly, JWH133 treatment lowered ferroptosis-related markers, mitigated hyperoxia-induced oxidative stress, and restored GPX4 expression. The abolition of JWH133’s protective effects after GPX4 knockdown in both mouse lungs and MLE-12 cells strongly supports the notion that CB2 activation protects against HALI mainly through GPX4-dependent inhibition of ferroptosis. In addition, we examined HO-1, a stress-inducible enzyme involved in redox regulation and ferroptosis. Although its effects are context dependent, many studies support a primarily cytoprotective role for HO-1 via its antioxidant and detoxifying functions [ , ]. In our model, hyperoxia upregulated HO-1 expression in lung tissue, consistent with activation of an endogenous defense response, and JWH133 attenuated this increase; notably, this effect was absent when GPX4 was silenced. Together with the GPX4 data, these findings suggest that CB2 activation engages multiple antioxidant pathways—including HO-1–mediated regulation of redox status and iron handling—to curb ferroptosis and oxidative injury in HALI. Further studies will be required to define the specific contribution of HO-1 to the overall protective actions of JWH133.
This study has several limitations. First, although we focused on GPX4 as a central ferroptosis regulator, we did not systematically examine other ferroptosis-related proteins or fully define their interactions with GPX4 in the context of HALI. Additional work will be required to clarify how other components of the ferroptosis machinery integrate with CB2 signaling. Second, multiple forms of regulated cell death—including apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis—likely contribute to HALI pathogenesis. The relative contributions and temporal dynamics of these pathways across different stages of lung injury were not comprehensively addressed in our experiments and warrant further investigation. Third, our in vitro studies relied on MLE-12 cells as a model of alveolar type II–like epithelial cells. MLE-12 cells are widely used to study hyperoxia-induced epithelial injury, oxidative stress, and cell death pathways, and epithelial cells are primary targets of hyperoxic damage in the lung [ ]. However, MLE-12 cells are immortalized and exhibit lower basal CB2 expression than lung-resident immune cells, which are major sites of CB2 signaling in vivo [ ]. Consequently, this model does not fully represent immune–epithelial interactions or the species differences between mice and humans. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as mechanistic support for epithelial CB2–GPX4 pathways rather than a complete representation of CB2 signaling in HALI. Future studies using primary human lung epithelial cells, and immune–epithelial co-cultures will be important to validate and extend these observations.
In summary, this study is the first to demonstrate the therapeutic potential of JWH133, a CB2 receptor agonist, in HALI. JWH133 significantly prolonged survival, reduced lung edema, and decreased inflammatory cytokine production and reactive oxygen species generation in hyperoxia-exposed mice. It also suppressed NF-κB activation and increased GPX4 expression, and these protective actions were recapitulated in MLE-12 cells under hyperoxia conditions. Notably, GPX4 knockdown abolished the benefits of JWH133, highlighting GPX4 as a critical effector in CB2 receptor-mediated protection against HALI. These findings identify a CB2-GPX4-ferroptosis axis as a key mechanism limiting hyperoxia-induced lung injury and suggest that pharmacological targeting of this pathway may represent a promising strategy for mitigating oxygen toxicity–related lung damage.

Compared with the vehicle group, JWH133 did not reduce cell viability, indicating absence of overt cytotoxicity at this concentration. Data are shown as mean ± SD from three independent experiments.
Supplementary Material 2. Supplementary Fig. 2: Validation of CB2 receptor expression in MLE-12 cells.(A) Representative Western blot analysis of CB2 protein expression in MLE-12 cells exposed to room air or hyperoxia. A specific immunoreactive band was detected at approximately 40 kDa, consistent with the predicted molecular weight of CB2. β-actin served as the loading control. (B) Quantitative real-time PCR analysis confirming CB2 mRNA expression in MLE-12 cells. Relative mRNA levels were normalized to gapdh and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Data are shown as mean ± SD from three independent experiments. *P < 0.05, **P< 0.01, compared with the room air group.
