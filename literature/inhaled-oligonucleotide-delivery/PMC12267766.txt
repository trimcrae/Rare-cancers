## Abstract
SNRPB2, a spliceosome component, is well known to participate in the pre-splicing of mRNA and enhance the apoptosis induction impact of ixazomib on multiple myeloma cells. However, its precise effect on hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains unexplored. Here, we demonstrate that high level of SNRPB2 is significantly associated with poor outcomes in HCC patients. Additionally, inhibiting SNRPB2 may accelerate ferroptosis, thereby suppressing HCC tumor development. Moreover, SNRPB2 is regulated by the SNHG4/miR-204-5p axis, and increased SNHG4 could reverse the effect of SNRPB2 knockdown. Finally, SNRPB2 deficiency increases the sorafenib sensitivity of HCC cells. Conclusively, our study uncovers the previously unknown role of SNRPB2 in HCC, reveals the related ceRNA regulatory axis of SNRPB2, and identifies a novel ferroptosis regulating protein, suggesting SNRPB2 appears to be a promising target for HCC therapy.

## Introduction
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common type of liver cancer making up more than 80% of all cases, and it ranks fourth in terms of cancer mortality and sixth in terms of cancer morbidity worldwide [ , ]. Although early-stage HCC may be treatable via liver transplantation, ablation, or resection, the vast majority of patients present with unresectable disease [ ]. Despite significant advances in the treatment of HCC, including chemotherapy, radiofrequency thermal ablation (RFTA), targeted drugs (sorafenib and lenvatinib), and immunotherapy [ – ], patient outcomes remain unsatisfactory. Thus, understanding the molecular mechanism of HCC and discovering treatment targets are critically important goals.
Ferroptosis is a newly identified type of regulated cell death that is marked by the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [ , ]. Intracellularly, iron works as a pro-oxidant by reacting with hydrogen peroxide to generate lipid ROS [ ], which are typically transformed into non-toxic lipid alcohols by glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and glutathione (GSH) [ ]. Increasing research has shown that the ferroptosis-regulating proteins, such as GPX4, p53, and solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11), have significant impacts on tumor progression regulation and act as effective targets for HCC therapy [ – ]. Hence, it is critical to investigate if other possible proteins may regulate ferroptosis and contribute to the aetiology of HCC to improve HCC patient survival.
Small Nuclear Ribonucleoprotein Polypeptide B2 (SNRPB2), a spliceosome component, is known to be involved in mRNA pre-splicing and to be associated with stem loop IV of U2 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein (U2 snRNP) [ ]. To the best of our knowledge, the precise functions of SNRPB2 and its precise molecular mechanisms in cancer remain largely unknown. SNRPB2 knockdown has been shown to improve the cytotoxic and apoptotic impacts of ixazomib on multiple myeloma cells [ ]. Additionally, recent study demonstrated that SNRPB2 knockdown induces apoptosis in esophageal cancer, suggesting broader roles in cell death modulation [ ]. Given the shared upstream regulators between apoptosis and ferroptosis, we propose SNRPB2 may act as a nodal regulator of divergent cell death pathways. Furthermore, bioinformatics analysis based on The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)—LIHC according to a prior study showed that SNRPB2 overexpression was associated with poor prognosis in HCC [ ]. However, experimental verification of the prognostic value and tumorigenic impact of SNRPB2 in HCC is still lacking, and the exact mechanism of SNRPB2 regulating HCC progression has not been investigated previously.
Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs), a heterogenic category of RNAs, regulate gene expression and affect cellular function through interactions with DNA, RNA, or protein substances [ ], and play multifaceted roles in diverse disease mechanisms including, neuropsychiatric disorders, autoimmune conditions, and malignancies [ – ]. In HCC, abnormal regulation of lncRNAs is closely linked to vascular formation, proliferation, invasiveness, apoptosis, and ferroptosis [ – ]. Many lncRNAs serve as sponges for natural microRNAs (miRNAs) or competitive endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs) [ – ]. Small nucleolar host gene 4 (SNHG4) has been implicated in many malignancies, including enhancing colorectal cancer malignancy and immune escape via upregulation of MET via inhibition of miR-144-3p [ ], accelerating osteosarcoma progression through sponging miR-224-3p [ ], and promoting renal cell carcinoma malignancy via the miR-204-5p/RUNX2 axis [ ]. However, the role of SNHG4 in ferroptosis is unknown, and the precise molecular mechanism by which SNHG4 contributes to the growth of HCC is still not fully understood.
Thus, it motivates us to investigate the tumorigenic effects of SNRPB2 on HCC, explore the specific mechanism of SNRPB2 regulating HCC progression, and further elucidate the relationship between lncRNAs and ferroptosis in HCC, which could shed light on the molecular mechanisms involved in HCC development and therapy.

## Results

## SNRPB2 is up-regulated in HCC
The results of the pan-cancer study conducted by TCGA and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) showed that SNRPB2 mRNA expression was increased in 28 different forms of cancer (Fig.  A), including HCC (Fig.  B). In addition, the GSE46408 dataset indicated that SNRPB2 was overexpressed in HCC (Fig.  C). SNRPB2 expression was also found to be significantly associated with histological grade (Fig.  D). Furthermore, SNRPB2 was higher in HCC cell lines compared to normal liver cell line using the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) dataset (Fig. A). Meanwhile, we demonstrated that SNRPB2 had a higher mRNA level in HCC compared to normal liver tissues utilizing 76 clinical tissue pairings (Table , Fig.  E). To investigate SNRPB2 protein expression levels in HCC further, we first used the CPTAC database to discover that SNRPB2 was raised in liver cancer (Fig.  F). Then, we found the expression of SNRPB2 was increased in liver cancer cell lines compared to the normal liver cell line WRL68 (Fig.  G), and considerably greater in clinical HCC tissue than paracancerous normal tissues (Fig.  H). Moreover, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining revealed that SNRPB2 was upregulated in HCC (Fig.  I, J). Taken together, these findings show that SNRPB2 is overexpressed in HCC.
 

## SNRPB2 shows great prognostic and diagnostic performance in HCC
To investigate the association between SNRPB2 expression and HCC patient prognosis, we first utilized the K–M plotter to discover that HCC patients with increased SNRPB2 expression had a worse prognosis of overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), and progression-free interval (PFI) from the TCGA-LIHC database (Fig.  A–C). In addition, a study of 76 HCC patients at Zhuzhou Central Hospital discovered that patients with elevated SNRPB2 IHC scores had unfavorable outcome (Fig.  D). Then, using univariate and multivariate cox regression analyses, we revealed that pathological T and M stage, tumor status, and SNRPB2 expression level had a high clinical value in predicting patients’ prognosis (Fig.  E, F). To further study SNRPB2’s diagnostic value in HCC, we determined that SNRPB2 had an outstanding performance (area under the curve (AUC) = 0.925) in distinguishing HCC from normal controls by plotting the receiver operating curve (ROC) (Fig.  G). Furthermore, the time-dependent ROC curve revealed that SNRPB2 had specific prediction accuracy (AUC = 0.698, 0.631, and 0.636) with respect to the survival rates of HCC patients at 1, 3, and 5 years (Fig.  H). Moreover, a nomogram incorporating SNRPB2 expression and several critical clinical characteristics was used to predict the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities of HCC patients (Figs.  I, S1B). Conclusively, SNRPB2 is related to an unfavorable outcome and has a high diagnostic value in HCC.
 

## SNRPB2 knockdown inhibits HCC progression
To further examine the biological functions of SNRPB2 in HCC, we knocked down SNRPB2 in HepG2 and Hep3B cells that expressed high levels of SNRPB2 (Fig.  A, B). Following that, Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) analysis revealed that SNRPB2 deletion significantly impeded the proliferation of HepG2 and Hep3B cells (Fig.  C, D). Colony formation experiments demonstrated that SNRPB2 reduction decreased the capacity for colony formation (Fig.  E). As shown by transwell migration assays (Fig.  F), SNRPB2 depletion significantly inhibited the migration of HepG2 and Hep3B cells. Then, nude mice were subcutaneously injected with SNRPB2-knockdown HepG2 cells in order to validate the oncogenic effects of SRNPB2 on HCC in vivo. The results of the measurements indicated a significant reduction in both tumor size and weight in those mice with SNRPB2 deletion (Figs.  G, H, S1C). And the IHC experiment revealed that Ki-67 was positively associated with SNRPB2 expression (Fig.  I). In conclusion, we prove that the SNRPB2 knockdown reduces HCC growth.
 

## SNRPB2 Silencing promotes the ferroptosis of HCC cells
Exploring the cell fate of HCC cells with SNRPB2 knockdown is of interest. Thus, we compared the therapeutic efficacy of several medications, including the apoptosis inducer VCR, the ferroptosis inducer RSL-3, the autophagy inducer Rapamycin, and the pyroptosis inducer LPS + Nigericin. The results demonstrated that the ferroptosis inducer RSL-3 had a considerable cell death inducing effect when SNRPB2 was deleted (Fig.  A, B). Furthermore, the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) results unveiled that many pivotal ferroptosis-related pathways, such as “Ferroptosis”, “Oxidative stress response”, “Iron ion homeostasis”, and “Glutathione metabolic process”, had substantial differences in enrichment between high and low SNRPB2 expression groups (Fig.  C, Figure S2). To further demonstrate the effect of SNRPB2 on ferroptosis in HCC cells, we treated HepG2 and Hep3B cells with RSL-3 and discovered that SNRPB2 depletion lowered clonogenic potential in both (Fig.  D, E). In addition, the IHC experiment on xenograft tumor revealed that SNRPB2 knockdown dramatically increased the expression level of 4-HNE, a biomarker of ferroptosis (Fig.  F). Then, we further examined RSL-3-induced ferroptosis in the presence or absence of ferrostatin, a ferroptosis inhibitor, to demonstrate that SNRPB2 deletion boosted RSL3-induced HCC cell growth inhibition (Fig.  G, H). Furthermore, we discovered that SNRPB2 deletion also enhanced the intracellular concentrations of lipid ROS (Fig.  I), ferrous iron (Fig.  K), and reduced total GSH (Fig.  J). Collectively, our findings suggest that the deletion of SNRPB2 accelerates HCC cell ferroptosis.
 

## SNHG4 regulates SNRPB2 expression by competing for miR-204-5p
By sequestering their target miRNAs, lncRNAs, which play a major role in carcinogenesis, regulate the expression of downstream mRNAs [ ]. We discovered 35 miRNAs that potentially target SNRPB2 using the databases miRDB, miRWalk, Targetscan, and miRabel (Fig.  A). Then, via CancerMIRNome, we identified 117 miRNAs in HCC that were substantially linked with prognosis, with only 5 miRNAs, one of which is miR-204-5p, at the intersection of the two sets (Figs.  B, S3A). Since researches have indicated that miR-204-5p may inhibit a range of malignancies, including HCC [ – ], and miR-204-5p was reduced in HCC according to TCGA-LIHC (Fig. S3B), we conducted further investigation on the association between miR-204-5p and SNRPB2. The correlation analysis showed a negative relationship between miR-204-5p and SNRPB2 in clinical HCC samples (R= − 0.325, p = 0.004) (Fig.  D). Furthermore, luciferase reporter assays revealed a marked decrease in luciferase activities following transfecting miR-204-5p and the SNRPB2-WT, but not the SNRPB2-MUT in HepG2 cells (Fig.  C, E). Moreover, miR-204-5p reduced SNRPB2 expression after SNRPB2-WT but not mutant SNRPB2 (Fig.  F), proving that miR-204-5p directly targeted SNRPB2. Using the ENCORI platform, we then discovered 29 validated lncRNAs that can target miR-204-5p (Table S2), among which SNHG4 was raised in HCC and exhibited the most significant correlation with HCC patient prognosis (Figs. S3C, D, G). The negative association between SNHG4 and miR-204-5p was previously reported in gastric cancer and renal cell carcinoma [ , ], and we now illustrate it in HCC. We first employed luciferase reporter assays to show the suppression of luciferase activity of SNHG4-WT in HepG2 cells transfected with miR-204-5p, whereas no marked alteration in that of SNHG4-MUT (Fig.  H, I). Furthermore, we discovered a remarkable reduction in SNHG4 expression after transfection of miR-204-5p and SNHG4-WT, but not mutant SNHG4 (Fig.  J), indicating that SNHG4 may use miR-204-5p as a ceRNA. SNHG4 overexpression also increased SNRPB2 expression in HepG2 cells (Fig.  K). Together, these results indicate that SNHG4 functions as a positive upstream regulator of SNRPB2 in HCC by binding directly to miR-204-5p.
 

## SNHG4 reverses the effect of SNRPB2 knockdown
To further identify whether the oncogenic capabilities of SNRPB2 were regulated by SNHG4, we ectopically expressed SNHG4 in SNRPB2 knockdown HepG2 cells (Fig.  A). The biological function experiments revealed that SNHG4 overexpression effectively rescued the cell growth (Fig.  B), colony formation (Fig.  C), and migratory arrests (Fig.  D) triggered by SNRPB2 depletion. In addition, we found that overexpressing SNHG4 in HepG2 cells reversed the enhanced RSL3-induced growth inhibition (Fig.  E), intracellular concentrations of lipid ROS (Fig.  F) and ferrous iron (Fig.  H), and decreased GSH (Fig.  G) caused by SNRPB2 knockdown. Taken together, SNHG4 could reverse the tumor suppression and ferroptosis boosting induced by SNRPB2 deletion.
 

## SNRPB2 contributes to the resistance of HCC to Sorafenib
Given the significance of SNRPB2 in HCC carcinogenesis, we investigated if SNRPB2 was a cancer hallmark for sorafenib resistance in HCC. Initially, sorafenib-resistant (SR) HepG2 cell line was generated in vitro. By comparing the viability of sensitive and resistant cells subjected to varying concentrations of sorafenib, we observed that SR cells exhibited reduced susceptibility to sorafenib (Fig.  A). Then, we examined SNRPB2 expression in HCC and HCC-SR cells in GSE140202 to discover that SNRPB2 was considerably elevated in SR cells (Fig.  B). In addition, RT-qPCR (Fig.  C, D) confirmed the high level of SNRPB2 expression in SR cells. To further determine if SNRPB2 was associated with sorafenib resistance in HCC, we discovered that SNRPB2 deletion increased HCC cell susceptibility to sorafenib (Fig.  E) and resulted in raised lipid ROS levels (Fig.  F). All of this evidence suggests that HCC cells’ resistance to sorafenib may be mediated in a positive manner by SNRPB2. Finally, we used the RNAactDrug database [ ] to look for effective drugs that might target SNRPB2 to enhance the prognosis of HCC patients. The drug sensitivities of 2-(1-piperazino)naphthazarin (Fig.  G) and thiosangivamycin (Fig. S4A) were shown to be substantially linked with SNRPB2 mRNA levels. Furthermore, the prediction binding model of 2-(1-piperazino)naphthazarin/thiosangivamycin and SNRPB2 showed low binding energy as “− 6.546” (Fig.  H) and “− 5.949” (Fig. S4B), indicating the certain potential of targeting SNRPB2 to improve SR in the treatment of HCC.
 

## Discussion
HCC, which ranks third in terms of cancer-related mortality on a global scale, has an alarmingly high recurrence rate within 5 years, mainly owing to uncontrolled proliferation and metastasis [ ]. Additionally, prognosis prediction is considerably more challenging due to the substantial heterogeneity of HCC and the presence of numerous risk factors [ ]. Therefore, efforts should be undertaken to fully comprehend the mechanism and identify the targets of HCC progression. SNRPB2 is involved in pre-mRNA splicing as a component of the spliceosome, and has been reported to be closely linked to the drug resistance of ixazomib on multiple myeloma cells [ , ]. However, the role of SNRPB2 and its related regulatory mechanisms in cancers are still largely unknown, and the precise mechanism of SNRPB2 in the progression of HCC remains unexplored. Here, we thoroughly investigated the expression, prognostic and diagnostic value, effect on tumorigenesis and ferroptosis, corresponding ceRNA regulatory axis, and the specific role in the sorafenib resistance of SNRPB2 in HCC.
As a critical component of the splicesome, SNRPB2 has been proven to exhibit a higher expression level and be associated with poor outcomes in multiple myeloma patients [ ], while the SNRPB2 expression profile in cancers still remains largely unknown, and the expression level and clinical implications of SNRPB2 in HCC also remain elusive. In this study, we initially assessed the level of SNRPB2 expression in pan-cancer to find that SNRPB2 was upregulated in most cancer types. Then, we showed SNRPB2 significantly overexpressed in HCC relative to normal liver tissue by employing TCGA + GTEx, Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), and HPA, and the results of RT-qPCR, western blot, and IHC on clinical HCC tissues further demonstrated it. In addition, Kaplan-Meier plotter indicated that OS, DSS, and PFI were unfavorable in HCC patients with elevated SNRPB2 expression, which was further supported by the survival analysis of HCC patients from Zhuzhou Central Hospital. Elevated SNRPB2 expression was an independent risk factor for OS in HCC, as determined by cox regression analysis. Moreover, the ROC curve analysis demonstrated SNRPB2’s superior HCC diagnostic ability. Taken together, SNRPB2 is overexpressed and exhibits great prognostic and diagnostic values in HCC.
A recent study indicated that knockdown of SNRPB2 may result in inhibition of aggressive behavior in multiple myeloma cells [ ]. However, the precise impact of SNRPB2 expression on the malignant phenotype of HCC is still unknown. Here, we identified that SNRPB2 deletion effectively reduced the proliferation, colony formation, and migration of HCC cells in vitro, and the prevention of tumor growth was further confirmed using xenograft nude mouse model with SNRPB2 knockdown. Conclusively, these findings indicate that SNRPB2 could facilitate the development of HCC.
Ferroptosis is a newly identified type of non-apoptotic iron-dependent cell death mediated by lipid peroxidation [ ]. Emerging evidence suggests that ferroptosis induction can effectively inhibit tumor progression, highlighting its therapeutic potential across various cancer types. This approach offers promising opportunities for developing precision therapies through either ferroptosis induction or inhibition. A critical step in this direction involves identifying reliable biomarkers that can guide these targeted treatment strategies [ – ]. Several clinical and preclinical medications, such as RSL-3 and sorafenib, have been shown to trigger ferroptosis and have anti-tumor effects in HCC [ ]. In this study, we discovered that SNRPB2 was critical in ferroptosis-related pathways, including “Ferroptosis”, “Oxidative stress response”, “Iron ion homeostasis” and “Glutathione metabolic process” as determined by GSEA. The colony formation experiment showed that the depletion of SNRPB2 decreased the clonogenic potential of HCC cells treated with RSL-3. And IHC tset indicated that 4-HNE, an indicator of ferroptosis, was negatively associated with SNRPB2 expression. In addition, the CCK8 analysis revealed that inhibiting SNRPB2 increased ferroptosis of HCC cells. Moreover, SNRPB2 deletion decreased GSH while increasing lipid ROS and ferrous iron. In conclusion, our study is the first to show that SNRPB2 is an essential regulator of ferroptosis and could be potentially targeted for the elimination of HCC cells.
LncRNAs, functioning as ceRNAs, play a crucial role in HCC carcinogenesis by modulating downstream mRNAs expression via target miRNA sequestration [ , ]. Growing evidence indicates that aberrant ceRNA regulation closely associates with ferroptosis in cancer cells, such as LINC00336 inhibits ferroptosis promoted by miR-6852 in lung carcinoma when used as a ceRNA [ ], and the NEAT1-miR-362-3p-MIOX axis is crucial in HCC ferroptosis initiation [ ]. Here, we first demonstrated that SNRPB2 was regulated by the SNHG4/miR-204-5p axis through public database prediction, correlation analysis, luciferase reporter assays, and RT-qPCR. In addition, biological function experiments revealed that suppression of proliferation, colony formation, and cell migration of HepG2 cells with SNRPB2 deletion was reversed by the overexpression of SNHG4. Moreover, enhanced SNHG4 could rescue HCC cell ferroptosis as indicated by the reduced cell death, lipid ROS level and ferrous iron and increased GSH in SNRPB2 knockdown HepG2 cells. Together, our findings suggest that SNRPB2 is regulated by the SNHG4/miR-204-5p axis, and SNHG4 could reverse the tumor suppression and ferroptosis triggering induced by deletion of SNRPB2, as a ceRNA of miR-204-5p.
Sorafenib resistance continues to be a treatment obstacle and is associated with a poor prognosis in HCC [ ]. Consequently, a thorough understanding of the fundamental mechanism that underlies sorafenib resistance in HCC could potentially improve the effectiveness of chemotherapy and direct clinical medication. Herein, we revealed that SNRPB2 was elevated in SR HCC cells by bioinformatics analysis and detection of the SNRPB2 expression level in SR HepG2 cells. Additionally, CCK8 assays demonstrated that SNRPB2 deletion increased the sensitivity of HCC cells to sorafenib. Moreover, flow cytometry analysis indicated that deletion of SNRPB2 raised the lipid ROS level of HepG2 cells treated with sorafenib, indicating SNRPB2 inhibition may enhance sorafenib-induced ferroptosis by depleting glutathione, disrupting GPX4-mediated antioxidant protection, and iron homeostasis dysregulation via expanded labile iron pools amplifies Fenton-driven lipid peroxidation. Last, we used drug sensitivity analysis and molecular binding prediction to reveal the potential of targeting SNRPB2 to improve the likelihood of unfavorable prognosis in HCC. These findings suggest that SNRPB2 is expected to be a novel potential molecular therapeutic target for enhancing life quality and sorafenib resistance in HCC patients. While our findings elucidate the in vitro essential role of SNRPB2 in sorafenib resistance in HCC, we acknowledge the inherent limitations of this cell-based approach, and the pharmacokinetic profiling and in vivo validation is critical for directing translation to therapeutic contexts.
In conclusion, our study indicated that elevated SNRPB2 expression is an independent prognostic risk factor in HCC. Additionally, we demonstrated that SNRPB2 accelerates HCC progression by inhibiting ferroptosis. Moreover, the SNHG4/miR-204-5p axis could regulate SNRPB2 to reverse the tumor inhibition induced by the deletion of SNRPB2. Furthermore, we discovered that SNRPB2 has a role in sorafenib resistance in HCC. Thus, SNRPB2-targeted therapy, particularly through the development of small-molecule inhibitors to disrupt its ferroptosis-suppressive activity, may synergize with existing ferroptosis inducers, while combinatorial regimens integrating SNRPB2 inhibition with sorafenib could overcome drug resistance via dual modulation of iron metabolism and kinase signaling pathways. However, the specific molecular mechanism and signaling pathways of SNRPB2 regulating ferroptosis and sorafenib resistance have not been investigated in this study, which could be our next research direction.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture, plasmids, sirnas, and chemicals
HCC cell lines HepG2, Hep3B, Huh-7, and LM3 were kindly supplied by the Cancer Research Institute of Central South University. The American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) offered WRL68. Cells were cultivated in DMEM medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and grown in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.
SNRPB2-siRNA was supplied by Ruibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou. The SNHG4 cDNA clone was obtained from Genechem, Shanghai. The MiR-204-5p overexpression vector was provided by GenePharma Co., Ltd., Shanghai. Transfecting plasmids and siRNA were executed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen).
Ferroptosis inducer RSL-3 (S8155), ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (S7243), apoptosis inducer VCR (S1241), autophagy inducer Rapamycin (S1039), and pyroptosis inducer LPS (S7850) + Nigericin (S6653) were provided by Selleck. Sorafenib (T0093L) was obtained from TOPSCIENCE.

## Western blot analysis
The gathered cells were lysed in a protease inhibitor cocktail-containing IP lysis buffer. Following SDS-polyacrylamide gel separation, the total protein from cell lysis was deposited onto a PVDF membrane. At 4 °C overnight, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies anti-SNRPB2 (825670, Zenbio) and anti-β-actin (A5441, Sigma), after which secondary antibodies were applied for a duration of 2 h. The ChemiDox XRS + image formation system was used to visualize the bands. The PVDF membrane was strategically cut into vertical strips containing target molecular weight regions prior to hybridization to enable parallel probing of multiple proteins of interest under identical experimental conditions. This common practice minimizes batch variability while conserving antibodies and membrane material.

## RT-qPCR
TRIzol (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan) was utilized to isolate total RNA, while cDNA was synthesized employing a PrimeScriptTM RT reagent kit (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan) containing gDNA Eraser. The ABI 7500 and the FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master were utilized to conduct RT-qPCR. To normalize relative gene expression levels, β-actin was employed. The RT-qPCR primer sequences are detailed in Table S3.

## Immunohistochemistry
The complete procedure has been described in this manuscript before [ ], and patients who donated tissues for IHC staining received informed consent. The primary antibodies used for IHC in this study containing SNRPB2 (1:300, 825670, Zenbio), 4-HNE (1:50, Abcam, ab46545), and Ki-67 (2 µg/ml, 790–4286, Roche). Zhenqin Gao, a pathologist at Zhuzhou Central Hospital’s Department of Pathology, confirmed the liver cancer biopsies.

## Cell proliferation, migration, and colony formation assays
Cell viability was assessed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions utilizing the CCK8 reagent. 4 × 104 cells with different treatments were planted in a chamber for migration tests. The cells lining the upper surface of the chamber were eliminated after 24 h, and methanol was used to preserve the chamber, which was then stained with 0.5% crystal violet. For the colony formation experiments, 1000 cells were seeded into each well of six-well plates. After approximately two weeks, the cells underwent methanol fixation and 0.5% crystal violet staining.

## Nude mice and study approval
The second Xiangya hospital of Central South University’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved all animal experiments. Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. provided all of the female nude mice at four weeks of age. HCC xenograft model was established via subcutaneous injection of 4 × 106 HCC cell suspensions (derived from the HepG2 cell line) into the back of nude mice, with tumor growth monitored every two days by caliper measurements. The nude mice were maintained under sterile, pathogen-free conditions with stringent temperature and humidity control. Certified laboratory personnel conducted daily health assessments to ensure animal welfare. The endpoint analyses including tumor weight/volume quantification, consistent with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals for subcutaneous hepatoma modeling. On day 22 post-injection, once statistically significant differences in tumor volume between experimental groups were first observed, all mice were euthanized using carbon dioxide inhalation, adhering to institutional ethical guidelines for humane endpoints.

## Measurement of lipid ROS and total GSH and iron assays
For the lipid ROS assay, cells were trypsinized and resuspended in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS and 10 µM C11-BODIPY (D3861, Thermo Fisher) after being treated for 24 h with 10 µM RSL-3. The specimens were subsequently incubated in the dark at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 30 min. Two washes with PBS were performed to eliminate any residual C11-BODIPY. C11-BODIPY581 = 591’s fluorescence was quantified via utilizing a flow cytometer (BD FACSCantoTM II) equipped with a Texas red channel and a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) green channel.
In order to detect total GSH, cells were digested and collected in centrifuge containers following a 24 h incubation with 10 µM RSL-3. After this, 3 volumes of ice-cold 5% metaphosphoric acid were introduced, followed by two cycles of freezing and thawing with liquid nitrogen and 37 °C water. After 10 min of centrifugation at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C, the supernatant was utilized in accordance with the protocol to determine total GSH.
The complete procedure has been described in this manuscript before [ ]. The measurements of ferrous iron (Fe2+) and total iron were conducted utilizing the Iron Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA).

## Dual luciferase reporter gene assay
The complete procedure has been described in this manuscript before [ ]. Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, E1910 and E1960) was employed to test how miR-204-5p affects SNRPB2/SNHG4 activity.

## Generation of SR cell lines
To generate SR cells, HepG2 cells were exposed to sorafenib at 5% of the IC50 concentration and gradually increased to 10% of the IC50 concentration until the maximum tolerated dose (10 µM) was attained. Comparative IC50 analysis revealed a 4.5-fold increase in HepG2-SR cells (24.1 ± 3.1 µM) versus parental cells (5.3 ± 0.4 µM, p < 0.001), confirming acquired resistance. To preserve the acquired resistance, HepG2-SR cells were grown continuously at 1 µM sorafenib concentration.

## Statistical analyses
Every experiment was conducted a minimum of three times, except for those involving nude mice. The data were presented in the form of the mean ± SD or SEM. For all statistical analyses, GraphPad Prism 8.0 was utilized. The Student’s t test and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were employed, respectively, to compare two or more groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.
