## Abstract
The ABCA4 protein plays an essential role in mammalian vision, ensuring the correct localization of all-trans-retinal within the visual cycle. Mutations in the ABCA4 gene are responsible for the juvenile maculopathy, Stargardt disease (STGD1). We investigated the most common variant underlying STGD1 phenotype in a rat model carrying the ortholog to the human c.5882G > A/p.(Gly1961Glu) (G1961E) in ABCA4. While the pathogenicity of this variant has recently been questioned, we examine here whether the ortholog rat variant is associated with vitamin A toxicity in the retina. By crossing the rat line with a rat line deficient in ABCA4 protein, we reveal a more pathogenic phenotype in line with compound heterozygosity, making the model suitable for testing of gene, cell and pharmacological therapies.
Keywords: Stargardt disease, genetically modified rat, G1961E variant, ATPase Binding Cassette Transporter 4

## Introduction
The ATP-binding cassette (ABC) family of transporters utilises the energy of ATP-hydrolysis to achieve unidirectional transport of phospholipids across the cell membrane, often carrying along a chemical substrate cargo [ ]. The first eukaryotic ABC importer to be identified was ABCA4, which is primarily expressed in the retina, specifically in the specialised discs of the photoreceptor outer segment (POS) where it plays a critical role in the visual cycle [ ]. These discs are loaded with the retinal chromophore needed for vision, 11-cis-retinal, which, when isomerised by light, forms the aldehyde all-trans-retinal (ATR). Additionally, the discs are rich in phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), a phospholipid that readily forms a Schiff base conjugate with retinal, known as N-retinylidene-phosphatidylethanolamine (N-Ret-PE). Through its ATPase domain, ABCA4 catalyses the flip N-Ret-PE, from the inner leaflet (lumen side of the disc space) to the outer leaflet (cytosolic side of the disc) [ ].
The reactivity that makes retinal an efficient chromophore also makes it an unstable agent that could be hazardous to the cells biochemistry if it accumulates inappropriately. If clearance of retinal into the cytosol does not occur, retinal may form potentially toxic bisretinoids, including diretinoid-pyridinium-ethanolamine (A2E), the ultimate product of condensation of two molecules of ATR and one molecule of PE. A2E and its precursors are the predominant entities found in the autofluorescent material lipofuscin, observed in aging eyes and in cases of retinal degeneration [ ]. The hollow discs that extend in the OS region from the photoreceptor cell compound this risk, as retinal can accumulate in disk spaces. The debris accumulates in retinal pigment epithelium cells (RPE), acting as a toxic component and photosensitizer of RPE lipofuscin, which is detected by its autofluorescence during ophthalmological imaging [ , ].
The gene encoding the 2273 amino-acid polypeptide chain that forms the tandem halves of ABCA4 was identified in 1997 [ , ]. In the intervening years, over 1000 mutations of the gene have been linked to Stargardt disease (STGD1), cone-rod dystrophy and autosomal recessive retinitis pigmentosa [ ]. STGD1 (OMIM #248200) is a autosomal recessive juvenile macular degeneration with a loss of central vision linked to foveal cone loss [ ]. It has been exclusively associated with mutations in ABCA4, most often missense mutations. The c.5882G > A/p.(Gly1961Glu) variant (G1961E) is by far the most prevalent, although its frequency varies significantly by geographic region, present in approximately 20% of the STGD1 European population and over 50% of cases of STGD1 in South Asia [ , ]. This variant, immediately prior to the protein’s C-terminal second nucleotide-binding domain (NBD2), is associated with a delayed onset of symptoms and a pathology limited to the parafoveal region of the macula, originally giving the G1961E-associated pathology a severity grade of mild to moderate [ ]. The variant is generally identified in compound heterozygosity with other more pathogenic ABCA4 variants, where, according to the additive pathogenicity model, it may add to the detrimental effects of the primary mutation [ , ]. However, Lee et al. (2022) reported that the G1961E variant exhibits clinical dominance, in a cohort of patients under 50 years of age. These G1961E-carrying patients have a prognosis for lower severity disease, based on autofluorescence fundus imaging, regardless of the variant allele in trans [ ]. Furthermore, in several cases, the same coding and non-coding missense variants have been identified on the same chromosome with G1961E, suggesting the presence of modifier variants in cis is necessary for pathogenicity and clinical penetrance of the G1961E allele [ , ]. Even in the absence of coding or cryptic modifiers, it is predicted that homozygous G1961E alleles can still result in disease expression under environmental pressure, such as an advanced age or excess of vitamin A, making the effects of G1961E highly variable and individual-dependent.
We chose to model the c.5882G > A/p.(Gly1961Glu) locus by altering the orthologous region in rats, to see if the variant, in the absence of known founder cis-variants, would yield a phenotype. The interest in investigating the phenotype is in part motivated by the recent paper by Müller et al. (2025) showing highly efficient base correction of the G1961E mutation in mice and non-human primates [ ]. The high efficacy of this approach makes it of interest for the clinic, provided that this variant can be shown to produce a deleterious phenotype that is additive or compounding with other mutations. If so, the clinical benefit of specifically targeting this locus for correction would be significant, given the high prevalence of the variant among STGD1 patients.
While mice have been valuable for understanding the pathogenesis of STGD1, their small eye size reduces the reproducibility of subretinal injections and increases the risk of cataract, posing a challenge for in vivo imaging [ ]. Here, we instead focused on the rat, characterising the phenotype of rats genetically modified to carry the orthologous G1961E mutation in homozygosity, as well as in compound heterozygosity with an Abca4 null allele. The findings from this model provide insights into the prognosis of individuals carrying the G1961E variant and can be of benefit for further testing of therapeutics targeting this common variant.

## Results

## Sequence analysis of human and rat ABCA4
Genetically modified rats to model the mutation G1961E were produced using CRISPR-Cas9 and single-stranded oligodinucleotides (ssODNs) [ , ]. The rat and human ABCA4 NBD2 domain show 88% identity at the protein level. In both the human and the rat proteins, the target glycine is in the highly conserved sequence of the second cytoplasmic domain of the NBD2 and is in proximity to the nucleotide-binding site (GVNGAGKT) ( ). The protein alignment suggests that a mutation of the glycine in position 1938 into a glutamic acid may lead to similar deleterious effects to those observed in the mutant G1961E of the human ABCA4 protein. To confirm this is indeed the orthologous residue, structural predictions were made using AlphaFold [ ]. Considerable efforts have allowed the structure of ABCA4 to be resolved by cryo-electron microscopy (cryoEM) in its relaxed state and with its retinal ligand N-ret-PE [ ]. We compared the prediction results for rat ABCA4 in wild-type and mutant forms to the cryoEM-resolved human ABCA4 structure (PDB: 7LKP). The two highest-ranked prediction outputs had similar pLDDT scores (76.39 for model_3 and 74.60 for model_2), a metric assesses the accuracy of the prediction, with scores ranging from 0 to 100 [ ]. Scores above 70 are generally considered to reflect high confidence. Overall, model_2 showed higher structural similarity (TM-score = 0.948) to human ABCA4 compared to model_3 (TM-score = 0.771), and as such, we chose to use model_2 output for structural visualisation and comparisons ( ) [ ].
 The corresponding rat residue to 1961 of human ABCA4 in terms of position within the protein folds is indeed the glycine at position 1938 ( ). It was noted that altering the glycine to a glutamic acid caused no evident changes in secondary structure of the flanking nucleotide-binding region containing the Walker A motif in the human or rat predictions ( ). Analysis was also performed using the PROVEAN (Protein Variation Effect Analyzer) web server tool (http://provean.jcvi.org) to establish whether the predicted impact on the biological function for the rat Abca4 G1938E mutation would be the same as for the human ABCA4 G1961E mutation [ ]. Similar PROVEAN score obtained for the human variant G1961E (−6.589) and the rat G1938E variant (−6.956), indicating a deleterious effect with high confidence on ABCA4 function by the mutation of the glycine at this position.
Based on initial in silico modelling, the rat Abca4 gene was targeted for disruption by SpCas9 protein microinjection into the pronucleus of Sprague–Dawley rat zygotes as a ribonuclear protein (RNP) complex, with a single guide RNA targeting the nuclease to exon 42 of Abca4. A 112-nt ssDNA oligo carrying the required G-to-E substitution was co-delivered with the RNP complex and oocytes screened for homology directed repair (HDR) events with introduction of G1938E change in the Abca4 gene leading to the knock-in ‘E/E’ line ( ). Two albino Sprague–Dawley rat transgenic lines, carrying the specific mutation (E/E1 and E/E2) were analysed in parallel up to 9 months of age. A PCR product from cDNA produced from the rat retinas RNA was sequenced and shown to express the expected nucleotide changes in exon 42 ( ). Once it was confirmed that similar ocular in vivo phenotypes were present in both lines, one line was maintained for further breeding and biochemical analysis. The number of rats used from each genotype for each experiment is listed in .
 While in homozygosity the pathological phenotype caused by a mutation may be mild, it could, in theory, under the additive pathogenicity model, have a more severe disease phenotype if present in heterozygosity with another ABCA4 mutation [ ]. However, the most recent study examining genotype–phenotype correlation in a cohort of patients with STGD1 due to complex ABCA4 alleles showed a mild to moderate phenotype persists regardless of the mutation in trans, while other studies show patients with more variable compound phenotypes [ , ]. We can model compound heterozygosity in the transgenic rats by crossing the E/E model with a transgenic Abca4 rat carrying a full deletion of exon 42 and previously shown to have an accumulation of retinal dimers in the retina at 3 months of age [ ]. This rat has 85% reduction in the levels of ABCA4 protein and for convenience is termed here as a knock-out Abca4−/− (−/−). The product of the E/E and −/− cross is the compound heterozygote Abca4E/− (E/−) ( ).

## Expression level of ABCA4 with NBD2 mutations in rat retinal cells
Equivalent amounts of protein from purified membrane and cytosolic fractions were loaded on a gel to compare levels between the four genotypes. Unexpectedly, standard western blotting revealed no difference in membrane levels of ABCA4 in WT/WT, E/E and E/− retinas despite the E/− genotype having a hypomorphic allele that should lead to a significant reduction in protein ( ). Repeated WB experiments show similar results, with full length blots shown in . At this concentration of loaded protein (40 μg), the mislocalised cytosolic fraction cannot be detected. Therefore, cytosolic levels were quantified using the more sensitive assay of capillary electrophoresis, or ‘Simple Western’ ( ). Once again, similar amounts of ABCA4 protein were detected in the cytosol in WT/WT, E/E and E/− rats ( ). It is possible that upregulation of levels through a post-translational modification of the G1938E protein allows a compensation for the null ABCA4 allele in trans in the E/− model. This is supported by the slight increase in membrane levels observed in standard western blotting for the E/E retinas ( ).
 ABCA4 carries out its function in the outer segment (OS) of the photoreceptors, where its preferred ligand, N-ret-PE, is abundant. The localisation of ABCA4 was examined by immunohistochemistry on retinal sections of rats at 6 months of age ( ). A fraction of ABCA4 signal is evident in the inner segment between the outer nuclear layer (ONL) and the OS of E/E and E/− rats. This region is magnified in the third panel of where the mislocalisation to the inner segment of the E/− rat photoreceptors is marked between the photoreceptor OS and the ONL. A similar retention of a minor fraction of protein was detected by Lee et al. (2021) when the G1961E human allele was expressed in trans in mouse retinas [ ].
 

## Structural and ultrastructural analysis of the Abca4E/E retina
To determine if the mutation had an impact on organisation of the retina, we measured cone OS length by opsin staining or peanut agglutinin (PNA) staining in rat retinal sections ( ). Using L/M/S opsins labelling, we find no significant differences in average length in both central and peripheral retina between WT/WT and E/E rats at 3 months ( ). The distribution of lengths is however changed, with WT/WT rats exhibiting much longer and shorter cone lengths compared to E/E rats. At 3 months of age we did not have available E/− retinas; however, a similar result is found when the whole cone OS length is measured by PNA labelling of WT, E/E and E/− rat retinal sections at 12 months of age, with length distribution limited in E/E and E/− rats compared to WT/WT ( ).
 ABCA4 is localised in rim edges of OS discs and may induce a change in ultrastructure and therefore change in OS length if the protein is misfolded. We examined the photoreceptors in electron microscopy, and found no evident difference between WT/WT, E/E and E/− ultrastructure of the OS ( ). Furthermore, spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT) showed no difference in retinal thickness with age between all genotypes (data not shown).

## Biochemical effects of the ABCA4E/E protein from rat retinas
As the G1961E mutation is close to the NBD2 ATPase domain, it was originally proposed to destabilise the ATP-dependent transport of retinal by ABCA4. However, the recombinant protein generated in HEK293 cells has been shown to be vulnerable to detergent solubilisation, impacting the folding on artificial liposomes and therefore preventing a response in ATPase assay [ , ]. We sought to investigate how the native ABCA4 protein from the OS of photoreceptors of rats behaves in proteoliposomes in ATPase assays. For this, we purified the protein by immunoprecipitation from isolated rat OS discs. Up to 12 rat retinas (n = 6 rats) were used from animals aged between 5 and 8 months at time of euthanasia, to produce each pool of OS from which the ABCA4 was immunoprecipitated ( ). The purified protein ( ) was complexed onto artificial liposomes generated by a mix of brain fatty acids and cholesterol ester. The quality of the dialysed proteolipid mix was assessed by running one batch on silver nitrate gel and by dynamic light scattering (DLS) measures. Three different batches of proteoliposomes were prepared for the WT/WT and E/E protein and tested in a microtiter luminescence-based ATPase assay as described previously [ ]. The first preparation using lipids dialysed with ABCA4 protein from WT rat retinas showed an increase in luminescence in a microtiter ATPase assay with increasing ABCA4 proteolipid. This supports the solubility and correct folding of the WT protein with a dose-dependent increase in ATPase activity ( ). It is expected that in conditions of higher concentrations of retinal, the activity of the ABCA4 transporter is increased. However, the ATPase activity of the homozygous mutant is reduced by an average of 33% with 40 μM ATR, or almost 50% reduction of the ATPase activity compared to the WT ABCA4 ( ). It is notable that the proteolipid batches may vary in the concentration of protein-containing liposomes to empty liposomes, yielding variable results. Sun et al. (1999) have shown the human recombinant G1961E protein variant has drastically reduced basal activity with no activation by retinal in vitro [ ]. Additionally, Garces et al. (2018) have shown that while the solubility of this mutant is the same as WT, its folding is likely impacted during preparation and fails to produce a proteolipid that can support ATPase activity [ ]. These studies link this defect in ATPase activity not directly to the function of the protein, but rather to a failure to reconstitute this variant in a test tube. This effect following detergent solubilisation may also impact the purified protein from the rat membranes studied here, leading to the reduced ATPase signal for the mutant protein.
 

## Bisretinoid accumulation and fundus autofluorescence in Abca4E/E and Abca4 E/− rats
Increasing autofluorescence (AF) has also been extensively described in the Abca4−/− mouse where bisretinoids may be quantified from whole eyes post-mortem by imaging, HPLC and mass spectrometry [ , ].
The in vivo consequences of defective ABCA4 should be evident with the increased accumulation of bisretinoid with age, as the protein fails to remove all-trans-retinal from the inner to the outer lumen of POS. The G1961E variant is described to cause minimal changes in the fundus of Stargardt patients [ , ]. Here we have adapted an automated pipeline using CellProfiler v4.2.8 software to analyse the fundus images extracted from the Heidelberg Spectralis imager. Representative images for the 3-month fundus ( ) and the 12-month fundus ( ) are shown. Since an internal calibrator was not integrated on the fundus imager, it was not possible to extract AF background and normalise between acquisitions. Therefore, the level of autofluorescent speckling could not be accurately quantified. Instead, LC-HRMS offers the most effective approach for detection of bisretinoids in rat retinas in terms of sensitivity and specificity [ ]. In the E/E rat we find the level of A2E is initially decreased at 3 months of age with an increase only emerging at later ages. By 12 months of age, a 1.8-fold increase in A2E compared to WT/WT is detected for the homozygous E/E condition. This is a modest drop compared to the 5.9-fold increase found for the knock-out −/− rat. For the E/− compound heterozygote genotype, there is no change measured at 3 months of age while a 3.5-fold increase in A2E level is measured at 12 months of age ( ). A2GPE, a precursor in the synthetic cascade leading to multiple vitamin A dimer products, surprisingly shows a 1.5-fold decrease in E/E eyes at 3 months of age and does not show any significant difference compared to WT/WT at 12 months of age. In the E/− model, a 1.7-fold and 2.2-fold increase in A2GPE is found at 3- and 12-months respectively ( ). While the changes in A2E and A2GPE are lower in the E/− compared to the knock-out, these retinoids represent just a fraction of the toxic bisretinoid species that may arise due to a dysfunctional visual cycle. Dimers of all-trans-retinal (dATR) have been suggested to have even more toxic effects on the photoreceptor cell than lipid-conjugated dimers such as A2E [ ]. The level of dATR is found to have an average 4.4-fold increase in the knock-out −/− genotype compared to WT/WT, while a 14.9-fold average increase is found for the E/− rat retinas ( ). The combined mass spectrometry data suggests that bisretinoid accumulation rises more steeply in the double mutant rats compared to the homozygous knock-in (E/E) or knock-out (−/−) rats and is indicative of a compound heterozygous Abca4 phenotype.
 

## Functional impact of the mutations on the retina in Abca4E/E and Abca4E/− rats
The electroretinogram (ERG), which measures the amplitude of the voltage response across the cornea to flashes of defined light intensity, is a commonly used measure of retinal function and is used here the evaluate the consequences of the G1938E mutation in homozygosity and in compound heterozygosity with the exon 42 knock-out. Amplitude responses to flashes of incrementally increasing intensity on the dark-adapted retina offer a measure of scotopic (rod photoreceptor) and photopic (cone photoreceptor) function. The a-wave amplitude response for dim light scotopic responses and are significantly reduced at 3 months in the −/− genotype however, this difference is no longer significant at 12 months ( ). Wave amplitudes for the high intensity flash reflecting cone function are found to be significantly changed between E/E and E/− at 3 months with the difference no longer evident at 12 months. As the Sprague–Dawley background is susceptible to cataract with age, the number of animals available with at 12 months without non-retinal ocular abnormality is significantly reduced and this impacts the data. The electropherogram lowest and highest amplitude traces for rod & cone b-waves, for each genotype, are shown in . The modest difference in ERG reflects a mild phenotype, and as the rat retina lacks a cone dense region, this does not model a maculopathy but may reflect a global impact of a compromised visual cycle on the tissue.
 

## Discussion
The extensive allelic heterogeneity of the human ABCA4 gene is reflected in a continuum of disease severity that correlates with the predicted effect on the proteins transport function [ ]. It is harder to place the G1961E mutation of exon 42 on this continuum as there is great variation in phenotypes between these patients depending on their age and geographic distribution [ , ]. The interest of modelling the G1961E mutation comes primarily from its prevalence among patients and motivated by the option of efficiently repairing this mutation by base-editing. Identifying whether environmental or genetic factors have a greater influence in the penetrance may help in prognosis of G1961E-associated STGD1. Moreover, determining whether G1961E editing is of clinical relevance depends on determining the level of pathogenicity associated with this variant; not as easy to achieve in patients with penetrance effects due to other genomic polymorphisms and environmental stresses. G1961E patients typically have milder fundus changes with abnormal macular cone mediated responses which may only be detected on the multifocal electroretinogram [ ]. Under an additive pathogenicity model that has been proposed for ABCA4 [ ], the pathology should be more severe when found in compound heterozygosity with a more pathogenic ABCA4 mutation, which we sought to model here in the rat. However, in a recent study, clinical dominance of the G1961E allele was reported from the subset of patients carrying this allele in trans with another mutation within a group of 112 individuals [ ]. Accordingly, a compound heterozygous E/− model would be expected to have a phenotype closer to a E/E phenotype than a −/− phenotype. Our functional and biochemical studies of the rat retina point to E/− being closer to a −/− phenotype.
In the retinas of compound heterozygote E/− offspring, A2E does not accumulate as rapidly compared to the −/− retinas. However, the potentially more toxic dimers of all-trans-retinal accumulate to double the level in E/− compared to −/−, supporting a compounding effect of the glycine substitution (‘E’ allele) on a genetic background of haplo-insufficiency (“- “allele) [ ]. Furthermore, a small though significant drop in photoreceptor function is noted at 3 months in comparing the E/E to the E/− line. However, as the ERG is particularly sensitive to polymorphisms in certain modifier genes a crucial next step is to fully sequence the genomes of the Abca4E/E and Abca4E/− lines [ ]. There are other technical limitations of our study. We were obliged to use the Sprague–Dawley rat as it is the strain found to be most amenable to production of genetically modified rats [ ]. However, this albino is subject to eye malformations and cataract with age making it necessary for us to apply strict exclusion criteria and limiting the number of rats available for study at 12 months of age.
Without a macula, rodent models are limited in the ocular phenotypes they can represent and the phenotype of a bull’s eye maculopathy in patients with the G1961E mutation cannot be recapitulated in a rat model. These models nonetheless hold value for treatment prognostics. Given the relatively high allele frequency of this variant, targeting this variant when present on one allele, even if it is not the primary mutation, may be a viable therapeutic approach for a significant number of Stargardt patients [ ]. This is of interest in the context of a recent study by Muller et al. (2025) which showed very high efficacy of base-editing for correcting the G1961E mutation in rodent, macaques and human tissue [ ]. The most promising therapy for STGD1 currently under clinical evaluation is ALK-001, a deuterated form of vitamin A that can be taken orally in addition to the vitamin A already recycling in the eye. This modified form of retinal is slow to dimerise and generate bisretinoids thus offsetting the pathology and thus far yielding encouraging trial results to obtain orphan drug designation (Gildeuretinol) [ ]. However, care may be necessary in the selection of patients; if the prevalent G1961E mutation is particularly sensitive to the accumulation of excess retinal then this may limit the efficacy of a deuterated vitamin-A treatment. Models such as the rat described here can be used to further evaluate promising future treatment strategies for Stargardt disease.

## Materials and methods

## 
In silico analysis
cDNA sequence of rat Abca4 was obtained from Ensembl, transcript ID: ENSRNOT00000017878.7. Protein sequences of human ABCA4 and rat ABCA4 were obtained from the UniProt database. Accession numbers are P78363 and A6HVF7, for human and rat, respectively.

## Molecular structure prediction
Protein structure was predicted using AlphaFold2 (version 2.3.2), accessed on the Institut Français de Bioinformatique Core Cluster platform. The prediction was performed using full-length rat ABCA4 sequence as input, with default settings and CPU relaxation. High resolution human ABCA4 cryo electron microscopy-resolved structure was obtained from the RCSB protein data bank, PDB ID: 7LKP. Human and rat ABCA4 structures were visualised using open-source PyMol (version 2.6.0a0). To assess human and rat ABCA4 structural similarity, TM-score analysis was conducted, performed with US-align [ ]. TM-score is a metric used for assessing the topological similarity of protein structures. TM-scores range between 0 (no similarities) and 1 (perfect match). AlphaFold output files, TM-score results, and PyMOL visualisation files are available upon request.

## Transgenic rat generation
A knock-in rat with an equivalent amino acid substitution to the G1961E mutation in humans (Abca4E/E rat) was generated at the Transgenese Rat et Immunophenomic Platform (TRIP) at the INSERM UMR 1064. The CRISPR-Cas9 technology combined with homologous directed repair (HDR) was used to produce the desired point mutation in the rat embryos of strain RjHan:SD (Janvier-Laboratories, France) as described [ ]. The selection of the optimal sgRNA (CCTCCAGTCCAGCAGTAGAT CGG) was carried out by the TacGene facility (http://biophysique.mnhn.fr/site/TACGENE) in Paris. The 112-nucleotide oligonucleotide targeting exon 42 for the homologous recombination was designed by TRIP and ordered from Integrated DNA technologies (https://eu.idtdna.com/pages) with the following specifications: 4 nmol ultramer (synthesis scale) and standard desalting. The sequence is: 5′- G*T*T TCT TTG AAT TTT ACA GGT TTA CTC AGG CTC CTC CAG TCC AGC AGT AGA cCG cTT ATG TGT CGA GGT CCA CCC TGG GGA GGT GGG TAC CAT ACC AAC GGA CAT GAA AT*A* G − 3′ where bases substitutions to introduce the expected mutation are in bold, substitutions to avoid cleavage after donor DNA insertion are lowercase, introduction of a restriction site (AccI) to facilitate the genotyping is underlined, asterisks represent phosphorothioate modificated bases. Animals were maintained at the Boisbonne Centre (ONIRIS, Nantes-Atlantic College of Veterinary Medicine, Food Science and Engineering, Nantes, France) under a 12/12 h light/dark cycle with food and water ad libitum. All experiments involving animals were conducted in accordance with the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology statement for the use of animals in ophthalmic and vision research. Colony productions were approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of the Pays de Loire (France) and the Ministry of Higher Education and Research (APAFIS permit numbers 9639 and 16 586). Genotyping is described in . For all experiment’s rats were euthanised by intracardiac injection of sodium pentobarbital (Vetoquinol, Lure, France) following inhalation of isoflurane gas.

## Expression analysis and immunohistochemistry

## Western blot
Samples (retinas) for western blot analysis were lysed in SIE buffer (250 mM sucrose, 3 mM imidazole pH 7.4, 1% ethanol, and 1% NP-40, protease inhibitor) to extract ABCA4 protein. After lysis, ABCA4 samples were denatured at 37°C for 15 min in 1X Laemli sample buffer supplemented with 4 M urea. The antibodies used for immuno-blotting are as follows: monoclonal anti-ABCA4 3F4(1:500, RRID: AB_1118540), monoclonal anti-β-actin (1:2000, RRID: AB_476744), monoclonal anti-GAPDH (1/1000, RRID: AB_1078991).

## Capillary electrophoresis (‘simple western’)
For membrane and cytosolic separation, the Mem-Per Plus kit (Thermofisher, Cat. #89842) was used according to manufacturer’s instructions. Protein extracts were prepared at a final concentration of 0.4 mg/ml (40 nl sampled in the capillaries: 80 ng of total protein analysed) and processed using the ‘Jess’ automate (from Bio-Techne) and the 66–440 kDa capillary cartridge from Bio-Techne (Cat. #SM-W008) and an EZ Standard Pack (Cat. #PS-ST03EZ), according to the manufacturer instructions. The anti-ABCA4 polyclonal antibody (1/500, RRID: AB_2745804) was used as primary antibody. Then, the anti-mouse Detection Module from Bio-Techne was used, according to the manufacturer instructions. For relative quantification of ABCA4 expression levels, protein normalisation was performed via total protein quantification using RePlexTMModule and Total Protein Detection Module from Bio-Techne on the same capillary, according to the manufacturer instructions. Data analyses was performed using the ‘Compass for SW’ software.

## Immunohistochemistry on frozen sections
After euthanasia, rat eyes were immediately enucleated, a hole made in the cornea and the globes fixed for 2 h in 4% paraformaldehyde. After dissection, sucrose cryoprotection and embedding in OCT compound, the retinas were cut into 10 μm sections. The slides were left with primary anti-ABCA4 antibody (RRID: AB_2745804) overnight at 4°C and treated with secondary antibodies at 37°C for 1 h. The slides were mounted and examined using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Nikon A1RSi). Images were processed using Fiji [ ].

## Immunohistochemistry on paraffin-embedded retinal sections
Whole eyes were cleaned of extra muscle and tissue and eyes were fixed with Bouin’s solution for 24 h at room temperature, before sequential ethanol washes over the course of five days at increasing concentrations of 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 100%. The eyes were then further dissected before inclusion in paraffin blocks. 4 μm sections were cut on a microtome and treated by deparaffinization using toluene/ethanol and by antigen retrieval using citrate acetate before staining with L/M (1/300, RRID: AB_177456) and S opsin (1/300, RRID: AB_177456). Following permeabilization and blocking primary antibody staining was carried out at 4°C overnight. Secondary Alexa Fluor antibody staining was carried out at room temperature for 1 h before counterstaining. Slides were scanned 48 h after embedding the sections using prolong gold mounting medium using the Hamamatsu NanoZoomer (Bacus Laboratories, Inc). The NDP.View2 analysis software was used to make measurements on the retinal scans: rectangles of 1.4 × 1 mm were designated in three regions of the peripheral and three regions of central retina. Measurements were made using the ruler tool of the outer segments of each of these rectangles and the values recorded from retinas of 5 rats for each age and genotype.

## Sample preparation and observation by scanning transmission electron microscopy
For chemical fixation, the rat retina was cut into small blocks of approximately 1 mM3 which were immersed in 1.8% glutaraldehyde (AgarScentific, Cat. #AGR1010) in 0.2 M phosphate buffer pH 7.2 for 2 h at room temperature. After washing in 0.2 M phosphate buffer, samples were post-fixed at room temperature in (a) 1% osmium tetroxide in aqueous solution for 1 h (AgarScentific, Cat. #AGR1022) with 1.5% potassium ferricyanide(III) (Sigma, Cat. #P3289). After, the samples were rinsed in ultrapure water, then dehydrated in a graded series of ethanol (30%, 60%, 90% and 100%) and acetone (50% and 100%) and finally impregnated in Agar 100 epoxy resin (AgarScentific, Cat. #AGR1031). The following day, the samples were placed in small moulds filled with resin and polymerised (37°C then 60°C) for 1 week. Ultrathin sections (80 nm) were then cut using a ‘Diatome’ diamond knife and and placed on thin bar gold grids (DeltaMicroscopies, Cat. #DT200-Au). Ultrathin sections were contrasted with Uranyless (DeltaMicroscopies, Cat. #11000) and lead citrate (DeltaMicroscopies, Cat. #11300). The grids were coated with a thin layer of platinum (0.8 nm) using a Leica EM ACE600 vacuum sputter. Finally, the grids with the samples were observed using a Zeiss GeminiSEM 300 scanning electron microscope equipped with a STEM detector. Observations were made between 20 and 30 kV, with a 7.5 μm diaphragm and a working distance of 4.4 mM.”

## Proteoliposome-based assays
ABCA4 was purified from CHAPS-solubilised retinal membranes obtained from 12 eyes of 4–8-month-old age-matched WT/WT and E/E knock-in rats using a Rim-3F4 immunoaffinity column. To achieve retinal membranes were solubilised in column buffer [10 mM HEPES, 1 mM dithiotreitol, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mg/ml brain polar lipids (BPL)] and 0.002% cholesteryl hemisuccinate from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AB) in the presence of 18 mM CHAPS detergent and applied to an immunoaffinity matrix in which the purified Rim-3F4 antibody was directly coupled to agarose. The reaction mixture was gently mixed for 1 h at 4°C, before washing and elution using 10 mM CHAPS buffer containing 0.2 mg/ml 3F4 peptide (generated by ProteoGenix, https://www.proteogenix.science/). The sample (1 mg) was added to BPL at a ratio 1:200 and dialysed against reconstitution buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 8.0, 0.15 M NaCl, and 1 mM dithiothreitol) for 24 h with 2 changes to remove the detergent and reconstitute the protein into liposomes. The protein concentration was determined on silver-nitrate stained SDS gels and proteoliposome diameter measured by Dynamic light scattering (DLS) ( ). DLS was performed using a Malvern zetasizer nano ZS. The calibration was controlled beforehand by using 30 and 300 nm solution of Nanosphere size standard. 50 μl of liposome were placed in a specific cuvette (DTS0118 from Malvern) for analysis.

## ATPase assay
The ATPase activity of the reconstituted protein was determined using the ADP-Glo Kinase Assay Kit (Promega, Cat. #V6930) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Initially a standard curve of luminescence corresponding to the conversion of ATP to ADP was generated using WT proteolipid prep 1 ( ). Up to 30 μl of ABCA4 reconstituted into liposomes and immersed in reconstitution buffer was incubated at 36°C with 250 μM ATP or an inert AMP-PNP analogue with or without all-trans-retinal for 30 minutes. The reaction was stopped by addition of the ADP-Glo reagent before incubation for a further hour at room temperature. ADP-Glo kinase detection reagent was then added before analysis on a victor luminometer (Perkin-Elmer). For presentation the basal reading at 0 μm ATR was set at 1 and readings normalised accordingly.

## Mass spectrometry
All solvents were LC–MS grade and purchased from Biosolve (Valkenswaard, Netherlands) unless otherwise specified. Rats were euthanised under dim red light and eye cups extracted in the dark, snap frozen in light-safe Eppendorf and stored at −80°C for further processing. A 2:1 methanol/chloroform mix was added to the frozen eyecups with retinyl acetate as an internal standard. Two sterile metal beads were added to each tube and the sample homogenised for 2 minutes using a tissuelyser (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Following 10-minute centrifugation at 20000 × g the supernatants were transferred to screw-top glass vials and all solvent removed under a gentle stream of nitrogen. The dried samples were solubilised in a mixture of isopropanol/acetonitrile/water (50:25:25, v:v:v, 100 μl) prior mass spectrometry analyses. Liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) analyses were performed on a Synapt™ G2 HRMS Q-TOF mass spectrometer equipped with an electrospray ionisation (ESI) interface operating in the positive mode and an Acquity H-Class® UPLC™ device (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). Samples were injected (10 μL) onto an Acquity® Charge Surface Hybrid (CSH) C18 column (2.1 mM × 100 mM, 1.7 μm; Waters Corporation) held at 55°C. The mobile phase was composed of an acetonitrile/water (60:40, v:v) mixture as solvent A and an isopropanol/acetonitrile (90:10, v:v) mixture as solvent B, each containing 10 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 0.1% formic acid. The elution was carried out using a multistep gradient of solvent B in solvent A over 22 min at a constant flow rate of 400 μl/min. The elution started at 40% solvent B, followed by a linear gradient up to 43% solvent B over 2 min. Solvent B was immediately increased up to 50%, and ramped up to 54% over 10 min. Next, solvent B was increased to 70%, then ramped to 99% over 6 min, and maintained at 99% for 2 min. The eluent composition returned to the initial conditions, and the LC column was equilibrated for 2 min prior the next injection. The full-HRMS mode was applied for detection (mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) range 50–1200) at a mass resolution of 30 000 full-widths at half maximum. The ionisation settings were as follows: capillary voltage, +2 kV; cone voltage, 30 V; desolvation gas (N2) flow rate, 900 L/h; desolvation gas/source temperatures, 550/120°C. Leucine enkephalin solution (2 μg/ml, 50% acetonitrile) was infused at a constant flow rate of 10 μl/min in the lockspray channel, allowing for correction of the measured m/z throughout the batch (theoretical m/z 556.2771 in positive mode). Data acquisition and processing were achieved using MassLynx® and TargetLynx® software (version 4.1, Waters Corporation). Biomarkers were extracted from the detected variables using their exact mass measured ±5 ppm, elemental compositions, retention times in comparison to reference standards, fragmentation patterns). Extracted ion chromatograms were obtained at m/z ratio of 592.4618, 746.4550, 551.4253 and 329.2481 for A2E, A2GPE, dATR and retinyl acetate, respectively. Chromatographic peaks were then integrated and normalised with that of internal standard.

## 
In vivo imaging and analysis

In vivo imaging was performed one week before each ERG using the Spectralis® HRA + OCT imaging system (Heidelberg Engineering Inc). Contact lenses were not used for this imaging and the camera did not have an internal calibrator for fundus acqusition. Animals were placed on an adjustable platform, and the camera aligned perpendicular to the animal directly in front of the eye. The NIR reflectance mode in fundus imaging using the 820 nm laser was used for alignment and to ensure equivalent illumination over the 55° acquisition field for each animal. Images were recorded in automatic real time mode with imaging carried out in a mid-peripheral region superior to the optic disc. Fundus analysis was carried out by importing all the fundus images into the images module of CellProfiler v4.2.8 image analysis software [ , ].

## Electroretinogram
After overnight dark-adaptation, rats were sedated by gas anaesthesia followed by intramuscular injection of a mixed cocktail of xylazine/ketamine (Rompun® 2%—Imalgène 1000) and maintained by inhalation of isoflurane gas. ERGs were recorded bilaterally with contact lens electrodes following pupil dilation (Tropicamide, Novartis) and local anaesthesia (Chlorhydrate oxybuprocaine, Théa, France). The rats were dark-adapted overnight before recording the dark-adapted 0.01 cd/m−2 white flash ERG (rod response) with a minimum of 2 seconds between flashes. The combined rod-cone response was evoked using white 3.0 cd/m−2 white flashes with 10 seconds between each flash. The rats were then exposed to a 15-minute light adaptation before recording the cone single-flash response using a 3.0 cd/m−2. Approximately 10 stimulations were carried out at each intensity of flash to obtain the stabilised measure between left and right eyes.

## Statistical methods
Statistical analyses were performed in JupyterLab or GraphPad Prism version 8.0.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California USA). For comparisons involving three or more groups, the choice of statistical test was determined based on the distribution and variance of the data. When the Shapiro–Wilk test indicated a normal distribution for all groups and the Brown-Forsythe test showed homogeneity of variances, parametric tests were employed. Specifically, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. In cases where the data did not meet the assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variances, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test was used, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For comparisons involving two groups, a Student’s t-test was used when the data were normally distributed and variances were homogeneous; otherwise, the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test was applied. All tests were run as two-tailed. Significance levels were reported using asterisks (*) for parametric tests and hash symbols (#) for non-parametric tests.
