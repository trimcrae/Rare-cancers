## Abstract

In
situ immunization (ISI) has emerged as a promising approach to bolster early phases of the cancer immunity cycle through improved T-cell priming. One class of ISI agents, oncolytic viruses (OV), has demonstrated clinical activity, but overall benefit remains limited. Mounting evidence suggests that due to their inherent vulnerability to antiviral effects of type I IFN, OVs have limited activity in solid tumors expressing stimulator of interferon genes (STING) and/or retinoic acid–inducible gene I (RIG-I). Here, using a combination of pharmacologic, genetic, and in vivo approaches, we demonstrate that VAX014, a bacterial minicell–based oncolytic ISI agent, activates both STING and RIG-I and leverages this activity to work best in STING-positive and/or RIG-I–positive tumors. Intratumoral treatment of established syngeneic tumors expressing STING and RIG-I with VAX014 resulted in 100% tumor clearance in two mouse models. Antitumor activity of VAX014 was shown to be dependent on both tumor-intrinsic STING and RIG-I with additive activity stemming from host-intrinsic STING. Analysis of human solid tumor datasets demonstrated STING and RIG-I co-expression is prevalent in solid tumors and associates with clinical benefit in many indications, particularly those most amenable to intratumoral administration. These collective findings differentiate VAX014 from OVs by elucidating the ability of this agent to elicit antitumor activity in STING-positive and/or RIG-I–positive solid tumors and provide evidence that STING/RIG-I agonism is part of VAX014’s mechanism of action. Taken together, this work supports the ongoing clinical investigation of VAX014 treatment as an alternative to OV therapy in patients with solid tumors.

## Introduction
Immunotherapy with systemically administered immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has resulted in remarkable treatment outcomes, albeit only in a limited number of patients across multiple solid tumor indications ( ). As we continue to unravel the cellular and molecular underpinnings of the cancer immunity cycle, it has become increasingly evident that the pharmacodynamic activity of current ICB agents primarily targets the later steps of the cycle (i.e., during and/or following T-cell priming; refs. , ). For example, ICB directed against PDCD1/CD274 (PD-1/PD-L1) immune checkpoint axis works to prevent, and to some degree reverse, exhaustion of previously activated tumor-specific T cells ( ). Given the strict requirement for sufficient T-cell priming in this scenario, it is no surprise ICB does not work well in patients who have not adequately primed a T-cell response. This can occur in tumors that are less immunogenic, have escaped the immune response through immunoediting, or have acquired deficiencies in antigen processing/presentation machinery in the face of immunologic pressure ( , ). Thus, agents or cellular approaches designed to address the early innate steps of the cancer immunity cycle to improve T-cell priming are an attractive therapeutic complement to potentiate ICB therapy.
One approach to bolster de novo T-cell priming that has gained substantial attention in recent years is in situ immunization (ISI), the therapeutic principle of which is to use the tumor itself as the source of tumor antigens necessary for immune recognition and activation, by direct intratumoral (i.t.) injection of an immunostimulatory agent ( – ). Oncolytic viruses (OV) have arguably been the best-in-class ISI agents developed to date as evidenced by recent market approvals for talimogene laherparepvec (IMLYGIC) and nadofaragene firadenovec-vncg (ADSTILADRIN) for treatment of melanoma and nonmuscle invasive bladder cancer, respectively ( , ). However, efficacy data from pivotal trials for first-generation OVs and earlier-stage trials for second-generation OVs “armed” with immunomodulatory transgene(s) exhibit plateauing overall response rates (ORR) coinciding with a low frequency of durable complete responses (CR) in the monotherapy setting ( , ). Trials combining OVs with ICB generally produce higher overall response rates and CRs, but the observed increases are typically incremental, and response durability remains a challenge ( – ). Nonetheless, data from trials investigating OVs in combination with ICB are encouraging, support the rationale for future combination of ISI agents with ICB, and warrant further investment, particularly on the ISI agent side of the equation.
Mounting evidence supports that the therapeutic ceiling of OVs may stem from their ineffectiveness in tumors intrinsically positive for cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (CGAS; cGAS)/stimulator of interferon genes (STING1; STING) and/or retinoic acid–inducible gene I (RIGI; RIG-I)–like receptors (RLR; refs. – ). This is a direct result of these pathways to promote a type I interferon (IFN)mediated antiviral state, leading to rapid elimination of the virus ( , ). This basic limitation applies to all OVs because activation of the cGAS/STING pathway is triggered by DNA viruses and RLRs by RNA viruses upon recognition of the viral genome in the cytosol following release from the endosomal compartment ( ). This creates a therapeutic paradox because, on one hand, the type I IFN response to infection counteracts the ability of the virus to replicate and lyse tumor cells yet at the same time is required to spur innate immune activation and promote functional maturation of antigen-presenting cells to facilitate cogent antitumor T-cell priming ( , ). Therefore, there is compelling rationale to develop new oncolytic agents for use in type I IFN-competent tumors (e.g., STING-positive and RLR-I–positive tumors) to circumvent this issue.
Our previous work has demonstrated VAX014, a clinical-stage, tumor-targeted oncolytic agent based on recombinant bacterial minicells (rBMC), facilitates ISI and synergizes with ICB in multiple murine solid tumor models with diverse immunophenotypes ( – ). This novel replication incompetent agent utilizes an rBMC surface-localized bacterial protein, invasin, to selectively target tumor-associated α3β1 and/or α5β1 integrins in the tumor-specific open and unligated conformation to drive endocytosis and intracellular delivery of a preformed membrane pore-forming protein toxin, perfringolysin O (PFO; refs. – ). Upon liberation in the endosome, PFO sequentially induces endosomal membrane disruption, cytosolic entry, and rapid plasma membrane permeabilization resulting in oncolysis ( , ). In this report, we use a series of pharmacologic and genetic approaches to demonstrate for the first time that VAX014 co-activates the STING and RIG-I pathways while exerting its rapid oncolytic effects. We show that i.t. administration of VAX014 to established solid tumors intrinsically positive for STING and RIG-I results in a 100% CR rate in multiple immune-competent tumor models and that targeted genetic ablation of tumor-intrinsic Sting1 (STING) and/or Rigi (RIG-I) compromises antitumor activity, supporting that VAX014 activates these two pathways as a component of its antitumor pharmacodynamic activity in vivo. Loss of antitumor activity was more pronounced when STING was absent from tumors (in comparison with RIG-I) and absence of STING in the host led to a further reduction in activity, indicating a role for host STING as a component of response to VAX014. To address the translational aspect of our nonclinical findings, we analyzed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database to reveal co-expression of STING and RIG-I is prominent in most human solid tumor indications and is often associated with better clinical outcomes, particularly in those indications most amenable to i.t. administration. Taken together, this work establishes VAX014 as a novel oncolytic agent suitable for use in STING-positive and/or RIG-I–positive solid tumors, which may represent a major advancement over OVs in the field of ISI.

## Materials and Methods

## Cell lines
MB49 murine urothelial carcinoma cells were a gift from Dr. W.T. Godbey (Tulane University, RRID:CVCL_7076) and the MC38 murine colorectal carcinoma cell line was provided by Dr. Steven Fiering (Dartmouth College, RRID:CVCL_B288). The J774A.1 (Cat# TIB-67, RRID:CVCL_0358), LLC1 (Cat# CRL-1642, RRID:CVCL_4358), HeLa (Cat# CCL-2, RRID:CVCL_0030), A375 (Cat# CRL-1619, RRID:CVCL_0132), RT4 (Cat# HTB-2, RRID:CVCL_0036), T24 (Cat# HTB-4, RRID:CVCL_0554), BxPC3 (Cat# CRL-1687, RRID:CVCL_0186), PANC1 (Cat# CRL-1469, RRID:CVCL_0480), and HT29 (Cat# HTB-38, RRID:CVCL_0320) cell lines were purchased from the ATCC. MB49, MC38, J774A.1, LLC1, HeLa, A375, and RT4 cells were maintained in DMEM (Corning, Cat# 10-170-CV), supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated FBS (HI-FBS, Corning, Cat# 35011CV or Gibco, Cat# 10438026) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S, Gibco, Cat# 15140-122). T24, BxPC3, PANC1, and HT29 cells were maintained in RPMI (Corning, Cat# 10-040-CV) + 10% HI-FBS + 1% P/S. 293-Dual mSTING [ISG-SEAP/KI-(IFN-β)Lucia] cells were purchased from InvivoGen (ISG/KI-IFN-b, InvivoGen, Cat# 293d-mstg, RRID:CVCL_A8BP) and grown in DMEM + 10% HI-FBS + 1% P/S supplemented with 100 µg/mL Normocin (InvivoGen, Cat# ant-nr-05), 10 µg/mL blasticidin (InvivoGen, Cat# ant-bl-05), 100 µg/mL hygromycin (InvivoGen, Cat# ant-hg-1), and 100 µg/mL Zeocin (InvivoGen, Cat# ant-zn-1) to retain the secreted embryonic alkaline phosphatase/luciferase (SEAP/Luc) reporter construct. Cell lines were authenticated and tested for Mycoplasma contamination by each respective original vendor and were not reevaluated throughout the course of this work. Each cell line was passaged at least one after thawing before use in any experiments.
Unless otherwise noted, all cells were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 in a humidified tissue culture incubator. At the time of subculture or use in a cellular assay, cells were washed once in 1× PBS (Corning, Cat# 21040CV) and either enzymatically detached by incubation with 0.5 to 3 mL TrypLE Express Enzyme (Gibco, Cat# 12604-013) for 5 to 10 minutes at 37°C with 5% CO2 or physically detached with a cell scraper. Cells were washed once in culture media (5–10 mL of DMEM or RPMI containing 10% HI-FBS and 1% P/S), resuspended in 5 to 10 mL media, and subcultured into a new flask, prepared for plating, or processed for flow cytometry.

## Recombinant bacterial minicells
Both VAX014 rBMCs (Escherichia coli rBMCs with immune-attenuated LPS, containing rBMC surface-localized invasin from Yersinia pseudotuberculosis and cytosolic PFO) and control VAX-I rBMCs (E. coli rBMCs with immune-attenuated LPS, containing rBMC surface localized invasin but lacking PFO) were produced by Vaxiion Therapeutics. The structure, function, and production methods of VAX014 rBMCs (referred to as “VAX-IP” in previous reports) and control VAX-I rBMCs have been described elsewhere ( ). VAX014 rBMCs used in these studies met all applicable current product specifications utilized for clinical release. More detail on rBMC production methods utilized for this study can be found in Supplementary Methods S1.

## Mice
Six- to eight-week-old female C57BL/6J mice (Cat# 000664, RRID:IMSR_JAX:000664) or Goldenticket C57BL/6J-Sting1gt/J mice (Cat# 017537, RRID:IMSR_JAX:017537) were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. Mice were housed 3 to 5 per cage in microisolator cages and supplied with standard bedding, and chow and water were provided ad libitum. All mice husbandry and treatment were conducted in accordance with the NIH and American Association for Laboratory Animal Science regulations, and all experimental procedures were approved by the San Diego State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee under protocol numbers 19-06-004Mc and IACUC-22-064.

## Flow cytometry
Cell suspensions were pelleted at 133 to 1,050 × g for 5 to 10 minutes, supernatant aspirated, and cell pellets resuspended in 0.2 to 1 mL media or wash buffer comprising 1× PBS + 10% HI-FBS ± 0.1% (w/v) sodium azide (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# S8032). Cell concentrations were then measured using the Scepter 2.0 cell counter (Millipore, Cat# PHCC20060) or Scepter 3.0 cell counter (Millipore, Cat# PHCC30000) with 40 µm (Millipore, Cat# PHCC40050 or PHCC340050) or 60 µm (Millipore, Cat# PHCC60050 or PHCC360050) tips or hemocytometer. Samples were diluted in wash buffer to obtain 2.0 × 105 – 1.0 × 106 cells/100 µL in microcentrifuge tubes (Fisher Scientific, Cat# 05-408-137) or 96-well cell culture v-bottom plates (Corning, Cat# 3894), centrifuged where needed at 133 to 1,050 × g at 4°C for 5 minutes, and resuspended in 100 µL wash buffer. Fc receptor–blocking antibody (5–10 µg/mL) was added to each tube/well containing 2.0 × 105 to 1.0 × 106 cells and incubated at 4°C for 20 minutes, followed by incubation with primary antibody or their corresponding isotype for an additional 30 minutes at 4°C. Cells were then washed twice in wash buffer by centrifugation at 133 to 1,050 × g for 5 minutes at 4°C. Where required, secondary detection antibody was added, and cells were incubated for another 30 minutes at 4°C. Cells were then washed three times at 133 to 1,050 × g for 5 minutes, resuspended in 200 to 500 µL wash buffer, and analyzed on a Stratedigm flow cytometer (Stratedigm) or CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter). The gating strategy consisted of gating on live cells via forward and side scatter followed by doublet exclusion. Where used, mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of samples was recorded, and fold increase in expression was calculated using the formula: Fold increase = MFI of treated sample ± corresponding inhibitor/mean MFI of untreated samples ± corresponding inhibitor (where applicable). For raw flow cytometry data, histograms were generated using the CellCapTure 3.1 (Stratedigm) or CytExpert 2.6 (Beckman Coulter, RRID:SCR_017217) software. Specific antibody concentrations used across studies are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

## 
In vitro analysis of PD-L1 and MHC-I surface expression
Cell suspensions were diluted in culture media to obtain 5.0 × 105 to 1.0 × 106 cells/well, seeded into six-well plates (Corning, Cat# 3506 or 3516) at 3 mL per well, and allowed to adhere for 4 to 6 hours. Next, cells were left untreated or treated with VAX014 or VAX-I rBMCs at a multiplicity of infection (MOI; rBMCs-to-mammalian cell ratio) equal to 300:1 (results in ∼50% cell death; EC50), or DMXAA (InvivoGen, Cat# tlrl-dmx) at 30 µg/mL. Cells were centrifuged at 671 × g for 10 minutes and incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2 for ∼20 hours. Cells were then washed once with 1 to 3 mL of 1× PBS, detached from the plate via cell scraping in culture media, and processed for flow cytometry against PD-L1 and/or major histocompatibility complex I (MHC-I). Where indicated, the murine TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) inhibitor, amlexanox (Amx; InvivoGen, Cat# inh-amx), was added at a concentration of 100 µg/mL, and/or the STING inhibitor, H-151 (InvivoGen, Cat# inh-h151), was added at a concentration of 0.4 to 4 µg/mL at the time of cell plating.

## Supernatant transfer experiments
To prepare supernatants for transfer, cells were cultured and treated with VAX014 or VAX-I rBMCs or DMXAA as described above. After ∼20 to 24 hours, supernatants were collected, clarified by centrifugation at 133 to 671 × g for 5 to 10 minutes, and passed through 0.2 µm filters (Corning, Cat# 431219, or Fisher, Cat# SF-SFCA-2230-S) before being transferred to treatment naïve recipient cells as outlined below.
Treatment-naïve recipient cells were plated as described in the previous section and allowed to adhere for 4 to 6 hours. Supernatants were then transferred, and cells were incubated for an additional ∼20 hours before being processed for PD-L1 and MHC-I expression via flow cytometry. Where indicated, supernatants were subjected to a heat-treatment step at 65°C for 30 minutes prior to supernatant transfer. In experiments involving functional blockade of interferon alpha and beta receptor 1 (IFNAR1), the treatment-naïve recipient cells were preincubated with the IFNAR1 blocking antibody (BioLegend, Cat# 127322, RRID:AB_11149116) at 40 µg/mL for 1 hour prior to supernatant transfer and then maintained at 10 µg/mL during the 20-hour incubation with transferred supernatant. In experiments where supernatants from DMXAA-treated cells were used, treatment-naïve recipient cells were pretreated with 4 µg/mL H-151 at the time of initial naïve cell plating to prevent STING activation in recipient cells from residual DMXAA in transferred supernatants, and an additional 1.7 µg/mL H-151 was added to experimental supernatants to maintain inhibition.

## Quantification of murine IFN-β in tissue culture supernatants via ELISA
Cells were plated at 5.0 × 105 to 1.0 × 106/well and treated with VAX014 rBMCs (MOI 300), VAX-I rBMCs (MOI 300), or DMXAA (30 µg/mL). Where indicated, Amx (100 µg/mL) or H-151 (0.4–4 µg/mL) was incorporated at the time of cell plating. After ∼20 hours, supernatant samples were collected and frozen at −70°C until analysis using the LumiKine Xpress mIFN-β 2.0 ELISA assay (InvivoGen, Cat# luex-mifnbv2). Per the manufacturer’s instructions, the murine IFNB1 (IFN-β; referred to as mIFN-β) capture antibody solution was prepared (5 µg/mL), and 50 µL was added to each well of a 96-well ELISA plate, which was then covered with an adhesive seal and incubated overnight at room temperature. The next day, the capture antibody solution was removed from the plate by inversion, and 200 µL per well of blocking solution consisting of 1× PBS +3% (w/v) BSA (Calbiochem, Cat# 2930) + 0.05% (v/v) Tween 20 (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# P1379) was added to each well and allowed to incubate for 2 hours at 37°C. After 2 hours, blocking solution was removed from the ELISA plate by inversion, and samples were added in duplicate (50 µL per well) as was a standard curve of mIFN-β ranging from 2,000 to 15 pg/mL. Lucia-conjugated detection antibody (30 ng/mL, 50 µL per well) was immediately added to each test well. The plate was covered with an adhesive seal and incubated for 2 hours at 37°C and then washed three times with 1× PBS +0.05% (v/v) Tween 20, followed by addition of the QUANTI-Luc Plus Solution (50 µL per well). Luminescence was measured using the SpectraMax M3 plate reader (Molecular Devices) at wavelengths between 250 and 800 nm with an integration time of 0.1 to 0.5 seconds. The SoftMax Pro 5.4 software (Molecular Devices, RRID:SCR_014240) was used to record data and generate a standard curve based on the four-parameter logistic equation to quantify mIFN-β concentration in the samples. All samples below the quantification limit for the assay were recorded and graphed as zero.

## Quantification of murine IFN-α in MB49 supernatants via ELISA
MB49 cells were plated at 5.0 × 105/well in six-well plates and treated with VAX014 (MOI 300), VAX-I (MOI 300), or DMXAA (30 µg/mL). After ∼20 hours of coincubation, supernatant samples were collected and frozen at −70°C until use in the PBL Mouse IFN-α ELISA kit (PBL Assay Science, Cat# 42120-1). The standard curve ranging from 800 to 12.5 pg/mL was prepared by diluting the mIFN-α standard in DMEM per the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples, standards, and blanks were added in duplicate (100 µL/well) to the ELISA plate, which was supplied precoated with the capture antibody. The primary detection antibody solution was prepared following the manufacturer’s instructions and was added (50 µL/well); the plate was covered with an adhesive film, incubated for 1 hour at room temperature while shaking at 450 rpm, and then moved to 4°C for an additional 20 to 24 hours. Next, the wash and horseradish peroxidase (HRP) solutions were prepared per the manufacturer’s instructions, the contents of the plate were emptied, and the plate was washed four times with 300 µL/well diluted wash solution. HRP solution was then added (100 µL/well), and the plate was incubated for 2 hours at room temperature while shaking at 450 rpm. The plate was then washed four times with wash solution (300 µL/well) and emptied by inversion, and 100 µL/well of the TMB Substrate Solution was added. The plate was incubated in the dark for 15 minutes at room temperature before adding 100 µL/well stop solution. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using the SpectraMax M3 plate reader, and the optical density (OD) values were plotted using a four-parameter logistic fit for the standard curve using the SoftMax Pro 5.4 software. The standard curve was used to quantify the concentration of mIFN-α present in the test samples. Raw OD values of the blank and samples were plotted with the raw OD of the lowest detectable step on the standard curve (25 pg/mL).

## Analysis of VAX014-mediated TLR3 activation
VAX014-mediated Toll-like receptor 3 (TLR3) activation was evaluated using MB49 and J774A.1 cells, the latter of which is known to express TLR3 and respond to Poly (I:C) ( ). Cells were seeded into six-well plates at 5.0 × 105/well. After cells adhered to plates, cells were either left untreated or treated with the TLR3 agonist Poly (I:C) (InvivoGen, Cat# tlrl-pic) at a concentration of 0.65 µg/mL, VAX-I rBMCs, or VAX014 rBMCs (MOI 300 for MB49 and MOI ≥30 for J774A.1). After treatment, plates were centrifuged at 671 × g for 10 minutes and incubated overnight (∼20 hours). The next day, cells were enzymatically detached from plates and evaluated for surface expression of PD-L1 and MHC-I on the CytoFLEX flow cytometer as described above. For supernatant transfer experiments, J774A.1 supernatants were collected, clarified of cell debris, filter sterilized, and placed onto treatment-naïve MB49 cells for 20 hours. Supernatant-treated cells were then evaluated for PD-L1 and MHC-I surface expression by flow cytometry. For all treated MB49 and J774A.1 supernatants, the concentration of IFN-β was measured via ELISA as described above.

## Western blotting
Histocultures were washed at least one time with ice-cold 1× PBS and either RIPA buffer (Alfa Aesar, Cat# J63306) or RIPA extraction buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 89900) with 1% (v/v) protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 78441) was added to cells at a ratio of 100 µL per ∼1.0 × 106 cells. Cells were then scraped, pooled across conditions where necessary, agitated via intermittent shaking of the plate by hand or via tube rotator for 5 to 30 minutes at 4°C, and sonicated where needed for 30 seconds to facilitate complete lysis. Lysates were clarified of cell debris by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 minutes and stored at −70°C until further use. The total protein concentration of each lysate was measured via Bradford assay (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# B6916) or bicinchoninic acid assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 23227). Cell lysates were diluted in SDS (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# L4390) buffer to obtain 11.5 mg/mL SDS, heated at 99°C for 5 minutes, and an equal concentration of protein (ranging between ∼20 and 55 µg) was loaded for each sample into wells of an 8% to 16% Tris-glycine gel (Life Technologies, Cat# XP08162BOX). For each target, a recombinant protein was included as a positive control, and a protein ladder was used for size reference (Life Technologies, Cat# LC5625 or LC5925). Gels were electrophoresed in 1× running buffer consisting of 3.03 mg/mL Tris base (J.T. Baker, Cat# 4109-02) containing 14.3 mg/mL glycine (Fisher Scientific, Cat# BP381-1) and 1 mg/mL SDS in water for 1 hour at room temperature at 170 V. Once complete, the gel apparatus was disassembled, and gels were equilibrated in 1× Towbin transfer buffer [3.03 mg/mL Tris base, 14.3 mg/mL glycine, 20% (v/v) methanol (Fisher Scientific, Cat# A412-4) in water] twice for 15 minutes each. Proteins in gels were then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Life Technologies, Cat# LC2001) or polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Millipore, Cat# IPVH00005) at a maximum of 25 V for 30 to 45 minutes using the Trans-Blot semi-dry transfer apparatus (Bio-Rad). Transfer membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C in blocking buffer consisting of 5% (w/v) skim milk in wash buffer [1× PBS ± 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20] and then incubated with primary antibody (diluted in blocking buffer) for 1 hour at room temperature with gentle agitation or at 4°C overnight. Membranes were then washed three times in wash buffer for 5 minutes each and then incubated with secondary antibody (diluted in blocking buffer) for 1 hour at room temperature. For blots targeting phosphorylated interferon regulatory factor 3 (pIRF3), the blocking step was performed in blocking buffer consisting of 5% (w/v) skim milk in 1× TBS + 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20 (TBST) at room temperature for 1 to 2 hours; the blot was washed three times in 1× TBST for 5 minutes each and incubated with primary antibody [diluted in 1× TBST + 5% (w/v) BSA] overnight at 4°C. Membranes were washed three times in wash buffer for 5 minutes each prior to incubation with secondary detection antibody for 1 hour at room temperature with gentle agitation.
After secondary detection antibody incubation, all blots were washed four times for 5 minutes each in wash buffer and incubated for 5 minutes with the Amersham ECL Plus visibility reagent (GE, Cat# RPN2232) prior to visualization using the UVP BioSpectrum AC Imaging System. For blots that utilized a biotin-conjugated/streptavidin HRP tertiary detection reagent system to enhance signal, streptavidin HRP was added to the membrane and incubated for an additional hour; membranes were washed 4 times for 5 minutes each in wash buffer and incubated with ECL reagent. Where necessary, the blot was stripped using the Restore Western Blot Stripping Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 21059) for 15 to 40 minutes at 37°C. Successful stripping was confirmed by performing two tests. To test the removal of the HRP enzyme conjugate, the blot was reincubated with ECL reagent and visualized. To test for removal of the primary antibody, the blot was reincubated with secondary antibody and washed, the ECL reagent was added, and the blot was visualized for any remaining signal. Once successful stripping was confirmed, membranes were reblocked overnight prior to probing for a secondary target protein.
The ratio of pIRF3 to total IRF3 was calculated using densitometry (VisionWorks LS software, version 8) and the following equations:Sample normalization factor = Mean density of vinculin in untreated sample/Mean density of vinculin in treated sampleCorrected IRF3 = Mean density of IRF3 in sample × Sample normalization factorCorrected pIRF3 = Mean density of pIRF3 in sample × Sample normalization factorRatio of pIRF3 to IRF3 = Corrected pIRF3/Corrected IRF3

## Murine STING and human IFN-β reporter assays
Prior to the assay, 293-Dual mSTING cells were grown to 70% confluency in their respective media containing selective antibiotics. Flasks were washed with 1× PBS, and adherent cells were collected using media. Cells were centrifuged at 133 × g and resuspended in media with no selective antibiotics, and concentration was measured using the Scepter 2.0 cell counter and 60 µm tips. Cells were seeded at 50,000/well (180 µL/well) into a 96-well tissue culture CellBIND plate (Corning, Cat# 3300) in media without selective antibiotics and allowed to adhere for 4 hours at 37°C with 5% CO2 before treatment with VAX014 rBMCs or VAX-I rBMCs at MOI 70 (EC50 of 293-Dual mSTING cells). At the time of rBMC addition, DMXAA (10 µmol/L, 20 µL of 30 µg/mL) was added to control wells. Following addition of rBMCs, plates were centrifuged at 671 × g for 10 minutes and incubated overnight. Supernatants were collected (∼20 hours) and assayed for SEAP enzymatic activity (mSTING reporter) and luciferase activity (huIFN-β transcriptional reporter). For SEAP enzymatic activity, 20 µL of supernatant was added to 180 µL QUANTI-Blue solution (InvivoGen, Cat# rep-qbs) in a flat-bottom, 96-well clear plate (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# 655101) and incubated for 2 hours at room temperature. Luciferase activity was analyzed by adding 20 µL of supernatant to 50 µL of QUANTI-Luc solution (InvivoGen, Cat# rep-qlc4lg1) in a flat-bottom, 96-well white wall plate (Corning, Cat# 3610). SEAP activity was measured by absorbance at 655 nm, and luciferase activity was immediately measured as luminescence signal at 100 ms using the luminometer function of the SpectraMax M3 plate reader. Absorbance and luminescence values of the wells containing media without cells were subtracted from raw values to account for background signal.

## Targeted genetic ablation of STING and/or RIG-I via CRISPR-Cas9
Cells were cultured as described above, plated in six-well plates at 1.75 × 105 cells/well in 3 mL DMEM + 10% HI-FBS, and incubated for 24 hours. Cells were then dual-transfected with CRISPR-Cas9 plasmid DNA targeting murine STING (Sting1 gene, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat# 428364) and/or murine RIG-I (Rigi gene, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat#432915) along with a corresponding homology-directed repair (HDR) plasmid (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat# 428364-HDR or 432915-HDR) using a ratio of 2 µg total plasmid DNA to 10 µL UltraCruz Transfection Reagent (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat# 395739) per the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 hours, cells were visualized on the EVOS fluorescence microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific), successful co-transfection was confirmed via expression of GFP (from the CRISPR-Cas9 plasmid) and red fluorescent protein (RFP; from the HDR plasmid), and transfected cells were exposed to a 5- to 8-day selection in DMEM + 10% HI-FBS + 5 to 10 µg/mL puromycin dihydrochloride (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat# 108071). Surviving cells were then prepared for clonal dilution as follows. Cells were washed one time in 1× PBS, enzymatically detached, washed once in DMEM + 10% HI-FBS, and resuspended in DMEM + 10% HI-FBS with no puromycin. The concentration of cells was measured using a hemocytometer, and suspensions were diluted to a target concentration of 8 cells/mL in media and plated in 96-well CellBIND plates at 100 µL per well (approximately 0.8 cells/well). Plates were incubated until colonies began to grow and expand in approximately half of the seeded wells. For those wells with colonies, media was replaced when necessary to maintain growth until a sufficient number of cells were available for expansion. Following expansion, prospective clones derived from single colonies that were RFP(+)- and puromycin-resistant were frozen down for functional characterization (e.g., loss of STING activity in response to DMXAA). Following functional characterization, select clones were subject to removal of the RFP and puromycin resistance genes introduced from the CRISPR/Cas9 procedure.
To remove the RFP- and puromycin-resistant genes, STING and/or RIG-I knockout (KO) cells were plated in six-well plates (3 mL/well) at a concentration of 3.33 × 104 cells/mL (1 × 105 cells/well) and incubated for 24 hours. Cells were then transfected with a Cre vector (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat# 418923) using a ratio of 4 µg plasmid DNA to 10 µL UltraCruz Transfection Reagent per the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 hours, cells were prepared for clonal dilution as described above. Plates were incubated until colonies began to grow, and media were replaced when necessary. Single colonies that were RFP(−)- and puromycin-sensitive were expanded, and functional characterization was reconfirmed. To create the MB49 double KO cells (deficient in both STING and RIG-I), STING was knocked out in the MB49 RIG-I KO cell line, or RIG-I was knocked out in the MB49 STING KO cell line using the same procedures as outlined above.

## Analysis of oncolytic activity of VAX014 via lactate dehydrogenase assay
For analysis of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) in supernatants, cells were seeded in 96-well CellBIND plates at a concentration of 5.0 × 104 cells/mL (5,000 cells/well). Plates were either incubated for 4 hours or overnight and then treated with a titration of VAX014 rBMCs (or saline control) that yielded MOI’s ranging in 10,000:1 to 4:1. Plates were centrifuged at 671 × g for 10 minutes and incubated for an additional 2 hours at which time supernatants were removed for analysis. Fresh media was added, and remaining cells were then used in cell viability assays as described below. The level of secreted LDH in the supernatant was assayed using the CytoTox 96 Non-Radioactive Cytotoxicity Assay kit following the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega, Cat# G1780). Assay controls included a 100% viable cells-only control, a 0% viable media-only control, and a 100% lysis control. Absorbance was measured at 490 nm using the SpectraMax M3 spectrophotometer. To determine the MOI of VAX014 rBMCs that resulted in half-maximal LDH release (LDHhalf-max) for each data point, percentage of LDH release was calculated using the following formula and plotted in GraphPad Prism 9: [Net OD (490 nm – media only – average 0% LDH release)/average 100% net LDH release] × 100. Individual data points were plotted, and a sigmoidal dose–response (nonlinear regression) analysis generated by GraphPad Prism 9 (RRID:SCR_002798) was used to determine LDHhalf-max values.

## Cell viability assay
Cells were either plated and treated with VAX014 or VAX-I for 24 hours using the procedures described for the LDH assay above or were assayed directly from LDH assay plates by adding 10 µL/well PrestoBlue Cell Viability Reagent (Life Technologies, Cat# A-13261) and incubating for an additional 2.5 to 5 hours. Assay controls included a 100% viable cells-only control and a 0% viable media-only control. Fluorescence was measured at 560 ex/600 em with 590 cutoff via SpectraMax M3 spectrophotometer. To calculate the EC50 of VAX014 rBMCs against each cell type, percentage of viability was calculated using the following formula and plotted in GraphPad Prism 9: [Net fluorescence (raw fluorescence value – average 0% viable control)/average 100% net viable control] × 100%. A sigmoidal dose–response (nonlinear regression) analysis generated by GraphPad Prism 9 was used to determine EC50 values.

## VAX014 cytotoxicity in the presence of type I IFN
To assess the impact of high-dose type I IFN on VAX014 cytotoxicity against MB49 and LLC1 cells, cells were seeded at 2,000 cells/well (100 µL/well) in a 96-well tissue culture CellBIND plate. Cells were allowed to adhere to the plate for 4 hours prior to the addition of 1.0 × 106 U/mL universal type I IFN-α (PBL Assay Science, Cat# 112001). After 24 hours of incubation with universal type I IFN, VAX014 rBMCs were added at MOI titrations ranging from ∼10,000:1 to 4:1, plates were centrifuged for 10 minutes at 671 × g, and plates were incubated overnight. The following day, cell viability was evaluated as described above.

## Tumor implantation
Prior to implantation of intradermal (i.d.) tumors, cells were grown to 50% to 80% confluency in T75 flasks in their respective media. On the day of implantation, cells were rinsed once in 30 mL 1× PBS, detached from cell culture flasks using TrypLE Express enzyme, and resuspended in 30 mL media. Cells were then centrifuged for 5 minutes at 133 × g, supernatant was removed, and cells were washed once more with 25 mL 1× PBS and resuspended in 1 mL 1× PBS. The concentration of cells was measured using a Scepter 2.0 cell counter or Scepter 3.0 cell counter and 40 µm (MB49) or 60 µm (MC38) tips and suspensions were adjusted to 4.167 × 106 cells/mL in 1× PBS and kept on ice until implantation.
Mice were prepared for tumor implantation by shaving the fur on the right flank 1 to 7 days prior to the start of each study. On the day of implantation, mice were anesthetized via inhalation of isoflurane at 3% to 5% (v/v) mixed with pure oxygen at a flow rate of 0.6 to 1 L/minute. Single i.d. tumors were implanted on day 0 by injecting 30 µL (equivalent to 1.25 × 105 cells) tumor cell suspension into the dermis of each mouse using an insulin syringe and a 29-gauge needle (Excel International, Cat# 26028). Tumors were measured via digital caliper (Fisher Scientific, Cat# 06-664-16) beginning on day 4 to 5 after implantation and approximately every other day thereafter. Tumor volumes (mm3) were calculated using the following formula: π/6 × length × width × width. Mice were monitored every day for health observations by vivarium staff, and endpoint for animals was expiration due to tumor burden (tumor exceeded 1,500 mm3 or exhibited excessive ulceration) or signs of pain and distress, upon which mice were euthanized via CO2 asphyxiation.

## Intratumoral treatment with VAX014
Prior to treatment initiation, mice were randomized into treatment groups to evenly distribute tumor size among groups. Mice were housed and grouped according to model and treatment group to minimize confounding factors. Intratumoral treatment began when tumors reached a mean volume of ∼30 to 50 mm3. Saline (Teknova, Cat# S5815) or VAX014 rBMCs diluted in saline (1.5 × 108 rBMCs) was injected directly into tumors in a 30 µL dose volume using an insulin syringe and 29-gauge needle as previously described ( ). Weekly i.t. treatments continued until tumors reached a CR, a total of six i.t. doses were given, or the animal reached termination criteria, whichever occurred first.

## Immune cell depletion
Depletion of CD8α+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, or NK cells in the single i.d. MC38 tumor model was achieved by i.p. administration of depletion antibodies against CD8α (Leinco Technologies, Cat# C380, RRID:AB_2737483; 0.1 mg/dose), CD4 (Leinco Technologies, Cat# C1333, RRID:AB_2737452; 0.1 mg/dose), or NK1.1 (Leinco Technologies, Cat# N123, RRID:AB_2737553; 0.1 mg/dose) in 100 µL of 1× PBS (Corning, Cat# 21040CV). Animals not receiving depletion antibodies were injected with 100 µL of 1× PBS. Depletion antibodies were administered on the day of tumor implantation and on each of the 3 days leading up to it. Subsequently, depletion antibodies were given weekly, 1 day before each i.t. treatment. On day 12, depletion was confirmed by flow cytometry using blood samples. Injection of depletion antibodies or vehicle controls was stopped when tumors achieved CR or termination criteria were met.

## Immunotranscriptome analysis
Immunotranscriptome data were generated following i.t. treatment of i.d. MB49 wild-type (WT) tumors in a previous report from our group ( ). In this report, we specifically analyzed target genes as indicated herein from the same immunotranscriptome dataset. Briefly, tumor RNA was extracted using RNeasy protect kit (Qiagen, Cat# 74124) following the manufacturer’s instructions at indicated time points and evaluated using the nCounter Mouse PanCancer Immune Profiling Panel (NanoString Technologies, Inc.). ## qRT-PCR analysis of IFN-β expression in human cell lines
Human tumor cell lines were seeded at 5.0 × 105 cells/well (3 mL) in a six-well plate and allowed to adhere for 4 hours prior to treatment. Cells were left untreated or treated with VAX-I or VAX014 at their respective EC50 MOI for an additional 4 hours. Where indicated, the TBK1 inhibitor BX795 (InvivoGen, Cat# tlrl-bx7-2) was added at 6 µg/mL at the time of cell plating. Following treatment, total mRNA was isolated using RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Cat# 74104) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were disrupted with the Buffer RLT lysis buffer (Qiagen, Cat# 79216), pooled and homogenized via rotor–stator, suspended further with 70% ethanol, and applied to a spin column collection tube. The membrane within the spin column was washed with Buffer RW1 (Qiagen, Cat# 1053394) before the addition of RNase-Free DNase I (Qiagen, Cat# 79254) to further remove genomic DNA contamination from the RNA samples. Spin columns were washed an additional time with Buffer RW1 and then twice with Buffer RPE (Qiagen, Cat# 1018013). Once dried, RNase-free water was added to the spin column, and purified mRNA was eluted and quantified. Concentration of RNA samples was determined using the NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID:SCR_018042), and all samples were adjusted to 500 ng/µL using cDNA Synthesis Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# M1661) containing reverse transcriptase in RNase-free water. Negative controls without reverse transcriptase were included for each sample. Samples were incubated in the Mastercycler (Eppendorf) first for 10 minutes at 25°C, then 15 minutes at 50°C, and finally for 5 minutes at 85°C to convert the RNA to cDNA. Experimental cDNA samples and corresponding controls were plated in triplicate with IFN-beta-FAM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 4331182), GAPDH-VIC probes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 4448489), TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix for qPCR (Applied Biosystems, Cat# 44-445-56), and DNase-free water in each well. Relative IFNB1 (IFN-β) gene expression of the samples was quantified using the QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID:SCR_018712) and software. Cycle threshold (CT) values were obtained from each well using the “comparative CT” method and were normalized to the housekeeping gene, GAPDH. Fold gene expression of CT values was calculated using the 2(−ΔΔCT) method ( ).

## Expression analysis of STING, RIG-I, and type I IFN(s) across solid tumors from TCGA
Progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), RNA-seq by Expectation Maximization (RSEM)–normalized STING1 (STING), RIGI (RIG-I), and type I IFN (IFNA1, IFNA7, and IFNA8) mRNA expression datasets for all solid tumor indications available within TCGA Pan-Cancer Atlas were retrieved from the cBioPortal database (RRID:SCR_014555, www.cbioportal.org; refs. – ). Before analysis of these data, patient samples lacking PFS, OS, or RNA-seq expression data were excluded. Respective 25th percentile expression values for STING and RIG-I mRNA expression were established from all solid tumors and utilized as the threshold cutoff for high and low expression on an indication-by-indication basis. To orthogonally examine the best STING and/or RIG-I expression cutoff values, the online survival analysis tool, Kaplan–Meier plotter, was used (RRID:SCR_018753, www.kmplot.com; Supplementary Fig. S1; ref. ). The percentages of patients with high STING and/or RIG-I expression were then generated from these data. Because expression of type I IFN(s) is tightly regulated, the threshold for defining elevated expression of all isoforms of type I IFN genes available in the database was set at an RSEM of 0.

## Results

## VAX014 facilitates PFO-dependent type I IFN production to upregulate surface expression of PD-L1 and MHC-I
Our previous work demonstrated that PD-L1 is an actionable adaptive immune resistance mechanism to VAX014 in the orthotopic MB49 bladder tumor model and PD-L1 upregulation in response to VAX014 was found to be preserved in vitro ( ). In this work, we set out to explore the possible molecular mechanisms behind PD-L1 upregulation. In initial in vitro experiments, we found treatment with VAX014 led to an observed ∼13-fold increase in the surface expression of PD-L1 and a ∼4-fold increase in the surface expression of MHC-I in MB49 cells ( ). Upregulation of both was dependent on PFO, as treatment with the same concentration of VAX-I rBMCs (rBMCs containing the tumor targeting invasin component of VAX014 but lacking the pore-forming protein PFO) did not result in increased surface expression of either PD-L1 or MHC-I. Based on these results, we hypothesized that VAX014 may stimulate release of type I IFN in MB49 cells, a known mediator of PD-L1 and MHC-I upregulation ( ). Thus, we conducted a series of in vitro experiments to explore the potential role of type I IFN(s) in the upregulation of PD-L1 and MHC-I in MB49 cells following treatment with VAX014.
 Provided type I IFNs are secreted cytokines, we performed a series of experiments in which we transferred cell culture supernatants from VAX014-treated MB49 cells onto naïve MB49 recipient cells and assessed surface expression levels of PD-L1 and MHC-I in the recipient cells by flow cytometry ( ). Because type I IFNs are heat-labile, we also included an experimental condition utilizing heat-treated supernatants. Results from these experiments indicate that the factor(s) responsible for the upregulation of PD-L1 and MHC-I is a heat-labile factor that emerges in cell culture supernatants after exposure to VAX014, which is consistent with the known features of type I IFNs ( ). Building on these findings, we repeated the supernatant transfer experiments under conditions in which the naïve MB49 recipient cells had been preincubated with a function-blocking antibody against the IFNAR-1 subunit of the IFN-α receptor, which was confirmed to be expressed by MB49 cells, and found that type I IFN receptor blockade abolished VAX014-mediated PD-L1 and MHC-I upregulation ( ). We then probed for soluble type I IFN(s) in VAX014-treated MB49 supernatants and found elevated levels of IFN-β ( ), but not IFN-α (Supplementary Fig. S2). Because the release of IFN-β occurred only in conditions in which cells were killed by VAX014, we investigated the contribution of type I IFN in VAX014-mediated cell killing and found that the presence of type I IFN had no effect (Supplementary Fig. S2).

## VAX014 activates STING and another TBK1-mediated IFN-β production pathway in vitro
Next, we set out to identify which signaling pathway(s) contribute to IFN-β production by MB49 cells in response to VAX014 by evaluating each of the three known cellular sources of IFN-β production: endosomal TLR3 activation by double stranded RNA (dsRNA), cytosolic STING activation by cyclic dinucleotides (CDN), and cytosolic RLR activation by cytosolic 5′-triphosphate–capped single stranded RNA (ssRNA) or dsRNA ( – ). Following activation by their respective ligands, each of these pathways converges on the TBK1/IRF3 pathway, resulting in successive phosphorylation of TBK1 and pIRF3, which ultimately transactivates IFN-β ( ). Because our previous work demonstrated VAX014 is internalized into target cells by endocytosis, we first investigated the role of TLR3. We utilized the J77A4.1 murine macrophage cell line, which expresses TLR3 and responds to the TLR3 agonist, Poly(I:C), and found endosomal TLR3 does not contribute to IFN-β production by MB49 or murine J77A4.1 cells in response to VAX014 under the tested conditions (Supplementary Fig. S3; ref. ). This result is consistent with the aforementioned findings in which PD-L1 and MHC-I upregulation was dependent on the presence of PFO, which forms pores in endosomal membranes after internalization and degradation of VAX014, suggesting that the mediator(s) of IFN-β production reside not in the endosome but within the cytosol. Therefore, we set out to systematically evaluate the cytosolic mediators of IFN-β production, namely STING and RLRs.
It is known that rBMCs contain all the molecular constituents of parental bacteria including bacterial CDNs, which characteristically activate STING ( ). Therefore, we hypothesized that bacterial CDNs within VAX014 might mediate STING activation. To investigate this possibility, we first determined the cGAS/STING expression status of the MB49 cell line via Western blot, which revealed detectable amounts of STING but not cGAS ( ). In the absence of cGAS, MB49 cells cannot enzymatically generate the eukaryote-specific CDN 2'3'-cGAMP STING agonist from cytosolic dsDNA. Therefore, the MB49 cell line provides a unique in vitro system reliant solely on the presence of exogenous STING agonists for STING activation. To assess whether VAX014 stimulates STING to elicit IFN-β production in MB49 cells, we treated cells with VAX014 or DMXAA, a murine STING agonist, in the presence or absence of an irreversible murine STING inhibitor (H-151), and then evaluated the surface expression of PD-L1 and MHC-I by flow cytometry ( ). We found that VAX014 and DMXAA led to similar levels of upregulation of PD-L1 and elevated expression of MHC-I. Activation of murine STING by VAX014 and DMXAA was also confirmed in a commercially available reporter cell line (Supplementary Fig. S4). Inhibition of STING with H-151 reduced PD-L1 expression nearly to baseline in MB49 cells treated with DMXAA. In contrast, the same inhibitor reduced the PD-L1 expression of VAX014-treated MB49 cells by only half. A similar trend was observed among pIRF3:IRF3 ratios ( ).
 Based on these results, we hypothesized that in addition to activating STING, VAX014 may also activate one or more additional cytosolic nucleic acid receptor pathways, possibly from the RLR family, to induce IFN-β production. As mentioned, STING and RLRs converge on the TBK1/IRF3 pathway to transcriptionally activate IFN-β. Therefore, we next tested the effect of the competitive TBK1 inhibitor, Amx, on the upregulation of PD-L1 and MHC-I in MB49 cells treated with either VAX014 or DMXAA ( ). Pharmacologic inhibition of TBK1 led to a near complete loss in upregulation of both PD-L1 and MHC-I along with relative reductions in pIRF3/IRF3 ratios ( ), further supporting that VAX014 mediates activation of STING and at least one other TBK1-dependent pathway in vitro. Importantly, the MC38 murine colon carcinoma cell line was found to phenocopy the MB49 cell line in vitro, providing an orthogonal line of evidence to support that VAX014 activates STING and at least one other TBK1-dependent IFN-β production pathway in another murine tumor cell line (Supplementary Fig. S5).
To further confirm STING activation by VAX014, we next genetically ablated STING from MB49 cells (MB49STING KO) via targeted deletion using CRISPR-Cas9 and evaluated the impact loss of STING had on the production of IFN-β in response to VAX014 ( ). Successful knockout of STING in three isolated candidate clones was confirmed first via Western blot ( ; Supplementary Fig. S6) followed by assessment of responsiveness to DMXAA, sensitivity to VAX014, retention of the alternative TBK1-mediated IFN-β response to VAX014, and preservation of the type I IFN receptor and signaling pathway (Supplementary Figs. S6–S9). Given the comparable results across all MB49STING KO cell lines, a single clone was used for further analysis. The selected MB49STING KO cell line was completely unresponsive to DMXAA in comparison with MB49WT cells, confirming elimination of STING function ( ). In contrast, treatment of MB49STING KO cells with VAX014 rBMCs was associated with a reduction but not elimination of IFN-β production ( ), with commensurate reductions in IRF3 phosphorylation ( ) and surface expression of PD-L1 or MHC-I ( ) in comparison with MB49WT cells. Inhibition of TBK1 by Amx in VAX014-treated MB49STING KO cells led to an even greater reduction that approached baseline in each of these readouts. Taken together, these results further support the hypothesis that VAX014 activates STING in addition to another TBK1-centric pathway to trigger IFN-β production in MB49 cells. Comparable results were observed when STING was genetically ablated in the murine MC38 tumor cell line (Supplementary Fig. S10).
 

## VAX014 additionally activates RIG-I to facilitate IFN-β production in vitro
As mentioned above, rBMCs contain all the molecular constituents of parental bacteria, which also include bacterial RNA species such as tRNA, rRNA, and long-lived mRNA ( , ). Because prokaryotic tRNAs and many mRNA molecules fall within the size range recognized by RIG-I (<1,000 bp) with many of the former exhibiting tertiary structures resembling dsRNA, we next investigated the role of RIG-I in the response to VAX014 ( ; refs. , ). We first confirmed that MB49WT cells express RIG-I by Western blot before proceeding with targeted genetic deletion of the RIG-I gene by CRISPR-Cas9 ( ). Western blotting identified three isolated MB49RIG-I KO candidate clones, each demonstrating successful knockout of RIG-I and preservation of STING expression ( ; Supplementary Fig. S11). These prospective clones were functionally characterized and found to have comparable features, including functional integrity of the STING pathway, sensitivity to VAX014, and preservation of the type I IFN receptor signaling pathway (Supplementary Figs. S11–S14). Similar to MB49WT, the single MB49RIG-I KO clone selected for further analysis was still responsive to DMXAA with respect to upregulation of PD-L1 and MHC-I ( ), IFN-β production ( ), and phosphorylation of IRF3 ( ). Treatment with VAX014 in the MB49RIG-I KO cells led to a marked reduction in all three of these readouts compared with MB49WT cells that was completely abolished by pharmacologic inhibition of STING with H-151 ( ). Together, these results provide strong evidence supporting IFN-β production in response to VAX014 is dependent on STING and RIG-I. Comparable results were also observed in MC38RIG-I KO cells (Supplementary Fig. S15).
 To confirm STING and RIG-I are the sole contributors to the production of IFN-β in response to VAX014 in this system, we genetically ablated both STING and RIG-I in MB49 cells (MB49STING/RIG-I KO) using CRISPR-Cas9. Successful knockout of both genes was confirmed in candidate clones by Western blot as a primary screen prior to functional characterization that included evaluating the preservation of VAX014 sensitivity, loss of responsiveness to VAX014 with respect to PD-L1 upregulation and IFN-β production, and retention of the type I IFN receptor signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. S16). Given the comparable characteristics of all candidate clones, one MB49STING/RIG-I KO cell line was chosen for further analysis. The dual genetic ablation of STING and RIG-I resulted in a complete elimination in PD-L1 upregulation and IFN-β production by VAX014, confirming that the IFN-β production in response to VAX014 is mediated exclusively by both STING and RIG-I in the MB49 cell line ( and ). Again, comparable results were observed in MC38 cells deficient in both STING and RIG-I (MC38STING/RIG-I KO; Supplementary Fig. S17). Given these results, we did not further investigate the involvement of any other cytosolic nucleic acid receptor signaling pathways in these tumor cell lines before proceeding to in vivo evaluation.

## Tumor-intrinsic STING and RIG-I contribute to the antitumor activity of VAX014 in vivo
Having established that VAX014 activates both STING and RIG-I to facilitate production of IFN-β in vitro, we next set out to determine if the presence of tumor-intrinsic STING or RIG-I influences the antitumor activity of VAX014 in vivo. Prior to performing these studies, we confirmed the expression of TBK1, STING, and RIG-I was maintained in MB49WT tumors by immunotranscriptome analysis (Supplementary Fig. S18) and characterized the ability of both the MB49STING KO and MB49RIG-I KO tumor cell lines to reliably establish i.d. tumors with growth rates comparable with those of MB49WT tumors (Supplementary Fig. S19). Having validated the model for these features, we initiated pharmacology studies to compare the impact of tumor-intrinsic STING or RIG-I on the antitumor activity of VAX014 ( ). In MB49WT tumors, weekly i.t. administration of VAX014 led to a 100% CR rate resulting in long-term survival of all mice ( ). In contrast, the CR rate of mice bearing MB49STING KO tumors was reduced to 60%, indicating the antitumor activity of VAX014 is partially dependent on tumor-intrinsic STING ( ). VAX014 treatment in MB49RIG-I KO tumors resulted in a CR rate of 87%, providing evidence that tumor-intrinsic RIG-I also contributes to the antitumor activity of VAX014 ( ). Finally, we evaluated the efficacy of VAX014 in MB49STING/RIG-I KO tumors and found that only 33% of mice completely responded to therapy ( ). Taken together, these results highlight that tumor-intrinsic STING and RIG-I both contribute to the antitumor activity of VAX014 in the MB49 model.
 Like the MB49WT model, we also observed a 100% CR rate in MC38WT tumors in vivo and the antitumor activity was completely dependent on CD8+ T cells (Supplementary Fig. S20). However, the efficacy of VAX014 was entirely dependent on tumor-intrinsic STING in the MC38 model, with a 0% CR rate in VAX014-treated mice bearing MC38STING KO tumors (Supplementary Fig. S21). Given the stringency of this result, we did not further evaluate the effect of tumor-intrinsic RIG-I or host-intrinsic STING signaling on VAX014 efficacy in the MC38 model. No evidence of overt toxicity including but not limited to cachexia, febrile illness, ruffled fur, hair loss, lethargy, or grooming habits was observed in response to i.t. treatment with VAX014 in any of the models employed in these studies.

## Host STING also contributes to the antitumor activity of VAX014 in vivo
Results from the above-described studies indicate STING may play a more prominent role in the antitumor activity of VAX014 in comparison with RIG-I. STING agonists have been shown to activate both tumor- and host-intrinsic STING to exert antitumor effects ( , – ). In line with this, immunotranscriptome analysis of MB49WT tumors treated with VAX014 demonstrated upregulation of both IFN-β and several isoforms of IFN-α compared with saline-treated tumors (Supplementary Fig. S22); however, VAX014 does not facilitate the production of IFN-α in vitro (Supplementary Fig. S2). Therefore, these immunotranscriptome data suggest a role for host production of type I IFN upon treatment with VAX014 that may be due to activation of host STING. To analyze this, we conducted a series of studies in the STING null Goldenticket mouse ( ; ref. ). It is important to note that due to the rigorous 100% CR rate of VAX014-treated MB49WT tumors in WT mice, we could reduce the number of animals required to assess host contribution by eliminating saline-treated controls across all groups. In the WT host, treatment of MB49WT tumors with VAX014 again yielded a 100% CR rate that was reduced in MB49STING KO tumors with no appreciable delay in time to response ( ). Surprisingly, VAX014 treatment of MB49WT tumors in the STING null host also led to a 100% CR rate with no appreciable delay in time to response ( ). However, when STING was absent in both the tumor and the host, the CR rate decreased to 42%. Taken together, these results demonstrate that host STING signaling also plays a role in the antitumor activity of VAX014. Consistent with the WT host models described above, no overt toxicity was observed in response to i.t. treatment with VAX014.
 

## STING/RIG-I co-expression and elevated type I IFN correlates with improved clinical outcome across solid tumor types
Based on our nonclinical data, we next aimed to assess the possible translational potential of our findings. To this end, we evaluated publicly available RNA-seq data from the TCGA database for the prevalence of co-incident STING and RIG-I expression as well as the expression of multiple isoforms of type I IFN within human solid tumor types and queried whether elevated expression correlated with clinical outcome ( ). For the evaluation of STING and/or RIG-I, threshold cutoffs were established to categorize patient tumor data into high- and low-expression categories by determining the first quartile value from the raw expression data across all solid tumors for STING and RIG-I, respectively. Using this approach, we found STING and RIG-I were co-expressed at a high level across most solid tumor indications ( ). In addition, we found co-expression of both was associated with a significant PFS benefit across all solid tumor types although this varied from indication to indication ( ). The PFS benefit was particularly true for those solid tumor indications most amenable to direct i.t. administration, including breast invasive carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma of head and neck, and skin cutaneous melanoma. Similar trends were observed for OS (Supplementary Fig. S23). Confining the analysis to either STING or RIG-I alone yielded similar trends for each but highlighted that co-expression was associated with the deepest clinical benefit in these indications (Supplementary Figs. S24 and S25). Because type I IFN(s) are known to be activated by the STING and/or RIG-I pathways, we next used the same database to evaluate the impact of type I IFN expression on clinical outcome. There we found that elevated expression of several IFN-α isoforms (IFN-α1, IFN-α7, and IFN-α8) was associated with survival advantage across solid tumor types ( ). Finally, to gain a preliminary understanding of whether our nonclinical findings about the ability of VAX014 to activate STING and RIG-I pathways to elicit TBK1-mediated production of IFN-β may translate clinically, we performed in vitro experiments across a panel of human tumor cell lines ( ; Supplementary Fig. S26). These studies revealed that VAX014 facilitates a PFO-dependent increase in TBK1-mediated IFN-β gene expression across all the STING-positive and RIG-I–positive human solid tumor cell lines tested. Taken in the context of our findings with the murine models, these results support the use of VAX014 to treat STING-positive and/or RIG-I–positive human solid tumors.
 

## Discussion
ISI is growing in popularity as an approach to cancer immunotherapy due to its potential to generate oligoclonal, patient-specific, antitumor T-cell responses with limited additive immune-related toxicity when combined with systemic ICB ( – ). Most of the current ISI agents fall into three broad categories including TLR agonists, cytosolic nucleic acid receptor agonists, and OVs, and the number of clinical trials investigating these agents has grown exponentially over the past 5 years ( ). Although OVs have demonstrated both clinical and commercial proof-of-principle as monotherapy and in combination with ICB, they have limited activity in STING-positive and RIG-I–positive tumors and are cumbersome to use ( – , , – ). Recognizing these limitations has created a need for new, easier to use oncolytic ISI agents that work in STING-positive and RIG-I–positive solid tumors.
In this report, we use a combination of pharmacologic, genetic, and in vivo approaches to establish the ability of VAX014, a nonviral targeted oncolytic agent based on rBMCs, to co-activate STING and RIG-I and exert potent antitumor effects in solid tumors intrinsically positive for these innate immune signaling molecules. In vivo pharmacology studies demonstrated a 100% tumor clearance rate following weekly i.t. treatment of MB49WT or MC38WT tumors, both of which co-express STING and RIG-I. In the MB49 model, absence of tumor-intrinsic STING in MB49STING-KO tumors or RIG-I in MB49RIG-I-KO tumors resulted in reduced antitumor activity following i.t. administration of VAX014, with the absence of STING having the greatest negative impact on tumor growth, clearance, and survival rates. The loss of both tumor-intrinsic STING and RIG-I in MB49STING/RIG-I-KO tumors led to an additive reduction in antitumor activity of VAX014 compared with either alone, supporting that both pathways are activated and play a role in the response to VAX014 in vivo. The greater dependence on STING versus RIG-I in vivo in the MB49 model may be related to the ability of bacterial and/or host CDNs to traverse gap junctions to activate STING in neighboring tumor and/or other host cells, whereas larger RNA molecules (RIG-I agonists) may not ( ). Along these lines, we observed the production of both IFN-β and IFN-α in immunotranscriptomes from VAX014-treated MB49 tumors yet only saw production of IFN-β in vitro, implicating host involvement. Indeed, host STING was also shown to contribute to the antitumor activity of VAX014 in the MB49 model but only if tumor-intrinsic STING was absent. This result was somewhat unexpected, and the underlying cause is under investigation. As mentioned above, one possibility may be that host STING plays a bystander role in this model, which at first glance is consistent with the notion that VAX014 is highly selective for integrin-expressing tumors. In that context, the activation of tumor-intrinsic STING coinciding with tumor cell lysis occurs first and may be sufficient for immune activation, ultimately masking the contribution of host STING. Removal of tumor-intrinsic STING would then allow for the contribution of host STING to become evident once antitumor activity is assessed in the STING null mouse. The absolute requirement of tumor-intrinsic STING for antitumor activity in the MC38 model in WT mice confounds this precept, however, because there was no appreciable contribution of host STING in that model. At the same time, several recent reports indicate that STING agonism by CDNs results in the activation of multiple innate immune pathways that result in type I IFN-independent adaptive immune-mediated antitumor effects ( ). Although this will require more effort to dissect, it may be that VAX014-mediated agonism of tumor-intrinsic STING in the MB49 tumor model leads to activation of multiple innate immune pathways, whereas in the MC38 model it may not. Whatever the disparities in mechanism(s), it should not subtract from our finding that VAX014 exerts potent antitumor effects in STING and RIG-I positive solid tumors across multiple immune-competent models.
The ability of VAX014 to work in STING-positive and RIG-I–positive tumors may portend well for clinical translation. This may be particularly true in the context of combination with ICB, in which multiple recent clinical and preclinical reports demonstrate a strong association between low STING or RIG-I expression levels in tumors with poor prognosis, a lack of immune infiltration, and unresponsiveness to ICB ( – ). Our analysis of STING and RIG-I expression data from the TCGA RNA-seq database presented here demonstrates prevalent co-expression of these two genes across solid tumor types, which was associated with greater PFS and OS. We examined the TCGA data to explore the prevalence and impact on clinical outcome among those indications most anatomically amenable to i.t. injection including cutaneous melanoma, squamous cell carcinoma of head and neck, and breast carcinoma and found that co-expression of STING and RIG-I was frequent and associated with positive clinical outcome, suggesting that agonism of either or both pathways may provide additional clinical benefit in these patient populations. We present additional evidence about the potential benefit of agonism of these pathways as it was determined that multiple isoforms of type I IFN, a known downstream innate target of STING and/or RIG-I activation, were also associated with clinical benefit. Although the prevalence of elevated expression of these isoforms was not as high as that of STING and/or RIG-I, this may be owing to recent findings that STING activates multiple innate immune pathways following its activation as described above ( ). Previous attempts to utilize innate immune modulators such as TLR, STING, and RIG-I agonists in these indications have been met with minimal clinical benefit ( ). However, none of these agents have oncolytic activity, which inherently limits the scope and scale of tumor antigen availability and effective T-cell priming therewith. On the other hand, OVs are more equipped to bolster the availability of tumor antigens, but by their very nature, they invert the first step (adequate antigen availability) and second step (activation of innate immunity) of the cancer immunity cycle. For example, the OV infection process triggers early activation of innate antiviral immune signals (a type I IFN response) within 6 to 18 hours, whereas the completion of the viral lifecycle (viral burst) generates available tumor antigens later ( , , ). In concept, this creates a temporal disparity and discoordination in the sequence of immunologic events of the cancer immunity cycle, the net result of which is suboptimal T-cell priming. VAX014 differs from these previous approaches because its rapid oncolytic activity promotes earlier tumor antigen release (within 2 hours) while concurrently activating innate immune signaling via co-activation of STING and/or RIG-I. This may better coordinate peak antigen availability with the height of innate immune activation to result in more robust T-cell priming. Encapsulation and tumor-targeted delivery of STING and/or RIG-I agonists by VAX014 rBMCs may be an important product feature in light of recent nonclinical data demonstrating i.t. injection of high-dose “free” STING agonist results in T-cell apoptosis in the TME and deficient systemic antitumor immune response attributable to inadequate antitumor T-cell priming ( ). Elucidating the prospective role(s) of these mechanisms with respect to VAX014 as well as an evaluation of combination with ICB in the context of loss of tumor-intrinsic STING and/or RIG-I will be the focus of future work.
In summary, this report features VAX014 as a first-in-class nonviral oncolytic agent that provides strong antitumor activity in STING-positive and RIG-I–positive tumors. It is worth noting that VAX014 is currently being evaluated for safety and efficacy following i.t. administration in subjects with injectable solid tumors refractory to standard treatment options in a phase 1 basket trial in which it will be evaluated in combination with systemic ICB in the dose expansion phase (NCT 05901285). If successful, VAX014 may provide additional benefits over OV-based therapies because it is a sterile, replication-incompetent product that does not require BSL-2 containment to manufacture or prepare for clinical use. This feature should also eliminate the requirement for patients and/or caretakers to collect and return dressings or sequester from immunocompromised and pregnant family members while on therapy as is the current practice for current OV recipients. These additional features, combined with the ability of VAX014 to be utilized in STING-positive and RIG-I–positive solid tumors, may combine to provide better clinical outcomes as well as improved patient access, compliance, and quality of life.
