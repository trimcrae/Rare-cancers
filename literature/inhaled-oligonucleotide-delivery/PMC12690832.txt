## Abstract

## Background
Tremors and rigid muscles are symptoms of Parkinson’s disease (PD), which affects about 1% of the global population.

## Purpose
To investigate the integrated therapeutic benefits of the phytopharmaceutical berberine and mucoadhesive nanoliposomes, administered via the nose-to-brain route, to enhance bioavailability, facilitate blood-brain barrier penetration, and augment efficacy against neurodegenerative progression in Parkinson’s disease (PD).

## Study design
This study aimed to develop and characterize chitosan-decorated liposomes (chitosomes) loaded with berberine (BER) for targeted brain delivery via the intranasal route.

## Methods
In vitro characterization of BER loaded chitosomes was conducted including measurement of particle size, zeta potential, EE % and in vitro drug release. The optimized formulation was intranasally administered in rats with rotenone-induced PD animal model, motor function and coordination were investigated besides testing the levels of different biomarkers and histopathological examination.

## Results
The prepared chitosomes had a particle size of 312 nm, zeta potential of 34 mV, and high encapsulation efficiency (89.3%) and sustained drug release. In vivo assessments conducted in a rotenone-induced PD animal model revealed notable improvements in motor function and coordination. Biochemical evaluations showed that BER chitosomes reduced α-synuclein by 49% and raised dopamine levels by 55% as compared to the model group. Additionally, BER chitosomes significantly decreased oxidative stress markers by a 67% decrease in NF-κB levels and a one-fold increase in Nrf2. Histological examination showed a noticeable reduction in neuronal degeneration and Lewy body formation.

## Conclusion
Our findings imply that BER chitosomes are a viable intranasal delivery tool for the efficient treatment of PD due to their enhanced bioavailability. and greater nasal mucosa penetration.

## Introduction
Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurological condition characterized by muscular rigidity, tremors, speech, and gait abnormalities [ ]. In people over the age of 65, the prevalence of PD is approximately 1%. It usually begins between the ages of 40 and 70 [ ]. Dopamine deficiency in the nigrostriatal pathway is one of the main causes of PD. Tremors, bradykinesia, and postural instability manifest following a 70–80% depletion of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra (SN) [ ] and subsequently, dopamine release in the striatum is impaired [ ]. In addition to the existence of Lewy bodies (intracellular cytoplasmic proteins), mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress play vital roles in PD progression [ , ]. Lewy bodies are aberrant alpha-synuclein aggregates formed within nerve cells. This proteinopathy influences the brain by blocking the generation of substances needed for diverse brain activities, such as thinking, coordination of motion, behavior, and mood [ ]. Furthermore, researchers have reported the presence of mitochondrial abnormalities, indicating a causal relationship between mitochondrial dysfunction and PD. Such mitochondrial abnormalities have been pathologically proven, particularly in sporadic PD patients, against a background of higher oxidative stress and raised iron levels in brain [ ]. Along with the aforementioned causes, environmental, genetic, are all risk factors for PD development and progression [ ]. Men are three times more likely than women to have PD [ ].
Currently, dopaminomimetics and monoamine oxidase B (MAO-B) inhibitors are utilized to suppress dopamine decomposition in PD therapy, nevertheless, they simply treat symptoms and have severe side effects [ ]. These medications offer symptomatic relief for motor symptoms. High therapeutic dosages, frequent administration, significant presystemic metabolism (first-pass effect), enzymatic breakdown, organ toxicity, and limited brain availability owing to the blood-brain barrier (BBB) are some of the challenges that these therapies face [ ]. The BBB consists of firmly sealed cellular junctions on CNS blood capillaries that protect the brain from toxins and polar pharmaceuticals [ , ]. Recently, phytomedicine has shown prophylactic and therapeutic potential in PD due to its antioxidant [ – ], anti-inflammatory [ ] and antiapoptotic effects [ , ].
Berberine (BER) is a yellowish, crystalline, quaternary isoquinoline alkaloid that has been extracted from different Chinese herbs, such as Cortex phelloderm, Mahonia bealei and Rhizoma coptidis [ – ]. BER possesses antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anticarcinogenic, antibacterial and antifungal effects in addition to its role in dermatological disorders, including vitiligo [ ]. BER is a promising medication for treating PD patients’ motor dysfunction and cognitive impairment [ ] because it can inhibit both MAO-A and MAO-B [ ]. Wang et al. [ ] found that treatment of BER in PD may stimulate gut microbiota to produce L-DOPA through vitamin-like actions, therefore exerting clinical benefits [ ]. Additionally, BER prevents the activation of the LRR, NACHT, and PYD domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome during PD-related inflammation and restores autophagy activity that safeguards dopamine neurons [ ]. In a mouse model of PD’s produced by 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-trerahydropyridine (MPTP/P), BER decreased the loss of neurons and dopaminergic fibres in the substantia nigra pars compacta and the striatum, respectively. Animal behavior investigations have revealed that the dysfunction of coordination and movement balance has gotten better [ ].
Delivery of medicines to the brain is difficult because of the necessity to cross the BBB. The intranasal (nose-to-brain) administration method offers a number of advantages, as it avoids first pass effect, is less intrusive, provides a broad surface area for absorption, and reduces nontargeted delivery to minimize systemic exposure [ , ]. Intranasal delivery has been effectively employed to deliver various therapeutic agents for the management of various neurodegenerative disorders [ – ]. Nose-to-brain administration is an innovative drug delivery technique designed to bypass the BBB and directly administer medication to the brain, utilizing either nanoemulgel for enhanced glioblastoma treatment [ , ] or chitosan (CS) formulations for schizophrenia management [ ]. One of the constraints of nose-to-brain administration of medicines is the speed with which pharmaceuticals are cleared by the nasal mucociliary clearance system. As a result, the use of mucoadhesive polymers, particularly CS, for the preparation of nanocarriers for intranasal delivery has received special attention [ ].
CS-decorated liposomes, or chitosomes, emerge as a viable strategy for drug delivery due to the unique properties of the chitosan coating. CS is a cationic polysaccharide polymer with biodegradable and biocompatible properties. Additionally, it possesses mucoadhesive characteristics that can facilitate drug permeability by opening tight junctions. Furthermore, chitosan has demonstrated intrinsic anti-inflammatory properties by inhibiting tumor necrosis factor alfa (TNF-α) secretion [ ]. By virtue of these beneficial attributes, chitosan nano-coating, combined with liposomal drug delivery, merges their advantages while reducing their respective limitations. Consequently, chitosomes may serve as an effective strategy for brain targeting of BER by circumventing its poor membrane permeability across the BBB.
BER loaded nanoformulations directed for intranasal administration have been previously reported in several studies. Wang et al. prepared in situ BER loaded thermosensitive gels for depression management [ ]. Mazahir et al. reported the preparation of BER loaded hydrogel for intranasal administration to counter act okadaic acid-induced cognitive deficit in mice [ ]. Also, Sun et al. the preparation of stem cell exosomes loaded with BACE1 siRNA and Berberine the Treatment of Alzheimer’s Disease [ ]. Moreover, Abo El-Enin et al. reported the preparation of BER loaded CS coated nanostructured lipid carriers for brain targeting through the intranasal administration. They conducted brain accumulation studies in addition to pharmacokinetics [ ]. Furthermore, Bahndare et al. prepared ascorbic acid surface modified BER loaded liposomes and investigated their potential for the management of Alzheimer’s disease [ ].
Therefore, this study is pioneering in evaluating the brain targeting of BER through intranasal delivery of chitosomes via the olfactory mucosa for PD management. This study is the first to report on the formulation of intranasal BER chitosomes for PD, highlighting their ability to address the restrictions of current therapies and improve patient outcomes. The main aim of the current study is to encapsulate BER in mucoadhesive chitosomes to increase their retention time in the nasal mucosa to enhance their brain uptake ensuring a fast onset of action and effectiveness at lower doses.

## Materials
Lipoid® S100 (l-α-phosphatidylcholine) was bought from Lipoid AG (Ludwigshafen, Germany). Berberine chloride was obtained from Baoji Guokang Bio-Technology Co., Ltd., China. Chitosan (Mw = 50,000 − 190,000 Da) and a degree of deacetylation > 90% was obtained from Primex BioChemicals AS (Avaldsnes, Norway). All other chemicals and reagents used were of analytical grade.

## Methodology

## Preparation of blank and BER-loaded liposomes
Nanoliposomes were produced and coated with medium-weight chitosan. In brief, 90 mg of lipoid S 100, 10 mg of cholesterol and 20 mg of Tween 80 were completely dissolved in absolute ethanol using a magnetic stirrer [ ]. Following that, the ethanolic phospholipid solution was added dropwise to 20 ml of distilled water using a syringe while magnetically stirring at 800 rpm. The mixture was then stirred for one hour at room temperature. Finally, the liposomal preparation was maintained overnight in a refrigerator to stabilize before subsequent characterization [ ].
To prepare drug-loaded liposomes, BER concentration (2 mg/ml) was dissolved in the aqueous phase before the addition of phospholipid ethanolic solution to the stirred aqueous medium, after which the preparation proceeded as previously stated.

## Preparation of BER-loaded chitosomes
CS coating was done on BER-loaded liposomes using the previously reported titration method [ ]. Briefly, CS solution (100 mg%) was made by dissolving it in acetate buffer (0.1 M, pH 4.4) and stirring vigorously overnight. Different doses of CS solution (2, 4, and 8 mg %) were applied dropwise to a fixed volume of liposomal suspension at room temperature while stirring with a gentle magnetic stirrer (500 rpm). The mixture was mixed for 1 h at room temperature, then the formulations were refrigerated overnight for equilibrium.
The different compositions of liposomes and chitosomes are listed in Table  .
 

## Particle size and zeta potential measurements
The mean particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were measured utilizing a Zeta sizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instrument, UK) at a scattering angle of 173° at a temperature of 25 °C. Prior to measurements, all generated formulations were diluted with recently filtered distilled water at a ratio of 1:50. Samples were subjected to sonication for 5 min and thereafter measured in triplicate over a duration of 10 min [ , ].

## Percentage entrapment efficiency (EE %)
The Centrisart®-I tube (MWCO 300 kDa, Sartorius AG, Gottingen, Germany) was used to indirectly measure the entrapment efficiency. To extract free, unencapsulated medications, BER-loaded liposomes and chitosomes were centrifuged for 30 min at 5000 rpm. Next, using UV spectrophotometry at 349 nm, the concentration of free unencapsulated BER was determined [ ].
 

## Drug release and release kinetics
An in vitro release study was performed using dialysis bags (Visking® 36/32, 21 mm, MWCO 12,000–14,000; Serva, Heidelberg, Germany). The bags were steeped in distilled water for 24 h before being firmly sealed. BER solution, F3 and F5, each containing a quantity of berberine corresponding to 2.5 mg, were then put in stoppered glass vials containing 10 ml of acetate buffer (pH 5) as a dissolving medium [ ]. During the experiment, the vials were kept at 32 °C ± 0.5 °C and shaken at 100 rpm in a thermostatic shaking water bath. A 2-ml sample volume was taken at regular intervals to maintain sink conditions and replaced with an equivalent volume of fresh buffer. The drug release profiles were determined by plotting the average cumulative percentage of BER released at various intervals. The spectrophotometric analysis determined the drug concentration by comparing the UV absorbance at 349 nm to a matching blank buffer solution [ , ]. Each experiment was conducted in triplicate. The data are presented as means ± SDs. The release data were fitted to DD solver software’s zero-order, first-order, and Higuchi models.

## Morphological characterization
The morphology of blank liposomes and the selected chitosomes were evaluated using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEM-1400; JEOL, Japan) after staining with 1% uranyl acetate.

## Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)
FTIR spectra were recorded for BER, CS, Lipoid S 100, their physical mixture and BER chitosomes by (Agilent Cary 630 FTIR spectrometer, USA) at 700–4400 cm− 1.

## Differential scanning colorimetry (DSC)
The thermal properties of the BER, CS, Lipoid S 100, their physical mixture and BER chitosomes were explored using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) (DSC Model 6, Perkin Elmer Instruments, Waltham, MA, USA) with a heating rate of 10 °C/min from 10 °C to 350 °C under nitrogen.

## In vivo study

## Animals
Thirty-two male Sprague‒Dawley rats, weighing 200 ± 20 g, were purchased from the animal house of the Medical Research Institute, Alexandria. Rats were housed in the animal house of the Physiology Department, Faculty of Medicine, Alexandria University. Animals were supplied with normal food and water ad libitum. The rats were housed in standard circumstances of temperature (24 ± 2 °C) and humidity (40 ± 4%), with a twelve-hour light-dark cycle. The Research Ethics Committee at Damanhour University approved the National Research Council’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Ref. No. 124PO39), and all animal experiments complied with the ARRIVE guidelines and the U.K. Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act, 1986.

## Chemical and drug preparation
Rotenone and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were purchased from Sigma‒Aldrich Co., USA. Rotenone (10 mg) was dissolved in 0.2 ml of DMSO (100%) and diluted with 19.8 ml of olive oil to a final concentration of 0.5 mg/ml [ ].

## Intranasal drug administration
Animal handling and administration of intranasal drug were performed according to Hanson et al. [ ], the rat was handled and inverted in a way that ensure preventing the movement of the head and the neck is parallel to the floor, the micropipette tip is near the nostril at 45-degree angle. The drug is dropped slowly in a small droplet to allow the inhalation of the drug then the animal is held in this position for 15 s, the procedure is repeated to the other nostril [ ].

## Experimental design
Rats were divided into four equal groups at random with different drug regimens as shown in Fig.  .
 Normal group: Rats were subcutaneously injected with DMSO + olive oil for four weeks during the induction period and continued without treatment.
Model group: Rats were subcutaneously injected with (2 mg/kg/day) rotenone for four weeks and continued without treatment [ ].
In the BER-Chitosomes group, the rats were subcutaneously injected with (2 mg/kg/day) rotenone for four weeks. Then, the rats were intranasally administered berberine chitosomes (0.5 mg/kg/day) for four weeks [ ].
In the BER-solution group, the rats were subcutaneously injected with (2 mg/kg/day) rotenone for four weeks. Then, the rats were intranasally administered berberine solution (0.5 mg/kg/day) for four weeks [ ].

## Behavioral tests
Behavioral tests were conducted on day 57 and lasted for seven consecutive days. All rats were subjected to all behavioral tests.

## Open field test
An open field test was conducted utilizing open field apparatus consisting of 72 × 72 cm2 and 36 cm high white wood walls in order to evaluate the function of locomotor rodents. The floor was split into sixteen squares with an 18 × 18 cm2 central square. There was a video camera on top of the box. For three minutes, the rats were allowed to traverse the area while being timed by a timer. Between tests, the equipment was cleaned with 70% ethyl alcohol. Measurements of the rats’ motor activity included the number of crossed lines (horizontal activity), the frequency of rearing (vertical activity), and the amount of time the rat spent in the center (latency time) [ ].

## Rotarod test
A rotarod device was used to test motor activity, which comprises of a rotating spindle with power supply and grids underneath the moving roller where the rat can safely fall. To optimize performance, Pretraining the animals was done for the rotarod test. The pretraining comprised of four sessions on three successive days adopting an accelerating protocol that began at 5 rpm and increased to 20 rpm in 5 min; three different trials conducted in each session, separated by at least 20 min of rest. Using the same accelerated technique, the last test was conducted on day four, and the latency to fall was recorded [ ].

## Beam walking test
The balance beam walking test is a useful tool for evaluating balance and coordination. With a lamp on one end and a non-transparent box on the other, this test apparatus is made up of a beam that is 80 cm long, 0.5 cm broad, and 50 cm above the ground. For a maximum of 60 s, the rats were permitted to roam at 30, 50, and 70 cm from the end of the beam that contained the training bulb. Three trials were carried out daily for three consecutive days for every rat. After that, during testing, the rats are allowed to walk down the beam, just like they did during training [ ]. Carter et al. developed a scoring system; a number between 1 and 7 denotes a rodent’s capacity to use all four limbs as well as their ability to balance and coordinate [ ].

## Sample collection
On day 64, rats were euthanized by intra-peritoneal thiopental injection (70 mg/kg, i.p.) [ ] and their brains were promptly removed, dissected, cleaned in ice-cold isotonic saline, and weighed. The sagittal halves of the brain were separated. After the striatum was sliced, the right half was preserved in Bouin’s fixative for histological and immunohistochemical evaluation, while the other half was homogenized with an ice-cold homogenizer in phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4). For five minutes, the homogenate was centrifuged at 8000 rpm. For additional biomarker tests, the supernatant was stored at -80 °C.

## Biomarker assay
The homogenate was used for the evaluation of biomarkers such as total antioxidant capacity (catalog number: ab65329), rat NFE2L2 (catalog number: E-EL-R1052), rat IL-1β (catalog no.: E-EL-R0012), rat NF-κB (catalog number. MBS 453975), dopamine kit (catalog number: EU0392), rat Bax (catalog number: ER0512), rat NLRP (catalog number: ER1965), rat Bcl-2 (catalog number: CSB-E08854r), and rat alpha synuclein (catalog number: CSB-EL021912RA) were used. The biomarkers were measured according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

## Histopathological evaluation and immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Bouin’s fixative was used to fix the midbrain samples, followed by xylol clearing, graded alcohol dehydration, and paraffin embedding. After cutting paraffin slices to a thickness of roughly five microns using a rotatory microtome, the slices were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). A Ventana Benchmark XT automated system (Ventana Medical System, Tucson, AZ, USA) was used to perform IHC on four-micron sections for the rabbit monoclonal antibody GFAP (EP672Y, Cell Marque, CA, USA). The stained sections were photographed using an Olympus® CX41 light microscope in bright field mode and an Olympus® SC100 digital camera.

## Statistical analysis
All findings are shown as means ± SDs. Student’s t test was used to examine the colloidal characteristics of nanocarriers (p < 0.05). To evaluate differences between in vivo experimental groups, a post-hoc Tukey’s test was used after a one-way ANOVA for all the data. GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Prism Software Inc.) was utilized for the analysis. Statistical significance was defined as p-values less than 0.05.

## Results and discussion

## Preparation and characterization of liposomes and chitosomes
Lipid nanocarriers, including liposomes, are highly applicable nanosystems. They are biocompatible and biodegradable and demonstrate lower toxicity than polymeric nanocarriers [ ]. Phospholipids demonstrate excellent biocompatibility and significant amphiphilicity, making them a key excipient for nanoformulations and improving therapeutic applications in drug delivery of both hydrophilic and lipophilic drugs [ ]. Compared with conventional liposomes, flexible liposomes with edge activators such as tween 80 are considered better drug carriers and can pass through pores that are smaller than their size as a result of the high curvature of the phospholipid bilayer [ ]. Flexible anionic liposomes were effectively formulated using an ethanol injection method that is characterized by simplicity and reproducibility [ ]. Ethanol was chosen due to its reduced toxicity compared to other organic solvents and its ability to improve the elasticity of vesicle membranes. The incorporation of ethanol in formulated vesicles for topical application has been shown to enhance vesicles’ penetration [ ].
Tween 80 was incorporated in the vesicles to obtain deformable vesicles that assure efficient mucosal penetration [ ]. Also, cholesterol was included in the preparation to enhance the stabilization of the outer phospholipid layer [ ].
Following the preparation of the anionic liposomes, they were coated with CS to prepare CS-decorated liposomes or chitosomes. Several studies have reported the preparation of CS coated phospholipid based nanocarriers including chitosomes and investigating their potential for brain targeted delivery [ – ].The interaction between mucin and CS at the molecular level was investigated and proved using a turbidimetric assay by Guerini et al. [ ]. The interactions between mucin and CS in mucoadhesion are intricate, encompassing electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic effects. In the nasal mucosa, mucin (pKa 2.6) exhibits ionized sialic acid residues, resulting in an overall negative charge for the glycoprotein. Conversely, CS (pKa 6.5) at the same pH contains a significant proportion (>90%) of amino groups in their ionic form. Moreover, upon hydration, the polymer chains exhibit enhanced flexibility, enabling them to interpenetrate mucin molecules and initiate the mucoadhesion process [ ]. Nevertheless, CS can temporarily open tight junctions between epithelial cells, leading to improving the permeability and enhancing bioavailability of encapsulated drugs [ ].
The pH of BER chitosomes was found to be 5.5, which supports their physiological compatibility with the nasal mucosa and facilitates convenient delivery.
BER loaded chitosomes has been previously prepared by Panda et al. [ ] who investigated their potential in promoting wound healing. Yet, their method of preparation and the factors studied were quite different from ours. They prepared BER loaded chitosomes by the ethanol injection technique. They added lipid phase ethanolic solution containing BER into an aqueous phase composed of 1% CS in one step. On the other hand, our procedure included the preparation of BER liposomes followed by their coating with CS solution. Also, Panda et al. used different concentration of phosphatidyl choline and one CS concentration meanwhile we used one phosphatidyl concentration and different CS concentrations (2, 4 and 8 mg %) besides the incorporation of cholesterol to render the vesicles more stable.
Also, Nguyen et al. reported the preparation of BER loaded CS coated liposomes for the oral delivery of BER. They adopted thin film hydration technique for the preparation of liposomes followed by their subsequent coating with CS solution and the final BER concentration was 0.5 mg/ml [ ]. In our study we adopted the ethanol injection technique and BER concentration in the final formulation is 2 mg/ml.
Table demonstrated the composition of liposomal and chitosomal formulations. The effects of drug loading and CS coating on the particle size, zeta potential, PDI, and EE % of the different preparations were investigated. The choice of Lipoid S100 and CS concentrations was based on the results of a previous study of the authors Haidy Abbas and Manal El Sheikh that reported the preparation of luteolin loaded chitosomes and investigating their potential in Alzheimer’s disease management through the intranasal route [ ].
Results demonstrated in Table showed that loading liposomes with BER (2 mg/ml) increased the particle size of F1 (blank) from 176 ± 2.08 nm to 220 ± 1.08 nm in F2 (Student’s t-test, p < 0.05). The results presented align with earlier studies [ , , ]. This effect could be attributed to the inclusion of positively charged BER within the lipid bilayer, enabling interactions with negatively charged phospholipids. Additionally, a 1.34-fold decrease in the zeta potential of BER-liposomes compared to the blank liposomes (F1) further supports these results.
 The effect of the CS coat on the vesicular size was also investigated (Table ). Coating the prepared liposomes with different concentrations of CS resulted in a significant increase in their particle size (Student’s t test, p < 0.05) from 220 ± 1.08 nm (F2) to 301 ± 3.73 nm (F3), 312 ± 2.18 (F4) and 558 ± 4.98 (F5). This finding is in agreement with the previous findings of Abbas et al. [ ], who reported that increasing the CS concentration increased the particle size [ ]. The PDIs of the various formulations varied from 0.255 ± 0.07 (F1) to 0.419 ± 0.03 (F4), suggesting the homogeneity of the prepared formulations till 4 mg % CS. Abd el Azim et reported that formulations has homogenous distribution with PDI values up to 0.4 [ ]. On the other hand, excess usage of chitosan (F5) results in insufficient adsorption of chitosan on the liposome surface. As a result, particle aggregation occurs, leading to poor homogeneity (PDI 0.717 ± 0.08) [ ].
Regarding the zeta potential investigations, liposomal formulations F1 and F2 exhibited negative surface charges (-24 ± 1.88 for F1 and − 18 ± 1.45 for F2). Conversely, there was an inversion of the surface charge from negative zeta potential in liposomes (F1 and F2) to positive zeta potential in chitosomes (F3, F4, and F5), attributed to the cationic charge of CS, highlighting the efficacy of the CS coating [ , ]. CS is well known for its capability to coat the surfaces of negatively charged liposomes as a result of the electrostatic interactions between the positively charged amine (NH3+) group of CS and the negatively charged polar head group of phospholipids [ , – ]. Additional processes, including hydrogen bonding between the polysaccharide and the phospholipid head groups, may be also involved in the CS coating process [ ].
Results demonstrated significant increase in zeta potential magnitude of F4 compared to F3, while no significant increase in surface charge magnitude up on increasing CS between F4 and F5. This “steady state effect” in surface charge, may be attributed to the liposomal surface saturation with CS.

## EE %
The encapsulation of BER in liposomes (F2) showed a relatively low EE % (79.2 ± 2.02). This might be due to the restricted quantity of BER that can be captured in the phospholipid layer of anionic liposomes besides, the hydrophilic drug is typically encapsulated with low efficiency due to the presence of a large volume of external aqueous hydration medium, So, very little content of hydrophilic drug can be entrapped within the aqueous core of the liposome [ ]. On the other hand, Chitosomes reportedly improve the EE% due to the effect of CS on the stabilization of the membrane bilayer [ ]. Coating liposomes with CS resulted in an increase in the EE % of chitosomes compared with that of their corresponding liposomes. The EE % values increased in F3 and F4 chitosomes (CS concentration 2 mg % and 4 mg%) to 80.3 ± 1.65% and 81.9 ± 1.90% respectively. Conversely, F5 showed a decrease in the EE % (77.1 ± 1.26%) among all the prepared formulations [ ].
In conclusion, previous findings suggested that CS concentration of 4 mg%, (F4) yielded the most promising quality attributes regarding the size (312 ± 2.18 nm), homogeneity (PDI 0.419 ± 0.03) surface charge (34 ± 2.01 mv), and the highest EE% 81.9 ± 1.90% Therefore, F4 is used for further investigations.

## Drug release and release kinetics studies
The cumulative in vitro release profiles of BER solution, F2 and F4 are illustrated in Fig. . The BER solution was completely released within 4 h. F2 and F4 showed sustained release biphasic release patterns that extended for 24 h. with an initial burst effect. This sustained release profile confirmed the efficient nanoencapsulation of BER. After 30 min, F4 and F2 released 15.45% and 16.21%, respectively. This may result from the time required for drug partitioning to the aqueous medium from either liposomes or chitosomes. It was noticed that the BER release rate from formulation F2 was lower than that from F4. This difference can be attributed to the weakly acidic dissolution medium (pH 5), where chitosan becomes protonated, leading to swelling of the chitosan coating layer and consequently higher BER release. Additionally, the presence of repulsive forces between the positively charged chitosan and the protonated BER molecules further contributes to this effect. These results align with previously reported data on the encapsulation of DOX in CS/PLGA nanoparticles [ , ].
 Regarding the release kinetics, Table displays the coefficients of variation (R2) after the release data were fitted to the zero, first order, and Higuchi equations. The highest R2 values showed that the BER release from all formulations was in line with first-order release kinetics. Such release kinetics explained that the drug solution is confined inside a porous membrane, and diffusion occurs [ ]. From the aforementioned results, F4 was chosen for morphological examinations and in vivo studies.
 

## TEM
The TEM micrographs of blank liposomes (F1) and selected BER-loaded chitosomes (F4) shown in Fig.  (A and B, respectively) demonstrated spherical vesicles with no aggregation or undesirable structures, indicating the homogeneity of the prepared nano-dispersions. Additionally, a dark chitosan coating is visible over the vesicular surface of BER-loaded chitosomes (Fig.  B).
 

## FTIR
FTIR spectra of Lipoid S100, CS, BER, their physical mixture and BER chitosomes are demonstrated in Fig. . The FTIR spectrum of lipoid S100 demonstrated free hydroxyl stretching bands at 3438 cm− 1 and at 1734 cm− 1 (C = O) of ester linkage [ , ]. CS IR spectrum displayed characteristic peaks at 3343, 1636, 1375 cm− 1 that correspond to O-H, amide and C-O (primary alcoholic) groups, respectively [ , ]. A strong band in the region 2869–3544 cm− 1 represents N-H and O-H stretching band, as well as the intramolecular hydrogen bonds in CS structure [ ].
 BER spectrum displayed several characteristic peaks at 2909 cm− 1 and 2852 cm− 1 that represent C–H stretching (alkanes), 1504 cm− 1 (aromatic C–C vibrations), 1102 cm− 1 (ring deformation and C–H bending and 1034 cm− 1 (C–H vibrations) these findings coincide with Lam et al. [ ] and Battu et al. findings [ ]. The physical mixture of BER individual components displayed the main characteristic peak in each component.
On the other hand, some changes were noted in BER chitosomes spectrum suggesting effective interactions between Lipoid S 100 and CS that further confirmed effective CS coating. In BER chitosomes spectrum the broadening of OH group absorption bands may be ascribed to complex formation between the Lipoid S 100 and CS. This broadening occurs as a result of the presence of more hydroxyl groups and the possibility of new intermolecular hydrogen bond formation [ ].
Moreover, the characteristic peak of carbonyl of Lipoid S100 that appeared in its original spectrum and in the physical mixture was shifted from 1734 cm− 1 to 1636 cm− 1 in BER chitosomes spectrum and this peak shift was accompanied with reduced peak intensity. The decrease in the frequency indicates a strengthening of the hydrogen bonds or even a formation of new hydrogen bonds between the components [ ].

## DSC
DSC is one of the most applicable thermal analysis methods to demonstrate the thermal behavior of a substance (Fig. ). In addition to some thermodynamic variables, DSC can give information about transitions temperatures, degree of crystallization, melting, and heat capacity [ ]. Lipoid S 100 thermogram exhibited distinct endothermal peaks starting at 165 °C, indicative of the transition from the gel state to the liquid crystal state [ ]. Meanwhile, the DSC thermogram of BER displays three endothermic peaks. These findings are along with previous reports of Mehra et al. and Solanki et al. [ , ]. The three endothermic peaks appeared at 113 °C, 158 °C and 207 °C. The high-intensity peak is intense which affirms the crystalline nature of the BER. The DSC of BER chitosomes did not display characteristic sharp peaks suggesting the amorphous nature of BER chitosomes [ ].
 

## In vivo studies

## Effect of BER chitosomes and BER solution on motor activity, coordination, and balance in rat model of parkinsonism
The degeneration of dopaminergic neurons is responsible for motor dysfunction in PD patients [ ]. In the current study, the motor function, balance and coordination of the rats were assessed by the open field test, rotarod test and beam walking test. Rotenone significantly decreased motor activity. It was found that horizontal activity (the number of crossed lines) and the vertical activity (the frequency of rearing) significantly decreased by 64% (p < 0.0001) and 70% (p < 0.0001), respectively, in the open-field test. However, there was a significant increase in the latency time (open-field test) of twenty times (p < 0.0001) when compared to the normal group. In comparison to the normal group, the results (Fig. D and E) showed a substantial reduction in the latency period (on the rotarod test) of 90% (p < 0.0001) and a significant decrease in the motor coordination score (on the beam walking test) of 62% (p < 0.0001). Chen CN et al.‘s findings were consistent with the present findings [ ]. BER chitosomes significantly improved their vertical activity (frequency of rearing), horizontal activity (number of crossed lines), the latency period (rotarod test), and the motor coordination score (beam walking test) by 500% (p < 0.0001), 890% (p < 0.0001), 1319% (p < 0.0001), and 161% (P < 0.0001), respectively, in comparison to the model group. They also significantly decreased the latency time (open-field test) by 90% (p < 0.0001), in comparison to the model group. However, BER solution treatment greatly increased the rats’ motor activity by increasing their vertical activity (frequency of rearing) and horizontal activity (number of crossed lines), decreasing their latency time (open-field test), increasing their latency period (rotarod test), and improving their motor coordination (beam walking test) by 290% (p < 0.0001), 350% (p < 0.0001), 83% (p < 0.0001), 751% (p < 0.0071), and 145% (p < 0.0001), respectively, in comparison to those of the model group. Treatment with BER-containing chitosomes improved motor activity to a greater extent. It has been reported that berberine improved the decrease in motor balance and coordination in MPTP/P-induced PD mice, which was accompanied by attenuated dopaminergic neural loss [ ].
 

## Effect of BER chitosomes and BER solution on dopamine and α-synuclein in rat model of parkinsonism
In earlier studies, rotenone decreased the level of dopamine and increased oxidative stress along with marked degeneration of dopaminergic neurons [ ]. Moreover, rotenone upregulates α-synuclein, a hallmark of parkinsonism [ ]. In the model group, dopamine levels were significantly reduced by 43% (p < 0.0001), and α-synuclein levels were significantly increased by 1.5 times (p < 0.0001), according to the current data (Fig. A and B). In contrast, BER sol. and BER chitosome administration raised dopamine levels by 48% and 55% at (p < 0.0001), respectively, and reduced α-synuclein levels by 40% and 52% at (p < 0.0001), respectively, in comparison to the model group. Berberine protects against dopamine depletion and α-synuclein upregulation, as reported in previous studies, this finding was correlated with the improvement of the number of normal dopaminergic nerves observed in both groups with preference to BER chitosome group [ , ]. The levels of α-synuclein and dopamine in the two berberine formulations differed significantly.
 

## Effect of BER chitosomes and BER solution on GFAB in rat model of parkinsonism
Astrocytes, specific glial cells, play pivotal roles in maintaining brain homeostasis and plasticity and provide neuroprotection by halting neurotoxic insults. Glial fibrillary acid protein (GFAB) is considered one of the main filament proteins that maintains the structural integrity of astroglial cells. GFAB was used to assess astroglial cell activity [ ]. During the early stage of PD, neuroinflammation activates astrocytes, which results in astrogliosis, as evidenced by increased GFAB levels [ ]. The pathology of Parkinson’s disease is influenced by a number of mechanims that lead to astrocyte dysfunction, which harms other neurons. One of these mechanisms is α-synuclein accumulation, which is inversely proportional to the GFAB level and subsequent astrocyte activity [ ]. Replenishment of functional astocytes is promising for halting PD progression [ ]. Several approaches target not only neurons but also astrocytes for the neurodegenerative disorder manegment by restoring the neuroprotective function of astrocytes [ ]. As mentioned, the current study showed that parkinsonian rats exhibited only a few GFAP-positive star‐shaped astrocytes scattered in the substantia nigra (Fig. B). Normal rats exhibited many GFAP‐positive star‐shaped astrocytes with thin regular glial fibers in the substantia nigra (Fig. A).
 Compared with the model group, the BER chitosome group exhibited many GFAP-positive astrocytes densely stained (Fig.  C). On the other hand the BER sol. group (Fig.  D) showed fewer GFAP‐positive astrocytes compared to the BER chitosomes group. The current study was the first to assess the effect of berberine on GFAB, and the effect of BER on restoring astroglial activity was greater than that of BER solution.

## Effect of BER chitosomes and BER solution on histological changes in rat model of parkinsonism
The current histological results showed normal dopaminergic neurons with vesicular nuclei (Fig. A), while the midbrain of the rotenone-induced parkinsonism model rats showed degenerated neurons that appeared smaller in size and decreased in number. The neurons were irregularly damaged cells with vacuolations in the neuropil. Additionally, many Lewy bodies were observed in the cytoplasm (Fig. B and C). These findings were reported in earlier studies showing that rotenone induced neural degeneration with shrunken neurons and pyknotic nuclei in rats [ ]. The midbrains of BER chitosomes group demonstrated a noticeable elevation in the number and size of normal neurons with vesicular nuclei and fewer degenerated neurons and Lewy bodies (Fig. D), while the BER sol. group showed a mild improvement in the number of degenerated neurons along with noticeable Lewy bodies, but these numbers were less than those observed in the model group. (Fig. E). A previous study revealed that BER protected against neuronal cell death by inducing the antioxidant HO-1 [ ].
 

## Effect of BER chitosomes and BER solution on oxidative stress and neuroinflammatory and apoptosis biomarkers in rat model of parkinsonism
An imbalance in reactive species production and metabolism results in oxidative stress, apoptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction and thus dopaminergic neuronal loss in PD. Nrf2 provides protection against cellular damage and α-synuclein accumulation [ ]. Rotenone causes oxidative damage by inducing oxidative stress and depleting antioxidants such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), reduced glutathione (GSH) and catalase [ ].
The results of the present study (Fig. A and B) revealed that rats injected with rotenone exhibited significant decreases in Nrf2 and TAC, by 55% (p < 0.0001) and 58% (p = 0.0002), respectively. Earlier studies reported that chronic rotenone exposure results in decreased expression of Nrf2 [ ] and decreased levels of the antioxidants TAC and SOD [ ]. However, treatment with BER chitosomes or BER solution increased the level of Nrf2 by 113% (p < 0.0001) and 62% (p < 0.0002), respectively. Moreover, treatment with BER chitosomes or BER solution significantly increased the TAC by 149% (p < 0.0001) and 53% (p < 0.0337), respectively. In comparison with control rats, BER-treated rats exhibited greater increases in the levels of the antioxidants Nrf2 and TAC. These results coincide with a previous study showing that the anti-inflammatory effect of berberine involves Nrf2 activation and that the role of berberine as an anti-inflammatory agent is dependent on Nrf2 in a systemic acute inflammation mouse model [ ]. Additionally, berberine increases TAC in lead acetate-induced liver toxicity rat model [ ].
 Aggregated alpha-synuclein triggers the NLRP3 inflammasome by activating NF-κB, which in turn triggers the cleavage of IL-1β and IL-18. The release of proinflammatory cytokines influences the progression of neurodegenerative conditions [ ]. The NLRP3 inflammasome indirectly activates apoptosis by upregulating Bax and downregulating Bcl-2 [ ]. In the present study (Fig. A, B and C), rotenone significantly increased the levels of the inflammatory markers NF-κB, the NLRP3 inflammasome and IL-1β by two- and half-fold (p < 0.0001), four- and half-fold (p < 0.0001) and four-fold (p < 0.0001), respectively. Activation of NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB in rotenone model of Parkinson’s model induce neurodegeneration in substantia nigra [ ]. However, treatment with BER chitosomes or BER solution significantly decreased the level of NF-κB by 63% (p < 0.0001) or 46% (p < 0.0001), respectively, compared to that in the model group. Additionally, compared with the model group, the BER chitosomes and BER sol. groups exhibited significantly decreased levels of the NLRP3 inflammasome 67% (p < 0.0001) and 40% (p < 0.0001), respectively. Moreover, compared with those in the model group, the levels of IL-1β in the BER chitosomes and BER solution groups decreased significantly by 72% (p < 0.0001) and 65% (p < 0.0001), respectively. BER chitosomes excelled the BER solution in reducing the levels of NF-κB, the NLRP3 inflammasome and IL-1β. Previous studies reported that berberine potentially treats inflammatory vascular injury by abolishing NLRP3 inflammasome activation via Ca2+ signaling [ ].
 Figure A, B shows a significant decline in the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and a notable increase in the proapoptotic protein Bax; however, treatment with BER chistosomes and BER solution significantly decreased the level of Bax and increased the level of Bcl-2. Moreover, Fig. C shows a significant increase in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio in the model group by four- and half-fold (p < 0.0001) compared to that in the normal group. Nevertheless, treatment with BER chitosomes and BER solution significantly reduced the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio by 76% (p < 0.0001) and 62% (p < 0.0001), respectively, compared to that in the model group. The effect of BER chitosomes on attenuating the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio exceeded that of BER solution. An earlier study revealed that rotenone upregulates the proapoptotic markers Bax, p53 and caspase-3 and downregulates anti-apoptotic markers in the human liver [ ]. However, berberine improves renal function in renal injury rat models by suppressing Bax expression and stimulating Bcl-2 expression [ ]. BER chitosomes improved Parkinsonism-like disease in a rat model due to their superior nasal mucosa penetration, increased bioavailability, and increased concentration in the brain. The antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, and antiapoptotic effects of intranasal BER-induced chitosomes could provide potential effective treatments for Parkinson’s disease in a rat model.
 

## Conclusion
In conclusion, this pioneering study offers a promising therapeutic strategy for PD management by developing mucoadhesive chitosan-coated liposomes (chitosomes) loaded with BER for brain targeting. Our findings demonstrate that this mucoadhesive, nano-enabled system successfully overcomes the primary bottleneck of conventional therapies (the blood-brain barrier) leading to significantly enhanced neuro-restoration, reduced oxidative stress, and suppressed neuroinflammation in a PD model. Looking forward, the implications of our work extend beyond these compelling preclinical results. The successful nose-to-brain delivery achieved by chitosomes holds significant broader impact for neurological disorders, offering a blueprint for repurposing other poorly brain-permeable therapeutic molecules. Regarding clinical translation, the use of biocompatible and biodegradable components (chitosan and phospholipids) presents a favorable safety profile, potentially streamlining the regulatory pathway. Future studies will focus on comprehensive toxicology, long-term dosing regimens, and scaling up the manufacturing process to meet Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards, which are critical steps toward human trials. The industrial potential of this platform is substantial. As a non-invasive, self-administerable therapy, it promises to greatly improve patient compliance and quality of life compared to invasive procedures. This, combined with a potentially robust intellectual property position around the formulation, makes it an attractive candidate for partnership with the pharmaceutical industry for further development and commercialization. Ultimately, this work lays a robust foundation for a transformative therapeutic paradigm in PD, moving us closer to an effective and patient-centric treatment.
