## Abstract
Elevated lipid peroxidation (LPO), usually present in the tumour microenvironment (TME), is profoundly implicated in antitumour immunity and may be targeted for the development of new antitumour therapies. However, tumour cells may also rewire their metabolism to survive elevated LPO. Here, we report a novel and nonantioxidant mechanism by which tumour cells benefit from accumulated cholesterol to restrain LPO and ferroptosis, a nonapoptotic form of cell death characterized by accumulated LPO. Modulating cholesterol metabolism, especially LDLR-mediated cholesterol uptake, shifted the susceptibility of tumour cells to ferroptosis. Elevation of cellular cholesterol content specifically restrained LPO triggered by GSH-GPX4 inhibition or oxidizing factors in the TME. Furthermore, depletion of TME cholesterol by MβCD efficiently enhanced the antitumour efficacy of ferroptosis in a mouse xenograft model. Distinct from the antioxidant effect of its metabolic intermediates, the protective role of cholesterol was ascribed to its ability to decrease membrane fluidity and promote lipid raft formation, which affects the diffusion of LPO substrates. A correlation between LPO and lipid rafts was also found in tumour tissues from renal cancer patients. Together, our ﬁndings have identified a general and nonsacrificial mechanism by which cholesterol suppresses LPO, which can be exploited to enhance the efficacy of ferroptosis-based antitumour strategies.
Keywords: Lipid peroxidation, Cholesterol, Tumour microenvironment, Ferroptosis, LDLR, Lipid raft

## Introduction
Malignant cancer cells are usually exposed to higher endogenous oxidative stress than normal cells. This feature has been exploited as an antitumour strategy because excessive reactive oxidative species (ROS) are toxic to cancer cells by oxidizing important cellular molecules. In particular, ferroptosis, a nonapoptotic cell death modality characterized by accumulated LPO, has recently attracted wide attention as a potential therapeutic strategy for combating cancers [ , , ]. It has been shown that therapy-resistant cancer cells across diverse mesenchymal cell-state contexts, as well as drug-tolerant cancer persister cells, exhibit vulnerability to the lipid peroxidase pathway centered on GPX4 [ , , ]. Cysteine depletion, either by deletion of a system Xc− subunit or by administration of a drug that depletes cysteine and cystine, induces tumour-selective ferroptosis and inhibits pancreatic tumour growth [ ]. Interestingly, tumour-infiltrating CD8+ T cells are also reported to harness the elevated LPO in tumour cells to amplify their cytotoxic functions [ ]. Accordingly, low-dose arachidonic acid can enhance tumour ferroptosis and elevate spontaneous and immune checkpoint blockade-induced antitumour immunity [ ].
Meanwhile, cancer cells can adapt to survive under elevated oxidative stress through diverse mechanisms, including remodelling their metabolism. Elevated cholesterol levels in the TME, either by biosynthesis or uptake, are a common feature of various cancers. In particular, the mevalonate pathway to synthesize cholesterol may be hijacked by cancer cells to counteract LPO. This pathway not only affects the synthesis of CoQ and GPX4 [ , ], two key regulators of lipid peroxidation, but also provides several antioxidant intermediates, e.g., squalene [ ] and 7-dehydrocholesterol [ ], acting as radical-trapping agents to suppress LPO. Moreover, a metabolic derivative of cholesterol, 27-hydroxycholesterol, is reported to trigger ferroptosis resistance by chronic selection of cells with increased uptake and/or lipid biosynthesis, including cholesterol [ ]. Additionally, cholesterol uptake is critical for cellular survival and proliferation in cancers deficient in de novo cholesterol biosynthesis. Blockade of LDLR can efficiently inhibit tumour growth in ALK + anaplastic large T-cell lymphoma [ ], and depleting scavenger receptor B1 (SCARB1) elevates cellular ROS in clear cell renal cell carcinoma [ ]. Mechanistically, targeted reduction of cholesterol uptake using HDL nanoparticles (via SCARB1) abolishes GPX4 expression and causes ferroptosis in cholesterol-addicted lymphoma cells by activating the compensatory response to increase cholesterol biosynthesis [ ]. However, whether cancer cells that function normally in cholesterol biosynthesis can benefit from accumulated cholesterol in the TME to confront elevated oxidative stress remains unknown.
In the present study, we identified a novel nonantioxidant mechanism by which free cholesterol suppresses ferroptosis in cancer cells with normal cholesterol biosynthesis. We found that elevated cholesterol content reduced membrane fluidity while promoting lipid raft formation and subsequently restricted the LPO rate. LDLR-mediated cholesterol uptake restrained membrane LPO and reduced tumour ferroptosis susceptibility, both in vitro and in a mouse model. Cholesterol depletion from the TME enhanced the antitumour activity of a ferroptosis inducer against malignant melanoma in mice. Our ﬁndings have revealed a previously unidentiﬁed protective mechanism of TME cholesterol against LPO and its implications for ferroptosis-based antitumour therapy.

## Materials and methods
2.1.

## Chemicals
Erastin, RSL3, ML210, and liproxstatin-1 (LIP-1) were purchased from Selleckchem. Cyclophosphamide, paclitaxel, U18666A, cisplatin and 5-fluorouracil were obtained from TargetMol. Rosiglitazone, MβCD, avasimibe, cholesterol, betulin and IFN-γ were obtained from MedChemExpress. LDL, ox-LDL, HDL and dil-LDL were obtained from Guangzhou Yiyuan Biological Technology Co., Ltd. Soy phospholipid mixture, methyl linoleate, linoleic acid, lovastatin and adriamycin were purchased from Sigma.

2.2.

## Cell line culture
A549, A375, HT1080, MB-MDA-231, 786-O, and CAKI-1 cells were obtained from the National Infrastructure of Cell Line Resource of China. Persister A549 cells (erastin-resistant A549 cells) were established by exposing A549 cells to graded concentrations of erastin (5–20 μM) over approximately 4 months as published [ ]. A549, Persister A549, and A375 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (VivaCell, Shanghai), CAKI-1 cells were cultured in McCoy's 5A (PM150710B, Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd), HT1080 cells were cultured in α-MEM (VivaCell, Shanghai), and MB-MDA-231 and 786-O cells were cultured in 1640 (VivaCell, Shanghai) with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin/streptomycin (H8610, HAKATA) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator. All cell lines were cultured in 10-cm plates and then transferred into a 96-well plate for cell viability, a 24-well plate for lipid peroxidation and cells in a 12-well plate were stained by using Calcein-AM (abs42014734, absin) to count cells.

2.3.

## Patient specimen collection
All tissue specimens were collected at the Department of Pathology, Beijing Friendship Hospital, with informed consent from all patients according to protocols approved by the institutional ethics committee of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University (approval no. 2022-P2-230-01). Tumour sections were reviewed by experienced pathologists at Beijing Friendship Hospital according to the World Health Organization classification criteria. Tissues were formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded. The patient information is summarized in .

2.4.

## Xenograft assay
All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Peking University (Approval No. LA2021274) and were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the United States National Institutes of Health (Eighth edition; revised 2011). Male BALB/c nude mice (5–6 weeks old) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Beijing, China). All mice were housed under constant temperature and humidity in a temperature-controlled room under a 12 h light/dark cycle environment, were given free access to food and water and were maintained under the supervision of veterinary staff.
For cell line-derived xenograft experiments, 100 μL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 1x10 [ ] A375 cells was injected subcutaneously into the right abdominal flank of the mice. When the tumour volume was approximately 50 mm3, the mice were allocated randomly to different treatment groups. Tumour size- and body weight-matched controls were used for all the studies. MβCD was dissolved in PBS and subcutaneously injected into mice at a dose of 50 mg/kg every 2 days. ML210 was dissolved in 5% DMSO/10% ethanol/50% PEG 400/35% PBS to create a 25 mg/mL solution and subcutaneously injected into the mice around the tumour at a dose of 50 mg/kg every two days. The tumour volume was measured every 2 days until the endpoint and calculated according to the equation volume = length × width [ ] × 1/2. After 20 days, the mice were sacrificed under isoflurane inhalation (1.5%).

2.5.

## Histology and immunohistochemistry
Patient specimens and mouse cell line-derived xenografts were formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded. Embedded tissues were sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or immunofluorescence (IF) analysis. For immunofluorescence staining, after dewaxing and hydration, the sections were subjected to antigen repair to reveal epitopes by using sodium citrate antigen retrieval solution (C1032, Solarbio). Then, the sections were incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (AP36L014, Life-iLab) for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against 4-HNE (ab46544, goat, 1:100, Abcam) and FLOT1 (ab133497, rabbit, 1:100, Abcam). The next day, sections were washed and incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody DyLight 488 Conjugate anti-goat IgG (E032231, donkey, 1:200, EarthOx) or DyLight 594 Conjugate anti-rabbit IgG (E032421, donkey, 1:200, EarthOx) for 90 min at room temperature. Then, anti-fade mounting medium with DAPI (S2100, Solarbio) was mounted. Fluorescence images of sections were acquired by the Vectra® Polaris™ Automated Quantitative Pathology Imaging System.

2.6.

## Transcriptomics
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (15,596,026, Invitrogen, USA) according the manufacturer's instructions, and genomic DNA was removed using DNase I (#2270A, TaKara Bio, Shiga, Japan). RNA samples (OD260/280 = 1.8–2.2, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0, RIN≥6.5, 28S:18S ≥ 1.0, >1 μg) were used to construct a sequencing library. Messenger RNA was isolated according to the poly(A) selection method by oligo (dT) beads, and double-stranded cDNA was synthesized by using a SuperScript double-stranded cDNA synthesis kit (#11917010, Invitrogen) with random hexamer primers (#20022654, Illumina). The paired-end RNA-seq sequencing library was sequenced with the Illumina HiSeq xten/NovaSeq 6000 sequencer (2 × 150 bp read length). The raw paired end reads were trimmed and quality controlled by SeqPrep and Sickle with default parameters. The clean reads were separately aligned to the reference genome with orientation mode using HISAT2 software [ ]. The mapped reads of each sample were assembled by StringTie in a reference-based approach [ ]. A log2 (fold change) > 1 and a P-adjusted value (Bonferroni-corrected)≤0.05 were considered statistically significant.

2.7.

## Cell Counting Kit-8
Cells were seeded on 96-well plates at a density of 3500. The next day, the cells were treated with different lethal compounds (RSL3, ML210 or erastin) and modulators (MβCD, LDL or ox-LDL) for 24 h. Viable cells on 96-well plates were measured using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (C0005, TargetMol). Briefly, 10 μL CCK-8 reagent was added to 100 μL medium per well for 1 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in an incubator. The absorbance at a wavelength of 450 nm was determined by a Thermo Scientific Multiskan SkyHigh microplate reader.

2.8.

## Live-cell imaging
Cells were seeded on 12-well plates at a density of 5 × 104. After treatment as described above, cells were cultured in medium with Calcein-AM (1 μM) for 30 min at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in an incubator to label live cells. Then, the cells were washed twice with cold PBS, and at least five random fields per well were captured with an Olympus IX83 inverted microscope. Image analysis was performed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0. software.

2.9.

## Lipid peroxidation assay
Lipid peroxidation was measured by C11-BODIPY 581/591 (D3861, Invitrogen). Cells were seeded on 24-well plates and incubated overnight. After treatment with compounds for the indicated times, the medium was removed, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. Thereafter, 300 μL of medium containing C11-BODIPY (5 μM) was added and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. The cells were harvested and resuspended in 200 μL of medium. Lipid peroxidation levels were measured using flow cytometry (BD FACSCalibur) (λex: 488 nm). There were three replicate samples in each group, and 1 × 104 cells in each sample were analysed.

2.10.

## Cholesterol in tumour interstitial fluid (TIF)
Tumour interstitial fluid was collected as previously described [ ]. For MβCD-treated TIF, when the xenograft tumour volume reached 1000 mm3, MβCD was subcutaneously injected into mice at a dose of 50 mg/kg twice in 24 h. After that, the mice were sacrificed, and the tumours were dissected. Then, the tumours were wrapped in nylon net filters (NY2004700, Millipore Sigma) and placed in 50 mL tubes on ice. Tumours were spun at 1000×g for 15 min at 4 °C. TIF was collected into a new tube and purified by fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC). A Superose™ 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) was used on an AKTA purifier 100. Liquid (0.5 mL) was collected from every fraction for cholesterol content analysis. An Amplex® Red Cholesterol Assay Kit (A12216, Invitrogen) was used to measure cholesterol following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, 50 μL of reaction buffer containing 300 μM Amplex® Red reagent containing 2 U/mL HRP, 2 U/mL cholesterol oxidase and 0.2 U/mL cholesterol esterase mixed with 50 mL samples incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. The fluorescence intensity (λex = 550/λem = 590 nm) was measured.

2.11.

## Tissue or intracellular cholesterol
Tissue or intracellular cholesterol was detected by Amplex® Red as described above or filipin fluorescence staining (SAE0088, Sigma‒Aldrich) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For intracellular cholesterol quantitation, treated cells were collected into duplicate tubes and resuspended in 200 μL of cholesterol extraction buffer, chloroform: isopropanol: NP-40 = 7:11:0.1 (v/v/v). After sonication, the mixtures were centrifuged for 10 min at 15,000 g, and all of the liquid (organic phase) was collected under vacuum to remove trace organic solvent. Lipids were dissolved, and cholesterol was detected using an Amplex® Red Cholesterol Assay Kit as described above. The other was used to determine protein concentrations by a BCA Protein Assay Kit (B5000, LABLEAD Inc.) for normalizing cholesterol content. For tissue cholesterol, approximately 25 mg of tumour tissue was added to 400 μL of cholesterol extraction buffer and homogenized to extract cholesterol. For filipin fluorescence staining, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 50 μg/mL filipin fluorescence dye in PBS for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Cells were washed with PBS and imaged using an Olympus IX83 inverted microscope.

2.12.

## Modification of membrane cholesterol using cholesterol
Cellular cholesterol was modified using cholesterol as previously described [ ]. Briefly, cholesterol was dissolved in 100% ethanol and added dropwise over a period of 30 min to a solution of 100 mg/mL MβCD in PBS, which was heated to 80 °C to achieve a final sterol stock concentration of 1.5 mg/mL. The concentration of cholesterol-coated MβCD was calculated according to cholesterol. Cholesterol-coated MβCD was directly added to the cells to modify cellular cholesterol.

2.13.

## Depleting lipoproteins from fetal bovine serum (LDFBS)
KBr (41.2 g) was added to 126 mL of FBS and mixed well at a density of 1.21 g/mL. After filling the centrifuge tubes, all tubes were sealed according to the manufacturer's instructions and ultracentrifuged for 10 h at 4 °C using a Beckman Optima XPN-100 and a Type 100 Ti rotor at 90,000 rpm. After that, the bottom fraction (LDFBS) of each tube was smoothly harvested to avoid disturbing the gradient and quickly to prevent a change in the gradient caused by diffusion. A dialysis membrane (cut off value of 8 kDa) was used to dialyze the LDFBS to remove the KBr. Every 10 mL of LDFBS using 1 L DPBS was dialyzed for 4 h at 4 °C and repeated two times for complete removal of KBr. Then, the LDFBS was filtered though a 0.2-μm filter into a sterile container, and lipoprotein depletion was verified by using an Amplex® Red Cholesterol Assay Kit.

2.14.

## Soy phospholipid peroxidation
The soy phospholipid mixture was dissolved in chloroform at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. The phospholipid mixture, TBH and ferric chloride were then added to 0.05% sodium deoxycholate Tris-HCl buffer (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.8), resulting in final concentrations of 100 μg/mL phospholipids, 100 μM TBH, 100 μM ferric chloride and different concentrations of cholesterol. The mixture was sonicated to form a suspension and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. Thereafter, the mixture was centrifuged at 15,000×g for 5 min, and 50 μL of supernatant was used to measure the MDA content following the manufacturer's instructions (S0131S, Beyotime Biotechnology). Briefly, 50 μL of supernatant was mixed with 100 μL of TBA working solution and 1.5 μL antioxidants, and the mixture was then incubated at 95 °C for 60 min. When the samples were at room temperature, the MDA-TBA adduct fluorescence intensity (λex = 532/λem = 553 nm) was measured.

2.15.

## Linoleic acid peroxidation
5 mM linoleic acid and different concentrations of cholesterol dissolved in chloroform were added to 0.05% sodium deoxycholate Tris-HCl buffer (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.8) at a ratio of 1:9 (v/v). After sonicating the mixture to form a suspension, approximately 400 μL of sample in a 1.5 ml centrifuge tube was irradiated by a 300 W Xenonarc lamp with a 405 nm cut-off filter (∼5 mW/cm2) for 2 h at room temperature. Then, the MDA content was tested as described above.

2.16.

## Decomposition of methyl linoleate hydroperoxide
Methyl linoleate hydroperoxide (MLH) was prepared through the oxidation of methyl linoleate as previously described [ ] ( ). Methyl linoleate hydroperoxide (10 mM) and different concentrations of cholesterol dissolved in chloroform were added to 0.05% sodium deoxycholate Tris-HCl buffer (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.8) at a ratio of 1:9 (v/v). After sonicating the mixture to form a suspension, the samples were incubated at 37 °C overnight. The next day, the MDA content was tested as described above.

2.17.

## Xenograft lipid peroxidation
Approximately 25 mg of tumour tissue was added to 200 μL RIPA buffer with protease inhibitor cocktail (PC0020, Solarbio). The tumour tissue was homogenized and centrifuged at 15,000×g for 5 min to collect the supernatants for MDA content and tissue protein concentration analysis. The MDA content was measured using a TBARS experiment as described above. Tissue protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (B5000, LABLEAD Inc.) to normalize the MDA content.

2.20.

## Measurement of reduced glutathione
The relative GSH concentration in cells was assessed using a GSH/GSSG Ratio Detection Assay Kit (S0053, Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, cellular lysates were diluted to 1:80 for GSH analysis, and serial dilutions of GSH and GSSG stock standards were prepared as standards. 2.21.

## Enzymatic tumour disaggregation
The samples were washed twice with PBS and then rinsed with serum-free 1640 media (31870082, Gibco) under sterile conditions. The samples were split into parts (approximately 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 mm) with scissors. In addition, 10% FBS 1640 media (v/v) containing 1 mg/mL Type I Collagenase (17100017, Gibco) covered the samples at 37 °C for disaggregation. Tumour particles were pipetted vigorously by using a 10 mL pipette to enhance disaggregation every 45 min. After approximately 3 h, the samples were passed through a 70 μm Cell Strainer (352350, Falcon). The suspensions were centrifuged for 5 min at 300 g at room temperature. The cells were collected and cultured in 10-cm plates for further experiments.

2.22.

## Lipid raft staining
Lipid raft labelling was performed using the Vybrant™ Alexa Fluor™ 555 Lipid Raft labelling kit according to the manufacturer's protocol (V34405, Invitrogen). Briefly, treated cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, labelled with Alexa-Fluor-594-conjugated CTx-B at 4 °C for 10 min and cross-linked with anti-Ctx-B antibody for 15 min. After that, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min. Nuclei were stained with DAPI dihydrochloride (C0065, Solarbio). Images were captured using a Nikon A1R SI confocal microscope following standard immunofluorescence staining and mounting techniques.

2.23.

## Membrane fluidity test
Plasma membrane fluidity was assessed by the fluorescence polarization anisotropy value using a dye that integrates into the cellular membrane, 1-(4-trimethylammoniumphenyl)-6-phenyl-1,3,5-hexatrienep-toluenesulfonate) (TMA-DPH) (T19068, TargetMol). Briefly, cells suspended in serum-free media containing TMA-DPH dye (2 mM) were collected and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min. The anisotropy of TMA-DPH fluorescence was measured at Ex 355 nm/Em 460 nm wavelengths. Fluorescence polarization was calculated according to the following formula: P = (Ivv − Ivh)/(Ivv +2Ivh) as previously described [ ].

## Statistical analysis
All data are represented as the mean ± SEM. All statistical analyses were calculated using GraphPad Prism 9.3.0 software. Differences between two groups were analysed by Student's t-test, whereas differences between multiple groups were determined by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA. The results of cell culture experiments were collected from at least three independent replicates. Volumes or weights from at least five tumours in each group were plotted. For all panels, statistical tests were two-sided where applicable; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001; NS, not significant. No statistical methods were used to predetermine the sample size. The investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment because of obvious identification.

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Cholesterol in the TME correlates with ferroptosis in vivo
GPX4 inhibition by small molecules, e.g., RSL3 and ML210, is a canonical way to trigger LPO and ferroptosis. Despite their in vitro efficacy, the in vivo activity is usually limited, which may be partially ascribed to the TME effect. We therefore detached tumour cells from the mouse xenografts and compared their sensitivity to RSL3 with those cultured in vitro all along. As expected, the xenograft-derived A375 cells exhibited lower sensitivity to RSL3 than the in vitro cells ( A).
 We then examined the relevance of TME cholesterol in ferroptosis. ML210 was first injected subcutaneously around the tumour to trigger LPO in vivo. Because LDL is usually the primary cholesterol carrier in blood and interstitial fluid [ ], its uptake was measured in xenograft-derived cells through the Dil-LDL assay. The ML210-treated cells exhibited a significantly higher LDL uptake rate than the vehicle-treated cells ( B). Accordingly, higher cellular cholesterol levels were also identified in ML210-treated cells ( C). In summary, these results indicate that TME cholesterol correlates with LPO and ferroptosis in vivo.

3.2.

## LDLR-mediated cholesterol uptake modulates ferroptosis susceptibility
Motivated by the relevance of LDL uptake in ferroptosis, we found that LDLR-related genes are highly correlated with the efficacy of ferroptosis inducers (e.g., erastin, ML210 and RSL3) based on data mined from the Cancer Therapeutics Response Portal (CTRP) [ ] ( A). These LDLR-related genes include LDLRAP1 (an adapter protein of LDLR), IDOL (inducible degrader of LDLR) and PCSK9 (a protein that induces LDLR degradation). Experimentally, when LDLR or LDLRAP1 was knocked down through siRNA ( ), HT1080 cells became more sensitive to RSL3 ( B–C). Consistently, the C11-BODIPY fluorescent assay showed that the downregulation of LDLRAP1/LDLR enhanced the LPO level triggered by RSL3. In contrast, downregulation of PCSK9 by siRNA ( ) to increase LDLR expression reduced the susceptibility of HT1080 cells to ferroptosis ( E). These results were also replicated when erastin was used as the ferroptosis inducer ( ).
 In transcriptomic analysis, we found no significant changes (FC > 2 and P-adjusted «0.05) in cholesterol metabolism, except LDLR, after RSL3 treatment (2 μM) ( F). LDLR expression was enhanced by approximately twofold, although this enhancement was not confirmed by PCR in cells treated with a slightly lower dose of RSL3 (0.5 μM) ( ). Given the usually abundant cholesterol in fetal bovine serum, short-term exposure to LPO (∼10 h) may not result in enhanced expression of LDLR. We therefore developed erastin-resistant A549 cells after long exposure, whose IC50 to erastin was shifted by ∼6 times ( G). As expected, these resistant cells exhibited a higher cholesterol uptake rate assayed by Dil-LDL and enhanced LDLR expression at both the mRNA and protein levels ( H–I). Accordingly, the cholesterol level in resistant cells was higher than that in normal cells ( J). These results can explain the previous finding that xenograft-derived cells from ML210-treated mice exhibited higher cholesterol levels and LDL uptake rates.

3.3.

## Cholesterol suppresses LPO and ferroptosis
We then supplied excessive LDL in the culture media to examine its effect on ferroptosis. In both HT1080 and A375 cells, LDL rescued the dead cells suffering from ferroptosis while suppressing LPO accumulation as assayed by C11-BODIPY fluorescence ( A–C, S3A-B). Measurement of MDA, a derivative of LPO, also confirmed the suppressive effect of LDL on LPO ( D). LDL addition increased the cellular cholesterol content ( ), while knockdown of LDLR decreased it significantly. ( D, S1E). Therefore, we attribute the ferroptosis-suppressive effect to cellular cholesterol accumulation. Because LDL contains components other than cholesterol, we further examined whether cholesterol is sufficient to suppress ferroptosis. Lipoprotein-depleted fetal bovine serum (LDFBS) was used to culture cells ( ), which slightly increased the sensitivity of A375 cells to RSL3. We found that the supplementation of free cholesterol was sufficient to protect cells from ferroptosis to an extent comparable to LDL supplementation ( E). Furthermore, we used MβCD (an efficient cholesterol chelator) to deplete cholesterol from cells ( ). It significantly increased the sensitivity of A375 cells to ferroptosis inducers F, ). In contrast, MβCD coated with cholesterol, which supplied cholesterol to cellular membranes, efficiently suppressed ferroptotic cell death ( F).
 Notably, cholesterol in LDL is transported to the plasma membrane through NPC1 after being endocytosed into lysosomes. Blocking NPC1 with U18666A restricted the further transport of cholesterol, despite the accumulation of cholesterol in lysosomes ( I, ). U18666A sensitized cells to RSL3 and significantly mitigated the protective effect of LDL ( H, ). This observation demonstrates the specificity of plasma membrane cholesterol in ferroptosis, consistent with the critical role of the plasma membrane in the execution of ferroptosis [ ].
In addition to LDL-cholesterol uptake, supplying HDL-cholesterol can also restrain LPO and protect cells from ferroptosis ( G, ). As expected, modulation of the de novo synthesis of cholesterol by inhibiting ACAT1 (an enzyme esterifying cholesterol, J, ), HMGCR ( K, ) or SREBPs ( ) also attenuated the susceptibility of cancer cells to ferroptosis.
We also studied the effect of LDL on RSL3 and erastin, and MβCD on RSL3 in a variety of cancer cell lines sensitive to ferroptosis, including breast cancer MDA-MB-231, colorectal cancer HCT116 and rental cancer CAKI-1, 786-O ( L, ). The modulatory trend was consistent in all cells we examined. In summary, elevated cholesterol content restrains LPO and ferroptosis, while restricted cholesterol uptake or cholesterol depletion promotes them.

3.4.

## Cholesterol suppresses LPO by decreasing membrane fluidity and promoting lipid raft formation
To explore how cholesterol restrains LPO, we first examined the effect of LDL on the pivotal GSH-GPX4 axis in ferroptosis, which directly reduces lipid peroxides. LDL alone or in combination with a ferroptosis inducer did not significantly affect GPX4 protein expression or GSH levels ( ). ACSL4, which converts fatty acids to fatty acyl-CoA esters, is another critical enzyme regulating sensitivity to ferroptosis [ ]. We again observed no effect of LDL on ACSL4 in RSL3-or erastin-induced ferroptosis ( ). The mevalonate pathway to synthesize endogenous cholesterol is widely implicated in regulating ferroptosis by affecting the synthesis of CoQ and GPX4 [ , ], as well as the antioxidant intermediates squalene [ ] and 7-dehydrocholesterol [ ]. By both transcriptomics and PCR analysis, we did not identify any significant differences in the RNA expression of the major genes regulating the biosynthesis of cholesterol after RSL3 or erastin treatment ( ). Moreover, the expression of LXRs, genes sensitive to oxysterols, including oxidized 7-dehydrocholesterol [ ], remained unchanged after RSL3 or erastin treatment [ ] ( ). Therefore, cholesterol is believed to act through a mechanism other than radical trapping to suppress ferroptosis.
Cholesterol, as a rigid lipid molecule, is known for its ability to affect the fluidity of cell membranes [ ]. Because LPO involves diffusive chain reactions of PUFA-PL, we hypothesized that membrane fluidity may be relevant to the execution of ferroptosis. By measuring the fluorescence polarization of TMA-DPH, we found that the membrane fluidity decreased at a late time after RSL3-treatment ( A). LDL supplementation, in combination with RSL3 or erastin, increased membrane fluidity at an early time (3 h), while MβCD treatment decreased the fluidity ( B, ). Moreover, we used two model LPO systems based on linoleic acid (initiated by UV irradiation) or soy phospholipid mixture (initiated by radical-generating reagents, iron and t-BuOOH) to study the attenuation effect of cholesterol. The LPO was monitored by UV–vis absorption (at 230 nm, characteristic of peroxides) and MDA assay in suspensions of linoleic acid and soy phospholipid mixture, respectively. We found that cholesterol at physiological concentrations (30–50% molar ratio) could slow the progression of LPO ( C–D). Additionally, we heated synthesized hydroperoxide methyl linoleate to mimic the instability of peroxides. We found that cholesterol also slowed the decomposition of peroxides under heat assayed by MDA ( E).
 Cholesterol can also form lipid rafts to change membrane fluidity. By staining the membrane raft marker proteins FLOT1 and GM1, we identified that RSL3 treatment afforded more membrane rafts than the control and LDL treatment alone ( F, ). In particular, RSL3 combined with LDL treatment afforded the densest membrane rafts. A similar trend was also found in renal cancer CAKI-1 cells ( ). Furthermore, cholesterol depletion by MβCD dramatically decreased the lipid rafts resulting from RSL3 treatment ( G, ). The trend of lipid raft formation is consistent with changes in membrane fluidity under these conditions. In sum, our results suggest that cholesterol suppresses LPO, likely by decreasing membrane fluidity.

3.5.

## Cholesterol accumulation restrains diverse LPOs in the TME and its protective effect is subject to LPOs
In addition to exogeneous drugs, some intrinsic factors in the TME can also result in elevated LPO. IFN-γ, secreted by immune cells, promotes tumour cell LPO by downregulating the subunits of system xc− and impairing the uptake of cystine by tumour cells. Abundant ox-LDL and glutamine deprivation are two other oxidative conditions that can lead to LPO. Indeed, LDL could restrain the LPO triggered by IFN-γ and glutamine deprivation ( A–B). In contrast, ox-LDL itself contains a large amount of oxidized lipids, which directly elevate LPO levels after incorporation into the membranes. These intrinsic lipid oxides could not be reduced by LDL, which confirmed again that cholesterol did not act through the radical-trapping mechanism ( C). Interestingly, LDL could still restrain the LPO and cell death triggered synergistically by ox-LDL and a ferroptosis inducer ( C). More importantly, ox-LDL, similar to RSL3, increased the formation of lipid rafts and retained higher cellular cholesterol levels ( D, ). These data demonstrate that the protective role of cholesterol also applies to diverse intrinsic LPOs in the TME.
 To examine the selectivity, we studied whether cholesterol exerts a protective effect on cells challenged with chemotherapy drugs. We examined five clinical drugs, including cisplatin, adriamycin and cyclophosphamide. These drugs target mainly DNA or microtubules instead of membrane lipids. As expected, the addition of LDL did not change the sensitivity of A375 cells to these clinical drugs ( E–F). In particular, cisplatin can also elevate cytoplasmic ROS levels (assayed by H2-DCFDA, G) and cause complicated cell death, which cannot be inhibited by LDL. Additionally, no formation of lipid rafts was observed under the combinational treatment of cisplatin with LDL ( H, ). Even 10 days of treatment with cisplatin still afforded no obvious formation of lipid rafts ( ). Therefore, the protective effect of cholesterol, as well as its lipid raft promotion, is subject to LPO in membranes.

3.6.

## Depletion of LDL-cholesterol in TME promotes ferroptosis in vivo
Inspired by our in vitro studies, we aimed to target TME cholesterol to enhance the antitumour efficacy of ferroptosis inducers. MβCD was chosen to deplete cholesterol in vivo due to its good biosafety. In mice engrafted with A375 cells, intraperitoneal injection of MβCD successfully lowered the cholesterol level in the tumour tissues ( A). To examine how cholesterol was depleted, we extracted the tumour interstellar fluid (TIF) and analysed the cholesterol sources by fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC). Both LDL and HDL were identified as the two main cholesterol sources. Interestingly, the intraperitoneal injection of MβCD dominantly depleted LDL cholesterol ( B).
 We then examined whether cholesterol depletion could enhance the antitumour efficacy of ferroptosis inducers in vivo. ML210 injected subcutaneously around the tumour exerted a mild inhibitory effect on the growth of xenografts ( C, S8A) without influencing body weight ( ). Consistent with the in vitro results, MβCD synergized with ML210 to inhibit tumour growth ( C, S8A). Fewer nuclei and more vacuoles were also identified in the hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining of tumour slices from the mice treated with ML210+MβCD ( ). Examination of LPO derivatives (4-HNE and MDA) showed that LPO dominated cell death in tumours after ML210 treatment, and MβCD significantly enhanced the severity of LPO, as revealed by both MDA and 4-HNE assays ( E–G). Moreover, we analysed the LDLR protein expression in tumour tissues and found that MβCD treatment significantly enhanced its expression ( D). This again confirms the critical role of LDLR in tumour cells undergoing ferroptosis in vivo. Collectively, these results demonstrate that TME cholesterol can be a potential target to synergize with ferroptosis inducers in antitumour therapy.

3.7.

## Lipid rafts are formed in vivo and are clinically implicated
We further investigated whether LPO promoted lipid rafts in vivo. The xenograft-derived cells from ML210-treated tumours showed increased formation of lipid rafts ( A), consistent with their higher uptake rate of LDL and cholesterol level ( B–C). Interestingly, lipid rafts in ML210-treated cells exhibited a clear distribution on the plasma membrane, indicating their relevance to ferroptosis-resistance. Moreover, we analysed clinical samples to confirm the potential correlation between lipid rafts and LPO. Histochemical immunoassay showed that 4-HNE and FLOT1 were both enhanced in the tumour sections compared with the adjacent sections originating from patients diagnosed with renal cancers ( B and C). More importantly, higher correlation efficiencies between 4-HNE and FLOT1 were identified in the tumour sections ( B). In particular, the overlap between 4-HNE and FLOT1 was mostly identified in the plasma membrane of tumour cells ( D). This distribution trend is similar to that in ML210-treated xenografts ( A). Furthermore, mined from The Human Protein Atlas database, we found that the lipid raft proteins FLOT1 and LDLR are prognostic markers in multiple cancers ( E, ). Interestingly, both exhibited a negative correlation with patient survival in renal cancers ( E). These findings consistently point to the clinical relevance of lipid rafts, which are formed by cholesterol and LPO, and their implications for targeting in antitumour therapy.
 

4.

## Discussion
Despite the promising prospects of ferroptosis, challenges remain to be addressed for new antitumour therapy strategies, especially new drugs, to be developed. One of these challenges is the metabolic plasticity of cancer cells when confronting assaulting stresses. For instance, an unexpected inhibitory role of energy stress on ferroptosis has been reported through activation of AMPK [ ]. Recent studies have indicated that aberrant lipid metabolism, including cholesterol metabolism, is associated with resistance to multiple treatment modalities [ , ]. Some of its precursors, squalene [ ] and 7-dehydrocholesterol [ ], can inhibit ferroptosis as LPO antioxidants.
In contrast to these antioxidant effects, we have identified a novel mechanism by which cholesterol suppresses LPO and ferroptosis in a nonsacrificial manner. In LPO, the oxidized products of PUFA-PLs impose complex modifications on the dynamics and structure of membranes, both at the lipid and protein levels [ ]. Numerous reports have shown that LPO can decrease membrane thickness and membrane fluidity while increasing packing defects and membrane permeability [ , , ]. Rigid cholesterol molecules are usually utilized by mammalian cells to counteract flexible PUFA-PLs to maintain membrane homeostasis [ , ]. Therefore, it is not unexpected that they can suppress LPO and ferroptosis from multiple aspects. First, cholesterol can reduce the membrane fluidity by increasing the packing order and thus slows down lateral diffusion. Because the chain reaction of LPO depends largely on the diffusion of PUFA-PLs and their oxidized derivatives, it is reasonable that accumulated cholesterol can reduce the rate of LPO. Our model systems confirmed that a high concentration of cholesterol indeed reduced the LPO in linoleic acid or soy phospholipid mixtures ( C–D). Second, cholesterol can decrease the membrane permeability resulting from PUFA-PLs and their oxidized products. In a mimic model of monolayers at the air‒water interface, 30 mol % cholesterol protected unsaturated phospholipids from oxidative damage, where cholesterol itself is not oxidized, and this nonsacrificial protection is explained by lowering the membrane permeability [ ]. Changes in membrane permeability are profoundly involved in the execution of ferroptosis, although the exact mechanism remains elusive [ , ]. Cholesterol is proposed to pair and stabilize oxidized phospholipid species based on molecular dynamic simulations, which may protect membrane bilayers from disruption [ ]. Our result that cholesterol stabilized hydroperoxide under heat provides experimental support for this hypothesis ( E). Third, changes in the membranes, particularly the formation of lipid rafts, can regulate protein-membrane interactions. Lipid rafts, enriched in both cholesterol and sphingomyelin, provide a platform for redox signaling and cell death signaling [ , ]. For instance, the ASM-mediated activation of autophagy plays a critical role in ferroptosis activation [ ]. Taken together, the accumulation of cholesterol may not only reduce the rate of LPO but also strengthen cancer cells against elevated LPO and ferroptosis.
We also observed that cholesterol promoted lipid raft formation during LPO in both a mouse model and clinical samples. Because lipid oxidizing factors are frequently present in the TME, especially in the form of ox-LDL, lipid rafts deserve more attention as a prognostic marker or as a therapeutic target in terms of their correlation with therapy-resistance [ , ].
LDLR is usually the main receptor that mediates cholesterol uptake from the TME. Our results showed that LDLR expression was enhanced by LPO both in vitro and in vivo. Supplementing LDL to increase membrane cholesterol levels efficiently restrained LPO and ferroptosis. However, this protective effect is specific to LPO, while it has no impact on clinical chemotherapy drugs, e.g., cisplatin, at least in A375 cells. Our results also provide an additional explanation for the previous report that LDLR is essential for cholesterol auxotrophic lymphomas [ ]. Based on the Human Protein Atlas database, LDLR was an unfavourable prognostic marker in multiple cancers, including renal cancer and pancreatic cancer ( E, ), which are vulnerable to LPO [ ].
Finally, since LPO is profoundly implicated in CD8+ T-cell immunity [ , ], targeting TME cholesterol warrants further investigation in cancer immunotherapy. Cholesterol depletion has been reported to enhance adoptive T-cell immunotherapy, although the mechanism is ascribed to cancer cell stiffening [ ]. Therefore, targeting TME cholesterol, through either uptake/biosynthesis inhibition or straightforward depletion, can be an efficient strategy to increase the antitumour efficacy of therapy strategies based on ferroptosis.
