Nanotechnology offers a promising avenue to overcome these limitations by enhancing tumor-selective drug delivery, enabling controlled release, and facilitating multimodal approaches that integrate therapy and imaging. This review systematically summarizes recent advances in nanoparticle applications for EC, focusing on strategies that target key molecular drivers, such as TP53, PI3K/PTEN, and immune checkpoints, and evaluates localized administration routes such as vaginal delivery. Preclinical studies, including the demonstration of JX06-loaded nanoparticles combined with metformin achieving approximately 86% viability inhibition in patient-derived EC cells, underscore the potential of nanoformulations to improve intracellular delivery and therapeutic efficacy. With continued advancement in translational research, nanotherapeutic platforms are expected to synergize with surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic treatments, thereby paving the way for more durable and personalized therapeutic options for EC patients. This review systematically summarizes the latest research progress in nanotechnology for EC treatment, providing a solid theoretical foundation and clear research directions to accelerate the clinical translation of nanomedicine in this field.

## Introduction
Endometrial cancer (EC) is a heterogeneous group of malignant neoplasms arising from the endometrial lining, particularly from the glandular epithelium. It is conventionally classified into two major subtypes based on pathological and hormonal profiles. Type I EC, typically linked to unopposed estrogen exposure, is estrogen-dependent and often associated with metabolic or endocrine disorders, such as obesity and polycystic ovary syndrome. This subtype tends to be well-differentiated, slow-growing, and has a relatively favorable prognosis. In contrast, Type II EC is estrogen-independent, exhibits aggressive biological behavior, and is frequently diagnosed at advanced stages with poor outcomes [ ]. Histopathologically, the most prevalent subtypes of EC include endometrioid adenocarcinoma, serous carcinoma, carcinosarcoma, and clear cell carcinoma [ ].
Globally, EC is a leading gynecologic malignancy worldwide, with approximately 200,000 new cases annually. In the United States, 65,950 cases were projected in 2022 [ ]. The principal risk factors and etiological factors contributing to the development of EC include obesity, prolonged estrogen excess, diabetes, hypertension, nulliparity, smoking, caffeine intake, early onset of menstruation, and late onset of menopause[ – ]. In addition, genetics factors also play a significant role in determining the risk, with women who have hereditary conditions such as Lynch syndrome being particularly prone to developing the disease[ ]. The main treatment options for EC include surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and hormone therapy. Typically, surgical intervention involves a total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and pelvic lymph node dissection, preceded by staging examinations of the intra-abdominal organs and pelvic lymph nodes. However, advanced or recurrent EC is often refractory to surgery alone, and the associated postoperative recovery can be protracted, requiring patients to adapt to significant changes in physical appearance and function.
Recent studies have shown a potential decline in quality of life, particularly among elderly individuals, following surgical procedures[ ]. Moreover, radiation therapy is routinely administered preoperatively or postoperatively to shrink tumor size or prevent local resurgence, yet it carries inherent risks of adverse effects and possible damage to adjacent healthy tissues, notably in organs, such as the bladder and rectum[ ]. Meanwhile, chemotherapy is reserved for patients with recurrent, metastatic, or high-risk cancer, but it is accompanied by drawbacks, such as adverse effects and the potential development of drug resistance[ ]. Therefore, while conventional approaches to EC management offer a certain extent of disease control, substantial challenges continue to arise. These challenges involve surgical risks, adverse reactions associated with radiation therapy, as well as the side effects and increasing incidence of drug resistance related to chemotherapy. Consequently, there remains an urgent need for further research aimed at refining current treatments or developing innovative therapeutic strategies to improve both treatment outcomes and quality of life for patients diagnosed with EC[ ].
Nanoparticles, with sizes ranging from 1 to 100 nm, are referred to as minute particles that have extensive utilization across diverse fields, such as chemistry, engineering, environment, and medicine, among others. In the context of EC treatment, nanoparticles have exhibited significant potential and importance. First, nanoparticles facilitate targeted drug delivery, with evidence indicating that Enhancer of Zeste Homolog 2 (EZH2) siRNA nanoparticles specifically targeted EC cells[ ]. Nanoparticles, due to their small size, penetrate cell membranes and tissue barriers more effectively, resulting in enhanced distribution and accumulation within the body. For instance, the blood–brain barrier frequently prevents the passage of numerous pharmacological agents into brain tissue, thus impeding effective interventions for neurological disorders. Nevertheless, nanoparticle-based drug delivery system (DDS) can overcome this barrier by optimizing particle size and surface properties, thereby facilitating therapeutic strategies for intracranial metastatic tumors. Furthermore, nanoparticles serve as effective drug carriers, enhancing drug solubility in water and stability[ ], extending their duration within the body, and modifying their metabolic rate and clearance, thereby increasing drug bioavailability. In addition, nanoparticles can enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy by overcoming drug resistance[ ]. They also contribute to the assessment and measurement of tumor severity and exhibit cytotoxic effects on EC cells[ , ], opening up promising therapeutic avenues for patients. Nanotechnology and nanorobotics offer innovative solutions for the diagnosis and treatment of gynecological tumors, significantly enhancing diagnostic accuracy and the efficacy of targeted therapies [ ].
This review provides a comprehensive overview of the diverse applications and significance of nanoparticles in the therapeutic management of EC, highlighting their roles in drug delivery, targeted therapy, photothermal therapy, imaging enhancement, cytotoxicity modulation, molecular therapy, and immunotherapy. The use of nanoparticles holds significant potential to enhance the efficacy and precision of EC treatment, offering promising prospects for the development of innovative and more effective therapeutic strategies in the future.

## Classification and functions of nanoparticles
Nanoparticles encompass a broad spectrum of materials with distinct physicochemical and biological properties, enabling their use across a wide array of biomedical applications. Based on their composition, nanoparticles can be categorized into several major types: metal-based, semiconductor, oxide, carbon-based, polymeric, biological, and composite nanoparticles. Each class exhibits unique characteristics that inform their therapeutic utility—for example, metal nanoparticles offer tunable optical and catalytic properties, while polymeric and biological nanoparticles provide high biocompatibility and tailored drug release kinetics. These material-specific features underpin their roles in cancer diagnosis, targeted therapy, imaging, and immune modulation.

## Semiconductor and composite nanoparticles
Semiconductor nanoparticles play a crucial role in bioimaging and sensing applications. Nanoplatelets, characterized by their high fluorescence intensity and chemical stability, enhance imaging sensitivity and resolution[ ]. On the other hand, chiral copper sulfide quantum dots are utilized for protein catalysis and analysis[ ]. The utilization of nanocomposite particles, composed of a variety of materials, spans diverse applications in drug delivery and therapeutic interventions. Notably, antiviral and antibacterial sulfated polysaccharide–chitosan nanocomposites have demonstrated efficacy in inhibiting lung cancer cells as part of a drug delivery system[ ].

## Metal and polymeric nanoparticles
Metal nanoparticles hold tremendous promise in cancer therapy, facilitating drug delivery, immunotherapy, radiotherapy, photothermal therapy, and angiogenesis inhibition. Their advantages over conventional drugs include surface modification with targeting molecules, such as antibodies or peptides, thereby achieving precise drug delivery[ ]. In addition, in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, metal nanoparticles accelerate tissue repair and healing processes[ ]. They also serve as sensors, capable of detecting biomarkers, such as amyloid-beta (Aβ) aggregates and tau proteins[ ]. Polymer nanoparticles find widespread applications in drug delivery, cancer vaccines, and bioimaging. In particular, nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems have shown potential in overcoming the blood–brain barrier, enabling drugs to reach the brain and provide sustained treatment for neurological diseases[ ]. Furthermore, polymer nanoparticles serve as contrast agents in bioimaging, enhancing the precision of imaging modalities, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and fluorescence imaging[ ]. In the field of regenerative medicine, chitosan/gelatin nanofiber composites self-assembled into bio-inks (CG–SNA) have been developed to support bone tissue repair[ ].

## Carbon, biological, and oxide nanoparticles
Biological nanoparticles, derived from biological materials and typically enveloped in biological membranes, such as cell membranes or extracellular vesicles, emulate cellular structures. These nanoparticles exhibit high stability, biocompatibility, and targeting specificity, making them ideal for applications in immunomodulation, regenerative medicine, cellular communication, and drug delivery. Research has demonstrated the strong affinity of specially processed biological nanoparticles toward cancer cells[ ]. Carbon nanoparticles exhibit promising potential in the field of biosensors, facilitating the identification of biomolecules, thus contributing to the early diagnosis of diseases. In addition, carbon nanoparticles can serve as sensitizers in radiotherapy, aid in gene transfer, and facilitate photothermal therapy. Meanwhile, owing to their versatile nature, oxide nanoparticles are utilized for targeted drug delivery, bioimaging, magnetic hyperthermia, and photothermal therapy. It is noteworthy that iron oxide magnetic nanoparticles have specific applications in brain imaging and drug delivery[ ].
In summary, various types of nanomaterials offer broad applications in biomedicine, including drug delivery, bioimaging, cancer therapy, and immunotherapy. The unique properties and functionalities of these nanomaterials position them as essential tools in the biomedical field, enhancing both treatment efficacy and diagnostic accuracy.

## Application of nanoparticles in EC treatment
Nanoparticle-based platforms have transformed the therapeutic landscape for EC by addressing key limitations of conventional therapies, notably inadequate targeting specificity, systemic toxicity, and drug resistance. This chapter systematically explores their applications across six critical areas, synthesizing key preclinical findings and assessing their emerging translational promise.

## Nanomedicine delivery system
Based on the established utility of nanoparticles as versatile carriers, this section focuses on their application in DDS for EC, highlighting their role in promoting tumor-specific accumulation and enabling controlled therapeutic release.
DDS represents a pivotal advancement in targeted cancer therapy. These platforms are engineered to transport therapeutic agents to specific disease sites, thereby enhancing drug accumulation in tumors while reducing systemic toxicity[ ]. Through mechanisms, such as encapsulation, surface functionalization, and controlled release, DDS improve drug solubility, bioavailability, and pharmacokinetics, addressing several limitations of conventional chemotherapy [ ].
Among various drug delivery platforms, such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, microspheres, and micelles, nanoparticles demonstrate distinct advantages for EC therapy due to their tunable surface chemistry, optimal size range of 10–200 nm, and ability to accommodate multifunctional payloads with controlled release profiles[ ].
Insect cuticle peptides (ICPs) have recently been explored as biocompatible and structurally tunable materials for nanoparticle fabrication. Leveraging their natural hollow geometry and solvent gradient-mediated self-assembly, researchers developed ICP-based nanocapsules capable of co-encapsulating both hydrophilic macromolecules and small-molecule drugs. These nanocapsules demonstrated high encapsulation efficiencies (> 85%) across all peptide variants. In vitro cytotoxicity assays (MTT) confirmed excellent biocompatibility, with cell viability maintained above 90% even at concentrations up to 400 μg/mL[ ]. These results indicate that ICP-derived nanoparticles function effectively as dual-delivery systems with minimal cytotoxicity, suitable for combinatorial therapies in EC.
Obesity is a well-established risk factor for EC, and its pathogenetic involvement is primarily mediated through the endocrine effects of adipose-derived stem cells (ASCs). In response, researchers have developed lipid-coated mesoporous silica nanoparticles (LCMSNs). These nanoparticles exhibit excellent stability, biocompatibility, and targeted delivery capabilities, enabling efficient transport of siRNA against plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1). By facilitating PAI-1 gene silencing, the system effectively inhibits the infiltration and oncogenic activity of ASCs. This approach offers a novel metabolic strategy for the treatment of EC. However, current evidence remains predominantly preclinical. For example, ITGA7-targeted mesoporous silica nanoparticles show enhanced uterine retention and effective gene silencing in obese mouse models[ ], while PEGylated nanoparticles co-delivering a ciprofloxacin derivative and paclitaxel demonstrate strong synergy in type II EC models[ ]. These studies highlight the promise of nanocarriers for combination therapy but share limitations, such as small animal cohorts and absence of human data. Furthermore, while active targeting strategies improve cellular uptake in vitro, their clinical translational value requires further validation.
Thymoquinone-loaded selenium nanoparticles (TQ–SeNPs) have demonstrated antiproliferative effects in EC cells, partially through downregulation of the MAPK signaling pathway[ ]. These findings indicate that the efficacy of TQ–SeNPs in suppressing tumor cell viability and disrupting key oncogenic pathways involved in disease progression in EC progression[ , ]. Thermosensitive liposomal nanoparticles (Dox-TSL) have been developed to enable spatiotemporally controlled drug release in response to localized hyperthermia. Upon mild heating (~  42 °C), Dox-TSL undergoes phase transition and rapidly releases encapsulated doxorubicin into the tumor interstitium. In vivo studies employing real-time confocal microscopy and histopathology revealed that this triggered intravascular release significantly increased unbound drug levels within the tumor microenvironment. As a result, Dox-TSL enhanced the exposure duration and penetration depth of doxorubicin into tumor tissues, outperforming both free drug formulations and PEGylated liposomes [ ]. These findings support the utility of temperature-responsive nanocarriers for improving tumor-selective drug delivery. A multifunctional nanocomposite, Cu₂O/Au–Pt@MOF@F127, was evaluated for its biodistribution and biocompatibility in murine models. Following intravenous administration, in vivo imaging demonstrated preferential accumulation of the nanocomposite in the liver, spleen, kidneys, and most notably, in tumor tissues within 12 h post-injection. Comprehensive assessments—including hematologic profiles, liver and renal function markers, and systemic inflammatory indices—revealed no significant deviations from baseline, indicating favorable safety and biocompatibility[ ]. These results underscore the potential of this composite as a clinically translatable nanocarrier for targeted cancer therapy.
Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), composed of gold atoms and typically ranging in size from 1 to 100 nm, possess remarkable catalytic and drug delivery properties owing to their large surface area and chemical reactivity. AS1411 serves as a typical representative of this class of therapeutic oligonucleotides. This G-quadruplex-forming aptamer not only effectively inhibits the proliferation of breast cancer cells and induces apoptosis, but also shows no significant toxicity toward normal cells[ ]. Leveraging this property, researchers developed an AS1411-conjugated gold nanoparticle (AS1411–AuNP) complex capable of efficiently loading imiquimod or the acridine orange derivative C8. In vitro assays demonstrated that the complex exhibited negligible toxicity toward normal cells, with cell viability maintained at 85.00% ± 7.01%. In contrast, a significant reduction in viability to 30.50% ± 1.76% was observed in HEC-1-A EC cells, indicating pronounced cancer-specific cytotoxicity[ ].
Crizotinib (CRZ) is a selective tyrosine kinase inhibitor that targets anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), c-ros oncogene 1 (ROS1), and hepatocyte growth factor receptor (c-MET). Gemcitabine (GEM), a deoxycytidine analogue, suppresses tumor progression by inhibiting DNA synthesis. However, its clinical application is constrained by a short plasma half-life, rapid renal clearance, and significant adverse effects. To address these limitations, researchers have developed crizotinib and gemcitabine co-loaded polymeric nanoparticles (CRZ@GEM-NPs), which have demonstrated good biocompatibility in animal studies. Experiments in EC cells showed that compared with free drugs, CRZ@GEM-NPs possess stronger cytotoxicity and more sustained drug release kinetics, resulting in significantly enhanced antitumor activity. These findings indicate that CRZ@GEM-NPs can effectively promote the synergistic antitumor effect of CRZ and GEM at the cellular level, significantly enhancing their cytotoxic activity against EC cells[ ]. It should be noted, however, that the CRZ/GEM combination does not represent a standard EC therapeutic regimen, and the biodistribution of CRZ@GEM-NPs within endometrial tissue remains uncharacterized. Further investigation into tissue-specific targeting is essential to fully evaluate the therapeutic potential of this nanoplatform and represents an important direction for future research.
Furthermore, the novel nanoparticle formulation JX06-NPs serves as an effective selective inhibitor that covalently binds to pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1), an enzyme crucial for the glycolytic pathway. Abnormal expression of PDK1 in cancer is associated with metabolic reprogramming and tumor cell proliferation, making it a promising therapeutic target. The research team used fluorescent dye labeling to investigate the uptake of JX06-NPs in EC cells, revealing that these nanoparticles are readily internalized by cancer cells, with uptake increasing over time. They also assessed the encapsulation efficiency, drug loading, and release characteristics of JX06-NPs, finding an encapsulation efficiency of 40% and a drug loading capacity of 4%. Drug release studies showed a gradual increase in the release of JX06-NPs under conditions with a pH of 7.4 and glutathione (GSH) concentration of 10 × 10 − 3 M. These results suggest that JX06-NPs are effectively internalized by EC cells and exhibit suitable drug release profiles. Furthermore, drug sensitivity assays using patient-derived EC cells (PDCs) demonstrated that the combination of JX06-NPs and metformin (Met) significantly inhibited cancer cell viability, showing an 86% inhibition rate compared to 79% inhibition with the combination of JX06 and Met alone. These findings suggest that JX06-NPs, by inhibiting PDK1 and synergizing with metformin, can effectively target the metabolic pathways of cancer cells, offering substantial potential for EC treatment[ ].
Polymer–lipid hybrid nanoparticles (PLHNPs) combine a polymeric core, typically composed of PLGA or PCL, with an outer lipid shell consisting of phospholipids, cholesterol, and DSPE–PEG. This hybrid structure integrates the benefits of high drug loading capacity and mechanical stability with a biomimetic surface profile. By suppressing initial burst release, enabling tunable drug release kinetics, and permitting facile ligand conjugation, PLHNPs also demonstrate improved mucosal and oral absorption. In oncology applications, exemestane-loaded PLHNPs have already outperformed free drug in oral exposure and tumor suppression in rodents[ , ]. Recent reviews further describe scalable one-pot syntheses and rational design knobs [ ]. For EC treatment, the platform is attractive for delivering hormone modulators or co-encapsulating cytotoxic agents with pathway inhibitors. The lipid outer layer additionally enables receptor-targeted uptake and supports localized administration through intrauterine or vaginal routes. Nevertheless, further research is needed, including dedicated in vivo studies using EC-relevant models, biomarker-guided particle design, and better understanding of how heterogeneous EPR effects and repeated-dose clearance influence treatment outcomes.
A consistent theme across studies is that nanoformulations enhance the intracellular delivery or retention of therapeutic agents. However, most nanoparticle studies in this area are conducted on a small scale, they typically utilize only one or two cell lines and a single animal model (often immunodeficient xenografts) and do not include long-term follow-up. These methodological constraints limit the reproducibility of the findings. Furthermore, it is important to note that none of the cited studies reported results from clinical trials involving EC patients. Thus, future research must address these methodological shortcomings and prioritize studies that can more robustly validate the translational potential of nanoformulations for EC therapy.
Despite improved delivery efficiency afforded by DDS, achieving precise tumor targeting remains challenging. To address this limitation, the subsequent section critically examines both active and passive targeting strategies developed to enhance therapeutic specificity in EC management.

## Application of nanoparticles in targeted cancer therapy
The efficacy of cancer radiotherapy and chemotherapy is often hindered by low targeting efficiency and negative effects on healthy tissues. Thus, there is a critical need for drug delivery systems that can specifically target tumor regions. Nanotechnology-based formulations have emerged as a pivotal area of research, aimed at enhancing biodistribution and minimizing adverse reactions. Nanoparticles, due to their small size, can infiltrate tumor tissues through gaps in the vascular endothelium, a phenomenon known as the EPR effect. This passive targeting strategy enables polymer nanoparticles to circulate for extended periods, accumulating preferentially at tumor sites by exploiting enhanced permeability via the EPR effect [ – ]. Notably, researchers have successfully developed self-assembling near-infrared (NIR) nanoparticles and determined a critical micelle concentration (CMC) of 0.942 μM by assessing their maximum emission wavelength at various concentrations. This finding suggests that even at low concentrations, nanoparticles remain stable and selectively accumulate at tumor sites via the EPR effect [ ]. By encapsulating specific therapeutic agents within these nanoparticle carriers, innovative nanomedicines are created that leverage the passive targeting strategies enabled by the EPR effect. This therapeutic approach has shown promising clinical efficacy, leading to improved patient adherence and reduced adverse effects. However, the effectiveness of passive targeting remains suboptimal, primarily due to the limited cellular uptake of these nanoparticles by tumor cells [ ]. To address this limitation, researchers have employed active targeting strategies by attaching tumor-specific ligands, such as antibodies, peptides, and oligosaccharides, to the nanoparticle surface. These ligands are designed to bind to receptors expressed on tumor cells, enhancing the accumulation of nanomedicines within tumor vasculature and promoting selective and efficient uptake [ ]. Active targeting strategies functionalize nanoparticles with ligands to bind tumor-specific receptors, thereby significantly enhancing therapeutic precision over passive EPR-based accumulation. Notably, studies have shown that aerosolized therapy using iron-based nanoparticles can directly deliver nanoparticles to lung tumor lesions. These nanoparticles are taken up by tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), which induce ferroptosis in cancer stem cells through iron metabolism, thereby inhibiting tumor growth [ ]. In addition to ligand type, factors such as the stability, shape, size, and ligand density of nanomedicines play a significant role in the targeted chemotherapy of cancer [ ]. Therefore, modifying the surface of nanomedicines with specific ligands holds great potential for achieving active drug targeting and improving therapeutic outcomes.
Paclitaxel is widely used as a first-line chemotherapy drug for EC. In one investigation, paclitaxel-loaded nanoparticles (PTX-NPs) based on polyhydroxybutyrate–co-hydroxyvalerate (PHBV) were synthesized via an optimized double emulsion–solvent evaporation technique. During the initial degradation stage (days 1–3), the nanoparticles developed branched surface architectures accompanied by a transient crystalline phase, providing mechanistic insight into their sustained release profile. The PTX-NPs demonstrated considerable antitumor efficacy against EC cells by inducing G2/M phase cell cycle arrest—a mechanistic behavior analogous to that of free paclitaxel. In addition, the nanocarrier exhibited a protective effect against premature drug release within 48 h, effectively mitigating nonspecific cytotoxicity and thereby supporting enhanced chemotherapeutic safety. These findings validate PHBV nanoparticles as a promising paclitaxel delivery platform for EC treatment, with their cost-effective production and structural tailorability facilitating clinical translation[ ]. Another study examined the combinatorial antitumor efficacy of the MEK inhibitor PD98059 with paclitaxel. In vitro assays demonstrated that PD98059 potentiated paclitaxel's cytotoxicity against human EC cells in a dose-dependent fashion. The combination therapy synergistically induced dual-phase cell cycle arrest at both G0/G1 and G2/M checkpoints and substantially lowered the IC50 of paclitaxel, proposing a novel therapeutic avenue to circumvent paclitaxel resistance. To further enhance the tissue distribution of PD98059, researchers developed hybrid nanoparticles composed of poly (lactic–co-glycolic acid)-b-polyethylene glycol and poly(amidoamine) (PLGA–PEG/PAMAM NPs). With an average diameter of 184 nm and an encapsulation efficiency of 23%, these nanoparticles significantly increased drug accumulation in the brain, heart, and kidney in vivo while demonstrating enhanced targeting efficiency toward EC tissue. These findings indicate that PLGA–PEG/PAMAM NPs, as a carrier for PD98059, hold dual potential for improving therapeutic efficacy and optimizing biodistribution in combination therapy with paclitaxel for EC[ ].
Brucea javanica oil emulsion (BJOE), derived from the seeds of the Brucea javanica plant, has been extensively used in the treatment of liver and lung cancers. H-MnO2–PEG, a hollow mesoporous manganese dioxide nano-shell, has been engineered for targeted drug delivery applications. A study evaluated the apoptotic effects of co-administering H-MnO2–PEG and BJOE on EC cells. Western blot analysis indicated that the combination of H-MnO2–PEG and BJOE resulted in a significant increase in the expression levels of key proteins, including Caspase-3, KRas, and Raf1, compared to the control group treated solely with H-MnO2–PEG. Notably, Caspase-3 plays a pivotal role in regulating programmed cell death, and its activation is a well-established inducer of apoptosis, serving as a critical biomarker for cellular demise[ , ]. Consequently, the synergistic application of H-MnO2–PEG and BJOE enhances the induction of apoptosis in EC cells more effectively [ ]. These findings emphasize the critical role of nanoparticles in enhancing chemotherapy and signal their increasingly important position in future EC therapeutics.

## Application of nanoparticles in photothermal therapy
Photothermal therapy (PTT) is a therapeutic approach that utilizes materials with high photothermal conversion efficiency. These materials are introduced into the body and selectively accumulate near tumor tissues. Upon exposure to external light, they efficiently convert light energy into heat, thereby inducing the destruction of cancer cells[ ]. To further enhance therapeutic efficacy in tumor treatment, magnetic polydopamine nanoparticles measuring 9.3 nm in diameter have been developed, demonstrating effective encapsulation of targeted therapeutic agents. These nanoparticles leverage the synergistic photothermal and photodynamic effects when exposed to near-infrared light[ ].
AuNP absorb light through surface plasmon resonance, a phenomenon enabling their interaction with laser light and subsequent generation of heat. This property underpins PTT, where AuNP are injected into tumor tissues and exposed to laser irradiation. The absorbed laser energy is converted into thermal energy by AuNP, raising the temperature of the tumor tissue and destroying tumor cells, thus achieving therapeutic effects. These results highlight the promising potential of AuNP as efficient photothermal agents for tumor therapy[ ].
In the study of gynecological cancers, significant progress has been made in investigating the fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) signaling pathway. FGFR mutations are identified as potential drivers in the development of EC, with numerous studies highlighting these mutations. Research on FGFR has led to the discovery of various targeted therapeutic strategies[ , ]. Among these, small molecule tyrosine kinase inhibitors are the most commonly employed and have shown promising results in clinical trials. However, these non-specific inhibitors are associated with certain toxic side effects in clinical settings. To address these issues, researchers are exploring alternative treatment strategies targeting FGFR. Notably, studies have shown that combining therapeutic agents with gold nanoparticles enables specific inhibition of FGFR, and when used in conjunction with photothermal therapy, can effectively ablate tumor cells. This strategy offers improved stability and tumor-targeting selectivity, resulting in significant suppression of tumor growth[ ]. Gold nanoparticles represent an emerging and effective therapeutic strategy due to their ability to specifically inhibit FGFR, resulting in substantial therapeutic efficacy. Future research will further investigate the potential of this treatment modality and identify additional targeted therapies for FGFR, thereby broadening the treatment options for gynecological cancers.
While PTT provides precise tumor ablation, early detection and surgical guidance are equally vital. The subsequent section examines how nanoparticle-enhanced imaging platforms can improve diagnostic accuracy and facilitate precise intraoperative navigation.

## Nanoparticle enhanced imaging for contrast-assisted localization
Accurate visualization of tumors and lymph nodes is essential for staging and surgical planning in EC. Nanoparticle-based contrast agents address this need by significantly enhancing imaging sensitivity and enabling real-time mapping of sentinel lymph nodes. Nanoparticles, when used as contrast agents in tumor imaging, can be tailored in terms of their surface properties and composition to enhance their detection and differentiation in tumor regions[ ]. Through surface modification or encapsulation with specific targeting molecules, selective recognition and localization of tumor targets can be achieved. For example, ultrasmall superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles, known as ferumoxtran-10 (combidex, AMAG Pharmaceuticals Inc., Lexington, MA), have been employed as MRI lymphographic agents, providing accurate lymph node localization for radiotherapy and predicting high-risk metastatic areas in pelvic cancers, including EC [ ]. This combined theranostic nanoplatform has been investigated in the context of breast and ovarian cancers. Engineered nanocarriers integrating both diagnostic imaging and therapeutic functions have demonstrated significant potential in improving treatment outcomes for gynecological malignancies [ ].
In addition, research has shown that nanoparticles can be utilized as tracers for detecting and locating sentinel lymph node (SLN). The use of carbon nanoparticles (CNPs) with a diameter of 150 nm as lymph node tracers has been found to improve the detection rate and track the metastasis of SLN in EC[ ]. By injecting nanoparticles into the cervical site, they move with the lymphatic fluid to the lymph nodes, enabling physicians to accurately determine the location of SLN. This approach not only enhances the detection rate of SLN but also reduces the need for extensive lymphadenectomy in the postoperative management of EC, thus minimizing overall surgical trauma and the risk of complications[ – ].

## Application of nanoparticles in immunotherapy
Nanoparticles can potentiate robust anti-tumor immunity by inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD), enhancing antigen presentation, and synergizing with immune checkpoint inhibitors. In the field of EC treatment, the integration of nanotechnology into immunotherapy is still in its early stages. However, several studies have shown promising results. The induction of an anti-cancer immune response involves several key steps. Initially, cancer cells release antigens that are captured by antigen-presenting cells (APCs). As tumor cells die, they emit danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that facilitate APC maturation. Once activated, APCs migrate to lymph nodes, where they present tumor antigens on major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, specifically MHCI and MHCII, to T cells. This presentation triggers an effector T-cell response that is specifically tailored to the cancer antigens. In the final phase, activated effector T cells infiltrate the tumor, recognizing and eliminating cancer cells via the interaction between the T cell receptor (TCR) and MHC I-presented antigens[ ].
The transformation of non-immunogenic tumor cells into an immunogenic state under external stimuli, leading to the initiation of an anti-tumor immune response, is known as ICD. ICD can be induced by certain chemotherapy or radiotherapy treatments, resulting in cancer cell death and the subsequent release of immune-activating signals. These signals, including DAMPs and pro-inflammatory cytokines, play a crucial role in presenting tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) to the adaptive immune system, thus triggering antigen-specific immune responses against a wide range of solid tumors[ ].
Nanoparticles enhance cancer immunotherapy primarily by inducing ICD. In studies using mouse models, nanoparticles were injected into tumor sites or directly delivered into tumor cells to observe changes in tumor growth and immune response. The results showed that nanoparticle-induced ICD could enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy. Moreover, studies have demonstrated that combining nanoparticles with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) significantly suppresses tumor growth and elicits systemic anti-tumor immune responses. In addition, chitosan oligosaccharide-based nanoparticles (159.87 ± 1.52 nm in diameter) function as adjuvants in immunotherapy. This material facilitates the maturation of APCs and enhances the expression of MHC molecules and co-stimulatory molecules, such as CD40 and CD86, thereby strengthening immune responses[ ].
In summary, nanoparticles improve cancer immunotherapy by inducing ICD. By loading drugs or photosensitizers onto nanoparticles and delivering them to tumor cells, nanoparticles can trigger tumor cell death and the subsequent release of TAAs, activating the immune system’s response against the tumor[ ].
The use of nanomedicine in combination with immunotherapy is growing. Recent studies have investigated the effects of administering doxorubicin HCl liposome injection (Doxil®; Janssen, USA), along with PD-1 or CTLA-4 targeting antibodies in mice with CT26 tumors. The results revealed a significant increase in the rate of complete tumor regression with this combined approach, suggesting that Doxil has the potential to enhance immunotherapy outcomes[ ]. Similarly, experiments using Annexin V-FITC and Caspase-3/7 staining demonstrated that PLGA nanoparticles loaded with oxaliplatin (NP-OXA) induced a higher rate of apoptosis in tumor cells compared to free oxaliplatin. Moreover, NP-OXA treatment led to increased levels of cytokines HMGB1 and ATP released by the tumor cells, indicating a more effective induction of ICD[ ]. These advancements can be attributed to the improved targeting capabilities of the nanomedicine formulation, resulting in intensified cytotoxicity and cytokine release.
DNA dendritic vaccines, nanoscale structures composed of DNA molecules, consist of an immunoadjuvant DNA chain that branches into multiple subunits, each carrying a distinct quantity of peptide antigens. Research has shown that these structures can be uptaken by dendritic cells, activating the immune system and yielding notable anti-cancer effects. In vivo experiments using a mouse model have demonstrated effective treatment of cervical cancer with DNA dendritic vaccines[ ]. These findings underscore the vital role of nanoparticles in immunotherapy and highlight their potential for future applications in EC treatment.
Despite its considerable potential, NP-based immunotherapy for EC faces several translational challenges. Although preclinical studies report promising outcomes, such as effective ICD induction and enhanced antigen presentation, their relevance is often limited by the use of non-EC or highly immunogenic animal models, which may overestimate therapeutic efficacy. Further constraints include small sample sizes, a lack of critical outcomes, such as survival or long-term memory, and undefined dosing schedules in combination regimens, all of which hinder reproducibility and generalizability.
Beyond efficacy, significant barriers remain. The immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment of EC may attenuate NP potency, while the complex manufacturing of immuno-nanoparticles and potential safety risks like uncontrolled cytokine release present additional hurdles. To bridge these gaps, future studies should prioritize EC-relevant models, incorporate larger cohorts with thorough immunophenotyping, and systematically address safety and clinical translation issues in a systematic manner.

## Cytotoxic effects of copper nanoparticles on EC cells
Beyond serving as delivery vehicles, certain nanoparticles exhibit intrinsic cytotoxicity through reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and free radical scavenging, providing a dual mechanism of action in EC. In a specific study, researchers synthesized copper nanoparticles (Cu NPs) using Allium noeanum extract and evaluated their potential as agents against EC in common human EC cell lines (Ishikawa, HEC-1-A, HEC-1-B, and KLE). Through in vitro MTT assays, it was observed that increasing Cu NPs concentrations led to a decrease in cancer cell viability, demonstrating the potential of Cu NPs as anti-EC agents[ ].
Furthermore, free radicals have been implicated in cancer cell proliferation and growth due to their ability to induce oxidative stress, base substitution mutations, activation of cell signaling pathways, and alterations in antioxidant systems[ ]. However, metallic nanoparticles have been reported to suppress tumor growth by scavenging free radicals[ , ].
Researchers also assessed the antioxidant properties of Cu NPs by evaluating their ability to scavenge DPPH radicals. The results showed that the ability of Cu NPs to clear DPPH radicals increased in a dose-dependent manner at various concentrations. These findings suggest that Cu NPs possess antioxidant properties, enabling them to neutralize free radicals, which may contribute to their cytotoxic effect on EC cells. Consequently, the use of Allium noeanum extract in the synthesis of Cu NPs demonstrates promising anti-EC activity, with their capacity to eliminate free radicals further highlighting their potential application in EC treatment (Fig.  ). ## Nanoparticles: molecular therapeutics and potential applications in EC
The pathological mechanisms underlying EC are complex, involving numerous signaling pathways and molecular alterations, including mutations in PTEN, PI3K, p53, K-ras, CTNNB1/β-catenin, PPP2R1A, and MSH6, as well as dysregulation of PD-1/PD-L1 signaling and estrogen activity. These factors play crucial roles in tumorigenesis, progression, and therapeutic outcomes. Although specific nanoparticle-based therapies targeting these molecular mechanisms in EC have not yet been reported, existing studies in other cancers suggest the potential for nanotechnology in targeted drug delivery and immune modulation. Given the promising results observed in other malignancies, such as breast cancer and ovarian cancer, there is considerable rationale for exploring similar nanoparticle-based approaches for EC. Therefore, while direct evidence in EC is lacking, the potential for nanotechnology to revolutionize treatment in EC remains highly promising and warrants further research. These findings offer promising prospects for developing innovative therapeutic strategies for EC using similar approaches. ## Restoring p53 function in EC through nanoparticle-mediated mRNA delivery
P53 is a crucial tumor suppressor gene, and its functional loss is closely linked to the initiation and progression of various malignancies[ ]. However, conventional drug and gene therapies encounter significant challenges in restoring p53 function due to mutations or deletions within its genetic sequence. Studies have indicated that TP53 mutation serves as an independent prognostic factor in EC, largely unaffected by conventional clinical characteristics. Even among patients diagnosed at an early stage according to the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) staging system or with low histological grade, the presence of TP53 mutation remains associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes [ ]. Notably, the majority of cases with p53 mutations exhibit alterations in the PI3K–AKT signaling pathway, which represents one of the characteristic metabolic hallmarks of tumors [ ]. Therefore, restoring TP53 expression and achieving precision therapy hold promise for improving the prognosis of patients with EC.
Researchers have developed a nanoparticle platform based on polymers and lipids for delivering p53 mRNA to tumor cells. These nanoparticles exhibit excellent stability and biocompatibility, facilitating efficient transfection in vivo. Experimental results show that p53–mRNA nanoparticles can successfully restore p53 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma models. Furthermore, the combination of p53–mRNA nanoparticles with everolimus (an mTOR inhibitor) significantly enhanced treatment outcomes by inhibiting tumor growth and showing substantial antitumor effects even in p53-deficient tumor models. Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescent analyses confirmed that p53–mRNA nanoparticles activate apoptotic pathways while concurrently mitigating autophagy pathway activation[ ].
These findings underscore the potential of nanoparticle-mediated p53–mRNA delivery as an effective strategy to restore p53 function and inhibit tumor progression (Fig.  A). This approach demonstrates translational relevance for EC management and offers new avenues for treating tumors associated with p53 mutations, representing a significant advancement in cancer therapeutics. 

## Targeting the PI3K signaling pathway in EC with nanoparticle-based therapies
The PI3K signaling pathway plays a central role in driving EC cell proliferation, survival, migration, and metabolic reprogramming. Under normal physiological conditions, the tumor suppressor PTEN counteracts this pathway through its lipid phosphatase activity. However, in EC, PTEN function is frequently lost through mechanisms, such as mutation, promoter methylation, or protein degradation, leading to constitutive activation of the PI3K pathway. This hyperactivation stimulates downstream Akt and mTOR signaling, which in turn promotes protein synthesis, ribosomal biogenesis, and suppression of autophagy, thereby accelerating tumor progression and contributing to therapy resistance[ – ]. Nanoparticle-based delivery systems offer a promising strategy for targeting this pathway. For instance, liposomal formulations of the PI3K inhibitor LY294002 have been shown to improve drug solubility and bioavailability, effectively block PI3K signaling, suppress protective autophagy, and induce apoptosis in cancer cells. Similarly, silica nanoparticles have been utilized to enhance LY294002 delivery and overcome drug resistance, an approach that has demonstrated preclinical efficacy in lung cancer models and holds translational potential for EC[ ]. In breast cancer models, gold nanorods conjugated with PI3K inhibitors have been shown to downregulate PI3Kα expression and inhibit Akt activation [ ] (Fig.  B). These nanocarriers improve the pharmacokinetic properties of PI3K pathway inhibitors and may provide an effective means to overcome therapy resistance driven by PI3K dysregulation in EC.

## Advancements in nanotechnology for targeted drug delivery in EC treatment
Nanotechnology has shown significant advantages in the treatment of diseases associated with the female reproductive tract, offering innovative approaches for clinical intervention. Through targeted drug delivery, nanocarriers can selectively interact with specific cells and tissues while minimizing systemic side effects, enhancing drug efficacy, and improving biocompatibility. The unique surface properties, size, shape, and compositional tunability of nanoparticles enable effective adhesion to mucosal surfaces, which improves drug permeability and absorption rates. This results in optimized drug release kinetics and enhanced stability[ ].
Studies have demonstrated the successful application of nanotechnology in inhibiting cervical tumor growth and enhancing the efficacy of anti-HIV medications, further validating its potential in vaginal drug delivery systems[ , ]. In a study, toad venom-loaded solid lipid nanoparticles (TV–SLN) and ground nano-realgar (NR) were prepared and combined in a temperature-sensitive in situ gel (TISG) for vaginal administration. The results demonstrated that TV–SLN/NR could significantly inhibit the proliferation of HeLa and SKOV-3 cells. The gel possessed strong bioadhesion and excellent biocompatibility. Moreover, the nanometerized toad venom and realgar components enhanced the anti-cervical and anti-ovarian cancer effects, suggesting that this approach has the potential to improve patient compliance and reduce local irritation[ ]. The advantages of nanotechnology in improving the efficacy and safety of vaginal drug delivery mechanisms make it a critical advancement in women's healthcare treatment modalities.
Vaginal delivery methods are essential in the treatment of EC. Researchers utilized TAT-fused Cre recombinase and the CRISPR/Cas9 editing system, administering them via targeted delivery to the endometrium in murine models. This approach effectively delivered Cre recombinase and CRISPR/Cas9 into the endometrium, facilitating gene editing and improving therapeutic outcomes[ ] (Fig.  C). This innovative technique allowed researchers to delineate the histopathological features of Pten/p53-deficient EC, offering new insights and strategies for its treatment. This technology highlights the viability of localized delivery for EC patients, potentially reducing drug-related adverse effects and improving the precision of tumor treatment. Given the significant potential and advantages demonstrated by nanotechnology in drug delivery for female reproductive tract disorders, nanoparticles hold great promise for the treatment of EC.

## Targeting β-catenin in EC: mechanisms and therapeutic implications
The CTNNB1 gene encodes β-catenin, a critical component of the Wnt signaling pathway. In EC, mutations in the CTNNB1 gene increase the stability of β-catenin, leading to its accumulation within the cell nucleus. This accumulation activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, promoting cellular proliferation and transcriptional regulation, which accelerates the proliferation and dissemination of cancer cells[ ]. Studies have shown that mutations in the CTNNB1 gene predominantly occur in exon 3, which contributes to the enhanced stability of β-catenin. These mutations are particularly prevalent in the Cluster II subtype of EC, which is characterized by young, obese individuals typically diagnosed with low-grade tumors[ ]. Patients in this subtype generally exhibit poorer prognoses compared to those with other subtypes. Therefore, mutations in CTNNB1 serve as driving factors in EC, promoting the development of low-grade and early stage disease in young women. These findings advance our understanding of the pathogenesis of EC and provide new targets for personalized treatment approaches.
DCR–BCAT is a lipid nanoparticle (LNP) formulation designed for intravenous delivery. It acts as an RNA interference-based agent that inhibits β-catenin. By targeting mutation sites in the CTNNB1 gene, DCR–BCAT suppresses the expression of β-catenin protein, thereby reducing the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. In addition, the expression of PD-L1 on the surface of EC cells is elevated. Therefore, co-administration of anti-PD-L1 antibodies can block PD-L1, converting “cold tumors” into “hot tumors” that are more recognizable to immune cells. Another role of DCR–BCAT is its ability to promote the expression of LAG-3, PD-1, and TIM-3 on CD8 + T cells. The recruitment of CD8 + T cells to the tumor microenvironment by IFN-γ facilitates the conversion of immunologically "cold" tumors to "hot", thereby augmenting antitumor immunity[ ] (Fig.  D).
In summary, DCR–BCAT re-engages immune recognition of tumor cells that previously exhibited immune evasion by inhibiting β-catenin expression. Moreover, it enhances T cell infiltration and anti-tumor activity, thereby augmenting the overall tumor response. These findings provide significant evidence for further research and the development of novel personalized therapeutic strategies targeting β-catenin.

## Restoring PTEN function in EC via nanoparticle-mediated mRNA delivery
PTEN, located on chromosome 10, encodes a dual phosphatase protein. Its lipid phosphatase activity induces G1/S cell cycle arrest through upregulation of p27 and promotes apoptosis by increasing caspase activity and suppressing Bcl-2 expression. Meanwhile, its protein phosphatase activity suppresses cell adhesion, migration, and MAPK signaling pathways[ ]. Loss of PTEN function leads to uncontrolled cellular proliferation, evasion of apoptosis, and enhanced migratory capacity. In EC, PTEN loss is observed in approximately 83% of established cases and 55% of precancerous lesions, highlighting its potential as an early biomarker for disease progression and prognosis.
Researchers have successfully synthesized mPEG–PLGA nanoparticles that effectively encapsulate PTEN mRNA for delivery into tumor cells in vivo. By facilitating the delivery of PTEN mRNA, these nanoparticles promote the restoration of PTEN expression, which in turn inhibits tumor growth and metastasis. The study began with comprehensive in vitro evaluations, assessing both the synthesis of these nanoparticles and their efficiency in delivering PTEN mRNA. Following this, in vivo antitumor experiments were performed using a murine model to evaluate the effects of nanoparticle-mediated PTEN mRNA delivery on tumor growth dynamics and the immune cell response. The results indicated that nanoparticles carrying PTEN mRNA significantly inhibited tumor growth while enhancing both the activity and infiltration of immune cells[ ] (Fig.  E).
In another study, researchers successfully synthesized aptamer-conjugated lipid nanoparticles (Apt–LNPs) for the encapsulation of PTEN mRNA. To evaluate the targeted intracellular delivery and expression efficiency of the mRNA mediated by this system, the research team treated both PD-L1-positive (PC3) and PD-L1-negative (LNCaP and A549) cell lines with either aptamer-conjugated LNPs or non-conjugated LNPs. Fluorescence imaging and flow cytometry analyses demonstrated that Apt–LNPs specifically bound to PD-L1-positive PC3 cells via receptor–ligand interactions and were internalized primarily through clathrin-mediated endocytosis. Compared to conventional LNPs, the aptamer-modified nanoparticles significantly enhanced endosomal escape of mRNA, thereby promoting subsequent protein expression.
In terms of antitumor efficacy, in vitro experiments demonstrated that Apt–LNP [PTEN mRNA] enhanced PTEN expression, suppressed AKT–Ser473 phosphorylation, reduced cell migration, and decreased cell viability in a dose-dependent manner. In vivo studies using tumor xenograft mouse models revealed specific accumulation of Cy5-labeled phospholipids in tumor tissues following the administration of Apt–LNPs. Compared with both the control group and the non-targeted LNP [PTEN mRNA] group, treatment with Apt–LNP [PTEN mRNA] resulted in significant tumor volume reduction and exhibited lower systemic toxicity. Further analysis indicated that Apt–LNP [PTEN mRNA] effectively restored PTEN expression, inhibited the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, and promoted tumor cell apoptosis [ ].
This research suggests that nanoparticle-mediated delivery of PTEN mRNA has the potential to restore PTEN functionality, suppress tumor progression, and bolster antitumor immunity. Consequently, this approach represents a promising novel strategy for cancer treatment, potentially offering more effective therapeutic options and improving patient survival rates.

## Targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 axis in EC immunotherapy: innovative nanoparticle-based approaches
PD-1 (Programmed Cell Death Protein 1) and its ligand, PD-L1 (Programmed Death-Ligand 1), play a crucial role in the immune checkpoint regulatory mechanism, significantly modulating immune system activity. PD-L1, a cell surface glycoprotein, inhibits the function of activated T cells by binding to PD-1, leading to a downregulation of the immune response. In EC, the overexpression of PD-L1 in both tumor and immune cells, particularly T cells, significantly impairs their ability to eliminate tumor cells[ ]. This key finding has directed research toward developing immunotherapeutic strategies that target the PD-1/PD-L1 axis for EC treatment.
Researchers have developed a sonoresponsive hydrogel nanocomposite called TPP ALG, which facilitates drug release through the encapsulation of anti-cancer agents triggered by ultrasound. Experimental results suggest that TPP ALG enhances anti-PD-L1 immunotherapy by remodeling the aberrant extracellular matrix (ECM) left behind after surgery for residual cancer[ ]. This study innovatively integrates ultrasound responsiveness with in situ gelation technology, offering a novel approach to augmenting immunotherapy.
In another study, NP@ESCu was developed by encapsulating ES–Cu within a reactive ROS-sensitive polymer, PHPM, which demonstrated excellent dispersibility and ROS responsiveness. Once administered in vivo, NP@ESCu selectively targeted EC cells. ROS disrupts the interaction between Escu and PHPM, releasing Cu2⁺ ions from the nanoparticles. Under the influence of FDX1, Cu2⁺ is reduced to Cu1⁺, promoting the aggregation of lipoylated DLAT and the synthesis of iron–sulfur (Fe–S) cluster proteins. This cascade enhances the expression of PD-L1 on EC cells, facilitating their interaction with exogenous αPD-L1 antibodies and transforming “cold tumors” into “hot tumors.” Furthermore, NP@ESCu promotes the maturation of dendritic cells (DCs), recruiting CD8 + T cells to target and eliminate EC cells. The combination of NP@ESCu and αPD-L1 significantly enhances anti-tumor therapeutic outcomes[ ] (Fig.  F). Integrating cuproptosis mechanisms with immunotherapy presents a promising cancer treatment strategy.
In glioma models, nanoparticles loaded with a STING agonist and modified with PD-L1 antibodies have demonstrated potent antitumor immunity. Although this approach has not yet been evaluated in EC, it represents a promising immunotherapeutic strategy worthy of future exploration[ ].

## Targeted nanoparticles for ER-positive breast cancer and potential applications in EC treatment
Fulvestrant, a selective estrogen receptor degrader (SERD), is commonly used in the treatment of estrogen receptor-positive (ER-positive) breast cancer. However, its widespread use is limited by poor solubility, the need for invasive muscle injections, and the development of drug resistance. To address these challenges, researchers have developed actively targeted modified nanoparticles to deliver fulvestrant to tumors via the bloodstream, improving its bioavailability and systemic tolerance. These nanoparticles also co-deliver abemaciclib, which helps prevent resistance associated with prolonged fulvestrant therapy. When fulvestrant and abemaciclib were encapsulated in nanoparticles (PPFA–cRGD), they showed effective tumor cell-killing effects in both in vitro organ models and in vivo orthotopic xenograft models of ER-positive breast cancer, with no significant adverse reactions observed[ ]. This nanoparticle-based therapeutic approach offers an opportunity for the sustained clinical application of fulvestrant and highlights its potential as a treatment for ER-positive breast cancer. In another study, researchers prepared polyethylene glycol–poly(lactic–co-glycolic acid) (PEG–PLGA) nanoparticles loaded with 2-methoxyestradiol (2-ME) and found that these nanoparticles achieved an encapsulation efficiency as high as 99.1%, with no significant burst release in vitro. The cytotoxicity of these nanoparticles toward human uterine leiomyoma cells (huLM) was dose-dependent, significantly reducing cell viability. In an immunodeficient leiomyoma xenograft mouse model, the nanoparticles reduced the volume of xenografted tumors by 51%. These results suggest that polymeric nanoparticles loaded with 2-ME could overcome the low bioavailability, poor solubility, and limited efficacy of 2-ME, representing a promising new approach for the treatment of uterine leiomyoma[ ]. Although the study primarily focused on uterine leiomyoma, the potential of 2-ME in EC treatment should not be overlooked, particularly given its anti-estrogenic properties and the efficient delivery capability of nanoparticles. Estrogen plays a critical role in the initiation and progression of EC, and 2-ME has been shown to inhibit tumor cell proliferation by interfering with estrogen receptor signaling pathways. Therefore, nanoparticle-loaded 2-ME may not only enhance the therapeutic efficacy of 2-ME but also offer a novel and effective strategy for the treatment of estrogen receptor-positive EC.
Although the majority of EC cases occur in premenopausal or postmenopausal women, during which serum estrogen levels are typically low, estradiol (E2) can enhance carcinogenesis even at relatively low serum E2 levels (below 90 pg/ml). In contrast, elevated estrogen levels may inhibit the onset of EC. Therefore, high serum E2 levels might suppress the carcinogenic process[ , ]. These findings suggest that estrogen plays a dual role in the pathogenesis of EC, with low levels promoting carcinogenesis, while high levels potentially inhibit it. Given this duality, Nanoparticle technology opens new avenues for the treatment of ER-positive breast cancer. However, further exploration and prospective studies are essential to evaluate its potential application in EC.

## Challenges of nanoparticles in the treatment of EC
Despite their potential in cancer treatment, nanomaterials face several challenges. In addition to their small size, nanoparticles lack uniform characteristics, requiring individual assessment due to variations in shape and size, which result in diverse physical and chemical interactions[ ]. This heterogeneity can lead to issues, such as instability during preparation, adding complexity to the development of nanodrugs. In addition, nanoparticles may accelerate drug clearance, reducing their effectiveness against tumors. For example, nanoparticles modified with PEG may experience accelerated serum clearance in vivo due to anti-PEG antibodies, leading to a shortened circulation time and compromising therapeutic efficacy[ ]. Furthermore, the inherent toxicity of nanoparticles is a significant concern, particularly due to their minuscule diameter and the EPR effect. Superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs), for instance, may induce cellular stress and cytotoxicity, damaging essential cell components, such as mitochondria, nuclei, and DNA, which can result in genetic and cellular mutations[ ]. Although gold nanoparticles and suspensions typically show low cytotoxicity, the cetyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB) stabilizer in gold nanorods can cause cytotoxicity, though this can be mitigated through PEGylation to remove CTAB[ ]. Thus, nanoparticle toxicity can be effectively managed through appropriate modifications.
Despite advancements in controlling drug release with nanomaterials, instability in drug concentration within the body remains a challenge, making it difficult to achieve the desired therapeutic effects[ , ]. The application of photothermal therapy in cancer treatment also faces limitations due to the need for precise control over temperature and heat distribution in tumor tissues. However, accurately measuring and managing these critical parameters remains a considerable challenge, particularly with the current restrictions of hyperthermia technology, which hinders the achievement of optimal therapeutic efficacy[ ]. In addition, while the cytotoxic effect of Cu NPs against EC has been demonstrated in vitro, further research and clinical trials are needed to verify their effectiveness and safety in human applications.
In the context of vaginal drug delivery systems (DDS), several challenges arise, including ensuring safety during prolonged use, evaluating effects on the reproductive tract microbiome, and mitigating potential accumulation of degraded nanoparticulate materials. Furthermore, the environmental implications of certain nanoparticles cannot be overlooked. Non-degradable nanoparticles may contribute to soil, water, and air pollution and complicate remediation efforts when integrated into plant ecosystems[ ]. The pathways through which nanoparticles may enter the human body such as through the respiratory tract, skin, and digestive tract along with their potential to cause toxic reactions, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress, must also be considered[ – ].
To advance nanotechnology in cancer treatment and overcome these challenges, future research should focus on improving the preparation processes and physicochemical characteristics of nanoparticles. This includes developing nanoparticles with improved stability and reduced toxicity through techniques, such as liquid-phase chemical methods, synthetic reactions, and 3D printing[ ], which would mitigate potential adverse effects on human health. Furthermore, optimizing nanoparticles' physicochemical properties to enhance their stability, targeting capabilities, and in vivo metabolism would facilitate large-scale production. Nanoparticles synthesized with environmentally friendly methods are expected to have broad application prospects in drug delivery, improving therapeutic outcomes while minimizing environmental and health risks[ , ]. Further research into targeted delivery technology and nanoparticle surface modifications is also essential. Using targeted delivery systems to directly transport nanoparticles to lesion sites can minimize damage to healthy tissues, while modifying nanoparticle surfaces can improve their distribution and targeting within the body, thus enhancing treatment effectiveness. In addition, integrating specific targeted ligands can increase the selectivity of nanoparticles for tumor cells. Combining nanodrugs with other therapeutic modalities, such as photothermal therapy, radiation, and immunotherapy, may further enhance the efficacy of nanoparticles in EC treatment[ , ]. For example, multifunctional Au@AgBiS2 nanoparticles, when used as efficient radiosensitizers and combined with radiotherapy, can induce cellular pyroptosis by activating caspase-3 and cleaving Gasdermin E, leading to enhanced cancer radio-immunotherapy[ ]. This synergistic treatment approach may also reduce the side effects typically associated with other therapies.
Toxicological and pharmacokinetic evaluation is a crucial step in the clinical translation of nanoparticle-based therapies. Research indicates that nanoparticles preferentially accumulate in the liver, spleen, kidneys, and tumor tissues, primarily mediated by the mononuclear phagocyte system and the EPR effect[ , ]. Although most polymeric and metallic nanoparticles demonstrate favorable short-term biosafety profiles, prolonged exposure may induce oxidative stress or mitochondrial dysfunction [ , ]. Surface modification with PEG enhances circulation half-life and reduces opsonization, but repeated dosing can induce anti-PEG immune responses, resulting in accelerated blood clearance and reduced therapeutic efficacy[ ]. Therefore, precise optimization of physicochemical parameters including particle size, charge, and surface chemistry is essential to achieve predictable biodistribution and minimize immunogenicity.
From a manufacturing perspective, the large-scale production of nanomedicines remains technically demanding, because even subtle variations in process parameters can alter their physicochemical properties and therapeutic performance. Maintaining batch-to-batch consistency requires strict control over particle size distribution, surface charge, and drug-loading efficiency. Regulatory authorities such as the FDA and EMA highlight the implementation of good manufacturing practice (GMP) and a quality-by-design (QbD) framework to enhance reproducibility and product stability[ – ]. According to the FDA’s guidance for industry on nanotechnology applications (2022) and the EMA’s reflection paper on nanotechnology-based medicinal products (EMA/CHMP/806058/2020), developers are required to provide comprehensive characterization data, including in vivo ADME, immunogenicity, and toxicological profiles, during preclinical and clinical submissions. The successful regulatory approval of nanodrugs such as liposomal doxorubicin and albumin-bound paclitaxel (Abraxane) underscores the pivotal role of stringent manufacturing control and regulatory alignment in translating nanoscale therapeutics into clinically effective treatments for gynecologic cancers.

## Future directions
Although nanotechnology has greatly advanced therapeutic strategies for EC, several future directions remain crucial for clinical translation.

## Clinical translation and personalized nanomedicine
Despite the growing number of promising preclinical research, few nanoparticle-based drugs have reached clinical trials in gynecologic oncology [ ]. Challenges such as large-scale production, reproducibility, and long-term biosafety must be addressed[ ]. To bridge this translational gap, future efforts should focus on GMP-compliant manufacturing, multi-center clinical trials, and personalized nanomedicine guided by genomic subtyping of EC, such as the presence of TP53 or PI3K/PTEN pathway alterations. Regulatory successes including Doxil and Abraxane underscore that with thorough validation of safety and efficacy profiles, nanomedicines can achieve clinical approval and offer meaningful therapeutic advances[ ].

## Integration of artificial intelligence and predictive modeling
The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning is set to transform the design and optimization of nanomedicine. By leveraging AI-driven algorithms, it becomes possible to predict nanoparticle–tumor interactions, optimize ligand density for enhanced active targeting, and identify optimal combinations of chemotherapeutic and immunotherapeutic agents[ ]. The incorporation of AI into pharmaceutical development and regulatory science is expected to be transformative[ ]. Future efforts should focus on creating AI-assisted predictive frameworks capable of patient stratification, drug response prediction, and safety assessment, thereby accelerating the advancement of precision oncology for EC.

## Biodegradable and green nanomaterials
Growing emphasis on sustainable biomedical solutions is driving the development of biodegradable, plant-derived, and biomimetic nanomaterials designed to reduce toxicity and environmental impact. Green-synthesized nanoparticles derived from Allium and Eclipta prostrata extracts have exhibited notable cytotoxic and antioxidant activities[ , ]. Future research should prioritize the exploration of environmentally friendly nanocarriers based on natural polymers such as chitosan, cellulose, and lignin to enhance biocompatibility and ensure safe metabolic degradation.

## Conclusion
In this review, we systematically summarized the multifaceted applications of nanoparticles in the therapeutic management of EC, encompassing drug delivery, targeted therapy, photothermal therapy, imaging enhancement, cytotoxicity modulation, molecular therapy, and immunotherapy. Nanoparticle-based platforms demonstrate substantial advantages, such as enhanced targeting capability, controlled drug release, improved bioavailability, and reduced systemic toxicity, positioning them as promising candidates for clinical translation. Notably, the integration of nanotechnology with molecular therapeutics targeting key oncogenic pathways, including TP53, PI3K/PTEN, CTNNB1, and PD-1/PD-L1, providing a robust foundation for precision and combination therapeutic strategies. Moreover, innovative administration routes, such as vaginal delivery, may further enhance therapeutic efficacy and patient compliance. While challenges related to tumor heterogeneity, nanoparticle stability, and long-term biosafety remain, ongoing advances in nanomaterial design, surface modification, and targeted delivery approaches are expected to address these limitations. Moving forward, translational studies and rigorously designed clinical trials will be crucial to validate the safety, efficacy, and long-term outcomes of nanomedicine in EC, ultimately facilitating the development of personalized, minimally invasive, and highly effective therapeutic strategies.
