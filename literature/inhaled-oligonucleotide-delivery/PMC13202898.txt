Currently, no single antiviral therapy exists that targets all three pathogens, highlighting a critical need for broad-spectrum interventions. Here, we report the development and preclinical evaluation of a multiplexed small interfering RNA (siRNA) cocktail formulated in lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) for intranasal delivery. The cocktail comprises siRNAs targeting conserved regions of the SARS-CoV-2 helicase, the RSV phosphoprotein, and the IAV polymerase acidic gene. We showed that these siRNA exhibited potent, sequence-specific antiviral activity in vitro, and their combined formulation retained efficacy across all three viruses. Importantly, intranasal administration of this multiplexed antiviral drug significantly reduced pulmonary viral loads and mitigated disease severity in murine models of SARS-CoV-2, IAV, and RSV infection. These findings demonstrate the feasibility and therapeutic potential of a multi-pathogen siRNA strategy using a single LNP delivery platform. This approach offers a flexible, broad-spectrum antiviral solution that could be deployed early in the course of respiratory illness or during outbreaks involving co-circulating respiratory viruses.

## Introduction
A major issue with viral respiratory illness is that clinically it presents as a common set of symptoms and identification of the cause requires testing that delays treatment, if there is one. This results in increased morbidity and mortality as well as unnecessary hospitalizations. Here we propose to solve this issue by developing an antiviral modality that treats the most common respiratory viruses, SARS-CoV-2, influenza A virus (IAV), and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) in a single drug formulation. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) have emerged as a promising class of antiviral agents, demonstrating potent efficacy [ ]. Its ability to silence specific viral genes offers a targeted approach to inhibit viral replication, subsequently reducing disease severity. For respiratory virus targeting, direct delivery of siRNAs into the respiratory tract intranasally serves as an ideal route. Intranasal delivery of siRNAs in vivo targeting RSV [ , ], IAV [ ] and SARS-CoV-2 [ , – ] can ameliorate lower respiratory tract viremia and confer clinical advantage. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) is an emerging and ideal platform for delivering antiviral siRNAs [ ]. LNPs have been shown to functionally deliver nucleic acid payloads to the lung following intranasal administration [ ]. LNP systems are typically composed of a zwitterionic lipid, an ionizable cationic lipid, poly (ethylene glycol)-lipid (PEG-lipid), cholesterol, and nucleic acid cargo. Unlike standard liposomes, we have developed stealth LNPs (sLNPs) that are specifically formulated to be stable in serum, circulate for long periods, and protect siRNA payloads from nucleases [ ]. We have also developed a lung-directed LNP formulation, dmLNP, a lung-directed LNP formulation composed of DOTAP with the ionizable lipid DLin-MC3-DMA [ ]. Indeed, we have successfully utilized these LNPs for delivering anti-SARS-CoV-2 and RSV siRNAs intranasally in relevant preclinical models [ , ]. Building on the success of this intervention, we aim to develop a ‘multi-warhead’ targeting approach whereby SARS-CoV-2, RSV and IAV targeting siRNAs are packaged into a single LNP formulation.

## Materials and methods

## Cells
Vero E6 (ATCC), Hep-2 (ATCC) and MDCK-2 (ATCC) cells were maintained in complete DMEM media (Gibco-Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) supplemented with 10% heat inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco- Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) and 1% of antibiotic/glutamine preparation (100 U/ml penicillin G, 100 µg/ml streptomycin sulphate, and 2.9 mg/ml of L-glutamine) (Gibco-Invitrogen, Waltham, MA).

## Virus
SARS-CoV-2 (Ancestral) – VIC1, B.1.351) were cultured in Vero E6 cells. RSV A2 (RSV-A2) and RS4 strains were cultured in Hep-2. IAV H1N1 (PR8) and H3N2 strains were cultured in MDCK cells. Viral supernatants were concentrated in Amicon ® Ultra-15 Centrifugal Filter units (Merck, Germany).

## siRNAs and in vitro antiviral efficacy determination
siRNA targeting highly conserved SARS-CoV-2 helicase region (siCOVID), RSV phosphoprotein gene (siRSV), and a non-targeting siRNA (siControl) were designed as we previously reported [ , , ] whereas IAV (siIAV) was designed against IAV polymerase acidic (PA) gene region, a crucial component of IAV RNA polymerase complex (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table ). All siRNAs are devoid of chemical modifications and were synthesised and purchased from Gene Pharma (Shanghai, China).

## In vitro antiviral efficacy determination
Cells at 70–80% confluency were transfected with 30nM of individual or multiplexed (each maintained at 30nM) siRNAs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Gibco- Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) before infecting with RSV (125 plaque forming units (PFU)), IAV (100 PFU) or SARS-CoV-2 (62.5 PFU). A viral immunoplaque assay was done 2–4 days post-infection (dpi) using the methylcellulose overlay method previously described [ ] using viral-specific antibodies kindly provided by Dr Chris McMillan (University of Queensland) against SARS-CoV-2 (CR3022), IAV (CR7114) and RSV (motavizumab).

## LNP synthesis and siRNA encapsulation
Dioleoyl trimethylammonium propane (DOTAP), dioleoylphosphatidylethanolamine (DOPE), 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC), Polyethylene Glycol (PEG)2000-C16 Ceramide conjugate, and cholesterol were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (St Louis, MI). D-Lin-MC3-DMA (MC3) was purchased from Med Chem Express (NJ, USA). For dmLNPs, lipids were prepared at a molar ratio of 40:25:10:22:3 (DOTAP: MC3:DSPC: Chol: PEG). For sLNPs, lipids were prepared at a molar ratio of 50:35:5:10 (DOTAP: DOPE: Chol: PEG). Lipids in ethanol were mixed with siRNAs in an aqueous phase at a cationic lipid to RNA molar ratio (N/P) of 3:1 using the NanoAssemblr Benchtop machine (Precision NanoSystems; Vancouver, BC, Canada). For dmLNP 60 µg of siCocktail or 20 µg of each siRNA (siCOVID, siRSV and siIAV) were complexed. On the other hand, sLNP-formulated 30 µg of siCocktail or 10 µg of each siRNA (siCOVID, siRSV and siIAV)) were complexed. After mixing, dmLNPs and sLNPs were then diluted 1:4 with 10 mM Tris-HCL to reduce the ethanol content before further diluting in 10 mM Tris-HCL to a final volume of 15 mL. dmLNPs and sLNPs were then further concentrated in a 10 kDa Amicon ultra-15 filter (Millipore; Burlington, MA, USA) via centrifugation at 3000 g for 90 min. The dilution and concentration steps were repeated with centrifugation for 120 min. LNP particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) were obtained using Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK) in PBS (Table  ). All measurements were carried out at room temperature (RT). RiboGreen (Gibco-Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) assay was performed to determine the encapsulation efficiencies of the formulated siRNA-LNPs.

 

## In vivo antiviral efficacy determination
For SARS-CoV-2 infection, male K18-hACE2 mice (8–10-week-old) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME) and bred in-house at the Griffith University Animal Resource Center. For IAV (6–10-week-old) and RSV (8–12-week-old) infection, randomly assigned male and female BALB/c mice purchased from Ozgene (WA, Australia). Mice were intranasally infected using the intranasal instillation technique with either 20µL final volume of ancestral SARS-CoV-2 strain (104 PFU), IAV H3N2 (104 PFU) strain, RSV RS4 strain (105 PFU) or PBS (mock (i.e., uninfected)) while under isoflurane anesthesia. Mice were subsequently treated with sLNP or dmLNP complexed siRNAs of indicated doses in the figure legends or PBS intranasally (20µL total volume) administered while under isoflurane anesthesia. For siRNA dosing on the day of infection (0 dpi), siRNA was administered 2 h prior to infecting mice with SARS-CoV-2. Lungs were either subjected to histological analysis or homogenized to determine infectious viral load by viral immunoplaque assay or, for SARS-CoV-2 infected samples, RT PCR analysis against the N gene (CDC primers from IDT - SARS-CoV-2 N1) relative to mouse β-actin as done previously [ ].

## Histological analysis of SARS-CoV-2 infected lung tissues
Lung tissues stored in 10% formalin were subjected to tissue processing in a Leica TP1020 automatic benchtop tissue processor (Leica Biosystems Company, Wetzlar, DEU) with sequential dehydration steps in upgraded alcohol, clearing in xylene solution, and infiltration in paraffin wax. Paraffin blocks of the tissue were cut into 4 μm-thick sections on labelled glass slides and stored at 37 °C until staining. The slides were stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) following standard protocol on a Leica ST4040 Linear Stainer (Leica Biosystems Company, Wetzlar, DEU). Images of the tissue were taken by a VS200 slide scanner (BX-51, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Histological characteristics were quantified using the Image J software (National Institutes of Health, USA). The heatmap quantification presents semi-quantitative histopathology scores for each pathological feature (necrosis, oedema, haemorrhage, inflammation) expressed as percentage of affected lung area per mouse, with each data point representing one individual mouse (n = 4 per group). Histopathological assessment was performed using a blinded, semi-quantitative scoring system where two independent observers evaluated the percentage (0-100%) of lung tissue exhibiting each pathological characteristic across multiple tissue sections per mouse. Scores were averaged between observers for each mouse, and individual mouse values are displayed in the heatmap format.

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using the statistical software package GraphPad Prism v10.6.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Error bars represent the standard deviation (SD) or standard deviation of the mean (± SEM) of observed values. One-way ANOVA test with a 95% confidence interval (p < 0.05), followed by a suitable post-hoc test, were done to compare the treatment and control groups in in vivo experiments. A Student’s T-test were also performed on all in vitro experiments.

## Results and discussion
Major respiratory viruses such as SARS-CoV-2, RSV, and IAV continue to impose an enormous health burden. RSV causes an estimated 177,000 hospitalizations and 14,000 deaths annually among older adults in the US [ ], and seasonal IAV is responsible for approximately 290,000–650,000 respiratory deaths globally each year [ ]. Moreover, co-infections of these viruses are increasingly recognized. Viral co-infections with IAV or RSV in ~ 8.4% of hospitalized COVID-19 patients, with IAV and SARS-CoV-2 co-infection significantly worsening clinical outcomes [ ]. Current antiviral treatment options are inadequate for these threats. No highly effective RSV-specific therapeutic is in routine use other than aerosolized ribavirin being the only approved antiviral reserved for severe cases due to its limited benefit) [ ]. IAV antivirals likewise face limitations. Viral evolution has already rendered entire drug classes (See review by Smyk et al. [ ]), obsolete, and even the newer polymerase inhibitor baloxavir exhibits a low barrier to resistance [ ]. For SARS-CoV-2, the rapid emergence of new SARS-CoV-2 variants has nullified most monoclonal antibody treatments and could potentially erode the efficacy of small-molecule antivirals over time [ ]. These gaps underscore the urgent need for novel, broad-spectrum antiviral strategies against respiratory viruses.
Here, we evaluated an intranasal multi-virus siRNA therapeutic to address this need. Three siRNAs targeting conserved genes of SARS-CoV-2 (siCOVID), RSV (siRSV), and IAV (siIAV) were formulated together (siCocktail) in a single LNP formulation. Building on our previous observations that anti-RSV and anti-SARS-CoV-2 siRNAs packaged in LNPs can reduce upper and lower respiratory tract infections [ , ], here we want to multiplex these siRNAs together with an anti-IAV siRNA into a single LNP formulation to see whether these effects can be observed. We re-verified that our previously designed siCOVID and siRSV, both targeting highly conserved viral genomic regions against SARS-CoV-2 and RSV respectively [ , ], can dampen viral infection in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 2). Similarly, siIAV which targets a conserved IAV region in the PA gene also significantly reduced IAV infection. Importantly, this antiviral effect was maintained when virus-specific siRNAs were multiplexed together (Supplementary Fig. 2), demonstrating that each siRNA retains significant antiviral activity within the cocktail formulation and supporting the feasibility of a single combination siRNA therapy for multiple viruses.
LNPs are an ideal platform for delivering siRNAs for antiviral therapy (see review by Idres et al. [ ]), . Our lab previously formulated an anti-SARS-CoV-2 siRNA in a lung-targeting LNP (dmLNP) that is efficacious when delivered via both the intravenous [ ] and intranasal [ , ] routes. To begin exploring the potential of our multiplexed siRNA strategy, we combined siRNAs targeting SARS-CoV-2, RSV and IAV into a single LNP formulation (siCocktail). Similar to siCOVID alone (Supplementary Fig. 3), intranasal administration of siCocktail significantly reduced lung viral load (Fig. ). The siCocktail treatment also markedly reduced lung necrosis, oedema, and haemorrhage, and moderate inflammation (Supplementary Fig. 4). These outcomes mirror the efficacy of the single-virus siRNA treatment and indicate that a multiplexed siRNA LNP can achieve robust in vivo antiviral effects against SARS-CoV-2. Notably, the intranasal route was effective and well-tolerated in this model, consistent with our recent work highlighting the utility of LNPs for pulmonary siRNA delivery [ , ]. This demonstrates that a multiplexed siRNA formulation can exert antiviral efficacy as well as its single counterpart.

 

Encouraged by this finding, we proceeded to testing siCocktail in an IAV and RSV animal infection model. Though siRNAs have been tested preclinically against in IAV infection models only non-LNP delivery modalities have been employed so far [ ]. Here, we explored the potential for the use of LNPs to deliver siIAV and the multiplexed siCocktail. Though siRNA antiviral efficacy when encapsulated in dmLNPs have been shown to work well for intranasal interventions for SARS-CoV-2 targeting in C57BL/6 mice (K18-hACE2 mice) [ ], we wanted to use an LNP formulation, sLNP [ ], that we previously used in Balb/c mice [ ]. Our preliminary observation revealed that siRNAs in dmLNPs are not well-tolerated in Balb/c mice as they are in C57BL/6 mice (K18-hACE2 mice) (Data not shown). Hence, subsequent studies in Balb/c mice utilized sLNP formulated siRNAs at a lower dose of siCocktail (i.e., 30 µg instead of 60 µg) to ensure our intervention is safe in these mice. Intranasal treatment of IAV-infected mice with both siIAV and siCocktail significantly reduced lung viremia (Fig. ). This demonstrates, for the first time, effective LNP-mediated siRNA therapy in an IAV infection model, as prior IAV siRNA studies mostly utilized non-LNP delivery methods (See review by Idres et al. [ ]), .

 

We then assessed this siRNA cocktail in an RSV infection model. Previous work showed that RSV-infected mice intranasally treated with siRSV reduced lung infection [ ]. However, we found that the RSV-A2 strain was poor at maintaining lung viremia and clinical disease [ ]. Here, we infected mice with a clinical RSV strain (RS4) for our in vivo testing as it establishes a better RSV infection model (Fig. ). In RSV-infected mice, intranasal siRSV monotherapy conferred measurable protection, and the multiplexed siCocktail achieved comparable efficacy (Fig. ). Intranasal treatment with siRSV and its multiplexed counterpart (siCocktail) dampened lung viremia (Fig. ). Thus, across three distinct in vivo models (SARS-CoV-2, IAV, and RSV) the intranasal multiplexed siRNA approach consistently dampened viral replication and disease, indicating broad-spectrum efficacy of this approach. In our study, treatment schedules and inoculum varied across models to optimize efficacy based on virus kinetics, mouse strains, and LNP tolerability (Supplementary Table 2). This dosing flexibility supports clinical translation as an intranasal spray enables prophylactic or therapeutic use without intravenous infrastructure. The intranasal route offers significant advantages over existing antivirals. This route bypasses intravenous administration barriers (e.g., first-pass organs), avoids systemic toxicity, and enables self-administered deployment as a nasal spray at early symptom onset before pathogen identification. Clinically, this flexibility supports multiple use cases. For instance, prophylactic dosing may be beneficial during outbreaks or high-risk exposures. On the other hand, intensive therapeutic regimens (daily for 2–3 days) are essential for acute respiratory illnesses requiring rapid viral suppression (e.g., IAV), whereas moderate therapeutic schedules can be employed for confirmed respiratory cases in paediatric or elderly populations (e.g., RSV outbreaks). Future pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) studies in human airway models will refine dose optimization and duration of protection to maximize translational potential of this broad-spectrum platform.

 

Despite the promising broad-spectrum antiviral efficacy observed in mice, the present study has several important limitations. First, it was conducted solely in murine models, so the translatability to humans remains uncertain without validation in more advanced models (e.g., ferrets or non-human primates) to ensure relevance to human physiology. Second, we did not characterize the PK or PD properties of the siRNA-LNP cocktail, nor the durability of its antiviral effect, leaving key questions about optimal dosing and the longevity of protection unanswered. In addition, the current efficacy data were obtained against only the specific virus strains tested, with no assessment of emerging SARS-CoV-2 variants, other IAV subtypes, or additional RSV strains beyond those in this study. Although siRSV specifically targets the RSV phosphoprotein P gene and lacks sequence homology to SARS-CoV-2 or IAV genomes, its administration reduced lung pathology in SARS-CoV-2 (Supplementary Fig. 3) and IAV (Fig. D) infection models. This off-target benefit may arise from immunostimulatory activity of unmodified siRNAs [ ], which can activate innate immune sensors, eliciting IFN and pro-inflammatory cytokines that indirectly suppress tissue viremia and inflammation. Future studies profiling host cytokine responses will clarify this mechanism. Finally, we did not profile host immune responses to the treatment, meaning any mucosal or systemic immunomodulatory effects are unknown. It is also important to note that even though our siRNAs target highly conserved, functionally essential regions, single siRNA strategies risk selection pressure-driven escape through point mutations or structural alterations. RNA viruses readily evolve resistance to monotherapy siRNAs within 3–20 passages, whereas combination approaches using 2–3 siRNAs per virus significantly delay or prevent escape [ – ]. Future formulations should incorporate multi-siRNA targeting per pathogen (e.g., dual siRNAs against two viral genes [ ]) to establish high resistance barriers, validated via serial passaging assays.
Progression of this multiplexed siRNA-LNP platform toward clinical application requires several critical next steps spanning preclinical validation, manufacturing scale-up, regulatory preparation, and early-phase clinical testing. Validation in higher-order animal models (e.g., ferrets and non-human primates) is essential to bridge murine efficacy to human translatability, particularly for confirming intranasal delivery kinetics, lung biodistribution, and immunological responses in anatomically relevant respiratory systems. Comprehensive PK/PD studies should characterize siRNA tissue retention, duration of target engagement (RISC loading), and optimal dosing intervals to inform human regimens. Cross-variant testing against emerging SARS-CoV-2, IAV subtypes (H1N1, H5N1), and diverse RSV strains will establish breadth of coverage and identify needs for sequence adaptation. Establishing scalable, good manufacturing practice (GMP)-compliant manufacturing of siRNA-LNPs with stringent quality control (i.e., purity, encapsulation efficiency, endotoxin levels, particle size consistency) is also critical for clinical translation [ ]. Importantly, intranasal siRNA against RSV (ALN-RSV01) has completed Phase 2b trials with acceptable safety profiles [ ], providing regulatory precedent for this route and modality. Finally, translating LNP formulations to ‘user-friendly’ intranasal spray devices requires compatibility testing (e.g., shear stress effects on LNPs, droplet size optimization for lung deposition), stability in device reservoirs, and human factors validation for self-administration. Lyophilized or spray-dried LNP formulations may enhance shelf stability and eliminate cold-chain requirements (see recent review by Tayyar et al. [ ]), , critical for pandemic deployment.
Collectively, our findings highlight the potential translational value of a multi-target siRNA antiviral delivered intranasally in LNPs. By addressing several major respiratory viruses simultaneously, this single formulation could offer a practical therapeutic option in clinical scenarios where rapid empiric treatment is needed before diagnostic confirmation of the pathogen. For instance, a cocktail siRNA nasal spray could be administered at early onset of viral respiratory illness to cover SARS-CoV-2, IAV, and RSV. This may improve outcomes especially in co-infection cases, without needing multiple separate drugs. The intranasal LNP platform could be readily amenable to outpatient use and could overcome prior delivery barriers for RNA therapeutics in the lung. While further development is required, the present proof-of-concept establishes that a broad-spectrum siRNA antiviral is achievable. This strategy could fill an important gap in our antiviral arsenal, providing a single, easy-to-administer treatment capable of suppressing multiple respiratory viruses and thus offering a timely therapeutic benefit in the face of evolving viral threats.
