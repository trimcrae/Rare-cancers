## Abstract
Phenylketonuria (PKU) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) gene. Previously, we have shown correction of the most recurrent PKU variant using both base editing and prime editing. In this work, we set out to screen base editors and single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) in vitro to identify sgRNA-editor combinations that would provide sufficient correction for phenotypic rescue of the next four most recurrent PKU variants. For three candidates, we established efficient corrective editing in vitro. We then assessed the off-target editing profile of each sgRNA-editor combination. For two of these variants, we demonstrated efficient corrective editing in the livers of humanized mouse models via adeno-associated viral (AAV) delivery. This work identifies base editing strategies for in vivo correction of the second and third most common pathogenic variants of PKU.
Keywords: MT: RNA/DNA Editing, phenylketonuria, base editing, AAV, CRISPR, liver

## Introduction
Disease-causing variants in the phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) gene result in the build-up of phenylalanine (Phe) in the blood. This condition, phenylketonuria (PKU), is due to the inability of patients to convert Phe to tyrosine, leading to blood Phe levels in untreated patients reaching more than 1,200 μmol/L. The standard of care is a strict low-Phe diet that can substantially reduce blood Phe levels, but often not to the recommended range of less than 360 μmol/L. High Phe levels can lead to the development of neuropsychiatric issues and neurological impairment. As alternatives or in conjunction with a low-Phe diet, there are two approved therapies: pegvaliase, an enzyme substitution therapy that directly processes Phe into tyrosine, and sapropterin, a cofactor of the PAH protein that can improve the activity of the PAH enzyme for a subset of variants. , , However, both therapies have drawbacks: pegvaliase can be immunogenic and carries a substantial risk of anaphylaxis, and, as a cofactor of PAH, sapropterin has no effect in patients with complete loss of enzyme function. Notably, many of the disease-causing variants of PKU are single-nucleotide alterations and thus amenable to correction by base editing. 
Base editing is a form of CRISPR gene editing that allows for efficient and precise changes of specific base pairs without generating double-strand breaks. Avoiding double-strand breaks minimizes the risk of treatment-associated DNA translocation and, therefore, the risk of oncogenesis. Adenine base editing converts adenosine to inosine through deamination, which is subsequently converted to guanine through DNA replication or repair. 
Base editing has shown promise in treating genetic disorders and has advanced to the clinic for conditions ranging from familial hypercholesterolemia to urea cycle disorders. , , Previous work, including our own, has indicated that restoration of ∼10% PAH function in the liver could ameliorate the worst clinical manifestations of PKU. , , , , We have previously demonstrated therapeutic Phe reduction in humanized mouse models of PKU using lipid nanoparticles containing mRNA coding for an adenine base editor (ABE) and a single guide RNA (sgRNA). , The studies reported here expand on our past work to identify sgRNA/ABE combinations for additional PKU variants.

## Results

## In vitro studies
The human hepatoma cell line HuH-7 was transduced with a lentivirus carrying a DNA cassette that included the six most common disease-causing variants of PKU ( A). We then screened ABE/sgRNA combinations for the second through fifth most frequent variants (as we have already reported editing solutions for the first and sixth most frequent variants). , Each variant was tiled with sgRNAs placing the variant at positions 3 through 9 (from the 5′ end) of the protospacer sequence ( B). For the variants we screened, none of the protospacers had standard Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 (SpCas9) NGG protospacer-adjacent motifs (PAMs). Therefore, we used the SpG and SpRY versions of ABE8.20, ABE8e, and ABE8.8, which have an altered PAM preference for NGN and NNN, respectively. , , Corrective editing was defined as next-generation sequencing (NGS) reads demonstrating reversion of the disease-causing variant. In cases where additional bystander adenines were edited, these edits are noted throughout the work.
 We began our process by screening ABE/sgRNAs combinations for each variant. As positive controls in each screen, we included our previously published editing solutions for the PAH c.842C>T variant, PAH1, and the PAH c.1222C>T variant, PAH4. , The PAH1 editing solution combines an sgRNA placing the c.842C>T variant at position 5 of the sgRNA with ABE8.8. PAH4 places the c.1222C>T variant at position 5 of the sgRNA and is paired with SpRY-ABE8.8. PAH1 and PAH4 have been shown to result in therapeutic levels of corrective editing in the livers of humanized mice carrying the corresponding PAH variants. We performed our screening for the c.1066–11G>A variant, the second most recurrent PKU variant, hereafter referred to as c.1066. This variant has four bystander adenines at positions −4, 7, 9, and 12 relative to the intended edit ( C). We performed splice analysis of the most intronic bystander, −4, and found this edit did not disrupt splicing ( ). The bystander at position 12 results in a non-synonymous change; the bystander at position 9 disrupts a splice acceptor. The editing combination that gave the highest level of corrective editing, with or without the intronic bystander at position −4, was SpG-ABE8e + guide 7 ( C). We used this sgRNA/editor combination for the additional studies in this work. This sgRNA/editor combination achieved a higher level of corrective editing compared to both positive controls PAH1 and PAH4.
For the PAH c.782G>A variant, hereafter referred to as c.782, there are four bystander adenines ( D). A bystander at position 1 relative to the intended edit results in a synonymous change. Bystanders at positions 9, 14, and 16 result in non-synonymous changes. The editing combination that resulted in the highest level of correction, with or without the synonymous change at position 1, was SpG-ABE8e + guide 3. We used this sgRNA/editor combination for the additional studies in this work.
For the PAH c.728G>A variant, hereafter referred to as c.728, there are two bystander adenines ( E). A bystander at position 1 relative to the intended edit results in a synonymous change. The bystander at position 7 results in a non-synonymous change. The editing combination that resulted in the highest level of correction, with or without the synonymous change at position 1, was SpG-ABE8e + guide 6. We used this sgRNA/editor combination for the additional studies in this work.
In addition to these variants for which we successfully identified credible ABE/sgRNA candidates, we assessed the PAH c.1315 + 1G>A variant ( ). We were unsuccessful in identifying any ABE/sgRNA combinations that even approached the corrective editing of the PAH4 control. We proceeded to characterize our lead ABE/sgRNA candidates for the c.1066, c.782, and c.728 variants.

## Off-target assessment
To evaluate off-target editing for our lead ABE/sgRNA candidates, we performed ONE-seq, an off-target nomination strategy. The results of this in vitro nomination strategy are summarized in A, where in vitro editing of the fragment containing the on-target site is shown with a red dot and other fragments that were edited in vitro are shown in gray. The top 49 ONE-seq nominated sites were assessed for off-target editing by targeted amplicon sequencing. This included all sites that had normalized ONE-seq scores exceeding 0.15. For the c.1066 variant, three candidate off-target (OT) sites showed substantial editing above baseline: OT5 (5.2%), OT6 (1.7%), and OT17 (6.5%), relative to on-target editing of 28.5% ( B). OT5 is in a non-coding region, OT6 is intronic, and OT17 is the wild-type c.1066 locus. For the c.782 variant, only the wild-type locus showed substantial editing above baseline ( C). The c.728 variant showed no substantial off-target editing at any of the sequenced candidate sites ( D).
 

## In vivo validation in humanized mice
To test our lead ABE/sgRNA candidates in vivo, we used C57BL/6J mice modified with one of two strategies. To model the c.1066 variant, homology-directed repair was used in mouse embryonic stem cells to replace the endogenous portion of the mouse Pah gene, from exon 10 through exon 12, with the orthologous human sequence, including the variant as reported previously. To generate mice carrying the c.782 variant, we used CRISPR-Cas9 in mouse zygotes to introduce the variant via homology-directed repair; other variations between the human and mouse sequences within 35 bp of the variant were also installed. Homozygous exon-humanized c.1066 mice showed a PKU phenotype with profoundly elevated Phe levels in the blood ( A). In contrast, the minimally humanized c.782 mice did not exhibit elevated levels of Phe, consistent with a recently published study and reflecting differences between human and mouse physiology. We used the same strategy to attempt to generate minimally humanized c.782 variant mice. However, despite multiple attempts, no founders carrying the c.728 variant were obtained.
 We performed a short-term study in which we treated homozygous exon-humanized c.1066 mice with a dual-AAV vector system encoding SpG-ABE8e (split between the two vectors and spontaneously reassembling via intein-mediated protein splicing) and our lead sgRNA for this variant. At baseline, the mice had blood Phe levels ranging from 2,180 to 2,640 μmol/L ( A). This would be characterized as severe PKU in human patients. Non-PKU littermates had blood Phe levels <120 μmol/L, similar to human profiles. We treated three c.1066 mutant mice with 5 × 1011 viral genomes (vg) of each AAV and three mice with PBS as a vehicle control. Over the course of 6 weeks, we saw reductions in Phe levels in AAV-treated mice averaging >95% ( A). This resulted in healthy phenotypic rescue in the AAV-treated mice, with Phe levels dropping below the 360 μmol/L considered to be healthy. We continued to monitor the mice for 8 weeks after treatment, at which point the mice underwent necropsy. We observed an average of ∼45% correction without bystander editing and an additional ∼9% correction with the tolerated intronic bystander edit ( B).
We also performed a short-term study in which we treated homozygous minimally humanized c.782 mice. Three mice were treated with the dual-AAV vector system encoding SpG-ABE8e and our lead sgRNA for the c.782 variant. Eight weeks following treatment, the mice underwent necropsy, at which point we observed an average of ∼15% correction without bystander editing and an additional ∼19% correction with the synonymous bystander edit ( C).
We attempted to induce elevated blood Phe levels by putting the mice on a high protein diet; however, this did not result in elevated blood Phe levels (not shown). Therefore, we could not demonstrate phenotypic rescue with corrective editing of the c.782 variant in vivo.

## Discussion
In earlier studies using CRISPR editing therapies in the context of PKU, single variants were modeled and editing solutions were identified. Here, we have expanded on those studies to take a wider scope and identify editing solutions to multiple disease-causing variants in rapid succession. Despite using a cell line containing heterologous fragments of PAH DNA, lead ABE/sgRNA solutions identified with in vitro screening proved to be efficacious in vivo at their respective genomic loci, albeit with differences in efficacy and substantial differences in bystander activity. The higher percentage of edited cells in vivo overall could be the result of persistent expression of the AAV construct as opposed to transient plasmid transfection in vitro. As we have noted previously, “one-and-done” Phe-normalizing therapies would be a substantial improvement for PKU patients who currently require lifelong chronic dietary and medical interventions for their disease. Furthermore, this strategy for identification and characterization of lead editor/sgRNA combinations could be applied to additional inborn errors of metabolism. The level of correction achieved here in vivo, ∼55% for the c.1066 variant and ∼35% for the c.782 variant (including tolerated bystander editing), indicates that a therapy with this ABE/sgRNA combination would lead to the amelioration of the high blood Phe levels that manifest in PKU patients. , ,

## Transduction and culture of HuH-7 cells
The transgene contained the above DNA cassette along with a puromycin selection marker. This transgene was transduced into HuH-7 cells, and these cells subsequently underwent antibiotic selection. HuH-7 cells were used as they are a hepatocyte-derived immortalized cell line that we and others have previously employed as a model of hepatocytes, our target cells. , , HuH-7 cells were maintained in DMEM (containing 4 mM L-glutamine and 1 g/L glucose, Thermo #11885084) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Thermo #16000044) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo #15140122) at 37°C with 5% CO2. The cells were then selected with 2 μg/mL of puromycin (Thermo # A1113803). The exogenous DNA cassette was then verified by sequencing, and screening of editor-guide combinations could begin.

## Transfection of editors and guides
Guides were ordered as forward and reverse oligos in the following format, forward: 5′-caccG + protospacer-3′, reverse: 5′-AAAC + reverse complement of the protospacer + C-3’. Guides were annealed and then cloned into pGuide (Addgene #64711) which had been digested with BbsI-HF (NEB #R3539M). For plasmid transfections, HuH-7 cells were seeded on 24-well plates (Corning) at 8 × 104 cells per well. At 4–6 h after seeding, cells were transfected at approximately 70%–80% confluency with 1.8 μL TransIT-LT1 Transfection Reagent (Mirus #MIR2300), 400 ng editor plasmid, and 200 ng guide plasmid per well according to manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were cultured for 72 h after transfection, media was removed, and genomic DNA was extracted using a gentle lysis protocol (described below).

## Gentle lysis
A room-temperature stable solution was generated consisting of 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5, Thermo #15630080), 100 mM KCl (Thermo #J63739.AE), 5 mM MgCl2 (Sigma #63069-100ML), and 5% glycerol (Thermo # 15514011). To perform DNA extraction, we combined 97.1 μL of this solution with 0.1 μL 0.10% Triton X-100 (Sigma #X100-500ML), 0.1 μL NEB proteinase K (800 U/mL, NEB #P8107S), and 2.5 μL 0.1 M dithiothreitol (Thermo #BP172-5) per well of a 24-well plate. The well was scraped, and the lysis solution along with the cells was transferred to a strip tube. The strip tube was incubated on the thermocycler with the following program: 65°C for 6 min, 98°C for 2 min, 4°C hold.

## Generation of PAH c.1066–11G>A variant heterozygous, c.1066–15G>A heterozygous, and c.1066–15G>A homozygous HuH-7 cells for splicing analysis
The methods were similar to those previously described. For generation of PAH c.1066–11G>A variant heterozygous HuH-7 cells, HuH-7 cells in a well of a 6-well plate were transfected with 9 μL TransIT-LT1 Transfection Reagent (Mirus #MIR2300), 1,500 ng prime editor plasmid (pCMV-PEmax-P2A-hMLH1dn), 750 ng epegRNA plasmid, and 750 ng nicking gRNA plasmid. The epegRNA was expressed from pU6-tevopreq1-GG-acceptor (Addgene #174038), with the following spacer sequence (plus an additional 5′ G to facilitate U6 expression) and PBS/RTT sequence cloned into the plasmid: 5′-AGTGATAATAACTTTTCACT-3′ and 5′-TAGGCCCTAAGTGAAAAGTTATTA-3’. The nicking gRNA was 5′- TCTCTGATAAGCAGTACTGT-3’.
For generation of the c.1066–15G>A heterozygous and homozygous HuH-7 cells, cells in a well of a 6-well plate were transfected with 9 μL TransIT-LT1 Transfection Reagent (Mirus #MIR2300), 1,500 ng SpRY-ABE8e plasmid, and 1,500 ng guide plasmid (spacer sequence 5′-TCACTTGGGGCCTACAGTAC -3′). These were ordered from Synthego; accordingly Synthego’s SpCas9 sgRNA standard modifications bear 2′-O-methyl analogs (OMe) on the first and last three bases and 3′ phosphorothioate internucleotide linkages (PS) between the first three and last two bases. Cells were dissociated with trypsin (Thermo #25200056) 48 h post-transfection and replated onto 10-cm plates (5,000 cells/plate) with conditioned medium to facilitate recovery; genomic DNA was isolated from the remainder of the cells as a pool to perform PCR and Sanger sequencing of the PAH c.1066–11G>A variant or c.1066–15G>A site. Single cells were permitted to expand for 7–14 days to establish clonal populations. Colonies were manually picked and replated into individual wells of a 96-well plate. Genomic DNA was isolated from individual clones, and PCR and Sanger sequencing was performed to identify PAH c.1066–11G>A variant heterozygous, c.1066–15G>A heterozygous, and c.1066–15G>A homozygous HuH-7 clones. One representative clone was expanded for use in subsequent studies. These cell lines are readily available from the authors via a Transfer of Research Material agreement with the University of Pennsylvania.

## Next-generation sequencing
PCR reactions were performed using NEBNext Polymerase (NEB #M0541L) using the primer sets listed in , designed with Primer3 v.4.1.0 (https://primer3.ut.ee/). The following program was used for all genomic DNA PCRs: 98°C for 20 s, 35× (98°C for 20 s, 57°C for 30 s, 72°C for 10 s), 72°C for 2 min. PCR products were visualized via capillary electrophoresis (QIAxcel, QIAGEN) and then purified and normalized via an NGS Normalization 96-Well Kit (Norgen Biotek Corporation #61900). A secondary barcoding PCR was conducted to add Illumina barcodes (Nextera XT Index Kit V2 Set A and/or Nextera XT Index Kit V2 Set D) using ∼15 ng of first-round PCR product as a template, followed by purification and normalization. Final pooled libraries were quantified using a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific #Q33226) and then—after denaturation, dilution to 10 pM, and supplementation with 15% PhiX—underwent sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq System. The amplicon sequencing data were analyzed with CRISPResso2v2 and scripts to quantify editing. For on-target editing, A-to-G editing was quantified at the site of the variant and at the site of the potential bystander adenine. For candidate off-target sites, A-to-G editing was quantified throughout the editing window (positions 1 to 10 of the protospacer sequence). Any sequencing reads containing one or more adenines changed to guanine within the editing window were considered edited at that site. In some cases, PCR amplicons were subjected to confirmatory Sanger sequencing, performed by GENEWIZ.

## ONE-seq
ONE-seq was performed as previously described. The human ONE-seq libraries for the sgRNAs were designed using the GRCh38 Ensembl v.98 reference genome. Sites with up to 5 mismatches and sites with up to 3 mismatches plus up to 2 DNA or RNA bulges, compared to the protospacer sequence, were identified with Cas-Offinder. This list of sites was deduplicated, and an additional 10 bp of DNA sequence was added to either side of these fragments using Bedtools. Barcodes were added, and this entire library of fragments was synthesized by Twist Biosciences. Recombinant editor protein was produced by GenScript. Each library was PCR amplified and subjected to 1.25× AMPure XP bead (Beckman #A63881) purification. After incubation at 25°C for 10 min in CutSmart buffer (NEB #B6004), RNP comprising 769 nM recombinant editor protein and 1.54 μM sgRNA was mixed with 100 ng of the purified library and incubated at 37°C for 8 h. Proteinase K (800 U/mL, NEB #P8107S) was added to quench the reaction at 37°C for 45 min, followed by 2× AMPure XP bead (Beckman #A63881) purification. The reaction was then serially incubated with EndoV (NEB #M0305S) at 37°C for 30 min, Klenow fragment (NEB #5288) at 37°C for 30 min, and NEBNext Ultra II End Prep Enzyme Mix (NEB #E7546S) at 20°C for 30 min followed by 65°C for 30 min, with 2× AMPure XP bead (Beckman #A63881) purification after each incubation. The reaction was ligated with an annealed adapter oligonucleotide duplex at 20°C for 1 h to facilitate PCR amplification of the cleaved library products, followed by 2× AMPure XP bead (Beckman #A63881) purification. Size selection of the ligated reaction was performed on a BluePippin system (Sage Science) to isolate DNA of 150–200 bp on a 3% agarose gel cassette (Sage Science #HTG3010), followed by two rounds of PCR amplification to generate a barcoded library, which underwent paired-end sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq System as described above. The analysis pipeline identified the barcode from each read and subsequently checked if each read was edited in the first 10 bp of the protospacer sequence. Read counts of edited fragments were log10 transformed and normalized to the read count of the edited on-target site. Sites were ranked based on this ONE-seq score. Subsequently, we performed a transfection in our transduced HuH-7 cells and performed targeted amplicon sequencing on the top 49 sites nominated by the in vitro reaction. This included all sites with in vitro editing exceeding 15% of the on-target site. Note: the WT c.728 site was a dropout during this targeted amplicon sequencing.

## Generation of a minimally humanized PKU mouse model
The PKU mouse model with one or more humanized PAH c.782 alleles was generated using in vitro transcribed Cas9 mRNA, a synthetic sgRNA (spacer sequence 5′-UGCACACAGUACAUUAGGCA-3′) (Synthego, see section on splice variants for additional detail), and a synthetic single-strand DNA oligonucleotide (Integrated DNA Technologies) with homology arms (70 bp each) matching the target site and harboring the c.782 variant and synonymous variants (bold with underline): 5′-AGTTTTAGGTTCTTTTCTGCTTTCCAGCTTGTACTGGTTTCCGCCTCCGTCCTGTTGCTGGCTTACTGTCCTCTCGGGATTTCTTGGGTGGCCTGGCCTTCCAAGTCTTCCACTGCACACAGTACATCAGACATGGATCTAAGCCCATGTACACACCTGAACCGTAAGTATCATTCTTCAGCTACCCCTGCCAACCACAA -3’. The mixture of the three components (50 ng/μL Cas9, 20 ng/μL sgRNA, 10 ng/μL ssDNA) was combined in a total volume of 50 μL and 1–2 pL was injected into the cytoplasm of fertilized oocytes from C57BL/6J mice at the Penn Vet Transgenic Mouse Core (https://www.vet.upenn.edu/research/core-resources-facilities/transgenic-mouse-core). Genomic DNA samples from founders were screened for knockin of the desired sequence in the PAH locus via homology-directed repair. Founders with the humanized c.782 allele were bred through two generations to obtain homozygous mice. This humanized PKU mouse model is readily available from the authors via a Transfer of Research Material agreement with the University of Pennsylvania.

## Mouse studies
Mice were maintained on a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, with a temperature range of 65–75°F and a humidity range of 40%–60%, and were fed ad libitum with a chow diet (LabDiet, Laboratory Autoclavable Rodent Diet 5010). Homozygous PKU mice were generated as littermates/colonymates via timed breeding. Genotyping was performed using PCR amplification from genomic DNA samples (prepared from clipped tails/ears) followed by Sanger sequencing. Age-matched female and male colonymates were used for experiments at 4 or 8 weeks of age with random assignment of animals to various experimental groups when applicable and with collection and analysis of data performed in a blinded fashion when possible. AAV were produced and quantified by PackGene Biotech, on a fee-for-service basis, using digital droplet PCR for genome titration and an ELISA for capsid titration. AAVs were administered to the mice at approximately 1×1012 vg/mouse via retro-orbital injection under anesthesia with 1%–2% inhaled isoflurane. Mice were euthanized 8 weeks after treatment, and 8 liver samples (two from each lobe) were obtained on necropsy and processed with the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (QIAGEN #69504) as per the manufacturer’s instructions to isolate genomic DNA. Euthanasia in all instances was achieved via terminal inhalation of carbon dioxide followed by secondary euthanasia through cervical dislocation or decapitation, consistent with the 2020 American Veterinary Medical Association Guidelines on Euthanasia. Next-generation sequencing results from the liver samples were averaged to provide quantification of whole-liver editing. Blood samples were collected via the tail tip at various time points (pre-treatment, d0, d7, d14, d21, d28, d42, and d56).
