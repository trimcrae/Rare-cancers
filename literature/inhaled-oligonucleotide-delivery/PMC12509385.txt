## Introduction
Glioblastoma (GBM), the most aggressive primary brain tumor, represents a formidable challenge in neuro-oncology due to its rapid progression and resistance to conventional therapies [ ]. Despite advancements in cancer treatment, prognosis remains poor, with a median overall survival of 15–18 months post-diagnosis and a five-year survival rate below 7%. With an incidence of 3.2 per 100,000 individuals, GBM’s clinical and societal burden underscores the need for therapeutic innovation [ , ]. Its aggressive phenotype arises from diffuse infiltration into healthy brain parenchyma, pronounced intratumoral heterogeneity, and adaptive mechanisms that evade cytotoxic, radiotherapeutic, and targeted interventions. These features collectively define GBM as a critical unmet need in oncology [ ].
The current standard of care—maximal safe resection followed by radiotherapy and temozolomide chemotherapy—provides limited clinical benefit. While this regimen extends survival marginally, recurrence is nearly universal, driven by residual invasive tumor cells and the selection of resistant glioblastoma stem-like cell (GSC) populations. Adjuvant approaches such as tumor-treating fields (TTFields) [ ] or anti-angiogenic agents like bevacizumab improve progression-free survival in subsets of patients but fail to significantly alter overall survival trajectories [ ]. These shortcomings reflect intrinsic biological barriers: an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) [ ] that attenuates immune surveillance, a blood-brain barrier (BBB) [ ] that restricts drug penetration, and dynamic genetic and epigenetic heterogeneity [ ] that promotes therapeutic escape. Addressing these challenges requires a mechanistic understanding of GBM’s resistance pathways and novel technologies to disrupt them.
Recent preclinical advances highlight promising strategies to overcome these obstacles. Energy-based modalities, including focused ultrasound [ ] and photodynamic therapy [ ], aim to enhance drug delivery or selectively ablate tumor tissue. Nanomedicine platforms [ ] engineered for BBB traversal and tumor-specific targeting are being tested for improved pharmacokinetic profiles. Immunotherapies—such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells, immune checkpoint inhibitors, and oncolytic viruses—seek to reprogram the TME or engage antitumor immunity [ ]. Concurrently, gene therapies [ ] targeting oncogenic drivers (e.g., EGFRvIII, CDKN2A or MET mutations) or CRISPR-based editing of resistance pathways exemplify efforts to achieve precision targeting. While these approaches diverge in mechanism, they share a common goal: circumventing the biological and physical constraints that render GBM intractable to conventional treatment.
This review summarizes preclinical advancements in GBM therapy from the past seven years (2019–2025), with a focus on experimental strategies that address key limitations of current paradigms. By evaluating energy-based interventions, nanomedicine, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, cell and gene therapy, I analyze their potential to improve drug delivery, overcome resistance, and modulate the TME. I further discuss challenges in clinical translation, including tumor evolution, biomarker identification, and the need for combinatorial regimens. This analysis aims to establish future research directions and accelerate the development of therapies capable of managing GBM.

## Search strategy
I searched the Ovid MEDLINE database on January 16, 2025, for publications from the past seven years in English. Both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and keywords were utilized. The search terms included “glioblastoma” [MeSH], “brain neoplasms” [MeSH], “models, animal” [MeSH], “disease models, animal” [MeSH], “control groups” [MeSH], “brain tumor”, “mice,” “mouse,” “murine,” “rat(s),” “control,” “groups,” “therapy,” and “therapeutic.” The terms were combined using AND/OR Boolean operators. To ensure comprehensive coverage of the scientific literature, an additional search was performed in PubMed using the same terms; however, preprints identified in PubMed were excluded from analysis as they had not undergone formal peer review. The search initially returned 105 results, which were included in the study. Criteria for exclusion were (i) studies without in vivo experiments performed (i.e. 3D models of GBM), and (ii) studies utilizing GBM models in which tumor cells were implanted into the mouse flank. Orthotopic models were a requirement for inclusion. After excluding in vitro-only studies as well as studies using subcutaneous injection as a primary GBM model, 63 studies were finally included in this review (Fig.  A, B). Outcomes analyzed in each study were tumor volume difference in treatment arms versus control arms, or overall survival. Additional biological endpoints such as immune infiltration, apoptosis, BBB penetration, etc., were frequently assessed. No methods were used to assess risk of bias. All studies were tabulated in MS Excel spreadsheets prior to analysis.

 

## Brief overview of new experimental GBM therapies
Over the past seven years, 63 experimental GBM therapy regimens have been investigated in preclinical studies using mouse (49) or rat (14) models (Fig.  C). In all cases, GBM cell lines were stereotactically implanted into the brain parenchyma, followed by various treatment interventions. The majority of studies utilized syngeneic models, implanting mouse-derived GBM cell lines into immunocompetent mice. However, several studies employed human GBM cell lines, primarily U87 (14 studies) and U251 (5 studies), in immunocompromised murine models. The most commonly used mouse GBM cell lines were GL261 (20 studies), CT2A (5 studies), and SB28 (2 studies), while C6 was the predominant rat GBM cell line, appearing in 7 studies (Fig.  C). Of note, several studies used rat C6 cell line as a mouse orthotopic xenograft (Fig.  C), raising issues about biological and clinical relevance of such studies.
Preclinical studies included in this review explored a range of novel therapeutic approaches (Fig.  ), including energy-based therapies (12 studies), nanomedicine-based therapies (12 studies), immunotherapies (7 studies), targeted therapies (10 studies), gene therapies (10 studies), and cell-based therapies (9 studies). Three other experimental treatments not falling into any category listed above were also included in this study. The following sections will provide a detailed analysis of these therapeutic strategies.

 

## Energy-based therapies
Energy-based therapies (EBT) have emerged as a promising approach in the treatment of cancer, offering several distinct advantages over traditional methods. These techniques—including radiofrequency ablation [ ], thermal ablation [ ], and high-intensity focused ultrasound [ ]—allow for precise targeting of tumor tissues while sparing surrounding healthy tissue. This targeted approach can reduce side effects and improve patient outcomes. Multiple studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of EBTs in treating various cancers, and in some cases, they may match the efficacy of traditional treatments while also reducing recovery time and complications [ ]. Currently, EBTs are used to complement rather than replace standard treatments such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy. Table  summarizes preclinical research employing EBTs to treat GBM in vivo.

 

Recent preclinical trials have evaluated focused ultrasound (FUS) combined with other treatments, yielding mixed results. For example, in one study involving U87 mouse xenografts, FUS was combined with copper-dopped carbon dots sonosensitizer [ ]. Strikingly, mice treated with combination therapy demonstrated 100% survival after 20 days of experiment [ ]. Similarly, sonodynamic therapy using intravenously administered sonosensitizing agent 5-aminolevulinic acid hydrochloride (5-ALA) achieved 100% survival in early stage 9 L rat gliosarcoma, and 65% survival in late-stage tumors after 80 days, compared with 0% in controls [ ]. In a more aggressive U87 rat xenograft model, the same regimen produced only a 2.5-fold reduction in tumor volume [ ]. The same group showed that in C6 rat model, this treatment regimen led to 50% survival rate after 65 days of experiment, whereas control rats succumbed to disease by day 20 [ ].
In contrast, some studies have found little to no benefit of ultrasound in treating murine GBM tumors. In one study, Park et al. [ ] combined FUS with photodynamic treatment (PDT), using 5-ALA as a sensitizer [ ]. In a C6 rat model, while PDT alone tumor volume threefold after 12 days of treatment, ultrasound alone had no effect, and the combination did not improve outcomes beyond PDT alone [ ]. In another study [ ], low-intensity FUS combined with intravenously administered microbubbles was used to transiently disrupt the BBB and enhance the delivery of cetuximab, an anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) antibody that failed in clinical trials presumably due to poor BBB permeability [ ]. No survival benefit was observed from adding microbubbles or FUS, regardless of cetuximab treatment in the U251 mouse model [ ]. Similarly, combining microbubbles with FUS did not improve survival in mice treated by cabazitaxel-loaded poly(2-ethyl butyl cyanoacrylate) nanoparticles (NPs) in a mouse P3 xenograft model [ ]. Along similar lines, combination of ultrasound hyperthermia with a single fraction 6 Gy radiotherapy did not outperform radiotherapy alone in a U87 xenograft model [ ].
Although heat-induced tumor cell killing appears to be a viable therapeutic approach, application of laser interstitial thermal therapy as a standalone treatment did not significantly ablate tumors in rat U251 xenografts, producing only limited thermal damage to cancer cells [ ]. However, photothermal techniques can be used to activate nanomedicine-based treatments in a targeted manner. For example, Liu et al. [ ] designed neutrophil-targeting nanotheranostics that incorporate a thermal‐responsive nitric oxide (NO) donor and adenosine A2A receptor (A2AR) inhibitor for near-infrared II fluorescence imaging‐guided combination therapy of orthotopic GBM. In a mouse C6 model, this approach achieved 100% survival after 40 days of experiment [ ]. Since NO can shift tumor-associated macrophages from an immunosuppressive M2-like phenotype to antitumorigenic M1-like phenotype [ ], this study highlighted the potential of targeting the TME to treat incurable cancers like GBM. Indeed, photothermal-triggered NO release in tumor cells led to an increase in CD8 + and CD4 + lymphocytes and a decrease in tumor-promoting Foxp3 + regulatory T cells [ ]. In this novel regimen, additional therapeutic benefit was provided by the A2AR inhibitor which blocked the inhibition of adenosine pathway, further boosting the antitumor immune response [ ]. Similarly, Ali et al. [ ] developed thermo-responsive magnetic NPs functionalized with folic acid/fluorescein ligands for enhanced chemotherapy of rat C6 GBM model. NPs were magnetically targeted to the brain, where they heated up to 43 °C and elicited tumor cell death. In addition, these NPs delivered doxorubicin, folic acid and fluorescein to further promote cancer cell demise [ ]. Rats receiving this combination therapy showed a 2.4-fold increase in survival compared to controls [ ]. Notably, the authors performed a safety profile of their complex therapy regimen, demonstrating its safety and tolerability. In a similar study, magnetic hyperthermia using Fe3O4-aminosilane-coated iron oxide NPs led to a tumor volume reduction by 32.8% in a C6 rat model [ ], further supporting the clinical potential of this approach.
Another approach is tumor electric fields therapy, in which electrodes are placed on the head to continuously generate three perpendicular electric fields. Although the effects of this therapy remain largely underexplored, one study reported a 2.3-fold reduction in U251 rat xenograft volume after 17 days compared to untreated controls [ ].

## Nanomedicine-based therapies
Nanomedicine-based therapies (NMT) represent another novel approach in cancer treatment, leveraging the unique properties of nanocapsules and NPs to enhance drug delivery and therapeutic efficacy. These nanoscale particles, composed of materials such as lipids, polymers, and metals like gold and silver, are designed to target cancer cells with high precision [ ]. Nanocapsules can encapsulate drugs, protecting them from degradation and ensuring controlled release at the tumor site, while NPs can be engineered to improve the solubility and bioavailability of therapeutic agents [ ]. Recent advancements in nanomedicine have demonstrated significant potential in improving the effectiveness of conventional treatments, such as chemotherapy and radiation therapy, by enhancing drug accumulation in tumor tissues and reducing systemic toxicity [ ]. For the ease of reading and understanding I will categorize available studies (Table  ) based on the chemical composition of NPs.

 

## Lipid-based NPs
Lipids are relatively non-toxic and bioavailable organic polymers that can be easily used to engineer NPs [ ], and their efficacy to treat GBM was evaluated in several studies. Brüßeler et al. [ ] created lipid NP formulations of the toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists CpG and polyinosinic/polycytidylic (pIpC) additionally loaded with doxorubicin. The authors hypothesized that targeted delivery of TLR agonists can skew macrophage polarization toward the antitumorigenic phenotype and increase macrophage phagocytosis of cancer cells [ ]. While this NP treatment extended mice survival by a third, the major drawback of this study was intracranial injection of NPs, which does not allow to control for vehicle delivery to the brain. Another study investigated lipid NPs decorated with surface transferrin to facilitate their delivery to the brain through receptor-mediated endocytosis [ ]. After loading these NPs with miltefosine, a potential proapoptotic drug used to treat leishmaniasis [ ], the authors delivered them intranasally to treat rats bearing U87 xenografts [ ]. Importantly, this novel treatment regimen extended rat survival by 85% without major adverse effects. When combined with standard of care TMZ treatment, the survival of rats was increased by more than 2.5-fold [ ]. While these studies are promising, it should be noted that lipid NPs and other conjugates predominantly home to the liver, which may limit their use to treat GBM and other CNS tumors. Also, lipid NPs have relatively large size compared to other counterparts, which may also restrict their potential to penetrate the BBB.

## Metallic NPs
Metals can be employed to develop therapeutic NPs, offering the significant advantage of serving as drug carriers [ ] or contrast agents for medical imaging techniques like MRI and PET [ ], as well as their capability for thermal therapy, as discussed above. Additionally, metals can sensitize tumor cells to radiotherapy. For instance, Goubault et al. [ ] developed radiosensitizing iron-gold NPs for treating mouse GL261 tumors. Following whole-brain radiotherapy (2 Gy/day for 5 days), mice receiving these NPs experienced a 40% increase in survival, compared to control animals [ ]. In a different approach, Georgiou et al. [ ] synthesized gold NPs labeled with the β-particle–emitting radionuclide 177Lu to treat orthotopic U251 xenografts in mice, with over 60% of treated mice surviving beyond 150 days, underscoring the regimen’s translational potential. However, the toxicity of this approach remains a major concern that requires further investigation.
Iron oxide NPs have also been designed to co-deliver cisplatin and siRNA targeting glutathione peroxidase 4, thereby inducing both ferroptosis and apoptosis in tumor cells [ ]. This combination achieved significant survival benefits in mice bearing U87 xenografts [ ]. Nonetheless, this strategy faces challenges: cisplatin’s low BBB permeability limits its use in GBM patients, and the transient effect of siRNA, compared to the sustained knockdown achieved with shRNA, diminishes its therapeutic applicability.
An interesting study was conducted by Li et al. [ ], who engineered hollow TiO2-coated persistent luminescent nanosensitizer NPs for ultrasound-amplified chemo/immunotherapy of GBM. These NPs were first incubated with isolated mouse neutrophils, which naturally infiltrate GBM lesions, and then injected into mice bearing GL261 tumors [ ]. The neutrophils acted as carriers for NPs that released ZnGa2O4:Cr³⁺ sonosensitizers for FUS. This approach demonstrated impressive therapeutic effects both when used alone and when loading anti-PD-1 antibody or paclitaxel into these NPs. After 90 days, more than 45% of the treated mice were still alive [ ].
It should be noted that while metal-based NPs show promise in preclinical studies, they also present significant challenges, including elevated toxicity, limited biocompatibility, and difficulties in clearance from the body, which may restrict their clinical application.

## Organic NPs
In contrast to metallic NMTs, organic NPs generally exhibit good tolerability and biocompatibility. For example, Wu et al. [ ] developed a novel theranostic approach for GBM treatment using rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) 15-coated biomimetic ZIF-8 nanoparticles loaded with docetaxel in an orthotopic C6 GBM-bearing mouse model. Treatment with these significantly reduced tumor volume—by approximately 2-fold—and improved survival compared to controls [ ]. The RVG15 peptide coating facilitates rapid penetration of the BBB by binding to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors on brain endothelial cells, thereby enhancing selective accumulation within tumor tissues. Once accumulated, the pH-responsive degradation of the ZIF-8 framework enables controlled release of docetaxel, inducing potent cytotoxic effects on tumor cells [ ]. Another study tested the effectiveness of doxorubicin-loaded poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) NPs in treating the rat 101.8 GBM model [ ]. The early therapy (starting on day 2 post tumor cell injection) significantly increased survival time by 141.4% compared to untreated rats, while late therapy (starting on day 8) increased it by 51.7% [ ]. Early therapy resulted in a 95.7% tumor growth inhibition, whereas in late therapy, GBM growth was inhibited by 31.8% [ ]. However, the major limitation of this study is the lack of free doxorubicin treatment group, which could help to evaluate the therapeutic benefits of NPs themselves.
It is worth mentioning the efforts of Lugani et al. [ ], who developed cyclodextrin nanoparticles, termed CANDI, to deliver a TLR7/8 agonist (R848) and a cIAP inhibitor (LCL-161) to tumor-associated myeloid cells. In the CT2A mouse model of GBM, CANDI nanoparticles efficiently entered the tumor microenvironment, accumulated in high concentrations, and were taken up by tumor-associated myeloid cells [ ]. CANDI accumulation was 68-fold higher in GBM tissue compared to normal brain tissue. This dual delivery effectively activated myeloid cells, promoted an anti-tumor immune environment, and controlled tumor growth, both as a monotherapy and in combination with anti-PD1 therapy, with 14.7-fold reduction in tumor volume [ ]. While this microenvironment-targeting approached proved effective in vivo, its efficacy in humans is yet to be investigated.
Diphenyl diselenide (PhSe)2 is an organoselenium compound with various pharmacological properties [ ]. Ferreira et al. [ ] investigated the in vivo antitumor effects of (PhSe)2 and its nanoencapsulated form (NC(PhSe)2) in the C6 rat model of GBM. Animals were subjected to repeated intragastric administration of (PhSe)2 or NC(PhSe)2 over a 15-day period. Both conventional (PhSe)2 and NC(PhSe)2 administration resulted in a similar 8.5-fold tumor volume reduction compared to the sham treatment group, indicating null effect of nanocapsules per se [ ]. Counterintuitively, administration of empty nanocapsules resulted in a 50% reduction in tumor volume [ ]. The treatments did not induce any significant alterations in plasma markers of hepatic and renal function, nor did they elicit changes in the oxidative balance parameters in the brain, liver, or kidneys. It should be noted that poor water solubility and low bioavailability of (PhSe)2 significantly limit its clinical application.

## Protein-based NPs
Proteins are naturally occurring biopolymers, making protein-based NPs non-toxic and biodegradable [ ]. Due to high stability of albumin in serum (with a half-life of 19 days), it could be utilized as a carrier of effective drugs directly to the tumor [ ]. Kamali et al. [ ] developed albumin nanoparticles loaded with an epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitor erlotinib, since approximately 40% of GBMs overexpress this oncogene [ ]. To facilitate BBB penetration, the authors conjugated these NPs to folic acid, since its transport to the brain is greatly facilitated by receptor-mediated endocytosis [ ]. In a C6 model, this novel effective conjugate could extend the rat survival by 4.7-fold, with most rats alive by 100th day of experiment [ ].

## Silica-based NPs
The porous structure of silica-based NPs provides a large surface area and high pore volume, enabling efficient loading of therapeutic agents [ ]. In addition. the abundant silanol (-SiOH) groups on their surface enable easy functionalization with targeting ligands for site-specific drug delivery [ ]. Shin et al. [ ] developed porous silicon NPs (termed pSiNPs) modified with octadecane to improve the stability and brain penetration of temozolomide (TMZ), a standard chemotherapy drug for GBM. Using an orthotopic GBM mouse model with U87 human GBM cells, the authors demonstrated that TMZ-loaded pSiNPs significantly reduced tumor size by up to 71.4% and increased survival rates by up to 2.5-fold compared to free TMZ treatment [ ]. Importantly, the mice treated with TMZ-loaded nanoparticles did not exhibit in vivo toxicity [ ], underscoring the preclinical potential of the pSiNP-based platform for the delivery of therapeutic agents to GBM.

## Cell therapies
Cell therapies have improved cancer treatment by harnessing the power of the immune system and engineered cells to target and eliminate tumors. These therapies involve modifying or utilizing patient-derived or donor cells, such as T cells [ ], natural killer (NK) cells [ ], and dendritic cells [ ], to enhance their anti-cancer activity. Among the most significant breakthroughs is chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, which has shown remarkable success in treating hematologic malignancies like leukemia and lymphoma. Other approaches include tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy and engineered NK cell therapies, expanding traditional treatment options. With ongoing advancements in gene editing and immune modulation, cell-based therapies continue to shape the future of personalized oncology (Table  ).

 

## CAR T-cell therapies
CAR-T-cell therapy has revolutionized the treatment of certain cancers, particularly those originating from hematopoietic cells [ ]. Whereas CAR-T-cell therapy in solid tumors is limited by poor T cell trafficking to the tumor site and antigen loss [ , ], this approach has shown promising results in mouse preclinical GBM models. Schaettler et al. [ ] isolated a T-cell receptor (TCR) specific for the Imp3D81N neoantigen (mImp3) associated with the GL261 cell line. The authors used this TCR to generate the mutant Imp3-Specific TCR TransgenIC mouse in which all CD8 T cells are specific for mImp3 [ ]. Adoptive cell therapy of these mlmp3-specific T cells in GBM-bearing mice resulted in 75% survival after 75 days of the experiment, enabling long-term cure for some animals [ ]. While this study has multiple pitfalls (for example, including the additional administration of recombinant IL-2 for CAR-T cell stimulation), it highlights the applicability of CAR-T cell therapy to treat GBM in the clinic.
Multiple studies recently attempted to enhance CAR-T cell therapy with cytokine therapy to better control GBM. For example, CAR-T cells were generated to express IL-7 and/or Flt3L, both potent cytokines promoting the differentiation and expansion of multiple hematopoietic cell lineages [ ]. Overexpressing these cytokines increased the proliferation of EGFRvIII-specific CAR T cells and elevated intertumoral dendritic cell populations in the EGFRvIII-mixed CT2A model [ ]. Furthermore, overexpression of Flt3L in EGFRvIII-specific CAR T cells promoted T cell diversity in comparison with control CAR T cells, even though this increase did not reach statistical significance [ ]. In GBM-bearing mice, intracranial injection of these CAR T cells led to 65% and 50% survival after 80 days of experiment for IL-7 and IL-7 + Flt3L CAR T cell groups, respectively, while control CAR T cells showed only 10% survival [ ].
Similarly, Agliardi et al. [ ] suggested a single intratumoral dose of IL-12 fused to the Fc portion of murine IgG3 in combination with systemic CAR-T cell therapy to treat established GBM tumors. In the mouse model of EGFRvIII-overexpressing GL261 cells, the authors showed that EGFRvIII-specific murine CAR T cells alone fail to control tumor growth [ ]. However, when this cell therapy was combined with a single dose of local IL-12 injection, mice demonstrated 75% survival after 75 days of the experiment [ ]. Notably, IL-12 administration reprogrammed the endogenous T cell compartment and reshaped the myeloid compartment within the glioma TME [ ], underscoring the potency of this cytokine to boost the antitumor immune response.
To overcome limitations associated with CAR T cell therapy of GBM tumors, Gu et al. [ ] engineered CAR T cells targeting IL13Rα2 and incorporating CD28, a co-stimulatory molecule on the surface of T cells. This study elegantly showed that IL-13Rα2-targeted CAR-T cells with the CD28 transmembrane domain provided significant survival and tumor growth benefits compared to control CAR-T cells [ ]. In the xenograft mouse models, mice treated with IL-13-CD28TM-28.BB CAR-T cells showed the longest survival, reaching over 90 days, while control CAR-T cells did not achieve similar results [ ]. Specifically, in the U373-GL xenograft model, mice treated with IL-13-CD28TM-28.BB CAR-T cells had significantly reduced tumor burden and improved survival rates [ ]. Similarly, in the U251-GL xenograft model, mice treated with IL-13-CD28TM-28.BB CAR-T cells exhibited the highest survival rates and effective tumor infiltration, whereas control CAR-T cells failed to infiltrate the tumors effectively [ ]. These findings indicated that the IL-13-CD28TM-28.BB CAR-T cells had superior anti-tumor efficacy, leading to improved survival outcomes and reduced tumor growth compared to the control CAR-T cells.
Oncolytic viruses, which selectively infect and kill tumor cells while stimulating an immune response, can be engineered to deliver therapeutic genes like IL-7, a cytokine essential for T cell survival and proliferation. Huang et al. [ ] demonstrated that, individually, oAD-IL7 oncolytic viruses and B7H3-specific CAR-T cells displayed moderate cytotoxicity, but their combination significantly enhanced T cell proliferation and reduced apoptosis in vitro [ ]. In the GBM19 mouse model, the combined therapy led to prolonged survival in GBM xenografts compared to either therapy alone, with 80% of the mice treated with the combination therapy achieved long-term survival after 60 days of experiment, whereas the single therapies were ineffective [ ]. Interestingly, the combination therapy improved the proliferation and persistence of tumor-infiltrating B7H3-CAR-T cells, although it did not reverse T cell exhaustion [ ].
Finally, one very interesting study combined EGFR-vIII-specific CAR-T cell therapy with a strategy targeting tumor vasculature. Since vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) blockade led to vascular normalization and improved prognosis of patients with metastatic colorectal cancer [ ] (but not in those with GBM), and increased CAR-T trafficking in other cancers [ ], Dong et el [ ]. hypothesized that this potent TME-targeting approach may synergize with CAR-T cell therapy in EGFR-vIII-overexpressing GSC005 or CT2A mouse GBM models. The mouse survival was extended by 2.1-fold in the CT2A model and by 2.6-fold in the GSC005 model, compared to controls, and this therapy increased the infiltration of CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, and the cytotoxic granzyme B + TNF-α + CD8 + T cells in tumors of both models [ ].

## NK cell therapies
NK cells are cytotoxic to cancer cells and especially potent against tumors with low levels of HLA phenotype or complete HLA loss [ ]; therefore, transfer of engineered or naïve NK cells may be beneficial in patients where CAR-T therapy is ineffective due to antigen loss [ ]. Shevtsov et al. [ ] engineered NK cells targeting Hsp70, a protein frequently overexpressed on the plasma membrane of GBM cells [ ]. After stimulation with Hsp70 peptide ex vivo, NK cells displayed elevated levels of the functional markers CD94, CD69, and CD56, whereas their cytotoxicity against cultured GL261 cells significantly increased compared to unstimulated NK cells [ ]. When these NK cells were intravenously injected to treat established GL261 mouse tumors, the authors observed a 2.5-fold improvement of survival, and combination of this cell therapy with checkpoint inhibitor a-PD-1 resulted in even greater survival benefit [ ].
An interesting idea was proposed by Chen et al. [ ], who treated GBM with a combination of NK cell therapy and hormonal therapy using progesterone and abiraterone. The authors found a dramatic reduction (> 80%) in U87 brain xenograft luciferase signal in a combination therapy group, suggesting that hormonal modulation may significantly boost the NK cell therapy in mice [ ].
Tran et al. [ ] investigated the potential enhancement of antitumor effects by combining NK cell therapy with vasculature-normalizing drug bevacizumab and chemotherapy irinotecan in the U87 mouse model. The authors expanded NK cells, which were then activated by K562 feeder cells expressing OX40 ligand and membrane-bound IL-18 and IL-21 [ ]. In mice, the combination of NK cells with pharmacotherapy significantly reduced tumor volume and delayed tumor growth in intracranial GBM U87 xenografts [ ]. However, only the direct intratumoral injection of NK cells combined with pharmacotherapy significantly improved survival (by 2.25-fold) in the intracranial GBM model [ ].

## Gene therapies
Gene therapy is a rapidly evolving approach to cancer treatment, leveraging genetic modification to enhance therapeutic efficacy, modulate the TME, or sensitize cancer cells to existing therapies [ , ]. Current strategies include (i) direct genetic reprogramming of tumor cells to induce apoptosis [ ], (ii) immune-enhancing gene delivery to stimulate antitumor responses [ ], and (iii) gene-editing technologies aimed at correcting oncogenic mutations or disrupting tumor-promoting pathways [ ]. Preclinical and early clinical studies have demonstrated promising outcomes, with vector-based gene transfer, CRISPR-mediated modifications, and suicide gene therapy emerging as viable strategies. Despite substantial challenges—including efficient gene delivery, intratumoral heterogeneity, and potential off-target effects—ongoing advancements in viral and non-viral vector systems, as well as tumor-targeted delivery platforms, continue to refine the therapeutic potential of gene-based interventions for cancer (Table  ).

 

## Gene-directed radiotherapy
Viral delivery of genes and vectors to the brain is restricted by the BBB, yet several preclinical models suggest feasibility of this approach. Gene therapy based on sodium iodide symporter (NIS) appears promising because it enables radioiodide application for effective tumor imaging and treatment [ ]. NIS is a protein responsible for the uptake of iodide, and gene therapy methods can be used to overexpress NIS in tumor cells, enabling them to concentrate radioactive iodine and thus be selectively killed [ ]. Kitzberger et al. [ ] established a mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-mediated NIS gene transfer in the GL261 mouse model of GBM. After the injection of stem cells, NIS-expressing MSCs actively populated tumor lesions in the brain and subsequent 131I therapy yielded a significant extension of mouse survival by 50% in treated animals compared to the control group or MSCs without the NIS transgene [ ]. In a similar study, Shi et al. [ ] engineered bone marrow-derived MSCs (BMSCs) to co-express NIS and the angiogenesis inhibitor kringle 5 (K5) for treating mouse U87 xenografts. Mice treated with BMSCs, followed by administration of the radioisotope 188Re, exhibited significant tumor growth inhibition, reduced tumor angiogenesis, and 83% increased survival compared to control groups [ ]. Importantly, the authors demonstrated that 188Re could selectively eliminate the therapeutic BMSCs post-treatment, as monitored by 125I micro-CT imaging [ ].

## Suicide gene therapy
Overexpression of a gene capable of cancer cell killing is a simple and feasible strategy. Two studies explored the possibility of delivering herpes simplex virus type 1-thymidine kinase (HSV-1 TK) into GBM cells [ , ]. When expressed by tumor cells, HSV-1 TK can phosphorylate an exogenously administered prodrug ganciclovir to convert it into a cytotoxic compound, leading to cell death [ ]. Both studies employed the mouse U87 xenograft to successfully deliver HSV-1 TK into GBM lesions via MSCs or retroviral semi-replicating vector, achieving impressive survival results. In the study by Villatoro et al. [ ] (MSC-aided delivery) 65% mice survived after 90 days of the experiment, whereas Kang et al. [ ] (retroviral delivery) reported a striking 100% survival after > 150 days of the experiment. While these preclinical findings are promising, a phase I-II clinical trial of HSV-1 TK therapy in patients with recurrent GBM showed only marginal clinical benefit [ ], questioning the applicability of this approach.
Another study established an adeno-associated virus and phage (AAVP) system to deliver cytotoxic tumor necrosis factor (TNF) gene into mouse U87 xenografts [ ]. Intravenous injection of the virus led to a reduced tumor burden (4 to 7-fold for different AAVP concentrations), with gene therapy-treated tumors displaying a reduction in tumor vasculature and an increase in apoptosis, compared to untreated controls [ ].

## Immunomodulatory gene therapy
Several groups aimed to boost the immunogenicity of GBM in mice. Birocchi et al. [ ] engineered hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) producing IFN-α or IL-12 via an inducible system. In the GB2 mouse GBM model, the release of IFN-α in the TME resulted in a strong antitumor response, with 35% mice surviving for more than 150 days of the experiment, whereas all control mice succumbed to disease by day 70 [ ]. More importantly, the authors further improved their system with an inducible molecular switch (dihydrofolate reductase), allowing conditional release of IFN-α as needed [ ]. Similarly, Montoya et al. [ ] used the SB28 mouse GBM cell line to assess the therapeutic efficacy of retroviral replicating vector (RRV)-mediated delivery of interferon regulatory factor 8 (IRF8). In vivo, RRV-IRF8 treatment significantly inhibited tumor growth—as evidenced by lower bioluminescence imaging signals—and substantially extended overall survival; for example, median survival increased from approximately 17 days in control groups (including untransduced SB28 and RRV-EMPTY treated tumors) to 27–28 days in RRV-IRF8 treated groups [ ]. These effects were accompanied by a reprogramming of the tumor immune microenvironment, with enhanced infiltration of cytotoxic CD8 + T-cells and dendritic cells, and a reduction in immunosuppressive markers on myeloid cells [ ]. Finally, intertumoral injection of an oncolytic virus expressing a full-length αCD47-IgG1 antibody (blocking the “don’t eat me” signal in cancer cells [ ]) resulted in complete tumor eradication in 80% of mice bearing GBM43 orthotopic GBM lesions [ ].

## TME targeting
GBM stem cells are an aggressive tumor subpopulation with the ability to self-renew, differentiate into various cell types, resist therapy, and initiate tumor recurrence [ ]. To tackle this subpopulation, Kiyokawa et al. [ ] employed the murine GBM stem cell line 005 implanted orthotopically in C57BL/6 mice. In order to target GBM stem cells, the authors proposed to break down the hyaluronan, a non-protein component of the extracellular matrix previously associated with cancer progression [ ], including GBM [ ]. Treatment with hyaluronidase-expressing oncolytic adenovirus ICOVIR17 significantly prolonged mouse survival compared with controls (median survival increased from approximately 22.5 days to 28 days) and altered the TME by reducing hyaluronan levels, increasing CD8⁺ T-cell and macrophage infiltration, and upregulating PD-L1 expression [ ]. Furthermore, when ICOVIR17 was combined with anti-PD-1 therapy, survival was further extended (median survival nearly doubled to 43.5 days), with some animals achieving long-term remission [ ], underscoring the therapeutic benefit of extracellular matrix targeting.
Another gene therapy strategy involves targeting GBM angiogenesis. Chicken ovalbumin upstream promoter transcription factor 2 (COUP-TFII) is a major transcriptional regulator of angio- and lymphangiogenesis [ ], and genetic knockdown of this important gene in tumor cells was hypothesized [ ] to inhibit sprouting vasculature in growing GBM lesions. Wang et al. [ ] synthesized hyperbranched polyethylenimine NPs loaded with shRNA stably silencing the COUP-TFII gene. Administration of these NPs resulted in a 2-fold reduction of tumor area at the study endpoint compared to control mice [ ]. This antitumor effect was attributed to a reduction in TIE2 + and CD31 + tumor vessels and an increase in infiltrating cytotoxic lymphocytes [ ]. However, a major limitation of this approach is the intracranial infusion of NPs, which does not consider biodistribution, homing, and BBB penetration of shRNA nanocarriers.

## Immunotherapies (excluding gene and cell therapies)
Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) and therapeutic cancer vaccines have emerged as effective strategies to enhance antitumor immunity, including difficult-to-treat cancers like melanoma [ ]. Despite the highly immunosuppressive TME [ ], ICB therapies targeting PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 pathways aim to restore T cell activity, while vaccine-based approaches seek to prime the immune system against tumor-specific antigens. Recent studies have explored personalized neoantigen vaccines [ ], dendritic cell-based platforms [ ], and combination strategies integrating ICB with immune-stimulatory agents to enhance therapeutic efficacy [ ]. Although clinical translation remains challenging [ ], ongoing efforts to optimize patient selection, combinatorial regimens, and biomarker-driven approaches continue to refine the potential of ICB and cancer vaccines for GBM treatment. In recent years, new experimental therapies for GBM included ICB enhancement or vaccine development (Table  ).

 

## Immune checkpoint blockade
One innovative strategy proposed sustained, localized delivery of ICB by implanting biodegradable polymer hydrogels loaded with anti-PD-1 into the inguinal lymph nodes of mice [ ]. In the GL261 murine model, 50% of mice treated with these hydrogels survived after 45 days of the experiment, in contrast to the control group, which received intraperitoneal injections of anti-PD-1 and did not survive beyond day 24 [ ]. Interestingly, intracranial injection of the anti-PD-1-loaded hydrogels did not improve survival, likely due to the reduced dose necessitated by limited intracranial space [ ].
Metformin is an anti-diabetic medication with potent antitumor properties due to systemic reduction of insulin levels and the induction of energetic stress [ ]. Li et al. [ ] combined metformin with anti-PD-1 therapy to treat mouse GL261 brain tumors, showing significantly increased tumor-infiltrating CD4 + T cells and decreased regulatory Tregs, compared to anti-PD-1 alone [ ]. This immunological reprogramming was achieved due to metformin’s ability to reduce histone acetylation at the C-C chemokine receptor 8 (CCR8) promotor and inhibit CCR8 expression by upregulating AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)-activated sirtuin 2 (SIRT2). In mice, combination therapy extended survival in mice by 1.7-fold compared to standalone therapies [ ], suggesting that metformin could be clinically repositioned to treat GBM patients.
Antihypertensive angiotensin receptor blocker losartan was tested to mitigate ICB-induced cerebral edema, a side effect that often restricts the use of ICB in GBM patients [ ]. It was shown that ICB induces cerebral edema by disrupting the blood-tumor barrier through an inflammatory response [ ]. Losartan prevented edema by reducing the expression of specific matrix metalloproteinases (MT-MMP-1 and − 2) in tumor endothelial cells. In both GL261 and 005 mouse models of GBM, this reprogramming of the TME enhanced anti-tumor immunity and increased survival rates, with 20% of mice cured by losartan alone and 40% when combined with standard care [ ]. Additionally, losartan-treated tumors showed improved perfusion, reduced tumor volume, and decreased hypoxia and immunosuppression [ ]. Thus, losartan could theoretically be used in combination with ICB to enhance its potency against GBM; however, further research is needed in this area.
BTLA (B- and T-lymphocyte attenuator) is a co-inhibitory checkpoint structurally related to PD-1 and is highly expressed on tumor-specific T cells in several cancers, including GBM [ ]. BTLA’s ligand, Herpes virus entry mediator, is overexpressed in high-grade gliomas and is associated with the suppression of T cell-mediated anti-tumor activity in the GBM TME [ ]. Choi et al. [ ] explored the efficacy of combining anti-PD-1 and anti-BTLA antibodies in treating the mouse GL261 GBM model. This combination therapy significantly improved long-term survival rates in mice (75%) compared to monotherapy with either antibody alone (20% for anti-PD-1 and 0% for anti-BTLA) [ ]. Furthermore, the combinatorial therapy enhanced the activation of CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, evidenced by increased IFN-γ expression, and reduced the presence of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells in the TME [ ].
Lastly, whereas intraperitoneal delivery of recombinant IFN-γ in combination with temozolomide has led to a reduction in murine GBM tumor volume [ ], multiple clinical trials using IFN-γ for cancer treatment have failed due to high toxicity and limited efficacy [ , ], casting doubt on its utility as an immunotherapeutic agent.

## Cancer vaccines
Given that many GBM tumors exhibit EGFR mutations [ ], EGFRvIII-specific vaccine has been evaluated in combination with flagellin B (FlaB), a bacterial protein derived from Vibrio vulnificus that functions as a potent immune adjuvant [ ] by binding to Toll-like receptor 5 (TLR5) on various immune cells, thereby activating them. In mice bearing EGFRvIII-expressing GL261 GBM tumors, Choi et al. [ ] evaluated the effects of intranasal administration of either PEGFRvIII alone, FlaB alone, or their combination. The combination treatment led to a significant reduction in tumor growth, with the tumor growth ratio dropping from 9.58 in the control group to 4.13 in the PEGFRvIII plus FlaB group—a reduction of 65% compared to controls [ ]. Furthermore, the survival time increased notably from a mean of 34.4 days in controls to 46 days in the combination group [ ]. Mice treated with combination therapy exhibited a marked increase in CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cell infiltration and a reduction in immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs) within the TME [ ], suggesting that FlaB acts as a potent adjuvant to boost the anti-tumor immune response elicited by the peptide vaccine.
Long multi-epitope peptides from two MHC-I peptides (BIRC597 − 104 and EphA2682–689) were tested to induce antitumor immune response in a mouse GL261 model of GBM [ ]. While the vaccine treatment was not effective per se, its combination with anti-PD-1 antibody treatment and immunotherapy drug lenalidomide resulted in more than 2.2-fold reduction in GBM volume, with 40% of mice surviving after 60 days of the experiment [ ]. Notably, the lenalidomide and anti-PD-1 regimen without the vaccine did not effectively control tumor burden [ ].

## Targeted therapies
Targeted therapy has fundamentally shifted cancer treatment by focusing on the molecular drivers of tumor growth rather than relying on non-specific cytotoxic approaches [ ]. In many cancers, including lung, breast, and colorectal, the introduction of agents like tyrosine kinase inhibitors and monoclonal antibodies has led to improved survival outcomes and better quality of life. In GBM, recent preclinical studies suggest that targeted therapies may extend survival by disrupting key oncogenic pathways. For example, data indicate that even modest improvements in pathway inhibition can lead to statistically significant benefits, with some experimental models showing survival gains of 20–30% compared to controls [ , ]. This section reviews the latest preclinical advancements in targeted therapy for GBM (Table  ).

 

## Cell cycle targeting
Classical targeted therapy aims at cell cycle vulnerabilities to stop oncogenic signaling. Suppressing the cell cycle progression and DNA damage repair via small molecule inhibitors appeared as a potent therapeutic strategy against GBM. Sequential inhibition of PARP (Rucaparib) and BET (Birabresib) in U87 GBM xenografts showed promising results, with mice demonstrating an 8-fold reduction in the bioluminescence tumor signal at the study endpoint for sequential combination therapy, compared to monotherapies [ ]. Of note, this regimen showed mild toxicity in mice [ ], indicating its applicability in human disease.

## Metabolic targeting
GBMs are notable for high expression of the tryptophan catabolizing enzymes indole-2,3-dioxygenase-1 (IDO-1) and tryptophan-2,3-dioxygenase-2 (TDO2), and high expression of these enzymes correlates with poor prognosis [ ]. Enhanced expression of IDO1 and TDO2 leads to greater catabolism of tryptophan, increasing levels of the tryptophan metabolite kynurenine, and enhancing activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) [ ]. Tryptophan catabolism by IDO1 and TDO2 is critical within the TME, as lowered tryptophan and elevated kynurenine each confers an anti-immune response, by attenuating CD8 + T cell infiltration and promoting Treg differentiation [ ]. Bickerdike et al. [ ] developed a novel dual inhibitor of IDO1 and TDO2, AT-0174 and tested its efficacy to treat GBM in combination with TMZ. Whereas AT-0174 treatment alone did not impact tumor growth or survival, the combination of AT-0174 and TMZ synergistically enhanced the anti-tumor effect compared to chemotherapy alone [ ]. Combination treatment resulted in zero mortality until day 44, and 74% mice survived to the end of the study (day 54). Additionally, the combination increased CD8 + T cell expression and decreased Treg infiltration.
To explore novel therapeutic strategies for GBM, Ali et al. [ ] investigated the effects of targeting various microenvironmental and metabolic components in the syngeneic GL261 mouse model, both as monotherapies and in combination. Among eight treatment regimens tested, the 20-hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acid synthesis inhibitor HET0016 demonstrated the greatest efficacy, extending survival by 32% as a single agent [ ]. Interestingly, combining HET0016 with TMZ yielded only a modest additional benefit, with a survival increase of approximately 35%, indicating a lack of strong synergy. Compared to controls, HET0016-treated mice showed a significant rise in tumor-infiltrating CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, but no notable changes in the populations of CD11b+, Ly6G+, or Ly6C + cells [ ]. These findings suggest that inhibition of hydroxylase pathways in arachidonic acid metabolism may promote immune reprogramming in GBM and offer improved tumor control.

## TME targeting
In solid tumors like GBM, endothelial cells forming aberrant sprouting vasculature represent a major TME hallmark. Vascular normalization has shown potential in the treatment of certain solid tumors, suggesting that anti-angiogenic therapies could offer a promising approach to improving prognosis in GBM. In the G9 intracranial xenograft mouse model, anti-VEGF treatment with the B20 antibody—a murine analog of bevacizumab—extended median survival from 10 to 24 days [ ]. Notably, B20 treatment significantly reduced intratumoral hemorrhage and tissue viscoelasticity (as shown by MRI), likely due to modifications in the tumor vasculature [ ]. However, as a pilot study, these findings require further validation in models that more closely mimic clinical conditions. Additional histopathological analysis, including CD31 and TIE2 staining, is needed to better characterize vascular changes induced by this therapy.
Another prominent TME component in GBM is a fibrotic niche, which often develops following multimodal treatments such as surgery and radiotherapy [ ]. The interaction between GBM cells and the surrounding dense extracellular matrix activates several signaling pathways that support tumor cell survival and contribute to recurrence. It was shown that inhibition of treatment-associated fibrosis extends survival in mice treated with CSF-1R inhibitor BLZ945. In the mouse model of GL261 tumors, oral administration of BLZ945 led to a 55% survival after 120 days of experiment, whereas vehicle treated mice died by day 10 after tumor cell implantation [ ]. Furthermore, combining BLZ945 with other TME-influencing drugs inhibiting TGF-β signaling (galunisertib) and generalized inflammation (dexamethasone) led to almost 100% survival of mice after 3 months of experiment [ ]. Mechanistically, BLZ945 treatment appears to reprogram tumor-associated macrophages (TAM), inducing phenotypic changes in perivascular fibroblast-like cells that are primarily responsible for extracellular matrix deposition in GBM [ ]. This important study suggests that targeting innate immune cells may remodel the fibrotic tumor microenvironment and reduce the likelihood of GBM recurrence following surgical resection.
A novel therapeutic strategy has recently been proposed that targets microtube-associated gap junctions between adjacent GBM cells to limit tumor growth, invasion, and resistance to chemotherapy. De Meulenaere et al. [ ] investigated the efficacy of tonabersat, a connexin modulator, in the F98 glioblastoma rat model. Animals were randomized into four groups: control (saline and DMSO), tonabersat alone, standard treatment (radiotherapy and temozolomide), and standard treatment combined with tonabersat. Tonabersat as a monotherapy did not significantly slow tumor growth relative to controls [ ]. While the standard treatment initially delayed tumor progression, recurrence was observed. In contrast, the combination of tonabersat with standard treatment achieved the greatest tumor suppression, with a twofold reduction in tumor volume compared to the control group [ ]. These findings clearly indicate that tonabersat may enhance the efficacy of conventional GBM therapies and holds promise as an adjuvant strategy to improve treatment outcomes.

## Targeting resistant GBM stem cells
Since GSCs represent the most aggressive tumor cell population in GBM lesions [ ], targeting these resilient cells may significantly improve patient outcomes. It was recently discovered that peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) is exclusively expressed in mesenchymal (MES) GSCs, and ligand activation of endogenous PPARγ suppressed cell growth and stemness in MES GSCs. In vivo studies using MES 83 cells in an orthotopic xenograft mouse model revealed that PPARγ activation by an anti-diabetic medication pioglitazone increased mouse survival by approximately 30% compared to the vehicle control group [ ]. Mechanistically, PPARγ activation suppressed proneural-mesenchymal transition (PMT) by inhibiting the STAT3 signaling pathway, which resulted in cell cycle arrest and cancer cell death [ ]. Importantly, large-scale bioinformatic analysis further revealed an association between PPARγ upregulation and poor overall and disease-free survival of GBM patients and found that PPARγ expression was distinctly upregulated in MES GBM [ ]. Therefore, repositioning of clinically available pioglitazone to treat patients with GBM may be a key to combating this disease.

## Hormonal targeting
Whereas androgen receptors (AR) are traditionally a therapeutic target for prostate cancer, AR is expressed in a significant percentage (approximately 40%) of GBM cases. Zalcman et al. [ ] investigated the efficacy of AR antagonists in treating GBM in mice. Administration of enzalutamide significantly extended the lifespan of mice (by 40%) bearing intracranial U87 xenografts compared to controls, demonstrating a dose-response relationship. A newly reformulated version of bicalutamide, Bic-sol, showed improved pharmacokinetics and significantly extended the survival of mice with intracranial GBM in two different mouse models [ ]. In the U87 model, Bic-sol, both as monotherapy and in combination with TMZ, demonstrated significant efficacy compared to the vehicle-treated group and TMZ monotherapy [ ]. In the mouse model of glioma-initiating cells ZH-161, Bic-sol monotherapy also significantly prolonged the lifespan of mice compared to TMZ or the vehicle [ ]. These preliminary findings suggest that AR antagonists could be useful in treating individuals with AR + GBM.

## GBM immune microenvironment targeting
While ICB therapy regimens have demonstrated limited efficacy in treating GBM lesions clinically, targeting other components of the immune microenvironment could bear fruit. Urbantat et al. [ ] investigated the role of the CXCL2/CXCR2 signaling pathway in GBM and the efficacy of a combined therapy using TMZ and a CXCR2 antagonist SB225002 (SB). The authors observed a significantly decreased infiltration of tumor-associated microglia/macrophages (TAM) in recurrent tumors, while high TAM infiltration in primary tumors was associated with reduced overall survival [ ]. Notably, CXCL2 expression was significantly reduced in recurrent GBMs compared to primary tumors, whereas IL8 expression was significantly increased in recurrent tumors [ ]. In a GBM mouse model with GL261 tumor cells, the combined treatment of TMZ and SB was well tolerated and resulted in a 75% reduction in tumor volume compared to the control group [ ], with TMZ alone achieving a 61% reduction, indicating that in this setting the combination treatment did not outperform TMZ alone.
An interesting link between endocannabinoid receptors and an antitumor immune response was explored by Duan et al. [ ], who showed that many GBM tissues express higher levels of the endocannabinoid receptor 2 (CB2R) as well as elevated endocannabinoid production. Administration of CB2R antagonist AM630 to mice bearing GL261 GBM tumors resulted in a 30-fold reduction of the bioluminescence tumor signal at 21 days post tumor implantation [ ]. However, experimental data from this study is still very preliminary and more research is needed to clarify the role of CB2R inhibition in GBM management.

## Other therapies
Herbal medicine has been explored as a potential approach to improving GBM management. Shikonin, a bioactive compound derived from the root of Lithospermum erythrorhizon, is a key component of traditional Chinese medicine with documented anti-tumor properties [ ]. In a preclinical study, purified shikonin was intracranially infused at concentrations of 60, 75, and 90 mM into SB28 and CT2A mouse GBM models, resulting in a ~ 50% increase in survival [ ]. However, combining shikonin with TMZ did not produce a synergistic effect in either model, though shikonin alone was as effective as chemotherapy [ ].
Similarly, a methanol extract of Hyssopus officinalis (hyssop) was intraperitoneally injected into rats with C6 GBM tumors, leading to a modest 36% improvement in survival [ ]. This increase was accompanied by a higher frequency of TUNEL-positive apoptotic tumor cells compared to controls.
In another study, Larrouquère et al. [ ] investigated the effects of sodium selenite (Na2SeO3), a toxic inorganic salt, on GBM progression in a mouse model using stem-like R2J-GS cells. Orally administered Na2SeO3 reduced tumor volume twofold, outperforming TMZ therapy [ ].

## Conclusions
After detailed analysis of new experimental therapies for GBM, several conclusions can be made:

Publications reporting new experimental therapies for GBM are on the rise, with 15 articles published in 2024, versus only 7 in 2019 (Fig.  B), indicating a growing attention to this unmet clinical need.
Among all the treatment modalities tested in the last seven years, EBTs showed the highest number of studies displaying negative results (4 out of 12), whereas those reporting positive results mostly show modest effects of this therapy. Clearly, while EBTs proved effective against certain cancer types [ ], its potential to control GBM does not look promising. Furthermore, the application of EBT to the brain faces major limitations such as collateral damage to surrounding healthy tissue and limited penetration depth. It is possible that the efforts focused on developing new EBTs to target GBM may benefit from exploring other therapeutic approaches.
Surprisingly, experimental immunotherapy was among the least studied approaches, with only 7 studies conducted in the last seven years. More importantly, most of these studies (5 out of 7) focused on improving the response to anti-PD-1 therapy, highlighting the lack of innovative approaches in the field. Given the success of ICB to effectively treat multiple cancer types, it is crucial to develop new alternative strategies to boost the antitumor immune response in patients with GBM. That said, several studies categorized under “cell therapy” or “gene therapy” involved some sort of immune modulation, offering some optimism in this regard.
Almost half of the reviewed studies (26 out of 63) employed xenogeneic models of GBM, implanting human GBM cells into mice or rats, or injecting rat GBM cells into mice (Fig.  D). Xenografts typically grow slower and take longer time to reach the endpoint, compared to syngeneic models, and consequently, provide a wider window for treatments or other interventions. Furthermore, they enable studying a particular molecular subtype of GBM. However, severely immunodeficient animals required for such studies do not fully recapitulate the interactions between cancer cells and host cells, especially considering an important role of adaptive immunity in antitumor defense.
A significant limitation across the reviewed studies is the absence of clinically relevant models such as humanized mice [ ] or patient-derived xenografts (PDX), which offer a more accurate representation of human disease compared to cancer cell lines. Although cost and technical difficulties in PDX implantation have hindered their use, overcoming these obstacles with emerging technologies is crucial for advancing preclinical GBM research.
Despite the availability of spontaneous mouse models of GBM [ ], none of the reviewed studies utilized them. Although orthotopic injection models partially mimic disease progression, they fail to capture the early pathophysiological events and their effects on the TME. Going forward, innovative therapies that target both immune and stromal components should be tested in more clinically relevant models to better predict their translational potential.

It should be noted that study models described in this review have multiple inherent limitations. Orthotropic injection of tumor cells into the brain parenchyma is accompanied by tissue damage and associated local inflammatory response, both of which create a non-physiological milieu affecting cancer cell proliferation, migration, and survival [ ]. Furthermore, this tumor implantation model bypasses early stages of cancer initiation and the microenvironmental changes associated with the growth and progression of microscopic lesions [ ]. Therefore, all the experimental studies discussed in this article should be interpreted with caution, as even those demonstrating outstanding therapeutic results in vivo could lead to null results clinically.

## Future directions
Despite major advances in understanding GBM biology, clinical outcomes for patients remain poor, in part due to limitations in both preclinical modeling (Fig.  ) and clinical trial design. While murine models have been indispensable in therapy development, their clinical translatability is restricted by their inability to fully replicate the complexity of the human GBM–immune interface (as well as other components of the human TME). This is particularly relevant as the field shifts toward immunotherapy and combination regimens. Recent innovations such as humanized mouse models—immunodeficient mice engrafted with a human immune system—allow for the implantation of PDXs [ ], thereby offering a more faithful representation of tumor–immune interactions. Though resource-intensive, these models are poised to play a critical role in preclinical testing, especially for immune-modulating therapies. At the same time, alternative platforms including cerebral organoids [ ], organotypic slice cultures [ ], and computational models [ ] are emerging as faster and more scalable systems for drug screening, though they still lack the full immunological and structural complexity of in vivo models.

 

Translational efforts must also address inefficiencies in clinical trial design. Traditional trials are often slow, underpowered, and lack molecular stratification, contributing to the repeated failure of targeted therapies in late-stage development. Innovative frameworks such as AGILE [ ] and INSIGhT [ ], which utilize adaptive platform designs to dynamically expand or terminate treatment arms based on interim efficacy data, may accelerate drug development. Parallel efforts like SPECTA [ ] and real-time molecular profiling-based trial assignments aim to better match patients with candidate therapies, although logistical hurdles remain. The integration of biomarkers, expanded use of phase 0 “window-of-opportunity” studies, and increased enrollment of GBM patients into early-phase trials are all essential steps toward improving translational efficiency. Ultimately, the convergence of more predictive preclinical models and smarter clinical trials holds promise for finally translating scientific insights into effective treatments for GBM.
