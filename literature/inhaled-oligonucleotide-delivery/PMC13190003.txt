## Abstract

## Background
Previous research indicated that asthma patients were with abnormal glutathione (GSH) metabolism. However, the role of glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic (GCLC), as the key enzyme for GSH synthesis, remains ambiguous in asthma pathogenesis. Our study aimed to elucidate the role of GSH metabolism and GCLC in asthma.

## Methods
We collected serum from healthy controls, asthma patients, and asthma bagg albino/c (BALB/c) mouse induced by house dust mite (HDM) to analyze GSH/oxidized GSH (GSSG) ratio. In vivo, we administered L-Buthionine-(S,R)-sulfoximine (BSO) or combined with ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) prior to asthmatic challenge to explore the role of GCLC in asthma. In vitro, MLE-12 and Beas-2B cells were stimulated with BSO alone or combination with Fer-1 or GSH to analyze ferroptosis-related indicators and oxygen species (ROS) production. The association between GCLC and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) was assessed by using reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and western blot.

## Results
We found that GSH/GSSG ratio decreased in asthma patients and mice. Additionally, inhibition of GCLC aggregated airway inflammation and remodeling in asthma by mediating GSH metabolism. Furthermore, fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO) regulated N6-methyladenosine (m6A) demethylation of GCLC. We further found that GCLC inhibited epithelial cells ferroptosis and oxidative stress, decreasing the production of T helper 2 cells (Th2) inflammation cytokines. In addition, GCLC regulated HMGB1 expression, ultimately inhibiting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT).

## Conclusions
In asthma, GSH metabolism is abnormal and GCLC plays a protective role. GCLC inhibits ferroptosis in airway epithelium and HMGB1 release, ultimately alleviating airway inflammation and airway remodeling. Our studies reveal that GCLC related GSH metabolism is as a new mechanism of asthma, providing a potential therapeutic target.

Keywords: Asthma, glutathione (GSH), glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic (GCLC), high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), ferroptosis

## Key findings
• Our work found that glutathione (GSH) metabolism exhibited abnormalities, and glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic (GCLC) played a protective role in asthma. Mechanistically, GCLC inhibited ferroptosis in epithelial cells, and alleviated oxidative stress. Additionally, inhibition of GCLC activated high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) release, ultimately promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT).

## What is known and what is new?
• Asthma is a chronic airway inflammatory disease caused by heterogeneous factors. However, the underlying mechanism of airway remodeling remains unclear. Prior research indicated that asthma is associated with abnormal GSH metabolism. The role of the key synthesis enzyme, GCLC, in asthma pathogenesis had not been elucidated.
• This study newly identifies that GCLC plays a protective role in asthma, by regulating GSH metabolism and inhibiting airway epithelial ferroptosis. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the association between GCLC and HMGB1 release, then affecting EMT.

## What is the implication, and what should change now?
• This study indicated that GCLC-mediated GSH metabolism and the subsequent activation of HMGB1 as a central novel mechanism and a promising therapeutic target for asthma.
• Further research is needed to assess the utility of GSH as a biomarker. To be considered for clinical application in asthma, its effective concentration and optimal mode of administration must also be determined.

## Introduction
Asthma is characterized as a chronic and inflammatory condition of the airways, driven by heterogeneous factors ( ). About 10% of asthma patients show inadequate therapeutic response, which is related to airway remodeling, thus seriously impairing quality of life ( , ). However, the underlying mechanism of airway remodeling remains unclear. Therefore, more research is required to prevent and control the progression of asthma and to further explore more effective treatments for patients.
In physiological status, glutathione (GSH), the most important antioxidant, exists predominantly in the reduced form—GSH ( , ). The disruption of cellular homeostasis induces the formation of oxidized GSH (GSSG) and an imbalance of the GSH/GSSG ratio, thereby driving oxidative damage and programmed cell death ( , ). Several clinical studies confirmed that the GSH level and GSH/GSSG ratio were low in asthma patients ( ). In contrast, other studies found that mild asthmatic patients had a higher GSH levels in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) ( ). Therefore, there are discrepancies in reported GSH levels in asthma. The aim of this article is to explore the GSH level, function, and mechanism in asthma.
The synthesis of GSH is divided into two steps and respectively catalyzed by glutamate cysteine ligase (GCL) and GSH synthetase ( , ). Glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic (GCLC) is one of the catalytic subunits of GCL ( ). Impairment of the GSH-dependent lipid peroxide scavenging system results in reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, thereby inducing cell damage and death ( ). Therefore, the activity of GCLC is closely related to maintaining redox balance and regulating programmed cell death. It is reported that GCLC involves in the development of cancers, inflammation, and degenerative diseases ( - ). However, the function of GCLC in asthma is unclear. Therefore, we focus on the role of GCLC and illuminate its specific mechanism in asthma.
Abnormal GSH metabolism can induce oxidative stress and programmed cell death, which is known as ferroptosis ( ). The function of ferroptosis is crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis and eliminating malignant cells, playing an important role in diseases ( - ). Additionally, cell ferroptosis leads to release of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP). HMGB1 participates in gene transcriptional regulation, cell proliferation, and other cell activities ( , ). In asthma, damage to the epithelial barrier triggers the release of HMGB1. Once released, HMGB1 promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a vital mechanism contributing to various diseases ( ). In asthma, EMT is a key pathological process of airway remodeling ( ). The mechanism by which GCLC regulates HMGB1 release in asthma requires further study. Here, the study is intended to demonstrate the association between GCLC and HMGB1 in regulating airway remodeling, which is promising to help us further understand the pathogenesis of asthma.
Therefore, we aim to elucidate GSH metabolism, as well as the role of GCLC in asthma, and clarify the mechanism. Additionally, we investigate whether GCLC regulates the expression of HMGB1 by inhibiting airway epithelial cells ferroptosis, further alleviating airway inflammation and remodeling. This study is helpful for gaining a new understanding of asthma pathogenesis by delineating GCLC-mediated GSH metabolism and the subsequent activation of HMGB1, thereby providing a potential treatment for asthma. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR and ARRIVE reporting checklists (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0009/rc).

## Methods

## Clinical samples
The serum and clinical data from asthmatic patients (n=55) and health controls (n=51) were respectively from Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Naval Medical University (Shanghai Changzheng Hospital) and Department of Laboratory Diagnosis of Third Affiliated Hospital of Naval Medical University. Additionally, the clinical study was approved by the ethics committee of Shanghai Changzheng Hospital (No. 2024SL055). The Third Affiliated Hospital of Naval Medical University was informed of and agreed on the study. All patients provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Participants comprised males and females aged between 18 and 60 years. Health controls were people for physical examination without asthma history. According to “Guidelines for the prevention and management of bronchial asthma (2020 and 2024 edition)”, asthma patients were diagnosed and classified severity as mild, moderate, or severe ( , ). Exclusion criteria included active autoimmune diseases, acute phase of infection, concomitant chronic respiratory diseases, immunocompromised status, and pregnancy or lactation.

## Animals
Female bagg albino/c (BALB/c) mice aged 6–8 weeks with a body weight range of 18–23 g were used in experiment. All mice were supplied by Si Bei Fu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (SF35928584) and were bred in specific pathogen-free conditions. Bedding and cages were replaced 3 times per week. The animal experiment was approved by the ethics committee in Shanghai Changzheng Hospital, in compliance with the national and institutional guidelines for the care and use of animals. The mice were randomly allocated to treatment and control groups based on a computer-generated sequence of random numbers. 20 µL house dust mite (HDM) were administered intranasally for sensitization and challenge. GSH (100 mg/kg, i.g., Targetmol, Boston, USA), L-Buthionine-(S,R)-sulfoximine (BSO) (100 mg/kg, i.p., Targetmol) or ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) (5 mg/kg, i.p., Targetmol) were administered before every challenge. Mice were euthanized under anesthesia 24 hours after the last challenge. The animal experiments included 3–5 mice in each group.

## Collection of BALF
Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed via a tracheal cannula. The lungs were instilled with 800 µL of phosphate buffered saline and repeated three times. About 2 mL of BALF was collected per mouse. Subsequent centrifugation of the BALF (3,000 rpm, 4 °C, 10 min) collected the supernatant. After the cell pellet was resuspended with freezing solution, total cells in BALF were counted under the microscope.

## Cell culture
Both mouse lung epithelial cell line—MLE-12 (Shanghai Mi Di Er Biotechnology Co. Ltd., RCL701064-C, Shanghai, China) and human lung airway epithelial cell line—Beas-2B (Shanghai Mi Di Er Biotechnology Co. Ltd., RCL701063-C) were cultured in DMEM [with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and a combination of penicillin-streptomycin antibiotics].

## Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)
RNA was extracted by Trizol reagent (ThermoFisher, Waltham, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using SuperScript III (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) to generate complementary DNA (cDNA). RT-PCR was carried out to quantify the messenger RNA (mRNA) abundance of different genes by using SYBR Green master mix (Vazyme). All primes are provided in Tables S1,S2.

## Western blot
Proteins were extracted using IP lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) with 1% PMSF (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA). Following separation by SDS-PAGE, proteins were transferred to NC membranes, and blocked by 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA). After incubation with primary and secondary antibodies, and washed after each incubation, the NC membranes were imaged. Primary antibodies for western blot used in this study are listed below: GCLC (Proteintech, 12601-1-AP, AB_2278734, Wuhan, China), GPX4 (Proteintech, 67763-1-Ig, AB_2909469), HMGB1 (Abcam, ab18256, AB_444360, Cambridge, USA), E-cad (Proteintech, 20874-1-AP, AB_10697811), α-SMA (Proteintech, 14395-1-AP, AB_2223009), β-actin (Proteintech, 66009-1-Ig, AB_2687938), and GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig, AB_2107436).

## RNA stability assay
MLE-12 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with 1 µM of actinomycin D (MedChemExpress, HY-17559, Monmouth Junction, USA). The mRNA abundance of the target gene was quantified using RT-PCR.

## GSH and GSSG measurement
GSH and GSSG levels were determined by using GSH kits (Beyotime, S0053). All steps were performed according to the protocol about GSH and GSSG measurement.

## Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay
MDA levels were determined by using MDA kits (Beyotime, S0131M). All steps were performed according to the protocol about MDA measurement.

## Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay
For cell growth assays, MLE-12 or Beas-2B cells were seeded in 96-well plate (5×103 cells/well). Subsequently, the culture medium with different stimulators was removed at 72 h, adding FBS-free DMEM with 10% CCK8 reagent (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, C6005, Suzhou, China) was added. 96-well plate was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, and the absorbance was then measured at 450 nm.

## ROS assay
MLE-12 or Beas-2B cells were treated with different stimulators or transfection small interfering RNA (siRNA). After 24 h of stimulation, the culture stimulators were removed, washed by phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and replaced with FBS-free medium added 10 µM DCFH-DA (Biosharp, BL714A, Hefei, China) for 30 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, the culture medium with DCFH-DA was removed and washed by PBS. Images were captured and stored by using a microscope.

## Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
Mouse HMGB1 levels were measured by using ELISA kits (JONLNBIO, JL13702, Shanghai, China). All steps were according to the protocol about HMGB1 level.

## Statistical analysis
Data collation and analysis were performed by Microsoft Excel, GraphPad Prism 10.0 and SPSS. All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Following confirmation of normal data distribution, intergroup variables were compared by using student’s t-test or analysis of variance (ANOVA). *, **, ***, ****, and ns, are denoted P values of <0.05, 0.01, 0.001, 0.0001, and not significant in all figures, respectively. One of three repeated experiments with similar results is presented in figures.

## Results

## GSH metabolism was downregulated in asthma
To investigate the GSH metabolism in asthma, we collected serum from healthy controls and asthmatic samples (Table S3). Compared with healthy controls, the ratio of GSH/GSSG was decreased, indicating GSH metabolic abnormalities in asthma ( , Figure S1A). Additionally, we constructed asthmatic mice models induced by HDM (Figure S1B-S1D). The GSH/GSSG levels of lung tissues, serum and BALF were reduced in asthmatic mice ( , Figure S1E). To further elucidate the role of GSH metabolism in asthma, we administered GSH intraperitoneally to asthmatic mice ( ). In asthmatic mice, GSH treatment reduced total cell counts in BALF and the levels of the key cytokines of T helper 2 cells (Th2) inflammation Interleukin-4 (IL-4), Interleukin-5 (IL-5), and Interleukin‑13 (IL-13) in lung tissues ( ). Furthermore, subepithelial fibrosis and mucus production were relieved in GSH-treated asthma group ( ). Additionally, the production of Th2 inflammatory cytokines was observably enhanced in GSH-only group compared to control ( ). In contrast, key pathological features of asthma remained unchanged ( ). Overall, these results demonstrated the downregulation of GSH metabolism in asthma.
 

## Expression of GCLC increased in asthma mediated by FTO
To confirm GCLC participated in GSH metabolism, we treated MLE-12 and Beas-2B cells with BSO, a GCLC inhibitor. The GSH/GSSG ratio showed a concentration- and time-dependent decrease in response to BSO stimulation ( , Figure S2A). Additionally, the knockdown of GCLC in MLE-12 cells also reduced the GSH/GSSG level ( , Figure S2B,S2C). Furthermore, we found the expression of GCLC was increased in HDM-induced asthmatic mice ( ), expressing highly in lung tissues, especially in airway epithelial cells ( ). It is reported that GCLC exists N6-methyladenosine (m6A) methylation site ( ). Fat mass and obesity-associated protein (FTO), a m6A demethylase, catalyzes the demethylation reaction of m6A site on RNA ( ). We found the expression of FTO was increased in asthma mice (Figure S2D). We hypothesized that FTO regulated m6A demethylation of GCLC, maintaining the stability of GCLC mRNA. To validate the hypothesis, we knocked down FTO in MLE-12 cells, which resulted in decreased GCLC expression ( , Figure S2E). Additionally, RNA stability assay demonstrated that FTO regulated GCLC mRNA stability by demethylation ( ). Therefore, GCLC, which was regulated by FTO, governed GSH metabolism.
 

## GCLC showed a protective role in asthma
Based on these results, we further explored the role of GCLC in asthma. We administered BSO prior to asthmatic challenge ( ). In the BSO-treated asthma group, total cell counts in BALF significantly increased ( ). Furthermore, the production of Th2 inflammatory cytokines enhanced in BSO-treated asthma group compared with asthmatic group ( ). BSO treatment led to aggregate cell infiltration, subepithelial fibrosis and mucus production ( ). Additionally, administration of BSO alone did not induce significant changes in the levels of Th2 inflammatory cytokines and in key pathological features, compared to the control group ( ). Meanwhile, the GSH/GSSG ratio of lung tissues, BALF and serum were severely reduced in asthma mice administered with BSO ( , Figure S3A-S3C). Compared with control, mice treated with BSO alone exhibited a reduction in the GSH/GSSG ratio, confirming that GCLC is essential for maintaining GSH metabolism in asthma ( , Figure S3A-S3C). Overall, these results implicated that GCLC played a protective role in asthma.
 

## GCLC inhibited ferroptosis in airway epithelium
We further hypothesized that the mechanism by which GCLC regulated ferroptosis in airway epithelium. Compared with asthma mice, the levels of MDA, were reduced in asthma mice treated with GSH ( ), while they were increased in asthma mice treated with BSO ( ). In vitro, BSO stimulation leaded a concentration- and time-dependent increase in MDA levels in epithelial cells ( , Figure S4A,S4B). Furthermore, cell viability declined with increasing concentrations of BSO ( , Figure S4C). We also found that GSH supplementation reversed the BSO-induced elevation in MDA levels ( , Figure S4D). Meanwhile, the increase in MDA level caused by GCLC decline was suppressed by GSH treatment ( ). Furthermore, co-treatment with BSO and GSH enhanced cell viability ( , Figure S4E). We then detected ferroptosis-related markers and found that GCLC did not regulate the expression of GSH peroxidase 4 (GPX4), acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), and solute carrier family 7 member 11 (SLC7A11) (Figure S4F-S4K). This finding suggested that GCLC regulated ferroptosis primarily through GSH depletion rather than by modulating the expression of ferroptosis regulators. To investigate whether ferroptosis is a key mechanism, we administered asthmatic mice with BSO together with Fer-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor ( ). The MDA levels decreased in BSO combination with Fer-1 group ( ). Furthermore, Fer-1 treatment attenuated airway inflammation and remodeling ( ). Therefore, GCLC inhibition induced ferroptosis in epithelial cells, and the effect was reversed by Fer-1 or GSH, confirming the mechanism of GCLC in regulating cell ferroptosis.
 

## ROS production and HMGB1 release regulated by GCLC
It is reported that ferroptosis triggers oxidative stress in cells ( ). To investigate whether GCLC-mediated inhibition of ferroptosis alleviates oxidative stress, we assessed ROS production in epithelial cells treated with BSO. We found that the ROS production showed a concentration-dependent enhancement in response to BSO stimulation in epithelial cells ( , Figure S5A). Additionally, treatment with Fer-1 or GSH reversed BSO or GCLC knockdown-induced elevation in ROS production, which indicated that GCLC inhibited oxidative stress in epithelium ( , Figure S5B). To further research the mechanism of GCLC in epithelial cells, we focused on oxidative stress-induced HMGB1 release. We found that HMGB1 release increased when treated with BSO or GCLC knockdown, indicating that HMGB1 acted as a downstream effector of GCLC ( , Figure S5C-S5F). The upregulation of HMGB1 expression induced by BSO or GCLC knockdown was suppressed by treatment with Fer-1 or GSH ( , Figure S5C-S5F). Together, these data demonstrated that downregulation of GCLC triggered ROS accumulation and subsequent HMGB1 dysregulation.
 

## GCLC inhibited EMT by GSH metabolism
HMGB1 is well-established for its role in promoting EMT in other pathological conditions ( ). Given this, to further investigate the function of GCLC in airway remodeling, we focused on its regulatory effect on the EMT process. To confirm the hypothesis, we treated MLE-12 and Beas-2B cells with BSO and assessed EMT markers. We observed that BSO treatment induced characteristic EMT changes in MLE-12 and Beas-2B cells, marked by downregulation of E-cadherin and upregulation of α-SMA, while the addition of Fer-1 or GSH reversed these changes ( ). Furthermore, GCLC knockdown in MLE-12 cells induced EMT, as shown by decreased E-cadherin and increased α-SMA expression ( ). To determine whether GCLC regulates EMT via HMGB1, we treated MLE-12 cells with BSO in combination with either glycyrrhizic acid (an HMGB1 inhibitor) or recombinant mouse HMGB1 (rmHMGB1). The results demonstrated that exogenous supplementation with rmHMGB1 exacerbated EMT, whereas inhibition of HMGB1 attenuated the EMT induced by BSO (Figure S6A-S6D). Similarly, in GCLC-knockdown MLE-12 cells, treatment with glycyrrhizic acid or rmHMGB1 produced comparable outcomes (Figure S6E-S6H). Collectively, our data supported the conclusion that inhibition of GCLC aggravated EMT in epithelial cells.
 

## Discussion
In this study, we found evidences from clinical samples and HDM-induced asthma mice that the GSH level and GSH/GSSG ratio were decreased in asthma. Moreover, the extra supplementation GSH relieved airway inflammation and remodeling. Additionally, we found the expression of GSH synthesis related enzyme GCLC increased in asthma. Mechanically, GCLC inhibited epithelium ferroptosis and alleviated oxidative stress. We further delineated a downstream molecule HMGB1. GCLC regulated HMGB1 expression, further restricting EMT. These findings illustrated the protective role of GSH and GCLC, proposing a therapeutic strategy for asthma.
We demonstrated the downregulation of GSH metabolism in asthma. In line with our results, Ammar et al. ( ) found that patients with asthma had lower GSH level in plasma compared to controls, and data showed a pronounced reduction in GSH level in patients with uncontrolled asthma. In contrast, Smith et al. ( ) found that GSH concentration in BALF was higher in mild asthmatic patients compared with healthy subjects. The contrary results were explicable. In the mild asthma stage, GSH level in patients can be compensated, forming a protective adaptation mechanism. However, as in exacerbation stage, continuous oxidative stress will induce large consumption of GSH and an imbalance in the GSH/GSSG ratio. Therefore, the severity of asthma, inadequate control, and the type of samples tested can affect the concentration of GSH. More research is needed to assess the utility of GSH as a biomarker.
We found that extra supplementation GSH may alleviate airway inflammation and remodeling. Additionally, HMGB1 release was increased in asthma mice treated with BSO, indicating GSH metabolism in the regulation of HMGB1. Since HMGB1 plays an important role in asthma, GSH supplementation may alleviate asthma by targeting HMGB1. Unexpectedly, we found the levels of inflammatory cytokines also increased in control with GSH group. The result implied that the excessive intake of GSH disrupts normal redox signaling and imposes a metabolic burden on cells ( ). Despite the increase in inflammatory cytokines, there were no significant pathological changes in the GSH-only group, indicating the safety of GSH as a therapy. Consistent with our findings, Bagnato et al. ( ) administrated GSH by nebulized inhalation to adults with asthma, and an improvement in lung function was observed in the GSH-treated asthmatic patients compared to controls, demonstrating the alleviating effect of GSH on asthma. However, Marrades et al. ( ) found that administration of GSH to mild asthma patients led to adverse outcomes, including elevated airway resistance, reduced lung function, and aggravated asthma symptoms. This discrepancy may be attributed to the frequence of GSH administered, differences in patient race or divergent endpoints across the trials. Furthermore, the accumulation of sulfite production, a byproduct of GSH metabolism, may release inflammatory mediators and cause bronchospasm. Therefore, for the potential clinical application of GSH in asthma patients, the effective concentration and optimal mode of administration must be further explored.
Furthermore, we observed an upregulation in the expression of GCLC in asthma. Despite the upregulation of compensatory mechanism, GCLC function remained inadequate to counter the overwhelming oxidative burden, resulting in GSH depletion and a consequent decrease in the GSH/GSSG ratio. A potential reason could be that the availability of GSH precursor, cysteine and glycine, often limit GSH synthesis ( , ). The asthmatic airway may exhibit dysregulation in the cystine-glutamate antiporter, limiting cysteine uptake and rendering elevated GCLC futile ( , ). Additionally, the accelerated consumption of GSH may outpace its synthesis. The oxidative and nitrosative stress in asthma, coupled with potential hyperactivation of GSH-utilizing enzymes like GSH S-transferases in detoxification processes, can deplete GSH faster than even an upregulation of GCLC ( ).
In addition to its role in ferroptosis, GCLC deficiency depletes GSH and can thereby induce broad oxidative injury and exacerbate inflammation through additional mechanisms ( , ). In acute lung injury, overexpression of GCLC inhibits apoptosis of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells via the PI3K/AKT/Foxo1 pathway, thereby alleviating pulmonary inflammation ( ). In intestinal inflammation, loss of GCLC in T helper 17 (Th17) cells, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and reduced phosphorylation of 4E-BP1, resulting in suppressed interleukin-22 (IL-22) translation and ultimately increasing susceptibility to infection-induced intestinal damage ( ). Our data indicate that ferroptosis serves as the predominant mechanism driving BSO induced EMT in epithelial cells. We propose future studies to further investigate the broader effects of GCLC.
This study has some limitations. Firstly, in addition to antioxidant capacity, GSH is essential for regulating the balance between T helper 1 cells (Th1) and Th2 cytokine responses during antigen challenge ( ). While our study focused on the protective function of GSH in combating oxidative stress and ferroptosis in asthma, other regulatory mechanisms likely exist and warrant future exploration. Additionally, our data suggested that FTO enhanced GCLC mRNA stability through demethylation. Nevertheless, a key focus for future work will be to identify the exact m6A site involved and underlying regulatory mechanism.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, our work demonstrate that the downregulation of GCLC along with the aberrant GSH metabolism promoted ferroptosis and HMGB1 release, exacerbating airway inflammation and airway remodeling. The findings indicate that GCLC related GSH metabolism is as a new mechanism of asthma, providing a potential target for treatment.
