## Abstract

## Background
Pulmonary fibrosis occurs when myofibroblasts and the extracellular matrix (ECM) excessively accumulate in the lung interstitium. Detailed studies elucidating the role of cleavage and polyadenylation-specific factor 6 (CPSF6) during fibroblast-myofibroblast transition (FMT) remain limited.

## Methods
A silica-induced mouse pulmonary fibrosis model and a transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) induced fibroblast activation model were performed to investigate the role and mechanism of CPSF6. The expression of CPSF6 and profibrotic markers was determined by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis, Western blotting, and immunofluorescence assays. Dual-luciferase reporter gene assays and RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays were performed to investigate the association between RUNX1 3' untraslated region (3′UTR) and miR-30a-5p.

## Results
CPSF6 was downregulated in silica-induced murine fibrotic lung tissues and primary fibroblasts. Silencing CPSF6 via adeno-associated virus (AAV) in vivo exacerbated pulmonary fibrosis, while ectopic CPSF6 expression mitigated the condition. TGF-β1 effectively decreased CPSF6 expression in fibroblasts (MRC-5 and mouse primary lung fibroblast). CPSF6 overexpression blocked the profibrotic effect of TGF-β1, while inhibition of CPSF6 was found to trigger FMT. Notably, Runt-related transcription factor 1 (RUNX1), a crucial transcription factor implicated in fibrosis, was identified as a downstream effector of CPSF6. Mechanistically, CPSF6 loss favored the utilization of the proximal poly (A) site in the 3'UTR of RUNX1, leading to the production of a short 3'UTR RUNX1 transcript and more protein by avoiding being targeted for degradation by miR-30a-5p.

## Conclusions
CPSF6 is implicated in the pathogenesis of fibrosis through its function in fibroblast activation, thereby enhancing the mechanistic understanding of fibrotic progression.

## Introduction
Pulmonary fibrosis refers to a broad category of lung diseases characterized by the inappropriate formation of scar tissue in the lungs. This condition gradually affects the lung interstitium, leading to difficulties with gas exchange, shortness of breath, a reduced quality of life, and ultimately, respiratory failure and death [ ]. Various factors can contribute to the initiation and progression of lung fibrosis, including microbial agents, radiotherapeutic and chemotherapeutic treatments, as well as occupational and environmental toxins [ ]. One specific condition associated with silica exposure is termed silicosis, a complex form of occupational pulmonary fibrosis affecting workers [ ]. Around the world, silicosis remains a significant medical challenge, with current treatments primarily focusing on slowing disease progression [ ]. It is well known that alveolar epithelial cells, fibroblast proliferation and differentiation into myofibroblasts (namely activated fibroblasts), as well as excessive extracellular matrix accumulation, enhance pulmonary fibrosis progression [ , ]. Therefore, focusing on these changes is considered a promising approach to treating pulmonary fibrosis.
Fibroblast activation is a crucial factor in the progression of fibrotic diseases [ ]. During the process of fibrogenesis, resting fibroblasts transform into myofibroblasts, which are characterized by their secretion of extracellular matrix components and the contraction of actomyosin-based stress fibers [ , ]. Transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) is a pivotal pathway involved in fibroblast activation under both physiological and pathological conditions [ ]. It is essential for the sustained activation of fibroblasts, operating through a synergistic regulation of both the canonical Smad-dependent and non-canonical pathways [ ]. TGF-β1 upregulates α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), a hallmark of myofibroblasts, and enhances the deposition of pathological extracellular matrix [ ]. Identifying central checkpoints and mechanisms of TGF-β1-induced fibroblast activation may open new avenues for potential therapies aimed at targeting fibrotic diseases [ ].
Alternative polyadenylation (APA) is a molecular mechanism involved in pre-mRNA processing, where multiple polyadenylation sites are utilized, resulting in the generation of transcripts of varying lengths from a single gene [ ]. APA can occur at polyadenylation sites (PASs) located within 3' untranslated regions (3' UTRs), introns, or internal exons. The position of a polyadenylation (PA) site significantly impacts both the length and coding potential of an mRNA, thereby contributing to the diversification of mRNA and protein [ , ]. Six key factors have been identified to facilitate 3' end processing, including cleavage and polyadenylation specificity factor (CPSF), cleavage stimulation factor (CstF) protein complexes, cleavage factors I and II (CFIm and CFIIm), poly(A) polymerase (PAP), and poly(A) binding protein II (PABII) [ , ]. The CFIm complex, which consists of a small subunit called CFIm25 (also referred to as NUDT21) alongside two alternative large subunits, CFIm68 (also known as CPSF6) and CFIm59 (also known as CPSF7), serves as a crucial pre-mRNA 3' end processing factor exclusive to metazoans, which facilitates the assembly of 3' end processing factors on pre-mRNA in vitro [ , ]. Dysregulation of the CFIm-mediated APA represents a common characteristic among many disease states, including cancer, cardiovascular disease, and lung fibrosis [ , , ]. Therefore, the CFIm complex and its components could be potential therapeutic targets for pulmonary fibrosis.
In this study, we identified that CPSF6 was downregulated in silica-induced murine fibrotic lung tissues and fibroblasts treated with TGF-β1. In addition, CPSF6 knockdown exhibits profibrotic effects in vivo and causes a fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition (FMT) in vitro. In contrast, CPSF6 overexpression alleviated silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis progression. Mechanistically, we observed that the knockdown of CPSF6 upregulates the shortening of the 3' UTR of runt-related transcription factor 1 (RUNX1), thereby enhancing the pro-fibrotic activity of RUNX1 by stabilizing its mRNA. Additionally, inhibiting RUNX1 negated the profibrotic effects associated with CPSF6 knockdown. In summary, this study reveals a novel function of APA in pulmonary fibrogenesis and proposes a promising therapeutic strategy for pulmonary fibrosis, involving the overexpression of CPSF6 or the inhibition of the pro-fibrotic effects of RUNX1.

## Materials and methods

## Animal studies
All procedures received approval from the Ethics Committee of Binzhou Medical University (Ethical approval number: 2022083). Male C57BL/6J mice, aged six weeks, were obtained from Pengyue Co., Ltd. (Jinan, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. To induce fibrotic alterations, the mice were administered 50 mg/kg of SiO2 (Sigma, USA) dissolved in 0.05 mL of sterile saline, or an equivalent volume of sterile saline alone, via a single intratracheal instillation. The mice were euthanized at intervals of 7, 14, and 28 days post-treatment, and their pulmonary tissues were collected and preserved at -80 °C.
Adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) vectors encoding short hairpin RNA targeting CPSF6 (sh-CPSF6) or CPSF6 were constructed by Gene Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). A total of 48 four-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 8 per group): control, AAV-sh-Cpsf6, AAV-sh-Cpsf6 + SiO₂, SiO₂, SiO₂+AAV-control, and SiO₂+AAV-Cpsf6. All subjects received intranasal administration of 50 µL of AAV9 suspension at a concentration of 1 × 10¹¹ v.g./mL. Twenty-one days following viral administration, the animals were subjected to intratracheal instillation of either 50 mg/kg SiO₂ in 50 µL saline (experimental groups) or 50 µL saline (control group). Four weeks after silica challenge, the mice were euthanized; lung tissues were excised and promptly snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for storage at − 80 °C.

## Histopathology
Fresh mouse lung tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and immersed overnight. After being embedded in paraffin, 5 μm-thick sections were prepared and stained using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Masson staining, and immunohistochemical analysis of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA). The sample images were captured by scanning with a Pannoramic Scanning Electron Microscope.

## Cell culture and treatment
MRC-5 cells (Procell Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) were cultured in Minimal Essential Medium (MEM), while primary lung fibroblasts derived from mice were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). Both culture media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Life Technologies/Gibco, Gaithersburg, MD). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. For transcription inhibition assays, cells were treated with 2 µM actinomycin-D (Gibco) and harvested at specified time points. Total RNA was subsequently extracted for quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis.

## Cell transfection
CPSF6 siRNA, RUNX1 siRNA, miR-30a-5p mimic, miR-30a-5p inhibitor, and CPSF6 plasmid were procured from GenePharm (Shanghai, China). Transfection procedures were conducted utilizing riboFECTCP Reagent (Ribobio, Guangzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

## RNA isolation and real-time PCR
Total RNA was isolated from murine tissues and cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Scientific Pierce, USA). Reverse transcription was performed with HiS-cript II Q RT SuperMix for mRNA, and miRNA 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (by stem-loop) (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) with stem-loop primers for microRNAs. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) assays employed ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (mRNA targets) or miRNA Unimodal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) on a QuantStudio 3 machine (Thermo Fisher, USA), strictly adhering to manufacturer protocols.

## Western blot analysis
Proteins extracted from cells were obtained using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, China). For mouse tissues, total protein extraction was conducted with T-PER Tissue Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Scientific Pierce). Protein concentrations were measured employing the BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime). The proteins underwent electrophoresis on 10% SDS polyacrylamide gels and were subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. These membranes were then blocked with 5% skimmed milk, washed with TBST, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies included: Collagen I (ab34710, Abcam), α-SMA (ab32575, Abcam), CPSF6 (15489-1-AP, Proteintech), RUNX1 (4336T, Cell Signaling Technology), and GAPDH (AC002, Abclonal). The membranes were incubated with a secondary antibody, and luminescence was subsequently measured using the Tanon Automatic Chemiluminescence Image Analysis System (5200, Tanon). GAPDH was utilized as a loading control.

## Immunofluorescence experiment
The MRC-5 cells or primary fibroblasts derived from mouse tissue were cultured in a 35 mm confocal dish for a period of 48 to 72 h. Following cultivation, the cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Subsequently, blocking was performed using 1% goat serum at room temperature for one hour, after which the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting α-SMA (ab32575, Abcam), Collagen I (ab34710, Abcam), or RUNX1 (4336T, Cell Signaling Technology). The next day, secondary antibodies conjugated with Cy3 or FITC (goat anti-rabbit, Beyotime, China) were applied for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescent imaging was performed to visualize the stained cells, which were then captured using a Leica fluorescence microscope.

## Rapid amplification of cDNA 3′ ends (3′ RACE) assay
Total RNA was extracted from MRC-5 cells with TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Scientific Pierce, USA). To identify the 3′-end genomic sequence of RUNX1, RNAs were analyzed using 3′-RACE with the HiScript-TS 5′/3′ RACE Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting 3′RACE products were then checked via 2.0% agarose gel electrophoresis.

## RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP)
RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays were performed using the Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Beyotime, China). In brief, 1 × 10⁷ MRC-5 cells were lysed in 200 µL RIP buffer supplemented with protease and RNase inhibitors. Cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with rotation in the presence of either 10 µg of anti-AGO2 antibody (ab32381, Abcam) or normal rabbit IgG-coated magnetic beads. Subsequent to incubation, the complexes were captured using protein A/G magnetic beads. Following rigorous washing steps, the bound RNAs were treated with proteinase K and RNase-free DNase I, and subsequently extracted. Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) was employed to assess the enrichment of RUNX1 mRNA and miR-30a-5p.

## Collagen gel contraction assay
Type I collagen gels were formulated in 24-well plates utilizing a rat tail-derived collagen solution at a final concentration of 1 mg/mL (Xinyou Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China; Cat#200110), 0.1 M NaOH, and 10 × PBS. Following gel solidification, fibroblasts were introduced within the matrices and cultured for 48 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Gel contraction was assessed by measuring the decrease in surface area, specifically through observed changes in the surface area of the gels.

## Statistical analysis
All experiments were conducted and reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), based on a minimum of three independent trials. To compare two groups, a two-tailed unpaired t-test was utilized for data conforming to a normal distribution. When evaluating multiple groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed, with Dunnett’s test subsequently applied. Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 17.0. A p-value under 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

## Results

## Cpsf6 is downregulated during silica-induced pulmonary fibrogenesis
To clarify the mechanisms underlying pulmonary fibrosis, a murine model was established through intratracheal instillation of silica at a dose of 50 mg/kg, consistent with prior dose optimization studies. Silica exposure induced pronounced disruption of alveolar architecture and the formation of fibrotic nodules at 28 days post-exposure, as verified by Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining (Fig.  A). Moreover, Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated substantial collagen deposition in the lungs at this time point (Fig.  B). These findings were further validated through hydroxyproline analysis (Fig.  C). Immunohistochemical analyses showed increased α-SMA-positive areas within fibrotic tissues (Fig.  D). Consistent with these histological findings, qRT-PCR and western blot analyses corroborated the upregulation of fibrotic markers, specifically Collagen I and α-SMA, in the lungs of mice exposed to silica, thereby establishing a robust model of pulmonary fibrosis (Fig.  E, F, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Notably, CPSF6 expression was markedly downregulated in fibrotic lung tissues relative to controls (Fig.  G, H, Supplementary Fig. 1B). Similarly, in primary lung fibroblasts derived from mice, there was a concomitant increase in the expression levels of Collagen I and α-SMA, alongside a decrease in CPSF6 expression, consistent with the observations in silica-treated lung tissues (Fig.  I-K, Supplementary Fig. 1C). Collectively, these findings suggest that CPSF6 may play a regulatory role in the pathogenesis of pulmonary fibrosis.
 

## Knockdown of Cpsf6 exacerbates pulmonary fibrosis induced by silica in vivo
To investigate the role of Cpsf6 in the progression of pulmonary fibrosis, we designed adeno-associated viral (AAV) shRNAs for Cpsf6 silencing. Following a period of 21 days post-administration of AAV-sh-Cpsf6, the mice were subjected to a single intratracheal instillation of silica dust suspension, which was maintained for 28 days (Fig.  A). Subsequently, lung tissues were excised and subjected to qRT-PCR to assess the expression levels of Cpsf6. Consistent with expectations, intranasal administration of AAV-sh-Cpsf6 elicited a significant decrease in Cpsf6 expression within pulmonary tissues (Fig.  B). Histopathological analysis through H&E staining, Masson’s trichrome staining, and immunohistochemical detection of α-SMA collectively confirmed that silencing of Cpsf6 markedly exacerbates pulmonary fibrosis (Fig.  C). Then, the data were further validated through hydroxyproline quantification analysis (Fig.  D). As expected, the expression levels of fibrosis-associated markers, specifically Collagen I and α-SMA, were markedly upregulated in lung tissue following the silencing of Cpsf6 (Fig.  E, F, Supplementary Fig. 2A). Furthermore, Western blot analysis revealed that Cpsf6 knockdown led to an increased expression of these fibrosis markers at the protein level in primary fibroblasts (Fig.  G, Supplementary Fig. 2B). Collectively, these data showed the regulatory function of cpsf6 in silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis.
 

## Overexpression of Cpsf6 alleviates silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis
To confirm the therapeutic potential of Cpsf6 in the progression of pulmonary fibrosis, mice were administered AAV-Cpsf6 intranasally to induce overexpression of Cpsf6 (Fig.  A). As shown in qRT-PCR, CPSF6 mRNA expression in lung tissues was significantly increased in the SiO₂ + AAV-Cpsf6 group compared to the SiO₂ + AAV-control group (Fig.  B). Histopathological examination revealed that Cpsf6 overexpression markedly mitigated silica-induced pulmonary fibrotic alterations (Fig.  C) and decreased hydroxyproline content in lung tissues (Fig.  D). Furthermore, pulmonary tissue levels of Collagen I and α-SMA were assessed via western blot analysis and qRT-PCR. The overexpression of Cpsf6 significantly attenuated the silica-induced elevation of both Collagen I and α-SMA at the protein and mRNA levels (Fig.  E, F, Supplementary Fig. 3A). Consistent with these findings, Western blot analysis revealed a notable decrease in the protein expression of Collagen I and α-SMA following AAV-Cpsf6 treatment in primary lung fibroblasts isolated from mice, thereby reversing the upregulation induced by SiO₂ (Fig.  G, Supplementary Fig. 3B). These observations suggest that overexpression of CPSF6 alleviates silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis.
 

## CPSF6 is downregulated in active pulmonary fibroblasts induced by TGF-β1
To further elucidate our findings, we established an in vitro model of fibroblast activation, given that fibroblasts are the primary effector cells in pulmonary fibrosis. MRC-5 fibroblasts were exposed to increasing concentrations of TGF-β1 (0, 1, 2, and 5 ng/mL) for 48 h. Western blot analysis demonstrated a dose-dependent increase in fibrosis-associated markers, including collagen I and α-SMA, confirming successful fibroblast activation (Fig.  A, Supplementary Fig. 4A). Based on these results, 5 ng/mL TGF-β1 for 48 h was chosen for subsequent experiments. Immunofluorescence analysis corroborated these findings, showing enhanced expression of α-SMA and collagen I in TGF-β1–treated cells compared with controls (Fig.  B, C). Consistent with fibroblast activation, TGF-β1 treatment significantly increased cell viability and proliferation (Fig.  D, E).
 Having established this comprehensive activation model, we subsequently examined the expression profile of CPSF6. RNA sequencing data revealed a pronounced downregulation of CPSF6 in fibroblasts stimulated with TGF-β1 (Fig.  F). This finding was validated by PCR and Western blot analyses, which confirmed substantial reductions in CPSF6 mRNA and protein levels following TGF-β1 treatment (Figs.  G, H, Supplementary Fig. 4B). Additionally, TGF-β1 treatment was associated with an increased staining intensity of Collagen I and α-SMA, coupled with a decrease in CPSF6 expression (Fig.  I, J). Moreover, decreased CPSF6 expression was also observed by Western blot in activated primary mouse lung fibroblasts (Fig.  K, Supplementary Fig. 4C). Collectively, these data indicate that CPSF6 is consistently downregulated during TGF-β1-induced fibroblast activation, highlighting its potential role in this process.

## CPSF6 plays a key role in fibroblast-myofibroblast transition (FMT)
To investigate whether CPSF6 is required for fibroblast-myofibroblast transition (FMT), we conducted loss-of-function experiments utilizing small-interfering RNAs (siRNAs). The qRT-PCR assay was performed to confirm the effective knockdown of CPSF6 (Fig.  A). Consistent with the hypothesis, CPSF6 depletion resulted in an enhancement of profibrotic phenotypes, as indicated by increased expression levels of key fibrotic markers (Fig.  B, Supplementary Fig. 5A). Immunofluorescence staining revealed augmented deposition of collagen I and elevated α-SMA expression in MRC-5 cells and mouse primary lung fibroblasts following CPSF6 knockdown (Fig.  C, Supplementary Fig. 5B). Additionally, cell proliferation assays—including EdU and MTT—demonstrated that CPSF6 deficiency significantly promoted MRC-5 cell proliferation (Fig.  D, E). To further substantiate the profibrotic implications of CPSF6 siRNA treatment in MRC-5 cells, collagen gel contraction assays were performed. These functional assays demonstrated that CPSF6 knockdown substantially increased gel contraction capacity (Fig.  F).
 Conversely, overexpression of CPSF6 effectively counteracted TGF-β1-induced FMT. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed a significant downregulation of CPSF6 mRNA levels following TGF-β1 stimulation, while overexpression of CPSF6 via plasmid transfection successfully reinstated its expression levels (Fig.  G). Notably, Western blot analysis demonstrated that CPSF6 upregulation markedly suppressed the expression of fibrotic markers such as α-SMA and Collagen I in response to TGF-β1 (Fig.  H, Supplementary Fig. 5C). Functionally, cell viability assays indicated that CPSF6 overexpression mitigated the TGF-β1-induced increase in cellular proliferation (Fig.  I, J). Immunofluorescence staining further corroborated these results, illustrating that enforced CPSF6 expression attenuated the accumulation of α-SMA and collagen I in TGF-β1–treated fibroblasts (Fig.  K, Supplementary Fig. 5D). Collectively, these findings underscore the pivotal role of CPSF6 as a regulator of FMT, primarily by negatively regulating the expression of critical fibrotic markers and mitigating pro-fibrotic cellular responses.

## RUNX1 is a downstream functional target of CPSF6
To determine whether RUNX1 functions as a downstream target of CPSF6 within pulmonary fibroblasts, mechanistic investigations were conducted utilizing MRC-5 cell lines alongside primary lung fibroblasts isolated from murine models. The findings revealed that silencing of CPSF6 markedly upregulated RUNX1 expression in MRC-5 cells, whereas overexpression of CPSF6 resulted in a significant reduction of RUNX1 protein levels, thereby indicating an inverse relationship in their regulatory dynamics (Fig.  A-C, Supplementary Fig. 6A, B). Further, loss-of-function assays employing TGF-β1-stimulated MRC-5 cells and primary murine lung fibroblasts were performed to assess the functional role of RUNX1. RUNX1 silencing by siRNA markedly suppressed the expression of fibrotic markers, including Collagen I and α-SMA, as confirmed by Western blot analysis and immunofluorescence staining (Fig.  D, E, Supplementary Fig. 6C). Additionally, proliferation assessments using MTT and EdU assays demonstrated that RUNX1 knockdown impeded fibroblast proliferation induced by TGF-β1 (Fig.  F, G). Moreover, RUNX1 downregulation was correlated with a notable increase in collagen gel size relative to TGF-β1-treated controls (Fig.  H). Altogether, the data suggested that the role of RUNX1 is a critical downstream effector of CPSF6, mediating its regulatory influence on fibrotic marker expression and fibroblast activation.
 

## RUNX1 mediates the CPSF6 function during fibroblast activation
To establish whether RUNX1 functionally mediates the role of CPSF6 in fibroblast activation, a series of rescue experiments was conducted in MRC-5 cells. Consistent with prior expectations, the knockdown of CPSF6 resulted in a significant increase in the expression of the myofibroblast markers α-SMA and collagen I. Subsequent inhibition of RUNX1 effectively reversed this increase, indicating a mediating role (Fig.  A, Supplementary Fig. 7A). Additionally, immunofluorescence staining for α-SMA and Collagen I confirmed the anti-fibrotic effects resulting from RUNX1 knockdown (Fig.  B). Functionally, the silencing of RUNX1 diminished fibroblast proliferation induced by CPSF6 knockdown, as assessed by EdU and MTT assays (Fig.  C, D). Moreover, the reduction in RUNX1 expression alleviated the enhanced contractile capacities observed following CPSF6 siRNA treatment (Fig.  E). In contrast, gain-of-function experiments provided evidence that overexpression of RUNX1 effectively counteracts the inhibitory effects of CPSF6 on TGF-β1–induced fibroblast activation. Specifically, the suppression of collagen I and α-SMA by CPSF6 overexpression was reversed upon co-transfection with a RUNX1 plasmid (Fig.  F, Supplementary Fig. 7B). Immunofluorescence analyses visually corroborated that RUNX1 overexpression restored the expression levels of α-SMA and collagen I (Fig.  G). Additionally, functional assays demonstrated that RUNX1 overexpression enhanced the cell viability and gel contraction capacity (Fig.  H, I). These results support the hypothesis that the role of RUNX1 is a pivotal downstream mediator of CPSF6 in facilitating fibroblast activation and the expression of fibrotic markers.
 

## CPSF6 knockdown induces 3’UTR shortening of RUNX1 to regulate its expression
Through comprehensive transcriptome-wide sequencing analyses, we have identified that CPSF6 influences the length of the 3’UTR of mRNAs encoded by various genes, including the pivotal fibrogenic transcription factor RUNX1 [ ]. Consequently, we designed PCR primers to assess the two transcript variants of RUNX1 in MRC-5 cells. The results from qRT-PCR and agarose gel electrophoresis indicated that the knockdown of CPSF6 resulted in a decrease in the long 3′UTR isoform of RUNX1, while simultaneously increasing the short 3′UTR isoform (Fig. A, C). Conversely, overexpression of CPSF6 yielded the opposite effect, with an increase in the long 3' UTR isoform and a decrease in the short 3’ UTR isoform (Fig. B). Furthermore, our findings revealed that RUNX1 mRNAs possessing short 3′UTRs exhibited greater stability compared to those with long 3′UTRs (Fig. D). Consistent with these observations, luciferase reporter assays indicated a significantly higher luciferase activity in constructs bearing the short 3′UTR compared to those with the long 3′UTR (Fig. E).
 Subsequently, we identified potential microRNAs (miRNAs) that may contribute to the reduced expression of RUNX1 in response to CPSF6 knockdown. The long RUNX1 3′UTR isoform was shown to contain multiple potential miRNA binding sites when compared to the short 3′UTR isoform, as predicted by three distinct online tools: TargetScan, miRwalk, and miRDB. To refine this list further, an additional criterion was applied (GSE32538), requiring that the identified miRNAs be predicted to suppress pulmonary fibrosis. Based on the bioinformatics analysis, five miRNAs were identified as potential binders to the long 3′UTR RUNX1 isoform (Fig.  F). Among these, miR-30a-5p exhibited the highest expression levels in MRC-5 cells (Fig.  G). In comparison to the control group, the miR-30a-5p mimic significantly reduced the luciferase reporter activity of the long 3′UTR, with no notable effect on the luciferase activity of the short 3′UTR (Fig.  H). Conversely, the inhibition of miR-30a-5p increased the luciferase reporter activities of the long 3′UTR, while the activities of the short 3′UTR remained unchanged (Fig.  I). These findings suggest that miR-30a-5p may directly bind to the sites within the long 3′UTR of the RUNX1 isoform. Next, an RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay utilizing an AGO2 antibody indicated that endogenous long 3′UTR RUNX1 isoforms and miR-30a-5p were predominantly enriched in the AGO2 antibody group, thereby further establishing the interaction between miR-30a-5p and the long 3′UTR (Fig.  J). Moreover, as anticipated, the expression of RUNX1 exhibited a negative correlation with the expression levels of miR-30a-5p at the protein level (Fig.  K, L, Supplementary Fig. 8A).

## Discussion
Occupational silica exposure is a primary risk factor of silicosis, a chronic, progressive fibrotic disease of the lung marked by ECM accumulation, sourced from myofibroblasts [ ]. Understanding the pathological processes and regulatory aspects of silicosis is an unmet need to explore potential therapeutic targets. Given the limited availability of primary human lung tissue specimens, the MRC-5 human fetal lung fibroblast cell line was employed in this study as an in vitro model of pulmonary fibrosis. This cellular model effectively mimics key pro-fibrotic features, including proliferation, differentiation, and ECM production. To improve the translational relevance of the results, fibroblasts isolated from mice were also included in the experiments to better reflect the in vivo disease environment. Furthermore, we successfully established a silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis mouse model via intratracheal administration of silica suspension, and used TGF-β1 to induce fibroblast activation instead of silica in vitro. Our findings indicate that the expression of CPSF6 is downregulated during silica-induced pulmonary fibrogenesis and TGF-β1-induced fibroblast activation. Additionally, the knockdown of CPSF6 exerted profibrotic effects, while CPSF6 overexpression partly inhibited fibrogenesis. Mechanistically, we observed that RUNX1 was an effective downstream molecule of CPSF6, its expression was negatively associated with CPSF6, and mediated the role of CPSF6 in fibrogenesis.
Alternative polyadenylation (APA) occurs in over 70% of human genes and leads to the production of multiple transcripts with varying 3’ ends. This process significantly increases the diversity of mRNAs and the proteins that can be derived from a single gene [ ]. Recent studies have revealed various APA regulatory mechanisms that promote the development and progression of several human diseases, including pulmonary fibrosis [ ]. A critical mechanism of APA regulation involves the modulation of the expression levels of core polyadenylation machinery components. For example, CFIm25, a subunit of CFIm, plays a crucial role in fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation. Its levels are reduced in the lungs of humans and mice with pulmonary fibrosis, as well as in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) fibroblasts [ ]. Further supporting this association, a stiff matrix down-regulates the CFIm subunits CFIm68, CFIm59, and CFIm25, promoting an APA-dependent increase in collagen and fibronectin production in primary human lung fibroblasts and a bleomycin-induced mouse model of lung fibrosis [ ]. Notably, APA has been associated with numerous other pathological processes involved in fibrosis. NUDT21 regulates the utilization of the 3’UTR of LOXL2 through APA, thereby modulating LOXL2 expression and contributing to pulmonary fibrosis by influencing extracellular matrix cross-linking [ ]. Moreover, NUDT21-mediated APA controls SASP expression and fibroblast senescence, processes that may further drive the development of IPF [ ]. In addition, our previous studies demonstrated that CPSF6 loss mediates LDHA 3’UTR shortening to promote fibroblast glycolysis and pulmonary fibrosis [ ]. In this study, our data also indicated that CPSF6 was downregulated in lung tissue from mice with silica-induced fibrosis, as well as in fibroblasts stimulated with TGF-β1. Furthermore, the knockdown of CPSF6 demonstrates profibrotic effects in vivo and induces FMT in vitro.
RUNX1 is an evolutionarily conserved transcription factor that has been shown to regulate cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival in multiple cell lineages. It has been reported that the expression level of RUNX1 is upregulated in lung fibroblasts treated with TGF-β1, and knocking down RUNX1 leads to a downregulation of the myofibroblast marker α-SMA and extracellular matrix protein levels, suggesting that RUNX1 is a critical molecule influencing fibroblast activation [ ]. Mechanistic studies have found that the expression level of RUNX1 is regulated by various factors. For example, the RNA-binding protein HuR can affect the stability of RUNX1 mRNA by binding to the regulatory element in the 3'UTR of RUNX1 mRNA [ ]. Another study indicates that miR-450b-5p in lung fibroblasts can influence the expression of RUNX1 at the post-transcriptional level by binding to the 3' UTR region of RUNX1 [ ]. Additionally, RUNX1 is negatively regulated by miR-101, which inactivates the TGF-β1/Smad2 signaling pathway, thereby alleviating myocardial infarction and cardiac fibrosis [ ]. In accordance with these reports, our data also showed that RUNX1 was increased during FMT induced by TGF-β1; however, knocking down RUNX1 blocked the profibrotic effect of TGF-β1. Notably, a negative relationship was observed between CPSF6 and RUNX1 expression, and RUNX1 mediated the impact of CPSF6 in regulating fibroblast proliferation and ECM production.
Based on early studies utilizing expressed sequence tags and more recent analyses employing high-throughput sequencing, it is evident that APA is prevalent, particularly occurring in the 3' UTR of mRNAs [ , ]. This phenomenon can lead to alterations in the structure of 3'UTRs or generate various RNA isoforms characterized by differing lengths of their 3' UTRs. Longer 3' UTRs tend to provide more binding sites for microRNAs (miRNAs) and RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), as well as promote a greater variety of alternative RNA secondary structures [ ]. Several mechanisms of gene regulation involving 3' UTR-APA have been identified, including miRNA-mediated regulation at the 3’ UTR of RNA molecules [ ]. Therefore, variations in transcript length can influence mRNA stability, translation, subcellular localization, tissue distribution, and ultimately, protein functionality. Here, we observed that CPSF6 loss increased the expression of the short-3'UTR transcript of RUNX1, but inhibited the long-3'UTR transcript expression. Delayed mRNA decay was also observed in CPSF6 knockdown fibroblasts compared with controls, and long-3'UTR transcripts degraded faster than short-3'UTR transcripts following actinomycin D treatment. These findings demonstrated that low-level CPSF6 could stabilize RUNX1 mRNA and increase its protein expression level through regulating APA.
Considering that microRNAs targeting 3'UTRs to regulate gene expression have been widely reported, we screened five miRNAs predicted as targets of RUNX1 by bioinformatics analysis. We confirmed that miR-30a-5p was capable of binding to it using luciferase and RNA pull-down assays. MiR-30a-5p has been shown to regulate fibroblast proliferation in multiple fibrotic diseases, including cardiac fibrosis and keloids [ , ]. Another study reveals that the decreased expression of miR‑30a-5p in the BALF of patients with IPF, along with the consequential increase in TAB3 expression, may be a crucial factor in IPF progression [ ]. However, the relationship between miR-30a-5p and RUNX1 is still unknown. In the present study, we observed that the protein expression of RUNX1 long-3'UTR transcripts was downregulated in fibroblasts after miR-30a-5p mimic treatment, whereas it was upregulated in miR-30a-5p knockdown fibroblasts. These findings suggested that knockdown of CPSF6 increased the shortening of the 3'UTR of RUNX1 production and increased the expression level of RUNX1, in part due to avoiding the incorporation of miR-30a-5p binding sites into the 3′UTR. Besides, CPSF6 knockdown induced fibroblast activation, however, RUNX1 knockdown abrogated the increase of fibrotic marker production, proliferation levels, and migration activity induced by CPSF6-siRNA. Ectopic expression of CPSF6 blocked the profibrotic function of TGF-β1, whereas its effect was reversed by RUNX1 plasmid. Collectively, our findings indicate that the downregulation of CPSF6 facilitates fibroblast activation, an effect that may be partially ascribed to its role in augmenting RUNX1 expression.
Although RUNX1 is identified as a functional downstream effector of CPSF6 in this study, we cannot exclude the possibility that CPSF6 regulates fibrogenesis via other molecular mechanisms. Since the length of the 3' UTR can be affected by various RNA-binding proteins involved in the APA machinery, as well as other RNA processing events, other regulatory factors likely work alongside CPSF6 to influence fibrogenesis. Moreover, CPSF6 is decreased during pulmonary fibrosis and TGF-β1-induced fibroblast activation, however, few reports exist on the underlying molecular mechanisms that regulate its expression. Notably, studies in related contexts have demonstrated that microRNAs serve as crucial intermediaries connecting TGF-β1 signaling to the expression of NUDT21 [ ]. Therefore, we cannot dismiss the possibility that TGF-β1 similarly downregulates CPSF6 expression by modulating the levels of specific microRNAs. Remarkably, CPSF6-mediated APA appears to exhibit diverse patterns across cell types and disease states [ – ]. Lastly, CPSF6 is a ubiquitously expressed regulator of APA that may influence the expression of multiple genes, thereby presenting certain challenges in clinical translation. These insights underscore the need for further studies to explore the complex mechanisms of CPSF6 and APA across different disease contexts.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, our study revealed a novel CPSF6-mediated mechanism underlying the pathology of pulmonary fibrosis (Fig.  ). Under pathological conditions, CPSF6 reduction could increase the shortening of the 3' UTR of RUNX1 mRNA and enhance its protein expression, in part, by avoiding miR-30a-5p-mediated degradation. These events collectively contribute to the induction of fibroblast activation and silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis. Overall, our findings further elucidate the critical role of epigenetic regulation in fibroblast activation and silica-induced pulmonary fibrosis, thereby enriching the mechanistic framework underlying fibrotic pathogenesis.
