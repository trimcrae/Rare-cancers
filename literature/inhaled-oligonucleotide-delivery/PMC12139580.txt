## ABSTRACT
Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) rates are rising due to the increasing prevalence of cardiac metabolic disorders, particularly diabetes mellitus (DM). While revascularization procedures such as coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) restore blood flow, they can also exacerbate myocardial ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury, for which current cardioprotective strategies remain insufficient. MicroRNAs are critical regulators of gene expression, but the role of miR‐100‐5p in I/R injury remains unclear. This study demonstrates that miR‐100‐5p is significantly upregulated in myocardial tissue during I/R injury, leading to the downregulation of protein arginine methyltransferase 5 (PRMT5). This reduction in PRMT5 impairs its capacity to methylate and inactivate Phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), resulting in disruption of the PI3K‐AKT signaling pathway, increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and aggravated myocardial damage. Using antisense oligomers to inhibit miR‐100‐5p, we restored PRMT5 expression, reactivated PI3K‐AKT signaling, and reduced cardiomyocyte death, thereby mitigating myocardial injury. Our findings identify the miR‐100‐5p/PRMT5/PI3K‐AKT axis as a key regulatory pathway in myocardial I/R injury and highlight miR‐100‐5p as a potential therapeutic target to protect the heart during revascularization procedures.
Keywords: cardiomyocyte death, miR‐100‐5p, myocardial I/R injury, PI3K‐AKT pathway, PRMT5, PTEN

MiR‐100‐5p overexpression downregulates PRMT5, leading to increased PTEN activity and reduced AKT signaling. This molecular cascade promotes cardiomyocyte apoptosis, enhances fibrotic remodeling, and exacerbates cardiac dysfunction following myocardial ischemia–reperfusion injury. Inhibition of miR‐100‐5p with antisense oligonucleotides restores PRMT5 expression, attenuates PTEN activation, preserves AKT survival signaling, mitigates myocardial damage, and represents a promising therapeutic strategy to improve cardiac outcomes after reperfusion therapy. ## Background
The incidence of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) continues to rise, largely driven by the increasing prevalence of certain cardiometabolic diseases such as diabetes mellitus (DM), which significantly elevate morbidity and mortality risks [ , , ]. Revascularization therapies, including CABG and PCI, have revolutionized AMI management by restoring blood flow to the ischemic myocardium [ , , ]. Nevertheless, these interventions paradoxically introduce ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury, a complex pathophysiological response due to the sudden reintroduction of blood to previously ischemic tissue [ , , ]. I/R injury is characterized by excessive oxidative stress, inflammation, and widespread cardiomyocyte apoptosis, often resulting in long‐term myocardial dysfunction [ , ]. Patients with conditions like DM are particularly susceptible to I/R injury due to metabolic disturbances, which amplify oxidative stress and inflammation [ , , ]. This heightened risk emphasizes the urgent need for effective therapeutic strategies to protect against I/R injury, especially in individuals with cardiometabolic disorders.
Among the various therapeutic targets, PRMT5 has emerged as a pivotal regulator of gene expression, oxidative stress responses, and apoptotic pathways [ , , , ]. PRMT5 plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis during stress, significantly protecting myocardial tissue [ , ]. Emerging evidence indicates that dysregulation of PRMT5 severely compromises its ability to modulate oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, leading to increased myocardial damage [ , ]. For instance, reduced PRMT5 activity is associated with worse cardiac outcomes due to impaired angiogenesis and defective regulation of apoptosis under ischemic conditions [ , ]. In addition, PRMT5 plays a key role in the methylosome, a multiprotein complex that includes components like MEP50 and CLNS1A, which are involved in arginine methylation and contribute to gene regulation and cellular signaling [ , , ]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms governing PRMT5 during I/R injury is essential for developing targeted cardioprotective therapies tailored to individual patient needs.
MicroRNAs have gained recognition as critical regulators of cardiovascular health and diseases [ , , , ]. Among them, miR‐100‐5p has been implicated in endothelial dysfunction and diabetic vascular complications [ , ]. Research suggests that miR‐100‐5p directly targets PRMT5 [ ], leading to its downregulation and potentially worsening I/R injury by impairing protective signaling pathways essential for cell survival, such as the PI3K‐AKT axis. However, further study is needed to clarify the specific mechanisms by which miR‐100‐5p and PRMT5 influence these pathways during I/R injury.
This study investigates the mechanistic relationship between miR‐100‐5p and PRMT5 in the heart, particularly in the context of I/R injury. Our findings indicate that miR‐100‐5p is a key modulator of I/R injury in the myocardium by suppressing PRMT5. Targeting miR‐100‐5p restores PRMT5 function, enhancing the heart's ability to withstand the harmful effects of reperfusion. These findings propose a novel therapeutic strategy to protect the heart during revascularization procedures, with the potential to improve clinical outcomes for patients undergoing CABG or PCI. Future research will focus on optimizing miR‐100‐5p targeted therapies and further clarifying the molecular pathways through which PRMT5 confers cardioprotection.

## Methods
2.1.

## Animal Protocols
All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (Approval Number: 2021‐KY‐0940‐20). Experiments complied with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals issued by the National Academy of Sciences and the National Institutes of Health. Male C57BL/6J mice, aged 8–10 weeks, were maintained in a specific‐pathogen‐free (SPF) facility under controlled environmental conditions. Mice were randomly assigned to groups using a random number generator to ensure unbiased allocation. Inclusion criteria included male mice aged 8–10 weeks and a pathogen‐free health status. Exclusion criteria included any signs of illness or protocol deviations prior to or during the experiment. Attrition (e.g., animals excluded due to complications) was recorded and reported.
Mice received a tail vein injection of miR‐100‐5p agomir, antagomir, or a scrambled control (GenePharma Co. Ltd., Suzhou, China) at a dose of 4 nmol per mouse. One day after miRNA injection, acute cardiac I/R injury surgery was performed as previously described [ ]. Acute cardiac I/R injury was induced by ligation of the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery for 45 min, followed by reperfusion. Mice were sacrificed either at 1 or 7 days post‐reperfusion to assess early‐stage and sustained effects. Sham‐operated mice underwent the same procedures except for LAD ligation. To reduce bias, investigators were blinded to the treatment groups during surgery, data collection, and analysis. Power analysis was performed using G*Power software to determine the minimum sample size required for statistical significance (power > 80%, α = 0.05).

2.2.

## Echocardiography
Cardiac function was assessed using echocardiography (VisualSonics, Vevo 2100) as previously described [ ]. Mice were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane, and the left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) were measured over three consecutive cardiac cycles using the M‐mode long‐axis view of the left ventricle at the papillary muscle level. To minimize variability, a blinded observer performed the echocardiographic analysis.

2.3.

## Histological Analysis
Hearts were rapidly excised, rinsed in ice‐cold PBS, fixed with 4% formaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. For Hematoxylin and Eosin (H/E) staining, sections were deparaffinized in xylene (3 × 5 min) and rehydrated through graded ethanol solutions (100%, 95%, and 70%, 2 × 3 min each) to distilled water. They were then stained in hematoxylin for 5 min, rinsed in running tap water for 5 min, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol (1% HCl in 70% ethanol), and blued in 0.2% ammonia water for 3 min. Eosin staining was performed for 1 min. Sections were subsequently dehydrated in 95% ethanol (2 × 2 min), cleared in xylene (3 × 2 min), and mounted with a xylene‐based medium under coverslips.
To identify apoptotic cardiomyocytes, heart sections were subjected to TUNEL and cardiac troponin T (cTnT) co‐immunostaining. After TUNEL labeling (In Situ Cell Death Detection Kit, Roche), sections were incubated with a primary antibody against cTnT (1:200, Abcam #ab45932) overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 594‐conjugated secondary antibody (1:500, Thermo Fisher). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were captured using a confocal microscope, and TUNEL+ cTnT+ cells were counted in five random high‐power fields per section. Results were expressed as the percentage of double‐positive cells per total cTnT+ area.
For CD68 immunofluorescence staining, sections were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h and then incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti‐CD68 primary antibody (#ab283654, Abcam). After PBS washes, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Following further PBS washes, nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min, and sections were mounted with the anti‐fade mounting medium.

2.4.

## Cell Culture and Treatment
AC16 human cardiomyocytes and H9C2 rat cardiomyoblasts were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). AC16 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 (Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium: Nutrient Mixture F‐12; Gibco), and H9C2 cells were cultured in high‐glucose DMEM (Gibco), each supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; #A5670701, Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) procedure was performed as previously described [ ]. Briefly, cells were cultured in glucose‐ and FBS‐deprived medium in a hypoxic chamber for 4 h, then replaced with a complete medium and reoxygenated for 1 h.
For transfection studies, AC16 and H9C2 cells were transfected with miR‐100‐5p mimic, miR‐100‐5p inhibitor (antisense oligonucleotide, ASO), or non‐specific scrambled controls using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen), following the manufacturer's protocol. Transfections were typically performed using 40 nM of mimic or ASO, unless otherwise indicated. Cells were transfected with 20 or 40 nM concentrations of the mimic or inhibitor for dose–response experiments. The following sequences were used: miR‐100‐5p mimic sense strand (5′–3′): AACCCGUAGAUCCGAACUUGUG; antisense strand (5′–3′): CAAGUUCGGAUCUACGGGUUUU; and miR‐100‐5p ASO (5′–3′): CACAAGUUCGGAUCUACGGGUU. The effects of miR‐100‐5p modulation on gene expression and downstream functional outcomes were subsequently assessed in a dose‐dependent manner.

2.5.

## 
RNA Isolation and Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR)
Total RNA was isolated from cardiac tissues or cultured AC16 and H9C2 cardiomyocytes using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA concentration and quality were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific). For cDNA synthesis, 500 ng of RNA was reverse‐transcribed using the Reverse First‐Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (TaKaRa). Real‐time qPCR was performed using SYBR green Master Mix (Yeason) on a Roche Light Cycler 480 PCR system (Roche). For miRNA quantification, total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent. Reverse transcription was carried out using the miScript II RT Kit (Qiagen), and miR‐100‐5p was measured with TaqMan Gene Expression Assay (Applied Biosystems), with U6 small nuclear RNA serving as the internal control. To ensure accuracy and normalization, the stability of U6 expression across all experimental conditions was validated, as shown in Figure  . All the primers used are listed in Table  .

2.6.

## Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)
Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels were measured in mouse blood samples to evaluate myocardial injury. After centrifugation at 4°C for 5 min at 3000 rpm, the supernatant was collected and stored at −80°C until analysis. Using commercial ELISA kits from Abcam, the assays were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Standards and samples were added to pre‐coated 96‐well plates, followed by incubation and washing. A detection antibody was introduced, and the resulting colorimetric reaction was measured with a microplate reader. LDH concentrations were calculated based on standard curves, providing quantifiable markers of myocardial injury due to ischemia–reperfusion.

2.7.

## 
PRMT5 3′ UTR Constructs
The 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of the PRMT5 gene was cloned into a suitable reporter vector downstream of the firefly luciferase gene to facilitate the assessment of microRNA interactions. The cloning process involved amplifying the PRMT5 3’ UTR sequence via PCR and inserting it into a reporter vector, ensuring correct orientation and frame for luciferase expression. To further investigate microRNA interactions, a mutant version of the PRMT5 3′ UTR was created by introducing specific nucleotide substitutions to disrupt the predicted microRNA binding sites. These mutations were carefully selected to alter the base pairing regions critical for microRNA recognition, effectively abolishing its ability to bind and regulate PRMT5 expression. Detailed sequences of both the wild‐type and mutant constructs are provided in the supplemental data, allowing for a comprehensive understanding of the modifications made and their implications for miRNA‐mediated regulation. The detailed sequences of both the wild‐type and mutant constructs are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

2.8.

## Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay
AC16 and H9C2 cells were plated in 24‐well plates and co‐transfected with the wild‐type or mutant PRMT5 3′ UTR constructs, along with a Renilla luciferase control vector, to normalize transfection efficiency. For miRNA targeting assessment, cells were co‐transfected with a microRNA mimic known to target PRMT5 or a negative control mimic. Following a 24–48 h incubation period, cells were lysed using a Passive Lysis Buffer, and luciferase activity was measured using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System. Firefly luciferase activity was measured first, followed by Renilla luciferase measurement using a luminometer. The firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity, allowing for the comparison of luciferase expression between the wild‐type and mutant constructs as well as the evaluation of the impact of microRNA on luciferase activity.

2.9.

## Western Blotting
Total protein was extracted from heart tissues or cultured AC16 and H9C2 cells with RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP‐40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS) supplemented with a 1× protease cocktail inhibitor (Roche). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS‐PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Thermo Scientific). Protein blots were blocked with 5% milk and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing, membranes were incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Pierce). β‐actin was used as an internal control. The antibodies used are listed in Table  .

2.10.

## Methylation Assays
In vitro and semi‐in vitro methylation assays were conducted to evaluate PRMT5‐mediated methylation of PTEN. For the in vitro assays, purified PRMT5 was prepared at a concentration of 200 ng/μL and incubated with recombinant PTEN (500 ng) in a methylation buffer consisting of 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM DTT, and 50 μM [methyl‐3H]‐S‐adenosylmethionine (3H‐SAM) at 30°C for 1 h. The reaction was terminated by adding a loading buffer, and the samples were subjected to scintillation counting to measure the incorporation of 3H‐SAM, indicating methylation of PTEN. The results were recorded on film to visualize the methylation status.
For the semi‐in vitro assays, cell lysates from AC16 and H9C2 cells subjected to H/R treatment, with or without miR‐100‐5p and PRMT5 overexpression, were prepared using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of lysate (30 μg) were incubated in the same methylation buffer with 3H‐SAM as described above, and the methylation status of PTEN was assessed by film to visualize the level of PTEN methylation under different treatment conditions.

2.11.

## Proliferation and Apoptosis Assays
Cell proliferation was assessed using the BrdU proliferation assay. AC16 and H9C2 cells were plated in 96‐well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then incubated with 10 μM BrdU for 2 h, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde. After permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X‐100, cells were treated with anti‐BrdU antibody (1:200 dilution) for 1 h. The bound antibody was detected using a secondary antibody conjugated to HRP, and a substrate solution was added for color development. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm.
Apoptosis rates were measured using the Caspase 3/7 assay kit (Promega). AC16 and H9C2 cells were seeded in 96‐well plates and treated as specified. After the treatments, Caspase 3/7 activity was quantified by adding the caspase substrate, and fluorescence was measured at 485 nm excitation and 520 nm emission, following the manufacturer's instructions.

2.12.

## Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Measurement
DCFDA (2′,7′‐dichlorofluorescin diacetate) was used to measure intracellular ROS levels. AC16 and H9C2 cells were treated with 10 μM DCFDA for 30 min at 37°C in the dark, followed by H/R treatment. After washing with PBS, fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader at 485 nm excitation and 520 nm emission. The intensity was normalized to the total protein concentration, which was determined using a BCA protein assay kit.

2.13.

## Statistical Analysis
All in vitro experiments were triplicated to ensure reproducibility and statistical significance. All data were presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10. Data were analyzed using appropriate statistical tests: unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐test for comparisons between two groups, one‐way ANOVA for comparisons among three groups, and two‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test for comparisons among more than three groups. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results
3.1.

## Upregulation of miR‐100‐5p and Downregulation of PRMT5 in Myocardial I/R Injury
Understanding the interplay between microRNAs and their target genes is crucial for elucidating the mechanisms underlying cardiovascular diseases and myocardial injury. In this study, we first investigate the expression levels of miR‐100‐5p and PRMT5 in the context of I/R injury; qPCR analysis was performed on myocardial tissue collected after 12 and 24 h of reperfusion. Results showed a significant increase in miR‐100‐5p levels and a corresponding decrease in PRMT5 expression at both time points (Figure  , n = 4/group). Western blot analysis further validated this trend, revealing reduced PRMT5 protein levels in I/R models compared to sham controls, consistent with the mRNA findings (Figure  , n = 4/group). Immunofluorescence and in situ hybridization analyses revealed the co‐localization of PRMT5 and miR‐100‐5p within myocardial tissues, indicating a potential regulatory relationship between these molecules during I/R injury (Figure  ). To extend these findings to an in vitro model, AC16 and H9C2 cells were subjected to H/R treatment. Quantitative PCR analysis indicated that, similar to the in vivo results, miR‐100‐5p levels were significantly elevated, while PRMT5 levels were notably reduced after H/R treatment (Figures  , Figures  ; n = 3/group). Western blot analysis further corroborated these findings, demonstrating decreased PRMT5 protein expression in response to H/R conditions (Figure  , Figure  ; n = 3/group).
 
3.2.

## 
MiR‐100‐5p Regulation of PRMT5 and Cardiomyocyte Viability During I/R
To further elucidate the regulatory effects of miR‐100‐5p on PRMT5 and its implications for cardiomyocyte health under ischemia/reperfusion conditions, we conducted a series of in vitro experiments using AC16 and H9C2 cardiomyocytes subjected to H/R treatment. This model allowed us to investigate the temporal dynamics of miR‐100‐5p and PRMT5 expression. We observed a dose‐dependent increase in miR‐100‐5p levels, which was associated with a corresponding, concentration‐dependent decrease in PRMT5 mRNA and protein expression, as demonstrated by qPCR and Western blot analyses (Figures  , Figures  ). A dual luciferase assay further confirmed the direct targeting of PRMT5 by miR‐100‐5p, showing reduced luciferase activity in the wild‐type PRMT5 3′‐UTR, with no changes in the mutant construct (Figure  , Figure  ). Next, we assessed the functional consequences of modulating PRMT5 expression. Western blot analysis indicated a reduction in PRMT5 levels following H/R treatment, which was reversed upon reintroducing PRMT5 into AC16 and H9C2 cells (Figure  , Figure  ). Notably, PRMT5 overexpression significantly restored cell proliferation that had been compromised during H/R (Figure  , Figure  ). A caspase 3/7 assay demonstrated that H/R treatment increased apoptosis rates, which were notably reduced by PRMT5 overexpression (Figure  , Figure  ). Similarly, H/R‐induced ROS production was significantly diminished in cells overexpressing PRMT5 (Figure  , Figure  ).
 To elucidate the underlying signaling pathways, we examined the phosphorylation status of key signaling proteins. miR‐100‐5p was shown to reduce the activation of PI3K and AKT, whereas PRMT5 overexpression reinstated their phosphorylation (Figure  , Figure  ). Finally, qPCR analysis revealed that miR‐100‐5p decreased mRNA levels of anti‐apoptotic factors B‐cell Lymphoma 2 (Bcl‐2) and B‐cell lymphoma‐extra large (Bcl‐xL) and increased the pro‐apoptotic factor Bcl‐2‐associated X protein (BAX), whereas PRMT5 overexpression restored the expression levels of these apoptotic regulators (Figure  , Figure  ).
Collectively, these results highlight a novel role of miR‐100‐5p in regulating PRMT5 expression and its downstream effects on cardiomyocyte viability, oxidative stress response, and apoptosis during I/R injury.

3.3.

## In Vivo Studies on miR‐100‐5p's Role in Myocardial I/R Injury
To translate our in vitro findings into a more physiological context, we performed in vivo experiments to investigate the role of miR‐100‐5p in myocardial I/R injury. Mice were injected with miR‐100‐5p agomir or scrambled control via tail vein injection and followed by either myocardial I/R injury or a sham operation as a control. As anticipated, miR‐100‐5p overexpression significantly reduced PRMT5 and protein levels in cardiac tissues (Figure  ). Next, we assessed the impact of miR‐100‐5p on cardiac injury markers and cardiac function. I/R injury alone impaired cardiac function considerably, and overexpression of miR‐100‐5p exacerbated this dysfunction. In parallel, serum levels of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), a marker of myocardial injury, were markedly elevated in the miR‐100‐5p agomir group compared to the I/R‐only group (Figure  ). Given the established role of apoptosis in myocardial I/R injury, we performed co‐immunostaining for TUNEL and cardiac troponin T (cTnT) to identify apoptotic cardiomyocytes specifically. Merged images confirmed that the majority of TUNEL‐positive cells were also positive for cTnT, indicating cardiomyocyte‐specific apoptosis. Quantification revealed a significant increase in apoptotic cardiomyocytes in the miR‐100‐5p overexpression group compared to the control group at 1 day (Figure  ) and 7 days post‐reperfusion (Figure  ). Cardiac function was assessed by echocardiography, which showed that miR‐100‐5p overexpression significantly exacerbated I/R‐induced cardiac dysfunction. Both ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) were markedly reduced at 1 and 7 days post‐reperfusion in the miR‐100‐5p group (Figures  ). Histological analysis at 7 days post‐I/R revealed increased myocardial damage and structural disorganization in miR‐100‐5p agomir‐treated hearts, as shown by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (top row, Figure  ). Picrosirius red staining and quantitative analysis demonstrated more pronounced collagen deposition in the perivascular regions and myocardial interstitium in the I/R + miR‐100‐5p agomir group compared to I/R‐only and sham controls (middle and bottom rows, Figures  ). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that miR‐100‐5p exacerbates myocardial injury by promoting cardiomyocyte apoptosis, worsening cardiac dysfunction, and driving fibrotic remodeling following I/R injury.
 
3.4.

## Regulation of PRMT5 and PTEN by miR‐100‐5p in I/R Injury
To investigate the role of miR‐100‐5p in enhancing I/R injury, we examined the expression and activity of PRMT5 and PTEN in AC16 and H9C2 cells subjected to H/R treatment. Western blot analysis indicated a reduction in PRMT5 levels and increased phosphorylated PTEN (p‐PTEN) levels under H/R conditions (Figure  , Figure  ). Further analysis demonstrated that miR‐100‐5p transfection led to a dose‐dependent decrease in PRMT5 protein levels and a corresponding increase in p‐PTEN level, confirming the regulatory role of miR‐100‐5p in modulating the PRMT5‐PTEN axis (Figure  , Figure  ). Quantitative PCR results further revealed that miR‐100‐5p transfection resulted in a dose‐dependent reduction of PRMT5 mRNA levels. Conversely, overexpression of PRMT5 induced a concentration‐dependent increase in PRMT5 mRNA, along with a slight decrease in PTEN mRNA (Figure  , Figure  ). This suggests that PTEN activation primarily occurs through post‐translational modifications (PTMs) rather than changes in mRNA expression. Interaction analysis confirmed that PRMT5 directly interacts with PTEN, and elevated miR‐100‐5p during H/R decreased the binding affinity between these proteins (Figure  , Figure  ). In vitro methylation assays demonstrated that PRMT5 directly methylates PTEN (Figure  ). Semi‐in vitro assays showed that H/R treatment significantly decreased PTEN methylation, with further reductions observed upon miR‐100‐5p treatment; however, PRMT5 overexpression restored PTEN methylation levels (Figure  , Figure  ). To assess the functional consequences of modulating miR‐100‐5p levels, BRDU proliferation assays indicated that antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) targeting miR‐100‐5p improved cell proliferation following H/R (Figure  , Figure  ). Additionally, caspase 3/7 assays revealed that H/R treatment increased apoptosis rates in a dose‐dependent manner, which were progressively attenuated by increasing concentrations of ASO treatment (Figure  , Figure  ). DCFDA assays confirmed that ROS levels increased dramatically after H/R treatment, and this increase was reduced in a dose‐dependent fashion by ASO treatment (Figure  , Figure  ). Western blot analysis of PTEN, PI3K, and AKT phosphorylation indicated decreased PRMT5 expression led to diminished PTEN methylation and subsequent PTEN activation during H/R, thereby suppressing PI3K‐AKT activity (Figure  , Figure  ). Notably, ASO treatment restored PRMT5 expression and reduced PTEN activation in a dose‐dependent manner. Finally, qPCR analysis of apoptotic regulators revealed that H/R treatment decreased anti‐apoptotic Bcl‐2 and Bcl‐xL levels while upregulating the pro‐apoptotic factor BAX. ASO treatment reversed these effects in a dose‐dependent manner, restoring anti‐apoptotic gene expression and attenuating BAX upregulation (Figure  , Figure  ). These findings underscore the critical role of miR‐100‐5p in enhancing I/R injury by suppressing PRMT5‐dependent PTEN methylation and activity, with significant implications for cardiomyocyte survival and function during ischemic conditions.
 
3.5.

## Therapeutic Potential of Targeting miR‐100‐5p
Given that miR‐100‐5p overexpression aggravated myocardial I/R injury, we investigated whether inhibiting miR‐100‐5p could mitigate reperfusion‐induced cardiac damage. Mice were injected with either miR‐100‐5p antagomir or control antagomir via tail vein injection, followed by I/R or Sham operation. As shown in Figures  , treatment with miR‐100‐5p antagomir significantly upregulated PRMT5 mRNA and protein levels in cardiac tissues. Cardiac echocardiography demonstrated that miR‐100‐5p inhibition significantly improved EF and FS compared to I/R controls, attenuating I/R‐induced dysfunction (Figure  ). Furthermore, serum LDH levels were markedly reduced in the miR‐100‐5p antagomir group compared to I/R controls (Figure  ). H&E staining demonstrated that I/R injury caused extensive cardiomyocyte death, and structural disruption was partially reversed by miR‐100‐5p antagomir overexpression (Figure  ). In line with the findings, co‐immunostaining for TUNEL and cardiac troponin T (cTnT) confirmed that cardiomyocyte apoptosis was significantly decreased in the miR‐100‐5p antagomir group compared to I/R‐only mice (Figures  ). Additionally, CD68 immunostaining revealed reduced macrophage infiltration in the hearts of miR‐100‐5p antagomir‐treated mice (Figure  ). Together, these findings highlight the pathological role of miR‐100‐5p in promoting myocardial injury and inflammation during I/R and demonstrate that targeting miR‐100‐5p represents a promising therapeutic strategy for reducing cardiac damage following ischemia–reperfusion.
 

4.

## Discussion
While timely revascularization is the standard treatment for AMI, the procedure paradoxically triggers additional cardiomyocyte death and worsens cardiac dysfunction [ , ]. Despite advancements in cardiovascular therapies, no effective pharmacological intervention has been established to mitigate the damage caused by I/R injury. To evaluate acute and sustained effects of reperfusion injury, we assessed cardiac function and apoptosis at 1 and 7 days post‐I/R. In this study, we identified a significant upregulation of miR‐100‐5p in myocardial tissue following I/R injury, demonstrating that miR‐100‐5p exacerbates cardiac damage in this context. Mechanistically, miR‐100‐5p targets and suppresses PRMT5 expression, disrupting PRMT5′s regulatory control over PTEN and promoting PTEN activation. Notably, inhibition of miR‐100‐5p using antisense oligomers significantly attenuated I/R‐induced cardiac injury, highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting miR‐100‐5p. These findings establish miR‐100‐5p as a key regulator of myocardial I/R injury, acting through the suppression of PRMT5 and the consequent disruption of PTEN activity. Targeting miR‐100‐5p offers a promising therapeutic strategy to protect the myocardium from I/R‐induced damage and improve clinical outcomes in patients undergoing reperfusion therapy.
MiRNAs, highly conserved small non‐coding RNAs, have been identified as key regulators of gene expression, playing a pivotal role in the development and progression of cardiovascular diseases [ ]. Their potential as biomarkers and therapeutic targets has gained increasing attention recently. During myocardial I/R injury, numerous miRNAs exhibit altered expression in cardiomyocytes, including miR‐26 and miR‐193, which have been identified as biomarkers for myocardial I/R [ , ]. Our study centered on miR‐100‐5p, revealing its significant upregulation in mice subjected to I/R and cardiomyocytes exposed to H/R conditions. Our findings demonstrate that miR‐100‐5p promotes reperfusion‐induced cardiomyocyte death, inflammation, cardiac dysfunction, and fibrotic remodeling. Moreover, the role of miR‐100‐5p has been widely explored in the context of other human diseases. For instance, miR‐100‐5p released from apoptotic cortical neurons is an endogenous Toll‐like receptor 7/8 ligand contributing to neurodegeneration [ ]. Furthermore, miR‐100‐5p regulates various cancers [ , , ], highlighting the crucial functions of miR‐100‐5p in human pathological conditions.
To explore the therapeutic potential of targeting miR‐100‐5p, we employed miR‐100‐5p antagomir and demonstrated that inhibiting miR‐100‐5p significantly reduced myocardial I/R injury. This protective effect was achieved by restoring the expression of PRMT5 and mitigating the downstream consequences of PRMT5 suppression on PTEN activation. Together, our study identifies miR‐100‐5p as a promising therapeutic target for reducing myocardial injury during I/R, expanding the growing body of evidence supporting the therapeutic utility of miRNA modulation in cardiovascular diseases. While the in vitro experiments revealed statistically significant yet moderate changes in miR‐100‐5p and PRMT5 expression, the effects observed in vivo were more substantial. This discrepancy likely reflects the complex and integrated nature of the in vivo cardiac environment, where multiple cell types, including immune cells, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts, interact dynamically during I/R injury. Inflammatory mediators, neurohumoral signaling, and paracrine communication may further amplify the pathological effects of miR‐100‐5p dysregulation in vivo.
Although our findings demonstrate a significant role for miR‐100‐5p in promoting cardiomyocyte injury following I/R, it is important to consider that multiple cardiac and immune cell populations may contribute to miR‐100‐5p expression in vivo. Prior studies have shown that miR‐100‐5p is expressed not only in cardiomyocytes [ ] but also in endothelial cells [ ] and activated macrophages [ ]. These non‐cardiomyocyte sources could amplify local or systemic miR‐100‐5p‐mediated effects via paracrine signaling or immune modulation. Furthermore, systemic delivery of miR‐100‐5p agomirs or antagomirs may impact multiple cardiac cell types, contributing to the broader physiological consequences observed in vivo. Future single‐cell profiling studies will be valuable in delineating the specific cellular origins and context‐dependent regulation of miR‐100‐5p during myocardial ischemia–reperfusion. These considerations further underscore the translational significance of miR‐100‐5p as a multifaceted therapeutic target for myocardial protection.
In our investigation into the mechanisms by which miR‐100‐5p exacerbates myocardial I/R injury, we identified PRMT5 as a direct target of miR‐100‐5p. PRMT5 is a key enzyme that catalyzes the symmetric dimethylation of arginine residues on target proteins [ ]. This PTM entails adding methyl groups to specific arginine residues, thereby affecting the function, localization, and interaction of the modified proteins [ , ]. PRMT5 is widely recognized for its role in methylating histone proteins, which are essential for maintaining chromatin structure and regulating gene expression [ ]. By modifying histones like H2A, H3, and H4, PRMT5 regulates chromatin compaction, thereby controlling the accessibility of transcriptional machinery to DNA [ , , ]. Beyond histones, PRMT5 also methylates a wide range of non‐histone proteins, affecting their function, stability, and interactions. For example, PRMT5 methylates the tumor suppressor p53, modulating its transcriptional activity and influencing key cellular processes like apoptosis and cell proliferation [ , ]. Although PRMT5 has been extensively studied in cancer biology, where it influences tumor growth and progression, recent research has highlighted its critical functions in cardiovascular biology. In the context of cardiovascular disease, PRMT5 plays a critical role in regulating processes such as vascular development, cardiomyocyte survival, and the response to ischemic injury [ , , ]. In glucose metabolism, in vivo experiments showed that islet‐specific knockout of Prmt5 reduced insulin gene expression and impaired glucose tolerance and glucose‐stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) test [ ]. DM is a significant risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, especially AMI, where it substantially increases the risk of morbidity and mortality [ , , ]. Therefore, miR‐100‐5p may serve as a key regulator in DM, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target to reduce myocardial damage and improve outcomes in diabetic patients undergoing revascularization.
In further exploring the downstream signaling pathways through which PRMT5 modulates myocardial I/R injury, we identified PTEN as a direct target of PRMT5. Our findings demonstrate that PRMT5 directly binds to and methylates PTEN. Moreover, we confirmed that PRMT5 downregulates PTEN activity, and interestingly, this effect was significantly reversed by overexpression of miR‐100‐5p mimics, which attenuated the reduction in phosphor‐PTEN levels induced by PRMT5 overexpression. PTEN is a crucial phosphatase that dephosphorylates phosphatidylinositol‐3,4,5‐trisphosphate (PIP3), a lipid second messenger generated by PI3K, thereby inhibiting the activation of AKT [ ]. As a result, PTEN serves as a key negative regulator of AKT signaling, a pathway that plays a vital role in promoting cell survival and preventing apoptosis, especially under stress conditions like myocardial I/R injury [ , ]. During myocardial I/R injury, PTEN acts as a central modulator of cellular responses to ischemic stress. By inhibiting AKT activation, PTEN facilitates the expression of pro‐apoptotic proteins such as BAX while simultaneously suppressing anti‐apoptotic factors like Bcl‐2, leading to cardiomyocyte death and myocardial damage [ , ]. By restricting AKT activity, PTEN disrupts critical cellular survival pathways, resulting in heightened cell death and prolonged inflammatory responses that further aggravate tissue damage. Notably, we observed these regulatory effects at 1 and 7 days post‐reperfusion, indicating that miR‐100‐5p‐mediated PTEN‐AKT modulation contributes to early‐phase apoptosis and sustained myocardial dysfunction. As a result, the PTEN‐AKT signaling pathway is recognized as a key regulator of cell survival, apoptosis, and inflammation during myocardial I/R injury. Our findings reveal that miR‐100‐5p aggravates myocardial I/R injury by targeting PRMT5, which in turn influences PTEN activity and downstream AKT signaling. This positions miR‐100‐5p as a critical mediator in the progression of myocardial I/R injury by regulating the PRMT5‐PTEN‐AKT axis.

## Conclusion
This study uncovers key molecular mechanisms underlying the role of miR‐100‐5p in myocardial I/R injury. Our findings reveal that miR‐100‐5p contributes to early and sustained cardiac injury by downregulating PRMT5, which leads to increased PTEN activity and disruption of the PI3K‐AKT signaling pathway. This chain of events promotes cardiomyocyte apoptosis, oxidative stress, and myocardial damage. Notably, blocking miR‐100‐5p with antisense oligomers restores PRMT5 expression, reduces myocardial injury, and enhances cardiomyocyte survival. These results highlight the role of miR‐100‐5p as a critical regulator of myocardial I/R injury and suggest that targeting this microRNA could be a promising therapeutic strategy to alleviate reperfusion‐induced cardiac injury. Such an approach may have significant clinical implications for improving outcomes in patients undergoing revascularization procedures like CABG and PCI.
