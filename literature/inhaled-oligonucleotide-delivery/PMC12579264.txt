## Introduction
Bronchial asthma (BA), commonly termed asthma, is a highly heterogeneous chronic respiratory disorder characterized by recurrent wheezing, dyspnea, chest tightness, and cough. As the most prevalent non-communicable chronic disease in children, asthma affects approximately 334 million individuals globally, with its prevalence continuing to rise . Allergic asthma, the most extensively studied subtype, occurs in both pediatric and adult populations. Despite advances in understanding its immunopathological mechanisms, critical knowledge gaps persist in disease management and therapeutic targeting.
Emerging evidence underscores microRNA (miRNA or miR) as promising biomarkers for asthma . MiRNAs are small non-coding RNAs ubiquitously present in eukaryotes, including plants, animals, and humans, which post-transcriptionally regulate gene expression. Since the landmark discovery of the first miRNA in Caenorhabditis elegans (1993) and the subsequent identification of human let-7 miRNA , miRNA research has rapidly expanded into diverse physiological and pathological processes. In respiratory medicine, dysregulated miRNA expression has been implicated in multiple lung diseases, including asthma , .
Current asthma diagnosis primarily relies on clinical symptom assessment and exclusion criteria, lacking reliable molecular biomarkers with high reproducibility and specificity. Multiple studies have revealed that miRNAs participate in asthma pathogenesis by regulating target genes. In this study, we employed Illumina high-throughput sequencing to conduct exploratory analysis of miRNA expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from asthmatic children, followed by systematic screening and experimental validation. Using animal models and in vitro experiments, we preliminarily investigated the underlying mechanisms, thereby providing novel insights for clinical and basic research on childhood asthma.

## Materials and methods

## Collection of specimens
5 asthmatic children and 5 healthy children were enrolled in the Department of Respiratory Medicine, Children’s Hospital Affiliated to Soochow University. Peripheral venous blood samples were collected for miRNA high-throughput sequencing. In addition, 48 children with asthma who were admitted to the respiratory department of our hospital were selected, and 20 healthy children who underwent physical examination at the same time were selected as controls. qPCR was used to verify the sequencing results. Within 24 h after admission, 2 mL peripheral blood was collected from the children and the normal control group by EDTA-K2 anticoagulant blood collection tube, and mononuclear cells were isolated within half an hour. This study was approved by the Ethics committee of the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University(Clinical Ethics Number: 2020CS100). All procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki, and relevant national regulations. We confirm that the informed consent of all subjects and/or their legal guardians has been obtained.
This study adheres to the STROBE guidelines for observational studies. The completed checklist is provided as Supplementary File 1.

## Animal experiments
Seven-week-old BALB/c mice (SPF grade, female), weighing about 18 g, were purchased from Zhaoyan (Suzhou) New Drug Research Center Co., LTD., and fed routinely. The animal experiment operators held the certificate of laboratory animal Practitioners in Jiangsu Province. All surgical procedures were performed under 50 mg/kg pentobarbital anesthesia, and maximal efforts were taken to minimize suffering. Euthanasia was carried out by dislocation of the cervical spine following AVMA Guidelines. Each animal experiment complied with the protocols approved by the Ethics Committee of Suzhou University (No. SUDA20210512A01). And the study protocol was conducted in accordance with ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments) guidelines. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

## Isolation of peripheral blood mononuclear cells
Mononuclear cell isolation solution (stemcell technology) was stabilized to room temperature (15–25℃) before use. 3mL of the solution was added to the test tube, and the blood was diluted with an equal volume of PBS buffer containing 2% fetal bovine serum. Add the diluted blood to the test tube containing the separation solution with minimal mixing; Centrifuge at 800 g for 20 min at room temperature and turn off the brake. The upper liquid layer was removed, the mononuclear cell layer was aspirated, and the mononuclear cells were washed once with medium. Finally, 2mL RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa) was added to the extracted mononuclear cells to lyse the cells, and the cells were stored in the refrigerator at -70℃ for further use.

## Total RNA isolation
A 1/5 volume of RNAiso Plus chloroform was added to the above cell lysate and mixed upside down until the solution was completely emulsified. Stand at room temperature for 5 min, centrifuge at 12,000 g at 4℃ for 15 min, suck the supernatant into another centrifuge tube, add the same volume of isopropanol as the supernatant just sucked out, mix thoroughly, and place at room temperature for 10 min. Centrifugation: 12,000 g at 4 ° C for 10 min, RNA; The supernatant was discarded, and the same amount of 75% ethanol was added to the centrifuge tube. The RNA precipitate was washed twice and centrifuged at 7500 g for 5 min at 4 ° C. An appropriate amount of ribonuclease free water was added to dissolve the precipitate.

## High-throughput microarray sequencing
Agarose gel electrophoresis was used to observe the extracted total RNA bands, and Nanodrop was used to measure 260/280, 260/230 and concentration. The library was constructed, and the quality of the library was identified by Agilent 2100 Bioanalysis system. Fifty-one cycles of sequencing were performed on an Illumina NextSeq 500 according to protocol.

## RT-qPCR

## RT-qPCR detection of volunteer specimens
The All-in-OneTM miRNA qRT-PCR Detection Kit 2.0 (Genecopiea) was used to detect miRNA in peripheral blood mononuclear cells of 48 asthmatic children and 20 healthy children. Primer sequences are shown in Table  . (1) cDNA Preparation: Template RNA was thawed on ice, while 5× PAP/RT Buffer II and ddH₂O (enzyme- and DNase-free) were thawed at room temperature (15–25 °C). All RNA reagents were gently mixed by flicking, followed by brief centrifugation. The reverse transcription reaction was assembled in RNase-free tubes on ice using 1 µg RNA, 1 µL of 2 U/µL Poly A Polymerase, 1 µL SureScript™ RTase Mix (20×), 4 µL of 5× PAP/RT Buffer, and ddH₂O to a final volume of 20 µL. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 60 min and then at 85 °C for 5 min. The cDNA product was diluted fivefold with sterile ddH₂O to 100 µL and stored at − 20 °C. (2) Real-Time qPCR: A primer mix was prepared with 38 µL forward primer (2 µM) and 38 µL universal reverse primer (2 µM); 2 µL of this mix was used per reaction. Each 10 µL qPCR reaction contained 5 µL of 2× All-in-One qPCR Mix, 1 µL miRNA forward primer (0.2 µM final), 1 µL universal adaptor PCR primer (0.2 µM final), 1 µL cDNA, and 2 µL ddH₂O. The plate was centrifuged at 1900 rpm for 3 min before running. Amplification was performed with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 55 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 20 s.

 

## RT-qPCR detection for animals and in vitro experiments
Reverse Transcription of miRNA to cDNA : cDNA synthesis for miRNA was performed using the miRNA 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (by stem-loop) (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To remove genomic DNA, a reaction mixture consisting of RNase-free ddH₂O, 5× gDNA Wiper Mix, and total RNA (10 pg–1 µg) in a total volume of 10 µL was gently mixed and incubated at 42 °C for 2 min. For first-strand cDNA synthesis, 10 µL of the previous reaction mixture was combined with stem-loop primer (2 µM), 10× RT Mix, HiScript II Enzyme Mix, and RNase-free ddH₂O to a final volume of 20 µL. The synthesis was carried out under the following thermal conditions: 25 °C for 5 min, 50 °C for 15 min, and 85 °C for 5 min.
Reverse Transcription of mRNA to cDNA : Reverse transcription of mRNA was conducted using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd). Genomic DNA was removed by preparing a mixture containing RNase-free ddH₂O, 4× gDNA Wiper Mix, and template RNA (1 pg–1 µg) in a total volume of 16 µL, followed by incubation at 42 °C for 2 min. For cDNA synthesis, 16 µL of this mixture was mixed with 4 µL of 5× HiScript III qRT SuperMix. The reverse transcription reaction was performed at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by heat inactivation at 85 °C for 5 s.

## Quantitative PCR (qPCR) reaction
The qPCR reaction was prepared according to the following protocol. A mixture consisting of 7.0 µL of RNase-free ddH₂O, 10.0 µL of SybrGreen qPCR Master Mix (2×), 1.0 µL of Forward Primer (10 µM), 1.0 µL of Reverse Primer (10 µM), and 1.0 µL of cDNA template was thoroughly mixed in a PCR tube, yielding a total reaction volume of 20.0 µL. After mixing, the reaction solution was aliquoted into a 96-well plate with three technical replicates per sample. The plate was sealed with a transparent film and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 2 min at 4 °C to remove air bubbles and ensure consistent sample distribution. Amplification was performed under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 600 s (1 cycle), followed by 50 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 32 s. A dissociation curve analysis was subsequently carried out by heating to 95 °C for 10 s, cooling to 65 °C for 60 s, and reheating to 95 °C for 15 s. A final incubation at 40 °C for 30 s concluded the run.
QPCR primer sequences are shown in Table  . Mouse miRNAs were searched for sequences at https://www.mirbase.org/ and primers were designed using Vazyme miRNA primer design software. miR-31-5p reverse transcription primer sequence: 5’-GTCGTATCCAGTGCAGGGTCCGAGGTATTCGCACTGGATACGACCAGCTA; miR-224-5p reverse transcription primer sequence: 5’-GTCGTATCCAGTGCAGGGTCCGAGGTATTCGCACTGGATACGACAACGGA, miR-134-5p reverse transcription primer sequence: 5’-GTCGTATCCAGTGCAGGGTCCGAGGTATTCGCACTGGATACGACCCCCTC, miR-139-3p reverse transcription primer sequence: 5’-GTCGTATCCAGTGCAGGGTCCGAGGTATTCGCACTGGATACGACCTCCAA.

 

## Establishment of OVA-induced allergic inflammation mouse model
Mouse were intraperitoneally injected with 200 µl OVA sensitization solution on day 0, 7, and 14, respectively. From the 21st day, the mouse were aerosolized with 3%OVA aerosol solution with the assistance of an ultrasonic nebulizer for at least 30 min each time for 7 days. Peripheral blood and lung tissues were collected 24 h after the last challenge. Control mouse were treated with PBS according to the same procedure.

## Noninvasive pulmonary function test in mouse
The mouse were aerosolized with 0,6.25, 12.5, 25, and 50 mg/mL Methacholine (Mch) in turn. The inhalation time was 1 min, and the reaction time was 3 min. The airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) of mouse was measured. The mean value of reaction time enhanced expiratory pause (Penh) at each concentration was used as an index to evaluate AHR. Penh and classical pulmonary function tests were highly correlated, Penh = PEF/PIF(PEF stands for peak expiratory flow, PIF stands for peak inspiratory flow.

## Pathological section of lung tissue from mouse
The lung tissues of mouse were immediately fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, and the paraffin sections were prepared by dehydration, transparency, wax immersion, embedding, sectioning, and drying. Hematoxylin and eosin staining and Periodic Acid-Schiff stain were performed, respectively.

## In vitro experiments with A549 cells
A549 cells (5 × 105) were induced by 10 µg/well HDM, and the cells were collected 24 h later. qPCR was used to detect the mRNA levels of miR-31-5p, TBXA2R, CCL11, CCL24, and CCL26.

## Report of the dual luciferase assay
293T cells (from ATCC), A549 cells (from ATCC) were routinely cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS (1.5 mg/L Glutamine, 100 U/ml Penicillin, 100 µg/ml Streptomycin). Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 saturated humidity incubator. Transfection treatments were performed according to Table  grouping.

 

## Western blot
Total protein was extracted, and concentrations were normalized across groups using 1× PBS. Samples were mixed with 4× SDS loading buffer, denatured at 100 °C for 10 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 1 min. Supernatants were loaded onto 10% SDS-PAGE gels. Proteins were separated using vertical electrophoresis (80 V, 30 min; 120 V, 70 min). PVDF membranes (Millipore) were activated in methanol. Gels, filter papers, and membranes were equilibrated in Transfer Buffer to assemble the transfer sandwich. Proteins were transferred at 80 V for 90 min. Membranes were blocked for 2 h, incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-TBXA2R antibody (ab134959, Abcam; 1:1000), washed with PBST (3 × 10 min), incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (111-035-003, Jackson ImmunoResearch; 1:10,000) for 1 h at room temperature, and washed again. Signals were detected using ECL substrate on a Tanon 4200 system (Tanon Science & Technology, China). Band intensities were quantified via densitometry using Gel-Pro Analyzer software (Media Cybernetics, USA).

## Statistical analysis
The data were analyzed and plotted by Graphpad Prism(v10, https://www.graphpad.com) and SPSS(v30.0, https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics). All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The differences between two experimental groups were evaluated using the student t-test, and the differences between more than two experimental groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The data obtained from the volunteer specimens were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney test. the Receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) was used to calculate the Area under the curve (AUC) of the corresponding variable, and the maximum Youden index was the best sensitivity and specificity. ## Results

## High-throughput sequencing was used to analyze the differential expression of MiRNAs in peripheral blood between asthmatic and healthy children
This result was obtained through high-throughput sequencing with a limited sample size (n = 5), mainly as an exploratory screening to identify dysregulated candidate miRNAs in asthma. Given the inherent technical variability of high-throughput sequencing and the risk of false positives (especially low-abundance transcripts), these results need to be independently verified and should be interpreted as hypothesis production rather than clear evidence of biological relevance. The differentially expressed miRNAs were identified using the thresholds of |log2FC| ≥log2(1.5) (a 1.5-fold change), a P-value ≤ 0.05, and an average CPM value ≥ 1 within the group. These differentially expressed miRNAs were then visualized using a clustered heatmap and a volcano plot to display the differences (Fig.  A, B). GO enrichment analysis revealed that target genes of upregulated miRNAs were associated with 1,441 biological processes (BP), 199 cellular components (CC), and 254 molecular functions (MF) (Fig.  C). For downregulated miRNAs, target genes were linked to 936 BP, 145 CC, and 119 MF terms (Fig.  D). KEGG pathway analysis further demonstrated that upregulated miRNA targets were enriched in 73 signaling pathways, while downregulated miRNA targets participated in 37 pathways (Fig.  E, F).

 

## Verification and analysis of the main differentially expressed MiRNAs
Based on sequencing and the previous experimental results of our research group, we selected 10 miRNAs exhibiting significant intergroup differences and low intragroup variability, including five upregulated miRNAs (hsa-miR-193b-3p, hsa-let-7 g-3p, hsa-miR-31-5p, hsa-miR-4521, hsa-miR-106a-3p) and five downregulated miRNAs (hsa-miR-432-5p, hsa-miR-382-5p, hsa-miR-134-5p, hsa-miR-224-5p, hsa-miR-139-3p) in asthmatic children (Fig.  A). RT-qPCR validation was performed in an expanded cohort of PBMCs from 48 asthmatic children and 20 healthy controls. Notably, our qPCR validation revealed that miR-31-5p and miR-106a-3p were downregulated in children with asthma, which contrasts with our initial high-throughput sequencing results. In contrast, miR-134-5p and miR-224-5p were significantly upregulated (P < 0.05), while the other six miRNAs showed no significant differential expression (P > 0.05) (Fig.  B). To further validate these findings, an OVA-induced allergic inflammation mouse model was established. Histopathological analysis of lung tissue sections revealed pronounced inflammatory cell infiltration in alveolar spaces (Fig.  D, HE staining, 200×) and marked airway mucus hypersecretion with goblet cell hyperplasia (Fig.  D, PAS staining, 200×) in the asthma group compared to controls. The expression of miR-31-5p in PBMC of OVA-sensitized mouse decreased (P < 0.01), while the expressions of miR-134-5p and miR-224-5p increased (P < 0.05), which was consistent with the results of studies on children. However, miR-106a-3p expression was not detected in the mouse model (Fig.  C).

 

## Expression analysis of some MiRNAs and verification of the role of miR-31-5p
To investigate the correlation between differentially expressed miRNAs in children’s peripheral blood mononuclear cells and clinical history, asthmatic children were stratified by disease severity (mild, moderate, severe), family history of rhinitis/allergy, personal allergy history, and gender. Comparative analysis revealed no statistically significant associations between these clinical variables and miRNA expression levels (P > 0.05) (Fig.  A). ROC curve analysis of the four differentially expressed miRNAs (miR-134-5p, miR-224-5p, miR-106a-3p, and miR-31-5p) demonstrated AUC values ranging from 0.7 to 0.9 (Table  ). Notably, miR-31-5p exhibited the highest AUC (0.864; 95% CI = 0.761–0.967), with optimal sensitivity (0.800) and specificity (0.894) at a cut off value of 0.00079, suggesting its pivotal role in asthma progression. In the OVA-induced allergic inflammation mouse model, tracheal intubation was performed to administer saline (NC group) or lentiviral miR-31-5p (miR-31-5p group), alongside healthy controls (Control group). Histopathological analysis of lung tissues revealed severe inflammatory cell infiltration, mucus hypersecretion, and goblet cell hyperplasia in the NC group (Fig.  C, HE and PAS staining, 200×). These pathological features were markedly attenuated in the miR-31-5p-treated group. Pulmonary function tests demonstrated dose-dependent increases in Penh values with escalating Mch concentrations. At Mch = 50 mg/ml, the NC group exhibited significantly higher Penh values compared to both the Control and miR-31-5p-treated groups (P < 0.05) (Fig.  D, E).

 

 

## Determination of target genes of miR-31-5p
Given the potential critical role of miR-31-5p in asthma pathogenesis, we screened its target genes using miRDB, miRWalk, and TargetScan 7.2 databases, yielding 595, 47,350, and 477 potential targets, respectively. Intersection of these datasets yielded 140 candidate targets. Among these, the top 20 predicted targets aligned with high-scoring candidates identified via high-throughput sequencing (Table  ). Three asthma-relevant genes were prioritized: sphingomyelin synthase 1 (SGMS1), SH2 domain-containing protein 1 A (SH2D1A), and thromboxane A2 receptor (TBXA2R). Transcriptional analysis of these targets in pediatric peripheral blood revealed significant upregulation of TBXA2R in asthmatic children compared to healthy controls (P < 0.0001), whereas SH2D1A exhibited an inverse trend. No significant differences were observed for SGMS1 (Fig.  A). Given the reduced expression of miR-31-5p in asthmatic children, we focused on TBXA2R for mechanistic exploration. Dual-luciferase reporter assays robustly validated TBXA2R as a direct target of miR-31-5p. In the wild-type TBXA2R 3’UTR group, miR-31-5p transfection significantly reduced luciferase activity, indicating direct binding. Conversely, no suppression occurred in the mutant TBXA2R 3’UTR group or in cells co-transfected with non-specific miRNA (miR-NC) (Fig.  B, C). These findings confirm that miR-31-5p directly binds to the TBXA2R 3’UTR to suppress its expression.

 

 

## The expression of miR-31-5p in A549 cells was down-regulated by HDM and IL-4, while the expression of TBXA2R and eosinophilic chemokines was increased
It is well-established that eosinophil chemokines (CCL11, CCL24, CCL26) produced by airway epithelial cells play a critical role in asthma pathogenesis. Previous studies have demonstrated that miR-31-5p is also expressed in epithelial cells. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that both house dust mite (HDM) and interleukin-4 (IL-4) stimulation significantly reduced miR-31-5p expression in A549 cells compared to controls (HDM: P < 0.05; IL-4: P < 0.01), with IL-4 exhibiting a more pronounced suppressive effect (Fig.  ). These findings align with results from human specimens and in vivo animal experiments. Furthermore, IL-4 stimulation significantly upregulated the expression of TBXA2R and eosinophil chemokines (CCL11, CCL24, CCL26) in A549 cells (P < 0.05). In contrast, HDM stimulation increased CCL11 and CCL26 expression (P < 0.05), while no statistically significant differences were observed in TBXA2R and CCL24 expression compared to controls.

 

## MiR-31-5p regulates the expression of TBXA2R and eosinophilic chemokines in A549 cells
As shown in Fig.  and A represents the densitometric analysis of Fig.  B. Compared to the control group, both IL-4 and HDM, either alone or in combination, significantly increased TBXA2R protein expression in A549 cells. Conversely, miR-31-5p stimulation markedly reduced TBXA2R expression (P < 0.01). Furthermore, the IL-4 + miR-31-5p and HDM + miR-31-5p groups exhibited statistically significant decreases in TBXA2R levels compared to their respective miR-31-5p-free groups (P < 0.01) (Fig.  A, B). Similarly, in the IL-4 + miR-31-5p treatment group, mRNA expression of eosinophil chemokines CCL11 (P < 0.01), CCL24 (P < 0.05), and CCL26 (P < 0.01) was significantly reduced compared to the IL-4 + non-targeting control miRNA (miR-NC) group(Fig.  C).

 

## Discussion
Over the past three decades, clinicians and researchers have gained substantial insights into the characteristics and pathophysiological mechanisms underlying various asthma subtypes, leading to the development of enhanced diagnostic and therapeutic strategies to support personalized interventions. In recent years, the role of miRNA in asthma pathogenesis has garnered increasing attention. miRNAs are endogenous, evolutionarily conserved non-coding regulatory small RNA molecules that play critical roles in post-transcriptional regulation of cellular signaling pathways .
We initially identified candidate miRNAs through exploratory screening using high-throughput sequencing. While this approach provides broad coverage, it carries substantial risks of technical artifacts and false discoveries, particularly given the limited sample size. Consequently, these data primarily serve to generate mechanistic hypotheses rather than establish causal relationships. Clustering heatmaps and volcano plots (Fig.  A and B) were generated using a predefined differential expression threshold of |fold change| ≥ 1.5. GO enrichment analysis revealed miRNA target genes associated with metabolic regulation, cell differentiation, transcriptional control, and nervous system development (Fig.  C, D). KEGG pathway analysis highlighted involvement in Th1/Th2/Th17 cell differentiation, PI3K-Akt signaling, Ras signaling, and HIF-1 signaling pathways (Fig.  E, F) , . Studies have shown that the differentiation of Th1/Th2 and Th17 plays an important role in the immunopathological mechanism of asthma. For example, miR-21, miR-221, miR-145, etc. can affect the occurrence and development of asthma immune inflammation by regulating the differentiation of Th1 and Th2 , .
Based on literature review and preliminary experimental data from our group, we selected ten miRNAs exhibiting pronounced inter-group differences and minimal intra-group variation for further validation using RT-qPCR with an expanded sample size. The results demonstrated that, compared to the healthy control group, asthmatic children exhibited decreased expression of miR-31-5p and miR-106a-3p, increased expression of miR-134-5p and miR-224-5p, and no significant changes in the remaining miRNAs (Fig. B). In contrast, sequencing data indicated upregulation of miR-193b-3p, miR-let-7 g-3p, miR-31-5p, miR-4521, and miR-106a-3p, along with downregulation of miR-432-5p, miR-382-5p, miR-134-5p, miR-224-5p, and miR-139-3p in asthma patients (Fig. A). This discrepancy may be attributed to multiple factors: the limited sample size used for high-throughput sequencing—only 5 asthmatic and 5 healthy children—which provides insufficient statistical power to reliably detect hundreds of differentially expressed miRNAs, thereby constraining the robustness of the findings; technical biases during library preparation, specifically the use of a dual-adapter ligation method that is known to introduce significant bias in the detection of low-abundance miRNAs ; differences in statistical methods between the miRNA-seq and validation experiments; biological heterogeneity among individual samples, in which bulk sequencing may average out opposing expression trends across unrecognized subpopulations ; and potential dynamic temporal regulation of miRNA expression, as the miRNA-seq and validation assays were conducted at different time points following treatment. Notably, the qPCR findings for miR-31-5p were consistent with subsequent results in this study and were normalized to a stable reference gene (U6), supporting their reliability. Its observed downregulation at later stages suggests potential negative feedback regulation, possibly mediated by target mRNA accumulation. Future studies analyzing time-course expression and epigenetic modifiers may help resolve this kinetic paradox. Although the discordance between the two platforms requires further experimental validation, the qPCR data strongly support the relevance of these miRNAs as candidate mediators of the observed phenotypic effects.
We assessed the effects of asthma severity, family history of allergic rhinitis or atopy, personal allergy history, and sex on the expression of differentially expressed miRNAs validated by qRT-PCR. Analysis revealed no significant influence of these clinical parameters on miR-31-5p, miR-106a-3p, miR-134-5p, or miR-224-5p expression under the current sample conditions (Fig.  A). Asthma is a highly heterogeneous disease. The clinical parameters we used (e.g., self-reported allergy history) may not fully capture the underlying molecular endotypes associated with miR-31-5p expression. The consistent dysregulation of miR-31-5p in children with asthma—independent of the specific clinical parameters we tested—may suggest that it plays a more fundamental and universal role in the core pathophysiology of asthma (e.g., regulating eosinophil chemotaxis via TBXA2R), rather than serving as a biomarker for specific clinical subtypes or severity levels. Its role may be more evident at the functional level rather than as a marker of circulating severity. ROC analysis revealed that miR-31-5p exhibited the largest AUC value, underscoring its pivotal role in asthma progression (Table  ; Fig.  B), a finding corroborated by murine model studies. In our OVA-induced allergic inflammation mouse model, peripheral blood analysis demonstrated reduced miR-31-5p expression in OVA-challenged mice compared to PBS controls (Fig.  C), consistent with prior reports. Intratracheal delivery of miR-31-5p lentivirus attenuated OVA-induced airway hyperresponsiveness and inflammation (Fig.  C–E), further validating its therapeutic potential.
In this study, we observed downregulated expression of miR-31-5p in children with asthma. However, other studies have reported elevated miR-31 levels in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) from asthmatic children, where it contributes to disease progression by modulating inflammatory factors . miR-31-5p has also been shown to be overexpressed and play similar roles in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and allergic disorders. Nevertheless, the same literature notes that one previous study found slightly lower miR-31-5p expression in airway epithelial brushings from asthma patients compared to healthy controls . Further review reveals that miR-31-5p is an inflammation-associated miRNA implicated in various diseases including autoimmune disorders, metabolic diseases, and tuberculosis. For example, miR-31-5p is downregulated in PBMCs of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) patients, where it targets SLC15A4 to activate the IRF5 pathway, promoting IFN-α and ISG expression ; restoration of miR-31-5p can suppress excessive interferon responses. It has also been proposed as a diagnostic biomarker for pediatric tuberculosis, with significantly lower expression in patients accompanied by elevated levels of innate immune cytokines including IL-6, TNF-α, NF-κB, and IFN-γ . Conversely, miR-31-5p exhibits opposing roles in other conditions. Prospective data indicate that miR-31-5p is markedly overexpressed in the skin of hidradenitis suppurativa patients compared to healthy controls, with higher levels in lesional versus peri-lesional tissue . Similarly, it is upregulated in ulcerative colitis (UC), where its antisense oligonucleotide can shift macrophages from pro-inflammatory M1 to anti-inflammatory M2 polarization, enhance intestinal barrier function, and suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activation . These findings underscore the multifunctional nature of miR-31-5p across diverse diseases, highlighting its potential as both a diagnostic biomarker and a therapeutic target. Given these complex and context-dependent roles, we conducted preliminary investigations to elucidate how miR-31-5p may contribute to asthma pathogenesis in our experimental system.
Based on integrated analyses of miRDB, miRWalk, TargetScan, and high-throughput sequencing data, we identified three Th1/Th2 differentiation-associated target genes: TBXA2R, SH2D1A, and SGMS1. Transcript levels of these genes were measured in human PBMC specimens (Table ; Fig. A). Asthmatic samples exhibited downregulated SH2D1A and upregulated TBXA2R expression. Given the reduced miR-31-5p levels in asthmatic children, we focused on TBXA2R, a member of the seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) superfamily that modulates diverse downstream cascades, including calcium influx, cell migration, proliferation, and apoptosis . TBXA2R is highly expressed in thromboxane A2-targeted tissues, such as erythroleukemia cells, vascular and bronchial smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, and epithelial cells . Thromboxane A2 activates TBXA2R in bronchial smooth muscle cells, triggering intracellular calcium mobilization, bronchoconstriction, smooth muscle hyperplasia, and airway remodeling—key drivers of chronic airway inflammation in asthma . Studies have shown that ramatoban acting on TBXA2R on the surface of Th2 cells can prevent allergic rhinitis in OVA-sensitized mouse. Other data indicate that the strong inhibitory effect of TBXA2R signaling on the weak binding capacity of CD4 + T cells -DC cells helps to improve the overall quality of adaptive immune response , . In this experiment, the dual-luciferase report confirmed the targeted negative regulatory effect of miR-31-5p on TBXA2R (Fig. B, C). Therefore, we have reason to propose that miR-31-5p may play a certain role in childhood asthma by inhibiting the expression of TBXA2R.
Eosinophil chemokines (CCL11, CCL24, CCL26) produced by airway epithelial cells are well-established mediators of asthma pathogenesis . Notably, miR-31-5p is also expressed in epithelial cells . To investigate its regulatory role, we stimulated A549 cells with IL-4 and HDM. Both stimuli significantly reduced miR-31-5p expression compared to controls. As anticipated, IL-4 enhanced eosinophil recruitment and suppressed apoptosis, concomitant with marked overexpression of CCL11, CCL24, and CCL26 (Fig. ). Intriguingly, IL-4 exerted stronger pro-inflammatory effects than HDM, likely attributable to its direct cytokine-driven mechanisms. Furthermore, IL-4- and HDM-stimulated A549 cells exhibited elevated TBXA2R expression. This upregulation stems from miR-31-5p’s negative regulatory role: IL-4/HDM-induced miR-31-5p downregulation attenuates its suppression of TBXA2R, thereby amplifying receptor responsiveness.
To further validate our hypothesis, we conducted rescue experiments in A549 cells. Protein-level analysis demonstrated that both IL-4 and HDM significantly induced TBXA2R expression. However, miR-31-5p supplementation markedly attenuated this upregulation, partially confirming its suppressive role on TBXA2R (Fig. ). Notably, co-treatment with IL-4 and miR-31-5p resulted in significantly lower expression of eosinophil chemokines (CCL11, CCL24, CCL26) compared to IL-4 alone. These findings align with prior reports indicating that TBXA2R activation in IL-4-primed bronchial smooth muscle cells drives eosinophil accumulation and robust chemokine release in asthmatic airways . Given the confirmed negative regulatory relationship between miR-31-5p and TBXA2R (Fig. B, C), we propose that miR-31-5p modulates eosinophil chemokine production in airway epithelial cells via TBXA2R suppression, thereby influencing asthma progression. Although the A549 cell line serves as a valuable tool for studying alveolar epithelial responses, we acknowledge its limitations in modeling asthma pathogenesis, as it cannot replicate the complex immunopathology of asthmatic airways. Asthma primarily involves proximal airways, and as a transformed alveolar type II-like cell line, A549 lacks key features of asthmatic bronchial epithelium. Despite this, their well-documented utility in modeling chemokine production by respiratory epithelial cells and high genetic tractability make them a suitable system for dissecting the miR-31-5p/TBXA2R axis—a core regulatory mechanism further confirmed in primary bronchial cells and patient-derived samples. To better validate the role of the miR-31-5p/TBXA2R axis in asthmatic immunopathology, we plan to pursue further studies using air-liquid interface (ALI) cultures of primary human bronchial epithelial cells (pHBECs) from asthmatic donors.
In this study, RT-qPCR was employed to validate candidate miRNAs in both clinical samples and a murine asthma model, with a focused investigation on the role of miR-31-5p in asthma pathogenesis. We demonstrated that miR-31-5p directly targets TBXA2R. In vitro experiments using IL-4 and HDM-stimulated A549 cells showed decreased expression of miR-31-5p, accompanied by increased TBXA2R levels and enhanced eosinophil chemokine responses, supporting the involvement of the miR-31-5p/TBXA2R axis in asthmatic immunopathology. We acknowledge several limitations of this study. Firstly, due to the relatively small sample size used for high-throughput sequencing, miRNA screening must be regarded as exploratory, and the expression differences observed between children with asthma and the control group require further verification through external experiments and longitudinal data. Secondly, due to the limitations of time and resources, functional verification mainly focused on miR-31-5p, while other promising candidates (miR-106a-3p, miR-134-5p and miR-224-5p) were not tested. Solving these limitations will be the main focus of our future research.

## Conclusion
MiR-31-5p is downregulated in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of children with asthma and regulates the production of eosinophil chemokines in A549 through its negative regulation of TBXA2R.
