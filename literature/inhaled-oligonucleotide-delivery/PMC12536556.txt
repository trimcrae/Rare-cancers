## Abstract
Acute lung injury (ALI) and its severe form, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), are critical conditions characterized by disruption of the alveolar-capillary barrier, uncontrolled inflammation, and progressive hypoxemia, with mortality rates exceeding 40 %. While conventional therapies such as mechanical ventilation and glucocorticoids can partially alleviate symptoms, they face limitations, including low drug delivery efficiency, systemic toxicity, and inability to precisely regulate pathological microenvironments. Nanoparticles (NPs), which leverage their unique size effects, surface modifiability, and multifunctional integration capabilities, demonstrate advantages in targeted delivery, reduced systemic side effects, and multifunctional therapeutic potential and are emerging as novel tools to overcome current therapeutic bottlenecks. This review systematically summarizes the classification, mechanisms of action, and applications of NPs in ALI/ARDS treatment, while critically evaluating translational challenges and future development trajectories.
Keywords: ALI, Nanoparticles, Targeted, Anti-inflammatory, Antioxidant, Tissue regeneration

1.

## Background
Acute lung injury (ALI) and its severe form, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), which represents different stages of the same disease continuum, have been described clinically for decades. In 1994, the American-European Consensus Conference Committee established the first unified definition for ARDS/ALI, outlining three core diagnostic criteria: 1) acute onset of bilateral diffuse pulmonary infiltrates confirmed by chest X-ray; 2) oxygenation index (PaO2/FiO2) thresholds (ALI ≤300 mmHg, ARDS ≤200 mmHg); and 3) pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) ≤ 18 mmHg or clinical exclusion of left atrial hypertension. These criteria enable clinicians to diagnose the condition via routine tests (chest X-ray and arterial blood gas) [ ]. Subsequent studies revealed that 29 % of ALI patients presented PAWP >18 mmHg, with most having normal cardiac output, suggesting that pulmonary edema might stem from fluid overload during resuscitation rather than from cardiogenic factors [ ]. Therefore, echocardiography is recommended to exclude cardiac-related edema. To enhance objectivity, the 2012 Berlin definition reclassified ARDS into three categories based on PaO2/FiO2 (mild ≤300 mmHg, moderate ≤200 mmHg, severe ≤100 mmHg), replacing the ALI terminology with a general descriptive framework [ ]. In 2023, the Berlin criteria were expanded to include non-intubated ARDS and resource-limited ARDS diagnosis, retaining the three-tier severity system and introducing the SpO2/FiO2 ratio as an auxiliary metric. The Berlin criteria were explicitly defined for intubated ARDS patients [ ]. Despite ongoing refinements, ARDS remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients.
ARDS incidence varies significantly across studies. One study reported rates of 79/100,000 for mild ARDS and 59/100,000 for moderate/severe ARDS [ ]. However, more recent data indicate a declining U.S. incidence from 81/100,000 in 2001 to 38/100,000 in 2008 [ ]. A recent multinational prospective study conducted across 459 ICUs in 50 countries revealed that ARDS was present in 10.4 % of ICU admissions and 23.4 % of mechanically ventilated patients, with a severity distribution of 30.0 % mild, 46.6 % moderate, and 23.4 % severe ARDS [ ].
The mortality rate associated with ARDS remains high, with hospital mortality of 43 % at 90 days for moderate to severe cases [ ]. Although distinguishing the direct fatality proportion of ARDS patients from risk factors and comorbidities is challenging, the mortality rate for patients with sepsis-associated ARDS is estimated to range from 27 % to 37 % [ ]. Notably, during outbreaks of respiratory infectious diseases, ARDS-related mortality among ICU patients has increased significantly, reaching 67 %–85 % in the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic and 52.2 % during the 2002–2003 severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) outbreak [ , ]. Although most ARDS survivors regain normal or near-normal lung function, many continue to experience functional limitations related to muscle weakness, reduced physical capacity, or sequelae of critical illness [ , ]. Cognitive impairment is also highly prevalent, affecting nearly half of survivors after 2 years [ , ]. These studies demonstrate that ARDS has become a global health challenge, and effectively preventing its progression, reducing mortality, and improving patient prognosis remain challenging tasks.
The pathophysiological process of ALI begins with the effects of various direct or indirect injurious factors, including acute pulmonary ischemia-reperfusion (I/R), infection, mechanical ventilation-associated injury, shock, trauma, and transfusion-related complications. These pathogenic triggers induce dual pathological processes-inflammatory cascade activation and alveolar-blood barrier disruption-leading to abnormal elevation of pulmonary microvascular and alveolar wall permeability, thereby causing diffuse interstitial and alveolar edema ( ) [ , ]. These pathological alterations trigger acute hypoxic respiratory insufficiency or even respiratory failure, ultimately progressing to ARDS. The pathogenesis of ARDS involves multiple pathway interactions, primarily encompassing several core mechanisms: energy metabolism disturbances due to mitochondrial dysfunction, elevated oxidative stress and proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α and IL-6), dysregulation of vascular endothelial permeability, abnormal activation of apoptotic pathways, initiation of endoplasmic reticulum stress signaling, and impaired autophagic clearance [ ]. These mechanisms act synergistically or independently to induce structural damage to the lung parenchyma and gas exchange dysfunction, forming the hallmark pathological features of ARDS.
 Clinically, mechanical ventilation and pharmacological therapy are the primary approaches for managing ALI, yet both treatments have significant side effects [ ]. Mechanical ventilation, a cornerstone therapy for ALI, uses increased ventilators to improve gas exchange in lung tissue, alleviate respiratory distress, and increase pulmonary compliance. However, abnormal gas distribution between alveoli and the lung interstitium during ventilation may induce ventilator-associated lung injury, exacerbating disease progression [ , ]. Pharmacological interventions for ARDS primarily target pathophysiological processes, including inflammation suppression (corticosteroids, statins, interferon-β, and sivelestat), pulmonary edema reduction (pulmonary surfactants, β2-agonists), selective vasodilation promotion (e.g., nitric oxide), and alveolar epithelial and vascular endothelial repair facilitation (e.g., keratinocyte growth factor) [ ]. The unique physiological barriers of the lungs result in relatively low drug delivery efficiency, leading to markedly reduced therapeutic efficacy and numerous adverse effects that limit clinical application [ ]. For example, low-dose NO inhalation or intratracheal surfactant administration effectively improves oxygenation in ALI patients but fails to significantly reduce mortality [ , ]. Prolonged antibiotic use may also induce drug resistance and gastrointestinal complications, with therapeutic responses resulting in significant heterogeneity among ARDS patients [ ].
In recent years, the medical roles of NPs have attracted widespread attention. NPs can increase the solubility of insoluble drugs, improve drug bioavailability, achieve accumulation at pathological sites, prolong circulation time, and enable controlled-rate drug delivery. Owing to their artificially modifiable surface properties, NPs can accumulate in inflamed lungs through passive, active, or physicochemical targeting strategies, thereby enhancing drug efficacy and significantly reducing side effects [ , ]. Additionally, NPs can be loaded with multiple drugs, enabling synergistic effects through multiple pharmacological mechanisms [ , ]. With a deeper understanding of ALI pathogenesis, various drug-loaded or nondrug-loaded NPs based on lipids, peptides, organic macromolecules, extracellular vesicles, and inorganic nanoparticles have been developed [ , , , ]. These materials can specifically target the lungs to address the pathophysiological characteristics of ALI and enable the development of immunomodulatory therapies.
This review systematically elaborates on the unique advantages of NPs in ALI treatment, focusing on the mechanisms and therapeutic efficacy of various NPs, including recent advancements in liposomes, polymeric micelles, and metal nanoparticles. It also objectively discusses current challenges, such as in vivo metabolic safety, environmental toxicity, and clinical translation barriers, providing theoretical foundations for future applications of nanoparticle in clinical treatment of respiratory diseases.

2.

## Overview of the nanoparticles
In recent years, the rapid development of nanotechnology has led to new ideas and methods for the treatment of ALI. Owing to their unique physicochemical properties and biocompatibility, NPs have become crucial tools in drug delivery, targeted therapy, and pulmonary disease treatment. The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) defines NPs as nano-objects with all the external dimensions at the nanoscale, where the lengths of the longest and shortest axes of the NPs are not significantly different [ ]. NPs are nanostructures with dimensions up to 100 nm that are applicable in all directions (x, y, and z) [ ]. They possess a large specific surface area and unique surface effects, enabling them to carry substantial amounts of drug molecules. Morphologically, NPs can exist in loose or agglomerated forms, crystalline structures with single or polycrystalline solids, or irregular shapes [ ]. They exhibit diverse shapes, sizes, and structures: spherical, cylindrical, conical, tubular, hollow low-core, helical, etc [ ]. Currently, numerous reviews have documented NP applications across various fields, including food science, drug delivery, antimicrobial activity, and theranostics [ , , ]. In particular, in medicine, NPs have emerged as vital carriers for drug delivery systems, diagnostic imaging, and therapy because of their excellent biocompatibility and targeting capabilities. NP formulations integrating diagnostic and therapeutic agents have been comprehensively applied to various diseases, such as cancer, neurological disorders, antimicrobial therapy, cardiovascular diseases, liver diseases, and even kidney conditions [ , ]. However, the widespread use of NPs has also raised concerns about their biosafety and environmental impacts. Although many NPs demonstrate outstanding performance, their potential toxicity to organisms and long-term environmental effects require further evaluation [ ]. Therefore, balancing the functionality of NPs while minimizing their adverse effects on organisms and the environment remains a critical challenge in current research.
2.1.

## Classification
As depicted in , NPs are generally classified into three categories based on their composition: organic, inorganic, and carbon-based [ ].
 2.1.1.

## Organic nanoparticles
This category of NPs is composed of proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, polymers, or other organic compounds, typically with particle sizes ranging from 1 to 200 nm ( A) [ ]. Their structure and function can be flexibly modulated through chemical modifications and processing techniques. The materials and types are classified into polymeric nanoparticles, lipid nanoparticles, and biomacromolecular nanoparticles.
Polymeric nanoparticles are composed of natural or synthetic macromolecules. Commonly used polymers include PLA, PLGA, PEG, and chitosan, making them among the most widely applied organic NPs [ ]. They are morphologically categorized into nanospheres and nanocapsules, each exhibiting distinct drug delivery mechanisms. Nanospheres, which exhibit high drug-loading efficiency, feature solid spherical matrices with homogeneously dispersed drug molecules via adsorption or encapsulation and are suitable for both hydrophilic and hydrophobic small-molecule therapeutics. In contrast, nanocapsules feature a core-shell structure in which active compounds are encapsulated within a liquid-solid core surrounded by a polymeric shell. This architecture enables controlled drug release through adjustable shell characteristics, such as biodegradability and material composition, and allows precise regulation of release kinetics while maintaining compound stability.
Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) are nanocarriers prepared from lipid materials and are categorized into liposomes and solid lipid nanoparticles. Liposomes are vesicular structures formed by phospholipid bilayers and contain a hydrophilic core and hydrophobic bilayer membrane capable of encapsulating both water-soluble and lipid-soluble drugs [ ]. Solid lipid nanoparticles are composed of solid lipid matrices coated with emulsifiers or surfactants, offering high physical stability and controlled-release properties [ , ].
Biomacromolecular nanoparticles are composed of natural biomacromolecules, offering excellent biocompatibility and specific biological functions, including peptide-based NPs, protein NPs, and polysaccharide NPs. Peptide-based NPs are self-assembled or synthetically prepared nanoparticles formed by amino acid chains via peptide bonds. The biocompatibility and biodegradability of these materials make them suitable for drug delivery, targeted therapy, and bioimaging. Protein NPs derived from proteins, such as gelatin or albumin, are used in drug delivery and diagnostic imaging. Polysaccharide NPs, including chitosan and hyaluronic acid, are widely studied for their biocompatibility and immunomodulatory functions [ ]. Among the most representative examples are dendrimers, micelles, liposomes, and ferritin [ ]. These materials are biodegradable and non-toxic. Micelles and liposomes, classified as nanocapsules with hollow interiors, are sensitive to heat and electromagnetic radiation [ ]. These NPs, which are constructed from natural or synthetic macromolecules, allow structural and functional tuning via simple chemical modifications. Owing to these properties and advantages, NPs have immense potential in precision medicine, such as for targeted drug delivery and cancer therapy [ , ].

2.1.2.

## Inorganic nanoparticles
Inorganic nanoparticles are particles of inorganic materials with sizes ranging from 1 to 100 nm and are typically composed of metals, metal oxides, semiconductors, or their composite materials ( B). Inorganic nanoparticles exhibit unique physicochemical properties, such as quantum size effects, surface effects, and large specific surface areas, enabling their widespread application in energy, catalysis, environmental remediation, and biomedicine [ ]. The materials are categorized into metal nanoparticles, metal oxide nanoparticles, and semiconductor nanomaterials, according to their material composition.
Metal NPs are composed of single metallic elements (e.g., gold, silver, copper) and exhibit excellent UV–visible light sensitivity and electronic, catalytic, and antibacterial properties [ ]. Additionally, some metal NPs possess unique thermal, magnetic, and biological characteristics, making them critical materials for nanodevice fabrication [ ]. Gold nanoparticles (AuNPs) exhibit exceptional optical characteristics and biocompatibility, enabling significant utilization in tumor photothermal therapy, drug delivery systems, and biosensing technologies [ ]. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) are widely employed in medical device coatings and antimicrobial applications because of their broad-spectrum antibacterial efficacy [ ]. Platinum nanoparticles (PtNPs), recognized for their superior catalytic activity, show promising potential in electrochemical energy conversion devices, such as fuel cells and antioxidative therapeutic interventions.
Metal oxide nanoparticles, which are typically composed of compounds such as titanium dioxide (TiO2), zinc oxide (ZnO), and iron oxide, derive their structural stability from strong ionic interactions between positively charged metal ions and oxygen anions [ ]. Iron oxide nanoparticles are extensively employed as contrast agents in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and have led to significant advancements in magnetic-targeted tumor therapy [ ]. Titanium dioxide and zinc oxide nanoparticles exhibit notable photocatalytic activity for organic pollutant degradation while also serving as functional components in commercial products such as sunscreens and antimicrobial formulations [ ].
Semiconductor nanomaterials, consisting of semiconductor elements or compounds, such as silicon, zinc oxide, and cadmium sulfide, exhibit electronic properties intermediate between those of nonmetals and metals, rendering them critical components in optoelectronic, optical, and electronic device engineering [ , ]. Silicon nanoparticles exhibit notable biocompatibility, chemical stability, and surface functionalization capabilities, which facilitate their broad utilization in drug delivery, gene transport, and theranostic applications [ ]. Mesoporous silica nanoparticles, characterized by their porous architectures and high drug-loading efficiency, further exemplify the biomedical versatility of this material class.

2.1.3.

## Carbon-based nanoparticles
Nanoparticles composed entirely of carbon are termed carbon-based nanoparticles [ ]. Common carbon-based nanoparticles include carbon nanotubes, graphene, fullerenes, and their derivatives (e.g., graphene oxide and multi-walled carbon nanotubes) ( C). Carbon nanotubes are one-dimensional nanomaterials formed by carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal honeycomb lattice and rolled into hollow tubular structures. They can be classified into single-walled nanotubes and multi-walled nanotubes based on the number of layers. Owing to their high surface area, mechanical strength, excellent conductivity, and chemical stability, these materials have broad application prospects in electronics, energy, and biomedicine [ ]. Fullerenes, which are composed of 60 carbon atoms arranged in a soccer ball-like structure, exhibit unique electronic properties and biocompatibility [ , ]. Graphene and its oxide derivatives are widely used in drug carriers, gene delivery, and antimicrobial material development because of their excellent biocompatibility and functional ability [ ]. Additionally, carbon-based nanoparticles have significant applications in catalysis, energy storage, and environmental pollution remediation, such as enhancing catalytic reaction rates for water pollution treatment [ ]. With advancing research, the multifunctionality and diversity of carbon-based nanoparticles will play an increasingly vital role in future nanotechnology applications.

2.1.4.

## Nanozymes
Unlike the compositional categories discussed previously, nanozymes represent a functional class of NPs defined by their intrinsic enzyme-mimicking catalytic activities ( D). Since the term was first introduced in 2004, this rapidly evolving interdisciplinary field has generated thousands of research outputs [ ].These materials-which may be organic, inorganic, or carbon-based-catalyze biochemical reactions through mechanisms such as electron transfer, surface adsorption, and substrate-active site interactions [ ].
Oxidoreductase-like nanozymes functionally emulate natural oxidoreductase enzymes through catalytic redox reactions. Peroxidase-mimicking nanozymes, such as iron oxide nanoparticles and carbon quantum dots, catalyze substrate oxidation in the presence of hydrogen peroxide, enabling applications in tumor therapy and biosensing platforms [ ]. Oxidase-like nanozymes, including cerium oxide and silver oxide nanoparticles, directly employ molecular oxygen as an electron acceptor during catalytic processes. Additionally, superoxide dismutase (SOD)-mimetic nanozymes (e.g., manganese oxide nanoparticles) facilitate the disproportionation of superoxide anions, contributing to anti-inflammatory and antioxidant therapeutic strategies.
Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are porous crystalline materials formed by coordination between metal ions and organic ligands. Owing to their high surface area, tunable pore structures, and multifunctional active sites, MOFs are ideal for developing nanozymes [ ]. Oxidoreductase-like MOF nanozymes utilize metal nodes as redox-active centers for electron transfer. Hydrolase-like MOF nanozymes leverage abundant metal nodes and pores to mimic natural hydrolases, e.g., phosphatase-like MOFs hydrolyze phosphate esters to degrade organophosphorus pesticides. Multifunctional MOF nanozymes exhibit dual enzymatic activities under different conditions. For example, Fe-Zr-MOFs demonstrate both peroxidase-like and oxidase-like activities for disease diagnosis and biosensing.

2.2.

## Advantages
Pneumonia and its sequelae, such as ALI, pose unique challenges for treatment and critical care clinicians. These challenges have garnered widespread attention because of the persistent impact of SARS-CoV-2 in recent years [ ]. As an emerging therapeutic platform, NPs present significant advantages over other methods, demonstrating broad therapeutic potential, particularly in ALI. In recent years, NPs have been gradually applied to ALI treatment, as this approach enhances drug targeting and biosafety [ ]. Common nanoparticles used for ALI therapy include liposomal nanoparticles, peptide-based nanoparticles (PNPs), inorganic nanoparticles, and organic nanoparticles. Owing to their size effects and surface modifiability, NPs can carry multiple drugs for targeted delivery, improving therapeutic efficiency. Additionally, NPs can be directly delivered to lesion sites via the airway, reducing systemic drug side effects by bypassing metabolic pathways.
Various nanoparticles (e.g., liposomes and polymeric nanoparticles) have shown efficacy in animal models for suppressing inflammation, oxidative stress, and alveolar epithelial damage. Gan Zhao et al. achieved precision treatment of viral pneumonia via macrophage-targeting lipid nanoparticles (MacLNPs), which significantly enhanced macrophage-targeted delivery efficiency and improved viral lung injury through in vivo siRNA delivery, providing a reference for future macrophage-targeted therapies [ ]. Jiayang Yan et al. developed molybdenum nanodots (MNDs), leveraging their ultrasmall size, excellent biocompatibility, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging capacity to alleviate pulmonary oxidative stress and inflammation in mice, suggesting that these materials are promising therapeutic agents for ALI [ ]. Furthermore, liposomal nanoparticles, which maintain drug stability during systemic circulation through structural encapsulation, effectively prevent premature release before cellular uptake and exploit the inflammation and increased vascular permeability typically associated with ALI to achieve targeted lung injury therapy [ , ]. PNPs also enable targeted drug delivery, and their biodegradable nature allows natural decomposition and absorption postdelivery, reducing long-term toxicity risks [ ]. For inorganic nanoparticles, their lung-targeting mechanisms often rely on combining physicochemical properties with biological strategies, with surface modifications enhancing their targeting capabilities [ ].
In summary, nanoparticles provide precise and efficient approaches for treating lung injury, with promising prospects for clinical application. With the continuous advancement of nanotechnology, the design and preparation of nanoparticles will become more refined and safer, offering the potential for further development in treating ALI, various pulmonary diseases, and even other tissue pathologies in the future.

3.

## Mechanism of the role of nanoparticles in the treatment of ALI
3.1.

## Targeted drug delivery to injured sites
In the clinical treatment of ALI, achieving drug delivery to the lesion area represents a core challenge for enhancing therapeutic efficacy. Owing to their unique physicochemical properties and functional designs, NPs enable accurate delivery of therapeutic agents to ALI lesions, significantly improving drug bioavailability while reducing systemic toxicity [ , ]. Current strategies for targeting nanoparticles focus on mitigating excessive inflammatory responses and restoring pulmonary function through the inhibition of inflammatory cells, neutralization of toxins and cytokines, reduction of inflammatory mediators, and restoration of the alveolar-blood barrier ( A). The targeted delivery mechanisms can be categorized primarily into passive targeting ( B) and active targeting ( C), with therapeutic effects further enhanced by optimizing the enhanced permeability and retention effect. In addition, pulmonary delivery of nanoparticles has also been exploited in ARDS treatment by leveraging direct interactions with the lung environment and abnormal pathological conditions ( D).
 Passive targeting refers to the natural accumulation of nanoparticles in the lungs through systemic circulation, leveraging the organ's distinct physiological and pathological features (e.g., dense capillary networks, high perfusion rates, and increased vascular permeability in inflammatory regions). This process is governed by physicochemical parameters such as nanoparticle size, morphology, and surface charge. Studies indicate that nanoparticles smaller than 100 nm more readily penetrate pulmonary vascular endothelial cell gaps for tissue retention, with 50-nm spherical particles demonstrating significantly greater lung-targeting efficiency than 200-nm particles [ ]. Notably, particles in the 50–100 nm range effectively penetrate endothelial barriers while avoiding rapid renal clearance of smaller particles (<10 nm), exhibiting superior enrichment in inflammatory regions [ ]. Additionally, surface charge characteristics influence particle retention through electrostatic interactions. Positively charged particles show markedly greater cellular uptake efficiency and lung-targeting capabilities than their neutral or negatively charged counterparts because of interactions with negatively charged cell membranes [ ].
Active targeting strategies employ surface modification of nanoparticles with specific ligands (e.g., antibodies, peptides, carbohydrates) to achieve selective binding with overexpressed receptors in lung tissues (e.g., ICAM-1, VCAM-1), thereby enabling drug delivery to pathological sites. In ALI therapy, this approach primarily targets critical components such as endothelial cells, neutrophils, macrophages, and damaged mitochondria [ ]. Experimental data revealed that anti-ICAM-1 antibody-modified nanoparticle significantly enhance endothelial cell targeting efficiency, whereas formyl peptide-modified particles increase neutrophil uptake [ , ]. These findings provide critical theoretical foundations for developing high-efficiency pulmonary drug delivery systems.
Physicochemical targeting delivery has also been widely employed in the treatment of ARDS, targeting the inflammatory microenvironment characterized by extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, overproduction of ROS and proteases, and a lower pH. Research has demonstrated that nanoparticles fabricated from polydopamine (PDA), which possesses free radical scavenging capability, significantly ameliorate lung injury by exerting notable anti-inflammatory effects through direct reaction with ROS and decomposition of hydrogen peroxide [ ]. Additionally, pH-responsive polymeric micelles have been designed for ARDS therapy; this novel system enables the encapsulation of hydrophobic TPCA-1 and facilitates the responsive release of the drug under acidic conditions [ ].

3.2.

## Improvement of drug penetration and retention effects
In the treatment of ALI, it is critical for therapeutic efficacy that drugs effectively penetrate pulmonary lesion tissues and maintain sufficient residence time at the target site. Owing to their unique physicochemical properties, NPs exhibit significant advantages in enhancing drug penetration and retention effects.
3.2.1.

## Mechanisms for enhancing drug penetration
NPs markedly improve drug penetration into lung tissue through multiple mechanisms, thereby increasing the drug concentration at lesion sites and improving therapeutic outcomes. First, the controllability of their particle size and surface charge facilitates their penetration through the alveolar epithelial barrier into inflammatory regions. In addition, the sustained release properties of the nanoparticles extended the retention time of the drugs in the lungs, providing continuous therapeutic support for ALI.
Concerning the regulation of physical properties, the size and morphology of nanoparticles play a decisive role in their penetration capability. Differences in particle size can significantly alter their physicochemical properties and in vivo behavior [ ]. NPs of smaller size (typically less than 100 nm) can more readily traverse the inter endothelial gaps of pulmonary capillaries and the tight junctions between alveolar epithelial cells, thereby achieving effective penetration into lung tissue. Studies have shown that NPs in the 30–80 nm size range can effectively cross pulmonary capillary endothelial gaps and alveolar epithelial tight junctions, achieving optimal distribution in both the alveolar cavity and lung interstitium [ ]. Morphological studies demonstrated that high-aspect-ratio rod-like/filamentous particles traverse biological barriers more efficiently than conventional spherical particles do. In macrophage phagocytosis models, elongated particles exhibit a 40 % reduction in the cellular uptake rate. Conversely, acute inflammation models reveal enhanced selective phagocytosis of rod-shaped polymeric particles by neutrophils. This differential phagocytic behavior indicates the potential for targeted delivery strategies in neutrophil-dominated inflammatory pathologies [ ].
Moreover, modifying the nanoparticle surface by conjugating hydrophilic polymers, targeting ligands, or functional groups can alter the surface properties, thereby increasing the cell affinity and penetration capability. Modifications with polyethylene glycol (PEG), fluorinated groups, and other materials can overcome the mucus barrier and improve tissue penetration. Specifically, the hydrophilic “cloud-like” structure formed by PEGylation can reduce nonspecific protein adsorption on nanoparticles, decrease clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system, prolong circulation time, and increase the likelihood of reaching pulmonary lesion sites [ ]. Recent studies have demonstrated that NPs with dual modifications of PEG and fluorination enhance transmucosal transport efficiency, providing an innovative solution to overcome multiple pulmonary biological barriers [ ].

3.2.2.

## Strategies for enhancing drug retention effects
To ensure that drugs exert long-lasting effects at pulmonary lesion sites, enhancing the lung retention of nanoparticles is of paramount importance [ ]. By optimizing the physicochemical properties of nanoparticles and designing appropriate drug delivery systems, their lung residence time can be effectively prolonged, thereby enabling precise controlled release.
Among the regulatory strategies, improving the physicochemical properties of nanoparticles is a key approach to extending lung retention. An increase in the hydrophilicity of nanoparticles can significantly slow their clearance from the lung. By modifying the nanoparticle surface with hydrophilic groups or polymers, their dispersibility and stability within lung tissue can be improved. Studies have shown that NPs coated with hydrophilic chitosan exhibit lung retention times several times longer than those of unmodified particles. This is primarily attributed to chitosan, a natural amino polysaccharide that possesses excellent biocompatibility and controllable degradation properties; positively charged chitosan nanoparticles (CSNPs) can form strong interactions with red blood cells [ , ]. Based on this property, researchers have successfully developed a red blood cell-chitosan nanoparticle system (RBC-MPSS-CSNP) loaded with methylprednisolone succinate (MPSS), to prolong drug circulation time, promoting targeted accumulation in the lung, and simultaneously avoiding capture by the reticuloendothelial systems of the liver and spleen [ ].
Furthermore, adjusting the surface charge of NPs can also influence their retention time in the lung. By modulating the surface potential, NPs can generate electrostatic attraction with the surface charges of lung epithelial cells, thereby enhancing retention. Comparative studies on charged nanoparticle carriers have shown that positively charged carriers accumulate significantly more in lung tissue than negatively charged carriers do, with a corresponding improvement in cellular uptake efficiency [ , ]. This difference is attributed to the fact that cationic materials can interact with anionic components of lung tissue (such as pulmonary surfactants) via passive targeting mechanisms [ , ]. The negatively charged nanoparticles gelated the lipid membrane locally, whereas the positively charged nanoparticles enhanced their fluidity. The results show that surface charge is the key factor regulating the biological effects of nanoparticles [ ].

3.2.3.

## Reducing systemic toxicity and increasing local efficacy
In the clinical treatment of ALI, traditional drugs such as dexamethasone sodium phosphate, a representative glucocorticoid, are widely used in inflammatory disease management because of their potent anti-inflammatory effects [ ]. Although this drug has shown potential value in treating ARDS, its low selectivity often leads to systemic adverse effects at high doses [ ]. Studies have confirmed that glucocorticoids may induce systemic side effects such as neutrophilia, osteoporosis, hyperglycemia, and gastrointestinal bleeding, with infection risk being positively correlated with dosage. These toxic effects may be amplified, particularly in ARDS patients with multiple organ dysfunction [ , ]. Therefore, developing targeted drug delivery systems to increase therapeutic precision and reduce systemic toxicity has become a research priority in this field.
The emergence of nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems has overcome the limitations of traditional drugs. First, NPs can effectively protect drug stability, improve pharmacokinetic properties, and prevent toxicity caused by free drug accumulation in nontarget organs [ ]. For example, researchers developed iRGD peptide-modified liposomes encapsulating nimbolide (iRGD-NIMLip), which significantly increased pulmonary drug accumulation and reduced systemic distribution compared with traditional dexamethasone [ ]. Similarly, L-arginine-functionalized curcumin-loaded liposomes targeting macrophage-specific receptors demonstrated precise targeting of M1 macrophages, enhancing curcumin enrichment in the lungs while reducing organ accumulation [ ]. Pulmonary surfactant protein A (SP-A) antibody-modified dexamethasone-loaded immunoliposomes (SPA-DEX-NLP) improved dexamethasone release efficiency by 40-fold and significantly lowered free drug concentrations in circulation [ ]. Neutrophil membrane-coated liposomes encapsulating αFGF (αFGF@NMLs) utilize biomimetic recognition to prolong pulmonary retention and prevent multiorgan diffusion [ ].
Second, the intelligent drug-release properties of NPs mitigate the risks of high peak concentrations associated with traditional administration. For example, pH-sensitive poly(β-amino ester) (PBAE)-modified daptomycin liposomes (Spe@HNPs) trigger drug release in the acidic microenvironment of inflamed tissues, reducing nonspecific exposure in healthy tissues. The positively charged PBAE surface also disrupts bacterial cell walls, alleviating bacterium-induced ARDS [ ]. Mannosylated ciprofloxacin liposomes achieve gradient drug release via lung-specific receptor-mediated endocytosis, significantly reducing hepatic and renal accumulation [ ].
Additionally, NPs can integrate multiple therapeutic components to enable combination therapy through a single system, enhancing drug delivery capacity via synergistic codelivery and reducing polypharmacy toxicity. Current combination strategies focus on integrating anti-inflammatory or antioxidant drugs, including dexamethasone, resveratrol, curcumin, and LPS-binding peptides, with gene therapy [ , , , ]. Compared with that of PEI, the R7L10 peptide system, which delivered curcumin and plasmid DNA, presented greater pulmonary uptake efficiency and lower cytotoxicity [ ]. The pHO-1/PEI-DA/LBP ternary complex more effectively reduced TNFα, IL-6, and IL-1β levels than did LBP or pHO-1/PEI-DA alone [ ]. Compared with lipofectamine and PEI, R3V6 peptide micelles/complexes, which are formed via charge interactions with siRNA, demonstrated superior siRNA delivery efficiency, synergistically alleviating inflammation [ ].
Despite their reduced systemic toxicity, the clinical translation of nanoparticles faces challenges. For example, combining multiple drugs in NPs to achieve synergy complicates scaled production and quality control. Some nanoparticles may activate inflammatory pathways, exacerbating inflammation. Although nanomedicine improves drug delivery efficiency, only ∼1 % of intravenously administered nanoparticles reach tumor sites, highlighting persistent off-target effects [ ]. Thus, optimizing precise targeting strategies to enhance drug delivery remains a critical need.

3.3.

## Antiinflammatory and antioxidant effects
The core pathological features of ALI and ARDS are dysregulated pulmonary inflammatory responses, characterized by cytokine storms, inflammatory cell infiltration, and alveolar-interstitial protein edema, ultimately leading to diffuse lung damage. Studies indicate that nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) activation is significantly elevated in the alveolar macrophages of ARDS patients, a mechanism linked to oxidative stress induction and Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-mediated recognition of microbial ligands such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [ , ]. Activated NF-κB exacerbates M1 macrophage-driven inflammatory injury by increasing the levels of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8) and promoting the production of reactive ROS, including SOD and hydrogen peroxide [ ]. NPs have a unique potential for anti-inflammatory and antioxidant regulation through targeted delivery and functional design.
3.3.1.

## Targeting the suppression of inflammatory signaling pathways
NPs exert inhibitory effects by delivering anti-inflammatory molecules or directly intervening in inflammatory signaling pathways, such as the NF-κB pathway. Studies demonstrate that TLR4 is highly expressed in inflammatory cells during viral infections, and its elevated levels correlate with exacerbated ALI induced by viral infections and acid insult models [ , ]. NF-κB is significantly activated in ALI models, and its mediated release of inflammatory cytokines-including specific adhesion molecules such as CD11b/CD18 and ICAM-1-aggravates ARDS [ , ]. Notably, NF-κB inhibitors reduce serum levels of TLR4 and NF-κB, inhibit LPS-induced inflammation, which is crucial for mitigating LPS-ALI [ , , ].
Intratracheal delivery of cerium oxide nanoparticles (CNPs) conjugated with anti-inflammatory miR-146a significantly suppresses TGF-β, IL-6, and IL-8, alleviating coronavirus-associated ARDS-induced pulmonary fibrosis [ ]. PEG-coated AuNPs inhibit NF-κB upregulation and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS)/nitric oxide (NO) production in LPS-stimulated macrophages; their intraperitoneal administration reduces proinflammatory cytokines (IFN-γ and IL-6), NO levels, and reverses LPS-induced fibrosis [ , ]. Li et al. developed SP-A nanobody-modified liposomes to specifically target type II alveolar epithelial cells, delivering methylprednisolone to suppress NF-κB signaling and significantly mitigating bleomycin-induced lung injury without off-target effects [ ]. Mannose receptor-targeted liposomes co-deliver dexamethasone to dually inhibit NF-κB activation and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) phosphorylation, thereby reducing inflammatory cytokine release and apoptosis [ ]. Resveratrol-loaded nanosystems exert therapeutic effects by inhibiting the NF-κB and PI3K/Akt-MAPK pathways [ , ]. Additionally, pH-responsive NPs (e.g., poly(β-amino ester)) enable triggered release of IKK-2 inhibitors in inflammatory microenvironments, effectively reducing IL-6 and TNF-α levels [ ]. These studies highlight the multidimensional regulatory potential of nanoparticles in modulating inflammatory pathways.

3.3.2.

## Macrophage polarization regulation
Phenotypic imbalance (proinflammatory M1/anti-inflammatory M2) in alveolar macrophages is pivotal to ALI progression. During the early stages, pathogen-associated molecular patterns activate M1 polarization via TLRs, driving proinflammatory cytokine release and neutrophil recruitment [ , ]. In the resolution phase, M2 polarization promotes tissue repair [ ]. Nanoparticle systems modulate disease progression by targeting macrophage polarization. For example, hexapeptide-modified AuNPs are internalized by alveolar macrophages, attenuating TLR4 signaling cascades to inhibit NF-κB, reduce inflammation, and promote M2 polarization [ ]. Ding et al. developed pH-sensitive β-cyclodextrin-based nanosystems to deliver miR-223 to M1 macrophages [ ]. Sun et al. designed soft liposomes (PSLipos-L-NAC) to prolong macrophage interactions, significantly enhancing lung repair [ ].

3.3.3.

## ROS scavenging and mitochondrial function restoration
Mitochondrial dysfunction is closely linked to ALI. NPs alleviate oxidative stress and restore mitochondrial function by mimicking natural enzyme activity or delivering antioxidants. CNPs leverage their multivalent redox states to scavenge ROS and stabilize biologics such as microRNAs [ ]. Porous Se@SiO2 nanospheres exert antioxidant effects by stabilizing the mitochondrial membrane potential, increasing respiratory chain activity, and sustainably releasing selenium [ , ]. Jin et al. designed sialic acid-modified microspheres to block mitochondrial apoptosis via ROS scavenging while inhibiting NF-κB signaling [ ]. Li et al. engineered single-atom Pt/CeO2 catalysts to regulate mitochondrial metabolic pathways, promoting ATP synthesis and ROS clearance [ ].
In summary, current nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems combat ALI through three mechanisms: 1) modulating inflammatory signaling pathways (e.g., NF-κB and TLR); 2) reprogramming macrophage polarization, and 3) restoring mitochondrial dysfunction. These strategies provide novel directions for developing precise and effective clinical therapies.

3.4.

## Epigenetic regulation
Epigenetic regulation refers to the modulation of gene expression without altering the DNA sequence and is achieved through mechanisms such as DNA methylation, histone modifications, and noncoding RNAs. During ALI pathogenesis, pathological factors such as inflammation and oxidative stress can induce aberrant epigenetic modifications, which further dysregulate gene expression and exacerbate disease progression. Thus, epigenetic intervention holds significant clinical potential for ALI therapy.
Nanoparticle-delivered epigenetic drugs that target DNA methylation can modulate inflammatory responses in ALI. For example, hypomethylation of proinflammatory gene promoters (e.g., TNF-α and IL-6) or hypermethylation of anti-inflammatory genes (e.g., IL-10) may aggravate inflammation. NPs (e.g., liposomes, polymeric NPs) encapsulating DNA methyltransferase inhibitors (e.g., 5-azacytidine, 5-AZA) or TET demethylase activators can be delivered to lung vascular endothelial cells or macrophages to reverse abnormal methylation patterns and mitigate inflammatory damage. For example, glucocorticoid-loaded nanoparticles (IOH-NPs) significantly reduce neutrophil infiltration by suppressing proinflammatory gene expression while minimizing systemic side effects [ ].
Histone deacetylases (HDACs), which are overexpressed in ALI, contribute to the silencing of anti-inflammatory genes. NPs loaded with HDAC inhibitors (e.g., trichostatin A (TSA)) increase histone acetylation, restoring the expression of antioxidant genes (e.g., Nrf2) and alleviating oxidative stress [ ]. Additionally, AI-designed LNPs such as FO-32 and FO-35 efficiently deliver gene-editing tools (e.g., CRISPR-dCas9) to precisely regulate histone modifications, increasing the accuracy of pulmonary gene therapy [ ].
NPs can also deliver anti-inflammatory miRNAs (e.g., miR-146a) or siRNAs targeting proinflammatory lncRNAs (e.g., MALAT1), suppressing NF-κB signaling and reducing alveolar epithelial cell apoptosis [ ]. For example, AuNPs functionalized with the CLPFFD peptide inhibited neutrophil migration and markedly attenuated ALI-associated inflammation [ ]. Furthermore, mesenchymal stem cell-derived extracellular vesicles enhance lung repair by modulating the IDH2/TET metabolic-epigenetic axis [ ]. In epigenetically enhanced stem cell therapy, researchers have employed epigenetic editing to suppress β2-microglobulin super-enhancer (B2M-SE), generating “immune-privileged” universal MSCs (GLOBES). These cells exhibit reduced HLA-I expression to evade T-cell recognition while avoiding NK cell activation, demonstrating enhanced survival and reparative efficacy in ALI models, thereby offering a novel strategy for off-the-shelf cell therapies [ ].
Despite the immense potential of nanoparticle-mediated epigenetic regulation in ALI, challenges remain in long-term safety, targeting efficiency, and clinical translation. Future research may integrate AI-optimized nanocarrier designs (e.g., FO-32/FO-35 LNPs) with multifunctional platforms (e.g., theranostic NPs) to advance precision medicine. NPs, through epigenetic modulation, provide reversible and precise therapeutic strategies for ALI. The combination of gene-editing and stem cell technologies holds promise for overcoming current treatment limitations.

4.

## The application of nanoparticles in the treatment of ALI
NPs are utilized in the treatment of ALI through mechanisms such as targeting and anti-inflammatory effects. Specific examples and case studies illustrating these applications are summarized in .
 4.1.

## Lipid nanoparticles
LNPs have emerged as pivotal delivery vehicles for ALI therapy because of their superior biosafety, high drug-loading capacity, and excellent modifiability. LNPs, which are composed of bilayer lipid structures with sizes ranging from 20 to 2000 nm, enable the precise targeting of pulmonary inflammatory sites through the optimization of physicochemical properties (e.g., particle size and surface charge) and surface modifications (e.g., antibodies and ligands) [ , ]. Their core advantages include 1) protection of therapeutic agents from enzymatic degradation and rapid clearance, thereby extending the systemic circulation time [ ]; 2) direct alveolar delivery via inhalation administration to minimize systemic exposure ( C) [ , ]; 3) enhanced drug accumulation in pathological tissues through surface-anchored targeting molecules (e.g., pulmonary epithelial cell antibodies) [ ]; and 4) prolonged blood circulation duration and improved pulmonary accumulation through optimized lipid composition, preventing premature drug release [ , ]. These characteristics establish LNPs as efficient, safe, and versatile drug delivery platforms with broad application prospects.
 In ALI management, LNPs demonstrate remarkable therapeutic potential. First, LNPs significantly enhance drug targeting and bioavailability. Surface-modified LNPs achieve specific accumulation in pulmonary inflammatory regions, with studies showing 3-5-fold higher drug concentrations than free drugs [ , , ]. A study designed Pac@liposome/Pac-lipo which incorporated pachypodol (hydrophobic anti-inflammatory flavonol extracted from Pogostemon cablin Benth) into liposomes; the Pac-lipo formulation demonstrated superior efficacy over free Pac by significantly attenuating lung index, restoring pulmonary function, suppressing cytokine expression (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, iNOS), reducing BALF protein/cellularity, repairing epithelial/endothelial barriers, and alleviating histopathological injury [ ].
Second, LNPs have increased anti-inflammatory and antioxidant efficacy. A previous study utilized glutaraldehyde-mediated crosslinking of human serum albumin (HSA) and DNase-I to prepare DNase-I/HSA nanoparticles. Following nebulized endotracheal administration, these nanoparticles effectively cleared cell-free mitochondrial DNA (cf-mtDNA) in the pulmonary microenvironment. This intervention inhibited the cf-mtDNA/TLR9-mediated activation of alveolar macrophages, thereby attenuating sepsis-associated ALI ( A) [ ]. Another study developed an inhalable biomimetic sequential drug delivery nano platform (termed D-SEL) for combination therapy for ALI. This dual-phase release profile reshaped pulmonary immune homeostasis by downregulating proinflammatory cytokines and upregulating anti-inflammatory cytokines, thereby promoting pulmonary tissue regeneration ( B) [ ]. A MacLNP was engineered through systematic screening of LNPs conjugated with a macrophage-specific F4/80 antibody. The optimized MacLNPs demonstrated efficient siRNA delivery to suppress transforming growth factor beta-activated kinase 1 (TAK1) expression, effectively mitigating inflammatory cascades and presenting a precision therapeutic strategy for pulmonary inflammatory disorders ( D) [ ]. By concurrently encapsulating anti-inflammatory drugs and antioxidants, LNPs achieve synergistic therapeutic effects against ALI's dual pathology of inflammation and oxidative stress [ ]. Recent innovations include the use of vitronectin-modified LNPs, which increase anti-inflammatory mRNA delivery efficiency through integrin αvβ3 receptor-mediated endothelial targeting [ ].
Third, LNPs mitigate systemic adverse effects. Compared with conventional administration routes, localized delivery significantly reduces off-target distribution. CRISPR-Cas9 delivery via LNPs exemplifies this advantage, demonstrating precise pulmonary targeting with minimal systemic exposure and immunogenicity [ ].
Despite these advances, clinical translation faces challenges: 1) serum protein interactions alter biodistribution [ ]; 2) manufacturing limitations of conventional methods (e.g., film hydration, solvent diffusion), including poor reproducibility, excessive surfactant requirements, and inadequate control over particle size/encapsulation efficiency [ ]; and 3) potential immunogenicity requiring further evaluation, as nanoparticle-immune cell interactions may induce subclinical immune responses despite apparent biocompatibility [ ]. Future research should focus on optimizing targeting strategies and production technologies to facilitate the clinical implementation of LNP-based ALI therapies.

4.2.

## Nanozymes
Nanozymes, a class of nanoscale materials with enzyme-mimicking activities, ingeniously integrates the dual advantages of natural enzymes and chemical catalysts to efficiently promote enzyme-substrate chemical reactions under relatively mild conditions. For example, cerium oxide (CeO2) and Prussian blue-based nanozymes not only display SOD-like activity but also possess catalase (CAT)-like functionality, demonstrating exceptional antioxidant properties and self-cascade catalysis unattainable by natural enzymes or small-molecule catalysts ( A and D) [ ]. These remarkable features position nanozymes as ideal substitutes for natural enzymes in chemical analysis, biosensing, and environmental remediation while also showing significant advantages and broad application prospects in medical fields.
 Various nanozymes that exhibit antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antitumor, and antibacterial effects have been identified. For example, Liang Minmin et al. demonstrated that doxorubicin-loaded H-ferritin nanocages can specifically target and eliminate tumor cells through single-dose injection [ ]; Wu Xiaoshuai et al. designed a nanozyme (P@Co) comprised of polydopamine NPs loading with ultra-small Co, combining with near infrared irradiation, which could efficiently scavenge intracellular ROS and suppress inflammatory responses against ALI [ ]; Researchers employed ultrasmall copper-based nanoparticles for reactive ROS scavenging and inflammation mitigation ( C) [ ]. Shi Xueyin et al. investigated an inhalable pro-efferocytosis nanozyme (AOzyme@ACM) that mimics apoptotic bodies to increase macrophage recognition of “eat-me” signals, eliminate intracellular ROS overload, suppress inflammatory responses, promote M2 phenotypes macrophage polarization, and effectively alleviate sepsis-induced ALI in mice, suggesting a promising strategy for treating sepsis-associated ALI ( B) [ ].
In ALI therapy, nanozymes have demonstrated remarkable efficacy. First, nanozymes exhibit superior antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Second, nanozymes achieve precise targeting through surface modifications. A previous study demonstrated that surface-engineered DNase-I nanomotors (DNase-I/HSA NMs) effectively cleared cell-free mitochondrial DNA in the lungs after inhalation, alleviating sepsis-induced ALI [ ]. Third, certain nanozymes facilitate pulmonary tissue repair and regeneration.
Despite these advantages, nanozyme applications face several challenges. (1) Suboptimal targeting efficiency: Zhang Zhiping et al. reported that engineered biomimetic hybrid vesicles (DHA@ANeu-DDAB), despite showing good inflammatory-targeting ability and biocompatibility in ARDS treatment, exhibited significant hepatic/splenic accumulation following intravenous administration, reducing pulmonary drug concentrations [ ]. (2) Biocompatibility concerns: nanozymes may provoke immune responses, particularly with repeated dosing, potentially exacerbating lung injury [ ]. (3) Stability and controlled release limitations: maintaining drug release sustainability at inflammatory sites and ensuring stability under complex physiological conditions (e.g., inflammatory microenvironments) require optimization [ ]. (4) Unclear clinical mechanisms: Clinical data remain limited, with most studies in preclinical stages. The multitarget mechanisms involving diverse cell types and signaling pathways in inflammatory microenvironments warrant further elucidation [ ].
In conclusion, nanozymes offer novel therapeutic strategies for ALI through ROS scavenging, inflammatory pathway inhibition, and tissue repair mechanisms. Future research should prioritize targeted delivery optimization, safety evaluation, and mechanistic exploration to facilitate clinical translation.

4.3.

## Inorganic nanoparticles
Inorganic NPs, as functional materials with crystalline dimensions ranging from 1 to 100 nm, can be classified into four categories based on composition: 1) metallic nanomaterials (e.g., Au, Ag, and Cu); 2) metal oxide nanomaterials (e.g., ZnO, CuO, and TiO2); 3) inorganic semiconductor nanomaterials; and 4) carbon-based nanomaterials (e.g., graphene and carbon nanotubes). These materials demonstrate significant therapeutic potential in ALI treatment through their unique surface properties, quantum size effects, and biocompatibility [ ].
Surface-modified inorganic nanomaterials enable precise drug delivery. For example, mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNs) loaded with dexamethasone and functionalized with TNFR1-targeting peptides (TNFR-Dex-MSNs) effectively reduce TNF-α and IL-1β levels through lysosomal drug release following internalization by M1 macrophages [ ]. AuNPs mitigate LPS-induced pulmonary inflammation by suppressing iNOS-mediated excessive NO production [ ].
Concerning anti-inflammatory and antioxidant mechanisms, AuNPs protect the pulmonary architecture from LPS-induced morphological alterations by inhibiting ROS overproduction [ ]. ZnO nanoparticles induce oxidative stress and inflammation via cGAS-STING pathway activation, which can be ameliorated by RU-521 inhibitors [ ]. Silica nanoparticle (SiNP)-induced lysosomal damage and autophagic dysfunction are alleviated through ROS/PARP/TRPM2 pathway inhibition ( A) [ ]. CeO2 nanoparticles exhibit multivalent redox properties to scavenge ROS and block TLR4/NF-κB signaling, thereby mitigating oxidative stress [ ]. While CeO2 shows promise as a ROS-scavenging nanoparticle, its slow elimination raises concerns about its toxicity. Surface modification strategies (e.g., SiO2 coating) significantly reduce CeO2 and Se nanomaterial toxicity while preserving their radical-scavenging capacity.
 
4.4.

## Organic nanoparticles
Organic nanoparticles, nanoscale particles fabricated from organic materials, exhibit unique physicochemical properties. These particles typically demonstrate excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, granting them broad application prospects in biomedical fields. Additionally, surface modifications can enhance their targeting capabilities and functionality. The intrinsic characteristics of organic nanoparticles make them promising candidates for ALI therapy.
As drug delivery vehicles, organic nanoparticles protect therapeutic agents from environmental degradation (e.g., light, heat, and oxidation), thereby improving their pharmaceutical stability. Through nanotechnology, poorly soluble drugs can be encapsulated within organic nanoparticles to increase aqueous solubility and bioavailability. Targeted delivery of anti-inflammatory drugs, antibiotics, or other therapeutics to the lungs increases local drug concentrations while improving therapeutic efficacy. Precise control over nanoparticle release profiles enables sustained and controlled drug delivery, prolonging the therapeutic duration and reducing the dosing frequency.
Organic nanoparticles safeguard drugs from oxidative or photolytic degradation via physical encapsulation or chemical conjugation while improving the dispersibility of hydrophobic compounds. For example, poly(β-amino ester)- stimuli-responsive nanoparticles achieve pH-dependent drug release through acid-sensitive linkages, with surface PEG-biotin modifications extending the circulation time and enhancing pulmonary targeting ( B) [ ].
In anti-inflammatory and antioxidant therapies, organic nanoparticles may exhibit intrinsic anti-inflammatory effects to mitigate pulmonary inflammation. In LPS-induced ALI models, N-acetylcysteine-grafted polyurethane nanoparticles (NPT@NPs) have demonstrated significant efficacy in alleviating pulmonary edema, reducing inflammatory cell infiltration, suppressing myeloperoxidase expression, lowering proinflammatory cytokine levels, and reversing apoptosis [ ]. Cyclodextrin framework-based NPs leverage biodegradability and antioxidant properties as inhalable therapeutic platforms for ALI ( C) [ ]. These innovations not only increase local drug concentrations but also extend therapeutic windows through sustained-release mechanisms.
In addition to drug delivery, organic nanomaterials show extensive potential in regenerative medicine. NPs can serve as drug carriers, tissue engineering scaffolds, or bioactive factors that promote cellular regeneration. For example, rare-earth-based inorganic-organic hybrid biomaterials with tailored bioactivity have been developed for wound healing and skin regeneration [ ].
Despite their therapeutic promise, organic nanoparticles face challenges, including biocompatibility optimization, drug release stability, and scalable production. Advances in nanotechnology are expected to yield novel NPs for more effective ALI treatment and personalized and precision therapies may become feasible for improving patient outcomes.
In conclusion, organic nanoparticles hold substantial potential for ALI treatment. Continued optimization of synthesis techniques and surface modification strategies may address current limitations, accelerating their clinical translation and widespread application in ALI management.

4.5.

## Peptide-based nanoparticles
PNPs are 1–100 nm nanostructures formed via self-assembly of functional peptide sequences or their combination with polymers, lipids, or other excipients [ ]. These nanoparticles incorporate bioactive peptides, including antimicrobial peptides, cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs), targeting peptides, and chemically modified variants, to confer specific biological activities, while auxiliary materials enhance stability and functionality.
To address the rapid hepatic clearance of LNPs during systemic administration, researchers have developed amphiphilic PBNs for ALI therapy. The amphiphilic nature of these peptides is critical for cell-penetrating peptide-lipid membrane interactions [ ]. CPPs, short peptides capable of traversing cell membranes via endocytosis or direct translocation, facilitate the intracellular delivery of bioactive cargo (e.g., drugs, nucleic acids) by forming transient pores or microvesicles ( A and C) [ , ].
 Recent advances have focused on amphiphilic PNP systems that achieve pulmonary enrichment through passive targeting (lung vascular leakage) and active targeting (ligand modification). These systems specifically target endothelial cells, neutrophils, and immune cells (e.g., dendritic cells, T/B cells) to modulate immune responses and attenuate inflammation [ ]. In terms of delivery mechanisms, intracellular acidic environment-responsive PNPs release therapeutic nucleic acids. R3V6 peptide-mediated siRNA delivery in LPS-induced ALI models enhances siRNA efficiency with low cytotoxicity/immunogenicity. GALA peptide-modified multifunctional envelope-type nanodevices (MENDs) improve endosomal escape [ ]. LL37 peptide-loaded bovine serum albumin nanoparticles (LL37 PNPs) demonstrate sustained antimicrobial/anti-inflammatory effects via controlled LL37 release [ ]. Compared with 13-nm PNPs (P12(G13)), 20-nm PNPs (P12(G20)) suppress TLR4 signaling, reducing monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) and macrophage inflammatory protein-1β (MIP-1β) levels by ∼50 % and ∼60 %, respectively, and significantly ameliorate alveolar pathology [ ]. These findings highlight the advantages of PNPs in terms of cytokine suppression, survival improvement, and lung injury mitigation [ ].
Although no clinical trials are currently exploring PNPs for ALI/ARDS, some antimicrobial PNPs are nearing clinical application: OP-145 (LL-37-derived peptide), which is completing phase II trials for gram-positive infections, and NVB-302, which is completing phase I trials for Clostridioides difficile infections [ ]. Despite their antimicrobial efficacy, challenges persist in terms of in vivo stability, toxicity, and scalable production. Balancing ligand affinity with nonspecific uptake remains critical for optimizing targeting specificity [ , ].
Future research priorities include developing multitarget synergistic systems, optimizing pH-responsive release mechanisms, enhancing gene silencing efficiency, and establishing standardized biosafety evaluation frameworks [ ].

4.6.

## Carbon-based nanoparticles
Nanoparticles composed entirely of carbon are termed carbon-based materials. Common carbon-based nanoparticles include carbon nanotubes, graphene, fullerenes, and their derivatives (e.g., graphene oxide and multi-walled carbon nanotubes).
4.6.1.

## Fullerenes
Fullerenes are hollow, spherical, ellipsoidal, or other shaped nanomaterials composed of carbon atoms, with a structure resembling a soccer ball, also known as “buckyballs.” The C60 molecule is the most prevalent fullerene and consists of 60 carbon atoms. Other fullerene molecules, such as C70, C84, and C90, exhibit distinct properties depending on their carbon atom count. Fullerene molecules demonstrate excellent chemical and thermal stability, high electrical conductivity, and a hollow structure suitable for gas adsorption and sensing. They can also form supramolecular structures via π-π interactions with other molecules or materials [ ].
Studies indicate that the fullerene derivative C60(ONO2)7 ± 2 may hold significant clinical potential in preventing and treating ALI. First, C60(ONO2)7 ± 2 exhibits antioxidant activity, effectively scavenges reactive ROS to reduce oxidative stress, maintains cell membrane integrity, and mitigates lung damage. It also suppresses inflammatory cytokine release and neutrophil adhesion, thereby attenuating inflammatory responses. Second, C60(ONO2)7 ± 2 releases NO, which has potent anti-inflammatory effects while significantly reducing pulmonary arterial pressure and vascular resistance, alleviating pulmonary edema, and improving vascular function [ ]. However, further research is needed to address the toxicity and mechanistic uncertainties of fullerenes and their derivatives to increase their clinical safety [ ].

4.6.2.

## Carbon NTs
Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) are hollow tubular structures formed by rolling graphene sheets. They are categorized as single-walled carbon nanotubes (from a single graphene layer) or multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs, from concentric graphene layers). CNTs exhibit exceptional strength, elasticity, electrical conductivity, and thermal conductivity, making them promising drug carriers. Their chemically stable surfaces can be functionalized via chemical or physical modifications to reduce cytotoxicity, enhance biocompatibility, and improve drug delivery efficiency and tissue targeting [ , ].
Studies have shown that MWCNTs functionalized with high-molecular-weight hyaluronic acid significantly reduce inflammatory cell counts, cytokine levels, collagen deposition, and mucus cell metaplasia in murine lung tissue ( B) [ ]. Surface conjugation with PEG decreases immunogenicity and cytotoxicity. Metal ion-functionalized CNTs enhance the efficacy of photothermal therapy. Anticancer drug-loaded CNTs enable targeted cancer treatment, whereas gene/nucleotide-conjugated CNTs facilitate gene therapy. The combination of functionalized CNTs with tumor biomarkers allows early clinical cancer diagnosis. Although CNTs show potential in treating neurodegenerative and infectious diseases, their organ-specific toxicity necessitates rigorous evaluation to optimize their targeted delivery and functionalization for nanomedical applications [ ].

4.6.3.

## Graphene
Graphene is a single-layer, two-dimensional material composed of sp2-hybridized carbon atoms and is characterized by exceptional strength, hardness, toughness, and ultrahigh specific surface area. Graphene and its derivatives exhibit significant antibacterial and antifungal activity. Studies have revealed that graphene oxide treatment increases the 72-h survival rate of mice infected with multidrug-resistant bacteria to 80 %, with hematoxylin-eosin staining demonstrating markedly reduced lung tissue damage, inflammation, and neutrophil infiltration [ ]. Thus, graphene oxide represents a potential antimicrobial material for managing pulmonary infections.
Graphene can be functionalized via heteroatom doping or conjugation with polymers/organic molecules, resulting in good biocompatibility, low cytotoxicity, and renal clearance. It shows promise in treating neurodegenerative diseases, diabetes, and infections, as well as in targeted drug delivery, cancer therapy, biosensing, and bioimaging for clinical diagnostics [ ].
However, the antimicrobial mechanisms of graphene remain incompletely understood, and high concentrations of graphene may induce cytotoxicity [ ]. Further research is needed to elucidate these mechanisms and develop safer, more efficient graphene-based antimicrobial materials that synergize with other agents to form advanced drug delivery systems [ ].

4.7.

## Overview of nanotechnology development and its medical potential
The application of nanotechnology in medicine originated in the early 20th century, with lipid nanoparticles used as anticancer drug delivery carriers pioneering the field of nanomedicine [ ]. Breakthroughs in nanoimaging technologies, driven by advancements in quantum dot fluorescent probes and iron-based nanoparticle MRI contrast agents, have provided novel diagnostic tools for precision medicine [ ].
Today, medical applications of nanotechnology have become increasingly sophisticated. Targeted delivery via nanoparticles enables the precise localization of drugs to specific tissues or organs, facilitating accurate clinical diagnosis, immune system modulation, and even gene therapy [ ].
Current technology translation has established a complete pipeline from fundamental research to clinical application. Future interdisciplinary integration is expected to propel precision medicine toward intelligent and personalized development.

5.

## Translational medicine pathway
NPs remain in the early stages of development for the treatment of ALI and have not entered clinical trials, although they have demonstrated significant potential in the clinical management of ALI. Nanomedicines traverse multiple stages from laboratory development to clinical implementation. These processes commence with the discovery and design phase, wherein novel nanoparticles are conceptualized and synthesized. Subsequent preclinical evaluation involves rigorous in vitro and in vivo testing to assess safety and efficacy. This is followed by manufacturing process development, which scales up production while maintaining stringent quality control. The regulatory submission phase then entails presenting comprehensive datasets to governing agencies-a critical prerequisite before advancing to clinical trials where nanomedicine safety and efficacy undergo human subject testing. Among these stages, regulatory submission presents exceptional challenges. The complex interplay between nanoparticle properties and biological systems complicates the assessment of physicochemical and toxicological characteristics, thereby impeding regulatory decision-making [ ]. Each nanomedicine's unique attributes-including nanoparticle type, surface properties, administration route, and morphology-necessitate tailored regulatory approaches.
Although no nanoparticle-based therapies have yet entered clinical trials specifically for ALI, summarizes representative ongoing and recently completed nanomedicine trials targeting pulmonary diseases (including exploratory studies potentially relevant to ALI mechanisms). These investigations provide critical preliminary evidence and data foundations for future clinical development of ALI nanotherapeutics.
 5.1.

## Preclinical research phase
According to the FDA's 2022 updated Guidance for Industry, preclinical toxicology studies of nanoformulated drugs under GLP compliance require the establishment of systematic evaluation strategies based on ICH guidelines. For drug products containing nanomaterials, particular attention should be given to the influence of their physicochemical properties on their biological effects. Compared with conventional materials, nonbiodegradable nanocomponents may exhibit long-term accumulation effects, with a significantly greater ability to cross the blood-brain barrier and placenta [ , ]. Radioisotope or fluorescent labeling techniques are recommended for biodistribution tracking, but validation of labeling-induced alterations in nanoparticle behavior is essential.
Route-specific evaluation protocols must be established. Topical administration requires investigations into follicular penetration and photosensitivity modulation [ ]. Subcutaneous injection necessitates the assessment of the degree of nanomaterial-induced sensitization risk [ , ]. Inhalation formulations should focus on altered pulmonary distribution patterns and clearance kinetics [ ]. Intravenous formulations demand monitoring of hemocompatibility and shifts in tissue distribution. Oral formulations require verification of bioavailability enhancement and potential accumulation risks.
Before study initiation, the batch-to-batch reproducibility of nanomaterials must be confirmed via validated characterization methods (e.g., dynamic light scattering, electron microscopy) to ensure consistency in size distribution, surface charge, and aggregation state. LNP-mRNA formulations are predominantly manufactured through rapid mixing technology, wherein precise control of process parameters - including pH, flow rate ratios, and mixing dynamics-enables exceptional batch-to-batch reproducibility [ ]. Absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) comparative studies and toxicology bridging strategies may reduce redundant testing for modified nanoformulations, but critical safety parameters such as placental penetration must be specifically evaluated, with separate nanomaterial control groups if warranted. In vitro testing conditions should be adjusted according to nanomaterial dissolution/aggregation characteristics to ensure reliability.

5.2.

## Phase I clinical trial design
The clinical development of nanomaterial-containing drug products must adhere to policies and guidelines related to IND, NDA, ANDA, and BLA submissions for safety and efficacy studies.
5.2.1.

## General considerations
Nanomaterial-containing drugs fall into two categories: 1) nanomaterials used as therapeutic components (e.g., nanocrystal suspensions) or 2) nanocarrier-loaded therapeutics (e.g., liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and dendrimers). For the latter, pharmacokinetic (PK) evaluation must address both released therapeutics and carrier-specific PK profiles. NPs may exhibit biological activities independent of payload (e.g., immunogenicity), necessitating assessment of their impact on safety/efficacy. Traditional bioequivalence principles based on plasma exposure equivalence may not apply because of altered tissue distribution via endocytosis or increased permeability and retention effects. For medium/high-risk nanoformulations (e.g., intravenously administered targeted therapies), comparability in disposition to reference products must be demonstrated through nonclinical and clinical evidence (e.g., exposure-response relationships and tissue distribution).

5.2.2.

## Clinical study design
For drug development strategies, low-risk drugs typically require plasma PK-based bioequivalence (BA) comparability studies to bridge safety and efficacy to reference products. In contrast, medium/high-risk drugs necessitate comprehensive evaluations, including single/multiple-dose PK studies and related BA investigations, with oral formulations specifically requiring fed-state BA evaluations. For BA bridging with medium/high-risk drugs, single-dose ADME studies may provide supplemental evidence for therapeutic component disposition comparability; however, comparative safety/efficacy studies become mandatory when PD biomarkers are unavailable. Endpoint selection allows scientifically validated alternatives such as response rate substitution for progression-free survival. Superiority claims should employ sequential testing strategies, first establishing noninferiority before demonstrating superiority. The indication scope is generally restricted to reference product-approved uses unless new indication-specific clinical trials are conducted.
NJLCG1402, a multicenter single-arm phase I/II trial in advanced NSCLC, explored the recommended dose, safety, and antitumor activity of biweekly albumin-bound paclitaxel nanoparticles (nab-PTX). The phase I segment established an MTD of 150 mg/m2 as the recommended phase II dose through dose escalation (100, 125, 150 mg/m2), evaluating the ORR and grade ≥3 treatment-related adverse events [ ]. Nucleoside-modified mRNA encapsulated in LNPs has been successfully used in the development of safe and highly effective COVID-19 vaccines. The phase 1, open-label, dose-escalation, multicenter trial (NCT05477186) evaluated the safety and immunogenicity of a booster dose of LNP-encapsulated mRNA COVID-19 vaccine (CV0501). Results demonstrated that CV0501 was well-tolerated and elicited robust immune responses not only against the antigenically matched Omicron BA.1 variant but also cross-reactive immunity toward wild-type SARS-CoV-2 (WT), other Omicron subvariants, and the Delta variant [ ].To specifically induce CD8 + responses, another clinical trial used the candidate vaccine PepGNP-Covid19, composed of synthetic class I major histocompatibility complex (MHC-I)-selective SARS-CoV-2 peptides anchored on self-adjuvanted gold nanoparticles that serve as a vehicle for peptide delivery. The results showed that vaccinations were safe. No serious adverse events were reported. Most of the adverse events were mild and two adverse events of special interest related to the product (fever and fatigue) [ ].
The spillover effect of LNP-mRNA technology and the great success of COVID-19 mRNA vaccine Boosting RNA nanotherapeutics with V-ATPase activating non-inflammatory lipid nanoparticles to treat chronic lung injury, This breakthrough not only validates the efficacy and relative safety of LNP systems but also significantly accelerates the maturation of their production processes, characterization methods, and regulatory pathways. It removes key technical and regulatory barriers for developing inhalable LNP delivery systems to deliver therapeutic mRNA (such as anti-inflammatory cytokines and antioxidant enzymes) or siRNA for ALI/ARDS treatment. The trials also address critical issues including long-term pulmonary retention safety, potential local irritation or immunogenicity, batch consistency, and large-scale manufacturing -all essential challenges that must be resolved for the clinical translation of future ALI/ARDS nanotherapies [ ].

5.3.

## Phase II/III adaptive design
"Basket” trial development represents a novel paradigm where enrollment eligibility is based on specific genomic alterations regardless of histology. Initial applications aimed at determining whether drugs targeting genomic alterations in one cancer type demonstrate efficacy in others with identical mutations. Given increasing approvals of genomically targeted therapies and the observation that these alterations transcend tumor types, this approach holds considerable potential for expanding precision oncology applications.
NC-6004, a micelle-based nanoparticle that improves cisplatin release and tumor-selective distribution, employs differentiated design strategies across development phases. UK phase I trials revealed a 230-fold prolonged half-life and an 8.5-fold increase in the area under the curve (AUC) in advanced solid tumors, establishing an MTD of 90 mg/m2 [ ]. Asian phase I/II trials (NCT02043288) using 3 + 3 modified Fibonacci escalation increased the MTD to 120 mg/m2 with gemcitabine (RP2D: 90 mg/m2) [ ]. Subsequent multicenter Ib/II trials (NCT02240238) implemented Bayesian N-CRM modeling, adaptively increasing the MTD to 135 mg/m2 (50 % higher than conventional methods) without significant neuro/renal toxicity [ ]. A phase II basket trial evaluated NC-6004 + gemcitabine in 34 squamous NSCLC, 50 biliary tract, and 13 cisplatin-eligible bladder cancer patients and reported median PFS times of 3.9, 4.3, and 6.8 months, respectively [ ].
Concerning cellular targets, alveolar macrophages express TLR-pattern recognition receptors that are central to innate immunity. TLR4 recognizes bacterial LPS and endogenous damage-associated molecules, initiating immediate and adaptive immune responses. Cross-species evolution has led to variations in TLR4 ligand affinity, gene expression patterns, and tissue distribution. Therefore, interspecies differences in pulmonary TLR4 expression ( ) must be considered when designing clinical trials targeting TLR4.
 
5.4.

## Nanoparticle systems evaluation for clinical translation feasibility
The clinical translation of nanoparticle-based therapies for ALI/ARDS requires comprehensive evaluation of targeting efficiency, biosafety, cost-effectiveness, and manufacturing scalability. Drawing on current research data, this section provides a systematic comparison of six major nanoparticle categories ( ).
 LNPs and polymeric particles lead the field in clinical translation. LNPs have demonstrated industrial viability in nucleic acid delivery (e.g., COVID-19 mRNA vaccines), although the ABC phenomenon associated with PEG requires resolution, such as through the development of polypeptide alternatives. Polymeric particles (e.g., PLGA/PEG) benefit from biodegradability and established manufacturing lines, enabling rapid translation; however, optimization of drug loading and mitigation of burst release effects remain necessary [ , ]. PNPs, despite higher production costs, offer irreplaceable cell-specific targeting capabilities for precision immune regulation, such as macrophage reprogramming [ ]. Nanozymes confer unique advantages in antioxidant therapy owing to their multi-enzyme activities, yet the long-term toxicity concerns of their metallic components (e.g., SiO2-coated CeO2 nanoparticles) require resolution [ ]. Regarding inorganic and carbon-based materials, significant translation limitations exist. Rigorous long-term toxicity assessments are imperative for gold/silver nanoparticles due to risks of organ accumulation (e.g., liver/spleen) and for carbon materials due to their pulmonary fibrosis potential [ , ]. Consequently, their use is currently recommended only for short-term applications, such as diagnostics or localized antimicrobial therapy.

6.

## Conclusions and prospects
While current clinical trials of NPs for pulmonary diseases predominantly focus on chronic conditions (e.g., asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD]) or tumors, the accumulated expertise in delivery technology platforms (particularly inhalable liposomes), therapeutic mechanisms (modulation of immune/inflammatory/oxidative stress responses), and clinical development experience has established a robust foundation for advancing nanotherapies targeting acute critical illnesses such as ALI/ARDS into clinical research. Close monitoring of the progress and outcomes of these trials will facilitate prediction of clinical translation pathways and strategic planning for ALI/ARDS-targeted nanotherapeutics.
Despite promising preclinical outcomes, the widespread application of nanoparticles faces three major challenges: 1) biosafety concerns, such as pulmonary accumulation toxicity of inorganic nanomaterials; 2) insufficient targeting efficiency, leading to off-target effects due to nonspecific distribution; and 3) complexity in scalable production, with difficulties in ensuring batch-to-batch consistency. Additionally, the interaction mechanisms between nanoparticles and the immune system remain incompletely elucidated, and long-term toxicity evaluation frameworks require refinement. A comprehensive understanding of the interactions between nanoparticles, the immune system, and lung cells will facilitate the development of safer and more efficient nano-based therapies for ALI.
To optimize lung-targeted drug delivery efficacy, identification of lung-specific receptors through rigorous characterization enables spatially precise therapeutic action in pulmonary substructures [ ]. The advancement of nanomedicine relies on deep interdisciplinary convergence among materials science, molecular biology, and clinical medicine. Collaborative efforts are needed to clarify nanoparticle metabolic pathways in vivo and establish standardized safety assessment systems, leverage organoid models and computational simulations to predict clinical efficacy and foster industry-academia partnerships to address scalable production and cost-control challenges. Artificial intelligence (AI) and nanotechnology are pivotal to realizing the goal of precision medicine-delivering tailored optimal treatments for individual cancer patients. The recent convergence of these fields is enabling enhanced patient data acquisition and refined nanomaterial design [ ].
Nanotechnology is poised to establish a comprehensive chain from molecular intervention to tissue regeneration in ALI therapy: precision subtyping in the early stage via nanodiagnostics; targeted delivery of anti-inflammatory/antioxidant drugs in the middle stage via intelligent carriers; and lung functional reconstruction in the late stage via bioactive scaffold materials. This framework will significantly reduce mortality, improve long-term patient outcomes, and provide a new paradigm for ALI drug therapy.

7.

## Risk-benefit analysis
Despite the significant advantages of NPs in drug delivery systems, numerous potential risks remain. When foreign entities such as NPs enter the human body, they are rapidly recognized by the host immune system. Opsonization of NPs occurs almost immediately after intravenous injection, leading to their recognition by mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) cells [ ]. Consequently, the vast majority of NPs are cleared from the circulatory system before reaching their target sites and accumulate in organs of the reticuloendothelial system (e.g., the liver and spleen) [ ]. Based on this phenomenon, researchers have focused on identifying nanoparticle characteristics that influence immune recognition and have developed various strategies to evade MPS detection, including conjugation with PEG and the use of the CD47 protein [ , ]. However, these strategies introduce new challenges.
7.1.

## Organ accumulation of the NPs
First, NPs tend to accumulate in nontarget organs. Using positron emission tomography (PET), a well-established nuclear imaging technique based on radiotracer/isotope detection, combined with computed tomography (CT), the dynamic biodistribution of nanoparticles can be monitored immediately after injection [ , ]. One study utilized this approach to compare the biodistribution differences of 68Ga-labeled calcium phosphate nanoparticles with those of silica shells via various administration routes. PET-CT dynamic monitoring was performed at 5 min, 15 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, and 4 h postadministration, followed by ex vivo analysis at 5 h. The results revealed that after tail vein injection, the nanoparticles rapidly entered the lungs, followed by redistribution to the liver and spleen. In contrast, intramuscular, intratumoral, or soft tissue injections led to nanoparticle retention at the injection site, with less than 10 % of the nanoparticles entering the bloodstream. Ex vivo data at 5 h aligned with the in vivo observations: the intravenous group exhibited nanoparticle accumulation in the spleen (28 % IA/g), lungs (21 % IA/g), and liver (10 % IA/g), whereas uptake in other organs remained below 3 % IA/g. The intramuscular and soft tissue injection groups presented organ uptake rates below 2 % IA/g. For intratumoral injection, enhanced blood distribution due to tumor vascularization resulted in minor accumulation in the spleen (5.7 % IA/g), lungs (3.7 % IA/g), and liver (3.5 % IA/g) [ ]. Another study using PET/CT to monitor PEGylated vs. non-PEGylated magnetic nanoporous silica nanoparticles (MNPSNPs) revealed that both 68Ga-labeled MNPSNPs predominantly accumulated in the liver and spleen [ ]. Similarly, a study on PEGylated NPs (89Zr-CPC634) encapsulating docetaxel demonstrated that 89Zr-CPC634 followed the expected biodistribution patterns, accumulating mainly in the liver, spleen, and kidneys. In clinical settings, patients injected with 89Zr-CPC634 underwent PET/CT scans at 24, 96, and 144 h postinjection. Biodistribution analysis confirmed accumulation in the spleen, liver, and bone marrow, with stable low %IA/kg in the extremities and declining renal %IA/kg, suggesting renal excretion [ ].

7.2.

## Accelerated blood clearance phenomenon induced by PEG
PEGylation enhances colloidal stability and reduces interactions with immune components (e.g., proteins and immune cells). To date, more than 21 PEGylated nanomedicines (e.g., PEGylated liposomal doxorubicin) have received FDA approval [ ]. However, repeated administration of PEGylated “stealth” liposomes paradoxically triggers MPS recognition. This pharmacokinetic anomaly, termed the accelerated blood clearance (ABC) phenomenon, significantly reduces bioavailability and efficacy while increasing adverse effects due to altered drug distribution, posing a major barrier to clinical translation [ , ]. Anti-PEG antibodies are key drivers of ABC [ , , ]. In animal studies, initial PEGylated nanoparticle injections induce anti-PEG antibody production, which binds subsequent PEGylated particles, activates the complement system, and accelerates clearance [ ]. Clinically, elevated anti-PEG IgM/IgG in patients receiving PEGylated biologics correlates with reduced efficacy and increased toxicity [ , ]. These findings indicate that PEGylated nanoparticles exhibit immunogenicity, with anti-PEG antibodies undermining efficacy via ABC. While ABC effects are less pronounced in chemotherapy due to high lipid doses or cytotoxic drug-mediated suppression of splenic B cells, ABC may exacerbate the effects of non/low-cytotoxic agents requiring repeated dosing (e.g., plasmid DNA or immune stimulants) [ , ]. Future strategies must balance maintaining nanoparticle performance with minimizing immunogenicity to advanced delivery systems.

7.3.

## Environmental toxicity
The exponential growth of NP applications has raised global concerns about their environmental impacts. When NPs enter terrestrial and aquatic systems, they undergo dissociation, dissolution, and aggregation under environmental conditions, affecting food chains. NP toxicity depends on environmental factors and physicochemical properties. The identified fate mechanisms include bioaccumulation, biotransformation, and biomagnification [ ]. Extensive ecotoxicological studies have been conducted on bacteria, algae, zooplankton, fish, and other organisms.
NPs entering marine environments pose significant threats, inducing cytotoxicity and oxidative stress in microalgae and copepods [ , ]. NPs reduce fish hatching rates, increase embryo mortality, and cause cellular swelling, edema, vacuolar degeneration in intestinal epithelial and hepatic cells, and spinal deformities [ ]. Even low concentrations (e.g., AgNPs: EC50 for Chlorella vulgaris = 5 μg/mL; zebrafish = 10.09 μg/mL; Daphnia magna = 1.8 μg/mL) impact aquatic life via trophic transfer. Increased exposure reduces microalgal biomass (via decreased photosynthetic pigments) and increases mortality in D. magna and zebrafish [ ].
Plants, as primary producers, are highly sensitive to nanoparticles. Phytotoxicity may influence pollutant transfer across trophic levels [ ]. Recent studies have shown that CNPs accumulate in plants via air/soil exposure and are transferred trophically, e.g., from lettuce (Lactuca sativa) to snails. Foliar zinc oxide nanoparticles increase wheat grain zinc content, but excess zinc may cause neurotoxicity and immune dysfunction [ , , ]. In a lettuce-cricket-mantis system, soil lanthanum oxide nanoparticles absorbed by lettuce were biomagnified in mantises [ ].
Current evidence highlights the toxicity of NPs to lower trophic organisms and their potential transfer to higher levels, underscoring the need for stringent regulatory measures.
