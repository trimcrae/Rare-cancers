In this study, the role of adrenoceptor alpha 2 A (ADRA2A) in asthma was investigated using bioinformatics, in vitro, and in vivo models. Differential gene expression analysis identified ADRA2A was significantly upregulated in asthmatic tissues. BEAS-2B cells treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) exhibited increased expression of ADRA2A, along with elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and apoptosis. Knockdown of ADRA2A in these cells reduced inflammation and apoptosis, while overexpression exacerbated these effects. In an ovalbumin (OVA)-induced asthma mouse model, ADRA2A knockdown led to a significant reduction in airway hyperresponsiveness, pulmonary edema, inflammatory cell infiltration, and goblet cell hyperplasia. The phosphorylation of extracellular signal-regulated kinases 1 and 2 (ERK1/2), a key signaling pathway involved in inflammation and apoptosis, was also reduced following ADRA2A knockdown, both in vitro and in vivo. In conclusions, these findings suggest that ADRA2A contributes to the pathogenesis of asthma by modulating the ERK signaling pathway, making it a potential therapeutic target for managing airway inflammation and remodeling in asthmatic patients.

## Introduction
Asthma is a chronic inflammatory condition of airways involving symptoms such as coughing, wheezing, chest tightness, and variable airflow limitation [ ], which are often triggered by environmental allergens, including house dust mites, pollen, and animal dander, as well as non-specific stimuli such as tobacco smoke and air pollution [ ]. This complex and heterogeneous condition is affected via genetic predisposition combined with external factors, with atopy and allergic sensitization defined as significant risk factors [ ]. In addition, increased viral infections during early childhood and exposure to environmental pollutants further enhance the progression and exacerbation of asthma [ , ].
Epithelial inflammation and dysfunction are crucial to asthma pathogenesis, contributing to the chronicity and severity of the disease [ , ]. The bronchial epithelium acts as a physical barrier and actively participates in the immune response to inhaled irritants and allergens [ ]. In asthmatic individuals, the airway epithelium, which serves as a protective barrier against environmental insults, is frequently damaged, leading to a compromised epithelial barrier [ ]. This disruption is characterized by the ciliated cell detachment, the development of epithelial cell aggregates termed Creola bodies in sputum, and enhanced allergen permeability, which allows for deeper infiltration of environmental triggers into the airway tissue [ , ]. A key component of this dysfunction is the loss of E-cadherin, a critical cell-cell adhesion molecule. The reduction in E-cadherin disrupts tight junctions (TJs) and adherens junctions (AJs), leading to increased permeability of epithelium and promoting airway remodeling [ , ]. This remodeling includes subepithelial fibrosis, goblet cell hyperplasia, and elevated smooth muscle mass, which collectively contribute to the chronicity of asthma and the exacerbation of airway hyper-responsiveness [ ]. In turn, the compromised barrier function further heightens airway sensitivity to environmental triggers and the production of proinflammatory cytokines that exacerbate airway inflammation [ , ]. Dysfunctional epithelial cells secrete excessive amounts of cytokines, including IL-6 and IL-8, subsequently recruiting immune cells to the inflammation site, perpetuating the inflammatory cycle, and contributing to airway remodeling [ ]. Therefore, identifying factors modulating epithelial functions and inflammation might be crucial for developing targeted therapies aimed at restoring epithelial integrity and mitigating the inflammation in asthma.
Herein, bioinformatic analysis was first conducted to identify adrenoceptor alpha 2 A (ADRA2A) as a key gene upregulated in asthma. In vitro experiments were then performed using BEAS-2B cells, where ADRA2A was knocked down or overexpressed to assess its effects on lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammation, apoptosis, and extracellular signal-regulated kinases 1 and 2 (ERK1/2) pathway activation. In vivo, the impact of ADRA2A knockdown upon airway inflammation, hyper-responsiveness, and tissue remodeling was examined using an ovalbumin (OVA)-induced asthma mouse model. These combined approaches were designed to investigate the effect of ADRA2A on asthma development.

## Materials and methods

## Bioinformatics analysis
Transcriptomic data were obtained from the public databases, including three OVA and aluminium hydroxide-induced asthma mice samples and three control samples (PRJNA789695) [ ] and microarray data from two OVA-induced mouse asthma models and two control samples (GSE180287) [ ]. Each dataset was analyzed independently using the fold-change (FC) method for differential expression (i.e. without merging datasets). The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were screened based on |logFC| >3 and p < 0.01. Overlaps between the two databases yielded 25 genes showing consistent directional changes. These overlapping genes were submitted to functional enrichment using Metascape (http://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1) and disease association via DisGeNET, to annotate pathways and disease relevance of the candidates.

## Cell culture and LPS stimulation
Human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B) were procured from ATCC (CRL-3588; Manassas, USA) and kept within DMEM (Gibco, Grand Island, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. For the inflammation model, cells were subjected to a 12-h treatment with various concentrations of lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) to induce an inflammatory response. To specifically inhibit ERK signaling, BEAS-2B cells were treated with 1 µM ERK1/2 inhibitor (U0126, HY-12031 A, MedChem Express, Monmouth Junction, USA) for 24-h [ ]. Control cells were subjected to treatment with an equivalent volume of sterile PBS.

## Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay
The CCK-8 kit (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) was employed as per the protocols of the manufacturer to assess cell viability. In short, cells were grown in 96-well plates (5,000 cells/well), followed by an overnight incubation. After treatment, the cell culture medium was supplemented with CCK-8 reagent, followed by a 2-h incubation at 37 °C. Next, a microplate reader (BioTek, Biotek Winooski, USA) was applied to measure the absorbance at a wavelength of 450 nm.

## Cell transfection
BEAS‑2B cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well in 6‑well plates and grown to ~ 70% confluency in complete medium. To assess the role of ADRA2A, BEAS‑2B cells were transfected with either ADRA2A siRNA (GenePharma, Shanghai, China) for knock‑down or an ADRA2A overexpression plasmid. For transfection, 2 µg of plasmid DNA (either ADRA2A‑overexpression vector or empty vector) or 50 pmol ADRA2A siRNA (or non‑targeting siRNA control) was diluted in 100 µL serum‑free medium. Transfections were performed using OriTrans®PEI-X or OriTrans®PEI-DNA (ORI2311, ORI2305, Ori‑Bio, Changsha, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were transfected for 24 h before LPS treatment. The sequences of the over‑expression plasmid and siRNAs are listed in Supplementary Table . Transfection efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR and western blot of ADRA2A expression in each group, and all experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates.

## Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was extracted from BEAS-2B cells and lung tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The purity and quantity of RNA were assessed by spectrophotometry to verify 260/280 and 260/230 ratios. RNA samples with A260/A280 ratios between 1.9 and 2.1 and A260/A230 ratios above 2.0 were used for subsequent experiments. RNA integrity was confirmed by gel electrophoresis. Samples that did not meet quality thresholds were excluded. For each experimental condition, three independent biological replicates (n = 3) were prepared. Using 1 µg of total RNA, reverse transcription into cDNA was performed with the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan). SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, USA) on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) was utilized to perform qRT-PCR. GAPDH served as an internal control, and relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Prior to analysis, the stability of GAPDH expression was assessed across experimental groups to confirm minimal variation under inflammatory and control conditions. The used specific primers were listed in Supplementary Table .

## Flow cytometry analysis of apoptosis
The Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit (BD Biosciences, San Jose, USA) was employed as per the protocols of the manufacturer to assess BEAS-2B cell apoptosis. After being harvested and rinsed with PBS, cells were resuspended within a binding buffer and then stained using Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI). A FACSCanto II flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) was utilized to analyze stained cells, and FlowJo software (BD Biosciences) was applied to analyze data. The Annexin-FITC signal is shown as the horizontal coordinate and the PI signal as the vertical coordinate. The Annexin-FITC+/PI+ cells (Q3) are normal cells, the Annexin-FITC+/PI− cells (Q4) are early apoptotic cells, the Annexin-FITC−/PI+ cells (Q2) are middle and late apoptotic cells, and double negatives cells (Q1) are necrotic cells. The sum of the early and late apoptotic cells constituted the total number of apoptotic cells, which was presented as the percentage of the total cells.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for cytokine detection
The commercial ELISA kits (Cusabio, Wuhan, China; TNF-α: CSB-E04740h/ CSB-E04741m, IL-6: CSB-E04638h/CSB-E04639m, IL-8: CSB-E04641h, IL-4: CSB-E04634m, IL-13: CSB-E04602m) were employed following the manufacturer’s protocols to measure tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin 6 (IL-6), IL-8, IL-4, and IL-13 concentrations within the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) or cell culture supernatants. In addition, an ELISA kit (439807, Biolegend, San Diego, USA) was applied to quantify OVA-specific IgE levels within serum. A microplate reader (BioTek) was used to measure absorbance for all ELISA assays at a wavelength of 450 nm, and standard curves generated with known concentrations of the respective analytes were applied to calculate concentrations.

## OVA-induced asthma model in mice
Female Balb/c mice (6–8 weeks) were procured from Hunan SJA laboratory animal Co. Ltd. (Changsha, China) and used for in vivo experiments, with 6 mice per group. Firstly, mice were subjected to sensitization with 25 µg of ovalbumin (OVA, Sigma-Aldrich) emulsified in 1 mg of aluminum hydroxide in 200 µl PBS, administered via intraperitoneal injection on days 0, 7 and 14. Then, on days 21 to 28, mice were subjected to a challenge with 2% OVA aerosol in a closed chamber (20 × 30 × 20 cm3) for 30 min. Mice were allocated into the following groups (N = 6 per group): Control, OVA, OVA + Lv-shNC (control lentivirus), and OVA + Lv-shADRA2A (lentivirus containing shADRA2A sequence). Lentivirus (1 × 108 TU/mouse) was administered via tail vein injection 48 h before the first OVA aerosol challenge. To verify the role of the ERK signaling in asthma mice model, 12 asthma mice were randomly divided into 2 groups (N = 6 per group): OVA, and OVA + U0126 (ERK1/2 inhibitor). The 30 mg/kg U0126 was given by intraperitoneal administration 2 h before and 6 h after each OVA aerosol challenge [ ]. Finally, after the last time OVA challenge for 24 h, the mice were sacrificed to collect BALF and lung tissues.

## Preparation of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)
Following the final OVA challenge, mice were sacrificed, and the lungs were lavaged using 1 mL of sterile PBS through a tracheal cannula to collect bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). The harvested BALF was subjected to 10-min centrifugation at 300 × g at 4 °C. The supernatant was maintained at − 80 °C for future cytokine analysis, with the cell pellet being resuspended in PBS.

## Wright-giemsa staining
The cell suspension obtained from BALF was cytospun onto glass slides and allowed to air dry, followed by staining with the Wright-Giemsa staining method to identify and quantify the total number of inflammatory cells, eosinophils, neutrophils, lymphocyte, and macrophage. A light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was applied to observe the stained slides and cell differentials were calculated by counting at least 200 cells per slide.

## Bronchial hyperresponsiveness (BHR) assay
Following the final OVA challenge, a whole‑body plethysmograph system (PLY4111; Buxco Electronics Inc, Wilmington, USA) connected to a ventilator (Model 683; Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, USA) was applied to assess bronchial hyperresponsiveness. Mice were anesthetized with 2% pentobarbital sodium, and tracheostomized before being mechanically ventilated. A graded series of methacholine aerosols (0, 6.25, 12.5, and 25 mg/mL) was delivered, and airway resistance (RL) was recorded for each dose.

## Histological analysis
Lung tissues were harvested from the mice following sacrifice, followed by 24-h fixing at room temperature (RT) within 10% neutral-buffered formalin. After fixation, the tissue samples were dehydrated with a series of graded alcohol, cleared with xylene, and paraffin-embedded. A microtome was used to cut the tissues into 4-µm thick slices, which were then mounted on glass slides.
For the examination of general lung tissue morphology and inflammation, slices were subjected to staining with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). In short, slices were deparaffinized with xylene, rehydrated through graded alcohol, subjected to 5-min staining with hematoxylin, and then 2-min counterstaining with eosin. Slices were then dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with a synthetic resin. To detect ADRA2A level in mice lung tissues, slices were subjected to immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining. Briefly, lung tissue slices were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and incubated with primary antibody against ADRA2A (14266-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) overnight at 4 °C. After washing, sections were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP) secondary antibody, and staining was developed using a 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB) regent. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted with synthetic resin.
To evaluate goblet cell hyperplasia and mucus production, adjacent slices were subjected to staining with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) reagent, and then deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to 10-min treatment with periodic acid. After washing, slices were subjected to 15-min staining with Schiff’s reagent, and then counterstained with hematoxylin. After the staining procedures, slices were dehydrated, cleared, and coverslipped using a permanent mounting medium. A light microscope (Olympus) was employed to examine stained slices.
Inflammation score and goblet cell hyperplasia score (PAS score) in the lungs were obtained by blinded assessment [ ]. Inflammation was scored as follows: score 0 (no inflammatory cells were observed), score 1 (inflammatory cells were occasionally observed), score 2 (bronchi were surrounded by 1–3 layer of inflammatory cells), score 3 (bronchi or vessels were surrounded by 4–5 layer of inflammatory cells), and score 4 (most bronchi or vessels were surrounded by more than 5 layer of inflammatory cells). The pathological changes were quantified according to a modified five-point scoring system (scores 0–4) based on the percentage of goblet cells in the epithelium: score 0 (no goblet cells), score 1 (< 25%), score 2 (25–50%), score 3 (51–75%), and score 4 (>75%). The mean inflammation score or the mean goblet cell hyperplasia score was calculated for each mouse.

## Immunoblotting
Radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors was used to extract proteins from BEAS-2B cells and lung tissues. The bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was employed to determine protein contents. Following electrophoresis by Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), equal protein amounts were electroblotted from the gel to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked using 5% skim milk, followed by incubation with primary antibodies against ADRA2A (14266-1-AP), caspase-3 (19677-1-AP), cleaved caspase-3 (ab2302 (Human)/ab214430 (mouse), Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Bax (50599-2-Ig, Proteintech), Bcl-2 (12789-1-AP, Proteintech), p-ERK (sc-81492, Thr 202 and Tyr 204 phosphorylation sites, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, USA), and ERK (16443-1-AP, Proteintech). HRP-labeled secondary antibodies were used, and an ECL kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was applied to detect the blot signals, with normalization to GAPDH.

## Statistical analysis
The cell experiments were performed for three biological replicates. The animal experiments were performed for six biological replicates. All experimental data were expressed in terms of mean ± standard deviation (SD). GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Prism, La Jolla, USA) was applied to perform statistical analyses. The Shapiro-Wilk test was conducted to assess the data distribution normality. For normally distributed data, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was performed to assess differences between groups, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied to assess differences among groups. For dose–response curves, we used a two-way ANOVA (factors: group × dose) with Tukey’s post-hoc test to assess differences across groups and doses. For non-normally distributed data, non-parametric tests such as the Mann-Whitney U test were carried out to assess differences between groups, and the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test were used to assess differences among groups. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Bioinformatics and clinical sample analysis identifying asthma-related factors
For the identification of key genes related to epithelial inflammation and dysfunction in asthma, transcriptomic analysis was performed on GSE180287, which includes two OVA-induced murine asthma models and two control samples, and PRJNA789695, consisting of three murine asthma samples and three control samples. Differential expression analysis identified 91 DEGs from PRJNA789695 and 292 DEGs from GSE180287 using the threshold of |logFC|>3, p < 0.01, with 25 overlapping DEGs showing consistent expression alteration trends (Fig.  A). Then, 25 overlapping DEGs were applied for Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment using Metascape, revealing associations with processes including the modulation of leukocyte proliferation, positive modulation of MAP kinase activity, inflammatory response, and negative regulation of immune effector processes (Fig.  B). DisGeNET analysis linked these genes to airway diseases, allergic asthma, and other related conditions (Fig.  B). Among the DEGs, Adra2a, interleukin 5 receptor subunit alpha (Il5ra), anterior gradient 2 (Agr2), and chitinase-like protein 4 (Chil4) were related to the inflammatory response and remarkably increased within the asthma samples relative to normal control group within both datasets (Fig.  C; Supplementary Table ).

 

## Evaluation of key factors in LPS-induced inflammation model using BEAS-2B cells
For a further selection of key factors, BEAS-2B cells, a human bronchial epithelial cell line, were subjected to 12-h treatment with increasing concentrations of LPS (1 µg/ml, 5 µg/ml, 10 µg/ml, 25 µg/ml, 50 µg/ml) to induce an inflammatory response. The CCK-8 assay was applied to assess cell viability, thereby determining the optimal LPS concentration for subsequent experiments, indicating that cell viability was decreased dose-dependently, with remarkable reduction observed at higher LPS concentrations; 5 µg/ml LPS was used for the following experiments as it was the minimum dose inhibiting cell viability (Fig.  A). Following 5 µg/ml LPS treatment, ELISA was employed to measure the secretion levels of inflammatory factors TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8 within the culture supernatant. A significantly increase of all three cytokines was observed, confirming LPS-induced inflammatory response (Fig.  B). Apoptosis was evaluated in BEAS-2B cells after LPS exposure using Annexin V/PI staining followed by flow cytometry; an increase of apoptotic cells was observed after LPS treatment relative to normal controls (Fig.  C). To further explore the impact of LPS upon key inflammatory mediators, the expression levels of ADRA2A, IL5RA, and AGR2 were quantified in BEAS-2B cells (human did not have CHIL4 gene, therefore, it was deleted in the following investigation). LPS treatment led to a significant upregulation of ADRA2A expression, along with moderate increases in IL5RA and AGR2 expression (Fig.  D). Therefore, ADRA2A was selected for the following investigations.

 

## ADRA2A knockdown attenuates LPS-induced inflammation and apoptosis in BEAS-2B cells
As for the specific effects of ADRA2A on epithelial inflammation and functions, BEAS-2B cells were subjected to transfection with small interfering RNA against ADRA2A (si-ADRA2A) to achieve ADRA2A knockdown, which was confirmed by qRT-PCR and western blot. ADRA2A mRNA and protein level was notably suppressed by ADRA2A knockdown (Fig.  A-B). Following transfection, the cells were subjected to 12-h exposure to 5 µg/ml LPS. The CCK-8 assay was performed to assess cell viability. The LPS stimulation considerably reduced cell viability in comparison with normal controls, while ADRA2A knockdown partially restored cell viability, indicating a protective effect against LPS-induced cytotoxicity (Fig.  C). ELISA was applied to measure the levels of pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8) within the culture supernatant. LPS exposure significantly increased the secretion of these cytokines, and this effect was mitigated by ADRA2A knockdown, suggesting a reduction in the inflammatory response (Fig.  D). Annexin V/PI staining was employed to evaluate cell apoptosis with flow cytometry. LPS treatment markedly increased BEAS-2B cell apoptosis, which was significantly reduced in cells with ADRA2A knockdown, indicating that ADRA2A contributes to LPS-induced apoptosis (Fig.  E). Protein contents of apoptosis-associated markers, such as caspase-3, cleaved caspase-3, Bax, and Bcl-2, were analyzed. LPS stimulation elevated the cleaved caspase-3/caspase-3 ratio and Bax proteins, while reducing Bcl-2 levels. Knockdown of ADRA2A attenuated these effects, further supporting the protective role of ADRA2A knockdown in reducing apoptosis in response to LPS (Fig.  F).

 

## ADRA2A overexpression exacerbates LPS-induced inflammation and apoptosis in BEAS-2B cells
Then, an ADRA2A-overexpressing plasmid (OE-ADRA2A) was transfected to achieve ADRA2A overexpression within BEAS-2B cells, and the overexpression was confirmed using qRT-PCR and western blot. ADRA2A mRNA and protein expression levels showed to be dramatically increased within the OE-ADRA2A group in comparison with the vector control group (Fig.  A-B). After confirming overexpression, cells were subjected to 12-h treatment with 5 µg/ml LPS, and the CCK-8 assay was conducted to assess cell viability. LPS treatment reduced cell viability, and this effect was further exacerbated by ADRA2A overexpression, suggesting that ADRA2A overexpression increases susceptibility to LPS-induced cytotoxicity (Fig.  C). ELISA was applied to measure the secretion levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-8 within the culture supernatant. ADRA2A overexpression significantly increased the LPS-induced secretion of these inflammatory factors compared to the vector control, indicating an enhanced inflammatory response (Fig.  D). Annexin V/PI staining was employed to assess cell apoptosis with flow cytometry. LPS-induced apoptosis was shown to be remarkably elevated within ADRA2A-overexpressing cells relative to the vector control group, demonstrating that ADRA2A overexpression promotes LPS-induced apoptosis (Fig.  E). Consistently, LPS treatment increased the cleaved caspase-3/caspase-3 ratio and Bax proteins, while reducing Bcl-2 levels. These effects were further amplified by ADRA2A overexpression, indicating that ADRA2A enhances the apoptotic response to LPS in BEAS-2B cells (Fig.  F).

 

## ADRA2A knockdown ameliorates OVA-induced asthma-like features in model mice
After confirming the in vitro effects of ADRA2A knockdown/overexpression on epithelial inflammation and dysfunction, the OVA-induced asthma model was established in mice, ADRA2A knockdown was achieved by lentivirus injection, and the in vivo effects of ADRA2A knockdown were investigated. Immunoblotting was used to assess ADRA2A protein levels within lung tissue samples from OVA-induced asthmatic mice. The results confirmed that ADRA2A was considerably elevated within the OVA group in comparison with normal controls. ADRA2A knockdown via lentiviral shRNA (Lv-shADRA2A) effectively reduced its expression, as demonstrated by decreased protein levels relative to the OVA + Lv-shNC group (Fig.  A). Airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) was measured upon methacholine challenge. OVA treatment significantly increased airway resistance (RL) compared to the control group, indicating heightened AHR. However, ADRA2A knockdown in the OVA + Lv-shADRA2A group significantly attenuated this response, suggesting improved airway function (Fig.  B). The wet-to-dry weight ratio of lung tissues, an index of pulmonary edema, showed to be significantly elevated within the OVA group compared to controls, whereas ADRA2A knockdown reduced this ratio, indicating a reduction in edema and fluid accumulation in the lungs (Fig.  C). H&E and PAS staining were employed to perform histological analysis of lung tissue sections, revealing extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells within the peribronchial and perivascular areas of OVA-treated group, which was notably reduced in the ADRA2A knockdown group. PAS staining showed increased goblet cell hyperplasia and mucus production within the OVA group, which were significantly decreased following ADRA2A knockdown (Fig.  D). Moreover, the inflammatory and PAS scores were notably elevated within the OVA group compared to controls, whereas ADRA2A knockdown reduced these scores (Fig.  E-F). IHC staining results showed that ADRA2A protein level was enhanced by OVA treatment, while knockdown of ADRA2A inhibited ADRA2A protein level (Fig.  D and G). Wright-Giemsa staining was utilized to quantify the total number of inflammatory cells, eosinophils, neutrophils, lymphocyte, and macrophage within BALF. OVA treatment resulted in a significantly increase within all cellular types, reflecting robust airway inflammation, whereas ADRA2A knockdown markedly decreased the number of these inflammatory cells, suggesting a diminished inflammatory response (Fig.  H). ELISA was applied to measure OVA-specific IgE levels within serum. The OVA group exhibited elevated IgE levels in comparison with normal controls, revealing an allergic response. ADRA2A knockdown resulted in a remarkable decrease of IgE levels, suggesting a reduction within allergen-induced sensitization (Fig.  I). qPCR was performed to assess the mRNA levels of inflammatory mediators (TNF-α and IL-6), and Th2 cytokines (IL-4 and IL-13) within lung tissue samples. Both cytokines were considerably increased within the OVA group, while ADRA2A knockdown led to a marked decrease in their expression levels, indicating a reduction in inflammation (Fig.  J). Consistently, ELISA revealed that TNF-α, IL-6, IL-4, and IL-13 were remarkably elevated within the OVA group and were reduced following ADRA2A knockdown (Fig.  K). In addition, OVA treatment increased the cleaved caspase-3/caspase-3 ratio and Bax proteins, while decreasing Bcl-2 expression, indicating enhanced apoptosis (Fig.  L). ADRA2A knockdown significantly mitigated these effects, suggesting a protective role against apoptosis within the lung tissue samples of asthmatic mice (Fig.  L).

 

## ADRA2A knockdown inhibits the ERK signaling in vitro and in vivo
ADRA2A could mediate ERK1/2 phosphorylation [ ]; ERK1/2 activation is involved in apoptosis and inflammatory responses within human airway epithelial cells [ , ]. As a result, the effects of ADRA2A upon the ERK pathway activation were investigated. p-ERK and ERK protein contents were determined within BEAS-2B cells and OVA-induced asthma model mice. Figure shows that the phosphorylation of ERK1/2 (Thr 202/Tyr 204) was significantly elevated in LPS-stimulated BEAS-2B cells and OVA-induced mice lungs, whereas partially decreased by ADRA2A knockdown. In LPS-stimulated BEAS-2B cells, ADRA2A overexpression further promoted the phosphorylation of ERK1/2.

 

## Effects of ERK1/2 inhibition on LPS-induced inflammation and apoptosis in BEAS-2B cells and OVA-induced asthma mice
To further investigate the role of ERK1/2 signaling in asthma, the ERK1/2 inhibitor U0126 was treated the LPS-stimulated BEAS‑2B cells and the OVA-induced asthma mice. Under LPS treatment, BEAS-2B cells transfected with OE-ADRA2A showed a marked increase in p-ERK1/2 protein compared to control group; this elevation was significantly reduced upon treatment with U0126 (Fig.  A). CCK-8 assay results showed cell viability was inhibited by ADRA2A overexpression, while the effect of ADRA2A overexpression was partially rescued by U0126 (Fig.  B). The inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α) and cell apoptosis were promoted by ADRA2A overexpression and then reduced with U0126 treatment (Fig.  C-D). Bcl-2 protein level was suppressed by ADRA2A overexpression and partially restored with ERK inhibition (Fig.  E).

 

In the in vivo asthma model, U0126 treatment notably inhibited p-ERK1/2 protein level, but did not affect ADRA2A protein expression in lung tissues from OVA-asthma mice (Fig.  A). Functional measures results demonstrated that airway hyperresponsiveness (Fig.  B), lung wet/dry weight ratio (Fig.  C), inflammatory infiltration (Fig.  D-E), goblet cell hyperplasia (Fig.  D and F), BALF inflammatory cell counts (Fig.  G), serum OVA-specific IgE (Fig.  H), and lung cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, IL-4, IL-13 levels (Fig.  I-J) were all significantly reduced with U0126 intervention. These results supported that the ADRA2A-ERK1/2 axis plays a modulatory role in both epithelial cell death/inflammation in vitro and allergic asthma phenotypes in vivo.

 

## Discussion
In this study, ADRA2A emerged as a key gene associated with epithelial inflammation and dysfunction in asthma. In vitro, manipulating ADRA2A in LPS-treated BEAS-2B cells altered cytokine release and apoptosis, and in vivo ADRA2A knockdown improved hallmark features of OVA-induced asthma. The reduction in ERK1/2 phosphorylation in both settings suggested ADRA2A drived asthma-related pathology via the ERK pathway. Thus, ADRA2A represented a promising target for therapeutic intervention in airway inflammation and remodelling in asthma.
ADRA2A (also α2A-AR), which belongs to the G-protein-coupled receptor superfamily [ ], has been increasingly recognized for its effect on regulating immune responses and inflammation, particularly within the lung. Studies have demonstrated that norepinephrine (NE) released by the sympathetic nervous system can influence the immune system, with the α2A-AR subtype exerting a vital effect on this process [ ]. Activation of α2A-AR has been shown to contribute to the pro-inflammatory response, as evidenced by increased levels of inflammatory factors including TNF-α and IL-6, particularly during disorders including sepsis and acute lung injury [ – ]. Moreover, blocking α2A-AR was found to prevent LPS-induced pulmonary damage by reducing alveolar-capillary barrier permeability, inflammatory cell infiltration, and cytokine production, thereby attenuating lung inflammation and injury [ ]. Taken together, α2A-AR acts as a crucial mediator of inflammation within the lung, indicating that it has the potential to be a therapeutic target for inflammatory lung disorders. In this study, analysis of two datasets, GSE180287 and PRJNA789695, revealed 25 overlapping DEGs that were consistently altered in asthma mice’ lung tissues. GO enrichment and DisGeNET analysis further linked these genes to inflammatory processes and airway diseases, highlighting ADRA2A as a potential regulator in asthma. In vitro experiments using LPS-treated BEAS-2B cells confirmed that ADRA2A expression was significantly upregulated following LPS exposure, alongside increases in pro-inflammatory cytokines and apoptosis. Therefore, ADRA2A might exert a crucial effect on asthma pathogenesis.
The effect of ADRA2A on epithelial inflammation and dysfunction in asthma is further elucidated by examining its impact on epithelial inflammation and apoptosis in vitro and overall lung function in vivo. Within asthma, the airway epithelium is frequently exposed to inflammatory stimuli such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which could trigger a cascade of immune responses and promote cellular damage in epithelial cells [ ]. Consequently, epithelial damage leads to increased permeability of the epithelial barrier, allowing allergens and pathogens to penetrate deeper into the airway tissue, exacerbating local inflammation, and contributing to airway remodeling and chronic disease progression [ ]. The damaged epithelium further perpetuates inflammation by releasing cytokines and chemokines, recruiting and activating immune cells, thereby sustaining a cycle of ongoing inflammation and tissue injury characteristic of asthma [ ]. Herein, LPS-induced release of inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-8) was observed within BEAS-2B cells, which was partially improved by ADRA2A knockdown but further enhanced by ADRA2A overexpression, suggesting that ADRA2A contributes to the bronchial epithelial inflammation. In addition, the enhanced epithelial cell apoptosis serves as a critical mechanism via which several environmental factors exacerbate asthma. For instance, in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that exposure to PM2.5 affected the experimental asthma severity via promoting the apoptosis of epithelial cells [ ]. In this study, LPS-induced epithelial apoptosis was significantly attenuated in ADRA2A knockdown cells but enhanced by ADRA2A overexpression, suggesting that ADRA2A enhances epithelial cell apoptosis in response to inflammatory stimuli, contributing to the airway remodeling and epithelial barrier dysfunction seen within asthma.
 In vivo, ADRA2A knockdown within an OVA-induced asthma mouse model significantly improved AHR, a key feature of asthma characterized by excessive airway narrowing under stimuli [ ]. Decreased AHR indicates that ADRA2A contributes to the exaggerated typical airway constriction of asthma. Additionally, the wet-to-dry weight ratio of lung tissues, an index of pulmonary edema and fluid accumulation [ ], was reduced following ADRA2A knockdown, suggesting that ADRA2A plays a role in promoting lung inflammation and edema. Histological analysis further revealed that ADRA2A knockdown mitigated inflammatory cell infiltration and goblet cell hyperplasia, the latter being a hallmark of mucus overproduction in asthma that contributes to airway obstruction [ ]. The reduction in these pathological features upon ADRA2A knockdown indicates its involvement in sustaining the chronic inflammatory and remodeling processes in the asthmatic lung. Furthermore, ADRA2A knockdown decreased the levels of OVA-specific IgE in the BALF, reflecting a reduction in allergen-caused sensitization, which is a key driver of asthma exacerbations [ , ]. These findings suggest that ADRA2A could contribute to modulating the immune response to allergens. The accompanying reduction in the mRNA expression and protein levels of inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-6 [ , ], following ADRA2A silencing, further supports its role in promoting inflammation in asthma, consistent with our in vitro findings.
Regarding the underlying signaling pathways that might mediate the effects of ADRA2A on epithelial inflammation and dysfunction, as well as asthma-like features in mice models, the ERK signaling attracted the attention. Erk1/2 activation is involved in apoptosis and inflammatory responses in human airway epithelial cells [ , ]. A growing body of evidence from multiple in vitro investigations suggests that inhibiting ERK might decrease asthma-associated inflammatory alterations [ ]. As previously reported, an asthma model of advanced eosinophilia in mice demonstrated the effects of ERK inhibitors on promoting various inflammation/remodeling [ ]. Recent studies have shown that inhibiting ERK1/2 signaling can reduce IgE production in lung tissue [ ]. In the study, ERK1/2 inhibitor restrained ADRA2A-promoted inflammation and apoptosis in BEAS-2B cells and relieved OVA-induced asthma-like features in model mice. Notably, ADRA2A could mediate ERK1/2 phosphorylation [ ]. Herein, ADRA2A knockdown led to a remarkable reduction within Erk1/2 phosphorylation (at Thr 202 and Tyr 204 phosphorylation sites), both in vitro and in vivo, revealing that ADRA2A might exert its pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic effects via the ERK signaling activation to regulate OVA-specific IgE levels.
The limitations of this study still warrant consideration. Firstly, our findings showed that modulation of ADRA2A affected ERK1/2 phosphorylation and downstream apoptotic and inflammatory responses; however, the precise upstream mechanism remains to be delineated. Classically, ADRA2A is a G protein-coupled receptor that classically inhibits adenylyl cyclase, reducing intracellular cyclic AMP (cAMP) [ ], which may in turn relieve PKA-mediated suppression of the MAPK/ERK pathway [ ]. At the same time, experimental data from other systems suggest that α₂-adrenergic receptors can also activate ERK1/2 through arrestin/βγ-subunit-mediated signalling independent of cAMP reduction [ ]. Without measurement of cAMP, PKA/EPAC activity or βγ-subunit involvement in our current work, we cannot definitively favour one pathway. Secondly, while we used the human-derived BEAS‑2B bronchial epithelial cell line for mechanistic studies, and this line is widely used in asthma and epithelial‑inflammation research, it must be acknowledged that BEAS‑2B cells are immortalized and may not fully replicate the physiological characteristics of primary human bronchial epithelial cells. Future studies should assess intracellular cAMP levels, PKA/EPAC activity, and use selective inhibitors of these pathways to clarify how ADRA2A engages ERK signalling in asthma. Moreover, the roles of ADRA2A should further validate in primary bronchial epithelial cells or human asthmatic tissues in future work.

## Conclusions
Our findings highlight that ADRA2A functions as a critical mediator in epithelial inflammation and dysfunction in asthma, reinforcing its potential as a therapeutic target for airway inflammation and remodeling in asthma. By modulating ADRA2A expression, the key pathological features of asthma, including cytokine production, epithelial apoptosis, and airway responsiveness, were regulated, and the involvement of ERK signalling was demonstrated. These data suggest that therapeutic interventions aimed at ADRA2A, like receptor antagonists or small-molecule inhibitors, could complement existing asthma treatments by targeting epithelial and signalling abnormalities. Furthermore, ADRA2A may serve as a biomarker of epithelial injury or pathway activation in asthma, enabling asthma patient stratification and monitoring of therapeutic response.
