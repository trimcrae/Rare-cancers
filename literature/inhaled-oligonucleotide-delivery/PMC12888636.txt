## Abstract
Pulmonary diseases, encompassing asthma, lung cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and pulmonary fibrosis, constitute major contributors to global morbidity and mortality, imposing substantial burdens on healthcare systems worldwide. While RNA-based therapeutics have emerged as promising tools for modulating disease pathophysiology at the molecular level, achieving efficient and lung-specific RNA delivery remains a significant challenge that limits clinical translation. Unlike previous reviews that primarily catalog delivery system performance metrics, this review uniquely integrates structure-function design principles with clinical translation insights, providing mechanistic understanding of how specific physicochemical parameters govern pulmonary tropism and therapeutic efficacy. We systematically examine both synthetic and biologically derived carriers, with particular focus on lung-targeted delivery strategies including inhalation, intravenous targeting, and local pulmonary administration. We critically analyze lessons learned from clinical trial failures, including ALN-RSV01 and MRT-5005, to identify key barriers to successful translation. Furthermore, we discuss the translational outlook of these systems, encompassing formulation stability, immunological compatibility, and scalable manufacturing considerations. By bridging mechanistic understanding with clinical development challenges, this review provides a roadmap for accelerating the clinical translation of RNA therapeutics for pulmonary diseases.

## Introduction
Pulmonary diseases—including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), and lung cancer—rank among the leading causes of global morbidity and mortality, profoundly compromising patient quality of life and imposing substantial healthcare burdens [ ]. These pathologies arise from complex multifactorial interactions involving genetic susceptibilities, environmental pollutants, and microbial pathogens, resulting in heterogeneous disease manifestations that challenge therapeutic intervention [ ]. The COVID-19 pandemic underscored the inherent vulnerability of the respiratory system and exposed critical deficiencies in existing preventative and therapeutic modalities, thereby catalyzing a global intensification of research into fundamental lung disease mechanisms and innovative treatment paradigms [ , ].
Conventional therapies emphasize symptomatic management rather than disease etiology, limiting long-term efficacy [ ]. RNA-based therapeutics—including antisense oligonucleotides(ASOs), small interfering RNAs(siRNAs), circular RNA(circRNA), and messenger RNAs(mRNAs)—offer an alternative approach by modulating gene expression to restore functional proteins or suppress pathogenic protein synthesis at the molecular level [ , ]. ASOs are single-stranded, chemically modified oligonucleotides that hybridize to complementary RNA targets, inducing gene silencing through RNase H1-mediated degradation, steric blockade of ribosomal translation, or modulation of pre-mRNA splicing [ ]. siRNAs are double-stranded RNA duplexes that load into the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), guiding sequence-specific cleavage and degradation of target mRNAs, resulting in potent and sustained gene silencing [ ]. In contrast, mRNAs are engineered transcripts that encode therapeutic proteins; upon delivery into the cytoplasm, they are translated by the host ribosomes to produce functional proteins, enabling applications such as protein replacement therapy, vaccination, or genome editing [ ]. circRNA, a covalently closed single-stranded nucleic acid characterized by a continuous loop structure lacking free 5’ and 3’ termini. In comparison to conventional linear mRNA, circRNA possesses several key biophysical and functional advantages, including substantially heightened resistance to exonuclease-mediated degradation (owing to the absence of free ends), extended protein expression kinetics resulting from enhanced stability, and reduced immunogenicity, characteristics that collectively position circRNA as a potentially transformative modality in therapeutic development [ ].While ASOs, circRNA and siRNAs primarily function to downregulate gene expression, mRNAs facilitate transient protein expression. These approaches herald a paradigm shift in pulmonary medicine by offering therapeutic interventions tailored at the molecular level [ , ]. Nevertheless, the pulmonary delivery of RNA therapeutics remains constrained by the physicochemical instability of RNA, rapid enzymatic degradation, and poor tissue specificity, all of which limit therapeutic outcomes [ – ]. To address these formidable barriers, both viral and non-viral nanocarrier platforms have been extensively investigated as RNA delivery vehicles. Non-viral systems—particularly lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), polymeric nanoparticles, peptide-based vectors, and extracellular vesicles such as exosomes—have demonstrated considerable translational promise attributable to their favorable safety profiles, structural modularity, and scalable manufacturing routes [ ]. The clinical validation of RNA-based therapeutics, exemplified by the FDA-approved siRNA therapeutic Patisiran and multiple LNP-formulated mRNA vaccines, compellingly underscores the transformative potential of nanotechnology-enabled delivery platforms [ , ]. Although numerous reviews have cataloged biomaterial performance metrics in RNA delivery applications, relatively few have systematically addressed the mechanistic strategies specifically required for effective mucosal barrier penetration and cell-type-specific targeting within the complex and heterogeneous lung microenvironment [ ].Recent technological breakthroughs in nanocarrier design have demonstrated the feasibility of achieving lung-specific gene modulation with clinically relevant efficacy. For instance, Xue et al. engineered novel siloxane-derived ionizable lipids (SiLNPs)that, upon intravenous administration in murine models, exhibited predominantly lung-localized biodistribution as quantified by mass spectrometry-based organ analysis. These SiLNPs mediated robust luciferase reporter gene expression that was markedly enriched in pulmonary tissues, with minimal off-target expression detected in the liver, spleen, or kidneys [ ].Complementing this lipid-based approach, Jensen et al. developed cationic polymer-based dry powder inhalation formulations that demonstrated 73% target gene silencing efficiency coupled with optimized aerodynamic characteristics enabling deep-lung deposition and alveolar targeting [ ].Extending peptide-based delivery strategies, Ge et al. introduced fluorinated helical polypeptides capable of enhancing siRNA bioavailability by 240-fold through synergistic improvements in mucus penetration kinetics and alveolar macrophage uptake, ultimately achieving 96% TNF-α mRNA knockdown at a therapeutically relevant dose of 0.2 mg/kg in acute lung injury models [ ]. In parallel with these synthetic approaches, biologically inspired delivery systems are gaining substantial momentum as viable alternatives. Li et al. engineered epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-targeted exosomal nanocarriers for delivery of survivin-targeting siRNA in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) preclinical models. These biomimetic carriers exhibited preferential accumulation in lung tissue-resident NSCLC tumor cells and achieved efficient, tumor-specific silencing of the survivin anti-apoptotic gene [ ].Representing a paradigm shift in protein-based delivery, Segel et al. established the SEND (Selective Endogenous eNcapsidation for cellular Delivery) platform by rationally reprogramming PEG10 retrotransposon-derived capsid proteins to assemble into programmable virus-like particles (VLPs) capable of selective RNA cargo packaging and targeted cellular delivery. This modular platform enables facile cargo reprogramming through untranslated region (UTR) sequence modifications, offering versatility for diverse therapeutic applications [ ]. The clinical translation potential of lung-targeted RNA delivery systems has been increasingly validated through emerging therapeutic applications across diverse respiratory pathologies, including cystic fibrosis (CF), lung cancer, and viral infections such as COVID-19 [ ]. To provide a comprehensive overview of the most advanced and clinically relevant developments in this rapidly evolving field, Table systematically summarizes current and ongoing clinical trials of RNA delivery systems specifically targeting pulmonary tissues, as cataloged in the U.S. National Library of Medicine clinical trial registry (ClinicalTrials.gov). This review presents a comprehensive and critical synthesis of emerging nanoengineered RNA delivery strategies specifically tailored for pulmonary therapeutic applications (Fig.  ). Through systematic analysis of structure-function correlations, biodistribution mechanisms, and the physicochemical parameters of nanomaterials that govern pulmonary tropism, we elucidate the fundamental principles by which advanced material design translates into enhanced therapeutic indices and improved clinical outcomes. Furthermore, we critically evaluate current limitations and propose innovative engineering solutions to bridge the translational gap between promising preclinical findings and successful clinical implementation, thereby accelerating the development of next-generation RNA therapeutics for pulmonary diseases.
 

## Clinical challenges in pulmonary RNA delivery
Despite the considerable therapeutic promise of RNA-based medicines for respiratory diseases, the clinical translation of inhaled RNA therapeutics has been hampered by a series of interconnected challenges that span biological, technical, and disease-specific dimensions [ ]. The lung, while offering direct access to respiratory epithelium and reduced systemic exposure, has evolved sophisticated defense mechanisms that efficiently recognize and eliminate foreign nucleic acids [ ]. These innate barriers, combined with the technical demands of aerosol formulation and the heterogeneous pathophysiology of target diseases, have contributed to a clinical landscape marked by more setbacks than successes [ ]. To provide a foundation for understanding the rational design of delivery systems discussed in subsequent sections, this chapter first examines the biological barriers inherent to the respiratory tract, then synthesizes critical lessons from clinical trials that have failed to meet efficacy endpoints, and finally addresses the disease-specific considerations that must inform the development of targeted pulmonary RNA therapeutics.

## Biological barriers to pulmonary RNA delivery
Successful RNA delivery to the respiratory tract necessitates overcoming multiple sequential biological barriers, each presenting distinct challenges that collectively determine therapeutic efficacy [ ]. The mucociliary clearance system constitutes the first line of defense, wherein inhaled particles encounter a complex protective mucus layer enriched with mucins, antimicrobial peptides, and secretory immunoglobulins that collectively trap and eliminate foreign materials through coordinated ciliary beating [ ]. Upon penetrating the mucus barrier, RNA carriers must subsequently traverse the epithelial barrier, which exhibits structural heterogeneity throughout the respiratory tract—ranging from the pseudostratified ciliated epithelium of the conducting airways to the remarkably thin alveolar-capillary membrane optimized for gas exchange [ ]. The presence of tight junctions between epithelial cells restricts paracellular transport, thereby necessitating either transcellular uptake mechanisms or transient junction disruption for effective RNA passage [ ]. Additionally, the pulmonary surfactant system in alveolar regions presents both opportunities and challenges, as it can interact with delivery vehicles in ways that may enhance or impair their stability and cellular uptake [ ]. Immune surveillance represents another critical obstacle, with alveolar macrophages actively patrolling the alveolar space and rapidly phagocytosing foreign particles, while dendritic cells and other immune effectors may trigger inflammatory responses upon recognition of exogenous RNA [ ]. Finally, the ubiquitous presence of RNases in respiratory secretions poses a constant threat to RNA integrity, mandating the incorporation of protective formulations or chemical modifications [ ]. The heterogeneous nature of these barriers across different anatomical regions of the respiratory tract—from the upper airways to the deep alveolar spaces—necessitates carefully tailored delivery strategies capable of navigating this complex biological landscape while preserving RNA stability [ ].
The biological barriers encountered during respiratory RNA delivery exhibit significant route-dependent variations [ ]. Inhalation administration primarily confronts the mucociliary clearance mechanism, airway epithelial barriers, and alveolar macrophage-mediated phagocytosis [ ]. Conversely, intravenous delivery necessitates overcoming systemic dilution effects, reticuloendothelial system clearance, pulmonary capillary endothelial barriers, and the blood-gas interface [ ]. Intratracheal administration encounters airway surface liquid dilution, mucociliary transport, and localized immune responses, while intranasal delivery is constrained by nasal mucus entrapment, rapid ciliary clearance, and anatomical volume limitations [ ]. Irrespective of the administration route, all approaches must address ubiquitous challenges including RNase-mediated degradation and immune surveillance mechanisms [ ]. Thus, carrier design requires route-specific optimization of particle size, surface properties, and release kinetics to maximize barrier penetration and therapeutic efficacy [ ].

## Lessons from clinical trial failures
Several lung-targeted RNA therapeutics have demonstrated favorable safety profiles but failed to achieve clinical efficacy, providing valuable insights for future development:

## ALN-RSV01 (Alnylam Pharmaceuticals)
This inhaled siRNA targeting respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) nucleocapsid protein completed Phase II trials with excellent tolerability. However, the treatment failed to demonstrate significant antiviral efficacy compared to placebo. Post-hoc analysis revealed that insufficient siRNA delivery to infected epithelial cells and rapid mucociliary clearance limited therapeutic exposure [ ].

## MRT-5005 (Translate Bio)
This nebulized mRNA encoding functional cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulatorCFTR) protein for cystic fibrosis showed dose-dependent increases in percent predicted FEV1 (ppFEV1) in Phase I/II trials. However, subsequent larger trials did not meet primary efficacy endpoints. Key challenges included variable pulmonary deposition across patients, limited duration of CFTR protein expression (typically < 14 days), and the need for frequent repeat dosing [ ].
These outcomes highlight critical gaps including: (1) the need for improved mucus penetration to reach target cells, (2) extended residence time in the lung to maintain therapeutic concentrations, (3) more efficient cellular uptake mechanisms, and (4) sustained therapeutic protein expression through optimized mRNA design or delivery systems.

## Disease-specific considerations for pulmonary RNA delivery
The distinct pathological characteristics of pulmonary diseases exert profound influences on both the effectiveness of therapeutic interventions and the strategic selection of appropriate RNA targets, thereby necessitating disease-specific considerations in delivery system design [ ]. Variations in cellular composition, receptor expression profiles, and tissue architectural organization across different lung pathologies modulate therapeutic accessibility and cellular uptake efficiency [ ]. In diseases such as CF and COPD, aberrant mucus hypersecretion and impaired mucociliary clearance mechanisms create formidable physical barriers that substantially restrict drug penetration depth and uniform distribution throughout affected lung regions [ ]. Pulmonary fibrosis is pathologically characterized by excessive extracellular matrix accumulation and progressive alterations in epithelial integrity, which collectively impede the delivery of therapeutics to target cell populations embedded within fibrotic tissue [ ]. In lung cancer, pronounced heterogeneity in receptor expression patterns and the presence of dense stromal tissue components can severely impede uniform distribution within the complex tumor microenvironment [ ]. Moreover, the primary cellular and molecular targets for RNA-based interventions differ substantially among diseases: in CF, airway epithelial cells and ion channel genes such as theCFTR constitute the main therapeutic focus; in COPD, both epithelial and immune cells are targeted to modulate inflammatory cascades and oxidative stress pathways; in pulmonary fibrosis, fibroblasts and myofibroblasts are addressed to inhibit excessive collagen deposition and profibrotic signaling; and in lung cancer, malignant cells or cancer stem cells are targeted to regulate oncogenes, tumor suppressors, or immune checkpoint molecules [ ].

## Synthetic nano vehicles for pulmonary RNA therapeutic delivery systems
Overcoming the biological barriers described above requires delivery systems that can be rationally engineered to fulfill specific mechanistic requirements at each stage of the delivery cascade [ ]. Synthetic vehicles are chemically engineered delivery platforms constructed from rationally designed molecular components, whose physicochemical properties—including size, surface charge, and stimuli-responsiveness—can be precisely controlled through chemical synthesis [ ]. LNPs utilize ionizable lipids that undergo protonation in acidic endosomal environments (pH ~ 5–6) to enhance electrostatic interactions with anionic cellular membranes and nucleic acid cargo, while fusogenic phospholipids facilitate the formation of non-lamellar hexagonal phases within endosomes, thereby destabilizing endosomal membranes and promoting RNA release into the cytoplasm [ ]. Polymer-based nanocarriers employ functionalized polymeric materials, including cationic polymers or pH-responsive amphiphilic copolymers, to facilitate efficient cellular internalization, provide protective shielding for nucleic acid cargo against enzymatic degradation, and trigger endosomal membrane disruption in acidic intracellular compartments [ ]. Peptide-based vectors incorporate cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) or receptor-targeting peptides to enable receptor-mediated endocytosis, while fusogenic or endosomolytic motifs derived from viral fusion proteins facilitate intracellular barrier penetration [ ]. Through systematic optimization of chemical composition, particle size distribution, surface charge density (ζ-potential), and stimuli-responsive molecular features, these diverse platforms collectively enhance intracellular RNA delivery efficiency and therapeutic efficacy [ ].

## Lipid nanoparticles
LNPs have emerged as the most clinically advanced delivery platform for pulmonary RNA therapeutics, owing to their capacity to protect RNA from enzymatic degradation, enhance pharmacokinetic stability, and facilitate efficient lung delivery [ ]. Their structural composition—ionizable lipids, cholesterol, helper phospholipids, and PEG-lipids—enables effective RNA encapsulation while optimizing biodistribution [ ]. LNPs systematically overcome biological barriers including cellular membrane penetration, endosomal escape limitations, and cytosolic RNase degradation [ ].

## Key formulation challenges
Balancing transfection efficiency with biocompatibility remains critical. Cationic lipids effectively facilitate RNA uptake but can induce dose-dependent inflammatory responses [ ]. PEGylation enhances colloidal stability and reduces immune recognition, yet excessive PEGylation impairs cellular uptake through the “PEG dilemma” [ ].
Anti-PEG immune responses represent a significant consideration for repeated pulmonary LNP administration. Studies have demonstrated that anti-PEG IgM and IgG levels increase following LNP exposure, potentially reducing transfection efficiency upon repeated dosing [ ].Cholesterol incorporation improves membrane fluidity and reduces cytotoxicity—particularly relevant for inhaled formulations requiring repeated dosing [ ].

## Next-Generation ionizable lipids
Recent technological advances in lipid chemistry and rational structural modifications have yielded next-generation LNP formulations with enhanced RNA delivery properties and improved therapeutic windows [ ]. One particularly noteworthy innovation involves the development and application of siloxane-based ionizable lipids (SiLNPs). Xue et al. introduced novel SiLNPs that, following tail vein injection in murine models, improved mRNA delivery specifically to the liver, lung, and spleen tissues, achieving markedly higher transfection efficiency and more favorable biodistribution profiles (Fig. A). The Si₅-N14 LNP formulation displayed optimal physicochemical properties for in vivo delivery (Fig. B). Bioluminescence imaging confirmed lung-specific luciferase expression with minimal off-target accumulation in other organs (Fig. C). RT-qPCR analysis further demonstrated successful CRISPR-mediated gene knockout in endothelial cells (Fig. D) [ ].
 

## Active targeting approaches
LNPs have also been extensively investigated for receptor-targeted RNA delivery, specifically aimed at enhancing delivery specificity and selectivity to distinct pulmonary cell populations through active targeting mechanisms [ ]. For instance, luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone (LHRH)peptide-conjugated LNPs have been successfully employed to deliver therapeutic siRNA and chemotherapeutic agents specifically to LHRH receptor-expressing lung cancer cells via inhalational administration. Compared to non-targeted LNP formulations delivered intravenously, this direct pulmonary administration approach achieved substantially improved therapeutic efficacy with reduced systemic toxicity and off-target effects [ ]. Additionally, Tagalakis et al. designed siRNA nanocomplexes targeting airway epithelial sodium channels (ENaC) in CF disease models, resulting in enhanced ion transepithelial transport and improved epithelial function [ ]. Similarly, Otsuka et al. explored the therapeutic potential of vitamin A-conjugated liposomes (VA-lip-siRNA HSP47) for targeted inhibition of collagen-producing myofibroblasts in pulmonary fibrosis. In their comprehensive preclinical study, male Sprague-Dawley rats were initially treated with intratracheal bleomycin (BLM) instillation to induce progressive pulmonary fibrosis. Subsequently, the animals received intravenous injections of VA-lip-siRNA HSP47 three times weekly according to two distinct therapeutic regimens: a preventive administration schedule (initiated on day 1 and continued through day 21 post-BLM challenge) and a therapeutic intervention schedule (initiated on day 15 when fibrosis was established and continued through day 35 post-BLM treatment). This vitamin A receptor-targeted delivery strategy effectively attenuated fibrosis-associated pathological manifestations in the experimental pulmonary fibrosis models. Importantly, the results convincingly demonstrated that VA-lip-siRNA HSP47 mitigated established pulmonary fibrosis not only under the preventive administration regimen but alsocriticallyin the therapeutic intervention setting, thereby supporting this targeted drug delivery system as a promising and translatable therapeutic approach for managing refractory and progressive pulmonary fibrosis [ ].

## Ligand-free strategies
Beyond active targeting approaches that utilize specific ligand conjugation, ligand-free LNP-based strategies have emerged as an increasingly attractive alternative for achieving effective nucleic acid delivery to extrahepatic tissues, including the lungs [ ]. These approaches leverage the intrinsic physicochemical properties of LNP formulations—such as particle size distribution, surface charge characteristics, and lipid compositional ratios—to modulate biodistribution profiles and enhance tissue-specific accumulation without requiring complex surface modifications [ ]. Sun et al. demonstrated the potential of composition-optimized, ligand-free LNP formulations for achieving highly efficient in vivo stem cell genome editing in pulmonary tissues. Rather than employing ligand-based active targeting, the researchers systematically developed lung-targeting LNP formulations through comprehensive optimization of lipid composition and molar ratios, thereby enabling unprecedented genome editing efficiency in lung stem cells following simple intravenous administration. The optimized ligand-free LNP formulation achieved over 70% CRISPR-mediated genome editing efficiency in lung stem cells within activatable tdTomato reporter mice, with remarkably sustained transgene expression persisting in more than 80% of lung epithelial cells for an extraordinary duration of 660 days, thereby demonstrating both editing efficiency and long-term therapeutic durability. When applied therapeutically to CF treatment, the NG-ABE8e mRNA–sgR553X LNP system successfully corrected over 95% of disease-causing CFTR DNA sequences and functionally restored CFTR chloride channel activity in patient-derived bronchial epithelial cells to therapeutic levels comparable with those achieved by Trikafta (elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor), the current gold-standard CFTR modulator therapy. Most notably, this ligand-free targeting approach successfully corrected pathogenic R553X nonsense mutations in approximately 50% of lung stem cells in CF mouse models, providing compelling proof-of-concept for potentially curative gene therapy approaches [ ]. This work highlights how rational lipid composition optimization, without requiring targeting ligands, can achieve tissue-selective nucleic acid delivery and enable transformative stem cell genome editing for potential disease cures.

## High-throughput combinatorial lipid screening
High-throughput combinatorial lipid screening coupled with structure-guided rational design have collectively led to the development of novel biodegradable LNP formulations specifically optimized for inhalable RNA therapeutics and CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing applications [ ]. Xue et al. synthesized an extensive combinatorial library of 180 structurally diverse cationic liposomes, subsequently performing systematic high-throughput screening to identify top-performing LNP formulations specifically optimized for CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing in orthotopic lung cancer models (Fig. E, F) [ ]. Li et al. employed sophisticated combinatorial synthesis and systematic screening strategies to identify biodegradable ionizable lipid candidates that demonstrated high efficiency in both genome editing and therapeutic RNA delivery to lung epithelial cells. Notably, their optimized biodegradable LNP formulations, when administered to mice via direct intratracheal instillation at a dose of 0.25 mg/kg mRNA, exhibited excellent tolerance and compatibility with repeated dosing regimens, thereby suggesting substantial potential for clinical application in gene therapy for congenital lung diseases requiring long-term treatment [ ]. The novel ionizable lipid component H1L1A1B3 exhibited a four-fold enhancement in circRNA transfection efficiency in Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) experimental models compared to ALC-0315—the clinically validated ionizable lipid component utilized in the Pfizer/BioNTech BNT162b2 mRNA COVID-19 vaccine formulation. This advanced LNP platform also demonstrated robust immune activation capacity with favorable safety profiles. A single intratumoral administration of H1L1A1B3 LNPs loaded with circRNA encoding the immunostimulatory cytokine interleukin-12 (IL-12) induced a potent and durable antitumor immune response in syngeneic Lewis lung carcinoma models, resulting in significant tumor growth inhibition and regression. Comprehensive immunological characterization of treated tumor tissues revealed substantially increased infiltration of CD45⁺ leukocytes and cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells [ ].

## Disease-specific applications
Beyond applications in general RNA delivery, LNP technology has been tailored and optimized to target specific pulmonary diseases with distinct pathophysiological features, including lung cancer and IPF [ ]. In a complementary therapeutic approach, Robinson et al. developed an advanced LNP-based delivery system for chemically modified and stabilized CFTR mRNA, successfully restoring functional chloride ion transport in primary bronchial epithelial cells (CVCL_HL93 cell line) isolated from CF patients for up to 14 days in vitro culture—thereby outperforming earlier-generation liposomal CFTR DNA gene therapy approaches and remarkably matching the therapeutic efficacy of ivacaftor, a clinically approved CFTR potentiator [ ]. Lokugamage et al. optimized LNPs for nebulized RNA delivery to lung tissue, fine-tuning the LNPs’ composition, molar ratios, and structure by incorporating neutral or cationic helper lipids and PEG. In this optimized system, each mouse was administered a dose of 13.5 µg mRNA for pulmonary delivery. This optimization enabled the LNPs to achieve superior mRNA transfection efficiency in the lung tissue and protected mice from a lethal H1N1 influenza challenge [ ]. Bai et al. developed a nanoparticle system for inhalable siRNA delivery targeting IL11 to treat IPF. This system consists of biodegradable PLGA-PEG copolymer-based LNPs incorporated with the cationic lipid G0-C14, and was administered at a paclitaxel (TAX) dose of 2.5 mg/kg. The application of a multifunctional nanostructured lipid carrier-based delivery system not only markedly boosted the therapeutic efficacy against NSCLC but also holds promise for reducing the adverse side effects associated with current treatments [ ].

## PLGA-modified
To enhance inhalable siRNA-based lung tumor therapy, Wang et al. developed a strategy to optimize LNPs via poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA): they fabricated shell-core polymer-lipid hybrid nanoparticles (HNPs) using microfluidics—specifically by coating PLGA onto siRNA-loaded cationic liposomes. Characterization confirmed the core-shell structure and tunable rigidity of HNPs. Functionally, the designed HNPs exhibited muco-inert properties, including improved stability in mucus and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), good biocompatibility, enhanced mucus penetration, and efficient cellular uptake in vitro. Notably, HNP1 achieved 84.83% transfection efficiency in A549 cells—comparable to lipoplexes and Lipofectamine 2000—and its tumor permeability in A549-3T3 tumor spheroids was 1.88-fold higher than that of lipoplexes. After cellular internalization, HNPs were observed to undergo both endosomal escape and lysosomal exocytosis; in A549-3T3 spheroids, HNP1’s transfection efficiency (39.33%) was 2.26-fold that of lipoplexes. Additionally, HNPs maintained excellent stability during nebulization with a soft mist inhaler. They highlight that PLGA-modified HNP system—especially HNP1—holds great potential for inhalable siRNA delivery in lung cancer therapy [ ].A dry powder formulation of cationic lipid-modified PLGA nanoparticles was developed by Jensen et al. for pulmonary siRNA delivery, demonstrating dose-dependent gene silencing efficiency, achieving 73% silencing with 25% (w/w) DOTAP incorporation in NSCLC cell line H1299 stably expressing EGFP (EGFP-H1299) in vitro. This formulation, when spray-dried into microparticles, maintained aerodynamic properties suitable for lung deposition, preserving gene silencing efficiency and physicochemical stability [ ].

## Inhaled dry-powder LNP formulations
While liquid nebulized formulations have dominated pulmonary LNP delivery development, inhaled dry-powder formulations offer distinct advantages including improved stability, extended shelf-life without cold-chain requirements, and more convenient patient administration [ ]. Recent advances have demonstrated the feasibility of converting LNP-mRNA formulations into inhalable dry powders while preserving RNA integrity and biological activity.
Spray-drying represents the most widely investigated technique for dry powder LNP production. Zimmermann et al. developed spray-dried siRNA-LNP formulations using trehalose and mannitol as stabilizing excipients, achieving aerodynamic diameters suitable for deep lung deposition (MMAD: 2–5 μm) while maintaining > 80% siRNA encapsulation efficiency post-processing [ ]. Critical process parameters including inlet temperature, feed rate, and atomization pressure influence particle morphology and aerosol performance. Gordon et al. recently demonstrated that optimized spray-drying conditions can preserve mRNA-LNP transfection efficiency at levels comparable to freshly prepared liquid formulations. Their dry powder formulations achieved fine particle fractions (FPF) > 40% and maintained luciferase mRNA expression in murine lungs following intratracheal administration [ ]. Importantly, the dry powder formulations exhibited improved stability at room temperature compared to liquid counterparts, addressing a major limitation for clinical deployment.
The clinical translation of dry-powder LNP technology is exemplified by ongoing trials such as NCT06928922, which evaluates an inhaled mRNA-LNP dry powder vaccine for respiratory infections. These developments represent a significant advancement toward patient-friendly, stable pulmonary RNA therapeutics suitable for widespread clinical use.

## Modularity and platform nature
Notably, the inherent modularity and platform nature of LNP technology enables remarkably rapid adaptation for diverse therapeutic applications through systematic and independent variation of multiple design parameters, including lipid component selection (ionizable lipids, helper phospholipids, cholesterol, and PEG-lipids), RNA cargo specifications (mRNA, siRNA, or guide RNA), and critical formulation parameters such as lipid-to-RNA molar ratios, mixing flow rates, and buffer compositions [ ]. This modularity extends far beyond simple component substitution to encompass sophisticated rational design principles wherein each individual lipid component can be independently and systematically optimized: ionizable lipids can be selected from extensive combinatorial libraries encompassing hundreds of structural variants exhibiting different apparent pKa values (ranging from 6.0 to 7.5) and diverse structural features (tail length, branching, and head group chemistry); helper phospholipids can be chosen to precisely modulate membrane fluidity, phase transition temperatures, and fusogenic properties; cholesterol content can be systematically adjusted to fine-tune membrane mechanical stability, permeability characteristics, and nanoparticle rigidity; and PEG-lipid concentrations can be carefully optimized to achieve the optimal balance between prolonged circulation time and efficient cellular uptake, thereby circumventing the “PEG dilemma” [ ]. The scalability of LNP platforms as versatile genetic medicine delivery vehicles is particularly evident and well-established in large-scale manufacturing processes, wherein standardized microfluidic mixing technologies, rigorous quality control protocols, and comprehensive analytical characterization methodologies can be readily adapted and transferred across different RNA therapeutic products while consistently maintaining narrow particle size distributions (polydispersity index < 0.2), high RNA encapsulation efficiencies (typically > 90%), and reproducible biological performance characteristics across manufacturing batches [ ]. This manufacturing scalability extends favorably to regulatory approval pathways, wherein the substantial platform knowledge, safety databases, and manufacturing experience gained from one successfully approved LNP-RNA therapeutic product can substantively inform, de-risk, and accelerate the preclinical and clinical development of subsequent LNP formulations targeting different diseases or tissue types. This platform approach reduces both development timelines (potentially by 2–3 years) and cumulative costs compared to developing entirely novel delivery systems de novo for each individual therapeutic application [ ]. The platform nature and technological versatility of LNP technology is further exemplified and validated by its demonstrated compatibility with diverse RNA therapeutic modalities, including conventional mRNA for protein replacement therapy, siRNA for gene silencing, ASOs for splice modulation, and emerging RNA genome editing tools such as CRISPR guide RNAs and base editor guide RNAs, all of which can be efficiently formulated and delivered using highly similar core LNP architectures with only minor composition modifications [ ]. This versatility, combined with the enabling capability to rapidly screen thousands of formulation variants using automated high-throughput robotic platforms and machine learning-guided optimization algorithms, positions LNPs as truly modular, scalable, and adaptable delivery vehicles that can be systematically and rationally optimized for specific therapeutic objectives while simultaneously leveraging accumulated platform knowledge, manufacturing infrastructure, and regulatory precedents. Future technological developments and innovations may productively focus on optimizing surface modifications and internal lipid compositions to further enhance biocompatibility profiles and cell-type targeting specificity for diverse pulmonary cell populations [ ]. Continued innovations in lipid chemistry, including advanced PEGylation strategies (such as cleavable PEG linkers or branched PEG architectures) and novel lipid-ligand conjugates, could help achieve optimal balance between surface charge characteristics and biocompatibility, thereby minimizing immunogenicity and inflammatory responses while maintaining high transfection efficiency [ ]. Additionally, receptor-targeted strategies incorporating cell-specific ligands (such as antibody fragments, peptides, or aptamers) hold substantial promise for further improving lung-specific delivery precision and cellular uptake selectivity [ ]. High-throughput combinatorial screening techniques coupled with advanced imaging technologies (including intravital microscopy and single-cell analysis) will continue to play critical and enabling roles in systematically refining LNP formulations, thereby enabling increasingly precise and personalized therapeutic applications [ ]. The strategic integration of personalized medicine approaches, including patient-specific formulation optimization based on genetic backgrounds and disease biomarkers, may further enhance LNP therapeutic efficacy for individual patients, ultimately improving treatment outcomes across a wide spectrum of pulmonary conditions [ ]. In conclusion, while progress has been achieved in LNP-based RNA delivery systems over the past decade, continued interdisciplinary research and sustained technological innovation will remain essential for systematically addressing current limitations and fully unlocking their transformative potential as an advanced and versatile therapeutic platform for treating diverse lung diseases. 

## Polymers
Polymers have emerged as versatile carriers for RNA therapeutics in pulmonary diseases [ ]. Their ability to modulate pharmacokinetic profiles and achieve targeted lung delivery has enhanced the therapeutic efficacy and safety of RNA therapies, positioning them as viable alternatives to viral vectors [ ].

## Chemical structure and design strategies
Polyethyleneimine (PEI) contains repeating ethylamine units with primary, secondary, and tertiary amines at approximate ratios of 1:2:1 in branched forms. High molecular weight PEI (25 kDa) exhibits dose-dependent cytotoxicity (IC₅₀ ~10 µg/mL in epithelial cells) due to excessive membrane destabilization from high cationic charge density [ ]. To address this limitation, Feng and colleagues developed fluorinated low molecular weight PEI (1.8 kDa) by introducing perfluorinated segments that reduce overall charge density while enhancing hydrophobic membrane interactions [ ].
Alternative cationic polymer architectures have been developed to improve RNA binding specificity. Guanidinium-functionalized polynorbornene (PONI-Guan) employs guanidinium headgroups that form bidentate hydrogen bonds with RNA phosphate groups in addition to electrostatic interactions, providing stronger binding affinity than primary amines alone [ , ]. Poloxamine-peptide hybrids combine tetra-functional poloxamine (Tetronic®) containing four poly(ethylene oxide)-poly(propylene oxide) arms with self-assembling peptide sequences, creating amphiphilic structures that spontaneously form nanoparticles upon mixing with nucleic acids [ , ].
Dendrimers represent a distinct class of polymeric carriers characterized by well-defined branched architecture and precise molecular weight control. Polyamidoamine (PAMAM) dendrimers are synthesized through iterative Michael addition and amidation reactions, generating generation-dependent structures with exponentially increasing surface groups (G4: 14,215 Da with 64 surface amines; G5: 28,826 Da with 128 surface amines) [ – ]. To mitigate the inherent cytotoxicity of cationic dendrimers, Shi et al. modified PAMAM with 2,3-dimethylmaleic anhydride (DMMA), creating acid-labile amide bonds that undergo hydrolysis at pH < 6.8, converting surface charge from negative to positive specifically in the acidic tumor microenvironment. An alternative biocompatibility strategy was employed by Adams et al., who synthesized poly(spermine acrylamide) (P(SpAA)) dendrimers incorporating natural spermine—a tetraamine present in all eukaryotic cells—into the polymer backbone [ ].

## Mechanisms of RNA complexation and intracellular delivery
Cationic polymers form polyplexes with RNA through cooperative electrostatic interactions between polymer amines and RNA phosphate groups, with the nitrogen-to-phosphate (N/P) ratio serving as a critical parameter governing complexation efficiency. PEI polyplexes typically require N/P ratios of 5–10 for complete RNA complexation and optimal transfection [ ]. PAMAM G5 dendrimers, with 128 surface amines providing high local charge density, achieve cooperative RNA binding with dissociation constants in the nanomolar range (Kd ~ 10⁻⁹ M), while their 5.4 nm diameter enables efficient cellular internalization via clathrin-mediated endocytosis [ , ].
Endosomal escape represents a critical bottleneck in polymer-mediated RNA delivery. The “proton sponge” mechanism, characteristic of PEI and amine-rich dendrimers, exploits the buffering capacity of polyamines across the endosomal acidification range (pH 7.4→5.0). Continued proton influx drives compensatory Cl⁻ and H₂O accumulation, increasing osmotic pressure from approximately 50 mOsm to greater than 300 mOsm, ultimately rupturing the endosomal membrane and releasing cargo into the cytosol [ ].
Chemical modifications can enhance cellular uptake through alternative mechanisms. Fluorinated PEI (1.8 kDa) achieved six-fold higher endocytosis efficiency than unmodified PEI (25 kDa) in H1299 non-small cell lung cancer cells, attributed to fluorophilic interactions with membrane lipids that facilitate direct membrane translocation independent of endocytic pathways [ ]. Charge-conversion strategies offer another approach to optimize intracellular trafficking. DMMA-modified PAMAM dendrimers maintain negative surface charge (ζ-potential: −15 mV) at physiological pH 7.4, minimizing nonspecific interactions during systemic circulation. Upon reaching the acidic tumor microenvironment (pH 6.5–6.8), DMMA hydrolysis exposes underlying primary amines, converting ζ-potential to + 20 mV within 2 h and enabling efficient tumor cell uptake [ ].
Polymer structure also influences biodistribution and tissue targeting. PONI-Guan polyplexes demonstrated preferential accumulation in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-inflamed lung tissue following intravenous injection, achieving tissue concentrations 3–4 fold higher than in healthy lung parenchyma, attributed to enhanced vascular permeability at inflammatory sites [ ].

## Therapeutic efficacy in pulmonary disease models
Polymer-based RNA delivery systems have demonstrated therapeutic efficacy across multiple pulmonary disease models. In non-small cell lung cancer, fluorinated low molecular weight PEI facilitated siRNA delivery to H1299 xenografts following subcutaneous administration, achieving significant tumor growth inhibition compared to unmodified high molecular weight PEI controls (Fig. A) [ ]. Shi et al. demonstrated that DMMA-modified PAMAM dendrimers achieved approximately 70% tumor penetration and 65% VEGF gene silencing in A549 xenograft models following intravenous administration, with minimal observable cytotoxicity [ ]. Lu and colleagues further advanced dendrimer-based delivery by developing a supramolecular system integrating PEG-grafted adamantane with ligand-functionalized dendrimers, which effectively reduced VEGF expression, inhibited tumor angiogenesis, and suppressed tumor progression in xenograft models following tail vein injection [ ]. For metastatic lung disease, Aqil et al. demonstrated that milk-derived exosome-coated polymer nanoparticles enabled effective gene knockdown in melanoma lung metastases following systemic administration [ ].
 In inflammatory lung disease, PONI-Guan polyplexes targeting TNF-α achieved greater than 70% gene knockdown in vitro and more than 80% TNF-α silencing in inflamed lung tissue of LPS-challenged mice at a siRNA dose of 0.28 mg/kg administered intravenously (Fig. B) [ ].
For cystic fibrosis gene therapy, Guan et al. developed poloxamine-peptide hybrid nanoparticles that efficiently encapsulated CFTR mRNA and plasmid DNA. These formulations enhanced gene expression in human bronchial epithelial cells (BEAS-2B and 16HBE) and restored CFTR function in CF mouse models following intratracheal administration, with minimal associated toxicity [ ].
The translational potential of dendrimer systems was further demonstrated in human tissue models. Adams et al. showed that P(SpAA) dendrimers outperformed conventional lipid-based transfection agents (Lipofectamine) in ex vivo human lung explants, achieving efficient gene silencing with minimal toxicity, suggesting improved biocompatibility of spermine-based architectures in clinically relevant tissue [ ].

## Current limitations and engineering solutions
Despite promising preclinical results, several challenges constrain clinical translation of polymer-based RNA carriers. Cytotoxicity remains a primary concern, particularly for high molecular weight cationic polymers; current mitigation strategies include fluorination of low molecular weight PEI and incorporation of natural polyamines such as spermine into dendrimer backbones [ , ].
Nonspecific cellular uptake and rapid systemic clearance of cationic particles have been addressed through charge-conversion strategies (DMMA-PAMAM) and PEGylation, which reduce off-target accumulation during circulation while enabling charge exposure at disease sites [ ]. Endosomal entrapment, which affects approximately 70% of internalized polyplexes, continues to limit cytosolic delivery efficiency; enhanced proton sponge capacity through increased amine density and conjugation of membrane-active peptides represent ongoing optimization approaches [ ].
Manufacturing considerations present additional challenges for clinical development. Branched PEI exhibits substantial batch variability with polydispersity indices (PDI) ranging from 2.5 to 4.0, complicating quality control and regulatory approval. Dendrimers offer superior batch consistency (PDI < 1.1) but require multi-step synthesis procedures (10 + steps for high-generation structures), increasing production costs and complexity [ ]. Future development should focus on simplified synthesis procedures, lower-generation dendrimers (G3–G4) with optimized surface modifications, and incorporation of stimuli-responsive elements to expand applications in precision pulmonary medicine [ ].

## Peptide-based delivery vectors
Peptide-based vectors (typically < 50 amino acids) represent a mechanistically distinct class from protein carriers (> 50 amino acids with complex tertiary structures) [ ]. Peptides primarily function through cell-penetrating or membrane-destabilizing activities, while larger protein carriers leverage capsid-forming abilities and receptor-specific interactions [ ]. This section focuses on peptide-based approaches for pulmonary RNA delivery.

## Peptide chemistry and design principles
Bioactive peptides are emerging as promising RNA delivery vectors due to their biocompatibility, efficient cellular penetration, and ability to form stable RNA complexes [ ]. Cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs) typically consist of 4–40 amino acid residues and can be categorized into cationic, amphipathic, and hydrophobic types based on their physicochemical characteristics [ ]. Cationic CPPs, exemplified by TAT-derived sequences (RKKRRQRRR) and polyarginine chains (R8, R9), contain abundant positively charged residues that facilitate electrostatic condensation with negatively charged RNA phosphate backbones to form nanoscale complexes typically ranging from 50 to 200 nm [ ]. The guanidinium groups of arginine residues are particularly critical for RNA binding, as they can establish bidentate hydrogen bonds and salt bridges with nucleotide phosphates while maintaining stable positive charges across physiological pH conditions due to their exceptionally high pKa value of approximately 13.8 [ ]. Amphipathic CPPs such as RALA, CADY, and KALA possess distinct hydrophobic and hydrophilic domains that enable spontaneous self-assembly into nanoparticles upon RNA complexation, with many adopting α-helical conformations characterized by repeated hpphppp patterns (h = hydrophobic, p = polar) that position charged residues along one helical face [ ]. The incorporation of histidine residues into peptide sequences has proven particularly valuable, as the imidazole side chain (pKa ~ 6.0) provides pH-responsive buffering capacity that enhances endosomal escape efficiency [ ].Modern rational design approaches increasingly leverage computational tools including molecular dynamics simulations and machine learning algorithms to predict peptide-membrane interactions, optimize sequence composition, and identify novel CPP candidates with enhanced delivery properties while minimizing cytotoxicity [ ].

## Cellular uptake and endosomal escape mechanisms
CPP-mediated cellular internalization proceeds through two principal routes: energy-independent direct membrane translocation involving transient pore formation or inverted micelle mechanisms, and energy-dependent endocytic pathways including macropinocytosis, clathrin-mediated endocytosis, and caveolae-dependent uptake [ ]. The relative contribution of each pathway varies considerably depending on peptide concentration, sequence composition, cargo properties, and target cell type [ ]. Following endocytic uptake, CPP-RNA complexes become sequestered within acidifying endosomal compartments, necessitating efficient escape mechanisms to access cytoplasmic RNAi machinery [ ]. Histidine-rich peptides such as LAH4 and H3K4b exploit the “proton sponge” effect, wherein progressive protonation of imidazole groups during endosomal acidification triggers osmotic swelling and membrane rupture through chloride ion influx and water accumulation [ ]. Alternatively, amphipathic peptides undergo pH-triggered conformational transitions from disordered states to membrane-active α-helical structures that destabilize endosomal membranes at acidic pH while remaining inactive at neutral conditions [ ]. For pulmonary applications, peptide-based delivery systems must additionally overcome airway-specific barriers including the mucus layer containing anionic mucin glycoproteins, surfactant protein interactions in alveolar spaces, and rapid clearance by alveolar macrophages [ ]. Recent developments include PEGylated surfactant-mimetic KL4 peptides that achieve efficient mRNA expression in deep lung regions via dry powder inhalation, and cyclic disulfide-constrained amphipathic peptides that demonstrate effective gene silencing in pulmonary tissues with enhanced cytosolic release upon intracellular reduction [ ]. Key optimization strategies include incorporation of D-amino acids or non-natural residues for proteolytic stability, PEGylation for improved mucus penetration, and conjugation with targeting ligands for cell-type specificity [ ].

## Pulmonary therapeutic applications
Structural variability in amino acid sequences presents challenges in optimizing stability, bioavailability, and safety [ – ]. To address these challenges, researchers have employed high-throughput peptide screening and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to systematically identify peptides with high siRNA delivery efficiency and favorable tissue safety profiles. Men et al. combined computational modeling with scRNA-seq analysis to optimize peptide-based carriers for lung-targeted siRNA delivery (Fig. A). Through sequential screening and transcriptomic profiling at the cell subtype level, a high-performance 12-amino acid peptide, DP7-C, was identified. They delivered the DP7-C/Cy5-siRNA complex, where Cy5 (a near-infrared fluorescent dye) was used to label siRNA. When the DP7-C/Cy5-siRNA complex was administered intravenously, intense fluorescent signals were detected in lung tissues shortly after injection (Fig. B). The fluorescence signal peaked within the first hour, with lung-localized fluorescence accounting for over 72% of the total signal (Fig. C, D). Additionally, flow cytometric data provided further evidence of the complex’s lung-targeting capability, as well as its ability to support the prolonged retention of siRNA within lung tissue—specifically reflected by the detection of a persistent population of Cy5-positive (Cy5⁺) cells in lung tissue (Fig. E). Following hydrophobic modification, this peptide demonstrated enhanced lung tissue specificity, accompanied by improved stability and transfection efficiency in pulmonary cells [ ].
 siRNA shows significant potential for treating various lung diseases, but the lack of safe, efficient pulmonary siRNA delivery systems has impeded its clinical translation. Rico et al. showed that the synthetic peptide PEG12-KL4 offers notable advantages for siRNA delivery in NSCLC: it efficiently mediates EGFR and PD-L1 knockdown in NSCLC cell lines HCC827 and NCI-H1975, outperforming the commercial reagent Lipofectamine 2000. Confocal fluorescence imaging suggested PEG12-KL4 may support siRNA silencing by aiding endosomal escape or bypass. Functionally, co-knocking down EGFR and PD-L1 in NCI-H1975 cells enhanced CD8⁺ T cell-mediated cancer killing more than single PD-L1 knockdown, highlighting synergistic value of dual-target silencing for NSCLC immunotherapy. Biodistribution assays further revealed critical effects of administration route: intravenous injection of PEG12-KL4/siRNA complexes led to poor lung targeting (siRNA accumulated in the liver), while intratracheal delivery enabled lung-specific siRNA enrichment and delayed renal excretion—providing key insights for delivery optimization. They confirmed the feasibility of co-delivering EGFR/PD-L1-targeting siRNAs via PEG12-KL4 as a promising NSCLC therapy, emphasizing that pulmonary siRNA delivery is superior to intravenous administration for such lung disease applications [ ]. Xu et al. screened known cell-penetrating peptides to identify one capable of forming a highly efficient anti-inflammatory complex with TNF-α siRNA, designated as SAR6EW/TNF-α siRNA. This complex effectively delivers TNF-α siRNA into the cytoplasm, enabling potent gene silencing both in vitro and in vivo. To optimize pulmonary delivery, the team incorporated lactose and triarginine as coexcipients and refined the spray-drying process, resulting in a micrometer-scale powder with spherical, porous structures—features that enhance aerosol dispersion and lung-targeting efficiency. Importantly, the dry powder formulation and manufacturing process-maintained siRNA stability and structural integrity. In intratracheal administration to acute lung injury (ALI) model mice, the SAR6EW/TNF-α siRNA complex powder exhibited lung-specific gene silencing activity and markedly alleviated ALI-induced inflammatory symptoms. Xu et al. thus highlight this formulation as a promising candidate strategy for the clinical treatment of respiratory diseases [ ].
Despite the potential of peptide-based delivery, a key challenge remains in overcoming pulmonary mucus and cellular barriers, which can hinder RNA diffusion and uptake. To address this issue, Ge et al. developed bifunctional helical polypeptides incorporating guanidine and fluorine modifications to enhance mucus penetration, cellular uptake, and gene silencing efficiency. The guanidine moieties facilitated strong electrostatic interactions with RNA molecules, while the α-helical structure promoted efficient uptake into macrophages. The addition of fluorination modifications increased mucus penetration by approximately 240-fold, improving pulmonary siRNA transport. When the optimized P7F7 polypeptide was co-administered with TNF-α siRNA via intratracheal delivery, a 96% gene knockdown efficiency was achieved at 0.2 mg/kg, leading to potent anti-inflammatory effects in acute lung injury models [ ].
Peptide-based RNA carriers offer several advantages over conventional lipid- and polymer-based systems, including intrinsic biocompatibility, tunable degradation rates, and improved receptor-mediated endocytosis [ ]. Their ability to self-assemble into nanocomplexes with RNA enhances stability, while their sequence modularity allows for precise control over targeting properties, minimizing off-target effects. These unique attributes position peptide-based vectors as promising candidates for lung-specific RNA delivery, particularly for conditions such as CF, pulmonary fibrosis, and lung cancer. However, key challenges remain, including the need for greater peptide stability, improved pharmacokinetics, and more efficient intracellular RNA release.
Future advancements in peptide-based RNA therapy will likely focus on designing multifunctional peptides that integrate membrane-penetrating properties with receptor-targeting moieties, ensuring higher specificity for lung epithelial and immune cells [ ]. Additionally, the incorporation of stimuli-responsive elements, such as pH-sensitive or oxidative stress-activated motifs, may allow for controlled RNA release in inflamed lung environments, further enhancing therapeutic outcomes [ ]. Emerging AI-driven peptide optimization strategies are also expected to play a crucial role in refining sequence motifs for enhanced RNA binding, cellular uptake, and intracellular trafficking [ , ].
While significant progress has been made, continued research and technological innovation will be required to fully realize the clinical potential of peptide-based RNA therapeutics [ ]. By leveraging advances in peptide engineering, bioinformatics-driven design, and controlled-release formulations, peptide vectors have the potential to revolutionize targeted RNA therapies for lung diseases—providing a safer and more efficient alternative to conventional delivery platforms such as viral vectors (e.g., adenoviral, lentiviral) and early-generation non-viral systems (e.g., bare liposomes, cationic polymers) [ ].

## Non-synthetic nano vehicles for pulmonary RNA therapeutic delivery systems
In contrast to the engineered mechanisms of synthetic systems, non-synthetic nanovehicles harness evolutionarily optimized biological machinery to achieve nucleic acid delivery [ ]. Viral vectors exploit receptor-mediated cell entry mechanisms refined over millions of years of host-pathogen co-evolution, utilizing capsid proteins for cellular recognition and conformational changes in acidic endosomes to facilitate membrane penetration and cargo release [ ]. Protein-based carriers similarly leverage receptor-mediated endocytosis through ligand-receptor interactions refined over evolutionary timescales, offering intrinsic targeting capabilities and predictable biodegradation pathways [ ]. Extracellular vesicles, meanwhile, deliver cargo through membrane fusion and endogenous trafficking pathways that cells have established for intercellular communication, thereby engaging native uptake machinery without introducing foreign structural components [ ]. The following section examines how these naturally derived mechanisms can be harnessed for pulmonary RNA therapeutics.

## Viral vectors

## Adenoviral vectors
Adenoviral vectors are widely utilized in RNA therapy for lung diseases, particularly in lung cancer treatment, due to their high transduction efficiency and broad host range [ ]. These vectors can deliver therapeutic genes either in replication-deficient or replicative forms, with oncolytic adenoviral vectors specifically engineered to proliferate within tumor cells, leading to tumor-specific lysis and enhanced immune activation [ ]. One of the major advantages of adenoviral vectors is their ability to amplify therapeutic gene expression within tumor tissues, ensuring widespread transgene distribution and improved treatment efficacy [ ]. Additionally, capsid modifications and tumor-specific promoters further increase the specificity of adenoviral vectors for cancer cell targeting. These vectors can efficiently deliver pro-apoptotic genes, tumor suppressors, and immune-stimulatory cytokines, inducing tumor cell death and suppressing lung cancer progression [ ]. Additionally, combining adenoviral vectors with cytokines, such as interleukin-10 (IL-10), can boost antitumor immunity and enhance the therapeutic effect, especially in immunocompetent mice [ ].
Recent advancements have led to the optimization of adenoviral vectors for use in RNA therapy applications, with improvements including enhanced RNA encapsulation efficiency, reduced off-target effects, and improved tissue-specific delivery to pulmonary tissues [ ]. For instance, Ad-SLPIEGFR amiR, a novel adenoviral vector, was designed to deliver miRNA targeting EGFR, reducing tumor cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis in nasopharyngeal carcinoma models [ ]. Similarly, adenoviral-mediated miR-141-3p delivery has been shown to attenuate pulmonary fibrosis by targeting Spred2, demonstrating potential for treating fibrotic lung diseases [ ]. Recombinant adenoviruses are currently undergoing clinical trials as gene therapy vectors and anti-cancer agents, with ongoing efforts incorporating CRISPR-Cas9 and miRNA technologies to further enhance their RNA delivery efficiency and therapeutic potential [ ]. Despite these promising advances, adenoviral vectors still face challenges, including immune responses, transient transgene expression, and limited repeated dosing efficacy, requiring continued optimization to improve their safety and long-term effectiveness [ ].

## Lentiviral vectors
Lentiviral vectors provide an effective strategy for sustained RNA delivery, offering long-term gene expression following a single administration [ ]. Their large packaging capacity allows for the efficient delivery of long RNA sequences, making them ideal for mRNA, lncRNA, and antisense RNA-based therapies [ ]. A key advantage of lentiviral vectors is their low inflammatory response, allowing for repeated administration without triggering significant immune reactions, unlike adenoviral and AAV-based vectors [ ].
One of the most notable applications of lentiviral vectors in lung therapy is their ability to target alveolar macrophages (AMs), a critical component in lung immune responses [ ]. Andrew et al. demonstrated that lentiviral RNAi against p65 (RelA), a key NF-κB subunit, suppressed lung inflammation and neutrophilic chemokine expression in vivo via intratracheal instillation. In this study, lentiviral-mediated NF-κB inhibition successfully reduced lung neutrophilia in LPS-stimulated models, highlighting its potential for chronic lung disease treatment (Fig. A). Additionally, a lentiviral-based reporter system was developed to monitor real-time NF-κB activity, offering insights into gene expression dynamics during inflammation (Fig. B-C). In LPS-induced inflammatory models, alveolar macrophages have been reported to persist in the alveolar space for several weeks after the insult. Even following high doses of LPS, a substantial proportion of GFP-labeled resident alveolar macrophages remained detectable, indicating their long-term maintenance after inflammatory injury (Fig. D) [ ]. Zohreh et al. investigated the inhibition of Cdc42 expression in Calu-6 lung cancer cells as a potential strategy for targeting tumor cell migration and proliferation. Lentiviral shRNA targeting Cdc42 was delivered to Calu-6 lung cancer cells, achieving significant gene knockdown and reduced cell viability. The study demonstrated that lentiviral-mediated shRNA delivery effectively inhibited Cdc42 expression, reducing cell viability over time. These findings suggest that lentiviral vectors could serve as efficient tools for gene silencing, offering a promising approach to inhibit lung cancer cell proliferation through modulation of Cdc42 expression [ ]. Jasmin et al. explored the use of human-induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived lung organoids for investigating pulmonary disease mechanisms. The research utilized the HDF-mRNA cell line to optimize LVV transduction efficiency in lung organoids. An optimized transduction protocol was developed for iPSC-derived lung organoids, achieving efficient gene delivery validated by scRNA-seq analysis. This optimized transduction protocol may serve as a valuable tool for studying specific cellular and molecular mechanisms in development and disease models using human iPSC-derived lung organoids [ ].
 Despite these advantages, lentiviral vectors face several challenges, including the complexity of production, potential risks of host genome integration, and biosafety concerns [ ]. Engineering pseudotyped lentiviral vectors that enhance targeting specificity while reducing integration-associated risks remains a major focus for ongoing research [ ]. While clinical translation of lentiviral RNA therapies is still in progress, these vectors hold great potential for long-term pulmonary gene therapy applications [ ].

## Adeno-associated virus vectors
AAV vectors have been extensively explored for lung-targeted gene therapy, showing great potential for treating genetic and inflammatory lung diseases [ ]. The first clinical trial utilizing recombinant AAV (rAAV) for a monogenic disorder was conducted in CF patients, marking a significant advancement in pulmonary gene therapy [ ]. Different AAV serotypes, including AAV1, AAV5, AAV6, and AAV9, have demonstrated lung transduction capabilities, with AAV1 showing strong tropism for human airway epithelial cells, whereas AAV5 exhibits superior transduction efficiency in murine and primate lung models [ ].
Liang et al. developed a dual-AAV CRISPR platform, enabling precise gene editing in the mouse lung via injected intratracheally (Fig. A-B), achieving 19–26% transduction efficiency in club and ciliated epithelial cells using AAV5-based Cas9 and guide RNA delivery (Fig. C-D) [ ]. This system highlights AAV’s potential for precise genome editing in lung tissues. Similarly, Zhang et al. used AAV9 to deliver the Tspyl2 gene, targeting CDA1 expression in pulmonary fibrosis models. This gene therapy approach successfully inhibited TGF-β signaling, reducing inflammation and fibrosis in bleomycin-induced PF models [ ].
 Additionally, Arvind et al. conducted AAV serotype screening for lung stem cell targeting, identifying AAV5, AAV4, and AAV8 as efficient vectors for gene delivery to distinct alveolar cell populations. Specifically, AAV5-mediated gene transduction demonstrated strong gene editing potential in alveolar type 2 (AT2) stem cells, highlighting AAV’s utility in lung regenerative medicine (Fig. E-G) [ ].
AAV vectors offer significant advantages for RNA delivery to the lungs, including their ability to transduce a wide range of cell types with high efficiency and specificity. Non-invasive administration methods, such as intranasal injections, further enhance their clinical potential [ ]. AAVs are also known for their safety profile, as they generally do not integrate into the host genome, reducing the risk of insertional mutagenesis [ ]. However, challenges remain, including serotype-specific tropism, immune responses that can reduce transduction efficiency upon repeated administration, and the limited size of the AAV genome, which restricts the amount of genetic material that can be delivered [ ]. Despite these limitations, AAV vectors remain a powerful tool for RNA delivery in lung-targeted gene therapies, with ongoing efforts to optimize their use for clinical applications.

## Retroviral vectors
Retroviral vectors remain a widely used platform for stable gene delivery, efficiently integrating transgenes into the host genome to enable long-term gene expression [ ]. Zhang et al. developed a retroviral vector system to efficiently deliver a K-ras antisense RNA into human lung cancer cells(H460a). The antisense gene was successfully integrated into the genome of H460a cells, leading to specific inhibition of the mutant K-ras protein, significant suppression of cell proliferation, and reduced tumorigenicity in an orthotopic lung cancer model [ ]. Hiroki et al. evaluated the use of replication-competent RRV for gene delivery in experimental lung cancer models. RRVs carrying reporter or therapeutic genes, demonstrated efficient viral replication, high tumor transduction, and potent cytotoxic effects upon prodrug activation. These findings highlight the potential of RRV-mediated gene therapy as a novel strategy for targeting pulmonary malignancies [ ]. However, their application in lung RNA delivery is limited, as retroviral vectors preferentially target dividing cells, while most lung epithelial cells are non-dividing, reducing their transduction efficiency for pulmonary therapies [ ].
Despite this limitation, researchers continue to explore the use of retroviral vectors for lung gene therapy, particularly for conditions that involve dividing cell populations, such as lung cancer stem cells [ ]. However, the requirement for biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) containment in production and concerns regarding insertional mutagenesis restrict the widespread clinical application of retroviral vectors for pulmonary gene therapy [ ].

## Exosomes
Exosomes, small extracellular vesicles (~ 100 nm in diameter) with a lipid bilayer structure, have emerged as promising non-synthetic carriers for RNA delivery in pulmonary therapies [ ]. Secreted by most cell types, exosomes play a crucial role in intercellular communication, facilitating the transfer of macromolecular cargo, including RNA, proteins, and lipids, to recipient cells [ ]. Due to their natural origin, exosomes offer several advantages over synthetic delivery systems, including biocompatibility, immunological tolerance, and the ability to transport functional biomolecules to target tissues with minimal immune response [ ]. Their ability to efficiently cross biological barriers, makes them particularly attractive for lung-targeted RNA therapies [ ].
Recent advances in exosome engineering and RNA nanotechnology have enabled the development of targeted exosome-based RNA therapeutics [ ]. Li et al. developed an exosome-based siRNA delivery platform targeting survivin, an anti-apoptotic protein overexpressed in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). As illustrated in Fig. A, exosomes were first loaded with therapeutic siRNA, then surface-decorated with an EGFR-targeting aptamer (EGFRApt) to achieve tumor-selective delivery. Biodistribution studies in H596 NSCLC xenograft mice demonstrated a shift in targeting specificity upon aptamer functionalization (Fig. B). Unmodified Exo/siSurvivin predominantly accumulated in the liver with minimal tumor uptake. In contrast, EGFRApt/Exo/siSurvivin exhibited rapid and persistent tumor-specific localization, with strong fluorescent signals appearing at the tumor site as early as 4 h and maintained through 48 h post-injection. Notably, ex vivo imaging confirmed intense tumor accumulation while significantly reducing hepatic sequestration, with negligible off-target distribution in the heart, spleen, lung parenchyma, and kidney. This EGFR-mediated active targeting strategy enabled efficient endosomal escape and potent survivin knockdown in lung tumor tissues, achieving an IC₅₀ of 20 nmol/kg and markedly enhanced chemosensitivity, highlighting the potential of aptamer-guided exosomes as precision nanocarriers for lung cancer therapy [ ].
 In PF, Qiu et al. developed a milk-derived exosome (M-Exos) platform loaded with siRNA targeting TGF-β1, a key driver of fibrotic progression. Administered via nebulization in a bleomycin-induced PF mouse model, MsiTGF-β1 effectively reduced inflammation, extracellular matrix (ECM) collagen and fibronectin. This system efficiently delivered siRNA to lung tissues, silenced TGF-β1 mRNA, and inhibited the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway, a critical process in fibrotic lung remodeling [ ].
Aqil et al. further demonstrated the potential of bovine milk-derived exosomes as biocompatible and scalable siRNA delivery platforms. In cell culture and xenograft models, these exosomes efficiently delivered siRNA to pulmonary cells, exhibiting enhanced antitumor activity in lung cancer [ ]. Similarly, Zhang et al. explored the role of inhaled exosomes in modulating pulmonary immune responses, showing that serum-derived exosomes can efficiently transport siRNA and miRNA to lung macrophages. This approach significantly modulated inflammation without triggering adverse immune responses, highlighting exosomes’ potential in treating inflammatory and immune-related lung disorders [ , ].
Exosomes hold significant promise for RNA-based pulmonary therapies, owing to their ability to transport siRNA, miRNA, and other RNA molecules across biological barriers, including the pulmonary epithelium [ ]. Their lipid bilayer composition provides structural stability and protection against enzymatic degradation, minimizing RNA degradation risks [ ]. However, several challenges remain in their clinical application, including low exosome yields during isolation, limited understanding of biodistribution, and batch-to-batch variability [ ]. Moreover, optimizing RNA loading, improving targeting specificity, and enhancing controlled RNA release mechanisms are critical for maximizing therapeutic efficacy [ ]. Despite these challenges, ongoing advancements in exosome engineering, large-scale production techniques, and precision targeting strategies continue to push the boundaries of exosome-based RNA therapies, making them a highly promising tool for lung disease treatment and regenerative medicine.

## Protein carriers

## Structural characteristics and lung-targeting mechanisms
Protein Carriers represent a distinct class of RNA delivery vehicles that leverage evolutionarily optimized biological structures for nucleic acid transport. Unlike synthetic carriers that rely on engineered electrostatic or hydrophobic interactions, protein carriers employ receptor-mediated endocytosis pathways refined over evolutionary timescales, offering intrinsic biocompatibility and predictable biodegradation [ ]. The pulmonary vasculature presents unique opportunities for protein-mediated targeting, as the lung capillary bed represents the first microvascular network encountered by intravenously administered particles and expresses distinct surface markers that can be exploited for selective delivery [ ].
Albumin, the most abundant plasma protein (35–50 g/L in human serum), has emerged as a versatile platform for pulmonary RNA delivery due to its extended circulation half-life (approximately 19 days), natural tumor-accumulating properties via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, and receptor-mediated uptake through gp60 and SPARC pathways overexpressed in many lung tumors [ , ]. Cationic modification of bovine serum albumin (CBSA) introduces positive surface charge through chemical amination, enabling electrostatic complexation with negatively charged siRNA while preserving the protein’s inherent lung-targeting properties. Importantly, systemic delivery of cationic vectors has been shown to mediate specific and efficient transfection within the lung, primarily through interaction with serum proteins that redirect biodistribution toward pulmonary tissues [ ].
Ferritin nanocages, composed of 24 self-assembling subunits forming hollow spherical structures with 8 nm internal and 12 nm external diameters, provide a distinct protein-based delivery platform [ ]. The transferrin receptor 1 (TfR1/CD71), which mediates ferritin internalization, is significantly overexpressed in lung cancer cells compared to normal bronchial epithelial cells, providing an endogenous targeting mechanism for pulmonary malignancies [ ]. Furthermore, ferritin’s hollow architecture enables encapsulation of therapeutic cargo through pH-dependent disassembly/reassembly processes, while its external surface can be chemically or genetically modified with targeting ligands for enhanced lung specificity [ ].

## Albumin-based systems for pulmonary RNA delivery
Han and colleagues developed a charge-density-controllable siRNA delivery system using CBSA with optimized isoelectric points for treating lung metastatic cancer [ ]. By systematic surface modification of BSA, CBSA variants with different cationization degrees were synthesized and evaluated for siRNA binding capacity, delivery efficiency, and cytotoxicity. The optimized CBSA formed stable nanosized particles (approximately 150–200 nm) with siRNA through electrostatic complexation and protected siRNA from nuclease degradation. Intravenous administration of CBSA/siRNA nanoparticles demonstrated significant accumulation in lung tissue, attributed to the interaction between cationic carriers and pulmonary vascular endothelium. When formulated with Bcl-2-specific siRNA, CBSA/siRNA nanoparticles achieved efficient gene silencing that induced notable cancer cell apoptosis and inhibited lung metastasis progression in murine models [ ].
Building upon this platform, Zhao et al. developed biomimetic nanoparticles (CBSA/siS100A4@Exosome) featuring a CBSA/siS100A4 core encapsulated within an exosome membrane shell [ ]. This hybrid architecture combined the siRNA-condensing capacity of CBSA with the immune-evasive properties of exosomal membranes. In postoperative lung metastasis models, these biomimetic nanoparticles effectively accumulated in pulmonary tissues and achieved significant S100A4 gene silencing, resulting in inhibition of metastatic progression (Fig. A) [ ].
 For NSCLC harboring KRAS mutations, Merkel and colleagues prepared BSA nanoparticles by the desolvation method to deliver siRNA targeting the KRAS G12S mutation [ ]. The BSA nanoparticles exhibited favorable physicochemical properties with high encapsulation efficiency and sustained nucleic acid release. In KRAS G12S-mutant A549 lung adenocarcinoma cells, siRNA-loaded BSA nanoparticles achieved efficient cellular uptake, appropriate intracellular distribution, and significant gene knockdown that interfered with cancer hallmarks including uncontrolled proliferation and migration [ ].
To address chemotherapy resistance in NSCLC, combination strategies employing albumin-based carriers have demonstrated synergistic efficacy. Chen et al. developed cabazitaxel-loaded human serum albumin nanoparticles (CTX-HSA-NPs) combined with TGFβ−1 siRNA lipid nanoparticles for treating paclitaxel-resistant lung cancer [ ]. The HSA-based formulation improved the safety profile of conventional chemotherapy while the siRNA component targeted TGFβ−1-mediated drug resistance mechanisms (Fig. ). This combination approach inhibited drug resistance-related protein expression while maintaining potent antitumor activity, demonstrating the potential of protein-siRNA hybrid systems for overcoming therapeutic resistance in lung malignancies [ ].
 An innovative albumin-binding strategy was reported by Bhattacharya and colleagues, who synthesized siRNA conjugated to a diacyl lipid moiety (siRNA-L2) that rapidly binds endogenous albumin in situ following systemic administration [ ]. Compared with unmodified siRNA, siRNA-L2 exhibited 5.7-fold increased circulation half-life, 8.6-fold enhanced bioavailability, and reduced renal accumulation. In preclinical tumor models, siRNA-L2 achieved 19-fold greater tumor accumulation and demonstrated complete lack of toxicity at doses up to 10 mg/kg, sharply contrasting with reported toxicity of synthetic nanoparticle carriers. The tumor-to-liver ratio of 40:1 for siRNA-L2 compared favorably with 3:1 for conventional polyplexes, highlighting the potential of albumin-hitchhiking strategies for selective pulmonary tumor delivery (Fig. B–E) [ ].

## Ferritin nanocages for lung-targeted delivery
Ferritin-based delivery systems exploit TfR1-mediated internalization for selective targeting of lung cancer cells. TfR1 is expressed in most lung cancers and serves as an indicator of poor prognosis in NSCLC patients, with expression levels significantly higher than in normal bronchial epithelial tissues [ ]. Wu et al. demonstrated that blocking TfR1 function, either through siRNA knockdown or antibody blocking, significantly inhibited lung adenocarcinoma cell proliferation by targeting the oncogene KRAS, validating TfR1 as a therapeutic target and highlighting the rationale for TfR1-directed protein carriers [ ].
Muzykantov and colleagues investigated ferritin nanoparticles for pulmonary vascular immunotargeting, comparing ICAM-1 and PECAM-1-directed delivery strategies [ ]. Anti-ICAM-1 antibody-conjugated ferritin nanoparticles (anti-ICAM Ab/FNPs) demonstrated pulmonary targeting efficiency of 160.9 ± 6.5% injected dose per gram lung tissue, significantly superior to other tested nanocarrier formulations including immunoliposomes and polymer particles. This exceptional lung targeting was attributed to the high density of ICAM-1 on pulmonary endothelial cells combined with the favorable biodistribution properties of ferritin nanocages [ ].
Luo et al. employed EDC/NHS-catalyzed esterification to conjugate hyaluronan (HA), a ligand of CD44, to the ferritin nanocage surface, generating HA-ferritin complexes for lung cancer-targeted drug delivery [ ]. CD44 is overexpressed in many lung cancer subtypes and cancer stem cells, providing an additional targeting mechanism beyond TfR1. The dual-targeting HA-ferritin system demonstrated enhanced accumulation in CD44-positive lung tumors and improved therapeutic efficacy compared to unmodified ferritin carriers [ ].

## Transferrin receptor-targeted protein-polymer hybrids
The integration of transferrin or TfR-binding ligands with polymer carriers has yielded hybrid systems with enhanced lung cancer specificity. Ramesh and colleagues created transferrin-modified DOTAP: cholesterol liposomal nanoparticles (TfNP) for HuR siRNA delivery to lung cancer [ ]. TfR expression was confirmed in multiple lung cancer cell lines, including A549 and HCC827, providing the biological basis for targeted delivery. HuR-TfNP efficiently downregulated HuR and HuR-regulated proteins in lung cancer cells, resulting in reduced cell viability (> 60% reduction), inhibition of cell migration and invasion, and induction of G1 cell-cycle arrest culminating in apoptosis. Importantly, HuR-TfNP activity in normal MRC-9 lung fibroblasts was negligible, demonstrating tumor selectivity. In vivo biodistribution studies confirmed that fluorescently labeled TfNP localized preferentially in tumor tissues, and therapeutic studies showed significant tumor growth inhibition in both subcutaneous and metastatic lung tumor models [ ].
Merkel and colleagues developed TfR-binding peptide (HAIYPRH)-conjugated polyethylenimine (HAI-bPEI) for selective siRNA delivery to TfR-overexpressing lung cancer cells [ ]. The HAI-bPEI conjugate maintained physicochemical properties comparable to unmodified bPEI in terms of size, zeta-potential, and siRNA condensation efficiency. Confocal microscopy and flow cytometry revealed that HAI-bPEI selectively delivered siRNA to H1299 cells (high TfR expression) compared with A549 or H460 cells (lower TfR expression). Desferrioxamine pretreatment, which upregulates TfR expression through iron depletion, enhanced siRNA uptake in treated cells, confirming the TfR-dependent delivery mechanism. Competition studies with free transferrin further validated the receptor-specific cellular uptake of HAI-bPEI polyplexes [ ].

## Emerging protein capsid technologies
Beyond naturally occurring proteins, engineered protein capsids derived from endogenous retroviral elements offer programmable platforms for RNA delivery. Segel et al. identified mammalian Gag homologs, including PEG10, that selectively bind their own mRNA and facilitate vesicular secretion [ ]. By modifying the untranslated regions flanking target genes, PEG10’s cargo specificity can be reprogrammed, enabling the development of the SEND system. This engineered PEG10-based platform allows for packaging, secretion, and targeted delivery of specific RNA molecules, representing a modular approach for therapeutic RNA delivery. Preliminary studies demonstrated successful delivery of SpCas9 mRNA via SEND to HEK293FT cells, achieving detectable gene editing at target loci [ ].
Additionally, human paraneoplastic Ma antigen (PNMA2) proteins have been engineered to form stable icosahedral capsids capable of encapsulating RNA [ ]. Cryo-electron microscopy studies confirmed the structural integrity of engineered PNMA2 capsids and their capacity for nucleic acid packaging. While pulmonary-specific data remain limited, the endogenous origin of PNMA proteins suggests favorable immunogenicity profiles for clinical translation. Further studies are needed to assess tissue-specific targeting and therapeutic efficacy in lung disease models [ ].
Protein-based nanocarriers for pulmonary RNA delivery face several challenges requiring continued optimization. Cargo loading capacity remains constrained by the physical dimensions of protein nanocages; ferritin’s 8 nm internal cavity limits encapsulation to relatively small RNA payloads, potentially excluding larger mRNA constructs [ ]. Immunogenicity concerns, while reduced compared to viral vectors, persist for non-human proteins such as bovine serum albumin, necessitating humanized alternatives for clinical applications [ ]. Future development should focus on expanding cargo capacity through protein engineering approaches, developing fully human protein carriers to minimize immunogenicity, and incorporating stimuli-responsive elements for triggered release in the pulmonary microenvironment [ ]. The integration of multiple targeting ligands on protein surfaces, combined with the intrinsic tumor-homing properties of albumin and ferritin, may enable precision targeting of specific lung cancer subtypes or pulmonary cell populations [ ]. By leveraging the favorable biodistribution, biocompatibility, and receptor-targeting capabilities of protein-based systems, these carriers hold significant promise for next-generation pulmonary RNA therapeutics.

## Comparative analysis of delivery platforms for pulmonary RNA therapeutics
The rational selection of delivery vehicles for pulmonary RNA therapeutics necessitates a nuanced understanding of how each platform’s inherent physicochemical properties interact with the unique physiological barriers of the respiratory system. Rather than seeking a universally superior carrier, the field is increasingly recognizing that optimal platform selection must be guided by specific therapeutic objectives, target cell populations, and clinical contexts.

## Mechanistic considerations in synthetic carrier design
Lipid nanoparticles represent the most clinically validated platform, with their success in COVID-19 mRNA vaccines establishing a regulatory and manufacturing precedent. The ionizable lipid component—exemplified by SM-102 and ALC-0315—confers pH-dependent membrane destabilization that facilitates endosomal escape, a rate-limiting step in cytoplasmic RNA delivery [ ]. However, this same ionizable character presents particular challenges for pulmonary applications. Upon aerosolization, the shear forces generated during nebulization can compromise LNP structural integrity, leading to premature cargo release and diminished transfection efficiency [ ]. Recent innovations addressing this limitation include charge-assisted stabilization strategies utilizing peptide-lipid conjugates that enhance colloidal stability during nebulization while maintaining transfection competence [ ].
The selective organ targeting (SORT) paradigm has fundamentally altered LNP design philosophy for extrahepatic delivery. By incorporating permanently charged lipids such as DOTAP at defined molar ratios (10–50%), investigators have demonstrated preferential lung accumulation following intravenous administration [ ]. Mechanistically, this tropism shift correlates with altered protein corona composition—specifically, enrichment of vitronectin and other integrin-binding proteins that redirect LNP trafficking toward pulmonary endothelial cells expressing αvβ3 integrins [ ]. This endogenous targeting mechanism circumvents the need for exogenous targeting ligands but simultaneously limits cellular specificity within the lung microenvironment.
Polymer-based carriers, particularly poly(β-amino esters) (PBAEs), offer complementary advantages including enhanced chemical tunability and reduced manufacturing complexity compared to multi-component LNP formulations [ ]. Recent high-throughput screening of 166 PBAE formulations identified poly(β-amino-thio-esters) incorporating ethane dithiol modifications that demonstrate exceptional nebulization stability and species-agnostic delivery across mice, hamsters, ferrets, and non-human primates [ ]. The structure-function relationships governing polymer performance remain incompletely characterized, however, with seemingly minor structural modifications producing dramatic shifts in transfection efficiency—a phenomenon that complicates rational design efforts [ ].

## Biological carriers: navigating the biocompatibility-scalability Trade-off
Extracellular vesicles, particularly exosomes, possess intrinsic attributes that synthetic carriers cannot readily replicate: endogenous membrane composition conferring reduced immunogenicity, native cell-targeting moieties, and demonstrated capacity for endosomal escape via direct membrane fusion [ ]. Lung-derived exosomes exhibit superior mRNA and protein cargo distribution to bronchioles and parenchyma compared to both HEK293-derived exosomes and conventional liposomes following nebulization [ ]. This organotropism likely reflects retention of tissue-specific surface markers from parental cells—a feature that could theoretically be exploited for targeting specific pulmonary cell populations.
The fundamental limitation constraining exosome clinical translation lies not in biological performance but in manufacturing scalability. Current isolation methodologies yield heterogeneous populations with batch-to-batch variability in size distribution (30–150 nm), surface marker expression, and cargo loading efficiency [ ]. RNA loading into exosomes typically achieves 10–25% efficiency compared to > 90% for optimized LNP formulations, necessitating substantially higher vesicle quantities to deliver equivalent RNA doses [ ]. Furthermore, the absence of standardized quality control metrics and regulatory guidance for exosome-based therapeutics introduces additional uncertainty into clinical development pathways [ ].
Adeno-associated viral vectors occupy a distinct niche, offering unparalleled transduction efficiency and the potential for sustained transgene expression through episomal persistence in non-dividing cells [ ]. Seven clinical trials evaluating AAV-CFTR for cystic fibrosis collectively demonstrated safety but failed to achieve clinically meaningful improvements in pulmonary function [ ]. Post-hoc analyses attributed this therapeutic gap to insufficient transduction of airway epithelial cells, suboptimal promoter activity, and the immunological barrier posed by pre-existing neutralizing antibodies [ ].
The seroprevalence of anti-AAV neutralizing antibodies represents a particularly consequential limitation. Global epidemiological studies indicate that 30–60% of adults harbor antibodies against common serotypes, with AAV2 exhibiting the highest seroprevalence and AAV5 the lowest [ ]. Even low-titer antibodies (≥ 1:5) can substantially diminish transduction efficiency, effectively excluding a significant proportion of potential patients from AAV-based therapies [ ]. While alternative serotypes derived from non-human primates (AAVrh10, AAVrh74) demonstrate reduced seroprevalence, cross-reactive immunity remains a concern [ ]. The immunological memory established following initial AAV exposure further precludes repeated administration—a critical constraint for chronic pulmonary conditions requiring sustained therapeutic intervention [ ].

## Peptide and protein-based systems: precision versus efficiency
Cell-penetrating peptides offer a modular platform wherein targeting domains, endosomolytic sequences, and nucleic acid-binding motifs can be combined within a single molecular architecture [ ]. Arginine-rich sequences such as TAT and oligoarginine facilitate electrostatic complexation with anionic RNA while promoting cellular uptake through both endocytic and direct translocation mechanisms [ ]. The p5RHH peptide has demonstrated transfection efficiencies approaching lipofectamine in certain cell types, attributable to its enhanced endosomal membrane disruption capacity [ ].
The principal limitation of peptide-based carriers resides in their pharmacokinetic vulnerability. Circulating proteases rapidly degrade unmodified peptides, resulting in half-lives measured in minutes rather than hours [ ]. Chemical modifications including D-amino acid substitution, cyclization, and PEGylation can extend circulation time but frequently compromise cell-penetrating activity or introduce immunogenic epitopes [ ]. For pulmonary delivery specifically, the protease-rich environment of airway surface liquid and inflammatory exudates in diseased lungs may further accelerate peptide degradation, potentially limiting therapeutic applicability to conditions with minimal airway inflammation.
Protein-based carriers, including engineered antibody fragments and receptor-targeting fusion proteins, provide the highest degree of cellular specificity among non-viral platforms [ ]. By exploiting receptor-mediated endocytosis pathways, these carriers can theoretically achieve selective transfection of specific pulmonary cell populations—an attribute particularly relevant for diseases affecting discrete cellular compartments. The rate-limiting step remains endosomal escape; protein carriers generally lack the membrane-disruptive properties of ionizable lipids or fusogenic peptides, resulting in substantial lysosomal degradation of internalized cargo [ ].

## Strategic framework for platform selection
The comparative analysis presented herein supports a context-dependent approach to delivery platform selection rather than identification of a universally optimal carrier. For acute interventions requiring transient, high-level protein expression—such as anti-inflammatory cytokine delivery in acute respiratory distress syndrome—LNP platforms offer the most favorable risk-benefit profile given their established safety record and manufacturing infrastructure. For genetic disorders requiring durable correction, AAV vectors remain compelling despite immunological limitations, particularly for pediatric populations with lower seroprevalence [ ]. For applications demanding precise cellular targeting within heterogeneous lung tissue architecture, hybrid systems incorporating biological targeting moieties onto synthetic scaffolds represent a promising developmental direction [ ].
The convergence of advances in lipid chemistry, polymer science, and biological carrier engineering suggests that next-generation pulmonary RNA therapeutics will increasingly employ rationally designed hybrid platforms that integrate the complementary strengths of multiple delivery modalities. Realizing this potential will require continued investment in fundamental structure-activity relationships, development of physiologically relevant preclinical models, and establishment of standardized metrics for comparative platform evaluation.

## Conclusions and future perspectives
The development of targeted RNA delivery systems has significantly advanced pulmonary medicine, offering opportunities to address the molecular underpinnings of diverse lung pathologies. Considerable progress has been achieved in the design and optimization of both synthetic and non-synthetic delivery platforms [ , ]. Despite significant advances, several critical challenges must be addressed to enable clinical translation.

## Critical challenges and current limitations

1) Toxicity. LNPs exhibit dose-dependent cytotoxicity and inflammatory responses mediated by pattern recognition receptors [ ]. Certain polymer compositions trigger cytotoxicity depending on molecular weight and degradation products [ ]. Viral vectors with integrating capacity carry insertional mutagenesis risks [ ].

2) Immunogenicity. LNP components can activate innate immune pathways [ ]. Peptide-based vectors may elicit immune responses due to recognition of non-self-sequences [ ]. Viral vectors face robust humoral and cellular immunity, with pre-existing neutralizing antibodies in substantial patient populations severely limiting repeated dosing [ ]. Protein-based carriers may trigger immunogenicity depending on source and modifications [ ].

3) Manufacturability. Exosomes and biological vesicles face significant hurdles in large-scale production, batch-to-batch variability, and heterogeneous cargo loading [ ]. Technical challenges in surface modification without compromising vesicle integrity further complicate clinical translation [ ].

4) Stability. LNPs exhibit insufficient circulation stability in biological fluids [ ]. Peptide-based vectors undergo rapid enzymatic degradation, resulting in abbreviated circulation half-life [ ]. Polymer-based systems show variable performance depending on composition [ ]. Protein-based carriers often exhibit poor stability under physiological conditions [ ].

5) Delivery Efficiency. LNPs are constrained by non-specific tissue distribution [ ]. Polymer-based carriers exhibit variable transfection efficiency [ ]. Exosomes show relatively low RNA loading efficiency compared to synthetic systems [ ]. Protein-based carriers demonstrate limited endosomal escape capacity [ ].
These limitations underscore the need for continued rational design and optimization to achieve safe, efficient pulmonary RNA therapeutics.

## Future directions and priority research areas

1) Cell type heterogeneity and disease-relevant therapeutic targeting. The lung exhibits pronounced cellular heterogeneity, encompassing alveolar epithelial cells (type I and II), airway epithelial cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and resident immune cells such as alveolar macrophages, dendritic cells, and lymphocytes [ ]. Alveolar type II cells are critical for surfactant production and repair after injury, making them prime targets in acute respiratory distress syndrome and viral infections [ ]. Type I alveolar cells, involved in gas exchange, are important in strategies addressing alveolar damage [ ]. Airway epithelial cells regulate mucociliary clearance and barrier integrity, serving as key targets in cystic fibrosis, asthma, and COPD [ ]. Endothelial cells maintain vascular homeostasis, and their dysfunction contributes to pulmonary hypertension and inflammatory injury [ ]. Fibroblasts mediate tissue remodeling and fibrosis, representing targets in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and post-injury fibrosis [ ]. Immune cells, including macrophages, dendritic cells, and lymphocytes, orchestrate inflammation and antiviral responses, providing potential targets for immunomodulation or antiviral RNA therapies [ ]. Understanding the distribution, functional specialization, and disease-specific relevance of these diverse lung cell populations is therefore essential for designing precise, efficient, and safe RNA delivery systems tailored to specific pulmonary diseases.

2)Mechanistic strategies for effective mucosal penetration and cellular targeting. The viscosity of mucus and active mucociliary clearance impose direct limitations on carrier diffusion and stability, while the heterogeneity of lung cell populations determines cellular sensitivity and uptake efficiency [ ]. Carrier properties, including particle size, surface chemistry, charge, and structural stability, critically influence their ability to traverse the mucus layer, evade clearance mechanisms, and achieve intracellular delivery [ ]. Interactions with the pulmonary microenvironment, such as mucus composition, surfactant presence, and immune surveillance, further modulate delivery performance [ ]. The choice of administration route is a pivotal factor in pulmonary RNA therapeutics. It should be guided by considerations including the targeted lung region (upper vs. lower airway), the relevant cell populations, the physicochemical compatibility of the carrier with the delivery environment, the required dosing frequency, and the need to minimize systemic exposure and off-target effects [ ].

3) Physicochemical determinants of pulmonary delivery efficacy. The efficacy of RNA nanocarriers for pulmonary delivery fundamentally depends on the intricate interplay between their structural architecture and lung-specific biological functionality, wherein precise physicochemical parameters collectively dictate alveolar deposition patterns and retention kinetics [ ]. Particle size represents the primary determinant of pulmonary tropism, with an optimal size range of 100–500 nm enabling deep lung penetration to the alveolar region while circumventing rapid alveolar macrophage uptake, whereas submicron LNPs incorporating ionizable lipids (pKa 6.0–6.5.0.5) demonstrate enhanced alveolar epithelial cell internalization through size-dependent transcytosis mechanisms and receptor-mediated endocytosis [ ]. Surface charge density critically influences interactions with pulmonary surfactant proteins SP-A and SP-D, wherein slightly anionic carriers (ζ-potential: −10 to −30 mV) exhibit prolonged alveolar residence time by minimizing opsonization and surfactant-mediated clearance, contrasting sharply with highly cationic polymer-based systems that show rapid mucociliary elimination despite demonstrating superior initial cellular uptake [ ]. The hydrophobic-hydrophilic balance governs penetration efficiency through the pulmonary surfactant monolayer, with amphiphilic peptide-based delivery vectors incorporating lung-specific surfactant protein B (SP-B) mimetic sequences achieving optimal surface tension reduction and favorable alveolar spreading coefficients [ ]. Morphological parameters significantly impact aerodynamic deposition behavior, wherein elongated viral vectors and rod-shaped carriers (aspect ratio > 3:1) demonstrate preferential deposition in terminal bronchioles through enhanced inertial impaction mechanisms, while spherical exosome-based carriers favor alveolar sedimentation via Brownian motion and gravitational settling [ ]. Protein-based carriers, particularly ferritin nanocages and albumin conjugates, can leverage lung-specific receptor targeting through surface modification with pneumocyte-selective ligands such as integrin αvβ6 and the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), thereby enhancing cell-type specificity [ ]. Density-dependent gravitational settling becomes critically important for carriers with densities > 1 g/cm³, wherein hollow polymeric microspheres achieve optimal lung deposition through reduced aerodynamic diameter despite possessing larger geometric dimensions [ ]. Surface functionalization with pulmonary surfactant protein mimetics or phosphatidylcholine derivatives enhances biocompatibility and substantially reduces inflammatory responses, while incorporation of stimuli-responsive elements responding to pathologically elevated glutathione concentrations (2–10 mM) characteristic of diseased lung tissue enables selective intracellular payload release [ ]. The strategic integration of mucoadhesive properties through chitosan coating or thiomer incorporation extends pulmonary residence time, creating a temporal advantage for sustained RNA expression, while incorporation of lung-targeting peptides derived from angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) or surfactant proteins provides cell-specific uptake mechanisms essential for therapeutic efficacy in respiratory diseases [ ].
Future research should address several critical areas to optimize RNA delivery to pulmonary tissues [ ]. Enhancing targeting specificity remains a top priority, which may be achieved by incorporating receptor-targeted ligands or modifying delivery systems to improve binding affinity for specific lung cell types [ , ]. Improving the biocompatibility of non-viral vectors, particularly by developing novel lipid formulations or biodegradable polymers, will help reduce adverse immune reactions while maintaining high transfection efficiency [ ]. Protein-based carriers, inspired by natural viral components, present an exciting avenue for the development of precision-targeted, low-immunogenicity RNA therapeutics [ ].
A deeper understanding of the interactions between RNA delivery vehicles and the pulmonary microenvironment is also crucial for further optimizing therapeutic outcomes [ ]. Advanced imaging technologies and high-throughput screening techniques will facilitate detailed biodistribution analysis, allowing researchers to systematically evaluate novel delivery strategies in preclinical and clinical settings. Additionally, personalized medicine approaches may greatly enhance therapeutic success, by tailoring RNA delivery systems based on individual patient characteristics, such as genetic mutations, disease pathology, and immune responses [ ].
The integration of combinatorial delivery strategies is another promising area for exploration [ ]. Hybrid approaches, such as combining lipid nanoparticles with receptor-specific ligands or engineering exosome-derived systems to work synergistically with synthetic polymers, may overcome the limitations of individual platforms, leading to higher targeting precision and improved therapeutic efficiency [ ]. These multimodal delivery approaches could optimize RNA uptake while minimizing systemic toxicity, making them more clinically viable for long-term applications [ ].
In conclusion, while significant advancements have been made in developing targeted RNA delivery systems for pulmonary diseases, further optimization and innovation are essential to overcome existing challenges. By focusing on improving targeting specificity, minimizing off-target toxicity, and ensuring stable and efficient RNA transport to lung tissues, future developments will drive RNA therapeutics closer to widespread clinical application. The continued evolution of RNA delivery technologies, coupled with an expanding knowledge of pulmonary disease mechanisms and patient-specific factors, will be pivotal in unlocking the full therapeutic potential of RNA-based treatments, ultimately transforming the landscape of pulmonary medicine.
