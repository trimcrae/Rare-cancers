## Abstract
Gastric cancer (GC) remains a major global health problem and demands more effective therapeutic approaches. In this study, we developed a novel biomimetic nanovesicle system—cancer cell membrane-coated liposomal nanocarriers loaded with silibinin (CLip@Sil)—designed for targeted therapy against GC. By integrating network pharmacology with transcriptomic profiling, we identified 13 key target genes, significantly enriched in the PI3K/AKT and IL-17 signaling pathways, with HSP90AA1 emerging as a central regulatory node. Further mechanistic investigation revealed that silibinin exerts its anticancer effect by modulating the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) axis, thereby suppressing the PI3K/AKT pathway. The engineered CLip@Sil system demonstrated enhanced tumor-targeting capacity via its biomimetic cancer-membrane coating, enabling selective drug delivery and accumulation in tumor tissues. In vitro studies confirmed that CLip@Sil inhibited GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while promoting apoptosis. In vivo, CLip@Sil significantly suppressed primary tumor growth and lung metastasis, extended overall survival, and exhibited minimal systemic toxicity. These findings highlight the value of combining competing endogenous RNA regulatory networks with nanomaterial based targeted delivery systems for GC therapy. The CLip@Sil platform offers a promising direction for the future development of precise and biomimetic nanotherapeutics in oncology.
Keywords: Gastric cancer, Silibinin, Network pharmacology, Heat shock protein 90 alpha family class member 1, Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/Protein kinase B signaling pathway

## Introduction
Gastric cancer (GC) is a widespread malignant disease of the digestive system. Reports from the International Agency for Research on Cancer indicate that it is the third leading cause of cancer related deaths worldwide, surpassed only by lung and liver cancers, which makes it a major global health concern [ , ]. Due to the lack of specific symptoms in its early stages, GC often presents with nonspecific signs such as upper abdominal discomfort and appetite loss, leading to diagnosis at advanced stages in the majority of patients [ , ]. At this point, patients typically lose the opportunity for curative surgery and must rely on multimodal treatments including chemotherapy, molecular targeted therapy, or immune checkpoint inhibitors, yet the overall efficacy remains limited [ , ]. The aggressive nature of GC, characterized by high metastatic potential, frequent recurrence, and strong drug resistance, results in a persistently low five-year survival rate of less than 30 % [ , ]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop more precise, safer, and better targeted treatments to enhance patient outcomes and quality of life.
Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has demonstrated antitumor potential by modulating key oncogenic signaling pathways such as the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) pathway and the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway [ , ]. Silibinin, a major flavonolignan derived from Silybum marianum, is known for its antioxidant and anticancer properties, such as promoting apoptosis, inducing cell cycle arrest, and reducing drug resistance [ , ]. In GC, silibinin has been shown to activate the tumor suppressor protein p53, inhibit signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), and suppress epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) via the p38 MAPK signaling pathway. It also exhibits synergistic cytotoxicity when combined with paclitaxel [ , , ]. In addition, silibinin suppresses GC cell proliferation by promoting caspase-dependent apoptosis and regulating the cell cycle [ , ], and its inhibitory effect on Helicobacter pylori highlights its potential relevance in GC prevention [ ]. Nevertheless, the extremely low aqueous solubility and limited oral bioavailability of silibinin [ ] greatly limit its effective tumor accumulation and hinder its further clinical translation, underscoring the need for an advanced delivery system to improve its stability, in vivo distribution, and tumor-targeting efficiency.
Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) participate broadly in tumor initiation and progression, with the competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) mechanism becoming a major focus [ , ]. As molecular sponges, lncRNAs competitively bind microRNAs (miRNAs) to modulate downstream messenger RNA (mRNA) expression and thereby regulate cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [ , ]. In GC, the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 axis is implicated in cancer-related phenotypes [ ], and HSP90AA1 functions in signal transduction, cell cycle control, and apoptosis evasion, correlating with cancer progression [ , ]. The PI3K/AKT pathway, often activated by dysregulated non-coding RNAs, represents a key oncogenic target [ , ]. Network pharmacology, as a systems biology approach, supports multi-target mechanism discovery for natural compounds and, when integrated with multi-omics data, aids in identifying regulatory axes and critical pathways.
Biomimetic nanotechnology has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance tumor-selective drug delivery [ , ]. Cancer cell membrane–coated nanoparticles provide immune evasion, biocompatibility, and homotypic recognition, thereby improving tumor accumulation and uptake [ , ]. Membrane-retained adhesion and immunoregulatory proteins, including the CD47-SIRPα axis, further reduce phagocytic clearance and prolong circulation [ , , , ]. In contrast, liposomes rely mainly on EPR-mediated passive targeting, which is often insufficient for efficient delivery [ ]. Combining liposomes with cancer cell membranes enables dual passive-active targeting while enhancing stability and tumor-site internalization [ ]. This strategy helps overcome silibinin's poor solubility and limited in vivo delivery. Importantly, no prior study has applied a GC cell membrane–liposome hybrid system for silibinin delivery, highlighting the structural and mechanistic innovation of our CLip@Sil platform.
This study applies network pharmacology, differential expression analysis, and molecular docking to systematically identify the key molecular targets of silibinin in GC, with particular focus on the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 regulatory axis and the PI3K-AKT pathway. In addition, we develop a biomimetic GC cell membrane-coated liposomal system (CLip@Sil) to improve the tumor-targeted delivery of silibinin. By integrating mechanistic investigation with a tailored nanodelivery strategy, this work aims to provide a comprehensive molecular basis for the anticancer actions of silibinin and to offer a translationally promising platform for the precision delivery of natural compounds.

## Materials and methods
2.1.

## Materials and instrumentation
The major materials and instruments used in this study included silibinin (Sigma-Aldrich, S0417), lipids such as DOPC and DSPE-PEG2000 (Avanti Polar Lipids), and cholesterol (Sigma-Aldrich), as well as standard cell culture reagents including RPMI-1640, DMEM, FBS, and penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). Key analytical and characterization instruments comprised an HPLC system (Agilent 1260 Infinity II), a dynamic light scattering analyzer (Zetasizer Nano ZS90, Malvern), a transmission electron microscope (HT7700, Hitachi), a confocal laser scanning microscope (TCS SP8, Leica Microsystems), and a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter). Molecular analyses were performed using a real-time PCR system (QuantStudio 6 Flex, Applied Biosystems), a gel imaging system (ChemiDoc XRS+, Bio-Rad), and an ultracentrifuge (Optima XPN-100, Beckman Coulter). Unless otherwise specified, all chemicals were of analytical or chromatography grade, and all antibodies and fluorescent dyes were purchased from Abcam, Cell Signaling Technology, Proteintech, or Thermo Fisher Scientific, with detailed information provided in the corresponding methodological subsections.

2.2.

## Identification of potential silibinin targets
The absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) properties of silibinin were assessed using the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology Database and Analysis Platform (TCMSP, https://tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.php). Candidate target genes were predicted by integrating data from four complementary databases: (1) Bioinformatics Analysis Tool for Molecular mechANism of Traditional Chinese Medicine (BATMAN-TCM, http://bionet.ncpsb.org.cn/batman-tcm/), with a threshold prediction score >15; (2) PharmMapper (http://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/), which identifies targets based on molecular structure similarity; (3) Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb, https://www.dgidb.org/), which catalogs known drug-gene interactions; and (4) TCMSP, which was also used for cross-validation. Overlapping targets identified across these databases were prioritized for further analysis.

2.3.

## Collection of GC-associated genes
GC-related genes were systematically collected from three major public databases: (1) the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, http://ctdbase.org/), selecting genes with an inference score >20; (2) GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/), including genes with a relevance score >5; and (3) DisGeNET (http://www.disgenet.org/), a platform for disease–gene association data. Venny 2.1 was used for visualization, and genes present in at least two of the three databases were retained as candidate GC-related genes for downstream analyses.

2.4.

## Identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)
RNA sequencing data (normalized as FPKM-UQ) and miRNA sequencing data from GC samples were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas-Stomach Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-STAD) cohort via the UCSC Xena browser. Raw data were processed and normalized using the "limma" package (v3.56.2) in R software (v4.3.1). Differential expression analysis was conducted between tumor tissues (n = 375) and adjacent normal tissues (n = 32). Differentially expressed mRNAs (DEmRNAs) and lncRNAs (DElncRNAs) were defined as those with |log2FC| > 0.5 and adjusted p-value (Benjamini-Hochberg) < 0.05. Differentially expressed miRNAs (DEmiRNAs) were identified using a stricter threshold of |log2FC| > 1.0 and adjusted p-value <0.05. Identified DEGs were categorized as upregulated or downregulated for downstream analysis.

2.5.

## Intersection analysis of silibinin targets and GC-associated genes
Intersection analysis was performed using Venny 2.1 to identify common genes among predicted silibinin targets, GC-related genes, and DEmRNAs. The platform calculates overlapping gene sets among the three datasets and generates a three-set Venn diagram for visual representation and subsequent mechanistic exploration.

2.6.

## Functional enrichment analysis
Functional enrichment analysis of silibinin target genes was conducted using the “clusterProfiler” package (v4.8.1) in R software (v4.3.1). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses were performed with an adjusted p-value below 0.05 based on the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Overlapping genes were further analyzed with the DAVID platform, applying a p-value cutoff of 0.05. GO annotations were grouped into biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). Disease Ontology (DO) enrichment analysis of silibinin target genes was also conducted using the “clusterProfiler” package, with significantly enriched terms (p < 0.05) mapped to relevant disease associations.

2.7.

## Construction of protein-protein interaction (PPI) network
PPI data for silibinin target genes and intersecting genes were retrieved from the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING, version 12.0, http://www.string-db.org/), with the species limited to Homo sapiens. Only interactions with a combined confidence score >0.4 (medium confidence or higher) were retained for network construction. The resulting PPI network was visualized, annotated, and analyzed in Cytoscape software (v3.9.1). Key nodes were identified based on degree centrality to prioritize potential hub genes for further analysis.

2.8.

## ceRNA network analysis
To construct the ceRNA regulatory network, the interactions between DEmiRNAs and intersecting genes were first predicted using miRWalk 3.0, and binding sites were cross-validated by at least 2 additional prediction tools, including TargetScan and miRDB. Next, DElncRNAs that potentially interact with these DEmiRNAs were identified using the lncBase v2.0 database, with a focus on conserved miRNA response elements (MREs). A pearson correlation analysis between DElncRNAs and intersecting genes was conducted using RNA-seq data from TCGA-STAD. Only pairs with a correlation coefficient (r) > 0.15 and p < 0.05 were retained. Subsequently, negatively correlated DElncRNA-DEmiRNA pairs (r < −0.15, p < 0.05), along with their corresponding DEmiRNA-mRNA interactions, were integrated to assemble the ceRNA network. The final network was visualized in Cytoscape and annotated according to expression patterns and interaction confidence.

2.9.

## Molecular docking analysis of silibinin with core targets
Molecular docking analysis was conducted to assess the interaction strength between silibinin and key proteins from the protein interaction network. The three dimensional structure of silibinin (PubChem CID 31553) was downloaded and energy minimized with the MMFF94 force field in Avogadro. Protein structures were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB, https://www.rcsb.org/). Protein preprocessing was conducted in AutoDockTools (v1.5.6), including removal of water molecules, addition of polar hydrogens, and assignment of Gasteiger charges. A docking grid box of 20 × 20 × 20 Å was centered at the active site of each protein. Docking was performed using AutoDock Vina (v1.2.3) with an exhaustiveness parameter set to 8. The binding affinity was evaluated based on the lowest binding free energy (kcal/mol), where lower scores indicate more stable binding.

2.10.

## Clinical tissue samples
Paired gastric tumor tissues and matched adjacent noncancerous samples were obtained from 23 patients who underwent surgical treatment at Changzheng Hospital in Shanghai, China (Patient ID CH-2216). All participants provided written informed consent before surgery and had not received neoadjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy. The institutional ethics committee approved the study protocol, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent revisions (Ethical Approval Number: 2024SL014).

2.11.

## Cell culture and treatment
Human GC cell lines MKN-45 (SCSP-5473) and HGC-27 (SCSP-5263) were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and authenticated using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. MKN-45 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (11875093, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS; A5669701, FBS; Gibco), 100 IU/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (SV30010, HyClone). HGC-27 cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; 11965092, Gibco, USA) with the same supplements. Cells were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. For experiments, cells in logarithmic growth phase were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates. After overnight adherence, they were treated with silibinin (10, 20, or 40 μM; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. S0417) or vehicle control (0.1 % DMSO, 11965092) for 24, 48, or 72 h. Treated cells were harvested for subsequent functional assays. All cultures were maintained between passages 15 and 25 and were confirmed to be mycoplasma free.

2.12.

## Cell transfection
miR-383-5p mimics, miR-383-5p inhibitors, and small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting HSP90AA1 (si-HSP90AA1#1: 21-nt guide: 5′-ACAUCAACUGGGCAAUUUCUG-3′, passenger: 5′-GAAAUUGCCCAGUUGAUGUCA-3′; si-HSP90AA1#2: guide: 5′-UGAUGAAUUUGAAAUGAGCUC-3′, passenger: 5′-GCUCAUUUCAAAUUCAUCAGA-3′) were synthesized by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). Overexpression plasmids for SNHG1 (oe-SNHG1) and HSP90AA1 (oe-HSP90AA1) were obtained from GeneChem (Shanghai, China).
MKN-45 and HGC-27 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well in complete DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS. After 24 h of adherence, transient transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000001, Invitrogen, UK) following the manufacturer's protocol. Each well received 50 nM of miRNA mimics/inhibitors, 100 nM siRNA, or 2 μg plasmid DNA. Transfected cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 for 48 h to ensure transfection efficiency. For rescue experiments, cells were co-transfected with oe-SNHG1 and/or oe-HSP90AA1, followed by treatment with 40 μM silibinin for 24 h. Transfection efficiency was verified using RT-qPCR for lncRNAs and miRNAs, and Western blot (WB) for protein expression [ ].
Experimental Groups: (1) Overexpression and interference groups: oe-NC, oe-SNHG1, mimic NC, miR-383-5p mimics, inhibitor NC, miR-383-5p inhibitors, si-NC, si-HSP90AA1#1, si-HSP90AA1#2, and oe-HSP90AA1; (2) Silibinin-based groups with SNHG1 overexpression: Silibinin + oe-NC + mimic NC, Silibinin + oe-SNHG1 + mimic NC, and Silibinin + oe-SNHG1 + miR-383-5p mimics; (3) Silibinin-based groups with miR-383-5p inhibition: Silibinin + si-NC + inhibitor NC, Silibinin + miR-383-5p inhibitor + si-NC, and Silibinin + miR-383-5p inhibitor + si-HSP90AA1; (4) Silibinin + oe-NC and Silibinin + oe-HSP90AA1.

2.13.

## Dual-luciferase reporter assay
To investigate the interaction between miR-383-5p and its predicted binding sites located in the 3′ untranslated regions (3′UTRs) of SNHG1 and HSP90AA1, wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) target sequences were cloned into the pmirGLO dual-luciferase reporter vector (Promega, USA). Four constructs were generated: SNHG1-WT, SNHG1-MUT (with mutations at the miR-383-5p binding site), HSP90AA1 3′UTR-WT, and HSP90AA1 3′UTR-MUT. MKN-45 and HGC-27 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and co-transfected with 500 ng of each reporter plasmid and 50 nM miR-383-5p mimic or negative control (NC, RiboBio). After 48 h, luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega), with Renilla luciferase activity used for normalization.

2.14.

## RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay
RNA-protein interactions, particularly those within the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), were evaluated using the Magna RIP kit (Millipore Sigma, Cat. No. 03–110). MKN-45 and HGC-27 cells (2 × 107 cells per sample) were lysed in RIP lysis buffer containing protease and RNase inhibitors. Cell lysates were incubated overnight at 4 °C with magnetic beads coupled to an anti-Ago2 antibody (1:50, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. SAB3900065) or normal mouse IgG (1:100, Millipore Sigma, Cat. No. 12–371) as a negative control. After washing, co-immunoprecipitated RNAs were extracted using TRIzol LS reagent (Invitrogen). The purified RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Cat. No. RR037A), followed by RT-qPCR using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Cat. No. Q711-02) to detect the enrichment of SNHG1, HSP90AA1, and miR-383-5p within the Ago2-containing complex.

2.15.

## Liposome preparation and silibinin encapsulation
A lipid mixture consisting of DOPC (Cat. No. 850375C, Avanti Polar Lipids, USA), cholesterol (Cat. No. C8667, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and DSPE-PEG2000 (Cat. No. 880120P, Avanti Polar Lipids, USA) at a molar ratio of 65:30:5 was dissolved in chloroform. The solvent was evaporated at 50 °C using a rotary evaporator (R-100, Buchi, Switzerland) to form a thin lipid film. The film was hydrated with PBS and sonicated using a VCX130 ultrasonic processor (Sonics, USA) to obtain uniform liposomes. Silibinin (Cat. No. S0417, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added at a drug-to-lipid mass ratio of 1:10, and further sonication was applied to facilitate drug loading, resulting in Lip@Sil.

2.16.

## Fabrication of CLip@Sil
Human GC HGC-27 cells were cultured under standard conditions until reaching the logarithmic growth phase. Cells were harvested after two washes with pre-chilled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and subjected to membrane extraction using a commercial Cell Membrane Protein Extraction Kit (Cat. No. SM005, Invent Biotech, USA), in combination with ultracentrifugation at 100,000×g (Beckman Optima XPN-100, USA). The extracted cell membranes were stored at 4 °C for further use. Membrane protein concentration was quantified using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Cat. No. 23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).
The isolated HGC-27 cell membranes were mixed with Lip@Sil at a membrane protein-to-lipid mass ratio of 1:1. The mixture underwent three cycles of freeze-thawing at −80 °C and was subsequently extruded 21 times through 0.2 μm polycarbonate membranes using a Mini-Extruder (Avanti Polar Lipids, USA) to form the biomimetic nanovesicles, termed CLip@Sil.
Verification of Membrane Protein Integrity: To confirm the preservation of membrane protein profiles, samples from whole cells (Cell), extracted cell membranes, uncoated liposomes (CLip), and CLip@Sil were analyzed. After BCA quantification, 40 μg of protein per sample was loaded onto 10 % SDS-PAGE gels (Cat. No. 4561033, Bio-Rad, USA), followed by membrane transfer. WB was performed using primary antibodies against Pan-cadherin, COXIV, Histone H3, and Na+/K+-ATPase (see antibody details in the WB section). After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL), protein bands were visualized using the ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA).

2.17.

## Immunofluorescence (IF) Co-localization to validate membrane-liposome fusion
To confirm the successful fusion of cell membranes and liposomes, the membrane component of CLip@Sil was labeled with DiR dye (Cat. No. D12731, Thermo Fisher, USA), while the liposomal core was labeled with C6 (Cat. No. C21492, Thermo Fisher, USA). After incubation, labeled vesicles were fixed onto glass slides and stained with DAPI (Cat. No. D1306, Invitrogen, USA) to visualize nuclei. Co-localization of DiR (red) and C6 (green) fluorescence was observed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, TCS SP8, Leica Microsystems, Germany), indicating successful membrane-liposome fusion.

2.18.

## Characterization of nanovesicles
Particle Size and Zeta Potential Measurement: The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of CLip@Sil and Lip@Sil were measured using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) instrument (Zetasizer Nano ZS90, Malvern Instruments, UK). Samples were diluted in PBS to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL and measured at 25 °C. Each formulation was analyzed in triplicate, and the average particle size and zeta potential values were recorded.
Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM): The morphology of CLip@Sil, Lip@Sil, and the extracted cell membrane was observed by TEM. Samples were dropped onto formvar/carbon-coated copper grids, negatively stained with 1 % phosphotungstic acid (pH 7.4), air-dried, and imaged using TEM (HT7700, Hitachi, Japan) to assess vesicle structure and core-shell features.

2.19.

## Encapsulation efficiency and pH-Responsive drug release
The content of silibinin in CLip@Sil and Lip@Sil was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a 1260 Infinity II system (Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with a C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase was methanol and water at a 75:25 ratio, and detection was performed at 288 nm. Encapsulation efficiency was calculated after ultrafiltration (10,000×g, 30 min) by subtracting the amount of free drug from the total drug content. For pH-dependent release analysis, CLip@Sil vesicles were loaded into dialysis bags (MWCO 8000 Da) and incubated in PBS at pH 5.0 or pH 7.4 with continuous shaking at 37 °C. Samples were collected at 2-h intervals, and the amount of silibinin released was measured by HPLC. The release profiles were used to evaluate tumor microenvironment (TME) responsiveness.

2.20.

## Serum stability assay
CLip@Sil nanoparticles were incubated at 37 °C in either PBS or RPMI-1640 medium (11875–093, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10 % FBS (10270–106, Gibco, USA) for up to 72 h. Particle size changes were monitored at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h using a DLS analyzer (Zetasizer Nano ZS90, Malvern Instruments, UK) to evaluate colloidal stability under physiological conditions.

2.21.

## Hemolysis assay
Fresh whole blood was obtained from healthy mice and washed three times with 0.9 % NaCl to prepare a 2 % red blood cell (RBC) suspension. CLip@Sil was added at varying concentrations (6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, 200, 400, and 800 μg/mL). Normal saline (0.9 % NaCl) served as the negative control and distilled water as the positive control. Samples were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 10 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax M5, Molecular Devices, USA) to calculate the hemolysis rate.

2.22.

## Cellular uptake assay
CLip@Sil and Lip@Sil were labeled with the fluorescent dye C6 (Cat. No. C21492, Thermo Fisher, USA) and incubated with MKN-45 and HGC-27 cells for 4 h. After incubation, cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA, Cat. No. 158127, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and stained with DAPI (Cat. No. D1306, Invitrogen, USA). Intracellular green fluorescence was examined using a CLSM (TCS SP8, Leica Microsystems, Germany) to evaluate uptake and localization patterns.
For flow cytometry, MKN-45 and HGC-27 cells were incubated with C6-labeled CLip@Sil or Lip@Sil for 4 h, washed three times with PBS, and collected into single-cell suspensions. Fluorescence intensity was measured on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA) using the FL1 channel, and data were processed with FlowJo software (version 10.8).

2.23.

## Intracellular trafficking assay (lysosomal Co-localization)
HGC-27 cells were seeded onto coverslips and incubated with C6-labeled CLip@Sil for 0.5, 2, 4, and 6 h. During the last 30 min of incubation, LysoTracker™ Red (Cat. No. L12492, Invitrogen, USA) was added to stain endo-/lysosomes. After PBS washing, cells were fixed and counterstained with DAPI. Co-localization of green (CLip@Sil) and red (lysosomes) fluorescence was visualized by CLSM (Leica Microsystems, Germany) to evaluate intracellular trafficking.

2.24.

## Cell proliferation assay (cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8))
MKN-45 and HGC-27 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 3000 cells per well in 100 μL of complete medium and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then cultured for 24, 48, and 72 h. At each time point, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (CK04, Dojindo, Japan) was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using a GloMax® Discover microplate reader (Promega, USA). To evaluate the effects of nanoparticles on cell viability, MKN-45 and HGC-27 cells were treated with blank vesicles (CLip), free silibinin (50 μg/mL), Lip@Sil, or CLip@Sil for 24 h. After treatment, CCK-8 reagent was added, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm to assess cell viability.

2.25.

## Transwell migration and invasion assays
Transwell chambers with 8 μm pore membranes (Corning, USA) were used to assess cell migration and invasion. For invasion assays, the upper chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel diluted 1:8 in serum-free DMEM; migration assays were performed using uncoated inserts. A total of 2 × 104 MKN-45 or HGC-27 cells suspended in serum-free DMEM were seeded into the upper chamber, while the lower chamber was filled with DMEM containing 20 % FBS as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 24 h at 37 °C, non-migrated or non-invaded cells on the upper surface were removed. The migrated/invaded cells on the bottom surface were fixed with 4 % PFA, stained with 0.5 % crystal violet, and imaged under an inverted microscope. Five random fields per insert were selected for cell counting in ImageJ.

2.26.

## RT-qPCR
Total RNA from cells or tissues was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Cat. No. 15596026). RNA purity and concentration were evaluated by the A260/A280 ratio (≥1.8). For mRNA analysis, reverse transcription was carried out using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Cat. No. RR037A). For miRNA, the TaqMan miRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Cat. No. 4366596) was used with 10 ng input RNA. qPCR was conducted using SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme, Cat. No. Q711-02) or TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Cat. No. 4440038) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System. Each 20 μL reaction followed these cycling conditions: SYBR Green: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 34 s; TaqMan: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. GAPDH and U6 snRNA were used as internal references for mRNA and miRNA, respectively. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in . All assays were performed in triplicate.

## WB analysis
Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Cat. No. P0013B, Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Cat. No. P0012S, Beyotime, China). Equal amounts of protein (20–40 μg) were separated by 10 % SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Cat. No. IPVH00010, Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked at ambient temperature for 2 h with 5 % non-fat milk in PBS containing 0.1 % Tween-20 (PBST), followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: Rabbit anti-Pan-cadherin (1:1000, ab51034, Abcam, UK), Rabbit anti-COXIV (1:1000, 4850, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), Rabbit anti-Histone H3 (1:2000, 4499, CST, USA), Rabbit anti-Na+/K+-ATPase (1:1000, 3010, CST, USA), Rabbit anti-HSP90AA1 (1:500, ab314005, Abcam, UK), Rabbit anti-E-cadherin (1:1000, 3195S, CST, USA), Rabbit anti-PI3K (1:500, ab154598, Abcam, UK), Rabbit anti-p-PI3K (1:1000, ab191606, Abcam, UK), Rabbit anti-AKT (1:500, ab8805, Abcam, UK), Rabbit anti-p-AKT (1:500, ab38449, Abcam, UK), Rabbit anti-GAPDH (1:10000, 10494-1-AP, Proteintech, China; used as loading control). After washing with PBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:5000, ab205718, Abcam, UK) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL kit (Cat. No. 32106, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and imaged with a ChemiDoc XRS + system (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (v1.53t) and normalized to GAPDH expression.

## Xenograft tumor model and in vivo silibinin therapy
Male BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old, average weight 15.21 ± 0.32 g) were purchased from Silaike Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions (22 ± 1 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Changzheng Hospital (Ethical Approval Number: 2024SL014, Shanghai, China) and conducted in compliance with the 3Rs principle of animal welfare. Exponentially growing HGC-27 cells were digested with 0.25 % trypsin-EDTA (Cat. No. 25200072, Gibco, USA), resuspended in serum-free DMEM at a concentration of 5 × 106 cells/mL, and mixed 1:1 with Matrigel (Cat. No. 356234, Corning, USA). Each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of 100 μL of the cell suspension (5 × 106 cells) into the right flank using a 27-gauge needle (Cat. No. 305109, BD Biosciences, USA). After two weeks [ ], when the tumor volume reached approximately 50 mm3, mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5/group): Saline, Silibinin (50 mg/kg), Lip@Sil, and CLip@Sil. Treatments were administered via tail vein injection every 3 days (200 μL/mouse) for 28 consecutive days. Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured weekly using calipers, and tumor volumes were calculated as follows: Volume (mm3) = ½ × L × W2.
On day 28, mice were euthanized using CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation. Tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed. Portions of the tumor tissue were fixed in 4 % PFA for histological analysis, while the remaining tissue was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA and protein extraction. Mouse body weights were recorded weekly to assess systemic toxicity. Survival data were collected for 60 days to evaluate therapeutic efficacy [ , ].

## In vivo fluorescence imaging and biodistribution analysis
To assess the in vivo distribution of CLip@Sil, DiR dye (Cat. No. D12731, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to label CLip@Sil and a free DiR control. CLip@Sil and DiR were incubated at a 50:1 mass ratio in the dark for 30 min. Free dye was removed via ultrafiltration (Amicon Ultra-15, Cat. No. UFC901024, Millipore, USA). In the free DiR group, DiR was dissolved in DMSO and diluted to a final concentration of 1 mg/kg.
HGC-27 cells (5 × 106) were resuspended in PBS, and 100 μL of cell suspension was injected subcutaneously into the right dorsal region of BALB/c nude mice. When tumor volumes reached ∼50 mm3, tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 5/group) and intravenously injected with DiR-labeled CLip@Sil or free DiR (2 mg/kg DiR in 100 μL). Whole-body fluorescence images were acquired at 1, 3, 12, and 24 h post-injection using the IVIS Spectrum Imaging System (PerkinElmer, USA). At 24 and 48 h, mice were sacrificed, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) and tumors were collected for in vitro imaging using the same system to evaluate the nanoparticles' biodistribution and tumor accumulation [ ].

## Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining
Following euthanasia, tumors and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested and fixed in 4 % PFA (Cat. No. P6148, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 24 h. Tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50 %, 70 %, 80 %, 95 %, and 100 %; Cat. No. E7023, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), embedded in paraffin using a Leica EG1160 embedding system, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. The sections were stained with H&E to evaluate tissue morphology and identify any treatment-related toxicity.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and TUNEL assay
Paraffin-embedded tumor tissues were used to prepare tissue microarrays. IHC staining was performed following standard protocols. The following primary antibodies were used: Mouse anti-DNMT1 (1:200; Cat. No. sc-271729, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), Mouse anti-HSP90AA1 (1:200; Cat. No. ab79849, Abcam, UK), Rabbit anti-HSP90AB1 (1:200; Cat. No. ab32568, Abcam, UK), Mouse anti-TOP2A (1:200; Cat. No. ab219321, Abcam, UK), Rabbit anti-E-cadherin (1:400; Cat. No. 3195S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), Mouse anti-Ki67 (1:800; Cat. No. 9449, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with biotinylated secondary antibodies (goat anti-mouse/anti-rabbit, Cat. No. BA1050/BA1054, BOSTER, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Signal development was performed using a DAB chromogenic kit (Cat. No. K3468, Agilent, USA), and hematoxylin was used for counterstaining. Slides were dehydrated and mounted for microscopic examination.
Two independent pathologists, blinded to clinical data, scored the staining results. Staining intensity was graded as 0 (none), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), or 3 (strong); the percentage of positive cells was scored as 0 (<5 %), 1 (6–25 %), 2 (26–50 %), 3 (51–75 %), or 4 (>75 %). The final IHC score was calculated by multiplying intensity and proportion scores (range: 0–12). A score ≤4 was defined as negative, while ≥5 was considered positive.
TUNEL assay was conducted using a commercial apoptosis detection kit (Cat. No. 11684817910, Roche, Switzerland) to assess apoptotic cells in tumor sections. All stained sections were visualized using an upright microscope (ECLIPSE Ni, Nikon, Japan) and imaged with a digital pathology scanner (Aperio ScanScope, Leica Biosystems, Germany) for quantitative analysis.

## Lung metastasis model and metastatic analysis
A lung metastasis model was established by injecting 5 × 106 HGC-27 cells into the tail vein of BALB/c nude mice. After 8 weeks, the animals were euthanized and lung tissues were harvested for macroscopic examination. Visible metastatic nodules were counted manually. Lung sections were further examined by H&E staining to characterize histopathological alterations and to assess the anti-metastatic effects of the treatments [ ].

## Statistical analysis
All in vitro experiments were independently performed in triplicate unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0; GraphPad Software, USA). Normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. A two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test was used for pairwise comparisons, whereas multiple group differences were examined by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc analysis. For non-normally distributed or heteroscedastic data, non-parametric tests such as the Mann-Whitney U test or the Kruskal-Wallis test were used. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value <0.05.

## Results
3.1.

## Multi-omics data integration reveals molecular targets and regulatory networks of silibinin in GC
In this study, a comprehensive integration of multiple databases was employed to systematically identify molecular targets and regulatory pathways through which silibinin may exert its anti-tumor effects in GC ( A). A total of 61 potential silibinin targets were identified from BATMAN, DGIdb, TCMSP, and PharmMapper databases ( ). Meanwhile, 3692 GC-associated genes were compiled from CTD, GeneCards, and DisGeNET, retaining only those found in at least two databases ( B– ). Transcriptomic analysis between GC tissues and normal gastric tissues identified 4319 DEmRNAs, 381 DElncRNAs, and 104 DEmiRNAs (|log2FC| > 1, FDR <0.05; C and D, ). By integrating silibinin targets, GC-related genes, and DEmRNAs via a Venn diagram approach, 13 overlapping candidate genes were identified ( E), including HSP90AB1, HSP90AA1, EHMT2, CYP3A4, TOP2A, AKR1C1-3, CNR1, ZBTB16, DNMT1, CASK, and PTPN13. These genes represent high-confidence targets through which silibinin may exert anti-GC effects, primarily involved in epigenetic regulation (e.g., DNMT1, EHMT2) and stress response pathways (e.g., HSP90AA1/AB1).
 GO enrichment analysis of the 61 predicted targets using the DAVID database identified 66 BP, 17 CC, and 56 MF. BP terms were predominantly related to xenobiotic metabolism, heme catabolism, and steroid metabolic processes. CC annotations suggested these targets are mainly localized to the basolateral plasma membrane, cytosol, and cytoplasm. MF terms were enriched in androgen 3-alpha, 17-beta-dehydrogenase activity, bile acid binding, and NAD+-dependent 17-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase activity ( ). Additionally, summarizes the top five enriched KEGG pathways among the 61 genes, including bile secretion, metabolic pathways, steroid hormone biosynthesis, pyruvate metabolism, and retinol metabolism. outlines the top 10 DO terms, such as malignancies, hormone-related disorders, reproductive organ developmental abnormalities, and adverse drug reactions. The 13 overlapping genes (e.g., HSP90AB1, HSP90AA1, DNMT1) were notably enriched in pathways including chemical carcinogenesis-receptor activation, interleukin-17 (IL-17) signaling, and PI3K/AKT signaling ( ). Full enrichment results are provided in .
 Based on the 61 predicted silibinin targets, a PPI network was constructed with a minimum confidence threshold of 0.4 (medium confidence). The resulting network contained 61 nodes and 82 edges, representing putative direct and functional interactions among target proteins ( ). Network analysis showed an average node degree of 2.7 and a clustering coefficient of 0.38, suggesting a moderate level of connectivity among the identified targets. A focused subnetwork was further constructed using the 13 overlapping genes (i.e., the intersection of silibinin targets, GC-related genes, and DEmRNAs), comprising 11 nodes and 15 edges after removal of unconnected proteins ( ). Among these, HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, and DNMT1 showed the highest connectivity (≥4), suggesting their potential roles as central regulators in silibinin-mediated anti-GC mechanisms.
To explore post-transcriptional regulatory interactions, a ceRNA network was constructed by integrating negatively correlated DEmiRNAs, DElncRNAs, and the 13 overlapping genes ( ). miRNA-target predictions were sourced from the miRcode and StarBase databases. The resulting network consisted of 231 nodes (79 DEmiRNAs, 139 DElncRNAs, and 13 overlapping genes) and 1779 edges. Specifically, the network included 950 upregulated DEmiRNA-downregulated DElncRNA pairs, 750 downregulated DEmiRNA-upregulated DElncRNA pairs, and 79 DEmiRNA-mRNA regulatory pairs (including 52 upregulated miRNA-downregulated gene pairs and 27 downregulated miRNA-upregulated gene pairs). Topological analysis using Cytoscape's Edge-weighted Spring Embedded Layout revealed that DNMT1, TOP2A, HSP90AB1, and HSP90AA1 function as key hub genes in the ceRNA regulatory network, each connected by at least 15 edges ( ). These findings are consistent with their well-documented roles in epigenetic regulation (DNMT1) and stress response (HSP90AA1/AB1), supporting their critical involvement in silibinin's anti-GC activity.
Molecular docking analysis using AutoDock Vina revealed that silibinin exhibited strong binding affinities with four key proteins identified from the PPI network: DNMT1, TOP2A, HSP90AB1, and HSP90AA1, with predicted binding energies of −10.5 kcal/mol, −8.4 kcal/mol, −8.3 kcal/mol, and −8.0 kcal/mol, respectively. These results suggest high binding stability between silibinin and the enzymatic active sites of the target proteins. Specifically, silibinin formed hydrogen bonds with MET-696 and ARG-1574 in DNMT1 and engaged in hydrophobic interactions with TRP-1170 and ASN-1578 ( F). In the case of HSP90AA1, hydrogen bonds were formed at ASP-93 and GLY-137, while hydrophobic interactions occurred with ALA-55, ASN-51, and PHE-138 ( G). Similar hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions were observed between silibinin and both HSP90AB1 and TOP2A ( H and I). These molecular interactions further support the hypothesis that silibinin binds stably to the functional domains of these proteins, particularly those involved in epigenetic regulation (e.g., DNMT1) and stress response (e.g., HSP90AA1/AB1). Structurally, these findings provide a mechanistic basis for silibinin's ability to interfere with oncogenic signaling pathways in GC.

3.2.

## Silibinin targets the mir-383-5p/SNHG1/HSP90AA1 ceRNA axis to suppress GC progression
To assess the clinical relevance of the predicted hub genes and the proposed ceRNA axis ( A), IHC analysis was carried out on 23 paired GC and adjacent non-tumor tissues. Protein expression levels of DNMT1, TOP2A, HSP90AA1, and HSP90AB1 were examined. These proteins are functionally associated with epigenetic regulation (DNMT1), modulation of DNA topology (TOP2A), and cellular stress responses (HSP90AA1/AB1). The analysis revealed that all four proteins were markedly elevated in GC tissues relative to adjacent non-tumor samples, with detectable localization in both the nucleus and cytoplasm ( B and C), consistent with their biological functions. Among these, HSP90AA1 exhibited the most pronounced overexpression (p < 0.001), supporting its critical role in GC progression by stabilizing oncogenic proteins, promoting metabolic reprogramming, and facilitating EMT. The elevated expression of HSP90AA1 makes it a promising therapeutic target for silibinin in GC and warrants further mechanistic investigation.
 Pearson correlation analysis identified SNHG1 as the lncRNA most strongly correlated with HSP90AA1 expression in GC, while miR-383-5p emerged as the most likely miRNA targeting SNHG1 ( ). Dual-luciferase assays further verified direct binding between miR-383-5p and the predicted sites within the SNHG1-WT ( D) and HSP90AA1 3′UTR-WT constructs ( E). Co-transfection of miR-383-5p mimics into MKN-45 and HGC-27 cells resulted in a 60–70 % reduction in luciferase activity from both SNHG1-WT and HSP90AA1-WT vectors ( F and G), whereas the MUT showed no such suppression. RIP using an anti-Ago2 antibody demonstrated that SNHG1, miR-383-5p, and HSP90AA1 were significantly enriched within the RISC. Specifically, SNHG1 and miR-383-5p exhibited a 4.5-fold enrichment, while HSP90AA1 showed a 3.8-fold enrichment relative to the IgG control ( H and I), indicating statistically significant interactions (p < 0.001).
To assess the antitumor activity of silibinin, MKN-45 and HGC-27 cells were exposed to 10, 20, and 40 μM of the compound. CCK-8 assays demonstrated a clear dose-dependent suppression of cell proliferation ( ). Consistently, Transwell migration and invasion assays showed a significant reduction in cell motility ( ). Mechanistically, silibinin treatment resulted in a 2.5–4.2-fold upregulation of the tumor-suppressive miR-383-5p, a 60–75 % downregulation of the oncogenic lncRNA SNHG1, and a 55–65 % decrease in HSP90AA1 protein expression ( J).
Collectively, these findings suggest that silibinin suppresses GC progression by modulating the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 ceRNA axis, providing mechanistic insights into its anticancer potential.

3.3.

## Silibinin suppresses GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion via modulation of the SNHG1/mir-383-5p/HSP90AA1 axis
Based on preliminary findings, we selected 40 μM silibinin for subsequent experiments and established two intervention strategies to evaluate the regulatory role of the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 axis in silibinin-mediated suppression of GC cell malignancy ( A). We first verified the transfection efficiency of the overexpression and knockdown constructs in MKN-45 and HGC-27 cells. RT-qPCR analysis showed that oe-SNHG1 markedly increased SNHG1 expression in both cell lines compared with the oe-NC group ( ). Similarly, transfection with miR-383-5p mimics significantly elevated miR-383-5p levels, whereas miR-383-5p inhibitors reduced its expression relative to their corresponding NC groups ( ). WB analysis demonstrated that both si-HSP90AA1#1 and si-HSP90AA1#2 effectively silenced HSP90AA1 protein expression compared to si-NC, with si-HSP90AA1#2 exhibiting superior knockdown efficiency; this construct was therefore used in subsequent experiments. Conversely, oe-HSP90AA1 overexpression significantly elevated HSP90AA1 levels in both cell lines ( ).
 In the first set of experiments, which included the Silibinin + oe-NC + mimic NC, Silibinin + oe-SNHG1 + mimic NC, and Silibinin + oe-SNHG1 + miR-383-5p mimic groups, we examined whether miR-383-5p could mitigate the pro-tumorigenic effects of SNHG1 overexpression. CCK-8 and Transwell assays demonstrated that SNHG1 overexpression significantly increased cell proliferation, migration, and invasion compared with the Silibinin + oe-NC + mimic NC group. Notably, co-transfection with miR-383-5p mimics largely reversed these changes, demonstrating that miR-383-5p counteracts the SNHG1-induced malignant behaviors in GC cells ( B and C; ).
The second set of intervention experiments aimed to investigate whether miR-383-5p regulates the downstream target HSP90AA1. This group included: Silibinin + si-NC + inhibitor NC, Silibinin + miR-383-5p inhibitor + si-NC, and Silibinin + miR-383-5p inhibitor + si-HSP90AA1, along with Silibinin + oe-NC and Silibinin + oe-HSP90AA1 groups to validate the functional role of HSP90AA1. CCK-8 and Transwell assays demonstrated that treatment with Silibinin + miR-383-5p inhibitor + si-NC markedly enhanced the growth, motile behavior, and invasive capability of MKN-45 and HGC-27 cells compared with the Silibinin + si-NC + inhibitor NC group. Notably, knockdown of HSP90AA1 (Silibinin + miR-383-5p inhibitor + si-HSP90AA1) reversed these effects ( D and E; ), indicating that HSP90AA1 acts as a critical downstream mediator of miR-383-5p. In parallel, HSP90AA1 overexpression further confirmed its functional significance. Compared with the Silibinin + oe-NC group, cells in the Silibinin + oe-HSP90AA1 group showed markedly increased proliferative and invasive behavior, supporting the role of HSP90AA1 in promoting malignant phenotypes in GC cells ( F and G; ).
These findings demonstrate that silibinin inhibits cell growth, migratory activity, and invasive potential via modulation of the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 pathway.

3.4.

## Silibinin suppresses the PI3K/AKT pathway via the SNHG1/mir-383-5p/HSP90AA1 axis
Guided by the KEGG enrichment analysis, we investigated the PI3K/AKT pathway, in which the chaperone protein HSP90AA1 serves as an important regulator. To assess how silibinin influences this cascade, we measured HSP90AA1, E-cadherin, and the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and AKT (p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT) in MKN-45 and HGC-27 cells. As shown in , silibinin treatment produced a dose-dependent decrease in HSP90AA1 expression and in the phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT, together with an increase in E-cadherin levels.
Further analysis of the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 axis showed that SNHG1 overexpression (Silibinin + oe-SNHG1 + mimic NC) markedly elevated HSP90AA1 levels, increased the p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT ratios, and reduced E-cadherin expression compared with the Silibinin + oe-NC + mimic NC group. Co-transfection with miR-383-5p mimics (Silibinin + oe-SNHG1 + miR-383-5p mimics) reversed these alterations, leading to reduced HSP90AA1 expression, diminished PI3K and AKT phosphorylation, and restored E-cadherin levels ( A, ). RT-qPCR results further confirmed the ceRNA regulatory interactions. Compared to the Silibinin + oe-NC + mimic NC group, the Silibinin + oe-SNHG1 + mimic NC group exhibited significantly increased SNHG1 expression and decreased miR-383-5p expression. However, miR-383-5p restoration by miR-383-5p mimics (in the Silibinin + oe-SNHG1 + miR-383-5p mimics group) significantly upregulated miR-383-5p expression without altering SNHG1 levels ( B, ).
 Compared with the Silibinin + si-NC + inhibitor NC group, treatment with Silibinin + miR-383-5p inhibitor + si-NC markedly increased HSP90AA1 expression, elevated the p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT ratios, and reduced E-cadherin levels in both MKN-45 and HGC-27 cells. These alterations were reversed in the Silibinin + miR-383-5p inhibitor + si-HSP90AA1 group, where HSP90AA1 knockdown led to decreased HSP90AA1 expression, reduced PI3K and AKT phosphorylation, and restored E-cadherin levels ( C, ). RT-qPCR further showed a pronounced reduction in miR-383-5p expression following Silibinin + miR-383-5p inhibitor + si-NC treatment relative to the Silibinin + si-NC + inhibitor NC group. Importantly, silencing HSP90AA1 (Silibinin + miR-383-5p inhibitor + si-HSP90AA1) did not alter miR-383-5p levels, confirming that miR-383-5p acts upstream of HSP90AA1 ( D, ). Moreover, HSP90AA1 overexpression in the Silibinin + oe-HSP90AA1 group further elevated HSP90AA1 expression, p-PI3K/PI3K, and p-AKT/AKT ratios, while decreasing E-cadherin expression, compared to the Silibinin + oe-NC group ( E, ).
Thus, these findings confirm that silibinin suppresses PI3K/AKT pathway activation in GC cells by modulating the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 regulatory axis, providing mechanistic insight into its antitumor efficacy.

3.5.

## Construction of CLip@Sil
We successfully fabricated a biomimetic nanovesicle drug delivery system, CLip@Sil, by coating Lip@Sil with membrane proteins derived from HGC-27 GC cells. The synthesis process is illustrated in A. After isolating the cell membrane and quantifying membrane protein concentrations using the BCA assay, the purity and integrity of the membrane proteins were validated by SDS-PAGE and WB. Results showed preservation of cell membrane-specific protein profiles in both CLip and CLip@Sil, with strong Pan-cadherin expression and absence of COXIV and Histone H3, indicating successful purification of membrane proteins without mitochondrial or nuclear contamination ( B). IF colocalization demonstrated strong overlap between DiR-labeled cell membrane and C6-labeled Lip@Sil signals, confirming successful membrane coating ( C). DLS analysis revealed average particle sizes of approximately 116 nm for Lip@Sil and 135 nm for CLip@Sil, with Zeta potentials of −4.2 mV and −15.6 mV, respectively, suggesting effective incorporation of negatively charged membrane components ( D and E). TEM showed classical nanovesicle morphology in the cell membrane, with bilayer phospholipid membranes. Both Lip@Sil and CLip@Sil appeared as uniform, spherical particles. Notably, CLip@Sil exhibited a distinct core–shell architecture, further supporting successful membrane coating ( F).
 HPLC analysis demonstrated silibinin encapsulation efficiencies of 83.5 % and 85 % for Lip@Sil and CLip@Sil, respectively ( G). Drug release studies conducted under different pH conditions revealed that silibinin release from CLip@Sil reached 82 % at pH 5.0 within 24 h, while only 45 % was released at pH 7.4, suggesting robust pH-responsive release behavior in the acidic TME ( H).
Together, CLip@Sil—constructed using GC cell membranes—exhibits favorable properties including high membrane fusion efficiency, structural stability, excellent drug-loading capacity, and acid-responsive release, making it a promising candidate for targeted anticancer therapy.

3.6.

## CLip@Sil exhibits favorable in vitro stability, biocompatibility, and targeted cellular uptake
We further evaluated the in vitro performance of the biomimetic nanovesicle CLip@Sil, focusing on its colloidal stability, biosafety, and tumor-targeting cellular uptake. The experimental workflow is outlined in A. Serum stability assays demonstrated that CLip@Sil maintained a consistent particle size over 72 h of incubation in PBS or culture medium containing 10 % serum, indicating excellent colloidal stability ( B). Hemolysis assays showed that the hemolytic rate of CLip@Sil remained below 5 % across a concentration range of 6.25–200 μg/mL, confirming its good hemocompatibility ( C). Furthermore, CCK-8 assays revealed no significant cytotoxicity of the empty nanovesicle CLip on MKN-45 and HGC-27 GC cells ( D), supporting its in vitro biosafety.
 To examine their tumor-targeting uptake and intracellular localization, C6-labeled CLip@Sil and Lip@Sil were incubated with MKN-45 and HGC-27 cells. CLSM demonstrated that both nanovesicles were internalized, but CLip@Sil showed markedly higher green fluorescence intensity and cytoplasmic accumulation than Lip@Sil, suggesting more efficient cellular uptake ( E). Quantitative flow cytometry analysis confirmed significantly higher intracellular fluorescence in the CLip@Sil group compared to Lip@Sil in both cell lines ( F), further validating its superior uptake efficiency.
To investigate intracellular trafficking, we used LysoTracker Red to label endo/lysosomal compartments. CLSM imaging showed that green fluorescence from C6-labeled CLip@Sil appeared in the cytoplasm within 2 h post-treatment. Maximum colocalization with red lysosomal fluorescence was observed at 4 h, followed by a decrease at 6 h, indicating time-dependent endosomal escape and intracellular transport of CLip@Sil ( G).
These findings suggest that CLip@Sil exhibited excellent in vitro stability, biocompatibility, and enhanced tumor cell-targeting uptake efficiency, underscoring its promise as a safe and efficient drug delivery platform.

3.7.

## CLip@Sil suppresses the malignant behavior of GC cells by regulating the SNHG1/mir-383-5p/HSP90AA1 axis
We further evaluated the antitumor efficacy of Lip@Sil and CLip@Sil in MKN-45 and HGC-27 GC cells and investigated their regulatory effects on the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 axis and the PI3K/AKT pathway, as outlined in A. CCK-8 assays revealed that, compared with the PBS control, both Silibinin and Lip@Sil significantly reduced the viability of HGC-27( B) and MKN-45( ) cells. Notably, CLip@Sil led to a further significant reduction in cell viability compared with Lip@Sil. Transwell assays demonstrated that Silibinin and Lip@Sil markedly inhibited cell migration and invasion, while CLip@Sil exhibited the strongest inhibitory effect, resulting in significantly fewer migratory and invasive cells than Lip@Sil ( C, ). RT-qPCR analysis showed that SNHG1 expression was significantly downregulated and miR-383-5p was upregulated in both the Silibinin and Lip@Sil groups relative to the PBS group. These changes were further enhanced in the CLip@Sil group ( D, ). Consistent with these findings, WB demonstrated that HSP90AA1 expression, together with the p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT ratios, was significantly reduced, whereas E-cadherin levels were elevated in the Silibinin and Lip@Sil groups relative to PBS. Again, CLip@Sil exhibited a stronger regulatory effect than Lip@Sil ( E, ).
 Collectively, these findings indicate that CLip@Sil suppresses GC cell growth, motility, and invasive activity by strengthening silibinin-mediated regulation of the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 axis and the PI3K/AKT pathway.

3.8.

## CLip@Sil enhances the antitumor efficacy of silibinin via targeted delivery
To validate the in vivo biodistribution and therapeutic efficacy of CLip@Sil, we established a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model using HGC-27 cells in nude mice ( A). We first established a subcutaneous xenograft model by inoculating HGC-27 cells into nude mice, and performed in vivo biodistribution imaging using CLip@Sil labeled with the near-infrared dye DiR. Following intravenous administration, fluorescence images were acquired at 1, 3, 12, and 24 h using the IVIS imaging system. As shown in B and C, compared with the free DiR group, CLip@Sil showed gradual accumulation at the tumor site beginning at 3 h post-injection, with strong fluorescence signals persisting up to 24 h. Meanwhile, liver fluorescence gradually declined over time. In contrast, free DiR predominantly accumulated in the liver and spleen, indicating that these organs are the major sites of nonspecific DiR distribution. These results suggest that CLip@Sil possesses enhanced tumor-targeting capability in vivo. By 48 h, the fluorescence signal had markedly diminished, indicating efficient nanoparticle clearance without unwanted in vivo accumulation ( C).
 Next, tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into four groups (Saline, Silibinin (50 mg/kg), Lip@Sil, and CLip@Sil), and treated via tail vein injection every 3 days for 28 days. Tumor volumes were monitored weekly. Results showed that all treatment groups significantly suppressed tumor growth compared to the saline control, with CLip@Sil achieving the most pronounced tumor reduction compared to both Silibinin and Lip@Sil ( D). At the endpoint, tumor weight measurements were consistent with the volume data, confirming CLip@Sil's superior antitumor efficacy ( E and F). Importantly, no significant body-weight alterations were detected among the treatment groups, suggesting favorable systemic biocompatibility of the nanoformulation ( G). IHC staining revealed that Ki67 expression (a proliferation marker) was significantly decreased in all treatment groups compared to Saline, and was lowest in the CLip@Sil group. TUNEL staining further confirmed increased apoptosis in tumors treated with CLip@Sil ( H).
H&E staining showed enhanced necrosis in tumor tissues across all treatment groups, with CLip@Sil inducing the most extensive necrosis, while no apparent pathological damage was observed in major organs ( ). Additionally, Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated a significant prolongation of median survival in all treatment groups compared with Saline, with CLip@Sil again providing the greatest survival benefit ( I). These results suggest that CLip@Sil exhibits favorable antitumor efficacy and safety in vivo.
To further elucidate the molecular mechanism, tumor tissues were harvested for RT-qPCR and WB analyses. Relative to the Saline group, SNHG1 expression was markedly decreased and miR-383-5p was significantly increased in all treatment groups, with the most pronounced alterations observed in the CLip@Sil group ( ). At the protein level, both p-PI3K/PI3K and p-AKT/AKT ratios were substantially decreased in all treatment groups relative to Saline, with CLip@Sil producing the strongest suppression ( ). IHC staining further demonstrated that HSP90AA1 expression was downregulated, and E-cadherin expression was upregulated in all treatment groups compared with the Saline group, with the CLip@Sil group again showing more substantial changes ( ). These results indicate that CLip@Sil suppresses PI3K/AKT signaling by regulating the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 axis.
Additionally, to investigate the anti-metastatic potential of CLip@Sil, a lung metastasis model was established via tail vein injection of HGC-27 cells. Macroscopic evaluation showed a marked reduction in metastatic lung nodules in all treatment groups compared with the PBS control, with CLip@Sil producing the most pronounced decrease ( ). H&E staining corroborated these findings, indicating that CLip@Sil has a stronger inhibitory effect on GC metastasis ( ).
Collectively, these results demonstrate that CLip@Sil not only exhibits excellent tumor-targeting and therapeutic efficacy but also precisely interferes with the PI3K/AKT signaling by regulating the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 axis, thereby effectively suppressing GC progression and metastasis in vivo.

4.

## Discussion
Silibinin, a natural flavonoid, has shown notable antitumor activity in multiple solid malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma, lung cancer, and breast cancer [ , , ]. It exerts multi-targeted therapeutic effects by modulating diverse signaling pathways, arresting the cell cycle, and inducing apoptosis [ , , ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that silibinin suppresses GC cell proliferation and migration via pathways such as STAT3 and p38MAPK [ , ]. However, a comprehensive understanding of its multitarget regulatory network remains lacking, particularly with regard to the involvement of non-coding RNAs and the coordinated modulation of upstream and downstream signaling axes.
By integrating data from databases such as TCMSP and PharmMapper with TCGA transcriptomic profiles, we identified 13 candidate genes associated with silibinin's antitumor activity in GC. PPI network analysis further refined this to 11 core targets, highlighting DNMT1, TOP2A, HSP90AB1, and HSP90AA1 as key regulatory nodes. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses revealed that these targets are primarily involved in cell cycle regulation, chemical carcinogenesis, IL-17 signaling, and the PI3K-AKT pathway, underscoring their functional relevance in GC pathogenesis. Among them, HSP90AA1 showed the highest overexpression in GC tissues and is known to be essential for protein stability and signal transduction. Molecular docking also revealed a strong interaction between silibinin and HSP90AA1 (approximately −8 kcal/mol). Additionally, TOP2A and DNMT1 were identified as functionally significant in cell proliferation and epigenetic regulation, making them attractive targets for further investigation.
HSP90AA1, a key member of the heat shock protein family, functions as a molecular chaperone essential for maintaining the stability of various oncogenic proteins. Its overexpression has been closely associated with poor prognosis in GC patients [ , ]. In this study, we found that silibinin downregulated HSP90AA1 expression in a dose-dependent manner, as demonstrated by both WB and IHC analyses. Given its role in promoting GC cell migration, invasion, and potentially affecting treatment response, HSP90AA1 represents a pivotal oncogenic factor in GC. Molecular docking analysis confirmed strong interaction between silibinin and HSP90AA1, suggesting that HSP90AA1 may serve as a direct molecular target of silibinin. Additionally, we demonstrated that HSP90AA1 is regulated by the SNHG1/miR-383-5p ceRNA axis, positioning it as a downstream effector within this regulatory network. This indirect regulation via non-coding RNAs offers novel insights into the biological role of HSP90AA1 and expands our understanding of its mechanistic contribution to GC.
In recent years, the ceRNA regulatory mechanism has garnered significant attention in cancer research, revealing the intricate crosstalk between lncRNAs and miRNAs [ , , ]. In the present study, we identified a functional ceRNA axis wherein SNHG1 competitively binds to miR-383-5p to regulate the expression of HSP90AA1. This interaction was experimentally validated through dual-luciferase reporter and RIP assays, confirming the direct targeting relationships among the three molecules. Upon silibinin treatment, SNHG1 and HSP90AA1 levels were significantly downregulated, whereas miR-383-5p was markedly upregulated, suggesting that silibinin exerts its effect at least in part through modulation of this ceRNA network. Previous studies have characterized SNHG1 as an oncogenic lncRNA in various cancers, while miR-383-5p has been widely reported to function as a tumor suppressor [ ]. By regulating this axis, silibinin indirectly inhibits the PI3K/AKT pathway, reflecting its capacity for multi-tiered modulation of tumor signaling. This regulatory mechanism may also be relevant across multiple cancer types, underscoring its potential as a broadly applicable therapeutic target.
The PI3K–AKT pathway is a key regulator of cell growth, motility, survival, and metabolism, and represents one of the most critical oncogenic cascades in GC [ , , ]. Silibinin suppresses this pathway by modulating the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 axis, leading to reduced HSP90AA1 expression and a marked decrease in AKT phosphorylation. SNHG1 primarily regulates signaling networks through miRNA-mediated ceRNA interactions rather than direct physical association with PI3K or AKT [ ]. HSP90AA1, functioning as a molecular chaperone, binds AKT to prevent its degradation and maintain its conformational stability, thereby supporting sustained phosphorylation and pathway activation [ , ]. This regulatory pattern indicates that the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 axis modulates PI3K-AKT activity indirectly. Consistent with this mechanism, silencing SNHG1 or HSP90AA1 results in significant reductions in p-PI3K and p-AKT levels. In vivo data further corroborate this signaling modulation, as CLip@Sil treatment markedly suppresses tumor growth, reduces pulmonary metastasis, and prolongs survival. Unlike direct PI3K or AKT inhibitors, silibinin achieves pathway inactivation by targeting an upstream non-coding RNA axis, suggesting higher selectivity and potentially lower systemic toxicity, and providing a distinct mechanistic paradigm for natural-compound-based–based modulation of oncogenic signaling networks.
The CLip@Sil system developed in this study utilizes HGC-27 cell membrane-coated liposomes to construct stable and uniform biomimetic nanovesicles, significantly enhancing the delivery efficiency and tumor-targeting capacity of silibinin. This system features a favorable size distribution and pH-responsive drug release behavior, enabling efficient drug release in the mildly acidic TME. TEM and DLS confirmed its physical stability, while C6-labeling and confocal microscopy demonstrated efficient cellular uptake and lysosomal escape. In vivo imaging showed tumor-specific accumulation of CLip@Sil with no observable toxicity. Animal studies revealed that CLip@Sil markedly enhanced the anti-tumor effect, reduced metastasis, promoted necrosis, and extended survival. Compared to free drug administration, this biomimetic nanoplatform offers advantages in both bioavailability and safety, establishing it as a promising strategy for the delivery of natural anticancer agents.
This study integrates network pharmacology, differential expression omics, and molecular experiments to establish a closed-loop approach from prediction to validation in mechanism exploration. By combining multiple databases, we improved the precision of target identification and avoided the limitations of conventional trial-and-error experimental strategies. The use of PPI network analysis and functional enrichment enabled effective localization of core targets, while molecular docking and mechanistic validation further enhanced the reliability of the predictions. This systematic and integrative approach is highly adaptable to the investigation of complex mechanisms underlying natural compounds and provides a valuable framework for guiding future experimental designs. Moving forward, this strategy could be extended to explore traditional Chinese medicine formulas, multi-target drugs, and combination therapies, thereby advancing the modernization of traditional medicine research.
In summary, this study demonstrates that silibinin inhibits GC progression by modulating the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 axis, thereby suppressing PI3K–AKT signaling. The biomimetic nanoplatform CLip@Sil significantly enhances silibinin delivery efficiency and therapeutic efficacy, demonstrating excellent tumor-targeting capability and biocompatibility. This study connects mechanistic discovery with technological development, demonstrating the value of integrating natural products into nanomedicine. It also highlights the pivotal contribution of the ceRNA network to silibinin's anticancer activity. Despite the promising findings, some limitations remain, including the lack of clinical sample validation, unassessed tissue specificity and inter-patient heterogeneity, and unclear pharmacokinetic characteristics. Future studies should incorporate clinical specimens, large animal models, and combination immunotherapies to facilitate clinical translation.

5.

## Conclusion
This study innovatively developed a biomimetic nanovesicle system (CLip@Sil) derived from GC cell membranes for the targeted delivery of the natural compound silibinin. It systematically elucidated the mechanism by which silibinin suppresses malignant phenotypes of GC by modulating the SNHG1/miR-383-5p/HSP90AA1 axis and inhibiting the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. CLip@Sil not only enhanced tumor-specific accumulation and intracellular uptake of the drug, but also significantly inhibited tumor growth and metastasis in both cellular and animal models, demonstrating excellent biocompatibility and therapeutic efficacy. The system exhibited strong targeting capability and precise regulatory effects.
The scientific significance of this study lies in its identification of a novel antitumor mechanism of silibinin through ceRNA network modulation, thereby expanding the molecular foundation of natural compound-based therapies for GC. Moreover, the study proposes a membrane protein-modified drug delivery strategy, offering a new direction for the design of biomimetic nanocarriers. From a clinical standpoint, CLip@Sil offers a promising approach to improve the efficacy and safety of silibinin, highlighting its strong potential for translation.
Future research should involve multi-center validation with clinical samples to investigate the mechanistic and therapeutic variations of silibinin across different GC subtypes. Additionally, further exploration of ceRNA regulatory networks may uncover more synergistic targets, paving the way for multi-targeted combination therapies and advancing the development of silibinin and its derivatives toward precision treatment of GC.
