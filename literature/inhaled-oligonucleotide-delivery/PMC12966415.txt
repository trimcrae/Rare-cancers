## ABSTRACT
Tissue‐nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNAP) has emerged as a crucial regulator of neuronal circuit formation and maintenance; however, the complexities of its sex‐ and cell type‐specific roles within microglia remain largely unexplored. To address this critical knowledge gap, this study examined how TNAP deficiency differentially affects microglial morphology, function, and signaling in both male and female mice, and investigated its broader implications for neurodevelopment and disease susceptibility. Using Alpl

+/+
 (wild‐type) and Alpl

−/−
 (TNAP knockout) mice, we conducted behavioral assessments at postnatal Days 13–14 to evaluate early neurobehavioral outcomes. Microglia were subsequently isolated for molecular, metabolic, and morphological analyses. TNAP‐deficient mice of both sexes exhibited profound physiological deficits, including stunted growth and significant sensorimotor impairments, confirming effective TNAP knockout and indicating that systemic TNAP loss affects multiple cell types beyond microglia. At the cellular level, TNAP loss induced notable morphological changes in microglia, characterized by enlarged cell soma and shortened processes, hallmarks of microglial activation. Molecular profiling revealed upregulation of neuroinflammatory and phagocytic markers, implicating TNAP as a modulator of the innate immune response. Furthermore, metabolic analyses uncovered a dramatic shift in tryptophan‐kynurenine metabolism, with increased quinolinic acid production signifying a transition to a neurotoxic, pro‐inflammatory state. Additionally, TNAP‐deficient microglia displayed extensive dysregulation in purinergic signaling pathways, exemplified by increased expression of key purinergic receptors, and acquired a senescent phenotype evidenced by elevated canonical senescence gene expression. Given the influence of TNAP deficiency on multiple cell populations, some observed microglial phenotypes may result from altered intercellular signaling or indirect effects. To delineate cell‐autonomous effects, siRNA‐mediated TNAP knockdown was performed in primary microglia isolated from wild‐type (WT) mice. TNAP depletion modulated inflammatory responses, suggesting an intrinsic role for TNAP in microglial regulation; however, these effects may not fully recapitulate the extent of deficiency observed in vivo. Overall, TNAP emerges as a key modulator of microglial structure and function, with its dysfunction potentially increasing susceptibility to neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders. This highlights the potential of TNAP as a therapeutic target for central nervous system health and disease.

 

Using male and female tissue‐nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNAP) knockout (KO) and wild type (WT) mice, we show TNAP loss impairs growth and sensorimotor function and induces marked microglial morphological changes (enlarged soma, retracted processes). TNAP‐deficient microglia upregulate neuroinflammatory, phagocytic, and senescence markers; shift kynurenine metabolism toward neurotoxicity; and exhibit dysregulated purinergic signaling. These findings reveal TNAP as a key regulator of microglial structure, metabolism, and homeostasis, with broad implications for central nervous system (CNS) health and disease.

2.2

## The Estimation of Animal Numbers, Randomization and Blinding
Power and sample size estimations were performed using “PS: power and sample size calculation” software (version 3.1.6; Department of Biostatistics, Vanderbilt University, USA) as documented in our earlier publications (Hamood et al.  ). Power calculation was performed with significance level (α) of 0.05 (two‐tailed), power of the study (1–β) of 80%, and variances (δ, σ, and m) estimated from our preliminary data. All procedures adhered to the principles of Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement (the 3Rs) to minimize unnecessary use of experimental animals. Male and female WT and KO mice were allocated to designated experimental groups without the use of randomization procedures. Laboratory personnel were blinded to the experimental group assignments.

2.3

## General Physiological Assessment
Although TNAP KO mice were born with normal weight, most of the TNAP KO mice became progressively sickened and died within 2 weeks after birth (Tasevski et al.  ). Thus, our analysis covered early postnatal development. The general physiological conditions of mouse pups were monitored daily, and the body weight and body length (from nose‐to‐rump) of the pups were measured on postnatal Day 13–14 (P13–14).

2.4

## Behavioral Tests
All behavioral tests were performed at the age of P13‐14 in WT (n = 52; 26M/26F) and KO (n = 40; 20M/20F) mice. These tests were completed between 8 am and 12 pm. The mice were habituated in the test room for at least 30 min before the behavioral tests. The lab personnel were blinded to the experimental groups. The same animal underwent all tests in the following order: body weight and body length measurement, righting reflex, grip strength, and open field. There was a 20–40 min break after each test for the animal to rest and recover from the previous test.
2.4.1

## Righting Reflex
The righting reflex was used to assess the motor ability for a mouse to return to a prone position from a supine position (Tasevski et al.  ). The mouse was placed on its back on a bench pad, held in position for 5 s, and then released. A stopwatch was used to record the time when the mouse returned to the prone position or the mouse was removed when the criterion time of 30 s was reached.

2.4.2

## Grip Strength
Muscular strength was evaluated with a grip strength test using a grip strength meter (catalog number: BIO‐GS4; BIOSEB, FL, USA) as previously described (Ghannam et al.  ). In brief, the grip strength meter was positioned horizontally, and the animals were held by the tail and lowered toward the apparatus. The animals were allowed to grab the metal grid and were then pulled backwards in the horizontal plane. The force applied to the grid was recorded as the peak tension. Each animal underwent the grip strength test in three consecutive trials. The results were recorded and averaged for each animal.

2.4.3

## Open Field
The open field test was performed as previously published (Hamood et al.  ; Davila‐Valencia et al.  ; Ghannam et al.  ; Tasevski et al.  ). The open field arena was a large rectangular transparent Plexiglas arena (40 × 25 × 20 cm) with the center region size of 20 × 10 cm2. Each mouse was placed in the center of the arena and allowed to freely explore the arena for a duration of 5 min. Upon completion of the test, the mouse was returned to the home cage. The videos were analyzed using ToxTrac software according to the manufacturer's instructions (Tasevski et al.  ). The total distance traveled, average mobile speed, mobility rate and exploration rate were recorded for each animal, averaged, and compared between WT and KO groups.

2.5

## Isolation of Primary Microglia
Microglial isolation was performed as previously described (Brewer and Torricelli  ; Ghannam et al.  ; Fan et al.  ). In brief, animals were euthanized after completion of behavioral tests. Euthanasia was conducted by administering 5% Fluriso (Isoflurane, USP) (catalog number: 502017, VetOne, MWI Animal Health, ID, USA) via inhalation until respiratory arrest occurred, followed by decapitation to ensure death. Brains were harvested and rinsed in HBSS (catalog number: 14175095, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) on ice. Meninges were removed and the neocortex tissues were micro‐dissected as previously described (Hamood et al.  ). Brain tissues were transferred to HABG solutions (60 mL Hibernate A [catalog number: HA, BrainBits LLC, IL, USA], 1.2 mL B27 [catalog number: 17504044, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA], 0.176 mL L‐Glutamine [200 mM] [catalog number: A2916801, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA]), and minced (~0.5 mm) on ice. Brain tissues were incubated in HABG solution for 8 min at 30°C in a Boekel shaking incubator with a shaking speed of 90 rpm (Cole‐Parmer, Vernon Hills, IL, USA). Tissues were transferred to papain solutions (12 mg papain [catalog number: LS003119, Worthington Biochemical Corp. NJ, USA] per 6 mL HA minus calcium [catalog number: HA‐Ca; BrainBits LLC, IL, USA], 0.015 mL L‐Glutamine [200 mM]), and incubated for 30 min at 30°C in a Boekel Scientific Incubator Shaker II (catalog number: 136400; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) with a shaking speed of 90 rpm. Tissues were washed in HABG solution for 5 min at room temperature, triturated with a sterile pipette for 45 s, and sat at room temperature for 1 min. The supernatants were collected, and the trituration was repeated two times. The supernatants were combined and centrifuged in OptiPrep Density Gradient Medium (catalog number: D1556, MilliporeSigma, MA, USA) at 800×g for 15 min at 22°C, and the fractions of microglia were collected as previously described (Brewer and Torricelli  ; Ghannam et al.  ). Cells were washed in HABG solutions and centrifuged at 200×g for 2 min at 22°C. Supernatants were removed, and cells were washed in HBSS solution and centrifuged at 200×g for 2 min at 22°C. Cell pellets were harvested for RNA isolation and ELISA evaluation.

2.6

## 
RNA Isolation and Quantitative Real‐Time‐PCR (qPCR)
Total RNA was extracted from the isolated microglia [WT = 12 (6M/6F); KO = 12 (6 M/6F)] using TRIZOL (catalog number: 15596018; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA samples were quantified using a Nanodrop ND‐2000 Spectrophotometer (RRID:SCR_018042; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Single‐stranded complementary DNA (cDNA) was reverse transcribed from RNA using the High‐Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit with RNase inhibitor (catalog number: 4374966; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). qPCR was performed with iTaq(tm) Universal SYBR(R) Green Supermix (catalog number: 1725125; Bio‐Rad, CA, USA) and a CFX connect real‐time PCR detection system (RRID:SCR_026760; Bio‐Rad, CA, USA). Amplification conditions included 30 s at 95°C, 40 cycles at 95°C for 5 s, and 60°C for 30 s. Primers were custom designed (Table  ) and ordered from Integrated DNA Technology (RRID:SCR_025813; Coralville, IA, USA). The comparative threshold cycle (Ct) method was used to assess differential gene expressions. The WT group was the control reference group, and glyceraldehyde 3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was the housekeeping gene. Gene expression levels for each sample were normalized to the expression level of GAPDH within a given sample (ΔCt); the differences between the reference group and experimental group were used to determine the ΔΔCt. The 2−ΔΔCt gave the relative fold changes in gene expression.
 
2.7

## Immunohistochemistry
WT mice (n = 10, 5 M/5F) and KO mice (n = 10, 5M/5F) were euthanized following completion of behavioral testing. Euthanasia was performed by administering 5% Fluriso (Isoflurane, USP) (catalog number: 502017, VetOne, MWI Animal Health, ID, USA) via inhalation until cessation of respiration was observed, followed by transcardial perfusion with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) (catalog number: BP24384; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) to ensure death. Brains were harvested and fixed in 10% formalin (catalog number: SF98‐4; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) for 48 h. Samples were cryoprotected with 30% sucrose (catalog number: S8501; MilliporeSigma, MA, USA) in PBS. Coronal sections (20 μm) were cut using a cryostat (RRID:SCR_025772; Leica Microsystems, IL, USA). The sections (1:6 series) were incubated with rabbit anti‐IBA1 (Ionized calcium‐binding adapter molecule 1) (RRID:AB_839504; 1:250; Cat# 019‐19741; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, VA, USA) and rat anti‐CD68 (cluster of Differentiation 68) antibody (RRID:AB_2572857; 1:250; Cat# 14068182; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) overnight at 4°C. Sections were subsequently washed and incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies (RRID: AB_2535794; RRID: AB_141637; 1:250; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) for 2 h at room temperature. Slides were dried and cover‐slipped with mounting media with DAPI (catalog number: F6057; MilliporeSigma, MA, USA). Images were acquired using a Nikon Eclipse TS2R fluorescent microscope (RRID:SCR_027146; Nikon, NY, USA).
For microglial morphology analysis, the brain sections (1:6 series) were treated with hydrogen peroxide (catalog number: H1009; MilliporeSigma, MA, USA) for 10 min at room temperature to inactivate endogenous peroxidase enzymes. After washing, slices were incubated with rabbit anti‐IBA1 (RRID: AB_839504; 1:250; catalog number: 019‐19741; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, VA, USA) overnight at 4°C. Sections were subsequently washed and incubated with biotinylated goat anti‐rabbit IgG antibody (H + L) (RRID: AB_2313606; 1:250; catalog number: BA‐1000‐1.5; Vector Laboratories, CA, USA) for 4 h at room temperature. After washing, sections were incubated with VECTASTAIN ABC‐AP Kit (RRID: AB_2336801; catalog number: AK‐5200; Vector Laboratories, CA, USA) for 1.5 h at room temperature and developed using DAB Substrate Kit (3,3′‐diaminobenzidine) (RRID: AB_2336382; catalog number: SK‐4100; Vector Laboratories, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Slides were dried and cover‐slipped with mounting media (catalog number: F6057; MilliporeSigma, MA, USA). Images were acquired using a Nikon Eclipse TS2R fluorescent microscope (RRID:SCR_027146; Nikon, NY, USA).

2.8

## Cell Morphological Analysis
All slides were coded and analyzed by personnel blinded to the experimental groups. Studies have shown brain region‐specific microglial morphology and function (Grabert et al.  ; De Biase et al.  ; Ayata et al.  ); therefore, we randomly selected microglial cells from layer 2–3 primary motor cortex (Bregma 1.5–0 mm) in an unbiased fashion from WT mice (n = 10, 5M/5F) and KO mice (n = 10, 5 M/5F). IBA1 was used to label the soma and processes of microglial cells. Images of microglial cells (60×, 5 cells/animal) were randomly acquired, and the camera settings were kept the same among all the images. The cells that met the following criteria were analyzed: (1) the cell body and processes completely contained within the image; (2) cells sufficiently stained to allow for tracing processes. The soma size and the length of processes were evaluated using ImageJ software (RRID:SCR_003070; RRID:SCR_002074; ImageJ, National Institutes of Health, NIH, USA) as previously described (Zhang et al.  ). In brief, the area of the soma was traced using Fiji ImageJ software with boundary contour tracings and converted into “μm2” based on the parameter settings of the original images. The total process length of microglial cells was traced with “NeuronJ” (RRID:SCR_002074; Fiji ImageJ software, NIH, USA). In brief, the image projections were converted to 8 bits. The microglial processes were traced and analyzed with NeuronJ according to the software manual (Zhang et al.  ).

2.9

## Co‐Localization Analysis
The co‐localization of CD68+/IBA1+ cells was analyzed using the “Coloc 2” function in Fiji ImageJ (RRID:SCR_003070; National Institutes of Health, NIH) following the manufacturer's instructions (Davila‐Valencia et al.  ). In brief, images were split into separate channels and were converted to 8‐bit. Background subtraction was performed using Fiji ImageJ following the manufacturer's instructions. The co‐localization was analyzed using Kendall's Tau Rank Correlation test, and Costes' randomization was set at 10. Kendall's Tau Rank Correlation test quantifies the spatial correlation between two image channels by assessing the agreement in the ranking of their pixel intensities. Specifically, it measures how consistently the relative order of intensities in one channel corresponds to that in the other. A value of 1 indicates perfect concordance, while a value of −1 signifies perfect discordance. To ensure the accuracy of analysis, data were manually checked with co‐localization analysis as described previously (Zhang et al.  ; Zhang et al.  ; Hamood et al.  ; Faulkner et al.  ).

2.10

## Quinolinic Acid (QA) ELISA

Isolated microglia [WT = 12 (6M/6F); KO = 12 (6M/6F)] were rinsed in ice‐cold PBS (pH 7.4) and weighed before homogenization. Because the protein yield from a single mouse was low, microglia from two mice were pooled for protein extraction and ELISA measurement. Cells were homogenized in PBS on ice with a Bel‐Art ProCulture cordless homogenizer (catalog number: 17‐000‐201, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) and subjected to three freeze–thaw cycles. The homogenates were centrifuged for 10 min at 1500×g and the supernatants were collected. Protein concentration was measured using the Bradford method with Bio‐Rad Protein Assay Dye (catalog number: 5000006, BioRad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. The ELISA was performed using the QA ELISA kit (catalog number: MBS8807108; MyBioSource Inc.; CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, 50 μL standards, blanks and samples were added into the appropriate wells, respectively. 50 μL of Biotinylated‐Conjugate (1×) was added to each well, mixed well and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. After washing, 100 μL of Streptavidin‐HRP (1×) was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. After washing, 90 μL of Substrate Solution was added to each well and incubated for 20 min at 37°C in the dark. 50 μL of Stop Solution was added to each well and measured at 450 nm on a plate reader.

2.11

## Microglial Phagocytosis Assay
Microglial cells were isolated from male and female WT (n = 6, 3M/3F) and KO (n = 6, 3M/3F) mice. The phagocytosis assay was modified from published protocols (Lian et al.  ). Briefly, microglia were seeded at a density of 50 000 cells/cm2 and cultured for 48 h in a humidified incubator maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2. The culture media, consisting of DMEM (catalog number: 11995065; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; catalog number: A5256801; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), was replaced with DMEM containing 0.01% fluorescent latex beads (catalog number: L1030; MilliporeSigma, MA, USA) and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. Following incubation, the microglia were washed thoroughly and fixed with 10% formalin (catalog number: SF98‐4; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) for 15 min. Subsequently, the microglia were incubated overnight at 4°C with rabbit anti‐IBA1 antibody (RRID: AB_839504; dilution 1:250; catalog number: 019‐19741; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, VA, USA). After washing, microglia were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibody (RRID: AB_141637; dilution 1:250; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) for 2 h at room temperature. Slides were mounted using mounting media containing DAPI (catalog number: F6057; MilliporeSigma, MA, USA). Fluorescent images were acquired at 40× magnification using a Nikon Eclipse TS2R microscope (RRID: SCR_027146; Nikon, NY, USA). The co‐localization between beads and IBA1 was quantitatively assessed using Kendall's Tau rank correlation coefficient, as outlined in the preceding methodology section.

2.12

## 
siRNA Transfection in Primary Microglia Isolated From WT Mice
Microglial cells were isolated from WT male and female mice (n = 12, 6 M/6F). For each sex, microglia from two individual mice were combined to generate a single biological sample, resulting in three pooled samples per sex. These samples were then randomly assigned to one of three experimental groups: (1) scrambled RNA control (Scr), (2) siRNA treatment, or (3) transfection efficiency control. This allocation yielded three biological replicates per sex per experimental condition, with each replicate comprising microglial cells pooled from two distinct animals.
In brief, isolated primary microglial cells (50 000 cells per well) were seeded into 24‐well plates. Cells were maintained in DMEM (catalog number: 11995065; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (catalog number: A5256801; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), 50 ng/mL mouse GM‐CSF recombinant protein (catalog number: PIRMGMCSF20; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), and 100 ng/mL mouse M‐CSF recombinant protein (catalog number: 315‐02‐50UG; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Cultures were incubated until reaching 60%–80% confluence prior to transfection. A predesigned Alpl TriFECTa dicer‐substrate siRNA (DsiRNA) kit was ordered from IDT (catalog number: mm.Ri.Alpl.13; Integrated DNA Technologies Inc. Iowa, USA). The kit consisted of three target‐specific siRNA duplexes, a negative control (DS NC1), a TYETM 563 DS transfection efficiency control, and a HPRT‐S1 DS positive duplex control. The nucleotide sequences for each siRNA duplex are detailed below: mm.Ri.Alpl.13.1: 5′‐CAGUAUGAAUUGAAUCGGAACAACC‐3′; mm.Ri.Alpl.13.2: 5′‐CAAUGGUAGAUUACGCUCACAACAA‐3′; mm.Ri.Alpl.13.3: 5′‐GGAACAGAAGUUCGCUAUCUGCCTT‐3′.
Equal volumes of mm.Ri.Alpl.13.1, mm.Ri.Alpl.13.2, and mm.Ri.Alpl.13.3 (each at a final concentration of 10 μM) were combined to generate a pooled siRNA mixture. siRNA (10 μM), scrambled RNA (10 μM), TYETM 563 (10 μM), and Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (catalog number 13778030; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) were each diluted separately in Opti‐MEM I Reduced Serum Medium (catalog number 11058021; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) following the manufacturer's recommended protocols. The diluted siRNA, scrambled RNA, or TYETM 563 solutions were combined with the diluted Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent at a 1:1 ratio. The mixtures were gently mixed and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The resulting complexes were added to microglial cells according to experimental group assignment. Cells were then incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 for 24 h. The culture media were replaced at 24 h post‐transfection, and cells were subsequently maintained for an additional 48 h. Total RNA was then extracted, and target gene expression levels were quantified by qPCR as previously described. Transfection efficiency (%) was calculated as the proportion of TYETM 563‐positive cells relative to the total number of cells, multiplied by 100.

2.13

## Statistical Analysis
All statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 6 (RRID:SCR_002798; Version 6.04; CA, USA). All values were expressed as mean ± SEM. The D'Agostino‐Pearson omnibus test and the Shapiro–Wilk test were used to assess normality. Student's t‐test (two‐tailed) or Mann–Whitney U test (two‐tailed) was used for two‐group comparisons. One‐way or two‐way ANOVA and Bonferroni multiple comparison tests were used for multiple group comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses. No outlier tests were performed, and all data points were retained for analysis.

## Results
3.1

## 
TNAP Deficiency Caused Failure to Thrive and Reduced Muscle Strength
Developmental delays were observed in the TNAP KO mice, which are consistent with our previous reports (Zhang et al.  ; Tasevski et al.  ). Upon two‐way ANOVA analysis (genotype, sex) and Bonferroni multiple comparison test, both the body weight [F
(1,88) = 448.30, p < 0.0001] and body length [F
(1,88) = 319.80, p < 0.0001] were significantly lower in male (p < 0.0001) and female (p < 0.0001) KO mice, compared with the male and female WT mice based on genotype (Figure  ). The response time in the righting reflex significantly increased in both male (p < 0.0001) and female (p = 0.0004) KO mice, compared with the male and female WT mice based on genotype [F
(1,88) = 34.69, p < 0.0001] (Figure  ). The grip strength significantly decreased in both male (p < 0.0001) and female (p < 0.0001) KO mice, compared with the male and female WT mice based on genotype [F
(1,88) = 499.10, p < 0.0001] (Figure  ). In the open field test, upon two‐way ANOVA analysis (genotype, sex), the distance traveled [F
(1,88) = 68.51, p < 0.0001], speed [F
(1,88) = 77.62, p < 0.0001], mobility rate [F
(1,88) = 104.30, p < 0.0001] and exploration rate [F
(1,88) = 53.82, p < 0.0001] significantly decreased in both male (p < 0.0001) and female (p < 0.0001) KO mice, compared with the male and female WT mice (Figure  ). There were no sex differences in these behavioral outcomes.

3.2

## 
TNAP Deficiency Changed Microglial Morphology
The brain was significantly smaller in the KO mice, compared to the male and female WT mice, which is consistent with our previous study (Tasevski et al.  ) (Figure  ). To confirm the deficiency of TNAP, the mRNA expression of TNAP was measured in microglia isolated from both male and female WT and KO mice. Upon two‐way ANOVA analysis (genotype, sex) and Bonferroni multiple comparison test, we found that the TNAP expression was significantly decreased in the microglia in male (p < 0.0001) and female (p < 0.0001) KO mice, compared with the male and female WT mice [F
(1,20) = 80.23, p < 0.0001] (Figure  ). However, it is unknown if or how TNAP deficiency regulates microglia development and maturation.
 Microglia are multifaceted in both function and morphology and play a crucial role in development and maintenance of neural circuits (Paolicelli et al.  ). Microglia in rodents increase in size and assume a ramified morphology during the 2–4 postnatal weeks (Orlowski et al.  ). To investigate the impact of TNAP deficiency on microglial morphology, we measured the mRNA expression of IBA1 in isolated microglia from WT and KO mice. Upon two‐way ANOVA (genotype, sex) and Bonferroni multiple comparison test, IBA1 expression significantly increased in both male (p = 0.0009) and female (p = 0.0041) KO mice, compared with the male and female WT mice based on genotype [F
(1,20) = 29.91, p < 0.0001] (Figure  ). No sex difference was shown between males and females.
Next, we compared microglial morphology using IBA1 immunohistochemistry. We found that the area of soma [F
(1,16) = 18.32, p = 0.0006] of the microglia significantly increased in both male (p = 0.0089) and female (p = 0.0286) KO mice, and the length of the process [F
(1,20) = 122.05, p < 0.0001] significantly decreased in both male (p < 0.0001) and female (p < 0.0001) KO mice, compared with the male and female WT mice based on genotype (Figure  ).

3.3

## 
TNAP Deficiency Increased Neuroinflammatory Markers in Microglia
The enlarged soma and shortened process length observed in KO microglia may reflect altered microglial function, including changes in their neuroinflammatory and phagocytic responses. To investigate the impact of TNAP deficiency on microglial neuroinflammation, we measured the mRNA expression of TNF‐α (tumor necrosis factor‐alpha), IL‐1β (interleukin‐1 beta), IL‐6, TGF‐β1, iNOS (inducible nitric oxide synthase), TLR4 (toll‐like receptor 4), NLRP3 (nucleotide‐binding domain, leucine‐rich repeat, and pyrin domain‐containing protein 3), and STING (stimulator of interferon genes) in isolated microglia from WT and KO mice. (1,20) = 24.88, p < 0.0001], IL‐1β [F
(1,20) = 36.50, p < 0.0001], TGF‐β1 [F
(1,20) = 26.56, p < 0.0001], iNOS [F
(1,20) = 33.66, p < 0.0001], TLR4 [F

## 
TNAP Deficiency Increased Microglial Phagocytosis Markers
CD68 (Cluster of Differentiation 68), SPP1 (The secreted phosphoprotein 1), and TREM2 (triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2) are associated with Aβ plaque phagocytosis of microglia (Grubman et al.  ). THIK‐1 (Tandem pore‐domain Halothane Inhibited K+ channel) regulates microglial ramification, motility, and cytokine release (Madry et al.  ), and promotes microglial phagocytosis via the maintenance of hyperpolarization (Izquierdo et al.  ). To investigate the impact of TNAP deficiency on microglial phagocytosis, we measured the mRNA expression of CD68, SPP1, TREM2, and THIK‐1 in isolated microglia from WT and KO mice. Upon two‐way ANOVA (genotype, sex) and Bonferroni multiple comparison test, CD68 mRNA expression significantly increased in both male (p = 0.0011) and female (p = 0.0001) KO mice, compared with the male and female WT mice based on genotype [F
(1,20) = 41.02, p < 0.0001] (Figure  ). There were significant changes in SPP1 mRNA expression based on genotype [F
(1,20) = 6.48, p = 0.0193] and interaction (genotype × sex) [F
(1,20) = 4.88, p = 0.0390]. SPP1 significantly increased in male KO mice, compared with the male WT mice (p = 0.0062) and female KO mice (p = 0.0132) (Figure  ). TREM2 mRNA expression significantly increased in both male (p = 0.0440) and female (p = 0.0355) KO mice, compared with the male and female WT mice based on genotype [F
(1,20) = 12.84, p = 0.0019] (Figure  ). THIK‐1 mRNA expression significantly increased in the male KO mice, compared with the male WT (p = 0.0002) and female KO (p = 0.0253) mice based on genotype [F
(1,20) = 24.25, p < 0.0001] (Figure  ).
 We further assessed the protein expression of CD68 in microglia in brain tissues by performing immunohistochemistry with antibody staining. Our results showed a significantly increased expression of CD68 in microglia in both male (p < 0.0001) and female KO (p < 0.0001) mice compared to their WT counterparts based on genotype [F
(1,16) = 158.1, p < 0.0001] (Figure  ).
Microglial phagocytic activity is markedly modulated by interactions with surrounding cells, notably neurons, astrocytes, and other immune cell populations (Beiter et al.  ). To assess whether TNAP deficiency induces microglial alterations in a cell‐autonomous manner, independent of influences from other cell types, we isolated primary microglia from both male and female WT and KO mice. The cells were cultured under identical conditions for 72 h prior to evaluation of phagocytic activity. Upon two‐way ANOVA (genotype, sex) and Bonferroni multiple comparison test, phagocytic activity, as measured by the co‐localization of fluorescent beads with microglial cells, was significantly elevated in both male and female KO mice compared to male (p = 0.0018) and female (p = 0.0033) WT mice based on treatment [F
(1,8) = 47.92, p = 0.0001] (Figure  ).

TNAP Deficiency Changed Microglial Tryptophan Metabolism
Neuroinflammation shifts the tryptophan metabolism along the kynurenine pathway (KP) toward oxidative and potentially cytotoxic metabolites (Schwarcz et al.  ). To investigate the impact of TNAP deficiency on microglial tryptophan metabolism, we measured the mRNA expression of IDO‐1 (indoleamine‐2,3‐dioxygenase‐1), KMO (kynurenine monooxygenase), KYNU (kynureninase), 3HAO (3‐hydroxyanthranilate 3,4‐dioxygenase), and QPRT (quinolinate phosphoribosyltransferase) in isolated microglia from WT and KO mice. Upon two‐way ANOVA analysis (genotype, sex) and Bonferroni multiple comparison test, there was no significant difference in IDO mRNA expression (Figure  ). KMO mRNA expression significantly increased in the female KO (p = 0.0105) mice, compared with the female WT mice based on genotype [F
(1,20) = 9.06, p = 0.0069] (Figure  ). The mRNA expression of KYNU [F
(1,20) = 41.59, p < 0.0001] and 3HAO [F
(1,20) = 36.49, p < 0.0001] significantly increased in both male (KYNU: p = 0.0017; 3HAO: p = 0.0036) and female (KYNU: p < 0.0001; 3HAO: p = 0.0002) KO mice, compared with the male and female WT mice based on genotype (Figure  ). QPRT mRNA expression significantly increased in the female (p = 0.0168) KO mice, compared with the female WT mice based on genotype [F
(1,20) = 13.05, p = 0.0017] (Figure  ). There was no sex difference in the mRNA expression of the tryptophan signaling pathway markers.
 Microglia regulate KP balance by preferentially producing oxidative metabolites, including QUIN (quinolinic acid), a known neurotoxin and gliotoxin (Guillemin et al.  ). We further measured the protein level of QUIN in isolated microglia from WT (n = 12, 6M/6F) and KO (n = 12, 6M/6F) mice using ELISA. Due to the low protein yield from microglia isolated from a single animal, cells from two animals were pooled for protein extraction and subsequent ELISA measurements, yielding three data points per group, with each data point representing two animals. The Shapiro–Wilk test confirmed that the data were normally distributed, so a two‐way ANOVA (genotype, sex) and Bonferroni multiple comparison test was used for the QUIN analysis. QUIN significantly increased in the male KO mice (p = 0.0267), compared with the male WT mice based on genotype [F
(1,8) = 14.88, p = 0.0048] (Figure  ).

## 
TNAP Deficiency Alters Microglial Purinergic Signaling and Enhances Microglial Senescence
Studies have shown that TNAP regulates neuronal differentiation and axonal growth via purinergic signaling by cleaving all forms of adenosine phosphates (Sebastian‐Serrano et al.  ). Our previous study has shown that TNAP deficiency significantly increases levels of cellular basal respiration and ATP production (Zhang et al.  ). Nevertheless, the potential role of TNAP in orchestrating microglial development and function via purinergic signaling pathways remains largely unexplored, representing a significant gap in our understanding of neuroimmune regulation.
Purinergic receptors at microglial cells exemplify different microglial activation states. P2RY12 is highly expressed on quiescent and activated anti‐inflammatory microglia (Sipe et al.  ). P2RY12 steers the microglial process extension, migration, and the transition of resting to amoeboid microglia (Haynes et al.  ). Microglial P2RY6 expression increases in response to acute inflammation and/or excitotoxicity and facilitates microglial phagocytosis (Dundee and Brown  ). The ATP‐gated ionotropic P2RX7 functions as a small cation channel and triggers membrane permeabilization, leading to microglial activation, proliferation, and release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, proteases, and reactive oxygen/nitrogen species (Koizumi et al.  ; Janks et al.  ).
To investigate the impact of TNAP deficiency on microglial purinergic signaling, we measured the mRNA expression of P2RX7, P2RY6, and P2RY12 in isolated microglia from WT and KO mice. Upon two‐way ANOVA (genotype, sex) and Bonferroni multiple comparison test, the mRNA expression of P2RX7 [F
(1,20) = 26.64, p < 0.0001], P2RY6 [F
(1,20) = 48.07, p < 0.0001], and P2RY12 [F
(1,20) = 44.66, p < 0.0001] significantly increased in both male (P2RX7: p = 0.0032; P2RY6: p = 0.0002; P2RY12: p = 0.0005) and female (P2RX7: p = 0.0032; P2RY6: p = 0.0001; P2RY12: p = 0.0001) KO mice, compared with the male and female WT mice based on genotype (Figure  ). There was no sex difference in the mRNA expression of purinergic receptors in isolated microglia.
 Studies have demonstrated that P2RX7 activation can promote microglial senescence (Wei et al.  ). The induction of senescence markers such as p16, p21, and p53 in microglia is associated with the development of a senescence‐like phenotype, which is characterized by cell cycle arrest, DNA damage, and the secretion of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and chemokines (Rim et al.  ). To further investigate this, we assessed the mRNA expression of the senescence markers p16, p21, and p53 in isolated microglia from both WT and KO mice. We found that the mRNA expression of p16 [F
(1,20) = 33.31, p < 0.0001] and p21 [F
(1,20) = 35.28, p < 0.0001] significantly increased in the isolated microglia of both male (p16: p = 0.0160; p21: p = 0.0047) and female (p16: p < 0.0001; p21: p = 0.0002) KO mice, compared with the male and female WT mice based on genotype (Figure  ). There were significant changes in p53 mRNA expression based on genotype [F
(1,20) = 69.26, p < 0.0001], sex [F
(1,20) = 12.06, p = 0.0024], and interaction (genotype × sex) [F
(1,20) = 12.32, p = 0.0022]. p53 significantly increased in the isolated microglia from the male (p = 0.0056) and female (p < 0.0001) KO mice, compared with the male and female WT mice. Furthermore, p53 significantly increased in the isolated microglia from the female KO mice, compared with the male KO mice (p = 0.0002) (Figure  ).

## Knockdown of TNAP in Microglia Isolated From WT Mice Resulted in Altered Expression of Inflammatory Markers
Our previous study demonstrated that global TNAP deficiency is associated with a reduction in brain volume and induces alterations across multiple cell types, including neurons, astrocytes, and microglia (Tasevski et al.  ). Therefore, the observed neuroinflammatory response may result from dysfunction across multiple cellular lineages, contributing to phenotypic changes in microglia. To further investigate the specific role of TNAP deficiency in microglial function, we employed siRNA‐mediated knockdown of TNAP in microglia isolated from male and female WT mice.
Transfection efficiency across all experimental groups was 98.82% ± 0.73% (Figure  ). No statistically significant differences were observed between microglial cells derived from male and female mice; therefore, cells from both sexes within the same treatment group were combined for subsequent data analyses. TNAP mRNA expression significantly decreased in the siRNA group, compared with the scrambled RNA group [t

## Discussion
Patients with infantile HPP show a variety of symptoms, including failure to thrive, reduced muscle strength, delayed motor development, and seizures (Vogt et al.  ; Reis and Lazaretti‐Castro  ). In the present study, we have demonstrated that TNAP deficiency results in delayed growth and maturation of both male and female KO mice, indicated by significantly lower body weight and shorter body length, suggesting decreased overall health (Rush et al.  ; Salles  ). We have further demonstrated that TNAP deficiency causes sensorimotor deficits, indicated by diminished righting reflex, reduced grip strength, and decreased motor function. Our results are consistent with previous studies, in which both children and animals with HPP have muscle weakness, impaired mobility, and increased risk for behavioral health challenges (Pierpont et al.  ; Zhang et al.  ; Hogler et al.  ; Tasevski et al.  ). The above results have demonstrated that this mouse model of HPP represents clinical HPP manifestations that are seen in patients and can be used to investigate the impact of TNAP deficiency on microglial development and function. It should be noted that TNAP deficiency induces broad effects across multiple brain cell types and is associated with substantial systemic alterations (Zhang et al.  ; Tasevski et al.  ). Consequently, the behavioral phenotypes observed in this study are best interpreted as reflective of effective TNAP knockout modeling, rather than being exclusively attributable to microglial‐specific deficiency. Although our data demonstrate pronounced changes in microglial function, these findings do not allow for the exclusive attribution of the observed behavioral outcomes to microglial alterations alone.
Microglial cells are the innate immune cells in the CNS and play crucial roles in brain development and homeostasis (Li and Barres  ). Microglial cells possess a plethora of functions (Kettenmann et al.  ; Zhan et al.  ). Beyond neuroimmune response and phagocytic capacity, microglial cells also promote synaptic plasticity, neuronal survival and neurogenesis (Ueno et al.  ). Microglial cells display morphophysiological and transcriptomic diversity (Hammond et al.  ; Vidal‐Itriago et al.  ), which are correlated with their diverse reactivity and function. Growing evidence indicates that TNAP plays an important role in neuronal development and function and contributes to the synthesis of different neurochemicals (e.g., serotonin, dopamine, and adenosine) (Fonta et al.  ). Studies have shown that the absence of TNAP decreases myelination and synaptogenesis in the cerebral cortex, reduces the g‐ratio (axon diameter/fiber diameter) in the white matter of the spinal cord, and delays the maturation of Purkinje cells of the cerebellum (Narisawa et al.  ; Hanics et al.  ; Tasevski et al.  ). However, the impact of TNAP deficiency on microglia development and function is still unclear. In this study, we found that TNAP deficiency alters microglial morphology, as evidenced by an enlarged soma and shortened processes. These morphological changes were accompanied by increased expression of neuroinflammatory and phagocytic markers, as well as disruptions in tryptophan‐kynurenine metabolism, including elevated levels of the neurotoxic metabolite quinolinic acid. Additionally, we demonstrated that TNAP deficiency disrupts purinergic signaling, reflected by upregulation of purinergic receptors, and promotes the development of a senescent phenotype in microglia.
TNAP regulates purinergic transmission in the brain and controls cellular proliferation, differentiation and migration by modulating extracellular ATP levels (Sebastian‐Serrano et al.  ). The ectophosphatase‐ and the ectonucleotidase‐related functions of TNAP contribute to the balance between pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory effects (Graser et al.  ). Impaired TNAP ectophosphatase activity causes PPi accumulation and initiates inflammation processes by triggering NLRP3 inflammasome and TLR activation (Mulay et al.  ). NLRP3 is a cytosolic multiprotein complex that promotes the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines IL‐1β and IL‐18 (Yang et al.  ). TNAP's ectonucleotidase activity controls the level of inflammation by converting the pro‐inflammatory ATP to the anti‐inflammatory nucleotide adenosine, while upregulation of TNAP exhibits anti‐inflammatory effects via ATP degradation and production of adenosine (Graser et al.  ). NLRP3 activation is closely tied to the activation of low‐affinity ATP‐gated P2RX7 receptors. TNAP prevents P2RX7 receptor activation by hydrolyzing ATP; whereas the absence of TNAP increases availability of extracellular ATP and activates purinergic receptors (Diez‐Zaera et al.  ; Sebastian‐Serrano et al.  ). In addition to P2RX7, the two‐pore domain K+ channel THIK‐1 has been recently identified to regulate the release of IL‐1β in response to purinergic stimulation from rodent microglia (Madry et al.  ). In the present study, TNAP deficiency increased P2RX7 expression, which may be partially responsible for the increased inflammatory response of microglia. Microglial phagocytosis relies on specific receptors and downstream signaling pathways (Krasemann et al.  ). The upregulation of SPP1, TREM2, and P2RY6 in the KO mice can increase microglial phagocytosis of synapses, which may lead to deficits in synaptic transmission and cell loss (Lee et al.  ; De Schepper et al.  ; Dundee et al.  ). Although TNAP does not directly participate in tryptophan metabolism, it can indirectly influence the kynurenine pathway by modulating inflammation (Xue et al.  ). The increased inflammatory response in KO microglia may be responsible for shifting the kynurenine pathway toward neurotoxicity by increasing quinolinic acid.
In general, P2RY12 expression is downregulated during the inflammatory phenotype shift (Mildner et al.  ). In the present study, the expression of P2RY12 is upregulated in the KO microglia. Evidence indicates that signaling via P2Y12 receptors undergoes age‐dependent alterations, in which the expression of P2Y12 significantly increased in aged microglia and P2RY12 activation in aged microglia becomes less dynamic and ramified (e.g., retraction and elimination of processes), which is the opposite compared with the young microglia (e.g., increase in process extension) (Damani et al.  ). We have previously shown that TNAP deficiency decreased Purkinje cell proliferation and increased cell death in the cerebellum of KO mice (Tasevski et al.  ). Therefore, the elevated expression of P2RY12, together with increased levels of cell senescence markers such as p16 and p21, may suggest premature microglial aging in KO mice.
Previous studies have demonstrated that LPS stimulation leads to increased levels of IL‐6 and TNF in heterozygous TNAP+/− mice. Furthermore, inhibition of TNAP in neutrophils significantly exacerbates ATP‐induced activation and secretion of IL‐1β, collectively suggesting that TNAP exerts anti‐inflammatory effects (Gamez‐Belmonte et al.  ; Bessueille et al.  ). siRNA‐mediated knockdown of TNAP resulted in elevated IL‐1β and reduced TGF‐β1 levels, which may be indicative of a pro‐inflammatory phenotype. However, as other pro‐inflammatory markers did not exhibit significant changes, the overall impact of TNAP deficiency on the inflammatory profile requires further investigation.
There are some limitations of this study. Studies have shown that there are sex‐specific differences in the density, morphology, and phagocytic activity of microglia (Han et al.  ). In our study, we did not find a substantial sex difference between male and female microglia. This may be because our analysis only covered a period of 14 days postnatal development due to the short life span of the KO mice (Tasevski et al.  ). Secondly, microglia have diverse morphology and function. Although we isolated microglia from the brain regions of WT and KO mice, they can still include a heterogeneous group of microglia cells (Tan et al.  ). Therefore, single‐cell transcriptomics analysis can be used to investigate microglia cell populations, understand cellular diversity, and model transcriptional dynamics. Thirdly, global TNAP knockout induces dysfunction across multiple neural cell lineages, including neurons, astrocytes, and microglia, thereby confounding the interpretation of cell type‐specific effects. In this study, we employed siRNA‐mediated knockdown of TNAP in microglia isolated from WT mice. While this intervention resulted in changes to inflammatory marker expression, the effects may not fully recapitulate the phenotype observed in vivo with complete TNAP deficiency. To elucidate the cell‐autonomous role of TNAP in microglia, future studies employing conditional, microglia‐specific TNAP knockout models or targeted in vivo knock‐in approaches to selectively express TNAP in microglia are warranted. These methodologies would facilitate a more precise characterization of the direct effects of TNAP deficiency on microglial function, independent of alterations in other neural cell types.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, the results from the current study have demonstrated that TNAP deficiency causes abnormal development of microglia, accompanied by dysregulation of neuro‐inflammation, phagocytosis, and tryptophan metabolism in an infantile HPP model. These changes may be due to the dysregulation of the purinergic pathway resulting from TNAP deficiency. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study that has revealed an important role of TNAP in microglial development and function. These findings significantly advance the field by uncovering new molecular links between TNAP activity, microglial physiology, and broader neuroimmune processes, opening avenues for targeted therapeutic strategies in neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders.
