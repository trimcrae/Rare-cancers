Identifying clinically actionable targets to overcome this resistance is therefore essential for developing effective combination strategies with ICB. In this study, using transcriptomic data from cancer patients treated with programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) therapy and established mouse preclinical PD-1 blockade-resistant models, we identify transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) as a crucial mediator that enables tumor cells to resist cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL)-mediated killing, thereby facilitating immune escape from PD-1 therapy. Mechanically, TRPV1 enhances autophagy-dependent secretion of epidermal growth factor (EGF), which in turn activates epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling, ultimately leading to tumor cell resistance to CTL-mediated cytotoxicity. Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of TRPV1 sensitizes resistant tumors to PD-1 blockade by restoring anti-tumor immune response. Thus, our findings highlight TRPV1 as a promising therapeutic target for overcoming resistance to PD-1 blockade and provide a strong rationale for combining TRPV1 antagonists with anti-PD-1 therapy.

## Introduction
Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy has revolutionized cancer treatment by enhancing T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity, resulting in substantial clinical success across various cancer types, particularly melanoma – . However, the majority of patients fail to achieve durable clinical benefit due to the emergence of resistance to programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockade , . Therefore, future research must focus on precisely elucidating the underlying mechanisms and identifying actionable targets to effectively overcome resistance to PD-1 blockade.
Accumulating evidence indicates that intrinsic properties of tumor cells not only drives tumorigenesis but also impair critical processes necessary for effective anti-tumor immunity, including cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL)-mediated tumor cell killing , . Moreover, recent studies have reported that CTL-mediated tumor cell death is essential for initiating or sustaining anti-tumor immune responses by providing tumor antigens to CTLs, a process central to the cancer immunity cycle – . These findings underscore the importance of tumor cell susceptibility to CTL-mediated cytotoxicity in enhancing the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy. In this regard, we previously reported that increased autophagosome abundance in tumor cells promotes cell death resistance to multiple treatment modalities, including chemotherapy and immunotherapy, by upregulating myeloid leukemia 1 (MCL1), an anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family protein, through hyperactivation of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling , . Inhibiting autophagosome formation was sufficient to overcome resistance to tumor cell death and achieve sustained disease control, highlighting autophagosome formation as a key mechanism of therapeutic resistance , . These observations suggest that targeting autophagosome biogenesis in tumor cells might represent a promising strategy to overcome resistance in PD-1 blockade. Therefore, given the lack of clinically available inhibitors of autophagosome formation, identifying actionable targets that regulate this process in PD-1 blockade-resistant tumors is urgently need.
The transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) channel, a calcium-permeable ion channel, is involved not only in pain perception but also in various pathophysiological processes, such as inflammation and autophagy , . Recently, TRPV1 has emerged as a potential therapeutic target in oncology due to its overexpression in several cancers, such as cervical, breast, lung, hepatocellular, colorectal, and pancreatic cancers , . Moreover, TRPV1 activity has been associated with therapy-resistant phenotypes characterized by anti-apoptotic signaling, reduced T cell infiltration, and a fibrotic tumor microenvironment (TME) , , , . We previously demonstrated that TRPV1 promotes resistance to cisplatin-induced apoptosis by enhancing autophagy-mediated EGFR activation, suggesting its potential as an actionable target to mitigate the pathogenic role of autophagy in treatment-resistant cancers . Despite increasing interest in TRPV1 as a therapeutic target, its role in mediating resistance to PD-1 blockade remains poorly understood.
In this study, using transcriptomic data from cancer patients treated with programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) therapy and established mouse preclinical PD-1 blockade-resistant models (B16 P3 and Yumm2.1 P3), we demonstrate that TRPV1 promotes resistance to PD-1 blockade. Mechanistically, TRPV1 enhances autophagy-dependent secretion of EGF, which in turn activates EGFR signaling and confers resistance to CTL-mediated tumor cell killing. Notably, inhibition of TRPV1 using AMG9810, a selective antagonist originally developed as an analgesic, sensitizes resistant tumors to PD-1 blockade by restoring anti-tumor immune responses. Thus, our findings provide proof-of-concept evidence supporting TRPV1 as a promising therapeutic target for overcoming resistance to PD-1 blockade.

## Results

## TRPV1 expression is associated with poor survival rates and resistance of tumor cell to CTL-mediated killing
To identify potential targets underlying resistance to PD-1 blockade, we initially analyzed transcriptomic data from melanoma patients classified as responders (R; patients who achieved complete or partial response) or non-responders (NR; patients with progressive disease) to anti-PD-1 therapy . Among the most prominent gene ontology categories, those related to calcium ion transport were significantly enriched in NR compared to R (Fig.  a). Recent studies have demonstrated that dysregulated calcium signaling can disrupt essential cellular functions, thereby contributing to disease progression, including cancer – . To further investigate the association between calcium signaling and resistance to PD-1 blockade, we analyzed the expression levels of representative calcium ion transport-related genes, such as TRPA1, TRPC1, TRPC3, TRPM7, TRPV1, TRPV4, TRPV6, ORAI1, ORAI3, STIM1, and STIM2, in R and NR. Among these genes, TRPV1 was significantly upregulated in NR compared to R (Fig.  b). Furthermore, melanoma patients with high TRPV1 expression exhibited significantly worse overall survival rates (Fig.  c). These findings suggest that elevated TRPV1 expression may contribute to resistance to PD-1 blockade, leading to poor clinical outcomes in melanoma patients. 
To characterize the role of TRPV1 in conferring PD-1 blockade-resistant properties, we next measured TRPV1 protein levels in the PD-1 blockade-resistant cell lines B16 P3 and Yumm2.1 P3, which were derived from their respective PD-1 blockade-sensitive parental cell lines B16F0 P0 and Yumm2.1 P0. Consistent with the clinical data, TRPV1 expression was markedly elevated in P3 cells compared to P0 cells (Fig.  d). We previously demonstrated that P3 cells are resistant to apoptotic death induced by cognate CTLs, whereas P0 cells remain sensitive to these CTLs , . Given the elevated TRPV1 expression in P3 cells, we hypothesized that TRPV1 might contribute to CTL-resistant phenotype of tumor cells. To confirm this, P3 cells were treated with siRNA targeting GFP or Trpv1 (Fig.  e), followed by incubation with OVA-specific cognate CTLs for 4 h. Compared to control, Trpv1 knockdown increased the sensitivity of P3 cells to CTL-induced apoptosis (Fig.  f), suggesting a crucial role of TRPV1 in mediating resistance of tumor cells to CTLs-mediated killing.
We further considered the possibility that TRPV1 might regulate tumor cell sensitivity to CTLs by modulating the antigen presentation processes. However, MHC class I (H2-Kd, Dd) expression was comparable between siGFP- and siTrpv1-treated P3 cells (Fig.  g), indicating that differences in antigen presentation do not account for the above results. Instead, Trpv1-silenced P3 cells displayed increased sensitivity to in vitro liposomal delivery of granzyme B (GrB), a key effector molecule in CTL-mediated cytotoxicity (Fig.  h). Furthermore, TRPV1 overexpression in B16 P0 cells reproduced the CTL-resistant phenotype observed in B16 P3 cells (Fig.  i,j). Together, these findings suggest that TRPV1 contributes to PD-1 blockade resistance by rendering tumor cells impervious to apoptotic death by CTLs, independent of T cell recognition.

## TRPV1 promotes resistance to CTL-mediated killing through autophagy-mediated hyperactivation of EGFR/MCL1 signaling axis
We next sought to elucidate the mechanism by which TRPV1 confers resistance to CTL-mediated killing in tumor cells. In our previous study, we demonstrated that TRPV1 promotes an anti-apoptotic phenotype in response to cisplatin by enhancing autophagy-mediated hyperactivation of EGFR signaling . To investigate whether a similar mechanism underlies TRPV1-driven resistance to CTLs, we treated B16 TRPV1 cells with AMG9810, a specific TRPV1 antagonist. Interestingly, TRPV1 inhibition reduced autophagosome formation, as indicated by a marked decrease in the conversion of LC3B-I to LC3B-II. This inhibition impaired autophagy-dependent EGF secretion and attenuated EGFR activation (Fig.  a). We previously reported that hyperactivated EGFR promotes anti-apoptotic property of tumor cells to cisplatin or cognate CTLs via upregulation of anti-apoptotic protein MCL1 , . Consistent with this, AMG9810 treatment resulted in decreased MCL1 expression compared to controls (Fig.  a). Furthermore, TRPV1 inhibition increased the sensitivity of tumor cells to GrB-induced apoptosis in a dose-dependent manner (Fig.  b). These results support the notion that TRPV1 may contribute to tumor cell resistance to CTL-mediated killing, at least in part, through autophagy-dependent activation of the EGFR/MCL1 signaling axis. 
We further investigated the role of autophagy in TRPV1-mediated acquisition of resistance to CTL killing. To directly link autophagy to the TRPV1-induced phenotype, we silenced Atg7, a central component of autophagosome formation, in B16 TRPV1 cells using siRNA. Knockdown of Atg7 abolished autophagosome formation and led to a reduction in secreted EGF, phosphorylated EGFR, and MCL1 levels (Fig.  c). In addition, Atg7 knockdown significantly increased tumor cell sensitivity to CTL-mediated killing (Fig.  d), indicating that autophagy plays a crucial role in TRPV1-dependent resistance of tumor cells to CTL-mediated apoptosis. Together, these results demonstrate that TRPV1 induces tumor cell resistance to CTL-mediated killing through autophagy/EGFR/MCL1 molecular axis.

## The TRPV1/EGFR axis is conserved in human lung and gastric cancer patients
We previously reported that the functional properties of the TRPV1/EGFR axis are conserved in both lung and gastric cancer patients . To further explore the potential relationship between the TRPV1/EGFR axis and responsiveness to anti-PD-1 therapy across different cancer types, we analyzed transcriptomic data from lung and gastric cancer patients in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohort. Responsiveness to T cell-mediated immunotherapy was inferred based on the expression scores of seven previously validated gene sets indicative of increased T cell infiltration and anti-tumor immune response, hereafter referred to as anti-tumor immune states . Interestingly, we found that TRPV1 expression was upregulated in immunotherapy-resistant patients with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD) (Fig.  a). These results suggest that the TRPV1/EGFR axis might contribute to resistance to PD-1 blockade in lung and gastric cancer patients. 
We next examined whether TRPV1 inhibition could overcomes resistance to PD-1 blockade by suppressing EGFR signaling in cancer patients. To address this, we established a TRPV1 inhibition score (TIS) as a reliable indicator of inhibited TRPV1 activity in tumors. TIS was derived from a gene set that was downregulated following AMG9810 treatment in A549 cells , with the average expression of the gene set multiplied by a negative coefficient to facilitate interpretation (Fig.  b). Interestingly, we found that TIS was inversely correlated with a gene signature representing EGFR signaling activity (Fig.  c) and positively associated with a gene signature for apoptotic cell death (Fig.  d), suggesting that TRPV1 inhibition may enhance apoptosis by attenuating EGFR signaling in tumors. To assess the clinical relevance of TRPV1 inhibition, we analyzed patient survival data and found that individuals with high TIS values exhibited significantly improved overall survival rates (Fig.  e). Together, our findings suggest that TRPV1 inhibition might reverse resistance to PD-1 blockade by promoting CTL-induced apoptosis, ultimately leading to improved clinical outcomes in patients with lung and gastric cancer.

## TRPV1 is a potential target for controlling multimodal therapy-resistant human cancer cells
To investigate whether resistance to CTL-mediated killing driven by the TRPV1/EGFR axis is conserved in TRPV1high lung and gastric cancer cell lines, we selected H1299 (lung cancer) and MKN28 (gastric cancer) cells, both of which exhibit elevated TRPV1 expression and were previously shown to be refractory to multimodal therapies . We found that treatment with AMG9810 markedly reduced autophagosome formation and attenuated autophagy/EGFR/MCL1 signaling axis in both cell lines (Fig.  a). Furthermore, AMG9810 treatment significantly enhanced GrB-induced apoptotic cell death (Fig.  b). These results demonstrate that the TRPV1/autophagy/EGFR/MCL1 axis remains functionally active in TRPV1high lung and gastric cancer cells and highlight TRPV1 as a promising therapeutic target for overcoming resistance in these malignancies. 

## TRPV1 inhibition renders tumors susceptible to PD-1 blockade
Based on our in vitro and in silico observations, we hypothesized that in vivo administration of the TRPV1 inhibitor AMG9810 could overcome resistance to PD-1 blockade. To assess the preclinical therapeutic potential of TRPV1 inhibition, we evaluated the efficacy of PD-1 blockade in C57BL/6 mice bearing B16 P3 tumors. Mice were treated with anti-PD-1 antibody in combination with a chitosan hydrogel containing AMG9810, according to the treatment schedule described in Fig.  a. While anti-PD-1 monotherapy had little effect on PD-1 blockade-resistant tumors, the combination treatment significantly reduced tumor growth (Fig.  b). Notably, 80% of the mice receiving the combination therapy survived up to day 50 post-tumor challenge, whereas all mice in the other treatment groups had succumbed by that time point (Fig.  c). These results indicate that TRPV1 inhibition could effectively sensitive tumors to PD-1 blockade in vivo. 
Recent studies have reported that tumor cell death induced by anti-cancer agents can lead to the release of tumor antigens, thereby priming a subsequent auto-loop of anti-tumor immunity , – . In line with this, we observed a significantly higher proportion of apoptotic tumor cells in the combination treatment group compared to either monotherapy group (Fig.  d). We thus hypothesized that combining TRPV1 inhibition with PD-1 blockade may reprogram the TME from an immune-refractory to an immune-permissive state by reducing tumor cell resistance to T cell-mediated cytotoxicity. Supporting this, we observed that both total CD8+ T cells and tumor-reactive CD8+ T cells producing GrB were markedly increased in the combination group compared to the control groups (Fig.  e, f). Furthermore, analysis of transcriptomic data from melanoma patients treated with PD-1 blockade revealed a positive correlation between the TIS and gene signatures associated with apoptosis, T cell infiltration, and T cell-mediated anti-tumor responses (Fig.  g–i). Taken together, these results demonstrate that TRPV1 inhibition reprograms the immune phenotypes of tumors from non-T cell inflamed to T cell-inflamed state, thereby overcoming the resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy.

## Discussion
Although numerous preclinical studies have proposed strategies to overcome resistance to ICB, clinical trials have yielded inconsistent results, highlighting the urgent need to better understand the mechanisms underlying ICB resistance , , . Therefore, future research should aim to establish novel proof-of-concept frameworks based on mechanistic insights into innovative therapeutic targets capable of overcoming resistance to ICB therapy. In this study, using transcriptomic data from cancer patients and mouse preclinical PD-1 blockade-resistant models, we identify that resistance to PD-1 blockade is closely associated with autophagy-mediated EGFR activation driven by TRPV1 (Fig.  ). These finding shed new light on the role of tumor-intrinsic ion channels in modulating anti-tumor immunity and support the potential of TRPV1 as a biomarker for selecting patients who may benefit from anti-PD-1 therapies. 
Given that the TRPV1/EGFR axis has been identified as a key regulator in the development of therapy-resistant phenotypes , and that TRPV1 inhibition has recently been emerged as a potential strategy for overcoming therapeutic resistance and alleviating cancer-related pain in various solid tumors , our results suggest that TRPV1 inhibition may offer an effective approach to counteract PD-1 blockade resistance. Here, we explored the therapeutic potential of combining a TRPV1 inhibitor with anti-PD-1 therapy. Pharmacological inhibition of TRPV1 using AMG9810 restored sensitivity to CTL-mediated cytotoxicity and enhanced the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy in B16 P3 tumors, which were previously unresponsive to PD-1 blockade. Moreover, the combination treatment significantly increased the infiltration of effector CD8⁺ T cells into the tumors, indicating a phenotypic shift from non–T cell-inflamed to T cell-inflamed tumors. It is well established that tumor cell death induced by CTLs can result in the release of tumor antigens, which in turn prime further anti-tumor immune responses, a process central to the cancer-immunity cycle . Recent studies suggest that stimulating the cancer-immunity cycle can help reprogram the TME from an immune-refractory to an immune-permissive state , . In line with this, our findings indicate that TRPV1 inhibition promotes an immune-permissive TME by overcoming resistance to CTL-mediated killing and reinvigorating the cancer-immunity cycle (Fig.  ).
Autophagy is frequently hyperactivated in solid tumors and has been associated with tumor progression and therapeutic resistance , – . Accordingly, autophagy has attracted considerable interest as a therapeutic target in refractory cancers. To date, chloroquine (CQ) and hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) remain the only autophagy-targeting agents used in clinical settings. These agents inhibit autophagic flux by deacidifying lysosomes and blocking the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes . While CQ has shown therapeutic benefit in glioblastoma patients, achieving effective intratumoral concentrations requires high doses, raising concerns regarding clinical feasibility , . Furthermore, CQ has been reported to induce non-conventional forms of autophagy, such as secretory autophagy, leading to the release of immunosuppressive cytokines like IL-6 and IL-8, which may promote an immune-refractory TME . These limitations underscore the urgency to identify actionable targets capable of effectively regulating early-stage autophagy in refractory cancers, rather than late-stage autophagy. In this study, we demonstrate that TRPV1 is overexpressed in PD-1 blockade-resistant tumors and facilitates autophagosome formation, thereby contributing to resistance to PD-1 blockade. Inhibition of TRPV1 reduced autophagosome formation and restored susceptibility to CTL-mediated killing. Furthermore, knockdown of the autophagy gene ATG7 abrogated autophagosome formation and sensitized TRPV1-overexpressing tumor cells to CTL-induced apoptosis. These findings suggest that autophagosome formation is a critical mechanism by which TRPV1 confers resistance to PD-1 blockade. Although the precise molecular mechanisms by which TRPV1 regulates autophagosome formation remain to be elucidated, our results provide a rationale for targeting TRPV1 as a novel approach for modulating autophagy in the clinical setting.
Cross-resistance, where resistance to one treatment confers resistance to others, poses a significant barrier to effective cancer therapy by limiting therapeutic options and contributing to poor clinical outcomes , . Identifying molecular drivers of cross-resistance is therefore essential to improving the long-term efficacy of cancer treatment. Clonal evolution under the selective pressure of therapy has been recognized as a major mechanism of cross-resistance, as resistant clones gain survival advantages under continued drug exposure – . Consistent with this, our previous studies revealed that chemotherapy and immunotherapy selectively enrich for cancer cell populations expression NANOG, a transcriptional factor associated with stemness. These NANOG+ cells exhibit resistance to multimodal therapies via enhanced anti-apoptotic signaling, implicating NANOG as a central mediator of cross-resistance , , , – . Building on this, our recent findings identify TRPV1 as a downstream target that regulates NANOG-mediated resistance. While further validation is necessary to establish the clinical efficacy of TRPV1 inhibitors in overcoming cross-resistance, targeting TRPV1 may offer a promising strategy to suppress therapy-resistant cancer subpopulations and improve patient outcomes.
In this report, we demonstrate that TRPV1 promotes resistance to PD-1 blockade by suppressing tumor cell apoptosis in response to CTL-mediated killing. Mechanistically, TRPV1 drives an anti-apoptotic phenotype through autophagy-mediated hyperactivation of EGFR/MCL1 signaling. Inhibition of TRPV1 using AMG9810, a potential pain-relieving agent, sensitizes resistant tumors to PD-1 blockade and restores anti-tumor immunity by reactivating the cancer-immunity cycle. Thus, we provide proof-of-concept evidence that TRPV1 inhibition could be a viable therapeutic approach to control PD-1 blockade-resistant cancer and cancer-associated pain.

## Materials and methods

## Mice
Six- to eight-week-old C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Central Lab. Animal Inc. (Seoul, Korea). All animal experiments were approved by the Korea University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (KOREA-2022-0005) of the Korea University and conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

## Cell lines
B16F0 (ATCC, CRL-6322), H1299 (ATCC, CRL-5803), and MKN29 (JCRB Cell Bank, 0253) cell lines were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) or the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources Cell Bank (JCRB Cell Bank, Osaka, JPN). The Yumm2.1 cell line was kindly provided by Marcus W. Bosenberg of Yale University . All cell lines were sourced between 2010 and 2022 and underwent testing for mycoplasma using the Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA). The authenticity of each cell line was verified through short tandem repeat (STR) profiling conducted by IDEXX Laboratories Inc., ensuring their use within six months of testing. The generation and maintenance of PD-1 blockade-resistant B16 P3 and Yumm2.1 P3 cell lines have been previously described. All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator with a humidified chamber.

## Chemical reagents
The compound AMG9810 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, A2731) was utilized in this study. It was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO).

## siRNA constructs
The following synthetic small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) were synthesized by Bioneer (Daejeon, KOR): Non-specific GFP (green fluorescent protein), 5’-GCAUCAAGGUGAACUUCAA-3’ (sense), 5’-UUGAAGUUCACCUUGAUGC-3’ (antisense); mouse Trpv1 no. 1 5’- GGACAAGUGGGACAGAUUU-3’ (sense), 5’-AAAUCUGUCCCACUUGUCC-3’ (antisense); mouse Trpv1 no. 2 5’-UCACUCUCCAGAGGUCUGU-3’ (sense), 5’-ACAGACCUCUGGAGAGUGA-3’ (antisense); mouse Atg7 no. 1 5’-GCAAGCGAAAGCUGGUCAU-3’ (sense), 5’-AUGACCAGCUUUCGCUUGC-3’ (antisense); mouse Atg7 no. 2 5’-CAGACAAGAAGCUCCUUCU-3’ (sense), 5’-AGAAGGAGCUUCUUGUCUG-3’ (antisense). For in vitro delivery, the cells were transfected with 100 pmol of siRNAs using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, San Jose, CA, USA, 11668027) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were used for the experiment 16 h after transfection.

## DNA constructs
The pcDNA 3.1-TRPV1 plasmids were procured from Cosmogenetech (Seoul, KOR).

## Western blot analysis
Lysate extracted from a total of 1 × 105 cells was used to conduct Western blot analyses. Primary antibodies used included TRPV1 (1:3000; Abcam, Cambridge, UK, ab6166), LC3B (1:3000; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA, 2775S), pEGFR (1:3000; Cell Signaling Technology, 3777S), EGFR (1:3000; Cell Signaling Technology, 4267S), MCL1 (1:3000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-819), EGF (1:1000; Abcam, ab206106), ATG7 (1:3000; Cell Signaling Technology, 8558S), and β-actin (1:5000; MBL, Nagoya, JPN, M177-3). Following primary antibody incubation, appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) were employed, specifically anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (1:5000; Enzo, Farmingdale, NY, USA, ADI-SAB-300-J), and anti-mouse IgG-HRP (1:5000; Enzo, ADI-SAB-100-J). Immunoreactive bands were visualized using the chemiluminescence ECL Detection System (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA), with signals detected through a luminescent image analyzer (LAS-4000 Mini, Fujifilm, JPN). Membranes were cut prior to antibody incubation to enable efficient probing of multiple targets on the same blot and to minimize antibody consumption. The corresponding uncropped blots are provided and clearly documented in the Supplementary Fig.  .

## CTL-mediated apoptosis assay
Tumor cells were harvested by trypsinization and washed once with DMEM containing 0.1% fetal bovine (FBS), resuspended, and labeled in 0.1% DMEM with 10 μM CFSE (Molecular Probes, Eugene, OR, USA, 11524217) for 10 min in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. Then, the CFSE-labeled tumor cells were resuspended in 10 μM OVA peptide containing 1 mL of DMEM. After peptide-pulsing for 1 h, the cells were incubated for 4 h with OVA-specific cognate CD8+ T cells at an E/T ratio of 1:1. The frequency of apoptotic cells was analyzed by staining with anti-active caspase-3 antibody (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA, 560626) and performing flow cytometry.

## Granzyme B-mediated apoptosis assay
Recombinant human granzyme B (Enzo, BML-SE238-5000) was incubated with BioPorter Reagent Sigma-Aldrich, BPQ24) at 25 °C for 5 min. The tumor cells were incubated with BioPorter-granzyme B complexes for 4 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, the cells were stained for active caspase-3 as indicator of apoptosis and analyzed using flow cytometry.

## Bioinformatic analyses from published clinical databases
To investigate the clinical relevance in patients treated with PD-1 blockade, we analyzed the published melanoma dataset from Abril-Rodriguez et al., (dbGaP accession number: phs001919) , where patients underwent PD-1 blockade therapy and pre-treatment biopsy samples underwent RNA sequencing. Raw sequencing reads were quality-checked using FastQC and trimmed using Trimmomatic to eliminate low-quality bases and adapter sequences. The cleaned reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using the STAR aligner . Gene-level read counts were generated using HTSeq-count . DESeq2 was employed for data normalization and for adjusting for variable sequencing depth across samples. The count data were transformed using the variance stabilizing transformation (VST) function in DESeq2 to facilitate batch correction . To mitigate batch effects resulting from different experimental conditions, we utilized the ComBat-seq package on the VST-transformed data. This method efficiently minimized unwanted variation while retaining biologically relevant differences. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the negative binomial model in the DESeq2 package. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out with EnrichR, an online platform for identifying significantly enriched pathways and biological functions related to differentially expressed genes (DEGs) .
Gene expression data for LUAD and STAD samples, profiled by TCGA, were collected from the Firehose BROAD GDAC data repository (https://gdac.broadinstitute.org/). Clinical data were also retrieved from the same source. Previously defined apoptosis, EGFR activity score, T cell-mediated anti-tumor response, and T cell infiltration gene expression signatures were utilized , . We employed the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) algorithm, implemented in the R package’s gene set variation analysis (GSVA), to calculate the defined gene signature scores for each sample using the default parameters from the GSVA package . Pearson’s correlation was utilized to quantify the individual associations between TRPV1 inhibition score and EGFR activity score for each tumor type. The samples were stratified into 2 groups based on their TRPV1 inhibition score (low, high) for the latter analysis. Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier analyses evaluated the association between TRPV1 inhibition score and survival, using the 50th percentile as cutoff thresholds.

## Tumor treatment experiments
To characterize the in vivo resistance to PD-1 blockade conferred by TRPV1, C57BL/6 mice were inoculated subcutaneously with 5 × 10 B16 P3 cells per mouse. Nine days following the tumor challenge, mice received an intratumoral injection of either DMSO or AMG9810 (0.05 mg/kg)-loaded chitosan hydrogel. One day later, anti-PD-1 (BioXcell, Lebanon, NH, USA) or isotype antibody control was administered via intraperitoneal injection at 100 μg per mouse every 3 days, as outlined in Fig.  A. This treatment regimen was repeated for 2 cycles. The mice were cared for and monitored for tumor burden and survival under the protocol KUIACUC-2022-0005 authorized by the Korea University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Tumor volumes were estimated using calipers by measuring longest dimension (length) and the longest perpendicular dimension (width), applying the formula (L × W2)/2. Tumor volumes were assessed every 2–3 days until either the survival end point was reached or the tumor size limit, as defined by KUIACUC protocols, was met (mice with tumor volumes reaching 1000 mm3 were considered to have reached the endpoint). CO2 inhalation was used to euthanized mice. At least five mice were included in each group for all of the experiments. Mice were randomized before treatment.

## Tumor digestion, cell isolation, and flow cytometric analysis
To analyze the immune cells in tumors, treated mice were euthanized on day 18 following tumor inoculation and the tumors were harvested. The tumors were dissected into fragments by cutting and subsequently digested with collagenase (0.5 mg/mL, Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) and DNase (1 μg/mL, Millipore) at 37 °C for 45 min. The digested samples were then filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and washed with PBS buffer. The cell pellets were incubated with red blood cell (RBC) lysis buffer to lyse the RBCs. The resulting cell suspensions were stained for both intracellular and extracellular protein markers of interest, and the stained samples were analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) alongside CellQuest Pro software. The staining antibodies included: anti-CD3, anti-CD8, anti-granzyme B, and anti-active caspase-3 (all from BD Biosciences).

## Statistics
All data presented are representative of at least three independent experiments. Statistical differences were assessed using either Student’s t-test (two-tailed, unpaired), Mann–Whitney test, one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA, or Log-rank test with GraphPad Prism software version 10. Results yielding two-tailed p values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
