Department of Obstetrics and Gynaecology, Monash University, VIC 3168, Melbourne, Australiahttps://orcid.org/0000-0001-9664-4182MalhotraAtul Premature birth is defined as a baby born less than 37 weeks of gestation and affects approximately 10% of births worldwide. , Among the diseases of prematurity, one of the most common causes of morbidity and mortality are preterm lung injuries. , Preterm lung injury refers to a range of respiratory diseases, such as respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) and pulmonary hypertension. BPD, also known as chronic lung disease of infancy, is the most frequent complication of extreme premature birth. 
Over time, varying definitions of BPD have been used and multiple clinical phenotypes have evolved. , With advances in neonatal medicine, BPD has transitioned from a fibrocystic disease of late preterm infants to one of impaired lung development predominantly affecting infants born less than 29 weeks of gestation. The definition of BPD refers to “oxygen dependence for at least 28 postnatal days (P28).” , Characterized by marked impairment in parenchymal lung development, BPD can be associated with life-long airway and pulmonary vascular complications. , Those at greatest risk of developing BPD are infants born at a lower gestational age and birth weight. , Importantly, knowledge surrounding the multifactorial pathogenesis of this disease is still developing and gaps in our understanding continue to be identified. , For these reasons, preterm lung injuries, particularly BPD, remain a challenging disease for neonatologists to manage. , 
Despite advances in preventing and treating preterm lung injuries, specifically the introduction of antenatal corticosteroids and surfactant replacement, the prevalence of preterm lung injury continues to remain high. , In clinical practice, there are limited effective treatment modalities for preterm lung injuries such as BPD and the current standard of care remains largely supportive. , In an attempt to ameliorate the wide range of devastating sequelae associated with preterm lung injuries, research into the potential therapeutic effects of cell therapies has gained considerable momentum over the past decade. One such emerging therapy is umbilical cord blood (UCB)-derived therapy. With benefits of low immunogenicity, anti-inflammatory properties and ease of accessibility, UCB-derived cells and other UCB derivatives offer many therapeutic benefits when compared to other therapies. , , 
In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of UCB-derived therapy for preterm lung injury in both preclinical and clinical studies. We quantified UCB efficacy for preterm lung injury via meta-analysis and provided a narrative review of the safety profile described in published preclinical and clinical studies.

## Methods
This systematic review and meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA). Subgroup analysis followed protocol outlined in The Cochrane Handbook. , The review protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD42023442217).

## Selection criteria

## Types of studies
Published preclinical and clinical studies of any study design were included in this review. Studies of any publication date and language were eligible, however, the full article must have been accessible in English to be included in the review. Protocols, literature reviews, and conference abstracts were excluded.

## Types of participants
Both animal models and human participants were eligible. All animal models that modeled a “preterm lung injury,” regardless of the gestational age the animal was born, were included in this review. Human participants were defined as any preterm born infant, ie, <37 weeks of gestation, diagnosed with a preterm lung injury or at high risk of developing a preterm lung injury in the context of early phase clinical trials.

## Types of interventions
Umbilical cord blood-derived cells (UCBCs), UCB derivatives, and UCB ex vivo expanded cell therapies were eligible. Examples included UCB-derived exosomes and ex vivo expanded UCB-derived hematopoietic progenitor cells.

## Types of comparisons
In preclinical studies, a comparator arm of injury or injury plus placebo was required to be eligible for the review. Preclinical studies that did not have a form of injury control arm were excluded. In clinical studies, control arms such as a historical case matched control group or other therapies were included in the review due to the limited number of early-phase clinical trials available.

## Primary outcomes
To investigate the efficacy of UCB-derived therapy for preterm lung injury using preclinical lung injury markers of angiogenesis, level of alveolarization, apoptosis, pro-inflammatory cytokines, anti-inflammatory cytokines, pulmonary macrophages, fibrosis, tissue-to-airspace ratio, and reported outcomes of efficacy in human studies.

## Secondary outcomes
To investigate the safety profile surrounding UCB-derived therapy for preterm lung injury in preclinical and clinical studies. Specifically, via the outcomes of survival, weight, and other reported safety effects.

## Search strategy
The databases of MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), ClinicalTrials.gov, and WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform were searched for published preclinical and clinical studies on March 24, 2023. To ensure no new studies were missed, the search strategy was repeated in conjunction with additional citation searching in November 2023 (EP). The full search strategy is detailed in . No language, date of publication, or other filter restrictions were applied to the search.

## Study selection process
Studies yielded from each database search were exported into Covidence Systematic Review Software. Deduplication was automatically performed by Covidence and then manually performed (EP, JS) to ensure no duplicates were missed. To identify studies that met the inclusion criteria, title, and abstract screening was performed independently by 2 reviewers (E.P., J.S.). Protocols, reviews, and other ineligible study designs were excluded at the stage of title and abstract screening. Full papers available in English were then retrieved and full-text screening was assessed by 2 independent reviewers (E.P., J.S.). Any discrepancies between reviewer decisions in the screening process were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (T.N.).

## Data collection
Two review authors (E.P., J.S.) independently extracted relevant data from included studies into a spreadsheet. Standard data extraction, as recommended by The Cochrane Handbook was implemented to extract relevant data. Characterization of UCB-derived therapies was reported according to the International Society of Cellular Therapy (ISCT) criteria. In clinical studies outcomes relating to UCB-derived therapy efficacy and safety were extracted where a direct comparison between matched controls existed. Outcomes not measured against a control, such as temporal physiological parameters and cytokine levels were therefore excluded from data extraction. Where multiple time points were used to measure a single outcome, data reported at the last time point was extracted. Where quantitative data was not provided in text or tables, PlotDigitizer (version 2.6.9) was used to extract data from figures. Where data was not reported in the study or inappropriately presented to use PlotDigitizer, corresponding authors were contacted a minimum of 3 times via email for relevant data.

## Data synthesis and statistical analysis
Preclinical and clinical studies were analyzed separately. Where 3 or more studies reported quantitative data for the primary outcome, a meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager (5.4.1). Where continuous data was reported, a random effects, inverse variance model was used to calculate standardized mean difference (SMD) and 95% confidence interval (CI). Where dichotomous data was reported, a random effects, inverse variance model was used to calculate a pooled relative risk and 95% CI. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and thresholds for interpretation following guidelines recommended by The Cochrane Handbook. , Importantly, the interpretation of the I2 statistic was also guided by factors such as the magnitude of the treatment effect, direction of effect, and study sample size. For studies with multiple relevant and distinct treatment arms, each treatment arm was included as a separate entry in the forest plot. For outcomes where multiple markers could be used, a hierarchy was developed to minimize heterogeneity across studies. The lung injury marker most used in the included studies for each particular outcome was given a higher ranking in the hierarchy. The hierarchies used for relevant markers of lung injury are detailed in .

## Subgroup analysis
We planned to perform a subgroup analysis to investigate if the intervention effect varied with different patient populations and intervention characteristics. For each primary outcome, we aimed to perform a subgroup analysis of the following pre-specified potential effect modifiers:
Animal species.
Lung injury model.
Timing of UCB-derived therapy administration.
Route of UCB-derived therapy administration.
UCB-derived therapy dose.
Number of UCB-derived therapy doses.
Subgroup analysis was performed when a minimum of 10 study observations were included in the meta-analysis. Additionally, a minimum of 2 subgroups, comprising of a minimum of 4 study entries each, was required for the given potential effect modifier to be investigated via subgroup analysis. When interpreting subgroup differences, we planned to consider the key criteria of (i) presence of statistically significant subgroup difference defined as a chi-squared P value of less than 0.1 (ii) the covariate distribution, (iii) the plausibility of the observed treatment effect, (iv) the importance of the observed treatment effect, and (v) the presence of confounding variables. , 

## Quality assessment
Risk of bias assessment for animal studies was performed using the “Systematic Review Centre for Laboratory Animal Experimental” (SYRCLE) risk of bias tool. Risk of bias assessment for human studies was performed using the “Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies-of Interventions” (ROBINS-I) tool for non-randomized trials and the Cochrane “Risk-of-Bias Tool” for randomized trials (RoB 2). For each study, 2 review authors (E.P., C.P., or J.S.) independently performed a risk of bias assessment and any discrepancies were resolved by discussion with a third review author. In meta-analyses which included at least 10 studies, publication bias was further investigated by funnel plot analysis. Assessment of publication bias via funnel plot asymmetry for continuous data presented as SMD was performed using the recommended sample size precision estimate of 1/√n, where n is the sample size. , The certainty of evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach. , 

## Results

## Search results
A PRISMA flow chart summarizing the study selection process is presented in . A total of 2230 citations were identified through the search strategy and after deduplication, there were a total of 1684 unique citations. After the screening process, a total of 29 studies (32 papers) were included in the systematic review. There were 23 preclinical studies and 6 clinical studies (9 papers).

## Characteristics of included studies
Characteristics of included preclinical studies are summarized in . Species consisted of mostly small animal models, specifically rats (52%, n = 12) and mice (39%, n = 9). Nearly, all preterm lung injury models were models of BPD, including 16 studies using a hyperoxia-induced BPD model (70%). The age at which the injury was induced ranged from in utero to P14. Studies investigated several UCB-derived therapies, though the majority investigated MSCs (42%, n = 10) and mononuclear cells (MNCs, 26%, n = 6). Notably, a lack of UCB-derived therapy characterization according to ISCT criteria was observed across the majority of studies. For example, in reporting UCB-MSCs according to the ISCT criteria, 5 of 10 studies reported MSC phenotype population expression and no studies reported the MSC requirement of the cells being adherent to plastic in standard culture conditions. The route of UCB-derived therapy administration varied across studies, mainly comprising intratracheal (IT, 35%, n = 10), intravenous (IV, 28%, n = 8), and intraperitoneal (IP, 24%, n = 7). Eighteen studies used a single-dose regimen and 5 studies included a multiple dose regimen.
 Characteristics of included clinical studies are summarized in . From a total of 6 trials, 103 preterm infants received UCB-derived therapy. Five studies were phase I trials investigating safety and feasibility and 1 study was a randomized controlled trial investigating safety and efficacy. Inclusion criteria varied across studies, particularly the gestational age the infant was born (23 to 35 weeks of gestation). Across all studies, the number of participants was of small scale, with the number of participants administered UCB-derived therapy ranging from 5 to 33. The type of UCB-derived therapy investigated included MSCs (50%, n = 3), MNCs (33%, n = 2), and whole UCB (17%, n = 1). Limited reporting of UCB-derived therapy characterization was observed in the majority of studies, with 4 of 6 studies not reporting UCB-derived therapy cell population according to recommendations provided by the ISCT. The UCB-derived therapy administration routes included IT (50%, n = 3) and IV (50%, n = 3). Timing of UCB-derived therapy administration ranged from P1 to P14. All studies implemented a single-dose regimen and UCB-derived therapy cell dosage ranged from 1 × 107/kg to 5 × 107/kg.
 

## Primary outcomes in preclinical studies
Fifteen small animal studies assessed the level of alveolarization using the markers of mean linear intercept (MLI, n = 11), mean chord length (MCL, n = 3), or mean alveolar area (n = 1). No studies were excluded due to missing data. After the inclusion of multiple treatment groups within studies, a total of 35 study entries were included in the meta-analysis. As shown in , the meta-analysis indicated the administration of UCB-derived therapy significantly improved the level of alveolarization (SMD, 1.32, 95%CI [0.99, 1.65], P < 0.00001).
 Ten small animal studies and 1 large animal study investigated the outcome of angiogenesis using the markers of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF, n = 5), von Willebrand factor (VWF, n = 4), or formation of branch points (n = 2). Three studies were excluded due to the inability to retrieve missing data. , , After the inclusion of multiple treatment groups, a total of 20 study entries were included in the meta-analysis. Overall, as seen in , the administration of UCB-derived therapy was associated with a statistically significant improvement in angiogenesis when compared to injury controls (SMD, 1.53, 95%CI [0.87, 2.18], P < 0.0001). The single study investigating angiogenesis in a large animal model demonstrated no statistically significant improvement in an appropriately grown lamb with ventilation-induced lung injury (VILI) and a statistically significant decrease in a fetal growth restricted (FGR) lamb with VILI.
Five small animal studies assessed the outcome of apoptosis using the marker of terminal deoxynucleotidyl biotin-dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL, n = 5). One study was excluded due to missing data. After the inclusion of multiple treatment groups, 11 study entries were included in the meta-analysis. As seen in , the meta-analysis revealed the administration of UCB-derived MSC therapy was associated with a statistically significant improvement in apoptosis (SMD, 1.27, 95%CI [0.57, 1.97], P = 0.0004).
Lung inflammation was investigated by assessing pro-inflammatory cytokines and anti-inflammatory cytokines. Ten small animal studies and 2 large animal studies assessed pro-inflammatory cytokines using the markers of IL-1α (n = 3) IL-1β (n = 4), IL-6 (n = 4), TNF-α (n = 1), or inflammatory foci score (n = 1). One study was excluded due to the inability to retrieve missing data. After the inclusion of multiple treatment groups, a total of 23 entries were included in the meta-analysis. Overall, as seen in , the meta-analysis revealed the administration of UCB-derived therapy was associated with a significant reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokines (SMD, 1.32, 95%CI [0.73, 1.92], P < 0.0001). Out of the 2 studies that used a large animal model, both study entries demonstrated no significant difference. Six small animal studies and 2 large animal studies assessed the outcome of anti-inflammatory cytokines using the markers of IL-10 (n = 5) and hepatocyte growth factor (HGF, n = 3). One study was excluded due to the inability to obtain missing data. After the inclusion of multiple treatment groups, 18 study entries were included in the meta-analysis. As evidenced in , the meta-analysis showed the administration of UCB-derived therapy was associated with a statistically significant increase in anti-inflammatory cytokines (SMD, 1.68, 95%CI [1.03, 2.34], P < 0.0001). In regard to the effect seen in large animal studies, all 3 large animal study entries revealed no statistically significant difference.
 Six small animal studies and 2 large animal studies investigated the outcome of pulmonary macrophages using the markers of ED1 (n = 4), CD45 (n = 2), or CD11b (n = 1). Two studies were excluded from the meta-analysis due to the inability to retrieve missing data. , After the inclusion of multiple treatment groups, a total of 15 study entries were included in the meta-analysis. Overall, as seen in , the meta-analysis revealed the administration of UCB-derived therapy was associated with a significant improvement in the level of pulmonary macrophages (SMD, 2.65, 95%CI [1.57, 3.73], P < 0.00001). The 3 study entries using large animal models demonstrated no statistically significant difference when compared to an injury control group. , 
 Four small animal studies and 2 large animal studies measured lung fibrosis using the marker of collagen levels in lung tissue (n = 6). No studies were excluded due to missing data. After the inclusion of different treatment groups in studies as separate study entries, 12 study entries were included in the meta-analysis. As seen in , the meta-analysis revealed administration of UCB-derived therapy is associated with a statistically significant improvement in lung fibrosis (SMD, 1.55, 95%CI [0.64, 2.46], P = 0.0009). Of the 3 study entries performed In large animal models, 2 study entries demonstrated no significant difference and 1 study entry which used an FGR lamb model with VILI showed a statistically significant increase in lung fibrosis.
The outcome of the tissue-to-airspace ratio was assessed in 3 small animal studies and 1 large animal study. No studies were excluded due to missing data. After the inclusion of multiple treatment groups from studies, a total of 8 study entries were included in the meta-analysis. Overall, as shown in , the meta-analysis revealed administration of UCB-derived therapy was associated with no statistically significant difference in tissue-to-airspace ratio when compared to injury controls (SMD, −0.23, 95%CI [−0.72, 0.27], P = 0.37). The 2 large animal study entries revealed no statistically significant difference.

Six of the 13 studies reported a statistically significant difference in favor of UCB-derived therapy administration being associated with a higher weight when compared to the injury control group. , , , The remaining 7 studies reported no significant difference in weight. , , , , , , Finally, 8 preclinical studies investigated other safety effects associated with the administration of UCB. , , , , , Two of the 8 studies reported a difference in safety effects. ## Clinical studies
Six clinical studies (9 papers) were identified. The primary outcomes of this review investigated in these clinical studies are summarized in . A sufficient number of studies included an injury control arm to allow for meta-analysis to be undertaken for the development of BPD as well as BPD severity. As demonstrated in , the administration of UCB-derived therapy, specifically UCB-MNCs or UCB-MSCs, was not associated with a statistically significant reduction in the development of BPD (RR, 0.93, 95%CI [0.73, 1.18], P = 0.55), mild BPD (RR, 1.45, 95%CI [0.87, 2.41], P = 0.15), moderate BPD (RR, 0.85, 95%CI [0.30, 2.41], P = 0.77), or severe BPD (RR, 0.51, 95%CI [0.22, 1.17], P = 0.11). Of importance is the significant heterogeneity between the studies included in each meta-analysis. Chang et al /Ahn et al was a phase 1 dose-escalation trial using a historical case-matched comparison group, Yang et al /Ren et al and Zhuxiao et al were non-randomized trials and Ahn et al/Ahn et al was a phase II double-blinded randomized control trial , , 
 Across all clinical studies, 4 individual study outcomes were observed to show a statistically significant difference with the administration of UCB-derived therapy. As seen in , Ahn et al observed a statistically significant difference in oxygen use at 5 years follow-up, Ahn et al observed a significant difference in the development of severe BPD in favor of UCB-derived MSC therapy administration at 2 years follow-up, Zhuxiao et al observed a significant difference in the development of moderate or severe BPD in favor of UCB-derived MSC therapy administration and Ren et al observed a significant difference in duration of mechanical ventilation in favor of UCB-derived MNC therapy administration. , , , Additionally, further significant analyses were performed in specific studies. Ahn et al performed a subgroup analysis comparing infants born at 23-24 gestational weeks (GW) and 25-28 GW. They observed the development of severe BPD was significantly reduced in the 23-24 GW subgroup, but not the 25-28 GW subgroup. In contrast to this, at 5 years follow-up Ahn et al observed only the subgroup of infants born 25-28 GW had a lower incidence of emergency department (ED) visits both before and after adjusting for gender and family history of asthma. Additionally, at 5 years follow-up the outcome of composite respiratory morbidities (respiratory symptoms associated with ED visits, hospital readmissions, and oxygen use) was observed to be statistically significant. However, once gender and family history of asthma were adjusted for, the difference was not statistically significant. Zhuxiao et al investigated the immunomodulatory properties of UCB-derived MNC therapy and observed a statistically significant decrease in immune cells and cytokines after UCB-derived MNC therapy administration. Specifically, CRP, IL-10, T cells in lymphocyte percentage, CD4T cells in lymphocyte percentage, and T-regulatory cells in CD4+ T-cell percentage. Similarly, Kotowski et al observed a statistically significant difference in several cytokines and growth factors in favor of the administration of UCB when compared to red blood cells. 
Safety effects associated with the administration of UCB-derived therapy were extensively explored across clinical studies. These are summarized in . All clinical studies observed the administration of the UCB-derived therapy trialed to be safe and feasible. Importantly, there were no significant differences in the survival between those who received UCB-derived therapy versus the control group. In considering other reported safety effects, at 5 years follow-up Ahn et al observed a significant difference in the incidence of ED visits in favor of UCB-derived MSC administration. Ahn et al also observed a lower incidence of developmental delay in the motor function domain for the 25-28 GW subgroup analysis in favor of UCB-derived MSC administration. Both these significant differences were present before and after adjusting for gender and family history of asthma. Furthermore, Ahn et al observed a statistically significant difference in weight in favor of UCB-derived MSC administration. Finally, Zhuxiao et al observed a significant difference in the rates of pneumonia-related hospitalization, Bayley Scales of Infant Development-Second Edition mental developmental index (BSID-II MDI) score, and neurodevelopmental delay in favor of UCB-derived MNC administration. 

## Quality assessment
The risk of bias assessment of included studies is summarized in . Using the SYRCLE risk of bias tool, most preclinical studies were judged as unclear across multiple types of bias. This was concluded due to the limited methodological reporting of randomization processes, animal care, blinding of investigators, and unavailability of published protocols. Of note, 9 studies were judged as having a high risk of selection bias due to not describing a form of randomization in animal selection. Using the ROBINS-I tool, the risk of bias in the 5 non-randomized clinical studies was assessed as low across several types of bias. Importantly, 4 of 5 studies were judged as having a high risk of bias in measurement of outcomes bias due to not reporting blinding of outcome assessors. Finally, the risk of bias in the included randomized clinical study was assessed using Cochrane RoB 2 tool and determined to be low across most types of bias.
Following the GRADE approach to rating overall quality of evidence, the study design determined the initial quality of evidence. Randomized studies, (n = 23 preclinical studies, n = 1 clinical study) began with an initial high quality of evidence (+4) and non-randomized studies (n = 5 clinical studies), began with a low quality of evidence (+2). The quality of evidence seen in preclinical studies was downgraded due to the “serious” risk of bias as assessed by SYRCLE risk of bias tool (−1) and “very serious” risk publication bias as concluded by asymmetry in funnel plot analysis seen in (−2). Quality of preclinical evidence was upgraded due to the large magnitude of effect seen across several results (SMD > 0.8, +1). Thus, the certainty of evidence seen in preclinical studies was judged as “low” (+2). Clinical evidence of both non-randomized and randomized studies was downgraded for imprecision of effect estimates due to the small sample size and lack of intervention effect seen in the performed meta-analyses (−1). In assessing clinical studies, the remaining criteria in the GRADE guidelines were judged as “not serious” and no factors were applicable in upgrading the certainty of evidence. Thus, in following the GRADE guidelines, the overall quality of clinical evidence was assessed as “very low” certainty for the non-randomized clinical studies and “moderate” certainty for the clinical randomized study.

## Discussion
In this systematic review and meta-analysis of 23 preclinical studies and 6 clinical studies, we have systematically evaluated the current evidence of UCB-derived therapy for preterm lung injury and revealed several limitations as well as knowledge gaps to guide further research. Seven of the 8 markers of lung injury assessed in preclinical studies via meta-analysis were associated with statistically significant differences in favor of UCB-derived therapy when compared to an injury control. In contrast, the meta-analyses which assessed the effect UCB-derived therapy had on the development and severity of BPD in early-phase clinical studies, demonstrated no statistical difference. Thus, this paper has revealed a current translational gap that exists in the efficacy of UCB-derived therapy for preterm lung injury between preclinical and clinical studies. Importantly, these results should be considered in the context of a low certainty of available evidence according to the GRADE guidelines.

## Efficacy of UCB-derived therapy for preterm lung injury in preclinical and clinical studies
There are several factors to discuss in relation to the differences seen in efficacy between the preclinical and clinical studies analyzed. One important factor to consider is the types of animals used in preclinical studies. Of the preclinical studies, 91% (n = 21) used a rodent animal model to investigate preterm lung injury, which is associated with significant limitations. Of note, there are fundamental differences in lung capacity, alveolar size, and expression of gene-modulating processes. , At the molecular level, it is documented that certain inbred mouse strains have shown a protective response to hyperoxia when compared to outbred mouse strains. In addition to this, to mimic preterm lung injuries, rodent models are born at term and then disease-like states are induced through a variety of methods such as hyperoxia induction. Thus, with the majority of included preclinical studies using mouse or rat models, we strongly recommend other animal models, such as rabbits and large animals be used in future research. Moreover, preterm lung injuries encompass a range of disease states and each disease includes multifactorial causes. For instance, the etiologies of BPD include invasive mechanical ventilation, exposure to hyperoxia, sepsis, and genetics. , Despite this, 70% of preclinical studies included in this review employed a hyperoxia-induced model of BPD. Thus, in conjunction with increasing the diversity of animal types used, we strongly recommend the investigation of preterm lung injury models other than BPD and hyperoxia-induced BPD.
Due to the significant number of preclinical studies included in this review, it is essential to consider the limited number of clinical trials currently published and the heterogeneity between these trials. Of most importance, is to acknowledge the clinical studies included in this review were early phase I and II trials. Five of the 6 clinical studies were non-randomized, 1 study included in the meta-analyses incorporated a historical case-matched comparison group and all studies were significantly underpowered in relation to the number of participants enrolled. Additionally, the gestational age infants were born varied considerably between studies, spanning from 23 to 35 weeks of gestation. This variation in gestational age may have played a role in the lack of significant differences found in the meta-analyses. Notably, in a 2017 study by Anh et al, there was a significant reduction in the development of severe BPD in a group of infants born at a gestation age 23-29 weeks with birth weight 500-1250 g who received UCB-derived MSCs via IT route. Similarly, Zhuxiao et al observed a statistically significant reduction in the development of moderate or severe BPD in infants <32 weeks who received UCB-derived MNCs via IV route. In addition to this, in the single published randomized control trial by Ahn et al in 2021, via subgroup analysis they found development of severe BPD was significantly reduced in infants born 23-24 weeks of gestation but not 25-28 weeks of gestation. In evaluating the current clinical evidence, our review highlights the potential for UCB-derived therapy to be therapeutic for preterm lung injuries, specifically BPD, for a specific population of extreme prematurity. Of significant note, there is an ongoing multicenter phase II trial investigating the efficacy of IT-delivered UCB-MSCs in infants 23-25 weeks of gestational age (NCT03392467). This study has 60 enrolled participants and is estimated to be completed in early 2024.

## Safety of UCB-derived therapy for preterm lung injury
This review provides further evidence for the administration of UCB-derived therapy to be safe and feasible in premature infants with lung injuries, in particular BPD. From the 12 preclinical studies that reported survival, there were no statistically significant differences observed. In conjunction with this finding, of the 13 preclinical studies which reported weight, 6 studies demonstrated a significant difference in favor of UCB-derived therapy administration. Despite these observations suggesting UCB-derived therapy to be safe in preclinical studies, it is important to acknowledge that over half of the preclinical studies (13 studies) did not report key safety outcomes. Thus, in identifying a lack of safety outcomes investigated, this review urges future preclinical research to investigate the safety profile seen with the administration of UCB-derived therapy. Unlike preclinical studies, the primary aim of early phase I and II clinical trials has been focused on safety and feasibility. Critically, across all clinical studies, the administration of UCB-derived therapy, specifically UCB-derived MNCs, MSCs, and whole UCB, appeared safe across a range of outcomes. The observed safety of UCB-derived therapy administration in premature infants is consistent with the current evidence seen in UCB-derived therapy being a safe and feasible therapy for a range of neonatal conditions. , To provide further confidence in the long-term safety profile of UCB-derived therapy for preterm lung injury, future clinical trials should continue to implement protocols that follow-up patients over a prolonged period of time.

## Limitations
The interpretation of results must be considered within the limitations of this review. First, as identified in the results, the certainty of evidence evaluated in this review was judged as “low.” A significant reason for this was the high risk of publication bias, insufficient reporting of methodology in preclinical studies, small sample sizes, and non-randomization in clinical studies. In addition to this, not all results were able to be included in this review. Despite our best efforts to obtain missing data from authors, a number of studies were excluded from meta-analyses. There were also several reports unable to be retrieved in full text or in English during the screening process. It is important to acknowledge that with the inclusion of such papers, the results and conclusions observed in this review may have altered.
Another limitation of this review is the significant heterogeneity that existed between the included studies. Examples of such heterogeneous characteristics include variation in animal species, preterm lung injury models, UCB-derived therapy subtypes and derivatives, route of administration, dosage, number of doses, and animal sex as well as differences in measurement tools used in studies. Of particular importance is the heterogeneity that existed between the UCB-derived cell types and products across studies. Notably, within each meta-analysis multiple cell types and products were included due to the limited number of studies currently investigating UCB-derived therapy for preterm lung injury. Although all products included in the review were derived from UCB, there are clear biological differences between UCB-derived therapy subtypes. Further contributing to this heterogeneity, was the poor reporting of UCB-derived cell subtypes according to ISCT guidelines. As a result of such heterogeneity, the certainty and validity of the results in the review are significantly reduced. Although this paper undertook subgroup analysis to explore these heterogeneous factors, the small number of studies within each subgroup significantly limited the ability to draw meaningful conclusions from the performed subgroup analyses. Furthermore, the lack of functional outcomes investigated in preclinical studies resulted in meta-analyses of preclinical studies being limited to biomarkers of preterm lung injury.
Finally, another major limitation of this review was the inclusion of multiple treatment groups within a single study as separate study entries in performed meta-analyses. While this enabled the inclusion of multiple cell subtypes and routes of administration, where there was a limited number of studies within a meta-analysis, the effect seen in a single study had the potential to significantly influence the overall SMD. Therefore, additional caution should be taken when evaluating meta-analyses with a limited number of studies.

## Future directions
This review has identified several areas to focus future research. Beginning with preclinical literature, additional research into the differences in efficacy seen in the type of UCB-derived therapy, route of administration, timing of administration, dose, and number of doses is warranted. Future research should investigate UCB-derived cell types or derivatives other than MNCs or MSCs, preterm lung injury models other than the rat or mouse hyperoxia-induced BPD, use large animal studie,s and investigate functional as well as long-term safety outcomes. To produce higher quality preclinical evidence, future research should also pre-register study protocols and report methodology in greater detail, specifically randomization processes, allocation concealment, baseline characteristics such as sex and weight, UCB-derived therapy dose in cells/kg, and sample numbers used in each assessed outcome. In addition to this, future studies should consider the issues of heterogeneity and lack of product characterization that have been observed between studies in this review and identified in previous literature. Thus, future studies should define the type of UCB-derived cell or cell-derivative according to the ISCT criteria. Additionally, further clinical studies investigating the safety, feasibility, and efficacy of UCB-derived therapy in multi-center randomized control trials would provide much needed data to elucidate the effect UCB-derived therapy has on preventing or ameliorating preterm lung injuries in infants. In conjunction with this, the effect of population characteristics particularly gestational age and birthweight appears to be a potential area of promising research.

## Conclusions
This review systematically evaluated the available evidence for UCB-derived therapy for preterm lung injury. In both preclinical and clinical studies, the administration of UCB-derived therapy appeared safe and feasible. Although preclinical studies indicated the administration of UCB-derived therapy to be efficacious across several outcomes, there is limited evidence available in clinical studies regarding the efficacy of UCB-derived therapy for infants with preterm lung injuries. Additional research in large animal models and clinical studies investigating differing population and intervention characteristics is necessary to further advance the translation of UCB-derived therapy for preterm lung injury.
