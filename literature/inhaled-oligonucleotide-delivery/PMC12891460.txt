## Introduction
Asthma is a chronic respiratory disease that affects millions of individuals worldwide, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 300 million cases globally . The condition is characterized by airway inflammation, hyperresponsiveness, and obstruction, leading to significant morbidity and mortality , . The exacerbation of asthma symptoms is often linked to environmental factors, particularly air pollution, with particulate matter (PM) such as PM(2.5) being a major contributor . PM(2.5), defined as particulate matter with a diameter of less than 2.5 µm, poses serious health risks due to its ability to penetrate deep into the respiratory system and induce inflammatory responses . Despite existing research on PM(2.5)'s effects on respiratory health, the specific pathways and biological processes that mediate its impact on asthma exacerbation remain inadequately explored.
Ferroptosis, driven by iron-dependent phospholipid peroxidation, is a novel form of cell death . The mechanism of ferroptosis involves an imbalance between oxidative damage and antioxidant defenses . In the regulatory network of ferroptosis, glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) is considered a key protective factor . GPX4 prevents ferroptosis by reducing lipid hydroperoxides and phospholipid peroxides through the use of glutathione (GSH), thereby interrupting the lipid peroxidation chain reaction . Accumulating evidence suggests that GPX4 is closely associated with asthma. Studies have shown that GPX4 can regulate the binding of the PEBP1-15LOX complex to its substrate, PUFA-PE, limiting phospholipid peroxidation and thereby intervening in the ferroptosis process . Moreover, overexpression of methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) regulates the m6A modification and stability of GPX4 mRNA, thereby reversing the IL-13-induced increase in intracellular iron, ROS, and lipid peroxidation, while also restoring GSH levels and alleviating asthma-related inflammatory responses . Although ferroptosis is associated with asthma, its specific regulatory mechanism in the process of airway inflammation and injury remains unclear.
Long-term exposure to PM(2.5) is linked to changes in DNA methylation, affecting genes related to the cell cycle, immune response, and inflammation . DNMT3A, a pivotal DNA methyltransferase, is integral to the epigenetic regulation of gene expression . DNMT3A is expressed abnormally high in airway epithelial cells of asthma patients, and is associated with decreased lung function and increased reticular basement membrane thickness, suggesting that it may be involved in airway inflammation and remodeling . Furthermore, DNMT3A is more prominently expressed in Th2 cells compared to Th1 cells during differentiation, exerting a regulatory effect by manipulating the methylation status of the IL-13 gene to prevent its excessive expression. Mice with conditional Dnmt3a deficiency exhibit severe airway inflammation and increased levels of IL-13, indicating that DNMT3A plays a crucial role in inhibiting Th2-mediated inflammatory responses . Overall, DNMT3A plays a multifaceted role in asthma: participating in chronic airway remodeling by affecting airway structure, while concurrently inhibiting excessive activation of Th2 cells by regulating the methylation status of cytokine genes.
In the present study, we discovered that PM(2.5) triggered asthma exacerbation in a ferroptosis-dependent manner. The overexpression of GPX4 markedly mitigated the PM(2.5)-induced increase in ROS and MDA, as well as the release of inflammatory factors. Moreover, we observed that PM(2.5) enhanced the DNA methylation of GPX4 by regulating DNMT3A expression, subsequently leading to ferroptosis. The correlations between GPX4 methylation status and IL-6/8, along with GPX4 protein levels, were confirmed in clinical samples. These findings provide valuable insights into the intersection of environmental health, epigenetics, and asthma exacerbation.

## Methods and materials

## Cell culture
Normal human bronchial epithelial (NHBE) cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA) and cultured in BEGM™ Bronchial Epithelial Cell Growth Medium (Lonza, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

## Treatment of cells
Standard reference material of PM(2.5) (catalog number: SRM2786) from NIST (Gaithersburg, USA) was used in this study . NHBE cells unless specifically stated were treated with PM(2.5) at a concentration of 100 μg/mL for 24 h. Liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1, catalog number: HY-12726) and (1S,3R)-RSL3 (RSL3, catalog number: HY-100218A) were purchased from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). NHBE cells were treated with Lip-1 or RSL3 at 1 μM for 24 h. 5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine (5-Aza-CdR, catalog number:189825) was purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (Shanghai, China), and CpG Methyltransferase (M.SssI, catalog number: EM0821) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Carlsbad, USA). NHBE cells were treated with 5-Aza-CdR or M.SssI at 1 μM for 24 h. Cockroach extract (CRE, catalog number: B46) was purchased from GREER Laboratories (North Carolina, USA). NHBE cells were treated with CRE at a concentration of 50 μg/mL for 24 h.

## Mice studies
All juvenile female BALB/c mice, aged three weeks, were obtained from the Center for Experimental Animals at Nanjing Medical University (Nanjing, China). The Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments at Nanjing Medical University approved the animal experiments (approval no. 2005020). A total of fifty BALB/c mice were randomly allocated into five distinct groups, with ten mice assigned to each group: the phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) group, the cockroach extract (CRE) exposure group, the CRE + PM(2.5) group, the CRE + Lip-1 group, and the CRE + PM(2.5) + Lip-1 group. An asthma model in C57BL/6 mice induced by CRE was established following previously reported methods . In summary, juvenile BALB/c mice underwent sensitization via intratracheal inhalation of 20 µg of CRE (sourced from Greer Laboratories, USA) per mouse on days 0 through 4, followed by challenges on days 10, 11, 12, and 13 with the same dosage of CRE. Control mice were administered PBS during both the sensitization and challenge phases instead of CRE. In certain experiments, mice received treatments of PM(2.5) (5 mg/kg) or Lip-1 (10 mg/kg) during the CRE challenge, delivered via intratracheal inhalation in a final volume of 50 μL. On day 14, the mice were euthanized, and lung tissues were excised for analysis to assess pulmonary inflammation. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected through two successive lavages of the lung using 0.8 mL of ice-cold PBS. Mice were euthanized with carbon dioxide following the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. Carbon dioxide exposure using a gradual-fill method with a displacement rate from 30 to 70% of the chamber volume/min. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Nanjing Medical University. The animal study has been reported according to ARRIVE 2.0 guidelines.

## Subjects
Starting in July 2018, the researchers enlisted a cohort of 30 pediatric patients from the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University. The diagnosis of asthma in these children was performed in accordance with the guidelines established for the diagnosis, prevention, and management of childhood asthma as outlined in the 2016 edition published by the Chinese Medical Association. The peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were obtained from the cases and controls above with appropriate informed consent in the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University. All the study populations underwent a comprehensive medical evaluation to ensure the accuracy of the diagnosis. This study was approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University (approval number: 081102). We declared that the research was carried out according to the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

## PM(2.5) exposure assessment
The distribution of PM(2.5) concentration was derived from data provided by the China National Environmental Monitoring Center (CNEMC) and Qingyue Data (https://data.epmap.org). The PM(2.5) concentration at the site during the 30 days preceding the recruitment of subjects for this study was documented as the average concentration in the case–control study. XGeocoding software was utilized to ascertain the latitude and longitude of the subjects’ residences and monitoring locations. Inverse distance weighted interpolation (IDW) is a significant spatial interpolation technique within ArcGIS software (version 10.2), which was employed to assess the individual PM(2.5) exposure concentrations .

## Transfection
siRNA designed to specifically target DNMT3a, along with scrambled oligonucleotides (si-NC), were procured from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The transfection agents utilized in this study included Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (catalog number: 13778100, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA). One day before transfection, plate 1 × 10^5 cells in 500 µl of growth medium without antibiotics in 24-well plates. The cell density is 50% confluent at the time of transfection. 20 nM siRNA duplex with the diluted Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX was added to each well containing cells to incubate for 24 h. The pcDNA3.1-GPX4 plasmid, as well as the corresponding empty vector, was synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Additionally, Lipofectamine™ 3000 (catalog number: L3000015, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA) was employed as the transfection reagent. One day before transfection, plate 1 × 10^5 cells in 500 µl of growth medium without antibiotics in 24-well plates. The cell density is 70% confluent at the time of transfection. 500 ng DNA-Lipofectamine™ 3000 complexes were added to each well containing cells to incubate for 24 h.

## Transient transfections and luciferase reporter assays
The cells were cultured in 96-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 10^4 cells per well. The cell density is 70% confluent at the time of transfection. 100 ng of luciferase reporter plasmids and 4 ng of pRL-TK plasmid were introduced into the cells with the aid of Lipofectamine™ 3000 to incubated for 24 h. Following treatment with PM(2.5), 5-Aza-CdR, or M.SssI for 24 h, a Luciferase assay was executed using the Dual-luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA). The luciferase activity was adjusted relative to the activity of pRL-TK.

## Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) proliferation assay
Cell viability was assessed through the CCK-8 proliferation assay (Dojindo, Japan). A total of 3 × 10^3 cells per well were plated in 96-well plates, with three replicates for each condition. Cell medium in 96-well plates was removed and replaced with 100 μL fresh culture medium with PM(2.5) at concentrations of 0, 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL. After 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 μl of CCK-8 reagent was added, and the cells were incubated under standard conditions for 1 h. Subsequently, the absorbance at 450 nm was quantified using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA).

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
NHBE cells were cultured in each well of 24-well plates, and the supernatant was harvested 48 h post-treatment with PM(2.5) at concentrations of 0, 25, 50, and 100 μg/mL for 24 h or following the overexpression of GPX4. Fasting blood samples were obtained using serum separator tubes (BD Biosciences, USA). Following the formation of clots, samples were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min to isolate the serum. The concentrations of IL-6 and IL-8 were determined using an ELISA assay, adhering strictly to the protocols provided by the manufacturer (BD Biosciences, USA). These concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve generated using recombinant kit standards. The resulting data were analyzed with Microplate Manager 6 software (Bio-Rad, USA).

## Immunofluorescence
NHBE cells were plated in 6-well culture plates and allowed to incubate overnight. Following exposure to either CRE or PM(2.5), the cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde at a temperature of 37 °C for 15 min. After three washes with PBS, the cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 and subsequently blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GPX4. To visualize the nuclei, DAPI was employed as a staining agent. Imaging was conducted using fluorescence microscopy.

## Immunohistochemistry
Subsequent to the excision of lung tissue, the specimen was promptly immersed in formalin, followed by fixation and embedding in paraffin. The lung tissue sections underwent a deparaffinization and rehydration process. To inhibit endogenous peroxidase activity, the sections were treated with a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution for a duration of 15 min. The tissue sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary GPX4 antibody, followed by a one-hour incubation with a secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP). All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. After staining with DAB, the sections were examined using light microscopy.

## Measurement of ROS, lipid peroxidation, and mitochondrial integrity
5 × 105 cells were first seeded onto a 6-well plastic plate overnight, before incubation with 100 µg/mL of PM(2.5) for 6 h. Then, the levels of ROS, both cellular and mitochondrial, were quantified by employing DCFH-DA (25 µM, Sigma) or MitoSOXTM Red (5 µM, Invitrogen) staining for a duration of 30 min at 37 °C. Following this, the samples underwent analysis via flow cytometry. To evaluate lipid peroxidation, cells were stained with 2 μM BODIPY-C11 (Invitrogen) or assessed for MDA levels utilizing a lipid peroxidation MDA assay kit (Beyotime, China), in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. The integrity of the mitochondria was gauged by assessing the mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψ) through the application of the fluorescent dye JC-1 (BD, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting samples were subsequently analyzed through flow cytometry.

## Western blot
Proteins were isolated from NHBE cells and PBMCs using RIPA lysis buffer (Keygentec, China), supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to inhibit protein degradation. The concentration of proteins was determined utilizing the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce, USA). Equal protein quantities were separated via SDS-PAGE gels (Bio-Rad, USA), transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA), and subjected to blocking with 5% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at room temperature for one hour. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting GPX4 (1:1000, Abcam) and DNMT3A (1:1000, Abcam). Following this, membranes were subjected to incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, CST, USA) for one hour. Signal detection was achieved using ECL, and band intensity was quantified utilizing ImageJ software.

## Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells and blood samples employing TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), with subsequent synthesis of cDNA using Primescript RT Reagent (Takara, China). The qRT-PCR was conducted using the StepOne Plus Real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA) in conjunction with SYBR® Premix Ex Taq™ Reagent (Takara, China). The primers utilized for qRT-PCR are detailed in Table . The relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

## Bisulfite-sequencing PCR (BSP)
Genomic DNA was extracted from NHBE cells and PBMCs employing a genomic DNA isolation kit (Biovision, USA). The bisulfite conversion technique was applied to modify the isolated genomic DNA utilizing the EpiTect Fast DNA Bisulfite Kit (Qiagen, Germany). The PCR was performed under the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 4 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 40 s. The analysis of PCR outcomes was conducted using an ABI 7900HT Real-Time PCR System (ABI, USA). The purified PCR products were subsequently cloned into the pTG19-T vector (Generay, China), and ten individual clones were sequenced. The results of Sanger DNA sequencing are shown in Table .

## Sample size calculation
Sample size calculations were conducted using PASS, version 2021 (NCSS Statistical Software). Correlation was examined by Pearson’s correlation. The correlation coefficient between the percentage of GPX4 methylation and the relative IL-6 concentration obtained from the preliminary experiment was 0.6. With a dropout rate of 20%, A sample size of 24 achieves 90% power to detect a difference of −0.6 between the null hypothesis correlation of 0 and the alternative hypothesis correlation of 0.6 using a two-sided hypothesis test with a significance level of 0.05.

## Statistical analysis
All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) derived from three independent experiments. The statistical evaluation was performed through variance tests (two-way ANOVA and one-way ANOVA). Comparisons between data sets were conducted utilizing a two-tailed, unpaired t-test. *p < 0.05 was considered as the significance level; the data were marked as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

## Results

## Inhibition of ferroptosis mitigates PM(2.5)-induced asthma exacerbation
To determine the effect of PM(2.5) on asthma aggravation and its potential link to ferroptosis, we developed a murine model of PM(2.5)-induced asthma exacerbation utilizing CRE as an allergen. HE and PAS staining showed that the CRE-induced mice treated with PM(2.5) (CRE + PM(2.5) group) exhibited significant infiltration of peribronchial and perivascular inflammatory cells, along with pronounced mucus production in the epithelial layers, compared to the PBS group and CRE group. Compared to the CRE + PM(2.5) group, treatment added with Lip-1 (ferroptosis inhibitors) significantly reduced the allergic inflammation described above and suppressed mucus secretion (Fig.  A). Furthermore, the total cell count (Fig.  B), the levels of eosinophils (Fig.  C), neutrophils (Fig.  D), and lymphocytes (Fig.  E) in the BALF were significantly lower in the CRE + PM(2.5) + Lip-1 group compared with the CRE + PM(2.5) group. The total number of macrophages has no significant difference among the groups above (Fig.  F). Additionally, the increased levels of IL-6 and IL-8 in the lung tissue induced by CRE + PM(2.5) were reversed by Lip-1 treatment (Fig.  G). 

## Overexpression of GPX4 reduces ROS levels and inflammatory cytokine release induced by PM(2.5)
PM(2.5) reduced NHBE cell viability in a dose- and time-dependent manner. We found that there was no significant toxicity after 24 and 48 h of PM(2.5) exposure (Figure A, B). However, the cell viability of PM2.5 decreased significantly after the treatment of PM(2.5) for 72 h (Figure C). We then exposed NHBE cells to PM(2.5) for 24 h, resulting in increased secretion of IL-6 and IL-8 in a dose-dependent way (Figure D–E). The PM(2.5) concentration of 100 μg/mL was chosen for subsequent experiments. As illustrated in Fig.  A, qRT-PCR analysis revealed a significant downregulation of GPX4 mRNA expression following PM(2.5) treatment. Immunofluorescence staining confirmed a decrease in GPX4 protein expression in lung tissues in the CRE + PM(2.5) group compared with the CRE group (Fig.  B, upper panel). Also, decreased GPX4 was observed in NHBE cells exposed to PM(2.5) (Fig.  B, lower panel). Cell viability, evaluated using the CCK-8 assay, was compromised following treatment with PM(2.5) or the ferroptosis inducer RSL3 for 72 h. Overexpression of GPX4 significantly restored cell viability under these conditions (Fig.  C). Excessive ROS production under oxidative stress triggers lipid peroxidation, generating MDA, which damages mitochondrial membrane integrity and causes loss of mitochondrial membrane potential. This process promotes iron accumulation, enhances iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, and ultimately induces ferroptosis . Compared with the control group, PM(2.5) induced a high concentration of ROS (Fig.  D and E) and MDA (Fig.  F) and decreased mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig.  G) in the NHBE cells. However, these changes were reversed by the overexpression of GPX4. In addition, GPX4 overexpression can also alleviate the increased levels of IL-6 and IL-8 caused by PM(2.5) treatment (Fig.  H). Collectively, all experimental findings suggest that GPX4 overexpression alleviates PM(2.5)-induced oxidative stress and inflammation in NHBE cells. 

## PM(2.5) concentration influences GPX4 promoter methylation levels
The findings of the genome-wide association study (n = 8397) revealed that the concentration of PM(2.5) in the surrounding environment is intricately linked to DNA methylation, which may serve as a conduit between air pollution and the onset of human diseases . Consequently, we propose that PM(2.5) may modulate the expression of GPX4 by regulating its DNA methylation level. Using the MethPrimer website analysis, we identified the CpG island in the promoter region of GPX4 (Fig.  A). Bisulfite sequencing PCR (BSP) revealed that PM(2.5) exposure significantly elevated the methylation level of the GPX4 promoter (Fig.  B and C). The methylation status of CpG islands within the promoter region consistently governs gene expression by impacting its promoter activity. We found that the GPX4 promoter activity decreased after PM(2.5) treatment (Fig.  D). M.SssI, serving as the hypermethylation agent, was employed to examine the influence of hypermethylation on the GPX4 promoter activity. The findings revealed that DNA hypermethylation considerably diminished the GPX4 promoter activity (Fig.  E). Furthermore, 5-Aza-CdR prompted a significant enhancement in promoter activity in NHBE cells compared to controls (Fig.  F). 

## DNMT3A serves as a crucial gene that regulates GPX4 promoter methylation
To further investigate the molecular mechanism by which PM(2.5) influences the methylation of the GPX4 promoter region, we evaluated the expression of DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), including DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B, in NHBE cells. As illustrated in Fig.  A, PM(2.5) exposure did not affect DNMT1 or DNMT3B expression; however, a notable upregulation of DNMT3A was observed. Silencing DNMT3A decreased DNMT3A expression (Fig.  B) and a corresponding increase in GPX4 expression (Fig.  C). Western blot analysis indicated that DNMT3A knockdown led to a reduction in DNMT3A protein levels alongside an elevation in GPX4 protein levels (Fig.  D). Importantly, the knockdown of DNMT3A abolished PM(2.5)-induced GPX4 hypermethylation (Fig.  E), suggesting DNMT3A is required for PM(2.5)-induced GPX4 hypermethylation. To determine whether DNMT3A was involved in PM(2.5)-induced GPX4 decrease at mRNA levels, we knocked down DNMT3A expression in PM(2.5)-treated cells. The results indicated that DNMT3A knockdown abolished PM(2.5)-induced GPX4 decrease (Fig.  F). These results indicate that PM(2.5) modulates GPX4 expression via DNMT3A-mediated DNA methylation. 

## The methylation level of GPX4 is associated with the secretion of IL-6/IL-8
To confirm the findings in vitro, we further examined the clinical significance of GPX4 methylation in childhood asthma by analyzing peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from patients with varying degrees of asthma severity. Patients’ information is shown in Table . BSP analysis revealed that patients with severe asthma displayed markedly higher GPX4 methylation levels compared to those with mild/moderate asthma (Fig.  A and B). Western blot analysis indicated that GPX4 protein levels were significantly diminished in severe asthma patients (Fig.  C and D). Moreover, IL-6 and IL-8 levels were elevated in the plasma of severe asthma patients (Fig.  E and F). Correlation analysis revealed a positive relationship between GPX4 methylation status and IL-6/8 levels (Fig.  G and H). In contrast, GPX4 methylation status showed a negative correlation with GPX4 protein levels (Fig.  I). 

## Discussion
The increasing prevalence of asthma, particularly in urban areas with high levels of air pollution, necessitates a deeper understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying this respiratory condition . Current literature has established a correlation between PM(2.5) exposure and the exacerbation of asthma symptoms; however, the specific pathways and molecular interactions remain inadequately explored . This study addresses this gap by investigating the role of ferroptosis in PM(2.5)-induced asthma exacerbation, focusing on the epigenetic regulation of the GPX4 gene (Fig.  ). 
We found that the critical role of GPX4 in mediating the effects of PM(2.5) exposure on asthma exacerbation through ferroptosis. Overexpression of GPX4 can reduce ROS, MDA levels, and IL-6/8 levels in the PM(2.5) treatment group, and alleviate the decrease in cell membrane potential levels. The downregulation of GPX4 leads to elevated levels of ROS, which are known to trigger inflammatory responses. This relationship illustrates how the molecular regulation of GPX4 directly influences the inflammatory phenotype observed in asthma exacerbation. The generation of ROS not only directly leads to lipid peroxidation but also damages mitochondrial function, leading to a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential, further aggravating ROS generation and forming a positive feedback loop . Along with mitochondrial dysfunction, ferroptosis is also accompanied by the release of pro-inflammatory factors (such as IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β) . These factors stimulate the generation of more ROS by activating the NF-κB signaling pathway and other mechanisms . Expand the inflammatory response and further promote the occurrence of lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis .
We highlight the potential for therapeutic interventions targeting the DNMT3A-GPX4 axis. By modulating the expression of GPX4, either through demethylation strategies or by inhibiting the activity of DNMT3A, it may be possible to restore the protective effects of GPX4 against oxidative stress and inflammation. Some studies have identified new DNMT3A inhibitors through structure-based virtual screening and biological assays. These small molecule inhibitors inhibit DNMT3A activity by binding to the S-adenosyl-L-methionine (SAM) pocket and have demonstrated promising in vitro inhibitory effects . Additionally, molecular simulation studies have revealed the binding mechanism of selective DNMT3A inhibitors, highlighting the crucial role of Arg688 in DNMT3A’s interaction with inhibitors for selectivity . Collectively, these discoveries establish DNMT3A as a potential target for intervention in asthma exacerbation linked to environmental pollution. However, translating this exciting biology into clinical utility presents significant challenges that must be thoughtfully considered. Systemic inhibition of DNMT3A can have broad epigenetic effects beyond the intended target genes. This raises concerns about genomic instability, disrupted cellular differentiation, and potential carcinogenic risk with long-term use , which is a primary concern for a chronic disease like asthma. The foremost strategy to achieve feasibility is inhalation therapy. Developing inhaled formulations of a DNMT3A inhibitor would maximize lung exposure while minimizing systemic plasma levels, thereby drastically reducing the risk of on-target off-tissue toxicity. Future research should focus on inhaled drug modalities and detailed biomarker studies to harness the potential of this novel therapeutic strategy while successfully navigating its associated challenges.
Several limitations should be noted. First, the method used to estimate individual PM(2.5) exposure may not fully capture personal exposure levels, which can be influenced by indoor air quality and mobility. Second, we focus primarily on GPX4 and DNMT3A, but other genes and pathways involved in ferroptosis, inflammation, or epigenetic regulation may also contribute to PM(2.5)-induced asthma exacerbation. A broader omics approach could provide a more comprehensive view. Finally, the clinical sample size is relatively small, which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings.
In conclusion, we found PM(2.5) exacerbates asthma through a ferroptosis-dependent mechanism linked to GPX4 promoter methylation. Findings reveal that PM(2.5) exposure elevates GPX4 methylation, leading to reduced gene expression and the occurrence of ferroptosis, which correlates with heightened inflammatory responses. We highlight the significance of DNMT3A in mediating these epigenetic modifications, suggesting potential therapeutic targets for asthma management.
