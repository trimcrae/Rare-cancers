## Abstract
Background: Acute lung injury (ALI) is characterized by excessive inflammation and oxidative stress, with macrophages playing pivotal roles in disease progression. The differential survival mechanisms of macrophage subsets during ALI remain poorly understood. Methods: We performed integrated analysis of ALI datasets (GSE11434, GSE237260, GSE264032) and isolated CD14 + macrophages from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of ALI patients. Murine alveolar macrophages (MH-S) were polarized to M1/M2 phenotypes and subjected to ferroptosis induction. HIF-1 A knockdown and lactate supplementation experiments were performed to investigate metabolic regulation. Myeloid-specific HIF-1 A knockout mice were generated and subjected to LPS-induced ALI. Results: Transcriptomic analysis revealed ferroptosis as a key pathway in ALI, with macrophages showing the highest expression of ferroptosis-related genes. ALI-derived macrophages exhibited enhanced ferroptosis-defense gene expression and elevated lactate production. M1 macrophages demonstrated superior ferroptosis resistance compared to M2 macrophages, associated with higher baseline expression of antioxidant genes and enhanced HIF-1 A signaling. Mechanistically, HIF-1 A in M1 macrophages promoted lactate metabolism, which activated ferroptosis-defense genes through histone lactylation. Myeloid-specific HIF-1 A knockout prevented M1 macrophage survival advantage, reduced inflammatory tissue damage, and alleviated ALI severity. Conclusions: HIF-1 A-mediated lactate metabolism confers ferroptosis resistance in M1 macrophages through epigenetic activation of antioxidant defense genes. This metabolic reprogramming represents a novel therapeutic target for modulating macrophage survival and inflammation in ALI.

## Introduction
Acute lung injury (ALI) and its severe form, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), represent critical conditions characterized by rapid onset of widespread inflammation in the lungs, leading to increased pulmonary vascular permeability, loss of aerated tissue, and hypoxemia [ ]. The global incidence of ARDS ranges from 10.1 to 86.2 cases per 100,000 person-years, with mortality rates remaining unacceptably high at 35–46% despite advances in supportive care [ ]. Clinically, ALI/ARDS presents with acute onset of bilateral pulmonary infiltrates, severe hypoxemia (PaO2/FiO2 ratio ≤ 300 mmHg for ALI, ≤ 200 mmHg for ARDS), and absence of left atrial hypertension [ ]. Histologically, the condition is characterized by diffuse alveolar damage featuring hyaline membrane formation, alveolar epithelial cell death, neutrophilic inflammation, and increased alveolar-capillary membrane permeability [ ]. Common etiologies include pneumonia, sepsis, aspiration, and trauma, with the COVID-19 pandemic highlighting the devastating impact of viral-induced ALI [ ]. Current therapeutic approaches remain largely supportive, focusing on lung-protective ventilation strategies, fluid management, and prone positioning, while specific pharmacological interventions targeting the underlying inflammatory cascades have shown limited success in clinical trials [ ]. The lack of effective targeted therapies underscores the urgent need to better understand the molecular mechanisms driving ALI pathogenesis, particularly the role of immune cell dysfunction in perpetuating lung injury [ ].
Oxidative stress and dysregulated inflammation constitute fundamental pathophysiological mechanisms driving the progression and severity of acute lung injury [ ]. During ALI, activated neutrophils, macrophages, and other immune cells generate excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species, overwhelming the lung’s antioxidant defense systems and leading to widespread cellular damage [ ]. This oxidative burden directly damages the alveolar-capillary barrier by promoting lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA strand breaks, ultimately compromising epithelial and endothelial cell integrity [ ]. Concurrently, the inflammatory response becomes self-perpetuating through the activation of multiple signaling pathways, including nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), and inflammasomes, which drive the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [ ]. The crosstalk between oxidative stress and inflammation creates a vicious cycle where ROS activates inflammatory pathways, while inflammatory mediators further stimulate ROS production, amplifying tissue damage and impairing the resolution of inflammation [ ]. Alveolar macrophages, as the primary resident immune cells in the lung, play a pivotal role in orchestrating these processes, with their polarization state determining whether they promote tissue damage through pro-inflammatory M1 activation or facilitate repair through anti-inflammatory M2 responses [ – ]. Understanding the molecular mechanisms that regulate macrophage function and their metabolic reprogramming during ALI represents a critical avenue for developing targeted therapeutic interventions.
Macrophage polarization plays a crucial role in determining the balance between lung injury progression and tissue repair, with distinct M1 and M2 subsets exhibiting opposing functional characteristics during ALI pathogenesis [ ]. M1 macrophages, also known as classically activated macrophages, are characterized by their pro-inflammatory phenotype and are typically activated by interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [ ]. These cells produce high levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), while exhibiting enhanced phagocytic and microbicidal activities that contribute to pathogen clearance but also promote tissue damage [ ]. In contrast, M2 macrophages, or alternatively activated macrophages, can be further subdivided into M2a, M2b, and M2c subtypes, each with distinct anti-inflammatory and tissue repair functions [ ]. M2 macrophages are characterized by the expression of arginase-1 (Arg1), IL-10, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and various growth factors that promote angiogenesis, collagen synthesis, and epithelial cell proliferation [ ]. During the resolution phase of ALI, the transition from M1 to M2 polarization is essential for clearing apoptotic cells, reducing inflammation, and restoring tissue homeostasis [ ]. Dysregulation of this M1/M2 balance, with prolonged M1 activation or impaired M2 responses, contributes to persistent inflammation, delayed wound healing, and progression to pulmonary fibrosis [ , ]. However, how these two macrophage subsets respond to oxidative stress conditions during lung injury and whether oxidative stress differentially affects their polarization and functional capabilities remains poorly understood.
The objective of this study was to investigate how M1 and M2 macrophages differentially respond to oxidative stress and ferroptosis during acute lung injury, and to determine whether these responses relate to their differential survival and functional imbalance in mediating inflammatory damage. Through bioinformatics analysis of published scRNA datasets, we found that M1 and M2 macrophages exhibit distinct expression patterns of ferroptosis-related genes and antioxidant enzymes during acute lung injury. To validate these findings clinically, we isolated CD14 + macrophages from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of ALI patients and characterized their ferroptosis responses. We then employed murine alveolar macrophages (MH-S) polarized to M1/M2 phenotypes to investigate their differential sensitivity to ferroptosis induction and elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms through HIF-1 A knockdown and lactate supplementation experiments. Finally, we generated myeloid-specific HIF-1 A knockout mice and subjected them to LPS-induced ALI to assess the role of HIF-1 A in macrophage survival and tissue damage in vivo. The findings provide important insights into the role of HIF-1 A-mediated lactate metabolism in conferring ferroptosis resistance to M1 macrophages through histone lactylation, and may inform targeted therapeutic strategies for modulating macrophage survival and inflammatory responses in ALI/ARDS patients.

## Materials and Methods

## Bioinformatics Analysis
Bulk RNA-seq datasets GSE11434 and GSE237260, and single-cell RNA-seq dataset GSE264032 were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Quality control analysis was performed on GSE264032, yielding 13,926 cells which were clustered using Leiden algorithm with a resolution of 0.3 and visualized using UMAP projection. Cell type annotation was performed using ACT (http://xteam.xbio.top/ACT/). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (version 1.38.3) for GSE237260 and limma package for GSE11434, with criteria of |log2FoldChange| > 1 and adjusted p-value < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler (version 4.6.2) with p.adjust < 0.05 and count > 1. Intersection analysis of differentially expressed genes across datasets was visualized using ggvenn package. Protein-protein interaction networks were constructed using STRING database and visualized using Cytoscape.

## Study Participants and Sample Collection
The study included 10 participants: 5 patients with acute lung injury (ALI) and 5 healthy controls. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the institutional review board of medical research ethics (Approval number 2025-03-007-K001). Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples were collected following standard clinical protocols and immediately processed for cell isolation. CD14 + monocytes/macrophages were isolated from BALF samples using the EasySep™ Human Monocyte Isolation Kit (Cat#100–0697, STEMCELL Technologies) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Isolated cells were resuspended at 1 × 10^6 cells/mL in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS.

## Cell Line and Culture Conditions
Murine alveolar macrophage cell line MH-S (Cat#iCell-M078, CellVerse) was cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Cat#11875093, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cat#10270106, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Cat#15140122, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Cells were seeded at 2 × 10^5 cells/well in 6-well plates for most experiments. For M1 polarization, MH-S cells were treated with 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Cat#L2630, Sigma-Aldrich) and 20 ng/mL interferon-γ (IFN-γ, Cat#315-05, PeproTech) for 48 h. For M2 polarization, cells were treated with 20 ng/mL interleukin-4 (IL-4, Cat#214 − 14, PeproTech) for 48 h.

## Pharmacological Treatments
Ferroptosis was induced using 500 µM ferrous sulfate (FeSO4, Cat#F7002, Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h. Ferroptosis inhibition was achieved using 1 µM Liproxstatin-1 (Cat#SML1414, Sigma-Aldrich) co-treatment. Lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) was inhibited using 10 µM AZ-33 (Cat#S0108, SelleckChem) for 24 h. Exogenous lactate supplementation was performed using 10 mM sodium lactate (Cat#L7022, Sigma-Aldrich).

## siRNA Transfection
For HIF-1α knockdown, cells (1 × 10^5 cells/well in 12-well plates) were transfected with 50 nM HIF-1α siRNA (Cat#sc-35562, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) or negative control siRNA (Cat#sc-37007, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Cat#13778150, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for analysis.

## Flow Cytometry Analysis
For flow cytometry analysis, 1 × 10^6 cells were harvested and washed twice with PBS containing 2% FBS. Cells were stained with appropriate antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark, washed twice, and resuspended in 300 µL PBS for analysis. Macrophage polarization was assessed using PE-Cy™7 Mouse Anti-Human CD86 (1:100, Cat#561128, BD Biosciences) and APC Anti-human CD206 (1:100, Cat#321109, BioLegend). For mouse cells, PE-Cy™7 Anti-Mouse CD86 (1:100, Cat#105014, BioLegend) and CD206-APC (1:100, Cat#17–2061-82, Invitrogen). were used. Cell viability was determined using Live/Dead Viability/Cytotoxicity Kit (Cat#L3224, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 2 µM calcein AM and 4 µM ethidium homodimer-1. Lipid peroxidation was assessed using 5 µM BODIPY™ 581/591 C11 (Cat#D3861, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at 37 °C. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) production was measured using 10 µM 2’,7’-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFDA, Cat#D6883, Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at 37 °C. All flow cytometry data were acquired using BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software v10.8.

## Protein Analysis
Total protein was extracted from 2 × 10^6 cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Cat#89900, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Cat#11697498001, Roche) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Cat#04906845001, Roche). Protein concentration was determined using BCA protein assay kit (Cat#23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Primary antibodies used included: HIF-1 A (1:1000, Cat#14179, Cell Signaling Technology), SLC7A11 (1:1000, Cat#12691, Cell Signaling Technology), HMOX1 (1:1000, Cat#82206, Cell Signaling Technology), Histone H3 (1:2000, Cat#4499, Cell Signaling Technology), Lactyl-Histone H3 (Lys14) (1:1000, Cat#PA5-116897, Invitrogen), HIF-2 alpha (1: 1500, Cat#57921, Cell Signaling Technology), and β-actin (1:5000, Cat#3700, Cell Signaling Technology). Secondary antibodies included HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, Cat#7074, Cell Signaling Technology) and anti-mouse IgG (1:5000, Cat#7076, Cell Signaling Technology). Blots were developed using ECL reagent (Cat#32106, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and imaged using ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Bio-Rad).

## RNA Extraction and qPCR
Total RNA was extracted from 1 × 10^6 cells using TRIzol reagent (Cat#15596026, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg total RNA using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Cat#4368814, Applied Biosystems). Quantitative PCR was performed using 20 ng cDNA template with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Cat#4309155, Applied Biosystems) on ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR System. Each reaction was performed in triplicate with β-actin as internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method.

## Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
ChIP assays were performed using SimpleChIP® Plus Sonication Chromatin IP Kit (Cat#56383, Cell Signaling Technology) with 4 × 10^6 cells per immunoprecipitation. Chromatin was cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min and sonicated to generate 200–500 bp fragments. Immunoprecipitation was performed overnight at 4 °C using 5 µg of antibodies: Histone H3 (Cat#4620, Cell Signaling Technology), Lactyl-Histone H3 (Lys14) (Cat#PA5-116897, Invitrogen), and normal rabbit IgG (Cat#2729, Cell Signaling Technology) as negative control. Immunoprecipitated DNA was analyzed by qPCR using primers targeting promoter regions (−500 to + 200 bp relative to transcription start site) of ferroptosis-defense genes. Results were normalized to input DNA and expressed as fold enrichment over IgG control.

## Biochemical Assays
Lactate levels were measured using Lactate Assay Kit (Cat#MAK064, Sigma-Aldrich) from 1 × 10^6 cells. Free iron levels were determined using Iron Assay Kit (Cat#MAK025, Sigma-Aldrich) from 2 × 10^6 cells. Cell cytotoxicity was assessed using LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Cat#37291, Cell Signaling Technologies) from 50 µL culture supernatant. All assays were performed according to manufacturer’s instructions.

## Electron Microscopy
Cells (2 × 10^6) were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (Cat#16220, Electron Microscopy Sciences) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 2 h at 4 °C. Samples were post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide (Cat#19110, Electron Microscopy Sciences) for 1 h, dehydrated through graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, 100%), and embedded in epoxy resin (Cat#14120, Electron Microscopy Sciences). Ultrathin Sect. (70 nm) were cut using ultramicrotome (Leica UC7), stained with 2% uranyl acetate and 0.2% lead citrate, and examined using transmission electron microscope (JEOL JEM-1400Plus) at 80 kV.

## Animal Studies
All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval KMMUD2025050). HIF1A flox/flox mice and LysM-Cre C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences (Guangzhou, China). Myeloid-specific HIF-1α knockout mice were generated by crossing HIF1A flox/flox mice with LysM-Cre mice. Mice were backcrossed to > 99% C57BL/6 background before experimental use. Genotyping was performed by PCR using tail DNA with specific primers for Cre recombinase and floxed HIF1A alleles.
Eight-week-old WT or HIF1A KO male mice (20–25 g, n = 6 per group) were subjected to LPS-induced acute lung injury by intratracheal instillation of 2 mg/kg LPS (Cat#L2630, Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in 50 µL sterile saline under 2–3% isoflurane anesthesia. Control mice received equal volume of sterile saline. Mice were euthanized 4 days post-treatment by CO2 asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation for analysis. BALF was collected by cannulating the trachea and lavaging the lungs three times with 1 mL sterile PBS. Total BALF volume was centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min, and cell pellets were resuspended for analysis. Lung tissues were either fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histology or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for molecular analysis. F4/80 + macrophages were isolated from BALF using MagniSort™ Mouse F4/80 Positive Selection Kit (Cat#8802-6863-74, Invitrogen) according to manufacturer’s protocol. Isolated cells (typically 1–2 × 10^5 cells per mouse) were immediately processed for flow cytometry or molecular analysis. Lung tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm thickness, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histological assessment. BALF cytokine levels (TNF-α, IL-1β) were measured using mouse cytokine ELISA kits (Cat#88–7324-88, Cat#88–7013-88, Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Lung edema was assessed by measuring the wet/dry weight ratio. Briefly, the right lung was excised, weighed immediately to obtain wet weight, and then dried in an oven at 60 °C for 72 h until constant weight was achieved to obtain dry weight. The wet/dry ratio was calculated to evaluate pulmonary edema severity. Alveolar-capillary barrier permeability was assessed by measuring albumin concentration in BALF using the Mouse Albumin ELISA Kit (Cat#ab108792, Abcam) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Neutrophil infiltration was assessed by measuring myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity in lung tissue homogenates using the MPO Assay Kit (Cat#ab105136, Abcam) following manufacturer’s protocol.

## Statistical Analysis
All cell experiments were repeated at least three times independently. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Sample sizes of animal study were determined based on power analysis (α = 0.05, β = 0.2) and previous literature. Statistical comparisons were performed using unpaired Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple group comparisons using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant, and all statistical tests were two-tailed.

## Results

## Transcriptomic Analysis Reveals Oxidative Stress and Ferroptosis-Related Gene Expression Changes in Acute Lung Injury
To comprehensively characterize the molecular landscape of acute lung injury (ALI), we performed differential gene expression analysis on two independent bulk RNA-seq datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO): GSE11434 and GSE237260. Differential gene expression analysis revealed 332 upregulated and 621 downregulated genes in GSE11434, and 147 upregulated and 39 downregulated genes in GSE237260, as visualized in volcano plots (Fig.  A). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that the most significantly enriched biological processes were related to response to oxidative stress, regulation of smooth muscle cell proliferation, and muscle cell proliferation (Fig.  B, upper panel). Notably, KEGG pathway analysis identified several critical signaling cascades including the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, HIF-1 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, and ferroptosis (Fig.  B, lower panel), suggesting that oxidative stress responses and regulated cell death mechanisms may be involved in ALI pathogenesis.
 

## Single-Cell RNA Sequencing Identifies Macrophages as Key Cell Population in ALI and Reveals Conserved Ferroptosis-Related Gene Signatures
Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis of the GSE264032 dataset identified 13,926 cells across 10 distinct cell types, with macrophages representing the most abundant immune cell population (1366 cells) in ALI samples (Fig.  C). Since macrophages became the most abundant immune cells, this highlights the critical roles of their persistence in ALI pathogenesis. We then identified macrophage-related genes by performing intersection analysis of differentially expressed genes from the bulk RNA-seq datasets (GSE11434 and GSE237260) with single-cell macrophage DEGs, revealing 11 overlapping genes between all three datasets (Fig.  D). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of these intersected genes highlighted ferroptosis as the most significantly enriched pathway, along with other key pathways including p53 signaling, NF-kappa B signaling, TNF signaling and HIF-1 signaling pathways (Fig.  E). Comparative analysis of fold changes for these intersected genes across all three datasets revealed that the majority of genes showed consistent upregulation in ALI, especially in scRNA-macrophage samples, indicating macrophages as the major contributors to ferroptosis-related gene expression (Fig.  F). Cell-type specific expression analysis in scRNA data further confirmed the dramatic upregulation of these ferroptosis-related genes in ALI macrophages compared to other cell types (Fig.  G), indicating that ferroptosis regulation may contribute to ALI-associated macrophage survival and function.

## Isolation and Characterization of Alveolar Macrophages from BALF Samples Reveals Enhanced Ferroptosis-Defense Gene Expression in ALI
To validate our bioinformatics findings, we isolated CD14 + macrophages from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples using anti-CD14 magnetic beads from both control and ALI patients. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a shift toward pro-inflammatory M1 polarization in ALI macrophages, with increased CD86 + cells and decreased CD206 + cells compared to controls (Fig.  A). Cell viability assessment using live/dead staining demonstrated reduced viability in ALI-derived macrophages, particularly in the M2 population (Fig.  B). Lipid peroxidation analysis using BODIPY C11 staining revealed increased oxidative lipid levels in ALI-associated M2 macrophages (Fig.  C), indicating higher propensity of M2 macrophages towards ferroptosis. Overall, there was elevated free iron levels (Fig.  D) and enhanced ROS production (Fig.  E) in ALI-associated macrophages. Quantitative PCR analysis demonstrated significant upregulation of anti-ferroptosis defense genes in ALI macrophages, including Slc7a11, Hmox1, Mt1, Txnrd1, and PIK3CA (Fig.  F). Western blot analysis confirmed increased protein expression of key ferroptosis regulators (Slc7a11 and Hmox1) in ALI samples, as well as the HIF-1 A protein involved in HIF-1 signaling (Fig.  G). However, HIF-2α expression did not show significant changes between ALI and control samples. Additionally, lactate measurement revealed elevated lactate production activity in ALI macrophages (Fig.  H). Based on the increased oxidative burden in ALI macrophages and the discrepancy of survival and lipid peroxidation status between M1 and M2 macrophages, we hypothesize that metabolic reprogramming may serve as a protective mechanism against ferroptosis in surviving macrophages. We further explored HIF-1 A-dependent lactate metabolism as a potential survival pathway in ALI-associated macrophages.
 

## Ferroptosis Induction Differentially Affects M1 and M2 Macrophage Survival
To investigate the differential sensitivity of macrophage subsets to ferroptosis, we treated human lung BALF-derived macrophages with FeSO4 to induce ferroptosis, with or without the ferroptosis inhibitor Liproxstatin-1. M1 macrophages maintained higher viability compared to M2 macrophages following ferroptosis induction, and this differential survival was rescued by Liproxstatin-1 treatment (Fig.  A). Lipid peroxidation analysis using BODIPY C11 staining confirmed that M2 macrophages were more susceptible to oxidative lipid damage than M1 macrophages (Fig.  B), demonstrating that M2 macrophages exhibit greater vulnerability to ferroptosis-mediated cell death than M1 macrophages.
 To further validate our findings, we used the murine alveolar macrophage cell line MH-S and induced polarization to M1 (LPS + IFN-γ treatment) and M2 (IL-4 treatment) phenotypes. Flow cytometric analysis confirmed successful M1 and M2 polarization (Fig.  A). Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that M1 macrophages exhibited higher baseline expression of ferroptosis-defense genes including Slc7a11, Ptgs2, Hmox1, Mt1, Txnrd1, and PIK3CA compared to M2 macrophages (Fig.  B). Western blot analysis confirmed differential protein expression patterns, with M1 macrophages showing enhanced HIF1A, Hmox1 and Slc7a11 expression, with no significant changes observed in HIF-2α (Fig.  C).
 Following ferroptosis induction with FeSO4 treatment, LDH release assays demonstrated that M2 macrophages exhibited greater cytotoxicity compared to M1 macrophages, which was rescued by Liproxstatin-1 co-treatment (Fig.  D). BODIPY C11 staining confirmed higher oxidative lipid damage in M2 macrophages following FeSO4 treatment, with protective effects observed upon Liproxstatin-1 treatment (Fig.  E). Electron microscopy revealed characteristic ferroptotic morphological changes, including mitochondrial shrinkage and cristae damage, which were more pronounced in M2 macrophages and prevented by Liproxstatin-1 treatment (Fig.  F). These results suggest that M1 macrophages possess enhanced ferroptosis resistance compared to M2 macrophages, as well as enhanced HIF-1 signaling.

## Lactate Metabolism Regulates Ferroptosis Resistance Through Epigenetic Modifications
To investigate the role of lactate metabolism in ferroptosis resistance, we treated polarized MH-S macrophages with AZ-33, a potent lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) inhibitor. Lactate production assays revealed that M1 macrophages produced higher baseline lactate levels compared to M2 macrophages, and AZ-33 treatment effectively reduced lactate production in both subsets (Fig.  A). Quantitative PCR analysis demonstrated that LDHA inhibition significantly downregulated ferroptosis-defense genes, with more pronounced effects observed in M1 macrophages (Fig.  B). Western blot analysis confirmed that AZ-33 treatment reduced protein expression of Slc7a11 and Hmox1 in both macrophage subsets, while HIF1A levels remained unchanged in M1 macrophages, indicating that lactate metabolism operates downstream of HIF-1 A signaling (Fig.  C).
 To explore the epigenetic mechanisms underlying lactate-mediated gene regulation, we performed lactyl-histone 3 (Lys14) analysis and found that AZ-33 treatment reduced H3K14 acetylation levels in M1 macrophages (Fig.  D). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP-qPCR) analysis using histone H3 and lactyl-histone H3 antibodies revealed that while total histone H3 occupancy remained unchanged across all treatment groups, H3K14 lactylation at the promoter regions of ferroptosis-defense genes (Slc7a11, Hmox1, Mt1, Txnrd1, PIK3CA) was significantly elevated in M1 macrophages and dramatically reduced following AZ-33 treatment in both M1 and M2 subsets (Fig.  E). These findings suggest that lactate metabolism may promote ferroptosis resistance through histone lactylation-mediated epigenetic activation of antioxidant defense genes.

## HIF-1 A Regulates Ferroptosis Resistance Through Lactate-Mediated Histone Lactylation
To investigate the upstream regulation of lactate metabolism in ferroptosis resistance, we first performed HIF-1 A knockdown experiments in MH-S macrophages using siRNA. Western blot analysis confirmed effective HIF-1 A knockdown, which resulted in reduced expression of downstream targets Slc7a11 and Hmox1, particularly in M1 macrophages (Fig.  A). Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that HIF-1 A silencing significantly downregulated ferroptosis-defense genes in M1 macrophages (Fig.  B). HIF-1 A knockdown also reduced lactate levels in M1 macrophages (Fig.  C), and decreased H3K14 lactylation levels (Fig.  D). ChIP-qPCR analysis revealed that HIF-1 A knockdown significantly reduced H3K14 lactylation at the promoter regions of ferroptosis-defense genes (Slc7a11, Hmox1, Mt1, Txnrd1, PIK3CA) while leaving total histone H3 occupancy unaffected (Fig.  E). These results suggest that HIF-1 A acts as an upstream regulator of lactate metabolism, which in turn promotes histone lactylation-mediated activation of ferroptosis-defense genes in M1 macrophages.
 To confirm that lactate acts downstream of HIF-1 A in conferring ferroptosis resistance, we performed rescue experiments by supplementing lactate in HIF-1 A knockdown M1 macrophages. Lactate production assays confirmed that exogenous lactate supplementation restored lactate levels in HIF-1 A knockdown cells (Fig.  A), and also rescued the expression of ferroptosis-defense genes that were downregulated following HIF-1 A silencing (Fig.  B). Western blot analysis confirmed that lactate treatment restored antioxidant protein levels and enhanced H3K14 lactylation in HIF-1 A knockdown cells (Fig.  C). Following FeSO4 induction, LDH release assays demonstrated that lactate supplementation significantly reduced cytotoxicity in HIF-1 A knockdown M1 macrophages (Fig.  D). Similarly, ROS production was decreased (Fig.  E) and lipid peroxidation levels were reduced (Fig.  F) upon lactate supplementation, indicating restored ferroptosis resistance. Electron microscopy revealed that lactate supplementation prevented ferroptotic morphological changes including mitochondrial damage in HIF-1 A knockdown cells (Fig.  G). These results together indicate that lactate acts as a key downstream effector of HIF-1 A signaling in promoting ferroptosis resistance through histone lactylation-mediated activation of antioxidant defense genes.
 

## Macrophage-Specific HIF-1 A Knockout Prevents M1 Survival and Shows Protective Effects in an ALI mouse model
Next, we generated myeloid-specific HIF-1 A knockout mice by crossing HIF1A flox mice with LysM-Cre C57BL/6 mice. Mice were subjected to LPS-induced ALI, and lung tissue analysis revealed reduced tissue damage in HIF-1 A knockout mice compared to wild-type controls following LPS treatment (Fig.  A). Quantitative assessment further confirmed these protective effects: the lung wet/dry ratio was significantly lower in HIF-1 A knockout mice following LPS challenge, indicating reduced pulmonary edema. BALF albumin concentration was also markedly decreased in knockout mice compared to wild-type controls after LPS treatment, reflecting preserved alveolar-capillary barrier integrity. Additionally, MPO activity in lung tissue was significantly attenuated in HIF-1 A knockout mice, indicating reduced neutrophil infiltration (Fig.  A). Together, these quantitative data provide comprehensive evidence that myeloid-specific HIF-1 A deletion protects against the cardinal features of ALI.
 Flow cytometric analysis of BALF-derived F4/80 + macrophages showed altered polarization patterns, with HIF-1 A knockout mice exhibiting reduced M1% (CD86 + cells) and relatively preserved M2% (CD206 + cells) in the LPS-induced ALI group (Fig.  B). qPCR analysis of additional polarization markers further confirmed these findings, demonstrating that LPS strongly induced M1 markers (iNOS and IL-1β) in wild-type mice, while HIF-1 A knockout significantly attenuated this induction. Conversely, M2 markers (Arg1 and Ym1) were remained similarly suppressed in both wild-type and HIF-1 A knockout mice following LPS challenge (Fig.  B). Together, these data indicate that myeloid-specific HIF-1 A deletion selectively impairs M1 macrophage polarization and survival. Consistently, free iron levels (Fig.  C) and ROS production (Fig.  D) were elevated in HIF-1 A knockout macrophages following LPS treatment. Quantitative PCR analysis demonstrated reduced expression of ferroptosis-defense genes in HIF-1 A knockout macrophages (Fig.  E), while Western blot analysis confirmed decreased antioxidant protein levels in the absence of HIF-1 A (Fig.  F). Lactate production was significantly impaired in HIF-1 A knockout macrophages (Fig.  G), and cell viability staining revealed that HIF-1 A knockout prevented M1 macrophage survival advantage in LPS models, with both M1 and M2 macrophages showing similar reduced viability (Fig.  H). Lipid peroxidation analysis confirmed increased oxidative lipid levels in HIF-1 A knockout M1 macrophages (Fig.  I), and BALF cytokine analysis showed reduced inflammatory markers in HIF-1 A knockout mice following LPS treatment (Fig.  J). These results collectively suggest that macrophage-specific HIF-1 A knockout attenuates the survival advantage of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages and may alleviate inflammatory tissue damage during acute lung injury.

## Discussion
In this study, we provide evidence that M1 macrophages exhibit enhanced ferroptosis resistance compared to M2 macrophages during ALI, which is associated with HIF-1 A-mediated lactate metabolism. Our transcriptomic analysis identified ferroptosis and HIF-1 signaling as enriched pathways in ALI, with clinical validation suggesting that surviving macrophages display enhanced ferroptosis-defense mechanisms and elevated lactate production. Mechanistically, HIF-1 A appears to promote lactate metabolism in M1 macrophages, contributing to epigenetic activation of antioxidant defense genes through histone lactylation and potentially conferring protection against oxidative cell death. Using myeloid-specific HIF-1 A knockout mice, we observed that loss of this survival advantage in M1 macrophages was associated with reduced inflammatory tissue damage and attenuated ALI severity. These findings reveal a metabolic-epigenetic axis that may contribute to macrophage subset survival during lung injury and suggest HIF-1 A-lactate signaling as a potential therapeutic target for modulating inflammatory responses in ALI.
Our findings align with emerging evidence that the dynamic changes of macrophage phenotypes in pathophysiological conditions are associated with metabolic reprogramming, with M1 macrophages preferentially relying on glycolysis and M2 macrophages relying on mitochondrial-dependent oxidative phosphorylation [ – ]. Previous studies have established that HIF-1 A-induced glycolysis is essential for M1 macrophage activation and inflammatory responses, with the JNK/COX-2/HIF-1 A axis promoting glycolytic shifts that sustain pro-inflammatory phenotypes [ – ]. While these studies focused on glucose metabolism and initial polarization, our work extends this paradigm by demonstrating that HIF-1 A also orchestrates lactate metabolism as a survival mechanism that operates independently of polarization status. The concept that metabolic reprogramming dictates macrophage functional states has been well-documented across various inflammatory diseases and cancer contexts, where metabolic shifts influence both polarization and therapeutic responses [ – ]. Our study provides a novel perspective by identifying lactate-mediated histone modifications as a previously unrecognized layer of metabolic control that specifically governs ferroptosis resistance rather than metabolic polarization per se. This finding is particularly relevant given the multifaceted role of macrophages as orchestrators of tissue repair, where their survival and persistence can determine whether inflammatory responses resolve appropriately or contribute to sustained tissue damage [ ]. The identification of this metabolic-epigenetic survival mechanism offers new therapeutic opportunities for modulating macrophage longevity and inflammatory outcomes in ALI.
The role of HIF-1 A in mediating ferroptosis resistance extends beyond macrophages and has been extensively documented across various pathological contexts, including solid tumors, cervical cancer, gliomas, and acute kidney injury [ – ]. In cancer cells, HIF-1 A drives ferroptosis resistance through multiple mechanisms including lactate production with SLC1A1 activation, upregulation of KDM4A-mediated H3K9me3 modifications, and PI3K/AKT pathway-dependent SLC7A11 expression [ – ]. Additionally, HIF-1 A can activate the Hippo-YAP signaling pathway and establish mitochondrial-dependent protective mechanisms against ferroptosis [ , ]. Our findings in M1 macrophages during ALI align with these established mechanisms while revealing a novel lactate-lactylation axis that induces ferroptosis defense gene expression (such as SLC7A11, PI3KCA, Hmox1). This suggests that HIF-1 A-mediated ferroptosis resistance represents a conserved cellular survival strategy across diverse cell types and disease contexts.
Histone lactylation has emerged as a critical epigenetic mechanism governing gene expression and functional changes in macrophages, with lactate serving as both a metabolic byproduct and a signaling molecule that directly modifies chromatin structure [ – ]. This post-translational modification has been shown to regulate diverse cellular processes, from hepatic stellate cell activation in liver fibrosis through hexokinase 2-mediated pathways to gene expression changes during ischemia-induced muscle regeneration in macrophages [ , , ]. Our findings extend this growing body of evidence by demonstrating that histone lactylation specifically governs the expression of ferroptosis defense genes in M1 macrophages during ALI. The lactate-dependent epigenetic activation of antioxidant pathways represents a novel mechanism by which metabolic reprogramming translates into functional survival advantages. This is particularly significant given that histone lactylation in macrophages has been shown to be predictive for gene expression changes during tissue repair processes, suggesting that this epigenetic modification serves as a molecular switch that coordinates metabolic status with transcriptional responses [ , ]. Our identification of ferroptosis defense genes as direct targets of lactylation-mediated regulation provides new insights into how M1 macrophages leverage metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk to survive oxidative stress environments. While we acknowledge that HIF-1 A-mediated glycolysis and histone lactylation are established phenomena, our study distinctly advances the field by demonstrating their functional convergence in regulating ferroptosis resistance specifically in ALI-associated macrophages. The causal relationship between lactylation and the survival phenotype is supported by several lines of evidence: LDHA inhibition abolished both H3K14 lactylation at ferroptosis-defense gene promoters and their expression without affecting HIF-1 A levels, and exogenous lactate supplementation rescued ferroptosis resistance in HIF-1 A-deficient cells. These findings suggest that lactylation functions as a direct mediator rather than a correlative epiphenomenon of glycolytic flux. While the survival advantage of M1 macrophages may initially benefit antimicrobial defense during early ALI, the preferential survival and expansion of M1 macrophages ultimately contributes to sustained inflammatory damage by prolonging pro-inflammatory responses beyond the acute phase. This highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting the HIF-1 A-lactate-lactylation pathway to promote timely inflammatory resolution and prevent excessive M1-driven tissue damage in ALI.
Our findings that M1 macrophages exhibit enhanced ferroptosis resistance aligns with emerging evidence demonstrating differential ferroptosis susceptibility between macrophage subtypes, where M2 macrophages are generally more vulnerable to ferroptotic death due to higher polyunsaturated fatty acid levels, increased 15-lipoxygenase expression, and differential GPX4 autophagic degradation [ , ]. This phenotype-specific ferroptosis susceptibility has significant pathological implications, as demonstrated in rheumatoid arthritis where focal iron overload preferentially induces M2 ferroptosis while sparing M1 macrophages, thereby skewing the inflammatory balance toward sustained tissue damage [ ]. Similarly, in ulcerative colitis and nasopharyngeal carcinoma, the ERK-cPLA2-ACSL4 axis mediates M2 ferroptosis while ACSL4 promotes M1 polarization, creating a feed-forward loop that perpetuates inflammation [ , ]. Our identification of the HIF-1 A-lactate-lactylation pathway as a mechanism underlying M1 ferroptosis resistance provides molecular insights into this differential susceptibility pattern. Therapeutic interventions targeting ferroptosis, such as HMGB1/TLR4/STAT3 axis inhibition or APOC1 suppression, have shown promise in promoting M2 survival and M1-to-M2 transformation, suggesting that modulating macrophage ferroptosis susceptibility represents a viable strategy for inflammatory disease treatment [ , ].
It is important to acknowledge that multiple cell death pathways, including pyroptosis, necroptosis, apoptosis, ferroptosis, and PANoptosis, are directly implicated in ALI/ARDS pathogenesis [ ]. Inflammatory pyroptosis triggered by NLRP3 canonical inflammasome activation plays a central role in sepsis-related ALI/ARDS, where the TWIK2 potassium efflux channel mediates inflammasome-induced inflammation [ ] and subsequent IL-1β release suppresses VE-cadherin transcription, contributing to lung injury [ ]. Noncanonical caspase-11-mediated endothelial pyroptosis also underlies endotoxemia-induced lung injury [ ], with caspase-7 serving to repair gasdermin pores [ ]. There may be potential crosstalk between ferroptosis and pyroptosis during ALI, as oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation can activate inflammasome signaling, while inflammatory cytokines may influence iron metabolism and antioxidant capacity. Our findings suggest that HIF-1 A-mediated metabolic reprogramming may have broader implications for multiple cell death modalities during ALI, warranting future investigation into these interconnected pathways.
Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our clinical validation cohort was limited to 5 ALI patients and 5 controls, and larger prospective studies are needed to confirm the translational relevance of our findings. Second, the CD14 + population isolated from BALF represents a heterogeneous mixture of monocytes and macrophages, and future single-cell analyses would provide more precise characterization of subset-specific responses. While MH-S cells are well-established models for alveolar macrophage research, primary bone marrow-derived or alveolar macrophages may better recapitulate in vivo metabolic properties. Furthermore, FeSO4-induced ferroptosis is widely used, the complex oxidative environment of the injured lung involves multiple concurrent stressors that are not fully captured by this model. While our pharmacological approaches using LDHA inhibition and lactate supplementation support a causal role for lactate metabolism, genetic manipulation of lactate transporters or LDHA would provide additional mechanistic confirmation. Of note, the protective effects of myeloid-specific HIF-1 A knockout should be interpreted considering the dual roles of macrophages during lung injury, where M1 activation is essential for early host defense but persistent accumulation exacerbates tissue damage. Our LPS model represents sterile inflammation, and caution is warranted when extrapolating to infectious models where early M1 responses are critical for pathogen clearance. Therapeutic targeting of HIF-1 A-lactate signaling would therefore require careful consideration of timing and disease context.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, our study reveals a novel HIF-1 A-lactate-lactylation axis that confers ferroptosis resistance specifically to M1 macrophages during ALI, providing mechanistic insights into how metabolic reprogramming drives macrophage survival and inflammatory persistence. Future investigations should focus on developing targeted therapeutics that selectively disrupt this pathway to promote timely M1-to-M2 transition, while exploring the translational potential of lactylation inhibitors as adjuvant therapies for ALI and other inflammatory diseases where macrophage phenotype switching is therapeutically beneficial.
