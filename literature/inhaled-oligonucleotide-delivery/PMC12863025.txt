## ABSTRACT
Seed oil accumulation is an important process affecting seed quality, and regulatory factors modulating this process remain less understood, especially in soybean. In this study, through RNA‐seq and gene co‐expression network analysis, we identified a single MYB (Myeloblastosis)‐type transcription factor GmMYB331, which promotes seed oil accumulation in soybean seeds and enhances seed size/weight as well. Transgenic soybean plants with mild GmMYB331 overexpression showed higher total fatty acid contents in seeds and higher seed yield per plant compared to the control plants. In contrast, transgenic soybean plants with strong GmMYB331 overexpression showed only increased seed size/weight but much reduced seed yield per plant, along with an altered plant architecture. Knocking out GmMYB331 by CRISPR/Cas9 produced mutants with less total fatty acids, smaller seeds, and less seed weight, indicating that the gene is required for oil accumulation and seed size/weight control. GmMYB331 may achieve these functions by differential binding to the gene promoters and activation of the downstream genes, namely, GmOLEO1/2/4 for oil accumulation in mild overexpressing plants and GmCYCD2;2 for seed size/weight increase in strong overexpressing plants. Our study reveals a possible mechanism involving differential regulation by GmMYB331 toward oil accumulation and seed size/weight increase. Manipulation of the GmMYB331 gene may facilitate breeding for high‐oil and/or ‐yield soybean cultivars.
Keywords: MYB, oil content, seed size/weight, seed traits, transcription factor

The soybean transcription factor GmMYB331 regulates oil content and seed size. Moderate expression of GmMYB331 significantly enhanced seed oil accumulation, and strong expression promoted increased seed size. This research provides a genetic resource and theoretical foundation for high‐yield and quality breeding in soybean.

## INTRODUCTION
Soybean is an important crop for oil, protein, food, and feed. The developmental process of soybean seeds directly impacts oil accumulation and yield potential. In recent years, with the rapid advancement of genomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics, and other omics technologies, significant progress has been made in dissecting key seed traits, including oil accumulation, seed size/weight control, and other aspects during soybean seed development ( ;  ).
After years of research, the regulatory networks of oil biosynthesis in soybean have been gradually elucidated, leading to the identification of key transcription factors involved in seed oil biosynthesis. Notable regulators include GmDOF (DNA binding with one finger) ( ), GmbZIP123 (basic leucine zipper domain) ( ), GmMYB73 ( ), GmDREBL (dehydration‐responsive element‐binding‐like) ( ), GmLEC1a/GmLEC1b, GmLEC2 (LEAFY COTYLEDON1/2) ( ), GmWRI1 (WRINKLED1) ( ), GmABI3 (ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE3) ( ), and the GmNFYA (nuclear factor‐YA)–GmZF351–GmZF392 (tandem CCCH zinc finger protein) module ( ;  ). It is worth mentioning that GmWRI1, GmLEC1, GmLEC2, and GmABI3 are homologues or orthologues of the core transcription factors in the LAFL (LEC1, ABI3, FUS3, LEC2) network characterized in Arabidopsis ( ), which are known to regulate seed development, oil accumulation, and storage protein biosynthesis ( ;  ;  ). In parallel, several important genes regulating soybean seed size/weight have also been identified, including GmGA20OX (Gibberellin 20 oxidase) ( ), GmPP2C‐1 (Phosphatase 2C) ( ), GmCYP78A5 (Cytochrome P450 family) ( ), GmGA3ox1 (gibberellin 3‐oxidase1) ( ), GmFtsH25 (filamentation temperature‐sensitive protein H) ( ), GmJAZ3 (jasmonate ZIM‐domain protein 3) ( ), GmPLATZ (Plant AT‐rich sequence and zinc‐binding protein) ( ), GmSWEET10 (Sugars Will Eventually be Exported Transporters 10) ( ), and so on.
The relationships among key seed traits, such as seed size/weight, oil content, and protein content, are diverse and complex. Generally, a positive correlation exists between oil content and seed size/weight. For instance, studies have shown that critical enzymes in the oil biosynthesis pathway (including Phosphatidic acid phosphohydrolase, PAH, and diacylglycerol acyltransferase, DGAT) ( ;  ), sugar transporter (such as GmSWEET10) ( ), and regulatory factors (such as WRI1) ( ) can influence both oil accumulation and seed size/weight simultaneously. In contrast, oil content and protein content in soybean seeds are typically negatively correlated ( ), likely due to the plant's fine‐tuning metabolic control that allocates energy either toward oil biosynthesis or storage protein production. For example, several genes such as GmOLEO1 (oleosin protein 1), GmSWEET10s, and GmST05/GmMFT (Seed Thickness 05/Mother of FT and TFL1) have been found to enhance oil content in soybean seeds, while inadvertently reducing protein content ( ;  ;  ). Conversely, some genes, including GmJAZ3, GmSDP1 (SUGAR DEPENDENT1), and POWR1 (Protein, Oil, Weight, Regulator 1), act to suppress oil accumulation while promoting protein synthesis ( ;  ;  ). The correlation between seed size and protein content is quite complex. Wild soybean varieties typically have small seeds, low oil content, and high protein levels, whereas cultivated soybeans generally have larger seeds with higher oil content ( ;  ;  ). However, this does not necessarily indicate a negative correlation between seed size and protein content. In fact, some studies have identified genes that can simultaneously enhance or suppress both protein accumulation and seed size ( ).
Soybean breeding strategies are not solely focused on improving an individual seed trait; rather, maximizing yield remains the primary objective ( ). Among the various traits, seed size plays an important role in influencing crop yield ( ). In scenarios where the number of seeds remains unchanged, an increase in seed size/weight directly contributes to higher yield. For example, soybean mir396adf and mir396cdf mutant plants, generated through the knockout of the miR396 genes, produced significantly larger seeds and demonstrated increased yield in field trials ( ). Similarly, overexpression of the GmSW17 (Seed Width 17) gene also led to increases in both seed size/weight and overall yield ( ). However, some studies have reported contrasting findings, where increased seed size/weight did not necessarily translate into higher yield. Under certain conditions, a trade‐off may occur. For instance, gmga3ox1 mutant plants had smaller seeds but produced higher yields ( ), suggesting a complex and context‐dependent relationship between seed size and yield ( ). These findings collectively indicate that while larger seeds are often associated with increased yield, this relationship is not universally consistent.
Breeding high‐yield and ‐quality soybean cultivars presents a significant challenge, as improvements in seed quality can inadvertently reduce seed yield. For example, an inverse relationship exists between protein content and seed yield ( ). Researchers have long focused on developing high‐oil and ‐yield soybean cultivars through conventional breeding methods ( ). With substantial progress in identifying genes involved in the molecular regulation of oil biosynthesis and other seed traits, it is now essential to assess their impact on seed yield. Notably, GmWRI1b has been shown to enhance seed oil content and improve yield‐related traits in stable transgenic soybean lines ( ). While several genes regulating oil accumulation and seed size/weight have been identified, discovering additional regulatory factors that influence multiple seed traits would be of great value for advancing molecular breeding in soybean.
In this study, we conducted RNA‐seq analysis and constructed a gene co‐expression network using developing seeds from both high‐oil and low‐oil soybean cultivars to identify hub genes associated with oil accumulation and seed trait regulation. We identified a single MYB transcription factor, GmMYB331, which positively regulates both oil content and seed size/weight. Interestingly, GmMYB331 shows distinct regulatory preferences between oil content and seed size/weight. Mild overexpression enhances seed oil accumulation, whereas strong overexpression promotes seed size/weight. This suggests that GmMYB331 may function through differential binding to target genes involved in oil biosynthesis and seed size/weight regulation. Our findings reveal a novel mechanism by which GmMYB331 differentially regulates downstream genes to confer distinct agronomic traits, offering a promising strategy for developing soybean cultivars with tailored phenotypes for diverse molecular breeding objectives.

## RESULTS

## Identification of GmMYB331 by gene co‐expression network analysis
We initiated a study to identify hub genes associated with soybean oil accumulation by performing RNA‐seq and gene co‐expression network analyses using developing seeds from high‐oil and low‐oil cultivars. The high‐oil cultivars typically show oil contents exceeding 22.5% of dry weight, whereas the low‐oil cultivars generally contain protein levels above 46% and oil contents below 20% of dry weight ( ). Developing seeds at the S4 stage were collected from each cultivar and subjected to RNA‐seq analysis with three biological replicates ( ). These RNA‐seq data were utilized to construct the gene co‐expression network through a two‐step Pearson correlation analysis ( ). In the first round, we calculated the correlation between gene expression levels and seed oil content. Genes showing a strong positive correlation (R > 0.75, FDR < 0.01) were selected for the second round, which involved evaluation of pairwise correlations among these genes. In this step, a stricter threshold (R > 0.85, FDR < 0.01) was applied to identify robustly co‐expressed genes. Genes that were positively correlated with more than 100 other genes were considered hub genes and used to construct the final co‐expression network. This analysis led to the identification of 15 node genes (N1–N15) forming the core of the network ( ; ).
 To validate the reliability of the constructed co‐expression network, a few of these node genes were functionally tested for their roles in regulating fatty acid biosynthesis using a soybean transgenic hairy root system. In total, eight genes were tested, and six of these (Glyma.11G234900, Glyma.09G194500, Glyma.01G135500, Glyma.02G179500, Glyma.03G258200 and Glyma.20G184500) could significantly enhance the total fatty acid content in transgenic hairy roots. One gene (Glyma.10G173100) slightly enhanced the FA content in transgenic hairy roots, whereas Glyma.17G034900 did not affect the FA content in transgenic hairy roots ( ). Among the six genes that positively affected the total FA content, it was found that N15 conferred the highest ratio of fatty acid increase in transgenic hairy roots. The node N15 (Glyma.20G184500) encodes an MYB‐type transcription factor protein of 331 amino acids, and was named as GmMYB331. Since GmMYB331 belongs to a single MYB type ( ), and research on single MYB‐type transcription factors, especially on seed oil biosynthesis, is rather limited, it was selected for further investigation.
We compared the expression levels of MYB331 in H4‐stage seeds between 123 accessions of wild soybeans (Glycine soja) and 114 accessions of cultivated soybeans (Glycine max). The average expression level of MYB331 was significantly higher in cultivated soybeans compared to wild soybeans ( ). Moreover, a significant positive correlation was observed between MYB331 expression levels and seed oil content in cultivated soybean accessions ( ). In contrast, no correlation was found between MYB331 expression and seed oil content in wild soybeans ( ). These findings suggest that MYB331 may play a role in regulating oil accumulation in cultivated soybeans.
Subcellular localization assays revealed that the GFP‐tagged GmMYB331 protein co‐localized with both the nuclear marker HY5 and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) marker, indicating that GmMYB331 is localized in both the nucleus and the ER ( ). Expression analysis showed that GmMYB331 is highly expressed in flowers and seeds at various developmental stages, while its expression is very low in other tested organs ( ). Furthermore, a transcriptional activation assay in protoplasts demonstrated that the BD–GmMYB331 fusion protein shows transcriptional activation activity ( ).

## Generation of stable GmMYB331‐overexpression transgenic soybean and evaluation of the phenotypes
To verify the role of GmMYB331 in regulating seed oil accumulation, we first measured the fatty acid content in GmMYB331 transgenic Arabidopsis. Homozygous independent transgenic lines with varying levels of transgene expression were identified, and these plants displayed growth patterns comparable to those of wild‐type (WT) plants (Col‐0) ( ). Notably, five of the transgenic lines showed a significant increase in seed oil content ( ). In addition, the thousand‐seed weight of these transgenic Arabidopsis plants was also significantly higher than that of the WT plants ( ). These findings suggest that GmMYB331 may play a role in regulating both oil accumulation and seed weight.

GmMYB331, driven by the 35S promoter, was then transformed into soybean cultivar Jack to generate stable overexpression lines. Several homozygous transgenic lines were identified, and based on the expression levels of the transgene, the transgenic soybean plants were categorized into two groups ( ). One group (OE‐6, OE‐9), referred to as mild expressors, showed a moderate increase in GmMYB331 expression—approximately 10‐fold higher than that in WT plants. The other group (OE‐3, OE‐4, and OE‐10), classified as strong expressors, showed extremely high levels of transgene expression, reaching tens of thousands of times higher than those in WT plants ( ).
 Phenotypic variations among these transgenic plants were assessed at various developmental stages. In terms of trifoliate leaf morphology, the mild expressors (OE‐6, OE‐9) closely resembled WT plants, with similar angles between the lateral and central leaflets ( ). In contrast, the strong expressors (OE‐3, OE‐4, and OE‐10) displayed a significantly wider angle compared to WT plants ( ). Additionally, the petiole length of trifoliate leaves and the overall stem length in the mild expressors were comparable to those of WT plants, whereas the strong expressors showed significantly increased values in both traits ( ).
Regarding plant architecture, the mild expressors maintained phenotypes similar to those of WT plants, whereas the strong expressors showed pronounced stem curling ( ). This curling phenotype emerged specifically at the flowering stage (R1 stage), as seedlings of strong expressors remained upright prior to this developmental phase ( ). The curled stems of strong expressors were rigid rather than pliable. Biochemical analyses showed that while the lignin content in the stems of mild expressors remained at WT levels, strong expressors showed a relatively higher lignin content. Notably, both mild and strong expressors had reduced cellulose content compared to WT plants ( ). This altered cell wall composition likely contributes to the rigid and curled stem phenotype observed in strong expressors (OE‐3, OE‐4, and OE‐10). Furthermore, strong expressors also showed an earlier podding phenotype compared to WT plants, whereas mild expressors showed developmental timing similar to that of WT plants ( ).

## Overexpression of GmMYB331 affects seed size/weight and seed oil accumulation in transgenic soybean plants
Seed and other agronomic traits were evaluated at the maturation stage of the GmMYB331‐transgenic plants ( ). The mild expressors OE‐6 and OE‐9 showed modest increases in seed size/weight by 10.93% and 4.92%, respectively. Notably, these lines showed substantial improvements in seed yield per plant, with increases of 39.66% and 15.95%, respectively, compared to the JACK control plants ( ). In contrast, the strong expressors produced larger seeds with significantly greater seed weight—increasing by 26.26%, 16.39%, and 30.74%—but showed markedly reduced seed yield per plant compared to JACK plants ( ). These findings suggest that mild overexpression of GmMYB331 can substantially enhance seed yield per plant, whereas strong overexpression may reduce overall yield while promoting increased seed size and weight.
 The fatty acid content of transgenic soybean seeds was further analyzed. The mild expressors showed significant increases in the total fatty acid content, with OE‐6 and OE‐9 showing 11.92% and 13.08% increases, respectively, compared to WT plants. In contrast, the strong expressors did not display a significant increase in the total fatty acid content ( ). Fatty acid composition analysis revealed that oleic acid (C18:1) and linoleic acid (C18:2) were notably elevated in the two mild expressors. However, the three strong expressors showed no consistent changes or only slight fluctuations in fatty acid composition ( ). Oil bodies in the mature seeds were observed under an electron microscope. Results showed that oil bodies in GmMYB331 transgenic soybean are significantly smaller than those in WT plants ( ). Oil body numbers were also counted. The two mild expressors had slightly more oil bodies than those of WT plants, whereas the strong expressors had slightly fewer oil bodies than those of WT plants ( ). The total protein content was also determined using the Dumas combustion method ( ) and results showed that the protein content of mild expressors was comparable to that of the WT plants (JACK), whereas the protein content of strong expressors was significantly higher than that of the WT plants ( ). These results suggest that mild overexpression of GmMYB331 promotes total oil accumulation in soybean seeds.

## 
GmMYB331 is needed for regulating soybean seed traits
To further elucidate the role of GmMYB331 in regulating seed traits, knockout mutants were generated in the soybean cultivar Tianlong 1 (TL1) background using CRISPR/Cas9 (clustered regularly interspaced palindromic repeats/CRISPR‐associated protein 9) technology targeting two specific sites. Three independent mutants (myb331‐2, ‐3, ‐4) with different edited harboring distinct gene‐editing mutations were obtained. These mutations led to premature termination of the GmMYB331 gene ( ). While the overall growth and seed yield of the mutants were comparable to those of WT plants (TL1) ( ), the mutants showed significantly smaller seed size and reduced 100‐seed weight ( ). Additionally, the total fatty acid content in the mutant seeds was markedly decreased, with individual fatty acid compositions showing a general downward trend accompanied by some fluctuations ( ). Protein content analysis showed that the total protein content of myb331‐3 was significantly higher than that of WT plants, while the protein content of the other two mutants was similar to that of WT plants ( ). These findings suggest that GmMYB331 plays a critical role in maintaining both seed size/weight and oil accumulation in soybean seeds.
 

## GmMYB331 binds to the promoter regions of genes related to oil accumulation and seed size/weight control
To identify the downstream genes regulated by GmMYB331, RNA‐seq analysis was performed on middle‐stage seed samples from two strong expressors (OE‐3 and OE‐4) and two mutants (myb331‐2 and myb331‐3), each with three biological replicates. Venn diagram analysis revealed that OE‐3 and OE‐4 shared 620 commonly upregulated genes and 68 downregulated genes ( ;  ); myb331‐2 and myb331‐3 shared 20 commonly upregulated genes and 1,025 downregulated genes ( ;  ). Volcano plot analysis further highlighted four oleosin protein encoding genes (GmOLEO1‐1, GmOLEO1‐2, GmOLEO2, and GmOLEO4) ( ) as being commonly upregulated in the two strong expressors, while in the two mutants, similar OLEO genes were downregulated. GmOLEO4 was not highlighted in myb331‐2 and GmOLEO1‐2 was not highlighted in myb331‐3 simply because their fold change is not as high as the other three ( ). Quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR) validated the RNA‐seq results, showing elevated expression levels of these four OLEO genes and GmACP4 (also identified from the RNA‐seq assay) in GmMYB331‐transgenic plants compared to WT controls ( ). Additionally, a previously characterized regulatory module involved in oil accumulation, containing three transcription factors, namely, GmNFYA, GmZF351, and GmZF392 ( ), also showed increased expression in GmMYB331‐transgenic plants. Moreover, qRT‐PCR results showed that two genes associated with cell division or seed size regulation, GmCYCD2;2 ( ;  ) and GmUGT85A3 ( ), were also significantly upregulated ( ). Collectively, these findings suggest that GmMYB331 may enhance oil accumulation by upregulating key oil biosynthesis genes (GmOLEO1/2/4, GmACP4) and oil‐regulatory transcription factors (GmNFYA, GmZF351, GmZF392). Furthermore, GmMYB331 may contribute to increased seed size and/or yield by promoting the expression of GmCYCD2;2 and GmUGT85A3.
 We further examined whether GmMYB331 directly binds to the promoter regions of its downstream target genes using an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA). A previous study revealed that MYB‐type TF binds to two types of core elements: type I, CNGTT[G/A] and TNGTT[G/A], and type II, ACC[A/T]A[A/C][T/C] and ACC[A/T][A/C/T][A/C/T] ( ). Three kinds of core elements, ACC[A/T][A/C/T][A/C/T], CNGTT[G/A], and TNGTT[G/A], were found in the promoter regions of the downstream genes and were further confirmed by the EMSA assay ( ). The results showed that GmMYB331 specifically recognizes and binds to the promoters of GmOLEO1/2/4 and GmACP4, as the protein–DNA complex bands were completely abolished upon the addition of excess unlabeled competitor probes ( ). In addition, GmMYB331 was also found to bind to the promoters of seed size/weight‐related genes, including GmCYCD2;2 and GmUGT85A3. However, this binding appeared to be less specific, as the presence of 200‐fold excess of unlabeled competitor probes only partially reduced the intensity of the protein–DNA complex bands rather than eliminating them ( ). These findings suggest that GmMYB331 directly binds to the promoters of oil biosynthesis‐related genes with high specificity, while its interaction with seed size/weight‐related gene promoters is relatively weaker and less specific.
The interaction between GmMYB331 and the promoters of its downstream target genes was further validated by chromatin immunoprecipitation, followed by quantitative PCR (ChIP‐qPCR), in OE‐3 transgenic plants harboring GmMYB331‐4×MYC, using an anti‐MYC antibody. The ChIP signal was significantly higher in OE‐3 plants compared to the control JACK plants for the promoter fragments of GmOLEO1‐1 and GmOLEO2, indicating direct binding of GmMYB331 to these promoters ( ). To assess whether GmMYB331 regulates the transcriptional activity of its downstream genes, a dual‐luciferase (LUC) reporter assay was performed ( ). The results showed that GmMYB331 significantly enhanced the promoter activity of all seven tested downstream genes ( ,  ). Among these, GmOLEO1‐1, GmNFYA, GmZF351, and GmZF392 have previously been shown to play key roles in soybean oil biosynthesis and accumulation ( ;  ). Furthermore, to investigate the function of GmOLEO2, we generated Arabidopsis transgenic lines overexpressing this gene. The transgenic Arabidopsis seeds showed significantly higher seed oil content but lower seed weight compared to wild‐type controls ( ), demonstrating that GmOLEO2 also contributes to oil accumulation and the regulation of seed size/weight. Collectively, these results indicate that GmMYB331 directly binds to and activates the expression of multiple downstream genes involved in fatty acid biosynthesis and seed size/weight regulation.

## GmMYB331 shows specific regulatory differences between genes regulating oil accumulation and seed size/weight
We speculate that the phenotypic difference shown by GmMYB331 overexpression at different expression levels in mild expressors OE‐6 and OE‐9, and strong expressors OE‐3, OE‐4 and OE‐10, is likely due to its differential ability to recognize and bind to different sets of downstream genes, namely, oil‐related genes and seed size/weight‐related genes. Therefore, we conducted a cis‐element analysis of the promoter sequences of these downstream genes and annotated the possible MYB‐binding elements on the promoter sequences. These cis‐elements that MYB‐type transcription factors may recognize and bind to have been identified in a previous study ( ). The results showed that the distribution densities of MYB‐binding elements in the promoter sequences of the two types of genes were quite different. The promoter sequences of genes that regulate oil accumulation appeared to have a higher density of MYB‐binding elements compared to the genes that regulate seed size/weight ( ). We selected promoter regions from GmOLEO1‐1, GmCYCD2;2, and GmUGT85A3 for EMSA competition assays (red boxes in  ). The results showed that the GmMYB331 can bind to the GmUGT85A3 and GmCYCD2;2 gene promoter elements with high intensity, and the corresponding cold probes can remove about 40%–50% of the band intensity by competition ( ). In contrast, when the promoter sequence of GmOLEO1‐1 was used as a cold probe in the assay, it could remove 75%–90% band intensity by competition ( ). These results suggest that the specificity of GmMYB331 binding to the GmOLEO1‐1 promoter is much higher than its binding ability to the promoters of GmCYCD2;2 and GmUGT85A3.
 

## 
MYB331 promoter haplotypes are associated with oil content and seed size in wild and cultivated soybeans
We sequenced the promoter regions of the MYB331 gene in 47 wild soybeans and 69 cultivated soybeans. Comparative and phylogenetic analyses revealed that these sequences can be classified into three haplotypes ( ). In wild soybeans, the three haplotypes account for 47%, 42%, and 11%. In cultivated soybeans, the corresponding proportions are 6%, 39%, and 55% ( ). Among these soybeans, haplotype 1 has the lowest oil content and seed size, followed by haplotype 2, while haplotype 3 has the highest values for both oil content and seed size ( ). The results indicate that the haplotypes of wild soybeans are primarily associated with low oil content and small seeds, while cultivated soybeans show the opposite characteristics. The haplotypes of some cultivated soybeans can still be further genetically modified to the optimal genotype—designated as haplotype 3.

## DISCUSSION
In this study, through RNA‐seq and gene co‐expression network analysis, we identified GmMYB331, a single MYB‐type transcription factor gene, as a central hub gene that is both sufficient and necessary for promoting oil accumulation and seed size/weight in soybean. This regulatory function appears to be mediated by the activation of oil biosynthesis‐related and seed development‐related genes, with GmMYB331 showing differential promoter‐binding affinity in mild expressors versus strong expressors. Our findings reveal a novel mechanism of gene expression regulation via differential promoter binding for distinct biological processes. This mechanism offers potential strategies for engineering soybean varieties with customized oil content and seed size/weight to meet diverse breeding objectives.
Based on the number of repeat sequences (R) in their DNA‐binding domains, MYB transcription factors are classified into four types (1R–4R). Among them, the R2R3‐MYB proteins, which belong to the 2R type, are the most abundant in plants ( ). These R2R3‐MYB transcription factors are known to play diverse roles in plant organ development, responses to biotic/abiotic stresses, and the regulation of secondary metabolite biosynthesis ( ). In contrast, GmMYB331, identified in the present study, is not a typical R2R3‐MYB protein. Instead, it contains a single MYB domain located at its N‐terminal region, classifying it as a 1R‐type MYB transcription factor ( ).
Single MYB‐domain transcription factors are relatively understudied, particularly in relation to agronomic traits such as seed development. In this study, we demonstrated that the single MYB‐domain transcription factor GmMYB331 promotes oil accumulation in transgenic soybean seeds under mild transgene overexpression, while strong overexpression enhances seed size/weight and alters leaf morphology and overall plant architecture ( ,  ). Interestingly, our previous study also identified a single MYB‐domain regulator, GmMYB73, which lacks a conventional regulatory domain but positively regulates oil accumulation in soybean. GmMYB73 was shown to interact with GL3 (GLABRA3) and EGL3 (ENHANCER OF GLABRA3) to repress GL2, a negative regulator of oil biosynthesis, while activating PLDα1 expression to promote lipid accumulation in transgenic plants ( ). Whether GmMYB331 and GmMYB73 functionally intersect in oil regulation remains to be elucidated. In other plant species, several R2R3‐MYB transcription factors have been implicated in oil biosynthesis regulation. For instance, CzMYB1 from green algae regulates lipid metabolism and triacylglycerol (TAG) accumulation ( ). VfMYB36, identified in Euphorbiaceae, increases seed oil content by enhancing linolenic acid levels ( ). In Jatropha curcas, JcMYB1 directly upregulates DGAT1 expression, thereby boosting oil content in transgenic seeds ( ). Conversely, in Arabidopsis thaliana, MYB89 acts as a negative regulator by inhibiting key oil biosynthesis genes such as WRI1 and L1L ( ), while AtMYB73 fine‐tunes oil metabolism through the downregulation of FAE1 (Fatty Acid Elongase1), BCCP2 (biotin carboxyl carrier protein isoform 2), and PKP‐β1 (pyruvate kinase β subunit 1) ( ).
GmMYB331 has a long C‐terminal sequence in addition to its N‐terminal MYB domain, distinguishing it from the single MYB‐domain regulator GmMYB73, which lacks a regulatory domain. This structural difference suggests that GmMYB331 retains a complete transcriptional regulatory function ( ). Indeed, our results confirm that GmMYB331 can directly bind to and activate the expression of multiple downstream target genes ( ). Interestingly, overexpression of GmMYB331 in soybean results in two distinct phenotypic classes. Mild overexpression significantly increases seed oil content and slightly enhances seed size/weight, without affecting the overall plant architecture. In contrast, strong overexpression leads to a marked increase in seed size/weight and induces pronounced changes in plant morphology—including altered leaf shape and twisted stems—while only marginally improving seed oil content. Notably, yield per plant is moderately improved in mild expressors, whereas it is significantly reduced in strong expressors ( ,  ). The divergent effects on oil content and seed size/weight among different overexpression levels may result from differential binding and activation of corresponding downstream genes. However, the mechanism underlying the yield reduction observed in strong expressors remains unclear. One plausible explanation is that the altered plant architecture—specifically, changes in leaf structure and stem morphology—may disrupt the source‐to‐sink energy flow from photosynthetic tissues to developing seeds, likely through impaired vascular transport. Importantly, despite the varied phenotypic consequences of GmMYB331 overexpression, knockout mutants show a significant reduction in both seed oil accumulation and seed size/weight ( ), indicating that GmMYB331 is essential for maintaining these traits at appropriate levels.
The direct targets of GmMYB331 include GmACP4 and four GmOLEO genes ( ). GmACP4 is involved in oil synthesis and metabolism ( ), while GmOLEO1 promotes oil accumulation in seeds ( ). These findings clearly indicate that GmMYB331 plays a regulatory role in oil biosynthesis and metabolism. Regardless of whether they are mild expressors or strong expressors, all the transgenic soybean seeds had higher GmOLEO gene expression than that of WT plants ( ), but only mild expressors showed enhanced FA content ( ). OLEO genes encode oleosin proteins, which are involved in the storage step of lipids, representing the process of oil body formation, rather than the de novo FA biosynthesis ( ;  ). In the strong expressors, significant phenotype alterations, such as twisting, were observed ( ). These morphological changes may impair soybean growth and potentially affect energy allocation. If energy supply is insufficient, fatty acid biosynthesis may also be compromised. Without an adequate supply of fatty acids, even if the storage mechanism is activated, it cannot effectively enhance seed oil content. Normally, genes that promote seed oil accumulation could also play positive roles in regulating seed weight/size ( ;  ;  ;  ). However, there are also exceptions. It was reported that overexpression of GmOLEO1 significantly increased seed oil content, but reduced seed size ( ). In this study, GmMYB331 activates several GmOLEO genes. Consistent with GmOLEO1, oil body size was reduced if these GmOLEO genes were upregulated ( ). In fact, oil bodies show considerable heterogeneity in size ( ,  ). It appears that the best use of space is when small oil bodies intersperse among large ones; thus, the seed may accommodate a greater number of oil bodies and accumulate higher oil content ( ).
In addition, we identified GmUGT85A3, which encodes a UDP‐glucosyltransferase (UGT), as another potential target of GmMYB331. In Arabidopsis, UGT85A3 catalyzes the O‐glucosylation of cytokinins (CKs), leading to the reversible inactivation of zeatin‐type CKs. Overexpression of UGT85A3 driven by the 35S promoter in Arabidopsis has been shown to affect ovule development, resulting in reduced fertility and decreased number of seeds. Interestingly, the few fertile seeds that did develop were significantly larger, likely due to excess nutrient availability ( ). A similar phenomenon may occur in soybean lines with strong GmMYB331 expression, potentially due to sustained activation of GmUGT85A3. However, it is important to note that the regulation of seed size/weight by GmMYB331 is likely not mediated solely by GmUGT85A3. Another target gene, GmCYCD2;2, may play a more prominent role, as its expression pattern correlates more closely with the observed increases in seed size/weight in GmMYB331 strong expressors ( ,  ). D‐type cyclins, such as CycD2, are known to promote cell division ( ), and transcription factors that activate CycD2 genes, such as PLATZ, have been shown to enhance seed size/weight in both soybean and rice ( ).
GmMYB331 shows stronger binding affinity and specificity to the promoter of GmOLEO1‐1 compared to its binding to the promoters of GmUGT85A3 and GmCYCD2;2. This leads to the hypothesis that, under identical conditions, GmMYB331 preferentially regulates oil biosynthesis and accumulation, while its influence on seed size/weight is dependent on its residual regulatory capacity. This could explain the gradient phenotypes observed in GmMYB331 overexpression lines. In mild expressors, the expression level of GmMYB331 is relatively low. After binding to the promoters of oil biosynthesis‐related genes, the remaining protein may be insufficient to effectively regulate less specific targets. As a result, these plants primarily show an increase in seed oil content, with only a slight enhancement in seed size/weight ( ). In contrast, strong expressors show over a 10,000‐fold increase in GmMYB331 expression compared to the WT plants. In these lines, after strongly activating oil biosynthesis genes, the excess GmMYB331 protein is sufficient to also upregulate the less specific target genes, such as GmCYCD2;2 and GmUGT85A3, thereby leading to a significant increase in seed size/weight ( ,  ).
 Taken together, we identified GmMYB331 as a single MYB transcription factor that positively regulates both oil accumulation and seed size/weight in soybean. Fine‐tuning the expression of this gene—specifically achieving a moderate increase—may lead to soybean plants with enhanced oil content and high yield potential. Achieving this goal would require careful control of GmMYB331 expression levels, possibly through the use of optimized or seed‐specific promoters in genetic engineering. In the process of molecular breeding, it is also crucial to consider the downstream targets of transcription factors. For instance, UGT85A3, which plays a role in the deactivation of cytokinins, should not be constitutively activated, as continuous expression may have unintended consequences. A promising strategy could involve a combined genetic approach—such as overexpressing GmMYB331 while simultaneously knocking out or knocking down GmUGT85A3. Our findings on the molecular mechanism of GmMYB331 provide valuable insights for improving both the quality and yield of soybean.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Plant materials

GmMYB331 overexpressing soybean were generated in the Jack genetic background, with a 4× Myc tag fused to the C‐terminal of the coding sequence and driven by the 35S promoter. The GmMYB331 overexpressing soybean plants were generated by the Da Bei Nong Group (https://www.dbn.cn/ch/home). Gene‐edited GmMYB331 knockout mutants were developed using the TL1 cultivar. The knockout mutants were generated by WIMI BIOLOGY (http://www.wimibio.com/). Transgenic positive seedlings were screened using PAT/BAR colloidal gold‐based strip tests (YouLong Biotech, China). The expression level of GmMYB331 was detected using seeds of the H4 stage ( ) by RT‐PCR. Soybean seeds were grown in Beijing, one plant per pot, with a ratio of soil:vermiculite:turfy soil = 1:1:1. Soybean plants were grown in a greenhouse under controlled conditions: the daytime temperature was maintained at 26°C and the nighttime temperature was maintained at 22°C, with a 16 h/8 h (day/night) photoperiod. Illumination was provided by a light source, JIUPO‐2835LED‐612 (Fujian Jiupo Biotechnology Co. Ltd., China). Transgenic soybeans of the T3 generation and homozygous mutants were used for further phenotype analysis. Agronomic traits, such as seed weight/plant, leaf angel, petiole length, and stem length, are measured from the plant. Arabidopsis plants used in this study belonged to the Columbia‐0 (Col‐0) ecotype, and transient expression assays were carried out in Nicotiana benthamiana.

## Co‐expression network analysis
Soybean cultivars from the Huang‐Huai‐Hai (HHH) region can be broadly classified into typical high‐ and low‐oil types ( ). To identify key genes involved in oil biosynthesis and accumulation, we utilized transcriptome data previously generated in our laboratory from these HHH region cultivars ( ). The analysis was performed by calculating Pearson correlation coefficients between gene expression levels and seed oil content (i.e., gene‐to‐oil content correlation). Genes with a correlation coefficient (R) > 0.75 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01 were selected for further investigation. Subsequently, we examined gene‐to‐gene expression correlations among these selected genes. Pairs with R > 0.85 and FDR < 0.01 were retained and visualized using Cytoscape software ( ).

## 
Agrobacterium rhizogene‐mediated transformation of soybean hairy roots
The coding sequences of GmMYB331 (Glyma.20G184500, N15), Glyma.09G194500 (N4), and Glyma.01G135500 (N6) were cloned into the pBIN438 vector using a homologous recombination approach. The hypocotyls of 1‐week‐old soybean seedlings (Kefeng1 cultivar, KF1) were inoculated with Agrobacterium rhizogenes strain K599 harboring the recombinant pBIN438 constructs, following the protocol described by  . Post‐inoculation, seedlings were maintained under high‐humidity conditions for approximately 2 weeks to promote the development of hairy roots at the wound sites. Once hairy roots emerged, the original roots were removed, and the seedlings were transferred to water for an additional 2–3 weeks of growth. The harvested hairy roots were ground into fine powders using liquid nitrogen, and then lyophilized. The resulting dry powder was used for fatty acid extraction.

## Transcriptional activation assay
The transcriptional activation assays were conducted using Arabidopsis protoplasts and N. benthamiana leaves. For Arabidopsis protoplasts, the procedure followed the protocol described by  . The reporter plasmid was constructed by inserting five GAL4 binding elements and a firefly luciferase (LUC) gene into the pUC19 vector. A pTRL vector containing a Renilla LUC gene was used as an internal control to normalize transfection efficiency. The coding sequence of GmMYB331 was cloned into the pRT‐BD vector, while pRT‐BD‐VP16 served as the positive control. For assays in N. benthamiana leaves, the protocol followed that of  ,  ). The reporter plasmid was generated by inserting the promoter region of the target gene and the LUC gene into the pCambia2300 vector. A pTRL vector containing a Renilla LUC gene driven by the 35S promoter was used as the internal control. GmMYB331 was cloned into the pBIN438 vector for effector expression. LUC activity was measured using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA) with a GloMax 20‐20 Luminometer, following the method described by  . Relative luminescence values were calculated by normalizing firefly LUC activity to Renilla LUC activity. All experiments were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

## Fatty acid content analysis
A total amount of 10 mg of powdered Arabidopsis or soybean seeds was used for fatty acid extraction, following the method described by  ,  . FA content was analyzed using a gas chromatography (GC) system (GC‐2014; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The concentrations of individual FA species were normalized to the internal standard: heptadecanoic acid (17:0).

## Oil body observation
The collected seed samples were fixed with a fixative solution consisting of 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and 2.5% glutaraldehyde (GA) dissolved in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB), followed by vacuum application. The samples were left at room temperature for 2–4 h, after which the fixative was replaced and stored overnight at 4°C. They were then immersed in a mixture of 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4) and 0.8% potassium ferrocyanide and kept in the dark for 3 h. Subsequently, the samples were immersed in a 1% uranyl acetate (UA) solution and stored overnight at 4°C in the dark. The samples were dehydrated using a gradient series of ethanol concentrations, followed by immersion in acetone. They were then infiltrated with resin and left overnight. Polymerization was carried out at 60°C for 72 h. After solidification, the samples were sectioned into 60–70 nm slices using an ultramicrotome, followed by double staining with 3% uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Finally, the sections were examined under an electron microscope. The gray lipid globules indicate oil bodies. Areas of these oil bodies were measured using ImageJ software ( ).

## RNA‐seq analysis
Given that the H4 stage (mid‐stage) is the beginning of the exponential period for oil accumulation ( ), seeds at this stage were collected for RNA extraction. Total RNA was isolated using TRNzol Universal RNA reagent (Tiangen, China) for leaf samples and a CTAB (cetyltrimethylammonium bromide)‐based method for seed samples. RNA sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq. 6000 platform. Raw reads were trimmed to remove adapters and low‐quality sequences using FASTP (v0.19.7). Clean paired‐end reads were aligned to the soybean reference genome Wm82.a2.v1 (downloaded from the Phytozome website) using HISAT2 (v2.1.0) ( ). Gene expression levels were quantified with FeatureCounts (v2.0.1) ( ). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq. 2 package in R (v3.12) ( ) on the basis of the criteria of |Log2(fold change)| > 1 and adjusted P‐value < 0.05. Overlapping genes were analyzed using Venn diagrams, and volcano plot analysis was conducted using the R package ggplot2 ( ).

## Electrophoretic mobility shift assay
Single‐stranded DNA fragments containing the promoter regions of target downstream genes were chemically synthesized and 5'‐biotin‐labeled (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). Double‐stranded DNA probes were generated by annealing complementary single‐stranded oligonucleotides through a thermal cycling process: the mixture was heated to 75°C for 5 min to ensure complete denaturation, followed by slow cooling to room temperature to allow proper hybridization. DNA–protein binding interactions were analyzed using the LightShift™ Chemiluminescent Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA) Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. The specific DNA probes used for the EMSA experiments are listed in  .

## Quantitative ChIP‐PCR analysis
Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were conducted following a modified protocol of Nature Protocol ( ), using a polyclonal anti‐Myc antibody (MBL, Japan). Quantitative PCR was performed with the Roche LightCycler 480 II system using the SYBR Green qPCR kit (TB Green; Takara, Japan). The primers used for the qPCR analysis are listed in  .
