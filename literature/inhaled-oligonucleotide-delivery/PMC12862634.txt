Department of Clinical Sciences, North Carolina State University College of Veterinary Medicine, Raleigh, NC, United States DM involves the progressive degeneration of the spinal cord’s white matter, ultimately extending to the lower motor neurons. Early clinical signs include pelvic limb proprioceptive deficits, which are often asymmetric with spastic paraparesis consistent with a thoracolumbar myelopathy. Signs progress to paraplegia, followed by muscle atrophy, a loss of segmental reflexes, tetraparesis, dysphonia, dysphagia, and incontinence, leading to flaccid tetraplegia and hypoventilation. Progression from the onset of signs (stage I) to paraplegia (stage II) occurs over a median of 10 months, with further progression to tetraplegia occurring over another 18-36 months. , , , Although German Shepherd dogs are predisposed, other breeds, such as Boxers, Rhodesian Ridgebacks, Chesapeake Bay Retrievers, and Pembroke Welsh Corgis, also are over-represented, and the disorder has an estimated prevalence of 0.19% and occurs in over 90 breeds. , , Despite over 50 years of research, no effective treatment exists for DM. Physical rehabilitation and a wide range of supplements are recommended to slow progression with unclear benefits. , , 
The discovery that DM is associated with toxic gain-of-function mutations in the superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) gene has led to the proposal that DM is a canine analog of familial SOD1 amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and provides treatment opportunities. The E40K mutation causes the SOD1 protein to misfold and aggregate within neurons and astrocytes, leading to axonal degeneration, neuronal dysfunction, and neuronal death. , , Studies on purified E40KSOD1 protein demonstrated normal enzymatic activity, favoring the hypothesis that lesions arise from the toxic properties of misfolded SOD1 rather than from a loss of function. Proposed mechanisms of toxicity include the disruption of axonal transport, mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and neuroinflammation. This pathophysiology makes the SOD1 gene an attractive therapeutic target for gene silencing, although the mechanisms of toxicity remain debated. The U1 Adaptor oligonucleotide (U1AO) gene silencing technology offers an opportunity to decrease the expression of the mutated SOD1 gene by means of a novel mechanism of action based on the inhibition of polyA-tail addition, which is an obligatory step for nearly all eukaryotic mRNA. , The U1AOs are single-stranded oligonucleotides named for the U1 small nuclear ribonucleoprotein (U1 snRNP) complex, which mediates their gene silencing action. The U1 snRNP, a pre-mRNA splicing factor, has a non-splicing function of silencing certain genes by suppressing the polyadenylation of their pre-mRNA. The U1AO technology uses this mechanism by tethering U1 snRNP to the target pre-mRNA‘s terminal exon, causing transcript degradation. The U1AOs offer lower toxicity and easier delivery than small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) and RNase H-antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs). 
Given that prior studies in mice showed U1AOs to be efficacious and safe, we hypothesized that they could be used to silence the SOD1 gene in dogs. We administered U1 Adaptors to dogs intrathecally with the aim of determining their safety, tolerability, and the degree of SOD1 silencing.

## Materials and methods
We developed and evaluated a novel gene silencing therapy, a U1 Adaptor oligonucleotide targeting the canine SOD1 gene (U1cSOD1). The agent was produced following good manufacturing practices and supplied as a 5 mg/mL solution in phosphate-buffered saline. Additional details are provided in Figures S1-S5, indicating >90% purity, low endotoxin units (EU; ≤0.10 EU/mg) and stability in solution over a 3-year storage period (polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis [PAGE] and 32P-Northern blotting).
Our study was performed in 2 phases. The first phase utilized 8 dogs and aimed to determine the intrathecal dosing technique, dose, safety, and SOD-1-silencing efficacy in 7 healthy dogs and 1 dog with late-stage DM. The second phase, consisted of 4 dogs diagnosed with early stage DM, and it assessed the feasibility, safety, and tolerability of repeat dosing and explored the impact of dose frequency and dose escalation. The use of biologics was approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee. All animal work was approved by the institutional animal care and use committee (IACUC) at the authors’ institution (North Carolina State University IACUC protocol numbers 17-104 and 18-143) and the clinical work had additional approval from the hospital board of the authors’ institution. All animal owners reviewed and signed an informed consent form.

## Animals

Phase 1: Six purpose-bred healthy adult beagles, 1 purpose-bred healthy hound, and 1 mixed breed male neutered dog diagnosed with stage III DM (DM stages and scoring are provided in ).
 
Phase 2: Four dogs diagnosed with DM ( ). Inclusion criteria were diagnosis of DM based on a consistent clinical history, neurologic examination findings, genetic testing confirmation of homozygosity for the E40K SOD1 mutation, the absence of spinal cord compressive lesions on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and the absence of inflammatory central nervous system (CNS) disease based on MRI findings and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis; and DM stage I (hind limb proprioceptive deficits and paresis, able to walk outside unassisted). Exclusion criteria included comorbidities that might affect the study through premature death or that would make anesthesia for monthly treatments inappropriate, orthopedic disease that would impact gait assessments, and blood test results that would make adverse effects of treatment difficult to detect (eg, increased liver enzyme activities).

## Study design
We first evaluated U1cSOD1 in healthy dogs to determine initial dosing and safety data before performing a study in clinically affected dogs.

## Phase 1
The first 4 dogs were treated in pairs, with 1 dog receiving the vehicle and the other dog receiving U1cSOD1 at a dosage of 1.5 mg/kg (extrapolated from rodent data for other genes ) and euthanized on day 5. The pairwise comparison allowed the verification of the silencing of the target gene and informed dose selection. Based on these data, the fifth and sixth dogs both were treated with U1cSOD1 at a dosage of 1.5 mg/kg and euthanized on day 5. The seventh dog (hound) received a dosage of 3 mg/kg and was euthanized on day 30 to verify the duration of silencing and the tolerance of a higher dose. The eighth dog, with stage III DM was treated with 1.5 mg/kg and was euthanized on day 5. The timing of procedures is summarized in .
 The day before the procedure (T0 in ), physical and neurologic examinations were performed, and blood was drawn for a CBC and a serum biochemistry panel. The next day (D1), dogs were anesthetized (premedication with hydromorphone [Fresenius Kabi, Lake Zurich, Il] and dexmedetomidine [Zoetis, Parsippany, NJ], followed by induction with propofol [Anmed Pharmacy, Anderson, SC] and maintenance with inhaled isoflurane [Piramal Critical Care, Bethlehem, PA] oxygen, and a nitrogen mix). The dogs were placed in right lateral recumbency, a 2- by 3-inch area of hair was shaved over the fifth and sixth lumbar vertebrae, and routine surgical preparation was performed using povidone iodine and alcohol. The area was draped, and a window was cut in the drape over the fifth/sixth lumbar interspace. A 20-gauge spinal needle was placed percutaneously until CSF flow was seen, and a CSF sample was taken for routine analysis and banking at −80°C. A 24-gauge pediatric epidural catheter (ReCathCo, Allison Park, PA) was fed through the spinal needle into the subarachnoid space and threaded cranially as far as possible. The needle and guidewire were removed, CSF backflow was confirmed, and then an administration set, attached to a 0.2-mm filter, a 3-way stockcock, and a syringe containing the therapeutic agent or vehicle, and another syringe containing phosphate buffered saline (PBS) for irrigation was attached to the catheter. This set-up is depicted in (illustrating the apparatus used connected to a spinal needle) and also was used in all clinically affected dogs. The drug or vehicle was infused at a rate of 1 mL per minute by a timed, slow-manual bolus, followed by 0.5 mL of PBS to propel all of the test material through the filter and the administration set into the intrathecal space. The catheter was left in place for 2 minutes post-injection and then removed. The dog was tilted head down by 30° for 15 minutes, and then recovered from anesthesia. After the first 2 dogs, the method was modified to a simple injection using a spinal needle (22 or 20 gauge depending on the dog’s size) positioned with the needle bevel pointing cranially. After recovery from anesthesia, the dogs were monitored daily by physical and neurologic examinations, and blood was drawn for repeat CBC and biochemistry 48 hours and 5 days after the procedure.
 Dogs were euthanized using a combination of acepromazine and propofol, followed by an overdose of barbiturate (Euthasol; Virbac, WestLake, TX) administered IV, a CSF sample was collected for analysis and frozen at −80°C, and the CNS was removed as quickly as possible. Samples were taken from the CNS ( ), and brain samples were taken unilaterally, whereas spinal cord segments approximately 5 mm thick were taken from each region. All samples were divided with 1 set being snap frozen for Northern blot analysis and another set placed in RNAlater (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) for reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis. The same sampling protocol was used to bank samples from the kidney and liver for analysis. All remaining tissues were processed for histopathologic analysis by immersion-fixation in 10% neutral buffered formalin. The brain was allowed to fix for 5 days before further trimming. The samples were embedded in paraffin, and 5 μm sections were cut, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and evaluated by a pathologist.

## Phase 2
The treatment protocols for this phase are provided in . Dogs diagnosed with DM underwent longitudinal physical and neurologic examinations before each treatment, with the videotaping of gait and reflex assessments, and their disease was staged ( ). A CBC, serum biochemistry panel, and urinalysis were performed before all treatments. Dogs were anesthetized using a protocol determined by the anesthesiologist on duty. The injections of U1cSOD1 at 1.5 mg/kg were performed using a spinal needle placed at the L5/6 interspace, following the same protocol as described for phase 1. Needle placement was confirmed by the backflow for CSF, and fluoroscopy was used if needle placement was challenging. Before each injection, CSF was collected and underwent routine analysis, as well as being snap-frozen and banked for the measurement of U1cSOD1 concentrations. Sampling was limited to treatment events during lumbar punctures because more frequent collection would be onerous for dogs and owners. Our aim was to assess whether U1cSOD1 was detectable in the CSF at the end of each treatment period. After the period of tilting, the dogs were recovered from anesthesia and placed in the wards for observation overnight. In order to determine peak serum concentrations and elimination via the urinary tract, blood was collected 1, 4, and 24 hours post-procedure, and urine samples were taken every 2 hours for 24 hours. When the dogs were unable to walk because of disease progression, a urinary catheter and a closed urine collection system were placed and used to sample urine. Blood was centrifuged at 2000g for 5 minutes, and serum was banked in 10 μL aliquots. Dogs were re-examined 24 hours after dosing and again before discharge to their owners. Treatments and evaluations were repeated monthly until a dose escalation to 3 mg/kg and increasing the injection interval to 2 months near the end of the study ( ). The higher dose was justified by the safety of prior 1.5 mg/kg injections in DM dogs 1-2, which supported advancing DM dogs 3-4 to 3 mg/kg after only 2 lower-dose treatments.
 The study ended when owners opted for euthanasia because of the progression of neurologic signs. At that time, the euthanasia and sampling protocols described for phase 1 were followed. Veterinary pathologists (D.T. and S.S.) examined all samples for lesions consistent with DM and evidence of neurotoxicity or other adverse reactions.

## Sample processing
All CSF, serum, and tissue samples were placed in RNAlater and frozen, after which they were processed by one of the investigators (S.I.G.). Phase 1 samples were processed for each pair of dogs to inform the dosing of subsequent dogs. Phase 2 samples (brain regions and organs) were processed immediately after the dogs were euthanized. Ribonucleic acid (RNA) was extracted from the snap-frozen tissue using a Trizol-based homogenization method, and a Bead Bug apparatus (Benchmark Scientific, Sayreville, NJ), and titanium beads.

## RT-qPCR and 32P-Northern blotting
Ribonucleic acid from each tissue sample underwent RT-qPCR analysis following the same method used for cell culture screening as described in Figure S1. Data normalization used the 2-∆∆Ct method and equal RNA input for all RT-qPCR reactions. Duplex, rather than monoplex qPCR, was performed to increase rigor and decrease variability. 32P-Northern blotting on serum, urine, CSF, and tissue RNA (see Figure S5) was used both to quantify U1cSOD1 and to verify that it was full-length rather than being deactivated by truncation. 32P-Northern blotting is highly sensitive (picograms U1cSOD1) and rigorous in its quantification and ability to distinguish truncated (n-1, n-2, n-3) products from full-length U1cSOD1.

## Results

## Phase 1
Six of the healthy dogs were female beagles, ranging in weight from 7.5 to 8.9 kg and in age from 12 to 21 months, and the seventh healthy dog was a male neutered hound, which weighed 27 kg and was 54 months old. Placement of the subarachnoid catheters proved challenging in the first 2 dogs because of their small size. The technique, therefore, was modified to inject directly from a 20- or 22-gauge spinal needle for all remaining dogs. The first injection achieved a level of silencing that was acceptable (attaining between 50 and 75% silencing ) and, thus, 1.5 mg/kg was used for the 6 beagles.
All dogs recovered from anesthesia uneventfully, and no changes were detected on physical and neurologic examinations or on blood or CSF analysis, and no microscopic findings attributed to U1cSOD1 were identified on histopathological review. The SOD1 silencing and U1cSOD1 tissue concentrations at day 5 after injection are shown in (values in ). The highest SOD1 silencing and tissue concentrations of U1cSOD1 were found in the spinal cord, with both decreasing with increasing distance from the injection site. The hound dog received a 3 mg/kg dose of U1cSOD1 and with euthanasia on day 30 showed persistent silencing of SOD1 expression, with tissue concentrations that were higher than those of dogs dosed at 1.5 mg/kg in all CNS regions ( and ). The male neutered dog with stage III DM had an estimated age of 9 years and weighed 21.6 kg. The dog was non-ambulatory with thoracic limb paresis, paraplegia, and marked muscle atrophy of the pelvic limbs with absent patellar reflexes bilaterally. Withdrawal reflexes were intact in all 4 limbs, but decreased in the pelvic limbs. The dog had dysphonia and intermittent fecal and urinary incontinence. No adverse effects occurred after the intrathecal administration of 1.5 mg/kg U1cSOD1, and the neurological signs were unchanged during the 5 days before euthanasia. Suppression of SOD1 expression and U1cSOD1 tissue concentrations were comparable to those observed in the beagles ( and ).
 

## Phase 2
Details of the 4 dogs that were recruited into the study, and the number, dose, and frequency of treatments, are provided in Tables S2 and S3. All dogs showed progression of their clinical signs of DM ( ). Two dogs were euthanized when signs of fecal incontinence appeared, and 1 dog died because of metastatic hemangiosarcoma. The final dog was lost to follow-up during the coronavirus pandemic. When last evaluated this dog was paraplegic. Across the 4 dogs, the range of time for progression from onset to stage II was 7-13 months, with a median of 9.5 months and a mean of 9.75 months. No clinically relevant changes in blood test results were observed over the course of the study, which is indicative of a good safety profile. The procedure was performed successfully in all dogs at each planned injection. One of the dogs (Dog 4) had severe osteoarthritis affecting the articular processes of the caudal lumbar spine, and needle placement was challenging but successful at each attempt. Adverse events were unusual. Two dogs each had 1 episode of hemodynamic instability during their injections. In both instances, the back flow of CSF after needle placement was extremely rapid before the attachment of the administration set. In the first dog, approximately halfway through the injection, the dog’s heart rate and blood pressure started to increase, a decrease in the plane of anesthesia was suspected, and a small IV bolus of propofol was administered. The propofol did not have any effect, and a slight resistance to injection was noted. The remainder of the injection was given very slowly, and at the time of recovery, the dog was dysphoric with tail and pelvic limb twitching. Mannitol (Hospira IN, Lake Forest, IL) was administered at 0.5 g/kg IV over 20 minutes, and the clinical signs resolved over a period of 1 hour. These changes did not recur with subsequent injections in this dog. The second dog followed a similar course, with dramatic backflow of CSF on needle placement and then an increase in blood pressure and heart rate. A standard dose of the injection was administered rather than a double dose as planned, and the injection was terminated. The dog recovered from anesthesia without incident, and this response was not seen in subsequent injections. Out of 26 treatments (34 including the phase 1 dogs) these adverse events happened twice, and both appeared to be related to CSF pressure.
 

## Blood test results and CSF findings
Although the dogs were older, and their blood test results were not entirely normal at the outset (eg, dog 1 had low serum albumin and low calcium concentrations), no clinically relevant changes in blood test results obtained before each treatment session were observed. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis was performed on 27 occasions when performing injections; 2 of these samples had macroscopic blood contamination. The analyses identified normal to increased protein concentrations ranging from 31.4 to 79.5 mg/dL over the course of the study. White blood cell counts in the CSF ranged from 0 to 10 cells/μL, with mononuclear pleocytosis. Two samples had increased CSF white blood cell counts (22 and 29 cells/μL, dogs 3 and 4) but both had macroscopic blood contamination. In subsequent samples from the same dogs, the white blood cell counts were not abnormally high.

## SOD1 silencing in CNS and U1cSOD1 concentrations in urine, plasma, CSF, and CNS
The U1cSOD1 spiked transiently approximately 1 hour after injection, and then was rapidly eliminated from the plasma and undetectable by 24 hours after administration ( and ). Urine concentrations peaked approximately 12 hours after administration and were under 0.5 μg/mL by 24 hours ( and ), but still were detectable at 30 days after administration. Higher concentrations were seen when larger doses were administered. Concentration of U1cSOD1 in the CSF was measured in 16 samples, 2 samples before treatment (undetectable), 2 samples taken 8 weeks after a 3 mg/kg dose (390 and 490 ng/mL) and 12 samples taken 4—6 weeks after a 1.5 mg/kg dose (median, 43.5 ng/mL; range, 13—190 ng/mL).
 The time from the last treatment to euthanasia and tissue sampling was 10 weeks for Dog 1, 12 weeks for Dog 2 and 5 weeks for Dog 3 with the differences being a consequence of owner scheduling requests or the coronavirus pandemic shutdown. Brain and spinal cord tissue concentrations of U1cSOD1 showed a gradient, with high concentrations present within the spinal cord, and decreasing concentrations the more cranial the site sampled ( and ). The extent of SOD1 silencing was calculated using the vehicle-treated healthy dogs as the control and showed inadequate silencing in regions of the CNS farther from the injection site ( ). Combining these results shows that the best silencing was achieved in the cervical region, with a median of 37% of normal.
 

## Histopathology

## Phase 1
All dogs from phase 1 underwent complete necropsies. Mild focal axon degeneration was observed in the caudal lumbar spinal cord of the first 2 beagle dogs, which was attributed to trauma secondary to the placement of the intrathecal catheter. Similar changes were not noted in subsequent dogs, in which injections were made via a spinal needle. In the dog with stage III DM, a microscopic evaluation of the spinal cord was consistent with advanced DM, and associated pelvic limb skeletal muscle atrophy was noted. No microscopic findings attributed to U1cSOD1 were observed in any dog.

## Phase 2
Three dogs from phase 2 underwent complete necropsies. Microscopically, all dogs had bilaterally symmetric nerve fiber degeneration and demyelination in the dorsolateral and ventromedial white matter, consistent with DM ( ). No evidence of additional pathology was noted in the lumbar intumescence near the injection site, but 1 dog had some fibrosis in the meninges at the injection site. No microscopic findings attributable to adverse effects of U1cSOD1 were noted in any dog, and no evidence of increased inflammation was noted in the CNS. No histopathological findings suggestive of toxicity were observed in the other organs. All 3 dogs examined were older and had changes consistent with aging, such as mitral valve disease. No evidence of hepatic or renal toxicity was observed. Dog 2 had metastatic hemangiosarcoma, with neoplastic cells identified in the spleen, liver, and heart, with additional evidence of early neoplastic change in the thyroid and adrenal glands.
 

## Discussion
We demonstrated that the intrathecal administration of a U1AO targeting canine SOD1 (U1cSOD1) is both safe and capable of decreasing SOD1 expression in the CNS of healthy and DM-affected dogs. Our study represents the first evaluation of U1AO technology in a non-rodent mammalian species with a naturally occurring disease and an extended duration of treatment. We recognize that DM-affected dogs might have different levels of SOD1 expression, and any further development of this therapeutic agent should include DM dogs receiving a vehicle alone.
Initial testing of U1cSOD1 in healthy dogs provided dosing and safety data before moving to diseased dogs. Healthy dogs and 1 stage III DM dog were treated with a single intrathecal dose, whereas DM-affected dogs were treated monthly (2-7 treatments) followed by 2 to 3 treatments at an escalated dose at 2-month intervals, with 2 dogs treated for 10 months. All dogs tolerated the procedure and test compound without adverse effects, other than 2 episodes of hemodynamic instability during injections. Histopathologic analysis of CNS tissues confirmed the diagnosis of DM and identified no adverse findings related to treatment, but more subtle neuroinflammation cannot be ruled out without a comparison to vehicle-treated animals. No clinically relevant changes in hematologic, biochemical, or CSF variables were observed over the course of treatment, indicating that the procedure and test compound were safe.
Decreasing CNS SOD1 expression has shown therapeutic benefits in ALS rodent models, with even partial knockdown delaying disease onset and extending survival. These findings supported subsequent clinical trials and the accelerated approval of tofersen, an ASO targeting SOD1 mRNA, by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). The approval was based on biomarker changes rather than clear functional improvements and has sparked a debate emphasizing the need to explore alternative platforms such as the U1 Adaptor. These clinical trials have clearly demonstrated a decrease in CSF neurofilament concentrations, supporting the hypothesis that lowering neuronal SOD1 production decreases neuronal death. A recent publication in rodent ALS models emphasizes that preclinical work remains active. Defining an appropriate SOD1 silencing level remains imprecise, largely because such experiments are challenging in the relevant target population. Evidence from rodent and primate models of SOD1 ALS suggests that silencing levels of 50%–75% can substantially alter the disease trajectory. Concerns exist that lowering SOD1 expression could cause harm by removing an antioxidant pathway, particularly if, in vivo, the E40KSOD1 aggregates do inhibit such activity. , Consequently, researchers in the field do not recommend 100% silencing of the gene.
Studies in humans do not report the percentage of SOD1 silencing. Instead, decreases in CSF SOD1 protein concentrations of approximately 30% have been observed, but its correlation with the accumulation of misfolded protein in the spinal cord remains unclear. Progression of neuronal injury is reflected by CSF neurofilament light (NFL) concentrations, and these are used as a biomarker in clinical trials in humans. However, variability among individuals and disease stages limits interpretation in small cohorts. , In our study, the CSF U1cSOD1 concentration was prioritized over NFL quantification because of sample volume and cost constraints.
The positive safety profile seen in dogs in our study is consistent with previous studies in mice showing U1AOs to be efficacious and safe. The first large in vivo study (>900 mice, > 110 treatment groups), targeted 2 different oncogenes and demonstrated the mechanism of action both in vitro and in vivo. Efficacy was achieved at doses far below those reported for ASOs or siRNAs, with no immune response or organ toxicity observed.
Although our study focused on dose escalation and safety over a formal assessment of efficacy, disease progression in treated DM dogs was consistent with previously reported natural history data. The time to stage II (median, 9.5; range, 7-13 months), aligned closely with the published median of 10 months (range, 6-12) in untreated DM-affected dogs, supporting the safety of U1cSOD1. The small number (n = 4) of DM-afflicted dogs provides a baseline for further work but limits statistically based conclusions. Furthermore, treatment did not improve gait scores, suggesting that earlier intervention may be required. In this safety and dose escalation trial, we recruited DM-affected dogs in stage I, but allowed gait scores as high as 6 (on the cusp of stage II), whereas a future efficacy trial should recruit dogs with scores <3. Recent clinical trials suggested that treatment should be instituted as early in the disease course as possible , and currently, a phase 3 ALS trial is recruiting preclinical patients. 
A key finding of our study is that U1cSOD1 achieved targeted SOD1 silencing in both brain and spinal cord tissue in a large animal model. This finding represents an important result, considering that U1AO technology offers several potential advantages over viral or RNAi-based approaches, including sequence specificity, low immunogenicity, and scalable, non-viral manufacturing. However, the persistence of clinical decline despite treatment emphasizes the importance of early intervention and current limitations in CNS distribution. Although gene silencing was achieved in the brain, the overall delivery efficiency was suboptimal, likely because it had to spread from the injection site, producing a decreasing gradient from the lumbar spinal cord. Although the initial and most severe lesions in DM are focused on the thoracolumbar spinal cord, as DM progresses involvement of the entire spinal cord and brainstem occurs. This lack of broad U1AO distribution in dogs also was seen when U1AOs were administered intrathecally in mice. Future studies should incorporate an improved formulation to allow crossing of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) by IV administration to achieve a more uniform distribution. An alternative approach would be infusion via a ventricular catheter or by cerebellomedullary cisternal administration.
The conservative dosing reflected safety priorities while maintaining ability to achieve a significant level of SOD1 silencing in healthy dogs. After intrathecal administration, a rapid spike and then elimination of U1cSOD1 occurred from the plasma, with longer persistence in the urine suggesting transient renal residency. Naked, unformulated oligonucleotides, such as U1cSOD1, have an extremely short serum half-life, typically less than 5 minutes. The increased U1cSOD1 concentrations in the kidneys of DM dogs are consistent with reports of renal accumulation of oligonucleotides. 
Benchmark silencing levels vary for each disease and are difficult to determine, as indicated by the fact that highly potent FDA-approved GalNac-siRNAs achieve >70% decrease in the liver but only a 30%-40% decrease in the CNS. We administered U1cSOD1 at 1.5 mg/kg, escalating the dosage cautiously in 1 healthy dog to evaluate the tolerability and durability of the response. The goal was to increase the interval between doses in the DM-affected dogs and to achieve more SOD1 suppression along the neuraxis. Future studies should explore higher dosages to determine whether increased CNS exposure can improve target knockdown and the therapeutic effect.
Intrathecal administration in dogs requires general anesthesia, posing a burden for the dogs, owners, and the research team, in addition to adding cost. General anesthesia adds risk, particularly in the older dog population that suffers from DM. In addition, although rare, 2 apparent instances of increased CSF pressure occurred and interfered with the intrathecal injections. Careful evaluation of the anesthesia protocols in both dogs in which this complication occurred did not identify any likely underlying cause, and in both cases, the dogs underwent subsequent injections using the same technique with no adverse effects. A product that can cross the BBB would simplify administration and improve CNS distribution. Future studies will evaluate BBB-crossing peptides conjugated to U1AOs that, unlike ASOs and siRNAs, retain activity when conjugated. This property is related to the U1AO’s unique mechanism of relying only on base pairing, unlike ASOs and siRNAs that require host enzymatic function that is decreased upon conjugation to peptides. Future work also will refine delivery, dose, schedule, and combination strategies to enhance therapeutic efficacy. If confirmed in trials with objective clinical, molecular, and imaging endpoints, this strategy could represent a novel therapeutic approach.
Our study had some limitations, including the lack of both vehicle-treated affected dogs and the determination of protein concentrations. Quantification of total and aggregated SOD1 should be included in future investigations comparing treated with placebo groups. As a consequence of limited funding, RT-qPCR was prioritized over immunohistochemistry or Western blotting because RT-qPCR is relatively sensitive, quantitative, and straightforward to perform. Efforts to measure the CSF SOD1 protein concentration failed for technical reasons, but the RNA data provided a solid foundation for future development.
Despite its limitations, our study provides foundational in vivo evidence that U1AO-mediated gene silencing is safe, scalable, and feasible in a large mammalian model. The approach holds promise for improving disease outcomes and ultimately could result in a cure. Such a goal is not only of interest in veterinary medicine for DM but also as a valuable model for ALS in humans, because both diseases have similar pathological mechanisms, including mutations in the SOD1 gene. , This shared pathophysiology emphasizes the importance of DM research in contributing to the broader field of neurodegenerative disease therapeutics.
