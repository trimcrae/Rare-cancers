While PCT2, enriched in adult mouse cortex, mediates ischemic neuronal death via interactions with WAVE1 and Bcl-xL, perinatal isoforms (A2-PCTs, namely PCT3 and PCT4) remain poorly defined. Given the vulnerability of the developing brain to hypoxia-induced neurological deficits, here we asked whether A2-PCTs contribute to ischemic damage in the developing cortex. In primary cortical neurons, knockdown of PCTs mitigated cell death induced by oxygen-glucose deprivation. Consistently, using A2-PCTs knockout mice subjected to middle cerebral artery occlusion, we observed significantly reduced cortical damage in younger animals, implicating A2-PCTs as pro-death factors in hypoxic conditions. Mechanistically, A2-PCTs formed a tripartite complex with Bcl-xL and WAVE1. Overexpression of A2-PCTs in HEK293 cells resulted in elevated cell mortality, and as revealed by SPLICS (a split-GFP-based contact site sensor)-based imaging of mitochondria-ER contact sites (MERCs), increased its localization to MERCs. Furthermore, A2-PCTs interacted with GRP75, a MERCs-tethering protein, in a Bcl-xL/WAVE1-dependent manner. A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex increased IP3R-mediated calcium transfer from the ER to mitochondria, leading to cytosolic and mitochondrial calcium overload, which was attenuated by IP3R inhibition. In Neuro2a cells subjected to oxygen-glucose deprivation, PCTs knockdown similarly suppressed pathological calcium flux. Our study identifies A2-PCTs as key regulators of MERCs-mediated calcium dysregulation in neonatal stroke, pointing to their potential as therapeutic targets for mitigating ischemic brain injury in the developing brain.

## Introduction
Stroke, a leading cause of disability and mortality worldwide, occurs when cerebral blood flow is disrupted , . 80% of the cases are ischemic stroke, which results from vascular occlusion and triggers a complex cascade of events culminating in neuronal death – . Intracellular calcium overload is a well-established key mediator of ischemic neuronal injury, with higher calcium levels correlating with poorer clinical outcomes . Pediatric ischemic stroke, particularly in neonates, often leads to delays in diagnosis and specific neurological sequelae such as seizures .
Pancortin (PCT), also known as Noelin1 or olfactomedin1 , was first cloned in a mouse by us . The four splicing isoforms of PCT share a central B-region (part B) with two variants on the N-terminal (part A1 or A2) and C-terminal (part C1 or C2) sides, respectively . Among them, A1-PCTs (PCT1: A1-B-C1, and PCT2: A1-B-C2) are mainly expressed in the cortex of adult mice, while A2-PCTs (PCT3: A2-B-C1, and PCT4: A2-B-C2) are expressed in the cortex during development , . PCT2 was previously reported to biochemically associate with mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) fractions (as revealed by cox1 and glucose-regulated protein-78 (GRP78) expression in subcellular fractionation), and to interact with Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein verprolin homologous-1 (WAVE1) and the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-xL in a mitochondria-associated protein complex, leading to a novel cascade of ischemic neuronal death in adult mice . Based on this picture, we focused on the potential functions of perinatally dominant A2-PCTs (PCT3 and PCT4) in pediatric ischemic stroke pathogenesis. We thus explored whether A2-PCTs also associate with Bcl-xL and WAVE1 and play a role in pediatric ischemic stroke outcomes.
The primary function assigned to the WAVE1 protein is to act as a nucleation promoter for the Arp2/3 complex. In basal conditions, WAVE1 binds to the outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM), but it is largely inactive due to Cdk5-mediated phosphorylation . Notably, the cytoskeletal remodeling capacity of WAVE1 exhibits spatiotemporal coordination with Bcl-family-mediated calcium homeostasis , . Bcl-xL is an anti-apoptotic protein in the B-cell lymphoma (Bcl-2) family of proteins. It is primarily known for its role in regulating apoptosis by preventing pore formation and subsequent caspase activation at the OMM. Recent studies have revealed its potential functions in regulating calcium-related cell death: In the mitochondria and ER contact sites (MERCs), the ER-localized inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor (IP3R) and the OMM-localized voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC) are bridged by GRP75 to form a tethered complex between the two organelles, enabling calcium transfer from ER to mitochondria . The BH4 (Bcl-2 homology) domain of Bcl-xL binds and inhibits the activation of VDAC . Studies have shown that ischemic stroke prognosis can be influenced by manipulating Bcl-xL or applying calcium inhibitors . We previously demonstrated that PCT-like immunoreactivity was localized by the ER . Later, Cheng et al. show that PCT-2 interacts with WAVE1 and Bcl-xL in a mitochondria-associated protein complex that mediates ischemic neuronal death . However, the underlying biological mechanisms leading to cell death, particularly the role of developmental PCT isoforms and their potential impact on calcium signaling at MERCs, remain elusive. We here demonstrate that the A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex forms at MERCs, where it interacted with GRP75 to exacerbate IP3R/VDAC-mediated calcium transfer, ultimately leading to mitochondrial calcium overload and neuronal death.

## Results

## Acute deficiency of PCTs mitigates hypoxia-induced neuronal death in cultured mouse cortical neurons
To validate the roles of PCTs in developmental neuronal vulnerability, we applied a knockdown strategy in naïve mouse primary cortical neurons. We first screened shRNAs targeting the conserved B-region of all PCT isoforms (PCT1-4, Fig. a). We overexpressed PCT1-4 as well as 5 constructed shRNA against the four isoforms in HEK293T cells which express no endogenous PCTs and conducted western blot assays (Fig. b). shPCTs #5 achieved the highest knockdown efficiency for PCT1-4 (Fig. c, Supplementary Fig. 1). We next transfected this shPCTs #5 at days in vitro (DIV) 4 in primary cortical neurons from embryonic days (E)16 mice, and at DIV5 subjected them to oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD), an in vitro model of neonatal ischemia (Fig. d). While OGD treatment induced robust cell death compared to the control, primary cultured neurons transfected with the shPCTs #5 exhibited significantly reduced cell death ratio compared to the OGD treatment (86% death in OGD vs. 34% in control, p < 0.001; vs. 62% death in OGD + KD, p < 0.01; Fig. e), indicating that PCTs are critical mediators of hypoxic injury in developing neurons.

 

## A2-PCTs form a pro-death complex with Bcl-xL and WAVE1
A previous study established the pro-apoptotic role of PCT2 (one of the A1-PCTs) through Bax activation . We investigated whether A2-PCTs orchestrate cell death through the same complex assembly as PCT2 molecular partners. We first explored the association between A2-PCTs and cell death in vitro. Through the exogenous expression of A2-PCTs, Bcl-xL, and WAVE1 in HEK293 cells, we observed a notable increase in cell death (> 30% death in P3 + P4+B + W vs. <10% in tdTomato control; p < 0.001, Fig. a, b). To ensure the robustness of this quantification, we performed a parallel evaluation using a fluorescence-based live/dead cell viability assay, which confirmed that the A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex significantly induces cell death consistent with our Trypan Blue data (Supplementary Fig. 2a). In order to exclude the effects of single components on cell death, we next exogenously expressed Bcl-xL, WAVE1, PCT3, PCT4, or A2-PCTs in HEK293 cells, and they did not induce cell death (Supplementary Fig. 2b). To elucidate the interaction among A2-PCTs, Bcl-xL, and WAVE1 proteins, we conducted exogenous expression in HEK293T cells followed by western blot and immunoprecipitation (IP) assay (Fig. c-e; Supplementary Figs. 3–5). IP assay revealed distinct interaction patterns for the two isoforms. PCT3 exhibited robust interactions with both Bcl-xL and WAVE1, suggesting a strong direct binding capacity. In contrast, PCT4 showed weaker interactions. While IP of PCT4 co-precipitated Bcl-xL (Fig. c), the reciprocal pull-down of Bcl-xL resulted in barely detectable PCT4 bands (Fig. d), and PCT4 binding to WAVE1 was notably faint (Fig. e). This weaker detection is likely attributable to the lower expression stability of PCT4 observed in lysates (Fig. c-e, lysate) and potentially lower binding affinity compared to PCT3. Nevertheless, the presence of the tripartite complex components suggests that while PCT3 forms a stable core, PCT4 participates with lower efficacy.

 

## A2-PCTs knockout reduces cortical damage in neonatal ischemic stroke models
A2-PCTs predominate among the four PCT splicing isoforms during murine development . To investigate the neuroprotective role of A2-PCTs in the context of ischemic stroke, we conducted middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) experiments utilizing 2-week-old and 5-week-old A2-PCTs knockout (KO) mice (Supplementary Fig. 6) and wild-type (WT) counterparts (Fig. a-d). Our investigations revealed discernible damage to both the cortex and striatum in both knockout and wild-type mice (Fig. b, d). Statistical analyses unveiled varying degrees of cortical and striatal damage induced by MCAO in the KO and WT mice. Specifically, MCAO resulted in approximately 20% damage to the striatum region in both groups. However, the extent of infarct volume in the cortex was notably milder in the A2-PCTs KO mice (10% vs. 20% in wild-type; p < 0.05, Fig. b). Notably, this disparity was exclusively evident in the 2-week-old mice, with no such distinction observed in the 5-week-old cohort (Fig. d). This observation aligns with our in vitro findings that PCTs knockdown reduces cell death ratio. Collectively, these findings suggest that the downregulation of A2-PCTs mitigates neuronal death of the developing neocortex in the context of stroke. It should also be noted that the lack of protection in older mice is consistent with the developmental shift wherein A1-PCTs (PCT1 and PCT2) are fully expressed in the cortex in 4 weeks after birth .

 

## A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex localizes to both mitochondria and ER
Based on our findings (Fig. ), PCT3 and PCT4 exhibited different binding affinities for Bcl-xL and WAVE1. To elucidate the subcellular localization of the A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex, we performed immunocytochemical analyses in HEK293T cells. We exogenously expressed PCT3 or PCT4 with Bcl-xL and WAVE1. PCT3 and PCT4 showed minimal mitochondrial colocalization when expressed alone (Pearson’s correlation coefficient = 0.54 in P3, 0.70 in P3 + B + W, 0.59 in P4, 0.69 in P4 + B + W; p < 0.01; Fig. a-d). In the presence of Bcl-xL and WAVE1, PCT3 exhibited enhanced colocalization with mitochondria (Fig. a, right; Fig. c) while PCT4 did not (Fig. b, right; Fig. d). Bcl-xL itself showed robust mitochondrial localization both alone and in complex with WAVE1 and PCT3 or PCT4 (Pearson’s correlation coefficient = 0.81 in B, 0.81 in B + P3 + W, 0.80 in B + P4 + W; Fig. e). Given that Bcl-xL is known to localize to both mitochondria and ER membranes under physiological conditions , and our previous biochemical studies suggested PCTs’ association with ER , we next examined whether the PCT3/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 and PCT4/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex also localize to the ER. Co-staining with ERseeing revealed that PCT3, PCT4, and Bcl-xL all exhibited substantial ER colocalization when assembled in the tripartite complex (Fig. f-j). PCT3 demonstrated stronger ER association than PCT4 (Pearson’s correlation coefficient = 0.57 in P3, 0.67 in P3 + B + W, 0.45 in P4, 0.60 in P4 + B + W; p < 0.05, Fig. h, i). Bcl-xL exhibited a stronger ER association with PCT3 or PCT4 (Pearson’s correlation coefficient = 0.42 in B, 0.71 in B + P3 + W, 0.57 in B + P4 + W; p < 0.05, p < 0.001, Fig. j). Furthermore, by using a monoclonal antibody targeting the B portion of PCT, our immunoelectron microscopy analysis revealed that PCT is localized at MERCs, corroborating this at a higher resolution (Supplementary Fig. 7). Together, the low-resolution colocalization analyses and high-resolution electron microscopy analysis indicate that the PCT3/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 and PCT4/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex adopts a dual localization pattern at both mitochondrial and ER membranes, positioning it strategically at the interface between these two organelles—a characteristic feature of MERCs-associated protein complexes.

 

## A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL-K233S/WAVE1 complex localizes to both mitochondria and ER and A2-PCTs/ΔBH4-Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex induces cell death
To further investigate the role of Bcl-xL mitochondrial and ER targeting in complex assembly, we generated Bcl-xL-K233S, a mutant deficient in mitochondrial outer membrane localization (Pearson’s correlation coefficient = 0.80 in B vs. 0.20 in B-K233S; p < 0.001, Fig. a, b). The Bcl-xL-K233S mutant showed a preferable localization pattern to the ER than Bcl-xL (Pearson’s correlation coefficient = 0.42 in B vs. 0.67 in B-K233S; p < 0.001, Fig. c). Exogenous expression of A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL-K233S/WAVE1 complex did not alter cell death (> 30% death in P3 + P4 + B-K233S + W vs. <10% in tdTomato control; p < 0.001, Fig. d). While Bcl-xL-K233S alone showed minimal mitochondrial colocalization, the presence of PCT3 or PCT4 together with WAVE1 colocalized to mitochondria (Pearson’s correlation coefficient = 0.22 in B-K233S vs. 0.64 in B-K233S + P3 + W, p < 0.001; vs. 0.53 in B-K233S + P4 + W, p < 0.05; Fig. e), as well as to the ER (Pearson’s correlation coefficient = 0.67 in B, 0.68 in B-K233S + P3 + W, 0.62 in B-K233S + P4 + W; Fig. f), suggesting that A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL-K233S/WAVE1 complex localizes to both mitochondria and ER. Since we evaluated the colocalization with a light microscope, it should be noted that the term ‘colocalization’ here includes the localization just by the mitochondria and/or ER. Collectively, the fact that cell death persists and complex formation remains the same localization pattern suggests that the complex can assemble bilaterally or spans the mitochondria-ER interface, with PCT3 exhibiting stronger dual-organelle targeting capacity than PCT4. The dual localization of the A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex suggested a potential functional role at the mitochondria-ER interface. We next generated a BH4-deleted mutant (ΔBH4-Bcl-xL, Fig. g) and assessed its impact on cell viability. It is shown that BH4 domain of Bcl-xL, but not that of Bcl-2, selectively targets VDAC1 and inhibits apoptosis by decreasing VDAC1-mediated Ca2+ uptake into the mitochondria . Remarkably, co-expression of A2-PCTs, WAVE1, and ΔBH4-Bcl-xL resulted in dramatically enhanced cell death (> 50%; p < 0.001, Fig. h), suggesting that loss of BH4-mediated VDAC inhibition exacerbates the pro-death activity of the complex. Importantly, application of the intracellular calcium chelator BAPTA-AM (10 µM) significantly suppressed cell death induced by Bcl-xL, Bcl-xL-K233S, and ΔBH4-Bcl-xL in complex with A2-PCTs and WAVE1 (Fig. i), demonstrating that calcium dysregulation is the critical mechanism underlying A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1-mediated cell death.

 

## A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex increases MERCs and interacts with GRP75
Based on these findings (Figs.  and ), we hypothesized that the A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex exerts its effects at the MERCs. To test our hypothesis, we procured the mitochondria-ER contact indicator tools SPLICS (a split-GFP-based contact site sensor) -L and SPLICS-S , , a sensor that fluoresces in proximity to the mitochondria and ER (Supplementary Fig. 8a; SPLICS-L fluoresces to the mitochondria and ER with a distance range of ≈ 40–50 nm, while SPLICS-S fluoresces to a distance of ≈ 8–10 nm). We first tested whether exogenously expressing SPLICS restrained cell death induced by Bcl-xL, WAVE1, and A2-PCTs, and the results demonstrated that the utilization of SPLICS-L or SPLICS-S did not reduce cell death (cell death < 10% in SPLICS vs. > 30% in A2-PCTs, Bcl-xL, and WAVE1, p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 8b). Subsequently, we compared the proportion of SPLICS-positive cells under different conditions. Exogenous expression of Bcl-xL and WAVE1 did not increase the proportion of SPLICS-positive cells, but the expression of the A2-PCTs, Bcl-xL, and WAVE1 led to a notable increase in the proportion of SPLICS-positive cells (17.5% in SPLICS-L positive, p < 0.01, 12.5% in SPLICS-S positive, p < 0.05 vs. 10% in control, Fig.  a). The increase in SPLICS-positive cells suggests that A2-PCTs, Bcl-xL, and WAVE1 played a role in promoting the formation of mitochondria-ER contact sites. Additionally, we found that exogenous expression of A2-PCTs, Bcl-xL, and WAVE1 exhibited high colocalization of A2-PCTs with SPLICS (SPLICS-L: Pearson’s correlation coefficient = 0.92 in PCT3 + Bcl-xL+WAVE1, 0.51 in PCT4 + Bcl-xL+WAVE1, 0.90 in PCT3 + PCT4+Bcl-xL+WAVE1, p < 0.001; SPLICS-S: Pearson’s correlation coefficient = 0.96 in PCT3 + Bcl-xL+WAVE1, 0.39 in PCT4 + Bcl-xL+WAVE1, 0.88 in PCT3 + PCT4+Bcl-xL+WAVE1, p < 0.001; Fig.  b), which suggested that A2-PCTs and MERCs are structurally linked. We recorded fluorescent signals at three-hour intervals on HEK293T cells expressing SPLICS under different conditions (Fig.  c, d). SPLICS began to be expressed at 6 h post-transfection and reached a peak at 24 h post-transfection. Moreover, the IP results showed the interaction between the A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex and the MERCs tethering protein GRP75. PCT3, and PCT4 were co-immunoprecipitated with GRP75 in the presence of WAVE1 and Bcl-xL (Fig.  e, Supplementary Fig. 9), contrasting with the lack of interactions with GRP75 in single PCT3 or PCT4 expression.

 

## A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex triggers calcium dysregulation through IP3R-mediated ER-to-mitochondria calcium transfer
The suppression of cell death induced by A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex using BAPTA-AM suggested a potential role of A2-PCTs in regulating inter-organellar calcium signaling at MERCs. To monitor the impact of the A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex on calcium dynamics directly, we performed time-lapse calcium imaging using organelle-specific fluorescent calcium indicators. First, using the cytosolic calcium indicator GCaMP6 (Fig. a, top), we found that co-expression of A2-PCTs, Bcl-xL, and WAVE1 induced a progressive elevation in cytosolic calcium levels, which reached a peak at 18 h post-transfection. In parallel, we used the mitochondrial calcium indicator CEPIA3mt (Fig. b, top) and ER calcium indicator CEPIA1er (Fig. c, top) to assess mitochondrial and ER calcium content. We observed an elevation of mitochondrial calcium levels and a corresponding depletion of ER calcium stores in HEK293T cells expressing the A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex, with PCT3/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex inducing more pronounced calcium accumulation than PCT4/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex. Notably, expression of Bcl-xL and WAVE1 alone did not significantly alter calcium dynamics in any compartment, confirming that A2-PCTs are essential drivers of this calcium dysregulation. To exclude potential bias arising from variations in cell numbers, we quantified cell density under bright-field microscopy at the 18-hour time point in the CEPIA3mt experiment, revealing no significant differences among the groups (Supplementary Fig. 10). This indicates a robust disruption of mitochondrial calcium homeostasis unconfounded by cell survival rates at the cytosolic calcium signal peak time.

 

Given that the IP3R-GRP75-VDAC axis is the primary conduit for ER-to-mitochondria calcium transfer at MERCs , we examined whether the A2-PCTs-mediated calcium dysregulation depends on IP3R activity. Treatment with 50 μM 2-Aminoethyl Diihenilborinate (2-APB),(micro molar) a pharmacological inhibitor of IP3R, markedly attenuated the changes in cytosolic, mitochondrial, and ER calcium induced by the A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex (Fig. a-c, bottom), demonstrating that IP3R-mediated calcium release from ER was required for calcium dysregulation. Additional calcium imaging data using Bcl-xL-K233S and ΔBH4-Bcl-xL mutants (Supplementary Fig. ), showing similar patterns of calcium dysregulation, further supported the central role of A2-PCTs in this process.
To validate these findings in a relevant neuronal model of ischemia, we examined calcium dynamics in Neuro2a cells subjected to oxygen-glucose deprivation and reperfusion (OGD/R), a condition known to induce calcium overload. We first confirmed the expression of PCT1-4, Bcl-xL, and WAVE1 in Neuro2a cells (Fig.  d). Neuro2a cells were transfected with shPCTs or shScramble control following OGD/R, the cytosolic calcium levels in the control group peaked at 18 h post-transfection. We similarly performed bright-field cell counting at this 18-hour time point and confirmed that there were no significant differences in cell numbers between the groups. In contrast, Neuro2a cells with knockdown of PCTs (OGD/R + KD) exhibited significantly reduced cytosolic and mitochondrial calcium levels compared to the OGD/R group (Fig.  e, f). Conversely, ER calcium stores were significantly higher and better preserved in the OGD/R + KD group (Fig.  g). This loss-of-function data mirrors the results in HEK293T cells, confirming that A2-PCTs are critical drivers of the pathological calcium flux from the ER to mitochondria during ischemic stress.

## Schematic representation of the composition of A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 at MERCs
The HEK293/293T cell experiments provided a steady dynamic environment and a clear framework for mechanistic insights. Based on our study, we proposed a schematic representation of the composition of A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 at MERCs (Fig. ). In our experiments, using the SPLICS tool, we observed that the exogenous expression of A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 significantly enhanced SPLICS signals. Under physiological conditions, A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 promoted MERCs by binding GRP75. The precise molecular architecture bridging GRP75 to VDAC in this complex remains to be fully defined. In our model, we depict this interface with a spatial gap and a question mark to propose the involvement of putative auxiliary linkers or to reflect a dynamic, regulatable coupling . Whether the complex functions are equivalent to both membranes or whether bilateral complexes can simultaneously bind GRP75, and the precise involvement of GRP75 in MERCs require further investigation. The absence of Bcl-xL’s BH4 domain triggered VDAC activation-mediated cell death . The Bcl-xL-K233S mutation (lysine 233 substituted with serine), which ablated mitochondrial localization signals , could be rescued via A2-PCTs/WAVE1 complex formation. Furthermore, genetic ablation of A2-PCTs disrupted the A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex assembly, consequently ameliorating calcium flux.

 

## Discussion
Our study identified PCT3 and PCT4 as critical drivers of ischemic neuronal death in the developing neocortex by forming a mitochondria-associated complex with Bcl-xL and WAVE1 and interacting with the MERCs-tethering protein GRP75, thereby elevating mitochondrial calcium levels. This work is the first to dissect the isoform-specific role of A2-PCTs in pediatric stroke, providing direct evidence linking A2-PCTs to MERCs reinforcement.
Developmentally, the specific neuroprotection is observed in 2-week-old KO mice, but not in 5-week-old mice. A2-PCTs knockout likely attenuates calcium overload in 2-week-old mice, while the lack of protection in 5-week-old mice may be attributed to the presence of A1-PCTs expression in the cortex at this stage, which abolishes the protective effect of A2-PCTs deletion. While our shRNA strategy targeted all PCT isoforms, the protective phenotype observed in the A2-PCT (PCT3/4) specific KO mice suggests that the developmental A2-PCT isoforms are the primary mediators of ischemic neuronal death in the neonate. Bcl-xL and WAVE1 are expressed in the cortex throughout developing stages and participate in mitochondrial-associated multiprotein complexes – . While exogenous Bcl-xL and WAVE1 can enhance MERCs activity alone, they are not cytotoxic without the A2-PCTs “trigger” in this context, suggesting that A2-PCTs act as a pathological reinforcer under ischemic stress. Under pathological conditions like neonatal stroke, where excitotoxicity triggers massive calcium influx , , the pre-formed A2-PCTs-containing MERCs likely act as “super-highways” for calcium uptake into mitochondria.
In terms of isoform specificity, our biochemical data revealed that PCT3 exhibits significantly more robust physical interactions with Bcl-xL and WAVE1 compared to PCT4 (Fig. ), while the combined expression produced additive cell death and calcium flux effects. Since the primary difference between these isoforms lies in their C-termini (C1 for PCT3 vs. C2 for PCT4), the C1 domain likely confers higher binding affinity for the tripartite complex , . This affinity gap explains why PCT3 is a more potent driver of MERCs formation and cell death than PCT4, despite their similar expression patterns. The structural differences between C1 and C2 domains also likely enable PCT1 (the predominant isoform in mature cortex-expressed A1-PCT) to exert greater functional dominance over PCT2.
We observed that Bcl-xL-K233S, a mutant lacking mitochondrial targeting capacity, regained mitochondrial co-localization and pro-death activity when co-expressed with A2-PCTs and WAVE1. This suggests that the A2-PCTs/WAVE1 sub-complex can effectively recruit or anchor Bcl-xL at the ER-mitochondria interface, overriding its intrinsic localization deficit. Furthermore, we found that the ΔBH4-Bcl-xL mutant, which lacks the VDAC-inhibitory BH4 domain, induced significantly higher cell death rate compared to wild-type Bcl-xL in the presence of A2-PCTs. Since the BH4 domain of Bcl-xL is essential for keeping VDAC in a closed conformation to prevent calcium overload, this result implies that while the A2-PCTs complex promotes physical tethering via GRP75, the “opening” of VDAC channels remains the execution step for mitochondrial calcium overload and subsequent membrane potential collapse. The exacerbated lethality of the ΔBH4-Bcl-xL mutant confirms that A2-PCTs drive death primarily by facilitating the structural machinery (MERCs) for calcium transfer, which becomes catastrophic when VDAC gating is uninhibited.
Our calcium imaging results further dissected this pathway. The application of 2-APB, a specific IP3R inhibitor, effectively blocked the calcium accumulation in mitochondria induced by the tripartite complex. This pharmacological evidence pinpoints the IP3R on the ER side as the upstream calcium source, confirming that A2-PCTs do not merely disrupt general mitochondrial homeostasis but specifically amplify the IP3R-GRP75-VDAC calcium transfer axis.
Regarding the structural topology of these contact sites, it is intriguing that A2-PCTs expression increased signals for both SPLICS-S (8–10 nm) and SPLICS-L (40–50 nm). This structural flexibility may allow the complex to accommodate the dynamic movements of organelles while maintaining efficient calcium tunneling across varying inter-organellar gaps. In addition, since PCTs could exist in dimerized form , it is possible that A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex forms a dimer and/or multi-mer, then works in the MERCs.
MERCs facilitate calcium transfer for energy metabolism and engage proteins like GRP75 , . The structural optimization that A2-PCTs form the complex at MERCs (SPLICS-L ≈ 40–50 nm, SPLICS-S ≈ 8–10 nm, and ER-mitochondria in electron microscopy) suggests that the A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex does not lock MERCs into a single rigid distance. Instead, it likely facilitates a pleomorphic tethering structure or stabilizes dynamic contact sites that fluctuate between tight and loose states , . This MERCs facilitation is highly likely to directly trigger mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening , and lead to neuronal death. While our imaging and interaction data strongly support the MERC localization of the A2-PCTs/Bcl-xL/WAVE1 complex, future studies employing subcellular fractionation will provide important biochemical validation of its precise partitioning between mitochondrial-associated and ER membranes.
The broader implications of our work may extend to neuroinflammation. Emerging evidence shows that MERCs dysregulation in cerebral ischemia triggers inflammation-independent mechanisms, e.g., NLRP3 inflammasome activation , . The observed Ca²⁺ overload in our study also resonates with recent findings that hexokinase 2 dissociation from the VDAC serves as a key initiator of NLRP3 inflammasome activation and calcium flux , , raising the possibility that A2-PCTs-mediated MERCs disruption may similarly prime inflammatory responses.
In conclusion, our study positions A2-PCTs as developmental reinforcers of MERCs calcium flux. By physically bridging Bcl-xL, WAVE1, and GRP75, A2-PCTs create a robust platform for ER-to-mitochondria calcium transfer. Targeting the interaction interfaces of A2-PCTs, specifically their high-affinity C1 domain or their binding with GRP75, may offer novel therapeutic strategies for mitigating calcium overload in neonatal ischemic brain injury.

## Methods

## Animals
Animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 and were approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee, The University of Osaka and the Animal Research Committee, University of Fukui. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. For primary neuron culture, pregnant ICR mice (Japan SLC Inc., Shizuoka, Japan) at E16 were rapidly killed by cervical dislocation with prior confirmation of anesthesia with isoflurane (Pfizer, New York, USA) to ensure unconsciousness for cortical dissection. Generation of A2-PCTs KO mice is described in a dedicated paragraph below. For ischemia models, A2-PCTs KO mice and wild-type littermates (male, 2-/5-week-old) were backcrossed with CB-17 mice (CLEA Japan Inc., Tokyo) for MCAO surgery as described , . For electron microscopic studies, details were previously described . All surgeries and subsequent analyses were performed blindly for genotype. Mice were maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle at 21–23 °C with free access to water/chow.

## Plasmid vector constructions
For shRNA constructs, oligonucleotides were annealed and ligated into the AgeI/EcoRI site of pLKO.1 vector (Plasmid # 10878, Addgene) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The control vector (pLKO.1-shScramble) was constructed by insertion of an oligonucleotide containing the sequence 5′-CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCG-3′ into the pLKO.1 vector as previously described . The target sequences of shPCT #1–5 against the B part of PCTs are as follows:
shPCT#1: 5′- gcaggtgtatctgcacagtgg − 3′.
shPCT#2: 5′- ggagagccataagcagcatct − 3′.
shPCT#3: 5′- catgtctcaatccatagaagt − 3′.
shPCT#4: 5′- ctcagagagacctgcagtacg − 3′.
shPCT#5: 5′- ctactggagaaggtgcaaaac − 3′.
For protein expression, plasmid vectors constructed using the pCAGGS-5MCS vector so that the cDNAs were driven under a CAG promoter. The cDNA of each PCT was subcloned into the pCAGGS vector in the previous study . Each PCT cDNA with a FLAG epitope sequence was subcloned into the pCAGGS-FLAG vector in this study. For pCAGGS-WAVE1-V5, the murine WAVE1 cDNA was subcloned into the BglII-NheI sites of the pCAGGS-V5 vector. For pCAGGS-Myc-Bcl-xL, the murine Bcl-xL cDNA was subcloned into the BglII-XhoI sites of the pCAGGS-Myc vector. The vectors expressing the mutant forms of Bcl-xL were constructed using the KOD-plus-mutagenesis kit (Toyobo, Tokyo, Japan) based on the pCAGGS-Myc-Bcl-xL. The primer pairs for the mutant forms of Bcl-xL were as follows: ΔBH4-Bcl-xL: 5’-AAGAGATCTAGCCCGGCCGTGAATGGAGCC-3’ and 5’-AAGCTCGAGCTTCCGACTGAAGAGTGAGCC-3’, and Bcl-xL-K233S: 5’-AAGAGATCTTCTCAGAGCAACCGGGAGCTG-3’ and 5’-AGCTCGAGTCAGCTCCGACTGAAGAGTGA-3’.

## Cell culture
HEK293 cells (National Institute of Health Sciences, Tokyo, Japan), HEK293T cells (American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), Manassas, VA, USA), Neuro2a cells (ATCC) were cultured in 1× Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Nichirei Biosciences Inc., Tokyo, Japan), 100 U/ml penicillin (Meiji Seika Pharma Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and 100 µg/ml streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, Missouri, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with a 5% CO2 air supply. For cell counting, immunocytochemistry, and colocalization analyses, HEK293 and HEK293T cells were cultured on four-well chambered glass coverslips (Matsunami Glass Ind., Ltd., Osaka, Japan) coated with 0.0033% poly-L-lysine (PLL, Sigma-Aldrich, diluted in distilled water). For immunoprecipitation, HEK293T cells were cultured in 10 cm plastic dishes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). In transfection, cells were transfected using polyethyleneimine (PEI, Polysciences, Inc., PA, USA) in accordance with the provided protocol. For calcium signal measuring, HEK293T and Neuro2a cells were cultured in 24-well plastic dishes (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

## Western blot assay
At 24 h post-transfection, each cell sample in 6-well culture plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was washed with 1000 µl ice cold phosphate buffered saline (PBS), transferred into a microcentrifuge tube with 1000 μl PBS (micro litter with small case). The cells were collected by centrifugation at 1000 x g at 4 °C for 10 min and lysed in 100 µl of lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, proteinase inhibitor cocktail (Nacalai Tesque)) and by sonicating for 10 s three times on ice, and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min with gentle rocking. Cell debris was removed by centrifuging at 15,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min to get the supernatant lysates. One µl cell lysate was mixed with 9 µl 1× SDS sample buffer (0.1% 2-Mercaptoethanol, 0.0005% Bromophenol blue, 10% glycerol, 2% electrophoresis-grade SDS, pH 6.8 63 mM Tris-HCl) and heated at 95 °C for 5 min. Cell lysates were separated through SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (EMD Millipore/Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). The membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk (Morinaga Milk Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) in TBST (0.05% Tween-20, 50 mM Tris-HCl, 138 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl) at room temperature for 1 h, and were incubated with the following horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated antibodies (Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd., Osaka, Japan): anti-FLAG-HRP antibody (1:1000), or anti-Myc-HRP antibody (1:1000) in room temperature for 1 h. Unbound antibodies were washed off by shaking the membranes in TBST three times for 15 min each. The peroxidase activity was detected using HRP substrate (EMD Millipore/Merck).

## Knockdown efficiency test
The knockdown efficiency of shRNA was determined in HEK293T cells by transfection with pCAGGS-PCT1-FLAG, pCAGGS-PCT2-FLAG, pCAGGS-PCT3-FLAG, or pCAGGS-PCT4-FLAG. Forty-eight hours after transfection, transfected cells were collected and extracted with SDS sample buffer and analyzed by western blotting. The intensities of bands were quantified and normalized with those for α-tubulin (anti-α-tubulin-HRP antibody, 1:1000, MBL, Carlsbad, USA) by software ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, MA, USA).

## Primary mouse cortical neuron culture
Cortical neurons were isolated from E16 ICR mouse embryos. Twenty-four-well culture plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were coated with 300 µl 1× PEI diluted in sterile Na₂B₄O₇ buffer (pH 8.4), incubated at room temperature for at least 15 min, then rinsed three times with sterile water and air-dried under sterile conditions. Embryos were rapidly extracted into ice-cold PBS. Cortices were dissected free of meninges under a stereomicroscope, pooled into a sterile tube. Papain solution was prepared by combining the following in D-PBS: Fifty µl DL-Cysteine-HCl (Sigma-Aldrich, 20 mg/ml), 50 µl bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, 20 mg/ml), 100 µl glucose (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 250 mg/ml), 50 µl papain (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1000 U/ml), 50 µl DNaseI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10 mg/ml), and 15 µl MgCl₂ (Sigma-Aldrich, 1 M). The solution was gently mixed and warmed to 37 °C before use. Cortical tissue was incubated in 5 ml papain solution for 20 min with gentle swirling. The digestion was terminated with 1 ml of horse serum (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Tissues were gently triturated in plating medium using pipettes to obtain single cells, filtered through a 40 μm cell filter, then centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min at 22 °C. Plating medium was prepared by combining 50% Neurobasal medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 25% horse serum, 25% HEPES-buffered saline, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cell pellet was resuspended in plating medium. Viability and concentration were calculated by Trypan Blue (Nacalai Tesque) staining on a cell counting plate. Cells were diluted to approximately 2 × 10⁵ cells/ml in plating medium and seeded onto PEI-coated plates at 1 × 10⁵ cells per well in a 24-well plate. Plates were placed in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator. After 4 h, the plating medium was replaced with culture medium. Culture medium was prepared by combining the following in Neurobasal medium: 2% B-27 plus supplement, 0.5 mM GlutaMAX supplement (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

## Primary cortical neuron transfection
Transfection of cortical neurons was performed at DIV4 using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientifi). For each well of a PEI-coated 24-well plate, 0.95 µg endotoxin-free pLKO.1-shPCTs #5, or 0.95 µg endotoxin-free pLKO.1-shScramble, and 0.05 µg endotoxin-free pCAGGS-tdTomato were diluted in 50 µl neurobasal medium, while 2 µl Lipofectamine 2000 was separately diluted in 50 µl neurobasal medium and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Solutions above were combined, gently mixed, and incubated for 20 min at room temperature to form DNA-lipid complexes. The DNA-lipid complex mixture (100 µl/well) was mixed with pre-warmed Neurobasal medium supplemented with 0.2 M L-Glutamine. The culture medium was aspirated and replaced with the neurobasal medium with DNA-lipid complex mixture, followed by incubation in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator at 37 °C for 4 h. After incubation, the transfection medium was aspirated and replaced with fresh culture medium. Transfected neurons were maintained for 24 h prior to the OGD assay.

## OGD assay
For OGD experiment on primary cortical neurons, the medium was aspirated, and primary cortical neurons were washed twice with a pre-warmed distilled PBS, and replaced with glucose-free DMEM (Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) supplemented with DAPI (1:1000, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) to assess cell viability via dye exclusion. Note that DAPI was applied to unfixed cells to label those with compromised plasma membranes (dead neurons), while intact viable neurons excluded the dye. Immediately after replacing the medium, the culture plates were placed into a multi-gas incubator (MCO-5MUV-PJ, Panasonic, Osaka, Japan) pre-equilibrated to hypoxic conditions. The cells were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in an atmosphere containing 5% oxygen, 5% CO2​, and balanced with N2​. Then the cells were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA)/PBS for 30 min and washed with PBS three times. The cells were then incubated with blocking solution for 1 h, and primary antibodies (see Supplementary Table) were diluted in blocking solution overnight at 4 °C to distinguish cultured neurons. After washing with PBS, the signals were visualized using fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (see Supplementary Table) for 1 h. Cells were washed in PBS three times prior to observation with a confocal microscope LSM880 with Airyscan (ZEISS, Jena, Germany). Cells labeled by tdTomato and polyclonal anti-MAP2 (Synaptic Systems, Goettingen, Germany) antibody were counted as living neurons, and cells labeled by tdTomato, MAP2, and DAPI were counted as dead neurons. Cell death ratio was calculated as a percentage that the number of dead neurons divided by the total number of dead and living neurons.

## Generation of A2-PCTs knockout mice
Conventional methods with targeted recombination in ES cells were used for the generation of A2-PCTs knockout mice. To eliminate A2-PCTs protein, we designed the targeting vector to replace A2-PCTs exon 1 with d2EGFP. A2-PCTs knockout mice were maintained on the C57BL/6J background strain. The A2-PCTs knockout mice showed normal growth and reproduction. The genotypes were determined by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using genomic DNA. A2-PCTs homozygotes were identified using the primer pairs (5’-AGAGTGGGTCCCTGAGTTCC-3’ and 5’-GCCTCCGCTCTGCATATTTA-3’) for wild-type allele and (5’-AGAGTGGGTCCCTGAGTTCC-3’ and 5’-CGTCGCCGTCCAGCTCGACCAG-3’) for mutant allele, respectively. The absence of A2-PCTs protein expression in the cerebral cortex was confirmed by immunoblot analysis.

## Induction of permanent focal cerebral ischemia
Permanent focal cerebral ischemia was induced by electrocoagulation of the middle cerebral artery (MCA). General anesthesia was induced and maintained by inhalation of 3% and 1.5% halothane (7025 Rodent Ventilator, Ugo Basile, Italy), respectively. Body temperature was maintained at 36.5–37 °C using a heat lamp and animal blanket controller (ATB-1100, Nipponkoden, Tokyo, Japan) throughout the surgical procedure and recovery periods. Mice were sacrificed at 24 h after MCAO with a lethal dose of isoflurane. Infarction of brain tissue was evaluated using 1% 2, 3, 5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC; Sigma-Aldrich) for 20 min at 37 °C. Images of sections were captured using a microscopic digital camera system (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and %infarct volume was quantified using an image analysis program (BZ-2: Keyence, Osaka, Japan), which compared data in the infarct-affected hemisphere to that in the contralateral hemisphere. All MCA surgeries and subsequent infarct analyses were performed by experimenters blinded to genotypes. Animal allocation to surgical groups was randomized.

## Application BAPTA-AM and 2-APB
BAPTA-AM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was dissolved in DMSO according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To inhibit intracellular calcium elevation, 10 µM of BAPTA-AM was added to HEK293 cells that were transfected with pCAGGS-WAVE1-V5, pCAGGS-PCT3-FLAG, or pCAGGS-PCT4-FLAG, and pCAGGS-Myc-Bcl-xL, pCAGGS-Myc-Bcl-xL-K233S, or pCAGGS-myc-ΔBH4-Bcl-xL. The same amount of DMSO was added as controls. Cells were stained, and images for cell counting were acquired as described below. In the calcium signal measurement experiment, 50 µM of 2-APB (Selleck, Houston, USA) was added to HEK293T cells at 3 h post-transfection when the DMEM medium was renewed. The same amount of DMSO was added as controls.

## HEK293 cell counting
HEK293 cells were cultured in 4-well chambered glass coverslips. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, cells were stained with Hoechst 33,342 (Nacalai Tesque) for 5 min to visualize the nucleus, and pretreated with 1×trypsin for 1 min, followed by application of PBS to detach from the 4-well chambered glass coverslips. To visualize living and dead transfected cells, 20 µl of evenly mixed cells for each transfected cell group were aspirated and mixed with 20 µl of Trypan blue following 5 min incubation in a 37 °C humidified incubator with a 5% CO2 air supply. Ten µl of Trypan blue-stained cells was aspirated into a cell-counting plate. Images of cells were acquired from bright field, blue, and red channels using an inverted microscope (Axio Observer A1, ZEISS) with a 10X objective lens. For each cell sample, images were acquired from the four corners of the cell-counting plate. Cell counting was performed manually using ImageJ. Cells fully observable in the acquired images, double-labeled with Trypan blue and tdTomato, were counted as dead cells, and double-labeled with Hoechst 33,342 and tdTomato were counted as living cells. Cell death ratio was calculated as a percentage that the number of dead cells divided by the total number of dead and living cells, and an average percentage was calculated of the images acquired from four areas of the cell-counting plate for each transfection group. We excluded the crowded cell mass that was not fully separated to get a specific number of cells.

## Live/dead cell viability assay
To validate the cell death results obtained from Trypan Blue staining, a fluorescence-based cell viability assay was performed using the Live or Dead™ Cell Viability Assay Kit Green/Red Dual Fluorescence (AAT Bioquest, Pleasanton, CA, USA). HEK293 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and transfected as described above. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, the culture medium was removed, and cells were gently washed with PBS. Cells were then incubated with the working solution containing CytoCalcein™ Green to label viable cells and Propidium Iodide to label dead cells for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The number of red fluorescent (dead) and green fluorescent (live) cells was counted from at least four random fields per well, and the cell death rate was calculated as the percentage of propidium iodide-positive cells out of the total cell number (green + red).

## Immunoprecipitation (IP) assay
At 48 h post-transfection, each cell sample in 10 cm plates was washed and collected as described above. For IP, 5 µl of Protein G Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was mixed with 1 µg of anti-FLAG antibody (Santa Cruz, TX, USA), anti-Myc antibody (Santa Cruz), or anti-V5 antibody (Invitrogen, MA, USA) in 1000 µl of the lysis buffer and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min with gentle rocking. The antibody-bound Dynabeads were rinsed three times in the lysis buffer and mixed with 400 µl of the lysate and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h with gentle rocking. The protein complex precipitated with the antibody-bounded Dynabeads was rinsed in the lysis buffer five times, resuspended in 10 µl of 1×sample buffer, and heated at 95 °C for 5 min to unbind the immunoprecipitated proteins.
For input groups, 1 µl of the cell lysate was mixed with 9 µl 1× sample buffer and heated at 95 °C for 5 min. Cell lysates or immunoprecipitated proteins were subjected to western blot as described above.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Cells were cultured in 4-well chambered glass coverslips, which were precoated with PLL. At 24 h post-transfection, MitoTracker (Thermo Fisher Scientific) or ERseeing (Funakoshi Co.,Ltd, Tokyo, Japan) was added to each well, and cells were fixed with 4% PFA according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Primary antibodies (see Supplementary Table) were diluted in serum blocking solution (1% normal goat serum and 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS) overnight at 4 °C to analyze the distribution of A2-PCTs and Bcl-xL species, respectively. After washing with PBS, the signals were visualized using secondary antibodies (see Supplementary Table) at room temperature for 1 h. Cells were washed in PBS three times prior to DAPI staining (1:1000, Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan), followed by washing in PBS three times. Cell samples were mounted with Fluoromount (Sigma-Aldrich).

## Colocalization evaluation
Images of HEK293 cells stained as above were acquired by a confocal microscope LSM880 with Airyscan (ZEISS) with a 63X objective lens, processed with Airyscan processing, and exported by ZEN (Version 3.10) in a JPEG format with 100% quality (no compression). Exported images were processed with ImageJ. ROIs were delineated based on dispersed independent cell shape edges, and Pearson’s correlation coefficient was calculated with the Colocalization Coloc2 plugin. The Student’s t-test and Tukey’s test were performed to compare colocalization of PCT3 and PCT4 with mitochondria, Bcl-xL with Bcl-xL-K233S, and Bcl-xL-K233S between groups using the results of Pearson’s correlation coefficient with GraphPad Prism 8.0.

## Immuno-electron microscopic studies
Preparation of monoclonal antibody targeting KFKQVEESHKQHLA of part B and the immuno-electron microscopic studies were described before .

## SPLICS colocalization analysis
MERCs were detected with SPLICS Mt-ER Long P2A (a gift from Marisa Brini & Tito Calì, Addgene plasmid # 164107) and SPLICS Mt-ER Short P2A (a gift from Marisa Brini & Tito Calì, Addgene plasmid # 164108) . HEK293T cells were cultured in 4-well chambered glass coverslips, which were precoated with PLL. Medium was removed at 24 h post-transfection, and cells were rinsed briefly in warm PBS, then fixed immediately in 4% PFA for 10 min and washed three times in PBS. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies (see Supplementary Table) in the blocking solution overnight at 4 °C, then were rinsed in PBS three times and incubated with secondary antibodies (see Supplementary Table) and DAPI (1:1000) in distilled PBS for 1 h at room temperature, followed by three times PBS rinse. Coverslips were mounted with Fluoromount. Image acquisition and processing were described as above.

## Calcium signal measurement
HEK293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates as mentioned above. In order to measure calcium signals from different protein expression, pAAV-CAG-GCaMP6s , pCMV-CEPIA3mt (a gift from Masamitsu Iino Addgene plasmid # 58219) and pCMV-G-CEPIA1er (a gift from Masamitsu Iino Addgene plasmid # 58215) transfection was carried out 24 h prior to transfecting (1) pCAGGS-5MCS , (2) pCAGGS-Myc-Bcl-xL and pCAGGS-WAVE1-V5, (3) pCAGGS-PCT3-FLAG, pCAGGS-Myc-Bcl-xL, and pCAGGS-WAVE1-V5, (4) pCAGGS-PCT4-FLAG, pCAGGS-Myc-Bcl-xL, and pCAGGS-WAVE1-V5, and (5) pCAGGS-PCT3-FLAG, pCAGGS-PCT4-FLAG, pCAGGS-Myc-Bcl-xL, and pCAGGS-WAVE1-V5. Images of cells were acquired every 3 h until 24 h post-transfection in the green channel with an inverted microscope (Axio Observer A1, ZEISS) with a 10X objective lens. For 2-APB experiments, 50 µM 2-APB was administered with the new medium 3 h post-transfection. Utilize ImageJ software to perform background subtraction and subsequently measure the integrated density values for each image.

## RT-PCR assay
Total RNA was extracted from Neuro2a cells using QIAGEN RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, Netherlands) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and quality were measured in a NanoDrop 1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).The cDNA was prepared using the SuperScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PCT1-4, Bcl-xL, and WAVE1 expressions were identified using the primers: PCT1: 5’-CGCTGCTGAAGATCGGGGTC-3’ and 5’-CAGGAATCAAAGGCCTAAGTTCATC-3’; PCT2: 5’-CGCTGCTGAAGATCGGGGTC-3’ and 5’-GGTTCAAACTATTCATGGGATGCAT-3’; PCT3: 5’-TGGCTGTGAGACATGCAACC-3’ and 5’- CAGGAATCAAAGGCCTAAGTTCATC-3’; PCT4: 5’-TGGCTGTGAGACATGCAACC-3’ and 5’-GGTTCAAACTATTCATGGGATGCAT-3’; Bcl-xL: 5’-CTTTGAGCAGGTAGTGAATGAACTC-3’ and 5’-CTCTAGGTGGTCATTCAGATAGGTG-3’; WAVE1: 5’-CTCTCAATATCCTCACCCCTTACAG-3’ and 5’-ACTTCAATATGCTTGTGTAGGAGGT-3’.

## OGD reperfusion (OGD/R) assay
For OGD/R experiment on Neuro2a cells, Neuro2a cells were transfected with 0.5 µg endotoxin-free pLKO.1-shPCTs #5, or 0.5 µg endotoxin-free pLKO.1-shScramble, and 0.5 µg endotoxin-free pAAV-CAG-GCaMP6s, pCMV-CEPIA3mt and pCMV-G-CEPIA1er following the same transfection protocol as described above. After about 6 h post-transfection, the culture medium was replaced with glucose-free DMEM as described above. The Neuro2a cells were then subjected to a multi-gas incubator for 1 h. Medium of Neuro2a cells was replaced with pre-warmed DMEM, and cells were placed back in a normal culture condition and calcium signal measurement was performed as above.

For western blot and IP, membranes were visualized and imaged using the LAS-3000 imaging system (FUJIFILM, Japan). ## Statistical statement
All statistical data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (CA, USA). The statistical test of experiments was performed on at least three independent experiments. Data are presented as the mean ± SEM. For comparisons between two groups, the unpaired t-test was used. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test was performed. Data were tested for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test) prior to ANOVA. The sample size was determined based on previous similar experiments, and no data points were excluded from the analysis. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. p < 0.05: indicated by * (one star), p < 0.01: indicated by ** (two stars), p < 0.001: indicated by *** (three stars). Randomization and blinding were used during experiments and outcome assessment.
