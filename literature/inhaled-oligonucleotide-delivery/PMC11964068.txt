## Abstract

## Background
The metabolic flexibility of endothelial cells is linked to their phenotypic plasticity. Frataxin is critical in determining the iron metabolism and fate of endothelial cells. This study aimed to investigate frataxin‐mediated metabolic remodeling during the endothelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition (EndoMT).

## Methods and Results
Endothelial cell‐specific frataxin knockout and frataxin mutation mice were subjected to angiotensin II to induce hypertension. EndoMT and cardiac fibrosis were assessed using histological and protein expression analyses. Fatty acid oxidation (FAO) in microvascular endothelial cells was measured using a Seahorse XF96 analyzer. We showed that inhibition of FAO accompanies angiotensin II‐induced EndoMT. Frataxin knockout mice promote EndoMT, associated with increased cardiac fibrosis following angiotensin II infusion. Angiotensin II reduces frataxin expression, which leads to mitochondrial iron overload and subsequent carbonylation of sirtuin 3. In turn, carbonylated sirtuin 3 contributes to the acetylated frataxin at lysine 189, making it more prone to degradation. The frataxin/sirtuin 3 feedback loop reduces hydroxyl‐CoA dehydrogenase α subunit–mediated FAO. Additionally, silymarin is a scavenger of free radicals, restoring angiotensin II–induced reduction of FAO activity and sirtuin 3 and frataxin expression, improving EndoMT both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, frataxin mutation mice showed suppressed EndoMT and improved cardiac fibrosis.

## Conclusions
The frataxin/sirtuin 3 feedback loop has the potential to attenuate angiotensin II–induced EndoMT by improving FAO.

## Research Perspective

## What Is New?

Lack of frataxin accelerates angiotensin II–induced endothelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition and decreases fatty acid oxidation levels.
The decrease in frataxin results in the carbonylation of sirtuin 3, leading to the hyperacetylation of frataxin, and the frataxin/sirtuin 3 feedback loop reduces hydroxyl‐CoA dehydrogenase α subunit–mediated fatty acid oxidation.
Silymarin, a scavenger of free radicals, restores angiotensin II–induced reduction of fatty acid oxidation activity and sirtuin 3 and frataxin expression and improves endothelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition both in vitro and in vivo.

## What Question Should Be Addressed Next?

The frataxin/sirtuin 3 feedback loop should be evaluated in patients with hypertensive cardiomyopathy. Pharmacological targeting of endothelial cell metabolism may prove to be an effective therapeutic approach for endothelial dysfunction.

The endothelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition (EndoMT) is a biological process in which endothelial cells (ECs) lose their specific markers, such as vascular endothelial cadherin and CD31, and gain mesenchymal cell markers, such as α‐smooth muscle actin (α‐SMA) and FSP‐1 (fibroblast‐specific protein 1). This results in cells acquiring myofibroblast‐like characteristics, including contractile function, enhanced migratory phenotypes, and increased extracellular matrix production. EndoMT is associated with various cardiovascular diseases, including cardiac fibrosis, valve calcification, atherosclerosis, and pulmonary arterial hypertension. Targeting this process therapeutically may provide a potential treatment for cardiovascular diseases.
Recent research has shed light on the importance of the relationship between the plasticity of the EC structure, function, and metabolic reprogramming. Silent vascular ECs rely on glucose glycolysis and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) to maintain redox homeostasis and their functions. , , Mitochondrial FAO is the primary pathway for fatty acid degradation, which is vital for energy homeostasis. However, during the EndoMT conversion process, ECs increase glycolysis while inhibiting FAO. The regulatory mechanism of mitochondrial FAO in hypertensive EndoMT progression remains unclear.
Frataxin is primarily located in the mitochondria. It is vital for creating iron–sulfur clusters, which are essential for many cellular processes, including the Krebs cycle, electron transport chain components of complexes I to III, and fatty acid β‐oxidation. , The absence of frataxin can cause oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and changes in lipid metabolism. , Previous studies have shown that frataxin is regulated by transcriptional and posttranslational modifications, including acetylation. , , When frataxin is highly acetylated, it is negatively associated with the expression levels of the deacetylase sirtuin 3. Frataxin deficiency has also been shown to cause sirtuin 3 carbonyl modification. However, whether the interaction between frataxin and sirtuin 3 would affect FAO involved in EndoMT is unclear.
Angiotensin II is the main effector peptide of the renin–angiotensin system and plays a critical role in initiating the progression of EndoMT. In this study, we propose that angiotensin II inhibits the expression of frataxin and promotes hypercarbonylation modification of mitochondrial proteins. Subsequently, sirtuin 3 carbonylation modification leads to hyperacetylation of mitochondrial proteins. Moreover, hyperacetylation of frataxin accelerates sirtuin 3 carbonylation modification, inhibiting FAO activity and promoting EndoMT.

## Methods
This study includes no data deposited in external repositories. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Detailed information on the sources of materials used in the experiment is provided in Table  .

## Animal Models
Frataxinfl/fl and Cdh5‐CreERT2 mice were purchased from Cyagen (Guangzhou, China). Frataxinfl/fl mice were crossed with Cdh5‐CreERT2 to generate endothelial cell–specific frataxin‐targeted knockout mice. Frataxinfl/fl Cdh5‐CreERT+ (knockout) mice and their littermate controls, frataxinfl/fl Cdh5‐CreERT− (wild type [WT]), were used in these studies. FrataxinK189R mice were constructed using the CRISPR/CAS9 technique (Cyagen). The mice were housed under a 12‐hour light/dark cycle and were given free access to water and a standard laboratory diet. All experiments were carried out in a blinded manner, with measurements remaining blinded regarding mouse information until the final analysis. All animal procedures were approved following the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals established by Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine and were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Institutes of Health Publication, Eighth Edition, 2011).
Eight‐week‐old mice were used in this experiment. All mice were anesthetized with a continuous flow of 1% to 2% isoflurane and subsequently administered either saline or 1000 ng/kg per minute angiotensin II for 14 days via subcutaneously implanted Alzet mini‐osmotic pumps. Frataxin knockout mice and their WT littermates received intraperitoneal injections of 40 mg/kg of tamoxifen for 5 consecutive days, followed by pump implantation. For silymarin intervention, C57BL/6J mice were pretreated with 30 mg/kg per day of silymarin for 7 days before receiving angiotensin II. Mice of both sexes were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a random number generator and allocation concealment. The systolic blood pressure was measured every 2 days by tail‐cuff plethysmography (BP‐2000, Visitech Systems, Apex, NC). After 2 weeks of treatment, animals were euthanized by an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg body weight). Heart tissues were obtained and stored at −80°C or fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde until analysis.

## Cardiac Microvascular Endothelial Cells Isolation
We generated a mouse model with reduced frataxin expression in ECs (knockout) by crossbreeding mice carrying frataxinfl/fl with Cdh5‐CreERT2 mice. Following tamoxifen injection, to confirm the specific frataxin knockout in endothelial cells, we isolated cardiac microvascular endothelial cells using the CD31 antibody attached to magnetic microbeads after enzymatic digestion of the hearts. The hearts were digested into single cell suspension by trypsin and collagenase. The cell suspension was then incubated with the magnetic beads according to the instructions for CD31 magnetic beads. CD31‐positive cells were isolated from the cell suspension using a Miltenyi Biotec sorting column. 

## Echocardiography
A high‐frequency ultrasound device (Vevo 2100, VisualSonics, Toronto, Ontario, Canada) was used for ultrasound imaging. The mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane in an induction chamber and maintained with continuous inhalation of a mixture of isoflurane (1.5%) and oxygen. The following parameters were measured in the parasternal short‐axis view by 2‐dimensional M‐mode imaging: left ventricular (LV) posterior wall at end‐diastole, LV posterior wall end‐systole, LV end‐diastolic volume, LV end‐systolic volume, and interventricular septum thickness during diastole and systole. Cardiac function was estimated by LV ejection fraction, which was derived using the following formula: Ejection fraction = (LV end‐diastolic volume − LV end‐systolic volume)/ LV end‐diastolic volume × 100%.

## En Face Experiment
The mouse thoracic aorta was cut along the long axis after gently removing the perivascular adipose tissue. The tissue was then fixed in precooled 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 to 30 minutes and washed 3 times with 1× PBS. Next, the vessel was transferred to a 12‐well plate containing 0.5 mL of permeabilizing solution (0.25% Triton X‐100 in PBS) per well and permeabilized for 10 minutes. After washing in PBS 3 times, the tissue was incubated in 5% BSA for 30 minutes to block nonspecific antibody binding sites. Subsequently, the primary antibody (CD31 1:200, α‐SMA 1:200) was added and incubated overnight at 4°C. After washing 3 times in PBS, the tissue was incubated with the secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. Finally, the nuclei were stained with 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole, and confocal microscopy was performed as soon as possible after sealing the coverslip. 

## Cell Culture and EndoMT Assay
Murine brain‐derived microvascular endothelial cells (MECs) were purchased from American Type Culture Collection and cultured in 1.5 g/L NaHCO3 DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin. Cardiac MECs were purchased from Chi Scientific (Jiangsu, China) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS, 100 μg/mL endothelial cell growth factor, 100 μg/mL heparin, and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin. Human umbilical vein ECs (HUVECs) and human embryonic kidney 293T cells were maintained in an incubator containing 95% O2 and 5% CO2 at 37°C. To induce EndoMT in vitro, we seeded the cells in 12‐well plates. Afterward, they were placed in serum‐free DMEM containing 1% BSA overnight, followed by stimulation with or without angiotensin II (1 μmol/L in 1% BSA DMEM) for 24 hours.

## Cell Wound Healing Assay
The cells were seeded onto 6‐well plates and allowed to reach 75% confluency. A sterilized pipette tip was used to create a straight scratch on the cell monolayer with consistent force. The cells were washed 3 times with PBS and treated with or without angiotensin II for 24 hours. Wound healing was monitored and photographed using a Zeiss AxioVert A1 microscope. The rate of closure was calculated using the following formula: (initial wound width − wound width after 24 hours)/(initial wound width) × 100%. 

## Plasmids and Lentiviral Vectors
Human embryonic kidney 293T cells were transfected with Flag–hydroxyl‐CoA dehydrogenase α (HADHA)WT, Flag‐HADHAK644R (the arginine mutant, mimicking lysine deacetylation), or Flag‐HADHAK644Q (the glutamine mutant, mimicking lysine acetylation). Plasmids were used to transfect 293T cells with Lipofectamine™ 3000 according to the manufacturer's instructions.
To silence the frataxin or sirtuin 3 expression, DNA oligonucleotides were used to generate short hairpin RNA against the open reading frame of frataxin or sirtuin 3. We inserted the sequence of frataxin short hairpin RNA (5′‐CCTCCAGATTCTGAA‐CATCAA‐3′) into the lentiviral short hairpin RNA GV298 vector (sequence of origin: U6‐MCS‐Ubiquitin‐Cherry‐IRES‐puromycin) or sirtuin 3 short hairpin RNA (5′‐GAGGATCCCCGGGTACCGGTCGCCACCATGGTGGGGG‐CCGGCATCAG‐3′) into the lentiviral GV115 vector (sequence of origin: pGCSIL‐004‐hU6‐CMV‐EGFP). To overexpress frataxin or sirtuin 3, we synthesized the subcloned full‐length cDNA for frataxin or sirtuin 3. We inserted them into the lentiviral CV224 vector (sequence of origin: CMV‐MCS‐3FLAG‐SV40‐Neomycin) and GV366 (sequence of origin: CMV‐MCS‐HA‐SV40‐Neomycin). Scramble sequences were used as negative controls. Cells were seeded into 24‐well plates. When the confluence of cells reached 20% to 30%, the cells were transduced with the lentiviruses at multiplicity of infection 30 for 48 hours. After 72 hours of culture, gene expression was examined using fluorescence microscopy or Western blotting.

## Immunostaining
Cells were fixed with an acetone and methanol (1:1) solution at −20°C for 10 minutes. After washing 3 times with PBS, the cells were incubated with a solution of 0.01% Triton X‐100 and 5% BSA for 30 minutes to permeabilize and block nonspecific binding sites. The cells were then washed briefly and incubated with primary antibodies, including CD31 (1:200), α‐SMA (1:200), vimentin (1:200), and FSP‐1 (1:200) at 4°C overnight. After washing 3 times with PBS, the cells were incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies of the corresponding species for 1 hour at room temperature. The cell nuclei were stained with 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (1:5000). Samples were examined using a Zeiss Axiovert A1 microscope, and images were analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Baltimore, MD).

## Immunoprecipitation and Coimmunoprecipitation
Human embryonic kidney 293T cells and HUVECs were transfected with Ha‐sirtuin 3, Flag‐HADHAWT, Flag‐HADHAK644Q, or Flag‐HADHAK644R plasmids. After cells were lysed with immunoprecipitation lysis, 500 μg of protein lysates was used for immunoprecipitation or coimmunoprecipitation. The Pierce coimmunoprecipitation kit (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, MD) was used to determine the interaction between sirtuin 3 and HADHA according to the manufacturer's instructions. Bound proteins were eluted with reducing SDS lysis buffer, and immunoblotting was performed.

## Carbonylated Protein Analysis
Protein carbonylation was detected using the OxyBlot Protein Oxidation Detection Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, protein carbonyls were labeled with 2,4‐dinitrophenylhydrazine and detected by Western blotting. 

## Western Blot Analysis
Equal amounts of protein were separated on SDS‐PAGE gels and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in Tris‐buffered saline with 0.1% Tween‐20 at room temperature for 1 hour. Primary antibodies against frataxin (1:2000), CD31 (1:1000), α‐SMA (1:1000), vimentin (1:1000), sirtuin 3 (1:1000), acetylated lysine (1:1000), Ace frataxin K189 (1:1000), HADHA (1:1000), and tubulin (1:6000) were used for immunoblotting analysis at 4°C overnight. Then, the membranes were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase conjugate for 1 hour at room temperature. Proteins were visualized using the enhanced chemiluminescence detection system and Amersham Biosciences 600 Imager (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL).

## Palmitate Oxidation Rate Assay
The Seahorse XF Palmitate Oxidation Stress Test Kit was used to assess FAO via the oxygen consumption rate of living cells (MECs and HUVECs) on an XF96e Extracellular Flux Analyzer. Briefly, cells were incubated overnight in a substrate‐limited medium (Seahorse XF DMEM supplemented with 0.5 mmol/L glucose, 1 mmol/L glutamine, 1% FBS, and 0.5 mmol/L carnitine). Before testing, the medium was replaced with FAO assay medium (Seahorse XF DMEM supplemented with 2 mmol/L glucose and 0.5 mmol/L carnitine). Cells were incubated in a CO2‐free incubator for 1 hour. Then, palmitate‐BSA was added to the 96‐well Seahorse assay plate. Etomoxir (4 μmol/L) was added to the negative control wells 10 minutes before the measurement. Oxygen consumption was measured at baseline and under different metabolic conditions following the addition of 1.5 μmol/L oligomycin, 2 μmol/L carbonyl cyanide‐4‐(trifluoromethoxy)phenylhydrazone, and 0.5 μmol/L antimycin/rotenone. 

## Liquid Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry–Based Proteomics
We used proteomics to identify differences in gene expression between cardiac MECs with angiotensin II treatment. The sample was first ground using liquid nitrogen, and then the cell powder was transferred to a 5‐mL centrifuge tube and sonicated 3 times on ice using a high‐intensity ultrasonic processor (Scientz) in lysis buffer to extract the protein. After the extraction, protein extraction was digested in trypsin and then labeled with a tandem mass tag kit. Then, the sample was fractionated by high pH reverse‐phase high‐performance liquid chromatography using a 300 Extend C18 column (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). To enrich Kac peptides, tryptic peptides dissolved in nuclear and cytoplasmic extraction buffer (100 mmol/L NaCl, 1 mmol/L EDTA, 50 mmol/L Tris–HCl, 0.5% NP‐40, and pH 8.0) were incubated with prewashed antibody beads (PTM Biolabs, Hangzhou, China) at 4°C overnight with gentle shaking. The beads were washed 4 times with nuclear and cytoplasmic extraction buffer and twice with ddH2O. The bound peptides were eluted from the beads using 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid. The eluted fractions were combined and vacuum dried. The resulting peptides were cleaned with C18 ZipTips (Millipore, Burlington, MA) according to the manufacturer's instructions, followed by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry analysis.

## Identification of HADHA Acetylation Site by Liquid Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry
The study used cardiac macrophages from WT and sirtuin 3 knockout mice to quantify lysine acetylation through proteomic analysis. This was achieved by performing a quantitative lysine acetylome analysis using tandem mass tag labeling and affinity enrichment, followed by high‐resolution liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry analysis. The modified HADHA sequence was also analyzed via mass spectrometry.

## Statistical Analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using Prism 8.0 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA). The data were presented as mean±SEM. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. In cases of comparisons involving >2 groups, the Brown–Forsythe test was first used to assess similar variances. Subsequently, 1‐way ANOVA (1 independent variable: treatment) and 2‐way ANOVA (2 independent variable: treatment and genotype) followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test were used. For the data that did not exhibit similar variances, parametric Welch ANOVA with Dunnett's T3 post hoc test was applied. Mann–Whitney U test for 2 groups or Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn's multiple comparison tests were used for nonnormally distributed variables. The detailed statistical analysis for each experiment is described in the respective figure legends. The biological replicates for each group are indicated in the figure legends, and representative images were selected to accurately depict the group mean/average on the basis of all available data. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## 
Frataxin Deficiency Accelerates Angiotensin II–Induced EndoMT

Frataxin is a mitochondrial protein and iron chaperone essential for iron–sulfur clusters that has been linked to maintaining endothelial homeostasis. To explore the mechanism of frataxin ablation in angiotensin II–induced EndoMT in vivo, we created a mouse model lacking frataxin expression in ECs (knockout) by breeding mice carrying frataxinfl/fl with Cdh5‐CreERT2 mice. Following enzyme digestion of the hearts, we isolated cardiac MECs using the CD31 antibody coupled with magnetic microbeads. The CD31+ cells showed a deficiency in frataxin protein expression in frataxin knockout mice, as determined by a Western blot assay (Figure  through ). The frataxinfl/fl‐CreERT2−/− mice (WT) and frataxin knockout mice were treated with either 1000 ng/kg per minute angiotensin II or saline for 14 days. The administration of angiotensin II led to a similar increase in systolic blood pressure levels in both WT and frataxin knockout mice (Figure  ). However, frataxin deficiency led to a decrease in compensatory effects caused by myocardial hypertrophy, including ejection fraction and interventricular septum thickness during systole and interventricular septum thickness during diastole (Figure  through ). Hematoxylin and eosin staining and wheat germ agglutinin staining showed that angiotensin II induced hypertrophy (Figure  through ). Frataxin deficiency also caused more severe myocardial fibrosis (Figure  and ).
The en face assay demonstrated that angiotensin II caused decreases in CD31 and frataxin expression levels in the endothelium of the aorta in WT mice. Furthermore, the decreased CD31 expression induced by angiotensin II was increased in frataxin knockout mice (Figure  and ). Additionally, angiotensin II increased α‐SMA expression in WT mice, which was further exacerbated in frataxin knockout mice (Figure  and ). In vitro, the gain or loss of frataxin function affected angiotensin II–induced EndoMT. MECs were infected with short hairpin RNA–frataxin to knock down frataxin expression and with a lentivirus‐mediated frataxin (LV‐FXN) to induce frataxin overexpression. Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses showed that the reduction of frataxin exacerbated the decrease in CD31 expression and the increase in vimentin, α‐SMA, and FSP‐1 expression levels caused by angiotensin II (Figure  through and through ), while frataxin overexpression reversed the angiotensin II–induced EndoMT (Figure through and through ). In addition, frataxin deletion enhanced cell migration ability, whereas frataxin overexpression weakened it (Figure   and ). These results suggest that frataxin deficiency accelerates angiotensin II–induced EndoMT.
 

## 
Frataxin Deficiency Promotes Metabolic Alteration During Angiotensin II–Induced EndoMT

To investigate the molecular events occurring during angiotensin II–induced EndoMT, we performed a quantitative proteomic analysis based on tandem mass tag labeling in an in vitro EndoMT model. The volcano plot and K‐means clustering heat maps in Figure  through illustrate the differentially expressed proteins induced by angiotensin II treatment. Compared with the control group, 120 proteins were upregulated, while 165 proteins were downregulated. Bioinformatics analyses revealed that most upregulated proteins were annotated as cytoskeleton and extracellular matrix, while most downregulated proteins were involved in fatty acid transport, metabolism, and energy production. These results suggest that EndoMT may involve not only changes in the cytoskeleton but also alterations in fatty acid and carbohydrate metabolism.
 Next, we examined the fatty acid metabolism requirements of MECs using the Seahorse XF FAO assay. In this assay, palmitate‐BSA was provided as a source of fatty acids, and the uptake of fatty acids was inhibited using etomoxir, an inhibitor of carnitine palmitoyl transferase I. The results showed that MECs displayed an elevated OCR profile in the presence of palmitate, indicating increased oxidative phosphorylation. However, this effect was suppressed when etomoxir was added, suggesting that MECs were taking up and oxidizing fatty acids. Moreover, a direct assessment of palmitate oxidation revealed a significant decline in endothelial FAO during angiotensin II–induced EndoMT. Knockdown of frataxin exacerbated the angiotensin II–inhibited endothelial FAO, whereas frataxin overexpression reversed the angiotensin II–inhibited endothelial FAO. These results confirm that a decrease in FAO accompanies angiotensin II–induced EndoMT, and they confirm that frataxin is inversely related to FAO (Figure  through ).

## 
Frataxin/Sirtuin 3 Feedback Loop
Frataxin is a mitochondrial protein crucial for maintaining iron homeostasis and oxidative stress. We investigated the correlation between oxidative stress and intracellular iron accumulation in MECs lacking frataxin. Based on the results shown in Figure  and , the level of mitochondrial Fe2+ induced by angiotensin II significantly increased in MECs compared with the control group. Knockdown of frataxin exacerbated the angiotensin II–induced increase in mitochondrial Fe2+ levels, while frataxin overexpression reversed the angiotensin II–induced increase in mitochondrial Fe2+ levels in MECs.
 Protein carbonylation is an irreversible protein modification promoted by reactive oxygen species. To assess protein carbonylation, the carbonyl groups in the protein side chains were derivatized with 2,4‐dinitrophenylhydrazine to form 2,4‐dinitrophenylhydrazone (DNP‐hydrazone). The level of 2,4‐dinitrophenol expression detected by Western blotting was then used as an indicator of protein carbonylation. Our results showed a pronounced increase in protein carbonylation, including the mitochondrial deacetylase sirtuin 3 in frataxin‐deficient MECs (Figure  through ). The carbonylation of sirtuin 3 reduced its activity, as demonstrated by the hyperacetylation of mitochondrial proteins (Figure  and ). In contrast, overexpression of frataxin reduced protein carbonylation and restored the function of sirtuin 3 (Figure  and ). This is in line with prior research indicating that the adduction of carbonyl groups may directly inhibit sirtuin 3 in the context of frataxin deficiency. Moreover, the hyperacetylation observed in frataxin deficiency may result from the impairment of sirtuin 3 (Figure  and ). In our previous study, we provided evidence that sirtuin 3 can deacetylate frataxin at lysine 189. We generated an antibody that specifically recognized acetylated frataxin K189 by immunizing rabbits with K189‐acetylated peptides. Western blot analysis revealed increased levels of acetylated frataxin at lysine 189 in frataxin‐deficient ECs (Figure  and ). Our study confirms that acetylation modification of frataxin is involved in its degradation. To investigate the effect of protein synthesis inhibition on the degradation of endogenous frataxin, we used cycloheximide to inhibit protein synthesis in ECs. As a result, the stability of frataxin decreased rapidly within 8 hours. Additionally, frataxin degraded more quickly in ECs with sirtuin 3 knockdown, while overexpression of sirtuin 3 had the opposite effect (Figure  and ).
In summary, angiotensin II reduced frataxin expression, which led to the deterioration of reactive oxygen species and subsequent sirtuin 3 dysfunction due to carbonylation. The dysfunction of sirtuin 3 contributed to the acetylation of frataxin at lysine 189, making it more prone to degrade. These changes resulted in the frataxin/sirtuin 3 feedback loop (Figure  ).

## Silymarin Improves Angiotensin II–Induced EndoMT Via the Frataxin/Sirtuin 3 Feedback Loop
Silymarin, a mixture of flavonolignans, is extracted from Silybum marianum seeds. In our study, MECs were pretreated with silymarin at different concentrations before angiotensin II stimulation. Our findings showed that increased silymarin concentration gradually slowed the decrease in sirtuin 3 and frataxin caused by angiotensin II (Figure  and ). Nevertheless, in cells transfected with short hairpin frataxin, silymarin was ineffective in reversing the angiotensin II–triggered decrease in sirtuin 3 (Figure  and ). Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that silymarin reversed angiotensin II–induced EndoMT by increasing CD31 expression and decreasing α‐SMA, vimentin, and FSP‐1 expression (Figure  through ). Additionally, silymarin inhibited angiotensin II–induced lipid peroxide production as detected by Liperfluo, a fluorescent probe that detects intracellular lipid peroxide directly (Figure  and ). Furthermore, silymarin restored FAO by inhibiting angiotensin II (Figure  and ). To investigate the effect of silymarin on EndoMT in vivo, we administered mice intragastric saline or silymarin (30 mg/kg per day) 7 days before 14 days of angiotensin II treatment. We performed blood pressure and echocardiography in mice, and we found that silymarin did not alter the angiotensin II–induced increase in blood pressure, LV posterior wall end‐systole, and LV posterior wall at end‐diastole (Figure  through ). The en face assay showed that silymarin restored the angiotensin II–induced decrease in sirtuin 3 expression (Figure  and ) and increase α‐SMA expression (Figure  and ). Moreover, hematoxylin and eosin, wheat germ agglutinin staining, and Masson staining showed that angiotensin II–induced hypertrophy and myocardial fibrosis. Silymarin restored angiotensin II–induced myocardial hypertrophy and fibrosis (Figure  through ). These results suggest that silymarin improved angiotensin II–induced EndoMT via the frataxin/sirtuin 3 feedback loop.
 

## 
Sirtuin 3 Interacts With HADHA

We investigated the relationship between the frataxin/sirtuin 3 feedback loop and FAO during EndoMT. Using quantitative acetylation proteomics analysis, we compared the FAO‐associated acetylation levels between WT and sirtuin 3 knockout (sirtuin 3−/−) mice. Our findings revealed that 13 FAO‐associated proteins were upregulated in the sirtuin 3−/− mice, and HADHA had the most acetylation sites regulated by sirtuin 3 of the proteins (Figure  and ). Notably, the lysine acetylation modification at the K644 site was considerably altered (Figure  ). Furthermore, this site is highly conserved in various species, including Drosophila hydei, Equus asinus, Canis lupus familiaris, Mus musculus, and Homo sapiens (Figure  ). HADHA is a subunit of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein, which catalyzes the last 3 steps of mitochondrial β‐oxidation of long‐chain fatty acids. To investigate the interaction between sirtuin 3 and HADHA, we used 293T cells and performed coimmunoprecipitation experiments. Proteins were coimmunoprecipitated using either anti‐sirtuin 3 (Figure  ) or anti‐HADHA (Figure  ) antibodies, and the pull‐down samples were immunoblotted with HADHA and sirtuin 3 antibodies. Our results indicated an endogenous interaction between HADHA and sirtuin 3. Furthermore, we transiently transfected Ha‐sirtuin 3 (Figure  ) or Flag‐HADHA (Figure  ) into 293T cells, and the expression levels of HADHA and sirtuin 3 were detected after immunoprecipitation with either Ha‐conjugated beads or Flag beads. Additionally, cells treated with short hairpin sirtuin 3 showed increased levels of HADHA acetylation, whereas overexpressing sirtuin 3 significantly decreased HADHA acetylation levels (Figure  through ).
 To investigate whether acetylation of HADHA K644 mediated HADHA activity, we transfected HUVECs with Flag‐HADHAWT, Flag‐HADHAK644R (the arginine mutant, mimicking lysine deacetylation), or Flag‐HADHAK644Q (the glutamine mutant, mimicking lysine acetylation). The level of acetylated HADHA was verified by immunoprecipitation assay and Western blotting. Cells transfected with Flag‐HADHAK644Q showed higher levels of acetylated HADHA (Figure  and ). Moreover, Flag‐HADHAK644Q reduced CD31 expression and increased α‐SMA levels, promoting the cells towards EndoMT (Figure  and ). Flag‐HADHAK644Q also caused impairment of fatty acid metabolism more than Flag‐HADHAWT (Figure  and ). Conversely, Flag‐HADHAK644R enhanced FAO and preserved the endothelial cell phenotype (Figure  through ).

## Deacetylated Frataxin Attenuates Angiotensin II–Induced EndoMT

We investigated the function of deacetylated frataxin in vivo by generating frataxin mutant mice (frataxin K189R) with an arginine mutation at the K189 site to mimic lysine deacetylation (Figure  ). After infusing angiotensin II for 14 days into the WT and frataxin K189R mice, we observed that angiotensin II elevated systolic blood pressure in both groups (Figure  ). However, en face staining revealed that angiotensin II increased acetylated frataxin K189. Acetylated frataxin K189 was not upregulated in frataxin K189R mice (Figure  and ). Additionally, angiotensin II increased α‐SMA expression in WT mice but not in frataxin K189R mice (Figure  and ). We used CD31 to identify the endothelial region before administering the immunoglobulin G antibody (Figure  ). A serial echocardiographic analysis showed that angiotensin II increased ejection fraction, LV posterior wall at end‐diastole, and LV posterior wall end‐systole in WT mice; however, LV posterior wall at end‐diastole and LV posterior wall end‐systole were significantly reduced in frataxin mutant mice (Figure  through ). Hematoxylin and eosin and wheat germ agglutinin staining revealed that angiotensin II induced hypertrophy, with frataxin mutant mice showing reduced cross‐sectional area (Figure  through ). These results indicate that deacetylated frataxin might mitigate angiotensin II–induced EndoMT.
 

## Discussion
The phenotypic plasticity of ECs is linked to metabolic pathways. However, the mechanisms by which ECs adjust their cellular metabolism to drive EndoMT are not yet fully understood. This study provides evidence for the following: (1) Lack of frataxin accelerates angiotensin II–induced EndoMT and reduces FAO; (2) frataxin deficiency induces carbonylation of sirtuin 3, resulting in hyperacetylation of frataxin; (3) the frataxin/sirtuin 3 feedback loop attenuates HADHA‐mediated FAO; and (4) silymarin improves EndoMT via the frataxin/sirtuin 3 feedback loop. Based on these findings, we suggest intervening in dysregulated metabolism could delay EndoMT progression.
Although ECs rely on a balanced glycolytic/oxidative metabolism for their phenotypic and functional homeostasis, most studies have primarily focused on glycolysis in ECs under physiological and pathological conditions. , Our study found that ECs rely on FAO as a significant energy source, as they can take up and oxidize fatty acids. Using bioinformatics analyses based on proteomics data, we found that angiotensin II–induced EndoMT might involve changes in the cytoskeleton and in fatty acid and carbohydrate metabolism. Especially during angiotensin II–induced EndoMT, we observed a decrease in palmitate oxidation, consistent with the findings of Xiong et al, that endothelial FAO restrains EndoMT. These results confirmed that FAO can support de novo deoxynucleotide triphosphate synthesis and maintain the tricarboxylic acid cycle for redox homeostasis in ECs. 

An increasing body of research indicates that EndoMT is a characteristic of aging ECs and can significantly impact cardiovascular disease, as well as other conditions marked by fibrosis and microvasculature loss. Fleenor et al found that EndoMT is triggered in human aortic endothelial cells due to replicative aging. Ramadhiani et al indicated that senescent ECs worsen pulmonary fibrosis possibly by accelerating EndoMT. We suggested EndoMT induced by angiotensin II is a feature of senescent ECs that contributes to cardiac fibrosis.
Frataxin is a highly conserved protein in the mitochondrial matrix that is critical for assembling iron–sulfur clusters. These clusters are necessary for enzymes involved in the Krebs cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, and fatty acid β‐oxidation. Reduced frataxin expression leads to EC senescence, less polymerized actin, and paracellular permeability. Our study demonstrated that frataxin has a positive effect on FAO during angiotensin II–induced EndoMT, as evidenced by gain‐ and loss‐of‐function experiments. This is consistent with previous studies that frataxin loss impaired oxidative metabolism within the mitochondria. , Based on these results, we concluded that lack of frataxin accelerates angiotensin II–induced EndoMT by reducing FAO.
The absence of frataxin disrupts mitochondrial iron balance, which leads to respiratory chain enzyme dysfunction and an overproduction of free radicals. Similarly, we found that knockdown of frataxin exacerbates the angiotensin II–induced elevation in mitochondrial Fe2+ levels. This transient metal reacts with H2O2 to generate the highly reactive hydroxyl radical through the Fenton/Haber–Weiss reaction. Therefore, mitochondrial iron overload leads to free radical generation, which can alter the mitochondrial genome and proteome through oxidative stress. This study revealed that a deficiency of frataxin affects the redox state of ECs, increasing protein carbonylation levels. Specifically, mitochondrial sirtuin 3 is affected by this carbonylation process. Carbonylation of sirtuin 3 by 4‐hydroxynonenal has been reported in liver mitochondrial extracts of ethanol‐consuming mice. The carbonylation of sirtuin 3 impedes its allosteric activity, resulting in a surge of protein hyperacetylation. Cycloheximide is a eukaryote protein synthesis inhibitor that we used to inhibit protein synthesis in ECs. Moreover, our research showed that acetylation of frataxin during the hyperacetylation process accelerates the cycloheximide‐mediated degradation of frataxin. The stability of acetylated protein is a complex process. In some cases, protein acetylation could accelerate protein degradation. A prior study demonstrated that acetylation of autophagy protein 5 regulates the autophagic degradation of pyruvate kinase isozyme M2. Tomiyama et al reported that hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1α enhances its interaction with the E3 ubiquitin ligase von Hippel–Lindau, leading to protein ubiquitin degradation. Therefore, carbonylated sirtuin 3 and acetylated frataxin were found to interact with each other, leading to a feedback loop between the 2 proteins. It is believed that increased sensitivity to oxidative stress contributes to frataxin deficiency.
Silymarin is an extract from the milk thistle plant (S marianum) containing various flavonolignans. One possible antioxidant mechanism of silymarin is scavenging free radicals and chelating free iron and copper directly. Interestingly, silymarin was found to inhibit angiotensin II–induced lipid peroxide production and restore the reduced FAO capacity and sirtuin 3 and frataxin expression levels in MECs. These results suggest that silymarin may improve angiotensin II–induced EndoMT via the frataxin/sirtuin 3 feedback loop. Therefore, we suggest that excessive mitochondrial oxidative stress may be the main cause of the frataxin/sirtuin 3 feedback loop.
Endothelial frataxin deficiency causes an increase in hyperacetylation of mitochondrial proteins, which is linked to mitochondrial oxidative stress. Our findings show that this hyperacetylation can be reversed by sirtuin 3, a deacetylase enzyme localized in the mitochondria. Sirtuin 3 is known to activate many mitochondrial enzymes involved in fatty acid β‐oxidation, amino acid metabolism, and the electron transport chain by deacetylating enzymes. Specifically, we found that sirtuin 3 directly deacetylates the HADHA 644‐lysine residue in the mitochondria. HADHA is one of the subunits of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein for FAO. This is consistent with previous reports that have shown hyperacetylation of HADHA to be associated with decreased enzyme activity. , Deng et al identified acetylation of K255 on HADHA, and blockade of HADHA Lys‐255 acetylation attenuated myocardial fibrosis in obese mouse hearts. Thapa et al reported that hyperacetylation of HADHA Lys‐350/Lys‐383/Lys‐406 significantly decreased HADHA activity in response to an acute high‐fat diet. Our results suggest that HADHA lysine 644 determines HADHA activity and has a fine‐tuning role in FAO activity while decreasing angiotensin II–induced EndoMT. These results indicate that HADHA activity is regulated by multiple reversible acetyl‐lysines that respond to different environmental stressors. Our previous study demonstrated that mice lacking sirtuin 3 have hyperacetylated frataxin at lysine 189, which has a fine‐tuning effect on frataxin activity. Deacetylation of K189 reduces mitochondrial iron overload and lipid peroxidation. Wagner et al reported that highly acetylated frataxin negatively correlates with sirtuin 3 expression levels. Additionally, deacetylated frataxin shows resistance to cycloheximide‐mediated degradation. Frataxin mutant mice (frataxin K189R) with an arginine mutation at the K189 site, which mimics lysine deacetylation, can attenuate angiotensin II–induced EndoMT.
One limitation of the current study is that frataxin K189R mouse with a global mutation, and the impact of deacetylating frataxin on mitochondrial function may affect myofibroblasts. It would be beneficial to use mice with mutations specifically targeting endothelial cells. Furthermore, incorporating human tissue into the research would amplify its translational importance.

## Conclusions
This study found that endothelial FAO plays a critical role in regulating EndoMT. Angiotensin II reduces frataxin expression, leading to reactive oxygen species deterioration and sirtuin 3 dysfunction due to carbonylation. In turn, carbonylated sirtuin 3 contributes to frataxin acetylation, promoting its degradation. This creates the frataxin/sirtuin 3 feedback loop, which coordinately suppresses HADHA‐mediated FAO. Thus, pharmacological targeting of endothelial cell metabolism may prove to be an effective therapeutic approach for endothelial dysfunction.
