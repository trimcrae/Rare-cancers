This study reports the identification and genomic characterization of Ulleung virus (ULLEV), a newly discovered genetic lineage of Orthohantavirus jejuense harbored by Crocidura utsuryoensis, a shrew species endemic to Ulleung Island, Republic of Korea. In 2009, a surveillance effort involving 62 shrews revealed a molecular prevalence of 64.5%. Metagenomic next-generation sequencing identified four viral variants, with ULLEV Cu09-33 fully characterized. Comparative genomic and phylogenetic analyses confirmed ULLEV as a distinct subtype within O. jejuense and suggested that ULLEV and Jeju virus (JJUV) share a common ancestor, with divergence shaped by long-term co-evolution with their hosts and geographic isolation. Haplotype network analysis of mitochondrial DNA detected eight unique haplotypes in C. utsuryoensis, distinct from C. shantungensis populations, indicating genetic isolation. While incongruences among the tripartite genomes of ULLEV and JJUV suggested independent evolutionary trajectories, evidence for reassortment remains insufficient. These findings highlight the ecological significance of geographically isolated environments as reservoirs for unique hantavirus lineages and underscore the need for expanded genomic surveillance and phylogenetic studies to assess the zoonotic potential and public health implications of ULLEV.

## Introduction
 Hantaviruses (order Bunyavirales, family Hantaviridae, genus Orthohantavirus) are enveloped, single-stranded, and negative-sense RNA viruses with a segmented genome comprising large (L), medium (M), and small (S) RNA segments . These segments encode three distinct functional proteins: the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), glycoproteins (Gn and Gc), and nucleocapsid (N) proteins. In humans, orthohantaviruses are zoonotic pathogens responsible for hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome (HFRS) in Eurasia and hantavirus cardiopulmonary syndrome (HCPS) in the Americas . The Old World hantavirus group, which includes Orthohantavirus hantanense (HTNV), O. seoulense (SEOV), O. dobravaense (DOBV), and O. puumalaense (PUUV), accounts for over 100,000 annual cases of HFRS, with case fatality rates (CFRs) ranging from 1 to 12% . In contrast, New World hantaviruses such as O. sinnombreense (SNV) and O. andesense are significantly more lethal, accounting for approximately 200 annual cases of HCPS, with a CFR of nearly 40% . These viruses cause chronic asymptomatic infections in reservoir rodents, with human infections primarily resulting from the inhalation of aerosolized particles or contact with fomites contaminated by rodent excreta, including urine, feces, and saliva .
Hantaviruses are maintained in nature by persistent infections in small mammalian hosts belonging to the orders Rodentia, Eulipotyphla, and Chiroptera, with evidence of long-term co-divergence between viruses and their hosts , . Based on phylogenetic relationships and host specificity, hantaviruses have recently been reclassified into four genera: Orthohantavirus, which includes rodent- and some shrew-borne viruses; Thottimvirus, comprising shrew-associated lineages; Mobatvirus, associated with moles and bats; and Loanvirus, which encompasses bat-borne strains . The first shrew-borne hantavirus, Thottimvirus thottapalayamense, was identified in the Asian house shrew (Suncus murinus) in India , . Since then, genetically distinct hantaviruses have been detected in various shrew species, including Artybash virus in Sorex caecutiens, Boginia virus in Neomys fodiens, Bowé virus in Crocidura douceti, Cao Bằng virus in Anourosorex squamipes, and Seewis virus in S. araneus, highlighting the extensive phylogenetic and geographic diversity of shrew-borne hantaviruses worldwide – . In the Republic of Korea (ROK), C. lasiura is recognized as the primary reservoir host for T. imjinense (MJNV), which has also been reported in China – . In addition, C. shantungensis, the second-most abundant shrew species in the ROK, which is widely distributed across both the Korean Peninsula and Jeju Island, serves as the natural reservoir of Jeju virus (JJUV), classified within the species Orthohantavirus jejuense , . C. utsuryoensis, which is morphologically distinct from C. shantungensis, is an insular shrew taxon endemic to Ulleung Island, located approximately 120 km east of the Korean Peninsula .
Next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies have significantly advanced virology by enabling whole-genome sequencing, metagenomic analyses, and studies on transmission dynamics, pathogen genomic epidemiology, and vaccine development – . The ability to sequence complete viral genomes is essential for improving diagnostic accuracy, identifying genetic markers associated with virulence, and tracing the origin and spread of infectious disease outbreaks – . In hantavirus research, various NGS-based approaches, including sequence-independent single-primer amplification, target enrichment using virus-specific oligonucleotide probes, and amplicon sequencing, advanced our understanding of viral evolution and epidemiology – . For example, deep sequencing with multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) has revealed the global phylogeographic distribution of SEOV in clinical and reservoir host samples, highlighting distinct evolutionary divergences among lineages . Similarly, genome-wide studies of HTNV using amplicon-based NGS have identified important phylogenetic and epidemiological links between patients with HFRS and their rodent hosts, offering insights into potential sites of viral exposure in the ROK , .
In this work, we identified and characterized a distinct genetic lineage of O. jejuense associated with C. utsuryoensis, a shrew species endemic to Ulleung Island in the ROK. Building on the recognized diversity of shrew-borne hantaviruses, this research aimed to advance our understanding of their genomic and evolutionary dynamics. This report provides important insights regarding the role of island ecosystems as reservoirs of emerging viral lineages and underscores the need to evaluate their potential public health implications.

## Results

## Epidemiological survey for ulleung virus (ULLEV)
In 2009, 62 C. utsuryoensis shrews were captured from two sites on Ulleung Island, Na-ri and Sadong-ri (Fig.  ). Molecular analyses of ULLEV detection indicated an overall prevalence of 64.5% (40/62) (Table  ). The infection rate was higher in female shrews (68.6%, 24/35) than in male shrews (59.3%, 16/27). Stratified by body weight, the prevalence was 61.3% (19/31) in shrews weighing < 5 g, 70.4% (19/27) in those weighing between 5.1 and 7.5 g, and 50% (2/4) in individuals weighing 7.6–10 g.

 

 

## Genomic sequencing and characterization of ULLEV
Using metagenomic NGS (mNGS), the genomic sequences of the four variants, including ULLEV Cu09-32, ULLEV Cu09-33, ULLEV Cu09-39, and ULLEV Cu09-56, were obtained from lung tissues of shrews (Supplementary Table 1). Detailed information on the number of viral reads and sequencing depth is presented in Supplementary Table 2. The genome of ULLEV Cu09-33 was completely characterized in this study. The uncovered regions within the tripartite RNA genome of ULLEV Cu09-33 were resolved using conventional reverse-transcription PCR (RT-PCR) and rapid amplification of cDNA ends PCR (RACE PCR). The 3′ and 5′ termini sequences of the ULLEV Cu09-33 genome were successfully recovered for all three genomic segments. The 3′ terminal sequences, conserved across the tripartite genome, were identified as 5′-GGA GUC UAC UAC UA-3′. In contrast, the 5′ terminal sequence was determined to be 5′-UAG UAG UAU GCU CC-3′, displaying partial complementarity due to a mismatch at nucleotide position 9 and a noncanonical U–G base pairing at position 10.
The full-length L segment of ULLEV Cu09-33 encompasses 6,589 nucleotides and encodes an RdRp comprising 2,157 amino acids. This segment features a 3′ untranslated region (UTR) comprising 80 nucleotides (positions 1–80) and a 5′ UTR consisting of 35 nucleotides (positions 6,555–6,589). Similarly, the complete M segment, spanning 3,575 nucleotides, encodes a glycoprotein precursor of 1,143 amino acids. Its 3′ UTR extends across 100 nucleotides (positions 1–100), whereas the 5′ UTR is 43 nucleotides in length (positions 3,533–3,575). The S segment of 1,725 nucleotides encodes an N protein comprising 428 amino acids. The 3′ UTR encompasses 402 nucleotides (positions 1–402), while the 5′ UTR spans 36 nucleotides (positions 1,690–1,725).

## Genomic characterization and comparative analyses of ULLEV
The nucleotide sequences of the L, M, and S segments of ULLEV Cu09-33 were 84.1–84.3%, 84.8–85.5%, and 86.2% identical, respectively, to those of JJUVs (Table  ). Amino acid sequence analysis revealed divergences ranging from 2.4 to 2.5% for the L segment, 2.9–3.1% for the M segment, and 1.9–2.6% for the S segment. N-linked glycosylation sites were predicted in the Gn and Gc regions at positions 142, 355, 407, 936, and 1058 (Supplementary Fig. ). The WAASA motif, which is a highly conserved amino acid sequence characteristic of hantavirus glycoproteins, was observed at positions 652–656.

 

## Phylogenetic and co-evolutionary analyses of ULLEV
In this study, phylogenetic analyses were performed using nucleotide sequences of the L, M, and S genomic segments of ULLEV and representative JJUV variants. The resulting trees revealed well-supported geographic clustering within O. jejuense (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig. ). The L segment of ULLEV demonstrated a genetic relationship with JJUV strains from Jeju Island, ROK, while the M and S segments clustered with JJUV strains originating from mainland of ROK. The phylogenetic relationship between ULLEV (associated with C. utsuryoensis) and JJUV (associated with C. shantungensis) reflects the host-virus co-divergence dynamics associated with their respective Crocidurian shrew species (Fig.  ).

 

 

## Haplotype network analysis of C. utsuryoensis collected from ulleung island
Haplotype network analysis identified 43 haplotypes derived from the mitochondrial Cytochrome b (CYTB) sequences of the 109 Crocidurian shrews (Fig.  ). Within the ROK, seven haplotypes were observed on the mainland, 11 on Jeju Island, and eight on Ulleung Island. Populations of C. shantungensis from mainland ROK appear to have evolved from the ancestral populations in China, forming two distinct haplogroups. In contrast, the group comprising C. utsuryoensis from Ulleung Island, which included eight haplotypes shared by 61 individuals, was genetically isolated from Chinese populations and represented a unique and distinct haplogroup.

 

## Discussion
Here, we report for the first time, the identification and characterization of ULLEV, a newly identified genetic lineage of O. jejuense harbored by C. utsuryoensis on Ulleung Island, ROK. ULLEV Cu09-33 was completely characterized using mNGS and RACE PCR. The tripartite genome of ULLEV comprised 6,589 nucleotides in the L segment, 3,575 nucleotides in the M segment, and 1,725 nucleotides in the S segment, with conserved 3′ and 5′ terminal sequences, which is consistent with that observed in other members of the family Hantaviridae . Comparative genomic analyses revealed nucleotide sequence similarities of 84.1–84.3%, 84.8–85.5%, and 86.2% for the L, M, and S segments, respectively, between ULLEV Cu09-33 and JJUV. N-glycosylation profiling indicated that the ULLEV glycoprotein exhibited a pattern identical to that of JJUV. Despite this similarity, the phylogenetic analysis demonstrated a clear evolutionary divergence between ULLEV and JJUV. Hantavirus species classification is traditionally based on the ecological niches associated with specific reservoir hosts and genetic diversity. The International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses currently defines a species demarcation threshold of at least 7% amino acid sequence divergence in the glycoprotein precursor (GPC) and N protein. However, Maes et al. proposed increasing these thresholds to > 12% for the M segment and > 10% for the S segment, based on phylogenetic analyses of complete genome sequences . In the present study, the differences in the amino acid sequences of ULLEV and JJUV in the GPC and N proteins were < 4%, supporting the classification of ULLEV as a distinct subtype within O. jejuense.
Ulleung Island, a volcanic landmass approximately 120 km east of the Korean Peninsula in the East Sea, was formed during the Cenozoic era, with major eruptions occurring 5 − 2.5 million years ago, as determined by seismic reflection studies . Since its formation, the island has remained isolated even during periods of lower sea levels associated with glacial maxima , . This prolonged isolation has driven significant genetic divergence and evolution of endemic species . In the present study, haplotype network analysis of mitochondrial CYTB revealed that C. utsuryoensis, which harbors ULLEV, shares a genetic relationship with C. shantungensis populations; however, no evidence of gene flow was detected. These findings suggest that C. utsuryoensis may have been infected by a common ancestor of ULLEV and JJUV before geographical isolation. Over time, C. utsuryoensis harboring ULLEV possibly underwent genetic differentiation influenced by ecological specialization or niche expansion under the island’s unique pressures, including resource limitation and the absence of predators.
Orthohantaviruses pose a significant and ongoing threat to public health worldwide, with HFRS being particularly prevalent in the ROK – . In the ROK, approximately 400 cases of HFRS are reported annually, with a mean fatality rate of 1–4% (accessed Feb. 10, 2025, https://dportal.kdca.go.kr/pot/is/summaryEDW.do). Approximately 70% of these cases are associated with HTNV carried by Apodemus agrarius, while the remaining cases are attributed to SEOV hosted by Rattus norvegicus and R. rattus or other unidentified pathogens . The present study reports the first identification of an orthohantavirus, designated ULLEV, circulating in C. utsuryoensis, a shrew species endemic to Ulleung Island, a geographically isolated region of the Korean Peninsula. Although ULLEV is genetically closely related to JJUV, the zoonotic potential and pathogenicity of both viruses remain unclear. Since the implementation of nationwide disease surveillance by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency in 2000, no cases of HFRS have been documented on Ulleung Island (accessed Feb. 10, 2025, https://dportal.kdca.go.kr/pot/is/summaryEDW.do). However, the absence of reported cases does not necessarily indicate the absence of human infection. Given the limited epidemiological data, it is not possible to determine whether ULLEV poses a risk to human health. The lack of confirmed cases may be attributed to underdiagnosis, low-level virus circulation, limited serological surveillance, or asymptomatic infections. Furthermore, the pathogenicity of orthohantaviruses can vary substantially depending on the genotype. For instance, DOBV is known to cause a broad range of clinical outcomes, ranging from severe disease to asymptomatic infection, despite high genetic similarity among its genotypes , , . These observations underscore the need for future ecological and seroepidemiological studies to assess the potential public health risk associated with ULLEV and related orthohantaviruses.
Segmented RNA viruses have a remarkable capacity to influence molecular evolution through genome reassortment, fostering genetic diversity that results in distinct phylogenetic patterns across individual genome segments . Genetic exchanges among hantaviruses primarily occur between variants that are either closely related within the same lineage (intra-lineage) or across different but genetically similar lineages (inter-lineage) , . Notably, reassortment events often involve the M segment, which may be more adaptable to exchange or may provide a selective advantage when reassorted, whereas the L and S segments typically require greater genetic compatibility . For example, SNV frequently undergo reassortment in natural environments and laboratory settings, particularly affecting the M segment . Similarly, the reassortment of the M segment has been observed in DOBV during in vitro experiments, including between genotypes carried by different reservoir hosts, indicating the potential for cross-host reassortment . However, segment exchanges involving the L or S segments have also been documented in HTNV, SEOV, and PUUV, suggesting that the compatibility requirements for reassortment can vary across hantavirus species , . Phylogenetic analysis revealed incongruences between the tripartite genomes of ULLEV and JJUV, suggesting that each segment evolved independently. In particular, the L segment of ULLEV sampled on Ulleung Island clustered with JJUV from Jeju Island, whereas the M and S segments grouped with strains from mainland of ROK. However, our analysis was constrained by the use of partial genome sequences from mainland JJUV, which were relatively short and did not completely capture the diversity of potential reassortment events. This limitation underscores the need for further research, including the generation of complete genome sequences and expanded geographic sampling to better understand reassortment dynamics.
This study had several limitations that warrant further investigation. (1) Phylogenetic analysis was limited to partial genome sequences, as few full-length JJUV sequences—particularly those originating from the Korean Peninsula—are available in public databases. (2) Due to limited temporal sampling and the small number of available ULLEV sequences (collected only in 2009), precise molecular dating and estimation of viral introduction or divergence times were not feasible. (3) Attempts to isolate the virus for detailed characterization were unsuccessful under the experimental conditions used. (4) The taxonomic status of C. utsuryoensis remains uncertain due to the lack of nuclear genomic data and interbreeding studies. Although we followed existing Korean literature, additional evidence is needed to resolve its classification. Future studies should aim to generate complete genome sequences, extend spatiotemporal sampling of hosts and viruses, apply molecular clock models, and improve isolation techniques. These efforts will enhance our understanding of the biology and evolutionary history of ULLEV and related orthohantaviruses.
In conclusion, to our knowledge, we report for the first time the identification and characterization of ULLEV hosted by C. utsuryoensis on Ulleung Island, ROK. Phylogenetic analyses supported this classification as a distinct subtype within the O. jejuense. Our findings suggest that C. utsuryoensis acquired ancestral strains of ULLEV and JJUV before geographical isolation, with subsequent genetic differentiation driven by the island’s unique ecological pressures. Incongruences between the tripartite genomes of ULLEV and JJUV indicate the independent evolution of each segment. These results highlight the ecological importance of Ulleung Island as a reservoir for unique hantavirus lineages and underscore the need for ongoing surveillance and molecular studies to assess the potential public health risks of ULLEV and related orthohantaviruses.

## Methods

## Ethics and sample collection
The 62 shrew specimens used in this study were acquired from a previous hantavirus surveillance program . All trapping activities and experimental procedures involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Korea University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (KU-IACUC) and the Korean Ministry of Environment, following the KU-IACUC-approved protocol (#2010 − 212). All methods are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org) and were carried out in accordance with institutional, national, and international regulations for animal research.
The animals were captured in 2009 at two distinct locations on Ulleung Island, ROK, using Sherman live traps (8 × 9 × 23 cm; Sherman, Tallahassee, FL, USA). Following capture, the shrews were anesthetized with isoflurane and humanely euthanized via cervical dislocation by trained personnel. Lung and liver tissues were then excised and stored at − 80 °C until use.

## Mitochondrial DNA analysis
Liver tissues were processed for total DNA extraction using the TRI reagent (Ambion, Austin, Texas, USA). The CYTB gene of the mitochondrial DNA was amplified using PCR using previously described universal primers . Each 25 µL reaction mixture contained 2.5 mM of dNTPs (ELPIS Biotech, Daejeon, ROK) and 1 U of Super-Therm Taq polymerase (JMR Holdings, London, UK). The initial thermal cycling protocol involved denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 8 cycles comprising denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, primer annealing at 50 °C for 1 min, and extension at 72 °C for 2 min. Subsequent cycling included 30 additional cycles with denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, annealing at 55 °C for 1 min, and elongation at 72 °C for 2 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. PCR was performed using a ProFlex PCR system (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

## RT-PCR
Total RNA was extracted from the lung tissues of shrews using the TRI reagent following the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using a high-capacity RNA-to-cDNA kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). A random hexamer and OSM55 (5′-TAG TAG TAG ACT CC-3′) were used for the synthesis. Amplification of target sequences was performed using first-round and nested PCRs in a 25 µL reaction volume. Each reaction contained 0.625 U of Ex Taq DNA polymerase (TaKaRa BIO, Japan), 2.5 µL of 10X Ex Taq buffer, 2 µL of dNTP mixture (2 mM each), 10 pmol of each primer (final concentration: 0.4 µM), and 1.5 µL of the cDNA template. Oligonucleotide primer sequences and cycling conditions were described previously , . All PCRs were performed using a ProFlex PCR system (Life Technologies).

## mNGS
Library preparation was performed using the NEBNext Ultra II directional RNA-sequencing kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). mRNA (without rRNA and tRNA) was isolated using the poly(A) RNA selection kit (Lexogen, Vienna, Austria). cDNA was synthesized from the isolated mRNA and subsequently fragmented according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Libraries were indexed and subjected to PCR enrichment. The amplified libraries were quantified using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer system with a DNA high sensitivity kit (Agilent Technologies, Amstelveen, Netherlands) and a library quantification kit with the StepOne Real-Time PCR system (Life Technologies). NGS was performed using a NovaSeq 6000 sequencing system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

## NGS data analysis
Virome analysis was performed using an in-house VirPipe pipeline that incorporates primary analytical methods with end-to-end functionality . The first step involved preprocessing the sequencing reads with Minimap 2 to remove genomic sequences associated with the reservoir host . Filtered reads were subsequently subjected to de novo assembly to construct viral contigs using the SPAdes assembler . The assembled contigs were further analyzed and annotated using the Nucleotide Basic Local Alignment Search Tool . In the second approach, the viral reads were aligned to the ULLEV Cu09-33 reference genome using CLC Genomics Workbench (v.22.0.2; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Variants were identified and consensus sequences were extracted.

## RACE PCR
To determine the 3′ and 5′ terminal sequences of the ULLEV genome, RACE PCR was performed using the 3′- and 5′-RACE system for cDNA amplification, version 2.0 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

## Phylogenetic analysis
Tripartite genomic sequences of ULLEV were aligned with reference strains using the MUSCLE algorithm . Reference sequences used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 3. Phylogenetic analyses were performed for each segment using the best-fit evolutionary substitution models. A rigorous branch-swap filter was applied to enhance the reliability of the tree reconstruction. These optimal evolutionary models were implemented using the maximum likelihood method in MEGA 7 . The stability and robustness of the phylogenetic topologies were evaluated using bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates.

## Co-divergence analysis
A tanglegram algorithm was constructed to evaluate phylogenetic associations between hantaviruses and their primary reservoir hosts. Central auxiliary lines depict the correspondence between nodes in the respective phylogenies. To optimize the reconciliation analysis, the complexity of the dendrograms was systematically reduced prior to conducting a comprehensive evaluation. This analytical approach was executed using the dendextend package in R .

## Haplotype network analysis of crocidurian shrews
Haplotypes of the mitochondrial CYTB from representative Crocidurian shrews, including sequences of C. utsuryoensis collected on Ulleung Island, were analyzed using DnaSP v5.0 . A haplotype network was constructed using the median-joining algorithm in PopART v1.7, with the epsilon parameter set to zero . The nodes in the network were color-coded according to the geographic origin of the DNA sequences using visualization tools integrated into PopART v1.7.

## N-glycosylation site analysis
N-glycosylation site analysis of glycoproteins was conducted by submitting the complete amino acid sequences to the NetNGlyc 1.0 server (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk) using the default settings for all parameters .
