## Background
Sepsis, characterized by a severe systemic inflammatory response leading to organ failure, often results in acute respiratory dysfunction in approximately half of affected individuals. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying pulmonary impairment in sepsis is critical for advancing clinical interventions. This study aimed to investigate the role and mechanism of histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC2) in the progression of septic lung injury (SLI).

## Methods
To establish the SLI model, cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) was performed in vivo. Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining was used to assess pathological changes in lung tissue. For in vitro experiments, lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced macrophages were employed to model SLI. Macrophage activation was evaluated by analyzing the expression levels of F4/80, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and CD86. Protein–protein interactions were analyzed via co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP), while transcriptional regulation was examined using luciferase assays and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP).

## Results
The findings demonstrated that HDAC2 knockdown mitigates SLI severity and suppresses macrophage activation. Mechanistically, HDAC2 directly interacts with SOX2 to inhibit its acetylation and destabilize the protein. Furthermore, SOX2 binds to the STAMP2 promoter region and modulates its transcriptional activity.

## Conclusion
Collectively, the HDAC2/SOX2/STAMP2 signaling axis represents a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of septic lung injury.

## Introduction
Sepsis, a potentially fatal condition characterized by systemic organ failure, arises from dysregulated host responses to infectious agents [ , ]. It is one of the most common clinical critical illnesses and is often accompanied by multi-organ failure (MOF), among which the lung is the most susceptible target organ. Patients experience persistent lung inflammation during the onset of sepsis. Clinical investigations have demonstrated that nearly half of individuals diagnosed with sepsis subsequently develop pulmonary complications, specifically acute lung injury or its more severe form, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ALI/ARDS) [ , ].
At the cellular and molecular levels, the mechanism by which sepsis causes ALI/ARDS is complex, involving imbalanced inflammatory responses, cell death, endothelial barrier destruction, microthrombosis, immune dysfunction, and mitochondrial dysfunction. However, the exact molecular mechanism by which sepsis causes ALI/ARDS is not fully understood. Although extensive research has been conducted to explore the pathogenesis and treatment of sepsis-induced lung injury, effective therapies remain limited [ , ]. Consequently, elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms underlying sepsis-induced ALI/ARDS represents a critical research priority, offering potential therapeutic avenues for sepsis management.
Histone deacetylases (HDACs), a class of enzymatic proteins, mediate the deacetylation process at lysine residues in both histone and non-histone substrates, thereby modulating chromatin structure and gene expression [ ]. They play critical roles in structural modification and gene expression regulation. At present, 18 human HDACs have been identified, categorized into four major classes based on their homology with yeast: Class I includes HDAC1, HDAC2, HDAC3, and HDAC8, which exhibit ubiquitous tissue expression [ , ]. HDAC1, HDAC2, and HDAC8 are predominantly localized in the nucleus, whereas HDAC3 can shuttle between the nucleus and cytoplasm due to its nuclear localization and export signals. HDAC1, HDAC2, and HDAC3 are class I HDACs that often function as catalytic subunits within multi-protein co-repressor complexes [ , ]. HDAC3 serves as the catalytic subunit of SMRT and NCoR complexes. Among classical class I HDAC family members, HDAC2 has been extensively implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of multiple disease states [ ].
Macrophage-specific HDAC2 deficiency significantly mitigates LPS-induced innate immune activation in septic models, providing enhanced resistance to both endotoxin shock and E. coli-mediated sepsis in murine systems [ ]. HDAC2 modulates inflammatory responses through transcriptional regulation of c-Jun and PAI-1 expression in macrophage populations following lipopolysaccharide stimulation [ ]. Additionally, pharmacological inhibition of HDAC2 has been shown to potentiate the renoprotective effects of dexmedetomidine, an α2-adrenergic receptor agonist, in sepsis-induced renal dysfunction [ ]. However, the molecular pathways by which HDAC isoforms contribute to pulmonary pathology during sepsis remain poorly characterized and warrant further investigation.
In this study, we established comprehensive experimental models of sepsis-associated acute lung injury, encompassing both in vitro and in vivo systems, to systematically elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying HDAC2-mediated pathological processes. We hypothesized that HDAC2 mediates septic lung injury by regulating macrophage activation and SOX9 acetylation.

## Materials and methods

## Collection of clinical specimens
Blood samples were collected from 30 sepsis patients and 30 age-matched healthy controls at The First Affiliated Hospital of Jinzhou Medical University. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the hospital’s Ethics Committee. Peripheral venous blood (5 mL per subject) was drawn into EDTA-coated tubes under sterile conditions, promptly centrifuged, and plasma was stored at − 80 °C until PCR analysis. All procedures adhered to institutional ethical guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki.

## Establishment of septic lung injury model in mice
Adult male C57BL/6 mice (28 ± 3 g) were maintained under controlled environmental conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, 18–22 °C, 40–60% humidity) with unrestricted access to standard chow and water. Following a 7-day acclimatization period, animals were randomly assigned to four experimental groups: sham-operated controls, CLP-induced sepsis, CLP + lv-sh-NC, and CLP + lv-sh-HDAC2. The sepsis-induced acute lung injury (ALI) model was established via cecal ligation and puncture (CLP). After a 12-h fasting period, mice were anesthetized using 5% isoflurane inhalation and positioned in a supine orientation. Following abdominal sterilization, a 1.0 cm midline incision was created to expose the cecum, which was then ligated and punctured three times using an 18-gauge needle prior to repositioning within the peritoneal cavity. Sham-operated controls underwent identical surgical procedures without cecal puncture.
For HDAC2 knockdown, lentiviral vectors encoding either sh-HDAC2 or sh-NC (0.2 mL, 1 × 10⁹ pfu/mL) were administered via tail vein injection 96 h prior to CLP induction.

## Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining
Histopathological analysis was conducted using HE staining. Briefly, lung tissue specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and then embedded in paraffin. Tissue Sects. (5 μm thickness) were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for 5 min each. Morphological assessment was performed using light microscopy.

## Cell cultrure
The RAW264.7 murine macrophage cell line (American Type Culture Collection, ATCC) was cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were maintained under standard culture conditions at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere.

## Cell transfection and treatment
Cells (4 × 105 cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and assigned to different groups for transfection. Treatments included siRNAs targeting HDAC2, SOX2, and STAMP2 (si-HDAC2, si-SOX2, si-STAMP2), overexpression plasmids for HDAC2 and SOX2, and negative controls (si-NC and empty vector). Transfection was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Catalog No. 11668500, Invitrogen, USA). After transfection, macrophages were treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 100 ng/mL; Sigma-Aldrich) for 12 h to induce an inflammatory response, thereby establishing an in vitro cellular model.

## Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)
RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (Jiancheng Bioengineering, Nanjing, China) and quantified with a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer. RNA concentrations were adjusted to 500 ng/μL prior to cDNA synthesis, which was performed using a commercial reverse transcription system (Vazyme Biotech, R233-01). Quantitative PCR was carried out with HiScript Q RT SuperMix (Vazyme Biotech, R323-01) under the following standardized cycling conditions: 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 15 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 70 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the endogenous control.

## MPO, IL-1β, TNF-ɑ, IL-6 and MCP-1 content determination
The concentrations of MPO (A044-1–1), IL-1β (H002-1–1), TNF-α (H052-1–1), IL-6 (H007-1–1), and MCP-1 (H115-1–1) in cellular and tissue samples were quantified using commercially available assay kits (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

## Western blot
Protein extraction was conducted using RIPA lysis buffer, followed by quantification with a BCA protein assay kit (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA). Protein samples (50 μg/lane) were separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with a rapid protein-blocking buffer, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies (1:1000 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. The following antibodies were used: anti-HDAC2 (H2663), anti-SOX2 (SAB5701206), anti-acetylation (AB3879), anti-STAMP2 (ABC341), and anti-GAPDH (AB2302) from Sigma-Aldrich. Following thorough washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (AP510, Sigma-Aldrich) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein signals were visualized using a ChemiDoc™ XR + imaging system (Bio-Rad).

## Protein stability assessment
Cells were treated with cycloheximide (CHX, 100 μg/mL; Yuanye Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for designated time points (0, 6, 12, and 24 h). SOX2 protein expression dynamics were evaluated via Western blot analysis.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
ChIP assays were performed using the Pierce™ Agarose ChIP Kit (Thermo Scientific, catalog no. 26157, Waltham, MA, USA) to investigate SOX2-mediated regulation of the STAMP2 promoter. Briefly, 1 × 10⁷ cells per group were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min, followed by quenching with glycine. After PBS washing, cells were lysed and centrifuged. The supernatant was treated with micrococcal nuclease (MNase) digestion (10 min on ice) and subsequently processed with lysis buffer, sonication, and centrifugation. Chromatin samples were diluted in IP buffer and incubated with anti-SOX2 antibody overnight at 4 °C for immunoprecipitation. Protein A/G beads were added to capture protein-DNA complexes, followed by washing and elution. DNA was purified using spin columns and quantified by qPCR to assess STAMP2 promoter enrichment.

## IHC
Paraffin-embedded lung tissue Sects. (5 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and incubated in 3% H₂O₂ to quench endogenous peroxidase activity. Antigen retrieval was conducted using 10 mM citrate buffer (Yuanye, Shanghai, China) with microwave heating for 3 min. After blocking with normal goat serum (30 min), sections were incubated with a primary anti-F4/80 antibody (Abcam; 4 °C, overnight) and a species-specific secondary antibody (Abcam; 37 °C, 1 h). Immunohistochemical staining was visualized and analyzed using a light microscope (Olympus, Japan).

## Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
The interaction between HDAC2 and SOX2 was investigated using Co-IP assays with magnetic bead technology (Pierce, 14321D; Thermo Fisher), followed by immunoblot analysis. Cell lysates containing antibody-bound protein complexes were incubated overnight at 4 °C, then captured using Protein A/G magnetic beads for 1 h at room temperature. The resulting immunoprecipitates were subsequently analyzed via western blotting to confirm the specific protein interaction.

## Statistical analysis
Data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences between groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA p < 0.05 was considered statistically different.

## Results

## HDAC2 was up-regulated in septic lung injury
Initial investigation of HDAC2 expression patterns in sepsis-induced pulmonary injury demonstrated significant upregulation in patient-derived blood samples, as determined by qPCR analysis (Fig.  A). Consistently, HDAC2 levels were increased in the lung tissue of CLP mice (Fig.  B). Subsequently, macrophages were treated with LPS, revealing that LPS stimulation elevated HDAC2 mRNA levels (Fig.  C). Furthermore, western blot analysis confirmed that protein expression of HDAC2 was upregulated in the lung tissue of CLP mice and in macrophages following LPS treatment (Fig.  D, E). Similarly, LPS stimulation upregulated HDAC2 protein expression in macrophages (Fig.  F, G). 

## Knockdown of HDAC2 inhibited the activation of macrophage
To further confirm whether HDAC2 could inhibit macrophage activation, we treated macrophages with LPS. si-HDAC2 treatment significantly reduced HDAC2 protein levels (Fig.  A). Subsequently, HDAC2 knockdown suppressed LPS-induced iNOS expression (Fig.  B). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that LPS treatment significantly increased CD86-positive cell percentages, while HDAC2 silencing reduced this effect (Fig.  C). ELISA quantification demonstrated that HDAC2 silencing significantly decreased IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and MCP-1 concentrations in the experimental samples (Fig.  D–G). 

## HDAC2 inhibits the acetylation of SOX2 and its protein stability
As a deacetylase, HDAC2 regulates substrate acetylation to modulate their functional activity. We observed that HDAC2 suppressed both the acetylation and expression levels of SOX2 (Fig.  A). Subsequently, Co-IP assays confirmed direct interaction between HDAC2 and SOX2 (Fig.  B). To identify potential acetylation sites in the SOX2 sequence, bioinformatics analysis predicted lysine residues K10, K67, and K119 as candidate sites. To determine which sites mediate HDAC2 binding, we generated site-specific mutants in an expression vector. Immunoprecipitation (IP) assays demonstrated that, compared to the WT group, mutants at K10 and K119 exhibited significantly increased SOX2 acetylation. This effect was further amplified under HDAC2 overexpression conditions (Fig.  C). SOX2 acetylation levels in the K10 and K119 mutant groups were markedly higher than those in the WT and K67 mutant groups (Fig.  D). These findings indicated that HDAC2 binds to SOX2 at lysine residues K10 and K119. Furthermore, assessment of SOX2 protein stability revealed that CHX treatment combined with HDAC2 overexpression significantly reduced SOX2 protein half-life (Fig.  E). 

## SOX2 regulated the transcription of STAMP2
Given the previously uncharacterized role of SOX2 in sepsis-associated pulmonary injury, we investigated its mechanistic contribution to this pathological process. As SOX2 is a transcription factor, we screened for potential SOX2 target genes and identified STAMP2, as STAMP2 has been reported to ameliorate sepsis-induced acute lung injury. Transfection with an SOX2-overexpressing vector increased SOX2 protein levels (Fig.  A) and upregulated STAMP2 expression (Fig.  B). Bioinformatics analysis identified putative SOX2 binding sites within the STAMP2 promoter region (Fig.  C, D). Luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that SOX2 overexpression enhanced the luciferase activity of a reporter construct containing the wild-type (WT) STAMP2 promoter sequence (Fig.  E). ChIP assays confirmed direct binding of SOX2 to the STAMP2 promoter (Fig.  F). Luciferase reporter assays further revealed that HDAC2 overexpression inhibited SOX2-mediated transcriptional activation of STAMP2 (Fig.  G). Western blot analysis demonstrated that HDAC2 overexpression reversed the SOX2-mediated upregulation of STAMP2 protein expression (Fig.  H). 

## SOX2 knockdown reversed the effects of HDAC2 knockdown
Having established HDAC2-mediated regulation of SOX2 acetylation, we next investigated the functional consequences of this interaction in sepsis-associated pulmonary injury using targeted rescue experiments. The results demonstrated that siRNA targeting SOX2 significantly reduced SOX2 protein levels (Fig.  A). Functional analyses revealed that SOX2 depletion reversed the effects of HDAC2 knockdown, which had promoted STAMP2 expression (Fig.  B) while suppressing iNOS (Fig.  C), CD86 (Fig.  D), and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β (Fig.  E), TNF-α (Fig.  F), IL-6 (Fig.  G), and MCP-1 (Fig.  H). These findings indicate that HDAC2 mediates its protective effects in septic lung injury through modulation of SOX2 activity. 

## STAMP2 silencing reversed the effects of HDAC2 knockdown
Following confirmation of SOX2-dependent regulation of STAMP2 transcription, we evaluated the role of STAMP2 in HDAC2-mediated pathogenic mechanisms during sepsis-induced pulmonary injury using complementary rescue experiments. The results demonstrated that siRNA targeting STAMP2 significantly reduced STAMP2 protein levels (Fig.  A). Functional analysis of the HDAC2-STAMP2 axis revealed that STAMP2 inhibition reversed HDAC2 silencing-mediated suppression of iNOS, CD86, and pro-inflammatory cytokine release (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, and MCP-1) (Fig.  B–G). These findings indicate that HDAC2 mediates its protective effects in septic lung injury through modulation of the STAMP2 pathway. 

## HDAC2 knockdown alleviated the lung injury induced by sepsis
As HDAC2 was upregulated in septic injury, we hypothesized that it may contribute to sepsis pathogenesis. To investigate this, we established a murine model of sepsis via CLP. Lentiviral delivery of sh-HDAC2 via tail vein injection significantly reduced HDAC2 protein levels in mice (Fig.  A). HDAC2 depletion increased SOX2 and STAMP2 protein expression in CLP-induced sepsis models (Fig.  B). Histopathological analysis revealed distinct morphological differences: sham-operated controls exhibited intact alveolar architecture with smooth walls and no exudates, whereas CLP-induced specimens displayed erythrocyte infiltration, inflammatory cell accumulation, alveolar septal thickening, and partial alveolar collapse. Notably, HDAC2 knockdown significantly attenuated these pathological changes, restoring pulmonary histoarchitecture (Fig.  C). Additionally, HDAC2 silencing reduced myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity and decreased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 (Fig.  D–G). Macrophage activation plays a critical role in septic injury progression. Subsequent analysis of macrophage activation in lung tissues revealed that immunohistochemistry (IHC) demonstrated marked F4/80 upregulation in CLP-induced mice, which was significantly suppressed following HDAC2 knockdown (Fig.  H). These findings indicate that HDAC2 inhibition mitigates sepsis-induced pulmonary injury and macrophage activation. 

## Discussion
This investigation provides insights into the functional role and molecular mechanisms of HDAC2 in sepsis-associated pulmonary injury. Our findings demonstrated significant HDAC2 upregulation in septic lung tissue, and genetic suppression of HDAC2 resulted in reduced severity of pathological manifestations. Consistent with prior studies demonstrating HDAC2-mediated inhibition of LPS-induced macrophage inflammation in endotoxemia models [ , ], we confirmed that HDAC2 attenuates LPS-induced macrophage activation, thereby supporting previous observations.
Macrophage-driven inflammation is governed primarily by key signaling pathways [ , ]. Studies in liver injury and sepsis models demonstrate that modulation of NF-κB and Akt cascades plays a critical role in regulating macrophage polarization (e.g., M2 phenotype) and inflammatory responses [ – ]. These pathways converge to functionally control cytokine production and tissue damage, providing a mechanistic basis for targeting upstream epigenetic regulators like HDAC2 in sepsis. HDAC2 has been shown to regulate LPS-triggered innate inflammatory responses in macrophages [ ]. Our investigations further revealed that HDAC2 influences macrophage activation, confirming and expanding upon prior understanding of its functional role in macrophage biology. Previous studies have characterized HDAC2 as a crucial epigenetic regulator that ameliorates sepsis-associated lung injury through inhibiting c-Jun nuclear import and the transcriptional activation of the PAI-1 gene. As a histone deacetylase, HDAC2 regulates the transcription of various genes, including PDK1 [ ], IL-17A [ ], YY1 [ ], and TLR4 [ ]. Here, we found that HDAC2 directly binds to SOX2 protein and inhibits its acetylation, thereby reducing its protein stability.
As a key member of the SOXB1 transcription factor family, SOX2 functions as a critical regulator of stem cell pluripotency. Its transcriptional diversity, mediated by dynamic interactions with multiple cofactors, plays a pivotal role in both embryonic development and adult stem cell maintenance [ , ]. SOX2 has been implicated in the progression of various cancers, including colorectal and gastric cancer [ – ]. However, its role in sepsis remains unclear. In a previous study, SOX2 was found to mediate the effects of long non-coding RNA SOX2OT on hippocampal neurogenesis and cognitive function in a mouse model of sepsis-associated encephalopathy [ ]. Here, we demonstrate that SOX2 is a substrate of HDAC2. HDAC2 directly binds to SOX2 and inhibits its stability by regulating SOX2 acetylation. Furthermore, rescue experiments revealed that SOX2 silencing reversed the effects of HDAC2 on macrophage activation, thereby confirming the functional interaction between HDAC2 and SOX2.
STAMP2 (STEAP4), a six-transmembrane domain protein, functions as a critical modulator of metabolic homeostasis through its dual regulatory roles in inflammation and metabolic signaling pathways [ ]. Previous studies have demonstrated that STAMP2 is upregulated in arthritis model mice [ ]. In human obese patients, STAMP2 expression is also elevated and closely associated with the macrophage marker CD68 [ ]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that STAMP2 might act as a regulatory modulator in macrophage-mediated inflammatory processes. Henrik ten Freyhaus et al. reported that STAMP2 controls intermediate metabolites, regulates macrophage inflammatory responses, and influences atherosclerosis progression [ ]. As a transcription factor, SOX2 is known to regulate the transcriptional activity of target genes. Here, we aimed to identify SOX2’s downstream targets. Through comprehensive analysis of genes implicated in sepsis-associated pulmonary injury pathogenesis, STAMP2 emerged as a potential key regulator. Using ChIP and luciferase reporter assays, we demonstrated that SOX2 directly regulates STAMP2 transcription. Furthermore, rescue experiments revealed that STAMP2 silencing abolished the effects of HDAC2 on macrophage activation, confirming the functional interplay between these regulators. These findings indicate that HDAC2 modulates macrophage inflammatory responses via the SOX2-mediated regulation of STAMP2 expression.
This study has several limitations. First, the findings are based on RAW264.7 macrophage models and murine in vitro systems, which may not fully recapitulate the complex interplay between parenchymal and non-parenchymal cells in sepsis-induced lung injury. Second, while HDAC2-SOX2/STAMP2 signaling was shown to modulate macrophage polarization and cytokine production, the direct role of pyroptosis remains unexplored. Third, direct measurements of lung edema or vascular permeability were not performed, limiting the assessment of barrier dysfunction. Furthermore, the structural domains mediating HDAC2–SOX2 binding and the functional consequences of SOX2 acetylation on DNA binding or co-factor interactions require structural and biochemical validation. Future research should validate these mechanisms in primary human macrophages, in vivo sepsis models, and human clinical samples to assess translational relevance. Additionally, investigating the crosstalk between pyroptosis and polarization, the role of STAMP2 in endothelial barrier integrity, and the organ-specific dynamics of HDAC2/SOX2/STAMP2 signaling will deepen our understanding of sepsis pathogenesis and identify novel therapeutic targets.
Taken together, this study demonstrates that HDAC2 regulates SOX2 in septic lung injury models and mediates sepsis-induced injury. SOX2, in turn, governs STAMP2 transcription. Our findings suggest that the HDAC2/SOX2/STAMP2 signaling axis represents a promising therapeutic target for the treatment of sepsis-induced pulmonary injury.
