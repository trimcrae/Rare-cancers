## Abstract
Metabolic diseases, including type 2 diabetes mellitus, obesity, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disorders, are increasingly recognized as conditions driven not only by metabolic imbalance but also by dysregulated cell death pathways. Beyond apoptosis, ferroptosis and pyroptosis have emerged as pivotal non-apoptotic cell death pathways linking metabolic stress, oxidative injury, and chronic inflammation. Ferroptosis is characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, while pyroptosis is mediated by inflammasome activation and gasdermin-dependent membrane pore formation, both of which contribute to progressive tissue dysfunction in metabolic organs. Growing evidence highlights ion channels and G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) as critical upstream regulators of these processes. Ion channels governing Ca²⁺, K⁺, and Fe²⁺ fluxes modulate redox balance, mitochondrial integrity, and inflammasome activation, thereby shaping cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis and pyroptosis. In parallel, GPCRs integrate hormonal, lipid, and microbial-derived signals to influence antioxidant defenses, iron homeostasis, and inflammatory signaling cascades. This review provides a comprehensive synthesis of current knowledge on the roles of ion channels and GPCRs as pharmacological regulators of ferroptosis and pyroptosis in metabolic diseases. This article discusses mechanistic insights, pathway cross-talk, and emerging therapeutic strategies, including repurposed drugs, small-molecule inhibitors, and nutraceuticals targeting these surface receptors. By framing metabolic diseases as disorders of regulated cell death signaling, this review underscores ion channels and GPCRs as promising, druggable targets for next-generation interventions aimed at halting metabolic inflammation and tissue injury.

## Introduction
Metabolic diseases such as type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), obesity, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD/NASH), and atherosclerosis are complex disorders characterized by chronic metabolic dysfunction and low-grade inflammation. A growing body of evidence indicates that dysregulated cell death pathways pathways play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of these conditions [ , ]. Whereas research historically focused on apoptosis, recent paradigms highlight that ferroptosis and pyroptosis, two non-apoptotic forms of programmed cell death that critically contribute to metabolic tissue injury and inflammation. For example, chronic inflammation (“metaflammation”) in obesity and diabetes involves persistent activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and pyroptotic cell loss, while lipid peroxidation-driven ferroptosis has been implicated in insulin resistance and organ damage [ , ]. These diseases can be viewed as disorders of regulated cell death signaling, wherein metabolic stress, immune dysregulation, and cell death failures intertwine.
Ion channels and G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) have emerged as central “control nodes” in these processes, integrating metabolic cues with cellular stress and death pathways. Ion channels orchestrate ionic homeostasis and membrane potential, thereby influencing redox state and inflammasome activation. Likewise, GPCRs being master regulators of hormonal, lipid, and neurotransmitter signaling modulate intracellular second messengers (cAMP, Ca2+, etc.) that can tip the balance between cell survival and death. Together, ion channels and GPCRs form an accessible pharmacological interface to manipulate ferroptosis and pyroptosis. Targeting these surface regulators is attractive because many are druggable with existing small molecules or biologics, making them promising therapeutic entry points [ – ]. Importantly, a pharmacological approach to modulate regulated cell death may fill a critical gap in current treatment of metabolic diseases, which often addresses metabolic symptoms (hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia) but not the underlying cell injury and inflammatory self-amplification [ , ]. This review posits that ion channels and GPCRs act as key pharmacological switches connecting metabolism, redox stress, and inflammasome signaling, and thus represent strategic targets to quell tissue damage in metabolic disorders.
In this context, the present review systematically synthesizes and critically evaluates current evidence on ion channels and G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) as upstream pharmacological regulators of ferroptosis and pyroptosis in metabolic diseases, with particular emphasis on how ionic fluxes (Ca²⁺, K⁺, and Fe²⁺) and GPCR-mediated signaling converge to drive redox imbalance, inflammasome activation, and tissue injury. The novelty of this review lies in its integrative, mechanism-centered perspective that unifies ferroptosis and pyroptosis within a single regulatory framework, positioning ion channels and GPCRs as central control nodes linking metabolic stress, oxidative damage, and inflammatory cell death. Unlike previous reviews that address these pathways in isolation, this article reframes metabolic diseases as disorders of dysregulated cell death pathways signaling rather than solely metabolic imbalance, thereby identifying ion channels and GPCRs as highly druggable, translational targets for next-generation therapeutic strategies aimed at disrupting the self-amplifying cycle of metabolic inflammation and organ dysfunction.

## Overview of ferroptosis and pyroptosis in metabolic diseases

## Ferroptosis: iron-dependent lipid peroxidation in metabolic tissues
Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of regulated necrotic cell death driven by lethal accumulation of lipid peroxides in cellular membranes [ , – ]. Mechanistically, ferroptosis is caused by disruption of the cellular antioxidant network (notably depletion of glutathione or inactivation of glutathione peroxidase 4, GPX4) leading to unchecked peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA)-containing phospholipids [ ]. Iron catalyzes these reactive oxygen species (ROS)-driven lipid peroxidation reactions, so dysregulated iron metabolism is a key determinant of ferroptotic susceptibility [ , ]. In contrast to apoptosis, ferroptotic cells undergo distinct morphological changes most prominently shrunken mitochondria with reduced cristae and dense membranes, while nuclei remain intact [ , ]. Ultimately, excessive lipid peroxidation damages membranes and can cause catastrophic plasma membrane rupture (a necrotic-like endpoint) [ ].
In metabolic disease contexts, multiple tissues exhibit ferroptosis that links metabolic stress to cell dysfunction:

Liver (NAFLD/NASH): The liver in NASH often accumulates excess iron (hemosiderosis), which exacerbates oxidative injury. Studies show that hepatic iron overload promotes lipid peroxidation and cell death in NASH, whereas iron chelation (deferoxamine) or lipophilic antioxidants (liproxstatin-1, vitamin E) ameliorate liver injury. Ferroptosis is strongly implicated in the progression of fatty liver to steatohepatitis: ferroptotic death of hepatocytes releases damage signals that trigger inflammation and fibrosis. In a NASH mouse model, inhibition of ferroptosis significantly reduced hepatic inflammation and fibrosis, underscoring the pathologic role of ferroptosis in metabolic liver disease [ , , ].
Pancreatic β-cells (T2DM): Pancreatic islet β-cells are highly susceptible to oxidative stress due to low baseline expression of antioxidant enzymes. Emerging evidence suggests that ferroptosis contributes to β-cell failure in T2DM [ , ]. Chronic high glucose and lipotoxicity can deplete glutathione and inactivate GPX4, precipitating lipid peroxidation in β-cells. Indeed, diabetic models show iron accumulation in islets and lowered GPX4; interventions like quercetin (a natural iron chelator/antioxidant) protect β-cells, improve insulin secretion, and reduce ferroptosis markers [ , ]. These findings align with the concept that ferroptotic loss of β-cells leads to insufficient insulin and worsened glycemic control. Thus, ferroptosis may be a mechanism linking glucolipotoxic stress to T2DM progression.
Adipocytes (Obesity): In obese adipose tissue, hypertrophic adipocytes experience high oxidative stress and often exhibit cell death. While adipocyte apoptosis was classically described, recent work hints that ferroptosis and pyroptosis also occur. Adipocytes have substantial PUFA content that can undergo peroxidation. Obesity is associated with altered iron distribution for example, adipose may sequester iron due to inflammation-induced hepcidin, creating a pro-ferroptotic environment. Although direct evidence of adipocyte ferroptosis is still emerging, one study found that treating obese mice with ferrostatin-1 improved adipose function and insulin sensitivity (suggesting underlying ferroptosis was alleviated) [ , , ]. Additionally, macrophage-driven inflammation in adipose tissue can supply ROS that trigger nearby adipocyte ferroptosis, blurring the lines between metabolic dysfunction and iron-catalyzed cell death.

Notably, ferroptosis in metabolic organs often feeds a vicious cycle of dysfunction. For instance, ferroptotic death of hepatocytes or adipocytes can release danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and oxidized lipids, which recruit immune cells and activate inflammasomes in those cells [ ]. This can incite pyroptosis (inflammatory cell death) in immune cells, compounding tissue damage. Overall, ferroptosis represents a fundamental mechanism by which metabolic derangements (iron overload, hyperlipidemia, hyperglycemia) translate into organ injury in metabolic diseases [ , ].

## Pyroptosis: inflammasome-driven cell death in metabolic inflammation
Pyroptosis is a form of lytic, pro-inflammatory programmed cell death executed by the pore-forming protein gasdermin and typically triggered by activation of inflammasomes (e.g., the NLRP3 inflammasome) [ , ]. Upon sensing danger signals, pattern-recognition receptors like NLRP3 assemble a multimeric inflammasome complex with the adaptor ASC and pro-caspase-1. Caspase-1 activation then cleaves the gasdermin D (GSDMD) protein, whose N-terminal fragments oligomerize to form large pores (~ 10–20 nm) in the plasma membrane [ ]. These GSDMD pores cause an ionic flux and cell swelling, eventually leading to plasma membrane rupture and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 that were concurrently processed by caspase-1 [ , ]. Pyroptosis is thus a highly inflammatory form of cell death unlike ferroptosis, it directly releases cytokines and can amplify immune responses. It is “programmed” in the sense that it depends on caspase enzymatic activity and the gasdermin pore-former, distinguishing it from accidental necrosis.
In metabolic diseases, chronic sterile inflammation is a driving pathology, and pyroptosis has been identified as a critical mediator linking metabolic stress to inflammatory damage [ , ]:

Obesity and Insulin Resistance: Adipose tissue of obese individuals is infiltrated by macrophages that secrete inflammasome-related cytokines (IL-1β, IL-18), contributing to insulin resistance. Pyroptosis has been observed in both adipose macrophages and adipocytes. Notably, hypertrophic adipocytes can undergo pyroptosis: obese adipocytes show activated caspase-1 and GSDMD pores, suggesting that as fat cells become over-stressed, they die by pyroptosis and release IL-1β. This induces a vicious cycle of local inflammation (impairing insulin signaling) a phenomenon termed “metaflammation” where metabolic and inflammatory pathways converge. Mice lacking NLRP3 or caspase-1 have improved insulin sensitivity on high-fat diets, highlighting that inflammasome-driven pyroptosis in fat promotes systemic metabolic derangements [ , ].
Type 2 Diabetes and Islet Inflammation: In T2DM, chronic hyperglycemia and lipids can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome in pancreatic islet cells and macrophages. Pyroptosis of pancreatic β-cells has been reported under glucotoxic conditions, leading to reduced β-cell mass and impaired insulin secretion [ ]. Even more, pyroptosis contributes to diabetic complications: for example, in diabetic cardiomyopathy and nephropathy, elevated NLRP3 activation and GSDMD pore formation have been detected in cardiac myocytes and renal cells, respectively. The importance of NLRP3/pyroptosis in diabetes is evidenced by pharmacological studies e.g., the diabetes drug metformin was found to inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation and cardiomyocyte pyroptosis, reducing myocardial injury. Similarly, GLP-1 receptor agonists (incretin mimetics used for T2DM) can suppress NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis in diabetic rodents, as discussed later [ , , ].
NAFLD/NASH: The progression from simple fatty liver to steatohepatitis involves inflammatory cell death in the liver. Hepatocytes in NASH patients often show pyroptotic features (ballooning degeneration with IL-1β release). The NLRP3 inflammasome is highly expressed in NASH livers, and genetic or pharmacological inhibition of NLRP3 greatly attenuates liver inflammation and fibrosis. For instance, treating NASH models with an NLRP3 inhibitor or knocking out NLRP3 reduces the release of IL-1β/IL-18 and protects against hepatocyte injury [ , ]. This implicates pyroptosis as a key driver of the inflammatory and fibrotic cascade in fatty liver disease.
Atherosclerosis and Vascular Complications: Chronic vascular inflammation in atherosclerosis is fueled in part by cholesterol-induced inflammasome activation in macrophages of arterial plaques. Cholesterol crystals and oxidized LDL can activate NLRP3 in plaque macrophages, leading to IL-1β release and pyroptotic death of these cells [ ]. Pyroptosis of macrophages contributes to plaque necrotic core formation. Importantly, the purinergic P2X7 ion channel (activated by extracellular ATP) has been implicated in this process: P2X7  triggers K+ efflux and NLRP3 activation in macrophages within atherosclerotic lesions, exacerbating inflammation. Inhibition of P2X7 or NLRP3 in experimental atherosclerosis markedly reduces IL-1β release and plaque inflammation [ ]. Additionally, endothelial cells under hyperglycemic or hyperlipidemic stress may undergo pyroptosis, compromising the vascular barrier. A recent study in diabetic mice showed endothelial pyroptosis (via NLRP3) was associated with accelerated atherosclerosis, whereas treatment with melatonin (a natural antioxidant) inhibited endothelial pyroptosis and slowed plaque progression [ , ].

Cross-talk Between Ferroptosis and Pyroptosis, although ferroptosis and pyroptosis are distinct pathways, they are not isolated in metabolic pathology, in fact, they can mutually modulate each other’s activation (Fig.  ). Oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation (hallmarks of ferroptosis) can act as signals that trigger inflammasome activation and pyroptosis. For example, in macrophages, excessive lipid peroxides can be sensed via a TLR4-TRIF-ROS pathway or by oxidized phospholipid detection by caspase-11 and caspase-4, leading to NLRP3 inflammasome assembly. A striking demonstration of this cross-talk was shown in sepsis models: myeloid cell-specific GPX4 deletion (which induces rampant lipid peroxidation, i.e., ferroptosis-like conditions) led to uncontrolled caspase-11 activation and gasdermin D cleavage, causing macrophage pyroptosis and a cytokine storm [ , , ]. In essence, ferroptosis-associated DAMPs and oxidized lipids can serve as the “priming” or activating signals for pyroptosis. Conversely, pyroptosis can create a pro-oxidant environment that may promote ferroptosis in neighboring cells. When pyroptotic cells form GSDMD pores, the resultant K+ efflux and cell swelling leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS generation [ , ]. These ROS can diffuse to nearby cells and potentially initiate lipid peroxidation there. Also, inflammatory cytokines from pyroptotic cells (like IL-1β) can induce nitric oxide and other radicals in tissues, potentially tipping the balance toward ferroptosis under conditions of iron overload. Therefore, in metabolic disease milieus, pyroptosis and ferroptosis often occur together and amplify one another. This underscores the need for an integrated approach to modulate both forms of cell death to break the cycle of inflammation and tissue injury. Having introduced the concepts of ferroptosis and pyroptosis in metabolic diseases, this section now delves deeper into how specific ion channels and GPCRs regulate these cell death pathways.
 Beyond serving as an energy-producing organelle, mitochondria act as a central execution platform for regulated cell death in metabolic diseases. Mitochondrial iron accumulation, particularly within the inner mitochondrial membrane, facilitates localized Fenton reactions that initiate lipid peroxidation, thereby sensitizing cells to ferroptosis. Concurrently, impaired mitochondrial antioxidant systems, including reduced GPX4 activity and glutathione depletion, exacerbate mtROS production, amplifying oxidative damage. Recent evidence demonstrates that mitochondrial lipid peroxidation products not only propagate ferroptotic death but also act as potent danger-associated molecular patterns capable of triggering inflammasome activation, thereby mechanistically linking ferroptosis to downstream pyroptotic signaling [ – ].

## Ion channels as pharmacological regulators of ferroptosis
Ion channels govern the flow of ions (K+, Na+, Ca2+, Cl−, Fe2+, etc.) across membranes, thereby controlling cell excitability, volume, and signaling pathways. During ferroptosis, profound changes in membrane integrity and ion homeostasis occur, and recent studies indicate that certain ion channels actively contribute to ferroptotic cell death [ , , ]. In metabolic disease settings, stress signals (e.g., oxidative stress, mechanical stretch, and metabolic derangements) can modulate ion channel activity, thus influencing ferroptosis sensitivity. This section discusses key channel types involved in ferroptosis: calcium-permeable channels, iron transporters and other redox-sensitive channels, as well as potassium/chloride channels that affect membrane potential.

## Calcium channels (TRP channels and VGCCs)
Excessive intracellular Ca2+ is a well-known mediator of cell injury and can promote ferroptosis via multiple mechanisms. Ca2+ overload can activate Ca-dependent enzymes (like phospholipases) that generate lipid peroxides, disrupt mitochondrial function leading to ROS generation, and increase cytosolic oxidative stress. In ferroptosis, a surge of Ca2+ influx is observed as cells undergo lipid peroxidation and membrane damage [ , ]. Two major sources of this Ca2+ are voltage-gated and mechanosensitive calcium channels:

Transient Receptor Potential (TRP) Channels: TRP channels are a family of cation channels, many of which are Ca2+-permeable and activated by stress signals (temperature, mechanical stretch, oxidative cues). During ferroptosis, mechanosensitive TRP channels become important. A recent breakthrough identified Piezo1 (a mechanically activated cation channel) and certain TRP channels as key drivers of ferroptosis execution. Lipid peroxidation increases membrane tension and curvature, which directly activates mechanosensitive channels like Piezo1 and possibly stretch-sensitive TRP channels (e.g., TRPV2, TRPC1). Activation of these channels causes a massive influx of Ca2+ (and Na+) and efflux of K+, collapsing the normal ionic gradients (K+ leaking out, Na+/Ca2+ flooding in). This ionic storm leads to cell swelling and further membrane tension increase, ultimately facilitating plasma membrane rupture (the end stage of ferroptosis) [ , , ]. In support, pharmacologically blocking TRP channels can delay or prevent ferroptosis: the broad cation channel blocker ruthenium red significantly inhibited ferroptotic death induced by GPX4 inhibition. Likewise, genetic deletion of Piezo1 or co-inhibition of TRP channels (with 2-APB) was shown to rescue cells from ferroptosis, indicating these channels act cooperatively to drive lethal ionic influx. TRP channels also link metabolic stress to ferroptosis in disease models. For instance, TRPM2, a ROS-activated calcium channel, was found to mediate ferroptosis in ischemia-reperfusion (I/R) injury: TRPM2-knockout mice had significantly less lipid peroxidation and tissue damage in renal I/R injury [ , ]. This suggests that under oxidative stress (as occurs in metabolic syndrome or I/R events), TRPM2 opens to admit Ca2+, thereby exacerbating ferroptotic damage. Other TRP channels, such as TRPM7 (a ubiquitously expressed Mg2+/Ca2+ channel) and TRPC channels, may also contribute to ferroptosis when abnormally activated during metabolic stress, although direct evidence is still emerging. Overall, Ca2+-permeable TRP channels amplify ferroptotic death by linking membrane perturbations to catastrophic ionic derangements.
Voltage-Gated Calcium Channels (VGCCs): Metabolic tissues like pancreatic β-cells, muscle, and neurons possess VGCCs that open in response to membrane depolarization, allowing Ca2+ entry. While VGCCs are crucial for physiological functions (e.g., insulin secretion in β-cells), their overactivation under pathological conditions can promote oxidative injury [ ]. In hyperglycemic states, β-cells experience repeated depolarization and VGCC-mediated Ca2+ influx, potentially leading to ER stress and mitochondrial overload of Ca2+. This can generate ROS and may tip cells into ferroptosis if antioxidant defenses are concurrently impaired. In cardiomyocytes of diabetic models, Ca2+ overload is known to contribute to cell death and has been speculated to have a ferroptotic component (especially since diabetic hearts show iron accumulation and lipid peroxidation). Calcium channel blockers (like L-type channel blockers used for hypertension) might incidentally reduce ferroptotic stress by limiting Ca2+ influx, although this needs further validation. Another calcium pathway is store-operated Ca2+ entry (SOCE), mediated by ORAI1 channels. Notably, ORAI1 was reported to mediate harmful Ca2+ influx during oxidative stress, contributing to cell death [ , ]. In summary, excessive Ca2+ influx through VGCCs or SOCE channels under metabolic stress conditions can synergize with iron-catalyzed ROS to trigger ferroptosis. Therapeutically, moderate inhibition of pathological Ca2+ entry may be protective against ferroptosis-driven tissue injury in metabolic diseases, though care must be taken not to disrupt normal Ca2+ signaling needed for metabolism.

## Iron transporters and redox-sensitive channels
Given ferroptosis’s dependence on iron, proteins that transport or regulate iron are de facto modulators of this death pathway. Two key players are the divalent metal transporter-1 (DMT1) and the transferrin receptor (TfR1), which import ferrous (Fe2+) and transferrin-bound iron into cells, respectively. Upregulation of DMT1 or TfR1 increases the labile iron pool, thus accelerating Fenton chemistry that produces ROS and lipid peroxides [ , ]. In metabolic disease states, iron metabolism is often perturbed: for example, adipose tissue in obesity and liver in NAFLD show increased TfR1 expression and iron uptake in response to local or systemic iron overload. This predisposes cells to ferroptosis. Indeed, T2DM and metabolic syndrome patients frequently have elevated ferritin and transferrin saturation, correlating with markers of oxidative stress. In diabetic models, reducing iron uptake or promoting iron storage can mitigate tissue damage, e.g., overexpression of ferritin (iron-storage protein) or treatment with iron chelators protect against diabetic cardiomyopathy by preventing iron-driven ferroptosis [ , ].
Besides classical iron transporters, certain ion channels and transporters sensitive to redox status can influence ferroptosis:

Voltage-Gated Proton Channels (HV1) and NADPH Oxidase: While not an “ion channel” for cations, HV1 channels extrude protons from cells (especially in immune cells) to maintain charge balance during NADPH oxidase activation. Hyperactivation of NADPH oxidase (which produces superoxide) in metabolic inflammation can deplete antioxidant reserves and indirectly promote ferroptosis by raising ROS [ , ]. HV1 might facilitate this by sustaining NADPH oxidase activity. Inhibiting HV1 has been shown to reduce oxidative damage in some contexts, which could be explored in ferroptosis modulation.
Redox-Gated Ion Channels: Some channels are directly gated by oxidative conditions. TRPM2, mentioned earlier, is a prime example, it opens in response to ADP-ribose and ROS, allowing Ca2+ and Zn2+ influx. Zn2+ overload via TRPM2 or other routes can also contribute to cell death; interestingly, zinc has been implicated in oxidative stress-induced death (sometimes called oxytosis). Blocking TRPM2’s redox activation (pharmacologically or via knockout) alleviated ferroptosis in renal and hepatic I/R models [ ]. Another channel influenced by cellular redox is the NRF2-regulated cystine/glutamate antiporter (system Xc−). System Xc− itself is a transporter, not an ion channel, but it exchanges glutamate– for cystine2–. Its activity maintains glutathione levels and thus prevents ferroptosis; interestingly, membrane potential and associated ion gradients can affect its function (e.g., depolarization can reduce cystine uptake). In diabetes, high glucose can impair system Xc− function and glutathione synthesis, effectively sensitizing cells to ferroptosis [ , , ].
Transferrin and Transferrin Receptor Cycling: In hyperinsulinemic or inflammatory states, there is often increased transferrin receptor expression on cells (as seen in some diabetic nephropathy models) [ ]. This enhances iron uptake. Conversely, ferroportin, the iron export channel, can be downregulated by hepcidin in inflammation, trapping iron inside cells [ ]. Net effect: metabolic inflammation leads to intracellular iron loading (high TfR1, low ferroportin), creating fertile ground for ferroptosis. Therapies that modulate these pathways (e.g., hepcidin inhibitors or minihepcidins to adjust ferroportin, TfR1 antagonists) are conceptual strategies to reduce ferroptosis in diseases like NASH or diabetic heart failure where iron plays a role [ , ].

In summary, channels and transporters that govern iron and redox balance DMT1, TfR1, ferroportin, TRPM2, and others significantly impact ferroptosis. Metabolic diseases often skew the expression or activity of these proteins toward a pro-ferroptotic state (iron overload, high ROS). Addressing this by normalizing iron fluxes (e.g., with chelators, hepcidin modulators) or inhibiting redox-gated channels can be a viable approach to curtail ferroptosis-induced damage.

## Potassium and chloride channels: membrane potential and ferroptotic susceptibility
Unlike Ca2+ and Fe2+, the roles of potassium (K+) and chloride (Cl−) channels in ferroptosis are less direct but still noteworthy. Ferroptosis involves drastic changes in membrane electrophysiology: as lipid peroxidation progresses, the plasma membrane’s properties alter (increased permeability to ions, possible nanopore formation, and Na+/K+ pump dysfunction). This leads to membrane depolarization and ion gradient collapse (K+ efflux, Na+ influx) as part of the execution phase [ , ]. The activity of K+ and Cl− channels can influence how readily these ionic changes occur and how the cell responds:

K+
Channels: Under normal conditions, many cells maintain a negative resting membrane potential largely through K+ efflux via potassium channels. If these channels are highly active (hyperpolarizing the cell), the driving force for Ca2+ entry might be increased, potentially exacerbating Ca-driven peroxidation. Conversely, K+ channel opening can also alleviate cellular energy strain by hyperpolarizing and reducing spontaneous electrical activity (in excitable cells). The net effect of K+ channel modulation on ferroptosis is context-dependent. Some studies have noted that blocking certain K+ channels delays cell swelling and death in necrotic processes. In ferroptosis, K+ efflux is observed as a late event (via non-selective cation pores or channels) [ ], but it is unclear if earlier activation of specific K+ channels makes cells more or less prone to ferroptotic damage. Interestingly, one report suggests that cells with a pre-depolarized membrane (less negative inside) were somewhat resistant to ferroptosis induced by cystine starvation, possibly because depolarization reduced cystine import efficiency (though this mechanism remains to be fully elucidated) [ ]. On the flip side, K+ efflux is absolutely required for inflammasome activation (pyroptosis), but for ferroptosis its role is more permissive, it accompanies the process rather than triggers it.
Some specific K+ channels to consider in metabolic contexts: K_ATP channels in β-cells (which link metabolism to membrane potential) closure of K_ATP depolarizes cells and could influence oxidative stress. While primarily studied in insulin secretion, one study found that the K_ATP blocker glyburide also had an off-target effect of reducing NLRP3 inflammasome activation, hinting interplay between K+ flux and cell death signaling [ , ]. In vascular smooth muscle and cardiomyocytes, K+ channel openers (like pinacidil) can protect against ischemic injury (some attribute this to reduced Ca2+ overload), so examining if they also mitigate ferroptosis in those cells is warranted.

Cl−
Channels: Chloride ions help regulate cell volume and electrical neutrality. During ferroptosis, as Na+ enters cells, Cl− often follows (either through channels or transporters) to maintain electrochemical balance, contributing to osmotic swelling. A recent preprint suggested that chloride ions are essential determinants of ferroptosis, proposing that anion flux influences membrane potential and cell volume during ferroptotic death. In that model, blocking Cl− channels or lowering extracellular Cl− provided some protection from ferroptosis, presumably by reducing the water influx and membrane rupture that mark late-stage ferroptosis [ , , ]. Additionally, the loss of KCl via GSDMD pores in pyroptosis causes cell shrinkage, whereas in ferroptosis, accumulation of NaCl inside causes swelling; thus Cl− dynamics may differentiate or modulate these death processes. One chloride channel of interest is VRAC (Volume-Regulated Anion Channel), which activates during cell swelling to release Cl− and osmolytes. In ferroptosis with early membrane dysfunction, VRAC might activate and either delay death by counteracting swelling or, paradoxically, contribute to death by releasing glutathione or other protective osmolytes (since VRAC can leak glutathione disulfide). This remains an area of active research.

In summary, potassium and chloride channels indirectly shape ferroptosis by controlling the membrane potential and volume changes. Their pharmacological modulation could fine-tune the threshold for ferroptotic damage. For example, mild activation of K+ efflux (to prevent Ca2+ overload) or inhibition of Cl− influx (to reduce cell swelling) might alleviate ferroptosis. However, these interventions must be balanced, as too much K+ loss or altered ion balance can itself be harmful. Understanding the precise ion flux sequence in ferroptosis will help identify which K/Cl channels are viable drug targets.

## Pharmacological modulators of channels in ferroptosis
Given the involvement of ion channels in ferroptosis, a range of pharmacological modulators have been explored to control this cell death pathway:

Cation Channel Inhibitors: Blocking the mechanosensitive cation channels that drive ferroptotic execution has shown promise in preclinical studies. Ruthenium red, a broad inhibitor of TRP channels and Piezo1, significantly inhibited ferroptosis triggered by GPX4 inactivation. Similarly, GsMTx4 (a peptide toxin that specifically inhibits mechanosensitive channels like Piezo1) could reduce calcium influx and prevent membrane rupture in ferroptosis models. These inhibitors point to a novel strategy: using ion channel blockers as “ferroptosis suppressors” in diseases where ferroptosis is pathogenic (e.g., using Piezo1 inhibitors to protect the heart from ferroptotic damage after ischemia). Indeed, a study showed that Piezo1 genetic knockout, or pharmacologic inhibition, preserved cell viability in GPX4-knockout cells. Notably, combining Piezo1 inhibition with a TRP channel blocker (2-APB) had synergistic protective effects, underscoring that multiple channels contribute and may need co-targeting [ , , ].
Calcium Channel Blockers: Classical L-type calcium channel blockers (e.g., nifedipine, verapamil) are widely used for cardiovascular diseases. While not studied explicitly for ferroptosis, they could theoretically mitigate Ca2+-driven aspects of ferroptosis in heart or pancreas by limiting Ca2+ overload. There is emerging interest in repositioning such drugs if future research confirms a benefit. Additionally, drugs targeting store-operated Ca2+ entry (like BTP2 which blocks ORAI1 channels) might reduce oxidative Ca2+ loading in cells, thus lowering ferroptotic risk under metabolic stress [ , , ].
Iron Chelators and Transport Modulators: Though not “ion channels,” iron chelators (e.g., deferoxamine, deferiprone) are key pharmacological tools to inhibit ferroptosis by reducing redox-active iron. In metabolic disease models, deferoxamine has been shown to attenuate ferroptosis-mediated damage (for example, improving liver histology in NASH and cardiac function in diabetic cardiomyopathy by lowering iron availability) [ , ]. Agents that modulate iron transport such as mini-hepcidins (to reduce hepcidin and thus increase iron efflux) or transferrin mimetics are more experimental but could indirectly act as ferroptosis modulators. Metformin, interestingly, has mild iron-chelating properties and was shown to upregulate ferritin; this is hypothesized to contribute to its observed inhibition of cardiac ferroptosis in diabetic animals [ , ].
Lipid Peroxidation Inhibitors: Although not ion channel modulators, it’s worth noting that compounds like ferrostatin-1 and liproxstatin-1 specifically trap lipid radicals and have become “gold standard” ferroptosis inhibitors. In a sense, they pharmacologically compensate for the loss of GPX4 activity. These small molecules have been successful in numerous preclinical models (AKI, stroke, etc.) [ , ]. While their direct clinical use remains distant, they underscore the druggability of ferroptosis. Ion channels could be considered upstream targets in the same pathway e.g., combining a ferrostatin (to stop peroxidation) with a Piezo1 blocker (to prevent ion-driven membrane rupture) might yield additive protection.
Repurposed Drugs & Experimental Compounds: Some existing drugs not originally developed for ferroptosis have shown activity in this arena. For instance, zileuton (a 5-LOX inhibitor for asthma) can reduce lipid peroxide formation; vitamin E (α-tocopherol), an over-the-counter antioxidant, was one of the first recognized inhibitors of ferroptosis [ ] and has been used in clinical trials for NASH (with mixed results, possibly due to inadequate delivery or stage of disease). Selenium supplementation can boost GPX4 activity, indirectly curbing ferroptosis relevant since many metabolic disease patients are selenium-deficient. On the channel side, propofol, an anesthetic with membrane-stabilizing effects, has been noted to protect neurons from ferroptosis partly by blocking lipid peroxidation and possibly stabilizing ion channel function in membranes (propofol is a known Na+channel blocker and antioxidant) [ ].

In summary, there is a toolbox of pharmacological agents to modulate ion channel activity and related ferroptotic processes. Table  provides a summary of representative ion channel targets in ferroptosis, their modulators, and effects in metabolic disease models. As our understanding of channel involvement in ferroptosis grows, so will opportunities to refine these pharmacological interventions ideally toward selective inhibition of pathological channel activity without disturbing normal physiology.
 

## Ion channels in pyroptotic signaling and inflammasome activation
Ion fluxes are not only consequences of pyroptosis (as cells form pores and rupture) but also triggers and regulators of the inflammasome activation that leads to pyroptosis (Fig.  ). The NLRP3 inflammasome, in particular, is exquisitely sensitive to changes in cytosolic K+Ca2+, and other ionic events [ , ]. Certain ionotropic receptors and channels on the plasma membrane serve as upstream sensors that link extracellular danger signals to intracellular inflammasome assembly.
 

## K+ efflux as a central pyroptotic trigger, the role of P2X7 and pannexin
A hallmark of NLRP3 inflammasome activation is a drop in intracellular potassium concentration. K+ efflux is both necessary and sufficient for NLRP3 activation in most contexts. Low intracellular [K+] acts as a permissive signal that induces NLRP3 to undergo conformational change and oligomerize with NEK7 (NIMA-related kinase 7), an event required for inflammasome assembly [ ]. This K+ efflux can occur through various “holes” in the plasma membrane:

P2X7 Receptor (P2 × 7R): P2X7 is a trimeric ATP-gated cation channel highly expressed on immune cells (macrophages, monocytes, etc.) and also found in some metabolic tissues (e.g., adipose tissue macrophages and liver Kupffer cells). When extracellular ATP (a danger signal often released by stressed cells) binds P2X7, the channel opens to allow K+ to flow out and Na+/Ca2+ to flow in. Prolonged activation of P2X7 can even form a larger pore either by dilating itself or by recruiting another pore protein. Activation of P2X7 is one of the most potent triggers of NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis. The mechanism: ATP release (e.g., from damaged hepatocytes or adipocytes) stimulates P2X7 on nearby macrophages; within seconds, K+ efflux through P2X7 lowers cytosolic K+, which licenses NLRP3 to assemble, leading to caspase-1 activation and IL-1β/IL-18 release [ , , ]. P2X7 thus connects an extracellular metabolic danger signal (ATP is often high in areas of cell injury or stress) to inflammatory cell death. In atherosclerotic plaques, for instance, dying cells release ATP which activates P2X7 on infiltrating macrophages, resulting in IL-1β release that furthers plaque inflammation. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of P2X7 has been shown to attenuate pyroptosis and inflammation in models of atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, and diabetic retinopathy. P2X7 knockout mice have reduced IL-1β levels and smaller atherosclerotic lesions. Pharmacologically, specific P2X7 antagonists (e.g., A740003, AZD9056, JNJ-47965567) effectively block ATP-induced IL-1β release in immune cells. Notably, P2X7 inhibitors showed benefit in preclinical models of cardiac injury and arrhythmia post-MI by reducing inflammasome-mediated tissue damage. However, clinical trials of P2X7 antagonists in chronic inflammatory diseases (like rheumatoid arthritis) have so far been underwhelming, possibly due to insufficient target engagement or redundant pathways [ , , ]. Nonetheless, P2X7 remains a prime target for mitigating pyroptosis in metabolic inflammation, especially given its druggability and the pathological role of extracellular ATP in metabolic tissues (e.g., ATP from necrotic adipocytes triggering macrophage pyroptosis in obesity).
Pannexin-1 Hemichannels: Pannexin-1 is a membrane channel that can form large, non-selective pores (similar to connexins). During non-canonical inflammasome activation (triggered by cytosolic LPS via caspase-11/4/5), pannexin-1 channels are cleaved by caspase-11, causing ATP release to the extracellular space. This ATP then autocrinely or paracrinely activates P2X7, leading to K+ efflux and full NLRP3 inflammasome activation. In other words, pannexin-1 provides the conduit for ATP release that engages P2X7 in the pathway of caspase-11-driven pyroptosis (important in septic shock and possibly in metabolic endotoxemia). Blocking pannexin-1 (e.g., with the inhibitor carbenoxolone or by gene KO) can reduce ATP release and subsequent P2X7-mediated pyroptosis [ , ]. While pannexin channels are less studied in metabolic disease, they could play a role in situations of cellular stress where endogenous TLR ligands or gut microbiota LPS leak into circulation (metabolic endotoxemia in obesity) activating caspase-11.
Two-Pore Domain K+
Channels (K2P): These are leak K+ channels helping set resting membrane potential. There is evidence that some K2P channels (such as TREK-1, TWIK-2) might be involved in the K+ efflux during inflammasome activation [ ]. For instance, when cells swell or undergo membrane disturbance, K2P channels can allow K+ to exit. If these channels are deleted, NLRP3 activation can be blunted in some cases. However, P2X7 and GSDMD pores likely overshadow their contribution under most conditions.
Gasdermin D Pores: Once caspase-1 is active, GSDMD pores form in the membrane and themselves allow K+ efflux (along with other ions). GSDMD pores are large and non-selective (they let even big molecules like IL-1β through), so they cause a massive collapse of ion gradients. Importantly, the initial K+ efflux needed for NLRP3 activation (to activate caspase-1) comes from channels like P2X7/pannexin, but once GSDMD pores form, they perpetuate K+ efflux and ensure the pyroptotic cell fully loses its osmotic stability. One intriguing aspect is that GSDMD pores lack ion selectivity (10–20 nm holes), so they even allow large fluxes of Ca2+ and Na+. As discussed below, Ca2+ entry through GSDMD pores can paradoxically trigger membrane repair mechanisms as a last-ditch regulatory step [ , , ].

In metabolic diseases, targeting the K+ efflux step has therapeutic potential. P2X7 antagonists are a clear example: by preventing K+ efflux, they keep NLRP3 off. As mentioned, they reduced lesion size in atherosclerosis and inflammation in diabetic models [ ]. Another approach is the small molecule MCC950, a direct NLRP3 inhibitor (it likely stabilizes NLRP3 in an inactive conformation regardless of K+ levels). MCC950 has shown remarkable efficacy in preclinical metabolic disease models (improving insulin sensitivity, reducing steatohepatitis and atherosclerosis) [ ]. While not an ion channel modulator per se, it validates the idea that blocking that pathway (which is initiated by ion flux) yields metabolic benefits. In sum, preventing or modulating K+ efflux via P2X7, pannexin, or downstream NLRP3 blockade is a viable strategy to prevent pyroptotic damage in metabolic inflammation.

## Ca2+ influx and mitochondrial dysfunction in pyroptosis (cross-talk with ferroptosis)
Calcium signaling is a double-edged sword in inflammasome regulation. Moderate Ca2+ signaling can promote certain inflammatory pathways, while excessive Ca2+ can activate cellular stress responses that either amplify or dampen inflammasomes. Several studies indicate Ca2+ influx (or release from intracellular stores) can potentiate NLRP3 inflammasome activation, although often in concert with K+ efflux rather than as a standalone trigger [ , ]. Mechanistically, how might Ca2+ promote pyroptosis?
Dysregulated mitochondrial calcium handling represents another critical pathological node. Excessive Ca²⁺ influx through ion channels and GPCR-coupled pathways leads to mitochondrial calcium overload, collapse of mitochondrial membrane potential, and opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore. These events promote mtROS generation and mitochondrial DNA release, both of which are well-recognized activators of the NLRP3 inflammasome. In metabolic disease contexts, including diabetic cardiomyopathy and NAFLD, this calcium–mitochondria axis has been shown to directly facilitate caspase-1 activation and gasdermin-mediated pyroptosis [ – ].

Mitochondrial Damage and ROS: One prominent theory is that Ca2+ influx leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, which then produces ROS and releases mitochondrial DNA both potent NLRP3 activators. When Ca2+ floods the cytosol (e.g., via P2X7 opening or other channels), mitochondria take up Ca2+ excessively, causing depolarization and opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore. This results in the generation of superoxide and leakage of mitochondrial DNA into the cytosol. Mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) are known to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome by oxidizing associated proteins or stimulating upstream signaling [ , ]. Mitochondrial DNA in the cytosol can also directly bind and activate NLRP3. Thus, Ca2+ influx indirectly triggers pyroptosis through mitochondrial distress. For example, in high-glucose conditions, elevated intracellular Ca2+ (partly due to ER stress and store-operated Ca2+ entry) has been linked to increased mtROS and NLRP3 activation in endothelial cells and renal cells, contributing to diabetic complications. Using mitochondria-targeted antioxidants or Ca2+ chelators in such models suppresses inflammasome activity, supporting this mechanism [ , , ].
Ca2+-Sensitive Signaling Molecules: Ca2+ can activate certain kinases (PKC, CAMKII) or phosphatases (calcineurin) that modulate inflammasome components. For instance, Ca2+-activated calcineurin can dephosphorylate certain sites and promote NLRP3 assembly. Some have proposed that Ca2+ release from the ER via IP3 receptors (which can occur if GPCRs like angiotensin II trigger Gq signaling) might prime or activate inflammasomes by calcineurin-mediated dephosphorylation of NLRP3. Additionally, Ca2+ can prompt lysosomal destabilization in cholesterol-laden macrophages, Ca2+ influx (triggered by crystals) causes lysosome rupture, releasing cathepsin enzymes that then activate NLRP3 [ , ].
Ca2+
and Gasdermin Pores: As noted, once gasdermin D pores form, Ca2+ rushes into the cell (since extracellular Ca2+ is 1 mM vs. nanomolar inside). This late-stage Ca2+ surge actually serves a regulatory role: it triggers the recruitment of the ESCRT-III membrane repair machinery to patch the holes. The cell essentially tries to save itself from lysis by shedding damaged membrane regions. If ESCRT-III successfully repairs the membrane, IL-1β release can be blunted (as IL-1β secretion in pyroptosis partly relies on complete lysis). Thus, Ca2+ entry through GSDMD pores is a negative feedback to pyroptosis, it doesn’t stop inflammasome activation but can reduce cell lysis and cytokine release [ , , ]. In practical terms, enhancing this Ca2+-mediated repair might be a strategy to limit pyroptosis-induced tissue damage without preventing upstream inflammasome roles in host defense. However, in metabolically stressed cells with compromised ATP, the repair may fail, leading to full-blown pyroptosis.

Given this, what about pharmacological modulation? Agents that prevent mitochondrial Ca2+ overload (like cyclosporine A, which blocks the mitochondrial permeability transition pore) have been shown to reduce NLRP3 activation in some studies. In diabetic cardiomyopathy, cyclosporine A reduced NLRP3 inflammasome and improved outcomes, presumably by preserving mitochondria. Similarly, calcium inhibitors like BAPTA-AM (a Ca2+ chelator) can inhibit NLRP3 activation in vitro by preventing Ca2+-triggered events (though BAPTA-AM is not specific and not a systemic drug). On the flip side, agonists of Ca2+-permeable channels (like high ATP on P2X7, or toxins like nigericin that form K+/H+ pores causing Ca2+ influx indirectly) are commonly used to experimentally activate inflammasomes. In metabolic disease, it stands to reason that reducing unnecessary Ca2+ influx e.g., through TRP channel blockers if those channels contribute to Ca2+ dysregulation in a tissue might mitigate inflammasome hyperactivation. For instance, TRPV4 (a mechanosensitive Ca2+ channel) is upregulated in diabetic kidney; inhibiting TRPV4 was found to reduce NLRP3-mediated renal inflammation in a mouse model of diabetic nephropathy (by limiting Ca2+-induced ROS release in tubules). This showcases the potential of targeting Ca2+ influx routes to calm pyroptotic signaling.
In summary, Ca2+ influx is a key secondary signal in pyroptosis, mainly through causing mitochondrial ROS release which then activates inflammasomes. It also has a complex role in later stages by engaging membrane repair. Balancing Ca2+ signals enough for normal cell function but not so much to trigger pyroptosis may be a therapeutic tightrope. Agents that protect mitochondria from Ca2+ overload or that block pathological Ca2+ entry (without disturbing physiological calcium-dependent processes) could emerge as adjunct therapies to limit pyroptosis-driven damage in metabolic diseases.

## Pharmacological inhibition of pyroptosis via ion channels
Several pharmacological strategies aim to inhibit pyroptosis by targeting ion channel pathways:

P2X7 Receptor Antagonists: As described, P2X7 is a prime target. Drugs like AZD9056 (AstraZeneca) and CE-224,535 (Pfizer) were developed for inflammatory conditions and showed good safety profiles. Although efficacy in rheumatoid arthritis was modest, interest remains for other applications. In metabolic disease models, P2X7 antagonists (e.g., A740003) have successfully reduced NLRP3 inflammasome activity: one study showed A740003 decreased macrophage IL-1β production and improved insulin sensitivity in obese mice (by reducing adipose tissue inflammation). Another found that blocking P2X7 ameliorated diabetic nephropathy in rats by dampening caspase-1 and IL-1β in renal tissues. These findings are encouraging, suggesting P2X7 blockers as potential anti-pyroptosis therapeutics in metabolic syndrome, atherosclerosis, and diabetic complications [ , ].
Panx1 Channel Inhibitors: Though fewer specific inhibitors exist, drugs like probenecid can block pannexin-1 channels. Probenecid (an old gout medication) has been shown to inhibit ATP release via pannexin and subsequently reduce inflammasome activation in some immune cell studies. While speculative, probenecid or novel Panx1 inhibitors might reduce pyroptosis in conditions of non-canonical inflammasome activation (e.g., by gut microbial LPS in NAFLD). Carbenoxolone is another Panx inhibitor, but it’s not very specific (also affects gap junctions) [ , ].
K+
Modulators: Completely blocking K+ efflux is not straightforward as it’s a fundamental cellular process. However, glyburide, a sulfonylurea antidiabetic, was serendipitously found to inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation at high concentrations [ ]. Initially, glyburide’s effect was thought to be via blocking the K_ATP channel and thus preventing K+ efflux. But later evidence suggests glyburide acts upstream of NLRP3 (possibly on a regulatory factor rather than directly on K+ flux). Regardless, glyburide and related sulfonylureas have been used as proof of concept inflammasome inhibitors in research. The caveat is that effective anti-inflammatory doses may cause hypoglycemia due to their primary action on insulin secretion, so they are not practical inflammasome drugs for non-diabetics.

Another approach: Potassium supplementation providing extra K+ extracellularly can prevent K+ efflux (diminishing the gradient). Indeed, culturing cells in high- K+ media blocks NLRP3 activation, and in mice, high- K+ infusion reduced inflammasome-driven IL-1β release (though obviously this is not a sustainable therapy due to cardiac risks of hyperkalemia). It underscores how tightly NLRP3 is coupled to K+ levels.

Ca2+
Pathway Inhibitors: Targeting Ca2+-related steps, like using mitochondrial antioxidants (Mito-TEMPO, etc.) or Ca2+ uptake blockers, has been mentioned. Additionally, calcineurin inhibitors (e.g., tacrolimus, cyclosporine) may indirectly reduce NLRP3 by maintaining phosphorylation that prevents inflammasome assembly, this is part of the rationale for why cyclosporine A reduced inflammation in some diabetic models. However, calcineurin inhibitors have many effects and significant toxicity, so their use would be limited to severe cases [ – ].
Direct NLRP3 Inhibitors: Though not acting on channels, it’s worth including MCC950 (also known as CRID3), a small molecule that specifically inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome oligomerization. MCC950 has shown impressive efficacy in models of T2DM, gout, and MSU crystal-induced inflammation by essentially blocking pyroptosis upstream. For metabolic disease, MCC950 improved glucose tolerance and reduced IL-1β in obese mice [ ]. While MCC950 itself is not yet approved (and may have off-target effects on liver at high doses), it serves as a lead compound for developing safe inflammasome inhibitors [ ]. Companies are actively developing NLRP3 inhibitors for NASH and diabetic kidney disease, their mechanism ties into ion channels insofar as they render cells “blind” to the ionic triggers.
Anti-Inflammasome Biologics: Instead of blocking channels, one can neutralize the outputs of pyroptosis. IL-1β neutralizing antibodies (like canakinumab) and the IL-1 receptor antagonist anakinra are already in clinical use for autoinflammatory syndromes. The landmark CANTOS trial with canakinumab showed reduced cardiovascular events in post-myocardial infarction patients by damping inflammation, indirectly supporting the concept that less IL-1β (often from pyroptotic macrophages) benefits outcomes [ , ]. In T2DM, anakinra improved glycemic control in some studies by reducing systemic inflammation. While these biologics don’t prevent pyroptotic cell death per se, they mitigate its inflammatory damage. They can be seen as downstream modulators complementing ion channel-directed strategies.

The ability to pharmacologically “tune” the ion fluxes that drive inflammasome activation provides a powerful approach to reduce chronic inflammation in metabolic disorders.

## GPCRs as master pharmacological switches of ferroptosis
GPCRs (G protein-coupled receptors) constitute a large superfamily of cell surface receptors that respond to hormones, lipids, neurotransmitters, and metabolites. They transduce signals primarily via coupling to G proteins (Gs, Gi/o, Gq/11, etc.) that regulate second messengers like cyclic AMP (cAMP), Ca2+, and lipid signaling pathways [ , ]. Because of their central role in metabolism and inflammation, GPCRs act as master regulators that can tilt cellular fate toward survival or death under stress conditions. This section explores how metabolic GPCRs and others influence ferroptosis, mainly by modulating cellular antioxidant systems and lipid metabolism:

## Metabolic GPCRs (GLP-1R, GIPR, FFARs) and ferroptosis
Metabolic hormones often signal through GPCRs, which can modulate the cell’s redox environment and susceptibility to ferroptosis:

GLP-1 Receptor (GLP-1R): The GLP-1R is a Gs-coupled receptor expressed in pancreatic β-cells, cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and some neurons. Activation of GLP-1R (by the incretin hormone GLP-1 or pharmacologic agonists like exenatide and liraglutide) raises intracellular cAMP, which in turn activates PKA and Epac pathways. This signaling has broad cytoprotective effects: it enhances insulin secretion, but also activates antioxidant and anti-apoptotic pathways in cells. GLP-1R signaling has been shown to counteract ferroptosis. Recent studies in diabetic models found that GLP-1 analogs upregulate the Nrf2 pathway, the master regulator of antioxidant responses [ , ]. For example, in a rat model of type 2 diabetic cardiomyopathy, liraglutide treatment increased nuclear Nrf2 levels and expression of its target genes (heme oxygenase-1, ferritin heavy chain, glutathione peroxidase, etc.), which led to reduced myocardial lipid peroxidation and prevention of ferroptotic cell death. Liraglutide-treated diabetic hearts showed preserved GPX4 protein and less malondialdehyde (an indicator of lipid peroxidation), correlating with improved cardiac function. In vitro, GLP-1 agonist added to high-glucose exposed cardiomyocytes or renal tubular cells mitigated the drop in glutathione and blocked the accumulation of lipid ROS, thereby suppressing ferroptosis. Mechanistically, cAMP/PKA from GLP-1R can phosphorylate and inhibit GSK-3β, which leads to stabilization of Nrf2 (since active GSK-3β otherwise triggers Nrf2 degradation); additionally, GLP-1R via Epac/Rap1 improves mitochondrial function and reduces oxidative stress. Collectively, GLP-1 receptor agonism indirectly raises the cell’s oxidative stress threshold increasing glutathione synthesis, upregulating GPX4, and promoting iron sequestration in ferritin [ – ]. This renders cells more resistant to ferroptotic triggers. Indeed, GLP-1 analogs are not only anti-diabetic but appear cardio- and nephro-protective in trials, possibly via these anti-ferroptotic and anti-inflammatory mechanisms [ ]. The emerging field of cardio-renal-metabolic medicine has keen interest in GLP-1R agonists’ ability to reduce oxidative tissue damage.
GIP Receptor (GIPR): Similar to GLP-1, glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) is an incretin hormone; its receptor (GIPR) is Gs-coupled on pancreatic and some CNS cells. Although less studied than GLP-1R, recent research suggests GIPR activation can also protect against ferroptosis. In an aging mouse model, GIP treatment alleviated ferroptosis-related brain damage by activating Epac/Rap1 and downstream antioxidant pathways. Essentially, GIP-Raising cAMP promoted the expression of antioxidant enzymes and maintained glutathione, similar to GLP-1R effects [ ]. Furthermore, a dual agonist (tirzepatide) that activates both GLP-1R and GIPR has been reported to induce ferroptosis in cancer cells (glioblastoma) while being cytoprotective in normal tissues, hinting at complex context-specific effects [ ]. In metabolic disease, GIP’s role is nuanced, it can be maladaptive in obesity but pharmacologically, co-agonism of GIPR with GLP-1R (tirzepatide) has shown remarkable benefits in glycemic control and weight loss. It’s speculated that part of tirzepatide’s end-organ benefits (kidney and liver protection in diabetes) might derive from reducing oxidative stress and cell death, possibly through combined GLP-1R/GIPR signaling enhancing cellular resilience to ferroptosis.
Free Fatty Acid Receptors (FFARs): Several GPCRs respond to fatty acids and their metabolites, linking lipid levels to cellular responses. Notable ones include FFAR1/GPR40 (long-chain FFA receptor on β-cells), FFAR4/GPR120 (omega-3 fatty acid receptor on macrophages and adipose), and FFAR2/GPR43 (short-chain FFA receptor on immune and gut cells). These receptors can couple to Gq and/or Gi pathways, affecting intracellular Ca2+ and cAMP. Their influence on ferroptosis is indirect but significant via metabolic modulation [ , ].
GPR120 (FFAR4): Activation of GPR120 by ω−3 fatty acids (like DHA, EPA) has anti-inflammatory effects, it promotes an M2 anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotype via β-arrestin-2 signaling and inhibits pro-inflammatory NF-κB [ ]. By reducing chronic inflammation, GPR120 signaling likely lowers the ambient ROS in tissues and can upregulate cellular stress defenses. Indeed, in adipocytes and liver, omega-3 activation of GPR120 reduces NADPH oxidase activity and increases adiponectin, an anti-oxidative adipokine. In obese mice, GPR120 agonism attenuated adipose tissue inflammation and improved insulin sensitivity [ ]. Although not directly shown to inhibit ferroptosis, these changes would make an environment less prone to iron-catalyzed oxidative death. On the other hand, one must note that ω−3 PUFAs themselves are substrates for lipid peroxidation. If not accompanied by sufficient antioxidants, a high ω−3 membrane content could actually increase ferroptosis risk. However, the body typically converts some ω−3 to anti-inflammatory mediators (resolvins, protectins) which signal through other GPCRs to quell inflammation. Taken together, GPR120 activation in metabolic tissues can be seen as beneficial, tilting balance away from inflammation-induced ferroptosis (with the caveat of requiring antioxidants to guard the more unsaturated membranes).
GPR40 (FFAR1): Expressed on β-cells, GPR40 enhances glucose-stimulated insulin secretion when activated by fatty acids (coupling via Gq to raise Ca2+). Chronic high fat can overstimulate β-cells through GPR40, potentially leading to ER stress and oxidative stress. There was interest in GPR40 agonists as diabetes drugs (to boost insulin), but one such drug (fasiglifam) was halted due to liver toxicity. It’s possible uncontrolled GPR40 activation might contribute to oxidative damage; conversely, partial activation might strengthen β-cell function and antioxidant responses (insulin itself has antioxidant effects via PI3K/Nrf2 in some contexts). No direct data on GPR40 and ferroptosis yet, but given β-cells can undergo ferroptosis in T2DM [ ], and GPR40 modulates β-cell stress, it’s a subject for future research.
Short-Chain FFA Receptors (GPR43/GPR41): These receptors (activated by acetate, propionate, butyrate produced in gut) modulate immune function and metabolism. GPR43 (FFAR2) is on neutrophils and adipocytes, coupling to Gi/Gq. Activation of GPR43 by propionate has been shown to improve insulin sensitivity and reduce inflammatory cytokines in obese mice. Butyrate (through GPR109A, a receptor for niacin and butyrate) has anti-inflammatory effects on macrophages, increasing IL-10 and suppressing NF-κB. Butyrate also can epigenetically upregulate Nrf2 target genes. A study showed sodium butyrate reduced high-glucose-induced endothelial pyroptosis by inhibiting NLRP3 [ , ]. While that effect may be partly due to HDAC inhibition by butyrate, GPRs likely play a role too. So, short-chain fatty acids via their GPCRs can enhance antioxidant defenses and reduce inflammasome activity, indirectly mitigating ferroptosis risk (since an inflamed oxidative milieu fosters ferroptosis).

In essence, metabolic GPCRs like GLP-1R, GIPR, and FFARs act as “switches” that coordinate metabolism with cellular stress responses. When these GPCRs are engaged by their ligands or pharmacologic agonists, they generally activate pathways (cAMP/PKA, PI3K/Akt, AMPK) that lead to upregulation of antioxidant capacity (e.g., Nrf2, HO-1) and downregulation of pro-oxidant and pro-inflammatory signaling [ , ]. This integrated response often translates to inhibition of ferroptosis cells are better equipped to neutralize lipid peroxides and sequester iron. The therapeutic implication is clear: drugs that activate beneficial metabolic GPCRs (like GLP-1 analogs or fish-oil derived GPR120 agonists) may confer organ protection in metabolic diseases by preventing the ferroptotic death of key cell types (β-cells, hepatocytes, cardiomyocytes, etc.). Ongoing clinical successes of GLP-1R agonists in reducing NASH progression and heart failure hospitalizations lend credence to this idea, even if not originally designed with ferroptosis in mind.

## Lipid and eicosanoid GPCRs: PPAR-linked signaling and lipid ROS control
Cells are constantly exposed to lipid signaling molecules from free fatty acids to eicosanoids (prostaglandins, leukotrienes, lipoxins) many of which act through GPCRs. These GPCRs can strongly influence inflammation and oxidative stress, thus impacting ferroptosis:

Prostaglandin Receptors (EP1-4, DP1-2, etc.): Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) is particularly interesting. PGE2 has a paradoxical role: it can inhibit ferroptosis in certain cancer cells by activating its receptor EP2/EP4 (Gs-coupled, raising cAMP). In the context of metabolic disease, however, PGE2 is often pro-inflammatory in joint with other cytokines. That said, EP4 receptor activation triggers cAMP/PKA similar to GLP-1R. A study indicated that EP4 agonism can mimic some of PKA’s protective effects on NLRP3 inflammasome indeed PGE2 via EP4 was shown to induce PKA phosphorylation of NLRP3, contributing to suppression of inflammasome assembly [ , ]. This suggests that in tissues where PGE2 is produced (adipose tissue produces some PGE2, macrophages produce PGE2 in late phase inflammation), PGE2 might feedback to limit excessive inflammasome activation (a built-in brake). High PGE2 could also activate EP2/4 in parenchymal cells, leading to Nrf2 activation and increased COX-2 expression which produces more PGE2 a complex loop. In any case, GPCRs for eicosanoids can significantly alter redox signaling. For instance, leukotriene B4 receptor (BLT1) on macrophages (Gi-coupled) promotes NADPH oxidase activity and inflammatory chemotaxis; blocking BLT1 reduces oxidative burst and could indirectly lower ferroptotic damage to tissues in e.g. diabetes (where neutrophil and macrophage oxidative bursts cause tissue injury). Conversely, lipoxin A4 and resolvins signal via GPCRs like FPR2/ALX and ChemR23 to turn off inflammation and upregulate antioxidant enzymes. These pro-resolving lipid GPCRs (though not heavily studied in metabolic disease yet) represent potential anti-ferroptotic targets, as they coordinate the safe clearance of dead cells and restoration of redox balance.
PPAR-Linked Signaling: While PPARs (α, δ, γ) are nuclear receptors, GPCR signaling often intersects with PPAR pathways. For example, GPCR-induced cAMP can activate AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which then phosphorylates and activates PPARα coactivator (PGC-1α). PGC-1α boosts mitochondrial antioxidant capacity by inducing enzymes like superoxide dismutase and catalase. GPCRs that raise cAMP (β-adrenergic, GLP-1R, etc.) thereby indirectly enhance PPARα/δ activity, improving fatty acid oxidation and reducing toxic lipid accumulation that could feed ferroptosis. On the flip side, some GPCRs (like certain cannabinoid receptors or GPCRs activated by saturated fatty acids) might suppress PPAR activity and worsen lipid dysregulation. Notably, GPR120 activation leads to downstream PPARγ activation in macrophages (due to uptake of omega-3 that serve as PPARγ ligands) and drives anti-inflammatory M2 polarization [ , ]. PPARγ activation upregulates CD36 and other scavenger receptors which can help clear oxidized lipids beneficial to prevent ferroptosis in neighboring cells, though excessive CD36 uptake of oxidized LDL can cause foam cell formation. Additionally, PPARγ upregulates glutathione biosynthesis genes in certain contexts and has been shown to protect hepatocytes from oxidative stress. Thiazolidinediones (PPARγ agonists), used in T2DM, reduce liver fat and have anti-inflammatory effects, which might include lowering susceptibility to ferroptosis (some studies in NASH models suggest pioglitazone reduced markers of ferroptosis in the liver along with inflammation).

In summary, lipid-sensing GPCRs and the pathways they influence (like PPAR signaling) are intricately tied to the cellular handling of oxidative stress and lipid peroxides. By modulating lipid metabolism (balancing storage vs. oxidation) and inflammatory output, they affect the buildup of ferroptosis triggers (like PUFA-PLs and ROS). Pharmacologically targeting these (e.g., using omega-3 supplements to engage GPR120, or EP4 agonists for resolution phase enhancement) may support the prevention of ferroptotic damage in metabolic organs by promoting a more anti-oxidative, pro-resolving state.

## Orphan GPCRs and emerging targets
The GPCR universe includes many orphan receptors GPCRs with endogenous ligands not well identified some of which are highly expressed in metabolic tissues and immune cells. As research unravels their functions, new links to cell death regulation are emerging. A few worth mentioning:

GPR68 (OGR1): A proton-sensing GPCR enriched in inflammatory sites (acidic microenvironments). It can couple to Gq/11. In macrophages, acidosis via GPR68 can induce cytokine production. Possibly relevant in atherosclerotic plaques (which can be acidic) GPR68 might contribute to inflammasome priming. No direct link to ferroptosis yet, but acidosis could affect iron solubility and ROS generation [ ].
GPR39: A zinc-sensing GPCR in pancreatic cells and gut. Zn2+ is a cofactor for many antioxidant enzymes (Cu/Zn-SOD) and also modulates insulin signaling. GPR39 activation (by high Zn or agonists) triggers Gq and Gs pathways that have been shown to increase insulin secretion and GLP-1 release. Some studies indicate GPR39 agonism may protect β-cells from cytokine-induced apoptosis. Could it also bolster anti-ferroptotic defenses? Possibly, since Zn2+ flux through GPR39 can activate MAPK and mTOR pathways which upregulate defensive proteins [ ].
GPR21: An orphan receptor (knockout improves insulin sensitivity). GPR21(−/−) mice had reduced macrophage inflammation in adipose tissue. If a ligand or inhibitor for GPR21 is found, it might reduce metaflammation and thus pyroptosis in obesity [ ].
Cannabinoid Receptors (CB1/CB2) and GPR55: The endocannabinoid system (lipid mediators) has mixed roles. CB1 (Gi/o) in liver promotes lipogenesis blocking CB1 is being explored for NASH (though systemic CB1 blockade had psychiatric side effects). Interestingly, CB1 activation can increase ROS production in liver; CB1 antagonists reduced liver oxidative stress and inflammation, so they might indirectly curb ferroptosis in NASH. CB2 (Gi/o, expressed in immune cells) is generally anti-inflammatory when activated CB2 agonists reduced NLRP3 activation in some studies. GPR55 (a cannabinoid-related receptor) is pro-inflammatory in macrophages; inhibitors might reduce inflammasome activity [ , ].
Adhesion GPCRs: A unique class that often modulate immune cell tissue infiltration. For example, GPR56 and GPR97 are involved in macrophage function; their roles in cell death aren’t known, but as adhesion GPCRs they might influence how dead cells are cleared (efficient clearance prevents secondary necrosis which otherwise would cause lipid peroxidation and inflammasome activation) [ ].

New druggable GPCR targets are also being discovered via phenotypic screens. For instance, TAAR1 (trace amine receptor) agonists are in trials for diabetes (they improve glycemic control). TAAR1 in pancreatic α-cells reduces glucagon and can alter inflammation in adipose. Its impact on oxidative stress remains to be seen [ , ]. In summary, the landscape of GPCRs in metabolic regulation is extensive, and current understanding likely represents only an initial view of how these receptors intersect with ferroptosis and pyroptosis. As orphan GPCRs become deorphanized and their signaling pathways are elucidated, new opportunities may emerge to fine-tune these cell death mechanisms. Conceptually, GPCRs function as a multifaceted control panel governing cell survival–death decisions under metabolic stress. Targeting GPCRs frequently engages multiple downstream protective pathways—including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic reprogramming responses—thereby producing a polyphonic effect that is particularly advantageous for preventing regulated cell death.

## GPCR-mediated control of pyroptosis and metabolic inflammation
Having discussed GPCR influences on ferroptosis (Fig.  ), attention now turns to their roles in pyroptosis and inflammasome-driven inflammation in metabolic diseases. GPCRs on immune cells and parenchymal cells modulate inflammasome priming, assembly, and the consequent inflammatory milieu. Key themes include the balance between Gs and Gi signaling (and thus cAMP levels) in controlling NLRP3 activation, the recruitment of immune cells via chemokine GPCRs that sustain chronic inflammation, and emerging pharmacological ligands that target GPCRs to reduce pyroptotic inflammation.
 

## GPCR regulation of inflammasome assembly: Gi vs. Gs (cAMP-PKA-NLRP3 Axis)
One of the most important discoveries in NLRP3 inflammasome biology is that elevating intracellular cAMP can suppress NLRP3 activation [ ]. This ties GPCR signaling directly to pyroptosis control: Gs-coupled receptors (which increase cAMP) tend to be inflammasome-inhibitory, whereas Gi-coupled receptors (which decrease cAMP) can be inflammasome-potentiating. The molecular mechanism is twofold:

PKA Phosphorylation of NLRP3: cAMP activates protein kinase A (PKA), which can directly phosphorylate NLRP3 on a specific serine (Ser295 in human NLRP3). This phosphorylation event inhibits NLRP3’s ATPase activity (required for its oligomerization) and promotes NLRP3 binding to 14-3-3 proteins, keeping it in an off state [ , ]. Notably, pharmacological agents that raise cAMP (like forskolin or dibutyryl-cAMP) or natural ligands like bile acids (which activate the Gs-coupled bile acid receptor TGR5) have been shown to induce this inhibitory phosphorylation on NLRP3, thereby blocking inflammasome activation [ ]. One illustrative example: TGR5 agonists (bile acids such as tauroursodeoxycholic acid) increase cAMP in macrophages; this leads to PKA-mediated NLRP3 Ser295 phosphorylation and suppression of IL-1β release. This is referred in literature as “PKA has the gall to oppose NLRP3” (a pun noting bile acids’ effect) [ ].
Direct cAMP Binding to NLRP3: Beyond PKA, there is evidence that cAMP itself can bind NLRP3 (or an inflammasome component) allosterically to prevent its assembly. An older study noted that high cAMP levels inhibited inflammasome activation even in PKA-deficient cells, implying a PKA-independent mode of action [ ]. The exact binding and effect are not fully characterized, but it underscores that cAMP is a powerful second messenger gating pyroptosis.

Given this paradigm, GPCRs that modulate cAMP become key regulators:

Gs-coupled receptors (↑cAMP) such as β-adrenergic receptors, A2A adenosine receptors, GLP-1R/GIPR, EP2/EP4 prostaglandin receptors, TGR5 (bile acid receptor), D1 dopamine receptors, etc., generally inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activity [ , ]. For instance, the adenosine A2A receptor on macrophages, when stimulated, raises cAMP and was found to inhibit NLRP3-mediated IL-1β release and pyroptosis [ , ]. In microglia, an A_2A agonist prevented LPS/ATP-induced NLRP3 activation and downstream pyroptosis, whereas A_2A blockade or deletion exacerbated IL-1β release [ ]. Another example: ββ2 agonists have lower IL-1β levels in sputum, hinting at this effect. In obesity, sympathetic nervous system activity might similarly restrain adipose inflammasome to a degree via β-adrenergic signaling (though chronic catecholamines have other effects too).
Gi-coupled receptors (↓cAMP) such as CCR2/CCR5 (chemokine receptors), A1 and A3 adenosine receptors, some free fatty acid receptors like GPR43 (part Gi), α2mokine) on macrophages is Gi; its activation not only recruits macrophages to tissues but also lowers cAMP in those cells, which could favor NLRP3 activation if a priming signal is present. Indeed, obesity elevates MCP-1/CCR2 signaling leading to more macrophages and presumably a more inflammasome-prone state. Genetic or pharmacologic CCR2 ablation reduces adipose tissue IL-1β and improves insulin sensitivity [ , ], consistent with reduced pyroptotic inflammation. Another interesting case is the Adenosine A1 receptor (A1R, Gi): it is expressed in many tissues including heart and kidney. Activation of A1R in the brain was shown to protect from stroke by reducing excitotoxicity, but in immune cells A1R could enhance inflammasome. Conversely, A1 agonist in a cerebral I/R model reduced NLRP3 possibly A1 on neurons (Gi can reduce neurotransmitter release and oxidative stress). So, GPCR effects can be cell-type specific; nonetheless, broadly Gi signaling is pro-inflammatory in innate cells due to low cAMP.

Hence, the GPCR-G protein “binary code” (Gs vs. Gi) critically dictates inflammasome outcomes. This concept is being exploited, for instance, dopamine D1 receptor agonists (Gs-coupled) are being looked at for anti-inflammatory effects in glial cells; melanocortin receptor 1/3 (MC1/3R) agonists (Gs-coupled) also show NLRP3 inhibition which is part of how melanocortin therapy reduces gout flares. Additionally, bile acid-based drugs that target TGR5 (Gs) are in development for NASH these might not only improve metabolism but also directly quell liver inflammasomes via PKA phosphorylation of NLRP3 [ ].
In the context of metabolic disease, one could imagine a therapy that biases signaling towards Gs on immune cells (perhaps via a biased ligand that stimulates only the Gs arm of a normally Gi-coupled receptor). This is an area of GPCR biased agonism: e.g., engineered GLP-1R agonists that preferentially activate cAMP vs. β-arrestin could maximize anti-inflammatory, anti-pyroptotic actions while minimizing side effects. In summary, GPCRs fine-tune inflammasome assembly largely through the cAMP-PKA axis: pro-cAMP signals serve as “brakes” on NLRP3, whereas anti-cAMP signals are “accelerators.” Pharmacologically modulating GPCRs to adjust this balance offers a powerful means to control pyroptosis in metabolic inflammation.

## Chemokine and purinergic GPCRs: immune cell recruitment and chronic metabolic inflammation
Chronic inflammation in metabolic diseases is sustained by continuous recruitment and activation of immune cells in metabolic tissues. GPCRs are central to these processes, particularly chemokine receptors (which guide leukocyte trafficking) and purinergic GPCRs (which respond to nucleotides and nucleosides in the inflamed milieu):

Chemokine Receptors (CCR2, CCR5, CXCRs): In obesity and NAFLD, elevated chemokines (MCP-1/CCL2, RANTES/CCL5, CXCL1, etc.) drive monocytes and other leukocytes into adipose tissue and liver. CCR2 on monocytes/macrophages binds MCP-1 and is notorious for its role in obesity-induced inflammation [ ]. Mice lacking CCR2 have less macrophage accumulation in fat and are protected from insulin resistance. Similarly, CCR5 deficiency (receptor for CCL5) reduces liver fibrosis in NASH by altering macrophage polarization [ ]. These receptors are Gi-coupled beyond recruitment, when macrophages engage CCR2/CCR5 in tissues, their cAMP stays low, which as discussed favors inflammasome activation. So chemokine signaling not only brings in more inflammasome “players” but also primes them for pyroptosis by the Gi effect. Dual CCR2/CCR5 antagonists (like cenicriviroc) have shown promise in clinical trials for NASH fibrosis, presumably by interrupting this inflammatory cascade [ , ]. Reducing monocyte infiltration means fewer cells to undergo pyroptosis; plus, the macrophages present may be less activated. Another chemokine axis is CXCL8 (IL-8) and its receptors CXCR1/2 on neutrophils relevant in atherosclerosis and possibly diabetic neutrophil dysfunction. CXCR2 drives neutrophil influx, which can release inflammasome-activating signals (like proteases that generate IL-1). CXCR2 antagonists are being considered to reduce neutrophil-driven tissue damage in ischemic injuries, which could apply to metabolic ischemia (like diabetic PAD). However, caution is needed as impairing host defense is a risk.
Purinergic GPCRs (Adenosine receptors P1, P2Y receptors): Adenosine A2A receptors (Gs-coupled, anti-inflammatory) and adenosine A1 receptors (Gi-coupled, potentially pro-inflammatory in immune cells) have already been introduced. During metabolic stress, extracellular ATP and ADP levels may increase, released from dying cells or hypoxic tissues. While ATP mainly acts on P2X7 (ion channel) to induce pyroptosis, its breakdown product adenosine accumulates and signals through P1 GPCRs (A1, A2A, A2B, A3). A2B receptors (Gs and Gq coupling) are interesting in metabolic contexts: they are upregulated in hypoxia and inflammation (including in adipose tissue of obese mice). A2B activation on macrophages can increase IL-10 and decrease TNF, similar to A2A, but A2B on other cells (like in gut or lung) can promote fibrosis if overactivated [ , ]. In obesity, A2B knockout mice were leaner and more insulin sensitive, suggesting a complex role. A possibility is that A2B on adipocytes or endothelium has metabolic effects that overshadow immune ones. Meanwhile, A3 (Gi) is generally pro-inflammatory on neutrophils but has some anti-inflammatory effects in other cells (A3 agonists are tested for arthritis). The purinergic landscape is thus cell-type specific. Nonetheless, boosting the adenosine tone (e.g., using dipyridamole to inhibit adenosine uptake, or low-dose methotrexate which increases extracellular adenosine) has been shown to have systemic anti-inflammatory effects, some of which likely come from dampening inflammasomes in chronic disease. These approaches have been tried in cardiovascular inflammation (the CIRT trial with methotrexate, which failed to show benefit in part because dose was too low to elevate adenosine enough).

The P2Y family of nucleotide GPCRs also deserves mention. P2Y2 and P2Y6 (Gq) are expressed on macrophages; P2Y2 senses ATP/UTP and can induce NLRP3 via Ca2+ mobilization; P2Y6 senses UDP and has been implicated in IL-1β production in some models. P2Y12, famous on platelets (target of clopidogrel), is also on microglia; P2Y12 deletion in macrophages was reported to reduce IL-1β in a gout model, possibly by affecting phagocytosis of crystals and subsequent inflammasome activation. Given that P2Y12 inhibitors (clopidogrel, ticagrelor) are widely used in metabolic patients with CVD, it’s interesting to speculate they might have a slight ancillary effect on inflammation (indeed ticagrelor has some anti-inflammatory effects beyond platelet inhibition) [ , ].
In chronic metabolic inflammation, blocking chemokine GPCRs (to reduce immune cell influx) and modulating purinergic GPCRs (to tilt towards an anti-inflammatory state) are two complementary strategies. The former is like cutting off reinforcements to the battleground, and the latter is disarming the ones already there by inducing a more anti-inflammatory phenotype.
Translating the above insights into therapies, a number of GPCR-targeted pharmacologics either in use or in development aim to reduce pyroptosis and inflammation in metabolic disease:

GLP-1 and GIP Analogues: As discussed, GLP-1R agonists (liraglutide, semaglutide, etc.) and the dual agonist tirzepatide not only improve metabolism but also exhibit anti-inflammatory properties. Liraglutide and exenatide were shown to inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis in diabetic rodent models of NAFLD and cardiomyopathy [ , ]. Clinically, GLP-1 agonists reduce circulating CRP and might lower IL-1 levels. These drugs are now being tested in NASH clinical trials, with some success in reducing liver inflammation and steatosis. Their mechanism likely includes less lipotoxicity (so less priming of inflammasomes) and direct GPCR signaling in liver immune cells to suppress inflammasome (through cAMP/PKA/Nrf2 as noted).
CCR2/CCR5 Antagonists: Cenicriviroc (dual CCR2/5 blocker) in a phase II NASH trial (CENTAUR) reduced liver fibrosis and inflammation over one year, particularly in patients with high inflammatory drive. While cenicriviroc didn’t ultimately get approved (phase III results pending or not meeting certain endpoints), it demonstrated the principle that modulating chemokine GPCRs can alter the pyroptotic inflammatory milieu in metabolic disease. Another CCR2 antagonist, CCX872, showed reduced liver macrophage content and fibrosis in a NASH mouse study [ ]. These drugs could reduce pyroptosis simply by fewer monocytes in tissue (less substrate for inflammasome) and by altering macrophage polarization (CCR2 blockade pushes macrophages to a more reparative phenotype with less IL-1β).
A2A Adenosine Agonists: Regadenoson, a selective A2A agonist, is FDA-approved for cardiac stress testing. It causes systemic vasodilation and immunosuppression (A2A on various immune cells). There’s interest in using low-dose regadenoson or other A2A agonists for inflammatory diseases. Preclinical studies in atherosclerosis models show that A2A activation limits lesion inflammation and promotes an M2 macrophage state. An A2A agonist could theoretically be repurposed in conditions like diabetic nephropathy to reduce inflammasome-driven damage in kidneys (since A2A on mesangial cells and macrophages there would raise cAMP). However, side effects (hypotension, tachycardia) are concerns [ , ].
β-adrenergic Agents: While chronic β-agonist use is not desirable systemically, interestingly mirabegron (a β3-adrenergic agonist used for overactive bladder) was found to stimulate brown fat and improve glucose metabolism in diabetics. β3 in adipose also leads to release of anti-inflammatory adipokines. Some studies noted reduced macrophage infiltration in adipose with β3 activation. Perhaps β3 agonists (which mostly act on fat and less on heart) could reduce adipose pyroptosis indirectly by creating a metabolically healthier, less hypoxic environment in fat [ , ].
Angiotensin and Adrenergic Blockers: Metabolic syndrome often involves overactive renin-angiotensin system. Angiotensin II via AT1 receptors (Gq coupled) induces ROS production in vascular cells and macrophages, which can prime inflammasomes. AT1 blockers (losartan, telmisartan) not only reduce blood pressure but also show anti-inflammatory effects in vessels and kidney partly by reducing NADPH oxidase and NF-κB activity. There is evidence that losartan can reduce NLRP3 inflammasome markers in diabetic kidneys, improving function. Similarly, β1-adrenergic blockers (like metoprolol) reduce the sympathetic drive that could otherwise modulate immune cells (there are β-adrenergic receptors on immune cells too) [ – ]. One example: in a mouse model of myocardial infarction, metoprolol was shown to reduce inflammasome activation in cardiac macrophages and limit the infarct size (metoprolol is thought to alter monocyte behavior via β1, though β1 on macrophages is not classic possibly an indirect effect through hemodynamics and less NE).
Biased GPCR Ligands: This is a cutting-edge approach where ligands are designed to selectively activate only beneficial pathways downstream of a GPCR. For instance, TRV027 is an AT1 receptor biased ligand that blocks Gq (the harmful vasoconstrictor pathway) but allows β-arrestin signaling (which has cardioprotective effects). In theory, a biased CCR2 ligand might block Gi (chemotaxis/inflammation) but stimulate β-arrestin that induces an IL-10 response. This hasn’t been achieved yet but is conceptually possible [ , ].

To sum up, targeting GPCRs to mitigate pyroptosis is a multifaceted strategy: one can reduce the triggers (infiltrating cells via chemokine receptors), shift the intracellular signaling (cAMP via adenosine, GLP-1, etc.), or alter the tissue environment (via hormonal GPCRs like angiotensin and adrenergic systems). Many of these GPCR drugs are already in use for other indications, presenting opportunities for drug repurposing.  Importantly, mitochondrial-centered regulation of ferroptosis and pyroptosis exhibits disease-specific phenotypic consequences. In type 2 diabetes, mitochondrial ferroptosis in pancreatic β-cells contributes to progressive insulin insufficiency, while pyroptotic signaling in islet-associated macrophages exacerbates local inflammation. In NAFLD/NASH, hepatocyte mitochondrial dysfunction promotes ferroptotic cell loss, which subsequently fuels Kupffer cell pyroptosis and fibrogenic signaling. Similarly, in atherosclerosis, mitochondrial oxidative stress within plaque macrophages enhances inflammasome activation and pyroptotic necrotic core expansion, accelerating plaque instability [ – ].

## Integrated ion channel-gpcr networks governing ferroptosis-pyroptosis crosstalk
Biological systems rarely operate in isolation. Ion channels and GPCRs do not function as silos; rather, they form an integrated network of membrane signaling that ultimately determines a cell’s fate under stress. This section synthesizes how ion fluxes and GPCR signaling intersect to regulate the balance of ferroptosis and pyroptosis, especially in metabolic disease contexts where both forms of cell death may co-occur. At the cell membrane, ion channels and GPCRs often reside in close proximity and can influence each other’s activity. For example, GPCRs can modulate ion channel function via second messengers, and conversely, ion fluxes can modulate GPCR signaling (through membrane potential changes or ionic co-factors). A few illustrative points of crosstalk (Fig.  ):
 
GPCRs Modulating Ion Channels: A classic example is GPCR regulation of K+ and Ca2+ channels. Gs-coupled GPCRs increase cAMP, which via PKA can phosphorylate and alter the gating of certain ion channels. In immune cells, PKA phosphorylation of pannexin-1 has been shown to reduce its opening, thus limiting ATP release and subsequent P2 X7 activation [ , ]. G_i-coupled GPCRs, by reducing cAMP, may leave pannexin or P2X7 in a more permissive state for opening during stress. Also, GPCRs like adenosine A2A can activate KATP channels in some cells (hyperpolarizing the membrane), which might indirectly reduce Ca2+ influx and inflammasome activation. In pancreatic β-cells, GLP-1R (Gs) influences voltage-gated Ca2+ channels via PKA, enhancing insulin secretion; simultaneously, by boosting antioxidant defenses, it may protect those β-cells from Ca2+-induced ferroptosis. Thus, one GPCR signal (GLP-1) coordinates both metabolism (insulin release via Ca2+) and survival (through antioxidant/anti-ferroptosis pathways).
Ion Channels Modulating GPCR Signaling: Ion fluxes can feed back on GPCR pathways too. Membrane depolarization (from K+ efflux or Na+ influx) can activate voltage-sensitive enzymes (like adenylyl cyclase isoform AC5/6 which are inhibited by Ca2+). If ferroptosis-related Na+ influx and depolarization occur, it might reduce cAMP production, indirectly tilting cells toward more inflammasome activity a vicious cycle. Additionally, ions like Ca2+ can directly interact with GPCR kinases or β-arrestin, affecting GPCR desensitization. There is evidence that during sustained P2X7 activation (with Ca2+ influx), GPCRs such as P2Y and others can become desensitized due to high intracellular Ca2+ interfering with their recycling [ , ].
Redox and Second Messenger Convergence: Many GPCR pathways converge on transcription factors like NF-κB and Nrf2. Ion fluxes (especially K+ efflux and Ca2+ influx during pyroptosis) also activate NF-κB and MAPKs as part of the stress response. For instance, K+ efflux triggers the MAPK p38 in macrophages, which then contributes to inflammasome priming. Meanwhile, GPCRs like A2A or GLP-1R can inhibit p38 via cAMP/PKA or activate AMP-kinase (AMPK) which in turn inhibits NF-κB. So there’s a convergence where ion-channel signals and GPCR signals either synergize or antagonize at the level of key signaling nodes. In metabolic inflammation, this means the outcome (pro-death vs. pro-survival) depends on the integration of all these signals [ , ].
Node of NEK7: NEK7 is the kinase that physically interacts with NLRP3 in low-K+ conditions to allow inflammasome assembly [ ]. Interestingly, NEK7’s activity could be influenced by phosphorylation from other pathways. GPCR signals that activate kinases (PKA, PKC, etc.) might modify NEK7 or NLRP3 post-translationally, altering the need for K+ efflux. It has been reported that NEK7-deficient cells require more intense stimulation for NLRP3 activation, implying NEK7 is a point where multiple signals converge. Ion flux gives NEK7 access to NLRP3; concurrently, a GPCR like EP4 causing PKA activation could phosphorylate either NEK7 or NLRP3, impeding their interaction. So the cross-talk might literally occur on the inflammasome complex assembly [ , ].
Metabolic Node of mTOR: mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin) is a nutrient sensor often upregulated in overnutrition. mTOR signaling inhibits autophagy, which is a process that can clear damaged mitochondria and iron, thus preventing ferroptosis and inflammasome activation. Ion channels (like TRPM2 Ca2+ influx) can activate mTOR via Ca2+-calmodulin pathways; GPCRs (like GPR40 in β-cells or FFARs) can also activate mTOR via PI3K/Akt. Chronic mTOR activation can be double-edged on one hand, it supports cell growth and antioxidant production; on the other, it prevents autophagic clearance of inflammasome-activating debris. Thus, integrated signals determine mTOR’s net effect on cell death susceptibility. Caloric restriction or AMPK activation (via GPCRs like adiponectin receptors, which are GPCR-like) inhibits mTOR, which in some cases reduces inflammasome activation by promoting mitophagy. This is a higher-order integration of metabolic state with cell death regulation, where GPCR hormonal signals (leptin, adiponectin) and nutrient-driven ion flux (like amino acid transporter associated currents) together modulate pyroptosis/ferroptosis propensity [ , ].

The conceptual novelty here is to appreciate the cell as a system where membrane signals serve as hubs integrating diverse inputs metabolic cues, danger signals, mechanical stress and channel them into unified downstream decisions: whether to initiate cell death, and if so, by which mechanism. Ion channels (like P2X7, TRP, Piezo1) and GPCRs (like CCR2, A2A, GLP-1R) physically and functionally interact at the membrane and coordinate the crosstalk between ferroptosis and pyroptosis. For instance, consider a fatty liver under lipotoxic stress: free fatty acids can activate GPCRs on Kupffer cells causing some initial inflammation; dying hepatocytes release ATP which activates P2X7 on those Kupffer cells, causing pyroptosis; the pyroptotic Kupffer cells release IL-1β which signals through IL-1R (a GPCR-like ILR family receptor) on hepatocytes and stellate cells, leading to more oxidative stress and even ferroptosis in hepatocytes and fibrogenesis in stellate cells. Meanwhile, if the person is treated with a GLP-1 agonist, the GLP-1R on hepatocytes and macrophages raises cAMP, making hepatocytes more resistant to ferroptosis and macrophages less prone to pyroptosis [ , ]. This web of interactions is complex but can be deciphered into targeted intervention points.
The integrated approach implies that combination therapies might be necessary for example, blocking a deleterious ion channel (like P2X7) and activating a protective GPCR (like A2A or GLP-1R) could have synergistic effects in reducing tissue damage. This is because one addresses the immediate trigger (ion flux for inflammasome) and the other fortifies the cellular environment (cAMP/Nrf2 for ferroptosis resistance). In conclusion of this section, understanding the integrated network of ion channels and GPCRs provides a framework to identify key “hubs” or nodal points for therapeutic intervention. In metabolic diseases, two such hubs emerge: (1) the membrane ionic environment (K+, Ca2+, etc.) which can be pharmacologically modulated to avoid excessive inflammasome activation and membrane damage, and (2) the GPCR-governed intracellular second messenger milieu (cAMP, DAG/Ca2+, β-arrestin pathways) which can be steered towards cytoprotection. By targeting these hubs, the vicious cycle of ferroptosis–pyroptosis that drives metabolic tissue injury may potentially be uncoupled.

## Pharmacological targeting strategies in metabolic diseases
Building on the mechanistic insights above, this section outlines concrete strategies to pharmacologically target ion channels and GPCRs in order to mitigate ferroptosis and pyroptosis in metabolic diseases. Approaches span traditional small-molecule drugs, newer biologics/peptides, and even nutraceuticals and lifestyle interventions, reflecting a broad arsenal to modulate these pathways (Fig. ).
 

## Small-molecule modulators
Small-molecule drugs have the advantages of cell permeability, oral availability, and often broad tissue distribution useful for systemic metabolic diseases. Key categories include:

Ion Channel Blockers/Modulators: These have been touched on in Sects. 3 and 4. For metabolic disease, the most relevant are probably P2X7 antagonists, which are small heterocyclic compounds. Example: AZD9056 (an oral P2X7 blocker) was safe in humans and although it failed in rheumatoid arthritis, its mechanism (reducing IL-1β) could be repurposed for T2DM or NASH, where IL-1β plays a role. Another is Dapagliflozin (an SGLT2 inhibitor for diabetes) interestingly, beyond glycemic control, dapagliflozin was shown to reduce NLRP3 inflammasome activation in diabetic hearts [ , ]. This effect might be indirect (via improved mitochondrial function and ketone utilization) or direct (some SGLT2 in macrophages? not confirmed). It highlights that known metabolic drugs can have “dual action.” Calcium channel blockers (e.g., amlodipine) widely used for hypertension in diabetics could theoretically reduce Ca2+-triggered NLRP3 activation in kidneys or vasculature, though this is a secondary benefit at best. Sodium channel blockers (like ranolazine for angina) have been noted to reduce NLRP3 in experimental settings possibly by stabilizing cell membranes against depolarization.
Incretin mimetics (GLP-1, GIP agonists): These are peptides (see 8.2), but small-molecule agonists for GLP-1R are under investigation. A non-peptide GLP-1R agonist would be a game-changer for oral therapy. None are approved yet, but some compounds have been identified with activity. If developed, they might become part of combination pills for diabetes that also address inflammation.
DPP-4 Inhibitors: These are small molecules (sitagliptin, linagliptin) that raise endogenous GLP-1/GIP by preventing their breakdown. There is evidence DPP-4 inhibitors reduce NLRP3 inflammasome markers and improve vascular inflammation in diabetes [ ]. Possibly due to higher GLP-1, but also they inhibit the enzyme DPP-4 which itself can act as an adipokine promoting inflammation.
Chemokine Receptor Antagonists: Most are small molecules (except some antibodies). CCR2 and CCR5 antagonists (like cenicriviroc actually a small molecule combination) and pure CCR2 blockers (CCX140, PF-04634817) have been trialed in diabetic nephropathy and NASH. PF-04634817 lowered urinary IL-1β in diabetic patients (suggesting less inflammasome activity). None are yet approved for metabolic indications, but the concept is alive [ , ].
Adenosine Receptor Agents: Dipyridamole, a decades-old drug, elevates extracellular adenosine by inhibiting uptake. It’s used for stroke prevention, but also reduces inflammation (it’s been shown to ameliorate experimental liver fibrosis by increasing A2A receptor signaling). Perhaps adding dipyridamole to NASH treatment could enhance anti-inflammatory outcomes it’s an example of drug repurposing. Adenosine analogues like adenosine amine congener (AAD) are potent A2A agonists; their drawback is systemic vasodilation. Efforts are ongoing to develop site-targeted adenosine agonists (e.g., incorporating them into nanoparticles that go to inflamed tissue) [ , ].
β3-Adrenergic Agonists: Mirabegron is a small molecule that was recently shown to stimulate brown fat thermogenesis in humans at higher doses than used for OAB. This led to improved glucose metabolism. There’s speculation that chronic β3 activation could reduce liver fat and inflammation (brown fat acting as a sink for fatty acids, plus release of anti-inflammatory cytokines like IL-10 from brown fat). Trials are starting for mirabegron in NAFLD. If it works, pyroptosis might be reduced in liver simply by less lipotoxicity and perhaps some direct cAMP effect in liver macrophages (though β3 in liver is minimal, but could act in adipose and indirectly benefit liver) [ , , ].
Sigma-1 Receptor Agonists (not a GPCR but chaperone): There is interplay with ER stress and inflammasomes. Arguably out of scope, but drugs like ardeparin (antidepressant fluvoxamine’s off-target) activate Sigma-1 and reduce inflammation and have shown benefit in COVID cytokine storm, potentially could apply to metabolic cytokine storms (e.g., extreme NASH flares) [ ].
Ferroptosis Inhibitors: Ferrostatin-1, liproxstatin-1 are tool compounds not drugs. But vitamin E (α-tocopherol) is a small molecule antioxidant that specifically breaks lipid peroxidation chain reactions. In the TONIC and PIVENS trials for NASH, high-dose vitamin E improved NASH histology in non-diabetic patients [ ]. This is likely through reducing ferroptosis and other oxidative stress-induced cell death in hepatocytes. Concerns exist about long-term high-dose vitamin E (slight increase in mortality in some analyses, possibly from hemorrhagic stroke risk or cancer risk), but for NASH the benefits were deemed to outweigh risks in certain subgroups. Iron chelators like deferasirox (oral) could be repurposed for NAFLD with high ferritin; small studies show liver enzymes improvement. In T2DM with high ferritin, repeated phlebotomy improved insulin sensitivity indicating iron reduction benefits metabolic state, probably by reducing ferroptotic damage in muscle and β-cells.
NLRP3 Inflammasome Inhibitors: Aside from MCC950 (in preclinical), some companies have developed analogues entering trials (e.g., Inzomelid by Inflazome, now Roche). Also, OLT1177 (Dapansutrile) is an orally active beta-sulfonyl nitrile that inhibits NLRP3, it’s in trials for gout and heart failure. A metabolic disease trial would be logical, given the mechanism. These are not ion channel or GPCR modulators, but directly hit the convergence point of those signals. They illustrate an alternative: rather than tweak upstream signals, just block the final common pathway (inflammasome) though one loses some specificity and might risk immunosuppression [ – ].

Combining small molecules may yield additive effects. For instance, a statin + SGLT2 inhibitor + GLP-1 agonist regimen in a high-risk diabetic patient could address dyslipidemia, hyperglycemia, and provide anti-inflammatory/antioxidant benefits (statins reduce oxLDL and maybe NLRP3 priming; SGLT2i reduce renal and cardiac inflammasome activation; GLP-1 receptor agonists are known to reduce susceptibility to both pyroptosis and ferroptosis) [ , ]. Indeed, outcome trials (like EMPA-REG, LEADER) show these combinations dramatically lower cardiovascular events and may slow kidney disease likely because they are collectively reducing chronic inflammatory cell death in vessels and organs.

## Biologics and peptides
Biologic therapies (antibodies, recombinant cytokines, peptide hormones) offer high specificity and often target extracellular mediators, which can be very effective in interrupting inflammatory cascades:

GLP-1 and GIP analogs: These are peptide drugs (e.g., exenatide, liraglutide, semaglutide for GLP-1; tirzepatide for dual GLP-1/GIP). They require injection but some are long-acting weekly. The role of GLP-1R agonism has been extensively discussed; to reiterate, liraglutide has been shown to reduce hepatocyte and cardiomyocyte pyroptosis in diabetic models, while exenatide attenuates NASH-associated inflammation by inhibiting pyroptotic pathways [ , ]. These peptides are already in use for T2DM and obesity, and clinical trials are expanding to NASH (with promising results for semaglutide). So they represent a prime example where a hormone analog fixes metabolic parameters and beneficially modulates cell death pathways. GIP analogs alone aren’t in use (native GIP is less efficacious for glucose control, and some evidence suggests in obesity GIP might be pro-inflammatory). However, in combination with GLP-1 (tirzepatide) it seems overall anti-inflammatory and weight-reducing. Glucagon analogs (another peptide) combined with GLP-1 are in development for fatty liver glucagon activates fat oxidation in liver, which could reduce surplus substrates for ferroptosis.
Anti-IL-1 and Related Cytokine Therapies: Anakinra (IL-1 receptor antagonist), Canakinumab (anti-IL-1β antibody) these biologics directly neutralize the major output of pyroptosis. They don’t stop pyroptosis from happening in cells, but they prevent the IL-1β signaling that leads to further inflammation and insulin resistance. Indeed, in T2DM, anakinra improved glycemic control modestly and reduced CRP levels. Canakinumab in the CANTOS trial reduced cardiovascular events in post-MI patients and intriguingly patients who got canakinumab had lower incidence of new-onset T2DM (likely because IL-1 drives insulin resistance) [ , ]. Despite high cost and infection risk, there is serious consideration of these drugs for subgroups of NASH or high-risk diabetics with inflammation. There’s also anti-IL-18 (GSK1070806) being studied IL-18 is the other pyroptosis cytokine, implicated in atherosclerosis and NAFLD fibrosis.
TNF and IL-6 Inhibitors: Though TNF and IL-6 are not directly pyroptosis outputs, they’re part of the inflammatory network. Anti-TNF biologics (like in rheumatoid arthritis) improve insulin sensitivity in RA patients, but in straightforward T2DM trials they didn’t do much possibly because IL-1β is more important in metabolic inflammation than TNF. IL-6 is interesting: it can be pro or anti-inflammatory. IL-6 blockade (tocilizumab) improves glycemia in type 1 diabetes and might have benefit in atherosclerosis, but it also could reduce an IL-6 mediated anti-inflammatory feedback (IL-6 helps induce IL-1ra and IL-10). So targeting upstream cytokines is tricky [ , ].
Biologics targeting chemokines or adhesion: e.g., anti-CCL2 antibodies or CCR2-antibodies could achieve similar to small-molecule CCR2 inhibitors, potentially with more specificity. Anti-ICAM/VCAM antibodies could reduce immune cell entry into metabolic organs, albeit systemically blocking those might cause infection risk or impaired immune surveillance [ ].
Antibodies enhancing efferocytosis: This is novel pyroptotic and ferroptotic cells need to be cleared by phagocytes to resolve inflammation. Therapies that boost efferocytosis (like anti-CD47 “don’t eat me” signal blockers) are being tested in atherosclerosis, where they help macrophages clear dead cells in plaques, potentially reducing necrotic cores. Early clearance of ferroptotic cells prior to secondary necrosis may limit DAMP release, although anti-CD47–based strategies are associated with anemia resulting from unintended red blood cell opsonization [ , ].
Peptides targeting ion channels: Not many in clinic; though interestingly, FX06 (a fibrin-derived peptide) can block pannexin-1 channels. It was tested in Ebola for vascular leak. Possibly such peptides could be delivered to dampen pyroptosis-associated secondary ATP release. But that’s speculative.
Interleukin-22 (IL-22): A cytokine (rather than an antibody) IL-22 is being tested for NASH because it helps hepatocytes regenerate and resist oxidative stress. IL-22 signaling upregulates anti-oxidant proteins and could reduce ferroptosis in hepatocytes. In pyroptosis, IL-22 also tilts macrophages to a less inflammatory state via STAT3. This “regenerative” cytokine therapy is still experimental but is a biologic approach to push the system towards resolution and away from cell death [ , ].
Vaccines: Perhaps an unconventional mention one could theoretically vaccinate against certain cytokines or mediators if chronic elevation (like an IL-1β vaccine none exists yet, or an IL-1α/β trap molecule). Given metabolic diseases are chronic, a long-term therapy like a vaccine might be thought of, but none is near reality.

## Nutraceuticals and natural products
Diet and supplements can profoundly affect systemic redox state and inflammation. In metabolic syndrome, where diet often contributes to pathology, nutraceutical interventions can be both preventive and therapeutic:

Omega-3 Fatty Acids: Fish oil (EPA/DHA) is arguably the best studied. As noted, omega-3s activate GPR120, leading to anti-inflammatory effects. They also get incorporated into membranes, making them more susceptible to peroxidation which sounds bad for ferroptosis, but EPA/DHA also are substrates for resolvin synthesis that actively resolve inflammation. Clinically, high-dose EPA (4 g/day icosapent ethyl) in the REDUCE-IT trial reduced cardiovascular events by 25% in high-risk patients, and reduced CRP modestly. How? Possibly by stabilizing plaques (making macrophages more prone to efferocytosis and less inflammasome-active) and by lowering triglycerides (less lipotoxicity). Omega-3 supplements in NASH trials have shown mixed results they improve liver fat but not necessarily inflammation strongly, perhaps dose or subject selection issues. But likely, sufficient omega-3 intake (or a diet like Mediterranean diet rich in omega-3) reduces overall pyroptosis in adipose and vessels by shifting macrophages to an M2 phenotype and providing mild antioxidant effect (via upregulating Nrf2 target genes) [ , ].
Antioxidant Supplements: Vitamin E we discussed. Others: Vitamin C (recycles vitamin E, and directly scavenges ROS, though not lipid radicals in membrane). Trials of vitamin C in diabetes complications show some benefit in endothelial function but no large hard outcomes proven. N-acetylcysteine (NAC) supplies cysteine for glutathione synthesis it’s a logical ferroptosis inhibitor booster. In NAFLD, small trials of NAC with vitamin E had additive effect on liver enzymes and histology. NAC may also inhibit NLRP3 by reducing ROS. Polyphenols (e.g., resveratrol, curcumin, quercetin): These often activate Nrf2 and are anti-inflammatory. Resveratrol in rodents reduces NLRP3 and liver ferroptosis; in humans, data is limited and bioavailability issues. Curcumin from turmeric is a mild Nrf2 activator and NF-κB inhibitor; it showed some ALT reduction in NASH patients but need more evidence. Ginseng/Ginsenosides (Rg5 etc.) have been noted to inhibit NLRP3 in diabetic mice and reduce kidney injury. Traditional Chinese medicine often has herbs that target these pathways as per preclinical studies (as Li et al. reviewed). For example, berberine (from Coptis chinensis) activates AMPK and Nrf2, improves insulin sensitivity, and in some studies reduced hepatic NLRP3 and lipid peroxides. Sulforaphane (from broccoli sprouts) is a potent Nrf2 inducer; in diabetic models it prevented retinopathy by Nrf2 activation and NLRP3 inhibition [ , ]. A trial of sulforaphane-rich broccoli sprout extract in T2DM showed reduced oxidative stress and improved glucose control modestly.
Minerals: Selenium is essential for GPX4. Marginal selenium deficiency could promote ferroptosis. Supplementation should be careful (selenium has a U-shaped benefit curve), but ensuring adequate intake is likely protective (some parts of world with low selenium in soil see higher T2DM complications). Magnesium low Mg is pro-inflammatory; Mg supplement improves insulin sensitivity and might reduce CRP. Mechanistically, magnesium deficiency can cause endothelial cells to produce more ROS and NLRP3 activation; thus, repletion might calm things [ , ].
Fiber and Short-Chain Fatty Acids: A high-fiber diet increases production of SCFAs like butyrate in the gut. Butyrate, as noted, reduces kidney pyroptosis [ ] and likely adipose inflammation by promoting Treg cells (which secrete IL-10). A healthy gut microbiome that produces SCFAs and secondary bile acids (activating TGR5) can be considered an endogenous brake on inflammasomes. Therefore, prebiotics and probiotics that enhance SCFA production might indirectly protect from pyroptosis and ferroptosis triggered by endotoxins or diet. Some studies show probiotic supplements reduce LPS levels and liver inflammation in NAFLD meaning less caspase-11 activation perhaps.
Caloric Restriction and Ketogenic Diet: Fasting and CR elevate ketones (β-hydroxybutyrate) which is an HDAC inhibitor and NLRP3 suppressor. BHB directly inhibits NLRP3 via preventing Asc speck formation according to one study. Ketogenic diets have lots of fat (which might cause other issues) but produce BHB they’ve been seen to reduce inflammation in some contexts (but high fat can also activate inflammasomes, so it’s context-specific). Intermittent fasting appears to reduce inflammasome markers in human adipose tissue, likely through weight loss but also metabolic reprogramming [ , ].
Functional Foods: This concept ties in e.g., olive oil (rich in oleocanthal, which has ibuprofen-like anti-inflammatory effect, plus MUFAs which are less pro-ferroptotic than PUFAs), green tea (EGCG polyphenol, an Nrf2 activator and iron chelator), coffee (contains cafestol, etc., associated with lower NAFLD incidence, perhaps via adiponectin increase and Nrf2 activation), nuts (vitamin E and arginine content, arginine -> more NO which at moderate levels can signal anti-inflammatory but at high can cause RNS; overall nut consumption is anti-inflammatory in epidemiology).

In essence, nutritional therapy represents an accessible means of modulating the same pathways targeted by pharmacological interventions. It often has fewer side effects, but may be milder in effect and compliance is a challenge. Precision nutrition tailoring diets to individual risk (e.g., high-iron stores? adopt a low-red-meat diet; high inflammation? ensure ample omega-3 and antioxidants) could complement pharmacotherapy. For example, a patient on an NLRP3 inhibitor might benefit from concurrent vitamin E to handle any alternative oxidant paths, or a patient on a GLP-1 agonist might maximize benefit with a diet that provides plenty of Nrf2-activating veggies (to further reduce ferroptosis).
To close out this section: the battle against metabolic disease is moving beyond just controlling glucose or lipids it’s now about preserving cellular health by preventing undue cell death. Ion channels and GPCRs are at the forefront of this effort, with a therapeutic toolkit ranging from metformin to dietary interventions such as broccoli sprouts. The next sections will consider how to bring these strategies into clinical reality (translation, patient stratification) and what challenges remain (like ensuring specificity, avoiding immune suppression, etc.).

## Translational and clinical implications
Translating the above strategies into clinical practice requires careful consideration of patient selection, biomarkers to monitor efficacy, and combination with standard therapies:

Drug Repurposing Opportunities: As noted, many approved drugs (for diabetes, hypertension, etc.) have unexploited benefits on ferroptosis/pyroptosis pathways. Clinicians may start leveraging this knowledge by preferentially using drugs with dual benefits. For example, in a T2DM patient with NAFLD, choosing an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 agonist over other glucose-lowering drugs not only improves diabetes but also likely reduces liver inflammation and cell death [ ]. In patients with advanced atherosclerosis, adding low-dose colchicine (anti-microtubule, reduces NLRP3 assembly) is already being done after the COLCOT trial (where colchicine reduced MI recurrence). Similarly, post-hoc analysis of the CANTOS trial (canakinumab) intriguingly showed reduced incident diabetes [ , ]. So rheumatologists and cardiologists are informing endocrinologists that targeting inflammation yields metabolic perks. It’s foreseeable that in the future a cardiometabolic clinic might routinely check inflammatory biomarkers (like high-sensitivity CRP, IL-18, or even novel ones like a caspase-1 activity assay) and adjust therapies accordingly. If a diabetic patient has elevated CRP despite good glucose control, that might prompt adding an anti-inflammatory agent (like icosapent ethyl fish oil or colchicine) to quell underlying pyroptotic inflammation.
For pyroptosis: circulating IL-1β and IL-18 are direct readouts, but IL-1β is often low-level and IL-18 can come from other sources (NK cells). A better marker might be the presence of GSDMD fragments in blood (as cells lyse, the N-terminal fragment could be detectable). Indeed, one can measure GSDMD-N in tissue samples by western blot to confirm pyroptosis; translating that to a blood ELISA would help monitor diseases like Still’s or MAS, and possibly metabolic disease severity. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is a crude marker of cell lysis; in NASH trials, LDH changes could reflect less hepatocyte pyroptosis or ferroptosis. For ferroptosis: malondialdehyde (MDA) or lipid peroxidation products in blood or urine (like isoprostanes) might indicate systemic ferroptotic activity [ , ]. There’s also a specific plasma marker called 4-HNE (4-hydroxynonenal) that correlates with lipid peroxidation levels. In diabetes, elevated 4-HNE and MDA are often seen. If a therapy is effective, these markers would be expected to decrease. Another interesting marker: acyl-carnitine profiles in situations of ferroptosis, cell membranes yield specific oxidized phospholipids that might be reflected in unusual acyl-carnitines in blood (some metabolomics studies can identify a “ferroptosis signature”). For pyroptosis, serum amyloid A and ferritin often rise (ferritin is acute phase reactant but also a marker of macrophage activation syndrome which is pyroptosis driven). In fact, hyperferritinemia in metabolic syndrome may partly result from widespread inflammasome activation (macrophages release ferritin when IL-1 high). Some propose a “ferroinflammatory” index combining ferritin and inflammatory cytokines to identify patients with both iron dysregulation and inflammation.
Patient Stratification (Precision Medicine): Not all patients benefit equally from anti-inflammatory or anti-ferroptotic therapies. It will be important to identify those with a high degree of regulated cell death driving their disease. For example, in NASH, patients with elevated IL-1β or NLRP3 in liver biopsy might respond to anakinra or MCC950, whereas others might not need that. In T2DM, perhaps measuring caspase-1 activity in peripheral monocytes (there are fluorogenic assays) could tell us who has “metaflammation.” Those with high baseline inflammasome activity might be good candidates for add-on therapy like colchicine or canakinumab, whereas it might be wasted on those with low inflammation. Genetic markers too: polymorphisms in NLRP3 or GPX4 might predispose to more severe disease; e.g., some NLRP3 gain-of-function SNPs are associated with T2DM complications. If identified, those patients could benefit from early aggressive anti-inflammatory treatment. On the ferroptosis side, patients with genetic hemochromatosis (HFE mutations) often get diabetes due to iron overload destroying β-cells (likely via ferroptosis). These patients should definitely have iron reduction, and they might benefit from vigilant use of antioxidants or GPX4-supporting nutrients [ , ].
Tissue-specific Delivery: One challenge is that systemic inhibition of cell death pathways could impair host defense (e.g., NLRP3 is needed to clear pathogens). To mitigate this, future therapies might aim for tissue targeting. For instance, a liver-directed nanoparticle carrying an NLRP3 siRNA could treat NASH without affecting immunity elsewhere. Or inhaled forms for lung inflammasome diseases, etc. For metabolic disease, targeting visceral adipose might be key since that’s an inflammation hub perhaps by using carriers that home to adipose (some researchers are working on brown-fat targeted drug delivery via certain peptides). GPCR ligands could also be made tissue-selective by coupling them to large molecules that only penetrate certain vascular beds [ , ].
Combination Therapies: It is likely that combining metabolic, anti-inflammatory, and anti-oxidant agents will give the best outcomes. For example, an ideal NASH regimen in 5 years might be: a GLP-1 analog (for weight loss + anti-pyroptosis), a FXR agonist (to improve metabolism and reduce liver fat, though FXR agonists have complex immunometabolic effects), plus a low-dose NLRP3 inhibitor. Already, there’s a trial combining cenicriviroc (CCR2/5 blocker) with a FXR agonist. Similarly, in diabetic kidney disease, one could see SGLT2 inhibitor + MCC950 in a trial to see if it dramatically slows progression by addressing hemodynamics and inflammation simultaneously. Of course, cost and safety multiply with combinations, so careful stepwise addition and monitoring will be needed [ ].
Lifestyle and Surgery: It’s worth noting the most effective “treatment” for NASH and T2DM remission is bariatric surgery, which drastically reduces adipose mass and its inflammation. Bariatric surgery leads to lower IL-1β and CRP, likely because the visceral fat (a hotbed of macrophage pyroptosis) is reduced. So in a sense, that is a mechanical intervention that reduces the source of pyroptosis. Encouraging significant weight loss (via diet or surgery) should always be part of the plan to reduce the burden of these cell death processes. Meanwhile, exercise releases myokines like IL-6 (transiently, which paradoxically lead to an anti-inflammatory state long-term) and improves antioxidant enzyme profiles in tissues, meaning it raises thresholds for ferroptosis [ , ]. So lifestyle interventions remain fundamental, with pharmacotherapy as an adjunct.
Monitoring and Long-Term Safety: Chronic administration of inflammasome inhibitors necessitates careful monitoring for infection risk, although patients with poorly controlled metabolic disease are already prone to infections; therefore, the net clinical benefit must be carefully weighed. Also, completely blocking ferroptosis might have downsides e.g., ferroptosis is thought to suppress tumors by killing cells that have dodgy metabolism [ ]. Could long-term ferroptosis inhibition increase cancer risk? Possibly if given indiscriminately. However, using context-limited approaches (like tissue targeting or short-term induction during flares) can mitigate that.
Regulatory and Endpoints: For FDA approval of these novel approaches, showing improvement in hard outcomes is key. Surrogate endpoints (like ALT reduction in NASH or improved insulin sensitivity in T2DM) might not suffice. Trials might need to be large to show reduction in progression to cirrhosis, prevention of MI, etc. The field of cardio-metabolic-renal outcomes will likely incorporate inflammatory markers soon. A future diabetes trial might stratify by CRP and test an anti-IL-1 in those high-CRP diabetics for cardiovascular endpoints. Another aspect is cost-effectiveness: many biologics are expensive (e.g., canakinumab is $200k/year), so identifying those who truly need it (precision medicine) is critical for viable use.

This review provides a conceptual and mechanistic framework that has direct implications for future medical development in metabolic diseases. By positioning ferroptosis and pyroptosis as mitochondria-centered and pharmacologically regulatable processes, this work shifts the therapeutic paradigm from symptomatic metabolic control toward targeted modulation of regulated cell death pathways.
From a drug development perspective, ion channels and GPCRs represent particularly attractive targets due to their accessibility, druggability, and extensive existing pharmacopoeia. Many clinically approved drugs—including calcium channel blockers, GLP-1 receptor agonists, P2X7 antagonists, and repurposed anti-inflammatory agents—already interact with pathways identified in this review, highlighting immediate translational potential. Importantly, this framework supports rational drug repurposing strategies, whereby existing cardiometabolic therapies may be re-evaluated for their capacity to suppress ferroptosis or pyroptosis at the mitochondrial level.
Furthermore, this study underscores mitochondria as a convergence point for ionic flux, redox imbalance, and inflammasome activation, suggesting that future therapeutic approaches may benefit from combination strategies. For example, co-targeting mitochondrial iron handling and surface receptor signaling may provide superior protection against metabolic organ injury compared to single-pathway interventions.
Beyond pharmacotherapy, the insights presented here also inform biomarker development. Mitochondrial lipid peroxidation products, iron-handling proteins, and inflammasome-related molecules may serve as predictive or monitoring biomarkers to stratify patients most likely to benefit from ferroptosis- or pyroptosis-targeted interventions. Collectively, this work lays a mechanistic foundation for next-generation precision medicine approaches aimed at halting metabolic inflammation and tissue degeneration rather than merely correcting downstream metabolic abnormalities.
In summary, the translation of ion channel and GPCR modulators into metabolic disease treatment is already underway in some form (with repurposed drugs in trials and some nutraceutical recommendations), but it will accelerate as our mechanistic understanding grows. The next frontier will be to integrate these therapies into the standard of care in a rational, personalized way hitting the right target, in the right patient, at the right time, to maximally preserve tissue function while minimizing side effects.

## Challenges, knowledge gaps, and future directions
While the potential for targeting ion channels and GPCRs in ferroptosis/pyroptosis is exciting, several challenges and open questions remain:

Selectivity vs. Systemic Effects: Ion channels and GPCRs often have broad expression. For instance, blocking P2X7 will dampen inflammasomes but also T-cell responses to infections (P2X7 helps fight TB by inducing macrophage death that kills the intracellular bacteria). Similarly, activating Nrf2 systemically might promote tumorigenesis if done long-term (since some cancer cells hijack Nrf2 to survive) [ , ]. There is a need for strategies that localize treatments or enable controlled downregulation of pathways rather than complete pathway shutdown. One idea is on-demand therapy: treat with an inflammasome inhibitor during anticipated metabolic “hits” (e.g., postprandial periods for those with huge dietary fat loads or during acute illness to prevent glucose excursions from turning into cytokine storms). But is that practical? Possibly in hospital settings or with an implantable pump. More realistic is developing drugs that are inherently selective, e.g., a modified MCC950 that only accumulates in high ROS environments (something like a prodrug that is activated by ROS, so it naturally targets inflamed sites).
Tissue-Specific Signaling Nuances: A complete understanding of how these pathways differ across tissues is still lacking. For example, pyroptosis in neurons vs. in macrophages both occur but have different consequences (in brain, pyroptosis of a microglia might propagate inflammation causing neurodegeneration; in macrophage in liver, it might fibrogenesis). Ferroptosis is beneficial in cancer therapy (killing cancer cells) but detrimental in neurons (drives ALS/Parkinson’s) [ ]. So a drug that inhibits ferroptosis might help Alzheimer’s but could theoretically blunt a chemotherapy’s effect. In metabolic syndrome, most therapeutic contexts address the patient systemically; however, certain physiological processes may warrant preservation [ , ]. Perhaps combining a targeted cancer therapy with a local cardio-protective ferroptosis inhibitor could prevent chemo-induced cardiac damage without protecting the tumor. These complex scenarios suggest that a “context-aware” therapy, maybe guided by biomarkers or smart drug delivery, is needed.
Combination Therapies and Unknown Interactions: Combining anti-ferroptotic and anti-pyroptotic therapies might have unexpected interactions. Reducing pyroptosis may alter iron handling in macrophages, which could either raise or lower ferroptosis in adjacent cells. Potential feedback loops may exist; for example, NLRP3 blockade could theoretically increase oxidative stress if IL-1β–mediated hepcidin induction—which normally promotes iron sequestration—is diminished. Further systems biology modeling is required to predict the outcomes of multi-target modulation.
Long-term Outcomes and Safety: Chronic inhibition of inflammation can lead to opportunistic infections; chronic antioxidant use can impair some physiological signaling (ROS are also second messengers). For example, high-dose vitamin E in some studies was linked to increased risk of hemorrhagic stroke and prostate cancer mechanistically unclear, but maybe overly suppressing some oxidative processes or affecting vitamin K metabolism. Also, interfering with cell death might allow damaged cells to persist – could that lead to accumulation of dysfunctional cells? It’s known that preventing apoptosis in some contexts can predispose to cancer. For pyroptosis/ferroptosis, which often kill dangerously stressed cells, blocking them might allow those cells to linger and perhaps contribute to cancer or other pathologies [ ].
AI and Drug Discovery: On the horizon, AI-driven modeling could help find new modulators. AI can screen for molecules that bind combined patterns (e.g., a molecule that is both an antioxidant and a P2 × 7 inhibitor). AI could also analyze patient data to identify those with “inflammasome signatures” who will benefit from certain therapies. This intersects with precision medicine mentioned before.
Emerging Targets: There are other forms of regulated cell death like necroptosis (mediated by MLKL) and autophagy-dependent cell death. These interact with pyroptosis/ferroptosis too (e.g., MLKL pores can cause K+ efflux and thus inflammasome activation, a cross-talk between necroptosis and pyroptosis; inhibiting one may shunt cell death to another pathway known as “cell death compensation”). It should be considered that suppression of pyroptosis may result in compensatory activation of necroptosis, and vice versa [ ]. So an integrative approach tackling multiple forms might be needed in some disease stages. Perhaps a “pan-RCD modulator” will be developed that gently modulates all death pathways to keep them in check without completely blocking any single one.
Clinical Trial Design: A challenge is proving in humans that modulating these pathways changes disease trajectory. For instance, an NLRP3 inhibitor might reduce ALT and IL-1 in NASH, but regulators will want to see fibrosis improvement or less progression to cirrhosis. That takes time. Trials might need surrogate endpoints like imaging or multi-omic markers that are accepted by authorities. The field should work on validating biomarkers (like the ones mentioned above) as surrogate endpoints for clinical trials (e.g., change in IL-18 or Ferritin could correlate with future development of diabetes complications, etc.). Also, patient acceptance, if the addition of canakinumab injections is proposed for high-risk diabetes, patient acceptance remains uncertain, Possibly if it clearly reduces heart attacks, but the risk/benefit must be communicated.
Cost and Accessibility: Biologics are expensive. Some small molecules like colchicine or omega-3 or statins are cheap and accessible and can be widely used if evidence supports. But something like a new inflammasome inhibitor might cost a lot, limiting its use to subsets or wealthier healthcare systems. Emphasis on repurposed generics (like colchicine which is cheap, or maybe designing analogs of existing cheap drugs to tweak them for these pathways) is a practical direction.

Future Directions in research should address: (1) Better mapping of ferroptosis and pyroptosis events in human tissues (through advanced imaging and single-cell sequencing of death markers in biopsies). (2) Clinical trials of inflammasome or ferroptosis inhibitors in metabolic disease (some are starting, e.g., colchicine in diabetes, IL-1 antagonists in CKD, etc.). (3) Development of organ-specific modulators (e.g., NLRP3 inhibitors that accumulate in kidney to treat diabetic nephropathy, thereby limiting systemic exposure). (4) Investigating lifestyle synergy: can intense exercise or specific diets replace the need for certain drugs by naturally modulating these pathways? If so, incorporate that into treatment guidelines. (5) Personalized scheduling: maybe patients could cycle drugs to avoid tolerance or side effects, e.g., take an IL-1 blocker only during winter when infection risk is lower, or pulse-dose an antioxidant around periods of known high oxidative stress (like around a surgical procedure or an acute illness that might exacerbate metabolic disease). (6) Safety nets, if the immunosuppress with anti-IL-1, maybe concurrently vaccinate patients against common infections or prophylactic antibiotics in certain situations to mitigate risk.
Despite the mechanistic insights synthesized in this review, several important limitations and unresolved questions must be acknowledged. First, much of the current evidence linking ferroptosis and pyroptosis to metabolic diseases remains derived from in vitro systems and animal models. Direct confirmation of these regulated cell death pathways in human metabolic tissues, particularly through well-characterized patient cohorts, is still limited.
Second, the precise temporal and spatial dynamics of ferroptosis and pyroptosis in metabolic disease progression remain poorly defined. It is unclear whether these pathways act as initiating events or predominantly as downstream amplifiers of tissue injury, and whether they occur sequentially or concurrently within specific cell populations. This uncertainty complicates therapeutic targeting and highlights the need for longitudinal and single-cell resolution studies.
Third, although mitochondria are increasingly recognized as central regulators of both ferroptosis and pyroptosis, direct causal evidence linking specific mitochondrial targets—such as iron transporters, calcium handling proteins, or redox enzymes—to disease outcomes in humans is still lacking. Moreover, the heterogeneity of mitochondrial phenotypes across tissues further limits the generalizability of current findings.
Finally, pharmacological modulation of ion channels and GPCRs carries inherent risks due to their essential physiological roles. Many proposed interventions lack sufficient specificity, raising concerns regarding off-target effects and long-term safety. Robust clinical trials and improved target selectivity will therefore be essential before these strategies can be translated into routine clinical practice.
Addressing these limitations will require integrative approaches combining human multi-omics, advanced imaging of mitochondrial function, and well-designed interventional studies. Such efforts are essential to validate the feasibility of targeting regulated cell death pathways as a therapeutic strategy in metabolic diseases.
In summary, while challenges exist, they are surmountable with careful, innovative strategies. The potential reward preventing amputations, kidney failure, heart attacks, and cirrhosis by tackling cell-death-driven inflammation is enormous. The interplay of ion channels and GPCRs in cell death is a promising therapeutic nexus, but it must be navigated with nuance to avoid tipping the body’s delicate homeostasis too far. Continued research, including translational studies bridging bench and bedside, will illuminate the safest and most effective ways to exploit these insights.

## Conclusions
Metabolic diseases, once viewed purely as disorders of fuel imbalance, are now understood to be diseases of aberrant cell fate and immune activation. Chronic metabolic stress triggers regulated cell death pathways notably ferroptosis and pyroptosis which in turn drive the organ dysfunction and inflammation characteristic of conditions like T2DM, obesity, NAFLD, and atherosclerosis. Ion channels and GPCRs sit at the heart of this process as versatile “control hubs.” They translate metabolic and danger signals into the language of ions and second messengers, thereby governing the tipping point between cellular survival and death.
This review highlighted how specific ion channels (such as Ca2+-permeable TRP channels and the ATP-gated P2 × 7 pore) and GPCRs (such as the GLP-1 receptor and chemokine receptors) intricately regulate ferroptotic and pyroptotic pathways in metabolic tissues. Ion channels modulate the ionic milieu K+ efflux, Ca2+ influx, iron entry that can ignite or suppress these death programs. GPCRs orchestrate intracellular signaling networks (cAMP-PKA, G protein cascades, β-arrestin routes) that converge on oxidative stress responses and inflammasome priming. Importantly, these two classes of proteins do not work in isolation but form an integrated membrane signaling network, making them ideal pharmacological targets.
From a therapeutic standpoint, the field is entering an era in which “cell death modulation” may emerge as a pillar of metabolic disease management, complementing traditional glucose and lipid control. Ion channel blockers (like P2 × 7 antagonists) and GPCR ligands (like incretin mimetics or chemokine inhibitors) are poised to reduce tissue damage by preventing excessive ferroptosis and pyroptosis. Early clinical trials and repurposing studies are encouraging: for instance, GLP-1 analogs not only lower glucose but also quell liver inflammation and β-cell stress; SGLT2 inhibitors improve cardiac outcomes possibly by dampening NLRP3 inflammasome activity in the heart; and anti-IL-1 therapy in diabetics lowered cardiovascular events in a landmark study. These hint that targeting these pathways is both feasible and beneficial.
Nevertheless, achieving the full promise of this approach will require surmounting challenges such as ensuring specificity (to avoid undermining host defenses), identifying patients who will benefit most, and integrating these therapies safely with existing treatments. Ongoing advances in drug delivery, biomarker development, and precision medicine will be critical. On the horizon, combination therapies may emerge in which metabolic disease is addressed not only with conventional “metabolic drugs,” but also with an “inflammasome modulator” and a “ferroptosis protector,” enabling a truly multi-pronged attack on the disease process.
In conclusion, ion channels and GPCRs emerge from the shadows of metabolic regulation to take center stage as pharmacological control hubs of cell death and inflammation. By targeting these nodes, the self-perpetuating cycle of metabolic dysfunction and tissue injury may be intercepted. This paradigm shift opens new therapeutic frontiers, treating metabolic diseases not only by normalizing blood chemistry but by actively modulating the fundamental cell fate decisions that underpin health and disease. The work ahead is to translate these insights into therapies that are effective, safe, and accessible a challenge worth undertaking given the potential to reduce the global burden of metabolic disorders. With continued research and clinical innovation, modulating ferroptosis and pyroptosis via ion channels and GPCRs could herald a new era of disease modification and organ protection in metabolic medicine, ultimately improving outcomes and quality of life for millions of patients worldwide.
