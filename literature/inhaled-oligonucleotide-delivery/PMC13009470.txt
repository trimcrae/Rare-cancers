This study identifies macrophage-derived lactate as a key mediator of fibroblast phenotypic remodeling via monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1)-mediated histone H3 lysine 23 lactylation (H3K23la) in HS. Elevated lactate levels and MCT1 expression were observed in HS tissues, with macrophages in stiff mechanical microenvironments identified as the primary lactate source. Lactate influx through MCT1 upregulated H3K23la, thereby promoting transcriptional activation of profibrotic genes HEY2 and COL11A1. Mechanistically, HEY2 activated YAP1/SMAD2 signaling, while COL11A1 stabilized MCT1 to enhance lactate transport, forming a positive loop that amplified fibrosis. Fibroblast-specific Mct1 deletion or pharmacological inhibition of Mct1 in male mice reduced collagen deposition, accelerated wound healing, and attenuated scar formation. Our findings redefine the macrophage-fibroblast crosstalk in HS and establish the MCT1-H3K23la-HEY2/COL11A1 axis, particularly its self-reinforcing loop, as a novel therapeutic target.

## Introduction
Cutaneous wound repair aims to restore tissue integrity and skin homeostasis through a precisely regulated healing cascade . However, extensive and deeper injuries frequently lead to pathological hypertrophic scar (HS), a fibroproliferative disorder characterized by excessive deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM) components and persistent inflammation. HS represents a pathological outcome of aberrant wound healing , . Approximately 35% linear surgical wounds and up to 72% burn wounds would develop into HS. Accompanied by pruritus, pain, and functional impairment, HS significantly affects patients’ quality of life and psychological well-being. Therefore, a deeper understanding of HS pathogenesis and progression is essential for identifying potential therapeutic targets and biomarkers of HS .
Current pathophysiological models emphasize fibroblast hyperactivation and aberrant ECM synthesis as central to HS development. While transient fibroblast activation is part of normal wound repair, persistent activation results in the transformation of fibroblasts to myofibroblasts and drives HS formation . Thus, targeting activated fibroblasts and modulating their fibrotic behavior is the main strategy for controlling HS formation. Emerging evidence suggests that metabolic reprogramming, from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis during the progression of various organic fibrotic diseases, suggesting metabolic dysregulation as a potential therapeutic target of fibrosis , . Notably, both HS and keloid, pathological outcomes of wound healing, display markedly elevated glycolysis level . Moreover, macrophages are major contributors to metabolic reprogramming in many diseases , , and promote fibroblast differentiation through TGF-β1 secretion during wound healing . Besides, metabolic reprogramming not only generates glycolytic intermediates to support the anabolic requirements of proliferating cells but also inevitably leads to elevated production of metabolic byproducts such as lactate . Beyond its traditional role as metabolic waste, lactate functions as a signaling molecule, an energy substrate, and an immunoregulatory molecule , . Lactate may originate not only from endogenous glycolysis but also originate from other cells via the lactate transport system. Some cells actively extrude lactate to prevent intracellular accumulation, maintain pH balance, and provide lactate for oxidative metabolism in highly oxidative tissues and organs , . Monocarboxylate transporters 1 (MCT1), a member of the solute carrier family 16 (SLC16), is primarily responsible for lactate transport . It is the most broadly expressed among MCTs 1 to 4 and is often highly expressed in cells with high oxidative activity, such as oxidative muscle fibers . Recent studies have highlighted the importance of MCT1 in fibrosis , . However, few studies demonstrate a direct link between the metabolic reprogramming of macrophages and fibroblast phenotypic remodeling in the progression of HS via lactate transport. How metabolic microenvironment created by macrophages orchestrate activation of fibroblast remains poorly understood.
Recent studies have identified lactate as a significant regulator of epigenetic modification alteration known as histone lactylation . Histones modulate biological processes by regulating the expression and activity of downstream genes . Histone lactylation plays a pivotal role in diverse cellular physiological mechanisms, including signal transduction, transcription regulation, metabolic homeostasis, epigenetic modifications, and immune responses – . Recently, the relationship between histone lactylation and fibrosis has attracted considerable attention . However, a few strategies involving histone lactylation treatment to overcome and shift molecular landscape of HS have been achieved. Thus, a better understanding of histone lactylation in the pathology of HS still requires further investigation. Besides, how metabolic reprogramming reshapes epigenetic alterations, particularly lactylation in HS, is still unclear.
This study provides compelling evidence that MCT1-mediated lactate influx modulates gene expression through histone lactylation, specifically via elevated H3K23la in HS-associated myofibroblasts. We demonstrate significantly increased lactate accumulation and upregulated expression of lactate-generative and transport-related genes in HS, with marked overexpression of MCT1. Further analysis revealed that macrophages are the primary contributors to the high-lactate metabolic microenvironment in HS, driving lactate accumulation and H3K23la levels through MCT1. Using the cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag) technique, we identified H3K23la enrichment at the promoters of profibrotic genes, including Hes Related Family BHLH Transcription Factor with YRPW Motif 2 (HEY2) and Collagen Type XI Alpha 1 Chain (COL11A1). Mechanistically, transcription HEY2 transcriptionally activates YAP1 expression, thereby stimulating the YAP1/SMAD2 pathway and promoting fibrotic processes. Concurrently, COL11A1 enhances lactate transport efficiency by interacting with MCT1, creating a positive feedback loop that exacerbates intracellular lactate accumulation in fibroblasts. Pharmacological inhibition of MCT1 and silence of HEY2 and COL11A1 attenuated fibroblast activation and collagen deposition. Notably, fibroblast-specific Mct1 deletion in a murine full-thickness skin defect model enhanced wound healing, reduced scar formation, and downregulated fibrotic gene expression. These findings unveiled a role of histone lactylation, particularly H3K23la, in HS pathogenesis. Disruption of the macrophage glycolysis-MCT1-H3K23la-HEY2/COL11A1 axis and its associated feedback loop represents a promising therapeutic strategy for HS treatment.

## Results

## Elevated glycolysis and lactate transport capacity in hypertrophic scar
The elevated glycolytic activity correlates with the progression of various fibrosis diseases and has been documented in the pathogenesis of keloid formation , . Considering the metabolic reprogramming in fibrotic diseases, we revealed lactate levels in the scar tissue of HS patients. Colorimetric assay demonstrated that lactate levels were markedly elevated in HS tissue compared to normal skin (NS) tissue (Fig.  ). To characterize the metabolic phenotype, non-targeted metabolomic analysis of HS and healthy tissues revealed a distinct metabolic profile in patients (Fig.  ). HS tissue exhibited a significant decrease in the key TCA cycle intermediate, citric acid, and a concomitant increase in the glycolytic metabolite, phosphoenolpyruvic acid (Fig.  – ). KEGG analysis of the different metabolites revealed enrichment in the central carbon metabolism, as well as pathways critical for biosynthesis and proliferation, including amino acid biosynthesis, mTOR signaling, PI3K-Akt signaling, and linoleic acid metabolism (Fig.  ). To further validate the metabolic profile and overcome the limitations of non-targeted metabolomics in quantifying small polar acids, we performed targeted energy metabolomics which revealed a distinct metabolic signature in HS tissues (Fig.  ). Lactate ranked as the third most upregulated metabolite (log2FC) following Guanosine Triphosphate (GTP) and DL-Glycerate (Fig.  ). Consistent with a shift toward aerobic glycolysis, quantitative analysis confirmed significantly elevated levels of Lactate, Pyruvic acid, and ATP in HS tissues (Fig.  ). KEGG pathway analysis of these metabolites highlighted enrichment in Pyruvate metabolism, Carbon metabolism, and Glycolysis (Fig.  ). Next, RT-qPCR analysis of the expression of genes related to glycolysis (LDHA, HK2, PKM2 and PFKFB3), lactate transport (MCT1 and MCT4) and OXPHOS (PDHA1, CS, IDH1 and IDH2) showed a significant increase of lactate-related genes in HS, particularly MCT1 (Fig.  ). Specifically, the mRNA levels of PDHA1, IDH1 and IDH2 were significantly decreased compared to normal skin (Fig.  ). Moreover, we profiled the expression of these genes in NS and HS using western blotting and the result showed that the protein level of MCT1, MCT4 and PKM2, which were markedly increased in HS, yet IDH1 and IDH2 were significantly decreased (Fig.  and ). This concurrent upregulation of the glycolytic pathway and downregulation of key OXPHOS components provides strong evidence of a metabolic switch towards aerobic glycolysis in the HS microenvironment. Despite having some minor differences, similar patterns were observed in IHC assay and immunofluorescence results (Fig.  D, ), which indicated that the expression of MCT1 and the overall capacity for glycolysis are significantly elevated in HS tissue compared to NS. Therefore, we hypothesized that MCT1 primarily participates in the regulation of skin fibrosis in HS. Furthermore, we performed immunofluorescence co-staining of MCT1 along with antibodies against marker for myofibroblasts (α-SMA) and HS tissue displayed increased co-localization between MCT1 and α-SMA fluorescence (Fig.  ). To quantitatively validate this observation, we calculated the Pearson’s correlation and Overlap coefficient, which revealed a significantly higher level of colocalization between MCT1 and α-SMA in HS tissues compared to NS tissues (Fig.  ). RT-qPCR results showed that the mRNA expression level of MCT1 was significantly increased in human hypertrophic scar fibroblasts (HSFs) compared to normal human skin dermal fibroblasts (NSFs) (Fig.  ). Together, these findings indicated that MCT1 expression is upregulated in HS, primarily in myofibroblasts. Cumulatively, our data revealed that an enhanced level of lactate accumulation and a metabolic switch happened in HS tissue and MCT1 has a potential role in transferring extracellular lactate into fibroblasts to promote phenotypic remodeling. 

## Lactate is a key facilitator of phenotypic remodeling of human skin fibroblasts
To clarify whether the increased lactate in HS is associated with fibroblast phenotypic remodeling into myofibroblasts, we assessed the expression of fibrotic markers in NSFs treated with different concentrations of sodium lactate (NaLac). At a lactate concentration of 10 mM, fibrotic markers such as COL1A1 and α-SMA reached maximal expression levels, while COL3A1 achieved the highest at 15 mM (Fig.  and ). Consequently, a lactate concentration of 10 mM was selected as the optimal stimulatory concentration. In addition, lactate could enhance the proliferation and migration capacity of NSFs (Fig.  – ). Notably, lactate was identified as a profibrotic factor in skin tissue.

## Macrophages are a major source of lactate accumulation within the scar metabolic microenvironment
Given the significant up-regulation of lactate content in HS, it is important to explore the cellular origins of lactate within the fibrotic microenvironment. To address this, we established an in vitro system utilizing polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) hydrogel-coated plates to recapitulate the elevated mechanical stiffness characteristic of hypertrophic scar. We fabricated substrates with Young’s moduli of 2 kPa (physiologically compliant) and 50 kPa (pathologically stiff) to simulate normal and fibrotic mechanical microenvironments, respectively , . Comparative lactate quantification was performed in three primary skin dermal cell populations: macrophages (THP-1 cells), vascular endothelial cells (HUVECs), and dermal fibroblasts (NSFs), which were seeded on the surface of PDMS hydrogel-coated plates. Biochemical analyses revealed that macrophages exhibited significantly elevated extracellular lactate accumulation and intracellular production compared to control conditions (Fig.  ). No significant stiffness-dependent lactate variations were observed in endothelial cells or fibroblasts (Fig.  ). Subsequent stimulation with TGF-β, a master regulator of fibrogenesis and collagen deposition , revealed that macrophages demonstrated the most pronounced lactate elevation, although moderate increases were also detected in endothelial cells and fibroblasts (Fig.  ). Metabolic profiling through extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) measurements provided functional validation, indicating that macrophages cultured on 50 kPa stiff substrates exhibited enhanced ECAR, reflecting higher levels of glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve under the stimulation of high substrate stiffness (Fig.  ). TGF-β stimulation produced comparable metabolic activation patterns (Fig.  ). To definitively assess the metabolic switch, we calculated the ATP production rates. Macrophages cultured on 50 kPa stiff substrates or under TGF-β stimulation exhibited a significant shift where ATP production from glycolysis (glycoATP) markedly exceeded that from oxidative phosphorylation (mitoATP) (Fig.  and ). However, endothelial and fibroblast cultures showed marginal ECAR increases and no significant shift in the glycoATP/mitoATP ratio under stiff conditions (Figs.  E, , S3C and S3D). 
To validate in vivo that macrophages are a primary source of lactate during scar formation, we isolated primary macrophages, vascular endothelial cells and dermal fibroblasts from fresh NS and HS tissues using magnetic activated cell sorting (MACS) and protein-level validation was performed on these cells using western blotting (Fig.  – ). In line with previous results, primary macrophages from HS tissues showed a profoundly elevated extracellular lactate accumulation and intracellular production, but lactate levels in fibroblasts and endothelial cells remained constant (Fig.  ). In addition, enhanced ECAR level and glycoATP/mitoATP ratio was observed in primary macrophages from HS tissues compared to control (Fig.  and ). This macrophage-specific metabolic shift was corroborated by the selective upregulation of glycolytic enzymes HK2 and LDHA within CD68+ macrophages in HS tissue (Figs.  I, ), with no corresponding change in CD31+ endothelial cells (Fig.  and ). To further rule out the contribution of other immune cell populations to the lactate-rich microenvironment, we also performed immunofluorescence co-staining of key glycolytic enzymes (HK2 or LDHA) with markers for mast cells (Tryptase) and T cells (CD3) in human NS and HS tissues. Quantitative analysis revealed no statistically significant differences in the number of glycolytic-enzyme-positive mast cells or T cells (Fig.  – ). Crucially, we utilized clodronate liposomes to specifically deplete macrophages in the full-thickness skin defect model on the dorsal region of C57BL/6 mice. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed a significant depletion of F4/80-positive macrophages in the scar tissue of clodronate liposomes-treated mice compared to saline-treated controls (Fig.  ). Importantly, this macrophage depletion resulted in a marked decrease in lactate levels within the scar tissue (Fig.  ). Collectively, these in vivo findings strongly support our conclusion that macrophages are a key contributor to the lactate-rich microenvironment in HS. This investigation identifies macrophages as the predominant lactate source in mechanically stressed fibrotic microenvironments, with both substrate stiffness and TGF-β signaling independently potentiating the shift towards glycolysis in macrophages in HS.

## Macrophages-derived lactate accumulation facilitates skin dermal fibroblasts phenotypic remodeling via MCT1
Our previous findings demonstrated the upregulated expression of MCT1 in myofibroblasts and lactate accumulation from macrophages in HS microenvironment. Therefore, it was important to explore the involvement of MCT1 and macrophages-derived lactate in fibroblasts phenotypic remodeling. As shown in Fig.  , conditioned medium (CM) of different substrate stiffness induced THP-1 cells (Mφ-CM@2 kPa, Mφ-CM@50 kPa) and HUVECs (EC-CM@2 kPa, EC-CM@50 kPa) were added into NSFs to induce fibrosis. Elevated fibrotic markers expression observed in NSFs stimulated by Mφ-CM@50 kPa was decreased in NSFs treated with AZD3965 (Fig.  B, ), a potent and selective MCT1 inhibitor that could disrupt cancer cell metabolism by blocking lactate transport . Furthermore, AZD3965 reversed the increased fibroblasts phenotypic remodeling induced by treatment with CM originating from macrophages cultured on 50 kPa stiff substrates, including the cell migration and proliferative capacity and the mRNA expression of profibrotic biomarkers (Fibronectin, SPARC, POSTN, TNC, CD248 and SMAD2) (Fig.  and ). These results demonstrated that Mφ-CM@50kPa-induced phenotypic remodeling in NSFs is caused by lactate transport from macrophages to fibroblasts via MCT1. To further validate this finding, we decreased the lactate production using the LDHA inhibitor oxamate. Consistent with our hypotheses, a reduction in lactate production levels in macrophages cultured on 50 kPa stiff substrates via oxamate significantly attenuated fibroblasts phenotypic remodeling (Fig.  and ). Furthermore, AZD3965 also could mitigate fibrosis in NSFs treated with CM of macrophages stimulated by TGF-β (Fig.  ). In contrast, conditioned medium from ECs stimulated by high substrate stiffness or TGF-β exhibited negligible effects on the expression of fibrotic markers (Fig.  and ). Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that macrophages had a notably increased glycolysis capacity in HS microenvironment, and MCT1 plays a key role in transferring macrophages-derived lactate into fibroblasts to promote phenotypic remodeling. 

## Histone lactylation is increased and the H3K23la level is specifically elevated in myofibroblasts in HS
To investigate the mechanism by which increased lactate levels promote scar formation, we assessed the levels of protein lactylation in five pairs HS tissues and NS tissues. Higher levels of global/pan-lysine lactylation (Pan Kla) were observed in HS tissues (Fig.  ). Notably, a distinct band around histone locations was observed, prompting us to focus on alterations in histone lactylation. We examined several previously confirmed sites of histone lactylation and found that only the H3K23la level was markedly elevated in HS tissue (Fig.  ). However, the expression of other common H3 and H4 lactylation sites, including H3K9la, H3K14la, H3K18la, H3K27la, H4K5la and H4K12la, did not change. Furthermore, we also found that there was a clear dose-dependent increase in H3K23la level under the different concentration of NaLac (Fig.  ). To examine histone lactylation changes in different skin dermal cell types, we performed immunofluorescence co-staining of H3K23la along with antibodies against markers for vascular endothelial cells (CD31), macrophages (CD68), and myofibroblasts (α-SMA). Most cells displayed H3K23la fluorescence (Fig.  ), and no obvious differences in H3K23la intensity were observed in vascular endothelial cells or macrophages between NS and HS tissue (Fig.  ). In contrast, the H3K23la signal intensity was significantly elevated in myofibroblasts as compared with myofibroblasts in HS tissue or NS tissue (Fig.  G, ). We isolated myofibroblasts from HS followed by western blotting analysis with antibodies against Pan Kla and H3K23la, which revealed that HSFs had a significant upregulation of the expression of Pan Kla and H3K23la compared with NSFs (Fig.  ). These results indicated that H3K23la was the primary differentially affected histone lactylation modification in the myofibroblasts of HS tissue. 

## MCT1 and P300 are potential regulators and writers of histone lactylation in HSFs
Previous findings have highlighted that MCT1 facilitated the transport of macrophage-derived lactate into fibroblasts, promoting their differentiation into myofibroblasts in HS microenvironment. To explore the regulatory influence of MCT1 on histone lactylation, we used MCT1 knockdown (si-MCT1) treatment in HSFs. Fig.  and demonstrated that si-MCT1-1 exhibited the highest knockdown efficiency. Therefore, we selected si-MCT1-1 for subsequent experiments. Fibrotic markers (COL1A1, COL3A1 and α-SMA) and histone lactylation H3K23la examined by western blotting and immunofluorescent staining revealed MCT1 silencing attenuated fibrosis and H3K23la level in HSFs (Fig.  and ). Furthermore, MCT1 silencing in HSFs resulted in a substantial reduction in cell proliferation by using Ki-67 immunofluorescent staining (Fig.  ). Wound scratch and Transwell assays revealed that MCT1 silencing also significantly impaired HSF migration (Fig.  ). These results suggest that MCT1 as potential regulator of histone lactylation, promoted phenotypic remodeling of fibroblast in HS.
P300, a histone acetyltransferase, is a transcriptional coactivator involved in chromatin remodeling and gene regulation . Recent studies suggest that P300 may also modulate histone lactylation . However, its involvement in HS progression remains understudied. We used P300 knockdown (si-P300) treatment in HSFs and selected si-P300-1 due to its higher knockdown efficiency (Fig.  and ). Under si-P300 treatment, the expression of fibrotic markers and histone lactylation H3K23la similarly decreased (Fig.  and ). Additionally, proliferation and migration capacity were weakened when P300 was silenced (Fig.  and ). These findings suggested that P300, the potential writer of histone lactylation, promotes the progression of fibroblasts in HS.

## H3K23la activates HEY2 and COL11A1 transcription in HS
Epigenetic modulation of histone post-translational modifications governs transcriptional activation and repression of target genes . To investigate the functional relevance of H3K23la in HS pathobiology, we conducted genome-wide CUT&Tag assays employing H3K23la-specific antibodies. Comparative analysis revealed pronounced enrichment of H3K23la peaks in HS tissues relative to NS control (Fig.  ). The HS cohort exhibited 16,860 differential H3K23la peaks, with 18.69% mapping to promoter regions. Specifically, 7424 upregulated peaks in HS tissues accounted for 25.26% located in promoter sequences (Fig.  ). HS tissues demonstrated 2313 upregulated and 49 downregulated lactylation peaks within promoter regions compared with NS tissues (Fig.  ). The GO and KEGG pathways enrichment analysis of HS-specific promoter-associated peaks identified significant associations with carbohydrate derivative biosynthetic process, positive regulation of fibroblast migration, collagen-containing extracellular matrix, and hypertrophic scar-related pathways such as TGF-β signaling pathway that are involved in glycolysis and scar formation (Fig.  ). We also conducted an RNA-seq assay to screen the potential target genes regulated by H3K23la in mRNA levels. When compared with the NS tissue, there were upregulated 1515 genes and downregulated 1341 genes in HS tissue (Fig.  ). Enrichment profiling of upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) highlighted similar pathways to CUT&Tag data (Fig.  ). These findings indicated that histone lactylation H3K23la lactylation promotes transcriptional programs underlying extracellular matrix deposition and fibroblast activity, and TGF-β signaling pathway activation, which were closely related to the pathological mechanisms of HS. 
By intersecting the gene sets among CUT&Tag and RNA-seq data, we identified 21 differential expressed genes that exhibited significantly increased mRNA levels and enhanced enrichment of the H3K23la signal at the promoter region in HS group (log2Fold Change > 2, P value  <  0.05) (Fig.  ). Given that both the CUT&Tag and RNA-seq enriched in the TGF-β signaling pathway and collagen-containing extracellular matrix, we focused on the genes highly likely involved in scar formation: HEY2 and COL11A1. Notably, HEY2 is a transcription factor closely associated with the Notch signaling pathway, primarily through repressing downstream target genes to regulate cardiovascular system development, organogenesis, and cell differentiation , . COL11A1 is an extracellular matrix protein that plays a role in various cancers by promoting mechanisms such as oncogenic proliferation, angiogenesis, and drug resistance, and it is closely associated with the activation of cancer-associated fibroblasts , . In addition, COL11A1 might be associated with keloids formation . Consequently, we decided to investigate these two genes.
Specifically, the peaks identified candidate genomic loci at HEY2 and COL11A1 genes showed that the levels of H3K23la at promoters of these genes were elevated (Fig.  ). A ChIP-qPCR analysis to further confirm that the histone lactylation H3K23la was enriched at the HEY2 and COL11A1 promoters in HS group compared with NS group (Fig.  ). Consistently, RT-qPCR and western blotting analysis showed that the mRNA and protein levels expression levels of HEY2 and COL11A1 were significantly elevated in HS tissue (Fig.  I, ). IHC analysis revealed a significant increase in HEY2 and COL11A1 expression in HS tissue (Fig.  ). Furthermore, immunofluorescence assays between H3K23la and HEY2 or H3K23la and COL11A1 demonstrated markedly upregulated colocalization in HS tissue samples compared to NS tissue (Fig.  and ). These findings collectively implicate H3K23la-mediated epigenetic upregulation of HEY2 and COL11A1 in HS progression.

## The high levels of HEY2 and COL11A1 are associated with phenotypic remodeling of fibroblasts
To investigate the effects of HEY2 and COL11A1 in HS progression, we detected their expression in fibroblasts. The transcription levels of HEY2 and COL11A1 were significantly higher in HSFs compared to NSFs (Figure  ). Immunofluorescence double staining against HEY2 and COL11A1 for α-SMA showed that upregulation of HEY2 and COL11A1 was specifically colocalized in HS dermal myofibroblasts (Fig.  A, ), which aligns with the elevated H3K23la levels previously detected in HS dermal myofibroblasts (Fig.  , and ). These results support the notion that HEY2 and COL11A1 exhibited elevated expression predominantly in myofibroblasts of HS. 
To assess the function of HEY2 and COL11A1 in HS, we used siRNA transfection to knock down HEY2 or COL11A1 expression in HSFs. We selected the siRNAs (si-HEY2-1 and si-COL11A1-3) which exhibited the highest knockdown efficiency for subsequent experiments (Figs.  – ). Knockdown of either HEY2 or COL11A1 resulted in a significant reduction in the expression of fibrotic markers (COL1A1, COL3A1 and α-SMA) for HSFs compared to the control group (Fig.  C, ). Immunofluorescence results demonstrate consistent expression patterns (Fig.  E, ). Additionally, cell migration and proliferation capabilities of HSFs were diminished by knockdown of HEY2 or COL11A1 (Fig.  , and ). These findings indicated that HEY2 and COL11A1 play critical roles in the phenotypic transition of fibroblasts in HS, and their inhibition can reverse this phenomenon.

## HEY2 promotes fibroblasts phenotypic remodeling via upregulated YAP1 expression
To investigate the mechanism underlying high HEY2 expression in HS, we searched the GTEx, FIMO, JASPAR, and GTRD databases for the genes that might be regulated by transcription factor HEY2 in HS. This led to the identification of six candidates: SDK1, KLHDC4, HERC1, YAP1, TMEM204 and CDK5R1 (Fig.  ). Yes-associated protein 1 (YAP1), a key effector of the Hippo signaling pathway, promotes fibroblasts proliferation, migration, and ECM overproduction in hypertrophic scar . Furthermore, we found that YAP1 expression in HS tissues was significantly increased compared to that in NS tissues (Fig.  ) and analysis of GTEx data revealed a positive association between the mRNA levels of HEY2 and YAP1 (Fig.  ). The JASPAR database was used to predict potential HEY2-binding sites in the YAP1 promoter. Three predicted binding sites were present in this genomic segment (Fig.  ). Subsequently, a ChIP-qPCR assay was conducted to confirm the binding of HEY2 to the YAP1 promoter region in HS (Fig.  ). YAP1 could interact with SMAD pathway, amplifying profibrotic signals, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target to mitigate HS , . Knockdown of HEY2 resulted in a significant reduction in the expression of YAP1, SMAD2 and P-SMAD2 (Fig.  ). Immunofluorescence co-staining of SMAD2 or P-SMAD2 along with α-SMA in HSFs showed that the fluorescence intensity of fibrotic marker and SMAD2 pathway were diminished by silencing of HEY2 (Fig.  G, ). These results indicated that HEY2 increased YAP1 transcription by interacting with the YAP1 promoter, contributing to scar formation via YAP1/SMAD2 pathway in HS. 

## COL11A1 aggravates lactate accumulation in fibroblasts by combination of MCT1
To clarify how COL11A1 promotes fibrosis and contributes to fibroblasts phenotypic remodeling in HS, we utilized BioGRID database to find the proteins interactions about COL11A1. Figure  demonstrated that 22 proteins interact with COL11A1, including SLC16A1, also known as MCT1 . To confirm this interaction, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments revealed a significant interaction between COL11A1 and MCT1 (Fig.  B, ). Their colocalization was also assessed in NSFs and HSFs via immunofluorescence analysis, which revealed an upregulated colocalization of COL11A1 and MCT1 within the region of the cytoplasm adjacent to the membrane of HSFs compared to NSFs (Fig.  ). We co-stained myofibroblasts (α-SMA) with antibodies against COL11A1 and MCT1 in NS and HS tissue. Myofibroblasts with strong COL11A1 and MCT1 colocalization signals in HS tissue (Fig.  ). While our Co-IP and immunofluorescence data confirmed a physical interaction between COL11A1 and MCT1, the functional consequence of this binding remained unclear. To determine precisely how COL11A1 binding might modulate MCT1’s activity, we performed molecular modeling. Through computational simulations utilizing AutoDock Vina software, we identified key amino acid residues mediating the COL11A1-MCT1 interaction. Specifically, residues 52, 81, and 121 in the COL11A1 N-terminal domain formed hydrogen bonds with residues 160, 303, and 315 of MCT1 (Fig.  ). We then mutated the predicted binding sites between COL11A1 and MCT1 (Fig.  ). Co-IP assays suggested that the interaction between COL11A1 and MCT1 was effectively impeded by mutations in the TRIM34-binding site (FLAG-COL11A1-MUT) or UPF1- binding site (His-MCT1-MUT), whether introduced individually or in conjunction (Fig.  ). Molecular dynamics simulations revealed enhanced structural stability in the COL11A1-MCT1 complex compared to MCT1 monomer, as evidenced by lower root mean square deviation (RMSD) values (Fig.  ). Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis demonstrated reduced structural variability in complex-associated MCT1, suggesting COL11A1-mediated stabilization of MCT1 conformation (Fig.  ). 
Next, we hypothesize that COL11A1 may modulate lactate transport through its interaction with MCT1. Based on the structures of the COL11A1-MCT1 complex and MCT1 protein depicted in Fig.  , we performed molecular docking by introducing lactate as a small molecule compound. The results demonstrated that lactate existed conserved hydrogen bonding with MCT1 residues ARG-313 (2.8/2.3 Å) and SER-154 (1.9 Å), irrespective of COL11A1 presence (Fig.  ). Dynamic simulations of ternary complexes showed superior structural stability in COL11A1-MCT1-lactate systems compared to MCT1-lactate pairs, with reduced ligand RMSD values (Fig.  ). Binding free energy calculations revealed strengthened lactate affinity in the ternary complex, mediated by enhanced interactions with PHE-367, PRO-155, LEU-281, and LEU-305 (Fig.  ; and Supplementary Table  ). To test these results, we assessed the effects of COL11A1 protein on intracellular lactate levels in HSFs under conditions supplemented with 10 mM exogenous lactate. The results revealed a positive correlation between COL11A1 protein concentration and intracellular lactate levels, suggesting that COL11A1 may enhance lactate accumulation within cells (Fig.  ). Given that histone lactylation is dependent on the presence of lactate and considering our finding that H3K23la modification promotes the expression of COL11A1, it seems plausible that there could be a positive feedback loop between lactate accumulation and H3K23la that propagates the phenotypic switch that we observed in the fibroblasts of HS tissue. The simulations provide a structural and energetic basis for how COL11A1 enhances MCT1’s lactate transport affinity, thereby closing the loop.

## Mct1-specific ablation in fibroblasts speeds up wound healing and ameliorates collagen deposition in a wound model of c57 male mice
We next achieved specific deletion of Mct1 in mouse fibroblasts by crossing Mct1f/f mice with Col1a2CreERT mice (Fig.  ). 4-week-old mice were given 20 mg/kg tamoxifen (intraperitoneal injection) for 5 consecutive days. Mice were divided into four groups: Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre-, Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre-+NaLac, Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre+, Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre++NaLac groups. During the sixth week, we created a full-thickness skin defect model on the dorsal region of mice. On the day of model construction, 100 µL NaLac (10 mM) was administered perilesionally. Subsequent administrations of 100 µL were performed sub-eschar every two days. Systematic photographic documentation was performed at days 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14 post-wounding (Fig.  ). As is shown in Fig.  , these photographs clearly showed that the wound closure rate was higher in the wound of Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre+ mice than Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre- mice. As expected, treatment with NaLac exhibited a delayed wound closure rate (Figs.  B, ). Next, tissue samples were collected at 21 days post-injury for histological analysis, and we rarely observed the hypertrophic scar features in the Mct1 knockout wounds as well (Fig.  ). However, under the condition of lactate treatment, these hypertrophic phenotypes significantly were evident (Figs.  D, ). Histological analysis with H&E staining revealed that wounds in the Mct1 knockout group exhibited a more organized skin architecture, characterized by a well-formed epidermis with rete ridges and signs of hair follicle regeneration, and a dermis with reduced inflammatory cell infiltration compared to the control group. In contrast, the control and NaLac-treated groups showed features of hypertrophic scarring, including a thickened, flattened epidermis and a disorganized dermal structure (Fig.  ). Masson staining showed obviously reduced blue collagen deposition and collagen fibers were arranged in a smoother, more orderly pattern in the Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre+ compared to Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre- mice (Fig.  ). Quantitative analysis confirmed these observations, showing a significantly reduced collagen area and dispersion index—indicating more orderly collagen alignment—in the Mct1 knockout group (Fig.  and S12A). The Sirius red staining results were also consistent with this result (Fig.  ). The collagen I to III ratios of groups were reduced in Mct1 knockdown mice compared to the control mice (Fig.  D, ). Meanwhile, NaLac treatment resulted in enhanced collagen synthesis, disorganized collagen alignment, and elevated collagen I to III ratios, as notably observed in the histological analysis (Fig.  ). These data indicated a shift from a fibrotic to a more regenerative healing phenotype in Mct1 knockdown mice. Additionally, immunofluorescence staining analysis conducted at 21 days post-injury demonstrated that a decrease of α-SMA and H3K23la expression in Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre+ mice (Fig.  ). Besides, western blotting showed obviously decreased levels of fibrotic markers (Col1a1, Col3a1 and α-SMA) and lactylation modification downstream products (Col11a1 and Hey2) in scar tissue of Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre+ compared to Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre- mice (Fig.  ). These findings suggested that fibroblasts-specific Mct1 knockout could inhibit scar formation and fibroblast phenotype transformation in full-thickness skin defect model. Furthermore, we isolated mouse fibroblast cells from Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre+ and Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre- mice. The expression levels of fibrotic markers and H3K23la increased in NaLac-treated fibroblasts and decreased in Mct1 knockout fibroblasts (Fig.  ). To further confirm the role of Mct1 in HS progression, RT-qPCR data showed that the mRNA expression of profibrotic biomarkers (Fibronectin, SPARC, POSTN, TNC, CD248 and SMAD2) were also significantly increased in NaLac-treated fibroblasts compared to Mct1 knockout fibroblasts (Figure  ). Collectively, these results suggested that the pro-fibrotic signaling driven by lactate and MCT1 creates a pathological microenvironment that simultaneously impairs the rate of wound closure and drives the excessive fibrotic gene expression characteristic of hypertrophic scar, mediated by increased H3K23la levels in fibroblasts. 

## Pharmacological inhibition of MCT1 or elimination of macrophages as a strategy for scar treatment
To further identify the role of MCT1 in the development of scar, six-week-old adult C57BL/6 mice were randomly allocated into four experimental groups: Control, Control + NaLac, AZD3965, and AZD3965 + NaLac. Full-thickness skin defects were surgically induced in the dorsal region of the six-week mice. Notably, the administration of AZD3965 markedly speeded up wound healing compared to the lactate-treated groups (Figs.  A, ). H&E, Masson and Sirius red staining demonstrated that decreased collagen synthesis, smoothed collagen alignment, and diminished collagen I to III ratios in lactate-treated mice at 21 days post-injury following AZD3965 treatment (Fig.  and ). Moreover, as evidenced by immunofluorescent staining, α-SMA signal levels significantly decreased in AZD3956-treated mice but reversed by lactate treatment (Fig.  ). Fig.  and showed that the depletion of macrophages using clodronate liposomes in the late stage of wound healing significantly reduced the secretion of lactic acid during scar formation. To validate the role of in vivo macrophage depletion, reducing the source of lactate, in scar formation, we then performed H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining on the collected scar tissue at post-injury day 21. The results indicated that clodronate liposomes significantly reduced scar formation in the dorsal wounds of mice and promoted hair follicle regeneration, suggesting that macrophages play an important pathological role in the late stage of wound healing (Figure  ). Collectively, MCT1 inhibition via AZD3965 or clearance of the source of lactate may be an effective therapeutic strategy for hypertrophic scar. 

## Discussion
Our study establishes an axis linking metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic regulation in hypertrophic scar pathogenesis (Fig.  ). Initially, we redefine the upstream trigger of fibroblast activation by identifying mechanically stressed macrophages as the key lactate producers. The stiff microenvironment induces macrophages to adopt a hyperglycolytic phenotype, producing lactate that is shuttled into fibroblasts via MCT1, which has a significant upregulation in hypertrophic scar tissue. Second, this lactate influx primes H3K23la, activating profibrotic genes HEY2 and COL11A1. HEY2 amplifies fibrotic signaling via YAP1/SMAD2, while COL11A1 stabilizes MCT1 to enhance lactate transport, creating a self-reinforcing loop that perpetuates fibrosis. Critically, targeting this pathway via fibroblast-specific Mct1 deletion or pharmacological inhibition (AZD3965) mitigated the core pathology. This intervention resulted in two concomitant benefits: an acceleration of wound closure, likely reflecting a more rapid resolution of the pro-fibrotic inflammatory phase and a reduction in the excessive collagen deposition that defines the quality of the scar. This work provides the evidence that macrophage-derived lactate fuels HS progression through MCT1-dependent histone lactylation, redefining lactate as a metabolic-epigenetic mediator rather than a passive byproduct. These findings position MCT1 as a therapeutic target and establish histone lactylation as an epigenetic driver of HS progression.
HS persists as a prevalent post-traumatic/surgical complication, presenting significant clinical management challenges in dermatologic surgery. Current therapeutic advancements targeting hypertrophic scar have been progressing at a slow pace. Metabolic disorders are risk factors for fibrosis progression. Cellular metabolic reprogramming supporting proliferation and cellular processes represent a key fibrotic hallmark , , . However, limited investigations address HS metabolism, with most research focusing on keloid pathophysiology. Evidence indicated keloid fibroblasts (KFs) upregulated glycolytic genes under hypoxia, shifting from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis , exhibiting cancer-like bioenergetics with lactate overproduction . Targeting KF metabolic reprogramming thus represents an emerging keloid treatment strategy , . Furthermore, recent research indicated that lactate could promote collagen expression, proliferation, and migration in keloid fibroblasts through H3K18la . Notably, HSFs showed no glycolytic elevation , consistent with our findings. Our combined metabolomic approach clarifies the metabolic landscape of HS. While non-targeted analysis provided a broad overview, targeted energy metabolomics allowed for the precise quantification of glycolytic intermediates. A critical evaluation of the most upregulated metabolites was necessary to identify the pathogenic driver. We excluded N-Desmethylaminopyrine as a pharmacological artifact. Among endogenous metabolites, while GTP and DL-Glycerate were highly upregulated—reflecting the increased biosynthetic demand of proliferating fibroblasts—we prioritized lactate for mechanistic investigation. Although the skin comprises a heterogeneous population of cells, our systematic analysis identifies macrophages as the principal source of lactate. Under high-stiffness conditions, macrophages displayed exclusive metabolic reprogramming, suggesting divergent pathogenic mechanisms between HS and keloids. We extended this paradigm by demonstrating substrate stiffness drived macrophages (not fibroblasts) glycolytic activation. Piezo 1-KO macrophages exhibit attenuated inflammation and enhanced healing, confirming mechanometabolic adaptations in fibrosis . Stiffness-activated macrophages dominated lactate production, transducing mechanical signals into metabolic output. While adipocytes are key components of the skin stroma, their contribution to the lactate pool in HS was considered less prominent due to spatial constraints; the significantly thickened reticular dermis in HS creates a physical barrier that separates the fibrotic core from the subcutaneous adipose layer , limiting the potential for adipocyte-derived paracrine metabolic signaling to reach dermal fibroblasts. Additionally, our supplementary data confirmed that vascular endothelial cells and other resident immune cells, such as mast cells and T cells, do not exhibit the robust glycolytic upregulation observed in macrophages. This lactate-rich microenvironment epigenetically reprograms fibroblasts via H3K23la, activating fibrotic gene transcription. MCT1 inhibition disrupts this crosstalk, revealing its dual lactate transport/epigenetic regulation role, which was a cancer-associated concept implicated in fibrosis. Our data thus redefines macrophage-fibroblast interactions as mechanometabolic-epigenetic circuits central to HS pathology.
Aerobic glycolysis, commonly recognized as the Warburg effect, is characterized by the preferential generation of energy through glycolysis, even in the presence of adequate oxygen levels, which results in substantial production of lactate . Though traditionally viewed as a waste product of glycolysis, Zhang et al. provided evidence that lactate has the potential to act as an epigenetic regulator by modifying histone lysine residues through lactylation . It is enriched by the promoters of glycolytic genes and exacerbates microglial dysfunction in Alzheimer’s disease by increasing glycolytic activity . Our investigation detected lactate accumulation within the HS microenvironment, providing substrate for histone lactylation. We have now further confirmed that this lactate accumulation arised from a clear metabolic shift, where the upregulation of glycolysis is coupled with the downregulation of oxidative phosphorylation. We also unveiled that H3K23la, a unique lactylation modification, promoted fibroblast phenotypic remodeling in HS models both in vitro and in vivo, establishing lactylation as a fibrosis-specific regulatory checkpoint. This metabolic-epigenetic circuit provides a compelling explanation for how the fibrotic phenotype is maintained and amplified in HS, effectively ‘locking’ the fibroblasts in a profibrotic state and driving the persistent nature of the disease. Recent studies have demonstrated elevated expression of H3K18la in HS tissue compared to NS, which may be attributed to the high heterogeneity of HS tissue . Given reports indicating the self-limiting nature of HS, inherent variability exists in tissue samples collected from different anatomical sites and at varying time points . However, our HS specimens were primarily collected from extremities, with strict temporal limitations to 3-6 months post-injury, corresponding to the proliferative phase of hypertrophic scar development, which could mitigate the impact of specimen heterogeneity to a certain extent. MCT1 is concentration dependent, ATP independent, saturable, stereospecific, competitively inhibited by other monocarboxylates, sensitive to temperature, and stimulated by [H+] gradients. Lactate transported by MCT1 plays an active role in promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and enhancing TCA flux in skeletal muscle . Our study demonstrated the upregulation of MCT1 in myofibroblasts of HS, which facilitates the transport of lactate into fibroblasts to promote fibroblast phenotypic remodeling. Besides, we also found that P300 as the potential writer respectively involved in regulating histone lactylation in HS. All these results suggest that lactylation participates in the development of HS.
Studies have shown that HEY2 accelerates cardiac failure by suppressing fatty acid oxidation and promoting ROS-mediated oxidative stress, indicating its significant role in mitochondrial respiration and energy metabolism . However, our data demonstrated that lactylation modification at H3K23la in hypertrophic scar tissue stimulates HEY2 expression. While HEY2 is an important transcriptional repressor, subsequent database screening revealed that HEY2 induces YAP1 expression, which in turn promotes fibroblast phenotypic transition through SMAD2. We found that histone lactylation could regulate YAP1/SMAD2 pathway in HS. Besides, various investigations indicated that COL11A1 serves as a critical prometastatic molecule in the tumor microenvironment and a biomarker , , . COL11A1 could regulate cancer-associated fibroblasts phenotypic transitions through extracellular signaling mechanisms . The selective upregulation of COL11A1 in HS, independent of its heterotrimeric partners COL11A2 and COL2A1 , is driven by metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk. The genes for each alpha chain are subject to independent transcriptional control. Specifically, the lactate-rich HS microenvironment promotes H3K23la enrichment at the COL11A1 promoter, selectively activating its transcription. Furthermore, the COL11A1 protein exhibits a non-canonical function by directly interacting with the lactate transporter MCT1. Molecular modeling shows this interaction stabilizes MCT1 and enhances its lactate affinity, potentiating lactate transport. This establishes a pathogenic, self-reinforcing positive feedback loop (lactate-H3K23la-COL11A1-MCT1), which sustains the scar microenvironment. These results aligned with emerging evidence implicating positive feedback networks in fibrosis progression, reframing the fibrosis disease as a self-reinforcing cascade initiated by primary perturbations rather than a linear stepwise sequence . Furthermore, while our co-immunoprecipitation and computational data support an interaction between COL11A1 and MCT1, it is critical to consider the potential involvement of other proteins that may mediate or stabilize this complex in the cellular milieu. A prime candidate is the chaperone protein CD147, which is indispensable for the membrane localization and functional stability of MCT1 . We thus hypothesize that COL11A1 forms a ternary complex with CD147 and MCT1. This complex would anchor the lactate transporter, enhancing its activity and thereby amplifying pro-fibrotic signaling. Collectively, metabolic-transcriptional highlighted the multifaceted nature of HS pathogenesis while revealing multiple targetable points for therapeutic intervention.
Our findings in HS pathogenesis point to a self-amplifying “mechano-metabolic vicious cycle,” rather than a simple linear causality between matrix stiffening and lactate production. We posit that initial wound healing processes, including inflammation and ECM deposition, stiffen the tissue microenvironment . This mechanical cue, as demonstrated in vitro and in vivo, triggers a metabolic shift in macrophages towards increased glycolysis and lactate secretion. Subsequently, fibroblast uptake of macrophage-derived lactate via MCT1 instigates H3K23la-dependent pathological activation and excessive ECM production. This not only intensifies fibrosis but also further stiffens the matrix, locking in a positive feedback loop that drives HS formation. Crucially, our work identifies this amplification loop as a therapeutic target. Pharmacological inhibition of MCT1 validated this concept, effectively accelerating wound closure and mitigating scar formation. This demonstrates that therapeutically interrupting this lactate-driven, mechano-metabolic crosstalk can steer the repair process away from pathological fibrosis, even after the initial injury. Though the potential of this strategy to reverse established, chronic scar remains a key area for future studies, as our current investigation centered on the proliferative phase of scarring.
Limitations of the current study. While our study identifies a glycolysis-MCT1-H3K23la-HEY2/COL11A1 axis HS progression, three principal limitations warrant acknowledgment. First, establishing a direct causal link between H3K23la deposition and HS progression remains challenging due to the complex and dynamic nature of histone modifications that resist perturbation through single-nucleotide mutations - a limitation inherent in studies investigating histone post-translational modifications in disease pathogenesis. Second, our in vitro substrate stiffness models, employing PDMS hydrogel-coated plates, only partially mimic fibrotic microenvironments and fail to replicate the multicellular crosstalk and cytokine gradients observed in vivo. Future studies should employ organoid systems or three-dimensional cell culture platforms to better model scar tissue complexity. Third, while our murine full-thickness excisional wound model partially recapitulates late-phase hypertrophic scar formation, interspecies differences in healing mechanisms persist: murine dermis containing the panniculus carnosus facilitates contractile healing through regenerative processes. However, the primary in vivo objective of our study was to elucidate the specific genetic function of fibroblast-expressed Mct1 in a fibrotic healing context. This required a genetically knockout model allowing for conditional gene deletion, for which the mouse is the most established and feasible system. We chose the splinted wound model as the best available tool to investigate this specific molecular mechanism, while recognizing that future validation in larger animal models that more closely mimic human skin would be a valuable next step.

## Methods

## Human subjects
Postoperative discarded hypertrophic scar tissues were procured from patients diagnosed with hypertrophic scars at the Department of Burns and Cutaneous Surgery of Xijing Hospital, Air Force Medical University, along with normal skin samples from age-matched non-hypertrophic scar patients. Collected tissue samples were immediately trimmed and either immersed in fixative solution (4% paraformaldehyde, Servicebio, G1101) or rapidly cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen, followed by storage at -80 °C for subsequent processing. Except for questionnaire-based assessments, all participants were screened based on medical history, laboratory tests, and physical examinations. A total of ten hypertrophic scar tissue samples and fifteen age-matched normal control skin samples were collected. Sex was not considered as a specific variable in the study design or analysis. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and no financial compensation was provided for their participation. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Xijing Hospital, Air Force Medical University (KY20252080-C-1). Subject baseline characteristics of individuals with hypertrophic scars and controls are presented in the Supplementary Table  .

## Animal models
All male mice in this study were from the C57BL/6 background. Mct1f/f mice were kindly provided by Prof. Yan Chen (Shanghai Institute of Nutrition and Health, China) and Col1a2-creERT mice were purchased from Shulaibao (Wuhan) Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). Col1a2-creERT mice were maintained as hemizygous by breeding with C57BL/6. To generate Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre+ mice, Mct1f/f mice were crossed with Col1a2-creERT mice, which would yield two groups of mice (Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre+ and Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre-). Mice genotypes were confirmed via PCR amplification of DNA extracted from tail biopsies. Other 6-8 weeks old C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Air Force Medical University Animal Center (Xi’an, China). All mice were maintained at 18 ± 3 °C under a 12-h light/dark cycle, with a relative humidity of 50 ± 5%, in a filtered, pathogen-free environment. Ad libitum access to food and water was provided throughout the study. All animal surgeries were performed with inhalation anesthesia with isoflurane at a concentration of 1–2% in oxygen at 3 L min−1.
Mice with Mct1 specific knockout in fibroblasts were induced by tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich, T5648). Briefly, tamoxifen was dissolved in corn oil (Sigma-Aldrich, C8267) to a final concentration of 20 mg/ml. To ablate Mct1 in fibroblasts, 4-week-old mice were given 20 mg/kg tamoxifen by intraperitoneal injection for 5 consecutive days. In the sixth week, after the mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, their back hair was removed at approximately 2 cm below the ear. On the following day, a translucent, single-sided adhesive silicone ring (outer diameter: 14 mm, inner diameter: 10 mm and thickness: 0.6 mm) was securely affixed to the midline of the hairless dorsal area. Subsequently, a sterile scalpel was used to create a full-thickness circular wound along the inner circumference of the silicone ring. For lactate treatment groups, NaLac (Sigma-Aldrich, L7022) was dissolved in sterile water and prepared at a concentration of 10 mM. On the day of model induction, 100 µL was administered perilesionally. Subsequent administrations of 100 µL were performed sub-eschar every two days. To examine the anti-fibrosis function of pharmacological inhibition of MCT1, C57BL/6 mice aged 6 to 8 weeks were randomly assigned to four groups (Control, Control + NaLac, AZD3965, and AZD3965 + NaLac). AZD3965 was administered with a solution prepared in 0.5% hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (Sigma-Aldrich, H7509) and 0.1% Tween 80 (Servicebio, GC204001) at a dose of 100 mg/kg, administered twice daily (BID) via oral gavage for 21 days. To identify the source of lactate in the scar microenvironment in vivo, C57BL/6 mice aged 6 to 8 weeks were randomly assigned to three groups (Control, Clodronate liposome and Nalac). Clodronate liposomes (Yeasen, 40337ES) were administered of 40 µL sub-eschar every three days starting from day 7 post-injury with a solution prepared at a dose of 50ug/ml . Systematic photographic documentation was conducted at days 0, 3, 7, 10, and 14 post-wounding. On day 21, tissue collection was conducted for subsequent protein extraction and histopathological analysis to facilitate comprehensive biochemical assessment. All mouse experiments were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of the Air Force Medical University (20250030).

## Isolation and culture of dermal fibroblasts from human normal skin and hypertrophic scar
Normal skin tissue and hypertrophic scar tissue were collected clinically and immediately transferred to a sterile workbench. The tissues were rinsed three times with prechilled PBS. The tissues were trimmed into elongated strips and then washed three times with 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122) solution dissolved in PBS. Normal skin tissue was incubated overnight (14–18 h) at 4 °C on a shaker in 2.5% sterile Dispase Ⅱ solution (Sigma-Aldrich, D4693) dissolved in DMEM basic medium (Gibco, 11885500). The next day, the epidermis and dermis were gently separated using forceps. However, scar tissue could directly separate into dermis and epidermis using scissors instead of incubating in 2.5% Dispase Ⅱ solution. Both types of dermal tissues were minced into chylous tissue blocks and then evenly seeded into 25 cm² culture flasks using a pipette, with each block measuring approximately 0.3 × 0.3 cm. The tissue blocks were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 6–8 h, after which 2 ml of complete medium (DMEM basic medium + 10% FBS (Vazyme Biotech, F103)) was added. The medium was replaced every two days. When confluent fibroblasts with a spindle-shaped morphology were observed surrounding the tissue blocks, the cells were passaged. Third- to Sixth-generation fibroblasts were used for subsequent experiments.

## Isolation and culture of dermal fibroblasts from Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre+ and Mct1fl/flCol1a2-cre-mice skin
Neonatal mice (1–2 days postnatal) were anesthetized and immersed in 75% ethanol for 5 minutes. The skin was rapidly dissected and placed in PBS containing 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin, rinsed three times. The skin tissue was incubated overnight at 4 °C on a shaker (10–16 hours) in 2.5% sterile Dispase II solution (65 mg Dispase II + 26 ml DMEM basic medium). The following day, the epidermis and dermis were separated using forceps, and the dermal tissue was minced into a chylous mixture. An equal volume of DMEM basic medium containing 1% Collagenase Type I (Sigma-Aldrich, SCR103) was prepared and mixed with the dermal tissue, followed by incubation at 37 °C on a shaker for enzymatic digestion. After 1 hour, the reaction was neutralized with an equal volume of complete medium (containing 10% FBS). The mixture was sequentially filtered through 100-μm and 70-μm filters. After centrifugation at 200 g for 5 minutes, the primary mouse dermal fibroblasts were seeded into culture flasks and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Third- to Sixth-generation fibroblasts were used for subsequent experiments. For Mct1 knockout, 0.5 μM 4-hydroxytamoxifen (MedChemExpress, HY-16950) was added to the medium and cultured for 3 days before treating with sodium lactate (Sigma-Aldrich, L7022). After treating with sodium lactate (10 mM) or vehicle (PBS) for another 24 h, fibroblasts were collected for subsequent experiments.

## Isolation of primary macrophages, vascular endothelial cells and dermal fibroblasts using magnetic activated cell sorting (MACS)
Primary macrophages, vascular endothelial cells and dermal fibroblasts were isolated from mouse scar tissues using magnetic separation (MACS) Technology (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, wash human skin tissue sample in PBS. After scraping off the subcutaneous fat using a scalpel, tissues were dissociated using Whole Skin Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-101-540). CD68-positive macrophages were labeled with PE Mouse Anti-Human CD68 (BD Biosciences, 556078). CD31-positive vascular endothelial cells were labeled with PE Mouse Anti-Human CD31 (BD Biosciences, 566125). Vimentin-positive dermal fibroblasts were labeled with PE Mouse Anti-Human Vimentin (BD Biosciences, 562337). After combined with Anti- R-Phycoerythrin (PE) Magnetic Particles (BD Biosciences, 557899), cells separated using a MACS Column (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-021-101), and centrifuged at 400 g at 4 °C for 5 minutes. The supernatant was discarded, and the isolated primary cells were retained for subsequent use.

## PDMS Hydrogel-coated plates
PDMS Hydrogel of varying stiffness were fabricated using protocols . These silicone dielectric gels were prepared using Dow Corning Sylgard 527 (Part A and B, Ellsworth Adhesives, 527 SIL DIELECT GEL). The components of Part A and Part B were mixed in specific volumetric ratios to achieve the desired mechanical properties. For the 2 kPa gel, the A: B ratio was 1.2: 1, while for the 50 kPa gel, the A: B ratio was 0.3: 1. Cell culture plates were coated with the hydrogel and incubated for a duration of 16–18 hours at 60 °C. For 6-well plates, 2 mL of gel was applied to each well, and for 24-well plates, 0.4 mL of gel was applied to each well. After 24 hours, the gels were functionalized with fibronectin (1 μg/mL in PBS, Sigma-Aldrich, F2006) for 4 hours at 37 °C, followed by subsequent rinsing with sterile PBS.

## Cell culture and treatment
The human cell lines THP-1 and HUVEC cells were sourced from Procell Life Science & Technology. THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, 11875500) and HUVECs were cultured in DMEM basic, low glucose medium (Gibco, 11885500), which were supplemented with 10% FBS (Vazyme Biotech, F103) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122). NSFs, HSFs and mouse skin dermal fibroblasts were cultured in DMEM basic, low glucose medium (Gibco, 11885500) supplemented with 10% FBS (Vazyme Biotech, F103) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122). For high matrix stiffness culture conditions, cells were seeded onto PDMS hydrogel-coated plates. After adhering for 24 h, supernatants were collected, or subsequent experimental procedures were initiated. To validate whether macrophage-derived lactate enters fibroblasts via the MCT1 lactate transport channel, supernatants from THP-1 cells and HUVECs cultured under 2 kPa and 50 kPa conditions for 24 h were mixed at a 1:1 ratio with DMEM complete medium (DMEM basic medium with 10% FBS) to stimulate NSFs. AZD3965 (100 nM) was added to the respective treatment groups. The lactate production inhibitor Oxamate (10 mM) was used to pre-treat THP-1 cells and HUVECs prior to supernatant collection after 24 h. THP-1 cells and HUVECs were pretreated for 24 h with TGF-β (10 ng/ml, Proteintech, HZ-1011). This concentration was selected as a well-established and widely used concentration in the literature for effectively inducing a fibrotic response in in vitro models of hypertrophic scarring and fibrosis , . All drugs were pH-neutralized before treatment.

## Histone extraction
Histones were isolated from tissues or cells via a standardized acid-extraction protocol . Briefly, tissue or cellular samples were harvested and resuspended in lysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl₂, and 1 mM DTT, pH 8.0) supplemented with protease inhibitors for nuclear extraction. The isolated nuclei were subsequently resuspended in acidic extraction buffer (0.2 M H₂SO₄) through an overnight incubation at 4 °C, and histones were extracted followed by centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C. The supernatant fractions were collected, and histone pellets were precipitated using trichloroacetic acid (TCA, Sigma-Aldrich, T8657) to a final concentration of 35% on ice. Following extensive washing and drying, the histone pellets were resuspended in deionized water and the samples concentration was quantified by Pierce Rapid Gold BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, A53225). Histone were loaded onto a 15% SDS‒PAGE gel and subjected to western blot analysis.

## Western blotting
Skin tissues or cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (NCM Biotech, WB3100) containing PMSF (Beyotime, ST506). The protein concentration of lysates was measured by Pierce Rapid Gold BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, A53225) and was adjusted to the same final concentration. The denatured proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, IPVH00010). After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Beyotime, ST506) for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by three washes with TBST, and subsequent incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The immunoreactive proteins were then detected using SuperFemto ECL Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech, E433) and visualized with ChemiDoc Imaging System (BIO-RAD, USA). Equal loading of proteins was verified by immunoblotting for β-Actin and Histone 3. All the antibodies used in immunoblot analysis are listed in the Supplementary Table  .

## Protein co-immunoprecipitation
For the protein co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay, cells were collected and lysed in IP lysis buffer (Pierce Classic Magnetic IP/Co-IP Kit, Thermo Scientific, 88804) containing PMSF (Beyotime, ST506). After centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 10 min, the supernatant was collected and incubated overnight with 5 μg of target antibodies or IgG at 4 °C. Protein A/G magnetic beads (Pierce Classic Magnetic IP/Co-IP Kit, Thermo Scientific, 88804) were added to a 4 °C shaker for 4 h. The immunoprecipitated protein complexes were washed with Co-IP wash buffer and resuspended in protein-loading buffer. Finally, the immunoprecipitated proteins were separated using SDS-PAGE and analyzed via immunoblotting. Plasmid construction, protein expression, and protein purification were performed by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). All the antibodies used in Co-IP analysis are listed in the Supplementary Table  .

## RNA isolation and qRT‒PCR assays
All primers were designed, synthesized, and PAGE-purified by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Total RNA was extracted using Trizol (Invitrogen, 15596018) and a total of 500 ng of RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, RR036A). The cDNA was subjected to qRT-PCR analysis with the TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, RR820A) using a CFX96 real-time PCR instrument (BIO-RAD, USA). The reaction mixture was predenatured at 95 °C for 10 min, denatured at 95 °C for 15 s, annealed at 60 °C for 1 min and entered a melting-curve phase with 40 cycles of amplification. The levels of target mRNAs were normalized to those of β-Actin. The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table  .

## siRNA transfection
All siRNAs were designed, synthesized, and PAGE-purified by Tsingke Biotech (Beijing, China). SiRNA was dissolved to a working concentration of 50 nM. Equal numbers of HSFs were plated on 6 or 24-well plates and then subjected to siRNA transfection by following the manufacturer’s instructions with Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, L3000015). Cells were harvested for subsequent experiments at 48 h after transfection. The sequences of siRNA are listed in Supplementary Table  .

## Wound scratch assay
NSFs or HSFs were cultured in 6-well cell culture plates and grown to 90–100% confluence, scratching wounds were generated using a 200 µL sterile pipette tip. The shedding of the cell debris was washed off with PBS. After changing the serum-free medium, the culture was maintained for 24 h. siRNA transfection was performed prior to cells being cultivated to 90–100% confluence, while other treatments involved sustained stimulation of the cells throughout the period before and after the wound scratch test. Photographs were captured at 0 and 24 h after wound generation using an FSX100 microscope (Olympus, Japan). Subsequently, the wound areas were quantified using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

## Transwell assay
The migration abilities of NSFs and HSFs cells were evaluated using a chamber of 8-µm transwell inserts (Corning Life Science, USA). 2 × 104 Cells were plated in the upper transwell chamber of a 24-well plate in 200 µL medium without fetal bovine serum, while the lower chamber was filled with 700 µL medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum as the attractant. siRNA transfection was performed prior to Transwell assay, while other treatments supplemented in lower chamber medium. After the 24 h incubation, the migrated cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, G1101) and stained with Crystal Violet Staining Solution (Beyotime, C0121). The stained cells were photographed using an FSX100 microscope (Olympus, Japan).

## Non-targeted metabolomic
Non-targeted metabolomic were performed by Tgene Biotech (Shanghai, China) Co., Ltd. 100 mg solid sample was added to a 2 mL centrifuge tube and a 6 mm diameter grinding bead was added. 800 μL of extraction solution (methanol: water = 4:1 (v:v) containing four internal standards (0.02 mg/mL L-2-chlorophenylalanine, etc.) were used for metabolite extraction. Samples were ground by the Wonbio-96c (Shanghai wanbo biotechnology co., LTD) frozen tissue grinder for 6 min (-10 °C, 50 Hz), followed by low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 min (5 °C, 40 kHz). The samples were left at -20 °C for 30 min, centrifuged for 15 min (4 °C, 13000 g), and the supernatant was transferred to the injection vial for LC-MS/MS analysis. The LC analysis was performed on a Vanquish UHPLC System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Chromatography was carried out with an ACQUITY UPLC ® HSS T3 (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm) (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The column maintained at 40 °C. The flow rate and injection volume were set at 0.3 mL/min and 2 μL, respectively. For LC-ESI ( + )-MS analysis, the mobile phases consisted of (B2) 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (v/v) and (A2) 0.1% formic acid in water (v/v). Separation was conducted under the following gradient:0 ~ 1 min, 8% B2; 1 ~ 8 min, 8% ~ 98% B2; 8 ~ 10 min, 98% B2; 10 ~ 10.1 min, 98% ~ 8% B2; 10.1 ~ 12 min, 8% B2. For LC-ESI (-)-MS analysis, the analytes was carried out with (B3) acetonitrile and (A3) ammonium formate (5 mM). Separation was conducted under the following gradient: 0 ~ 1 min, 8% B3; 1 ~ 8 min, 8% ~ 98% B3; 8 ~ 10 min, 98% B3; 10 ~ 10.1 min, 98% ~ 8% B3; 10.1 ~ 12 min, 8%B3. Mass spectrometric detection of metabolites was performed on Q Exactive (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with ESI ion source. Simultaneous MS1 and MS/MS (Full MS-ddMS2 mode, data-dependent MS/MS) acquisition was used. The parameters were as follows: sheath gas pressure, 40 arb; aux gas flow, 10 arb; spray voltage, 3.50 kV and -2.50 kV for ESI (+) and ESI (-), respectively; capillary temperature, 325 °C; MS1 range, m/z 100-1000; MS1 resolving power, 70000 FWHM; number of data dependent scans per cycle, 10; MS/MS resolving power, 17500 FWHM; normalized collision energy, 30 eV; dynamic exclusion time, automatic.

## Non-targeted metabolomic data preprocessing and analysis
The raw data were firstly converted to mzXML format by MSConvert in ProteoWizard software package (v3.0.8789) and processed using R XCMS(v3.12.0) for feature detection, retention time correction and alignment. Key parameters settings were set as follows: ppm=15, peakwidth=c (5, 30), mzdiff=0.01, method=centWave. The batch effect was then eliminated by correcting the data based on QC samples. Metabolites with RSD > 30% in QC samples were filtered and then used for subsequent data analysis. The metabolites were identified by accuracy mass and MS/MS data which were matched with HMDB, massbank, KEGG, LipidMaps, mzcloud and the metabolite database build by Tgene Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The molecular weight of metabolites was determined according to the m/z (mass-to-charge ratio) of parent ions in MS data. Molecular formula was predicted by ppm (parts per million) and adduct ion, and then matched with the database. At the same time, the MS/MS data from quantitative table of MS/MS data, were matched with the fragment ions and other information of each metabolite in the database, so as to realize the MS/MS identification of metabolite. Two different multivariate statistical analysis models, unsupervised and supervised, were applied to discriminate the groups (PCA; PLS-DA; OPLS-DA) by R ropls (v1.22.0) package. The statistical significance of P value was obtained by statistical test between groups. Finally, combined with P value, VIP (OPLS-DA variable projection importance) and FC (multiple of difference between groups) to screen biomarker metabolites. By default, when P value < 0.05 and VIP value > 1, we think that metabolite were considered to have significant differential expression.

## Targeted energy metabolomic data preprocessing and analysis
Non-targeted metabolomic were performed by Metware Metabolism Biotech (Wuhan, China) Co., Ltd. HPLC-grade acetonitrile (ACN) and methanol (MeOH) were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). MilliQ water (Millipore, Bradford, USA) was used in all experiments. All of the standards were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and Zhenzhun etc. Formic acid was bought from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The stock solutions of standards were prepared at the con centration of 1 mg/mL in MeOH and other solutions. All stock solutions were stored at -20 °C. The stock solutions were diluted with MeOH to working solutions before analysis. After the sample was thawed and smashed, an amount of 0.05 g of the sample was mixed with 500 µL of 70% methanol/water. The sample was vortexed for 3 min under the condition of 2500 r/min and centrifuged at 12000 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C. Take 300 μL of supernatant into a new centrifuge tube and place the supernatant in -20 °C refrigerator for 30 min, Then the supernatant was centrifuged again at 12000 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation, transfer 200 μL of supernatant through Protein Precipitation Plate for further LC-MS analysis. All those metabolites were detected by MetWare (http://www.metware.cn/) based on the AB Sciex QTRAP6500 LC-MS/MS platform. Significantly regulated metabolites between groups were determined by VIP and absolute Log2FC (fold change). VIP values were extracted from OPLS-DA result, which also contain score plots and permutation plots, and was generated using R package MetaboAnalystR. The data was mean centering before OPLS-DA. In order to avoid overfitting, a permutation test (200 permutations) was performed. Identified metabolites were annotated using KEGG compound database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/), annotated metabolites were then mapped to KEGG Pathway database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html). Pathways with significantly regulated metabolites mapped to were then fed into MSEA (metabolite sets enrichment analysis), their significance was determined by hypergeometric test’s P Values.

## CUT&Tag
CUT&Tag were performed by Wuhan IGENEBOOK Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Wuhan, China). Briefly, CUT&Tag assays were performed by using Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina Pro (Vazyme Biotech, TD904). Briefly, NS and HS samples were dissociated into nucleus suspension, nucleus were harvested and incubated with concanavalin A coated magnetic beads for 15 min at RT. Then, bead-bound nucleus were resuspended and incubated with H3K23la antibody (PTM-1413RM, PTM BIO) and IgG (2729, Cell Signaling Technology) overnight at 4°C. Afterwards, goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (35401, Cell Signaling Technology) was added to this mixture for 30 min at RT. The magnet stand was used to remove unbound antibodies. pA/G-Tn5 adapter complex was added at this step at RT for 1 h, then remove unbound pA-Tn5 protein. Next, cells were resuspended in tagmentation buffer at 37°C for 1 h. After that, immunoprecipitated DNA was saved. To amplify libraries, 21 µL DNA was mixed with 2 µL of a universal i5 and a uniquely barcoded i7 primer, using a different barcode for each sample. A volume of 25 µL NEBNext High-Fidelity 2X PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs, M0541) was added and mixed. The sample was placed in a Thermocycler with a heated lid using the following cycling conditions: 72 °C for 5 min (gap filling); 98 °C for 30 s; 14 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s and 63 °C for 30 s; final extension at 72 °C for 1 min and hold at 8 °C. Library was sequenced on Illumina novaseq 6000 with PE 150 method.

## CUT&Tag data analysis and visualization
Trimmomatic (version 0.36) was used to filter out low-quality reads. Clean reads were mapped to the GRCh38.p14 genome by Bwa (version 0.7.15). Samtools (version 1.3.1) was used to remove potential PCR duplicates. MACS2 software (version 2.1.1.20160309) was used to call peaks by default parameters (bandwidth, 300 bp; model fold, 5, 50; p value, 0.00001). If the midpoint of a peak is located closest to the TSS of one gene, the peak will be assigned to that gene. HOMER (version 3) was used to predict motif occurrence within peaks with default settings for a maximum motif length of 12 base pairs. ClusterProfiler in R package was employed to perform GO and KEGG enrichment analysis. The GO and KEGG enrichment analysis were calculated using hypergeometric distribution with a q value cutoff of 0.05. The data were visualized using the IGV (version 2.19.3).

## Total RNA extraction, RNA-seq library construction and sequencing
RNA-seq were performed by Wuhan IGENEBOOK Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Briefly, the total RNA was extracted using Trizol method. All the RNA samples were assessed for their integrity using Qsep400 instrument. To construct RNA libraries with the VAHTS mRNA-seq V8 Library Prep Kit for Illumina, 1 μg of total RNA was used. The procedure included PolyA-selected RNA extraction, RNA fragmentation, random hexamer primed reverse transcription, and 150nt paired-end sequencing by Illumina Novaseq 6000.

## Bioinformatics analysis of RNA-seq
The adapter and low-quality reads were filtered out through Cutadapt (version 1.11). Clean reads were mapped to the GRCh38.p14 reference genomes by Hisat2 (version 2.1.0), allowing up to two mismatches. These genes were subjected to alignment against public protein databases; NR (RefSeq non-redundant proteins). Featurecount (v1.6.0) was used for transcript abundance estimation and normalization of expression values as FPKM (Fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped). Differentially expressed genes were identified with edgeR with a filter threshold of FDR < 0.05 and |log2FoldChange | > 1. ClusterProfiler in R package was employed to perform GO and KEGG enrichment analysis. The GO and KEGG enrichment analysis were calculated using hypergeometric distribution with a p-value cutoff of 0.05.

## ChIP-qPCR
Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays were performed by Wuhan IGENEBOOK Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Briefly, 2-4 g samples were washed twice in cold PBS buffer and cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 minutes at room temperature and then quenched by addition of glycine (125 mmol/L final concentration). Afterwards, samples were lysed, and chromatins were obtained on ice. Chromatins were sonicated to get soluble sheared chromatin (average DNA length of 200–500 bp). 20ul chromatin was saved at –20 °C for input DNA, and 100ul chromatin was used for immunoprecipitation by primary antibodies and Anti-IgG (2729, Cell Signaling Technology) respectively. 10 μg of antibody was used in immunoprecipitation reactions at 4 °C overnight. The next day, 30 μL of protein G beads (10004D, Invitrogen) were added and the samples were further incubated for 3 h. The beads were next washed once with 20 mM Tris/HCL (pH 8.1), 50 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.1% SDS; twice with 10 mM Tris/HCL (pH 8.1), 250 mM LiCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 1% deoxycholic acid; and twice with 1×TE buffer (10 mM Tris/HCL at pH 7.5 and 1 mM EDTA). Bound material was then eluted from the beads in 300 μL of elution buffer (100 mM NaHCO3 and 1% SDS), treated first with RNase A (final concentration 8 μg/mL) during 6 h at 65 °C and then with proteinase K (final concentration 345 μg/mL) overnight at 45 °C. Immunoprecipitated DNA was amplified by PCR using their specific primers. The enrichment values were normalized to the input sample and calculated using the 2^(-ΔΔCt) method. All primers are listed in the Supplementary Table  .

## Measurements of lactate
The levels of lactate in frozen tissues, culture medium and cells were determined using commercially available L- Lactate Assay kit (65331, Abcam) following the manufacturer’ s instructions.

## Seahorse assay
The extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) of the THP-1 cells, HUVECs and NSFs were assessed according to the manufacturer’s protocol using a Seahorse XFe96 Analyzer (Agilent Technologies, US). On the day of the assay, cells were seeded into a Seahorse 96-well microplate (103794-100, Agilent Technologies) at a density of 2 × 104 cells (THP-1 cells and HUVECs) or 1 × 104 cells (NSFs) per well, followed by high substrate stiffness or THF-β treatment for 10 days before measurement. The next day, the growth medium in the wells was replaced with Seahorse XF DMEM medium (103680-100, Agilent Technologies), which was freshly supplemented with glutamine (4 mM) and warmed to 37 °C. For ECAR measurement, glucose, oligomycin and 2-deoxyglucose were added to final concentrations of 10 mM, 2 μM and 100 mM, respectively. The ECAR were measured three times at baseline and three times after each drug was added, with the average taken from multiple measurements calculated. The ECAR was normalized to the cell number at the end of the experiments. Data were analyzed using Seahorse XF 96 Wave software (version 2.2), and the results were represented as mpH/min.

## Immunohistochemistry
The skin and scar tissues from patients and mice models were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, G1101) overnight and embedded in paraffin, and 5-µm-thick sections were prepared. Tissue sections were deparaffinized, followed by rehydrated in xylene and graded concentrations of ethanol. Quenching of endogenous peroxidase activity was achieved using 3% hydrogen peroxide, while antigen retrieval was performed with an EDTA buffer. The tissue was uniformly covered with 3% BSA in the tissue chemical circle and closed at room temperature for 30 min. Then, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C in a humid chamber with the indicated primary antibodies. The DAB chromogenic reagent for histochemical kit (Servicebio, G1212) was employed to visualize the staining after 1 h incubation with secondary antibodies at room temperature. The antibodies used in this study are listed in the Supplementary Table  .

## Immunofluorescence staining
The skin and scar tissues from patients and mice models were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, G1101) overnight and embedded in paraffin, and 5-µm-thick sections were prepared. Put the tissue section in 3 changes of xylene, 10 min each, then dehydrate in 3 changes of pure ethanol for 5 min each, wash in distilled water. After the antigen retrieval was performed, it is naturally cooled. Put the slide for 5 min PBS (PH7.4) and shake it on a decoloring shaker for 3 times, each time for 5 minutes. Thereafter, 10% BSA was used to block the tissue at room temperature for 60 min. Thereafter, the tissues were incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C. After washing thrice with PBS, the tissues were incubated in the dark with secondary antibodies for 1 h and DAPI for 10 min at room temperature. Images were captured using a laser confocal scanning microscope (Zeiss, Germany). The antibodies used in this study are listed in the Supplementary Table  .
For cells Immunofluorescence staining, NSFs or HSFs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, G1101) for 20 min and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Servicebio, G1204) for 20 min at room temperature. Thereafter, 3% BSA was used to block the cells at room temperature for 60 min. Cells were subsequently incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C. After washing three times with PBST, the cells were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h and with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Beyotime) in the dark for 10 min at room temperature. Images were captured using an FSX100 microscope (Olympus, Japan). Quantitative analysis of colocalization was performed using the ImageJ software (v1.53, USA) equipped with the JACoP Plugin. The antibodies used in this study are listed in the Supplementary Table  .

## H&E, Masson, and Sirius red staining
Wound and scar tissue at the dorsal skin of mice were studied using tissue cross-sections. At 14 days post full-thickness skin wound modeling, the wound and surrounding normal skin were dissected out and embedded within an OCT compound (Sakura, 4583). The portion containing the wound was cut consecutively into 5 μm sections. To assess the wound healing, scar formation, collagen content, and fibrosis level, sections were stained with H&E staining (C0105, Beyotime, China), Masson (G1006, Servicebio, China), and Sirius red (PH1099, Phygene, China) for fibrosis analysis according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Images were captured using a bright-field microscope (CX33, Olympus, Japan). Quantitative analysis was performed using the ImageJ software (v1.53, USA).

## Molecular docking
Structural predictions of the MCT1 protein (PDB ID: 7cko) and COL11A1 protein (UniProt ID: P12107) were initially conducted using AlphaFold3. Subsequent protein structure optimization involved protonation state determination at physiological conditions (pH 7.0) through the H + + web server (version 3.0), followed by Amber14SB partial charge assignment using UCSF Chimera software. Molecular docking simulations were performed with AutoDock Vina (version, 1.2.5) employing the empirical iterative scoring function ITScorePP, where negative binding scores indicate favorable molecular interactions with magnitude reflecting binding affinity strength. The docking protocol was configured to generate 100 output conformations, with the top 10 scored conformations selected for analysis. The highest-ranked conformation based on docking score underwent three-dimensional structural visualization using PyMOL (version, 2.5.7). For lactate introduction studies, Gasteiger partial charges were assigned via the prepare_ligand4 script in MGLtools (version, 1.4.2). Subsequent docking experiments with AutoDock Vina (version, 1.2.5) utilized the lactate-binding site as the docking centroid within a cubic box (15 Å edge length), generating 20 conformations that were ranked by binding scores. The conformation exhibiting maximal absolute score magnitude was selected for subsequent analytical procedures.

## Molecular dynamics simulations
The molecular dynamics simulations were conducted using the Gromacs (version, 2023.2) software package. The AMBER99SB-ILDN force field was employed for the protein system, while small molecules were processed through AmberTools21 to acquire AM1-BCC charges and parameterized using the GAFF force field. The TIP3P water model was implemented within a cubic periodic boundary system encompassing both protein and ligand components. Non-bonded interactions were treated with a 1.0 nm cutoff for both electrostatic and van der Waals forces, complemented by the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method for long-range electrostatic corrections. Simulations utilized a 2 fs integration time step under isothermal-isobaric conditions (303.15 K maintained via V-rescale thermostating and 1 bar pressure regulated through Parrinello-Rahman barostat). The system underwent sequential energy minimization, followed by 200 ps NVT ensemble equilibration and 100 ps NPT ensemble equilibration. Production dynamics were extended to 100 ns for trajectory sampling. Structural stability was analyzed through root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) calculations using native Gromacs utilities. Binding free energy estimations were performed via the gmx_MMPBSA toolkit (version 1.6.4) employing molecular mechanics/Poisson-Boltzmann surface area methodology.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0). Image analysis was performed using Fiji (ImageJ). All samples and mice were randomly allocated to experimental groups on a per-experiment basis. Because measurements are quantitative, data from the experiments are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise described. After using the Shapiro–Wilk test and the Brown–Forsythe tests to confirm that the data were normally distributed and equal in variance, appropriate statistical tests were selected. The two groups were compared using Student’ s unpaired t-test. Multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Where data did not conform to a normal distribution in both groups, analysis was performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical parameters can be found in figures and figure legends.
