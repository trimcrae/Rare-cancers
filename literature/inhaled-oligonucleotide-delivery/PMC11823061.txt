## Abstract
Myofibroblasts accumulation contributes to airway remodeling, with the mechanisms being poorly understood. It is steroid-insensitive and has not been therapeutically targeted in asthma. In this study, we explored the potential of yes-associated protein (YAP) as a therapeutic target for myofibroblasts formation in asthma, by revealing the novel role and mechanisms by which YAP activation in type II alveolar epithelial (ATII) cells promotes the fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition in vitro and in vivo. By performing immunofluorescence staining, we showed that myofibroblasts were increased in the bronchial walls and alveolar parenchyma in clinical asthmatic and house dust mite (HDM)-induced mouse lung samples. This was accompanied by YAP overexpression and nuclear translocation in ATII cells, and connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) upregulation. In vitro, HDM or combination of rhIL-1β with rhTNF-α upregulated and activated YAP in human primary ATII cells and A549 cells, but not in the bronchial epithelial cells, BEAS-2B. This effect was mediated by F-actin polymerization and could be suppressed by pretreatment with latrunculin A but not budesonide. Inhibition of YAP/transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) in A549 cells by pretreatment with YAP/TAZ siRNA or verteporfin, but not budesonide, impaired the fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition in vitro. In vivo, verteporfin partly or completely prevented HDM-induced bronchial or alveolar myofibroblast accumulation, and significantly suppressed CTGF expression and collagen deposition in mouse lungs, without profoundly affecting airway inflammation. Our results provide novel mechanistic insights into airway remodeling, and holds promise for the development of novel therapeutic strategies.

## Introduction
Airway remodeling is the key feature of asthma, which is characterized by airway cellular and structural changes due to goblet cell hypertrophy, airway smooth muscle (ASM) cell hyperplasia, the accumulation of fibroblasts and myofibroblasts, and changes in the composition and mass of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins [ ]. Corticosteroids are the standard treatment for asthma; however, airway remodeling and airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) are hardly reversed, making this disease incurable [ , ]. Studies on childhood asthma indicated that epithelial damage and subepithelial fibrosis occur early in infancy, before airway inflammation can be detected [ ]. Furthermore, airway remodeling correlates with asthma severity and exhibits significant structural and cellular differences between controlled and uncontrolled asthma [ ], which highlights the importance of targeting airway remodeling in disease control. Despite the importance, our knowledge of the development of airway remodeling remains incomplete, and effective targeting strategies are urgently needed.
Myofibroblasts are the main cell type responsible for subepithelial fibrosis in asthma, which is attributed to hyperactive myofibroblast production of ECM proteins [ ]. Moreover, myofibroblasts may also play important roles in sustained airway inflammation and AHR development. This is associated with the ability of myofibroblasts to overproduce inflammatory mediators and the unique persistent contractile apparatus of myofibroblasts compared to ASM cells [ , ]. Clinical observations suggested that myofibroblasts accumulate in not only subepithelial but also alveolar compartments in asthmatic patients and are higher in uncontrolled asthma [ , ]. Although experimental evidence remains limited, it has been suggested that the accumulation of myofibroblasts in the lung periphery is associated with the deposition of ECM components and alterations in peripheral lung mechanics [ , ]. In patients with uncontrolled asthma, abnormalities in acinar ventilation heterogeneity (Sacin), rather than in conducting airways (Scond), have been negatively correlated with FEV1 (forced expiratory volume in one second, percent predicted) and positively correlated with the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) score, underscoring the significance of peripheral lung dysfunction in determining asthma severity [ ]. Furthermore, in well-controlled asthma, persistent and irreversible acinar ventilation heterogeneity has been consistently observed [ ]. Importantly, this abnormality in asthmatic peripheral lung function is independent of eosinophilic inflammation and refractory to inhaled corticosteroid treatment, indicating the significance of tissue remodeling in peripheral lung function [ ]. These studies underscore a potential role of myofibroblasts in influencing not only the conducting airways but also the peripheral lung function, and position this cell type as a possible therapeutic target. While the deposition of myofibroblasts in the bronchial wall has been identified and linked to multiple resources involving fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition (FMT), epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and fibrocytes, the accumulation of myofibroblasts in the lung parenchyma remains poorly understood [ , , ].
Yes-associated protein (YAP, also known as YAP1) and its homolog transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ, also known as WWTR1), are the core downstream effectors of the Hippo signaling cascade, which is a fundamental pathway that was firstly identified in Drosophila and controls organ size and tissue regeneration [ ]. YAP is activated via nuclear translocation and binding to TEA domain transcription factors (TEADs) to promote the transcription of multiple target genes, including profibrotic factors such as connective tissue growth factor (CTGF) [ ]. Thus, it is not surprising that YAP has been implicated in fibrosis across multiple organs and diseases [ – ]. In the liver, YAP activity correlates with the accumulation of reactive ductal cells and myofibroblasts, which are essential in the progression of fibrosis in non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) [ ]. Using a CCL4-induced liver fibrosis mouse model, Du and colleagues demonstrated that YAP regulates hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation, ferroptosis susceptibility, and senescence, with its inhibition reducing fibrosis by promoting HSC senescence [ ]. Similarly, in the pancreas, YAP has been suggested to activate pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs), leading to enhanced fibrotic activity [ ]. In idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), YAP activity is markedly upregulated in epithelial cells, where it interacts with mTOR and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways to promote epithelial cell proliferation, migration, and loss of polarity, contributing to alveolar remodeling and fibrotic tissue deposition [ ]. Despite these findings, the potential role of YAP in fibrotic changes in asthma and whether it can be therapeutically targeted remain largely unexplored. Therefore, the current study aimed to verify whether YAP plays a role and could be a potential therapeutic target in myofibroblasts formation in asthma, and explore the underlying mechanisms.

## Materials and methods

## Study design
To determine the clinical significance of YAP activation in asthma, we collected lung sections from asthmatic and non-asthmatic donors, and examined YAP and CTGF expression, and myofibroblasts accumulation by performing immunofluorescence staining. The mechanisms by which YAP activation in ATII cells promotes myofibroblast formation were investigated in vitro with at least three replicates. In vivo studies were conducted using an HDM-induced asthma mouse model to verify our clinical observations, and to explore the therapeutic potential of inhibiting YAP in asthma. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups, and all histological analysis were performed in a double-blind manner.

## Patient characteristics
Paraffin-embedded lung sections were obtained from 15 lobectomy patients, including 7 asthmatics and 8 non-asthmatics, at the Fifth Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University, from 2019 to 2021 (demographic data outlined in Table  ). All patients were also diagnosed with lung cancer, and the lung samples were collected from normal regions at least 5 cm away from the tumor. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University (approval no: K22-1).
 

## HDM-induced mouse asthma model
Female BALB/c mice aged 6–8 w were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China) and maintained in specific-pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12/12 h light–dark cycle. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University. The HDM-induced asthma mouse model was established following our previously reported protocol [ ]. The mice were sensitized by an intraperitoneal (ip) injection of 20 μg of HDM extract (XPB820D3A25, Greer Laboratories, Lenoir, North Carolina, USA) plus 1 mg of aluminum hydroxide (A1577, Sigma Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) in 0.1 mL of saline on d 0 and d 14, followed by intranasal instillation with 10 μg of HDM in 30 μL of saline on d 21, 23 and 25. Control mice received ip injection of 0.1 mL saline and intranasal instillation of 30 μL of saline. For the drug treatment groups, the HDM-sensitized mice were additionally pretreated with an ip injection of 50 mg/kg verteporfin (HY-B0146, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, New Jersey, USA) or intranasal instillation of 1 mg/kg budesonide (HY-13580, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, New Jersey, USA) 1 h or 0.5 h prior to each HDM intranasal challenge, respectively. Moreover, 2.5% or 6.7% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Hybri-Max™, Sigma, Darmstadt, Germany) was administered by ip injection or intranasal instillation as the vehicle control. Twenty-four hours after the last challenge, the mice were sacrificed with a cocktail of 2,2,2-tribromoethanol (500 mg/kg) mixed with dexmedetomidine hydrochloride (0.5 mg/kg), after which serum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), and lung lobes were collected for subsequent measurements, following the procedure that we previously reported [ , ].

## Cell culture and study design
The human primary ATII cells were purchased from iCell Bioscience Inc. (Shanghai, China), and maintained in the iCell primary epithelial cell culture medium (PriM-iCell-001, iCell). The primary ATII cells at passage 2 and 3 were used for the experiment. The A549 cell line was purchased from ATCC (VA, USA) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% pen/strep (Gibco). The HFL-1 cell line (human lung fibroblasts) was purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and cultured in Ham’s F-12 K (PM150910, Procell) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% pen/strep (Gibco). The BEAS-2B cell line was obtained from ATCC (VA, USA) and cultured in BEGM ™ Bronchial Epithelial Cell Growth Medium BulletKit ™ (CC-3171 & CC-4175, Lonza). To determine whether YAP can be activated in vitro, the primary ATII cells, A549, and BEAS-2B cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105/well, starved for 24 h and stimulated with 10 µg/mL HDM or 1 ng/mL recombinant human interleukin-1 beta (rhIL-1β) with or without 1 ng/mL recombinant human tumor necrosis factor alpha (rhTNF-α), and cell lysates were collected at 0, 15, 30, 60, 120, and 240 min. In the drug pretreatment groups, A549 cells were pretreated with 1 µmol/L budesonide (HY-13580, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, New Jersey, USA), 0.25 µmol/L latrunculin A (Lat-A, GC15671, GlpBio, USA), or 1 µmol/L verteporfin (HY-B0146, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, New Jersey, USA), followed by treatment with 10 µg/mL HDM or 1 ng/mL rhIL-1β. For FMT analysis, HFL-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105/well, serum starved for 24 h and stimulated with increasing concentrations of HDM (10, 50, 100 µg/mL), rhIL-1β (1, 10, 20 ng/mL), and recombinant human transforming growth factor-beta 1 (rhTGF-β1, 1, 10, 20 ng/mL) for 4 h and then lysed. Additional HFL-1 cells were incubated with the supernatants of A549 cells stimulated with HDM or rhIL-1β for 48 h. Control HFL-1 cells were incubated with culture medium containing with HDM or rhIL-1β only. Then, the cells were fixed in 4% formalin for immunocytochemical staining.

## Immunofluorescence staining for the lung sections
Immunofluorescence staining was performed following the protocol that we previously reported [ ]. Lung slides were dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated in descending concentrations of ethanol to distilled water, followed by antigen retrieval in the citric sodium solution (P0083, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and heating for 20 min at 95 °C. After washing briefly in water, the sections were permeabilized with 0.3% of Triton-X 100 (P0096, Beyotime, China) for 20 min. The sections were then blocked in 5% goat serum (PH0424, Phygene, China) supplemented with 1% BSA (G5001, Servicebio, China) and 0.3% of Triton-X 100 for 1 h. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, B548117, Sangon Biotech, China), the sections were incubated overnight with the primary antibodies against vimentin (1:100 diluted, 5741, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA), alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA, 1:1000 diluted, A2547, Sigma Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany), yes-associated protein (YAP, 1:100 diluted, 14,074, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA), non-S127 phospho- YAP (non-pS127 YAP, 1:100 diluted, ab205270, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), pro-surfactant protein C (pro-SPC, 1: 200 diluted, ab3786, Millipore, Burlington, USA), connective tissue growth factor (CTGF, 1: 100 diluted, sc-101586, Santa Cruz, USA), diluted in PBS with 1% BSA. In parallel settings, the isotype controls or secondary antibody-only controls were prepared by incubating the sections overnight with mouse IgG isotype (1:100 diluted, 5415S, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA), rabbit IgG isotype (1:100 diluted, 3900S, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA), or PBS containing 1% BSA only to distinguish nonspecific signals and confirm the specificity of the observed staining. The results for the secondary antibody-only controls are shown in Figure . On the second day, the slides were washed and then incubated with Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200 diluted, 4414S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) or Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (1:200 diluted, 4408S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) for 1 h. This was followed by nuclear staining with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, 1:1000 diluted, 564907, BD Biosciences, USA) for 10 min. After intense washing in PBS, the slides were mounted with Antifade (PH0429-2, Phygene, China) and stored in the dark at 4 °C till required.

## Mouse serum and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) collection
The mice were anesthetized by the cocktail of 2,2,2-tribromoethanol (500 mg/kg) mixed with dexmedetomidine hydrochloride (0.5 mg/kg), and the blood was collected through cardiac puncture. After collection, the blood samples were allowed to sit for 2–4 h at room temperature before centrifuging at 2,400g for 10 min. The serum was carefully collected and stored at −80 °C until needed. Following the blood collection, the lung lobes were aspirated with 3 times repeats of 0.5 mL/each by a 20 G needle (4253566-03, BRAUN, Germany) to collect the BALF. The BALF was centrifuged at 1000g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected and stored at −80 °C till needed. The collected cell pellets in the BALF were resuspended in 0.85% ammonium chloride (NH4Cl) (cat no. 213330, Sigma Aldrich, USA) and incubated for 5 min at room temperature to lyse the red blood cells. This was followed by cell centrifugation at 1000g for 5 min at 4 °C, and cell resuspension in 200 μL of RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA, A1933, Sigma Aldrich, USA). Total cell count was performed under the microscope after mixing the cells with 0.4% typan blue (JC0314, Jingxin, China) at a 1:1 ratio.

## Total RNA extraction and real-time quantitative RT-PCR (RT-qPCR)
Total RNA was extracted from the mouse lung using FastPure Cell/ Tissue Total RNA Isolated Kit V2 (RC112-01, Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and quality of isolated RNA were determined by the Nanodrop2000 UV–Vis Spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher, USA). 1 μg of RNA was subjected to cDNA synthesis using the NovoScript® Plus All-in-one 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (E047-01A, NovoProtein, China). The qPCR was performed in duplicates on a CFX96™ Real-Time system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USAA) using a SYBR Green-based qPCR kit (E096-01B, Novoprotein, China). For each qPCR reaction, 1 μL of cDNA (10 ng) mixed with 19 μL of qPCR master mix containing 0.8 μL of 5 μM primers (5 μmol/L each for the forward and reverse primer) was added to a 96-well PCR plate with plate sealing. The qPCR program was run as follows: 94 °C for 10 min, 40 cycles of 94 °C for 15 s, and 60 °C for 1 min. All mouse primers were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and the sequences were listed in Table . Relative gene expression was calculated using the comparative method 2−ΔΔCt by normalizing to the house-keeping Rpl13a.

## Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining
The left lung lobe of the mouse was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PH0427, Phygene, China) for 24 h, embedded in paraffin fixation, and cut into 5 μm thick sections. The lung sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (PH0516-2, Phygene, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Additional sections were stained for collagen using Masson’s trichrome staining kit (G1340, Solarbio, China) by following the manual. The Pannoramic digital scanner (3D Histech, P250 FLASH, Budapest, Hungary) was used to scan the images, and semi-quantification analysis was performed to determine the airway inflammatory cells infiltration and collagen deposition scores (5 bronchi/mouse and 5 random views of alveolar parenchyma/mouse were scored and averaged) following the criteria listed in Figure & .

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
The total and house dust mite (HDM)-specific IgE levels in the serum were determined using the BD OptEIA™ Mouse IgE ELISA kit (555248, BD Biosciences, USA) and Mouse Anti-HDM IgE Antibody Assay Kit (3037, Chondrex, USA) respectively. The levels of interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-13 in the BALF were measured using BD OptEIA™ Mouse IL-4 Set (555232, BD Biosciences, USA) and IL-13 Mouse Uncoated ELISA kit (88-7137-88, ThermoFisher, USA), respectively. All procedures were following the manufacturers’ instructions.

## Immunocytochemistry staining for the cell culture
Cells seeded on the coverslip (BS-14-RC, biosharp, China) were fixed with 4% formaldehyde at room temperature for 20 min. After washing in PBS, the cells were incubated with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 15 min. This was followed by cell blocking in PBS containing 5% goat serum and 1% BSA at room temperature for 30 min. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies against vimentin (1:100 diluted, 5741, Cell signaling, USA), alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA, 1:1000 diluted, A2547, Sigma Aldrich, USA), and YAP (1:100 diluted, 14,074, Cell signaling) diluted in blocking buffer. After three washes with PBST (1 × PBS containing 0.05% v/v Tween-20), cells were incubated with secondary antibodies Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200 diluted, 4414S, Cell signaling, USA) or Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (1:200 diluted, 4408S, Cell signaling, USA) diluted in blocking buffer supplemented with 3% BSA for 1 h. After washing, 1 μg/mL of DAPI diluted in PBS was applied to stain the nuclei for 10 min. Additional cell slides were incubated with TRITC Phalloidin (1:1000 diluted, MX4405, MKBio, China) in PBS for 1 h to stain F-actin. Finally, the slides were mounted with Antifade and images taken by the Olympus FV1000 confocal microscope (Olympus, USA) under 200 × magnification.

## Small interfering RNA (siRNA) transfection
YAP, TAZ or nontargeting (NT) siRNA and GP-transfect-Mate transfection reagents were purchased from GenePharma Biotechnology (China) (siRNA sequencing listed in Table ). A549 cells were seeded on 6-well plates at a cell density of 1 × 105/well, cultured for 2 d to reach 80% confluence, and then transfected with YAP and TAZ siRNA (20 µmol/L) or NT siRNA (20 µmol/L, negative control) with GP-transfect-Mate transfection reagent (5 µL/well). After 2 d, the siRNA-transfected A549 cells were lysed or stimulated with 10 µg/mL HDM or 1 ng/mL rhIL-1β in serum-deprived medium for subsequent experiments.

## Protein extraction and Western blotting
Protein extraction and Western blotting were performed following the protocol that we previously reported [ , ]. The cultured cells or the mouse lung tissues were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (P1045, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) on ice for 30 min. The lysates were then centrifuged at 18,000g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected and stored at −80 °C until needed. Soluble protein concentrations were determined using the BCA protein assay kit (PH0326, Phygene, China) following the manual’s instructions. Protein lysates (20–50 µg) were denatured and separated on a 10% sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). This was followed by membrane blocking with Tris-buffered saline/0.05% Tween-20 (TBST) containing 5% w/v skim milk for 1 h at room temperature. After washing with TBST three times for 10 min each, the membranes were incubated at 4 °C overnight with the primary antibodies against YAP (1:1000 diluted, sc-101199, Santa Cruz), phosphor-S127 YAP (1:1000 diluted, 13,008, Cell Signaling Technology), TAZ (1:1000 diluted, ab224239, Abcam), non-pS127 YAP (1:1000 diluted, ab205270, Abcam), vimentin (1:1000 diluted, 5741, Cell signaling), α-SMA (1:1000 diluted, A2547, Sigma), CTGF (1:1000 diluted, ab6992, Abcam), glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH, 1:5000 diluted, 2118S, Cell signaling Technology), or β-actin (1:100,000 diluted, AC026, ABclonal, USA). Subsequently, the membranes were incubated for 1 h with the secondary horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated IgG (1:3000 diluted for HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG, 7074S, Cell signaling; 1:5000 diluted for HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG, 7076S, Cell Signaling Technology). After membrane washing with TBST, enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) was conducted using the GenSuper ECL kit (JXE0011, Jingxin, China), and the protein bands were visualized on the iBright FL1500 system (Thermofisher, CN, USA). Densitometry was performed using ImageJ software (1.53c, Wayne Rasband, National Institutes of Health, USA), and GAPDH or β-actin were used to normalize the equal sample loading.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.3.0, USA). An unpaired t test with Welch’s correction was used for comparisons between two groups. For multiple comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by an uncorrected Dunn’s test or nonparametric test followed by an uncorrected Fisher’s LSD test was used where appropriate. The data are presented as the mean ± SEM, and a P value less than 0.05 was considered significant. All numbers (n) in the figure legends represent biological replicates, and the graphs are generated using data from all biological replicates.

## Results

## Myofibroblasts accumulated in clinical asthmatic and HDM-induced mouse lung samples
Lung sections were collected from asthmatic and non-asthmatic donors and stained for vimentin and α-SMA. Non-muscle cells that were positive for both cell markers were considered myofibroblasts. Thus, we observed significant upregulation of the staining intensity of vimentin or α-SMA, as well as peribronchial and alveolar myofibroblast numbers in asthmatic lung sections compared to non-asthmatic controls (Fig.  a–d). Furthermore, we established an HDM-induced asthma mouse model, which exhibited typical asthma features, including structural changes and inflammatory cell infiltration in bronchial and alveolar walls, significant increases in serum total IgE and the transcript levels of IL-4, IL-6, and IL-13 in the lungs compared to those in saline-treated control mice (Fig.  a, b). With this mouse model, we consistently observed increased staining intensities for vimentin and α-SMA, as well as myofibroblast accumulation in the bronchial and alveolar areas in HDM-induced mouse lungs (Fig.  c–f).
 

## YAP was activated in ATII cells in asthmatic lung samples and CTGF was upregulated
We observed a ubiquitous upregulation of YAP expression in asthmatic alveolar cells, and strikingly, YAP was preferentially translocated to the nucleus in ATII cells, which stained positive for pro-surfactant protein C (pro-SPC) (Fig.  a). This finding was consistent with our observations in the HDM-induced asthma mouse model (Fig.  b, c) and was further supported by the increased levels of total YAP and non-phosphorylated S127 (non-pS127) active form of YAP in the lung lysates of HDM-treated mice (Fig.  d). In comparison, S127 phosphorylated YAP (pS127 YAP), representing the nonactive form of YAP, as well as the ratio of the active form to total YAP remained unchanged (Fig.  d). Next, we showed that CTGF was significantly upregulated in both clinical asthmatic and HDM-induced mouse lungs compared with controls (Fig.  ). Strikingly, a significant portion of CTGF signals were found to be localized around ATII cells, which might suggest a role of YAP activation in ATII cells in mediating CTGF overproduction (Fig.  a, b).
 

## HDM activated YAP in ATII cells rather than in bronchial epithelial cells
We found that non-pS127 YAP was significantly upregulated and underwent nuclear translocation in alveolar cells to a higher extent than in the bronchial epithelium (Fig.  a, b). Moreover, alveolar cell numbers also increased in HDM-induced mouse lungs (Fig.  b). Next, we stimulated human primary ATII cells or bronchial epithelial cells, BEAS-2B, with HDM. As a result, HDM could upregulate not only the total YAP but also non-pS127 active YAP levels in ATII cells at different time points (Fig.  c). The YAP homolog TAZ also tended to increase at 30- and 60-min after HDM stimulation, although statistical significance was not reached. Interestingly, the ratio of non-pS127 active YAP to total YAP remained unchanged over time (Fig.  c), suggesting that HDM induced YAP activation primarily through the upregulation of YAP expression in ATII cells. In comparison, HDM failed to induce expression of non-pS127 active YAP or TAZ in BEAS-2B cells, but downregulated active YAP or TAZ at 240 min (Fig.  d). These in vitro results were consistent with our in vivo observations that HDM-induced YAP activation was more prominent in ATII cells than in the bronchial epithelium.
 

## HDM or rhIL-1β induced YAP activation through cytoskeleton reorganization, which could be suppressed by latrunculin A (Lat-A) but not budesonide
Since the human primary ATII cells spontaneously differentiated to type I alveolar epithelial (ATI) cells after 2 passages in culture, we turn to use A549 cells, a widely used ATII cell model for mechanistic studies. By stimulating A549 cells with HDM, we consistently observed upregulated expression of total and non-pS127 active YAP in A549 cells at 15, 30, 60, and 120 min, without significant changes in the ratio of active YAP (Fig.  a). The expression of TAZ was also significantly increased at the same timepoints (Fig.  a). To find out whether inflammatory mediators are also capable to induce YAP activation, we stimulate A549 cells with the combination of rhIL-1β and rhTNF-α, two key pro-inflammatory mediators in asthma pathogenesis. Strikingly, rhIL-1β combined with rhTNF-α also induced total YAP, non-pS127 active YAP, and TAZ in A549 cells, but to different magnitudes (Fig.  b). In comparison, the ratio of active YAP decreased at 15, 30, and 60 min following the combined stimulation and returned to baseline levels after 120 min (Fig.  b). These findings, along with previous observations, suggesting that HDM or the combination of cytokines primarily regulate YAP activation by modulating its expression levels rather than altering the proportion of its active form. Based on this, the subsequent experiments primarily focus on the alteration of YAP expression in different settings.
 Next, we sought to explore the underlying mechanism of YAP upregulation. Considering that IL-1β and TNF-α may activate distinct signaling pathways, despite some overlap in mechanisms such as NF-κB activation, we stimulated the cells with rhIL-1β alone in the subsequent experimental settings to minimize interference from signaling crosstalk. Strikingly, evidence suggests that IL-1β expression is associated with neutrophilic airway inflammation, steroid resistance, and increased asthma severity, highlighting the clinical significance of this study [ ]. As such, the activation of YAP by HDM or rhIL-1β in A549 cells was validated by the immunofluorescence staining, and we observed a significant upregulation of YAP staining intensity and nuclear translocation following stimulation (Fig.  c). Importantly, this upregulation was not inhibited by the pretreatment of budesonide, a clinically used corticosteroid to treat asthma (Fig.  c). By performing Western blotting, we also showed that budesonide failed to inhibit the upregulated expression of YAP and TAZ after stimulation with HDM or rhIL-1β (Fig.  d). Not only that, TAZ expression tended to increase in the budesonide pretreated groups, although it did not reach statistical significance (Fig.  d). Next, we examined F-actin levels because YAP and TAZ are mechanical sensors and may be regulated by cytoskeletal reorganization [ ]. In A549 cells, HDM or rhIL-1β significantly induced F-actin polymerization at 15 min, and YAP was upregulated and translocated to the nucleus (Fig.  a). Consistently, budesonide did not inhibit YAP activation or F-actin polymerization. When we pretreated the cells with the potent F-actin polymerization inhibitor Lat-A, YAP and TAZ expression levels were significantly decreased in the HDM and rhIL-1β treatment groups (Fig.  b).
 

## HDM or rhIL-1β promoted CTGF production in A549 cells through YAP activation
We showed that increasing concentrations of HDM or rhIL-1β promoted CTGF expression in A549 cells which was comparable to that induced by TGF-β1 (Fig.  a). To explore whether this effect was mediated by YAP activation, we performed loss-of-function analysis by siRNA transfection. Compared with the nontargeting (NT) siRNA transfection control, efficient knockdown of YAP and TAZ in A549 cells significantly suppressed not only basal but also HDM- or rhIL-1β-induced CTGF production (Fig.  b). To confirm this finding, we next pretreated the cells with verteporfin, a potent inhibitor of YAP/TAZ capable of inhibiting both YAP/TAZ expression and activity. As a result, we found that verteporfin significantly inhibited YAP, TAZ, and CTGF expression levels induced by HDM or rhIL-1β (Fig.  c).
 

## YAP/TAZ activation in A549 cells promoted FMT in vitro through CTGF secretion
In HFL-1 cells, CTGF promoted the expression of α-SMA to a level comparable to that of rhTGF-β1 (Fig.  a, b), indicating that CTGF is a potent FMT stimulator. Next, we incubated HFL-1 cells with culture supernatants collected from A549 cells. While control culture medium containing HDM or rhIL-1β alone did not show any effect on α-SMA expression in HFL-1 cells, HDM- or rhIL-1β-stimulated A549 supernatants significantly increased α-SMA staining intensity in HFL-1 cells (Fig.  c). Importantly, this upregulation was greatly suppressed by pretreating A549 cells with verteporfin or YAP/TAZ siRNA (Fig.  c). In comparison, budesonide pretreatment only inhibited α-SMA in A549 cells in the HDM groups to some extent, but not in the rhIL-1β groups (Fig.  c).
 

## In vivo verteporfin treatment significantly suppressed myofibroblast formation and collagen deposition and had limited effects on airway inflammation
To verify the therapeutic potential of targeting YAP in asthma, we used verteporfin, or budesonide as the positive drug control, in the HDM-induced mouse asthma model. Strikingly, both verteporfin and budesonide significantly inhibited the peribronchial and alveolar myofibroblast accumulation in HDM-induced mouse lungs compared with the vehicle controls (Fig.  a, b). Moreover, compared with budesonide treatment, verteporfin exhibited a stronger inhibitory effect on alveolar myofibroblast accumulation (Fig.  b). This inhibition was accompanied by a significant reduction in CTGF expression in the verteporfin group (Fig.  c). Both verteporfin and budesonide significantly reduced collagen deposition in the alveolar areas of HDM-induced mouse lungs to similar levels, but the effect of verteporfin on peribronchial collagen deposition was milder than that of budesonide (Fig.  d). ELISA showed that verteporfin and budesonide similarly decreased total IgE or HDM-IgE levels in HDM-induced mouse serum (Fig.  a). Correspondingly, airway inflammatory cell infiltration scores were decreased in both drug treatment groups (Fig.  b). However, when inflammatory cell infiltration and cytokine secretion in BALF or cytokine transcript levels in the mouse lungs were compared, only budesonide showed significant inhibition of all factors, while verteporfin did not show any significant effects (Fig.  c, d).
 

## Discussion
While most studies on asthma have focused on large airways, it has been demonstrated that the pathological alterations in asthma are not only within the central airway but also in distal airways, including the alveoli [ ]. Importantly, the pathological changes in peripheral airways may have distinct importance in disease progression and control. Early studies on alveolar inflammation indicated that infiltrating eosinophils, macrophages, and CD4+ T cells in alveoli but not conducting airways were the main contributors to the loss of lung function in nocturnal asthma [ – ]. Additionally, mast cell-associated alveolar inflammation and alveolar T helper type-2 immunity have been linked to uncontrolled asthma [ , ]. Moreover, myofibroblast accumulation in the alveolar parenchyma is a key feature of uncontrolled asthma compared to controlled asthma and is accompanied by significant deposition of ECM proteins [ ]. This evidence raises questions about the mechanisms of alveolar myofibroblast formation, how alveolar myofibroblasts contribute to disease progression, and whether myofibroblast formation is sensitive to steroid treatment. Indeed, myofibroblast accumulation in the asthmatic alveolar parenchyma has been repeatedly reported; however, the pathological consequences of this effect, as well as the mechanisms of alveolar myofibroblast formation in asthma, remain unknown [ ]. In one clinical study by Kariyawasam HH and colleagues, submucosal myofibroblasts were increased at 24 h after allergen challenge and continued to increase until d 7, with diminished cellular inflammation. This increase correlated with increased ECM expression and airway reactivity at d 7, thus linking myofibroblasts to airway remodeling and AHR development [ ]. Despite a lack of direct evidence, it is reasonable to propose that alveolar myofibroblasts exert similar effects and contribute to structural changes and mechanics of small airways.
In our study, we consistently observed myofibroblast accumulation in both clinical asthmatic lung sections and HDM-induced asthmatic mouse lungs. Although we did not examine other cell markers of myofibroblasts, a previous report indicated that α-SMA-positive cells, particularly in the lung parenchyma, are characteristic of myofibroblasts under electron microscopy [ ]. More importantly, for the first time, we identified that YAP was activated in asthmatic ATII cells and played a role in myofibroblast formation in asthma. ATII cells are the most abundant epithelial cell population in alveolar walls and are an important physical and immunological barrier in tissue homeostasis [ ]. ATII cell impairment has been recognized as a key driving factor in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) pathogenesis [ ]. This effect has been linked to the loss of epithelial integrity due to ATII apoptosis and senescence, the profibrotic ATII phenotype interacting with mesenchymal cells, and the modulatory function of ATII cells in orchestrating adaptive and innate immunity [ – ]. Comparatively, the role of ATII cells in asthma has rarely been studied. In the current study, we, for the first time, provided evidence that ATII plays a role in myofibroblast formation in asthma through YAP signaling. We showed that targeting YAP/TAZ with verteporfin completely prevented alveolar myofibroblasts in vivo and decreased alveolar CTGF expression. Notably, peribronchial myofibroblast accumulation also decreased with verteporfin treatment but was less prominent, which indicate other mechanisms involving airway resident and infiltrated cells, such as ASM cells, monocytes, eosinophils, neutrophils, and vascular smooth muscle cells.
The role of YAP activation in promoting fibroblast activation and maturation has been recognized in numerous reports [ , , ]. The mechanisms are associated with signaling cascades in fibroblasts that are dependent or independent of TGF-β signaling to promote FMT, and linked to matrix stiffness and cytoskeletal regulation [ , ]. In addition to fibroblasts and myofibroblasts, an increased ratio of nuclear located YAP was also identified in ATII cells in IPF patients, along with a significant upregulation of total YAP levels in IPF and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) patients’ lung tissues [ ]. This phenomenon was supported by an earlier single-cell RNA sequencing study that recognized aberrant activation of Hippo/YAP in IPF epithelial cells [ ]. This activation may lead to epithelial dysfunction and the formation of a profibrotic ATII phenotype, which has not yet been fully elucidated. In our study, we demonstrated that YAP was localized to the nucleus in asthmatic ATII cells more prominently than in bronchial epithelial cells, and there was ubiquitous upregulation of total and non-pS127 YAP expression. This finding suggests an overlapping role of YAP in fibrotic changes in chronic respiratory diseases, including asthma, IPF, and COPD. Importantly, we identified that allergens, such as HDM, and rhIL-1β and are both potent stimuli that activate YAP in ATII cells. Indeed, HDM-induced injury in airway epithelial cells, such as the loss of epithelial integrity and DNA damage, as well as HDM-induced epithelial-derived cytokine secretion, are recognized as key events that initiate asthma pathogenesis [ , ], and our study provides another mechanism by which HDM exposure contributes to airway remodeling. In addition to HDM, IL-1β and TNF-α also promoted YAP upregulation and nuclear translocation. Interestingly, these two proinflammatory cytokines have been linked to a severe, steroid-refractory asthma phenotype [ , ], and the capacity of IL-1β and TNF-α to activate YAP and induce CTGF expression in ATII cells provides a mechanism underlying persistent airway remodeling in severe asthma.
The evidence that myofibroblast accumulation, particularly in alveolar walls, is the key feature of uncontrolled asthma raised the concern that myofibroblast formation is steroid-resistant [ ], and targeting myofibroblast formation is of beneficial in reversing steroid insensitivity for asthma control. However, the direct effects of steroids on myofibroblast formation have not been fully investigated, and the conclusions of previous reports are controversial [ , ]. This inconsistency could be due to differences between human asthma and experimental mouse models, routes and durations of steroid treatments, and the involvement of inflammatory factors. In our study, budesonide treatment effectively attenuated myofibroblast accumulation in vivo not only in peribronchial areas but also in the lung parenchyma. However, the in vitro study showed that budesonide could not suppress the FMT induced by IL-1β-stimulated A549 cell supernatant. Considering the capacity of inhaled budesonide to inhibit inflammation, we proposed that budesonide-mediated prevention of myofibroblast accumulation in vivo was largely dependent on a decrease in the secretion of inflammatory mediators. Comparatively, airway inflammation independent myofibroblast formation is insensitive to steroids, which needs to be targeted for persistent airway remodeling.
Our in vitro experiments suggested that YAP activation in ATII cells contributes to steroid resistance in myofibroblast formation and thus is a potential therapeutic target. Notably, the effect of steroids on YAP activation has been reported in breast cancer which demonstrated that glucocorticoid receptor stimulation increased YAP expression and activation in vitro and in vivo through cytoskeletal regulation [ ]. Although we did not perform an experiment to investigate whether budesonide directly affected YAP activation, we did observe an increasing trend in the expression of TAZ, the homolog of YAP, in budesonide-pretreated cells stimulated with HDM or IL-1β. Our results and the report in breast cancer mention above suggest that myofibroblast accumulation and accompanying ECM deposition are not only refractory to but also could be a side effect of long-term use of steroids in asthma patients.
There is an emerging role of YAP/TAZ in both innate and adaptive immunity. YAP and TAZ play important roles in the differentiation and function of Th17, regulatory T (Treg), and dendritic cells and exert context-dependent effects to promote or suppress immunity in diseases [ – ]. Additionally, YAP or TAZ interact with intracellular pattern recognition receptor signaling and are vital for antiviral or antimicrobial responses [ – ]. The contribution of YAP to regulating asthma immunity has been suggested by Zhou J and colleagues, which is implicated in asthma severity and linked to the Th17-cell differentiation [ ]. Our study did not focus on the role of YAP in the immune system, but we did find that verteporfin significantly reduced serum IgE levels in vivo that were comparable to the effect of budesonide, and the semi-quantitatively analyzed airway inflammatory cell infiltration scores were also decreased. However, when we further determined the total cell counts in BALF and measured the expression of asthma-associated Th2 cytokines, there was no statistic difference between the verteporfin group and the vehicle control group. These results suggest that YAP/TAZ plays a role in allergic reactions with limited effects on airway inflammation.
The differential trends in serum IgE levels, both total and HDM-specific, and Th2 cytokine expression in the BALF following verteporfin treatment are intriguing and may reflect the heterogeneity of asthma pathogenesis. While asthma is typically recognized as an allergic disorder mediated by IgE-mediated adaptive immunity, non-allergic asthma constitutes a significant portion of cases, with its prevalence varying across different age groups [ ]. Various cell types, including airway structural cells (e.g., airway epithelial cells) and innate immune cells (e.g., type 2 innate lymphoid cells [ILC2s] and dendritic cells), play critical roles in mediating IgE-independent airway inflammation in both allergic and non-allergic asthma [ – ]. ILC2s, in particular, are highly potent producers of Th2 cytokines, including IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, in response to allergens like HDM as well as epithelial-derived cytokines (e.g., IL-25, IL-33, and thymic stromal lymphopoietin [TSLP]) released during allergen challenges to the airway epithelium [ – ]. Notably, the activation of ILC2s is recognized as an early event in initiating eosinophilic inflammation, even in the absence of IgE [ ]. These findings underscore the importance of IgE-independent mechanisms in asthma pathogenesis and highlights diverse cellular players contributing to airway inflammation across different asthma endotypes.
In our study, verteporfin treatment in HDM-exposed mice resulted in a significant decrease in serum IgE levels, while the expression of Th2 cytokines remained unchanged. This suggests that inhibition of YAP has limited effects on IgE-independent airway inflammation, likely involving the activation of airway epithelial cells, innate immune cells, and the subsequent inflammatory cascades. Conversely, the results also imply that YAP may play a role in IgE production through pathways independent of Th2-type immunity. Although studies on YAP’s role in regulating B cells’ function are limited, evidence suggests that Mst1, an upstream regulator of YAP, modulates B-cell receptor (BCR) activation [ ]. This observation underscores the need for further exploration of YAP’s involvement in antibody production and its contribution to allergic reactions.
Our HDM-induced mouse model may also contribute to the observed differences in IgE and Th2 cytokine levels influenced by verteporfin. Numerous studies employing HDM-induced models with repeated intranasal HDM inhalation have demonstrated significant asthma-like features, such as eosinophilic airway inflammation and airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR). However, evidence suggests that these HDM-induced features are likely driven by the innate immune response to components within the HDM extract rather than an IgE-dependent allergic reaction [ ]. In contrast to human cases of HDM-allergic asthma, which are characterized by high serum levels of HDM-specific IgE, repeated intranasal HDM administration in mice induces only a weak IgE response, suggesting that a systemic sensitization phase prior to intranasal HDM challenge may be necessary [ ]. Moreover, in an HDM-induced rat model, the use of the adjuvant Alum™ was shown to be essential for HDM to elicit its full pro-inflammatory effects, significantly enhancing IgE production and airway inflammation [ ]. In light of these findings, we applied systemic sensitization with HDM in the presence of the adjuvant aluminum hydroxide, demonstrating that a relatively low dose of HDM administration could significantly induce total and HDM-specific IgE. Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying HDM-induced asthma features in murine models remain incompletely understood and may vary depending on experimental protocols, including HDM dosage, administration route, frequency, and duration [ ]. Therefore, it would be necessary to employ different models to validate our findings.
This study has several limitations. First, due to the difficulties in accessing tissues from asthma patients and healthy donors, nontumor lobectomy samples were included, and this might produce biases in observations. However, we consistently observed similar effects in the HDM-induced asthma model and thus believe that the impact of cancer on our study was minimal. Second, all asthmatic patients enrolled in this study received steroids treatment and thus we could not exclude a direct effect of steroids on YAP activation or myofibroblast accumulation. Comprehensive studies need to compare asthma patients who do or do not take steroids, as well as steroid-sensitive and steroid-refractory cases. Third, we focused on YAP in this study and did not differentiate the roles of YAP and TAZ in vitro and in vivo. In most studies, YAP and TAZ are homologous genes and play overlapping roles in regulating cellular function. However, differential roles of YAP and TAZ have also been identified in tumor biology [ ], and this needs to be examined in future investigations. Finally, we showed that verteporfin suppressed allergic reactions and airway inflammation in vivo, which may significantly affect myofibroblast formation and collagen deposition. In this context, more in-depth studies need to be performed to investigate the role of YAP in airway remodeling and airway inflammation.

## Conclusions
In the present study, we showed that YAP was significantly upregulated and nuclear translocated preferentially in asthmatic ATII cells, which was further verified in our HDM-induced asthma mouse model. By performing in vitro studies, we demonstrated that HDM, as well as rhIL-1β alone or in combination with rhTNF-α, were both able to activate YAP in ATII cells through F-actin polymerization, which was steroid insensitive. More importantly, inhibiting YAP in ATII cells significantly impaired the FMT in vitro, indicating the therapeutic potential of targeting YAP in asthma. To strengthen this finding, we further applied verteporfin, a potent YAP inhibitor, in the HDM-induced asthma mouse model, and found that verteporfin application partly or completely prevented bronchial or alveolar myofibroblasts accumulation in HDM-induced mice, and the collagen deposition was also reduced. This holds promise for the development of novel therapeutic strategies to target airway remodeling.
