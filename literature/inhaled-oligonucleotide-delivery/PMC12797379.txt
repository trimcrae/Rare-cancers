## Abstract

## Introduction
Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD), encompassing ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD), is increasingly prevalent in China. Traditional treatments often have limited efficacy and safety, whereas molecular targeted therapies provide more precise and effective options with fewer adverse effects. The pathogenesis of IBD involves dysregulated inflammatory pathways including NF-κB, IL-23/Th17, and TGF-β. Major targeted therapies include TNF-α inhibitors, which are effective for induction and maintenance of remission (particularly when combined with immunomodulators), integrin antagonists, JAK inhibitors, limited IL-6 pathway inhibitors, and emerging agents targeting additional pathways. Biomarkers such as miR-21 and transcriptomic signatures support personalized treatment selection. Molecular targeted therapy has evolved from single-target strategies to multi-pathway modulation, improving clinical management. Nevertheless, challenges persist, including primary non-response, secondary loss of response, and safety risks. Further research is needed to refine sequencing strategies, integrate validated biomarkers into clinical practice, and optimize risk–benefit profiles.

## Background
The global burden of IBD continues to rise, particularly in newly industrialized regions such as China, where urbanization and lifestyle changes have accelerated incidence rates. Conventional therapies—aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, and traditional immunomodulators—often fail to sustain long-term remission in moderate-to-severe cases and are associated with cumulative toxicities such as bone marrow suppression and organ injury, underscoring an urgent need for precision-based interventions. This review synthesizes recent advances in molecular targeted therapy for IBD, clarifies mechanisms underlying key targets, and evaluates the efficacy and safety of emerging agents to inform clinical decision-making and future research. We performed a comprehensive literature search (2014–2025) in PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library using keywords such as “inflammatory bowel disease,” “molecular targeted therapy,” “TNF inhibitors,” “JAK inhibitors,” and “biomarkers.” This search identified over 500 clinical trials, meta-analyses, and mechanistic studies. The evidence highlights the transformative role of targeted agents in increasing remission rates while revealing variability in response across patient subgroups and uncertainties regarding long-term safety. Core issues include optimizing therapeutic sequencing to prevent primary non-response and secondary loss of response, validating and integrating biomarkers (e.g., miR-21) for stratified treatment, and balancing efficacy of multi-pathway modulators against risks such as opportunistic infections and cardiovascular events.

## Introduction
IBD comprises chronic inflammatory disorders of the gastrointestinal tract, primarily UC and CD. In recent years, IBD prevalence has risen steadily in China [ ]. Compared with traditional regimens, molecular targeted therapies more precisely address pathological mechanisms, enhance efficacy, reduce adverse effects, and enable personalized treatment. As research advances, these strategies offer new possibilities for improving outcomes. This article reviews current progress to guide clinical practice and related research.

## Pathophysiology
Key inflammatory pathways implicated in IBD include NF-κB, IL-23/Th17, and TGF-β [ – ]. These pathways regulate immune cell activation and inflammatory mediator release. Therapeutic intervention can modulate inflammation and improve symptoms, making them important targets in IBD treatment.
As shown in Fig.  , current and emerging targeted therapies act via blocking extracellular signals, interfering with intracellular signaling, and modulating leukocyte trafficking. Molecular targets can directly or indirectly attenuate inflammation (e.g., TNF-α inhibitors reduce inflammatory mediator release and mucosal inflammation), regulate immune cell activation, proliferation, and cytokine production (e.g., pathways influencing Th17 differentiation), and promote repair and stabilization of the intestinal mucosal barrier, thereby reducing permeability to inflammatory factors and limiting tissue damage. These mechanisms help control disease progression and alleviate symptoms, providing more effective clinical options for patients with IBD.
 

## Molecular targets and agents

## TNF-α inhibitors
Figure illustrates TNF‑α regulatory mechanisms and transcriptional pathways, linking molecular targets to disease‑specific contexts. Commercial TNF‑α inhibitors differ in precise binding sites, functional domains, and downstream effects along the TNF‑α signaling cascade. Infliximab, adalimumab, and golimumab are full‑length IgG1 monoclonal antibodies that bind both soluble and transmembrane TNF‑α at the extracellular level, preventing activation of TNFR1 and TNFR2. This blockade interrupts recruitment of TRADD, TRAF2, and FADD, subsequently inhibiting both apoptotic (via Caspase‑8/3) and pro‑inflammatory pathways (via RIP, NIK, CIAPs, MEKK1/3/4/6/7, ASK1, IKK, JNK, MAPK), and suppressing transcription factors NF‑κB and AP‑1. Fc‑dependent effector functions facilitate complement‑dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) and antibody‑dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), eliminating TNF‑α‑expressinginflammatory cells. Reverse signaling through transmembrane TNF‑α can trigger apoptosis of activated immune cells and downregulate cytokine release [ ].
 Certolizumab pegol, a pegylated Fab’ fragment, also neutralizes soluble and membrane‑bound TNF‑α but lacks an Fc region, avoiding CDC/ADCC while still interrupting TNFR1/TNFR2‑mediated intracellular signaling. This design reduces placental transfer risk [ , ].
Disease‑specific mechanisms reflect the signaling context. In IBD (CD, UC, pediatric CD), TNFR1/TNFR2 blockade dampens NF‑κB/MAPK activation in gut‑homing lymphocytes, restores epithelial barrier integrity, and limits mucosal infiltration. In RA, AS, PsA, and PP, inhibition of TNFR1‑linked MAPK/JNK and NF‑κB signaling in synovial fibroblasts or keratinocytes resolves joint, axial, or skin inflammation. In NIPPU, interference with TRAF2‑ and FADD‑mediated inflammatory and apoptotic pathways helps control ocular immune activity.
Multiple meta-analyses indicate that infliximab is highly effective for inducing clinical response and remission [ , ], often serving as first-line therapy in pediatric IBD; adalimumab reduces treatment failure risk and fewer extraintestinal manifestations are reported [ ]. Biomarkers have been identified that predict pediatric IBD treatment response [ ].
Combination strategies have gained traction. In pediatric patients, targeted enteral nutrition plus infliximab increased the likelihood of clinical remission compared with infliximab alone (OR 2.73; 95% CI 1.73–4.31; p < 0.01) [ ]. Combining TNF-α inhibitors with immunosuppressants improved clinical remission and reduced relapse (RR 0.89; 95% CI 0.80–0.98), with particularly notable effects in active CD [ ]. Adding immunomodulators can reverse anti-drug antibodies in patients on TNF-α inhibitors, restoring clinical responses in 74% by lowering or eliminating antibody levels [ ].
A meta-analysis of 46 studies (37 CD, 8 UC, 1 pouchitis) found that after failure of an initial TNF-α inhibitor, a second TNF-α inhibitor remained effective in CD: after infliximab failure, remission was achieved in 43% and clinical response in 63%. In UC, studies evaluating switching from infliximab to adalimumab reported remission rates from 0% to 50%. Adverse events in CD ranged from 0% to 81%, mostly mild; serious adverse events occurred in 0–21%, and discontinuation rates were below 20% [ ].

## Integrin antagonists
Leukocyte migration from the bloodstream into inflamed intestinal mucosa is central to IBD pathogenesis. The multistep model of leukocyte recruitment is summarized in Fig.  . Integrins are heterodimeric adhesion proteins that facilitate leukocyte binding to endothelial cells. By blocking leukocyte migration to inflamed tissue, integrin antagonists are important therapies in IBD.
 Integrins mediate leukocyte adhesion and trafficking, and their systemic inhibition can disrupt immune surveillance in multiple tissues. As seen with natalizumab, non-selective α4 integrin blockade—including α4β1 in the CNS—can permit JC virus reactivation and cause progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), a rare but life-threatening complication [ , ]. This underscores that integrin antagonists can carry significant off-target risks when they affect non-gut pathways.
To reduce systemic toxicity, newer therapies such as vedolizumab exploit gut-selective targeting via α4β7–MAdCAM-1 blockade, limiting interference with CNS immune surveillance and respiratory tract lymphocyte trafficking [ – ]. Pharmacokinetic studies confirm minimal systemic immunosuppression and no observed PML cases in the pivotal GEMINI trials.Etrolizumab, by contrast, inhibits both α4β7–MAdCAM-1 and αEβ7–E-cadherin, aiming to block lymphocyte gut homing and retention in the intestinal epithelium. Preclinical data suggest potential superior efficacy through reduction of intraepithelial cytotoxic lymphocytes. However, results from Phase III UC trials (e.g., HICKORY, LAUREL, GARDENIA) have been mixed—some demonstrated induction benefits, while others failed to sustain remission or outperform anti-TNF comparators [ ]. Safety was generally favourable and comparable to placebo, with no new systemic immune suppression signals, but the absence of clear superiority has delayed regulatory progression. Biomarker analyses, such as baseline ITGAE expression, may refine patient selection for β7 blockade and help reconcile differences between mechanistic promise and heterogeneous trial outcomes [ ].

## Vedolizumab
Vedolizumab (VDZ) is a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody that inhibits the α4β7 integrin on leukocytes, suppressing intestinal inflammation by blockingα4β7 and mucosal addressin cell adhesion molecule − 1 (MAdCAM-1) interaction [ ]. The GEMINI trials demonstrated efficacy in induction and maintenance without increased risk of serious or opportunistic infections [ ]. Meta-analyses report UC clinical remission rates of 32%, 39%, and 46% at week 14, 6 months, and 12 months, respectively; rates in CD are slightly lower at 30%, 26%, and 30% [ ]. During induction, VDZ outperformed placebofor clinical response (RR 1.55), clinical remission (RR 1.90), and mucosal healing (RR 1.53). In maintenance, it showed sustained clinical response (RR 1.65), clinical remission (RR 1.92), and steroid-free remission (RR 2.22), with serious adverse events risk comparable to placebo [ ].
VDZ demonstrates durable benefit in CD and UC. In a GETAID study, after switching to VDZ, steroid-free clinical remission at one year was 62.3% after conventional therapy and 44.7% after TNF-α inhibitors [ ]. Therapeutic drug monitoring(TDM) studies indicates that drug concentrations in UC responders were significantly higher than in non-responders, with a similar trend observed in CD patients; concentrations greater than 18–20 µg/ml during induction therapy and greater than 10–12 µg/ml during maintenance therapy were associated with clinical remission, with higher concentrations correlating with endoscopic remission [ ]. In PSC-IBD, where 79.6% had failed other biologics, clinical remission rates were 72.6% and 77.1% at 6 months and 1 year, respectively, with endoscopic remission rates of 47.1% and 58.1%, comparable to non-PSC patients [ ]. Colonic expression of ITGA4/ITGB7 correlates with local inflammation, supporting VDZ efficacy in colonic IBD via blockade of the MAdCAM-1 pathway [ ].
VDZ is generally safe, but attention should be paid to its association with a few serious adverse events, particularly arthralgia. Associations with spondyloarthritis and peripheral arthritis have been described in case series, but retrospective data do not demonstrate a causal relationship [ ].

## Natalizumab
Natalizumab is a selective adhesion molecule inhibitor targeting the α4 integrin subunit of leukocytes. It functions by blocking the interaction of α4β1 and α4β7 integrins with VCAM-1 and MAdCAM-1, making it the first effective anti-integrin drug for inducing and maintaining remission in patients with CD. However, due to its targeting of the β1 integrin α4β1 expressed in the central nervous system, its use is associated with an increased risk of progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) due to activation of the John Cunningham (JC) virus. The drug was withdrawn from the market in 2005 due to rare adverse reactions but was reintroduced in 2006 under the strictly monitored TOUCH risk management program. As of February 2017, among 82,732 patients treated, 102 cases of PML had been reported.

## Etrolizumab
Etrolizumab is a humanized IgG1 monoclonal antibody targeting β7. It works by blocking the interaction of α4β7 with MAdCAM-1 and the interaction of αEβ7 with E-cadherin, thereby reducing the transport of lymphocytes to the intestine and their retention in the epithelium. Mouse models and genetic analyses have shown that dual blockade of α4β7 and αEβ7 can more effectively reduce the accumulation of cytotoxic intraepithelial lymphocytes and inflammatory CD8+ T cells in the intestinal mucosa of patients with CD [ ].

## Janus kinase inhibitors
Janus kinase(JAK) inhibitors target intracellular Janus kinases, blocking pro-inflammatory signaling pathways implicated in IBD [ ]. Several JAK inhibitors have progressed from clinical trials to clinical use, including tofacitinib, filgotinib, peficitinib, and upadacitinib. Figure depicts JAK/TYK2-related cytokine signaling in IBD. The JAK/STAT signaling pathway transduces signals from numerous cytokine families implicated in IBD pathogenesis. Cytokine receptors pair with distinct JAK isoforms: γc chain cytokines (IL‑2, IL‑4, IL‑7, IL‑9, IL‑15, IL‑21) signal via JAK1 and JAK3; gp130 family cytokines (IL‑6, IL‑11, OSM) utilize JAK1/JAK2; and IL‑12/IL‑23 receptors recruit JAK2 and TYK2. Ligand binding activates JAK autophosphorylation, phosphorylation of receptor tails, recruitment of STAT transcription factors, and transcriptional programs driving Th1 and Th17 differentiation, pro‑inflammatory cytokine release, and activation of innate immune cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells [ , ].
 JAK inhibitors interrupt these cascades by competitively binding the ATP site of JAK kinases, preventing receptor phosphorylation and STAT activation, thus downregulating inflammatory gene transcription. The breadth of cytokine blockade depends on isoform selectivity: pan‑JAK inhibitors target multiple nodes, whereas selective agents aim to maximize efficacy while reducing off‑target effects.
Tofacitinib (JAK1/JAK3 >JAK2) met al.l primary efficacy endpoints for moderate‑to‑severe UC in the OCTAVE trials [ ], earning FDA approval for UC. However, Phase II CD studies failed to meet induction and maintenance endpoints [ ], possibly due to: (1) disease heterogeneity with variable dominance of IL‑12/23‑TYK2‑dependent pathways; (2) compartmentalization of inflammatory circuits in transmural CD lesions, reducing drug penetration; (3) dosing/exposure limits insufficient to fully suppress TYK2‑mediated IL‑23 signaling; and (4) partial JAK3 inhibition increasing infection susceptibility without enhancing mucosal healing.
These insights have driven the shift toward selective JAK1 or TYK2 inhibitors (upadacitinib, filgotinib) in CD, with the aim of improving therapeutic windows by maximizing IL‑23/Th1/Th17 pathway blockade while limiting effects on γc cytokines involved in immune homeostasis [ , ]. Early data suggest stronger mucosal healing signals and potentially lower immunosuppressive adverse event rates compared to less selective agents.
A meta-analysis of 35 randomized controlled trials showed significantly higher clinical remission (RR 3.16; 95% CI 2.03–4.92) and endoscopic remission (RR 3.99; 95% CI 2.36–6.75) in UC compared with placebo; upadacitinib was associated with histologic improvement (RR 2.63; 95% CI 1.97–3.53). In CD, JAK inhibitors improved induction of clinical remission (RR 1.53, 95% CI 1.19–1.98) and endoscopic remission (RR 4.78, 95% CI 1.63–14.06) versus placebo, with infection risks similar to placebo [ ], although herpes zoster risk was increased (RR 1.40, 95% CI 1.18–1.67) [ ].
Tofacitinib (JAK1/JAK3 inhibitor) demonstrated efficacy comparable to that of biologics in TNF-α inhibitor–naive UC and maintains superiority over placebo; it is approved for moderate to severe UC. Trials in CD did not meet primary endpoints and development was halted [ , ]. Selective JAK1 inhibitors filgotinib and upadacitinib have shown efficacy in CD [ , ]. At 10 mg BID, tofacitinib has a relatively higher frequency of serious adverse events [ ], whereas adverse events with filgotinib and upadacitinib are similar to placebo in reported trials.

## Interleukin-6 (IL-6)
IL-6 is a multifunctional cytokine produced by various cell types and acts through multiple signaling modes. Figure summarizes IL-6 signaling modes and therapeutic blockade. IL-6 initiate classical signaling by binding membrane IL-6R (which recruits gp130 and activates downstream pathways such as JAK), and trans signaling by binding soluble IL-6R (sIL-6R). IL-6 influences immune regulation and promotes hepatic synthesis of acute-phase proteins. Elevated IL-6 and sIL-6R levels in IBD support IL-6 pathway inhibition as a therapeutic strategy. Tocilizumab and PF-04236921 have completed clinical trials, while other programs have been terminated due to insufficient efficacy or safety concerns [ ]. Tocilizumab has shown remission potential in active CD, though efficacy varies among IBD subgroups.
 

## Other emerging molecular targets

## New MAdCAM-1 inhibitors
PF-00547659, a fully human monoclonal antibody targeting MAdCAM-1, selectively reduces lymphocyte gut homing for moderate to severe UC. In a Phase II trial (NCT01620255; n=357), subcutaneous dosing every four weeks yielded remission rates significantly higher than placebo across active treatment arms. Serious adverse event incidence was 5.5% (placebo), 14.1% (7.5 mg), and 1.4% (22 mg), indicating overall safety and tolerability, with the best effects observed at 22.5 mg and 75 mg.

## Mirikizumab
Mirikizumab is a humanized IgG4 monoclonal antibody targeting the IL-23 p19 subunit. In the Phase III LUCENT-1 trial, intravenous 300 mg significantly improved clinical remission at week 12 versus placebo (24.2% vs. 13.3%, p < 0.001), with all secondary endpoints also superior. Treatment-related adverse events were comparable to placebo, with lower rates of serious adverse events and discontinuations; clinical response, endoscopic healing, histology, and symptom scores improved significantly, indicating favorable safety [ ].

## IL-12/23 inhibitors
IL-12 and IL-23 are heterodimeric cytokines composed of p35/p40 and p19/p40 subunits, respectively, sharing the p40 subunit and signaling via JAK pathways to induce Th1 and Th17 differentiation [ ]. Expression is upregulated in IBD, and IL23R and IL12B polymorphisms confer susceptibility [ , ], supporting inhibitor development. Ustekinumab received FDA/EMA approval in 2016 for refractory CD. Among primary responders with CD, annual risks of loss of response and dose escalation were 21% and 25%, respectively; 58% of secondary non-responders regained clinical response after dose escalation. In UC, 35% required dose escalation (annual risk 18%), with 58% regaining symptom relief [ ]. Case reports support efficacy in CD-related small bowel lesions and pediatric patients [ , ]. Further research is needed to evaluate durability and dose optimization in UC.

## S1P receptor modulators
Sphingosine-1-phosphate(S1P) receptor modulators treat IBD by blocking lymphocyte egress from lymph nodes into systemic circulation. Figure shows S1P1/5-mediated lymphocyte retention. Ozanimod is approved by the FDA for moderate to severe UC. Meta-analysis shows associations with clinical remission (RR 2.52, 95% CI 1.88–3.39) and endoscopic remission (RR 2.39, 95% CI 1.07–5.33) [ ]. Potential arthritis risk requires further clarification to optimize personalized strategies.
 

## Inhibitor of carbohydrate sulfotransferase 15 (CHST15)
Carbohydrate sulfotransferase 15(CHST15) is involved in biosynthesis of sulfated glycosaminoglycans and is associated with intestinal inflammation and fibrosis. GUT-1, a double-stranded RNA oligonucleotide targeting CHST15, was evaluated in a Phase IIa study of 28 UC patients unresponsive to conventional treatments. Single-dose administration (25 nM, 250 nM) was compared with placebo; the primary endpoint was endoscopic improvement at weeks 2 and 4. In the 250 nM group, endoscopic improvement was 71.4%, clinical remission 57.1%, and histological improvement 42.9%, significantly higher than the 25 nM group (0%) and placebo (28.6%, 14.3%, 0%). GUT-1 reduces CHST15 expression, inhibiting mucosal inflammation and fibrosis, and may serve as an inducible therapy to regulate tissue remodeling in UC [ ].

## Clinical trials

## Histological results of vedolizumab and adalimumab in the treatment of UC (VARSITY)
The VARSITY study demonstrated that VDZ showed higher clinical remission, endoscopic improvement, and histological remission than adalimumab in UC. VDZ resulted in lower histological activity and superior combined histological and endoscopic improvement [ ].

## Long-term efficacy and safety of upadacitinib in patients with CD (CELEST)
The CELEST extension confirmed that upadacitinib maintains efficacy in CD for up to 30 months, with good tolerability. Endoscopic improvement continued through 24 months, and the safety profile remained consistent after 30 months [ ].

## Efficacy and safety of TEV-48,574 in UC and CD
In a Phase IIb study (NCT05499130), the anti-TL1A antibody TEV-48,574 demonstrated antifibrotic and anti-inflammatory effects in moderate to severe UC and CD, supporting Phase III development [ ].

## Guselkumab plus golimumab combination induction therapy for UC
A Phase IIa randomized controlled study showed that the combination therapy with guselkumab and golimumab was superior to monotherapy for clinical response, remission, and endoscopic improvement in moderate to severe UC, with similar adverse event rates to monotherapy [ ].

## Efficacy of GSK2831781 in UC
The study of GSK2831781 (LAG-3 antibody) in active UC (NCT03893565) was terminated early due to failure to reduce LAG-3+ cells in colonic mucosa and lack of therapeutic effect, despite clearance of related cells in peripheral blood [ ].

## Comparative efficacy/safety
Systematic reviews and network meta-analyses indicate that infliximab has the highest clinical response and remission rates for induction in moderate to severe UC, while ozanimod is most effective for inducing mucosal healing. Tofacitinib shows the highest clinical remission rates during maintenance; VDZ and tofacitinib are associated with higher infection rates, with no significant differences in severe adverse events. Overall, infliximab may be the optimal comprehensive option [ ]. For CD, TNF-α inhibitors, ustekinumab, and VDZ provide the strongest evidence as first- and second-line biologic therapies for moderate to severe disease, while tofacitinib and methotrexate have moderate evidence, and thalidomide has lower-quality evidence [ , – ]. Combination strategies with JAK inhibitors, S1P modulators, and biologics significantly improve clinical and endoscopic remission in refractory IBD, although long-term safety remains uncertain [ – ]. Future studies should assess the potential role of antioxidant supplements and the long-term efficacy and safety of combination regimens [ ] to support precision treatment (Table ).
 

## Challenges and sequential therapy
Molecular targeted therapy for IBD has significantly advanced clinical outcomes, yet substantial challenges remain in achieving durable remission, minimizing safety risks, and determining optimal therapeutic sequencing. Central issues include primary non-response (PNR), secondary loss of response (SLR), long-term safety monitoring, and the integration of combination or sequential strategies informed by real-world experience and biomarker-guided approaches.

## Primary and secondary loss of response
Despite the availability of diverse biologics and small molecules, approximately 30–40% of patients exhibit primary non-response at induction, and nearly one-third of initial responders experience secondary loss of response within two years of therapy. The mechanisms underlying these phenomena are multifactorial and include immunogenicity with development of anti-drug antibodies, subtherapeutic drug concentrations, high baseline inflammatory burden, target and pathway redundancy, and host factors such as phenotype and microbiome heterogeneity. For example, the formation of anti-drug antibodies against TNF-α inhibitors accelerates drug clearance and reduces efficacy, which can be mitigated through TDM and the addition of immunomodulators [ ]. Serum trough concentration–response analyses for VDZ have demonstrated that drug exposure correlates with symptomatic and endoscopic remission [ ]. Similarly, pathway compensation may explain secondary escape to interleukin (IL) 12/23 blockade, with meta analytic estimates showing annual SLR rates of 18–21% for ustekinumab [ ].
Practical strategies to overcome PNR and SLR include TDM guided dose escalation, switch within or between mechanistic classes, immunomodulator co therapy to reduce immunogenicity, and proactive monitoring with fecal calprotectin and periodic endoscopy to guide early intervention. These approaches support the modern “treat to target” paradigm integrating clinical, biochemical, and mucosal endpoints.

## Safety considerations
Long-term safety remains a pivotal determinant of therapy choice and sequencing. Natalizumab, the first effective anti adhesion biologic, demonstrated clear clinical benefit but was limited by a 4–5 per 1,000 incidence of PML linked to JC virus reactivation [ , , ]. Gut selective alternatives such as vedolizumab have largely supplanted natalizumab due to absence of central nervous system involvement.
S1P receptor modulators, including ozanimod and etrasimod, introduce an oral, lymphocyte retention mechanism effective for UC but require caution regarding dose dependent cardiac conduction effects, transient bradycardia, hepatic enzyme elevation, and rare macular edema [ ]. Baseline electrocardiography, liver enzyme monitoring, and ophthalmologic evaluation are therefore recommended.
JAK inhibitors provide rapid clinical improvement but carry boxed warnings for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), venous thromboembolism (VTE), and malignancy, particularly in high risk populations [ , ]. Increased herpes zoster incidence also warrants pre treatment vaccination and regular safety laboratory assessment. Interleukin 12/23 and IL 23 specific inhibitors generally demonstrate favorable long term tolerability, although extended follow up beyond three years is still limited.
Consequently, individualized safety surveillance is essential. JC virus screening is mandatory for natalizumab, cardiovascular and thrombotic risk evaluation for JAK inhibitors, and hepatic and cardiac assessments for S1P modulators. Integration of these safety frameworks optimizes long-term outcomes and patient retention on therapy.

## Combination and sequential strategies
Given the multifaceted immune dysregulation and variable response across IBD phenotypes, rational combination or sequential therapy approaches have gained prominence. Dual biologic therapy is under active evaluation: in the Phase IIa VEGA trial, induction with guselkumab plus golimumab achieved higher 12 week rates of clinical remission and endoscopic improvement than monotherapy, without excess adverse events [ ]. Real world evidence supports the feasibility of dual targeted therapy combining biologics and small molecules in refractory disease, demonstrating improved remission and acceptable short term safety [ , ].
Therapeutic sequencing is another crucial component of precision management. After anti TNF failure or intolerance, vedolizumab and ustekinumab are favored for their safety and gut selectivity, whereas JAK and S1P modulators provide oral options for patients preferring non injectable therapy or requiring systemic anti-inflammatory potency. Optimization of dosing through TDM before transitioning agents can prevent unnecessary switches and preserve therapeutic classes for future lines.
Emerging algorithms integrate pharmacokinetic and mechanistic data with treat to target endpoints, incorporating clinical, endoscopic, and histologic criteria. These frameworks, together with evolving biomarker tools such as integrin expression or microRNA signatures, are expected to refine patient stratification for appropriate sequencing and combination use [ , ].

## Evidence gaps and future imperatives
Despite considerable progress, limitations persist. Direct head-to-head comparisons between advanced therapies remain scarce, and network meta analyses are limited by heterogeneity and reliance on indirect evidence. Most pivotal trials underrepresent Asian populations, leaving pharmacogenetic and metabolic modifiers underexplored despite rising IBD prevalence in Asia. Long term real world studies exceeding three years are needed to clarify cumulative risks of infection, malignancy, and cardiovascular events with both biologics and small molecules.
Future directions should prioritize integrated multi omics profiling, standardized definitions of loss of response, and pragmatic comparative trials assessing sequential and combination strategies. Strengthening longitudinal registries will enable correlation of molecular mechanisms with therapeutic outcomes and safety in real-world practice, ultimately advancing personalized, mechanism-based care in IBD.

## Biomarkers and future directions
Recent advances in biomarker discovery for IBD are increasingly oriented toward multi-layered, multi-omics approaches that integrate transcriptomics, proteomics, microbiome profiling, and quantitative systems pharmacology (QSP) modeling. This strategy enables more accurate prediction of treatment responses, disease progression risk, and optimal therapeutic sequencing, thereby advancing precision medicine in both UC and CD.

## Transcriptomic predictors
High-throughput RNA sequencing of intestinal biopsies has identified baseline mucosal transcriptomic signatures predictive of anti-TNF response—such as upregulation of TNF signaling modules for infliximab responders and elevated IL23R expression for ustekinumab-sensitive patients [ , ]. Longitudinal multi-tissue transcriptomic studies also demonstrate patient-specific determinants of drug efficacy, emphasizing the importance of integrating individual expression profiles into treatment algorithms. These signatures may also aid in early discontinuation of ineffective therapies, reducing exposure to unnecessary adverse effects. Proteomic and Soluble Mediators.
Circulating protein biomarkers provide non-invasive insights into therapeutic prognosis. C-reactive protein (CRP) remains a robust general marker of inflammation, but newer candidates such as oncostatin M (OSM) are emerging as predictive of non-response to anti-TNF therapy [ ]. Serum proteomics can also detect pathway-specific alterations; for example, anti-TNF treatment induces anti-inflammatory glycosylation shifts in the IgG N-glycome, which may act as a dynamic indicator of treatment benefit [ ]. Integrating proteomic datasets with transcriptomic profiles improves feature robustness for predictive modeling.

## Microbiome features
Microbial dysbiosis—characterized by reduced diversity and depletion of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii—has been linked with poor response to biologics and unfavorable disease prognosis [ ]. Quantitative indices of dysbiosis may serve as adjunctive tools for patient stratification, particularly when combined with metabolomics to capture functional microbiome activity (e.g., tryptophan catabolism, short-chain fatty acid synthesis). Interventions that modulate microbiota composition could be deployed alongside targeted drugs to enhance response durability.

## Integrative multi-omics and QSP modeling
Combining transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and microbiome datasets into integrative prediction frameworks represents a transformative step in IBD management. Multi-omics profiles can feed QSP models that simulate immune–microbiome–barrier interactions over time, enabling in silico testing of therapeutic sequencing strategies [ ]. Such models also facilitate predictive enrichment in clinical trials, ensuring allocation of novel agents to biologically relevant subgroups and expediting regulatory approval.

## Future directions
To fully realize the potential of multi-omics-guided precision medicine in IBD, efforts should focus on standardizing biomarker assays for transcriptomic, proteomic, and microbiome features to ensure robust cross-center validation, expanding reference datasets to incorporate diverse ethnic populations and treatment-refractory patient cohorts, and integrating these tools into clinical decision support systems linked to electronic health records to enable real-time prediction of therapeutic response and adverse event risk. In parallel, co-development of drug–biomarker pairs, where targeted therapy approval is coupled with a validated companion diagnostic, will be crucial to optimize patient stratification, guide rational sequencing of therapies, and improve long-term outcomes in UC and CD.
By advancing from single-biomarker reliance to multi-omics integration, precision IBD care can achieve greater predictive and prognostic accuracy, guide rational selection and sequencing of targeted therapies, and ultimately improve long-term patient outcomes.

## Personalized strategies
Effective personalized treatment in IBD requires integrating disease type (UC or CD), activity, extraintestinal manifestations, surgical history, prior treatment responses (e.g., to anti-TNF agents), and patient-specific characteristics. First-line biologic selection should balance efficacy with safety—TNF inhibitors or vedolizumab are often preferred in UC depending on clinical context [ , , ], while vedolizumab and ustekinumab may be favored in CD for their gut selectivity and favorable safety profiles [ , ]. Therapeutic sequencing benefits from pharmacokinetic guidance, with proactive TDM used to optimize dosing, mitigate immunogenicity, and delay or avoid premature switching [ , , ].
The expanding arsenal of targeted therapies, including IL‑23 inhibitors and JAK or S1P modulators, offers new opportunities to tailor treatment to the underlying immunopathology and patient preferences [ , , ]. Integration of validated biomarkers—such as oncostatin M for anti‑TNF non‑response [ ], mucosal transcriptomic signatures predicting biologic efficacy [ , ], and microbiome‑derived dysbiosis indices [ ]—into point-of-care decision tools can enhance stratification accuracy. Multi‑omics data combined with QSP modeling [ ] allow simulation of immune–microbiome–barrier interactions, informing rational drug combinations or sequencing that address pathway redundancy and heterogeneity.
In clinical practice, embedding these approaches into intelligent decision support systems linked to electronic health records can facilitate real‑time prediction of response, risk, and optimal intervention [ ]. As biomarker-driven algorithms mature and real‑world registries expand, personalized therapy in IBD is poised to evolve from empiric selection toward mechanism-based precision care, with the dual aims of maximizing long-term remission and minimizing safety risks.

## Conclusion
Molecular targeted therapy for IBD has evolved from single‑pathway inhibition to multi‑target, mechanism‑based strategies informed by advances in immunology, microbiome research, and biomarker science. Current therapeutic classes—including anti‑TNF agents, integrin antagonists, IL‑12/23 and IL‑23 inhibitors, Janus kinase inhibitors, and S1P modulators—have substantially improved remission rates and durability in moderate to severe disease, particularly for patients unresponsive to conventional treatments. These agents act through suppression of aberrant immune activation, modulation of leukocyte trafficking, and restoration of mucosal barrier integrity, enabling more personalized selection based on disease phenotype, molecular signatures, and prior treatment history.
Despite these gains, 30–40% of patients experience primary or secondary loss of response, driven by immunogenicity, pharmacokinetic variability, pathway redundancy, and host–microbiome heterogeneity. Long‑term safety concerns—including thrombotic risks with JAK inhibitors, cardiac and hepatic events with S1P modulators, and rare neurological complications with non‑selective integrin blockade—necessitate robust, individualized surveillance frameworks. Translational gaps remain for emerging targets such as tryptophan metabolism, epithelial repair pathways, and fibrosis‑related mediators, and the integration of multi‑omics prediction models into routine care is still limited.
Future progress will rely on deepening understanding of immune–microbiome–barrier interactions to identify and validate predictive biomarkers, refining rational combination and sequencing strategies to overcome therapeutic resistance, and embedding multi‑omics data into intelligent clinical decision support systems. With ongoing innovation in drug development, companion diagnostics, and real‑world data integration, molecular targeted therapy may ultimately shift from symptom control toward disease modification and potential cure, setting a benchmark for precision medicine in complex immune‑mediated diseases.
