## Abstract

## Purpose
This study aims to elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying the therapeutic effect of AS-IV in combination with Tan IIA in ameliorating cardiac fibrosis by inhibiting excessive collagen cross-linking following myocardial infarction (MI).

## Methods
MI model mice were administered AS-IV and Tan IIA for a duration of four weeks. After the treatment was completed, serum and cardiac specimens were collected from the mice to assess cardiac collagen fiber deposition, collagen cross-linking, cardiac function, copper homeostasis, lysyl oxidase (LOX) content, and the expression of related molecules. Meanwhile, utilizing an in vitro model of myofibroblasts and employing CTR1 overexpression or siRNA-mediated CTR1 silencing and recombinant LOX, we systematically investigated the effects of AS-IV and Tan IIA on key phenotypic features in myofibroblasts, including cross-linking and copper levels.

## Results
Our results demonstrate that AS-IV and Tan IIA significantly inhibited excessive collagen cross-linking in infarcted hearts, reduced cardiac collagen deposition, and consequently attenuated adverse cardiac remodeling while preserving cardiac function. Mechanistically, AS-IV and Tan IIA suppress intracellular copper accumulation by downregulating the CTR1/ATOX1 axis in the copper transport pathway within myofibroblasts. This downregulation subsequently reduces both the expression and enzymatic activity of LOX, a copper-dependent enzyme, thereby inhibiting excessive collagen cross-linking in these cells. Moreover, AS-IV and Tan IIA demonstrate potential in restoring abnormal copper homeostasis after myocardial infarction and modulating copper distribution within myofibroblasts.

## Conclusion
AS-IV and Tan IIA attenuate collagen cross-linking in myofibroblasts by downregulating the CTR1/ATOX1/LOX axis, thereby reducing aberrant collagen cross-linking and deposition in the infarcted heart and ultimately inhibiting cardiac fibrosis.

Keywords: myocardial fibrosis, cross-linking, lysyl oxidase, copper ion transport

## Introduction
Myocardial infarction is one of the primary factors leading to heart failure. Following the extensive death of cardiomyocytes due to infarction, the heart activates a self-repair mechanism, producing substantial amounts of collagen and other extracellular matrix components. However, excessive extracellular matrix deposition can increase cardiac stiffness, which triggers cardiac systolic and diastolic dysfunction. Simultaneously, diminished cardiac compliance reduces nutrient delivery and exacerbates cardiomyocyte atrophy and death, , ultimately establishing a detrimental feedback loop.
Cardiac fibroblasts, which serve as pivotal effector cells responsible for the synthesis and secretion of extracellular matrix, can be induced by various inflammatory factors and chemokines to undergo activation and transformation into myofibroblasts following cardiomyocyte necrosis. Subsequently, these myofibroblasts produce and secrete increased amounts of extracellular matrix. , Research has found that lysyl oxidase (LOX) levels significantly increase in cardiac fibroblasts from heart failure patients. Excessive production of LOX facilitates collagen cross-linking and enhances the ability of collagen to resist degradation by matrix metalloproteinases, thereby resulting in increased collagen deposition and contributing to the progression of myocardial fibrosis. LOX enzyme inhibitors or antibodies that specifically block LOX can effectively suppress the progression of cardiac fibrosis and enhance cardiac function. , 
LOX is a copper-dependent amine oxidase whose activity is regulated by intracellular copper homeostasis. Intracellular copper transport relies on copper chaperones, indicating that the level of copper chaperone proteins significantly influences LOX activity. Copper transporter protein 1(CTR1) / antioxidant 1 (ATOX1) is the major intracellular pathway for the delivery of copper to LOX. , Research has demonstrated that elevated expression of CTR1 in renal myofibroblasts enhances LOX activity, promoting collagen cross-linking and contributing to renal fibrosis. This finding suggests that inhibition of the CTR1/ATOX1/LOX pathway in myofibroblasts may ameliorate abnormal collagen cross-linking and suppress fibrotic progression.
Astragalus flavone and Salvia miltiorrhiza are the classic combinations of traditional chinese medicine for the treatment of heart failure and have remarkable therapeutic efficacy on ischemic heart disease. 
Astragaloside IV (AS-IV) and tanshinone IIA (Tan IIA) are the two herbs’ main bioactive compounds, respectively. Previous studies have shown that both are effective in inhibiting the process of myocardial interstitial fibrosis. Among them, AS-IV was shown to ameliorate pathological fibrosis after myocardial infarction, while Tan IIA exerts therapeutic effects by targeting collagen metabolism. We thus hypothesized that the synergistic effect of AS-IV and Tan IIA may inhibit post-infarction fibrosis by correcting abnormal collagen cross-linking and reducing excessive extracellular matrix deposition. Through a series of experiments, we systematically verified that AS-IV and Tan IIA combination therapy significantly ameliorates adverse cardiac remodeling after myocardial infarction. The cardioprotective effects are primarily manifested through inhibiting myocardial fibrosis and pathological cardiomyocyte hypertrophy. At the molecular level, this therapeutic mechanism involves the specific suppression of the CTR1/ATOX1/LOX axis in myofibroblasts, effectively reducing excessive collagen cross-linking and abnormal deposition. The dual-targeting efficacy of this drug combination, which modulates copper homeostasis and the CTR1/ATOX1/LOX axis simultaneously, presents a promising therapeutic strategy for developing novel, multi-mechanistic interventions against post-infarction cardiac fibrosis. The present study also revealed that AS-IV and Tan IIA play a critical role in maintaining cardiac copper ion homeostasis and can modulate the expression of copper-related proteins by regulating the subcellular distribution of copper ions.

## Materials and Methods

## Drugs and Main Reagents
AS-IV (Cat No. B20564) and Tan IIA (Cat No. B20257) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and both compounds exhibited a purity of ≥98%. Transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β, Cat No. HY-P7118) was obtained from MedChemExpress. The CTR1 and LOX overexpression plasmids, as well as the CTR1 small interfering RNA, were all purchased from Hanheng Biotech Co., Ltd. Additional key reagents, including antibodies and assay kits, are listed in Table S1.

## Animal Treatment and Cell Culture
This experiment used 5-week-old male C57BL/6J mice purchased from Vital River (Beijing, China). All mice were housed in specific pathogen-free facilities, provided with standard laboratory chow, maintained under a controlled light-dark cycle, and given ad libitum access to water and food. The entire procedure of the animal experiments was conducted in strict compliance with the ethical guidelines for animal research and received formal approval from the Ethics Committee of Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No. SDUTCM20210629001). In the first stage, 35 mice were randomly allocated to the sham-operated group (n=5) or the left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) ligation group (n=30) using a computer-generated randomization sequence. These mice underwent LAD ligation, in which the sham group was only threaded without ligation, and the local ventricular anterior wall turned white or gray under naked eye observation after ligation, suggesting that myocardial infarction model were successfully established. After LAD ligation, 10 died within 24 hours post-surgery, primarily due to arrhythmias or cardiac rupture. These 10 mice were excluded from further analysis. Electrocardiographic assessment confirmed successful model establishment in all surviving mice. The 20 surviving MI mice were randomly assigned into four groups (MI, AS-IV, Tan IIA, Co, n=5 each) using a second computer-generated randomization sequence.
Upon completion of the grouping process, each drug group received the corresponding concentration via intraperitoneal (IP) injection. AS- IV and Tan IIA are primarily administered via IP injection for two principal reasons. First, both compounds have poor oral bioavailability due to low water solubility and extensive first-pass metabolism, Intravenous bolus administration of Tan IIA is further limited by an extremely short elimination half-life, making it impractical for long-term daily dosing in small rodents. IP administration bypasses the intestinal absorption barrier and first-pass effect, yielding higher and more consistent systemic exposure than oral gavage. Second, IP administration of each agent has been extensively validated in chronic cardiovascular studies. We therefore applied the same route for the combined regimen to maintain methodological consistency.
Based on prior literature reports, , , as well as the mouse species, administration methods, and the expertise of our research group, the dosages of AS-IV and Tan II A were determined. Specifically, each mouse in the AS-IV group was administered AS-IV at a dose of 2 mg/kg/day, while each mouse in the Tan IIA group received Tan IIA at 15 mg/kg/day. Mice in the Co group were given both AS-IV at 2 mg and Tan IIA at 15 mg/kg/day. The administration volume for all mice was set at 100 μL per 30 g of body weight. The sham group and the model group received an equal volume of saline. The drug was prepared using a vehicle consisting of 10% anhydrous ethanol and 90% corn oil, followed by ultrasonic treatment for 30 minutes. The drugs were administered for 4 weeks. No mortality was observed among the mice during the 4-week treatment period. Following transthoracic echocardiography, mice were deeply anesthetized by inhalation of isoflurane (4% for induction and 2% for maintenance). Upon loss of the toe‑pinch reflex, blood samples were obtained from the retro‑orbital venous plexus. The mice were then euthanized via cervical dislocation. Hearts were rapidly excised through a thoracotomy, rinsed with ice‑cold normal saline to remove residual blood, and processed for subsequent detection. It should be noted that allocation concealment was not implemented in this study.
Human cardiac fibroblasts (HCF) were obtained from Priscilla Biotechnology (Wuhan, China). The cells were cultured in dishes containing specialized media designed explicitly for HCF. These cultures were maintained in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2, and the cells were passaged every 3 to 4 days. After cell plating, the cells were divided into a control group, model group, AS-IV group, Tan IIA group, and combination drug (Co) group. Before the experiment, HCF was intervened in a medium containing 10 ng/mL TGF-β for 48 h to activate fibroblasts into myofibroblasts to construct a myofibroblast model. Based on the effects of the drugs on cellular activity and the expression of Collagen I protein, we determined the appropriate doses of AS-IV and Tan II A for cell treatment. AS-IV and Tan II A were dissolved in DMSO to prepare stock solutions at concentrations of 40 mg/mL and 1 mg/mL, respectively, and subsequently diluted to appropriate working concentrations prior to use. After the model was successfully established, each drug group was given the corresponding concentration of drugs (AS-IV group, 40 μg/mL AS-IV; Tan IIA group, 2 μg/mL Tan IIA; Co group, 40 μg/mL AS-IV and 2 μg/mL Tan IIA) were intervened for 24 h. Four hours before the end of the intervention, 50 μmol/L of CuCl2 solution was added to the medium. The control group cells were consistently cultured in a dedicated medium.

## Ultrasound Imaging
After anesthetizing mice with isoflurane, the small animal ultrasound imaging system was utilized to detect the left ventricular short axis M-mode echocardiographic images, and measurements were taken for the ejection fraction (EF), fractional shortening (FS). All echocardiographic examinations, including both image acquisition and data analysis, were conducted by investigators blinded to the experimental group assignments. The blinding codes were maintained until all analyses were completed.

## Pathological Staining
After fixing the mouse heart tissue with 4% paraformaldehyde, the heart tissue sample was dehydrated and transparent, then embedded in paraffin and sliced, and the paraffin sections were dewaxed and rehydrated. Paraffin sections in hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, respectively. Sirius red stained the slice drops with Sirius red stain solution. In Masson staining, the slices were immersed in hematoxylin, eosin, and aniline blue staining solutions for staining. After staining, the sections were dehydrated, transparent, sealed, and observed under the fluorescence microscope. To minimize detection bias in histological evaluation, a rigorous blinding protocol was implemented. Briefly, one independent investigator collected and coded all heart tissue sections as A, B, C, D, and E, and maintained the key linking codes to the actual group identities. The pathological sections were then randomly distributed to the histology operator, who was completely blinded to the group allocation throughout the staining and imaging procedures. The decoding was performed only after all quantitative data were collected.

## Immunofluorescence Staining
After deparaffinization and rehydration, the paraffin-embedded cardiac tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval. In cell experiments, cells are first fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde. The repaired paraffin sections and paraformaldehyde fixed cells were treated with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 minutes and then sealed with 5% BSA at room temperature for 1 hour. Then the diluted primary antibody (α-SMA, 1:500; CTR1, 1:250; ATOX1, 1:250; LOX, 1:250) was added dropwise to the slices or cells and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following a 2-hour incubation with the secondary antibody at room temperature, an anti-fluorescence quenching agent containing DAPI was applied, and the sample was subsequently examined under a fluorescence microscope.

## Detection of Copper Ion Content
We weighed appropriate heart tissue from the infarct area and mixed it with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 10 μL PBS per mg of heart tissue. The tissue was lysed using a tissue grinder. The lysate was then placed in a refrigerator at −80 °C for 30 minutes and in an incubator at 37 °C for 15 minutes. The supernatant was collected by centrifugation after subjecting the sample to three freezing and thawing cycles. Serum samples were collected from each group of experimental animals. In the cell experiment, the cell precipitate from each group was gathered, and 400 μL of distilled water was added to each 4–6 x 10^6 cell and mixed thoroughly. The suspension underwent repeated freezing and thawing between −80 °C and 37 °C three times, and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation. The content of copper ions was measured using a tissue copper/serum copper/cell copper content detection kit, with specific operational steps performed according to the kit instructions. A single-point standard of 20 nmol/mL CuSO₄, prepared through sequential dilution from the provided 10 mmol/L stock standard, was run in parallel with each batch. The copper concentration was calculated using the formula: [20 × (A_sample − A_blank) / (A_standard − A_blank)] / protein concentration (g/L), which was determined by the BCA assay.

## Copper Probe Detection
After the intervention, the cells were incubated in a 1 μmol/L CuCl2 solution in a 37°C incubator for one hour. Following this incubation, the cells were treated with a 10 μmol/L copper ion probe solution for 30 minutes at 37°C, after which they were imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

## Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)
Following the drug intervention, serum samples from each group were collected to measure the levels of N-terminal prob-type natriuretic peptide (NT-ProBNP) and B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) using ELISA kits in mice with myocardial infarction. Additionally, an appropriate amount of heart tissue from the infarcted area of myocardial infarction mice was weighed and lysed using RIPA buffer, and the supernatant was collected. The levels of LOX and deoxypyridinoline (DPD) in the heart tissue of these mice were quantified using ELISA kits specific for mouse LOX and DPD. In the cell-based experiments, supernatants from each group of cells were collected following drug intervention, and the concentrations of LOX and DPD in these supernatants were measured using human LOX ELISA kits and human DPD ELISA kits, respectively.

## LOX Activity
The supernatant from each group of cells was collected following drug intervention, and the procedure was conducted according to the instructions provided by the LOX assay kit. The fluorescence values in the supernatant of each group of cells were measured using a fluorescence microplate reader.

## Hydroxyproline Assay
Tissues from the infarcted region of the hearts of infarcted mice were selected, and these tissues were digested using an alkaline hydrolysis solution. In the cellular experiment, the cell supernatant from each group was collected following the cellular intervention. The procedure was conducted according to the instructions in the hydroxyproline assay kit. Finally, the supernatant was added to 96-well plates (200 μL per well), and the absorbance value was measured using an enzyme counter.

## Western Blot
Cardiac tissue or cellular proteins from the infarcted area were extracted using RIPA lysate, and protein concentrations were quantified with a BCA protein assay kit. Protein loading amounts ranged from 15 to 30 μg. Following separation on 10% SDS-PAGE gels for standard proteins or 10% TRICINE gels for small-molecule proteins (<10 kDa). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with specific primary antibodies: Collagen I (1:2000), Collagen III (1:600), CTR1 (1:1000), ATOX1 (1:1000), LOX (1:1000), COX17 (1:1000), CCS (1:1000), ATP7A (1:1000), and ATP7B (1:1000). Membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (GAPDH, β-actin, 1:10000) at room temperature for 1 hour. The specific procedure was conducted in accordance with the standard Western Blot operational protocol.

## RT-qPCR
Cellular RNA was extracted using the Trizol reagent. Reverse transcription was done using RT SuperMix with a template input of 1 μg. Gene amplification was performed using Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix. The primer sequences used are shown in Table S2.

## Molecular Docking
In this study, AutoDockTools (version 1.5.7) was used to prepare the compounds astragaloside IV (AS-IV) and tanshinone IIA (Tan IIA), as well as the human copper transporter 1 (CTR1) protein (CTR1 structural data retrieved from the UniProt database, UniProt ID: O15431), and to define the docking grids. AutoDock Vina (version 1.2.3) was then used to search for optimal docking conformations, with the pose showing the highest binding affinity selected as the final result. The output was visualized using PyMOL (version 2.3.0), and two-dimensional interaction diagrams were generated with LigPlot+.

## Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA)
Soluble proteins were extracted from cardiac fibroblasts using 0.1% Triton X‑100 and divided into three aliquots in EP tubes labeled as AS‑IV, Tan IIA, and DMSO. Each aliquot was then incubated with either AS‑IV (40 μg/mL), Tan IIA (2 μg/mL), or an equivalent volume of DMSO at room temperature for 1 h. Following treatment, the protein extracts from each group were further aliquoted into seven PCR tubes and subjected to heating for 3 min at temperatures ranging from 37 °C to 67 °C, then immediately cooled on ice for 2 min. After centrifugation at 20,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected, combined with 5× SDS loading buffer, and denatured at 95 °C for 5 min. Finally, CTR1 protein levels were assessed by Western blot.

## Statistical Analysis
All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0 and GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.3). The experimental data are presented as mean ± SEM. For experiments with a single independent variable, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied when data conformed to assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, followed by Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc comparisons. If normality or equal variance assumptions were violated, the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis H-test was used, with pairwise comparisons evaluated by Mann–Whitney U-test and further adjusted via the Bonferroni method. In experiments involving two independent variables, two-way ANOVA was performed provided that normality and variance homogeneity were satisfied. Where a significant interaction effect was observed, post hoc intergroup comparisons were carried out using Šidák test. For data that did not meet the prerequisites for two-way ANOVA, the nonparametric Scheirer-Ray-Hare test was employed as a two-factor alternative. Statistical significance levels are denoted as follows: * (P < 0.05), ** (P < 0.01), and ***(P < 0.001). Values with P > 0.05 are considered not significant (ns).

## Results

## AS-IV and Tan IIA Improve Cardiac Remodeling and Enhance Cardiac Function After Myocardial Infarction
To investigate the effects of AS-IV and Tan IIA on cardiac remodeling and function, we established a myocardial infarction model in mice. We administered AS-IV and Tan IIA via daily intraperitoneal injections, maintaining this treatment regimen for four weeks. Echocardiographic results indicated that mice in the MI group exhibited significantly impaired cardiac function compared to the sham group, as evidenced by a marked decrease in left ventricular EF and FS ( – ). Concurrently, serum levels of BNP and NT-proBNP were significantly elevated ( and ). After treatment with AS-IV, Tan IIA, and their combined interventions, the cardiac function of mice with myocardial infarction significantly improved ( – ). Among the treatment options, the combination of AS-IV and Tan IIA demonstrates greater efficacy in reducing NT-ProBNP levels compared to medication alone. These results suggest that AS-IV and Tan IIA can attenuate myocardial injury and protect cardiac function.

 We further evaluated the effects of AS-IV and Tan IIA on cardiac fibrosis and cardiomyocyte hypertrophy. Masson and Sirius red staining differentially identified collagen fibers (blue and red) against myocardial fibers (red/yellow). Both methods consistently revealed diminished collagen accumulation in perimyocardial regions across all drug-treated groups ( ). In addition, HE staining showed that in sham-operated mice, cardiomyocytes maintained structural integrity with orderly alignment and physiological intercellular spacing ( ). In contrast, myocardial infarction mice showed structural disarray, featuring atrophied or lysed cardiomyocytes replaced by eosinophilic fibrous scar tissue ( ). Notably, AS-IV, Tan IIA, and their combination significantly alleviated myocardial damage in infarcted mice, while concurrently reducing interstitial expansion and fibrous tissue deposition. The results of HE staining confirmed that the combined medication offered more effective protection of the myocardial tissue ( ). In addition, we evaluated myocardial hypertrophy, a key indicator of cardiac remodeling. WGA staining indicated that all three treatments effectively inhibited cardiomyocyte hypertrophy ( ). This finding was further corroborated by the cardiac weight index (CWI) results and the heart weight-to-tibia length ratio (HW/TL). Notably, AS-IV, Tan IIA, and Co interventions significantly reduced both CWI and HW/TL in infarcted mice, with the combination treatment demonstrating the most pronounced effects and optimal stability ( and ). In conclusion, the aforementioned data indicate that AS-IV and Tan IIA effectively reduce cardiac fibrosis, inhibit cardiac remodeling, and protect cardiac function. Furthermore, their combined use demonstrates enhanced efficacy.

## AS-IV and Tan IIA Suppress LOX-Driven Cardiac Collagen Cross-Linking and Fibrotic Deposition
Cardiac fibrosis is characterized by excessive collagen synthesis and deposition. Pathological collagen cross-linking enhances fiber stability, rendering the fibers resistant to enzymatic degradation and promoting collagen accumulation in the myocardial interstitium. To investigate whether AS-IV and Tan IIA ameliorate cardiac fibrosis through suppression of excessive collagen cross-linking and deposition, we systematically evaluated collagen synthesis and deposition in infarcted mouse hearts using three complementary metrics including collagen I and III protein expression, hydroxyproline quantification, and collagen volume fraction. Simultaneously, collagen cross-linking was assessed by measuring deoxypyridinoline (DPD) content, a specific cross-linking amino acid. As shown in – , AS-IV, Tan IIA, and Co inhibited collagen I and III protein expression, decreased hydroxyproline and collagen volume fraction, and significantly reduced DPD content. The results from the DPD and CVF assays indicated that the efficacy of Co was greater than that of AS-IV or Tan II A, although the difference was not statistically significant. In contrast, the hydroxyproline assay results demonstrated that the therapeutic effect of Tan IIA was superior to both AS-IV and Co. These findings suggest that AS-IV and Tan IIA may alleviate cardiac fibrosis by inhibiting collagen cross-linking and deposition. LOX is a crucial enzyme involved in the cross-linking of collagen. To further investigate whether AS-IV and Tan IIA can mitigate collagen cross-linking and deposition by inhibiting LOX, we assessed the protein expression and content of LOX of the infarcted tissue. The results indicated that AS-IV, Tan IIA, and Co reduced both the content and protein expression of LOX ( and ). Myofibroblasts are the primary effector cells involved in cardiac fibrosis. We assessed LOX expression in myofibroblasts using fluorescence double staining with antibodies targeting LOX and the myofibroblast marker α-SMA. The results indicated that the fluorescence intensity of α-SMA+ LOX+ was diminished ( ), suggesting that AS-IV, Tan IIA and Co can inhibit LOX expression in myofibroblasts. In particular, Co demonstrated the most significant inhibitory effect on the expression of LOX in myofibroblasts ( ).

 

## AS-IV and Tan IIA Regulate CTR1/ATOX1 Copper Transport Pathway in Myocardial Infarction Model Mice
LOX is an enzyme that depends on copper ions for its activity. To investigate whether AS-IV and Tan IIA downregulate LOX through modulation of the CTR1/ATOX1 copper transport pathway, we assessed the protein expression levels of CTR1 and ATOX1 in the cardiac infarction region. The results showed that AS-IV, Tan IIA and Co inhibited the expression of CTR1 and ATOX1 proteins in the infarcted heart tissues ( – ). The effect of Co is more pronounced compared to both AS-IV and Tan IIA. Furthermore, immunofluorescence co-localization staining proved that AS-IV and Tan IIA reduced the protein expression of CTR1 and ATOX1, specifically in the myofibroblasts ( – ). Myocardial ischemia triggers cardiac copper dyshomeostasis characterized by myocardial copper deficiency and hypercupremia. We measured copper concentrations in the serum and infarcted cardiac tissues to investigate whether AS-IV and Tan IIA-mediated downregulation of the CTR1/ATOX1 pathway exacerbates disturbances in copper homeostasis. Interestingly, AS-IV, Tan IIA, and Co were found to restore cardiac copper homeostasis, as indicated by the observed increase in cardiac copper levels ( ) and a corresponding decrease in serum copper levels ( ). Furthermore, the combined administration of AS-IV and Tan IIA demonstrated a significantly enhanced capacity to improve cardiac copper homeostasis compared to their individual applications.

 

## AS-IV and Tan IIA Inhibit TGF-β-Induced Collagen Cross-Linking in Myofibroblasts via LOX-Dependent Downregulation
Building on our previous findings that AS-IV and Tan IIA can inhibit the expression of LOX in myofibroblasts within the myocardial infarction area; we established an in vitro model of cardiac myofibroblast fibrosis induced by TGF-β to elucidate further the regulatory effects of AS-IV and Tan IIA on LOX expression and collagen cross-linking in myofibroblasts. During the model validation phase, the expression levels of the myofibroblast marker α-SMA were assessed using Western blotting and immunofluorescence techniques. The results indicated that the protein expression of α-SMA was significantly elevated in the TGF-β-treated group compared to the control group, as evidenced by increased fluorescence intensity. This finding confirms the successful establishment of the in vitro fibrosis model (Figure S1A). We employed the CCK-8 cell viability assay in conjunction with Western blotting to evaluate the expression of collagen I, thereby determining the optimal concentrations of AS-IV and Tan IIA. The experimental data revealed that AS-IV exhibited minimal cytotoxicity within the concentration range of 2.5–80 μg/mL (Figure S1B), with a significant reduction in collagen I protein expression observed at 40 μg/mL compared to the control group (Figure S1C). Similarly, Tan IIA demonstrated low cytotoxicity at concentrations of 0.5–2 μg/mL (Figure S1B) and significantly inhibited collagen I expression at concentrations of 1 μg/mL and above (Figure S1D). Notably, the combination of AS-IV and Tan IIA reduced the cytotoxicity of Tan IIA (Figure S1B). Furthermore, it enhanced the inhibitory effect of AS-IV on collagen I expression (Figure S1E). Based on these findings, we selected 40 μg/mL of AS-IV and 2 μg/mL of Tan IIA for subsequent experimental interventions. We evaluated collagen synthesis and cross-linking levels from multiple perspectives to elucidate the regulatory effects of AS-IV and Tan IIA on collagen metabolism. Western blotting analysis demonstrated that the expression levels of collagen I and collagen III were reduced in the AS-IV, Tan IIA, and co-treatment groups ( ). Hydroxyproline content was decreased as detected by hydrolysis assays ( ), and ELISA analysis revealed a reduction in the collagen cross-linking marker DPD ( ), suggesting that AS-IV and Tan IIA can simultaneously inhibit both collagen synthesis and cross-linking processes. Among the three treatment methods, Co exhibited the most pronounced inhibitory effect on hydroxyproline formation, while Tan IIA showed the greatest efficacy in reducing DPD levels. Further investigation into the regulatory mechanism of LOX revealed that the protein expression of LOX was diminished in the group treated with AS-IV, Tan IIA, and Co ( ). ELISA detection further confirmed the decreased protein content of LOX ( ), and enzyme activity assays showed a significant reduction in its activity by 10.3%–15.5% (p < 0.01, ). All three treatments significantly reduced LOX expression and activity in myofibroblasts, with the combination of AS-IV and Tan IIA exhibiting the most robust inhibitory effect on LOX activity. These multidimensional data confirm that AS-IV and Tan IIA significantly inhibit LOX expression and function.

 

## AS-IV and Tan IIA Target the CTR1/ATOX1 Pathway in Myofibroblasts and Regulate the Redistribution of Copper Ions
In our preliminary animal experiments, we observed that AS-IV and Tan IIA significantly inhibited the expression of the copper transporter CTR1 and its chaperone protein ATOX1 in infarcted myocardial tissue. This inhibitory effect was associated with the co-localization of myofibroblasts. To elucidate the specific regulatory effects of AS-IV and Tan IIA on the copper transport pathway involving CTR1 and ATOX1 in myofibroblasts, this study systematically examined the expression changes of related molecules using RT-qPCR, Western blotting, and immunofluorescence techniques. The experimental data showed that consistent with the animal experimental findings, AS-IV, Tan IIA, and their co-treatment reduced both the transcriptional and protein expression levels of CTR1 and ATOX1 ( – ). Given the inhibitory effect of AS-IV and Tan IIA on the copper transport pathway, we further measured intracellular copper content using a copper assay kit and a rhodamine copper ion fluorescent probe. The quantitative analysis indicated that AS-IV, Tan IIA, and their co-treatment decreased the copper ion concentration in myofibroblasts, with consistent results from both detection methods ( and ). The combination of AS-IV and Tan II A exerted a significantly stronger inhibitory effect against copper overload in myofibroblasts than either compound administered alone. Considering the critical role of copper chaperone proteins such as ATP7A, ATP7B, CCS, and COX17 in maintaining intracellular copper homeostasis, we assessed the expression changes of these proteins using Western blotting. The results revealed that AS-IV, Tan IIA, and their co-treatment normalized the abnormal expression of these proteins, specifically inhibiting the expression of ATP7A, ATP7B, and COX17 while increasing the expression of CCS ( ). This differential regulatory effect suggests that AS-IV and Tan IIA may precisely regulate copper metabolic homeostasis by redistributing the intracellular copper ion pool.

 

## Overexpressing CTR1 or Silencing CTR1 and Recombinant LOX Both Impaired the Effects of AS-IV and Tan IIA on Ameliorating TGF-β-Induced Collagen Cross-Linking in Myofibroblasts
Using plasmid transfection and siRNA interference technology, we established models for CTR1 overexpression ( ) and CTR1 knockdown combined with LOX reconstitution ( ) in myofibroblasts. To systematically evaluate the effects of AS-IV and Tan IIA intervention on intracellular copper homeostasis and extracellular matrix remodeling, we quantified the collagen cross-linking-specific marker DPD, matrix protein collagen I, hydroxyproline concentrations, LOX content and copper ion levels. The experimental results demonstrated that CTR1 overexpression in myofibroblasts promoted collagen production and cross-linking, as shown by elevated levels of collagen I, hydroxyproline, DPD. Conversely, AS-IV, Tan IIA, and Co suppressed the expression of these collagen markers, consistent with previous reports. Importantly, CTR1 overexpression mitigated the inhibitory effects of AS-IV, Tan IIA, and Co on collagen synthesis and cross-linking in myofibroblasts ( – ). A corresponding trend was observed for intracellular LOX and copper levels, further supporting the coherence of these results ( – ). In CTR1-silenced myofibroblasts with LOX reconstitution, Quantitative analysis revealed that CTR1 knockdown combined with LOX reconstitution increased hydroxyproline levels ( ), collagen I expression ( ), DPD contents ( ) and LOX contents ( ). Although AS-IV, Tan IIA, and Co intervention partially counteracted these increases, their inhibitory efficacy was diminished compared to untransfected myofibroblasts. Furthermore, basal copper levels showed no significant alteration, suggesting the potential existence of compensatory copper transport mechanisms ( ). Notably, AS-IV, Tan IIA, and Co failed to suppress copper accumulation in CTR1-deficient cells ( ). These findings collectively indicate that CTR1 is an upstream target through which AS-IV and Tan IIA inhibit LOX-mediated collagen cross-linking and deposition. The pharmacological effects on collagen homeostasis were mechanistically linked to CTR1-dependent regulation of copper ion dynamics.

 

## Binding Verification of AS-IV and Tan IIA with CTR1
Cimetidine served as the positive control for molecular docking, given its well-characterized function as a CTR1 inhibitor. Docking simulations revealed that both AS-IV and Tan IIA could bind to human CTR1, with computed binding energies of −7.2 kcal/moL and −8.1 kcal/moL, respectively (Tables S3 and S4), indicating a high binding affinity. Notably, the binding energy for each monomer was more favorable than that of the reference inhibitor cimetidine (−4.8 kcal/moL; Table S5 and ). Mechanistic analysis of the binding poses indicated that AS-IV formed two hydrogen bonds with residues at the CTR1 site involving Arg-15 ( ), while Tan IIA formed one hydrogen bond with Ser-501 ( ). Hydrophobic interactions further contributed to stabilizing the AS-IV/CTR1 and Tan IIA/CTR1 complexes. Supporting these findings, CETSA results demonstrated that treatment with AS-IV or Tan IIA, unlike the DMSO control, significantly stabilized CTR1 against thermal denaturation, providing further evidence for a direct interaction between these compounds and the CTR1 protein ( ).

 

## Discussion
Currently, the management of cardiac fibrosis primarily emphasizes controlling risk factors, largely due to the lack of targeted therapies. The development of traditional anti-fibrotic drugs, especially those targeting individual molecules, faces significant challenges due to the unique pathophysiology of the heart and the necessity for precise spatiotemporal modulation. Given the extensive experience of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) in managing cardiovascular diseases, it represents a promising avenue for identifying agents and monomers that can combat cardiac fibrosis. Consequently, this study proposes novel candidate therapeutics aimed at addressing post-myocardial infarction cardiac fibrosis.
In the clinical practice of TCM for treating cardiovascular diseases, boosting qi invigorating blood is a prevalent therapeutic approach. AS-IV and Tan IIA are the primary components of the representative herb pair, astragalus flavone and salvia miltiorrhiza, the representative pharmacological agent for boosting qi invigorating blood. Based on the C57BL/6J mouse myocardial infarction model, this study systematically investigated the effects of AS-IV and Tan IIA intervention on cardiac fibrosis extent and cardiac function. The research results indicate that AS-IV and Tan IIA significantly alleviate cardiac fibrosis and promote the recovery of cardiac function. Further mechanistic studies revealed that AS-IV and Tan IIA inhibit collagen cross-linking in myofibroblasts by regulating the CTR1/ATOX1/LOX signaling axis, thereby reducing abnormal extracellular matrix deposition. This mechanism may represent one of the critical pathways underlying its cardioprotective effects. It is worth highlighting that this study elucidated the anti-cardiac fibrosis mechanism of AS-IV and Tan IIA from the perspective of the molecular correlation between copper homeostasis regulation and collagen cross-linking for the first time.
Previous studies have demonstrated that AS-IV can effectively suppress cardiac fibrosis induced by isoproterenol and hypoxia. , In contrast, Tan IIA has also ameliorated cardiac fibrosis in renal hypertensive rats. Our research demonstrates that AS-IV and Tan IIA can effectively suppress the progression of pathological cardiac remodeling following myocardial infarction. Specifically, AS-IV and Tan IIA significantly reduce myocardial collagen fiber deposition, attenuate cardiomyocyte pathological hypertrophy, and improve cardiac function, further confirming the therapeutic potential of AS-IV and Tan IIA in mitigating cardiac fibrosis. Meanwhile, this study reveals a synergistic anti-fibrotic effect between AS-IV and Tan IIA. Compared with monotreatment, the dual-agent regimen exhibits significantly enhanced efficacy and stability in most measured parameters. Pharmacologically, the combination of AS-IV and Tan IIA effectively addresses individual compound limitations; AS-IV reduces the cytotoxicity of Tan IIA, while Tan IIA substantially improves the bioavailability of AS-IV, collectively optimizing therapeutic performance. This finding aligns with the conclusions of prior studies, thereby providing a robust pharmacodynamic foundation for applying TCM compatibility, guided by the theory of boosting qi invigorating blood in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases. Notably, the low bioavailability of AS-IV and Tan IIA poses a major challenge to their clinical translation. In recent years, advanced drug delivery systems including liposomes, nanoparticles, and extracellular vesicles derived from TCM have shown considerable promise in overcoming the poor bioavailability of herbal bioactive components. , .
Collagen fibers are formed primarily from collagen precursors outside the cell through a cross-linking process. However, excessive collagen cross-linking can increase collagen fibers’ stiffness, making them difficult to degrade. When the rate of collagen fiber formation surpasses the degradation rate, this imbalance facilitates the accumulation of collagen fibers within the myocardial interstitium, potentially impairing myocardial function. This study demonstrated that AS-IV and Tan IIA significantly attenuated excessive collagen cross-linking in the infarcted heart region. Cardiac fibroblasts serve as the primary effector cells responsible for collagen synthesis. Following myocardial infarction, the elevated secretion of TGF-β triggers cardiac fibroblasts’ transformation into myofibroblasts, subsequently leading to enhanced collagen synthesis. Our research demonstrated that AS-IV and Tan IIA significantly attenuated TGF-β-induced collagen synthesis in myofibroblasts and inhibited cross-linking in vitro.
Mechanistically, LOX is an enzyme primarily utilizing collagen as a substrate, facilitating the cross-linking reaction of amino acids within collagen. Previous studies have demonstrated that the expression of the LOX is upregulated in the infarcted heart region and partially colocalizes with myofibroblasts. , Moreover, the function of LOX is regulated by the level of gene transcription and protein expression and LOX activity. Our results showed that AS-IV and Tan IIA can significantly suppress the expression and activity of LOX in cardiac infarction regions and TGF-β-induced myofibroblasts.
Since LOX is a copper-dependent enzyme, abnormalities in copper homeostasis within the body can directly affect its normal function. CTR1 mediates the cellular uptake of copper, and ATOX1 subsequently delivers copper to LOX via ATP7A/ATP7B-mediated transport, ensuring its optimal catalytic activity. Our research findings demonstrate that AS-IV and Tan IIA effectively inhibit the CTR1/ATOX1 pathway in infarcted heart tissue, as well as in myofibroblasts induced by TGF-β, thereby inhibiting LOX activity through the reduction of copper transport to the LOX enzyme. Through experiments involving the overexpression of CTR1 and the silencing of CTR1 recombinant LOX, we further confirmed the regulatory effect of AS-IV and Tan IIA on the CTR1/ATOX1/LOX signaling pathway. CTR1 overexpression attenuates the inhibitory effects of AS-IV and Tan IIA on copper accumulation and collagen cross-linking in TGF-β-induced myofibroblasts. After silencing CTR1 and reconstituting LOX, AS-IV and Tan IIA failed to reverse intracellular copper accumulation. Consistent with the findings from CTR1 overexpression experiments, this intervention reduced the inhibitory effects on collagen cross-linking, thereby demonstrating that CTR1 functions as an upstream regulatory target of AS-IV and Tan IIA.
Following myocardial infarction, cardiac copper ion depletion occurs concomitant with elevated serum copper concentrations. Our research found that although AS-IV and Tan IIA inhibited CTR1, it ameliorated copper homeostatic abnormalities. Specifically, AS-IV and Tan IIA increased the concentration of copper ions in the heart while decreasing their concentration in the serum. The restoration of copper homeostasis in the heart can be attributed to the ability of AS-IV and Tan IIA to reduce cardiomyocyte damage and restore copper uptake in these cells. Intriguingly, diminished cardiac copper ion levels paradoxically enhanced LOX activity and upregulated gene expression, a phenomenon mechanistically linked to intracellular copper redistribution. Intracellular copper distribution is primarily regulated by copper chaperone proteins that bind to specific target proteins. Besides the ATOX1-ATP7A/ATP7B copper transfer pathway, other copper chaperone proteins include the superoxide dismutase one copper chaperone (CCS) and the cytochrome c oxidase copper chaperone (COX17), among others. These molecular escorts specifically deliver copper to distinct targets; CCS transports copper to superoxide dismutase, while COX17 is responsible for delivering copper to cytochrome oxidase deficient homolog 1(Sco1) and Sco2, respectively. , Our findings demonstrated that AS-IV and Tan IIA suppress copper accumulation in TGF-β-induced myofibroblasts and downregulates the expression of ATP7A, ATP7B, and COX17 while upregulating CCS expression. Under reduced intracellular copper levels, these copper chaperones exhibited paradoxical expression patterns, suggesting that AS-IV and Tan IIA ameliorate intracellular copper distribution.
This study has several limitations. First, allocation concealment was not implemented after randomization, which could have led to selection or performance bias. In particular, during drug administration and daily observations, the awareness of group assignments among operators might have inadvertently influenced their actions. Nevertheless, all procedures adhered to a strict standardized protocol, and all data were accurately recorded. Second, the precise mechanism by which AS-IV and Tan IIA restore abnormalities in cardiac and blood copper homeostasis remains to be elucidated. Furthermore, it is necessary to clarify whether the regulatory effects of AS-IV and Tan IIA on copper homeostasis in other target cells in the heart, such as cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and macrophages, differ from their effects on myofibroblasts. What’s more, the present study is constrained by its use of single fixed doses of AS‑IV and Tan IIA. Although chosen based on robust prior evidence, the lack of comprehensive dose–response and combination analyses means the optimal ratio and interaction type remain undefined. Subsequent work should employ expanded dose ranges alongside formal interaction analyses to clarify these parameters. Last, this study, although lacking in vivo validation experiments using CTR1 overexpressed mice, provides compelling evidence that AS-IV and Tan IIA ameliorate abnormal collagen cross-linking following myocardial infarction and exert anti-cardiac fibrosis effects through the regulation of the CTR1/ATOX1/LOX pathway.

## Conclusion
In summary, our study reveals that AS-IV and Tan IIA effectively inhibit collagen cross-linking and collagen synthesis in myofibroblasts by downregulating the CTR1/ATOX1/LOX pathway. This subsequently ameliorates abnormal collagen cross-linking and deposition in the heart following myocardial infarction, thereby significantly inhibiting the progression of cardiac fibrosis ( ).
