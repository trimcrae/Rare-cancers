Intrinsic microbiome-derived short-chain fatty acid (SCFAs) like acetate modulates immune cell function and metabolism has been well studied. However, it remains poorly investigated about the effects and the underlying mechanism of exogenous acetate in acute inflammation like sepsis. Here, we observed that serum acetate accumulates in patients undergoing abdominal gastrointestinal surgery and in septic mice. Short exposure to high-dose exogenous acetate protects mice from sepsis by inhibiting glycolysis in macrophages, both in vivo and in vitro. Hypoxia-inducible factor 1 subunit alpha (HIF-1α) stabilization or overexpression reverses the decreased glycolysis and pro-inflammatory cytokine production in macrophages and abrogates acetate’s protective effect in septic mice. Meanwhile, we also found acetyl-CoA synthetase-2, but not GPR41 or GPR43, plays a key role in acetate’s immunosuppressive effect. Acetate transiently increases acetyl-coenzyme A production, promoting histone acetylation and decreasing acetyl-transfer to NF-κB p65. These findings suggest that short exposure to mM-level acetate inhibits macrophage immune response linked to HIF-1α-dependent glycolysis. Taken together, we demonstrate short-term exposure of exogenous acetate could regulate inflammatory responses through attenuating HIF-1α-dependent glycolysis.

## Introduction
Sepsis, characterized by an inappropriate amplified systemic inflammatory response resulting in multi-organ failure and mortality, is the leading cause of death in the intensive care unit. The excessive innate immune response, including the overwhelming production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, plays a critical role in the pathophysiology of sepsis during its early stage [ , ].
Immunomodulation by short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) provides essential insights into metabolic reprogramming and protein modifications during inflammation [ , ]. While the role of SCFAs derived from gut microbiota in regulating homeostasis and tissue-resident immune cells has been extensively studied [ ], the effects of exogenous SCFAs on infection remain less understood. Acetate functions both as a nutritional source and as a ligand for a small group of broadly expressed G protein-coupled receptors(GPCRs) [ ]. It can signal from the extracellular compartment to the cytoplasm by activating GPR41 or GPR43, or it may penetrate into cells where it is converted into acetyl-CoA by acetyl-coenzyme A synthetase (ACSS1) or ACSS2 [ ], Acetate demonstrates potential as an immune regulator affecting Th17/Treg Cells [ ], neutrophils [ ], and PBMCs [ ], exerting subsequent effects on inflammation through mechanisms such as acetylation, fatty acid metabolism, and signaling transduction. Recent studies have shown that acetate accumulates in host serum within hours following systemic single bacterial infections and enhances T cell function within adoptive immunity systems [ ]. However, the impact of short-term exposure to acetate during acute inflammation remains controversial, with its underlying mechanisms still incompletely defined.
Macrophages, as the primary producers of cytokines during sepsis, depend on glycolysis to execute their effector functions [ – ]. The increased influx of glucose and subsequent glycolytic activity are regulated by a complex interplay of factors, including HIF-1α and AKT-mTOR signaling pathways [ – ], as well as processes such as histone acetylation. Recent studies have underscored the role of endogenous acetate derived from glucose in macrophage glucose metabolism and its integration with mitochondrial metabolism via the acetate-acetyl-CoA pathway [ ]. Our prior research has shown that acetate can inhibit the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome via a GPR43-dependent mechanism [ ]. However, it remains unclear how exogenous acetate affects the immune function of macrophages during systemic inflammation through metabolic and functional reprogramming.
In the present study, we observed that serum acetate accumulates in systemic inflammation. Short-term exposure to high-dose exogenous acetate protect septic mice by inhibiting HIF-1α/glycolysis in macrophages. Additionally, we identified ACSS2, rather than GPR41 or GPR43, as a key player in mediating acetate’s immunosuppressive effects. Acetate transiently enhances acetyl-coenzyme A production, which promotes histone acetylation while simultaneously reducing acetyl transfer to NF-κB p65. These findings suggest that brief exposure to mM levels of acetate inhibits the immune response of macrophages through a mechanism linked to HIF-1α-dependent glycolysis.

## Materials and methods

## Patient samples
The study received approval from the Changhai Hospital Ethics Committee (Shanghai, China; approval no. CHEC2018-080). Serum samples were collected from patients undergoing major abdominal gastrointestinal surgery who were infused with either 500 ml of Ringer’s acetate solution (n = 6) or Ringer’s solution (n = 6) at Shanghai Changhai Hospital between December 2018 and October 2019. We included 12 patients aged between 48 ∼ 65 years, ASA physical status I, who underwent selective colorectal cancer resection, surgery duration less than 2 h. Maintenance of anesthesia, hemodynamic management, and surgical procedures were performed according to the departmental standard operating procedures. Blood samples were taken 3 h after surgery for relevant tests.

## Mice
Male C57BL/6 mice between 7 ∼ 10 weeks of age were obtained from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal (Shanghai, China). Mice were housed in cages with constant-flow air exchange and fed standard lab chow and water ad libitum. All animal experiments were performed with the approval of the Scientific Investigation Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Naval Medical University.

## Sepsis models
Polymicrobial sepsis was induced by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) model in mice by the method described by Baker et al. [ , ]. In brief, anesthesia was induced with sevoflurane inhalation (2 ∼ 4%). The cecum was exposed through an anterior median abdominal incision and ligated at 50% distance from its root. A through-and-through puncture was made on the anti-mesenteric side with a 22-gauge needle and a small amount of feces was extruded through the puncture holes to ensure perforation. The cecum was then replaced in its original location, and the peritoneum and skin were sutured in turn, and the mice were injected subcutaneously with 1 ml of saline solution. Sham mice underwent the above process except for ligation and puncture. Depending on the experiment, mice were euthanized either at 6 h after surgery to harvest blood or at 12 h after surgery to harvest livers and lungs. For the survival assessment, animals were observed for up to 7 days. GM-CSF (37.5 µg/kg, PeproTech) [ ], IFN-γ (1 µg/mouse, PeproTech) [ ] or DMOG (Dimethyloxallyl Glycine) (320 mg/kg, MedChem Express) [ ] were injected 3 h before CLP. For the LPS-induced sepsis, mice were injected intraperitoneally with the indicated dose of LPS derived from Escherichia coli 0111:B4 (Sigma-Aldrich) diluted in 100 µl saline.

## Treatment of acetate
For in vivo experiments, 500 ul PBS with or without acetate (500 mg/kg or indicated dose) was injected intraperitoneally 2 h after the establishment of the septic animal models. In the study of macrophages, 10 mM acetate was treated 30 min before LPS (100 ng/ml) stimulation, unless indicated.

## Tissue histology
Mice subjected to sham or septic models were sacrificed at the indicated times after surgery. Wholelung and livers samples for histological examination were excised, perfused with 10% formalin, and placed in fresh formalin for an additional 48 h. Routine histological techniques were used to paraffin-embed tissue, and 3-µm sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin.

## Measurements of pro-inflammatory cytokines and organ injury markers
Commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were used to measure the concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β in serum or culture medium according to the manufacturer’s instructions of R&D Systems. Serum levels of aspartate transaminase (AST) and serum urea (BUN) were measured by the Clinical Pathology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Naval Medical University using standardized techniques (Automatic Analyzer, HITACHI).

## Bacterial clearance
Bacterial counts were performed on peritoneal lavage fluid. Peritoneal lavage fluid samples were harvested under aseptic conditions and serially diluted in sterile saline and cultured on tryptic soy agar pour plates. Plates were incubated at 37℃ for 24∼48 h, and colony counts were performed by direct visualization.

## Macrophages isolation and culture
Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) Bone marrow were collected and cultured for 6 days in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FCS), 10 ng/ml recombinant mouse M-CSF (PeproTech) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin on 100 mm dish as standard protocols. On day 7, BMDMs were harvested, seeded and incubated overnight before use. Peritoneal macrophages (PM) Briefly, mice peritoneal exudate cells were collected by lavage with PBS, 4 days after intraperitoneally injection with 2.5 ml of 3% sterile thioglycollate (Sigma-Aldrich). Cells adhered overnight in RPMI 1640 with 10% FCS at 37 °C and 5% CO2 atmosphere before use.

## Lactate dehydrogenase cytotoxicity assay
Cytotoxicity was measured using a lactate dehydrogenase cytotoxicity assay, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Pierce LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit; Thermo). Briefly, BMDMs were plated as 4 × 104 cells/well in 100 µl. After treatment, 50 µl culture supernatant was collected and mixed with 50 µl LDH Substrate Mix. Absorbance at 490 nm was measured after 30 min incubation at room temperature.

## Gene expression analysis
Total cellular RNA was prepared using the Trizol Reagent (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) followed by RNA cleanup with the RNeasy Mini kit (TaKaRa) as recommended by the manufacturer. SYBR green-based quantitative RT-PCR was performed using the Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus® Real-Time PCR System. Primer sequences used are listed in Supplemental Table 1. The 2-ΔΔCt change-in-cycling-threshold method was used for calculation of relative changes in expression. Signals were normalized by β-actin, and compared with control groups.

## Western blotting
Approximately 2 × 106 BMDMs or PM were lysed on ice into radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer containing complete Mini Protease Inhibitor (Roche), phosphatase inhibitor cocktails (Sigma-Aldrich), 5 µM trichostatin A, and 10 mM nicotinamide (deacetylase inhibitors) and cleared by centrifugation at 20,000 g. The protein content in cleared supernatants was estimated by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Pierce), and 10 to 50 µg of protein were denatured in Laemmli buffer, separated on a 6 to 15% gel by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% bovine serum albumin in tris-buffered saline (TBS) and stained overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Secondary antibodies were incubated for at least1 h at room temperature. Pierce ECL Western Blotting Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 32106) was used to detect protein signals. ImageJ was used for quantifying the band intensity and signals for a-tubulin were used as loading control.

## Flow cytometry assay
For Glut1 staining, stimulated BMDMs were harvested, blocked and incubated with isotype or Glut-1 antibody (Abcam, ab652) and F4/80 antibody at 22℃ for 30 min according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Flow cytometry assays were then performed. For in vitro 2-NBDG (2-[N-(7-nitrobenz-2-oxa-1,3-diazol-4-yl) amino]-2-deoxy-D-glucose) staining, stimulated BMDMs were washed twice with PBS and incubated with 0.1% 2-NBDG (Life, N13195) in 1640 free of FCS or glucose at 37℃ for 15 min. Then BMDMs were harvested and stained F4/80 under the standard protocol. For in vivo 2-NBDG staining experiments mice received 2-DG (2 g/kg) or vehicle control intravenously 30 min before receiving LPS (5 µ g) intravenously. Acetate (500 mg/kg) was administrated i.p 30 min before LPS stimulation. 1 h later, 2-NBDG (50 µ g) was also injected intravenously and mice were killed 15 min later. Splenic Gr1−CD11b+F4/80+ macrophages were analyzed by flow cytometry for the uptake of 2-NBDG. All data were acquired using the FACS Calibur (BD Biosciences) and analyzed by FlowJo software (Tree Star, Ashland, OR, USA).

## Metabolic activity analysis
Medium lactate and glucose were determined using Automatic Analyzer (Hitachi High-Tech, 7600–120, Japan). For real-time analysis of the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) and the oxygen consumption rate (OCR), BMDMs were analyzed using an XF-96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience). In brief, BMDMs were seeded onto an XF-96 bio-flux plate (Seahorse Bioscience) at a pre-optimized final concentration of 5 × 104 cells/well in 200 µl and either left unstimulated or stimulated with 100ng/ml LPS in the presence of acetate (10 mM) or not. 1 h after LPS stimulation, cell culture medium was washed and changed with 200 µl XF assay medium. Then, the cell culture plate was placed in a CO2-free incubator for at least 0.5 h. Inhibitors (0.5µM Oligomycin, 1 µM FCCP (Carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone), 2DG (2-Deoxy-D-glucose), 1 µM Rotenone, 1 µM antimycin A) were added to the appropriate port of the injector plate, and run on XF-96 Extracellular Flux Analyzer according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## siRNA silencing and overexpression studies
Primary mouse peritoneal macrophages were grown to 70 ∼ 80% confluence in 1640 containing 10% FBS before RNA interference against mouse GPR41 or GPR43 using the INTERFERin transfection reagent (Polyplus Transfection). RNA interference was performed using siRNAs targeted and compared with a control/scrambled siRNA (GenePharma). Cells were seeded and maintained overnight, then transfected with gene-specific siRNAs via INTERFERin (Polyplus) for 36 ∼ 48 h, and stimulated with LPS (100 ng ml). For Hif-1α overexpression assay in RAW 264.7 cells were transfected using Jet-Pei macrophages (Polyplus Transfection). The plasmids were transfected using lipofactamine 3000 (Invitrogen) for overexpression of Hif-1α. The primers for siRNA and expression plasmid of HIF-1α were listed in Supplemental Table 1.

## Statistical analysis
Data are reported as mean ± SD (except for survival curves). Survival rates (n = 7–10) were expressed as percentages, and analyzed by using the log-rank test. Other data were analyzed with the unpaired Student t test or Oneway-anova test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The correlation between IL-6 and acetate was compared using Spearman rank correlation analysis. The results are representative of two or three independent experiments with five mice in each group.

## Result

## Acetate is increased in serum under inflammation and can protect from septic death
To investigate the effects of acetate in inflammation, we retrospectively examined the levels of acetate and inflammatory cytokines in serum of patients undergoing major abdominal gastrointestinal surgery (Clinical characteristics data see Supplemental Table 2). Our data demonstrated a linear relationship between serum acetate levels and IL-6 concentrations in these patients. In the Spearman correlation rank analysis, we found that acetate was positively correlated with IL-6 (r = 0.3733, p < 0.05) (Supplemental Fig. 1.1 A). As expected, comparing with the control group (infusion of 500 ml Ringer’s solution), serum acetate levels were significantly elevated 3 h after surgery in patients receiving acetate treatment (infusion of 500 ml Ringer’s acetate solution) (Fig.  A). The abdominal surgery-induced upregulation of IL-6 was notably reduced in patients treated with acetate (Fig.  B). Our findings also showed acetate significantly reduced the LPS-stimulated secretion of IL-6 and TNF-α in isolated CD14+ monocytes derived from healthy volunteers (Fig.  C). We subsequently employed the CLP model, a systemic polybacterial infection model, to investigate the serum level of acetate. Our findings revealed a linear correlation between serum acetate levels and IL-6 in septic mice (r = 0.6351, p < 0.01) (Supplemental Fig. 1.1 B). Serum acetate levels were elevated 2.2-fold at 6 h and 4.3-fold at 24 h (Fig.  D) after CLP. Interestingly, the serum acetate levels remained unchanged following the sole administration of exogenous acetate, while CLP treated with acetate significantly elevated the serum acetate levels (Supplemental Fig. 1.1 C, D). Moreover, acetate levels in major organs remain unchanged or exhibit a decrease (Supplemental Fig. 1E). These findings indicate that systemic inflammation plays a crucial role in the induction of intrinsic acetate.

 

In addition, we observed that the exogenous administration of acetate significantly improved the survival rates of CLP mice in a dose-dependent manner (Fig.  E)., reduced the expression of inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β) in serum after 24 h of CLP (Fig.  F), decreased hepatic and renal functions markers (ALT and BUN) (Supplemental Fig. 1.1 F, G), and improved the pathology of lung (Fig.  G). The analysis of cell counting changes in the inflammatory infiltrating cells within the livers of CLP mice (Supplemental Fig. 1.1 I, J) revealed no significant differences between the acetate-treated group and the sham group. Administration of acetate at a concentration of 50 mM in drinking water for three days prior to CLP significantly enhanced survival rates (Supplemental Fig. 1.1 K). More importantly, the intraperitoneal administration of acetate at 2/4 h post-CLP still significantly improved the survival rates (Fig.  H). The data indicate the potential preventive and therapeutic effects of acetate in the context of sepsis. In BMDMs. We observed that acetate concentrations as low as 10 mM significantly inhibited the production and expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig.  I, J). Notably, no significant changes in cell viability were detected even at a concentration of 100 mM (Supplemental Fig. 1.2 A). Furthermore, both acetate and acetic acid demonstrated a dose-dependent inhibition of inflammatory cytokine production induced by LPS (Supplemental Fig. 1.2 B, C). The pH of the culture media (data not shown) remained unchanged with the lowest dose of acetate (10 mM), which was used for subsequent research.
Sepsis can occur as a result of various bacterial, viral, or fungal infections [ ]. To further explore the effects of acetate on other TLR signaling pathways, we stimulated BMDMs with poly(I:C) (a ligand for TLR3) and CpG (1 µM; a ligand for TLR9). Acetate significantly inhibited cytokine production induced by all three ligands (Fig.  K), indicating that its effects are not limited to TLR4. These findings suggest that acetate may have a broader role in the context of both bacterial and viral infections.
Ligation of TLR4 results in the activation of nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein (MAP) kinases, ultimately promoting cytokine transcription. The inhibitory effect of acetate on NF-κB phosphorylation was observed as early as 15 min (Fig.  L), while the MAP kinase pathways (ERK, JNK, and p38) in BMDMs remain unchanged (Supplemental Fig. 1.2 D). Furthermore, we observed that the LPS induction of M2 polarization genes, Arg1, Rentnla and Mgl2 (Supplemental Fig. 1.2 E), in acetate-pretreated BMDMs were unaffected compared to LPS group, which implied that acetate exposure may not be able to switch the polarization of macrophage. These data suggest that acetate ameliorates inflammatory cytokine production and negatively regulates NF-kB signaling in macrophages.

## Acetate ameliorate inflammatory response through glycolysis attenuation
Metabolic rewiring from oxidative phosphorylation toward glycolysis is essential for mounting efficient pro-inflammatory responses in macrophages. It has been suggested that acetate can regulate glucose metabolism [ , ]. To further investigate the role of glucose metabolism in the immuno-suppressive effect of acetate in macrophages, we first assessed glucose uptake in the culture medium of BMDMs. The glucose levels in the supernatant increased upon stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS), whereas treatment with acetate resulted in a slight yet significant reduction in extracellular glucose levels (Fig.  A). Accordingly, lactate production in culture medium decreased in acetate-treated macrophages upon LPS stimulation (Fig.  A). Next, we used the fluorescent glucose analog 2-NBDG to measure glucose uptake in BMDMs. In line with the increased lactate secretion, glucose uptake was significantly decreased in acetate-treated BMDMs after 0.5 h treatment with LPS (Supplemental Fig. 2A). To investigate the glucose uptake in septic mice, 2-NBDG was intravenously injected after challenge of LPS. Injection of LPS resulted in a mild increase in the uptake of 2-NBDG by splenic macrophages. However, acetate-treated mice showed a lower uptake of 2-NBDG (Fig.  B). These findings indicate that acetate dampened LPS-driven glucose uptake both in vitro and in vivo. Additionally, we found a decrease in essential glycolysis enzymes such as PFKFB3, PKM2 and the main glucose transporter, Glut1 (Fig.  C, Supplemental Fig. 2B). These results indicate that acetate suppressed the metabolic shift toward glycolysis in LPS-stimulated macrophages.

 

We further assessed the effect of acetate on the glucose metabolism of macrophages. The glycolytic and mitochondrial metabolic activity was measured using a Seahorse analyzer. Results from the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) demonstrated that upon 1 h stimulation of LPS, supplementation of acetate resulted in a significant decrease in glycolysis (Fig. 2D, E), whereas OCRs were
unchanged compared to medium control (Fig.  F, G). Acetate (10 mM) alone greatly enhanced mitochondrial OXPHOS, as evidenced by increased basal respiration as early as 1 h after treatment (Fig.  G). Overall, our results indicate that glycolysis was suppressed in acetate-treated macrophages upon immune stimulation.
Next, to investigate whether the glycolysis suppression was essential for the effect of acetate, we used IFN-γ or GM-CSF, which have been shown to enhance glycolytic activity in vitro and in vivo [ , ]. Pre-incubation with IFN-γ or GM-CSF (Fig.  H, I) resulted in nearly completely abrogating the effect of acetate on LPS-induced production of cytokines. As expected, decreased LPS-driven mRNA expression of Glut1, PFKFB3, HK2, and MCT4, as well as the flow cytometry analysis of Glut1 of BMDMs by acetate, were all reversed by IFN-γ or by GM-CSF (Supplemental Fig. 2C-F), indicating the enhancement of glycolysis could reverse the anti-inflammatory effect of acetate. Together, these findings suggest that acetate acts as a key modulator of the immune response of macrophages by affecting glycolysis.

## Stabilization of Hif-1α reverse the anti-inflammatory effect of acetate in vitro and in vivo
To elucidate how acetate mediates decreased glycolysis in BMDMs, we measured the expression levels of the AKT/mTOR/ Hif-1α pathway [ ] which regulates glucose metabolism. Acetate dampened the expression of HIF-1alpha after LPS stimulation in BMDMs (Fig.  A) and the overexpression of Hif-1α abrogated the suppression of IL-6 and TNF-alpha induced by acetate in RAW264.7 cells (Fig.  B). Our results showed that the phosphor- and total AKT remains unchanged in the absence or presence of acetate. The phosphoredmTOR was decreased in acetate treated macrophage 15 min after LPS stimulation. (Supplemental Fig. 3A, B). In the presence of mTOR pathway activator MHY1485 [ ] and 3BDO which enhanced the expression of p70S6K, the downstream target of mTOR (Supplemental Fig. 3C), the suppression of IL-6 and TNF-alpha induced by acetate remains unaltered (Supplemental Fig. 3D). Meanwhile, mTOR inhibitor rapamycin did not reverse the immuno-suppressive effect of acetate (Supplemental Fig. 3D), which suggesting a less possibility of involvement of mTOR axis in the immuno-suppressive effect of acetate. Thus, these data suggeste that Hif-1α, but not AKT/mTOR, regulate the immuno-suppressive effect by acetate.

 

Degradation of HIF-1α by the proteasomes is tightly regulated by its prolyl hydroxylation [28]. Since the mRNA level of Hif-1α was not decreased in acetate treated BMDMs (Supplemental Fig. 3E), we asked whether acetate influences the degradation of Hif-1α protein which is proteasome dependent. We found that proteasome inhibitor MG132 abolished acetate-mediated Hif-1α decrease, as well as the cytokine suppression (Fig.  C, Supplemental Fig. 3F). Furthermore, the expression of hydroxyl-Hif-1α was increased as early as 15 min in acetate treated cells (Fig.  D). Also, we found that DMOG [ ], a competitive inhibitor of Hif-1α prolyl hydroxylase that stabilize the Hif-1α, abolished acetate-mediated alteration in cytokine production (Fig.  E), phosphor- and total p65 (Fig.  F), glucose uptake and lactate excretion (Supplemental Fig. 3G) as well as the mRNA level of glycolysis-related enzymes (Supplemental Fig. 3H). We then injected mice with DMOG before CLP and acetate treatment to evaluate the essential role of hydroxyl-Hif-1α in vivo. Our results showed that the DMOG abrogated the acetate-mediated alleviation of the pathology of liver and lung (Supplemental Fig. 3I). Similarly, DMOG reversed the decreased TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β in serum (Fig.  G) and restored the decreased hepatic and renal functions marker AST and BUN (Fig.  H) induced by acetate compared with control mice. Moreover, DMOG significantly impaired the prolonged survival in acetate treatment CLP mice (Fig.  I). Thus, our result suggest acetate may ameliorate inflammatory cytokine production through increasing the proline hydroxylation and subsequent degradation of Hif-1α.

## Acetate promotes histone acetylation but decreased acetyl-transfer to p65
Exogenous acetate can be transported through transmembrane diffusion or signal via GPR41 or GPR43 [ ]. We and other researchers have found GPR43 has a crucial role in the protection of acetate on innate immune responses, including those of macrophages and neutrophils. Unexpectedly, mRNA of GPR41 and GPR43 was found downregulated in acetate-exposed BMDMs (Supplemental Fig. 4A), and neither the knockdown of GPR43 or GPR41, separately or in combination, reversed the effect of acetate on LPS-induced production of cytokines (Supplemental Fig. 4B, C), suggesting that GPR43 and GPR41 may not be involved in the anti-inflammatory effect of acetate. Next, we found an increased uptake of acetate by macrophages exposed to LPS and exogenous acetate for 6 h (Fig.  A). After cellular uptake, acetate is quickly converted into acetyl-CoA, as our result showed that cellular acetyl-CoA transiently increased in 30 min after LPS exposure in BMDMs pretreated with acetate (Fig.  B). There are two enzymes that have proved to be capable of using acetate as a substrate: acetyl­CoA synthetase 1 (ACSS1) and ACSS2 [ ]. Apart from ACSS1 and ACSS2, ATP Citrate Lyase (ACLY) also accounts for the acetyl-CoA pool expanding in acetate treated memory CD8+ T Cells [ ]. Thus, we measured ACSS1, ACSS2 and ACLY expression and found LPS induced significant increase of ACSS1, ACSS2 and ACLY, however only ACSS2 increased in acetate combined LPS stimulation (Fig.  C). Furthermore, an ACSS2 inhibitor (Fig.  D) or knockdown of ACSS2 (Fig.  E), but not knockdown of ACSS1 or ACLY (Supplemental Fig. 4D, E), reversed the effect of acetate on LPS-induced production of cytokines, supporting the notion that ACSS2 involved in the anti-inflammatory effect of acetate. Together, these results suggeste transmembrane-diffused acetate synthesized to acetyl-CoA by ACSS2 participates in anti-inflammatory effects.

 

We next investigated the subsequent effects of the increased acetyl-CoA. As ACSS2-produced acetyl-CoA is the substrate for protein acetylation, we assessed the impact of acetate on acetylation. Increased acetyl-CoA usually promotes the histone acetylation in nucleus [ ]. We found acetate rapidly promoted the level of H3K9ac, H3K18ac as well as H3K27 in LPS-stimulated and unstimulated macrophages within 15 min (Fig.  F), (Supplemental Fig. 4F). Consistently, histone 4 (H4) acetylation at lysine K8, K5 and K12 was markedly enhanced by acetate exposure relative to control macrophages. (Fig.  G), (Supplemental Fig. 4G). Although global histone acetylation was promoted, we found acetyl transferase (CBP) and lysine de-acetyl transferase (HDAC1,3,4 and Sirt1,3,6) were all downregulated in the acetate treated BMDMs compared with LPS-stimulated macrophages (Fig.  H) (Supplemental Fig. 4H, I). Moreover, neither garcinol, an inhibitor of acetyltransferases [ ], nor TSA, an inhibitor of de-acetyl transferase, could reverse acetate-mediated cytokine suppression or the decreased phosphor-p65 and Hif-1α (Fig.  I-L). And the knockdown of Sirt1/3/5 (Supplemental Fig. 4J) also showed limited influence on acetate effects. These results suggested histone acetylation, though enhanced by acetate treatment, is not essential for acetate-mediated suppression of cytokine production.
Histone acetylation forms an acetyl-pool for acetyl-cycle for histone and non-histone modifications. As acetate decreased acetyl transferase, this may suppress the non-histone acetylation. Indeed, we found acetate treatment decreased the acetylation of Lys310 (Fig.  M), a most-studied acetylation residue of p65 [ , ]. And the total acetylation of p65 was also decreased (Fig.  N). Previous study reported the enhanced acetylation of p65 promoted the inflammatory response in macrophage. Thus, these results suggested acetyl-modification of p65 may involve in acetate-mediated anti-inflammation.
Together, these results show that the acetate enhanced the histone acetylation but suppressed the acetyl-transfer to p65.

## Lipid metabolism may not involved in the immuno-suppressive effect of acetate
Activation of the de novo lipogenesis and cholesterol biosynthesis pathways is also crucial for the inflammatory response to LPS [ – ]. After ligation with CoA by ACSS2, acetate can be converted to acetyl­CoA, which could be carboxylated by acetyl­-CoA carboxylase­1(ACC1) and condensated by fatty acid synthase (FASN) into fatty acids (Fig.  A). To evaluate the role of lipid metabolism, we measured the key genes involved in de novo lipogenesis (ACC and FASN) and found ACC and FASN were induced in mRNA level in acetate-treated BMDMs (Fig.  B) (Supplemental Fig. 5A). Next, we assessed the free fatty acid level by LC-MS. While LPS treatment significantly increased free fatty acid levels in control macrophages, we observed no changes in free fatty acid levels in acetate pretreated BMDMs compared to LPS group (Supplemental Fig. 5C). Moreover, the knockdown of ACC or FASN (Fig.  C), or the administration of C75, an inhibitor of FASN (Fig.  D), failed to reverse the cytokine suppression induced by acetate. Thus, these data suggested the immuno-regulatory effect of de novo lipogenesis was not found observed in this setting.

 

In the mevalonate pathway of cholesterol synthesis, Hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA synthase 1 (HMGCS1) is the key enzyme. We found fluvastatin, the inhibitor of HMGCS, cannot reverse the suppressive effect of acetate (Fig.  E). Furthermore, while the expression of key enzyme of fatty acid oxidation, carnitine palmitoyl transferase 1 A (CPT1A) was upregulated (Supplemental Fig. 5B), the knockdown of CPT1A failed to reverse the suppressive effect of acetate (Fig.  F). Together, our current data suggest that the lipid metabolism in macrophages stimulated by LPS for 6 h was not changed by acetate, and lipid metabolism might not be involve in the immuno-suppressive effect of acetate.

## Discussion
Knowledge about acetate regulation of immune responses is enlarging. Acetate Ringer’s solution (35mM ∼ 45mM) [ ] has been widely used in clinical practice for decades. Early findings showed that serum levels of lL-6 were lower in patients exposed to acetate-containing hemodialysis fluids compared to acetate-free dialysate. Acetate Ringer’s was found to decrease days with ventilator and lower the incidence of respiratory failure compared to lactate-buffered group [ ]. These researches suggested that, acetate, either intravenous or intraperitoneal, may suppress inflammatory responses. In line with these previous findings, our retrospective analysis of postoperative patients supported that serum IL-6 at 3 h after surgery was significantly decreased in acetate Ringer’s group. Although short time exposure of acetate has been known to ameliorate the inflammatory response, the underlying mechanisms have not been fully understood.
The inflammatory regulatory effects of acetate are influenced by various factors, including the duration and dosage of acetate treatments, as well as metabolic reprogramming in resting and activated immune cells. Memory CD8+ T cells showed opposite effects of glycolysis and immune response to pathogen infection depending on timing of exposure of acetate [ , ]. Whereas the role of acetate in the regulation of the innate immune is less well studied than that of adaptive immune [ , ]. In innate immune research, several evidences pointed acetate as potent treatment to improve host defense. Contrary to our finding, it has been reported that under long-term exposure of gastrointestinal-source acetate (for days), alveolar macrophages showed increased pathogen phagocytosis and IL-1β production through elevating the glycolysis/Hif-1α axis [ ] which was different from our study. It is known that alveolar macrophages have an M2-like phenotype which utilize fatty acid oxidation to support its energy demands. Acetate, especially long-exposure acetate, promotes lipid synthesis [ ]. In our study, short exposure acetate (6 h) seems failed to switch the phenotype of the activated macrophages to M2 and showed little impact on lipid metabolism in BMDMs/PMs stimulated by LPS. BMDMs is considered as naïve macrophages M0 and PMs have an M1-like phenotype.M1 macrophages rely primarily on glycolytic metabolism, mediated by HIF-1α. Macrophage polarization may be used to explain the almost opposing immune response and mechanism of acetate between alveolar macrophages and BMDMs/PMs, which were used in our current study. Therefore, our study added novel information for the short-term exposure of acetate in the modulation of inflammatory responses.
Elevation of intracellular acetyl-CoA usually lead to enhanced acetylation, which is in accordance with the present study showing that short exposure of acetate as early as 15 min elevated the histone acetylation. The enhanced histone acetylation, on the one hand, promotes transcriptional activity and, on the other hand, functions as metabolite reservoirs. In human cells, de-acetylation of ∼ 0.1% of histone lysine residues yield the release of large amounts of acetate at concentrations of up to ∼ 3 µM [ ]. The histone-released acetate that directs to acetyl-modification requires the cooperation of HDACs/SIRTs and HATs [ ]. Previous study reported that under the low concentrations of acetate (0.5mM ∼ 5mM) treatment for 18 h ∼ 72 h, the expression of HADC, SIRT and HATs was not suppressed and the histone acetylation dynamics remained intact [ ]. These lead to the acetate-mediated enhanced inflammatory response of macrophage. However, in the present study, we found that short-term exposure to 10mM exogenous acetate decreased their expression which may inhibit the histone-acetyl recycle. Moreover, the inhibition or knockdown of these enzymes could not abrogate the acetate-mediated inflammatory suppression. Therefore, these results suggest that the histone-originated acetylation is not essential for this process.
It is interesting to note that ACSS2, a crucial enzyme synthesizing acetyl-CoA from acetate, is essential for the anti-inflammatory effects of exogenous acetate [ ], suggesting exogenous acetate-originated acetyl-CoA is essential, which may explain the decreased glycolysis induced by acetate. We also measured the fatty acid metabolism to investigate whether acetate-induced acetyl-CoA altered the lipid metabolism. We noticed little alteration on fatty acid in acetate treatment. And the inhibition or knockdown of key enzymes could not reverse the acetate effects, ruling out the involvement of lipid metabolism in acetate regulation on inflammation.
Last but not least, we demonstrated acetate could promote the hydroxyl-modification of Hif-1α which eventually degrade through proteasome process. Prolyl hydroxylation is a well-studied post-translational modification for the metabolic regulation of Hif-1α. Multiple factors could modulate the Hif-1α through prolyl hydroxylase domain (PHD) proteins, and we report acetate could activate the hydroxylation of Hif-1α and subsequent degradation, which has not been reported. Our finding provided novel knowledge on the metabolic regulation of acetate on inflammation.
In summary, our data demonstrate a distinct effect of exogenous acetate in immunomodulation of macrophage that showed protective effects in murine model of sepsis. Short-term exposure of acetate induces the decreasing of glycolysis and promoted the prolyl hydroxylation and degradation of Hif-1α, which suppressed the inflammatory response of macrophage. The present study highlighted a suppressive role of acetate with underlying mechanism in the modulation of inflammation.
