## Abstract
Alveolar epithelial Type II (ATII) cells are closely associated with early events of Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). Proteostasis dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction are known causes of decreased proliferation of alveolar epithelial cells and the secretion of pro‐fibrotic mediators. Here, a large body of evidence is systematized and a cascade relationship between protein homeostasis, endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and fibrotropic cytokines is proposed, providing a theoretical basis for ATII cells dysfunction as a possible pathophysiological initiating event for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.
Keywords: ATII cell, IPF, Mitochondria, mtDAMP, Proteostasis

By systematizing a large body of evidence and propose a cascade relationship between protein homeostasis, endoplasmic reticulum stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and pro‐fibrotic factor, providing a theoretical basis for ATII cells dysfunction as a possible pathophysiological initiating event for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.

## Introduction
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is usually a progressive disease whose main pathological features are fibrosis of the lung interstitial tissue and destruction of the alveolar structure.[
 , , 
] Alveolar type II (ATII) cells are considered to be the core pathogenesis of IPF.[
 , , , , , 
] In the background of triggers (e.g., genetic, environmental, epigenetic, and gerontology), the presence of conditions such as aging, proteostasis imbalance, endoplasmic reticulum (ER)stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction may be the main reasons why ATII cells release pro‐fibrotic factors and thus drive IPF.
ATII cells are particularly sensitive to proteostasis because they need to produce large amounts of surfactant proteins.[
 , 
] Loss of proteostasis due to pro‐fibrosis triggers (such as aging and the environment, etc.) or genetic variations (such as surfactant mutations) can lead to the accumulation of misfolded or damaged proteins.[
 , , , 
] There are two possible mechanisms to deal with these abnormal proteins in the cell: 1) the unfolded protein response (UPR), which facilitates protein synthesis, protein folding, and protein degradation; 2) Mitochondria help degrade abnormal proteins that accumulate in the cytoplasm through the “mitochondria as guardian in cytosol” (MAGIC) procedure.[
 , 
] The occurrence of these two mechanisms is beneficial in the short term, but persistent UPR and MAGIC may cause damage to mitochondria. Protein kinase R–like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) locates in the ER and is activated when unfolded proteins accumulate in the ER, which is an important pathway for UPR. Proteins downstream of PERK can cause damage to healthy mitochondria, such as activating Transcription Factor 4 (ATF 4).[
 
] And damage to mitochondria is not negligible when PERK is continuously activated. MAGIC is a mechanism by which mitochondria help maintain cytoplasmic proteostasis and has been observed in yeast and human cells.[
 , 
] Moreover, abnormal proteins entering the interior of the mitochondria may disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis, which has been validated in neurodegenerative diseases.[
 
] For ATII cells, the event that triggers fibrotic factors leading to an imbalance in protein homeostasis within ATII may occur much earlier than IPF. Therefore, we believe that the mitochondrial dysfunction of ATII cells may be caused by the above mechanisms. Moreover, during IPF pathogenesis, the autophagy pathway in the epithelium may be dysregulated or impaired which may lead to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria, which may be confirmed by the large amount of mitochondrial damage observed in ATII cells of IPF patients.[
 , , 
] Studies have shown that various factors secreted by ATII cell appear to be important in the progression of fibrosis.[
 , , 
] And mitochondrial dysfunction may be one of the main reasons ATII cells are able to secrete pro‐fibrotic factors, mitochondrial components and metabolites have been shown to play pro‐inflammatory roles as damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMP), but these substances may also play pro‐fibrotic roles, such as mitochondrial dysfunction associated with PINK1 deficiency leading to fibrosis through the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA).[
 
] Thus, other mitochondrial components and metabolites may also cause ATII cells to secrete fibrotic factors by acting on targets in the cytoplasm of ATII cells, or be released into the extracellular space to recruit and activate adaptive immune cells.[
 , , , 
] In conclusion, the intracellular and extracellular action of mitochondria damage‐associated molecular patterns (mtDAMP) causes ATII cells and immune cells to release pro‐inflammatory and pro‐fibrotic factors, and the rapid increase of these factors may disrupt the lung factor balance, making the lungs tend to move in the direction of IPF rather than in the direction of immune pneumonia.
Although many articles have reported in detail the role of proteostasis imbalance, ER stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction in IPF, few review articles have combined these very important concepts. Therefore, after reviewing the extensive literature, we linked these important concepts through integrated stress response (ISR) and MAGIC, which provides a theoretical basis for better understanding why ATII cells are unable to effectively repair damaged epithelium and secrete large amounts of pro‐fibrotic factors (Figure  ). In addition, the various therapeutic approaches related to the content discussed are described in detail in this paper to provide reference for the implementation of treatment in the early stage of IPF development.

## Pathogenic Factors of IPF Lead to Proteostasis Imbalance in ATII Cells
Protein homeostasis is the regulation of protein production, transport, maintenance, and degradation. Abnormalities in any of these can lead to lung disease.[
 
] he cell's own integrated network of pathways prevents aggregation, misfolding, or accumulation of damaged proteins, thereby maintaining the stability and function of the cellular proteome.[
 
] In the lung, as a cell that repairs and secretes proteins, protein misfolding has a significant impact on ATII cell. Aggregation of misfolded proteins has been reported is considered to be a hallmark feature of ATII cells in IPF patients.[
 , , , 
]

The link between protein mutations and lung fibrosis is one of the key findings in the field.[
 
] And, like other professional secretory cells that synthesize large amounts of proteins, ATII cells are estimated to secrete anywhere from several hundred to tens of thousands of proteins per second.[
 , 
] The existence of these situations can give us an important insight: For ATII cells that need to secrete large amounts of protein, even in the absence of mutations, Pathogenic factors of IPF (e.g., environmental, physiological, and pathological stresses) can cause ATII cell proteins to become unbalanced.
2.1.

## Senecence
Studies have shown that senescence of ATII cell leads to spontaneous pulmonary fibrosis.[
 
] In patients with IPF, the development of fibrosis appears to be dependent on three factors: excessive epithelial damage due to prolonged exposure to inhalation injury factors, aging, and genetic susceptibility.[
 
] Stem cell senescence is driven by micro‐ and macro‐environmental stressors.[
 
] The combined effect of these events leads to premature senescence of alveolar epithelial stem cells.[
 
] In fact, almost all markers of cellular and molecular aging are exaggerated or appear prematurely in IPF. Senescence is accompanied by a decline in protein homeostasis, which is the basis of many age‐related protein misfolding diseases, characterized by the accumulation of unfolded or damaged proteins.[
 , 
] Relatively few studies have been conducted on protein homeostasis during aging in the lungs compared to other tissue types. ATII cell has specific protein requirements. It is worth exploring whether loss of protein homeostasis positively affects the development of IPF. IPF induced by mutations in the surfactant protein C gene (SFTPC) may provide insights into the importance of proteostasis during aging. Pulmonary fibrosis is a disease that usually emerges in the sixth or seventh decade, and these mutations indicate that although the culprit gene is transcribed early, clinical symptoms do not manifest over an extended period of time. This suggests that the consequences of mutations that cause aggregation tendencies or misfolding proteins require age‐related breakdown of proteostasis to drive disease. And for ATII cells that need to secrete proteins, the gradual loss of this age‐related protein homeostasis network may accumulate misfolded proteins within the cell in non‐mutational conditions, such as ribosomal stalling. Although under normal conditions ≈30% of newly synthesized proteins do not fold correctly.[
 
] But in the case of ATII cell, which is prematurely aged and has a specific requirement for proteins, intracellular ribosome stalling may be the main reason for the presence of a large number of misfolded proteins. In nematodes and yeasts, ribosomal stalling increases with cellular aging, leading to proteostasis disturbance and nascent polypeptide aggregation.[
 , 
] Decreased protein degradation capacity in senescent cells may also be the key to unfolded protein aggregation and misfolded protein folded protein formation. The cell itself has the ability to clean up misfolded proteins, and the ubiquitin‐proteasome system and the autophagosome‐lysosome pathway are the two main protein hydrolysis pathways inside the cell.[
 , 
] The activity of these two major protein hydrolysis systems declines with age.[
 
] In summary, with reference to these observations in cryptic nematodes, yeast and neurological disorders, and in conjunction with the strength of ATII cell secreted proteins, it can be surmised that when ATII cell senesces, the suspension of its internal ribosomes is more likely to result in proteostasis imbalance and the accumulation of misfolded proteins.

2.2.

## Toxic Environment and Mutations
In the development of the IPF, the lung is continuously faced with a wide range of biochemical disturbances. For ATII cell, which has a high requirement for protein synthesis, the toxic environment is likely to result in the accumulation of deleterious translation, protein misfolding, and protein‐protein interaction disruptions, resulting in proteotoxic stress. Studies have shown that stress reactions such as heat stress, oxidative stress, or exposure to toxic substances (e.g., cadmium) cause some of the proteins to unfolding, thereby increasing the risk of their accumulation. Factors such as smoking, viral infections (major herpesvirus), and occupation are known to increase the risk of developing IPF.[
 , , , , , , 
] This may be related to ERs, smoke, occupation and viruses are important factors in ERs, which has been identified as an upstream mediator of fibrosis in many disease states such as IPF.[
 , 
] ER stress refers to proteostasis imbalance that lead to an accumulation of unfolded proteins, moreover the most studied proteostatic changes in IPF are triggered by ER stress. Therefore, the toxic environment may affect the protein homeostasis of ATII cells in many ways.
For those with inherited respiratory diseases such as IPF, there is an important link between the presence of misfolded proteins and mutations in underlying genes.[
 
] Surfactant protein C gene (SFTPC) telomerase reverse transcriptase mutations track disease progression in multiple members of the same family. This mutation results in surfactant protein C (SPC) misfolding, which reduces protein export to the secretory pathway and leads to accumulation of misfolded protein in ATII cell.[
 
]

2.3.

## Decreased Chaperone Protein Expression
Chaperone proteins play important roles in maintaining protein homeostasis, including folding newly synthesized proteins, transporting polypeptides to organelles, breaking down protein complexes, and regulating protein activity. A proteomic analysis of ATII cells isolated from young and old mice revealed a maladaptive breakdown of the protein homeostatic network with age, and an important role for the co‐chaperone adaptive response (CARE) network in the processing of chronically misfolded proteins in aging lung.[
 
] Heat shock protein 70 (HSP70) is the chaperone protein of highest interest that prevents aggregation, promotes refolding of misfolded denatured proteins, solubilizes aggregated proteins, and cooperates with cellular degradation mechanisms to remove aberrant proteins and protein aggregates.[
 
] HSP70 levels were shown to be significantly reduced in the lung of IPF patients, which may be related to common pathogenic factors in IPF, such as reduced HSP70 due to telomere dysfunction.[
 , 
] Human telomere syndrome is the most common clinical manifestation of IPF.[
 
] Mutations in genes involved in telomere length maintenance (telomerase reverse transcriptase, telomerase RNA component, Poly (A)‐specific ribonuclease, and regulator of telomere elongation helicase 1) increase the risk of developing IPF.
In patients with IPF, alveolar epithelial cells not only have shorter telomeres than their littermates, but also ATII cell have the shortest telomere lengths compared to club cells and myofibroblasts from the same individuals.[
 , 
] In addition to dealing directly with aggregated unfolded or misfolded proteins, in both of these protein hydrolysis systems, molecular chaperones work together to recognize misfolded protein species and help maintain them in a degradable state.[
 , , 
]

Interestingly, Heat shock cognate 71 kDa protein (HSC70) (a member of the HSP70 family) can also serve as a key protein in exploring ATII cell protein homeostasis. Its role in maintaining protein homeostasis is more in the degradation of proteins by autophagy. HSC70 is able to reduce intracellular unfolded or misfolded proteins.[
 
] HSC70 and its co‐chaperone Bcl2‐associated athanogene 3 (BAG3) Activating transcription factor  participate in the degradation of aggregates in a process known as chaperone‐assisted selective autophagy.[
 , 
] HSC70 is also involved in the degradation of soluble cytoplasmic proteins containing KFERQ sequence through chaperone‐mediated autophagy in lysosomes and endosomal microautophagy in late endosomes and multivesicular bodies.[
 
] Currently, there are fewer reports on HSC70 in IPF, and further studies are needed to determine whether HSC70 is reduced in the same way as HSP70.

## Mechanism of Mitochondrial Damage Caused by Proteostasis Imbalance
Abnormally enlarged and swollen mitochondria have been observed in ATII cells from IPF patients. These abnormal mitochondria may have been present before IPF occurred, and this may be related to the excess misfolded or unfolded proteins produced after protein imbalance. Here are two ways that misfolded or unfolded proteins can cause damage to mitochondria.
3.1.

## Activated ISR Causes Damage to Mitochondria
Regarding the relationship between ISR and mitochondria, most studies seem to be more concerned with the role of ISR in the positive regulation of mitochondria via mitochondrial unfolded protein response (mtUPR), whereby damaged mitochondria repair themselves by activating ISR.[
 , , , 
] In contrast, this paper focuses on the fact that prior to the onset of IPF, without involving mitochondrial stress (mitochondria are not damaged), other cellular stresses are also capable of activating ISR, and that ISR activated under these circumstances (mainly proteostasis imbalance) cause damage to mitochondria via mtUPR or other means.
3.1.1.

## Activation of the ISR
The ISR is regulated by four different kinases, protein kinase R–like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), general control nonderepessible 2 (GCN2), protein kinase R (PKR), and heme‐regulated inhibitor (HRI), which are activated by different stressors and focus on phosphorylation of a single serine residue in the α‐subunit of the eukaryotic initiation factor 2 (eIF2) complex. Transcripts selectively translated during ISR typically encode proteins involved in stress response pathways, such as ATF4, activating transcription factor 5 (ATF5), and C/EBP Homologous Protein (CHOP), which are upregulated in the ATII cell of IPF.[
 , , , , 
]

When there is proteostasis imbalance in intracellular, PERK may be the most important stressor for the intracellular occurrence of ISR. PERK is an ER‐localized type I protein that is activated when unfolded or misfolded proteins are present in the ER.[
 , 
] As mentioned above, various factors present before the onset of IPF can lead to proteostasis imbalance in cellular ATII cells and the production of large amounts of unfolded or misfolded proteins. This suggests that the PERK protein may be activated in ATII cell and trigger the ISR.
In addition, given the requirement for ATII cell protein synthesis and the large number of mitochondria, speculate that the number of ribosomes within ATII cell may also be high. Although internal ribosome stalling has not been reported in ATII cells in IPF, ribosome stalling appears to be positively correlated with senescent cells. Ribosome stalling activates GCN2, and this activation results from the interaction between GCN2 and ribosomal P strands, a complex that promotes translational elongation.[
 
] In a mouse model of ribosome stalling‐induced neurodegeneration, GCN2 was found to phosphorylate eIF2α.[
 
]

For PKR and HRI. The environment may play an important role in the activation of the ISR as a key causative factor in IPF. It has been shown that during a viral infection, when the viral double‐stranded RNA accumulates in the cells, it activates the PKR kinase and triggers the ISR.[
 
] HRI can also be activated by protein misfolding in the cytoplasm.[
 
] In summary, ATII cell may have the conditions to activate the ISR during the non‐IPF formation phase.

3.1.2.

## Mitochondria Damaged by ISR
The role of proteins such as ATF4 downstream of the ISR on mitochondria may be twofold, and this view may be supported by studies that have found that ATF5 acts upstream of ATF4 during mitochondrial stress, in contrast to what is observed during ERs,[
 , 
] suggesting that the sequence of the protein's action is critical for a better understanding of transcriptional responses to mitochondrial dysfunction. In short, on the one hand, damaged mitochondria act as upstream mediators to activate the ISR and achieve repair of mitochondria by preferentially translating specific transcription factors to activate mtUPR.[
 
] On the other hand, other intracellular factors (e.g., imbalanced proteostasis, causative factors of IPF) activate the ISR prior to the formation of IPF, and the activated ISR acts as an upstream mediator to damage healthy mitochondria. This is the main point of this paper, that some aspects of the ISR may be detrimental to cellular mitochondria (Figure  ).
 A large body of evidence from mammalian systems highlights ISR as a core element of the mtUPR.[
 , 
] When imbalanced proteostasis, ribosomal stalling, and toxic environments activate the ISR by acting on different kinases, mtUPR is also activated in the absence of damage to mitochondria. In certain pathophysiological situations, transient mitochondrial stress and induction of mtUPR appears to be beneficial as it increases the expression of mitochondrial chaperones and proteases, thus promoting mitochondrial homeostasis.[
 , , 
] However, persistent mtUPR inevitably leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, including its morphology and mitochondrial autophagy, and is the cause of many diseases, such as mtUPR signaling driving the propagation of mutant mitochondrial genomes and ultimately inborn metabolic disorders.[
 , , , , , , 
]

For ATII cells with dysregulated protein homeostasis, PERK kinase activated by unfolded or misfolded proteins may be the main cause of mitochondrial damage. The following are the main arguments in favor of this view: First, there are various challenges affecting protein homeostasis during the developmental stage of IPF, second, Proteostasis imbalance have the ability to act as upstream mediators directly on PERK kinase, and third, Several substances downstream of PERK kinase B‐cell lymphoma‐2(e.g., CHOP, ATF4, and ATF5) cause damage to healthy mitochondria, and these factors are detected as elevated in IPF lung, this is the main part of the description.
In addition to upregulating proteins capable of affecting mitochondria through mtUPR, these factors also cause damage to mitochondria in other ways. CHOP, which is positively regulated by PERK‐ATF4, can induce apoptosis through ERs. Mechanism of CHOP‐induced apoptosis is related to B‐cell lymphoma‐2 (BCL‐2), which possesses the ability to regulate the outer mitochondrial membrane permeability.[
 
] Its effect on the permeability of the outer membrane a noteworthy function, which determines whether or not internal mitochondrial material can be released into the cytoplasm. ATF4, an upstream mediator of CHOP and ATF5 expression, also causes damage to mitochondria. It has been shown that the ER unfolded protein response in alveolar epithelial cells leads to mitochondrial dysfunction in an ATF4‐dependent manner, and the mitochondrial hyperfusion associated with PERK may be determined by ATF4.[
 , 
] A large number of abnormally enlarged and swollen mitochondria can be observed for a long period of time in ATII cell of IPF, which may be related to excessive mitochondrial fusion and altered membrane permeability.[
 , 
]

Mammalian ATF5 is a functional ortholog of activating transcription factor associated with stress–1 (ATFS‐1), and there is evidence that ATF5 is able to rescue mtUPR in nematodes when ATFS‐1 is knocked out.[
 
] In nematodes, ATFS‐1 directly coordinates the input capacity of the mitochondrial network by inducing a mitochondrial quality control programme.[
 
] The possibility that ATF5 causes damage to mitochondria is explored with reference to the current status of ATFS‐1. In nematodes, ATFS‐1 is transported to mitochondria and degraded by lon peptidase 1 (LONP‐1) under physiological conditions. During mitochondrial stress, the mitochondrial input of ATFS‐1 is impaired, resulting in the translocation of ATFS‐1 to the nucleus.[
 
] Nuclear accumulation of ATFS‐1 results in intracellular production of mitochondrial precursor proteins for the restoration of oxidative phosphorylation.[
 
] In response to this situation, we can ask the question: for ATII cell with dysregulated protein homeostasis, PERK may lead to a large increase in intracellular ATF5. Will the excess ATF5 accumulate in the nucleus, producing excess mitochondrial precursor proteins and causing mitochondrial damage? (Figure  ) Supported by the above evidence, it is worthwhile to further validate that unfolded or misfolded proteins cause damage to mitochondria via PERK kinase.

3.2.

## Mitochondria Damaged by Misfolded Proteins Entering the Mitochondria
Senescent ATII cells are exposed to a variety of external and endogenous stresses that lead to a decrease in the capacity of the protein homeostatic network and proteome integrity, resulting in the accumulation of misfolded and unfolded proteins.
In yeast cells, when intracellularly Unfolded or misfolded proteins accumulate to a certain extent, mitochondria play a “part‐time role” in cellular protein homeostasis by MAGIC.[
 
] Does this mechanism exist in mammalian cells? Several scholars have studied human cells and demonstrated that in these cells, FUN14 Domain Containing 1 (FUNDC1)/HSC70 interactions promote the translocation of unfolded or misfolded cytoplasmic proteins from cytoplasm of the cell to mitochondria and their degradation by mitochondrial proteases, thereby protecting protein homeostasis(Figure  ).[
 
]

 Although this translocation is dependent on the FUNDC1/HSC70 or translocator of the outer mitochondrial membrane (TOM)/translocase of inner mitochondrial membrane (TIM) complex, and knockdown of their subunit will reduce the translocation of proteins into mitochondria.[
 , , 
] the ability of aggregates formed by unfolded or misfolded proteins to enter the mitochondria depends primarily on adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and chaperone proteins. It has been shown that the MAGIC pathway of mitochondrial homeostasis It has been shown that the MAGIC pathway of mitochondrial homeostasis appears to play a “part‐time” role only when glucose levels are high and the cell is dependent on glycolysis for ATP production.[
 
] In IPF, in correlation with the above findings, there is a general upregulation of aerobic glycolysis in lung tissue.[
 , 
] In addition, ATFS‐1, found in nematodes, upregulates the expression of glycolytic pathways and innate immunity genes.[
 , 
] The mammalian transcription factor ATF5, which is regulated similarly to ATFS‐1, thus ATF5 may also contribute to the glycolytic pathway. In terms of molecular chaperones, it has been found in yeast cells that HSP104 is involved in the import of aggregated proteins into them, possibly by dissociating proteins from aggregates to allow them to enter the import channel, and that defects in cytosolic HSP70 can also result in the entry of misfolded proteins into the mitochondrion and an elevation of mitochondrial stress.[
 
] In IPF, telomere dysfunction in ATII cell reduces HSP70. This evidence suggests that unfolded or misfolded proteins in ATII cell have the potential to enter mitochondria.
Assuming that there is a mechanism by which misfolded or unfolded proteins (e.g., FUNDC1/HSC70) enter mitochondria in ATII cell, it is possible to speculate on how aberrant mitochondria arise in ATII cell with reference to damage to mitochondria caused by misfolded proteins in nematode worms, yeast cells, and neurodegenerative diseases. In yeast cells, it has been found that an excessive burden of misfolded proteins in mitochondria disrupts mitochondrial biosynthetic activity or disrupts the homeostasis of mitochondrial proteins, leading to a sustained decline in function, which in turn leads to mitochondrial dysfunction(Figure  ).[
 
] Misfolded proteins can also enter the mitochondria of nematodes and cause damage. The nematode protease LONP‐1 normally degrades ATFS‐1 in mitochondria, but when there are too many misfolded proteins in the mitochondria, it preferentially degrades the misfolded proteins, which leads to the accumulation of ATFS‐1 throughout the mitochondrial network, triggering the accumulation of harmful mtDNA in the mitochondria and damaging the mitochondria.[
 
] Moreover, inhibition of LONP1 may further lead to the aggregation of a subpopulation of mitochondrial proteins, similar to the aggregation induced by the knockdown of DNAJA3, the co‐chaperone of mtHSP70.[
 
]

A recent study reported that many aggregation‐prone proteins associated with neurodegenerative diseases (ND) accumulate in mitochondria, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction.[
 , 
] For mature mitochondria, the increased mitochondrial protein homeostatic burden, i.e., the increased levels of unfolded proteins in mitochondria, may promote the aggregation of proteins in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle enzymes, thereby accelerating metabolic dysfunction (Figure  ). Defects in mitochondrial TCA cycle caused by misfolded proteins have been observed in a variety of neurodegenerative diseases.[
 , 
] The aggregation of enzymes in the TCA cycle enzymes not only disrupts the structure of the mitochondrial respiratory chain but also produces toxic metabolites (Figure  ). For example, in nematode studies,D‐2‐hydroxyglutarate dehydrogenase was found to be a TCA enzyme, which is involved in the clearance of the metabolite D‐2‐hydroxyglutarate (D‐2HG), avoiding the inhibition of the respiratory chain and the reduction of the mitochondrial ATP synthase activity when D‐2HG accumulates, which leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and energy damage.[
 
] And if misfolded proteins result in the aggregation of D‐2‐hydroxyglutarate dehydrogenase, this may lead to the accumulation of 3‐hydroxypropionate (3‐HP).[
 
] 3‐HP caused mitochondrial dysfunction and ultrastructure defects, presumably by inhibiting the membrane‐shaping activity of the MICOS subunit IMMT‐1. Inhibition of IMMT‐1 due to 3‐HP will most likely lead to loss of the respiratory chain cristae structure, resulting in a swollen mitochondria.[
 
] Notably, electron microscopic analysis of muscle biopsy samples performed in propionic aciduria showed extensive mitochondrial swelling and cristae interruption.[
 
] This is the same morphology as observed in IPF for mitochondria.

## Damaged Mitochondria in ATII Cell Promote IPF Formation
Cytokines secreted by ATII cells and lung immune cells are strongly associated with the development of IPF.[
 , , 
] Imbalances in cytokine secretion may be a key cause of abnormal cellular function and immune disorders in the lung. In the immune response, cytokines are classified as type 1 (Th1) and type 2 (Th2), and an imbalance between them promotes pulmonary fibrosis.[
 , 
] Evidence that ATII cell undergoes transformation and secretes cytokines during IPF formation suggests that the mediator mixture driving the fibrosis response is produced by the epithelium.[
 , , , 
]

The cytokines secreted by immune cells appear to be influenced by ATII cells. It has been shown that damaged ATII cell can act as an upstream mediator to recruit and activate immune cells, which may be related to the substances secreted by ATII cell.[
 , , 
] For the large number of senescent or apoptotic ATII cells that are always present before and after the onset of IPF, the damaged mitochondria within them release a large number of substances that may have an effect earlier or greater than that of the immune cells. Intracellularly, substances (e.g., mtDNA) released by damaged mitochondria contribute to the secretion of pro‐fibrotic factors by ATII cells by acting on a number of mechanisms, such as cyclic GMP‐AMP synthase (cGAS)‐stimulator of interferon response cGAMP interactor 1 (STING1) channel.[
 
] Extracellularly, many mitochondrial components can act as DAMPs, causing immune cells to secrete large quantities of pro‐fibrosis and pro‐inflammatory factors by activating pattern‐recognition receptors (PRRs). Moreover, these factors released into the mesenchymal may promote the transdifferentiation of ATII cells into abnormal epithelial cells, such as Keratin5+  cells.[
 
]

## Alterations in Mitochondrial Membrane Permeability
Mitochondria are double‐membrane structures with an inner and outer mitochondrial membrane (OMM).[
 
] The mitochondrial membrane separates the mitochondrial contents from their cognate PRR.[
 
] Mitochondrial membrane permeability determines whether mitochondrial components can be released into the cytoplasm or extracellular space, and is a key factor that explore how mitochondrial components can promote IPF by influencing ATII cell and immune secretory cytokines.
Permeability of the mitochondrial inner membrane may be achieved by opening the hitherto undefined permeability transition pore complex (PTPC).[
 
] Mitochondrial inner membrane‐ PTPC is positively regulated by peptidylprolyl isomerase F(PPIF). In contrast to the mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP)‐driven apoptosis mechanism, inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) permeability is determined by rapid ATP depletion, calcium overload and oxidative stress. ISR and ERs triggered by dysregulation of protein homeostasis can lead to excessive mitochondrial production of ROS and calcium overload, providing conditions for altered permeability of the inner mitochondrial membrane.
Outer mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), determined by the BCL‐2 protein family.[
 , 
] Under physiological conditions, MOMP is actively blocked by the anti‐apoptotic members of the BCL‐2 family of proteins, whereas in the presence of apoptotic stimuli (e.g., endoplasmic reticulum stress), pro‐apoptotic members of the BCL‐2 family are activated, and they will oligomerize to the outer mitochondrial membrane to precipitate MOMP. PERK, which is most affected by proteostasis, may be the main reason for the changes in mitochondrial outer membrane permeability in ATII cell. PERK‐ATF4 positively regulated CHOP is able to alter the permeability of the outer mitochondrial membrane by inhibiting the anti‐apoptotic protein BCL‐2. Outer mitochondrial membrane permeability by inhibiting the anti‐apoptotic protein BCL‐2.[

4.2.

## Decreased Autophagy Leads to Accumulation of Damaged Mitochondria
In IPF, a large number of aberrant mitochondria were present in ATII cell, which may be related to the decrease in autophagy capacity. Autophagy is an important means of maintaining cellular homeostasis, and a decrease in autophagy results in the accumulation of damaged macromolecules and organelles in the cell. transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β), NAD+‐dependent deacetylases (sirtuins), and PTEN‐induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1) are the main reasons for the lack of autophagy in ATII cell.
In IPF, TGF‐β is one of the most abundant factors. Some scholars have demonstrated that TGF‐β can elevate mitochondrial membrane potential by inhibiting the activity of mitochondrial complex V.[
 
] This phenomenon may be associated with the fact that complex V serves as the sole proton diffusion channel. The mitochondrial membrane potential is closely associated with mitochondrial autophagy, where an increase in membrane potential can result in a reduction of mitochondrial autophagy.[
 
] TGF‐β inhibits autophagy by activating the autophagy inhibitor mTORC1.[
 
] Persistent activation of mTORC1 in senescent and IPF cells leads to reduced autophagy.[
 
] Sirtuins are able to promote autophagy, but low levels of SIRT1 were observed in lung biopsies of IPF.[
 
] Moreover, telomere dysfunction, a common cause of IPF, can inhibit SIRT1 by inducing p53.[
 
] PINK1 is commonly used as a factor to sense mitochondrial protein inputs, and when mitochondrial protein inputs are impaired, PINK1 complexes with TOM and initiates a mitochondrial autophagy response to remove the damaged mitochondria.[
 , 
] Evidence that damage to the PINK1/Parkin pathway plays a key role in the pathogenesis of age‐related pulmonary fibrosis.[
 , 
] Moreover, it has been shown that ERs in ATII cells impairs mitochondrial autophagy by activating ATF3 and repressing transcription of the PINK1 gene.[
 , 
]

4.3.

## Intracellular and Extracellular Roles of Mitochondrial Substances
Damaged mitochondria in ATII cell release mtDNA, mtROS, ATP, second mitochondria‐derived activator of caspases (SMAC) and cardiolipin. These substances react intracellularly to enable ATII cell to secrete cytokines and recruit immune cells, and extracellularly to activate immune cells, thereby further increasing pro‐fibrotic factor levels in the lung environment. These events are likely to disrupt the balance of cytokines in the lungs, leading to the development of IPF.
The main cytokines released by ATII cells are tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), Interferon (INF), Interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), Interleukin‐18 (IL‐18), and Interleukin—1β (IL‐1β).[
 , 
] And the appearance of these cytokines may be determined by the release of mtDNA, mtROS, ATP, SMAC, and cardiolipin from damaged mitochondria in association with intracellular proinflammatory mechanisms. These cytokines secreted by ATII cell can also be referred to as senescence‐associated secretory phenotype (SASP), and although SASP is secreted in senescent cells, non‐senescent ATII cell containing damaged mitochondria are also may by capable of secrete the above cytokines.
4.3.1.

## Transcruption Factor A Mitochodrai
Transcription factor A mitochondria (TFAM) is a nuclear coding protein that has an important role in mtDNA metabolism. TFAM promotes TNF‐α release from reactive dendritic cells via toll‐like Receptor (TLR).[

4.3.2.

## Mitochondrial DNA, ROS, and Cardiolipin
Within the cytoplasm, mtDNA promotes cytokine secretion by ATII cell in two ways. mtDNA in the cytoplasm is sensed by the cGAS‐STING signaling pathway, which induces TBK1‐IRF3‐IFN signaling and promotes the expression of inflammatory factors.[
 , , 
] In addition, TBK1 activates the nuclear factor kappa‐B (NF‐κB) signaling pathway through phosphorylation, thereby increasing the expression of interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and TNF‐α.[
 
] IL‐6 may act on ATII cell and exert an antifibrosis effect at an early stage, but acts on fibroblasts and macrophages and exerts a pro‐fibrosis effect at a later stage.[
 
] For fibroblasts, IL‐6 promotes IPF by activating signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling and regulating fibroblast proliferation and apoptosis.[
 
] In addition, macrophage‐induced IL‐6/soluble form of the IL‐6R (sIL‐6Ra) complexes promote extracellular matrix (ECM) production and cell proliferation by stimulating lung fibroblasts.[
 
] TNF‐α can cause pro‐fibrosis manifestations in fibroblasts.[
 
] In addition, TNF‐α induces the expression and activation of adhesion molecules, which act as the easy adhesion of leukocytes and platelets to vascular surfaces.[
 
] And in the BLM‐PF model, TNF‐α was observed to induce angiotensin II‐dependent ATII cell apoptosis.[
 
]

In addition to this, mtDNA activation of intracytoplasmic NLPR3 causes ATII cell to release pro‐fibrosis factors. NLPR3 inflammasome are essential for ATII cell cytokine secretion.[
 
] NOD‐, LRR‐ and pyrin domain‐containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome sense dysfunctional mitochondria, suggesting a link between mitochondrial damage and inflammatory diseases.[
 
] Exogenous substances such as bleomycin (BLM) mediate lung fibrosis injury associated with inflammasome activation.[
 
] This is supported by cytokines secreted by epithelial cells, such as IL‐1β and IL18, which are key cytokines whose secretion is dependent on the activation of inflammasome.[
 
]

mtDNA cause cells to release IL‐1β and IL‐18 through activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, resulting in localized inflammatory lesions.[
 
] IL‐1β may be a major driver of IPF development, which is related to the broader pro‐fibrosis expression of IL‐1β. In addition to its ability to recruit immune cells, IL‐1β induces epithelial wound repair, which may be related to IL‐1β’s ability to promote the differentiation of ATII cell into epithelial type I cells (ATIs).[
 
] However, the IL‐1β signaling pathway, which is consistently upregulated in IPF patients, inhibits this pathway, suggesting that disruption of IL‐1β homeostasis hinders lung epithelial repair.[
 
] Moreover, IL‐1β and IL‐6 induced pulmonary epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) to promote fibrosis through TGF‐β signaling and STAT3 signaling, respectively, with IL‐1β also promoting IL‐6 release through enhanced TGF‐β signaling.[
 , 
] IL‐1β also stimulates fibroblasts to synthesize collagen and produce fibulin.[
 
] In a BLM model, the pro‐inflammatory cytokine IL‐18 induces senescence in lung fibroblasts by down‐regulating anti‐aging proteins.[
 
] In addition, IL‐18 is involved in the development of pulmonary fibrosis by inducing EMT through up‐regulation of alpha Smooth Muscle Actin (α‐SMA), the transcription factor Snail‐1 and down‐regulation of E‐cadherin.[
 
] Moreover, in vitro evidence that IL‐18 promotes fibroblast senescence also suggests that IL‐18 induces EMT.[
 
] Similar to mtDNA, intracellular mtROS and cardiolipin also cause cells to release IL‐1β and IL‐18 through activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, resulting in localized inflammatory lesions.[
 , 
]

In addition to intracellular responses, mtDNA also exerts pro‐fibrosis effects extracellularly. mtDNA molecules are potent activators of toll‐like Receptor 9 (TLR9).[
 
] TLR9 is found in immune cells such as macrophages, B cells, neutrophils, and plasma cells. It has been shown that injection of mtDNA into mice leads to a severe inflammatory response in the lung.[
 
] This may be related to the entry of mtDNA into immune cells. The mtDNA released outside the cytoplasm may be phagocytosed by the immune cells mentioned above. After entering the immune cells, the mtDNA will be recognized by TLR9, which triggers a downstream signalling cascade via myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (MyD88), activating Mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) and the nuclear transcription factor NF‐κB to promote the release of IL‐6 and TNF‐α.[
 
]

4.3.3.

## ATP
ATP can have two distinct roles, intracellular and extracellular, and plays a key role in regulating inflammatory responses. In the cytoplasm, ATP is capable of causing cells to release IL‐18 and IL‐1β by activating NLRP3. When ATP is released into the extracellular space, ATP constitutes an important signaling molecule involved in the development of many inflammatory diseases by binding to purinergic purinergic 2 (P2) receptors.[
 , 
] Injured or dying cells are known to release ATP. Purinergic 2 × 7 receptor (P2×7R) is an ionotropic receptor located primarily on macrophages and T cells.[
 , 
] ATP induces NLRP3 inflammasome activation via the P2×7 receptor, leading to the release of IL‐18 and IL‐1β from immune cells.[
 
] It was also shown that P2×7R agonists stimulated morphological maturation of dendritic cells and promoted NF‐κB expression, and co‐immunoprecipitation experiments verified the interaction between P2×7R and NF‐κB.[
 
] And ATP‐mediated P2×7 signaling resulted in increased T cell initiation and CD4+ T cell numbers.[
 
] Moreover, a portion of the CD4+ cell population in IPF expresses relatively high levels of TGF‐β and IL‐17A and may promote fibrosis through signal transducers.[
 
]

4.3.4.

## N‐formyl Peptides
N‐formyl peptides are released extracellularly after cell rupture. N‐formyl peptides are able to chemotaxis immune cells and act on platelets.[
 , 
] Extracellular N‐formyl peptides act as potent neutrophil activators after binding to formyl peptide receptor 1 or indirectly chemotaxis neutrophils by inducing IL‐8 secretion.[
 , 
] Neutrophils have rarely been observed in studies of IPF lung tissue, but there is some evidence that neutrophils contribute to IPF by releasing cytokines and participating in the remodeling of the ECM.[
 , 
]

4.3.5.

## SMAC
Intracellularly, SMAC‐driven fibrosis appears to primarily involve altered NF‐κB signaling and excessive IL‐8 secretion by cells through this channel.[
 , 
] Serum IL‐8 levels were negatively correlated with lung function were negatively correlated.[
 
] And high and low plasma levels of IL‐8 were associated with a median survival of 1.9 and 5.1 years, respectively.[
 
]

Extracellular, SMAC may have a greater effect on T cells. It has been shown that SMAC mimetics can exert direct immunomodulatory effects on T cells, e.g. by reprogramming the differentiation of CD4+ T cells from a T helper 17 (Th17)‐type phenotype to a Th2‐type phenotype.[
 
] The majority of T cells found in IPF lungs tend to be Th2 type,[
 
] but no evidence has been found to demonstrate whether SMAC drives IPF by acting on T cells. IL‐13 secreted by Th2 phenotype T cells is important for fibrosis. Interleukin‐13 (IL‐13) and its induced proteins may accelerate the lung fibrosis process by inhibiting the epithelial wound healing to accelerate the process of pulmonary fibrosis.[
 
] In addition, IL‐13 exacerbates lung fibrosis by activating a pro‐fibrosis M2 macrophage phenotype and inducing fibroblasts to differentiate into myofibroblasts through multiple mechanisms.[
 , , 
]

## Anti‐Fibrotic Drugs in Relation to the Content of this Article
Numerous small molecules or compounds are currently in clinical trials for fibrosis. Published data on these compounds were listed in Table  .
 5.1.

## Cre Recombinase mRNA
Starting from the telomere side, the current target therapeutic directions are to counteract telomere shortening or to counteract DNA‐damage response (DDR) activation. Tert transgene expression has been induced by microinjection of cre recombinase mRNA (Cre mRNA) into single‐cell‐stage embryos, and animal experiments have shown that the expression of tissue‐specific telomerase in the intestine prevents telomere shortening. Telomerase induction rescues intestinal senescence and low cell proliferation while restoring tissue integrity, inflammation, and age‐dependent microbiota dysbiosis.[
 
]

5.2.

## Danazol
Patients with telomere disease show significant telomere lengthening after taking the synthetic sex hormone danazol. After 2 years of treatment with danazol, the telomere attrition rate was greatly improved. Danazol treatment not only prevented telomere loss, but at the end of the study, telomeres increased by an average of 386 bp.[
 
]

5.3.

## Adeno‐Associated Virus
Telomere dysfunction, especially induced by loss of telomere protein TRF1 or TRF2 in II type alveolar epithelial cells, leads to impaired regeneration, inflammation, and fibrosis.[
 , 
] In order to counteract telomere shortening, telomerase activity can be induced by stimulating the expression of endogenous TERT or exogenous delivery of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT). Consistently, the therapeutic benefits of adeno‐associated virus (AAV)‐mediated TERT delivery have been observed in animal models of aplastic anemia, IPF, and Alzheimer's disease. Expression of telomerase in II‐type alveolar epithelial cells using AAV results in reduced TAF, reduced inflammation, and improved lung function in telomerase mutants and wild‐type aged mice.[
 , 
]

5.4.

## Antisense Oligonucleotide
Antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) are a new class of drugs targeting RNA. Telomere ASOs (tASOs) can specifically reduce the activation of DDR at telomere in mice, and improve the health span and lifespan of premature senility animal model, which provides a preliminary but promising basis for its clinical application.[
 
] Selective inhibition of telomere DDR with tASOs targeted tncRNA, as demonstrated in cultured cells and mice, also reduced the markers of cell senescence and apoptosis, reduced the expression of SASP cytokines, and improved histopathology, resulting in prolonged lifespan.[
 
]

5.5.

## Telomerase Activator 65
Telomere length defect of fibroblasts in IPF.[
 
] Telomerase Activator 65 (TA‐65) stimulates telomerase activity, prolongs telomerase dependent short telomeres and rescues DNA damage TA‐65 and related molecules in MEF haploids with insufficient telomerase RNA components. TA‐65 and related molecules have been identified as effective telomerase activators in human immune cells, neonatal keratinocytes and fibroblasts.[
 
]

5.6.

## 4‐phenylbutyric Acid
Many chemical chaperones, including 4‐phenylbutyric acid (4‐PBA) and its derivatives, have been shown to have anti‐amyloid activity and prevent misfolded protein aggregation. By adding partially folded proteins and stabilizing their more compact natural structures, 4‐PBA can reduce the formation of unfolded aggregates and increase the pool of folding intermediates. It can also change the structure of heat shock proteins (HSPs) to increase the exposure of hydrophobic surface and face the stress caused by misfolding.[
 
]

5.7.

## Inflammatory Factor Inhibitor
Inflammation plays a role in the pathophysiology of protein‐misfolding diseases. The research on misfolding in AD is more mature. In neurological diseases, inflammation is regulated by microglia, astrocytes, invasive leukocytes, and innate immune cells in the cerebral cortex. At present, safe and effective inflammatory factor inhibitors, such as IL‐6 and TNF, are used to treat protein misfolding disease.[
 
]

5.8.

## Transfer Mitochondria
It is believed that mitochondrial transfer as a new mode of intercellular communication can effectively replace defective mitochondria. There are many different ways to transfer mitochondria, including microinjection, incubation with intact purified mitochondria, cell attachment mediated by gap junctional channels, and direct transfer from donor cells such as MSC.[
 
] MSC transfers mitochondria directly to recipient cells through spontaneously generated cytoplasmic bridges called tunnel nanotubes (TNT).[
 
] It is more effective to induce MSC (iPS‐MSC) derived from pluripotent cells and transfer mitochondria to co‐cultured bronchial epithelial cells. As mitochondrial transfer is a relatively new research direction, there are still gaps to be filled in the field of fiber. The mechanism, function, and potential clinical application of mitochondrial transfer in fibrosis need to be further studied.

5.9.

## 8‐Oxoguanine DNA Glycosylase and Thyroid Hormone
As a key enzyme for base excision and repair of pulmonary fibrosis, 8‐oxoguanine DNA glycosylase (Ogg1) is associated with cis‐aconitase 2 (Aco‐2), which can prevent mtDNA damage, p53 mitochondrial translocation, and internal apoptosis in ATII cell.[
 
] In addition, thyroid hormone can increase biogenesis by activating PGC‐1 α and promote mouse mitochondrial autophagy through PINK1, which helps to inhibit mitochondrial mediated apoptosis and reverse ATII cell mitochondrial abnormality induced by bleomycin.[
 
]

5.10.

## MicroRNA Let‐7
Endometrial stem cells (MenSC) derived from menstrual blood are a new type of adult stem cells derived from human menstrual blood that have been used to prevent acute lung injury.[
 
] Some scholars have found that human MenSCs protects the lungs from pulmonary fibrosis through its immunosuppressive and anti‐fibrosis functions. Human MenSC‐derived exosomes attenuate BLM‐induced pulmonary fibrosis and alveolar epithelial cell injury.[
 
] Importantly, exosome Let‐7 is a key protective factor of MenSC, which inhibits ROS activation and mtDNA damage by targeting LOX1 to regulate NLRP3 signal transduction.

5.11.

## Metformin
Metformin can reduce the development of pulmonary fibrosis by inhibiting NOX4. Metformin‐mediated AMPK activation inhibits TGF‐ β‐induced NOX4 expression.[
 
] It is reported that the activity of AMPK in fibrosis areas is low. AMPK‐deficient fibroblasts reduced basal oxygen consumption, mitochondrial reserve capacity, and maximum respiration. In the bleomycin model, metformin reverses pulmonary fibrosis through AMPK.[
 
] Metformin can activate AMPK reprogramming of IPF fibroblasts by reducing mTOR activation and promoting autophagy and down‐regulating the steady‐state level of ECM protein. AMPK activation also upregulates mitochondrial biosynthesis. In addition, it restored the sensitivity of myofibroblasts to internal apoptosis, especially apoptosis induced by antimycin A (mitochondrial inhibitor of ETC complex II).[
 
]

5.12.

## Pirfenidone
Pirfenidone can inhibit the overexpression of TGF‐ β 1 gene, thus inhibit the biological activity of fibroblasts, inhibit the proliferation of fibroblasts, reduce the synthesis and accumulation of matrix collagen, and inhibit the proliferation and expression of ECM, and finally play a good role in anti‐pulmonary fibrosis.[
 
] And studies have shown that pirfenidone can inhibit the synthesis of TNF‐ α and IL series of inflammatory factors, increase the production of anti‐inflammatory cytokines IL10, reduce inflammatory reaction and play an anti‐inflammatory effect.[
 , 
] The early use of pirfenidone has strong antioxidant activity and can effectively inhibit the production and release of inflammatory factors.

5.13.

## Nintedanib
Nintedanib is a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, which mainly acts on epidermal growth factor receptor, vascular endothelial growth factor receptor and platelet‐derived growth factor receptor.[
 
] The reduction of FVC in patients with IPF and severe gas exchange disorder was similar to that in patients with mild conditions. The practical experience of IPF patients supports the effectiveness of Nintedanib in slowing the progression of Progressive interstitial lung disease (ILD). In clinical trials and real‐world studies, Nintedanib has a controllable tolerance in patients with fibrosis ILD.[
 
]

5.14.

## Pamrevlumab
Pamrevlumab is a recombinant human antibody that binds to connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), which can recognize and bind CTGF and prevent it from activating downstream fibrogenic signal transduction. It has become a potential therapy for IPF and has entered phase III clinical trials.[
 
]

5.15.

## Bexotegrast
Bexotegrast (PLN‐74809), developed by Pliant Therapeutics, is a small molecular drug, an ITGAV&ITGB1 inhibitor, and an ITGAV&ITGB6 antagonist. At present, the highest stage of research and development of this drug in the world is clinical II, which is used to treat adult respiratory distress syndrome, primary sclerosing cholangitis, and IPF.[
 
]

5.16.

## N‐aceltylcysteine
N‐acetylcysteine (NAC) is a precursor of the endogenous antioxidant glutathione (GSH), a natural derivative of L‐arginine. It has antioxidant and free radical scavenging effects, and can inhibit the excessive proliferation and differentiation of lung fibroblasts and lymphocytes. Long‐term administration of NAC is effective, safe, and reliable. Moreover, N‐acetylcysteine combined with pirfenidone was superior to pirfenidone alone in the treatment of patients with advanced IPF.[
 
]

6.

## Conclusion
In vivo and in vitro studies have shown that fibrosis is a highly dynamic process. A dominant hypothesis has emerged suggesting that epithelial type II cell drives the disease process, this may be related to the substances released by ATII cell, known as DAMPs, can trigger a profibrosis inflammatory response. ATII cells are the first cells to be exposed to IPF pathogenic factors, for ATII cell with specific protein demand, these factors cause proteostasis disequilibrium of lung epithelial cell, leading to the accumulation of unfolded or misfolded protein aggregates. Due to the presence of ISR and MGIAC, unfolded or misfolded proteins interfere with internal mitochondrial reactions and rupture the mitochondrial structure, which may be the main reason for the large number of non‐normal mitochondria observed in ATII cell. And it means that internal mitochondrial material can enter the cytoplasm, with ATII cell occupying half of the mitochondria in the lungs, and that internal material released from damaged mitochondria (such as the observed mtDNA) can play a key pro‐fibrosis role as DAMP. This process may occur prior to the onset of IPF and allows the lung environment to evolve toward fibrosis by disrupting the factor balance in the lung. Although this article mainly makes a systematic exposition of the above viewpoints, some contents still need to be further discussed. In conclusion, although few studies have combined the important concepts closely related to IPF, such as proteostasis imbalance, ER stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, we have cascaded these concepts together through ISR and MGAIC and further summarized the potential mechanisms by which this cascade may trigger the fibrotic remodeling process of IPF.
