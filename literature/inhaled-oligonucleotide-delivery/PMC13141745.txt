## Abstract
Cancer combination therapy is widely used to address tumor heterogeneity, multidrug resistance, and dose-limiting toxicities associated with conventional monotherapy. However, the clinical benefits of free-drug combinations are often compromised by mismatched pharmacokinetics, inconsistent intratumoral drug ratios, and poor solubility or stability of individual agents. Nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs), composed of a solid-liquid lipid matrix, have emerged as a biocompatible nanoplatform capable of overcoming these limitations through high drug-loading capacity, improved colloidal stability, and tunable release behavior. This review aims to summarize the role of NLCs as co-delivery systems for cancer therapy. A literature search was conducted across databases to identify studies reporting NLC-based co-delivery strategies for anticancer applications. By enabling the co-encapsulation of multiple cargos inside a single carrier, NLCs facilitate synergistic interactions, maintain synchronized biodistribution, and enhance intracellular accumulation at tumor sites. These attributes support the co-delivery of diverse therapeutic modalities, including chemotherapeutics, genetic materials, and stimuli-responsive particles such as photodynamic, photothermal, and magnetic hyperthermia agents. The review briefly examines the structural features, formulation considerations, and preparation techniques of NLCs, followed by discussions of the rationales for co-loading strategy and representative applications across various cancer types. Challenges related to formulation complexity, manufacturing scalability, safety, and regulatory translation remain important considerations, whereas advances in rational formulation design and data-driven development strategies may accelerate the clinical translation of NLC-based co-delivery systems and expand their potential in cancer therapy.
Keywords: Nanostructured lipid carriers, Co-delivery system, Combination therapy, cancer therapy

## Introduction
Cancer imposes a tremendous burden on global health, with approximately 10 million deaths and 20 million new cases recorded in 2022, and projected to reach 35 million new cases by 2050 ( ; ). The incidence of cancer is strongly influenced by multiple risk factors, including tobacco use, alcohol consumption, unhealthy diet, obesity, physical inactivity, environmental carcinogens, and oncogenic infections ( ; ). Preventive measures, including vaccination, lifestyle modification, early detection through screening, and chemoprevention strategies, have contributed to reducing the incidence and mortality of certain cancers. Nevertheless, once malignant transformation occurs, therapeutic intervention becomes necessary, and treatment outcomes remain highly variable depending on the tumor type, stage, and molecular characteristics.
Current cancer treatment strategies generally involve a combination of therapeutic modalities, including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, hormone therapy, and immunotherapy ( ; ). Surgical resection remains a keystone treatment for localized tumors, whereas radiotherapy and chemotherapy are widely employed for both primary and metastatic disease ( ; ; ). More recently, molecularly targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors have demonstrated promising clinical benefits in specific patient populations ( ; ; ). Despite these advances, significant limitations remain. Conventional chemotherapeutic agents often exhibit poor aqueous solubility, unfavorable pharmacokinetics, and severe systemic toxicity due to their lack of selectivity toward cancer cells ( ; ). Single-targeted therapies frequently encounter resistance resulting from tumor heterogeneity, genetic mutations, and adaptive signaling pathways ( ; ). Immunotherapies, although highly effective in certain cases, benefit only a subset of patients and may cause immune-related adverse events ( ; ). These limitations highlight the need for improved therapeutic strategies capable of enhancing drug selectivity, minimizing systemic toxicity, and overcoming resistance mechanisms.
Combination therapy has emerged as an increasingly adopted strategy for cancer treatment. It exploits the principle that drugs acting on distinct molecular targets or pathways can produce synergistic effects, wherein the combined efficacy exceeds the sum of the individual effects ( ). Synergistic combinations can achieve the same therapeutic effects at lower doses, thereby reducing toxicity. However, co-administering free drugs rarely delivers the desired ratio to tumors because of disparate pharmacokinetics and biodistribution. In addition, due to the solubility differences of each drug, plasma protein binding and metabolism can lead to sub-optimal or antagonistic drug concentrations ( ). Nanoparticle-based delivery systems provide a solution by encapsulating multiple agents within a single carrier, ensuring that they share the same pharmacokinetic fate, enabling precise control of dosing ratios and protecting labile compounds from degradation.
Nanotechnology has yielded a variety of drug delivery vehicles, such as liposomes, polymeric micelles, dendrimers, polymer–drug conjugates, and inorganic nanoparticles. Among these, lipid-based nanoparticles offer excellent biocompatibility because they use physiological lipids as building blocks. The first generation of lipid nanoparticles, solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs), consists of solid lipids stabilized by surfactants. SLNs provide controlled release and protect drugs from degradation, but suffer from limited drug-loading capacity and the risk of drug expulsion during storage as the lipid matrix crystallizes ( ; ). Nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs) have been conceived as the next generation of lipid nanoparticles, which can overcome these limitations by incorporating a liquid lipid into the solid lipid matrix. The resulting imperfections and amorphous domains increase the number of voids available for drug accommodation, thereby enhancing the loading capacity and preventing drug leakage ( ). Importantly, NLCs can support multiple drug delivery mechanisms relevant to cancer therapy. Passive tumor targeting can be achieved through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, whereas active targeting strategies can be implemented by functionalizing the surface of the NLCs with various ligands ( ). In addition, NLCs can be engineered to exhibit triggered drug release in response to tumor-specific stimuli, such as acidic pH, elevated glutathione levels, enzymatic activity, or externally applied stimuli, including light or alternating magnetic fields ( ). NLCs also possess the capacity to enhance lymphatic drug transport following oral administration, enabling partial bypass of hepatic first-pass metabolism and potentially facilitating drug delivery to metastatic or lymph-associated tumors ( ). Despite these potentials, the development of NLC-based drug delivery systems still faces several challenges in terms of formulation complexity, large-scale manufacturing, long-term safety, and regulatory considerations.
While NLCs have been widely studied as carriers for single agents, there is growing interest in leveraging them as co-delivery platforms for cancer therapy. Reviews discussing NLCs often focus on the formulation and physicochemical properties of single-drug systems, whereas reviews on combination therapy typically highlight polymeric or inorganic carriers ( ; ; ). A comprehensive analysis of NLCs designed specifically for the co-delivery of multiple therapeutic agents remains lacking. This review aims to provide an overview of NLCs as multifunctional co-delivery systems for cancer therapy. This article briefly summarizes the structural characteristics, formulation considerations, and preparation methods of NLCs, followed by a discussion of the rationales for co-loading multiple therapeutic agents. Representative studies involving NLC-based co-delivery systems across various cancer types and therapeutic modalities are discussed, including combinations of chemotherapeutics, genetic materials, and photo- or magneto-therapeutics agents. Finally, current challenges and future directions are presented to offer perspectives on the continued development and clinical translation of NLC-based co-delivery platforms in cancer treatment.

## Methodology
The systematic search employed the keywords “nanostructured lipid carriers” AND (“co-delivery” OR “codelivery” OR “dual drug” OR “combination”) AND “cancer” across several databases, including Scopus, PubMed, ScienceDirect, and EBSCOhost. The search was restricted to English-language articles published between January 2015 and June 2025. Studies were included if they reported the design or evaluation of NLCs co-loaded with at least two agents, provided physicochemical characterization data (such as particle size, polydispersity index, zeta potential, and encapsulation efficiency), and evaluated anticancer efficacy in vitro or in vivo. Based on our literature search, the majority of reported NLC-based co-delivery systems in cancer therapy involve chemotherapeutic agents. Therefore, the studies discussed in this review predominantly describe chemotherapeutics combined with other chemotherapeutics, genetic materials, or photo-/magneto-therapeutic agents, whereas reports describing co-delivery systems that do not involve chemotherapeutic agents remain limited in the current literature.

3.

## An overview of NLCs
3.1.

## Structure
NLCs are nanosized colloidal systems comprising a lipid core surrounded by a surfactant layer ( ). They are formulated by mixing a solid lipid with a liquid lipid in specific ratios, followed by emulsification and solidification. The introduction of liquid lipids disturbs the crystalline order of the solid matrix, creating defects and amorphous regions that can trap the active molecules ( ). NLC archetypes, that is, imperfect, multiple, and amorphous, can be obtained using different lipid compositions, resulting in distinct characteristics. In the imperfect type, a small fraction of liquid lipid is mixed with a predominant solid lipid. The resulting matrix retains a crystalline structure but with imperfections that reduce the lattice packing density and provide space for drug molecules. Imperfect NLCs often show high drug loading but may exhibit lower entrapment efficiency because drugs near the surface can migrate to the aqueous phase ( ). In contrast, multiple-type NLCs use higher amounts of liquid lipid, which leads to phase separation within the solid matrix, resulting in oil nano-compartments embedded in a solid lipid scaffolding. Drugs dissolve predominantly in oil droplets, leading to a very high entrapment efficiency and a sustained release profile ( ). In the amorphous or structureless NLC, the selection of specific lipids or lipid blends that do not crystallize after cooling retains the amorphousness of the matrix. This eliminates crystalline domains that can exclude drugs, resulting in maximal drug accommodation and lowering the risk of polymorphic transitions ( ). However, amorphous NLCs may have lower mechanical stability and thus require careful optimization of surfactants ( ).
 
3.2.

## Formulation considerations
The rational selection of appropriate excipients is a critical step in the preparation of NLCs, as the type and proportion of each component strongly influence the physicochemical properties, stability, drug-loading capacity, and in vitro or in vivo performance of the final formulation.
3.2.1.

## Lipids
Solid lipids influence the melting point and matrix integrity, which concurrently facilitate controlled drug expulsion. Frequently used solid lipids include triglycerides (e.g., glyceryl behenate, glyceryl monostearate), waxes (e.g., cetyl palmitate), fatty acids (e.g., stearic acid), and partial glycerides (e.g., glyceryl palmitostearate) ( ; ; ; ; ). These materials must be biocompatible, biodegradable, and solid at physiological temperatures. Liquid lipids create structural imperfections and solubilize lipophilic drugs, thereby increasing the drug loading and entrapment efficiency of NLCs. Medium-chain triglycerides (MCT), oleic acid, and various oils are among the widely used liquid lipids ( ). In addition to their role as excipients, certain liquid lipids can also be utilized as active agents when they possess intrinsic pharmacological activities ( ; ).
In addition to their structural function, lipid components of NLCs may contribute to tumor targeting due to the altered lipid metabolism observed in many cancer cells. Rapidly proliferating tumor cells exhibit increased lipid demand to support membrane biosynthesis, energy production, and oncogenic signaling pathways ( ; ). Consequently, cancer cells frequently overexpress lipid uptake mechanisms, including low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptors and fatty acid transport proteins ( ). NLCs may therefore partially exploit this metabolic feature, as their lipid components can interact with cellular lipid transport pathways, facilitating enhanced cellular uptake and intracellular trafficking ( ). This intrinsic affinity between lipid-based nanocarriers and lipid-hungry tumor cells may contribute to improved tumor accumulation and therapeutic delivery.

3.2.2.

## Surfactants and co-surfactants
Surfactants stabilize the oil–water interface and prevent aggregation. Nonionic surfactants, such as polysorbate 80 and poloxamers, are commonly used owing to their low toxicity. Furthermore, using polysorbate 80 is also advantageous because it can inhibit P-gp, thereby reversing drug resistance and enhancing drug uptake ( ). Meanwhile, ionic surfactants such as cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), dimethyldioctadecylammonium bromide (DDAB), and sodium deoxycholate can impart surface charge and may be more cytotoxic ( ; ). Beyond surfactants, co-surfactants such as ethanol and propylene glycol can further reduce interfacial tension and aid emulsification, thereby improving the formation and stability of NLCs ( ; ).

3.2.3.

## Therapeutic cargos
NLCs are highly versatile and can encapsulate a wide range of chemotherapeutic and diagnostic agents. Hydrophobic small molecules such as paclitaxel and docetaxel, can be efficiently incorporated into the lipid matrix, where they benefit from enhanced solubility, sustained release, and protection from premature degradation ( ; ). For nucleic acids such as DNA, siRNA, miRNA, and mRNA, encapsulation relies on electrostatic interactions, because these negatively charged hydrophilic molecules cannot partition into the lipid core. Thus, they are localized at the lipid-aqueous interface or electrostatically complexed with cationic lipids incorporated into the NLC ( ; ; ).
NLCs can also carry stimuli-responsive cargo, including agents for photodynamic therapy (PDT), photothermal therapy (PTT), and magnetic hyperthermia therapy (MHT). PDT agents such as indocyanine green and chlorin e6 produce cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) upon light irradiation, whereas PTT agents such as gold nanoparticles convert near-infrared light into localized heat to ablate tumors or trigger drug release ( ; ; ). Similarly, superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) act as MHT agents, generating heat under an alternating magnetic field ( ). In a co-delivery setting, it is crucial to optimize the ratio between the different cargos to maximize synergistic efficacy while minimizing drug competition or premature release ( ).

3.2.4.

## Functionalizing agents and other excipients
To enhance tumor targeting, formulating NLCs that are functionalized with ligands is often considered as the best strategy. Folic acid and hyaluronic acid are among the most widely used, as they can bind to folate and CD44 receptors, respectively, which are overexpressed in several cancer cells ( ; ). Various peptides can also be employed as targeting moieties owing to their excellent tumor-homing properties, each targeting specific receptor ( ; ). Correspondingly, using antibodies such as cetuximab, conatumumab, and rituximab as decorating agents may improve cell uptake, which subsequently leads to higher cell death ( ; ; ). In addition, glycoproteins such as transferrin and lactoferrin have been employed to exploit their respective receptor pathways, which are upregulated in many cancer cells ( ; ).
Cationic lipids, such as 1,2-dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium-propane (DOTAP) and N-[1-(2,3-dioleyloxy)propyl]-N,N,N-trimethyl-ammonium chloride (DOTMA), can also be incorporated as additional components to enable electrostatic complexation with negatively charged biomolecules such as DNA, siRNA, and mRNA ( ; ). This strategy not only allows stable gene-loaded NLCs formation with high conjugation efficiency but also enhances cellular uptake and facilitates gene silencing in resistant cancer cells, thereby supporting combined drug-gene therapy approaches ( ).

3.3.

## Preparation methods
3.3.1.

## High-pressure homogenization
High-pressure homogenization (HPH) remains the most widely applied method and is available for both hot and cold variations. In hot HPH, lipids and drugs are melted, emulsified with a heated aqueous surfactant solution, and then subjected to homogenization at pressures of 100-2000 bar, yielding nanoparticles typically below 200 nm upon cooling. Conversely, cold HPH requires solidification of the drug-lipid melt, followed by dispersion into a surfactant solution prior to homogenization ( ). While hot HPH is efficient and well-suited for scale-up, cold HPH offers a better alternative for heat-sensitive drugs, although it may result in larger particles and broader size distributions ( ).

3.3.2.

## High-shear homogenization and ultrasonication
High-shear homogenization and ultrasonication-based preparations offer a simple procedure, both with similar nanosizing mechanisms. Solid lipids are melted above their melting point, blended with the liquid lipid, and subsequently mixed with the surfactant solution. The resulting primary emulsion was then subjected to homogenization or sonication to reduce the particle size ( ). However, beyond technical simplicity, the use of a homogenizer or sonicator probe warrants attention owing to the possibility of overheated samples. Thus, optimization of the duration of homogenization or sonication should be considered ( ).

3.3.3.

## Solvent-emulsification
Solvent-based methods, including solvent-emulsification evaporation and solvent-emulsification diffusion, rely on dissolving lipids in either water-immiscible solvents such as cyclohexane and chloroform, or water-miscible solvents such as ethanol and ethyl acetate. Upon mixing with an aqueous surfactant phase, the solvent diffuses or evaporates, precipitating lipids to form NLCs ( ). These methods require relatively low energy inputs and are suitable for thermolabile drugs. Nevertheless, the careful removal of residual solvents is mandatory, and scaling up the process remains challenging.

3.3.4.

## Hot-melt extrusion
Hot-melt extrusion (HME) is adapted to continuous, solvent-free pharmaceutical production. During the process, the main NLC components are pumped into and mixed inside the extruder under a controlled temperature. Other excipients are then inserted into specific feeding zones upon extrusion, forming a pre-emulsion that is dispersed and size-reduced by sonication or homogenization ( ; ). Despite its good scalability, the use of the HME method remains rarely explored, possibly due to the lab-scale nature of many NLC studies ( ).

3.3.5.

## Phase-inversion temperature
The phase-inversion temperature (PIT) technique involves reversing the emulsion type and heavily relies on the temperature-dependent properties of non-ionic surfactants. At high temperatures, the system forms a water-in-oil emulsion, which, upon rapid cooling, undergoes inversion to an oil-in-water system, producing stable nanoparticles ( ). This approach avoids excessive use of energy and organic solvents but is highly sensitive to surfactant composition and offers less precise control over particle size compared with homogenization techniques ( ).

3.4.

## General characteristics
3.4.1.

## Particle size, polydispersity index, and morphology
Particle size is one of the most critical attributes of NLCs, where a smaller particle size translates to a larger surface area, subsequently facilitating higher drug absorption. When applied as drug delivery systems for cancer therapy, nanosized particles may achieve a balance between prolonged circulation and tumor penetration by the EPR effect ( ). Particle size also strongly influences the mechanism and efficiency of cellular uptake. Nanoparticles with sizes below approximately 200 nm are more readily internalized by cancer cells through energy-dependent endocytic pathways, such as clathrin-mediated and caveolae-mediated endocytosis ( ). Smaller nanoparticles generally exhibit higher cellular uptake efficiency because their reduced steric hindrance facilitates internalization, whereas larger particles may experience limited endocytosis and slower intracellular trafficking ( ). However, it is important to note that particles that are too small risk rapid renal clearance ( ). Controlling the NLC size within an optimal nanoscale range is essential to maximize cellular internalization while maintaining favorable biodistribution. The polydispersity index (PDI) reflects particle size uniformity. In lipid-based nano-formulations, a PDI below 0.3 is generally accepted as indicative of a narrow distribution that helps ensure predictable biodistribution and reproducible performance ( ). In systems co-loading multiple cargos, the size and PDI are of greater importance. Two drugs or a drug plus nucleic acids can compete for space or destabilize the lipid matrix ( ). Therefore, a formulation that maintains a constant size and PDI, even under increased loading, is preferred.
In addition to particle size, morphological observations using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) or transmission electron microscopy (TEM) can provide further insight into particle shape and surface structure. These features affect how cells perceive and internalize the particles, as well as how stable they remain under biological stress ( ). Although spherical nanoparticles are most commonly produced in lipid-based systems because of their thermodynamic stability, deviations from spherical geometry can alter interactions with the cellular membrane. Rod-like nanoparticles may exhibit prolonged membrane contact and altered internalization kinetics, whereas spherical particles generally demonstrate more efficient and uniform uptake due to isotropic membrane wrapping ( ; ). Particle shape properties is particularly important in NLCs co-loading drugs with PTT agents, such as gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), where different geometries render to different cell uptake behavior and targeting specificity ( ; ; ).

3.4.2.

## Surface charge
The surface charge, which is commonly measured by the zeta potential, exerts a strong influence on stability, where its higher absolute values (> ±30 mV) promote electrostatic repulsion between particles thus suppressing aggregation during storage ( ). Surface charge also plays a critical role in determining the interaction between nanoparticles and the cellular membrane. Because the plasma membrane is predominantly negatively charged due to the presence of phospholipids and glycoproteins, positively charged nanoparticles generally exhibit stronger electrostatic interactions with cell membranes, which may enhance cellular uptake through adsorptive endocytosis ( ). Functionalizing NLCs with positively-charged components such as chitosan is considered a feasible strategy for this purpose ( ; ). On the contrary, a neutral or mildly negative surface charge tend to exhibit weaker direct membrane interactions but may benefit from reduced nonspecific protein adsorption and prolonged systemic circulation ( ; ).

3.4.3.

## Entrapment efficiency
Entrapment efficiency (EE) indicates the fraction of loaded drug that remains associated with the carrier versus being free, whereas drug loading expresses the mass ratio of the drug to the total mass in the nanoparticle ( ). A high EE and reasonable loading are critical for delivering a therapeutic dose without excessive carrier mass or unintended release. In single-cargo NLCs, a high EE is more accessible because the lipid matrix can be optimized for one drug. However, in co-delivery systems, one cargo may compete with the other for incorporation or displace it upon storage or release ( ; ). Thus, optimizing the lipid composition, surfactants, and preparation parameters is essential to obtain the desired results. For example, the preparation of NLCs containing 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) and resveratrol (RSV) using the hot HPH method resulted in EE values of 60.35% and 93.65% for 5-FU and RSV, respectively. Upon changing the preparation method to a modified double emulsion, the EE of 5-FU and RSV increased to 71.62% and 97.03%, respectively, which was selected as the preferred formulation in the subsequent performance study ( ).

3.4.4.

## Drug release
The evaluation of in vitro release provides insight into the behavior of NLCs after administration. By testing under simulated physiological conditions, researchers can predict whether a formulation will provide a burst, sustained, or stimulus-responsive release. For cancer therapy, sustained release is often desirable because it maintains therapeutic drug levels at the tumor site while minimizing systemic peaks and toxicity ( ; ).
In co-delivery systems, the cargo can be released simultaneously or sequentially, depending on the therapeutic strategy. Simultaneous release implies that multiple therapeutics are liberated from the carrier at essentially the same time, which is useful when drugs act synergistically or when the same onset is required ( ). In a study on NLC co-loaded with docetaxel and tariquidar, almost superimposed biphasic profiles of both drugs were observed, with a burst release during the first 6 h followed by a sustained release until 24 h ( ).
In sequential or staggered release, one agent (such as a sensitizer, inhibitor, or modulator) must act before the second agent takes effect. Sequential release allows spatiotemporal control, improving therapy by timing the action of one cargo to prepare the tumor microenvironment, suppress resistance pathways, or modulate signaling before the delivery of the “killer” drug ( ; ; ). For example, an N-acetyl-D-glucosamine (NAG)-guided NLC system was designed to co-load paclitaxel and gemcitabine, with dual responsiveness to intracellular redox and pH conditions. In this formulation, paclitaxel is first released in a reductive tumor environment, followed by the delayed release of gemcitabine under acidic conditions ( ).

3.4.5.

## Stability
The stability of NLCs is a critical determinant of their therapeutic performance and a prerequisite for their clinical translation. A stable system should preserve particle size, PDI, surface charge, and drug content throughout its intended shelf life, while resisting aggregation, polymorphic transitions, or premature drug leakage ( ). Stability testing typically involves storing formulations under refrigerated (4 °C), ambient, and accelerated conditions to assess their robustness under different environmental stresses. The International Council for Harmonisation (ICH) guidelines Q1A(R2) recommend stability testing under long-term (25 ± 2 °C / 60 ± 5% RH), intermediate (30 ± 2 °C / 65 ± 5% RH), and accelerated (40 ± 2 °C / 75 ± 5% RH) conditions for at least six months, depending on the dosage form ( ). Although these guidelines were originally designed for conventional pharmaceuticals, they provide a valuable reference framework for nanomedicines, including NLCs, to ensure product quality, safety, and efficacy during storage and distribution.
For co-loaded systems, stability demands become more stringent because two or more cargos may partition unevenly or displace each other within the lipid matrix, causing leakage or altered release profiles over time ( ; ). Using lipid mixtures that form amorphous matrices and employing protective coatings such as PEGylation are common strategies to mitigate these risks ( ; ). Another feasible strategy to improve stability is the lyophilization of the NLC dispersion into a dry powder. Lyophilization reduces moisture-driven degradation, helps preserve the spatial arrangement of multiple cargos within the lipid matrix, and minimizes cargo leakage during storage ( ). Cryoprotectants such as trehalose, mannitol, dextrose, or lactose are employed to maintain particle size, PDI, and entrapment efficiency after drying and reconstitution ( ).

4.

## Rationales for co-loading therapeutic agents into NLCs
4.1.

## Synergistic effect and dose reduction
The co-delivery strategy is fundamentally motivated by the recognition that tumor progression and therapeutic resistance are governed by multiple, interconnected signaling pathways rather than a single dominant molecular target. Co-loading therapeutic agents into a single carrier simultaneously targets these pathways, thereby improving the potential of synergistic therapeutic effects. In addition, nanoparticle-based co-delivery systems can help maintain optimal drug ratios in vivo, which are often difficult to achieve with free drug combinations due to differences in physicochemical properties, pharmacokinetics, and tissue distribution ( ; ). Lipid-based nanoparticles, such as NLCs, can facilitate synergistic interactions through several mechanistic routes. First, the co-encapsulation of multiple agents within a single nanocarrier ensures their synchronized delivery to the same tumor cells, thus increasing the probability that both agents act on complementary molecular targets within the same cellular environment ( ; ). Second, lipid-based nanocarriers can enhance intracellular accumulation of poorly soluble or membrane-impermeable drugs, allowing each component of the combination to reach therapeutically relevant concentrations ( ). Third, the lipid matrix may enable the differential localization of cargos within the NLC structure, which can influence release kinetics and intracellular trafficking ( ).
Interactions between drugs in combination therapy are commonly evaluated using the Chou-Talalay method, which provides a theoretical framework based on the median effect principle. In this approach, the combination index (CI) quantitatively describes the nature of drug-drug interactions, where CI < 1 indicates synergism, CI = 1 indicates additivity, and CI > 1 indicates antagonism ( ; ). These interaction patterns can be visualized using an isobologram, where combinations falling below the line of additivity indicate synergistic interactions, which means that lower doses of each drug are required to achieve a given effect, whereas combinations above this line signify antagonism ( A). This principle also accounts for the effect level of the drug combination through the fraction affected (fa), which represents the proportion of cells inhibited or killed by the treatment ( ). The CI-fa plot highlights the effect-dependent nature of drug interactions, demonstrating that the degree of synergy may vary across different fa values rather than remain constant ( B). CI values progressively decrease with increasing fa, indicating that synergistic interactions become more pronounced at higher levels of biological responses.
 Such effect-dependent synergy is particularly relevant in cancer therapy, as it suggests that optimal drug combinations may achieve maximal cooperative benefit at therapeutically meaningful levels of tumor inhibition rather than at low, sub-effective doses. In this context, the dose reduction index (DRI) is a critical complementary parameter, reflecting the extent to which the dose of each drug can be reduced in a combination regimen to achieve a given therapeutic effect relative to monotherapy ( ). This is particularly important because a reduced dose leads to lower toxicity and minimizes adverse side effects ( ). As a case in point, NLCs co-loaded with docetaxel and curcumin at a 1:2 ratio achieved a CI value of 0.32 with 0.95 fa, indicating a strong synergistic effect. The same formulation also showed a DRI of 3.4, indicating that each drug dose could be reduced by 3.4-fold while preserving anticancer activity. Furthermore, the dual-loaded NLCs provided improved safety-relevant parameters as demonstrated by an in vitro hemocompatibility study ( ).

4.2.

## Overcoming multidrug resistance
Multidrug resistance (MDR) is a major biological barrier to successful cancer chemotherapy and remains one of the principal causes of therapeutic failure. MDR describes the ability of cancer cells to develop resistance to multiple anticancer drugs, either as an intrinsic property of the tumor or as an acquired response following repeated chemotherapy exposure ( ). MDR is driven mainly by the overexpression of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp), breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP), and multidrug resistance-associated proteins (MRPs), which actively expel anticancer agents from tumor cells via ATP-dependent efflux, thus lowering intracellular drug concentration below therapeutic thresholds. This phenomenon is particularly pronounced in monotherapy settings that employ chemotherapeutics that are substrates of transporters, including doxorubicin, docetaxel, or paclitaxel ( ). Inhibition of ABC transporters has frequently been proposed as a potential strategy to reverse MDR.
In this context, co-loading chemotherapeutics and MDR modulators into NLCs offers a rational approach to overcome efflux-mediated resistance ( ). Co-encapsulation enables both agents to be delivered to the same resistant cancer cells at a controlled ratio, addressing the limitation of free-drug combinations, where differential pharmacokinetics may lead to asynchronous intracellular exposure ( ). For instance, combining a potent P-gp inhibitor, such as tariquidar, with docetaxel into NLCs evidently enhanced cytotoxicity that led to an increased number of apoptotic cells when compared to single-drug NLCs. This is due to the higher docetaxel accumulation, which is achievable because of P-gp suppression by tariquidar ( ). A similar outcome was observed in NLCs co-encapsulating doxorubicin and β-lapachone. Notably, β-lapachone modulates MDR through NQO1-mediated redox cycling, which disrupts the cellular redox balance and depletes ATP. As a result, ATP-dependent efflux processes were impaired, thus hindering P-gp activity ( ).
 An additional advantage of co-delivery using NLCs is the intrinsic properties of lipids and surfactants. Lipid-based components (e.g., hard fat, glyceryl monooleate, and gelucires) as well as nonionic surfactants (e.g., poloxamers and polysorbates) used in NLC formulations have been reported to inhibit P-gp activity or alter transporter expression, thereby contributing to a supplementary MDR-reversal effect alongside the loaded modulators ( ; ; ; ). Collectively, encapsulating first-line chemotherapeutics together with ABC transporter modulators into lipid-based nanocarriers, such as NLCs, provides a coherent strategy to overcome one of the most persistent obstacles in cancer therapy.

4.3.

## Spatiotemporal drug release
Deliberate programming of drug release at a specific space and time has become increasingly important when designing co-delivery systems. Spatiotemporal control regulates where and when individual components of a drug combination are released, rather than strictly enforcing simultaneous exposure. This concept arises from the recognition that the therapeutic outcome of combination therapy is often sequence-dependent, with one agent acting as a sensitizer, modulator, or adjuvant, priming the tumor microenvironment to enhance the activity of a second agent as the primary cytotoxic agent ( ). In such cases, premature release of the cytotoxic drug may be suboptimal or counterproductive if the tumor cells have not yet been rendered vulnerable. Conceptual analyses of sequential delivery systems highlight that appropriate temporal ordering can enhance pathway inhibition, suppress compensatory signaling, and reduce the likelihood of resistance development ( ).
A carefully developed nanoparticle-based co-delivery system can fulfill this purpose while also overcoming common problems of combination therapy such as mismatched release kinetics which can negate synergy or exacerbate toxicity ( ; ). In particular, NLCs may provide a versatile platform for implementing spatiotemporal release strategies because they can be engineered to preferentially release one payload earlier than the other based on differences in drug localization, lipid affinity, or stimulus sensitivity. One study demonstrated that curcumin exhibited a markedly faster release profile than temozolomide from co-encapsulated NLCs ( A-D). This behavior was attributed to the higher lipophilicity of curcumin, which promotes its preferential localization within the outer lipid layers of the NLC matrix, thus facilitating more rapid diffusion into the release medium compared to temozolomide ( E). This sequential release was considered beneficial, where curcumin functions as a chemosensitizer that enhances oxidative stress and disrupts survival signaling prior to temozolomide-mediated DNA damage ( ).
 Stimuli-responsive NLC systems provide an additional mechanism for achieving spatiotemporal drug release by exploiting the biochemical characteristics of tumor microenvironments. Among the most widely investigated triggers are pH-, redox-, and enzyme-responsive mechanisms. Tumor tissues and intracellular endosomal/lysosomal compartments typically exhibit mildly acidic conditions (pH 5.0–6.0), which can promote drug release from NLC matrices containing acid-labile linkages or pH-sensitive lipids ( ; ). Redox-responsive systems exploit the elevated intracellular concentration of glutathione (GSH) in tumor cells, which is several-fold higher than that in extracellular fluids. The Incorporation of disulfide bonds or other redox-sensitive linkers within the NLC structure enables rapid cleavage in the reductive intracellular environment, thereby triggering the release of the encapsulated cargos ( ). In addition, enzyme-responsive NLCs can exploit the overexpression of tumor-associated enzymes such as NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase-1 (NQO1) and lipases that degrade specific lipid components of the nanocarrier ( ; ). Through these mechanisms, NLCs can achieve site-specific and condition-dependent drug release, allowing the sequential or controlled liberation of therapeutic agents within tumor tissues, further strengthening their utility as platforms for spatiotemporally programmed combination therapy.

## NLCs as co-delivery systems in cancer treatment
5.1.

## Co-delivery of chemotherapeutics in various cancers
NLCs are particularly well suited for co-delivery systems because their flexible lipid matrix enables the encapsulation of multiple agents while improving the physicochemical properties of the loaded drugs and coordinating intracellular delivery. Among the numerous types of cancer, dual-drug NLCs have demonstrated enhanced cytotoxicity, increased apoptosis, improved tumor penetration, and more favorable pharmacokinetic and biodistribution profiles. The key characteristics of co-loaded NLCs and their therapeutic outcomes in various cancers are summarized in .
 5.1.1.

## Brain cancer
Gliomas, an aggressive type of brain cancer, pose major therapeutic challenges due to the restrictive blood-brain barrier and the inherent resistance of tumor to conventional chemotherapy. NLCs have been developed to co-deliver multiple agents via complementary mechanisms to overcome these limitations. In one study, the combined delivery of temozolomide (TMZ) and curcumin (CUR) demonstrated strong synergism, reducing glioma C6 cell viability to below 40% at 10 μg/mL, enhancing cellular uptake, inducing S-phase cell cycle arrest of approximately 34%, and significantly decreasing tumor volume in vivo to approximately 200 mm3 after 15 days of treatment. The improvement in therapeutic performance was attributed to the sequential drug release from the NLC matrix because of the different localization of CUR and TMZ. CUR sensitizes cancer cells by disrupting signaling pathways. Subsequently, TMZ was released and exerted cytotoxicity by inducing damage through the mismatch repair of methylated adducts within the DNA of cancer cells that triggers apoptosis ( ).
In another study, TMZ was co-encapsulated with vincristine in an optimized NLC formulation, achieving markedly greater cytotoxicity (IC50 = 0.23 μM) against U87 MG glioblastoma cells. This demonstrated a 2.4-fold dose advantage compared to a single TMZ-NLC and a 5-fold dose advantage compared to dual-drug SLNs ( ). Correspondingly, in vivo results of co-loaded NLCs showed a tumor inhibition rate of up to 83.17%, which is considerably higher than that in the other treatment groups. These dual-drug NLC formulations provide coordinated delivery across the blood-brain barrier and amplify the therapeutic response by integrating distinct cytotoxic mechanisms, suggesting that NLC-mediated co-delivery represents a promising strategy for overcoming glioma resistance and improving clinical outcomes in brain cancer therapy.

5.1.2.

## Breast cancer
Breast cancer is one of the most widespread malignancies, and its therapeutic resistance and off-target toxicity often limit the success of conventional chemotherapy. NLC-based co-delivery systems have been designed to synchronize multiple drugs with synergistic pharmacological actions. Di et al. co-encapsulated doxorubicin (DOX) and cisplatin (CDDP) into NLCs to overcome drug resistance in MCF-7/ADR cells. The dual formulation produced the highest cytotoxicity, reduced cell viability to below 30%, and increased the late-apoptotic fractions compared to the single-drug systems. In vivo, DOX-CDDP NLCs yielded up to 80% tumor volume reduction and prolonged survival compared to free drugs and single-loaded NLCs. This improvement was attributed to complementary DNA intercalation and topoisomerase II inhibition by DOX together with DNA crosslinking by CDDP, which jointly intensified genotoxic stress and apoptotic signaling while lowering effective doses ( ).
In another study, DOX was co-loaded with DHA into NLCs radiolabeled using Technetium-99 m (Tc-99 m), representing a theranostic platform that combines imaging and therapy. An in vivo study in 4 T1-bearing BALB/c mice revealed that the radiolabeled NLC-DHA-DOX exhibited biphasic blood clearance, high tumor-to-muscle (target-to-non-target) uptake ratios, and a tumor growth inhibition rate of nearly 90%. Importantly, cardiac and hepatic toxicity markers were notably lower than those for free DOX. The authors attributed these results to the dual role of DHA as both a structural lipid that promotes tumor penetration and a bioactive polyunsaturated fatty acid that enhances oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation within tumor membranes. Radiolabeling with Tc-99 m allowed dynamic monitoring of biodistribution, confirming prolonged circulation and preferential tumor accumulation, illustrating the role of NLCs, both as drug delivery and diagnostic tools ( ).
DOX was also employed as a co-encapsulated agent alongside sclareol, a diterpene exhibiting pro-apoptotic and stress-inducing properties, in a study by Borges et al. The combination at a 1:1.9 M ratio yielded synergistic cytotoxicity (CI < 1) in both MDA-MB-231 and 4 T1 cells, with IC50 values around 6.39–6.6 μM. In vivo, NLC-DOX-sclareol achieved marked tumor volume suppression (∼75%) in 4 T1 tumor-bearing mice. Moreover, the free DOX-sclareol combination caused body weight loss, behavioral changes, and hematologic toxicity, whereas NLC-DOX-sclareol preserved its efficacy without adverse effects. Mechanistically, sclareol enhanced endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondrial stress, as reflected by elevated creatine-kinase (CK) activity, thereby amplifying DOX-induced apoptosis, while the nanocarrier modulated biodistribution and toxicity ( ).
Another breast cancer therapeutic agent, such as exemestane (EXE), can also be beneficial when incorporated into NLCs. EXE is classified as a BCS class IV drug owing to its low solubility and permeability. Gupta et al. employed a Box-Behnken Design to optimize NLCs containing EXE and thymoquinone (THY). Owing to enhanced absorption, the EXE-THY-NLCs significantly improved cellular uptake and cytotoxicity in MCF-7 cells, reducing the IC50 to approximately 12.5 μM. The therapeutic improvement was also attributed to the combination of EXE and THY, which simultaneously suppressed estrogen biosynthesis through aromatase inhibition, induced oxidative stress-mediated apoptosis, and mitigated inflammatory signaling. Their cooperative actions enhance the apoptotic response and reduce hormone-dependent proliferation, indicating potential for improved outcomes in estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer ( ).
In addition to common anti-cancer agents, NLC co-delivery systems have also been applied to drug repurposing strategies that integrate agents with non-oncologic origins but recognized anticancer potential. For instance, artemether (ART), a semi-synthetic antimalarial, has demonstrated potent anticancer activity by inducing oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in tumor cells. In an ART-DHA-NLCs study, the dual-drug system significantly increased cytotoxicity against MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells by 12.3- and 400-fold, respectively. It also inhibited cell migration by approximately 60%, indicating its strong antimetastatic potential. Moreover, flow cytometric and confocal analyses revealed rapid nanoparticle internalization and enhanced intracellular accumulation. The authors suggested that apoptosis may be ROS-mediated, attributed to synergistic interplay between peroxide-driven oxidative action of ART and lipid-peroxidation activity of DHA, which collectively disrupted redox homeostasis and selectively targeted malignant cells while sparing non-tumorigenic MCF-10A cells ( ).
Similarly, Kumari and Dang proposed a repurposed approach involving metformin (MET), a well-established antidiabetic agent, through co-loading with THY in an NLC formulation. This dual-drug system exhibited strong cytotoxicity in MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 cells, with IC50 values of 20.26 μg/mL and 15.26 μg/mL, respectively. It was inferred that MET activates the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway, which downregulates mTOR signaling, thereby constraining cell growth and triggering both cell death and cell cycle arrest. Concurrently, THY, a potent NF-κB inhibitor, enhances apoptosis by elevating oxidative stress and suppressing pro-survival transcriptional programs. The study demonstrated that cell cycle progression following MET-THY-NLC treatment was markedly halted. In MCF-7 cells, only 2.65% of cells progressed to the S phase and 3.2% to the G2/M phase, whereas in MDA-MB-231 cells, the proportions were 3.6% and 3.0%, respectively, which were significantly lower than those in untreated controls. These findings confirmed a pronounced G0/G1-phase arrest, that corresponds to p53 activation, DNA damage, and modulation of cyclin-dependent kinases that restricted entry into the synthesis phase ( ).

5.1.3.

## Hepatocellular carcinoma
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) ranks among the top causes of cancer-related mortality owing to its late presentation, underlying liver dysfunction, and highly heterogeneous molecular profile ( ; ). Despite advances in diagnostics and therapies, the efficacy of systemic treatments remains modest in many cases, highlighting the need for therapeutic platforms that can simultaneously improve drug delivery, target multiple oncogenic pathways, and overcome the complex tumor microenvironment barriers inherent to HCC.
In a recent study, Cordeiro et al. developed beeswax-based NLCs co-loaded with DOX and 4-nitrochalcone (4NC) using a double-emulsion melt-dispersion technique. The resulting nanoparticles were semi-spherical, with a size of approximately 200 nm, and remained colloidally stable before and after lyophilization. The encapsulation efficiencies reached 98.6% (4NC) and 84.3% (DOX), confirming the capacity of nanosystems to entrap both hydrophilic and lipophilic drugs. Cytotoxic screening against several tumor lines (HepG-2, A172, MCF-7) revealed that the 4NC-DOX-NLCs produced the most pronounced effect on HepG-2 hepatocarcinoma cells, with IC50 of 8.3 μg/mL (4NC) and 12.7 μg/mL (DOX), representing approximately a 2-fold improvement over single-drug NLCs and far lower than free-drug forms. The combination index (CI = 1.06) denoted an additive to mildly synergistic effect, whereas the selectivity index (SI > 2) indicated preferential toxicity toward cancer cells relative to McCoy normal fibroblasts. Flow cytometric showed interesting results, where intracellular ROS levels in 4NC-DOX-NLCs were higher than those in 4NC-NLCs but lower than those in DOX-NLCs, despite the dual system yielding the highest apoptosis rate (∼50%). This suggests that the moderate antioxidant activity of 4NC attenuates DOX-induced oxidative bursts, thus tempering excessive ROS damage while preserving apoptotic efficacy and a favorable balance between potency and safety in HCC therapy ( ).
In another study, the utilization of the “unification of medicines and excipients” principle incorporated pharmacologically active oils as functional lipids in the formulation. The prepared NLCs contained naringin (NG) and coix seed oil (CSO), a traditional Chinese medicinal oil with inherent antitumor and immunomodulatory properties. CSO functioned simultaneously as a liquid lipid and therapeutic agent. The optimized NG-CSO-NLCs exhibited a 3.24-fold lower IC50 against HepG-2 cells than free NG and induced apoptosis 2.12- and 9.28-fold higher than control NLCs containing conventional lipids (neodecanoate triglycerides or oleic acid). The co-loaded NLCs also achieved superior drug release under both physiological (pH 7.4) and acidic (pH 5.0) conditions, showing an initial burst followed by sustained diffusion over 36 h. In vivo, intraperitoneal administration (20 mg/kg for 10 days) markedly reduced tumor volume in HepG-2-bearing BALB/c mice and was accompanied by upregulation of the cytokines IL-6 and IL-10, which was attributed to the immunomodulatory action of CSO that promotes macrophage activation and enhances systemic antitumor immunity ( ).

5.1.4.

## Lung cancer
Lung cancer is one of the most frequently diagnosed malignances worldwide, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for nearly 85% of all cases ( ). NLCs serve as an advantageous platform for standard lung chemotherapies, which often suffer from poor solubility and systemic toxicity. In a study by Wang et al., NLCs co-encapsulating paclitaxel (PTX) and DOX enhanced cytotoxicity by up to three-fold compared to single-drug NLCs and nine-fold compared to free drugs. Against NCI-H460 cells, the combination achieved the lowest IC50 value of 0.021 μM for both PTX and DOX. Moreover, in vivo studies in NCI-H460 xenograft-bearing BALB/c nude mice showed a tumor inhibition rate of 84% without significant body weight loss ( ). Another taxane-based combination, docetaxel (DTX), was co-encapsulated with CUR. The optimized DTX-CUR-NLCs displayed a mean diameter of approximately 150 nm with high EE for both cargoes and reduced the viability of NCI-H460 cells at a 1:2 DTX/CUR ratio owing to the highly synergistic combination (CI = 0.32). Additionally, a hemolysis study revealed the safety of the formulation at 20 mg/mL DTX doses ( ).
Du and Yin introduced NLCs co-delivering an amphiphilic etoposide prodrug (EtpP) and CDDP with an average particle size of 177 nm. The dual-drug formulation synergistically enhanced cellular uptake by up to 57% and cytotoxicity in both A549 and CDDP-resistant A549/DDP cells. Furthermore, EtpP-CDDP NLCs prompted 2.3- and 1.5-fold increases in caspase-3 activity compared to free drug and non-prodrug etoposide-CDDP-NLCs, respectively, reflecting superior apoptotic efficiency. An in vivo study using tumor-bearing female BALB/c mice showed that the injected dual-drug NLCs provided more sustained plasma retention and higher drug distribution, which translated to superior tumor inhibition down to 200 mm3 with negligible change in body weight. These findings indicate that the modification of etoposide into its prodrug enhanced bioavailability and facilitated more effective co-localization with CDDP inside tumor cells, which subsequently triggered caspase-3-mediated apoptosis ( ).
In another study, Elzayat et al. combined gefitinib (GEF) and azacitidine (AZT) in biodegradable NLCs prepared using the melt-emulsification ultrasonication method, achieving an IC50 of less than 3 μg/mL in A549 cells, which is lower than those of single-drug NLCs and non-NLCs. The synergistic effect of the dual system arises from the distinct mechanisms of both drugs. GEF, an epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) tyrosine-kinase inhibitor, suppresses aberrant EGFR phosphorylation and downstream proliferative signaling. In contrast, AZT, a DNA-demethylating agent, expresses tumor-suppressor genes that subsequently induce cell death. Drug-release studies revealed sustained release of both agents, illustrating prolonged exposure and improved intracellular delivery. Additionally, the formulation exhibited excellent hemocompatibility, inducing <5% hemolysis at concentrations lower than 5 μg/mL, indicating suitability for systemic administration ( ).
The advantage of NLCs in NSCLC therapy was also demonstrated by the co-delivery of erlotinib (ERL) and resveratrol (RES). ERL-RES-NLCs successfully reduced A549 cell viability to 12.63% after 48 h and increased apoptosis by up to 85.5%, with a 3.3- to 4.1-fold elevation in ROS levels, compared to single-loaded ERL- and RES-NLCs. In addition, cell cycle analysis revealed prominent G2/M-phase arrest. Notably, this was correlated with the upregulation of proteins, such as p53, which is responsible for the regulation of DNA damage-dependent apoptosis, as well as pro-apoptotic caspases, such as caspase-3 and caspase-9. ERL-RES-NLCs also reduced the expression of ERK1/2, which plays critical roles in cell proliferation, and pro-survival proteins such as survivin and BCL-2, further highlighting the role of single-carrier co-loaded NLCs in multiple apoptosis mechanisms ( ).

5.1.5.

## Skin cancer
Skin cancer, which includes melanoma and non-melanoma variants, is largely driven by ultraviolet (UV)-induced oxidative and DNA damage ( ). Despite progress in surgery and immunotherapy, topical chemotherapy remains vital, particularly for non-melanoma skin cancers. NLCs have emerged as feasible carriers for multidrug cargoes to improve dermal drug permeation, sustain release, and enable synergistic multi-drug combinations while minimizing systemic toxicity.
In a recent study, de Carvalho et al. formulated NLCs containing PTX and cannabidiol (CBD), resulting in a low-crystalline matrix with a homogeneous particle size of approximately 200 nm, allowing the encapsulation of both hydrophobic agents. In B16-F10 murine melanoma cells, the dual-drug system reduced viability by >75% within 24 h, outperforming free drugs and single-drug NLCs. Combination index analysis revealed synergism (CI < 1) at 24 h, demonstrating enhanced combinatory cytotoxicity. However, after 48 h, this effect was no longer observed; instead, an antagonistic interaction occurred (CI > 1) at the tested concentrations. The authors argued that this time-dependent shift suggests that prolonged exposure modulates the PTX-CBD interaction differently when both drugs are co-encapsulated, warranting further investigation into how NLC-mediated release kinetics influence combination behavior over time ( ).
CBD has also been combined with 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) in single-carrier NLCs with an average diameter of 206.6 nm and a zeta potential of −34.5 mV, which were further formulated into a gel (FU-CBD-NLCs-gel). In vitro, the NLC-based gel exhibited stronger antiproliferative effects in non-melanoma A431 cells, as indicated by a higher G0/G1 cell cycle arrest (81.8%) compared to the conventional gel (75.7%). Additionally, DAPI staining indicated that the cells treated with NLCs exhibited higher levels of shrinkage and apoptosis than those treated with non-NLCs. Ex vivo Franz cell assays showed that the formulated NLC gel achieved higher cumulative permeation and greater deposition in both the epidermis and dermis. A dermatokinetic study further confirmed enhanced skin retention, with the epidermal Cmax and AUC values being substantially higher for the 5-FU-CBD-NLCs ( ). Similar results were obtained by Iqubal et al. and Imran et al., who formulated NLC-based gels, containing 5-FU-RES and quercetin (QUE)-RES, respectively ( ; ). These outcomes confirmed the ability of NLCs as co-delivery systems to improve cutaneous bioavailability and sustain the local presence of drugs during skin cancer therapy.

5.1.6.

## Other cancers
In addition to the aforementioned major cancers, NLC-based co-delivery systems have also been explored for a range of other malignancies, including colorectal, gastric, head and neck, oral, and prostate cancers, as well as leukemia and lymphoma. The rationale for these applications remains to enhance oral or local bioavailability, remodel tumor pharmacokinetics, and combine complementary mechanisms of action within a single nanoplatform.
Fukumori et al. developed NLCs for the oral co-delivery of PTX and tributyrin (TB), a butyrate prodrug with epigenetic and pro-apoptotic activity, designated for colorectal cancer treatment. In HCT-116 monolayer cells, dual-drug NLCs showed stronger cytotoxicity than PTX alone and markedly reduced clonogenic survival, indicating an impaired ability of surviving cells to form colonies. In three-dimensional spheroid models, the co-loaded system suppressed spheroid growth and integrity more efficiently than single-drug or free-drug treatments, reflecting improved penetration and sustained exposure. Mechanistic studies have shown altered actin cytoskeleton organization and downregulation of P-gp, consistent with the partial reversal of multidrug resistance and enhanced intracellular accumulation of PTX in colorectal tumor cells ( ).
Piperine–quercetin (P-Q) NLCs have been investigated in FaDu oral squamous carcinoma cells for head and neck and oral cancers. The study showed that P-Q-NLCs produced significantly greater cytotoxicity than free quercetin or free piperine, whereas flow cytometric analyses confirmed enhanced apoptosis associated with mitochondrial membrane depolarization. In addition, confocal imaging and in vivo biodistribution of coumarin-6-labeled NLCs demonstrated efficient distribution throughout the oral cavity, including the tongue, buccal mucosa, and salivary glands, after administration ( ). This highlights the suitability of NLCs for local delivery in head and neck cancers, where locoregional control is critical. In a related oral cancer study, Shete et al. formulated silymarin (SMR) and MET co-loaded NLCs within a thermosensitive, mucoadhesive in situ gelling system for application to KB oral cancer cells. The SMR-MET-NLC thermogel combined nanoscale carriers with prolonged residence on the mucosa, significantly lowered cell viability, and yielded an IC50 in the sub-micromolar range compared to the unencapsulated combined SMR-MET. This underscores the role of NLCs in coupling the repurposed drug MET with a flavonolignan antioxidant (SMR) in a platform tailored for sustained exposure to oral lesions ( ).
In another study, Fahmy designed an NLC formulation co-loading fluvastatin (FLV) with the antioxidants alpha-lipoic acid (ALA) and ellagic acid (EA) for prostate carcinoma treatment. The FLV-ALA-EA NLCs were approximately 85 nm in diameter, with high entrapment efficiency, an extended-release profile, and reduced IC50 of FLV. Cell cycle analysis showed a 1.44-fold increase in the pre-G1 phase compared to unencapsulated FLV-ALA-EA. In addition, caspase-3 expression was markedly elevated, indicating robust induction of apoptosis beyond that achieved by the free drug combination. These findings suggest that co-encapsulation of a statin with redox-active co-agents in NLCs can potentiate mitochondrial stress and programmed cell death in androgen-independent prostate cancer ( ).

5.2.

## Co-delivery of anticancer drugs and genetic materials
The integration of chemotherapeutics with genetic materials, such as DNA and siRNA, has gained increasing attention as a strategy to simultaneously modulate oncogenic signaling and amplify cytotoxic responses. Gene transfection, which involves the introduction of exogenous DNA or gene-silencing sequences into cells, enables the upregulation of tumor suppressors in the case of DNA delivery or the targeted downregulation of oncogenic transcripts when siRNA is used ( ; ; ). In a drug-gene co-delivery setting, carriers that can protect nucleic acids from degradation while accommodating small molecule drugs are of utmost importance. With their hybrid solid-liquid lipid matrices and adjustable surface chemistry, NLCs are well-suited to this purpose, thus enabling concurrent encapsulation and improving the stability of both therapeutic modalities. A concise overview of these formulations is presented in .
 5.2.1.

## DNA
DNA-based therapeutics have become an important addition to chemotherapy in cancer treatment, as plasmid DNA can modulate oncogenic signaling, restore tumor-suppressor genes, and enhance immune activation at the transcriptional level ( ; ). However, naked DNA suffers from rapid nuclease degradation, inefficient cellular uptake, and poor intracellular trafficking, necessitating a nanocarrier capable of protecting genetic cargo while promoting effective transfection ( ).
Chen et al. reported the use of a combination of TMZ and plasmid-enhanced green fluorescent protein (pEGFP) DNA in NLCs for the therapy of gliomatosis cerebri. The optimized TMZ-DNA-NLCs exhibited a particle size of approximately 179 nm and a positive zeta potential of approximately +23 mV, with 83% TMZ encapsulation efficiency and up to 91% gene-loading capacity, illustrating strong plasmid complexation. In vitro, dual-loaded NLCs suppressed U87 MG glioma cell growth more effectively than the free TMZ solution. In vivo, TMZ-DNA-NLCs, along with single-loaded DNA-NLCs, produced more than 40% gene transfection, outperforming the naked DNA and lipofectamine-DNA groups. Flow cytometry confirmed robust EGFP expression within tumor tissues, indicating that the lipid matrix effectively protected the plasmid, facilitated uptake, and enabled functional gene expression, whereas the co-delivered TMZ contributed additional cytotoxicity ( ).
In a separate study, bombesin-functionalized NLCs co-loaded with PTX and pEGFP DNA have been evaluated in lung cancer models. The non-functionalized PTX-DNA-NLCs demonstrated moderate gene expression levels, reaching approximately 30% in vitro and in vivo within 72 h, and consistently outperformed the free DNA solution. However, these transfection efficiencies remained lower than those of their functionalized NLC ( ). A similar pattern was observed in transferrin-functionalized DOX-DNA co-delivery system, where non-functionalized DOX-DNA-NLCs generated increasing proportions of DNA-positive cells over time, surpassing the naked DNA group yet remaining below the performance of the functionalized NLCs ( ). Both studies showed that even without targeting ligands, the incorporation of chemotherapeutics and DNA within NLCs can achieve meaningful gene transfection and enhanced intracellular plasmid delivery. The role of functionalization will be further discussed at later section.

5.2.2.

## siRNA
In cancer studies, siRNA is increasingly employed as a highly specific gene-silencing modality that can suppress oncogenic pathways by promoting the sequence-directed degradation of complementary mRNA ( ; ). It can also strongly potentiate the effects of cytotoxic drugs in a co-delivery system. Nonetheless, similar to DNA, siRNA performance in pure form is limited by its inherent characteristics, and may be improved using nano-based delivery systems.
Şenel et al. designed DOTAP-enriched NLCs containing DTX, which was electrostatically combined with Eph-family siRNAs (EphA1, EphA2, and EphB3). The optimized NLCs were approximately 165 nm in diameter, with a positive zeta potential of +38 mV and > 92% drug entrapment efficiency. The siRNA successfully bound to the NLC surface and produced a transfection index ranging from 65% to 79% in A549, Beas-2B, MCF-7, and MCF-10A cells. Furthermore, DTX-siRNA NLCs showed cytotoxicity even at DTX doses lower than those of the commercial product Taxotere, subsequently resulting in a greater time-dependent reduction in cell viability. This reflects the contribution of siRNA in Eph-receptor silencing, which concomitantly enhances the delivery performance of DTX ( ).
In another study, multifunctional NLCs were developed to co-deliver either PTX or gefitinib (GEF) together with EGFR-targeting siRNA to NSCLC cells. The system integrated complementary therapeutic approaches, including (i) functionalization using luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone (LHRH) peptide to enhance receptor-mediated endocytosis, (ii) delivery of small drug molecules that suppress EGF tyrosine kinase signaling pathway by GEF, (iii) triggering apoptosis by PTX, and (iv) delivery of siRNA that prevents de novo EGFR synthesis by inducing mRNA degradation. Notably, NLCs were able to reduce EGFR expression in both GEF-resistant PC9-GR and EGFR-inhibitor-resistant H-1975 cells ( ).
A complementary strategy was also demonstrated by Kim et al. in the development of chitosan-coated NLCs for the co-delivery of PTX and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) siRNA, denoted as P-NLC-Chi-siRNA. P-NLC-Chi measured around 124 nm, which swelled up to 182 nm after complexation with siRNA. The co-loaded NLCs maintained a positive surface charge and preserved siRNA integrity for at least 24 h in the serum. Drug-resistant MCF-7/ADR cells exhibited significantly elevated MDR1 (P-gp) mRNA expression compared to MCF-7 cells, confirming a multidrug-resistant phenotype ( A). Treatment with free PTX further upregulated MDR1 expression, whereas P-NLC-Chi and P-NLC-Chi-siRNA markedly suppressed MDR1 expression, indicating the attenuation of P-gp-mediated efflux ( B). Consistently, rhodamine-123 accumulation assays showed significantly enhanced intracellular fluorescence following NLC treatment relative to free PTX, confirming the functional inhibition of P-gp activity and improved intracellular retention ( C). In parallel, PD-L1 mRNA expression was significantly downregulated in both MCF-7 and MCF-7/ADR cells following treatment with P-NLC-Chi-siRNA, whereas treatment with free siRNA or PTX alone produced minimal gene-silencing effects, highlighting the necessity of carrier-mediated siRNA delivery ( D-E). In vivo, P-NLC-Chi-siRNA simultaneously produced superior tumor-growth suppression and activated both CD4+ helper and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells within tumor tissue, indicating that the formulation synergistically combines chemotherapy-induced killing with immune reactivation. Fluorescence imaging further demonstrated prolonged systemic circulation and enhanced tumor accumulation of NLCs labeled with 1,1'-dioctadecyl-3,3,3',3'-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide (DiR) compared to free dye, where sustained tumor localization observed up to 24 h post-administration ( F). Ex vivo organ imaging confirmed preferential accumulation of NLCs in tumor tissue alongside reduced nonspecific distribution to major organs ( G), which was quantitatively supported by fluorescence intensity analysis ( H). These findings collectively demonstrate the advantages of using NLCs in to achieve synergistic chemoimmunotherapeutic efficacy ( ).
 

5.3.

## Co-delivery of anticancer drugs and PDT/PTT/MHT agents
Photodynamic therapy (PDT), photothermal therapy (PTT), and magnetic hyperthermia therapy (MHT) have been explored as alternative modalities for cancer treatment, each relying on distinct energy-activated mechanisms to induce tumor cell death. Co-loading chemotherapeutics with PDT/PTT/MHT agents enables multimodal synergy, where oxidative stress, thermal injury, or magnetic heating amplifies drug-induced cytotoxicity and may aid in bypassing resistance pathways ( ; ; ). The use of NLCs as co-delivery systems is beneficial primarily because of their capacity to protect against systemic adverse effects commonly associated with these agents ( ). The following subsections discuss these systems according to the agent type, with representative settings and outcomes presented in .
 5.3.1.

## PDT agents
Photodynamic therapy relies on the activation of photosensitizers by an external light source to generate cytotoxic ROS. Upon excitation, photosensitizers undergo an electron transition from a ground singlet state (S0) to a very short-lived excited singlet state (S1), which is followed by a shift toward a more stable excited triplet state (T1) via intersystem crossing. Subsequently, the excited triplet photosensitizer interacts with the surrounding molecular oxygen through either an electron or proton transfer (Type I mechanism), generating ROS, such as superoxide and hydroxyl radicals, or through direct energy transfer (Type II mechanism) to produce singlet oxygen ( , right). These reactive species induce oxidative damage to cellular components, ultimately leading to cell death via apoptosis, necrosis, or immunogenic pathways ( ; ). As PDT efficacy depends on photosensitizer localization, oxygen availability, and light penetration, nanoparticulate systems, such as NLCs, offer distinct advantages by improving photosensitizer solubility, enabling tumor-specific delivery, and synchronizing the release of PDT agents with chemotherapeutics.
 Indocyanine green (ICG) is a clinically approved near-infrared (NIR) fluorescent dye that has long been used to assess cardiac output, liver function, and tissue perfusion ( ). When irradiated with NIR light, ICG can also act as a photosensitizer that generates singlet oxygen and other ROS, making it suitable for PDT ( ). However, its clinical use is hindered by rapid degradation, aggregation, and short circulation times. Ding et al. addressed these limitations by co-encapsulating ICG with PTX in surface-modified NLCs for hepatocellular carcinoma therapy. The NLCs markedly improved ICG photostability compared to free ICG, with fluorescence intensity remaining above 70% after prolonged xenon-lamp irradiation, whereas free ICG dropped to approximately 20%. Under NIR exposure, PTX and ICG release was higher (approximately 67% and 83% at 24 h, respectively) in NLCs than in non-irradiated NLCs (15% and 29%, respectively). 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) assays confirmed efficient singlet-oxygen generation, as evidenced by a time- and concentration-dependent decrease in DPBF absorbance. Intracellular staining using 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) in HepG2 cells showed much stronger green fluorescence for NLCs plus laser than for free ICG, indicating higher ROS production ( A-B). Furthermore, the co-loaded NLCs demonstrated significantly lower cell viability and pronounced damage in HepG2 spheroids compared to single-loaded NLCs and the marketed drug, Taxol ( C-D). Correspondingly, in vivo study in HepG2-bearing mice revealed that the injected NLC formulation exhibited up to 28.48-fold higher tumor accumulation compared to free ICG after 8 h treatment ( ).
 In a similar study, ICG served as a PDT agent co-loaded into TMTP1 peptide-modified NLCs with gambogic acid (GA), a hydrophobic xanthone capable of triggering caspase-3 and gasdermin E (GSDME)-dependent pyroptosis. Photostability assays under 808 nm laser irradiation showed that free ICG rapidly photobleached, whereas the NLCs maintained their absorption and photothermal conversion over multiple on/off cycles, confirming that the lipid matrix protected ICG from degradation. For PDT, a low-intensity 808 nm laser at 0.5 W/cm2 for 5 min was used, and temperature monitoring confirmed that the maximum temperature in cell cultures remained around 30 °C, indicating a PDT-dominated rather than a PTT-dominated effect. In HeLa and TC-1 cervical cancer cells, the irradiated combined drug NLCs generated the highest intracellular ROS levels, subsequently inducing pyroptotic events, such as cell membrane swelling. Correspondingly, pyroptosis was further highlighted by a higher release of lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), lower ATP levels, and a high proportion of Annexin V-FITC+/PI+ cells. Furthermore, western blotting analysis revealed that NLCs offered robust caspase-3 activation and GSDME cleavage. These effects were markedly attenuated by the ROS scavenger N-acetylcysteine, confirming ROS-dependent caspase-3/GSDME-mediated pyroptosis. In vivo, the study showed that irradiated NLCs yielded the strongest tumor growth inhibition in TC-1 xenografts and enhanced antitumor immunity by promoting dendritic cell maturation and CD8+ T-cell infiltration ( ).
Chlorin e6 (Ce6) is another photosensitizer commonly used as a PDT agent. Ce6 efficiently generates singlet oxygen upon red-light irradiation but suffers from hydrophobicity and aggregation in aqueous media. Zhang et al., developed folic-acid (FA) modified NLCs co-loading PTX and Ce6, which provided improved colloidal stability and enhanced delivery of both cargos. In vitro PDT experiments employed a 660 nm red laser at 1 W/cm2 for 5 min, producing a marked increase in intracellular ROS and significantly higher cytotoxicity in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells following irradiation of PTX-Ce6-NLCs compared to non-irradiated controls and other formulations. Notably, Ce6 exhibited distinct intracellular behavior in this nanocarrier system. Confocal microscopy showed that free Ce6 rapidly diffused into cells but was largely localized within lysosomes, whereas Ce6 delivered via NLCs accumulated more intensely and displayed a broader intracellular distribution. After 24 h, Ce6 encapsulated in NLCs dissociated from the carrier and escaped from the endosome, ensuring that the released Ce6 remained photoactive. Intracellular ROS detection assays further confirmed that after laser exposure, a strong fluorescence of DCF (an oxidized form of DCFH-DA) was observed, particularly in the PTX-Ce6-NLC group, indicating effective ROS generation and validating that Ce6 retained its PDT capability. In vivo, higher tumor accumulation was demonstrated in the NLC-treated group, which was further supported by ex vivo imaging results. The enhanced biodistribution translated to a superior tumor inhibition, particularly in the groups receiving laser irradiation ( ). These findings highlight that the NLC matrix not only improves Ce6 solubility and delivery but also facilitates its endosomal escape and intracellular activation, thereby maximizing PDT efficacy when combined with PTX.

5.3.2.

## PTT agents
Photothermal therapy employs agents that convert light energy into heat, thereby driving localized thermal injury in tumor tissues. Unlike PDT, which relies primarily on photosensitizer-mediated generation of ROS, PTT induces cell death through controlled hyperthermia, typically achieving temperatures above 42 °C ( ). Photothermal conversion occurs when PTT agents absorb light and undergo electronic excitation from the ground state to an excited singlet state, followed by rapid relaxation back to the ground state, predominantly through non-radiative vibrational dissipation rather than radiative emission ( , left). This process converts absorbed photon energy into heat, leading to a localized temperature rise that disrupts cellular membranes, denatures proteins, and induces irreversible thermal damage ( ; ). The use of nano-based materials, either as agents themselves or as carriers, may improve photoconversion efficiency, prolong systemic circulation, and facilitate tumor-specific accumulation, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of PTT. Lipid-based nanocarriers such as NLCs build upon these advantages by offering biocompatibility, high loading capacity for PTT agents, and protection to thermolabile cargos ( ). Accordingly, co-loaded NLCs may serve as flexible delivery platforms that can encapsulate diverse PTT agents and chemotherapeutics within a single platform, allowing multimodal tumor ablation and improved therapeutic precision.
Okuyucu et al. designed a multifunctional NLC system co-loading DOX-oleic acid (DOX-OA), the chemosensitizer verapamil (VERA), and gold nanorods (AuNRs) to overcome multidrug-resistant breast cancer. Upon NIR irradiation (808 nm at 2.3 W for 5 min), the AuNR-integrated NLCs produced rapid temperature elevation, reaching up to 43.4 °C, which markedly enhanced drug release and cytotoxicity against MDA-MB-231R cells compared to the non-irradiated groups. NLCs containing DOX-OA + VERA+AuNRs also decreased cell viability more effectively than DOX-VERA-NLCs, reflecting the photothermal contribution of AuNRs in addition to P-gp inhibition of VERA ( ). Another study by the same group further explored the differing performances of AuNPs with different geometries, termed AuNSs (spheres), AuNRs (rods), and AuNCs (cubes). Although all geometries produced some inherent cytotoxicity even without irradiation, AuNR-loaded NLCs achieved the most efficient photothermal conversion owing to their longitudinal plasmon resonance in the NIR region. Moreover, under 808 nm irradiation, AuNR-NLCs generated higher temperature increases than with AuNS- or AuNC-NLCs, which translates into superior hyperthermia-induced cell killing in breast cancer cells, underscoring how particle shape critically determines PTT potency within lipid nanocarriers ( ).
A similar approach also reported in a study that incorporated either AuNRs or AuNSs with camptothecin (CPT) to treat ovarian cancer. Correspondingly, AuNR-containing NLCs exhibited the strongest photothermal effect, which was substantially higher than AuNS-NLCs at comparable gold concentrations. This intense localized heating synergized with topoisomerase-I inhibition ability of CPT leads to pronounced cell growth suppression, where the irradiated NLCs containing CPT + AuNRs outperformed the other groups. Moreover, multi-loaded NLCs evidently increased the expression of caspase-3 and caspase-9, further confirming the advantage of the chemo-PTT combination to trigger mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis ( ).
In addition to gold nanoparticles, NIR organic dyes, such as IR780, have also been employed as PTT agents. IR780 is an NIR-absorbing dye often used in both PDT and PTT settings and has a strong optical absorbance in the 700-900 nm range and high fluorescence yield; however, its lipophilicity limits systemic administration ( ). To overcome this limitation, Li et al. developed NLCs that encapsulated IR780 in the lipid core and coated the particle surface with AMD3100, a CXCR4 antagonist, for simultaneous anti-metastatic activity and photothermal tumor ablation. Under 808 nm irradiation at an intensity of 1 W/cm2, IR780-AMD-NLCs rapidly elevated the tumor temperature from 33.5 to 49.3 °C, whereas free IR780 increased the temperature only to 43.2 °C. In 4 T1-luc breast cancer cells and BALB/c mice models, the photothermal response and apoptosis were strongly amplified upon irradiation, reducing cell viability to 20% and yielding complete tumor regression in vivo. Importantly, AMD3100 surface functionalization conferred strong tumor tropism, leading to significantly enhanced IR780 accumulation in tumors relative to non-targeted NLCs and free IR780, while simultaneously blocking CXCR4-mediated invasion and lung metastasis. Consequently, the formulation demonstrated a dual-action therapeutic profile: CXCR4 inhibition for anti-metastatic effects and NIR-triggered photothermal ablation for primary tumor eradication ( ).

5.3.3.

## MHT agents
Magnetic hyperthermia therapy (MHT) involves the use of magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) as localized heat transducers, with the final purpose being similar to that of PTT. When exposed to an external alternating magnetic field (AMF), MNPs convert the input electromagnetic energy into heat, which is primarily driven by Néel or Brownian relaxation ( ). Néel relaxation arises from the reorientation of the magnetic moment within the nanoparticle core across an anisotropic energy barrier, whereas Brownian relaxation originates from the physical rotation of the nanoparticle in response to the applied magnetic torque ( ; ). In both cases, the delayed magnetization response relative to the oscillating field leads to magnetic losses that are dissipated as thermal energy into the surrounding microenvironment. The goal of MHT is to induce temperatures in the range of 42-46 °C (mild hyperthermia) for apoptosis, or above 46 °C (thermoablation) for cell necrosis and complete tumor regression ( ). Its therapeutic outcome is mainly influenced by nanomaterial properties (size, composition, coating), AMF parameters (power, frequency), and biological targets (cell thermal susceptibility, tumor size) ( ).
 A co-loading strategy using NLCs can enhance MHT by creating a system that addresses the major limitations of individual treatments, thus facilitating multimodal therapy in which the localized heat generated by the MNPs under an AMF enhances the therapeutic effect of the co-loaded drug by increasing tumor cell sensitivity and vascular permeability. For instance, NLCs co-loaded with superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs) and methotrexate (MTX) exposed to 200 G AMF at 869 kHz for 20 min resulted in a higher percentage of apoptotic MDA-MB-231 cells than SPION-NLCs alone under similar conditions. This can be achieved despite the bulk temperature rising only to approximately 32 °C, which is well below the classical macroscopic hyperthermia at ≥42 °C. Confinement within the solid matrix of NLCs can hinder Brownian relaxation, which is a major heat generation mechanism for magnetic nanoparticles in fluid suspensions, consequently resulting in a limited macroscopic hyperthermia effect. However, the resulting localized heating at the nanoscale is sufficient to promote cell death via apoptosis ( ). In a separate study, NLCs containing MNPs and DTX also reportedly provided better safety toward normal human lung fibroblast cells (MRC5) compared to commercial DTX, underlining another benefit of NLCs as a potentially safer platform in chemo-magnetic-based cancer therapy ( ).

5.4.

## Co-delivery with functionalized NLCs
Although the passive targeting effect underpins many NLC formulations, surface functionalization has been increasingly investigated as a potential strategy to further enhance tumor selectivity, cellular internalization, and therapeutic precision. Following systemic administration, NLCs may passively accumulate in tumor tissues via EPR effect, while functionalized NLCs can further improve tumor localization through active targeting via ligand-receptor interactions on cancer cells. Subsequent cellular uptake occurs through receptor-mediated endocytosis, followed by intracellular trafficking that leads to endosomal escape ( ). After escaping from endosomal compartments, tumor-associated stimuli, such as acidic pH, enzymatic activity, or redox conditions, may trigger controlled drug release ( ).
 Building on these principles, NLC surfaces can be engineered with various targeting ligands, allowing for co-delivery systems to actively exploit receptor-mediated recognition and tumor-specific molecular signatures ( ). This approach is particularly advantageous for combination therapies, as it improves the synchronized delivery of multiple cargos to the same target cells. summarizes representative ligand-functionalized NLC and their roles in enhancing co-delivery performance.
 5.4.1.

## Antibodies
Functionalizing the surface of NLCs with antibodies can exploit the high specificity of antigen–antibody recognition to actively target cancer cells that overexpress defined surface receptors. Conatumumab, a synthetic antibody belonging to the TNF-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) family, binds to the extracellular domain of death receptor 5 (DR5) which plays a key role in apoptosis. Liu and co-workers explored the performance of conatumumab-decorated NLCs to co-deliver an irinotecan prodrug and quercetin in a colorectal cancer therapy. Antibody conjugation significantly enhanced cellular uptake and cytotoxicity compared with non-decorated NLCs with the same cargos. The functionalized dual-loaded system achieved stronger growth inhibition and apoptosis induction, which was attributed to improved receptor-mediated internalization and simultaneous exposure of cancer cells to DNA-damaging chemotherapy and flavonoid-mediated chemosensitization. Furthermore, in vivo results indicated that conatumumab-functionalized NLCs showed superior tumor growth suppression and higher drug accumulation at the tumor site, while reducing off-target distribution ( ).
A similar targeting concept was applied in lung cancer using cetuximab-functionalized NLCs co-loaded with PTX and 5-demethylnobiletin. Cetuximab targets EGFR, which is frequently overexpressed in non-small cell lung cancer. Antibody modification increased cellular uptake in A549 and PTX-resistant A549/PTX cells while maintaining minimal uptake in normal lung fibroblasts, demonstrating tumor-selective internalization. The functionalized co-delivery system exhibited synergistic cytotoxicity (with a CI value below 1) and produced the most pronounced tumor growth inhibition in vivo compared to non-functionalized NLCs and free drugs. Importantly, cetuximab-functionalized NLCs achieved higher tumor drug accumulation and reduced cardiac and renal exposure, underlining how antibody conjugation enhances both therapeutic efficacy and safety in combination chemotherapy ( ).

5.4.2.

## Folic acid
The application of folic acid (FA) as a functionalizing material arises from the overexpression of folate receptors (FRs) in a wide range of epithelial malignancies, whereas most normal tissues show limited FR expression. When conjugated to NLCs, FA enables receptor-mediated endocytosis, which enhances the intracellular accumulation of co-loaded therapeutics and improves the pharmacological synergy of combination regimens. Raikwar et al. developed FA-conjugated NLCs co-loading PTX and DOX to overcome multidrug resistance in breast cancer. The functionalized NLCs showed markedly enhanced cytotoxicity in MCF-7/ADR cells, achieving a GI50 value of 1.04 μg/mL, which is approximately three-fold lower than its non-functionalized counterpart ( ).
A similar strategy was conducted in an NSCLC study by Rawal et al., where FA-decorated NLCs co-delivered DTX and CUR (FA-DTCR-NLCs) formulated at a predefined synergistic molar ratio. The intracellular uptake of FA-DTCR-NLCs in NCI-H460 cells was significantly enhanced through receptor-mediated endocytosis, resulting in superior cytotoxicity compared with non-functionalized NLCs and free-drug formulations. This study further explored the modulation of multiple pathways associated with tumor growth and progression. FA-DTCR-NLC treatment markedly increased p53 expression, a key tumor-suppressor protein involved in the DNA damage response, cell-cycle regulation, and apoptosis induction, thereby restoring apoptotic signaling that is often impaired in cancer cells. In parallel, a significant reduction in Ki-67 expression was observed, indicating strong suppression of proliferative activity across the active phases of the cell cycle. The formulation also exerted pronounced anti-angiogenic effects, as evidenced by the downregulation of CD34, a marker associated with tumor neovascularization and poor prognosis. Additionally, FA-DTCR-NLCs increased E-cadherin expression, suggesting reinforcement of epithelial cell-cell adhesion and attenuation of invasive and metastatic potential. Collectively, these results demonstrate that FA-mediated targeting not only enhances the intracellular delivery of DTX and CUR but also enables coordinated modulation of proliferation, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and invasion pathways, resulting in robust antitumor efficacy in NSCLC ( ).
Beyond conventional FA functionalization, Ma et al. introduced surface modification of NLCs using pH-responsive acid-cleavable FA (cFA), co-functionalized with a human-derived cell-penetrating peptide (dNP2) to co-deliver gambogic acid (GA) and PTX. Compared to NLCs functionalized with non-cleavable FA or single ligand groups, cFA/dNP2-GA/PTX-NLCs demonstrated enhanced intracellular delivery under acidic tumor conditions, resulting in increased cytotoxicity and cellular uptake. This illustrates how FA targeting can be synergistically combined with additional functional elements to further optimize co-delivery performance in complex tumor microenvironments ( ).

5.4.3.

## Hyaluronic acid
Hyaluronic acid (HA) is a naturally occurring, biodegradable polysaccharide that is widely employed as a targeting ligand for nanocarriers owing to its high affinity for the cluster of differentiation-44 (CD44) receptor, which is overexpressed in numerous cancer cells. Functionalization of NLCs with HA serves a purpose similar to that of FA, namely to promote receptor-mediated endocytosis, enhance tumor accumulation, and improve intracellular retention of the delivered drugs. Several studies have demonstrated that HA-decorated NLCs consistently enhance the co-delivery of chemotherapeutic agents across different tumor types by exploiting CD44-mediated targeting. In a breast cancer study, HA-decorated NLCs were shown to significantly enhance the co-delivery of baicalein (BCL) and DOX, particularly in drug-resistant MCF-7/ADR cells. Compared to non-decorated NLCs and free drug combinations, HA-functionalized systems achieved the highest cytotoxicity and strongest synergistic effects in vitro, along with superior tumor growth inhibition in vivo ( ).
HA functionalization has also been successfully integrated with prodrug strategies to enhance co-delivery performance. In gastric cancer models, HA-coated NLCs co-encapsulating a 5-FU-stearic acid prodrug and CDDP demonstrated markedly enhanced cytotoxicity in BGC823 cells and the strongest tumor growth inhibition in vivo compared to uncoated NLCs and other groups. This lipid prodrug approach improved the encapsulation and sustained release of 5-FU, whereas HA-mediated targeting increased tumor cell uptake, enabling synergistic antitumor activity at reduced systemic toxicity ( ). A similar concept was applied in a pancreatic cancer study, where HA-coated NLCs co-delivering gemcitabine and BCL prodrugs showed efficient uptake by CD44-overexpressing AsPC-1 cells and significant tumor suppression in murine models ( ).
In addition to HA-only functionalization, more complex surface-engineered NLCs incorporating HA in combination with additional targeting ligands have been developed to achieve hierarchical and site-specific delivery. Basso et al. reported a multifunctional NLC platform for glioblastoma therapy in which HA served as a biocompatible backbone for the conjugation of multiple complementary targeting ligands. HA enabled CD44-mediated recognition and provided a versatile scaffold for further surface engineering, whereas FA was incorporated to exploit the overexpression of FRs in glioblastoma cells. In parallel, the cRGDfK peptide was introduced to target αvβ3 and αvβ5 integrins, which are also overexpressed in both glioblastoma cells and tumor-associated vasculature. The system was further refined by the inclusion of the H7K(R2)2 peptide, a pH-responsive moiety designed to take advantage of the acidic glioblastoma microenvironment. Their investigations revealed that the cellular internalization of these peptide-lipid nanoconstructs was governed by multiple endocytic pathways. Uptake was significantly inhibited by cytochalasin B, colchicine, and sucrose, indicating the involvement of macropinocytosis, clathrin- or caveolin-independent pathways, and clathrin-mediated endocytosis. This multimodal mechanism underscores the benefit of combining HA with additional ligands and peptides, as it enables nanocarriers to engage diverse internalization routes rather than relying on a single receptor or pathway. In summary, this study demonstrates the multirole of HA, not only as a targeting ligand, but also as a modular platform that supports hierarchical functionalization, leading to enhanced uptake, improved tumor specificity, and superior therapeutic delivery in highly heterogeneous tumors such as glioblastoma ( ).

5.4.4.

## Peptides
Peptide ligands are another widely employed functionalization material in NLCs particularly owing to their high binding specificity and low immunogenicity. When used in co-delivery systems, peptides may promote receptor-mediated endocytosis and enhance the intracellular accumulation of multiple therapeutic cargoes. Arginyl-glycyl-aspartic acid (RGD) peptides have been extensively investigated because of their strong affinity for αvβ3 integrins, which are overexpressed in tumor cells and angiogenic endothelium. RGD-functionalized NLCs co-loaded with DOX and sildenafil citrate (SC) demonstrated significantly enhanced anticancer efficacy in lung cancer models. Compared with DOX–SC solutions and non-RGD NLCs, RGD-modified systems increased cytotoxicity by approximately 19% and 14%, respectively; enhanced intracellular DOX uptake; and elevated apoptotic cell populations to >44% in A549 cells. SC acted as a chemosensitizer by reversing MDR through downregulation of Nrf2 and ABCC1, where the inclusion of RGD enhanced the targeting ability of the NLCs, ensuring efficient integrin-mediated internalization ( ).
Variants within the RGD family have also been engineered to broaden receptor engagement and tumor penetration. E-[c(RGDfK)2], a cyclic RGD dimer with a high affinity for αvβ3 integrins, was co-employed with FA to functionalize NLCs to deliver DOX-HCl and salvianolic acid A for multi-therapeutic purposes. Cellular uptake studies revealed pronounced receptor-dependent internalization patterns in lung and breast cancer cell lines. In A549 lung cancer cells, which express high levels of αvβ3 integrins but relatively low levels of folate receptors, uptake was significantly higher for E-[c(RGDfK)₂]-modified NLCs than for FA-only systems. Conversely, in MCF-7 breast cancer cells, where folate receptors are abundant but αvβ3 expression is minimal, FA-modified NLCs showed greater internalization. In 4 T1 and MDA-MB-231 cells, which co-express both receptors, dual-modified NLCs achieved the highest uptake among all formulations. These receptor-specific uptake profiles translated into superior in vivo performance, with tumor inhibition rates approaching 90.7%, along with reduced renal toxicity and significant suppression of Ki-67 expression ( ).
Arginyl-glycyl-glutamic acid (RGE) and its modification, R9dRG, have also been developed for similar purposes as the RGD peptide. In addition to αvβ3 integrin targeting ability, RGE and R9dRG can also bind to the neuropilin-1 (NRP-1) receptor, thereby enabling dual-receptor recognition. In a 4 T1 model, which overexpresses both αvβ3 integrin and NRP-1, RGE- or R9dRG-modified NLCs co-delivering PTX and gambogic acid achieved markedly improved cellular uptake, with cell inhibition rates approaching 90%, and tumor growth inhibition exceeding 60% in vivo compared to non-modified systems. These findings demonstrate that multi-receptor-recognizing peptides can substantially enhance therapeutic performance by addressing receptor heterogeneity within tumors ( ).
In addition to integrin-targeting peptides, homing and resistance-modulating peptides have been incorporated to address chemoresistant tumors. NLCs functionalized with RIPL, a peptide that selectively binds to Hpn-expressing cancer cells, and co-delivered DTX and tariquidar showed a 2.2-fold increase in cytotoxicity and a 1.9-fold increase in tumor inhibition compared to combined single-drug NLCs in resistant breast cancer models. This formulation induced pronounced G2/M phase arrest, with up to 56.2% of the cells arrested. This reflects the role of RIPL which selectively binds to Hpn, thus promoting effective mitotic blockade by DTX and enhancing intracellular co-delivery via P-gp inhibition by tariquidar ( ).
Another peptide-guided strategy was represented by the aforementioned bombesin-functionalized NLCs targeting the gastrin-releasing peptide receptor, which is particularly relevant to lung cancer. In the co-delivery systems encapsulating DOX and plasmid DNA, two different preparation methods were compared: pre-decorated bombesin NLCs, in which bombesin was conjugated prior to drug loading, and post-decorated bombesin NLCs, in which surface modification was performed after NLC formation. Notably, the post-decorated bombesin NLCs consistently outperformed the pre-decorated systems. The post-bombesin formulation achieved a significantly lower IC50 value (4.5 μM) than the pre-bombesin NLCs (8.1 μM) and increased cytotoxicity in NCI-H460 cells by approximately 2-fold relative to the pre-bombesin NLCs and 6-fold relative to free DOX. Furthermore, gene transfection in pre-bombesin NLCs was lower than that in post-bombesin NLCs, possibly because the former covers bombesin under the surface of the nanocarriers, thus lacking selectivity. This was supported by in vivo results, where post-bombesin NLCs produced tumor inhibition rates of up to 76%, substantially higher than those of pre-bombesin NLCs and non-functionalized groups. In summary, these findings underscore the importance of ligand presentation and fabrication sequences in peptide-functionalized NLCs ( ).

5.4.5.

## Proteins
Protein-based ligands offer distinct advantages for the functionalization of NLCs owing to their intrinsic biological recognition capabilities and established roles in receptor-mediated transport. Transferrin (Tf), a type of glycoprotein originating in an iron binding site, has been extensively employed as a tumor-targeting ligand due to the marked overexpression of Tf receptors on numerous cancer cells, reaching up to 100-fold higher levels than normal tissues ( ). In lung cancer therapy, Tf-decorated NLCs have been developed for the co-delivery of PTX and plasmid DNA. Tf was conjugated via PEG-phosphatidylethanolamine (PEG-PE) linkers of different molecular weights (5 and 10 kDa), resulting in formulations denoted as Tf5k-PTX-DNA-NLC and Tf10k-PTX-DNA-NLC. PEGylation plays a critical dual role by reducing nonspecific clearance while simultaneously acting as a flexible spacer to improve Tf accessibility to its receptor. The optimized Tf-modified NLCs achieved IC50 values in the range of 3.35–7.12 μM, exhibited higher cytotoxicity than free PTX, and increased tumor inhibition rates by approximately 51% relative to the controls. Notably, the gene transfection efficiency of Tf5k-PTX-DNA-NLCs was also higher than that of both Tf10k-PTX-DNA-NLCs and lipofectamine-based delivery systems. These findings highlight how Tf functionalization combined with PEG engineering can effectively enhance both chemotherapeutic efficacy and gene delivery performance in lung cancer ( ).
Lactoferrin (Lf), a member of the Tf family, represents another protein ligand with strong relevance for brain tumor such as glioblastoma. Lf receptors are expressed on glioblastoma cells, making Lf particularly attractive for facilitating receptor-mediated transcytosis across the blood-brain barrier (BBB). In a study by Zhang et al., Lf was used in combination with an RGD peptide to functionalize NLCs co-loaded with temozolomide and vincristine. This protein-peptide targeting strategy enabled the simultaneous engagement of Lf receptors on glioblastoma cells and αvβ3 integrins on tumor-associated endothelial cells. The co-functionalized NLCs exhibited strong synergistic effects, with a CI value below 1, cellular uptake exceeding 70%, and a reduction in cell viability to below 20% at 100 μg/mL. The in vivo tumor inhibition rates surpassed 70%, significantly outperforming single-ligand-modified and single-drug NLCs. In addition to the ability of RGD to enhance localization within the tumor vasculature, Lf-mediated targeting contributed to the growth suppression of U87MG cells via downregulation of cyclin D1 and D4. This demonstrates that dual-ligand-modified NLCs serve as an effective platform for co-loaded drugs to overcome BBB limitations, beneficial as a therapy for glioblastoma ( ).

5.4.6.

## Other ligands
In addition to the abovementioned classical targeting ligands, alternative functional moieties have been integrated into NLCs to exploit tumor-specific physicochemical and metabolic features. These approaches emphasize responsiveness to the tumor microenvironment or receptor-mediated uptake mechanisms distinct from conventional ligand-receptor pairs, offering additional flexibility in designing co-delivery systems. For instance, tumor tissues typically exhibit a pH of approximately 5–6, in contrast to the pH of 7.4 in normal tissues; therefore, tumor acidity becomes relevant when designing site-specific delivery systems. Cao et al. utilized a hydrazone that was incorporated into mPEG-DSPE to obtain pH-sensitive NLCs co-loaded with DOX and β-elemene (ELE) for lung cancer treatment. This system remains stable at physiological pH but undergoes cleavage under acidic conditions. The pH-responsive NLCs exhibited comparatively stronger synergistic effects than DOX-ELE injection, cellular uptake of more than 70%, and a reduction in cell viability to below 30% at 100 μg/mL against A549/ADR cells. In vivo, tumor inhibition rates reached up to 82.9%, significantly outperforming non-pH-responsive NLCs and other formulations, emphasizing the advantages of using an acid-sensitive material to improve drug performance within the tumor microenvironment ( ).
Metabolic targeting has also been used as an alternative ligand strategy. In NSCLC, N-acetyl-D-glucosamine (NAG) has been used as a glucose receptor-targeting ligand to functionalize polymeric NLCs to co-deliver gemcitabine and PTX. Prior to NLC fabrication, both drugs were covalently conjugated to poly(6-O-methacryloyl-d-galactopyranose) (PMAGP) using hydrolyzable ester linkers, enabling precise ratiometric control over dual-drug loading. The resulting NAG-modified NLCs exhibited pronounced synergistic cytotoxicity, with an IC50 of 0.64 μM and a CI value of 0.414, outperforming unmodified NLCs and free-drug systems. Moreover, acid-facilitated drug release was significantly higher at pH 6 than at pH 7.4, aligning with tumor-specific acidity. Flow cytometry and confocal microscopy further confirmed the enhanced cellular uptake and intracellular accumulation in A549 cells via glucose receptor-mediated endocytosis ( ).

6.

## Challenges and future directions
Despite substantial progress in the design and preclinical evaluation of NLC-based co-delivery systems for cancer therapy, their implementation in clinical practice remains limited. The gap between lab results and clinical implementation does not reflect lack of therapeutic potential, but rather a convergence of manufacturing, biological, regulatory, and translational challenges that become increasingly pronounced as formulation complexity increases ( ). In particular, co-loaded NLC systems introduce additional layers of variability related to multidrug incorporation, release coordination, and in vivo behavior, all of which must be addressed to ensure consistent performance and safety. The following sections outline the key challenges currently facing the field and discuss potential strategies and future directions for successful translation.
6.1.

## Manufacturing scale-up and reproducibility
At the laboratory scale, NLCs are commonly produced using batch-based techniques, in which tight manual control of the processing conditions is feasible. These approaches often exhibit high sensitivity to critical material attributes (CMAs), such as the concentration of drugs, lipids, or surfactants, as well as critical process parameters (CPPs), such as homogenization speed or time, temperature, or cooling rate. Even modest variations in these parameters can lead to substantial changes in critical quality attributes (CQAs), including particle size, PDI, drug loading, and release behavior ( ). These challenges are further amplified in co-loaded NLC systems. Unlike single-drug formulations, co-delivery platforms must maintain consistent drug ratios, spatial distribution of multiple cargos within the lipid matrix, and coordinated release profiles. Scaling-up using the traditional one-variable-at-a-time (OVAT) approach is considered inefficient for multicomponent systems because the complex interactions between formulation variables and process parameters are not well captured ( ).
To address these limitations, quality by design (QbD) principles have been increasingly applied during NLC manufacturing. Within the QbD framework, the relationships between CMAs and CPPs with CQAs are systematically identified through design of experiments (DoE). This multivariate approach enables the identification of design spaces in which CQAs remain within acceptable limits, thus minimizing batch-to-batch variability and enhancing reproducibility during scale-up ( ). Integrating QbD with certain production techniques, such as HME, can be advantageous to obtain consistent CQAs in both laboratory and larger-scale settings. As a continuous manufacturing approach, HME enables solvent-free production under well-controlled thermal and mechanical conditions ( ; ). The implementation of process analytical technology (PAT) further strengthens QbD-driven production by enabling the real-time monitoring and control of process variables during manufacturing, thus providing an overall robust development of NLCs ( ).

6.2.

## Safety and biodistribution
Although NLC matrices are generally considered non-toxic owing to the use of highly biocompatible lipid excipients, the same cannot be assumed for loaded cargo. Most anticancer agents exert their effects by interfering with fundamental cellular processes, such as DNA replication, mitosis, or signal transduction, which are not exclusive to cancer cells ( ). Consequently, off-target toxicity in healthy tissues remains a major clinical concern, particularly during prolonged treatment. Off-target concerns are also compounded by the intrinsic biodistribution profile of many nanoparticulate systems, where substantial fractions of the injected dose are sequestered by the reticuloendothelial/mononuclear phagocyte system (RES/MPS), limiting delivery to target tissues ( ). In co-loaded NLCs, translational complexity may further increases when inorganic cargos such as AuNPs or SPIONs are included, as their non-biodegradability can promote prolonged retention in the liver and spleen, raising concerns regarding chronic toxicity and long-term safety ( ; ).
As discussed in previous sections, the application of various ligands can improve the targeting capabilities of NLCs. However, in vivo performance of ligand-mediated targeting nanocarriers may be compromised by protein corona formation when entering the bloodstream, which subsequently alters the active targeting ability of the ligands into a passive one ( ; ). In this context, biomimetic strategies serve as complementary approaches, in which NLCs are functionalized using cell membrane-derived coatings or bioinspired components that exploit endogenous self-recognition signals to evade macrophage uptake. This reduces RES sequestration, thereby increasing the probability of tumor exposure ( ). An alternative and conceptually important future direction is to reduce off-target deposition by modulating host biology rather than further increasing nanocarrier surface complexity. Activation of interferon-λ (IFN-λ) signaling has been shown to tighten the endothelial and epithelial barriers in healthy tissues, which in turn limits nonspecific nanoparticle uptake and reduces accumulation in major clearance organs ( ). For co-loaded NLC systems, such host-modulation strategies may offer a means to broaden the therapeutic window by reducing off-target organ exposure while preserving effective tumor delivery, thus mitigating the potential safety risks associated with nanoparticle-based therapies.

6.3.

## Regulatory and translational considerations
The regulatory evaluation of nanomedicines remains inherently complex owing to their multifunctional nature and specific physicochemical properties, which can significantly influence pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and safety profiles. Regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA) currently adopt a risk-based, product-specific approach for drug products containing nanomaterials, emphasizing comprehensive characterization, manufacturing control, and justification of nano-enabled attributes rather than relying on rigid definitions ( ; ). However, the absence of harmonized regulations continues to pose challenges for formulators, particularly for complex co-delivery systems in which formulation and therapeutic functions are tightly integrated.
Currently, regulatory guidelines specific to NLCs are lacking. Nevertheless, several well-established guidelines for liposomal drug products may provide practical reference points. Both the FDA and EMA have issued detailed recommendations addressing the chemistry, manufacturing, controls, physicochemical characterization, stability, pharmacokinetics, and comparability of intravenous liposomal formulations ( ; ). Given the structural and compositional similarities between NLCs and liposomes, particularly their lipid-based structures, an analogous regulatory strategy can be considered appropriate for the early translational planning of NLC-based systems.
Clinical trials that explicitly investigate NLC-based formulations for cancer management are also non-existent; the only identified study is a non-cancer-related registered trial (trial identifier: NCT05912335). In contrast, liposome-based cancer therapies are well-represented in clinical development, with numerous trials evaluating both single-agent and combination regimens (NCT05086822 and NCT05086848). This disparity highlights a significant translational gap and underscores the importance of aligning NLC development with existing regulatory frameworks and clinically validated nanocarrier paradigms. Bridging this gap will require robust preclinical evidence as well as early regulatory engagement and trial design informed by the successful clinical trajectory of liposomal nanomedicines.

6.4.

## Integration of data-driven design with personalized medicine
Advancements in multidrug-based cancer treatment are increasingly driven by the integration of combinatorial chemistry and data-driven methodologies. Combinatorial chemistry involves the generation of vast arrays of structurally diverse chemical libraries through the repetitive linkage of various building blocks, a process that is traditionally labor-intensive ( ). By employing machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI), researchers can now move beyond trial-and-error, using "big data" to scan billions of bits of information and identify relevant patterns for the design of synergistic combination therapies ( ; ). This data-driven approach is also deeply intertwined with network pharmacology, which facilitates a paradigm shift from "one target-one drug" to "network-targeted" multicomponent therapeutics. Unlike conventional methods, network pharmacology systematically analyzes the complex interactomes involved in cancer and identifies how multiple drugs can concurrently perturb disease-associated networks to maximize their efficacy while minimizing off-target effects ( ; ). In the context of personalized medicine, these tools allow for the integration of multi-scale longitudinal data, such as genomics and clinical records, to develop individualized "biomarker signatures" ( ).
ML and AI also open new directions for the engineering of lipid-based nanoparticles, which often involves exhaustive screening to achieve drug-lipid compatibility. Platforms such as AGILE, which combines deep learning with combinatorial chemistry, have successfully performed high-throughput screening to evaluate over 10,000 candidate lipids, identifying optimal structures for nucleic acid delivery that would have been unfeasible to discover through manual synthesis ( ). From a formulation viewpoint, the integration of artificial neural networks (ANNs) with other frameworks, such as fuzzy logic or DoE, enables the sequential optimization of formulation variables. These hybrid approaches have been successfully applied to the design of polymer-functionalized nanoparticles and mannosylated NLCs, allowing precise control over the particle size and physicochemical properties that are essential for improved targeting performance ( ; ). Additionally, ML algorithms including Bayesian optimization and XGBoost are being applied to streamline the manufacturing of lipid nanoparticles, providing real-time quality control over particle size, polydispersity, and encapsulation efficiency ( ; ). Therefore, a holistic, multi-framework strategy enables the convergence of combinatorial chemistry, network pharmacology, and data-driven design, positioning NLC-based co-delivery systems as adaptable platforms for personalized and precision-guided cancer therapy.

7.

## Conclusion
NLCs are highly adaptable and biocompatible nanocarriers for the co-delivery of multiple therapeutic agents in cancer therapy. Their hybrid solid-liquid lipid architecture enables high loading capacity, improved colloidal stability, and programmable release profiles that are difficult to achieve with conventional carriers. NLC-based co-delivery systems can preserve synergistic drug ratios, enhance intracellular accumulation, overcome multidrug resistance, and enable spatiotemporal control of therapeutic action. These advantages have been widely demonstrated across diverse cancer types using combinations of chemotherapeutic agents, either with other chemotherapeutics, genetic materials, or energy-based modalities such as PTT, PDT, and MHT agents. Surface functionalization with targeting ligands further enhances tumor selectivity and intracellular delivery of co-loaded cargos.
Despite these advances, the clinical translation of NLCs remains constrained by formulation complexity, scale-up reproducibility, long-term safety, and regulatory standardization. Addressing these challenges will require rational excipient selection, robust optimization strategies, and early integration of regulatory-aligned development frameworks. Importantly, future research on NLC-based co-delivery should adopt data-driven and personalized medicine approaches. The integration of QbD, AI, and patient-specific biological data may enable the rational tailoring of lipid composition, drug combinations, and release kinetics to accommodate tumor heterogeneity and individual therapeutic responses. Collectively, such strategies are expected to accelerate the clinical translation of NLC-based co-delivery platforms and broaden their roles in the evolving landscape of oncology.
