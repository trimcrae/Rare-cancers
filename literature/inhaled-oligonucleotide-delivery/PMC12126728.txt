A diagram for mechanisms underlying Kyn regulation of fibroblast differentiation, proliferation and migration. On one hand, TGF-β blunts Ahr expression via TGFβR1/SMAD3/MBD2 signal. ## Introduction
Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is a fatal fibrotic lung disease without any options to halt disease progression. Feasible evidence suggests that aberrant metabolism of amino acids may play a role in the pathoetiology of PF. However, the exact impact of kynurenine (Kyn), a metabolite derived from tryptophan (Trp) on PF is yet to be addressed.

## Objectives
This study aims to elucidate the role of kynurenine in both the onset and advancement of PF.

## Methods
Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry was employed to assess Kyn levels in patients with idiopathic PF and PF associated with Sjögren’s syndrome. Additionally, a mouse model of PF induced by bleomycin was utilized to study the impact of Kyn administration. Furthermore, cell models treated with TGF-β1 were used to explore the mechanism by which Kyn inhibits fibroblast functions.

## Results
We demonstrated that high levels of Kyn are a clinical feature in both idiopathic PF patients and primary Sjögren syndrome associated PF patients. Further studies illustrated that Kyn served as a braking molecule to suppress fibroblast functionality, thereby protecting mice from bleomycin-induced lung fibrosis. The protective effects depend on AHR, in which Kyn induces AHR nuclear translocation, where it upregulates PTEN expression to blunt TGF-β mediated AKT/mTOR signaling in fibroblasts. However, in fibrotic microenviroment, the expression of AHR is repressed by methyl-CpG-binding domain 2 (MBD2), a reader interpreting the effect of DNA methylation, which results in a significantly reduced sensitivity of Kyn to fibroblasts. Therefore, exogenous administration of Kyn substantially reversed established PF.

## Conclusion
Our studies not only highlighted a critical role of Trp metabolism in PF pathogenesis, but also provided compelling evidence suggesting that Kyn could serve as a promising metabolite against PF.

## Introduction
Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) serves as a prototype of chronic and progressive interstitial lung disease (ILD) . PF could be developed from identifiable triggers such as infection (e.g. Corona Virus Disease 2019), silicosis and connective tissue diseases (CTD) , , while a proportion of patients are idiopathic PF (IPF) featured by the unknown disease etiologies . Unfortunately, there is no effective drug available thus far to halt the progression of PF. This contributes to its 5-year mortality rate being approximately 80 % following diagnosis . Therefore, there is a clear and immediate necessity to develop novel and safe strategies against PF.
Generally, PF is characterized by the formation of fibrotic foci consisted of myofibroblasts, fibroblasts and other types of cells, which secrete a myriad of extracellular matrix (ECM) into the alveolar architecture , . In the fibrotic microenvironment, fibroblasts are the main effector cells , . Specifically, sustained fibroblast differentiation, proliferation and migration triggered by a variety of fibrotic mediators contribute to the formation of fibrotic foci and subsequent lung architecture remodeling , . Therefore, repression of fibroblast activation has been noted to dramatically attenuate PF initiation and progression , .
More recently, studies have provided evidence supporting the critical role of altered amino acid metabolism in both the initiation and progression of PF , , which emerges as a promising direction for PF treatment. In particular, alterations in the metabolism of glycine, glutamine and arginine have been noted to drive the profibrotic processes , , . Therefore, to a certain extent, PF could be considered as a metabolic disease . Unlike the above mentioned three amino acids, tryptophan (Trp), an essential amino acid, and its metabolites have been acknowledged for their role in regulating a wide array of biological processes . More than 95 % of Trp is catalyzed by the rate-limiting enzyme tryptophan-2,3-dioxygenase (TDO), primarily in the liver, or by indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) in other organs except for liver, to kynurenine (Kyn) through the kynurenine pathway (KP). Previous studies suggested that Kyn is involved in the pathogenesis of tumor, chronic rhinosinusitis and obesity via its interaction with the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), a transcription factor responsible for mediating cellular responses to both xenobiotic compounds and endogenous ligands , , , . A recent study demonstrated feasible evidence that patients with silicosis, a type of PF caused by inhaled large amounts of crystalline silica dust, manifest increased serum Kyn . Nevertheless, the main origin of Kyn and its specific effects on PF have not been fully elucidated.
In this study, we demonstrated increased levels of Kyn in patients with pulmonary fibrosis (IPF and primary Sjögren syndrome) and in mice with bleomycin-induced fibrosis, and administering exogenous Kyn effectively reversed pulmonary fibrosis without causing adverse effects. The study also revealed that fibrotic conditions led to heightened expression of IDO1, resulting in elevated Kyn production. Kyn subsequently interacted with AHR, facilitating its movement into the cell nucleus and increasing PTEN expression, ultimately suppressing AKT/mTOR signaling in fibroblasts and slowing the progression of fibrosis. However, the suppression of AHR by MBD2 in the fibrotic microenvironment reduces fibroblast responsiveness to Kyn, disrupting the regulatory mechanism and worsening pulmonary fibrosis. These results underscore the importance of Kyn in mitigating pulmonary fibrosis and indicate its potential as a therapeutic target.

## Materials and methods

## Antibodies and reagents
Antibodies against IDO1, GAPDH, COLLAGEN 1, α-SMA, Ki-67, p-AKT (Ser473), AHR and PTEN were sourced from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA, USA), while antibodies against FIBRONECTIN and FSP1 were ordered from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA) and Merck & Co., Inc. (Kenilworth, NJ, USA), respectively. Antibodies against p-AKT (Thr308), p-mTOR (Ser2448) and mTOR were purchased from Cell Signalling (Danvers, MA, USA). Recombinant human or mouse TGF-β1 were derived from Proper Tech (Rocky Hill, NJ, USA). Phalloidin was obtained from Yeasen (Shanghai, China). BLM was purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). Pirfenidone was supplied by the Beijing Continent Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd (Beijing, China).

## Ethics statement
Human specimens were obtained from Tongji Hospital, with informed consent acquired from all individuals involved. Approval for the studies was granted by the Human Assurance Committee of Tongji Hospital (Approval no. TJ-IRB20210966). Experimental protocols involving animals were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji Hospital (Approval no. TJH-201901016).

## Human specimens
Sixty IPF patients, 25 pSS-PF patients, 41 pSS patients, and 136 healthy volunteers, while lung tissues from 9 non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients and 9 IPF patients were collected during surgery. IPF was diagnosed based on the consensus diagnostic criteria of the American Thoracic Society (ATS) and European Respiratory Society (ERS) . The diagnosis of pSS was based on fulfillment of the criteria established by the American-European Consensus Group (AECG) . pSS-PF diagnosis was identified with high-resolution computed tomography (including honeycombing, intraocular reticulation, traction bronchiectasis and bronchiolectasis). The exclusion criteria: Individuals with any of the following conditions: bronchial asthma, COPD, pneumothorax, pleural effusion. Those with active infections within the past 4 weeks. Individuals with a history of or current malignancy. Severe renal impairment: creatinine clearance < 30 ml/min/1.73 m2. Individuals with liver disease or severe liver impairment: ALT or AST > 2 times the upper limit of normal. Clinical data are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

## Animals
Ido1flox/flox mice were produced utilizing the CRISPR–Cas9 system by the Gempharmatech (Nanjing, China). Specifically, two loxP sequences were introduced into the introns surrounding exon 4 of Ido1, as depicted in Supplemental Fig. 5a. Conventional Ido1 knockout (Ido1-/-) mice and Ahrflox/flox mice were procured from the Jackson Laboratory. The Col1α2-CreERT2+ transgenic mice were provided from the Shanghai Model Organism Center. As previously reported , Col1α2-CreERT2+Ido1flox/flox mice (Ido1-CKO) or Col1α2-CreERT2+
Ahrflox/flox mice (Ahr-CKO) were produced by mating Col1α2-CreERT2+ mice with Ido1flox/flox mice or Ahrflox/flox mice for the targeted deletion of Ido1 or Ahr in fibroblasts by 5 consecutive days of tamoxifen induction, respectively. Their littermates, Col1α2-CreERT2-Ido1flox/flox (Ido1-C) or Col1α2-CreERT2-Ahr flox/flox (Ahr-C), were utilized as control subjects. Conventional Ahr knockout (Ahr-/-) mice were produced through the mating of CMV-Cre mice with Ahrflox/flox mice. C57BL/6 mice (WT mice) were sourced from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

## Induction of PF by bleomycin (BLM)
Ido1-C, Ido1-CKO, Ahr-C, Ahr-CKO, Ido1-/-, Ahr-/- and WT mice (8–9 weeks old, male) were anesthetized using pentobarbital sodium (60 mg/kg) and subsequently received intratracheal administration of 1.5 mg/kg BLM in 50 μl of PBS via a high-pressure atomizing needle (Cat: BJ-PW-M, Bio Jane Trading Limited, Shanghai, China). Mice receiving an equal volume of PBS served as control subjects. For Ido1-/- and WT mice, Kyn (20 mg/kg) was subcutaneously injected every other day from days 2 to 20 following BLM challenge. For therapeutic experiments, Trp (400 mg/kg), Kyn (40 mg/kg) and Pfd (300 mg/kg) were orally administered daily from days 10 to 20 following BLM induction. On day 21 following BLM challenge, the mice were euthanized for the analysis of PF development and severity.

## Liquid Chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis
LC-MS analysis was conducted to quantify Trp and Kyn levels using established techniques . The samples were prepared and injected into an HPLC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with an Ultimate XB-C18 HPLC column (Shanghai, China), as previously described .

## Histological analysis
The left lung was infused with 0.2 ml of freshly prepared 4 % neutral buffered paraformaldehyde and then submerged in fresh 4 % neutral buffered paraformaldehyde for 24 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the lung tissue was embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were sectioned into 5 μm slices for histological examination, following established protocols . For immunostaining, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against FSP1, IDO1, α-SMA, and COLLAGEN 1, followed by staining with either Alexa Fluor 594-labeled anti-rabbit/mouse or Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-mouse/rabbit antibodies (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA, respectively.

## Culture and treatment of primary lung fibroblasts
As previously described , fibroblasts were isolated from patients with IPF, control subjects, and mice. Briefly, lung tissues were finely chopped into 1 mm3 fragments and cultured in the flask for 24 h to allow fibroblasts migrate out from tissue debris and adhere to the flask bottom. The adhered cells were then cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10 % FBS (Moregate, Bulimba, Australia) at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 humidified incubator. For stimulation, fibroblasts were co-cultured with either a low dose (100 μg/ml) or a high dose (200 μg/ml) of Kyn for 24 h, followed by induction with either TGF-β1 (10 ng/ml) or H2O2 (300 μM) for additional 24 h.

## Western blot analysis
Western blot was performed following previously reported methods . In brief, the samples were disrupted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with proteinase inhibitor (Roche, Wuhan, China). An identical volume of the lysates was electrophoresed on 10 % SDS-polyacrylamide gels, then blotted onto PVDF sheets. After blocking with 5 % nonfat milk for 1 h at room temperature, the sheets were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies. Post-washing, the membranes were treated with an HRP-linked secondary antibody (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 60 min at room temperature, and the reactive bands were visualized using chemiluminescence reagents (Thermo Scientific, Ulm, Germany). Images were captured using X-ray film. Analysis was performed using Image Lab 6.0.1 software.

## Real-time PCR
Real-time PCR was conducted as outlined in prior studies . Initially, RNA was initially isolated from fibroblasts and lung tissues utilizing the TRlzol reagent (Takara, Dalian, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA) was employed to assess the concentration and purity of the extracted RNA. Subsequently, complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed with an M−MLV reverse transcriptase kit (Invitrogen, CA, USA). RT-PCR was conducted on a Real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). Actb was used for normalization. The primer sequences were detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

## Hydroxyproline assay
The hydroxyproline content in lung homogenates was determined utilizing a hydroxyproline assay kit (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) following the provided guidelines. The levels of hydroxyproline in lung tissues are expressed as micrograms of hydroxyproline per milligram of lung tissue.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) and PTEN promoter reporter assay
ChIP assays were performed utilizing a ChIP assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) as described previously . Initially, 1 × 106
Ido1-/- fibroblasts were treated with Kyn for 24 h followed by cross-linking with formaldehyde. The prepared diluted soluble chromatin solution was incubated overnight at 4 °C with rotation, either with an AHR antibody or a normal rabbit IgG. Subsequently, the mixtures were incubated with protein A + G agarose beads, and the protein-DNA complexes were eluted. The eluted DNA underwent ChIP-PCR using the indicated primers. The primers utilized for Pten were: F: 5′- CGG TGT GGG CAG ATC CAG AAC-3′, R: 5′- TCC ACG AGC CGT CCG CAG-3′. For the Pten promoter luciferase reporter assays, a dual luciferase reporter assay system (Promega, Madison, WI) was utilized as per the guidelines provided by the manufacturer.

## Cell proliferation assay
The proliferation of fibroblast was evaluated using the EdU assay (Ribobio, Guangzhou, China) following established procedures . Initially, fibroblasts were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 103 cells per well and incubated overnight. Subsequently, the cells were treated with EdU for 2 h, followed by incubation with 100 µL of Apollo reaction cocktail and 100 µL of Hoechst 33342. Proliferating cells were observed and scored under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Shinjuku, Japan).

## Cell migration assay
The assessment of cell migration utilized Transwell inserts equipped with a membrane (8.0 mm pore size, Corning, MA, USA) following established methods . Briefly, the upper chamber was inoculated with 2 × 10^4 cells per well in a medium containing 2 % serum, whereas the medium in the lower chamber contained 10 % serum to serve as a chemoattractant. Following a 24-hour incubation at 37 °C, cells that had traversed the membrane filter were dyed using 0.1 % crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), following the provided guidelines.

## Biochemical assay
On day 21 post-BLM induction in mice, serum samples were obtained and preserved at −80 °C. Biochemical assays for analysis of liver, kidney, and heart function were conducted at the Department of Clinical Laboratories of Tongji Hospital with 200 μl of serum for each mouse.

## Statistical analysis
Group analyses were performed using Graph Pad Prism software (version 5.0) (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The comparison of two experimental groups was executed through the Student’s t-test for paired data or the Student’s t-test with Welch’s correction for non-paired data. When assessing more than two groups, one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s correction was utilized. Data are depicted as mean ± SD/SEM unless indicated otherwise. A p < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant in all instances.

## Results

PF is featured by the increased Kyn

To investigate the metabolism of Trp in PF pathogenesis, we initially examined circulating Kyn and Trp levels in patients with IPF (n = 60), pSS-PF patients (n = 25), pSS patients (n = 41) and healthy volunteers (n = 136). We found that Kyn levels were significantly elevated in PF patients compared to healthy volunteers (IPF: 0.613 ± 0.309 μg/ml, pSS-PF: 0.588 ± 0.248 μg/ml, pSS: 0.422 ± 0.161 μg/ml, healthy volunteers: 0.299 ± 0.069 μg/ml) ( a). Conversely, Trp concentrations exhibited distinct variations in each PF setting (IPF: 8.030 ± 3.056 μg/ml, pSS-PF: 12.300 ± 3.760 μg/ml, pSS: 8.180 ± 2.237 μg/ml, healthy volunteers: 8.816 ± 1.569 μg/ml) ( b). The ratio of Kyn to Trp (KTR), reflecting the activation of IDO1, was significantly elevated in PF patients compared to healthy volunteers (IPF: 0.085 ± 0.053, pSS-PF: 0.530 ± 0.031, pSS: 0.529 ± 0.018, healthy volunteers: 0.035 ± 0.009) ( c). Similarly, the concentration of Kyn were increased in the lung tissues from patients with IPF ( d-f). In line with those observations, a 2.1-fold increase in IDO1 expression was observed in IPF patients compared to Control subjects ( g). 
In consistent with the data obtained from humans, significantly higher levels of Kyn in the serum samples and lung tissues were detected in BLM-induced fibrotic mice ( h and k). Interestingly, the BLM-induced mice displayed higher serum Trp concentrations ( i) but comparable Trp levels in lung tissues ( l) compared with PBS-treated mice. BLM-induced mice also exhibited an increased KTR ( j and m). Similarly, a significant upsurge in IDO1 expression was observed in mice post-BLM induction, as depicted in n. Taken together, these findings highlight that PF is characterized by elevated levels of Kyn.

## Kyn rescues Ido1-/- mice from BLM-induced lung fibrosis
Ido1 deficient (Ido1-/-) mice were subsequently used to investigate the impact of Kyn on the development of PF. Ido1 deficiency was confirmed by genotyping (Supplemental Fig. 1a) and analysis of Kyn, Trp and KTR (Supplemental Fig. 1b-d). After 21 days of BLM induction, the Ido1-/- and WT mice were euthanized. The Ido1-/- mice exhibited exacerbated lung fibrosis, as demonstrated by H&E, Sirius Red, and Masson staining of lung sections ( a), and by Ashcroft scores indicating the severity of PF ( b). Hydroxyproline, a crucial component of all types of fibrillar collagen , was found to be elevated in Ido1-/- mice ( c). This increase was associated with higher levels of the fibrotic markers FIBRONECTIN and COLLAGEN 1, both at the mRNA ( d) and protein levels ( e). Moreover, the Ido1-/- mice displayed marked body weight loss along with increased mortality following high dose (4 mg/kg) of BLM induction (Supplemental Fig. 2a and b). 
The exogenous Kyn (20 mg/kg) was next administered as described ( f) to check whether supplementation of Kyn rescues fibrotic phenotype in Ido1-/- mice. Remarkably, treatment with exogenous Kyn significantly attenuated BLM-induced lung fibrosis ( g-k). Specifically, Kyn almost completely rescued the fibrotic phenotype in Ido1-/- mice compared to the WT mice, evidenced by the histological analysis of lung sections ( g), Ashcroft scores ( h), lung hydroxyproline levels ( i), and the expression of fibrotic markers ( j and k). Taken together, our data support that Kyn, a Trp metabolite resulted from IDO1 catalysis, plays a critical role in PF pathogenesis.

## Kyn represses fibroblast activation and migration
Immunostaining of IDO1 in lung sections was next conducted to identify the cell types responsible for the enhanced Kyn in PF patients. IDO1 expression was virtually absent in lung sections from control subjects but was prominently expressed in IPF patients, particularly in α-SMA+ myofibroblasts ( a). This pattern of expression was similarly observed in lung sections from fibrotic mice ( b). In alignment with these observations, TGF-β1-treated fibroblasts exhibited substantially higher levels of Kyn and KTR compared to those treated with PBS (Supplemental Fig. 3a-c). Therefore, activated fibroblasts were characterized as one of the major cell types to produce Kyn in PF settings. 
To delve deeper into the investigation, a Col1α2-CreERT2+Ido1flox/flox mouse model was established (defined as Ido1-CKO mice). The deficiency of Ido1 in fibroblasts was triggered by administration of tamoxifen through intraperitoneal injection for five consecutive days (Supplemental Fig. 3a), starting 12 days before BLM induction. The control group consisted of littermates (the Col1α2-CreERT2- Ido1flox/flox mice, termed as Ido1-C mice) who also received tamoxifen injections (Supplemental Fig. 4a). The deletion of Ido1 was validated through genotyping of the KO allele (Supplemental Fig. 4b) and Cre allele (Supplemental Fig. 4c), which was further verified by co-immunostaining of IDO1 and COLLAGEN 1 (Supplemental Fig. 4d). These mice were then euthanized 21 days after the BLM induction. Indeed, fibroblasts were one of the major cell types to produce Kyn during the course of PF development, as evidenced by a 53.9 % decrease of circulating Kyn levels in the Ido1-CKO mice following BLM induction ( c). Similar as the Ido1-/- mice ( a-e), the Ido1-CKO mice showed exacerbated lung fibrosis, as manifested by the histological analysis ( d) and assessment of Ashcroft scores ( e) along with remarkably higher hydroxyproline contents ( f) and expression of fibrotic markers ( g and h). 
Primary Ido1-/- mouse lung fibroblasts (PMLFs) and primary human lung fibroblasts (PHLFs) were induced by TGF-β1 in conjunction with Kyn. As expected, TGF-β1 robustly facilitated the differentiation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, demonstrated by the marked upregulation of FIBRONECTIN, α-SMA, and COLLAGEN 1 in both PMLFs ( i and j) and PHLFs (Supplemental Fig. 5a and b). In sharp contrast, the addition of Kyn notably inhibited the TGF-β1-induced differentiation of fibroblasts ( i and j, Supplemental Fig. 5a and b). Co-immunostaining of above cultured cells for FSP1 and α-SMA further confirmed the data ( k and Supplemental Fig. 5c). It is noteworthy that Kyn attenuated fibroblast differentiation isolated from IPF patients in a dose dependent manner ( l-n). Additionally, Co-immunostaining for α-SMA and FSP1 was performed on lung sections from Ido1-/- and WT mice following BLM induction, revealing a significantly higher number of differentiating fibroblasts (FSP1+α-SMA+ cells, Supplemental Fig. 6a) in comparison to WT mice. Next, we assessed the impact of Kyn on fibroblast proliferation. Similarly, less amount of EdU-positive cells was detected in TGF-β1 + Kyn treated PMLFs than that treated with TGF-β1 alone (Supplemental Fig. 6b), and similar results were obtained in PHLFs (Supplemental Fig. 6c). Consistently, an increased presence of FSP1+Ki-67+ cells was identified in Ido1-/- mice (Supplemental Fig. 6d). Moreover, Kyn significantly reduced the ability of PMLFs and PHLFs to migrate through the transwell membrane (Supplemental Fig. 6e and f). Collectively our data indicate that fibroblast produces Kyn, which in turn suppresses its functionality. 

## Kyn regulates fibroblast function through the AHR-PTEN axis
Next, we aimed to dissect the mechanisms by which Kyn regulates fibroblast functionality. Recently, we demonstrated that AHR serves as a Kyn receptor in adipocytes . Therefore, these results led to the investigation of Kyn's influence on AHR expression and its subcellular location in fibroblasts. Kyn did not manifest a detectable impact on Ahr expression in fibroblasts (Supplemental Figure 7), but it significantly facilitated AHR nuclear translocation upon TGF-β1 challenge ( a and b). Next, an analysis was conducted to compare the temporal changes in expression of p-SMAD2 and p-SMAD3, the two paramount molecules in TGF-β canonical signaling. Unexpectedly, Kyn did not show a significant impact on p-SMAD2 and p-SMAD3 expression ( c). This observation rendered us to embark on the AKT/mTOR signaling, which was also found critical for fibroblast activation , proliferation and migration . Indeed, TGF-β1 strikingly activated the AKT/mTOR signaling in fibroblasts, as evidenced by the high levels of p-AKT (e.g. p-Thr308 and p-Ser473) and p-mTOR ( d) expression, while addition of Kyn robustly abrogated the increase in p-AKT and p-mTOR levels induced by TGF-β1, while the total levels of AKT and mTOR remained unchanged ( d). Given that phosphatase and tensin homologue (PTEN) serves as a inhibitor for AKT activation , we detected its expression in Kyn treated fibroblasts. Kyn potently induced PTEN expression ( e and f).
Since Kyn was found to mediate the nuclear translocation of AHR, which serves as a transcription factor, the correlation between Ahr and Pten expression in the lungs and fibroblasts post-BLM or TGF-β1 treatment was analyzed. Indeed, a strong correlation between Pten overexpression and Ahr levels was found, with a positive correlation observed both in the lung (R2 = 0.5935, g) and fibroblasts (R2 = 0.7754, h). Furthermore, in silico analysis revealed 2 potential binding sites of AHR within the Pten promoter ( i, upper panel). ChIP assays confirmed that AHR selectively bound to the Pten promoter, specifically in the area located −1,380 bp upstream of the transcriptional start site (TSS, TSS as + 1) ( i, lower panel), which was further validated by the promoter reporter assays ( j and k). Importantly, depletion of AHR expression ( l and m) or inhibition of PTEN by VO-Ohpic trihydrate ( n and o) in fibroblasts substantially abrogated the suppressive effect of Kyn on fibroblast differentiation, indicating that Kyn represses fibroblast functionality depends on the AHR/PTEN axis.

## DNA methylation mitigates AHR expression and loss of AHR in fibroblasts attenuates BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis
The above data support that Kyn could prevent pulmonary fibrosis by repressing fibroblast functionality. It seems, however, contradictory to the observation of higher levels of Kyn detected in PF patients and fibrotic mice. To delve deeper into this paradox, the expression of AHR was examined. We noted that TGF-β1 significantly repressed AHR expression in fibroblasts ( a and b), which was confirmed in murine fibrotic lungs ( c-d) and in fibroblasts isolated from IPF patients ( e). Moreover, re-analysis of the microarray data in fibroblasts derived from SSc-ILD or Control subjects (GSE40839) revealed downregulated AHR in SSc-ILD patients as well ( f).
Following the observation of TGF-β1′s repressive effect on AHR expression, fibroblasts were treated with SB431542 (a TGF-β receptor 1 kinase inhibitor) and SIS3-HCL (a SMAD3 inhibitor), respectively. The administration of either SB431542 or SIS3-HCL notably restored AHR expression that had been suppressed by TGF-β1 stimulation ( g), indicating TGF-β canonical signaling attenuates AHR expression. Recently, we illustrated that the TGF-β canonical signaling induces a global DNA hypermethylation in fibroblasts , and a CpG island within the Ahr promoter was identified ( h). We thus assumed that TGF-β1 attenuates AHR expression by inducing its promoter hypermethylation. Indeed, targeted bisulfite DNA sequencing revealed hypermethylated CpG DNA in the Ahr promoter after TGF-β1 treatment ( i), while addition of 5-Azacytidine, a specific DNA methyltransferase inhibitor, robustly abrogated TGF-β1 induced decrease of AHR expression in fibroblasts ( j). Considering MBD2′s role as a “reader” that deciphers DNA methylome-encoded instructions in the context of fibroblast differentiation , we then checked whether depletion of MBD2 abolishes the impact of TGF-β on AHR expression. Indeed, AHR expression was completely recovered in TGF-β challenged fibroblasts once MBD2 was depleted or silenced ( k and l). Together, those data support that canonical TGF-β signaling triggers hypermethylation of DNA within the Ahr promoter. MBD2 subsequently interacts with this methylated CpG DNA to suppress AHR expression in fibroblasts, which strikingly blunted the sensitivity of Kyn, the braking molecule to fibroblasts.
To further address the role of AHR in PF pathogenesis, mice with a fibroblast-specific deletion of Ahr (Col1α2-CreERT2+Ahrflox/flox, referred to as Ahr-CKO) were created, with their littermates (the Col1α2-CreERT2-Ahrflox/flox mice, referred to as Ahr-C mice) serving as controls (Supplemental Figure 8a). Ahr deficiency in fibroblasts was confirmed by genotyping (Supplemental Figure 8b) and co-immunostaining of COLLAGEN 1 and AHR (Supplemental Figure 8c). The above mice were subjected to PF induction as described earlier. As expected, Ahr-CKO mice exhibited more severe lung fibrosis, as indicated by histological analysis ( m) and Ashcroft scores ( n). Consistently, hydroxyproline was markedly up-regulated in lung homogenates from Ahr-CKO mice ( o) coupled with substantially enhanced expression of FIBRONECTIN and COLLAGEN 1 ( p and q). Furthermore, the co-immunostaining of α-SMA and PTEN revealed diminished PTEN expression in myofibroblasts from Ahr-CKO mice (Supplemental Figure 9).

## Oral administration of kynurenine efficiently reverses established PF
To assess Kyn's therapeutic potential, an initial pilot study aimed to determine the optimal dosage of Kyn. Oral Kyn administration at 40 mg/kg remarkably increased the circulating Kyn levels to around 10 μg/ml within 1 h, and a steady decrease was then observed and dropped to 0.2 μg/ml after 24 h of administration ( a). Similar trend was detected in the lung of mice after Kyn administration ( b). Subsequently, mice underwent daily intragastric administration of Trp, Kyn, and pirfenidone (Pfd) after 10 days of BLM induction as outlined ( c), respectively. Kyn treatment resulted in a 2-fold increase in circulating Kyn levels compared to untreated BLM mice ( d). However, oral administration of Trp only exhibited a trend of Kyn increase in the serum but not in the lung tissues ( e). It was worthy of note that Kyn manifested comparable therapeutic effect as that of Pfd, as demonstrated by H&E, Sirius red and Masson staining of lung sections ( f), analysis of Ashcroft scores ( g), and total contents of lung hydroxyproline ( h), as well as reduced expression of fibrotic markers at both protein ( i) and mRNA ( j and k) levels. Importantly, daily oral administration of Kyn was well-tolerated and deemed safe in mice, with no detected toxicity to the heart, liver, kidney, and intestinal tract (Supplemental Figure 10). Together, these data support that oral administration of Kyn could be a feasible therapeutic option against PF.

## Discussion
PF is a group of serious lung diseases that affect the respiratory system. Unfortunately, current therapeutic strategies for PF failed to halt disease progression and are coupled with severe side effect . Herein, we demonstrated that IPF patients, pSS-PF patients and fibrotic mice exhibited higher Kyn levels. We illustrated that fibroblast is the major cell type to produce Kyn in PF settings. As Kyn is a Trp metabolite resulted from IDO1 catalysis, and therefore, depletion of Ido1 significantly decreased circulating Kyn levels in mice following BLM challenge. However, mice deficient in Ido1 displayed exacerbated lung fibrosis, while Kyn supplementation completely rescued the disease phenotype. Mechanistically, Kyn binds to AHR, by which it facilitates AHR nuclear translocation, where AHR transcribes Pten expression to blunt AKT/mTOR signaling, by which Kyn inhibits fibroblast functionality. However, TGF-β stimulation leads to fibroblast undergoing a DNA hypermethylation in the Ahr promoter via TGFβR1/SMAD3, and MBD2 binds to this methylated CpG DNA, thereby repressing AHR expression and blunting the sensitivity of Kyn to fibroblasts. Therefore, oral administration of Kyn substantially reversed fibrotic progression absent of detectable side effect, and depletion of Mbd2 restored AHR expression following TGF-β stimulation in fibroblasts. Those findings not only underscore the pivotal role of Trp metabolism in the pathogenesis of PF, but also provide compelling evidence that Kyn could serve as an effective therapeutic agent against PF.
Accumulated evidence indicates that PF is distinctively featured by the metabolic reprogramming and mitochondria dysfunction , . Metabolic irregularities within alveolar epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages play a significant role in the abnormal synthesis of collagen and the dysregulated remodeling of the interstitial space . In particular, changes in the metabolism of non-essential amino acids such as glutamine, arginine, and glycine are key factors driving the profibrotic cellular phenotype observed in IPF , . Therefore, to a certain extent, IPF is likely a metabolic disease. l-tryptophan, distinct from non-essential amino acids, is an essential amino acid that must be obtained through daily diet, with over 95 % of free Trp being metabolized via the KP . The initial and rate-limiting step of KP is catalyzed by TDO or IDO, leading initially to the formation of N-formylkynurenine, and then kynurenine . TDO is primarily expressed in the liver , whereas IDO1 is found in a variety of cell types including macrophages, microglia, neurons, epithelial cells, and fibroblasts . IDO2, the isotype enzyme of IDO1, is primarily detected in monocytes and dendritic cells with low catalytic activity . We demonstrated that IDO1 is the major rate-limiting enzyme for KP in PF settings, as a 96.5 % of KTR decrease was noted in the Ido1-/- mice.
Previous study suggested feasible evidence that Kyn modulates the expression of MMP-1 and collagen 1 in dermal fibroblasts . Herein, we found that IPF and pSS-PF patients exhibited significantly increased Kyn levels in the serum and lung tissues. Consistently, higher circulating Kyn was also noted from silicosis patients and positively correlated with disease severity . These observations prompted us to assume that Kyn orchestrates fibroblast function contributing to PF development. However, in contrast to our assumption, mice deficient in Ido1 were more susceptible to BLM-induced PF, and supplementation of exogenous Kyn robustly rescued the PF phenotype in Ido1-/- mice. To further address these unexpected results, we generated fibroblast specific Ido1 knockout mice. Intriguingly, fibroblasts were characterized as one of the major cell types to produce Kyn in PF settings. This was supported by a 53.9 % reduction in circulating Kyn levels in the Ido1-CKO mice after BLM induction. Furthermore, mice with Ido1 deficiency in fibroblasts exhibited exacerbated lung fibrosis. We then treated fibroblasts isolated from the Ido1-/- mice, control and IPF patients with Kyn, and noted that Kyn significantly alleviated fibroblast differentiation, proliferation and migration. These data collectively indicate that Kyn suppresses fibroblast functionality to attenuate PF progression, and therefore, the increase of circulating Kyn in PF patients could be likely a compensated phenomenon.
The next critical question is how Kyn regulates fibroblast functionality. Previous studies suggested that the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway as crucial for fibroblast differentiation, proliferation, and migration , , . We thus next embarked on the role of Kyn in AKT/mTOR signaling. Indeed, Kyn remarkably suppressed TGF-β induced AKT and mTOR phosphorylation in fibroblasts. These observations led us to concentrate on PTEN, an inhibitor of the AKT/mTOR pathway . In line with our expectation, Kyn significantly enhanced PTEN expression in fibroblasts, suggesting that Kyn inhibits AKT/mTOR signaling probably through enhancing PTEN expression. Exactly, addition of PTEN inhibitor completely abolished the suppressive effect of Kyn on fibroblasts. To address more detailed mechanism underlying Kyn regulation of PTEN expression, we focused on AHR, the receptor of Kyn. We first noted that Kyn facilitates AHR nuclear translocation. Intriguingly, the levels of AHR decreased following 60 min of stimulation, likely attributed to a rapid ubiquitylation and subsequent degradation via the 26S proteasome pathway . Previous studies have demonstrated that AHR functions as a transcription factor within the nucleus , . In our current research, we demonstrated that AHR is essential for the transcription of Pten expression, which in turn inhibits TGF-β-stimulated AKT/mTOR signaling during the activation of fibroblasts. Therefore, conventional or fibroblast specific knockout of Ahr exacerbated BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis (Supplemental Figure 11 and m-q). It is crucially important to note that the Kyn/AHR/PTEN/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway was significantly dampened in fibrotic conditions, with the expression of AHR in fibroblasts being nullified following TGF-β induction. Consistently, AHR was downregulated in SSc fibroblast . However, the mechanisms underlying decreased AHR expression is yet to be addressed. In our recently study, we observed that TGF-β triggers a widespread DNA hypermethylation and an increase in MBD2 expression in fibroblasts . Following TGF-β1 stimulation, we detected hypermethylation of CpG DNA within the Ahr promoter. Inhibition DNA methylation by 5-Azacytidine or knockout of MBD2 restored AHR expression following TGF-β induction. Those data perfectly address the phenomenon that the PF patients manifest higher levels of circulating Kyn but are still coupled with disease progression.
The most thrilling finding in this report is the investigation into the use of Kyn in the context of PF. In particular, Kyn could be orally administrated, which is more feasible and convenient in clinical practice. Importantly, oral administration of Kyn (40 mg/kg) once a day sufficiently maintained the Kyn levels above 0.2 μg/ml and 27.2 μg/g in serum and lung tissues, respectively. More excitingly, oral administration of Kyn robustly reversed the established PF, and its therapeutic effect is comparable to that of pirfenidone, an FDA approved drug for IPF treatment. Since Kyn itself is a natural metabolite of Trp, no toxic side effect was detected with the dose employed. Indeed, intravenous Kyn (5 mg/kg) infusion in healthy volunteers showed no serious adverse events . Therefore, Kyn could be a safe and effective diet product against PF in clinical practice.
Some limitations were also noted in our studies. The number of PF patients in each cohort for lung homogenates was relatively small. Although statistical significance was detected in term of IDO1 expression in fibrotic lungs, but more PF lungs are necessary to further confirm the conclusion. Moreover, we demonstrated evidence supporting that fibroblasts are among the principal cell types producing Kyn in PF environments. However, other cell types, including macrophages and alveolar epithelial cells, might also generate Kyn throughout the progression of PF, and the mechanisms underlying Kyn generation in different PF settings could be distinctive. For instance, Kyn production in IPF patients could be caused by the altered IDO1 activity absence of enhanced circulating Trp, while increased IDO1 activity along with elevated Trp could be responsible for the higher circulating Kyn in pSS-PF patients. Therefore, elucidation of the mechanisms for Kyn generation in each type of PF patients could be helpful for developing effective therapeutic strategies. Similarly, we demonstrated that Kyn attenuates fibroblast functionality and protects the mice from BLM-induced PF. but we cannot completely rule out the potential role of Kyn in other cell types in the context of PF.

## Conclusions
In summary, our research provided convincing evidence that high levels of Kyn are a clinical feature in PF patients. Studies in multiple animal models illustrated that Kyn potently shields mice from BLM-induced lung fibrosis by suppressing fibroblast functionality. Kyn promotes the nuclear translocation of AHR, which then specifically attaches to the Pten promoter, enhancing its expression. PTEN, in turn, mitigates the TGF-β-induced activation of AKT/mTOR signaling in fibroblasts. A particularly groundbreaking finding of our study is the recognition of Kyn as a safe and potent Trp derivative for PF treatment. Taken together, our study highlighted a role of Trp metabolism in PF pathogenesis, and demonstrated convincing evidence for Kyn, a Trp metabolite, to abrogate PF progression in fibrotic models.
