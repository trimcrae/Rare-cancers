## Abstract
Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) represent a paramount global health burden, with their pathogenesis rooted in a dynamically dysregulated tissue microenvironment characterized by sustained oxidative stress, compromised nitric oxide bioavailability, hyperactive thrombogenesis, and calcium homeostasis disruption. This review moves beyond the conventional view of polymers as inert drug carriers. Instead, it articulates a shift toward regarding them as intelligent, stimuli-responsive catalytic platforms. These platforms can actively sense and remodel the pathological microenvironment. We meticulously detail the design principles underpinning these systems, encompassing enzyme-mimetic copolymer networks that replicate natural catalytic cycles, self-assembled gas therapy nanoparticles with geometrically confined active sites, intelligent hydrogels enabling on-demand therapeutic release, and surface-functionalized vectors for precise endothelial targeting. A key differentiator is the inherent advantage of polymeric architectures. By leveraging dynamic covalent bonding, tunable topology, and biocompatible backbones, these systems achieve superior biosafety and more seamless integration with biological processes than conventional metal-based nanocatalysts. The review critically evaluates their transformative therapeutic performance across a spectrum of CVDs, including acute myocardial infarction, atherosclerotic plaque stabilization, and ischemia–reperfusion injury, thereby establishing a transformative design philosophy for catalytic cardiovascular medicine. Finally, we delineate the prevailing translational hurdles, such as long-term biosafety and scalable manufacturing, and outline future directions, positing that polymer nanocatalysis is poised to become a cornerstone of next-generation, personalized theranostic strategies against CVDs.

## Introduction
Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) remain one of the leading causes of global morbidity and mortality, with ischemic heart disease (IHD), stroke, heart failure, and atherosclerosis representing a substantial portion of this burden [ – ]. As a persistent and growing threat to global health, it is crucial to continuously monitor the prevalence and impact of CVDs to inform healthcare strategies and optimize resource allocation. In 2021, an estimated 612 million people worldwide were affected by CVDs, which accounted for nearly 27% of all global deaths [ ]. This underscores the urgent need for ongoing surveillance and effective interventions to mitigate the widespread and increasing impact of these diseases.
Nanocatalysts-structures with components between 1 and 100 nm—are revolutionizing biomedicine by bridging biological and physical sciences [ ]. Their unique nanoscale dimensions confer distinctive structural, chemical, mechanical, and biological properties not observed in their bulk counterparts [ ]. In nanomedicine, these particles are of paramount interest, powering advances in biosensors, microfluidics, tissue engineering, and drug delivery systems [ – ]. They function as sophisticated delivery agents by encapsulating or having therapeutic drugs attached to them, enabling precise targeting to tissues and controlled release [ , ].
Nanocatalysts for CVDs is an emerging interdisciplinary field that integrates nanotechnology with catalytic processes for biomedical applications [ ]. The core aim is to employ biocompatible nanocatalysts to trigger specific catalytic reactions within distinct disease microenvironments, transforming low-toxicity substances-either endogenous or externally delivered-into potent therapeutic and imaging agents [ , ]. Through intelligent responsive design (e.g., pH-sensitive or enzyme-triggered release), these nanocatalysts achieve targeted accumulation at disease sites and spatiotemporally controlled drug release. This significantly enhances treatment precision and reduces systemic toxicity [ – ]. Surface functionalization further improves biocompatibility and circulation half-life, and the nano-catalytic effect opens new avenues for enzyme-mimetic therapies, particularly in scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS)[ – ]. In addition, the engineered intelligent nanoparticle enables concurrent diagnostic monitoring and feedback in real-time, integrating these capabilities into a cohesive theranostic system [ , ]. These collective attributes are driving a paradigm shift in cardiovascular treatment from conventional pharmacological intervention toward intelligent and personalized therapeutic approaches. As summarized in Fig.  , this paradigm shift encompasses intelligent design strategies, diverse therapeutic applications, specific pathophysiological targets, and key considerations for safety and breakthroughs in nanocatalytic medicine for CVDs.
 The aim of this study is to provide a comprehensive summary of the therapeutic targets and applications of nanocatalytic medicine in the treatment of CVDs. By analyzing their mechanisms and effectiveness in modulating disease-related microenvironments, this research explores the potential of nanocatalysts in enhancing treatment outcomes. A key focus is the translational pathway, tackling hurdles in biosafety, manufacturing, and regulation. By positioning polymer nanocatalysis as a new therapeutic paradigm, it provides a roadmap for next-generation, catalyst-driven treatments, which highlights emerging trends and promising directions for advancing CVDs therapy.

## Pathophysiological targets amenable to nanocatalytic intervention
As depicted in Fig.  , the pathological microenvironment of CVDs is characterized by interconnected molecular and cellular events such as localized ROS bursts, nitric oxide (NO) signaling imbalance, heightened thrombotic propensity, and calcium homeostasis disruption. These processes are often spatially heterogeneous, self-amplifying, and operate within feedback loops, presenting a therapeutic challenge for conventional small-molecule drugs due to their lack of selectivity, inability to maintain effective concentrations at target sites, and failure to simultaneously modulate multiple pathological circuits. However, nanocatalytic medicine offers a pioneering strategy to intervene in these catalytically targetable circuits by providing spatially precise and responsive modulation, which could overcome the selectivity issue illustrated in Fig.  .
 

## Scavenging reactive oxygen species and stabilizing mitochondria
In contexts of oxidative stress, a self-perpetuating cycle of mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS overproduction defines a core pathological axis [ ]. This state is initiated by metabolic perturbation and bioenergetic compromise, leading to a burst of ROS that directly inflicts damage on mitochondrial integrity [ , ].
Markers such as dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) mediate ROS-induced mitochondrial damage and fission via site-specific phosphorylation. Specifically, phosphorylation at Ser616 promotes fission, whereas phosphorylation at Ser637 inhibits it [ ]. This dysregulation of mitochondrial dynamics results in fragmented mitochondria that further produce ROS, creating a feedback loop that exacerbates oxidative stress and accelerates cell death through apoptosis. Mitochondrial dysfunction is further compounded by impaired electron transport chain (ETC) activity, leading to a decrease in ATP production, which disrupts cellular energy homeostasis and contributes to cardiomyocyte injury [ ]. This cascade reveals precise, actionable targets for catalytic intervention. These include: [ ] directly scavenging pathological ROS, [ ] fine-tuning mitochondrial dynamics via specific signaling nodes (e.g., phosphorylation events), and [ ] restoring dysregulated metabolic flux and bioenergetic homeostasis [ , ].
Furthermore, the dysregulated microenvironment harbors additional synergistic nodes for catalytic modulation. The upregulated expression of key surface receptors-exemplified by metabolic regulators such as insulin receptors-enables targeted engagement to restore nutrient-sensing pathways and activate pro-survival signaling cascades, including the PI3K/AKT axis [ , ]. However, sustained hyperactivation of PI3K/AKT signaling may inadvertently promote pathological hypertrophy or oncogenic pathways in non-target tissues, necessitating careful control of catalytic specificity. In parallel, enhancing the endogenous antioxidant machinery within mitochondria, for instance through the precise modulation of core enzymes like superoxide dismutase 2 (SOD2) via post-translational mechanisms, provides a direct means to counteract oxidative insult at the organellar level [ ]. Thus, a concerted catalytic strategy emerges, integrating extracellular receptor-directed metabolic reprogramming with intramitochondrial redox stabilization to cooperatively reestablish cellular homeostasis [ ].

## Restoring nitric oxide homeostasis at the endothelium
In CVDs, endothelial cell dysfunction refers to a range of maladaptive changes in cellular function that significantly affect hemostasis and thrombosis regulation, local vascular tone, redox balance, and the management of both acute and chronic inflammatory responses within the arterial wall [ ]. Endothelial cells play a key role in maintaining vascular homeostasis through the production of NO, a potent vasodilator that regulates blood vessel tone, inhibits platelet aggregation, and promotes vascular smooth muscle relaxation [ – ]. Nevertheless, during the progression of CVDs, the production of NO is significantly reduced due to endothelial cell injury, which disrupts vascular function [ ]. Meanwhile, elevated levels of ROS exacerbate endothelial damage by scavenging NO, generating peroxynitrite (ONOO −), and inducing oxidative stress [ , ]. This imbalance between ROS and NO further aggravates vascular damage and accelerates disease progression, making it crucial to find ways to restore NO production and rebalance the vascular microenvironment.
Catalytic strategies can be engineered to simultaneously address these interconnected nodes within the dysregulated microenvironment. A core intervention involves the in-situ restoration of NO bioavailability, counteracting its pathological depletion [ ]. A complementary and synergistic approach is the catalytic scavenging of excessive ROS, which prevents the oxidative consumption of NO and inhibits the formation of damaging secondary metabolites [ , ]. The concurrent application of these dual strategies—replenishing cytoprotective NO signaling while eliminating pathogenic ROS—creates a powerful positive feedback loop. This integrated catalytic framework effectively breaks the self-perpetuating cycle of redox imbalance, enabling the recalibration of the microenvironment and the re-establishment of vascular homeostasis.

## Modulating the mechano-thrombotic microenvironment
Arterial thrombosis is a multifaceted cardiovascular condition primarily induced by the rupture or erosion of atherosclerotic plaques, triggering platelet aggregation and the formation of thrombi that obstruct the arterial lumen [ ]. Within a dysregulated hemostatic microenvironment, a pathologically elevated shear stress resulting from luminal constriction serves as a key mechanical trigger [ , ]. This shear stress induces mechanical stimuli on the surface of vascular endothelial cells, activating the Piezo1 channel, a mechanosensitive cation-selective ion channel [ ]. The activation of Piezo1 facilitates the influx of calcium ions (Ca2+), which, in turn, regulates endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and NO production. NO, a key regulator of thrombus formation, inhibits platelet adhesion and aggregation, thus playing a protective role against thrombosis [ ]. Nevertheless, at elevated shear stress, the Piezo1 channel activity decreases, leading to reduced Ca2+ influx and diminished NO production [ , ]. Meanwhile, the microenvironment is marked by a pronounced oxidative and inflammatory state, characterized by an accumulation of ROS which further consume residual NO, promote platelet hyperactivity, and fuel endothelial dysfunction [ ].
In the thrombotic microenvironment, key features include the presence of activated platelets and damaged endothelial cells, accompanied by elevated oxidative stress, NO deficiency, and a pronounced inflammatory response, all contributing to thrombus stability and recurrence [ , ]. This cascade establishes several interconnected, catalytically targetable nodes: modulating mechanosensitive signaling to restore calcium-dependent NO synthesis, replenishing bioavailable NO to reinstate its anti-thrombotic and vasoprotective functions, and scavenging excess ROS to break the cycle of oxidative damage and inflammatory amplification. An integrated catalytic strategy that simultaneously addresses NO deficiency and ROS overload can therefore disrupt the self-sustaining thrombotic circuit, promoting resolution of the aberrant microenvironment [ ].

## Correcting calcium dysregulation and inflammatory signaling
Within a dysregulated stress microenvironment, a self-amplifying pathological circuit is established through the convergence of three interrelated axes: loss of calcium homeostasis, a burst of ROS, and activation of inflammatory cell death pathways [ , ]. The core driver is a collapse in calcium signaling, where overload—potentiated through defined axes such as the CaSR/PLCγ1/IP3R cascade—severely disrupts mitochondrial function, triggering a surge in ROS. This oxidative stress further damages calcium-handling organelles like the sarcoplasmic reticulum, creating a vicious cycle of calcium-ROS cross-talk [ , ]. Simultaneously, this microenvironment activates inflammatory death pathways, for instance through the cleavage of the executioner protein gasdermin D (GSDMD) by specific caspases, which drives pyroptosis and the release of pro-inflammatory mediators [ ].
This coordinated cascade defines actionable targets for catalytic modulation: rectifying dysregulated calcium flux, scavenging pathogenic ROS, and intercepting the critical proteolytic events that drive inflammatory cell death. Catalytic platforms can be engineered to co-modulate these interconnected nodes simultaneously. By jointly targeting the upstream initiators of ionic imbalance and the downstream effectors of inflammatory rupture, such a multi-target strategy synergistically disrupts the self-amplifying network, enabling the restoration of microenvironmental homeostasis [ ].
Building on this landscape of pathological targets, the rational design of polymer nanocatalysts is driven by the need to actively sense and remodel these specific disease microenvironments. The following section details the key engineering strategies that enable such precise, catalytic intervention.

## Design strategies for cardiovascular polymer nanocatalysts
The design of cardiovascular polymer nanocatalysts marks a decisive transition from passive carriers to architecturally integrated, functionally active catalytic platforms. This advance is rooted in the inherent programmability of polymers, which enables precise control over catalytic motifs, stimuli-responsiveness, and biointerfacial properties. Rather than merely encapsulating therapeutics, modern approaches embed catalytic function directly within the polymer framework through enzyme-mimetic networks, polymer–metal hybrid active sites, or stimulus-triggered self-assembly. These strategies collectively allow nanomaterials to execute localized catalytic reactions, such as ROS elimination, gas release, or ion modulation, within the pathological niche with spatiotemporal precision. The following sections detail these design blueprints, illustrating how polymer-specific features are leveraged to create intelligent catalysts capable of dynamically intervening in cardiovascular pathophysiology.

## Enzyme-mimicking polymer networks
The fabrication of enzyme-mimicking polymer networks, known as nanozymes, employs effective synthetic strategies to emulate natural enzymatic activity and enhance biomedical functionality [ ]. Controlled radical polymerization techniques enable precise incorporation of catalytic motifs into copolymer backbones, yielding nanostructures with tunable peroxidase-like or SOD-like activities [ , ]. These networks can be engineered into nanoparticles exhibiting potent ROS-scavenging ability. Nonetheless, the long-term stability and potential immunogenicity of such synthetic networks in vivo remain unclear, requiring further investigation into their biodegradation pathways. To emulate natural enzymes, recent advances have leveraged polyphenol-based structures as versatile platforms [ , ]. These natural polyphenols, such as tannic acid, epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), and oligomeric proanthocyanidins (OPCs), form stable network architectures through oxidative self-polymerization or copolymerization [ , ]. These networks can be engineered into nanoparticles or hydrogels exhibiting potent ROS-scavenging ability, crucial for mitigating oxidative stress in microenvironment of CVDs [ ]. Furthermore, their rich phenolic chemistry allows facile functionalization with therapeutic nucleic acids or targeting motifs via covalent or supramolecular interactions.
This polyphenol platform is exemplified in a therapeutic strategy for MIRI. In one study, encapsulating bioactive compounds such as resveratrol (RES)—a polyphenolic compound with potent antioxidant properties that scavenges ROS, mitigates oxidative damage, and promotes cell survival [ ]—and oligomeric OPCs, a type of flavonoid polyphenol, known for their strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities. The antioxidant mechanisms of RES and OPC function at multiple tiers, integrating direct radical scavenging with profound modulation of cellular defense and homeostatic pathways. Primarily, their phenolic structures enable direct donation of electrons or hydrogen atoms to neutralize ROS, with OPC typically exhibiting greater potency due to its higher density of phenolic hydroxyl groups [ , ]. Beyond this chemical quenching, both polyphenols operate through pivotal cellular signaling networks. They effectively attenuate the inflammatory response by downregulating key mediators such as TLR4 and NF-κB, and restore apoptotic balance by decreasing the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio. Critically, a core identified mechanism is the potent activation of Sirt1, a NAD + -dependent deacetylase and central circadian regulator. Sirt1 upregulation is instrumental in enhancing cellular stress resistance and metabolic adaptation [ ]. Furthermore, they promote the expression of other circadian components like Per2. This synergistic action combines direct ROS scavenging with the regulation of inflammation, apoptosis, and crucially, the circadian rhythm machinery. Building on these fundamental antioxidant properties, the practical translation into therapeutic nanoformulations establishes their comprehensive cytoprotective efficacy against oxidative damage in contexts such as MIRI. This efficacy is visually demonstrated in Fig.  , which outlines the nanoparticle preparation and their circadian rhythm-dependent therapeutic strategy, and further corroborated by the detailed histopathological, functional, and molecular outcomes presented in Fig.  [ ].
 Further sophistication is achieved through the rational design of multicomponent systems. A recently developed strategy in stent development involves incorporating bioactive compounds like epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), a key component of green tea, and Tanshinone IIA sulfonic sodium (TSS), both recognized for their vascular protective properties. EGCG and TSS exert synergistic antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects through complementary molecular mechanisms, particularly within the cardiovascular context [ , ]. EGCG primarily functions as a direct ROS scavenger, neutralizing free radicals via its catechol and gallate moieties [ ]. It also chelates pro-oxidant metal ions and upregulates endogenous cellular defense enzymes through the Nrf2/ARE pathway. In parallel, TSS, a derivative of the traditional compound Danshen, demonstrates potent anti-inflammatory activity. It protects endothelial function and mitigates inflammation by suppressing key pro-inflammatory signaling pathways, notably NF-κB [ ] . Critically, their combination exhibits enhanced efficacy. The EGCG-TSS complex, stabilized by π-π stacking, synergistically modulates pivotal signaling cascades. They cooperatively activate the PI3K/Akt/eNOS pathway, which is crucial for NO production, endothelial cell survival, and vasodilation, while in concert to inhibiti the NF-κB pathway [ ]. This dual action reduces the expression of inflammatory cytokines and adhesion molecules. By simultaneously quenching ROS at the chemical level and suppressing inflammatory ROS generation at the transcriptional level, the EGCG-TSS complex effectively ameliorates oxidative stress and its downstream consequences, thereby creating a protective microenvironment conducive to vascular healing and re-endothelialization.
Ultimately, enzyme-mimicking polymer networks embody a biomimetic therapeutic intelligence. They are engineered not merely as inert carriers but as dynamic catalytic interfaces that diagnose, interrogate, and remodel the pathological microenvironment. By merging catalytic efficiency with biological specificity, they offer a powerful strategy to disrupt self-amplifying cycles of cardiovascular injury and steer tissue toward functional recovery.

## Polymer–metal hybrid nanocatalysts encapsulated or grafted onto biocompatible backbones
While enzyme-mimicking polymer networks often rely on organic motifs, another powerful strategy integrates inorganic catalytic centers within polymeric architectures. Nanoscale metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are gaining attention for their unique properties, including multiple catalytic centers, ideal size, biocompatibility, and biodegradability, making them promising for cancer and ischemic disease treatments [ ]. To harness these properties for biomedical use, synthesis methods prioritize biocompatibility. Solvothermal or microwave-assisted techniques under mild conditions are commonly employed, often utilizing low-toxicity metal ions and biocompatible linkers [ ]. To enhance applicability in drug delivery, “green” synthesis routes avoiding toxic solvents have been developed. Drug encapsulation is achieved via one-pot synthesis, post-synthetic diffusion, or mechanochemical methods, ensuring high loading efficiency through host–guest interactions [ , ]. Surface functionalization with polymers, lipids, or targeting ligands is further applied to improve stability, bioavailability and specific targeting, while reducing immunogenicity and optimizing biodistribution [ ].
This synergistic design is powerfully exemplified by the evolution of MOF nanozymes. In the hypoxic, ROS-dense microenvironment of ischemic myocardium, early MOF designs demonstrated the utility of single metal sites for scavenging specific oxidants. The field progressed by engineering dual-metal MOFs, where two distinct metal centers are spatially organized within a single organic framework to execute enzyme-mimetic cascades. For instance, Cu-TCPP-Mn was synthesized by incorporating copper and manganese into tetrachloroporphyrin (TCPP) [ ]. The polymer-based MOF nanozyme functions as a biomimetic scaffold. Its organic ligand (TCPP) provides precise coordination pockets that chelate transition metal ions, such as Cu2+ and Mn2+, to form stable M–N₄ active centers. This engineered chelation creates dual catalytic sites within a single nanostructure: the MnN₄ center facilitates the reversible Mn(III)/Mn(IV) redox cycle, mimicking SOD to catalyze the dismutation of superoxide anions (O₂•⁻) into hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). Subsequently, the adjacent Cu-active sites within the same framework catalyze the decomposition of the generated H₂O₂ into water and oxygen, mimicking catalase (CAT) activity. Thus, the polymer matrix itself does not directly participate in redox reactions but is essential for organizing and stabilizing these metal centers, enabling an efficient cascade catalytic pathway that sequentially neutralizes ROS, as evidenced by its lower Km and higher catalytic efficiency compared to its single-metal counterpart [ , ].
Building on the concept of spatial and functional integration, core–shell architectures represent a further refinement in hybrid design. A notable example is the gold-selenium nanoparticle. The Au–Se core–shell nanoparticles (AS-I/S NCs) represent a promising strategy for the treatment of MI/RI. These nanoparticles are designed with gold nanorods (Au NRs) as the core, which provide strong near-infrared (NIR) photoacoustic imaging capabilities due to their high optical absorption and tunable surface plasmon resonance (SPR). The shell of the nanoparticle is composed of nanoselenium (Se), which acts as a powerful antioxidant, capable of scavenging ROS and alleviating oxidative stress [ ]. The catalytic mechanism of the AS-I/S nanoparticles primarily resides in the Se shell, which mimics and potentiates the activity of endogenous selenoenzymes such as glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and thioredoxin reductase (TrxR). In the ROS-rich microenvironment of ischemia–reperfusion injury, the surface Se atoms act as catalytic centers that continuously cycle between different oxidation states (e.g., Se⁰, Se2⁻, Se4⁺), thereby facilitating the efficient reduction of peroxides and superoxide anions. This redox cycling is enhanced by the local electronic interaction with the gold nanorod core, which serves as an electron-transfer platform, further lowering the activation energy for ROS scavenging. Moreover, the chitosan-stabilized structure ensures high dispersion and accessibility of catalytic Se sites, while the ROS-responsive degradation of the shell enables sustained release of bioactive Se species, creating a feedback-driven catalytic therapy. Consequently, the nanoparticle not only directly neutralizes oxidative radicals but also restores the cellular antioxidant defense system by upregulating GPx and SOD activity, thereby breaking the vicious cycle of oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. This integrated catalytic design enables potent, long-lasting antioxidant efficacy with minimal off-target effects, representing a biomimetic catalytic strategy for targeted treatment of MIRI [ , ]. The underlying molecular mechanism, showcasing the inhibition of ROS, restoration of antioxidant enzymes, and modulation of key survival pathways, is systematically elucidated in Fig.  .
 In summary, polymer–metal hybrid nanocatalysts exemplify a mature design strategy in catalytic nanomedicine. They are no longer simple composites but are intelligently architected systems where the polymer and metal components cooperate synergistically. This cooperation enables them to sense, diagnose, and catalytically intervene in the evolving cardiovascular microenvironment with precision and adaptability, representing a cornerstone for next-generation targeted therapies.

## Self-assembling catalytic micelles and vesicles for gas therapy
Gas therapy has gained attention in biomedicine for its ability to modulate critical biological processes such as oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular signaling. Conversely, the clinical application of gaseous agents is fundamentally limited by their low solubility, rapid diffusion, and inability to achieve targeted, sustained release at disease sites [ ]. Nanotechnology provides a transformative solution to these challenges. Stimuli-responsive nanoparticles enable the targeted delivery and controlled, on-demand production of gas directly within pathological microenvironments (e.g., via pH, enzymes, light, or ultrasound) [ , ]. This approach not only overcomes the pharmacokinetic limitations of traditional inhalation or dissolution methods but also allows for precise spatiotemporal control, enhanced therapeutic efficacy, and the facilitation of synergistic combination therapies. The synthesis of advanced nanoparticles for cardiovascular gas therapy prioritizes precise targeted delivery and subsequent localized gas release within the pathological microenvironment [ ]. This objective is realized via as gas-generating prodrugs such as L-Arg or S-allyl-L-cysteine encapsulated within or covalently conjugated to biocompatible nanocarriers, including polymeric nanoparticles or liposomes [ , ].
The unique advantage of polymeric platforms for catalytic gas therapy in CVDs lies in their ability to perform localized, stimulus-responsive gas delivery that precisely modulates the pathological microenvironment. As exemplified by the in situ H₂S-releasing stent coating, a polymer brush system covalently conjugated with a thiol-activated donor (e.g., PEG-ABTA/RGD) achieves sustained, controlled release of H₂S triggered by endogenous glutathione. This design enables multifunctional regulation directly at the implant–tissue interface: it simultaneously enhances antithrombogenicity by inhibiting fibrinogen adsorption and platelet activation, promotes rapid endothelialization via integrin-mediated EC adhesion and migration, suppresses smooth muscle cell proliferation and synthetic phenotype switching, and allevates oxidative stress and inflammation by scavenging ROS and polarizing macrophages toward the M2 phenotype. Unlike systemic gas administration or conventional drug-eluting stents, this polymeric catalytic approach provides spatiotemporal precision, long-term stability under physiological shear stress, and synergistic bioactivity that addresses the competing requirements of inhibiting restenosis while promoting healing [ , ]. From a materials engineering perspective, the synthesis of gas-released nanocatalyst contributes to vascular healing and help reduce the risk of restenosis, offering a promising strategy for improving the long-term performance of vascular implants.
While such localized implant-based strategies excel in interfacing with specific anatomical sites, many cardiovascular pathologies, such as atherosclerosis, present as diffuse systemic conditions requiring targeted systemic intervention. Peng et al. designed osteopontin (OPN)-modified nanoliposomes, named as CZALO. It achieves prolonged circulation and accumulates in atherosclerotic plaques, where they are internalized by macrophages. Within these cells, the nanomaterials serve as catalytic reactors that not only scavenge ROS via enzyme-mimetic activity but also supply L-arginine for in-situ conversion into NO by upregulated inducible NO synthase. The produced NO exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing the NF-κB pathway, thereby reducing the transcription of key pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, while concurrently inhibiting endothelial adhesion molecule expression and mitigating the senescence-associated secretory phenotype. This integrated approach merges targeted nanocatalysis with spatially controlled gas release, enabling dual-pathway intervention against oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, thus representing a distinct therapeutic advance over conventional monotherapies in CVDs [ , ].
Self-assembling catalytic systems for gas therapy address the pharmacokinetic challenges of gaseous mediators through spatiotemporally controlled in situ generation. Stimuli-responsive release of gasotransmitters (e.g., NO, H₂S) allows precise modulation of vascular tone, inflammation, and thrombosis. This strategy exemplifies targeted catalytic synthesis within diseased microenvironments, transforming inert precursors into potent therapeutic agents on demand.

## Stimuli-responsive hydrogels & injectable scaffolds
Synthetic strategies for stimuli-responsive hydrogels and injectable scaffolds involve engineering biocompatible polymers capable of sol–gel transitions or property modulation upon exposure to physiological stimuli. This is achieved by functionalizing natural or synthetic polymers with crosslinkable groups responsive to ROS accumulation, pH, or enzymatic activity [ , ]. Hydrogels have emerged as a pivotal biomaterial in cardiovascular therapy owing to their unique physicochemical properties. Their high water content and tunable porosity recapitulate a hydrated, biomimetic microenvironment that enhances cell viability, nutrient diffusion, and vascular perfusion—key factors for supporting angiogenesis and tissue integration. As drug delivery vehicles, hydrogels provide controlled and sustained release of therapeutic agents, such as growth factors, anti‑inflammatory molecules, and exosomes, through modifiable pore architectures, thereby minimizing systemic toxicity and improving local bioavailability [ , ]. Functionally, hydrogels offer remarkable versatility: their mechanical properties can be engineered to match the compliance of cardiac tissue, ensuring mechanical compatibility under dynamic physiological conditions [ ]. Conductive hydrogels restore electrophysiological coupling in infarcted myocardium, while bioadhesive formulations enable stable integration with the cardiac surface [ ]. These multifunctional attributes—combined with inherent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and stimuli‑responsiveness—establish hydrogels as an enabling platform for advanced cardiovascular regeneration, predictive drug screening, and real‑time physiological monitoring [ ].
Repairing solid organ damage requires biomaterials that integrate structurally with compromised tissues while providing durable topographic and biochemical support. This need drives the development of stimuli‑responsive hydrogels and injectable scaffolds. These materials undergo in situ sol‑gel transition, conforming to tissue defects and establishing a bioactive matrix for localized, cue‑responsive therapeutic release. In one study, phenylboronic acid-crosslinked hydrogel system provides a uniquely sophisticated platform for cardiac repair by orchestrating the spatiotemporal delivery of therapeutic nanoparticles in response to the evolving MI microenvironment. The hydrogel itself, formed via dynamic boronate bonds between the novel small-molecule crosslinker N(BA)₃ and a mussel-inspired PVA-DA polymer, serves as a responsive depot implanted directly at the infarct site. Its intrinsic ROS-scavenging ability via boronate bond cleavage provides immediate antioxidant relief. Crucially, the hydrogel’s structure is engineered to sequentially release its encapsulated cargos: upon encountering the high-ROS early MI environment, it rapidly releases EGCG@Hyp&Arg nanoparticles. These nanoparticles deliver a combined anti-inflammatory (via hyperoside and EGCG) and pro-angiogenic (via L-arginine-derived NO) punch precisely when needed. Subsequently, the hydrogel provides sustained, long-term release of the anti-fibrotic drug galunisertib from its co-embedded PLGA microspheres to combat late-stage adverse remodeling. Thus, the hydrogel platform transcends being a simple carrier; it is a microenvironment-sensing, stage-adaptive therapeutic system that integrates rapid nanoparticle action with prolonged microsphere release, enabling a comprehensive "antioxidant-inflammation-angiogenesis-fibrosis" intervention within a single injectable formulation, thereby addressing the multidimensional pathology of MI more effectively than static delivery systems [ ].
Additionally, the synthesis of an injectable hydrogel for CVD treatment involves the creation of a dynamic, responsive system capable of delivering therapeutic agents in a controlled manner within the ischemic myocardial environment [ ]. For example, one hydrogel is developed using a Schiff base reaction to cross-link oxidized hyaluronic acid (OHA) with carbohydrazide-modified collagen (Col-CDH), forming a stable network at physiological pH (7.4) that can be disrupted in an acidic environment (pH 6.8), characteristic of MI sites. This pH-sensitive bond enables the on-demand release of encapsulated therapeutic agents, such as metformin and mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes (MSC-Exos), precisely at the site of injury, maximizing their efficacy. To further enhance the functionality of the hydrogel, carboxylated multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) were incorporated into the formulation, providing a conductive network that optimizes the microenvironment for enhanced electrical conductivity. This conductive hydrogel not only supports the sustained and localized delivery of drugs but also facilitates synchronized myocardial contraction and promotes tissue regeneration by improving the mechanical and electrical properties of the damaged heart tissue [ ].
Stimuli-responsive hydrogels and injectable scaffolds act as dynamic, tissue-integrated catalytic depots. Their ability to sense pathological cues (e.g., pH, ROS) and stage-adaptively release therapeutics supports a phased treatment strategy—addressing acute inflammation followed by sustained regenerative signaling. This biomimetic approach bridges catalytic function with structural and electrical support for comprehensive cardiac repair.

## Surface engineering for endothelial targeting
Surface engineering is essential for directing nanomaterials to specific vascular endothelial sites in CVDs [ , ]. Surface modification of nanomaterials serves as a pivotal strategy to enhance their therapeutic performance in cardiovascular applications. Through precise tailoring of surface properties, nanoparticles can achieve extended blood circulation, targeted drug delivery, and multifunctional capabilities [ ]. For example, PEGylation, the covalent conjugation of polyethylene glycol chains, effectively mitigates opsonization and phagocytic clearance, prolonging systemic circulation and promoting passive accumulation at pathological sites via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect [ , ]. Furthermore, functionalization with specific ligands, such as antibodies, peptides, or polysaccharides, enables active targeting toward receptors upregulated on inflamed endothelial cells, activated platelets, or macrophages within atherosclerotic plaques or ischemic myocardium [ , ]. The primary strategy involves functionalizing nanoparticle surfaces with ligands that recognize endothelial biomarkers, particularly cell adhesion molecules like vascular cell adhesion molecule-1(VCAM-1) and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) [ – ]. This ligand-directed approach enhances cellular internalization and local drug bioavailability while minimizing off-target effects. Additionally, engineered surfaces can be designed to co-deliver combination payloads—including anti-inflammatory agents, antioxidants, and pro-angiogenic factors, enabling synergistic therapeutic outcomes [ ].
These surface engineering principles are translated into advanced nanocatalysts that combine targeting, prolonged circulation, and therapeutic action. A concrete example that integrates several of these principles is the VHPK-PLGA@COL nanoparticle. The synthesis of nanoparticles (VHPK-PLGA@COL) for targeted CVD therapy is a strategic advancement in drug delivery systems, particularly in the treatment of AS. These NPs are composed of poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) and poly (ethylene glycol) (PEG), both biocompatible and biodegradable components that contribute to the stability and prolonged circulation of the nanoparticles in the bloodstream [ , ]. The addition of PEG serves to protect the nanoparticle from rapid clearance by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), thereby enhancing the circulation time and bioavailability of the drug encapsulated within the particles. Furthermore, the surface of the nanoparticles is modified with the VHPK peptide, a molecular ligand that specifically targets VCAM-1 expressed on the surface of activated endothelial cells in atherosclerotic plaques. This modification ensures the precise targeting of inflammatory sites, thereby optimizing drug delivery to the affected areas and minimizing systemic off-target effects. The encapsulation of colchicine (COL) within the VHPK-PLGA@COL nanoparticles allows for controlled, sustained release of the drug at the site of action, which significantly enhances its therapeutic efficacy in reducing atherosclerotic burden while mitigating the adverse effects typically associated with COL, such as gastrointestinal disturbances and multiorgan toxicity [ ]. This targeted delivery system represents a promising approach to overcoming the clinical limitations of COL in the treatment of atherosclerotic CVDs, ensuring improved drug efficacy with reduced toxicity [ ].
In a nutshell, surface engineering confers spatial precision to nanocatalytic systems, enabling active targeting and prolonged circulation. By decorating nanoparticles with targeting ligands and polymers, this strategy ensures high local catalytic concentration at disease sites while minimizing systemic exposure. It transforms nanocatalysts into guided therapeutic vectors, maximizing efficacy and safety through intelligent biodistribution.

The therapeutic translation of polymer nanocatalysts is fundamentally demonstrated by their capacity to actively reprogram the complex, dynamic microenvironments underlying cardiovascular pathogenesis. ## Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) & ischemia–reperfusion injury
The MI microenvironment evolves through a dynamic, multi-stage pathological cascade. Following ischemic insult and reperfusion, a surge of ROS creates a state of profound oxidative stress. This directly triggers cardiomyocyte death through apoptosis, necrosis, and ferroptosis [ – ]. This necrotic damage initiates a robust inflammatory phase characterized by the infiltration of neutrophils and pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, which release cytokines that further propagate tissue injury and matrix degradation. The subsequent transition to the repair phase is often dysregulated, leading to excessive fibroblast activation, maladaptive extracellular matrix deposition, fibrosis, and adverse ventricular remodeling [ ]. At the same time, the infarct zone exhibits impaired electrical conduction and inadequate neovascularization, severely compromising functional recovery. In this context, nanocatalysts serve as versatile catalytic platforms to actively reprogram this hostile microenvironment. They can be engineered to deliver antioxidants to scavenge ROS, immunomodulators to shift macrophage polarization from a pro-inflammatory M1 to a reparative M2 phenotype, and pro-survival agents to mitigate cell death. Furthermore, they function as targeted carriers for pro-angiogenic factors to restore perfusion, and for anti-fibrotic or genetic therapeutics to inhibit maladaptive remodeling and promote the expression of reparative proteins [ , ]. By simultaneously addressing these interconnected pathological axes, nanocatalyst-based strategies catalytically reverse the detrimental infarct microenvironment, steering it toward a state conducive to functional tissue repair. Nanocatalysts can be engineered to deliver antioxidants and immunomodulators, demonstrating efficacy in animal models. However, the translatability of these findings to human patients is limited by species-specific differences in pathophysiology, as highlighted in the safety consideration section. The diverse mechanisms and comparative pharmacological effects of various nanocatalysts in the myocardial ischemic microenvironment are schematically contrasted in Fig.  .
One approach focuses on directly remodeling the infarcted tissue’s physical and biochemical environment. Zhang et al. designs FPDA hydrogel, which is engineered to catalytically remodel the hostile MI microenvironment by targeting three key pathological processes: excessive oxidative stress, impaired electrical conduction, and adverse inflammation. Its antioxidant components (α-tocopherol and polydopamine) synergistically scavenge ROS, while the conductive PEDOT:PSS network restores bioelectrical signal propagation [ , ]. Its compliant and adhesive properties provide mechanical support. In a rabbit MI model, the hydrogel demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy. Four weeks post-implantation, it markedly improved cardiac function, evidenced by increased left ventricular ejection fraction and fractional shortening. Treatment substantially reduced infarct size and fibrosis, promoted cardiomyocyte maturation (elevated α-actinin and Connexin-43), and enhanced angiogenesis within the infarct zone, confirming its potent role in facilitating structural and functional repair [ ].
In contrast, another strategy aims to modulate the neural regulation of the heart to prevent fatal arrhythmias. The Pt nanoshells (PtNP-shells) target the autonomic nervous system within the MI microenvironment via precise photothermal neuromodulation. Their near-blackbody absorption enables rapid and accurate temperature control, selectively activating TRPV1 ion channels in the nodose ganglion (parasympathetic) at 41.0–42.9 °C and TREK1 channels in the left stellate ganglion (sympathetic) at 45.0–46.9 °C [ , ]. In beagle dog models of ischemia–reperfusion injury and acute MI, this strategy demonstrated significant efficacy. Photothermal activation of the parasympathetic pathway reduced ventricular arrhythmias (VAs), lowered myocardial injury markers, and prolonged the ventricular effective refractory period. Conversely, photothermal inhibition of the sympathetic pathway prior to coronary ligation decreased the incidence of sustained ventricular tachycardia and fibrillation, reduced premature ventricular beats, and elevated the ventricular fibrillation threshold. These results validate the nanocatalyst’s ability to stabilize cardiac electrophysiology and protect against malignant Vas [ ].
A third therapeutic avenue simultaneously targets dysregulated immune cells and oxidative stress. The immunomodulatory nanoemulsion complexes (NE-ABR-Cu₂O) catalytically target two key pathological features of the MI microenvironment: excessive neutrophilic inflammation and oxidative stress. The nanoparticles disrupt the S100A8/A9-NLRP3-IL-1β signaling pathway in infiltrating neutrophils via the encapsulated inhibitor ABR2575, while integrated ultrasmall Cu₂O nanoparticles act as multi-enzyme mimics to scavenge diverse ROS. In a murine AMI model, this dual-targeting strategy demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy. Treatment markedly reduced infarct size and improved cardiac function, evidenced by increased left ventricular ejection fraction and fractional shortening. It effectively suppressed the infiltration of pro-inflammatory Ly6C-high monocytes and neutrophils into the infarcted heart, while promoting a reparative M2 macrophage phenotype. Furthermore, it attenuated myocardial apoptosis and inflammatory cytokine (IL-1β, IL-6, MCP-1) expression. Longitudinal studies confirmed sustained benefits, including inhibited adverse ventricular remodeling and fibrosis at 28 days post-MI, highlighting its robust cardioprotective potential [ ].

## Atherosclerosis stabilization
AS is primarily caused by abnormal lipid metabolism at the plaque sites, where the retention of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) in the arterial walls leads to its uptake by macrophages, resulting in foam cell formation [ ]. This process activates inflammation, further contributing to plaque formation, which is a key feature of early atherosclerotic lesions [ , ]. Current therapeutic approaches for atherosclerosis emphasize three main strategies: preventing foam cell formation, promoting cholesterol efflux, and mitigating inflammation [ ].
Consequently, genetic nanotherapeutic strategies are being developed to simultaneously tackle lipid accumulation, inflammation, and impaired cholesterol clearance within plaques. The siTTENPs platform is a state-of-the-art multivalent nanotherapeutic designed to target macrophages in the lesion sites, silence the TRPM2 gene, and enhance cholesterol efflux. In an ApoE − / − mouse model, siTTENPs treatment significantly reduced atherosclerotic plaque area, improved lipid metabolism, and decreased inflammatory cytokines. The siTTENPs group showed a 3.8-fold reduction in plaque burden compared to controls, alongside lower total cholesterol, LDL-C, and triglycerides, and higher HDL-C. In addition, histological analysis showed reduced cholesterol crystal content and improved macrophage polarization. These results suggest that siTTENPs effectively target atherosclerotic plaques, reduce plaque formation, and modulate lipid and inflammatory profiles, offering a promising strategy for atherosclerosis treatment [ ]. The in vivo anti-atherogenic efficacy of this platform, including reduced plaque burden, improved lipid profile, and attenuated inflammation, is comprehensively validated in Fig.  .
 Complementing genetic regulation, alternative designs directly manipulate the plaque’s biochemical landscape through integrated catalytic activities. In the atherosclerotic plaque microenvironment, the engineered nanomedicine Col@HMnO₂-MM exerts multifunctional catalytic activity against key pathological drivers: it efficiently scavenges ROS (H₂O₂, •OH, O₂⁻), decomposes H₂O₂ to generate O₂ and thereby alleviates hypoxia, leading to downregulation of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) [ , ]. This in turn upregulates the cholesterol transporters ABCA1 and ABCG1, promoting cholesterol efflux from macrophages. In APOE⁻/⁻ mice on a high-fat diet, treatment with Col@HMnO₂-MM significantly attenuated atherosclerosis progression, as evidenced by reduced plaque burden and lipid content, decreased serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, CRP), diminished macrophage infiltration, and enhanced plaque stability. The nanoparticle demonstrated synergistic efficacy superior to monotherapies, with no observable long-term toxicity, highlighting its potential as a targeted, multifunctional strategy for precise atherosclerosis therapy [ ].
Beyond modulating the plaque’s biochemical microenvironment, intercepting specific cell death signaling pathways offers another avenue to suppress inflammation and enhance plaque stability. Hu et al. constructs a CM@MS nanoparticle that targets key pathological processes in atherosclerosis. The nanoparticle catalyzes the release of Zn2⁺ from lysosomes by activating the MTC1 channel, thereby inhibiting the NLRP3/caspase-1/GSDMD pyroptosis pathway in macrophages. Additionally, its carbon nanodot component scavenges ROS, synergistically alleviating oxidative stress and inflammation within the plaque microenvironment. In ApoE⁻/⁻ mice fed a high-fat diet, treatment with CM@MS NPs significantly attenuated disease progression: it reduced aortic plaque area and lipid accumulation, decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β), suppressed macrophage infiltration and MMP-9 expression, and promoted M1-to-M2 macrophage repolarization. The platform also modulated adaptive immunity by reducing CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and increasing regulatory T-cells, demonstrating potent, multifaceted efficacy in stabilizing atherosclerotic plaques [ ].

## Thrombosis & ischemic stroke
Ischemic stroke (IS), primarily resulting from cerebral embolism, leads to impaired blood flow and oxygen deprivation, which in turn triggers ischemia–reperfusion injury and the generation of ROS [ ]. The accumulation of ROS worsens tissue damage through inflammatory responses and contributes to the "no-reflow" phenomenon, aggravating neuronal injury [ , ]. Traditional thrombolytic treatments such as tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) have limited effectiveness including hemorrhage and brain edema due to their brief half-life and potential side effects [ ]. To overcome these limitations, a recently developed therapeutic approach utilizing nanocatalytic therapeutics has been developed.
Moving beyond singular thrombolysis, advanced designs integrate catalytic neuroprotection with active targeting. These multifunctional nanocatalysts aim to concurrently dissolve clots, penetrate the blood–brain barrier, and mitigate reperfusion injury. The multifunctional peptide-templated MnO₂ nanozyme (PNzyme/MnO₂) precisely targets key pathological events: it binds fibrin via the CREKA motif, is thrombin-activated to release the thrombolytic peptide GRPAK, crosses the blood–brain barrier through T7-mediated transferrin receptor binding, and homes to apoptotic neurons via the CLEVSRKNC sequence. Parallelly, its cascade SOD- and CAT-mimicking activities potently scavenge reperfusion-induced ROS. In rodent stroke models, PNzyme/MnO₂ treatment markedly reduced cerebral infarct volume (from ~ 477.7 mm3 to ~ 20.1 mm3), significantly improved neurological function, shifted microglial polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, and attenuated pro-inflammatory cytokine release. The nanozyme demonstrated superior efficacy compared to standard therapies (tPA plus Edaravone), underscoring its potential as an integrated therapeutic agent that synergistically combines thrombolysis with neuroprotection for ischemic stroke treatment [ ]. These findings demonstrate PNzyme/MnO2’s promising neuroprotective properties, potentially enhancing recovery in IS through both thrombolysis and anti-inflammatory mechanisms.
Complementing enzymatic catalysis, biomimetic strategies leverage natural membrane coatings for precision. This approach combines cellular camouflage with controlled release of gaseous mediators and thrombolytic drugs, enabling synergistic intervention at the thrombus site. For example, the MPB-NO-UK@PM nanoparticle consists of a mesoporous MPB framework incorporated with sodium nitroprusside (SNP) for NO release, loaded with urokinase (UK) for thrombolysis, and coated with platelet membrane (PM) for targeted delivery and immune evasion [ , , ]. This biomimetic composite enables site-specific fibrinolytic therapy with enhanced stability and biofunctionality. In a rat carotid thrombosis model, MPB-NO-UK@PM under NIR irradiation achieved near-complete thrombolysis. The residual thrombus area was only 25.6 ± 3.7%, which was significantly smaller than that with UK monotherapy (67.2 ± 2.9%) or non-irradiated controls (44.4 ± 5.4%). This nanoparticle further reduced ROS and inflammation markers (TNF-α, IL-6), and notably prevented thrombosis recurrence, maintaining vessel patency without re-occlusion for 7 days post-treatment, demonstrating synergistic and sustained thrombolytic efficacy [ ].

## Heart failure
The heart failure is defined by a maladaptive microenvironment featuring compromised electrical conduction, sustained oxidative stress, low-grade chronic inflammation, and aberrant extracellular matrix remodeling [ ]. This pathological landscape disrupts electromechanical synchrony, induces cardiomyocyte apoptosis and hypertrophy, and activates fibroblasts, resulting in progressive interstitial fibrosis [ ]. The consequent impairment of gap junction–mediated intercellular communication further aggravates electrical instability and contractile dysfunction. In this context, electroactive nanocatalysts present a promising strategy to counteract these hallmarks through multienzyme-mimetic catalysis that attenuates oxidative injury, modulates dysregulated inflammatory signaling, and restores bioelectrical integrity [ ]. By directly scavenging ROS, resolving inflammatory cascades, and re-establishing conductive cell–cell interfaces, such nanocatalysts target the core drivers of adverse remodeling, thereby offering a catalytic approach to mitigate heart failure progression.
To address the dysregulated electromechanical and fibrotic microenvironment in heart failure, nanocatalytic strategies are designed to restore bioelectrical integration and modulate maladaptive remodeling. The inhalable cardiac-targeting peptide (CTP)-modified calcium phosphate (CaP) nanoparticle, TP-10@CaP-CTP, functions to precisely modulate the heart failure microenvironment by dual-targeting cardiomyocytes and cardiac fibroblasts under pathological conditions. It achieves site-specific delivery and release of the PDE10A inhibitor TP-10, thereby elevating intracellular cAMP and cGMP levels. This action attenuates cardiomyocyte hypertrophy via the cAMP/AMPK pathway and suppresses fibroblast activation, proliferation, and extracellular matrix synthesis via the cGMP/PKG pathway. In a pressure overload-induced heart failure mouse model, inhalation of TP-10@CaP-CTP significantly improved survival, reduced cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis, and enhanced both systolic and diastolic function, demonstrating superior therapeutic efficacy and biosafety compared to non-targeted formulations or intravenous administration [ ].
Beyond modulating intracellular signaling, cell-mimetic membrane coatings offer a biomimetic approach to enhance cardiac targeting and drug accumulation within fibrotic tissues. Li et. Al designed PM&EM/JQ1 NPs nanoparticles fabricated by coating JQ1-loaded cores, which play a pivotal role in treating and reversing fibrotic diseases, with fused membranes from platelets and erythrocytes [ ]. The platelet membrane confers active targeting to collagen and activated fibroblasts via surface glycoproteins, while the erythrocyte membrane prolongs systemic circulation. This biomimetic design enhances JQ1delivery to fibrotic cardiac tissues, improves local drug accumulation, and reduces systemic toxicity. PM&EM/JQ1 NPs exhibited remarkable therapeutic efficacy in a transverse aortic constriction (TAC)-induced heart failure murine model. Daily administration via tail vein injection significantly enhanced cardiac function, as evidenced by increased ejection fraction (58.49 ± 5.5%) and left ventricular fractional shortening (30.37 ± 3.44%), compared to free JQ1 (48.9 ± 5.16% and 24.25 ± 3.09%) or conventional JQ1 NPs. These results demonstrate that PM&EM/JQ1 NPs markedly improve systolic function and ameliorate cardiac remodeling, highlighting their potential as a targeted nanotherapeutic strategy for the treatment of heart failure and fibrosis [ ].

## Hypertension
The pulmonary hypertension (PH) microenvironment is characterized by pathological vascular remodeling involving endothelial dysfunction, abnormal proliferation and hypertrophy of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs), and perivascular inflammation with infiltration of immune cells such as macrophages, neutrophils, and T lymphocytes [ , ]. This microenvironment is further complicated by extracellular matrix deposition and a pro-thrombotic state. Building on their unique physicochemical properties, nanomaterials can catalyze therapeutic interventions within the PH microenvironment by primarily targeting its inflammatory and vasoconstrictive components [ ]. Functionally engineered nanoparticles can be designed to deliver anti-inflammatory agents (e.g., cytokine inhibitors or macrophage polarization modulators) directly to perivascular inflammatory sites, mitigating the release of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 from activated immune cells. Furthermore, nanocatalysts serve as excellent carriers for gas-releasing therapeutics, such as NO donors or carbon monoxide (CO)-releasing molecules [ , ]. These nanoformulations enable controlled, targeted release of vasodilatory gases at the diseased pulmonary vasculature, counteracting endothelial dysfunction and abnormal vasoconstriction. By combining anti-inflammatory action with localized gasotransmitter delivery, nanocatalyst-based strategies offer a dual-pathway approach to modulate the pathological PH microenvironment.
Within this hypertension management system, nanocatalysts are integral to the precise electrocatalytic generation and delivery of NO. Gold nanoparticles (Au NPs) and copper-nitrogen-doped carbon nanoribbons (Cu-NC NBs) function as highly efficient electrocatalysts, selectively producing NO for vasodilation. Concomitantly, platinum nanoparticles (Pt NPs) on the microneedle surface act as nanozymes to scavenge ROS, thereby minimizing side reactions and ensuring therapeutic efficacy and safety. This synergistic nano-material design enables intelligent, closed-loop blood pressure control [ ].
In addition, CaHF NPs demonstrate significant therapeutic potential in hypertension management through their potent reactive oxygen/nitrogen species (RONS) scavenging and anti-inflammatory properties. These NPs function as multi-enzyme mimetics, exhibiting superoxide dismutase, peroxidase, glutathione peroxidase, and catalase-like activities that collectively alleviate oxidative stress. In stress-induced hypertensive rat models, CaHF NPs effectively reduced blood pressure and suppressed renal sympathetic nerve activity. The underlying mechanism involves restoration of redox homeostasis, reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, inhibition of cellular apoptosis, and preservation of microglial cell functionality [ ] (Fig , Table  ).
 

## Safety & regulatory considerations

## Safety concerns
The primary challenges in terms of safety lie in ensuring biocompatibility and controlling potential toxicity. A comprehensive assessment of their biosafety must be conducted before clinical translation. Unlike natural enzymes, the metabolic pathways and in vivo fate of nanozymes remain unclear and require further investigation [ ]. This is particularly relevant for nanozymes which enter cells, as they may induce oxidative stress or inflammatory responses, potentially leading to chromosomal or DNA damage [ , ].
One of the most critical concerns is the potential accumulation of metal ions in the body. Many nanocatalysts are metal-based, and the long-term retention of these ions within tissues can lead to toxic effects [ ]. Additionally, the number of clinical trials investigating the use of nanocatalytic materials in CVDs remains relatively limited [ ].
While preclinical studies have shown promising results, more robust clinical trials are necessary to thoroughly evaluate the safety, efficacy, and long-term outcomes of these materials in human patients. Significant challenges impede the clinical translation of nanocatalysts. Key limitations include unresolved biosafety concerns, such as chronic toxicity and bioaccumulation of non-degradable components, potential immunogenicity of biomimetic coatings, and disruption of metal ion homeostasis [ ]. Manufacturing complexities hinder the reproducible scaling of nanocatalysts, with poor control over critical quality attributes like size and drug loading. Furthermore, relying too heavily on rodent models limits our ability to accurately predict human efficacy and chronic toxicity [ , ]. Furthermore, regulatory obstacles present another challenge, as the approval process for new nanomedicines can be both time-consuming and costly, delaying their potential introduction to clinical practice. Currently, the clinical translation landscape remains in its early stages. ## Solutions

## Advancing translational models
Overreliance on rodent models constrains the predictive accuracy of preclinical studies for human efficacy and chronic toxicity. To address this limitation, a systematic strategy integrating alternative small animal models and advanced in vitro platforms can be adopted. The zebrafish offers a valuable complementary model due to the high similarity in cardiac electrophysiology to humans, with a heart rate range of 120–180 bpm, closely resembling that of adult humans. This model facilitates real-time, visual assessment of cardiac dynamics, including heart rhythm and blood flow, in a living organism [ ]. Concomitantly, the development and standardization of sophisticated in vitro models can reduce initial dependence on whole-animal studies. Complex three-dimensional (3D) in vitro systems, such as cardiac organoids derived from human pluripotent stem cells, replicate key aspects of the human cardiac microenvironment. These organoids exhibit spontaneous contraction and multicellular architecture, enabling evaluation of nanocatalyst effects on rhythm, contractility, and long-term toxicity. Unlike traditional 2D cultures or animal models, 3D cardiac spheroids and organoids better mimic human tissue organization, electromechanical coupling, and functional responses, thereby mitigating species-specific discrepancies [ ]. Furthermore, perfusion-controlled 3D heart models can simulate dynamic pharmacokinetic conditions, while multi-organ chips—for instance, integrated liver–heart platforms—allow investigation of metabolite-induced cardiac toxicity, an aspect difficult to recapitulate in rodents [ ]. These human-relevant microphysiological systems represent a fundamental advance toward enhancing the translational reliability of preclinical nanocatalyst testing.

## Intrinsic safety by design
To ensure the biocompatibility and long-term safety of nanocatalytic therapies, adopting a “safety-by-design” philosophy is imperative. As demonstrated by Rotello et al. [ ], this can be achieved through the rational selection of biodegradable polymer backbones (e.g., PONI) and natural oil phases, coupled with the molecular-level incorporation of degradable linkers—such as disulfide or ester bonds—that are cleavable by endogenous biomolecules (e.g., glutathione or esterases). Such strategies fundamentally reduce the risk of long-term bioaccumulation associated with non-degradable nanocatalysts. Furthermore, comprehensive preclinical safety profiling should encompass multi-dimensional assessments, including cytotoxicity, hemocompatibility (e.g., hemolysis assays), and immunogenicity (e.g., inflammatory cytokine release). This rigorous, integrated approach establishes a reliable safety foundation to facilitate the clinical translation of nanocatalytic platforms.

## Toward clinical-safe nanocatalysis
To advance the clinical translation of nanocatalytic therapies, establishing a rigorous safety profiling framework is essential. A recent study exemplifies this approach by introducing metal-free nanozymes, thereby fundamentally circumventing the long-term toxicity risks associated with metal ion leaching [ ]. Furthermore, the study implemented a comprehensive biocompatibility assessment protocol, encompassing in vitro cytotoxicity, in vivo blood biochemistry and inflammatory cytokine profiling, and histopathological evaluation of major organs, which collectively validated the material’s excellent biosafety profile. Equally important is the adoption of a “gentle therapy” strategy, such as maintaining photothermal temperatures below 45°C. This paradigm shifts catalytic activity toward cytoprotective pathways, including autophagy, rather than exclusively inducing apoptosis, effectively broadening the therapeutic window while enhancing safety. Together, this integrated model provides a robust template for the systematic preclinical safety evaluation of nanocatalytic agents.

## Breakthrough &artificial intelligence for nanocatalytic therapeutics

## Benefits for nanocatalysts
Nanocatalytic materials, such as nanoparticles and nanostructured catalysts, have garnered attention for their potential to revolutionize cardiovascular therapy. One of the key advantages is their biocompatibility. Due to their nanometer-scale size and surface modifications, these materials are often less likely to induce toxic responses compared to conventional drugs or materials [ , ]. This makes them an ideal candidate for long-term applications, especially in the delicate environment of the cardiovascular system.
Moreover, targeted drug delivery is another significant benefit of nanocatalytic materials. Nanoparticles can be engineered to specifically target diseased tissues, such as those affected by atherosclerosis or MI, ensuring that therapeutic agents are delivered precisely to the site of action [ , , ]. This targeted approach not only enhances the therapeutic efficacy but also minimizes systemic side effects, which are often associated with traditional drugs. Additionally, nanocatalytic materials have demonstrated the ability to achieve sustained and controlled drug release with high flexibility. Through the design of nanoparticles with responsive surface properties, drugs can be released gradually over an extended period, maintaining therapeutic concentrations for longer durations [ , ]. This prolonged drug action is particularly beneficial in the context of CVDs, where consistent treatment is often required to prevent disease progression and manage symptoms. ## Engineered intelligent nanoparticle
The burgeoning integration of intelligent nanomaterial platforms is of notable impact. These platforms are poised to fundamentally reconceptualize therapeutic paradigms for CVDs. These systems transcend conventional nanomedicine by embodying dynamic functionality—engineered to sense and respond to the pathophysiological microenvironment with high specificity [ , ]. Through the incorporation of stimuli-responsive materials, they achieve precise spatiotemporal control, enabling targeted biodistribution to afflicted vasculature or myocardium, and subsequent release of therapeutics triggered by disease-specific cues such as localized pH shifts, enzymatic activity, ROS response or redox gradients [ – ]. This innate “intelligence” not only enhances intervention precision but also facilitates real-time diagnostic monitoring and feedback, effectively merging treatment and diagnostics into a unified theranostic strategy [ , ]. Consequently, these adaptive platforms offer a transformative approach to ameliorating complex cardiovascular conditions, promising improved efficacy and personalized patient management.
Capitalizing on the deep penetration and high spatial resolution of photoacoustic imaging, an intelligent theranostic nanoparticle has been designed for diagnosing and treating early-stage atherosclerosis through localized lipid modulation and removal. The system utilizes a π-conjugated polymer-based imaging probe that enables non-invasive and precise photoacoustic visualization of atherosclerotic plaques. Therapeutically, it incorporates a triple-function lipid-managing nanocomplex that concomitantly inhibits cellular lipid uptake, promotes cholesterol efflux, and facilitates extracellular lipid degradation, resulting in efficient regression of plaque lesions. This integrated platform provides a promising scientific basis and potential clinical strategy for atherosclerosis theranostics [ ] . Another study constructs three-dimensional flower-like cage structures from controllably-structured MOFs and iron oxide nanoparticles (FeNPs), which exhibit low thermal conductivity and pronounced photothermal conversion efficiency. Coupled with nano-matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry (NMALDI-MS), the platform allows high-throughput and high-accuracy metabolomic profiling of serum from patients with MI, HF or their combination. By applying fully connected neural network (FNN) and ensemble machine learning (EML) models to deeply analyze metabolic fingerprints, five potential biomarkers were identified with a validation accuracy of 98%, demonstrating an ingenious paradigm for the precision diagnosis of CVDs [ ].

## Conclusion and perspectives
Polymer-based nanocatalytic medicine represents a fundamental paradigm shift in cardiovascular therapeutics, moving from passive drug delivery toward active and localized catalytic intervention. This review systematically illustrates how the rational design of polymeric systems—including enzyme-mimetic networks, hybrid nanozymes, stimuli-responsive carriers, and dynamic hydrogels—enables spatiotemporally controlled catalytic reactions within complex pathological microenvironments. This strategy maximizes therapeutic efficacy by generating bioactive molecules in situ or efficiently neutralizing harmful species while significantly minimizing systemic off-target effects.
Current research has firmly established the considerable potential of polymer nanocatalytic platforms for targeting core pathological mechanisms in CVDs. Their value is multifaceted: enabling precise targeting and controlled release through surface engineering and intelligent responsiveness; integrating multiple functions such as catalytic ROS scavenging, anti-inflammation, pro-angiogenesis, and anti-fibrosis within a single platform for synergistic action; and supporting real-time monitoring and therapeutic adjustment through theranostic capabilities, thereby advancing personalized medicine.

## Current limitations and critical gaps
Despite this promising outlook, it is essential to recognize existing limitations and unresolved challenges. Beyond the frequently cited issues of long-term biosafety and scalable manufacturing, several critical gaps remain. First, the tension between catalytic specificity and system complexity has not been adequately addressed. Although designed to mimic enzymatic function, most nanozymes lack the high substrate specificity of natural enzymes. This raises concerns about off-target catalytic reactions in healthy tissues during prolonged or high-dose exposure. Second, the dynamic evolution of the disease microenvironment is often oversimplified. Many platforms are engineered to respond to a single static signal, whereas pathologies such as MI or atherosclerosis progress through distinct, temporally shifting phases. Existing designs seldom possess the adaptive intelligence required to modulate their function in alignment with this biological timeline. Third, there is an overreliance on phenomenological validation. Although numerous studies demonstrate that nanocatalysts can reduce infarct size or plaque burden, mechanistic insights frequently remain at the level of associated signaling pathways. A deeper, quantitative understanding of in vivo catalytic kinetics, the precise molecular targets of catalytically generated species, and their downstream systems-level effects is urgently needed.

## Concrete directions for future development
To overcome these bottlenecks and advance the field from proof-of-concept toward clinical translation, future research should converge on several strategic fronts. First, efforts must focus on developing truly intelligent and adaptive catalytic systems. Next-generation nanocatalysts should advance beyond single-stimulus responsiveness, prioritizing systems capable of integrating multiple biological inputs to execute staged, context-appropriate therapeutic actions. Second, safety-by-design must be prioritized, with clear metabolic clearance pathways established. This includes adopting fully degradable polymer backbones, exploring catalytic centers based on endogenous essential metals or entirely metal-free catalytic polymers to eliminate leaching risks, and incorporating activatable clearance mechanisms post-therapy. Furthermore, convergence with artificial intelligence and computational materials science should be accelerated. AI can aid in designing polymer sequences with tailored catalytic functions, predicting in vivo behavior, and analyzing complex multimodal data from theranostic platforms. In parallel, combining nanocatalytic activity with emerging modalities such as sonogenetics or electroceuticals may open new avenues for precise cardiovascular intervention. Finally, there is a pressing need to establish more human-relevant translational models. This requires the development and standardization of advanced human cell-based systems and the implementation of large-animal studies that better recapitulate human cardiovascular anatomy and pathophysiology as a new standard in preclinical evaluation.

## A pragmatic roadmap for clinical translation
The clinical translation of polymer nanocatalysts, while challenging, is achievable through a phased and collaborative strategy. Initial applications will likely focus on localized delivery scenarios—such as stent coatings, injectable hydrogels for myocardial repair, or catheter-based targeting of atherosclerotic plaques—to minimize systemic exposure. For systemic administration, rigorous pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies are essential. Crucially, a regulatory science framework for such actively responsive nanomedicines must be proactively developed in collaboration with regulatory agencies, with clear definition of critical quality attributes and standardized safety assessment protocols. Exploring synergistic combinations with established therapies, such as statins or antiplatelet agents, may also accelerate clinical impact by building upon known safety profiles.
In summary, polymer nanocatalytic medicine constitutes a dynamic and promising frontier in cardiovascular therapy. Its ultimate success depends on a shift from technology-driven exploration to clinically anchored development. By rationally addressing existing limitations, fostering interdisciplinary integration, and adhering to a pragmatic, safety-focused pathway, polymer nanocatalysis can evolve from a revolutionary laboratory concept into a cornerstone of future precision cardiovascular care, contributing decisively to alleviating the global burden of CVDs.
