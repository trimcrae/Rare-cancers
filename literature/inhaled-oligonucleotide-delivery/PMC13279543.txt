## Abstract

## Background
Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a progressive disease driven by pulmonary vascular remodeling, largely due to the abnormal proliferation and phenotypic switching of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells.

## Methods
Levels of topoisomerase II α were evaluated in the lungs from patients with idiopathic PAH, 2 rodent PAH models (SU5416 combined with hypoxia and monocrotaline‐induced), and in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells stimulated with platelet‐derived growth factor BB (PDGF‐BB). The therapeutic potential of amonafide, a topoisomerase II inhibitor, was also evaluated in these models. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses were used to identify amonafide‐regulated pathways. The role of neurotensin receptor 1 (NTSR1) was further investigated through overexpression and knockdown experiments in pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells.

## Results
Topoisomerase II α was significantly upregulated in the lungs of patients with idiopathic PAH, SU5416 combined with hypoxia‐, and monocrotaline‐induced rodent PAH models, and PDGF‐BB‐stimulated pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells. This upregulation was associated with increased DNA damage and apoptosis resistance. Amonafide treatment markedly improved hemodynamics, attenuated right ventricular hypertrophy, and suppressed pulmonary vascular remodeling in both animal models. In vitro, amonafide inhibited PDGF‐BB‐induced pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell proliferation, migration, apoptosis resistance, and DNA damage. Mechanistically, amonafide downregulated NTSR1 expression and inhibited the phosphoinositide 3‐kinase/protein kinase B/mammalian target of rapamycin  signaling pathway. Crucially, NTSR1 overexpression abolished the beneficial effects of amonafide, whereas NTSR1 knockdown enhanced them.

## Conclusions
Our findings unveil the pivotal role of amonafide in PAH pathogenesis and suggest that targeting topoisomerase II α and NTSR1 may be a promising therapeutic approach for treating PAH.

## Research Perspective

## What Is New?

Topoisomerase II α is identified as a key regulator of pathological vascular remodeling in pulmonary arterial hypertension.
The inhibitor amonafide ameliorated pulmonary arterial hypertension in preclinical models by targeting topoisomerase II α and its newly identified downstream effector, neurotensin receptor 1, to suppress pulmonary vascular cell dysfunction.

## What Question Should Be Addressed Next?

Critical questions for clinical translation include establishing a safe therapeutic window for chronic use and validating the long‐term efficacy in reversing established vascular remodeling.

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a progressive disease characterized by pulmonary vascular remodeling, increased pulmonary vascular resistance, elevated right ventricular afterload, and right heart failure. , , , The condition is defined as a mean pulmonary artery pressure >20 mm Hg, pulmonary vascular resistance >2 Wood units, and a pulmonary arterial wedge pressure of ≤15 mm Hg at rest. , , The pathophysiology of PAH is attributable to the multifactorial dysfunction of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs), , which is characterized by excessive proliferation, migration, resistance to apoptosis, and vasoconstriction. , , , Despite the focus of current therapeutic strategies on primarily targeting endothelium‐dependent pulmonary vasomotor tone through the prostacyclin, endothelin‐1, and nitric oxide pathways, , , patients with PAH still experience low survival rates. The principal reason is that these strategies predominantly facilitate vasodilation yet fail to effectively reverse pulmonary vascular remodeling, thereby conferring an unfavorable long‐term prognosis for patients with PAH. , 

Recent mechanistic studies have advanced our understanding of PASMC dysfunction in PAH. A pivotal study identified the YTH N6‐methyladenosine RNA binding protein F1 (YTHDF1)/DNA topoisomerase II α axis as a key regulator of pulmonary vascular remodeling. The study demonstrated that YTHDF1 mediates m6A modification of topoisomerase II α mRNA to enhance its translation, and elevated topoisomerase II α expression in PASMCs activates the phosphoinositide 3‐kinase/protein kinase B/mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K/Akt/mTOR) pathway, driving proliferation, migration, and inflammation, which are processes that exacerbate PAH. Recent studies have identified topoisomerase II α as a pivotal therapeutic target for reversing vascular remodeling in PAH.
Despite recent therapeutic advances, current PAH strategies continue to focus on endothelium‐dependent vasomotor tone (eg, prostacyclin, endothelin‐1, and nitric oxide pathways) and fail to address the underlying PASMC‐driven remodeling. Consequently, patients face poor long‐term survival, highlighting the urgency of novel therapies targeting molecular drivers of PASMC dysfunction such as topoisomerase II α. The phenotypic and molecular similarities between PAH and cancer, including uncontrolled cell proliferation, apoptosis resistance, and metabolic reprogramming, have spurred interest in repurposing antineoplastic agents for PAH. , , , Topoisomerase II α, an enzyme essential for DNA replication, is overexpressed in both cancer cells and PASMCs from patients with PAH and models, making it a promising cross‐therapeutic target.
Amonafide, a DNA intercalator with potent anticancer activity via topoisomerase II α inhibition, represents a novel candidate for PAH therapy. Characterized by its 3‐amino‐1,8‐naphthalimide scaffold, , amonafide induces cancer cell apoptosis by disrupting DNA double strands and inhibiting topoisomerase II α activity. , , , Critically, its role in PAH has never been investigated, which is an important gap our study addresses.
In this study, we demonstrate that amonafide attenuates platelet‐derived growth factor BB (PDGF‐BB)‐induced proliferation and migration of PASMCs and ameliorates hemodynamic parameters and vascular remodeling in PAH models. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses further identify neurotensin receptor 1 (NTSR1) as a key target of amonafide. These findings underscore the potential of amonafide as a novel therapeutic strategy for PAH.

## METHODS

## Data Availability Statement
The data underlying this article are available in the article and its online supplementary material. Raw microscopy images will be shared on reasonable request to the corresponding author.

## Human Lung Samples
Lung tissue samples from patients with idiopathic PAH (IPAH) and donor controls were obtained from Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital, Tongji University. IPAH was diagnosed in 4 individuals according to group 1 criteria after excluding other known causes of pulmonary hypertension. All patients underwent right heart catheterization and were demonstrated to have severe PAH. IPAH samples were collected during lung transplantation, whereas control samples were obtained from perinodular lung tissue of patients undergoing surgical resection for small pulmonary nodules. The study protocol for tissue biopsy was approved by the ethics committee of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital (approval number K23‐022Z) and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each individual patient or the patient's next of kin.

## Animal Experimental Design and Hemodynamic Measurements
Male Sprague‐Dawley rats (180–200 g) and C57BL/6J mice (18–22 g), aged 6 to 8 weeks, were purchased from Si Pei Fu Biotechnology (Beijing, China). All animals were housed in a carefully controlled specific pathogen‐free environment (constant temperature of 25 °C and 50% relative humidity) under a 12‐hour light/dark cycle, with unrestricted access to sterile food and water. All experimental procedures strictly adhered to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were ethically approved by the Ethics Committee of Henan University (approval number HUSOM2021‐058) in accordance with the European Parliament (2010/63/EU). Studies were performed in accordance with the Laboratory Animal Welfare Act, while adhering to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and Animals in Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments guidelines. No criteria were set for including or excluding animals. Due to the certain number of groups involved in the study, we conducted separate experimental sessions with distinct cohorts of mice or rats. Within each cohort, we evaluated the homogeneity of baseline performance and randomly allocated the animals to the experimental groups. Randomization was executed using a computerized random number generation method, independently of the study investigators. Throughout the study, all investigators remained blinded to the treatment conditions.
A SU5416 combined with hypoxia (SuHx)‐induced mice model of PAH (n=8 per group) and a monocrotaline‐induced rat model of PAH (n=12 per group) were used to assess the effects of amonafide on the development of PAH. In all experiments, littermates or vehicle‐treated animals served as controls. Specifically, male Sprague‐Dawley rats received subcutaneous injection of either 60 mg/kg monocrotaline (number C2401; Sigma‐Aldrich) or saline. From day 15, rats were administered daily intraperitoneal injection of amonafide (3 mg/kg, HY‐10982; MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) or equivalent volumes of sterile saline. For the SuHx‐induced mice model, male C57BL/6J mice were subjected to weekly subcutaneous injections of SU5416 (20 mg/kg body weight) or saline for 3 weeks, followed by 3 weeks of hypoxia exposure (10% O2). On day 15, the mice received daily intraperitoneal injections of amonafide (6 mg/kg) or saline. At the end of the 2‐week treatment period, the animals were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg), and a microtip pressure transducer catheter (1.2F for mice or 1.4F for rats; Millar Instruments) or a polyethylene 50 catheter for rats was carefully positioned in the right ventricle. Right ventricular systolic pressure and mean pulmonary artery pressure were monitored continuously for a period of 5 minutes using a PowerLab data acquisition system (AD Instruments, Colorado Springs, CO). Right ventricular hypertrophy was quantified through the right ventricular hypertrophy index, as determined by the ratio of right ventricle weight relative to left ventricle plus septum weight (right ventricle/left ventricle+septum). The right lungs were snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen for future Western blot and quantitative polymerase chain reaction analyses, whereas the left lungs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and paraffin‐embedded for histopathological examination.

## Histopathological Analysis
Lung tissues were sectioned into 4‐μm‐thick slices and then subjected to elastin‐van Gieson staining, adhering to an established protocol for detailed morphological examination. The external and internal diameters of the arteries were quantified by Image J software (Media Cybernetics), and pulmonary vascular remodeling was determined using the formula ([external vessel diameters–internal vessel diameters]/external vessel diameters)×100, as described in a previous study. 

## Immunofluorescence Analysis of Lung Tissue
The 4‐μm paraffin‐embedded lung tissue sections were deparaffinized using xylene and rehydrated by a graded series of ethanol solutions. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in citrate buffer (10 mM, pH 6.0) at 100 °C for 10 minutes. Subsequently, the sections were blocked with 5% BSA and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following incubation of the primary antibody, Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 594‐conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 1 hour at room temperature. Following a wash with PBS, the sections were stained with DAPI (C0060, 10 μg/mL; Solarbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China) for 10 minutes at room temperature in the absence of light. Imaging was conducted using an Olympus IX53 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The primary antibodies used in this study were anti‐von Willebrand factor (Abcam, Shanghai, China; ab287962, 1:50) and anti‐α‐smooth muscle actin (Abcam, Shanghai, China; ab7817, 1 μg/mL).

## Cell Culture
The primary rat PASMCs were isolated from adult Sprague‐Dawley rats as previously described, whereas the human primary PASMCs (hPASMCs) were obtained from iCell Bioscience (HUM‐iCell‐a009). The cells were cultured in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 U/mL streptomycin at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cell passages ranging from 3 to 6 were used for subsequent experimental procedures.

## Cell Viability Assays
To evaluate cellular viability, the culture supernatant was removed and substituted with a solution of methylthiazolyldiphenyl‐tetrazolium bromide (M8181, Solarbio, Beijing, China). The cells were incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 4 hours. Subsequently, the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 570 nm using a microplate reader.

## Cell Proliferation Assays
A total of 8×103 cells were seeded into each well of a 96‐well plate and allowed to adhere to the surface. After a 24‐hour period of serum starvation, the cells were subjected to the intervention. The 5‐ethynyl‐2′‐deoxyuridine solution was prepared in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, using a dilution ratio of 1:1000. Subsequently, 100 μL of the 5‐ethynyl‐2′‐deoxyuridine medium was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 hours. Following the incubation period, the medium was removed, and the cells were fixed, permeabilized, and washed according to the instructions provided with the reagent. Finally, Hoechst 33342 dye was added at a volume of 100 μL per well and incubated for 15 minutes before imaging with an Olympus IX53 fluorescence microscope (Olympus).

## Transwell Assays
To investigate cell migration, the culture supernatant was removed, and the cells were gently rinsed 3 times with PBS. Subsequently, the samples were dried and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes. Following this, the Transwell chambers (725 301; NEST Biotechnology, Jiangsu, China) were then stained with a 0.1% crystal violet solution (G1063; Solarbio, Beijing, China) for a further 30 minutes. After rinsing the Transwell chambers with tap water at a slow flow rate to remove any residual crystal violet staining, they were then air‐dried and documented using a microscope (Olympus IX53).

## Wound‐Healing Assay
Before a 24‐hour period of serum starvation, the wells of 6‐well plates were seeded with 8×104 PASMCs per well. Subsequently, the cell monolayer was subjected to a mechanical scratch and washed twice with PBS. Imaging of the scratches was conducted immediately after scratching (0 hours) and after a 24‐hour incubation period, and quantitative analysis of the migration data was performed using Image J software.

## Immunofluorescence Analysis of PASMCs

To evaluate the impact of amonafide on the PDGF‐BB‐induced hPASMCs phenotype, immunofluorescence staining was performed. Cell slides were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 minutes. Following 2 washes with PBS, the membranes were permeabilized with 0.05% Triton X‐100 for 10 minutes, and the sections were washed in PBS and blocked with 5% BSA before being incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used were anti‐osteopontin antibody (Proteintech, Wuhan, China; 22 952‐1‐AP, 1:50) and anti‐transgelin 22 (SM22) antibody (Proteintech, Wuhan, China; 60 213‐1‐Ig, 1:500). Subsequently, Alexa Fluor 647 or Alexa Fluor 594‐conjugated secondary antibodies were applied at 37 °C for 30 minutes. After washing with PBS, the cells were stained with phalloidin (200 nM; Solarbio, Beijing, China; CA1620) for 30 minutes without light at room temperature and then stained with DAPI for 10 minutes and mounted with an anti‐fluorescent attenuation sealing sheet (Solarbio, Beijing, China; S2100). The confocal microscopy images were performed on a Nikon AX confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

## 
Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase‐Mediated dUTP Nick End‐Labeling Staining
hPASMCs were seeded onto slides in 24‐well plates and cultured in complete DMEM medium containing 10% FBS at 37 °C and 5% CO2 in an incubator. Once the cells had reached 50% to 60% confluence, they were transferred to a serum‐free medium for 24 hours to synchronize the cell cycle. The cells were then randomly divided into the following experimental groups. The control group continued to be cultured in serum‐free medium for 24 hours. The PDGF‐BB induction group received recombinant human PDGF‐BB (final concentration: 20 ng/mL) in the serum‐free medium for 24 hours to create an in vitro proliferation model. After the experimental treatment, the medium was discarded, and the cells were gently washed 3 times with PBS. Then, 1 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde solution was added to each well, and the cells were fixed at room temperature for 30 minutes. After fixation, the cells were washed 3 times with PBS for 5 minutes each time. Apoptosis detection was performed using the CF488 Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase‐Mediated dUTP Nick End‐Labeling Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit from Wuhan Servicebio. All operations were performed strictly in accordance with the instructions, taking care to avoid light exposure. The confocal microscopy images were performed on a confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon, AX, Tokyo, Japan).

## Western Blot Analysis
Nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were extracted for subcellular fractionation using a commercial kit (Beyotime; P0027) following the manufacturer's protocol. Protein extracts were prepared from each group, and their concentrations quantified using the bicinchoninic acid assay. The protein samples were adjusted to uniform concentrations and boiled at 100 °C for 10 minutes with sample buffer. Subsequently, 20 μg of each protein extract was subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane via the wet transfer method. The membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 2 hours at room temperature, after which it was incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following antibodies: anti‐SM22 (60213‐1‐Ig), osteopontin (22952‐1‐AP), PCNA (proliferating cell nuclear antigen) (10205‐2‐AP), cyclin D1 (60186‐1‐Ig), mTOR (66888‐1‐Ig), GAPDH (60004‐1‐Ig), histone H3 (17168‐1‐AP), and β‐tubulin (10068‐1‐AP), which were obtained from Proteintech (Wuhan, China); anti‐topoisomerase II α (ET1607‐59), BCL‐2‐associated X protein (BAX) (ET1603‐34), B‐cell lymphoma 2 (BCL‐2) (HA721235), cyclin‐dependent kinase 1 (CDK1) (ET1607‐51), and cyclin‐dependent kinase 4 (CDK4) (ET1612‐23), which were obtained from Huabio (Hangzhou, China); anti‐survivin (38031ES60), which was provided by Yeasen (Shanghai, China); anti H2A histone family member X (H2AX) (bs‐2560R), which was obtained from Bioss (Beijing, China), anti‐phosphorylated H2AX (γ‐H2AX) (ES1326), which was provided by ELK Biotechnology (Wuhan, China); anti‐phosphoinositide 3‐kinase p85 alpha (PI3K p85 α) (GB11525), which was provided by Servicebio (Wuhan, China); anti‐phospho‐PI3K p85 α (Tyr607) (AF3241), which was obtained from Affinity (Jiangsu, China); and anti‐AKT (number 4691), p‐AKT (phosphorylated AKT) (Ser473) (number 4060), and p‐mTOR (phosphorylated mTOR) (Ser2448) (number 5536), which were provided by Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA). Following washing 3 times the membranes in Tris‐buffered saline with Tween 20 for 10 minutes each, they were incubated with secondary antibodies, anti‐rabbit immunoglobulin (Ig) G (number 7074; Cell Signaling Technology) or anti‐mouse IgG (number 7076; Cell Signaling Technology) conjugated to horseradish peroxidase in Tris‐buffered saline with Tween 20 at room temperature. GAPDH, histone H3, or β‐tubulin were used as internal references for semiquantitative analysis, and their relative content was expressed as a ratio. Densitometry analysis was performed using Image J software.

## 
RNA Isolation and RNA‐Sequencing Library Preparation, Sequencing, and Analysis
Total RNA was extracted from cell pellets using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Subsequently, RNA purity, concentration, and integrity were assessed using a Nanodrop‐2000 spectrophotometer. Starting with 3 μg of qualified RNA as input material, sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Preparation Kit for Illumina (NEB England BioLabs) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (performed by BioNovogene, Suzhou, China).
Analysis of the transcriptomic data proceeded as follows. First, principal component analysis was performed using the prcomp function in R (version 4.4.3) within the RStudio environment, based on the top 2000 highly variable genes. Data processing was performed using the tidyverse suite of packages. Visualization was accomplished by generating a parallel coordinate plot with the ggplot2 package to display the distribution of sample scores across the first 10 principal components. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the DESeq2 software (version 1.20.0). This software performed library size normalization on the raw read counts, calculated hypothesis test probability values via its generalized linear model, and applied the Benjamini‐Hochberg method for multiple hypothesis testing correction to control the false discovery rate. Genes meeting the criteria of an adjusted P value <0.05 and |log2FoldChange|>1 were defined as differentially expressed.
Based on the resulting DEGs list, functional enrichment analysis was further conducted using the clusterProfiler package (version 3.8.1). Gene Ontology enrichment analysis was performed, with Gene Ontology terms exhibiting an adjusted P value <0.05 considered significantly enriched. Similarly, enrichment analysis for the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was completed using the same software. Visualization of the results (eg, heatmaps) was implemented in the R environment using packages such as heatmap and ggplot2. 

## Metabolomics Analysis by Liquid Chromatography‐Mass Spectrometry
A metabolomic analysis of each group of cells was performed by BioNovogene (Suzhou, China). Briefly, cell precipitates were prepared for liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry in accordance with the standard protocols established by BioNovogene. Chromatographic separations were performed using a Thermo Vanquish system, equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column. Mass spectrometry experiments were conducted on a Thermo Q Exactive Focus mass spectrometer using spray voltages of 3.5 kV and −2.5 kV in positive and negative modes, respectively.
Following data acquisition, raw mass spectrometry files were converted to mzXML format using MSConvert within the ProteoWizard software package (version 3.0.8789). Subsequent data preprocessing, including peak detection, filtering, and alignment, was conducted with the R package XCMS (version 3.12.0), applying the following parameters: bandwidth=2, parts per million=15, peak width=c (5, 30), mass‐to‐charge ratio window width=0.015, mass‐to‐charge ratio difference=0.01, and method=centWave. A support vector regression‐based correction using quality control samples was then applied to minimize systematic variation, and metabolites exhibiting a relative standard deviation >30% across quality control samples were excluded to ensure data reliability.
For metabolite identification, spectral matching was performed against multiple public and in‐house databases, including Human Metabolome Database, MassBank, LipidMaps, mzCloud, KEGG, and a proprietary standard library, with a mass accuracy threshold of <30 parts per million. Level 1 identification was based on precursor ion *m/z* and adduct pattern from mass spectrometry1 spectra, whereas level 2 identification relied on matching experimental tandem mass spectrometry fragmentation patterns to reference spectra in the databases.
To identify differential metabolites, both unsupervised and supervised multivariate analyses were used. Principal component analysis was performed using R (version 4.4.3) within the RStudio environment. Model robustness was evaluated via permutation testing, with model fit summarized by R2X and R2Y (explained variance) and predictive capability indicated by Q2. Differential metabolites were selected by integrating univariate and multivariate criteria: a P value <0.05 from statistical hypothesis testing, a variable importance in projection score >1 derived from orthogonal partial least squares‐discriminant analysis, and the fold change between experimental groups. Metabolites satisfying both P<0.05 and variable importance in projection >1 were considered statistically significant biomarkers. Finally, functional interpretation of the differential metabolites was performed using MetaboAnalyst for pathway enrichment and topological analysis. Enriched metabolic pathways were visualized with the KEGG Mapper tool.

## Multi‐Omics Joint Analysis
The correlation between transcriptomics and metabolomics data was assessed using Pearson correlation analysis, and the corresponding P values were calculated. For all associated substances, dimensionality reduction algorithms were applied to extract representative features from each omics data set. The relationships between differential metabolites and genes were visualized with a heatmap. Subsequently, a logistic regression model was trained to classify the sample groups. The classification probability derived from the model was used to plot the receiver operating characteristic curves, and the area under the curve was calculated as the performance metric. Using the KEGG database, pathways that were coenriched by the differential genes and metabolites were identified.

## 
Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction Assays
The quantitative polymerase chain reaction assays were conducted in accordance with previously published methods. The total RNA extraction was prepared using TRIzol, following the manufacturer's instructions. The quantity of the RNA samples was determined using a Nanodrop‐2000 spectrophotometer. A transcription factor first‐strand cDNA synthesis kit (Vazyme RT kit; Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was used for the synthesis of cDNA templates from total RNA. The primer sequences used for quantitative polymerase chain reaction are provided in Table  . Quantitative polymerase chain reaction evaluation was performed using a real‐time polymerase chain reaction system (StepOnePlus; Vazyme) and a master mix (ChamQ Universal SYBR). The expression levels of the target genes were normalized using GAPDH as a reference. Gene expression levels were determined using the 2^−△△Ct method.

## Molecular Docking of Amonafide With NTSR1

To further investigate the potential interaction between amonafide and NTSR1, an in silico docking approach was used. Briefly, the crystal structure of NTSR1 (PDB ID: 8JPF) and the 3‐dimensional structure of amonafide (CID: 50515) were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) and the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), respectively. Using a cavity‐detection‐guided blind docking algorithm implemented via the Cavity‐Detection Guided Blind Docking web server (CB‐Dock2), the ligand amonafide was docked into the NTSR1 receptor. To comprehensively explore the conformational space of the binding interaction and ensure the robustness of the results, 5 independent docking simulations were performed for the receptor‐ligand pair. The binding interface and intermolecular interactions of the selected optimal complex conformation were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL (Molecular Graphics System) and LigPlot+ software. The predicted binding affinity of amonafide with NTSR1 was presented as kilocalories per mole, which is lower than the −5.0 kcal/mol that was considered as the stable binding between ligand and receptor. 

## Molecular Dynamics Simulation Protocol
Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted for NTSR1 with amonafide complexes using GROningen MAchine for Chemical Simulations 2023.2. The small molecule (amonafide) was preprocessed using Amber99sb‐ildb, applying the General Amber Force Field force field and adding hydrogen atoms. The restricted electrostatic potential charges for the small molecule were calculated using Gaussian 16W. The protein‐ligand system was modeled using the Amber99sb‐ildn force field. The system underwent energy minimization using the steepest descent algorithm, ensuring that any steric clashes or unrealistic geometries were resolved before further simulation. The equilibration process was then performed in 2 phases: 1 000 000 steps of NVT (constant number of particles, volume, and temperature) equilibration and 1 000 000 steps of NPT (constant number of particles, pressure, and temperature) equilibration. After equilibration, a 100 ns simulation was run with a 2 femtoseconds timestep, saving coordinates every 10 picoseconds. Upon completion of the molecular dynamics simulation, key parameters of the complex were computed using the built‐in GROningen MAchine for Chemical Simulations tool, including root mean square deviation, root mean square fluctuation, and hydrogen bond interactions.

## Overexpression of NTSR1

The hPASMCs were infected with adenovirus‐NTSR1 (Ad‐NTSR1) or Ad‐CONTROL at approximately 50% confluence and subsequently cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS for 6 hours. After serum starvation for 24 hours, the cells were treated with recombinant human PDGF‐BB (220‐BB; R&D Systems) or vehicle or PDGF‐BB+amonafide for an additional 24 hours. Subsequently, the cells were used for cell viability, proliferation, and migration assays as described above.

## 
NTSR1 Small Interfering RNA Transfection
To validate the role of NTSR1 in mediating the therapeutic effects of amonafide in PAH, we performed NTSR1 knockdown in hPASMCs. According to the manufacturer's standard protocol, NTSR1 small interfering RNA or CONTROL small interfering RNA (50 nmol/L) were transiently transfected into cultured hPASMCs with 60% confluence by using lipofectamine 2000 (KGA9704‐1.5; Keygenbio, Jiangsu, China). After transfection for 6 hours, the medium was replaced by amonafide solution (3 μmol/L) or PDGF‐BB (20 ng/mL) in DMEM followed by the treatment mentioned earlier. Cell proliferation, viability, and migration were assessed by 5‐ethynyl‐2′‐deoxyuridine, Transwell, wound‐healing assay, and methylthiazolyldiphenyl‐tetrazolium bromide, respectively, as described earlier.

## Statistical Analysis
Continuous data are presented as mean±SEM. For comparisons between 2 independent groups, the exact Mann‐Whitney U test was used. This test is a form of permutation test based on the distribution of the ranks, and no large‐sample approximations were used. To support the robustness and transparency of the Mann‐Whitney U test results, permutation tests with 10 000 random shufflings were performed to generate empirical null distributions. Comparisons among >2 groups were performed using 1‐way analysis of variance with Tukey post hoc test for multiple pairwise comparisons or the Kruskal‐Wallis test with the Dunn post hoc test, as appropriate. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (Dotmatics).
DEGs analysis was performed using the R package DESeq2 (version 1.20.0). DEGs were identified with the following criteria: a false discovery rate‐adjusted P value <0.05 using the Benjamini‐Hochberg method, and |log2FoldChange| >1. All raw P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the method described above. For metabolomic data, metabolites with a variable importance in projection score >1 from the orthogonal partial least squares‐discriminant analysis model and a P value <0.05 from univariate analysis were considered significant.

## RESULTS

## Topoisomerase II α Is Upregulated in Lungs of IPAH, Experimental Rodent PAH Models, and PDGF‐BB‐Induced PASMCs

To investigate the alteration of topoisomerase II α in PAH, we examined its expression in human lung tissues. Immunofluorescence and Western blot revealed significantly higher topoisomerase II α protein levels in lungs from patients with IPAH compared with healthy donors (Figure  and ). To extend this clinical observation, we systematically evaluated topoisomerase II α expression in experimental models. In a SuHx mouse model of PAH that was confirmed by elevated right ventricular systolic pressure, increased right ventricular hypertrophy index, and medial wall thickening of pulmonary vessels (Figure  ), topoisomerase II α protein was significantly upregulated in lung tissues (Figure  and ). Similarly, in monocrotaline‐induced rat PAH model (Figure  ), topoisomerase II α expression was markedly elevated in the lungs (Figure  and ). In vitro, stimulation of synchronized human and rat PASMCs with PDGF‐BB robustly induced topoisomerase II α protein expression (Figure  through ). In PDGF‐BB‐stimulated hPASMCs, this upregulation was accompanied by increased levels of the DNA damage marker γ‐H2AX and the anti‐apoptotic proteins BCL‐2 and survivin, along with decreased proapoptotic BAX and a reduction in terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase‐mediated dUTP nick end‐labeling‐positive cells, indicating concomitant DNA damage and apoptosis resistance (Figure  through ). Together, these findings indicate that topoisomerase II α undergoes significant changes during PAH progression and may play a critical role in regulating pulmonary vascular remodeling.
 

## Amonafide Attenuated Pulmonary Vascular Remodeling in a SuHx‐Induced PAH Mice Model
The in vivo therapeutic effect of the topoisomerase II α inhibitor amonafide was evaluated using the SuHx‐induced PAH mice model (Figure  ). Amonafide significantly reduced right ventricular systolic pressure in SuHx‐induced PAH mice (Figure  and ), accompanied by a marked reduction in right ventricular hypertrophy index (Figure  ), medial thickening of small arteries (Figure  and ), and the proportion of fully muscularized small vessels in SuHx‐induced mice (Figure  and ). In addition, amonafide suppressed proliferation and cell‐cycle progression in lung tissues from SuHx mice, evidenced by decreased PCNA, cyclin D1, CDK1, and CDK4 expression (Figure  and ). Furthermore, Western blot analysis revealed that amonafide treatment significantly increased the protein levels of BAX, while decreasing the expression of BCL‐2, survivin, and γ‐H2AX, indicating that amonafide ameliorated apoptosis resistance and DNA damage in the lung tissues of the SuHx‐induced PAH model (Figure  and ). As such, these findings collectively imply that amonafide represented a promising strategy for preventing SuHx‐induced PAH.
 

## Amonafide Mitigated Pulmonary Vascular Remodeling in Monocrotaline‐Induced PAH Rat Model
We further evaluated amonafide in monocrotaline‐induced rat model of PAH (Figure  ). Treatment with amonafide significantly attenuated the monocrotaline‐induced increases in right ventricular systolic pressure, mean pulmonary artery pressure, and right ventricular hypertrophy index (Figure  through ). This effect was not observed in rats receiving amonafide following intraperitoneal saline injection. Consistently, amonafide reduced pulmonary vascular remodeling, evidenced by decreased medial wall thickness and muscularization of distal arterioles (Figure  through ). Molecular analysis of lung tissue revealed that amonafide downregulated the proliferation marker PCNA, as well as key cell‐cycle proteins including cyclin D1, CDK1, and CDK4 in lung tissues of monocrotaline‐treated rats (Figure  through ). Furthermore, amonafide reversed apoptosis resistance (increased BAX; decreased BCL‐2 and survivin) and mitigated DNA damage (decreased γ‐H2AX) in the monocrotaline model (Figure  and ). These results demonstrate that amonafide effectively ameliorates monocrotaline‐induced PAH by improving hemodynamics, attenuating vascular remodeling, and correcting underlying molecular dysregulation in proliferation, apoptosis resistance, and DNA damage.
 

## Amonafide Suppressed the Proliferation, Migration, Apoptosis Resistance, and DNA Damage in PDGF‐BB‐Stimulated PASMCs

We next evaluated the effect of amonafide on PDGF‐BB‐stimulated hPASMCs and found that it significantly inhibited their proliferation and migration (Figure  through ). Similarly, amonafide elicited a substantial decrease in cell proliferation and migration in rat PASMCs following PDGF‐BB stimulation, consistent with observations in hPASMCs (Figure  ). Furthermore, amonafide promoted a phenotypic shift from a synthetic to a contractile state in hPASMCs, as indicated by immunofluorescence analysis (Figure  ). Western blot analysis confirmed this phenotypic modulation, showing that amonafide downregulated the synthetic marker osteopontin, while upregulating the contractile marker SM22 (Figure  and ). Concomitantly, amonafide treatment reduced the expression of the proliferation marker PCNA and the key cell‐cycle regulator cyclin D1 (Figure  and ). Amonafide also counteracted PDGF‐BB‐induced apoptosis resistance, evidenced by increased levels of the proapoptotic protein BAX and decreased levels of the anti‐apoptotic proteins BCL‐2 and survivin. Additionally, it attenuated DNA damage, as indicated by reduced γ‐H2AX expression (Figure  and ). Together, these findings demonstrate that amonafide ameliorated PDGF‐BB‐induced proliferation, migration, phenotypic switching, apoptosis resistance, and DNA damage in PASMCs.
 

## Transcriptome and Metabolomic Analyses of Amonafide‐Mediated Inhibition on PDGF‐BB‐Induced Proliferation and Migration of PASMCs

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying amonafide's inhibition of PAH progression, we performed integrated transcriptomic (RNA‐sequencing) and metabolomic profiling in PDGF‐BB‐stimulated hPASMCs treated with or without amonafide. Principal component analysis of transcriptomic data revealed clear separation between the PDGF‐BB and PDGF‐BB+amonafide groups (Figure  ). Correlation analysis of the significantly DEGs revealed their potential involvement in analogous biological processes, underscored by strong correlations and consistent trends (Figure  ). We identified 2255 DEGs (1242 upregulated and 1013 downregulated; |log2 foldchange|>1, adjusted P value <0.05) upon amonafide treatment (Figure  ). Subsequent Gene Ontology and KEGG pathway analyses revealed an enrichment of 407 Gene Ontology entries, with notable differences in biological processes such as nuclear division and chromosome segregation, and mitotic nuclear division. The DEGs exhibited notable enrichment in cellular components, including the chromosomal region, chromosome, centromeric region, and kinetochore. In contrast, molecular function was predominantly linked to DNA‐dependent adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) activity and single‐stranded DNA‐dependent ATP‐dependent DNA (Figure  ). Furthermore, the KEGG enrichment analysis revealed 319 significantly enriched metabolic pathways, with the cell cycle emerging as a particularly prominent feature (Figure  ).
 Furthermore, we quantified and compared the metabolites in cells subjected to amonafide intervention versus PDGF‐BB treatment using liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry, which effectively classified the metabolites into 2 distinct groups through principal component analysis (Figure  and ). A total of 55 differential metabolites were identified, comprising 20 that were upregulated and 35 that were downregulated (Figure  ). Subsequent to the coordinated Z score profiles of these metabolites point to their functional association in common biological processes (Figure  ). These metabolites were enriched in pathways involved in biosynthesis and energy metabolism, such as arginine biosynthesis and central carbon metabolism in cancer (Figure  ). Collectively, these multi‐omics profiles demonstrate that amonafide broadly modulates gene expression networks centered on cell‐cycle regulation and concurrently reshapes metabolic pathways in PASMCs.

## Amonafide Suppressed the Proliferation and Migration of PASMCs Through Downregulating the Expression of NTSR1

To identify downstream effectors of amonafide, we integrated our transcriptomic and metabolomic data sets. Correlation analysis revealed a strong association between DEGs and altered metabolites (Figure  and ). Joint pathway analysis implicated these molecules in 84 distinct metabolic pathways (Figure  and ). We focused on the most enriched pathway, neuroactive ligand‐receptor interaction, and selected candidate genes whose expression changes induced by PDGF‐BB were reversed by amonafide treatment (CHRNA1, NTSR1, GRID1, CHRNA5, F2RL1, CALCRL) (Figure  ). Subsequently, quantitative polymerase chain reaction validation identified NTSR1 as the most significantly downregulated gene upon amonafide exposure in PDGF‐BB‐stimulated hPASMCs (Figure  ). We further examined NTSR1 expression in clinical specimens and found that its protein level was significantly elevated in lung tissues from patients with IPAH compared with healthy donors (Figure  ). Consistently, NTSR1 protein levels were elevated in lung tissues from both monocrotaline‐ and SuHx‐induced rodent models of PAH and in PDGF‐BB‐stimulated PASMCs, and this upregulation was effectively attenuated by amonafide treatment (Figure  ).
 Finally, molecular docking analysis revealed that amonafide could effectively interact with NTSR1, with the optimal binding mode illustrated in Figure  . The calculated binding energy between amonafide and the active sites of NTSR1 was −8.2 kcal/mol. Additionally, amonafide formed several hydrogen bonds with specific amino acid residues of NTSR1, including arginine (166), valine (308), tyrosine (364), leucine (108), asparagine (360), valine (159), leucine (162), serine (163), leucine (305), and valine (367) (Figure  ). To further validate the stability and conformational dynamics of the NTSR1–amonafide complex, we performed a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation based on the docking results. The root mean square deviation, a key metric for assessing structural stability, showed initial fluctuations but converged to around 0.35 nm after 50 ns, indicating that the complex reached a stable binding conformation (Figure  ). Analysis of the root mean square fluctuation revealed relatively low fluctuations in peptide residue mobility, suggesting that the complex maintained structural rigidity and reduced flexibility upon ligand binding (Figure  ). Furthermore, we calculated the Gibbs free energy landscape using GROningen MAchine for Chemical Simulations scripts g_sham.py and xpm2txt.py, based on root mean square deviation and radius of gyration values. The resulting free‐energy profile displayed a deeply embedded low‐energy basin, reflecting high thermodynamic stability and consistent convergence of the system throughout the simulation (Figure  and ). These simulation results corroborate the molecular docking prediction, demonstrating that amonafide forms a stable and compact complex with NTSR1. Together with the observed downregulation of NTSR1 expression both in vitro and in vivo, these data identify NTSR1 as a direct target of amonafide and suggest that modulation of the NTSR1 pathway contributes to its therapeutic effects on pulmonary vascular remodeling in PAH.

## Amonafide Inhibited the PI3K/AKT/mTOR Signaling Pathway and Improved Pulmonary Vascular Remodeling
NTSR1, a member of the G protein‐coupled receptor family, has been shown to facilitate cell growth and metabolic reprogramming via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in various disease models, including tumors. , However, it remains unclear whether NTSR1 regulates pulmonary vascular remodeling in PAH through a similar mechanism. Western blot analysis revealed that phosphorylation of PI3K, AKT, and mTOR was significantly increased in PDGF‐BB‐stimulated PASMCs and in lung tissues from both SuHx and monocrotaline rodent models. Amonafide treatment effectively suppressed this hyperactivation (Figure  ). However, small interfering RNA‐mediated knockdown of NTSR1 in proliferating PASMCs did not reduce AKT phosphorylation, demonstrating that NTSR1 is not a requisite upstream regulator of AKT in this context (Figure  ). Therefore, the inhibition of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway by amonafide is independent of its downregulation of NTSR1. Instead, this effect is likely a direct consequence of its primary pharmacological action as a topoisomerase II α inhibitor. These results define a dual mechanistic framework for amonafide; it directly targets topoisomerase II α to alleviate DNA damage and consequent PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway hyperactivation, while concurrently downregulating NTSR1 to disrupt its distinct proproliferative signaling network.
 

## Amonafide Attenuated NTSR1‐Overexpression‐Mediated hPASMCs Proliferation, Migration, Apoptosis Resistance, and DNA Damage Under PDGF‐BB Stimulation
To determine whether NTSR1 mediates the therapeutic effects of amonafide, we performed gain‐of‐function studies in hPASMCs. Adenoviral overexpression of NTSR1 (Ad‐NTSR1) (Figure  through ) significantly potentiated PDGF‐BB‐induced proliferation and migration, as measured by methylthiazolyldiphenyl‐tetrazolium bromide, 5‐ethynyl‐2′‐deoxyuridine incorporation, Transwell, and wound‐healing assays. Amonafide treatment effectively suppressed these NTSR1‐driven enhancements (Figure  through ). Furthermore, NTSR1 overexpression exacerbated the phenotypic transition of PDGF‐BB‐stimulated hPASMCs from a contractile to a synthetic state, as evidenced by increased expression of the synthetic marker osteopontin and decreased levels of the contractile marker SM22. Notably, amonafide administration attenuated these NTSR1‐mediated changes (Figure  and ). Concomitantly, NTSR1 overexpression amplified PDGF‐BB‐induced apoptosis resistance (decreased BAX, increased BCL‐2 and survivin) and DNA damage (increased γ‐H2AX). Again, amonafide counteracted these molecular alterations (Figure  and ). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that NTSR1 is a functional mediator of pathological PASMC activation and that amonafide attenuates proliferation, migration, phenotypic switching, apoptosis resistance, and DNA damage, at least in part by antagonizing NTSR1 signaling.
 

## 
NTSR1 Knockdown Potentiated the Therapeutic Effects of Amonafide on PDGF‐BB‐Induced Proliferation, Migration, Apoptosis Resistance, and DNA Damage in hPASMCs

To further define the role of NTSR1, we performed loss‐of‐function studies using small interfering RNA‐mediated knockdown of NTSR1 in hPASMCs (Figure  and ). Functional studies demonstrated that amonafide alone significantly inhibited PDGF‐BB‐induced proliferation and migration, and NTSR1 knockdown potentiated these inhibitory effects (Figure  through ). In addition, amonafide markedly alleviated the PDGF‐BB‐induced phenotypic transition of hPASMCs from a contractile to a synthetic state, as evidenced by decreased expression of the synthetic marker osteopontin and increased levels of the contractile marker SM22. Importantly, NTSR1 knockdown further promoted this phenotypic reversal (Figure  and ). At the molecular level, the combination of amonafide and NTSR1 small interfering RNA produced a greater correction of PDGF‐BB‐induced dysregulation in apoptosis resistance and DNA damage markers than either intervention alone, leading to a more pronounced increase in BAX and greater decrease in BCL‐2, survivin, and γ‐H2AX (Figure  and ). These results demonstrate that NTSR1 knockdown synergizes with amonafide to potently inhibit pathological PASMC activation, defining it as a key therapeutic target through which amonafide improves pulmonary vascular pathology in PAH.
 

## DISCUSSION
PAH is a life‐threatening cardiovascular disease characterized by progressive pulmonary vascular remodeling and right heart failure. , , , Accumulating evidence implicates dysregulated cell proliferation, migration, and survival of PASMCs as central drivers of vascular remodeling in PAH. , , , , , , This study systematically elucidates the critical role and therapeutic potential of topoisomerase II α and its inhibitor amonafide in PAH. Furthermore, we identify a novel mechanism involving NTSR1 and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis.
Topoisomerase II α is a key regulator of cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and cell cycle progression, , and has been implicated in lung pathologies including cancer and injury. , , Its upregulation has been documented in patients with PAH and experimental pulmonary hypertension models. , , , , We found that topoisomerase II α expression was significantly upregulated in lungs from patients with IPAH, experimental rodent PAH models induced by monocrotaline or SuHx, and PASMCs stimulated with PDGF‐BB. Notably, we found that PDGF‐BB‐induced PASMCs not only exhibited hyperproliferation but also showed elevated levels of the DNA double‐strand break marker γ‐H2AX, increased anti‐apoptotic proteins BCL‐2 and survivin, decreased pro‐apoptotic protein BAX, and a reduction in terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase‐mediated dUTP nick end‐labeling‐positive cells. This evidence collectively indicates that PASMCs under PAH pathological conditions sustain significant DNA damage and acquire a cancer‐like phenotype of apoptosis resistance, wherein the upregulation of topoisomerase II α likely plays a key role.
Amonafide, a DNA‐intercalating agent that disrupts topoisomerase activity, possesses established anticancer properties. , , , Yet, its pharmacological effect in PAH remains unclear. We demonstrate here that amonafide significantly attenuated pulmonary vascular remodeling in both monocrotaline‐ and SuHx‐induced PAH models in vivo. At the cellular level, amonafide robustly suppressed PDGF‐BB‐induced PASMC proliferation and migration, and reversed the pathological shift from a contractile to a synthetic phenotype. More importantly, amonafide treatment significantly alleviated the apoptosis resistance and DNA damage observed in both in vivo and in vitro models. These findings extend amonafide's efficacy beyond mere proliferation inhibition to a broader context of correcting the apoptosis‐proliferation imbalance and genomic instability.
Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses identified NTSR1 as a key downstream effector of amonafide. The neurotensin/NTSR1 axis, implicated in cancer progression and recently in PAH pathogenesis via activating transcription factor 6 activation, was found to be upregulated in PAH and suppressed by amonafide. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulations have been instrumental in providing robust structural biological evidence for the direct interaction between amonafide and NTSR1, forming a stable complex with high affinity, as indicated by a binding energy of −8.2 kcal/mol, maintaining conformational stability throughout 100 ns of simulation. This theoretically suggests that amonafide may function as a novel NTSR1 antagonist.
The PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway is a well‐established regulator of PASMC proliferation and migration. Previous studies have demonstrated that topoisomerase II α can activate this pathway in various pathological conditions, particularly in cancer. Therefore, as a topoisomerase II α inhibitor, amonafide may exert its protective effects by suppressing the activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, thereby attenuating excessive PASMC proliferation and migration. Our study revealed that amonafide significantly suppressed the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in lung tissues from SuHx‐ and monocrotaline‐induced rodent models of PAH, and in PASMCs stimulated with PDGF‐BB. Notably, knockdown of NTSR1 did not attenuate AKT phosphorylation, indicating that NTSR1 does not directly regulate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. Thus, the inhibition of PI3K/AKT/mTOR by amonafide is independent of NTSR1 and is likely mediated through its primary role as a topoisomerase II α inhibitor. These findings support a dual mechanism of action for amonafide; it directly targets topoisomerase II α to alleviate DNA damage and inhibit the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, which is crucial for cell growth and survival, while also independently modulating NTSR1 to interfere with its specific proproliferative signaling network.
Furthermore, both gain‐of‐function (overexpression) and loss‐of‐function (knockdown) experiments have confirmed the pivotal role of NTSR1 in phenotypic regulation. The overexpression of NTSR1 has been shown to enhance proliferation, migration, phenotypic switching, and apoptosis resistance in PDGF‐BB‐induced PASMCs, as well as exacerbate DNA damage. Furthermore, it has been observed to counteract the therapeutic benefits of agents like amonafide. Conversely, knockdown of NTSR1 mimicked and further enhanced the therapeutic effects of amonafide. This complementary genetic evidence robustly substantiates that NTSR1 serves as the critical target mediating amonafide's amelioration of pulmonary vascular remodeling.
Although our findings demonstrate the therapeutic promise of amonafide in PAH, its clinical translation faces clear challenges. As an antineoplastic agent, amonafide carries known toxicity risks, such as bone marrow suppression, which require careful evaluation in the context of long‐term PAH treatment. , Subsequent research should focus on optimizing the therapeutic window through dose exploration and developing targeted delivery systems, such as inhaled formulations, to enhance pulmonary specificity and reduce systemic exposure. Furthermore, combining amonafide with existing PAH medications represents a promising direction. Its dual mechanism of action may synergize with standard drugs, including endothelin receptor antagonists and phosphodiesterase‐5 inhibitors, potentially improving efficacy while allowing for reduced dosages of individual agents. Future work is needed to clarify these synergistic mechanisms and establish feasible clinical combination regimens.

## CONCLUSIONS
This study identifies topoisomerase II α as a key regulator in PAH pathogenesis, which is consistently upregulated in human tissues and experimental models. We show that its inhibitor, amonafide, attenuates vascular remodeling and improves hemodynamics in rodent PAH models. Mechanistically, amonafide targets NTSR1, suppressing the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. This dual action reverses hallmark pathological features in PASMCs, including hyperproliferation, phenotypic switching, apoptosis resistance, and DNA damage. These findings reveal a novel topoisomerase II α/NTSR1 signaling axis in PAH and support the further development of amonafide as a promising therapeutic candidate.
