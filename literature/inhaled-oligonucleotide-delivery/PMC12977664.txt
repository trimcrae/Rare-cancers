However, their expanding application has raised concerns regarding biosafety, particularly their potential threat to maternal and fetal health. To evaluate this risk, this study established a pregnant rat model, focusing on the placenta as a potential target organ, to investigate the developmental toxicity and potential interventions associated with the use of TiO₂ nanozymes (TiO₂ NZs) as therapeutic agents during pregnancy. The results revealed that gestational intake of TiO₂ NZs led to fetal growth restriction, abnormal placental weight increase, and induced placental energy metabolism disruption along with excessive autophagy activation. Surprisingly, when attempting to reverse these toxic effects, we found that TiO₂ NZs suppressed AMPK expression, prompting Compound C and phenformin to unconventionally regulate energy imbalance-induced autophagy via non-AMPK/mTOR pathway-dependent mechanisms. This resulted in a complex scenario where the two drugs produced inverted effects-“aggravation” vs. “alleviation”-during intervention. These findings indicate that despite the significant medical value of TiO₂ as a nanozyme, they pose non-negligible safety risks, and pharmacological interventions may trigger unexpected effects. Therefore, while advancing their clinical application, it is crucial to prioritize in-depth mechanistic studies and the development of precise intervention strategies, especially ensuring the long-term health and safety for maternal and fetal populations.

## Introduction
Titanium dioxide nanozymes (TiO₂ NZs) have emerged as a typical material in the field of nanotechnology [ ]. Their exceptional photocatalytic performance and stability have elevated them beyond ordinary nanomaterials, establishing them as a highly sought-after nanozyme with extensive applications in catalysis, energy, and environmental remediation [ ]. Nanozymes are nanomaterials with intrinsic enzyme-like catalytic activities, whose catalytic efficiency and selectivity are strongly influenced by substrate adsorption/recognition at surface active sites. In particular, engineering artificial substrate-binding/recognition sites (e.g., via a “modular structure” strategy) has been proposed as an effective way to enhance substrate specificity of nanozymes [ ]. Nanomaterial-enabled photodynamic therapy has been extensively investigated using diverse nanoplatforms, where tumor targeting and tumor microenvironment–responsive designs are widely adopted to improve accumulation and therapeutic specificity [ ]. TiO₂ NZs are regarded as a highly promising novel therapeutic strategy in various areas, including photodynamic/photothermal therapy for tumors [ ] and interventions for inflammatory diseases (such as oral inflammation and colitis) [ ], largely due to their efficient catalytic activity. In addition, TiO₂-based nanozymes have been reported as glucose-responsive therapeutic platforms, including applications in diabetic wound healing [ ].
However, while research enthusiasm is intensely focused on exploiting their therapeutic value, a critical issue has been relatively overlooked, such as the systematic assessment of their biosafety, particularly the long-term effects following their application as nanomedicines in humans. These safety concerns have not been fully addressed. In fact, TiO₂ already enters our bodies through a more ubiquitous and intimate route: as a food additive (E171) [ ], it is widely used in products like candies, chewing gum, and toothpaste [ ]. Despite its prevalence, a rigorous and comprehensive evaluation of its health risks to humans, especially vulnerable populations such as pregnant women and fetuses, remains lacking.
Current evidence estimates that adult daily intake of TiO₂ ranges from 0.2 to 0.7 mg/kg, while a higher level for children [ ]. For pregnant women, TiO₂ entered through diet and inhalation is almost unavoidable [ ]. More alarmingly, the placenta, the critical barrier guarding fetal development, has been identified as a target for TiO₂ invasion [ ]. In vitro studies using 3D placental models show that TiO₂ can traverse and compromise the structural integrity of the placental barrier [ ]. Animal studies further reveal that orally administered TiO₂ accumulate in placental tissue, leading to impaired placental development, functional disruption, and ultimately, adverse pregnancy outcomes [ , ]. Although these phenomena have been observed, the molecular mechanisms by which TiO₂ NZs induce placental dysfunction and fetal developmental abnormalities, as well as strategies for effective intervention, remain a “black box” urgently requiring investigation.

 

To solve this problem, we aimed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying TiO₂ NZs-induced placental dysfunction and fetal growth restriction. We focus on the roles of disrupted-energy metabolism and autophagic dysregulation, with particular emphasis on the central role of the AMPK/Akt-mTOR signaling axis. A key objective is to decipher the paradoxical effects of AMPK-targeting pharmacological interventions. Preliminary evidence of TiO₂ treatment suggests their actions may bypass canonical AMPK pathways, and a schematic diagram of the proposed mechanism is shown in Fig.  . These findings from this study are expected to provide crucial mechanistic insights into the developmental toxicity of this prominent nanozyme and reveal how they induced metabolic stress “hijacks” canonical signaling networks, thereby offering a solid theoretical foundation for assessing its risks to maternal-fetal health.

## Materials and methods

## Materials
Titanium dioxidenanozymes (TiO₂ NZs, CAS No. 13463-67-7, 718467) and bulk titanium dioxide (b-TiO₂, CAS No. 1317-80-2, 224227) were commercially available from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Compound C (an AMPK inhibitor, HY-13418 A) and phenformin (an AMPK activator, HY-16397) were bought from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA).Bafilomycin A1 (B1793) and chloroquine (C6628) were gained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).Primary antibodies of AMPK (ab32047), p-AMPK (ab23875), Akt (ab8805),andp-Akt (ab38449)were supplied by Abcam (Cambridge, UK).The secondary antibody produced by CY3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (ab6939) is a product of Abcam (Cambridge, UK).Primary antibodies against mTOR (2972), p-mTOR (5536), and LC3A/B (4108) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA).And the primary antibody of GAPDH (AF1186),BCA protein assay kit (P0010S), ATP assay kit (S0026), Trizol reagent (R0016), and cDNA synthesis kit (D7168M) were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Lipofectamine 2000 (11668019) was acquired from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA).

## TiO₂ NZs characteristics measurement and dispersion
All characteristics of TiO₂ NZs and b-TiO₂ were measured according to our previous report [ ]. In detail, TiO₂ NZs and b-TiO₂ were added to double-distilled water or complete medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11875093), vortexed at high speed, sonicated (100 W, 30 min), and diluted for better dispersion before analysis. For cell treatment, TiO₂ NZs and b-TiO₂ were suspended in the medium to prepare a stock solution (1 mg/ml), and gradually diluted to the designated concentrations. For the animal study, the stock solution was dissolved in 0.5% methylcellulose (Sigma-Aldrich, M7027) according to our previous study [ ], and diluted to the designated concentrations before application. All solutions were freshly prepared and sonicated before diluting or administrating to animals and cells.

## Animals and treatments
Adult Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were sourced from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All animal procedures were conducted strictlycompliance with the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals (State Council, China) and were approved by the Animal Ethical Committee of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No.: IACUC24040115). Female rats were paired with males at a ratio of 1:2 after one week of rest and adaptation. The day when the vaginal plug appeared was recorded as gestation day (GD) 0.5. TiO₂ NZs or b-TiO₂ administration began from GD 5 to GD 18, with a dose of 5 mg/kg/day via gavage, based on environmentally relevant exposure level reported in humans [ ]. The females were euthanized on GD 18, the pregnancy outcomes were recorded, and the placenta autophagy was examined. For therapeutic treatment, Compound C and phenformin were orally administrated at the doses of 0.2 mg/kg/day or 2 mg/kg/day, respectively, starting from GD 7, according to previous reports [ , ]. The control rats were administrated with 0.5% methylcellulose or saline solution according to the body weight. The pregnancy outcomes were recorded, and the tissues were collected for further examination after the female rats were euthanized following intraperitoneal injection of 4% pentobarbitalon GD 18.

## Cell culture and treatment
The HTR-8/SVneo (HTR) cell line was procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC CRL-3271, Manassas, USA). Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11875093), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, 10099141 C) and penicillin–streptomycin solution (Gibco, 15140122, 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin), at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. The culture medium was refreshed daily. The HTR cells were treated with TiO₂ NZs (100 µg/ml) for 24 h, and Compound C (10 µM), as well as phenformin (1 mM), was separately added to the treated cells for another 24 h. Bafilomycin A1 (10nM) and chloroquine (40µM) were employed as positive controls to inhibit autophagic flux in cells [ , ].

## Transmission electron microscope (TEM) analysis
Treatment with TiO₂ NZs or b-TiO₂ (100 µg/ml) was administered to the cells for 24 h once they reached 60% confluence. After harvest, cells were fixed overnight using 1% glutaraldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 16220) and washed twice with phosphate buffer. Osmium tetroxide (1%) (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 19171) was used for post-fixation, and a graded acetone solution (Sigma-Aldrich, 179124)was used for dehydration. Following embedding in Araldite resin (Araldite 502 and EMbed 812 kit, Electron Microscopy Sciences, 13940), the cell pellets were processed into ultrathin sections. These sections were then stained with lead citrate (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 17800)and uranyl acetate (Electron Microscopy Sciences, 22400) for examination of cellular ultrastructure using a Hitachi H7600 transmission electron microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

## Immunofluorescence
Cells were plated on glass-bottomed dishes (In Vitro Scientific) and subjected to respective treatments for 24 h. Subsequently, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, P0099-100 ml) for 30 min and permeabilized with Triton X-100 (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, P0096-100 ml). Autophagosomes were labeled by sequential incubation with an anti-LC3 primary antibody (4108, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA)overnight at 4 °C and a CY3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Abcam, UK, ab6939). Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, C1002) (EX/ES: 358 nm/461 nm), and imaging was performed on a Carl Zeiss LSM 880 confocal laser scanning microscope (Jena, Germany).

## Earle’s balanced salts solution (EBSS) induced autophagy
HTR cells were maintained in complete medium and collected upon proper density. Then the cells were washed and suspended with Earle’s Balanced Salt Solution (EBSS) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 24010043). After the cells were seeded into the culture dishes at a proper density and cultured for another 6 h, cell autophagy levels were assessed via immunofluorescence using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 880, Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany).

## Western blotting
After a 24 h treatment, cells were harvested and lysed for 10 min using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, P0013B). The lysates were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatants were collected for total protein quantification with a commercial BCA assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, P0010S). Concurrently, placental tissues from the maternal side were collected and rinsed with pre-cold PBS to remove residual blood. Then lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, P0013B) was added and incubated for 30 min. Following sonication on ice, protein concentrations were determined using the BCA assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, P0010S). The protein expression levels were determined with specific primary antibodies as follows: anti-AMPK antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK, ab32047), anti-p-AMPK antibody (Abcam, ab23875), anti-Akt antibody (Abcam, ab8805), anti-p-Akt antibody (Abcam, ab38449), anti-mTOR antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA, 2972), anti-p-mTOR antibody (CST, 5536), and anti-LC3A/B antibody (CST, 4108). The following detection was performed using an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Beyotime Biotechnology, A0208). GAPDH (Beyotime Biotechnology, AF1186) served as the loading control protein. Protein expression levels were semi-quantified with Image-J software (NIH, USA).

## Intracellular and placental ATP measurement
The cells were cultured and treated with TiO₂ NZs or b-TiO₂ for 24 h before collection. Intracellular ATP levels were measured by using a commercial ATP assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, S0026). Cellular ATP concentrations were indicated by the fluorescence intensity resulting from the firefly luciferase-catalyzed fluorescent reaction under ATP conditions. Placental ATP levels were examined in freshly collected placenta samples, and total protein concentration was used to standardize the ATP content. ATP levels in the prepared samples were measured using the above methods.

## Small interfering RNA (siRNA) synthesis, overexpression vector construction and transfection
The siRNAs targeting the Akt and AMPK genes were synthesized by Invitrogen (Shanghai, China). For AMPK overexpression plasmid construction, the gene sequence was obtained from PubMed (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/). The gene was cloned into a plasmid vector with a FLAG tag, purified and amplified for the following research. For transfection, siRNAs and plasmids were delivered into HTR cells using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA, 11668019) as per the manufacturer’s protocol, with an empty plasmid vector serving as the negative control. When the cells reached proper densities, 50 nM siRNAs or plasmids as well as their corresponding negative controls, were added into the cell medium. Following 6–12 h of incubation, the medium was refreshed, and protein expression was assessed by Western blotting.Akt siRNA: 5’-CGGAGAAGAACGUGGUGUATT-3’ and non-targeting control: 5’-UACACCACGUUCUUCUCCGTT-3’; AMPK siRNA: 5’-CCUUGAUGUGGUAGGAAAATT-3’ and the corresponding non-targeting control: 5’-UUUUCCUACCACAUCAAGGTT-3’.

## Realtime PCR (RT-PCR)
RT-PCR was employed to assess the mRNA expression levels of AMPK, Akt and GAPDH genes after cells were transfected with their corresponding siRNAs, as well as the overexpression vector of AMPK. Total RNA was isolated from cells using Trizol reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, R0016), followed by cDNA synthesis with a commercial kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, D7168M). Quantitative PCR was subsequently performed on an ABI 7900 system (USA). The primers used for gene amplification were listed as follows: AMPK Forward: ACAGCCGAGAAGCAGAAACA, Reverse: CTTCACTTTGCCGAAGGTGC; Akt Forward: CAGGATGTGGACCAACGTGA, Reverse: AAGGTGCGTTCGATGACAGT; GAPDH Forward: CAAATTCCATGGCACCGTCA, Reverse: AGCATCGCCCCACTTGATTT. mRNA levels were normalized to GAPDH (GAPDH serving as the internal control gene), and the 2^(–ΔΔCT) method was used to calculate relative expression.

## Molecular docking
The initial structure of Akt1 (PDB: 4EJN) was retrieved from the Protein Data Bank. Molecular docking was performed with the CDOCKER protocol in Discovery Studio. Prior to docking, the protein structure was prepared by adding hydrogens, assigning bond orders, removing extraneous water molecules, and incorporating missing atoms with partial charges. The co-crystallized ligand’s position defined the active site. Ligands (Compound C and phenformin) were energy-minimized using the MMFF force field. All visualizations were generated within Discovery Studio.

## Statistical analysis
All data are presented as mean ± SD and were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 5.0. Intergroup differences were assessed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test, with a P < 0.05 considered as statistically significant.

## Results

## Characterization of TiO₂ NZs and effects of TiO₂ NZs on birth outcomes
The morphology of TiO₂ NZs and b-TiO₂ in double-distilled water was observed using a transmission electron microscope (TEM) (Fig.  A). Both the TiO₂ NZs and b-TiO₂ were nearly spherical, with the primary diameter of approximately 21 nm and 0.2-1.0 μm, respectively, which are consistent with the manufacturers’ report. Figure  B showed the average diametersand size distributions of both TiO2 NZsand b-TiO2 in double distilled water. The results indicated that the average hydrodynamic diameter of TiO2 NZs was about 39.99 ± 1.57 nm, whereas that of b-TiO2 was significantly larger (747.7 ± 15.6 nm).Correspondingly, their polydispersity index (PDI) values were 0.336 and 0.444, respectively. The zeta potentials of the two materials were also determined. TiO₂ NZs exhibited a zeta potential of approximately + 20.43 ± 0.4 mV, whereas b-TiO₂ showed a value of about − 32 ± 1.6 mV.And the purity of these two kinds of particles was all above 99% as the manufacturer’s report.
The overall procedure of animal study was illustrated in Fig.  C with 8 pregnant rats in each group. We found that maternal weight showed no difference between the control and b-TiO₂ groups, whereas maternal weight significantly increased following TiO₂ NZs treatment (Fig.  D). Meanwhile, TiO₂ NZs significantly reduced the total fetal weight (P < 0.05), average fetal weight (P < 0.01) and average fetal length (P < 0.001), while these parameters remained unchanged in the b-TiO₂-treated group (Fig.  E). In contrast, no significant difference in fetal number was observed among the three groups (Fig.  F), but fetal size in TiO₂ NZs-treated group was smaller (Fig.  G).
The placenta is one of the most critical organs influencing fetal growth. Despite similar placental sizes observed in all three groups (Fig.  G), placenta weight was markedly increased in the TiO₂ NZs group (P < 0.001, Fig.  H), accompanied by signs of placental congestion.Further analyses revealed that ATP content was markedly reduced (P < 0.01, Fig.  I) and the LC3-II/I ratio was markedly elevated (P < 0.001, Fig.  J) in the TiO₂ NZs group, while no change in ATP was found in the b-TiO₂ group, suggesting that TiO₂ NZs led to energy deficiency and autophagy.

 

Considering that b-TiO₂ had no significant effects on pregnancy and birth outcomes, Compound C and phenformin were administered to TiO₂ NZs-treated rats to reverse the TiO₂ NZs-induced energy-related adverse pregnancy and birth outcomes. Representative images of fetuses from each group are shown in Fig. A.Compared to the control group, TiO₂ NZs significantly reduced fetal length and weight (P < 0.01). These changes were mitigated by phenformin (P < 0.05), but exacerbated by Compound C (P < 0.05). It is well known that Compound C inhibits AMPK to reduce autophagy [ ], while phenformin acts in the opposite way [ ]. Intriguingly, our results showed controversial changes in both the Compound C and phenformin-treated groups. Specifically, Compound C accelerated adverse birth outcomes, whereas phenformin reversed adverse birth outcomes. To reveal the possible reasons behind this controversy, HTR-8/Svneo (HTR) cells, a placenta-derived cell, were employed for further study.

## Effects of compound C/phenformin on TiO₂ NZs and b-TiO₂-induced autophagy
To further confirm that the controversial effects were caused by TiO₂ NZs instead of b-TiO₂, HTR cells as a model cell line were chosen for further study by monitoring autophagy and intracellular ATP levels. As shown in Fig.  B-C, both TiO₂ NZs and b-TiO₂ elevated autophagy levels relative to the control group. Specifically, a significant reduction in ATP level was observed in TiO₂ NZs-treated group (P < 0.001), whereas the b-TiO₂ group showed no change. Further studies were followed subsequently.
Immunofluorescent results showed that the autophagosome accumulated after TiO₂ NZs and b-TiO₂ treatment. In TiO₂ NZs-exposed cells, autophagy was surprisingly increased when Compound C was added, while decreased by phenformin treatment. However, when Compound C or phenformin was added to the b-TiO₂ groups, the phenomenon was opposite (Fig.  D-E). Based on the TiO₂ treatment-mediated different changes, the following studies were focused on TiO₂ NZs. Figure  F shows the TEM images, which demonstrate that TiO₂ NZs were internalized by HTR cells and primarily located in the cytoplasm. Swollen mitochondria were more frequently observed in cells treated with TiO₂ NZs than in control cells (Fig.  G), these changes were mainly energy-related.

 

Furthermore, we found that autophagosome accumulation was increased in TiO₂ NZs-exposed cells after adding bafilomycin A1 (Fig.  A). Compound C promoted the accumulation of autophagosomes in TiO₂ NZs-exposed cells, and this effect was enhanced by bafilomycin A1 (Fig.  B). Chloroquine also promoted the accumulation of autophagosomes in TiO₂ NZs-exposed cells, but this effect was inhibited by phenformin (Fig.  C). These findings indicate that Compound C and phenformin have opposing effects on the autophagic changes induced by TiO₂ NZs. In addition, to verify the contrary effects of Compound C and phenformin after TiO₂ NZs treatment, these two drugs were used to treat control cells and starvation-induced cell models, respectively. We observed that Compound C and phenformin increased autophagy in the absence of TiO₂ NZs (Fig.  D-E), but in starvation condition Compound C treatment could reduceautophagy (Fig.  F). Consistent with AMPK activation upon starvation, Compound C treatment reduced autophagy levels, indicating a potential dependence on AMPK activity. The contrary effects observed in TiO₂ NZs treated cells could be related to AMPK dysfunction.

 

## Mechanism analysis of compound C/phenformin on TiO₂ NZs and b-TiO₂-induced autophagy
To elucidate the mechanism of AMPK dysfunction induced by TiO₂ NZs, we assessed the expression of key proteins by western blotting. As shown in Fig.  A, TiO₂ NZs significantly downregulated the levels of AMPK, p-AMPK, mTOR, and p-mTOR (P < 0.01). The expression of AMPK was inversely related to autophagy activation, implying that the autophagy induced by TiO₂ NZs was not regulated by the AMPK/mTOR pathway. Furthermore, direct comparison between treatment groups demonstrated that, though co-treatment with Compound C further reduced p-AMPK levels, and co-treatment with phenformin increased p-AMPK expression compared to the TiO₂ NZs-only group, the changes between these two groups were not significant.These results suggest that the interaction between AMPK and Compound C was weakened due to the lower expression. To test this hypothesis, Compound C/phenformin was individually added to control cells, in which AMPK protein was normally expressed, and the change in p-AMPK levels was significantly evaluated. Compound C and phenformin differentially modulated AMPK phosphorylation without altering total AMPK levels: p-AMPK was significantly decreased by Compound C and increased by phenformin (P < 0.01) (Fig.  C-D).

 

Given that TiO₂ NZs downregulated AMPK, we hypothesized that Compound C and phenformin might shift their targeting to Akt. To validate this hypothesis, we performed molecular docking analysis, which revealed that Akt was a potential target of Compound Cindependent of AMPK. Most Akt inhibitors are ATP-competitive inhibitors that bind to the ATP binding sites of Akt and interfere with its biological functions (Fig. E). Additionally, a novel inhibition mode for Akt1 has been reported, which could stabilize the auto-inhibited conformations of kinases (Fig. F) [ ]. Among these molecules, AKT1-4EJN shares a similar nucleus structure with Compound C, so we hypothesized that Compound C may act as an auto-inhibition inductive inhibitor. The molecular docking method was used to explore the molecular mechanism. Compound C was docked into the binding site formed by two Akt1 molecules (Fig. G) with similar conformation as the AKT1 inhibitor complex (PDB code: 4EJN; Fig. H). Further docking analysis was also carried out between phenformin and Akt. As shown in Fig. I, phenformin was docked at the binding site of a single Akt molecular, and the overlapping images (Fig. J) showed the relative binding sites of both Compound C and phenformin with Akt(s). Docking at the similar sites on Akt1, Compound C and phenformin shared an overlapping region (indicated by the blue frame). The upper interaction region was specific to Compound C (Fig. K-L), suggesting that Compound C may have a different role from phenformin in regulating Akt activity. In addition, molecular docking revealed a higher binding affinity for Compound C (-9.869 kcal/mol) than for phenformin (-8.683 kcal/mol), as indicated by the glide score (Fig. M).

## Effects of compound C/phenformin on the Akt/mTOR pathway after TiO₂ NZs treatment
To evaluate Akt as a candidate target of Compound C and phenformin, we assessed its expression following TiO₂ NZs treatment. The results showed that levels of both total Akt and p-Akt remained unaltered (Fig.  A).Based on the hypothesis that Akt is a potential target of Compound C or phenformin following treatment with TiO₂ NZs, the Akt expression was examined when Compound C/phenformin was added to control cells. Compound C or phenformin did not significantly alter total Akt expression, whereas p-Akt levels were significantly decreased by Compound C and increased by phenformin compared with the control (Fig.  B–C). Then the expression of the Akt pathway was further examined after Compound C/phenformin was added following TiO₂ NZs. As shown in Fig.  D-E, total Akt expression remained unchanged across groups, whereas p-Akt levels were significantly decreased by Compound C and increased by phenformin in the presence of TiO₂ NZs (P < 0.001).Meanwhile, mTOR expression was markedly reduced following TiO₂ NZs treatment (P < 0.001), and remained unchanged when Compound C or phenformin was subsequently added. In contrast, co-treatment with Compound C or phenformin correspondingly decreased or increased p-mTOR levels relative to the TiO₂ NZs-only group (P < 0.01), implicating the Akt/mTOR pathway in TiO₂ NZs-induced autophagy. In addition to vs. control comparisons, the p-Akt and p-mTOR levels in the “TiO₂ NZs + Compound C” group were significantly decreased relative to the “TiO₂ NZs” group alone (#P < 0.05, ##P < 0.01), whereas the “TiO₂ NZs + phenformin” group showed a significant increase in these phosphorylated proteins ($P < 0.05, $$P < 0.01 vs. TiO₂ NZs group). This direct contrast visually reinforces that when AMPK is downregulated by TiO₂ NZs, Compound C inhibits while phenformin activates the Akt/mTOR pathway, mechanistically explaining their opposing effects on autophagy regulation.

 

Autophagy levels were examined when AMPK expression was inhibited by a specific siRNA to confirm that Akt was an alternative target of Compound C and phenformin. Figure  F showed that AMPK expression was significantly reduced at both the mRNA and protein levels following transfection with AMPK siRNA (P < 0.001). Furthermore, the autophagy level was substantially reduced in the AMPK siRNA group compared to the control. When Compound C was subsequently added, the autophagy level was then increased. However, when phenformin was used, the autophagy level was decreased (Fig.  G). To further verify that the contrary change in autophagy was caused by AMPK dysfunction, an AMPK overexpression vector was used to enhance the AMPK expression after TiO₂ NZs treatment. Figure  H indicated that when AMPK expression was up-regulated using the overexpression vector in TiO₂ NZs-treated cells (P < 0.001), the autophagy level initially increased and then decreased after the addition of Compound C (Fig.  A). When Compound C was replaced with phenformin after AMPK expression was enhanced, the autophagy level changed in the opposite way (Fig.  B). To directly test Akt serves as the alternative target mediating the opposing effects of Compound C and phenformin under AMPK-deficient conditions, we knocked down Akt expression using siRNA in TiO₂ NZs-treated cells. The corresponding siRNA was used for further study to identify the role of Akt after TiO₂ NZs treated. The results showed that upon Akt knockdown in TiO₂ NZs-treated cells (Fig.  C, P < 0.001), the autophagy level was increased. Following Akt knockdown, the regulatory effects of Compound C and phenformin on autophagy were attenuated or abolished. This serves as one of the most direct pieces of evidence supporting that Akt acts as an alternative target. After Compound C was subsequently added, the autophagy remained unchanged and was lower than that in the TiO₂ NZs + Compound C group, where Akt was normally expressed. Although phenformin did not significantly alter autophagy following Akt knockdown in TiO₂ NZs-treated cells, the level remained elevated compared to the TiO₂ NZs + phenformin group (Fig.  D).

 

## The relationship of AMPK and mTOR pathway re-regulates autophagy
To investigate whether the AMPK/mTOR pathway regulates autophagy upon AMPK enhancement in TiO₂ NZs-treated cells, we performed western blotting. After restoring AMPK expression, Compound C suppressed p-AMPK and activated p-mTOR, whereas phenformin elevated p-AMPK, leading to mTOR inhibition and reduced p-mTOR (P < 0.001; P < 0.01; Fig.  A-B).

 

To verify the mechanism in the animal models, key proteins were examined in the placenta tissues of each group. Each western band was a combinational sample of 5 randomized tissues. As shown in Fig.  C, TiO₂ NZs treatment did not alter AKT/p-AKT expression but significantly downregulated AMPK, p-AMPK, mTOR, and p-mTOR (P < 0.05). A concomitant increase in the LC3-Ⅱ/Ⅰ ratio (P < 0.05) indicated a high level of induced autophagy. Critically, direct statistical comparison between intervention groups showed that TiO₂ NZs + Compound C further suppressed p-mTOR compared to TiO₂ NZs alone (P < 0.05), while TiO₂ NZs + phenformin significantly restored p-mTOR levels relative to the TiO₂ NZs-only group (P < 0.05). These in vivo data corroborate the cell-based findings, demonstrating Compound C’s aggravating effect and phenformin’s alleviating effect on the Akt/mTOR pathway in a physiological context. Despite the slight inconsistency between the key proteins and cell lines, these results agreed that phenformin would decrease autophagy and Compound C would increase autophagy after TiO₂ NZs treatment.

## Discussion and conclusion
Our study reported a paradigm-shifting mechanism: TiO₂ NZs treatment subverts the fundamental logic of conventional pharmacology. By suppressing AMPK expression, TiO₂ NZs force a target “switch” where Compound C and phenformin—typically acting through AMPK—redirect their effects to differentially modulate the Akt/mTOR axis (inhibitory vs. activating, respectively), thereby producing opposing outcomes on autophagy regulation.This work establishes a new model of nanomaterial–biological interaction, demonstrating that nanomaterials can effectively “hijack” cellular signaling networks by reprogramming core regulatory nodes. Consequently, attempts to counteract nanomaterial-induced toxicity—particularly in nanomedicine applications—must account for this signaling rewiring. Without such consideration, therapeutic interventions risk triggering unpredictable “reversed effects” due to the altered signaling landscape imposed by the nanomaterials themselves.
To begin the study, the characteristics of TiO₂ NZs were firstly measured. The nanozyme in this study had a different hydrodynamic diameter compared to those in another study we published [ ], which is possibly due to the different types of cell culture medium used to disperse TiO₂. Ions in the cell culture medium can significantly affect the surface charge of, as well as the hydrodynamic diameter [ ]. The TiO₂ NZs in this research were relatively well-dispersed and reliable for the following study.
Maternal treated with TiO₂ NZs from GD5 to GD18 significantly reduced average fetal weight and length, indicating adverse effects on embryonic development. This finding aligns with a previous report [ , ]. It has been reported that maternal administration of TiO₂ NZs can penetrate the placental barrier and induce neuronal toxicity through autophagy [ ]. Therefore, we mainly focused on the placental autophagy in the following study. Autophagy can be induced by many risk factors, with energy depletion being the most common [ ]. Our results revealed that the placenta weights were increased after TiO₂ NZs treatment, suggesting placental congestion and energy deficiency. And the further decreased ATP level in placenta confirmed the lack of energy. Meanwhile, TiO₂ have been reported to produce ROS and decrease ATP level, which are also recognized as triggers of autophagy [ ]. In summary, TiO₂ NZs are more likely to induce placenta autophagy via energy-related pathways, in which AMPK protein plays a crucial role. Therefore, in the following studies, Compound C and phenformin were applied to reverse the TiO₂ NZs-induced adverse pregnancy and birth outcomes.
Compound C is a well-known AMPK inhibitor, which can reduce energy-related autophagy via AMPK-dependent pathways [ ]. Phenformin is an AMPK activator that has been reported to increase autophagy levels, in contrast to Compound C, also by targeting AMPK [ ]. Surprisingly, our results showed that Compound C and phenformin presented contrary effects on reversing the TiO₂ NZs-induced adverse pregnancy outcomes. Specifically, Compound C aggravated autophagy and inhibited embryo growth, while phenformin inhibited autophagy and reversed embryo growth restriction. To reveal the underlying mechanism, we used the placenta-derived cells for further exploration.
After treatment with TiO₂ NZs, the cellular autophagosome clearly accumulated, and the subsequent addition of bafilomycin A1 indicated that the accumulation of autophagosomes resulted from the enhanced autophagy level. Our previous study showed that TiO₂ had no effects on blocking autophagy flux [ ], therefore, we focused on enhancing autophagy level here. It was reported that TiO₂ could enter cells and accumulate in various organelles, including lysosome and endoplasmic reticulum, thereby impairing their functions [ , ]. Although TiO₂ could hardly enter mitochondria due to their bilayer membrane structure, plenty of studies indicated that mitochondrial morphology and functions were severely affected by TiO₂ NZs [ , ]. Our results showed swollen mitochondria and decreased ATP, which is consistent with previous study findings. Then Compound C and phenformin were applied to investigate how they regulate autophagy after TiO₂ NZs treatment.
Consistent with the animal study, we also found that the TiO₂ NZs-induced autophagy level was increased by Compound C and decreased by phenformin in HTR cell models, which were opposite to their original effects. To confirm that these contrary effects were related to TiO₂ NZs treatment, Compound C and phenformin were added to cells in the absence of TiO₂ NZs. And we found that both Compound C and phenformin increased autophagy, the change induced by phenformin was consistent with the hypothesis, while that induced by Compound C was not. Previous studies suggested that Compound C could induce autophagy via an AMPK-independent pathway in certain cell types [ , , ], in which AMPK was activated; therefore, Compound C was further added to the starving cells. The results showed that Compound C inhibited autophagy in this condition. These results demonstrated that AMPK is a pivotal molecule through which Compound C and phenformin regulate autophagy under standard conditions, corroborating previous findings [ , ]. The target regulation of Compound C/phenformin on AMPK might be altered after TiO₂ NZs treatment.
Our subsequent analysis confirmed that TiO₂ NZs downregulated AMPK protein. This aligns with our prior finding that TiO₂ induce microRNA-101-3p overexpression [ ], a known regulator that targets AMPK mRNA for degradation. This mechanistic link has been further validated in an independent study [ ]. Therefore, we hypothesize that the decreased AMPK concentration would prevent it from interacting with Compound C or phenformin. The decrease in AMPK protein abundance reduces its available binding sites for drugs, which in turn amplifies the relative prominence of originally non-specific drug-target interactions, such as those with Akt.Our study is in accordance with a previous studyreportingthat Compound C might regulate AKT via an AMPK-independent pathway [ ]. To verify the candidate target of Compound C/phenformin, molecular docking was applied to predict their interactions.
The results of molecular docking demonstrated that Akt was a candidate target of both Compound C and phenformin. Compound C connected two AKT1 molecules by binding them at both ends of the structure, which may block the key residues of AKT1 for its phosphorylation. And phenformin might act as an activator to stimulate Akt phosphorylate. Therefore, the role of Akt in autophagy regulation was further examined after TiO₂ NZs treatment. In contrast to the established role of the Akt/mTOR pathway in autophagy regulation [ – ], our results showed unaltered Akt/p-Akt alongside decreased mTOR/p-mTOR, indicating that TiO₂ NZs-induced autophagy occurs independently of the canonical Akt/mTOR axis. Under conditions of AMPK dysfunction, treatment with Compound C or phenformin altered p-Akt expression, consequently activating the Akt/mTOR pathway to modulate autophagy. In addition, a previous study revealed that Compound C was nonspecific targeted to AKT [ ], which supported our results. However, the reason why Compound C and phenformin no longer regulate AMPK in these conditions is not fully explained. After careful analysis of these results, we hypothesized that Compound C and phenformin target Akt protein when their primary target (AMPK) is unavailable.
We further confirmed that Akt was an alternative target of Compound C and phenformin when AMPK expression was reduced, by inhibiting both AMPK and Akt expression, as well as restoring AMPK expression in TiO₂ NZs-treated cells. All these results proved that Akt replaced AMPK as the target of both Compound C and phenformin in regulating autophagy after TiO₂ NZs treatment. The reversal of effects observed when Compound C and phenformin bind to Akt primarily stems from the differential roles of the AMPK/mTOR and Akt/mTOR pathways in autophagy regulation—AMPK activation promotes autophagy, whereas Akt activation suppresses it—rather than being attributable to alterations in the structure-activity relationship of the drug targets. To validate the results obtained in vitro, the key proteins were further verified in the tissue samples from each group of rats. The key proteins showed similar but not identical trend with the results in cell lines, which might be due to the individual differences. However, the controversial roles of Compound C and phenformin in autophagy regulation were consistent with the pregnancy outcomes, as well as with the cell line results. These results offer critical insights into the protein-mediated biological effects of nanozyme, advancing our understanding of their toxicological profile. The limitations of this study include the selection of only one time point, making it difficult to reveal protein changes at different time points and the pathways for autophagy regulation. Additionally, the effects of knocking down or over-expressing key molecules on pregnancy outcomes have not been validated in animal models.
This study unveils a previously underrecognized phenomenon in the field of nanomaterials: nanomaterials may fundamentally alter the action patterns and behaviors of known pharmacological agents by reshaping key nodes within intracellular signaling networks (such as the expression levels of core kinases). This finding not only serves as a safety warning but also offers a new paradigm for understanding the complex interactions at the “nano-bio” interface. The phenomenon of “signaling pathway hijacking” provides novel insights and considerations for designing nanomaterial-based combination therapies or assessing their safety during special physiological periods (such as pregnancy).
The limitations of this study are mainly reflected in the following aspects: Firstly, as an exploratory study focused on revealing the novel phenomenon of drug effect reversal induced by titanium dioxide nanozymes (TiO₂ NZs), this research has not systematically investigated dose-response and time-effect relationships, nor has it conducted in-depth toxicokinetic and biodistribution analyses of the material. Secondly, due to current constraints, the actual exposure levels of titanium dioxide nanozymes in human populations could not be accurately assessed, and the conclusions drawn are currently supported only by animal experimental evidence, lacking direct validation from human data. Additionally, the scope of the study was limited to a single material system and did not test a broader range of nanomaterials, thus making it impossible to determine whether the observed phenomenon is an isolated occurrence or a more common yet underrecognized mechanism, which still requires further research for verification. In future studies, efforts should be made to expand into multi-material and multi-dose systems and incorporate human population studies to clarify whether this mechanism represents a rare event or a potential common pattern in nano science.
In conclusion, maternal treated with TiO₂ NZs perturbs placental energy homeostasis and drives pathological autophagy, culminating in adverse pregnancy outcomes. More importantly, our study uncovers a paradigm shift in autophagy regulationfrom AMPK-dependent to Akt/mTOR-dependent control-under nano stress. This mechanistic breakthrough not only deepens molecular understanding at the chemistry-biology interface but also elevates the relevance of our work to public health protection and environmental health policy, and the development of targeted strategies to mitigate nanomedicine-associated risks.
