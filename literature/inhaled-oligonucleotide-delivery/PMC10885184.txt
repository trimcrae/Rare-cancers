## Abstract

## Objectives
Skin barrier disruption is a significant problem of the older population in an aging society. It is characterized by increased transepidermal water loss and decreased skin water content, and particulate matter (PM) is a social issue that can contribute to the exacerbation of skin inflammation. Thus, addressing this problem is urgent.

## Methods
Skin barrier‐disrupted mouse models were induced by two methods using acetone application or tape‐stripping. This study investigated the antioxidative and anti‐inflammatory properties of the Siegesbeckia herba extract (SHE) on PM‐induced changes in skin barrier‐disrupted mouse models. To examine changes in skin water content, inflammatory cytokines, and keratinocyte differentiation markers, mouse models were treated with vehicle 100 μL, PM10 100 μL (100 μg/mL), SHE 100 μL, or PM10 100 μL (100 μg/mL) plus SHE 100 μL.

## Results
SHE preserved skin hydration in the skin barrier‐disrupted mouse models regardless of the presence of PM10. SHE also inhibited the upregulation of inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin (IL)‐1β, IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐8, and tumor necrosis factor‐α and normalized the downregulation of keratinocyte differentiation markers against PM10 in skin barrier‐disrupted mouse models.

## Conclusions
This study elucidated the therapeutic effects of SHE against PM10 in skin barrier‐disrupted mouse models.

## SHE reduced the high levels of inflammatory cytokines in PM10‐treated, tape‐stripping‐induced skin barrier‐disrupted mouse models
Moreover, the expression levels of inflammatory cytokines in tape‐stripping‐induced skin barrier‐disrupted mouse models were checked. SHE inhibited the upregulation of IL‐1β, IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐8, and TNF‐α mRNAs and proteins in the mouse models by tape‐stripping. Similar to the previous experiment, skin barrier‐disrupted mouse models treated with PM10 showed a statistically significant increase in inflammatory cytokines compared with the model not treated with PM10 (Figure  ). Moreover, SHE demonstrated a clear reduction in inflammatory cytokines in the tape‐stripping‐induced skin barrier‐disrupted models, whether treated or not treated with PM10.

## SHE inhibited the downregulation of keratinocyte differentiation markers in both skin barrier disruption models treated with PM10

Finally, immunofluorescence staining was performed on dorsal skin samples from skin barrier‐disrupted mouse models treated with PM10 and/or SHE. Staining for loricrin, involucrin, and filaggrin was conducted to check for keratinocyte differentiation. The expression levels of loricrin, involucrin, and filaggrin were downregulated in skin barrier‐disrupted mouse models treated with PM10. These expressions were reversed when co‐treated with SHE (Figure  ).

## DISCUSSION
In the International Classification of Diseases, xerosis cutis (synonyms: dry skin, xerosis, and xeroderma) is defined as a deficiency in hydrolipids (ICD10: L85.3) in the skin. Xerosis can be caused by exogenous (e.g., environmental) or endogenous (e.g., inflammatory skin disorders and aging) factors. The decreased skin water content in patients with xerosis is related to the impairment of skin barrier function and/or lack of moisturizing factors. Whether xerosis in older people should be considered a disease or a part of the aging process is being debated, and some authors even describe xerosis as a low‐grade atopic eczema. Two methods are known for making artificial skin barrier‐disrupted mouse models, namely, acetone application and tape‐stripping. , Rissmann et al. suggested that barrier disruption caused by acetone treatment is mainly caused by the removal of corneocytes, and it is also observed by tape‐stripping. The results of this study confirmed an increase in the levels of inflammatory cytokines such as IL‐1β, IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐8, and TNF‐α in the two skin barrier‐disrupted models and revealed a decrease in the expression of keratinocyte differentiation markers such as loricrin, involucrin, and filaggrin. These facts are consistent with previous reports. , However, to understand the precise mechanism, further in‐depth research based on the results of this study is necessary.
Another interesting point is that the change in inflammatory responses in skin barrier‐disrupted mouse models appears to be more pronounced with PM treatment. However, research on the effects of treating healthy skin with PM alone is still limited. According to a recent study by Kim et al., PM2.5‐induced TNF‐α plays a crucial role in the PM2.5‐mediated inhibition of filaggrin. This effect was observed in PM‐treated mouse models, which was not specifically investigated in our study. Therefore, exploring the changes that occur with PM treatment alone would be interesting. The size of the PM used in the study may be relevant, and further research on this aspect is necessary.
In a previous study, PM10 upregulated serum IgE concentration and increased spleen weight in DNCB‐induced AD mouse models. Moreover, PM10 enhanced mast cell counts, inflammatory cytokines, including IL‐1β, IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐17α, IL‐25, IL‐31, and TSLP, and keratinocyte differentiation markers, including loricrin and filaggrin in DNCB‐induced AD mouse models. Based on these findings, the increase in IL‐4 levels in this study could be understood, and it might provide clues to the mechanisms underlying the occurrence of atopic dermatitis in older people. Atopic dermatitis in older patients represents a newly defined subgroup with complex and multifactorial etiology. It is associated with various factors, including the aging process, skin dysbiosis, impaired sweat production, and differences in lifestyles. , 

SHE has been gaining attention recently for its various pharmacologic effects, including antioxidant and anti‐inflammatory properties. Previously, the antioxidant effects of SHE on PM10‐induced changes in DNCB‐induced atopic dermatitis mouse models and the anti‐inflammatory effects of SHE against PM10‐treated cultured human sebocytes and/or ORS cells were investigated. In the present study, SHE preserved the skin water content in animal models, reduced the inflammatory cytokines, and inhibited the downregulation of keratinocyte differentiation markers against PM10 in acetone‐treated and tape‐stripping‐induced skin barrier‐disrupted mouse models. Accordingly, SHE may have anti‐inflammatory properties and repair skin barrier functions in dry skin.

## CONCLUSIONS
The results of this study elucidated that SHE preserved skin hydration in skin barrier‐disrupted mouse models, regardless of the presence of PM. Moreover, SHE inhibited the upregulation of inflammatory cytokine and normalized keratinocyte differentiation markers against PM10 in skin barrier‐disrupted mouse models. Thus, SHE could be a promising anti‐inflammatory and moisturizer agent. Further research may advance the development of therapeutic interventions for skin barrier disruption.
