## Abstract
Aspartate-specific cysteine protease (Caspase)-8 plays a critical role in initiating pyroptosis by mediating cleavage and activation of gasdermin-D (Gsdmd) during Yersinia infection. However, the role of Caspase-8 in pyroptosis after myocardial infarction (MI) and its underlying mechanism remains elusive. Here we show that Caspase-8 is essential for cardiomyocyte pyroptosis post MI and Caspase-8/Gsdmd signaling pathway is activated in hearts of male infarcted mice. The inhibition of Caspase-8 in male mice rescues the decline of cardiac function and cardiomyocyte pyroptosis following MI. Dnmt3a is further shown to impact DNA methylation on the CpG island of Caspase-8 promoter and regulate Caspase-8 expression. Dnmt3a overexpression protects myocardium from MI in male mice by inhibiting cardiomyocyte pyroptosis. Moreover, reduction of Dnmt3a in MI is attributed to the increased ubiquitination. Our study identifies a regulatory axis, Dnmt3a/Caspase-8/Gsdmd, which drives ischemic heart injury by promoting cardiomyocyte pyroptosis, and suggests potential therapeutic targets for heart diseases.
Subject terms: Myocardial infarction, DNA methylation

Caspase-8 plays a key role in initiating pyroptosis, but its contribution to myocardial infarction has remained unclear. Here, the authors show that ischemia-induced degradation of Dnmt3a leads to demethylation and upregulation of Caspase-8, amplifying Gsdmd cleavage and driving cardiomyocyte death.

## Introduction
Cardiovascular disease is one of the major causes of death worldwide, and its precise pathology remains unclear. Myocardial infarction (MI) is an acute, life-threatening medical condition caused by the blockage of a coronary artery, leading to ischemia and, eventually, cell death in the affected heart area. Adult myocardium possesses poor regenerative capacity, and MI thus leads to cardiac scarring and cardiac remodeling that increase the risk of heart failure. The extent of myocardial injury after MI and the risk of developing heart failure are positively correlated in patients. Several therapeutic strategies, including inhibiting pathological cardiomyocyte death, attenuating inflammation, and inducing regeneration, have been proposed for protecting MI survivors against heart failure .
Pyroptosis is a novel type of programmed cell death that has recently been reported to occur in various diseases, notably in cardiomyocytes after myocardial infarction . It features pore formation on the plasma membrane, cell swelling and plasma membrane disruption, which distinct from necroptosis and apoptosis . Mechanistically, the inflammasome-mediated Caspase-1 activation results in the cleavage of Gsdmd to trigger the pore-forming in the cell membrane, inducing cell membrane rupture. At the same time, Caspases-1 cleaves pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their active forms, which secreted via pores subsequently . In addition, upon activation by specific inflammasome stimuli, Caspase-11 has been found to trigger Caspase-1-independent macrophage death and Caspase-1-dependent IL-1β and IL-18 production, it revealed a unique pro-inflammatory role for Caspase-11 . Mechanistically, Caspase-11 can be directly bound and activated by intracellular lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in a TLR4-independent manner and cleaves Gsdmd to execute pyroptosis . The two major, well-characterized pathways of pyroptosis are the canonical and non-canonical pathways. They are recognized as core mechanisms that mediate pyroptosis across a range of pathologies .
In 2018, Orning et al. reported activated Caspase-8 is capable of directly cleaving Gsdmd to produce the pore-forming N-terminus of Gsdmd to trigger pyroptosis in macrophages with Yersinia infection, and the process is independent of Caspase-1 and −11 . This study firstly proposed a potential network between Caspase-8 and pyroptosis. In previous studies, Caspase-8 plays an important role in tumorigenesis and tissue damage as Caspase-8/Caspase-3 apoptotic pathway , . However, severe ischemia and inflammation are the typical pathological features of myocardial infarction, and whether cardiomyocytes similarly undergo Caspase-8-mediated pyroptosis during myocardial infarction remains unknown. Investigating this possibility will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of post-MI cell death mechanisms.
DNA methylation is an important epigenetic mechanism that regulates gene expression and downstream biological processes, which closely relies on DNA methyltransferase (DNMT). The human genome encodes five DNMTs: DNMT1, DNMT2, DNMT3a, DNMT3b and DNMT3l. DNMT1, DNMT3a and DNMT3b are canonical cytosine-5 DNMTs, DNMT2 and DNMT3l do not possess catalytic DNMT activity . Several reports have suggested that DNA methylation plays a crucial role in cancer, immunity and inflammatory responses. The occurrence and development of many cancers, including lung cancer, colon cancer, breast cancer and leukemia, are closely related to DNA methylation. Many oncologic drugs targeting DNA methylation have been successfully applied in clinical practice – . However, the understanding of DNA methylation in cardiovascular disease is still very limited. So far, no novel therapeutic drug or therapeutic strategy for cardiovascular disease based on DNA methylation was applied in clinical practice. Recent studies have found that lncRNA-CPR directly interacts with DNA methylase Dnmt3a in mouse cardiomyocytes, and then binds to the promoter region of the Mcm3 gene, resulting in the methylation of CpG sites. This methylation inhibits Mcm3 expression, leading to the suppress of cardiomyocyte proliferation and the cardiac repair process . In addition, a newly reported longitudinal study of 11,461 people showed that 1895 patients developed coronary heart disease during an average of 11.2 years of follow-up. The 53 methylated CpG sites were significantly associated with the incidence of ischemic heart disease. Changes in CpG locus genes also included genes related to calcium regulation and serum calcium related risk genes, suggesting that serum-derived DNA methylation is closely associated with the risk of coronary heart disease and related diseases .
In this study, we investigated the potential role of Caspase-8 in pyroptosis after MI, and revealed the underlying mechanism by which it is regulated by DNA methylation. We demonstrated that Dnmt3a is downregulated in post-MI cardiac tissue, resulting in hypomethylation of the Caspase-8 promoter region. This hypomethylation facilitates the specific binding of the methylation-sensitive transcription factor Hes1 to the Caspase-8 promoter, thereby enhancing Caspase-8 transcription. Our findings establish a theoretical framework-the Dnmt3a/Caspase-8/Gsdmd axis for understanding cardiomyocyte pyroptosis after MI, which may represent a potential therapeutic strategy to attenuate MI progression.

## Results

## Caspase-8/Gsdmd signaling pathway is activated in MI mouse hearts
Previous studies have shown that Caspase-8 plays an important role in the cleavage of Gsdmd and the initiation of cellular pyroptosis in various diseases. However, its involvement has not been reported during acute MI. To clarify this, we constructed an acute MI mouse model via the LAD coronary artery ligation for 24 h. TTC staining result presented increased cardiac infarct area in MI hearts by an intuitive way, which indicated that the MI mouse model was constructed successfully (Supplementary Fig.  ). Notably, our findings revealed a significant increase in the expression of Caspase-8 at the mRNA level in the infarct border zone (Fig.  ). Furthermore, there was a significant increase in the expression of Caspase-8 and its cleaved fragments (Caspase-8-p18) at the protein level in a synchronized manner (Fig.  ). In terms of morphology, there was a noticeable increase in the fluorescence intensity of Caspase-8 in the myocardial infarction margin tissue of MI mice (Supplementary Fig.  ). Notably, both Gsdmd and its cleaved, active N-terminal fragment (Gsdmd-N), were significantly increased in the infarct border zone of MI mouse hearts (Fig.  ). Caspase-8 triggers the cleavage of Gsdmd has previously been observed in macrophages infected with Yersinia . In order to detect this model of cleavage in cardiomyocytes, we transfected neonatal mouse ventricular cardiomyocytes (NMVCs) with a Caspase-8 overexpression plasmid to observe the expression of Gsdmd. The result showed Caspase-8 triggers the cleavage of Gsdmd obviously in NMVCs (Fig.  ). To further investigate this interaction, we performed molecular docking analysis using HDOCK, which predicted that Caspase-8 forms 11 hydrogen bonds, 31 hydrophobic interactions, and 12 salt bridges with Gsdmd, primarily localized to the linker domain and C-terminal region (Supplementary Fig.  ). To supporting that, our co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments confirmed binding between cleaved Caspase-8 and Gsdmd, with preferential interaction at the C-terminus (Supplementary Fig.  ). Thus, our findings indicated that Gsdmd might serve as a potential primary substrate for Caspase-8 in the myocardium following MI.
 

## Forced overexpression of Caspase-8 induces cardiomyocyte pyroptosis via Gsdmd in male mice
In vivo study, AAV9 vectors carrying either a negative control (AAV9-CMV-GFP) or Caspase-8 overexpression construct (AAV9-CMV-GFP-Caspase-8) were delivered into mice via tail vein injection. Four weeks after AAV9-Caspase-8 infection (10 × 1010vg/mouse, see Fig.  for the experimental timeline) Caspase-8 overexpression in the myocardium was verified by qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis (Supplementary Fig.  and ). Echocardiography results showed a significant decrease in fractional shortening (FS) and ejection fraction (EF) in the hearts of mice with forced overexpression of Caspase-8, compared to the negative control group. This result suggested that Caspase-8 overexpression is responsible for a decline in heart function (Fig.  ). We also analyzed the cardiac structure indicator, as shown in supplementary Fig.  . The left ventricular internal diameter (LVID) (dilation and systole) significantly increased, accompanied by decreased left ventricular posterior wall thickness (LVPW) (systole); while no significant changes in interventricular septum (IVS) (dilation and systole) and left ventricular mass (LV Mass). LVID is often considered as an indicator of heart failure. LVPW thinning is usually caused by cell death or intraventricular high pressure. Increases in IVS thickness and LV Mass are occurred in cardiac hypertrophy. Taken together, these changes of cardiac structure indicator in AAV9-NC and AAV9-Caspase-8 group mice demonstrated overexpression of Caspase-8 tend to induce ventricular enlargement following large amounts cell death. By H&E staining, the loose cardiac structure as well as diffuse inflammatory response were presented in the Caspase-8 overexpression group (Fig.  ). Consistently, the results of electron microscopy showed Caspase-8 overexpression clearly induced mitochondrial swelling, and formation of membrane pores in cardiomyocytes (Fig.  ). Meanwhile, accompanied by rupture of the cell membrane, large amount of LDH and inflammatory factors (IL-1β and IL-18) were released into the blood in Caspase-8 overexpression mice, which identified as typical features of pyroptosis (Fig.  ). Then, the expression of related proteins was determined through western blot analysis, as shown in Fig.  , the expression of Gsdmd-N was significantly increased in the AAV9-Caspase-8 group compared to the AAV9-NC group, similar trends were observed for IL-1β and IL-18. In addition, there was a noticeable increase in the membrane localization of Gsdmd-N due to Caspase-8 overexpression (Fig.  ). Therefore, cardiac specific overexpression of Caspase-8 could promote myocardial injury characterized by inducing pyroptosis.
 To determine whether the pathological effects of Caspase-8 are mediated by Gsdmd, we constructed cardiomyocyte-specific Gsdmd knockout mice (Gsdmd‑CKO), with Gsdmd-flox mice (Gsdmdflox/flox) serving as controls (generation strategy in Fig.  ). These mice, along with controls, were then treated with AAV9-CMV-GFP-Caspase-8. Western blot analysis confirmed the successful Gsdmd knock-out in Gsdmd-CKO mice. Furthermore, the cleavage of Gsdmd induced by Caspase-8 overexpression was absent in Gsdmd-CKO mice (Fig.  ). An echocardiographic evaluation revealed the effective improvement of myocardial function in the Gsdmd-CKO group compared with the control after Caspase8 overexpression (Fig.  ). The release of inflammatory factors (IL-1β and IL-18) were also obviously downregulated in Gsdmd-CKO mice treated with AAV9-Caspase-8 (Fig.  ). In pathology, cardiomyocyte-specific overexpression of Caspase-8 triggered marked inflammatory infiltration, and knock-out of Gsdmd abolished this effect (Fig.  ). Overall, loss of Gsdmd alleviates Caspase-8-induced cardiac dysfunction and inflammatory injury.
 

## Targeted inhibition of Caspase-8/Gsdmd axis protects against myocardial ischemia injury
To further investigate the role of Caspase-8 in MI-induced cardiac pyroptosis, we employed Z-IETD-fmk to inhibit Caspase-8 specifically during MI. This treatment alone showed cardioprotective efficacy on cardiac function and structure (Supplementary Fig.  ). However, given that Caspase-8 is a known upstream inhibitor of RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL-dependent necroptosis pathway, and its suppression would potentially enhance necroptosis. To isolate the pyroptosis-specific effects, we co-administered necrostatin-1 to block RIP1-mediated necroptosis. This approach is justified as prior studies demonstrated that the necroptosis blockade does not affect the LDH release, IL-1β maturation, or Gsdmd cleavage , . Therefore, mice were ultimately treated with a combination of Z-IETD-fmk and necrostatin-1. The experimental scheme is depicted in Fig.  . Mice subjected to the LAD ligation following the intravenous injections of Z-IETD-fmk (4 mg /kg) 0.5 hour and necrostatin-1 (3.5 mg/kg) injection via caudal vein for 1 h – . The inhibition efficiency of Caspase-8 was verified by Western blot, and the protein levels of Caspase-8 and Caspase-8-p18 were significantly reduced in the hearts of mice treated with Z-IETD-fmk (Supplementary Fig.  ). Echocardiographic assessment showed that EF and FS were markedly declined in MI mice, whereas they were conspicuously rescued in combination inhibitor administered MI mice, indicating the cardiac protection provided by Caspase-8 inhibition in MI (Fig.  ). As shown in Fig.  , Caspase-8 blockage also improved myocardial structural disorder and inhibited inflammatory cell infiltration. Furthermore, Caspase-8-inhibition resulted in the attenuation of myocardial ultrastructural damage, including disordering of local myofilament dissolving, mitochondrial swelling, and membrane pore formation (Fig.  ). Consistently, the enhanced LDH release induced by MI was also reduced in the combination inhibitory mice (Fig.  ). Caspase-8-inhibition also significantly diminished the protein expression of Gsdmd-N, IL-1β, and IL-18 in MI mouse hearts (Fig.  ). In addition, the accumulation of Gsdmd-N on the membrane induced by MI was also alleviated (Fig.  ). These results indicated that Caspase-8 exerts an important effect on cardiomyocyte pyroptosis during MI.
 Given that Gsdmd is a key substrate of Caspase-8, we subjected Gsdmd-CKO mice to LAD ligation to investigate its specific role in MI-induced injury. Echocardiographic assessment revealed that, similar to Caspase-8 inhibited mice, Gsdmd-CKO mice maintained the better left ventricular systolic function compared to Gsdmdflox/flox controls (Fig.  ). Histological evaluation by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining further confirmed a marked reduction in inflammatory infiltration within the myocardium of Gsdmd-CKO mouse hearts (Fig.  ). Furthermore, Gsdmd-CKO mice demonstrated a significant attenuation of MI-induced elevations in circulating inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 (Fig.  ). These data demonstrated that genetic ablation of Gsdmd specifically in cardiomyocytes attenuates MI-induced ventricular dysfunction and mitigates the associated inflammatory injury. Collectively, our findings highlight the potential of targeting both Caspase-8 and Gsdmd to mitigate injury after myocardial infarction.

## DNA hypomethylation lead to an increase of Caspase-8 in cardiomyocytes
Previous studies have indicated that promoter hypomethylation is linked to increased expression of multiple genes. Huang et al. observed a noteworthy decrease in CpG methylation levels under hypoxic conditions compared to normoxic conditions . To further explore the underlying mechanism of this abnormal expression of Caspase-8, we identified a CpG island near the TSS of the Caspase-8 gene that was suitable for methylation modification using Methprimer online (Supplementary Fig.  ). The specific CG sites are illustrated in Fig.  . Subsequently, we utilized BSP to analyze the methylation levels of this specific region at the population and individual levels, the results showed a striking decrease in DNA methylation of Caspase-8 gene in MI hearts (Fig.  ). We also detected the mRNA and protein levels of Caspase-8 in NMVCs treated with 5-Azacytidine (5aza), that is a potent inhibitor of DNA methyltransferase. The expression of Caspase-8 mRNA and protein levels (Caspase-8/Caspase-8-p18) were up-regulated markedly in response to treatment with 5aza (10 μM) in NMVCs for 24 h (Fig.  ). DNA methylation is regulated by two main groups of DNMTs. DNMT1 is involved in maintaining DNA methylation, and DNMT3a/3b are responsible for initiating denovo DNA methylation . The expression of Dnmt3a was found to be significantly decreased in the border zone of MI mouse hearts, while Dnmt1 and Dnmt3b levels were significantly elevated reversely (Fig.  ). To investigate the underlying mechanism of Dnmt3a in regulating the DNA hypomethylation of Caspase-8, we conducted molecular docking to predict the binding ability between Dnmt3a and Caspase-8. The results showed 9 hydrogen bonds between the Dnmt3a protein molecule and Caspase-8 DNA structure (Supplementary Fig.  ). This preliminary analysis promoted us to further investigate the functional relationship between Dnmt3a and Caspase-8 further. Consequently, we performed a ChIP assay, which demonstrated a reduction in Dnmt3a binding within the myocardium of MI mice compared to sham-operated controls (Fig.  ). Furthermore, NMVCs were transfected with the Dnmt3a overexpression plasmid and specific siRNA. Interestingly, along with the overexpression or silencing of Dnmt3a in NMVCs, Caspase-8 consistently showed negative regulation by Dnmt3a at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig.  ). Meanwhile, CpG island methylation was detected using methylation-specific PCR (MSP), the methylation level of Caspase-8 gene was found to be elevated upon Dnmt3a overexpression (Fig.  ), and decreased with Dnmt3a knockdown (Fig.  ). Therefore, these results indicated that Dnmt3a is involved in the methylation of Caspase-8 and the reduction of Dnmt3a promotes Caspase-8 expression, potentially contributing to cardiomyocytes pyroptosis after MI.
 

## Dnmt3a exerts cardioprotective effects on MI injury
To investigate the function of Dnmt3a in cardiomyocytes post MI, Dnmt3a was overexpressed in vivo by using AAV9-CMV-GFP-Dnmt3a (AAV9-Dnmt3a) delivered via tail vein injection in mice. After six weeks of AAV9-Dnmt3a infection, Dnmt3a overexpression in the myocardium was successfully verified by qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis (Supplementary Fig.  ). After inducing the MI mouse model by LAD coronary artery ligation for 24 h (Fig.  ), we observed a marked decrease in EF and FS. However, Dnmt3a overexpression increased both parameters, which indicated a significant improvement in the MI-induced cardiac function impairment (Fig.  ). Furthermore, the cardiac structural disorder could also be improved by Dnmt3a overexpression, noteworthy, and inflammatory cells infiltrating was significantly reduced by Dnmt3a overexpression (Fig.  ). In addition, the ultrastructure changes of myocardium, such as mitochondrial swelling, membrane rupture and disorganized sarcomere arrangement were also attenuated by Dnmt3a overexpression (Fig.  ). Furthermore, Dnmt3a overexpression also significantly attenuated the MI-induced increase in serum levels of LDH and the inflammatory factors (IL-1β and IL-18) (Fig.  ). These findings suggested that overexpression of Dnmt3a protects the myocardium from post-MI damage.
 

## Dnmt3a suppresses cardiomyocyte pyroptosis mediated by Caspase-8
To determine whether Dnmt3a exerts an effect on Caspase-8 and cardiomyocyte pyroptosis following MI, we conducted further investigations using MI mice with AAV9-mediated Dnmt3a overexpression. We found that overexpression of Dnmt3a by AAV9-Dnmt3a infection significantly inhibited the elevated Caspase-8 mRNA expression caused by MI (Fig.  ). In addition, the protein expression of Caspase-8/Caspase-8-p18 and pyroptosis-related factors, such as Gsdmd-N, IL-1β, and IL-18, also showed a significant decrease in response to Dnmt3a overexpression in MI hearts (Fig.  ).
 To explore the mechanism, we further investigated if the protective effect of Dnmt3a was attenuated by Caspase-8. To test this, knocked down of Dnmt3a in NMVCs using siRNA and showed a significant upregulation of Gsdmd-N expression. However, the existence of si-Caspase-8 canceled the changes of Gsdmd-N expression upon less of Dnmt3a (Fig.  ). Furthermore, Dnmt3a knockdown triggered an increase in LDH release, and this elevated level was stabilized upon additional Caspase-8 knockdown (Fig.  ). The PI uptake assay presented that silencing Dnmt3a resulting in an increase of PI-positive cells, whereas the simultaneous knockdown of Caspase-8 significantly attenuated this increase (Fig.  ). Therefore, we concluded that the silence of Dnmt3a in cardiomyocytes induced pyroptosis through Caspase-8/Gsdmd signaling pathway.

## Enhanced Dnmt3a ubiquitylation in the MI border zone
Simultaneously, we noticed that the mRNA level changes of Dnmt3a were not synchronized with the protein level in MI mouse hearts (Fig.  ), thus we further validated it using a hypoxia-induced NMVCs model. Consistent with the in vivo data, hypoxic NMVCs presented a significantly decrease in Dnmt3a protein but negligible changes at the mRNA level (Fig.  ). We therefore hypothesized that the degradation of the Dnmt3a protein may be enhanced under hypoxic conditions. To investigate the underlying mechanisms responsible for the rapid degradation of Dnmt3a after hypoxia, we initially examined the stability of the Dnmt3a protein in both normoxic and hypoxic cardiomyocytes. Interestingly, following protein synthesis inhibition by cycloheximide (CHX), the half-life of Dnmt3a protein in hypoxic NMVCs was markedly shortened to approximately 12 h, whereas it exceeded 24 h under normoxic conditions (Fig.  ). It indicates that the Dnmt3a protein stability is substantially reduced in hypoxic cardiomyocytes. Importantly, the effect of hypoxia on Dnmt3a was restrained in the presence of the proteasome inhibitor MG132 in cardiomyocytes (Fig.  ). Enhanced ubiquitination of Dnmt3a in hypoxic cardiomyocytes was confirmed by Co-IP analysis, comparing hypoxic and normoxic conditions (Fig.  ). Consistent with this cellular finding, a parallel Co-IP analysis of myocardial tissue lysates revealed markedly higher Dnmt3a ubiquitination in MI mice compared to sham-operated controls (Fig.  ). Supplementary Fig.  verifies Dnmt3a ubiquitination using si-Dnmt3a. Furthermore, inhibiting the autophagolysosomal degradation pathway with 3-Methyladenine (3-MA) did not rescue the hypoxia-induced decrease in Dnmt3a protein levels (Supplementary Fig.  ). These findings suggest that hypoxia-induced downregulation of Dnmt3a protein depends on the ubiquitin-proteasome system.
 

## Hes1 binds and regulates Caspase-8 via Dnmt3a-mediated local hypomethylation
DNA methylation is a well-established epigenetic mark associated with transcriptional repression. Mechanistically, methylation at CpG island inhibits transcription factor (TF) binding, ultimately resulting in gene silencing . In our study, reduced Dnmt3a expression may decrease DNA methylation at the CpG site within the Caspase-8 gene locus. We therefore hypothesized that this demethylation could expose these sites, thereby facilitating TF binding and transcriptional activation. We utilized the UCSC and JASPAR online database for the prediction of TFs. Then applied the methylation-sensitive SELEX database to screen out TFs that sensitive to methylation. Four cardiac-enriched, methylation-sensitive TFs were selected: Tfec, Mycn, Hes1, and Zbtb33 (Fig.  ). Subsequently, functional ChIP analysis confirmed their binding to the Caspase-8 promoter and revealed that Dnmt3a differentially modulates these interactions (Fig.  ). Overexpression of Dnmt3a reduced the binding of Mycn and Hes1 (markedly in Hes1) but enhanced that of Tfec and Zbtb33. Conversely, Dnmt3a knockdown produced the opposite effects (markedly in Hes1). To further investigate the regulatory relationship between Hes1 and Dnmt3a on Caspase-8, we transfected NMVCs with a Hes1 overexpression plasmid. The results showed that Hes1 significantly upregulated both the mRNA and protein levels of Caspase-8, and this effect dramatically enhanced by Dnmt3a knockdown (Fig.  ). Collectively, these results indicated that Dnmt3a-mediated demethylation of specific CpG island renders the Caspase-8 promoter accessible for Hes1 binding, enabling their cooperative transcriptional activation.
 

## Discussion
This study reveals the Caspase-8-dependent pyroptotic pathway in MI, the Dnmt3a/Caspase-8/Gsdmd axis, which as a key mechanism driving pyroptosis and myocardial injury after myocardial infarction. Our data demonstrated that Caspase-8 is markedly upregulated in post-MI hearts, where it directly cleaves Gsdmd to drive cardiomyocyte pyroptosis, thereby exacerbating cardiac injury. DNA methylation changes mediated by Dnmt3a reduction responsible for the effects of Caspase-8 on pyroptosis in MI. Reduced Dnmt3a decreases methylation at the Caspase-8 promoter, which in turn permits binding and transcriptional activation. The functional interaction between Hes1 and Dnmt3a-mediated DNA methylation precisely regulates Caspase-8 expression in the process of MI. These findings uncover an important regulatory mechanism in which Dnmt3a-mediated DNA methylation controls pyroptosis via a Caspase-8-dependent pathway, and provide epigenetic strategies along with therapeutic targets for inhibiting pyroptosis in the clinical cardioprotection of ischemic heart disease.
Caspase-8 is the molecular switch for multiple cell death, including apoptosis, necroptosis and pyroptosis, involved in the occurrence and development of various diseases . Caspase-8 plays an important role in tumor biology, as summarized by Ranadip et al. In cancers such as hepatocellular carcinoma, breast cancer, and ovarian cancer, Caspase-8 is downregulated. Its reduction enables tumor cells to escape apoptosis, thereby promoting tumor growth, invasion, angiogenesis, metastasis, and therapy resistance, which collectively contribute to poor clinical outcomes. Conversely, in colorectal, cervical, and renal cancers, Caspase-8 is often up-regulated. In these cases, it appears to drive tumor progression through non-apoptotic functions, including the enhancement of cell motility and angiogenesis . Despite this established complexity in oncology, its pathophysiological role in cardiovascular diseases is largely unexplored. Several reports indicate that cardiovascular diseases are characterized by extensive cardiomyocytes apoptosis and necroptosis . In the rat model of acute MI and I/R, increased Caspase-8 activity promoted cardiomyocyte apoptosis, arrhythmias, and late left ventricular remodeling , . The cell death primarily depended on the active RIPK1-FADD-Caspase-8 complex for apoptosis and RIPK1-RIPK3-MLKL complex for necroptosis , . Previous studies on cardiomyocyte apoptosis after MI typically focused on a time window ranging from 1 day to 4 weeks – . In contrast, cardiomyocyte pyroptosis mostly concentrated on the early phase, particularly within 24 hours , , likely because pyroptosis is a key driver of the early inflammatory burst during MI. Our work identifies upregulated Caspase-8 as the mechanism that cleaves Gsdmd to execute this early pyroptosis in cardiomyocytes, thereby establishing a specific molecular axis in this field.
Our work advances Dnmt3a from a common methyltransferase to a critical switch controlling cell death patterns in myocardial ischemia. Previous studies have specifically reported that the knockout of Dnmt3a in hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes compromised its normal function, such as altered contractility due to changed expression of contractile genes, lipid accumulation due to deregulated PPARγ and impaired glucose metabolism due to HIF-1α instability . In this study, we demonstrated that Dnmt3a overexpression rescues its MI-induced downregulation and, via its methyltransferase function, inhibits Caspase-8 and Gsdmd activation, thereby markedly reducing cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and improving cardiac function. This directly links Dnmt3a-mediated cardioprotection to pyroptosis inhibition. It was previously reported that a ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (E2)-Ube2b could degrade Dnmt3a in the dorsal hippo-campus of the brain . We also have detected the ubiquitination degradation of Dnmt3a in hearts of MI, meanwhile, we excluded the autophagolysosomal degradation pathway.
Typically, the changes of DNA methylation regulate itself expression mostly correlate with TFs, because the loss of a methyl group on the base enhances chromatin accessibility. With the development of new methods to better profile TFs binding and DNA methylation, the views on the sensitivity of TFs to DNA methylation have started to change. Yin et al. analyzed 542 human TFs with methylation-sensitive SELEX (systematic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment), and found 350 TFs’ ability of binding to DNA affected by the methylation of cytosine CpG dinucleotides. These TFs divided into methyl-minus group and methyl-plus group, as its name suggests, the methyl-minus group prefers to bind unmethylated motifs, and the methyl-plus group preference for methylated motifs . This suggested the changes of methylation on the DNA sequence may attract TFs with different properties, that is possible methyl-minus or methyl-plus. In our work, we detected whether Tfec, Mycn, Hes1, and Zbtb33, which belong to methylation-sensitive TFs, bound to the locus with changed methylation on Caspase-8 gene. Compared with common TFs, methylation-sensitive TFs are more plausible to connect with DNA methylation.
By combining bioinformatic screening with experimental validation, we pinpointed Hes1 as the key methylation-sensitive transcription factor. Zheng et al. demonstrated Hes1 was upregulated after MI and participates in the processes such as angiogenesis and cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Its suppression alleviated ischemic injury, including apoptosis and the progression of cyanotic congenital heart disease, as Hes1 is a target gene of microRNAs – . In our study, we found that Hes1 binding to the Caspase-8 promoter is blocked by hypermethylation but enabled by Dnmt3a-deficiency-induced hypomethylation in MI mouse hearts. This mechanism explains the specific upregulation of Caspase-8 following Dnmt3a downregulation, thereby pinpointing a crucial upstream target for intercepting the pyroptosis cascade in MI.
Despite the robust findings of this study, there are some limitations that need to be discussed. The interaction of active Caspase-8 and Gsdmd is complexed, it is mainly due to autohydrolysis and activation of pro-Caspase-8 protein. This limited further exploration of their binding role. Moreover, Caspase-8 have a wide range of substrates including other pyroptosis effector. In a follow-up study, we would consider the spatial structure analysis to investigate the binding role of active-Caspase-8 with Gsdm family proteins. Another limitation of this study lies in the selection of the genetic model. We employed only a cardiomyocyte-specific Gsdmd knockout rather than a systemic knockout strategy. Although this approach enabled us to precisely analyze the critical role of cardiomyocytes in the pyroptosis injury process, it may not fully capture the broader systemic effects of Gsdmd inhibition. Given that systemic Gsdmd knockout mice simultaneously suppress pyroptosis in infiltrating immune cells (such as neutrophils and macrophages), they may exhibit a more pronounced protective phenotype against acute myocardial infarction. The systemic inhibition could more comprehensively mitigate inflammatory damage. However, such a systemic knockout model also carries inherent drawbacks, as it disrupts pyroptosis-dependent inflammatory signaling across all cell types, which may inadvertently impair the initiation of necessary tissue repair processes. Finally, we acknowledge that the use of only male mice in this study is a limitation. While this approach was specifically adopted to eliminate the potential confounding effects of endogenous estrogen and the estrous cycle on cardiovascular outcomes, thereby allowing for a clearer isolation of the mechanism under investigation from known sex hormone-mediated variability, it restricts the direct generalizability of the findings to female subjects. Further studies incorporating female animals are warranted to determine whether the observed effects are conserved across sexes or modulated by sex hormones.
In conclusion, this study illustrated the Dnmt3a/Caspase‑8/Gsdmd signaling pathway, demonstrating its activation during the early phase of myocardial infarction and its molecular mechanism in directly cleaving Gsdmd to induce cardiomyocyte pyroptosis. By establishing an actionable pathway linking epigenetic dysregulation, transcriptional activation, and inflammatory cell death, our findings provide additional directions for the precise diagnosis and targeted therapy in ischemic heart disease.

## Methods

## Animals
The male mice (C57BL/6 background) (6-8 weeks old, 22-25 g) obtained from Changsheng Biotechnology Company, Liaoning, China were selected to assure accuracy and consistence of results in this study. Only male mice were used in this study. This approach was taken specifically to eliminate the potential confounding effects of endogenous estrogen and the estrous cycle on cardiovascular outcomes, thereby isolating the mechanism under investigation from known sex hormone-mediated variability . All mice were maintained in a temperature-controlled facility with 12 hs light/dark cycle at 23 ± 3 °C and 50–60% humidity. All animal experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University (Approval Number: HMUIRB20170020). This study was conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Eighth Edition) and the Chinese guidelines for the Review of Welfare and Ethics of Laboratory Animals.

## Mouse experiments
Gsdmdflox/flox mice were designed and constructed by Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (Guangzhou, China). Cardiomyocyte-specific knockout (Gsdmd-CKO) mice were generated by breeding the Gsdmdflox/flox with Myh6-Cre mice. The mice were treated with Necrostatin-1 and Z-IETD-fmk before the construction of the MI model. Recombinant adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) vectors carrying Dnmt3a and Caspase-8 overexpressed plasmid were injected into 6 weeks old mice through the tail vein at a dose of 1 × 1011 viral genomes (vg) per animal. The virus was provided by Hanbio Biotechnology Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China). Four and six weeks after injection, MI surgery and experimental inventions were carried out. Animals in each group above were randomly assigned. The MI model operator was blinded to group allocation. No statistical methods were used to pre-determine sample sizes. Male C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized with 2% 2,2,2-Tribromoethanol (0.1 mL/10 g body weight) intraperitoneally (i.p.). The anesthetized mice were immobilized and intubated for ventilate with a frequency of 110 breaths per minute (maintained by UGO BASILE S.R.L. Italy). Next, we ligated the left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) of the exposed heart by 7-0 nylon 1-2 mm from the lower edge of the left atrium, this blockage lasted for 24 h. Ischemia is confirmed by ST-segment elevation in ECGs and sudden local pallor of the myocardium. Animals were excluded from the study if they expired during anesthesia or surgical instrumentation, developed severe intercurrent illness unrelated to the experimental procedure, or exhibited technically inadequate recordings or tissue samples that precluded reliable analysis. All animals were monitored daily throughout the study period, and any mouse displaying signs of distress, pain, or moribund condition was promptly euthanized to minimize suffering in accordance with institutional animal welfare guidelines. At the predetermined experimental endpoints, mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide (CO₂) inhalation followed by cervical dislocation as a secondary method to confirm death, and tissues were then harvested immediately for subsequent histological and molecular analyses.

## Echocardiography
To determine the cardiac function of mice in different treatment groups, the M-mode echocardiography of hearts was acquired by Vevo1100 Imaging System (SN 0429MM, Canada) equipped with a 10-MH2 phased-array transducer. A minimum of six consecutive cardiac cycles were obtained each mouse, left ventricle (LV) ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) were collected as assessments of cardiac function. These data are presented as the average of measurements of three consecutive beats.

## TTC Staining
To determine the infarct size, we removed the intact hearts from mice and frozen it immediately in − 80 °C for 8–10 min and sliced the myocardium into 2 mm thick slices. The slices were incubated with 2% 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (Cat. G3005, Solarbio, China) for 20 min at 37 °C in the dark before being imaged.

## Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining
To detect cardiac histological morphological changes, including inflammatory reaction and tissue damage, H&E staining was used. Briefly, the hearts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for at least 24 h, then dehydrated and paraffin-embedded by dehydrater followed by collection of serials 5 μm sections cut by paraffin slicer (HM 340E, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for H&E staining. The sections were dewaxed in xylene and dehydrated through an alcohol gradient and then stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Excess dye was rinsed off by PBS and the tissue sections were sealed with a neutral resin seal sheet for observation under light microscopy (SN 6A43410, Olympus BX53F, Japan).

## Electron microscope
The heart sample was fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and rinsed with 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffered saline (PBS). The samples were fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide at 4 °C for 2 h and stained with 1% uranyl acetate. After dehydrated in ethanol, the samples were embedded in epoxy resin and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The photographs were captured by a JEM-1200 electron microscope (JEOL Ltd, Japan).

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
Mouse serum was separated from whole blood by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 min. Cell culture medium was collected and centrifuged at 4 °C for 5 min at 1000 rpm to remove dead cells or debris. The concentrations of LDH (SN 14092214, Bio Tek, USA), IL-1β (MM-0040M1, MEIMIAN, China), and IL-18 (MM-0169M1, MEIMIAN, China) were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance of each well at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

## Isolation of adult mouse cardiomyocytes
The hearts were rapidly separated from mice and the coronary arteries perfused via the aorta with buffer containing calcium (in mM: NaCl 123, KCl 5.4, HEPES 10, NaH2PO4 0.33, MgCl2 1.0, glucose 10 and CaCl2 0.2.) to facilitate expelling luminal blood. Then the hearts were retrogradely perfused with calcium-free buffer (in mM: NaCl 123, KCl 5.4, HEPES 10, NaH2PO4 0.33, MgCl2 1.0 and glucose 10.) and type II collagenase buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) (1 mg/mL) on a constant-flow Langendorff apparatus for about 10 min. Cell suspension settled naturally at room temperature for 20 min and discard supernatant, KB solution (in mM: glutamic acid 70, taurine 15, KCl 30, HEPES 10, KH2PO4 10, EGTA 0.5, glucose 11 and MgCl2.6H2O 0.5 in deionized water, adjusts pH to 7.4) resuspended cell pellet and added paraformaldehyde for immobilization. The fixed cells were washed and spread on slides used for immunofluorescence staining.

## Neonatal mouse ventricular cardiomyocytes (NMVCs) culture and treatment
NMVCs were isolated and cultured from the ventricles of neonatal C57BL/6 mice (1–3 days old). Briefly, the hearts were collected from the neonatal mice. After washing off the excess blood in sterile PBS solution, the hearts were transferred to a 50 mL centrifuge tube containing a mixture of 60% PBS and 40% trypsin. Then, the ventricular myocardium was isolated and digested with type II collagenase buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) (0.8 mg/mL) in a rotary shaker at 37 °C. The obtained cells were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min and resuspended by high glucose DMEM complete medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. After 1.5 h incubation (5% CO2, 95% humidified air, 37 °C), NMVCs were isolated from all species of cells via a differential time adherent method and incubated for another 48 h under the same conditions. NMVCs were used in experiments after the cells formed a confluent monolayer and contracted in synchrony. To induce hypoxia injury, NMVCs were incubated with hypoxic condition (1% O2, 99% N2, 37 °C) for 24 h. The Dnmt3a, Caspase-8 and Hes1 overexpression pcDNA3.1 plasmid were generated by Hanbio Technologies (Shanghai, China). Small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting Dnmt3a (sequence: GCACAACAGAGAAACCTAA) and Caspase-8 (sequence: GCGCAGACCACAAGAACAA) was obtained from Ribio Technologies (Guangzhou, China). pcDNA3.1 vector and nonspecific siRNA (si-NC) were used as negative controls. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The whole process of cell culture is mycoplasma-free.

## Hoechst 33342 and Propidium Iodide (PI) Double Staining
To determine the cell death in diverse treatment groups, the Hoechst/PI staining assay was performed using Hoechst/PI staining (CA1120, Solarbio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells seeded in 6-well plates were washed once with PBS gently and then incubated with mixed staining solution at 37 °C for 15 min, protected from light. Photographs were captured with an OLYMPUS Microscope (SN 4L43432, Japan).

## Western blot analysis
The total protein samples were purified from mouse myocardium and NMVCs with RIPA Lysis Buffer containing 1% protease inhibitor in an ice bath. Protein quantification was performed using the BCA Protein Assay Kit. Then proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (7.5%–2%) and transferred to a nitrocellulose filter membrane. The membranes were blocked in tris-buffered saline containing 5% milk for 2 h and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h in darkroom. The membranes scanned and analyzed with the Odyssey Infrared Imaging System (NE 68504, LI-COR, USA). Western blot strips were measured by area × OD using Odyssey v1.2 software and normalized to β-actin as a loading control.
Primary antibodies including rabbit anti-Dnmt3a antibody (1:1000, ab188470, Abcam, USA), rabbit anti-Caspase-8 antibody (1:500, 4790, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), rabbit anti-Cleaved Caspase-8 (1:500, 8592S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), rabbit anti-Gsdmd antibody (1:500, ab219800, Abcam, USA), rabbit anti-IL-1β antibody (1:500, 16806-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), rabbit anti-IL-18 antibody (1:500, 10663-1-AP, Proteintech, USA), rabbit anti-ubiquitin antibody (1:200, sc-8017, SANTA CRUZ, USA) and mouse anti-β-actin antibody (1:10000, AF7018, Affinity, USA). The secondary antibody, including IRDye® 800CW Goat anti-Mouse IgG (1:10000, 925-32210, LI-COR, USA) and IRDye® 800CW Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (1:10000, 925-32211, LI-COR, USA).

## Co-immunoprecipitations (Co-IP)
To determine the interaction between proteins, a total of 1000 μg protein obtained from the cultured NMVCs and myocardium through splitting by IP lysis buffer containing 1% protease inhibitor. The lysates were precleared with Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (HY-K0202, Sigma, Germany) for 1 h at 4 °C to eliminate nonspecific binding and then incubated with 10 μg Caspase8-p18 primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, 30 μL beads were added to the mixture and incubated with gentle shaking overnight at 4 °C. The beads were collected by magnetic separation, and the co-immunoprecipitated products bound to the beads were eluted with the elution buffer. Then, SDS-PAGE Protein Sample Loading Buffer was added into the elution buffer and boiled for 10 min. The supernatant was taken for Western blot analysis. The antibodies used for Co-IP were Caspase-8-p18 (8592S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) and Dnmt3a (20954-1-AP, proteintech, USA).

## RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis
Total RNA samples were extracted from mouse ventricular myocardium and cultured NMVCs by using Trizol Reagent. The concentration of RNA was determined by measuring the absorbance ratio of 260/280 nm using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Total RNA (1 μg) was reverse transcribed to cDNA using a Reverse Transcription kit (TOYOBO, Japan) by GeneAmp® PCR System 9700 (Applied Biosystems, USA). The Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO, Japan) on the Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, 7500 FAST, USA). The relative expression level was analyzed by using 2-ΔΔct method based on Ct values, and normalized to the level of 18 s as an internal control. The sequences for all the primers used for qPCR are listed as following: Dnmt3a: Forward primer: 5’-GGGGACAAGAATGCTACCAA-3’, Reverse primer: 5’-GCTTCTCCACACACACCACT-3’; Caspase-8: Forward primer: 5’-ACAAACCTCGGGGATACTGTC-3’, Reverse primer: 5’-AGTGCAGTCGTCGTAAGATA
CTA-3’; 18 s: Forward primer: 5’-CCTGGATACCGCAGCTAGGA-3’, Reverse primer: 5’-GCGGCGCAATACGAATGCCCC-3’.

## DNA extraction and DNA methylation-specific PCR (MSP)
To determine the methylation status of the CpG island on the Caspase-8 gene, we carried out an MSP experiment. Total DNA was extracted from NMVCs using the Genomic DNA Extraction kit (Cat: DP304-02, TIAGEN, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Bisulfite treatment of genomic DNA was performed using the CpGenomeTM Turbo Bisulfite Modification Kit (Cat: S7847, Merck, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. PCRs were performed with Methylation-specific PCR Kit (Cat: EM101, TIAGEN, China) using DNA as templates, the products were used for agarose gel electrophoresis to identify gene methylation. The MSP primers were designed using the MethPrimer website (http://www.urogene.org/methprimer), the sequences are presented in following: Caspase-8: Unmethylated-Forward Primer: 5’-TTGGAAAGTTTAAGTTAGTGGAG-3’, Unmethylated-Reverse Primer: 5’-CCACCAATAAAATCTCAACCC-3’; Caspase-8: Methylated-Forward Primer: 5’-GGTTCGGAAAGTTTAAGTTAGC-3’, Methylated-Reverse Primer: 5’-CACCGATAAAATCTCGACCC-3’.

## Immunofluorescence
Frozen sections (6 μm) of ventricular myocardium and acute isolated cardiomyocytes coated on slides were used for immunofluorescence staining. Samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 1 h at room temperature. After 2 h of blocking with 10% normal goat serum, samples were incubated with mixed primary antibodies against Caspase-8 (1:100, A0215, ABclonal, USA), Gsdmd-N (1:100, ab215203, Abcam, USA), and α-actinin (1:200, A7811, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) overnight at 4 °C. Then, samples were incubated with the fluorescence-conjugated anti-rabbit/ anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h, followed by nuclei staining with DAPI for 15 min at room temperature in the darkroom. Pictures were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (LSM 880, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
To detect the binding relationship of DNA methyltransferase or TF with the gene, ChIP assay was performed using the PierceTM Agarose ChIP Kit (26156; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, acute isolated cardiomyocytes or NMVCs from different groups were cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 mins at room temperature, and the lysate was prepared with an appropriate lysis buffer containing protease inhibitor. Then the lysates were subjected to ChIP assays using antibodies against Dnmt3a (IMG-268A, Novus, USA), Hes1 (NBP1-47791, Novus, USA), Mycn (NB200-109, Novus, USA), Tfec (13547-1-AP, Proteintech, USA) and Zbtb33 (PA5-110588, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 12 h at 4 °C. DNA was captured with streptavidin-coated magnetic beads. DNA complexes were eluted, digested with proteinase K, and analyzed by qPCR. (Caspase-8: Forward primer: 5’-CAGAGCAGTCGGGTTCCTTT-3’, Reverse primer: 5’-GGTCCTGAGCCAAACTACAA-3’)

## Statistical analysis
All data were presented as mean ± SEM and were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 9.2.1 software. The intergroup comparisons of one variable were evaluated by t test and one-way ANOVA, two or more variables by two-way ANOVA. The experimental data all conform to the normal distribution, and the variance is similar between the comparison groups. The results were considered statistically significant when P-values are less than 0.05.
