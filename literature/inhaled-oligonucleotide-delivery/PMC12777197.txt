## Abstract

## Background
Neonatal Hypoxic-Ischemic Encephalopathy (HIE) is a critical lifelong disabling neonatal disease with insufficient effective therapies. Signaling molecules derived from Astrocyte-Neuron Crosstalk have the potential to improve the prognosis, but relevant HIE research is limited.

## Methods
We established hypoxic–ischemic brain damage (HIBD) in postnatal day 7 rat pups using the Rice–Vannucci model, and verified the injury both structurally and hemodynamically by histopathology and laser speckle contrast imaging. Whole-brain single-cell RNA sequencing was performed at P10, and proteomic profiling was conducted on astrocytes subjected to oxygen–glucose deprivation. We charted astrocyte diversity in situ using multiplex immunofluorescence. Thyroid hormones and signaling pathways were quantified by ELISA and Western blotting. Therapeutic efficacy was assessed by histopathology and the Morris water maze.

## Results
A brain-wide single-cell transcriptomic atlas revealed a strong transcriptional coupling between astrocytes and neurons. Among astrocytes, we identified and functionally characterized a Dio2⁺Slc1a2⁺ subpopulation with metabolic and neuroprotective features, which was markedly depleted after HIBD. Pseudotime trajectories showed dynamic astrocyte state transitions over time, contextualizing the loss and reparative potential of the Dio2⁺ subpopulation. In vivo and in vitro, boosting astrocytic Dio2 or delivering exogenous Triiodothyronine(T3) enhanced neural repair and improved behavior across both early and long-term stages.

## Conclusions
We defined a previously unrecognized neuroprotective astrocyte subpopulation and revealed temporal heterogeneity in astrocytic inflammatory responses in HIE. We also identified a T3-mediated astrocyte–neuron crosstalk mechanism that mitigated HIBD, highlighting a tractable therapeutic avenue.

## Introduction
Neonatal hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) is a severe neurological condition caused by acute perinatal asphyxia, leading to cerebral tissue injury and functional impairment. Beyond early mortality, survivors often face lifelong epilepsy, cerebral palsy, and cognitive deficits, burdening families and health systems [ ]. Although current care is largely supportive, including hemodynamic stabilization, seizure control, and fluid management, no disease-modifying therapy exists.Currently, the first-line clinical treatment for neonates with HIE is hypothermia therapy. Although this therapy can significantly reduce the mortality rate of affected neonates, it still cannot completely prevent the occurrence of neurological sequelae in some neonates with moderate to severe HIE [ , ]. Therefore, systematically analyzing the pathophysiological mechanisms of HIE and exploring potential complementary or alternative therapeutic strategies hold important clinical significance and scientific value for improving the prognosis of these neonates.
Astrocyte–neuron crosstalk is a central determinant of neuronal fate within the neurovascular unit(NVU) [ ]. Through metabolic provisioning, signal transduction, and immune–inflammatory modulation, astrocytes shape both acute injury responses and subsequent repair [ ]. While the Astrocyte-Neuron Lactate Shuttle (ANLS) was once cited to account for how astrocytes and neurons mitigate neuronal death, its adequacy in ischemia–reperfusion is increasingly being questioned [ , ]. However, practical observations indicate that the lactate surge during reperfusion can aggravate injury, and isotope-tracing studies show that most activity-induced lactate is cleared via lymphatic and vascular pathways rather than fueling neurons. This theory–observation gap suggests that alternative astrocyte-derived mediators may more effectively sustain neuronal bioenergetics under stress [ ]. The discrepancy between theory and empirical evidence suggests the need to explore other substances involved in Astrocyte-Neuron Crosstalk that can enhance the intrinsic energy supply of neurons.
Triiodothyronine (T3) is one such candidate [ , ]. Within the neurovascular unit, astrocytes are the primary source of endogenous T3 in the brain. They preferentially take up thyroxine (T4) from the systemic circulation via Slco1c1 (also known as OATP1C1), and catalyze the conversion of T4 to biologically active T3 by virtue of highly expressed type II deiodinase (Dio2).Then, T3 is released into the extracellular space via monocarboxylate transporter 8 (MCT8) for utilization by neurons.Accumulating studies shows that T3 is essential for neural development and synaptic maturation, and preclinical studies in adult ischemic models suggest neuroprotective benefits of T3 administration [ , ]. Dio2 overexpression or T3 treatment could improve endometrial epithelial-mesenchymal transition in intrauterine adhesions by promoting autophagy [ ]. However, in the context of neonatal HIE, the specific role of T3 in astrocyte–neuron communication and its therapeutic potential remain largely untested.
Here, using the Rice–Vannucci model in P7 rat pups and brain-wide single-cell RNA sequencing at P10, we map the astrocyte–neuron landscape after hypoxic–ischemic injury. We identify a Dio2⁺Slc1a2⁺ astrocyte subpopulation with metabolic and neuroprotective features that is selectively diminished in HIBD. Based on the biological functions of the highly expressed genes in this astrocyte subpopulation, Dio2⁺Slc1a2⁺ astrocytes can prevent excitotoxicity by clearing glutamate from the synaptic cleft and maintain T3 homeostasis within the NVU by providing sufficient T3. The reduction of this astrocyte subpopulation in HIBD is bound to cause an imbalance in glutamate and/or T3 homeostasis.Trajectory and intercellular communication analyses, together with in vivo/in vitro validation, indicate that reduced Dio2 associates with dampened secretion of neuroprotective factors and impaired astrocyte–neuron coupling. Either upregulating Dio2 expression in astrocytes or directly supplementing with T3 can ameliorate autophagic impairment and reduce the duration of ISR in both cell types. This improvement further enhanced the Astrocyte-Neuron Crosstalk, ultimately leading to better outcomes in both the acute phase and long term after HIBD.
In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that upregulating Dio2 expression in astrocytes not only alleviates the stress burden of astrocytes themselves but also exerts a positive regulatory effect on neurons by enhancing T3 shuttling crosstalk between astrocytes and neurons.

## Methods

## Animal models and experimental procedures
In this study, postnatal day 7 (P7) Sprague-Dawley rat pups, weighing between 20 and 25 g and procured from Zhejiang Vital River Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd., were utilized. The hypoxic-ischemic brain damage (HIBD) model was induced using a modified Rice-Vannucci protocol [ , ]. Under isoflurane anesthesia, the left common carotid artery was ligated, and the subjects were subsequently exposed to hypoxic conditions (8% O₂) at 37.5 °C for a duration of 2 h. Immediately following the hypoxic event, pups in the treatment group received intraperitoneal injections of triiodothyronine at a dosage of 25 µg/kg, whereas those in the control group were administered an equivalent volume of solvent to serve as a sham treatment. These injections were administered every 24 h over a period of 3 consecutive days. Post-procedure, the pups were returned to their maternal care.In accordance with the “3R Principle” and while considering both statistical requirements and the need to meet the error degrees of freedom, a total of 176 rat pups were used in this study.

## Single-cell RNA-sequencing
Rat pups brain tissues (0.1–0.5 cm³) were kept on ice in Tissue Storage Solution during transport. On arrival, they were minced into ~ 0.5 mm² pieces and incubated in a dissociation cocktail at 37 °C for 15–20 min. The cell suspension was filtered to remove debris, centrifuged, and resuspended in RBC lysis buffer for 1 min. After washing with DMEM and centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in PBS, cleared of debris, and counted. The cell concentration was adjusted to 700–1200 cells/µL for further experiments.

## Library and sequencing
Single-cell suspensions were processed using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform, and sequencing libraries were prepared utilizing the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3 (10x Genomics), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq platform, employing 150 bp paired-end reads.

## Basic bioinformatics analysis
After generating FASTQ files, genome alignment was conducted using Cell Ranger v.7.1.0 (10X) with a tailored RGSC 7.0 reference genome. In the R environment, Seurat v.4.3.0 was employed to exclude low-quality cells—defined as those with fewer than 200 or more than 5,000 detected genes, or mitochondrial read proportions exceeding 10%. Prior to dataset integration, the filtered samples underwent normalization via the SCTransform method.​.
The integrated dataset was first scaled, followed by UMAP-based dimensionality reduction that utilized the top 30 principal components. Unsupervised Louvain clustering was carried out on a shared nearest neighbour graph, with the final clustering resolution set to 0.8. To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each cluster, the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat was employed; meanwhile, GO enrichment analyses for each cluster were implemented using ClusterProfiler v.4.6.2. Manual annotation of each cluster was conducted by leveraging DEGs and GO enrichment processes.​.
For differential gene analysis between samples, the FindMarkers function in Seurat was used, which adopted the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Bonferroni correction for multiple-testing adjustment. Cell-type assignment for each cluster was manually determined by integrating the expression patterns of canonical markers and relevant literature findings. Additionally, heatmaps, dot plots, and violin plots—used to visualize the expression of markers for cell-type identification—were generated through the DotPlot function in Seurat.

## Dimensionality reduction clustering analysis for subpopulation identification of major cell types
Initially, 3,000 highly variable genes were identified utilizing the `highly_variable_genes` function in Scanpy. Following data normalization, dimensionality reduction was conducted through principal component analysis (PCA), with the number of principal components determined based on the PCA variance ratio and PCA loadings. Subsequently, a cell-cell k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) network was constructed using the integrated PCA results, with edge weights calculated via the Jaccard coefficient. Cell clusters were then identified using the Louvain algorithm with a resolution parameter set to 0.5. Finally, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cell cluster were identified using Scanpy’s `rank_genes_groups` function with a t-test, and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was employed for the visualization of clustering results.

## Differential expression and gene set enrichment analysis
This study used Scanpy (v1.9.3) to perform differential expression analysis on single-cell transcriptomic data to identify DEGs across cell types. Cells were grouped by sample information, and data for each group were extracted. Leiden clustering identified cell subpopulations, filtering for clusters with multiple samples per group. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was then used to find significant DEGs in valid subpopulations, resulting in differential expression profiles for each group.

## Transcriptomic correlation analysis between cell types
For each cell type, we computed the mean expression levels of all genes and evaluated their correlations utilizing Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Heatmaps were subsequently generated using the ComplexHeatmap package version 2.4.386.

## Cell cycle state analysis
The determination of individual cell cycle positions was conducted utilizing a data-driven algorithm provided by the R package “tricycle.” Target cell subsets were extracted from Seurat objects and subsequently converted into the SingleCellExperiment format. Cycle space projection was executed via the “project_cycle_space” function, with staging informed by the Schwabe model. Visualization of the cycle distribution was accomplished using UMAP and ggplot2. The analysis predominantly employed tools within the R environment, including tricycle, Seurat, scater, dplyr, and ggplot2.

## Trajectory inference and differential analysis
Trajectory analysis was conducted primarily using Monocle 3 version 0.2.299. To align with the data characteristics, diffusion map DC values were scaled by a factor of 100. Cell clustering was executed using the `cluster_cells` function with parameters set to `partition_qval = 0.05` and `num_iter = 1`. Trajectory learning was facilitated through the `learn_graph` function, and pseudotime was assigned using the `order_cells` function, with root cells designated based on prior knowledge. Intra-branch differential gene analysis was performed utilizing the `graph_test` function with the `neighbor_graph` parameter set to “principal_graph.” Expression levels were processed using loess smoothing with a span of 0.5. Heatmaps illustrating the top 1000 differential genes were generated using ComplexHeatmap version 2.4.386.

## Cell-cell communication analysis
Intercellular communication was examined utilizing the CellphoneDB and CellChat analytical tools. Initially, gene symbols within Seurat objects were converted to rat gene symbols and formatted according to the requirements of CellphoneDB. For the CellphoneDB analysis, parameters were configured to 50 iterations, with a precision of 3, and a minimum threshold of 10% of cells in each group expressing a particular gene. Receptor-ligand pairs with a p-value of less than 0.05 were filtered, with a focus on critical communications. The CellChat analysis workflow encompassed data preprocessing, subpopulation selection, communication network construction, database selection, identification of highly expressed genes and interactions, probability calculation, screening of significant communications, and pathway analysis. Ultimately, a comprehensive landscape of intercellular communication was derived through the integration of these networks.

## Laser speckle contrast imaging
Cerebral blood flow (CBF) was evaluated using laser speckle contrast imaging with the PeriCam PSI System (PeriCam PSI HR; Perimed, Sweden). Specifically, 48 h post-surgery, the rat pups were anesthetized with isoflurane (3–4% for induction and 1–2% for maintenance via inhalation). Subsequently, their scalps were incised to adequately expose the cranium. The subjects were positioned under a 650-nm red laser, and raw speckle images were captured with a camera positioned 14 cm above the cranial surface. During the measurement process, the rat pups were maintained on an electric heating blanket to ensure a stable body temperature of 37 °C. The laser speckle images and relative CBF within the region of interest (ROI) were analyzed using Perisoft software (Perimed, Sweden). Following the completion of measurements, the scalps were sutured, and the rat pups were returned to their mothers after fully recovering from anesthesia.

## Pathological staining
Following hypoxic-ischemic (HI) injury, brain tissue specimens were collected on the third day post-injury. The rat pups were anesthetized with isoflurane, after which cardiac perfusion was performed using 25 ml of sterile saline, followed by an equal volume of 4% paraformaldehyde. The brain samples were subsequently immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stored at 4 °C for 24 h before being embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded blocks were sectioned into slices 5 μm in thickness to facilitate the visualization of hemispherical integrity and functional neurons in coronal sections of the cerebral cortex and hippocampus. Sections were positioned and matched using hippocampal anatomical landmarks, selecting the mid-posterior hippocampal level (this level corresponds to an anteroposterior coordinate of approximately − 2.4 mm from bregma).The brain sections were subjected to deparaffinization and hydration processes, followed by staining with hematoxylin and eosin (H & E) or Nissl solution (Sohrab, Beijing, China). The results of the histological staining were evaluated and documented using an optical microscope, and quantitative analysis was conducted using ImageJ software.

## Multiplexed immunohistochemistry
The paraffin-embedded tissue sections underwent deparaffinization and rehydration. Following antigen retrieval, the slides were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity and subsequently blocked with 10% goat serum. The slides were then incubated overnight with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and the corresponding tyramide signal amplification (TSA) the following day. After counterstaining the nuclei with DAPI, quenching tissue autofluorescence, and sealing the slides, imaging of the sections was conducted using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope and processed with Fiji software. The antibodies utilized in this study included Dio2 (HUABIO, ER1907-46,1:100), Slc1a2 (HUABIO, HA601403,1:2000), Aldh1l1 (Proteintech, 17390,1:200), Pcna (Proteintech, 10205,1:2000), and Fabp7 (Proteintech, 51010-1-AP,1:100).The fluorescent dyes used were TYR-520 (green) for Slc1a2 or Fabp7 detection, TYR-570 (yellow) for Aldh1l1 detection, TYR-690 (red) for Dio2 or Pcna detection.

## Morris water maze
The Morris Water Maze (MWM) test is a behavioral experiment designed to evaluate cognitive function in animals. We utilized the MWM test 21 days post-HI injury to examine the learning and memory capabilities of the test animals, where they swam to locate a hidden submerged platform. A black circular pool, 140 cm in diameter and 50 cm in height, was set up in a room devoid of noise and light disturbances. The water level in the pool was 1 cm above the active platform. The water was colored black using non-toxic black ink, and the pool was divided into four equal sections. Rats underwent training for 6 days, after which the platform was removed. Swimming paths, time taken to find the platform, and the number of times the platform was found were recorded. The experiment was carried out using the SLY-WMS Morris Water Maze experimental system.

## Cell culture and treatment
The CTX TNA2 cell line was purchased from Xiamen Immocen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. This cell line was established from primary cultures of type 1 astrocytes derived from the frontal cortical tissue of 1-day-old rats. The cultures were transfected 3 days after initial plating with a DNA construct containing the simian virus 40 (SV40) early oncogenic region, under the transcriptional control of the human GFAP promoter with the murine phosphoglycerate kinase gene promoter (pGFA-SV-Tt) and pPGK-neo. Transfectants were selected using G418 and cloned. Cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, in a constant temperature and humidity incubator (5% CO₂, 37℃).
Primary cortical neurons were isolated from the cerebral cortex of Sprague-Dawley rats within 24 h of birth. The rat pups were disinfected with 75% ethanol, followed by brain dissection to separate the cerebral cortex, which was then rinsed with cold PBS. The tissue was digested with papain-EDTA at 37 °C for 15 min and centrifuged; the resulting pellet was seeded onto poly-D-lysine-coated culture plates. After incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 6 h, the medium was replaced with Neurobasal Medium containing B27, L-glutamine, and antibiotics (Penicillin-Streptomycin).
To simulate hypoxic-ischemic brain damage (HIBD) in vitro, primary neurons were exposed to an oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) model for a duration of 2 hours [ , ].Specifically, during OGD incubation of neurons, after removing the neuronal culture medium, the neurons were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 70011–044) preheated to 37 °C. Subsequently, OGD buffer (containing 116 mM sodium chloride, 5.4 mM potassium chloride, 0.8 mM magnesium sulfate, 26.2 mM sodium bicarbonate, 1 mM sodium dihydrogen phosphate, 1.8 mM calcium chloride, 0.01 mM glycine, pH 7.4) was added to the neuronal culture system; the culture system was then incubated in a 37 °C hypoxic incubator for 2 h, with the incubator connected to an oxygen sensor/monitor (Biospherix, Inc., New York, USA). The oxygen concentration inside the incubator was maintained below 1% by continuously purging nitrogen gas into the incubator. Meanwhile, the neurons were subjected to different corresponding treatments according to experimental requirements: for example, triiodothyronine (T3) treatment (at a concentration of 60 nM/mL) was applied during OGD; or a co-culture system was established with astrocytes during OGD; or proteins were directly extracted from primary neurons after OGD for subsequent experiments.
The OGD treatment process for astrocytes was basically the same as that for neurons. The difference was that the OGD duration for astrocytes was 8 h, and the astrocytes were also given different corresponding treatments based on experimental needs: for instance, after OGD, the culture medium was replaced and reoxygenation treatment was performed for different durations, or proteins were directly extracted from astrocytes after OGD for subsequent experiments.
When a co-culture system with primary neurons needed to be established, astrocytes first underwent 6 h of OGD treatment alone in the upper chamber of a Transwell insert, and then were subjected to an additional 2 h of OGD treatment synchronously with primary neurons.

## Overexpression and knockdown of Dio2 in Ctx tna2 cells
The Dio2 overexpression plasmid and siRNA were created by OBiO Technology. Ctx tna2 cells (0.5 × 10⁶) were plated in 6-well plates, and after 24 h, transfected with 4.0 µg plasmid using 10 µl Lipofectamine 2000 and 600 µl DMEM per well. Six hours later, cells were washed with PBS and cultured in complete DMEM for 24 h.

## Western blotting
Total proteins were extracted from brain tissues and cells using RIPA lysis buffer, where the brain tissues were derived from rat pups in the Sham group, HIBD group, and HIBD + T3 group. Protein concentrations were then determined with a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein (60 µg for in vivo samples, 40 µg for in vitro samples) were loaded onto 10% SDS-PAGE gels and subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 3 h, the membranes were washed 3 times with 1× TBST and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C.
The antibodies used included β-actin(Proteintech; 66009-1-Ig), Dio2 (Zenbio;#161625),GFAP(Proteintech;16825-1-AP),P62(Zenbio;#382862),LC3B(Proteintech;81004-1-RR),mTOR(Proteintech; 28273-1-AP), p-mTOR (Proteintech; 80596-1-RR), AMPK (Zenbio; #R380431),p-AMPK(Zenbio;#R381164),ERK (Proteintech; 11257-1-AP), p-ERK(Zenbio;#310065),AKT(Proteintech;10176-2-AP),p-AKT(Proteintech;66444-1-Ig), Eif2α(Proteintech;11170-1-AP),and p-Eif2α(Proteintech; 68023-1-Ig). Image grayscale values were collected and analyzed using Image J software.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay
According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the time periods and samples required for the experiment were selected. Complement Component 3 (JONLNBIO, JL10560); T3 (Abbexa, abx513003); fT3 (Abbexa, abx350117).

## Statistical analysis
All statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism software (Version 9.0, GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, California, USA). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (mean ± SEM). All data were derived from no fewer than 5 independent experiments. Student’s t-test was applied for comparisons between two groups; when comparing multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) was initially performed, and subsequent post-hoc multiple comparisons were carried out using Tukey’s test.

## Result

## A single-cell transcriptome atlas of the whole brains of P10 rat pups in the HIBD and sham groups
The brief process of this study was presented in Fig. A. We established a classic unilateral hypoxic–ischemic brain damage (HIBD) model in P10 rat pups; laser speckle flow imaging confirmed successful left common carotid artery ligation (Fig. B, Fig. S1A). Histopathology at 72 h showed characteristic ipsilateral cortical and hippocampal injury with neuronal loss and disorganized cytoarchitecture, whereas sham brains retained normal Nissl body morphology (Figs. C–D, Figs. S1B–C), recapitulating features of neonatal hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy.
 To delineate cell type–specific transcriptional responses, single-cell RNA-seq was performed on brains from three HIBD and three sham pups, yielding 53,259 high-quality cells (Figs. S1D-E). Unsupervised clustering resolved eight major cell classes (Fig. E): astrocytes (SLC1A3, AQP4), neurons (TUBB3), oligodendrocytes (CNP), oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OLIG1), microglia (TREM2), endothelial cells (FLT1), fibroblasts (DCN), and mural cells (RGS5) (Fig. S2A). HIBD induced marked compositional remodeling, with reduced astrocyte and endothelial proportions and a modest relative increase in neurons (Fig. F, Fig. S2B), consistent with a recent report [ , ]. Spearman correlations of aggregated gene expression were used to briefly assess cross–cell type similarity, revealing neurons most closely resembled astrocytes at 72 h (Fig. S2C).
Across HIBD replicates, mature oligodendrocyte and perivascular astrocyte states were selectively depleted. Despite minimal groupwise shifts in the global cell-cycle profile (Fig. S2D), cell type–resolved analysis showed M/G1 accumulation in astrocytes and endothelial cells, while the modest neuronal “cycling” likely reflects progenitor/immature neuron-like populations rather than division of mature neurons (Fig. G). Differential expression and pathway analyses indicated broad immune–inflammatory activation, including cGAS–STING–TNF-α signaling (Fig. H), defining a distinct injury-induced cellular state landscape in neonatal HIE.
Together, these data establish a high-resolution atlas of early HIBD responses, highlighting selective glial depletion, cell type–specific cell-cycle alterations, and robust immune–inflammatory activation.

## Unique metabo-neuroprotective astrocytes downregulated in HIBD
We identified five astrocytic subpopulations in unbiased clustering (Fig. A; Figs. S3A–B), including a metabolic-neuroprotective subtype characterized by co-expression of Dio2/Slc1a2/SerpinF1, alongside proliferative, neural progenitor-like, synaptogenesis-associated, and signaling-regulatory states (Figs. B–C). Differential expression and intersection analyses delineated discrete subtype signatures: hierarchical clustering yielded well-segregated DEG modules and volcano plots nominated representative high-confidence markers (Fig. D; Figs. S3C, D).
 Following HIBD, scRNA-seq analysis revealed transcriptomic shifts in astrocyte subpopulations, including contraction of a Dio2+/Slc1a2+/SerpinF1+ metabolically neuroprotective subset. To assess the functional impact of these changes at the tissue level, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence staining for markers representing key astrocytic functions: Dio2 (thyroid hormone metabolism), Slc1a2 (glutamate transport), Fabp7 (lipid metabolism), and Pcna (active proliferation). Quantitative analysis in cortical sections revealed significant reductions in the density of astrocytes expressing each marker after HIBD (Figs. E–F; Figs. S3E–F), indicating broad functional impairment across metabolic, transport and proliferative capacities.
The concordant decline across all functional markers—rather than compensatory upregulation of any—suggests that HIBD induces widespread astrocyte dysfunction through combined effects of cell loss and functional downregulation in surviving cells (Figs. S3G-H). Notably, even proliferative markers (Pcna) decreased, indicating compromised regenerative capacity. This tissue-level functional decline complements the transcriptomic remodeling observed by scRNA-seq, revealing that injury-driven changes in cell state composition translate into diminished neuroprotective capacity at the tissue scale. From a therapeutic perspective, interventions targeting thyroid hormone-dependent mitochondrial bioenergetics, glutamate buffering, and astrocyte proliferation may be essential for restoring functional neuroprotection [ – ].
Collectively, our data support an injury-driven remodeling of astrocyte state architecture, in which a vulnerable Dio2+SerpinF1+ metaboneuroprotective subset is selectively diminished while synaptogenic/neural progenitor-like states persist or expand, with important implications for the trajectory of lesion evolution.

## Functional states and differentiation trajectories of metabo-neuroprotective astrocytes
Hallmark gene set scores was utilized for each group. Overall, HIBD showed higher scores for complement-related pathways and lower scores for the Wnt/β-catenin, TGF-β and UPR pathways. There was also a general reduction in PI3K–AKT–mTOR and Notch, with no upregulation of mTORC1 (Figs. S4A–D). At the subpopulation level, proliferative astrocytes had the highest G2/M checkpoint scores, while the Metabo-neuroprotective subpopulation had the lowest. Cross-pathway comparison showed consistently lower scores for the latter across the Wnt/β-catenin, TGF-β, Jak/STAT3, PI3K–AKT–mTOR and UPR pathways (Figs. A–H). This aligns with the Metabo-neuroprotective subpopulation’s reduced proliferative profile.
 Compared to the control group, the distribution of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) varied among astrocyte subtypes. Neural progenitor-like astrocytes had the highest counts, while Metabo neuroprotective astrocytes had the lowest. Gene set analyses (GO/KEGG) across subtypes revealed thyroid hormone signalling to be a common theme. While this signal was detectable in multiple astrocyte groups, including neural progenitor-like and homeostatic/structural-leaning subtypes, it was most coherent in the Metabo neuroprotective population. In this group, DEGs were enriched for lipid and sterol biosynthesis alongside thyroid hormone–responsive programs, including “response to thyroid hormone” and the “thyroid hormone signaling pathway.” Several ISR/proteostasis-associated chaperones and RNA-processing factors were found to cluster within these thyroid hormone-linked terms, suggesting a connection between thyroid hormone signalling and the integrated stress response in this metabolically biased, neuroprotective state (Figs. I–J; Figs. S4E–H).
Using Monocle3, we created a pseudotime trajectory for astrocytes originating in the neural progenitor-like state. Along this trajectory, the cells were divided into three clusters that corresponded to the early, middle and late phases of astrocyte differentiation. The early phase was characterised by programmes linked to neuronal differentiation, glial cell development, gliogenesis and the regulation of axon extension. The middle phase centred on thyroid hormone metabolism, with enrichment for thyroid hormone generation and metabolic processes alongside modulators of proteostasis, such as the negative regulation of peptidase activity. The late phase featured nuclear and cell-cycle control, including the positive regulation of transferase and protein kinase activities, the regulation of protein localisation to the nucleus, the assembly of non-membrane-bounded organelles, and the regulation of the mitotic cell cycle (Figs. A–B).
 In line with the three-phase trajectory, pseudotime profiling of key astrocyte genes revealed phase-specific dynamics. Dio2 and Slc1a2 peaked in the thyroid hormone–metabolic window, after which they declined. Meanwhile, structural/reactive and progenitor-like markers (Gfap、Vim、S100b、Fabp7、Hopx) increased towards the late phase, and Vegfa exhibited delayed elevation, consistent with this transition (see Fig. C). These kinetics suggest that the attenuation of the Dio2/Slc1a2 metabolic module occurs prior to the upregulation of the structural/reactive and nuclear/cell-cycle programmes. Western blotting corroborated this temporal order in vivo: DIO2 protein levels decreased at 24 h relative to 3 h after HIBD, while GFAP levels increased more slowly over the same period (Figs. D–E).
Taken together, these observations support the working hypothesis that a transient thyroid hormone-linked metabolic phase occurs between a homeostatic baseline and subsequent structural/proliferative remodelling, potentially creating conditions conducive to later inflammatory phenotypes [ ].

## Dio2 suppression with impaired neuroprotective astrocyte signaling in HIBD​
During HIBD, astrocyte subpopulations and neurons form a dynamic communication landscape mediated by ligand–receptor interactions. To delineate pathway-level changes, we applied CellChat to model and compare interaction networks between astrocyte subsets and neurons in Sham versus HIBD brains, quantifying communication strength from a curated ligand–receptor database and visualizing network architecture (Figs. S6A-B). This analysis identified crosstalk from metabolically neuroprotective astrocytes to synaptogenic astrocytes via the growth factor pleiotrophin (PTN), implicating paracrine regulation of synaptogenesis. Pathway enrichment further highlighted NCAM-related signaling, pointing to roles in adhesion, migration, and synapse assembly (Fig. S6C).
In HIBD versus Sham, astrocyte-centered communication networks show increased interaction count and strength with other cells—particularly with neural progenitor-like astrocytes (Figs. A). Further analysis of astrocyte-derived signaling mediators revealed a significant downregulation in the expression of several key neuroprotective factors, including PTN, a change predicted to weaken astrocyte support for neurons and worsen injury after HIBD (Fig. C). Mapping sources and targets of astrocyte-secreted cues further revealed an near-complete loss of neuronal reception of PTN and related signals in HIBD (Fig. D; Fig. S7A).
 Given the temporal specificity of Dio2 in metabo-neuroprotective astrocytes and its putative protective role, we assessed the global expression levels of Dio2 in astrocytes within the brains of postnatal day 10 (P10) rat pups from both Sham and HIBD groups. Immunostaining results indicated a significant reduction in the global expression of Dio2 in astrocytes in the HIBD group (Fig. E; Fig. S7B). This observation suggests a possible association with a decrease in the number of Metabo-neuroprotective Astrocyte subpopulations. Western blot analysis corroborated these findings, revealing that although Dio2 exhibited a transient increase during the ultra-acute phase, its overall protein expression in the brains of P10 rat pups in the HIBD group demonstrated a downregulated trend (Fig. F; Fig. S7C), aligning with previous phenotypic analyses.

## Dio2 promotes autophagy initiation to regulate the integrated stress response status of astrocytes
To analyse astrocytic molecular cascades in the context of ischaemia-hypoxia, we combined proteomics and single-cell transcriptomics using the CTXNA2 OGD model (Fig. A). Proteomic analysis revealed a coordinated remodeling of the astrocyte proteome following OGD treatment (Fig. B), with the remodeling centered on extracellular matrix (ECM) components and early autophagy initiators. Specifically, we observed a clear segregation between the control and OGD-treated groups (Fig. S8A), and identified 236 upregulated proteins and 118 downregulated proteins (Fig. S8B). A notable proportion of these proteins mapped to extracellular or plasma membrane categories (Fig. C), consistent with the ongoing changes in the pericellular interface. Concurrently, CEBPB, ULK1, and Beclin1 were reduced (Fig. D; Fig. S8C) [ – ], suggesting the attenuation of autophagy initiation. Functional enrichment underscored a “dual-axis” signature involving ECM remodeling and mitophagy (Fig. E). Integration with single-cell HIBD data showed Dio2 expression tracking with ULK1 and other autophagy-related transcripts (Fig. S8D), supporting the hypothesis that Dio2 helps sustain early autophagy initiation under metabolic stress.
 Given the inferred impairment of autophagy initiation, we profiled Dio2 dynamics across OGD/reoxygenation (OGD/R). The results showed that Dio2 increased 6 h after OGD-induced injury followed by reoxygenation(Fig. F; Fig. S9A). This temporal pattern was highly consistent with the reduction in excess SQSTM1/p62 protein and the re-equilibration of the LC3B-II/LC3B-I ratio (Fig. G; Figs. S9B-C). Loss- and gain-of-function experiments supported the existence of a causal link. Dio2 knockdown reduced the levels of early autophagy markers, whereas overexpression increased them (Fig. H; Figs. S9D–F). To investigate the mechanism of autophagy initiation, we examined canonical upstream signals, including p-ERK, p-AKT, p-AMPK and p-mTOR (Fig. I). Although Dio2 cannot alter the phosphorylation level of AKT, it can change the phosphorylation of ERK and AMPK, and downstream modified mTOR phosphorylation (Fig. J and Figs. S9G-J), which is consistent with the regulation of autophagy initiation by the ERK/AMPK–mTOR pathway. At the systems level, phosphorylated eIF2α increased during OGD and returned to baseline after reoxygenation; however, Dio2 depletion delayed this recovery, maintaining elevated p-eIF2α levels (Fig. K; Fig. S9K). Together, these data suggest that, following a metabolic insult, Dio2 promotes the early re-engagement of autophagy and facilitates the resolution of the ISR.
Collectively, these findings advance a model in which Dio2 functions as a metabolic–signaling node that sustains early autophagy initiation via ERK/AMPK–mTOR modulation and thereby shapes ISR dynamics during recovery from metabolic insult.

## Promoting astrocytic T3 release through Dio2 upregulation to mitigate neuronal damage and enhance prognosis in HIBD
Extensive literature establishes T3 as a regulator of interneuron specification (Arx, Dlx1 pathways), progenitor/stem cell maintenance (Sox2), neuronal maturation, and post-injury plasticity [ – ]. Our earlier multi-omics analysis identified a coordinated co-decline of Dio2 and transcripts initiating autophagy, implicating a dual vulnerability that couples reduced hormone bioactivation to impaired proteostasis. This convergence led us to propose that elevating astrocytic Dio2 would restore autophagy and mitigate ISR activation in astrocytes, while simultaneously increasing paracrine T3 to bolster neuronal structural and transcriptional resilience.
Vivo and in vitro experiments were designed respectively to verify the therapeutic effects of T3 and the crosstalk between astrocytes and neurons (Figs. A-B). Systemic administration of T3 after HIBD partially reversed the injury-associated downregulated expression of Arx, Dlx1, and Sox2 (Fig. C; Figs. S10A-C). The preservation of these markers carries dual biological significance: support of the interneuron lineage (Arx/Dlx1) may curb post-ischemic circuit disinhibition, whereas sustaining Sox2 helps maintain the progenitor reservoir, providing a basis for subsequent repair and synaptic remodeling.
 To extend our in vivo findings, we employed a Transwell (contact-independent) co-culture system for our studies. The results demonstrated that OGD-stressed neurons were protected under two scenarios: either when supplemented with exogenous T3, or when in indirect contact with astrocytes overexpressing Dio2. Specifically, compared with the OGD group, these neurons exhibited significantly higher expression levels of Sox2, Tbr1, and Sfrp1 (Fig. D; Figs. S11D-F). The rise in Tbr1 is noteworthy because Tbr1 helps maintain cortical laminar identity and guide projection patterns [ ]. Likewise, increased Sfrp1 points to engagement of Wnt pathway components, consistent with a shift toward a pro-neurogenic milieu [ , ]. Direct hormone quantification further showed that both total and free T3 were elevated in astrocyte cultures overexpressing Dio2 (Figs. S10G-H), indicating that the protective effect largely reflects enhanced local delivery of bioactive T3 from astrocytes rather than a purely systemic hormone action.
At the mechanistic level, T3 treatment restored neuronal autophagy marker dynamics and facilitated the decline of phosphorylated eIF2α (Fig. E; Figs. S10I-K). Because prolonged ISR activation can hinder protein synthesis recovery and synaptic plasticity, its attenuation—together with autophagy re-engagement—suggests a coordinated proteostasis reset. Notably, these neuronal changes mirror the Dio2–autophagy–ISR axis identified in astrocytes, implying a conserved cross-cell stress-resolution route that integrates metabolic signaling with degradation and translational control pathways.
These molecular and cellular benefits resulted in preservation and functional improvement, including better-preserved cytoarchitecture on haematoxylin and eosin (H & E) staining, increased Nissl-positive neuronal counts (Figs. F–G; Fig. S11A) and enhanced learning and memory in the Morris water maze (Fig. H; Figs. S11B-C). These outcomes are consistent with sustained interneuron function and cortical circuit maturation.
Collectively, the data delineate a cross-compartment model (Fig. ) in which astrocytic Dio2 elevation enhances localized T3 availability to simultaneously restore autophagy initiation and alleviate ISR burden in both glia and neurons, while stabilizing transcriptional programs linked to cell lineage and maturation. This integrated endocrine–proteostasis tuning attenuates acute injury cascades and supports long-term neurofunctional outcomes. While causal dissection will benefit from future studies involving Dio2 inhibition or thyroid receptor antagonism, the present findings position astrocyte Dio2 as a strategic node that links metabolic hormone activation to stress adaptation and circuit preservation in HIBD.
 

## Discussion
Neonatal HIE is a leading cause of neonatal mortality and long-term neurodevelopmental disability. According to epidemiological data, the incidence of HIE in live births is approximately 3‰ to 6‰, and its incidence may increase significantly in regions with limited medical resources [ ].Hypothermia therapy is the first-line standard treatment for neonates with HIE, which can significantly reduce the mortality rate of affected infants. However, this therapy still has limitations: some surviving infants may develop long-term neurodevelopmental sequelae (such as cognitive impairment, motor dysfunction, etc.) in the later stage [ ]. In recent years, a critical shift has occurred in the therapeutic concepts and strategies for HIE, moving from traditional passive life support (e.g., maintaining circulatory and respiratory stability) to active neuroprotective and reparative interventions. The innovation of this concept and strategy aims to intervene in the disease process from the perspective of pathophysiological mechanisms, providing an important direction for reducing the incidence of neurodevelopmental sequelae and improving the long-term prognosis of neonates with HIE [ ].
In-depth exploration of the activation mechanism of astrocytes constitutes a crucial link in unraveling the pathogenic mechanisms of HIE. During the acute phase of the disease, the abnormally excessive activation of these cells leads to the massive secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, which in turn exacerbates brain tissue damage and is closely associated with poor prognosis [ , ]. Based on this, targeted intervention in their biological behaviors demonstrates substantial clinical translational value [ ].Traditionally, astrocytes have been dichotomized into “protective” versus “pro-inflammatory” states, a binary that has yielded contradictory interpretations—for example, both enhancing and suppressing GFAP expression have been reported to improve outcomes [ , ]. Our single-cell RNA-seq analysis of HIBD reframes this view by revealing both subpopulation-specific and shared astrocytic programs that maintain persistent crosstalk with neurons. We identify a Dio2/Slc1a2-high “metabolic–neuroprotective” astrocyte subpopulation that is depleted after HIBD. Although this cluster was defined by differential gene expression, Dio2 is also expressed across other astrocyte clusters, indicating that the capacity for T3 synthesis is not confined to a single subset. Functionally, Dio2 overexpression increases astrocytic T3 synthesis, supporting a central role for the Dio2–T3 Axis in astrocyte–neuron communication and neuroprotection. Trajectory analyses with multi–time-point validation further show that astrocytes transition along a temporal continuum—from quiescent to protective to pro-inflammatory states—challenging static categorizations and aligning with observations in aging models.
Mechanistically, multi-omics and functional assays converge on impaired autophagy initiation as a proximal trigger of the integrated stress response (ISR) that biases astrocytes toward pro-inflammation. Enhancing Dio2 restores autophagy initiation, attenuates ISR signaling, and reorients astrocyte fate away from pro-inflammatory trajectories. These findings position the Dio2–T3 Axis as a leverage point for reprogramming astrocyte states following HIBD.
Taken together, our results support a shift from a binary view of astrocyte reactivity toward a time- and metabolism-informed framework, in which thyroid hormone metabolism represents a tractable axis of state regulation. Placing the Dio2–T3 pathway upstream of autophagy initiation and ISR modulation, the proposed Astrocyte–Neuron T3 Shuttle offers a parsimonious account linking metabolic signaling, stress responses, and astrocyte–neuron communication after HIBD. This perspective suggests that astrocytes can actively influence neuronal recovery and that interventions designed with intercellular communication in mind may be more effective than targeting isolated endpoints.
Future work integrating Astrocyte-Neuron Triiodothyronine Shuttle (ANTS) with spatially resolved and human-relevant models will be important to refine mechanisms, assess generalizability, and evaluate translational potential in neonatal HIE. Our data come from rat pups, and ethical and practical constraints precluded direct human sampling; confirmation in human-relevant systems is therefore needed before moving toward preclinical studies. In addition, pronounced regional heterogeneity and the lack of spatial resolution in conventional single-cell RNA-seq limit in situ evaluation of ANTS; combining spatial transcriptomics with multiplex imaging should help localize Dio2–T3 activity and astrocyte–neuron coupling. Finally, although our in vitro data show that astrocytes release T3 and mitigate neuronal injury, the in vivo routes of delivery—via specific transporters, carrier proteins, or extracellular vesicles—remain to be defined; targeted perturbations and tracer-based approaches will be important to resolve these mechanisms.

## Conclusions
In conclusion, our study underscores the critical role of Astrocyte-Neuron Crosstalk in HIE. Metabo-neuroprotective astrocytes demonstrate pronounced temporal characteristics. The upregulation of Dio2 in astrocytes enhances the levels of T3, which subsequently mitigates the ISR by promoting autophagy, thereby reducing damage to both astrocytes and neurons. Consequently, the supplementary administration of T3 emerges as a promising therapeutic strategy for HIE.
