Using young and old wild-type mice, alongside old heterozygous Klotho-deficient mice, we administered soluble α-Klotho (sKL) daily for 10 weeks to elucidate the impact of α-Klotho deficiency and its supplementation. ## Introduction
Stem cells have the ability to self-renew and can differentiate into various cell types. They play essential roles in tissue homeostasis, repair, and regeneration throughout an organism’s lifespan. As organisms age, stem cells decline in number and function, leading to a decrease in tissue regeneration and repair capacity. This decline is thought to contribute to the onset and progression of many age-related diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, chronic kidney diseases, muscle atrophy, and neurodegenerative disorders. Mechanistically, genomic instability, telomere shortening, epigenetic alterations, and mitochondrial dysfunction have been reported to contribute to stem cell aging , .
The α-Klotho is known as an anti-aging hormone . The serum levels of klotho decline with aging and in the elderly population . Experimental studies show that mice with homozygous hypomorph Klotho display several features of “accelerated aging” , and the mice with overexpression of klotho had extended lifespan . Previous report suggests a critical role of α-klotho in the regulation of stem cells through suppressing Wnt signaling, inhibiting cell senescence, and preserving stem cells in the skin . The α-Klotho is indispensable for proper skeletal muscle regeneration following injury . There is an age-dependent decline in muscle regeneration capacity, which has been attributed to an inability to activate the Klotho promoter. The compromised muscle repair observed in aged mice can be ameliorated by α-Klotho supplementation at crucial physiological time points . In addition, α-Klotho also contributes to wound healing, as its deficiency impairs stem cell regulation in both the skin and small intestine .
In this study, we provide robust evidence of klotho regulation of physiological cellular proliferation to maintain healthy turnover and homeostasis in multiple organs. To comprehensively test our hypothesis, we examined tissues with distinct baseline turnover rates, allowing us to elucidate Klotho’s effects across a spectrum of regenerative capacities from rapidly renewing to slowly replicating cell populations. Our findings shed light on a mechanism downstream of α-klotho, through Sirt1 regulation of cell cycle regulators and the DNA-damage response pathway (CHK2, checkpoint kinase 2) that are conserved in various cells with different turnover rates and regenerative capacities. Sirt1, a key protein in longevity-related signaling, declines in multiple organs with aging. Sirt1 functions to regulate genomic stability, reduce oxidative stress, modulate cardiac function , promote cell cycle progression, and tissue regeneration in multiple tissues , making it a potential target mediator of Klotho’s effects on stem cell proliferation. Downstream of Klotho and Sirt1, CHK2 is a key regulator of the DNA-damage response, which suppresses cell cycle progression in the presence of genomic instability. Age-dependent activation of CHK2 is linked to reduced stem cell proliferation and impaired regeneration. Klotho supplementation may counteract these effects by modulating CHK2 activity.
To investigate these mechanisms, we focused on basal crypts of small intestines with a high turnover rate, kidney glomerular and tubular epithelial cells with a moderate turnover rate, and adult cardiomyocytes with a very low turnover. Klotho deficiency aggravates decreased cellular proliferation in aging, and klotho supplementation restores the proliferative markers; the manipulation of klotho to affect cell cycle markers is also associated with the manifestations of other aging phenotypes.

## Results

## Klotho deficiency in aging is potentiated by heterozygous hypomorph klotho and partially restored by soluble klotho supplementation
Circulating klotho declines with advancing age in the elderly population . To examine the role of circulating klotho on stem cells aging, we examined the effect of aggravated Klotho deficiency in old heterozygous Klotho hypomorph (OKL+/−) mice and the effect of supplementation with the exogenous soluble α-Klotho (sKL), which contains the KL1 subunit, the N-terminal sequence of full-length transmembrane Klotho. Figure illustrates the mouse study design. The 17–23-month-old wild-type (OWT) and old heterozygous Klotho-deficient (OKL+/−) mice received a daily intraperitoneal sKL injection (0.01 mg/kg/day) for 10 weeks. BrdU (100 mg/kg) was injected before the endpoint to label whole-body proliferating cells. Figure  showed a huge decrease of more than 12-fold in serum klotho levels from young wild-type (5–6-month-old) to old (17–21 months) mice. Supplementation by sKL increased circulating klotho levels, although improving klotho levels is more obvious in the OKL+/− and when they were initiated earlier (before 20 months of age, Fig. ). 
In this study, we investigated the effect of klotho deficiency and supplementation on cellular proliferation and the aging phenotypes of three organs with different regenerative capacity: the small intestines with a fast turnover rate and high regenerative capacity, the kidney with moderate turnover, and the heart with very low turnover and regenerative capacity.

## Klotho ameliorates proliferative markers in the basal crypts of the small intestine
Paneth cells, found in the crypts adjacent to the intestinal stem cells. are secretory cells producing antimicrobial substances modulating microbiome, host immunity, and intestinal stem cell niche. The number of Paneth cells increased significantly in OKL+/− mice (Fig. ). The increase in Paneth cells in OKL+/− mice was attenuated by sKL (Fig. ). Mucin-producing Goblet cells also increased in the aged small intestine (Fig. ), suggesting a reactive response to chronic stressors, such as inflammation and infections. Supplementation of sKL attenuated the age-related increase of Goblet cells in OKL+/− mice. Intestinal stem cells, which reside in the basal crypts, rapidly proliferate to regenerate the epithelium. We therefore measured crypt cell proliferation with BrdU labeling. Proliferating intestinal crypt cells were significantly reduced in aged mice (significant for both OWT and OKL+/−), and sKL supplementation restored the BrdU-positive cells in the basal crypts of OWT (p = 0.16) and OKL+/− mice (p < 0.05, Fig. ). Taken together, sKL promoted regenerative cells in the intestinal crypts and attenuated age-dependent reactive increases in both Paneth and Goblet cells. 

## Klotho deficiency is associated with aggravated kidney aging and decreases in cellular proliferative markers
As an endocrine organ producing the klotho “anti-aging hormone”, the kidney also depends on klotho for cellular turnover or homeostasis to maintain its physiological function. Figure  shows age-dependent deterioration of glomerulosclerosis. At young age, more than 90% of glomeruli are normal (score 0), and the remaining ~10% show minimal sclerosis (score 1). At old age, these fractions decreased to ~50% of normal glomeruli (score 0) and ~35% of minimally sclerosed glomeruli (score 1), whereas the fractions of moderate to severe glomerulosclerosis increased to 15%. OKL+/− potentiated the fraction of glomerulosclerosis (score 2–3) to nearly 50%. Supplementation of sKL decreased glomerulosclerosis to ~10% in OWT (p = 0.2) and to 12% in OKL+/− (p < 0.001, Fig. ). In parallel with the progression of glomerulosclerosis, the age-dependent increase in interstitial fibrosis was also potentiated in OKL+/− (increased by nearly 2-fold). SKL significantly ameliorated interstitial fibrosis for both OWT and OKL+/− (Fig. ) 
Under physiological conditions, the kidney has a relatively low, albeit significant, cell turnover rate. Figure shows that in WT mice, the BrdU-labeled tubular epithelial cells dropped by ~50% from young to old, and this is further decreased in OKL+/− mice. Likewise, glomerular epithelial cells, including parietal cells and visceral epithelial cells (podocytes), have substantially decreased BrdU labeling, indicating a prominent decrease in proliferation (turnover) in both old WT and KL-deficient mice. Supplementation of sKL increased the BrdU labeling of both tubules and glomerular cells. Taken together, sKL promotes cellular turnover and ameliorates age-dependent glomerulosclerosis and interstitial fibrosis in the kidneys. 

## Klotho regulates basal cardiomyocyte proliferative markers
The mammalian heart is considered post-mitotic, as most cardiomyocytes (CM) stop dividing after the first week of birth in mice, although increasing evidence has shown a low turnover rate of adult CM . To investigate the role of Klotho in CM proliferation, we measured cell cycle markers in the heart of YWT, OWT, and OKL+/− mice treated with saline or sKL. OWT hearts displayed a significant decrease in BrdU-, Ki67-, and pH3-positive CMs, and the decrease in these markers was further exacerbated in OKL+/− mice (Fig. ). Treatment with sKL for 10 weeks significantly increased BrdU- and Ki67-positive cardiomyocytes in both OWT and OKL+/− mice, and significantly increased pH3-positive CMs in OKL+/− (Fig. ). We observed ~5-fold increase in the number of BrdU+ CM in OKL+/− mice treated with sKL and this increase was ~2.5-fold in OWT group treated with sKL (Fig. ). We observed the same trend in the number of Ki67+ CMs with a 4-fold increase in OKL+/− mice treated with sKL and a 3-fold increase in OWT mice treated with sKL (Fig. ). The quantitation of pH3+ CMs showed a 3.5-fold increase in OKL+/− mice treated with sKL and ~1.5-fold increase in OWT group treated with sKL (Fig. ). 

## Klotho deficiency is associated with aggravated cardiac aging
Cardiac aging is characterized by left ventricular hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction, and interstitial fibrosis . By echocardiography, we demonstrate that LV mass substantially increased by ~2-fold in OWT (p < 0.001 vs. YWT) and this further increased in OKL+/− (p = 0.055 OKL+/− vs OWT). Treatment with sKL significantly ameliorated LVM in OWT and OKL+/− (p < 0.001 for both, Fig.  ). Consistent with the increase in LV mass, the LV end diastolic volume significantly increased in both OWT and OKL+/−. The sKL significantly attenuated the LV dilation in OWT (Fig. ). The LV end-systolic volume was significantly dilated only in the OKL+/− group (Fig. ). The sKL had no obvious effect on LVESV (Fig. ). The LV systolic function, measured by ejection fraction (%), was not significantly altered across all groups (Fig. ). The diastolic function, measured by Tissue Doppler imaging of mitral annulus (E’/A’), was significantly decreased in OWT (p < 0.001 vs YWT). Treatment with sKL improved E’/A’ in OWT (p = 0.14) and OKL+/− (p = 0.0019, Fig. ). 
Collectively, our data show that supplementing with sKL resulted in a significant reduction in left ventricular hypertrophy, improvement in diastolic dysfunction, and enhancement of cell cycle proliferative markers in old WT and klotho-deficient mice.

## Klotho supplementation ameliorated mediators of the DNA-damage response pathway
Accumulation of DNA damage with age activates DNA repair pathways, which at the same time suppress cell cycles to avoid propagation of damaged DNA. The DNA-damage response pathway plays a crucial role in regulating stem cell proliferation and maintaining genomic stability.
Gamma-H2Ax, a biomarker for DNA double-strand break (damage), was increased in OWT kidney (p < 0.001) and heart (p = 0.08), and this was potentiated in OKL+/− (p < 0.001 for both kidney and heart). The sKL treatment for 10 weeks significantly attenuated these increases across all groups (Fig. ). Two molecules involved in the DNA-damage response pathway, Ataxia-Telangiectasia Mutated (ATM) and Checkpoint kinase-2 (CHK2), were also elevated in aged kidneys and hearts. In kidneys, phospho-ATM (Ser1981) and total ATM were significantly increased in OWT and even more in the OKL+/−, and these were attenuated by sKL (p < 0.01 for p-ATM and total ATM in OKL+/−, and p < 0.05 for total ATM in OWT, Fig. ). In the heart, the increases in p-ATM and total ATM were significant for OKL+/−, both of which were significantly attenuated by sKL, while for OWT, there was a significant increase in total ATM (Fig. ). The downstream master regulator CHK2, a serine-threonine checkpoint effector kinase, is specifically activated and phosphorylated by ATM. The levels of phosphorylated and total CHK2 were elevated in the OWT and OKL+/− groups (all significant except for p-CHK2 in the OWT kidney), while sKL treatment suppressed phosphorylated and total CHK2 levels in both the kidney and heart of the OWT and OKL+/− groups (all significant except for p-CHK2 in the kidney, Fig. C, D). In intestinal tissue, the decrease in Sirt1 and increases in γH2Ax, phospho- and total ATM, phospho- and total CHK2 were significant in OKL+/−, and these were ameliorated by sKL (Supplementary Fig. ). The changes in OWT were in the same direction but not significant, suggesting greater variability in normative intestinal aging. 

## Klotho supplementation increases proliferation markers in cardiomyocytes, kidney tubular cells, and human progeroid fibroblasts, mediated through Sirt1 signaling
We next investigated the detailed underlying mechanism through which Klotho regulates cardiomyocyte proliferation in vitro. We treated human induced pluripotent stem cells-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPS-CM, ATCC 1026, normal donor) (Fig. ) with sKL in the presence or absence of Sirt1 inhibitor, Ex527 (10 μM). Measurement of cell proliferation markers revealed that sKL significantly increases hiPS-CM proliferation, marked by a significant increase in Ki67- and pH3-positive cardiomyocytes (Fig. ). Klotho-induced CM proliferation was abolished by Ex527, indicating that sKL promotes cardiomyocytes proliferative markers through Sirt1 (Fig. ). 
CHK2 is a key regulator of the DNA-damage response pathway, acting as a checkpoint kinase that inhibits cell cycle progression in response to genomic instability. We tested whether CHK2 expression was modulated by sKL. The DNA-damage marker, γH2Ax, was significantly increased in Sirt1-siRNA-treated hiPS-CMs, and sKL treatment did not significantly alter its expression level in the absence of Sirt1 (Fig. ). Similarly, sKL suppressed the mediator of the DNA-damage pathway, p-CHK2 expression in hiPS-CM. However, upon inhibition of Sirt1, the effect of sKL was abolished (Fig. ). We further examined whether suppression of CHK2 is sufficient to induce cell cycle markers in vitro. We observed a significant increase in Ki67- and pH3-positive cardiomyocytes upon CHK2 suppression in hiPS-CMs (Fig. ). To examine whether the sKL effect is also dependent on Sirt1 in kidneys, we repeated the experiments in HK2 human kidney tubular epithelial cells. As shown in Fig. , sKL significantly suppressed p-CHK2 in HK2 cells, and this effect was abolished by either Sirt1 siRNA or EX527. Both methods of Sirt1 inhibition successfully suppressed Sirt1 and significantly increased γH2Ax DNA-damage marker, and sKL had no effect on any of these signals (Fig. ), suggesting that Sirt1 is one of the mediators downstream of sKL.
To reinforce our findings, we tested fibroblasts from a patient with accelerated aging Werner syndrome (WS, provided by the University of Washington). WS is a progeria syndrome with limited proliferation capacity and defective DNA-damage response. We treated control and WS fibroblasts with sKL in the presence or absence of Sirt1 inhibitor (Ex527) and quantified Ki67+ fibroblasts. As expected, saline-treated WS fibroblasts had a significantly lower proliferation rate (Supplementary Fig. ). Interestingly, adding sKL significantly increased Ki67+ fibroblasts in both control and WS fibroblasts. Sirt1 inhibitor Ex527 resulted in a significant reduction of proliferation rate from 80% to 60% in control fibroblasts and from 62% to 42% in WS fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. ), suggesting that Sirt1 is required for the sKL-induced increase in cell cycle markers.
To confirm the role of Sirt1 in mediating sKL effect on the heart, we performed an in vivo experiment on mice with a cardiomyocyte-specific deletion of Sirt1 (cSirt1KO). We administered sKL daily for 10 weeks to cSirt1KO mice (Sirt1fl/fl-MHC-Cre+, ~7-month-old) and their wild-type littermates (Sirt1fl/fl without Cre). We examined the presence of cell cycle markers of cardiomyocytes following administration of either sKL or saline. Our results showed that sKL administration did not affect the number of Ki67- or pH3-positive cardiomyocytes in the cSirt1KO mice (Supplementary Fig. ). In parallel, treatment of sKL for 10 weeks failed to rescue the decreased LVEF (%) in these cSirt1KO mice (Supplementary Fig. ), consistent with the in vitro findings that Sirt1 is required for the beneficial effect of sKL.

## Discussion
Age-dependent decline in mammalian organ function is associated with a decline in their regenerative potential due to aging of stem cells and their microenvironment . Stem cell proliferation and differentiation, essential for tissue homeostasis, replenish cells lost to natural attrition or injury. However, as organisms age, these cells decline in number and functionality, significantly reducing tissue repair and regeneration capabilities. The mechanistic diagram of the current study is summarized in Fig. . 
In this study, we reported the critical role of α-klotho in maintaining resident stem cell proliferation across three tissues with varying regenerative capacities. Circulating klotho naturally decreases with age, and this decline is more pronounced in old heterozygous klotho (OKL+/−) mice, leading to exacerbated aging phenotypes and a reduction in proliferative markers across multiple organs. Our findings demonstrate that supplementation with soluble klotho (sKL) effectively reversed these changes, enhancing cell proliferation markers and attenuating aging changes in high-turnover tissues like the small intestine, medium-turnover tissues such as the kidney, and cardiomyocytes with a very low turnover rate. Our findings also suggest that modulation of CHK2, the master regulator of the cell cycle, is a critical mechanism underlying the increased proliferative rates noted in these tissues in response to sKL treatment. In response to DNA damage, ATM recruits Sirt1 to the site of DNA damage. Sirt1 has been reported to act as a regulator of CHK2 activity in managing the cell cycle progression . Our findings show that Sirt1 levels were significantly reduced in multiple organs of aged mice. The sKL for 10 weeks restored Sirt1 levels in both OWT and OKL+/− organs (Fig. A, D, Supplementary Fig. ). Mechanistically, Sirt1 was required for the sKL suppressive effect on CHK2 (master inhibitor of cell cycle) and the proliferative effect of sKL on cardiomyocytes and progeroid fibroblasts. This dependency on Sirt1 was also observed in the kidney tubular epithelial cells (Fig. ). Restoration of Sirt1 in response to sKL treatment mitigated DNA-damage pathways and inhibited CHK2. Thus, sKL enhanced cell cycle markers via Sirt1 regulation of CHK2 and the DNA-damage response pathway. Taken together, our data provides robust evidence linking α-klotho, Sirt1, DNA-damage response, and CHK2 in the regulation of cellular turnover in aging, across multiple cell types with different regenerative capacity.
Kidney tubular epithelial cells are the primary site of Klotho synthesis, producing both the full-length transmembrane Klotho and its secreted or circulating form . The full-length transmembrane αKlotho (~130 kDa) in kidney tubular epithelial cells functions as a co-receptor for fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR), binding to fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) , which plays a crucial role in phosphate metabolism. The secreted form or soluble Klotho (sKL), generated by β-secretases cleavage, consists of the KL1 domain , acts as a hormone affecting distant tissues that do not express Klotho . The role of klotho as an “anti-aging hormone” is evident as the mice with homozygous Klotho deficiency exhibit “accelerated aging” and early mortality , while mice overexpressing Klotho extended lifespan, partly through the IGF1 pathway .
The circulating Klotho protein dropped about 30–40% in 12-month-old mice . To elucidate the role of Klotho, we manipulated serum Klotho levels by using old heterozygous Klotho mice (which have lower serum Klotho than old WT littermates) and sKL supplementation, which partially restored serum Klotho levels. This strategy allowed us to elucidate the role of Klotho in the context of aging and augmented Klotho deficiency-induced phenotypes. We used heterozygous Klotho mice, since homozygous hypomorph Klotho mice have a short life expectancy of ~4 months, with several complications related to renal failure and abnormal phosphate metabolism , in addition to “accelerated aging” phenotypes. Here, we demonstrated robust evidence of the roles of α-klotho regulation of cell proliferation in natural aging without injurious stimuli, across tissues with different regenerative capacities. These conserved mechanisms are observed in small intestinal stem cells in the basal crypts with a high turnover rate, kidney glomerular and tubular epithelial cells with a low turnover rate, and adult mammalian cardiomyocytes with a very low turnover rate. Similar mechanisms are confirmed in human iPSC-derived cardiomyocytes, HK2 human kidney tubular epithelial cells, and progeria fibroblasts from a Werner syndrome patient.
As the intestine ages, it undergoes changes in its structure, such as developing larger crypts and villi. Aging alters the relative abundance of cell types: an increase in Paneth cells and goblet cells, and a decrease in stem cells. Changes in cell composition are consistent with the impaired intestinal crypt regenerative function , . The implicated mechanisms likely involve age-related decline in Wnt and Sirt1 . Previous research aligns with our findings in aged Klotho-deficient mice, where loss of Sirt1 signaling was linked to increased numbers of Paneth and Goblet cells and disrupted intestinal homeostasis. Paneth cells produce Notum, an extracellular Wnt inhibitor that impairs stem cell function and regeneration of intestinal epithelium . Loss of Sirt signaling increases susceptibility to intestinal inflammation and alters the microbiome, potentially through Paneth cell activation and release of antimicrobial products . Similarly, Wnt and Sirt1 signaling, which are critical for intestinal stem cell proliferation, also exhibit age-related decline , . One mechanism by which soluble Kotho supplementation reverses age-related changes in intestinal cell type abundances and proliferation within crypts could be related to the restoration of Sirt1 and Wnt signaling . Furthermore, previous studies show that calorie restriction promotes stem cell function in an mTORC1- and Sirt1-dependent fashion, suggesting that Klotho and calorie restriction may counteract aging changes in the small intestine through a shared mechanism , .
We and others previously reported that kidney aging is characterized by increased glomerulosclerosis, tubular atrophy, and interstitial fibrosis , . Our current data show that OKL+/− mice had a significantly higher fraction of moderate to severe glomerulosclerosis and a higher percentage of interstitial fibrosis. The sKL substantially ameliorated both glomerulosclerosis and interstitial fibrosis (Fig. ). Consistent with this, the prominent decreases of cell proliferative markers in the tubular epithelial cells and glomerular cells in OWT and OKL+/− were significantly restored by sKL treatment (Fig. ). This relatively low physiological cell turnover rate in the kidney is critical to maintain cellular homeostasis. Indeed, renal cells have a pronounced ability to proliferate after injury, which undermines that both tubular and glomerular epithelial cells are able to regenerate in response to injury . Our data emphasizes the importance of restoring klotho to maintain healthy regenerative capacity, particularly in the context of kidney aging.
Recent studies have challenged the long-held belief that adult cardiomyocytes are post-mitotic, suggesting that they may have a limited capacity for proliferation . This potential for proliferation is regulated by a complex interplay of signaling pathways and transcription factors, including Notch, Hippo, Wnt, Akt, and Jak/Stat . Aging results in both structural and functional alterations in the heart, as well as the depletion of cardiomyocytes due to elevated cardiomyocyte apoptosis and reduced cardiomyocyte production. It is estimated that the CM turnover rate is 1–2% per year in a young healthy human heart, but this decreases to <0.5% in the elderly . The decline in CM proliferation may contribute to decreased cardiac functional reserve in aging, which may predispose to heart failure, increasing the susceptibility to acute injury, and rendering the profound need to replenish these lost cardiomyocytes. Klotho deficiency further decreased the already limited capacity of cardiomyocyte regeneration in aged hearts, and treatment with soluble α-Klotho increased cardiomyocyte cell cycle markers in aged hearts (Fig. ). The mechanisms involve Klotho-Sirt1 regulation of CHK2 and the downstream cell cycle-dependent kinases and DNA-damage response pathways . As heart failure exponentially increases in old age, our demonstration of enhanced cardiomyocyte proliferation in aging in response to sKL has critical values and great clinical relevance, and may provide insight into the importance of CM proliferation in the aged heart. We speculate that the decline of CM proliferation may reduce cardiac reserve and predispose to heart failure in aging. Interestingly, suppression of the DNA-damage response pathway using Klotho supplementation allowed efficient cell cycle reentry of cardiomyocytes both in vitro and in vivo, and this is mediated through Sirt1-CHK2 signaling.
In skeletal muscle stem cells (satellite cells), age-related declines in α-Klotho drive mitochondrial dysfunction in progenitor cells, impairing muscle regeneration. However, supplementation with klotho can restore mitochondrial function and enhance muscle regeneration . Moreover, Klotho antagonizes age-enhanced Wnt signaling . Conversely, decreased klotho and increased Wnt in aging would drive skeletal muscle progenitor cells toward a pro-fibrotic state .
Our study has a limitation of an inadequate sample size (underpowered) to achieve statistical significance for some parameters, even after using a wider age range of mice. The latter is needed to synchronize the injection of soluble klotho (all from the same batch) to control for variability of intervention and to reduce the amount of labor during 10 weeks of injection. Nevertheless, we successfully demonstrated the effect of klotho deficiency in aging (and augmented deficiency in OKL+/−) and the effect of sKL supplementation, particularly when the data from both OWT and OKL+/− and the corresponding treated groups were put into this context. Another limitation is that the sKL dose determination of 0.01 mg/kg/d is based on limited published papers , , which might not be adequate to increase serum Klotho levels in some mice (based on a single serum test, Fig. ). However, we did observe the beneficial effects across multiple organs. Future study of soluble klotho pharmacokinetics and higher doses of sKL may be tested to achieve stronger beneficial effects.
In conclusion, this study provides robust evidence of the conserved critical regulatory roles of circulating klotho in multiple tissues with different inherent regenerative potentials, at least partly through Sirt1 and the downstream CHK2 pathway. Interestingly, supplementation of klotho enhanced the proliferative capacity of iPSC-CM (which have declining proliferative capacity during the maturation process) as well as progeroid fibroblasts, highlighting klotho’s role in moderating DNA damage, thus enhancing cell cycle progression in various tissues during aging. Our study also supports the potential translational application of soluble klotho to enhance regenerative potential in multiple tissues.

## Methods

## Animal studies
All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the University of Iowa and/or Johns Hopkins University. All animal procedures conform to the guidelines from Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes or the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The mice were housed in a 12:12 h light: dark cycle in a temperature-controlled room in the animal housing room with free access to water and food. Carbon dioxide inhalation was used for euthanasia of mice. Since our preliminary studies did not show significant differences in cardiac function between male and female mice (Supplementary Fig. ), both sexes aged 2–4 months and 17–23 months with a C57BL/6J genetic background were used in this study. OKL+/− and OWT mice were injected with recombinant Klotho (Peprotech, catalog #100-53) (0.01 mg/kg/day, IP) daily for 10 weeks.

## Echocardiography
Transthoracic echocardiography was carried out on conscious mice that were gently restrained, utilizing the Vevo 2100 VisualSonics System (Toronto, ON, Canada). Mice were administered 0.1 mg of midazolam, a benzodiazepine known for its anxiolytic and muscle relaxant properties, prior to the echocardiography, with no observed impact on cardiac function. Imaging was performed with a 30-MHz linear array transducer, capturing parasternal short and long axis views at a frame rate between 180 and 250 Hz. Images were analyzed offline using Vevo 2100 analysis software (v.1.5). Using 2D echocardiography, we outlined the endocardial and epicardial borders in the short-axis view during both diastole and systole. LV length was measured from these borders to the LV outflow tract in both diastole and systole. The biplane area–length method was used to calculate LV mass and ejection fraction. The LV systolic function was measured by the (LVEDV-LVESV)/LVEDV × 100, or LV ejection fraction (%). Diastolic function was evaluated using tissue Doppler imaging of the mitral annulus along with conventional mitral inflow.

## Blood collection
Blood samples were collected from mice at two time points via the submandibular vein using a minimally invasive technique to ensure efficient sample retrieval while minimizing distress. Mice were briefly restrained using manual restraint. A lancet (5 mm depth) was used to puncture the submandibular vein in the area located just caudal to the mandible. Blood was allowed to flow freely into collection tubes without applying excessive pressure to prevent hemolysis. Hemostasis was achieved using sterile gauze or gentle finger pressure applied to the puncture site. Mice were monitored post-procedure for any signs of distress, bleeding, or complications, and were returned to their cages after full recovery.

## Klotho ELISA assay
We utilized the Mouse Klotho ELISA Kit (Catalog No: MBS2516159, MyBiosource) to measure serum Klotho levels. In summary, we introduced 100 μL of each diluted standard, blank, and sample dilution (20 µl serum + 80 µl Reference Standard &Sample Diluent) into a pre-coated micro-ELISA plate. We allowed it to incubate for 90 min at 37 °C. After removing the liquid from each well, we promptly introduced 100 μL of Biotinylated Detection Ab working solution into each well and allowed it to incubate for 1 h at 37 °C. After removing the solution from each well, we introduced 350 μL of wash buffer into each well, allowed it to soak for 1–2 min, and then removed the solution from each well and dried it by patting it on clean absorbent paper. The washing process was performed three times. Subsequently, we introduced 100 μL of HRP conjugate working solution into each well and subjected it to a 30-min incubation period at 37 °C. After removing the solution from each well, the washing process was repeated five times. Subsequently, we introduced 90 microliters of Substrate Reagent into each well and subjected it to incubation for approximately 15 min at a temperature of 37 °C, ensuring that the plate was shielded from any exposure to light. Subsequently, we introduced 50 μL of Stop Solution into each well in the identical sequence as the substrate solution. Ultimately, we measured the optical density (OD value) of each well simultaneously using a microplate reader that had been warmed and set to 450 nm.

## BrdU labeling and quantitative analysis
BrdU was diluted in PBS to make a sterile solution of 10 mg/mL. For mice, we injected the BrdU solution at a concentration of 100 mg/kg, 24 h, 6 h and 30 min before euthanasia. To assess the quantity of BrdU-positive cells in renal tubules, between 5 and 12 regions of interest (ROIs) were designated, spanning both the cortex and juxtamedullary areas. Each ROI measured about 2.4 mm2, encompassing approximately 775 renal tubule cross-sections. The density of BrdU-positive cells was determined by dividing the total count of positive cells within the ROIs by the total area of these ROIs. Similarly, to gauge BrdU expression in glomeruli, we quantified the BrdU-positive cells in a minimum of 100 glomeruli. This count was then standardized by the number of glomeruli assessed to calculate the average expression per glomerulus. To determine the BrdU density in the small intestine, BrdU density was calculated for each individual crypt by dividing the number of BrdU-positive cells by the total cell count in that crypt. Subsequently, these values were normalized across the sampled crypts to obtain the overall BrdU density. BrdU density in the heart was determined by the percentage of BrdU-positive myocytes relative to the total number of myocytes analyzed.

## Immunostaining
Mouse hearts, kidneys, and intestines were collected, fixed in OCT, frozen, and stored at −80 °C. The frozen OCT sections were then sectioned at a thickness of 10 μm using a cryostat, and the slides were prepared for immunostaining. Frozen sections were allowed to reach room temperature for 30 min, rehydrated in PBS for 5 min, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 14 min, washed twice with PBS for 5 min each, and permeabilized in PBT (0.25% Triton X-100/PBS, 10 ml of 20% Triton X-100 in 800 mL PBS) for 5 min. The slides were blocked with PBT containing 10% normal Donkey serum for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies diluted in PBT were applied (a complete list of primary antibodies is provided in Table ), and the slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following three 10-min washes with PBT, the slides were incubated with a secondary antibody (Invitrogen, 1:200) diluted in PBT for 2 h at room temperature. The samples were then washed three times with PBT for 10 min each and counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (1 μg/ml) diluted in PBS for 10 min. After a brief PBS wash, slides were mounted with VECTASHIELD mounting medium for imaging. Stained slides were stored at 4 °C, and images were captured using a Zeiss LSM710 confocal microscope. The intensity of fluorescence staining was quantified using ImageJ software. All staining procedures were performed on 3–12 samples per group, with 1–2 sections per tissue. 

## Western blotting
After tissue homogenization in RIPA buffer (Millipore Sigma, R0278) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors, protein concentration was quantified using the BCA protein assay (Pierce, Thermo Fisher). Equal amounts of protein (40 μg per sample) were denatured at 95 °C for 5 min in Laemmli buffer and then subjected to SDS-PAGE electrophoresis on mPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris polyacrylamide gels (Millipore Sigma, MP41G15). Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Immobilon-P, Millipore) using a wet transfer system at 40 V for 3.5 h at 4 °C. Membranes were then blocked with 4% BSA (2 g of bovine serum albumin in 50 mL of TBS-T: Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h at room temperature.
Membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in 4% BSA/TBS-T, with the specific antibodies and dilutions listed in Table . The following day, membranes were washed three times for 5 min with TBS-T before incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit or anti-mouse, 1:5000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. After additional washes, protein bands were visualized using SuperSignal™ West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Thermo Fisher, 34095) and imaged using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad).
For loading control, membranes were stripped and reprobed with an antibody for GAPDH. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (NIH), normalized to loading controls, and expressed as fold change relative to control samples.

## Masson’s trichrome staining
To assess collagen deposition and fibrosis, tissue sections were stained using Masson’s Trichrome stain following standard histological procedures. Briefly, paraffin-embedded tissue blocks were sectioned at 4–6 μm thickness, mounted on glass slides, and deparaffinized in xylene (2 × 5 min) followed by rehydration through a graded series of ethanol (100%, 95%, 70%) and distilled water.
Tissues were then incubated in Bouin’s solution at 56 °C for 1 h to enhance staining intensity, followed by washing in running tap water to remove excess fixative. Slides were sequentially stained as follows: Weigert’s iron hematoxylin (to stain nuclei) for 5–10 min, followed by differentiation in acid alcohol (1% HCl in 70% ethanol) and rinsing in running water. Biebrich scarlet-acid fuchsin solution for 10–15 min to stain cytoplasm and muscle fibers. Phosphotungstic/phosphomolybdic acid solution for 5–10 min to differentiate collagen from muscle. Aniline blue for 5 min to stain collagen fibers. 1% acetic acid for 2 min to enhance contrast, followed by washing in distilled water. Slides were then dehydrated through increasing ethanol concentrations (70%, 95%, 100%) and cleared in xylene before being coverslipped with Permount. Collagen deposition was analyzed under bright-field microscopy, and fibrotic areas were quantified using ImageJ (NIH), expressed as a percentage of total tissue area.

## Assessment of glomerulosclerosis, interstitial fibrosis, and small intestinal aging
Kidney sections (2 μm) were stained with PAS and Masson trichrome. Using PAS stain, we scored every glomerulus within one section, according to the degree of glomerulosclerosis as summarized: score 0 for completely normal glomerulus; score 1 when there is mild mesangiosclerosis, defined by mesangial expansion <50%; score 2 for moderate sclerosis, defined by mesangial expansion greater than 50%, with extensive loss of capillary loop structure and podocytes and more extensive Bowman capsule thickening; score 3 for severe glomerulosclerosis, defined by complete or nearly complete (>75%) glomerulosclerosis with extensive adhesion to Bowman capsules, shrinkage of glomerular tufts with little or no visible/patent capillaries. The percentage of glomeruli in each score is presented separately and later combined to test the difference in significant glomerulosclerosis (score 2–3). All glomeruli of the kidney section are scored by kidney pathologists, blinded to the treatment groups. The degree of interstitial fibrosis is quantified by % of blue staining by ImageJ.
Quantitative analysis of Paneth cells, Goblet cells, and BrdU IHC was performed by a board-certified Gastrointestinal Pathologist (D.B.), blinded to the treatment groups.

## Generation of induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes and siRNA transfection
Human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPS cells) were cultured on plates coated with Matrigel (Corning Life Sciences) and maintained in a medium consisting of 75% serum-free medium (SFM) and 25% mTeSR+. For cardiomyocyte differentiation, iPS cells were passaged using a gentle cell dissociation reagent (STEMCELL) and seeded at a density of 3.5 × 105 cells per well in 12-well plates. Once the cells reached 95% confluency, differentiation was initiated by adding 3 mL of CM differentiation medium (RPMI 1640 with 2% B27 minus insulin) and 5 µM CHIR. On the first day, an additional 2 mL of RPMI 1640 with 2% B27 minus insulin was added per well. On the second day, 1 mL of the same medium was added to each well. On the third day, the old medium was replaced with fresh RPMI 1640 containing 2% B27 without insulin and 5 µM IWR-1. On the fifth day, the medium was refreshed with RPMI 1640 containing 2% B27 without insulin. On the seventh day, the medium was switched from CM differentiation to CM culture medium (RPMI 1640 supplemented with 2% B27). On the ninth day, cardiomyocytes were purified using RPMI 1640 medium without glucose, supplemented with 2% B27 and lactic acid (sodium L-lactate, Sigma–Aldrich). From day 14, tri-iodo-L-thyronine (T3; 100 nmol/L) and dexamethasone (1 μmol/L) were added to promote cardiomyocyte maturation. Cardiomyocytes were used approximately 25–35 days post-differentiation.
CHK2 siRNA (siRNA ID.s22119, Thermo Fisher) was used to transfect hiPSC-CMs. The siRNA was diluted to 100 nM in RPMI 1640 and Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. Cardiomyocytes were transfected for 24 h and utilized for subsequent experiments within 48 h.

## Fibroblast cell culture
Human fibroblasts, including Werner syndrome (WRN) fibroblasts and control fibroblasts (82-6 hTERT), were obtained from the University of Washington and maintained under standard culture conditions. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Invitrogen, Cat. #11965-118) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone, Cat. #SH30910.03) and 1× penicillin-streptomycin (Pen/Strep; Invitrogen, Cat. #15140-122). Cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere.

## Statistical analyses
Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software) or Stata 10. Results were represented as the average ± standard error for data with a normal distribution or as percentages for categorical information. To assess differences among groups, we utilized one-way ANOVA for evaluations involving multiple groups, followed by Sidak post-hoc tests for pairwise comparisons post-ANOVA. For comparisons between only two groups, we employed the Student’s t-test. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
