## Abstract

## Background
Chemoresistance remains a major challenge in colorectal cancer (CRC) treatment. Metabolic reprogramming is a cancer hallmark, with the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) playing a ceral role. This study aimed to investigate the role of the long noncoding RNA MAFG-AS1 in CRC chemoresistance and its underlying mechanism.

## Methods
MAFG-AS1 expression was analyzed using TCGA data. Functional roles were assessed via RNA interference, overexpression, RNA pull-down, RIP, metabolic flux analysis, isotope tracing, and xenograft models, combined with qPCR, Western blot, flow cytometry, and IHC. Statistical analyses used t-tests and ANOVA.

## Results
MAFG-AS1 was upregulated in CRC, driven transcriptionally by c-Myc. It directly bound to and stabilized G6PD homodimers, enhancing its enzymatic activity. This activated the PPP, increased NADPH production, reduced ROS levels, maintained redox homeostasis, and promoted CRC cell proliferation and tumor growth. MAFG-AS1 knockdown sensitized CRC cells to cisplatin and oxaliplatin.

## Conclusion
MAFG-AS1 promotes metabolic reprogramming and reduces platinum sensitivity in CRC by enhancing G6PD-dependent PPP flux and redox buffering capacity. Targeting the MAFG-AS1–G6PD axis may represent a potential strategy to improve the efficacy of platinum-based chemotherapy.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture
Human colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) cell lines (HCT116, HT29, RKO, SW480, Caco2, WiDr) and the immortalized nonmalignant colorectal epithelial line NCM460 were cultured in DMEM at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. All cell lines (Supplementary Table ) were regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination by PCR and authenticated by STR profiling. Cells within 15 passages after thawing were used for experiments. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded COAD tissues and matched adjacent normal tissues were obtained from the Shanxi Cancer Hospital Biobank.

## Small interfering RNA (siRNA) and short hairpin RNA (shRNA) oligos
SiRNA and shRNA oligonucleotides were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China) and TSINGKE Biological Technology (Beijing, China), respectively. siRNAs were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000(Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#L3000015), and all oligonucleotide sequences are provided in Supplementary Table .

## Inducible shRNA knockdown
Inducible MAFG-AS1 knockdown cell sublines were established following a previously described methodology [ ]. Annealed shRNA oligos were inserted into the FH1 tUTG inducible knockdown vector digested with BsmBI (New England Biolabs; Cat#R0580S) and XhoI (New England Biolabs; Cat#R0146S), followed by ligation with T4 DNA ligase (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#EL0014). Lentiviral particles were generated by co-transfecting HEK293T cells with FH1 tUTG shRNA plasmid (44 µg), pMDLg/pRRE (22 µg), pMD2.G (13.2 µg), and pRSV Rev (11 µg). Viral supernatants were used to transduce HCT116 or HT29 cells to establish inducible knockdown sublines. MAFG-AS1 knockdown was induced by doxycycline treatment, and shRNA sequences are provided in Supplementary Table .

## Plasmids
The FH1-tUTG plasmid was a kind gift from A/Professor M. J. Herold (Walter and Eliza Hall Institute of Medical Research, Australia). The pMDLg/pRRE plasmid (Addgene, Cat#12251), pMD2.g plasmid (Addgene; Cat#12259), and pRSV-Rev plasmid (Addgene; Cat#12253) were obtained from Addgene. The pcDNA3.1-MAFG-AS1, pcDNA3.1-c-Myc constructs were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China).

## Quantitative PCR (qPCR)
Total RNA was isolated from cultured cells with the GeneJET RNA Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific; Cat#K0731) following the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg total RNA using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa; Cat#RR047A; Dalian, China), and 12.5 ng of the resulting cDNA was amplified by quantitative PCR on a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method with normalization to the geometric mean of three reference genes (18 S rRNA). All primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table .

## Western blotting and reagents
In brief, cells were harvested and lysed with cell lysis buffer [10mM Tris-HCl (pH7.6), 140mM NaCl, 0.5mM CaCl2, 0.5mM MgCl2, 0.02% NaN3, 1% Triton X-100 and a proteinase inhibitor cocktail (#12352200, 6135 Roche)]. Equal amounts of the protein extracts were subjected to SDS-PAGE and analyzed by Western blotting. Antibody details and reagents are provided in Supplementary Tables and .

## Cell viability
Cell viability was assessed using the Fluorometric-Blue Cell Viability Kit (#ab102501, Abcam) per the manufacturer’s protocol. Fluorescence intensity (Ex/Em = 405/460 nm) was measured with a Synergy 2 multi-detection microplate reader (BioTek).

## Cell cycle
Cell cycle distribution was analyzed by flow cytometry following propidium iodide (PI) staining. Briefly, cells were fixed in 70% ethanol, treated with RNase A, and stained with PI before analysis on a BD FACSCanto flow cytometer.

## G6PD activity assay
G6PD activity in cell lysates was determined using a commercial colorimetric kit (BioVision), with absorbance measured at 450 nm. Briefly, cells were harvested and lysed, and clarified lysates were obtained by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Equal amounts of protein were incubated with the reaction mixture containing NADP⁺ and glucose-6-phosphate at 37 °C for the indicated time. The generation of NADPH was monitored by measuring absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader. G6PD activity was normalized to total protein concentration and expressed relative to control samples.

## Apoptosis
Apoptotic cells were quantitated using the FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit (#556547, BD Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were harvested, washed twice with cold PBS, and resuspended in 1× binding buffer at a concentration of 1 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Cells were incubated with FITC-conjugated Annexin V and propidium iodide for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. After incubation, samples were analyzed by flow cytometry using a BD FACSCanto instrument within 1 h. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software, and apoptotic cells were defined as Annexin V–positive populations.

## Colony formation assay
Cells were seeded in 6-well plates (2,000 cells/well) and cultured for 10–14 days. Colonies were fixed with methanol, stained with 0.5% crystal violet, and imaged using a Bio-Rad Gel Doc XR+ system. Quantitative analysis of colony area and staining intensity was performed using the ColonyArea plugin in ImageJ.

## Immunofluorescence (IF)
IF was performed as previously described. Briefly, cells grown on coverslips were fixed with 4% formaldehyde and permeabilized with a buffer containing 0.1% Triton X-100. After incubation with primary antibodies, cells were hybridized with CY3-conjugated MAFG-AS1 RNA probes in the dark. Coverslips were mounted using Pro-Long™ Glass Antifade Mountant with NucBlue stain, and images were acquired under consistent settings on a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope. Images were acquired on a Leica TCS SP8 confocal laser-scanning microscope using identical acquisition settings across conditions and were processed and quantified using ImageJ.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
FFPE tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After blocking endogenous peroxidase and nonspecific binding, sections were incubated overnight with anti-Ki-67 antibody, followed by secondary antibody incubation and DAB development. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. Staining was evaluated independently by two blinded investigators using an immunoreactive score (IRS) based on the percentage of positive cells and staining intensity.

## Metabolic flux analysis
Isotope tracing HCT116 cells subjected to different treatments were cultured in glucose-free DMEM supplemented with 10 mM [1,2-¹³C] glucose and 10% dialyzed fetal bovine serum for 1 h at 37 °C, a labeling duration chosen to capture early metabolic flux prior to isotopic steady state and to minimize secondary carbon recycling. Cells were immediately harvested, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and extracted with 1 mL of ice-cold (− 40 °C) 50% aqueous methanol. Samples were incubated on dry ice for 30 min, thawed on ice, and further extracted with 0.4 mL chloroform. After vortexing for 30 s, samples were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the upper aqueous phase was collected and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Water-soluble metabolites were analyzed using a Q Exactive PLUS hybrid quadrupole–Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) coupled to hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC). Separation was performed on an XBridge BEH Amide column (150 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.5 μm; Waters) at 25 °C with a flow rate of 150 µL/min and an injection volume of 5 µL. Mobile phase A consisted of 95:5 H₂O: acetonitrile supplemented with 20 mM ammonium acetate and 20 mM ammonium hydroxide (pH 9.4), and mobile phase B consisted of 100% acetonitrile. Mass spectra were acquired in negative ion mode with a resolution of 140,000 at m/z 200, an automatic gain control target of 1 × 10⁶, and a scan range of m/z 75–1000. Isotope tracing data were analyzed using El-MAVEN software with correction for natural isotope abundance and normalized to total cellular protein content. Each condition included five independent biological replicates.

## Subcellular fractionation
Cells were lysed in hypotonic buffer to separate cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected as the cytoplasmic fraction. The pellet was then lysed in high-salt buffer to extract nuclear components. Both fractions were clarified by high-speed centrifugation, and the resulting supernatants were used for RNA isolation.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)
Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was performed using a commercial kit per manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, crosslinked cells were lysed and sonicated to shear chromatin. Lysates were immunoprecipitated overnight with anti-E2F1 antibody or control IgG, followed by bead capture, washing, and DNA purification. Precipitated DNA was analyzed by PCR/qPCR with gene-specific primers.

## Extracellular Acidification Rate (ECAR) and Oxygen Consumption Rate (OCR)
ECAR and OCR were measured using the Seahorse XF24 analyzer with the Glycolysis Stress Test Kit and Cell Mito Stress Test Kit, respectively. Briefly, 20,000 cells/well were seeded and assayed after 24 h. Measurements were taken in base medium following sequential injections of specific metabolic modulators (glucose, oligomycin, and 2-DG for ECAR; oligomycin, FCCP, and Rot + AA for OCR). Data were analyzed using the respective Seahorse software packages and normalized to cell count.

## Measurements of cellular ATP, lactate, glucose, and ROS levels
Cellular ATP, lactate, and glucose levels were measured using commercially available assay kits (Abcam and Promega) following the manufacturers’ instructions. Cells were collected and lysed as required for each assay, and cell debris was removed by centrifugation. Equal amounts of lysate were used for subsequent measurements. Luminescence or absorbance signals were recorded using a BioTek Synergy 2 microplate reader. Where applicable, values were normalized to total protein content or cell number.
Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were evaluated using both a plate-based luminescence assay and a flow cytometry–based method. For plate-based detection, cells were incubated with the ROS-sensitive reagent and analyzed using a microplate reader. For flow cytometric analysis, cells were stained with the indicated fluorescent probe and analyzed on a BD FACSCanto flow cytometer. Data were processed using FlowJo software, and ROS levels were expressed relative to control conditions.

## NADPH measurements
Cellular NADPH levels were quantified using the Amplite™ Colorimetric NADPH Assay Kit (AAT Bioquest, 15272). Briefly, lysates from 5 × 10^6 cells were prepared and centrifuged. The supernatant was then incubated with the reaction mixture at room temperature for 2 h, and the absorbance was measured at 460 nm using a microplate reader.

## Biotin RNA Pulldown (RPD)
Biotin-labeled RNAs were synthesized by in vitro transcription followed by 3′-end biotinylation. For each pull-down reaction, 4.5 µg of biotinylated RNA probe was incubated with cell lysates prepared from approximately 2 × 10⁷ cells in binding buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 10% Glycerol, 0.5% Nonidet P-40, 1 mM DTT, cOmplete™ EDTA-free Protease Inhibitor Cocktail and RNase inhibitors and sonicated (10 s on/off cycles for 1 min). The binding reaction was performed overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation. On the following day, streptavidin beads were added to the RNA–protein complexes and incubated for 3 h at 4 °C. Beads were washed three times with wash buffer containing 150–300 mM NaCl to reduce nonspecific interactions. Bound proteins and RNAs were subsequently analyzed by immunoblotting and nucleic acid gel electrophoresis. Biotinylated LacZ RNA was used as a negative control. Probe sequences are shown in Supplementary Table . Information on antibody and primers used in this study is shown in Supplementary Tables and , respectively.

## RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)
The RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the Magna RIP kit. Briefly, approximately 2 × 10⁷ cells were lysed in hypotonic buffer supplemented with RNase inhibitor and protease inhibitor prior to centrifugation. The clarified cell lysates were immunoprecipitated with 2.5 µg of the indicated antibody or IgG control overnight at 4 °C, followed by rotation with magnetic beads for 3 h. After washing with RIP wash buffer, the bead-bound immunocomplexes were subjected to immunoblotting analysis and RNA isolation. Information on the antibodies and primers used in this study is provided in Supplementary Tables and .

## In vitro transcription
RNAs for downstream applications were synthesized in vitro. Briefly, plasmid templates containing a T7 promoter were linearized with BstBI (New England Biolabs, #R0519S) and served as templates for transcription using the TranscriptAid T7 High Yield Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #K0441). Where specified, the synthesized RNAs were subsequently 3’-end labeled with desthiobiotin using the Pierce RNA 3’ End Desthiobiotinylation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #20163).

## Crosslinking assay
Protein dimerization of G6PD was evaluated by DSS crosslinking. Briefly, 3 × 106 cells were resuspended in conjugation buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 8.0) and subjected to sonication. After centrifugation, the clarified lysates were incubated with DSS at a final concentration of 2 mM for 30 min at 37 °C. The crosslinking reaction was quenched by addition of Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) to a final concentration of 20 mM and incubated for 15 min. Samples were subsequently analyzed by immunoblotting. Samples without DSS treatment were included as negative controls.

## RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)
RNA-seq was performed by GENEWIZ (Suzhou, China). Total RNA sequencing was conducted by GENEWIZ. Sequencing libraries were prepared from poly(A)-selected mRNA and run on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system (RRID: SCR_016387). The resulting reads underwent quality control with FastQC (RRID: SCR_014583), were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 using STAR (RRID: SCR_015899), and gene-level counts were generated with featureCounts (RRID: SCR_012919). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (RRID: SCR_015687), which employs a negative binomial model; genes with an adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05 (Wald test with Benjamini-Hochberg procedure) were defined as differentially expressed.

## Silver staining and mass spectrometry analysis
Proteins pulled down with biotin-labeled MAFG-AS1 or LacZ RNA were separated by SDS-PAGE and visualized using a standard silver staining protocol. Specific protein bands unique to the MAFG-AS1 group were excised, subjected to in-gel digestion, and analyzed by LC-MS/MS on an Agilent 6540 mass spectrometer. Protein identification was performed using Mascot software against the MSPnr100 database.

## Mouse models
All animal studies were approved by the Animal Laboratory Center of Zhengzhou University (ZZU-LAC2025090915; Zhengzhou, China) and conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. To establish xenograft models, 4-week-old male BALB/c-Nude mice (Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., China) were randomly and subcutaneously injected in the dorsal flank with 1 × 10⁷ HT29.shMAFG-AS1 cells suspended in 100 µL of solution (n = 6 per group). Randomization was performed at the time of tumor cell implantation to ensure unbiased group allocation. The study was conducted in a randomized and double-blind manner, and investigators responsible for tumor measurement and data analysis were blinded to treatment assignments. Mice received intraperitoneal injections of CDDP (2.5 mg/kg), Dox (2 mg/kg or 1 mg/kg), or vehicle control every other day. Body weight and clinical symptoms were monitored throughout the study. Tumor volume was assessed using the formula V = L × W²/2. Humane endpoints were strictly followed: mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation and cervical dislocation if the tumor volume exceeded 2000 mm³, ulceration developed, or body weight loss surpassed 15%. Mice that satisfied these criteria were euthanized by inhalation of carbon dioxide followed by cervical dislocation. The number of mice used for each group was determined on the basis of prior experience with xenograft studies using similar models, and it was sufficient to observe significant differences in tumor growth. The number of mice used for each group is mentioned in the figure legends.

## Statistical analysis
The statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Differences between groups were determined by a two-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. A P value of less than 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

## Replicates and experimental design
All experiments were performed with three or five biological replicates (n = 3 or n = 5), unless otherwise specified. For multi-well assays, each biological replicate included two to three technical replicates. In vivo experiments were analyzed with each mouse considered an independent biological replicate, and group sizes are indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Statistical analyses were conducted using biological replicates.

## Introduction
Colorectal cancer (CRC) ranks as the third most commonly diagnosed and the second most lethal malignancy worldwide [ – ]. To date, CRC has emerged as one of the most critical challenges for healthcare systems across numerous countries. Although early-stage CRC is potentially curable through surgical resection with or without adjuvant chemotherapy, patients with advanced CRC, particularly those with liver metastases, still face unsatisfactory clinical outcomes and markedly reduced survival [ – ]. This poor prognosis is largely due to the development of therapeutic resistance in CRC cells. Thus, there is an urgent need to clarify the underlying molecular mechanisms and to identify novel therapeutic targets.
Metabolic reprogramming is a defining hallmark of cancer, enabling tumor cells to meet increased demands for energy and biosynthetic precursors while maintaining tight control over [ – ]. Central to this balance is the dynamic regulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [ , ], which act as a double-edged sword in cancer biology. At physiological levels, ROS function as key signaling molecules that activate pro-tumorigenic pathways, including HIF-1α and NF-κB, thereby enhancing cancer cell proliferation and metastasis [ ]. In contrast, excessive ROS accumulation leads to oxidative stress, causing irreversible damage to cellular macromolecules and ultimately inducing apoptosis. To maintain this delicate equilibrium, cancer cells develop sophisticated adaptive strategies, among which the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) plays a critical role [ ]. As the primary source of cellular NADPH, the PPP sustains redox homeostasis by multiple mechanisms: regenerating reduced glutathione (GSH) to detoxify ROS [ ], supporting the thioredoxin antioxidant system, and providing ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotide biosynthesis. Notably, the rate-limiting enzyme glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) is frequently upregulated in malignancies, forming a metabolic hub that coordinates redox regulation with anabolic growth [ ]. This integrated control allows cancer cells to maintain ROS within a narrow range that promotes tumorigenesis while avoiding cytotoxicity.
The critical position of PPP in cancer redox regulation has revealed novel therapeutic vulnerabilities. Emerging strategies targeting this axis - including G6PD inhibition and modulation of its regulatory networks represent promising approaches to disrupt the metabolic adaptations that underlie treatment resistance [ ]. Further elucidation of PPP-mediated redox control mechanisms may yield innovative combination therapies that simultaneously target energy metabolism and oxidative stress pathways in malignant cells.
Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have attracted considerable attention owing to their pivotal roles in cancer initiation, progression and therapy resistance [ – ]. Recent research has revealed that the interplay between lncRNAs and oxidative stress is central to maintaining redox homeostasis in tumour cells [ ]. For instance, in colorectal cancer, LINC01615 promotes the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) via hnRNPA1‑mediated G6PD mRNA splicing, increasing NADPH, reducing ROS, and supporting survival and chemoresistance [ ]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, LINC01532 similarly enhances G6PD splicing through hnRNPK, lowering ROS and driving lenvatinib resistance. Knockdown of either lncRNA restores drug sensitivity, highlighting the PPP‑regulating lncRNA axis as a potential therapeutic target [ ]. In addition, in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma, hypoxia-induced NLUCAT1 reduces ROS by transcriptionally upregulating antioxidant genes, including aldehyde dehydrogenase 3A1 (ALDH3A1), glutathione peroxidase 2 (GPX2), glutaredoxin (GLRX), and pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4),thereby promoting tumor cell proliferation [ ].These findings not only elucidate the diverse regulatory mechanisms of lncRNAs in tumor antioxidant defense but also provide a theoretical foundation for novel anti-cancer strategies targeting the lncRNA-oxidative stress axis.
Here, we provide evidence that the long non-coding RNA MAFG-AS1 promotes G6PD homodimerization and activation, resulting in increased NADPH production and enhanced ROS scavenging. By lowering ROS levels, MAFG-AS1 not only supports colorectal cancer cell survival, proliferation, and tumorigenicity, and enhances tolerance to platinum-induced DNA damage. These findings highlight a previously unrecognized role of the MAFG-AS1–G6PD axis in metabolic adaptation to chemotherapy stress and suggest that targeting MAFG-AS1 may represent a potential strategy to improve the efficacy of platinum-based therapy in COAD.

## Results

## The upregulation of MAFG-AS1 in colorectal cancer is mediated by c-Myc
Given that aberrant activation of c-Myc coordinates extensive gene regulatory networks that critically contribute to the initiation and progression of diverse human malignancies [ ], and that an increasing number of lncRNAs have been recognized as integral components of c-Myc–mediated transcriptional programs, we sought to systematically identify downstream lncRNA effectors potentially involved in cancer development across multiple tumor types [ ]. To this end, we performed a comprehensive analysis of lncRNA expression profiles from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), we identified a subset of lncRNAs that were consistently upregulated across at least 50 genes in 20 examined cancer types compared with their corresponding normal tissues(Fig. a).We focused our analysis on colorectal cancer and observed that among the highly expressed lncRNAs identified in our screen, one in particular, v-maf avian musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma oncogene homolog G (MAFG) antisense RNA1 (MAFG-AS1), exhibited the highest expression level in colorectal cancer (Fig. b).In situ hybridization (ISH) validation confirmed this observation, demonstrating elevated MAFG-AS1 expression in a cohort of 112 formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) COAD tissues relative to paired adjacent normal colorectal samples (Fig. c, d; Supplementary Table ). It is noteworthy that MAFG-AS1 expression showed no significant differences between groups stratified by disease stage, tumor grade, patient sex, or age at diagnosis (Supplementary Table ), suggesting that MAFG-AS1 upregulation is a common early event in the pathogenesis of COAD. Disease-free interval (DFI) analysis was performed in patients with stage II–III colorectal cancer from the TCGA cohort. Patients were stratified into high and low MAFG-AS1 expression groups based on the median expression level. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that patients with high MAFG-AS1 expression had significantly shorter DFI compared with those with low expression (Fig. e). Indeed, MAFG-AS1 expression levels were increased by several dozen to several hundred-fold in colorectal cancer cells compared to normal colorectal epithelial cells (Fig. f).
 We will further investigate whether MAFG-AS1 is directly regulated by c-Myc and delineate its potential functional contribution to tumorigenesis. To explore the mechanism underlying enhanced transcriptional activation of the MAFG-AS1 gene in COAD cells, chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) data from the Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE) consortium revealed binding peaks of c-Myc [ ]—a critical mediator of c-Myc-dependent transcriptional activation—at the c-Myc binding region (cMyc-BR) of the MAFG-AS1 promoter (Fig. g). Accordingly, c-Myc co-precipitated with the MAFG-AS1 promoter in human colorectal cancer cell lines (HCT116, HT29, and RKO), and siRNA-mediated silencing of c-Myc reduced MAFG-AS1 expression (Fig. h, i; Supplementary Fig. a, b). Conversely, c-Myc overexpression upregulated MAFG-AS1 (Fig. j; Supplementary Fig. c). Thus, the expression of MAFG-AS1 in colorectal cancer cells is driven by c-Myc. Supporting this conclusion, analysis of the TCGA database revealed a significant positive correlation between the expression levels of c-Myc and MAFG-AS1 (Fig. k).

## MAFG-AS1 promotes COAD cell proliferation and tumorigenicity
We next investigated the functional significance of MAFG-AS1 in COAD. siRNA-mediated knockdown of MAFG-AS1 suppressed the viability of HCT116, HT29 and RKO cells (Fig.  a, b; Supplementary Fig.  a, b). This suppression was accompanied by a reduction in clonogenic capacity (Fig.  c, d; Supplementary Fig.  c, d). In contrast, MAFG-AS1 overexpression moderately enhanced the proliferation of HCT116 and HT29 cells (Fig.  e, f; Supplementary Fig.  g, h).
 To facilitate further studies, we established HCT116 and HT29 sublines—HCT116.shMAFG-AS1 and HT29.shMAFG-AS1—in which MAFG-AS1 was conditionally knocked down by shRNA upon doxycycline (Dox) induction (Fig. g; Supplementary Fig. e). Dox treatment reduced MAFG-AS1 expression, inhibited cell viability, and impaired clonogenic ability (Fig. h-j; Supplementary Fig. f). Upon Dox withdrawal, MAFG-AS1 levels were restored, leading to the recovery of clonogenic capacity (Fig. i, j). In nu/nu mice bearing COAD xenografts derived from subcutaneous implantation of HT29.shMAFG-AS1 cells, Dox administration suppressed tumor growth (Fig. k, l). This suppression was associated with a significant decrease in the proportion of Ki67-positive cells (Fig. n, o), indicating reduced proliferation [ ]. Cessation of Dox treatment restored MAFG-AS1 and Ki67 expression, concomitant with tumor regrowth (Fig. k-o). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MAFG-AS1 promotes COAD cell proliferation and tumorigenicity both in vitro and in vivo.

## MAFG-AS1 maintains redox homeostasis and promotes cancer cell survival via cytoplasmic function
To investigate the underlying mechanism for the suppressed proliferation following MAFG-AS1 knockdown, we analyzed cell cycle distribution and apoptosis by flow cytometry. The results indicated that the growth-inhibitory effect was primarily attributable to cell cycle arrest at the G0-G1 phase (Fig.  a; Supplementary Fig.  b), with no significant increase in apoptosis (Supplementary Fig.  a). To explore the biological processes affected by MAFG-AS1 knockdown, GO enrichment analysis was performed on genes upregulated in HT29 cells. The top 30 significantly enriched GO terms are shown in Fig.  b as a bar plot, ranked in ascending order of P value. The results indicated that reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolic pathways were significantly enriched upon MAFG-AS1 knockdown (Fig.  b), suggesting an antioxidant role of MAFG-AS1 in cancer cells. Consistently, siRNA-mediated knockdown of MAFG-AS1 markedly increased endogenous ROS levels in HCT116 and HT29 cells, as measured by CellROX™ Deep Red flow cytometry (Fig.  c), whereas its overexpression reduced ROS accumulation (Fig.  d). Importantly, the reduced viability of HCT116 and HT29 cells induced by MAFG-AS1 knockdown was partially rescued by the ROS scavengers glutathione (GSH) and N-acetylcysteine (NAC) (Fig.  e, f; Supplementary Fig.  c, d). Collectively, these results indicate that MAFG-AS1 maintains redox homeostasis to protect cancer cells from oxidative stress.
 Subcellular fractionation analysis revealed that MAFG-AS1 was predominantly localized in the cytoplasm of HCT116 and HT29 cells, with minor nuclear distribution (Fig.  g; Supplementary Fig.  e). This cytoplasmic predominance was further confirmed by in situ hybridization, implying that MAFG-AS1 exerts its function mainly in the cytoplasm of colorectal cancer cells (Fig.  h; Supplementary Fig.  f).

## MAFG-AS1 promotes oxidative PPP flux and glycolytic pathways
As a central metabolic regulator, ROS dynamically remodels cellular metabolic networks through dual mechanisms to coordinate glucose metabolic flux [ ]. This precise redox regulation enables ROS to establish a dynamic equilibrium between redox balance (NADPH/NADP⁺) and energy supply (ATP generation) according to microenvironmental demands. To investigate the metabolic functions of MAFG-AS1, we employed [1,2-¹³C]-glucose isotope tracing in HCT116 cells. Isotope tracing revealed that MAFG-AS1 depletion significantly attenuated both the oxidative PPP flux, as quantified by M1-labeled ribose, and the glycolytic pathway, measured via M2-labeled intermediate metabolites (Fig. a-g; Supplementary Fig. a). These findings collectively demonstrate that MAFG-AS1 promotes oxidative PPP flux and glycolysis in colorectal cancer cells.
 Consistent with the impaired glycolysis, MAFG-AS1 knockdown significantly decreased lactate production (Fig.  l), glucose uptake (Supplementary Fig.  b), and ATP levels (Fig.  j). The attenuation of the oxidative PPP was further confirmed by a reduced NADPH level (Fig.  k), whereas MAFG-AS1 overexpression produced the opposite effects (Supplementary Fig.  c-f). Moreover, Seahorse metabolic flux analyses in vitro confirmed these observations, showing that MAFG-AS1 depletion reduced both oxygen consumption and glycolytic rate (Fig.  h, i).

## MAFG-AS1 interacts with G6PD
To elucidate the mechanism underlying MAFG-AS1-mediated metabolic reprogramming, we hypothesized that MAFG-AS1, like many other lncRNAs, exerts its effects through protein interactions. Mass spectrometry analysis of specifically excised silver-stained bands identified 44 potential MAFG-AS1-binding partners, with G6PD being a primary candidate (Fig. a; Supplementary Table ). Among these candidates, G6PD—the rate-limiting enzyme of the PPP—emerged as a cytoplasmic antioxidant enzyme of particular interest. Computational structural prediction and subsequent protein-RNA docking using HDOCK [ ] yielded a plausible binding model between MAFG-AS1 and G6PD. Interface analysis predicted specific contacts between G6PD residues and MAFG-AS1 nucleotides, primarily involving electrostatic interactions with the phosphate backbone and the formation of hydrogen bonds (Fig. b).
 To experimentally validate this predicted interaction, we performed RNA pull-down (RPD) and RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays in HCT116 and HT29 cells. RPD analysis detected G6PD in precipitates using antisense probes targeting endogenous MAFG-AS1 (Fig.  d). Consistently, RIP assays demonstrated co-immunoprecipitation of MAFG-AS1 and G6PD protein in HCT116 and HT29 cells using G6PD antibodies (Fig.  c). In addition, in vitro–transcribed MAFG-AS1 interacted with recombinant G6PD in a cell-free system, as demonstrated by RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) analysis (Fig.  e), supporting the existence of a direct interaction.
Deletion mapping of full-length and mutant MAFG-AS1 constructs revealed that sequences within exon 2 are essential for G6PD binding (Fig.  f, g, i). Further subdivision of exon 2 revealed that deletion of exon 2 or inclusion of the longer truncation nt 990–1895 resulted in reduced association with G6PD, whereas removal of the nt 1488–1895 fragment had little effect on binding. This suggests that sequences within nt 990–1488 contribute to G6PD interaction(Supplementary Fig.  a). Conversely, G6PD domain 2 (aa 202–515), which mediates G6PD dimerization, specifically interacted with MAFG-AS1 (Fig.  h).

## MAFG-AS1 enhances G6PD activity and homodimerization independently of p53
We next sought to determine the functional impact of the MAFG-AS1–G6PD interaction. In HCT116 and HT29 cells, siRNA-mediated knockdown of MAFG-AS1 suppressed G6PD activity (Fig.  a), while its overexpression potentiated G6PD activity (Fig.  b). Critically, G6PD protein abundance remained unchanged under these conditions (Fig.  c, d). To determine whether the observed metabolic defects were mechanistically linked to impaired G6PD function, Flag-tagged G6PD was ectopically expressed in MAFG-AS1–silenced HT29 and HCT116 cells. G6PD overexpression significantly restored G6PD enzymatic activity (Supplementary Fig.  a, b) and NADPH production (Supplementary Fig.  c, d) following MAFG-AS1 knockdown. Consistent with improved redox capacity, intracellular ATP levels (Supplementary Fig.  e) were partially recovered. Importantly, G6PD reconstitution also significantly rescued cell number in both cell lines (Fig.  e, f), indicating that impaired cell growth upon MAFG-AS1 depletion is, at least in part, mediated through loss of G6PD activity. In contrast, a binding-deficient MAFG-AS1 mutant lacking nucleotides 990–1488 failed to rescue cell number, G6PD enzymatic activity, NADPH levels, or intracellular ATP levels (Supplementary Fig.  f-i).These results demonstrate that the MAFG-AS1–G6PD binding is functionally critical for G6PD activation, likely by fine-tuning the enzyme’s conformation.
 Since G6PD functions as a homodimer, we examined whether MAFG-AS1 promotes dimer formation. Chemical crosslinking with DSS revealed that MAFG-AS1 knockdown reduced G6PD dimers while increasing monomers in HCT116 and HT29 cells. Co-immunoprecipitation of HA-tagged and Flag-tagged G6PD confirmed homodimer formation in HCT116 cells (Fig.  g, h). Two-step RIP assays demonstrated that anti-Flag antibodies precipitated Flag-G6PD, HA-G6PD, and MAFG-AS1 from total protein extracts, while subsequent anti-HA immunoprecipitation retrieved Flag-G6PD and MAFG-AS1 (Fig.  i), suggesting that MAFG-AS1 forms a ternary complex with the G6PD homodimer. Collectively, these findings indicate that MAFG-AS1 facilitates and supports G6PD homodimer formation.
Previous studies established that MAFG-AS1 promotes p53 degradation in cancer cells [ ]. Given that p53 reportedly binds and inhibits G6PD [ ], we investigated p53’s involvement in MAFG-AS1-mediated G6PD activation. Surprisingly, p53 co-knockdown did not rescue the suppression of G6PD activity caused by MAFG-AS1 knockdown in HCT116 and HT29 cells (Fig. j, k). Similarly, p53 knockout HCT116 cells showed no rescue of G6PD activity. Supporting this, p53 co-depletion did not ameliorate the MAFG-AS1 knockdown-induced reduction in G6PD dimers (Supplementary Fig. j, k). These results demonstrate that MAFG-AS1 activates G6PD by promoting its homodimerization through p53-independent mechanisms.

## MAFG-AS1 inhibition sensitizes COAD to DNA-damaging therapies
We therefore evaluated the role of MAFG-AS1 in modulating the sensitivity of colorectal cancer cells to cisplatin (CDDP) and oxaliplatin (OXA), which exert their therapeutic effects primarily through DNA damage [ ]. Using TCGA colorectal cancer samples, we analyzed the association between MAFG-AS1 expression and predicted cisplatin sensitivity. Predicted cisplatin IC50 values were inferred using the pRRophetic computational drug response model. Correlation analysis revealed a modest but statistically significant positive association between MAFG-AS1 expression and predicted cisplatin IC50 (Supplementary Fig. a; p = 0.0379), indicating that higher MAFG-AS1 expression was associated with reduced predicted cisplatin sensitivity. Consistent with these predictions, MAFG-AS1 knockout synergized with CDDP or OXA to suppress cell viability and induce enhanced apoptosis in HCT116 and HT29 cells (Fig. a, b; Supplementary Fig. b, c). This apoptotic induction was evidenced by the upregulation of the pro-apoptotic proteins PUMA and BAX (Supplementary Fig. d, e). To directly assess whether MAFG-AS1 modulates tolerance to platinum-induced DNA damage, we quantified γH2AX (phosphorylated histone H2AX at Ser139), a well-established marker of DNA double-strand breaks [ ], following cisplatin or oxaliplatin treatment. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that platinum exposure induced robust γH2AX foci formation, indicating increased DNA damage accumulation. Notably, MAFG-AS1 knockdown further enhanced γH2AX signal intensity under platinum stress, suggesting impaired DNA damage tolerance. Importantly, co-treatment with NAC substantially reduced γH2AX accumulation in MAFG-AS1–deficient cells and partially restored cellular resistance to platinum treatment(Fig. c, d). These results provide direct functional evidence that MAFG-AS1 limits platinum-induced DNA damage through a redox-dependent mechanism.
 In xenograft models established with HT29.shMAFG-AS1 cells, the combination of Dox-induced MAFG-AS1 knockdown and CDDP/OXA treatment produced significantly greater tumor growth inhibition than either treatment alone (Fig.  e, f; Supplementary Fig.  f-h). The enhanced tumor growth suppression was associated with a marked reduction in Ki67-positive cells and an increase in TUNEL-positive staining, confirming impaired proliferation and induced apoptosis (Fig.  g-j). Collectively, these findings identify MAFG-AS1 inhibition as a promising therapeutic strategy for sensitizing COAD to DNA-damaging agents.

## Discussion
Cancer cells rely on finely tuned redox balance to support proliferation and adapt to metabolic and genotoxic stress [ , ]. In this study, we found that the long non-coding RNA MAFG-AS1 is a previously unrecognized regulator of this process. We demonstrate that the lncRNA MAFG-AS1 directly binds G6PD and facilitates formation of its active homodimer, leading to enhanced PPP activity, increased NADPH generation, and improved antioxidant capacity. This mechanism supports tumor cell proliferation and tumor growth (Fig. ), and highlights a non-transcriptional role of lncRNAs in regulating redox homeostasis.
 Previous work has described MAFG-AS1 as an oncogenic lncRNA that is aberrantly expressed in a wide range of malignancies, including breast, lung, liver, bladder, colorectal, gastric, and pancreatic cancers [ – ]. Increased MAFG-AS1 expression is frequently linked to more aggressive disease features and unfavorable clinical outcomes [ ]. At the mechanistic level, most studies to date have focused on transcriptional or post-transcriptional regulation, with MAFG-AS1 commonly functioning as a competing endogenous RNA that shapes microRNA activity and downstream gene expression programs involved in tumor growth, invasion, and therapy resistance [ – ]. By contrast, our data point to a different mode of action. Rather than regulating gene expression, MAFG-AS1 directly associates with the metabolic enzyme G6PD and promotes formation of its active homodimer, leading to increased enzymatic activity. This post-transcriptional, non-transcriptional mechanism broadens the functional scope of MAFG-AS1 and suggests that lncRNAs can directly influence metabolic flux by modulating enzyme assembly.
Recent studies have further highlighted the pivotal role of G6PD in tumor metabolism and redox homeostasis [ , ]. As the key rate-limiting enzyme of the oxidative branch of PPP, G6PD plays a central role in maintaining cellular redox balance through NADPH production [ – ]. Emerging research has identified that lncRNAs can regulate G6PD function through diverse molecular mechanisms. For instance, in multiple myeloma, lncRNA PDIA3P interacts with transcription factor c-Myc to promote c-Myc-mediated transcriptional activation of G6PD, whereas in malignant melanoma, the tumor-suppressive lncRNA GAS5 binds to G6PD to reduce its protein stability [ ]. Our study uncovers a novel regulatory paradigm: lncRNA MAFG-AS1 does not affect G6PD expression levels but rather directly interacts with G6PD enzyme molecules to promote homodimer formation. Specifically, MAFG-AS1 forms stable ternary complexes with G6PD, and its depletion significantly reduces G6PD dimerization, with this regulatory mechanism operating independently of the classical p53 signaling pathway.
DNA-damaging agents (such as platinum-based drugs and PARP inhibitors) eliminate tumor cells primarily by inducing DNA breaks [ , , ]. However, the repair of DNA damage requires substantial NADPH and nucleotide precursors, both of which rely on sustained PPP activity [ – ]. Oxaliplatin-based regimens such as FOLFOX and CAPEOX remain the standard chemotherapy backbone for colorectal cancer [ ], yet their therapeutic benefit is frequently constrained by cumulative neurotoxicity and the development of acquired chemoresistance [ ]. Increasing evidence suggests that metabolic and redox reprogramming contributes to reduced platinum sensitivity, underscoring the need for therapeutic strategies that disrupt adaptive metabolic defense mechanisms [ , ]. Our findings reveal that MAFG-AS1 enhances PPP flux, providing tumor cells with increased NADPH and biosynthetic substrates, thereby promoting efficient recovery from genotoxic stress. Consistent with this, loss of MAFG-AS1 significantly sensitizes colorectal cancer cells to DNA-damaging agents, including CDDP and OXA. Moreover, MAFG-AS1 inhibition markedly improves the therapeutic response to CDDP in COAD xenograft models.
Together, these findings suggest that targeting MAFG-AS1, or more broadly suppressing PPP-dependent metabolic reprogramming, may provide an effective strategy to overcome resistance to DNA-damaging chemotherapy in COAD. However, several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. First, the structural basis and regulatory dynamics of the MAFG-AS1–G6PD interaction remain insufficiently defined. The absence of high-resolution structural evidence and systematic mutational analysis limits precise understanding of how MAFG-AS1 promotes G6PD dimerization and enzymatic activation. Second, the translational relevance of this pathway requires further validation. Our in vivo experiments are restricted to cell line–derived xenograft models and lack confirmation in genetically engineered mouse models, patient-derived xenografts, or prospective clinical cohorts. Although retrospective analyses suggest an association between elevated MAFG-AS1 expression and reduced platinum sensitivity, comprehensive survival analyses (including overall survival and progression-free survival) and clinically annotated treatment-response datasets remain limited, precluding definitive conclusions regarding its prognostic or predictive value. Third, metabolic plasticity may attenuate the long-term therapeutic impact of targeting the MAFG-AS1–G6PD axis. Alternative NADPH-generating pathways and adaptive metabolic rewiring could compensate for PPP suppression, and the potential emergence of acquired tolerance under prolonged therapeutic pressure has not been systematically evaluated. In addition, possible toxicity to normal tissues dependent on PPP activity warrants careful investigation.
Future studies integrating multi-omics analyses, advanced disease models, and structure-informed molecular approaches will be necessary to elucidate the spatiotemporal regulation and systems-level consequences of this pathway, thereby providing a more rigorous foundation for metabolism-targeted combination strategies.
In conclusion, our study supports a scaffold-like contribution of MAFG-AS1 to G6PD homodimer formation, highlighting a previously underappreciated non-transcriptional mechanism influencing pentose phosphate pathway activity in tumor cells. Distinct from previously reported transcriptional mechanisms, MAFG-AS1 represents a class of functional lncRNAs that regulate metabolic enzyme activity by modulating protein–protein interactions and enzyme oligomerization. This groundbreaking discovery significantly expands our understanding of non-coding RNA mechanisms in tumor metabolic reprogramming while identifying potential therapeutic targets for novel metabolism-targeted strategies that could be combined with conventional DNA-damaging therapies.
