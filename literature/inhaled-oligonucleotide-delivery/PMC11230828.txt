## Abstract

## Background:
Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a common cause of respiratory failure in many critically ill patients. Although inflammasome activation plays an important role in the induction of acute lung injury (ALI) and ARDS, the regulatory mechanism of this process is still unclear. When cells are stimulated by inflammation, the integrity and physiological function of mitochondria play a crucial part in pyroptosis. However, the underlying mechanisms and function of mitochondrial proteins in the process of pyroptosis are largely not yet known. Here, we identified the 18-kDa translocator protein (TSPO), a mitochondrial outer membrane protein, as an important mediator regulating nucleotide-binding domain, leucine-rich repeat, and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation in macrophages during ALI.

## Methods:
TSPO gene knockout (KO) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced ALI/ARDS mouse models were employed to investigate the biological role of TSPO in the pathogenesis of ARDS. Murine macrophages were used to further characterize the effect of TSPO on the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. Activation of NLRP3 inflammasome was preformed through LPS + adenosine triphosphate (ATP) co-stimulation, followed by detection of mitochondrial membrane potential, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and cell death to evaluate the potential biological function of TSPO. Comparisons between two groups were performed with a two-sided unpaired t-test.

## Results:
TSPO-KO mice exhibited more severe pulmonary inflammation in response to LPS-induced ALI. TSPO deficiency resulted in enhanced activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, promoting more proinflammatory cytokine production of macrophages in LPS-injured lung tissue, including interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-18, and macrophage inflammatory protein (MIP)-2. Mitochondria in TSPO-KO macrophages tended to depolarize in response to cellular stress. The increased production of mitochondrial damage-associated molecular pattern led to enhanced mitochondrial membrane depolarization and pyroptosis in TSPO-KO cells.

## Conclusion:
TSPO may be the key regulator of cellular pyroptosis, and it plays a vital protective role in ARDS occurrence and development.

Keywords: Acute respiratory distress syndrome, Acute lung injury, 18-kDa translocator protein, Macrophage, NLRP3 inflammasome, Inflammation

## Introduction
Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is an acute respiratory disease characterized by diffuse alveolar infiltration.[ , , ] The inflammatory reaction leads to damage to the pulmonary capillary endothelium and alveolar epithelium, resulting in an increase in alveolar capillary permeability, which clinically manifests as refractory hypoxemia and progressive respiratory distress.[ ] An epidemiological study of 459 intensive care units (ICUs) from 50 countries showed that patients with ARDS accounted for 10.4% of ICU patients, and 35–46% of patients died during hospitalization.[ ] In recent years, there is a substantial increase in people’s attention to ARDS, and the related obstacles and challenges brought by this syndrome have become increasingly prominent.
There is mounting evidence showing that pyroptosis plays a key role in ALI or ARDS caused by bacteria or viruses. Pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) produced by Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and influenza A virus can activate the pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome.[ , , ] Oligomeric NLRP3 and the proteins in the proinflammatory caspase subfamily can interact with the adaptor protein apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase recruitment domain (ASC) that contains pyrin domain (PYD) and caspase recruitment domain (CARD).[ ] The key mechanism of pyroptosis involves caspase-mediated cleavage events. N-terminus of Gasdermin D (GSDMD-N) promotes the formation of pores, and then mature interleukin (IL)-1β is secreted to the outside of cells through an unconventional protein secretion pathway, which strongly promotes vasodilation and immune cell extravasation, producing an IL-17-producing T helper (Th17) immune response and promoting interferon (IFN)-γ production by natural killer (NK) and type 1 T helper (Th1) cells.[ ] Recently, it was found that NLRP3-knockout (KO) mice showed mild lung symptoms and lung epithelial injury upon lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced ALI.[ ] Lack of GSDMD or inhibition of pyroptosis by disulfiram can significantly alleviate LPS-induced inflammation in an ARDS model.[ ]
The translocator protein (TSPO) located in the outer membrane of mitochondria was originally characterized as a peripheral benzodiazepine receptor.[ ] It is an evolutionarily conserved 18-kDa transmembrane protein that participates in a variety of cell biological processes. TSPO was initially thought to play a vital role in regulating mitochondrial cholesterol transport to facilitate the production of steroid hormones. However, TSPO-KO mice showed a normal survival rate, normal fertility, and a normal ability to produce steroid hormones, strongly refuting the previously proposed function of TSPO in regulating cholesterol transport and steroid biosynthesis.[ ] Interestingly, recent studies have shown that TSPO plays an important role in the occurrence and development of inflammatory diseases. The myocardial TSPO signal of model mice increased one week after myocardial infarction.[ ] This early inflammation-related signal can predict the subsequent functional outcome. Under induction by the dextran sodium sulfate (DSS), the colon of TSPO-KO mice showed more severe inflammatory lesions, and many proinflammatory M1 macrophages were recruited.[ ] Positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) with TSPO as the target showed lung inflammation in ALI rats.[ ] The expression level of TSPO in the lungs gradually increased with the aggravation of inflammatory injury. In addition, our previous study showed that TSPO deficiency caused mitochondrial metabolic function imbalance, significantly reduced oxidative phosphorylation, and a shift in cell metabolism from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis.[ ]
In this study, we aimed to explore the biological role of TSPO in the pathogenesis of ARDS through the TSPO-KO and LPS-induced ALI/ARDS mouse model. By studying the effect of TSPO on the activation of inflammasome and the release of a large amount of inflammatory factors in macrophages during ALI, we can further clarify the molecular mechanism by which TSPO regulates cell pyroptosis. In this study, the interactions among mitochondrial metabolism (TSPO protein), inflammasome activation, and cell pyroptosis are expected to be elucidated, enabling people to further understand the molecular basis of the occurrence and development of inflammatory diseases.

## Methods

## Experimental animals
All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (IBMS, CAMS) (No. ACUC-A01-2019-013). The TSPO-KO mice were set up in our laboratory on a C57BL/6J background, and the TSPO heterozygous KO mice were described previously.[ ] The wild type and TSPO-KO mice were obtained from the littermates of het/het (TSPO+/−) breeding and co-housed throughout the whole experiment. All mice were maintained in specific pathogen-free conditions at the Experimental Animal Center of IBMS-CAMS. TSPO-KO mice and wild-type (WT) littermates aged 8–10 weeks were selected as experimental mice and for tissue isolation after the mice were anesthetized by avertin.

## LPS-induced model of ALI
After the intraperitoneal injection of avertin (0.1 mL/10 g), the age- and sex-matched mice received an intratracheal instillation of 50 μL of LPS (Escherichia coli 055:B5, 1 mg/kg, Sigma, Saint Louis, Missouri, USA) or the same volume of phosphate buffered saline (PBS). The same volume of air was injected, and the mice were kept vertical for 5 min to ensure the uniform distribution of drugs in the lungs. The mice were sacrificed 48 h after LPS/PBS administration.

## Flow cytometry
For ex vivo analysis of cell populations in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) at 48 h post-LPS administration, the obtained BALF cells were washed in 1× PBS and passed through 40-μm filters to achieve single-cell suspensions. The cells were blocked in 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and then stained with antibodies against the surface proteins Siglec-F (APC/Cyanine7, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), cluster of differentiation (CD) 64 (PE, BioLegend), CD11b (PerCP-Cy5.5, BD Biosciences, Bergen, New Jersey, USA), and Ly6G (APC, BioLegend) in the dark at 4°C for 30 min. Flow cytometry was performed on CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), and Flow-Jo software (https://www.flowjo.com) was used for post-acquisition analysis.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
BALF samples were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant fractions were collected and stored at –80°C. The levels of the inflammatory proteins IFN-γ, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, MIP-2, IL-1β, and IL-18 in the supernatants were measured by ELISA (CUSABIO, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. BALF protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

## Cell culture
Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were differentiated from bone marrow cells isolated by washing the mouse femur marrow cavity with Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM). The cells were then centrifuged for 5 min at 300 × g and resuspended in BMDM culture medium (DMEM, 20% fetal bovine serum [FBS] , 20 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor [M-CSF] [Novoprotein, Jiangsu, China]). Cells were fed with an additional 10 mL of fresh BMDM culture medium every two days. BMDMs were harvested on day 6 and then plated into 24-well plates at 2.5 × 105 cells/well. To analyze NLRP3 inflammasome activation, cells were stimulated with 100 ng/mL LPS (Sigma) for 3 h followed by 5 mmol/L adenosine triphosphate (ATP) (MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA) for 2 h.

## Western blotting analysis
Protein was extracted from cells or lung tissues using radio immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (0.79% Tris, 0.9% NaCl, 1% sodium deoxycholate, 1% Triton X-100, 1 mmol/L ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid [EDTA], 0.1% or 1.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate [SDS], pH 8.0) with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked for 2 h at room temperature (RT) in tris buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) with 5% skim milk. The blots were incubated overnight at 4°C with antibodies against the following proteins: NLRP3 (AdipoGen, San Diego, CA, USA), ASC (AdipoGen), Caspase-1 (AdipoGen), Caspase-11 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Caspase-3 (CST, Boston, Massachusetts, USA), GSDMD (Abcam), Gasdermin E (GSDME) (Abcam), TSPO (Abcam), IL-1β (CST), and IL-18 (Abcam). After the blots were incubated with anti-mouse or anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China) at room temperature (RT) for 1 h, the signal was developed with SuperSignal West Pico Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) and a CliNX ChemiScope 3400 (CliNX Science Instruments Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Experiments were repeated for three times.

## JC-10 mitochondrial membrane potential assay
JC-10 fluorescence probe was used to measure mitochondrial membrane depolarization in TSPO-KO macrophages. The probe exists as red aggregates in the healthy mitochondrial matrix. When mitochondrial membrane potential loss and cell damage occur, it gradually changes into a green monomer form.[ ] JC-10 storage solution (Abcam) was added to 5 mL of buffer solution, and the solution was fully mixed. After LPS/ATP or carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) (10 μmol/L, MCE) treatment, the prepared working solution was added to the cells, and the cells were incubated in the dark at 37°C for 30 min. Images were visualized using a Zeiss LSM 780 confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), and the average fluorescence intensity was calculated.

## Reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay
To detect mitochondrial superoxide, MitoSOX (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used. MitoSOX Red storage solution (5 mmol/L) was added to DMEM at a ratio of 1:1000 to a final concentration of 5 μmol/L. After LPS/ATP treatment, the prepared MitoSOX Red working solution and Hoechst 33342 staining solution (Invitrogen, 1:2000) were added to the cells, and the cells were incubated in the dark at 37°C for 10 min. Images were visualized, and the mean fluorescence intensity was calculated using a Zeiss LSM 780 confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss).

## Living cell fluorescence labeling assay
To observe the morphology of pyroptotic cells, Calcein AM (Invitrogen) storage solution was added to DMEM at a ratio of 1:2000 to a final concentration of 2 μmol/L. The prepared Calcein AM working solution and Hoechst 33342 staining solution were added to the cells, and the cells were incubated in the dark at 37°C for 10 min. Images were taken on a Leica STELLARIS 5 confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) equipped with a 63× oil immersion objective. Quantification of pyroptotic cells was conducted manually.

## Histological assessment
Forty-eight hours after LPS inoculation, the lungs of mice were inflated, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and embedded with paraffin. Sections (4 mm) were sliced for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The slides were stained with hematoxylin for 10 min and moved into the differentiation solution (75% ethanol with 0.5% hydrochloric acid) for 10 s to remove the non-specifically bound dye. Then, the slides were further washed with running water. After the nuclei returned to a blue color, the slides were dyed with eosin for 2 min. Images of lung sections were taken with a Leica DM6B upright microscope (Leica). Lung histopathological assessment was performed by averaging the scores from one blinded and one non-blinded scorer according to the scoring system of the American Thoracic Society.[ ] The lung injury score varies from 0 to 2 where 0 is no injury and 2 is the maximal injury. Lung histopathological assessment was performed by averaging the score from one blinded and one non-blinded scorer.

## Statistical analysis
All results are expressed as the mean ± standard error of mean (SEM). Comparisons between two groups were performed with a two-sided unpaired t-test. All calculations of significance were determined using GraphPad Prism 8 software (https://www.graphpad.com/scientific-software/prism/), and the results are expressed as P-values. The threshold for significance was determined based on a P-value <0.05.

## Results

## TSPO-KO mice exhibited more severe pulmonary inflammation in response to LPS-induced ALI
To investigate the roles of TSPO in ALI, TSPO-KO mice and their WT littermates were subjected to ALI induction via intratracheal instillation of LPS. We hypothesized that systemic TSPO deficiency would increase pulmonary inflammation. Therefore, to minimize the potential for false-negative results associated with a saturated biologic response to LPS, we conducted an initial dose–response experiment in TSPO-WT mice to determine the appropriate dosage and duration of LPS that would induce inflammation but not saturate the total number of infiltrating cells in the alveolar air space [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B977].
We observed lung tissue damage in mice 48 h or 72 h after administering three LPS doses of 1 mg/kg, 3 mg/kg, or 5 mg/kg. Histological examination demonstrated that LPS at 3 mg/kg and 5 mg/kg caused severe lung consolidation [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B977]. The air in the alveolar cavity decreased or disappeared, and a large amount of exudates were filled in it, especially at 72 h, which may lead to no distinct difference between TSPO-KO and WT mice. No dense area appeared in the lung tissue in response to 1 mg/kg LPS stimulation. However, more obvious interstitial edema, thickening of alveolar walls, alveolar collapse, and structural destruction were shown in lung tissue in the TSPO-KO mice than in WT at 48 h (t = 7.975, P = 0.0013) [Figure  A, B], whereas TSPO-KO mice did not exhibit significant pathological changes after administration of PBS. We also observed a significant increase in BALF protein content in the lungs of TSPO-KO mice treated with LPS compared with that of LPS-induced WT mice (t = 2.706, P = 0.0170) [Figure  C], indicating that pulmonary microvascular leakage was more serious in the KO mice.
 A dosage of 1 mg/kg LPS induced a significant increase in the total number of white blood cells (WBC) in the BALF of TSPO-KO mice 48 h after administration (t = 4.029, P = 0.0038) [Figure  A, B]. The proportion of alveolar macrophages in the BALF of TSPO-KO mice decreased (t = 3.364, P = 0.0099) [Figure  C, D], while the proportion and count of neutrophils were significantly elevated (t = 3.965, P = 0.0041; t = 3.111, P = 0.0144) [Figure  E, F], consistent with the proinflammatory phenotype with TSPO deficiency.
 

## TSPO deficiency leads to more proinflammatory cytokine accumulation in BALF
Inflammatory stimulation triggers macrophages activation, leading to robust cytokine release called the occurrence of cytokine storm. At the late stage after LPS-induced injury, the levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, TNF-α, MIP-2, IL-1β, IL-18, and IFN-γ, were measured in the BALF [Figure  ]. Results showed that the expression of IFN-γ, which is considered the main effector cytokine in many cytokine storm diseases,[ ] was significantly upregulated after LPS exposure [Figure  A], while TNF-α levels were decreased [Figure  B]. However, there were no significant differences in IFN-γ and TNF-α levels between LPS-induced TSPO-WT and TSPO-KO mice, indicating that TSPO deficiency does not affect the release of non-inflammasome-related cytokines. The level of MIP-2, a neutrophil chemokine, was remarkably higher in LPS-induced TSPO-KO mice than that in the WT mice (t = 4.018, P = 0.0017) [Figure  D]. These results are consistent with the outcomes of flow cytometry [Figure  E, F]. Interestingly, we observed a marked increase in IL-1β and IL-18 levels in LPS-induced TSPO-KO mice (t = 3.146, P = 0.0084; t = 3.336, P = 0.0059) [Figure  E, F], indicating the activation of inflammasomes triggered by Caspase-1 pathway. No difference in IL-6 levels was observed at 48 h [Figure  C]. In conclusion, TSPO-KO mice exhibited a stronger inflammatory cytokine storm triggered by the inflammasome activation in the lungs, which prompted us to determine whether TSPO deficiency affected the regulation of inflammasome-related pathways.
 

## TSPO deficiency resulted in enhanced activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway in LPS-injured lung tissue
The inflammasome is a large cytoplasmic multiprotein complex that serves as a pattern recognition receptor (PRR) that senses infection and endogenous risk factors and is an important part of the host innate immune defense.[ ] The NLRP3 inflammasome pathway includes canonical inflammasomes that mediate Caspase-1 activation and non-canonical inflammasomes that promote Caspase-11/4/5 activation.[ ] The excessive activation of inflammasomes causes a strong inflammatory reaction and triggers inflammatory cell death, that is, pyroptosis, which leads to immune damage in the body. Thus, we performed Western blotting to examine inflammasome-related proteins in the lung tissue of mice at 48 h post-LPS exposure.
First, it was noted that the expression of TSPO was rapidly upregulated in response to inflammatory stimulation (t = 5.685, P = 0.0047) [Figure  ]. Second, it was found that compared with LPS-induced TSPO-WT mice, LPS-induced TSPO-KO mice exhibited a significant increase in activated NLRP3 inflammasomes in the lung tissue (t = 4.682, P = 0.0094) [Figure  ]. The levels of the adaptor protein ASC and inflammatory caspase protein (Caspase-1/11) were also markedly increased [Figure  ]. Activated caspase proteins can directly cleave GSDMD to trigger pyroptosis[ ]; and can also cleave IL-1β and IL-18 precursors to convert them into mature functional proteins. The high level of caspase protein cleavage in LPS-induced TSPO-KO mice corresponds to the high level of GSDMD cleavage (t = 10.09, P = 0.0005), and accordingly, mature IL-1β and IL-18 levels also increased significantly (t = 4.274, P = 0.0129; t = 5.55, P = 0.0052) [Figure  ]. Since the pro-IL-18 is constitutively expressed,[ ] no difference in its expression was observed in unstimulated lung tissue. Third, GSDME was recently identified as a new pore-forming molecule that is activated after Caspase-3-mediated cleavage and regulates the pyroptosis process in primary dying cells without inducing apoptosis.[ , ] Unexpectedly, GSDME was also highly expressed in LPS-induced TSPO-KO lung tissue (t = 10.89, P = 0.0004; t = 6.89, P = 0.0023) [Figure  ]. Moreover, no differences were observed in the expression of pyroptotic cell death mediators or inflammasome components in the lung tissue of WT and TSPO-KO mice when they were treated with PBS.
 

## TSPO deficiency does not promote the release of IL-1β from neutrophils
Although studies have shown that GSDMD is specifically cleaved by Caspase-11 during LPS-induced NETosis,[ ] we did not observe the cleavage of gasdermin protein in recruited neutrophils. The NLRP3-Caspase-1-GSDMD pathway was not activated, and there was no obvious difference between TSPO-KO and WT mice [Supplementary Figure 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/B977]. Interestingly, the expression level of the Caspase-3-GSDME axis was inhibited, and IL-1β expression was significantly reduced in TSPO-KO neutrophils (t = 4.773, P = 0.0088). In short, these data suggest that pyroptosis is not the main death procedure for neutrophils.
The above results collectively indicate that TSPO deficiency may promote the pyroptosis of LPS-activated alveolar macrophages, followed by the release of a great quantity of cytokines, leading to more severe ALI in TSPO-KO mice. Therefore, we focused our attention on pulmonary macrophages.

## Mitochondria in TSPO-KO macrophages are prone to depolarization in response to cellular stress
Mitochondrial dysfunction is a well-known trigger of programmed cell death pathways.[ ] Our previous research revealed that the loss of TSPO caused mitochondrial network disruption and fragmentation.[ ] Mitochondrial fragmentation leads to changes in mitochondrial membrane potential.[ ] To determine how the TSPO alters mitochondrial dynamics, we initially qualitatively assessed the mitochondrial network in BMDMs from WT and TSPO-KO mice. The signal of JC-10 aggregates was lower in resting TSPO-KO BMDMs compared with that in TSPO-WT BMDMs (t = 5.319, P = 0.0018), as well as in LPS-induced TSPO-KO compared to LPS-induced TSPO-WT BMDMs (t = 6.923, P = 0.0004) [Figure  A, B], indicating that TSPO deficiency caused enhanced depolarization of the mitochondrial membrane of BMDMs.
 

## Inflammasome activation triggers additional mitochondrial stress in TSPO-KO macrophages
To define how mitochondrial homeostasis is altered by inflammasome activation in TSPO-KO BMDMs, we measured mitochondrial membrane potential at 2 h post-LPS/ATP treatment by immunofluorescence confocal microscopy. The activation of NLRP3 inflammasome further reduced the JC-10 fluorescence intensity of WT and TSPO-KO BMDMs, which was indicative of NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent mitochondrial depolarization. Furthermore, the stimulated TSPO-KO macrophages exhibited more severe mitochondrial membrane potential damage than WT (t = 3.306, P = 0.0079) [Figure  A,B].
Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) is a mitochondrial damage-associated molecular pattern (mtDAMP) with connections to both inflammasome activation and cell death.[ ] We used the mitochondrial-targeted superoxide indicator MitoSOX to define the repertoire of free radicals in TSPO-KO BMDMs during NLRP3 inflammasome activation. The signal was significantly higher in TSPO-KO macrophages after LPS/ATP treatment (t = 7.214, P <0.0001) [Figure  C, D], indicating enhanced mitochondrial depolarization accompanied by the accumulation of mtROS in the cytoplasm.
Based on the above results, it can be concluded that the production of mtDAMPs in cells increases due to potential mitochondrial defects, resulting in additional mitochondrial stress in TSPO-KO cells.

## TSPO-KO promotes the NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated cell death of macrophages
The combination of LPS and toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) provides the first signal, triggering nuclear transcription factor-kappa B (NF-κB)-mediated upregulation of NLRP3 expression.[ ] At this moment, the morphology of WT and TSPO-KO macrophages remained intact [Figure  A], even though their mitochondrial membrane potential had a marked decline. The ATP acts on macrophages, leading to the influx of Ca2+ and efflux of K+ through P2X7 channels, and the assembly of the inflammasome occurs.[ ] Under a light microscope, the dying cells showed obvious swelling, and the cytoplasm displayed characteristic vacuolation. The cells labeled by Calcein AM showed chromatin pyknosis, membrane integrity destruction, and endoplasmic reticulum expansion [Figure  A, B]. Compared with those in the control group, more TSPO-KO BMDMs showed pyroptotic characteristics (t = 8.122, P <0.0001) [Figure  A, B], suggesting that TSPO deficiency accelerated pyroptosis. Taken together, these outcomes indicate that TSPO is involved in mitochondrial quality control and has a unique cytoprotective effect in inflammatory injury.
 

## Discussion
TSPO was originally identified as the peripheral receptor of benzodiazepines, which is different from the benzodiazepine receptor in the central nervous system, especially in the cerebral cortex. Its main coding sequence has been highly conserved throughout evolution from bacteria to mammals and is predicted to form a hydrophobic protein with five transmembrane domains, which is located in the outer mitochondrial membrane.[ ] Although TSPO has been widely detected in many organs, its expression in the steroid hormone-producing cells of the adrenal gland, testis, and ovary seems to be particularly high.[ ] A previous study showed that the TSPO ligands PK11195 and Ro5-4864 could induce the production of steroid hormones in testicular stromal cells and adrenocortical tumor cells.[ ] Therefore, TSPO was once considered a channel that mediates cholesterol transport from the outside of mitochondria to the inner membrane, which is a rate-limiting step of steroid synthesis. However, an increasing number of studies on the function of TSPO have changed people’s view on its involvement in steroid production. A TSPO conditional KO mouse model showed that the lack of TSPO in testicular interstitial cells did not affect testosterone production in vivo.[ ] A research group proved in subsequent studies that the systemic KO of TSPO also did not affect the survival, fertility, or ability to produce steroid hormones of mice.[ ]
The refutation of the function of TSPO in regulating cholesterol transport and steroid biosynthesis was unexpected. Although the structure, biochemistry, and pharmacology of TSPO have been described in detail, its exact physiological and pathological functions are still unclear. At this stage, research on the basic function of TSPO based on structure–function relationships involves a variety of cellular biological processes, including endoplasmic reticulum-related protein degradation, autophagy, mitochondrial respiration, angiogenesis, and tumorigenesis. TSPO can induce the misfolding of the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-1 envelope and promote its degradation through the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-associated protein degradation (ERAD) pathway.[ ] In terms of mitochondrial homeostasis and quality control, an imbalance in the ratio of TSPO to voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC) 1 leads to the excessive production of ROS and hinders the recruitment of p62 and the mitochondrial relocation of light chain 3 (LC3), thus inhibiting phosphatase and tensin homolog deleted on chromosome 10-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1)-Parkinson disease protein 2 (PARK2)-mediated protein ubiquitination and mitochondrial autophagy and leading to dysfunctional mitochondrion aggregation in cells.[ ] Our previous research showed that TSPO KO caused mitochondrial metabolic function imbalance, significantly reduced oxidative phosphorylation, and a shift in cell metabolism from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis, resulting in increased angiogenesis and tumor growth.[ ]
The high expression of TSPO on macrophages and its level being further upregulated in response to inflammatory stimuli have gradually attracted attention to other inflammatory diseases. One week after myocardial infarction, CD68+ cells in the infarcted area of model mice were activated, and the myocardial TSPO signal was increased. This early inflammation-related signal can predict subsequent adverse clinical outcomes.[ , ] TSPO triggers microglia to produce ROS through the NADPH oxidase 1 (NOX1) axis, and the cytosolic level of Ca2+ increases, leading to retinal degeneration in patients. The combination of the synthetic ligand XBD173 and TSPO can limit the inflammatory reaction and prevent ROS damage, thus inhibiting phagocytic activation and pathological angiogenesis.[ ] Our previous research showed that TSPO plays a crucial protective role in inflammatory bowel disease occurrence and development. Cell death and ROS accumulation in TSPO-KO macrophages are GSDMD dependent.[ ] The combination of TSPO-specific ligands and PET imaging has been widely used to map the macrophage-driven inflammation after infection at the organ level. In the lungs of rhesus macaques infected by the Ebola virus, the combination of DPA-714 and TSPO gradually decreased, indicating the depletion of alveolar macrophages. The increase in bone marrow distribution volumes indicates that the depletion of systemic immune cells stimulates hematopoietic activation, which may be related to prognosis.[ ]
In this study, we used an LPS-induced ALI/ARDS mouse model to investigate the role of TSPO in inflammatory diseases. Normal lung physiologically comprises a single layer of alveolar epithelial cells passing through the distal alveolar capillaries, which facilitates rapid gas exchange. In the process of acute inflammation, the endothelial lining ruptures, leading to increased alveolar-capillary permeability and decreased lung compliance. Microbial components are recognized by TLRs on alveolar macrophages through their PRRs, resulting in a cytokine storm entering the air space, inevitably exacerbating inflammatory damage. TSPO-KO mice showed more severe injury than WT mice. The concentration of total protein in BALF was significantly increased in TSPO-KO mice, which reflected the accumulation of extravascular protein, mainly due to the increase in alveolar capillary permeability. The severity of ALI is largely affected by the migration of neutrophils into the lungs in response to activated alveolar macrophages.[ ] Our quantitative analysis of neutrophils in BALF also confirmed this statement; that is, the number of neutrophils in the alveolar space of TSPO-KO mice with more severe histopathology was significantly higher. Western blotting experiments confirmed that the expression of TSPO was rapidly upregulated in response to inflammatory stimulation in lung tissue. However, the lack of TSPO led to significant activation of NLRP3 inflammasomes and increased cell death.
GSDME is expressed at a high level in bronchial epithelial cells (http://www.Biogps.org), we believe that the upregulation of GSDME-N may be the result of LPS stimulation of bronchial epithelium. At the cellular level, due to potential mitochondrial defects, the production of mtDAMPs in TSPO-KO macrophages increased, resulting in additional mitochondrial stress in the cells and accelerating inflammatory cell death. GSDMD forms pores in mitochondria, amplifying cell death signals via the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) or cytochrome c,[ , ] or inhibiting the production of type I IFN and its negative regulatory effect on cell death by limiting the expression of the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase/stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS/STING).[ ] TSPO may play a “neutralizing” role in it, by binding to the gasdermin protein targeting the mitochondrial membrane, thereby maintaining mitochondrial integrity and homeostasis of the intracellular environment. In brief, our data support the hypothesis in which TSPO balances the distribution of GSDMD between the mitochondrial membrane and cytoplasmic membrane to regulate cell death.
There are also some deficiencies in the study that needs further modification and improvement in the future. Further investigation is needed to determine whether there is an interaction between TSPO and gasdermin protein in macrophages to achieve their balance and whether restoring TSPO expression after TSPO-KO can inhibit the cleavage of gasdermin protein and reverse cell death. However, our previous research has shown that when TSPO-WT macrophages are transfected with exogenous TSPO protein, the NLRP3 inflammasome in cytoplasm tends to increase, and the expression level of GSDMD-N is significantly upregulated, indicating that the role of TSPO is double-edged. Further study may also need to explore whether inhibitors of GSDMD pore formation or siRNA knockdown of GSDMD can restore TSPO-KO macrophages to WT levels under inflammatory stimulation, to further elucidate the mechanism of TSPO in the activation of inflammasomes and the signaling pathway of cell pyroptosis. Besides, further study is needed to clarify the reason for the accumulation of mtROS in TSPO-KO BMDMs, and to link defects in mitochondrial homeostasis with cell death by depleting mtROS.
A growing number of studies support the key role of inflammasome components in promoting ARDS pathogenesis.[ , , ] Our work supports the unique cytoprotective effect of TSPO after NLRP3 inflammasome activation and provides new clues to further clarify inflammatory disease pathogenesis. Since TSPO can serve as a new drug target, our research provides a reliable theoretical basis for drug development for severe inflammatory diseases such as ARDS.
