## Introduction
Nanoplastics (NPs) cause inflammation and toxicity because they can accumulate in various body organs after ingestion, inhalation, and exposure [ ]. On the other hand, there are conflicting results regarding the effects of NPs as a risk factor for human health in cells and laboratory animals [ , ]. In many studies, some inflammation or toxicity was detected in several human cells after treatment with polystyrene (PS), polyethylene (PE), or polypropylene (PP)-NPs of various sizes, but the treatment concentrations were different [ – ]. In addition, these responses were accompanied by various physiological responses, such as apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, histamine release, and oxidative stress [ – , ]. Only a few studies reported that some types of NPs, including polyethylene terephthalate (PET), PS, and carboxy-modified PS, cannot induce significant cytotoxicity in specific human cells, except at high concentrations [ – ]. Furthermore, conflicting results on the toxicity of NPs in various mammalian cell lines have been detected in laboratory animals. Numerous studies have shown that NPs cause significant toxicity or inflammation in various tissues of animals [ – ]. In addition, significant alterations in the various physiological responses, including lipid metabolism, microbiota dysbiosis, and intergeneration effects, were induced in the specific organs of animals after a treatment with PS-NPs of various sizes [ , – ]. Nevertheless, some PS particles have no significant effects on the toxicity, inflammation, and behavioural consequences in animals [ , ].
Most animal studies examined the mechanisms for NPs toxicity in orally administered animals. Other administration routes were used restrictively. Among these other routes, inhalation has attracted considerable attention because the concentration of NPs in the air has increased rapidly [ ]. Significant changes in the various physiological responses were detected in rats and mice that inhaled NPs of several sizes for various periods. Fourteen days of PS-NPs inhalation caused significant changes in the inflammatory proteins, pulmonary function, and fibrosis-related markers in SD rats [ ]. In addition, the behavioural changes accompanied by inflammation and acetylcholine esterase (AChE) inhibition were detected in mice that inhaled PS-NPs, while ventilator dysfunction, fibrotic pathological changes, cytoskeleton rearrangement, and an increase in inflammatory cells were induced in C57BL/6 mice after the inhalation of tire-wear NPs [ , ]. Similar responses on the alveolar epithelial injury, inflammatory response, and fibrosis development were induced in mice and rats after the intratracheal administration of PS-NPs [ , ]. On the other hand, no study has investigated the profile of the global genes during inflammation and fibrosis induced by PS-NPs inhalation in the lungs of the ICR mice.
This study characterised the changes in global gene expression in the lungs of ICR mice in response to inflammation and fibrosis induced by the inhalation of PS-NPs. The present study revealed several gene functional groups and individual genes that can be considered biomarkers in response to NPs inhalation in the lungs of ICR mice.

## Materials and methods

## Preparation and characterisation of PS-NPs
PS-NPs with a mean particle size of 0.4–0.6 μm and density of 1.04–1.06 g/cm3 were obtained in an aqueous suspension with a concentration of 25 mg/mL from Sigma–Aldrich Co. (St. Louis, MO, USA). Their morphology and size were analysed by SEM/EDX spectroscopy (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), and the Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments Inc., Malvern, UK). They had a circular shape with a regular size, specific number-distribution size (593.83 ± 7.53 d.nm), and zeta potential (− 35.98 ± 0.26 mV), as described in a previous study[ ] (Fig.  A and B). This zeta potential is normally sufficient to promote the colloidal stability of NPs. On the other hand, the initial concentration of commercial NPs (2.5%, 25 mg/mL) was high as being suspension type exhibiting aggregation-like behaviour. Before animal inhalation, the NPs suspensions were dispersed thoroughly by sonication to prevent aggregation and diluted sequentially with water to the appropriate concentration. 

## Experimental design of the animal study
The animal protocol to characterise the gene profile was reviewed and approved by the Pusan National University-Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (PNU-IACUC) based on the ethical procedures for scientific care (Approval Number PNU-2020-2654). All ICR mice (male) were maintained at the Pusan National University-Laboratory Animal Resources Center, accredited by the Korea Food and Drug Administration (KFDA) (Accredited Unit Number-000231), and The Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care (AAALAC) International (Accredited Unit Number; 001525). They were provided access to a standard irradiated chow diet (Samtako BioKorea Inc., Osan, Korea) and water ad libitum. Throughout the experiment, mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) state under a strict light cycle (on at 08:00 h; off at 20:00 h) at 23 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 10% relative humidity.
Briefly, 7-week-old ICR mice (n = 24) were assigned to a 1 × PBS inhaled group (Vehicle, n = 6) or PS-NPs inhaled group (NP, n = 18) after quarantine for 1 week (Fig.  B). The NPs inhaled groups were divided into a low concentration NPs inhaled group (LoNP, n = 6, 4 μg/mL, 1.7 ng/cm3 of the external concentration), medium concentration NPs inhaled group (MiNP, n = 6, 8 μg/mL, 3.4 ng/cm3 of the external concentration), and high concentration NPs inhaled group (HiNP, n = 6, 16 μg/mL, 6.8 ng/cm3 of the external concentration). All mice of each NPs inhaled group in a single chamber (25 × 30 × 25 cm) inhaled 8 mL of a NPs solution at a 0.25 mL/min rate using a non-heating compressor nebuliser (NE-C28, MRON DALIAN Co. LTD, Dalian, China) every day. In contrast, the Vehicle-inhaled group received the same volume of 1 × PBS solution for the same time (Fig.  A). During inhalation for 2 weeks, the physiological condition of all mice in each group was monitored regularly at 10 a.m. every day. There were no occurrences of severely ill or dead animals. After the final inhalation, all mice were euthanized with CO2 gas, after which the lung, liver, kidney, intestine and whole blood samples were acquired and stored at − 70 °C in Eppendorf tubes until assay. 

## Measurement of body weight, organ weight, food intake, and water consumption
Throughout the experimental duration, body and food weight, and drinking water volume were measured daily in the Vehicle, LoNP, MiNP, and HiNP-inhaled groups at 10:00 a.m., using an electrical balance (Mettler Toledo, Columbus, OH, USA) and a measuring cylinder, respectively. All measurements were taken twice to ensure accuracy, and the average food intake and water consumption were calculated using the measured data. In addition, the weight of the four organs, including the liver, kidney, intestine, and lung, was measured using the method used to measure the body weight after the euthanasia of animals.

## Biodistribution of NPs in the lungs of mice
Whole lung tissue samples of each mouse were homogenised with PolytronVR Homogenizer PT 3100 D (KINEMATICA, Luzern, Switzerland) and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm (1411×g) for 5 min (C1015 micro prime centrifuge; Centurion Scientific Ltd, Stoughton, Chichester, UK). The resulting supernatant was discarded, and the remaining pellet was digested in 2 mL of piranha solution (H2SO4:H2O2 = 7:3) for 4 h at room temperature. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the pelleted beads were resuspended in 2 mL of dH2O. The fluorescence of NPs was measured using a fluorescence reader (Spectramax iD5, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA). ## Whole blood analysis and serum biochemistry
After the final inhalation of NPs, all mice were fasted for 8 h, and anaesthesia was induced by intramuscular injection of Alfaxan (JUROX Pty Limited, Rutherford, Australia, 0.03–0.05 mL/10 g body weight intramuscular (IM)), and blood was then collected from the abdominal veins using a 1 mL syringe attached to a needle (26 SWG). Blood analysis and serum biochemistry were performed on all collected samples. Whole blood was placed in plain-capped bottles containing ethylenediaminetetraacetate (EDTA). The component levels in blood were analysed using an automated cell counter (Beckman–Coulter Inc., Miami, FL, USA) with the standard calibration, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The levels of white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), haemoglobin (HGB), haematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration (MCHC), platelets (PLT), and mean platelet volume (MPV) were measured in duplicate for each sample.
The serum was obtained for biochemical analysis by centrifuging the whole blood at 1,500 × g for 15 min. The serum biochemical components, including alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and low-density lipoprotein (LDH), were assayed using an Automatic Serum Analyzer (Hitachi 747; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). All assays were measured in duplicate using fresh serum.

## Enumeration of immune cells in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)
After anesthetising the ICR mice with an intramuscular injection of Alfaxan (JUROX Pty Limited), the mouse lungs were lavaged three times with cold 1 × PBS (yield: 80%, total volume = 0.8 mL). After centrifugation of BALF at 2,000 ×g or 5 min at 4 °C, the pellets were used for cell analysis. The total cells of the BALF pellet were attached to a slide glass (Marienfeld, Lauda-Königshofen, Germany) using a cytospin (5 min, 500 rpm, Hanil Electric, Wonju, Korea), and fixed in methanol for 5 min. These slides were stained with Diff-Quik Solutions 1 and 2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 1 min and rinsed three times. The slides were covered, after which the number of total cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and lymphocytes were counted in 1 mm2 under optical microscopy at 400 × magnification.

## Histopathological analysis
Lung tissues collected from the Vehicle, LoNP, MiNP, and HiNP-inhaled groups were fixed in 10% formalin for 48 h. The tissue samples were then embedded in paraffin wax, which was cut into 4 μm thick sections and stained with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Sigma-Aldrich Co.). The scoring criteria for histopathological changes were applied by modifying the method used in the previous study [ ]. These changes included five different items: (a) alveolar congestion, (b) haemorrhage, (c) desquamation of airway epithelial cells, (d) infiltration or aggregation of neutrophils in airspace or vessel wall, (e) thickness of the alveolar wall. Also, each item was scored on a 5-point scale: 0 = minimal damage, 1 = mild damage, 2 = moderate damage, 3 = severe damage, and 4 = maximal damage. The pulmonary parenchymal area (PA), average vessel wall thickness, and the number of alveolar septal (NS) in the lung sections were analysed by Image J program 1.52a (NIH, Bethesda, ML, USA), as described in a previous study [ ].
Mucin staining analysis was achieved by fixing the lung tissue collected from the mice of all subset groups in 10% formalin for 48 h, embedding the samples in paraffin wax, and sectioning them into 4 μm thick slices, which were then deparaffinised with xylene and rehydrated. The mounted tissue sections were rinsed with distilled water and stained with an Alcian blue-periodic acid Schiff solution (IHC World, Woodstock, USA), after which the distribution of goblet cells in stained lung sections was observed by optical microscopy.

## Western blotting analysis
The Pro-Prep Protein Extraction Solution (Intron Biotechnology Inc., Seongnam, Korea) was used to prepare the total proteins from the lung tissue of the Vehicle and LoNP, MiNP, and HiNP-inhaled groups, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Protein homogenates were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm at 4 °C for 5 min, after which the total protein concentrations were determined using a SMARTTM Bicinchoninic Acid Protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). Approximately 30 µg of the proteins were subjected to 4–20% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) for 2 h, and the resolved proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes for 2 h at 40 V. The membranes were then probed with the following primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: anti-β-catenin (Cell Signaling Technology Inc., Cambridge, MA, USA; #9562S), anti-TGF-β1 (Santa Cruz BioTechnology Inc., TX Dallas, USA; #sc-130348), anti-smad2/3 (Cell Signaling Technology Inc.; #5678S), anti-p-smad2/3 (Cell Signaling Technology Inc.,; #8828), or anti-β-actin (Sigma–Aldrich Co.). The membranes were then washed with a washing buffer (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 2 mM KH2PO4, and 0.05% Tween 20), followed by incubation with 1:1,000 diluted horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Zymed Laboratories, South San Francisco, CA, USA) for 2 h at room temperature. The blots were developed using a Chemiluminescence Reagent Plus kit (Pfizer Inc., Gladstone, NJ, USA). Signal images of each protein were then acquired using a digital camera (1.92 NP resolution) of the FluorChem® FC2 Imaging system (Alpha Innotech Corporation, San Leandro, CA, USA). The protein densities were semi-quantified using the AlphaView Program, version 3.2.2 (Cell Biosciences Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA).

## Quantitative real-time—polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis
Frozen lung tissue was briefly homogenised in RNA Bee solution (Tet-Test, Friendswood, TX, USA). The total RNA molecules were isolated by centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 15 min, after which RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (Allsheng, Hangzhou, China). Approximately 4 µg of the total RNA was annealed with 500 ng of oligo-dT primer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) at 70 °C for 10 min. The complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesised using the Invitrogen Superscript II reverse transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.). RT-qPCR was performed with the cDNA template obtained (2 µL) and 2 × Power SYBR Green (6 µL; Toyobo Life Science, Osaka, Japan) containing specific primers as follows: MUC-2, sense primer 5′-GCACA TTCCT TCGCA TCTTA AA-3′ and anti-sense primer 5′-AAAGC AAAGA ATGGA ACAGA AACTC-3′; Klf4, sense primer 5′-GGTGC AGCTT GCAGC AGTAA-3′ and anti-sense primer 5′-AAGTC TAGGT CCAGG AGGTC GTT-3′; TNF-α, sense primer: 5′-CCTGT AGCCC ACGTC GTAGC-3′ and anti-sense primer: 5′-TTGAC CTCAG CGCTG ACTTG-3′; IL-1β, sense primer 5′-GCACA TCAAC AAGAG CTTCA GGCAG-3′ and anti-sense primer: 5′-GCTGC TTGTG AGGTG CTGAT GTAC-3′; IL-4, sense primer: 5′-CCAGC TAGTT GTCAT CCTGC TCCTC -3′ and anti-sense primer: 5′-GTGAT GTGGA CTTGG ACTCA TTCAT GG -3′; IL-5, sense primer: 5′-GAGAA GGATG CTTCT GCACT TGAG -3′, anti-sense primer: 5′-CCACT CTGTA CTCAT CACAC CAAGG-3′; TGF-β1, sense primer 5′-GAGGT CACCC GCGTG CTA-3′, anti-sense primer 5′-TGTGT GAGAT GTCTT TGGTT TTCTC-3′; IL-10, sense primer 5′-CCAAG CCTTA TCGGA AATGA-3′ and, anti-sense primer 5′-TTTTC ACAGG GGAGA AATCG-3′ ; IL-18, sense primer: 5′-GTACA AAGAC AGTGA AGTAA GAGGA CT G-3′ and anti-sense primer: 5′-CTCCA TCTTG TTGTG TCCTG GAACA CG-3′; CTGF, sense primer: 5′-CATTA AGAAG GGCAA AAAGT GCA-3′ and anti-sense primer: 5′-TGCAG CCAGA AAGCT CAAAC T-3′; Fibronectin, sense primer: 5′-CATGG CTTTA GGCGG ACAA-3′ and anti-sense primer: 5′-CATCT ACATT CGGCA GGTAT GG-3′; procollagen type I alpha 2, sense primer: 5′-CTACT GGTGA AACCT GCATC C-3′ and anti-sense primer: 5′- GGGCG CGGCT GTATG AG-3′; lgkv14-126, sense primer: 5′-GCAGT GGATC TGGGC AAGA-3′ and anti-sense primer: 5′- CGGGC TCTCA CCATA CTGTA GAC-3′; Egr1, sense primer: 5′-CCATG AACGC CCATA TGCTT-3′ and anti-sense primer: 5′- GCTCA TCCGA GCGAG AAAAG-3′; Defa21, sense primer: 5′-TGGGC TCCTG CTCAC CAA-3′ and anti-sense primer: 5′- AGGGC AGAGA GGAGG ACAAG T-3′; RT-qPCR was performed for 40 cycles using the following sequence: denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 57 °C for 15 s, and extension at 70 °C for 60 s. The fluorescence intensity was measured at the end of the extension phase of each cycle. The threshold value for the fluorescence intensities of all samples was set manually. The reaction cycle at which the PCR products exceeded this fluorescence intensity threshold during the exponential phase of PCR amplification was considered the threshold cycle (Ct). Expression of the target gene was quantified relative to the housekeeping gene β-actin, based on a comparison of the Cts at a constant fluorescence intensity according to Livak and Schmittgen’s method [ ].

## Microarray analysis
Briefly, the total RNA was purified from frozen lung tissue (100 mg) using TransZol (TransGen Biotech Co. LTD, Beijing, China) (1 mL) solution based on the manufacturer’s protocol. The purity and integrity of the total RNA were evaluated by ND-2000 Spectrophotometer (NanoDrop, Wilmington, DE, USA) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA). In addition, the Affymetrix Whole transcript Expression array process was executed with GeneChipTM Whole Transcript PLUS Reagent Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific Inc.) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. cDNA was synthesised using the GeneChip Whole Transcript ANPlification Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific Inc.), as described by the manufacturer. The sense cDNA was fragmented and biotin-labelled with TdT (terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase) using the GeneChip Whole Transcript Terminal Labeling Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific Inc.). Approximately 5.5 μg of the labelled DNA target was hybridised to the Affymetrix GeneChip Mouse ST 2.0 Array (ThermoFisher Scientific Inc.) at 45 °C for 16 h. The hybridised arrays were washed, stained on a GeneChip Fluidics Station 450 (ThermoFisher Scientific Inc.), and scanned on a GCS3000 Scanner (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The signal values were calculated using the Affymetrix® GeneChip™ Command Console® Software (AGCC).
The data were summarised and normalised using a robust multi-average (RMA) method implemented in Affymetrix® Power Tools (APT, Affymetrix). The result with gene level RMA analysis was exported, and differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis was performed. The statistical significance of the expression data was determined using an independent t-test and the fold change in which the null hypothesis was that no difference exists among groups. The false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled by adjusting the p-value using the Benjamini–Hochberg algorithm. For the DEG set, hierarchical cluster analysis was performed using complete linkage and Euclidean distance as a similarity measure. Gene-Enrichment and Functional Annotation analysis for a significant probe list was performed using Gene Ontology (http://geneontology.org) and KEGG (http://kegg.jp). All data analysis and visualisation of differentially expressed genes were conducted using R 3.3.2 (www.r-project.org).

## Statistical analysis
The statistical significance of data from general analyses was evaluated using the One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA, SPSS for Windows, Release 10.10, Standard Version, Chicago, IL, USA), followed by a Tukey post hoc t-test for multiple comparisons. All values are expressed as the means ± SD and a p-value (p < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Accumulation of NPs in lung tissue after inhalation
This study first analysed whether inhaled NPs had accumulated in the lung tissue of ICR mice. The accumulation levels of NPs were measured in the lung tissue of ICR mice after inhalation with three different doses of NPs. As shown in Fig.  C, a high level of NPs was detected in the lung tissue of LoNP, MiNP, and HiNP-inhaled groups. Also, the kidney tissue showed levels similar NPs level to those measured in lung tissue although these levels were lower in the liver tissue than other tissues. These results suggest that inhaled NPs can reach the lung tissue of ICR mice successfully, and these tissues provide suitable conditions for analysing the inflammatory and toxic effects of NPs.

## Inflammatory effect of inhaled NPs in the lung tissue
The changes in the influx of leukocytes, histopathological changes, and cytokine expressions were analysed in the BALF and lung tissues of NPs-inhaled ICR mice for 2 weeks to determine if the NPs deposited in the lung tissue by inhalation can induce an inflammatory response. In the influx analysis of leukocytes, the total number of cells was enhanced remarkably in the three NPs inhaled groups compared to the Vehicle-inhaled group, but there was no difference in the LoNP-inhaled groups. Similar increase patterns were detected in the number of macrophages, neutrophils, and lymphocytes in the BALF of the NPs inhaled ICR mice. In particular, foamy macrophages related to inflammation stimulation and impaired immune function of macrophages were observed in the MiNPs and HiNPs inhaled group but not in the LoNP-inhaled group (Fig.  ). In mucin secretion analysis, the population of periodic acid-Schiff stained goblet cells on the section of lung tissue was expanded significantly in the NPs inhaled group compared to the Vehicle-inhaled group. Furthermore, these expansions were confirmed by Klf4 mRNA expression because this protein is considered a marker specific to goblet cells. The level of Klf4 mRNA expression was higher in the MiNP and HiNP-inhaled groups than in the Vehicle-inhaled group, even though there was no difference between the inhalation groups (Fig.  A and B). Furthermore, a similar increase pattern was observed in the mucin level, which was secreted from goblet cells. The level of MUC-2 mRNA expression was enhanced remarkably in only the HiNP-inhaled group compared to the Vehicle-inhaled group. In contrast, this level remained constant in the LoNP and MiNP inhaled groups (Fig.  C). In inflammatory cytokine analysis, the levels of six cytokines, including IL-4, IL-5, IL-10, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-18, were increased remarkably in most NPs inhaled groups after inhaling the NPs for 2 weeks. The levels of IL-4, IL-1β, and IL-18 mRNA expression were higher in the LoNP, MiNP, and HiNP-inhaled groups than in the Vehicle-inhaled group, whereas IL-10 and IL-5 were increased in only the MiNP and HiNP-inhaled groups. On the other hand, the TNF-α mRNA expression level was increased only in the HiNP-inhaled group but not in the LoNP and MiNP inhaled groups (Fig.  ). These results suggest that NPs inhalation for 2 weeks can cause pulmonary inflammation by stimulating the leukocyte influx into the BALF, increasing mucin secretion, and enhancing inflammatory cytokines in ICR mice. 

## Effect of inhaled NPs on the fibrosis in lung tissue
The change in the histopathological structure, expressions of pro-fibrotic marker genes, and TGF-β1 signalling pathway were measured in the lung tissue of ICR mice that had inhaled NPs for 2 weeks to determine if the inflammatory response in NPs-inhaled mice is accompanied by lung fibrosis. First, significant alterations were detected in the histopathological structure of lung tissue. The lung tissue score was significantly higher in the MiNP and HiNP-inhaled groups than in the Vehicle-inhaled group, even though the LoNP-inhaled group remained constant (Fig.  A and B). In addition, similar patterns were detected in the level of PA and average vessel wall thickness. These levels were higher in MiNP and HiNP-inhaled groups than in the LoNP-inhaled group (Fig.  A and B). On the other hand, the reverse pattern was observed in the number of NS. They decreased significantly in a dose-dependent manner after NPs inhalation (Fig.  A and B). Furthermore, a similar regulation pattern was observed in the expression levels of four pro-fibrotic marker genes: TGF-β1, pro-collagen I, fibronectin, and CTGF. Their levels were significantly higher in the NPs-inhaled groups than in the Vehicle-inhaled group (Fig.  C). These changes in the pro-fibrotic marker genes were accompanied by a change in the TGF-β1 signalling pathway. The expression levels of the TGF-β1, and β-catenin proteins and the phosphorylation levels of Smad2/3 were significantly higher in the NPs-inhaled group than in the Vehicle-inhaled group (Fig.  D). Therefore, the NPs inhalation-induced inflammatory response is tightly associated with the upregulation of fibrosis in the lung tissue of the ICR mice. 

## Characterisation of the changes in global genes expression in the lungs of the NP-inhaled mice in response to inflammation and fibrosis
The changes in the global gene expression in the lung of NPs inhaled ICR mice in response to inflammation and fibrosis were characterised. The total RNA extracted from the lung tissue of the NPs inhaled ICR mice for 2 weeks was hybridised into oligonucleotide microarrays. First, the differential distribution of global genes in normalised data was confirmed using a volume plot and scatter plot between the Vehicle and HiNP-inhaled group (Fig.  A and B). In addition, a multidimensional scaling (MDS) plot showed significant clustering between the groups based on their similarity (Fig.  A). In contrast, a two-way hierarchical clustering heatmap represented the genes with significant alterations in expression level after NPs inhalation according to the rate of change (Fig.  B). Compared to the Vehicle-inhaled ICR mice, 1,160 genes (lFCl ≤ 1.5) were detected as differentially expressed genes in the HiNP-inhaled mice. Among these, 791 genes were upregulated, and 369 genes were down-regulated in the HiNP-inhaled mice compared to the Vehicle-inhaled mice. On the other hand, statistical significance was detected in only 172 genes out of 1,160 differential expressed genes between the HiNP and Vehicle-inhaled group. After information analyses of each gene, 115 genes with clear symbols or IDs were selected to characterise the upregulated and downregulated gene list (Table ). Based on PANTHER/X ontology analyses, 115 selected genes belonged to 17 GO categories, including anatomical structure, binding, and biological regulation, even though there were differences in the number of genes (Table ). Furthermore, the genes upregulated and downregulated by NPs inhalation in the lung tissue of ICR mice were characterised. Of the 60 upregulated genes, the largest difference was detected in Igkv14-126 (7.43-fold), which was identified as an immunoglobulin-kappa variable region gene, followed by Egr1 (2.92-fold), Scel (2.47-fold), Lamb3 (1.99-fold), Upk3b (1.97-fold), and Ndufs8 (1.97-fold) (Table ). In addition, 55 genes, including Olfr417 (− 2.37), Olfr519 (− 1.97), Rps16 (− 1.89), Rap2b (− 1.88), and Vmn1r193 (− 1.86) were down-regulated after the HiNP inhalation (Table ). Furthermore, the KEGG pathway functional analyses showed that some of these genes were related to three important pathways, including the metabolic pathway (Vnn3, Hmgcs1, SNPd4, Ndufs8, Ept1, Agk, Idh3a, and Acca1b), human papillomavirus infection pathway, and olfactory transduction pathway (Fig.  ). In metabolic pathway, NP inhalation caused changes in the oxidation pathway of fatty acids, calcium signalling pathway, nitrogen metabolic pathway and nucleotide metabolic pathway (Fig.  B). The typical marker genes (Lamb3, Gsk3b, H2-Bl, and Atm) for lung diseases changed in human papillomavirus infection pathway (Fig.  C), while alterations on the several genes (Olfr417, Olfr390, Olfr446, Olfr605, Olfr519, and Olfr374) for the detection, recovery, and adaptation of olfactory were detected in olfactory transduction pathway after NPs inhalation (Fig.  D). Moreover, the expression level of two upregulated genes (lgkv14-126 and Egr1) and two down-regulated genes (MPtx2 and Defa21) were measured by RT-qPCR analyses to verify the reliability of the results obtained from the microarray analysis. Their expression patterns were similar to those observed in the microarray analysis of lung tissue (Fig.  ). Overall, NP inhalation for 2 weeks is closely involved in regulating the genes associated with the metabolic pathway, human papillomavirus infection, and olfactory transduction in the lung of ICR mice (Table ). 

## Hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity of inhaled NPs
Finally, this study examined whether the inhaled NPs for 2 weeks could cause hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity in ICR mice. The changes in the serum biochemical profile, histopathological structure, and total weight of the liver and kidney in the ICR mice exposed to the NPs for 2 weeks were analysed. Compared to the Vehicle-inhaled group, no significant differences were detected in the liver, kidney, lung, and intestine weight (Table ). A similar pattern was also detected in the serum parameters for hepatotoxicity, including ALP, AST, ALP, and LDH, as well as nephrotoxicity, including BUN. The concentration of these parameters was maintained at constant levels without a significant alteration between the Vehicle and NPs inhaled groups (Table ). Furthermore, there were no remarkable pathological alterations, including inflammation, necrosis, apoptosis, and fibrosis, in the liver sections of the NPs inhaled groups (Fig.  A). No nephrotoxicity symptoms, including degeneration and necrosis of the glomerulus and renal tubes, were detected in the histopathological structure of the kidney of the NPs inhaled groups (Fig.  B). These findings suggest that NPs inhalation cannot induce significant hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity in ICR mice. However, these results were not enough to determine the NOAEL (No observed adverse effect level) of NPs because the evaluation of this value should be required all data for systemic toxicity including reproductive toxicity, bone marrow toxicity and toxicity to immune organs. Furthermore, the levels of water consumption, feed intake, and body weight remained constant in the LoNP, MiNP, and HiNP-inhaled groups compared to the Vehicle-inhaled group (Supplement Table S1). In addition, a similar pattern was observed in the levels of all blood analysis parameters (Supplement Table S2). 

## Discussion
NPs can be taken up into the human body through various routes, including ingestion, dermal contact, entanglement, and inhalation, because they are widespread in the air, sea, and land [ ]. After uptake, NPs can cause significant toxicity with oxidative stress and inflammatory and immune system inability [ ]. On the other hand, the toxicity of NPs is limited because they are affected by the exposure concentrations, chemical properties, contamination, tissue uptake function, and individual susceptibility [ ]. Furthermore, most information on these effects of NPs in a mammalian system was obtained from orally administered animals; experiments using other exposure routes are minimal. In this study, inhalation was selected as the other exposure route to uptake NPs into the internal system of animals. The changes in the global gene expression were characterised in the lung of ICR mice during the inflammation and fibrosis induced by NPs for 2 weeks. Therefore, the present study provides novel information on the gene profiles associated with the inhalation of PS-NPs, even though many unconfirmed genes have been involved.
Inflammation in the lungs is induced by infections by various pathogens, including bacteria, parasites, and viruses, as well as exposure to some particles, such as toxins, pollutants, irritants, and allergens [ ]. This response has been detected in two different types: acute inflammation in pneumonia and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and chronic inflammation in asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [ ]. During this process, many inflammatory cells are activated and release cytokines and mediators to recruit other inflammatory cells onto the site of inflammation. In addition, the inhalation of NPs can induce several inflammatory responses in the lung tissue of mice and rats. The levels of TGF-β and TNF-α expression were significantly higher in the lung tissue of the PS-NPs-inhaled SD rats for 2 weeks, while the secretion of TNF-α and IL-6 was enhanced in the brains of mice that inhaled aerosol PS-NPs for 7 days [ , ]. The expression level of several inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6, increased in the lung of C57BL/6 mice and SD rats intratracheal administered with 0.1 nm–2.5 μm size of PS-NPs [ , ]. This study investigated the inflammatory response in the lung tissue of ICR mice inhaled with PS-NPs with a 0.5 μm diameter. The upregulated expression of six cytokines was detected in the lung tissue of the NPs-inhaled group. These findings are consistent with a previous study showing the inflammatory response of the lung tissue of NPs-inhaled animals, even though their size and treated periods differed.
Pulmonary fibrosis is defined as a condition in which the lung becomes damaged and scarred [ ]. This condition is accompanied by scarring and thickening of the tissue around and between the alveoli in lung tissue. This condition has various symptoms and complications, including shortness of breath, a dry cough, weight loss, nail clubbing, pulmonary hypertension, lung cancer, and pneumothorax [ ]. During fibrosis, pro-fibrotic mediators are secreted from the activated macrophages and neutrophils and promote the accumulation of myofibroblasts that produce extracellular matrix (ECM) [ , ]. In particular, some soluble mediators, including TGF-β, IL-6, and galectin-3 (Gal-3), stimulate the transformation of endothelial cells to myofibroblasts, while TGF-β and IL-6 promote the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) of alveolar endothelial type 1 (AT1) cells [ ]. In addition, the symptoms of pulmonary fibrosis were observed in the lung tissue of animals that inhaled NPs. SD rats that inhaled 0.1 μm PS-NPs for 2 weeks exhibited a significant decrease in pulmonary function and an increase in fibrosis-related markers, including TGF-β, TNF-α, and vimentin [ ]. A ventilatory dysfunction, fibrotic pathological changes, and deposition of collagen and E-cadherin were detected in the lung of the C57BL/6 mice after the inhalation of tire-wear NPs, while the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway and increased in α-SMA, vimentin, and collagen expression were observed in the same mice intratracheal administered with PS-NPs [ , ]. Some similar pathological changes, including alveolar destruction, were detected in the lung of the SD rats intratracheal installed by PS-NPs for 2 weeks [ ]. This study examined the pulmonary fibrosis in the lung of ICR mice after inhalation of 0.5 μm PS-NPs for 2 weeks. The expression levels of the pro-fibrotic marker genes and the pathological score were increased in the NPs-inhaled group, as shown in Fig.  . These two key parameters are consistent with previous studies that reported the inhalation effects of NPs on the pulmonary fibrosis of the lung tissue. Nevertheless, more studies are needed to understand their role and confirm the inhibition of the ventilatory function.
Microarray assays have been applied in various fields, including basic mechanism studies, target discovery for drugs, biomarker determination for disease, toxicogenomics, development of prognostic methods, and classification of diseases, even though metabolic analysis is also used for the same purpose [ – ]. Recently, this analysis was used to characterise the response to NPs in cells and mice, even though there have been very few studies. Seven hundred and seventy genes, including cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, TNF signalling pathway, and Hippo signalling pathway, were expressed differentially in human lung epithelial cells treated with 7.5 μg/cm2 PS, while 1951 genes, including ECM-receptor interaction, steroid biosynthesis, and PI3K-Akt signalling pathway changed significantly in the same cells treated with 7.5 μg/cm2 PS during induction of apoptosis [ ]. In addition, remarkable alteration in several genes related to the immune responses (STAT5A and DEFB132), immunoglobulin genes (IGKV1-12, IGHV3-38, IGLV3-27, IGLC2, LGHM, IGLV1-40, and IGLLC7), microRNAs (NEAT1, MIR1290, MIR4694, MIR4668, MIR4532, MIR4703, and MIR3165), and miR-related gene (MIR4713 and MIR4269) were detected in human microglial HMC-3 cells treated with PS-NPs [ ]. In particular, only one study examined the response to NPs in the specific tissues of mice. The hippocampus of the C57BL/6J mice administered PE for 2 weeks showed changes in the total 22 genes (14 increased genes and 18 decreased genes), which were related to the negative regulation of protein binding, transcription, DNA templates, and angiogenesis on GO terms [ ]. In the prefrontal cortex of these mice, 23 genes related to endothelial cell chemotaxis, mitotic cell cycle arrest, and cell migration were significantly changed after the administration of PE [ ]. In particular, the greatest fold changes were detected in early growth response protein 1 (EGR-1), followed by activity-regulated cytoskeleton-associated protein (ARC) and cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1A (CDKN1A) [ ]. The present study first characterised the change in global gene expression in the lung tissue of ICR mice in response to inflammation and fibrosis induced by PS-NPs inhalation for 2 weeks. A significant change in the NPs inhaled group was detected in 172 genes and three pathways, including the metabolic pathway, human papillomavirus infection, and olfactory transduction. Most of the genes detected in these results were different from those previously identified in the brain tissue after the administration of PE. The difference between the genes from this study and previous studies was attributed to differences in the target tissue used in the microarray experiments and the types of NPs administered to the mice. Furthermore, differences in the administration routes are expected to affect the number and class of altered genes significantly.
Two gene groups commonly changed in two different tissues of animals administered two types of NPs in two different routes were identified. First, EGR-1 was significantly increased in the lung tissue of PS-NPs inhaled ICR mice for 2 weeks and in brain tissue of C57BL/6 mice fed with PE-NPs for 2 weeks, but the rate of the increase was higher in lung tissue (2.92 fold) than the brain tissue (1.53 fold) [ ]. EGR-1 was identified as a transcriptional regulator capable of various environmental signals, such as hormones, growth factors, and neurotransmitters, and was involved in some cell functions, including cell growth, apoptosis, signal transduction, and cell proliferation [ , ]. This gene can respond to various biochemical signals produced by antigens and cytokines during the immune reaction and is associated with neuronal activity and many higher-order processes in the central nervous system [ , ]. Second, several Olfr genes were commonly changed in the lung tissue of PS-NPs-inhaled ICR mice for 2 weeks and the brain tissue of C57BL/6 mice fed with PE-NPs for 2 weeks. The expression of all the Olfr genes, including Olfr376, Olfr390, Olfr417, Olfr446, Olfr519, and Olfr605, decreased remarkably in the lung tissue of the ICR mice after PS-NPs inhalation. In the brain tissue, however, the expression levels of some genes (Olfr1303, Olfr435, and Olfr1352) were significantly higher in the hippocampus, and some genes (Olfr912, Olfr1442, and Olfr1284) were lower in the prefrontal cortex [ ]. Olfr is a G protein-coupled receptor that plays an essential role in the odorant detection process in olfactory sensory neurons and is encoded by the largest gene family in mice [ , ]. Their role in the reliable recognition and accurate processing of chemosensory information is vital in the sensitive and selective responsiveness of olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs) to odorous molecules and pheromones [ ]. On the other hand, there has been no research on the co-relationship between NPs and the above two gene groups until now. The present study provided novel evidence that EGR-1 and Olfr can be sensitive and broad biomarkers for inflammation and fibrosis induced by NPs inhalation. Nevertheless, further studies will be needed to determine the application possibility of Secl as a marker gene under clinical conditions.
Overall, the differential gene expression caused by inflammation and fibrosis induced by PS-NPs inhalation was characterised using the lung tissue from the Vehicle and NPs inhaled ICR mice. This study identified 115 genes with differential expression levels in response to the inflammation and fibrosis induced by PS-NPs inhalation in ICR mice for 2 weeks. This information on the gene profile might be useful for predicting the level of PS-NPs accumulation in lung tissue or evaluating the risk of PS-NPs. Nevertheless, this study had some limitations in that it did not evaluate the systemic toxicity of PS-NPs due to restrictions on the detection of PS-NPs concentration in the bloodstream after inhalation. Moreover, the lack of mechanism analyses of the effects of PS-NPs in the transcriptional regulation of brain-expressed genes should be considered a drawback of this study. Therefore, further research will be needed on the correlation between blood penetration and systemic toxicity of PS-NPs as well as between PS-NPs accumulation and the regulation of gene expression in the brain to understand the molecular effects of PS-NPs.
