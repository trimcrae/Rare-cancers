## Background
Macrophages exhibit distinct polarization states (M1/M2) in response to inflammatory stimuli. However, the molecular mechanisms regulating macrophage polarization during Aspergillus fumigatus infection remain incompletely understood.

## Objective
This study aims to investigate the role of signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) in modulating macrophage polarization during A. fumigatus-induced invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA).

## Methods
An IPA mouse model and THP-1-derived macrophages were used to evaluate STAT1 expression and macrophage phenotype. Techniques included immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, Western blotting, ELISA, flow cytometry, and RT-qPCR.

## Results
In vivo, IPA led to increased infiltration of M1 macrophages and elevated STAT1, P-STAT1, IFN-γ, and iNOS levels in lung tissues, while the expression of M2 markers (Arg1, FIZZ1) remained unaltered. In vitro, exposure to A. fumigatus conidia promoted STAT1 expression and M1 polarization in THP-1 cells, with no significant effect on the M2 marker Arg1. Both pharmacological inhibition of STAT1 with fludarabine and genetic silencing of STAT1 by siRNA significantly attenuated the expression of M1 markers and pro-inflammatory cytokines, while STAT1 overexpression enhanced the expression of M1 markers and pro-inflammatory cytokines.

## Conclusion
STAT1 is a central regulator of M1 macrophage polarization in response to A. fumigatus infection. Targeting STAT1 may represent a potential therapeutic approach for managing invasive aspergillosis by modulating host immune responses.

## Introduction
Invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) is a severe lung disease caused by the failure to effectively eliminate inhaled Aspergillus conidia. IPA primarily affects patients with hematologic malignancies, those undergoing stem cell or organ transplantation, and individuals with autoimmune disorders [ ]. Approximately 15–20% of leukemia patients die from IPA. In addition, the incidence of non-neutropenic IPA is steadily rising [ ]. Given its increasing prevalence and poor prognosis, further investigation into the underlying mechanisms of IPA is urgently needed.
Neutrophils and alveolar macrophages constitute the first line of innate immune defense against Aspergillus infections. As early as the nineteenth century, Elie Metchnikoff identified macrophages as large phagocytic mononuclear cells essential for immune surveillance [ ]. Macrophages are present in all tissues and exhibit high plasticity and heterogeneity, adapting their phenotype based on the surrounding tissue microenvironment [ ].
During infection, macrophages typically polarize into the M1 phenotype, which exerts strong antimicrobial and pro-inflammatory activity to support early host defense [ ]. However, pathogens have developed diverse mechanisms to evade immune responses by modulating macrophage function, thereby promoting survival, replication, and disease progression [ ]. Notably, the classical dichotomy of M1/M2 polarization is not always clearly observed in vivo, as many pathogens induce a tailored inflammatory response that varies by context [ ].
In this study, we demonstrated that M1 polarization is prominently induced both in IPA mouse lungs and in THP-1-derived macrophage models in vitro. Notably, STAT1 was significantly upregulated in IPA mouse lungs and in infected THP-1-derived macrophages. We further explored the role of STAT1 in macrophage polarization and investigated the mechanisms involved. Our findings indicate that STAT1 facilitates M1 polarization by regulating gene transcription and cytokine production. Understanding the STAT1–macrophage axis may provide valuable insights into novel immunotherapeutic strategies against IPA.

## Materials and methods

## Culture of THP-1 cells and macrophage differentiation
THP-1 cells, a human monocytic leukemia cell line, were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA), 100 μg/mL streptomycin, 100 U/mL penicillin (Gibco, USA), and 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Procell, China). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The approximate doubling time under these conditions was 48 h. For macrophage differentiation, THP-1 cells were treated with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; Sigma, USA) for 24 h.

## A. fumigatus cultivation and conidia isolation
Aspergillus fumigatus strain Af293 was cultured on Sabouraud dextrose agar plates at 28 °C for 5–7 days. Colonies were harvested using sterile PBS and homogenized to prepare a conidial suspension. Conidia were separated through sterile filtration and adjusted to a final concentration of 0.5–1 × 10⁸/mL. For FITC labeling, conidia were incubated overnight with 0.16 mg/mL FITC at 4 °C in the dark. After two washes with PBS to remove unbound dye, the labeled conidia were resuspended in PBS and stored at 4 °C protected from light.

## Stimulation of cells with conidia
THP-1-derived macrophages were prepared by seeding THP-1 cells at a density of 2 × 10⁶ cells per well in 6-well plates in RPMI 1640 medium containing 100 ng/mL PMA and 1% FBS. After 24 h of incubation, non-adherent cells were removed by washing with warm PBS, and fresh complete RPMI 1640 medium was added for a further 24-h culture. Subsequently, the differentiated macrophages were stimulated with A. fumigatus conidia at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 for 0, 12, 24, and 36 h. At each time point, culture supernatants and total cellular proteins were collected and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis.

## Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay
Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Briefly, THP-1-derived macrophages were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 100 μL of complete medium. After adherence and differentiation, cells were stimulated with A. fumigatus conidia at an MOI of 1 for 12, 24, and 36 h. At the end of each incubation period, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent (Biosharp, Hefei, China) was added directly to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA).

## Pharmacological inhibition and genetic silencing of STAT1
For STAT1 inhibition, THP-1-derived macrophages were pretreated with fludarabine (Selleck, USA) at a final concentration of 2.5 μM for 48 h prior to stimulation with A. fumigatus conidia.
Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting human STAT1 and a non-targeting scrambled negative control siRNA (si-NC) were designed and synthesized by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China).
The sequences were as follows:
Sense: 5ʹ-CTGTGATGTTAGATAAACA-3ʹ, and antisense: 5ʹ-GCAGCACAACATACGGAAA-3ʹ.
The transfection was performed using the riboFECT™ CP Transfection Kit (RiboBio, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, THP-1-derived macrophages were prepared in 24-well plates. For each transfection sample, 1.25 μl of 20 μM siRNA (si-STAT1 or si-NC) was diluted in 30 μl of 1X riboFECT™ CP Buffer. Then, 3 μl of riboFECT™ CP Reagent was added to the diluted siRNA, mixed gently, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min to form the transfection complexes. The complexes were then added directly to the cells cultured in complete medium without antibiotics. The final concentration of siRNA in the culture medium was 50 nM. After transfection for 6 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete RPMI 1640 medium. The cells were further cultured for 48 h to allow for efficient gene knockdown before subsequent stimulation with A. fumigatus conidia.

## Lentiviral vector-mediated overexpression
Lentiviral vectors encoding STAT1 were obtained from Hanbio Biotechnology (Shanghai, China) and used for overexpression in THP-1-derived macrophages. A preliminary titration experiment was conducted to determine the optimal multiplicity of infection. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well and transduced with lentiviral particles at dilutions of 1:50, 1:75, 1:100, and 1:200. After 24 h of incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh RPMI-1640 complete medium. Cells were cultured for an additional 72 h before harvesting for RNA and protein extraction. A parallel control group was infected with an empty lentiviral vector. Transfection efficiency was confirmed by green fluorescence using fluorescence microscopy. STAT1 expression was verified by Western blot and reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). Successfully transduced cells were passaged and used for downstream experiments.

## Mouse model of IPA
A mouse model of IPA was established using male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old, 20–25 g), purchased from Qinlong Shan Animal Breeding Farm (Nanjing, China). To induce immunosuppression, mice were administered intraperitoneal injections of cyclophosphamide (150 mg/kg; HengRui, China) once daily for three consecutive days before fungal challenge (days − 3, − 2, − 1 relative to day 0). Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 2% chloral hydrate (10 mg/kg) and then intratracheally inoculated with 1 × 10⁷ A. fumigatus conidia suspended in 100 μL of PBS using a venous catheter inserted into the trachea. After infection, left lung tissue, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), and serum were collected and stored at − 80 °C. The right lung was fixed in 10% formaldehyde for histological, immunohistochemical analysis, and immunofluorescence (IF) staining. Mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation at the end of the experiment. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanjing Jinling Hospital (approval no. 2022DZJKJDWLS-0103) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and the ARRIVE reporting standards.

## Immunohistochemistry analysis
Paraffin-embedded lung tissues were sectioned and subjected to deparaffinization, followed by rehydration through a graded ethanol series. After antigen retrieval, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD68, iNOS, and CD206. The following day, appropriate secondary antibodies were applied. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted with neutral resin. Tissue sections were examined under a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

## Immunofluorescence staining
Lung tissue sections were fixed in pre-cooled 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature, followed by permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 20 min. After blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against iNOS and CD86. The next day, Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated rabbit secondary antibodies (Life Technologies, USA) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Abcam), and the samples were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan) at 200 × magnification.

## Flow cytometry
To investigate the effect of Aspergillus fumigatus spores on the polarization of THP-1 macrophages, cells were incubated with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated anti-human CD11b, phycoerythrin (PE)-conjugated anti-human CD14, PE-conjugated anti-human CD86, and allophycocyanin (APC)-conjugated anti-human CD206 antibodies (Invitrogen, USA) at a concentration of 0.5 µg/test. A suspension of A. fumigatus conidia (1 × 108/mL) was used to induce macrophage polarization. After incubation for 3 and 6 h, cells were centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 10 min to remove the supernatant, then resuspended in 200 µL PBS. The cells were incubated at 4 °C for 30 min, followed by an additional centrifugation at the same speed for 10 min to remove excess PBS. The cells were then resuspended in fresh PBS and analyzed by flow cytometry. Double-positive cells for both CD11b and CD14 (≥ 85%) were considered macrophages. The proportion of CD11b + CD86 + cells was used to identify M1 macrophages, while CD11b + CD206 + cells were classified as M2 macrophages. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software version 10.0 (Tree Star, Inc., USA).

## Reverse transcription and real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)
Total RNA was extracted from macrophages in each group using Trizol reagent (Takara, Japan), and reverse transcription was performed to synthesize cDNA. The reaction was carried out using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Japan) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq Kit (Takara, Japan) on a Bio-Rad fluorescence PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA). Gene expression levels of TNF-α, iNOS, CD80, and CD86 were quantified using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal control. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table  .
 

## Western blot analysis
Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) containing 1% PMSF. Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein (20 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE on 10% polyacrylamide gels, then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in PBS at room temperature for 2 h. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies specific to STAT1, P-STAT1, iNOS, IFN-γ, FIZZ1, Arg1, β-Tubulin, and GAPDH. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Bio-Rad, USA) and detected using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA).

## ELISA assay
IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10, and IFN-γ levels in plasma and BALF supernatants were measured using ELISA kits (Elabscience Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 22.0, IBM, USA). Data are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student’s t-test, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Aspergillus fumigatus induces pulmonary inflammation and upregulates M1 macrophage polarization markers in mice
Histological analysis of H&E-stained lung sections revealed progressive pathology in A. fumigatus-infected mice. Controls showed minimal changes, whereas infected groups exhibited inflammatory cell infiltration, alveolar neutrophil recruitment, and tracheal edema with intrinsic layer thinning. These alterations were significantly more severe in the 48 h infection group (IPA 48 h) than at 24 h (IPA 24 h), demonstrating time-dependent aggravation of lung injury (Fig.  A). Western blot analysis revealed that the protein levels of STAT1, p-STAT1, IFN-γ, and iNOS were significantly elevated in lung tissues from the IPA group compared to the normal control (NC) group. In contrast, the levels of M2 markers, including Arg1 and FIZZ1, remained unchanged (Fig.  B). ELISA assays further confirmed that IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10, and IFN-γ concentrations in both plasma and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were markedly higher in IPA mice (Fig.  C–L). Immunohistochemistry showed a notable increase in the infiltration of CD68⁺ macrophages and iNOS⁺ M1 macrophages, accompanied by a reduction in CD206⁺ M2 macrophages in the IPA group (Fig.  A). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated significantly elevated expression of iNOS and CD86 in the lungs of IPA mice compared with NC mice (Fig.  B). These results demonstrate that during invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA), Aspergillus fumigatus induces pulmonary inflammation in mice while concurrently promoting M1-type macrophage polarization in vivo. 

## Impact of Aspergillus fumigatus on phenotypic markers and cytokines related to the polarization of human THP-1-derived macrophages
Flow cytometric analysis confirmed that PMA treatment successfully differentiated THP-1 cells into macrophages, with 94.2% of cells being double-positive for CD11b and CD14 (Supplementary Fig. 1). At baseline (0 h), the macrophages exhibited an M0 resting phenotype, with 3.47% CD11b⁺CD86⁺ (M1-type) cells and 2.47% CD11b⁺CD206⁺ (M2-type) cells (Fig.  A). Following stimulation with A. fumigatus conidia (MOI = 1), CCK-8 assays showed minimal cytotoxicity at 12 and 24 h, but a significant decrease in cell viability (< 50%) was observed at 36 h (Fig.  A). Therefore, subsequent analyses focused on the 12-h and 24-h time points. Stimulation with A. fumigatus induced a predominantly M1-polarized response. Western blot analysis revealed a time-dependent increase in the protein expression of STAT1, phosphorylated STAT1 (p-STAT1), and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), while the protein level of the M2 marker Arg1 remained unchanged (Fig.  B). This was supported by RT-qPCR, which showed significant upregulation of M1-related genes (TNF-α, iNOS, CD80, and CD86) (Fig.  C–F). Flow cytometric analysis further quantified this polarization shift. After 12 h of stimulation, the proportion of CD11b⁺CD86⁺ M1 macrophages increased to 73.1%, rising further to 91.9% at 24 h. A moderate increase in CD11b⁺CD206⁺ M2 macrophages was also observed (40.5% at 12 h and 54.0% at 24 h) (Fig.  B, C), suggesting that A. fumigatus induces a mixed but predominantly M1-polarized response. 

## The role of STAT1 in regulating macrophage polarization: effects of pharmacological inhibition, genetic silencing, and overexpression on M1/M2 markers and inflammatory cytokine production
To evaluate the role of STAT1 in macrophage polarization, THP-1-derived macrophages were pretreated with the STAT1 inhibitor fludarabine, transfected with STAT1-specific siRNA, or transduced with a STAT1 overexpression lentiviral vector, followed by stimulation with A. fumigatus for 12 and 24 h. Pharmacological inhibition of STAT1 with fludarabine significantly reduced the mRNA levels of TNF-α and iNOS at both time points (Fig.  A, B). This decrease was confirmed at the protein level, with ELISA showing a marked reduction in TNF-α secretion (Fig.  C) and Western blot analysis revealing suppressed expression of STAT1, phosphorylated STAT1 (p-STAT1), and iNOS proteins. In contrast, the expression of the M2 marker Arg1 remained unaltered (Fig.  D). Genetic silencing of STAT1 by siRNA produced a congruent phenotype, leading to a pronounced reduction in both TNF-α and iNOS mRNA (Fig.  A, B) and a concomitant decrease in TNF-α protein secretion (Fig.  C). Western blot analysis confirmed that siRNA-mediated knockdown suppressed STAT1, p-STAT1, and iNOS protein expression without affecting Arg1 levels (Fig.  D). Conversely, STAT1 overexpression markedly enhanced the mRNA expression of TNF-α and iNOS (Fig.  A, B) and significantly increased TNF-α release (Fig.  C). Western blot analysis confirmed elevated protein levels of STAT1, p-STAT1, and iNOS, while Arg1 expression was again unaffected (Fig.  D). Collectively, these results from complementary pharmacological and genetic approaches demonstrate that STAT1 specifically and positively regulates M1-type macrophage polarization through transcriptional and post-translational mechanisms. 

## Discussion
Invasive fungal disease (IFD) is a life-threatening infection that poses a growing global health challenge, particularly among immunocompromised individuals [ ]. Each year, over 6.55 million people are at risk of fungal diseases, and more than 3.75 million die as a result. Of these, approximately 2.55 million deaths (68%) are directly attributable to IFD [ ]. The burden of fungal infections has been further exacerbated in recent years by the COVID-19 pandemic, which has increased susceptibility to opportunistic fungal pathogens [ ].Among these pathogens, Aspergillus species are recognized as a major cause of IFD worldwide [ ].The respiratory system serves as the primary immunological barrier against fungal invasion. Humans continuously inhale airborne conidia from filamentous molds and dimorphic fungi, including Aspergillus, which must be rapidly eliminated to prevent infection [ ]. Individuals with weakened immune defenses are particularly vulnerable to these pathogens [ ]. Therefore, elucidating the host immune mechanisms, particularly those governing the early defense against inhaled Aspergillus conidia in vulnerable populations, is essential for developing novel and effective antifungal strategies.
Macrophages play a central role in the innate defense against fungal pathogens by engulfing A. fumigatus conidia and releasing acidified phagosomes and reactive oxygen species (ROS). This response is critical in the early stage of infection, especially before neutrophil recruitment [ ]. In the context of IPA, alveolar macrophage activation and polarization are essential components of the immune response. During initial fungal exposure, macrophages detect pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and differentiate toward the M1 phenotype, which contributes to pathogen elimination but may also promote lung tissue damage [ ]. In a 3D dynamic co-culture system, Chandorkar et al. demonstrated that Aspergillus fuscus spores are efficiently captured by monocyte-derived antigen-presenting cells, highlighting the active role of macrophages in antifungal defense [ ].Due to their phenotypic plasticity, macrophages are important targets for immunomodulatory therapy. The M1 phenotype is associated with fungal clearance, while suppression of M2 polarization has been shown to confer protection against infections with A. fumigatus, Cryptococcus neoformans, and Histoplasma capsulatum [ ]. However, the regulation of macrophage polarization is a complex, multi-step process involving numerous extracellular signals and intracellular mediators [ ].
Signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) is a member of the STAT protein family and plays diverse roles in immune regulation, inflammation, and tumor suppression [ , ]. STAT1 deficiency has been associated with enhanced tumorigenesis and impaired response to interferons. In mice lacking both STAT1 and p53, spontaneous or induced tumor formation occurs more frequently than in p53-deficient mice alone, and the antitumor effects of IFN-α are lost [ ]. Importantly, STAT1 is also essential in host defense against infectious diseases. Mutations or deficiencies in STAT1 can impair immune responses to a wide range of pathogens, including viruses, bacteria, and fungi [ , ]. In humans, STAT1 mutations are often detected in early life and are associated with clinical manifestations such as chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis (CMC) and other superficial or invasive fungal infections [ , ].Consistent with previous findings, our results demonstrate that STAT1 expression is significantly elevated in A. fumigatus-infected tissues and THP-1-derived macrophages. Functional assays revealed that both pharmacological inhibition of STAT1 (via fludarabine) and genetic silencing (via siRNA) suppressed M1 polarization and decreased the expression of TNF-α, iNOS, and phosphorylated STAT1. Notably, neither intervention altered the expression of the M2 marker Arg1, underscoring the specificity of STAT1 for the M1 program. In contrast, STAT1 overexpression enhanced both mRNA and protein levels of M1-associated markers, while Arg1 expression remained unaffected. These data suggest that STAT1 specifically and positively regulates M1 macrophage polarization during fungal infection. This aligns with prior studies reporting that STAT1 activation drives pro-inflammatory responses and promotes M1 differentiation [ – ].
Despite accumulating evidence supporting the involvement of STAT1 in macrophage polarization, the underlying molecular mechanisms by which A. fumigatus activates STAT1 signaling remain incompletely understood. Based on our experimental results and prior literature, we propose several potential mechanisms through which A. fumigatus may induce STAT1-dependent M1 polarization in macrophages. First, recognition of fungal cell wall components such as β-glucans by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), particularly Dectin-1, initiates intracellular signaling cascades involving spleen tyrosine kinase (Syk). This leads to downstream activation of transcription factors including STAT1 [ , ]. In addition, fungal extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from Aspergillus have been shown to upregulate CD86 expression and promote STAT1 phosphorylation in THP-1 macrophages via the Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)/MyD88 pathway [ , ].Furthermore, multiple surface receptors may synergize to modulate STAT1 activation. Dectin-1, the primary receptor for β-glucans, triggers the CARD9–NF-κB axis upon ligand binding, promoting the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α. These cytokines may, in turn, reinforce STAT1 signaling through autocrine or paracrine mechanisms [ ]. Meanwhile, TLR2 and TLR4 recognize other A. fumigatus components, such as galactomannan, and induce inflammatory gene expression via the MyD88-dependent pathway, further amplifying STAT1 activity [ ].Importantly, interferon-γ (IFN-γ) plays a pivotal role in promoting M1 macrophage polarization through direct STAT1 activation. In line with this, our study demonstrated a significant upregulation of IFN-γ in the lung tissues, BALF, and serum of IPA mice, which correlates strongly with the observed STAT1 activation and M1 polarization. Pre-treatment of macrophages with IFN-γ significantly enhances their responsiveness to fungal stimuli, promoting STAT1 phosphorylation and increasing the expression of M1 markers such as CD86 and iNOS [ ]. Our findings thus provide in vivo evidence that underscores the crucial role of the IFN-γ/STAT1 axis in driving antifungal M1 responses during invasive aspergillosis. These signaling networks may cooperate to promote a sustained STAT1-mediated pro-inflammatory state during IPA.
In conclusion, our study demonstrates that STAT1 is a critical regulator of M1 macrophage polarization during A. fumigatus infection. Through both in vivo and in vitro models, we show that STAT1 upregulation promotes the polarization of macrophages toward the M1 phenotype, characterized by the enhanced production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and iNOS. This process is pivotal for controlling early fungal growth but also contributes to tissue damage. Targeting STAT1-mediated polarization may thus represent a dual-edged therapeutic strategy, balancing fungal clearance and limiting excessive inflammation. However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this study. While we have established a clear mechanistic link between STAT1 activation and M1 polarization, the functional consequences of this polarization on direct antifungal immunity remain to be fully elucidated. Specifically, future studies are needed to determine how STAT1 modulation affects key macrophage effector functions—such as phagocytosis and fungal killing capacity—against A. fumigatus conidia and hyphae. In addition, the in vivo relevance of STAT1 signaling in host resistance awaits further validation using conditional knockout models to assess its impact on fungal burden, inflammatory pathology, and survival outcomes in invasive aspergillosis.
Our findings are consistent with previous studies that have highlighted the role of STAT1 in immune responses against various pathogens. For instance, studies have shown that STAT1 is essential for macrophage responses to bacterial infections [ ]. Moreover, other reports have linked STAT1 activation to enhanced resistance to fungal infections such as C. albicans and Mucor [ ]. However, the precise role of STAT1 in regulating inflammation in fungal infections remains a complex issue, as its activation can lead to both protective immunity and pathological tissue damage. This underscores the need for targeted therapeutic strategies that modulate STAT1 activity in a controlled manner. Overall, this study adds to the growing body of evidence suggesting that STAT1 is a crucial modulator of macrophage polarization in response to fungal pathogens. By advancing our understanding of the STAT1 signaling pathway, we open up new avenues for therapeutic intervention in invasive fungal diseases, particularly in immunocompromised patients, where the balance between immune activation and suppression is critically important.
