## Abstract

## Background
Acute lung injury (ALI) and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) are life-threatening conditions characterized by excessive inflammation, alveolar-capillary barrier disruption, and immunothrombosis. Mortality remains persistently high due to the lack of effective targeted therapies. Emerging evidence indicates that histone post-translational modifications (PTMs) play dual regulatory roles by modulating intranuclear gene transcription and, upon extracellular release, amplifying damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) signaling.

## Scope
This review proposes a unified pathological framework termed the “Nuclear Epigenetic Remodeling‒Extracellular DAMP Amplification Circuit.” By conceptualizing this as an inside-out pathological cascade, we integrate four principal histone modifications—acetylation, methylation, lactylation, and citrullination—and evaluate three-tiered therapeutic strategies alongside the unique multi-target potential of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM).

## Key Findings
Mechanistically, intranuclear HDAC3/6 drive inflammatory gene expression and pyroptosis, while cytoprotective SIRT1/3/6 suppress inflammasome activation and ferroptosis; concurrently, histone lactylation at H3K18 and H3K14 bridges glycolytic metabolism with ferroptosis and glycocalyx degradation; ultimately, externalized citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3) propagates a thrombo-inflammatory cascade driving NET-mediated immunothrombosis. Corresponding to this cascade, TCM-derived interventions collectively show promising potential to act across all therapeutic tiers by modulating the HDAC/SIRT acetylation axis, suppressing the PAD4–CitH3–NETs cascade, and regulating lactylation-linked metabolic pathways.

## Conclusion
We propose a precision medicine approach integrating dynamic epigenetic biomarkers (eg, serum CitH3 and H3K18la) with a multi-tiered intervention framework. This paradigm aims to shift ALI/ARDS treatment from empirical supportive care toward mechanism-based, individualized targeted therapies.

Keywords: acute lung injury, acute respiratory distress syndrome, histone modifications, extracellular histones, neutrophil extracellular traps, immune-related thrombosis, traditional Chinese medicine

## Introduction
Despite decades of intensive research, acute lung injury (ALI) and its severe manifestation, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), continue to pose substantial clinical challenges, with mortality rates remaining unacceptably high in the absence of effective targeted therapies. The pathological hallmarks of ALI/ARDS include sustained inflammatory activation, disruption of the alveolar-capillary barrier, dysregulated immune homeostasis, and microvascular thrombosis, which together accelerate the lethal transition to multiple organ dysfunction. Emerging clinical evidence has firmly established a strong correlation between elevated levels of circulating extracellular histones and nucleosomes and the development of endothelial dysfunction and adverse patient outcomes. , 
In recent years, a growing body of evidence has converged around a core mechanistic paradigm: histones and their post-translational modifications (PTMs) are not merely nuclear transcriptional regulators but also pivotal mediators of damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) signaling in ALI/ARDS. Within the nucleus, dysregulated epigenetic enzymes dictate cellular fate: histone deacetylases (HDAC3/6) have been shown to promote macrophage pyroptosis and compromise alveolar epithelial integrity, whereas SIRT1/3/6 provide endogenous cytoprotective effects by suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation and preserving mitochondrial quality control mechanisms such as mitophagy. Concurrently, recent discoveries have further highlighted the role of histone lactylation (eg, H3K18la, H3K14la) as a molecular nexus that integrates glycolytic reprogramming, ferroptosis susceptibility, and endothelial glycocalyx degradation, providing novel insight into epigenetic-metabolic crosstalk in lung injury. Crucially, bridging the intracellular and extracellular compartments, citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3) released into the systemic circulation acts as a potent DAMP, promoting NET formation, triggering TLR4-driven inflammatory signaling, and increasing endothelial permeability, ultimately driving immune thrombosis. 
Based on these mechanistic insights, we delineate a three-tier therapeutic architecture. The first tier comprises selective HDAC3/6 inhibitors and SIRT activators, which aim to restore intranuclear transcriptional homeostasis and preserve alveolar integrity. The second tier includes peptidyl arginine deiminase (PAD) inhibitors and anti-CitH3 monoclonal antibodies, designed to disrupt neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, thereby mitigating immune-mediated thrombosis. The third tier encompasses polyanionic agents such as heparin, recombinant thrombomodulin (rTM) and suramin, along with biomimetic nanoparticle-based delivery systems, all of which function to neutralize circulating histones and re-establish endothelial integrity. 
Notably, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has shown promising translational value due to its multi-component, multi-target, and systems-level regulatory features. Uniquely positioned to intervene across all three therapeutic tiers, TCM formulations may exert synergistic therapeutic benefits by modulating the SIRT1/p300 acetylation axis, suppressing NET formation, and maintaining endothelial barriers, with preliminary evidence supporting these effects in the context of COVID-19-induced ARDS. , 
This review introduces an integrated conceptual framework—the “Nuclear Epigenetic Remodeling–Extracellular DAMP Amplification Circuit”—to systematically elucidate the pathological cascades and therapeutic targets of ALI/ARDS. By framing histone-driven pathology not as isolated events but as a continuous, inside-out pathological cascade, special emphasis is placed on the translational potential of both targeted epigenetic therapies and holistic TCM-based interventions, with the ultimate goal of informing precision-targeted strategies for clinical application.

## Histone Modifications
Histone post-translational modifications (PTMs) at the nuclear level dynamically regulate chromatin accessibility, reshape transcriptional programs of inflammatory genes, and integrate cellular metabolic states. In the context of ALI and ARDS, these epigenetic mechanisms play central roles in disease onset and progression. The four principal classes of reversible histone modifications implicated in ALI pathogenesis include acetylation, methylation, lactylation, and citrullination. According to the “histone code” hypothesis, specific spatial and temporal combinations of these PTMs dictate chromatin states, ultimately modulating gene expression outputs and cellular fate decisions. 

## Histone Acetylation and Deacetylation
Histone acetylation, catalyzed by histone acetyltransferases (HATs) such as p300 and CREB-binding protein (CBP), generally promotes chromatin relaxation and transcriptional activation, whereas histone deacetylases (HDACs) reverse this process to enforce gene silencing. In the pathogenesis of ALI/ARDS, dysregulation of the acetylation–deacetylation balance has emerged as a critical driver of both inflammatory amplification and endothelial barrier dysfunction.

## Pro-Inflammatory HDACs (HDAC3/6)
HDAC3 plays a central role in driving macrophage pyroptosis and pulmonary inflammation via the cGAS-STING-NLRP3 signaling axis. During ALI, HDAC3 upregulation in alveolar macrophages facilitates the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytosol, activating cGAS-STING signaling, which in turn amplifies NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and gasdermin D (GSDMD)-mediated pyroptotic cell death. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of HDAC3 (eg, using RGFP966) has been shown to suppress this cascade, reduce IL-1β and IL-18 secretion, and attenuate pulmonary edema and inflammatory cell infiltration.
Beyond macrophages, HDAC3 also compromises alveolar epithelial barrier integrity by disrupting tight junction protein expression and promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) through activation of the TGF-β1/Smad3 pathway. HDAC6, another pro-inflammatory deacetylase, has been demonstrated to drive inflammatory signaling and caspase-1 activation in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced ALI models. The selective HDAC6 inhibitor CAY10603 blocks IκB phosphorylation and caspase-1 activation, thereby reducing cytokine production, leukocyte infiltration, and pulmonary edema. Similarly, Tubastatin A, another HDAC6 inhibitor, prevents hemorrhage-induced endothelial barrier dysfunction by enhancing tubulin acetylation and suppressing Rho signaling. 
However, the role of HDAC6 may be context-dependent. The apparently contradictory roles of HDAC6, pro-inflammatory in LPS-induced models but potentially anti-inflammatory via negative regulation of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), likely reflect differences in cell type, disease model, and timing of assessment. Menden et al studied immature pulmonary endothelial cells in the developing lung, where HDAC6 acts as a constitutive negative regulator of TLR4 signaling and maintains baseline homeostasis, whereas Liu et al and Bruhn et al examined acute endotoxin challenge in mature lung tissue, where HDAC6 activity drives inflammatory amplification. , These discrepancies underscore the importance of disease-stage stratification when evaluating epigenetic targets and caution against generalizing findings across different pathological contexts.

## Pro-Inflammatory HATs (p300/CBP)
The inflammatory landscape of ALI is not solely shaped by deacetylases; histone acetyltransferases (HATs) also serve as active amplifiers of pro-inflammatory transcription. p300, one of the most extensively characterized HATs, stabilizes the nuclear receptor RORγt, thereby enhancing Th17 cell differentiation and promoting IL-17-driven inflammatory responses in ARDS. In addition, p300/CBP functions as the catalytic writer for histone lactylation (H3K18la), a modification that bridges glycolytic metabolism to ferroptosis susceptibility and glycocalyx degradation, as discussed in detail in Histone Lactylation. Thus, p300/CBP exerts a predominantly pro-inflammatory role in ALI, acting both as a conventional HAT that acetylates inflammatory gene promoters and as the enzymatic mediator of pathological histone lactylation, thereby positioning p300/CBP as a key mechanistic node in the acetylation–lactylation crosstalk within the epigenetic regulatory network.

## Cytoprotective Sirtuins (SIRT1/3/6)
In contrast to the pro-inflammatory HDACs and p300, members of the Sirtuin family of NAD⁺-dependent deacetylases (including SIRT1, SIRT3, and SIRT6) exert cytoprotective effects by preserving mitochondrial homeostasis and suppressing inflammasome activation. SIRT1 directly deacetylates NF-κB p65, thereby inhibiting nuclear translocation and suppressing pro-inflammatory gene transcription. Additionally, SIRT1 prevents NLRP3 inflammasome assembly by deacetylating NLRP3 itself, reducing caspase-1 activation and downstream pyroptosis in macrophages. SIRT3, localized predominantly in mitochondria, maintains mitochondrial quality control by promoting Parkin-mediated mitophagy and limiting reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release, which trigger inflammasome activation and endothelial cell pyroptosis. SIRT6, on the other hand, suppresses the ERK1/2 pathway and enhances autophagy, thereby preventing apoptosis and tight junction degradation. 
Beyond the Sirtuin family, other HDAC members also contribute to the protective arm of the acetylation regulatory network. Notably, HDAC2, whose expression is frequently downregulated under chronic inflammatory conditions such as prolonged cigarette smoke exposure, plays a critical role in maintaining glucocorticoid responsiveness. Reactivating the HDAC2–Nrf2 axis, for example using the TCM-derived compound andrographolide, has shown promise in restoring glucocorticoid sensitivity and attenuating persistent inflammation. This finding not only highlights the therapeutic diversity within the HDAC/SIRT regulatory network but also provides a mechanistic rationale for integrating TCM-based epigenetic interventions, as further discussed in .
Beyond HDAC3 and HDAC6, other HDAC subfamilies may also participate in ALI pathogenesis. For instance, class IIa HDAC inhibitors such as TMP269 have demonstrated protective effects by reducing phosphorylation of myosin light chains, thereby mitigating endothelial contraction and enhancing vascular barrier function. Although their specific roles in ALI remain less characterized compared to HDAC3/6, these observations suggest that the HDAC superfamily harbors additional therapeutic targets that warrant systematic investigation.
Collectively, the acetylation–deacetylation axis represents a bidirectional regulatory system: selective inhibition of HDAC3/6 combined with pharmacological activation of SIRT1/3/6 may offer a balanced strategy to suppress excessive inflammation while preserving endogenous cytoprotective mechanisms. Meanwhile, targeting the HAT side of this axis, particularly p300/CBP, may provide additional therapeutic leverage, though the broad transcriptional coactivator functions of p300 necessitate careful evaluation of potential off-target effects (see for further discussion).

## Histone Methylation and Demethylation
Histone methylation, occurring predominantly at lysine residues such as H3K4, H3K9, H3K27, and H3K36, exerts context-dependent transcriptional effects through the opposing activities of methyltransferases (“writers”) and demethylases (“erasers”). It is a more stable epigenetic modification compared to acetylation and often exerts long-term effects on gene expression. In ALI/ARDS, dysregulation of histone methylation has been linked to aberrant fibroproliferation, metabolic reprogramming, and inflammatory polarization. 

## EZH2 and the H3K27me3 Repressive Mark
Enhancer of Zeste Homolog 2 (EZH2), the catalytic subunit of Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2), deposits the trimethylation mark H3K27me3 to silence gene transcription. In the context of ARDS-associated pulmonary fibrosis, EZH2 is significantly upregulated and drives fibroproliferative remodeling through two complementary mechanisms. In alveolar macrophages, EZH2 sustains the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype; its inhibition by 3-deazaneplanocin A (3-DZNeP) or siRNA shifts polarization toward the reparative M2 phenotype via the STAT/SOCS pathway and PPAR-γ activation. In alveolar epithelial cells, EZH2 promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) through the TGF-β1/Smad signaling axis; blockade of EZH2 suppresses EMT by downregulating TGF-β1, TGF-βR1, and Smad2 while upregulating the negative regulator Smad7. Together, these dual actions, namely macrophage repolarization and EMT suppression, attenuate collagen deposition and fibroproliferation, positioning EZH2 as a promising therapeutic target for post-ARDS pulmonary fibrosis. 

## SETD2 and H3K36me3 in Metabolic Homeostasis
In contrast to EZH2, the histone methyltransferase SET Domain Containing 2 (SETD2) deposits trimethylation at H3K36 (H3K36me3), a mark canonically associated with active transcription but shown in this context to suppress pro-inflammatory gene expression. In sepsis-induced ALI, SETD2 expression is downregulated in macrophages, leading to reduced H3K36me3 enrichment at the hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) promoter and consequent de-repression of HIF-1α, which in turn promotes glycolytic reprogramming and M1 macrophage polarization. Overexpression of SETD2 restores H3K36me3 at the Hif1a locus, suppresses HIF-1α-driven glycolysis and M1 polarization, and attenuates CLP-induced septic lung injury in mice. 
Notably, EZH2 and SETD2 represent a functionally antagonistic pair in ALI pathogenesis. EZH2 sustains M1 polarization and promotes EMT through TGF-β1/Smad signaling, whereas SETD2 suppresses M1 polarization by restraining HIF-1α-driven glycolysis. This functional antagonism has a well-established biochemical basis: H3K36me3 and H3K27me3 are mutually exclusive on the same histone tail, as H3K36me3 allosterically inhibits PRC2-catalyzed H3K27me3 deposition. The relative balance between SETD2 and EZH2 activity may therefore serve as a key determinant of whether the injured lung progresses toward fibrosis or resolves toward homeostasis, an insight with direct therapeutic implications for timing-based epigenetic interventions. EZH2 inhibitors may be most beneficial during the fibroproliferative phase to limit EMT and collagen deposition, while strategies to maintain SETD2 activity may be crucial during the acute inflammatory phase to prevent excessive M1-driven damage.

## JMJD3 and Context-Dependent H3K27me3 Demethylation
Jumonji domain containing protein 3 (JMJD3/KDM6B), a histone lysine demethylase, removes the repressive H3K27me3 mark and thus generally promotes transcriptional activation. However, the functional outcomes of JMJD3 activity in ALI are highly context-dependent and differ markedly between cell types. The opposing functions of JMJD3 (KDM6B) in different cell types during ALI can be attributed to several layers of context-dependent regulation:
First, cell-type–specific chromatin landscapes determine which genes are de-repressed after H3K27me3 removal. In alveolar epithelial cells, JMJD3 erases H3K27me3 at ferroptosis-related loci, promoting iron dysregulation and lipid peroxidation; JMJD3 inhibition (GSK-J4 or knockdown) preserves H3K27me3, sustains Nrf2-linked antioxidant defenses, and protects barrier integrity. This pro-ferroptotic effect was also confirmed in bronchial epithelial cells, where JMJD3 knockdown reduces ACSL4-dependent ferroptosis and injury. In contrast, in macrophages JMJD3 targets immunomodulatory genes: demethylation at the ADORA2A promoter limits caspase-3–mediated apoptosis and prolongs the inflammatory lifespan of M1 macrophages, while suppression of IL4i1 exacerbates LPS-induced ALI, mediated by coordinated promoter demethylation with dominant H3K4me3 removal. Together, these divergent epithelial (ferroptosis) versus macrophage (survival/immunoregulation) targets explain the cell-type–specific outcomes of JMJD3 activity.
Second, beyond its canonical H3K27me3 demethylation, JMJD3 can also demethylate H3K4me3 at the IL4i1 promoter in macrophages, directly silencing this immunosuppressive program and skewing macrophage polarization toward M1; similar dual-substrate activity has been reported in immune lineages, whereas epithelial cells appear to rely predominantly on H3K27me3 targeting.
Third, transcription-factor context further shapes JMJD3 function. In macrophages, JMJD3 collaborates with NF-κB: it is induced by NF-κB, recruited to NF-κB target promoters after LPS stimulation, and macrophage-specific JMJD3 deletion preserves H3K27me3 at these loci and attenuates inflammatory gene expression in vivo.
In epithelial cells, NF-κB is not the primary transcription factor partner. Instead, Nrf2 serves as the predominant cooperative factor: JMJD3 activity modulates H3K27me3 at Nrf2-regulated oxidative stress and ferroptosis gene loci, steering JMJD3’s regulatory output toward redox homeostasis rather than inflammatory signaling. This cell-type–specific transcription factor partnership provides a molecular explanation for why the same enzyme produces anti-apoptotic/pro-inflammatory effects in macrophages but pro-ferroptotic effects in epithelial cells.
Beyond JMJD3, other histone methyltransferases contribute to vascular remodeling in ALI. SET Domain Bifurcated 1 (SETDB1) and 2 (SETDB2), two H3K9-specific methyltransferases, are implicated in hypoxia-driven endothelial dysfunction: SETDB2 increases pulmonary microvascular endothelial cell (PMVEC) permeability and apoptosis, while SETDB1, upregulated by HIF-2α, induces endothelial-mesenchymal transition and senescence. 
Together, these findings underscore the necessity for cell-type-specific and context-dependent therapeutic strategies when targeting histone methylation in ALI: JMJD3 inhibition may be beneficial in alveolar epithelial cells for anti-ferroptosis protection, but could be detrimental in macrophages by prolonging M1 polarization and delaying inflammation resolution. , More broadly, the histone methylation landscape in ALI is shaped by functionally diverse and sometimes antagonistic enzymes: EZH2 sustains M1 polarization and promotes EMT, SETDB1/2 drive hypoxia-induced vascular remodeling, , SETD2 exerts a protective metabolic function by restraining HIF-1α, and JMJD3 produces dichotomous effects depending on its cellular environment. Phase-specific and cell-targeted delivery approaches (eg, macrophage-specific or epithelial-specific nanocarrier systems) will be critical for translating these methylation-directed therapies into clinical practice.

## Histone Lactylation
Histone lactylation, the covalent attachment of lactyl groups derived from lactate to lysine residues on histones, is a recently discovered member of a growing family of metabolically driven acylation modifications, including succinylation, malonylation, and benzoylation, that collectively bridge cellular metabolism with epigenetic regulation. Among these, lactylation has attracted particular attention in ALI/ARDS research because of its direct mechanistic links to glycolysis-driven inflammatory reprogramming, ferroptosis susceptibility, and endothelial glycocalyx degradation. 

## H3K18 Lactylation (H3K18la) and Ferroptosis
Lactate, the end product of glycolysis, accumulates to high concentrations in inflamed tissues and serves as the substrate for histone acetyltransferases p300 and CBP (p300/CBP)-catalyzed histone lactylation. In sepsis-induced ALI, elevated glycolytic flux in macrophages and alveolar epithelial cells leads to a marked increase in H3K18la at the promoters of ferroptosis-related genes, including solute carrier family 40 member 1 (SLC40A1, encoding ferroportin) and acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4). , H3K18la deposition at these loci promotes their transcriptional activation, thereby increasing iron export via SLC40A1 and enhancing lipid peroxidation via ACSL4, both of which are critical steps in the execution of ferroptotic cell death. , Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of glycolysis (eg, using hexokinase 2 inhibitors) or genetic suppression of lactylation-writing enzymes reduces H3K18la levels and significantly attenuates alveolar epithelial ferroptosis and lung injury. 

## H3K14 Lactylation (H3K14la) and Glycocalyx Degradation
The endothelial glycocalyx, a carbohydrate-rich layer on the luminal surface of vascular endothelium, serves as a critical barrier to inflammatory cell adhesion and microvascular thrombosis. In ALI, elevated H3K14la at the heparanase (HPA) promoter drives transcriptional upregulation of HPA, a key glycocalyx-degrading enzyme. HPA-mediated cleavage of heparan sulfate chains disrupts the structural integrity of the glycocalyx, increasing endothelial permeability and promoting neutrophil extravasation. Notably, the active ingredient composition of Jinshui Huanxian Formula (ECC-JHF) has been shown to suppress the SRC/HK2 signaling pathway, thereby reducing lactate-driven H3K14la at the HPA promoter and preserving glycocalyx integrity (see for detailed discussion). 

## Stage-Dependent Acetylation–Lactylation Crosstalk at H3K18
The competitive interplay between lactylation and acetylation at shared lysine residues, particularly H3K18, is dynamically regulated during ALI progression and can be conceptualized as a stage-dependent “metabolic-epigenetic switch”. In the early hyperinflammatory phase of ALI, the glycolytic burst in activated macrophages and neutrophils generates a rapid surge of intracellular lactate, which serves as the substrate for p300/CBP-catalyzed H3K18 lactylation (H3K18la). , This modification displaces acetylation at the same residue, promoting the transcription of pro-ferroptotic genes (eg, SLC40A1, ACSL4) and M1 polarization-associated inflammatory mediators. , Concurrently, elevated lactate and the associated increase in NADH/NAD⁺ ratio inhibit SIRT1 deacetylase activity, further disrupting the acetylation–lactylation equilibrium and creating a feed-forward inflammatory loop. , 
As the disease transitions to the resolution or fibroproliferative phase, declining glycolytic flux reduces lactate availability, while restoration of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation gradually replenishes the NAD⁺ pool, reactivating SIRT-mediated deacetylation and facilitating the re-establishment of H3K18 acetylation (H3K18ac). Additionally, class I HDACs (HDAC1-3) have been identified as histone lysine delactylases, capable of removing lactyl marks and thus actively contributing to the temporal clearance of lactylation during disease resolution. This biphasic shift, characterized by H3K18la dominance during the acute inflammatory phase and progressive H3K18ac re-establishment during resolution, suggests that the H3K18la/H3K18ac ratio may serve not only as an epigenetic indicator of ALI phase transition but also as a potential therapeutic target: early intervention strategies aimed at suppressing glycolysis (eg, HK2 inhibition) and restoring NAD⁺-dependent deacetylation may be most effective during the initial inflammatory phase, while later stages may benefit from combinatory approaches that consolidate the acetylation–lactylation rebalancing. , 
Together, these findings establish histone lactylation as a metabolic-epigenetic bridge that translates the cellular energetic state into pathological transcriptional outputs, with therapeutic implications for both metabolic targeting and epigenetic modulation. Several mechanistic questions nonetheless remain open: the lactylation machinery in ALI operates through well-characterized writers (p300/CBP) and erasers (class I HDACs), but the identity of downstream reader proteins has yet to be established in inflammatory contexts. Notably, Brg1, the catalytic subunit of the SWI/SNF chromatin-remodeling complex, has recently been identified as the first reader of H3K18la in iPSC reprogramming models and is recruited to lactylated promoters to activate downstream transcription. Whether Brg1 or analogous readers fulfill comparable roles in the ALI microenvironment represents an important direction for future investigation.

## Histone Citrullination and NET Formation
Histone citrullination, the conversion of arginine residues to citrulline by peptidyl arginine deiminases (PADs), represents a unique form of histone modification that is intimately linked to neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation and immunothrombosis in ALI/ARDS. 

## PAD4-Mediated Histone H3 Citrullination (CitH3) and NETosis
PAD4, a calcium-dependent enzyme predominantly expressed in neutrophils, catalyzes histone H3 citrullination at arginine residues (eg, R2, R8, R17), which promotes chromatin decondensation and the release of web-like structures composed of DNA, histones, and antimicrobial proteins that are collectively termed NETs. , In the context of ALI, excessive NET formation contributes to alveolar damage, microvascular occlusion, and systemic inflammation. , 

## CitH3-Driven Positive Feedback Loop
Once released into the extracellular space, CitH3 acts as a potent DAMP that can re-stimulate neutrophils and macrophages via TLR2 and TLR4 signaling, thereby establishing a self-amplifying inflammatory circuit. , Specifically, extracellular CitH3 activates TLR2 on neutrophils, triggering intracellular calcium influx and upregulating PAD2 expression. PAD2, in turn, further citrullinates intracellular histones, thereby priming additional rounds of NETosis, a phenomenon termed the “CitH3–PAD2 positive feedback loop”. , , This feed-forward mechanism perpetuates NET formation, escalates pulmonary inflammation, and exacerbates immune-mediated thrombosis.
Although the temporal peak of CitH3/NETs formation appears to coincide with maximal HDAC3 activation and SIRT1 suppression in the early inflammatory phase of ALI, a direct causal relationship has yet to be established through synchronized kinetic profiling. , The existing evidence supports a model of mechanistic convergence in which HDAC3-driven pyroptosis and SIRT1 inhibition amplify the inflammatory milieu that promotes neutrophil activation and NETosis, while CitH3 released during NETosis may reciprocally reinforce intranuclear pro-inflammatory programs via TLR4-mediated signaling. , , , Future studies employing temporally resolved, multi-parameter analyses (eg, simultaneous measurement of CitH3 kinetics, HDAC3/SIRT1 activity, and inflammatory mediators in the same ALI model) will be essential to establish definitive causal links and to determine whether targeting one arm of this circuit can disrupt the entire cascade.
From a translational standpoint, circulating CitH3 levels correlated strongly with ARDS severity, multi-organ dysfunction scores, and patient mortality, underscoring CitH3 as both a pathological effector and a clinically tractable biomarker. Beyond its role in the CitH3–PAD2 amplification loop, CitH3 functions as a central integrator of thromboinflammatory injury in the pulmonary microcirculation: NETs, composed of DNA–histone scaffolds coated with myeloperoxidase (MPO) and neutrophil elastase, directly stimulate endothelial cells to amplify thromboinflammatory responses. , Several co-regulatory mechanisms further reinforce this pathological axis. First, NADPH oxidase (Nox2) generates reactive oxygen species that coordinate platelet–neutrophil interactions and promote NET accumulation through redox-dependent signaling. Second, the Mac-1 integrin (CD11b/CD18) amplifies histone-induced NETosis; accordingly, Mac-1 blockade significantly attenuates LPS-induced pulmonary damage in preclinical models. Third, extracellular histones activate the complement cascade, generating C3a, which increases pulmonary vascular permeability and exacerbates endothelial dysfunction, highlighting a synergistic role of NETs and complement in propagating vascular injury. Together, these mechanisms position CitH3-driven NETosis as a convergence point for oxidative, adhesive, and complement-mediated injury in ALI.

## Therapeutic Targeting of the PAD4–CitH3 Axis
Given the central role of PAD4 in driving NET formation and the downstream pathological consequences of CitH3 release, PAD4 inhibitors such as Cl-amidine and GSK484 have been extensively investigated in preclinical ALI models. These inhibitors effectively reduce CitH3 levels, suppress NET formation, and attenuate lung injury and mortality in sepsis and pneumonia models. , Importantly, different TCM formulations converge on PAD4 inhibition through distinct upstream mechanisms, exemplifying TCM’s characteristic multi-pathway pharmacology (discussed in detail in ).
In summary, histone citrullination establishes a self-amplifying pathological axis that links PAD4-driven chromatin decondensation to extracellular CitH3 release, NET-mediated thromboinflammation, and multi-organ dysfunction. Targeted disruption of this axis by means of PAD inhibition, CitH3 neutralization, or NET degradation represents a promising therapeutic strategy, the translational dimensions of which are further explored in .

## Extracellular Histones as Damage-Associated Molecular Patterns (DAMPs)
Histones, once released from dying or activated cells into the extracellular space, transition from their canonical nuclear function as DNA-packaging proteins to potent damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that drive systemic inflammation, endothelial injury, and immunothrombosis. This dual identity of histones, as intracellular transcriptional regulators and extracellular pro-inflammatory mediators, represents a critical pathological axis in ALI/ARDS.

## Sources and Mechanisms of Histone Release

## Pyroptotic and Necrotic Cell Death
During ALI, multiple forms of lytic cell death, including pyroptosis, necroptosis, and necrosis, result in the passive release of nuclear contents, including histones and nucleosomes, into the extracellular milieu. Pyroptosis, driven by gasdermin D (GSDMD) pore formation following inflammasome activation, is a particularly prominent source of extracellular histones in sepsis-induced ALI. The HDAC3-cGAS-STING-NLRP3 axis, as detailed in , not only drives pyroptotic macrophage death but also ensures the concomitant release of citrullinated and lactylated histones that carry additional pro-inflammatory signaling capacity. , , 

## Neutrophil Extracellular Trap (NET) Formation
NETs represent an active mechanism of histone externalization wherein activated neutrophils expel web-like structures composed of decondensed chromatin decorated with histones (particularly CitH3), neutrophil elastase, and myeloperoxidase. , As discussed in , PAD4-mediated histone citrullination is the key initiating event in NET formation. Once released, NET-associated histones not only retain their cytotoxic properties but also serve as scaffolds for the assembly of prothrombotic complexes involving platelets, coagulation factors, and von Willebrand factor. , 

## Platelet Activation and Histone Secretion
Activated platelets release α-granule contents during systemic inflammation and interact bidirectionally with extracellular histones, contributing to the circulating histone pool and promoting endothelial dysfunction and microvascular thrombosis. 

## Pathological Effects of Extracellular Histones

## TLR4-Mediated Inflammatory Signaling
Extracellular histones, particularly H3 and H4, are recognized by Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2) on the surface of macrophages, endothelial cells, and platelets, thereby initiating downstream inflammatory cascades. , , Upon binding to TLR4, extracellular histones activate the MyD88-NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to the transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β). Importantly, the predominant circulating form of extracellular histones in critically ill patients is the nucleosome complex (a DNA-wrapped histone octamer) rather than isolated histone proteins. Nucleosomes themselves act as functional DAMPs, inducing prolonged expression of IL-1β and TNF-α, and amplifying lung inflammation. Distinguishing between free histones and nucleosome complexes is thus essential for both mechanistic studies and biomarker development, given their differing stability, immunogenicity, and receptor interactions.

## Endothelial Barrier Disruption
Extracellular histones directly compromise endothelial barrier integrity through multiple mechanisms. First, histones bind to phospholipids on the endothelial cell membrane, disrupting lipid bilayer organization and increasing membrane permeability. Second, histone exposure activates endothelial cell death pathways, including apoptosis and necroptosis, leading to cellular detachment and vascular leakage. Third, histones promote the internalization and degradation of VE-cadherin, a critical adherens junction protein, thereby disrupting intercellular junctions and increasing paracellular permeability. H4 activates heparanase, which degrades the heparan sulfate glycocalyx, perturbs the Ang/Tie2 signaling axis, and promotes syndecan-1 shedding, culminating in glycocalyx destruction and increased alveolar–capillary leakage. , 

## Immunothrombosis and Microvascular Occlusion
Extracellular histones are potent inducers of immunothrombosis, a process characterized by the coordinated activation of platelets, neutrophils, and the coagulation cascade within inflamed microvessels. Histones directly activate platelets by binding to TLR2 and TLR4 on the platelet surface, triggering intracellular calcium mobilization, degranulation, and expression of P-selectin and activated glycoprotein IIb/IIIa. Activated platelets aggregate at sites of NET deposition, forming microthrombi that occlude pulmonary capillaries and exacerbate ventilation-perfusion mismatch. Furthermore, histones activate the contact pathway of coagulation by promoting factor XII (FXII) activation on negatively charged NET-DNA scaffolds. Histones also inhibit the anticoagulant protein C pathway and promote tissue factor (TF) expression on monocytes and endothelial cells, thereby shifting the hemostatic balance toward a prothrombotic state. , The convergence of NET-associated CitH3, platelet activation, and coagulation cascade amplification creates a self-sustaining immunothrombotic circuit that is a hallmark of severe ALI/ARDS. This circuit not only contributes to acute respiratory failure but also increases the risk of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and multi-organ dysfunction. , This TLR4-driven signaling simultaneously creates a positive feedback loop in which inflammatory cytokines further activate neutrophils and macrophages, promoting additional pyroptosis and NET formation, which in turn releases more histones into the circulation, a pathological dynamic further amplified by concurrent HDAC3 hyperactivity and SIRT1 suppression in the nuclear compartment, as discussed in Histone Citrullination and NET Formation.

## Histone Citrullination and NETosis-Driven Thrombo-Inflammatory Axis
In contrast to unmodified H3/H4, citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3) serves as both a biochemical hallmark of NETosis and a central driver of immune thrombosis, orchestrating a neutrophil-centered cascade of inflammation, coagulation, and tissue injury. 

## Generation of CitH3 and NET Formation
CitH3 is generated via peptidylarginine deiminases (PADs), predominantly PAD4, the rate-limiting enzyme for NETosis, with PAD2 contributing notably in sepsis. Genetic ablation or pharmacologic inhibition of PAD4 (eg, using TDFA) mitigates LPS-induced ALI, curbs NF-κB nuclear translocation, and enhances survival. Both broad-spectrum PAD inhibitors and selective PAD2 antagonists reduce CitH3 formation, suppress NET generation, and markedly improve outcomes in lethal endotoxemia models. 

## CitH3-Driven Positive Feedback Amplification
CitH3 is not merely a downstream byproduct of NETosis but an active amplifier of inflammation. In macrophages and dendritic cells, CitH3 triggers the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to caspase-1 activation and substantial release of IL-1β and IL-18, worsening pulmonary damage. CitH3 also stimulates TLR2 signaling, promoting calcium-dependent PAD2 activation and nuclear translocation, thereby establishing a self-reinforcing loop in which CitH3 promotes PAD2 activation, leading to further CitH3 generation. Application of anti-CitH3 monoclonal antibodies breaks this cycle, offering significant protection in septic ALI. , , 

## Transcellular Amplification: Macrophage–Neutrophil Crosstalk
Beyond the intrinsic CitH3–PAD2 loop, extracellular histones also induce M1 polarization and cathepsin C release from alveolar macrophages, which further triggers NETosis through activation of the p38 MAPK/NADPH oxidase/reactive oxygen species (ROS) axis, establishing a transcellular amplification loop involving extracellular histones, macrophages, and neutrophils. Several co-regulatory mechanisms further reinforce NET accumulation within the pulmonary microvasculature. NADPH oxidase (Nox2) generates reactive oxygen species that coordinate redox-dependent signaling between platelets and neutrophils, promoting NET deposition. The Mac-1 integrin (CD11b/CD18) amplifies extracellular histone-induced NETosis; accordingly, Mac-1 blockade substantially alleviates LPS-induced lung injury in preclinical models. Additionally, NET-associated histones activate the complement cascade, generating C3a, which increases pulmonary vascular permeability and exacerbates endothelial dysfunction, underscoring a synergistic role of NETs and complement in propagating vascular injury. In severe viral ALI (eg, COVID-19), persistent NET deposition, tissue factor upregulation, and thrombomodulin loss reflect a sustained inflammatory-coagulatory coupling strongly associated with alveolar-capillary failure and multi-organ dysfunction. , 

## Translational Therapeutic Perspectives
These mechanisms represent actionable therapeutic targets. Humanized anti-CitH3 monoclonal antibodies improve oxygenation, reduce myeloperoxidase (MPO) infiltration, and suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activation, thereby supporting a therapeutic strategy that links anti-CitH3 neutralization to NET inhibition and lung protection. PAD4 inhibitors act upstream by suppressing CitH3 production and preventing NETosis at its source. In parallel, strategies for extracellular histone clearance, such as heparin, recombinant thrombomodulin (rTM), and polyanionic agents including suramin, attenuate vascular leakage and pulmonary edema through electrostatic neutralization and preservation of the endothelial glycocalyx. Emerging approaches, such as RNA aptamers and biomimetic nanoparticle-based delivery systems, enable more localized and tissue-specific neutralization of circulating histones. , , , , Taken together, the CitH3/PAD/NETosis axis represents a pivotal and therapeutically tractable pathway in ALI/ARDS, and the translational dimensions of these strategies are further explored in .

## Circulating Histone Levels as Biomarkers
Given the central pathological role of extracellular histones in ALI/ARDS, circulating histone levels, particularly CitH3 and nucleosomes, have been explored as potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. Multiple clinical studies have shown that elevated serum CitH3 levels at ICU admission are independently associated with increased mortality, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and a higher incidence of multi-organ failure in patients with sepsis-induced ARDS. Similarly, circulating nucleosome levels correlate with the severity of lung injury as quantified by the PaO2/FiO2 ratio and chest imaging scores. Importantly, serial measurements of serum CitH3 during the course of ALI/ARDS may provide dynamic information regarding disease trajectory and therapeutic response. Patients whose CitH3 levels decline during treatment exhibit better clinical outcomes, whereas persistently elevated or rising CitH3 levels portend a poor prognosis. These observations suggest that circulating CitH3 could serve as a real-time biomarker to guide the timing and intensity of histone-targeted interventions, as discussed in .
Synthesizing the mechanisms detailed in and ), illustrates the integrated pathological framework of ALI/ARDS as a tripartite “Nuclear Epigenetic Remodeling–Extracellular DAMP Amplification Circuit.” Within the intracellular compartment (Left panel), dysregulated epigenetic modifications—spanning acetylation, methylation, and lactylation—cooperatively reshape the transcriptional landscape, driving inflammatory polarization, metabolic reprogramming, and barrier dysfunction. Crucially, PAD4-mediated histone citrullination (Middle panel) acts as the critical mechanistic bridge; by driving chromatin decondensation and facilitating the active release of nuclear contents, it physically exports this intracellular epigenetic chaos into the microenvironment. Once externalized (Right panel), these modified histones (CitH3) and associated DNA webs act as potent DAMPs, igniting a TLR4-dependent thrombo-inflammatory cascade that culminates in extensive glycocalyx degradation and microvascular occlusion. Ultimately, this sequential progression emphasizes a key conceptual shift: nuclear epigenetic alterations and extracellular DAMP signaling are not isolated functional domains but form a continuous, inside-out pathological cascade. This systemic amplification trajectory not only dictates disease severity and biomarker kinetics but also provides a rational roadmap for the multi-target therapeutic interventions discussed in the subsequent sections.
 

## Therapeutic Strategies Targeting Histone Modifications
The dual pathological role of histones as nuclear transcriptional regulators and extracellular DAMPs provides multiple therapeutic intervention points. Aligning with the inside-out pathological cascade established in ), these strategies can be categorized into three major tiers: (i) intranuclear epigenetic modulation, (ii) inhibition of histone externalization and NET formation, and (iii) neutralization of circulating histones. This section systematically reviews preclinical and emerging clinical evidence for each therapeutic tier.

## Tier 1: Intranuclear Epigenetic Modification

## HDAC Inhibitors
Selective inhibition of pro-inflammatory HDACs, particularly HDAC3 and HDAC6, has emerged as a promising strategy to restore transcriptional homeostasis and suppress inflammatory gene expression in ALI/ARDS.
RGFP966, a highly selective HDAC3 inhibitor, has been extensively validated in preclinical ALI models. In LPS-induced ALI, RGFP966 administration suppresses the cGAS-STING-NLRP3 axis, reduces macrophage pyroptosis, and attenuates pulmonary edema and inflammatory cell infiltration. , Mechanistically, HDAC3 inhibition prevents the deacetylation of histones at the promoters of anti-inflammatory genes, thereby facilitating their transcriptional activation and resolving the inflammatory milieu. RGFP966 has also been shown to preserve alveolar epithelial tight junction integrity by blocking TGF-β1/Smad3-driven epithelial-mesenchymal transition. 
Tubastatin A and ACY-1215 are selective HDAC6 inhibitors that have demonstrated efficacy in hemorrhagic shock and endotoxin-induced ALI models. Tubastatin A enhances tubulin acetylation, suppresses Rho-associated kinase (ROCK) signaling, and strengthens endothelial barrier function, thereby reducing vascular permeability and pulmonary edema. , , ACY-1215 similarly attenuates inflammatory cytokine production and neutrophil infiltration in LPS-challenged mice. Notably, however, HDAC6 inhibition may exacerbate inflammation via the TLR4–MyD88 pathway under certain conditions, underscoring the context-dependency of this approach and the need for careful patient stratification in future clinical translation. 
Pan-HDAC inhibitors such as suberoylanilide hydroxamic acid (SAHA/vorinostat) and trichostatin A (TSA) have also been tested in ALI models. While these agents demonstrate broad anti-inflammatory effects, their lack of selectivity raises concerns about potential off-target toxicity, particularly the unintended inhibition of the cytoprotective SIRT family members. , Of note, TMP269, a class IIa HDAC inhibitor with a more restricted isoform profile, has shown the ability to reduce LPS-induced endothelial hyperpermeability, suggesting that intermediate selectivity strategies may offer a compromise between efficacy and safety. Therefore, selective HDAC3/6 inhibitors are generally preferred for ALI/ARDS applications, though the optimal isoform-targeting strategy may ultimately depend on disease phase and inflammatory context.

## SIRT Activators
Pharmacological activation of cytoprotective Sirtuins, particularly SIRT1, SIRT3, and SIRT6, represents a complementary strategy to HDAC inhibition. 
Resveratrol, a natural polyphenol and SIRT1 activator, has been shown to suppress NLRP3 inflammasome activation by promoting SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of NLRP3, thereby reducing caspase-1 activation and IL-1β secretion in sepsis-induced ALI. Resveratrol also enhances mitochondrial biogenesis and attenuates oxidative stress through SIRT1-PGC-1α signaling, providing additional cytoprotective benefits. SRT1720, a more potent synthetic SIRT1 activator, similarly attenuates alveolar epithelial apoptosis and preserves barrier function in LPS-induced ALI models. 
Honokiol, a SIRT3 activator derived from Magnolia officinalis, promotes mitochondrial quality control by enhancing SIRT3-mediated deacetylation of mitophagy regulators such as PINK1 and Parkin. In sepsis models, honokiol treatment reduces alveolar epithelial ferroptosis, preserves mitochondrial membrane potential, and attenuates lung injury. 
SIRT6, an NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase with roles in genome stability and metabolic regulation, has been shown to enhance autophagy, inhibit ERK1/2 signaling, and preserve endothelial tight junction integrity, thereby reducing apoptosis and vascular leakage in ALI models. Accordingly, compounds that upregulate SIRT6 activity represent an emerging avenue for barrier-protective intervention in ALI/ARDS. 
NAD⁺ precursors such as nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) and nicotinamide riboside (NR) boost intracellular NAD⁺ levels, thereby enhancing the enzymatic activity of all NAD⁺-dependent Sirtuins. NMN supplementation has been shown to attenuate LPS-induced ALI by reactivating SIRT1 and SIRT6, suppressing NF-κB signaling, and reducing inflammatory cytokine production. , 
Collectively, these findings suggest that combining SIRT activators with selective HDAC inhibitors may offer synergistic benefits by simultaneously restoring anti-inflammatory deacetylation, supporting mitochondrial homeostasis, and preserving barrier integrity—a multi-target approach warranting further validation in vivo. Notably, certain TCM compounds such as andrographolide appear to converge on the SIRT/HDAC axis through distinct upstream mechanisms, suggesting potential avenues for integrative pharmacological strategies, as discussed in detail in . 

## Histone Methylation Modulators
Targeting the histone methylation machinery offers stage-specific therapeutic opportunities, particularly during the fibroproliferative phase of ALI/ARDS.
GSK126 and EPZ-6438 (tazemetostat) are selective EZH2 inhibitors that have been repurposed for ALI/ARDS therapy. In bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis models, GSK126 administration prevents H3K27me3 accumulation at anti-fibrotic gene promoters, shifts macrophage polarization from the pro-fibrotic M2 to the anti-fibrotic M1 phenotype, and attenuates collagen deposition and lung stiffness. Importantly, EZH2 inhibition appears most effective when initiated during the early fibroproliferative phase (days 7–14 post-injury), highlighting the importance of therapeutic timing. , 
GSK-J4, a selective JMJD3 inhibitor, has shown efficacy in blocking alveolar epithelial ferroptosis in LPS-induced ALI by preserving H3K27me3 at ferroptosis-promoting loci and maintaining Nrf2 antioxidant pathway activity. However, as discussed in Histone Methylation and Demethylation, the cell-type–specific effects of JMJD3 necessitate targeted delivery strategies: epithelial-targeted JMJD3 inhibition may be beneficial, whereas systemic or macrophage-targeted inhibition could prolong M1 polarization and delay resolution. 

## p300/CBP Inhibitors
As established in , p300/CBP serves a dual enzymatic role in ALI/ARDS: it catalyzes both conventional H3K27 acetylation and lactate-driven H3K18 lactylation (H3K18la), positioning it as a convergence point between inflammatory transcription and metabolic-epigenetic reprogramming. A-485, a selective p300/CBP inhibitor, exploits this dual role by simultaneously reducing H3K27ac and H3K18la levels: the former destabilizes RORγt and limits secretion of Th17-related inflammatory mediators (IL-17A and IL-22), while the latter attenuates H3K18la-driven transcription of pro-ferroptotic genes such as ACSL4 and SLC40A1, thereby reducing alveolar epithelial ferroptosis susceptibility. , , This dual suppression of both the acetylation-dependent inflammatory axis and the lactylation-dependent ferroptotic axis distinguishes p300/CBP inhibition from the more upstream metabolic interventions (eg, HK2 inhibition) discussed in , offering an epigenetically targeted alternative that does not require global glycolytic suppression. However, as p300 acts as a coactivator for multiple transcription factors, its systemic inhibition may result in broad off-target effects, necessitating close immune monitoring and, ideally, cell-type–specific delivery strategies in clinical settings.

## Tier 2: Inhibition of Histone Externalization and NET Formation
The positive feedback loop between CitH3 and NET formation plays a pivotal role in propagating inflammation and immunothrombosis in ALI/ARDS. , Targeting this mechanistic bridge offers clear and mechanistically grounded interventions to prevent the export of intracellular pathological signals.

## PAD Inhibitors
PAD4 inhibitors represent a direct approach to blocking histone citrullination and NET formation.
Cl-amidine, a pan-PAD inhibitor, and GSK484, a selective PAD4 inhibitor, have both demonstrated efficacy in reducing CitH3 levels and NET formation in sepsis-induced ALI models. , In a cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) model of septic ALI, GSK484 administration significantly reduced serum CitH3 levels, attenuated neutrophil infiltration, decreased pulmonary microvascular thrombosis, and improved survival. Mechanistically, PAD4 inhibition prevents chromatin decondensation and blocks the expulsion of NET structures, thereby limiting both the direct cytotoxic effects of NETs and the release of NET-associated histones into the circulation. , 
BB-Cl-amidine is a second-generation pan-PAD inhibitor with improved pharmacokinetic properties, including better cell permeability and a longer half-life than Cl-amidine. It has shown enhanced efficacy in reducing NET burden and improving outcomes in murine models of ALI and sepsis. 
TDFA, a highly selective PAD4 inhibitor, effectively reduces CitH3 levels and NET formation and has been shown to improve survival across multiple preclinical ALI and endotoxemia models. 
Genetic evidence further corroborates these pharmacological findings: PAD4 knockout animals exhibited markedly reduced organ dysfunction following septic challenge, confirming PAD4’s central and non-redundant role in NETosis-driven injury. Notably, PAD4 inhibitors also exert NET-independent protective effects, as demonstrated in models of ischemic brain injury, suggesting that their therapeutic benefits may extend beyond direct suppression of NET formation. 

## Anti-CitH3 Antibodies
Monoclonal antibodies targeting citrullinated histone H3 offer a strategy to neutralize extracellular CitH3 after its release, thereby interrupting the CitH3-driven positive feedback loop. Humanized antibodies such as CIT-013 directly neutralize circulating CitH3, preventing its binding to TLR2/TLR4 on neutrophils and macrophages and thereby disrupting the re-stimulation of PAD2-mediated citrullination. Beyond ALI, this antibody class has also demonstrated efficacy in smoke inhalation–induced lung injury and in autoimmune settings such as systemic lupus erythematosus, where it reduces both local inflammation and NET burden. , This approach has the advantage of specifically targeting the pathological citrullinated form of histone H3 while sparing the physiological functions of non-citrullinated histones, a high-specificity profile that positions anti-CitH3 antibodies as viable candidates for clinical translation.

## DNase I
Recombinant human DNase I (dornase alfa) degrades the DNA backbone of NETs, thereby disassembling NET structures and reducing their pro-inflammatory and prothrombotic effects. In preclinical models of severe pneumonia and sepsis-induced ALI, aerosolized or intravenous DNase I administration reduces NET burden in the airways and circulation, improves pulmonary compliance, and enhances bacterial clearance. However, DNase I does not prevent the release of histones or neutralize their cytotoxic properties; thus, its efficacy may be limited in settings where circulating histone levels are very high. Combination strategies pairing DNase I with histone-neutralizing agents may offer synergistic benefits. 
Beyond these direct NET-targeting approaches, indirect upstream strategies provide complementary avenues for reducing NET deposition within the pulmonary microcirculation. As described in Transcellular Amplification: Macrophage–Neutrophil Crosstalk, blockade of the Mac-1 integrin (CD11b/CD18) attenuates histone-induced NETosis and alleviates LPS-driven lung injury, while antagonism of the C3a receptor (C3aR) mitigates complement-mediated vascular permeability and endothelial dysfunction downstream of NET activation. Incorporating such indirect strategies alongside PAD inhibitors, anti-CitH3 antibodies, and DNase I may thus enable a more comprehensive suppression of the NET-driven thrombo-inflammatory cascade in ALI/ARDS.

## Tier 3: Neutralization of Circulating Histones
Throughout the course of ALI/ARDS, circulating histones continuously drive endothelial injury, coagulation activation, and inflammatory amplification. Rapid neutralization or clearance of these DAMPs offers a direct therapeutic avenue that bypasses dependence on specific upstream triggers and is therefore applicable across diverse etiologies of ALI/ARDS.

## Heparin and Heparin-Based Therapeutics
Heparin, a polyanionic glycosaminoglycan, binds to positively charged histones with high affinity, thereby neutralizing their cytotoxic effects. In vitro studies have demonstrated that heparin forms stable complexes with extracellular histones, preventing histone binding to endothelial cells and platelets, and blocking histone-induced cytotoxicity. In vivo, prophylactic or therapeutic administration of unfractionated heparin (UFH) or low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) reduces circulating histone-mediated endothelial damage and improves survival in sepsis-induced ALI models. 
Beyond its anticoagulant properties, heparin exerts histone-neutralizing effects at concentrations lower than those required for anticoagulation, suggesting a potential role for non-anticoagulant heparin derivatives in ALI/ARDS therapy. Non-anticoagulant heparins, which retain histone-binding capacity while lacking anticoagulant activity, are being developed to mitigate bleeding risk in critically ill patients. 

## Recombinant Thrombomodulin (rTM)
Recombinant thrombomodulin (rTM) is an anticoagulant protein that not only activates protein C but also directly binds to and neutralizes extracellular histones. , In sepsis-induced ALI models, rTM administration reduces histone-mediated endothelial injury, attenuates pulmonary inflammation, and improves survival. , Importantly, the histone-neutralizing effect of rTM is independent of its anticoagulant function, as demonstrated using rTM mutants lacking protein C activation capacity. This dual mechanism of anticoagulation and histone neutralization positions rTM as a particularly attractive therapeutic candidate for ALI/ARDS with concurrent coagulopathy. 
Clinical trials of rTM in sepsis-associated DIC have shown promising safety profiles, and post-hoc analyses suggest potential respiratory benefits in patients with concomitant ALI/ARDS. Prospective trials specifically evaluating rTM in ARDS are warranted.

## Poly-Anionic Drugs and Aptamers
Suramin, a multi-anionic compound, neutralizes circulating histones and protects the endothelium. Similarly, inhalable anti-histone RNA aptamers offer targeted lung protection with reduced systemic exposure, enhancing both safety and efficacy. 

## Biomimetic Nanomaterials
Biomimetic nanocarriers, including platelet membrane-coated and red blood cell membrane-coated nanoparticles, have emerged as innovative platforms for histone neutralization. These nanocarriers mimic the surface charge and lipid composition of native cell membranes, enabling them to bind extracellular histones with high affinity and sequester them away from vulnerable endothelial and epithelial cells. In sepsis-induced ALI models, administration of platelet membrane-coated nanoparticles significantly reduces circulating histone levels, attenuates endothelial dysfunction, and improves survival. Importantly, these nanocarriers can be further functionalized to deliver therapeutic payloads (eg, anti-inflammatory drugs, antioxidants, or siRNA targeting pro-inflammatory genes) directly to sites of inflammation, providing a dual benefit of histone neutralization and targeted drug delivery. This platform represents a promising next-generation approach to managing histone-driven pathology in ALI/ARDS.
In summary, neutralizing extracellular histones provides a rapid, phase-independent therapeutic option that cuts off DAMP-driven inflammatory amplification without relying on transcriptional reprogramming or immune cell–intrinsic interventions. When combined with intranuclear epigenetic modulation (Tier 1) and inhibition of histone externalization and NET formation (Tier 2), this strategy completes a comprehensive, multi-level therapeutic framework. By simultaneously targeting upstream epigenetic drivers and downstream DAMP amplification, this intervention three-tiered approach systematically dismantles the inside-out pathological cascade, bridging immediate attenuation of acute vascular injury with longer-term resolution of lung inflammation and remodeling.

## Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) Interventions Targeting Histone Modifications in ALI/ARDS
Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), with its multi-component, multi-target, and systems-level regulatory philosophy, has been increasingly explored as a source of epigenetically active interventions capable of modulating histone modifications and their downstream pathological cascades in ALI/ARDS. Unlike single-target synthetic drugs, TCM formulations contain dozens to hundreds of bioactive compounds that can simultaneously intervene at multiple nodes within the inside-out pathological cascade, spanning the “nuclear epigenetic remodeling–extracellular DAMP amplification” circuit. , This section reviews mechanistic evidence for TCM interventions across three primary pathways: (i) modulation of the HDAC/SIRT acetylation axis, (ii) suppression of the PAD4–CitH3–NETs cascade, and (iii) regulation of lactylation-linked metabolic pathways.

## Modulation of the HDAC/SIRT Acetylation Axis
As shown in Pathway 1 of , TCM-derived compounds such as andrographolide, curcumin, and quercetin have been reported to converge functionally on the HDAC2/SIRT1 regulatory hub to restore acetylation homeostasis and suppress inflammatory gene transcription.
 Andrographolide: Andrographolide, the primary bioactive diterpene lactone derived from Andrographis paniculata, has been shown to upregulate HDAC2 expression and enhance its nuclear localization in cigarette smoke-exposed bronchial epithelial cells and macrophages. Mechanistically, andrographolide activates the Nrf2 signaling pathway, which has been linked to increased HDAC2 expression (including promoter-level regulation in some systems). Elevated HDAC2, in turn, deacetylates NF-κB p65 at lysine 310, thereby suppressing NF-κB-driven transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α. This HDAC2–Nrf2 axis reactivation not only attenuates baseline inflammation but also has been associated with restored glucocorticoid sensitivity in corticosteroid-resistant inflammatory models, providing a strong mechanistic rationale for exploring andrographolide-corticosteroid combination strategies in refractory ARDS. In LPS-induced ALI models, andrographolide administration reduces pulmonary neutrophil infiltration and decreases bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) protein concentration (a marker of alveolar-capillary barrier disruption). Importantly, studies using HDAC2 loss-of-function approaches suggest that HDAC2 is a key mediator of these anti-inflammatory effects, supporting a causal role for HDAC2 in vivo. 
Curcumin: Curcumin, a polyphenolic compound from Curcuma longa (turmeric), exhibits dual epigenetic regulatory properties. First, curcumin has been reported to inhibit p300/CBP histone acetyltransferases, thereby reducing acetylation at pro-inflammatory gene promoters and suppressing their transcription. Second, curcumin has been linked to SIRT1 activation, potentially through effects on cellular NAD⁺ metabolism (including modulation of NAMPT, the rate-limiting enzyme in NAD⁺ biosynthesis). This dual mechanism, involving HAT inhibition and SIRT1 activation, may synergistically shift the acetylation balance toward a less inflammatory transcriptional state.
Quercetin: Quercetin, a flavonoid present in many medicinal plants, including Scutellaria baicalensis and Ginkgo biloba, has been widely reported to enhance SIRT1 signaling, although the exact mode of activation may vary across models. In sepsis-induced ALI, quercetin administration enhances SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of NLRP3, thereby preventing inflammasome assembly and reducing pyroptotic cell death in alveolar macrophages. Quercetin also promotes SIRT1-dependent autophagy in alveolar epithelial cells, facilitating the clearance of damaged mitochondria and reducing oxidative stress. Furthermore, quercetin engages a SIRT1/Nrf2 transcriptional axis to upregulate ferroptosis-regulatory proteins including GPX4, thereby reducing lipid peroxidation and ameliorating ALI-associated ferroptotic pathology, extending the therapeutic relevance of SIRT1 activation beyond inflammasome suppression to the broader redox landscape of ALI.
Several additional TCM formulas and TCM-derived compounds further expand this SIRT1-centered regulatory network. Qidong Huoxue Decoction activates SIRT1 to suppress p300-mediated hyperacetylation, thereby attenuating inflammatory gene transcription in ALI models; Glycyrrhizic acid achieves a mechanistically analogous effect through the SIRT1/HMGB1 pathway, reducing extracellular HMGB1 release and downstream inflammatory signaling; Astragaloside IV upregulates SIRT1 to inhibit TGF-β1-induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition, collagen deposition, and pulmonary fibrosis, thus preserving lung architecture during the fibroproliferative phase; Ginsenoside Rg1 similarly leverages SIRT1 to alleviate endoplasmic reticulum stress and inflammatory cytokine production in LPS-challenged lung tissue. Additionally, syringin targets the SIRT1/STAT6/SLC7A11–GPX4 axis within the glutathione metabolic system, demonstrating anti-ferroptotic efficacy against ROS-induced lung injury. 
Beyond these individual compounds, multi-herb formulas used in respiratory infections and inflammatory lung conditions may contain constituents with reported HDAC-inhibitory or SIRT1-modulating activities; however, direct, formula-level validation of HDAC/SIRT engagement in ALI/ARDS remains limited and should be interpreted cautiously.
Collectively, these findings—spanning andrographolide’s HDAC2–Nrf2 reactivation, curcumin’s reported HAT inhibition and putative SIRT1 activation, quercetin’s inflammasome and ferroptosis suppression, and the SIRT1-centered effects of Qidong Huoxue Decoction, glycyrrhizic acid, astragaloside IV, ginsenoside Rg1, and syringin—sunderscore the potential of TCM for integrated intervention through coordinated tuning of HDAC2 function, SIRT1 activity, and p300/CBP-driven acetylation. This convergent strategy may rebalance acetylation across transcriptional, metabolic, and redox regulatory programs, with SIRT1 functioning as a multi-output node coordinating anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, and anti-ferroptotic responses through downstream effectors including NF-κB, HMGB1, Nrf2, and STAT6, offering multi-target mechanistic insights into the therapeutic capacity of TCM in ALI/ARDS.

## Suppression of the PAD4–CitH3–NETs Cascade
Pathway 2 of illustrates how several TCM formulas and TCM-derived compounds target the PAD4–CitH3–NETs cascade through different upstream mechanisms, exemplifying TCM’s characteristic multi-pathway pharmacology in dismantling the mechanistic bridge of histone externalization.
Taohe Chengqi Decoction (THCQ/THCQD): THCQ, a classical TCM formula, has been shown to mitigate sepsis-induced ALI through inhibition of PAD4-mediated NET formation. In a cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced ALI mouse model, THCQ improved survival and alleviated lung pathology, accompanied by reduced neutrophil and macrophage infiltration and decreased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. Critically, THCQ significantly decreased PAD4 protein expression and inhibited NET formation both in vivo and in vitro; moreover, molecular docking suggested that some active compounds within THCQ could bind tightly to the PAD4 active site, and PAD4 overexpression partially reversed THCQ’s inhibitory effects on NETs, supporting PAD4 as a key mechanistic node. These findings position THCQ as a PAD4-centered intervention that attenuates the CitH3–NETs–lung injury axis. , 
Jingyin Guibiao formula (JYGBF): JYGBF, a multi-herb TCM formula, has been reported to inhibit NETosis in a postinfluenza Staphylococcus aureus infection mouse model, with mechanistic evidence implicating suppression of the ERK/ROS axis in activated neutrophils. Consistently, JYGBF administration reduced NET formation and improved the hypercoagulable state. Chemical profiling identified acacetin as a critical bioactive component linked to NETosis inhibition via this pathway. Acacetin significantly reduces NET formation and ameliorates the hypercoagulable state by suppressing the ERK/ROS-PADI4 pathway in postinfluenza S. aureus-infected mice. 
Da-Yuan-Yin Decoction (DYY): DYY, traditionally used for damp-heat syndromes, has been shown to suppress PAD4 via the IL-23/JAK2/STAT3 transcriptional axis. In a damp-heat syndrome rat model incorporating LPS administration as a key inflammatory trigger, DYY significantly ameliorated LPS-associated lung tissue damage and downregulated the expression of PADI4, CitH3, and MPO specifically in pulmonary tissue, concurrently suppressing the IL-23/JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. These findings position DYY as a TCM formulation capable of attenuating ALI-relevant pathology through coordinated modulation of the PAD4–CitH3 axis and its upstream inflammatory signaling. 
Tanshinone IIA, a lipophilic diterpene quinone derived from Salvia miltiorrhiza (Danshen), represents an additional PAD4-targeting mechanism: it has been reported to reduce NET production and associated vascular inflammation, potentially by inhibiting PI3K/AKT-dependent signaling in activated neutrophils and thereby limiting PAD4 activation. 
Beyond direct or upstream suppression of PAD4, certain TCM formulas disrupt NET formation through an orthogonal mechanism: interruption of the pyroptosis–NETs amplification loop. Xuebijing Injection and Lianhua Qingke tablets inhibit the NLRP3/Caspase-11/GSDMD signaling cascade, preventing gasdermin D N-terminal (GSDMD-N) pore formation; in relevant experimental systems, genetic evidence supports a requirement for GSDMD activity in efficient NET release, establishing pyroptosis suppression as a functionally distinct but convergent route to NET inhibition. , 
Across these formulations, distinct upstream entry points—including PAD4-centered suppression (THCQ), ROS/ERK axis suppression (JYGBF), cytokine-driven transcriptional blockade (DYY), signaling-pathway interference affecting PAD4 activation (tanshinone IIA), and pyroptosis–NETs crosstalk disruption (Xuebijing, Lianhua Qingke) —converge on a common output of reduced NET burden. Conceptually, this diversity suggests that different TCM strategies may be better matched to different dominant drivers of NETosis, such as oxidative stress, systemic cytokine signaling, or inflammasome/pyroptosis activation, thereby supporting context-aware selection and rational combination in future translational studies.

## Regulation of Lactylation-Linked Metabolic Pathways
As illustrated in Pathway 3 of , histone lactylation has emerged as a potential mechanistic link between altered glycolytic metabolism and transcriptional programs relevant to lung inflammation and repair in ALI/ARDS. Accordingly, TCM may influence this metabolic–epigenetic interface by modulating glycolytic flux and lactate availability, thereby affecting histone lactylation levels; however, direct evidence in ALI/ARDS models remains limited and represents an emerging frontier.
Jinshui Huanxian Formula: The active ingredient composition of Jinshui Huanxian Formula (ECC-JHF) is a modern TCM-derived formulation developed for pulmonary fibrosis, comprising five bioactive constituents—icariin, isoliquiritigenin, nobiletin, peimine, and paeoniflorin—that collectively demonstrate therapeutic effects on fibrotic remodeling. In bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis models, ECC-JHF suppresses the SRC/hexokinase 2 (HK2) signaling axis, thereby reducing glycolytic flux and lactate production in lung fibroblasts. Reduced lactate availability is associated with decreased histone lactylation, leading to significant reductions in H3K18la levels. Suppression of H3K18la attenuates fibroblast activation and collagen deposition, thereby mitigating pulmonary fibrosis progression. While this evidence is derived from pulmonary fibrosis rather than ALI/ARDS, it provides a compelling rationale for examining whether lactylation-sensitive metabolic interventions can be leveraged in ALI settings.
TCM herbs: Although direct evidence for the modulation of histone lactylation marks (eg, H3K18la) by TCM in ALI/ARDS remains scarce, preclinical studies indicate that several widely used TCM herbs, including Astragalus membranaceus (astragalus), Salvia miltiorrhiza (danshen), and Panax ginseng (ginseng), enhance mitochondrial function and improve cellular metabolic homeostasis in lung tissues. These effects could indirectly attenuate lactate accumulation and thereby potentially dampen lactylation-associated inflammatory programs implicated in ALI/ARDS pathogenesis. , An overview of current TCM-based interventions targeting histone modifications in experimental models of ALI/ARDS is provided in , and representative TCM-derived agents with documented epigenetic activities are illustrated in .

 

## Systemic Anti-Inflammatory Effects and Multi-Target Regulation
Beyond their direct effects on histone modifications, some TCM formulations exert pleiotropic systemic anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory actions that may indirectly influence epigenetic states by attenuating upstream inflammatory cues known to drive pathological histone modifications. 
Xuebijing Injection (XBJ): XBJ, an intravenous TCM preparation widely used in Chinese ICUs for sepsis management, contains extracts from Carthamus tinctorius (safflower), Paeonia lactiflora (white peony root), Ligusticum chuanxiong (Szechuan lovage), Salvia miltiorrhiza (red sage), and Angelica sinensis (Chinese angelica). Clinical studies have demonstrated that XBJ administration in sepsis-induced ARDS is associated with reduced circulating inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β), improved oxygenation (PaO2/FiO2 ratio), and shortened ICU stay. While direct effects on histone modifications remain insufficiently characterized, XBJ’s modulation of NF-κB and MAPK signaling —pathways that can regulate HDAC3 and HATs such as p300/CBP—raises the possibility of indirect epigenetic effects that merit targeted validation.
Qingjin Huatan Decoction (QJHTD): As another example of indirect convergence on histone-related inflammatory amplification, network pharmacology identifies MAPK14, SRC, AKT1, EGFR, and CDK2 as core targets of QJHTD in ALI, and experimental data show that QJHTD inhibits Src phosphorylation in LPS-activated neutrophils and suppresses NETs formation. By limiting NET release (and thus extracellular histone exposure/CitH3-linked injury), QJHTD may dampen the extracellular drivers that perpetuate the inflammatory–epigenetic escalation in the lung microenvironment. 
Across these formulations, systemic anti-inflammatory effects and epigenetic regulation are functionally coupled rather than independent. By reducing kinase-driven inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, and metabolic derangements, TCM interventions may secondarily normalize the activity of epigenetic enzymes (eg, HDACs, sirtuins, and p300/CBP) and lessen lactate accumulation that fuels lactylation-associated transcriptional programs. Together, these mechanisms suggest a feedback-alleviating therapeutic logic in which the systemic suppression of inflammation can facilitate the restoration of epigenetic homeostasis, and vice versa.
From a TCM theoretical standpoint, the mechanistic landscape mapped across Modulation of the HDAC/SIRT Acetylation Axis–Systemic Anti-Inflammatory Effects and Multi-Target Regulation can be conceptually interpreted alongside syndrome differentiation. ALI/ARDS has been discussed in relation to syndromes including “lung qi obstruction”, “wheezing syndrome”, and “phlegm-heat obstructing the lungs”, commonly addressed through clearing heat, resolving phlegm, and promoting circulation. Rather than implying one-to-one equivalence, modern pharmacology offers testable mechanistic hypotheses that may partially align with these principles: “heat-clearing” herbs have been reported to engage antioxidant and inflammatory pathways (eg, SIRT1/Nrf2-related signaling) relevant to acetylation balance; “blood-invigorating” interventions may attenuate NET-associated immunothrombosis and PAD4-related NETosis; and “phlegm-resolving” strategies may support barrier function and airway clearance. This crosswalk provides a heuristic framework for mechanism-guided selection and combination of TCM interventions, which ultimately requires rigorous epigenetic readouts and clinical validation.

## Advantages and Challenges of TCM in ALI/ARDS Therapy

## Advantages
The principal advantage of TCM in modulating histone modifications lies in its systems-level, multi-target regulatory capacity. As illustrated in , individual TCM formulations can potentially intervene at multiple nodes within the epigenetic-inflammatory circuit: restoring HDAC/SIRT balance, suppressing PAD4–CitH3–NETs formation, and modulating lactylation-linked metabolism. This holistic approach aligns well with the complex, multi-factorial pathogenesis of ALI/ARDS, where single-target interventions have historically shown limited or inconsistent clinical benefit.
Furthermore, TCM formulations have been used clinically for centuries, with extensive empirical evidence supporting their safety profiles in specific contexts when appropriately prescribed and quality-controlled. The rapid deployment and widespread use of TCM during the COVID-19 pandemic, with multiple formulations integrated into national treatment guidelines in China, demonstrates the feasibility of TCM clinical translation in acute respiratory illnesses. 
TCM’s favorable safety profile further distinguishes it from conventional epigenetic-targeting agents such as pan-HDAC inhibitors or small-molecule PAD4 inhibitors, which carry documented risks of immunosuppression, genotoxicity, and off-target enzymatic inhibition. By contrast, the bioactive constituents of most TCM formulations may act through multi-target, pathway-modulating effects rather than complete enzymatic blockade, which could be better tolerated as adjunctive therapy in hemodynamically unstable ARDS patients when supported by careful monitoring. 
TCM also demonstrates potential mechanistic compatibility with frontline ARDS interventions. The HDAC2-Nrf2 reactivation induced by andrographolide has been linked to the restoration of glucocorticoid receptor sensitivity, providing a pharmacological rationale for exploring combination with corticosteroid therapy in steroid-refractory cases. Similarly, PAD4-suppressing formulations may synergize with anticoagulant strategies by reducing NET-driven coagulation activation, while lactylation-modulating agents could complement lung-protective ventilation by limiting injury-associated metabolic reprogramming.
Finally, the mechanistic evidence base underpinning TCM’s epigenetic actions has expanded, now encompassing selected genetic loss-of-function models (eg, HDAC2-knockout and GSDMD-knockout validation), molecular docking analyses of compound–target binding geometries, and omics-supported characterization of downstream transcriptional changes. This shift from empirical observation toward more mechanism-resolved evidence strengthens the scientific credibility of TCM-based epigenetic interventions and provides the conceptual precision for rational target selection in future clinical trial design. , 

## Challenges
Standardization and quality control: A major barrier to the global acceptance of TCM is the difficulty in achieving compositional standardization. Unlike single-molecule drugs, TCM formulations contain numerous bioactive compounds whose concentrations vary with botanical source, geographic origin, harvesting conditions, and preparation methods, leading to batch-to-batch variability that may influence pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic outcomes. Advanced analytical approaches such as UHPLC-HRMS and chromatographic fingerprinting enable multi-component profiling, yet challenges in inter-laboratory reproducibility, regulatory acceptance, and the definition of quality markers (Q-markers) persist. , Although international frameworks, including ISO/TC 249, are establishing quality benchmarks, harmonization between Eastern and Western regulatory systems remains incomplete. , 
Clinical validation requirements: Clinical evidence for TCM in ALI/ARDS remains dominated by single-center studies and observational reports, with relatively few large, multicenter, double-blind RCTs. Existing trials frequently employ open-label designs and demonstrate inconsistent reporting of randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding, indicating suboptimal adherence to CONSORT standards. Additional challenges include batch variability that complicates placebo design and dose standardization. Future trials should adopt validated clinical endpoints (eg, ventilator-free days, 28-day mortality, PaO2/FiO2) and integrate pharmacokinetic profiling with mechanism-linked biomarkers, including epigenetic and NET-related readouts, to strengthen translational relevance. 
Herb-drug interactions and safety concerns: Herb–drug interactions represent an important but underexplored safety issue in critically ill patients. Many TCM compounds modulate CYP450 enzymes and drug transporters, potentially altering the metabolism of corticosteroids, anticoagulants, and immunomodulators used in ALI/ARDS. For example, glycyrrhizic acid may influence glucocorticoid exposure through CYP3A4 inhibition and suppression of 11β-HSD2, while compounds such as curcumin can modify anticoagulant pharmacokinetics. However, current evidence is largely based on case reports or preclinical studies, highlighting the need for prospective pharmacokinetic investigations to define safe co-administration strategies. , 
Despite these challenges, accumulating mechanistic evidence supports the rational integration of TCM into modern ALI/ARDS management. Achieving this will require rigorous quality control, well-designed clinical trials, and systematic pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic studies to enable the evidence-based implementation of TCM-based epigenetic interventions.

## Future Perspectives and Translational Opportunities
The integration of histone modification biology into ALI/ARDS research has opened new avenues for mechanism-informed stratification and therapeutic development that go beyond the traditional paradigm of supportive care. By targeting specific nodes along the inside-out pathological cascade, this section outlines key translational opportunities and research priorities for the coming decade.

## Biomarker-Guided Precision Therapy

## Serum CitH3 and H3K18la as Candidate Dynamic Biomarkers
As discussed in , circulating levels of citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3) , , , , and lactylated histones (eg, H3K18la) have been reported to associate with disease severity and clinical outcomes in ALI/ARDS. The development of standardized, clinically validated assays (ideally point-of-care) for rapid quantification of these epigenetic biomarkers could enable the real-time stratification of patients into distinct therapeutic categories.
For example, patients with markedly elevated serum CitH3 levels (suggesting active NET formation and immunothrombosis) might preferentially benefit from NET/PAD4-directed strategies (eg, PAD4 inhibition or NET-neutralizing approaches), whereas those with elevated H3K18la (suggesting metabolic dysregulation with potential links to oxidative injury pathways, including ferroptosis in specific contexts) may be better candidates for glycolysis-targeting interventions or SIRT activation strategies. , Serial measurements of these biomarkers during treatment could provide dynamic feedback on therapeutic efficacy, allowing for adaptive treatment adjustments—a “treat-to-target” concept that remains to be validated in ALI/ARDS but has been successfully operationalized in rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease. 
Complementing these markers, HDAC/SIRT activity ratios, potentially measurable in bronchoalveolar lavage cells or circulating neutrophils, may serve as surrogate indicators of the inflammation–repair balance, predicting therapeutic responsiveness to enzyme-specific modulators. Furthermore, histone methylation marks, particularly H3K27me3 and H3K9me3, sampled at later disease stages, may provide candidate early warning signals of fibroproliferative remodeling and guide the timely transition to anti-fibrotic strategies. , , Together, this expanding panel of epigenetic biomarkers should be prioritized for prospective validation and integration into clinical trial designs to enable baseline stratification, dynamic treatment monitoring, and adaptive dosing adjustments, aligning ALI/ARDS trial methodology with biomarker-guided paradigms used in oncology and autoimmune disease. 

## Multi-Omics Integration
The next generation of precision ALI/ARDS therapy will likely integrate epigenetic biomarkers with genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic data to generate comprehensive patient-specific profiles. Machine learning algorithms trained on large multi-omics datasets may help identify distinct molecular endotypes and predict differential treatment response, thereby reducing reliance on the “one-size-fits-all” strategies that have limited prior ALI/ARDS trials. 

## Phase-Specific and Cell-Targeted Delivery
As highlighted throughout this review, many histone-modifying enzymes exhibit context-dependent and cell-type–specific functions. JMJD3, for instance, promotes ferroptosis in alveolar epithelial cells but suppresses apoptosis in macrophages. HDAC6 drives inflammation during acute endotoxin challenge but maintains TLR4 homeostasis in immature endothelium. , These complexities necessitate the development of cell-targeted and phase-specific delivery systems to decouple therapeutic efficacy from systemic untoward effect.

## Nanoparticle-Based Targeting
Advances in nanotechnology have enabled the engineering of nanoparticles that preferentially accumulate in specific lung cell types. For example, mannose-decorated nanoparticles can target alveolar macrophages via mannose receptor–mediated endocytosis, whereas cationic lipid nanoparticles may show enhanced uptake in alveolar epithelial cells. , Encapsulation of HDAC3 inhibitors in macrophage-targeting nanoparticles or JMJD3 inhibitors in epithelial-targeting nanoparticles is a plausible strategy to maximize local benefit while minimizing off-target effects. , 

## Inhalation Delivery
Aerosolized delivery of epigenetic modulators offers the advantage of high local drug concentrations in the lungs with reduced systemic exposure. Nebulized formulations of HDAC inhibitors, SIRT activators, and DNase I are under preclinical/early translational exploration for ALI/ARDS applications. , Inhalation delivery may be particularly well-suited for TCM-derived compounds with poor oral bioavailability, ensuring direct deposition at the site of alveolar injury.

## Temporal Optimization
The timing of epigenetic interventions may be as important as the choice of agent. EZH2 inhibitors, for instance, appear most effective in preclinical studies when administered during the fibroproliferative phase (days 7–14 post-injury) rather than the acute inflammatory phase. , Conversely, HDAC3 inhibitors and PAD4 inhibitors show maximal benefit when initiated early, during the hyperinflammatory phase. , Future clinical trials should incorporate phase-stratified enrollment criteria and adaptive designs that allow for the temporal optimization of interventions.
Nevertheless, the clinical translation of epigenetic therapies remains constrained by key barriers. Animal models often fail to replicate the complexity and variability of human ALI/ARDS, impeding the accurate prediction of therapeutic efficacy. Effective and safe targeted delivery systems for HDAC or PAD inhibitors, or anti-CitH3 agents, remain underdeveloped. Moreover, off-target toxicity, such as immunosuppression or cardiotoxicity, presents a significant safety concern. Overcoming these limitations will require next-generation preclinical platforms, such as lung organoids and lung-on-a-chip systems that more faithfully recapitulate the human pulmonary microenvironment, together with companion diagnostic tools that prospectively match patients to interventions based on their individual epigenetic profiles. , 

## Combination Strategies and TCM Integration

## Synergistic Multi-Tiered Interventions
Given the multi-factorial pathogenesis of ALI/ARDS, combination strategies that simultaneously target multiple nodes within the epigenetic-inflammatory circuit are hypothesis-generating but compelling. Rational combinations might include: (i) HDAC3 inhibition together with SIRT1 activation to rebalance the acetylation axis; , (ii) PAD4 inhibition together with NET/CitH3-neutralizing strategies to block both the bridge of externalization and the downstream DAMP injury; , (iii) Glycolysis-modulating interventions together with heparin to suppress lactylation-associated programs and neutralize extracellular histones simultaneously. , Such combinations will require careful evaluation of timing, immunosuppression risk, and coagulation/bleeding liability.

## TCM as a Multi-Target Platform
As detailed in , TCM formulations inherently provide multi-target regulation, simultaneously modulating HDAC/SIRT activity, suppressing PAD4–NETs formation, and intervening in metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk. The integration of standardized, quality-controlled TCM preparations with conventional epigenetic drugs could be explored for additive or synergistic benefits. For example, combining andrographolide (HDAC2-linked protective signaling) with a selective HDAC3 inhibitor may offer a way to reduce pro-inflammatory transcription while preserving cytoprotective HDAC2-associated effects. Similarly, pairing ECC-JHF with exogenous NAD⁺ precursors could be investigated to jointly reduce lactylation pressure and support SIRT activity. Importantly, potential herb–drug interactions and ICU polypharmacy constraints should be embedded into any such development plan.

## Clinical Trial Design for TCM-Drug Combinations
Rigorous evaluation of TCM-drug combinations will require adaptive clinical trial designs that can accommodate the dose-finding challenges of multi-component interventions. Platform trials with multiple arms testing different TCM formulations, synthetic epigenetic drugs, and their combinations, using shared control groups and biomarker-driven response-adaptive randomization, may offer an efficient path forward. 
Beyond conventional pharmacological and clinical approaches, the convergence of artificial intelligence, network pharmacology, and multi-omics–driven drug discovery offers a complementary route to accelerating TCM integration into precision epigenetic therapy. AI-assisted target deconvolution and network pharmacology modeling can map the polypharmacological space of complex TCM formulations, prioritizing compound–target pairs for experimental validation and reducing the empirical burden of mechanistic characterization. This computational layer, applied in conjunction with the standardization and regulatory frameworks detailed in , would provide the necessary methodological infrastructure to support broader clinical translation.

## Additional Mechanistic Questions
Three interconnected questions will guide the next phase of mechanistic inquiry. First, at the molecular level, the unresolved competition between lactylation and acetylation at shared residues, particularly H3K18, will determine whether glycolytic suppression definitively shifts this balance toward acetylation or whether compensatory writer and eraser recruitment limits sequential epigenetic targeting. Second, at the biological level, age-related NAD⁺ decline and sex-specific neutrophil priming may reshape baseline epigenetic states and responses to SIRT activators or PAD4 inhibitors, highlighting the need for population stratification in early trials. Finally, over the long term, whether transient histone modifications in acute ALI generate an “epigenetic memory” that predisposes survivors to chronic lung disease may be most clinically significant, positioning early epigenetic intervention as a strategy for both acute survival and long-term pulmonary recovery.

## Regulatory and Standardization Challenges
Realizing the therapeutic potential of TCM-based epigenetic interventions requires a coordinated translational continuum. The first priority is internationally harmonized quality standards built on consensus Q-markers that align traditional efficacy with modern regulatory criteria, enabling credible submissions and multicenter trials. On this foundation, robust pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies across diverse populations are needed to define dose–response relationships and identify predictive biomarkers. Parallel evaluation of herb–drug interactions using in vitro, in silico, and clinical pharmacokinetic approaches, particularly CYP450 and transporter-mediated mechanisms in critically ill patients, is essential given ARDS polypharmacy. Sustained progress also depends on GMP-certified manufacturing capable of batch-consistent formulations with validated bioactive profiles. Cross-disciplinary collaboration among traditional medicine experts, pharmacologists, regulators, and manufacturers will be crucial to translate TCM’s mechanistic promise into clinically deployable therapies.

## Conclusion
ALI/ARDS is a complex syndrome shaped by nuclear epigenetic reprogramming and extracellular DAMP-driven amplification. This review synthesizes current evidence and makes three key contributions to the field:
Mechanistic integration: We map four major histone modifications—acetylation, methylation, lactylation, and citrullination—into a unified “Nuclear Epigenetic Remodeling–Extracellular DAMP Amplification Circuit.” By conceptualizing this as an inside-out pathological cascade, we explicitly link intranuclear programs (HDAC3/6-associated pyroptosis, SIRT1/3/6-mediated protection, EZH2/SETD2 antagonism in fibrotic transition, and H3K18la/H3K14la as metabolic-epigenetic bridges) to extracellular cascades (CitH3–NETs–immunothrombosis), revealing previously unrecognized cross-axis connections.
Therapeutic architecture: Based on this framework, we propose a three-tier strategy: (i) intranuclear epigenetic modulation (HDAC inhibitors, SIRT activators, methylation modulators), (ii) disruption of the extracellular CitH3/NET axis (blocking the externalization bridge via PAD inhibitors, anti-CitH3 antibodies, DNase), and (iii) neutralization of circulating histones (heparin, rTM, biomimetic nanocarriers). Notably, TCM-based interventions can span all three tiers via multi-component pharmacology, concurrently modulating the HDAC/SIRT acetylation axis, suppressing PAD4–CitH3–NET formation, and regulating lactylation-linked metabolism.
Translational outlook: The convergence of biomarkers (eg, serum CitH3, H3K18la) with single-cell and spatial omics, network modeling, and biomarker-guided precision strategies could shift ALI/ARDS from supportive care toward mechanism-driven individualized therapy. Integrating TCM’s holistic principles with modern epigenetic paradigms adds a complementary, culturally informed dimension that warrants validation through multicenter trials and standardized molecular profiling.
Ultimately, translating histone modification-targeted therapies will require sustained collaboration across basic, translational, clinical, industrial, and regulatory stakeholders. With accelerating technological tools and persistent clinical urgency, the next decade may bring a long-awaited shift from empirical supportive care to precision-targeted intervention in ALI/ARDS.
