## Introduction
Ovarian cancer (OC) continues to be one of the most fatal gynecological malignancies, largely attributable to its high recurrence rate and the development of intrinsic or acquired chemoresistance – . While cisplatin is a fundamental component of first-line chemotherapy and initially elicits positive responses, the majority of patients eventually experience relapse due to the emergence of resistance , . A growing body of evidence suggests that cancer stem cells (CSCs) are pivotal in facilitating chemoresistance and tumor recurrence . These CSCs are characterized by their abilities for self-renewal, pluripotency, and pronounced tumorigenicity, which allow them to withstand conventional chemotherapy . In the context of OC, the persistence of ovarian cancer stem cells (OCSCs) exacerbates disease progression and is associated with poor clinical outcomes .
A substantial body of research has indicated that chemoresistance in OC is predominantly facilitated by the persistence of OCSCs, which play a critical role in tumor initiation, metastasis, and recurrence . These OCSCs are distinguished by their capacity to remain quiescent, withstand cytotoxic damage, and re-enter the cell cycle in response to external stimuli, thereby contributing to an inherent resistance to cisplatin and other chemotherapeutic agents. Consequently, targeting the principal regulatory mechanisms of OCSCs emerges as a promising approach to overcoming intrinsic cisplatin resistance. Among these regulatory factors, Bmi1 (B Mo-MLV insertion region 1) has been identified as a critical oncogene that encodes a transcriptional repressor belonging to the polycomb group family. Functionally, Bmi1 inhibits the Ink4a/Arf locus, a key tumor suppressor region that regulates cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis, thereby facilitating the self-renewal and survival of CSCs .
The overexpression of Bmi1 has been documented in various malignancies, including colorectal, gastric, breast, prostate, and non-small cell lung cancers, and is strongly associated with tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis , . In the context of OC, Bmi1 upregulation is linked to increased sphere-forming capacity, enhanced metastatic potential, and resistance to platinum-based chemotherapy . Mechanistically, Bmi1 contributes to chemoresistance through several pathways, including the repression of p53-mediated apoptosis, the activation of NF-κB-dependent DNA repair signaling, and interactions with autophagy-related regulators such as FOXA2 . Additionally, the expression of Bmi1 is influenced by epigenetic and microenvironmental factors, such as histone modifications, miRNA regulation, and the activation of the GLI1/BMI1 axis in response to chemotherapy-induced stress . Functional analyses employing CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockdown of Bmi1 in epithelial ovarian cancer cells have demonstrated that Bmi1 not only facilitates tumor growth and metastasis but also maintains stem-like properties essential for tumor recurrence . Collectively, these findings underscore the role of Bmi1 as a critical driver of ovarian CSC maintenance and suggest it as a promising therapeutic target for overcoming cisplatin resistance.
Pharmacological inhibition of Bmi1 has been demonstrated to disrupt CSC self-renewal and enhance the sensitivity of tumor cells to chemotherapeutic agents. Combination strategies that simultaneously target CSCs and the bulk tumor cells via conventional chemotherapy exhibit considerable therapeutic potential . Nevertheless, these strategies encounter significant obstacles, including inefficient drug delivery and systemic toxicity . Small-molecule inhibitors of Bmi1, such as PTC-209, demonstrate efficacy in suppressing CSC activity but are limited by inadequate tumor specificity . Similarly, siRNA-based therapeutics frequently face challenges related to instability and inefficient intracellular release. Consequently, the development of an effective co-delivery platform capable of concurrently administering chemotherapeutic agents and Bmi1-targeting siRNA could constitute a substantial advancement in overcoming drug resistance in OC .
In this study, we developed a cationic lipid nanocapsule system comprising a cisplatin prodrug core encased within a positively charged lipid shell, designed to electrostatically adsorb Bmi1-targeting siRNA (CCL@Bmi1 siRNA). The objectives of this system were to: (1) achieve synergistic therapy by utilizing cisplatin to eradicate non-stem tumor cells while siBmi1 silences CSC-associated signaling pathways; (2) facilitate targeted delivery to enhance tumor accumulation and reduce systemic toxicity; and (3) effectively reverse cisplatin resistance by inhibiting Bmi1-mediated DNA repair and CSC maintenance. Extensive in vitro and in vivo experiments were conducted to substantiate the dual antitumor efficacy of this nanocapsule system, offering a novel translational strategy for overcoming chemoresistance in OC.

## Materials and methods

## Gene expression and clinical correlation analysis
Bmi1 mRNA expression levels in OC tissues and normal control tissues were analyzed using TNMplot (http://www.tnmplot.com/), which integrates data from GEO, GTEx, TCGA, and TARGET databases. The correlation between Bmi1 expression and patient age was evaluated via UALCAN (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) with age-stratified subgroup analysis.

## Survival analysis
Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier plotter (http://kmplot.com/analysis/) to assess the association of Bmi1 expression with overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in OC patients. Analyses were stratified by histological subtypes (endometrioid OC, serous OC) and tumor grades (Grade 1–2, Grade 3). The log-rank test was used for statistical comparison, and hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated.

## Synthesis of cisplatin precursor cis-[Pt(NH3)2(H2O)2](NO3)2
The precursor to cisplatin, cis-[Pt(NH3)2(H2O)2](NO3)2 was synthesized following a method previously documented in the literature . In summary, a suspension containing 0.20 mmol of cisplatin was combined with 0.39 mmol of AgNO3 under light-restricted conditions and subsequently heated at 60 °C for a duration of 3 h. To remove the AgCl precipitate, the resulting mixture was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 15 min. The concentration of the synthesized compound was quantified by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP–MS) using an Agilent 7500 instrument (Agilent Technologies, USA).

## Preparation of CCL@Bmi1 siRNA nanocapsules
The formulation parameters were selected based on previously published studies with minor optimization through preliminary experiments , . Two microemulsions were created with Triton X-100, hexanol, cyclohexane, and water in a 1.02:1.04:3.94:0.3 ratio and stirred for 5 min. Each emulsion received 200 µL of 200 mM CaCl2 or cisplatin precursor and 200 µL of 200 mM Na2CO3. The cisplatin emulsion also included 100 µL of 20 mM DOPA and was stirred for 20 min. The emulsions were then combined and reacted for 24 h. Post-reaction, 16 mL ethanol was added, and the mixture was centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min to remove surfactants and cyclohexane. The precipitates were washed with ethanol and re-dispersed in 3 mL of chloroform to form CaCO3/cisplatin (CDDP@CaCO3 , CC) nanocapsules.
To create lipid-coated CC nanoparticles, a lipid mixture of 28 mg DOTAP, 21.3 mg cholesterol, and 12.6 mg mPEG-DSPE (molar ratio 40:55:5) was dissolved in 5 mL chloroform and mixed with 1 mL of CC suspension. After removing chloroform by rotary evaporation, the dried lipid films were hydrated with 2 mL deionized water, resulting in lipid-coated CaCO3/cisplatin nanoparticles (CCL). To determine the optimal binding ratio, CCL nanoparticles were complexed with Bmi1 siRNA at various weight ratios, specifically 40:1, 80:1, 120:1, 160:1, and 200:1. The mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 30 min to allow complex (CCL@Bmi1 siRNA) formation. The siRNA binding capability of the nanoparticles was then assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Samples were loaded onto a 2% (w/v) agarose gel, and electrophoresis was performed at 100 V for 30 min in 1× TAE buffer. The siRNA bands were visualized under a UV transilluminator. A DNA marker with a range of 100–3000 bp (G3360-01, Servicebio, China) was used to estimate fragment sizes.

## X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis
To verify cisplatin encapsulation in CaCO3 nanoparticles, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted. Lyophilized CaCO3 and CDDP@CaCO3 samples were analyzed on conductive carbon tape using an ESCALAB 250Xi spectrometer with an Al Kα X-ray source. Survey spectra were recorded from 0 to 1200 eV at 100 eV pass energy, and high-resolution spectra for Pt 4f, Ca 2p, C 1s, O 1s, and N 1s were obtained at 30 eV. Binding energies were calibrated to the C 1s peak (284.8 eV), and data analysis utilized Thermo Avantage software (v5.9921).

## Zeta potential analysis, PDI detection, particle size analysis
Zeta potential and particle size were measured using a Zetasizer analyzer (Malvern Panalytical, UK). Nanoparticles (5 µg) were dispersed in 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) and briefly sonicated to ensure homogeneity prior to analysis. Each sample was measured in triplicate, and the results were expressed as zeta potential (mV), particle size (nm), and polydispersity index (PDI).

## Platinum release and intracellular Pt concentration assay
The in vitro platinum (Pt) release behavior of the nanoparticles was evaluated by dispersing the formulations at a concentration of 1 mg/mL in fetal bovine serum (FBS) adjusted to different pH conditions. The suspensions were incubated at 37 °C under gentle agitation. At predetermined time points, samples were collected and centrifuged to separate the nanoparticles from the supernatant. The Pt content in the supernatant was quantified by ICP–MS. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and cumulative platinum release profiles were subsequently generated. To measure intracellular Pt concentration, cell suspensions were digested with 1 mL of concentrated nitric acid at 95 °C for 4 h. After cooling, samples were diluted to 5 mL with ultrapure water, filtered, and analyzed using an Agilent 7500 ICP-MS. The analysis was done in triplicate, with Pt concentrations determined from a standard calibration curve. The detection limit was 0.01 ng/mL, and results were expressed as nanograms of Pt per 5 × 105 cells.

## MTT
The SK-OV-3 and SK-OV-3/DDP cell lines were obtained from China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC, Wuhan University, Wuhan, China). Cultivation of the cells was done in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) enriched with 10% FBS, and they were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. In 96-well microplates, cells were seeded at a density of 2000 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight before being treated with specific compounds for 48 h. After the treatment phase, 10 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well. The culture medium was carefully removed following a 4-h incubation at 37 °C. The resulting formazan crystals were solubilized by the addition of 100 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to each well, accompanied by gentle agitation for 10 min. Absorbance was then measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader.

## Intracellular uptake
In the intracellular uptake experiments, SK-OV-3/DDP cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a concentration of 1 × 105 cells per well. After incubating overnight, the medium was replaced with a culture medium that contained either free Bmi1 siRNA-FITC or CCL@Bmi1 siRNA-FITC at the same concentrations. For fluorescence microscopy imaging, at 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 h post-incubation, the cells underwent three washes with pre-cooled PBS, were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and subsequently washed again with PBS. Fluorescence microscopy imaging was then conducted on the cells within the plates. For flow cytometry analysis of cellular uptake, at 0, 1, and 3 h post-incubation, the cells were subjected to trypsin digestion, centrifugation for cell collection, and resuspension in PBS for flow cytometry analysis. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software.

## Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis
Total RNA was extracted from treated SK-OV-3/DDP cells using TRIzol, and its concentration and purity were assessed by absorbance at 260/280 nm. cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of RNA using a Thermo Scientific reverse transcription kit. qRT-PCR was conducted with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix on a real-time PCR system, following a two-step protocol: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, then 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. The specific primers used are as follows: Bmi1, F: 5’-TGG ACT GAC AAA TGC TGG AGA-3’ R: 5’-GAA GAT TGG TGG TGG TTA CCG CTG-3’.

## Cell apoptosis and cell cycle
For the flow cytometry analysis of apoptosis and cell cycle, cells were categorized into four distinct groups: CDDP, CCL, CL@Bmi1 siRNA, and CCL@Bmi1 siRNA. In the apoptosis detection protocol, cells were collected, rinsed with cold PBS, and resuspended in a binding buffer. Cells were stained using an Annexin V–FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry using a BD Accuri C6 system (BD Biosciences, USA). For the cell cycle analysis, cells were fixed in 70% ethanol, washed, and stained with a PI/RNase staining buffer (Cell Cycle Detection Kit, BD Biosciences). Flow cytometry was then employed to assess the cell cycle distribution. All analyses were conducted utilizing a Cytoflex flow cytometer (Beckman, Indianapolis, USA), and data were processed with FlowJo software.

## RNA sequencing analysis
Total RNA was extracted from SK-OV-3/DDP cells post-treatment utilizing established extraction protocols. The purity, concentration, and integrity of the RNA were assessed to confirm their adequacy for sequencing applications. RNA samples meeting the quality criteria were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and preserved at − 80 °C. The construction of RNA sequencing libraries, sequencing processes, and subsequent bioinformatic analyses were performed by Wuhan KangSheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

## Mice
The C57BL/6J mice, aged between 4 and 6 weeks, were sourced from Beijing Huafukang Bioscience Technology Co., Ltd. They were kept in individually ventilated cages, with unrestricted access to standard chow and water, and a 12-h light/dark cycle was maintained. All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and relevant institutional regulations. At the end of the experimental protocol, mice (approximately 18 g in body weight) were euthanized by inhalation of 5% isoflurane anesthesia followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death. The Committee on Ethical Animal Experiments at Qinghai University Affiliated Hospital (The Clinical Medical School) reviewed and gave approval for all animal experiment protocols (Approval number: P-SL-2024-002).

## Primary ovarian cancer mouse model
To develop a primary ovarian cancer mouse model, plasmids overexpressing Bmi1 and Myc, with a combined total of 5 µg, were co-administered via injection into the ovaries of female C57BL/6J mice. Four weeks following the injection, when orthotopic tumors were established, the mice were randomly allocated into five experimental groups: Control (Ctrl), Cisplatin (CDDP), CCL, CL@Bmi1 siRNA, and CCL@Bmi1 siRNA. The mice received the designated treatments for 1 week. During this treatment period, the corresponding formulations were administered via tail vein injection four times per week. At the eighth week, the mice were euthanized to evaluate tumor weight.

## In vivo imaging and biodistribution study
In vivo imaging and biodistribution studies were performed in C57BL/6J primary ovarian cancer mouse model. The mice were anesthetized using 2% isoflurane and subsequently administered an intravenous injection of either CCL@Bmi1 siRNA-DiR or free DiR through the tail vein. Following the injection, imaging was conducted at intervals of 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, and 48 h utilizing a small animal live imaging system.

## Nude mouse xenograft model
In the xenograft model, SK-OV-3/DDP cells (4 × 10⁶) were subcutaneously inoculated into the right flank of female Balb/c nude mice. When the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm³, the mice were randomly assigned to five groups: Ctrl, CDDP, CCL, CL@Bmi1 siRNA, and CCL@Bmi1 siRNA. The mice received the indicated treatments via tail vein injection four times within 1 week. Tumor volume and body weight were recorded every 4 days. Tumor size was measured using calipers, and tumor volume was calculated according to the formula V = L × W² / 2, where L and W represent the tumor length and width, respectively. At the end of the experiment, the mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, weighed, and photographed for further analysis.

## Immunohistochemistry
Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin Sect.  (5 μm) were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and nonspecific binding was blocked using 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Sections were incubated with primary antibodies against Ki67 (1:200, Abcam, UK), Bmi1 (1:200, Servicebio, China), Cyclin D1 (1:100, Servicebio, China), P-gp (1:200, Servicebio, China), MRP (1:200, Abcam, UK) and CD133 (1:100, Abcam, UK) at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Servicebio, China). Immunoreactivity was visualized using a DAB substrate, counterstained with hematoxylin, and examined under a light microscope.

## Hematoxylin–Eosin (HE) Staining
The tumor samples were first fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and then dehydrated with a series of graded ethanol. Afterward, they were embedded in paraffin and sliced into Sect.  5 μm thick. After baking at 60 °C for 2 h, the sections were dewaxed in xylene for 10 min and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. The sections were soaked in hematoxylin for 5–10 min for HE staining, then differentiated with acid alcohol and turned blue in ammonia water. Next, eosin staining was performed for 5 min. The sections were then dehydrated, cleared in xylene, mounted with neutral gum, and examined under a microscope.

## Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed utilizing GraphPad Prism version 10.1 software. The significance between two groups was assessed using a two-tailed unpaired t-test. To compare multiple groups, a one-way ANOVA was used, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p-value below 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference.

## Results and discussion

## The expression of Bmi1 in OC
The expression and function of Bmi1 in OC have attracted considerable scholarly interest due to its involvement in tumor progression and resistance to chemotherapy – . As a member of the polycomb group proteins, Bmi1 plays a regulatory role in key carcinogenic processes across various cancers, including OC. Elevated expression levels of Bmi1 have been strongly associated with increased tumor malignancy and chemotherapy resistance in OC , . To investigate the prognostic significance of Bmi1 in OC, we conducted an analysis of its expression using several publicly accessible datasets. Initially, Bmi1 mRNA levels were assessed via TNMplot, utilizing data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project. A significant upregulation of Bmi1 was observed in OC tissues compared to normal controls (P = 4.27e-2, Fig.  A), indicating its potential role in oncogenesis. Conversely, no significant correlation was identified between Bmi1 expression and patient age when stratified by age group using the UALCAN platform (Fig.  B).
Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were conducted to evaluate the prognostic significance of Bmi1 expression across various histological and clinical subtypes. Elevated Bmi1 expression was significantly correlated with reduced overall survival (OS) in patients with endometrioid OC (log-rank P = 0.036; HR = 7.38; 95% CI 0.82–66.09; Fig.  C) and serous OC (log-rank P = 4.9e-05; HR = 1.42; 95% CI 1.2–1.69; Fig.  D). Additionally, increased Bmi1 expression was associated with decreased progression-free survival (PFS) in both Grade 1–2 tumors (log-rank P = 0.038; HR = 1.44; 95% CI 1.02–2.05; Fig.  E) and Grade 3 tumors (log-rank P = 0.039; HR = 1.21; 95% CI 1.01–1.45; Fig.  F). These results indicate that Bmi1 may play a role in tumor progression and treatment resistance, underscoring its potential utility as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target.

 

## Preparation and characterization of CCL@Bmi1 siRNA
Reverse-phase microemulsion technology offers substantial benefits in the synthesis of nanomedicine, chiefly by enabling precise control over nanoparticle size and morphology, thereby improving drug bioavailability and stability , . Research suggests that these systems act as biological reactors, facilitating the dissolution, separation, purification, and encapsulation of bioactive compounds, and thus provide innovative strategies for drug development. In this study, the CCL@Bmi1 siRNA delivery system was developed utilizing the reverse-phase microemulsion method. Compared with free cisplatin, the synthesized cisplatin precursor exhibited better compatibility with the CaCO₃ matrix, resulting in enhanced encapsulation efficiency and improved stability of the nanocapsules .
Transmission electron microscopy (TEM ) analysis revealed that CCL@Bmi1 siRNA nanoparticles exhibited a uniform spherical morphology with an average diameter of approximately 150 nm, indicating good dispersion and structural stability (Fig.  A). XPS analysis was performed to further confirm the successful encapsulation of cisplatin within the CaCO₃ nanoparticles. The XPS spectra of CaCO₃ and CDDP@CaCO₃ exhibited characteristic Pt 4f peaks in the CDDP@CaCO₃ group, which were absent in the pure CaCO₃ spectrum, thereby verifying that cisplatin was effectively incorporated into the nanocarriers (Fig.  B). The CCL@Bmi1 siRNA had a zeta potential of 9.33 ± 0.215 mV and a polydispersity index of 0.22 ± 0.008, indicating a good dispersion of the nanoparticles (Fig.  C and D). Using dynamic light scattering, the CCL@Bmi1 siRNA nanoparticles were found to have an average size of 155.77 ± 18.09 nm (Fig.  E).
As acid-sensitive nanomaterials, CaCO₃ exhibits distinct advantages in the delivery of therapeutic agents for tumor treatment. Its capacity to exploit the acidic conditions characteristic of the tumor microenvironment facilitates targeted drug delivery and release . Due to the Warburg effect, cancer cells favor glycolysis for energy generation even when oxygen is sufficient . This shift in metabolism results in lactic acid buildup, which lowers the pH of the tumor microenvironment. The typically low pH of the tumor microenvironment provides optimal conditions for acid-sensitive nanomaterials to initiate selective drug release at tumor sites . This mechanism not only enhances therapeutic efficacy but also reduces adverse effects on healthy tissues. Additionally, the high biocompatibility and low toxicity of CaCO₃ nanomaterials further underscore their potential as carriers for drug delivery.
The acidity-responsive characteristics of CCL@Bmi1 siRNA nanocapsules were assessed by dispersing the particles in buffers with varying pH levels. The kinetics of Pt release were subsequently analyzed, revealing that only 9.42% of Pt was released after 40 min in a buffer with a pH of 7.4. Conversely, at a pH of 6.8, 17.45% of Pt was released within the first 10 min, with the release increasing to 43.24% after 40 min. In a buffer with a pH of 6.5, both the rate and total quantity of Pt release were significantly enhanced, with 21.17% released at 10 min and 61.11% after 40 min (Fig.  F). These findings indicate that the release rate of platinum from CCL@Bmi1 siRNA nanocapsules is pH-dependent, with more rapid and substantial release occurring at lower pH levels. To evaluate stability, CL@Bmi1 siRNA and CCL@Bmi1 siRNA nanocapsules were incubated in a 10% FBS solution to simulate blood conditions. The findings indicated that CCL@Bmi1 siRNA maintained consistent size and PDI over 240 h, which can be attributed to the liposomal coating that conferred enhanced stability(Fig.  G and H). These results indicate that the lipid coating effectively protects the nanocapsules against aggregation and siRNA dissociation under simulated physiological conditions. The progressive disappearance of free siRNA bands with increasing CCL/siRNA weight ratios confirmed efficient complex formation, and an optimal binding ratio was identified based on complete gel retardation (Fig.  I). The encapsulation efficiency of cisplatin in CCL@Bmi1 siRNA nanocapsules was determined by ICP-MS and reached 82.1%, indicating efficient drug loading.

 

## The uptake of CCL@Bmi1 siRNA in resistant OC Cells
Chemoresistance in OC, particularly resistance to cisplatin, poses a significant clinical challenge that substantially limits therapeutic efficacy . A key mechanism contributing to this resistance is the diminished intracellular uptake and accumulation of cisplatin within tumor cells . To evaluate the potential of drug delivery systems in enhancing drug accumulation within resistant cells, intracellular Pt concentrations were quantified in both standard SK-OV-3 cells and cisplatin-resistant SK-OV-3/DDP cells following administration of free CDDP, CCL, CL@Bmi1 siRNA, and CCL@Bmi1 siRNA. As depicted in Fig.  A, Pt levels were consistent across all treatment groups in SK-OV-3 cells, measuring approximately 600 ng per 5 × 10⁵ cells, which suggests efficient drug uptake in cisplatin-sensitive cells. In contrast, SK-OV-3/DDP cells exhibited significantly lower Pt accumulation when treated with free CDDP, with levels at 200 ng per 5 × 10⁵ cells. Notably, Pt accumulation in SK-OV-3/DDP cells showed a progressive increase with CCL treatment (287 ng), CL@Bmi1 siRNA (375 ng), and reached its peak in the CCL@Bmi1 siRNA group, with 490 ng per 5 × 10⁵ cells.
Fluorescence microscopy demonstrated a temporal escalation in intracellular fluorescence intensity subsequent to incubation with CCL@Bmi1 siRNA in SK-OV-3/DDP cells (Fig.  B). Importantly, fluorescence signals were observable after 2 h and exhibited significant accumulation between 4 and 8 h, indicating effective internalization and intracellular retention of the nanocomplexes. Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed these observations. As shown in Fig.  C, the fluorescence peak of SK-OV-3/DDP cells treated with CCL@Bmi1 siRNA was markedly shifted to the right compared with control and free siRNA groups. Quantitative analysis demonstrated a significant increase in mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) in the CCL@Bmi1 siRNA group across different incubation times (Fig.  D), indicating superior cellular uptake efficiency mediated by lipid-coated nanocapsules. Furthermore, an extended incubation period of 48 h indicated that SK-OV-3/DDP cells treated with CCL@Bmi1 siRNA exhibited significantly higher intracellular fluorescence signals compared to the control group (Fig.  E and F). These findings suggest that the CCL@Bmi1 siRNA delivery system facilitates prolonged and effective internalization of siRNA in cisplatin-resistant OC cells.

 

## The anti-tumor effect of CCL@Bmi1 siRNA in resistant OC cells
To further investigate the antitumor efficacy of CCL@Bmi1 siRNA, its inhibitory effects on tumor cell proliferation were evaluated. We assessed the viability of SK-OV-3 and cisplatin-resistant SK-OV-3/DDP cells following a 24-h exposure to varying concentrations of CDDP, CCL, CL@Bmi1 siRNA, and CCL@Bmi1 siRNA. As illustrated in Fig.  A, CCL@Bmi1 siRNA demonstrated a more substantial inhibition of tumor cell proliferation in SK-OV-3 cells compared to CDDP. Notably, in cisplatin-resistant SK-OV-3/DDP cells, free CDDP exhibited a limited capacity to suppress tumor cell growth, whereas treatment with CCL@Bmi1 siRNA significantly reduced cell viability, indicating an effective reversal of cisplatin resistance (Fig.  B).
For gene silencing analysis, SK-OV-3/DDP cells were treated with CCL@Bmi1 siRNA corresponding to a Bmi1 siRNA concentration of 50 nM for 24 h prior to RNA extraction. As shown in Fig.  C, cells treated with CL@Bmi1 siRNA and CCL@Bmi1 siRNA exhibited a marked reduction in Bmi1 mRNA levels compared with the CDDP and CCL groups. These results demonstrate that the nanocapsule system efficiently delivers Bmi1 siRNA into resistant ovarian cancer cells and achieves effective gene silencing at the mRNA level. Also, downregulation of Bmi1 may inhibit pathways associated with resistance, and that CCL@Bmi1 siRNA represents a promising therapeutic strategy to restore cisplatin sensitivity in OC.
To further elucidate the underlying cellular responses, apoptosis was evaluated using FACS. Treatment with CDDP at a concentration of 2.5 µg/mL did not result in significant apoptosis in SK-OV-3/DDP cells after 24 h, which is consistent with the impaired apoptotic responsiveness observed in resistant cells. Conversely, treatment with CCL@Bmi1 siRNA significantly increased the apoptotic cell population, with apoptosis rates reaching 96.71% (Fig.  D–E). These findings suggest that the co-delivery of CDDP and Bmi1 siRNA effectively restores the induction of apoptosis in drug-resistant OC cells. Furthermore, cell cycle analysis demonstrated that treatment with CCL@Bmi1 siRNA resulted in a pronounced arrest in the G2/M phase, in contrast to treatment with CDDP alone (Fig.  F–G). This disruption of the cell cycle aligns with the observed increase in growth suppression and may contribute to the enhanced chemosensitivity noted. Taken together, these findings indicate that CCL@Bmi1 siRNA exhibit significant antitumor activity in cisplatin-resistant OC cells by augmenting cytotoxic effects, inducing apoptosis, and interfering with cell cycle progression, thereby highlighting their potential as a strategy to overcome chemoresistance.

 

## RNA sequencing analysis reveals transcriptional reprogramming induced by CCL@Bmi1 siRNA
To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the antitumor effects of CCL@Bmi1 siRNA, RNA sequencing was conducted on SK-OV-3/DDP cells treated with either CCL@Bmi1 siRNA or a control (Fig.  A). Differential expression analysis identified a substantial number of genes exhibiting significant upregulation or downregulation following treatment. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that these differentially expressed genes were predominantly associated with signal transduction, regulation of cellular processes, and response to stimulus (Fig.  B). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further revealed significant alterations in multiple signaling pathways, with the cAMP signaling pathway being notably enriched (Fig.  C). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) demonstrated a significant negative enrichment of the cAMP signaling pathway in the CCL@Bmi1 siRNA-treated group compared to the control (Fig.  D). Correspondingly, a heatmap of genes associated with the cAMP signaling pathway displayed coordinated expression changes post-treatment, suggesting a systematic suppression of this pathway (Fig.  E).
RNA-seq analysis demonstrated that silencing Bmi1 led to extensive transcriptional reprogramming in cisplatin-resistant OC cells. Enrichment analyses consistently identified the cAMP signaling pathway as a significantly impacted pathway following CCL@Bmi1 siRNA treatment. The coordinated downregulation of genes associated with the cAMP pathway suggests a functional suppression of this pathway, which has been linked to cancer cell survival and chemoresistance , . These transcriptomic changes align with the observed antitumor effects in vivo, such as reduced tumor proliferation and decreased expression of markers associated with cancer stem cells and drug resistance, thereby providing mechanistic evidence supporting the therapeutic potential of CCL@Bmi1 siRNA. Future work will focus on validating key pathway components and elucidating their functional significance in chemoresistance and cancer stemness.

 

## In vivo biodistribution profiles of CCL@Bmi1 siRNA
To more precisely replicate the native tumor microenvironment of OC, this study utilized an primary tumor model. Primary tumors form within the correct anatomical location and maintain essential interactions with the surrounding stroma, vasculature, and extracellular matrix, thus offering a more physiologically relevant context for tumor growth, progression, and therapeutic response . In this study, we developed a primary ovarian cancer model by co-injecting Bmi1 and Myc overexpression plasmids (totaling 5 µg) directly into the ovary. Bmi1 is known to enhance cancer stemness and chemoresistance, whereas Myc facilitates cell cycle progression and proliferation, collectively synergizing to drive tumor initiation. This model was used to evaluate the in vivo therapeutic effectiveness and pharmacodynamics of the CCL@Bmi1 siRNA delivery system.
At first, we examined drug delivery in mice with labeled CCL@Bmi1 siRNA, noting its presence in tumor tissues over different time periods. The findings showed that after CCL@Bmi1 siRNA was administered through the caudal vein, the fluorescence intensity peaked, indicating that CCL@Bmi1 siRNA circulated throughout the body for 8 h before starting to gather at the tumor site (Fig.  A). The biodistribution analysis of Pt in murine models demonstrated that CCL@Bmi1 siRNA facilitated rapid and substantial accumulation within tumor tissues, with peak levels observed at 8 h and sustained high intratumoral concentrations at 24 h. Furthermore, CCL@Bmi1 siRNA exhibited decreased Pt deposition in hepatic and renal tissues, suggesting a diminished risk of systemic toxicity. These findings suggest that the CCL@Bmi1 siRNA delivery system effectively enhances targeted cisplatin transport to tumor sites and ensures prolonged intratumoral retention. This system shows tumor-targeting specificity and beneficial pharmacokinetic characteristics due to its prolonged circulation. Importantly, Pt accumulation in the liver and kidneys was very low, indicating a reduced potential for systemic toxicity (Fig.  B–G).

 

## CCL@Bmi1 siRNA suppress tumor growth in primary ovarian cancer model
Following the successful establishment of the primary ovarian cancer mouse model, the animals were randomly allocated into five distinct groups: Ctrl, CDDP, CCL, CL@Bmi1 siRNA, and CCL@Bmi1 siRNA. The mice received their respective treatments via tail vein injection four times over the course of 1 week. At the fourth week, the mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were collected for subsequent analysis. As illustrated in Fig.  A–C, the administration of CCL@Bmi1 siRNA significantly inhibited tumor growth compared to the other groups. Importantly, the CCL@Bmi1 siRNA group demonstrated the lowest tumor weights, underscoring its superior antitumor efficacy in vivo.
To further clarify the biological effects of CCL@Bmi1 siRNA in vivo, immunohistochemical analyses were conducted on tumor tissues in Fig.  A. Ki67 was employed as a marker for cellular proliferation, whereas caspase-3 was utilized as an indicator of apoptosis. CD133 and Bmi1 were assessed as markers associated with OC stemness . Cyclin D1 was examined as a crucial regulator of cell cycle progression , and the multidrug resistance-related transporters P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and multidrug resistance-associated protein (MRP) were evaluated due to their recognized roles in mediating chemoresistance . As illustrated in Fig.  D, tumors from the group treated with CCL@Bmi1 siRNA exhibited a marked reduction in the expression levels of Ki67, CD133, Bmi1, Cyclin D1, P-gp, and MRP when compared to the other treatment groups. Conversely, there was a notable increase in tunel and caspase-3-positive staining, indicating an enhancement in apoptotic activity. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that CCL@Bmi1 siRNA treatment effectively suppresses tumor proliferation, diminishes cancer stem cell-associated signaling, promotes apoptosis, and decreases the expression of proteins related to multidrug resistance in primary ovarian cancer tissues.
Bmi1 has been recognized as a pivotal oncogenic regulator in OC, significantly contributing to the maintenance of CSC-associated characteristics, mediating epigenetic modifications, and promoting the development of chemoresistance. Elevated Bmi1 expression correlates with increased levels of CSC markers, such as CD133, and the activation of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, including MRPs and P-gp , . These transporters collectively facilitate the efflux of chemotherapeutic agents from tumor cells, thereby diminishing intracellular drug accumulation and reducing therapeutic efficacy , . Consistent with these mechanisms, the coordinated downregulation of Bmi1, CD133, P-gp, and MRP observed in tumor tissues following CCL@Bmi1 siRNA treatment substantiates the significant tumor growth inhibition and enhanced apoptotic activity observed in primary ovarian cancer mouse model. Through these pathways, Bmi1 contributes to tumor cell survival, metastatic potential, and resistance to platinum-based chemotherapy . Future studies will include functional cancer stem cell assays, such as sphere formation, to further validate the effects of CCL@Bmi1 siRNA on CSC self-renewal and stemness-related phenotypes.

 

## CCL@Bmi1 siRNA suppress tumor growth in OC xenograft mouse models
To further assess the in vivo antitumor efficacy of CCL@Bmi1 siRNA, a subcutaneous OC xenograft model was developed using nude mice , . This methodology facilitates the controlled introduction of a specific quantity of tumor cells into animals, resulting in consistent tumor development, minimal inter-individual variability, and a high tumor engraftment rate . Specifically, 4 × 10⁶ OC cells were inoculated into the right flank of each mouse. Once the tumor volumes reached approximately 100 mm³, the animals were randomly assigned to one of five groups: Ctrl, CDDP, CCL, CL@Bmi1 siRNA, and CCL@Bmi1 siRNA. The respective formulations were administered via tail vein injection four times over the course of 1 week. Tumor volume and body weight were regularly monitored throughout the treatment period to evaluate therapeutic efficacy and systemic safety.
Upon conclusion of the study, the mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were collected for subsequent analysis (Fig.  A). As illustrated in Fig.  B and C, the group treated with CCL@Bmi1 siRNA demonstrated the most significant inhibition of tumor growth, with a final tumor volume of 108.21 mm³, in contrast to 1022.27 mm³ in the control group, 775.27 mm³ in the CDDP group, 664.11 mm³ in the CCL group, and 276.76 mm³ in the CL@Bmi1 siRNA group. Correspondingly, tumor weights in the CCL@Bmi1 siRNA group were markedly lower than those observed in all other treatment groups (Fig.  D). Furthermore, longitudinal assessments indicated a sustained suppression of tumor growth throughout the observation period in the CCL@Bmi1 siRNA group (Fig.  E). Notably, no significant alterations in body weight were detected among the various treatment groups throughout the study (Fig.  F), suggesting the systemic tolerability of all formulations. Furthermore, immunohistochemical fluorescence analysis of tumor tissues derived from the xenograft model provided additional evidence supporting the antitumor efficacy of CCL@Bmi1 siRNA. As illustrated in Fig.  G, tumors treated with CCL@Bmi1 siRNA demonstrated significantly reduced expression levels of Ki67, CD133, Bmi1, Cyclin D1, P-gp, and MRP in comparison to the Ctrl, CDDP, CCL, and CL@Bmi1 siRNA groups. These findings indicate an effective suppression of tumor proliferation, cancer stem cell-associated signaling pathways, and drug efflux mechanisms in cisplatin-resistant OC.
Furthermore, serum biochemical assessments of liver and kidney function, along with HE staining of major organs, indicated no apparent hepatic or renal toxicity in BALB/c nude mice administered with CCL@Bmi1 siRNA (Fig.  ), thereby reinforcing the favorable biocompatibility of the delivery system. These findings, consistent with those obtained from the primary ovarian cancer model, demonstrate that CCL@Bmi1 siRNA exhibits potent antitumor activity in ovarian cancer xenografts while maintaining an excellent safety profile. The observed enhancement in therapeutic efficacy within this model further underscores the potential of Bmi1-targeted nanotherapeutic strategies to overcome cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer.

 

 

## Conclusions
In conclusion, we have successfully engineered lipid-coated CaCO₃/cisplatin nanocapsules for the co-delivery of Bmi1 siRNA (CCL@Bmi1 siRNA) and demonstrated their therapeutic potential in addressing cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer. This nanoplatform significantly enhanced the antitumor efficacy of cisplatin in drug-resistant ovarian cancer models, accompanied by the suppression of cancer stem cell-associated characteristics and the downregulation of multidrug resistance-related proteins in vivo. By concurrently modulating tumor proliferation, apoptosis, stemness, and drug efflux pathways, CCL@Bmi1 siRNA achieved substantial tumor growth inhibition while exhibiting minimal systemic toxicity, indicating a favorable safety profile. Collectively, these findings underscore the promise of Bmi1-targeted co-delivery nanotherapeutic strategies for the treatment of chemoresistant ovarian cancer and provide a robust foundation for future translational and clinical investigations. While this study highlights the potential of CCL@Bmi1 siRNA in addressing chemoresistance in OC, further research is necessary to validate the key molecular pathways and conduct functional CSC assays, alongside comprehensive long-term safety evaluations.
