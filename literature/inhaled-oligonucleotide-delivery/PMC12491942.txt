## Abstract
Objectives: Many chemicals have been used for industrial purposes, and some of them are carcinogenic to humans. However, the molecular mechanisms of their carcinogenetic effects have not been well understood. Reactive oxygen species are generated from industrial chemicals and contribute to carcinogenesis. Particles and fibers are accumulated in respiratory systems by inhalation exposure and cause chronic inflammation. Under inflammatory conditions, reactive nitrogen species are generated from inflammatory and epithelial cells. These species cause oxidative and nitrative DNA damage, leading to carcinogenesis. We carried out experiments on DNA damage induced by various industrial chemicals and investigated their molecular mechanisms.
Methods: We examined oxidative DNA damage induced by industrial chemicals using DNA fragments derived from human cancer-relevant genes by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Using immunohistochemistry and immunocytochemistry we also examined the formation of 8-nitroguanine (8-nitroG), a DNA lesion formed under inflammatory conditions, in lung tissues and cultured cells exposed to industrial chemicals.
Results: Benzene and o-toluidine metabolites caused oxidative damage to DNA fragments in the presence of Cu(II). H2O2 and Cu(I) were generated during oxidation of these chemicals and involved in DNA damage. 8-NitroG formation was observed in lung tissues of asbestos-exposed mice and humans. Carbon nanomaterials and indium compounds induced 8-nitroG formation in human lung epithelial cells via the release of damage-associated molecular patterns from exposed cells.
Conclusions: Various industrial chemicals are considered to induce carcinogenesis by causing oxidative and nitrative DNA damage. These findings provide an insight into risk assessment of industrial chemicals and prevention of carcinogenesis in workplaces.
Keywords: industrial chemicals, DNA damage, reactive oxygen species, nitric oxide, cancer, inflammation

Key points
What is already known on this topic: Some industrial chemicals are known to cause carcinogenesis by forming DNA adducts by the interaction of their metabolites with DNA bases. For example, carcinogenic aromatic amines are N-hydroxylated and converted to reactive nitrenium ions to form DNA adducts.
What this study adds: We performed experiments on DNA damage induced by industrial chemicals via the generation of reactive oxygen species and reactive nitrogen species. We found that reactive oxygen species are generated from industrial chemicals and their metabolites and cause oxidative DNA damage. In addition, particles and fibers cause inflammatory responses and the generation of nitric oxide, resulting in nitrative DNA damage.
How this study might affect research, practice, or policy: Our findings provide an insight into the evaluation of carcinogenic risks of industrial chemicals and prevention of carcinogenesis in workplaces.

## 1. Carcinogenicity and DNA damage induced by industrial chemicals
A large number of chemicals have been used for various industrial purposes. Some chemical compounds are hazardous and capable of causing carcinogenesis in exposed workers. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has classified industrial chemicals, such as organic solvents (benzene, 1,2-dichloropropane, and trichloroethylene), metal compounds (chromium(VI), nickel, arsenic, beryllium, and cadmium), aromatic amines (benzidine, 4-aminobiphenyl, benzidine, and o-toluidine), and particulate and fibrous substances (asbestos and silica dust) as Group 1 carcinogens (carcinogenic to humans). However, the molecular mechanisms of carcinogenesis induced by these chemicals have not been well understood.
DNA damage, including base modification and strand breaks, is an important first step in the process of carcinogenesis. Some industrial chemicals are known to form DNA adducts by interaction of their metabolites with DNA bases. For example, carcinogenic aromatic amines, such as 4-aminobiphenyl and 2-naphthylamine, are N-hydroxylated by cytochrome P450 and converted to reactive nitrenium ions, which subsequently bind to DNA. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are highly reactive forms of molecular oxygen, including hydroxyl radical (•OH), superoxide (O2•−), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and singlet oxygen (1O2). ROS are generated from multiple sources in biological systems, such as the mitochondrial matrix, inflammatory cells, metabolism of nucleic acids, and oxidation of chemical compounds. Excessive generation of ROS is involved in the pathogenesis of various human diseases, including cancer. ROS cause oxidative damage to cellular constituents, such as nucleic acids (DNA and RNA), proteins, and lipids. The representative product of oxidative DNA modification is 8-oxo-7,8-dihydro-2′-deoxyguanosine (8-oxodG). 8-OxodG is a mutagenic DNA lesion, because during DNA synthesis misincorporation of adenine occurs opposite 8-oxodG, leading to G → T transversions. 
Chronic inflammation contributes to a substantial part (~25%) of all cancer cases worldwide. In addition to infectious and inflammatory diseases, exposure to industrial chemicals, especially particulate and fibrous substances, causes chronic inflammation in biological systems. Under inflammatory conditions, reactive nitrogen species (RNS), including nitric oxide (NO), are generated from inflammatory and epithelial cells as well as ROS. 8-Nitroguanine (8-nitroG) is a mutagenic DNA lesion formed during inflammatory conditions. 8-NitroG is produced by the interaction of guanine with highly reactive peroxynitrite (ONOO−), which is formed by the reaction of O2•− with NO. 
To clarify the mechanism of carcinogenesis, we investigated oxidative DNA damage induced by various industrial chemicals using DNA fragments derived from human cancer-relevant genes. In addition, we examined 8-nitroG formation in cultured cells and lung tissues of experimental animals and humans exposed to industrial chemicals. ## 2. Oxidative DNA damage induced by industrial chemicals
ROS can be generated by oxidation and/or reduction of various chemicals. The Fenton reaction is a well-known iron-catalyzed reaction of H2O2 to generate •OH: Fe(II) + H2O2 → Fe(III) + OH- + •OH. The •OH radical is an extremely reactive species that damages all DNA bases (guanine, adenine, cytosine, and thymine) without marked site specificity. 1O2 is generated through reaction of carcinogenic metals with H2O2, and excitation of photosensitizers by ultraviolet irradiation. Unlike •OH, 1O2 specifically causes damage to guanine, because guanine has the lowest oxidation potential among the DNA bases and is most likely to be oxidized. Therefore, the site specificity of DNA damage is dependent on the types of ROS.
Using polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, we investigated DNA damage induced by industrial chemicals using 32P-labeled DNA fragments obtained from human cancer-relevant genes, such as the c-Ha-ras-1 proto-oncogene and the p53 tumor suppressor gene. Kawanishi et al demonstrated that sodium chromate(VI) caused DNA damage in the presence of H2O2 by generating •OH and 1O2, and first proposed that ROS participate in metal-induced carcinogenesis. Other carcinogenic metal compounds, including Fe(III)-nitrilotriacetate, cobalt(II), and nickel(II), also induced oxidative DNA damage in the presence of H2O2. Based on these studies, we investigated DNA damage mediated by ROS that are generated from the interaction of industrial chemicals with endogenous metal ions, as described below.

## 2.1. Benzene metabolites
Benzene is a widely used organic solvent, a petroleum byproduct, and a ubiquitous environmental pollutant. Benzene causes hematological disorders, including aplastic anemia and leukemia, in exposed workers. Benzene metabolism is important in exerting its toxicity. Benzene is metabolized to phenol by cytochrome P450 in the liver and then to hydroquinone (HQ) and catechol. These metabolites are further converted to quinones by peroxidase in the bone marrow. We examined DNA damage in cultured cells treated with benzene metabolites, HQ and 1,4-benzoquinone (1,4-BQ). Both metabolites induced DNA strand breaks in HL60 cells, derived from human promyelocytic leukemia cells, and 1,4-BQ caused DNA damage at lower concentrations than HQ. We investigated the mechanism of DNA damage induced by these metabolites using DNA fragments obtained from the c-Ha-ras-1 proto-oncogene. HQ induced DNA damage at preferably thymine and guanine residues in the presence of Cu(II). 1,4-BQ induced DNA damage in the presence of both Cu(II) and NADH, and showed a similar site specificity. Catalase and bathocuproine inhibited DNA damage induced by these metabolites, suggesting the involvement of H2O2 and Cu(I). These benzene metabolites induced no or weak DNA damage in the presence of Fe(II) and Fe(III). These results suggest that HQ is oxidized to the semiquinone radical and 1,4-BQ, which undergoes NADH-mediated reduction to the semiquinone radical to form a redox cycle. H2O2 and Cu(I) are generated during oxidation of these metabolites and form a metal-oxygen complex capable of causing DNA damage. 

## 2.2. o-Toluidine metabolites

o-Toluidine is an aromatic amine that has been used in the chemical industry as an intermediate for dyes, rubber chemicals, pesticides, and pharmaceuticals. o-Toluidine causes bladder cancer in exposed workers and has been evaluated as a Group 1 carcinogen by the IARC. We examined DNA damage induced by its major metabolite, 4-amino-3-methylphenol (4-amino-m-cresol) using DNA fragments of the c-Ha-ras-1 proto-oncogene and the p53 tumor suppressor gene. This metabolite caused DNA damage in the presence of Cu(II), but no or weak DNA damage occurred in the presence of iron. DNA damage was reduced by catalase and bathocuproine, similarly to benzene metabolites. It is speculated that H2O2 and Cu(I) are generated during the oxidation of 4-amino-3-methylphenol to the aminomethylphenoxyl radical to cause DNA damage. 

## 2.3. δ-Aminolevulinic acid
Lead has been used in a wide variety of products, including paint, ceramics, pipes and plumbing materials, batteries, and cosmetics. Lead and inorganic lead compounds have been evaluated as Group 2B and 2A carcinogens by the IARC, respectively. Unlike carcinogenic metals, such as chromium(VI), cobalt(II), and nickel(II), lead itself is incapable of causing oxidative DNA damage in a cell-free system. δ-Aminolevulinic acid (ALA) is a heme precursor, which is accumulated in lead-exposed subjects by inhibiting the activity of ALA dehydratase. We examined oxidative DNA damage induced by ALA as an indirect mechanism of lead carcinogenesis using DNA fragments obtained from the c-Ha-ras-1 proto-oncogene. ALA induced DNA damage at thymine residues and 8-oxodG formation in the presence of Cu(II), and DNA damage was reduced by catalase and bathocuproine. ALA caused no or weak DNA damage in the presence of iron. These results suggest that H2O2 and Cu(I) are generated during ALA auto-oxidation to 4,5-dioxovaleric acid and contribute to DNA damage. Alternatively, there is a possibility that two ALA molecules are condensed to a dihydropyrazine derivative and H2O2 and Cu(I) are generated during its oxidation. 
Copper is an important trace element and a cofactor of diverse enzymes. A substantial amount of the copper is located in the mammalian cell nucleus where it contributes to high-order chromatin structures. Therefore, endogenous copper ions may catalyze ROS generation from chemical compounds in biological systems to mediate oxidative DNA damage and play a role in carcinogenesis. In our studies, the above chemical compounds induced no or weak DNA damage in the presence of iron. Although the contribution of the Fenton reaction to DNA damage appears to be small in our experiments, its significance in biological systems needs further investigation.

## 3. Nitrative DNA damage induced by industrial chemicals
Inhalation exposure of particulate and fibrous substances causes chronic inflammation by their accumulation in respiratory systems, resulting in carcinogenesis. Under inflammatory conditions, ROS and RNS are generated from inflammatory and epithelial cells. NO, a representative RNS, is produced in these cells via the expression of inducible NO synthase (iNOS), whereas ROS are generated from multiple sources in addition to inflammation. NO reacts with O2•− to form highly reactive ONOO−, which interacts with guanine to form 8-nitroG and 8-oxodG. 8-NitroG formed in DNA is chemically unstable and easily released, resulting in the formation of an apurinic site. The apurinic site forms a pair preferably with adenine during DNA synthesis, resulting in G → T transversions. Alternatively, incorporation of adenine opposite 8-nitroG occurs during DNA synthesis, leading to G → T transversions. Therefore, 8-nitroG is a potentially mutagenic DNA lesion involved in inflammation-related carcinogenesis. Using immunohistochemistry and a specific antibody produced by our group, we have demonstrated that 8-nitroG was formed at the sites of carcinogenesis in various animal models and in patients with cancer-prone infectious and inflammatory diseases. We also found that particles and fibers used in industry cause 8-nitroG formation in lung tissues and cultured cells as described below.

## 3.1. Asbestos
Asbestos is a naturally occurring fiber that has been used for a wide variety of industrial purposes. Asbestos includes serpentine (chrysotile) and amphiboles (crocidolite, amosite, actinolite, anthophyllite, and tremolite), all of which are classified as Group 1 carcinogens by the IARC. Inhalation exposure of asbestos causes lung cancer and malignant mesothelioma of the pleura and peritoneum. Several possible mechanisms for asbestos-induced carcinogenesis have been proposed, such as irritation of the pleura, mitosis disruption, ROS generation, and activation of signaling pathways to upregulate proto-oncogenes. However, the precise mechanisms of carcinogenesis are still unclear.
We examined 8-nitroG formation in lung tissues of asbestos-exposed mice and humans by immunohistochemistry. In an animal experiment, asbestos fibers (chrysotile and crocidolite) were intratracheally administered to mice, and 8-nitroG formation was observed in bronchial epithelial cells. Crocidolite induced significantly more intense 8-nitroG formation than chrysotile, consistent with their carcinogenic potentials. We also examined the relationship between asbestos contents and the intensity of 8-nitroG formation in human lung tissues. In subjects without mesothelioma, staining intensity of 8-nitroG was significantly correlated with total asbestos and amphibole content, but not with chrysotile content. In mesothelioma patients, the staining intensity was not correlated with asbestos contents. These findings raise a possibility that 8-nitroG serves as a potential biomarker to estimate the extent of asbestos exposure. 
In addition to DNA damage, we examined microRNA (miRNA) expression in lung tissues of asbestos-exposed mice. miRNAs are short noncoding RNAs that suppress gene expression and participate in various human diseases including cancer. Microarray analysis showed that miR-21, an oncogenic miRNA, was significantly upregulated by both chrysotile and crocidolite, and its target tumor suppressor genes—programmed cell death 4 (Pdcd4) and reversion-inducing-cysteine-rich protein with kazal motifs (Reck)—were downregulated. Using real-time polymerase chain reaction, western blotting, and immunohistochemistry, we confirmed that asbestos exposure downregulated these genes at the transcriptional and translational levels. Alteration in miRNA expression patterns may contribute to asbestos-induced carcinogenesis in addition to DNA damage.

## 3.2. Carbon nanomaterials
Nanomaterials are defined as materials having structural features with at least 1 dimension of 100 nm or less. Carbon black (CB) is a nanomaterial used mainly in rubber products, and inhalation exposure causes lung tumors in experimental animals. CB has been evaluated as Group 2B by the IARC. We demonstrated that CB exposure induced 8-nitroG formation in RAW 264.7 macrophages and A549 lung epithelial cells. This process involves cellular uptake of CB through clathrin-mediated endocytosis. 
Carbon nanotubes (CNTs) have been recently used for a wide range of industrial purposes and make up the majority of carbon nanomaterials. However, intraperitoneal administration of CNTs causes mesothelioma in experimental animals, raising a concern of carcinogenicity in humans. The IARC classified a certain type of multi-walled CNT (MWCNT) as a Group 2B carcinogen. We examined 8-nitroG formation in A549 human lung epithelial cells treated with MWCNTs. These significantly increased 8-nitroG formation in exposed cells, which was largely suppressed by inhibitors of caveola- and clathrin-mediated endocytosis. To clarify the mechanism of DNA damage, we examined the role of Toll-like receptor (TLR) 9, which resides in endosomes and lysosomes. Small interfering RNA (siRNA) for TLR9 and antibodies against high-mobility group box-1 (HMGB1) and receptor for advanced glycation end-products (RAGE) reduced MWCNT-induced 8-nitroG formation. MWCNT exposure increased the release of HMGB1 and double-stranded DNA into the culture supernatant from the cells. These results suggest that MWCNTs induce cell injury or necrosis, and HMGB1 and CpG DNA are released into the extracellular space. The HMGB1-DNA complex binds to RAGE on the surface of neighboring cells and is taken into lysosomes, where CpG DNA is recognized by TLR9, leading to NO generation and 8-nitroG formation ( ). Damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) are endogenous molecules, including HMGB1, S100 proteins, heat-shock proteins, and DNA, that are released from damaged or dying cells. These molecules activate the innate immune system by interacting with pattern recognition receptors, such as TLRs. This study proposes a hypothesis that DAMPs released from exposed cells and the resulting inflammatory responses play a role in carcinogenesis induced by industrial chemicals. 
 In this study, we did not observe an increase in 8-oxodG formation in MWCNT-treated lung epithelial cells. This negative finding may be explained as follows. NO reacts with O2•− to form ONOO− and its conjugate acid ONOOH. ONOOH is decomposed into •OH and •NO2, which cause oxidative and nitrative DNA damage, respectively. Sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO3) exists abundantly in cell culture medium and under physiological conditions. Carbon dioxide (CO2) derived from bicarbonate reacts with ONOO− to form peroxynitrosocarbonate (ONOOCO2−), which undergoes homolysis into •NO2 and •CO3−. Therefore, MWCNTs are considered to preferably induce 8-nitroG formation rather than 8-oxodG in biological systems.

## 3.3. Indium compounds
Indium compounds have been used in manufacturing the displays of mobile phones, computers, and televisions. However, these compounds cause interstitial pneumonia in exposed workers. Indium compounds also cause lung cancer in experimental animals, raising a serious concern about carcinogenicity to humans. Indium phosphide and indium tin oxide (ITO) have been classified as Group 2A and 2B carcinogens by the IARC, respectively. To clarify the mechanism of indium-induced carcinogenesis, we examined 8-nitroG formation in A549 human lung epithelial cells. We used indium compounds, including nanoparticles of indium oxide (In2O3) and ITO and water-soluble indium chloride (InCl3). All indium compounds significantly increased 8-nitroG formation at low concentration of 5 ng/mL, relevant to the working environment. 8-NitroG formation was largely suppressed by siRNA and antibodies against HMGB1, RAGE, and TLR9. These results suggests that indium compounds induce DNA damage mediated by inflammatory responses via the HMGB1-RAGE-TLR9 pathway regardless of chemical properties. We examined 8-oxodG formation in calf thymus DNA treated with indium chloride (InCl3) and H2O2, but 8-oxodG formation did not increase (Y. Hiraku, MD, PhD, unpublished data, June 2011). Therefore, indium has no ability to generate ROS in a cell-free system unlike other carcinogenic metals, such as nickel and cobalt.
We also performed microarray analysis of gene expression in lung tissues of indium-exposed rats, which were exposed to In2O3 and ITO particles by intratracheal instillation. Microarray and bioinformatic analysis revealed that these indium compounds largely upregulated S100A9 and S100A8, which participate in immunomodulatory and inflammatory responses. Fluorescent immunohistochemistry revealed that S100A9 and S100A8 were expressed in alveolar epithelial cells and neutrophils in indium-exposed rats. S100 proteins are involved in proliferation, differentiation, inflammation, and migration. S100A9 mediates various respiratory diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder, infectious diseases, and lung cancer by forming a heterodimer with S100A8. S100 proteins may serve as DAMPs that contribute to indium-induced lung diseases via neutrophil-mediated inflammatory responses in addition to HMGB1.

## 4. Conclusions
We have investigated DNA damage induced by various industrial chemicals in relation to carcinogenesis. These chemicals cause oxidative and nitrative DNA damage via direct and indirect mechanisms. Benzene and o-toluidine metabolites caused oxidative DNA damage mediated by ROS directly generated through their oxidation. MWCNTs and indium compounds induced NO generation via DAMPs-mediated inflammatory responses and resulting nitrative DNA damage. These mechanisms may be ubiquitous in carcinogenesis induced by a wide variety of chemicals. Oxidative and nitrative DNA lesions, such as 8-oxodG and 8-nitroG, may serve as potential biomarkers to evaluate the carcinogenic risks of workers exposed to chemicals. In addition, the experimental systems to detect these DNA lesions may be used to evaluate the carcinogenicity of industrial chemicals. These findings provide an insight into the risk assessment and prevention of carcinogenesis in workplaces.
