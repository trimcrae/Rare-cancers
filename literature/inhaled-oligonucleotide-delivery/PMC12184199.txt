## ABSTRACT
Coxiella burnetii is responsible for Q fever, a zoonosis that mainly manifests as a mild acute infection and may persist in specific individuals causing severe complications, including endocarditis and vascular infections. Transmission mainly occurs through the inhalation of aerosols, underlining bacterial resistance to environmental stress. C. burnetii is an intracellular gram-negative pathogen that can infect and survive in monocytes and macrophages and replicate within acidic compartments. C. burnetii expresses several virulence factors, including lipopolysaccharide and a type IV secretion system, which are involved in hijacking several functions of the immune system and controlling cell death. This review summarizes research on C. burnetii infection from clinical presentation of Q fever to bacterial and host factors of virulence. It compiles immunological data associated with bacterial cure or persistence and discusses a potential link between C. burnetii-macrophage interaction and immune dysregulation of Q fever.

C. burnetii-induced human Q fever is a zoonosis that has been widely neglected. Among domestic ruminants, C. burnetii has been classified as a notifiable animal disease by the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) [ ] and the bacteria are known to be responsible for epizootics. Infected animals can develop metritis and mastitis, and these bacteria can also lead to increased morbidity and mortality (e.g. gestation abortions) in livestock. C. burnetii shedding was found to be higher in vaginal mucus and faeces than in milk during the first three weeks post-abortion or post-partum [ ].
The spread of C. burnetii from animals to humans has been reported in many countries. Serosurvey studies conducted in Europe and the Americas on herds and farms have demonstrated that the main animal reservoirs responsible for transmission to humans are domesticated ruminants, primarily goats, sheep, cattle, poultry, cats, dogs, and rabbits [ ]. However, this is not the case in Africa or Asia. Although there is good evidence of infection in herds of goats, sheep, and cattle in these regions, numerous seroprevalence studies have demonstrated that the highest percentage of carriage of the bacteria (60–80% of seroprevalence) is found in dromedary camel herds in Africa [ ]. In countries where populations of dromedary camels are very large, this species appears to be the main reservoir for C. burnetii. [ ] In the hot and dry regions of southern Europe, C. burnetii can be found in D. marginatus ticks of ruminants, whereas it can be found in H. dromedarii ticks of dromedary camels in the Maghreb [ , ].
However, even this notion of a “species barrier” does not correspond to what is observed in an ecosystem and it seems more rational to consider the transmission of the pathogen based on the model of its inter- and intra-species circulation [ ]. The emergence of an epidemic in humans is indeed the result of a double accident: the host-evolution of the pathogen and its amplification by societal factors.

## Update on Q fever
Q fever has been observed around the world, with varying prevalence depending on geographic areas. Some countries have a low seroprevalence (2.4%), such as the Netherlands, where a large Q fever outbreak was reported from 2007 to 2010, leading to 4000 reported cases. This was found to be related to goat farming in proximity to urban areas [ , ]. In contrast, African countries exhibit elevated C. burnetii seroprevalence [ ]. In Guiana, an outbreak of C. burnetii infection causes 24% of community-acquired cases of pneumonia, and it appears that bacterial clones involved in hyperendemic Q fever exhibit increased virulence [ ] C. burnetii can persist for long periods in the environment and infect a large spectrum of vertebrate and invertebrate hosts. Nevertheless, as stated above, its major reservoir consists of domestic ruminants [ ]. An increased frequency of Q fever cases was observed in southern France after a north-westerly wind and the lambing seasons. This observation is consistent with the fact that most human Q fever cases are observed after inhalation of C. burnetii-infected aerosols [ ]. The aerosol route of infection explains why C. burnetii could be used as a biological weapon [ ].

## Clinical presentation
Primary infection occurs after two or three weeks of incubation and is mostly asymptomatic (in approximately 60% of individuals) ( ). When it becomes symptomatic, it is referred to acute Q fever and mainly presents as pneumonia, hepatitis, and a flu-like syndrome. Cardiac involvement (pericarditis, myocarditis, acute endocarditis), neurological manifestations, and acute lymphadenitis can be observed [ ]. In a minority of patients, Q fever may become a chronic or persistent infection. Both terms are widely used and refer to debates about guidelines. Over several years, elevated titres of immunoglobulin (Ig)G1 directed against C. burnetii phase I were considered sufficient to differentiate primary Q fever from chronic Q fever [ ]. The variability of antibody production, the lack of information on the duration of the disease, and the description of acute C. burnetii-induced endocarditis led to a reappraisal of chronic Q fever, and some authors have proposed its replacement by C. burnetii persistent infectious foci [ ]. We have chosen to use the term “chronic/persistent Q fever” to reconcile these two points of view.
 
Q fever endocarditis is the usual form of chronic/persistent Q fever that affects the prognosis of the disease, with an overall mortality rate of 17% [ ]. The progression from primary infection to endocarditis is favoured when patients are men, over the age of 40, and present symptomatic or asymptomatic underlying valvular heart disease [ , ]. The incidence of endocarditis after acute Q fever is estimated to be approximately 40% in patients with valvulopathy [ ], and the risk of endocarditis is highest with aortic bicuspid and mitral valve prolapse [ ]. The clinical and biological presentation of Q fever endocarditis is non-specific, and diagnosis requires echocardiography and 18 F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) [ , ]. The clinical presentation of chronic/persistent Q fever is more complex. Vascular infections, such as neurological disorders and osteomyelitis, have been underestimated. Lymphadenitis is a new presentation of Q fever and opens the debate about the risk of lymphoma and the place of autoimmunity in Q fever [ , ].

## Diagnosis and treatment
The diagnosis of C. burnetii infection remains based on specific indirect tools in most routine laboratories but can be refined in specialized microbiology laboratories. Serology is the first-line diagnostic technique and relies on the detection of antibodies directed against phase II C. burnetii (obtained after several passages in cell cultures) and phase I C. burnetii (obtained from infected animals or individuals). The antibodies directed against phase II C. burnetii are markers of primary infection; they are detected one to two weeks after clinical onset and persist for three to six months. Anti-phase I C. burnetii antibodies are markers of chronic/persistent infections [ , ]. Molecular detection of C. burnetii based on targeting the 16S-23S ribonucleic acid (RNA), superoxide dismutase gene, com1 gene, and IS1111 repetitive element may be useful in seronegative primary infections [ ]. It has been suggested that high bacterial deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) load might be predictive of the risk of chronic/persistent Q fever [ ]. Culture with shell vial tests and immunohistochemistry of clinical samples may complete the diagnosis arsenal. Despite a polymorphism in clinical presentation, there is consensus regarding the management strategy for C. burnetii infection. Symptomatic primary infection benefits from doxycycline treatment (200 mg per day for two weeks), but other antibiotics, such as cotrimoxazole, can be efficient in cases of cyclin intolerance [ ]. Trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole is recommended by the Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) for treatment of acute Q fever in pregnant women. The treatment of Q fever endocarditis has been improved by early diagnosis and appropriate dual antibiotic therapy. This dual antibiotic therapy is based on the long-lasting association of doxycycline and hydroxychloroquine. Hydroxychloroquine is required to increase the pH of bacterial vacuoles and improve the bactericidal activity of the antibiotic [ , ]. The prognosis of Q fever endocarditis depends on the prophylactic treatment of primary Q fever in patients with valvulopathy [ ]. Nevertheless, the diversity of clinical practice imposes the need for randomised clinical trials to establish robust evidence-based protocols for managing patients [ ].

## Vaccines
Only one Q fever vaccine, known as Q-VAX, has been licensed in Australia since 1989, but its worldwide use has been limited by potential side effects in pre-sensitized individuals. Q-VAX is an inactivated whole-cell vaccine derived from the Henzerling phase I strain. Screening tests (serology and skin tests) may be useful to decrease the risk of side effects, but they are not mandatory [ , ]. Various vaccines against C. burnetii infection have been developed, but they do not provide protective immunity [ ]. Indeed, a vaccine strain constructed with a 32kb deletion including the Dot/icm region, which encodes a type IVB secretion system (T4BSS), has been tested in a guinea pig model of infection and underlined the indispensable role of this T4BSS for vaccine-induced protective immunity and post-vaccination hypersensitivity [ ]. Subunit vaccines have also been proposed; however, their ability to protect the host from C. burnetii is lower than that of Q-VAX. For instance, a subunit vaccine composed of C. burnetii com1 antigen, an outer membrane protein, formulated with a Toll-like receptor (TLR) triagonist, induced a strong immune response in a mouse model, but the resulting protection against C. burnetii was lower than that induced by Q-VAX [ ]. The development of new Q fever vaccines will require improvements in the identification of the immunological targets that program T and B cell responses. This should prevent vaccine-associated hypersensitivity. Finally, it is essential to define which individuals are candidates for vaccination in the context of vaccinal hesitancy in several countries around the world [ ]. In addition, as Q fever is a zoonosis, the development of veterinary vaccination of goats and cattle might also provide real benefits for human health [ ].

## Pathophysiology of Q fever

## Host determinants
The host factors, age, sex, and immunological status, have an impact on the symptomatology and prognosis of Q fever. Older men are more symptomatic than young and pregnant women [ , ]. Children are less symptomatic than adults [ ]. We found that bacterial burden and granuloma numbers were increased in tissues from 14-month old mice compared to that in younger mice [ ]. Despite the limitations of murine models of C. burnetii infection, these results underline the role of age in bacterial susceptibility. In addition, Q fever symptoms are more frequent in men than in women (male to female ratio: 5.5) despite similar exposure [ ]. Sex hormones may account for male predisposition, as shown in murine models. We showed that female mice are more resistant to C. burnetii than male mice, a characteristic which is lost after ovariectomy and restored by the administration of 17 β-estradiol [ ]. In a murine model of delayed-type hypersensitivity response to the C. burnetii vaccine, female mice displayed a more robust response than males, suggesting that males are less able to mount a protective response to C. burnetii than females [ ]. Investigation of gene expression signatures in mice infected with C. burnetii with or without castration revealed that a large proportion of genes were differentially modulated in males and females. Functional annotation of the modulated showed enrichment with inflammatory genes in males and, more surprisingly, enrichment with the circadian rhythm pathway [ , ]. We showed increased expression of PER2 (period circadian regulator 2), a negative regulator of the circadian oscillator, in males with acute Q fever [ ]. Recently, we showed that testosterone has a major impact on macrophages as compared with oestradiol, which increases the expression of immunoregulatory cytokines known to favour bacterial replication [ , ]. The other feature of host susceptibility to C. burnetii infection is the presence of an immune dysfunction. C. burnetii community-acquired pneumonia occurs in the context of immune suppression (diabetes, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), and corticosteroid treatment) [ ]. This susceptibility has been illustrated in several case reports (steroids [ ], anti-tumour necrosis factor (TNF)α [ ], and HIV [ ]. Nevertheless, except for epidemiological studies, data from humans are rare. The understanding of the pathophysiology of Q fever and its complications thus requires considering host factors not only in patients but also in exposed individuals.

## C. burnetii-associated immunopathology
The immunopathology of C. burnetii infection corresponds to tissue immune response to the microorganism and includes vascular, valvular and placenta lesions, lymphoma granuloma and autoimmune manifestations. The crosstalk between animal models’ data and translational studies is critical to understand the pathophysiology of Q fever.

## Valvular and vascular lesions
Endocarditis and vascular infections are the most severe complications of Q fever. Factors associated with progression to endocarditis after primary infection include being male, over the age of 40, and with underlying valvular heart disease [ ]. In contrast to infective endocarditis, in which inflammatory response and the presence of vegetation enable the diagnosis, histological analysis of cardiac valves from patients with Q fever endocarditis shows fibrosis, poor inflammation, and vascularization with minimal to no vegetation [ ]. The occurrence of endocarditis during Q fever evolution is associated with immune dysfunction, allowing C. burnetii to persist. Initial studies reported the occurrence of endocarditis in immunocompromised animals. Mice treated with cyclophosphamide exhibited C. burnetii disseminated infection [ ]. In rabbits and guinea pigs with mechanically induced valve lesions, C. burnetii was able to colonize valvular lesions and induced endocarditis [ , ]. In patients with underlying valvulopathy and Q fever, persistence of high levels of interleukin (IL)-10 is strongly associated with the development of infective endocarditis. An autoimmune mechanism has been suggested to account for the pathophysiology of Q fever endocarditis. First, autoimmune conditions like systemic lupus erythematosus may be complicated by endocarditis as Libman Sacks endocarditis [ ]. A C. burnetii-associated endocarditis was reported in a patient with systemic lupus erythematosus [ ]. The presence of anti-cardiolipin antibodies has been associated with the risk of Q fever endocarditis [ ] (see “2.3.2.3 Autoimmunity”). The presence of vascular lesions in persistent C. burnetii infections has been underestimated. Almost 80% of patients with vascular complications of Q fever had a vascular history consisting of aneurysms or vascular grafts. A necrotizing granulomatous response has been documented in symptomatic aneurysms. These lesions may increase the risk of aneurysm rupture [ ]. The mechanisms underlying vascular complications cannot be superimposed on atherosclerotic lesions. A study comparing the tissue immune landscape in an abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) associated with Q fever or atherosclerosis revealed a common inflammatory signature and some specificities [ ]. There is an accumulation of lymphocytes and large tertiary lymphoid structures in the adventitia of patients with Q fever. In Q fever-associated AAA, resident macrophages are polarized toward the M2 profile, and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and regulatory T cells are increased, in contrast to atherosclerotic AAA. Another specificity of Q fever-associated AAA is extensive fibrosis in the adventitia [ ], and M2 polarization of macrophages has been considered as a mechanism of fibrosis. The immunoregulatory signature of macrophages accounts for the low inflammatory microenvironment of Q fever endocarditis as compared with endocarditis due to extracellular pathogens in which macrophages are of the M1 type [ ]. The precise role of the immune response in the pathogenesis of vascular and valvular lesions associated with C. burnetii infection remains unclear. These questions underline the lack of a pertinent model to investigate the pathophysiology of vascular and valvular lesions in Q fever.

## Non-Hodgkin lymphomas
A link between bacterial infection and lymphoma has long been established [ ]. We reported that patients with chronic/persistent Q fever are at a higher risk of developing non-Hodgkin lymphomas (NHL) than patients with acute Q fever [ , ]. In these Q fever patients, overproduction of IL-10 by infected monocytes was found to be critical for sustaining the replication of C. burnetii and preventing the microbicidal activity of macrophages [ , ]. Interestingly, IL-10 enhances B cell survival, proliferation and differentiation [ ]. High levels of IL-10 are known to increase B-cell leukaemia/lymphoma 2 (BCL-2) expression in lymphoma cells [ ] and correlate with a worse prognosis in patients with diffuse large B cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [ ]. We identified a specific transcriptomic signature of C. burnetii-associated lymphadenitis among patients with chronic/persistent C. burnetii infection [ ]. These observations might support the hypothesis that lymphadenitis constitutes a step toward lymphomagenesis. In contrast, Weehuizen et al. investigated the incidence of mature B-cell non-Hodgkin in a Netherlands population between 2002 and 2017. In this population, which was subjected to a large outbreak, they found no association between Q fever and lymphoma [ ]. The answer to the question of the link between Q fever and lymphoma remains elusive and will largely depend on design quality of clinical studies.
We also investigated the impact of C. burnetii on cadherin switching, a hallmark of neoplasia [ ]. E-cadherin (E-cad) is a surface molecule that regulates cell-to-cell adhesion. After cleavage by sheddases, a soluble fragment (sE-cad) is released, which has been identified as a pro-carcinogenic inflammatory signal in several bacteria-induced cancers [ ]. sE-cad levels were increased in the sera of patients with acute and chronic/persistent Q fever [ ]. We found evidence that the cleavage of E-cad was mediated by a functional high-temperature requirement A (HtrA) sheddase encoded in the C. burnetii genome [ , ]. The cleavage of E-cad by the C. burnetii-encoded HtrA in macrophages leads to M2 polarization of the target cells and the induction of IL-10 secretion, which “disarms” the target cells and improves the intracellular replication of C. burnetii. This lifts the veil on the early stages of the infection process and suggests that deregulation of E-cad could be a central element in a long process that could lead, in rare cases, to the development of NHL.

## Autoimmunity
As observed in numerous infectious diseases, the biological markers of autoimmunity are frequently found in primary Q fever. They include anti-smooth muscle antibodies and anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies [ , ]. The clinical expression of autoimmune factors has rarely been described [ ]. Anti-phospholipid (aPL) antibodies have been described in acute Q fever and mainly consist of anti-cardiolipin (aCL) IgG, more frequently than aCL IgM, lupus anticoagulant, or anti-β2 glycoprotein [ ]. The presence of aCL antibodies is associated with the risk of thrombosis in patients with aPL syndrome. In a retrospective cohort of patients with acute Q fever between 2007 and 2015, a minority of patients with acute Q fever exhibited thrombosis and high levels of aCL IgG [ ]. aCL antibodies may also predict the risk of progression from acute Q fever to Q fever endocarditis [ ]. A new clinical entity is associated with acute Q fever endocarditis and the presence of aCL antibodies [ ]. Cardiolipin (CL) is a phospholipid that is present primarily in the inner mitochondrial membrane and is highly expressed in the heart. Various cardiovascular diseases are associated with defects in CL expression or metabolism [ ]. As C. burnetii exhibits notable tropism for the heart, one can speculate on the existence of shared epitopes between CL and pathogens as well as the possibility for oxidized phospholipids to create new antigenic targets. Molecular mimicry between pathogen antigens and host phospholipids such as CL may contribute to the development of autoantibodies [ ]. Furthermore, according to the “double hit” theory [ ] proposed to explain the pathophysiology of aPL syndrome, aPL could constitute the “first hit” in “predisposed” patients, leading to a procoagulant state, and after a “second hit” such as an infection, including C. burnetii infection, aPL could trigger the formation of a thrombus. Several questions remain unanswered. Do aCL IgGs recognize some determinants of C. burnetii? What is the pathogenicity of these autoantibodies and how can they favour the progression of Q fever endocarditis?

## Granulomas
Granulomas are tissue manifestations of cell-mediated immunity that are directed against C. burnetii. They are found in the bone marrow and liver and are often reported as clinical cases of primary Q fever. Granulomas are absent in patients with chronic/persistent Q fever [ ]. Although non-specific, the morphology of Q fever-related granulomas has attracted considerable attention. With a central open space and fibrin ring, they are referred to as doughnut granulomas [ ]. This granuloma morphology is not found in murine models of C. burnetii infection and careful comparative studies are needed. Q fever granulomas contain macrophages with an epithelioid morphology, multinucleated giant cells, neutrophils, and lymphocytes. Bacteria can be detected in granulomas, but they are considered paucibacillary in Q fever [ ]. Access to granulomas in human and animal models has been a serious barrier to mechanistic research. We developed an original model based on the incubation of human mononuclear cells with C. burnetii-coated sepharose beads. Monocytes initiate the formation of granuloma-like structures that are completed by lymphocytes, and some mature in multinucleated giant cells. These C. burnetii-stimulated granulomas provide a model for investigating granuloma formation and for application to patient studies [ ]. We conducted a comparative microarray analysis of gene expression in cells from C. burnetii or bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) granulomas. It showed that 50% of genes were specifically modulated in response to C. burnetii, notably genes associated with the interferon (IFN) response which were enriched [ ]. This model enabled us to determine how monocytes from patients with persistent Q fever were unable to form granulomas [ ]. This impairment is likely to be due to IL-10, which is overproduced by monocytes during persistent infection. We showed that overexpression of IL-10 in the myeloid compartment prevents granuloma formation in C. burnetii-infected mice [ ], suggesting that granulomas are protective against C. burnetii infection.

## Placenta
C. burnetii infections in pregnant animals or women leads to both maternal and foetal complications [ , ]. Interestingly, C. burnetii has been isolated from aborted animals and human placentas [ , ]. Our team has investigated the regulation of the placental immune system during C. burnetii infection. Previous studies have reported that C. burnetii is found in trophoblast cells, which represent the majority of placental cells [ , , ]. Using villous trophoblast BeWo and extra-villous trophoblast JEG-3 cell lines, we evaluated the cellular and molecular mechanisms involved in in vitro C. burnetii infection. C. burnetii Nine Mile (NM, the referent strain) I infects both cell lines, but replicates only within the phagolysosomes of BeWo cells [ ]. Six days post-infection, we observed an increase in the presence of C. burnetii (80%) in the lysosomal compartments. This infection is associated with a specific transcriptomic program consisting of more than 80% of upregulated genes involved in cell motility, apoptosis, cell-cell signalling, and both immune and inflammatory responses. Interestingly, the genes involved in the development of pregnancy were modulated following C. burnetii infection (early growth response protein-1 (EGR1) and N-Myc downstream regulated 1 (NDGR1) genes).
In macrophages isolated from the full-term placentas of healthy donors and in vitro samples infected with C. burnetii, we reported bacterial elimination and a specific M1 polarization profile that was distinct from M2 profile observed in monocyte-derived macrophage (MDM) [ , ]. This suggests that placental macrophages are not permissive for C. burnetii. Interestingly, we reported large secretion of IFNγ by placental macrophages following C. burnetii infection. Although the secretion of IFNγ by macrophages remains controversial [ ], a significant correlation between cytokine production and bacterial elimination has been reported [ , ]. In contrast, we found that C. burnetii NMI infection of dendritic cells (DC) from full-term healthy placental tissue did not lead to their maturation [ ], which may be deleterious for pregnancy development.
Finally, we report a new defence mechanism for C. burnetii NMI against mast cells from the placenta [ ]. We showed that the interaction between C. burnetii NMI and naive mast cells isolated from full-term healthy placental tissue [ ] led to the release of extracellular actin filaments containing antimicrobial agents that were capable of trapping and killing entrapped bacteria.
These data highlight the role of C. burnetii in placental tissues during pregnancy. However, these data are still limited and it would be interesting to evaluate the C. burnetii infection in more complex models, such as organoids or placental explants, as well as to investigate the placentas of naturally infected donors (animals or humans).

## Virulence of C. burnetii: molecular and cellular features
Attack rate of Q fever can be very high, and outbreaks with severe primary infection can arise from the circulation of few virulent or hyper virulent strains with increased zoonotic and epidemic potentials. In the Netherlands, from 2007 to 2011, an outbreak, the largest ever described, led to over 4,000 reported human cases, with a high hospitalization rate, while seroprevalence studies suggest that more than 40,000 people were actually infected [ ]. The outbreak was linked to proximity to infected aborting small ruminants [ ]. Another outbreak occurred in 2014, in a remote area of the Amazon Rain Forest in French Guiana. The outbreak was associated with higher prevalence of pneumonia and fever than in metropolitan France [ ] and was linked to capybaras, as C. burnetii DNA was found in their fecal samples [ ]. The disease spread through inhalation of contaminated particles, and being near brush cutters in use was associated with the development of the disease [ ]. Virulence of the Guiana and a Netherlands-derived strains have been examined and compared to the Nile Mile (NMI) strain in a mouse model of infection. When infecting immunocompetent Balb/C mice, no difference was observed between the strains. However, infection of SCID mice, which lack both T and B cells, revealed that the Guiana strain was the more virulent as demonstrated by 100% mortality and increased bacterial burden in organs, the Netherlands-derived strain was also virulent with 75% mortality while the NMI strain did not induce mortality [ ]. Advances in comparative genomics have modified our understanding of C. burnetii pathogenesis and the emergence of these virulent or hypervirulent strains, which arise from mutation, acquisition, or deletion of genomic features [ ].

## Genome and genomic features
The first complete genome sequence of C. burnetii NM phase I RSA493 was released in 2003 [ ]. It revealed a 1,995,275-bp chromosomal genome and 37,393-bp QpH1 plasmid, predicted to encode 2134 coding sequences, of which more than 700 are hypothetical [ ]. Genome analysis also revealed striking particularities because it possesses 29 dispersed insertion sequences, which favour high genomic plasticity, and 83 pseudogenes, suggesting that genome reduction is underway [ ]. Finally, in accordance with the strict intracellular nature of C. burnetii, the genome encodes numerous transporters and ion channels that allow the bacterium to import nutrients from its host cell while surviving the harsh conditions in its replicating vacuole [ ].
Recently, further knowledge has been provided by the pangenomic analysis of 75 C. burnetii strains isolated from ticks, various animals including mice, dogs, cats, and ewes, and humans from diverse regions of the world and with different disease presentations. The core- and pan-genomes consist of 1211 and 4501 genes, respectively, in favour of an open pangenome with several unique genes per strain ranging from 7 to 212. In addition, pathogenicity islands and insertion sequences have been found in all genomes, highlighting a high degree of genomic plasticity characterized by the chromosomal rearrangement of syntenic blocks and DNA insertions/deletions resulting from recombination between insertion sequences [ , ].
Genotyping and the genetic diversity of C. burnetii were initially addressed by restriction fragment length polymorphisms which delineate several genomic groups with distinct distribution and association with humans or animals [ , ]. This diversity was extended by complete genome hybridization, which revealed multiple polymorphisms associated with diverse cellular functions, and contributing to bacterial virulence and adaptability [ ]. Finally, more reliable methods including multiple locus variable number tandem repeat analysis [ ] or the highly discriminant multispacer sequence typing [ ] further improved the understanding of the epidemiology of C. burnetii infection.
Recently, essential C. burnetii genes were identified by screening a transposon library for growth and in vitro survival in axenic acidified citric cysteine medium (ACCM)-2, which mimics the conditions of the acidic replicative vacuole of C. burnetii. [ ] Analysis of 10,129 unique insertion mutants revealed 511 genes required for C. burnetii NMII replication in ACCM-2. Interestingly, 78% of these essential genes were conserved across all the sequenced C. burnetii strains, indicating their critical functions. A closer investigation of C. burnetii metabolic pathways showed that most of the genes from the peptidoglycan biosynthesis pathway, as well as those from the mevalonate and biotin pathways, were essential for C. burnetii growth [ ]. Transposon mutagenesis has also been applied for large-scale identification of C. burnetii virulence determinants [ , ]. Over 1000 generated mutants were isolated on ACCM-2 agar plates and further amplified for seven days in liquid ACCM-2 before high-content multi-phenotypic screening of Vero cells over seven days of infection, leading to the identification of several bacterial factors that regulate the key steps of C. burnetii infection, including internalization by host cells, vacuole biogenesis and intracellular replication, and interference with host cell apoptosis [ ]. A similar strategy applied to identify the genes required for the biogenesis of a mature Coxiella-containing vacuole (CCV) revealed that mutants defective in the Dot/Icm secretion system or the PmrA/B regulatory system were incapable of intracellular replication, whereas mutants for genes encoding several effector proteins translocated by the Dot/Icm system have intracellular growth defects [ ].

## Plasmids
All C. burnetii isolates carry one of four conserved plasmids, QpH1, QpRS, QpDV, or QpDG ( ), or a QpRS-like chromosomally integrated sequence. The plasmid size ranges from 37 to 55 kb, whereas that of the chromosomally inserted sequence is 18 kDa [ ]. These plasmids are considered to be critical for the infection process and have been linked to various disease manifestations. A comparison of QpH1, QpRS, QpDG, and the integrated plasmid sequences revealed that they are almost identical at the nucleotide level within the common DNA and that they share 22 open reading frames (ORFs), some of which encode Dot/Icm effectors [ ]. 
The QpH1 plasmid has been identified as a virulence factor essential for colonization of murine macrophages. Indeed, it has been shown that the C. burnetii NMII strain cured for the QpH1 plasmid grew normally in axenic media, but exhibited significant growth defects in Vero (a monkey kidney epithelial cell line) and THP-1 (a human monocytic cell line) cells and reduced virulence in mouse models [ ]. The QpH1 plasmid encodes several ORFs that are crucial for its maintenance and compatibility, including CBUA0037–0039 ORFs, which are essential for plasmid stability [ ]. In addition, QpH1 encodes a functional toxin-antitoxin system homologous to the HigB2/HigA2 system in Vibrio cholerae. [ ] It has further been shown that this toxin-antitoxin system is important for the intracellular growth and survival of C. burnetii, since overexpression of toxP resulted in a severe intracellular growth defect through the inhibition of protein translation, which was rescued by the co-expression of antitoxP, which binds to the toxP promoter as well as ToxP itself [ ]. Finally, the QpH1 plasmid encodes several T4BSS substrates (Cpe or Coxiella plasmid effectors) that are secreted into the host cytosol and target various subcellular sites, subverting host cell processes and facilitating bacterial replication and survival [ ]. Indeed, it has been shown that CpeB promotes the accumulation of the microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3)-II, a marker of autophagy, and contributes to bacterial virulence in mouse models [ ], while CpeC and CpeD were observed in ubiquitin-rich compartments and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), respectively, when expressed ectopically in the HeLa cell line [ ].
The QpRS plasmid is commonly found in isolates associated with chronic/persistent infections, such as endocarditis, but is rarely associated with acute Q fever [ , , ]. It is larger than QpH1 and contains unique DNA sequences not found in QpH1, indicating distinct evolutionary paths. Indeed, it has a unique 3.6kb EcoRI fragment (epsilon’) that encodes a 55kDa surface protein, suggesting the presence of specific virulence genes [ , ]. It also encodes hypothetical proteins that are translocated into host cells via Dot/Icm T4BSS, although their functions remain to be established [ ].
The QpDV plasmid has been associated with both acute and chronic/persistent Q fever isolates. It shares common origins with other C. burnetii plasmids but also exhibits unique characteristics that may reflect divergent evolution [ ]. QpDV encodes 34 proteins, of which 27 are shared with QpH1. However, it harbours sequences coding for four proteins not found in QpH1 and is predicted to encode the following functions: plasmid partition protein A, E surface protein, DNA helicase I putative protein traI, and Rh protein family [ ].
The QpDG plasmid is closely related to QpH1, and studies have indicated that they are likely to be identical [ , ]. QpDG was discovered in severely attenuated C. burnetii isolates collected from rodents in Dugway, UT (130), and has never been found in human isolates. Interestingly, QpDG encodes four specific Dot/Icm substrates (CpeI, CpeJ, CpeK, and CpeL) that have not been found in other C. burnetii isolates harbouring other plasmids [ ]. The fact that QpDG-harbouring strains were severely attenuated compared to other isolates suggests that these specific effectors influence the pathogenicity of C. burnetii.
The presence and type of plasmids carried by C. burnetii isolates are correlated with different disease manifestations. For instance, QpH1 is associated with acute Q fever, and QpRS is linked to chronic/persistent Q fever, such as endocarditis, and QpDV to abortion [ , ]. This was further confirmed recently by a pangenomic study that showed that acute infection isolates harboured either the QpH1 or QpDV plasmids and never the QpRS plasmid, suggesting that QpRS-harbouring C. burnetii strains that are associated with chronic/persistent infections cause asymptomatic primary infections [ ].

## Lipopolysaccharide
The full-length LPS of C. burnetii is recognized as the main virulence factor in animal models of infection. The bacterium exhibits significant variation in its LPS structure, which plays a crucial role in its pathogenicity and immune evasion mechanisms.
C. burnetii LPS undergoes phase variation, analogous to the smooth-to-rough LPS variation observed in gram-negative enteric bacteria. This phase variation occurs after several in vitro passages of virulent phase I C. burnetii in embryonated eggs or cell lines, resulting in the deletion of a large portion of 21 genes predicted to function in O-antigen biosynthesis to yield avirulent phase II LPS [ , ]. As a result, virulent phase I LPS is structurally complex and antigenically distinct from avirulent phase II LPS. This structural complexity of phase I LPS includes a unique disaccharide, galactosaminuronyl-alpha (1–6)-glucosamine, and other sugars, such as virenose and dihydrohydroxystreptose, which are absent in phase II LPS [ ]. Phase II LPS has a simpler composition and contains the core polysaccharide that is conserved between phase I and phase II LPSs. It contains, in addition to a 2-keto-3-deoxyoctulosonic acid-related, thiobarbituric acid-positive substance, only D-mannose and D-glycero-D-mannoheptose in high amounts [ ]. Intermediate forms of LPS with semi-rough characteristics have also been identified [ , ], indicating that different strains of C. burnetii may have evolved distinct LPS structures to evade host immune responses. Recently, the genetic basis of LPS phase conversion has been characterized, providing critical insights into the pathogenesis of C. burnetii. Indeed, phase I strains from different GGs exhibited similar phase transition kinetics after prolonged culture and accumulated mutations in LPS biosynthesis genes, six of which were further shown to be necessary for the production of phase I LPS [ ].
Finally, lipid A moieties from both phase I and phase II LPSs are likely to have identical structures, characterized by a classical backbone of diphosphorylated D-GlcN disaccharide, which is tetraacylated with longer acyl chains than most lipid A from enterobacterial strains [ ].
C. burnetii LPS plays a significant role in modulating the host immune response, and its atypical composition and structure are associated with the interference of several antibacterial processes that facilitate intracellular survival. Although we showed that phase I LPS was recognized by TLR4 leading to the production of inflammatory cytokines [ ], DS. Zamboni et al. reported that Lipid A from C. burnetii antagonized the activation of TLR4 by E. coli [ ].
On the other hand, C. burnetii LPS has been shown to be involved in the modulation of phagocytosis and bacterial survival in monocytes. In contrast to phase II bacteria, virulent phase I bacteria induce tyrosine phosphorylation of the Src-related kinases Hck and Lyn, which then promote cell membrane protrusions and down-modulate C. burnetii uptake [ ]. Reorganization of the cytoskeleton induced by phase I but not phase II bacteria also disrupts the colocalization of TLR2 and TLR4, leading to the absence of p38α Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) activation and interference with proper trafficking of bacteria/LPS to lysosomes [ , ]. Indeed, the absence of p38α MAPK prevents phosphorylation of Vps (vacuolar protein sorting)-41; the HOPS (homotypic fusion and protein sorting) complex is therefore not targeted to endosome-vacuole fusion sites, and guanosine triphosphate (GTP)-bound Rab7, which is required for phagosome–lysosome fusion is not recruited to the late phagosome [ ].
Furthermore, given that LPS represents the major interface between the bacteria and the host cell, it is highly possible that C. burnetii LPS participates in immune evasion and is involved in the atypical M2 activation program of human macrophages, characterized by the upregulation of the anti-inflammatory genes encoding IL-10, tumour growth factor (TGF)-β1, IL-1 receptor antagonist (ra), CCL18, mannose receptor, and arginase-1, and the repression of most pro-inflammatory genes, except for those encoding IL-6 and CXCL8 [ ]. This atypical M2 programme is likely to be associated with the persistence and replication of C. burnetii in the macrophages. Finally, LPS from C. burnetii participates in evasion of the host immune response by preventing the maturation of DC [ , ]. It has been shown that infection by phase I bacteria does not result in DC maturation, whereas avirulent phase II bacteria induce TLR4-independent DC maturation. Interestingly, the removal of LPS with trichloroacetic acid from phase I bacteria restores its ability to stimulate DC [ ].
Hence, the structural and antigenic diversity of C. burnetii LPS plays a critical role in its pathogenicity and its ability to evade the host immune system, although a major limitation with experiments using purified LPS is that it partly reflects the interaction of whole Coxiella with the host. Understanding these variations and their biological implications is essential for the development of alternative strategies against C. burnetii infection.

## Secretion systems
Bacteria continuously interact with their environment, mediated by their ability to transport proteins across membranes. Genome analysis has revealed that C. burnetii encodes a complete Sec-secretion pathway for the translocation of proteins into the periplasm. It also expresses components of T1SS in the presence of a tolC homolog. Genome analysis also suggests the possibility of type IV pilus (T4P)-mediated secretion, which is related to T2SS, as 13 T4P genes organized in predicted operons have been identified [ , 11] of which have homologs in Francisella novicida, which utilizes T4P-mediated secretion [ ]. However, pili-like structures are not observed at the surface of C. burnetii which expresses a complete and specialized Dot/Icm T4BSS, homologous to the Legionella pneumophila T4BSS [ ].
The contribution of T1P- and T4P-mediated secretion remains unclear in the context of pathogenesis. Mass spectrometry analysis of the secreted proteins revealed the presence of 105 proteins in axenic ACCM medium, 27 of which were confirmed [ ]. Although the majority were annotated as hypothetical, a few of them may play a role in the intravacuolar survival of C. burnetii. For example, C. burnetii secretes the superoxide dismutase SodC, which may detoxify the superoxide radicals generated by nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase in the phagosome [ ]. In addition, because C. burnetii is auxotrophic for 11 amino acids, including arginine [ ], it must be imported from the environment. Thus, CBU0482, annotated as secreted arginine-binding protein (ArtI) [ ], may bind and provide host arginine to C. burnetii for protein synthesis. Additionally, as arginine is the substrate of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), it is also possible that ArtI indirectly contributes to lowering oxidative stress by reducing the arginine levels available for iNOS. Finally, a protein with homology to zinc (Zn)2+ metalloendopeptidases, CBU1902, has also been shown to be secreted and is supposed to cleave and inactivate several hydrolases that are found in the CCV although no experimental evidence have supported this prediction [ ].
C. burnetii encodes functional components of a T4BSS, such as the L. pneumophila Dot/Icm system, which is crucial for manipulating host cellular processes and creating specialized vacuoles that support replication [ ]. It is therefore expected that C. burnetii and L. pneumophila T4BSS share similar structures and functions, although C. burnetii T4BSS is exclusively located at one pole of the bacterial membrane and lacks DotJ, DotV, and IcmR which are part of the inner membrane complex in L. pneumophila. [ ] The core complex of C. burnetii T4BSS consists of DotC, DotD, DotH, DotF, and DotG: DotC, DotD, and DotH assemble and form the outer membrane complex, DotG forms the central channel of the core complex and spans the inner and outer membrane, and DotF is integral to the inner membrane and participate in substrate recognition. DotL is the coupling protein that bridges the effectors to the complex, while DotM, DotN, IcmS, LvgA, and IcmW are primarily involved in substrate recognition and form the coupling protein complex. DotB is the cytoplasmic ATPase which is essential for the normal function of the T4BSS [ ]. It has been shown that the expression of Dot/Icm genes and a subset of effector coding genes are regulated by the two component system PmrA/B which responds to substrates generated by lysosomal proteases expressed in the CCV [ ]. In addition, a transposon mutagenesis screen recently identified EirA as a critical regulator of Dot/Icm effector translocation although the mechanism remains unknown [ ]. More than 150 candidate effectors have been identified to date. Although the precise functions of the majority of these effectors remain to be characterized, others have been investigated and can be roughly grouped into several categories [ ]. Some effectors manipulate membrane trafficking mediated by clathrin, Rab GTPases, or host phosphoinositides to participate in biogenesis and maintenance of the CCV [ ]. Other effectors interfere with host cellular pathways, including autophagy, such as CvpF, which stimulates the recruitment of LC3B to the phagosomal membrane for optimal vacuole development [ , ], signal transduction by actively triggering protein kinase A (PKA) activation and downstream signalling pathways [ ], and CaeB, which targets the conserved ER stress sensor IRE1 during ER stress [ ]. However, other effectors inhibit apoptosis, which is an important element of the host response for the clearance of micro-organisms. For example, the CaeA effector inhibits both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptosis, whereas AnkG modulates host cell transcription to prevent cell death [ , ]. Finally, some effectors target the innate immune response, such as the inhibition of caspase-11-mediated non-canonical activation of the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD)-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome [ ], CinF, which inhibits NF-κB signalling by dephosphorylating and stabilizing IκBα [ ], or EmcB, which inhibits RIG-I signalling [ ]. Taken together, these effectors play critical roles in manipulating host cellular processes, facilitating the formation and maintenance of specialized vacuoles, and ensuring successful intracellular replication.

## Interaction of C. burnetii with host cells
The potential of C. burnetii to infect different hosts including humans is also related to its ability to infect target cells and to survive within them. C. burnetii infects monocytes and macrophages in natural infection but can enter numerous cell types in vitro through phagocytosis [ ]. We and others have described the original mechanism of virulence-related control of infection ( ). The virulent strain of C. burnetii is poorly internalized via αvβ3 integrin. This low internalization efficiency is due to the inability to link αvβ3 integrin with CR3, a powerful phagocytic integrin. In contrast, natural avirulent variants of C. burnetii are efficiently internalized and αvβ3 integrin is colocalized with CR3 [ , ]. Expressed by monocytes and macrophages, integrin αVβ3 interacts with an unknown bacterial ligand expressed by C. burnetii through an arginine–glycine–aspartic acid (RGD) integrin binding domain [ ]. In contrast, the subunit β3 is little expressed in cells of the bronchial epithelium, suggesting that other receptors are involved in the interaction with non-professional phagocytic cells. Nevertheless, lung, endothelial, and epithelial cells can be infected with C. burnetii albeit to a lesser extent than monocytes and macrophages which constitute the main sites of infection [ ]. If C. burnetii virulence affects bacterial uptake by myeloid cells, the uptake of virulent NMI and avirulent NMII is similar when non-phagocytic cells are used [ ]. The phagocytosis of C. burnetii may also involve receptors for the Fc fragments of Igs. This internalization pathway is usually considered to be a microbicidal pathway. We have shown that the opsonization of micro-organisms with patient IgG increases C. burnetii uptake in large vacuoles, but does not impair their replication [ ]. The activation state of C. burnetii cells affects the fate of micro-organisms within the cells. This is even more critical with their usual targets, monocytes and macrophages, which are extremely sensitive to the presence of bacterial invaders. In the lungs, the presence of surfactants is an important regulator of host–pathogen interactions. Soltysiak et al. reported that surfactant protein D decreased C. burnetii uptake in alveolar cell lines [ ]. In non-phagocytic cells, alternative receptors have been proposed. Hence, CD44 with the participation of ezrin is involved in the phagocytosis of C. burnetii by HeLa cells [ ]. It is likely that other candidates will be uncovered in the future with the development of bacterial mutants.
 
The internalization of C. burnetii induces the formation of Coxiella-containing vacuoles (CCV) in which microorganisms survive and replicate. There is an extensive literature regarding the nature and the fate of these vacuoles. The initial studies using modest tools of cell biology concluded that C. burnetii replicates in acidic phagolysosomes. Indeed, we showed that C. burnetii Nine Mile strain phase I was found in acidic vacuoles that did not maturate in complete phagolysosomes [ ]. The availability of mutants from axenic cultures led to a reappraisal of the maturation of CCV [ , ] The pH of CCV is a critical step of C. burnetii survival in host cells. C. burnetii is found in an early phagosome based on the expression of EEA1 and RAB5 [ ]. When the phagosome maturates and becomes a late phagosome, it expresses RAB7 and lysosomal membrane proteins (LAMP)1/2 and loses RAB5 [ ]. It acquires a vacuolar ATPase that decreases the pH via a proton pump. The pH is then reduced to 4.5 when the phagosome fuses with the lysosome [ , ]. More recently, it has been shown that the luminal pH of CCV is closer to 5.2 and not 4.7 as initially reported and remains stable during cell infection [ ]. In addition, C. burnetii regulates the pH of its intracellular niche via the modulation of cholesterol composition of bacterial vacuole [ ]. It appeared that vacuole acidity was necessary for intracellular growth of C. burnetii as neutralization of vacuolar pH with lysomotropic amines impaired the growth of the NMI C. burnetii strain [ ]. These findings provide a mechanistic basis for the use of hydroxychloroquine in the treatment of persistent C. burnetii infections [ ].
The maturation of CCV, like phagosomes, is essential for its fusion with lysosomes [ ]. The maturation of the CCV leads to an increase in size, which is not characteristic of the classical phagosome during maturation [ , ]. Interestingly, the expansion of the CCV is affected when the simultaneous functions of RAB5 and RAB7 are inhibited [ ]. It should be noted that inhibition of RAB5 alone impacts the entry of the bacterium into the host cell [ ]. These data highlight a significant share of C. burnetii-specific bacterial proteins in the maturation and size of CCV in the host cell [ , ]. Many intracellular pathogens block the maturation of phagosomes at an early stage to avoid fusion with lysosomes and formation of phagolysosomes [ .]. C. burnetii does not block it but delays it. Recently, it has been shown that C. burnetii takes advantage of host sterol transport protein, ORP1, for the development of CCV and the control of bactericidal activity of the vacuole [ ]. The autophagy pathway and CCV biogenesis and maturation are intertwined as illustrated by autophagic protein LC3 decorating C. burnetii phase II replicative vacuole [ , ]. In a dynamic approach, LC3 is found around the CCV rapidly after internalization. The C. burnetii effector Cig2 promotes the fusion of autophagosome with CCV, which affects the maturation of vacuole [ ]. Although the central role of autophagy in cell infection by C. burnetii is not known, it is suggested that the nutrients present in the autophagy compartments may serve as fuel for replication and/or the small cell variant (SCV)/large cell variant (LCV) switch [ , ].
The replication of C. burnetii in host cell also depends on bacterial ability to control cell death. Indeed, C. burnetii acts by (1) inhibiting apoptotic pathways and (2) activating cellular survival factors. Inhibition of apoptotic pathways relies on the activation of “pro-survival” proteins from the AKT family, including ERK1 (MAPK) and ERK2 (MAPK1). Another anti-apoptotic action of the NMII strain is the interaction between BECN1 (autophagy initiator protein) and BCL2 (anti-apoptotic protein), which prevents cytochrome c release from mitochondrial membrane [ , ]. Conversely, C. burnetii is capable of inducing apoptosis via manipulating mitochondrial functionality [ ]. In this context, as previously described for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, [ ]. C. burnetii, mainly using phase II C. burnetii model, can inhibit apoptosis to promote its replication but also induce it to promote its dissemination.
The specificity of the intracellular cellular traffic of pathogens in mononuclear cells is their high sensitivity to the type of activation. Using human monocytes and macrophages, we did not find large multibacillary vacuoles but small vacuoles containing small numbers of C. burnetii [ , ]. In addition, we showed a partial defective acquisition of Rab7, a small GTPase involved in phagosome maturation within resting human monocytes and macrophages, which may interfere with downstream maturation events, including fusion with lysosomes and acquisition of lysosomal proteases such as Cathepsin D. This link with virulence is underlined by the acquisition of cathepsin D by the NMII C. burnetii-containing phagosome and its fusion with lysosomes [ ]. Regarding cell activation, IFNγ, the main activator of mononuclear cells, promotes C. burnetii killing by stimulating the colocalization of cathepsin D with CCV [ ]. IFNγ also control bacteria replication via the upregulation of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) known to deplete tryptophan. Silencing of IDO1 restores C. burnetii replication [ ]. More recently, based on small interfering RNA screen, the SNARE protein syntaxin 11 was associated with IFNγ-mediated impairment of C. burnetii replication. Cells deficient in syntaxin 11 were more permissive for C. burnetii replication and increasing syntaxin 11 production restricted bacterial replication [ ]. In contrast, IL-10, the immunoregulatory cytokine, interferes with the microbicidal response of mononuclear cells. The neutralization of endogenous IL-10 overproduced by patients with Q fever endocarditis enhances NMI C. burnetii killing by monocytes and restores the fusion of CCV with lysosomes [ , ].
The virulence of C. burnetii depends on intrinsic properties of the bacterium and its ability to establish a safe environment in target cells by controlling phagosome biogenesis and maturation and cell death. The intracellular life of C. burnetii is also affected by the activation of host cells, which reflects the type of immune response mounted against the microorganism.

## C. burnetii persistence
Although treatment with doxycycline and hydroxychloroquine significantly improves Q fever prognosis, relapses and/or chronic manifestations continue to be reported [ ], suggesting bacterial persistence in specific tissues.
We showed that C. burnetii persists in murine white and brown adipose tissue independently of the mouse strain or inoculation route, for at least four months post clinical recovery, providing strong evidence for adipose tissue as a target/reservoir for C. burnetii. We have also shown that the transfer of white adipose tissue from a convalescent mouse to naive immunodeficient mice resulted in the infection of these recipient animals. Adipose tissue is primarily composed of adipocytes but also contains minority cell types such as fibroblasts, macrophages, endothelial cells, and pericytes, collectively referred to as the “vascular stroma.” Immunohistochemical analysis of adipose tissue sections suggests two potential localizations of C. burnetii: adipocytes or immune cells including macrophages from vascular stroma. In adipocytes, C. burnetii was found in a compartment rich in LAMP1, a marker of late endosomes, but devoid of cathepsin D, a lysosomal marker [ ], suggesting that C. burnetii affects intracellular trafficking in both macrophages and adipocytes.
The inflammatory profile of adipocytes may favour the implantation and persistence of C. burnetii in adipose tissue. C. burnetii induced a transcriptional programme in adipocytes enriched with inflammatory genes [ ]. These findings add C. burnetii to the list of pathogens inhabiting adipose tissue [ , ]. They provide the basis for understanding the relapses of Q fever in patients with corticosteroids, which induce lipodystrophy, a condition favourable to the release of pathogens into systemic circulation and, consequently, the induction of relapses.

## Immune response to C. burnetii
C. burnetii stimulates the innate and adaptive arms of the immune response, which are associated with bacterial clearance. The specificity of C. burnetii infection is its ability to survive and replicate in the myeloid cells. This interaction suggests that C. burnetii can subvert the microbicidal mechanisms of monocytes and macrophages, protecting them from the aggressiveness of the immune system.

## Innate immunity
The innate immune system consists of soluble and cell effectors recruited by infection. It is necessary to distinguish the cell response to C. burnetii in vitro and cell responses observed ex vivo which also integrates the impact of systemic and tissue environments ( ). Among innate immune cells, C. burnetii interacts with myeloid and non-myeloid cells to activate them, but few are permissive to micro-organisms. Phase I and II strains of C. burnetii can infect murine neutrophils with a low efficiency. Apoptosis of infected neutrophils may favor the transfer of C. burnetii to macrophages [ ]. These combined results suggest that neutrophils cannot eliminate C. burnetii, but their infection can be considered as an evasion strategy to infect macrophages, although depletion of neutrophils with specific antibodies before intranasal infection with C. burnetii leads to more severe disease than that in untreated mice [ ]. Other innate immune cells participate in mounting the innate immune response in C. burnetii. Hence, in mice vaccinated with formalin-inactivated C. burnetii NMI, there was an increase in granulocytes that exhibited an eosinophil phenotype. In the absence of eosinophils, the disease worsened early after the challenge, but not after four weeks, suggesting that eosinophils may play an early protective role [ ]. 
Monocytes and macrophages are the main targets of C. burnetii, and they behave as a niche and effector of antibacterial innate immunity. Therefore, the same authors considered primary macrophages as an accurate tool for the identification of C. burnetii virulence factors [ ]. The interaction of C. burnetii with monocytes and macrophages leads to their activation, manifested as strong remodelling of the actin cytoskeleton, activation of the tyrosine kinase pathway, and cytokine production [ ]. It has been shown that TNFα is mainly expressed in response to C. burnetii (in cell cultures and animal models) among inflammatory mediators [ ]. It exerts an inhibitory effect on bacterial replication in human macrophages. IL-10, which is also induced by C. burnetii, reverses this inhibitory effect of TNFα [ ] C. burnetii-stimulated macrophage responses depend partly on TLR4 and TLR2. TLR4 is involved in cytoskeleton reorganization and cytokine production but does not have a mandatory role in C. burnetii replication [ ]. Zamboni showed the involvement of TLR2 in TNFα and IFNγ production by murine macrophages [ ]. We previously showed that TLR2 is involved in granuloma formation [ ]. In resting monocytes, in which C. burnetii survives without replication, bacteria induced M1-type polarization, likely under the control of IFNγ [ ] In contrast, in resting macrophages, in which C. burnetii replicates, bacteria elicit an atypical M2 type programme associating M2-related genes (CCL18, TGFB, IL1RA, ARG1) and M1-related genes (IL6) [ , ]. There are some limitations to these findings. First, the activation status of monocytes depends on the time course of infection. We showed that polarization occurs early in monocytes in response to IL-4 and IFNγ limiting the use of this dichotomy to investigate patients. Patients with Q fever do not exhibit polarization; monocytes from patients with acute Q fever overexpress genes related to early response (polarized), whereas monocytes from patients with chronic/persistent Q fever express genes related to late response (no longer polarized) [ ]. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) stimulated by C. burnetii release cytokines, which are decreased when TLR2, p38, JNK, and ERK are inhibited [ ]. In addition, cytokine production depends on gene polymorphisms. Indeed, polymorphisms in TLR1 and NOD2 reduced cytokine production, and polymorphisms in TLR6 decreased cytokine production in response to the Netherlands outbreak strain but not to the reference strain. The role of C. burnetii is illustrated by the observation that C. burnetii originating from cattle induces more inflammatory cytokines than isolates from goats, sheep, or chronic/persistent Q fever patients [ ]. Secondly, monocytes and macrophages are of blood origin and do not directly reflect the reality of tissue macrophages in contact with C. burnetii. Assuming infection by inhalation, alveolar macrophages are the first resident tissue macrophages that can be infected by C. burnetii. C. burnetii replicates in typical large CCVs and non-fused atypical vacuoles in human alveolar macrophages. As shown above, their activation depends on bacterial virulence. Virulent C. burnetii variants promote the activation of pro-survival and pro-inflammatory cytokines, in contrast to avirulent isolates [ ]. In primary murine alveolar macrophages, C. burnetii stimulates M2 polarization and bacterial replication. This relationship was observed in alveolar macrophages from mice devoid of IL-4, in which the replication of C. burnetii was impaired [ ]. Placental macrophages from full-term placentas [ , ] are not permissive for C. burnetii, despite an environment usually considered to be favourable for pregnancy. In fact, IFNγ produced by placental macrophages promotes bacterial elimination [ , ].
Natural Killer (NK) cells and DCs are present at the interface with the adaptive immune system. NK cells are cytotoxic and contribute to the microbicidal response by producing IFNγ. C. burnetii can infect NK cells without impairing the maturation and production of IFNγ or their lytic granules. Organisms isolated from NK cells retain their infectivity, evoking an escape mechanism [ ]. In Q fever, NK cell variations have been reported in a few divergent papers. No quantitative changes in NK cells have been reported in Australian patients with acute Q fever [ ]. In a French cohort of Q fever endocarditis patients, the number of NK cells decreased [ ]. However, the ability of C. burnetii to interfere with their maturation and activation is debatable. Similar studies have been conducted on DCs. Shannon et al. showed that NMI interferes with the maturation of human DCs, in contrast to NMII. Nevertheless, NMI could not prevent the maturation of DCs pulsed with E. coli. [ ] Matthiesen et al. later showed that NMI-mediated maturation of DCs results from an autocrine loop based on TGFβ. IFNγ reverses this response [ ]. By comparing the responses of myeloid DCs to different intracellular pathogens, we showed that the maturation of DCs pulsed with C. burnetii was partially blocked, which did not impair T cell proliferation [ ]. Hence, it is unlikely that susceptibility to C. burnetii and/or bacterial persistence is a consequence of a blockade of DC maturation. δT cells are involved in the primary infection. Vγ9δ2 increases during primary Q fever [ ]. We showed that monocytes infected with C. burnetii activate Vγ9δ2 T cells via the butyrophilin subfamily 3 member A3 (BTN3A) mechanism [ ]. This contributes to the protective response against C. burnetii [ ].
Similarly, cytokines are present at the interface of innate and adaptive immunity. C. burnetii stimulates the production of cytokines, including inflammatory cytokines, in cell lines, primary immune and non-immune cells, and animal models [ , ]. TLR are partly involved in C. burnetii-stimulated production of cytokines, and the contribution of each (TLR2, TLR4) is debated [ , ]. The impairment of TLR signalling affects the C. burnetii-stimulated inflammatory response. In MyD88 deficient mice, there was a decrease in inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and IFN-induced genes [ ]. C. burnetii T4BSS downregulates IL-17 signalling, known to promote the microbicidal response of macrophages and neutrophils [ ]. Cytokines contribute to the microbicidal response of myeloid cells to C. burnetii; however, their production during the evolution of the disease may participate in the pathophysiology of Q fever. An exacerbated inflammatory response was found in Q fever, specifically in Q fever endocarditis. High circulating levels of TNF, IL-6, type II TNF receptor, and IL-1ra are observed in Q fever [ ]. Patients with Q fever hepatitis exhibit higher circulating levels than those with pneumonia or fever alone [ ]. Hence, TNF and IL-1β production is increased in Q fever endocarditis, but not in primary infection [ , ]. The measurement of cytokine production by mononuclear cells from patients previously in contact with C. burnetii was used to evaluate myeloid cell memory. The stimulation of PBMC from Q fever patients with viable C. burnetii leads to reduced cytokine production (IL-6, TNF, IL-1β) than cells stimulated with heat-killed micro-organisms, underlining vaccinal interest [ ]. Individuals previously exposed to C. burnetii via natural or vaccine methods exhibit strong production of inflammatory cytokines compared to unexposed individuals [ ]. Type I IFN occupies an original place in the cytokines produced in response to C. burnetii. The role of this antiviral cytokine in bacterial infections is surprising [ ]. Mice lacking IFNAR were protected from C. burnetii infection. When recombinant IFNα is administered in the periphery, it exacerbates the disease, whereas its delivery in the lung is protective, likely through the down-modulation of the pulmonary inflammatory response, as shown for tuberculosis [ ]. Another report showed that C. burnetii does not induce type I IFN but blocks its production via two Dot/lcm effector proteins, EmcA and EmcB, which deconjugate the ubiquitin chains of RIG-I [ ]. However, it was shown that C. burnetii may interact with plasmacytoid DC and lead to IFNα production, supporting the idea that C. burnetii is able to induce type I IFN production [ ].

## Adaptive immunity
The intracellular nature of C. burnetii suggests that cell-mediated immune responses rather than antibody-mediated responses are protective ( ). 

## T cell compartment
The use of animal models has emphasized the central role of T cells in bacterial clearance. Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) mice that do not possess T and B cells are unable to eliminate NMI and NMII strains [ ]. Nude mice devoid of T cells are highly sensitive to phase I C. burnetii and exhibit histological lesions in response to phase II organisms [ ]. These two murine models suggest a central role for T cells in protection against C. burnetii. In wild-type mice, the depletion of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells increases susceptibility to C. burnetii, whereas the deletion of a single population, CD4+ or CD8+ T cells, does not affect the ability of mice to clear the infection [ ]. The transfer of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells to infected SCID mice allows for their cure. Administration of splenocytes or T cells isolated from phase I-immunized mice decreased bacterial burden and splenomegaly in SCID mice [ ]. Hence, T cells are required for a protective immune response to infection and vaccinal immunity.
Animal models in which major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules were invalidated underline their role in T cell-mediated protective immunity. MHC II-deficient mice exhibit increased susceptibility to C. burnetii compared to wild-type mice [ ]. The administration of inactivated NMI cannot prevent the development of infection in mice devoid of MHC II [ ]. MHC molecules expressed by DCs are essential for antigen-presenting functions. The role of DCs in innate immunity in C. burnetii has been debated (see “4.6 Innate immunity”).
In vitro experiments have highlighted the presence of C. burnetii-specific T cells in patients with Q fever and their polarization toward the Th1 profile. C. burnetii-specific T cells proliferate in response to antigens and differentiate into effector T cells with the Th1 phenotype [ ]. This polarization has also been observed in individuals vaccinated with the Q fever vaccine Q-VAX [ ]. Hence, the polarization of T cells suggests that IFNγ is an essential component of protective immunity. Mice devoid of IFNγ are susceptible to C. burnetii when high infection doses are used [ ]. Th1 differentiation requires the coordination of several genes under the control of the T-box transcription factor t-bet, also known as TBX21. We showed in mice knockout (KO) for t-bet that the infection was persistent, and the bacterial burden and number of granulomas were increased. The phenotype of C. burnetii-infected KO mice is associated with an increase in anti-inflammatory cytokines and decreased expression of microbicidal genes [ ]. An interesting experiment based on DCs pulsed with bacterial antigens showed that C. burnetii-specific T cells were polarized toward the Th1 profile. Only DC pulsed with Com1 or Mip showed a protective response and Th1 differentiation. In contrast, DCs pulsed with GroEL were not protective and induced the differentiation of T cells toward the Th2 profile [ ]. IFNγ stimulates a microbicidal response in different cell types through numerous mechanisms including the overexpression of IFN-stimulated genes. Some enzymes, such as iNOS synthase or NADH oxidase, are known targets of IFNγ. Mice KO for iNOS or NADPH oxidase are unable to control C. burnetii infections [ ]. Indoleamine 2,3-dioxygénase (IDO-1) is induced by IFNγ, and restricts the availability of tryptophan, while tryptophan supplementation enhances C. burnetii replication [ ] (see “3.5. Interaction of C. burnetii with host cells”). IFNγ is a major inducer of microbicidal activity in macrophages. We showed that IFNγ renders macrophages resistant to C. burnetii by allowing the fusion of infected phagosomes with lysosomes [ ]. IFN-stimulated apoptosis of C. burnetii-infected macrophages by upregulating TNF expression. This mechanism is likely to be effective within granulomas since the effect of IFNγ is lost when macrophage aggregates are dissociated [ , ].
It has been postulated that primary Q fever that evolves to complete cure is associated with a Th1 immune response and IFN-stimulated microbicidal activity in macrophages. In contrast, persistent infection results from defective IFNγ-mediated response. The Netherlands team showed that IFNγ production by mononuclear cells stimulated by C. burnetii was higher in cells from patients with chronic/persistent infection than in controls. The response to IFNγ, as measured by IFN-mediated upregulation of transcription factors, was maintained in patients with persistent infection [ ]. The measurement of IFNγ levels in supernatants from mononuclear cells stimulated in vitro by C. burnetii is useful for the detection of C. burnetii infection [ ]. The combination of IFNγ and IL-2 production enables the discrimination of patients with persistent infections from those who have previously contracted Q fever [ ].

## B cell compartment and antibodies
Antibodies are produced during C. burnetii infection and circulating anti-phase I and phase II antibodies are tools used to diagnose Q fever and discriminate between primary and chronic/persistent Q fever. It has been established that antibodies are not critical for the clearance of intracellular bacteria, although some controversy has been reported [ ]. This is the case for C. burnetii natural infection and vaccination. Initially, it was suggested that antibodies play a role in host protection against C. burnetii. The treatment of micro-organisms with specific antibodies increases their phagocytosis and killing by myeloid cells in vitro. [ ] However, the administration of immune serum to mice devoid of T cells before C. burnetii infection did not affect bacterial replication [ ]. B cell-deficient mice did not exhibit enhanced clinical expression or altered bacterial clearance. They increase the severity of histopathological lesions, suggesting that B cells are critical for the prevention of tissue damage [ ]. The passive transfer of serum from mice immunized with killed C. burnetii conferred bacterial protection [ ]. Different mechanisms may account for the protective role of the antibodies. The IgM specific to C. burnetii suppressed bacterial replication in splenocytes via neutralization-like mechanisms [ ]. Opsonization of C. burnetii with specific antibodies resulted in conflicting results. The opsonization of C. burnetii with immune serum increased stimulated bacterial uptake by guinea pig macrophages, but did not affect bacterial replication [ ]. Other studies have shown that bacteria opsonized by antibodies behave as unopsonized bacteria [ ]. We previously reported that C. burnetii opsonized by IgG isolated from patients with Q fever replicated in MDM, in contrast to unopsonized organisms. Enhanced replication was associated with the formation of large parasitophorous vacuoles that were apparently unable to fuse with lysosomes [ ]. In the latter example, antibodies might favour bacterial replication and a subverted immune response. Protection from the C. burnetii vaccine may require B cells and antibodies. Splenomegaly and increased bacterial burden were observed in B cell-deficient mice that received C. burnetii phase I vaccine, suggesting the critical role of B cells in vaccine protection. C. burnetii phase I vaccine stimulates the production of specific IgG and IgM. Among them, specific IgM contributes to protection, evoking a T cell-independent response [ ]. Fc receptors are not essential for protection since wild-type and FcR KO mice were equally protected when vaccinated [ ].

## C. burnetii-mediated immune subversion: IL-10
C. burnetii can interfere with several steps of innate and adaptive immunity in vitro and IL-10 is clearly involved in the pathogenicity of C. burnetii ( ). IL-10 is an immunomodulatory cytokine produced by a large number of immune cells including monocytes and macrophages [ ]. IL-10 is involved in the control, termination, and repair of inflammation. Overproduction of IL-10 has been associated with increased susceptibility to infections and microbial persistence, leading to chronic/persistent evolution of infectious diseases [ ]. There is converging evidence that the severity of C. burnetii infection is related to IL-10 levels. First, the treatment of human monocytes with IL-10 stimulates C. burnetii replication, whereas the organisms survive without replication in resting monocytes [ ]. Second, the use of mice overexpressing IL-10 in the myeloid compartment reproduced the features of chronic/persistent Q fever. Hence, transgenic mice were unable to eliminate C. burnetii, the infection was not associated with the presence of granuloma as in persistent infection, and levels of specific antibodies were high but did not provide protection as in infected patients [ ]. Third, IL-10 was overexpressed in patients with Q fever endocarditis, a clinical manifestation of bacterial persistence. Overproduction has predictive value for the risk of chronic/persistent Q fever. In patients with acute Q fever, IL-10 production was not different from that in controls, whereas it increased in patients with valvulopathy at risk of endocarditis and Q fever endocarditis. This finding was strengthened by the longitudinal follow-up of patients with valvulopathy in whom the persistence of elevated levels of IL-10 was associated with the development of endocarditis [ ]. The mechanisms underlying IL-10 overproduction are poorly understood and may reflect the existence of IL-10 polymorphisms. An association between a low IL-10 production and single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNP) at nucleotide position-592 has been reported. Individuals homozygous for the IL-10-592 allele had a lower risk of experiencing severe symptoms [ ]. The dysregulation of IL-10 in Q fever does not pre-exist in susceptible patients, since the cure of C. burnetii infection abrogates the overproduction of IL-10 [ ]. Similarly, the investigation of blood monocyte transcriptomes from patients with acute Q fever revealed changes in IL-10, IL-1ra, IL-6, and IL-1β at baseline, which was confirmed by the measurement of circulating cytokine levels.
 

## Conclusion
Although C. burnetii was discovered almost 90 years ago, new technologies over the last 15 years and the establishment of axenic cultures have allowed for a better exploration of this mysterious pathogen. Burette et al. reviewed the technological advances that enabled a better knowledge of bacterial virulence. They suggested that advanced bioinformatics will permit the identification of new sets of bacterial and host genes involved in C. burnetii pathogenesis [ ]. Numerous features of C. burnetii infection remain unexplained, and the translation of bacterial and host response findings to Q fever is a real challenge. The clinical presentation of persistent infection with valvular and vascular lesions is surprising for micro-organisms with initial pulmonary tropism and pose the question of bacterial tropism. Recently, the emergence of autoimmunity as a marker of the chronic/persistent evolution of Q fever has opened the way for understanding the role of autoimmunity in Q fever. The roles of age and sex in the prognosis of Q fever are specific to C. burnetii infection. Although the innate and adaptive aspects of the immune response to C. burnetii have been well investigated, the inefficiency of the immune response in persistent infections remains a mystery. The hypothesis that IL-10 is a key effector of immune subversion is largely supported by experimental data, but it is not known whether IL-10 dysfunction is a cause or a consequence. The development of new treatments not exclusively based on antibiotics will be necessary specifically in persistent lesions of C. burnetii infection. As Q fever is a zoonosis, a One Health approach must be deployed to understand the contagion, the impact of the environment of Q fever occurrence, and the development of new vaccines, including the reservoirs. The main concern regarding the pathophysiology of C. burnetii infection is the establishment of links between in vitro findings and observations in patients. Specific efforts are necessary to promote translational research in C. burnetii infections.
