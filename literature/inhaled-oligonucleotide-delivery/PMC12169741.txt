## Abstract
Saponins are a class of phytocompounds known for their amphiphilic properties. Here, we have evaluated incorporation of 40 saponins into a model lipid nanoparticle (LNP) formulation and evaluated their performance in vitro and in vivo. We reasoned that the surfactant activity of saponins could be beneficial in the context of cell and gene therapy due to the disruption of the intracellular membranes. We established formulation methodology to incorporate saponins into LNPs and measured their endosomal disruption and transfection efficiency with DNA barcode and mRNA cargoes. We identified two saponins—quillaic acid and macranthoidin B—that increase the LNP transfection efficiency and endosomal disruption. Saponin formulations demonstrated cargo-dependent activation of the innate immune system, as measured by the cell-based assays of interferon regulatory factor (IRF) and NF-κB pathway activation. Quillaic acid LNPs resulted in higher titers of anti-OVA IgG2a in the vaccination studies compared to a “naive” LNP control, which suggests a more Th1-biased immunopathology of these vaccines. As Th2-biased vaccines can trigger an allergic response, an mRNA vaccine with a balanced Th1/Th2 response is more favorable for translation into the clinic. Overall, quillaic acid may serve as an adjuvant for mRNA vaccines and potentially decrease the risk of vaccine-associated adverse events.
Keywords: lipid nanoparticle, adjuvant, vaccine, saponins, gene therapy, mRNA delivery, structure-activity relationships

## Introduction
The interest in natural products as medicines spans over the entire length of human history, owing to the widespread use of medicinal plants. A wealth of natural products isolated from plant and animal sources have emerged as pharmaceutical products to treat conditions including infectious diseases, cancers, and inflammation. , In addition to acting as small-molecule drugs, natural products may also be incorporated into nanomedicines to improve their drug delivery efficiency and bioavailability. A prominent example of bioinspired drug delivery platforms is lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), whose composition mimics cellular membranes. Typically, LNPs employ both natural (phospholipids and cholesterol) and synthetic (ionizable lipids and (polyethylene glycol [PEG]-conjugated lipids) components. Supplementing LNP formulations with naturally occurring compounds (e.g., phospholipids, sterols, and vitamins) has shown promise in nucleic acid therapy applications. , , , 
LNP-mediated mRNA delivery has proven to be generally safe and effective in vaccine applications. However, a small subpopulation of COVID-19 mRNA vaccine recipients report allergic reaction symptoms ranging from minor swelling and redness to life-threatening anaphylaxis. Notably, the immune response to LNP vaccines occurs regardless of the nucleic acid payload—even “empty” LNPs trigger immunogenic and reactogenic responses. , As a result, these responses may trigger the immune system and impede the mRNA translation into a target protein. The allergic response to LNP-based vaccines is the result of type Th2-biased immunopathology and is thought to be associated with the LNP components (especially PEG). , , On the other hand, Th1-biased response is more characteristic for response to intracellular pathogens (e.g., bacterial or viral infection) and associated with autoimmune reactions. Both Th1 (cell-mediated) and Th2 (humoral) responses are implicated in the immune response, with their ratio depending on the disease stage, the nature of the pathogen or vaccine agent, and patients’ immune state. Balancing Th1/Th2 responses through the rational choice of adjuvants can alleviate the risk and severity of allergic reactions without sacrificing efficacy, with the general suggestion that vaccines against infectious diseases should be designed to skew toward Th1-type response. Additionally, the relationship between immunopathology, immune response, and endosomal escape is not fully understood.
Saponins are a class of phytocompounds known for their amphiphilicity and mild surfactant properties. The capability of saponins to breach lipid membrane interfaces is the reason they found use as transfection reagents and vaccine adjuvants. A prominent representative of this class of compounds is QS-21, a phytocompound found in Quillaja saponaria bark. Due to their amphiphilic properties, saponins strongly interact with cholesterol and phospholipids in the lipid membranes. As a result, they can form pharmacoactive lipoplexes , and destabilize cellular membranes to assist with drug delivery. This propensity to aggregate with cell membrane lipids suggests that saponins could integrate into lipid-based drug delivery systems like LNPs, which can be utilized to modulate their reactogenicity. Early evidence suggests that QS-21 can load into LNPs with good yield and synergize with their inherent adjuvant activity. Therefore, we sought to investigate the effects of structurally diverse saponins in LNP formulations for messenger RNA (mRNA) delivery.
In this work, we have screened a library of 40 saponins individually integrated into a base LNP formulation containing ionizable lipid DLin-MC3-DMA. We identified the upper concentration boundaries that lead to destabilization of the LNPs and established their immunogenicity, extent of endosomal disruption, and in vitro and in vivo transfection efficacy. Several saponin candidates recurrently emerged in the studies for their increased endosomal escape and transfection efficiency in a cargo-dependent manner. One candidate—quillaic acid—was particularly useful for increasing mRNA transfection efficiency and introducing Th1 bias in the vaccination studies in mice. Our results suggest that saponins can be successfully incorporated in the LNP formulations for nucleic acid delivery and may be used to compensate the Th2-type bias in response to the LNP-based vaccines.

## Results
Saponin structural variety is quite broad, but, generally, they can be divided into triterpenoids and steroids (depending on the structure of lipophilic core) or aglycones and glycosides (depending on whether a hydrophilic sugar is conjugated to the lipophilic core). The scope of the 40 saponins used in this work is captured in . Since saponins often possess surface-active properties, they may destabilize the lipid membrane and compromise the stability of the LNPs. For that reason, we screened the incorporation of saponins into LNPs by multiple different approaches. The three-pronged approach to screening was as follows: (1) co-transfection, whereby we added free saponins (in ethanol) to standard LNP formulation solutions; (2) substitution, whereby cholesterol in the standard LNP formulation was fully substituted with equivalent molar amount of a saponin; and (3) addition, whereby 5, 10, 15, or 20 mol% of total lipid was supplemented with a saponin in the lipid/EtOH phase. All LNP formulations were based on the “standard” MC3 LNP formulation consisting of 50 mol% DLin-MC3-DMA, 38.5% cholesterol, 10% DSPC, and 1.5% DMG-PEG2K. This base formulation will be further denoted as MC3 LNPs.
 First, we assessed whether saponins have an immediate effect on cell viability and mRNA in vitro transfection efficiency in two model cell lines—HeLa (human cervical epithelium) and JE6 (human T lymphoblasts). To conduct the co-transfection screening, we first prepared and purified MC3 LNPs encapsulating FLuc mRNA. These LNPs had a normal size and encapsulation efficiency (∼80 nm diameter with PDI 0.07 and mRNA encapsulation efficiency of 99%) as determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and a modified RiboGreen assay. Then, cells were co-transfected with MC3 LNPs and saponin solution in ethanol at 5 mol% of total baseline lipids in MC3 LNP formulation. Since ethanol may also disrupt the cellular membrane, we added the equivalent volume of ethanol to the MC3 positive control wells. We found that most saponins were well tolerated by both HeLa and JE6 cells (viability >70%; ) and yielded a modest transfection improvement (2–3-fold compared to MC3 control). Two saponins stood out in the post-hoc analysis—ruscogenin, which improved transfection almost 10-fold in HeLa cells, and macranthoidin B, which demonstrated improved transfection in both tested cell lines and limited loss in cell viability. Therefore, we confirmed that saponins do not significantly interfere with mRNA transfection at this concentration and may even assist LNPs in intracellular delivery.
Next, we elected to substitute the cholesterol in the LNP formulation with saponins. Cholesterol is a major component of LNP formulation that affects not only the physical stability of LNPs but also LNP recognition by apolipoproteins, leading to LNP internalization and nucleic acid delivery. Modifications to cholesterol structure can improve mRNA transfection, , and so we hypothesized that saponins (especially those containing a steroid core) may also improve transfection. To evaluate the feasibility of this substitution approach, we prepared the LNPs via microfluidic mixing and measured their size, polydispersity, mRNA encapsulation efficiency, and transfection potency in vitro. Results of initial nanoparticle integrity screening are shown in . Two major trends immediately stood out. First, all saponins lead to major loss of mRNA encapsulation efficiency (<80% vs. 90% for the control Chol LNP). Second, nearly all saponins lead to an increase in nanoparticle size and polydispersity, but most saponin LNPs were still within standard quality control margins (<200 nm and PDI <0.4). These two observations indicate that while abundance of saponins can interfere with mRNA encapsulation processes, they still can incorporate into LNPs despite the presumed surfactant activity, likely due to association with phospholipids. In transfection studies ( ), we observed overall better cell viability in both HeLa and JE6 cell lines compared to the co-transfection method, but the substitution method failed to improve transfection efficiency. Therefore, complete cholesterol substitution is not a viable strategy for saponin incorporation into LNPs. This observation is in agreement with the previous observation for saponin QS-21 with strong hemolytic activity that may be quenched with cholesterol. 
The two strategies described above indicate that the simultaneous presence of saponins and cholesterol in the transfection mixture is necessary for successful LNP formation and mRNA transfection. Therefore, we decided to investigate whether saponins can be incorporated at the early LNP formation stage, compared to the co-transfection strategy where the LNP was pre-formed before administration. For that, LNPs were formulated to contain saponins at 5 mol% of total baseline lipids using the MC3 base formulation ( A). We noted that the majority of these LNPs exhibited similar characteristics to the cholesterol LNPs in terms of size, polydispersity, and mRNA encapsulation efficiency ( A and S4B). When these LNPs were transfected into HeLa and JE6 cell lines, cell viability remained at >80%, and there were 11 and 13 saponins, respectively, that improved mRNA transfection up to 50-fold in HeLa and up to 20-fold in JE6 cells ( B and 1C). The magnitude of improvement was rather unexpected, considering that co-transfection approach only improved mRNA transfection efficiency by 2- to 3-fold. There was also a large overlap between these groups—eight saponins (Hed, Gin CK, Gin Rg3, Gin Re, Pdio, Q, Mac B, and Bet) were universally beneficial in both cell lines. Of note, macranthoidin B (Mac B) once again enhanced mRNA delivery compared to standard LNPs (Chol). This observation suggests that integration of saponins into LNPs at the formulation stage is more beneficial for transfection. Based on these data, we identified a total of 16 saponins that could be promising in the future studies; of those, we further narrowed down the scope to seven saponins due to the natural abundance and commercial availability of these saponins.
 Similar trends were observed upon scaling up LNP preparation—saponin LNPs had similar size, polydispersity, and encapsulation efficiency to cholesterol LNPs (diameter of 60–90 nm, polydispersity index [PDI] <0.2, and entrapment efficiency [EE] >85%; A and 2B), were non-toxic to the cells, and had up to 10-fold improvement in transfection ( D). Transfection efficiency was dose-dependent in JE6 cells but not in HeLa, likely due to the mRNA expression saturation at higher doses ( D). Since incorporation of cholesterol derivatives can significantly impact LNP morphology despite only having an additional ethyl group, we sought to investigate whether LNP morphology is affected by the presence of saponins. Surprisingly, all LNPs had a normal, homogeneous spherical appearance in cryoTEM images regardless of the saponin introduced, nearly identical to cholesterol LNPs ( C). Additionally, we tested the fluidity of the lipid membrane of these LNPs using the Laurdan assay across a physiological range (pH 4.5–7.6). This assay allows for probing the hydration of the lipid membrane, which can indicate fluidity or rigidity of the LNP surface. Incorporation of saponins decreased generalized polarization (GP) values, suggesting a much higher lipid membrane hydration regardless of pH (lower GP values indicate higher water content due to the quenching of the probe). This was especially prominent for Hed C and Gin Rg3 LNPs, which had not only the lowest GP value at the most acidic pH but also the largest magnitude of GP change from neutral to acidic pH ( C). The higher water content at the lipid membrane interface suggests superficial localization of saponins that could not be revealed by cryoTEM.
 Intrigued by these results, we conducted a high-throughput study to investigate how saponins can affect intracellular processes in vitro and in vivo. We chose 14 saponins that lead to the transfection improvement in the previous studies and utilized DNA barcode system as cargo. First, we wanted to find the upper concentration boundary of these saponins at which LNPs lose their integrity. According to the results of the modified OliGreen assay, most saponins only destabilized the nanoparticles to a small extent (no more than 5% loss of encapsulation efficiency), although several saponins (Ast, a-Hed, Gin CK, and Soy Bb) lead to a major loss of EE ( A). Second, saponin detergent properties may disrupt endosomal membranes, and so we utilized an endosomal escape reporter cell line Gal8-GFP HEK293 to compare the extent of the endosomal membrane disruption. Upon endosomal rupture, GFP-tagged cytosolic galectin-8 binds to the inner leaflet of the endosome, which can be visualized in the form of punctate structures with fluorescent microscopy. The effects of saponins were variable and independent of amount—notably, the saponins Gin Rg1, Hec, Mac B, and Hed C showed a much higher degree of endosomal disruption ( B). These data indicate that there is a fine balance between maintaining LNP structural integrity and the ability to disrupt endosomal membranes. Third, keeping in mind that saponins may possess adjuvant activity and stimulate the immune system, we performed transfection of dual-reporter J774 murine macrophages to assess the extent of activation of NF-κB and interferon regulatory factor (IRF) pathways. NF-κB stimulation was not observed, except for the LNP containing 20 mol% quillaic acid (Q), which elevated the signal 2-fold compared to the MC3 LNP baseline ( C). In contrast, many of the saponin LNPs lead to stimulation of the IRF pathway compared to cholesterol LNP control, with quillaic acid LNP again leading to the highest extent of IRF pathway stimulation ( D). IRF activation was also dependent on the mol% of saponins introduced. Activation of IRF pathway, but not NF-κB, suggests that the saponin LNP recognition by Toll-like receptors (TLR) occurs at an endosomal and not cell surface level, in agreement with the proposed mechanism of action of LNPs. 
 Lastly, we investigated whether saponins have any effect on the biodistribution in vivo. Out of 57 barcoded saponin LNPs, eight were discarded due to the suboptimal encapsulation efficiency (<75%). We pooled 49 remaining saponin LNPs, added a naked barcode (for negative control), and injected the mixture intravenously and intramuscularly. Intravenous administration of the barcoded saponin LNPs led to some formulations showing increased delivery of barcodes to the liver and spleen (up to 3-fold greater as compared to cholesterol LNPs; E and 3F). DNA barcodes were also found in the lungs at the levels consistent with the Chol control; no significant barcode accumulation was detected in other organs. Once again, quillaic acid (Q) LNPs, especially at 20 mol%, lead to the highest DNA barcode accumulation in the spleen and liver; other notable saponins include hecogenin (Hec) and ginsenoside Re (Gin Re). DNA barcodes were also found in the inguinal lymph nodes after an intramuscular injection, as expected (due to the proximity to the injection site). Gin Re LNPs lead to the 2-fold higher accumulation of barcodes in the lymph nodes, unlike Q LNPs ( ). Barcodes did not appear to extravasate into the bloodstream to reach other organs. There was no linear correlation of the DNA barcode delivery efficiency with the amount of saponins introduced.
Four saponins repeatedly stood out in the barcode evaluation process—quillaic acid, macranthoidin B, hecogenin, and ginsenoside Re. With these results in mind, we wanted to see if these formulations would show higher mRNA transfection in vivo as compared to standard MC3 LNPs. The chosen formulations were GinRe (10 mol% added), Hec (10 mol%), Q (15 mol%), and Mac B (10 mol%). The LNPs were formulated with firefly luciferase mRNA and tested both in vitro in a more sensitive version of the galectin-GFP endosomal escape assay (Gal9-GFP HEK293 cell line ) and in vivo for firefly luciferase mRNA expression. Notably, the results differed significantly from those found with the DNA barcode cargo. All saponins lead to a significant increase in endosomal disruption events compared to MC3 control, with MacB and Q LNPs showing the highest extent of disruption (up to 3-fold compared to Chol LNP; A and 4B). In comparison, MacB LNPs loaded with DNA barcode lead to only 40% increase, and Q LNPs had 10 times fewer endosomal disruption events compared to Chol LNPs. Although some discrepancy can be attributed to the different type of galectin used in the reporter assay, inversion of the trend for Q LNPs likely indicates the variability in the nanoparticle properties depending on the cargo. This observation is further corroborated by in vivo experiments. Upon administering the LNPs to Balb/c mice intravenously, we found little difference in bioluminescence signal between treatments ( C). The pattern of bioluminescence was consistent with hepatic transfection, typical for LNPs. Despite an elevated number of endosomal events detected in Gal9-GFP assay, Q LNPs produced 2-fold lower bioluminescence signal than control LNPs. On the other hand, Q LNPs loaded with DNA barcode produced 10-fold fewer disruption events but had 2-fold higher accumulation in the liver ( B and 3F). Lastly, we also compared the extent of IRF activation in J774 macrophages and found that saponin mRNA-LNPs did not stimulate IRF pathway compared to control, which was not the case with DNA barcode LNPs ( D). No significant NF-κB activation was observed for mRNA LNPs. This was unexpected, since we anticipated significant activation of these pathways due to the intracellular disruption and recognition of mRNA ; however, this may be a limitation of the cell-based assay. Combined, these observations may indicate differential loading of the saponins into LNPs and deterioration of saponin mRNA-LNPs in the bloodstream.
 Finally, we evaluated adjuvant activity of saponin LNPs loaded with ovalbumin (OVA) mRNA in vivo. We chose MacB and Q LNPs as they caused the highest extent of endosome disruption, which could be a relevant predictor of adjuvant activity in local administration. Balb/c mice received two doses of OVA mRNA encapsulated in a MacB (10 mol% saponin added), Q (15 mol% saponin added), or control Chol LNP intramuscularly in the hind flanks in a prime-boost regimen (study plan shown in E) and were monitored for 28 days after first injection. Mouse blood serum samples were analyzed via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for three anti-OVA immunoglobulins—immunoglobulin G (IgG), IgG1, and IgG2a. Anti-OVA IgG titers can be used as a general measure of the adjuvant activity of LNP formulations (since the mRNA delivery efficiency was very similar between the formulations based on the Fluc mRNA results), whereas anti-OVA IgG1 and IgG2a titers can serve as a proxy for Th2/Th1 responses, respectively. As expected, the amount of all immunoglobulins was elevated compared to baseline after treatments and higher after the second dose ( F). No significant differences between treatments were observed for IgG and IgG1; however, the amount of IgG2a was significantly elevated for Q LNPs compared to Chol LNPs. Consequentially, we are able to compare the effect of saponins on the type of innate response determined by the ratio of IgG2a to IgG1. All treatments indicate more Th1-skewed response at 28 days compared to 14 days, which was expected due to the nature of the cell-mediated innate immune response and enhanced recognition of intracellular pathogen presented by LNPs. Q LNPs had a significant (2-fold) increase in IgG2a/IgG1 ratio compared to unmodified MC3 LNPs ( G) at 28 days after prime injection, which indicates an even more Th1-biased response.

## Discussion
The utility of mRNA therapies extends past vaccine applications toward gene editing and protein replacement therapies. Regardless of the application, LNPs will remain a popular choice as non-viral delivery vectors due to their compatibility with a wide range of genetic cargos and excellent tolerability, but their immunogenic and reactogenic properties must be carefully considered to alleviate the potential side effects and toxicity. This is especially true for individuals who may have a compromised immune status or underlying chronic conditions. In theory, the plug-and-play nature of LNPs may enable rapid generation of precision vaccines, where adjuvant components can be added to accommodate specific immune states of the patients. 
We determined that adding the saponins into the lipid solution during formulation is the best way to incorporate saponins into the LNPs while maintaining the nucleic acid encapsulation and the physical properties of the LNP. Overall, these saponins can be successfully incorporated into LNPs at batch sizes appropriate for cell and animal studies, endow LNPs with higher hydration at the surface without affecting the bulk nanoparticle properties, and improve transfection without compromising cell viability. While saponin-loaded LNPs showed increased endosomal escape in a cell-based assay, it did not correlate strongly with in vivo transfection efficacy—only modest in vivo transfection increase could be achieved with saponin LNPs compared to MC3 LNP control. Despite the evidence suggesting superficial localization of saponins in the LNPs, they do not seem to significantly change the composition of the protein corona that could enable LNPs to reach organs beyond the liver. Notably, the behavior of saponin LNPs in biological systems was strongly dependent on the cargo, as evident from the differences between mRNA and barcode-DNA-loaded LNPs in endosomal escape and immune activation assays. These differences likely arise due to differential endosomal recognition of the cargos by immune response sensors and differential stabilities of the cargos.
Out of 40 saponins initially included in the scope of this study, quillaic acid consistently showed excellent nanoparticle stability, enhanced endosomal escape, and elevated immune activation in both NF-κB and IRF pathways. While quillaic acid LNPs did not increase mRNA transfection after intravenous administration, they produced a more Th1-biased response after prime-boost vaccination regimen with OVA mRNA LNPs. This may indicate that quillaic acid LNPs could reduce the risk of vaccine-associated enhanced respiratory disease (VAERD), which has been associated with Th2-biased immune responses in children, and potentially act as a more efficient vaccine against intracellular pathogens. , We acknowledge that our findings do not capture all the intricacies of the immune response to saponin LNPs; nonetheless, our data suggest a correlation between saponin structure and intracellular processes and the capability to tailor immune response to LNP-based vaccines.
Quillaic acid is one of the compounds isolated from the Chilean soapbark tree Quillaja saponaria and is closely related to the known adjuvant QS-21, which is currently used in several vaccine products ( ). QS-21 contains a lipophilic acyl side chain and heavy glycosylation at both C3 and C28 carbon atoms in the quillaic acid core, which increase its bioavailability and adjuvant properties. , Therefore, it is not too surprising that quillaic acid was the most prominent saponin in our studies. However, macranthoidin B and hederacoside C are also quillaic acid derivatives glycosylated at C3 and C28, but do not contain an acyl chain. These saponins had higher levels of endosomal disruption compared to quillaic acid that did not correlate with the immune response or induction of IgG antibodies. Therefore, although glycosylation of the quillaic acid core seems to enhance endosomal escape of LNPs incorporating these derivatives, it is not sufficient to increase mRNA translation. Although QS-21 was not included in the scope of our study, Luan et al. report successful loading of QS-21 into MC3 LNPs and an associated increase in the adjuvant activity of the LNP treatment. Their report, however, described a more complex adjuvant system and did not discuss the synergy between QS-21 and LNPs. Exploring the structure-activity relationship of quillaic acid derivatives, as well as macranthoidin B and hederacoside C, as custom adjuvants in LNPs could be an interesting direction for future research for both infectious disease and cancer vaccine applications.
 

## Materials and Methods

## Materials
All saponin compounds were sourced from Cayman Chemical (Ann Arbor, MI) and dissolved in ethanol at 5 mM or 25 mM. mRNA was purchased from Trilink Biotechnologies (San Diego, CA; Firefly luciferase [L-7602] and ovalbumin [L-7610]-encoding mRNA were used in this study). 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC) was purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, AL); cholesterol was purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO); cryoEM grids, ethanol, RiboGreen reagent kit, 6-(p-Toluidino)-2-naphthalenesulfonic acid sodium salt (TNS reagent), all cell culture media and supplies, and OneGlo-TOX kits were purchased from Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA). HeLa and JE6 cell lines were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA). J774-Dual reporter macrophages were a gift from Prof. Conroy Sun (Oregon State University; originally sourced from Invivogen [catalog number j774d-nfis]).

## Preparation and characterization of the nanoparticles

## Formulation
LNPs were prepared via microfluidic mixing of ethanol (lipid) and aqueous (nucleic acid) phases in the NanoAssemblr Benchtop mixer (Precision NanoSystems). Ethanol solution comprised of ionizable lipid/phospholipid/cholesterol/DMG-PEG2k mixture at the molar ratio of 50/10/38.5/1.5 and a total lipid concentration of 5.5 mM. mRNA was mixed with water and 50 mM citrate buffer at pH 4.0, keeping the N/P (ionizable lipid:mRNA) ratio constant at 5.67. Mixing was performed at 9–12 mL/min and 3:1 volume ratio of aqueous:ethanol, respectively. Following formulation, LNPs were diluted in DPBS (pH 7.4) at 1:1 v/v ratio and dialyzed against 3 L of 1x PBS for 4 h at room temperature using a 10 kDa Slide-A-Lyzer dialysis cassette, before being transferred to a fresh PBS solution overnight. LNPs were concentrated after dialysis using an MWCO 100 kDa Amicon Ultra-4 mL centrifugal filter unit for in vitro and in vivo studies (Millipore, Burlington, MA). Various saponin addition approaches were evaluated: (1) spiking the final LNP solution (in PBS) with an ethanol solution of saponin at predetermined mol% (of total lipid amount in the base LNP formulation; co-transfection); (2) spiking the lipid phase with a predetermined mol% of saponin—5, 10, 15, and 20 additional mol% of total baseline lipids (which correspond to 4.8, 9.1, 13, and 19.7 mol% of total formulation, respectively); or (3) substituting cholesterol with an equivalent molar amount of saponin. LNP size and PDI were determined using DLS in the Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Panalytical Inc., Westborough, MA) by diluting 1,000-fold into a 1x PBS solution at 25°C. Quant-iT RiboGreen assay kit was used to estimate mRNA encapsulation efficiency and concentration.

## Membrane fluidity (Laurdan) assay
A Laurdan hydration assay was performed as described previously. Laurdan dye was first diluted to 100 μM in dimethylformamide (DMF) to prepare a stock solution. Citrate and phosphate buffers were titrated to achieve the desired pH and prewarmed at 37°C for 15 min prior to use. Nanoparticle solutions were diluted to reach a final lipid concentration of 50 μM per well (estimated from mRNA concentration and assuming w/w lipid-mRNA ratio of 20). Samples were thoroughly mixed with buffers and Laurdan dye at 50:1 M ratio in a black 96-well plate and incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the absence of light. Generalized polarization (GP) enables measurement of the differences in emission shift from a (gel) packed phase (440 nm) to a (liquid) disordered phase (490 nm) and was calculated as follows:
(Equation 1)GP=(I440−I490)/(I440+I490),where I is the intensity at respective wavelengths. Each assay contained three technical replicates.

## Cryo-TEM image acquisition and processing
Falcon III and K3 Summit cameras with DED at 300 kV were used to capture cryo-TEM images. The Vitrobot Mark IV system (FEI) was used to plunge-freeze a copper lacey carbon-film-coated Cryo-EM grid (Quantifoil, R1.2/1.3 300 Cu mesh). Two microliters of LNPs was dispensed onto the glow discharge grids in the Vitrobot chamber maintained at a temperature of 23°C and a relative humidity of 100% to freeze the samples. The sample was incubated for 30 s before being blotted with filter paper for 3 s before being submerged in liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen. The frozen grids were examined for any defects, clipped, and assembled into cassettes. The images were taken at an electron dose of 15–20 e−/Å2 and processed and analyzed using Fiji.

## In vitro studies

## Cell culture
Mammalian cell lines used to measure cytotoxicity and transfection efficiency included HeLa (human cervical epithelial cells), dual reporter J774 (murine macrophages), galectin-modified HEK293T/17 (human embryonic kidney cells), and Jurkat E6-1 (human T-lymphocytes). JE6, Gal8-GFP and Gal9-GFP HEK293T/17 cells, and HeLa cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Dual reporter J774 were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 5 μg/mL blasticidin, 100 μg/mL zeocin, 100 μg/mL normocin, 10% FBS, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All the cells were maintained in an atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37°C.

## In vitro transfection
For HeLa and JE6 transfection, a cell density of 4,000 cells/well was plated in white, clear-bottom 96-well plates and allowed to grow for 24 h at 37°C. LNP solutions were then added to the wells at identical volume, and the cells were incubated for an additional 24 h. Cell viability (Promega CellTiter Fluor Cell Viability Assay) and luciferase expression (Promega One-Glo Luciferase Assay) were assessed according to manufacturer’s protocols. Cell viability signal was used to normalize luciferase expression.

## Determination of immune pathway activation with J774 dual reporter macrophages
J774-Dual reporter murine macrophages were plated at a cell density of 50,000 cells/well, transfected at predetermined LNP dose, and incubated for 24 h. The quantification of IRF and NF-κB immune pathway activation was conducted according to the manufacturer’s protocol using Quanti-Luc and Quanti-Blue reagents, respectively. For IRF activation quantification, the luminescence signal for samples was first normalized by the signal found in untreated wells, then converted to logarithmic scale (base 2).

## Endosomal escape quantification by galectin-GFP imaging
HEK293T/17 Gal8-GFP cells were used to measure the extent of the endosomal escape; 96-well plates were precoated with poly-D-lysine and seeded with 293T/17 Gal8-GFP cells at 15,000 cells/well (100 μL per well). After an overnight incubation at 37°C, the cells were dosed with either individual LNPs or 100μM chloroquine (MP Biomedicals, LLC, Cat#193919) as positive control; 100 ng nucleic acid for each barcoded LNP was added to these cells. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, the cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with DAPI, and imaged on the CellInsight NXT HCS Platform with Cellomics software v6.4.4 (Thermo Scientific, Pittsburgh PA) with a 20× objective. The images were then analyzed using a custom CellProfiler imaging pipeline that included illumination correction, quantifying total cells counts from DAPI channel and total endosomal escape events identified as distinct puncta from GFP channel, and normalizing endosomal escape events by the total cell counts.
For Gal9-GFP HEK293T/17 imaging, ibidi 8-well chamber slides (Fitchburg, WI) were coated with poly-d-lysine (Thermo Fisher, Federal Way, WA) and rinsed three times with PBS prior to seeding HEK293T/17 Gal9-GFP reporter cells at 60,000 cells per well in complete media. After overnight incubation, respective buffered LNPs were added dropwise onto the media at a dose of 50 or 200 ng mRNA per well and incubated for 24 h. After incubation, media was gently aspirated, cells were then washed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 10 min at room temperature. Once cells were fixed, wells were gently washed with PBS two more times, and DAPI (Thermo Fisher, Federal Way, WA) was then added at 1:1000 in PBS for nuclear staining. Following DAPI staining, cells were washed again two times, aspirated, and mounted with coverslips using ProLong Diamond Antifade mountant (Thermo Fisher, Federal Way, WA). After the mountant had set, a thin layer of nail polish was utilized to prevent the drying of the sample. Reporter cells were imaged for GFP-positive puncta with a confocal Leica DMi8 microscope (Leica Microsystems) with an oil immersion objective at 40× to report maximum intensity projections. The Gal9-GFP recruitment was quantified using a custom macro written in ImageJ, which included Gaussian smoothing, finding local maxima of GFP puncta, and identifying number of cell nuclei with DAPI channel through using standard segmentation procedures. Gal9 recruitment was reported as the number of GFP puncta divided by the number of cells.

## In vivo studies

## Animals
Balb/c and C57BL6 mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). All mice used in the experiments were 1–6 months old, age-matched for each experiment, and handled in accordance with the animal welfare guidelines outlined by OHSU Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) (Protocol TR01_IP00001707) and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and Animal Research.

## Barcode design
DNA barcodes were single-stranded oligonucleotides, 61 bases long with three phosphorothioate bonds at each end (Integrated DNA Technologies). All oligonucleotide sequences and primers used in this study were previously published. Each barcode oligo contained a 10 bp barcode sequence and a 10 bp unique molecular identifier, as well as forward and reverse adapter sequences necessary for Illumina sequencing.

## Administration and tissue isolation
For intravenous and intramuscular injections, LNPs were diluted with sterile 1x PBS to a desired dose, and mice were sedated by inhalation of 2.5% isoflurane. The intravenous injection was performed by tail vein or retro-orbital injection, intramuscular injection was performed in the hind flanks of the mouse. Mice that received a dose of LNPs were housed until bioluminescence imaging for 24 h unless stated otherwise. Prior to bioluminescence imaging, the mice were sedated by inhalation of 2.5% isoflurane. The sedated mice then received intraperitoneal injection of D-luciferin at 150 mg/kg and rested for 10 min. Bioluminescence imaging was performed on the mice with various exposure times ranging from 1 to 300 s using IVIS Lumina XRMS (PerkinElmer). To evaluate the mRNA expression at an organ level, mice were euthanized and their internal organs (such as the liver, lungs, kidneys, spleen, lymph nodes, and heart) were excised and imaged.
For barcoding studies, BALB/c mice (N = 3) were administered the injection mix LNPs encapsulating barcode DNA and a naked barcode in PBS. Intravenous total dose was 19.6 μg (0.784 mg/kg total dose or 400 ng per formulation), and intramuscular dose was 9.8 μg total DNA dose (0.392 mg/kg or 400 ng per formulation). Six or twenty-four hours after the administration, mice were euthanized, and their organs were excised. Then, tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80oC until ready to be processed for sequencing.

## PCR amplification and Illumina sequencing
Tissues were homogenized in 1.5 mL tubes using disposable pestles. Tissue was lysed and DNA isolated using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue kit (Qiagen) and DNA concentration measured. A nested two-step PCR approach was used to amplify barcodes and prepare for Illumina sequencing. Barcode DNA was amplified from each crude DNA preparation using the HiFi HotStart ReadyMix kit (KAPA) with the following recipe: 12.5 μL Master Mix, 1 μL F Universal Primer (5 μM), 1 μL R Universal Primer (5 μM), 200 ng DNA template, H2O up to 25 μL. Cycling conditions were as follows: 22x: 20 s at 98°C, 15 s at 69°, 15 s at 72°, 1x: 30 s at 72°. PCR products were run out on 1.5% Tris-acetate-EDTA agarose, and bands were excised, pooled, and purified with Wizard SV PCR and Gel Clean-up System. Concentration of these purified PCR products, the barcode library, was quantified by KAPA Library Quantification Kit for sequencing. The library was loaded onto flow cells at 2 nM concentration. Multiplexed, next-generation sequencing runs were performed on an Illumina MiSeq machine with a v2 150 chip at the OHSU Molecular Technologies Core.

## Barcode count analysis
A custom Python script (https://github.com/antonyjozic/lnp_barcode_script) was used to extract raw barcode counts from sequencing data for each sample. These counts were normalized to the barcoded total number of reads in that sample to produce a % of reads containing the barcode value.

## Vaccination studies
The study protocol was as follows: in preparation for the study, at day 0, mice were ear-tagged, and their flanks were shaved. At days 0, 13, and 28, mouse blood samples were collected either through submandibular bleeds or through cardiac puncture (at the terminal time point). The blood was collected into 0.8 mL MiniCollect SST tubes (Greiner Bio) and spun at 1,500 g for 10 min at 4oC to extract serum. Serum samples were then transferred into storage tubes and immediately frozen and stored at −20oC until ready to use. At days 1 and 14, LNPs containing ovalbumin mRNA (OVA mRNA) were administered to the hind flanks of female, age-matched (6 weeks old at the start of the study) BALB/c mice at 0.04 mg/kg. Mice were monitored for any acute reactions at 4–6 h post-injections; no animal distress or irritation at the injection site was observed. Anti-OVA IgG, IgG1, and IgG2a ELISA were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol at 1:500 serum dilution (Chondrex, Inc., Woodinville, WA; catalog numbers 3011, 3013, and 3015).

## Statistical analysis
Data evaluation was performed using GraphPad Prism v10.4.2. One- or two-way ANOVA tests were employed to evaluate statistical significance, with Tukey’s multiple comparison test unless stated otherwise. Significant differences were indicated as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗p < 0.0001 related to controls unless stated otherwise.
