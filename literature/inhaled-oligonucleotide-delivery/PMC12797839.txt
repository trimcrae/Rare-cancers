## Background
The intratumoral microbiota plays dual roles in cancer progression and suppression, but its composition and functional mechanisms in endometrial carcinoma (EC) remain incompletely defined. This study aimed to characterize the EC intratumoral microbiome, elucidate microbial spatial localization, and identify bacteria with tumor-suppressive properties.

## Methods
Tumor and adjacent normal tissues from patients with EC were analyzed using 5R 16S rRNA sequencing to profile microbial communities, with fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) validating bacterial localization. Spatial transcriptomics (ST), single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), and FISH were integrated to map microbiota-niche cell interactions. RNA sequencing was performed on EC cells treated with bacterial supernatant. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from EC patients to mice was used to assess gut-tumor microbial crosstalk.

## Results
Collinsella aerofaciens (C. aerofaciens), Haloamaerobium gallinarum, and Massilia oculi were enriched in adjacent normal tissues, while Bacteroides vulgatus (B. vulgatus) and Delfia tsuruhatensis dominated tumor tissues. Tumors exhibited reduced microbial richness versus normal tissues. C. aerofaciens localized predominantly to smooth muscle cells and modulated the tumor microenvironment, as revealed by FISH and ST-scRNA-seq integration. RNA sequencing suggested that C. aerofaciens suppressed EC progression by activating the p53 signaling pathway. FMT experiments demonstrated gut microbiota-driven remodeling of the tumor microbiome.

## Conclusions
This study identifies C. aerofaciens as a novel tumor-suppressive bacterium in EC, with mechanistic evidence linking its activity to p53 pathway activation. Gut microbiota modulates intratumoral microbial composition, suggesting potential dual-target therapeutic strategies for EC.

## Introduction
Endometrial carcinoma (EC) is the most common gynecological malignancy in developed countries [ ] and ranks fourth among all female cancers [ ]. According to recent epidemiological data, an estimated 69,120 new cases and 13,860 deaths are projected in 2025 in America [ ]. EC pathogenesis is closely linked to metabolic syndrome, with obesity, hypertension, and diabetes serving as major risk factors [ ]. Clinical studies have further implicated metabolic abnormalities—including hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and elevated uric acid—as critical co-factors in EC development and progression [ – ].
Emerging evidence has revealed that microbial communities colonize not only barrier surfaces (e.g., gut, skin) but also previously considered “sterile” sites, including tumors [ – ]. Intratumoral microbiota have been detected in various cancer types [ – ], where specific taxa directly influence malignant progression through mechanisms such as inflammation [ , ], immune regulation [ , ], invasion, metastasis [ , ], and epigenetic modulation [ , ]. For instance, intratumoral Escherichia was associated with improved overall survival (16 vs. 11 months; HR = 0.73, p = 0.0065) in non-small cell lung cancer patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors [ ], while Paraburkholderia fungorum exhibits tumor-suppressive effects in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma through alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism [ ]. These findings highlight the microbiota’s potential as a therapeutic target or prognostic biomarker [ ].
In EC, studies suggest associations between uterine/gut microbes and carcinogenesis: uterine microbiota may promote EC through local inflammation and immune dysregulation [ ], while gut microbiota influence EC via metabolite secretion, hormonal modulation, and systemic immune effects [ ]. However, the role of intratumoral microbiota in EC remains poorly understood, despite its established relevance in other cancers. Given the uterus’s unique anatomical and immunological features, characterizing the EC tumor microbiome could unveil novel mechanisms of carcinogenesis and therapy resistance.
Here, we comprehensively profile the intratumoral microbiome in patients with EC using integrated multi-omics approaches, including 5R 16S rRNA sequencing, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), spatial transcriptomics (ST), and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). We identify Collinsella aerofaciens (C. aerofaciens) as a depleted taxon in EC tumors and localize it predominantly to smooth muscle cells (SMCs) and fibroblasts. Functional assays reveal its antitumor effects via p53 signaling pathway modulation, while fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments demonstrate gut microbiota-driven remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. This work bridges microbial ecology and EC biology, offering insights into microbial-based therapeutic strategies.

## Methods

## Tissue sample collection
Endometrial tumor tissues (n = 48) and adjacent non-tumor tissues (n = 20) were collected from patients with EC who underwent surgical resection at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology of the Southwest Hospital of Army Medical University. Specimens were immediately transferred to the laboratory on ice, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C for subsequent 5R 16S rRNA sequencing.

## Fecal samples collection
Fecal samples were collected from eight newly diagnosed EC, nine age- and BMI-matched healthy volunteers (control group). Exclusion criteria included: (1) gastrointestinal diseases; (2) prior antitumor treatment; (3) concurrent malignancies; (4) digestive symptoms (e.g., nausea, diarrhea) within 2 weeks before sampling. Samples were processed and stored as described for tissue samples.

## 5R 16S rRNA sequencing
Microbial genomic DNA was extracted from tissues using the FastPure Stool DNA Isolation Kit (MJYH, Shanghai, China). DNA integrity was assessed via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and concentration/purity was measured using a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific). Multiplex PCR amplification targeting five hypervariable regions (V2, V3, V5, V6, V8) of the 16 S rRNA gene was performed with barcoded primers (sequences listed in Table. ). Amplification conditions were: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, 29 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 53 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Negative control samples (sampling negative control, DNA extraction negative control, PCR amplification negative control) were also included. A scientifically rigorous background contamination filtration process was used to eliminate potential contaminant DNA introduced during sample preparation and storage, DNA extraction, PCR amplification, etc. Samples were collected in a sterile surgical environment, and instruments were sterilized between procedures. Negative controls (sampling, DNA extraction, and PCR) were included to filter background contamination.

 

Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Shanghai Labway Clinical Testing Co., Ltd.). Raw reads were processed using QIIME2, and taxonomic assignment was conducted against the SILVA database. Alpha diversity (Shannon, Chao1 indices) was calculated using the R package vegan, with inter-group differences analyzed via Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Beta diversity was assessed via Bray-Curtis distance-based Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA). Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) analysis (LDA > 2, p < 0.05) identified differentially abundant taxa. We removed taxa present in negative controls or with low abundance.

## 16S rRNA sequencing for fecal samples
High-throughput sequencing was completed by Shanghai Labway Clinical Laboratory Co., Ltd (Labway, Shanghai, China). Fecal microbial DNA was extracted using the same kit as for tissues. PCR targeted the regions (V4, V1-V3, V3-V4, V4-V5). Primer sequences listed in Table. . Sequencing and bioinformatic analysis were performed as described for 5R 16S rRNA sequencing, with rarefaction curves confirming sufficient sequencing depth.

## Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data analysis
scRNA-seq data of EC tumor tissues were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO: GSE173682) (Table. ). Raw data were processed using the R package Seurat (v5.1.0): cells with < 200 genes or > 20% mitochondrial reads were filtered out. Data were normalized via NormalizeData, and 2000 highly variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeatures. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed, followed by batch effect correction with Harmony (v1.2.0). Cells were clustered using UMAP and annotated via SingleR (v2.4.1) with reference to the CellMarker database.

## Spatial transcriptomics (ST) VisiumHD platform (Visium HD Spatial gene expression analysis for FFPE tissues)
High-throughput sequencing was completed by Shanghai Labway Clinical Laboratory Co., Ltd (Labway, Shanghai, China). Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue Section (5 μm) were processed using the Visium HD Spatial Gene Expression Kit (10 × Genomics). After H&E staining and imaging (Leica Scanner, 20 × magnification), sections were decrosslinked and hybridized with probes overnight at 50 °C. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (≥ 275 million read pairs per capture area). Reads were aligned to GRCh38 using Space Ranger (v3.0.0).
ST data were integrated with scRNA-seq using Seurat (v5.1.0). Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD) was applied to deconvolve cell types in spatial spots. The 16 μm spot size (with optimized probe capture via Visium CyteAssist) balances sensitivity and spatial granularity, allowing us to resolve fine-scale transcriptional patterns while maintaining robust gene detection. Ligand-receptor interactions were predicted using CellChat (v2.1.2), with a 250 μm interaction range to focus on local signaling.

## RNA sequencing(RNA-seq)
AN3CA cells were treated with 10% C. aerofaciens supernatant or PBS (control) for 48 h. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and mRNA was enriched with Oligo (dT) beads. cDNA libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (|log2FC| >1, p < 0.05), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.10.1).

## Bacterial strains and culture conditions
C. aerofaciens (BNCC371782) was cultured in modified PYG medium (BNCC363892) under anaerobic conditions (37 °C, 48 h). Bacterial supernatants were collected by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 4 °C, 15 min), filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and stored at 4 °C.

## Cell culture and functional assays
EC cell lines AN3CA and Ishikawa were cultured in DMEM (10% FBS, 37 °C, 5% CO₂). For proliferation assessments, two complementary assays were performed: 1 × 10⁴ cells/well were seeded in 96-well plates for the Cell Count Kit-8 (CCK-8, Bioground, BG0025) assay, and 2 × 10⁴ cells/well were seeded in 24-well plates for 5-Ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU, Beyotime, C0078S) staining. Both groups were treated with bacterial supernatant prior to detection. For migration/invasion assays: wound healing (0-h and 48-h images) and Transwell (8 μm pores, Corning, 3422) assays were performed, with quantification via ImageJ.

## Western blotting
Protein was extracted from tissues and cells using RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B), and detected using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo, 23227). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE (Beyotime, P0012A) and blotted onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skimmed milk for 1.5 h, and incubated overnight with primary antibodies against Occludin (CST, #91131S, 1:1000), Claudin1 (Abclonal, A11530, 1:1000), MMP9 (Abclonal, A25299, 1:1000), β-actin (Abclonal, AC026, 1:30000), PAI-1 (Proteintech, 66261-1-Ig, 1:1000), PTEN (Abclonal, A19104, 1:1000), FAP (CST, #66562, 1:1000), α-SMA (CST, #14968, 1:1000), BAX (Abclonal, A20227, 1:1000), p21(Abclonal, A19094, 1:1000), α-tubulin (Abclonal, A6830, 1:2000). Afterward, the proteins were incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 h. Bands were detected using an ECL kit (Abclonal, RM00021).

## RT-qPCR
Total RNA was isolated from cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) using TRIzol reagent (Takara, 9109). First-strand cDNA was synthesized with the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, RR047A). Real-time PCR was carried out on a CFX96 Touch system (Bio-Rad) with TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, RR820A) in a 10 µL reaction. Cycling conditions were 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 39 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 57 °C for 30 s (plate read). Melting-curve analysis ramped from 65 °C to 95 °C at 0.5 °C per 5 s. Relative gene expression was calculated by the 2^−ΔΔCt method using GAPDH as the endogenous control. Primers (5’→3’) are listed in Table. .

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA)
CAFs were treated with 10% C. aerofaciens supernatant for 48 h. Subsequently, the cell supernatants were centrifuged at 1000 × g and 4℃ for 15 min to remove cellular debris, and the resulting supernatants were harvested. The levels of cytokines, including transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1), interleukin (IL)-8, and IL-6, were determined using ELISA kits (ABclonal, RK00055, RK00011, RK00004).

## Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining
FFPE tissues were sectioned at 4 μm thickness, deparaffinized with xylene, and stained with H&E using a standard protocol (Beyotime, C0105M). Stained sections were imaged via optical microscopy.

## Immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Following the instructions of the IHC detection kit (Boster, SA1022), the sections were inactivated, antigen retrieved, and blocked. They were then incubated overnight with antibodies against Occludin (CST, #91131S, 1:100), Claudin1 (Abclonal, A11530, 1:100), MMP9 (Abclonal, A25299, 1:100), Ki-67 (Abclonal, A20018, 1:1000), PAI-1 (Proteintech, 66261-1-Ig, 1:100), PTEN (Abclonal, A2113, 1:1000), LPS (Abcam, ab8274, 1:100). Afterward, they were incubated with secondary antibodies for 30 min. DAB staining was performed, and the nuclei were stained with hematoxylin. Finally, imaging was performed under an optical microscope.

## Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)
FISH was used to detect bacteria in EC tumor tissues, adjacent tissues and mice tumor tissues. The probes used were EUB338 (Servicebio, 5’-CY3-GCCCCCCCCGTAGGAGT-3’), Collinsella aerofaciens (Hippobio, 5’-FAM-UAGCGAUUCAUACGGGAAGUAUUGAGGAUAUGCC-3’), Bacteroides vulgatus (Hippobio, 5’-CY3-UCUCAGCAAUAUCAUAGUAUGGCGACUUUUCAGAAG-3’) for specific binding to bacterial 16 S rRNA. After probe precipitation, the sample slides were pre-hybridized, hybridized, post-hybridization washed, and DNA counterstained. Finally, observation and photography were performed under a fluorescence microscope.

## Experimental mice
Five-week-old BALB/c nude mice sourced from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd., China. The mice adapted to the environment for 1 week and were raised under conditions of 12-hour light/dark alternation at 25 °C. At the end of the treatments, all mice were euthanized using CO2 inhalation.

## Cell-derived xenograft (CDX) mouse model
AN3CA cells suspended in sterile PBS were injected subcutaneously into the right axilla of nude mice (1 × 107 cells/mouse). Tumor volume were measured when the tumor diameter was greater than or equal to 5 mm. In animal experiments, the tumor volume in mice should not exceed 2000 mm³, which means that the diameter in any dimension should be less than 20 mm. This guideline is essential for adhering to animal welfare and ethical standards, ensuring that experimental animals do not suffer undue distress.

## Antibiotic treatments and fecal Microbiome transplantation (FMT)
Five-week-old nude mice were given oral antibiotic cocktails for 10 consecutive days to eliminate their own gut microbiome. The antibiotics (ATBx) were dissolved in sterile water, including vancomycin (0.5 g/L), ampicillin (1 g/L), neomycin sulfate (1 g/L), and metronidazole (1 g/L). After 10 days, feces of patients with EC/ healthy volunteers were diluted at 150 mg/ml in sterile PBS, inverted and mixed evenly, filtered through a 40 μm filter membrane to remove large particles, resulting in the final fecal suspension.
In the first FMT experiment, mice were divided into two groups: (1) control group (n = 9): provided with 100 µL of sterile PBS daily for 10 days, followed by 200 µL of sterile PBS for 22 days. (2) EC group (n = 8): provided with 100 µL of ABX daily for 10 days, followed by 200 µL of feces from patients with EC for 22 days. The second FMT experiment included three groups: (1) control group (n = 5): maintained on 100 µL of sterile PBS daily for 10 days and 200 µL of sterile PBS daily for next 20 days. (2) EC group (n = 5): underwent the same ABX and EC-FMT protocol as the first experiment. (3) NC group (n = 5): provided with 100 µL of ABX for 10 days followed by 200 µL of FMT from healthy volunteers for 20 days. Control mice received PBS during both phases to match the procedural stress of daily gavage across all groups. All experiments were conducted simultaneously under identical housing conditions to minimize batch effects.

## Statistical analysis
In this study, p-value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. We emphasized the importance of biological replication in our experiments, with at least three independent biological replicates for cell-based assays and at least five independent biological replicates for animal studies. Data analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism V9.0 (San Diego, CA) following the aforementioned procedures, ensuring both accuracy and reliability. These rigorous analytical methods provided robust support for our experimental conclusions.

## Results

## Differences in microbial diversity in endometrial carcinoma
Experimental design is illustrated in Fig.  . 5R 16S rRNA sequencing was employed to compare microbial communities between tumor tissues and adjacent tissues from EC patients, with clinical and biochemical characteristics summarized in Table  .

 

In tumor tissues, α-diversity indices—including Shannon (species evenness), Sobs (Observed Species), and Chao1 (estimated richness)—were significantly lower than those in adjacent tissues, whereas the Simpson index (dominance) was higher (p < 0.05; Fig.  A, Fig. A). These findings indicate reduced microbial richness and evenness, with increased community dominance, in EC tumor tissues. Furthermore, β-diversity analysis revealed limited separation between tumors and adjacent tissues: PCoA showed significant but weak clustering (ADONIS R² = 0.026, p < 0.05), suggesting overall microbial composition similarity between groups (Fig.  B). At the genus level, a Venn diagram confirmed greater microbial richness in adjacent tissues (Fig.  C). At the phylum level, EC tumor tissues exhibited significantly lower abundance of Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Thermi compared to adjacent tissues (Fig.  D). Differences in other phyla are detailed in Supplementary Fig. B-F.

 

LEfSe identified two dominant taxa in tumor tissues and 20 in adjacent tissues at the species level (Fig.  E). Tumor tissues were enriched in Bacteroides vulgatus (B. vulgatus) and Delftia tsuruhatensis, while adjacent tissues were dominated by Haloanella gallinarum, Massilia oculi, Collinsella aerofaciens (C. aerofaciens), Veillonella parvula, and Pediococcus pentosaceus. Serum Cancer Antigen 199 (CA199) exhibited a strong negative correlation with tumor tissue α-diversity (Shannon index; R² = 0.962, p < 0.05), indicating higher CA199 levels associate with reduced microbial evenness (Fig.  F). No significant correlations were observed for CA125, HE4, or CEA (Fig. G-I). Notably, metabolic syndrome markers—serum creatinine (SCr) and glomerular filtration rate (GFR)—also correlated negatively with microbial diversity (Fig. J-K), aligning with EC’ s metabolic dysregulation phenotype.

## Detection of bacterial components in the tumor microenvironment
To confirm bacterial presence in EC tumors and adjacent tissues, 5R 16S rRNA sequencing was initially performed. Histopathological analysis via H&E staining (Fig.  A) and Ki-67 IHC (Fig.  B, Fig. A) confirmed elevated proliferative activity and malignancy in tumors, as evidenced by significantly higher Ki-67 expression compared to adjacent tissues.

 

Given the low microbial biomass typically observed in tumor tissues, direct bacterial verification requires sensitive methods. LPS IHC (the bacterial cell wall component) was performed on EC tissues (Fig.  C), followed by FISH with custom DNA oligonucleotide probes targeting B. vulgatus and C. aerofaciens. Broad-spectrum bacterial probe EUB338 detected bacteria in both tumor and adjacent tissues (Fig.  D). while taxon-specific FISH revealed B. vulgatus enrichment in tumor tissues and C. aerofaciens dominance in adjacent tissues (Fig.  E-F).
Next, tumor and adjacent tissues were homogenized and cultured on Columbia blood agar plates, and Aerobic growth was observed in both tissue types, while anaerobic growth was restricted to adjacent tissues (Fig.  G-H), indicating greater microbial diversity in non-tumor tissues. Enriched colonies underwent 16 S rRNA sequencing, confirming overlap with 5R 16S rRNA sequencing results (Fig. B-C). Scanning electron microscopy was used to characterize C. aerofaciens morphology (abundant in adjacent tissues), as selected for subsequent experiments (Fig.  I).

## Spatial characteristics of cell types in the endometrial carcinoma microenvironment
Next, scRNA-seq was performed on 5 EC tumor samples, identifying diverse cell populations (Table. ). Using established marker genes [ ], 32,234 cells were annotated into seven major types: Fibroblasts, NK&T cells (natural killer cells and T cells), Epithelial cells, SMCs, Macrophages, B cells, and Mast cells. Cellular proportion analysis showed Fibroblasts as the most abundant population across all samples, with particularly high enrichment in Patient 1 and Patient 5, suggesting a critical role in the EC microenvironment (Fig. A). Epithelial cells—consistent with EC’s epithelial origin—were detected in all samples, while SMCs were less abundant, indicating they are not the primary stromal component. t-SNE and UMAP clustering visualized distinct separation of cell types, highlighting the complexity and heterogeneity of the EC tumor microenvironment (Fig. B, Fig. A). Marker gene expression confirmed cell type annotations: Epithelial cells (EPCAM, KRT18); Fibroblasts (DCN, COL1A1, COL1A2); SMCs (ACTA2, TAGLN, RGS5); B cells (IGKC, CD79A) (Fig. C).

 

To map the spatial distribution of cell types, 126,950 tumor spots and 120,100 adjacent tissue spots were analyzed via ST (1 sample each for tumor and adjacent tissue). H&E staining confirmed histopathological features of ST sections (Fig. D). Robust Cell Type Decomposition (RCTD) [ ] integrated scRNA-seq and ST data, enabling spatial localization of cell types at 16 μm resolution (Fig. E, Fig. B). Spatial clustering revealed heterogeneous distribution patterns, with distinct niches of Fibroblasts, Epithelial cells, and immune cells, reflecting the tumor microenvironment’s structural complexity. The combined use of scRNA-seq and ST identified cell-type-specific spatial gene expression signatures, providing insights into the functional organization of the EC microenvironment. This multi-omics approach enhances understanding of tumor-stroma interactions and disease mechanisms.

## C. aerofaciens DNA transcripts are enriched in fibroblasts and smooth muscle cells
To explore the interaction between bacteria and cells in EC development, we integrated spatial distribution information with bacterial localization characteristics. Based on LEfSe analysis (Fig. E), C. aerofaciens was selected for further study, and three representative regions were analyzed for cell spatial distribution (Fig. A), each consisting of a large overview image and an enlarged inset: NC (EC adjacent tissue), EC1 (EC tumor non-epithelial cell region, with cell type composition similar to adjacent tissue), and EC2 (EC tumor epithelial cell region), which exhibited distinct cell compositions and distributions (Fig. A-B). The enlarged views revealed that NC displayed uniform cell distribution, reflecting normal tissue structure and function; EC1 showed cell distribution patterns resembling NC but with focal aggregation or reduction of specific cell types; and EC2 was dominated by epithelial cells with redistribution of other cell populations. FISH analysis, performed on adjacent sections from spatial transcriptomics samples, demonstrated that C. aerofaciens was more abundant in adjacent tissues and predominantly localized to SMCs and fibroblasts across all three regions (indicated by red arrows), with a particular enrichment in SMCs (Fig. B); notably, C. aerofaciens was primarily intracellular in SMCs, with occasional extracellular localization, consistent with current models of microbiota-host interactions [ – ].

 

Given this stromal enrichment, we hypothesized that C. aerofaciens may directly influence CAFs in the tumor microenvironment. To test this, primary CAFs isolated from EC tumor tissues were co-cultured with C. aerofaciens supernatant, and Western blot analysis revealed a decrease in α-SMA (p < 0.01) and reduction in FAP (p < 0.05) (Fig. A), indicating suppression of myofibroblast differentiation; qPCR further showed significant downregulation of TGFβ1, COL1A1, LOX, MMP2, and TIMP1 (Fig. B), suggesting impaired ECM remodeling capacity. Transwell invasion assays confirmed fewer invading CAFs upon exposure to bacterial supernatant (Fig. C), consistent with reduced ECM degradation, while ELISA detected significant decreases in pro-tumorigenic factors including TGFβ1, IL-8, and IL-6 (Fig. D). Collectively, these results indicate that C. aerofaciens may exert antitumor effects by functionally reprogramming CAFs.
Analysis of interaction relationships among the 8 clustered cell types revealed that in NC, cell communication was critical for tissue homeostasis and function; in tumor tissues, EC1 regions were involved in angiogenesis and matrix remodeling, while EC2 regions showed tumor cells remodeled the microenvironment through interactions with stromal cells (Fig.  C). KEGG enrichment analysis of SMCs in the three regions (Fig.  D) showed that differential genes in NC were mainly enriched in pathways such as “Cytoskeleton in muscle cells” and “TNF signaling pathway”; in EC1, pathways including “Ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis” and “Adherens junction” were enriched; and in EC2, “Cell cycle” and “Tight junction” pathways were prominent, suggesting that SMCs in different regions may exhibit distinct functional states supporting tumor progression. These findings collectively suggest that C. aerofaciens may influence the tumor microenvironment by regulating host cell metabolism and signaling pathways.

## C. aerofaciens inhibits EC progression in vitro and in vivo
To systematically validate the antitumor effects of C. aerofaciens, we performed functional assays spanning in vitro cell models to in vivo xenografts. First, bacterial supernatant—enriched in metabolites, secreted proteins, and extracellular polysaccharides—was co-cultured with EC cells to assess impacts on proliferation, migration, and invasion. Using CCK8, EdU, wound healing, and Transwell assays, we examined effects on EC cell lines AN3CA and Ishikawa (Fig.  A). CCK8 results showed that C. aerofaciens supernatant inhibited cell proliferation in a dose-dependent manner, with higher concentrations exerting stronger inhibitory effects (Fig.  B, Fig. A), leading to the selection of 3% and 10% for subsequent experiments. EdU assays further confirmed proliferation inhibition, with 10% supernatant showing more significant effects (Fig.  C, Fig. B). In wound healing experiments, scratch healing rates were significantly lower in supernatant-treated groups compared to controls, with slower healing at higher concentrations (Fig.  D, Fig. C). Transwell assays demonstrated reduced cell invasion, with 10% supernatant decreasing invaded cells (Fig.  E, Fig. D), collectively indicating that C. aerofaciens supernatant suppresses EC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion.

 

To further investigate tumor-suppressive effects, a CDX model was established: five-week-old BALB/c nude mice were acclimated for one week, then subcutaneously injected with AN3CA cells to induce tumor formation (Fig.  F). Mice were divided into four groups: (1) tumor cells alone, (2) tumor cells + C. aerofaciens, (3) tumor cells + C. aerofaciens supernatant, and (4) tumor cells + C. aerofaciens + antibiotics (a mixture of penicillin, cephalosporin, and metronidazole to inhibit Gram-positive anaerobes). Post-treatment, tumor growth rates, volume, and weight were significantly lower in the C. aerofaciens, C. aerofaciens supernatant, and C. aerofaciens + antibiotics groups compared to controls, with the C. aerofaciens group trending toward smaller tumors than the C. aerofaciens + antibiotics group (Fig.  G-I). FISH analysis with C. aerofaciens-specific probes on tumor paraffin sections confirmed bacterial presence in the C. aerofaciens and C. aerofaciens + antibiotics groups, with a marked reduction in the latter (Fig.  J).
These results validate the accuracy of our in vivo experiments and highlight the tumor-suppressive potential of C. aerofaciens.

## C. aerofaciens inhibits EC cell growth by activating the p53 signaling pathway
Based on cell phenotype experiments showing superior tumor inhibition with 10% C. aerofaciens supernatant compared to 3%, we treated EC cells with 10% supernatant and used conventionally cultured EC cells as controls for RNA-seq analysis (Fig.  A). Clustering analysis revealed that C. aerofaciens significantly altered the transcriptomic profile of AN3CA cells (Fig.  B, Fig. A-B), with 101 genes upregulated and 151 genes downregulated (fold change > 1, p < 0.05) between the two group (Fig.  C). KEGG pathway analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs; count m ≥ 2) identified enrichment in tumor-relevant pathways, including the TNF signaling pathway, p53 signaling pathway, and MAPK signaling pathway (Fig.  D).

 

To explore broader biological processes, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed, revealing significant enrichment (FDR < 0.25) in hallmark pathways such as Cellular senescence (NES = 1.46), cell cycle (NES = 1.49), and Th17 cell differentiation signaling (NES = 1.40) (Table. ); notably, the p53 pathway ranked among the top enriched pathways (NES = 1.52), validating our KEGG findings. Previous studies have demonstrated that loss of p53 signaling confers a survival advantage to cancer cells, enabling abnormal proliferation despite DNA damage [ ], and p53 inactivation is closely associated with various cancers, including glioblastoma, breast cancer, prostate cancer, lung cancer, and uterine cancer [ ]. KEGG enrichment analysis further highlighted upregulation of p53 pathway-related genes such as PAI and PTEN (Fig. E); as the most extensively studied member of the PAI family, PAI-1 plays critical roles in inflammatory responses, cell migration, tissue remodeling, and cancer invasion. Western blot analysis confirmed that C. aerofaciens supernatant significantly increased PAI-1 and PTEN expression, indicating p53 pathway activation in EC cells (Fig. F, Fig. C).
Consistently, in vivo experiments showed that tumor tissues from mice in the C. aerofaciens and C. aerofaciens supernatant groups exhibited significantly higher expression of these proteins compared to controls, while expression was reduced in the C. aerofaciens + antibiotics group (Fig.  G, Fig. D), with similar results observed via IHC (Fig.  H). IHC analysis of paraffin-embedded tumor sections further revealed that Ki-67 expression in tumor cell nuclei was significantly higher in the control group than in the C. aerofaciens and C. aerofaciens supernatant groups (Fig.  I, Fig. E), suggesting that C. aerofaciens and its supernatant inhibit tumor cell proliferation.
We further confirmed p53 pathway activation in CAFs, where C. aerofaciens supernatant upregulated p21 and BAX expression; this activation was abolished by the p53 inhibitor Pifithrin-α (PFTα, 20 µM) (Fig. F), demonstrating p53 dependence in both tumor cells and CAFs. Together, our RNA-seq analysis and validation experiments indicate that C. aerofaciens inhibits EC cell growth by modulating the p53 signaling pathway.

## Gut microbiota may regulate intratumoral microbial landscape
To investigate links between intratumoral and gut microbiota, we depleted the gut microbiota of nude mice, induced subcutaneous tumor formation, and performed FMT using samples from patients with EC (Fig.  A). Post-treatment, the FMT (EC) group exhibited markedly accelerated tumor growth compared to non-FMT controls, with significantly higher growth rates, volumes, and weights (Fig.  B-C, Fig. A). 16S rRNA sequencing analysis of gut microbiota α-diversity revealed that the Shannon index of post-FMT fecal samples was lower than that of pre-FMT and antibiotic-pretreated mice (Fig.  D), indicating that EC patient-derived FMT significantly altered the diversity and composition of the murine gut microbiota. Notably, intratumoral microbiota in FMT-treated mice exhibited higher abundance of donor fecal microbiota relative to controls; however, no significant differences in intratumoral microbial composition, α-diversity or β-diversity were observed between FMT-treated and untreated mice (Fig. B-D), potentially due to insufficient FMT duration, as microbial compositional shifts in disease are typically gradual.

 

Western blot and IHC analyses demonstrated significantly decreased expression of Occludin and Claudin1, and increased MMP9 in the colon of FMT-treated mice (Fig.  E-F, Fig. E). Histological examination via H&E staining further revealed disrupted colonic crypt structures, reduced crypt numbers, dilated and congested interstitial vessels, and mucosal lamina propria hemorrhage in FMT-treated mice compared to controls (Fig.  F). IHC also detected higher Ki-67 expression in tumor tissues of the EC group versus controls, indicating elevated tumor cell proliferation (Fig.  G, Fig. F). To rule out allogeneic FMT effects, we performed parallel FMT using feces from healthy females (NC group; Fig.  H). After gut microbiota reconstitution, mice receiving patients with EC feces exhibited significantly higher tumor growth rates, volumes, and weights compared to the controls and the NC groups (Fig.  I-J, Fig. G). H&E staining showed colonic gland destruction and replacement by fibrous tissue with mild chronic inflammation in the EC group, whereas controls and the NC group maintained normal colon structures. The EC group also displayed reduced intestinal Occludin and Claudin1, and increased MMP9 expression relative to controls and the NC group (Fig.  K-L, Fig. H), alongside higher Ki-67 expression in tumor tissues (Fig.  M, Fig. I). These findings suggest that patients with EC exhibit gut microbiota dysbiosis, which compromises intestinal mucosal integrity and increases gut permeability. This may facilitate translocation of microbial communities or their metabolites into the bloodstream, thereby altering the intratumoral microbial landscape and promoting tumor progression.

## Discussion
Despite observations of intratumoral microbiota in early-stage EC [ ], definitive evidence linking specific microbial communities to EC pathogenesis remains elusive. Identifying key EC-associated microbiota and characterizing their spatial distribution could significantly advance clinical management strategies for this disease.
In this study, we performed 5R 16S rRNA sequencing on 48 EC tissues and 20 normal tissue controls, complemented by comprehensive clinical data analysis. Our findings revealed distinct microbial compositions in EC tumor tissues, characterized by a significant reduction in α-diversity compared to normal tissues. This observation aligns with recent research on lung adenocarcinoma, which similarly reported decreased bacterial α-diversity in tumor microenvironments [ ]. The diminished microbial α-diversity may contribute to immune suppression and metabolic perturbations within the tumor microenvironment, potentially facilitating tumor progression. EC is well recognized as a metabolic disease associated with risk factors including diabetes [ ], hyperlipidemia, and hypertension. Accordingly, our analysis of clinical parameters demonstrated negative correlations between the Shannon index and levels of CA199 and SCr. CA199 is established as a marker for EC progression and prognosis, while elevated SCr concentrations—even within normal ranges—have been associated with increased risk of metabolic syndrome components in a large cohort study of over 22,000 patients [ ].
Our results showed enrichment of Actinobacteria and Firmicutes phyla in EC tumor tissues, consistent with previous findings in breast cancer microbiomes [ ], suggesting these bacterial groups may play conserved roles in tumorigenesis. Similarly, Clostridium emerged as a key discriminative genus in EC through LEfSe analysis, paralleling observations of Clostridium symbiosum’s tumorigenic effects in colorectal cancer [ ]. Notably, Bacteroides vulgatus represented the most differentially abundant species in EC tissues. These tissue-specific microbial signatures likely reflect distinct host-microbe interactions shaped by hormonal or immune microenvironmental differences, highlighting both shared and cancer-specific microbiota associations that necessitate context-dependent analyses in microbiome-cancer research. This highlights the importance of selecting disease- and tissue-restricted markers for precision oncology, regardless of cancer type [ ].
C. aerofaciens, a commensal gut bacterium belonging to the Actinobacteria phylum, produces butyrate—a crucial metabolite for maintaining gut homeostasis and regulating inflammatory responses [ ]. As the most biologically active short-chain fatty acid derived from commensal bacteria, butyrate mediates host-microbiome crosstalk and influences physiological balance across multiple organ systems. Its function as a histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor enables epigenetic regulation of gene expression, specifically suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β and IL-8) [ ] while promoting anti-inflammatory IL-10 secretion [ ]. Butyrate exerts immunomodulatory effects primarily through G protein-coupled receptors (GPR41/GPR43) signaling pathways, which maintain intestinal immune homeostasis by regulating T cell polarization and differentiation into both effector and regulatory subsets [ ]. Additionally, butyrate enhances intestinal barrier integrity by upregulating tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Occludin, Claudin1) and stimulating mucin production [ ]. Emerging evidence indicates butyrate’s anti-carcinogenic potential through induction of apoptosis in cancer cells via modulation of BAX/BCL-2 pathways while stimulating p21 and p53 expression [ ]. These mechanistic insights align with our experimental findings demonstrating C. aerofaciens’ potent inhibitory effects on EC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in both in vitro and in vivo models, suggesting butyrate production may be a key mediator of its observed antitumor activity. Beyond butyrate metabolism, C. aerofaciens abundance correlates with altered bile acid profiles, suggesting potential involvement in bile acid metabolism regulation. Further studies should investigate whether C. aerofaciens directly converts primary to secondary bile acids or synergizes with other bile-acid-metabolizing bacteria [ ]. C. aerofaciens has also been extensively studied in relation to diabetes [ ] and rheumatoid arthritis [ ], where its anti-inflammatory properties may confer protective roles. Notably, recent research has linked anti-PD-1 antibody treatment to changes in C. aerofaciens abundance in fecal samples from melanoma patients, suggesting potential interactions with anti-tumor immune responses. While additional cohort studies are needed to validate its specific role in immunotherapy, these findings open new avenues for developing personalized immunotherapeutic strategies through gut microbiota modulation [ ]. Modulating gut microbiota composition—particularly enhancing C. aerofaciens abundance—may improve immunotherapy efficacy [ , ]. Furthermore, C. aerofaciens exhibits symbiotic beneficial properties in metabolic diseases like functional constipation [ ] and muscle repair [ ], yet may act as an opportunistic pathogen in neuro-gut axis disorders such as irritable bowel syndrome and premature ejaculation [ , ]. Our experimental results demonstrated primary localization of C. aerofaciens within SMCs and fibroblasts, with predominant colonization in SMCs. Regional analysis of cellular interaction patterns and KEGG enrichment analysis of SMCs revealed dynamic functional changes in supporting tissue homeostasis, tumor microenvironment remodeling, and cancer progression. In normal tissues, SMCs likely maintain vascular stability and tissue mechanical integrity. Within the tumor microenvironment, their functions appear regionally differentiated: in EC1 regions, interactions between SMCs, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells may primarily support angiogenesis and matrix remodeling; in EC2 regions, SMCs may contribute to microenvironment reshaping that promotes invasion and metastasis through tumor-stromal cell crosstalk. These region-specific adaptations suggest SMCs play dual roles in tumor progression—maintaining normal tissue functions while being “hijacked” by tumor cells to support malignant behavior. The preferential colonization of SMCs by C. aerofaciens indicates potential specific bacteria-host cell interactions that may alter SMCs functions through metabolism, secretion, or signaling pathway modulation. These findings provide new insights into tumor microenvironment complexity and identify potential targets for novel therapeutic strategies. Our study confirms the presence of intratumor microbiota in EC patients, suggesting bacterial DNA may serve as diagnostic or prognostic biomarkers, in addition to some biomarkers only could be detected in the blood [ ].
We further revealed that C. aerofaciens might activate the p53 pathway—a critical tumor suppressor involved in all major DNA repair mechanisms through direct and indirect interactions [ ]. The p53 protein exhibits multifaceted anticancer functions, including maintaining genomic stability through DNA repair, regulating cell cycle progression, inducing apoptosis, and modulating metabolic processes [ ]. Similar to natural compounds like Moringa oleifera extracts [ ] that show cancer-selective effects through cell cycle regulation, our observations demonstrate C. aerofaciens’ antitumor activity. Bacteria modulate the p53 signaling pathway to regulate cancer cell proliferation and apoptosis, with additional effects on p53-associated signaling cascades. Notably, bacterial colonization correlates with reduced Ki-67 expression, likely reflecting p53-mediated cell cycle regulation [ ]. Specific bacterial mechanisms include Helicobacter pylori’s CagA protein interacting with host cell p53 to inhibit its function and promote carcinogenesis [ ], and Shigella flexneri’s VirA virulence factor activating calpain-mediated p53 degradation [ ]. These findings highlight complex bacteria-p53 pathway interactions in cancer biology and suggest C. aerofaciens colonization may exert significant antitumor effects.
Previous studies have identified three primary routes of tumor microbial colonization: (1) invasion through compromised mucosal barriers [ , ], (2) spread from adjacent tissues [ ], and (3) hematogenous dissemination from distant sites like the gut and oral cavity [ , ]. Our research demonstrated that fecal microbiota from patients with EC alters tumor microbiota composition in mice. While our FMT experiments establish a causal role for EC-associated gut microbiota in tumor progression, the limited donor sample size necessitates caution due to potential inter-individual microbial variability. Nevertheless, consistent tumor-promoting effects across two independent experiments support the biological significance of these findings. Future studies will expand donor recruitment and optimize FMT duration to further elucidate intratumoral microbiota dynamics. These results, consistently reproduced in independent experiments, indicate gut microbiota plays a crucial role in EC progression through the gut-tumor axis. Recent studies in pancreatic and esophageal cancers similarly demonstrate gut microbiota’s capacity to modulate intratumoral microenvironments, reinforcing our findings [ , ]. Future studies should expand donor cohorts and investigate precise molecular mechanisms through which transplanted microbiota interact with tumor cells to influence cancer progression.
These translational gaps highlight the need for improved models bridging microbial mechanisms with clinical pathophysiology. While SPF nude mice provided an essential platform for proof-of-concept studies, future work should employ orthotopic EC models to more accurately recapitulate human tumor microenvironmental dynamics. Such models would enable investigation of: (1) C. aerofaciens interacts with host immune components, and (2) the relative contributions of immune-mediated versus direct tumor-suppressive mechanisms.
Collectively, this study reveals unique microbial compositions in EC tumor tissue and identifies the mechanism by which C. aerofaciens suppresses tumor progression through p53 signaling pathway activation. These findings provide a rationale for developing novel microbiome-based therapeutic strategies to improve EC patients’ outcomes. However, three key limitations warrant consideration: first, technical differences between 5R and conventional 16S sequencing prevent absolute quantitative comparisons across niches; second, our FMT model’s immunocompromised status and ectopic tumor location may underrepresent human disease complexity; and finally, while C. aerofaciens’ antitumor effects were robust across models, the responsible molecular mediators require metabolomic characterization. With further research and clinical validation, these findings may provide new approaches for EC treatment.

## Conclusions
In summary, our study identified C. aerofaciens as a potential tumor-suppressive bacterium that inhibits EC progression via the p53 signaling pathway, and demonstrates that gut microbiota can influence tumor microbiota composition. The study has limitations regarding sample size, and the molecular mechanisms by which these bacteria can be harnessed as targets for cancer therapy require further investigation. Therefore, future research should include larger, independent cohort studies to validate the specific effects of C. aerofaciens in immunotherapy. Furthermore, additional exploration of the interaction mechanisms between C. aerofaciens and host cells is necessary to develop personalized microbiome-based therapeutic strategies.
