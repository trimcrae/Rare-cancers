## Abstract
Diabetic kidney disease (DKD), characterized by progressive renal dysfunction, is a prevalent microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus and a leading cause of end-stage renal disease worldwide. Despite advances in glycemic and blood pressure control, the incidence and prevalence of DKD continue to escalate, posing a growing public health challenge. Extracellular vesicles, particularly exosomes, are nanometer-sized vesicles secreted by diverse cells and have emerged as key regulators of intercellular communication. By transferring molecular cargo—including proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids—they exert pleiotropic effects on cellular homeostasis and participate in both physiological and pathological processes. Accumulating evidence has revealed dynamic alterations in the quantity and composition of urinary exosomes under diabetic conditions, implicating these vesicles in the multifactorial pathogenesis of DKD and highlighting their promise as liquid biopsy biomarkers for DKD. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the landscape of research on exosomes in DKD. We elucidate their roles in molecular pathology, investigate their potential for diagnostic and prognostic biomarker discovery powered by multi-omics and machine learning approaches, and examine their implications for therapeutic applications, including their use as drug delivery vehicles and direct therapeutic agents. Looking forward, we highlight critical research gaps and future directions, emphasizing the need for artificial intelligence (AI)-integrated multi-omics analyses to decipher exosome heterogeneity, large-scale multicenter trials to validate biomarker efficacy, and innovative strategies to overcome barriers in clinical translation, ultimately paving the way for personalized medicine in DKD management.
Keywords: Diabetic kidney disease, exosomes, extracellular vesicles, urinary biomarkers, multi-omics, drug delivery systems

## Introduction
Exosomes are nanoscale (50–150 nm in diameter), lipid bilayer-enclosed extracellular vesicles (EVs) that are actively secreted by a wide range of living cells, spanning both prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms [ ]. Their biogenesis begins with the inward budding of the plasma membrane, followed by maturation through the endosomal pathway, ultimately leading to their release into the extracellular space. These vesicles are ubiquitously distributed across diverse biological fluids, including saliva, blood, urine, cerebrospinal fluid, synovial fluid, ascitic fluid, amniotic fluid, and breast milk [ ]. Beyond serving as carriers for waste elimination, exosomes also function as natural delivery vehicles for proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, facilitating intercellular signal transduction [ ]. Moreover, due to their disease-specific molecular signatures, exosomes have emerged as promising biomarkers for early diagnosis, disease monitoring, and prognostic assessment. The distinctive biological properties of exosomes—such as their ability to traverse biological barriers [ ], evade phagocytic clearance by monocytes and macrophages [ ], exhibit tissue-targeting specificity [ ], and demonstrate low immunogenicity [ ]—position them as highly favorable candidates for precision medicine applications. Notably, exosomes derived from various stem cell sources have been extensively investigated for their therapeutic potential, with promising results demonstrated in preclinical disease models.
Diabetic kidney disease (DKD), a prevalent and severe microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus (DM), has emerged as the primary contributor to end-stage renal disease worldwide [ , ]. DKD is predominantly characterized by persistent proteinuria, declining glomerular filtration rate, and a long-standing history of diabetes [ , ]. Although microalbuminuria has been established as a prognostic indicator for DKD, renal impairment often precedes its detectable onset, highlighting a critical diagnostic gap [ ]. Current clinical management of DKD faces substantial challenges due to the lack of reliable early biomarkers and targeted therapeutic options. Despite standard interventions—including glycemic optimization, antihypertensive therapy, lipid modulation, and proteinuria reduction—the disease progression remains aggressive, imposing significant burdens on healthcare systems and socioeconomic structures [ ]. EVs, particularly exosomes, mediate intercellular communication and actively participate in the initiation and progression of DKD. Notably, urinary exosomes, which are unable to traverse the glomerular filtration barrier, serve as indicative biomarkers of renal cell functionality. Their stability within urine samples positions them as promising liquid biopsy tools for noninvasive DKD diagnosis. Recent advancements in stem cell-derived exosome therapeutics and exosome-mediated drug delivery systems have opened new avenues for DKD treatment. Over the past decade, research on exosomes in DKD has evolved from initial descriptive studies to mechanistic investigations and now to translational applications in diagnosis and therapy. This journey has been marked by several seminal discoveries that have progressively shaped our understanding of the field. To visually summarize these key milestones and provide a historical context for the discussions that follow, we have constructed a graphical timeline of pivotal achievements in DKD exosome research ( ). While several excellent reviews have summarized the foundational roles of exosomes in cell-to-cell communication and their pathophysiological implications in DKD [ , ], they have not specifically addressed the unique challenges inherent to DKD. This review comprehensively examines the multifaceted roles of exosomes in DKD pathophysiology, diagnostic biomarker development, and innovative therapies, while also discussing both the persistent challenges and recent breakthroughs in this rapidly evolving field.

## The biogenesis and release of exosomes
The biogenesis of exosomes encompasses a sequence of intricate processes, namely vesicle formation, cargo sorting and encapsulation, and vesicle maturation. The plasma membrane undergoes invagination to create a cup-shaped structure, facilitating the internalization of extracellular fluid and assorted components, including proteins, lipids, metabolites, small molecules, and ions, in association with cell surface proteins. This process leads to the formation of early sorting endosomes (ESEs) [ ]. ESEs mature into late sorting endosomes (LSEs), ultimately giving rise to multivesicular bodies (MVBs).
MVBs, formed through the inward budding of the plasma membrane, can either fuse with lysosomes for degradation or fuse with the plasma membrane to release intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) in the form of EVs [ ] ( ). The regulatory mechanisms that dictate the trafficking of MVBs to either the plasma membrane or lysosomes remain to be elucidated, but the inhibition of one pathway potentially augments the alternative [ ].
 Since the early twenty-first century, extensive research has documented the pivotal role of the Endosomal Sorting Complex Required for Transport (ESCRT) in the biogenesis of ILVs, which are essential for vesicular transport [ ]. Notably, ESCRT-independent mechanisms also contribute substantially to exosome formation. Tetraspanins, for instance, play a crucial role in the exosome biogenesis pathway. They direct cargo toward MVBs, compartmentalize the endosomal membrane into functional domains, and enhance the secretion of certain compounds in exosomes [ , ]. Furthermore, membrane sphingolipids, particularly ceramides, significantly contribute to membrane deformation in an ESCRT-independent manner [ ]. Emerging evidence implicates Gi-coupled S1P1 receptors in MVBs maturation, though the precise mechanism requires further elucidation [ ]. Most recently, Rab31 GTPase has been identified as a novel regulator that initiates membrane budding within these microdomains [ ].
The biogenesis and secretion of exosomes are intricately regulated by a multitude of factors that encompass cellular signaling pathways, lipid metabolism, and cellular stress responses. Upon reaching the cell’s plasma membrane, MVBs undergo a standard process for vesicle docking and fusion with the cell membrane. Key components involved in this process include v-SNAREs (located on the vesicles), t-SNAREs (located on the target membranes), Rab GTPases, tethers, and other proteins.The fusion of MVBs with the plasma membrane is precisely regulated by Rab GTPases (e.g., Rab27, Rab35) and SNARE complexes, which ensure targeted docking and membrane fusion [ ].
Emerging evidence demonstrates intricate cross-regulation between cellular pathways and exosome biogenesis. Zou et al. have delineated that exosome secretion is negatively regulated by the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), a key cellular signaling pathway that also governs autophagy [ ]. Building upon this, lysosomal function is central to the regulation of exosome release. Previous studies have highlighted the role of sirtuin 1 (Sirt1) in modulating lysosomal acidification, a critical process for lysosomal function. Sirt1 deficiency has been shown to impair this acidification in breast cancer cells, leading to increased exosome secretion and enhanced tumor dissemination [ ]. This suggests that Sirt1 plays a pivotal role in the control of exosome release, with implications for cancer progression. Our research extends these findings by demonstrating that Sirt1 deficiency also inhibits the expression of A subunit of the lysosomal vacuolar-type H+ ATPase proton pump (ATP6V1A) in podocytes affected by DKD. This inhibition of ATP6V1A further suppresses lysosomal acidification and diminishes the fusion of MVBs with lysosomes, culminating in heightened exosome secretion and podocyte injury [ ]. Furthermore, Li et al. have discovered that endogenous reactive oxygen species can promote the secretion of inflammatory exosomes in podocytes by inhibiting transient receptor potential mucolipin 1 (TRPML1) channel activity [ ]. Previous studies have also reported that high-glucose conditions in vitro increase exosome secretion by podocytes, suggesting that metabolic stress can influence exosome biogenesis [ ].
This multifactorial regulation enables exosomes to function as both physiological mediators of intercellular communication and pathological vectors in disease progression.The emerging understanding of these regulatory networks provides novel therapeutic targets for conditions characterized by dysregulated exosomal signaling, including cancer metastasis and DKD.

3.

## Role of exosomes in DKD pathogenesis
When discussing the role of EVs in DKD, it is imperative to contextualize them within the broader framework of disease progression. As the most common microvascular complication of DM, DKD involves complex multifactorial mechanisms in its pathogenesis that remain incompletely understood. As our comprehension of intercellular communication and signaling has advanced, EVs have become increasingly recognized as a significant area of research interest. These vesicular structures are ubiquitously present both intra- and extracellularly, carrying a diverse array of biomolecules such as proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids. As a mode of intercellular communication, EVs play a pivotal role in modulating various cellular pathophysiological processes, including cell proliferation, apoptosis, and immune responses. The mechanism by which EVs convey signals to proximate cells involves the release of these biomolecules into the extracellular milieu, which in turn can modulate the behavior and functionality of neighboring cells. However, under pathological conditions, alterations in the release and composition of EVs may occur, consequently impacting the onset and progression of diseases.
3.1.

## Exosomes and inflammation
Initially, DKD was regarded as a metabolic renal disorder distinct from inflammatory kidney diseases. However, an increasing body of research has found that the diabetic milieu can induce inflammatory responses and activate oxidative stress within affected tissues and organs [ , ]. In recent years, EVs have been identified as pivotal mediators in the inflammatory processes associated with DKD.
Macrophage infiltration is a key pathological feature of DKD. Research by Zhu et al. has demonstrated that exosomes from high glucose-treated macrophages may activate macrophages and exacerbate kidney damage through the NF-κB p65 signaling pathway [ ] ( ). Additionally, studies have indicated that mesangial cells (MCs) internalize exosomes derived from macrophages treated with high glucose (HG), leading to the activation of inflammatory cytokines and the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, as well as defects in autophagic function [ ]. Studies have also reported that methyltransferase 14 (METTL14) derived from exosomes of M1 macrophages enhances apoptosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress in glomerular endothelial cells induced by HG through mediating PAQR3 m6A modification [ ].
 EVs have also been implicated in intercellular communication between renal and immune cells, modulating the phenotype and function of immune cells, such as macrophages and T cells, and promoting a pro-inflammatory polarization. Xiao et al. found that under hyperglycemic conditions, the release of Delta-like 4 (Dll4) from kidney tubular epithelial cells (TECs) via Epsin1-mediated exosomes activates Notch1 signaling in macrophages, accelerating tubulointerstitial inflammation. However, this effect can be significantly mitigated by knocking down Epsin1 [ ]. Notably, TEC-derived exosomal miR-19b-3p exhibits characteristic upregulation in both doxorubicin-induced chronic proteinuric nephropathy models and a battery of in vitro experimental systems. Upon internalization by macrophages, these exosomes promote M1 macrophage polarization via the NF-κB/SOCS-1 signaling pathway. High levels of miR-19b-3p were also detected in urinary exosomes of patients with DKD, correlating with the severity of tubulointerstitial inflammation [ ]. Jiang et al. reported that the incubation of primary TECs from DKD or HK-2 mTECs with lysophosphatidylcholine increased the release of EVs. These TEC-derived EVs (EVe) activated an inflammatory response in macrophages, leading to the release of macrophage-derived EVs (EVm), which in turn induced apoptosis in lysophosphatidyl choline-injured TECs. The study further found that leucine-rich α-2-glycoprotein 1-enriched EVe activated macrophages through a TGFβR1-dependent pathway, while tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand-enriched EVm induced apoptosis in injured TECs via a death receptor 5-dependent mechanism [ ].
Interestingly, EVs have also been observed to exhibit protective properties in certain contexts, such as by transporting anti-inflammatory factors or by promoting tissue repair mechanisms. Wang et al. discovered that exosomes derived from human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), which transfer miR-22-3p, protect podocytes and diabetic mice from inflammation by mediating the NLRP3 inflammasome [ ].

3.2.

## Exosomes and autophagy
Autophagy, a highly conserved intracellular process, is tasked with the degradation and recycling of misfolded proteins and nonfunctional organelles, thereby maintaining cellular homeostasis via the lysosomal pathway [ ]. In the context of DM, the regulation of autophagy is subject to changes in nutritional status and intracellular stress. These alterations can disrupt the balance of key autophagy-related signaling pathways, including Sirt1, Adenosine 5′-monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK), and the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR). Disruptions in these pathways may contribute to the exacerbation of organelle dysfunction, ultimately contributing to the onset and progression of DKD [ ].
EVs, notably exosomes, exert a complex and critical influence on DKD, modulating the autophagic process in renal cells through diverse mechanisms. Research has demonstrated that macrophage-derived exosomes can promote the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and induce autophagy deficiency in MCs, thereby exacerbating the progression of DKD [ ]. Moreover, specific microRNAs (miRNAs), such as miR-7002-5p, contained within exosomes derived from macrophages under conditions of HG, have been demonstrated to suppress autophagy in TECs by targeting autophagy related 9b (Atg9b), thereby contributing to renal injury [ ]. In contrast, miR-25-3p from M2 macrophage-derived exosomes has been observed to mitigate HG-induced podocyte injury through the activation of autophagy and the inhibition of dual specificity protein phosphatase 1 (DUSP1) expression [ ].
These findings reveal the double-edged role of exosomes in modulating renal cell autophagy, which may exacerbate pathological processes in DKD while also offering new therapeutic strategies. Collectively, the complex modulation of autophagy by exosomes in DKD is pivotal for the development of innovative therapeutic strategies, and a more profound comprehension of these mechanisms is imperative for the advancement of treatment modalities.

3.3.

## Exosomes and renal fibrosis
Renal tubulointerstitial fibrosis is recognized as the terminal pathway in the progression of DKD, a condition that currently lacks effective therapeutic options. Exosomes, derived from various renal cell types, have been shown to modulate renal fibrosis through the delivery of specific miRNAs and other molecular cargo.
Recent studies have identified the miR-196b-5p-EVs pathway as a novel mechanism in aldosterone-induced renal fibrosis in diabetes. These exosomes, rich in miR-196b-5p, facilitate the communication between proximal tubular epithelial cells (PTECs) and fibroblasts during the development of renal fibrosis, a process that is likely mediated via the STAT3/SOCS2 signaling pathway [ ]. Wen et al. reported a reduction in exosome secretion in the renal tissue of DKD mice compared to non-diabetic controls, and also observed that HG conditions decreased exosome secretion in mouse renal proximal tubular BUMPT cells. The study further investigated the effects of tubular cell-derived exosomes on fibroblasts, revealing that exosomes from BUMPT cells cultured under HG conditions could enhance the proliferation, induce morphological changes, and promote the substantial production of fibronectin, α-smooth muscle actin, and type I collagen in NRK-49F fibroblasts [ ]. Liu et al. elucidated that HNRNPA1-mediated exosomal sorting facilitates the transportation of cellular miR-483-5p from TECs into the urine. This process reduces the inhibitory effect of cellular miR-483-5p on MAPK1 and TIMP2 mRNAs, thereby augmenting extracellular matrix deposition and the progression of DKD-induced renal interstitial fibrosis [ ]. Additionally, it has been observed that exosomal circ_DLGAP4 levels are elevated in exosomes isolated from HG-treated MCs compared to those from normal subjects. Exosomal circ_DLGAP4 has been shown to promote the proliferation and fibrosis of MCs by sequestering miR-143 and targeting the ERBB3/NF-κB/MMP-2 axis [ ]. Furthermore, exosomes from satellite cells carrying the miR-23a/27a/26a cluster have been demonstrated to mitigate renal tubulointerstitial fibrosis in mouse models of DKD [ ].
These findings not only shed light on the molecular mechanisms underlying DKD fibrosis but also suggest potential therapeutic targets for intervention. The capacity of stem cell-derived EVs to inhibit and reverse fibrosis progression in mouse models of DKD underscores the therapeutic potential of exosome-based interventions in the management of DKD.

3.4.

## Exosomes and oxidative stress
Oxidative stress can promote inflammation by activating transcription factors that promote the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines [ ]. Current research has substantiated the pivotal roles of inflammatory responses and oxidative stress in the progression of DKD [ , ], which are associated with increased proteinuria, glomerular endothelial cell damage, and renal fibrosis, thereby precipitating the onset of DKD [ ].
It has been discovered that exosomes present in the serum of individuals with DKD can induce pyroptosis, an inflammatory form of cell death, and augment oxidative stress within cells. This process is mediated through interaction with the miR-4449/HIC1 pathway, suggesting that these exosomes may significantly contribute to the pathogenesis of DKD by promoting cellular damage and inflammation. Consequently, targeting the miR-4449/HIC1 pathway could represent a potential therapeutic strategy to ameliorate the effects of DKD [ ]. Interestingly, exosomes derived from MSCs have been observed to possess anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidative properties in a variety of diseases [ , ]. Meanwhile, exosomes from adipose-derived stem cells regulate the Nrf2/Keap1 pathway to alleviate inflammation and oxidative stress in DKD by targeting FAM129B, which may provide a potential therapeutic strategy for DKD [ ].

4.

## Exosomal biomarkers for early diagnosis
Exosomes, present in diverse bodily fluids such as blood, urine, and saliva, serve as potential biomarkers due to their cargo of bioactive molecules, including proteins and RNA. These vesicles reflect the physiological and pathological states of their cell of origin, and their alterations in the early stages of disease facilitate early detection. Monitoring exosome levels and composition aids in tracking disease progression and assessing treatment efficacy. Unlike invasive traditional tissue biopsies, exosome collection and detection are noninvasive, thereby reducing patient discomfort and risk while enhancing diagnostic feasibility and acceptance.
4.1.

## Comparison with traditional DKD biomarkers
While serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and albuminuria (e.g., urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, UACR) remain the cornerstone of DKD diagnosis and staging in current clinical practice, they possess inherent limitations [ , ]. These conventional markers primarily reflect functional changes and often become abnormal only after substantial renal damage has occurred, lacking the sensitivity for early-stage detection and the specificity to distinguish DKD progression from other forms of renal injury.
In contrast, exosomal biomarkers derived from urinary or plasma samples offer a paradigm shift by providing a cellular-level snapshot of the pathological processes within the kidney [ ]. As exosomes carry molecular cargo specific to their parental cells, they can detect subclinical cellular activation, stress, and apoptosis long before macroscopic functional decline is evident [ ]. This positions them as promising tools for early detection, improved risk stratification (identifying “rapid progressors”), and yielding mechanistic insights into ongoing renal damage.

4.2.

## Current exosome-derived biomarkers in DKD
Non-coding RNAs within EVs, specifically miRNAs, are emerging as novel biomarkers for the diagnosis and prediction of DKD progression. Studies have demonstrated that the urinary extracellular vesicle (uEV) levels of miR-133b, miR-342, and miR-30a are significantly elevated in individuals with type 2 DKD when compared to healthy controls. These biomarkers exhibit altered expression levels in patients with microalbuminuria, macroalbuminuria, and even normoalbuminuria, indicating early changes preceding albuminuria onset [ ] ( ). Studies have also identified significant upregulation of miR-21-5p and downregulation of miR-30b-5p in uEVs of type 2 DKD patients, associated with renal insufficiency and not specific to DKD alone [ ]. Further analysis has revealed a more than 2-fold upregulation of 14 miRNAs in DKD patients compared to healthy controls and diabetic patients without nephropathy, with miR-4534 and miR-516b-5p correlating with microalbuminuria levels [ ]. In the quest for biomarkers indicative of disease progression, research assessed a panel of uEV miRNAs previously related to DKD in type 2 diabetic patients. The study found that the progression of DKD is also marked by specific increases in miR-145 and miR-126 in uEVs from diabetic patients with albuminuria [ ]. Additionally, the increased expression of miR-192 in uEVs may hold potential utility in the early-stage diagnosis of DKD [ ]. A uEVs miRNA signature, including increased levels of let-7i-5p, miR-24-3p, and miR-27b-3p, and decreased levels of miR-15b-5p, has been identified to diagnose microalbuminuria, while miR-30a-5p levels are specifically modified in macroalbuminuria, suggesting a link to severe kidney damage [ ]. Prospective studies have identified serum exosomal miRNA profiles that differ between healthy volunteers and DKD patients, with several miRNAs being uniquely up-regulated in DKD patients and associated with the degree of albuminuria [ ].
 Additionally, mRNAs encapsulated within EVs are increasingly recognized as potential biomarkers for DKD. Notably, elevated levels of Wilms’ tumor 1 (WT1) mRNA and decreased levels of angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) mRNA in circulating EVs have been correlated with the early diagnosis of DKD, exhibiting significant associations with urinary microalbumin-to-creatinine ratios [ ]. Furthermore, uEV-derived CCL21 mRNA has been identified as a potential early biomarker for DKD, which may be associated with T cell infiltration and the chronic inflammatory processes characteristic of the disease [ ]. In the context of early-stage DKD, a downregulation of CDH2 and MCP-1 mRNAs in blood EVs has been observed, suggesting a potential protective role for these mRNAs and their utility as diagnostic markers [ ]. Additionally, increased levels of AEBP1 in DKD-derived EVs have demonstrated positive correlations with serum creatinine, 24-h urine protein, and serum cystatin-C, while negatively correlating with eGFR and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, underscoring its potential as a DKD biomarker [ ]. In a comparative study of type 2 DKD and type 2 DM patients, differential expression of numerous mRNAs, lncRNAs, circRNAs, and miRNAs in uEVs was detected, with a subset demonstrating potential as diagnostic biomarkers for type 2 DKD, as validated by machine learning (ML) algorithms [ ]. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of EV-encapsulated mRNAs to serve as noninvasive diagnostic tools for DKD.
Furthermore, proteins encapsulated within EVs have emerged as potential biomarkers for DKD. A study by Li et al. identified 47 phosphoproteins in uEVs that were significantly altered in patients with DKD, with phosphorylated aquaporin-2 at serine 256 (p-AQP2[S256]) and phosphorylated glycogen synthase kinase 3 beta at tyrosine 216 (p-GSK3β[Y216]) being validated as potential diagnostic markers through Phos-tag western blotting and immunohistochemical staining [ ]. Furthermore, alpha-1 antitrypsin (α1-AT) derived from uEVs has been proposed as a noninvasive biomarker for the early diagnosis of DKD and a predictor of renal function decline [ ]. The actin cytoskeleton is crucial for maintaining podocyte functions, and research has begun to explore whether urinary exosomal proteins related to its regulation are altered in DKD. It has been revealed that urinary exosomal proteins associated with the actin cytoskeleton, such as p21 protein-activated kinase 6 (PAK6) and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), are upregulated and correlate with renal function markers, suggesting their potential as noninvasive biomarkers for DKD diagnosis [ ]. Recent research advancements underscore the potential of EVs as diagnostic biomarkers for DKD, offering a novel noninvasive strategy for disease monitoring and progression prediction.

4.3.

## Diagnostic bottlenecks in exosome-based DKD detection
4.3.1.

## Standardization and heterogeneity challenges
The absence of standardized protocols for exosome collection, processing, and characterization has led to significant variability in exosomal purity, yield, and functional activity across different laboratories or production batches, ultimately compromising the reliability and comparability of exosome-based diagnostics. While mainstream isolation methods such as ultracentrifugation enable isolation of exosomes with higher purity, the procedure risks compromising exosomal membrane integrity due to shear forces during processing, thereby undermining the reliability of downstream biomarker detection. Furthermore, conventional isolation methods face inherent limitations in distinguishing exosomes from co-isolated contaminants (such as lipoproteins and cellular debris) that share overlapping physical characteristics with exosomes in biological samples [ ].
Exosomes are sensitive to temperature, pH, and freeze-thaw cycles. Repeated freeze-thaw cycles may cause exosome rupture or leakage of contents, thereby affecting the integrity of biomarkers. However, currently there is a lack of standardized formulations for storage buffers and criteria for storage duration, which compromises experimental reproducibility.
The current gold standards for exosome characterization—electron microscopy, NTA-based particle size analysis, and Western blot—primarily evaluate physical properties while lacking standardized metrics for functional activity assessments, such as targeted delivery efficiency and immunomodulatory capacity. Furthermore, significant discrepancies exist in the data normalization methodologies (e.g., reference miRNA selection) adopted across different studies, substantially hindering cross-study data integration.
Exosomes exhibit remarkable complexity and heterogeneity, with their composition and functionality being profoundly influenced by factors including cellular origin, physiological status, and pathological conditions. For instance, exosomes derived from distinct cellular sources (e.g., TECs, podocytes, and immune cells) demonstrate pronounced heterogeneity in their cargo profiles [ ]. The protein and miRNA signatures they carry may reflect diverse pathological processes (such as fibrosis, inflammation, or metabolic dysregulation), thereby complicating biomarker validation.
Even though they originate from the same source, different exosomal subpopulations nevertheless demonstrate functional heterogeneity. However, compositional variability within the subpopulations of exosomes from the same source remains inadequately investigated. This heterogeneity can be categorized based on physical parameters and molecular composition. Size is a critical determinant of exosomal content—a 150-nm vesicle possesses 25 times the surface area and 125 times the volume of a 30-nm vesicle, potentially enabling it to carry substantially more proteins and other cargo. Indeed, exosomes of different sizes isolated from the same source have been shown to display distinct biological functions. Furthermore, the cellular microenvironment may contribute to heterogeneity in the composition of exosomes, influencing their protein, lipid, and nucleic acid profiles. This inherent biological variability significantly amplifies the challenges associated with systematic screening and definitive characterization of exosome-derived biomarkers.

4.3.2.

## Limited sensitivity and specificity
The quantification of low-abundance biomarkers in urinary exosomes poses significant technical challenges, as conventional detection platforms such as enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and polymerase chain reaction often lack sufficient sensitivity to reliably capture subtle molecular fluctuations. This analytical limitation directly compromises diagnostic accuracy. Moreover, the inherent heterogeneity of exosomal subpopulations—originating from diverse renal cell types (e.g., podocytes, TECs and MCs)—results in overlapping biomarker profiles across distinct exosomal populations. Different exosomal subpopulations may carry overlapping biomarker profiles, thus increasing the risk of false-positive interpretations in DKD diagnosis. Furthermore, the nonspecific expression patterns of many candidate biomarkers across multiple disease states substantially undermine the pathognomonic specificity required for definitive DKD identification.
Current exosome research relying on single-omics approaches demonstrates significant limitations in the diagnosis of DKD. Given that the pathological mechanisms of DKD involve complex interactions across multiple pathways—such as inflammation, fibrosis, and metabolic dysregulation—focusing solely on one molecular type, be it miRNAs, proteins, or metabolites, fails to capture the comprehensive dynamics of the disease. Consequently, biomarkers identified through these limited approaches often lack sufficient specificity and robustness, hindering their ability to reliably distinguish DKD from other chronic kidney diseases or to meet clinical demands for differential diagnosis.

4.3.3.

## Technical barriers and clinical translation
As research on exosomes progresses, their potential applications are increasingly revealed. The reproducible isolation and enrichment of exosomes are crucial for elucidating their biological functions. However, the inherent heterogeneity of exosomes in size, composition, function, and cellular origin poses significant challenges to their separation. Moreover, most current isolation techniques fail to completely separate exosomes from lipoproteins with similar biophysical properties or from EVs derived from non-endosomal pathways, resulting in preparations of limited purity. Thus, the development of methods for highly efficient exosome enrichment remains a significant challenge and a critical prerequisite for reliable downstream analyses. Depending on the specific research objectives and applications, different isolation strategies may be employed. Commonly used methods include ultracentrifugation, density gradient centrifugation, polymer-based precipitation, size-exclusion chromatography, ultrafiltration, immunocapture, and microfluidic techniques ( ).
 Urinary exosomes have emerged as a highly promising source of diagnostic biomarkers for DKD, owing to their noninvasive accessibility, ease of collection, and suitability for repeated dynamic monitoring. However, their translation into clinical diagnostics faces several substantial limitations. Currently, there is a lack of globally standardized and stringent protocols for urine sample collection timing, storage conditions, use of preservatives, as well as for the subsequent isolation, quantification, and characterization of exosomes and the extraction of nucleic acids or proteins. Furthermore, significant variations in exosome yield and cargo composition may exist not only between individuals but also within the same individual, governed by factors such as fluid intake, sampling time, diet, physical activity, medication use, and health status [ ]. Research on urinary exosomes for DKD diagnosis remains largely confined to the preclinical stage, with a critical shortage of large-scale clinical trials to validate their clinical feasibility and utility. Existing studies are further limited by relatively small sample sizes and a predominance of single-center designs, underscoring the necessity for multicenter validation studies to confirm the generalizability of the findings.

4.4.

## Technological breakthroughs and emerging solutions
Urine collection and storage protocols present notable challenges, as many biobanks store urine specimens without pre-processing to remove cellular debris, which may necessitate the establishment of uEV-specific biobanking protocols or re-collection efforts. In any urinary sample, urinary proteins represent the most prevalent co-isolated contaminants in uEV preparations [ ]. Urinary protein abundance spans five orders of magnitude, with Tamm-Horsfall protein (THP), albumin, and approximately 20 other serum-derived filtration proteins constituting the highest-abundance species. Notably, THP not only co-isolates with uEVs, thereby compromising their purity, but also polymerizes into lattice-like networks capable of entrapping uEVs, ultimately diminishing uEV yield.
The International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) has released the third edition of its guidelines (MISEV2023) [ ], which advances the standardization of uEV detection. The guidelines recommend utilizing cell-free urine and cell-free urine biobanks for uEV research. Where applicable, methodologies and outcomes pertaining to the removal of uEV co-isolates/contaminants (e.g., THP, albumin, and other serum-filtered proteins) should be explicitly documented. To facilitate standardization, researchers are advised to collect both uEV-related data and non-EV urinary parameters (such as creatinine, prostate-specific antigen, or other clinically relevant markers) to estimate absolute or relative excretion rates. Given that optimal storage conditions may vary depending on EV composition and source, comprehensive reporting should include: cryopreservation protocols (e.g., flash-freezing in liquid nitrogen vs. controlled-rate freezing), suspension buffer composition (including cryoprotectants and additives), storage temperature, pre-analytical storage duration, thawing parameters (rate and temperature), and the number of freeze-thaw cycles. Particular emphasis is placed on minimizing freeze-thaw cycles through prudent aliquot strategies, as samples subjected to differing numbers of cryogenic cycles may not be directly comparable.
The integration of multi-omics approaches—combining proteomics, miRNA sequencing, and metabolomics—in exosome research is revolutionizing our understanding of DKD. Moving beyond single-omics paradigms, this strategy enables the construction of a comprehensive molecular network that captures the complex pathophysiological mechanisms underlying DKD. For instance, Liu et al. employed integrated serum exosomal proteomics and metabolomics to explore potential biomarkers for premature ovarian insufficiency, ultimately identifying optimal diagnostic markers through Random Forest ML combined with ROC curve analysis [ ]. Furthermore, a recent study utilizing ML approaches identified two exosomal biomarkers, CMAS and RGS10, associated with DKD [ ].
The high-dimensional, complex data generated from exosomal multi-omics studies provide an ideal application scenario for artificial intelligence and ML algorithms. Supervised ML models—such as Random Forest, Support Vector Machines (SVM), and LASSO regression—are particularly adept at mining these datasets to identify subtle patterns undetectable by conventional statistical methods and to select the most informative biomarker panels. For example, in a study on nasopharyngeal carcinoma, the combined use of LASSO, SVM-RFE, and Random Forest algorithms distilled hundreds of exosome-related genes into a five-gene diagnostic signature achieving a remarkable AUC of 0.990 [ ]. Similarly, researchers employed an integrative ML approach combining LASSO regression, SVM-RFE, and random forest algorithms to analyze exosome-related genes in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma. This study successfully identified 10 key exosome-related differentially expressed genes and constructed a clinical prediction model that demonstrated outstanding diagnostic accuracy (AUC = 0.983) [ ]. Applying similar artificial intelligence (AI)-driven pipelines to DKD exosome datasets could rapidly identify minimal yet highly effective biomarker combinations, offering diagnostic and prognostic performance superior to current clinical standards such as the albumin-creatinine ratio.
Given the pronounced heterogeneity inherent in exosomes, the analysis of individual exosomes emerges as a pivotal approach to elucidate their multifaceted roles in various disease contexts. Over the past few years, substantial progress has been achieved in the realm of individual exosome analysis, with notable advancements such as the proximity barcoding assay (PBA) and single-cell RNA sequencing. These advanced methodologies enable high-throughput interrogation of individual exosomes, thereby providing detailed insights into their molecular architecture and functional attributes. Wu et al. developed a highly sensitive and specific analytical method for the characterization of individual exosomes, thereby facilitating the investigation of exosome heterogeneity. Utilizing PBA, the researchers conducted a comprehensive profiling of 38 distinct surface proteins on exosomes isolated from diverse sources, such as serum, seminal plasma, and 16 distinct human cell lines. Their findings revealed that exosomes originating from different tissues display unique protein signatures, which have the potential to serve as tissue-specific biomarkers [ ]. Moreover, Guo et al. utilized PBA to analyze the surface proteome of individual exosomes, thereby discerning two markedly distinct exosome subpopulations characterized by ITGB3 positivity and ITGAM positivity, respectively. These subpopulations manifest disparate biological impacts throughout the progression of colorectal cancer, thus emerging as potential candidates for early diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic interventions [ ]. The advancement of individual exosome analysis technologies has provided novel tools and approaches for the diagnosis and prognosis of DKD. Through the analysis of the molecular composition of individual exosomes, researchers can elucidate early pathological alterations in DKD, track the trajectory of disease progression, and evaluate the efficacy of therapeutic interventions. Future research endeavors should focus on further validating the efficacy of these biomarkers and exploring additional markers and underlying mechanisms to enhance the prognosis of DKD.
Current isolation methodologies for exosomes are constrained by suboptimal processing speeds, low yields, and inadequate purity levels, significantly impeding both fundamental research and clinical implementation. To address these challenges, cutting-edge separation platforms have emerged with demonstrated performance enhancements. Research findings indicate that the ultrafast-isolation system enables high-efficiency exosome detection through its capacity for automated, label-free purification of exosomes from diverse biofluids. By employing negative pressure oscillations and dual-coupled oscillator-driven membrane vibrations, this system achieves exceptional purification efficiency, demonstrating superior performance in processing speed, purity, and yield compared to conventional separation techniques [ ]. Furthermore, integrated approaches combining microfluidic technology with surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) have been successfully applied for isolating plasma-derived exosomes. This methodology facilitates direct high-yield exosome isolation from human plasma while enabling biomarker analysis based on protein signatures, achieving an unprecedented detection sensitivity of 2 exosomes/μL. The entire analytical protocol requires merely 5 h with minimal sample consumption (50 μL plasma per assay) [ ]. Concurrently, Zhao et al. developed an automated centrifugal microfluidic disk system combined with functionalized membranes (Exo-CMDS) for exosome isolation and enrichment. This platform achieves high-quality exosome purification from minute blood samples (<300 μL) within 8 min, attaining optimal concentrations of 5.1 × 109 particles/mL, thereby realizing integrated processing of exosome separation and purification [ ].

5.

## Therapeutic applications and clinical translation
Exosomes are currently under active investigation for the treatment of DKD. They have demonstrated considerable potential both as therapeutic agents and as carriers for drug delivery. In the field of DKD treatment, current research predominantly focuses on the therapeutic prospects offered by exosomes themselves. The therapeutic role of exosomes in DKD will be discussed below, encompassing their application as therapeutic agents and as delivery systems.
5.1.

## Exosomes as a therapeutic agent in DKD
Stem cell therapy, as a novel regenerative approach, has been employed in the treatment of various conditions, including DKD [ ]. MSCs are present in diverse tissues, such as adipose tissue [ ], peripheral blood [ , ], dental pulp [ ], bone marrow [ ], and neonatal tissues, with particular abundance in the placenta [ ] and umbilical cord [ , ]. The therapeutic potential of exosomes, particularly those derived from MSCs, has become a novel frontier in DKD management. Compared to MSCs, the use of MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) for treating kidney diseases is distinguished by benefits such as reduced immunogenicity and tumorigenicity [ ]. MSC-EVs exert protective effects on podocytes and other renal cells through multiple mechanisms, including the inhibition of apoptosis, attenuation of fibrosis, and enhancement of autophagy, thus tackling the therapeutic challenges associated with DKD.
Podocytes, being highly differentiated epithelial cells that line the outer surface of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM), are crucial for maintaining the structural integrity of the GBM [ , ]. Studies have indicated that MSC-Exo protects podocytes and DKD mice from inflammation by modulating the NOD2 pathway [ ] ( ). Additionally, Duan et al. have confirmed that exosomal miRNA-26a-5p from adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADMSCs) inhibits HG-induced apoptosis in murine podocytes by downregulating the TLR4 and NF-κB/VEGFA signaling pathways [ ]. Jin et al. have demonstrated that ADMSC exosomal miRNA-215-5p suppresses the transcription of the ZEB2 gene, thereby attenuating the EMT of podocytes [ ]. Furthermore, Jin et al. have provided additional evidence that ADMSC-derived exosomes can inhibit the Smad1/mTOR signaling pathway through miRNA-486, promoting autophagy and inhibiting apoptosis in podocytes, which subsequently ameliorates the symptoms of DKD [ ].
 Anti-fibrotic effects are also a principal mechanism by which MSC-EVs treat DKD. Grange et al. have demonstrated that EVs from hepatic stellate cells and MSCs are capable of inhibiting and reversing the progression of glomerular and tubulointerstitial fibrosis in a murine DKD model through the downregulation of fibrosis-associated genes [ ]. Building upon these findings, research has indicated that human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UC-MSCs) significantly enhance renal function, curb inflammation and fibrosis, and forestall disease progression in a model of diabetes-induced chronic renal injury [ ]. Interestingly, UC-MSCs have been shown to impede EMT and renal fibrosis by transferring exosomes that target the Hedgehog/SMO signaling pathway [ ]. Nagaishi K. et al. have further elucidated the role of MSC-derived exosomes in suppressing the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, and fibrosis within the renal tubulointerstitium. These exosomes also downregulate the expression of transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) and maintain the expression of tight junction proteins, including ZO-1, thereby inhibiting EMT in TECs [ ]. Additionally, Ji et al. have identified that small EVs from MSCs can restore the equilibrium of the kinase ubiquitin system in DKD, thereby mitigating renal interstitial fibrosis. This restoration is mediated by the delivery of CK1δ/β-TRCP, which facilitates YAP ubiquitination and subsequent degradation in MCs [ ]. Furthermore, it has been established that MSC-derived EVs carrying miR-23a-3p can alleviate renal fibrosis and inflammation by inhibiting the KLF3/STAT3 signaling axis in DKD [ ].
Autophagy, a meticulously regulated cellular process, facilitates the degradation of cytotoxic protein aggregates and impaired organelles via lysosomal pathways, thereby enabling the recycling of mitochondrial energy sources essential for cellular viability [ ]. Impairment of autophagy in renal cells, particularly within podocytes and TECs, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of various renal pathologies, including DKD [ , ]. Jin et al. discovered that exosomes derived from adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs-Exo) can enhance autophagic flux in MPC5 cells and spontaneously diabetic mice by inhibiting the activation of the mTOR signaling pathway, thereby alleviating podocyte injury. Interestingly, their study identified that miR-486 downregulates the expression of Smad1 through direct targeting, and this reduction in Smad1 expression attenuates mTOR activation, culminating in enhanced autophagy and reduced podocyte apoptosis [ ]. Concurrent research has demonstrated that in STZ-induced diabetic rat models, MSC-EVs promote autophagy via the mTOR signaling pathway, thereby potentially decelerating the progression of DKD [ ]. Furthermore, Cai et al. have discovered that MSC-derived exosomal miR-125b induces autophagy and inhibits apoptosis in HG-treated human embryonic kidney epithelial cells by downregulating tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) [ ]. In summary, MSC-EVs represent a novel therapeutic modality for DKD, and ongoing research is intensively investigating their mechanisms of action, highlighting their potential in modulating autophagy and impacting the course of renal diseases.

5.2.

## Exosomes as delivery system in DKD
Like advanced drug delivery systems such as polymeric nanoparticles and liposome-encapsulated drugs, exosomes are increasingly attracting attention in enhancing therapeutic efficacy. With advantages such as compatibility with living tissues, low toxicity, the ability to transfer contents from one cell to another, non-immunogenicity, and specific targeting to various cells, exosomes have emerged as an excellent therapeutic carrier [ ]. Exosomes are capable of loading a variety of drug types, including small-molecule drugs (such as chemotherapeutic agents like doxorubicin and paclitaxel) [ , ], nucleic acids (such as DNA, mRNA, miRNA and siRNA) [ ], and proteins [ ]. Two predominant strategies are employed for the encapsulation of cargo within exosomes: endogenous and exogenous loading. The endogenous approach entails the introduction of target molecules into donor cells via genetic engineering or co-incubation. Following this, EVs are secreted by these donor cells, and the drug-loaded exosomes are subsequently isolated and purified. Despite its potential, this method is frequently impractical for a multitude of cargo types [ ], thereby compelling the adoption of in vitro loading techniques for purified exosomes. The process of loading cargo into exosomes necessitates traversal of the exosomal membrane barrier. Among the prevalent techniques utilized for this purpose are electroporation, co-incubation, sonication, chemical transfection, and the application of repeated freeze-thaw cycles [ ].
Recent advancements in the field of exosome-based drug targeting delivery have demonstrated substantial progress. Exosomes, as natural nanovesicles, exhibit intrinsic targeting properties due to their surface proteins, which facilitate specific interactions with recipient cells. For instance, integrins and adhesion molecules on the exosome surface can mediate selective targeting to particular tissues [ ]. To further enhance the targeting efficacy of exosomes, various engineering strategies have been employed to modify their surface properties. Specifically, the incorporation of targeting peptides has been shown to significantly augment the binding affinity of exosomes to target cells. For example, Tian et al. successfully engineered exosomes to display RGD-4C peptides on their surface, which markedly improved their targeting to ischemic brain tissue and enhanced anti-inflammatory effects, thereby offering a novel therapeutic approach for ischemic stroke [ ]. Similarly, human umbilical endothelial cell-derived exosomes modified with the GE11 peptide have been demonstrated to specifically target lung cancer cells and efficiently deliver vinorelbine, effectively inhibiting the growth and migration of these cancer cells [ ]. Moreover, gene editing techniques have been utilized to express specific targeting proteins in parent cells, which are subsequently incorporated into the exosome membrane. This strategy has been employed by Ji et al. who transfected skeletal muscle satellite cells with an adenovirus carrying the RVG peptide (Ad-RVG-Lamp2b) to produce RVG-miR-23a/27a/26a-Exos. These engineered exosomes have shown significant efficacy in improving renal tubulointerstitial fibrosis in a mouse model of DKD [ ].
Exosomes as next-generation therapeutic agents demonstrate significant potential in disease management, yet their clinical translation remains hindered by multifaceted technical, manufacturing, and regulatory challenges. Recent breakthroughs reveal that human platelet lysate (HPL) effectively addresses critical industrial-scale production limitations compared to conventional fetal bovine serum (FBS). The experimental data demonstrated that HPL-derived exosomes exhibited superior production consistency, with a 42% increase in extracellular vesicle yield and a concurrent 37% reduction in parental cell apoptosis compared to FBS-based protocols. Proteomic profiling revealed significant molecular advantages: HPL-EVs contained 2.3-fold higher levels of pro-angiogenic VEGF and 1.8-fold elevated Angiopoietin-1 expression relative to FBS counterparts, coupled with a 61% decrease in inflammatory IL-6 concentrations. Crucially, batch-to-batch analysis showed 89% compositional similarity across different HPL donor sources, markedly surpassing the 67% consistency observed in FBS-derived preparations [ ]. Despite these advancements, critical challenges persist in standardization of isolation protocols, optimization of targeting specificity, and longitudinal safety validation. Future progress will likely depend on engineered exosome platforms, AI-powered analytical systems for multi-omics data integration, and multicenter clinical trials to establish robust therapeutic paradigms.

5.3.

## Challenges and future perspectives of exosome-based therapeutics for DKD
5.3.1.

## Production and preparation challenges
The scalable production of exosomes is hampered by major technical bottlenecks, including inherent limitations in isolation and purification technologies, as well as a significant technology gap in large-scale manufacturing. Current technical frameworks struggle to simultaneously achieve both “quality” (high purity and structural integrity) and “quantity” (high yield and batch-to-batch consistency), which severely impedes the transition to clinical-grade applications. Existing methods for exosome isolation and purification all exhibit notable drawbacks. Ultracentrifugation, regarded as the “gold standard,” is characterized by prolonged processing times. The associated shear forces can compromise exosomal membrane integrity and induce aggregation, while its poor resolution in distinguishing contaminants of similar size (e.g., lipoproteins, protein aggregates) significantly affects downstream functional analyses [ ]. Polymer-based precipitation, though simple to perform, introduces substantial coprecipitated impurities (including the polymers themselves and serum proteins), resulting in low purity that critically undermines the reliability of functional studies. Immunoaffinity capture offers high specificity but is constrained by its dependence on costly antibodies, low capture efficiency, and poor scalability. Furthermore, antibody binding may alter the function of surface proteins on exosomes [ ]. Moreover, laboratory-scale techniques such as size-exclusion chromatography and ultrafiltration are inadequate for meeting clinical-grade requirements. The primary challenges include: (1) extremely low yield per processing run and high production costs; (2) difficulties in maintaining batch-to-batch consistency and exosome integrity during scale-up; and (3) the absence of efficient, closed, and Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant continuous production processes [ ]. In response, recent advances driven by multidisciplinary integration are yielding technological innovations. Microfluidic chip technology, particularly when integrated with immunoaffinity capture, acoustofluidics, and field-flow fractionation, enables high-throughput, high-purity, and low-damage exosome sorting [ ]. In addition, 3D bioreactor systems—employing perfusion bioreactors, low-shear mixing systems, and optimized 3D scaffolds (e.g., hydrogels, microcarriers)—are being developed to better mimic the in vivo microenvironment and support scalable exosome production [ , ].

5.3.2.

## Safety risks
Although native exosomes are generally regarded as non-tumorigenic and exhibit low immunogenicity, engineering modifications aimed at enhancing targeting capability or therapeutic function—such as the insertion of targeting peptides/antibodies into the membrane or the loading of therapeutic macromolecules/drugs—may introduce new safety concerns. The use of exogenous targeting ligands or overexpressed membrane proteins could potentially trigger unintended immune responses. Furthermore, if enhanced targeting is not sufficiently specific, it may lead to off-target effects and toxicity in healthy tissues. The potential toxicological implications of high-dose or long-term administration of engineered exosomes, such as accumulation in specific organs, remain insufficiently investigated.
Additionally, whether nucleic acids carried by exosomes—particularly long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and mRNAs—could be delivered into the nucleus of recipient cells and induce long-term effects, such as genomic integration or epigenetic modifications, warrants more rigorous long-term safety evaluation. Finally, exosomes derived from embryonic stem cells or induced pluripotent stem cells necessitate clear ethical review and regulatory frameworks to guide their clinical application.

5.3.3.

## Barriers to clinical translation
In recent years, exosomes have been increasingly investigated in clinical trials, with two primary types being utilized: those derived from human cells or samples, and those originating from plant sources. A search on ClinicalTrials.gov (as of August 2025) revealed that the total number of registered clinical studies related to exosomes has reached 257. Among these, 18 studies focusing on exosome-based therapies have been completed. A Phase I clinical trial has demonstrated that nebulized inhalation of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs is safe and effective in patients with pulmonary fibrosis [ ]. Additionally, a Phase I/II clinical trial confirmed that intranasal administration of ahaMSCs-Exos was safe and well tolerated, and a dose of at least 4 × 108 particles could be selected for further clinical trials [ ]. Notably, a phase II/III clinical trial conducted by Cairo University, Egypt, evaluated the use of mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD). The results demonstrated significant improvements in the treatment group in terms of eGFR, serum creatinine, urea, and UACR. Furthermore, plasma levels of TGF-β1 and IL-10 were markedly elevated, while TNF-α levels were significantly reduced. These outcomes indicate that cell-free umbilical cord blood mesenchymal stem cell-derived EVs are safe, ameliorate inflammatory and immune responses, and enhance overall renal function in patients with stage III–IV CKD [ ].
Despite promising outcomes in preclinical studies, achieving substantial clinical success requires considerable refinement in exosome-based therapeutics. A major obstacle is the lack of standardization across the entire workflow—from source and quality control to functional application—which is compounded by variability in exosome origin and integrity, disjointed quality assessment systems, challenges in in vivo delivery, and unresolved safety and ethical concerns. Addressing these issues demands the establishment of a closed-loop, end-to-end quality control framework spanning from bioproduction to clinical implementation.

6.

## Conclusions
This review has synthesized the multifaceted roles of exosomes in DKD, highlighting their significant potential as diagnostic biomarkers, therapeutic agents and delivery systems. However, the clinical translation of this potential faces significant challenges that require rigorous resolution. From a technological standpoint, the field urgently requires the standardization of isolation and characterization protocols. Current methods yield preparations of inconsistent purity and are plagued by co-isolated contaminants, which confound biomarker identification and functional studies. For diagnostic application, beyond technological refinements, large-scale, multi-center prospective clinical trials are imperative to validate the sensitivity, specificity, and clinical utility of proposed exosomal biomarkers against established standards. The inherent biological variability in exosome release due to factors like diet, exercise, and concomitant medications must also be quantified and accounted for in these studies. Furthermore, current research on exosomal biomarkers for DKD is almost exclusively based on comparisons with healthy controls and diabetic patients without kidney injury, whereas direct evidence for their utility in differential diagnosis against other CKD etiologies remains markedly scarce. This significant evidence gap constitutes a major translational obstacle. Regarding therapeutic development, the path is even more complex. Significant obstacles include: (1) the development of scalable GMP-compliant production systems to ensure batch-to-batch consistency and sufficient yield; (2) the engineering of exosomes to achieve precise targeting to renal cells while minimizing off-target accumulation; and (3) the comprehensive evaluation of long-term safety, immunogenicity, and pharmacokinetics through robust preclinical models. Finally, regulatory pathways for exosome-based diagnostics and therapies are still evolving. Defining critical quality attributes (CQAs) and obtaining approval from agencies like the FDA and EMA will require close collaboration between researchers, clinicians, and industry partners. The concerted effort to overcome these barriers is not merely an incremental improvement but a prerequisite for a paradigm shift from the current “one-size-fits-all” model to a future of precision nephrology. In this paradigm, exosomes are poised to be pivotal enablers. Their cell-specific molecular cargo—encompassing miRNAs, proteins, and metabolites—provides a high-resolution window into the pathological state of the kidney. However, the high-dimensionality of this exosomal “multi-omics” data necessitates the integration of AI and ML to unlock its full potential. AI algorithms can decipher complex patterns within exosomal data to move beyond simple diagnosis toward: (1) deconvoluting DKD heterogeneity into distinct molecular endotypes for precise patient stratification; (2) predicting individual trajectories of disease progression; and (3) forecasting response to therapies, thereby guiding personalized treatment decisions. Thus, the ultimate goal transcends developing exosome-based tests or drugs; it is to leverage exosomes as the core of an AI-powered framework for true precision medicine in DKD. Despite these formidable challenges, the field is advancing. Progress in single-vesicle analysis, microfluidic isolation technologies, and bioengineering offers tangible solutions to current limitations. The future clinical application of exosomes in DKD is therefore contingent not on mere optimism, but on a concerted, collaborative effort to overcome these defined technical, clinical, and regulatory barriers. Success in this endeavor would represent a major breakthrough in managing this debilitating disease.
