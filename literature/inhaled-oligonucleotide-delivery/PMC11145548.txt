However, the mechanisms underlying this association are not fully understood. In this study, we investigated the role of thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) in obesity-induced asthma. Asthma was induced by intranasal injection of a protease from Aspergillus oryzae in normal diet (ND)- or high fat diet (HFD)-fed mice to investigate the symptoms. We measured TXNIP expression in the lungs of patients with asthma and in ND or HFD asthmatic mice. To explore the role of TXNIP in asthma pathogenesis, we induced asthma in the same manner in alveolar type 2 cell-specific TXNIP deficient (TXNIPCre) mice. In addition, the expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines were compared based on TXNIP gene expression in A549 cells stimulated with recombinant human tumor necrosis factor alpha. Compared to ND-fed mice, HFD-fed mice had elevated levels of free fatty acids and adipokines, resulting in high reactive oxygen species levels and more severe asthma symptoms. TXNIP expression was increased in both, asthmatic patients and HFD asthmatic mice. However, in experiments using TXNIPCre mice, despite being TXNIP deficient, TXNIPCre mice exhibited exacerbated asthma symptoms. Consistent with this, in vitro studies showed highest expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in TXNIP-silenced cells. Overall, our findings suggest that increased TXNIP levels in obesity-induced asthma is compensatory to protect against inflammatory responses.

## Introduction
Obesity is a significant risk factor for asthma development in both children and adults [ ]. Increased fat content can activate innate immune responses, leading to systemic and airway inflammation, thereby exacerbating asthma symptoms [ , ]. Obesity results in elevated levels of free fatty acids (FFAs) in the plasma, promoting reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and thus impacting lung function in asthmatic subjects [ ]. The adipose tissue also generates numerous adipokines that can influence the onset of allergic airway diseases [ ]. Airway epithelial cells express receptors for adipokines, such as leptin and adiponectin [ ], and leptin resistance has been reported to cause bronchoconstriction and asthma [ ]. Several studies have suggested that weight loss interventions may be beneficial in the treatment of obesity-related asthma [ , , ]. Obesity contributes to the pathogenesis of asthma, but the underlying mechanism of this association is not yet understood.
Thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP), also known as vitamin D3 upregulated protein-1 (VDUP-1), serves as a regulator of oxidative stress and is associated with inflammation [ ]. The function of TXNIP is complex and can induce both beneficial and detrimental effects under various conditions. TXNIP plays an essential role in maintaining metabolism by activating inflammatory signals in response to stimuli, such as ROS [ , ]. In addition, several studies have shown that TXNIP plays protective and anti-inflammatory roles [ , ].
Owing to this dual function of TXNIP, it is increasingly being studied for its role in oxidative damage and inflammation-mediated diseases such as asthma. During the formation of allergen-specific memory Th2 cells, that induce allergic airway inflammation, TXNIP has been identified as a key molecule in regulating ROS metabolism [ ]. Additionally, increased levels of TXNIP have been observed in the lungs of ovalbumin (OVA)-induced asthmatic mice [ ].
Based on existing research, we identified the cellular activities that can induce asthma in mice fed a high fat diet (HFD) and confirmed the activity of TXNIP. Additionally, we used alveolar type 2 cell-specific TXNIP deficient (TXNIPCre) mice to verify the role of TXNIP in asthma induction.

## Materials and methods
2.1

## Human lung samples
Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded lung tissues from a healthy control and an asthmatic patient were obtained from commercial vendors (BioIVT, Westbury, NY, USA). Samples were collected from a 31-year-old healthy female and a 35-year-old asthmatic female. Detailed information on the human lung samples is provided in .

2.2

## Experimental animal model
2.2.1

## A mouse model of HFD-induced obesity
Female mice (6-weeks-old, C57BL/6) were purchased from ORIENT BIO (Seongnam, Republic of Korea), and all the experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the regulations of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chungnam National University (202310A-CNU-171). The animals had access to filtered, ultraviolet light-irradiated municipal tap water ad libitum. They were divided into two groups: one fed a normal diet (ND; ORIENT BIO) and the other fed a HFD (ORIENT BIO). Mice were checked once a week for weight changes based on their diet. Based on previous reports showing that obesity progresses in mice after 16 wks of HFD feeding [ ], we sacrificed five mice from each group at 13, 20, 26, 39, and 52 wks of age for analysis.

2.2.2

## Asthma model in mice fed with HFD
An asthma-induced mouse model was established in compliance with the regulations of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chungnam National University (202310A-CNU-171). Each group consisted of 26-week-old mice fed either ND or HFD, with seven mice per group. Asthma was induced by intranasal injection of a protease from Aspergillus oryzae (Asp; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), fungi closely associated with allergic diseases in humans [ , ], mixed with OVA (Sigma-Aldrich). When inhaled with OVA, the active protease stabilizes Th2 effector commitment to OVA and perpetuates allergic lung inflammation to induce allergic immunity [ ]. Each mouse was injected with 50 μl of a mixture consisting of 1 mg/mL Asp and 0.5 mg/mL OVA diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a ratio of 1:9 on days 0, 4, 8, 12, and 16. The mice were sacrificed within 24 h of the last challenge.

2.2.3

## Asthma model in TXNIPCre mice
TXNIPCre mice were generated by crossing TXNIPfl/fl mice with SFTPCCre transgenic mice (B6, 129S-Sftpctm1(cre/ERT2)Blh/J, #028054; Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME, USA). To confirm genotypes, all mice used in the study were genotyped by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for Cre gene using primers provided by the Jackson Laboratory according to standard genotyping protocols. Genomic DNA was extracted from tail tissue using a gDNA extraction kit (Samjung Bioscience, Daejeon, Republic of Korea) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The TXNIPfl/fl genotyping protocol included two primer reactions: forward, 5’−CTT CAC CCC CCT AGA GTG AT−3’; and reverse, 5’ –CCC AGA GCA CTT TCT TGG AC−3’. The TXNIPCre genotyping protocol used three primer reactions: forward, 5′–TGC TTC ACA GGG TCG GTA G−3’; reverse, 5’−ACA CCG GCC TTA TTC CAA G−3’; and wild type, 5’−CAT TAC CTG GGG TAG GAC CA−3’. Mice with the Cre+/+ genotype were treated with tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich) to activate the Cre gene. Tamoxifen was added to corn oil (Sigma-Aldrich) and shaken at 50 until completely dissolved to a final concentration of 20 mg/mL. Tamoxifen solution was injected intraperitoneally at a volume of 100 μl per mouse daily for 5 days. After 4 wks of tamoxifen treatment, both Cre−/− and Cre+/+ mice, with seven mice in each group, were induced with asthma using a mixture of Asp and OVA at a ratio of 1:9, as described above. The mice were sacrificed within 24 h of the final administration.

2.3

## Airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR)
AHR of each mouse was evaluated 24 h after the last intranasal injection using the FlexiVent FX2 (SCIREQ Inc., Montreal, Canada). Aerosolized PBS or methacholine (Sigma-Aldrich) at concentrations of 10, 20, and 40 mg/mL was administered via nebulization. Readings were obtained using a plethysmograph and averaged. These were expressed in units of enhanced pauses (Penh), as described previously, and is a valid indicator of changes in breath timing due to bronchoconstriction [ ].

2.4

## Analysis of Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid (BALF)
BALF is used to study the cellular influx of inflammatory cells (e.g., lymphocytes, macrophages, eosinophils, neutrophils, etc.) in animal models of inflammatory respiratory disease [ ]. As previously described [ ], the process of obtaining BALF involved the infusion of ice-cold PBS (700 μl) into the lungs of mice via a tracheal cannula, followed by withdrawal. This procedure was repeated twice. The collected BALF samples were centrifuged, the supernatant was utilized for biochemical analysis, and the cell pellet was resuspended in 500 μl of ice-cold PBS. Subsequently, 200 μl of the resuspended solution was attached to a slide using cytospin (Hanil Scientific Inc., Seoul, Republic of Korea) and stained with Diff-Quik® staining reagent (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan) according to the manufacturer's protocol for differential cell counting.
The remaining 200 μl of the solution was utilized for measuring intracellular ROS levels using the cell permeant reagent 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFDA; Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA). BALF cells were stained with DCFDA dye, attached to slides using cytospin, and observed at 488 nm under a confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).
The remaining solution was subjected to leukocyte population analysis by flow cytometry. After washing with the staining buffer (BD Biosciences, CA, USA), the cells were stained with anti-PerCP-CD45, anti-PE-CD11c, anti-APC-Siglec F, and anti-FITC-Ly6G (BD Biosciences, 1:100 dilution) on ice in the dark for 30 min. The cells were then washed twice with staining buffer and analyzed using the BD Accrui™ C6 Plus Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences).

2.5

## Cytokines assay
The levels of several cytokines in BALF, including interleukin (IL)-4, IL-5, IL-13, and IL-17A, were measured using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Raybiotech, Peach Tree Corners, GA, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol. Serum levels of total immunoglobulin (Ig)E were determined using an ELISA kit (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). The concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α in the serum were also quantified using ELISA kits (Raybiotech). The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an ELISA reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

2.6

## Biochemical analysis
The concentrations of glucose (GLU), triglyceride (TG), and total cholesterol (TCHO) in the serum were measured using the FUJI DRI CHEM 7000i instrument (FUJIFILM, Tokyo, Japan) and colorimetric slides. To determine the serum levels of FFAs (C-8 (octanoate) and longer fatty acids), serum samples were incubated with acyl-CoA enzyme mixture (Abcam, ab65341; sensitivity 2 μM) [ ], and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm with an ELISA reader (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Leptin and adiponectin concentrations were determined using ELISA kits (Crystal Chem, Elk Grove Village, IL, USA).

2.7

## Histological analysis
Sections of 4 μm thickness were prepared from paraffin-embedded tissues. Harris’ hematoxylin/eosin (H&E; TissuePro Technology, Gainesville, FL, USA) and periodic acid-Shiff (Sigma-Aldrich) were carried out to identify pathological features of Asp-induced allergic lung inflammation and mucus production in lung tissue. Liver tissues were also subjected to H&E to observe histological changes in response to HFD intake.
To evaluate the expression of TXNIP in lung alveolar type 2 cells, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence staining as described previously [ ], using anti-TXNIP (Abcam, 1:500 dilution) as the primary antibody. ImageJ software (Oracle Corporation, Austin, Texas, USA) was used for quantitative image analysis.

2.8

## Immunoblotting
To obtain total protein from the lung tissue, we homogenized the tissue using RIPA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with a protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich). Protein concentration was measured using the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and equal amounts (30 μg) of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE. Western blotting was performed as described by Kim et al., 2023 [ ]. The primary antibodies used were as follows: TXNIP (Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO, USA, 1:1000 dilution), NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3; Bioss Inc., Woburn, MA, USA, 1:1000 dilution), apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a caspase-recruitment domain (ASC; Novus Biologicals, 1:1000 dilution), Caspase 1 (full length) (Novus Biologicals, 1:1000 dilution), Cleaved Caspase 1 (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA, 1:1000 dilution), Pro IL-1β (Novus Biologicals, 1:1000 dilution), and Cleaved IL-1β (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000 dilution).

2.9

## In vitro experiments
2.9.1

## Cell culture and analysis of mRNA expression levels
The human lung epithelial cell line (A549 cells), was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA). To establish a stable cell line overexpressing TXNIP, A549 cells were transfected with control or TXNIP-expressing constructs (Ubigene, Guangzhou, Guangdong, China). Cells were cultured in F–12K medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 10 % inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; Corning, NY, USA) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 with 5 % CO2.
A549 control and TXNIP-overexpressing cells were seeded at a density of 1 105 cells/mL in a 6-well plate and cultured overnight. For TXNIP knockdown in A549 control cells, TXNIP siRNA was mixed with lipofectamine® RNAiMAX reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in Opti-MEM® medium, and the cells were treated according to the manufacturer's protocol. After 6 h of incubation at 37 with 5 % CO2, the medium was replaced with F–12K medium, and the cells were further incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed twice with PBS and starved for 4 h in F–12K medium without FBS. Cells were treated with recombinant human TNF-α protein (R&D systems, Minneapolis, USA) at a concentration of 1 ng/mL and incubated for 24 h. Total RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis were performed using a kit (Samjung Bioscience) and the mRNA expression levels of cytokines were analyzed by quantitative reverse transcription PCR.

2.9.2

## Cellular ROS assay in A549 cells
A549 control and TXNIP-overexpressing cells were seeded onto 8-well cell culture slides at a density of 2 104 cells/mL and incubated overnight. Following TXNIP gene silencing in A549 control cells and recombinant human TNF-α treatment, intracellular ROS levels were observed in each cell. As described above, the cells were stained with DCFDA dye (Abcam), fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, and mounted with DAPI solution. The cells were observed using a confocal microscope (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) at 405 nm (DAPI) and 488 nm (DCFDA).

2.10

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as mean standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, version 9 (GraphPad Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). For statistical comparisons between two groups, two-tailed t-test was used for normally distributed and equal variances, Welch's correction was used for normally distributed and unequal variances, and Mann-Whitney test was used for non-parametric tests. For comparisons of three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance with Tukey's post-hoc test was used for normally distributed data and Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normally distributed data. A p-value 0.05 and 0.01 was considered as statistically significant. Detailed information is provided in the figure legends.

## Results
3.1

## HFD mice display exacerbated asthma symptoms
We first induced asthma in ND and HFD mice using Asp and then evaluated their symptoms. At methacholine concentrations of 20 and 40 mg/mL, the Penh values of HFD mice were significantly higher than ND mice ( A). Stained BALF cell images showed that eosinophils counts were significantly higher in HFD mice than in ND mice ( B). This trend was also observed in flow cytometry analysis, with a signigicant increase in CD45+LY6G+ cells (neutrophils) and CD45+LY6G−CD11c−SiglecF+ cells (eosinophils) in HFD mice, while CD45+LY6G−CD11c+SiglecF+ cells (alveolar macrophages) were higher in ND mice ( C and D). 
Histological examination revealed infiltration of inflammatory cells into the peribronchial and perivascular regions of HFD mice compared to ND mice, along with increased mucin hyperproduction ( E). Furthermore, HFD mice exhibited higher levels of total IgE in the serum, consistent with elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines IL-4, 5, 13, and 17A, which are indicative of asthma-related inflammation ( F).

3.2

## Consumption of HFD leads to increased levels of FFAs, adipokines, and ROS in mice
ND mice gained weight until about 12 wks of feeding and then reached a plateau, while HFD mice were observed to continue to gain weight until about 28 wks of feeding ( A). We also examined the pathological changes in liver tissue after HFD exposure using H&E staining and found that the livers of 26 wks HFD-fed mice showed the most numerous small lipid droplets, degeneration of hepatocytes, and inflammation ( ). The serum concentration of GLU, TG, and TCHO were measured according to the duration of HFD consumption, and not significantly different from mice fed a ND in all periods ( ). We confirmed levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the serum of HFD-fed mice and found that they all increased with duration of HFD exposure, with the greatest increases observed between 20 and 26 weeks ( ). 
We compared the factors influencing asthma pathogenesis in HFD mice by examining their levels following different durations of HFD exposure. Serum FFA levels were highest at 13 weeks and remained relatively consistent at 20, 26, and 39 weeks ( B). The ratio of leptin to adiponectin, adipokines secreted by adipose tissue, increased with increasing duration of HFD consumption ( C). Intracellular ROS levels in BALF cells increased with extended exposure to HFD and remained similar at 26 and 39 weeks ( D).
Upon comparing these factors in ND and HFD mice challenged with Asp, we observed that FFA levels, leptin/adiponectin ratio, and ROS levels were higher in HFD mice than in ND mice ( ).

3.3

## TXNIP expression is upregulated upon the development of asthma symptoms
Elevated TXNIP has been observed in obese patients as well as in various animal models fed a HFD for various functional purposes [ , , , ]. To elucidate the pathogenesis of asthma in obesity, we screened potential proteins that functionally and physically interact with TXNIP ( ). Based on this analysis, IHC staining revealed a significant increase in TXNIP and NLRP3 protein expression in the asthmatic patient sample compared to healthy control ( A and ). This suggested that TXNIP and NLRP3 were involved in the pathogenesis of asthma. Similar to human samples, higher TXNIP and NLRP3 expression was observed in alveolar type 2 cells of HFD mice compared to ND mice ( B and ). Immunofluorescence staining of TXNIP results also revealed increased fluorescence signal in HFD asthmatic mice than ND asthmatic mice ( C). 
We also confirmed the expression of TXNIP and NLRP3 inflammasome-related proteins in the lung tissues of ND and HFD mice during the development of asthma by western blotting ( D and E). Increased TXNIP protein expression was observed in HFD asthmatic mice compared to ND asthmatic mice. In addition, activation of NLRP3 inflammasome was shown to be significantly higher in HFD mice than in ND mice, as evidenced by increased expression of NLRP3, ASC, Cleaved Caspase 1 and IL-1β proteins.
When comparing the differences between the expression of TXNIP and NLRP3 inflammasome-associated proteins over the duration of HFD exposure, TXNIP was highest at 26 weeks, and then decreased gradually, while NLRP3 and Cleaved IL-1β showed similar high expression at 39 and 26 weeks, respectively, and then decreased gradually ( ).

3.4

## TXNIPCre mice exhibit heightened asthma symptoms
To investigate the role of TXNIP in the pathogenesis of asthma, we genotyped mice born through house breeding ( ), then challenged Asp to mice with Cre−/− and Cre+/+ genes, respectively, to induce asthma and compared symptoms in the presence and absence of the Cre gene. The AHR measurements showed that inhalation of methacholine in TXNIPCre mice resulted in higher dose-dependent increase in Penh values than that in TXNIPfl/fl mice ( A). We also identified different types of inflammatory cells in BALF and found that the number of eosinophils was significantly higher in TXNIPCre mice than in TXNIPfl/fl mice ( B), which was similar to the results of the flow cytometry analysis ( C and D). Compared to TXNIPfl/fl mice, TXNIPCre mice showed increased inflammatory cell infiltration around the bronchial and goblet cells ( E). Total IgE in the serum and cytokine levels in BALF were determined by ELISA, with significantly higher concentrations of total IgE, IL-4, 13, and 17A in TXNIPCre mice than in TXNIPfl/fl mice ( F). 
IHC staining confirmed that TXNIP protein expression was reduced in TXNIPCre mice, specifically in lung alveolar type 2 cells ( G). As determined by western blotting, TXNIP expression levels were reduced in the lung tissues of TXNIPCre mice compared to those in TXNIPfl/fl mice. However, the expression levels of NLRP3 inflammasome-related proteins were increased ( H). These results suggest that TXNIP is a factor responsible for the protective effect against increased NLRP3 inflammasome and that TXNIPCre mice, that are deficient in TXNIP expression, exhibit more severe asthma symptoms.

3.5

## TXNIP expression results in decreased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and ROS in A549 cells
To confirm the effects of TXNIP in inflammatory conditions, we established a stable TXNIP-overexpressing A549 cell line. The mRNA expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α were highest in TXNIP silenced cells, and their levels were lowest in TXNIP-overexpressing cells ( A–C). In addition, to evaluate the effect of TXNIP expression on ROS levels, one of the cellular activities that can trigger asthma, we treated A549 cells with recombinant TNF-α protein, which can induce ROS formation [ ]. ROS were detected in cells as indicated by distinct green fluorescence in the cytoplasm. An increase in fluorescence intensity was observed around the nucleus in cells transfected with TXNIP siRNA, which was reduced in TNXIP-overexpressing cells ( D). This observation supported the results from TXNIPCre mice.

## Discussion
The prevalence of obesity has increased in recent years resulting in increased incidence of asthma [ ]. The association between obesity and asthma has been confirmed in several clinical studies [ , ], but the underlying mechanisms by which obesity triggers asthma-related inflammation remain unclear. In the present study, consistent with clinical studies, we observed increased asthma symptoms in HFD mice, including inflammatory cells and cytokines in BALF, IgE production in the serum, inflammation, mucus production, and AHR. Additionally, regarding the mechanisms involved in the development of asthma-related inflammation, we found increased expression of TXNIP and NLRP3 inflammasome in HFD mice compared to ND mice. Whereas, these NLRP3-mediated inflammatory signaling was increased in asthma-induced TXNIPCre mice compared to TXNIPfl/fl mice, which was supported by results of in vitro experiments showing increased inflammation when TXNIP was silenced.
In line with previous studies that investigated the association between obesity and asthma, we also observed that HFD mice (26 wks) developed more severe asthma symptoms than ND mice when asthma was induced using Asp. When we examined the mice based on the duration of HFD exposure, the highest plasma FFA levels were observed at 13 wks, after which they decreased and remained constant. Plasma FFA levels are typically elevated in obesity due to increased release from expanded adipose tissue [ ]. As fat increases, secreted insulin increases, which promotes the esterification of FFA and storage as TG, but the amount of FFA released into the circulation increases even more, resulting in a gradual decrease in the rate of FFA clearance [ , ]. It was also observed that prolonged exposure to HFD resulted in a proportional increase of not only leptin, depending on adipose tissue expansion, but also ROS production in lung tissues. According to Han et al., 2012 [ ], excessive fat intake causes FFA oxidation, which in turn increases ROS accumulation, leading to systemic inflammation. Numerous studies have shown that the oxidative stress caused by ROS plays a major role in the progression of asthma [ , , ]. Based on previous studies [ , ] and our results, HFD-induced obesity exacerbates asthma progression owing to increased FFA oxidation followed by increased ROS production.
To identify the mechanism related to this, we found that TXNIP expression was low in the healthy lung tissue, whereas its expression was significantly increased in the lung tissue from the patient with asthma. Consistent with this enhanced expression in the asthmatic patient sample, TXNIP was also upregulated in HFD asthmatic mice compared to ND asthmatic mice. TXNIP plays a key role in the development of several inflammatory diseases. Previous research has implicated TXNIP/VDUP-1 in lung development and asthma pathogenesis by the regulation of ROS levels and inflammation [ , ]. In particular, it interacts with NLRP3 inflammasome, which is important for the production of mature IL-1β, and is associated with several inflammatory diseases [ , ]. In the present study, elevated expression of TXNIP coincided with increased ROS levels, as well as higher expression levels of NLRP3 inflammasome proteins in HFD asthmatic mice. To clarify these observations, we used TXNIPCre mice to assess the role of TXNIP in asthma pathogenesis. Our data showed that the absence of TXNIP in the lung tissue exacerbated asthma symptoms followed by increased expression of NLRP3 inflammasome. Moreover, these results were confirmed through in vitro experiments. Silencing of TXNIP resulted in high expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which were significantly decreased in TXNIP-overexpressing cells. These results are in contrast with the findings of previous studies that TXNIP is involved in increased inflammation in OVA-induced asthma mouse model [ , ] and that the interaction between TXNIP and NLRP3 is increased under oxidative stress conditions, leading to an increased inflammatory response [ ]. This discordance may be due to the diverse functions of TXNIP in physiological and pathological processes, or compensatory upregulation as asthma advances. Previous studies have shown that TXNIP plays protective and anti-inflammatory roles and can be upregulated by compensatory mechanisms in alcohol-induced liver damage and in patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, where it facilitates autophagy and cell proliferation [ ]. Overall, TXNIPCre mice and in vitro experiments results suggested that increased TXNIP expression in asthmatic patients and asthma-induced mice may protect against the persistence of uncontrolled inflammation that occurs during asthma progression.
Our data showed that NLRP3 expression increased with elevated TXNIP levels in HFD asthmatic mice. Moreover, NLRP3 was expressed at higher levels in asthma-induced TXNIPCre mice. The specific mechanisms underlying the association between TXNIP and NLRP3 remain unclear. The NLRP3 inflammasome is constitutively activated by various agonists, among which ROS can also affect inflammasome activation [ ]. Previous studies have shown that TXNIP acts as a redox-sensitive ligand of NLRP3 for inflammasome activation [ ], while also exerting protective and anti-inflammatory effects by binding to NLRP3 and inhibiting its expression [ ]. In addition, it has been reported that TXNIP is independent of the signaling cascade that leads to NLRP3 activation [ , ]. Further studies using NLRP3 gene knockout in vivo or in vitro are required to elucidate the mechanisms of action of TXNIP and NLRP3 in asthma pathogenesis.
In conclusion, our results suggest that obesity exacerbates asthma progression due to increased ROS and adipokine levels. The absence of TXNIP worsens asthma due to decrease in the ability to suppress NLRP3-mediated inflammatory responses. This study provides a new perspective on the role of TXNIP in the pathogenesis of asthma in obesity.
