## Abstract
Exposure to airborne fine particulate matter (PM2.5) has been linked to increased risk of the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection, yet the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Here, by leveraging a fine-tuned foundation model of single-cell transcriptomics, we uncover shared transcriptional signatures between PM2.5 exposure and SARS-CoV-2 infection. We further validate this association using population-level epidemiological analyses and perform genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to identify genetic variants that modulate infection risk under PM2.5 exposure. In addition, we identify NPC1 as a key modulator involved in SARS-CoV-2 infection efficiency under virus-laden PM2.5 exposure through integrative functional genomic analyses and in vitro experiments. Our findings suggest that PM2.5 facilitates viral entry through an NPC1-modulated endo-lysosomal pathway, providing a mechanistic explanation for observed pollution-related susceptibility. By integrating artificial intelligence (AI)-guided transcriptomics, epidemiology, GWAS, functional genomics, and in vitro verification, our study elucidates how environmental and genetic factors jointly influence SARS-CoV-2 susceptibility. This work highlights how AI-assisted multi-omics integration systematically decodes the health impacts of environmental exposures from molecular to population levels and informs air quality policy and infectious disease preparedness.
Subject terms: Viral infection, Environmental sciences, Epidemiology, Genetics research, Risk factors

Using artificial intelligence, authors model how air pollution may increase susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2. NPC1 is identified via genetic and laboratory analyses as a key pathway that enhances viral entry after exposure to fine particulate matter.

## Introduction
In the past few years, the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) imposed a profound impact on human health , such as high fatality rates , , “long-COVID” symptoms , , and mental health problems , . Despite global interventions and extensive research efforts, the health burden posed by SARS-CoV-2 infection still persists, driven by factors such as recurrent infections , the emergence of novel viral variants , and other unresolved biological determinants , . Moreover, extensive studies suggested that environmental factors could interfere with SARS-CoV-2 infection, such as air pollution, temperature, and humidity , . However, the underlying mechanism is still elusive. Therefore, investigating the interaction between environmental factors and viral infections is crucial for preventing future pandemics.
Fine particulate matter, also named PM2.5, is particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter less than 2.5 μm, imposing a severe health burden to global public health . Ambient PM2.5 is a complex mixture of substances—including hydrocarbons, salts, dust, and microorganisms—originating from natural sources (e.g., wildfires, dust storms), human activities (e.g., fuel combustion, traffic, residential energy use), and secondary formation through atmospheric chemical reactions , . According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Interim Targets 1–4 (S-IT1–S-IT4) and the Air Quality Guideline (S-AQG), unhealthy PM2.5 exposure affected 96% of the world’s populated regions for at least one day, 82% for at least 1 week, and 53% for at least one full month in 2022 . Air pollution is now a leading contributor to premature mortality and disability, with a global disease burden that surpasses other major health risk factors, such as hypertension and smoking . The associations between PM2.5 exposure and a wide range of health outcomes have been extensively studied, including mortality , hospital admission , cardiovascular disease , neurological disorders , and respiratory syndromes . Moreover, recent studies have explored additional aspects of PM2.5’s impact on COVID-19-related outcomes, including its associations with COVID-19 hospitalization and mortality rates – . PM2.5 can function as a carrier of viruses and protect their viability and infectiousness , and may even facilitate viral infection and replication . However, few studies have revealed the molecular mechanism of the interaction between PM2.5 exposure and viral infection.
Recently, artificial intelligence (AI), particularly deep learning models leveraging attention mechanisms, has accelerated scientific discovery in the life sciences. Bioinformatics data of various modalities have been used for model training, such as proteins and nucleotides . Given the large data size and cell-level resolution, a variety of AI models employing distinct encoding methods have been trained on single-cell transcriptomic data. The knowledge embedded in pre-trained networks enables models to adapt to diverse downstream tasks, including cell clustering and annotation , gene and pathway prediction , and the discovery of novel cell types . Therefore, AI models utilizing single-cell transcriptomics have the potential to elucidate associations between PM2.5 exposure and SARS-CoV-2 infection at the level of gene expression network dynamics.
In this study, we aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which PM2.5 exposure may enhance host susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection. To achieve this, we developed an AI-driven framework integrating single-cell transcriptomics, genome-wide association analysis, functional genomics analyses, and in vitro validation. This multidisciplinary approach identified the transcriptomic signatures and genetic variants underlying environmentally modulated viral susceptibility, highlighting key gene–environment interactions relevant to COVID-19.

## Results

## Single-cell transcriptomic AI model on PM2.5 exposure classification
Previous studies have sought to elucidate the health effect of PM2.5 exposure; however, they have largely converged on well-established mechanisms such as inflammation and oxidative stress , which may stem from the omission of complex non-linear gene network interactions. To address this, we employed a rank-encoded single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) transformer architecture (Geneformer) to model gene network characteristics under PM2.5 exposure (Fig.  ). Single-cell transcriptomic profiles of mouse lung tissue under PM2.5 exposure and control conditions were collected (45,340 cells), providing a representative distribution for model training (Fig.  ). Subsequently, we fine-tuned the pre-trained Geneformer model (i.e., the fine-tuned model) by exposure-label classification (Fig.  ) with different data curation and layer freezing strategies (Supplementary Fig.  , described in “Methods”), which resulted in robust model performance. Moreover, to evaluate the impact of pretraining, we also trained a series of BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) models from scratch, each with an identical configuration but differing in model depth (the number of encoder layers, i.e., BERT-1L, BERT-2L, BERT-4L, BERT-6L). We found that as the training data size increased, the BERT models achieved performance comparable to that of the fine-tuned model, and all transformer-based models outperformed alternative machine learning approaches, including random forest and support vector machine (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). However, our results demonstrated that the fine-tuned model outperformed the BERT models when trained on a limited data size (Fig.  ). Together, these findings highlight the value of transfer learning in capturing complex gene network dynamics under environmental exposure.
 Next, we implemented two approaches to evaluate model generalization. First, we evaluated whether models trained exclusively on single-cell transcriptomic data could effectively classify bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) samples. We curated 71 bulk RNA-seq samples from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), comprising both PM2.5 exposure and control groups (Fig.  ). We predicted exposure status on the bulk RNA-seq data and observed that the performance of BERT models improved with increasing model depth; however, it remained inferior to the fine-tuned model (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Notably, performance was slightly lower for samples derived from non-lung tissues, suggesting potential tissue-context awareness. Second, we partitioned our scRNA-seq dataset into multiple subsets based on cell type annotations, such that each test set contained a single cell type while the remaining cell types were used for training. Compared to BERT models, fine-tuned models demonstrated robust performance on cell types that were absent from the training data (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). In summary, our results revealed that pre-trained weights facilitate downstream adaptation, enabling a comprehensive understanding of the gene expression networks under PM2.5 exposure. Moreover, our results also implied that PM2.5 exposure might induce similar molecular responses across cell types and organizational levels.

## Associations between PM2.5 and COVID-19 revealed by pathway-level characteristics and epidemiological analyses
To identify critical genes relevant to PM2.5 exposure, we conducted in silico perturbation using our transformer-based models. We averaged the outputs from the final transformer block to generate cell embeddings, which were subsequently visualized using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). We found that the fine-tuned model exhibited a clear decision boundary (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ), implying improved generalization enabled by pre-trained weights.
 Building on this, we conducted in silico gene deletion in the input sequences and compared the embedding shifts of perturbed cells to PM2.5 exposure and control clusters (Fig.  ). A substantial shift in exposure clusters following gene deletion suggests that the gene may contribute to cellular responses distinguishing PM2.5 exposure from the control condition. Genes exhibiting embedding shifts with statistical significance were selected for pathway enrichment analyses (Fig.  ). Enrichment analyses revealed that genes associated with PM2.5 exposure were enriched in COVID-19 and ribosome function pathways (Fig.  ). Additional enriched terms suggested mechanisms related to immune responses and viral genome translation following infection (Fig.  ). Specifically, genes enriched in COVID-19 pathway were associated with cellular stress (e.g., FOS, JUN), inflammation (e.g., NFKBIA, CASP1), and antiviral immune response (e.g., TLR7, ISG15, CXCL10), consistent with previous reports . Moreover, vesicle-related pathways (such as phagosome, lysosome) were also enriched, implying a mediating role (Fig.  ). We also performed in silico overexpression and consistently found enriched pathways related to COVID-19 (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). In addition, perturbation analyses identified substantial overlapping PM2.5-related genes and enriched pathways across different cell type groups (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Overall, our model not only captured well-established molecular alterations of PM2.5 exposure, but also pointed to a potential connection between PM2.5 and SARS-CoV-2 infection through shared pathway-level characteristics.
To validate these insights, we performed epidemiological analysis to investigate the association between PM2.5 exposure and SARS-CoV-2 infection in the UK Biobank participants, adjusting for multiple covariates (Supplementary Table  ). SARS-CoV-2 infection was defined by SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid antibody assay. We estimated individual PM2.5 exposure based on ambient PM2.5 level, residential address, and assay date. Significant positive correlations with SARS-CoV-2 infection were observed at both annual and monthly exposure levels, and findings remained consistent across sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table  ). To account for potential lagged effects , we further analyzed PM2.5 exposure across different time lags and found consistent positive associations (Fig.  ). Subgroup analyses also demonstrated consistent associations across population strata (Supplementary Fig.  ). These findings align with previous studies , , supporting an association between PM2.5 exposure and SARS-CoV-2 infection risk.

## Identification of genomic loci that modulate the association between PM2.5 exposure and SARS-CoV-2 infection
Despite the availability of large-scale datasets and advanced long-range modeling approaches, single-cell transcriptomic data remain inherently constrained to pinpoint core regulatory genes. This limitation arises from the complex cascade of downstream gene expression changes induced by PM2.5 exposure, which obscures the identification of upstream regulators contributing to increased susceptibility of SARS-CoV-2 infection. To elucidate the genetic mechanisms underlying the observed association between PM2.5 exposure and SARS-CoV-2 infection, we performed genome-wide association studies (GWAS) using data from UK Biobank participants. This analysis identified significant single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and potential effector genes (Fig.  ).
 First, we analyzed genetic variants associated with positive COVID-19 test results and identified significant variants at 3p21.31 with the lead SNP rs73062389, which is near SLC6A20 and LZTFL1 (Fig.  ). Consistently, previous GWAS have identified SLC6A20 and LZTFL1 as effector genes associated with COVID-19 – .
We next performed an additional GWAS to investigate gene–environment interactions between SNPs and PM2.5 exposure. This analysis identified marginally significant SNPs located within KCNJ3 on Chromosome 2 (Supplementary Fig.  ). However, existing evidence does not support an association between KCNJ3 and either SARS-CoV-2 infection or PM2.5 exposure, and its expression in the lung is minimal (Supplementary Fig.  ). To enhance statistical power to detect gene-level associations, we applied set-based association analysis using GCTA-fastBAT . We identified a cluster of SNPs significantly enriched at chromosome 18q11.2 (chr18:23,470,036–23,589,830, GRCh38; p = 1.5 × 10−6), exceeding the Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold (0.05/23582). These SNPs are located near NPC1 and RMC1 (C18orf8), suggesting potential regulatory roles for these genes (Fig.  ). Specifically, a significant positive gene–environment interaction was observed per risk allele (relative to the reference allele), such that the joint presence of higher PM2.5 exposure and risk alleles was associated with increased susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection (Supplementary Table  ).

## Identification of candidate causal variants and tissue-specific regulatory mechanism in lung
Causal SNPs with biological regulatory functions may not always reach genome-wide significance due to linkage disequilibrium. To prioritize potential causal variants, we focused on SNPs in strong linkage disequilibrium (r2 > 0.6) with the lead SNP rs1652376 (Supplementary Fig.  ). According to previous GWAS reports, these SNPs were associated with obesity-related traits , lipid metabolism , socioeconomic status , and cognition , but not COVID-19 (Supplementary Fig.  ). Although some variants are located within coding regions, they do not alter amino acid sequences. Furthermore, none are located within untranslated regions (UTRs) or conserved microRNA binding sites , nor are they predicted to affect mRNA splicing by SpliceAI (Supplementary Table  ). To investigate the regulatory potential of these SNPs, we examined DNase I hypersensitivity profiles across 95 different cell types from the Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE) dataset to identify open chromatin regions. Four variants (rs891387, rs1652376, rs2960578, and rs1788783) were located within open chromatin peaks, exhibiting cell type specificity (fibroblasts, epithelial cells) as well as tissue specificity (lung tissue) relevant to COVID-19 (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Among these, rs1788783 exhibited the highest relative signal intensity in lung-related cell lines. Histone H3 lysine 4 mono-methylation (H3K4me1) and chromosome conformation capture (3C) data further implied a potential cis-regulatory mechanism for these candidate variants (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ).
 Expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) analysis using the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) portal revealed that risk alleles of four candidate variants were associated with increased expression of NPC1 and RMC1 in lung (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Lung is the primary target organ of both PM2.5 exposure and SARS-CoV-2 infection. Bulk RNA-seq data from GTEx revealed that both RMC1 and NPC1 are highly expressed in the lung (Fig.  ) compared to other organs and tissues (Supplementary Fig.  ). Moreover, scRNA-seq data indicated that NPC1 and RMC1 are expressed across multiple lung cell types, particularly alveolar type 2 (AT2) cells, which play a key role in SARS-CoV-2 pathogenesis (Fig.  and Supplementary Figs.  and ). To assess potential causal effects of NPC1 and RMC1, we conducted a Mendelian randomization analysis based on summary statistics of GWAS and eQTL (Fig.  ). The results suggested that higher expression levels of NPC1 and RMC1 were associated with increased susceptibility to SARS-CoV-2 infection (Supplementary Table  ).
To further explore functional roles, we conducted in silico gene deletions of NPC1 and RMC1 in the fine-tuned model and examined the resulting gene network perturbations. We found that single-gene perturbation of NPC1 or RMC1 identified enrichment of pathways related to endocytosis and autophagy, consistent with their biological functions, whereas the COVID-19 pathway showed lower enrichment significance (Fig.  ). In contrast, co-deletion of NPC1 and RMC1 increased the enrichment significance of the COVID-19 pathway, suggesting a potential synergistic role of these two genes in SARS-CoV-2 infection (Fig.  ). Moreover, previous studies have linked NPC1, RMC1 to viral infection mechanisms. NPC1 plays a crucial role in intracellular cholesterol transport , and mediates viral genome release into the cytoplasm – . RMC1 is a scaffolding subunit of the MON1-CCZ1 complex, which serves as the guanine nucleotide exchange factor for RAB7A , . Notably, CCZ1 and RAB7A have been identified as regulators in viral infection and trafficking – . Collectively, these findings suggested a potential role of NPC1 and RMC1 in modulating SARS-CoV-2 infection in the context of PM2.5 exposure.

## Regulatory role of rs1788783 and NPC1 in SARS-CoV-2 infection under virus-laden PM2.5 exposure
Through the aforementioned in silico analyses, we identified candidate causal variants along with the potential effector genes NPC1 and RMC1. To validate the inferred molecular mechanism linking genomic variants, effector genes, and phenotype, we performed in vitro experiments involving genome editing and virus exposure assays. We employed CRISPR knockout (CRISPR–KO) in A549 alveolar basal epithelial adenocarcinoma cells to delete genomic regions encompassing the candidate variants (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Using western blotting, we found that among the four knockout sites, rs1788783 led to a marked reduction in NPC1 protein expression (Fig.  , c). In contrast, RMC1 protein levels did not show a clear difference between groups.
 Previous studies have shown that viruses maintain infectivity when loaded onto PM2.5 particles, entering host cells via receptor-independent endocytosis . As a key mediator of endo-lysosomal trafficking, NPC1 plays a critical role in post-endocytic viral proliferation process , , , . Therefore, we designed exposure experiments under solo viruses (the free virus control) and virus-laden PM2.5 (PM2.5-borne viruses) and evaluated viral infection by assessing the relative expression levels of open reading frame 1 (ORF1) and nucleocapsid (N) genes. First, we confirmed that exposure to virus-laden PM2.5 enhanced viral infection compared with solo viruses exposure at 24 h post infection (hpi) (Fig.  ). Moreover, compared with wild-type A549 cells, rs1788783 knockout cells exhibited attenuated viral infection under both exposure conditions. Using the pseudovirus carrying green fluorescent protein (GFP) and luciferase reporters, we further examined infection kinetics over time (Fig.  ). For both solo viruses and virus-laden PM2.5 exposure, no significant differences in viral infection were observed between wild-type and knockout cells at 12 and 24 hpi. However, wild-type cells exhibited markedly stronger luminescence intensity under virus-laden PM2.5 exposure at 48 hpi, compared to solo viruses. In contrast, knockout cells showed significantly reduced viral infection under both exposure conditions. These findings suggest a role for NPC1-modulated endo-lysosomal pathways in regulating late-stage viral entry kinetics .
To further validate the role of NPC1 in SARS-CoV-2 infection directly, we performed NPC1 knockdown using small interfering RNA (siRNA). Three siRNA molecules led to varying degrees of NPC1 protein knockdown (Fig.  ), and corresponding reductions in viral infection were observed. Notably, lower NPC1 expression levels were associated with reduced viral infection (Fig.  ).
To rule out the contribution of angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2)-mediated viral entry, we used DX600, a selective ACE2 inhibitor, to block ACE2 enzymatic activity. As expected, ACE2 inhibition reduced viral infection under both SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus and pseudovirus-laden PM2.5 exposures. However, viral infection remained consistently higher in the pseudovirus-laden PM2.5 group compared with pseudovirus alone (Fig.  , Supplementary Fig.  ), supporting an ACE2-independent entry mechanism. We further validated these findings across multiple cell lines, where varying degrees of NPC1 knockdown were associated with dose-dependent reductions in viral infection (Fig.  and Supplementary Fig.  ). Overall, these experiments provided evidence consistent with a regulatory role of NPC1 and its associated genomic variants in modulating SARS-CoV-2 infection under virus-laden PM2.5 exposure, offering mechanistic support linking omics findings with population-level associations.
 

## Discussion
Mounting epidemiological evidence has revealed a positive association between ambient particulate matter exposure and viral infection – , especially SARS-CoV-2 in recent years , . However, our understanding of the molecular interplay between PM2.5 exposure and SARS-CoV-2 infection remains limited. Previous studies have demonstrated that PM2.5 impairs the respiratory system , induces elevated immune responses , and reduces resistance to viral infection , . These findings are consistent with our results from the PM2.5 exposure classification model, which reveal that PM2.5 exposure and SARS-CoV-2 infection share similar pathway characteristics at the single-cell level, particularly those related to immune activation, viral response, and vesicle trafficking. This suggests that PM2.5 may induce molecular alterations that either mimic or potentiate key pathways exploited during viral infection, therefore increase host susceptibility to viral infection. Supporting this notion, previous studies have shown that PM2.5 exposure can directly upregulate ACE2 expression, the key receptor mediating SARS-CoV-2 entry, thereby providing mechanistic evidence for its role in enhancing viral susceptibility . Furthermore, researchers have explored the direct interplay between PM2.5 and viruses, reporting prolonged viral viability , , increased viral loading , , and wider multi-organ distribution , (Fig.  ). Building upon these findings, we further interrogated this phenomenon and identified the molecular mechanism by which rs1788783 and NPC1 promote SARS-CoV-2 infection in the context of PM2.5 exposure. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents one of the first attempts to validate the molecular mechanisms by which PM2.5 affects SARS-CoV-2 infection using an AI-driven, multidisciplinary approach (Fig.  ). Furthermore, our results underscore the need for air quality regulation, highlighting potential strategies for pandemic prevention.
 Previous GWAS have identified genetic variants associated with COVID-19 severity and susceptibility – . However, few have implicated loci in proximity to or functionally linked with NPC1. Our study highlights a synergistic interaction between NPC1 and PM2.5 exposure, potentially reflecting a specific viral entry mechanism. SARS-CoV-2 can enter host cells through two main pathways: (1) cell surface fusion, which relies on ACE2 and transmembrane serine protease 2 (TMPRSS2), and (2) endo-lysosomal entry via endocytosis and cathepsin-mediated activation . While the former is typically more efficient , our findings suggest that PM2.5 exposure enhances the less efficient endosomal entry route by promoting ACE2-independent viral uptake. Notably, NPC1 has been shown to facilitate viral entry for multiple viruses – , , including SARS-CoV-2 . Taken together, our results demonstrate a gene–environment interaction between PM2.5 exposure and NPC1: PM2.5 particles act as carriers for virus entry through receptor-independent endocytosis , , , , and deliver virions into the endo-lysosomal system , while NPC1 promotes their release into the cytoplasm , , (Fig.  ). Notably, this interaction mechanism may be specific to viruses that utilize the endo-lysosomal entry pathway, such as SARS-CoV, Ebola virus, and seasonal cold coronaviruses , , , while other viruses do not appear to be involved . However, whether NPC1 directly interacts with virus-laden PM2.5 or mediates its effects through indirect regulatory mechanisms remains to be experimentally validated.
In addition, extensive studies have suggested the role of cholesterol homeostasis in viral infection , , which is regulated coordinately by NPC1 and RMC1 , . However, some studies indicate that cholesterol is not a critical factor in NPC1-mediated viral entry . Furthermore, previous studies have implied an alternative interaction mechanism between PM2.5 and RMC1/RAB7A: PM2.5 exposure may enhance viral budding by interacting with lipid rafts on the cell membrane , while RAB7 facilitates the egress of β-Coronaviruses through lysosomal trafficking . Notably, both RMC1 and CCZ1, which function as guanine nucleotide exchange factor subunits regulating RAB7A, have been implicated in the modulation of viral infection . Nonetheless, limited knockout efficiency of RMC1 in our CRISPR-KO cell lines hindered direct validation of this hypothesis. Additionally, we identified AT2 cells as potential target cells for PM2.5-borne virus exposure, as they exhibit high expression levels of both NPC1 and RMC1 in the lung (Fig.  ). AT2 cells have also been recognized as a primary target of SARS-CoV-2 infection in the alveoli and are implicated in subsequent lung injury . However, the specific molecular mechanisms discussed above warrant further investigation.
Our study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, although we employed high-resolution PM2.5 exposure data based on current best practices, more advanced space-time modeling of individual PM2.5 inhalation could further enhance environmental epidemiological analyses . Second, the GWAS and gene–environment interaction analyses were conducted primarily in individuals of European ancestry from the UK Biobank, which may limit the generalizability. However, the biological mechanisms we identified—such as NPC1-modulated susceptibility—are likely conserved across populations. Moreover, gene–environment interaction studies face challenges of limited availability of cohorts with both high-quality environmental and genomic data, as well as an increased risk of false positives , which together contribute to the current scarcity of robust findings in this field. Future replication in ancestrally diverse populations will be crucial to validate and extend our conclusions. Finally, although we validated key findings in multiple well-characterized cell lines, future studies involving three-dimensional cell models, organoids, and animal systems will be necessary to confirm physiological relevance and extend mechanistic insights.
Our study underscores a key challenge in applying artificial intelligence to the life sciences: balancing model complexity with limited data availability. While transformer-based frameworks offer high model capacity and fitting power, they are also susceptible to overfitting, computational overhead, and reduced interpretability. However, the growing availability of high-quality single-cell transcriptomic data has enabled pre-trained models to capture biologically meaningful latent representations. Our results indicate that the pretraining–fine-tuning strategy employed by the fine-tuned model offers a significant advantage, as it consistently outperformed BERT models trained from scratch. This approach offers a computationally efficient and biologically interpretable solution, demonstrating how domain-specific pretraining provides strong inductive bias and enhances model generalizability for life science applications.
The application of AI has substantially accelerated research across interdisciplinary domains. Adapting AI models to analyze diverse environmental exposures and health outcomes offers a powerful strategy for uncovering novel associations and identifying potential biomarkers. These AI-driven approaches are poised to reshape research paradigms in epidemiology, environmental science, and toxicology. Collectively, our work demonstrates an “Omics-AI” approach for data-driven discovery. Looking ahead, the evolution of large-scale, multimodal AI models will enable more integrative, mechanistic, and predictive understanding of biomedical complexity, ultimately informing clinical and public health interventions.

## Methods

## Ethics
The collection of samples and data for this study conformed to all relevant ethical regulations. The research followed the ethical guidelines for laboratory animals of China Medical University (approval No. CMU2021230) and the Animal Care and Use Committee of Tianjin University Haihe Hospital (approval No. 2017-kt-04). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines. The study was conducted using the UK Biobank Resource. All UK Biobank participants provided written informed consent. Ethical information (http://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/ethics/) about UK Biobank is available online.

## Animal and PM2.5 exposure
Male C57BL/6J mice (Mus musculus), aged 6–8 weeks, were obtained from Sipeifu Bioscience Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). Animals were maintained on a C57BL/6 genetic background. Mice were housed and managed at the animal facility. Mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) facility under controlled environmental conditions with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, ambient temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity of 50 ± 10%. Airborne PM2.5 was collected by air PM sampler and multi-nozzle cascade impactor (MCI) (Tokyo Dylec Co.). The collected airborne PM2.5 was exposed to mice via the respiratory tract. The duration of ambient PM2.5 exposure was one month to investigate the relatively long-term physiological effects. Subsequently, fresh lung tissue of mice was cleaned and placed in tissue preservation solution. C57BL/6 mice were randomly assigned to the PM2.5 exposure group and the control group to minimize batch effects in the single-cell experiment. More details can be found elsewhere , .

## Single-cell library construction and quality control
Isolated total lung cells were pooled and processed using the Chromium Single Cell 3’ Reagent Kits v3 (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA). The pooled cells were then loaded onto the Chromium Single Cell Controller (10× Genomics) to generate single-cell gel beads in emulsion. The libraries were then pooled and sequenced on the NovaSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using a 150 bp paired-end configuration. Additionally, a subset of single-cell libraries was also prepared using the DNBelab C Series Single-Cell Library Prep Set and sequenced on a BGISEQ500 sequencer. The raw sequencing data were preprocessed and analyzed by the Cell Ranger Single-Cell Software Suite (v. 5.0.1) with default settings. The reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10). Seurat (version 5.1.0) was utilized for downstream analysis. The data underwent additional filtering based on the following criteria: (1) cells expressing fewer than 200 unique genes or more than 5500 unique genes; (2) cells with mitochondrial gene expression exceeded 15%. We applied Harmony to eliminate batch effects.

## Data preprocessing and encoding of single-cell transcriptomic data
Overall, we collected 45,960 cells of the PM2.5 exposure experiments from mouse lung tissue for single-cell RNA-seq. We used loompy, scanpy , anndata, scipy, numpy, and pandas to process the datasets into the.loom files, retaining key metadata in row and column attributes, such as quality control matrices, cell type annotation, and PM2.5 exposure labels. Then, we used the tokenizer from Geneformer to convert single-cell transcriptomic data from annotation data format to Hugging Face Datasets , with features including tokenized gene sequences, cell types, organ labels, and PM2.5 exposure, etc. This encoding method generated token sequences based on the rank of the gene expression level for each cell. Considering the pre-trained model setup and the sequencing depth of the currently used single-cell transcriptomic dataset, we selected 2048 genes with the highest expression levels to generate input sequences. In addition, we padded each cell’s input sequence to ensure consistent input length. This encoding method reduces the high-dimensional, sparse expression matrix to ranked tokens, not only optimizing the space occupancy of transcriptomic data, but also potentially alleviating the batch effect of different samples .

## Model architecture and training
To make full use of context knowledge on gene network dynamics, we fine-tuned the Geneformer pre-trained model for PM2.5 exposure classification. We used the checkpoint with an input size of 2048, 256 embedding dimensions, 4 attention heads, feed forward size of 512, and 6 encoder layers . We used “BertForSequenceClassification” from the Hugging Face Transformers library to fine-tune the pre-trained model. The number of labels for classification is set to 2 for distinguishing exposure label of “PM2.5” and “Control”. To compare performance with fine-tuned models, we trained BERT models from scratch with 1, 2, 4, 6 transformer encoder layers with randomly initiated weights. The activation function was ReLU (Rectified Linear Unit), with an attention dropout and hidden dropout rate of 0.02. The model used absolute positional embeddings with a maximum sequence length of 2048 tokens and a vocabulary size of 25,426. Training was conducted using the Adam optimizer with weight decay fix, a maximum learning rate of 5 × 10⁻⁵, linear learning rate scheduling with 500 warmup steps, and a weight decay of 0.001. A batch size of 26 was used, and all models were trained and evaluated on 4 NVIDIA A100-40G GPUs. Gradient checkpointing was disabled, and training was performed using PyTorch with Transformers version 4.28.0. The full model configuration and training scripts are available via our public GitHub and Hugging Face repositories to ensure full reproducibility.
To comprehensively evaluate the influence of input sequence length and padding on model performance, we trained the fine-tuned models under three data curation strategies: (1) keep all transcriptomic data, (2) keep transcriptomic data that included more than 856 non-zero genes, (3) keep transcriptomic data that included more than 2048 non-zero genes. The choice of 856 as the mild filtering threshold is based on its identification as a turning point in the data distribution. Finally, the three datasets contain 45,960, 36,118, and 24,231 cells, respectively. In addition, since exposure classification is quite different from the pre-trained task, we also fine-tuned models with different numbers of frozen layers (n = 0, 2, 4) to evaluate fine-tuning performance. Overall, we trained 9 groups of models to comprehensively evaluate the performance differences caused by different data curation and frozen layers. We used 5-fold cross-validation and reported the results as the area under the curve (AUC) ± standard deviation.
To test the generalization of our models, we collected bulk RNA-seq raw counts of PM2.5 exposure from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) , and accession numbers were listed as follows: GSE208331 , GSE220252 , GSE143709 , GSE220165, GSE182199 , GSE237795 , GSE155616 , GSE213620 , GSE215411 , GSE206862 , GSE244959 , GSE135262 . In these studies, we focused exclusively on tissues and cells from the PM2.5 exposure and control groups, resulting in a total of 71 included samples. We used these data to evaluate the fine-tuned model and BERT models. Finally, we evaluated model performance by comparing predicted and true labels, and reported the results as F1 score ± standard deviation.

## In silico perturbation
To test the impact of gene deletion or expression on the gene expression network, we performed in silico perturbations using our fine-tuned model , . In this model, each gene token is embedded in a 256-dimensional space and continuously updated during the forward pass of the transformer blocks. These embeddings capture the regulatory context and network interactions of a cell. The resulting cell-level embedding is computed as the average of all gene embeddings from the final hidden layer, effectively summarizing the overall transcriptional state.
Perturbing a specific gene in the input sequence—either by removing it (deletion) or artificially increasing its influence (overexpression via rank promotion)—alters the embeddings of other genes and consequently the overall cell state. By comparing the perturbed and unperturbed cell embeddings using cosine similarity, we can evaluate how much influence a given gene exerts on the cell state. In this study, we applied two types of perturbations: (1) in silico deletion, where a specific gene is removed from the token input sequence, and (2) in silico overexpression, where the gene is moved to the front of the token sequence, mimicking high expression or importance.
For the analysis of the perturbation results, we used two methods. (1) Cell embeddings were grouped based on classification labels (e.g., PM2.5 exposure vs. control), and centroids were computed to represent the average embedding of each group. Perturbation of each gene was performed, and the cosine similarity shift between perturbed and unperturbed embeddings was calculated to assess its influence on cell state alteration; (2) Gene embedding network analysis, in which we investigated how perturbing a target gene altered the embeddings of other genes, which were further interpreted using prior knowledge, such as Gene Ontology (GO) or Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway database to understand affected molecular programs.
To define high-impact genes, we followed an approach validated in the original Geneformer framework. Specifically, we: (1) computed cosine similarity between original and perturbed cell embeddings for each gene; (2) compared the distribution of cosine shifts for each perturbed gene to a null distribution generated from random gene perturbations using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test; (3) applied Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing; (4) designated genes with FDR-adjusted P values < 0.05 as high-impact genes. This procedure allowed us to prioritize genes with significant regulatory influence on cell state, and these high-impact genes were used for downstream interpretation of exposure-related transcriptional mechanisms.

## Study population and covariates
To investigate the association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and PM2.5 exposure, we used data from the UK Biobank and restricted the analysis to participants who agreed to take part in the coronavirus infection study. A total of 60,274 participants were included in the present analysis (37,268 females and 23,006 males; age range 52–86 years). Sex was determined based on self-report as recorded in the UK Biobank dataset. Blood samples were collected using the Thriva COVID-19 antibody test kit and analyzed for SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) antibodies using the Roche Elecsys Anti-SARS-CoV-2 assay. Results were integrated into the UK Biobank database. All UK Biobank participants provided written informed consent. No additional participant compensation was provided for this study, as analyses were conducted using existing UK Biobank data. Covariates were selected from the UK Biobank dataset, including demographic factors (age, sex, ethnicity, body mass index), socioeconomic factors (household income, education, urbanicity), dietary factors (vegetable intake, fruit intake), lifestyle variables (smoking, alcohol intake, physical activity, sleep duration), and vaccination history. Sex was included as a covariate in regression models, and sex-stratified analyses were conducted. Detailed population characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table  .

## Exposure
PM2.5 monitoring data were collected from 105 air quality stations managed by the UK Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (Defra) (https://uk-air.defra.gov.uk). These monitoring data were used to create a 0.01° × 0.01° dataset using the Ordinary Kriging (OK) interpolation method, a widely used approach for estimating air pollutants . The OK method predicts PM2.5 levels at specific locations by leveraging data from nearby monitoring stations and accounts for spatiotemporal correlations . Previous studies have demonstrated that the OK method performs well in areas with dense sampling, achieving accuracy comparable to that of land-use regression models . Ambient temperature was assessed using data from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts’ fifth-generation atmospheric reanalysis (ERA5) dataset (https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/), which has a horizontal resolution of 0.1° × 0.1°, even in remote areas with fewer monitoring sites. The exposure dataset provided comprehensive coverage for the entire UK, integrating observations from multiple sources with a high degree of accuracy. For all exposure variables used in our study, we generated lagged exposures for both annual and monthly levels. Environmental exposures were matched to each participant based on the longitude and latitude of their home address.

## Regression analyses
We adopted a generalized additive model to analyze the associations between PM2.5 exposure and SARS-CoV-2 infection, adjusting for multiple covariates, as described above. To identify potentially vulnerable populations, we performed subgroup analyses based on factors such as sex, age group, body mass index category (categorized as underweight, healthy, overweight, obese using cut-off points of 18.5, 25, and 30), ethnicity, physical activity, smoking, sleep duration, alcohol consumption, household income, and educational attainment. Moreover, to identify critical exposure windows, we investigated the associations between different PM2.5 exposure lags and SARS-CoV-2 infection. Additionally, we performed sensitivity analyses to validate the robustness of our results, applying various covariate adjustments. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1.

## Genome-wide association study
We performed logistic association analyses between genetic variants and SARS-CoV-2 infection. We applied quality control analysis based on the following criteria : information score ≥ 0.4, Minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.001, Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium P value −log10(P) ≤ 7, and inclusion of autosomal and biallelic variants. These inclusion criteria were consistent with previous studies on the same study population . For the GWAS analysis, SARS-CoV-2 infection (categorical variable, 0 or 1) was used as the primary phenotype and dependent variable, while the independent variables included SNP term, genotyping array, age, genetic sex, and 15 principal components to control for population stratification. We applied genomic control to minimize the probability of false-positive results arising from population structure confounding. Our study not only examined the direct effect of SNPs on the phenotype, but also analyzed the interaction between SNP and PM2.5 exposure in relation to the phenotype. The above genome-wide association analyses were implemented using Plink and Plink2 (www.cog-genomics.org/plink/2.0/) . We used R packages such as ggplot2 to visualize GWAS summary results, including Q-Q and Manhattan plots. Moreover, we utilized LocusZoom to visualize association signals, linkage disequilibrium, and nearby genes in local genomic regions.

## Post-GWAS analyses using public data resources
We used a series of datasets to refine SNPs of interest with potential biological functions. We utilized the GWAS Catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/) to find out whether SNPs of interest were reported to be associated with specific phenotypes in other GWAS. We used LDassoc to identify the linkage disequilibrium patterns and SNPs in high linkage disequilibrium with the lead SNP rs1652376 (r2 > 0.6). Furthermore, we applied TargetScan and SpliceAI to determine possible conserved microRNA binding sites and mRNA splicing.
To further investigate the potential biological functions, cell type specificity, and downstream regulated genes, we comprehensively integrated the chromatin accessibility, histone modification, and chromosome conformation capture data by track analyses. We incorporated DNase-seq, Histone ChIP-seq, and micro-C datasets from the ENCODE portal (https://www.encodeproject.org/) . High-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) data of IMR-90 and A549 were collected from https://3dgenome.fsm.northwestern.edu/. Liftover was used to convert the genome coordinates and annotations between different genome assemblies (GRCh38/hg38 and GRCh37/hg19). We applied pyGenomeTracks to visualize tracks from different datasets in specific genomic regions. In the preliminary analysis, we used the UCSC Genome Browser (http://genome.ucsc.edu) for quick visualization.
To study the expression of candidate genes at the organ and cell type levels, we used the GTEx portal (https://gtexportal.org) and multiple single-cell RNA-seq datasets. The multi-tissue gene expression level data were obtained from the GTEx Portal V8. Single-cell RNA-seq data of normal lung tissue and COVID-19 patients were downloaded from GEO (GSE171524). We used scanpy to visualize gene expression among cell types of single-cell transcriptomic data. Moreover, to verify the association between candidate genomic variants and regulated gene expression, we also obtained lung-tissue eQTL data from GTEx (https://gtexportal.org/home/snp).

## Mendelian randomization
To investigate the causal effect of gene expression on phenotype, we performed Mendelian randomization. In the framework of Mendelian randomization analysis, our outcome of interest (Y) is the infection of SARS-CoV-2. The gene expression (X) represents the exposure of interest, while the SNP/genetic variant (Z) acts as the instrumental variable. We use bxy to denote the effect size of gene expression (X) on the outcome of interest (Y). Similarly, bzy represents the effect size of the SNP (Z) on the outcome of interest (Y), and bzx is the effect size of the SNP (Z) on gene expression (X). ## Cell culture and genome editing
To study the potential regulatory mechanism of specific SNPs, A549 wild-type cells and CRISPR-KO cells were obtained from Ubigene Biosciences (China, #YC-C016). Cells were cultured in F12K medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Two guide RNAs (gRNAs) with appropriate Protospacer Adjacent Motifs (PAMs) were designed to target regions of the variants of interest, and plasmid DNA encoding CRISPR-Cas9 and gRNA was delivered into cells by electroporation. The sequences of gRNAs were provided in Supplementary Table  . After antibiotic screening, we used the TIANGEN DNA extraction kit to perform PCR near the gRNA target site (primer sequences provided in Supplementary Table  ). Genome editing was validated by PCR amplification followed by Sanger sequencing.
Primary human bronchial epithelial cells (HBECs) were provided by Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd (China, #CP-H009). Cells were seeded on rat-tail collagen type I-coated dishes and maintained in the complete bronchial epithelial basal medium with growth factors. Human bronchial epithelial BEAS-2B cells (Beyotime, China, #C6106) and African green monkey kidney epithelial Vero E6 cells (Ubigene Biosciences, China, #YC-A003) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA). Human lung adenocarcinoma epithelial Calu-3 cells (Ubigene Biosciences, China, #YC-A054) were maintained in minimum essential medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% nonessential amino acids (Gibco, USA), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Mouse alveolar epithelial MLE-12 cells (SUNNCELL, China, #SNL-414) were cultured in DMEM/nutrient mixture F-12 medium (Corning, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. All cells were maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO2 in humidified incubators.

## Protein extraction and western blot analyses
Cells were collected and lysed by radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor (Roche, Switzerland). After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm (12,500 × g) for 10 min to remove insoluble debris, the total protein contents were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., USA). Equal amounts of proteins (30 μg) were mixed with 5× protein loading buffer and resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). The separated protein was transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA) and blocked with 5% milk in Tris-Buffered Saline (TBS) with 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, including Niemann Pick C1 Monoclonal antibody (ab134113, Abcam, 1:1000), RMC1 Polyclonal antibody (20111-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), and Beta Actin Recombinant antibody (81115-1-RR, Proteintech, 1:5000). After incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (SA00001-2, Proteintech, 1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature, the immunoreactivity was detected with ImmobilonTM Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Millipore, USA) and ChemiDoc XRS+ System (Bio-Rad, USA). Uncropped blot images are provided in the Source Data.

## ACE2 inhibition and receptor-blocking assays
To inhibit the enzymatic activity of ACE2, cells were treated with 10, 20, and 50 μM DX600 (MCE, USA) at 37 °C for 1 h. ACE2 activity of cell lysates was assessed with the Fluorometric ACE2 Activity Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s directions. Fluorescence kinetics were measured by a VarioskanTM Flash multimode reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at an excitation wavelength of 325 nm and an emission wavelength of 393 nm.
To further verify the ACE2-independent infection process of PM2.5-loaded viruses, cells were pre-treated with 50 μM DX600 at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by infection of solo pseudovirus or PM2.5-loaded pseudovirus for 24 h. Pseudovirus infection was evaluated by luciferase assay via the VarioskanTM Flash multimode reader.

## SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus infection
SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus was procured from Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd (China). To prepare SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus-laden PM2.5 exposure, 50 µg/mL PM2.5 was co-incubated with 1 × 105 copies of viruses for 1 h at room temperature. The mixtures were washed three times with cold phosphate buffer saline (PBS) by centrifugation at 2000 rpm (500 × g) for 6 min to remove free pseudovirus. Afterwards, A549 cells and four KO cell lines were incubated with 50 µg/mL pseudovirus-laden PM2.5 at 37 °C for 1 h. After thoroughly washing with PBS, cells were cultured in the complete F12K medium for 12, 24, and 48 h. To test the pseudovirus infection, the luciferase luminescence was detected by a VarioskanTM Flash multimode reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

## RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analyses
After SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus infection, cellular RNAs were extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Total RNAs were quantified by a spectrophotometer (DeNovix, USA) and reverse transcribed into cDNAs using RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed through the SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (Accurate Biology, China) and CFX96 Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, USA). Relative gene expression was normalized to the GAPDH housekeeping gene. The primer sequences for this study are listed in Supplementary Table  .

## RNA interference (RNAi)
The small interfering RNA (siRNA) oligos targeting NPC1 and negative control siRNA (NC-siRNA) were synthesized by Genomeditech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Cells were plated and grown to 80% confluence in 6-well plates, followed by transfection with 100 pmol siRNAs using lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA). NPC1 protein expression levels in the transfected cells were examined by western blot analyses after 48 h. To evaluate the infection of SARS-CoV-2 pseudovirus, transfected cells were further infected with 50 µg/mL pseudovirus-laden PM2.5 or free pseudovirus at 37 °C for 24 h, and analyzed via SYBR Green-based qRT-PCR following the extraction of total cellular RNAs. The NPC1 siRNA sequences for this study are listed in Supplementary Tables  – .

## Fluorescence imaging analyses
Cells were infected with 50 µg/mL pseudovirus-laden PM2.5 or free pseudovirus for 12, 24, and 48 h. Afterwards, cells were extensively washed with PBS and fixed with paraformaldehyde (4%) for 20 min. The fixed cells were stained with 100 nM rhodamine-conjugated phalloidin (Solarbio, China) for 30 min, followed by staining with 100 nM DAPI (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 15 min at room temperature. After thoroughly washing, fluorescent signals were visualized through an LSM900 Airyscan laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

## Statistical analysis
Performance evaluations for selected AI models were conducted using 5-fold cross-validation to ensure the robustness of the results. All experiments were repeated at least three times independently (biological replicates). Statistical analysis was performed using two-sided Student’s t tests.
