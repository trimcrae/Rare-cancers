Microglia, the resident monocyte of the central nervous system, play a crucial role in the response to spinal cord injury. However, the precise mechanism remains unclear. To investigate the molecular mechanisms by which microglia regulate the neuroinflammatory response to spinal cord injury, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing dataset analysis, focusing on changes in microglial subpopulations. We found that the MG1 subpopulation emerged in the acute/subacute phase of spinal cord injury and expressed genes related to cell pyroptosis, sphingomyelin metabolism, and neuroinflammation at high levels. Subsequently, we established a mouse model of contusive injury and performed intrathecal injection of siRNA and molecular inhibitors to validate the role of ceramide synthase 5 in the neuroinflammatory responses and pyroptosis after spinal cord injury. Finally, we established a PC12-BV2 cell co-culture system and found that ceramide synthase 5 and pyroptosis-associated proteins were highly expressed to induce the apoptosis of neuron cells. Inhibiting ceramide synthase 5 expression in a mouse model of spinal cord injury effectively reduced pyroptosis. Furthermore, ceramide synthase 5-induced pyroptosis was dependent on activation of the NLRP3 signaling pathway. Inhibiting ceramide synthase 5 expression in microglia in vivo reduced neuronal apoptosis and promoted recovery of neurological function. Pla2g7 formed a “bridge” between sphingolipid metabolism and ceramide synthase 5-mediated cell death by inhibiting the NLRP3 signaling pathway. Collectively, these findings suggest that inhibiting ceramide synthase 5 expression in microglia after spinal cord injury effectively suppressed microglial pyroptosis mediated by NLRP3, thereby exerting neuroprotective effects.

## Introduction
The neuroinflammatory response that occurs after spinal cord injury (SCI) is a major contributor to poor post-injury prognosis. Microglia, the resident monocytes of the central nervous system, play a pivotal role in neuroinflammation (David and Kroner, 2011; Gaudet and Fonken, 2018; Yong et al., 2019). In the early stages of SCI, microglia phagocytose spinal cord tissue fragments and release neurotrophic and anti-inflammatory factors at the site of injury. This response reduces nerve damage and promotes tissue repair (Hu et al., 2015; Li et al., 2022). However, due to the almost catastrophic disruption that SCI causes in the local tissue environment, microglia tend to become hyperactivated. Consequently, they continuously secrete high levels of pro-inflammatory factors and cytotoxic substances, thus exacerbating neuroinflammation (Brennan et al., 2022). Severe nerve inflammation can lead to tissue damage and generate large amounts of cellular debris (Yong et al., 2019). One of the primary functions of microglia after SCI is to phagocytose cell and tissue debris. This process results in sphingomyelin accumulation in microglia, which disrupts lipid metabolism (Kopper and Gensel, 2018). Ceramides derived from sphingomyelin metabolism further exacerbate neuroinflammation, causing additional deterioration of the local tissue microenvironment (Liu et al., 2014). Nevertheless, the precise mechanism by which microglial sphingolipid metabolism promotes neuroinflammation after SCI remains unclear.
Several recent studies have shown that the neuroinflammatory response mediated by microglia is closely associated with microglial pyroptosis (Xiong et al., 2022; Moonen et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). Pyroptosis is a form of programmed cell death mediated by gasdermin, and is frequently induced by inflammasome production (Fricker et al., 2018; Swanson et al., 2019; Ding et al., 2024). There are four types of inflammasomes: NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain-associated protein (NLRP) 1, NLRP2, NLRP3, and absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2) inflammasomes, all of which are involved in central nervous system injury (de Rivero Vaccari et al., 2014). The NLRP3 inflammasome is the most widely studied in microglia and plays an important role in neuroinflammation (Liu et al., 2023; Müller et al., 2023). Tissue injury leads to activation of NLRP3 inflammatory vesicles, which results in the release of inflammatory factors, including interleukin (IL)-1β and IL-18, ultimately inducing pyroptosis. Pyroptosis causes cell membrane rupture, and this release of cellular contents activates an even more intense inflammatory response (Swanson et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). Thus, sphingomyelin metabolism, pyroptosis, and neuroinflammation are highly linked pathophysiological processes that occur after SCI and may share a common regulatory mechanism mediated by the microglia. While it is clear that sphingolipid metabolism triggers microglial pyroptosis after SCI, however, it remains unclear whether it also regulates neuroinflammation. Ceramide synthase (CerS) participate in ceramide synthesis, an important step in sphingomyelin metabolism, and different CerS family members catalyze the combination of fatty acid chains of different lengths with sphingosine to produce ceramides of different lengths. The C16 ceramide that Cers5 synthesizing promotes the development of inflammation by increasing the production of nitric oxide and TNF-α (Mu et al., 2024). Cers5-mediated sphingomyelin metabolism plays an important role in the subsequent inflammatory response after the body is stimulated by external stress. This process is achieved using the PLA2 signal pathway (Chao et al., 2019). Pla2g7, one of the PLA2, causes inflammation through the NLRP3 inflammasome (Lv et al., 2021), which plays an important role in pyroptosis (Toldo and Abbate, 2024). Therefore, we hypothesize that CerS5 induces pyroptosis and inflammation through the Pla2g7 and NLRP3 signaling pathway after spinal cord injury.
To investigate the regulatory function of microglia in pyroptosis, sphingomyelin metabolism, and neuroinflammation after SCI, and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset analysis of microglia at various time points post-SCI. Furthermore, we validated the regulatory relationships among pyroptosis, sphingomyelin metabolism, and neuroinflammation both in vivo and in vitro, thereby elucidating their molecular mechanisms.

## Methods

## Single-cell RNA sequencing dataset analysis
The scRNA-seq dataset used in this study was obtained from the FigShare database (https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.17702045.v2). The Seurat package (version 4.2.0) (Hao et al., 2021) in R was used to identify cell clusters. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed using ClusterProfiler software (version 4.4.4) (Wu et al., 2021). GSVA R software (version 1.44.5) (Hänzelmann et al., 2013) was used to identify signaling pathways enriched in microglial clusters, and gene sets were downloaded from the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb).

## Animals
The Animal Ethics Committee of South China Agricultural University approved all animal experiments (approval No. 2022F105) on August 25, 2022, and all experiments were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals published by the National Institutes of Health (National Research Council, 2011).
C57BL/6J mice (female, 8–10 weeks old, 18–22 g) were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China; license No. SCXK (Jing) 2019-0004). All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free environment with constant ambient temperature and humidity (22°C, 45% humidity) and maintained on a 12-hour light/dark cycle. The mice were randomly assigned to one of five groups (10 mice per group): (1) Sham group, with no SCI induced after opening the vertebral plate; (2) SCI group, with no intrathecal injection after striking the spinal cord; (3) SCI + si.CerS5 group, which were injected with 10 nmol CerS5 siRNA (si.CerS5) (synthesized by GenePharm, Shanghai, China; ) intrathecally around the notch immediately after contusion; (4) SCI + si.NC group, which were injected intrathecally with si.NC (10 nmol) (synthesized by GenePharm, Shanghai, China; ) around the notch immediately after contusion; (5) SCI + Darapladib group, which were injected intrathecally with Darapladib (a Pla2g7 inhibitor, 10 mg/kg, TargetMol, T6109, Boston, MA, USA) around the notch immediately after contusion; and (6) SCI + Vehicle group, which were injected intrathecally with vehicle (10% dimethylsulfoxide, 40% polyethylene glycol 300, 5% Tween-80, and 45% phosphate-buffered saline) around the notch immediately after contusion.
 Animals were pre-familiarized with the environment 1 week before the experiment and had ad libitum access to water and food throughout the procedure. To establish the mouse contusion SCI model, the first anesthesia was induced with 3% isoflurane (RWD, Shenzhen, China) inhalation and maintained with 1% isoflurane. Next, the dorsal skin over the thoracic vertebrae at the T9–T10 segment was incised, the muscles were separated, and the T10 vertebral plate was removed to expose the spinal cord at the T8 level. Then, the mice were immobilized, and a blow was delivered to the spine using a contusion device (Precision Systems and Instrumentation, LLC, Lexington, KY, USA), with the impact parameter set to 70 Kdyne. Successful contusion created a clear circular indentation in the spinal cord, with slight blood seepage around the indentation and paralysis of the hind limbs (McKillop et al., 2013).
The dura mater, muscles, and skin were then sutured layer by layer. Postoperatively, meloxicam (5 mg/kg, Macklin, Shanghai, China) was injected subcutaneously daily for 1 week following the procedure to manage pain. The bladder was emptied by abdominal compression once daily throughout the experiment, and the perineum and lower abdomen were cleaned after urination. Due to anatomical differences in the urethra, male mice have difficulty urinating after SCI, so to reduce the risk of animal mortality caused by urinary infection, only female mice were used in this study. At 14 days post injury (dpi), the mice were sacrificed, and the injured segment of the spinal cord was removed for further analysis.

## Basso Mouse Scale scoring
The Basso Mouse Scale (BMS) scoring system was utilized to assess SCI severity in the mice (Basso et al., 2006). Before scoring commenced, the mice were placed on a flat, open tabletop for 30 minutes to acclimatize them to the environment. Hind limb movements were observed and scored independently by two observers in a double-blinded manner, and the observation period lasted for 4 minutes. The BMS score ranged from 0 to 9 points, with a score of 9 indicating motor function similar to that of normal animals and a score of 0 representing complete loss of motor function.

## Cell culture conditions and treatment
BV2 microglia (China Center for Type Culture Collection, Wuhan, China, Cat# GDC0311, RRID: CVCL_0182) and PC12 cells (China Center for Type Culture Collection; Cat# GDC0006; RRID: CVCL_0481) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA, 11965118) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10099158) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, 15140122). When cells were reached 90% confluence, they were detached from the cell culture flasks using trypsin, diluted, and passaged to fresh flasks. In the BV2 cell interference assay, inflammation was induced by treating BV2 cells at 80% confluence with lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 1 μg/mL; Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 24 hours (Xu et al., 2021). Then, the medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed with phosphate buffered saline (Gibco, 10010001), resuspended in complete medium, and re-incubated for another 12 hours. The conditioned medium (CM) was collected, and PC12 cells were then cultured in the CM for 24 hours.
For the experiment involving BV2 cell treatment with ceramide, Daraplidib was added to complete medium at a final concentration of 2 μM, 24 hours prior to treatment. Subsequently, C16 ceramide (C912336, Macklin) was added at a final concentration of 20 μM, and the cells were re-incubated for 24 hours (Gaudioso et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024).
For the RNA interference experiments in BV2 cells, 30 nM of si.CerS5 or si.NC was transfected into BV2 cells using LipoRNAiTM Transfection Reagent (C0535, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 hours of transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium.

## Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay
The ceramide concentration in cell culture supernatants was determined using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Abmart, Shanghai, China, AB5960) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 10 μL of sample and 100 μL of HRP-labelled detection antibody were added to each well. After incubation for 30 minutes at 37°C, the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Synergy, Winooski, VT, USA).

## Hematoxylin and eosin staining
At 14 dpi, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and perfused with paraformaldehyde, and the injured segment of the spinal cord was excised. The tissue was then fixed in 10% neutral formalin, paraffin-embedded, and sliced into 14-μm thick sections. The sections were stained with a hematoxylin and eosin staining kit (Beyotime, C0105S) following standard procedures and observed under a light microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

## Immunofluorescence staining
At 14 dpi, mice were perfused with paraformaldehyde under anesthesia, and the injured segment of the spinal cord was removed. The excised tissue was then immersed in paraformaldehyde for 12 hours and sequentially dehydrated in 10%, 20%, and 30% sucrose solutions. The dehydrated tissues were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (SAKURA, Tokyo, Japan) and sliced into 14-μm thick sections using a frozen slicer (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The sections were incubated for 12 hours at 4°C with primary antibodies, including: mouse anti-ionized calcium binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba1; Servicebio, Wuhan, China, 1:300, Cat# GB12105, RRID: AB_2922434), rabbit anti-NLRP3 (1:300, Cat# ab263899, RRID: AB_2889890, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), rabbit anti-gasdermin D (GSDMD; 1:300, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, Cat# 20770-1-AP, RRID: AB_10696319), rabbit anti-CerS5 (1:300, Bioss, Beijing, China, Cat# bs-5082R, RRID: AB_10857681), rabbit anti-phospholipase a2 group VII (Pla2g7; 1:300, Proteintech, Cat# 29304-1-AP, RRID: AB_2918276), rabbit anti-nuclear factor kappa-B (NFkB) p105/p50 (1:500, Abcam, Cat# ab32360, RRID: AB_776748), and mouse anti-neuronal nuclei antigen (NeuN; 1:300, Proteintech, Cat# 66836-1-Ig, RRID: AB_2882179). After washing with phosphate buffered saline, the tissues were incubated for 2 hours at 25°C with secondary antibodies, including CoraLite 488 goat anti-mouse (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# SA00013-1, RRID: AB_2810983), CoraLite 488 goat anti-rabbit (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# SA00013-2, RRID: AB_2797132), and CoraLite 594 goat anti-rabbit (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# SA00013-4, RRID: AB_2810984). After washing again with phosphate buffered saline, the slides were covered with coverslips using antifade mounting medium containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (BL739B, Biosharp, Hefei, China). Finally, images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Ti2-U, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). For immunofluorescence staining, five biological replicates were performed. For each coronal section, three 1000-μm² boxes were drawn around the injury site. Within this region, the area occupied by CerS5+/GSDMD+ cells was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.53t; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA; Schneider et al., 2012), and the number of positively labeled cells was manually counted by a blinded investigator.

## TdT-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling staining
PC12 cell apoptosis was assessed by TdT-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) staining. The sections were incubated with the primary anti-NeuN antibody (1:300) overnight at 4°C and then stained using the TUNEL staining kit (Proteintech, PF00009) for 1 hour at 37°C. The sections were observed and photographed using a fluorescence microscope (Ti2-U, Nikon). Five randomly selected areas in the peri-injury region of each sample were analyzed using ImageJ software, and the mean values were used for statistical analysis.

## Western blot assay
Total protein was extracted from spinal cord tissue and BV2 cells using radio immunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Beyotime, P1050) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The concentrations of the extracted proteins were determined using a bicinchoninic acid kit (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA, 23227). Total protein was loaded onto a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel at 10 μg/well for electrophoretic separation, and then the proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. The membranes were blocked in 5% BSA for 1 hour and then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-NLRP3 (1:500, Abcam, Cat# ab263899, RRID: AB_2889890), rabbit anti-apoptosis associated speck-like protein containing CARD (ASC; 1:500, Zenbio, Chendu, China, Cat# 340097, RRID: AB_2921364), rabbit anti-caspase 1/p20/p10 (1:2000, Proteintech, Cat# 22915-1-AP, RRID: AB_2876874), rabbit anti-GSDMD (1:1000, NOVUS, Littleton, CO, USA, Cat# NBP2-33422, RRID: AB_2687913), rabbit anti-IL-1β (1:500, Zenbio, Cat# 511369, RRID: AB_3090194), mouse anti-IL-18 (1:2000, Proteintech, Cat# 60070-1-Ig, RRID: AB_2280158), rabbit anti-CerS5 (1:500, Bioss, Cat# bs-5082R, RRID: AB_10857681), rabbit anti-Pla2g7 (1:500, Proteintech, Cat# 29304-1-AP, RRID: AB_2918276), rabbit anti-NFκB p105/p50 (1:500, Abcam, Cat# ab32360, RRID: AB_776748), mouse anti-glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; 1:10,000, Proteintech, Cat# 60004-1-Ig, RRID: AB_2107436), and rabbit anti-β-actin (1:5000, Bioss, Cat# bs-0061R, RRID: AB_10855480). After washing, the membranes were incubated for 1 hour at 37°C with the following secondary antibodies: horse radish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated Affinipure goat anti-mouse IgG(H+L) (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-1, RRID: AB_2722565) and HRP-conjugated Affinipure goat anti-rabbit IgG(H+L) (1:1000, Proteintech, Cat# SA00001-2, RRID: AB_2722564). Optical density was detected using an enhanced chemiluminescent luminescence imaging system (5200, Tanon, Shanghai, China), and protein expression was normalized to housekeeping proteins (GAPDH and β-actin) using ImageJ software.

## Quantitative polymerase chain reaction
Total RNA was extracted from the spinal cord at the injury site using a Total RNA Kit (OMEGA, Norcross, GA, USA, R6394) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and reverse-transcribed to cDNA (Accurate Biology, Changsha, China, AG11706). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using QuantStudio5 (ThermoFisher) and 20 μL of reaction volumes comprising 2 μL of cDNA, 10 μL of SYBR Green Premix Pro Taq HS Premix (Accurate Biology, AG11701), 0.8 μL of the forward and reverse primers (20 pmol/μL), 0.4 μL of the ROX Reference Dye (Accurate Biology, AG11701), and sterile nuclease-free water. The PCR protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 10 seconds, followed by 40 amplification cycles. Each amplification cycle comprised denaturation at 95°C for 5 seconds and annealing/extension at 60°C for 30 seconds. Relative mRNA levels were determined using the 2–ΔΔCt method (Pfaffl, 2001). The primers used in this study are listed in .
 

## Statistical analysis
The sample size was determined based on previously established methods (Aflaki et al., 2012; Gao et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2019). Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5.0 for Windows, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA, www.graphpad.com). Specifically, two-tailed Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test were used. All results are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was defined as a P-value less than 0.05.

## Results

## MG1 microglia (IL1b+) are involved in neuroinflammation and express high levels of sphingomyelin metabolism-, pyroptosis-, and inflammation-related genes following spinal cord injury
A scRNA-seq dataset from the acute/subacute phase of SCI (14 days after SCI; Hu et al., 2023) was analyzed. The results revealed the presence of various cell types, including astrocytes, neurons, microglia, endothelial cells, oligodendrocytes, pericytes, erythrocytes, stromal cells, ependymal cells, neutrophils, and leukocytes (Additional Figure 1). In total, 8251 of these cells were identified as microglia and selected for further analysis. The microglia were categorized into three subsets (MG0, MG1, and MG2) based on a combination of differentially expressed genes (DEGs), canonical marker genes, and previously published spinal cord scRNA-seq datasets (Li et al., 2020b; A). The majority of microglia (95%) from the uninjured spinal cord belonged to the MG0 subset, which expressed homeostatic microglia genes, such as P2ry12, Tmem119, and Siglech (Hammond et al., 2019; B and C). At 3 dpi, the majority of microglia from the injured spinal cord had transitioned to one of two novel states: MG1 (25%) and MG2 (70%) ( C). These MG1 and MG2 microglia persisted in the injured spinal cord at 7 and 14 dpi ( C). Both the MG1 and the MG2 microglia expressed Spp1 (a marker of microglial activation) and Ms4a7 (which is mainly expressed in embryonic microglia and border macrophages) (Hohsfield et al., 2020; Shen et al., 2022; B). The MG1 microglial subset was newly defined in this study. The key distinction between the MG1 subtype and the other two subtypes lies in their concurrent high levels of expression of inflammation-related genes (IL1b and Nfkb1) and sphingomyelin metabolism-related genes (Pla2g7) ( B). The MG1 microglia subset emerged following SCI, peaking on the 3rd day and persisting throughout the acute/subacute phase ( C). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the genes that were upregulated in MG1 microglia compared with MG0 microglia were significantly enriched in positive regulation of response to external stimulus, leukocyte migration, and phospholipid binding ( D). Furthermore, GSVA analysis demonstrated prominent enrichment of the pyroptosis, ceramide, NFκB, and sphingolipid de novo biosynthesis pathways in MG1 microglia ( E). Analysis of microglial gene expression after SCI showed that sphingomyelin metabolism-related genes (Cers5 and Pla2g7), pyroptosis-related genes (Gsdmd, Nlrp3, and Casp1), and inflammation-related genes (Nfkb1 and Il1b) were highly expressed in MG1 microglia ( F).
 

## CerS5 expression is elevated after SCI
Next, we performed qPCR analysis to investigate the expression of CerS family members in mice at the site of SCI and in the corresponding position in the spinal cords of mice in the Sham group, 14 dpi, to confirm that sphingolipid metabolism was altered following SCI. The results revealed no statistically significant difference in CerS2 expression between the SCI and Sham groups (P > 0.05). However, a 0.6-fold decrease in CerS1 expression and 1.5-fold, 5.55-fold, and 2.9-fold increases in CerS4, CerS5, and CerS6 expression were observed in the SCI group compared with the Sham group, respectively ( A). To further validate the change in CerS5 protein expression level, WB assay and immunofluorescence staining were performed on spinal cord tissue collected 14 days after SCI. The results were consistent with the findings from scRNA-seq analysis and qPCR, confirming the upregulation of CerS5 expression after SCI ( B and C). Notably, the number of CerS5+ cells was significantly increased at the injury site in mice from the SCI group compared with at the corresponding area of the spinal cord in mice from the Sham group (P < 0.01; D and E).
 

## Cell pyroptosis is increased after spinal cord injury
Next, we investigated the expression and tissue localization of the pyroptosis-associated protein GSDMD at the injury site 14 days after SCI and in corresponding spinal segments in the Sham group to confirm that pyroptosis occurred after SCI. When compared with the Sham group, western blot analysis showed a significant increase in the expression of both GSDMD and its N-terminal fragment, GSDMD-N in the SCI group (P < 0.01; F and G). Additionally, immunohistochemical staining demonstrated a significant increase in the number of GSDMD-positive cells at the injury site compared with the Sham group (P < 0.01; H and I). These findings were in line with the results from the scRNA-seq dataset analysis.

## CerS5 knockdown effectively inhibits lipopolysaccharide-induced microglial pyroptosis
Although we observed elevated levels of microglial sphingolipid metabolism and cellular pyroptosis after SCI and validated these findings in a mouse model of SCI, the underlying relationship between sphingolipid metabolism and cellular pyroptosis remained unclear. To address this topic, we first established a neuroinflammatory model by stimulating BV2 cells with LPS to mimic microglial activation after SCI. Subsequently, we inhibited CerS5 expression of using an antisense RNA ( A and B), which led to a significant decrease in the expression of GSDMD, a protein associated with cell death (Sborgi et al., 2016; C and D).
 The central nervous system exhibits a high degree of complexity, with various cell types interacting in an intricate and orderly manner (Lénárt et al., 2024). Consequently, the microglial changes observed after SCI are likely to be influenced by the local tissue environment. To determine whether modulating CerS5 expression would inhibit microglial pyroptosis in a mouse model of SCI, we inhibited CerS5 expression in the injured spinal cord by intrathecal injection of si.CerS5 ( A and B). Subsequently, we examined GSDMD expression at the site of SCI to assess the impact of CerS5 inhibition on microglial pyroptosis. Both immunofluorescence and western blot results were consistent with in the results from the in vitro experiments, showing that inhibition of CerS5 expression resulted in a reduction in the number of GSDMD+ cells and in GSDMD expression compared with the SCI group ( C and D).
 

## The inhibitory effect of CerS5 knockdown on microglial cell pyroptosis is mediated by inhibition of NLRP3 signaling pathway activation
Our earlier results showed that CerS5 expression levels in microglia after SCI were closely related to pyroptosis. However, the molecular mechanism linking these two phenomena remained unclear. To investigate the molecular mechanism by which CerS5 inhibits pyroptosis, we examined activation of the classical NLRP3 cell death pathway after SCI. Immunofluorescence staining results showed a decrease in the number of NLRP3+ cells after inhibition of CerS5 expression in vivo ( A). Furthermore, western blot analysis of NLRP3, ASC, Caspase1, cleaved-Caspase1, IL-1β, and IL-18 expression at the injury site showed that the expression of all NLRP3 pyroptosis classical pathway-related proteins was reduced after inhibition of CerS5 expression ( B). These results demonstrate that CerS5 inhibits microglial pyroptosis after SCI by inhibiting activation of the NLRP3 signaling pathway.
 

## CerS5 knockdown inhibits Pla2g7 and p50 expression in the spinal cord after spinal cord injury
In addition to pyroptosis-associated proteins, the scRNA-seq dataset analysis results that Pla2g7 and p50 were highly expressed in MG1 microglia after SCI ( F). Therefore, we used a mouse model of SCI to investigate whether inhibiting CerS5 expression in vivo would affect Pla2g7 and p50 expression. Western blot analysis showed that inhibiting CerS5 effectively reversed the upregulation in Pla2g7 and p50 expression seen after SCI ( A). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed that there was a significant increase in the number of Pla2g7+ and p50+ microglia in the SCI group compared with the Sham group (P < 0.01), and that inhibiting CerS5 expression reversed this effect ( B and C).
 

## Pla2g7 is essential for CerS5-mediated regulation of pyroptosis
While we demonstrated that inhibiting CerS5 expression after SCI can affect expression of the pyroptosis-associated proteins Pla2g7 and p50, the underlying regulatory relationship remained unclear. To further investigate the relationships among these molecules, we first examined the expression of C16 ceramide, a product of CerS5 metabolic pathway, after SCI. The results showed that the C16 ceramide content of spinal cord tissue was significantly elevated after SCI. However, inhibiting CerS5 expression in vivo resulted in a marked reduction in C16 ceramide levels ( A).
 To simulate this phenomenon in vitro, we stimulated BV2 cells with C16 ceramide and then treated the cells with the Pla2g7 inhibitor Darapladib. The results demonstrate that treating BV2 cells with C16 ceramide induced high levels of p50, Pla2g7, and GSDMD expression in microglia after injury. Notably, inhibiting Pla2g7 expression suppressed p50 and GSDMD expression ( B). Immunofluorescence staining results further confirmed that treatment with the Pla2g7 inhibitor attenuated the ability of C16 ceramide, a downstream product of the CerS5 metabolic pathway, to promote microglial pyroptosis ( C).
To determine whether Pla2g7 is essential for CerS5-induced pyroptosis, we compared the effects of CerS5 and Pla2g7 inhibition on pyroptosis in BV2 cells stimulated with LPS. The results showed that both interventions effectively suppressed the expression of pyroptosis-associated proteins, thus confirming that Pla2g7 plays a crucial role in regulating the relationship between CerS5 and pyroptosis ( D). To further validate the role of Pla2g7 in the relationship between CerS5 and pyroptosis in vivo, we treated a mouse model of SCI with Darapladib and found that inhibiting Pla2g7 in vivo resulted in a significant reduction in the number of GSDMD+ microglia ( A), as well as decreased expression of NLRP3, ASC, Caspase1, Cleaved-Caspase1, GSDMD, IL-1β, and IL-18 ( B). These findings demonstrate that inhibiting Pla2g7 in vivo effectively attenuated microglial pyroptosis after SCI.
 

## CerS5 knockdown is neuroprotective in the context of spinal cord injury
We investigated whether inhibiting CerS5 expression would play a neuroprotective role in the context of SCI. To address this, we first stimulated normal BV2 cells and BV2 cells in which CerS5 expression was inhibited with LPS. We then incubated PC12 cells in conditioned media from both cell types and observed the effects on PC12 cell apoptosis. We found that inhibiting CerS5 expression in BV2 cells reduced the ability of the conditioned BV2 cell medium to induce apoptosis in PC12 cells ( A). Using a mouse model of SCI we found that inhibiting CerS5 expression markedly reduced neuronal apoptosis in vivo ( B). Furthermore, hematoxylin-eosin staining demonstrated that inhibiting CerS5 in vivo effectively reduced the area of the injured region ( C). Finally, the BMS scores indicated that inhibiting CerS5 expression improved motor recovery in mice after SCI ( D).
 

## Discussion
Microglia, the only mononuclear cells in the central nervous system, are considered the “first line of defense” after SCI (Saijo and Glass, 2011; Aguzzi et al., 2013; Hu et al., 2015). They play an important role in the pathophysiological changes that occur after SCI and are a promising target for SCI treatment (Brockie et al., 2024). Microglia phagocytose the sphingomyelin that is released when nerve tissue is destroyed by SCI, leading to severe sphingomyelin metabolism overload in these cells (Brennan et al., 2022). However, the effects of sphingomyelin metabolism overload on microglia and other cells in the tissue microenvironment during SCI remain unclear. Microglial pyroptosis is also thought to be an important cause of neuroinflammation and poor prognosis after SCI (Xu et al., 2021; Xiong et al., 2022). However, the effect of sphingomyelin overload on microglial pyroptosis and the mechanism underlying this effect was unclear. Therefore, in this study, we explored this issue.
Microglia can exist in a diverse range of functional states following SCI, and the roles they play in these different states vary significantly (Li et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2023). These functional differences directly impact the pathophysiological progression of SCI. Therefore, in this study, we initially analyzed a post-SCI scRNA-seq dataset to conduct a comprehensive analysis of microglial gene expression profiles. We found that microglia could be categorized into three distinct subsets after SCI. According to the marker gene expressed by each subcluster and the previous researchers’ definition of microglia subtypes (Milich et al., 2021), MG0 subset corresponded to homeostatic microglia (P2ry12, Siglech, Tmem119), MG1 subset corresponded to inflammation microglia (Il1b), and MG2 subset corresponded to migrating microglia (Igf1). The MG1 subset also highly expressed sphingomyelin metabolism-related genes (CerS5 and Pla2g7). Members of the CerS family (CerS1–6) play a crucial role in regulating the synthesis of ceramide, an intermediate product of sphingolipid metabolism. Therefore, we initially performed qPCR to investigate the expression of CerS family members following SCI (Chao et al., 2019; Gaudioso et al., 2023). Our results aligned with those from the single-cell analyses, revealing that CerS5 was the primary molecule involved in sphingolipid metabolism post-SCI. Notably, C16 ceramide, the product of CerS5 synthesis, is the primary ceramide implicated in neuronal death (Gaudioso et al., 2023). Consequently, we postulate that the elevated CerS5 expression seen in microglia after SCI contributes significantly to the poor prognosis associated with this condition. We confirmed that Cers5, a gene associated with sphingolipid metabolism, was overexpressed in a mouse model of SCI, and that microglia underwent pyroptosis following injury. When we inhibited CerS5 expression using siRNA in both cell and mouse models of SCI, microglial pyroptosis was reduced, indicating that upregulation of CerS5 expression post-SCI is crucial for the induction of microglial pyroptosis. We then demonstrated that the inhibition of microglial pyroptosis achieved by inhibiting CerS5 in vivo was mediated by suppression of the NLRP3 signaling pathway. In this classical pathway, ASC activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, which then mediates Caspase1 cleavage. Cleaved-Caspase1 then cleaves GSDMD protein to form active GSDMD-N, which perforates the cell membrane, leading to pyroptosis. Concurrently, Caspase1 promotes the production of IL-1β and IL-18, which are secreted out of the cell through the perforated cell membrane, thereby amplifying the inflammatory response (Devant and Kagan, 2023; Vande Walle and Lamkanfi, 2024). Therefore, CerS5-induced microglial pyroptosis induced by the NLRP3 pathway significantly contributes to exacerbation of the neuroinflammatory response after SCI. PLA2 and NFκb both influence activation of the NLRP3 signaling pathway (Lv et al., 2021). Consistent with this, our single-cell analysis revealed that MG1 microglia expressed Pla2g7 and Nfkb1 at high levels. Hence, we demonstrated that inhibiting CerS5 expression suppressed Pla2g7 and p50 expression following SCI. Activation of the NFκb signaling pathway is a key driver of NLRP3 signaling pathway activation (Li et al., 2020a; Lei et al., 2023). PLA2G7, a phospholipase A2 enzyme, plays a crucial role in phospholipid metabolism, and its lipid mediators contribute to intense inflammation during tissue injury. Thus, Pla2g7 may serve as a crucial link between sphingolipid metabolism and CerS5-mediated pyroptosis. In this study, we treated BV2 cells with C16 ceramide, a downstream product of CerS5 metabolic pathway, to mimic the impact of CerS5 expression on microglia post-SCI. We then inhibited Pla2g7 using Darapladib and found that the inhibitory effect of Pla2g7 on microglial cell death was achieved by suppressing the NLRP3 signaling pathway. A subpopulation of microglia expressing high levels of CerS5 induced pyroptosis after SCI by promotingC16 ceramide production, and this effect was closely related to activation of the NLRP3 signaling pathway by C16 ceramide. We also found that NFκB and Pla2g7 were essential for activation of the NLRP3 signaling pathway by C16 ceramide. Recovery from SCI depends largely on the number of surviving neurons, and neuroinflammation is an important cause of neuronal death after SCI. Therefore, we assessed the effects of inhibiting microglial CerS5 expression on neurological function in vivo. We found that inhibiting microglial CerS5 expression reduced the size of the SCI area and improved motor recovery after SCI by inhibiting neuronal apoptosis.
There were some limitations to our experimental design. Due to the limited number of animals available, our in vivo experiments focused solely on the 14-day time point following SCI, and we did not include earlier time points (3 and 7 days) for analysis. Also, in the in vitro experiments we used BV2 cells instead of primary microglia, which would have been more physiologically accurate.
In conclusion, our findings suggest that inhibiting microglial CerS5 expression after SCI effectively mitigates neuroinflammation by suppressing microglial pyroptosis, thereby exerting neuroprotective effects. Furthermore, we showed that this process involves C16 ceramide, a downstream product of CerS5 metabolic pathway, which activates the NLRP3 signaling pathway in a manner dependent on Pla2g7 and NFκB.
