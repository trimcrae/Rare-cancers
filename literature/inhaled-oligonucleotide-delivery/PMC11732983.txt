## Abstract
Cellular senescence (CS) is recognized as a critical driver of aging and age-related disorders. Recent studies have emphasized the roles of ion channels as key mediators of CS. Nonetheless, the roles and regulatory mechanisms of chloride intracellular channels (CLICs) during CS remain largely unexplored. In this study, we conducted RNA sequencing on bleomycin-induced senescent lung tissues from mice and identified Clic3 as the most significantly upregulated CLIC member. Furthermore, our findings revealed that the knockdown of CLIC3 mitigated intracellular chloride ion lose, mitochondrial dysfunction, nuclear enlargement, DNA damage, CS progression, and expression of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) triggered by bleomycin. Mechanistically, CLIC3 controls CS by translocating to the membrane where it interacts with extracellular signal-regulated kinase 7 (ERK7). Overall, our work demonstrates that the chloride intracellular channel CLIC3 modulates CS by repressing ERK7 activity and provides novel insights into the role of chloride channels.
Subject terms: Senescence, Ion channel signalling

Upon stimuli, chloride intracellular channel CLIC3 is induced and mitigates intracellular chloride ion lose, thereby controls mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS accumulation, DNA damage, and fibroblast cellular senescence by interacting with ERK7.

## Introduction
Cellular senescence (CS), which is described as a stable proliferation arrest in dividing cells and a secretion of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors, plays a significant role in aging, a broad spectrum of age-associated diseases, and cancer progression , . Recently, numerous CS markers, including cell cycle arrest, increasing senescence-associated β galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) activity, activation of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors (CDKIs), inhibition of proliferation marker Ki67, DNA damage, and SASP secretion, as well as their regulation pathways have been identified to recognize senescent cells both in vitro and in vivo. Existed articles have reported that blockage or activation of specific CS hallmarks or cascades allows an escape from CS , . Based on the characteristics of senescent cells, emerging studies have been dedicated to identifying anti-aging and anti-age-related pathology compounds. Notably, a class of drugs that selectively kill senescent cells (termed senolytics; including dasatinib + quercetin, FOXO4-DRI, and SSK1) and some small molecules that suppress the phenotypes of senescent cells without killing them (named senomorphics; such as rapamycin, metformin, and ruxolitinib) have exhibited promise in extending healthy expectancy or alleviating age-related diseases . However, most of these senotherapeutics require comprehensive investigation before clinical application to ensure that their specific targets are safe and standardized protocols are established.
Increasing lines of evidence have demonstrated that ions and ion channel proteins play crucial roles in CS and aging. For example, intracellular calcium contents were elevated in response to diverse senescence stimuli , and chelation of intracellular calcium ions contributed to a decrease in reactive oxygen species (ROS) and DNA damage . Moreover, the inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor type 2 (ITPR2) gated calcium transferring from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to mitochondria has been proven to trigger CS and aging , . In addition to calcium ions and channels, another study revealed that during oncogene-induced senescence (OIS), the potassium channel KCNA1 was upregulated and translocated from the cytoplasm to the membrane, thereby inducing a change in membrane potential and leading ultimately to CS . Furthermore, activation of oncogenic signal or p53 resulted in the overexpression of sodium channel SCN9A, and the induced SCN9A subsequently led to plasma membrane depolarization, together with repression of mitotic gene expression and CS . In addition, excessive accumulation of iron ions within nerve cells has been observed during aging and led to age-related neurodegenerative diseases . However, the roles and regulation of chloride intracellular channels during CS remain poorly understood.
In this study, we performed an RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of bleomycin-induced injured lung tissues in mice, as it is extensively acknowledged that CS and SASP play crucial roles in bleomycin-induced lung injury . We were then interested in the changes in chloride channels during bleomycin-induced lung injury. With the help of in vitro and in vivo validation, we found the chloride intracellular channel CLIC3 to be significantly induced during CS. CLIC3, a member of the chloride intracellular channel (CLIC) family, has been characterized as a tumor promoter or aggressor, and its high expression is associated with poor prognosis in various cancer types, including pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and breast cancer . However, the implication of CLIC3 in CS remains largely unknown. We thereby investigated the effects of CLIC3 during CS in this study and elucidated the mechanisms by which CLIC3 regulates CS.

## Results

## CLIC3 is elevated during aging and fibroblast cellular senescence
To thoroughly understand the altered genes during CS, we performed an RNA-seq analysis based on the bleomycin (Bleo)-induced lung injury mouse model (intratracheal instillation; refer to ref. for detailed methods). We assessed the distances between samples to evaluate the homogeneity of samples in the Bleo or negative control (NC) group (Fig.  ). We were interested in the ion binding factors according to Gene Ontology (GO) analysis (Fig.  ) and further focused on chloride channels. Then, based on the novelty in senescence regulation and high expression levels observed in Bleo vs. NC, the chloride intracellular channel genes (Clic1–Clic6) drew our interest (Fig.  ). We initially validated the expression of CLICs in the human lung fibroblast cell line MRC5, and the results indicated that only CLIC3 was significantly induced after Bleo and doxorubicin (Dox) treatment (Fig.  ). Therefore, we preliminarily screened out the Clic3 gene for further validation (Fig.  ). We subsequently confirmed the upregulation of Clic3 in both mRNA (Fig.  ) and protein (Fig.  ) levels in Bleo-induced lung injury mice (validation of lung aging in Fig.  ). To avoid any confusion regarding the influence of Bleo on Clic3 expression, we assessed the Clic3 level in naturally aged mice (20 months) vs. young mice (8 weeks) (validation of lung aging in Fig.  ) and found a striking increase of Clic3 in both mRNA (Fig.  ) and protein (Fig.  ) aspects. In addition, we used the fibroblast cell line WI38 to establish Bleo- and Dox-induced CS (Fig.  ), and we found that CLIC3 was upregulated in both mRNA (Fig.  ) and protein (Fig.  ) levels after therapy-induced CS. Taken together, these data indicate that CLIC3 is significantly elevated during aging, and different stresses induced fibroblast CS.
 

## Knockdown of CLIC3 alleviates chloride loss and mitochondrial dysfunction induced by bleomycin
Considering that CLIC3 functions as a chloride channel, we were then intrigued by whether the concentration of chloride ions is changed during CS and, if so, whether CLIC3 participates in this process. Therefore, we used the intracellular chloride ion indicator MQAE in WI38 and found a marked increase of MQAE staining, which indicates a decrease in chloride level after Bleo or Dox treatment (Fig.  ). Notably, the knockdown of CLIC3 resulted in a significant reversal of the decreased intracellular chloride ions triggered by Bleo, as evidenced by fluorescence staining (Fig.  ) and FACS analysis (Fig.  ). In addition, we introduced in this study the combined Cl−/pH genetic sensor (ClopHensor), and the results indicated that the knockdown of CLIC3 was sufficient to reverse the decrease in intracellular chloride ions triggered by Bleo (Fig.  ).
 To determine whether changes in intracellular chloride ions level could affect mitochondrial function, we measured the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP). First, we used mitochondrial uncoupler carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) was applied to induce the decrease in MMP (Fig.  ). Then, we observed a decline in MMP induced by Bleo and a recovery after knocking down CLIC3 (Fig.  ). As the decline of MMP often leads to reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation , we subsequently evaluated the cellular ROS level, and the results indicated that the knockdown of CLIC3 alleviated both mitochondrial ROS (mitoROS) (Fig.  ) and intracellular total ROS (Fig.  ) overproduction promoted by Bleo in fibroblasts. In addition, studies have demonstrated that tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle key genes are generally inhibited during aging . Thus, the role of CLIC3 in the regulation of mitochondrial TCA cycle genes was also evaluated in this study, and the results suggested that silencing CLIC3 triggered an increasing expression of the Bleo-repressed TCA cycle key genes, including phosphofructokinase (PFKM), pyruvate kinase (PKM), citrate synthase (CS), isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1), dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase (DLD), succinate dehydrogenase complex A (SDHA), fumarase (FH) and malic enzyme 1 (ME1) (Fig.  ). Altogether, these results suggest that CLIC3 controls intracellular chloride ion loss and mitochondrial functions during fibroblast CS.

## Knockdown of CLIC3 alleviates bleomycin-induced nuclear enlargement and DNA damage
Dysregulated mitochondrial function and accumulated intracellular ROS are known to induce nuclear damage . In addition, an enlarged nucleus, which can be promoted by errors in mitosis, has been regarded as a hallmark of genomic instability and senescence . We thus examined nuclear phenotype and observed enlarged nuclei characterized by increased average area and perimeter after Bleo treatment, whereas this enlargement was partially diminished after the knockdown of CLIC3 (Fig.  a–c). Moreover, we detected DNA damage using two markers: 53BP1 and p-H2AX. The results displayed a similar tendency as the nuclear phenotype in that the DNA damage induced by Bleo was largely alleviated after silencing CLIC3 (Fig.  d, e). Therefore, we summarize that the knockdown of CLIC3 contributes to the remission of Bleo-induced nuclear enlargement and DNA damage.
 

## Knockdown of CLIC3 delays bleomycin-induced cellular senescence and SASP secretion
Encouraged by these findings, we were next interested in exploring the roles of CLIC3 during CS. Notably, the Bleo-induced CS, as evidenced by increased SA-β-Gal activity, cell proliferation arrest, CDKN1A and CDKN2A upregulation and Ki67 inhibition, and p16 and p21 elevation, all largely reverted after inhibiting CLIC3 (Fig.  a–h). To gain a better understanding of the importance of CLIC3 in regulating CS, we used a common chloride channel inhibitor IAA-94, and the results revealed that both IAA-94 treatment and CLIC3 knockdown were sufficient to alleviate Bleo-induced CS (Fig.  ). However, adding IAA-94 to CLIC3 knockdown cells during Bleo treatment was not able to further diminish senescence phenotypes (Fig.  ), suggesting that CLIC3 may be key among chloride channels in the regulation of senescence.
 Furthermore, to investigate whether the SASP expression is controlled by CLIC3 or not, we searched the search-based exploration of expression compendium (SEEK) co-expression database and found that CLIC3 is positively correlated with nine SASP factors (insulin-like growth factor binding protein 6 (IGFBP6), interleukin 32 (IL32), interleukin 2 receptor subunit beta (IL2RB), C–X3–C motif chemokine receptor 1 (CX3CR1), interleukin 11 receptor subunit alpha (IL11RA), C–C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5), IGFBP3, C–X–C motif chemokine receptor 3 (CXCR3) and CXCR6) in lung noncancer samples from 64 datasets with co-expression threshold of 0.4 (Fig.  ). Subsequently, we further validated these observations from the SEEK database in fibroblasts, and the results suggested that the knockdown of CLIC3 strongly repressed the upregulated nine SASP genes induced by Bleo-treatment (Fig.  ). Taken together, these results confirm that the knockdown of CLIC3 delays Bleo-induced CS and SASP secretion.

## CLIC3 triggers cellular senescence by repressing ERK7
Next, we sought to understand how CLIC3 controls CS. A previous study has demonstrated that CLIC3 interacts with and forms a stable complex with mitogen-activated protein kinase 15 (MAPK15/ERK7) . To confirm this interaction, we thereby performed a co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP) experiment, which substantiated that CLIC3 indeed interacts with ERK7 (Fig.  , b). Subsequently, we assessed the expression of ERK7, and the results indicated that ERK7 was activated after silencing of CLIC3 in both fibroblasts (Fig.  ) and lung epithelial cells (Fig.  ). Furthermore, we stained proteins CLIC3 and ERK7 concurrently, and we observed the overlap of the two proteins together with a decrease in ERK7 coupled with an increase in CLIC3 expression during senescence (Fig.  ). Interestingly, we observed a tendency for translocation of CLIC3 to the membrane during senescence (Fig.  ). To verify this phenomenon, we extracted cellular membranes from the cytosol, and the results demonstrated that CLIC3 was elevated whereas ERK7 was inhibited in the membrane during senescence (Fig.  c, d). Interestingly, although the expression of total CLIC3 was induced, it was not significantly changed in the cytoplasm during Bleo treatment (Fig.  ).
 Considering that ERK7 has been shown to delay CS , we thereby silenced ERK7 using siRNAs to promote CS. Notably, we observed a strong increase in SA-β-Gal activity, cell proliferation arrest, accumulation of mitoROS and ROS levels, and elevated CLIC3, p21, p16, and p-H2AX protein expression after ERK7 inhibition. Interestingly, these phenomena all largely diminished when CLIC3 was simultaneously knocked down in either fibroblasts (Fig.  e–h) or lung epithelial cells (Fig.  , h). Meanwhile, we also introduced the ERK7 inhibitor CK2/ERK8-IN-1 (CK2) to promote fibroblast CS, and, as expected, similar outcomes to those observed with ERK7 repression occurred after silencing CLIC3 (Fig.  ). Collectively, these observations strongly confirm that CLIC3 controls fibroblast CS by interacting with ERK7.

## High CLIC3 level in lung tumors indicates poor outcomes
It has been established that CS plays an important role in tumor initiation and progression caused by its SASP secretion . In this study, we found that CLIC3 controls the expression of many SASP factors (Fig.  ). Therefore, it is plausible to speculate that CLIC3 may be associated with cancer progression by modulating SASP. We were first interested in the expression of CLIC3 in cancer cells. We subjected CLIC3 to the Kaplan–Meier plotter analysis (http://kmplot.com/analysis/), and its level was found to be significantly negatively correlated with survival possibility in lung cancer patients, excluding those who never smoked (as cigarette smoking has been linked to CS) (Fig.  ). To validate these database observations, we examined and confirmed that CLIC3 was highly expressed in lung cancer cells compared with normal lung epithelial cells at both mRNA (Fig.  ) and protein (Fig.  ) levels. These data suggest that accumulation of CLIC3 in lung cancer may lead to poor outcomes for patients. However, whether CLIC3 is involved in the regulation of tumor development remains to be elucidated.
 

## Discussion
Although some ion channel proteins have been identified as senescence regulators, the effects of chloride channels and these channels mediating intracellular chloride ions on CS are not well understood. In this study, we presented evidence that CLIC3 is induced during aging and CS. Our data demonstrated that the knockdown of CLIC3 alleviates intracellular chloride loss, mitochondrial dysfunction, nuclear enlargement, DNA damage, and consequently fibroblast CS and SASP secretion promoted by therapeutics. Furthermore, we unveiled that CLIC3 controls fibroblast CS by translocating to the membrane and interacting with ERK7. In addition, we found CLIC3 to be overexpressed in lung cancer cells, and its high expression indicated poor outcomes in lung tumor patients.
Studies on CS and aging have recently garnered considerable attention, particularly since the discovery of senolytics and senomorphics. However, insufficiently identified in vivo senescence-specific markers hinder advances in understanding anti-aging and anti-age-related disorders. In this study, we focused on the regulation of fibroblast CS by chloride channels. It has been reported that mammalian chloride channels contain five classes based on their regulation: cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulators, voltage-gated chloride channels, calcium-activated chloride channels, ligand-gated chloride channels, and volume-regulated chloride channels . Chloride channels are known to modulate various physiological processes, including cell volume, cell proliferation, immune tolerance, enzyme activities, and maintaining cellular electroneutrality, etc . Nevertheless, little is known regarding the roles of channel-mediated intracellular chloride signaling.
In this study, we screened and identified that CLIC3, which belongs to a novel chloride channel class, was strikingly overexpressed during CS and aging. Moreover, the knockdown of CLIC3 increased the Bleo-induced chloride loss, lessened nuclear enlargement, and delayed Bleo-triggered fibroblast CS. Intriguingly, although the ablation of another CLIC member (CLIC4) has been reported to promote mitoROS in cardiomyocytes , our results indicated no significant changes in ROS production after knocking down CLIC3 in proliferating cells, but not in senescent cells. These discrepancies may arise from differences in cell types and experimental conditions. Although the role of CLIC3 in regulating cancer progression and inflammation has been extensively studied , its regulation of CS remains largely inconclusive. Our data illustrated that CLIC3 controlled CS by migrating to the membrane and interacting with ERK7. Of note, the constitutively active ERK7 has been proven to modulate multiple cellular processes, including genomic integrity, autophagy, and cellular senescence, by phosphorylating specific targets in different subcellular compartments , . However, several open questions warrant further investigation. (1) Does CLIC3 interact with ERK7 directly or indirectly, and what are the specific targets of ERK7 during CLIC3-mediated senescence? (2) Do intracellular chloride ions mediated by CLIC3 function as second messengers to transmit senescence signals?
Importantly, we also observed a reversal of Bleo-induced mitochondrial dysfunction (exhibited by a decrease in MMP, accumulation of mitoROS, and d a decrease in TCA cycle key genes) by silencing CLIC3. As a critical energy supply unit, mitochondria have recently garnered considerable attention, and studies have made great progress in restoring mitochondrial functions to alleviate age-associated pathologies or extend a healthy lifespan. For instance, enhancing the mitochondria-ER contacts (MERCs) has been shown to induce mitochondrial membrane depolarization, mitoROS accumulation, and premature senescence . Moreover, a decrease in the expression of several TCA cycle key genes has been linked to age-related diseases . In addition, upon senescence stimulation, mitochondrial DNA is released to the cytoplasm and hence promotes SASP secretion, and interfering with this process contributes to counteracting age-associated sterile inflammation and improving healthspan .
Our data illustrated that the knockdown of CLIC3 was able to block the Bleo-induced SASP secretion. SASP has been known as a key player in the pathophysiological effects of senescent cells and is recently regarded as a dramatic target of senomorphics . We thus speculate that CLIC3 may represent an attractive therapeutic target against aging or age-related diseases in the future. Interestingly, we also observed that CLIC3 was highly expressed in lung cancer cells, but it remains to be elucidated (1) whether the overexpressed CLIC3 in various cancer types leads to tumor progression or metastasis and (2) whether silencing CLIC3 contributes to lung tumor inhibition. Interestingly, CLIC3 is negatively correlated with survival possibilities in cigarette-smoking lung cancer patients. It is worth investigating whether cigarette smoking promotes the expression of CLIC3 or intracellular chloride accumulation, particularly in lung cancer patients.
In conclusion, our study demonstrated that expression of CLIC3 and loss of intracellular chloride ions were induced during CS and aging. CLIC3 mediated ROS accumulation, DNA damage, and fibroblast CS by interacting with ERK7. Our work has shed light on the link between chloride channels and CS. Future efforts are required to investigate the precise burden of CLIC3-mediated fibroblast cellular senescence and the roles of CLIC3-mediated intracellular chloride signaling during aging and in age-related diseases.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture and reagents
The human lung fibroblast cell lines MRC5 and WI38 were kindly provided by the Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The human lung epithelial cell line BEAS-2B was from Procell, China. H1975, SPCA, and A549 were kindly from Yong Chen (Yangzhou University). All cells were authenticated before being used for experiments. Cells were cultured under an atmosphere of 37 °C with 5% CO2 in standard Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Life Technologies) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Procell), 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Solarbio) and GlutaMax. Cells were maintained in low concentrations of plasmocin (Invivogen) to guarantee mycoplasma-free culture condition. Bleomycin sulfate (HY-17565) (1 µg/ml for in vitro studies and 1.875 mg/kg for in vivo studies), MQAE (HY-D0090) (10 µM), IAA-94 (HY-12693) (10 µM), CK2/ERK8-IN-1 (CK2) (HY-135906) (10 µM), and CCCP (HY-100941) (10 µM) were purchased from MCE; doxorubicin (D7840) was from Solarbio and the final concentration was 100 nM in cells.

## Animals
This study used female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) obtained from the Institute of Comparative Medicine of Yangzhou University. The mice were housed in an animal facility with appropriate humidity, controlled temperature, and a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. Mice had ad libitum access to commercial food and water. Randomization was used to allocate experimental units of the mice to the indicated control or bleomycin treatment group. To minimize potential confounding factors, the order of treatment and measurements, as well as animal/cage locations, were randomized. The operators were blinded to the mouse group allocation during the experiment and when assessing the outcomes. All mouse experiments were approved by the Science and Technology Commission of the Affiliated Hospital of Yangzhou University, and all protocols followed the Principles of Laboratory Animal Care (NIH publication No. 85Y50, revised 1996). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. In brief, mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg), and the mice were sacrificed via cervical dislocation while under deep sedation. Lung tissues were dissected immediately and stored at −80 °C for further experiments.

## RNA-sequencing
The total amounts and integrity of RNA from mouse lung tissues were assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit with the 2100 Bioanalyzer system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Then, poly-T oligo–attached magnetic beads were used to purify mRNA from total RNA. In the First-Strand Synthesis Reaction Buffer (5×), fragmentations were performed using divalent cations under elevated temperatures and were used to build a library. The library was sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads. In brief, four fluorescently labeled dNTP, DNA polymerase, and splice primers were added to the sequenced flow cells and amplified. The sequencer captured these fluorescence signals and converted them into the sequencing peak, and then the sequence information of the measured fragments was obtained.
After sequencing, the pairs of terminal clean reads were aligned using HISAT2 (v2.0.5) after the index of the housekeeping genome was built. The read numbers were then calculated using FeatureCounts (v1.5.0-p3). The FPKM of each gene was calculated according to the length and read count. DESeq2 R package (1.20.0) was used for differential expression analysis. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment were analyzed using the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) tool (http://www.broadinstitute.org/gsea/index.jsp).

## Real-time quantitative PCR analysis
Total RNA was isolated using TRNzol (Tiangen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After quantification of the RNA concentration using Onedrop, a total of 500 ng RNA was reverse transcribed to synthesize cDNA using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara). The synthesized cDNA was then diluted 10 times. The quantitative PCR (qPCR) mix contained 1.5 μL of diluted cDNA as templates, 200 nM of each forward and reverse primer, and 1× Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (No Rox) (Yeasen). The reaction was run on a CFX96 Touch Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad) equipment with the following program: 95 °C for 5 min, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s, and then a melting curve from 65 °C to 95 °C with an increment of 0.5 °C for 5 s. Reactions were run in duplicates. The mean of PGK1 and HPRT1 (for human genes) or the mean of Pgk1 and Gapdh (for mouse genes) was used to normalize the acquired data, and the calculation was performed using the comparative Ct (2−ΔΔCt) method. Primer sequences used in this study are presented in Tables  and .

## Western blot
RIPA (Yeasen) was used to lyse cells, and a BCA kit (Thermofisher, USA) was applied to determine protein concentrations. For membrane and cytosol protein extraction, cells were collected and counted, and then membrane and cytosol proteins were extracted using a membrane and cytosol protein extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beyotime, P0033). SDS-PAGE electrophoresis was used to resolve the extracted proteins and the resolved proteins were then transferred to PVDF membranes (Bio-Rad). After that, the membranes were blocked in 5% milk in TBST and were then incubated overnight at 4 °C after adding indicated primary antibodies (Table  ). The next day, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The ECL Plus chemiluminescent system (Bio-Rad, USA) was applied to visualize bands.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Before adding cells, 12-well plates were placed with coverslips (Solarbio). On the indicated day after seeding cells, the coverslips with cells were removed from plates and fixed with ice-cold methanol for 10 min at −20 °C. Then, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies (Table  ) overnight at 4 °C. In the coming day, anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibodies were added to cells for 1 h at room temperature. After washed with 1× PBS Tween containing 20% FBS, DAPI Fluoromount G (SouthernBiotech) was added to cells to stain nuclei. The Nikon fluorescence microscope was used to acquire photos, and data were analyzed with Image J software.

## siRNA transfection
For the siRNA transfection experiments, the non-targeting siRNA control (siControl) and pool of siRNA targeting interested genes (Table  ) were transfected to the cells using lipofectamine™ 3000 transfection reagent (ThermoFisher, USA) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. In brief, cells were seeded in 12-well plates (SA-β-Gal or crystal violet staining: 2.5 × 105 cells per well; immunofluorescence staining: 4 × 105 cells per well) or 6-well-plates (RNA isolation: 5 × 105 cells per well; flow cytometry or western blot analysis: 8 × 105 cells per well). In the coming day, siRNAs, lipofectamine™ 3000, and DMEM without FBS or antibiotics were mixed for 15 min and added to the cells drop by drop. The next day, cells were changed with a normal growth medium. The final concentration of the siRNAs in the cells was 80 nM.

## Virus packaging and infection
The clopHensor vector was from GeneAdv China. In brief, lentivirus was packaged using 293 T cells after being transfected with gag-pol, env, and ClopHensor using Lipofectamine™ 3000 transfection reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific). Two days later, the supernatant from 293 T cells was collected, filtered, and mixed with a culture medium containing 8 μg/mL hexadimethrine bromide (Beyotime, China). Then the supernatant was dropped to target cells. The next day, cells were selected using 500 ng/ml puromycin (InvivoGen).

## Flow cytometry
Indicated cells from 6-well-plates were collected and incubated using different probes (Table  ) for 30 min at 37 °C temperature. Then, CytExpert for the DxFLEX (Beckman Coulter) platform was used for flow cytometry analysis. Data analysis was performed using the FlowJo v10 software.

## Senescence-associated β galactosidase assay and crystal violet staining
Regarding the SA-β-Gal staining experiment, cells were fixed for ≤5 min using 2% formaldehyde/0.2% glutaraldehyde after washed one time with 1× PBS. Then, the fixation solution was removed, and the cells were rinsed twice with 1× PBS. The SA-β-Gal staining solution was prepared according to ref. , and was then added to the cells. Cells were cultured overnight at 37 °C without CO2. Next day, remove the SA-β-Gal staining solution, wash and maintain the cells with 1× PBS.
For crystal violet staining experiment, cells were fixed for 5–15 min in 3.7% formaldehyde after washed one time with 1× PBS. Then remove fixation solution, rinse the cells twice with 1× PBS and stain the cells with 0.05% crystal violet staining solution overnight. Next day, remove the staining solution, wash the cells with 1× PBS, and scan the plates after drying.

## Co-immunoprecipitation
Protein A/G magnetic beads (MCE) were used for co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP) in this study. In brief, 293 T cells from 8 full dishes were collected and sonicated before protein extraction, and then the BCA kit (Thermo Fisher, USA) was used to determine protein concentrations. Subsequently, proteins were divided into the input, IgG, and CLIC3/ERK7 groups. At the same time, beads were incubated with the indicated antibodies (Table  ) for 2 h under 4 °C conditions. The extracted and divided proteins were mixed with the incubated beads overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the mixture was centrifuged, and the precipitates were collected after washing with lysis buffer more than 3 times. Then, the final samples were subjected to western blot analysis against indicated antibodies (Table  ).

## Statistics and reproducibility
Analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9. The D’Agostino & Pearson normality test was conducted before data analysis. Two-tailed student’s t-test was used for calculating P values for samples with two groups or single variable comparison. One-way or two-way ANOVA and subsequent Tukey’s multiple comparison tests were performed for multivariate comparisons with normal distribution. The Kruskal–Wallis and subsequent Dunn’s multiple comparison tests were used for non-normal distribution groups. Means are represented with ± SD. N was indicated in figure legends (ns, non-significant; *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001).
