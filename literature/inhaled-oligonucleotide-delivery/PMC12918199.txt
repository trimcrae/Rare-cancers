## Abstract
Extensive research has investigated the therapeutic potential of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) in treating infectious diseases and mitigating tissue damage caused by these diseases. MSCs, along with their exosomes, present a promising avenue for combating infectious diseases, particularly HACEK endocarditis (HE). MSCs possess immunomodulatory properties and aid in tissue repair, while exosomes carry bioactive molecules. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) released by MSCs demonstrate efficacy against a broad spectrum of microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, yeasts, and viruses. Key AMPs produced by MSCs include hepcidin, cathelicidin LL-37, and β-defensin-2. Furthermore, these AMPs have been found to interact with MSCs, influencing their proliferation, migration, and regeneration, indicating a broader biological impact than previously recognized. Preclinical studies have highlighted the potential of these AMPs to reduce inflammation, enhance cardiac function, and augment the efficacy of antibiotics against bacterial pathogens. This narrative review investigates the antimicrobial efficacy of MSCs, with specific emphasis on their therapeutic application against HACEK-associated infective endocarditis.

## Background
 Infective endocarditis (IE) is a significant cardiac condition characterized by inflammation of the endocardium, which can lead to severe complications [ ]. This inflammatory process is initiated by microbial infections from bacteria, fungi, or other microorganisms that enter the bloodstream and adhere to damaged cardiac areas [ ]. Fungal infections are particularly prevalent in individuals with specific cardiac devices or anatomical anomalies, complicating the pathophysiology of IE [ ]. For instance, patients with mitral stenosis, defined by the narrowing of the mitral valve, suffer from impaired valve function, obstructing blood flow into the left ventricle [ ].
IE pathogenesis progresses through preparatory valvular changes, bacterial adherence, and biofilm vegetation formation, which secretes virulence factors that degrade valvular tissue and create cavities, perpetuating the disease process [ – ]. Complications such as embolism arise when embolic material disrupts hemodynamic flow [ ]. Risk factors for IE include the presence of prosthetic heart valves, structural abnormalities of the heart, and congenital defects. The incidence of IE varies globally, with a notable gender predisposition observed, as males are generally more susceptible [ ]. The mortality rate associated with IE is approximately 25%, underscoring the importance of prompt therapeutic interventions. While the United States reports peak incidence rates, significantly lower rates are noted in Denmark, with a conspicuous lack of epidemiological data from regions such as Asia, Oceania, and Latin America [ , ].
The deployment of antibiotic therapy has significantly reduced mortality rates in cases of valve endocarditis, highlighting the importance of medical interventions. The HACEK group, comprising Haemophilus spp., Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Cardiobacterium hominis, Eikenella corrodens, and Kingella kingae, represents a diverse cluster of fastidious gram-negative bacteria that pose challenges to antibiotic treatment and have seen an increase in prevalence [ ]. Historically, these organisms were implicated in less than 5% of IE cases; however, recent studies indicate a prevalence ranging from 0.8% to 6% [ ]. The resistance of HACEK organisms to conventional antibiotics necessitates the exploration of innovative therapeutic strategies [ ].
Human mesenchymal stem or stromal cells (hMSCs), found in various anatomical sites such as bone marrow (BM-MSCs), adipose tissue (AT-MSCs), dental pulp (DP-MSCs), umbilical cord (UC-MSCs), placenta (PL-MSCs), and menstrual fluid (MenSCs), display versatile attributes. Their adaptability, homing ability to injured tissues, immune-privileged status, and lower tumorigenesis risk position them as promising candidates for cell-based therapies [ – ]. By leveraging their differentiation plasticity, immunomodulatory effects, angiogenic potential, and paracrine support, MSCs emerge as versatile tools with significant therapeutic potential [ – ]. Recent interest in utilizing MSCs for bacterial infection treatment is supported by in vitro studies demonstrating both direct and indirect mechanisms of action [ , , ]. MSCs have shown effectiveness in promoting bacterial clearance in preclinical models of sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and cystic fibrosis [ ]. Furthermore, MSCs express several antimicrobial peptides (AMPs)—including cathelicidin LL-37, human β-defensin-2 (hBD-2), hepcidin, and lipocalin-2 (Lcn2)—whose expression is modulated during infection and inflammation. For instance, bacterial preconditioning can induce the upregulation of LL-37, hBD-2, and hepcidin, while inflammatory stimuli can increase LL-37 and Lcn2 levels [ , ]. This review aims to explore the potential utilization of MSCs and MSC-derived exosomes (MSC-exos) as novel therapeutic approaches for HACEK endocarditis (HE), highlighting their mechanisms of action and clinical implications in managing this complex infectious disease.

## Methods
This narrative review employed a concept-driven literature search to gather relevant evidence on the antimicrobial potential of MSCs in HACEK endocarditis. Key databases—including PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar—were systematically explored from January 2000 to October 2025 using targeted keywords such as “mesenchymal stem cells,” “HACEK endocarditis,” “antimicrobial peptides,” “exosomes,” and “infective endocarditis.” Included studies comprised original preclinical research (in vitro and in vivo), clinical trials, and authoritative narrative or systematic reviews published in English. Non-peer-reviewed articles, case reports, and studies unrelated to MSC antimicrobial function or endocarditis pathophysiology were excluded. Although not a systematic review, this structured approach ensured comprehensive coverage of the evolving evidence base, enabling a cohesive narrative synthesis focused on therapeutic potential, mechanistic rationale, and translational challenges.

## Infectious endocarditis focuses on HACEK endocarditis
Despite recent advances in diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, IE continues to pose a formidable challenge with considerable mortality. The shifting demographic landscape, characterized by an aging population and a rising prevalence of healthcare-associated IE, has brought about significant changes in the epidemiology of the disease [ ]. IE is an uncommon condition, with an annual incidence estimated at 3 to 10 cases per 100,000 people. There is a notable gender discrepancy, with the disease occurring twice as frequently in men as compared to women. The current demographic of IE patients reflects an average age exceeding 65 years. This age trend is potentially linked to an increasing prevalence of predisposing factors in this population, such as artificial valves, acquired valve disease, diabetes mellitus, and hemodialysis [ , ]. Mortality rates associated with IE have demonstrated a range of 13 to 25%, with a noteworthy observation that 9–20% of patients experience mortality within the initial year following discharge. The IE mortality rate in Africa exceeds 26%. In developed countries, the annual incidence of IE is estimated at 3 to 9 cases per 100,000 individuals, with data indicating a rising trend between 1970 and 2013 [ , ]. In extensive cohort studies conducted across Europe, North America, and Oceania, staphylococci, streptococci, and enterococci were identified as responsible for 80–90% of IE cases. Although other microbes are detected in 5%−10% of IE instances, the latest European guidelines advocate targeting these gram-positive cocci for empirical treatment in severely unwell patients. The category of “other than staphylococci, streptococci, and enterococci” includes unusual microorganisms with documented tropism for cardiac valves, albeit accounting for a small proportion of IE cases. Recognizing the diversity of causative agents in IE is crucial for tailoring effective treatment strategies, particularly in cases involving unusual microorganisms [ ]. Rheumatic and congenital valvular heart diseases have traditionally stood out as paramount risk factors for the development of endocarditis, particularly among the younger population. However, in developed countries, the epidemiology of IE has experienced notable shifts, with emerging risk factors including the presence of intracardiac devices and degenerative valvular disease [ ].
HE predominantly impacts individuals with preexisting heart conditions or artificial valves. The condition is marked by a subtle and insidious progression, often leading to an average diagnostic delay ranging from 1 to 3 months. This group is inherent to the normal microbiota of the human oral and upper respiratory tract. Despite their low pathogenicity, they account for 1–3% of IE cases [ ]. HACEK microorganisms, characterized by slow growth and frequent colonization of the oropharynx, exhibit enhanced growth in culture medium supplemented with CO2. Standard blood culture methods may not detect these organisms unless enriched blood culture media are employed. These microorganisms contribute to various diseases, including periodontal infections, bacteremia, endocarditis, polymicrobial wound infections, otitis media, and abscesses. The pathogenesis of HE is attributed to bacterial colonization of the oropharynx, with subsequent dissemination to the vascular space triggered by trauma or local infection [ ]. IE results in the infection of the endocardium surface, with immune system-related illnesses manifesting across various organ systems. Cardiac manifestations in IE encompass valvular vegetation, abscess, periannular extension of infection, and myopericarditis. The variable presentation of IE—acute, subacute, or chronic—arises from diverse causative microorganisms, underlying cardiac conditions, and comorbidities. Symptoms like fever, night sweats, fatigue, and weight loss, alongside embolic phenomena in about 25% of patients, contribute to IE’s clinical complexity. Factors such as predisposing risk factors, heart murmurs, vasculitis, and embolic manifestations further contribute to IE development [ ]. Hemoculture serves as the primary diagnostic benchmark for IE, facilitating the detection of persistent bacteremia. Predominantly, positive hemoculture outcomes are prevalent in the majority of IE cases. However, in scenarios where blood cultures yield negative results despite clinical indications of IE, the adoption of adjunct microbiological testing methodologies becomes imperative. These modalities encompass serological assays and polymerase chain reaction (PCR), ensuring a comprehensive and precise diagnostic approach in diagnostically challenging cases [ ]. The implementation of continuously monitored automated blood culture systems, featuring enriched media, has significantly transformed the diagnostic landscape of HACEK bloodstream infections. This paradigm shift negates the necessity for prolonged blood culture incubation. Robust evidence from multiple studies indicates that over 99% of HACEK bacteria implicated in endocarditis can be reliably isolated within the standard 5-day incubation period [ ]. A comprehensive investigation into the correlation between HACEK bacteremia and the likelihood of endocarditis revealed notable heterogeneity within the HACEK group. The positive predictive value of positive blood culture(s) for endocarditis exhibited striking variability, ranging from 0% in the case of E. corrodens bacteremia to 100% for A. actinomycetemcomitans [ ]. Timely identification and intervention significantly impact the favorable prognosis of HE. Rigorous adherence to established guidelines is crucial, considering variables such as prosthetic versus native valve endocarditis, bacterial resistance profiles, and patient comorbidities. Medical intervention, primarily antibiotic therapy, achieves success in the majority of cases, with surgical options reserved for specific scenarios [ ]. Testing the antimicrobial susceptibility of HACEK isolates presents challenges due to their fastidious nature, limiting the efficacy of recommended methods. A study in Ontario, Canada, examining 241 HACEK isolates confirmed the difficulty in susceptibility testing, with notable resistance, especially in Aggregatibacter and Haemophilus spp. Resistance was often observed against multiple antimicrobial agents. Ceftriaxone emerged as the most effective cephalosporin, underscoring the utility of broad-spectrum cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones as optimal first-line therapies for HE. The intricate nature of these microorganisms may impede precise susceptibility testing, emphasizing the need for empirical therapeutic approaches [ ]. Although the HACEK group is typically not designated as challenging to treat or highly resistant, surgical intervention is warranted under certain circumstances. These include conditions such as heart failure, paravalvular extension, persistent infection despite appropriate antimicrobial treatment, and the need to prevent embolic events. These criteria for surgical intervention apply to both native and prosthetic valve IE caused by HACEK microorganisms [ , ].

## Mesenchymal stem cells
hMSCs are multipotent stem cells endowed with the ability for self-renewal and in vitro differentiation into diverse tissue types [ , ]. MSCs are categorized into two primary sources: adult MSCs, including those derived from BM, AT, peripheral blood, and DP, and neonatal tissue-derived MSCs obtained from the placenta, amniotic fluid, and UC. While BM-MSCs are the most commonly employed in clinical trials (CT), MSCs from birth-associated tissues exhibit noteworthy biological properties, including heightened proliferative capacity, extended lifespan, and versatile differentiation potential. Irrespective of the isolation source, extensive research is dedicated to investigating the regenerative capacity and immunomodulatory activity of MSCs in CT, particularly in the context of host versus graft reactions [ , ]. The fundamental criteria for characterizing hMSC include (a) their adherence to a plastic surface; (b) the expression of specific surface antigens, such as CD105, CD73, and CD90, while lacking expression of CD45, CD34, CD14 or CD11b, CD79a or CD19, and HLA-DR; and (c) their capacity for multipotent differentiation into osteoblasts, adipocytes, and chondroblasts under conventional in vitro tissue culture-differentiating conditions. Their adaptability, preference for homing to injured tissues, immune-privileged status, and reduced risk of tumorigenesis contribute to their attractiveness as a valuable tool in cell-based therapeutic applications [ – ]. Despite promising initial clinical outcomes associated with MSC-based therapy, there are substantial concerns regarding potential divergent effects on antimicrobial immunity, potentially leading to an elevated risk of infection [ , ]. Moreover, MSCs are generally considered immune-privileged due to low expression of MHC class II and costimulatory molecules; their immunogenic potential can increase with repeated dosing, donor variability, or prolonged culture. Tumorigenicity remains a concern, especially with long-term in vitro expansion, which may lead to chromosomal instability or transformation. Moreover, MSCs can interact with tumor microenvironments, potentially promoting angiogenesis or immune evasion [ ] (Table ).
 

## Mesenchymal stem cells and antimicrobial effect
MSC antimicrobial activity is divided into direct and indirect mechanisms. In an indirect capacity, they play a pivotal role in orchestrating the host immune response against pathogens by effectively balancing pro- and anti-inflammatory elements and enhancing phagocytic activity. On the direct front, MSCs actively contribute to antimicrobial defense through secreting a spectrum of AMPs, alongside expressing key molecules such as indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO) and interleukin (Fig. ) [ , ].

## hCAP-18/LL-37
The antimicrobial peptide hCAP-18/LL-37 (Cathelicidin), a member of the cathelicidin family, plays a pivotal role in both immunomodulation and direct antimicrobial activity. It is effective against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, primarily through bactericidal mechanisms. LL-37 targets multiple bacterial components beyond its characteristic disruption of bacterial membranes, making it a versatile molecule in combating infections [ , ].

## Hepcidin
Hepcidin is a key regulator of iron homeostasis in the body. It functions by inhibiting iron absorption in the small intestine and promoting the release of iron from reticuloendothelial cells. During infectious diseases, macrophages compete with bacteria for iron uptake. By impeding bacterial iron acquisition, hepcidin effectively inhibits pathogen growth and prevents its replenishment. This mechanism highlights the critical role of hepcidin in host defense against bacterial infections [ ].

## Lipocalin-2
Lipocalin-2 (LCN2) exerts its antimicrobial effects by binding to bacterial siderophores, which are molecules used by bacteria to scavenge iron. By sequestering iron from bacteria, LCN2 effectively inhibits bacterial growth. This function is particularly potent against pathogens such as Escherichia coli (E. coli), as LCN2 intercepts their siderophores. Notably, mice deficient in LCN2 exhibit increased susceptibility to infections and sepsis, underscoring the importance of this molecule in innate immunity [ , ].

## β-Defensins
β-Defensins are broad-spectrum AMPs that eliminate a wide range of invading microorganisms, including bacteria, viruses, and fungi. Beyond their direct antimicrobial activity, β-defensins also serve as immunoregulators, bridging innate and adaptive immune responses. Their dual functionality makes them essential components of the host defense system [ , ].

## Surfactant protein D
Surfactant protein D plays a crucial role in innate immunity by aggregating and opsonizing microbes, thereby enhancing their intracellular killing. Additionally, surfactant protein D directly impedes microbial growth by increasing membrane permeability through a mechanism dependent on its C-terminal domain. There is evidence suggesting that surfactant protein D may regulate the proliferation of Gram-negative bacteria in the airspace independently of macrophages, highlighting its multifaceted role in host defense [ , ].

## Indoleamine 2,3-Dioxygenase (IDO)
The antimicrobial action of IDO primarily occurs through the depletion of tryptophan, an essential amino acid required for bacterial growth. In addition to its antimicrobial effects, IDO exhibits immune regulatory functions, modulating the host immune response to infections. This dual role positions IDO as a key player in both antimicrobial defense and immune regulation [ – ].

## Cysteine proteases
Cysteine proteases destabilize biofilms formed by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), thereby enhancing the effectiveness of antibiotics that were previously tolerated by these biofilms. This mechanism highlights the potential of cysteine proteases as adjuvant therapies in combating antibiotic-resistant infections [ ].

## Inducible nitric oxide synthases (iNOS)
iNOS generates nitric oxide, a molecule with potent antimicrobial properties. Nitric oxide disrupts bacterial metabolism and contributes to the host defense against a wide range of pathogens. The generation of nitric oxide by iNOS underscores its importance in the innate immune response [ , ].

## Cystatin C
Cystatin C (CST3) exerts its antimicrobial effects by inhibiting bacterial growth and potentially contributing to membrane damage. While the exact mechanism remains under investigation, CST3’s ability to interfere with bacterial viability highlights its role in host defense [ , ].

## Innate defense regulator peptide IDR-1
The innate defense regulator peptide IDR-1 demonstrates significant anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This property suggests that IDR-1 may offer protection against MRSA-induced pneumonia in vivo. By modulating the inflammatory response, IDR-1 not only combats infections but also mitigates tissue damage caused by excessive inflammation [ , ].

## Elafin
Elafin, also known as peptidase inhibitor 3 (PI3), inhibits bacterial growth and may contribute to membrane damage. Its antimicrobial properties make it a valuable component of the host defense system, particularly in combating infections caused by pathogens that rely on proteolytic activity for virulence [ ]. Remarkably, preclinical models of sepsis, ARDS, and cystic fibrosis infection have underscored MSCs’ involvement in bacterial clearance [ , ]. MSCs exhibit constitutive expression of four AMPs: cathelicidin LL-37, hBD-2, hepcidin, and Lcn2. Importantly, these expressions are subject to modulation during infection and inflammation. Bacterial preconditioning of MSCs has been shown to induce an upregulation of LL-37, hBD-2, and hepcidin, while inflammatory stimuli during preconditioning lead to increased levels of LL-37 and Lcn2 (Table ; Fig. ). Experimental models further highlight that the AMPs derived from MSCs play a significant role in the observed bacterial clearance effect, emphasizing the direct enhancement of the innate immune response to bacterial infection by MSC treatment [ , ]. The antimicrobial efficacy inherent in MSCs via the mediation of AMPs has been investigated across distinct sources of stromal cells. Notably, diverse Mechanisms of Action (MoA) and variations in antibacterial range have been elucidated. These variances in the antimicrobial spectrum of MSCs potentially signify a specialized response aimed at the optimal production of AMPs, specifically tailored to counteract distinct pathogenic challenges [ , , ]. The investigation into the antimicrobial capabilities of BM-MSCs has centered on the pivotal role played by AMPs, notably LL-37 and hepcidin. The expression of LL-37 and hepcidin in both unstimulated and stimulated cultures of BM-MSCs within the human system has underscored their antimicrobial influence. BM-MSCs, when subjected to prior bacterial challenge, exhibit a direct impediment to the growth of E. coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), S. aureus, and Streptococcus pneumoniae. The inducible synthesis of LL-37 emerges as a discernible factor in inhibiting the in vitro growth of E. coli and P. aeruginosa. In contrast, antimicrobial peptides, namely hBD-2–3, Lcn2, and Surfactant Protein D (SP-D), manifest limited expression in BM-MSCs, lacking the requisite potency for eliciting antibacterial effects. BM-MSCs, through a contact-independent mechanism involving the release of LL-37, exhibit direct antimicrobial activity against S. aureus. This underscores the potential utility of BM-MSCs’ supernatants as adjunct therapeutic agents to conventional antibiotics. The upregulation of LL-37 levels following inflammatory preconditioning with IFNγ, IL-1β, or IL-12 stands in stark contrast to the undetected levels of hBD-2–3 in both unstimulated and stimulated BM-MSCs. Importantly, the expression of hepcidin in bacteria-stimulated BM-MSCs emerges as a central player in mediating both direct and conditioned medium-induced antimicrobial effects [ , , ]. The antimicrobial prowess of MSCs extends beyond AMPs. Upon inflammatory cytokine stimulation, BM-MSCs showcase an amplified expression of IDO, instigating a cell-autonomous, wide-ranging antimicrobial effect against clinically significant bacteria, protozoa (Toxoplasma gondii), and viruses (CMV, HSV-1). In murine BM-MSCs, IL-17-positive cells exhibit a robust antifungal response against C. albicans, with IL-17 playing a dose-dependent role; this effect is partially attenuated by anti-IL-17 antibodies. These findings underscore the intricate and varied antimicrobial mechanisms employed by MSCs [ , – ]. In vitro, UC-MSCs effectively inhibit E. coli growth. Microarray analysis shows upregulated TLR-2, TLR-4, and hBD-2 in UC-MSCs after E. coli exposure, suggesting TLR-4-mediated hBD-2 secretion contributes to antimicrobial effects. Antagonist or TLR-4 silencing abolishes hBD-2 increase and antibacterial effects, restored by hBD-2 supplementation. LL-37 and Lcn2 exhibit no significant upregulation [ , ]. The conditioned medium of Adipose Tissue-Derived MSCs (AT-MSCs) significantly reduced the growth rate of P. aeruginosa to levels comparable to the geneticin. Notably, the combination of AT-MSCs with geneticin demonstrated a potent antimicrobial effect against P. aeruginosa. This underscores the ability of AT-MSCs to enhance the antimicrobial effects of antibiotics, revealing a synergistic interaction between these stromal cells and geneticin [ , ]. Furthermore, in an E. coli-induced pneumonia model, administering BM-MSCs significantly reduced bacterial load, along with decreased total BAL cell and neutrophil counts, suggesting immune cell recruitment was not the primary clearance mechanism. Co-administration of BM-MSCs with a neutralizing anti-LL-37 antibody led to a tenfold increase in bacterial numbers, underscoring LL-37’s crucial role in BM-MSCs’ in vivo antimicrobial activity. Additionally, UC-MSCs demonstrated downregulation of the inflammatory response, enhanced bacterial clearance, increased hBD-2 secretion in BAL fluid, and protection against E. coli-induced pneumonia. Importantly, these antibacterial effects were nullified with TLR-4 siRNA transfection of UC-MSCs [ , , – ].
 
 

## Mesenchymal stem cell therapeutic effects in infection and injury models
In a porcine model of abdominal sepsis, both low- and high-dose groups treated with AT-MSCs demonstrated significant improvements in physical condition. These improvements were accompanied by elevated levels of IL-10 and TGF-β in the bloodstream, while concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1Ra, IL-1β, and IL-6 decreased noticeably. By the seventh day post-treatment, blood biomarker profiles were markedly enhanced in AT-MSC-treated subjects. Histological examination of tissue samples further revealed reduced inflammatory cell infiltration in peritoneal organs, indicating the anti-inflammatory and therapeutic potential of AT-MSCs in sepsis management [ ]. Preconditioning MSCs with antibiotics such as linezolid, vancomycin, meropenem, or cephalosporin at optimal concentrations significantly enhances their antimicrobial effects against MRSA and P. aeruginosa. This strategy not only preserves MSC viability but also represents a promising approach for combating antibiotic-resistant infections. The synergistic interaction between preconditioned MSCs and antibiotics highlights the potential of this combination therapy in addressing challenging microbial infections [ ]. The encapsulation of mouse AT-MSCs within collagen-fibrin hydrogel scaffolds demonstrated significant therapeutic benefits in a murine model of Bacteroides fragilis wound infection. AT-MSCs embedded in a collagen scaffold exhibited superior efficacy, reducing bacterial colony counts to 54 CFU/g compared to 97 CFU/g in a fibrin scaffold. Notably, the combined collagen-fibrin scaffold achieved the most substantial reduction in bacterial load, lowering colony counts to 29 CFU/g. This finding underscores the importance of scaffold selection in optimizing the antimicrobial effects of AT-MSCs [ ]. hMSC immunotherapy has shown promising anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial efficacy, particularly in conditions like cystic fibrosis (CF), which are characterized by chronic inflammation and recurrent infections. hMSCs modulate host immunity to resolve infections, offering a novel therapeutic avenue for managing CF-related complications. This dual functionality positions hMSCs as a valuable addition to the clinical armamentarium for CF treatment [ ]. In an equine model of joint infections caused by multidrug-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, the inclusion of Toll-like receptor (TLR)-activated BM-MSCs alongside vancomycin therapy resulted in significant improvements. Pain scores, ultrasound findings, quantitative bacterial counts, systemic neutrophil levels, serum amyloid A, synovial fluid lactate, and cytokine levels (IL-6 and IL-18) were all improved compared to the control group. These findings highlight the potential of TLR-activated MSCs as an adjunctive therapy in treating septic arthritis [ ]. The secretome of human dental pulp multipotent MSCs exhibits direct antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive (S. aureus) and Gram-negative (E. coli) bacteria, as well as oral pathogens such as Streptococcus mutans, Lactobacillus acidophilus, and Fusobacterium nucleatum. Specific components of the secretome, including hepatocyte growth factor, angiopoietin-1, IL-6, and IL-8, contribute to this antibacterial effect. This discovery suggests that dental pulp MSC secretome could serve as a novel therapeutic agent for combating bacterial infections [ ]. The application of a chitosan hydrogel incorporating placental multipotent mesenchymal stromal cell (MMSC) secretome (MSC-Ch-gel) effectively eradicated S. aureus from burn wounds in rats. No bacteria were detected in washings from the treated areas, and the treatment also reduced inflammation, promoted re-epithelialization, and facilitated the formation of well-vascularized granulation tissue. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of MSC-Ch-gel in managing infected burn wounds [ ]. hMSCs exhibit both antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties in vitro and in vivo, particularly in sustained infections caused by Mycobacterium avium and/or Mycobacterium intracellulare. This dual functionality makes hMSCs a promising candidate for treating chronic mycobacterial infections, where inflammation and bacterial persistence are major challenges [ ]. In a rodent model of polymicrobial sepsis induced by caecal ligation and puncture, CD362+-sorted umbilical cord-derived human mesenchymal stem cells (UC-hMSCs) demonstrated time-dependent efficacy. Administering UC-hMSCs 30 min after sepsis induction improved survival rates and reduced organ bacterial load in severe systemic sepsis. In moderate sepsis, delayed administration (4 h post-induction) was effective, enhancing serum lactate levels, facilitating bacterial clearance, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, and increasing AMPs in serum. These findings highlight the importance of timing in MSC-based therapies for sepsis management [ ]. Chitosan nanoparticles (CS-NPs) incorporated with mesenchymal stem cell-derived conditioned media (MSC-CM) demonstrated synergistic antibacterial activity against multidrug-resistant Vibrio cholerae strains. MSC-CM-CS-NPs effectively inhibited biofilm formation and enhanced bacterial eradication compared to control groups. Importantly, the antibacterial properties of MSC-CM remained intact even after nebulization, suggesting its potential for respiratory applications [ ]. Conditioned medium from adipose-derived human mesenchymal stem cells (AD-hMSCs) effectively suppressed the growth of S. aureus (ATCC 25923) under standard culture conditions, irrespective of the ongoing presence of ASCs. Treating ASCs with 1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D3 further enhanced their antimicrobial efficacy by increasing LL-37 expression. This finding highlights the potential of ASCs as a therapeutic agent for combating S. aureus infections [ ]. Conditioned media derived from BM and UC-MSCs effectively halted the proliferation of E. coli, S. aureus, and multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae clinical isolates. The antibacterial properties of MSC-CM persisted even after nebulization, with secreted AMPs such as IL-8 remaining largely intact. This suggests the potential of MSC-CM as a versatile therapeutic agent for respiratory and systemic infections [ ]. MSCs displayed notable spontaneous bactericidal activity against the MRSA strain S. aureus (USA300) in vitro. Additionally, soluble factors secreted by MSCs hindered biofilm formation and disrupted established biofilms. These secreted factors also synergistically enhanced the efficacy of antibiotics against drug-resistant bacteria. Activated MSCs reduced bacterial counts at wound sites and facilitated wound healing in S. aureus biofilm infections, highlighting their therapeutic potential in managing biofilm-associated infections [ ]. UC-hMSCs demonstrated direct antimicrobial capabilities against imipenem-resistant P. aeruginosa. Moreover, UC-hMSCs mitigated antibiotic resistance by preserving OprD2 expression in P. aeruginosa, suggesting their potential in reversing resistance mechanisms [ ]. Microvesicles (MVs) derived from human BM-MSCs enhanced alveolar fluid clearance and decreased lung protein permeability in an ex vivo perfused human lung model of severe E. coli pneumonia. Preconditioning MVs with polyinosinic: polycytidylic acid (Poly (I: C)) further augmented their antimicrobial efficacy, underscoring the potential of MV-based therapies in treating severe bacterial pneumonia [ ]. In mouse and dog models of chronic infections associated with biofilms, activated AT-MSCs combined with antibiotic therapy proved highly effective. Activated MSCs accumulated at wound margins, shifted infected tissue macrophages to an M2 phenotype, and enhanced bacterial killing partly through cathelicidin secretion. In pet dogs with chronic multidrug-resistant wound infections, repeated intravenous administration of activated allogeneic canine MSCs resulted in bacterial clearance and wound healing, demonstrating the translational potential of this approach [ ]. hMSC therapy attenuated acute lung injury induced by prolonged E. coli pneumonia in rats. This effect was attributed to enhanced macrophage phagocytic activity and increased concentrations of alveolar LL-37, leading to a reduction in lung bacterial burden. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of hMSCs in managing acute lung injury caused by bacterial infections [ ].In ex vivo perfused human lungs injured by live E. coli, BM-MSCs restored alveolar fluid clearance, alleviated inflammation, and exhibited antimicrobial activity. These effects were partially mediated by the secretion of keratinocyte growth factor, underscoring the multifaceted therapeutic potential of BM-MSCs in treating severe bacterial infections [ ]. ## Mesenchymal stem cells in HACEK endocarditis
Multiple studies have highlighted the ability of MSCs to regulate bacterial infections, as demonstrated in both in vivo and ex vivo models (Table ). The immunomodulatory characteristics of MSCs, particularly their influence on macrophages and neutrophils, play a pivotal role in shaping the immune response against bacterial pathogens. This complex interaction illustrates the therapeutic significance of MSCs in treating bacterial infections and points to the necessity for ongoing research into their use in infectious diseases [ ]. The antimicrobial attributes of MSCs emanate from their immunomodulatory prowess, orchestrated through the secretion of a spectrum of immunomodulators, including transforming growth factor beta (TGF-ß), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), prostaglandin E2, and AMPs like LL-37. This modulation encompasses both cell contact-dependent and independent mechanisms, affording MSCs the capacity to exert direct bactericidal effects and indirect regulation of the immune response’s acute phase. The intricate orchestration of these immunomodulatory pathways underscores the scientific intricacy and potential therapeutic significance of MSCs in the context of antimicrobial defense [ ]. Numerous in vivo experimental investigations have demonstrated the efficacy of allogeneic or xenogeneic MSCs across diverse disorders, encompassing conditions such as sepsis, hepatic failure, acute renal failure, and myocardial infarction [ ]. Regarding the tissue repair functions of MSCs, it was found that in ischemic rat myocardium, MSCs genetically modified via ex vivo retroviral transduction to overexpress Akt1, a pro-survival gene crucial for cell survival, inhibited cardiac remodeling by mitigating intramyocardial inflammation, collagen deposition, and cardiac myocyte hypertrophy. Moreover, Akt1-transduced MSCs restored myocardial volume more effectively than control-transduced cells, with observed effects dependent on cell dosage. Thus, Akt1-enhanced MSCs show promise for repairing infarcted myocardium, preventing remodeling, and improving cardiac performance [ ]. Intramyocardial transplantation of MSCs stands out as a promising approach for clinical intervention and therapy, given their potent capacity for cardiac repair. Serving as attractive tools for both pre-clinical and clinical trials, MSCs secrete a diverse range of soluble factors that effectively influence pathogenic cardiac remodeling, fibrosis, immune activation, and cardiac stem cell proliferation within damaged hearts. Moreover, MSCs demonstrate the ability to differentiate into cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and vascular smooth muscle cells, further enhancing their therapeutic potential [ ]. Within the cardiovascular system, MSCs exhibit a multifaceted protective role in myocardial preservation. They mitigate inflammation, promote differentiation of myocardial cells in infarcted regions, stimulate angiogenesis, enhance resistance to apoptosis, and hinder fibrosis. These properties render MSCs highly suitable for cardiovascular regenerative applications. In infectious cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), such as IE, immune cells are recruited to the site of infection, initiating an inflammatory cascade that can evolve into subclinical or chronic pathological states. In addition to MSCs differentiating into cardiovascular progenitor cells, extracellular vesicles (EVs) released by MSCs play a vital role in regulating cellular functions. These MSC-derived EVs have been shown to enhance cardiomyocyte autophagy via the AMPK/mTOR and Akt/mTOR pathways, promote angiogenesis through the hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α)/Jagged 1 pathway, and suppress cell apoptosis (via inhibition of survivin expression) while activating cell survival signaling pathways through various microRNAs (miRs). Moreover, recent studies have explored the genetic modification and coupling of MSCs with biological materials [ ]. Human MSC-exos enhance angiogenesis, proliferation, and migration of cardiac microvascular endothelial cells (CMECs), although miR-543 inhibition limits this effect. Upregulating miR-543 suppresses COL4A1 protein expression in HEK293T cells, and mutating the predicted COL4A1 site disrupts this association. In vivo, MSC-Exos significantly alleviate myocardial necrosis, neutrophil infiltration, and myocardial injury in rats with myocardial infarction (MI), promoting cardiomyocyte proliferation while upregulating miR-543 and suppressing COL4A1 [ ]. Beyond cellular therapy, MSC-derived exosomes represent a promising cell-free alternative for HE treatment. These nanovesicles carry a cargo of antimicrobial peptides (e.g., LL-37, hepcidin), immunomodulatory miRNAs (e.g., miR-543, miR-146a), and proteins that can simultaneously suppress HACEK growth and promote valvular repair. Specifically, exosomal miR-543 has been shown to downregulate COL4A1, a collagen component that impedes tissue remodeling, while LL-37 and hepcidin directly target Gram-negative membranes and iron metabolism, respectively. Importantly, exosomes lack the thrombogenic and immunogenic risks associated with whole-cell infusion, making them particularly suitable for endocarditis patients at high risk of embolism. Recent studies demonstrate that exosomes retain their bioactivity even after nebulization or lyophilization, suggesting feasible delivery routes for cardiac-targeted therapy. Injection of antimicrobial functions, MSCs may be a treatment candidate for further investigation in IE. MSCs mitigate tissue damage during infection by reducing the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, curbing excessive neutrophil recruitment, and limiting T cell proliferation at the injury site. Additionally, MSCs contribute to tissue regeneration either by differentiating into the necessary cell types or by releasing paracrine factors that facilitate tissue regeneration, differentiation, and wound healing [ ]. Furthermore, MSCs harbor both TLR and FPLR receptors, crucial for detecting pathogen-associated molecular patterns, suggesting their potential involvement in innate immune responses. Additionally, LL-37 can activate MSCs via a concentration gradient, eliciting their migration toward the site of infection [ ]. Exosomes derived from BM-MSCs were discovered to induce autophagy while simultaneously inhibiting apoptosis in hepatocytes under D-GalN/LP-induced conditions and in the case of coxsackievirus B3-induced myocarditis. Treatment with BM-MSCs-derived exosomes was observed to regulate AMPK/mTOR signaling in human cardiomyocytes in vitro, thereby promoting their survival [ ]. While this study provides valuable insights into the roles of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) in HACEK endocarditis, it is important to acknowledge several limitations. Firstly, the preclinical models utilized may not fully replicate the complexity of human infections, particularly the nuances of endocardial biofilm formation and systemic responses. Additionally, the translational applicability of our findings may be constrained by the inherent differences between animal models and human pathophysiology.

## Biological rationale for targeting HACEK with MSCs
Despite the absence of direct preclinical studies evaluating MSCs in HE models, a strong biological rationale supports their therapeutic potential. HACEK organisms are fastidious Gram-negative bacilli that share key pathophysiological features with pathogens for which MSC efficacy has been robustly demonstrated. Notably, HACEK species are capable of forming biofilms on cardiac valves, a hallmark of persistent infective endocarditis that limits antibiotic penetration and promotes immune evasion [ , ]. MSCs, particularly through the secretion of cysteine proteases and AMPs such as LL-37 and β-defensin-2, have been shown to disrupt biofilms formed by S. aureus and enhance antibiotic efficacy in chronic wound and joint infection models [ , ].
Furthermore, HACEK bacteria are Gram-negative and possess LPS-rich outer membranes, rendering them susceptible to AMPs that target membrane integrity. LL-37, hepcidin, and lipocalin-2—constitutively expressed and inducible in human MSCs—have demonstrated potent activity against Gram-negative pathogens including E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and K. pneumoniae [ ]. For instance, BM-MSCs significantly reduced E. coli burden in sepsis and pneumonia models via LL-37-dependent mechanisms, while UC-MSCs suppressed P. aeruginosa growth through TLR4-mediated β-defensin-2 secretion [ ].
In addition to direct antimicrobial effects, MSCs modulate the dysregulated immune response characteristic of HE. Persistent valvular inflammation in endocarditis leads to tissue destruction and embolic complications [ , ]; MSCs mitigate this by polarizing macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, reducing neutrophil infiltration, and secreting regenerative factors such as keratinocyte growth factor and VEGF [ , ]. Given these multimodal actions—biofilm disruption, Gram-negative bacterial clearance, inflammation resolution, and tissue repair—MSCs represent a biologically plausible and mechanistically justified therapeutic strategy for HACEK endocarditis, warranting targeted preclinical investigation.

## Future directions
The therapeutic landscape for HACEK endocarditis is evolving with the integration of MSCs and their exosomes, offering a promising avenue for clinical applications. Future research should focus on enhancing the efficacy of MSC therapies in conjunction with conventional antibiotics, particularly against resistant strains. Investigating optimal combinations of MSC therapy with existing antibiotic regimens and preconditioning MSCs with specific antimicrobial agents may yield synergistic effects that improve patient outcomes. Employing sophisticated animal models that accurately replicate human pathophysiology, including biofilm formation and immune response, will provide valuable insights into MSC behavior and efficacy. The transition from preclinical findings to clinical applications requires well-designed trials to evaluate the safety, efficacy, and optimal dosing of MSC therapies, considering factors such as timing relative to antibiotic administration and delivery routes. Additionally, standardization of MSC isolation and characterization processes is crucial for consistent therapeutic outcomes, alongside addressing challenges in exosome production and characterization. Establishing robust long-term monitoring protocols to assess safety and efficacy is essential. By focusing on these areas, researchers can pave the way for innovative MSC-based treatments for HACEK endocarditis, balancing clinical relevance with foundational research to tackle this complex infectious disease effectively.

## Safety considerations in MSC-Based therapy for endocarditis
While MSCs are generally regarded as safe due to their low immunogenicity and lack of MHC class II expression under standard conditions, several safety concerns must be addressed before clinical translation in HACEK endocarditis. First, repeated systemic administration—particularly in immunocompromised or critically ill patients—may induce MHC class II upregulation, triggering alloreactive T-cell responses [ , , ]. Second, although tumorigenicity is rare with early-passage MSCs, long-term in vitro expansion (> 10 passages) can lead to chromosomal instability and potential malignant transformation [ ]. Third, in the context of endocarditis, intravenous delivery of MSCs may theoretically interact with vegetation-associated thrombi, as MSCs express tissue factor and may modulate coagulation pathways [ , ]. Preclinical thrombosis models in IE are needed to evaluate this risk. Finally, donor-to-donor variability in MSC potency and secretome composition necessitates rigorous quality control and functional standardization prior to clinical use [ , ].

## Limitations of current preclinical models
A critical barrier to translating MSC therapy for HACEK endocarditis lies in the inadequacy of existing preclinical models. Most studies demonstrating MSC efficacy against bacterial infections employ models of sepsis, pneumonia, or skin wounds—conditions that do not replicate the unique microenvironment of endocardial vegetations, characterized by biofilm-embedded bacteria, low shear stress, and limited immune cell penetration. Moreover, HACEK endocarditis is typically indolent, with symptoms developing over weeks to months, whereas common rodent IE models (e.g., S. aureus catheter-induced) are acute and highly inflammatory. The lack of a validated HACEK-specific IE model—ideally using A. actinomycetemcomitans, the HACEK species with the highest positive predictive value for endocarditis—hinders mechanistic validation. Future work should prioritize the development of chronic, biofilm-based IE models in large animals (e.g., swine or canine) that better mimic human valvular pathology and pharmacokinetics.

## Conclusion
Future efforts must prioritize the development of HACEK-specific endocarditis animal models—ideally using A. actinomycetemcomitans in large animals with chronic biofilm-associated valvular vegetations—to directly validate the therapeutic efficacy and safety of MSC-based interventions. The application of MSCs and their exosomes in the treatment of IE offers considerable therapeutic promise. However, to fully realize this potential, it is crucial to address several challenges related to exosome production and standardization. Current methodologies often result in prolonged procedural timelines and inefficient isolation techniques, which can lead to suboptimal exosome yields. These issues not only impede clinical efficacy but also complicate the process of ensuring consistent quality and reproducibility of exosomes for therapeutic use. Moreover, effective clinical application necessitates careful consideration of dose optimization and delivery routes. Establishing the appropriate dosage is vital to maximize therapeutic benefits while minimizing potential side effects. Additionally, the routes of delivery must be strategically chosen to enhance bioavailability and target specific tissues, particularly in the context of cardiac applications. Concerns regarding off-target effects also warrant attention, as the diverse composition of exosomes—comprising various bioactive factors—may inadvertently affect non-target tissues, leading to unintended consequences. Therefore, a nuanced understanding of the exosomal content and its functional activities is essential for mitigating these risks. In summary, while MSC-derived exosomes hold great promise for the treatment of HACEK IE, advancing this field requires comprehensive research to address the challenges of production, standardization, dose optimization, delivery mechanisms, and potential off-target effects. A focused exploration of these aspects is critical for translating this innovative therapeutic approach into effective clinical applications. Future efforts must prioritize the development of HACEK-specific endocarditis animal models to directly validate the therapeutic efficacy and safety of MSC-based interventions.
