## Abstract
GLI1, a key transcription factor of the Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway, plays an important role in the development of cancer. However, the function and mechanisms by which GLI1 regulates gene transcription are not fully understood in gastric cancer (GC). Here, we found that GLI1 induced the proliferation and metastasis of GC cells, accompanied by transcriptional upregulation of INHBA. This increased INHBA expression exerted a promoting activity on Smads signaling and then transcriptionally activated GLI1 expression. Notably, our results demonstrate that disrupting the interaction between GLI1 and INHBA could inhibit GC tumorigenesis in vivo. More intriguingly, we confirmed the N6‐methyladenosine (m6A) activation mechanism of the Helicobacter pylori/FTO/YTHDF2/GLI1 pathway in GC cells. In conclusion, our study confirmed that the GLI1/INHBA positive feedback loop influences GC progression and revealed the mechanism by which H. pylori upregulates GLI1 expression through m6A modification. This positive GLI1/INHBA feedback loop suggests a novel noncanonical mechanism of GLI1 activity in GC and provides potential therapeutic targets for GC treatment.
Keywords: gastric cancer, GLI1, INHBA, m6A, progression

## INTRODUCTION
Gastric cancer is the fifth most common malignant tumor and the fourth most common cause of cancer‐related death worldwide. Patients with advanced gastric cancer (GC) have a poor prognosis, with a median survival of less than 1 year. Therefore, it is of great clinical importance to decipher the mechanism underlying GC progression and discover new therapeutic targets that will benefit patients in treatment.
The Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway is a central pathway in targeted cancer therapy. Recent work has shown that GLI1, a key downstream effector transcription factor of the Hh signaling pathway, plays an important role in the development of a number of cancers including GC. , , , , , GLI1 may be a driver of oncogenesis and its overexpression promotes tumorigenesis in murine mammary tissue. The effect of GLI1 is achieved through the transcription of downstream target genes such as MGMT, 
SDF1
 and MMP2/9. However, the function and mechanisms by which GLI1 operates to regulate gene transcription are not well understood in GC.
INHBA is an important ligand of transforming growth factor‐β (TGF‐β) signaling that activates the downstream Smad2/Smad3 signaling pathway by binding to serine/ threonine kinase receptors. Upon binding to its serine/threonine kinase receptors, Smad2/Smad3 is phosphorylated and interacts with Smad4. The Smad2/3/4 complex accumulates in the nucleus and binds to the promoter of target genes to regulate their transcription. Preliminary studies have suggested that INHBA is highly expressed in GC and related to disease progression. , , Transforming growth factor‐β signaling has been reported is a strong inducer of GLI1 expression as well as GLI‐dependent transcription. However, the possible interaction between TGF‐β signaling and GLI1 in GC has not been deciphered clearly.
Gastric carcinogenesis and progression are also influenced by epigenetic modifications. Recent research has shown that altered N6‐methyladenosine (m6A) methylation patterns caused by Helicobacter pylori infection of gastric epithelial cells are involved in the occurrence and development of GC. , , m6A is the most common modification in eukaryotic mRNAs, which is a dynamic and reversible process regulated by methylases (writers) and demethylases (erasers). Accumulating evidence shows that eraser FTO and reader YTHDF2 are involved in different stages of many types of hematomas and solid tumors and could be promising targets for cancer therapy. , GLI1 has been reported to be modified by METTL3/14 and IGF2BP2 is responsible for its sustained activation. Therefore, it will be interesting and important to determine whether H. pylori is involved in regulating the GLI1 expression through m6A modification and then contributes to the high expression of GLI1 in GC.
In this work, in order to better understand the GLI1‐mediated signaling pathway in GC, we identified direct GLI1 target genes using RNA sequencing. We discovered GLI1 elevated expression of INHBA. They form a positive feedback loop, wherein GLI1 transcriptionally upregulates INHBA, and conversely INHBA activates the downstream Smad2/Smad4 signaling pathway and Smad4 transcriptionally upregulates GLI1. Most importantly, our results indicated that disrupting the interaction between GLI1 and INHBA could inhibit the progression of GC. By investigating the upstream regulatory mechanisms of GLI1, we found that H. pylori infection activated GLI1 expression and significantly decreased the methylation level of the GLI1 promoter region in an m6A‐dependent manner.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS
2.1.

## Clinical samples
A total of 107 tumor samples from patients diagnosed with GC were collected at Jinan Central Hospital. These samples were collected consecutively from September 2018 to November 2021. This study was carried out in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Jinan Central Hospital. Informed consent was obtained from each patient involved in the research.

2.2.

## Cell lines and cell culture
Human GC cell lines AGS, HGC‐27, MGC‐803, MKN‐45, and MKN‐28 were purchased from IBCB (Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology, Chinese Academy of Science). AGS cells were cultured in Ham‐F12 medium (MACGENE) with 10% FBS (Hyclone). Other GC cell lines were maintained in DMEM (MACGENE) supplemented with 10% FBS. All these cells were cultured in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37°C.

2.3.

## 
Helicobacter pylori cultures
The H. pylori standard strains NCTC 11637, NCTC 26695, and PM SS1 were generously provided by Dr. Jihui Jia from the Department of Medical Microbiology at Shandong University. Helicobacter pylori strains were cultured in H. pylori Medium (Hopebio) supplemented with 7% defibrinated sheep blood (Hopebio). The cultures were maintained under microaerobic conditions at 37°C with 5% O2, 10% CO2, and 85% N2. Identification of the bacteria was undertaken through various tests including Gram staining, oxidase test, catalase test, and urease test. The concentration of bacteria was determined using Densimat.

2.4.

## RNA isolation and real‐time quantitative RT‐PCR
TRIzol reagent (CWBIO) was used for RNA extraction. RNA was quantified using a NanoDrop (Thermo Fisher Scientific). HiFiScript gDNA removal cDNA synthesis kit (CWBIO) and RevertAid RT kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used for reverse transcribed. Quantitative RT‐PCR (qRT‐PCR) was carried out using the UltraSYBR Mixture (Low ROX; CWBIO) on a LightCycler 480 Real‐Time PCR System (Roche). β‐Actin was regarded as internal standard control to normalize the data. The sequences of the primers are listed in Table  .

2.5.

## Western blot analysis
Total proteins were extracted with RIPA lysis buffer (CWBIO). The proteins were separated by 10% SDS‐PAGE and then electrophoretically transferred to the PVDF membranes. After blotting with 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature, the membranes were hybridized with primary Ab at 4°C overnight. Following hybridization, the membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary Abs and the bands were detected with an ECL detection reagent (Millipore). Details of the primary Abs and their respective dilutions can be found in Table  .

2.6.

## CCK‐8 assay
Cell proliferation was assessed with the CCK‐8 kit (Elabscience). Cells were added to 96‐well plates (3500 cells/well) before adding 10 μL CCK‐8 reagent to each well. Cell proliferation was determined by examining the absorbance at a wavelength of 450 nm after 2 h of incubation at 37°C.

2.7.

## 5‐Ethynyl‐2′deoxyuridine staining assay
Cell proliferation was assessed using the EdU staining assay (Elabscience). For the EdU assay (RiboBio), after seeding the cells in 96‐well culture plates (3500 cells/well), the cells were treated with EdU solution (100 μL/well) for 2 h, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, then stained with Apollo and Hoechst 33342.

2.8.

## Wound healing assay
Cells (1 × 105) were seeded into a 24‐well plate and grown. A wound was scratched vertically using a pipette tip and washed with PBS. After 48 or 72 h, the wound area was photographed until confluence. The wound areas were measured using ImageJ (version 1.51j8; National Institutes of Health).

2.9.

## Transwell assay
The Transwell was used to detect the migratory ability of GC cells. Cells (1 × 105) were suspended in 200 μL serum‐free medium and seeded into a 24‐well Boyden chamber (8 μm pore size; Corning), and 600 μL of 20% FBS medium was supplemented with the lower chamber. After 48 or 72 h, respectively, 4% formaldehyde and 0.1% crystal violet were used to fix and stain the cells covered in the lower surface of the chambers, respectively. Erasing the cells in the upper chamber, then counting the remaining cells adhered to the bottom chambers at 100× magnification under the microscope.

2.10.

## Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay
The total of m6A content in the treated GC cells was then determined using the EpiQuik m6A RNA Methylation Quantitative Kit (Epigentek). Briefly, negative and diluted positive control RNA was and 300 ng RNA were added to each well, followed by the addition of a mixture of capture Abs and detection Abs. After adding enhancer and color developing solutions, m6A signal was quantified colorimetrically by reading the absorbance at a wavelength of 450 nm.

2.11.

## Dot blot analysis
To examine the global mRNA levels, the m6A dot blot assay was carried out. Mark a grid on the nitrocellulose (NC) membrane with each sample measuring 0.7 cm × 0.7 cm. Total RNAs were extracted from every group, and spot them to the NC membrane. After being dried, 65°C for 30 min incubation was undertaken to eliminate secondary structure and then cross‐link it with UV for 10 min, then the membrane was blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. Anti‐m6A Ab (diluted at 1:3000) was added to above, treated RNA solution, and maintained overnight at 4°C. On the second day, after being washed three times with PBST for a total of 15 min, the membrane was incubated with HRP‐conjugated anti‐rabbit IgG secondary Ab at room temperature for 45 min, followed by five washes with 1× PBST buffer. The membrane was blotted using the ECL Western Blotting Detection Kit. Prepare a 0.02% methylene blue solution by dissolving 0.1% methylene blue in sodium acetate and staining the bands with this solution for 15 min as an internal reference.

2.12.

## Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay
The ChIP assay was carried out with the truChIP Chromatin Shearing Kit (Covaris) according to the manufacturer's protocol. In brief, GC cells were fixed with PBS containing 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. The cross‐linked chromatin DNA was sheared by sonication, followed by immunoprecipitation using an anti‐Flag Ab (CST). Normal rabbit IgG was included as a negative control. The chromatin Ab complexes were precipitated with magnetic beads and then analyzed by quantitative PCR (qPCR). The primers used for ChIP‐qPCR are listed in Table  .

2.13.

## Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation
Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP) was carried out using the standard manufacturer's protocol (Bersinbio). Briefly, total RNA was isolated from MKN‐45 cell by TRIzol and fragmented into 200–300 nt with RNA fragmentation reagents. Subsequently, RNA was incubated with anti‐m6A Abs or anti‐IgG (4°C with mixing, 4 h). After immunoprecipitation, RNA was eluted from the beads. The eluted RNA was subjected to qRT‐PCR analysis. The primers used for ChIP‐qPCR are listed in Table  .

2.14.

## Actinomycin D treatment and RNA stability assay
MKN‐45 cells were incubated with 2 mg/mL actinomycin D (MedChemExpress), then the total RNA was harvested at different times (0, 3, 6, 9, and 12 h). The relative remaining mRNA was detected by qRT‐PCR.

2.15.

## Animal experiments
We developed subcutaneous GC animal xenograft models to evaluate carcinogenicity in vivo. Twenty four mice (BALB/c nude, 5 weeks old) were randomly divided into four groups (NC + empty vector [EV], criGLI1 + EV, NC + INHBA, and criGLI1 + INHBA). Cells (7 × 106 in 100 μL PBS/group) were injected subcutaneously into the left flanks of nude mice. The volume (V = length × width2 × 0.5) of xenograft tumors was measured every other day. In addition, we developed tail vein metastasis models to evaluate the metastasis characteristics. Similarly, cells were injected into the tail vein of 24 nude mice. The mice were anesthetized with carbon dioxide and decapitated after 4 weeks, then the subcutaneous tumors were harvested and weighed. Liver and lungs of mice were dehydrated, fixed, and subjected to H&E staining (Solarbio) according to the manufacturer's instructions.
We also created an H. pylori‐infected mouse model. Twelve C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into two groups. Mice in each group were treated with 10% amoxicillin (0.2 g/kg), followed by gavage and water fasting for 12 h. This was followed by gavage treatment (0.2 mL/each) using 5% sodium bicarbonate solution with fasting and water deprivation for 1 h. Group 1 mice (control group, n = 6) were treated with PBS gavage. The mice from group 2 (SS1 group, n = 6) were infected with the SS1 strain by oral gavage (1 × 109 colony‐forming units/mouse) for 5 days. After 4 months of normal feeding, the mice were killed by carbon dioxide inhalation and dissected to examine their stomach tissue for further detailed analysis.

2.16.

## Immunohistochemistry analysis and H&E staining
Tumor tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and then embedded in paraffin. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), paraffin‐embedded slices (5 μm thick) were dewaxed twice in fresh xylene, followed by hydration using gradient alcohol. The slices were subjected to high‐temperature antigen retrieval in EDTA antigen retrieval buffer for 15 min. Sections were incubated with the primary Ab overnight at 4°C and then incubated with biotin‐labeled secondary Abs at room temperature for 1 h and finally stained with 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride. For H&E staining, we used an H&E staining kit (Solarbio) to directly stain the nucleus and cytoplasm. Following the staining process, the tissue slices were dehydrated using progressively higher concentrations of ethanol and xylene. To preserve the stained samples, we sealed them with neutral balsam. Information about the primary and secondary Abs used in IHC and H&E are listed in Table  .

2.17.

## Methylene blue staining for H. pylori

Stomach tissue was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and then embedded in paraffin. Paraffin‐embedded slices were deparaffinized twice in fresh xylene and then hydrated with gradient alcohol. Helicobacter pylori staining solution (Leagene) was used to directly stain the nucleus and cytoplasm. After the staining procedure, the tissue slices were dehydrated using progressively higher concentrations of ethanol and xylene, followed by sealed them with neutral balsam to preserve the stained samples.

2.18.

## siRNA and transfection
siRNA were purchased from RiboBio (Guangzhou). Transfections were carried out using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent. Sequences are listed in Table  .

2.19.

## Statistical analysis
GraphPad Prism 8.0 was used for statistical analysis. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments. Student's t‐test was used to determine the significance of differences between two groups, while one‐way ANOVA was used to analyze the significance of differences among multiple groups. The relationship between GLI1, INHBA, and clinicopathologic variables was determined by χ2‐test. Spearman's correlation was used to determine the expression correlation of two genes. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## RESULTS
3.1.

## GLI1 increases INHBA expression in GC
To interpret the key roles of GLI1 in GC, we first determined the protein levels of GLI1 in five GC cell lines. Western blot assay confirmed that the expression of GLI1 in MKN‐45 and AGS cell lines was lower than in other GC cell lines (Figure  ).We then infected MKN‐45 and AGS cells with the stable GLI1 overexpression vector, followed by transfection of EV (Figure  ). In contrast, the highest GLI1 expression was observed in HGC‐27 cells, so we constructed a GLI1 KO HGC‐27 cell line using CRISPER/Cas9 technology (Figure  ). To identify gene networks associated with GLI1 expression and GC development, we undertook RNA sequencing on GC cells with stable GLI1 overexpression. Gene enrichment pathway (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) analyses revealed that GLI1 downstream genes were preferentially enriched in the TGF‐β/Smad signaling pathway (Figure  ). The heatmaps of the top genes were then created to show their global expression changes. It showed remarkable upregulation of INHBA expression within the TGF‐β/Smad signaling pathway upon GLI1 overexpression (Figure  ). To gain insight into the relevance between GLI1 and INHBA in GC, we undertook an analysis using The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) datasets. Our results showed a positive correlation between the expression levels of GLI1 and INHBA in GC (Figure  ).
 
3.2.

## GLI1 and INHBA expression correlates with clinicopathologic characteristics and poor prognosis in GC
The clinical relevance of GLI1 and INHBA expression was analyzed in clinical GC samples from datasets. We searched the online UALCAN database and found that the mRNA expression of GLI1 and INHBA was consistently higher in GC tissues than those adjacent normal gastric tissues (Figure  ). Survival analysis using Kaplan–Meier plotter showed that patients with high GLI1 or INHBA expression had a shorter overall survival (OS) and disease‐free survival time (Figure  ). To assess the protein expression of GLI1 and INHBA in clinical GC samples, we undertook western blot analyses and IHC assay. The expression levels of GLI1 and INHBA protein in the GC tissues were also significantly higher than those in the normal tissues (Figures  and ). Analysis of the relationship between GLI1 and INHBA expression and clinicopathologic parameters showed that high GLI1 expression was positively associated with H. pylori infection (p = 0.006), degree of differentiation (p = 0.011), N classification (p = 0.024), and clinical staging (p = 0.013) (Table  ). The expression of INHBA significantly increased with increasing T classification (p = 0.02), N classification (p < 0.001), and clinical staging (p < 0.001) (Table  ).
 
3.3.

## GLI1 and INHBA form a positive feedback loop in GC cells
To further support the role of INHBA as a downstream effector of GLI1 in regulating gastric carcinogenesis, rescue experiments were carried out. As expected, upon overexpression of GLI1, the protein levels of INHBA, Smad2, N‐cadherin, survivin, and Bcl‐2 were significantly induced. These inductions were partially rescued by treating with INHBA knockdown (Figure  ). Furthermore, the inhibition of INHBA effectively counteracted the increase in expression of Smad2, proliferation, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition molecules induced by GLI1 overexpression (Figure  ). The TCGA analysis revealed a positive correlation between GLI1 and Smad2 mRNA expression in GC tissues (Figure  ). We also confirmed that Smad2 was regulated by GLI1 and INHBA at the transcriptional level by RT‐qPCR (Figure  ). Notably, decreased INHBA could downregulate the expression of GLI1, suggesting that GLI1 and INHBA form a positive feedback loop in GC cells.
 
3.4.

## GLI1‐INHBA feedback loop regulates GC cell proliferation and migration
We next investigated whether GLI1 can promote the proliferation and migration of GC cells through cooperation with INHBA. As shown in Figures  and , the overexpression of GLI1 significantly promoted cell proliferation of MKN‐45 and AGS cells, which was markedly restored by INHBA knockdown. In contrast, INHBA overexpression reverses the decrease in cell proliferation caused by GLI1 KO. Accordingly, Transwell assays and wound healing assays revealed that INHBA significantly suppressed cell migration of GLI1‐overexpressed MKN‐45 and AGS cells, whereas in HGC‐27 cells, INHBA rescued the decrease in cell migratory ability caused by GLI1 KO (Figures  and ). Overall, these data indicated that the GLI1‐INHBA feedback loop positively regulated GC cell proliferation and migration.
 
3.5.

## Mechanism underlying GLI1‐INHBA feedback loop
We next sought to determine the regulatory mechanism underlying the GLI1‐INHBA feedback loop. Given the critical role of GLI1 as a transcription factor, we reasoned that GLI1 could activate INHBA transcription. Transcription element analysis (http://jaspar.genereg.net) confirmed the GLI1 binding site within the INHBA promoter, as shown in Figure  . The transcriptional regulation of INHBA by GLI1 in GC cells was validated by ChIP‐PCR (Figure  ). More protein–DNA complexes in the overexpressed GLI1 group were pulled down by the Flag Ab compared to the control group. INHBA has been reported to promote GC progression through activation of the TGF‐β/Smad signaling pathway. , , Notably, Smads were able to bind to the GLI promoter and activate GLI transcription. We wondered whether Smad4 also transcriptionally regulates GLI1 expression in GC cells. We used siRNA to knock down Smad4 in MKN‐45 cells (Figure  ). The transcription factor binding site predicted by JASPAR revealed that there were two interaction sequences of Smad4 at 1418 and 1019 bp upstream of the transcription start site at the GLI1 promoter, and the binding was authenticated using ChIP‐PCR (Figure  ). Taken together, these data indicated that GLI1 functioned as a transcription factor and stimulated the transcription of INHBA. As a result, activated INHBA activates the transcription factor Smad4, which binds the promoter region of GLI1. This reciprocal interaction forms a positive feedback loop.
 
3.6.

## GLI1 and INHBA work together to promote the growth and metastasis of GC in vivo
Given the encouraging results of the GLI1‐INHBA feedback loop's ability to promote GC cell growth and migration in vitro, we investigated its potential role in the vivo model of GC. Similar results were obtained for the in vivo assay. Overexpressed GLI1 or INHBA promoted tumor growth (Figure  ). The combination treatment resulted in a stronger tumor promoting effect than overexpressed GLI1 or INHBA alone, as shown by tumor volume and tumor weight (Figure  ). Meanwhile, by quantifying the number of metastases in the lung and liver, we observed a significant decrease in tumor metastasis burden in the GLI1 KO group compared to the control group. However, lung and liver metastases were significantly increased in the INHBA overexpression group compared to the control group. Simultaneous overexpression of INHBA in the GLI1 KO group partially restores lung and liver metastases compared with GLI1 KO treatment alone (Figure  ). The subsequent IHC analysis revealed that GLI1 and INHBA treatment significantly induced the proportion of Ki‐67, N‐cadherin, and Smad4‐positive cells (Figure  ), indicating that GLI1 and INHBA work together to promote the growth and metastasis of GC in vivo.
 
3.7.

## 
Helicobacter pylori upregulates GLI1 expression through m6A demethylase FTO
Studies have reported that H. pylori has the ability to modify m6A methylation in GC cells. Consistent with this, GLI1 is involved in H. pylori‐induced metaplastic proliferative changes in the gastric epithelium. , To determine whether H. pylori infection plays an important role in regulating GLI1 expression through m6A modification, we investigated the association between GLI1 expression and m6A regulators using TCGA GC patient cohorts (Table  ). A significant positive correlation was found between the expression of GLI1 and FTO (Figure  ). Moreover, analysis in the GEO database (GSE178697) showed significantly upregulated GLI1 mRNA after overexpression of FTO in the GC cell line AGS (Figure  ). Next, we undertook RT‐qPCR and western blot analyses and found that GLI1 expression significantly decreased after inhibition of FTO at both mRNA and protein levels (Figure  ). The ELISA and m6A dot blot showed that FTO knockdown increased the total m6A levels of GC cells (Figure  ). Moreover, we infected MKN‐45 and AGS cells with H. pylori or transfected them with CagA plasmid. Western blot analysis showed that H. pylori infection significantly upregulated FTO and GLI1 protein levels in the GC cell lines (Figure  ). We next performed in vivo studies to verify our results with an H. pylori infection mouse model. On methylene blue staining, H. pylori showed a blue S‐shaped, short rod‐shaped, or small dot‐shaped appearance, with the background tissue staining blue (Figure  ).We undertook H&E staining to compare the gastric tissue in the PBS and SS1 groups, and there was significant gastric mucosal inflammation/lesion condition in the SS1 group compared to the PBS group (Figure  ). Western blot analysis revealed that the H. pylori‐infected group had significantly upregulated GLI1 and downregulated FTO expression in the gastric mucosa compared to the PBS group (Figure  ). Taken together, H. pylori upregulates the expression of GLI1 through the m6A demethylase FTO. Consequently, it would be worthwhile to explore the possible mechanisms involved in FTO‐mediated m6A demethylation of GLI1.
 
3.8.

## FTO upregulates GLI1 mRNA levels in an m6A‐YTHDF2‐dependent manner
To investigate the potential and direct m6A‐modified targets of GLI1, we used the m6A modification site predictor SRAMP (https://www.cuilab.c/sramp). Through this analysis, we found eight m6A modification sites with high confidence in the GLI1 CDS region (Figure  ). To verify FTO‐mediated m6A modification in GLI1, gene‐specific MeRIP‐qPCR analysis was undertaken. We found that silencing of FTO induced m6A peaks in the 3′‐UTR regions around GLI1 (Figure  ). Depending on the m6A reader, the m6A modification fulfills biological functions as a strong posttranscriptional modulator, as it promotes mRNA translation efficiency or affects mRNA stability. As the first and most well‐established m6A reader to be reported, YTHDF2 has a conserved m6A binding domain that allows it to recognize specific m6A sites on RNA. Therefore, it is very likely that YTHDF2 binds and degrades the GLI1 mRNA. We actually found that the half‐life of the GLI1 mRNA was significantly prolonged after knockdown of YTHDF2 in GC cells (Figure  ). Collectively, these results indicate that FTO upregulated GLI1 mRNA levels in an m6A‐YTHDF2‐dependent manner.

## DISCUSSION
GLI transcription factors contribute significantly to cancer progression as markers for activation of the Hh pathway and substantially influence cancer progression. In this study, we sought to identify the regulatory signaling pathway modulated by GLI1 in GC. We observed a novel positive feedback loop between GLI1 and INHBA in GC. Stepwise investigations revealed that H. pylori is involved in the regulation of GLI1 expression in an m6A‐dependent manner. These pathways are of great value in promoting the progression of GC. Therefore, targeted treatment of these vital molecules could provide new insights into the prevention and treatment of GC.
As an important member of the TGF‐β family, INHBA is strongly associated with tumorigenesis of several tumor types. , , , In this study, we found that INHBA emerged as a possible central target of GLI1 in GC. The expression levels of GLI1 and INHBA in tumor samples from GC patients are high compared to those of the normal group, which is consistent with previous observations, , and their high expression suggested poor OS in GC by Kaplan–Meier analysis. We also found a positive correlation between GLI1 and INHBA in patients with GC. INHBA is regulated by multiple mechanisms, including posttranscriptional and transcriptional processes. In a recent study, IGF2BP1 was observed to bind and stabilize INHBA mRNA, leading to the activation of Smad2/3 signaling in ESCC. Another study showed that circTHBS1 sponged miR‐204‐5p to promote the expression of INHBA. CP2 serves as the transcription factor for INHBA in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma. Here we identified the GLI1 transcription factor as a positive regulator of INHBA. This is in agreement with previous studies showing that GLI1 and INHBA are both involved in cancer progression.
Previous reports have shown that the activated canonical Hh signaling pathway is involved in the carcinogenesis of GC. , , , , In addition to the canonical activation of GLI by Smoothened (SMO), increasing evidence suggests that GLI transcription factors are also activated by epidermal growth factor (EGF), insulin‐like growth factor (IGF), TGF‐β, , mTOR, RAS, Notch and PKC. These findings in part explain the failure of clinical trials with SMO antagonists, urging the development of novel therapeutic strategies to inhibit noncanonical Hh signaling pathway. The TGF‐β/Smad signaling pathway is involved in the activation of the noncanonical Hh signaling pathway. , , The TGF‐β/GLI cross‐talk can influence cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival and also plays a role in tumor initiation and progression. Intriguingly, our study revealed that INHBA promotes the expression of the Smad2/Smad4 pathway. In a previous study, Smad4 was observed to physically interact with GLI. Our study illustrated that GLI1 induced GC cell proliferation and metastasis accompanied by transcriptional upregulation of INHBA; this increased INHBA expression exerted a promoting activity on Smad signaling and then transcriptionally activated GLI1 expression. We identified INHBA/Smad4 as a potent transcriptional inducer of GLI1, suggesting a novel noncanonical mechanism of GLI1 activity in GC. We undertook in vivo experiments to explore whether a positive feedback loop exists between GLI1 and INHBA and whether it had a significant effect on GC tumorigenesis. Injection of stable GLI1 overexpression or knockdown GC cells into BALB/c nude mice further supported the effect of GLI1 on tumor growth in vivo. Similarly, stable INHBA overexpression rescues the effect of GLI1 depletion on tumorigenesis. It would provide a novel therapeutic approach to cancer treatment.

Helicobacter pylori infection is the major risk factor for the development of GC. , The Hh/GLI1 signaling pathway has been clearly associated with H. pylori infection. Moreover, H. pylori infection has been shown to significantly upregulate the expression of FTO, , which has been shown to be closely related to multiple signaling pathways, such as the TNF‐α, ERK, WNT, and JAK2/STAT3 pathways. Our study further determined a significant increase in GLI1 and FTO levels in response to H. pylori infection. The role of m6A modifications on GLI1 has also been investigated in other tumor cells. GLI1 can be regulated by METTL3 through its catalytic activity. Dong et al. reported that GLI1 mRNA stability is facilitated by m6A methylation through the METTL3/METTL14/IGF2BP2 pathway in non‐small‐cell lung cancer. However, in our study, we found that GLI1 was positively regulated by FTO, while the mRNA stability of GLI1 was significantly reduced due to the binding of YTHDF2, dependent on m6A. Our present work highlighted a previously undescribed molecular mechanism for regulating mRNA stability of GLI1.
In conclusion, our study confirmed that GLI and INHBA influence GC progression and that this process depends on a positive GLI1/INHBA feedback loop. It offers a theoretical basis for the interaction between noncanonical Hh/GLI1 and TGF‐β/Smad signaling pathways. In addition, the study also revealed the mechanism by which H. pylori upregulates GLI1 expression through m6A demethylase FTO. These findings highlighted the clinical potential of GLI1/INHBA in the diagnosis and targeted therapy of patients with GC.
