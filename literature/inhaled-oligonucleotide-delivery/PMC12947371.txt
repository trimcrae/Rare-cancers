## Abstract
The ubiquitin-proteasome system is the primary system that mediates protein turn over through proteolytic activities in the 20S proteasome that catalyzes peptide hydrolysis. It is crucial for maintaining protein homeostasis and regulates many cellular processes including DNA repair, cell proliferation, and inflammatory responses. Immunoproteasome, a special class of proteasome with three distinct catalytic subunits β1i (LMP2), β2i (MECL-1), and β5i (LMP7), is induced in most cells in response to various stimuli. The subunits replacement alters proteasomal cleavage preference and enhances the generation of major histocompatibility complex I (MHC I) antigenic peptides. Immunoproteasome is involved in pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases by regulating T cells differentiation, macrophages polarization, proinflammatory cytokine production, and management of oxidative stress. In this review, we will discuss the impact of immunoproteasome dysfunctions in pulmonary diseases, such as asthma, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). Selective inhibition of immunoproteasome has the potential to serve as a therapeutic tool to address these significant health challenges. This review underscores the substantial role of the immunoproteasome in mediating the progression of various human lung diseases and highlights its potential as a therapeutic target, offering promising avenues for intervention in response to these health challenges.

## Graphical Abstract
This schematic illustrates the dual role of the immunoproteasome in inflammation. Under protective conditions (left), acute or viral lung insults trigger transient Th1 responses and M1 macrophage polarization, promoting antiviral immunity and proinflammatory defense. These mechanisms are facilitated by immunoproteasome-mediated antigen processing and cytokine production. In contrast, chronic lung disease (right panel) shifts the immune landscape toward sustained Th2 activation, M2 macrophage polarization, and progressive airway remodeling. In this setting, elevated immunoproteasome activity—particularly through the LMP7 (β5i) subunit—amplifies pathogenic Th2 responses by enhancing T cell survival, boosting cytokine output (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13), and reinforcing M2 polarization. This underscores the therapeutic paradox of immunoproteasome targeting in lung disease: while inhibition may offer anti-inflammatory benefits, it also poses potential risks to immune surveillance and host defense.

## Introduction
In physiological conditions, cellular maintenance is determined by homeostasis, which relies on the synthesis and degradation rate of proteins [ ]. Proteasomes, intricate protein complexes, break down unnecessary or damaged proteins through protease catalysis, facilitating protein degradation [ ]. The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) is also known as 26S proteasome and is responsible for the regulation and degradation of more than 80% of intracellular proteins [ ]. The 26S proteasome comprises a multi-catalytic complex with a 20S core particle and one or two regulatory particles. Three catalytically active β subunits (β1, β2, and β5) in the 20S core particle hydrolyses the unfolded polypeptide into short peptides or amino acids [ , ]. Proteins are selectively degraded by 26S proteasome via covalent attachment of polyubiquitin chains to lysine residues on the target proteins, which is carried out by a sequential, cascaded actions of the ubiquitin-activating enzyme (E1), the ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (E2), and the ubiquitin ligase (E3) [ ].
However, upon proinflammatory stimuli, such as bacterial endotoxin (LPS), viral infection, cytokines interferon-γ (IFN-γ), or tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α, the three constitutive β subunits are replaced by inducible catalytic β subunits (β1i, β2i, and β5i), leading to the formation of the immunoproteasome [ , ]. The structural shift endows the immunoproteasome with specialized proteolytic activity, enabling efficient degradation of pathogen-derived and stress-induced proteins [ , ]. It plays a critical role in generating antigenic peptides that are optimally suited for binding to MHC Class I (MHC 1) molecules [ ]. Antigen presentation through MHC I enables cytotoxic T cell recognition of intracellular pathogens and malignantly transformed cells, an essential facet of immune surveillance [ , ]. Other than generating peptides for antigen presentation, accumulating evidence suggests that the immunoproteasome has novel roles in regulation of proinflammatory cytokine production, facilitating protein homeostasis, oxidative stress response, neural function, cell differentiation, and cell signaling [ – ]. Immunoproteasome is often upregulated in a broad spectrum of conditions, including autoimmune disorders and inflammatory diseases [ , – ], suggesting a potential role in disease pathogenesis.
In the lungs, immunoproteasome activity is particularly significant in shaping the immune landscape during both acute and chronic inflammatory insults [ , – ]. Notably, its cell-type-specific influence on immune polarization and the resulting context-dependent inflammatory outcomes, especially within pulmonary tissues, remain active areas of investigation. Elevated immunoproteasome expression has been documented in a range of lung pathologies, including acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [ – ]. This upregulation may reflect both a compensatory response to cellular stress and a contributor to disease progression, underscoring the therapeutic paradox. On one hand, selective immunoproteasome inhibition can attenuate deleterious inflammation; on the other, it risks impairing antigen presentation and mucosal immunity. For instance, in viral infections, immunoproteasome induction supports antigen clearance and antiviral defense. Conversely, in chronic lung diseases such as COPD and IPF, persistent immunoproteasome activation may exacerbate tissue damage, whereas its inhibition can promote resolution by skewing macrophage polarization toward an M2 phenotype and reducing proinflammatory cytokine production.
This review explores the immunoproteasome’s dynamic roles in lung inflammation, emphasizing its cell-type-specific expression, its interplay with T-helper cell polarization, and its potential as a therapeutic target. A deeper understanding of these context-dependent functions is essential for developing precision interventions that mitigate disease without compromising host defense.

## Standard proteasome and immunoproteasome: structure and function

## Regulatory particles
The proteasome consists of various regulators that mediate the access of substrate proteins into the 20S core [ , , ]. One well-studied regulator is the 19S particle, which comprises of more than 18 different subunits and plays a significant role in ubiquitin- and ATP-dependent protein degradation in the cytoplasm. The 19S associates with the 20S in an ATP-dependent manner to form 26S proteasome [ ]. The 19S regulatory particle is responsible for recognition of ubiquitinated substrate, removal of ubiquitin, unfolding, and sorting the target substrates into the 20S for degradation in both standard proteasome and immunoproteasome [ , ].
The 11S (PA28) particle is an alternative regulatory subunit, which is ATP- and ubiquitin-independent proteasome regulator and forms a ring-shaped heptamer PA28 complexes. The 11S regulator is composed of two types of complexes: cytosolic PA28αβ heteroheptameric ring and nuclear PA28γ homoheptameric ring [ – ]. PA28αβ has been shown to promote clearance of oxidized proteins and facilitate the production of antigenic peptides for presentation on MHC molecules by working together with inducible catalytic β subunits [ ]. However, PA28αβ is not required for immunoproteasome assembly [ ] and is presented as PA28αβ−20S (Fig. ) [ ]. Unlike PA28αβ, PA28γ is located primarily in the nucleus and was shown to promote proteasomal degradation of numerous important regulatory proteins such as cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors p21, p19, and p16 [ , ], the nuclear steroid receptor coactivator-3(SRC-3), c-Myc, and p53 [ , – ]. PA28γ is unlikely to play a pivotal role in the MHC class I antigen processing/presentation system, suggesting that it may not be involved in immunoproteasome system.
 PA200, is similar to PA28γ, which is involved in nuclear proteolysis through ubiquitin-independent mechanisms [ , ]. PA200 has the same ability as 11S to enhance the proteolytic capacity of 20S proteasomal degradation of small peptides and unfolded proteins [ , ]. PA200 was found to increase 20S proteasome’s ability to degrade acetylated histones and unstructured proteins such as tau and Dnm1, but it decreased 20S proteasome and immunoproteasome’s ability to degrade oxidized proteins [ , , ].
Recent studies demonstrated that the 19S subunit were found in large complexes immunoprecipitated with PA28αβ, PA28γ, or PA200 antibodies, indicating that the regulators do indeed form some hybrid (19S–20S-regulator) proteasomes. PA28αβ, PA28γ, and PA200 all appear to form a hybrid proteasome with a 19S regulator at another end and are important regulators for induced immunoproteasome [ , ].

## 20S core particles
The 20S core particle is responsible for most of the intracellular protein degradation. The 20S core is a barrel-shaped complex composed of four stacked heptameric rings, the two outer α rings and the two inner β rings with each ring composed of seven distinct subunits as shown in Fig. . The outer α rings serve as docking sites for the regulatory particles and unfolded proteins to control entry of substrates into the 20S for degradation [ ]. Three major proteolytic activities of 20S are formed by the N-terminus Threonine 1(Thr1) of the catalytic β subunits: caspase-like (β1), trypsin-like (β2), and chymotrypsin-like (β5) [ ].
Upon multiple stimuli, an altered pattern can be generated by replacing immunosubunits, β1i/LMP2, β2i/MECL-1, and β2i/LMP7 [ , ]. The proteasome is commonly present in mammalian tissue under four subtypes, which differ by the combination of their catalytic subunits: standard proteosome, which contains the standard catalytic subunits β1, β2, and β5; immunoproteasome with inducible catalytic β subunits (β1i, β2i, and β5i); and two intermediate proteasomes, containing a mix of standard and immuno-subunits (β1–β2–β5i and β1i–β2–β5i) [ ] (Fig. ). The immunoproteasome exhibits improved post-acidic cleavage activity based on subunits exchange, resulting in subsequent loading onto the MHC-1 molecule and presents to the cytotoxic T cells [ , ]. In the thymus, a unique catalytic subunit β5t in place of β5 or β5i together with β1i and β2i forms the thymoproteasome, which is responsible for generating antigenic peptides specialized for positive selection of MHC I-restricted CD8+ T-cell [ ]. CD8+ T cells in β5t-deficient mice are defective in allogeneic and antiviral responses [ ].

## Role of Immunoproteasome in different respiratory diseases
The lung is the organ that links the body cavities and the environment, with the risk of environmental agents’ exposure, like toxic gas, aerosols, and pathogens. It is essential to clear the pathogens efficiently to protect the lung function. Failure of the defense mechanisms induces chronic pulmonary diseases with sustained inflammation and impaired lung function. Immunoproteasome driven by inflammation has shown to be involved in the pathophysiology of lung diseases particularly ARDS, asthma, COPD, and IPF [ , , , ] (Fig. ).
 

## Immunoproteasome in ALI/ARDS
The immunoproteasome actively participates in the inflammatory mechanisms underlying ARDS by facilitating the activation of NF-κB signaling and promoting oxidative stress. These processes lead to the enhanced transcription and release of proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, which contribute to the progression of lung injury. The immunoproteasome’s involvement in these pathways underscores its potential as a therapeutic target for modulating excessive inflammation in ARDS. Compared to healthy individuals, patients with ARDS exhibit increased levels of immunoproteasome subunits (β1i and β5i) and reduced proteasomal activity in both bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid and BAL cell pellets [ ]. Notably, proteasome regulators were absent in the extracellular alveolar proteasome of ARDS patients indicates that the extracellular alveolar immunoproteasome and/or intermediate-type proteasome is unlikely to originate from extravasation of circulating proteasomes or from cell lysis of the alveolar wall cells. Instead, their presence in extracellular alveolar space is more likely relates to the inflammatory processes occurring within lung tissue. These proteasomes may contribute to alveolar functional maintenance, protein clearance, antigen processing, as well as lung injury and repair. To clarify the role of immunoproteasome in extracellular alveolar space of ARDS patients and determine the mechanism of extracellular transport of the immunoproteasome, additional studies are required in the future.

## Immunoproteasome in viral lung diseases
Respiratory viral infections, including influenza and rhinovirus (RV), are major causes of viral pneumonia, ARDS, and other respiratory illnesses. RV is known as the primary cause of a common cold and is strongly associated with acute exacerbation of asthma and COPD [ ]. Immunoproteasomes induction during RV infection is critical to airway defense. IFN-γ is an important antiviral cytokine and inducer of immunoproteasomes during viral infections. Dimasuay et al. explored this in the context of RV infection, showing that IFN-λ upregulation in infected primary human airway epithelial cells increases expression of the immunoproteasome subunits β1i and β5i [ ]. The knockdown of IFN-λ receptor 1 by siRNA transfection significantly decreased β1i and β5i protein levels and led to higher viral load in RV-infected cells, indicating that upregulated IFN-λ signaling is partially responsible for immunoproteasome induction during RV infection. In vivo, β1i knockout mice were more susceptible to RV infection [ ]. Using a model with inducible airway epithelial-specific β5i-deficient mice, the study further demonstrated that β5i plays a critical role resolving RV-induced airway inflammation. β5i deficiency in primary human airway epithelial cells increases pro-inflammatory responses and viral load. Mechanistically, RV-induced β5i expression appears to inhibit inflammation by upregulation of the negative immune regulator A20/TNFAIP3 [ ]. However, the precise molecular mechanism by which β5i upregulates A20 in airway epithelial cells remains unclear.
Influenza virus remains the leading cause of respiratory viral death worldwide [ ]. Chen et al. demonstrated its importance during influenza infection using β1i-deficient mice, where the absence of β1i led to impaired antigen presentation and a reduced CD8+ T cell repertoire [ ]. Subsequent studies showed that the presentation of the immunodominant epitope PA224 was diminished in immunoproteasome-deficient mice post-influenza infection, alongside a decrease in total CD8+ T cells. Notably, while deficiency in either β1i or β5i has a defective CD8+ T cells repertoire, β2i deficiency maintained a wild-type-like immunodominance hierarchy [ ], suggesting that immunoproteasome plays a role in influenza viral infection.
In SARS-CoV-2 infection, MHC class I antigen presentation was strongly activated in the lungs of COVID-19 patients, correlating with the amount of SARS-CoV-2 RNA in the tissue. Additionally, activation of the genomic cluster associated with the immunoproteasome in the lung of COVID-19 patients is positively correlated with viral load [ ]. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of bronchoalveolar cells from patients with mild or severe COVID-19 revealed that immunoproteasome activation occurs in polarized M1 (CD68+) alveolar macrophages, which functionalities implicated in the defense response and lipid homeostasis during mild COVID-19. While the expression patterns and activities appeared to be impaired in the corresponding cell population in patients with severe COVID-19 [ ]. A study by Steigmann et al. using a mouse coronavirus model by intranasal infection C3H/HeJ mice with murine hepatitis virus strain 1 (MHV-1), demonstrated that immunoproteasome inhibitor ONX-0914 did not affect MHV-1 replication but it significantly worsened disease outcomes, including exacerbated virus-induced weight loss, mortality and inflammation in lungs and livers [ ]. This suggests that the immunoproteasome plays a protective role in controlling immunopathology during acute viral infection.
Taken together, these findings highlight the critical role of the immunoproteasome in host antiviral responses. Its inhibition may impair immune defense and exacerbate disease, indicating that immunoproteasome inhibition is not a viable therapeutic strategy for viral lung infections, including COVID-19.

## Immunoproteasome in asthma
Emerging evidence implicates immunoproteasome dysfunction as a novel pathogenic mechanism in chronic lung diseases, drawing growing interest for further investigation. Allergic asthma is a chronic lung airway inflammatory disease and driven by both innate and adaptive immune responses. The immunoproteasome plays a significant regulatory role in shaping the adaptive immune response in allergic inflammation. Its regulatory effects are highly cell type-specific and vary significantly depending on the nature of allergen exposure and the presence of viral co-infection. Upon ovalbumin (OVA) exposure, immunoproteasome subunits such as β5i are upregulated in dendritic cells and other antigen-presenting cells, enhancing the generation of antigenic peptides optimized for MHC class I presentation. This promotes the activation of CD4⁺ T cells and contributes to Th2 polarization, which then leads to the release of Th2 cytokines like IL-4, Il-5, and IL-13 [ ]. These cytokines in turn stimulate the group 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2s) activation and promote M2 macrophage polarization (Fig. ), a characteristic feature of allergic asthma [ , ]. Volkov et al. demonstrated that deficiency of β5i impairs the functional maturation of immature DCs, leading to defective antigen uptake and processing. This disruption compromises effective T cell priming and skews CD4⁺ T cell polarization, ultimately altering the balance of adaptive immune responses. β5i knockout mice exhibited reduced Th2 responses in OVA-induced asthma with decreased the influx of neutrophils, eosinophils, and Th2 cytokines in the BAL. The impaired antigen uptake and maturation of immature DCs in β5i KO mice likely contribute to the reduced Th17-mediated neutrophils infiltration in OVA-induced asthma. Although direct studies on the immunoproteasome in asthma remain limited, its known roles in cytokine regulation and T cell differentiation suggest that it may be a key modulator of Th2/Th17 dynamics, with Th2/Th17 hybrid cells potentially contributing to more severe airway obstruction and hyperreactivity [ ]. Interestingly, this attenuation was not observed in a house dust mite (HDM)-induced acute asthma model, suggesting a context-dependent role for the immunoproteasome in regulating dendritic cell-mediated Th2 immunity [ ]. Thus, antigen-specific interaction of DCs and Th2 cells upon immunoproteasome inhibition will be an interesting field for future research. The study by Oliveri et al. further confirmed the importance of the immunoproteasome in Th2 cells and airway inflammation. Oliveri et al. reported that inhibition of immunoproteasome by ONX-914 altered T helper cells polarization, leading to a significant reduction in Th2 response in the lungs of OVA- or HDM-challenged mice. This immunomodulatory effect was accompanied by reduced infiltration of eosinophils and dendritic cells in the lungs of OVA-challenged mice, whereas the impact was less pronounced in the HDM model, suggesting a context-dependent role of the immunoproteasome in allergic airway inflammation [ ]. Unlike OVA, HDM is a complex mixture of antigens that directly stimulates bronchial epithelial cells to release eosinophil-attracting/Th2 cytokines such as IL-25, IL-33, and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP). These epithelial-derived “alarmins” initiate and amplify allergic inflammation through epithelial–immune cell crosstalk [ ]. ILC2s in the lung respond rapidly to epithelial-derived alarmins, which are released in response to epithelial injury or stress, by promoting the production of cytokines such as IL-5, IL-9, and IL-13, driving hallmark feature of type 2 inflammation, including eosinophilic infiltration, mucus hypersecretion, and airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) [ ]. The role of immunoproteasome in ILC2s remains speculative and underexplored. Future studies investigating whether ILC2s express immunoproteasome subunits or are functionally influenced by them could open new avenues in understanding type 2 immunity and allergic inflammation. Current evidence suggests that immunoproteasome activity supports Th2 cell differentiation and cytokine secretion (e.g., IL-4, IL-5, IL-13), which drive eosinophilic inflammation, mucus hypersecretion, and AHR. However, the role of immunoproteasome in allergies and type 2 inflammation remains complex and somewhat controversial. Chen et al. identified an immunoregulatory role, showing that β5i deficiency enhances M2 macrophage polarization via upregulated IL-4 receptor alpha (IL-4Rα) expression and signaling [ ], although their conclusions were based on in vitro cell models. Extending these findings, Schaunaman et al. employed β5i-deficient mice, human precision-cut lung slices (PCLS), and IL-13-treated human airway epithelial cells treated with the immunoproteasome inhibitor ONX-0914. Their work revealed that the immunoproteasome facilitates IL-4Rα degradation, and its inhibition led to elevated IL-4Rα protein levels, increased eotaxin-3 release in IL-13-treated epithelial cells, and heightened airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR) in human PCLS. While these findings underscore the immunoproteasome’s role in modulating IL-4R-dependent pathways, the reliance on PCLS-based in vitro systems, lacking circulating immune cell input, introduces limitations in extrapolating to in vivo immune dynamics [ ]. In this context, immunoproteasome helps restrain allergic inflammation and viral susceptibility by limiting IL-4R-mediated signaling and supporting antiviral defense [ , ] (Fig. ).
 Respiratory viral infections in asthma often trigger a dysregulated immune landscape, marked by weakened antiviral defenses alongside elevated type 2 inflammatory responses [ ]. Immunoproteasome activation triggered by viral antigens within epithelial cells influences the expression and release of key alarmins IL-25, IL-33, and TSLP. These epithelial-derived cytokines drive the activation of ILC2s, which in turn secrete IL-5, IL-9, and IL-13, driving eosinophilic inflammation, mucus hypersecretion, and airway hyperreactivity. Concurrently, immunoproteasome upregulation in antigen-presenting cells including epithelial cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells enhances antiviral signaling through interferon induction. However, excessive immunoproteasome activation may also amplify alarmin release, tipping the immune milieu toward type 2 dominance and worsening inflammation in virally triggered asthma exacerbations. Viral co-infections may also provoke Th1/Th17, leading to increased IFN-γ and IL-17 A production and amplifying inflammation and tissue damage. Although immunoproteasome activation may mitigate allergic inflammation in the context of viral infection, its dysregulation can disrupt immune homeostasis, intensifying type 2-driven pathology and worsening asthma symptoms. Supporting its role in allergic inflammation and viral co-infection, β1i/β5i double knockout mice exposed to HDM and subsequently infected with rhinovirus A1B (RV-A1B) displayed markedly elevated lung eosinophilia and viral burden, with exacerbated airway inflammation [ ]. Overall, the immunoproteasome functions as a molecular integrator of inflammatory signals, shaping immune dynamics during virus-induced asthma exacerbations. The net effect of immunoproteasome activity in asthma is highly context-dependent, influenced by antigen burden, tissue-derived alarmins (e.g., IL-33, TSLP), cellular context, and immunoregulatory balance. The extent of immune amplification is further modulated by the cytokine milieu, viral load, and the responsiveness of airway epithelial cells.
Beyond adaptive immunity, asthma is increasingly recognized as a disease involving dysregulated innate immune responses. Multiple cell types including epithelial cells, stromal cells, monocytes, macrophages, and ILC2s become activated in response to allergen exposure, shaping the local immune environment and influencing Th2 cell responses [ , – ]. Therefore, future studies should dissect cell type–specific roles of the immunoproteasome across various models of allergen exposure, both acute and chronic, with or without concurrent viral infection, to clarify the mechanistic basis of its immune-regulatory effects.

## Immunoproteasome in COPD
The immunoproteasome has been extensively studied in the context of COPD in both human and murine lungs. Keller I, et al. characterized immunoproteasome function in COPD and lungs infected with murine gamma-herpesvirus-68 (MHV-68) [ ]. Immunoproteasome expression is generally low or absent in parenchymal and alveolar epithelial cells under normal conditions. However, its expression can be rapidly induced upon stimulation with inflammatory mediators such as IFN-γ, TNFα, or LPS [ ]. While immunoproteasome levels and activity in the MHV-68-infected lungs were significantly upregulated, these inducible expression patterns were notably absent in the explanted lungs from COPD patients, which confirmed earlier findings by Baker, et al. [ ].
Further, immunohistochemical analysis of end-stage COPD lungs revealed no significant changes in β1i expression in alveolar epithelial cells. However, a progressive decline in proteasome expression and activity was found to strongly correlate with lung function impairment [ ]. Kammerl et al. extended this research to cigarette smoking-induced murine models and patients with COPD or IPF. They observed reduced immunoproteasome expression and activity in BAL cells from patients with COPD and in isolated alveolar macrophages from patients with COPD or IPF [ ]. Interestingly, although there were no significant alterations in transcript and protein levels of immunoproteasome subunits in COPD and donor lungs, both standard and immunoproteasome activities were reduced in end-stage COPD lung tissue but remained unaffected in lungs of smoke-exposed mice, suggesting a context-specific regulatory mechanism.
The impact of cigarette smoke on immunoproteasome expression and activity was further confirmed in human blood-derived macrophages and spleen and lung immune cells exposed to smoke in vitro or in vivo [ ]. Analyses of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy young smokers and patients with severe COPD revealed divergent proteasome dynamics [ ]. Compared to nonsmokers, healthy young smokers displayed elevated 26S proteasome activity without changes in complex levels, whereas COPD patients exhibited increased 19S and 20S subunit levels but reduced 26S activity. This mismatch between expression and activity contributes to impaired protein degradation and accumulation of damaged proteins, a hallmark of systemic pathology in COPD. Notably, immunoproteasome assembly and activity were significantly elevated in PBMCs of COPD patients, contrasting with the previously observed reductions in BAL cells and lung tissues (Fig. ). Additionally, early-stage COPD patients showed increased β1i and β5i expression in primary alveolar type II cells [ ], suggesting stage-specific induction and functional adaptation. These discrepancies reflect complex compensatory mechanisms in proteostasis and immune regulation. Moreover, the correlation analyses revealed that higher levels of 26S proteasome activity in PBMCs were associated with better lung function (FEV₁/FVC), whereas elevated immunoproteasome activity correlated with greater impairment, positioning immunoproteasome activity as a promising circulating biomarker for COPD severity and disease progression (Table ) [ ].
 Cigarette smoke induces oxidative stress by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, which not only damages proteins but also upregulates immunoproteasome subunits in immune cells and lung tissue. This leads to increased antigen processing and pro-inflammatory cytokine production. The upregulation of immunoproteasome subunits in dendritic cells enhances antigen processing and presentation to naïve CD4 + T cells, driving their differentiation into Th1 and Th17 subsets, major contributors to chronic inflammation in COPD [ ]. Concurrently, macrophages and recruited monocytes are conditioned by the inflamed microenvironment and their interactions with dendritic cells and T cells, further amplifying the immunopathology of COPD. In early-stage COPD, elevated immunoproteasome expression in alveolar macrophages promotes antigen processing and presentation, triggering pro-inflammatory responses. However, in end-stage disease, oxidative modifications compromise immunoproteasome expression and activity in bronchoalveolar lavage cells and alveolar macrophages [ ]. This paradoxical shift, initial upregulation followed by functional decline, undermines antigen presentation and sustains maladaptive immune activation, accelerating tissue damage. In parallel, circulating monocytes are recruited to the lungs by chemokines elevated in COPD. This contributes to the expansion of the alveolar macrophage pool and drives pro-inflammatory M1 polarization, along with neutrophil recruitment. While the overall proportion of circulating monocytes exhibiting an M2-like phenotype is reduced in moderate and severe disease, the expression of M2 markers within this subset is markedly elevated in severe COPD, potentially reflecting a compensatory shift driven by diminished immunoproteasome expression and activity in end-stage disease. Impaired immunoproteasome activity, along with disrupted MHC I antigen presentation, may compromise adaptive immune responses, contributing to persistent infections and exacerbations in COPD and IPF. Transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1), released from M2 macrophages, epithelial cells, and other airway-resident cells, contributes to fibrosis and tissue remodeling while inhibiting epithelial and immune cell proliferation [ ]. Nevertheless, these macrophages are often dysfunctional, exhibiting impaired phagocytic capacity and an exaggerated inflammatory response, which further perpetuates tissue damage and disease progression [ ] (Fig. ).
Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress play a central role in COPD progression, tightly interwoven with persistent inflammation, epithelial injury, and the development of steroid resistance. Cigarette smoke induced damage in airway epithelial cells and alveolar macrophages disrupt mitochondrial homeostasis, triggering the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytosol and extracellular space, a hallmark of mitochondrial distress. The extracellular mtDNA functions as a damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP), activating inflammatory pathways including cGAS/STING, TLR9 and the inflammasome, leading to robust type I interferon responses and sustained inflammation [ – ]. Notably, mitochondrial stress can induce upregulation of immunoproteasome components, while aberrant immunoproteasome activity may exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction. This bidirectional interaction amplifies chronic inflammation through enhanced antigen processing and cytokine production, further driving Th1 and Th17 polarization (Fig. ). This reciprocal interaction suggests a feed-forward pathogenic loop. Targeting both components, perhaps with mitochondria-protective agents and selective β5i inhibitors, may offer a novel therapeutic strategy.
Recent single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) studies have provided insights into immunoproteasome heterogeneity in COPD alveolar macrophages (AMs), emphasizing a strong correlation between MHC class I antigen presentation, negative regulation of the cell cycle, and IFN-γ-mediated signaling across multiple airway epithelial cell types. scRNA-seq has identified four major AM superclusters based on IFI27 and APOC2 expression, each with distinct functional identities including chemokine-rich, interferon-responsive, cholesterol-handling, and metallothionein/small-cluster subsets [ ] (Fig. ). Immunoproteasome subunits are variably expressed across these superclusters, with elevated expressions particularly noted in inflammation-associated subsets. In COPD, shifts in supercluster composition, particularly the expansion of chemokine and interferon-responsive AMs, suggest a disease-associated reprogramming that may sustain chronic inflammation, perturb antigen processing via the MHC class I pathway, and impair resolution mechanisms. This heterogeneity reflects distinct immunometabolic states shaped by oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and chronic exposure to cigarette smoke. This cellular diversity underscores the nuanced interplay between innate immune adaptation and proteostasis in the pathogenesis of COPD. An integrative scRNA-seq study by Huang et al. profiled over 66,000 cells from lung tissues of COPD patients and age- and smoking-stratified controls [ ]. By independently evaluating the effects of COPD, aging, and smoking, the study highlighted monocyte-driven immunological dysregulation as a central component of COPD pathogenesis. Macrophages exhibited altered inflammatory signaling and smoking-dependent metabolic reprogramming, particularly affecting sphingolipid metabolism. The study also validated its findings by integrating publicly available scRNA-seq data from nine additional individuals, totaling over 110,000 cells, reinforcing the robustness of its conclusions [ ].
Emerging evidence suggests that immunoproteasome plays a key role in shaping macrophage metabolism, particularly in the context of chronic inflammatory diseases like COPD. COPD alveolar macrophages show signs of mitochondrial impairment, including reduced oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and increased lipid accumulation (e.g., cholesteryl esters), which are hallmarks of metabolic reprogramming [ ]. It modulates key signaling pathways such as NF-κB and STAT, which in turn influence metabolic reprogramming. NF-κB RelA acts as a metabolic checkpoint that restricts glycolysis and promotes OXPHOS, favoring an M2-like anti-inflammatory phenotype [ ]. Loss of RelA skews macrophages toward glycolysis, increasing lactate and ATP production while reducing mitochondrial respiration. STAT signaling, particularly STAT1 and STAT6, is also influenced by immunoproteasome activity and contributes to macrophage polarization and metabolic fate [ ]. Metabolic reprogramming in macrophages is a critical determinant of their functional polarization into pro-inflammatory (M1) or anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotypes. Given the impaired macrophage plasticity in COPD, dysregulated immunoproteasome function may exacerbate inflammatory persistence and hinder immune resolution, therapeutic targeting of the immunoproteasome presents a promising avenue for treatment.

## Immunoproteasome in IPF
IPF is a chronic and progressive fibrotic lung disease characterized by excessive extracellular matrix deposition, leading to impaired gas exchange and respiratory failure. While the precise role of immune mechanisms in IPF remains unclear, growing evidence from human studies and animal models indicates that aberrant immune activation plays a significant role in disease progression [ , ]. Dysregulated macrophage, particularly a pronounced shift toward the M2 phenotype, is a hallmark of IPF pathogenesis [ ]. BAL macrophages isolated from IPF patients exhibit a strong M2 phenotype, characterized by elevated secretion of the Th2-associated chemokine CCL18, which promotes fibroblast-driven collagen production and exacerbates fibrosis [ , ]. In support of this, a study by Murray et al. demonstrated that serum amyloid P can suppress M2 polarization and reduce collagen deposition in a bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis model [ ], highlighting a potential therapeutic avenue. Increased immunoproteasome levels have been reported in pulmonary fibrosis, suggesting a role in disease pathology. Notably, β5i deficiency selectively disturbs M2 polarization, but no effect in classical M1 activation [ ], supporting the concept that targeting β5i-assocaited immunoproteasome activity may regulate alternative alveolar macrophages activation, offering a potential therapeutic approach for IPF patients. scRNA-seq studies have identified distinct interferon signaling patterns in IPF, with interferon-gamma signaling upregulated in macrophages and cytotoxic T cells, whereas type I interferon signaling is elevated in other immune cell populations [ ]. This dysregulated immune activation may drive persistent epithelial stress, leading to chronic inflammation and fibrotic remodeling.
Mitochondrial dysfunction also contributes to IPF progression by promoting mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release, which subsequently activates the cGAS/STING signaling pathway and induces Type 1 IFN responses [ , ]. A recent study by Wang et al. demonstrated that cytoplasmic double stranded DNA in alveolar epithelial cells upregulates immunoproteasome and MHC class I antigen presentation, leading to CD8+ T cells activation via the cGAS/STING/type 1 IFN axis [ ]. Elevated numbers of CD8+ effector T cells in PBMCs of IPF patients further support their involvement in disease pathogenesis. This study provides the first evidence that aberrant type 1 IFN signaling and immunoproteasome activity in alveolar epithelial cells contribute to CD8+ T cells priming against stressed parenchymal cells in pulmonary fibrosis [ ]. The chronic activation of potentially autoreactive CD8+ T cells possibly results in immune cell exhaustion in IPF, which may offer new therapeutic options for treatment of IPF.
In parallel, histone degradation has emerged as a key mechanism in regulating chromatin structure, gene expression, and cellular responses to stress or damage [ ]. This process occurs through two primary mechanisms: ubiquitin-dependent and ubiquitin-independent pathways. While direct evidence of immunoproteasome-mediated histone degradation driving fibrosis is limited, emerging studies suggest a potential role. Under conditions of oxidative or inflammatory stress, immunoproteasomes can rapidly degrade intrinsically disordered regions of histones, bypassing the need for ubiquitin tagging. This activity may contribute to epigenetic instability, a hallmark of chronic diseases such as fibrosis, by altering chromatin accessibility and gene expression in fibroblasts and immune cells, promoting pro-fibrotic gene expression. Additionally, persistent immunoproteasome activity in fibrotic tissues could sustain both inflammatory signaling and epigenetic changes that reinforce fibrosis. Targeting immunoproteasome subunits like β5i/LMP7 could therefore offer a dual therapeutic strategy: modulating both inflammation and epigenetic remodeling.

## Immunoproteasome is a potential therapeutic target for treating lung inflammatory diseases
Given the importance of immunoproteasome in immune responses including antigen presentation, T cells activation and differentiation, macrophage polarization, cytokine productions, and protein homeostasis, making it an attractive therapeutic target for treatment of inflammatory disease, allergy, and autoimmune diseases.
Immunoproteasome is a predominantly inducible proteolytic machinery during acute respiratory infection [ ]. Although immunoproteasome inhibition decreases infection-induced inflammation and offers therapeutic benefits in several inflammatory disease models, it might not be a useful therapeutic strategy for respiratory viral infections. Immunoproteasome appears to be crucial for host responses to viral infection due to its importance for antigen presentation and in regulation of T cell and B cell activation in response to virus infection [ ]. ONX-914, an inhibitor of β5i and β1i, significantly reduced expression of antiviral genes MX1 and OAS1, and increased the viral load in RV-infected human tracheobronchial epithelial cells [ ]. A better understanding of the mechanism of immunoproteasome function during viral infection could assist in the development of vaccines and treatment strategies for viral infections. The anti-inflammatory potential of immunoproteasome inhibition may represent a more favorable approach for the treatment of pneumonia- or sepsis-related ARDS. Zhou et al. demonstrated that a pan-FGFR inhibitor LY2874455 attenuated pro-inflammatory cytokine production by promoting autophagic degradation of immunoproteasome via p62/SQSTM1-mediated cargo recognition. In mice, LY2874455 protected against LPS-induced acute lung injury and dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis through lowering levels of proinflammatory cytokines and immunoproteasomes [ ].
Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disease with no definitive cure. Its pathophysiology is primarily driven by type 2 inflammation orchestrated by Th2 and ILC2s. In murine models of allergic airway inflammation, immunoproteasome inhibition with ONX-0914 selectively suppresses Th2-mediated responses without affecting ILC2s, indicating a cell-type-specific mechanism in acute inflammation [ , ]. Targeting aberrant Th2 activity through immunoproteasome inhibition may offer a promising therapeutic approach to dampen IL-4 production and reduce eosinophilic infiltration. However, the role of the immunoproteasome in asthma appears context dependent. While ONX-0914 suppresses Th2-driven inflammation in some asthma models, other studies show that it may upregulate IL-4Rα expression and promote M2 macrophage polarization, leading to increased eotaxin release and airway hyperresponsiveness. These observations, however, are primarily derived from in vitro cell systems and PCLS-based models, which lack circulating immune components [ , ]. In rhinovirus-infected mice sensitized with HDM, immunoproteasome deficiency has been shown to exacerbate allergic airway inflammation [ ], underscoring its protective role in limiting viral-induced exacerbations and excessive inflammation. Supporting this, Chen et al., demonstrated that the immunoproteasome regulates IL-4-driven alternative macrophage polarization [ ]. Mechanistically, loss of immunoproteasome function either via β5i genetic deletion or pharmacological inhibition with ONX-0914 amplifies IL-4R signaling and M2 macrophage polarization, processes implicated in airway remodeling and chronic inflammation. These divergent outcomes likely reflect cell-type-specific roles of the immunoproteasome in Th2 cells versus macrophages under allergic conditions. Therefore, selectively targeting immunoproteasome activity in Th2 cells might represent a viable strategy for combination therapy in asthma, attenuating type 2 inflammation while minimizing the risk of viral susceptibility and M2-driven airway remodeling. For example, combining immunoproteasome inhibitors with dupilumab, an IL-4Rα antagonist used in eosinophilic or corticosteroid-dependent asthma, could suppress both upstream cytokine signaling and downstream immune cell activation. This dual approach may offer broader and more durable control of inflammation, particularly in mixed Th2/Th17 phenotypes.
Previous studies have highlighted the immunoproteasome’s regulatory role in alveolar macrophage polarization during acute infection and injury, underscoring the therapeutic potential of ONX-0914 in inflammatory lung diseases. In a study by Guo et al., intratracheal administration of ONX-0914 (5 mg/kg) significantly ameliorated experimental emphysema and airway inflammation, while improving lung function by modulating macrophage polarization [ ]. This study uncovered a novel mechanism by ONX-0914-mediated inhibition of M1 polarization via activation of the NRF1/NRF2-p62 signaling axis, while suppression of M2 polarization was achieved through transcriptional repression of IRF4. Encapsulation of ONX-0914 in PLGA nanoparticles (NanoONX-0914) enhanced its therapeutic efficacy in COPD models compared to the unencapsulated form, owing to improved targeting of the immunoproteasome in polarized macrophages. Intranasal administration of NanoONX-0914 showed a better therapeutic effect on lung function, inflammation, and immune cell infiltration in cigarette smoke + LPS induced COPD model. These findings from Guo’s study suggest that inhibition of immunoproteasome in macrophages holds promise therapeutic approach for COPD, although its efficacy could be limited in cases of end-stage disease or viral co-infection.
Aberrant inflammatory responses contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of IPF development. While pan-proteasome inhibitors have demonstrated antifibrotic effects across various tissues and animal models, their clinical application is limited by severe on-target toxicities associated with systemic 20 S proteasome inhibition [ , ]. In contrast, the immunoproteasome is upregulated in IPF patients, which offers a more selective therapeutic target, particularly in inflammation-activated immune cells. Li et al. provided compelling evidence that selective inhibition of the immunoproteasome using Compound 54, a potent and reversible β5i (LMP7) inhibitor, attenuates TGF-β1-induced fibroblast proliferation, activation, and collagen synthesis [ ]. In BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis models, Compound 54 exhibited antifibrotic efficacy comparable to nintedanib, a current standard of care therapy known to attenuate fibrotic signaling by targeting tyrosine kinase of PDGF, VEGF, and FGF receptors. In contrast, immunoproteasome inhibition may counteract maladaptive immune responses. A combination strategy targeting both pathways, fibroblast-driven fibrosis via nintedanib and immune dysregulation via immunoproteasome inhibition, could offer synergistic therapeutic effects but requires rigorous validation. Compound 54 has been identified as a highly potent and selective β5i (LMP7) inhibitor, with improved pharmacological properties compared to ONX-0914. The compound 54 like ONX-0914 has anti-inflammatory function by inhibiting NF-κB pathway, attenuating TNF-α and IL-6 release in LPS-stimulated THP-1 cells [ ]. However, ONX-0914 has shown to increase IL-4 triggered macrophage M2 polarization, a process implicated in IPF progression [ ]. This underscores the need to optimize ONX-0914’s dosing and delivery to mitigate adverse effects. In contrast, the reversible nature and improved selectivity of Compound 54 may offer a safer profile with a reduced risk of exacerbating M2-driven fibrosis.
As we discussed above, the immunoproteasome plays a pivotal role in immune regulation and has been increasingly implicated in the pathogenesis of immune-mediated disorders. In the context of lung disease, it is emerging as a valuable biomarker of immune activation and disease progression, with distinct expression patterns observed in alveolar macrophages, epithelial cells, and infiltrating lymphocytes across various pathological states (Table  ). ## Discussion
The immunoproteasome plays a pivotal role in respiratory immunity, garnering increasing attention for its therapeutic potential in preclinical models of lung disease. Its primary function involves generating peptides for MHC class I antigen presentation, thereby facilitating CD8⁺ T cell responses [ , , ]. Beyond antigen processing, the immunoproteasome modulates immune cell differentiation and activation, including B cells, T helper subsets, and macrophages shaping transcriptional programs and cytokine profiles [ , , , , , ].
Environmental insults can impair immunoproteasome function in the lungs, contributing to the pathogenesis of acute and chronic pulmonary diseases such as ARDS, asthma, COPD, and IPF [ ]. In these contexts, immunoproteasome expression is often upregulated, suggesting a role in disease progression. Selective inhibition of the immunoproteasome is being explored as an anti-inflammatory strategy in several lung disease models, with promising results in ARDS, asthma, COPD, and IPF [ – , , ].
However, therapeutic targeting must be approached with nuance. While immunoproteasome inhibition may benefit chronic inflammatory conditions, it could compromise antiviral immunity, given the immunoproteasome’s essential role in antigen presentation and lymphocyte activation during viral infections. In COPD, for instance, therapeutic efficacy may depend on disease stage, cellular source, and immunoproteasome activity levels to balance efficacy and minimize side effects.
Asthma presents additional complexity. Type 2 inflammation, driven by Th2 cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, underlies disease pathology in a substantial subset of patients. Emerging evidence indicates that both timing and cellular context of immunoproteasome inhibition critically shape Th2-mediated responses. In models of allergic airway inflammation, acute immunoproteasome inhibition attenuates Th2 responses, reduces IL-4 secretion, and limits eosinophilic infiltration. These effects suggest its potential as an adjunct therapy alongside existing biologics, such as anti-IL-13 monoclonal antibodies and the IL-4 receptor antagonist Dupilumab [ , ]. This underscores the therapeutic importance of timing, as transient inhibition can suppress effector functions without broadly compromising immunity. However, chronic or systemic inhibition of immunoproteasome might have more complex outcomes, potentially affecting antigen presentation or T cell priming differently depending on the cell type involved. Interestingly, both epithelial cells and DCs are affected differently: DCs show reduced antigen presentation capacity, while epithelial cells exhibit stress-modulated cytokine production. To dissect these dynamics, preclinical models with lineage tracing and cell-specific knockouts are indispensable for clarifying how immunoproteasome activity in Th2 cells, epithelial cells, and dendritic cells contributes to asthma pathogenesis. Given the conflicting data surrounding its role in allergic airway inflammation, further investigations using refined asthma models are essential to define the immunoproteasome’s precise function. If carefully balanced against risks like increased viral susceptibility or M2 macrophage-driven remodeling, selective targeting of the immunoproteasome in Th2 cells may represent a promising combinatorial approach in asthma treatment.
Despite recent advances, several critical knowledge gaps continue to limit the full therapeutic and diagnostic potential of the immunoproteasome. Although it exhibits enhanced efficiency under oxidative stress and immune activation, elevated immunoproteasome expression does not always correlate with functional activity, especially in diseases like end-stage COPD or severe viral infections. The molecular underpinnings of this disconnect, including post-translational modifications, oxidative damage, or subunit misassembly, remain poorly defined. The interplay between the immunoproteasome and other proteolytic systems such as autophagy and lysosomal degradation as well as signaling pathways like IFN-γ and NF-κB, is complex and incompletely understood, warranting further investigation.
Moreover, most studies focus on immune cells or cancer models, leaving the role of the immunoproteasome in non-immune cells such as epithelial, endothelial, or fibroblasts largely underexplored. To address these limitations, more refined experimental platforms, including tissue-specific animal models and organoid systems, are needed to dissect immunoproteasome function within specific disease microenvironments. The detection of immunoproteasome components in extracellular fluids like BAL is intriguing, but their origin, function, and impact on intercellular signaling or immune modulation are not well understood. While immunoproteasome inhibitors are being explored in preclinical models of COPD and asthma, challenges such as off-target effects, resistance mechanisms, and long-term safety continue to hinder clinical translation. Additionally, immunoproteasome subunits are emerging as promising biomarker candidates, particularly in inflammatory and immune-driven diseases like asthma and COPD. Their inducible expression under stress and inflammation makes them attractive for tracking disease activity and therapeutic response. However, current studies often vary in methodology, sample type (e.g., tissue vs. BAL fluid), and detection platforms, making cross-study comparisons difficult. Moreover, most data are derived from small cohorts or preclinical models, limiting generalizability. To advance clinical translation, standardized detection assays and well-powered longitudinal studies are essential to validate the predictive and prognostic utility of immunoproteasome subunits across diverse patient populations.

## Future directions
Substantial progress has been made in the development of immunoproteasome selective inhibitors and substrate-specific profiling tools, enabling a deeper understanding of their enzymatic selectivity and cellular functions. Recent advances in computational modeling, leveraging both structure-based and AI-driven approaches, have significantly accelerated the discovery of selective β5i targeted compounds. These advances pave the way to elucidate the biological roles of the immunoproteasome under pharmacological inhibition, including the identification of specific substrates, signaling cascades, and potential off-target effects.
Moreover, emerging evidence also points to a potential intersection between the immunoproteasome and neuro-immune communication pathways. Although direct evidence of immunoproteasome activity within sensory neurons remains limited, recent findings indicate that neuronal activation can intensify allergic inflammation by reprogramming protective immune populations into pro-inflammatory phenotypes. This raises the compelling hypothesis that immunoproteasome function in airway-innervating neurons may regulate local cytokine expression or antigen presentation, thus contributing to the inflammatory milieu of diseases such as asthma.
In parallel, the gut-lung axis has gained recognition as a key regulator of pulmonary immunity. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), notably butyrate and propionate, produced by gut microbiota can modulate systemic inflammation and immune cell function. While direct modulation of the immunoproteasome by SCFAs is still under investigation, SCFAs are known to influence NF-κB signaling, macrophage polarization, and histone acetylation, all of which intersect with immunoproteasome-regulated pathways. This opens the door to microbiome-targeted therapies that indirectly regulate immunoproteasome activity in lung diseases like asthma, COPD, and IPF.
Future studies will likely integrate single-cell transcriptomics, proteomics, and spatial transcriptomics to map immunoproteasome expressions and function across tissues and disease states. This could clarify its roles within neuro-immune communication and gut-lung crosstalk and provide insights into its function within chronic inflammatory microenvironments.
