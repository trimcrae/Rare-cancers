## Significance
Recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa (RDEB) is a rare and severe genetic disease caused by variants in COL7A1 encoding type VII collagen (C7). Patients develop recurrent blistering and erosions of the skin and mucosae leading to major local and systemic complications. We report two deep intronic loss-of-function variants leading to aberrant splicing of the COL7A1 transcript. Transfection of primary RDEB keratinocytes and fibroblasts with antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting these variants restored normal splicing of the COL7A1 mRNA and expression of the C7 protein. These small molecules are nonimmunogenic and can be delivered locally or systemically. This report highlights the therapeutic potential of ASOs for RDEB but also for any disease due to intronic splice variants at a distance of consensus splice sites.
Keywords: antisense oligonucleotides, mRNA splicing, type VII collagen, splice modulation, COL7A1

## Abstract
Recessive dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa (RDEB) is a rare and most often severe genetic disease characterized by recurrent blistering and erosions of the skin and mucous membranes after minor trauma, leading to major local and systemic complications. The disease is caused by loss-of-function variants in COL7A1 encoding type VII collagen (C7), the main component of anchoring fibrils, which form attachment structures stabilizing the cutaneous basement membrane zone. Alterations in C7 protein structure and/or expression lead to abnormal, rare or absent anchoring fibrils resulting in loss of dermal-epidermal adherence and skin blistering. To date, more than 1,200 distinct COL7A1 deleterious variants have been reported and 19% are splice variants. Here, we describe two RDEB patients for whom we identified two pathogenic deep intronic pathogenic variants in COL7A1. One of these variants (c.7795-97C > G) promotes the inclusion of a pseudoexon between exons 104 and 105 in the COL7A1 transcript, while the other causes partial or complete retention of intron 51. We used antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) mediated exon skipping to correct these aberrant splicing events in vitro. This led to increased normal mRNA splicing above 94% and restoration of C7 protein expression at a level (up to 56%) that should be sufficient to reverse the phenotype. This first report of exon skipping applied to counteract deep intronic variants in COL7A1 represents a promising therapeutic strategy for personalized medicine directed at patients with intronic variants at a distance of consensus splice sites.

Dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa (DEB) is a group of rare and severe dominantly or recessively inherited blistering diseases characterized by fragility of the skin and mucous membranes caused by COL7A1 variants encoding a type VII collagen. To date, approximately 1,200 distinct COL7A1 pathogenic variants have been reported in the Human Genome Mutation Database ( ). Around 43% of variants substitute an amino acid, 19% are splicing variants, 22% are small out-of-frame insertions or deletions, and 10% are nonsense variants. Several approaches based on gene, cell, or protein therapy are currently under development, but to date, a specific and accessible treatment for RDEB is lacking. Splice modulation strategy has been successfully applied to different genetic diseases. Its principle is to mask specific splice sites during mRNA maturation with antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) to either skip mutated exons while preserving the reading frame or to include exons to reframe the transcript. In particular, exon skipping is one of the strategies considered as a specific treatment for RDEBs ( – ). In the present study, we have used splice modulation to force skipping of a pseudoexon (PE) or intronic retention in COL7A1 pre-mRNA caused by intronic recessive COL7A1 variants and thus restore expression of a normal protein.

## Results

## Patient 1.
The proband is a 45-y-old man from unrelated healthy parents from French extraction ( ). The first blisters were reported at the age of 1 y. He presented with RDEB Inversa with blisters of his neck, the upper part of his back, his hands, beard, inguinal and perineal areas, and nail dystrophy (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). He had a history of esophagial stenosis which required dilatation. Ultrastructure examination revealed the presence of rare and rudimentary anchoring fibrils ( ). Immunostaining with mAb LH7.2 against the NC1 domain of C7 showed no detectable expression at the basement membrane zone (BMZ) ( ). A very weak expression of full-length C7 protein was detectable on keratinocytes extracts by western-blot analysis ( ).
 All COL7A1 exons and their flanking intronic regions were subjected to PCR and Sanger sequencing and revealed that the proband carried a nonsense variant in exon 85 inherited from his mother (c.6721C > T; p.Gln2241*). No additional variant was identified in the coding sequence of COL7A1 nor in intronic sequences flanking exon–intron junctions. Transcript analysis with 14 RT-PCRs along the COL7A1 cDNA revealed an abnormal transcript between exon 96 and exon 110 ( ). Cloning and sequencing of the abnormal transcript and genomic DNA identified a deep intronic variant in intron 104 inherited from his father (c.7795-97C > G) ( ). In silico analysis (Human Splicing Finder) predicted that the c.7795-97C > G substitution strengthens a cryptic intronic gt donor splice site leading to the activation of a potential complementary splice site in the proximity, an upstream intronic acceptor splice site, resulting in the inclusion of a 96 bp PE between exons 104 and 105 in the COL7A1 transcript ( ). This PE contains a TAG premature termination codon (PTC), predicting the absence of full-length protein synthesis. The small amount of C7 protein of apparently normal molecular weight produced by patient 1’s keratinocytes seen on western-blot analysis ( ) suggests that the intronic paternal variant may be leaky and allows for the synthesis of some normal COL7A1 transcript and that some nonsense read-through activity on the maternal variant may allow for low levels of full-length protein synthesis. This variant is the deepest COL7A1 intronic change reported so far and the first variant leading to the inclusion of a PE in RDEB.

## Patient 2.
The proband is a 32-y-old woman from consanguineous healthy parents from Türkiye ( ). The first blisters were reported at the age of 2 y. She presented with RDEB pruriginosa with pruriginous lichenified papules and nodules with a linear configuration, milia, and hypertrophic scarring on her upper and lower limbs. All her nails were dystrophic (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Her affected brother had intermediate RDEB with moderate pruritus, no prurigo-like lesions, and limited scars and blisters with no linear configuration on his elbows, dorsum of hands, knees, or pretibial regions. Immunostaining with mAb LH7.2 showed a drastically reduced expression at the BMZ ( ). A very weak expression of full-length C7 protein was detectable by western-blot analysis from keratinocyte extracts ( ).
 Next-Generation Sequencing and Sanger sequencing revealed that the proband was a compound heterozygote for two COL7A1 variants, a nonsense variant in exon 111 inherited from her father (c.8299G > T; p.Glu2767*), and a transition in intron 51 from her mother (c.4899+31G > A) ( ). Interestingly, patient 2, who was born to consanguineous parents, was not a homozygote but a compound heterozygote for two different recessive COL7A1 variants inherited from each parent. The occurrence of compound heterozygous patients in consanguineous families has previously been reported in several articles ( , ). It has been shown that this probability depends on the number of disease alleles at a given locus, on allele frequencies and on the extent of inbreeding. Cloning and sequencing of RT-PCR products between exon 47 and exon 53 revealed two abnormal transcripts ( ). The c.4899 + 31G > A substitution in intron 51 strengthens an intronic cryptic gt donor splice site resulting in partial retention of the first 31 nucleotides of intron 51. In addition, in silico analysis with Human Splicing Finder predicts that the variant increases the binding of SR proteins and in particular SRSF1 and SRSF5 resulting in complete retention of intron 51 ( ). These two abnormal transcripts lead to PTCs, predicting no synthesis of the full-length protein. The small amount of apparent normal molecular weight C7 protein detected by western-blot analysis in patient 2’s keratinocytes ( ) suggests that the intronic maternal variant is leaky and allows for the synthesis of low levels of normal transcript and full-length C7 protein, and that some basal nonsense read-through activity occurs on the paternal allele.

## Splice Modulation Strategy Applied to Deep Intronic Variants to Prevent Pseudoexon Formation and Intronic Retention.
We tested whether masking the deep intronic variants would reduce PE formation or intronic retention, respectively, in patients 1 and 2 and restore normal transcript expression and increase C7 protein expression. We designed chemically modified RNA ASO, with replacement of the phosphodiester bond with a phosphorothioate bond and addition of a methyl group (2’OMePS) or a methoxyethyl group (2’MOEPS) at 2’ carbon of the ribose. The capacity of these ASOs to reframe transcripts was evaluated by transfecting patient 1 and patient 2 fibroblasts and keratinocytes with doses ranging from 50 to 200 nM of each ASO. Best results were obtained with 100 nM of ASO which was subsequently used for further experiments. After 48 h, total RNA was extracted from transfected cells and RT-PCR was performed followed by capillary electrophoresis analysis of COL7A1 mRNA (SI Appendix, Figs. S3 and S4). The efficiency of each ASO was assessed by quantifying the area under each peak of the capillary electrophoresis (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). The results showed the efficacy of 2'OMePS and 2'MOEPS was comparable for both patients. In patient 1, the relative amount of PE decreased significantly from 35% to 5% in keratinocytes and from 31% to 3% in fibroblasts. In patient 2, the relative amount of partial retention also showed a marked reduction from 25% to 5% in keratinocytes and from 30% to 8% in fibroblasts. The relative amount of complete intronic retention decreased from 9% to 0.7% in keratinocytes and from 4% to 0.5% in fibroblasts.

## Skipping of the Pseudoexon or Prevention of Intronic Retention in Keratinocytes and Fibroblasts Restores Normal C7 Protein Levels from the Targeted Allele.
To show that skipping of PE, or preventing intronic retention in patients’ cells, can restore C7 expression, we transfected cultured primary keratinocytes and fibroblasts from patients 1 and 2 with 100 nM of 2′OMePS and 2’MOEPS ( and ). Cells were analyzed 72 h after transfection. Immunocytofluorescence analysis showed re-expression of C7 in cells treated with ASOs. Both ASOs seem to be equally effective, but skipping of PE or intronic retentions and thus C7 re-expression appears to be stronger in patient 1 and patient 2’s keratinocyte cultures than in the fibroblast cultures of these patients ( and ).
 Western-Blot analysis of transfected cells from patient 1 showed expression of C7 up to 50% of the normal amount in keratinocytes and 36% in fibroblasts with 2′OMePS and 2’MOEPS. In untreated cells, the basal level of C7 expression was 17% in keratinocytes and 14% in fibroblasts, respectively ( ). For patient 2, western-Blot analysis showed expression of C7 up to 56% of the normal amount in keratinocytes and 39% in fibroblasts. In untreated cells, the basal C7 expression level was 29% in keratinocytes and 15% in fibroblasts, respectively, compared to control human keratinocytes and fibroblasts ( ).

## Discussion
The identification of deep intronic variants that alter splicing is frequently inaccessible to exon-based diagnostic sequencing approaches. The study of RNA into the diagnosis workflow for mutation-negative patients has revealed that such variants represent an important class of disease-causing variants ( ). Our finding of two COL7A1 deep intronic variants, one of which leads to the inclusion of a pseudoexon, is an example. To date, there are approximately 1,200 reported distinct pathogenic variants in COL7A1, of which 19% are splicing variants. The deepest COL7A1 intronic variant reported is the c.6901-61C > G variant in intron 87 ( ), for which consequences on splicing have not been investigated and for which in silico analysis predicts no significant impact on splicing signals. The previously deepest intronic variant in COL7A1 with demonstrated consequences is the c.2587 + 40G > A substitution ( ). This variant resulted in the retention of the first 37 nucleotides of intron 19 leading to a frameshift and a PTC. The intronic c.7795-97C > G variant we report here is the deepest intronic variant identified in COL7A1, which results in the first report of a pseudoexonic insertion. PEs caused by variants that create or strengthen cryptic splice donor or acceptor sites, or affect exonic splicing enhancer interactions, have been identified in many other genes ( ), among which HBB, the gene encoding Hemoglobin subunit beta causing β-Thalassemia, ( ), DMD encoding dystrophin, causing Duchenne muscular dystrophy ( ) and in CFTR, causing cystic fibrosis ( ). In Mendelian disorders, conventional mutation detection strategies have focused on DNA-based analyses, including next-generation sequencing in the form of gene-targeted arrays, whole-exome sequencing (WES), and whole-genome sequencing (WGS). However, DNA-based NGS methods allow the identification of intronic variants in only 20%–50% of cases, leaving a large proportion of disease-causing variants located in the noncoding regions of the genes unknown ( ). In addition, while WGS can recognize deep intronic variants or variants located within regulatory elements, it may be difficult to conclude on their significance. Direct sequencing of RT-PCR products or RNA-seq analyses have recently been proposed to disclose the consequences of deep intronic variants impairing splicing and it is likely that identification of other novel COL7A1 variants in noncoding regions could be accomplished by analysis of RNA sequence variations. ( , ).
Deep intronic variants leading to pathogenic inclusion of a PE or partial retention of an intron in the mature transcript of a gene provide an ideal target for ASO-mediated exon skipping. Indeed, because of their distance from the consensus splice site, splice modulation strategies targeting these variants do not alter normal splice site sequences. Treatment of cells with ASOs targeting deep intronic variants induces skipping of PE or partial retention of an intron, leading to the synthesis of a normal COL7A1 mRNA transcript and the restoration of C7 protein. Our data demonstrate that splice modulation could be a relevant therapeutic strategy for COL7A1 splicing variants resulting in the inclusion of a PE or partial retention of an intron, because i) the restored protein is completely normal, in contrast with the truncated C7 re-expressed after exon skipping in RDEB (2-4), and ii) it is not necessary to reach full correction of the abnormal transcript as it has been estimated in mice that 30% of normal levels of functional C7 are sufficient to restore dermal-epidermal adherence ( , ).
Important factors must be considered and optimized before splice modulation therapies can be considered for trials in patients, including the nature of the chemical backbone and nucleotide modifications. Guidelines have been published for the design of ASOs ( ) and software such as Human Splicing Finder and ESEfinder software ( , ) help to define key splicing sequences in the pre-mRNA sequences. Recently, specific software have been developed, such as eSkip-Finder ( ) and Exosplice (https://exosplice.genomnis.com/) to design splice-modulating ASOs. The advantage of targeting intronic variants leading to splice defects is that, on the contrary to a targeted exon skipping strategy, the sequence altering the splicing (cryptic donor/acceptor site or ESE …) is already known and that masking a sequence encompassing the variants is usually efficient.
In the past 7 y, five splice switching ASOs have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and/or the European Medicines Agency (EMA), and/or the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare which obtained marketing authorization ( ). The most notable example of splice modulation is the exon inclusion achieved by Nusinersen, a 2’MOEPS modified ASO that is approved for all types of spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), a disease characterized by progressive loss of motoneurons and motor function. A currently ongoing trial where SMA patients are treated presymptomatically suggests that earlier treatment results in more significant therapeutic effects ( ). Since its market authorization, Nusinersen has demonstrated a favorable safety profile with no new safety concerns identified after 5 y of follow-up ( ). Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is another disease in which ASOs are actively being developed with 4 unconjugated PMOs ASOs being approved by the FDA (Eteplirsen, Golodirsen, Viltolarsen, and Casimersen) ( , ). Clinical studies have shown that these ASOs are well tolerated, but with poor dystrophin protein expression (ranging from 1% to 6% of healthy levels) and limited clinical benefit ( – ). It is difficult to compare efficacy and long-term safety of ASOs approved for DMD and SMA, as not only the disease and target tissues are different, but also the routes of administration and ASO chemistries are distinct. However, if adverse events have been observed in clinical trials or during the pharmacovigilance period, the drugs are overall well tolerated ( – ). Several placebo-control trials are ongoing to assess the clinical benefit of these PMOs in DMD patients, but the results of these studies are not yet available.
To date, studies on exon skipping for EB have used ASOs containing 2’OMe on a phosphorothioate (PS) backbone (2’OMePS), 2’MOE on a phosphorothioate backbone (2’MOEPS), and phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligomer (PMO) ( , , ). Recently a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled phase I/II clinical trial (NCT03605069) sponsored by Phoenicis Therapeutics started to evaluate safety and efficacy of topical applications of an ASO targeting exon 73 of COL7A1 to treat DEB patients carrying mutations in this exon (QR-313). However, the trial was terminated due to low enrolment, and no conclusions were disclosed.
ASO-mediated splicing modulation is also a very appealing approach for cryptic splicing variants. An ASO-based splicing modulation therapy is under development for Cystic Fibrosis patients carrying the c.3849+10 kb C > T variant in the CTFR gene leading to pseudoexon inclusion. Targeting this variant with a 2’MOEPS significantly increased the level of correctly spliced mRNA and completely restored CFTR function to wild-type levels ( , ). A proof-of-concept study of the feasibility of a personalized ASO treatment for a cryptic splicing variant that resulted in the inclusion of a PE was reported in a child with neuronal ceroid lipofuscinosis 7 (CLN7, a form of Batten’s disease), a rare and fatal neurodegenerative disease. Kim et al. designed Milasen, a 2’MOEPS ASO targeting a CLN7 variant, which showed efficacy in patient-derived fibroblasts. These results led to a patient-customized oligonucleotide therapy which was conducted under an expanded-access investigational clinical protocol authorized by the FDA ( ). ASOs are a class of drug particularly suitable for developing variant-targeted therapies, and there are now 3 patient-customized ASOs approved by the FDA ( ).
Other precision medicine strategies such as base or prime editing may be applied to target cryptic splice variants. The advantages of these genome editing approaches is that they allow permanent correction of targeted cells, but at present, in vivo delivery of BEs and PEs is still a challenge and correction efficiencies in vivo are limited, although the field is evolving rapidly ( ). Another issue might be the immune response toward base or prime-editors proteins if redosing is required to treat extended skin areas or mucosae as pre-existing immunity to several Cas9 proteins have been reported ( ).
There are challenges ahead for the development of these personalized treatments (splice switching or genome editing) targeting specific variants. These include the identification of the rare individuals amenable to these treatments ( ) and the cost to develop and produce these drugs while they target a limited number of patients. A crowdfunding model supported the development of Milasen, but it is unlikely to be applicable for every “n-of-one” drug. The N1C (N = 1 collaborative) and the n-Lorem foundation are two possible answers to these issues offering expertise and support for the development of ASOs for rare cases ( , ).
In 2023, a new gene therapy product (VYJUVEK®) developed by Krystal Biotech has been approved by the FDA. VYJUVEK is a topical gel containing HSV-1-based viral vectors encoding COL7A1 cDNA which has demonstrated both safety and efficacy in the treatment of open wounds in RDEB patients ( , ). The main drawbacks are that the vector is not capable to cross the skin barrier and the effect is transient, therefore the patient should be redosed. The vector can be formulated in eye drops to treat ocular complications of DEB, however the possibilities to treat mucosae is yet unknown as this product cannot be delivered systemically ( ). Even though this treatment is a major breakthrough in the management of chronic wounds in RDEB patients, it may not address all the complications of the disease, in particular mucosal involvement.
In vivo ASO-mediated exon skipping therapy offers several advantages: ASO are nonimmunogenic and can be administrated topically, systemically, by inhalation ( ) or even orally ( ) and they are easy to manufacture compared to gene and cell therapy products. The main drawbacks are that the treatment will involve a lifelong administration, as for the VYJUVEK topical gene therapy product and that it will be amenable to a subset of patients. However, the long half-life of the C7 protein (estimated to be 1 to 2 moin vivo) ( ) should allow for spaced ASO administration. Because skin is a particularly accessible organ, topical ASO application could be considered, although the skin barrier will impair efficient delivery to basal keratinocytes and dermal fibroblasts, because the molecular weight of ASOs used ranges between 7.8 and 9.6 kDa, exceeding the size limit for efficient transcutaneous delivery ( ).
In conclusion, we have characterized two deep intronic variants, one of which induces the inclusion of a pseudoexon while the other leads to intron retention. Furthermore, this study shows that in vitro splice modulation by ASOs of deep intronic variants can restore a level of normal C7 comparable to 50% of control, which should be sufficient to rescue C7 function. We have compared two different chemistries (2’OMePS vs 2’MOEPS), and it does not seem that one is better or more toxic than the other in vitro. Splice modulation applied to deep intronic variants in COL7A1 represents a type of personalized medicine directed at individual patients with private variants.

## Materials and Methods

## Consent.
The samples were obtained with appropriate signed informed consent from all participants. Consent forms were reviewed by the Imagine Institute IRB. The collection of RDEB patient samples was approved by the French Ministry of Higher Education, Research, and Innovation (DC-2019-3504).

## Mutation Screening.
The patients included in this study were referred to us for the diagnosis of RDEB on the basis of their clinical presentation. Blood samples were taken from the probands and their parents and a skin biopsy was taken for diagnostic purpose. Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes or cultured keratinocytes using standard methods. NGS sequencing was performed on a HiSeq2500 Illumina sequencer using a custom panel and confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Sanger sequencing was performed using the ABI Big Dye terminator 3.1 chemistry on an ABI 3100 DNA sequencer (ABI, Foster city, CA). Sequences were compared to the COL7A1 gene and transcript reference sequences under GenBank accession numbers NG_007065.1 and NM_000094.3, respectively.

## Cell Culture.
Primary keratinocytes and fibroblasts were isolated from patients’ skin biopsy samples or surgical skin margins of healthy donors. Keratinocytes were cultured on a feeder layer of irradiated mouse 3T3-J2 fibroblasts ( ) or in 0.06 nM of CaCl2 Epilife Medium (Life Technologies, Waltham, MA) for transfection experiments. Fibroblasts were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and a penicillin/streptomycin mixture (Life Technologies).

## ASO Design.
ASO design was performed as described previously ( ). The pseudoexon and its intronic junctions in the COL7A1 for case 1 and the intronic retention in the COL7A1 for case 2 were analyzed by Human Splicing Finder and ESEfinder software ( , ), to identify the splice sites and to find the potential exonic splicing enhancers (ESE). Several parameters are to be taken into account, such as the length of the ASO, the exon/ASO hybridization free energy, and the free energy of self-folding or dimerization, which can be computed using software like PerlPrimer, mfold, and OligoCalc. For this study, we used chemically modified RNA: 2′O methyl phosphorothioate (2′OMePS) oligonucleotides and 2′O methoxyethyl phosphorothioate (2′MOEPS) oligonucleotides. The sequences and chemistries of the ASOs are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. All ASOs were PAGE purified (Eurogentec, Angers, FR).

## Transfection Experiments.
Fibroblasts and keratinocytes were seeded in six-well dishes 3 d before transfection with Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Villebon-sur-Yvette, FR). Transfection experiments were performed using ASO concentrations to 100 nM in Optimem Medium ( ).

## Reverse Transcriptase-PCR Analysis.
For transfection experiments, cells were harvested 48 h after transfection. Total RNA were extracted from cultured cells using RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Courtaboeuf, FR). RNA was reverse transcribed using Superscript IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Villebon-sur-Yvette, FR) reverse transcriptase. Reverse transcription was followed by PCR analysis of exons 101 to 106 of the COL7A1 gene using primers: forward 5’-CAAGGGGGACATGGGTGA-3’; reverse 5’-CCCTTCACTCCCCGTTCA-3’ and PCR analysis of exons 47 to 53 of the COL7A1 gene using primers: forward 5’-AAAGGAGAAAAGGGAGATGTG-3’; reverse 5’-GCCACGCTCGCCTGCTTT-3’. Amplification products were separated by electrophoresis on 2% agarose gels, purified, and cloned in pGEM-T vector (Promega) for sequencing with BigDye version 3.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Villebon-sur-Yvette, FR) using SP6 and T7 primers. DNA fragment analysis by capillary electrophoresis with the GeneScan™ 400HD ROX™ Size Standard (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Villebon-sur-Yvette, FR) was performed to quantify the relative amount of the abnormal transcripts.

## Western-Blot Analysis.
The cells in keratinocyte and fibroblast cultures were scraped from their Petri dishes 72 h after transfection in a buffer containing 150 nM NaCl, 50 nM Tris-HCl (pH8), 5 nM EDTA, 1% NP40, and complete mini protease inhibitors cocktail (Roche), incubated for 30 min on ice, sonicated for 2 min, and centrifuged for 20 min at 18,000 g. Supernatants were precipitated by the addition of 5 volumes of cold acetone and incubated for 2 d at −20 °C. Then, precipitated proteins were pelleted by centrifugation for 30 min at 18,000 g. Pellets were solubilized in a 25 nM Tris-HCl buffer containing 9.5 mol/L of urea. Next, 50 µg of proteins in a Laemmli buffer were loaded on a 3 to 8% gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Villebon-sur-Yvette, FR) and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The LH7.2 antibody (Sigma Aldrich, Saint Quentin Fallavier, FR; C6805) (1:2000) and a polyclonal antibody against type VII collagen (gift of Mei Chen) for transfection experiments (1:10 000) were used and β-actin immunodetection served as a loading control. Signals were revealed with ECL+ reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Villebon-sur-Yvette, FR), and band intensity quantification was performed by gel densitometry (ImageLab software, Bio-Rad).

## Immunofluorescence Studies.
For type VII collagen staining on skin sections, 5 µM frozen skin sections were fixed in acetone at −20 °C for 10 min followed by 30 min incubation in blocking buffer with 3% BSA in PBS. C7 staining was performed using the mouse monoclonal LH7.2 primary antibody, for 2 h at room temperature at 1:2000, then skin sections were incubated for 1 h with the Alexa Fluor 555 goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Villebon-sur-Yvette, FR). Mowiol mounting medium containing DAPI (0.5 µg/mL) was used. Images were captured with a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope.
For type VII collagen staining on cultured cells, keratinocytes and fibroblasts were grown on glass slide, and 72 h after transfection, cells were fixed with cold methanol for 10 min at −20 °C followed by 30 min incubation in blocking buffer with 3% BSA in PBS. Cells were then processed for immunolabeling with a polyclonal antibody against type VII collagen (gift of Mei Chen) for 2 h. Coverslips were washed three times with PBS and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark with goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 555 diluted in blocking solution (1:600; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Villebon-sur-Yvette, FR). Mowiol mounting medium containing DAPI (0.5 µg/mL) was used. Images were captured with a Leica TCS SP8 SMD confocal microscope (Obj. 63X; Zoom: 0.75).

## Electron Microscopy.
Four-mm punch biopsies from each patient's perilesional skin were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, postfixed in 1% osmium-tetroxyde, dehydrated in a graded ethanol series, and embedded in Epon 812. Semithin sections (1 µm) were stained with toluidin blue. Ultrathin sections were selected, contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined with a Philips CM 10 transmission electron microscope.
