## Background
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most frequent and deadly tumors worldwide. Despite advances in targeted and immunotherapy, its overall survival rates remain low.TMEM88 has drawn a lot of attention for its roles in various cancers; nevertheless, its exact function and mechanisms in HCC are still unknown.

## Methods
TMEM88 expression was analyzed in 72 HCC tumors vs. adjacent tissues via qPCR/Western blot. Clinical correlations with survival, AFP, and grading were assessed. Functional assays (proliferation, migration, cell cycle) and Wnt/β-catenin signaling (GSK-3β/β-catenin) were evaluated in vitro. Tumor growth was validated in xenografts.

## Results
This study discovered that patients with high TMEM88 expression had significantly improved overall survival and recurrence-free survival, and high expression was associated with lower AFP levels and favorable pathological grading. Furthermore, overexpression of TMEM88 significantly reduced HCC cell proliferation and migration while decreasing the proportion of cells in the S phase of the cell cycle.Western blot analysis revealed that TMEM88 suppresses the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade by boosting GSK-3β activity and decreasing β-catenin levels. in vivo tumor xenograft models demonstrated that TMEM88 overexpression substantially inhibited tumor development.

## Conclusion
This study suggests that TMEM88 may exert tumor-suppressive effects in HCC, potentially through modulation of the GSK-3β/β-catenin pathway, and its higher expression appears to be associated with more favorable outcomes. These findings indicate that TMEM88 could serve as a prognostic biomarker and may have therapeutic relevance, although further validation is required before clinical application.

## Introduction
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the world’s sixth most prevalent malignancy and the second biggest cause of cancer-related deaths [ ]. Despite major advances in diagnostic and surgical techniques, the prognosis for HCC patients remains poor, with a five-year survival rate of only 30% after surgery [ ]. Emerging research suggests that poor outcomes are mostly due to a high recurrence rate, aggressive tumor development, and metastatic capability [ ]. Thus, understanding the molecular pathways behind HCC growth and metastasis is critical for developing more effective therapeutic approaches [ , ].
Previous studies have demonstrated that certain genes in hepatocellular carcinoma can be successfully expressed through eukaryotic expression vectors, show significant associations with clinicopathological characteristics, and, when stably transfected, markedly suppress tumor cell growth and proliferation both in vitro and in vivo, suggesting a potential role in the progression and metastasis of HCC [ – ], The transmembrane protein (TMEM) family consists of a broad set of members that are ubiquitously expressed on cellular membranes and are largely responsible for transmembrane signal transduction and intercellular material exchange [ ,  ]. Members of this family perform critical roles in maintaining cellular homeostasis, regulating signaling pathways, and influencing processes including cell proliferation and differentiation. The TMEM family has several subtypes, including TMEM16, TMEM88, TMEM43, TMEM106, and TMEM97, each with a unique biological function within cells.
TMEM88, a member of the transmembrane protein family, is formally known as transmembrane protein 88. Unlike catalytic enzymes, TMEM88 is a transmembrane protein that primarily exerts its functions by regulating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby influencing cell proliferation and migration [ – ]. Aberrant expression of TMEM88 has been closely associated with tumor progression in various malignancies, including breast cancer, ovarian cancer, and lung cancer. For instance, studies have revealed that TMEM88 is overexpressed in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and correlates with poor prognosis [ , , ]. Conversely, in ovarian cancer, high TMEM88 expression suppresses cancer cell proliferation and migration [ ]. However, the mechanistic role of TMEM88 in HCC remains inadequately explored [ , ].
Given the critical functions of TMEM88 in other cancers, this study aims to systematically analyze its expression and biological effects in HCC. By investigating the relationship between TMEM88 expression levels and patient prognosis in HCC, we seek to provide novel insights into its potential as a therapeutic target for this malignancy.

## Materials and methods

## Bioinformatics analysis
We used the GEPIA (Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis) database to investigate the expression levels of the TMEM88 gene across different stages of HCC, utilizing samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) dataset that were classified by cancer stage. Violin plots were created to show the distribution of TMEM88 expression at each stage. multivariate Cox regression analysis was first performed using the TCGA dataset, incorporating covariates such as tumor stage, patient age and gender.
Furthermore, the connection between TMEM88 expression levels and overall survival in HCC patients was investigated using the GEPIA and KMplot databases. HCC patient samples from the TCGA dataset were divided into high- and low-expression groups based on TMEM88 expression levels, and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis with log-rank testing was used to analyze survival differences and produce hazard ratios (HR). The KMplot database was used to validate the link between TMEM88 expression and survival outcomes. Survival curves, HR, and 95% confidence intervals were generated to confirm the statistical significance of survival differences (p-value < 0.05). A ROC curve analysis based on TMEM88 expression and patient survival outcomes was performed, from which the optimal cutoff value was determined.
Following that, the STRING v11.0 database (https://string-db.org/) was used to investigate protein-protein interactions involving TMEM88. An interaction network was created by specifying a minimum interaction score in order to find biological processes and related proteins that may include TMEM88.
Finally, the clusterProfiler R package was used to carry out Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis on TMEM88-related genes predicted by the STRING database. The analysis was classified as biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF), We used the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method for p-value adjustment to control the false discovery rate (p-value < 0.05). The enrichment data were shown using bubble plots and bar charts to show the functional categories enriched by TMEM88 and its interacting genes.

## Patients and specimens
From 2014 to 2017, 72 patients with HCC had resection surgery at the Department of Hepatobiliary Surgery at Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University. All surgically removed specimens were promptly frozen and kept at −80 °C until further use. All patients provided informed consent before giving tissue samples, and the study procedure was approved by Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital’s Institutional Review Board. These samples were then used to create tissue microarrays (TMAs).Inclusion criteria: (1) Patients with pathologically confirmed hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). (2) Availability of complete clinicopathological data, follow-up records, and sufficient tumor tissue for molecular analysis. (3) No preoperative anti-cancer treatment, such as transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), radiofrequency ablation, or systemic therapy. (4) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–1. Exclusion criteria: (1) Patients with recurrent HCC, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, or other concomitant malignancies. (2) Patients with severe liver dysfunction (Child–Pugh class C) or uncontrolled comorbidities. (3) Patients who received neoadjuvant or adjuvant therapy prior to tissue sampling. (4) Patients with incomplete clinical information, insufficient tissue quality, or lost to follow-up.

## Cell lines and culture
The hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines (HepG2, SK-Hep1, Huh7, and MHCC-97 H) and the normal liver cell line L-02 were sourced from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). These cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin, and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

## RT-qPCR
Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Takara, 9109), and complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized via reverse transcription using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Perfect Real Time) cDNA synthesis kit (Takara, RR036A), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA served as the template for quantitative PCR analysis, with specific primers designed to detect the expression of target genes, using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara, RR820A). The relevant primers are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Relative expression levels of each gene were normalized to the housekeeping gene GAPDH. Data are presented as fold changes in gene expression across experimental groups from three independent replicates.

## Immunohistochemistry
TMEM88 expression was assessed by immunohistochemistry using a TMEM88 antibody (Abcam), diluted at a 1:200 ratio. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed by heating in EDTA buffer (1 mmol/L, pH = 9.0). TMAs were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody. After one hour of incubation with the secondary antibody, the slides were stained with diaminobenzidine (DAB) and counterstained with hematoxylin under a microscope.

## Western blot
Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (CWBIO, CA) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail. Protein concentrations of the lysates were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (CWBIO, CA). Equal amounts of protein from each sample were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE (Beyotime, CA) and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore, MERCK, Germany). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody.

## Overexpressing TMEM88 and SiRNA transfections
we selected SK-Hep1 and Huh7 for subsequent functional assays as their baseline TMEM88 expression levels were moderate, a stable overexpression cell line was established using a lentiviral system as follows. the titer of the TMEM88-overexpressing lentiviral vector was 8.70 × 10^8 TU/mL, and that of the empty control vector was 1.04 × 10^9 TU/mL The TMEM88 plasmid (Obio Technology, Shanghai) was co-transfected with the lentiviral packaging plasmids psPAX2 (Obio Technology, Shanghai) and pMD2.G (Obio Technology, Shanghai) into HEK 293 T cells using linear polyethylenimine (PEI, Sigma). After 48 h of transfection, the viral supernatant was collected, filtered, and used to infect target cells. Following selection with puromycin (4 µg/ml, MCE), the surviving cell population was analyzed for overexpression by Western blot. The β-catenin plasmid (Obio Technology, Shanghai) was transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to standard protocols. Cells were transfected with siRNA specifically targeting GSK-3β (RIBIO) using the RNAiMAX reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the supplier’s protocol. A scrambled siRNA sequence served as a negative control to ensure specificity of the knockdown.

## Colony formation assay
In the colony formation assay, Sk-hep-1 and Huh-7 cells were seeded at a density of 500 cells per well in 6-well plates and transfected using an adenovirus system carrying the TMEM88 overexpression vector. The control group consisted of untransfected cells. Following seeding, cells were cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 10–14 days until visible colonies were formed. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. After staining, excess dye was removed by washing with PBS, and the cells were air-dried. Colonies were observed and photographed under a microscope. Colony counts were quantified using ImageJ software to assess cell proliferation.

## Edu proliferation assay
For the in vitro labeling assay, the BeyoClick™ EDU Cell Proliferation Kit was used in conjunction with Alexa Fluor 488 (Beyotime, CA) according to the manufacturer’s standard protocol.

## Cell cycle assay
In the cell cycle assay, flow cytometry was employed to assess the impact of TMEM88 on the cell cycle distribution of Sk-hep-1 and Huh-7 cells. The procedure was as follows: After culturing TMEM88 overexpression and control cells for 48 h, cells were harvested by trypsinization. The cells were then washed twice with cold PBS and fixed in 70% cold ethanol for at least 12 h at 4 °C. Following fixation, the cells were stained with a solution containing 50 µg/mL propidium iodide (PI) and RNase A, and incubated in the dark for 30 min to ensure uniform staining. After staining, DNA content was analyzed using flow cytometry, and cell cycle distribution was determined using ModFit software, which provided the proportions of cells in the G1, S, and G2/M phases.

## Transwell assay
Transwell inserts were placed in the wells of a 24-well plate. In the upper chamber, 5 × 10^4 cells were seeded in 200 µL of serum-free DMEM, while the lower chamber was filled with 600 µL of complete culture medium. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. After incubation, the cells on the lower surface of the Transwell membrane were washed twice with PBS, fixed in 95% ethanol for 10 min, and stained with 0.3% crystal violet for 10 min. The number of migrated cells was counted and photographed.

## In vivo tumor xenograft models
Sun Yat-Sen University’s Bioethics Committee approved all animal experiments. Female BALB/c-nu mice (3–5 weeks) were obtained from Gempharmatech Co., Ltd. were used for the study, with five mice per group. A total of 2 × 10^6 Huh-7 and TMEM88 overexpressing Huh-7 cells were subcutaneously injected into the dorsal flanks of the mice. Tumor diameter was measured every two days post-injection. Upon sacrifice, tumor growth rates and weights were compared between the two groups. Proliferation in tumor tissues was assessed using PCNA staining in both groups.

## Humane euthanasia of laboratory animals
For in vivo experiments, mice were euthanized by 5% isoflurane inhalation followed by cervical dislocation, with death confirmed by pupillary dilation, cessation of respiration and heartbeat, and loss of the corneal reflex. Sample size (n = 4 per group) was determined based on preliminary experiments and published studies to ensure sufficient statistical power while minimizing animal use. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups using a computer-generated sequence, and investigators were blinded to group allocation during data collection and analysis.

## Statistical analysis
All experiments were performed in triplicate independently, and all data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, USA). Significance was assessed using the t-test. The prognostic value of TMEM88 expression was evaluated by the Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test. Multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed to identify significant independent prognostic factors. p-values were calculated using a two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. * p < 0.05.

## Results

## Expression of the TMEM88 gene in hepatocellular carcinoma and its potential mechanistic role
This study examined the expression of the TMEM88 gene at various stages of hepatocellular carcinoma HCC (Stage I, Stage II, Stage III, and Stage IV). A violin plot depicts the expression distribution of TMEM88 in HCC patients at stages I–IV (Fig.  A). Significant changes in TMEM88 expression were seen between phases, implying that TMEM88 may be implicated in the progression of HCC.

 

Furthermore, HCC patient samples from the TCGA dataset were classified as high-expression or low-expression (n = 182 each group) based on TMEM88 expression levels. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to create survival curves (Fig.  B). The log-rank test findings revealed a significant survival difference between the two groups (p-value < 0.05), with the hazard ratio (HR) automatically generated by GEPIA indicating a better prognosis for the high-expression group. These data demonstrate that elevated TMEM88 expression is associated with improved overall survival in HCC patients, implying that it may serve as a good prognostic marker in hepatocellular carcinoma.
To better understand the functioning mechanisms of TMEM88, we used the STRING v11.0 database (https://string-db.org/) to created a protein interaction network for TMEM88 and its related genes (Fig.  C). The results showed that TMEM88 interacts closely with several transmembrane proteins and regulatory factors, including TMEM178A, TMEM94, DVL1, and KDM6B, implying that these genes may be involved in shared biological pathways, particularly cell signaling pathways relevant to hepatocellular carcinoma. Using the clusterProfiler R package, we carried out Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis on the TMEM88-related genes (TMEM178A, TMEM94, DVL1, and KDM6B) found in the STRING database. The enrichment analysis results were shown using bubble and bar plots, which demonstrated the level of enrichment in several functional categories (Fig.  D and E). The study found that TMEM88 and its related genes are highly involved in a variety of biological processes, including postsynaptic potential modulation, protein stability, and neurodevelopmental processes. Molecular function research shows that TMEM88 and associated genes play a crucial role in β-catenin binding and histone demethylation. These findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms of TMEM88 in hepatocellular carcinoma and set the framework for further investigation of its role in the disease.

## Downregulation of TMEM88 in HCC
To study the expression of TMEM88 in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), we measured the mRNA and protein levels of TMEM88 in 72 HCC specimens and their paired adjacent non-tumor liver tissues using qPCR and immunohistochemistry. TMEM88 mRNA and protein levels were significantly lower in HCC tissues (n = 72, p < 0.05) compared to non-tumor liver tissues (Fig.  A, B). We also used qRT-PCR and western blot analysis to look at the expression of TMEM88 in different HCC cell lines (HepG2, SK-Hep1, Huh7, MHCC-97 H) as well as the normal liver cell line LO2. In line with the tissue data, TMEM88 expression was much lower in HCC cell lines than in LO2 cells (Fig.  C, D). Overall, our results show that TMEM88 is downregulated in HCC.

 

## High TMEM88 expression predicts favorable prognosis
We further evaluated the prognostic significance of TMEM88 expression in HCC. The results demonstrated that patients with high TMEM88 expression exhibited significantly longer recurrence-free survival (Fig.  E). Moreover, high TMEM88 expression was associated with extended overall survival (Fig.  F). Low TMEM88 expression was found to correlate with higher pathological grades (G3 vs. G1 + G2) and elevated levels of alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) (Fig.  G). Collectively, these findings suggest that TMEM88 expression may serve as a critical marker of favorable prognosis in HCC.

## TMEM88 inhibits the proliferation and migration of hepatocellular carcinoma
To explore TMEM88’s cellular biological functions, we overexpressed TMEM88 in two HCC cell lines (SK-Hep1 and Huh7) using the pLVX-Puro-TMEM88 vector. Western blot and qPCR analysis indicated that TMEM88 expression increased 4.2-fold in SK-Hep1 cells and 5.04-fold in Huh7 cells when compared to the negative control (Fig.  A, B). The effects of TMEM88 on HCC cell proliferation were determined using colony formation assays and EDU incorporation. The results showed a considerable reduction in proliferation in TMEM88-overexpressing cells (Fig.  C, D). Flow cytometry analysis also revealed a decrease in the S-phase proportion of TMEM88-overexpressing HCC cells relative to controls (Fig.  E). Additionally, Western blot analysis revealed that TMEM88 overexpression reduced N-cadherin and vimentin levels while increasing E-cadherin expression (Fig.  F). Furthermore, transwell migration experiments revealed that overexpressing TMEM88 reduced the number of migrating HCC cells by more than 50% (Fig.  G).

 

## TMEM88 regulates β-Catenin expression via GSK-3β
TMEM88 inhibits the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. To investigate the underlying mechanism, we analyzed the mRNA levels of key downstream components and found that TMEM88 reduced cyclin D1 and c-myc expression, while β-catenin mRNA remained unchanged (Fig.  A), suggesting a post-transcriptional mode of regulation. Consistently, TMEM88 decreased β-catenin protein levels, indicating reduced protein stability (Fig.  B). We also observed increased GSK-3β and phosphorylated β-catenin (p-β-catenin) in the TMEM88-overexpressing group, implying that TMEM88 enhances GSK-3β activity to promote β-catenin phosphorylation and degradation (Fig.  B). Importantly, co-immunoprecipitation (CO-IP) further demonstrated that TMEM88 directly binds to GSK-3β(Figure S5), thereby suppressing the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Moreover, silencing GSK-3β with siRNA or inhibiting it with TDZD-8 reversed the TMEM88-induced decrease in β-catenin and reduced p-β-catenin levels (Fig.  C). Together, these results confirm that TMEM88 modulates β-catenin phosphorylation and degradation through direct interaction with GSK-3β, ultimately altering the Wnt/β-catenin signaling cascade.

 

## TMEM88 inhibits proliferation and migration via β-Catenin downregulation
To assess the effect of TMEM88 overexpression on cell proliferation, we conducted EDU and colony formation assays following β-catenin plasmid transfection (Figure S2-S4). The results demonstrated that β-catenin overexpression partially reversed the inhibitory effect of TMEM88 on cell proliferation (Fig.  A and B). Furthermore, we observed that, compared to the control group, transfection with the β-catenin plasmid increased the migration rate of TMEM88-overexpressing HCC cells (Fig.  C). Additionally, the inhibitory effects of TMEM88 on proliferation and migration were reversed when GSK-3β expression was silenced with siRNA or inhibited by the GSK-3β inhibitor TDZD-8 (Fig.  D and E).Collectively, these findings suggest that TMEM88 suppresses proliferation and migration in HCC cells by reducing the expression of β-catenin.

 

## TMEM88 inhibits HCC proliferation and migration in vivo
In this study, we looked at the inhibitory effects of TMEM88 overexpression on HCC proliferation and migration in vivo. A comparative tumor weight analysis demonstrated that TMEM88 overexpression considerably decreased total tumor weight (Fig.  A and B). Tumor weight was significantly larger in the control group compared to the TMEM88 overexpression group, indicating that TMEM88 overexpression effectively slows tumor growth. The comparison with a 95% confidence interval revealed that this inhibitory impact was statistically significant (p = 0.0289), supporting TMEM88’s potential involvement as a tumor suppressor. Tumor growth curves (Fig.  C) confirmed the inhibitory effect of TMEM88 overexpression on tumor growth.Throughout the trial, tumors in the control group continued to develop fast (Fig.  D), whereas tumors in the TMEM88 overexpression group grew substantially slower, demonstrating that TMEM88 plays an important role in limiting tumor progression. To visually assess tumor size, ex vivo tumor comparisons were performed. The results revealed a significant reduction in tumor volume in the TMEM88 overexpression group compared to the control group, giving more evidence for TMEM88’s inhibitory influence on tumor growth. This observation was consistent with the volume and weight results, highlighting TMEM88’s therapeutic potential as a tumor suppressor. Finally, histological investigation was carried out to determine the effect of TMEM88 on tumor tissue morphology (Fig.  E). Tumors from the control group showed dense tumor cell proliferation and irregular tissue architecture, whereas tumors from the TMEM88 overexpression group showed less cell proliferation activity and a more structured tissue structure. These findings indicate that TMEM88 may lower tumor malignancy by reducing tumour cell proliferation. Collectively, these experimental results significantly indicate TMEM88’s potential as a therapeutic target for cancer treatment.

 

## Discussion
HCC is one of the most common cancers globally, with a typically dismal prognosis, especially in advanced stages and in patients with associated cirrhosis [ , ]. Despite recent breakthroughs in targeted medicines and immunotherapy, the overall survival rate for HCC is still regrettably poor [ , ]. As a result, understanding novel molecular processes and devising successful targeted therapeutics is critical.
Recent research, both domestic and foreign, have underlined the critical significance of TMEM88 in various malignancies [ , , ]. However, research into the significance of TMEM88 in HCC is minimal. This study thoroughly explores the expression and molecular processes of TMEM88 in HCC, indicating that it plays an important role in suppressing cell proliferation and migration. Unlike previous findings in other malignancies, we revealed that TMEM88 has a tumor-suppressive effect in HCC. Previously, TMEM88 was found to enhance tumor growth in breast and lung malignancies; however, our findings show that TMEM88 expression is considerably downregulated in HCC, with high expression associated with a better prognosis. This implies that the activity of TMEM88 may change between malignancies, potentially exerting differential effects via diverse signaling pathways inside specific tumor microenvironments.
To learn more about the underlying processes of TMEM88 in HCC, we used the STRING database to perform GO functional enrichment analysis, built a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, and ran GEPIA survival analysis [ ,  ]. The high expression of TMEM88 was substantially related with increased overall survival compared to the low expression group (GEPIA P = 0.00083, KMplot P = 5.6e-7). Furthermore, we found substantial differences in TMEM88 expression levels throughout HCC stages (P = 0.00319), supporting the close relationship between high TMEM88 expression and a favorable prognosis.GO functional enrichment analysis shows that TMEM88 and its associated genes are involved in several biological activities, including postsynaptic potential modulation, protein stability, and neurological functions (P.adjust < 0.05). These mechanisms are intricately connected to tumor cell proliferation, differentiation, and signaling [ ,  ]. Furthermore, TMEM88 was discovered to interact with various transmembrane proteins and regulatory factors, including TMEM178A, DVL1, and KDM6B, implying that it may regulate cell proliferation, migration, and signaling via different routes [ ]. These findings lay the theoretical groundwork for future research into the complicated molecular pathways of TMEM88 in HCC.
In this work, we found a considerable downregulation of TMEM88 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), with expression levels strongly related to clinical prognosis. HCC patients with high TMEM88 expression had significantly longer relapse-free and overall survival, whereas low TMEM88 expression was strongly related with greater pathological grade and AFP levels. We used qPCR and immunohistochemistry to examine TMEM88 mRNA and protein levels in 72 HCC samples and matched neighboring non-tumor liver tissues. The results showed that TMEM88 expression was considerably lower in HCC tissues than in normal liver tissues.To further establish its tumor-suppressive activity, we increased TMEM88 levels in SK-Hep1 and Huh7 cells. TMEM88 overexpression significantly suppressed HCC cell growth and decreased the fraction of cells in the S phase. Furthermore, Transwell assays revealed that TMEM88 overexpression significantly reduced HCC cell migration and invasion while changing the expression of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers, as evidenced by a decrease in N-cadherin and vimentin levels and an increase in E-cadherin expression.These data imply that TMEM88 suppresses HCC cell growth and migration. This suggests that TMEM88 could act as a predictive biomarker and therapeutic target for HCC [ , ].
Our study reveals that TMEM88 plays a unique tumor-suppressive role in HCC, principally by regulating the GSK-3β-mediated Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Dysregulated activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in HCC is linked to tumor proliferation, treatment resistance, and metastatic potential [ , ]. TMEM88 inhibits this mechanism by increasing GSK-3β-mediated β-catenin degradation. TMEM88 lowers β-catenin protein expression by increasing phosphorylation, decreasing Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity. However, it does not significantly affect β-catenin mRNA levels. These data imply that TMEM88 suppresses tumors through post-transcriptional control of β-catenin.
GSK-3β and β-catenin are key factors in the development and progression of HCC. Dysregulation of the GSK-3β/β-catenin axis causes uncontrolled cell proliferation, increased motility, and invasion in HCC cells. Activation of β-catenin, a major component of the Wnt signaling pathway, stimulates tumor growth and metastasis. Abnormal GSK-3β activity affects β-catenin breakdown, compromising cellular homeostasis.Recent studies have highlighted the potential of targeting the GSK-3β/β-catenin signaling system to decrease HCC growth, providing innovative treatment methods [ , ]. We found that restoring β-catenin expression or inhibiting GSK-3β activity resulted in a dramatic reversal of TMEM88’s tumor-suppressive effects. TMEM88’s tumor-suppressive action relies on GSK-3β activity and β-catenin degradation. These findings underscore the crucial role of TMEM88 as a regulator of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, and support its reliance in reducing HCC cell proliferation and migration.
Furthermore, overexpression of TMEM88 significantly affected the expression of molecular markers associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [ , ]. We found that N-cadherin and vimentin were significantly downregulated (P < 0.01 and P < 0.05, respectively), while E-cadherin was overexpressed (P < 0.01). These changes suggest that TMEM88 inhibits tumor cell migration and invasion by decreasing the EMT process. Previous studies have shown that β-catenin signaling directly modulates the transcription of EMT-related genes, including those encoding E-cadherin repressors such as Snail and Slug [ ]. Therefore, we propose that TMEM88 suppresses β-catenin activity, thereby leading to a reversal of EMT marker expression. Nonetheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that TMEM88 also affects other EMT-associated pathways (e.g.,TGF-βor Notch signaling), which may cooperate with β-catenin to regulate EMT. Given that EMT is a crucial process in HCC cell metastasis, these findings emphasize the importance of TMEM88 in limiting tumor invasion and metastasis.
Previous studies have shown that numerous molecules can suppress hepatocellular carcinoma, thereby improving patient prognosis [ , ]. In this study, we not only established TMEM88’s involvement in reducing HCC cell growth and migration in vitro, but also proved its anti-tumor effects in vivo with animal models. We established an HCC xenograft model in mice and found that TMEM88 overexpression significantly inhibited tumor development (P < 0.05), demonstrating powerful anti-tumor action in vivo. Furthermore, TMEM88 overexpression was shown to significantly suppress tumor cell growth and migration inside the body. Furthermore, migration experiments confirmed the suppressive effect of TMEM88 on HCC cell metastatic capacity in vivo. These findings lay a solid foundation for the future development of TMEM88-based gene therapy techniques and point to its potential as a crucial therapeutic target in hepatocellular cancer treatment.
This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the clinical cohort was derived from a single center with a relatively limited sample size, which may introduce regional bias and restrict the generalizability of the findings. Second, although our results suggest that TMEM88 exerts tumor-suppressive effects in hepatocellular carcinoma, we did not perform gene knockout or knockdown experiments to directly verify the consequences of TMEM88 functional loss. Future studies should include both larger, multicenter patient cohorts and in-depth mechanistic investigations using genetic models to strengthen the causal evidence. In addition, exploring how TMEM88 modulation may be integrated with current therapeutic strategies for liver cancer could provide valuable translational insights.

## Conclusion
This study comprehensively elucidates the tumor-suppressive role of TMEM88 in HCC and shows that low levels of expression are associated with a poor prognosis. TMEM88 modulates the GSK-3β/β-catenin signaling pathway, limiting HCC cell proliferation and migration. These findings not only improve our understanding of the molecular mechanisms that underpin HCC, but also give a sound theoretical platform for exploring TMEM88 as a possible prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target.Future study should include larger sample sizes and multicenter validation to ensure that these findings are generalizable and clinically applicable. With the introduction of gene editing tools such as CRISPR, precise control of TMEM88 expression may be possible. Further investigation of TMEM88’s participation in additional signaling pathways, as well as the development of TMEM88-targeted therapeutic techniques, may provide novel therapy options for HCC patients.
