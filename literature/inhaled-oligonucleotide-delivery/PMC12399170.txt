## Highlights
•Myocardial microcirculation in SIMD: This study identifies myocardial microcirculatory damage as a core mechanism in SIMD, offering a novel therapeutic target for intervention.
•c-FLIP's protective role in myocardial microcirculation through the regulation of mitochondrial autophagy via FUNDC1: We demonstrate that c-FLIP regulates mitochondrial autophagy via FUNDC1, reducing mitochondrial damage and microcirculatory impairment, providing a potential treatment strategy for SIMD.
•JNK in the c-FLIP/FUNDC1–mediated mitochondrial autophagy pathway: In later stages, c-FLIP relies on JNK to regulate FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy, and the interaction between p-JNK and FUNDC1 is critical for autophagy homeostasis, offering new insights into SIMD mechanisms.
•Therapeutic potential for SIMD: Our findings highlight c-FLIP as a therapeutic target for reducing myocardial microcirculatory damage in SIMD.

## Summary
This study investigates the role of c-FLIP in sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction (SIMD), focusing on cardiac microcirculation and mitochondrial autophagy. Using SIMD rat and LPS-induced cardiac microvascular endothelial cell injury models, we found that c-FLIP deficiency disrupts mitochondrial homeostasis, exacerbating microcirculatory damage. c-FLIP differentially regulates mitochondrial autophagy via FUNDC1. Overexpression of c-FLIP balances autophagy, protects mitochondria, reduces inflammation, and ameliorates SIMD, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target.

Sepsis, a life-threatening organ dysfunction n caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, poses a significant global public health challenge. Among its complications, sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction (SIMD) stands as one of the most severe clinical problems with limited therapeutic options. Defined as an acute syndrome of cardiac failure in septic patients, SIMD primarily features reduced ejection fraction and left ventricular dilation, symptoms that may recover within 7 to 10 days. Epidemiological data indicate that the incidence of SIMD ranges between 18% and 40%, with a mortality rate reaching as high as 70% to 90% once developed. Despite over 3 decades of research, the complex pathophysiological mechanisms of SIMD remain largely undisclosed, leading to a scarcity of effective treatments. Therefore, exploring the pathogenic mechanisms and signaling pathways of SIMD, and identifying viable therapeutic targets are crucial for improving patient survival and quality of life.
The health of cardiac microcirculation is vital for maintaining heart function. Particularly under severe stress conditions such as infection, shock, and trauma, the integrity of microcirculation is key to ensuring the heart's proper functioning. , Microcirculatory damage can trigger myocardial inflammation and exacerbate cardiac diseases. , Current research on SIMD has mainly focused on direct myocardial damage, , whereas studies on upstream changes in myocardial microcirculation remain insufficient. This study explored the mechanisms of myocardial microcirculatory damage under SIMD, elucidating how it triggers myocardial inflammation and further myocardial injury, thereby providing a theoretical basis for developing new therapeutic strategies.
Mitochondria within microvascular endothelial cells play a crucial role in maintaining the normal structure and function of the microcirculation, and their impairment can lead to the disruption of the microcirculatory system. Mitochondrial autophagy is a key process that maintains cellular homeostasis by removing damaged and dysfunctional mitochondria. When mitochondrial autophagy becomes dysregulated, damaged mitochondria accumulate, leading to adenosine triphosphate (ATP) deficiency, calcium imbalance, and disruption of intracellular homeostasis. Furthermore, dysfunctional mitochondria release mitochondrial DNA and reactive oxygen species (ROS), which activate inflammatory pathways and oxidative stress. This combination of metabolic disturbances and inflammation further deteriorates cellular homeostasis, ultimately resulting in cellular and organ injury. , Considering that mitochondrial damage and the dysregulation of mitochondrial autophagy are likely major factors contributing to the impairment of myocardial microcirculation in SIMD, it is imperative to further elucidate these mechanisms. Doing so is essential for effectively repairing mitochondria and enhancing cardiac function.
In this context, cellular FLICE-inhibitory protein (c-FLIP) has multiple roles in maintaining cellular homeostasis, acting as a key regulator of physiological and pathological processes such as autophagy, apoptosis, and necrosis. c-FLIP demonstrates the ability to bidirectionally modulate autophagy, with particularly complex and underexplored roles in mitochondrial autophagy. , Moreover, c-FLIP confers significant protective effects in various cardiac pathologies, including heart failure, left ventricular hypertrophy, and abnormal cardiac development. , , Notably, the expression of c-FLIP in cardiac microvascular endothelial cells (CMECs) may play a decisive role in stabilizing myocardial microcirculation. At the same time, Fun14 domain-containing protein 1 (FUNDC1), a mitochondrial outer membrane receptor protein, mediates receptor-dependent mitochondrial autophagy by interacting with LC3 on autophagosomes. The activity of this autophagy pathway depends on the phosphorylation and dephosphorylation of FUNDC1 under various pathological conditions. For instance, in septic mice, decreased FUNDC1 dephosphorylation levels suppress mitochondrial autophagy, whereas antioxidant treatment restores dephosphorylation and enhances autophagic activity. In myocardial ischemia–reperfusion (I/R) injury, increased dephosphorylation of FUNDC1 correlates with enhanced mitochondrial autophagy and reduced I/R injury. Collectively, FUNDC1 functions as a crucial link between cellular stress responses and mitochondrial autophagy through its receptor-dependent mechanism. Additionally, c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK), a key stress-activated protein kinase, is extensively involved in the regulation of numerous physiological and pathological processes. Research has identified c-FLIP as an upstream modulator of the JNK pathway, modulating its signaling. For example, in cardiac remodeling models, c-FLIP mitigates apoptosis and fibrosis by inhibiting JNK signaling; Wang et al have shown that c-FLIP reduces inflammation by suppressing JNK phosphorylation. Furthermore, JNK signaling is crucial in regulating mitochondrial autophagy. Under hypoxic conditions, JNK activation phosphorylates BNIP3, enhancing its stability and facilitating Bnip3-mediated mitochondrial autophagy. JNK additionally modulates mitochondrial autophagy in neurons through the FUNDC1 pathway. Based on these mechanisms, it is hypothesized that c-FLIP may influence mitochondrial autophagy indirectly by regulating JNK activity.
This study proposes a new hypothesis that c-FLIP dynamically regulates mitochondrial autophagy activity via FUNDC1 to maintain the homeostasis of myocardial microcirculation. The absence of c-FLIP may lead to early overactivation and late suppression of mitochondrial autophagy, disrupting mitochondrial homeostasis, exacerbating myocardial microcirculatory damage, and ultimately resulting in SIMD. Using both in vivo and in vitro experimental models and various biotechnological approaches, this study aims to elucidate the protective role of c-FLIP in SIMD, comprehensively evaluate structural and functional changes in myocardial microcirculation, demonstrate that myocardial microcirculatory damage activates an inflammatory cascade leading to SIMD, and further investigate the stage-specific regulatory effects of c-FLIP on mitochondrial autophagy and the signaling mechanisms underlying myocardial microcirculatory damage. These findings may provide new therapeutic targets for understanding the pathogenesis of SIMD.

## Materials and Methods

## Establishment of an in vivo model of SIMD in rats
All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 85-23, revised 1996) and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xi'an Jiaotong University, Xi'an, China.
Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, aged 8 weeks and weighing 180 to 200 g, were purchased from the Experimental Animal Center of Hubei Province, China. The SIMD model was established following previously reported methods. , Cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) surgery included key steps such as intubation and continuous inhalation anesthesia with 5% isoflurane throughout the surgical procedure; disinfection of the anterior abdominal area followed by a 1.0-cm midline abdominal incision; exteriorization of the cecum with ligation 1.5 cm from the cecal tip, followed by perforation with an 18G needle 5 times to release partial intestinal contents. After perforation, the cecum was repositioned, and the abdominal incision was closed in layers. Postoperatively, rats were rehydrated subcutaneously with sodium lactate Ringer's solution at 37 °C at a dose of 3 mL/100 g, and measures were taken to ensure their comfort and warmth. Sham-operated rats underwent the same procedure without cecal ligation and perforation. The experimental rats were divided into sham (n = 40) and CLP (n = 40) groups. Each group was further subdivided into 5 subgroups based on specific postoperative time points (0 hours, 3 hours, 6 hours, 12 hours, and 24 hours) with 8 rats in each subgroup. Next, cardiac function was assessed at various postoperative intervals using echocardiography (40 MHz, X5 vet, Esaote) measuring left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS), the maximal rate of pressure rise (Dp/DT), and left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVDd). Successful model establishment was confirmed by significant cardiac dysfunction within a specific timeframe post-CLP. Heart and peripheral blood samples were collected at each postoperative time for further analysis. Rats were euthanized using CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death.
To investigate the impact of c-FLIP on SIMD, rats were injected via tail vein 36 hours before modeling with pDC315-mCMV-c-FLIP adenoviral plasmid (Ad-c-FLIP) or control adenoviral plasmid (Ad-NC) provided by Wuhan Biotao Biotechnology Co, Ltd, at a dose of 5 × 107 plaque forming units per rat. The experimental groups were organized as follows to establish the SIMD model and assess the effects of c-FLIP overexpression: Ad-NC + sham group (n = 8), Ad-c-FLIP + sham group (n = 8), Ad-NC + SIMD group (n = 8), and Ad-c-FLIP + SIMD group (n = 8).

## Establishment of an in vitro model of lipopolysaccharide-induced CMEC injury
First, isolation and culture of CMECs: CMECs were isolated from male 3- to 4-week-old SD rats. The anesthetic agent used was isoflurane, administered continuously via inhalation at a dose of 5%. Euthanasia was performed using CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation to ensure death. The hearts were disinfected using 75% alcohol, and tissue blocks approximately 1 mm3 in size were excised from the outer layer of the left ventricle, followed by a wash with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing double antibiotics. The tissue blocks were digested at 37 °C for 1 hour using 0.1% collagenase II (Sigma, C6885-25MG), then treated for 10 minutes with an equal volume of 0.1% trypsin (Biosharp, BL501A), and the reaction was terminated with fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were collected by filtration and centrifugation, then resuspended in DMEM (Servicebio, G4510) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% double antibiotics, 5 ng/mL vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) (Peprotech, 400-31), and 50 mg/L heparin sodium (Sigma, H3149). Cells were seeded onto culture dishes pretreated with poly-L-lysine (Procell, PB180523) and cultured at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. The medium, containing 10% FBS, was replaced after 24 hours and subsequently every 2 days until the cells reached the required density for passaging. The identity of CMECs was confirmed through CD34 immunofluorescence staining. The experiments utilized cells from the first to the third passages. Cells were detached from the culture surface using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA.
Next, various concentrations of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) (Sigma, L4391) were applied to CMECs for different durations to determine the optimal concentration and duration for successful in vitro modeling.
To investigate the effects of c-FLIP on CMECs, pDC315-mCMV-c-FLIP Ad-c-FLIP and Ad-NC were obtained from Wuhan Biotao Biotechnology Co, Ltd and used to infect CMECs.
To inhibit FUNDC1 activity on the basis of c-FLIP overexpression, CMECs infected with Ad-c-FLIP were subjected to siRNA knockdown of FUNDC1 activity 48 hours before LPS treatment using pDC316-ZsGreen-shRNA-rFUNDC1-183 adenoviral plasmid (siRNA-FUNDC1) and control adenoviral plasmid (siRNA-NC) provided by Wuhan Biotao Biotechnology Co, Ltd
To inhibit the JNK pathway, SP600125 (SP) (10 μmol/L, MCE, HY-12041) was used 36 hours before LPS treatment, and to activate the JNK pathway, anisomycin (Ani) (5 μmol/L, MCE, HY-18982) was used 12 hours before LPS treatment.

## Serum Biomarker Analysis
Peripheral blood was collected from the tail vein of anesthetized rats and centrifuged at 3,000 g for 15 minutes at 4 °C to isolate the serum, which was then stored at −80 °C until further analysis.
The concentrations of myocardial injury and inflammatory biomarkers, such as cardiac troponin I (cTnI), cardiac troponin T (cTnT), B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and procalcitonin (PCT), were measured using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits: cTnI (Elabscience, E-EL-R1253), cTnT (Elabscience, E-EL-R0151c), BNP (Elabscience, E-EL-R0126), IL-6 (FineTest, ER0042), TNF-α (FineTest, ER1393), and PCT (FineTest, Er1235). Assays were conducted according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a FlexStation 3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices), which detects colorimetric changes in the reaction wells, with the intensity of the color change being directly proportional to the concentrations of the biomarkers.
The activities of myocardial injury biomarkers creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and aspartate transaminase (AST) were quantified using biochemical assay kits: CK-MB (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, E006-1), LDH (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute), and AST (Elabscience, E-BC-K236-M). Each assay was performed strictly according to the manufacturer’s protocols. The enzymatic activities were determined by monitoring absorbance changes at 450 nm with a microplate reader, which reflects the rate of substrate conversion specified in each kit.

## Western blot analysis
Samples were lysed in ice-cold RIPA buffer containing 1 mmol/L phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride to ensure effective inhibition of proteases. The lysates were then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C. Equal amounts of protein were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Millipore). After transfer, the membranes were blocked for 2 hours at room temperature in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 and 5% bovine serum albumin. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: GAPDH (Xianzhi Biotechnology Co, Ltd AB-P-R 001, 1:1000), COXIV (Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co, Ltd, 11242-1-AP, 1:1000), c-FLIP (Affinity Biosciences, DF7010, 1:1000), total FUNDC1 (t-FUNDC1) (Cell Signaling Technology, 49240, 1:1000), phosphorylated FUNDC1 (p-FUNDC1) (Affinity Biosciences, AF0001, 1:1000), total JNK (t-JNK) (Affinity Biosciences, AF6318, 1:1000), p-JNK (Affinity Biosciences, AF3318, 1:1000), IL-6 (Affinity Biosciences, DF6087, 1:1000), IL-8 (Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co, Ltd, 27095-1-AP, 1:1000), TNFα (Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co, Ltd, 26405-1-AP, 1:1000), MMP9 (Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co, Ltd, 27306-1-AP, 1:500), LC3 (Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co, Ltd, 14600-1-AP, 1:2500), Tomm20 (Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co, Ltd, 11802-1-AP, 1:8000), p62 (Affinity Biosciences, AF5384, 1:1000), cyt C (Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co, Ltd, 66264-1-Ig, 1:20000), and caspase-3 (Wuhan Sanying Biotechnology Co, Ltd, 19677-1-AP, 1:500). After primary antibody incubation, the membranes were washed and incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 hours at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized and quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics).

## Electron microscopy of cardiac microvessels and CMECs
The ultrastructure of cardiac microvessels or CMECs was assessed using transmission electron microscopy. Samples of heart tissue or CMECs were fixed for 2 to 4 hours at 4 °C in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), followed by washing in the same buffer. The samples were then fixed for 2 hours at room temperature in 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4). After gradual dehydration through an ethanol series, the samples were infiltrated overnight in a mixture of acetone and Epon 812 resin, followed by embedding in pure resin and polymerization at 60 °C for 48 hours. Sections were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate for 15 minutes, and then air-dried overnight. Observations were made using an FEI Tecnai G20 TWIN transmission electron microscope (FEI Company).

## Immunofluorescence analysis
Tissue or cell samples underwent dehydration, clearing, paraffin embedding, and sectioning, followed by antigen retrieval. Nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 5% goat serum at room temperature for 30 minutes. The samples were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including: CD34 (Abcam, ab81289, 1:100), c-FLIP (Affinity Biosciences, DF7010, 1:100), caspase-3 (Proteintech, 66470-2-Ig, 1:100), caspase-9 (Proteintech, 66169-1-Ig, 1:100), endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Sc-376751, 1:100), endothelin 1 (ET-1) (Novus Biologicals, NB300-526, 1:100), vascular endothelial cadherin (VE-cadherin) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-9989, 1:100), t-FUNDC1 (Bioss Antibodies, Bs-13227R, 1:100), and p-JNK (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-6254, 1:100). The samples were subsequently incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 hour, followed by 4 washes with PBS with Tween. Finally, nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 minutes. Microscopic observation and image acquisition were performed using an Olympus BX53 microscope.

## Immunohistochemistry
Samples were dehydrated, cleared, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and then deparaffinized. The sections were repaired in 0.01 mol/L citrate buffer for 15 minutes, and endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were then blocked with 5% goat serum at room temperature for 30 minutes. Overnight incubation at 4 °C was performed with primary antibodies against albumin (Affinity, DF6396, 1:100) and neutrophil elastase (GeneTex, GTX03640, 1:100). This was followed by a 30-minute incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at 37 °C. Sections were stained with diaminobenzidine (DAB) and counterstained for 2 minutes, then dehydrated and coverslipped. Imaging and image analysis were conducted using an Olympus BX53 microscope.

## CCK-8 assay
CMECs were revived and passaged, then seeded at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well in a 96-well plate. After treating the cells with various concentrations of LPS for specified durations, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 hours. Cell viability was assessed by measuring the optical density at 450 nm.

## Hematoxylin and eosin staining of paraffin sections
To prepare sections for different observational purposes, tissue processing was adjusted accordingly. For sections focused solely on myocardial tissue structure, perfusion was performed to remove blood and reduce background interference from red blood cells. For sections aimed at visualizing red blood cell arrangement within myocardial microvessels, perfusion was omitted to preserve the natural distribution of red blood cells within the microvessels. Tissue samples were then dehydrated through an ascending alcohol series, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Deparaffinized sections were first stained with Mayer's hematoxylin to highlight nuclei, followed by staining the cytoplasm with 1% aqueous eosin. After staining, sections were dehydrated again through an ethanol series and cleared with xylene before being permanently mounted. The prepared slides were used for morphological analysis under a microscope, with lower magnification applied for observing myocardial tissue structure and higher magnification for examining red blood cell arrangement within the microvessels.

## Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction
Total RNA was extracted from samples using Trizol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and separated using chloroform (Sigma-Aldrich). cDNA was synthesized using the SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Amplification of cDNA was performed using the PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) along with specific primers. Quantitative analysis of RNA expression was conducted using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction primers used in this study included: Rat GAPDH primers, forward ACAGCAACAGGGTGGTGGAC and reverse TTTGAGGGTGCAGCGAACTT, primer size 253 bp; Rat FUNDC1 primers, forward CAAGACTATGAGAGCGATGACGAG and reverse AGTCACGCCACCCATTACTATCTG, primer size 156 bp.

## Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling assay
The terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay was performed using a kit provided by Promega Corporation. Samples underwent dehydration, clearing, paraffin embedding, and sectioning. Following this, the sections were treated with proteinase K and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, then washed and equilibrated. TdT enzyme reaction mixture was added to incubate the samples, followed by treatment with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide to inhibit endogenous peroxidases. The samples were then stained with streptavidin-HRP and DAB, followed by counterstaining, dehydration, clearing, and mounting. Apoptotic cells were identified and documented using microscopy.

## Mitochondrial membrane potential assay
The mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) was assessed using a Δψm assay kit produced by Biocytogen (C2006, Nantong). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, treated cells were incubated with the JC-1 staining solution. Subsequent quantitative analysis of Δψm was conducted using a Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX flow cytometer.

## Mitochondrial calcium detection
Mitochondrial calcium levels in CMECs were measured using the Rhod-2 AM cell-permeable Ca2+ fluorescent probe (Yeasen Biotechnology, 40776ES72). Following the manufacturer's instructions, treated cells were stained with diluted Rhod-2 AM dye for 20 minutes. Subsequently, calcium levels were quantitatively analyzed using a Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX flow cytometer.

## Mitochondrial ATP content assay
The ATP content in mitochondria was quantitatively analyzed using an ATP assay kit (Biocytogen, A095-1-1,). Mitochondrial samples were first subjected to preprocessing, homogenization, and heat shock. Subsequently, a series of preprepared reaction reagents were used to conduct standardized biochemical reactions. The concentration of ATP was determined by measuring the absorbance.

## Mitochondrial ROS assay
The production of mitochondrial ROS was assessed using a ROS assay kit (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, S0033S). The fluorescent probe DCFH-DA was used to measure mitochondrial ROS levels under various conditions. Quantitative detection was performed using a fluorescence spectrophotometer and fluorometer (Molecular Devices, Flexstation 3).

## Mitochondrial permeability transition pore assay
The opening status of mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) was assessed using an mPTP assay kit produced by Biocytogen (C2009S). In this assay, Calcein AM serves as a fluorescent probe that, once hydrolyzed by intracellular enzymes, emits fluorescence within the cell. Cobalt chloride (CoCl2) was used to quench the fluorescence in non-mitochondrial regions, thereby isolating the mitochondrial fluorescence. During the assay, Calcein AM and CoCl2 were mixed at recommended concentrations and added to the cells, which were then incubated in the dark. Flow cytometry was used to analyze the cells, evaluating the opening of mPTP based on changes in mitochondrial fluorescence intensity; a decrease in fluorescence indicates the opening of mPTP.

## Mitochondrial-lysosomal dual staining and confocal microscopy
In this study, we employed Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos (Beyotime, C1049B) and Lyso-Tracker Red (Beyotime, C1046) for dual staining of mitochondria and lysosomes in live cells. Cells were incubated with these probes at 37 °C for 60 minutes to label mitochondria and lysosomes, respectively. After incubation, the probe solution was removed, and the cells were washed 3 times with culture medium preheated to 37 °C. Subsequently, fluorescence-labeled cells were observed and imaged under an Olympus FV3000 laser confocal microscope.

## Transwell migration assay
The migration ability of CMECs was assessed using a Transwell assay. Cells were pretreated with serum-free medium and then seeded at a density of 3 × 105 cells/mL into the upper chamber of a 24-well Transwell plate equipped with an 8-μm pore polycarbonate membrane. The lower chamber was filled with DMEM containing 10% FBS to promote cell migration. After 24 hours of incubation, cells were stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 10 minutes, and migration was subsequently quantified.

## Transendothelial electrical resistance measurement of CMECs
Transendothelial electrical resistance (TER) is a critical parameter for assessing the ion conductance of the CMECs barrier, commonly used to evaluate the functional integrity of cell junctions. A decrease in TER typically indicates impaired barrier function due to contraction or adhesion failure in CMECs. For resistance measurements, cells were cultured in 6-well plates until they reached an appropriate density. Resistance testing was conducted using a Millipore Millicell ERS-2 ohmmeter set to the ohm measurement mode. The test involved measuring the resistance at 3 random points per well, ensuring that the electrodes did not contact the cells or the bottom of the culture plate. Recorded resistance values were calibrated against measurements from blank wells using the same method.

## Coimmunoprecipitation analysis
This study employed coimmunoprecipitation to investigate the interaction between phosphorylated JNK (p-JNK) and FUNDC1. Cells were lysed using a commercial lysis buffer (Amresco), and total protein concentration was determined after lysis using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime) to ensure consistent input protein amounts across samples. Each sample contained 500 μg of total protein diluted in 500 μL of PBS. To the lysate, 10 μL of p-JNK antibody (Abcam) or t-FUNDC1 antibody diluted 1:1,000 (Cell Signaling Technology) were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C to form antibody-protein complexes. For each sample, 30 μL of Protein A+G Agarose beads (Millipore, 50% slurry) were added to capture the complexes, and the incubation was continued for 6 hours at 4 °C. The complexes were collected by low-speed centrifugation (2,500 g, 4 °C, 5 minutes) and washed 3 times with PBS to remove nonspecific bindings. To ensure consistency, all samples were resuspended in 30 μL of 2× loading buffer for Western blot analysis, allowing direct comparison of band intensities. The protein complexes were then analyzed by Western blot to confirm their interactions.

## Statistical Analysis
Results are presented as the mean ± SEM. Differences between 2 groups were evaluated using Student's t-test, whereas differences among multiple groups were compared using 1-way analysis of variance with Tukey's post hoc test for multiple pairwise comparisons. Before comparisons, data were evaluated for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test to confirm their suitability for parametric analysis. The Kaplan-Meier curve with log-rank test was used to compare survival curves. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 7 (GraphPad Software, Inc.), and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Successful establishment of the SIMD rat model and observation of myocardial microcirculation impairment with suppressed c-FLIP expression
Echocardiographic assessments of LVEF, LVFS, Dp/DT, and LVDd were conducted to evaluate the establishment of the SIMD model. The results ( ) demonstrated that significant cardiac dysfunction was induced as early as 6 hours post-CLP, with further pronounced effects observed at 12 hours. This confirms that 12 hours post-CLP is an optimal time point for the successful establishment of the SIMD model. illustrates the survival status of rats post-CLP, indicating that their survival duration was adequate for completing all designated assessments within the planned timeframe.
 Cardiac injury markers in the blood ( ) showed a significant rise in cTnT at 3 hours post-CLP, and increases in CK-MB, cTnI, LDH, AST, and BNP from 6 hours, persisting until 24 hours. This indicates significant myocardial injury in the SIMD model at 12 hours post-CLP.
Analysis of inflammatory markers and infection indicators ( ) demonstrated significant elevations in IL-6 at 3 hours and in TNF-α and PCT starting at 6 hours post-CLP, continuing up to 24 hours. These findings indicate substantial changes in inflammation and infection status in the SIMD model at 12 hours post-CLP.
Histological examination of myocardial tissue via hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining ( ) revealed that myocardial fibers in the CLP group began to show vacuolar degeneration at 3 hours, with significant shrinkage by 6 hours. By 12 hours, the myocardial fibers exhibited extensive shrinkage and some dissolution, which were replaced by connective tissue. At 24 hours, there was widespread fiber shrinkage accompanied by severe hemorrhage, proliferative connective tissue, and pyknosis. These results confirm that the SIMD model established at 12 hours post-CLP caused significant structural damage to the myocardium.
Apoptosis in myocardial tissue was quantified using TUNEL assays ( and ), which showed a significant increase in apoptotic myocardial cells in the CLP group starting at 6 hours post-CLP and continuing through 24 hours. Activities of caspase-3 and caspase-9 ( and ) were significantly elevated at 6 hours post-CLP and continued to increase. These results confirm that the SIMD model induced significant myocardial apoptosis at 12 hours post-CLP.
 Immunofluorescence staining ( ) revealed that both the sham and CLP groups at 0 hours displayed well-organized myocardial fibers and abundant CD34-positive microvessels, with high expression of c-FLIP in myocardial microvascular endothelial cells. However, by 6 hours post-CLP, there was a significant decrease in CD34-positive microvessels and their c-FLIP expression, a trend that persisted until 24 hours post-CLP. These findings indicate that as time progresses post-CLP, both myocardial microvessels and their c-FLIP expression are significantly affected.
These studies conclusively confirm that the SIMD model was effectively established at 12 hours post-CLP, particularly noting significant impairment in myocardial microcirculation and suppression of c-FLIP expression at this critical time point. Hence, 12 hours post-CLP has been uniformly adopted as the establishment time for the SIMD rat model in subsequent in vivo experiments.

## Establishment of an in vitro LPS-induced CMEC injury model, revealing the inhibition of c-FLIP, mitochondrial damage, and changes in mitophagy activity, highlighting the key roles of c-FLIP, JNK, and FUNDC1
To determine the optimal concentration and duration of LPS intervention for effectively inducing CMEC injury, we observed CMEC morphology using optical microscopy and quantified cell proliferation with the CCK-8 assay. After 12 hours of treatment with various concentrations of LPS, optical microscopy showed significant suppression of CMEC growth, marked by a concentration-dependent reduction in cell density and diverse changes in morphology ( ). CCK-8 results showed that cell proliferation decreased significantly as LPS concentrations increased ( ). These findings confirm that 10 μg/mL of LPS is sufficient to induce notable CMEC injury. Subsequent time-dependent studies were conducted with 10 μg/mL LPS. Optical microscopy revealed that as the intervention duration increased, CMEC growth was significantly inhibited, particularly after 12 hours, which showed sparse cells and varied morphologies, and a significant increase in floating dead cells was observed at 24 and 36 hours ( ). The CCK-8 assay further confirmed that cell proliferation rates significantly decreased at 12, 24, and 36 hours post-treatment with 10 μg/mL LPS ( ). These results verify that a 12-hour or longer intervention with 10 μg/mL LPS can effectively induce CMEC injury, successfully establishing an in vitro LPS-induced CMEC injury model.
 Western blot analysis was conducted to monitor changes in c-FLIP expression over time ( and ). The results demonstrated a significant reduction in c-FLIP expression beginning at 12 hours post-LPS treatment, continuing to decrease up to 36 hours. This confirms the model's effectiveness in significantly suppressing c-FLIP expression, highlighting its suitability for studying intracellular regulatory mechanisms.
In summary, interventions with 10 μg/mL LPS for 12 hours and beyond, not only successfully established an in vitro LPS-induced CMEC injury model, but also caused significant suppression of c-FLIP expression. These findings underscore the model’s potential for simulating and investigating cellular injuries and their molecular mechanisms.
The LPS-induced CMEC injury model revealed significant mitochondrial damage and alterations in mitophagy activity, highlighting the critical roles of c-FLIP, JNK, and FUNDC1.
Initially, transmission electron microscopy was used to observe microstructural changes in CMECs after 12 hours of treatment with 10 μg/mL LPS ( ). In the control group, mitochondria were plentiful, with normal morphology and intact structures. By contrast, the LPS-treated CMECs displayed vacuolar degeneration, reduced mitochondrial numbers, abnormal morphologies, incomplete mitochondrial membranes, and disordered cristae, along with evident mitochondrial autophagy. These observations confirm that 10 μg/mL LPS for 12 hours can induce mitochondrial damage and trigger mitochondrial autophagy.
Subsequent Western blot analysis assessed changes in mitochondrial autophagy-related proteins within this in vitro LPS-induced CMEC injury model ( ). Early in the intervention, at 6 and 12 hours, the LC3 II/LC3 I ratio gradually increased, indicating enhanced formation of autophagosomes; the mitochondrial LC3 II/LC3 I ratio also rose, suggesting increased mitochondrial autophagy activity. The expression of the mitochondrial outer membrane protein Tomm20 gradually decreased, reflecting a reduction in mitochondrial numbers, whereas the degradation of the downstream autophagy marker p62 accelerated, indicating increased autophagic activity. However, after 24 hours of LPS intervention, the expression of mitochondrial autophagy-related proteins showed no significant difference from the control group. By 36 hours post-intervention, a significant reversal in the expression of these proteins was observed, indicating suppressed mitochondrial autophagy activity. These findings reveal the dynamic changes in mitochondrial autophagy in the LPS-induced CMEC injury model, with an increase in activity during the early stage, followed by suppression in the later stage as the intervention extends.
Further, at 36 hours post-LPS treatment, Western blot was employed to assess the expressions of c-FLIP, t-JNK, p-JNK, t-FUNDC1, and p-FUNDC1 ( ). Compared with the control group, expressions of c-FLIP were significantly down-regulated, whereas expressions of p-JNK/t-JNK and p-FUNDC1/t-FUNDC1 were significantly up-regulated in the LPS group. Separately, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction was used to measure FUNDC1 mRNA levels ( ), which were significantly down-regulated in the LPS group. These alterations highlight the critical roles of c-FLIP, JNK, and FUNDC1 in mitochondrial damage and autophagy processes during the later stages of the LPS-induced CMEC injury model.
Based on these experimental results, subsequent in vitro studies will continue using 10 μg/ml LPS for intervention, specifically targeting 12 and 36 hours as critical observation points to further analyze the specific intracellular mechanisms of enhanced and suppressed mitochondrial autophagy during these periods.

## Myocardial microcirculation injury induces activation of myocardial inflammatory responses leading to SIMD, whereas overexpression of c-FLIP can mitigate these damages and inflammatory responses
Firstly, to investigate changes in myocardial microcirculation and c-FLIP expression following tail vein injection of Ad-c-FLIP, we performed immunofluorescence staining analyses ( ). Results showed that, compared with the sham group, the SIMD group exhibited significant reductions in the number of CD34-positive microvessels and markedly decreased c-FLIP expression in myocardial microvessels. These changes were substantially reversed following c-FLIP overexpression, confirming that tail vein injection of Ad-c-FLIP can improve the state of myocardial microcirculation and significantly enhance the expression of c-FLIP within it.
 Secondly, to evaluate the effects of endothelial apoptosis, structural-functional changes, and the protective role of c-FLIP overexpression on myocardial microcirculation homeostasis:
Immunofluorescence staining analyses ( and ) revealed that compared with the sham group, caspase-3 activity significantly increased in the myocardial microcirculation of the SIMD group, which was reversed by c-FLIP overexpression. This indicates that c-FLIP can effectively reduce endothelial apoptosis in myocardial microcirculation.
Transmission electron microscopy ( ) showed that in the sham group, myocardial microcirculation lumens were full, and vessel walls were smooth without protrusions. Conversely, in the SIMD group, endothelial cell nuclei were swollen, and vessel walls irregularly protruded, leading to narrowed microvascular lumina. These abnormalities were not observed in the myocardial microcirculation of rats with c-FLIP overexpression, confirming the ameliorative effect of c-FLIP on structural damage to the myocardial microcirculation.
Immunofluorescence staining ( ) also showed that in the SIMD group, eNOS content was significantly reduced, and ET-1 expression was significantly increased compared to the sham group. These changes were reversed by c-FLIP overexpression, confirming that c-FLIP can improve the dysregulation of vasomotor control in SIMD.
Given the injuries, narrowing or blockage of the myocardial microcirculation, and vasomotor disorders can lead to slowed or interrupted blood flow. Accordingly, we used nonperfused myocardial HE staining to observe the arrangement of red blood cells. Normally, blood flow in myocardial microcirculation is laminar, with red cells arranged in a rosette pattern. When the microcirculation lumen is narrowed or blocked, flow becomes turbulent, causing red cells to appear spherical or stacked, indicating local blood stasis. As shown in , in the sham group, red cells were linearly aligned at the lumen center; in the SIMD group, they appeared spherically stacked. By contrast, red cell alignment in c-FLIP overexpressing rats was near normal. This suggests that c-FLIP overexpression helps maintain normal blood flow in myocardial microcirculation.
We also studied the role of c-FLIP in regulating the endothelial barrier of myocardial microcirculation. VE-cadherin, a key molecule, maintains the filtering and resistance functions of myocardial microcirculation endothelium by forming local gap junctions. Immunofluorescence staining results ( and ) showed that VE-cadherin expression was abundant and continuous on the myocardial microcirculation endothelium in the sham group. In the SIMD group, VE-cadherin expression was significantly down-regulated and discontinuous, reflecting the destruction of the myocardial microcirculation endothelial barrier; c-FLIP overexpression restored the expression and continuity of VE-cadherin. This supports the potential protective role of c-FLIP in maintaining the integrity of myocardial microcirculation endothelial barrier functions.
 Damage to the myocardial microcirculation barrier allows substances from the blood to permeate into myocardial tissue. Albumin, a large molecular protein that is normally confined to the vasculature, infiltrates myocardial tissue when this barrier is compromised. Immunohistochemical staining ( and ) revealed that, compared with the sham group, there was significant albumin permeation into the myocardial tissue in the SIMD group, whereas c-FLIP overexpression reduced this permeation. This suggests that c-FLIP overexpression can protect the integrity of the myocardial microvascular endothelial barrier and reduce abnormal permeation.
The myocardial microcirculation barrier serves as a crucial defense line preventing the infiltration of inflammatory cells and mediators. When this barrier is disrupted, inflammatory cells and damaging factors can directly permeate into the myocardial tissue, triggering myocardial inflammatory responses. This study explores the activation of myocardial inflammation and examines the impact of c-FLIP overexpression on this response:
Immunohistochemical staining was used to detect neutrophil elastase, a specific marker for neutrophils ( and ). It revealed a significant increase in neutrophil infiltration in the myocardial tissue of the SIMD group. Overexpression of c-FLIP was able to reverse this infiltration of inflammatory cells.
Western blot analysis indicated that, relative to the sham group, expressions of IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, and MMP-9 proteins were significantly elevated in the myocardial tissue of the SIMD group ( ). Overexpression of c-FLIP effectively suppressed these inflammatory molecules.
These findings demonstrate that under SIMD conditions, the destruction of the endothelial barrier of myocardial microcirculation facilitates the infiltration of inflammatory cells and mediators into the myocardium, activating a cascade of myocardial inflammatory responses. Overexpression of c-FLIP can mitigate this excessive myocardial inflammatory reaction.
Ultimately, myocardial microcirculation damage, luminal narrowing, vasomotor disorders, slowed or stagnant blood flow, and barrier disruption lead to the penetration of injurious factors into myocardial tissue, triggering a cascade of myocardial inflammatory responses that exacerbate myocardial damage and promote the development of SIMD. Previous studies have indicated that c-FLIP can preserve the homeostasis of myocardial microcirculation and inhibit myocardial inflammatory responses. The following section further explores the effects of c-FLIP overexpression on SIMD.
By evaluating cardiac function parameters such as LVEF, LVFS, Dp/DT, and LVDd ( ), a significant decline in these parameters was observed in the SIMD group compared to the sham group. However, overexpression of c-FLIP significantly improved cardiac function. Additionally, myocardial injury markers such as CK-MB, cTnT, and LDH were notably elevated in the SIMD group, with c-FLIP overexpression significantly reducing these markers' levels ( ). Histological examination by HE staining of myocardial tissue structure ( ) showed that, in the sham group, myocardial fibers were evenly arranged without any noticeable inflammatory cell infiltration, and erythrocytes were linearly arranged within myocardial microvessels. By contrast, the SIMD group exhibited disorganized myocardial fibers, local twisting, and notable mononuclear cell infiltration around myocardial microvessels, with erythrocytes forming spherical aggregates within the vessels. Following c-FLIP overexpression, myocardial tissue structure appeared normal. These results indicate that overexpression of c-FLIP can optimize cardiac function, reduce myocardial damage, and restore myocardial tissue structure, effectively ameliorating the pathological state of SIMD.

## c-FLIP differentially regulates the early enhancement and late suppression of mitochondrial autophagy activity in LPS-induced CMEC injury via FUNDC1, exerting comprehensive protective effects
Initially, we observed the changes in mitochondrial autophagy activity in CMECs induced by 12 hours of LPS treatment. Electron microscopy ( ) revealed intact CMECs and mitochondrial structures in the control group without noticeable autophagosome or autolysosome formation. By contrast, LPS treatment for 12 hours resulted in severe structural damage to CMECs and their organelles, including disorganized mitochondrial structures and cristae disruption, accompanied by an increased formation of autophagosomes and autolysosomes, indicating enhanced autophagic activity. Overexpression of c-FLIP significantly mitigated these structural damages and prevented the formation of significant autophagosomes or autolysosomes, suggesting c-FLIP as an upstream regulator of mitochondrial autophagy, whose protective effect is dependent on this process. Further, to test whether c-FLIP specifically regulates FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy, we silenced FUNDC1 in cells overexpressing c-FLIP. This resulted in severely disorganized mitochondrial structures and increased formation of autophagosomes and autolysosomes, indicating that c-FLIP might regulate mitochondrial autophagy activity through FUNDC1, playing a protective role in the early stages of LPS-induced CMEC injury.
 To further elucidate the early changes in mitochondrial autophagy activity in CMECs after LPS induction, we analyzed the expression of related proteins ( ). At 12 hours post-LPS treatment, there was a significant increase in both LC3Ⅱ/LC3Ⅰ and mito-LC3Ⅱ/mito-LC3Ⅰ ratios, accompanied by a decrease in Tomm20 expression and reduced levels of p62, indicating an enhancement of mitochondrial autophagy activity. Overexpression of c-FLIP then returned these indicators to levels nearly equivalent to those of the control group, demonstrating that c-FLIP regulates mitochondrial autophagy. Furthermore, silencing FUNDC1 in cells overexpressing c-FLIP led to an increased mitochondrial autophagy activity, indicating that c-FLIP modulates this process through FUNDC1.
In summary, c-FLIP, through FUNDC1-mediated pathways, effectively controls the enhancement of mitochondrial autophagy in the early stages of LPS-induced injury in CMECs, thereby playing a protective role.
Subsequently, we examined changes in mitochondrial autophagy activity in CMECs 36 hours after LPS induction ( and ). Western blot analysis indicated that LC3Ⅱ/LC3Ⅰ and mito-LC3Ⅱ/mito-LC3Ⅰ ratios significantly decreased, and Tomm20 expression and p62 accumulation increased, suggesting a suppression of mitochondrial autophagy activity in the late phase of LPS-induced CMEC injury. Overexpression of c-FLIP significantly restored these indicators, demonstrating its capability to reactivate mitochondrial autophagy during this phase.
To confirm whether c-FLIP specifically activates FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy, we noted ( and ) that post-36 hours of LPS treatment, the p-FUNDC1/t-FUNDC1 ratio increased, indicating FUNDC1 inactivation. Overexpression of c-FLIP restored FUNDC1 activity, suggesting that c-FLIP activates mitochondrial autophagy via FUNDC1. Further validation showed that silencing FUNDC1 after overexpressing c-FLIP suppressed mitochondrial autophagy, confirming c-FLIP's dependency on FUNDC1 for regulating mitochondrial autophagy in the late phase of CMEC injury.
Additionally, mitochondrial and lysosomal co-staining ( and ) post-36 hours of LPS intervention showed reduced colocalization, indicating suppressed mitochondrial autophagy activity. Overexpression of c-FLIP significantly increased this colocalization, indicating restoration of mitochondrial autophagy. However, silencing FUNDC1 under c-FLIP overexpression disrupted this fusion again. These results show that mitochondrial autophagy is reduced in the later stages of LPS-induced CMEC injury. c-FLIP plays a protective role by boosting FUNDC1-dependent mitochondrial autophagy.
In summary, c-FLIP significantly enhances mitochondrial autophagy activity in the late stages of LPS-induced CMEC injury by activating the FUNDC1-mediated autophagy pathway, thereby exerting a protective effect.

## c-FLIP, through FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy, maintains mitochondrial function, inhibits apoptosis, and restores endothelial function in the late phase of LPS-induced CMEC injury
Mitochondrial autophagy plays a critical role in sustaining mitochondrial function. In the late stages of LPS-induced CMEC injury, reduced mitochondrial autophagy leads to mitochondrial Ca2+ overload, spikes in mitochondrial ROS, decreased ATP production, increased mPTP opening (decreased non-opening of mPTP), and reduction in Δψm. Overexpression of c-FLIP can reverse these mitochondrial dysfunctions. Conversely, silencing FUNDC1 under conditions of c-FLIP overexpression causes a recurrence of these mitochondrial dysfunctions ( ). This indicates that in the late stages of LPS-induced CMEC injury, c-FLIP protects mitochondrial function through FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy.
 Mitochondrial autophagy supports cellular health by maintaining mitochondrial quality and function, whereas insufficient mitochondrial autophagy and mitochondrial dysfunction can activate mitochondrial apoptosis pathways, leading to cell apoptosis. This study found that during the late stage of LPS-induced CMEC injury, suppressed mitochondrial autophagy leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, which subsequently causes inhibited cell growth, reduced density, and altered cell morphology ( ); a significant decrease in cell proliferation rate ( ); Western blot ( ) and immunofluorescence ( and ) results show increased migration of cyt-C to the cytoplasm, decreased mito-cyt C, and increased expression of cyt-C, caspase-9, and caspase-3; Flow cytometry ( and ) shows an increased rate of apoptosis. Overexpression of c-FLIP can reverse these effects, but silencing FUNDC1 reinstates the signs of cellular damage and apoptosis. This indicates that during the late stage of LPS-induced CMEC injury, suppressed mitochondrial autophagy triggers cell damage and apoptosis, with c-FLIP providing protection through FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy.
 Finally, damage and apoptosis in CMECs impair their migratory and barrier functions. Transwell assays ( and ) show that 36 hours post-LPS induction, the chemotactic migration ability of CMECs significantly declines, which is restored by c-FLIP overexpression but declines again upon FUNDC1 silencing. TER assays ( ) show that 36 hours post-LPS induction, TER resistance decreases, indicating increased endothelial permeability and loss of barrier function. This effect is reversible by c-FLIP overexpression, but silencing FUNDC1 abolishes the improvement. In summary, c-FLIP plays a crucial role in protecting CMECs migratory ability and barrier function through FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy.

## In the late phase of LPS-induced injury, c-FLIP effectively protects CMECs by regulating FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy through JNK
To explore the regulatory mechanisms of c-FLIP on FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy, we consulted relevant literature and learned that JNK is positioned downstream of c-FLIP and upstream of FUNDC1, influencing the structure and function of specific cytoplasmic substrates. , , Preliminary experiments confirmed JNK's involvement in mitochondrial autophagy during the late phase of LPS-induced injury in CMECs ( and ). We hypothesized that c-FLIP’s regulation of FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy might depend on JNK activity. As demonstrated in , inhibiting JNK with SP improved FUNDC1 activity and mitochondrial autophagy in the late phase of LPS-induced CMEC injury, similar to the effects observed with c-FLIP overexpression. By contrast, activating JNK with Ani in c-FLIP-overexpressing cells led to FUNDC1 inactivation and suppressed mitochondrial autophagy. These findings confirm JNK's role upstream of FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy and reveal the mechanism by which c-FLIP regulates this process via the JNK pathway.
 Additionally, immunofluorescence staining showed that in the late phase of LPS-induced injury, inhibition of JNK with SP significantly increased the colocalization of mitochondria and lysosomes, mirroring the effects of c-FLIP overexpression. Conversely, reactivation of JNK with Ani in c-FLIP-overexpressing cells reduced the overlap of mitochondria and lysosomes. These observations ( and ) further underscore JNK's crucial role in the c-FLIP-regulated mitochondrial autophagy process, indicating that JNK activity significantly affects this autophagy.
To further investigate how JNK modulates FUNDC1 activity, we evaluated the interaction between p-JNK and FUNDC1. Coimmunoprecipitation experiments ( ) showed an interaction between p-JNK and FUNDC1 in the control group. However, this interaction was significantly weakened in the late phase of LPS-induced CMEC injury. Overexpression of c-FLIP or inhibition of JNK activity reversed this trend, whereas reactivation of JNK with Ani on a c-FLIP overexpression background again diminished the interaction between p-JNK and FUNDC1. Confocal microscopy observations ( and ) also displayed similar trends, with colocalization of p-JNK and FUNDC1 in CMECs, which significantly decreased in the late phase of LPS-induced injury. This decrease was reversible by c-FLIP overexpression or JNK inhibition but diminished again following reactivation with Ani. These results suggest that intrinsic interactions between p-JNK and FUNDC1 maintain the steady state of mitochondrial autophagy, external stimuli such as LPS can weaken these interactions, thereby decreasing mitochondrial autophagy activity, while c-FLIP can enhance these interactions, thus improving mitochondrial autophagy.

## Discussion
This study comprehensively delineates the protective role of c-FLIP in SIMD, involving JNK and FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy. Our key findings are as follows ( ):
1.In SIMD, there is a marked absence of c-FLIP expression on myocardial microcirculation.
2.The absence of c-FLIP is closely associated with various myocardial microcirculatory disorders such as endothelial apoptosis, luminal narrowing, dysregulated vasomotion, slowed blood flow, and barrier dysfunction, all of which trigger myocardial inflammatory responses, exacerbating myocardial damage and leading to SIMD; conversely, overexpression of c-FLIP significantly ameliorates these myocardial microcirculatory injuries and inflammatory responses.
3.c-FLIP differentially regulates mitochondrial autophagy in the myocardial microcirculation depending on FUNDC1 activity, with a loss of c-FLIP leading to an early enhancement and later suppression of mitochondrial autophagy, creating a dynamic imbalance.
4.Mechanistic studies indicate that in the late phase, c-FLIP exerts its protective effects on the myocardial microcirculation through the JNK-regulated FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy pathway, whereas the absence of c-FLIP leads to aberrations in this signaling pathway, resulting in dysregulated mitochondrial autophagy responses and mitochondrial homeostasis disruption, culminating in myocardial microcirculatory injury. In summary, our study unveils the comprehensive protective mechanisms of c-FLIP in SIMD, providing a scientific basis for future therapeutic strategies.
 This study successfully established a rat model of SIMD, allowing us to delve deeply into the pathogenesis of SIMD. Importantly, while the ultimate target of SIMD is the myocardium itself, we discovered that the microcirculation situated upstream of the myocardium significantly influences the disease process. The myocardial microcirculation is a delicate network of blood vessels within the heart, directly connected to myocardial cells, responsible for the delivery of blood, oxygen, and nutrients, as well as the removal of metabolic waste. , This system plays a crucial role in regulating myocardial perfusion, maintaining myocardial metabolism, and participating in myocardial contraction. Thus, any damage to the myocardial microcirculation directly affects the metabolic processes and physiological functions of myocardial cells, potentially triggering cardiac diseases. Current research on SIMD primarily focuses on direct myocardial injury, often overlooking the upstream microcirculation damage; meanwhile, existing studies on myocardial microcirculation are mostly centered on conditions such as myocardial I-R injury and diabetic cardiomyopathy, which are not SIMD. Given this context, our study concentrates on the myocardial microcirculation, observing phenomena such as apoptosis of endothelial cells, narrowing of vascular lumina, dysregulation of vasomotion, sluggish blood flow, and barrier dysfunction in the pathological state of SIMD. Further, we confirmed that these myocardial microcirculatory disorders trigger myocardial inflammatory cascades, exacerbating myocardial injury and ultimately leading to SIMD. Therefore, further investigating the mechanisms of myocardial microcirculation damage and identifying new therapeutic targets are crucial for improving SIMD, providing a foundation for the development of interventional drugs.
Mitochondria are irreplaceable in maintaining the stability of organ microcirculation, and their dysfunction is a primary cause of microcirculatory disorders and organ damage triggered by infections, shock, and trauma. Specifically, data suggest a close link between sepsis-induced organ dysfunction and mitochondrial damage, with evidence indicating that the extent of mitochondrial damage correlates closely with the prognosis of septic patients. In this study, we observed significant mitochondrial structural and functional impairments in CMECs following LPS induction, indicating this phenomenon as a crucial contributor to SIMD-related myocardial microcirculation damage. Mitochondrial damage often coincides with mitochondrial autophagy, which regulates mitochondrial quality and maintains their dynamic equilibrium; however, inappropriate mitochondrial autophagy may exacerbate cellular damage. Research indicates that mitochondrial autophagy is inhibited during myocardial microvascular I-R injury, suggesting its critical role in myocardial microcirculation damage. Additionally, the response of myocardial mitochondrial autophagy during sepsis is dual: in some cases, activating mitochondrial autophagy can protect the myocardium, , whereas in others, inhibiting mitochondrial autophagy may improve cardiac function. Our research further observed time-dependent changes in mitochondrial autophagy activity during LPS-induced CMEC injury, characterized by early enhancement and late suppression. During this dynamic process, early-stage mitochondrial autophagy, if moderately enhanced, may have a protective role by clearing damaged mitochondria and safeguarding cellular function; however, excessive enhancement could further compromise cell function. Although our study did not directly determine whether early mitochondrial autophagy was moderate or excessive, electron microscopy results from and revealed significant CMEC structural disarray, including substantial subcellular organelle damage, indicating marked CMEC injury. At the late stage, as mitochondrial damage worsened and autophagy activity was suppressed, mitochondrial homeostasis was disrupted, activating mitochondrial apoptotic pathways and ultimately leading to cell apoptosis. These findings confirm that an imbalance in mitochondrial autophagy is a key factor in SIMD myocardial microcirculation damage, suggesting that regulating the balance of mitochondrial autophagy within the myocardial microcirculation may be an effective strategy for improving SIMD. Furthermore, our study found that CMECs exhibited time-dependent changes in mitochondrial autophagy activity during injury, which disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis, exacerbated CMEC and myocardial microcirculation damage, and potentially weakened oxygen and metabolic support to downstream cardiomyocytes, thereby affecting mitochondrial autophagy in myocardial cells. Currently, no studies have confirmed whether myocardial cell mitochondrial autophagy exhibits similar time-dependent changes under similar induced injury conditions. We boldly speculate that the imbalance in mitochondrial autophagy in CMECs may have a complementary or synergistic relationship with autophagic responses in myocardial cells, jointly contributing to the pathology of SIMD. Future studies should further investigate the interactive effects of mitochondrial autophagy between myocardial microcirculation and myocardial cells to elucidate their specific roles and regulatory mechanisms in the pathogenesis of SIMD.
c-FLIP is a naturally occurring cysteine protease inhibitor increasingly recognized for its multifunctional role in cellular homeostasis. , It plays a critical regulatory role in autophagy, apoptosis, necrosis, and immune suppression, and its dysregulation is closely associated with the development and progression of inflammation, cancer, Alzheimer's disease, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. However, the role of c-FLIP in regulating mitochondrial autophagy remains understudied and controversial. For instance, some studies have found that c-FLIP positively regulates mitochondrial autophagy by enhancing the stability of Beclin-1, whereas others have shown that ophiopogonin B activates mitochondrial autophagy in human lung cancer cells by down-regulating c-FLIP expression. Despite the expression of c-FLIP in microvascular endothelial cells of the heart, lungs, and brain, its role in myocardial microcirculation has not been adequately explored. In our study, we observed a significant reduction in c-FLIP expression in the myocardial microcirculation during SIMD, closely linked to myocardial microcirculatory dysfunction. Further experiments demonstrated that overexpression of c-FLIP improved myocardial microcirculatory damage and the associated myocardial inflammatory response, significantly controlling the symptoms of SIMD. Continued research revealed that the absence of c-FLIP leads to an imbalance in mitochondrial autophagy, whereas its overexpression can protect mitochondria by regulating mitochondrial autophagy activity, alleviating CMEC damage, and improving myocardial microcirculation function. These findings highlight the central role of c-FLIP as a cardiac protector in the formation and progression of SIMD and suggest that c-FLIP-based regulatory strategies could offer new therapeutic directions for SIMD, laying a theoretical foundation for the development of future treatments.
Mitochondrial autophagy primarily occurs through receptor-dependent and receptor-independent pathways. The receptor-dependent pathways, including FUNDC1 and Bnip3, where Bnip3-mediated autophagy is generally considered lethal, contrast with the non–receptor-dependent PINK-Parkin pathway, which is potentially damaging under specific conditions. This study focuses on FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy, regarded as a protective signaling pathway in myocardial microcirculation following I-R injury, though its specific role in SIMD-induced myocardial microcirculation dysfunction has not been thoroughly documented. Our research demonstrates that the protective effects of c-FLIP in LPS-induced CMEC damage consistently rely on FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy. Specifically, during early stages where mitochondrial autophagy activity is heightened, c-FLIP mitigates cellular damage by modulating this activity, a protective effect associated with the FUNDC1 pathway. It is speculated that the protective role of c-FLIP may also involve the suppression of other detrimental mitochondrial autophagy pathways. In later stages, when mitochondrial autophagy is insufficient, overexpression of c-FLIP enhances FUNDC1 activity and reactivates mitochondrial autophagy, thus improving mitochondrial function, reducing cellular damage and apoptosis, and effectively protecting CMECs. This finding suggests that FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy is a protective signaling pathway and becomes the primarily affected mitochondrial autophagy pathway during late stages of SIMD myocardial microcirculation injury.
JNK plays a pivotal role in the progression of various diseases, particularly through modulating cellular stress responses and signaling pathways that affect the pathophysiology of diseases. Research has shown that c-FLIP can regulate the JNK pathway to influence various biological processes. For instance, Liu et al found that silybin, by activating the c-FLIP/JNK pathway, can ameliorate symptoms of non-alcoholic fatty liver in mice. Similarly, Wang et al and others discovered that c-FLIP can alleviate inflammatory responses by inhibiting JNK phosphorylation. Moreover, according to the literature , and studies by Wang et al and others, JNK is also involved in regulating mitochondrial autophagy, particularly the FUNDC1 pathway in neuronal cells. Against this backdrop, our study focuses on the role of JNK in SIMD-induced myocardial microcirculation injury and explores how c-FLIP regulates FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy. Through a series of experimental setups, including LPS-induced injury, c-FLIP overexpression, JNK inhibition, and JNK activation on the basis of c-FLIP overexpression, we have thoroughly confirmed the critical upstream role of JNK in FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy during late stages, revealing how c-FLIP potentially regulates this process through the JNK pathway.
A key research question is how JNK regulates FUNDC1 activity during the late stages of LPS-induced CMEC injury. Studies have shown that JNK can regulate the structure and function of nuclear transcription factors and specific substrate molecules in the cytoplasm, which are crucial for maintaining cellular function. The activity regulation of FUNDC1 is primarily managed through transcriptional regulation and post-translational phosphorylation modifications, where an increase in the baseline levels of dephosphorylated FUNDC1 can trigger its mediated mitochondrial autophagy. , Building on this theory, our study further explored the specific interactions between JNK and FUNDC1 during the late stages of LPS-induced CMEC injury. We found that the inherent interaction between p-JNK and FUNDC1 might help maintain the transcription and dephosphorylation levels of FUNDC1, thereby sustaining the homeostasis of FUNDC1 pathway-mediated mitochondrial autophagy. In the late stages of LPS-induced CMEC injury, the interaction between p-JNK and FUNDC1 is weakened, which is associated with a decrease in FUNDC1 mRNA and an increase in the inactive form of FUNDC1. These changes inhibit the mitochondrial autophagy pathway mediated by FUNDC1. To explain this phenomenon, it is considered that JNK regulates the activity of downstream proteins through phosphorylation, including transcription factors and other signaling proteins. We speculate that the weakened interaction between JNK and FUNDC1 could impact JNK-regulated transcription factors that directly manage the transcription of the FUNDC1 gene, thus reducing the expression of FUNDC1 mRNA. The reduced interaction implies a decrease in direct phosphorylation of FUNDC1 by JNK, yet an observed increase in p-FUNDC1 suggests possible involvement of other protein kinases enhancing FUNDC1 phosphorylation. Additionally, we observed that c-FLIP could enhance the interaction between p-JNK and FUNDC1, and promote the restoration of transcription and dephosphorylation levels of FUNDC1, thereby improving the activity of FUNDC1 pathway-mediated mitochondrial autophagy. Therefore, we suggest that JNK’s role in c-FLIP-regulated FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy might be realized through c-FLIP's influence on the interaction between these 2 proteins. These findings provide a new perspective on understanding the mechanism of JNK's role in SIMD myocardial microcirculation injury, particularly its potential importance in regulating FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy.
This study highlights the crucial role of c-FLIP in maintaining myocardial microcirculation homeostasis, particularly through its regulation of mitochondrial autophagy via FUNDC1. We discovered that in the early stages, c-FLIP regulates mitochondrial autophagy dependent on FUNDC1, and the absence of c-FLIP leads to enhanced mitochondrial autophagy activity. By contrast, during the late stages, the lack of c-FLIP results in the activation of the JNK pathway, subsequently inhibiting the FUNDC1-mediated mitochondrial autophagy. Whether it is the early enhancement or late suppression of mitochondrial autophagy, any alteration can lead to mitochondrial damage and further affect myocardial microcirculation, ultimately triggering SIMD. These findings not only underscore the complex interactions between c-FLIP and mitochondrial autophagy but also provide new therapeutic targets for the imbalance of myocardial microcirculation homeostasis and its related diseases, especially SIMD.

## Study limitations
First, this study reveals that in the early stages of LPS-induced CMEC injury, c-FLIP regulates mitochondrial autophagy activity via the FUNDC1 pathway, thereby exerting a protective role in the myocardial microcirculation. However, the specific mechanisms underlying the early enhancement of mitochondrial autophagy in this disease model have not been thoroughly investigated. Additionally, our in vitro experimental results need to be validated in in vivo SIMD models to ensure their applicability and relevance at different stages of myocardial microcirculation injury. Future research will focus on exploring the mechanisms of early mitochondrial autophagy enhancement in detail and aim to integrate in vitro experimental results more closely with in vivo model data to comprehensively understand the dynamic changes and regulatory mechanisms of mitochondrial autophagy in myocardial microcirculation injury. Furthermore, despite some progress, this study has not systematically explored the differential effects of myocardial microcirculation injury in the early and late stages, nor the differential regulation mechanisms of key proteins c-FLIP, FUNDC1, and JNK at different stages of myocardial microcirculation injury. This limitation indicates that future research needs to further design experiments to deeply investigate these differences, thereby enhancing our understanding of the pathogenesis of SIMD and its therapeutic potential. Finally, this study only used male SD rats to avoid hormonal variability associated with the female estrous cycle and to maintain consistency with existing cardiovascular research models. However, future studies should include female rats to investigate potential sex differences in SIMD and their impact on the pathology of myocardial microcirculation injury, thereby improving the translational relevance of the findings.

## Clinical Significance
This study reveals that c-FLIP regulates mitochondrial autophagy via FUNDC1 to protect myocardial microcirculation, providing a new direction for the treatment of SIMD. This study systematically investigates the critical pathological role of myocardial microcirculatory injury in SIMD, and proposes a novel strategy to alleviate SIMD symptoms by modulating myocardial microcirculation.
PerspectivesCOMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: This study enhances our understanding of the pathological mechanisms of SIMD and unveils a novel role of c-FLIP in protecting myocardial microcirculation, particularly through the regulation of mitochondrial autophagy via FUNDC1. It provides clinicians with a new theoretical basis to consider the protection and regulation of myocardial microcirculation in the diagnosis and treatment of SIMD.
COMPETENCY IN PATIENT CARE AND PROCEDURAL SKILLS: This study suggests that by modulating the c-FLIP/FUNDC1 signaling pathway, clinicians may gain new therapeutic approaches, particularly early intervention strategies to alleviate myocardial microcirculatory damage and improve clinical prognosis in SIMD. Clinicians need to master the assessment of myocardial microcirculatory injury and apply appropriate treatment strategies.
COMPETENCY IN PRACTICE-BASED LEARNING: Future studies will further deepen our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms of c-FLIP and explore how modulation of this pathway can improve myocardial microcirculation in SIMD patients. This process will promote joint learning and progress among clinicians and researchers.
TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK 1: The findings of this study regarding the regulation of mitochondrial autophagy and myocardial microcirculation through the c-FLIP/FUNDC1 signaling pathway provide a new translational outlook for the treatment of SIMD. c-FLIP can regulate mitochondrial autophagic activity at different stages, thereby maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and reducing myocardial microcirculatory injury. These findings provide a theoretical foundation for the development of specific drugs targeting c-FLIP and its signaling pathway, with significant clinical application potential.
TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK 2: Future research should design well-structured clinical trials to validate the clinical efficacy of c-FLIP regulation of mitochondrial autophagy activity. Additionally, clinical trials conducted through multidisciplinary collaborations can accelerate the application of new therapies.
