## Abstract

## Introduction
Lupus nephritis (LN), a severe complication of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), is characterized by persistent immune dysregulation and glomerular injury predominantly driven by macrophage infiltration and type I interferon (IFN-I) signaling. Crosstalk between injured podocytes and infiltrating macrophages is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to LN progression; however, the molecular mediators and their corresponding receptors remain poorly defined. This study investigates whether ANGPTL3 upregulation in podocytes activates macrophages through MSR1 and drives interferon-biased inflammation in LN.

## Methods
We employed secretome-based protein-protein interaction screening to identify ANGPTL3 receptors. The ANGPTL3-MSR1 interaction was supported by co-immunoprecipitation. In vitro, bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were stimulated with ANGPTL3-conditioned media, and gene expression was analyzed by RNA sequencing. MSR1 was silenced using siRNA. In vivo, a pristane-induced lupus mouse model was used to assess glomerular ANGPTL3/MSR1 expression, renal function, and macrophage infiltration. Clinical relevance was assessed using Nephroseq datasets and human LN kidney specimens.

## Results
ANGPTL3 was identified as an MSR1-interacting ligand through a cell-based membrane protein screening assay and further supported by co-immunoprecipitation. In vitro, ANGPTL3-conditioned media induced an interferon-dominant transcriptional response in BMDMs, including upregulation of Tnip3 and Isg20. These effects were attenuated upon MSR1 silencing, confirming receptor-dependent activation. Notably, ANGPTL3 had minimal effect on M1/M2 polarization markers, indicating selective regulation of interferon signaling. Analysis of Nephroseq datasets and LN biopsies demonstrated elevated ANGPTL3 and MSR1 expression, which correlated with histopathological severity. Single-cell RNA-seq confirmed MSR1 enrichment in glomerular macrophages. In vivo, pristane-induced lupus mice exhibited increased glomerular ANGPTL3, Synaptopodin loss, proteinuria, and colocalization of F4/80 and MSR1, supporting a pathogenic podocyte-macrophage axis.

## Conclusion
This study identifies the ANGPTL3-MSR1 axis as a critical pathway linking podocyte injury to macrophage-driven inflammation in LN, highlighting its therapeutic potential. Targeting this axis may disrupt maladaptive immune crosstalk, offering a novel strategy for LN management.

## Introduction
Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic and systemic autoimmune disease characterized by diverse clinical manifestations and multiple autoantibodies production. Lupus nephritis (LN), a leading cause of morbidity in SLE, represents one of its most severe complications [ ]. Despite advancements in immunosuppressive therapies, a substantial proportion of patients still progress to kidney failure, underscoring the critical unmet need for pathway-specific therapeutics. A key pathological hallmark of LN is immune complex deposition and inflammatory cell infiltration, with macrophages emerging as central drivers of renal injury [ – ]. Activated macrophages propagate glomerular damage via pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, oxidative stress, and dysregulated activation of pathways such as type I interferon (IFN-1) signaling, a defining feature of SLE [ , ]. Notably, IFN-1-inducible genes (e.g., ISG20, TNIP3) are unregulated in LN and correlate with disease severity [ ]. Podocyte injury, a cardinal feature of LN, plays a crucial role in glomerular dysfunction and proteinuria. Recently, the crosstalk between podocytes and immune cells, especially macrophages, has emerged as a critical driver of renal damage in LN. Both immune-mediated podocyte activation and their active participation in the renal immune microenvironment fuel LN progression, underscoring the significance of podocyte-immune cell crosstalk in disease pathogenesis [ , ]. Nonetheless, the mechanisms underlying macrophage activation-driven podocyte injury and glomerular dysfunction remain poorly understood, hampering the development of targeted therapies.
Angiopoietin-like protein 3 (ANGPTL3), a secreted glycoprotein, is increasingly recognized for its dual roles in lipid metabolism and tissue injury [ ]. Beyond its canonical function in regulating triglycerides via lipoprotein lipase inhibition, ANGPTL3 directly exacerbates podocyte injury by binding integrin αvβ3, leading to cytoskeletal destabilization and proteinuria [ , ]. Recent studies by our group revealed that ANGPTL3 deletion attenuates diabetic nephropathy by modulating macrophage polarization, suggesting a paracrine crosstalk between podocytes and macrophages [ , ]. In this study, we first report the significantly elevated ANGPTL3 expression in kidney tissues of LN patients, providing novel insights into its potential role in LN pathogenesis. However, the contribution of ANGPTL3 to macrophage-driven inflammation within LN, along with the mediating receptors, remains poorly understood.
Macrophage scavenger receptor 1 (MSR1), a myeloid cell surface receptor with pleiotropic functions in lipid uptake, pathogen recognition, and inflammatory signaling [ – ], has been linked to renal injury exacerbation in hyperlipidemic models through enhanced lipid accumulation and oxidative stress [ ]. However, its role in autoimmune-driven LN, particularly in the context of macrophage-podocyte crosstalk, remains unexplored. Intriguingly, MSR1 is enriched in glomerular macrophages of LN patients. Furthermore, building on the central role of renal macrophages in LN, we prioritized MSR1 for validation as a candidate receptor of ANGPTL3 and subsequently demonstrate a previously unrecognized mechanism by which MSR1 may transduce podocyte-derived pro-inflammatory signals to amplify renal inflammation in LN. Hence, in the present study, we investigated the effect of podocyte-secreted ANGPTL3 on macrophages via MSR1 and potential mechanism of IFN-1-driven renal injury in LN.

## Methods

## Cell Isolation and Cell Culture
Mouse bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were generated as previously described. Briefly, cells were isolated from bone marrow of C57BL/6J mice and were then cultured in the completed DMEM medium supplemented with 20% L929 conditioned medium. After 7 days of differentiation, cells were harvested and stained with F4/80 and CD11b antibodies for flow cytometry analysis. Successful differentiation was confirmed when over 95% of cells were double-positive for F4/80 and CD11b. The immortalized human podocyte cell line (a gift from Dr. Moin Saleem, University of Bristol, Southmead Hospital, Bristol, UK) were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 μg/mL penicillin/streptomycin, and 1× Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium (Gibco, USA) at 33°C under 5% CO2. HEK293E and HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM medium with 10% FBS.
To establish an in vitro model of LN-induced podocytes injury, podocytes were cultured for 12 h under standard conditions, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then exposed to experimental media supplemented with 2% FBS and 8% human serum sourced from either patient with LN (LN serum) or healthy individuals (normal control serum) for 48 h. Subsequently, all the cells were harvested for further analysis.

## Mice
Female BALB/c mice were obtained from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. and housed in a specific pathogen-free facility with food and water provided ad libitum. To induce a LN model, 8-week-old female mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of 0.5 mL pristane (2,6,10,14-tetramethylpentadecane; Sigma-Aldrich), whereas control mice received an equal volume of PBS and were then monitored for 7 months. Urine samples were collected every four weeks after the intervention, and kidneys were harvested at the experimental endpoint for analysis. Urinary albumin and creatinine concentrations were measured using an ELISA kit for mouse albumin (Chondrex, Inc.) and a creatinine assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute), respectively. All animal procedures followed ARRIVE 2.0 [ ].

## Sample Processing
Mice were euthanized at week 28th by CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation and kidneys harvested. Kidneys were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned for periodic acid-Schiff staining to assess renal/glomerular injury. Frozen sections were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS and incubated with primary antibodies (anti-ANGPTL3, anti-Synaptopodin, anti-CD68, anti-F4/80, anti-MSR1), followed by Alexa Fluor 488- or Cy3-conjugated secondary antibodies to evaluate the expression of these proteins in the kidney (glomerular ROIs). For histology and immunofluorescence, ∼20 glomeruli per mouse were quantified within predefined ROIs and averaged to yield one value per mouse; urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio was calculated from paired measurements.

## Plasmids
The Plasmid pCDNA3.1-hANGPTL3-3XFlag, pCDNA6.2-EmGFP-hMSR1, and pCDH-CMV-hANGPTL3 were constructed for this study. Plasmid extraction kits were purchased from OMEGA Bio-Tek (Norcross, GA, USA).

## Identification of ANGPTL3-Binding Membrane Proteins
To generate ANGPTL3-containing conditioned medium, HEK293E cells were transfected with pCDNA3.1-hANGPTL3-3XFlag using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Four days post-transfection, supernatants were collected and filtered through a 0.45 µm syringe filter. For membrane protein screening, HEK293E cells in 96-well plates were co-transfected with plasmids encoding 5,054 human membrane proteins and an RFP reporter vector (5:1 ratio). After 48 h, cells were incubated with 1 mL ANGPTL3-Flag or control conditioned medium at 4°C for 1 h, washed with PBS containing 2% FBS, and labeled with APC-conjugated anti-Flag antibody (BioLegend, USA) for 30 min. Binding was quantified using high-throughput flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, USA), and data were analyzed with FlowJo software. Candidate hits were ranked according to the percentage of APC-positive (anti-Flag) cells within the RFP-positive population relative to background signals obtained with control conditioned medium, and the top-ranking receptors were retained as candidate ANGPTL3-binding membrane proteins for subsequent prioritization. This screening assay evaluates ANGPTL3 capture on the cell surface in a cellular context.

## Bioinformatic Analysis of Clinical Data
The Nephroseq database (https://www.nephroseq.org/resource/main.html) was applied to analyze the expression of MSR1 and ANGPTL3 in human LN samples. The public scRNA-Seq dataset was obtained from kidney specimens from 24 LN patients [ ].

## Quantitative Real-Time PCR
Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol™ (Invitrogen, USA). cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg RNA using a PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan). Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA) on a real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, USA) with a two-step amplification protocol.

## SiRNA Mediated Knockdown
siRNAs targeting mouse Msr1 (GenePharma, China) were transfected into BMDMs via electroporation (Lonza Nucleofector, Switzerland). After 4 h of co-culture with podocyte supernatant, BMDMs were harvested, and Msr1 knockdown efficiency was assessed by qRT-PCR.

## RNA-Sequencing and Pathway Enrichment Analysis
Total RNA from BMDMs was isolated using TRIzol™ (Invitrogen, USA). RNA sequencing libraries were prepared by Novogene Co., Ltd. (China). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2, with significance thresholds set at |log2FC| >0.5 and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p value (padj) <0.05. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the clusterProfiler package in R to identify differentially enriched pathways between groups. GSEA results were reported as normalized enrichment score, nominal p value, and FDR q value, and pathways with FDR q < 0.05 were considered significant. The RNA sequencing data generated in this study have been deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE302297).

## Statistical Analysis
All analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism 9 and R 4.2.2. Comparisons between multiple groups used one-way ANOVA with the Student-Newman-Keuls post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. Two-group comparisons used Student’s t test for normally distributed data. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test; data that failed normality were analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U test. Colocalization of immunofluorescence signals was performed in Fiji (ImageJ, NIH) using the Coloc 2 plugin, and Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) quantified spatial overlap within predefined glomerular ROIs. Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) unless otherwise specified; two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Identification of MSR1 as a Novel Receptor for ANGPTL3
Given our prior observation that ANGPTL3 binds integrin ɑvβ3 to maintain podocyte function, we employed secretome-based protein-protein interaction screening to identify receptors mediating ANGPTL3’s effects. Using flow cytometry, we screened a human membrane protein library (5,054 genes) and identified 12 candidates, including the previously reported integrin ɑvβ3, which served as an internal positive reference supporting the validity of the screening platform. Applying pre-specified triage criteria – cell-surface localization, enrichment on renal macrophages in LN, and mechanistic plausibility – we prioritized macrophage-enriched scavenger receptor 1α (MSR1α) for early validation. MSR1α emerged as a previously unreported binding partner of ANGPTL3 ( a, b). Co-immunoprecipitation further supported the ANGPTL3-MSR1 association ( c, online suppl. Fig. 2; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000550803).
 

## The ANGPTL3-MSR1 Axis Drives Macrophage Activation via Interferon Signaling
RNA-seq of BMDMs stimulated with ANGPTL3 revealed 5,266 DEGs (padj < 0.05, |log2FC| > 0.5) ( a, b, online suppl. data). Pathway enrichment analysis highlighted robust activation of interferon signaling (e.g., Tnip3, Isg20), NF-kB, and TNF pathways ( c). GSEA confirmed enrichment of interferon-alpha response (normalized enrichment score = 2.21, FDR q < 0.05; d).
 To validate the role of MSR1, we treated Msr1-silenced BMDMs with ANGPTL3-enriched supernatants ( a, Supplementary Fig. 1). RNA-seq of these cells identified 262 DEGs, with interferon pathways remaining the top enriched ( b, Supplementary Data). Cross-analysis of DEGs (5,266 in ANGPTL3-treated vs. control BMDMs; 262 in Msr1-silenced vs. control) revealed 37 overlapping genes reinforcing MSR1’s critical role in mediating ANGPTL3-induced interferon signaling ( c, Supplementary Data), Notably, Tnip3 and Isg20 expression increased by 3.5 and 2.8-fold, respectively, in ANGPTL3-treated BMDMs (p < 0.05) but were suppressed by 65% and 58% upon Msr1 knockdown ( d). Msr1 knockdown efficiency was independently validated by qRT-PCR, demonstrating an approximately 70-75% reduction in mRNA levels relative to control siRNA (n = 6 biological replicates, online suppl. Fig. 1). M1/M2 polarization markers (Il1β, Tnfα, iNos) showed no significant changes (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating MSR1 specifically regulates interferon signaling.
 

## Clinical Correlation of ANGPTL3 and MSR1 with LN Severity
Podocyte ANGPTL3 expression was upregulated upon stimulation with serum from patients with lupus ( a). Analysis of Nephroseq data showed a trend toward increased ANGPTL3 expression in LN kidneys compared to normal controls (p = 0.0859; b), which may be consistent with cohort heterogeneity in LN. MSR1 expression was 2.3-fold higher in LN glomeruli compared to controls (p < 0.001; c), 1.5-fold higher in WHO Class III versus Class II (p = 0.03; d). Single-cell RNA-seq of LN kidney specimens from a public dataset confirmed predominant MSR1 expression in macrophages ( e), aligning with its proposed role in immune-mediated renal injury.
 

## Validation of ANGPTL3-MSR1 Interaction in a Lupus-Like Nephritis Mouse Model
An inducible lupus-like mouse model was established through intraperitoneal injection of pristane (pristane-induced lupus [PIL]) ( a). Pathological examination by periodic acid-Schiff staining revealed severe glomerulonephritis in pristane-treated mice, characterized by mesangial matrix expansion, glomerular atrophy, and thickened capillary walls/basement membranes, whereas controls showed normal glomerular structure ( b). Renal function assessed by urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio, deteriorated progressively in pristane-treated mice, peaking at 28 weeks (70.3 ± 17.5 mg/g vs. 16.3 ± 3.4 mg/g in the controls, p < 0.01; c). Immunofluorescence staining revealed minimal ANGPTL3 expression and preserved Synaptopodin signal in glomeruli of control mice. In contrast, pristane-treated model mice exhibited markedly increased ANGPTL3 expression and decreased Synaptopodin intensity, with spatial overlap between the two signals ( d). Furthermore, elevated expression of ANGPTL3, F4/80, and CD68 was observed in the glomeruli of PIL mice and LN patient kidney tissues compared to controls (p < 0.01; e–g), indicating macrophage infiltration and ANGPTL3 upregulation in injured glomeruli. Colocalization analysis demonstrated moderate spatial colocalization between MSR1 and F4/80 signals (Pearson’s r = 0.37; f), supporting the accumulation of MSR1-positive macrophages within the inflamed glomerular microenvironment.
 

## Discussion
LN, a severe manifestation of SLE, remains a cause of kidney failure due to persistent immune dysregulation and inflammation. Although current immunosuppressive regimens effectively attenuate acute inflammatory episodes in SLE, their inability to suppress chronic immune dysregulation and pathological tissue remodeling underscores a critical unmet need in therapeutic strategies. Our study elucidates a novel ANGPTL3-MSR1 signaling axis that bridges podocyte injury to macrophage-driven interferon signaling, offering mechanistic insights into LN pathogenesis and identifying a potential therapeutic target.
ANGPTL3 has traditionally been characterized for its role in lipid metabolism, where it inhibits lipoprotein lipase and endothelial lipase to regulate triglyceride-rich lipoprotein catabolism [ , ]. However, our findings redefine its biological significance by demonstrating its dual role in podocyte-macrophage communication. While ANGPTL3 binding to integrin αvβ3 disrupts podocyte architecture, its interaction with MSR1 on macrophages activates interferon signaling, amplifying renal inflammation. This duality mirrors ANGPTL3's emerging roles in other pathologies, such as tumor-associated fibroblast activation, underscoring its pleiotropic capacity to drive tissue-specific immune responses [ ].
Podocyte-associated ANGPTL3 may serves as a damage-associated molecular pattern, sensing injury and recruiting macrophages via MSR1. This notion is supported by the upregulation of ANGPTL3 in cultured podocytes upon stimulation with lupus patient serum ( a) and by in situ immunofluorescence showing spatial overlap between ANGPTL3 and the podocyte marker Synaptopodin in glomeruli in the LN-like setting ( d). This mechanism aligns with recent studies highlighting podocyte-macrophage crosstalk in diabetic nephropathy [ , ], where ANGPTL3 has been implicated in modulating macrophage phenotypes and inflammatory programs. However, our data reveal a paradoxical pro-inflammatory role for ANGPTL3 in LN, suggesting context-dependent regulation by disease-specific microenvironments.
MSR1 has been implicated in lipid uptake and pathogen recognition. Our discovery of MSR1 as an ANGPTL3 receptor introduces a paradigm shift in understanding its role in autoimmune diseases. Unlike its canonical ligands (e.g., acetylated LDL), ANGPTL3 binding induces a distinct signaling cascade that activates interferon-stimulated genes such as Tnip3 and Isg20. This aligns with recent evidence of MSR1-mediated inflammatory signaling in atherosclerosis [ ], where MSR1 deficiency attenuates macrophage activation. The specificity of MSR1 in mediating interferon responses, despite overlapping expression of other scavenger receptors like CD68, suggests a tailored mechanism for immune regulation. Crosstalk between MSR1 and interferon pathways may explain why targeted MSR1 inhibition suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines without affecting macrophage polarization. This functional specificity established MSR1 as one of the regulators of interferon-driven pathology in LN. Prior studies show that MSR1 can cooperate with TLR4 to activate IRF3, induce IFN-β, and signal through STAT1, providing a mechanistic route by which MSR1-TLR crosstalk biases macrophages toward an IFN-dominant transcriptional program [ , ]. Consistent with this mechanism, our RNA-seq demonstrated increased STAT1, aligning with the IFN-dominant, MSR1-dependent transcriptomic fingerprint we observe.
Our clinical correlations reveal elevated ANGPTL3 and MSR1 expression in LN glomeruli, with MSR1-positive macrophages infiltrating damaged regions. This mirrors findings in PIL models, where macrophage-driven interferon signaling correlates with renal injury severity. The moderate colocalization between MSR1 and F4/80 (a macrophage marker) implies a subset of macrophages specialized in ANGPTL3 sensing, potentially representing a therapeutic window for precision targeting. The translational potential of this axis is further supported by preclinical success of ANGPTL3 inhibitors (e.g., evinacumab) in dyslipidemia. However, our study highlights a critical caveat: systemic ANGPTL3 inhibition may inadvertently exacerbate macrophage activation in non-renal tissues, as seen in atherosclerosis models. Thus, localized delivery strategies (e.g., glomerulus-targeted nanoparticles) or dual-targeting approaches (e.g., MSR1/ANGPTL3 co-inhibition) may mitigate off-target effects.
While our study advances the understanding of LN pathogenesis, several limitations warrant consideration. First, our macrophage stimulation experiments primarily relied on conditioned media from ANGPTL3-overexpressing podocytes. Although this design models a paracrine milieu, it cannot fully exclude potential contributions from other co-secreted factors or overexpression-associated changes. Notably, ANGPTL3 levels in the conditioned media were quantified (online supplementary Fig. 1), supporting that macrophages were exposed to an ANGPTL3-enriched stimulus. Importantly, the receptor dependency is supported by the marked attenuation of interferon-stimulated gene induction upon MSR1 silencing; however, we did not perform ANGPTL3-specific perturbation experiments in the current study. Future work incorporating these orthogonal approaches will be important to further strengthen ligand-specific causality. Second, critical mechanistic gaps persist in understanding the structural determinants of the ANGPTL3-MSR1 interaction. High-resolution techniques (e.g., cryo-EM) are needed to elucidate how ANGPTL3 binding induces conformational changes in MSR’s collagen-like domain. Third, RNA-seq data revealed only 37 overlapping DEGs between ANGPTL3-treated and MSR1-silenced BMDMs, suggesting additional receptors (e.g., TLR4) or co-receptors (e.g., CD36) may modulate this axis. We did not systematically validate all screened candidates in parallel; therefore, potential cooperative roles warrant further investigation. Finally, acute vs. chronic ANGPTL3 exposure may elicit divergent macrophage responses. Single-cell trajectory analysis could map phenotypic transitions in LN progression.
In conclusion, this study unveils a paradigm-shifting ANGPTL3-MSR1 axis that drives interferon-mediated renal injury in LN. By elucidating how podocyte ANGPTL3 hijacks macrophage biology, we bridge a critical gap between immune dysregulation and end-organ damage. Targeting this axis offers a dual advantage: mitigating macrophage hyperactivity while preserving ANGPTL3’s lipid-regulatory functions, a balance essential for treating complex autoimmune diseases. Future therapeutic strategies leveraging this pathway could redefine care paradigms for LN, particularly in resistant or relapsing cases.
