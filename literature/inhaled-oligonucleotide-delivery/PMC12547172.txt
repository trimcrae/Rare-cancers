## Abstract
Nanocarriers have been regarded as the most potential therapeutic weapons to combat tumors and genetic diseases using protein drugs. However, nanocarriers often face many challenges during the delivery process. Due to the existence of cell membrane barriers, nanocarriers may be restricted extracellularly or degraded in lysosomes, thus unable to effectively deliver drugs to the target location. The biomimetic nanocarrier delivery systems have demonstrated unique advantages in the field of protein delivery. This review focuses on cutting-edge technologies such as biomimetic mineralized nanoparticles, Virus-like particles, and bacterium-like nanoparticles. These biomimetic nanocarriers exhibit great potential to bypass endocytosis and lysosomal restrictions due to their unique biomimetic properties. Additionally, this review reviews the latest progress of cytosolic protein drug delivery systems based on biomimetic systems in various medical research and clinical applications, including clinical targeted delivery, tumor therapy, and gene editing. It also compares the capabilities and limitations of these biomimetic cytosolic protein delivery systems with traditional nanocarriers, including factors that affect protein delivery efficiency, and emphasizes their potential to adopt targeted, sustainable, and minimally invasive treatment methods. Finally, this review proposes the current limitations and future research directions of protein delivery systems, expecting to provide more efficient solutions for disease treatment.
Keywords: Virus-like particles, Bacterial system, Mineralized nanoparticles, Exosomes, Cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers

## Introduction
Intracellular proteins are crucial for maintaining cellular homeostasis and mediating numerous biological functions [ ]. Their precise delivery can lead to the restoration of lost functions in diseased cells, the regulation of cellular responses, and the elucidation of complex signaling pathways [ , ]. Despite their great potential, due to the natural defense of cells, such as the cell membrane and endosome-lysosome system, delivered proteins may be degraded or inactivated [ , ]. Therefore, delivering proteins into the intracellular environment remains a great challenge. Traditional protein delivery methods, such as chemical permeation and electroporation, often face problems such as poor biocompatibility, lack of specificity, and inability to escape the endosomal compartment after internalization [ , ]. These limitations have prompted people to search for alternative strategies that can overcome these obstacles and promote efficient and targeted intracellular protein delivery.
To address these challenges, the research in this field has turned to biomimetic intracellular protein delivery carriers, utilizing natural principles to design novel delivery systems [ , ]. The design of biomimetic intracellular protein delivery carriers involves replicating the structures and functions of biological systems to create materials and devices with enhanced performance characteristics. The principles of biomimetic intracellular protein delivery are based on understanding and mimicking the mechanisms that nature uses to achieve specific functions. In the context of protein delivery, this might involve mimicking natural pathways for cellular uptake, such as receptor-mediated endocytosis, or designing materials similar to the extracellular matrix to promote cell adhesion and endocytosis [ , , ]. Biomimetic carriers offer unique advantages in the field of protein delivery. They can be designed to have high biocompatibility, reduced immunogenicity, and enhanced efficiency of cellular uptake [ ]. Additionally, by mimicking natural processes, these carriers can evade cellular defense mechanisms, thereby improving intracellular trafficking and reducing the degradation of delivered proteins.
The development of biomimetic intracellular protein-delivery systems has introduced a new strategy for overcoming the long-standing bottlenecks of conventional approaches and represents a major advance in the field. This review summarizes recent progress in the leading biomimetic platforms, including virus-like particles, bacterial systems (Bacterial vesicles and bacterial carriers), mineralized nanoparticles, exosomes (Derived from animals or plants), and cell membrane camouflaged nanocarriers (Erythrocyte, leukocyte, platelet and tumor cell membranes) ( ). The “cytosolic delivery of protein drugs” addressed here denotes the use of the protein itself as the active pharmaceutical agent; whether it is encapsulated as the core payload or conjugated onto the surface of a nanocarrier, the ultimate goal is to introduce the functional protein into the cytosol so that it can exert its therapeutic effect. Conversely, if a protein (e.g., an antibody) is merely appended to the nanocarrier surface to confer targeting capability, it serves only as a localization ligand and not as the therapeutic entity, and thus falls outside the scope of this review.
 Despite the considerable potential of protein-based therapeutics, their clinical translation is hampered by several inherent challenges. (1)Cellular membrane barriers: The phospholipid bilayer poses a significant physical and electrostatic barrier to macromolecular entry; (2)Endosomal/lysosomal degradation: Internalized proteins are often trapped in endosomes and degraded by lysosomal enzymes, preventing cytosolic delivery; (3)Low biocompatibility and immunogenicity: Many synthetic carriers trigger immune responses or exhibit cytotoxicity; (4)Lack of targeting specificity: Non-specific distribution leads to off-target effects and reduced therapeutic efficacy; (5)Inefficient endosomal escape: Most carriers lack mechanisms to facilitate escape from endosomal compartments; (6)Stability issues: Proteins are prone to denaturation during encapsulation, storage, and delivery. To overcome these obstacles, biomimetic nanocarriers have been engineered to mimic natural biological processes, thereby enhancing cellular uptake, improving endosomal escape, reducing immunogenicity, and enabling targeted delivery. The following sections will detail how each biomimetic system addresses these challenges through structural and functional innovations.
The illustration systematically elucidates the multi-level structural design of biomimetic nanocarriers and their delivery mechanisms tailored to different pathological microenvironments. It covers surface functionalization strategies based on targeting molecules or cell-penetrating peptides, intracellular targeting pathways with spatiotemporal specificity, customized loading modes of engineered protein drugs, and stimulus-responsive intelligent controlled-release systems integrated with Light- or glutathione (GSH)-sensitive materials.

## Virus-like particles (VLPs) as protein delivery vectors
2.1.

## Structure and function of VLPs
VLPs are nanoparticles self-assembled from one or more structural proteins of a virus. They mimic the natural structure of the virus, thus exhibiting excellent biocompatibility and efficient intracellular uptake [ , ]. VLPs do not contain the viral genetic genome, and therefore lack the ability to replicate or cause infection [ ]. Researchers can observe and evaluate the three-dimensional structure, size, and stability (e.g., salt tolerance) of VLPs by simulating the in vivo environment or using imaging techniques such as transmission electron microscopy (TEM), atomic force microscopy (AFM), and cryo-electron microscopy (Cryo-EM) [ ]. The stability of VLPs is governed primarily by the structural integrity of their protein shell and the strength of inter-subunit interactions. In general, VLPs withstand a variety of physicochemical insults, including UV irradiation, whereas their susceptibility to proteases varies with the precise capsid architecture. Because they lack DNA, VLPs are inherently resistant to DNases; however, they may be sensitive to RNases if they encapsidate RNA or possess RNA-binding properties [ , ].

2.2.

## Application of virus particles in protein delivery
VLPs are composed of the main structural proteins required for the assembly of viral capsids, but do not contain viral genome components. This makes them a safe and effective delivery vector [ , ]. VLPs have shown great potential in drug delivery and have become promising carrier drugs for transporting chemical drugs, genetic drugs, and peptides/proteins. They have virus-like permeability and strong retention, and VLPs interact with receptors on target cells through their specific surface proteins, allowing them to effectively target specific organs, tissues, or cells. They are able to encapsulate and deliver specific mRNA or proteins into cells and release these biomolecules. Further modifications enable VLPs to cross various physiological barriers, thus achieving efficient drug delivery and precise treatment. VLPs achieve cytosolic delivery primarily through two key mechanisms: (1) Receptor-mediated endocytosis: Specific proteins on the VLP surface (e.g., capsid proteins) recognize and bind to receptors on the host-cell membrane, efficiently triggering clathrin- or caveolin-dependent endocytic pathways that internalize the VLPs [ , ]. (2) Endosomal escape: Once inside the cell, certain VLPs mimic the behavior of their parent viruses. Structural elements such as fusion peptides undergo conformational changes in the acidic endosomal environment, enabling the VLPs to fuse with or disrupt the endosomal membrane [ ]. This releases the cargo proteins directly into the cytosol and effectively prevents their trafficking to lysosomes for degradation.
Therapeutic proteins delivered through VLPs are expected to play a significant role in the treatment of various diseases. For instance, they can be used in cancer therapy, genetic editing for inherited diseases. Previous research methods included using viral vectors such as AAV to deliver gene-edited DNA or mRNA, but these methods had some problems, such as the risk of DNA integration and off-target effects caused by long-term expression. In order to develop an efficient and safe gene editing method, Liu et al. developed the fourth generation eVLPs by optimizing the protein composition and structure of VLPs, and can efficiently package and deliver cytidine base editors or Cas9 RNPs [ ]. eVLPs can overcome the three major bottlenecks of protein delivery, namely effective packaging, release, and targeting, to efficiently deliver gene-editing proteins with minimal off-target effects. Furthermore, eVLPs can be utilized for in vivo delivery of base editors to enhance visual function in genetic blindness models in mice. The study also found that eVLPs showed low levels of off-target editing or DNA integration in vitro and in vivo, thereby improving the safety of treatment( ). These results suggest that eVLPs are a promising therapeutic macromolecular delivery carrier, combining the key advantages of viral and non-viral delivery strategies.
 The single-base editing systems developed to date mainly include cytosine base editor (CBE), adenine base editor (ABE), guanine base editor (GBE), and prime editor (PE). As key derivatives of the CRISPR–Cas9 system, these base editors enable precise single-nucleotide substitutions without introducing DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), offering a novel therapeutic strategy for genetic diseases caused by point mutations. Nevertheless, efficient delivery remains the critical bottleneck limiting their clinical translation. Taking PE as an example, the newest breakthrough in CRISPR technology, can catalyze all twelve possible base transitions and transversions, as well as small insertions and deletions, without relying on DSBs or exogenous donor DNA templates. However, their large molecular weight and structural complexity pose formidable delivery challenges, prompting researchers to develop advanced biomimetic nanocarriers and innovative delivery strategies [ ]. Chen et al. developed a versatile platform based on outer-membrane vesicles (OMVs) by fusing the CRISPR–dCas9 machinery to the outer-membrane protein ClyA. These engineered OMVs self-load and deliver diverse genetic cargoes while markedly enhancing target enrichment [ ]. In parallel, Cheng et al. introduced a platform (Peptide-Ionizable-Lipid Organ Targeting, PILOT) that coupled peptides with ionizable lipids to generate a diverse library of peptide-ionizable-lipid materials. Leveraging PILOT, researchers produced liver- and lung-homing LNPs that achieved 13.1 % and 7.4 % prime-editing efficiencies in vivo, respectively, providing powerful tools for organ-specific delivery of prime editors. Nevertheless, although the above biomimetic carriers are less immunogenic than viral vectors, certain lipid and peptide components can still elicit immune responses and require further optimization.
As an outstanding representative of non-viral delivery systems, VLPs currently dominate the clinical application of gene editing. Recently, David Liu's team further developed a gene editing system based on VLPs for the delivery of PE ribonucleoprotein complexes in vivo. This system optimizes the structure of PE and VLP through systems engineering, achieving higher editing efficiency than previously reported eVLP systems based on BE. In human cells, v3 and v3b PE-eVLPs achieved up to 170-fold improvement in editing efficiency. In a mouse model, a single injection of v3 PE-eVLPs can achieve a therapeutic level of PE editing in the retina and partially restore visual function. In addition, the transient delivery properties of PE-eVLPs can reduce the possibility of off-target editing and integration of oncogenes, thereby improving the safety of PE [ ]. The targeted delivery characteristics of VLPs enable them to achieve precise treatment of specific cell types, thus facilitating personalized medicine. Since VLPs do not contain viral genome components, they have a higher level of safety, reducing immunogenicity and infection risks. These milestones underscore the expanding utility of non-viral vectors in genetic engineering.

2.3.

## Strategies for enhanced targeting and controlled release of VLPs
Achieving precise targeting and controlled release of VLPs is crucial for improving their delivery efficiency. However, the current targeting and controlled release capabilities of VLPs still need to be enhanced. By modifying VLPs' surfaces with specific ligands or antibodies, precise control over their targeting can be achieved. These ligands or antibodies can specifically bind to receptors on target cells, thus enhancing the targeting ability of VLPs [ ]. Developing novel controlled release technologies, such as utilizing pH-sensitive materials, enzyme-sensitive materials, or photosensitive materials, can enable precise control over the release of VLPs. These technologies can trigger the release of VLPs based on specific conditions (e.g., intracellular pH levels, enzyme concentrations, or light intensity). By deeply integrating nanotechnology with gene-editing tools, a new generation of VLP delivery platforms can be created that enable precise, on-demand programming of particle size, surface ligands, and stimulus-responsive elements, concurrently boosting targeting accuracy and controlled-release performance [ , ].

## Bacterial systems as protein delivery vectors
In the field of biotechnology, bacterial systems have garnered significant attention due to their naturally occurring intracellular delivery capabilities [ , , ]. Through genetic engineering, bacterial systems can be modified to carry and deliver specific proteins into host cells. The utilization of bacteria for cytosolic protein delivery not only provides a novel biological tool, but also exhibits tremendous potential in drug delivery, gene therapy, and vaccine development [ , ]. The following will explore the principles and strategies, application examples, as well as limitations and directions for improvement of using bacterial systems for cytosolic protein delivery.
3.1.

## Principles and strategies for delivery by bacterial systems
Bacteria, as naturally occurring intracellular delivery systems, possess formidable survival capabilities and the ability to penetrate cell walls. They can transmit their genetic material or proteins through natural pathways such as infecting host cells. To achieve intracellular delivery of specific proteins, scientists have modified bacteria. This modification is primarily achieved through genetic engineering techniques, including: (1) Genetic recombination: Integrating the coding genes of the target protein into the bacterial genome enables bacteria to express and secrete bacterial vesicles carrying the target protein during growth. This method allows precise control over the expression level and timing of the gene, achieving efficient delivery of the target protein through bacterial vesicles [ , ]; (2) Construction of artificial vectors: Utilizing synthetic biology or chemical modification methods, artificial vectors are constructed to encapsulate the target protein within or on the surface of bacteria. These vectors can possess specific targeting and penetration properties, enabling accurate delivery of the target protein to specific locations within host cells [ , ]. Engineered bacterial systems bypass endosomal-lysosomal degradation by directly “injecting” or fusing their protein cargo into the cytosol, thereby breaking through the intracellular delivery barrier. Many bacteria, such as Salmonella and Listeria, inherently possess the ability to invade non-phagocytic cells. Through “molecular syringes” like the type III secretion system (T3SS), they directly inject effector proteins into the host cytosol [ ]. Modified bacterial systems can exploit this route to deliver therapeutic proteins [ ]. In addition, certain bacterial vectors are engineered to lyse within the phagosome after uptake, releasing their contents before the phagosome fuses with lysosomes [ ]. For example, Raman et al. reported a self-lysing intracellular Salmonella carrier that efficiently delivers the active protein CTCasp-3, markedly suppresses mouse hepatocellular carcinoma and triple-negative breast cancer, and prolongs survival [ ]. The researchers equipped the Salmonella with a PsseJ-LysE lytic circuit and constitutive GFP expression; upon invasion of cancer cells the bacteria rapidly lyse, releasing GFP directly into the cytosol. This PsseJ-LysE circuit renders the engineered Salmonella self-limiting and avirulent. An alternative strategy employs bacterial OMVs, which enter cells via membrane fusion or endocytosis. Wang et al. enriched high concentrations of the target protein within the OMVs of Escherichia coli and then exploited the natural delivery ability of OMVs to precisely transport the cargo into the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells. Across multiple cell lines, the OMV-based delivery system achieved protein transfection efficiencies of 90–97 % [ ].

3.2.

## Applications of bacterial systems in protein delivery
Bacterial systems leverage natural invasion mechanisms to bypass cellular membrane barriers, thereby enhancing cytosolic delivery. Compared to traditional protein delivery methods, bacterial delivery systems exhibit higher penetrability and targeting capabilities, better meeting the needs of biomedical treatments [ , ]. For instance, utilizing modified Escherichia coli to deliver anti-tumor drugs can directly transport the drugs into tumor cells through bacterial targeting, enhancing drug efficacy while reducing side effects. Bacteria can autonomously navigate to cancer tissues by expressing tumor-specific receptor ligands (such as EGFR antibody fragments) or responding to microenvironmental signals (such as hypoxia or low pH), thereby precisely delivering drugs to the tumor site [ , ]. Zhang et al. have developed a novel bacterial-based protein delivery system. They conducted systematic research and modification of the Photorhabdus virulence cassettes (PVC) within the extracellular contractile injection systems (eCISs) of bacterial origin. The modified PVCs are capable of efficiently and specifically delivering a variety of functional proteins (such as Cas9, base editors, and toxin proteins) into human cells and animal models( ). This achievement not only provides new insights for the development and optimization of protein delivery technologies but also offers robust technical support for applications in gene therapy, cancer treatment, and biological control [ ]. Furthermore, bacterial systems can be employed to deliver vaccine antigens, simulating natural infection processes to stimulate the immune response of the body and produce antibodies against specific pathogens [ ]. Engineered bacteria can be designed as “living drug factories” to continuously secrete therapeutic proteins (such as cytotoxins or immune modulators) locally in tumors, thereby prolonging the duration of drug action. The probiotic strain Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) has been genetically modified to serve as an anti-cancer vaccine delivery system, specifically optimized to boost biosynthesis efficiency and improve cytoplasmic release of neoepitope peptide arrays, aiming to safely stimulate targeted, potent, and sustained tumor-specific immune responses throughout the organism. [ ]. These examples not only prove the feasibility of bacterial systems in protein delivery but also provide strong support for further research and applications.
 
3.3.

## Limitations and improvement directions
3.3.1.

## Host immune response
As foreign substances, bacteria may trigger an immune response in the host, leading to inflammation or immune rejection, which may limit the application of bacterial delivery systems [ ]. The antigenic substances potentially present on the bacterial surface can further exacerbate the immune response, reducing or even invalidating the delivery effect. One strategy to circumvent the immunogenicity of live-bacterial therapy is to delete surface antigens such as lipopolysaccharide; however, this approach usually impairs the strain's ability to colonize. Therefore, selecting bacterial strains with low immunogenicity as delivery vectors can reduce the likelihood of host immune response, such as EcN and Lactic acid bacteria [ , ] Modifying the bacterial surface to reduce its antigenicity can also minimize immune reactions; for example, decorating the surface with capsular polysaccharide decreases immune recognition. Tetsuhiro et al. engineered EcN to modulate the amount of bacterial surface capsular polysaccharide (CAP) in response to the inducer isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside. Shielded by CAP, the bacteria can transiently evade immune attack, and the subsequent loss of encapsulation enables efficient in vivo clearance [ ].

3.3.2.

## Stability and safety of bacterial genomes
During genetic modification, the introduction of unstable genome elements or the occurrence of genetic mutations may affect the stability and safety of bacteria. Unexpected genetic recombination or mutations within the host cells may produce harmful effects. To address this, advanced gene editing techniques, such as the CRISPR-Cas9 system, can be employed to precisely control the genetic modification process, minimizing the introduction of unstable genome elements [ , ]. Strict safety assessments of the modified bacteria should be conducted to ensure they do not cause adverse effects on host cells or the organism. Developing new gene expression systems, such as using inducible promoters or stable expression vectors, can enhance the stability and controllability of gene expression [ , ].
In summary, bacterial systems for cytosolic protein delivery represent a biological tool with tremendous potential. The principle of using bacterial systems for intracellular protein delivery mainly relies on their natural ability to invade cells and genetic engineering techniques. By modifying bacteria to express and deliver specific proteins, precision therapy or regulation of host cells can be achieved [ ]. This strategy has broad application prospects in drug delivery, gene therapy, and vaccine development. With continuous technological advancements and optimizations, it is believed that more innovative methods will be developed in the future to better meet the needs of human disease treatment.

## Mineralized nanoparticles as carriers for protein cytosolic delivery
Leveraging their unique physicochemical properties, precise structural tunability, and excellent biocompatibility, biomineralized nanoparticles have emerged as an innovative platform in the field of protein delivery [ , ]. This section systematically elaborates on the construction strategies, functional characteristics, and biomedical applications of these nanocarriers, with a particular focus on their unique advantages in overcoming traditional delivery bottlenecks.
4.1.

## Construction strategies and property regulation of biomineralized nanoparticles
Biomineralized carriers are hierarchically structured, highly ordered composites synthesized through macromolecular templating or self-assembled organic aggregates [ ]. Proteins can precisely regulate the nucleation sites and crystallization orientation of inorganic precursors through their surface charge distribution, specific binding sites, and spatial conformation [ , ]. By optimizing key parameters such as protein concentration, pH gradient, and temperature field, precise control over the crystal structure of nanoparticles (Crystal form and crystal plane index), particle size distribution, and surface charge can be achieved, laying the foundation for customized delivery systems [ , ]. Biocompatibility and structural stability are crucial guarantees for the clinical application of these carriers. The inherent biofriendliness of proteins significantly reduces immunogenicity risks, while the physical barrier formed by the mineralization layer effectively enhances carrier stability [ ]. By introducing disulfide bond cross-linking or regulating the mineralization time (typically 2–24 h), nanoparticles can maintain their intact structure in physiological environments for over 72 h and achieve controlled dissociation in the acidic endosomal environment (pH 5.0–6.5). The morphology of mineralized nanoparticles (Typically spherical, 50–200 nm) can be precisely tuned by adjusting mineralization parameters such as protein concentration and pH [ ]. Nanoparticles with diameters in the 100–200 nm range favor the enhanced permeability and retention effect in tumors, while a uniform spherical shape promotes systemic circulation and cellular uptake compared to irregular aggregates [ , ].

4.2.

## Mechanisms and application progress of efficient protein delivery
By exploiting a pH-responsive dissolution mechanism, mineralized carriers simultaneously protect proteins from degradation and help them breach the endosomal barrier for highly efficient cytosolic delivery. The exceptional intracellular transport efficiency of these nanoparticles stems from their exquisitely engineered composition and architecture, which sensitively detect and respond to subtle pH changes within the cellular milieu. After entering cells via endocytosis, the particles become trapped in endosomes, where their mineralized domains immediately sense luminal acidification. The acidic environment then triggers disintegration of the nanoparticles themselves; for example, ZIF-8 decomposes in acid to release a surge of Zn2+ ions, provoking a sharp local increase in ionic strength that destabilizes the endosomal membrane while synchronously liberating the encapsulated proteins into the cytosol [ ].
In terms of optimizing delivery efficiency, various mineralization systems have emerged successively: (1) Carbonate/phosphate system: The amorphous calcium carbonate phosphate carrier developed by the team of Yu et al. successfully encapsulated cytochrome c and insulin-like growth factor, with a lysosomal escape efficiency 2.3 times that of traditional liposomes [ ] ( a). Amorphous mineralized matrices can significantly increase protein loading (up to 30 % w/w) while maintaining bioactivity. The mechanism lies in the lack of distinct crystal planes in the amorphous structure, which allows protein molecules to be uniformly dispersed within the mineralized matrix through hydrogen bonding networks and coordination interactions. In vitro evaluation systems have confirmed that model proteins (e.g., horseradish peroxidase) retain >85 % catalytic activity after encapsulation, with sustained-release kinetics fitting the Higuchi model; (2) Metal-organic framework (MOF) composite system: The ZIF-8 protein carrier constructed by the team of Chu et al. could be rapidly taken up by cells, with the proteins being effectively released and escaping from the endosomes and lysosomes of living cells into the cytoplasm while retaining their native activity [ ]. Ge et al.synthesized Cet@protein@ZIF-8N, which was subsequently used for the selective intracellular delivery of functional proteins to epidermal-growth-factor-receptor-overexpressed cells [ ]. Both in-cell and in vivo experiments confirmed that Cet@RNase A@ZIF-8N efficiently delivers RNase A while retaining its selective inhibitory activity. This strategy was also successfully applied to cell-type-specific gene editing by delivering a Cas9/sgRNA complex to knockdown the endogenous expression of glutathione peroxidase. Ye et al. developed a method for delivering DPSC lysate growth factors using ZIF-8 nanoparticles encapsulated with proteins [ ]. Using ZIF-8 as protein-encapsulated nanoparticles, a ZIF-8@DPSC lysate (ZIF-8@DL) with pH-responsive drug release was produced. ZIF-8@DL demonstrated superior cutaneous uptake, cutaneous penetration, and retention capacity in vivo compared to DPSC lysate. Subcutaneous microneedle injection of ZIF-8@DL into a mouse photoaging model resulted in significantly improved epidermal thickness, dermal collagen deposition, and cutaneous inflammation. (3) Albumin mineralization system: The single-molecule albumin template method established by the team of Chen et al.through histidine-Mn2+ coordination for in situ mineralization within the protein cavity, increased doxorubicin loading to 12.8 % and enhanced tumor targeting efficiency by 5.6 times [ ] ( b).
 In the field of disease treatment applications, innovative achievements in vaccine development and tumor catalytic therapy continue to break through. For example, the spike protein-manganese nanoadjuvant (MnARK) constructed by Wang et al. activated the cGAS-STING pathway, increasing neutralizing antibody titers and T-cell response intensity; The GOx-MnCaP/DOX nanoparticles constructed by the Huang et al. achieved a cascade reaction: glucose oxidation consumed and triggered H2O2 accumulation, Mn2+ catalyzed the production of ·OH, and combined with doxorubicin, the complete tumor remission rate was increased to 67 % [ ]. It is worth noting that the interfacial induction mineralization technology developed by Tan et al., by regulating the supersaturation of Ca2+ and the surface energy of the organic template, successfully prepared monodisperse nanoparticles [ , ]. In our previous studies, we utilized mineralized nanocarriers to effectively deliver protamine [ ]and porcine pancreatic elastase [ ] to tumor cells, thereby exerting an inhibitory effect on tumor growth. These works not only validate the unique advantages of biomineralization strategies in drug delivery but also provide theoretical support and technical reserves for the development of intelligent delivery systems and engineered biologics [ ]. Future research needs to further explore the molecular dynamics mechanisms of the mineralization process and develop new intelligent carrier systems with environmental responsiveness (ROS or ATP sensitivity) and active targeting mediated by integrin receptors.

4.3.

## Challenges and prospects of mineralized nanoparticles for protein delivery
While mineralized nanoparticles often exhibit good biocompatibility, specific materials or preparation conditions may trigger cytotoxicity or immune reactions [ , ]. To ensure their safety in clinical applications, comprehensive evaluations of the biological safety of mineralized nanoparticles need to be conducted through in vitro cell toxicity tests, in vivo animal experiments, and other means. When assessing the safety of nanoparticles, it is necessary to consider their physicochemical properties, compound toxicity, environmental factors, and exposure pathways. Furthermore, the intracellular degradation kinetics of mineralized nanoparticles must be tuned so that they disintegrate promptly after releasing their protein cargo, thereby preventing long-term cytotoxicity. By judiciously engineering the mineral composition (For example, converting amorphous calcium carbonate into single-crystalline calcium carbonate to enhance lattice stability) or by surface-coating with pH-sensitive polymers such as poly(ethylene glycol)-poly(aspartic acid), their dissolution rate within the acidic lysosomal environment (pH 5.0–6.5) can be precisely controlled [ , ].
Designing targeted delivery systems specific to certain cells or organelles is crucial for improving treatment efficacy [ , ]. Surface modification with hydrophilic groups, targeting molecules, and other techniques can enhance the stability and cell penetration ability of nanoparticles in body fluids. Preparing nanoparticles with suitable sizes can improve their diffusion and transport efficiency within cells. On one hand, molecules such as antibodies and peptides that specifically recognize cell surface receptors can be utilized to achieve active targeted delivery of nanoparticles. On the other hand, the EPR effect of tumor tissue can be exploited to achieve passive targeting of nanoparticles to tumor sites [ ].
In summary, mineralized nanoparticles face challenges such as degradability, biological safety, delivery efficiency, and targeting when delivering proteins into cells. However, by designing degradable materials, evaluating biological safety, optimizing delivery efficiency, and improving targeting strategies, it is hopeful to overcome these challenges and promote the application and development of mineralized nanoparticles in the field of protein delivery.

5.

## Exosomes as cytoplasmic protein delivery vectors
Since their discovery, exosomes, as crucial mediators of intercellular communication, have garnered widespread attention due to their unique biological characteristics [ , ]. These nanoscale vesicles secreted by cells carry a wealth of bioactive molecules, including proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids. Moreover, their natural cell-penetrating ability and low immunogenicity render exosomes immensely promising in the field of protein delivery [ ].
5.1.

## Structure and function of exosomes
Exosomes refer to small membrane vesicles containing nucleic acids and proteins, with a diameter typically ranging from 30 to 150 nm [ , ]. They possess a phospholipid bilayer structure, which enables them to transmit various bioactive molecules between cells [ ]. The membrane of exosomes is enriched with various proteins, such as the tetraspanin family (CD63, CD81, and CD9) and the heat shock protein family (HSP60, HSP70, HSPA5) [ ]. These proteins are not only related to the formation and release of exosomes, but also participate in the recognition and fusion processes between exosomes and target cells. Exosomes play a vital role in intercellular communication. They can carry proteins, lipids, RNA, and DNA from one cell and enter another cell through fusion or internalization, achieving intercellular information transmission [ ]. Proteins and RNA in exosomes can influence gene expression and protein synthesis in target cells, thereby regulating cellular physiological functions [ ]. Additionally, exosomes are involved in various physiological and pathological processes, such as immune regulation, tumorigenesis, and progression. For instance, during tumorigenesis and metastasis, exosomes can carry growth factors, enzymes, and other signaling molecules produced by tumor cells, promoting tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis [ ]. Simultaneously, exosomes can also influence the tumor microenvironment, promoting neovascularization and immune suppression [ , ].
The sources of exosomes mainly include animal and plant cells [ , ]. Animal cell-derived exosomes possess diverse biological activities. They are released into the extracellular matrix through fusion with the cell membrane after forming from intracellular vesicles resulting from the invagination of the cell membrane. Animal exosomes are rich in various proteins, lipids, and RNA, which play a crucial role in intercellular communication. Moreover, they possess low immunogenicity and good biocompatibility, making them potentially valuable in disease diagnosis and treatment [ ]. Compared with animal exosomes, plant exosome-like nanoparticles exhibit some similarities in structure and composition. Plant exosomes are also enriched with various bioactive molecules mentioned above [ ]. In recent years, researchers have discovered that plant exosomes exhibit significant regulatory effects in areas such as anti-tumor, immune regulation, intestinal diseases, and regenerative medicine. For example, houttuynia cordata-derived exosome-like nanoparticles significantly alleviated colitis symptoms in mice by inhibiting the NLRP3 signaling pathway and modulating the gut microbiota [ ]. Turmeric-derived nanovesicles (TNV), as a novel nanobiopharmaceutical, are used for targeted therapy of ulcerative colitis [ ]. Grapefruit-derived extracellular vesicle can inhibit the growth of human leukemia cells and bone marrow blast cells derived from leukemia patients [ ].These discoveries provide new directions for research on plant exosomes and lay a foundation for their application in the medical field.

5.2.

## Application of exosomes in protein delivery
5.2.1.

## The advantages of exosomes as natural nanocarriers
Exosomes exhibit low immunogenicity and natural targeting, addressing both safety and specificity challenges. As exosomes originate from cells themselves, they can fuse well with target cells to achieve efficient protein delivery. The key ability of exosomes to overcome biological barriers lies in their natural bio-interfacial properties. Exosomes typically enter cells via endocytosis (e.g., clathrin-mediated endocytosis or macropinocytosis) or through direct membrane fusion [ ]. Tetraspanins (such as CD9, CD63, and CD81) and integrins embedded in their membrane not only enhance targeting but also facilitate interactions and fusion with the cell membrane [ ]. Exosomes possess an intrinsic endosomal escape capacity: specific lipids within their lipid bilayer (e.g., phosphatidic acid) can destabilize membranes and promote fusion in the acidic endosomal environment [ ]. In addition, some exosome-associated proteins mediate membrane fusion, ensuring cargo release into the cytosol. For example, the cellular prion protein (PrPc) can be found in exosomes, facilitating the spread of neurodegenerative prion diseases [ ].
At the same time, specific molecules on the surface of exosomes, such as the tetraspanin proteins CD63, CD81, and CD9, can evade recognition and clearance by the immune system, thereby reducing immunogenicity [ , ]. Secondly, exosomes possess efficient protein loading and delivery capabilities. Exosomes can encapsulate proteins within themselves through fusion with intracellular vesicles, forming stable protein-exosome complexes. These complexes protect proteins from external environmental damage and release them inside target cells, achieving efficient protein delivery [ ]. Finally, exosomes demonstrate targeting and modulability. Specific molecules on the surface of exosomes can bind to receptors on the surface of target cells, enabling targeted delivery. Additionally, the targeting and delivery efficiency of exosomes can be further regulated through modification and alteration of their surface molecules [ ].

5.2.2.

## Applications of exosome-mediated protein delivery in disease treatment
Animal-derived exosomes from different sources have achieved remarkable results in protein delivery. Animal-derived exosomes typically originate from mammalian cells, such as tumor cells, immune cells, and stem cells (The extraction methods of exosomes are shown in ). Through engineering modifications, these exosomes can carry specific proteins to achieve precise delivery to target cells. Their advantages lie in their good biocompatibility and low immunogenicity, enabling stable existence in the body for a long time without being easily eliminated by the immune system. Wan et al. utilized exosomes derived from hepatic stellate cells to deliver CRISPR/Cas9 ribonucleoprotein complexes (exosomeRNP) to liver tissue for the treatment of liver diseases [ ].The exosomeRNP demonstrated robust liver-specific genome-editing activity and significant therapeutic efficacy in mouse models of liver injury, hepatic fibrosis, and orthotopic liver cancer. In mouse models of acute liver failure and hepatocellular carcinoma, indel mutation frequencies of 26.1 % and 21.3 % were achieved, respectively( ). Guo et al. demonstrated that bioprinted mesenchymal stem cell microfiber-derived extracellular vesicles could mitigate unilateral renal ischemia-reperfusion injury and fibrosis by inhibiting ferroptosis in tubular epithelial cells [ ]. Ma et al. reported the intracellular delivery of cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS) via an extracellular vesicle (EV)-based approach termed IDEA, which effectively packaged and delivered native proteins both in vitro and in vivo [ ]. The results demonstrated that EVs carrying cGAS activated interferon signaling and elicited enhanced antitumor immunity in multiple syngeneic tumor models. Furthermore, the combination of cGAS-loaded EVs with immune checkpoint inhibitors synergistically improved antitumor efficacy in vivo. Additionally, exosomes possess natural cell-penetrating abilities, enabling them to cross biological barriers like the blood-brain barrier to achieve targeted delivery to specific tissues. Han et al. designed exosomes equipped with a light-induced cargo protein release system, termed mMaple3-mediated Protein Loading into and Release from Exosomes (MAPLEX) [ ]. In this system, the cargo protein can be loaded into exosomes through fusion with an exosome membrane marker conjugated to a photocleavable protein (mMaple3), and subsequently released from the exosome membrane via light-induced cleavage upon blue light illumination. By delivering Cre recombinase, in vivo gene recombination was induced in Cre reporter mice. This approach led to a significant reduction in Bace1 expression in 5xFAD and 3xTg-AD mice(two Alzheimer's disease models), improved recognition memory deficits, and decreased amyloid pathology. However, there are also certain limitations in the application of animal-derived exosomes. Firstly, the separation and purification techniques for exosomes are not yet perfect, making it difficult to obtain highly pure and active exosomes [ , , ]. Secondly, the transportation of exosomes in the body may be affected by various factors, such as blood flow and enzymatic degradation, resulting in decreased delivery efficiency [ ].
 Recently, plant-derived exosomes have gradually become a research focus in the field of protein delivery [ ]. Compared with animal-derived exosomes, plant-derived exosomes have higher yields and lower costs, and are not subject to restrictions from animal pathogens and ethical issues. Plant-derived exosomes also possess natural advantages in protein delivery. Through genetic engineering techniques, they can synthesize and secrete exosomes carrying specific proteins within plant cells. These exosomes have evolved unique structures and functions within plants, enabling efficient delivery of proteins to target cells. Hou et al. encapsulated peptide-based cosmetic raw materials into engineered nanoscale Leontopodium alpinum-derived exosomes (pExo) to achieve the goal of a healthier and more radiant skin condition. In this approach, pExo serves as a carrier for the cosmetic peptides to cross the intact skin barrier, thereby enhancing their bioefficacy in skin beautification [ ]. However, the application of plant-derived exosomes also faces some challenges [ ]. Firstly, there are significant differences in the structure and function between plant and animal cells, which may alter the behavior of exosomes in animals. Secondly, plant-derived exosomes may contain components harmful to animals, requiring strict screening and identification.

5.3.

## Development and challenges of exosome-based delivery
Regarding the application of exosomes in protein delivery, research directions mainly include the following aspects:(1) Optimizing the isolation and purification techniques of exosomes to improve their purity and activity; (2) Conducting in-depth research on the biological characteristics of exosomes and exploring their role and mechanism in intercellular communication; (3) Achieving precise modification and functionalization of exosomes through genetic engineering and other technological means to meet the needs of different fields; (4) Strengthening basic and clinical research on exosomes in the field of protein delivery to promote their development in disease treatment, drug delivery, and other areas. Notably, the administration route directly determines the bioavailability and ultimate therapeutic outcome of exosome-encapsulated protein drugs, necessitating a tailored delivery strategy. Intravenous infusion enables systemic exposure, while the intrinsic tropism of exosomes can be exploited for localized injections. Recent studies have further explored nebulized inhalation, allowing exosomes to traverse pulmonary barriers owing to their excellent biocompatibility [ ]. For instance, Dinh et al. reported that inhalation of secretomes and exosomes derived from lung spheroid cells significantly promoted tissue repair in pulmonary fibrosis models [ ]. In general, as a nano-scale vesicle with natural cell-penetrating ability and low immunogenicity, exosomes show great application potential in the field of protein delivery.

6.

## Cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers for the delivery of cytosolic proteins
Cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers, as an emerging drug delivery system, have demonstrated high efficiency and safety in protein delivery by mimicking the structure and function of cell membranes [ , ]. This section will provide a detailed introduction to the preparation methods, delivery mechanisms, and applications in disease treatment of biomimetic nanocarriers derived from cell membranes such as red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, and tumor cells [ ].
6.1.

## Preparation and delivery mechanism of cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers
The preparation of cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers mainly includes three steps: cell membrane extraction, nanomaterial preparation, and cell membrane coating [ ]. Firstly, cell membranes are extracted from specific cells through physical or chemical methods, such as red blood cell membranes, white blood cell membranes, platelet membranes, and tumor cell membranes. Then, nanomaterials with specific shapes and sizes, such as nanoparticles, nanotubes, and nanosheets, are prepared using nanotechnology. Finally, the extracted cell membranes are combined with the nanomaterials, and the cell membranes are coated on the surface of the nanomaterials through physical extrusion, chemical cross-linking, or biological self-assembly methods to form cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers.
The delivery mechanism of cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers mainly relies on their mimicry of the structure and function of cell membranes [ ]. Cell membrane-coated nanocarriers mimic host cells to evade immune clearance, enhancing circulation time and targeting. The “camouflage” feature of cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers underlies their highly efficient delivery. Firstly, cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers facilitate fusion with or internalization into target cell membranes by mimicking natural membrane-fusion proteins (e.g., SNARE proteins) or membrane fluidity, rather than directly traversing the phospholipid bilayer [ ]. Secondly, specific proteins or glycoproteins carried on the surface of cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers can bind to receptors on the surface of target cells, achieving targeted delivery. These carriers inherit the surface antigens and receptors of their source cell membranes. For instance, leukocyte-membrane nanocarriers leverage adhesion molecules such as CD18 on their surface to interact with ligands (e.g., ICAM-1) on inflamed endothelium or target cells, facilitating their internalization [ ]. Tumor-cell-membrane carriers, in contrast, are efficiently taken up by cancer cells through homotypic targeting [ ]. Although entry is primarily mediated by endocytosis, the surface-functional molecules can modulate intracellular trafficking. For example, peptides engineered to undergo conformational changes or membrane fusion at endosomal pH (such as GALA) can be integrated onto the carrier surface or co-delivered, actively disrupting the endosomal membrane and enabling cytosolic release [ ].

6.2.

## Applications of cell membrane-biomimetic nanocarriers
6.2.1.

## Red blood cell (RBC) membrane biomimetic nanocarriers
RBC membrane biomimetic nanocarriers represent a cornerstone of biomimetic nanomedicine [ , ]. The exploration of RBCs as drug carriers has a long history, beginning with early attempts to load therapeutics into RBC ghosts. However, the field was revolutionized by the landmark work of Zhang et al., in 2013, who introduced the concept of a biomimetic nanosponge [ ]. This design involved coating polymeric nanoparticles with intact RBC membranes, creating decoys that could absorb pore-forming toxins and divert them away from cellular targets. This innovation elegantly addressed stability and immunogenicity challenges, effectively leveraging the natural longevity and immune evasion properties of RBCs conferred by membrane proteins like CD47. Research in the past five years has moved far beyond simple toxin neutralization, focusing on sophisticated functionalization and combination therapies.
Rather than relying solely on passive accumulation, recent strategies actively functionalize RBC-NPs for targeted delivery. For instance, Xia et al. exploited a lysine-acetylation reaction to create site-selective antibody-lipid conjugates that decorate RBC carriers, enabling precise, tumor-cell-targeted delivery. This strategy can be readily extended to surface-functionalize other membrane-bound vesicles or lipid nanoparticles, offering a versatile platform for antibody-directed drug delivery [ ]. RBC-NPs have been engineered to co-deliver immunomodulatory agents (e.g., checkpoint inhibitors like anti-PD-L1) and chemotherapy drugs. The RBC membrane cloak ensures prolonged circulation, allowing for enhanced synergistic antitumor efficacy and reduced systemic toxicity. Cao et al. constructed a novel biomimetic nanoplatform by fusing cancer cell membranes with red blood cell membranes. When combined with aPD-L1 therapy, it enables multimodal cancer immunotherapy and demonstrates both outstanding tumor-targeting capability and long-circulating characteristics [ ]. In addition, Sanaee, Maryam et al. used nanovesicles derived from human red blood cell membranes to encapsulate antibodies, creating a carrier for intracellular antibody delivery. The resulting Ab-loaded vesicles exhibited an optimal radius of 52 nm and an average encapsulation efficiency of 14 % [ ]. Modern designs incorporate stimuli-sensitive linkers or materials within the nanoparticle core. These “smart” RBC-NPs can release their cargo in response to specific tumor microenvironment cues (e.g., high glutathione, acidic pH, or overexpressed enzymes), achieving spatiotemporal control over drug release and improving therapeutic precision. For example, fibrin-targeted, RBC membrane coated dextran-tirofiban conjugate nanoparticles (T-RBC-DTC NPs) efficiently scavenge H2O2 and precisely release tirofiban on demand, enabling site-specific antithrombotic therapy [ ]. These recent advancements highlight a shift from using RBC-NPs as simple passive carriers to engineering them as active, multifaceted platforms for complex therapeutic applications, including targeted cancer therapy, immunotherapy, and treatment of neurological disorders.

6.2.2.

## White blood cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers
White blood cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers mimic the immune recognition and migration abilities of natural white blood cells, presenting potential applications in disease diagnosis and treatment. White blood cells are broadly classified into two principal categories: granulocytes and agranulocytes. Granulocytes comprise three distinct subtypes (neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils) distinguished by their cytoplasmic granule characteristics. The agranulocyte group consists of monocytes and lymphocytes, which exhibit non-granular cytoplasm. Monocytes further differentiate into specialized antigen-presenting cells, specifically macrophages and dendritic cells. Lymphocytes demonstrate functional diversity through three main populations: T lymphocytes (including cytotoxic T cells), B lymphocytes responsible for antibody production, and natural killer (NK) cells that mediate innate immune responses. By encapsulating specific drugs or proteins within these nanocarriers, they can achieve precise localization and delivery to diseased tissues. Neutrophil membranes can enhance the targeting of endothelial cells within plaques through the interaction between neutrophil membrane protein CD18 and endothelial cell membrane protein ICAM-1 [ ].Since leukocytes can pass through inflamed endothelial cells by utilizing the functions of membrane proteins, Fukuta et al. incorporated leukocyte membrane proteins into liposome membranes to endow the liposomes with functions similar to those of leukocytes, thereby enabling them to adhere to and actively pass through inflamed endothelial cells [ ]. Furthermore, they mimic the signal transduction function of natural white blood cells, triggering the body's own immune response and enhancing therapeutic effects. Cui et al. proposed a neutrophil-mimicking, metal-porphyrin-based nanodevice (FKPN) loaded with porcine pancreatic elastase for cancer therapy, which integrates the innate immune factor PPE, nuclear 1O2 generator porphyrin-NLS, metal node Fe3+, and neutrophil membrane (NM) [ ]. The tumor-targeting FKPN nanoplatform utilizes MOF nanostructures and NM coating for precise delivery. The released porcine pancreatic elastase (PPE) mimics neutrophil functions to induce histone H1-dependent selective cancer cell elimination. Simultaneously, the nuclear-localized porphyrin (Porphyrin-NLS), serving as an in situ singlet oxygen (1O2) generator, amplifies histone H1 nucleo-cytoplasmic translocation through laser-induced DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), thereby enhancing tumor eradication efficiency( ).
 The biological mechanism by which FKPN achieves selective cancer cell killing and immune system activation involves the synergistic action of PPE and a tumor-specific nuclear 1O2 generator. Adapted with permission [ ].

6.2.3.

## Platelet membrane biomimetic nanocarriers
Platelet membrane biomimetic nanocarriers possess superior hemostatic and coagulation functions, making them potentially useful in the treatment of hemorrhagic diseases and cardiovascular diseases. By encapsulating specific drugs or proteins within these nanocarriers, rapid localization and hemostasis at bleeding sites can be achieved. Gao et al. engineered functional platelet bodies (Fe@PLP-TR-A) to co-deliver angiopoietin-like 4 (ANGPTL4) and the ROS scavenger Fe3O4, thereby enhancing endothelial barrier protection and preventing myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury. Additionally, they mimic the adhesion and aggregation functions of natural platelets, promoting vascular repair and regeneration [ ]. Yu et al. developed a nanoparticle for cancer cell-targeted dual delivery of small molecules and proteins, leveraging the natural tumor-targeting ability of platelets. These nanoparticles specifically bind to metastatic human breast cancer cells and enhance the killing of tumor cells in mouse models by co-delivering tumor necrosis factor–related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) and doxorubicin [ ]. Fan et al. encapsulated GALA and granzyme B (GrB) into nanoparticles using a double orthogonal reaction and then conjugated these nanoparticles (GALA-GNGs-P) onto the surface of platelets via an acid-sensitive benzylhydrazone linker. Leveraging the wound-tropic nature of platelets, GALA-GNGs-P can actively accumulate at surgical trauma sites and release the nanogel in response to the acidic tumor microenvironment (TME), thereby facilitating deep penetration [ ] ( ).
 
6.2.4.

## Tumor cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers
Tumor cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers mimic the surface characteristics of tumor cells, enabling precise localization and delivery to tumor tissues [ , ]. By encapsulating specific drugs or proteins within these nanocarriers, targeted killing of tumor cells can be achieved while reducing side effects on other normal cells [ ]. While homotypic targeting promotes accumulation at the tumor site, further intracellular targeting can be achieved [ ]. By co-loading proteins with innate organelle-targeting signals (e.g., Nuclear Localization Signals, NLS) or by decorating the nanocarrier itself with mitochondrial-penetrating peptides, the therapeutic cargo can be directed to specific subcellular compartments, thereby enhancing its efficacy (a concept depicted in ).
Furthermore, they mimic the migration and invasion abilities of tumor cells, providing new ideas and methods for tumor diagnosis and treatment. HepM-PLGA nanoparticles were fabricated by coating homotypic HepG2 cell membranes onto poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles [ ]. In vivo studies demonstrated that HepM-PLGA could directly deliver Dox to tumor lesions in nude mice, resulting in a nearly 90 % reduction in tumor volume following treatment. Xie et al. encapsulated glucose oxidase (GOx)-loaded mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSN) with cancer cell membranes to achieve starvation therapy. The functionalized MSN can evade the immune system and exhibit enhanced cancer-targeting ability. Moreover, the CMSN-GOx complex can disrupt tumors and induce dendritic cell maturation to stimulate antitumor immune responses [ ] ( ).
 

6.3.

## Challenges of cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers
As an emerging drug delivery system, cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers have achieved efficient and safe protein delivery by mimicking the structure and function of cell membranes [ ]( ). Biomimetic nanocarriers derived from red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, and tumor cells hold broad application prospects in disease treatment [ ]. The long-term behavior and biocompatibility of cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers in vivo have not been fully investigated. Long-term injection or ingestion of these nanocarriers may have unknown effects on health, such as cumulative toxicity, immune reactions, or interference with normal biological processes. Current research mainly focuses on short-term delivery efficiency and biocompatibility, lacking long-term tracking and evaluation [ ].
 Additionally, further exploration and optimization of delivery mechanisms are crucial. Although cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers can mimic certain functions of natural cell membranes, their specific delivery mechanisms still require further investigation. This includes the interaction between the carrier and the cell membrane, the fusion process, and the release of proteins within the cell. Aspects such as delivery efficiency, stability, and targeting also need to be optimized [ ]. For example, ensuring the stability of the carrier in complex biological environments and further improving targeting while reducing non-specific binding.
Although protein delivery into cells using cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers faces some challenges, the development directions are clear. Through efforts in long-term safety evaluation, in-depth exploration and optimization of delivery mechanisms, personalized medicine, and precision medicine, we are hopeful to overcome these challenges and achieve widespread applications of biomimetic nanocarriers in the medical field. With the continuous improvement of preparation techniques and in-depth research on delivery mechanisms, cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers will play an increasingly important role in the field of biomedicine.

7.

## Conclusion and prospects
7.1.

## Biological inspiration and design principle
This review comprehensively examines the current research progress in biomimetic intracellular protein delivery vectors, analyzing the characteristics, mechanisms, applications, and challenges of various delivery vectors. The core of biomimetic nanocarrier design lies in “borrowing wisdom from nature”: the highly efficient delivery mechanisms evolved by virus, bacteria, biomineralization processes, exosomes, and natural cell membranes are translated into safe, precise, and controllable intracellular protein-drug platforms. VLPs retain the viral fusion proteins and endosomal escape machinery but are stripped of the viral genome, thereby replicating the high-efficiency “membrane-penetrating cell entry” route of viruses without any infection risk [ , ]. Engineered bacteria exploit their type III secretion systems or the natural hypoxia-tropism of anaerobic strains; after attenuation they serve as “living syringes” or their membrane vesicles continue to execute lesion-homing and cytosolic protein delivery [ , ]. Mineralized nanoparticles mimic the biomineralization that forms bone, shells, and teeth, allowing the therapeutic protein itself to act as a template that induces in-situ crystallization of a calcium-phosphate or metal-organic-framework shell. This shell shields the cargo from enzymatic degradation in circulation yet dissociates intelligently in the acidic tumor microenvironment or in the high-glutathione intracellular milieu to release the active protein on site [ , ]. Exosomes directly hijack the intercellular “express-delivery system”; thanks to the “self” identity proteins of their parent cell membrane and their membrane-fusion capacity, they naturally evade immune phagocytosis and enter target cells efficiently, and surface engineering can further enhance their homing to parent tissues or tumor sites [ ]. The CD47 ″don't-eat-me” signal of erythrocyte membranes, the inflammatory-adhesion receptors of leukocyte membranes, and the homotypic aggregation traits of cancer-cell membranes are extracted and fused onto synthetic nanoparticle cores, creating long-circulating protein carriers that can actively migrate toward inflammation or tumors and penetrate deeply into tumor tissue [ , ].
Each class of biomimetic nanocarrier possesses a unique combination of strengths and weaknesses, making them differentially suited for specific applications [ ]. The choice of platform should be guided by the specific requirements of the therapeutic scenario: For high-efficiency gene editing in vivo, engineered VLPs currently stand out due to their excellent delivery efficiency and targeting capability, despite concerns over immunogenicity which must be managed [ , ]. For robust tumor killing and modulation of the tumor microenvironment, bacterial systems offer unique advantages through their intrinsic tropism and high payload, but their application is likely restricted to severe cancers due to safety profiles. For applications requiring high biocompatibility and responsive release in acidic microenvironments (e.g., solid tumors, ischemic tissues), mineralized nanoparticles are a prime candidate, though they require additional engineering for active targeting [ , ]. For treatments targeting the central nervous system or requiring minimal immune activation (e.g., regenerative medicine and chronic diseases), exosomes hold great promise, but their clinical translation is currently hampered by manufacturing challenges. For achieving long circulation times and sophisticated targeting without complex synthetic chemistry, cell membrane biomimetic nanocarriers serve as a powerful platform, particularly for cancer and inflammatory diseases, albeit with a more complex production process. The ideal nanocarrier is dictated by a balance of efficiency, safety, manufacturability, and the specific biological barriers that need to be overcome for a given protein drug and disease target.

7.2.

## Biomimetic nanocarriers: how to breach biological barriers
By simultaneously orchestrating morphological, chemical and biological cues, biomimetic nanocarriers execute a “triple-camouflage” program that carries therapeutic proteins from the bloodstream to the interior of individual organelles: first, a PEG stealth layer combined with surface ligands enables prolonged circulation and precise tumor anchoring; second, disassembly is triggered on demand by organelle-specific micro-environmental signals such as acidic pH, over-expressed enzymes or elevated GSH, releasing the payload exactly where needed; third, mitochondria- or nucleus-homing peptides guide the freed proteins into the targeted sub-cellular compartments. This multilevel cooperation allows the carriers to evade immune surveillance, penetrate deep into tumor tissue and liberate their protein drugs on cue, achieving efficient vascular-to-organelle transit ( ).
 Immune evasion hinges on cloaking nanocarriers with an “invisibility coat” through surface engineering. PEGylation and ligand conjugation constitute a versatile “universal toolbox” whose components play complementary yet distinct roles on different biomimetic platforms. PEGylation supplies the stealth: for highly immunogenic VLPs or bacterial systems it curbs opsonization and prolongs circulation; for mineralized or membrane-coated cores it is often layered first, followed by an outer membrane, creating a “stabilize-then-hide” double lock; even exosomes, though intrinsically “self, " can be lightly PEGylated when ultra-long circulation or blood–brain-barrier crossing is desired, further shielding them from phagocytes. Ligand conjugation, in contrast, provides precision guidance: on mineralized particles that lack innate tropism it is the sole route to active targeting; on VLPs, ligands genetically fused or chemically coupled to the capsid redirect the virus beyond its natural tissue specificity; on exosomes, ligands reinforce innate homing or reroute them to new targets; and on cell-membrane-coated nanoparticles, additional ligands complement the adhesion molecules inherited from the source cell, building multivalent or super-targeted systems. In short, PEGylation minimizes immune clearance, ligand conjugation maximizes targeting accuracy, and the combinatorial use of the two across platforms ultimately dictates the in vivo fate of the nanomedicine.
Due to the microenvironment of organelles (Acidic pH, millimolar GSH, and organ-specific enzymes) differs sharply from that of normal tissue, researchers have engineered nanocarriers that act as sensors for these cues. For example, the pH inside tumor-cell endosomes/lysosomes (∼5.0) is markedly lower than that of the cytosol (∼7.4); therefore, carriers built from pH-responsive materials can be protonated, undergo charge reversal, or simply fall apart in this acidic milieu, releasing their payload and/or facilitating endosomal escape. For example, Zhang et al. developed a nanoplatform (Fe-EGCG@RSL3) self-assembled from epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) and Fe2+ to encapsulate the ferroptosis inducer RSL3 [ ]. This nano-formulation releases Fe2+ and RSL3 in a pH-responsive manner, efficiently triggering tumor-cell ferroptosis while simultaneously reshaping the tumor immune microenvironment (TME). This nanomedicine integrates polyphenol-based biomimetic chelation with smart TME responsiveness, converting “simple killing” into “immune activation.” It should be noted that VLPs and exosomes exploit fundamentally different endosomal-escape routes: VLPs mimic viruses and are routinely engineered for rapid, active rupture of the endosomal membrane, whereas native exosomes accomplish cytosolic delivery largely through a passive, cell-dependent process that is usually less efficient and highly variable with exosome source and cargo. For enzyme responsiveness, tumors frequently over-express proteases such as matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and cathepsins; nanocarriers can therefore be equipped with enzyme-cleavable linkers that are severed on site to liberate the active drug. Likewise, the cytosol and nucleus contain GSH (2–10 mM), three orders of magnitude higher than the extracellular fluid (∼20 μM); carriers that incorporate disulfide bonds are reduced and cleaved once inside the cell, triggering payload release [ ]. By harnessing these intellectually responsive cues unique to organellar microenvironments, nanocarriers can achieve pinpoint drug delivery within tumors or inflammatory lesions.
Decorating nanocarriers with organelle-specific targeting ligands is currently the most straightforward route to sub-cellular precision. By installing surface molecules that are recognized or bound by a particular organelle, the carriers are given an onboard “navigation system. “ Mitochondrial delivery typically exploits mitochondrial-targeting signal peptides or cationic triphenylphosphonium derivatives that accumulate in the inner membrane in response to the mitochondrial membrane potential; nuclear delivery relies on nuclear-localization signals that are recognized by cytosolic importins and actively ferried through the nuclear-pore complex; lysosomal targeting can be achieved either with materials that are inherently prone to lysosomal sequestration or by appending specific ligands that actively direct the carriers, enabling each organelle to be addressed with high accuracy.

7.3.

## Future perspectives
With the continuous advancement of nanotechnology, biomimetic delivery vectors will increasingly rely on the integration and innovation of nanotechnology. This includes developing novel nanomaterials, optimizing the preparation processes of nanovectors, and exploring the interaction mechanisms between nanovectors and biomolecules. Biomimetic delivery vectors will increasingly incorporate intelligent elements, such as responsive polymers and non-invasive treatments [ , ]. These intelligent elements can achieve precise control over drug release, meeting different therapeutic needs. Drug release rates can be intelligently adjusted based on changes in the tumor microenvironment, achieving more precise treatments [ ]. Multimodal delivery systems can utilize multiple delivery mechanisms simultaneously, achieving more efficient drug delivery. For instance, combining the penetration ability of VLPs and the targeting ability of bacterial vectors, multimodal delivery systems with stronger penetration and higher targeting can be developed. Additionally, multiple treatment modalities (Such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy and immunotherapy) can be integrated into a single delivery system for comprehensive treatment. To address the safety and immunogenicity issues of existing biomimetic delivery vectors, research will focus on reducing the immunogenicity and improving the safety of vectors in vivo through improved material selection, optimized preparation processes, and the introduction of immunosuppressive strategies [ ]. With the continuous development of genetic sequencing and bioinformatics technologies, biomimetic delivery vectors will increasingly be applied in the field of personalized medicine. By customizing personalized delivery systems based on patients' genotypes, disease types, treatment needs, and other factors, more precise and effective treatments can be achieved. As biomimetic delivery vectors become more widely used in clinical applications, regulatory policies also need to be continuously improved [ ]. Future research will need to focus on how to formulate scientific and reasonable regulatory policies to ensure the safety and effectiveness of biomimetic delivery vectors, while promoting their clinical applications and development.
Regarding clinical translation, while no biomimetic nanocarrier for cytosolic protein delivery has yet reached the market, their potential is immense. The success of adjacent technologies, such as lipid nanoparticles for mRNA delivery, validates the clinical viability of nanoparticle platforms. A search of the ClinicalTrials.gov platform reveals that more than one hundred intervention studies based on biomimetic carriers have been registered worldwide, with indications concentrated in critical diseases such as cancer and COVID-19. Taking NCT03608631 as an example, this Phase I trial is designed to determine the recommended dose and toxic side effects of mesenchymal stromal cell-derived exosomes carrying KrasG12D siRNA (iExosomes) in patients with KrasG12D-mutated metastatic pancreatic cancer; preliminary data suggest iExosomes may offer advantages over conventional regimens. Nevertheless, the transition from registration to first-patient enrollment still faces multiple bottlenecks (Regulatory approval, large-scale manufacturing, and quality-control standards) leaving numerous projects stalled at the initiation stage.
We anticipate that the first clinical applications will likely emerge in areas with high unmet need and favorable risk-benefit profiles, such as oncology (e.g., delivering cytotoxic proteins or gene-editing machinery) and monogenic diseases (e.g., VLP-mediated delivery of BE). The inherent targeting and biocompatibility of biomimetic systems offer a superior alternative to conventional delivery methods for these applications. However, translation hinges on overcoming key challenges in manufacturing scalability, thorough characterization of in vivo pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, and meticulous assessment of immunogenicity. Addressing these challenges through interdisciplinary efforts will pave the way for these innovative biomimetic platforms to transition from the laboratory to the clinic, ultimately realizing their potential to revolutionize the treatment of a wide array of diseases. In conclusion, the potential for protein delivery with biomimetic nanocarriers is tremendous, and more and more novel material or delivery vehicles emerge and can be uncovered by further scientific research.
