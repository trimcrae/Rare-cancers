## Abstract

## Background
Myocardial infarction (MI) remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. MFAP5 (microfibrillar‐associated protein 5) is an extracellular‐matrix associated secreted glycoprotein whose cardiac function is unknown. This study aimed to explore the origin, expression, function, and potential mechanisms of MFAP5 in MI.

## Methods
Single‐cell analysis and immunohistochemistry were used to determine the origin and expression of MFAP5. Global MFAP5‐knockout (KO) mice and adeno‐associated virus 9‐mediated cardiac fibroblast‐specific overexpression mice were used to investigate the function of MFAP5 in vivo. RNA sequencing and ingenuity pathway analysis revealed potential mechanisms. Coimmunoprecipitation and confocal microscopy identified molecular interaction domains. rMFAP5 (recombinant MFAP5 protein) and plasmid‐mediated intracellular overexpression revealed the effects of MFAP5 on cardiomyocytes under oxygen–glucose deprivation conditions.

## Results
MFAP5 was significantly increased in both human and mouse heart tissues during MI, predominantly derived from activated cardiac fibroblasts. In vivo, MFAP5‐knockout exacerbated cardiac dysfunction, enlarged infarct size and increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis during the acute phase of MI. Cardiac fibroblast‐specific overexpression of MFAP5 exerted a protective effect. In vitro, MFAP5 significantly inhibited apoptosis in cardiomyocytes under oxygen–glucose deprivation stimulation. Mechanistically, MFAP5 derived from fibroblasts interacted with the extracellular EGF (epidermal growth factor) repeats of the NOTCH2 receptor on the surface of cardiomyocytes via its MBD domain, promoting shedding of the extracellular fragment of NOTCH2 and generation of the NICD2 (NOTCH2 intracellular domain). MFAP5 inhibited cardiomyocyte apoptosis by activating NOTCH2 signaling.

## Conclusions
Fibroblast‐derived MFAP5 engages cardiomyocyte NOTCH2 to inhibit apoptosis and exerts cardioprotective effects after MI.

## Research Perspective

## What Is New?

This study reveals a novel fibroblast‐cardiomyocyte crosstalk and suggests that the MFAP5 (microfibrillar‐associated protein 5)‐NOTCH2 axis may serve as a potential target for rescuing cardiomyocyte viability post myocardial infarction.
Fibroblast‐derived MFAP5 is significantly increased in cardiac tissue post myocardial infarction and it inhibits apoptosis by interacting with NOTCH2 on cardiomyocytes.

## What Question Should Be Addressed Next?

Further research should aim to clarify whether MFAP5 has clinical translational potential as a biomarker or therapeutic target in myocardial infarction.

Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading global cause of mortality, with myocardial infarction (MI) being a major contributor. Under stress conditions, intercellular communication between cardiomyocytes and noncardiomyocytes significantly influences MI progression. Therefore, elucidating the molecular interactions and mechanisms underlying these communications has become a critical area in cardiovascular research. Extracellular matrix (ECM) and ECM‐associated proteins dynamically and multifunctionally regulate cardiac structure and function. Beyond structural scaffolding, ECM components remodel the tissue microenvironment, actively participate in cellular signaling, and modulate processes such as cell survival, apoptosis, inflammation, and fibrosis. The ECM is composed of a complex array of molecules, primarily including collagen fibers, elastic fibers, proteoglycans, and fibronectin, which provide structural and functional support for cells. ECM‐associated proteins are expressed under pathological conditions and regulate various cellular functions by interacting with cell surface receptors, proteases, hormones, and the cellular matrix. Following MI, significant alterations in cardiac ECM composition critically influence infarction progression. A deeper understanding of ECM and related proteins in the context of MI will facilitate the development of novel therapeutic strategies.
MFAP5 (microfibrillar‐associated protein 5), also known as MAGP‐2 (microfibril‐associated glycoprotein 2), is an ECM‐associated secretory glycoprotein capable of interacting with microfibril/elastin networks to mediate cell adhesion or existing as a soluble factor within the tissue microenvironment to bind cell surface receptors, activating downstream signaling pathways. , , Loss of MFAP5 function may directly affect vascular structure and function by altering ECM organization or elastin fiber deposition. Furthermore, ECM‐derived MFAP5 interacts with tumor cell surface receptors across various cancers, enhancing tumor cell proliferation. , , However, no studies have directly investigated the role of MFAP5 in the heart. Notably, the function of MFAP5 exhibits cellular and tissue specificity. For example, MFAP5 promotes endothelial cell (EC) sprouting and angiogenesis by inhibiting NOTCH1 signaling in ECs, yet it activates NOTCH1 signaling in non‐ECs. , The functional complexity and pleiotropic nature of ECM and associated proteins are further illustrated by contradictory findings regarding MFAP4, another member of the MFAP family, in cardiac tissues. Although MFAP4 knockout (KO) has been reported to ameliorate cardiac dysfunction and fibrosis induced by transverse aortic constriction and isoproterenol administration, a recent study demonstrated protective cardiac effects of MFAP4. , Our preliminary studies indicate significantly elevated cardiac MFAP5 expression during the acute phase of MI in mice. Given that the cardiac role of MFAP5 remains unclear and its effects might vary depending on tissue or cell specificity, elucidating the precise role and mechanisms of MFAP5 in MI is crucial.
This study investigates the expression patterns, sources, functional roles, and mechanisms of MFAP5 in the MI heart. Specifically, we demonstrate that during the acute phase of MI, activated cardiac fibroblasts (CFs) express and secrete MFAP5 into the extracellular space. MFAP5 binds via its matrix binding domains (MBD) to the EGF (epidermal growth factor)‐like repeats of NOTCH2 receptors on cardiomyocytes, promoting extracellular domain shedding and generating the NICD2 (NOTCH2 intracellular domain). This subsequently activates NOTCH2 signaling, suppresses cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and protects cardiac function. Collectively, our findings highlight MFAP5 as a critical mediator in fibroblast‐cardiomyocyte crosstalk. Targeting the MFAP5‐NOTCH2 axis presents a promising therapeutic strategy for reducing infarct size, preserving cardiac function, and preventing heart failure progression post MI.

## METHODS
Detailed methods are provided in Data  . The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.
 

## Human Cardiac Tissue Collection
Cardiac tissue was obtained from 8 autopsy specimens provided by the China Medical University Center of Forensic Investigation. This study included 4 male subjects who died from acute MI (AMI). The inclusion criteria required that autopsy and histopathological examination reveal no other fatal diseases besides pulmonary congestion and edema caused by AMI‐induced cardiac dysfunction. Four control cardiac specimens were obtained from age‐ and sex‐matched individuals who died in traffic accidents and had no history of heart disease. Their clinical characteristics are summarized in Table  . Tissue acquisition adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee for Medical Science Research of the First Hospital of China Medical University (Approval No. 2024‐936‐2). Consent for research use of explanted or the donor heart tissues was obtained in all cases.

## Gene Expression Analysis and Visualization
Original gene expression data (GSE236374) were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, comprising bulk RNA sequencing data of left ventricular myocardium from male C57BL/6J mice at various post‐MI time points.
Publicly available single‐cell RNA sequencing data from the Broad Institute Single Cell Portal (https://singlecell.broadinstitute.org/) were used to explore the cellular localization and expression profile of MFAP5 in human ischemic hearts. Further analyses were conducted using the Seurat package (v5.1.0) in R.

## Animals
Wild‐type (WT) C57BL/6 mice aged 10 to 12 weeks were purchased from Hfkbio (Beijing, China). C57BL/6‐background MFAP5 KO mice (Cat. NO. NM‐KO‐201713) were acquired from the Shanghai Model Organisms Center, which were generated by knocking out exons 3 to 8 of the Mfap5 gene. All animal experiments complied with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of China Medical University (CMUXN2023047).
To achieve specific overexpression of MFAP5 in CFs, we infected mice with adeno‐associated virus 9 (AAV9). The virus carried the CF‐specific TCF21 promoter (Hanbio, China) and expressed either an empty vector (AAV9‐negative control) or MFAP5 (AAV9‐MFAP5). Fourteen days after tail vein injection of AAV9 (1×1013 vg/mL), mice underwent MI or sham surgery, and cardiac tissue was collected at designated time points.
The mouse MI model was established by permanently ligating the left anterior descending artery of the coronary artery. In brief, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane inhalation. A small incision was made between the third and fourth intercostal spaces, and the heart was exposed by squeezing. The left anterior descending artery was ligated with 6‐0 silk suture. The chest and skin were immediately sutured after the surgery.
At specified time points post MI, mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation, and cardiac tissue was harvested. The infarct border zone was defined as myocardial tissue located 1 to 2 mm from the infarct margin.

## Echocardiography
Echocardiography was performed 7 days after MI, with the operator blinded to treatment groups. Mice were anesthetized with inhaled isoflurane, and echocardiographic examinations were conducted using the Vinno Imaging System. Long‐axis and short‐axis views were obtained in B‐mode, and parameters were measured across 3 cardiac cycles in M‐mode, calculating mean values.

## Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase dUTP Nick End Labeling
To assess cell apoptosis in heart tissue, heart specimens were harvested 2 days post MI. Staining was performed using the One‐Step Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase‐dUTP Nick End Labeling (TUNEL) Apoptosis Assay Kit (KTA2011, Abbkine) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Concurrently, cardiomyocytes were detected using anti‐ACTN2 (alpha‐actinin‐2) primary antibody (14221‐1‐AP, Proteintech), with cell nuclei visualized via DAPI. Images were visualized and captured using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan). Colocalization of TUNEL and ACTN2 was considered indicative of apoptotic cardiomyocytes.
To study cardiomyocyte death in vitro, cardiomyocytes were seeded onto culture dishes for immunofluorescence staining. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100. Subsequently, TUNEL assays were performed, and nuclei were visualized using DAPI. Finally, images were visualized and captured using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan).

## 
RNA Sequencing and Ingenuity Pathway Analysis
Left ventricular tissues were isolated from mouse hearts. RNA extracted was sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. DESeq2 was used for differential expression analyses between groups. Genes with fold‐change ≥1.5 and P≤0.05 were identified as differentially expressed genes. Ingenuity pathway analysis assessed upstream regulators, pathways, and regulator effects. Upstream regulators with an activation score |Z|≥2 (Z≥2 indicating significant upregulation, Z≤−2 indicating significant downregulation) and P<0.05 were considered biologically relevant.

## Cell Isolation, Culture, and Treatment
Sprague‐Dawley rats aged 1 to 2 days were used to extract neonatal rat cardiomyocytes (NRCMs). NRCMs were isolated using the Pierce Primary Cardiomyocyte Isolation Kit (88281, Thermo). Hearts were transferred to PBS for washing, then cut into small pieces and digested. To obtain relatively pure cardiomyocytes, the isolated cells were cultured for 90 minutes using the differential velocity adherence method. Nonadherent cells were collected as cardiomyocytes and cultured in a medium containing 10% FBS and antibiotics.
For the isolation and culture of adult mouse CFs, the hearts of adult mice were cut off and digested in HBSS containing 0.01% type II collagenase. The isolated cells were cultured for 90 minutes using the differential velocity adherence method, and then the supernatant containing the cardiomyocytes was removed. Adherent CFs were further cultured in medium containing 10% FBS and antibiotics.
H9c2 cardiomyocytes and 293T cells were obtained from the National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures (Shanghai, China) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and antibiotics.
To establish the oxygen–glucose deprivation (OGD) model, H9c2 cells were transfected with a MFAP5 plasmid or treated with rMFAP5 (recombinant MFAP5 protein) for 24 hours. Cells were then cultured under serum‐free, glucose‐free conditions in a hypoxia chamber (94% N2, 5% CO2, 1% O2) for designated durations.

## Western Blot
Mouse cardiac tissues or cultured cells were lysed in buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA protein assay kit. Proteins separated via SDS‐PAGE were transferred to PVDF membranes, blocked with 5% BSA, incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, washed, and incubated with secondary antibodies for 2 hours at room temperature. Bands were visualized using MicroChem (DNR Bio‐Imaging System) and quantified using ImageJ software. All Western blot results included 3 repeated experiments, and the statistical results were shown as the ratio to the internal control group.

## Coimmunoprecipitation
Cell lysates prepared under nondenaturing conditions were incubated with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C for 2 hours, followed by incubation with Protein A/G beads or antibody‐conjugated magnetic beads for 6 hours at 4 °C. Beads were washed 4 times, immune complexes were eluted in SDS sample buffer, and analyzed by Western blot.

## 
Real‐Time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was extracted from mouse heart using RNAiso Plus (9108, TaKaRa). Reverse transcription and amplification were conducted using PrimeScript RT reagent kit (RR047A, TaKaRa) and TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (RR820A, TaKaRa). Primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech, and GAPDH served as an internal control. Primer sequences are listed in Table  . Gene expression was calculated using the ΔCt method, with the 2−ΔΔCt method for comparison.

## Statistical Analysis
The data presented in this study originate from at least 3 independent experiments. All data are expressed as mean±SD. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were plotted to show survival rates. Statistical inference for small sample data with unknown population distributions was performed using permutation tests in R software (“coin” package). P values are retained to 2 significant digits, with scientific notation used for very small P values.

## RESULTS

## 
MFAP5 Is Elevated in MI Hearts and Is Mainly Secreted by Cardiac Fibroblasts
To elucidate the expression of MFAP5 in MI hearts, we first analyzed publicly available bulk RNA sequencing data (GSE236374) from murine cardiac tissues at multiple post‐MI time points. The results indicated significantly elevated MFAP5 expression during the acute phase post MI, which, despite decreasing in the chronic phase, remained substantially higher than sham‐operated group (Figure  ). We further analyzed publicly accessible single‐cell RNA sequencing data set (SCP1849) from human hearts available on the 10× Genomics platform. Differential expression analysis among cell subsets revealed that MFAP5 expression was predominantly localized to the PDGFRA‐positive fibroblas subpopulation (Figure  ).
To verify these findings experimentally, we established a mouse MI model by ligating the left anterior descending artery artery. Real‐time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) demonstrated significantly increased myocardial MFAP5 expression 7 days post MI, which declined at 28 days (Figure  ). Immunohistochemistry revealed predominant MFAP5 expression within the infarct and border zones (Figure  ). Immunofluorescence analysis in border‐zone myocardium confirmed colocalization of MFAP5 with periostin, the marker of activated CFs (Figure  ). Subsequently, we isolated and cultured CFs from sham‐operated and MI mice. RT‐qPCR showed increased mRNA expression levels of MFAP5 and markers of activated fibroblast, collagen I, and periostin, in CFs derived from MI heart (Figure  through ). Western blot of concentrated cell culture supernatants confirmed that CFs highly expressed MFAP5 and secreted it extracellularly (Figure  ).
We further evaluated MFAP5 expression in human cardiac tissues obtained from those who died of AMI and noncardiac causes (non‐AMI). Immunohistochemistry and Sirius red staining of adjacent sections revealed that MFAP5 expression was significantly elevated in myocardial tissue from AMI patients, primarily localized within fibrotic areas (Figure  ).
Collectively, these data demonstrate that post‐MI cardiac tissues express high levels of MFAP5, which is produced and secreted by activated CFs and primarily concentrated within infarct and border zones.

## Global MFAP5 Knockout Exacerbates Post‐MI Cardiac Dysfunction and Cardiomyocyte Apoptosis
To explore the role of MFAP5 in the heart in vivo, we generated global MFAP5 KO mice lacking exons 3 to 8 homozygous. MFAP5 KO mice displayed no developmental, reproductive, cardiac functional, lifespan, or behavioral abnormalities compared with WT littermates. To investigate the role of MFAP5 post MI, we subjected 10 to 12‐week‐old mice to left anterior descending artery ligation. Kaplan–Meier survival curves indicated significantly reduced post‐MI survival in MFAP5 KO compared with WT mice (Figure  ). Autopsies on mice revealed that MFAP5 KO did not significantly increase the rate of cardiac rupture. RT‐qPCR confirmed significant MFAP5 upregulation in myocardial tissues 7 days post MI, whereas expression was nearly undetectable in KO mice (Figure  ). Meanwhile, MFAP5 KO markedly increased the mRNA expression level of ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide), a marker of heart failure post MI (Figure  ). Echocardiography demonstrated severe cardiac dysfunction in KO mice 7 days post MI, characterized by reduced ejection fraction and increased left ventricular end‐systolic volume (Figure  through ). Sirius red staining revealed significantly larger infarct sizes in KO mice compared with WT controls (Figure  and ). The wall thickness of the infarct zone was slightly reduced in MFAP5 KO mice and with no significant differences in the collagen volume fraction, collagen quality, or number of α‐SMA (alpha smooth muscle actin)‐positive fibroblasts within the infarct zone (Figure  through ). These findings indicate that MFAP5 KO exacerbates heart dysfunction of mice in the acute phase of MI.
 Immunohistochemical staining for macrophage marker F4/80 showed increased numbers of activated macrophages in infarct and border zones post MI, with no significant difference between MFAP5 KO and control groups. Immunostaining for endothelial marker CD31 revealed no difference in microvascular density between the infarct and border zones of MFAP5 KO and WT mice (Figure  ).
To further characterize the role of MFAP5 in the acute phase of MI, we harvested mouse heart tissue 2 days post MI. TUNEL staining demonstrated significantly increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis in the border zone of MFAP5 KO mice (Figure  and ). Consistently, Western blot showed elevated C‐PARP (cleaved poly (ADP‐ribose) polymerase) apoptosis marker levels in MFAP5 KO mouse hearts (Figure  ).
These data indicate that in vivo MFAP5 deletion enhances cardiomyocyte apoptosis post MI, exacerbating cardiac dysfunction and expanding infarct size.

## Cardiac Fibroblast‐Specific Overexpression of MFAP5 Alleviates Post‐MI Cardiac Dysfunction and Cardiomyocyte Apoptosis
To investigate whether MFAP5 overexpression in vivo confers protective effects against AMI, we specifically overexpressed MFAP5 in mouse CFs using AAV9 carrying the CF‐specific TCF21 promoter and expressing an empty vector (AAV9‐negative control) or MFAP5 (AAV9‐MFAP5) (Figure  ). Fourteen days after tail vein injection, myocardial tissue from a subset of mice was analyzed by Western blot, and RT‐qPCR on isolated CFs confirmed successful overexpression of MFAP5 protein in AAV9‐MFAP5 mice (Figure  and ). The remaining mice underwent MI or sham surgery. Immunofluorescence confirmed MFAP5 overexpression in CFs within the infarct border zone and infarct zone (Figure  ). There was no significant difference in survival rates post MI between MFAP5‐overexpressing mice and control mice. (Figure  ). Echocardiography demonstrated that MFAP5 overexpression improved cardiac function post MI, manifested as increased ejection fraction and decreased left ventricular end‐systolic volume (Figure  through ). Sirius red staining revealed reduced infarct size in MFAP5‐overexpressing mice compared with controls post MI (Figure  and ). Wall thickness in the infarct zone showed a slight increase and no significant differences were observed in collagen volume fraction, collagen quality, or α‐SMA‐positive fibroblast numbers within the infarct zone (Figure  through ).
 TUNEL staining revealed that MFAP5 overexpression reduced cardiomyocytes apoptosis in the infarct border zone of the left ventricle (Figure  and ). Consistent with this, Western blot analysis demonstrated that MFAP5 overexpression decreased levels of the apoptosis marker C‐PARP in mouse hearts (Figure  ).
These findings indicate that in vivo MFAP5 overexpression mitigates cardiomyocytes apoptosis during the acute phase of MI, concurrently reducing cardiac dysfunction and infarct size in mice. In vivo MFAP5 overexpression exerts a protective effect against AMI.

## 
MFAP5 Inhibits Cardiomyocyte Apoptosis Under Oxygen–Glucose Deprivation Conditions In Vitro
To investigate protective effects of MFAP5 in cardiomyocytes exposed to ischemic and hypoxic conditions, we induced ischemic stress and apoptosis in H9c2 cardiomyocytes through OGD. TUNEL assays indicated reduced OGD‐induced apoptosis upon MFAP5 overexpression (Figure  and ). Western blot showed that MFAP5 overexpression inhibited increases in C‐C3 (cleaved caspase‐3) and C‐PARP induced by OGD (Figure  ; Figure  ). Additionally, cell counting kit‐8 assays revealed improved cell viability under OGD upon MFAP5 overexpression (Figure  ).
 Given animal experiments indicated that MFAP5 was highly expressed and secreted extracellularly by CFs post MI, we investigated whether exogenous rMFAP5 can inhibit apoptosis in H9c2 cardiomyocytes in vitro. TUNEL assays demonstrated reduced OGD‐induced apoptosis upon rMFAP5 administration (Figure  and ), which flow cytometry analysis confirmed (Figure  ; Figure  ). Western blot revealed reduced C‐C3 and C‐PARP elevations following rMFAP5 treatment (Figure  ; Figure  ). Cell counting kit‐8 results showed that rMFAP5 improved the viability of OGD‐stimulated cardiomyocytes (Figure  ). In addition, we verified the function of rMFAP5 in isolated and cultured NRCMs. Western blot results demonstrated that rMFAP5 also inhibits apoptosis in NRCMs under OGD conditions (Figure  ). We also explored whether CF‐derived MFAP5 could inhibit H9c2 cardiomyocyte apoptosis in vitro. Conditioned medium from CFs overexpressing MFAP5 also inhibited OGD‐induced elevation of C‐C3 and C‐PARP in cardiomyocytes (Figure  ).
In summary, these results indicate that both MFAP5 overexpression in cells, exogenous administration of rMFAP5, and extracellular MFAP5 derived from fibroblasts can inhibit OGD‐induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis.

## 
RNA Sequencing, Bioinformatics Analysis, and Identification of MFAP5 Downstream Targets
Both in vivo and in vitro experiments confirmed the cardioprotective effects of MFAP5 post MI via apoptosis reduction. We then explored underlying mechanisms through RNA‐sequencing of left ventricular tissues from WT‐sham, WT‐MI, and MFAP5 KO‐MI mice. Differential gene heatmaps illustrated significant MI‐induced myocardial gene expression changes, exacerbated by MFAP5 KO (Figure  ). Canonical pathway analysis based on ingenuity pathway analysis indicated significantly upregulated ventricular dysfunction‐associated pathways in MFAP5 KO mice (Figure  ). Upstream analysis based on ingenuity pathway analysis predicted NOTCH signaling inhibition following MFAP5 KO (Figure  ). Regulator network analysis further predicted exacerbation of ventricular dysfunction by MFAP5 KO through NOTCH signaling suppression (Figure  ).
 We next analyzed NOTCH family receptor expression in cardiomyocytes. We extracted the cardiomyocyte fraction from 2 publicly available single‐cell data sets (SCP498, SCP1849) for pesudobulk differential expression analysis based on cell type annotation. The results revealed NOTCH2 as the predominant receptor subtype expressed in cardiomyocytes from normal and ischemic human hearts, and NOTCH2 expression was further elevated in disease states (Figure  and ). In contrast, NOTCH1, NOTCH3, and NOTCH4 were minimally expressed. Prior studies corroborate exclusive NOTCH2 expression in adult cardiomyocytes, with NOTCH1 in noncardiomyocytes, NOTCH3 in smooth muscle cells, and NOTCH4 in ECs. , , 

These findings suggest MFAP5 mediates cardioprotection by activating NOTCH2 signaling on cardiomyocytes, thereby reducing apoptosis and ameliorating cardiac dysfunction post MI.

## 
MFAP5 Binds NOTCH2 Receptor on Cardiomyocyte Surface and Activates NOTCH2 Signaling
To explore the effect of exogenous MFAP5 on NOTCH2 signaling in cardiomyocytes, we assessed the level of NICD2 in H9c2 cells treated with rMFAP5. Western blot demonstrated that rMFAP5 increased NICD2 levels in cardiomyocytes. Additionally, OGD stimulation elevated NICD2 expression, which was further enhanced by rMFAP5 treatment (Figure  ). RT‐qPCR analysis indicated that exogenous MFAP5 upregulated transcription of the NOTCH2 target gene Hey2 (Figure  ). We next investigated whether MFAP5 promotes NOTCH2 extracellular domain shedding and subsequent NICD2 generation through direct binding to NOTCH2. Confocal immunofluorescence showed colocalization of exogenous MFAP5 with NOTCH2 receptors on H9c2 cardiomyocyte surfaces (Figure  ). Colocalization of exogenous MFAP5 protein with NOTCH2 receptor on the surface of NRCMs was also observed when rMFAP5 was added to the culture medium of isolated NRCMs (Figure  ). Coimmunoprecipitation confirmed interaction between MFAP5 and NOTCH2 in H9c2 cells overexpressing MFAP5 (Figure  ). To further characterize NOTCH2 extracellular shedding and NICD2 generation, we constructed a full‐length NOTCH2 plasmid tagged with HA (hemagglutinin) at the N‐terminus and Myc at the C‐terminus (Figure  ). Coexpression in 293T cells and subsequent coimmunoprecipitation using Myc‐beads demonstrated binding of MFAP5 to NOTCH2. Furthermore, Myc‐beads precipitated full‐length NOTCH2 (immunoblotting: HA‐300kDa) and NICD2 (immunoblotting: Myc‐110kDa), and MFAP5 overexpression significantly enhanced NICD2 generation (Figure  ). Coimmunoprecipitation using HA‐beads from supernatant medium confirmed that MFAP5 binds extracellular NOTCH2 fragments and promotes their shedding (immunoblotting: HA‐180 kDa) (Figure  ). Collectively, these results confirm that MFAP5 binds to the extracellular domain of NOTCH2, promoting extracellular shedding and intracellular generation of NICD2, thereby activating NOTCH2 signaling.
 

## Identification of Structural Domains Mediating MFAP5 and NOTCH2 Interaction
We subsequently identified the domain of MFAP5 responsible for NOTCH2 interaction. MFAP5 contains an N‐terminal RGD (arginine‐glycine‐aspartate) sequence, mediating binding to integrin αvβ3, and a C‐terminal MBD highly homologous to MFAP2. Previous studies indicated that both MFAP5 and MFAP2 bind NOTCH1 through their conserved MBD. Given structural similarities between NOTCH1 and NOTCH2 extracellular domains, we hypothesized that MFAP5 binds and activates NOTCH2 via its MBD. We constructed His‐tagged plasmids expressing either MBD‐deleted MFAP5 (MFAP5‐ΔMBD) or the isolated MBD domain (Figure  ). Coimmunoprecipitation results of whole‐cell lysis of 293T cells overexpressing full‐length NOTCH2 and full‐length MFAP5 or its truncated plasmids showed that both full‐length MFAP5 and MBD domain interacted with NOTCH2, whereas MFAP5‐ΔMBD did not. Furthermore, only full‐length MFAP5 and the MBD increased NICD2 levels, whereas MFAP5‐ΔMBD did not (immunoblotting: Myc‐110kDa). Coimmunoprecipitation from supernatant medium showed enhanced NOTCH2 extracellular fragment shedding by full‐length MFAP5 and MBD but not by MFAP5‐ΔMBD (Figure  ). These results demonstrate that the MBD of MFAP5 mediates its interaction with NOTCH2 and promotes extracellular shedding and NICD2 generation.
 We further investigated the NOTCH2 domain required for binding to MFAP5. Because the EGF repeats are crucial ligand‐binding domains of NOTCH receptors and previous studies indicated MFAP5 interacts with the EGF repeats of NOTCH1, , we hypothesized that MFAP5 binds NOTCH2 via its EGF repeats. We constructed a truncated NOTCH2 lacking EGF repeats (NOTCH2‐ΔEGF), tagged with HA (N‐terminal) and Myc (C‐terminal) (Figure  ). Coimmunoprecipitation in 293T cells showed binding of MFAP5 to full‐length NOTCH2 but not NOTCH2‐ΔEGF. Additionally, NICD2 generation was not enhanced by MFAP5 with NOTCH2‐ΔEGF (immunoblotting: Myc‐110 kDa) (Figure  ). Confocal immunofluorescence further validated these results in H9c2 cardiomyocytes. In cells overexpressing full‐length NOTCH2, MFAP5 increased NICD2 entry into the nucleus and there was colocalization of MFAP5 with NOTCH2. However, NOTCH2‐ΔEGF prevented MFAP5 from increasing nuclear signaling of NICD2, and colocalization of MFAP5 with NOTCH2‐ΔEGF could not be observed (Figure  ). Thus, the EGF repeats of NOTCH2 are essential for MFAP5 binding and subsequent NICD2 generation.

## 
MFAP5 Inhibits Cardiomyocyte Apoptosis by Promoting NOTCH2 Signaling
To verify whether the antiapoptotic effect of MFAP5 in cardiomyocytes is mediated via NOTCH2 signaling, we knocked down NOTCH2 expression in H9c2 cells using siRNA (small interfering RNA). TUNEL staining showed that exogenous MFAP5 significantly reduced OGD‐induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis, whereas NOTCH2 knockdown abolished this protective effect (Figure  and ). Flow cytometry analysis confirmed these findings, showing that NOTCH2 knockdown blocked rMFAP5‐mediated apoptosis inhibition (Figure  and ). Western blot demonstrated that NOTCH2 knockdown reversed the inhibitory effect of MFAP5 on C‐C3 and C‐PARP levels under OGD conditions (Figure  ; Figure  ). Additionally, cell counting kit‐8 assays indicated that rMFAP5 improved cardiomyocyte viability under OGD stress, which was blocked by NOTCH2 knockdown (Figure  ). MFAP5 overexpression in H9c2 cells yielded similar results, with NOTCH2 knockdown obstructing the inhibitory effect of MFAP5 on apoptosis and its restorative effect on cardiomyocyte viability (Figure  through ). Furthermore, we assessed whether deletion of the MBD abolished the antiapoptotic effects of MFAP5. Western blot showed that loss of the MBD domain eliminated the protective effect of MFAP5 against apoptosis (Figure  and ). These findings collectively demonstrate that MFAP5 suppresses cardiomyocyte apoptosis predominantly through binding and activation of NOTCH2 signaling.
 

## DISCUSSION
ECM‐associated proteins in the heart regulate cellular homeostasis and undergo profound changes under cardiac stress, resulting in altered signaling pathways and complex intercellular communication networks that are essential for modulating injury responses. , In this study, we explored for the first time the source and expression of the ECM‐associated protein MFAP5 during MI and defined its role and underlying mechanisms in attenuating cardiac dysfunction by suppressing cardiomyocyte apoptosis via NOTCH2 signaling. We present several novel findings: (1) activated CFs highly express MFAP5 and secrete it extracellularly during MI; (2) in vivo MFAP5 KO exacerbates cardiac dysfunction and cardiomyocyte apoptosis after MI; (3) CF‐specific overexpression of MFAP5 alleviates post‐MI cardiac dysfunction and cardiomyocyte apoptosis; (4) either recombinant MFAP5 protein or direct cardiomyocyte MFAP5 overexpression inhibits cardiomyocyte apoptosis under OGD; (5) MFAP5 interacts with the EGF repeats of NOTCH2 on cardiomyocytes via its MBD, promoting extracellular shedding of NOTCH2 and the generation of NICD2, thereby activating NOTCH2 signaling; and (6) the antiapoptotic effects of MFAP5 on cardiomyocytes are mediated specifically through its interaction with NOTCH2.
Cardiomyocyte apoptosis during the acute phase of MI is a primary cause of cardiomyocyte loss and subsequent cardiac dysfunction. Cardiomyocytes in the infarct border zone stimulated by injury can undergo apoptosis within hours to days, and these apoptotic cardiomyocytes will lead to an enlargement of the infarct size and worsening of cardiac dysfunction. , , , Because cardiomyocytes are terminally differentiated cells with limited regenerative capacity, strategies to prevent cardiomyocyte apoptosis in the acute phase after MI are often beneficial. , Here, we provide the first evidence that MFAP5, produced and secreted by activated CFs after MI, exerts a paracrine protective effect against cardiomyocyte apoptosis. These findings align with studies in other systems showing that MFAP5 promotes cell proliferation and survival. In intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, MFAP5 promotes cell growth and cell cycle progression. Similarly, cancer‐associated fibroblast‐derived MFAP5 enhances proliferation and metastasis in bladder cancer, and it stimulates EC proliferation in cultured mouse brain microvascular ECs, human microvascular ECs, and human umbilical vein ECs. , Additionally, we found that overexpression of MFAP5 in vivo confers additional protective effects during the acute phase of MI, which suggests that MFAP5 may hold therapeutic potential during the acute phase of MI.
NOTCH signaling is an evolutionarily conserved pathway crucial for regulating cellular processes such as fate determination, differentiation, proliferation, apoptosis, and regeneration. Through RNA sequencing and ingenuity pathway analysis, we identified NOTCH signaling as a critical pathway through which MFAP5 exerts cardioprotective effects post MI. The NOTCH family comprises 4 receptor subtypes: NOTCH1, NOTCH2, NOTCH3, and NOTCH4. Our analysis of single‐cell RNA sequencing data indicates that NOTCH2 is the primary expressed NOTCH family receptor subtype in cardiomyocytes under both healthy and ischemic cardiomyopathy conditions. Studies have shown that mature cardiomyocytes predominantly express NOTCH2, with minimal expression of NOTCH1 and other family members. NOTCH2 is known to regulate cardiac development and cardiomyocyte proliferation. Additionally, NOTCH2 inhibits apoptosis by suppressing the formation of the caspase complex, a key executor of apoptosis. Therefore, MFAP5 may target NOTCH2 to exert an antiapoptotic effect during the acute phase of MI. Our coimmunoprecipitation and confocal microscopy results demonstrated that MFAP5 directly interacts with NOTCH2 receptors on cardiomyocyte surfaces to suppress apoptosis, and such effect was reversed upon knockdown of NOTCH2. Indeed, NOTCH receptor subtypes exhibit distinct cardiac distributions. NOTCH1 and NOTCH2 are predominant in the developing heart, though NOTCH1 expression is negligible in mature cardiomyocytes. , NOTCH3 is localized primarily in smooth muscle cells, whereas NOTCH4 is restricted to ECs. NOTCH1 signaling can mitigate myocardial fibrosis, oxidative stress, and promote revascularization. , NOTCH3 alleviates cardiac dysfunction by enhancing pericyte/progenitor recruitment, vascular maturation, and reducing fibrosis. , NOTCH4 predominantly modulates EC function. Given the cell‐specific effects of MFAP5 on NOTCH signaling, further studies should investigate whether MFAP5 modulates NOTCH1, NOTCH3, and NOTCH4 signaling in other cardiac cell types post MI.
MFAP5 contains an N‐terminal RGD motif and a C‐terminal MBD. Studies in other tissues report that MFAP5 interacts with integrins, especially the αvβ3 subtype, via its RGD motif. During bone repair, MFAP5 regulates cell adhesion, migration, and survival by binding integrin αvβ3. In ovarian cancer, MFAP5 modulates EC survival through αvβ3 integrin signaling. Current evidence indicates MFAP5 primarily interacts with integrin β3, not β1. Integrin subtypes exhibit cell‐specific distributions in cardiac tissues; cardiomyocytes predominantly express integrin β1 but not the known MFAP5 partner integrin β3. , , Consistently, our study demonstrated no colocalization of MFAP5 with integrin β1 on cardiomyocytes (Figure  ), suggesting that integrin interactions do not mediate the cardiomyocyte‐specific effects of MFAP5. However, integrin β3, although absent from cardiomyocytes, is present in other cardiac cells. CFs express integrins β1, β3, and β5, and ECs and smooth muscle cells mainly express integrins β1 and β3. Therefore, future studies should investigate whether MFAP5 modulates integrin signaling in these other cardiac cell types post MI.
This study primarily focused on the effects of MFAP5 on cardiomyocyte apoptosis in the acute phase of MI in vivo experiments. In vitro experiments, MFAP5 can directly act on cardiomyocytes and inhibit OGD‐induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis in the absence of elastic fibers and other complex microenvironments. However, we cannot exclude the influence of MFAP5 on other cell types in the heart. As an ECM‐associated protein, MFAP5 is also known to regulate elastin fiber formation in other systems, such as alveolar tissues in the respiratory system. Post‐MI cardiac remodeling involves multiple cell types beyond cardiomyocytes, including macrophage recruitment and EC‐ and smooth muscle cell‐mediated angiogenesis, which critically influence on the survival and function of cardiomyocytes. , As a fibroblast‐derived secretory protein, MFAP5 may exert paracrine or autocrine effects on these additional cell types. The present study predominantly focused on cardiomyocyte apoptosis during the acute phase of MI. In vitro experiments, in the absence of elastic fibers and other complex microenvironments, show direct antiapoptotic effects of MFAP5 on cardiomyocytes under OGD conditions. Additionally, we observed no significant impact of MFAP5 on macrophage recruitment or angiogenesis during acute‐phase MI. Thus, the cardioprotective role of MFAP5 in acute phase of MI likely arises primarily from its direct antiapoptotic action on cardiomyocytes. Future research should clarify whether, during the chronic phase post MI, MFAP5 influences ECM elasticity or exerts paracrine effects on other cardiac cells, including fibroblasts, ECs, smooth muscle cells, and immune cells. Although we did not observe significant deaths due to cardiac rupture in MFAP5 KO mice during the acute phase of MI, we also did not observe significant differences in the fraction of collagen volume, collagen quality, or the number of α‐SMA‐positive fibroblasts in the infarct zone among the groups. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that MFAP5 may influence long‐term scar healing after MI by affecting fibroblast migration or scar contraction. Future studies are needed to assess MFAP5's impact on long‐term pathological cardiac remodeling following MI. Furthermore, although we did not observe significant effects of MFAP5 on macrophage recruitment or angiogenesis during the acute phase of MI, whether MFAP5 might exert effects on other heart cells (including fibroblasts, ECs, smooth muscle cells, and immune cells) during the chronic phase of MI warrants further investigation.
Furthermore, this study has the following limitations: (1) using global germline KO mice cannot exclude the possibility that MFAP5 functions in other tissues may influence AMI; and (2) in vitro experiments were primarily conducted using H9c2 cardiomyocytes. Although H9c2 cells provide a convenient model for signal transduction studies and expression of various exogenous proteins, they cannot fully replicate the biological characteristics of adult ventricular cardiomyocytes. Conclusions drawn from H9c2 cardiomyocytes are therefore limited.

## Conclusions
In summary, this study demonstrates that during acute phase of MI, activated CFs upregulate and secrete MFAP5, which interacts with NOTCH2 receptors on cardiomyocytes, promoting extracellular domain shedding and NICD2 generation. This activation of NOTCH2 signaling inhibits cardiomyocyte apoptosis, preserving cardiac function. Therefore, targeting the MFAP5‐NOTCH2 axis represents a promising therapeutic strategy to limit infarct size, protect cardiac function, and prevent heart failure progression post MI.
