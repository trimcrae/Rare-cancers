Current therapeutic strategies remain limited, highlighting the need for exploring novel agents and their underlying mechanisms. Triptolide (TP), an active component derived from Tripterygium wilfordii, has shown anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective properties , , but its specific role in alleviating ALI and the involvement of the lung-gut axis in metabolic regulation remain poorly understood. This study aims to investigate the therapeutic effects of TP on LPS-induced ALI, focusing on its impact on pulmonary edema and inflammatory injury. By analyzing the lung-gut axis using multi-omics approaches, we seek to clarify the metabolic network regulatory mechanisms through which TP exerts its effects. LPS-induced ALI model was established in Balb/c mice, with TP administered as the therapeutic intervention. Histopathological examination of lung tissues and detection of pro-inflammatory cytokines were performed to assess lung injury. Untargeted metabolomics via LC-MS/MS was used to identify differential metabolites in lung tissues and serum, while metagenomic sequencing analyzed changes in gut microbiota composition. Integrated multi-omics analysis was applied to explore associations between gut microbiota alterations, serum metabolites, and pulmonary bile acid levels. TP administration significantly reduced histopathological damage in lung tissues of ALI mice and decreased pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. Metabolomics profiling revealed distinct changes in key metabolites, including bile acids, amino acid derivatives, and energy metabolism intermediates, in both lung tissues and serum after TP treatment. Metagenomic analysis showed that TP restructured gut microbiota composition, with functional enrichment in glycolysis and thiamine metabolism pathways. Integrated analysis confirmed strong correlations between dynamic microbiota changes, serum metabolite profiles, and pulmonary bile acid levels, indicating a regulatory role of the lung-gut axis. This study demonstrates that TP alleviates pulmonary edema and inflammatory injury in ALI by modulating gut microbial ecology and function, which drives bile acid metabolic reprogramming and regulates metabolite interactions within the lung-gut axis. These findings provide novel insights into TP’s therapeutic mechanism and support its potential application in ALI treatment.

## Introduction
ALI is a syndrome characterized by acute respiratory insufficiency resulting from impaired gas exchange, which can be induced by direct or indirect injurious factors. In its most severe progressive form, it is defined as acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) . Common etiologies of ALI include sepsis, pneumonia, toxic inhalation, severe trauma, and shock. Current international treatment strategies for ALI primarily focus on mitigating inflammatory damage and preventing respiratory failure . Although anti-inflammatory agents such as corticosteroids and aspirin are widely used in clinical practice, their significant adverse effects limit therapeutic safety and efficacy , . Mechanical ventilation, as a respiratory support modality, is primarily employed to alleviate hypoxemia in patients. However, despite the availability of various treatment approaches, the mortality rate of ALI remains high, and patients’ quality of life remains suboptimal. Therefore, the development of safe and effective therapeutic agents to improve clinical outcomes in ALI patients represents a critical challenge in current ALI management.
Both clinical and experimental evidence has demonstrated the potent anti-inflammatory efficacy of TP , . Our preliminary studies using Balb/c mice have revealed that TP significantly attenuates inflammatory responses in an LPS-induced ALI model, with promising therapeutic effects. These findings suggest that TP has potential as a novel therapeutic agent for ALI. In the present study, we aim to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which TP ameliorates ALI associated with pulmonary edema and inflammation. To achieve this objective, we employed LPS to establish an ALI model in Balb/c mice and systematically evaluated the therapeutic effects of TP. Additionally, metabolomic and metagenomic analyses were conducted to identify dynamic changes in disease-related metabolites and gut microbiota following TP administration. By integrating multi-dimensional data, this study seeks to comprehensively uncover the mechanistic basis of triptolide in ALI treatment. The anticipated findings may provide a theoretical foundation for the potential clinical application of TP as an adjunctive therapy to mitigate ALI associated with pulmonary edema, thereby offering novel insights for optimizing therapeutic strategies.

## Materials and methods

## Acute lung injury (ALI) model induction
Mice were randomly allocated into three groups with predefined sample sizes consistent with the experimental design: Control group (n = 3): Intranasal instillation of 50 µL sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS); LPS-induced ALI group (n = 8): Intranasal administration of lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Escherichia coli O55:B5, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No.: L2880; white powder) at 10 mg/kg per mouse, dissolved in 50 µL PBS; LPS + Triptolide (TP) intervention group (n = 6): Intranasal instillation of LPS (10 mg/kg per mouse, dissolved in 50 µL PBS) to establish the ALI model first. As specified in the experimental protocol, TP (MCE, Cat. No.: HY-32735; white powder) was administered via intraperitoneal injection at 66.7 µg/kg per day for 7 consecutive days, starting 24 h after LPS administration. For TP preparation, the white powder was dissolved in 2% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No.: D2650) and further diluted with PBS to the final working concentration—this solvent concentration is widely used in preclinical studies to avoid non-specific biological interference, as supported by previous literature , . A detailed schedule of LPS and TP administration is provided in a separate supplementary Word document for clarity (Table ).

 

## Sample collection and processing
All mice were anesthetized with intraperitoneal pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) and euthanized via an overdose (100 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection), with death confirmed by 5 min of absent respiratory movement and heartbeat. Post-euthanasia sample collection was completed within 10 min to prevent metabolite degradation, following standardized protocols: Blood was collected via cardiac puncture, clotted at room temperature for 30 min, then centrifuged at 3,000 × g (4 °C, 15 min) to separate serum, which was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C. For bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), 1 mL pre-cooled sterile PBS was infused into the lungs in two 500 µL aliquots; collected BALF was centrifuged at 300 × g (4 °C, 10 min) to remove cells, then twice at 3,000 × g (4 °C, 10 min) to eliminate impurities, with the supernatant aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C. The excised lung was rinsed with pre-cooled PBS: the right inferior lobe was fixed in 4% pre-cooled paraformaldehyde for histopathology, and the remaining tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 5 min before storage at − 80 °C. All centrifugation and transfers were performed on ice or in a 4 °C cold room to preserve temperature-sensitive metabolites and biomacromolecules.

## Histopathological and inflammatory cytokine analysis

## Pulmonary histopathological evaluation
Lung tissues fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde were paraffin-embedded, sectioned (5 μm), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Blinded semi-quantitative scoring was conducted by two independent pathologists following guidelines from the An Official American Thoracic Society Workshop Report: Features and Measurements of Experimental Acute Lung Injury in Animals for experimental acute lung injury (ALI). Per the report’s recommendation to account for the patchy nature of ALI, 20 random fields were evaluated per sample, with scoring focusing on five key histological features of ALI using the report’s three-tiered criteria: neutrophils in alveolar spaces (scored 0 for no neutrophils, 1 for 1–5 neutrophils per field, 2 for > 5 neutrophils per field), neutrophils in the interstitium (scored using the same 0–2 scale as alveolar neutrophils), hyaline membranes/proteinaceous debris (scored 0 for absent, 1 for focally present, 2 for diffusely present), alveolar septal thickening (scored 0 for < 2× normal thickness, 1 for 2×–4× normal thickness, 2 for > 4× normal thickness, with septa adjacent to large vessels/airways excluded to avoid artifacts as noted in the report), and alveolar structure destruction (scored 0 for no destruction, 1 for focal disruption, 2 for diffuse disruption). The total injury score was calculated using the weighted formula specified in the report: [(20×alveolar neutrophils) + (14×interstitial neutrophils) + (7×hyaline membranes/debris) + (7×septal thickening) + (2×structural destruction)]/(number of fields×100), yielding a value ranging from 0 (no injury) to 1 (severe injury). All H&E-stained images were annotated with scale bars matching actual imaging parameters (60 μm for cellular/structural detail observation and 300 μm for global tissue assessment), which adheres to the report’s requirement for transparent dimensional documentation of histological images to ensure reproducible interpretation.

## Measurement of inflammatory cytokines
Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) supernatants were analyzed using commercially available ELISA kits (Mouse IL-1β, abs520001; IL-6, abs520004; IFN-γ, abs520007 and TNF-α, abs520010; Absin, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader, and cytokine concentrations were determined via standard curves.

## Untargeted metabolomics profiling

## Sample preparation
Lung tissues: 50 mg tissue samples were homogenized in 1 mL of 80% methanol-water solution, followed by ultrasonic disruption at 4 °C with parameters set as 120 W power, 40 kHz frequency, and 30 min duration. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min (4 °C), and the supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis.
Serum: 100 µL of serum was mixed with 400 µL of ice-cold methanol, vortexed vigorously for 1 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min (4 °C). The supernatant was subjected to nitrogen blowing at 40 °C with a flow rate of 2 L/min until concentrated to approximately 50 µL, then reconstituted with 100 µL of 80% methanol-water solution for LC-MS analysis.

## LC-MS analysis
Metabolite profiling was performed using a UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Chromatographic conditions: Separation was achieved on a Waters-BEH-C18 column (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm) with a column temperature maintained at 40 °C and an injection volume of 5 µL. Mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B) for positive ion mode; for negative ion mode, mobile phase A was 100% water containing 0.1% ammonia and 10 mM ammonium acetate, and mobile phase B was 100% acetonitrile. The gradient elution program was set as follows: 0–1.0 min, 98% A/2% B (flow rate: 0.3 mL/min); 1.0–8.0 min, linear gradient from 98% A/2% B to 1% A/99% B (flow rate: 0.3 mL/min); 8.0–10.0 min, 1% A/99% B (flow rate: 0.3 mL/min); 10.0–10.1 min, linear gradient back to 98% A/2% B (flow rate: 0.3 mL/min); 10.1–12.0 min, 98% A/2% B (flow rate: 0.3 mL/min) for column re-equilibration. Mass spectrometry parameters: Ionization mode: Positive and negative electrospray ionization (ESI); Spray voltage: 3500 V (positive), 2500 V (negative); Ion transfer tube temperature: 325 °C; Evaporator temperature: 300 °C; Sheath gas pressure: 30 Arb, auxiliary gas pressure: 10 Arb, purge gas pressure: 1 Arb; RF lens intensity: 60%; Orbitrap resolution: 60,000; Scan range: m/z 70–1200.

## Data analysis
Data ProcessingRaw data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for peak detection, alignment, and annotation. Compound identification was performed by matching with the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database. Permission to use KEGG database content in this Open Access publication has been obtained from Kanehisa Laboratories (permission document submitted to srep@nature.com).

## Gut microbiome metagenomic analysis

## Library preparation and sequencing
First, fecal genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany)—a kit optimized for microbial DNA isolation from fecal samples—to ensure high yield and purity of microbial genetic material. DNA concentration and integrity were verified using Qubit (for precise quantification) and Nanodrop (for assessing A260/A280 ratio, target range: 1.8–2.0), confirming all samples met the quality threshold for library construction. 200 µg genomic DNA was randomly fragmented by FEA Enzyme to an average size of 300–350 bp. The fragments were treated with End Prep Enzyme Mix for end repairing, 5’ phosphorylation, and 3’ adenylation, to add adaptors to both ends. Size selection of adaptor-ligated DNA was then performed by DNA Cleanup beads. Each sample was then amplified by PCR for 8 cycles using P5 and P7 primers, with both primers carrying sequences which can anneal with flow cell to perform bridge PCR and P7 primer carrying a six-base index allowing for multiplexing .Qualified libraries were subjected to PE150 double-end sequencing using an Illumina Novaseq sequencer, achieving an average sequencing depth of 6.2 Gbp per sample (range: 5.1–7.7 Gbp across all 14 samples). Post-sequencing quality control confirmed high data reliability: Q20 values (proportion of bases with sequencing error rate < 0.01) were ≥ 99.5%, Q30 values (proportion of bases with sequencing error rate < 0.001) were ≥ 97.9%, and GC content ranged from 46.4% to 48.8%, ensuring comprehensive coverage of gut microbial communities.

## Data analysis
Raw sequencing data were first processed to remove host DNA interference via two sequential steps: (1) Raw reads were aligned to the Mus musculus reference genome (GRCm39) using Bowtie2 software with parameters set as --end-to-end and --sensitive to identify host-associated sequences; (2) Reads with ≥ 90% identity to the host genome were filtered out, retaining only microbial-enriched clean reads for downstream analysis.Subsequently, adaptor sequences and low-quality reads were removed (filtering criteria: average quality value < 20 in 4 bp sliding windows, or terminal bases with quality < 3). Different index-labeled DNA libraries were mixed and subjected to PE150 double-end sequencing using an Illumina Novaseq sequencer , and data analysis was completed after the data were downloaded, with reference to metagenomic data processing strategies described previously , .

## Multi-omics integration and statistical analysis

## Spearman correlation network construction
Correlations between gut microbial genera (relative abundance) and serum/lung metabolite levels were computed in R (v4.2.1) using Spearman’s rank test (thresholds: |r| >0.6, p < 0.05). Networks were visualized in Cytoscape 3.9.1, where nodes represented microbial taxa and metabolites.

## Statistical methods
Data are presented as the mean ± SD. Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA or Kruskal - Wallis test (non-normal data) with Bonferroni correction for multiple testing. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism 9.0 and SPSS 26.0.

## Results

## Effects of triptolide on pulmonary histopathology
Histopathological assessment of lung tissues stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) revealed distinct morphological alterations among the experimental groups (Fig.  _a). In the LPS-induced ALI group, severe architectural distortion in the alveolar spaces was observed, accompanied by extensive inflammatory cell infiltration and pronounced pulmonary edema. In contrast, mice treated with TP exhibited markedly preserved alveolar integrity, significantly attenuated inflammatory cell accumulation, and substantially reduced tissue edema compared with the untreated ALI group. Blinded histopathological scoring based on the Szapiel criteria demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in lung injury scores following TP administration (p < 0.05). These findings indicate that triptolide effectively mitigates LPS-induced pulmonary damage by preserving alveolar structure and ameliorating pathological manifestations of lung injury. To further characterize local inflammatory cell infiltration in lung tissues, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed targeting specific cellular markers (Fig.  _c): CD3 for T lymphocytes, CD20 for B lymphocytes, CD68 for macrophages, and Vimentin for interstitial cells. Visual and semi-quantitative analysis of IHC results showed that the LPS group exhibited notably increased positive staining signals for all four markers compared with the Control group, indicating enhanced local infiltration of inflammatory cells and activated interstitial cells in the lung. In contrast, TP treatment clearly reduced the positive staining signals of these markers relative to the LPS group—confirming that TP can suppress the migration and aggregation of inflammatory cells to the lung tissue. This finding further supports TP’s protective effect against LPS-induced pulmonary pathological damage.

## Modulatory effects of triptolide on pulmonary cytokine expression
Using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), we systematically evaluated the regulatory effects of TP on key inflammatory mediators in an LPS-induced ALI model (Fig. 1_b). Quantitative analysis revealed that TP treatment significantly attenuated the expression levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6,TNF-α,KC/GRO, IL-10, compared to the LPS-challenged ALI group. These findings demonstrate that TP effectively suppresses the excessive secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, thereby interrupting the cascade amplification of pulmonary inflammatory signaling and alleviating the overall inflammatory burden in lung tissues. The observed cytokine profile suggests that TP may exert its therapeutic effects through targeted modulation of the inflammatory response pathway.

 

## Pulmonary metabolomic
To elucidate the mechanistic basis of TP as an adjunctive therapeutic agent against LPS-induced ALI, we performed untargeted metabolomic analysis of lung tissues from LPS-challenged and TP-treated (LPS + TP) mice using LC-MS/MS technology. To obtain reliable and high-quality metabolomics data, we performed quality control (QC) on the samples. The results indicated that our samples exhibited stability and high data quality (Fig.  _a). Using supervised pattern recognition methods, specifically partial least squares-discriminant analysis, we identified differences between groups and differential compounds. The results showed a clear separation between the samples from the LPS acute lung injury group and those from the TP intervention group (LPS + TP), indicating significant metabolic reprogramming following TP intervention with high analytical reproducibility (Fig.  _b). In positive ionization mode, we identified 445 metabolites, with 7 differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) meeting our selection criteria (VIP ≥ 1 and p ≤ 0.05) (Table  ). These DEMs encompassed vitamins, bile acids, and amino acid derivatives (Fig.  _c). Hierarchical cluster analysis distinctly illustrated differential metabolic signatures between experimental groups (Fig.  _d), where metabolite up-regulation (fold change > 1) and down-regulation (fold change < 1) were color-coded in orange and purple, respectively. In negative ion mode (NEG), seven significantly altered metabolites including Allocholic acid (Fold Change = 0.15, p < 0.01), Cholic acid(Fold Change = 0.15, p < 0.01), Dehydroascorbic acid(Fold Change = 1.38, p < 0.05), Ureidopropionase-3-ribonucleotide(Fold Change = 0.15, p < 0.05), Ursolic acid(Fold Change = 0.15, p < 0.01), α-Muricholic acid(Fold Change = 0.15, p < 0.01), and β-Muricholic acid(Fold Change = 0.15, p < 0.01), were identified. Most of them are concentrated in bile acids and nucleotide derivatives, and so on. When bile acids are abnormally accumulated, they can activate the inflammatory response through various pathways. Meanwhile, the correlations of metabolites reveal the synergistic changes among metabolites.

 

 

 

## Serum metabolomic
To elucidate the specific mechanisms underlying TP’s protective effects against ALI secondary to pulmonary edema, we conducted comprehensive untargeted metabolomic profiling of serum samples from two randomly assigned groups using LC-MS/MS technology. Principal component analysis (PCA) demonstrated clear separation between the LPS-induced ALI group and the TP intervention group (LPS + TP), indicating significant TP-induced metabolic alterations with high analytical stability (Fig. _a). A total of 210 metabolites were identified in serum samples through untargeted metabolomic analysis. Applying stringent selection criteria (VIP ≥ 1 and p ≤ 0.05), we detected 41 significantly altered metabolites in negative ion mode (NEG) (Table ). These differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs) were classified into several major biochemical categories: Amino acids and their derivatives, Fatty acids and related compounds, Carbohydrates, Alcohols and alcohol derivatives, Bile acids and associated metabolites. Cluster heatmap analysis confirmed pronounced metabolic divergence between experimental groups, with upregulated (fold change > 1, orange) and downregulated (fold change < 1, purple) metabolites demonstrating systematic variation. Among the 41 serum differential metabolites (DEMs), 13 exhibited significant upregulation while 28 showed downregulation (Fig. _b). KEGG pathway , enrichment analysis of differential metabolites identified the top 25 significantly altered metabolic pathways (p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. _c). Notably, serum metabolomic profiling demonstrated particularly robust enrichment in three critical pathways: purine metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism, and sphingolipid metabolism, Subsequent metabolite-metabolite interaction analysis (Pearson correlation, p < 0.05) demonstrated significant co-regulation patterns, suggesting tight metabolic network coordination in response to the experimental intervention (Fig. _d).

 

## Fecal metagenomics
To elucidate the specific mechanisms by which triptolide serves as an adjunctive therapy in the prevention of acute lung injury induced by pulmonary edema, we conducted a metagenomic analysis of fecal samples using 16 S rRNA sequencing to assess the gut microbiota composition. Based on principal component analysis (PCA) and multidimensional scaling (MDS) using Bray-Curtis distances, a significant separation in gut microbiota was observed between the LPS-induced ALI group and the triptolide intervention group (LPS + TP) (Fig.  _a). In the analysis of α- and β-diversity, the Shannon and Simpson indices were employed to assess the complexity of the bacterial communities. As illustrated, there were notable differences in both the Shannon and Simpson indices between the two groups, indicating that TP significantly reduced the species diversity of gut microbiota in the context of ALI (Fig.  _b). Composition analysis revealed that LPS significantly altered the species composition of the gut microbiota at the species level in mice with ALI (Fig.  _c). Moreover, TP exhibited a modulatory effect on the species composition of the gut microbiota in ALI mice, indicating its potential role in reshaping the gut microbial community. To further investigate the functional differences in microbial communities among the various groups, we employed the Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) method, with an original LDA score > 4 set as the threshold for identifying significantly differential functional pathways (LDA scores were log10-transformed for optimized visualization, and all displayed pathways met the original LDA score > 4 criterion) (Fig.  _d). The results indicated that TP significantly altered the functional profiles of the gut microbiota in mice with ALI. Notably, these changes were concentrated in pathways related to glycolysis VI, cis-vaccenate biosynthesis, polyunsaturated fatty acid synthesis, thiamine salvage II, as well as the synthesis of L-glutamate and L-glutamine, and γ-glutamyl synthesis.

 

## Macrogenomic and serum metabolomics
Serum plays a pivotal role in the complex physiological system, particularly in mediating pulmonary function regulation . As the primary circulating biological fluid, serum serves as a critical carrier of metabolic information and substances essential for systemic homeostasis . To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the lung-gut axis, we performed integrated analysis of serum metabolomics and gut metagenomics data, providing complementary insights into the metabolic and molecular mechanisms associated with physiological and pathological phenotypes. We conducted Spearman correlation analysis between serum differential metabolites and microbial taxa (Fig. _a). The results demonstrated significant associations between gut microbiome compositional changes and serum metabolic alterations. Significant correlations were observed between beneficial microbial taxa and distinct serum metabolites.
For example, g__Parasutterella exhibited strong positive correlations with the serum metabolites pseudouridine (r = 0.48, p < 0.05) and xanthurenic acid (r = 0.74, p < 0.01), while showing a significant negative correlation with S1P(d18:1) (r = −0.73, p < 0.01); g__Bifidobacterium displayed positive correlations with serum Uridine (r = 0.62, p < 0.05) and pseudouridine (r = 0.50, p < 0.05); Conversely, potentially pathogenic bacterial for example, the genus Dorea demonstrated a significant negative correlation with 1,3,7-trimethyluric acid(r = −0.76, p < 0.01). TP administration induced marked alterations in the relative abundance of both beneficial and pathogenic gut microbiota. These microbial compositional changes suggest their potential involvement in multiple metabolic processes, including the biosynthesis, biotransformation, and catabolism of serum-derived metabolites.

## Serum metabolome and lung metabolomics
To elucidate the mechanistic link between TP and lung injury induced by acute pulmonary edema through the gut-lung axis, we performed Spearman correlation analysis between pulmonary and serum metabolic profiles (Fig.  _b). Only correlations with a correlation coefficient (r) exceeding 0.5 were selected for subsequent visualization and biological interpretation. Our findings demonstrate a strong correlation between differentially expressed pulmonary metabolites and serum metabolites. Notably, the serum metabolite pseudouridine showed significant positive correlations (p < 0.05, |r| > 0.76) with multiple bile acid derivatives in pulmonary metabolomics, including beta-muricholic acid (r = 0.77, p < 0.01), alpha-muricholic acid (r = 0.77, p < 0.01), ursocholic acid (r = 0.76, p < 0.01), allocholic acid (r = 0.77, p < 0.1), cholic acid (r = 0.77, p < 0.01) and related compounds. Conversely, serum sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P(d18:1)) exhibited marked negative correlations (p < 0.05, |r| > 0.5) with these pulmonary bile acids. These results provide compelling evidence for a robust interconnection between serum and pulmonary metabolic networks, suggesting that these specific metabolites may serve as critical mediators in the metabolic regulation of lung injury pathogenesis.

 

## Discussions
This study employed an LPS-induced model of ALI coupled in Balb/c mice with multi-omics analysis to systematically investigate the mechanism by which TP ameliorates pulmonary edema-associated lung injury through metabolic network regulation via the gut-lung axis. Experimental results demonstrated that TP significantly attenuated LPS-induced histopathological damage in lung tissues and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, consistent with previously reported anti-inflammatory properties of TP – . Furthermore, untargeted metabolomics analysis identified key differential metabolites in both lung tissues and serum, including bile acids (allocholic acid, cholic acid, ursocholic acid, α-muricholic acid, β-muricholicacid, and others), amino acid derivatives (pseudouridine and others), lipidmediators (S1P(d18:1) and others), and energy metabolism intermediates (D-erythro-3-methylmalate and others). These findings provide novel insights into the metabolic alterations underlying TP-mediated protection in ALI. TP’s regulatory effect on gut microbiota and metabolic networks aligns with innovative strategies targeting ‘undruggable’ targets, such as the upregulation of PTPRO via siRNA-encapsulated antibody-conjugated nanoparticles to reverse drug resistance . Both approaches highlight the potential of targeting recalcitrant biological pathways.
TP treatment significantly reversed the abnormal accumulation of bile acid derivatives in pulmonary differentially expressed metabolites, suggesting a potential regulatory effect on gut microbiota functions. Notably, this reversal of pulmonary bile acid accumulation holds critical relevance for ALI pathogenesis, as accumulating evidence in sepsis-associated ALI/ARDS has highlighted that bile acid accumulation can exert direct deleterious effects on lung tissue: specifically, it inhibits the synthesis of pulmonary surfactant, a key lipid-protein complex essential for maintaining alveolar integrity and normal gas exchange. The reduction in pulmonary surfactant further induces alveolar collapse and impairs oxygenation, ultimately exacerbating pulmonary structural damage and inflammatory responses , Metagenomic analysis revealed that TP substantially remodeled the gut microbial structure in ALI mice, notably altering the relative abundances of probiotics and pathogenic bacteria. Functional enrichment analysis demonstrated that these microbiota changes were predominantly associated with metabolic pathways including glycolysis and thiamine metabolism. Integrated multi-omics analysis further uncovered significant correlations between dynamic gut microbiota alterations and serum differential metabolites, as well as between serum metabolites and pulmonary bile acid levels. Spearman correlation analysis identified pseudouridine and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) as the metabolites most strongly associated with pulmonary bile acids. Notably, serum pseudouridine exhibited significant positive correlations (p < 0.05, |r| > 0.48) with multiple bile acid derivatives in lung tissues, including β-muricholic acid, α-muricholic acid, ursocholic acid, allocholic acid, cholic acid, and others. Conversely, serum sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P(d18:1)) displayed pronounced negative correlations (p < 0.05, |r| > 0.6) with these pulmonary bile acid metabolites, indicating their potential functional interplay in TP-mediated metabolic regulation of lung injury. Of particular significance, pseudouridine is a significantly downregulated serum metabolite following TP intervention—demonstrating strong correlations with specific gut microbiota populations. Correlation analysis revealed a significant positive association between g.Bifidobacterium and pseudouridine levels (p < 0.05, |r| > 0.5), while g.Clostridium exhibited a marked negative correlation (p < 0.05, |r| > 0.6). Bifidobacterium is a well-characterized probiotic genus, mediating intestinal homeostasis through multiple mechanisms: epithelial cell adhesion, antimicrobial peptide secretion, mucin layer thickening, and immunomodulation to attenuate inflammatory responses . Notably, Clostridium species—conditional pathogens—exacerbate intestinal and systemic inflammation through the production of toxins and pro-inflammatory mediators . Pseudouridine, a modified nucleoside commonly found in RNA molecules, serves as a biomarker of systemic oxidative stress during pathological conditions. Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation triggers RNA oxidative damage, leading to pseudouridine release into circulation through RNA degradation . Mechanistically, PUS family enzymes catalyze the incorporation of pseudouridine-modified nucleotides into mRNA, thereby inhibiting PKR activation . PKR functions as a critical inflammasome regulator that directly interacts with multiple inflammasomes including NLRP3, NLRC1, and AIM2, orchestrating their activation and subsequent inflammatory cascades – . The TP-induced reduction in serum pseudouridine suggests a potential therapeutic mechanism: gut microbiota remodeling may enhance PUS enzyme-mediated pseudouridine incorporation into mRNA, thereby suppressing PKR-dependent inflammasome activation and mitigating lung injury progression. Furthermore, sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P(d18:1)) is a significantly upregulated serum metabolite following TP intervention, demonstrating robust correlations with two bacterial taxa: g.Parasutterella and g.Helicobacter. Among the bacterial taxa, g.Parasutterella was negatively correlated with S1P (d18:1) (p < 0.05, |r| > 0.7) and g. Helicobacter was positively correlated with S1P (d18:1) (p < 0.05, |r| > 0.6). Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) is a lipid signaling molecule that regulates immune cell migration, angiogenesis, and inflammatory responses by binding to its receptors (S1PR1-5) . Research has shown that S1P can modulate endothelial cell function and influence vascular permeability . During lung injury, S1P may reduce inflammation-related exudation and mitigate damage to lung tissue by regulating vascular permeability. In this study, the observed elevation in serum S1P levels was correlated with a decrease in pulmonary bile acid levels, suggesting that TP may activate the S1P-S1PR2 signaling pathway by upregulating serum S1P levels. This pathway has been shown to enhance the expression of the bile salt export pump (BSEP), thereby promoting the fecal excretion of bile acids , . The gut microbiota can convert bile acids into forms that strongly activate FXR, signaling the body to reduce bile secretion, while FXR acts as an upstream regulator that modulates the baseline levels of S1P , potentially promoting S1P production. This aligns with our findings, as the abnormal accumulation of bile acids can activate inflammatory responses through multiple pathways, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which exacerbate the inflammatory state . In this study, TP reduced the accumulation of bile acids in the body and maintained homeostasis, thereby alleviating pulmonary inflammation. TP may regulate pseudouridine and S1P metabolism by promoting the proliferation of beneficial bacteria (such as g. Bifidobacterium and g.Parasutterella) and inhibiting the growth of harmful bacteria (such as g. Clostridium and g. Helicobacter), ultimately impacting on the reprogramming of lung bile acid metabolism and mitigating inflammation-induced damage caused by pulmonary edema.

## Conclusions
In our study, the relationship between the gut-lung axis and the protective effects of ALI was examined by applying comprehensive metagenomic sequencing techniques and metabolomics to lung, serum, and fecal samples. The primary findings of this study are as follows: TP demonstrated significant protective effects against LPS-induced acute lung injury in mice, notably altering the pathological structure of lung tissue and reducing levels of inflammatory factors in the lungs. Furthermore, non-targeted metabolomic analysis revealed differential metabolites between the LPS and TP groups, indicating that TP significantly regulates metabolic changes in lung tissue and serum associated with acute pulmonary edema. The differential metabolites in lung tissue were primarily bile acids, including beta-Muricholic acid, alpha-Muricholic acid, Ursocholic acid, Allocholic acid, and Cholic acid. Excess bile acids can activate immune cells in the liver and lungs, triggering a cytokine storm mediated by nuclear factor NF-κB and compromising endothelial barrier function, a process that TP may reverse . In terms of serum differential metabolites, the analysis identified a range of compounds, including amino acids and their derivatives, fatty acids and their derivatives, carbohydrates, alcohols and their derivatives, as well as bile acids. Additionally, 16 S rRNA sequencing analysis revealed differences in microbial communities between the LPS model group and the TP treatment group, suggesting that TP significantly modulates changes in the gut microbiota associated with lung injury caused by acute pulmonary edema, leading to alterations in microbial richness. The abundance of beneficial bacteria (such as g. Bifidobacterium and g. Parasutterella) increased significantly, while the proliferation of potential pathogenic bacteria (such as g. Clostridium and g. Helicobacter) was suppressed. Lastly, in our multi-omics analysis, we performed Spearman correlation analysis between the 16 S rRNA metagenomic sequencing data and the serum metabolome, as well as between the serum metabolome and the lung metabolome. Notably, dynamic changes in the structure of the gut microbiota were closely correlated with the levels of a series of endogenous differential metabolites in serum, suggesting that the gut microbiota may participate in the synthesis, transformation, and degradation of serum metabolites. In this integrated analysis, we found that gut probiotics (such as g. Bifidobacterium and g. Parasutterella) and pathogenic bacteria (such as g. Helicobacter) exhibited the strongest correlation with the serum differential metabolites Pseudouridine and S1P.
Collectively, these findings demonstrate that TP may exert its therapeutic effects through gut microbiota modulation, subsequently driving metabolic reprogramming of bile acids via pseudouridine and S1P-mediated pathways. This microbiota-metabolite crosstalk appears to reshape the interaction network of key metabolites within the gut-lung axis, ultimately restoring metabolic homeostasis and attenuating pulmonary edema and inflammatory injury. Our study provides novel multi-omics evidence supporting TP’s potential as a therapeutic agent for ALI, while significantly advancing mechanistic understanding of metabolic regulation within the gut-lung axis.

## Study limitations
While this study clarifies the mechanism by which TP ameliorates LPS-induced ALI via gut-lung axis-mediated regulation of bile acid metabolism and gut microbiota, several limitations should be noted to provide a comprehensive interpretation of the findings. First, in terms of animal model selection, the experiment exclusively used male 6-week-old Balb/c mice. Male mice were chosen to avoid confounding effects from female hormonal cycle fluctuations on gut microbiota composition, bile acid metabolism, and immune responses—ensuring the stability of ALI-related endpoints. However, given reported sex differences in LPS-induced lung injury severity, cytokine profiles, and gut-lung axis homeostasis, this design may restrict the generalizability of results to female populations. Second, metabolomic and metagenomic analyses focused on the LPS and LPS + TP groups, omitting the control group. This approach was intended to prioritize characterizing TP’s ability to reverse LPS-induced metabolic and microbiota dysbiosis, but the lack of a healthy baseline limits the capacity to quantitatively distinguish between LPS-driven pathological changes and TP-specific regulatory effects. Additionally, while correlations were observed between increased abundances of beneficial gut bacteria and TP-mediated ALI alleviation, the causal role of these microbiota was not validated. These limitations do not undermine the core conclusion that TP improves ALI via the gut-lung axis, but instead point to directions for future research to further refine and extend the study’s findings.
