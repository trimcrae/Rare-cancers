## Abstract
Osteoporosis, the most prevalent skeletal disorder, is primarily driven by aberrantly increased osteoclast formation and/or activity. Targeting hyperactive osteoclasts remains the cornerstone of current therapeutic strategies. Crebanine (Cre), a natural isoquinoline-derived alkaloid with diverse pharmacological activities, has not yet been explored for osteoporosis treatment. This study aimed to evaluate the therapeutic potential of Cre against ovariectomy (OVX)-induced osteoporosis and elucidate its underlying mechanisms. Cre dose-dependently inhibited in vitro osteoclast differentiation, actin ring formation, and bone resorption by downregulating nuclear factor of activated T cells 1 (NFATc1) and key osteoclast-related genes. Simultaneously, Cre enhanced osteoblast differentiation and mineralization, upregulated osteoblast marker genes, and restored hydrogen peroxide-impaired alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity impaired by hydrogen peroxide, indicating dual regulation of bone remodeling. Mechanistically, Cre activated sirtuin 1 (Sirt1), promoting p65 deacetylation, inactivated IκB kinase (IKK), and stabilized IκBα, thus inhibiting nuclear factor-kappaB (NF-κB) signaling. Additionally, Cre reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) by upregulating antioxidant enzymes (heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), catalase) and suppressing nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) phosphate (NADPH) oxidases (NOX1/4). Furthermore, Cre specifically bound to the predicted site of receptor activator of NF-κB (RANK), blocking RANK ligand (RANKL)-RANK interaction and disrupting downstream protein kinase B (Akt) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathways. In the OVX mouse model, Cre significantly attenuated bone loss and osteoclastogenesis. Crucially, Cre showed no toxicity in liver or kidney function tests. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Cre exerts dual therapeutic effects, inhibiting osteoclastogenesis via Sirt1-mediated NF-κB/ROS suppression and promoting osteoblast activity, providing a promising therapeutic strategy for osteoporosis.
Keywords: Crebanine, Osteoporosis, Osteoclast, Sirt1, Acetylation, Reactive oxygen species

## Introduction
The maintenance of skeletal homeostasis relies on tightly regulated interactions between osteoclasts, responsible for bone matrix resorption, and osteoblasts, engaged in bone matrix synthesis [ ]. An imbalance in this remodeling process, often driven by excessive osteoclast activation or disproportionate bone resorption, promotes the development of osteolytic conditions, including osteoporosis, Paget disease, arthritis-associated skeletal inflammation, and bone metastases [ , , ]. Osteoporosis, marked by reduced bone mineral density (BMD), microstructural degradation, and increased susceptibility to fractures, is a widespread yet often asymptomatic condition of skeletal fragility [ ]. This estrogen-deficiency-related pathology predominantly affects postmenopausal women and represents a significant global public health concern [ ]. Although several therapeutic agents targeting bone formation or resorption have emerged in clinical practice, their limitations, such as suboptimal long-term outcomes and adverse effects, highlight the need for novel, safer therapeutic strategies [ ].
Osteoclasts, which are multinucleated cells that play a crucial role in the process of bone resorption, derive from hematopoietic progenitor cells through pathways that are influenced by receptor activator of nuclear factor-kappaB (NF-κB) (RANK) ligand (RANKL) [ ]. Their fusion and maturation are fundamentally dependent on the synergistic interactions of cytokines, especially macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) and RANKL [ ]. M-CSF specifically promotes the proliferation of osteoclast progenitors and increases the expression of RANK, establishing essential molecular conditions for osteoclast differentiation [ ]. The interaction of RANKL with its specific receptor RANK initiates the recruitment of various adapter proteins and enzymes, such as tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP). This process activates three keys downstream signaling pathways: NF-κB, phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt), and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) [ , ]. The intricate signaling pathways involved play a crucial role in the differentiation of osteoclasts and the resorption of bone matrix. This process is mediated by transcription factors (TFs) like c-Fos and nuclear factor of activated T cells 1 (NFATc1), which are responsible for regulating the expression of genes vital for the functionality of osteoclasts [ , ]. Essential markers of osteoclasts are influenced by these transcriptional events, including Acp5 (encoding TRAP), cathepsin K (Ctsk), and matrix metallopeptidase 9 (Mmp9), all of which play a significant role in the maturation of osteoclasts and their bone resorption activity [ , ]. Identification of these regulatory networks offers promising therapeutic targets for treating disorders associated with excessive skeletal resorption.
Previous research primarily targeted the IκB kinase (IKK) catalytic complex to inhibit IκBα phosphorylation and impair NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation, effectively suppressing RANKL-induced signaling [ ]. In addition to phosphorylation-dependent mechanisms, NF-κB transcriptional activity is precisely modulated by post-translational modifications, notably lysine acetylation at specific residues (e.g., p65 K310), affecting protein stability and DNA binding [ ]. RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis involves increased expression of histone acetyltransferase (HAT) p300, facilitating p65 K310 acetylation to enhance transcriptional activity and inflammatory mediator production [ ]. Conversely, sirtuin 1 (Sirt1), an NAD+-dependent deacetylase involved in aging, inflammation, and skeletal homeostasis, counteracts this process [ ]. Osteoclast-specific Sirt1 deficiency exacerbates bone resorption due to increased osteoclast activity [ ]. Additionally, RANKL stimulation elevates reactive oxygen species (ROS) in osteoclast precursors, creating a redox environment favorable for differentiation [ ]. Thus, pharmacological modulation of Sirt1 may present therapeutic benefits by reducing oxidative stress and regulating bone remodeling [ ].
Botanical compounds are increasingly recognized as valuable therapeutic candidates due to their diverse biological activities [ , ]. The genus Stephania contains numerous alkaloid-rich species with considerable medicinal potential [ ]. Crebanine (Cre), an aporphine alkaloid isolated from Stephania venosa rhizomes, has demonstrated a range of biological activities [ ]. Recent evidence suggests Cre possesses cardioprotective, neuroprotective, and antineoplastic effects through inhibition of tumor cell proliferation and invasion [ , , ]. Although Cre exhibits significant antioxidative and anti-inflammatory properties, its therapeutic potential in postmenopausal osteoporosis remains inadequately explored.
This study aimed to assess Cre's modulatory effects on RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis and bone loss associated with estrogen deficiency. Experimental results demonstrated that Cre-mediated upregulation of Sirt1 promoted p65 deacetylation and IκBα stabilization, effectively attenuating NF-κB signaling and reducing redox imbalance.

## Materials and methods
2.1.

## Reagents and antibodies
Cre (C20H21NO4, purity ≥ 99.54%, HY-N2255) and alendronate (Aln) (C4H12NNaO7P2, purity ≥ 98.0%, HY-108685) were commercially sourced from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). H2O2 was provided by Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Cell culture media (alpha minimal essential medium (α-MEM)) and penicillin-streptomycin solutions supplied by Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was procured through Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Recombinant murine cytokines (RANKL/M-CSF) came from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA). The antibodies employed for immunoblotting procedures were acquired from various suppliers: antibodies against ATPase H+ transporting V0 subunit D2 (V-ATPase-d2), CTSK, and NFATc1 were from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Santa Cruz, CA, USA); antibodies recognizing phospho-IKKα/β (Ser176/180), IKKβ, p65, acetyl-p65 (Lys310), acetylated lysine, p-Akt, total Akt, phosphorylated c-Jun N-terminal kinase (p-JNK), total JNK, phosphorylated extracellular signal-regulated kinase (p-ERK), total ERK, phosphorylated p38 (p-p38), and total p38 were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Beverly, MA, USA); Proteintech Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) supplied antibodies specific for catalase, heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1), integrin-β3, c-Fos, β-actin, and Lamin B; antibodies against Sirt1 and IκBα were sourced from Abcam (Cambridge, UK); antibodies detecting nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) phosphate (NADPH) oxidase 1 (NOX1), NOX4, osteopontin (OPN), collagen type I alpha 1 (COL1A1), runt-related TF 2 (RUNX2), and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6) were obtained from Affinity Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China).
The TRAP detection kits used in the experiments were provided by Sigma-Aldrich, while probes for visualizing F-actin structures were sourced from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.. Additionally, subcellular fractionation required NE-PER™ reagents, which were also obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kits utilized for determining protein concentrations were procured from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Furthermore, biochemical assay kits for measuring alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (CR), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) were obtained from Juancheng Bioengineering Institute (Suzhou, China).

2.2.

## Cell culture and osteoclast differentiation
Primary bone marrow macrophages (BMMs) were harvested from the tibiae and femurs of six-week-old C57BL/6 mice. After isolation, cells were maintained in complete α-MEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, antibiotics (penicillin-streptomycin, 1%), and 50 ng/mL M-CSF. Cultivation took place at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO2 and high humidity. When cell density approached roughly 90%, fresh medium was replenished every two days, and the cells were passaged accordingly. For osteoclast differentiation assays, BMMs were initially seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well in 96-well plates and grown in medium containing 30 ng/mL M-CSF. Following initial culture, differentiation into osteoclasts was induced by prolonged treatment with M-CSF (30 ng/mL), RANKL (50 ng/mL), and varying Cre concentrations ranging from 0.5 to 5 μM, or with an equal volume of vehicle as a control. Until multinucleated osteoclasts emerged, culture medium were changed every 48 h.

2.3.

## Cytotoxicity assay
Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assays were employed to determine potential cytotoxicity of Cre on BMMs. Briefly, BMMs were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well onto 96-well plates and cultured for 48 h. The cells were then incubated in the presence of M-CSF (30 ng/mL) with graded concentrations of Cre (0.5–5 μM). After exposure, 10% (v/v) CCK-8 solution diluted in phenol red-free medium was added, and incubation continued for another 2 h at 37 °C. Finally, absorbance values at 450 nm were measured using a multimode microplate reader.

2.4.

## TRAP, F-actin, and bone-resorption assay
Using a commercial detection kit from Sigma-Aldrich, cellular specimens were stained with TRAP after being rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (Biosharp, Shanghai, China). Light microscopy was used to quantify mature osteoclastic cells, which are multinucleated cells with three or more nuclei that display TRAP enzymatic activity. The differentiation of osteoclasts was analyzed cytoskeletally according to established fixation techniques [ ]. The cell membranes were first blocked with 4% bovine serum albumin (BSA), after which they were permeabilized for 15 min with 0.1% Triton X-100. TRITC-phalloidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) was used to label actin cytoskeletal structures for 1 h in the samples, while 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) was used to counterstain the nuclei. For quantitative examination of F-actin organization, morphometric measurement was carried out using U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH) ImageJ software, and fluorescence imaging was carried out using an Olympus IX83 microscope equipment.
Sterilized bovine-derived osseous substrates were aseptically distributed across 96-well culture plates prior to experimental use. BMMs were inoculated onto these mineralized matrices (1 × 104 cells/well). Osteoclastic differentiation was induced through continuous exposure to recombinant murine M-CSF (30 ng/mL) and RANKL (50 ng/mL) over a 5–7 days culture period. Following successful osteoclastic maturation, therapeutic intervention was implemented via administration of Cre (0, 2.5, or 5 μM) in complete medium for an additional 4–5 days incubation cycle. Post-treatment specimens were processed for ultrastructural analysis, involving careful substrate retrieval, critical point drying, and high-resolution imaging via field emission scanning electron microscopy (Hitachi SU8020, Hitachi High-Tech Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Quantitative assessment of resorptive activity was achieved through computational analysis of excavation pit surface areas utilizing NIH ImageJ software (v1.53t) with standardized threshold parameters.

2.5.

## Luciferase assay
RAW264.7 macrophage cells obtained from China's National Collection of Authenticated Cell Cultures were genetically engineered by introducing reporter plasmids expressing luciferase driven by NF-κB or NFATc1 promoters, enabling the measurement of transcriptional activity. These transfected macrophages were plated at densities adjusted specifically for each signaling pathway: 1.5 × 105 cells per well were used for NF-κB assessments, while a reduced density of 5 × 104 cells per well was employed for NFATc1 analyses. Before stimulation, cells were incubated for 60 min with increasing concentrations of Cre (0, 0.5, 1, 2.5, and 5 μM). Subsequently, to induce transcriptional activation, macrophages received 50 ng/mL RANKL treatment, i.e., NF-κB pathway activation lasted 6 h, whereas stimulation targeting NFATc1 required 24 h. Finally, luciferase activity was quantified using a detection assay provided by Promega Corporation (Madison, WI, USA).

2.6.

## Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)
Cre (5 μM) or the corresponding vehicle control (0.1% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)) was added to RAW264.7 macrophages for an incubation duration of 12 h. After treatment, ice-cold PBS was carefully employed to wash cells, followed by cell disruption using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with a cocktail of protease inhibitors. Cell extracts were distributed into aliquots, stabilized by placement on ice, and then subjected to incremental heating steps from 37 up to 65 °C at intervals of 4 °C, holding each temperature stable for 3 min using a thermal cycler with programmable settings. Selected aliquots were further subjected to repeated freeze-thaw cycles (three times) by alternating between liquid nitrogen and room temperature (25 °C) to achieve complete protein denaturation. Following centrifugation (4 °C, 20 min, 20,000 g), clear supernatants were mixed with Laemmli loading buffer, heated for 5 min at 95 °C, and subsequently separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). After transferring proteins onto membranes, immunoblotting was performed employing primary antibodies against Sirt1 at a 1:1000 dilution.

2.7.

## Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay
For SPR analysis, Biacore 8K equipment (GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ, USA) was used. Using traditional amine coupling techniques in a PBS buffer (pH 7.2–7.4) with 1% DMSO (BR100672, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA), a Sirt1 protein solution (20 μg/mL, produced in a pH 4.5 buffer) was attached to an S-series sensor chip (GE Healthcare). At 25 °C and a flow rate of 20 μL/min for 100 s, the binding interactions between immobilized Sirt1 and different concentrations of Cre were investigated. The binding data was adjusted by deducting signals measured from the surface of an uncoated reference chip. Using Biacore 8K Manager software (GE Healthcare), equilibrium dissociation constants (KD = 10.6 μM) and kinetic interaction models were computed and analyzed.

2.8.

## Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)
BMMs were seeded at 1 × 105 cells per well in six-well culture plates and cultured under conditions promoting osteoclastogenesis, supplemented with RANKL (50 ng/mL) in the presence of Cre (2.5 or 5 μM) for 6 days. After osteoclast induction for 6 days or osteoblast differentiation over a period of 7 days, total cellular RNA extraction was conducted with TRIzol™ reagent (Beyotime Biotechnology). One microgram of isolated RNA was reverse-transcribed to complementary DNA (cDNA). Subsequent qRT-PCR analysis employed SYBR Green master mix (Imgenex, San Diego, CA, USA) and murine-specific primers as follows: Acp5 (Forward: 5′-TGTGGCCATCTTTATGGT-3’; Reverse: 5′-GTCATTTCTTTGGGGCTT-3′); CTSK (forward, 5′-GGGAGAAAAACCTGAAGC-3’; reverse, 5′-ATTCTGGGGACTCAGAGC-3′); Atp6v0d2 (forward, 5′-GTGAGACCTTGGAAGACCTGAA-3’; reverse, 5′- GAGAAATGTGCTCAGGGGCT-3′); c-Fos (forward, 5′-GCGAGCAACTGAGAAGAC-3’; reverse, 5′-TTGAAACCCGAGAACATC-3′); MMP9 (forward, 5′-CTGGACAGCCAGACACTAAAG-3’; reverse, 5′-CTCGCGGCAAGTCTTCAGAG-3′); Cat (forward, 5′-CTCGCAGAGACCTGATGTCC-3’; reverse, 5′-GACCCCGCGGTCATGATATT-3′); Hmox1 (forward, 5′-GAGCAGAACCAGCCTGAACT-3’; reverse, 5′-AAATCCTGGGGCATGCTGTC-3′); Nqo1 (forward, 5′-GGTGCTGTCTGGCCATAACT-3’; reverse, 5′-AAATCCTGGGGCATGCTGTC-3′); ALP (forward, 5′-GCACCTGCCTTACCAACTCT-3’; reverse, 5′-GTGGAGACGCCCATACCATC-3′); RUNX2 (forward, 5′-TCAAGGGAATAGAGGGGATGC-3’; reverse, 5′-GGGAGGACAGAGGGAAACAAC-3′); and β-actin (forward, 5′-AAGATCAAGATCATTGCTCCT-3’; reverse, 5′- AGCTCAGTAACAGTCCGCCT-3′). There was a 5 min denaturation step at 94 °C in the thermal cycling protocol, and then 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 40 s, primer annealing at 60 °C for 40 s, and DNA extension at 72 °C for 40 s (in that order). The amplification process was finished with a last elongation step of 5 min at 72 °C. Gene expression levels were relative quantified using the comparative threshold cycle (CT) analysis method, with β-actin serving as an endogenous control.

2.9.

## Western blot (WB)
For protein analysis, BMMs were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well, then stimulated with RANKL (50 ng/mL) and M-CSF (30 ng/mL), either with or without Cre (5 μM), for indicated durations. Cells were lysed at various time points using RIPA buffer. NE-PER™ extraction reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) facilitated the separation of nuclear and cytoplasmic protein fractions [ ]. Subsequently, protein concentrations of the lysates were quantified via BCA protein assays. Equivalent amounts of protein samples underwent electrophoretic separation on 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gels, followed by transfer onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Following transfer, membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk solution for 2 h at room temperature. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies diluted as recommended by manufacturers: β-actin (1:20,000), NFATc1, and V-ATPase-d2 (1:500 each); and CTSK, p-IKKα/β, IKKβ, Lamin B, p65, IκBα, Ace-p65, p-Akt, Akt, p-JNK, JNK, p-ERK, ERK, p-p38, p38, TRAF6, catalase, HO-1, NOX4, NOX1, COL1A1, OPN, RUNX2, and Sirt1 (all 1:1000). Subsequently, extensive washing with Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) was performed prior to incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (diluted 1:4000) for 2 h. Protein bands were visualised by chemiluminescence using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc XRS + imaging system, and Image Lab software (v3.0) was utilized for densitometric analysis.

2.10.

## Immunoprecipitation (IP)
The Protein A/G IP Kit (22202; BeaverBio, Suzhou, China) was used for the IP experiments, and the protocols provided by the manufacturer were adhered to in every detail. Protein extracts were generated by first lysing BMMs. Isolation of target proteins was achieved by conjugating protein A/G magnetic beads with an NFATc1 antibody by incubating them overnight at 4 °C, followed by washing steps and thermal elution. The samples that were immunoprecipitated as well as the total protein (used as input controls) were separated electrophoretically using 10% SDS-PAGE and then transferred to PVDF membranes. After subjecting the samples to anti-acetylated lysine and anti-NFATc1 antibodies at dilutions of 1:1000 and 1:1,000, respectively, they were treated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Using Image-Lab software, the band signals were quantified following chemiluminescent detection.

2.11.

## Immunofluorescence staining
Before permeabilization, cells (BMMs) were fixed for 15 min in 4% PFA and then incubated in PBS with 0.3% Triton X-100 and 5% normal goat serum for 60 min. Following permeabilization, one diluted 1:200 rabbit and 1:200 mouse primary antibodies against p65 and NFATc1 were incubated at 4 °C for the night. We used species-specific fluorescent secondary antibodies to incubate the cells for 60 min after a thorough washing process (three 5-min cycles in PBST). Cell nuclei were labeled by DAPI staining (5 min, sheltered from light). A Nikon A1R confocal fluorescence microscope was used to capture the images.

2.12.

## ROS production assay
Intracellular ROS production was quantified utilizing a fluorescent dihydroethidium (DHE)-based probe (Beyotime Biotechnology). The BMMs were first stimulated with RANKL (50 ng/mL) for 24 h, and then treated with Cre at different doses (0–5 μM) over 48 h. After the treatment, the cells were subjected to standard physiological conditions for 30 min while being incubated with a fluorescent probe (1:1000 dilution). Following this, they were washed and pelleted using centrifugation. Using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), we were able to see the fluorescence signals that correlate to intracellular ROS. Then, we used ImageJ software to assess the intensity of these signals.

2.13.

## Cell transfection
Transient gene silencing using small interfering RNA (siRNA) reagents purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China) was performed following the provided manufacturer's instructions. BMMs were seeded and cultured overnight in either 96-well or 6-well plates. Liposomal complexes carrying siRNA (100 nM) were prepared in α-MEM medium and introduced into the cultured cells for 5 h. Following transfection, Sirt1 transcript knockdown efficiency was verified at 48 h, and corresponding protein suppression was confirmed at 72 h. Transfected macrophages were subsequently treated with M-CSF (30 ng/mL), RANKL (50 ng/mL), and Cre (5 μM) prior to further functional assessments.

2.14.

## Isolation and culture of osteoblasts
Calvariae were harvested from euthanized C57BL/6J mice and aseptically fragmented into small pieces (approximately 1–2 mm3). After thorough rinsing with PBS, the tissue fragments underwent sequential enzymatic digestion, initially with 0.25% trypsin-ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) for 20 min to eliminate fibroblastic contamination, followed by digestion with collagenase type I (0.1%, 2 mg/mL) at 37 °C for 30 min to release osteogenic progenitor cells. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation (1500 g, 5 min) and resuspended in DMEM enriched with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution. Viable cells (passages 2–3) exhibiting greater than 95% viability were employed to guarantee phenotype stability. All procedures were executed under sterile laboratory conditions.

2.15.

## Osteogenesis differentiation induction
Initially, osteoblasts were placed into 24-well culture plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured for a period of 24 h, allowing adequate cellular attachment before differentiation induction commenced. Daily replenishment of the osteogenic medium was performed, introducing fresh reagents comprising dexamethasone (100 nM), β-glycerophosphate (10 mM), and ascorbic acid (50 μg/mL). The progress of osteoblastic differentiation was assessed at defined intervals, on day 7 for alkaline phosphatase (ALP) enzymatic activity and on day 21 for matrix mineralization, employing techniques described in prior publications [ ]. ALP expression was visualised by applying a commercially available staining kit purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology. Mineralization of the extracellular matrix was detected using a 2% solution of Alizarin Red S (ARS) (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.), and subsequent quantitative analysis was conducted by spectrophotometric absorbance measurements at 520 nm for ARS staining and 570 nm for ALP using a microplate reader.

2.16.

## Ovariectomy (OVX)-induced osteoporosis mouse model
In this study, all animal experiments adhered to the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The experimental protocols involving animals were approved by the Animal Experimental Ethical Committee of the Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University (Beijing, China) (Approval No.: XW20230829–1), and they were designed to comply closely to the worldwide norms set forth by the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS) (Geneva, Switzerland). A group of female C57BL/6 mice, who were 10 weeks old when obtained, were kept in a controlled environment free of pathogens. We randomly assigned six mice each to one of five groups: sham-operated controls, OVX-only controls, OVX animals treated with Cre (low dose: 5 mg/kg), OVX animals treated with Cre (high dose: 10 mg/kg), and OVX animals given Aln (10 mg/kg). Bilateral OVX was performed under anesthesia with sodium pentobarbital to induce osteoporotic conditions, whereas sham-operated mice underwent identical surgical intervention without ovarian excision. Surgical wounds were disinfected postoperatively using iodophor. Beginning two weeks post-surgery, OVX mice received intraperitoneal injections of Cre (5 or 10 mg/kg) or Aln (10 mg/kg) every other day for six weeks [ ]. An aqueous solution containing 10% DMSO and 90% maize oil was given to the sham group and the untreated OVX set. Mice were euthanized by isoflurane inhalation when the treatment regimen was finished, and tissue samples were taken. Histopathological and micro-computed tomography (CT) imaging of femoral bones were performed. We used an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to evaluate serum biomarkers. Potential hepatic and renal toxicities were evaluated by blood biochemical parameters and histological assessment of liver and kidney tissue sections.

2.17.

## Micro-CT scanning and analysis
After euthanasia, femurs underwent fixation (24 h) in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF), followed by PBS rinses (24 h) to remove residual formalin. Fixed femurs were scanned by micro-CT (SkyScan1276; Bruker, Kontich, Belgium) using optimized imaging conditions (50 kV; current, 500 μA; aluminum filter thickness, 0.5 mm; incremental rotation angle, 0.4°; and voxel dimension, 9 μm). DataViewer and CTVox software programs reconstructed the acquired tomography data, providing segmented two-dimensional (2D) sections and 3D renderings. Morphometric parameters were quantitatively evaluated at the distal metaphyseal trabecular bone region, positioned approximately 1 mm below the growth plate. Using the CTAn software, various indices were calculated, including the bone volume/tissue volume (BV/TV), BMD (mg HA/cm3), trabecular number (Tb.N, mm−1), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th, μm), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp, μm).

2.18.

## Histological analysis
Femurs obtained from mice were initially immersed in 4% PFA solution for fixation lasting 48 h, followed by decalcification carried out in 14% EDTA solution at 37 °C over two weeks. After completion of decalcification, tissues were embedded within paraffin blocks and sectioned sagittally to produce slices of approximately 5 μm thickness. Histological analysis included hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to reveal trabecular bone microarchitecture, and TRAP staining to highlight osteoclast localization and distribution within these bone sections. Digital photos of the entire slide were captured using the MXII scanning system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). The parameters measured in the quantitative analyses were the following: osteoclast surface ratio (Oc.S/BS), osteoclast density (N.Oc/BS), and BV/TV (Bioquant Osteo, Nashville, TN, USA). Immunohistochemical staining involved deparaffinization of sections, heat-induced antigen retrieval, and subsequent blocking, followed by overnight antibody incubation at 4 °C (Sirt1 antibody, diluted 1:100). Subsequently, biotinylated secondary antibodies were applied. The use of diaminobenzidine (DAB) as a chromogen and hematoxylin counterstaining allowed for the detection of positive immunoreactivity. The images were captured using an Olympus brightfield microscope, and the staining intensities were evaluated semi-quantitatively using Media Cybernetics' Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software.

2.19.

## ELISA
To determine the influence of Cre on interactions involving RANK and RANKL, RAW264.7 cells were plated in six-well culture dishes and allowed to attach overnight at 37 °C. Following cell adhesion, cultures were treated with RANKL (50 ng/mL) alongside varied Cre concentrations (0, 2.5, or 5 μM) for a 72 h incubation. After harvesting, supernatants were clarified by centrifugation at 2500 r/min for 20 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The effectiveness of RANK-mediated ligand clearance was evaluated by ELISA analysis of the clarified medium, which quantified soluble RANKL.
Following clotting for 1 h and spinning for 15 min, serum was extracted from blood samples. The concentrations of biomarkers for bone resorption, namely TRAP-5b and C-terminal cross-linked telopeptides of type I collagen (β-CTx), were measured using ELISA kits developed for mice from Elabscience (Elabscience Biotechnology Inc., Wuhan, China), according to the protocols provided by the manufacturer.

2.20.

## Sirt1 activity assay
Sirt1 enzymatic activity was quantified using a commercial fluorometric detection kit (Sigma-Aldrich). Reaction mixtures were incubated under standard conditions (37 °C for 30 min). Fluorescence intensity measurements were acquired using a microplate reader (λex = 360 nm, λem = 450 nm).

2.21.

## Molecular docking
Chemical structures of relevant compounds were downloaded in.sdf format from PubChem, whereas crystallographic data for target proteins Sirt1 and RANK were obtained from the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics (RCSB) Protein Data Bank (PDB). Structural preprocessing was conducted in PyMOL software, involving removal of ligand molecules and water, followed by protonation state adjustments and charge assignment using AutoDock Tools (vina.scripps.edu). Refined structures were saved in pdbqt format. Molecular docking analyses were executed using PyRx with the AutoDock Vina algorithm to estimate binding affinity values (ΔG, kcal/mol). Docking interactions and conformational outcomes were visualised using PyMOL and Discovery Studio software for detailed structural interpretation.

2.22.

## Statistical analysis
Each experimental protocol was independently replicated three times to ensure reproducibility and reliability. Results from quantitative analyses are shown using box-and-whisker diagrams, where each data point is individually plotted. We used GraphPad Prism (version 8.0) to conduct the statistical assessments. When comparing two groups in an experiment, we used unpaired Student's t-tests for statistical evaluation. When comparing numerous conditions in an experiment, we used one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post hoc analysis when appropriate. P < 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance.

3.

## Results
3.1.

## Cre inhibits RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis in vitro
The molecular structure of Cre is depicted in A. Cytotoxicity assays performed using the CCK-8 method demonstrated that Cre (≤10 μM) did not significantly compromise BMM cell viability following a 48 h exposure period ( B). Since metabolic activity was only minimally affected at a Cre concentration of 10 μM, the upper limit of 5 μM was designated for subsequent studies. Osteoclast differentiation assays were subsequently conducted by concurrently exposing cells to M-CSF (30 ng/mL) and RANKL (50 ng/mL) along with varying Cre concentrations (0–5 μM). Results of TRAP assays revealed a dose-responsive inhibition by Cre on multinucleated osteoclast formation, with maximal suppression observed at a Cre concentration of 5 μM ( C and D). Furthermore, temporal intervention assays administering Cre at a concentration of 5 μM during distinct differentiation intervals (days 1–3, 3–5, 5–6, and 1–6) revealed the strongest inhibitory impact occurred during the early differentiation stage (days 1–3) ( E and F).
 
3.2.

## Cre impairs the F-actin belt formation and resorptive activity of osteoclasts
Differentiated osteoclasts form cytoskeletal F-actin rings essential for bone resorption [ ]. Following seven-day RANKL induction, phalloidin-nuclear co-staining revealed dose-dependent disruption of actin ring morphology in Cre-treated groups compared to controls ( A–C). Quantitative analysis demonstrated Cre-mediated suppression of actin ring diameter, correlating with treatment concentration. To further evaluate Cre's inhibitory effects on osteoclast-mediated bone resorption, bovine bone slices were utilized to quantify resorption pits. Additionally, TRAP staining validated the necessity of RANKL for osteoclastogenesis, as demonstrated by the complete absence of TRAP-positive multinucleated cells and bone-resorbing pits in control cultures treated solely with M-CSF ( D–G). In contrast, bone resorption pit area was significantly reduced after Cre treatment, indicating that Cre dose-dependently inhibited bone resorption activity ( D–G), These findings confirm that Cre effectively suppresses mature osteoclast bone resorption function in vitro. Additionally, osteoblasts were used to evaluate osteogenic differentiation and investigate Cre's effects on differentiation and mineralization in vitro. Cre was non-toxic to osteoblasts at concentrations up to 10 μM ( ). In addition, combined treatment with Cre and H2O2 partially restored ALP activity and mineralization levels, which had been reduced by H2O2 treatment ( ), and enhanced expression of COL1A1, OPN, and RUNX2 after seven days of osteogenic induction ( ). These data suggest that Cre promotes osteoblast marker gene expression, cell differentiation, and functional recovery.
 
3.3.

## Cre suppresses the RANKL-induced expression of osteoclast-specific genes proteins, and upstream NFATc1 activity
Complementary molecular investigations were conducted to elucidate the osteoclastogenesis-suppressing effect of Cre. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that RANKL stimulation increased osteoclastic markers (Acp5, Ctsk, Atp6v0d2, c-Fos, and MMP9) in bone marrow-derived macrophages, whereas treatment with 2.5–5 μM Cre significantly reduced their expression ( A–E). NFATc1, the key transcriptional regulator of osteoclast differentiation [ ], exhibited reduced luciferase reporter activity and nuclear translocation following Cre treatment, as demonstrated by dual-modality assessment (luciferase assay/immunofluorescent staining) ( F and G). To further clarify how Cre regulates NFATc1, we evaluated its acetylation status and subcellular localization. Co-IP assays showed that Cre treatment significantly decreased NFATc1 acetylation ( ). Both nuclear fractionation and immunofluorescence analyses confirmed that Cre inhibited RANKL-induced NFATc1 nuclear translocation, suggesting that deacetylation may impair its transcriptional activity ( ). Temporal immunoblotting analysis demonstrated Cre's regulatory effects: i) integrin-β3, V-ATPase-d2, and CTSK protein levels were suppressed at differentiation days 3 and 5; and ii) early-phase markers (c-Fos and NFATc1) showed marked reduction at day 3 ( H–M). These findings position NFATc1 activity modulation as a crucial mechanism underlying Cre's dual inhibition of osteoclastic differentiation and functional maturation.
 
3.4.

## Cre enhances the antioxidant enzyme expression to scavenge RANKL-induced intracellular ROS
To investigate how Cre modulates ROS generation induced by RANKL during osteoclastogenesis, fluorometric assays using DHE were performed. Confocal imaging showed a dose-dependent reduction in ROS accumulation in BMMs exposed to 2.5–5 μM Cre ( ). Immunoblot analysis revealed enhanced protein expression of the antioxidant defense enzymes HO-1 and catalase in Cre-treated groups ( ). To further elucidate Cre's dual redox-regulatory mechanism, the expression of NADPH oxidases (NOX1 and NOX4), key enzymes in RANKL-induced ROS generation, was assessed by WB ( ). Consistent with previous findings [ ], NOX4 and NOX1 protein levels significantly increased following RANKL stimulation but decreased markedly after Cre treatment at various doses ( ). Complementary qRT-PCR analysis confirmed dose-responsive transcriptional activation of antioxidant genes (Cat, Hmox1, and Nqo1) following Cre treatment ( ). This synergistic enhancement of antioxidant pathways indicates Cre's dual capability to alleviate oxidative stress by simultaneously inhibiting ROS generation and enhancing antioxidant defenses.

3.5.

## Cre suppresses osteoclast differentiation by blocking NF-κB signaling
Activation of the NF-κB pathway is central to osteoclast differentiation, potentially triggered by oxidative stress resulting from RANKL stimulation [ , ]. Under unstimulated conditions, NF-κB proteins remain confined in the cytoplasm due to their association with IκB-α proteins [ ]. Immunoblot analysis revealed that Cre treatment effectively blocked RANKL-stimulated IκB-α proteolysis and subsequent p65 nuclear translocation at 15/30 min post-induction ( A–C). Supporting immunofluorescent imaging of macrophages demonstrated Cre-mediated prevention of p65 nuclear accumulation following RANKL activation ( D and E). Luciferase reporter assays further verified concentration-dependent suppression of NF-κB transcriptional activity by Cre ( F). Kinase activity profiling showed that Cre inhibited RANKL-induced phosphorylation of IKKα/β ( G and H), which is essential for initiating IκBα phosphorylation and subsequent degradation. These findings collectively demonstrate that Cre effectively disrupts RANKL-driven NF-κB signaling.
 
3.6.

## Cre inhibits RANKL-induced activation of MAPKs signaling pathway
Furthermore, RANKL-mediated activation of Akt and MAPK signaling cascades is critically involved in osteoclast differentiation, viability, and functional activity [ ]. To assess Cre's influence on these pathways, we performed WB analysis to detect phosphorylation and protein expression related to Akt and MAPK signaling components, including ERK, JNK, and p38, at various time points post-RANKL induction ( ). As expected, RANKL robustly increased phosphorylation of Akt, JNK, ERK, and p38, reaching peak levels at 10 or 20 min, while Cre treatment significantly suppressed phosphorylation of these proteins ( ).
Interactions between RANKL and its receptor RANK on osteoclast progenitor cells are indispensable for initiating downstream signaling via NF-κB and MAPK pathways [ ]. Considering the observed inhibitory effects of Cre on canonical signaling induced by RANKL, we hypothesized that Cre may directly disrupt the RANKL–RANK interaction, thereby impeding downstream signaling events. Molecular modeling indicated strong Cre-RANK binding (ΔG = −6.20 kcal/mol) within the receptor's catalytic pocket, stabilized by hydrogen bonds ( ). To further confirm Cre's inhibition of RANKL binding to RANK, an ELISA quantified extracellular RANKL in the culture medium of RAW 264.7 cells. RANKL solution without cells served as a positive control. Membrane-bound RANK effectively neutralized extracellular RANKL, but this interaction was significantly inhibited by Cre ( ). In addition, RANKL-RANK interactions promote TRAF6 recruitment, thereby activating downstream signaling cascades. WB results showed that Cre treatment significantly reversed RANKL-induced TRAF6 expression ( ). In summary, Cre disrupts RANKL-RANK interactions and reduces TRAF6 expression, thereby inhibiting Akt and MAPK signaling pathways.

3.7.

## Cre inhibits p65 acetylation and activates Sirt1
Protein acetylation, a key epigenetic modification, regulates transcription by structurally and functionally modifying target proteins [ ]. Specifically, acetylation of the p65 subunit enhances NF-κB transcriptional activity by facilitating target gene activation [ ]. Comparative analysis demonstrated that Cre administration significantly inhibited RANKL-induced p65 acetylation ( A and B). Given the established role of Sirt1 as an NAD+-dependent deacetylase in attenuating NF-κB signaling via p65 deacetylation, computational docking was employed to investigate the interaction between Cre and Sirt1. Molecular modeling revealed robust Cre-Sirt1 binding (ΔG = −7.10 kcal/mol) within the enzyme's catalytic pocket, stabilized by hydrogen bonds and π−system interactions ( C–E). A subsequent CETSA further confirmed this interaction. RAW264.7 cells treated with Cre (5 μM) for 12 h exhibited enhanced stability of Sirt1 protein, indicating intracellular binding between Cre and Sirt1 ( ). SPR analysis determined the binding KD between Cre and Sirt1 as 10.6 μM ( F and G). These results collectively confirm a direct interaction between Cre and Sirt1. Immunoblotting and enzymatic activity assays confirmed dose- and time-dependent increases in Sirt1 expression and activity following Cre exposure, identifying Cre as a novel Sirt1 agonist ( H–M).
 To delineate the role of Sirt1 in Cre-mediated osteoprotection, siRNA-mediated Sirt1 knockdown was performed ( ). Immunoblot analysis revealed that Sirt1 silencing attenuated Cre-induced suppression of osteoclastic markers NFATc1 and CTSK ( ). Complementary TRAP-positive multinucleated cell quantification further supported these findings ( ). Acetylation profiling revealed increased p65 acetylation in Sirt1-deficient macrophages treated with RANKL and Cre compared to control groups, indicating Sirt1-dependent deacetylation mediated by Cre ( ). Given Sirt1's regulatory role in oxidative stress via its deacetylase activity, ROS modulation was examined. DHE fluorescence analysis demonstrated that Sirt1 knockdown abolished Cre-mediated suppression of ROS ( ). Correspondingly, immunoblotting showed decreased expression of antioxidant enzymes catalase and HO-1 ( ), along with increased expression of NADPH oxidases (NOX4 and NOX1) ( ). This mechanistic framework identifies Sirt1 activation as essential for Cre-mediated regulation of p65 acetylation and oxidative stress, contributing to its inhibitory effects on osteoclastogenesis.

3.8.

## Cre attenuates OVX-induced bone loss
To validate the anti-osteoporotic efficacy of Cre, an OVX-induced postmenopausal osteoporosis model was established ( A). Animals received biweekly intraperitoneal injections of Cre (5 or 10 mg/kg) or vehicle for six weeks post-surgery, with Aln serving as a positive control. No treatment-related mortality was observed. Furthermore, histological and biochemical analyses assessing in vivo toxicity indicated that Cre treatment did not affect liver or kidney structure and function compared to controls, confirming its safety for administration ( ). Micro-CT reconstruction demonstrated substantial BV reduction in OVX controls compared to sham-operated groups. Cre administration dose-dependently ameliorated trabecular deterioration caused by estrogen depletion. Quantitative Micro-CT parameters (BV/TV, Tb.N, Tb.Sp, and BMD) confirmed therapeutic effects, especially at the higher dose (P < 0.01 vs. OVX controls) ( B–G). No statistically significant differences were observed in Tb.Th among groups ( F). Histomorphometric analysis via H&E staining corroborated micro-CT results, demonstrating preserved trabecular architecture in Cre-treated groups ( H and I). Consistent with in vitro observations, TRAP-stained femoral sections revealed decreased osteoclast indices (N.Oc/BS and Oc.S/BS) following Cre treatment ( A–C). Notably, these in vivo results suggest that Cre has anti-osteoporotic efficacy comparable to Aln. Mechanistically, bone marrow Sirt1 immunoreactivity increased in proportion to Cre dosage, significantly exceeding levels observed in the Aln group ( D and E). In situ ROS quantification in bone sections demonstrated Cre's antioxidant potential to reverse OVX-induced oxidative stress ( F and G). Serum biomarker analysis showed reduced TRAP-5b and β-CTx levels in Cre- and Aln-treated groups ( H and I). Collectively, these results indicate that Cre possesses therapeutic potential against osteoporosis, effectively preventing estrogen deficiency-induced bone loss by inhibiting osteoclast formation and activity in vivo.
 

4.

## Discussion
Osteoporosis, a metabolic bone disorder primarily characterized by excessive osteoclastic activity, represents a major clinical challenge due to its critical role in pathological bone loss [ ]. Although therapeutic innovations have emerged, limitations persist regarding the sustained efficacy and safety profiles of current osteoclast-targeting agents. Consequently, there remains an urgent need to develop safe and effective therapeutic strategies for controlling osteoclast hyperactivity. Increasing evidence highlights plant-derived compounds as viable candidates for osteoporosis management [ ]. Cre, a Stephania-derived alkaloid, exhibits multifunctional bioactivity, including the attenuation of neuroinflammation and mitigation of oxidative damage [ , ]. Our findings demonstrate that Cre exerts osteoprotective effects through a dual-phase mechanism: i) suppression of ROS generation via Sirt1 activation and ii) epigenetic modulation through p65 deacetylation, effectively counteracting OVX-induced osteoclastogenesis and bone deterioration ( ).
 Osteoclasts are specialized multinucleated cells responsible for skeletal resorption, executing their function through sequential phases: matrix attachment, actin-mediated polarization, and vesicular transport [ ]. Consequently, therapeutic approaches targeting osteoclast hyperactivation constitute a primary goal in osteoporosis management. Our experimental data demonstrated that Cre concentration-dependently suppressed osteoclast maturation and bone-resorptive capacity in vitro. Functional analyses revealed Cre's dual capability to disrupt both osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption. The dose-dependent reduction in resorption pit area on bovine bone slices was consistent with Cre-mediated inhibition of F-actin ring formation, an essential cytoskeletal structure for sealing zone assembly and acid secretion during resorption. The absence of resorption pits in uninduced controls underscores the essential role of RANKL in osteoclast activation. By attenuating both differentiation and resorptive function, Cre may mitigate pathological bone loss through dual mechanisms: limiting differentiation of osteoclast precursors and impairing the activity of mature osteoclasts. These observations emphasize the potential of Cre to target multiple stages of osteoclast function. In addition, our results indicated that Cre not only inhibits osteoclast-mediated bone resorption but also promotes bone formation by maintaining osteoblast activity. This bidirectional regulatory property offers greater therapeutic promise than simple anti-resorptive agents (such as bisphosphonates). Complementary molecular profiling via immunoblotting and qRT-PCR assays demonstrated transcriptional downregulation of osteoclast differentiation markers (Integrin β3, MMP9, Acp5, CTSK, Atp6v0d2, and c-Fos). These genes are transcriptionally regulated by NFATc1 signaling cascades, collectively coordinating osteoclastic maturation and resorption. NFATc1 serves as a central orchestrator of osteoclast terminal differentiation, maintaining its activation through an autoregulatory transcriptional loop involving self-binding to its promoter [ ]. Molecular imaging and immunoblot analyses confirmed that Cre suppressed NFATc1 protein expression following RANKL induction. Additionally, NFATc1 transcriptional activity was significantly reduced, as indicated by luciferase reporter assays. Moreover, Cre-mediated activation of Sirt1 also targeted NFATc1. Reduced acetylation and impaired nuclear translocation of NFATc1 may explain Cre-mediated repression of osteoclast-specific genes (Acp5 and CTSK).
The NF-κB pathway is essential for osteoclastic maturation and functional activation. Upon RANKL-RANK interaction, phosphorylation of IκBα by the IKK complex triggers its proteasomal degradation, releasing NF-κB subunits for nuclear translocation and subsequent NFATc1 transcriptional activation [ , ]. Experimental data indicated that Cre-mediated activation of Sirt1 suppresses IKKα/β phosphorylation and p65 acetylation; however, the mechanistic interplay between these events remains complex. Although molecular interactions confirmed direct binding and activation of Sirt1 by Cre, inhibition of IKK phosphorylation likely occurs indirectly through Sirt1-dependent modulation of upstream regulators (e.g., the TRAF6/TAK1 axis), rather than through direct targeting of IKK. While inhibition of IKK stabilizes IκBα and thus restricts NF-κB nuclear translocation, Sirt1 concurrently reduces p65 transcriptional activity via deacetylation, suggesting these pathways function independently yet synergistically to enhance osteoclast suppression. Currently, no direct evidence links IKK phosphorylation to Sirt1 enzymatic activity; however, future studies should investigate potential crosstalk (e.g., Sirt1-mediated regulation of IKK-associated kinases) and determine whether Cre directly engages IKK, a question that remains unresolved. Clarifying these hierarchical relationships will refine the understanding of Cre's polypharmacological actions in bone remodeling. Emerging evidence implicates elevated ROS as critical modulators of osteoclastic commitment, potentially through potentiation of the NF-κB pathway [ ]. We propose that Cre's redox-regulatory capacity contributes to its interference with RANKL signaling, supported by its upregulation of antioxidant enzymes (catalase and HO-1) and suppression of ROS generation driven by the bone marrow niche and RANKL-induced NOX4 and NOX1 expression. These observations align with previous reports describing Cre's anti-inflammatory and antioxidative properties [ , ].
The transcriptional regulatory activity of NF-κB is modulated through post-translational modifications, notably kinase-mediated phosphorylation, and lysine acetylation of its p65 subunit, prerequisites for maximal transcriptional activation [ ]. Following nuclear translocation, this TF engages cis-regulatory promoter elements and recruit chromatin-modifying enzymes (HATs) to facilitate RNA polymerase II assembly. Such molecular interactions optimize TF-chromatin binding kinetics, thereby enhancing transcriptional efficiency [ ]. Regarding NF-κB acetylation during RANKL-driven osteoclast differentiation, the precise contribution of HATs remains controversial. Notably, histone deacetylases (HDACs) dynamically regulate NF-κB acetylation by enzymatically removing acetyl modifications, thus diminishing transcriptional activation. Experimental evidence implicates multiple HDAC isoforms, HDAC3, HDAC5, and the NAD+-dependent Sirt1, in RANKL-mediated NF-κB signaling [ ]. Computational simulations demonstrated significant binding affinity between Cre and Sirt1, an NAD+-dependent deacetylase known to govern osteogenic and osteoclastic lineage commitment, thus modulating bone remodeling balance [ ]. Therapeutic targeting of Sirt1 has demonstrated efficacy in suppressing osteoclastic maturation and resorptive activity [ ]. Our findings corroborate that Cre-mediated activation of Sirt1 attenuates RANKL-induced p65 acetylation. Genetic inhibition of Sirt1 using siRNA partially restored p65 acetylation levels and reversed the inhibitory effects of Cre on osteoclast-related ROS production.
Building upon previous cellular experiments, an OVX-induced rodent osteoporosis model was developed to assess the therapeutic potential of Cre for skeletal disorders. Quantitative micro-CT analyses, combined with histopathological assessments (H&E and TRAP staining), demonstrated that Cre administration significantly mitigated OVX-induced trabecular deterioration. Biochemical analyses revealed reduced serum levels of osteoclast activity markers and decreased ROS accumulation within bone tissues following treatment, aligning with prior in vitro findings. This investigation establishes Cre's capacity to counteract skeletal degeneration caused by estrogen deficiency. Aln, included as a positive control to contextualize Cre's anti-osteoporotic efficacy within existing treatment paradigms, demonstrated that Cre exhibited comparable effectiveness in reducing trabecular bone loss. This efficacy aligns with Cre's dual mechanism involving Sirt1-mediated inhibition of NF-κB/NFATc1 signaling and enhancement of antioxidant responses. Compared with bisphosphonates, Cre may offer a safer profile for long-term application. Future research should explore combination treatments or structural optimization to enhance Cre's therapeutic efficacy.
Several methodological limitations warrant consideration. Bone remodeling equilibrium relies on balanced interactions between bone-resorbing and bone-forming cells [ ]. While experimental results demonstrated Cre's efficacy in suppressing osteoclastogenesis and mitigating bone deterioration post-OVX, further studies are necessary to elucidate its regulatory effects on osteoblast activity and confirm potential anabolic effects. Additionally, although intraperitoneal administration of optimized Cre concentrations exhibited anti-resorptive properties in this model, oral delivery may offer improved patient adherence and safety, positioning it as a preferable therapeutic route. Further refinement of Cre's dosage regimen is imperative to minimize potential adverse effects during long-term osteoporosis treatment.

5.

## Conclusion
Collectively, our findings demonstrate that Cre dose-dependently suppresses RANKL-induced osteoclast maturation ex vivo and mitigates skeletal deterioration associated with estrogen deficiency in vivo. Mechanistically, Cre-mediated Sirt1 activation attenuates RANKL signaling through dual mechanisms: promoting p65 deacetylation and inhibiting IκBα proteolysis, thereby downregulating NFATc1 and osteoclast-related effector proteins. These coordinated molecular events lead to reduced osteoclast differentiation and resorption capacity. Notably, Cre treatment alleviated marrow oxidative damage in estrogen-deficient models, suggesting therapeutic potential for bone metabolic disorders. This mechanistic framework provides foundational insights for developing Sirt1-targeted interventions to treat pathological bone remodeling.
