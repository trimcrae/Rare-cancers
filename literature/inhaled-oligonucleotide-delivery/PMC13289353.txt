## Background
Silicosis is a severe occupational pulmonary fibrosis disease caused by silica dust inhalation, characterized by persistent inflammation and progressive fibrosis. While transcriptomic studies have identified numerous genes dysregulated, molecular mechanisms extend beyond mere changes in gene expression levels. Recent evidence suggests that post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms, particularly alternative splicing (AS), may play crucial roles in fibrotic processes. However, AS dynamics and functional significance in silicosis remain largely unexplored.

## Method
To investigate AS in silicosis pathogenesis, we analyzed RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) data from a mouse model, delineating the dynamic landscape of gene expression and AS events across disease stages. AS events were classified into two categories: those correlated with and independent of gene expression changes. We integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data to precisely map the cell type-specific expression patterns of these events. Through this analysis, we identified critical AS events, whose dynamic regulation and functional relevance during silicosis progression were validated using in vivo and in vitro models.

## Results
A total of 26,232 high-confidence AS events were identified, with Skipping Exon (SE) being the predominant type (75.6%, 19,832/26,232). These splicing events were derived from 7,524 parental genes, with 72% (5,422) harboring at least two events, indicating widespread splicing complexity. 1,363 (5.2%) high-confidence events were significantly regulated across disease stages, with 88 Differentially Expressed Alternative Splicing (DEAS) events exhibiting a substantial splicing shift (absolute delta Percent Spliced In (PSI) greater than 0.2). Strikingly, only 11.4% (10/88) of these DEAS events showed concordant gene-level changes, while a substantial proportion (88.6%, 78/88) occurred despite stable gene level expression. Parental genes were enriched in silicosis-related pathways, including smooth muscle cell migration, actin bundle assembly and the small GTPase-mediated signal transduction. Integrated scRNA-seq helped map expression to specific cell types and prioritize validation targets. The dynamic regulation of three top targets, including Clec4e- Retained Intron (RI), Wdr37- Alternative First Exon (AFE), and Tns3-AFE, was validated in mouse models. In vitro functional experiments showed that Clec4e isoforms (RI and non-RI) possess distinct functional properties; dysregulation of Clec4e-RI may relieve inflammatory inhibition. These results suggest a potential regulatory role of AS reprogramming in silicosis pathogenesis and indicate Clec4e splicing events as a possible target for inflammation regulation.

## Conclusion
This study presents a landscape of AS in silicosis, demonstrating that AS constitutes a critical layer of pathological regulation distinct from transcriptional alterations. We identified and validated that the Clec4e-RI splicing isoform may play a key role in the silicosis pathogenesis by modulating macrophage-related inflammatory responses.

## Introduction
Silicosis is an interstitial lung disease caused by the inhalation of crystalline silica particles, characterized by acute and chronic inflammatory responses that culminate in progressive pulmonary fibrosis and respiratory failure [ ]. Global epidemiological data reveal a persistent rise in both the incidence and mortality of silicosis, with China bearing a particularly heavy disease burden [ ]. Occupational exposure predominantly occurs in industries such as mining, construction, and stone processing [ ]. Despite its severe impact, current therapeutic options remain extremely limited; apart from lung transplantation, there are no effective pharmacological interventions to halt disease progression [ ].
The well-known pathology of silicosis is: alveolar macrophages (AMs), acting as the first line of defense, engulf silica particles, subsequently undergoing apoptosis or pyroptosis and releasing the ingested particles along with inflammatory mediators [ , ]. This, in turn, attracts further macrophage accumulation and promotes the secretion of abundant oxidants, proteases, and pro-inflammatory cytokines, leading to widespread cellular damage [ , ]. This escalating cycle ultimately results in progressive pulmonary inflammation, the formation of fibrotic nodules, and irreversible tissue remodeling.
Previous studies have confirmed that silica exposure significantly alters gene expression patterns in the lungs [ ]. Transcriptomic analyses of silicosis mouse models have revealed marked upregulation of multiple pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., Il-1β, Il-1α, Tnf-α, Ccl2) and pro-fibrotic genes (e.g., Mmp12, Tgfb1, Timp1) [ , ]. However, these studies have primarily focused on expression analysis at gene-levels, rather than detailed transcript levels, leaving post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms that fine-tune gene function incompletely understood. In eukaryotes, alternative splicing (AS) is a crucial post-transcriptional mechanism that generates multiple functionally distinct mRNA isoforms from a single gene, thereby significantly expanding proteomic diversity [ ]. With over 95% of human multi-exon genes undergoing AS, this process plays a pivotal role in regulating normal development and disease pathogenesis [ ].
Aberrant AS is closely linked to the progression of various diseases. In oncology, dysregulated splicing factors drive tumorigenesis by orchestrating metabolic reprogramming [ , ] and immune evasion [ , ], positioning them as promising therapeutic targets. Growing evidence suggests that AS, a pivotal dimension of post-transcriptional regulation, plays a crucial role in immune system responses to environmental stimuli and the maintenance of functional plasticity. By generating isoforms with distinct functionalities, AS modulates the intensity and duration of inflammatory signaling pathways [ , ]. However, the role of AS in silicosis, a classic chronic inflammatory disease induced by environmental dust exposure, remains largely unexplored. Although Zhao et al. [ ] utilized a rat model to demonstrate that silica exposure triggers large-scale AS alterations (particularly exon-skipping events), their study was limited to static observations at a single time point. Consequently, the dynamic regulation of AS as the disease transitions from acute inflammation to chronic fibrosis remains elusive. More importantly, there is currently a lack of in-depth investigations into the pathological regulatory functions of specific splicing events in silicosis. Therefore, systematic characterization of the transcriptome-wide dynamic evolution of AS in silicosis is of significant scientific value.
In this study, a comprehensive AS landscape in silicosis was detected through analyzing mouse RNA-Seq data. Thousands of AS events associated with disease progression were identified and characterized. We found that AS is widespread during silicosis progression and may represent an additional regulatory layer beyond gene-level expression changes. We validated the dynamic regulation of top AS events using in vivo mouse model and prioritized the Clec4e-RI transcript (an intron-retention isoform of C-type lectin domain family 4 member E) for functional investigation. Through in vitro functional assays, we found that the Clec4e-RI isoform exerts anti-inflammatory effects in macrophage-related experimental settings. Our findings provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms driving silicosis progression and open new avenues for the future exploration of splicing-based therapies and novel therapeutic targets.

## Materials and methods

## Data sources and preprocessing
In this study, we downloaded RNA-Seq data for 18 representative mice silicosis models from the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) database, CRA005925. The dataset included three non-exposed control mice and 15 silicosis model mice stratified into four progression time points: two weeks (n = 4), four weeks (n = 4), six weeks (n = 4), and ten weeks (n = 3) post-silica exposure. To capture distinct pathophysiological stages, samples were categorized into early and late stages based on the multi-omics findings of Wang et al. [ ]. Specifically, the 2-week and 4-week points, characterized by persistent inflammation and early-stage fibrosis, were defined as the early stage. The 6-week and 10-week points, marked by progressive fibrosis and tissue remodeling, were defined as the late stage [ ]. This classification served as the basis for subsequent comparative AS analysis. These data have been previously utilized in studies of transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics of mice silicosis, providing a rich biological context.

## Raw data quality control
To ensure data quality and eliminate potential technical biases, we first conducted a quality control analysis on the raw sequencing data (fastq files). Specifically, we employed the fastp tool (v0.23.4) to filter reads, removing low-quality sequences (e.g., reads with a Q score below 20) and potential contaminants, thereby obtaining high-quality clean reads [ ]. This step ensures the accuracy and reliability of the foundational data for subsequent analyses.

## STAR alignment
We utilized STAR [ ] (v2.7.11b) for sequence alignment, employing a two-pass mode to enhance the detection sensitivity of novel splice junctions. The reference genome index was constructed based on the respective species' genomic sequences and gene annotation files (GRCm38/mm10 for mouse and GRCh37/hg19 for human). In the first pass, STAR was run with standard parameters to generate splice junction intermediate files without outputting BAM files, focusing on the rapid detection of all potential junctions. This step yielded a splice junction table (SJ.out.tab) for each sample, containing the coordinates and read support for all detected junctions. In the second pass, the junctions identified in the first pass were provided to STAR as a priori information to re-build a more refined genome index. This process significantly improved the detection of novel splicing events. The second pass outputted coordinate-sorted BAM files, retaining essential attributes such as multi-mapping tags, alignment scores, mismatch counts, and splicing signals (via the parameters –outSAMattributes NH HI AS nM XS). Additionally, the –quantMode TranscriptomeSAM option was enabled to generate transcriptome-aligned BAM files for downstream transcript quantification. Throughout the alignment process, the versions of the reference genome and annotation files remained consistent with those used in subsequent AS detection. This two-pass strategy ensured the sensitive and accurate detection of both known and novel splice junctions, providing a reliable foundation for downstream AS identification and quantification.

## Identification of AS events
After alignment of RNA-seq reads by STAR, AS events were identified and quantified by rMATS [ ] (v4.2.0) using gene transcript annotations from gene annotation files (GRCm38/mm10 for mouse and GRCh37/hg19 for human). In order to maximize detection sensitivity in the initial phase, strict global filtering was subsequently applied. MARVEL [ ] (v1.4.0) was then utilized to extract splicing information from rMATS and integrate it with STAR-aligned junction results to identify additional AS events and calculate the Percent Spliced In (PSI) values. While rMATS systematically detects five classical AS types, including Skipping Exon (SE), Mutually Exclusive Exons (MXE), Alternative 5' Splice Site (A5SS), Alternative 3' Splice Site (A3SS), and Retained Intron (RI). To supplement the types of splicing events that rMATS fails to cover, we further use the MARVEL package to identify Alternative First Exon (AFE) and Alternative Last Exon (ALE) events. By creating a MARVEL object and utilizing the DetectEvents function, this function defines AFE and ALE events by calculating the frequency of connection between the first (or last) exon and the downstream (or upstream) exon.

## Calculation of PSI
To quantify AS shifts, we calculated the PSI for each identified event by integrating rMATS and MARVEL data, including AS name information and gene length information, with the splice junction counts obtained from STAR, and computed the PSI using the corresponding formula. ## Analysis of Differentially Expressed Alternative Splicing (DEAS) events
Using the quality-controlled PSI matrix, we employed the non-parametric Wilcoxon Rank-Sum Test to evaluate significant differences in PSI distributions between the Silicosis (Early- and Late- stage) and Control groups. The criteria for screening DEAS events were strictly set as: P-value less than 0.05 and an absolute change in mean PSI (|ΔPSI|, ΔPSI = PSIsilicosis—PSIcontrol) greater than 0.2 between groups. This strategy was designed to accurately identify candidate events undergoing substantial splicing frequency shifts during the progression of silicosis, providing highly reliable targets for subsequent functional studies.

## Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) analysis
Based on the alignment results, we performed differential expression analysis using DESeq2 [ ] (v1.34.0) to identify genes that show significant changes in early- and late-stage silicosis models compared to the control group. The criteria for selecting DEGs included an adjusted P-value (Padj) of less than 0.05 and absolute value of log value (base 2) of gene expression Fold Change (log2FC) greater than 1. This ensures the identification of genes with significant differences and biologically meaningful expression levels.

## Functional enrichment analysis
To preliminarily investigate functional changes across different stages, we performed functional enrichment analysis on the screened DEGs and parental genes of DEAS events. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler software [ ] (v4.2.2), with gene ID conversion facilitated by the Mm.eg.db package (version 3.14.0).

## Single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data acquisition and processing
This study utilized a pre-annotated single-cell dataset directly obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (Accession Number: GSE274372). The raw data were provided as a Monocle3 cell dataset (CDS) object, which contained comprehensive cell type annotation information. For subsequent analysis, the CDS object was converted into a Seurat [ ] (v4.3.0.1) object. The specific procedures were as follows: the raw expression matrix was extracted from the CDS object, and Ensemble gene IDs were converted to official gene symbols based on the GENCODE vM35 annotation file. Duplicate gene symbols were merged by calculating their mean expression values. Subsequently, a Seurat object was created using the processed expression matrix, and cell metadata (including cell type annotations) as well as UMAP coordinates from the original data were integrated into the object. Finally, the data were log-normalized (scale factor = 10,000). The annotated myeloid cell subset from this dataset was directly adopted for subsequent calculation of the average expression levels of target genes within this cell population.

## Establishment of the silicosis mouse model
To model the progression of human silicosis, a mouse model was established via a single intratracheal instillation of crystalline silica. Male C57BL/6 J mice (6–8 weeks old; purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd.) were acclimatized for one week and then randomly allocated into silica-treated and control groups at three terminal time points: weeks 1, 4, and 12 post-instillations (n = 4 per group per time point). Mice in the silica-treated groups were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital and then intratracheally instilled with 100 μL of a crystalline silica suspension (4 mg/100uL in sterile saline). Control mice underwent the same procedure but received an equal volume of sterile physiological saline. At their designated endpoints, all animals were euthanized, and lung tissues were collected for subsequent analysis.

## Histological examination
Lung tissues from each mouse were dissected and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The fixed tissues were then submitted to Hubei Baiaosi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for sectioning and staining. Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining was performed to evaluate general morphological changes, and Masson’s trichrome staining was used to assess collagen deposition and fibrosis extent. Whole-slide scanning was conducted using a VS200 slide scanner (Olympus), and the acquired images were processed and analyzed with OlyVIA software (Olympus).

## Cell culture and treatment
RAW264.7 cells (a generous gift from Dr. Jingwen Jiang, West China School of Public Health, Sichuan University) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere.
For silicon dioxide (SiO₂) stimulation, a sterile stock suspension (100 mg/mL in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)) was sonicated and diluted in complete culture medium to a final concentration of 100 μg/mL. Cells were exposed to this suspension for 24 h. Control cells were treated with an equal volume of PBS.

## Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells using RNA isolated Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of RNA were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript Q RT SuperMix for qRT-PCR Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.). Quantitative PCR was performed on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qRT-PCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.) to detect the mRNA expression levels of target genes. The relative expression of each gene was calculated using the 2^(− ΔΔCt) method, with β-actin serving as the internal reference control. All primer sequences used in this study are listed in Additional file: Table S1.

## Plasmid transfection
The Clec4e-nonRI and Clec4e-RI overexpression plasmids were constructed by cloning the respective sequences into the pcDNA3.1 (+) backbone and were custom-synthesized by Synbio Technologies. Transfection of these plasmids into RAW264.7 cells was performed using the Polyjet transfection reagent (Signagen) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

## RNA secondary structure and stability prediction
RNA secondary structure and thermodynamic stability were predicted using the RNAfold program from the ViennaRNA Package (version 2.6.4) [ ]. The full-length cDNA sequences of Clec4e-RI and Clec4e-nonRI isoforms were submitted to RNAfold with default parameters to calculate the Minimum Free Energy (MFE) structures. To account for differences in sequence length, Adjusted MFE (AMFE) values were calculated by normalizing MFE to sequence length (AMFE = MFE/length, kcal/mol/nt).

## Open Reading Frame (ORF) and Protein Sequence Analysis
ORF identification and protein sequence translation were performed using the NCBI ORFfinder tool [ ], with a minimum ORF length set to 75 nucleotides.

## Protein domain architecture prediction
Protein domain composition was analyzed using InterPro [ ] through the InterPro database. Predicted protein sequences of Clec4e-RI and Clec4e-nonRI were submitted for domain identification.

## Transmembrane domain prediction
Transmembrane helix prediction was conducted using TMHMM Server (version 2.0) [ ]. Protein sequences were analyzed with default parameters to identify transmembrane regions and predict membrane topology.

## Subcellular localization prediction
Protein subcellular localization was predicted using DeepLoc-2.0 [ ] (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/DeepLoc-2.0/).

## HDOCK website analysis
The HDOCK server is a protein–protein and protein–nucleic acid interaction structure prediction method that integrates free molecular docking and template-based modeling and is provided to users via an online platform [ ]. We downloaded the protein structures of FcRγ (PDB ID: 8YRJ, chains G and H) from the PDB database. Structural models for the Clec4e-RI and Clec4e-nonRI isoforms were generated using AlphaFold3 [ ]. We then used the HDOCK server to study the interaction patterns of Clec4e isoforms and FcRγ. Finally, we used PyMOL [ ] (v3.1) to analyze the interaction patterns.

## Statistics
Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.3). Prior to significance testing, data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality, and homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. For comparisons between two groups, data satisfying both normality and equal variance assumptions were analyzed using the unpaired Student’s t-test. For data exhibiting unequal variances, Welch’s t-test was utilized. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Result

## The dynamic landscape of AS events during silicosis progression in mouse models
To systematically investigate the dynamic regulation of AS events during the progression of silicosis, we collected RNA-seq data of lung tissue from silica-exposed mouse models [ ]. The experimental cohort consisted of 15 silicosis model mice (8 early-stage and 7 late-stage) and 3 normal control mice (Fig. A). The Marvel [ ] pipeline was used to systematically annotate AS across all samples and quantitatively assessed seven major types of AS events: SE, MXE, RI, AFE, ALE, A3SS, and A5SS [ ] (Fig. A). A total of 78,514 AS events were initially identified. To ensure robustness, stringent filtering criteria were applied: AS events had to be consistently detected across all samples, with a minimum of 10 reads spanning the splice junction and the average of PSI was no less than 0.05 [ ]. After filtering, 26,232 high-confidence AS events were retained, derived from 7,524 parental genes. SE is the most predominant AS event, constituting 19,832 (75.60%) of the high-confidence set (Fig. B). Further analysis revealed that 5,422 (72%) of parental genes produced at least two AS events (Fig. C), indicating widespread splicing complexity at the transcript level. Notably, individual genes were found to participate in multiple types of splicing regulation. For example, 29 genes exhibited five distinct types of AS events simultaneously: SE, RI, A3SS, A5SS, and AFE (Fig. B). Gene structure analysis indicated that genes undergoing multiple types of AS events were generally longer (Fig. D). Moreover, specific splicing types, including MXE and SE, are associated with longer genes (Fig. E), suggesting a correlation between gene length and splicing complexity. To explore abnormal AS events in silicosis, we evaluated the alterations in AS events between the silicosis and control groups. We identified 1,363 (5.2%) high-confidence AS events that were significantly differentially expressed across disease stages (Additional file: Table S2). Among these, 88 DEAS events exhibited a substantial splicing shift in the early- or late- stage (absolute delta PSI greater than 0.2) (method) (Fig. F) (Additional file: Table S3).
 To further explore the translational relevance of our findings, we performed a cross-species analysis utilizing publicly available human Idiopathic Pulmonary Fibrosis (IPF) RNA-seq datasets (Supplementary Figures S1-S2). Although direct transcriptomic data for human silicosis remains unavailable, comparing our mouse model with human IPF revealed a highly conserved landscape of AS reprogramming. Approximately 69% of the AS-associated genes identified in the human cohort overlapped with our mouse data. Furthermore, these shared genes were consistently enriched in cellular processes, such as RNA splicing, proteasome-mediated catabolism, and macroautophagy. These consistencies suggest that AS remodeling is a universal pathological feature of fibrotic lung diseases across mammalian species.

## Comparative analysis of gene expression and AS dynamics throughout silicosis progression
We next seek to explore the relationship between AS and gene expression regulation. To this end, we first identify DEGs associated with silicosis progression using DESeq2 (Methods). When comparing with the control group, we identified 877 up-regulated and 1,325 down-regulated genes in the early-stage, and 889 up-regulated and 1,018 down-regulated genes in the late-stage (Fig. A). Within these genes, 630 upregulated and 760 downregulated DEGs were overlapped in early- and late-stages, suggesting that these genes may play sustained roles throughout silicosis development (Fig. B) (Additional file: Table S4). Functional enrichment analysis using the GO database (Methods) showed that the commonly up-regulated genes were significantly enriched in immune-related pathways, including regulation of innate immune response, immune effector process, and cytokine-mediated signaling pathways, indicating persistent activation of inflammatory responses during silicosis progression (Fig. C). In contrast, the commonly down-regulated genes were primarily enriched in biological processes related to muscle tissue development, cell differentiation, and contractile function, suggesting a suppression of muscle-related pathways in the diseased state. These results align with current understanding of silicosis as a disease driven by chronic and persistent inflammation, coupled with a progressive decline in lung function characterized by reduced contraction and air exchange [ , ].
 Comparative analysis of the DEAS and DEG datasets showed that only 6.1% (83/1,363) of the splicing events were also dysregulated at gene-level. We focused on the 88 DEAS with a substantial splicing shift. Strikingly, while 11.4% (10/88) of these DEAS showed significant gene level expression changes, the majority (88.6%, 78/88) occurred independently (Fig. D, E). Further analysis of the 10 DEAS events overlapping with DEGs showed stage-specific regulatory patterns: early-stage featured two up-regulated and seven down-regulated events, while late stage had two up-regulated and five down-regulated events (Fig. F, G). A subset of four events displayed consistent regulation across stages: one up- and three down-regulated, corresponding to the genes Capg, Clec4e, Fnip2, and Rgs1 (Fig. H, I). These sustained splicing alterations may contribute to silicosis progression and warrant further investigation.
We next investigated the parental genes of the 78 independent DEAS events (Fig. A) (Additional file: Table S5) to determine the potential functional impact of splicing shifts in the absence of expression changes. Enrichment analysis using the GO Biological Processes (BP) annotation showed that the parental genes were associated with key features of silicosis progression [ , ], including smooth muscle cell migration, actin bundle assembly and the small GTPase mediated signal transduction pathway (Fig. B). These findings suggest that, even in the absence of significant changes in gene abundance, alterations in splicing isoforms may influence silicosis progression.
 Furthermore, we identified a core list of 21 DEAS events that were consistently dysregulated across both disease stages: 12 persistently upregulated and 9 persistently downregulated DEAS (Fig. C-F). This conserved signature reveals a program of splicing dysregulation that operates independently of gene expression changes. Together, our comparative analysis demonstrates that AS constitutes a predominant and distinct layer of the molecular reprogramming in silicosis.

## Identification of key AS events in myeloid cells from silicosis single cell transcriptomic data
Given that signals from bulk RNA-seq represent an average across diverse cell types, we leveraged scRNA-seq data to deconvolve these signals and pinpoint core functional cellular contexts of the core DEAS events. We obtained relevant scRNA-seq data from public databases (Fig. A), which included 9 silicosis model mice and 3 normal control mice [ ]. We focused on the myeloid compartment, as silica particles primarily targeting innate immune cells such as macrophages and neutrophils to initiate pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis processes [ – ]. We thus prioritize the core DEAS events based on their splicing shift (ΔPSI) and the expression abundance of their parental genes in myeloid cells (Fig. B). The results were further filtered to retain DEAS events whose parental genes showed myeloid-specific expression (Fig. C). This yielded three high-priority DEAS events, derived from Clec4e, Tns3, and Wdr37, each exhibited strong myeloid specific gene expression and high ΔPSI, respectively.
 Among these 3 DEAS events, the intron retention event in the Clec4e gene (Clec4e-RI) showed the highest significance in silicosis model samples, with a marked decrease in PSI value (Fig. G), suggesting reduced expression of the specific transcript (ENSMUST00000175995) retaining intron (Fig. D). Its parental gene Clec4e, also known as Mincle, serves as a key pattern recognition receptor in macrophages for detecting silica-induced cellular damage [ ]. It recognizes Damage-Associated Molecular Patterns (DAMPs) such as SAP130 and activates the Syk–NF-κB signaling pathway [ ], driving the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines including Tnf-α and IL-6 [ , ]. Furthermore, Mincle contributes to the maintenance of the M1-like macrophage phenotype and facilitates macrophage-to-myofibroblast transition [ ]. Together, our analysis suggests that the Clec4e-RI event may be a critical player modulating the Mincle-mediated signaling sensitivity, thus impacting macrophage inflammatory polarization and pro-fibrotic shift, and ultimately serving as a pivotal regulator in the inflammation-to-fibrosis transition.
The AFE event in the Tns3 gene was significantly upregulated in silicosis model samples (Fig. F), indicating increased expression of the specific splice variant (ENSMUST00000239091) during silicosis progression (Fig. E). Tns3, which coding Tensin 3, an integrin–actin cytoskeleton linker protein localized primarily to focal adhesions and fibrillar adhesions [ ], regulates α5β1 integrin positioning and fibronectin fibril assembly through its interaction with Talin, playing a central role in Extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [ ]. The observed AFE splicing alteration in the Tns3 gene may disrupt the ratio of different Tns3 transcript isoforms, thereby affecting fibronectin fibril assembly and ECM remodeling, and ultimately contributing to the development of pulmonary fibrosis.
The AFE event for Wdr37 is associated with transcript ENSMUST00000176922 (Fig. F), which is upregulated during silicosis progression (Fig. F). The Wdr37 protein forms a complex with PACS1 and plays a critical role in maintaining endoplasmic reticulum calcium homeostasis. Studies have shown that loss of Wdr37 leads to reduced IP3 receptor expression, impaired calcium release, and induction of endoplasmic reticulum stress and oxidative stress [ ]. Wdr37 has not been previously linked to silicosis progression. However, its myeloid-specific high expression and significantly differential AS suggest Wdr37 as a novel and promising target worthy of further investigation.

## Silica exposure induces progressive pulmonary inflammation and irreversible fibrosis in mice
To validate the dynamic expression patterns of AS events associated with Clec4e, Tns3, and Wdr37 under silicotic pathological conditions, we established a silicosis model using C57BL/6 J mice (Methods) (Fig. A). Histopathological analysis revealed a clear time-dependent progression in silicotic lungs. Following silica instillation, lung tissue showed inflammatory infiltration at week 1; progressed to inflammatory nodules with collagen deposition at week 4; and developed dense, irreversible silicotic nodules with severe fibrosis by week 12 (Fig. B). Meanwhile, the saline treated control group maintained normal pulmonary architecture (Fig. B). Consistent with histopathology, molecular analysis confirmed progressive inflammation and fibrosis. Compared with controls, qRT-PCR analysis showed that the pro-inflammatory cytokine inflammatory marker Interleukin (Il)−1α remained significantly upregulated throughout the experimental period, while tumor necrosis factor (Tnf) α showed a delayed but significant surge at weeks 4 and 12 (Fig. C-D). Although Fibronectin (Fn1) only showed a borderline statistically significance at weeks 4, the overall trend supported a fibrotic phenotype (Fig. E). Meanwhile, the fibrosis marker Col1a1 exhibited sustained upregulation from week 1 to 12 (Fig. F). Together, these morphological and molecular findings support the successful establishment of the silicosis model and provide an experimental framework for subsequent validation of candidate AS events.
 

## Validation of key AS events in silicosis pathogenesis
We validated three prioritized DEAS events in the mouse model using isoform-specific PCR primers (Fig. A) (Additional file: Table S1). The mouse Clec4e gene is approximately 2.5 kb in length and contains 6 exons. To accurately measure the relative abundance of the RI isoform, we designed two sets of specific primers (Fig. A, left): Primer Set 1 spans exon 3 (E3), the RI, and exon 4 (E4) to amplify the RI isoform; Primer Set 2 spans E3 and E4 junction to amplify the transcripts without RI (nonRI). qRT-PCR results (Fig. B) showed that the ratio of the RI to the nonRI transcript (RI/nonRI) for the Clec4e gene exhibited a progressive downregulation in the silicosis mouse model. Specifically, while only a border line statistical significance was observed at week 1, significant progressive downregulation was achieved at week 4 and 12 (Fig. B). This trend aligns well with our earlier analysis (Fig. G) and reflects the disease progression trajectory, which is characterized by increased inflammation levels over time.
 Similarly, two sets of primers were designed for the AFE events of Tns3 and Wdr37 genes, respectively (Fig. A, middle and right), where the inclusion of exon 1b (E1b) corresponds to AFE events with increasing PSI in the bulk RNA-seq analysis (Figs. F, E-F). qRT-PCR results again confirmed the upregulation of E1b transcripts as the disease progresses (Fig. C, D).
In summary, the in vivo qRT-PCR validation results suggest the downregulation of Clec4e-RI and the upregulation of Tns3-AFE and Wdr37-AFE, indicating the potential involvement of these specific DEAS events in the pathogenesis of mice silicosis.
Then, to elucidate the cell-specific regulatory mechanisms of these DEAS events, we focused on macrophages, the primary effector cells in silicosis [ , ]. And scRNA-seq analysis indicated that Clec4e, Tns3, and Wdr37 are specifically expressed in macrophages (Fig. E). Accordingly, we performed in vitro validation using the murine macrophage cell line RAW264.7 stimulated with SiO2. To confirm cellular activation, we assessed inflammatory markers. Analysis by qRT-PCR confirmed that SiO2 exposure significantly upregulated Il-1α and Tnf-α mRNA levels (Fig. F-G), validating that the cells successfully recapitulated the pro-inflammatory response observed in vivo. Having confirmed cellular activation, we next examined its impact on AS. Two distinct modes of splicing regulation in response to SiO2 were observed. The relative abundance of Clec4e-RI significantly decreased upon SiO2 stimulation (Fig. H), mirroring our in vivo sequencing data and confirming that this splicing event is modulated by silica stimulation within macrophages. Conversely, the relative abundance of Tns3-AFE showed no significant change (Fig. I), indicating that its increase in vivo is not the result of cell-intrinsic upregulation within macrophages. Instead, this disparity may be driven by the expansion of macrophages expressing this isoform and/or dysregulation in other cell types, warranting further investigation. In contrast, Wdr37-AFE was undetectable in RAW264.7 cells, implying its regulation may depend on other cell types or complex microenvironmental interactions. In summary, these in vitro findings suggest Clec4e-RI as a potential macrophage-intrinsic target. Consequently, subsequent studies will focus on Clec4e-RI to elucidate its molecular role in regulating pro-inflammatory macrophage phenotypes.

## Clec4e-RI and Clec4e-nonRI have antithetical functions in silicosis
Clec4e gene expression is closely associated with inflammatory responses, as demonstrated by prior research [ – ]. To determine the individual contributions of the two types of splicing isoforms under silicosis pathological conditions, we further investigated the specific functions of Clec4e-RI and Clec4e-nonRI in silicosis. We initially attempted to achieve isoform-specific knockdown of Clec4e-nonRI using siRNAs targeting its unique exon–exon junction. However, due to the nested architecture of the transcripts, these siRNAs resulted in unintended co-knockdown of the Clec4e-RI isoform (Supplementary Fig. S3), precluding definitive isolation of isoform-specific functions. Visualization of the RNA-seq data clearly showed that as silicosis progressed, the intron retention gradually decreased (Fig. A). We therefore overexpressed Clec4e-RI and Clec4e-nonRI separately in RAW264.7 cells (Fig. B, C). Overexpression of the Clec4e-nonRI isoform significantly increased inflammation, marked by the upregulation of Il-1α and Tnf-α (Fig. D, E). In contrast, overexpression of the Clec4e-RI isoform showed a suppressive effect (Fig. D, E). Together, we showed that Clec4e-RI and Clec4e-nonRI play distinct roles in silicosis-associated inflammation. Silica stimulation downregulates the anti-inflammatory isoform Clec4e-RI, shifting the splicing balance towards higher relative abundance of the pro-inflammatory isoform Clec4e-nonRI, thereby triggering and amplifying the inflammatory response.
 

## In silico prediction of the molecular features and potential function of Clec4e-RI
To further elucidate the molecular characteristics and potential biological function of the Clec4e-RI event, we conducted a comprehensive series of in silico analyses focusing on transcript stability, structural truncation, and protein interactions (Fig. , Method). First, regarding mRNA stability, RNAfold thermodynamic predictions yielded a MFE of −581.75 kcal/mol for Clec4e-RI and −641.30 kcal/mol for Clec4e-nonRI (Fig. A-B). To account for differences in sequence length, we normalized these values (AMFE = MFE/length). The resulting AMFE for Clec4e-nonRI and Clec4e-RI were −0.255 kcal/mol/nt and −0.260 kcal/mol/nt, respectively. This negligible difference suggests that intron retention of Clec4e does not significantly compromise the structural stability of the mRNA. At the protein level, ORFfinder sequence analysis revealed that the RI introduces a premature termination codon (PTC), leading to a truncated protein of 83 amino acids (aa) compared to the full-length 214 aa (Fig. C). This truncation directly impacts the domain composition; InterPro domain searching predicted the complete loss of the critical ligand-binding C-type lectin domain (CTLD) in Clec4e-RI (Fig. D). Notably, despite the absence of the extracellular recognition domain, both TMHMM and DeepLoc-2.0 consistently predicted that the transmembrane domain and cell membrane localization of Clec4e-RI remain intact (Fig. E-F). This finding suggests that while Clec4e-RI is incapable of ligand recognition, it remains anchored to the cell membrane, providing the structural prerequisite for competitive interactions with Clec4e-nonRI at the membrane level. Based on these structural features, we further explored the potential interaction between Clec4e-RI and downstream signaling molecules. It is well-established that the canonical Mincle receptor lacks an intracellular ITAM motif and must constitutively associate with the adapter protein FcRγ to form a functional signaling complex [ ]. Molecular docking simulations performed via HDOCK yielded high-confidence models for both isoforms (Fig. G), with Confidence Scores of 0.9806 and 0.9754 for nonRI and RI, respectively. According to HDOCK criteria, a Confidence Score above 0.7 indicates a high probability of binding, typically derived from docking scores of −200 or lower. In our study, both Clec4e-nonRI (−346.09) and Clec4e-RI (−334.06) achieved scores significantly more favorable than this −200 benchmark, suggesting that both isoforms may possess a robust structural basis for stable association with FcRγ (Additional file: Table S6). Although the nonRI variant showed a slightly more favorable docking score, the truncated RI isoform maintained a comparable binding affinity. Collectively, our evidence suggests that Clec4e-RI can localize to the cell membrane and bind to FcRγ, yet fails to recognize ligands due to the missing CTLD. Consequently, we propose that Clec4e-RI functions as a dominant-negative regulator. By competitively sequestering the limited pool of available FcRγ, Clec4e-RI may hinder the assembly of functional Mincle-FcRγ signaling complexes, thereby dampening downstream inflammatory signaling. This discovery may reveal a sophisticated regulatory mechanism of innate immunity through AS and provides a new perspective for understanding the dysregulation of the Mincle pathway in inflammatory diseases such as silicosis.
 

## Discussion
AS is increasingly recognized as a fundamental modulator of disease pathogenesis across various organ systems. Beyond representing a byproduct of dysregulated transcription, specific isoform switching often influences critical cellular responses. Aberrant AS regulation has been implicated in various conditions, including cancer [ ], neurodegenerative disorders [ , ], cardiovascular diseases [ , ], and fibrotic lung diseases. Within the pulmonary field, studies have primarily focused on IPF, identifying extensive isoform dysregulation in fibrotic lung tissues. For example, specific splicing alterations in genes encoding ECM proteins, such as the exclusion of exon 21 in POSTN and exon 4 in COL6A3, are closely associated with tissue remodeling [ ]. Furthermore, splicing factors including QKI, ESRP1, and RBFOX family members have been recognized as important modulators in Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and fibroblast activation [ , ]. By regulating the isoform switching of genes like CD44 and FGFR2, these factors participate in TGF-β mediated pathological processes [ , ].
Compared to the growing body of AS research in IPF, the investigation of AS in silicosis remains limited. While IPF is characterized by an obscure etiology and spontaneous progression, silicosis is triggered by a defined environmental exposure (SiO2) and is accompanied by a prominent, persistent early inflammatory phase driven by myeloid cells. In this study, we systematically delineated the dynamic alterations of AS events throughout the progression of silicosis. Our analysis showed that approximately 72% of genes exhibited multiple isoforms (Fig. B), with extensive AS events occurring not only in DEGs but also in a substantial number of non-DEGs. This observation is informative (Fig. D), implying that standard transcriptomic studies relying solely on gene abundance might overlook key molecules functionally regulated via AS mechanisms. While their total transcript levels remain stable, the shifting ratios of their isoforms may delicately modulate protein functions to facilitate disease progression.
The significant AS reprogramming observed during silicosis progression predominantly involves cytoskeletal organization and signal transduction systems (Fig. B). Splicing alterations within the actin filament bundle assembly pathway, involving genes such as Add3, Tpm1, and Tns3 (among the 78 gene independent DEAS, Additional file: Table S5), may provide a crucial structural framework for cell migration and myofibroblast activation [ ]. For instance, isoform switching of Tpm1 has been reported to modulate actin filament rigidity, a process that is closely associated with how fibroblasts sense tissue stiffness and subsequently polarize into profibrotic myofibroblasts [ ]. Simultaneously, the AS remodeling of the small GTPase signaling pathway appears to amplify pathological pro-fibrotic signaling. This pathway plays a central role in regulating actin rearrangement and the transcription of pro-fibrotic genes [ ]. Specifically, Arhgef1 (p115-RhoGEF, among the 78 gene independent DEAS, Additional file: Table S5) serves as a specific activator of RhoA, and its domain integrity is vital for regulating interaction kinetics with Gα13 and downstream signaling intensity [ ]. AS variants of Arhgef1 might alter its catalytic activity, protein–protein interactions, or subcellular localization. This could subsequently influence the spatiotemporal activation of RhoA signaling, potentially promoting actin rearrangement and profibrotic transcription. Further validation is warranted to dissect the exact functional consequences of these specific splice isoforms. Furthermore, the identified parental gene of AS events also encompass fibrosis-associated signaling molecules such as Tgfb1i1 and Fgfr3 (among the 78 gene independent DEAS, Additional file: Table S5) [ , ]. Together, these alterations suggest that AS reprogramming might act synergistically with cytoskeletal remodeling and growth factor signaling to facilitate silica-induced pulmonary structural changes. These findings indicate that AS is not merely supplementary to overall gene expression, but may represent a vital regulatory layer that influences the fibrotic process through expanding protein functional diversity.
By integrating bulk tissue data with single-cell transcriptomic data, we successfully resolved AS signals to specific cell populations, focusing on myeloid cells, which are the key effector cells in silicosis pathogenesis. In vivo experiments confirmed that the alteration trends of the Clec4e-RI, Tns3-AFE, and Wdr37-AFE events were highly consistent with bioinformatic predictions (Fig. B-D). However, the Tns3 and Wdr37-AFE events could not be stably detected in the in vitro macrophage model (Fig. H-I), suggesting that their splicing variations might be highly dependent on the complex in vivo microenvironment or specific tissue-resident cell states. Ultimately, we selected the Clec4e-RI event, which exhibited consistent cell-intrinsic responses, for functional assays. Overexpression experiments in macrophages laid a foundation for elucidating how this specific splicing variant precisely regulates inflammatory signaling pathways (Fig. B-E). This suggests that prior to the massive activation of fibroblasts, AS-mediated post-transcriptional reprogramming in macrophages may serve as an early responsive mechanism to dust exposure.
Although our study provides novel insights into AS reprogramming in silicosis, several limitations must be acknowledged. Primarily, due to constraints in experimental resources, the sample size for bulk RNA-seq (18 mouse samples: 3 controls, 8 early-stage silicosis, and 7 late-stage silicosis) is relatively limited, which inevitably restricts statistical power. Given these constraints, applying strict False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction would significantly compromise statistical power, potentially resulting in false negatives where meaningful events fail to pass the significance threshold. To address this limitation, we implemented rigorous compensatory measures. First, we focused only on AS events consistently detected across all samples with a mean PSI no less than 0.05. Second, we applied a stringent effect size threshold (absolute ΔPSI > 0.2), which is more conservative than the 0.05 to 0.1 thresholds commonly adopted in similar studies [ , ]. This approach effectively helps filter background noise. Third, we experimentally validated the selected core splicing events via RT-qPCR. Nevertheless, we recognize that the absence of FDR correction may increase the theoretical risk of false positives. Future studies involving larger-scale multicenter cohorts are warranted to further validate the robustness of these splicing signatures.
Additional technical and translational limitations also exist. Our functional characterization was conducted primarily in macrophage cell lines and lacks in vivo representation to fully capture the biological effects of key AS events. Future studies using primary cells, such as AMs derived from BAL, are needed to more accurately elucidate cell-type-specific splicing regulatory networks and their context-dependent roles in silicosis progression. Second, in our functional dissection of Clec4e isoforms, we encountered a common technical challenge inherent to AS research. Specific siRNAs designed to target the Clec4e-nonRI isoform led to unintended co-knockdown of the Clec4e-RI isoform, likely due to the nested architecture of the transcripts. This precluded the definitive isolation of isoform-specific functions via loss-of-function approaches. Future studies should employ more precise molecular tools, such as CRISPR-Cas9 based splice editors or antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) [ , ], essential to fully elucidate the distinct contributions of each splicing variant in silicosis-associated inflammation. Third, our structural insights into the Clec4e isoforms and their interactions with FcRγ are currently based on bioinformatic modeling and molecular docking simulations. While these computational predictions provide a robust theoretical framework for our dominant-negative hypothesis, they require future experimental validation, such as through Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) or Surface Plasmon Resonance (SPR), to definitively confirm the physical association and binding kinetics in a biological context. Finally, translating specific splicing events (e.g., Clec4e-RI) from mice to humans poses inherent challenges. This discrepancy may be attributed to the etiological differences between silica-induced specific immunity and idiopathic fibrosis, as well as the evolutionary divergence of intronic sequences and splicing regulatory elements between species. Technical disparities, such as variations in sequencing read lengths, might influence the capture efficiency of specific junctions. Therefore, while our study highlights the broad significance of AS in pneumoconiosis, future investigations utilizing patient-derived silicosis cohorts with long-read sequencing technologies are warranted to validate the exact human homologs of these critical splicing events.

## Conclusion
In conclusion, our study delineates a complex and dynamic AS landscape in silicosis and offers preliminary evidence for the potential involvement of AS in inflammatory and fibrotic processes. Our observations suggest that the downregulation of Clec4e-RI may shift the transcript ratio toward functional variants, thereby amplifying pro-inflammatory signaling and driving sustained inflammation. These findings provide a novel perspective on the molecular heterogeneity of silicosis and point toward specific AS events that may warrant further investigation as candidate targets for future splicing-based therapeutic interventions.
