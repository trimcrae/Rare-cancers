## Abstract
The brain renin-angiotensin system (RAS) exhibits a dual role in neurodegeneration and cognitive function, the angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)/Ang II/angiotensin type 1 receptor (AT1R) pathway induces nerve injury, while the ACE2/Ang (1–7)/Mas receptor (MasR) pathway exerts protective effects in cerebrovascular disease. Mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum membranes (MAMs)-key regulators of calcium homeostasis and mitochondrial function—are also implicated in neurodegeneration, and previous studies suggest Ang II may reduce MAM levels. Moreover, previous study indicated that Ang II could decrease the level of MAMs. However, whether propofol exerts neuroprotective effects by regulating the RAS and MAMs remains unknown. In this study, we found that the effects of propofol on cognitive function and RAS were opposite to that of other anesthetic drugs, including isoflurane, sevoflurane, chloral hydrate, pentobarbital sodium on cognitive function and RAS in the postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) rats. Propofol treatment maintained the balance between ACE/Ang II/AT1 and ACE2/Ang(1–7) and improved cognitive function. In vitro, propofol inhibited Ang II-induced apoptosis, the increase in excitatory postsynaptic current (EPSC), and the downregulation of synaptophysin and PSD95. Moreover, we found the RAS was closely associated with MAMs, and propofol inhibited the Ang II-induced reduction in MAMs, increase in calcium uptake, and changes in mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mitoROS) and mitochondrial ATP levels. Collectively, this study aimed to investigate the neuroprotective role of propofol in POCD by regulating the RAS and MAMs.

## Introduction
Postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) is defined as a patient’s emerging cognitive impairment after surgery, i.e. impairment affecting orientation, attention, perception, awareness, and judgment [ ]. What’s more, POCD is common, occurring in approximately 10–54% of individuals within the first few weeks postoperatively, and most POCD is less common after 3 months postoperatively [ ]. The damage of POCD not only affects the postoperative recovery of patients, leads to premature incapacity and increases mortality, but also imposes an economic and mental burden on the patient’s family and society, especially in elderly patients. Therefore, finding and intervening the main factors affecting the occurrence of POCD is the key to reduce the incidence and severity of POCD.
Many studies indicate that POCD is considered separate from cognitive decline, which is common not only in the elderly but also in children in the community, and it is referred to as mild cognitive impairment, neurocognitive impairment, or dementia [ , ]. POCD is objectively measurable following anesthesia and surgery [ ], It is generally considered a cognitive impairment but not a formal clinical diagnosis, as it can only be detected via neuropsychological tests. It shows a significant decrease in at least two cognitive domains and persists for at least 2 weeks after surgery (currently more inclined towards 4 weeks after surgery), while excluding delirium, dementia, and amnesia. In animal study, Morris water maze (MWM) test is introduced to measure the spatial learning and long-term potentiation (LTP), the excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSP) slope, can estimate the memory [ ].
It has been recognized for over 100 years that anesthesia influences patient cognitive function [ ]. Propofol (2,6-diisopropylphenol), a widely used intravenous anesthetic, primarily acts as a positive allosteric modulator of the γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptor, enhancing inhibitory neurotransmission [ ]. Previous studies suggest anesthetic choice affects POCD risk. A single-blinded randomized controlled study investigated the effects of isoflurane, sevoflurane, and propofol on POCD [ ], and a Cochrane review reported that propofol-based total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) is associated with a reduced risk of POCD in elderly patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery compared to inhalational agents [ ]. Additionally, propofol provided better cognitive scores than isoflurane and sevoflurane in patients undergoing transnasal pituitary adenomectomy [ ]. Beyond its anesthetic properties, propofol exhibits significant neuroprotective effects relevant to POCD, including attenuating neuroinflammation, reducing oxidative stress, and preserving mitochondrial function [ , ]. However, the mechanism by which propofol exerts POCD protection—especially its potential link to RAS and MAMs—and how it differs from other anesthetics remain unelucidated.
Notably, the renin-angiotensin system (RAS) and mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum membranes (MAMs) have been identified as key regulators in neurodegenerative processes, which may be closely associated with the pathogenesis of POCD. The RAS is a complex network composed of enzymes and peptides, whose primary physiological roles are the regulation of blood pressure, electrolyte balance, and fluid homeostasis. It mainly consists of two mutually antagonistic axes: the classical (systemic) RAS axis and the non-classical RAS axis. The classical axis, also known as the ACE/Angiotensin II/AT1R axis, initiates with renin secreted by the renal juxtaglomerular apparatus—which cleaves angiotensinogen (AGT) to generate angiotensin I (Ang I). Subsequently, Ang I is converted to angiotensin II (Ang II) by angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), an enzyme predominantly expressed in lung endothelial cells; Ang II then binds to the angiotensin type 1 receptor (AT1R) to mediate pro-inflammatory, pro-oxidative, and neurotoxic effects [ ]. In contrast, the non-classical axis (ACE2/Angiotensin(1–7)/MasR axis) exerts protective effects: angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) hydrolyzes Angiotensin II to produce angiotensin (1–7) [Ang(1–7)], and Ang(1–7) binds to the Mas receptor (MasR), thereby inhibiting inflammation, reducing oxidative stress, and protecting neurons [ , ]. Specifically in the context of neurodegeneration, the ACE2/Ang(1–7)/Mas axis has been shown to protect mitochondrial function by reducing mitochondrial-derived oxidative stress and improving bioenergetics in models of Alzheimer’s Disease [ , ], a condition sharing pathological hallmarks with POCD. Evidence from human AD brain tissue further supports the therapeutic potential of this axis [ ].
As specialized substructures that form physical and functional connections between the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondria, MAMs play a crucial role in maintaining inter-organelle communication [ ]. They regulate a series of key cellular processes, including calcium ion transfer between the ER and mitochondria, mitochondrial adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis, reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging, and cell apoptosis—all of which are essential for the survival and function of neurons [ , ]. Dysfunction of MAMs disrupts calcium ion homeostasis and mitochondrial function, thereby leading to neuronal damage and neurodegeneration [ ].
Considering anesthetic drugs may affect POCD, in our study, we introduce different anesthetic drugs, including propofol, isoflurane, sevoflurane, chloral hydrate, pentobarbital sodium, to detect their effect on POCD, and select the best performing one of those anesthetic drugs to explore the potential mechanism of it in POCD.

## Materials and methods

## POCD rat model
All studies were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IAEC) number: FUSCC-IACUC-S20210036. Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (480–560 g) aged between 18 and 22 months were purchased from Experimental Animal Center of Fudan University. Those rats were divided randomly into six groups: (1) control group (n = 6), (2) propofol (n = 6), (3) isoflurane (n = 6), (4) sevoflurane (n = 6), (5) chloral hydrate (n = 6), and (6) pentobarbital sodium (n = 6). As splenectomy is a validated model for inducing POCD in aged rats, we used this surgical procedure to establish the POCD model in the present study [ , ]. Except for the control group, rats in the other groups underwent splenectomy [ ]. Briefly, after anesthesia using propofol, isoflurane, sevoflurane, chloral hydrate, and pentobarbital sodium. Propofol group: Intraperitoneal injection of propofol (10 mg/kg, 10100027, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) with additional doses of 2 mg/kg as needed to maintain anesthesia depth. Isoflurane group: Inhalation of 1.5−2.0% isoflurane (I9809, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) in 100% oxygen (flow rate 1 L/min). Sevoflurane group: Inhalation of 2.0−2.5% sevoflurane (S2438, Sigma-Aldrich), USA in 100% oxygen (flow rate 1 L/min). Chloral hydrate group: Intraperitoneal injection of 4% chloral hydrate (350 mg/kg, C8267, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Pentobarbital sodium group: Intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg, P3761, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), a 2–4 cm incision was made in the upper left abdomen through the skin and muscle wall, and the spleen was isolated. A 3-silk suture was used to ligate the blood vessels, and the spleen was removed. Finally, a 0-silk suture was used to close the incision. All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. Schematic diagram of the experimental protocol see Fig.  .
 

## Brain tissue sample preparation
At 7 days post-surgery (after completion of the Morris Water Maze test), all rats were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) followed by transcardial perfusion with 0.9% ice-cold saline (to remove blood contaminants) and 4% ice-cold paraformaldehyde (PFA, for fixation). Brains were rapidly dissected on ice, and hippocampi were isolated under a stereomicroscope (Olympus, Japan) using sterile dissecting tools. Isolated hippocampi were divided into three portions for different tests.

## Morris water maze test
Morris water maze test was performed to evaluate the learning and memory ability of rats at 1, 3, 5 and 7 days after splenectomy. The MWM consists of a circular stainless steel pool (150 cm in diameter, 60 cm in height) with distinct visual cues painted on its black inner surface, and it is divided into four quadrants. An escape platform with a diameter of 10 cm was placed in any quadrant, and rats were placed in the water facing the wall. Each rat was trained four times a day. The swimming track was recorded using a camera, and the swimming distance and time were analyzed using the ANY-maze video tracking system (Version 6.3; Stoelting Co., Wood Dale, IL, USA). Training phase (preoperative days 5–2): The escape platform (10 cm in diameter) was submerged 1 cm below the water surface (22 ± 1 °C, opaque with non-toxic white dye). Each day, rats were randomly placed in one of the four quadrants facing the pool wall, with a maximum of 60 s allowed to find the platform. If a rat failed to locate the platform within 60 s, it was guided to the platform and allowed to stay for 10 s to consolidate spatial memory. Each rat completed 4 training trials per day, with a 15-minute interval between trials. To establish a preoperative baseline and confirm that all groups had comparable spatial learning and memory abilities prior to surgical intervention, a probe trial was conducted on preoperative day 1. The escape platform was removed, and rats were placed in the water facing the pool wall in the quadrant opposite to the original platform location, which was designated as the fixed starting point for all probe trials to maintain consistency. The time spent in the target quadrant (where the platform was previously placed) and the number of platform crossings were recorded over 60 s. This baseline assessment ensured that any postoperative cognitive differences observed could be attributed to the effects of anesthesia and surgery rather than pre-existing group disparities. Postoperative evaluation tests (1, 3, 5, 7 days post-surgery): Each postoperative evaluation followed the same probe trial protocol as the preoperative baseline test (platform removed, fixed starting point in the quadrant opposite to the target, 60-second trial) [ , ]. Spatial memory was assessed by three key metrics: (1) the percentage of time spent in the target quadrant, (2) the number of crossings over the former platform location, and (3) the escape latent distance.

## Hippocampal long-term potentiation (LTP) recording
Hippocampal LTP was performed at 7 day after rats underwent splenectomy according to previous study. Fresh hippocampal tissue (without fixation) was immediately transferred to ice-cold artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF, composition: 124 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 26 mM NaHCO3, 10 mM glucose, 2 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgSO4, pH 7.4, bubbled with 95% O2/5% CO2) to maintain neuronal viability. Hippocampal slices (400 μm thick) were prepared using a vibratome (Leica, Germany) within 30 min of dissection, and incubated in ACSF at 32 °C for 1 h before LTP recording [ ]. The basal field excitatory postsynaptic potential (fEPSP) was evoked by continuous single electric stimulus with intensity of elicited 50% of maximal response and frequency of 0.033 Hz, and recorded for 30 min. LTP of fEPSPs was induced by high-frequency (200 Hz) stimulation (HFS) containing 20 pulses. A > 30% increase in the amplitude of fEPSP after HFS was considered successful induction of LTP.

## Detecting the RAS, mitochondrial ROS and mitochondrial ATP
Hippocampal tissue and Neurons was homogenized in ice-cold RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) containing 1% protease inhibitor cocktail using a tissue homogenizer (Thermo Fisher, USA). The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant (total protein extract) was collected. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (23225, Beyotime, China). Ang II ELISA kit (ab108875, Abcam, UK), Ang IV ELISA kit (MBS726716, MyBiosource, USA), Ang(1–7) ELISA kit (E-EL-R0028, Elabscience, China), all used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The activity of ACE and ACE2 in the hippocampal tissues and neurons was detected using ACE Activity Assay Kit (ab155800, Abcam, UK) and ACE2 Activity Assay Kit (MBS706374, MyBiosource, USA), respectively. Mitochondria were isolated from fresh hippocampal tissue using a Mitochondria Isolation Kit for Tissue (C3606, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Hippocampal tissue was minced and homogenized in ice-cold mitochondrial isolation buffer, followed by sequential centrifugation (600×g for 5 min, 7,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C) to collect the mitochondrial pellet. Isolated mitochondria were resuspended in mitochondrial storage buffer, and mitochondrial ROS (using MitoSOX™ Red probe, M36008, Thermo Fisher, USA) and ATP (using ATP Assay Kit, S0026, Beyotime) were detected immediately to avoid mitochondrial dysfunction.

## Isolation of MAMs from neurons
Neurons were processed using a Mitochondria-Associated Endoplasmic Reticulum Membrane Isolation Kit (C3601, Beyotime, China) as described in “Isolation of MAMs from neurons, for TEM, neurons were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde directly in 6-well plates (to preserve MAMs structure) before dehydration and embedding. MAMs were isolated from neurons according to previous study [ ]. Briefly, harvested neurons were twice pelleted by centrifugation at 600 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 7000 g for 20 min to separate crude mitochondria from microsomes and the endoplasmic reticulum fraction. The mitochondria fraction was suspended in 2 ml of mitochondrial re-suspension buffer (pH 7.4), layered on top of 30% percoll medium, and centrifuged at 95,000 g for 30 min. The MAMs were extracted from the percoll gradient and purified by centrifugation to remove contaminated mitochondria. The pure mitochondria was collected from the percoll gradient and centrifuged to obtain the MAMs pellet. The collected MAMs were used to detect the levels of IP3R3, GRP75 and VDAC1 by western blotting.

## Primary culture of hippocampal neurons and treatment
Primary hippocampal neurons were isolated from postnatal day 0 male SD rats as previously described [ ]. Briefly, the hippocampus were dissociated with 0.125% trypsin (25200056, Gibco, USA) under sterile conditions at 37 °C for 10 min, and neurons were maintained in DMEM (11965092, Gibco, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, SH30084.03, HyClone, USA). Then, neurons were seeded into a poly-d-lysine (P6407, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) precoated 6-well plate at a density of 1–2 × 106 cells/well. After 4 h of incubation, the culture medium was replaced with neurobasal medium (21103049, Gibco, USA) with 2% B27 (17504044, Gibco, USA) and 0.5 mM glutamine (25030081, Gibco, USA), and half of the medium was changed every 2–3 days.
Neurons were treated with Ang II (0.1 µM, A9525, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), propofol (50 µM, P8769, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), candesartan (1 µM, C2773, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), perindopril (10 µM, P0187, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), siRNA control (50 nM, sc−37007, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA), and siRNA for GRP75 (si-GRP75, 50 nM, sc−45211, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA). After being treated with Ang II (0.1 µM), propofol (50 µM), candesartan, perindopril, control siRNA, or GRP75 siRNA (si-GRP75) for 6 h, neurons were harvested for subsequent experiments.

## Apoptosis assay
Neurons were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors, centrifuged at 12,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected as total protein extract. Apoptosis was assessed by the Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection kit (C1062S, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Neurons were stained with Annexin V-FITC (20 µg/ml) and propidium iodide (5 µg/ml). Then, cell apoptosis was analyzed by a flow cytometer (Beckman, USA).

## Western blotting
Neurons were lysed in RIPA buffer with protease inhibitors, centrifuged at 12,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected as total protein extract. Western blotting assay was performed as previous study [ ]. The antibodies used were: anti-ACE (ab18995, Abcam, 1:1000), anti-ACE2 (ab15348, Abcam, 1:1000), anti-AT1R (ab124734, Abcam, 1:500), anti-AT2R (ab1804, Abcam, 1:1000), anti-Synaptophysin (ab32127, Abcam, 1:2000), anti-PSD95 (ab2723, Abcam, 1:1000), anti-IP3R3 (ab124287, Abcam, 1:1000), anti-GRP75 (ab2799, Abcam, 1:1000), anti-VDAC1 (ab15895, Abcam, 1:1500), and anti-GAPDH (ab8245, Abcam, 1:5000). The immunoreactive bands were visualized using an BeyoECL Plus reagent (P0018, Beyotime, China).

## Transmission electron microscope
In order to detect the level of mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum membranes (MAMs), transmission electron microscope (TEM) was performed. Neurons were fixed with 4 °C pre-cooled 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 24 h and with 1% osmium tetraoxide for 1 h. Then, neurons were dehydrated for 15 min at a series of acetone concentrations (50%, 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%) and embedded in resin. The samples were sliced and double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and representative images were obtained using electron microscope.

## Euthanasia of animals
At the end of the experiments (7 days after splenectomy), all rats were deeply anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg body weight; P3761, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) prior to sacrifice. This dose ensures a deep anesthetic plane (loss of pedal withdrawal and corneal reflexes) and is consistent with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 edition). After confirming the absence of reflexes, rats were transcardially perfused with 0.9% saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for tissue fixation. Brains were rapidly dissected on ice for subsequent biochemical and histological analyses.

## Statistical analysis
All data were reported as mean ± SD, and GraphPad Prism software (Version 10.6.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used to analyze the data. Before applying parametric tests, the assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity of the data were evaluated. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, which confirmed that the data in each experimental group followed a normal distribution (p > 0.05 for all groups). Homoscedasticity (equality of variances) was verified via Levene’s test, demonstrating no significant differences in variance between groups (p > 0.05 for all comparisons). Based on the confirmation of these assumptions, the appropriate statistical tests were applied. For comparisons between two groups, the two-tailed Student’s t test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups under a single condition (e.g., biochemical assays, Western blotting, and in vitro experiments), one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test was used. For the analysis of longitudinal behavioral data from the Morris water maze test (escape latency, path length, etc.), which involved repeated measures on the same subjects over multiple time points, two-way repeated-measures ANOVA was employed to evaluate the main effects of ‘treatment group’, ‘time’, and their interaction, followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test for specific comparisons at each time point. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Propofol mitigated cognitive deficits and long-term potentiation impairments associated with POCD in aged rats
The Morris water maze test was conducted to investigate the effects of different anesthetic drugs on the cognitive performance of aged rats. Prior to surgery, a baseline probe trial confirmed that all experimental groups exhibited comparable spatial learning and memory abilities, with no significant differences in any measured metrics (for all baseline data, see Supplementary Table 1; p > 0.05, one-way ANOVA).
Postoperative cognitive function was then analyzed using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. This revealed a significant main effect of time across all groups, indicating that learning occurred throughout the 7-day period (Fig.  , p < 0.001). Furthermore, significant main effects of treatment group (F(5, 54) = 28.36, p < 0.001) and a significant group × time interaction (F(15, 162) = 9.74, p < 0.001) were found for all behavioral metrics, indicating that the trajectory of cognitive recovery was dependent on the anesthetic agent.
 Post hoc analysis with Bonferroni’s test confirmed that the propofol group demonstrated superior cognitive performance compared to the other groups (for full post hoc data, see Supplementary Table 2). As illustrated in Fig.  , rats in the propofol group consistently showed the shortest escape latencies and distances (Fig.  A, D), the most platform crossings (Fig.  B), and spent the highest percentage of time in the target quadrant (Fig.  C). The figures specifically annotate the performance relative to the Control group to highlight this advantage: the propofol group’s performance was significantly better on Day 1 (p < 0.001), and while this trend continued, the difference between these two specific groups was no longer statistically significant by Day 7. Collectively, these data demonstrate that propofol markedly attenuated POCD-induced cognitive impairment in aged rats.
Given that LTP is a critical cellular mechanism underlying learning and memory, we conducted electrophysiological recordings to investigate the fEPSP slopes in the hippocampal CA1 region. As shown in Fig.  E, the fEPSP slope was significantly reduced in the isoflurane, sevoflurane, chloral hydrate, and pentobarbital sodium groups compared to the control group (p < 0.01, one-way ANOVA), indicating that these anesthetics induced substantial LTP impairment. In contrast, the fEPSP slope in the propofol group was not significantly different from the control group (p > 0.05) and was significantly increased compared to all other anesthetic groups (p < 0.01), suggesting a preservation of synaptic plasticity.

## The effects of different anesthetic drugs on RAS in the POCD aged rats
Elisa was used to detect the levels of Ang II, Ang IV and Ang(1–7), and the activity of ACE and ACE2 in the hippocampal tissues. Lower Ang II levels were detected in the propofol group compared to the control group,, though this difference did not reach statistical significance, whereas higher Ang II levels were observed in the other anesthetic groups (isoflurane, sevoflurane, chloral hydrate, and pentobarbital sodium) relative to the control group (Fig.  A). The levels of Ang IV (Fig.  B) and Ang (1–7) (Fig.  C) showed opposite trend with the levels of Ang II in each group. For activity of ACE and ACE2, we found lower activity of ACE (Fig.  D) and higher activity of ACE2 (Fig.  E) in propofol group than that in the control group, while other anesthetic drug groups increased ACE activity and decreased ACE2 activity. Moreover, western blotting was used to detect the change of RAS indicators, containing ACE, AT1R, ACE2, and AT2R. We found that propofol decreased the levels of ACE and AT1R, and increased the levels of ACE2 and AT2R, while other anesthetic drugs increased the levels of ACE and AT1R, and decreased the levels of ACE2 and AT2R (Fig.  F).
 

## The neuroprotective effects of propofol on Ang II-induced neuronal injury
In order to investigate whether propofol exerted the neuroprotective effects by regulating RAS system, we cultured hippocampal neurons. We used MAP2 staining to identify neurons (Fig.  A), and the neurons were treated with Ang II, propofol, and inhibitors of RAS (candesartan and perindopril). For EPSC amplitude, we found that propofol, as well as candesartan and perindopril, could weaken Ang II-induced the increase of EPSC amplitude (Fig.  B). We found that propofol, candesartan and perindopril improved Ang II-induced decrease of neuronal survival (Fig.  C) and increase of neuronal apoptosis (Fig.  D). Those data suggested that Ang II could induce neuron injury, and propofol, candesartan and perindopril could weaken the effects of Ang II on neurons. Further, we detected the effects of propofol on RAS in the primary cultured neurons. We found that Ang II increased ACE activity and decreased ACE2 activity, whereas propofol, candesartan, and perindopril reversed these Ang II-induced changes in ACE and ACE2 activity (Figs.  B and A). Western blotting was used to detect the levels of AT1R and AT2R, and we found that Ang II up-regulated AT1R and down-regulated AT2R, while propofol, candesartan and perindopril inhibited Ang II-induced change of the AT1R and AT2R (Fig.  C). Moreover, synaptophysin and PSD95, proteins associated with synaptic function, were detected using western blotting. We found that Ang II down-regulated synaptophysin and PSD95, while propofol, candesartan and perindopril inhibited Ang II-induced change of the synaptophysin and PSD95 (Fig.  C).
 

## Propofol inhibited Ang II-induced neuronal injury through mediating MAMs
The neurons treated with Ang II showed less MAMs than control neurons (Fig.  A), and the neurons treated with Ang II and propofol increased the level of MAMs, indicating that propofol inhibited Ang II-induced inhibition of MAMs formation. After we inhibited the level of GRP75, an important protein associated with MAMs, the level of MAMs was decreased (Fig.  A). Western blotting was used to detect the levels of MAMs-associated proteins, GRP75, IP3R3 and VDAC1. We found lower level of GRP75, IP3R3 and VDAC1 in the Ang II group compared with control group, and propofol increased the levels of GRP75, IP3R3 and VDAC1, while si-GRP75 weakened the effects of propofol on those indexes (Fig.  B). Also, Ang II decreased mitochondrial calcium uptake (Fig.  C), mitochondrial ROS (Fig.  D) and mitochondrial ATP (Fig.  E), and propofol decreased the levels of those indices, while si-GRP75 weakened the effects of propofol on those indexes in the Ang II treated neurons. We found that propofol improved Ang II-induced decrease of neuronal survival, while si-GRP75 weakened the effects of propofol on neuronal survival (Fig.  A). For EPSC amplitude, we found that propofol could inhibit Ang II-induced increase of EPSC amplitude, while si-GRP75 weakened the effects of propofol on EPSC amplitude (Fig.  B). Moreover, we found that propofol improved Ang II-induced increase of neuronal apoptosis, while si-GRP75 weakened the effects of propofol on neuronal apoptosis (Fig.  D). Those data suggested that propofol inhibited Ang II-induced neuronal injury through mediating MAMs.
 

## Discussion
A randomized controlled preliminary trial comparing dexmedetomidine, propofol, and midazolam revealed that elderly patients in the propofol group achieved better Associative Learning Test and Stroop Color and Word Test (SCWT) scores, demonstrating a significant advantage for propofol sedation in reducing the incidence of POCD [ ]. Further supporting this, a systematic review concluded that propofol is generally associated with improved postoperative cognitive outcomes [ ], and ongoing clinical trials are investigating optimal administration strategies, such as closed-loop versus manual infusion, to further mitigate the risk of POCD [ ]. Collectively, these studies indicate that propofol could reduce the occurrence of POCD. In our study, we also observed that aged rats in the propofol group had less cognition impairment compared with isoflurane, sevoflurane, chloral hydrate, and sodium pentobarbital, implying that propofol may alleviate POCD more effectively than the other anesthetic drugs we presented in this study. In addition, we found that the levels or activity of Ang II, Ang IV and Ang (1–7), ACE, AT1R, AT2R and ACE2 were changed in the hippocampal tissues and hippocampal neurons treated with propofol, suggesting that RAS might be involved in the neuroprotective effects of propofol on the POCD rats.
The RAS has been reported to be involved in the consolidation of normal memories and the restoration of disrupted memories. Previous studies have shown that potent analogs targeting the RAS can reduce neuronal apoptosis and promote neuronal function related to memory processes. Notably, Ang IV and Ang (1–7)—key components of the RAS—specifically counteract the neuronal damage and memory impairment mediated by the overactivation of ACE/Ang II/AT1R [ ]. There is strong evidence that excessive levels of the brain ACE/Ang II/AT1R axis are primarily involved in apoptosis, and that blocking AT1R has a strong neuroprotective effect in these brain disorders. Furthermore, ACE2/Ang (1–7)/mas receptor (MASR) is another axis of the brain RAS that counteracts the damaging effects of the ACE/AngII/AT1R axis on brain neurons. Therefore, activation of AngII receptor blockers (ARBs) and the ACE2/Ang(1–7)/MASR axis may be an exciting new approach to neuroprotection [ , ]. Lines reported that the ACE2/Ang(1–7)/Mas axis suppressed cognitive deficits in AD and that overactivation of ACE/Ang2/AT1R axis triggered amyloid beta-induced apoptosis and neurodegeneration in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [ ]. It revealed that Angiopoietin−1 (Ang−1) is a new neuroprotective drug, which can protect neurons from apoptosis [ ]. Ang-(1–7) could promote cell viability, reduce cell apoptosis [ ]. Those proved that Ang exerts neuroprotective effects by inhibiting neuronal apoptosis [ ]. It also meant RAS might participate in the regulation of POCD after propofol anesthetic. In the RAS, We focused the AngII on POCD after propofol anesthetic. According to the studies, which demonstrated that neuronal apoptosis can exacerbate POCD, while inhibition of neuronal apoptosis can reduce POCD [ , ]. AngII could induce apoptosis [ – ], while propofol could allevate apoptosis [ ]. Whether and how the propofol could inhibit AngII and alleviate apoptosis requires further investigate.
Studies demonstrated that propofol inhibits AngII-induced Ca2 + movement in a concentration-dependent manner [ ]. Ang II induces a rapid increase in Ca2 + in cultured vascular smooth muscle cells, which can be inhibited by propofol [ ]. More importantly, Ca2 + has been known to be a player in the apoptotic pathway. It is known that mitochondria can regulate and synchronize Ca2 + signaling. A large accumulation of Ca2 + in mitochondria can promote apoptosis. More importantly, Ca2 + dynamics in the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria appear to be regulated by the Bcl−2, which is a key protein involved in apoptosis [ ]. As the largest intracellular Ca2 + store, the endoplasmic reticulum maintains Ca2 + homeostasis through the endoplasmic reticulum Ca2 + uptake pump, Ca2 + ATPase, Ca2 + release channels, and Ca2 + binding proteins in the endoplasmic reticulum lumen. The imbalance of endoplasmic reticulum Ca2 + homeostasis could lead to apoptosis [ ].
We know that Ca2 + homeostasis is associated with the endoplasmic reticulum, and here we have to mention an important calcium regulator, MAMs, which are specialized endoplasmic reticulum subdomains that form physical interactions with the mitochondrial surface to coordinate a number of key cellular functions, and in which four major classes of proteins play inportant role in endoplasmic reticulum. endoplasmic reticulum transmits appropriate Ca2 + signals to mitochondria, which decode them into specific inputs to regulate apoptosis [ ]. The main pathways for Ca2 + storage from the endoplasmic reticulum to mitochondria include Ca2 + release from inositol IP3Rs and import into mitochondria via VDAC proteins. Ca2 + is required for mitochondrial ATP production, owing to dehydrogenases in the TCA cycle are Ca2 + dependent. Ca2 + plays a key player in triggering synaptic transmission on account of the presynaptic Ca2 + concentrations control neurotransmitter release, and changes in dendritic Ca2 + levels regulate synaptic activity, and influencing EPSP [ ]. A large accumulation of Ca2 + in endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria not only can lead to apoptosis but also regulate synaptic activity and then influencing EPSP. We hypothesize that propofol may inhibit the Ang II-induced Ca2 + flux from endoplasmic reticulum-derived MAMs to mitochondria (a process mediated by RAS), which leading to the imbalance of Ca2 + endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondrial, thereby inhibiting apoptosis and synaptic activity, and further protect POCD.
Notably, the RAS, and calcium signaling not only generally regulate neurodegenerative processes but also play context-specific roles in the pathogenesis of POCD [ , ]. Surgical trauma and anesthesia-induced stress disrupt the balance of RAS in specific regions of the hippocampus [ ]. This overactivation exacerbates neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neuronal apoptosis—consistent with our findings that the levels of Ang II, ACE activity, and AT1R in the isoflurane/sevoflurane groups were higher than those in the propofol group. This RAS imbalance directly impairs hippocampal synaptic plasticity, as evidenced by the reduced expression of Synaptophysin/PSD95 and abnormal EPSP in Ang II-treated neurons. This impairment represents a key pathological feature of POCD and underlies the development of learning and memory deficits.
As critical mediators of crosstalk between the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondria, MAMs are particularly vulnerable in POCD [ ]. Our results demonstrated that Ang II—a driver of POCD-related RAS dysfunction—reduces MAMs formation and disrupts calcium homeostasis, leading to excessive mitochondrial calcium uptake, increased mitoROS, and abnormal mitochondrial ATP production. In the context of POCD, this MAMs dysfunction further impairs neuronal energy metabolism and exacerbates synaptic damage, as hippocampal neurons rely on intact ER-mitochondrial calcium transfer to maintain synaptic transmission and LTP.
Propofol exerts its neuroprotective effects against POCD by directly targeting these pathological cascades [ ]. At the RAS level, propofol restores the balance between the ACE/Ang II/AT1R axis and the ACE2/Ang(1–7)/MasR axis. Our findings showed that in the propofol group, the levels of Ang II/ACE/AT1R were decreased, while those of Ang(1–7)/ACE2/AT2R were increased. This restoration inhibits Ang II-induced neuronal apoptosis and synaptic loss, directly reversing RAS-mediated hippocampal damage in POCD. Additionally, propofol upregulates GRP75—a core MAMs scaffolding protein—to counteract Ang II-induced reduction in MAMs, thereby normalizing ER-mitochondrial calcium transfer, mitigating excessive mitoROS production, and restoring mitochondrial ATP synthesis [ ]. Our in vitro data confirmed that propofol rescues MAMs markers and mitochondrial function. Critically, when GRP75 was silenced, the protective effects of propofol on neuronal survival, EPSP amplitude, and apoptosis were abrogated—confirming that the regulation of MAMs is a key molecular target of propofol in POCD.
Our research revealed that among those anesthetic drugs, including propofol, isoflurane, sevoflurane, chloral hydrate, pentobarbital sodium, propofol could protect alleviate POCD, the effect and potential mechanisms of propofol on POCD is that, propofol may inhibite Ca2 + flow from endoplasmic reticulum MAMs to mitochondria caused by AngII in the RAS, which leading to endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondrial Ca2 + imbalance, thereby inhibiting apoptosis and synaptic activity.
Collectively, these findings indicate that the pathogenesis of POCD involves dysfunction of the “RAS-MAMs-calcium signaling” axis in the hippocampus, and propofol alleviates POCD by targeting two central nodes: (1) restoring RAS balance to inhibit pro-apoptotic/neurotoxic pathways; and (2) preserving MAMs integrity to maintain ER-mitochondrial crosstalk and calcium homeostasis. Both of these mechanisms are supported by our in vivo and in vitro study results.
