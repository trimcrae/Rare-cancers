## Abstract
Several studies have shown that activation of unfolded protein response and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress plays a crucial role in severe cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury. Autophagy occurs within hours after cerebral ischemia, but the relationship between ER stress and autophagy remains unclear. In this study, we established experimental models using oxygen-glucose deprivation/reoxygenation in PC12 cells and primary neurons to simulate cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury. We found that prolongation of oxygen-glucose deprivation activated the ER stress pathway protein kinase-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK)/eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 subunit alpha (eIF2α)-activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4)-C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP), increased neuronal apoptosis, and induced autophagy. Furthermore, inhibition of ER stress using inhibitors or by siRNA knockdown of the PERK gene significantly attenuated excessive autophagy and neuronal apoptosis, indicating an interaction between autophagy and ER stress and suggesting PERK as an essential target for regulating autophagy. Blocking autophagy with chloroquine exacerbated ER stress-induced apoptosis, indicating that normal levels of autophagy play a protective role in neuronal injury following cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury. Findings from this study indicate that cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury can trigger neuronal ER stress and promote autophagy, and suggest that PERK is a possible target for inhibiting excessive autophagy in cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury.

## Introduction
Cerebral ischemia results in irreversible neuronal damage and is frequently associated with elevated mortality rates, increased patient disability, and various clinicopathological manifestations (Johnston et al., 2009). Restoring cerebral blood flow through clinical intervention or thrombolytic agents often gives rise to cerebral ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury (Lancelot and Zimmer, 2010; Chomova and Zitnanova, 2016). Although I/R is essential for salvaging compromised brain tissue, it can also cause extensive neuronal damage. Upon I/R, a series of pathophysiological events is initiated in the infarct area, including peripheral inflammatory cell infiltration, microglial cell activation, and excessive production of inflammatory mediators and reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Huang et al., 2023; Qiu et al., 2023; Zhai et al., 2023). These events ultimately lead to the accumulation of misfolded and unfolded proteins within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) lumen, thereby exacerbating ER stress (Abolhasanpour et al., 2021). Oxidative stress caused by ROS plays a crucial role in the basic pathological progression of brain injury in ischemic stroke (Kahles and Brandes, 2012). It leads to cytotoxicity through oxidative damage of lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids with harmful consequences for the structure and function of cerebral tissue (Allen and Bayraktutan, 2009; Nathan and Ding, 2010). ROS also cause ER stress, which in turn can exacerbate ROS production and contribute to neuronal death after ischemia (Chomova and Zitnanova, 2016; Siket, 2016; Huang et al., 2019).
ER stress initiates the unfolded protein response (UPR) and activates autophagy through both the protein kinase RNA-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK) pathway and the inositol-requiring kinase 1 (IRE1) pathway (Gupta et al., 2016). Autophagy, a highly conserved catabolic process, is essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis by degrading and recycling surplus or damaged proteins and organelles via autophagosome formation and lysosomal degradation (Gatica et al., 2015). Autophagy has been found to play a crucial role in the pathological processes of cerebral I/R injury (Huang et al., 2019; Abolhasanpour et al., 2021; Noh et al., 2023). During the early reperfusion period, autophagy exerts a protective function by clearing damaged proteins and organelles. However, in the late reperfusion phase, autophagy activation results in autophagic cell death, causing cellular damage and ultimately leading to apoptosis (Huang et al., 2019). Consequently, modulating autophagy in the early reperfusion period presents a promising therapeutic strategy for treating I/R injury.
In SH-SY5Y cells, protective autophagy is activated to mitigate ER stress-mediated apoptosis. Gene knockdown experiments suggest that ER stress may regulate autophagy through the PERK/eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 subunit alpha (eIF2α)/activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4) signaling pathway (Liu and Zhang, 2020). Moreover, studies indicate that oxygen-glucose deprivation and reperfusion (OGD/R) treatment, which simulates cerebral I/R injury, results in mitochondrial damage and increased autophagy in SH-SY5Y cells (Park et al., 2018; Zeng et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2023). Pheochromocytoma cells (PC12) and primary hippocampal neurons, which possess properties of sympathetic neurons, are extensively used as cellular models for neurological diseases, including cerebral I/R injury (Hillion et al., 2005; Lahiani et al., 2018; Wiatrak et al., 2020). However, the relationship and regulatory mechanisms between ER stress and autophagy under OGD/R conditions in PC12 cells and primary hippocampal neurons remain largely unexplored (Song et al., 2017).
In the present study, we used OGD/R-treated PC12 cells and primary hippocampal neurons to examine the signaling pathways and regulatory mechanisms underlying ER stress-induced autophagy and apoptosis and the effect of autophagy on PERK signaling pathways and apoptosis. This study may shed light on the mechanisms of cerebral I/R injury and identify potential therapeutic targets.

## Methods

## Construction of an oxygen-glucose deprivation and reperfusion model
The PC12 cells (No. 108154, Cat# TCR9, RRID: CVCL_0481, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China) used in this study were acquired from the Shanghai Institute of Life Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The OGD/R model was constructed in PC12 cells as follows. Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat# 11966025,) in a CO2 incubator (air:CO2 = 95:5) until they reached 80% confluence. The medium was removed, and glucose and serum-free fresh DMEM (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA, Cat# 11966025) were added. The cells were subsequently incubated in low oxygen conditions (O2:CO2:N2 = 1:5:94) for 3, 6, 9, or 12 hours to induce OGD (Wang et al., 2019). After OGD, glucose and serum-free DMEM were replaced with regular DMEM supplemented with 100 µg/mL streptomycin, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 10% fetal bovine serum. Cells were then subjected to reperfusion at 37°C for 24 hours under air conditions of CO2:air = 5:95.
To evaluate the effects of inhibitors on the OGD/R model, cells subjected to 9 hours of OGD and 24 hours of reperfusion (9-hour/24-hour OGD/R treatment) were treated with either 100 nM of the PERK inhibitor GSK260641 (Merck, St. Louis, MO, USA, Cat# 516535) or 10 µM of the autophagy inhibitor chloroquine (CQ; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA, Cat# c6628) for 24 hours under air conditions of CO2:air = 5:95.

## Western blot analysis
To extract total cellular proteins, the cells were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors (Solarbio, Beijing, China, Cat# P0100) for 40 minutes on ice. The lysates were centrifuged at 13,400 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C. The protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid assay (Thermo, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat# 23227). In preparation for western blot analysis, a mixture of denatured total proteins (30 µg per well) and loading buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, Cat# P0015) was generated by boiling for 5 minutes. After centrifugation at 13,400 × g for 1 minute, the samples were loaded onto polyacrylamide gels.
The following primary antibodies were used (incubation for 12–14 hours at 4°C): glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78; monoclonal rabbit antibody, 1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK, Cat# ab21685, RRID: AB_2119834), microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3 (LC3; monoclonal rabbit antibody, 1:1000, CST, Beverly, MA, USA, Cat# 12741, RRID: AB_2617131), autophagy-related 6 homolog (ATG6/Beclin1; monoclonal rabbit antibody, 1:1000, CST, Cat# 3495, RRID: AB_1903911), sequestosome 1 (p62, monoclonal rabbit antibody, 1:1000, CST, Cat# 232142, AB_2798858), ATF4 (monoclonal rabbit antibody, 1:1000, CST, Cat# 11815, RRID: AB_2616025), PERK (monoclonal rabbit antibody, 1:1000, CST, Cat# 3192, RRID:AB_2095847), phosphorylation of PERK (p-PERK; monoclonal rabbit antibody, 1:1000, CST, Cat# 3179, RRID: AB_2095853), phosphorylation of eIF2α (p-eIF2α; monoclonal rabbit antibody, 1:1000, CST, Cat # 3398, RRID: AB_2096481), eIF2α (monoclonal rabbit antibody, 1:1000, CST, Cat# 5234, RRID: AB_10692650), beta actin (monoclonal rabbit antibody, 1:1000, CST, Cat# 4790, RRID: AB_330288), C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP; polyclonal rabbit antibody, 1:1000, Absin, Shanghai, China, Cat# abs135545, RRID: AB_2838000), B-cell leukemia/lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2; polyclonal rabbit antibody, 1:1000, Absin, Cat# abs131701, RRID: AB_2835021), and Bax (monoclonal rabbit antibody, 1:1000, Abcam, Cat# ab32503, RRID: AB_725631). Secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase anti-rabbit (1:3000, CST, Cat# 7074, RRID: AB_2099233) was incubated for 1 hour at 19°C. Gel electrophoresis was performed at a total of 30 µg per well. The Affinity TMECL kit (femtogram, Cat# KF8003, Affinity Biosciences, Cincinnati, OH, USA) was used to detect protein concentration. Images were captured using a chemiluminescent detection system (GeneGnome XRQ NPC, SYNGENE, Cambridge, UK).

## Immunofluorescence staining
PC12 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and subjected to OGD/R and immunofluorescent observation. The cells were thoroughly washed using prechilled phosphate buffer saline (PBS) three times for 5 minutes each. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 20 minutes. After three washes with PBS for 5 minutes each, the cells were permeabilized with 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 minutes. The coverslips holding the cells were washed twice with PBS and then blocked with 5% goat serum in PBS at room temperature (19°C) for 1 hour. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies against GRP78 (1:200) and LC3 (1:100) at 4°C overnight. The goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody Alexa-FluorTM 488 (1:500, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA, Cat# A11034, RRID: AB2576217) was used to detect GRP78 (1:300, incubated at 25°C for 1 hour), and the goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody Alexa-FluorTM 568 (Invitrogen, Cat# A11036, RRID: AB10563566) was used to detect LC3 (1:200, incubated at 25°C for 1 hour). The cell nuclei were stained with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Solarbio, Cat# c0065). The immunofluorescent images were captured using laser confocal microscopy (Olympus SpinSR10, Tokyo, Japan).

## Cell line and cultivation conditions
The cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (ThermoFisher, Cat# 10091148), 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 100 U/mL penicillin, and incubated at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. At 80% confluence, the culture medium was replaced with glucose-free DMEM to induce metabolic stress.

## Knockdown of the PERK gene by small interfering RNA
Ribobio (Guangzhou, China) synthesized and designed a small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting the PERK gene with a sequence of 5′-GAA CGA CAT TAA ACA CTC A-3′. PC12 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a final concentration of 1 × 105 cells/well. Once the cells had reached a confluence of 40%–50% in the 6-well plate, they were transfected with siRNA using riboFECTTM CP transfection reagents (Ribobio, Cat# C10511-05). The riboFECTTM CP (Ribobio, Cat # R11077.2) incorporates a Cy5 fluorescent label, serving as a negative control to verify the transfection efficiency of riboFECTTM CP, which contains only PERK siRNA without a Cy5 fluorescent label. The working concentration of siRNA was adjusted to 20 nM after 48 hours of siRNA transfection. The cells were then incubated under OGD/R conditions, following the methods described by Wang et al. (2019).

## Flow cytometry
Cell apoptosis was assessed using the Annexin-V and propidium iodide kit (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA, Cat# 556547). The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 1000 × g, washed twice with 1× PBS, digested with ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid-free trypsin, and incubated at 37°C for 1 minute. The digestion was stopped by adding an equal volume of complete medium (DMEM containing 100 µg/mL streptomycin, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 10% fetal bovine serum). The cells were then incubated with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide for 15 minutes at room temperature and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACSVERSEBD, New York, NJ, USA). The experiments were conducted in triplicate (Chen et al., 2018).

## Hoechst33342 staining
The PC12 cells were fixed using a 4% polyformaldehyde solution for 2 minutes and rinsed three times with 1× PBS. An amount of 200 µL of Hoechst33342 dye (Solarbio, Cat# C0030) was added to each well of the 12-well plate for cell incubation at room temperature for 2 minutes. After removal of Hoechst33342 dye, the PC12 cells were washed three times for 3–5 minutes each with 1× PBS. Finally, cellular apoptosis was measured under an epifluorescence microscope (Nikon Ci-E, Tokyo, Japan).

## Transfection of PC12 cells with the adenovirus harboring mRFP-GFP-LC3 cassette
PC12 cells were seeded on a cover slide and placed in a laser confocal cuvette (NEST, Wuxi, China, Cat# 801002) at a concentration of 1 × 105 cells/well. When the cells covered over 40%–50% of the slide, they were transfected with adenovirus carrying the mRFP-GFP-LC3 cassette (HB-AP2100001, Hanheng, Shanghai, China). The multiplicity of infection was 30, and after 8 hours of incubation, the medium with the virus was replaced with fresh medium. When the cells reached 80% confluence, they were subjected to OGD/R treatment. During the reperfusion phase, PC12 cells were subjected to 24 hours of reoxygenation in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and final concentrations of 100 nM GSK2606414 or 10 μM CQ. Autophagy was observed under a confocal microscope (Olympus SpinSR10).

## Autophagic vesicles by transmission electron microscopy
To collect cells, they were washed twice with 1× PBS, digested with 0.1% trypsin for 1 minute, and then centrifuged for 5 minutes at 800 r/min. The cell pellets were washed with 1× PBS and centrifuged at 1200 r/min for 10 minutes. The collected cell pellets were fixed overnight at 4°C with 3% glutaraldehyde. A 5-μL lysate was pipetted onto 200-mesh formvar/carbon-coated copper grids and incubated for approximately 5 minutes. The excess cell suspension was soaked with Whatman filter paper® (Whatman of Maidstone, UK), and grids were allowed to dry overnight. The dry grids were then stained for 10 minutes using a saturated uranyl acetate solution, followed by washing with 50% ethanol and air drying for approximately 3 hours. Imaging was performed at 60 kV using a Hitachi-7500 TEM (Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a digital camera, and images were captured at a magnification of ×245,000 (Ryu et al., 2020).

## Isolation and culture of primary hippocampal neurons
One hundred specific pathogen free-grade Sprague-Dawley rats within 24 hours after birth were obtained from the Guizhou Experimental Animal Center (No. SYXK (Qian)-2018-0001), Guizhou Medical University. The animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Review Committee of Guizhou Medical University (approval No. 1900805, February 27, 2019). All experiments were designed and reported according to the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines (Percie du Sert et al., 2020).
Newborn rats were anesthetized by ether inhalation (99.5% [v/v], Changshu Hongsheng Fine Chemical, Changshu, Jiangsu Province, China, Cat #10009318) for 4–5 minutes, and then sacrificed by immersion in 75% ethanol for 5 minutes. The experiments commenced once they exhibited no signs of vital activity. Rat brains were immersed in prechilled D-Hank’s balanced salt solution (Solarbio, Cat# H1045), and the hippocampus was isolated and cut into pieces with ophthalmic scissors after removal of meninges and blood vessels. After digestion with 0.25% trypsin at 37°C for 15 minutes, DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum was added to terminate the digestion. After washing with D-Hank’s balanced salt solution twice, 2 mL of Neurobasal-A complete medium (Neurobasal A medium containing 2% B27 [Gibco, Cat# 17504044], 1% glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 mg/mL streptomycin) was added to the cells and mixed by pipetting. The cells were then inoculated at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well on poly-L-lysine-coated 6-well plates and placed in an incubator (5% CO2, 95% air, 37°C) (Wang et al., 2020). Half-volume medium changes were performed every 3 days. After 10 days of incubation, the medium was changed to sugar-free DMEM and incubated in an incubator (94% N2, 5% CO2, 1% O2, 37°C) for 2 hours. After that, the medium was changed to Neurobasal-A complete medium with 60 nM iPERK or 5 μM CQ, and the cells were cultured in an incubator for 24 hours with air conditions of 5% CO2, 95% air, 37°C (Wang et al., 2019).
Three independent immunofluorescent double-staining replicates were used to identify the purity of primary hippocampal neurons. Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP; rabbit polyclonal antibody, 1:500, Abcam, Cat# ab7260, RRID: AB_305808) was used as a marker for primary hippocampal neurons. Neuronal nucleus antigen (NeuN; mouse monoclonal antibody, 1:500, Merck-Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany, Cat# MAB377, RRID: AB_2298772) was used as a marker for astrocytes. Antibodies were incubated at 4°C overnight. Sections were washed three times with PBS and stained with the following secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 hour: goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody Alexa-Fluor™ 488 (1:500) and goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, cyanine3 (1:500, Invitrogen, Cat# A10521, RRID: AB_2534030). Subsequently, five fluorescent images from randomly selected fields were used for quantitative analysis to determine the ratio of primary hippocampal neurons to the total cell population.

## Data and statistical analysis
The gray value of protein bands obtained from ImageJ (version 2.14.0, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) (Schneider et al., 2012) and the flow cytometry results were analyzed using Flowjo (version 10, BD Corp, Stanford, CA, USA). All experiments were repeated three times. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 22.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 6.01, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA, www.graphpad.com). Differences between groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance, and the results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. When the assumption of homogeneity of variances was met, we used the least significant difference test. If the assumption was not met, Dunnett’s T3 test was used. Statistical significance was denoted by a significance level of P < 0.05.

## Results

## Endoplasmic reticulum stress, autophagy, and apoptosis in the ischemia/reperfusion PC12 cell model
To establish an I/R PC12 cell model, we treated PC12 cells with OGD/R. We analyzed the changes in the expression levels of ER stress-related proteins GRP78, PERK, p-PERK ( A–C), autophagy-related proteins Beclin1 and LC3 ( D and E), and the antiapoptotic protein Bcl-2 ( F) by western blot. PERK levels were slightly increased after OGD/R treatment. The levels of p-PERK, Beclin1, and LC3II/LC3I proteins were significantly increased after OGD/R with 6-, 9-, and 12-hour OGD, and all proteins showed a trend of increasing expression levels with increasing OGD duration. GRP78 and p-PERK proteins reached their highest levels after OGD/R with 6- and 9-hour OGD, respectively. The increased level of p-PERK indicated that the PERK signaling pathway was activated, and the elevated level of GRP78 suggested that ER stress was induced after OGD/R treatment (Lee, 2005). Moreover, Bcl-2 was decreased in the 6-hour/24-hour, 9-hour/24-hour, and 12-hour/24-hour groups compared with the control group, suggesting the occurrence of apoptosis.
 Immunofluorescence assays showed that the fluorescence intensity of GRP78 ( G and I; P = 0.035) and LC3 ( H and J; P = 0.016) proteins were significantly higher in the 9-hour/24-hour group than in the control group, indicating that GRP78 and LC3 protein expression were upregulated after OGD/R treatment. Of all of the treatment durations, the 9-hour/24-hour group had the most distinct and reproducible ER stress, autophagy, and apoptosis patterns. Therefore, we selected the 9-hour/24-hour duration as the I/R model for further experiments.

## Endoplasmic reticulum stress–induced autophagy through PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP signaling pathway
The above results demonstrated that OGD/R induced ER stress and autophagy in PC12 cells. To investigate the role of the PERK signaling pathway in ER stress-induced autophagy, we used RNA interference technology to knock down the PERK gene. We first confirmed that PERK was silenced using fluorescence microscopy and western blot analyses. As shown in A–C, the prevalent expression of red fluorescence in most cells signified successful transfection and functional expression of siRNA vehicle in PC12 cells. Western blot analysis also showed a significant downregulation of PERK protein expression in the siPERK group compared with the control and blank groups ( D). These results demonstrated the effective knockdown of the PERK protein using siRNA in our study.
 Compared with that in the control group, the PERK protein showed no changes in the 9-hour/24-hour group ( E). We observed increased levels of GRP78 ( F) and significantly increased levels of p-eIF2α ( G, P = 0.016), ATF-4 ( H, P = 0.01), CHOP ( I, P = 0.02), Beclin1 ( J, P = 0.039), and LC3II/LC3I ( K, P < 0.01) in the 9-hour/24-hour group compared with the control group. These findings indicated that the PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP signaling pathway, ER stress, and autophagy were activated after OGD/R treatment. The protein levels of p-eIF2α ( G), ATF-4 ( H), and CHOP ( I) were decreased in the 9-hour/24-hour (siPERK) group compared with the 9-hour/24-hour group. Similarly, Beclin1 ( J) and LC3II/LC3I ( K) protein levels were also decreased in the 9-hour/24-hour (siPERK) group. These results suggest that the interference of PERK attenuated the activation of the PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP signaling pathway and autophagy. Thus, the PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP signaling pathway is crucial for ER stress-induced autophagy.

## Autophagic flux in the oxygen-glucose deprivation and reperfusion PC12 cell model
To investigate the autophagic flux in OGD/R-treated PC12 cells, we used GSK260641 and CQ to block signal transduction in the PERK pathway and autophagy flux, respectively (Jia et al., 2018). In the 9-hour/24-hour (iPERK) group, compared with the 9-hour/24-hour group, the GRP78 protein level was slightly decreased ( A). Moreover, in the 9-hour/24-hour (iPERK) group, p-eIF2α ( B; P = 0.016), ATF-4 ( C; P = 0.002), Beclin1 ( D; P = 0.013) and LC3-II/LC3I ( E; P = 0.022) were significantly decreased compared with those in the 9-hour/24-hour group. The protein p62 associated with autophagy exhibited no change ( F), whereas the Bcl-2 protein level was increased compared with that in the 9-hour/24-hour group ( G). Additionally, a slight decrease was observed in Bax levels ( H). To further validate the occurrence of apoptosis, we performed Hoechst33342 staining. Cellular apoptosis was decreased in the 9-hour /24-hour (iPERK) group ( A). These results indicated that inhibition of the PERK signaling pathway attenuated the expression of autophagy-related proteins, thereby diminishing autophagy and consequently leading to a reduction in cellular apoptosis.
 In the 9-hour/24-hour (CQ) group, we observed a slightly increased GRP78 protein level ( A) compared with that in the 9-hour/24-hour group. Additionally, in the 9-hour/24-hour (CQ) group, p-eIF2α ( B; P = 0.046), ATF-4 ( C; P = 0.032), Beclin1 ( D; P = 0.026), LC3II/LC3I ( E; P = 0.002) and p62 ( F; P = 0.001) protein levels were significantly increased compared with those in the 9-hour/24-hour group (P < 0.05). These results suggested that blocking autophagy enhanced PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP pathway transduction and upregulated PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP pathway-associated proteins in the 9-hour/24-hour (CQ) group. Furthermore, in the 9-hour/24-hour (CQ) group, the level of antiapoptotic protein Bcl-2 was decreased compared with that in the 9-hour/24-hour group ( G), which indicated that inhibiting autophagy may lead to apoptosis.
To investigate the autophagic flux in response to OGD/R, we used an adenovirus containing mRFP-GFP-LC3 expression cassette to monitor LC3 protein levels and quantified the autophagy flux. Compared with the control group, 9-hour/24-hour treatment induced autophagy in PC12 cells. In the CQ and 9-hour/24-hour (CQ) groups, an increase in autophagy flux was induced by 9-hour/24-hour treatment. Both the 9-hour/24-hour and 9-hour/24-hour (iPERK) groups exhibited red spots; however, the number and intensity of red dots were significantly lower in the 9-hour/24-hour (iPERK) group compared with the 9-hour/24-hour group. These results suggested attenuation of autophagy flux and reduction in autophagosome formation following the downregulation of the PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP pathway ( B).

## The interplay between endoplasmic reticulum stress–induced autophagy and apoptosis in the oxygen-glucose deprivation and reperfusion PC12 cell model
We further investigated the relationship between autophagy and apoptosis in the OGD/R PC12 cell model by measuring the apoptosis rate using flow cytometry and TEM. Flow cytometry analysis showed a significant increase in apoptosis rates after OGD/R ( A–F). The 9-hour/24-hour (iPERK) group had a significantly decreased apoptosis rate (12.34%) compared with that in the 9-hour/24-hour group ( G, P < 0.01). In contrast, the 9-hour/24-hour (CQ) group had an increased apoptosis rate (25.3%) compared with that in the 9-hour/24-hour group ( G). These results strongly suggested that inhibition of the PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP pathway decreased cell apoptosis in PC12 cells, whereas inhibition of autophagy flux led to a significant increase in apoptosis rate.
 We used TEM to investigate the number of autophagosomes in different treatment groups. No autophagosomes were observed in the control ( H) and iPERK groups ( I), and a few autophagosomes were observed in the CQ group ( J). Compared with autophagosomes in the 9-hour/24-hour group ( K), fewer autophagosomes were observed in the 9-hour/24-hour (iPERK) group ( L, red arrow), and more autophagosomes were observed in the 9-hour/24-hour (CQ) group ( M, red arrow). These results indicated that OGD/R treatment increased the number of autophagosomes in PC12 cells. Furthermore, inhibiting the PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP pathway decreased the number of autophagosomes. In contrast, inhibiting the binding of autophagosomes and lysosomes led to an increased number of autophagosomes.

## The interaction between ER stress-induced autophagy and apoptosis in primary hippocampal neurons
To investigate the potential interplay between ER stress and autophagy in primary hippocampal neurons after OGD/R treatment, we isolated primary hippocampal cells from newborn rats. Confocal microscopy analysis indicated that the isolated primary hippocampal neurons had a purity of approximately 83% ( A and B).
 In the 2-hour/24-hour group, the protein levels of GRP78 ( C), p-eIF2α ( D), LC3II/LC3I ( E), and Beclin 1 ( F) were increased compared with those in the control group. In contrast, the level of p62 was significantly decreased ( G; P = 0.031). These findings suggested that ER stress, the PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP pathway, and autophagy were activated after OGD/R treatment in primary hippocampal cells, which was consistent with the results observed in PC12 cells. In the 2-hour/24-hour (iPERK) group, the protein levels of GRP78 ( C; P = 0.043), p-eIF2α ( D; P < 0.01), LC3II/LC3I ( E; P = 0.019), Beclin 1 ( F; P = 0.048), and Bax ( I; P = 0.016) were significantly decreased compared with those in the 2-hour/24-hour group. Moreover, p62 was also marginally decreased ( G). In contrast, Bcl-2 was increased ( H), indicating that inhibition of the PERK protein downregulated the PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP pathway and attenuated autophagy in primary hippocampal cells. In the 2-hour/24-hour (CQ) group, compared with the 2-hour/24-hour group, p-eIF2α ( D), LC3II/LC3I ( E), Beclin 1 ( F), and p62 ( G) were significantly increased (P < 0.05). GRP78 was essentially unchanged ( C), whereas Bcl-2 was significantly decreased (P = 0.01; H), Bax was slightly increased ( I), but not statistically significant. These results suggested that the inhibition of autophagy flux by CQ shifted the autophagic flux to apoptosis, consistent with the results observed in PC12 cells.
These findings indicated that inhibiting the PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP pathway in primary hippocampal neurons under OGD/R conditions reduced ER stress, weakened autophagy, and decreased cell apoptosis.
Inhibition of autophagy flux by CQ resulted in the upregulation of the PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP signaling pathway-related proteins in a compensation mechanism and led to cell apoptosis.

## Discussion
ER stress and autophagy have received increasing attention for their role in neuronal damage caused by cerebral I/R. In this study, we showed that OGD/R injury activated the ER stress pathway PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP, which led to cellular apoptosis. Simultaneously, a surge in autophagic activity was also observed. Interestingly, attenuation of OGD/R-induced autophagy and cell apoptosis was achieved through the inhibition of PERK. However, it is noteworthy that inhibition of autophagic flux by CQ results in compromised protein degradation, which activates the PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 pathway and thereby contributes to neuronal impairment.
Cerebral ischemia results in a decrease in cerebral oxygen and energy availability, and an increase in glutamate excitotoxicity, elevating intracellular Ca2+, Na+, and extracellular K+ concentrations upon reperfusion. Our previous study demonstrated a reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential and an accumulation of ROS in both SH-SY5Y cells subjected to OGD/R and hippocampal neurons of vascular dementia rats (Liu et al., 2019). A previous study reported that excessive ROS can induce ER stress under pathological conditions, thereby exacerbating neuronal damage and death (Li et al., 2021).
ER stress is triggered by the accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins within the ER. The UPR is initiated to trigger adaptive mechanisms that sustain cellular homeostasis. This response is regulated by three ER membrane-anchored sensors: PERK, activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6), and IRE1. These sensors are activated in response to ER homeostasis disturbances (Badiola et al., 2011). Each of these sensors initiate specialized transcriptional programs mediated by distinct transducers, such as ATF4 (associated with PERK), cleaved ATF6 (associated with ATF6), and spliced XBP1 (associated with IRE1). These components directly orchestrate the transcription of chaperones or proteins implicated in redox homeostasis, protein secretion, lipid biosynthesis, or cell death programs. The UPR plays a critical role in maintaining cellular internal environment stability and maintaining normal physiological functions. However, prolonged and excessive UPR activation can ultimately lead to ER stress and trigger apoptosis. ER stress-related pathways are implicated in ischemic damage. For instance, OGD triggers ER stress by activating the PERK and IRE1 pathways, and OGD-mediated caspase-12 activation is contingent upon NMDA receptor-mediated calpain activation (Badiola et al., 2011). Other studies suggest that postischemic suppression of translation induced by PERK activation promotes recovery of neurologic function after stroke (Wang et al., 2020).
I/R triggers distinct and overlapping signaling pathways that contribute to cellular survival or death. The duration of the ischemic event largely determines the fate of neuronal cells upon reperfusion. Hence, timely intervention within a specific treatment window following cerebral ischemia is pivotal for mitigating brain damage and enhancing long-term recovery outcomes. In this study, we evaluated ER stress in PC12 cells subjected to varying durations of hypoxia and glucose deprivation (OGD 3, 6, 9, and 12 hours), followed by a 24-hour reperfusion. The results showed that GRP78 levels were increased during OGD/R (6 hours/24 hours), and p-PERK was significantly increased at 9 hours/24 hours.
Prolonged ER stress activation potentiates cell death via ATF4 and CHOP-mediated transcriptional responses (Ohoka et al., 2005). Consequently, we further investigated downstream PERK pathway proteins at the 9-hour OGD and 24-hour reperfusion interval. Our findings showed substantial increases in ATF4 and CHOP levels, suggesting activation of the eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP pathway, which may be an adaptive response aimed at alleviating ER stress by promoting the folding/assembly of accumulated proteins or attenuating protein translation. An increase in the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio and neuronal apoptosis was observed. CHOP, an apoptotic transcription factor induced by ER stress, is pivotal in ER stress-driven apoptosis. Previous studies showed that CHOP elevated the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio and induced cell apoptosis through caspase-3 cleavage (Cao et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2019). These studies are consistent with our findings.
Furthermore, we observed a progressive increase in the Beclin1 and LC3II/LC3I autophagy protein ratio after 6 hours/24 hours, with a time-dependent increase corresponding to the duration of OGD/R. Autophagy, a pivotal cellular catabolic mechanism, plays a central role in preserving cellular equilibrium and promoting cell survival. Notably, autophagy manifests a dual nature. Some studies demonstrate a protective role of autophagy against cerebral I/R injury (Zhang et al., 2014), whereas a growing body of evidence reports that excessive autophagic activation exacerbates neuronal damage. Autophagy inhibition diminished infarct size and enhanced neurological outcomes after cerebral ischemia (Feng et al., 2017). During the initial reperfusion phase, autophagy plays a protective role in cerebral I/R injury, during which it mediates the degradation of impaired organelles and misfolded proteins while recycling amino acids and nucleotides (Yan et al., 2011; Xia et al., 2013). However, when autophagy persists during the later reperfusion stage, it can lead to the excessive degradation of undamaged organelles and proteins, ultimately culminating in autophagic cell death and secondary harm to cells and tissues (Gao et al., 2012; Wu et al., 2015).
A previous study indicated that ER stress can trigger autophagy through the PERK pathway. Mutations at the phosphorylation site of eIF2α within PERK obstructed Atg complex formation and LC3 conversion (Kouroku et al., 2007). The PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP signaling cascade is pivotal in initiating the ER stress–autophagy response (Avivar-Valderas et al., 2013; Guo et al., 2015; Ji et al., 2015). Under ER stress conditions, PERK is activated and phosphorylates eIF2α, culminating in the selective induction of ATF4. This ATF4 induction not only triggers autophagy through the upregulation of numerous ATG genes, but also propels apoptosis by degrading XIAP and synergizing with CHOP under prolonged ER stress (Song et al., 2017). CHOP orchestrates the apoptotic response by modulating proapoptotic Bax and antiapoptotic Bcl-2 proteins.
To clarify the relationship between ER stress and autophagy within the framework of IR injury, we used the specific ER stress inhibitor GSK2606414, siRNA PERK, and the specific autophagy inhibitor CQ. Our findings indicate that inhibiting PERK mitigates excessive autophagy and cell apoptosis induced by IR. These results suggest that ER stress is an upstream factor in autophagy during IR injury and that ER stress-induced autophagy operates through the PERK pathway. These observations underscore the potential for ER stress to induce excessive autophagy, potentially tipping the balance toward IR injury-associated cellular death.
It was intriguing that using CQ to inhibit excessive autophagic flux induced by I/R did not yield the anticipated attenuation of ER stress; instead, it seemingly exacerbated ER stress-induced cellular apoptosis. Our results are consistent with previous studies showing a switch from autophagy to apoptosis when autophagy flux was blocked (Lugovaya et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020; Han et al., 2023). Subsequent double-label adenovirus experiments showed that autophagy flux inhibition by CQ led to the upregulation of proteins associated with the PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP pathway while concomitantly downregulating Bcl-2 protein expression. This phenomenon may arise from the inhibitory influence of CQ on autophagy, thereby culminating in the accumulation of a substantial pool of proteins that fail to undergo cytoplasmic degradation. As a result, this protein overload may increase ER stress and subsequent activation of CHOP transcription factors. This molecular cascade then suppresses Bcl-2 protein expression, ultimately culminating in cellular apoptosis. Furthermore, autophagy inhibition via CQ or lentiviral-mediated interference targeting either Atg5 or Atg7 rendered the cells susceptible to ER stress-driven cellular apoptosis, as evidenced by the increased levels of caspase-3 and a diminished antiapoptotic Bcl-2 response. These observations underscore the important role of efficient autophagy in bolstering cell survival (Bartolome et al., 2012).
Our investigation established that OGD/R induced ER stress and autophagy within neuronal populations. The extent of ER stress and autophagy was linked to the duration of OGD/R. With the increase of ER stress intensity, a concurrent increase was observed in the expression of proteins associated with the PERK/eIF2α-ATF4-CHOP axis. Moreover, the expression of autophagy-related signaling proteins increased in a time-dependent manner according to the duration of OGD/R. Enhanced autophagy contributes to the disposal of misfolded and unfolded proteins, effectively attenuating ER stress. However, cellular apoptosis ensues when autophagy fails to rectify ER stress. This triad of processes exhibits dynamic alterations. The siRNA-mediated silencing not only curbs PERK protein expression but also impedes the PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 pathway, reducing excessive autophagy induced by IR and consequently mitigating neuronal apoptosis. This highlights the potential for attenuating autophagy intensity by inhibiting the PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 pathway within the ER stress context. Using the autophagy inhibitor CQ inhibits autophagic flux and thereby disrupts accrued protein degradation. Consequently, this increases the activation of the PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 pathway and exacerbates neuronal apoptosis. However, whether the increase of autophagy can be harnessed to inhibit cellular apoptosis warrants further exploration.
This research stands as a preliminary endeavor. Gene knockout or overexpression techniques targeted at the PERK-eIF2α-ATF4 pathway are essential to unravel the intricate regulatory associations among autophagy, ER stress, and cell apoptosis after I/R treatment, both in vitro and in vivo. Subsequent in-depth basic research and comprehensive clinical trials are imperative to corroborate these complex relationships.
