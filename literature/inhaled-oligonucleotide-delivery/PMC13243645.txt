Although the hypothalamus is well-established as the central regulator of energy homeostasis through specialized neuronal circuits, the molecular mechanisms governing these pathways remain incompletely elucidated. In this study, we identify thymocyte selection-associated high mobility group box 3 (TOX3) as a critical metabolic regulator in hypothalamic pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC)-lineage cells in mice. By employing cell-type-specific genetic manipulation in murine models, we establish that conditional TOX3 ablation in POMC-lineage cells exacerbates diet-induced obesity and metabolic dysfunction, while its overexpression in these neurons confers robust metabolic benefits. This central modulation drives sympathetic activation of brown adipose tissues, resulting in enhanced thermogenesis and energy expenditure. Notably, TOX3 exhibits striking neuronal specificity, as both loss- and gain-of-function manipulation in agouti-related peptide (AgRP) neurons produce negligible metabolic consequences. Our work identifies previously unrecognized physiological roles of TOX3 in POMC-lineage cells that are essential for maintaining energy homeostasis in mice, thereby revealing therapeutic opportunities for metabolic disorders.

## Introduction
Obesity and its associated metabolic diseases, particularly type 2 diabetes and fatty liver disease, have emerged as a major global health challenge . Elucidating the intricate mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of obesity is critical for identifying potential preventive and therapeutic strategies. Fundamentally, obesity results from chronic energy homeostasis disruption due to sustained caloric intake exceeding expenditure , . Decades of research have established that energy balance is predominantly regulated by the central nervous system (CNS), with the hypothalamus serving as the central regulatory hub – .
The hypothalamic circuitry comprises several well-characterized nuclei, including the arcuate nucleus (ARC), which contains two interlinked but functionally antagonistic neuronal populations: anorexigenic pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) neurons and orexigenic agouti-related peptide (AgRP)/neuropeptide (NPY) neurons , . POMC neurons are established as master regulators of negative energy balance, exerting dual control through central suppression of food intake and activation of peripheral sympathetic nervous system (SNS) output-mediated adipose tissue catabolism , . Anatomical tracing studies have demonstrated that POMC neurons innervate both white (WAT) and brown adipose tissue (BAT), where norepinephrine (NE) release activates β3-adrenoceptors to stimulate lipolysis in WAT and promote thermogenesis in BAT – .
As first-order metabolic sensors, POMC neurons are uniquely positioned to integrate peripheral signals due to their anatomical localization proximity to the median eminence, a circumventricular organ with a fenestrated blood-brain barrier . This privileged location enables rapid detection of circulating metabolic cues, including nutrients (e.g., glucose, fatty acids) and hormones (e.g., leptin, insulin) , . Studies have demonstrated that elevated circulating leptin and insulin triggers a coordinated response in POMC neurons involving acute membrane depolarization and transcriptional upregulation of Pomc and subsequent processing into bioactive peptides , , – . These adaptations concurrently suppress feeding while enhancing energy expenditure through SNS-mediated adipose tissue remodeling. Importantly, disruption of this regulatory axis-whether through genetic variants, dietary stress, or inflammation-represents a critical node in the pathogenesis of obesity and its metabolic complications – .
Thymocyte selection-associated high mobility group box family member 3 (TOX3), a member of the high mobility group box superfamily, functions as an important transcriptional co-regulator . Human studies and murine models consistently demonstrate predominant TOX3 expression in the brain , . In vitro evidence indicates TOX3 regulates calcium-dependent transcription in neurons , exhibits neuroprotective effects , and contributes to CNS development , , suggesting vital roles in neural physiology. Notably, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have implicated TOX3 in polycystic ovary syndrome pathogenesis and insulin resistance . Most recently, TOX3 was identified as a novel regulator of hepatic gluconeogenesis that exacerbates diabetic pathophysiology , further underscoring its metabolic relevance. However, the precise physiological and pathophysiological roles of TOX3 in metabolic regulation, particularly in energy homeostasis, remain completely unexplored.
Here, we identify TOX3 as a POMC-lineage cell-specific guardian against obesity. Mechanistically, TOX3 expression in hypothalamic POMC-lineage cells promotes phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) degradation, enhancing protein kinase B (AKT) signaling to activate sympathetic-driven adipose catabolism. Genetic ablation of TOX3 in POMC-lineage cells exacerbates metabolic dysfunction, whereas its overexpression confers protection, with no significant effects observed in AgRP neurons. These findings position TOX3 as a critical molecular nexus linking hypothalamic signaling to peripheral energy metabolism.

## Results

## TOX3 is predominantly expressed in hypothalamic neurons
To characterize the expression patterns of TOX3, we first examined its distribution across major metabolic tissues in mice using a publicly available database. Our analysis revealed that Tox3 mRNA expression was most enriched in the brain compared to various peripheral tissues (Fig.  ). Subsequent qPCR analysis confirmed that Tox3 levels were significantly higher in the hypothalamus than in other brain regions (Fig.  ) or peripheral tissues (Fig.  ). To determine the cellular localization of TOX3 within hypothalamic cell populations, we performed RNAscope in situ hybridization for Tox3 combined with immunofluorescence labeling for neuronal nuclei antigen (NeuN, neuronal marker), vimentin (tanycyte marker), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, astrocyte marker), and ionized calcium binding adaptor molecule 1 (IBA1, microglia marker). The results revealed that Tox3 expression was predominantly neuronal, with most Tox3-positive cells co-expressing NeuN (90.54%), and almost all (>95%) NeuN-positive cells showing Tox3 expression (Fig.  ). In contrast, minimal colocalization was observed between Tox3 and markers of tanycytes, astrocytes, or microglia (Fig.  ). Consistent with these findings, analysis of a published single-cell RNA-sequencing dataset of the mouse hypothalamus confirmed that Tox3 was abundantly expressed in neurons, with negligible expression in tanycytes, astrocytes and microglia (Fig.  ). These findings establish that TOX3 is specifically and highly expressed in hypothalamic neurons. 

## Knockdown of TOX3 in hypothalamic neurons exacerbates diet-induced obesity and metabolic dysfunctions
Given the prominent hypothalamic expression of TOX3, we examined its expression under different metabolic conditions. Hypothalamic Tox3 mRNA levels were significantly downregulated in high-fat diet (HFD)-induced obese mice compared with chow diet (CD)-fed controls (Fig.  ). To investigate the role of hypothalamic neuronal TOX3 in energy homeostasis, we bilaterally injected HFD-fed mice with AAV-shCtrl or AAV-shTox3 viruses (tracked with GFP) (Fig.  ). Fluorescence imaging confirmed GFP expression in hypothalamic nuclei (ARC, VMH, DMH, LH, and PVN), with no detectable signal in extrahypothalamic regions such as the cortex (Fig.  ). AAV-shTox3 injection significantly reduced hypothalamic Tox3 expression without affecting other family members (Tox, Tox2, Tox4) (Fig.  ), while Tox3 levels remained unchanged in other regions including the thalamus, hippocampus and cortex (Fig.  ).
Hypothalamic Tox3 knockdown led to greater body weight gain (Fig.  ; Fig.  ). Post-mortem analyses revealed increased weights of liver, epididymal WAT (eWAT), inguinal WAT (iWAT), and BAT (Fig.  ), along with enlarged adipocytes in these depots (Fig.  ). Livers of AAV-shTox3-injected mice exhibited more and larger lipid droplets (Fig.  ) and elevated levels of hepatic triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and non-esterified fatty acid (NEFA) (Fig.  ). Compared with controls, mice with TOX3 knockdown in hypothalamic neurons displayed impaired glucose tolerance and reduced insulin sensitivity (Fig.  ), accompanied by significantly higher serum insulin levels (Fig.  ). Collectively, these results demonstrate that specific knockdown of TOX3 in hypothalamic neurons exacerbates HFD-induced obesity and metabolic dysfunction in mice.

## Ablation of TOX3 in POMC-lineage cells exacerbates diet-induced obesity and metabolic disturbance
Hypothalamic POMC neurons are known as crucial nodes in energy homeostasis , , . To determine TOX3 expression in these neurons, we generated POMC-lineage cell-specific tdTomato reporter mice (Pomc-cre;Ai9) and observed substantial co-localization of Tox3 mRNA with tdTomato-labeled POMC-lineage cells (Fig.  ). Interestingly, after 16 weeks of HFD feeding, Tox3 expression in POMC (tdTomato+)-lineage cells was significantly reduced compared to CD-fed controls (Fig.  ). A modest decrease in Tox3 expression was also observed in non-POMC (tdTomato-) cells (Fig.  ).
To investigate TOX3 function specifically in POMC-lineage cells, we generated POMC-lineage cell-specific TOX3 knockout mice (PTKO) by crossing Tox3flox/flox (LoxP) mice with Pomc-Cre mice (Fig.  ). These mice were further crossed with tdTomato reporter mice to visualize POMC-lineage cells. RNAscope analysis confirmed efficient and specific deletion of Tox3 in POMC-lineage cells (Fig.  ), with no effect on their distribution or quantity (Fig.  ), indicating that Tox3 is not required for the development or survival of POMC-lineage cells.
We then performed a comprehensive metabolic phenotyping screen in mice fed a standard CD or HFD. Under CD, deletion of Tox3 in POMC-lineage cells impacted no detectable changes in body length, body weight or body weight gain, and body composition in male mice (Fig.  ). Serum TC, TG, NEFA, and glucose levels were also comparable (Fig.  ). Moreover, we did not observe any difference in glucose tolerance or insulin sensitivity between genotypes (Fig.  ). Similar results were observed in female mice fed a CD (Fig.  ).
In contrast, when challenged with HFD, male PTKO mice showed increased body weight gain without changes in body length (Fig.  ; Fig.  ). Body composition analysis revealed elevated fat mass composition and reduced lean mass composition (Fig.  ). PTKO males also exhibited increased weights of eWAT, iWAT, and BAT (Fig.  ) and larger adipocytes in these depots (Fig.  ). Consistent with increased adiposity, eWAT from PTKO mice showed upregulated expression of proinflammatory genes (Mcp1, Tnfα, F4/80, Cd68, Cd11b, Cd11c) (Fig.  ) and more F4/80-positive cells (Fig.  ), suggesting exacerbated adipose inflammation. In addition, liver weight was higher in PTKO mice (Fig.  ), accompanied by larger liver size, greater lipid accumulation, and elevated hepatic TG, TC and NEFA (Fig.  ). Serum TC was elevated, whereas TG and NEFA remained unchanged (Fig.  ). PTKO mice displayed impaired glucose tolerance and disrupted insulin sensitivity (Fig.  ), together with higher serum insulin levels (Fig.  ). 
Similarly, when challenged with HFD, female PTKO mice exhibited increased body weight gain and fat mass composition, along with decreased lean mass composition (Fig.  ). Consistently, fat pads weights, including gonadal WAT (gWAT) and iWAT, were significantly increased in female PTKO mice (Fig.  ), with larger adipocyte sizes observed (Fig.  ). More lipid accumulated in liver tissues of female PTKO mice (Fig.  ).
These data indicate that loss of Tox3 in POMC-lineage cells exacerbates diet-induced obesity and metabolic dysfunction in both sexes. Since knockout of TOX3 did not show gender differences, we selected male mice for subsequent experiments.

## Overexpression of TOX3 in hypothalamic POMC-lineage cells mitigates HFD-induced metabolic dysfunction
Next, we evaluated whether gain-of-function TOX3 in hypothalamic POMC-lineage cells would be sufficient to ameliorate HFD-induced obesity and metabolic dysfunction. We generated POMC-lineage cell-specific TOX3-overexpressing mice (PTOE) via ARC injection of a Cre-dependent AAV (AAV-Tox3) into Pomc-Cre mice (Fig.  ). Controls received AAV-GFP (Fig.  ). Fluorescence imaging confirmed that GFP was specifically detected in the ARC, but not in other regions including the VMH, DMH, LH, cortex, and hippocampus (Fig.  ). We confirmed that hypothalamic Tox3 mRNA expression was upregulated in PTOE mice, while Tox, Tox2, and Tox4 were unaffected (Fig.  ). RNAscope analysis further confirmed that Tox3 was specifically overexpressed in POMC (tdTomato+)-lineage cells (Fig.  ). 
On a chow diet, metabolic parameters were comparable between groups (Fig.  ). Under HFD challenge, mice with hypothalamic POMC-lineage cell-specific TOX3 overexpression exhibited significantly lower weight gain compared with their controls (Fig.  ; Fig.  ). Body composition analysis revealed reduced fat weight, fat mass composition, and increased lean mass composition in PTOE mice (Fig.  ). Post-mortem analyses also showed decreased weights of eWAT, iWAT, and BAT depots (Fig.  ), along with smaller adipocyte sizes in eWAT and iWAT, as well as reduced lipid accumulation in BAT (Fig.  ). Inflammatory infiltration in eWAT was ameliorated, as reflected by lower mRNA levels of Mcp1, F4/80, Cd68, and Cd11b (Fig.  ) and fewer F4/80-positive cells (Fig.  ). Additionally, PTOE mice exhibited significantly lower liver weight (Fig.  ), accompanied by decreased lipid droplet accumulation (Fig.  ) and reduced hepatic levels of TG, TC, and NEFA (Fig.  ), indicating amelioration of hepatic steatosis. Serum TC levels were reduced in PTOE mice, while TG and NEFA remained unchanged (Fig.  ). Overexpression of TOX3 in POMC-lineage cells also improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity (Fig.  ), consistent with lower serum insulin levels (Fig.  ). Together, these findings demonstrate that specific overexpression of TOX3 in POMC-lineage cells is sufficient to ameliorate obesity and metabolic disturbances in mice fed an HFD.

## TOX3 in POMC-lineage cells controls energy expenditure and adipose tissue catabolism
To understand the underlying mechanisms of how TOX3 in POMC-lineage cells affects energy homeostasis, we conducted indirect calorimetry to assess metabolic parameters in both TOX3 loss- and gain-of-function mice upon HFD feeding. In male LoxP and PTKO mice, food and water intake did not differ significantly (Fig.  ). Instead, TOX3 deletion in POMC-lineage cells significantly decreased O2 consumption, CO2 production, and energy expenditure (Fig.  ; Fig.  ), without alterations in the respiratory exchange ratio (RER) or physical activity (Fig.  ). These results were accompanied by decreased rectal temperature in PTKO mice (Fig.  ). Notably, thermal images in the interscapular regions indicated that the temperature around BAT regions was markedly reduced upon TOX3 ablation (Fig.  ). Given that adipose tissue is a well-known organ in energy expenditure and obesity progression, we further conducted thorough studies to examine catabolic metabolism in both BAT and WAT. TOX3 deletion markedly downregulated BAT thermogenic genes (Ucp1, Pgc1α, Dio2, Cidea) (Fig.  ), while browning-related transcripts in iWAT were unaltered (Fig.  ). Western blot analysis and immunohistochemistry further confirmed significantly lower UCP1 protein in BAT of PTKO mice (Fig.  ). The thermogenesis in BAT utilizes fatty acids derived from white adipose lipolysis , . Coordinately, we found the expression or phosphorylation of lipolytic proteins were significantly lower in eWAT and iWAT (Fig.  ). In liver, genes promoting fatty acid uptake (Cd36) and lipogenesis (Srebf1) were upregulated, whereas genes involved in fatty acid oxidation (Cpt1α) and lipoprotein secretion (Apob, Mttp) were downregulated; meanwhile, skeletal muscle (SKM) metabolic markers remained unchanged (Fig.  ). 
We next investigated whether TOX3 overexpression in POMC-lineage cells could enhance energy expenditure. In metabolic cage studies, food and water intake were unaltered between Control and PTOE groups (Fig.  ). However, PTOE mice exhibited significantly higher O2 consumption, CO2 production, and energy expenditure (Fig.  ; Fig.  ), with no change in RER or locomotor activity (Fig.  ). Rectal and interscapular temperatures were also elevated in PTOE mice (Fig.  ). Expression of UCP1 and other thermogenic markers was increased in BAT (Fig.  ), while iWAT browning markers were unaltered (Fig.  ). Correspondingly, the expression of phosphorylation of lipolytic proteins were greatly increased in WAT (Fig.  ). In liver, genes promoting fatty acid uptake (Cd36) and lipogenesis (Srebf1, Acaca) were downregulated, while fatty acid oxidation (Cpt2) was increased; metabolic genes in SKM remained unaffected (Fig.  ). These results indicate that TOX3 in POMC-lineage cells affects obesity and metabolic disturbances through its regulation of adipose tissue metabolism and energy expenditure.

## TOX3 in POMC-lineage cells modulates adipose tissue metabolism through sympathetic nervous regulation
Adipose tissue receives direct sympathetic innervation, with SNS activity serving as a key regulator of adipose tissue metabolism , . To investigate whether TOX3 in POMC-lineage cells regulates energy homeostasis via the SNS pathway, we performed comprehensive analyses in both TOX3 loss- and gain-of-function models upon HFD feeding conditions.
In PTKO mice, tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) expression, a rate-limiting enzyme required for catecholamine synthesis , , was significantly decreased in BAT (Fig.  ; Fig.  ). Consistently, norepinephrine (NE) content was reduced in BAT, eWAT, and iWAT, but not in liver (Fig.  ; Fig.  ). Adrenergic receptors (Adrβ1, Adrβ2, and Adrβ3) were downregulated in BAT but unchanged in WAT depots (Fig.  ). NE turnover in BAT, based on NE content before and after injection of α-methyl-para-tyrosine (AMPT), an inhibitor of TH – , was also lower in PTKO mice (Fig.  ). In addition, the downstream effectors of the β3-adrenergic receptor signaling, phosphorylation of cAMP response element-binding protein (CREB) and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), were both downregulated in BAT of PTKO mice (Fig.  ). 
Cold exposure (4 °C) further revealed impaired thermoregulation in PTKO mice, which showed a more rapid decline in rectal and interscapular BAT temperature (Fig.  ). While body weight and adipose depot weights were unchanged at room temperature, cold-exposed PTKO mice showed higher weights of BAT, eWAT and iWAT (Fig.  ), along with attenuated cold-induced upregulation of UCP1 and TH (Fig.  ). To confirm SNS involvement, we administered the β3-adrenergic agonist CL316,243 – or saline to HFD-fed LoxP and PTKO mice (Fig.  ). We found that PTKO-induced higher BAT weight, lower thermogenic gene expression, and more lipid droplets were all abrogated by CL316,243 treatment (Fig.  ).
Conversely, PTOE mice showed elevated TH expression in BAT (Figs.  and ) and increased NE content in BAT, eWAT, and iWAT (Fig.  ; Fig.  ). Adrenergic receptors (Adrβ2 and Adrβ3) were upregulated in BAT but not in WAT (Fig.  ). NE turnover and phosphorylation of CREB and p38 MAPK were also enhanced in BAT of PTOE mice (Fig.  ; Fig.  ). To further confirm SNS involvement, we performed bilateral microinjections of 6-hydroxydopamine hydrobromide (6-OHDA), a selective catecholaminergic neurotoxin , , into the BAT pads of HFD-fed Control and PTOE mice (Fig.  ; Fig.  ). Four weeks post-injection, the decreases in body weight and BAT weight observed in PTOE mice with an intact SNS were largely reversed (Fig.  ; Fig.  ), with comparable expression of thermogenic parameters and BAT morphology between groups (Fig.  ). We also performed bilateral injections of 6-OHDA into both the eWAT and iWAT depots of HFD-fed Control and PTOE mice (Fig.  ). Interestingly, this intervention did not alter the observed reduction in body weight and fat mass composition, or the increase in fat mass composition, in PTOE mice (Fig.  ). These findings demonstrate that TOX3 in POMC-lineage cells regulates energy expenditure through β-adrenergic signaling in BAT.

## TOX3 controls the function of hypothalamic POMC-lineage cells
To provide insight into the central mechanisms through which TOX3 in POMC-lineage cells regulates system metabolism, we assessed whether TOX3 participates in the control of POMC-lineage cellular activity. HFD-fed Pomc-cre;Ai9 and PTKO;Ai9 mice were used for whole-cell current-clamp recordings (Fig.  ). We observed that POMC-lineage cells in PTKO;Ai9 mice exhibited a significant decrease in spontaneous firing rate compared to those in Pomc-cre;Ai9 mice, along with a trend toward reduced membrane potential (Fig.  ). Consistent with these electrophysiological findings, immunostaining revealed a significant reduction in c-FOS-positive POMC-lineage cells in PTKO;Ai9 mice (Fig.  ). Furthermore, hypothalamic POMC expression was significantly reduced in PTKO mice at both mRNA (Fig.  ) and protein levels (Fig.  ) compared to LoxP controls, while other neuropeptides (Cart, Agrp, and Npy) remained unchanged (Fig.  ). Conversely, TOX3 overexpression in POMC-lineage cells increased c-FOS immunoreactivity (Fig.  ) and upregulated hypothalamic POMC expression (Fig.  ), without altering Cart, Agrp, or Npy levels (Fig.  ). These data establish TOX3 as a regulator of POMC-lineage cellular activity and peptide expression, revealing its essential role in maintaining the function of POMC-lineage cells within the hypothalamus. 

## TOX3 modulates AKT signaling by regulating PTEN degradation
To elucidate how TOX3 affects the function of POMC-lineage cells, we performed single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) on hypothalamus of LoxP and PTKO mice (Fig.  ). Based on gene expression of specific markers , we classified the cells into 9 distinct clusters (Fig.  ; Fig.  ). The proportion of cells in each cluster did not differ significantly between groups (Fig.  ). Neurons were further subclustered into 26 subsets, including POMC neurons (Fig.  ; Fig.  ). Transcriptome analysis identified differentially expressed genes in POMC neurons of PTKO versus LoxP mice (Fig.  ). KEGG enrichment analysis showed enrichment of these genes in nervous system-related pathways, including insulin signaling (Fig.  ). We also conducted RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-sorted POMC-lineage cells from HFD-fed Pomc-cre;Ai9 and PTKO;Ai9 mice (Fig.  ). This analysis identified 448 upregulated and 647 downregulated transcripts in TOX3-deficient POMC-lineage cells (Fig.  ). Notably, KEGG analysis also highlighted enrichment in the Phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K)-AKT signaling pathway (Fig.  ). 
We further performed insulin administration experiments to confirm the involvement of TOX3 in insulin responses. As expected, while insulin robustly induced p-AKT (S473) expression in Pomc-cre;Ai9 mice, this effect was markedly attenuated in PTKO;Ai9 mice (Fig.  ). In vitro, Tox3 knockdown attenuated insulin-induced p-AKT (S473) expression (Fig.  ), whereas TOX3 overexpression enhanced this signaling (Fig.  ; Fig.  ).
To elucidate how TOX3 influences insulin signaling, we performed immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) on hypothalamic lysates from mice injected with AAV-Tox3-Flag. PTEN, a key phosphatase in the insulin pathway, was identified as a potential TOX3-interacting partner (Fig.  ). We further confirmed this interaction by additional Co-IP and western blot analyses (Fig.  ). Immunofluorescence staining also revealed the co-localization of PTEN and TOX3 in N2a cells (Fig.  ). TOX3 manipulation did not alter Pten mRNA levels (Fig.  ). However, TOX3 overexpression significantly decreased PTEN protein levels both in vivo (Fig.  ; Fig.  ) and in vitro (Fig.  ), suggesting post-translational regulation. Cycloheximide (CHX) chase assays in GT1-7 and N2a cells showed that TOX3 overexpression significantly accelerated PTEN degradation (Fig.  ; Fig.  ). Moreover, the proteasome inhibitor MG132 blocked the TOX3-mediated decrease in PTEN protein (Fig.  ), suggesting that TOX3 promotes proteasome-dependent PTEN turnover. Furthermore, restoring PTEN expression in TOX3-overexpressing cells suppressed insulin-induced AKT phosphorylation at Ser473 (Fig.  ; Fig.  ). These results demonstrate that TOX3 potentiates AKT signaling through post-translational suppression of PTEN.

## TOX3 in hypothalamic AgRP neurons is dispensable for energy homeostasis
AgRP neurons in the ARC represent another key population involved in metabolic regulation , . We therefore investigated whether TOX3 in AgRP neurons contributes to whole-body metabolism regulation. First, we characterized TOX3 expression in AgRP neurons under different conditions. In situ hybridization confirmed Tox3 expression in AgRP neurons (Fig.  ), though at markedly lower levels compared to POMC-lineage cells (Fig.  ). Tox3 expression was slightly decreased in AgRP neurons of diet-induced obese mice relative to chow-fed controls (Fig.  ).
To assess TOX3 function, we generated AgRP-specific knockout mice (ATKO) by crossing LoxP mice with Agrp-Cre mice (ATKO), which yielded efficient and specific deletion (Fig.  ). When fed a standard CD, no differences were observed in body length, body weight gain, body composition, adipose mass weights, glucose tolerance, or insulin sensitivity in male (Fig.  ) and female (data not shown) LoxP and ATKO mice. Similar results were observed in HFD-fed males (Fig.  ) and females (data not shown). Additionally, no changes in metabolic parameters were detected in mice challenged with chronic HFD feeding (Fig.  ).
We next overexpressed TOX3 in AgRP neurons by bilaterally injecting AAV-Tox3 or AAV-GFP into the ARC of HFD-fed male Agrp-Cre mice (Fig.  ). qPCR and in situ hybridization confirmed successful Tox3 overexpression (Fig.  ). Consistent with the knockout results, TOX3 overexpression in AgRP neurons did not affect body weight gain, body composition, adipose depot weights, adipocyte size, glucose tolerance, insulin sensitivity, or serum parameters (Fig.  ). Moreover, no significant differences in metabolic cage parameters were observed between ATOE and control mice (Fig.  ). Collectively, these data demonstrate that TOX3 in AgRP neurons is dispensable for whole-body energy homeostasis.

## Discussion
Recent GWAS have identified TOX3 as a putative obesity-related gene , yet its precise physiological functions and mechanistic contributions to metabolic regulation have remained elusive. Our study provides definitive evidence that TOX3 is abundantly expressed in hypothalamic neurons and plays a pivotal role in POMC-lineage cell-mediated regulation of diet-induced obesity and associated metabolic dysfunction. Using cell-type-specific genetic approaches, we demonstrate that conditional knockout of TOX3 in POMC-lineage cells aggravates diet-induced obesity and metabolic impairment, while its targeted overexpression confers significant metabolic protection. These effects are mediated through SNS regulation of adipose tissue thermogenesis and energy expenditure. Mechanistically, we reveal that TOX3 potentiates AKT signaling through physical interaction with PTEN, facilitating its proteasomal degradation, which is a previously unrecognized regulatory mechanism in metabolic control (Fig.  ). Intriguingly, TOX3 appears functionally dispensable in AgRP neurons with regard to systemic energy balance. Collectively, our findings uncover neurobiological mechanisms underlying TOX3’s metabolic actions and provide important insights into the pathogenesis of obesity. 
The ARC of the hypothalamus functions as a master regulatory center for energy homeostasis, with POMC neurons serving as critical metabolic integrators , , . These neurons demonstrate remarkable plasticity in responding to energy status, being inhibited during negative energy balance while activated by hormonal (e.g., leptin, insulin) and nutrient signals reflecting positive energy balance , . As first-order sensory neurons, they assimilate diverse metabolic inputs to maintain energy equilibrium. The insulin-PI3K-AKT signaling axis in POMC neurons has emerged as particularly crucial for systemic metabolic regulation , – , with recent studies revealing that insulin induces POMC neuronal depolarization through TRPC5 channel activation , , a mechanism essential for enhancing energy expenditure and protecting against diet-induced obesity. Beyond acute electrophysiological effects, insulin also stimulates Pomc gene transcription via AKT-mediated phosphorylation and dimerization events, contributing to metabolic improvement in obese and glucose-intolerant states . Our findings significantly extend this understanding by identifying TOX3 as a key upstream regulator of insulin signaling cascades in POMC-lineage cells. Specifically, we observed TOX3 deficiency impaired AKT signaling, diminished POMC expression, and compromised downstream metabolic responses. These results position TOX3 as a key molecular regulator of insulin sensitivity in POMC-lineage cells, potentially explaining its crucial role in energy homeostasis. Interestingly, previous studies have implicated TOX3 in the development of CNS , . In our work, however, we observed no difference in POMC-lineage cellular number or distribution between LoxP and PTKO mice, indicating that TOX3 is not required for the development or survival of POMC-lineage cells. Therefore, the observed reduction in POMC protein levels and the downregulation of POMC-lineage cellular functions likely reflect cell-autonomous functional impairment in mature POMC-lineage cells rather than a loss of neuronal populations.
Our study uncovers a previously uncharacterized regulatory mechanism in which TOX3 physically interacts with PTEN to modulate AKT signaling. PTEN serves as a crucial negative regulator of insulin signaling by catalyzing the conversion of phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-triphosphate (PIP3) to phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) in the PI3K pathway, thereby terminating AKT phosphorylation and influencing systemic metabolic regulation. For example, adipose tissue PTEN downregulation enhances AKT phosphorylation and suppresses lipolysis . Hepatic SR-A3 overexpression stabilize PTEN and reduces AKT phosphorylation . In the present study, we demonstrate that TOX3 overexpression reduces PTEN protein levels without affecting mRNA expression, indicating post-transcriptional regulation of PTEN by TOX3. We further establish that TOX3 promotes PTEN protein degradation, identifying the proteasome pathway as the primary mechanism for PTEN degradation. However, while we have identified this post-translational regulatory mechanism, several aspects require further investigation, including the structural basis of TOX3-PTEN interaction and the specific E3 ubiquitin ligases involved. Notably, we did not detect interactions between TOX3 and its previously reported binding partners (e.g., CREB, CITED1, FOXO1), suggesting context-dependent functional specificity of TOX3.
Our results demonstrate that TOX3 in POMC-lineage cells selectively regulates systemic energy expenditure with minimal impacts on appetite control. We propose that this functional specificity may stem from the well-documented heterogeneity of POMC neuronal subpopulations that serve distinct physiological functions , , although the precise mechanisms underlying this selectivity require further investigation. Specifically, we observed that TOX3 deletion in POMC-lineage cells significantly reduced energy expenditure, while its overexpression in hypothalamic POMC-lineage cells increased it-consistent with the established roles of POMC neurons in promoting energy dissipation , , , . Importantly, the RER remained unchanged in both loss- and gain-of-function models, indicating that the altered energy expenditure did not result from a shift in whole-body fuel preference. This pattern of metabolic changes points to alterations in basal metabolic rate and/or adaptive thermogenesis as the primary driver.
Importantly, we identified adipose tissue as the primary peripheral target mediating the metabolic effects of POMC-lineage cell-TOX3 signaling. This finding corroborates established neuroanatomical evidence demonstrating direct sympathetic innervation of adipose tissue depots by central neural circuits originating from POMC neurons , – . Specifically, we show that pharmacological activation of adipose tissue β3-adrenergic receptors restored metabolic function in PTKO mice, while sympathetic denervation of BAT substantially attenuated the phenotypic and transcriptional changes in BAT induced by TOX3 overexpression in POMC-lineage cells. These results provide compelling evidence for a TOX3-mediated hypothalamic-adipose neural regulatory axis originating in POMC-lineage cells. Notably, although TOX3 overexpression also increased NE content in WAT depots, sympathetic denervation experiments indicate that this modulation alone is not sufficient to drive the core body weight phenotype under our experimental conditions. Thus, while sympathetic tone to WAT may exert a modulatory role, it does not appear to be the dominant mediator of TOX3-dependent regulation of whole-body energy balance. Instead, our data support a model in which TOX3 in POMC-lineage cells preferentially enhances sympathetic output to BAT. The BAT then acts as the primary driver of increased energy expenditure, outweighing the relative contribution of sympathetic-mediated effects in WAT to the overall metabolic phenotype. However, several important questions remain unanswered, including whether hypothalamic POMC-lineage cell TOX3 directly modulates other peripheral metabolic processes - such as hepatic gluconeogenesis, hepatic lipid metabolism, pancreatic endocrine function, and SKM metabolism - as well as the molecular mechanisms underlying these potential effects.
Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although we have comprehensively characterized TOX3 expression and function in POMC-lineage cells and AgRP neurons, it is also expressed in other hypothalamic neuronal populations. Moreover, while minimal Tox3 expression was detected in tanycytes, astrocytes, and microglia, public scRNA-seq datasets indicate that Tox3 is enriched in oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), suggesting a potential role in glial biology. The physiological relevance of TOX3 in these other neuronal and non-neuronal cell types, particularly under metabolic challenge, represents an important avenue for future research. Second, our finding that HFD feeding markedly reduces hypothalamic TOX3 expression raises fundamental questions regarding the molecular mechanisms underlying this nutritional regulation, which require systematic elucidation. Third, the constitutive Pomc-Cre driver used here targets POMC-lineage cells rather than exclusively mature POMC neurons, including in extrahypothalamic lineages and developmental precursors , , complicating phenotypic attribution. The observed metabolic phenotypes, including effects on BAT thermogenesis and obesity development, are consistent with established functions of hypothalamic POMC neurons in energy balance regulation. However, we cannot definitively distinguish between contributions from mature POMC neurons and other POMC-lineage cells within the hypothalamus. While our AAV-based approach achieved adult-onset overexpression within the ARC, the loss-of-function model still carries the possibility of contributions from Tox3 deletion in off-target cell populations. The development and application of temporally controlled genetic tools will be necessary to precisely define the adult-specific functions of Tox3 in POMC neurons. Additionally, the lack of ideal cellular models that faithfully recapitulate POMC neurons remains a limitation in the field. Although GT1-7 and N2a cells have been used in prior studies , and in the present study as in vitro models for POMC research, neither fully recapitulates the molecular profile of mature POMC neurons. Future studies would benefit from developing more physiologically relevant systems—such as POMC-enriched cell lines or hypothalamic organoids—to better model cell-autonomous mechanisms in these neurons. Finally, while our murine models have provided mechanistic insights into TOX3’s role in POMC-lineage cells, we note that direct evidence linking TOX3 expression to obesity in humans is currently unavailable. Future studies that incorporate human datasets or relevant physiological models, such as hypothalamic organoids derived from human pluripotent stem cells, would help establish the translational relevance of this pathway in human energy homeostasis.
In conclusion, our study establishes a direct functional link between TOX3-previously uncharacterized in hypothalamic function-and a core metabolic signaling axis within the specific POMC-lineage cells central to energy homeostasis. We demonstrate that TOX3 modulates hypothalamic insulin signaling pathways, thereby regulating both POMC-lineage cellular activity and gene expression. These TOX3-mediated alterations in POMC-lineage cellular function directly control SNS outflow to adipose tissue, ultimately governing its metabolic activity. Moreover, we have identified a molecular interaction between TOX3 and PTEN that reveals a previously unrecognized mechanism of metabolic regulation. These findings significantly advance our understanding of TOX3’s physiological functions and highlight its therapeutic potential for the treatment of obesity and related metabolic disorders.

## Methods

## Mice
Pomc-Cre and Agrp-Cre strains were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (BarHarbor, ME, USA). Tox3flox/flox mice were kindly provided by Prof. Tao Huang at Shandong University. To generate cell type-specific Tox3 knockout mice, Tox3flox/flox mice were crossed with Pomc-Cre or Agrp-Cre mice to selectively delete Tox3 from POMC-lineage cells or AgRP neurons, respectively. The resulting mice are hereafter referred to as PTKO (POMC-lineage cell-specific TOX3 knockout) or ATKO (AgRP neuron-specific TOX3 knockout) mice, with Tox3flox/flox mice (designated as LoxP controls) serving as controls.
To assess TOX3 expression in hypothalamic POMC-lineage cells and AgRP neurons, we crossed Pomc-Cre or Agrp-Cre mice with Rosa26-lox-stop-lox-tdTomato (Ai9) reporter mice (The Jackson Laboratory) to generate Pomc-cre;Ai9 or Agrp-cre;Ai9 mice, respectively, in which POMC- or AgRP-expressing cells were labelled with tdTomato. To confirm TOX3 deletion specifically in POMC-lineage cells, Pomc-Cre and PTKO mice were crossed with Ai9 mice to generate conditional knockout mice or control mice with POMC-lineage cells were labelled by tdTomato, hereafter referred to as Pomc-cre;Ai9 or PTKO;Ai9 mice, respectively. Similarly, to confirm TOX3 deletion specifically in AgRP neurons, Agrp-Cre and ATKO mice were crossed with Ai9 mice to generate conditional knockout mice or control mice with AgRP neurons were labelled by tdTomato, hereafter referred to as Agrp-cre;Ai9 or ATKO;Ai9 mice, respectively.
The animals were housed under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle (lights on at 7:00 am, lights off at 7:00 pm) in a temperature-controlled room (22-24 °C) and had ad libitum access to food and water unless otherwise indicated. Standard environmental enrichment was provided. Mice were fed a standard chow diet (CD) (52 g/kg fat, cd-dossy, Chengdu, Sichuan, China) or a high-fat diet (HFD) (60% kcal from fat, D12492, Research Diets, NJ, USA). Weekly body weight measurements were taken between 6 to 20 weeks of age. All surgical and tissue collection were performed during the light phase. All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan University (No: 20230731003).

## Stereotaxic surgery
For hypothalamic neuron-specific TOX3 knockdown, we constructed neuronal-targeted AAV-hSyn-eGFP-miR30shRNA vectors targeting Tox3 (AAV-shTox3) or control (AAV-shCtrl). The miR30 backbone enhances shRNA processing and stability, promoting more potent and durable gene silencing compared to conventional shRNA designs . For TOX3 overexpression in hypothalamic neurons, we constructed neuronal-targeted AAV-hSyn-eGFP-Flag vectors targeting Tox3 (AAV-Tox3-Flag) or control (AAV-GFP-Flag). These viruses were packaged by the ObiO Technology (Shanghai, China). To induce the specific knockdown or overexpression of TOX3 in hypothalamic neurons, wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 J mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and then subjected to bilateral stereotaxic injections of AAV-shTox3/AAV-shCtrl (for knockdown), or AAV-GFP-Flag/AAV-Tox3-Flag (for overexpression) into the hypothalamus (coordinates: bregma, anterior-posterior, -1.5 mm; dorsal-ventral, -5.6 mm; lateral, ± 0.35 mm; 1000 nl per site).
To generate mouse models of Tox3 overexpression specifically in POMC-lineage cells or AgRP neurons, AAV-DIO-eGFP (AAV-GFP) and AAV-DIO-Tox3-eGFP (AAV-Tox3) vectors were generated, which were also packaged by ObiO Technology. To evaluate the therapeutic potential of TOX3 in the context of established obesity, Pomc-Cre or Agrp-Cre mice were first fed HFD for 8 weeks to induce obesity, and then anesthetized with isoflurane and received bilateral stereotaxic injections of either AAV-GFP or AAV-Tox3 vectors into the ARC (coordinates: bregma, anterior-posterior, -1.5 mm; dorsal-ventral, -5.85 mm; lateral, ± 0.3 mm; 500 nl per site).

## Body length and composition analysis
The body length (nose-to-anus length) of mice was determined by measuring the linear distance from the rostral tip of the nose to the caudal base of the tail . Body composition (lean and fat mass) was quantitatively assessed using the Small Animal Body Composition Analysis and Imaging System (MesoQMR23-060H-I; Nuimag Corp., Shanghai, China) at specified time points.

## Metabolic cage measurements
Metabolic parameters, including food intake, water intake, oxygen consumption (VO2), carbon dioxide production (VCO2), energy expenditure, RER, and locomotor activity, were measured using the Promethion Metabolic Monitoring System (Sale Systems International, LV, USA). Following a 2-day acclimation period, continuous measurements were recorded over 72 h. Data are presented as both hourly values over the 72 h-period and as averaged values across the light (07:00-19:00) and dark phases (19:00-07:00).

## Body temperature measurements in mice
Core body temperature was assessed via rectal measurement using a digital thermometer equipped with a specialized rectal probe (KEW, Nanjing, China), as previously described . Interscapular BAT surface temperature was determined by standardized infrared imaging technique using a calibrated infrared thermal imaging camera (FORTRIC, Shanghai, China), as previously described .

## Glucose tolerance test (GTT) and insulin tolerance test (ITT)
For the GTT, mice were fasted for 6 h before receiving an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of glucose dissolved in saline (2 g/kg body weight for CD-fed mice; 1 g/kg for HFD-fed mice). For the ITT, mice were fasted for 4 h prior to i.p. administration of insulin (0.75 U/kg for CD-fed mice; 1.5 U/kg for HFD-fed mice). Blood glucose levels were measured from the tail vein using an automatic glucose monitor (Accu-Check; Roche, Switzerland) at baseline (0 min) and at 15, 30, 60, 90, 120 min post-injection.

## Biochemical and hormone analysis in the serum and liver
Blood samples were collected from mice, and serum was isolated by centrifugation at 6,000 g for 10 min. For hepatic biochemical analysis, liver samples were homogenized in phosphate buffer solution (PBS) and extracted using chloroform: methanol (2:1, v/v), followed by centrifugation at 3,000 g for 10 min. The following commercial assay kits were used for biochemical and hormonal measurements: glucose, TC, and TG (Biosino, Beijing, China); NEFA (LabAssay NEFA, Wako Chemicals, VA, USA); insulin (ELISA kit #10-1247-01, Mercodia, Sweden).

## Norepinephrine turnover measurement
NE turnover was assessed using the AMPT method, as previously described , . Briefly, one cohort of mice was left untreated and sacrificed to obtain baseline tissue NE content. The remaining paired mice were injected with AMPT (300 mg/kg) at baseline, followed by a supplemental dose (150 mg/kg) 2 h later to sustain TH inhibition. Four hours after the initial AMPT injection, these mice were sacrificed, and adipose tissues were rapidly dissected, weighed, snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C until analysis. NE content in adipose tissues was quantified according to an established protocol . Briefly, tissues were homogenized in ice-cold extraction buffer (0.05 M hydrochloric acid in methanol) using a tissue dismembrator, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and 30 μL of tissue extract was mixed with 270 μL of 50% acetonitrile (in water) containing 0.05% ascorbic acid. After vortexing, the mixture was centrifuged again. Finally, 200 μL of the supernatant was analyzed by liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) (AB6500, AB SCIEX, USA). NE turnover was calculated according to the following formulas: k = (lg[NE]0-lg[NE]4)/(0.434 × 4) and K = k[NE]0, where k represents the rate constant of NE efflux, [NE]0 is the initial NE concentration, [NE]4 is the NE concentration after 4 hours, and K corresponds to NE turnover.

## Activation and denervation of sympathetic nervous system activity
For acute cold exposure experiments, 16-week-old chow-fed mice were exposed to 4 °C for 6 h under fasting conditions. Rectal and interscapular BAT temperatures were monitored at specified time points, and tissues were collected after 6 hours of cold exposure for further analysis.
For the activation of sympathetic nervous system activity, 14-week-old HFD-fed mice were fasted for 2 h before and after an i.p. bolus injection of CL316,243 (1 mg/kg body weight) administered at 10:00 am to minimize confounding effects of feeding. Tissues were collected 4 h post-injection for further analysis.
For the sympathetic denervation in BAT or WAT, HFD-fed Pomc-Cre mice first received bilateral stereotaxic injections of either AAV-GFP or AAV-Tox3. Subsequently, local denervation was performed by injecting 6-OHDA (10 mg/mL in saline containing ascorbic acid) into the adipose depots. As previously described , , 6-OHDA was administered at five distinct sites per BAT pad, or at 15 distinct sites per eWAT and iWAT pad (1 μL per site). Adipose tissues were collected four weeks after chemical denervation for subsequent analysis.

## Histological analysis
Mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation prior to tissue collection. Tissues including liver, eWAT, iWAT, and BAT were collected and fixed in 10% PBS neutral buffered formalin (NBF) for 48-72 h. Following fixation, the samples were dehydrated and embedded in paraffin according to a standard procedure and sectioned into 4-μm sections for further use. For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, tissue sections were processed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis, antigen retrieval was performed using citrate acid buffer, followed by blocking with 10% normal donkey or goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by appropriate secondary antibody incubation for 1 h at room temperature. Signal detection was achieved using chromogenic substrates. Lipid accumulation was assessed in liver cryosections (8 μm) using Oil Red O staining according to established protocols. The sections were visualized under optics microscope (Olympus, Japan) or laser scanning confocal microscopy (Nikon, Japan). Quantitative analysis of IHC staining was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

## Immunofluorescence and RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization
Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and transcardially perfused with saline followed by ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS prior to brain collection. For brain immunofluorescence staining, mice were transcardially perfused with saline followed by ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS. Brains were dissected and post-fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h at 4 °C, dehydrated in a graded sucrose series (10%, 20%, and 30% in PBS), and sectioned at 14 μm thickness. Brain sections were dried at 60 °C for 30 min, fixed in cold NBF for 15 min, and dehydrated through an increasing gradient ethanol. The slices were treated with hydrogen peroxide, and boiled in co-detection retrieval reagent, followed by primary incubation at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, they were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies and counterstained with DAPI.
For combined RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization and immunofluorescence staining, mouse brain sections were processed using the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Reagent Kit v2 and RNA-Protein Co-detection Ancillary Kit (323181 and 323100-USM, ACD, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, the brain slices were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The slices were then incubated with Protease Plus (40 °C, 30 min) followed by hybridization with the target probe (40 °C, 2 h) in a HybEZTM oven (ACD). Then, signal amplification was achieved using amplifiers AMP1-3 and visualized by TSA Vivid Fluorophore 650. After HRP blocker treatment, the slices were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies. Sections were then counterstained with DAPI for 2 min at room temperature and mounted.
Images were obtained using laser scanning confocal microscopy (Nikon, Japan; Zeiss, Germany). Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software (NIH). For quantitative assessment of overall signal intensity within a defined anatomical region, we first delineated the entire ARC as the region of interest (ROI) based on a reference atlas and DAPI staining. The fluorescent intensity within the ROI was measured for the specific channel using ImageJ. For co-localization analyses, we manually outlined the ARC as the ROI in each section, guided by DAPI staining and anatomical landmarks. Within this ROI, the tdTomato-positive or negative cells were identified using the corresponding fluorescence channel, and the mean fluorescence intensity of another signal was measured in each cell. The percentage of co-localized cells was calculated per animal and used for statistical analysis.

## Electrophysiology
Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings of neurons from Pomc-cre;Ai9 and PTKO;Ai9 mice fed with HFD were performed. Briefly, mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and then transcardially perfused with 95% O2 and 5% CO2 oxygenated ice-cold NMDG cutting solution comprising 93 mM NMDG, 93 mM HCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.2 mM NaH2PO4, 30 mM NaHCO3, 25 mM D-glucose, 20 mM HEPES, 5 mM Na-ascorbate, 2 mM thiourea, 3 mM Na-pyruvate, 10 mM MgSO4, and 0.5 mM CaCl2. The brain was quickly removed and immersed in ice-cold NMDG cutting solution. Coronal brain slices containing ARC (250 μm) were cut with a Leica VT1200s vibratome, recovered at 34  °C for 10-13 min in NMDG cutting solution, and then maintained at 25  °C in oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) (126 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgCl2, 26 mM NaHCO3, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, and 10 mM glucose) for 1 h until recordings. Slices were transferred to the recording chamber and perfused with oxygenated ACSF ( ~ 3 ml/min). Slices were visualized with infrared optics using an upright microscope equipped with a 40× water-immersion lens (Examiner. A1). Neurons from ARC were identified by their location and fluorescence. Whole-cell patch-clamp recording of action potential (AP) was obtained with a MultiClamp 700B amplifier, a Digidata 1440 A analog-to-digital converter, and pClamp 10.2 software (Molecular Devices). Voltage traces were sampled at 10 kHz and filtered at 2 kHz. Recording pipettes were filled with a solution containing: 135 mM K-gluconate, 5 mM KCl, 0.5 mM CaCl2, 10 mM HEPES, 2 mM Mg-ATP, 0.1 mM GTP, 5 mM EGTA, 300 mOsm. Resting membrane potentials (RMP) were measured within 1 min of break-in to the whole-cell configuration.

## Single-nucleus RNA sequencing and data processing
For single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq), hypothalamic tissues were rapidly dissected from HFD-fed LoxP and PTKO mice immediately after euthanasia by CO2 inhalation, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C until processing. All subsequent snRNA-seq procedures were performed by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd. Briefly, frozen tissues were thawed in lysis buffer and nuclei were isolated via gentle mechanical homogenization, followed by filtration and purification through a density gradient. Nuclei were resuspended in appropriate buffer, and their concentration was adjusted to approximately 1000 nuclei/μL. The suspension was then loaded onto the 10× Genomics Chromium Single Nuclei Gene Expression platform for library preparation and sequencing. Post-sequencing, FASTQ files were processed using CellRanger (10× Genomics) to generate raw count matrices by mapping RNAs to the transcriptome, assigning transcripts to individual cells, and removing duplicate reads. The resulting count matrices were processed using the Seurat package in R. Cell clusters were annotated based on canonical marker genes, which were cross-validated using CellMarkers 4.0 to ensure accurate cell-type identification.

## Neuron isolation, fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), and RNA sequencing
Hypothalamus from three Pomc-cre;Ai9 or PTKO;Ai9 male mice fed an HFD for 16 weeks were pooled per experimental sample. Single-cell suspensions were prepared using the Adult Brain Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-107-677) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, freshly dissected hypothalamus was rinsed in ice-cold Dulbecco’s PBS (D-PBS) and subsequently transferred into gentleMACS C Tubes (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-093-237) containing the enzymatic digestion mixture. Tissue dissociation was performed using the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator with Heaters (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-096-427). The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm nylon mesh and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 minutes at 4  °C, followed by removal of cellular debris and erythrocytes. The pelleted cells were resuspended in D-PBS, and cell viability was assessed using 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD; Beyotime Biotechnology, #C1053S, Shanghai, China). Fluorescence-activated cell sorting was conducted using a BD FACSAria SORP cell sorter (BD Biosciences) at the Flow Cytometry Core Facility. tdTomato-positive cells were selectively collected for subsequent RNA sequencing analysis, which was performed by Shi-Bio Technology (Wuhan, China).

## Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) and Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)
To identify TOX3-interacting partners in hypothalamic neurons, we generated a mouse model enabling neuron-specific overexpression of TOX3 within the hypothalamus. Four weeks post-injection, mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation, and hypothalamic tissues were immediately isolated and homogenized in IP lysis buffer. Lysates were incubated with anti-Flag antibody overnight at 4  °C, followed by incubation with Protein A/G magnetic beads (Selleck, #B23202) for 4 h at 4  °C. After extensive washing with lysis buffer, immunoprecipitates were resolved by SDS-PAGE. Gel sections were excised for in-gel tryptic digestion, and resulting peptides were analyzed by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Potential TOX3 interactors were defined as proteins exclusively identified in AAV-Tox3-Flag samples but absent in AAV-Flag controls.
For Co-IP validation, hypothalamic lysates from AAV-Tox3-Flag-injected mice were immunoprecipitated with either control IgG or anti-Flag antibody overnight at 4  °C, followed by Protein A/G bead capture (4 h, 4  °C). After washing, complexes were eluted and analyzed by SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting.

## Cell culture, transfections, and treatments
GT1-7 cells and N2a cells were kindly provided by Prof. Lu Gan at Sichuan University. These cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37  °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified atmosphere. To overexpress PTEN, the full-length coding sequence of mouse Pten was cloned into the pcDNA3.1-Myc vector, and cells were transfected with either Pten-Myc construct or empty vector control using LEPI transfection reagent. For TOX3 overexpression experiments, cells were transfected with vectors encoding either mouse Tox3-Flag or vehicle for 48 h. For TOX3 knockdown, cells were transfected with Tox3-specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) using Hiperfect transfection reagent (Qiagen, #301707) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For insulin signaling analysis, cells were serum-starved for 4 hours prior to stimulation with insulin (100 nM) for 0, 5, or 10 minutes at 37  °C.

## RNA extraction, reverse transcription, and quantitative PCR
Total RNA was extracted from tissues harvested from mice euthanized by CO2 inhalation using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality was assessed by measuring the A260/A280 ratio using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was generated by using a reverse transcription kit (Takara Bio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time (qPCR) was performed using a Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) with SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Target gene expression was normalized to 18 s rRNA, and the results were presented as fold change by comparison with the control groups. All primer sequences are provided in Table  .

## Protein extraction and Western blot analysis
Tissue samples harvested from mice euthanized by CO2 inhalation or cell samples were homogenized in lysis buffer. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4  °C to obtain clarified supernatants. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Equal amounts of proteins were loaded on SDS-PAGE gels, transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, and probed with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The following day, blots were rinsed and incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, then visualized using the LI-COR Odyssey System (Lincoln, NE, USA). β-Actin served as the loading control for normalization. All primary antibodies used are detailed in Table  . The uncropped blots were provided in the Source Data file.

## Statistics
All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Significance was determined by two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Sidak’s multiple comparisons test, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, and one-way ANOVA with post-hoc Tukey’s test as specified in the figure legends. Differences in the relationship between energy expenditure and lean body mass were analyzed by two-sided analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with lean body mass included as a covariate, using the online tool CalR2 (available at CalR2 (calrapp.org)). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The specific sample sizes (n values) for each experimental group are provided in the corresponding figure legends, representing biological replicates (individual mice). At least three independent experiments were repeated with similar results.
