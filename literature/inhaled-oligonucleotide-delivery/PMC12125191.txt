## Abstract
Bi-allelic autosomal recessive pathogenic variants in DNAJC12 lead to a constellation of neurological features, including young-onset Parkinson’s disease. DNAJC12 is a co-chaperone for enzymes involved in biogenic amines synthesis. In vitro, we discovered overexpressed DNAJC12 forms a complex with guanine triphosphate cyclohydrolase 1 (GCH1), a rate-limiting enzyme in the synthesis of tetrahydrobiopterin, a cofactor for biogenic amine synthesis. We also confirm DNAJC12’s interaction with tyrosine (TH) and tryptophan hydroxylases, paramount for dopamine (DA) and serotonin (5-HT) synthesis. In-vitro knock-down of DNAJC12 with a siRNA destabilizes DNAJC12-TH-GCH1 complex, whereas reciprocal co-overexpression of TH and GCH1 increases endogenous DNAJC12. Dnajc12 knock-out mice (DKO) exhibit reduced exploratory behavior at 3 months of age in open-field testing. In striatal tissue, total DA and 5-HT, and electrically evoked DA release are all reduced, with enhanced phosphorylation of Th at Ser31 and Ser40. DKO mice present models to develop/refine therapeutics approaches for biogenic amines disorders.

## Introduction
A major pathological hallmark of neurodegenerative diseases is the presence of intracellular and extracellular protein inclusions, indicative of altered proteostasis , . The maintenance of the cellular proteome requires molecular chaperones known as heat shock proteins (HSP), including HSP90, HSP70, HSP60, HSP40, and small HSPs. This diverse superfamily has a fundamental role in protein folding, trafficking, and degradation , . Biallelic, recessively inherited pathogenic variants in DNAJC12, a member of the DNAJC family, also referred to as HSP40, induce a multitude of neurological disorders, including young-onset Parkinsonism, infantile dystonia, developmental delay, intellectual disability, and neuropsychiatric disorders, which are associated with monoaminergic deficits – . Variants in DNAJC12 also account for hyperphenylalaninemia (HPA) in ~5% of patients, which is most frequently ascribed to variants in phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) or its cofactor tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4) , , , . Monoamines (also referred to as biogenic amines) consist of the catecholamines [dopamine (DA), noradrenaline and adrenaline] and serotonin (5-HT), which facilitate a plethora of functions in the central and peripheral nervous system (CNS/PNS) as neurotransmitters, trophic factors and hormones , – . The synthesis of catecholamines starts with the amino acid tyrosine (Tyr), which is obtained from the diet or via the liver, and the hydroxylation of phenylalanine (Phe) by PAH. In dopaminergic neurons, Tyr is hydroxylated by the rate-limiting enzyme tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) to form dihydroxyphenylalanine (levodopa), which is subsequently converted to DA by aromatic amino acid decarboxylase (AADC). The same biological pathway in adrenergic neurons and chromaffin cells in the adrenal medulla uses a series of overlapping metabolic steps, enzymes, and cofactors to synthesize noradrenaline and adrenaline , – . Conversely, 5-HT synthesis begins with the hydroxylation of tryptophan (Trp) to 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP) by tryptophan hydroxylase 1 (TPH1) in the PNS and TPH2 in the CNS, which is similarly converted into 5-HT by AADC , . Although its function has yet to be fully elucidated, DNAJC12 is widely regarded as a co-chaperone for aromatic amino acid hydroxylases (AAAHs), including PAH, TH, TPH1, and TPH2, all of which are co-factored by BH4 , , . As such, patients with DNAJC12 variants have a deficiency in brain DA and 5-HT as depicted by the reduction in the concentration of their metabolites, homovanillic acid (HVA) and 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) in their cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) – , .
Several members of the DNAJC family of co-chaperones besides DNAJC12 are implicated in Parkinsonism, including cysteine string protein-α (DNAJC5), auxilin (DNAJC6), receptor-mediated endocytosis-8 (DNAJC13), and cyclic G-associated kinase (DNAJC26) – . We have previously identified pathogenic variants in guanosine triphosphate (GTP) cyclohydrolase I (GCH1), the rate-limiting step in the conversion of GTP to tetrahydrobiopterin (BH4). Heterozygous patients with mutant GCH1 either present with postural dystonia in childhood that becomes generalized , or with late-onset Parkinson’s disease (PD) , . The former appears to be a forme fruste of the latter; moreover, as both occur within different individuals of the same families, with the same mutations, within genes in intersecting pathways. What underlies this profound difference in penetrance and presentation remains enigmatic . Dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra par compacta (SNpc) extend their axons via the medial forebrain bundle to the striatum (STR) where DA is released as a volumetric neuromodulator of synaptic transmission . Clearly, dystonia and/or parkinsonism in patients with DNAJC12 – , , or GCH1 , , , pathogenic variants can be attributed to DA dysregulation as each is responsive to levodopa treatment, a DA-replacement therapy. It is important to note that GCH1 deficiencies are complex, with many pathogenic variants associated with HPA, accompanied by a reduction in DA and 5-HT, while others are characterized by a reduction of DA without apparent HPA and 5-HT deficiency , . Hence, patients with DNAJC12 variants benefit from both orally administrated levodopa and 5-HTP – , . However, current treatment guidelines are evidence-based, cross-sectional, and lack consensus, and literature on longer-term follow-up is sparse. Anecdotally, those treated in the first 3 months of life may have an almost complete restoration of neurological function, but it is unclear if symptoms reappear or increase in severity with age. Hence, physiological models of Dnajc12 deficiency may illuminate the mechanistic role of DNAJC12 in biogenic amine synthesis, and reveal novel strategies to re-establish biogenic amine levels to help alleviate Parkinsonism, dystonia, and intellectual disabilities , , , .
The first homozygous pathogenic variant discovered in human disease was DNAJC12 c.79-2A>G (p.V27WfsTer14), which results in exon 2 skipping, transcript frameshift, and nonsense-mediated decay . Normally, exons 2 and 3 encode the DNAJC and Hsp70 interaction domains. Here we confirm/identify the interaction of DNAJC12 with DA and 5-HT synthesizing enzymes in HEK293FT cells. Further, we describe the generation of Dnajc12 knock-out mice (Dnjac12 p.V27WfsTer71; DKO), we characterize their biogenic amines and behavior, and focus on the role of Dnajc12 in DA synthesis and physiology.

## Results

## DNAJC12 interacts with TH, TPH, GCH1, and HSC70 in vitro
Recent unbiased mass-spectrometry in HEK293 cells overexpressing the co-chaperone DNAJC12 revealed its interaction with DA and 5-HT synthesizing enzymes TH and TPH2, respectively . To build upon this data, HEK293 cells transiently co-transfected with combinations of epitope-tagged DNAJC12 (GFP-DNAJC12), TH (FLAG-TH), TPH1 (FLAG-TPH1), and GCH1 (MYC-GCH1) were lysed and immunoprecipitated with GFP-agarose beads. GFP-tagged DNAJC12 co-immunoprecipitated FLAG-TH (Fig. ), FLAG-TPH1 (Fig. ), MYC-GCH1 (Fig. ), and endogenous heat shock cognate 70 (HSC70) (Fig. ), while empty vector GFP control did not, consistent with recent mass-spectrometry findings and other in vitro interaction studies . Furthermore, co-overexpression of MYC-GCH1 in HEK293 increased the interaction between FLAG-TH or FLAG-TPH1, GFP-DNACJ12, and endogenous HSC70 (Fig. ) while knock-down of DNAJC12 with a siRNA abolished the interaction between TH and GCH1, without reducing the total levels of these proteins (Fig. ). Unexpectedly, co-overexpression of TH and GCH1 increased levels of endogenous DNAJC12, while their individual expression had no effects (Fig. ).
 

## DKO mice have a loss of Dnajc12
Loss of Dnajc12 was confirmed in homozygous DKO mice in midbrain (MB; where the cell bodies of dopaminergic neurons reside) and striatum (STR; location of their terminals) by genomic PCR (Fig. ) and Western blotting (WB; Fig. ). The same lysates were immunoprecipitated with anti-Th antibody-coated beads, then eluted and subjected to WB analysis, which confirmed the Th–Gch1 interaction is not limited to overexpression systems and gave complementary findings (Fig. ).
 

## DKO mice have impaired locomotion and exploratory behavior in open field testing at 3 M
The behavior of homozygous DKO mice was examined at 3 M relative to their WT littermates. In automated open-field testing, DKO mice had a significant reduction in their ambulatory distance (Fig. ), ambulatory time (Fig. ), number of rears (Fig. ), and average speed (Fig. ), along with an increase in resting time (Fig. ), all of which are indicative of impaired locomotion/exploratory behavior. There were no deficits in grip strength (Fig. ) or motor coordination on a beam (Fig. ).
 

## Th protein and its phosphorylated forms are altered in MB and STR of 3 M DKO mice
As DNAJC12 is a chaperone for TH , we examined the consequences of its loss on Th protein and its phosphorylated forms in MB and STR and showed that total Th protein levels (Fig. ) were comparable between WT and DKO mice. These findings were consistent with the IHC showing comparable levels in the volume of Th-positive DA neurons in the SN (Fig. ). While Th immunoreactivity in the STR was not significantly decreased in DKO versus WT mice some decrement is evident (p = 0.0571, n = 4) (i). The total pSer31 and pSer40 were all elevated in MB (Fig. ); while pSer40 immunoreactivity was not significantly increased in the MB, some increase is obvious (p = 0.0643 n = 4) (Fig. ). In contrast, in STR Th pSer31 was significantly reduced while pSer40 was significantly increased compared to WT mice (Fig. ). Similarly, pSer40 immunoreactivity was increased (Fig. ). Gch1 expression was increased in STR (Fig. ), while reduced in MB (Fig. ), meanwhile Aadc (Fig. ) was significantly increased in both brain regions. Noradrenergic neurons of the locus coeruleus extend their diffuse projections into the STR releasing noradrenaline, while serotonergic neurons of the raphe nucleus extend their axons to the STR releasing 5-HT. We show that the levels of striatal noradrenaline transporter (NET) (Fig. ) and serotonin transporter (SERT) (Fig. ) were comparable between DKO mice and their WT littermates. Additionally, Hsc70 may influence DA synthesis, given it potentiates interactions with DA synthesizing enzymes, TH and AADC, and with the vesicular monoamine transporter (VMAT2), which packages DA into vesicles , . Similarly, 14-3-3 can modulate Th phosphorylation to control its activity, influencing DA synthesis . The protein expression of Hsc70 and 14-3-3 in MB and STR (Fig. ) were comparable between DKO and their WT littermates.
 

## DKO mice have reduced striatal DA and 5-HT
Biogenic amines in micro-dissected STR were measured by HPLC. Striatal DA (43.70 versus 58.86 ng/mg), and its metabolites 3,4 -dihydroxyphenylacetic acid (DOPAC) (3.20 versus 5.62 ng/mg) and HVA (4.05 versus 6.82 ng/mg) were markedly reduced in DKO mice compared to their WT littermates (Fig. ). DKO mice also exhibit reduced levels of striatal 5-HT (5.71 versus 9.38 ng/mg) and its metabolite 5-HIAA (0.83 versus 3.49 ng/mg) (Fig. ) and increased plasma levels of Phe (Fig. ) (71.51 versus 45.15 µM). However, other amino acids such as Tyr and Trp (Fig. ) were unchanged. Reduced striatal 5-HT coincides with the role of Dnajc12 as a co-chaperone for TPH2, the rate-limiting enzyme in the synthesis of 5-HT in the CNS, while increased Phe levels denote the role of Dnajc12 as a co-chaperone for PAH that catalyzes the conversion of Phe into Tyr, an essential amino acid required for DA synthesis. Interestingly, the loss of Dnajc12 did not change the levels of plasma biogenic amines as measured with mass-spectrometry (Fig. ). Elevated levels of Phe have been associated with impaired myelination in the brain; therefore, we examined the levels of oligodendroglial proteins, including myelin basic protein (MBP) and olig2, in Str and MB, which were comparable between WT and DKO mice (Fig. ).
 

## Loss of Dnajc12 reduces electrically-evoked DA peak response in striatal brain slices
To examine dynamic changes in striatal DA, we measured DA release and reuptake in ex vivo striatal slices with fast scan cyclic voltammetry (FSCV) (Fig. ). When electrically stimulated, slices from DKO mice have a significantly lower peak response compared to WT mice (0.14 versus 0.30 μM) (Fig. ), which concurs with reduced striatal DA. The decay constant tau was comparable between DKO and WT mice (415 versus 425 ms) (Fig. ), signifying unaltered reuptake kinetics of DA by terminals.
 

## Expression of endocytic and/or synaptic proteins is altered in STR of 3 M DKO mice
DNAJC12 interacts with TH, VMAT2, AADC, and HSC70, making it central to DA synthesis , . As such, its loss may alter endocytic proteins that foster the formation and recycling of synaptic vesicles, along with proteins involved in DA neurotransmission. Thus, we examined the expression of striatal endocytic and synaptic proteins, including other DNAJC family members implicated in Parkinsonism. There were no changes in proteins such as clathrin heavy chain (Chc) (Fig. ), vesicular associated membrane protein 2 (Vamp2) (Fig. ), and cysteine string protein-α (Dnajc5) (Fig. ). In contrast, synaptosomal associated protein 25 (Snap25) (Fig. ) was significantly reduced, while endo A1 (Fig e) was increased in DKO mice compared to WT, indicative of major changes in axonal endocytosis and synaptic vesicle recycling. Dopaminergic synaptic density in the STR was not altered in the DKO mice compared to WT, as illustrated by dopamine transporter (Dat) immunoreactivity (Fig. ).
 

## Discussion
Biogenic amine deficiency is a core feature of patients with DNAJC12 mutations, congruent with this protein’s role as a co-chaperone for DA and 5-HT synthesizing enzymes – . Hence, deficits in the DA and 5-HT systems are likely responsible for many (but not all) of the neurological problems observed in patients with DNAJC12 mutations , , , , . Earlier studies with unbiased mass-spectrometry in HEK293 cells overexpressing DNAJC12 revealed its interaction with TH, TPH1, and TPH2, which is confirmed in vitro by co-immunoprecipitation of transiently co-transfected GFP-tagged DNAJC12 with FLAG-TH, FLAG-TPH1, and untagged TPH2 in HEK293 cells , – . We also confirmed that DNAJC12 co-immunoprecipitates Hsc70, consistent with prior immunoaffinity-mass spectrometry , that may influence DA biosynthesis and packaging by its interaction with VMAT2, TH, and AADC , . We have also shown DNAJC12 interacts with GCH1, forming a complex with TH in vitro, and that this complex is destabilized when DNAJC12 is knocked-down, without markedly reducing levels of GCH1 or TH, which we similarly confirmed in MB tissues of WT and DKO mice. Overexpression of both TH and GCH1 enhances levels of endogenous DNAJC12, suggesting it is required for the stability of the former enzymes/complexes. Similarly, Cao and colleagues showed in HEK293 cells that Dnajc12 ensures stability and prevents degradation of TPH1 and TPH2 . Hence, modulation of DNAJC12-GCH1-TH levels with pharmaceutical agents, such as sapropterin dihydrochloride to supplement BH4, or replacement of DNAJC12 with adeno-associated viral delivery, are likely to be of therapeutic benefit for biogenic amine deficiencies. We extended in vitro studies to assess the loss of Dnajc12 on biogenic amine biology, physiology, and behavior in 3 M DKO mice homozygous for the Dnajc12 p.V27WfsTer71 allele. This genetic modification is analogous to DNAJC12 c.79-2A>G (p.V27WfsTer14), first discovered in a patient with young-onset Parkinsonism and results in nonsense-mediated decay . Comparative analyses in 3 M DKO and WT littermates indicate DKO has a significant reduction in locomotion and exploratory behavior. Bradykinesia is a core feature required for a diagnosis of PD. However, motor deficits in patients with pathogenic DNAJC12 variants are often preceded by neurodevelopmental and neuropsychiatric features, which may also explain reduced exploratory/locomotive behavior in the DKO mice. Additional testing in 3 M DKO mice, using an elevated-plus maze for anxiety-like behavior or Bussey-Saksida Rodent Touch Screen boxes for cognition, might reveal additional behavioral phenotypes.
In PD, the degeneration of DA neurons in the SNpc appears to start in striatal terminals, and is accompanied by reduced TH protein/activity in remaining neurons – . Nevertheless, the initial description of a patient with homozygous DNAJC12 c.79-2A>G (p.V27WfsTer14) suggests an enzymatic form of Parkinsonism for which longitudinal imaging revealed little evidence of progressive dopaminergic deficits . In DKO versus WT mice, striatal DA, its metabolites DOPAC/HVA, and the amplitude of evoked-released DA in ex vivo slices were all significantly reduced. In DKO mice, the loss of Dnajc12 appears to alter the interaction, stability, trafficking, and activity of Th, albeit culminating in reduced DA levels and behavioral deficits. As such, we expected Th levels and activity in our DKO mice to be reduced, as reported by Cao et al. , with other AAAHs including PAH, TPH1, and TPH2 in the liver, raphe nucleus, and pineal gland. Thus, we investigated the effects of Dnajc12 ablation on nigrostriatal DA synthesis by quantifying Th levels and phosphorylation in micro-dissected MB and STR. Phosphorylation of Th Ser31 and Ser40 was both elevated in the MB in DKO compared to WT, but in the STR, pSer31 was reduced, and only pSer40 was increased. TH activity and intracellular localization is known to be regulated through differential phosphorylation of 4 serine residues, Ser 9, 19, 31, and 40 . Phosphorylation of serine 9 and 19 does not directly affect activity but is part of a hierarchical cascade of activation. Phosphorylation at Ser31 appears to regulate TH stability/activity by targeting the enzyme to synaptic vesicles through interaction with VMAT2 and α-synuclein. Ser31 phosphorylation promotes transport along microtubules to axonal terminals, but effects on DA biosynthesis are indirect . In general, there is a greater L-DOPA and DA content in axons compared to the somato-dendritic compartment, which coincides with Th pSer31 levels , . The reduction of striatal Ser31 phosphorylation in our mice may be a consequence of mutant Dnajc12 destabilizing Th and its protein complexes . In contrast, the phosphorylation of Th at pSer40 increases activity by preventing end-product feedback inhibition by DA . Increased Th pSer40 in DKO mice in both STR and MB may reflect compensation to modulate its activity to meet the demands of DA synthesis from the loss of Dnajc12 , which is also congruent with Gch1 and Aadc expression in these regions. As a corollary, Dnajc12 loss altered striatal synaptic proteins, including SNAP25 and Endo A1. Synaptic function is highly dependant on the interaction of specialized protein complexes. SNAP25 is a core component of the SNARE complex essential for exocytotic release of neurotransmitters – . Its reduction in DKO mice might exacerbate the existing impaired dopaminergic neurotransmission and contribute towards reduced locomotion/exploratory behavior. In contrast, Endo A1, which plays a role in vesicle endocytosis, mitochondrial morphology, and autophagy – , is increased in STR of DKO mice, potentially reflecting compensation within and beyond DA terminals. These alterations in synaptic proteins at 3 M of age likely precedes potential loss of DA neuron cell bodies, and their striatal terminals based of Th and Dat IHC in the MB and STR; however, it remains to be seen if DA cell bodies and axonal degeneration will occur in aged DKO mice.
In addition, DKO mice have reduced levels of striatal 5-HT, attributed to the role of DNAJC12 as a co-chaperone for TPH2, the rate-limiting enzyme for the synthesis of 5-HT in the CNS , . 5-HT in the CNS is critical for neurite outgrowth, synaptogenesis, and neurogenesis , . In contrast, peripheral 5-HT has a role in gastrointestinal motility and inflammation , – , while adrenal catecholamines (including DA, adrenaline, and noradrenaline) are essential in autonomic responses, secreted into the bloodstream as hormones with effects on many peripheral organs , , , . Fortunately, Cao et al. recently addressed this issue using an independent Dnajc12 knock-out model in which CRISPR/Cas9 ablated exon 3 to result in nonsense-mediated decay . These authors validated the loss of Dnajc12 protein in various peripheral organs, and in the raphe nucleus, cerebellum, and pineal gland . Cao and colleagues did not examine peripheral catecholamines in their DKO model; however, they showed reduction in 5-HT levels in the blood and gut . Nevertheless, plasma biogenic amines were comparable between DKO and WT. This discrepancy might be explained by sample preparation, as the latter study used whole blood, while we used plasma. Moreover, basal levels of peripheral biogenic amines might be unaltered but differ with autonomic responses that might yet be evoked with stress stimuli (fight or flight) or pathogens. The levels of plasma Tyr and Trp, which are essential amino acids required for the synthesis of DA and 5-HT, respectively, were not altered in DKO mice. However, plasma Phe was markedly increased, consistent with mild HPA in human patients with pathogenic DNAJC12 variants. HPA can also potentiate DA and 5-HT deficiency as Phe is a competitive inhibitor of TH, TPH1, and TPH2 , . DNAJC12 patients greatly benefit from a Phe-restricted diet, highlighting the toxicity of HPA, for which the leading mechanistic hypothesis is impairment of myelination in the brain; however, this is not apparent at 3 M of age as levels of oligodendroglial cell markers Mbp and Olig2 are unaltered in MB and STR.
Enzymatic pathways of catecholamine synthesis are well documented, but the composition and relationship of many molecular components are unknown. Here we characterize DKO mice, which manifest reduced locomotion/exploratory behavior and biogenic amine levels, and mild HPA, which recapitulate the effects of DNAJC12 mutations in human disease . Nevertheless, as constitutive Dnajc12 loss perturbs the synthesis of multiple biogenic amines, we also developed homozygous cDKO mice to enable tissue and cell-specific ablation of Dnajc12 with somatic Cre recombinase. To illustrate, Dnajc12 has been successfully ablated in MB and STR via bilateral intrastriatal injection of AAV2
TH: Cre-eGFP in cDKO mice (Fig. ). The route of administration and AAV used did not provide optimal transduction of DA neurons; however, this approach may be improved via intranigral injection of the AAV, with alternative catecholaminergic promoters such as Pitx3, or by crossing cDKO with Dat-IRES-Cre mice to achieve consistent/uniform loss of Dnajc12 in DA neurons in their progeny. Hence, DKO mice and their floxed founders, cDKO, provide preclinical models to study pathobiology and test therapeutic strategies to increase levels and activity of biogenic amine enzymes or DA/5-HT synthesis. Interestingly, studies in PD report reduced BH4 concentration and GCH1 activity in the SNpc and STR, and low BH4 levels in CSF , . BH4 may also modulate TH levels and activity, but nevertheless, preclinical experiments have yet to prove conclusive , . Increased levels of BH4 may stimulate TH activity and DA release – , or might be toxic to catecholamine neurons , . Hence, DKO/cDKO mice will prove a valuable tool to uncover the mechanism, timing, and therapeutic effects of sapropterin dihydrochloride supplementation (BH4) in vivo. Despite many more patients with DNAJC12-deficient HPA being identified through the addition of DNAJC12 genetic testing in newborn screening, the optimal treatment for patients has yet to be defined and remains an unmet need; cDKO/DKO mice can help bridge this gap .

## Methods

## Cell culture
HEK293FT (Thermo-fisher Scientific; R70007) cell line was maintained in high glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagles Medium (DMEM), respectively, supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS (Gibco), 2 mM l-glutamine (Thermo-Fisher Scientific), and penicillin/streptomycin. Cultures were maintained in 5% CO2 at 37 °C . Transient transfection of cDNA was performed using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo-Fisher Scientific), while siRNA was transfected using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo-Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All siRNAs were purchased from Dharmacon™ (Colorado, USA).

## Co-immunoprecipitation
For experiments where epitope-tagged constructs were transiently overexpressed, HEK293FT cells were seeded in 10 cm dishes and transfected with the indicated plasmids (GFP-DNAJC12, FLAG-TH, FLAG-TPH1, and MYC-GCH1). At 48 h post-transfection, cells were washed once in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed in 1 mL of Cell lysis buffer (Cell signaling #9803S), supplemented with 1x Halt™ Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermofisher #78442) and incubated on ice for 30 min with gentle agitation every 15 min before being cleared by centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 12 min at 4 °C. Twenty microliters of cleared lysate was collected as input, and GFP or FLAG were recovered from the lysate using 20 μL of pre-equilibrated GFP-Trap (gta-20, ChromoTek) or FLAG M2 affinity gel (Millipore-Sigma; A2220) for 1 h at 4 °C. Beads were collected by centrifugation and washed 4–6 times with 1 mL of lysis buffer, followed by centrifugation before elution (10 min, 90 °C) in 2x SDS-loading buffer prior to standard SDS-PAGE and western blotting (WB). For experiments where endogenous Th was immunoprecipitated, micro-dissected midbrains (MB) from 3-month-old (3 M) WT and DKO mice were homogenized and centrifuged as described above. Cleared lysates were incubated at 4 °C for 16 h with 1 µg of mouse anti-tyrosine TH (Sigma, T2928) or IgG control (Abcam; ab37355) on an end-over-end rotator. Protein-antibody complexes were captured using 40 µL of pre-equilibrated Protein A/G Agarose slurry at 4 °C for 2 h on an end-over-end rotator. Beads were collected by centrifugation and washed 4-6 times with 1 mL of lysis buffer, followed by centrifugation, before elution (8 min, 90 °C) in 2x SDS-loading buffer prior to standard SDS-PAGE and WB.

## Animals
All breeding and maintenance were performed on a congenic C57BL/6J strain background. Jackson Laboratories used CRISPR/Cas9 technology to introduce a pair of loxP sites flanking exon 2 of the Dnajc12 gene, generating Cre-conditional Dnajc12 mice (cDKO). Heterozygous mice were then crossed with transgenic animals that express germline Cre recombinase (JAX stock #006888) to ablate the intervening sequence and generate constitutive knock-out progeny (DKO) (Fig. ). Heterozygous offspring were subsequently bred to homozygosity, ensuring loss of the Cre recombinase transgene in the process. Dnajc12 is a 5-exon gene that encodes 198 amino acids (ENSMUST00000043317.7; Uniprot ID: Q9R022). Our DKO model was designed to emulate the c.79-2A>G variant, which ablates exon 2 and causes Dopa-responsive non-progressive Parkinsonism (Straniero et al., 2018). In the mouse genome, splicing of exons 1 to 3 is predicted to result in a similar frameshift and nonsense-mediated decay but would otherwise yield Dnajc12 p.V27WfsTer71. Nevertheless, ablation of the Dnaj domain (amino acids 8–80 of the wild-type sequence; Unipro ID: SSF46565) would completely compromise the chaperone ability of Dnajc12. Mouse models and full sequence maps (.gb) are available on request. All animal procedures at the University of Florida were performed in accordance with a protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. All mice were single-sex group housed in enrichment cages after weaning at post-natal day 21. All mice were supplied with a standard rodent chow and water ad libitum. Ear notches were taken at post-mortem for post-hoc genotyping with the following primer sequences 5′-CCAGGCCTGGCTTTTATTTG-3′ and 5′-TTAAGTCCAAACCAGCTGCC-3′ using standard thermocycling conditions. All experiments, data processing, and analysis were conducted in a blinded manner.

## Behavior
At 3 M of age, both male and female WT (n = 16) and DKO (n = 18) mice were subjected to a battery of behavioral tests starting with open field test, Grip strength test, and lastly balance beam, which are detailed below. Mice were habituated for 1 h in the behavioral suite prior to any testing, which took place during the light phase of the vivarium light cycle. For open field testing, mice were individually placed in the center of a 19” × 19” × 19” acrylic box and allowed to freely explore for a duration of 30 min. Their activity in the open field arena (including their ambulatory distance, ambulatory time, resting time, number of rears, and average speed) was monitored via the activity monitor system, which uses an array of infrared (IR) photo beams to measure locomotion. The acrylic box consists of 3 sets of matching IR photo beams that project across the open field along x, y, and z-axes. The software detects when and where the animal disrupts the photo beams and allows the open field arena to be broken into areas of interest, depending on the experiment .
Neuromuscular function and muscular strength were assessed via the grip strength test, which measures the peak force required to make the animal release its grip . The test apparatus consists of a baseplate, a trapezoidal stainless-steel grid, and a force sensor. To measure grip strength, mice are gently lifted by the tail so that the forelimbs or hindlimbs can grasp onto the steel grid and then gently pulled away from the apparatus by the tail until they release their grip. The force exerted on the steel grid by the mouse is recorded by the force sensor and analyzed with the Grip Strength software (Maze Engineers, Skokie, IL, USA). Balance beams were applied to examine the sensorineural balance and coordination in mice. The mice were placed on an elevated narrow square beam of 100 cm in length, with an enclosed dark platform at the end of the beam , . Mice are trained on a beam with a width of 24 mm on day 1, and their performance is tested with a 12 mm beam on day 2. Videos are recorded from both sides of the beam and scored for total foot slips by trained personnel in a blinded manner.

## Adeno-associated viral vectors (AAV), AAV design, and tropism
Plasmid synthesis and viral production were completed under a service contract with VectorBuilder Inc. For AAV production and QC control, a transfer plasmid carrying Cre-recombinases under the control of 3.0 kb murine TH promoter was co-transfected with a rep-cap 2 plasmid to mediate AAV replication in HEK293T cells. Seventy-two hours after transfection, viral particles were harvested from supernatant and concentrated by polyethylene glycol precipitation. For in vivo grade ultra-purified AAV, viral particles were further purified and concentrated by cesium chloride gradient ultracentrifugation at >1012 GC/ml in PBS buffer. A qPCR-based approach measured AAV titer. The quality control includes titer measurement, sterility testing for bacteria and fungi, and mycoplasma detection. The AAV was stored in PBS-based buffer at −80 °C for long-term storage.

## Stereotaxic surgery in cDKO mice
3 M cDKO mice were injected bilaterally in the STR with 2 μL of AAV2 TH: Cre-eGFP (or sterile saline as control) to document their utility in enabling dopaminergic-neuron specific ablation of Dnajc12. Mice were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane in 1% oxygen and then secured into a Kopf stereotaxic apparatus (Model 900 Small Animal Stereotaxic Instrument, David Kopf Instruments). All surgical procedures were performed under aseptic conditions. The scalp was scrubbed with betadine, and midline incision was made. A burr hole (0.5 mm) was drilled in the skull at –1.7 mm caudal and 1.6 mm lateral from the bregma. A 30-gauge needle mounted to a 10 µl syringe (Hamilton) preloaded with virus was lowered 1.2 mm from the brain surface. A UMP2 Micro-syringe Injector and Micro4 Controller (World Precision Instruments) was used to inject the virus at a constant rate over 10 min, and the needle was allowed to rest for an additional 10 min after injection to allow for the solution to settle and disperse, limiting the possibility of the solution to efflux. Afterwards, the needle was raised slowly, the burr holes were sealed and then the scalp incision closed with surgical sutures. Mice were monitored post-surgically and administered analgesics overnight. Surgical sutures were removed 7–14 days post-surgery. Mice were euthanized after 6 weeks for analysis.

## Tissue collection and homogenization
Mice were deeply anaesthetized via inhalation of isoflurane and then intracardially perfused with PBS prior to microdissection of MB and STR. For analysis of total protein, micro-dissected tissues were homogenized at 20% w/v in 1x Cell Signaling Lysis Buffer (#9803S) with 1x Halt™ Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo-fisher #78442) using a 1 mL glass Dounce homogenizer (Wheaton) and placed on ice for 30 min before being cleared by centrifugation (14,000 × g at 4 °C for 20 min). The total protein concentration in the supernatant was measured with BCA assay (Pierce) , .

## SDS-PAGE and WB
20–40 µg of denatured lysates were resolved by SDS-PAGE on 4–20% TGX Criterion TGX pre-cast gels (Bio-rad) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes by semi-dry trans-Blot Turbo transfer system (Bio-rad). The membranes were blocked with either 50% Intercept tris buffered saline (TBS; 927-60001), 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in TBS-Tween (TBST), or 5% milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature (RT) followed by incubation overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffers depending on the protein of interests. After incubation with primary antibodies, the membranes were washed in TBST (3 × 5 min), followed by incubation for 1 h at RT with fluorescently conjugated goat anti-mouse or rabbit IR Dye 680 or 800 antibodies (Licor). The blots were washed in TBST (3 × 5 min) and scanned on a ChemiDoc MP imaging platform (Bio-Rad) , .

## Immunofluorescence (IHC) with confocal microscopy
For imaging, mice were anesthetized via inhalation of isoflurane, intracardially perfused with PBS, and their brains were rapidly extracted then post-fixed overnight at 4 °C in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Cryoprotection of the brains was performed with an increasing sucrose gradient (10%, 20%, 30% in PBS) before sagittal slices (30 µm) were obtained with a cryostat. Sections were blocked in 10% normal goat serum (NGS) in 0.3% PBST (1 h, 37 °C), followed by incubation of primary antibodies: chicken anti-Th (ab76442 Abcam; 1:500); Rat anti-Dat (1:500, Millipore, MAB369); guinea pig anti-Cre (1:250, Cell signaling), Th pSer40 (1:500, Sigma, T9573, 1:1000), rabbit anti-Vmat2 anti-sera (1:500, gift from the lab of Dr. Gary Miller) made in antibody solution (5% NGS + 0.3% PBST) overnight at 4 °C. The sections were washed (4 × 15 min in PBS), followed by secondary antibody incubation with species-specific Alexafluor IgG secondary antibodies (1 h at RT, Invitrogen; 1:500). Tissues were rewashed in PBS (4 × 15 min), then co-stained with DAPI (ThermoFisher Scientific, 1:5000) and mounted using Fluoromount-G® (0100-20, Southern Biotech). Images were acquired at 20, 40, or 60x field of view on a Nikon J438A Spinning Disc Confocal Microscope using NIS-Elements AR software (90 z-stack steps at 0.30 μm spacing) and deconvoluted , . Th, Th pSer40, Vmat2, and Dat immunoreactivity in the MB and/or STR was quantified with the ImarisTM software (Oxford Instruments).

## Electrophysiology
FSCV was used to assess evoked DA release and reuptake in 300 μm hemi-coronal slices of the dorsolateral STR, as previous , . Coronal striatal slices obtained from 3 M DKO mice with a vibratome were perfused with oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) at RT for ~1 h prior to the experiments. Stimuli are delivered by nickel-chromium bipolar electrodes (made in-house) placed in the dorsolateral STR, optically isolated (World Precision Instruments, USA), and controlled/sequenced with ClampEx software. Voltammetric responses were recorded, standardized, and analyzed with an Invilog Voltammetry system and software (Invilog), using carbon fiber electrodes (diameter: 32 μm, length: 30 μm, sensitivity: 21–40 nA/μM; Invilog) and placed within 100–200 μm of the stimulating electrode in the dorsolateral STR. Field activity was recorded to ensure the viability of the slice during the recording. Input/output paradigms were as previous , . At the end of each recording session, a three-point calibration of each carbon fiber electrode is conducted (final concentrations of DA in a CSF (μM): 0.5, 1.0, and 2).

## High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)
In brief, mice were deeply anaesthetized via inhalation of isoflurane and then intracardially perfused with ice-cold PBS. The STR was micro-dissected and freshly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Processing of frozen striatal tissues and HPLC were performed by the Emory HPLC Bioanalytical Core according to their protocol. In brief, striatal tissues were suspended in 300 μL solution of 0.1 M PCA and 0.1 mM EDTA, followed by sonication, then centrifugation of the homogenates at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant. The concentration of DA,5-HT, and their metabolites in the supernatant was determined by reverse phase HPLC with electrochemical detection using an ESA 5600 A CoulArray detection system equipped with an ESA Model 584 pump and an ESA 542 refrigerated autosampler.

## Metabolomics
Plasma samples for DKO and WT mice were sent to Creative Proteomics to analyze free amino acids and related metabolites, as per their protocol. To summarize, 20 μL of each plasma sample was added into an Eppendorf tube containing 80 μL of the internal standard solution (contains 41 isotope-labeled internal standards). The resultant mixture was sonicated for 5 min and centrifuged at 21,000 × g for 10 min before taking the supernatant. Fifty microliters of supernatant for each sample or the standard calibration solution was mixed with 100 μL of 20 mM dansyl chloride solution and 100 μL of borate buffer (pH 9), and allowed to react for 30 min at 40 °C. Aliquots of the resultant solutions (5 μL) were injected to run UPLC-MRM/MS on a Waters Acquity UPLC system coupled to a Sciex QTRAP 6500 Plus mass spectrometer equipped with a heated ESI source. LC separation was carried out on a C18 UPLC column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.8 μm) with 0.1% formic acid in water and 0.1% formic acid in an acetonitrile-isopropanol mixed solvent as the binary-solvent mobile phase for gradient elution (20% to 90% B in 20 min), at 55 °C and 0.3 mL/min.

## Statistical analysis
The data for the automated open field test was acquired with Activity Monitor 7 software (Med Associates Inc.). Data acquisition and extraction of the spike corresponding to the force exerted by mice were analyzed with Grip Strength Software (Maze Engineers). DA traces for FSCV were analyzed with Clampfit (Molecular Devices), and the densitometric analyses were performed with Image J software. Immunohistochemical images were acquired via NIS-Elements AR software and quantified with the ImarisTM software (Oxford Instruments). Normality testing and statistical analysis of all the data were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. The data is presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM).
