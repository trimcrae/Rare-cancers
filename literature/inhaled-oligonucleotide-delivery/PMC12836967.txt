## Background
JunB expression found to be upregulated in sepsis-induced myocardial injury (SIMI) tissue samples, yet its role in this disease remains unexplored‌. This study aimed to investigate the role and underlying mechanism of JunB in SIMI.

## Methods
In vitro and in vivo SIMI models were established by stimulating H9C2 cardiomyocytes with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and inducing sepsis via cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) in mice, respectively. In both models, JunB expression was modulated, and several key parameters were evaluated, including apoptosis, levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL-6 and TNF-α), oxidative stress, ferroptosis, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. Additionally, the activation of PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway was examined. To confirm the mechanism of JunB, a PI3K/Akt-specific inhibitor, LY294002, was applied in the cell model.

## Results
JunB expression was elevated in both SIMI models. In vitro, JunB downregulation significantly increased cell viability, SOD activity, and GPX4 expression, while it decreased IL-6 and TNF-α levels, reduced apoptosis, ROS accumulation, MDA content, and ATF6 expression. In contrast, JunB upregulation produced the opposite effects. In vivo, downregulation of JunB reduced serum levels of myocardial injury markers (CK-MB and cTnT), improved myocardial damage, and decreased myocardial inflammation, apoptosis, oxidative stress, ferroptosis, and ER stress. JunB knockdown activated the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway in both models. LY294002 treatment reversed the protective effects of JunB silencing on LPS-induced cell injury.

## Conclusions
Knockdown of JunB alleviates sepsis-induced myocardial inflammation, apoptosis, oxidative stress, ferroptosis, and ER stress by activating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. This suggests that JunB may be a potent therapeutic target for SIMI.

## Introduction
 Sepsis is a critical global health issue caused by dysregulated immune response to infection, which can lead to multiple organ dysfunction [ ]. Despite notable advancements in medical care and growing awareness of the condition, sepsis remains a formidable cause of morbidity and death worldwide [ ]. According to the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2017, there were 48.9 million cases of sepsis and 11.0 million sepsis-related deaths globally in 2017 [ ].
Sepsis-induced myocardial injury (SIMI) is the most prevalent severe complication of sepsis [ ]. Research has demonstrated that more than 50% of sepsis patients experience different degrees of cardiac dysfunction [ ]. Moreover, the mortality rate of SIMI patients is alarmingly high, ranging from 70% to 90% [ , ]. Studies have shown that inflammatory responses, apoptosis, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis are key mechanisms underlying SIMI [ – ].
Jun B proto-oncogene (JunB) is a member of the Jun transcription factor family. It plays vital roles in many biological processes, such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, inflammation, ER stress [ – ]. JunB has been shown to participate in the pathophysiological processes of the heart. For example, JunB expression was elevated in both diabetic rats and high glucose-treated H9C2 cells. Jianpi Qinghua formula, which can alleviate diabetic myocardial injury, decreased myocardial JunB expression [ ]. Myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury notably upregulated JunB expression, and the protective effects of Theogallin were associated with the downregulation of JunB [ ]. However, JunB expression was decreased in the cardiac tissues of heart failure rats and angiotensin II-treated H9C2 cells. Overexpression of JunB suppressed angiotensin II-induced apoptosis of H9C2 cells [ ]. JunB expression was higher in sepsis blood samples than in age-matched healthy controls [ ]. Moreover, by analyzing the microarray datasets (GSE53007 and GSE79962), Lu et al. [ ] demonstrated that JunB expression was obviously upregulated in SIMI tissue samples. Subsequently, they confirmed this expression profile in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-treated HL-1 cells and AC16 cells [ ]. However, the role of JunB in SIMI has not been reported.
In this study, we will explore the function of JunB in LPS-induced H9C2 cardiomyocyte model and cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) surgery-induced SIMI mouse model. The phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) signaling pathway modulates multiple physiological functions and has been found to attenuate SIMI [ ]. Jiang et al. [ ] have demonstrated that silencing of JunB induces the activation of PI3K/Akt pathway to promote macrophage polarization towards an M2 phenotype. However, Qi and coworkers revealed that downregulation of JunB reversed TGF-β1-induced the activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway in NIH/3T3 cells [ ]. Hence, we hypothesized that JunB might regulate the PI3K/Akt pathway in SIMI. Hence, we further analyzed the mechanism of JunB with a primarily focus on the PI3K/AKT pathway.

## Materials and methods

## Cell culture, LPS treatment, and transfection
Rat H9C2 cardiomyocytes were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and grown in DMEM (Hyclone, Logan, Utah, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA). As previously reported [ , ], the cells were stimulated with 10 µg/mL LPS for 12 h to establish a SIMI cell model.
siRNA targeting rat JunB (si-JunB) with the sequence of 5’-TTTCTCTTCACGACTACAAAC-3’ was synthesized by RiboBio (Guangzhou, China). A scrambled siRNA with the sequence 5’-ATGTTACACTGCACTCTAAGT-3’ served as a negative control. The full-length cDNA of rat JunB was amplified from LPS-treated H9C2 cells and cloned into the pcDNA3.1 plasmid to generate the pcDNA3.1-JunB plasmid (OE-JunB). For transfection, H9C2 cardiomyocytes were seeded into appropriate cell culture plates and incubated for 24 h to achieve approximately 70% confluence. Subsequently, the constructed plasmid or siRNA was transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen) based on the manufacturer’s instructions. 48 h post-transfection, the cells were stimulated with 10 µg/mL LPS.

## Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay
The viability of H9C2 cardiomyocytes in response to different treatments was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates with 1 × 104 cells/100 µL medium per well. After indicated treatments, the cells in each well were incubated with 10 µL of CCK-8 solution for 2 h. Then, the optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioRad, Richmond, CA, USA).

## Flow cytometry for cell apoptosis
Apoptotic rate of H9C2 cardiomyocytes under specific treatments was determined by Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (Absin, Shanghai, China). Cells were treated with 0.25% trypsin without EDTA for digestion. The cells were collected after centrifugation at 2000 rpm for 5 min. After washing with cold PBS, the cells were resuspended in 1×Binding Buffer to achieve a cell concentration to 1 × 106 cells/mL. Subsequently, 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC was added to the cell suspension and incubated for 15 min in the dark. Then, 5 µL of PI solution was added to the mixture and incubated for 5 min. Finally, the apoptotic rate of the cells was analyzed using a FACScan flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA).

## Reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and ferrous iron (Fe2+) concentration assay
ROS production in H9C2 cardiomyocytes was examined using the ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime), which employs the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA. 12 h following LPS stimulation, the cell culture medium of H9C2 cardiomyocytes was discarded. 10 µM DCFH-DA was added to the cells and incubated for 20 min at 37 °C. The residual DCFH-DA solution was washed away three time using PBS. A fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was used to capture images.
The concentration of intracellular Fe2+ in H9C2 cardiomyocytes was evaluated using the Iron Test Kit (Cat. No. S1066; Beyotime). Briefly, cells were digested with trypsin without EDTA. Subsequently, BeyoLysis™ Buffer H for Metabolic Assay was added to lyse the cells at 4 °C. After centrifugation at 12,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected to measure the Fe2+ concentration after determining the protein concentration with BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime). The samples were treated with hydrochloric acid for 30 min at 60 °C, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 5 min at 4 °C. After reaction, the OD value at a wavelength of 593 nm was measured using a microplate reader. The concentration of Fe²⁺ was determined according to the standard curve and normalized to the total protein concentration.

## In vivo mouse model
Male C57BL/6 mice, 6–8-week-old, were purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd (China) and housed in the SPF environment. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Ethics Committee of Soochow University (Approval No. SUDA20230213A02). Recombinant adeno-associated virus 9 (AAV9) targeting mouse JunB gene under the control of the cardiac troponin T (cTnT) promoter (AAV9-sh-JunB) and a corresponding control vector (AAV9-sh-NC) were synthesized by Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The AAV9-sh-JunB can achieve cardiomyocyte-specific JunB knockdown in mice. After acclimation, the mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 6 per group): sham, CLP, CLP + AAV9-sh-NC, CLP + AAV9-sh-JunB. In the CLP group, mice received CLP surgery to establish polymicrobial sepsis model as previously described [ , ]. Briefly, mice underwent a 1 cm midline laparotomy procedure after being anesthetized with isoflurane. The distal end of the cecum was ligated with a 4 − 0 silk suture and punctured using a 21-gauge needle. Subsequently, approximately 0.2 mL of feces were extruded. Then, the cecum was replaced in the peritoneal cavity, and the incision was closed with a 4 − 0 silk suture. Following CLP surgery, 1 mL of preheated sterile 0.9% normal saline was subcutaneously injected into the mice for fluid resuscitation [ – ]. Mice in the sham group underwent a laparotomy without ligation and puncture. In the CLP + AAV9-sh-JunB and CLP + AAV9-sh-NC groups, mice were injected with 2 × 1011 viral genomes of AAV9-sh-JunB or AAV9-sh-NC via tail vein four weeks prior to CLP surgery [ ], respectively. 8 h after surgery [ ], all mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation. After collecting the blood samples, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Heart tissue samples were collected for further analysis.

## Examination of myocardial injury markers
The blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min, and the serum was collected. The levels of creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) and cTnT in the serum were analyzed to evaluate myocardial injury using appropriate colorimetric assay kits (Cat. No. ml037723 and ml23145) from mlbio (Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s protocols.

## Histological analysis
Heart tissues were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin solution, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thick. After deparaffinization with xylene and hydration using serially diluted ethanol, the slides were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) according to standard protocols. To analyze the apoptosis of cardiomyocytes in heart tissues, the sectioned slides were stained using the Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (Tunel) Apoptosis Assay kit (Beyotime) based on the manufacturer’s instructions. Images of cardiac tissues were observed under a light microscope. The percentage of apoptotic cells was calculated by the ratio of Tunel-positive cells to the total number of DAPI-positive nuclei, with six random fields per group.

## Assessment of the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), interleukin (IL)−6, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α
The levels of MDA and SOD were determined in both H9C2 cardiomyocytes and heart tissue homogenates using specific kits (Cat. No. A003-4-1 and A001-1–2; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.
To examine the concentrations of IL-6 and TNF-α in H9C2 cardiomyocytes and mouse myocardial tissues, specific ELISA kits (mlbio) were used, following the manufacturer’s protocols.

## Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)
Total RNA was isolated from H9C2 cardiomyocytes using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Subsequently, reverse transcription reaction was implemented using the Universal RT-PCR Kit (M-MLV) (Solarbio, Beijing, China), and qRT-PCR reactions were carried out using the SYBR Green qPCR Premix (Universal) (Solarbio) on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster, CA, USA). The sequences of primers used are listed in Table  . The relative expression level of each gene was estimated by the 2−ΔΔCT method, normalized to GAPDH.

 

## Western blot
Total proteins were extracted from H9C2 cardiomyocytes and mouse cardiac tissues using the RIPA buffer containing 1% PMSF. The concentration of total proteins was measured by the BCA Protein Assay Kit. Equal amounts of protein from each sample were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) for protein separation, followed by transfer onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Non-specific band was blocked by 5% skimmed milk. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated at 4 °C overnight with primary antibodies, including JunB (1:800 dilution; ab128878), glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4, 1:1000 dilution; ab252833), activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6, 1:1000 dilution; ab37149), PI3K (1:1000 dilution; ab191606), p-PI3K (1:1000 dilution, ab138364), Akt (1:10000 dilution; ab179463), p-Akt (1:1000 dilution; ab38449), p-mTOR (1:1000 dilution, #5536), mTOR (1:1000 dilution, #2983) and GAPDH (1:10000 dilution; ab181602). The p-mTOR and mTOR primary antibodies were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA), while the others were sourced from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA). After being probed with HRP‑conjugated secondary antibody, the membranes were visualized by adding enhanced chemiluminescence reagent and imaged using a Tanon-5200 chemiluminescent imaging system (Tanon, Shanghai, China). The gray-scale intensity of bands was measured by ImageJ software. The relative protein content was then normalized to GAPDH.

## Statistical analysis
Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least 3 independent experiments. The results were analyzed by the GraphPad Prism 10.0 software (San Diego, CA). One-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to compare differences among multiple groups. P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

## Results

## Knockdown of JunB decreases the inflammation and apoptosis in LPS-exposed cardiomyocytes
JunB expression was elevated in H9C2 cells after LPS treatment (Fig.  A and B). Subsequently, JunB was upregulated or downregulated in LPS-induced H9C2 cells, respectively (Fig.  A and B). The pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α increased by LPS were dramatically inhibited by JunB knockdown, but further enhanced by JunB overexpression (Fig.  C and D). Compared with control, LPS treatment inhibited the viability of H9C2 cells and increased the rate of apoptosis, particularly in the late apoptotic phase (Fig.  E and G). Downregulation of JunB increased cell viability and suppressed cell apoptosis in LPS-exposed cells. Overexpression of JunB showed the opposite effects on cell viability and apoptosis (Fig.  E and F). While modulation of JunB expression did not affect the viability, apoptosis and inflammation of H9C2 cells (Supplemental Fig.  ). These results suggest that knockdown of JunB decreases the inflammation and apoptosis in LPS-exposed cardiomyocytes.

 

## Knockdown of JunB inhibits Endoplasmic reticulum stress, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis in LPS-stimulated cardiomyocytes
JunB has been demonstrated to modulate ER stress in pancreatic beta-cells [ , ]. Hence, we examined the levels of ER stress markers after modulating JunB expression in H9C2 cells. The mRNA expression levels of ATF6, CHOP, and GRP78 were all increased by LPS. Silencing of JunB dramatically decreased the expression of these genes, whereas their expression was increased by overexpression of JunB in LPS-exposed H9C2 cells (Fig. A). The protein levels of ATF6 were also examined by western blot, and the results were consistent with its mRNA expression (Fig. B). There is a reciprocal relationship between ER stress and oxidative stress in some cases [ , ]. And ER stress is responsible for different cell death modes, including apoptosis and ferroptosis [ , ]. Therefore, we further analyzed the influences of JunB on oxidative stress and ferroptosis. Compared with the control group, the contents of ROS and MDA were increased, while the activity of antioxidant enzyme SOD was decreased in the LPS group, indicating oxidative stress induced by LPS (Fig. C and E). Downregulation of JunB significantly inhibited the contents of ROS and MDA, and elevated SOD activity in LPS-stimulated H9C2 cells. Contrarily, overexpression of JunB revealed the opposite effects on ROS, MDA, and SOD levels (Fig. C and E). Additionally, LPS stimulation significantly elevated Fe2+ level and decreased GPX4 expression in H9C2 cells. These effects were reversed after JunB downregulation, but strengthened after JunB upregulation (Fig. B and F). These data indicate that downregulation of JunB weakens LPS-induced ER stress, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes.

 

## Downregulation of JunB activates the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway in LPS-exposed H9C2 cells
The effect of JunB on the activation of PI3K/Akt/mTOR was evaluated in LPS-stimulated H9C2 cells. As shown in Fig.  , LPS exposure notably reduced the levels of p-PI3K/PI3K, p-Akt/Akt, and p-mTOR/mTOR. Compared with LPS-treated only, transfection with JunB knockdown plasmid significantly increased the levels of p-PI3K/PI3K, p-Akt/Akt, and p-mTOR/mTOR, while transfection with JunB overexpressing plasmid further decreased their levels (Fig.  ). These results suggest that downregulation of JunB activates the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway in LPS-exposed H9C2 cells.

 

## Silencing of JunB inhibits LPS-induced injury in H9C2 cells through activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway
In the following, we examined whether silencing of JunB inhibited LPS-induced injury in H9C2 cells through activation of the PI3K/Akt pathway. As shown in Fig.  , the levels of p-PI3K/PI3K, p-Akt/Akt, and p-mTOR/mTOR were inhibited by the PI3K/Akt inhibitor LY294002. Compared with the LPS + si-NC + DMSO group, cell viability, SOD activity, and GPX4 protein level were decreased, whereas IL-6 and TNF-α levels, apoptotic cell number, MDA content, Fe2+ concentration, and ATF6 protein level were increased, in the LPS + si-NC + LY294002 group (Fig.  A and I). Moreover, LY294002 partially alleviated the effects of si-JunB on cell viability, apoptosis, inflammatory factors, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis in LPS-exposed H9C2 cells (Fig.  A and I). This suggests that downregulation of JunB inhibits LPS-induced injury in H9C2 cells, at least partially, through activation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway.

 

## Downregulation of JunB improves myocardial injury in a mouse model of sepsis-induced myocardial damage
JunB expression in the heart was significantly elevated after CLP surgery, and decreased after injection with AAV9-sh-JunB (Fig.  A). CLP surgery elevated serum myocardial injury markers (CK-MB and cTnT) as well as inflammatory markers (IL-6 and TNF-α) in myocardial tissues. These effects were alleviated by AAV9-sh-JunB treatment (Fig.  B and E). Histological analysis using H&E staining confirmed the attenuation of myocardial injury by JunB downregulation, as indicated by a more orderly arrangement of myocardial fibers, less cell necrosis, and reduced inflammatory infiltration compared with the CLP-treated group (Fig.  F). Tunel staining revealed decreased cell apoptosis in heart tissues of CLP-injured mice injected with AAV9-sh-JunB (Fig.  G and H). MDA content was elevated, and SOD activity was suppressed by CLP, the effects of which were reversed after JunB silencing (Fig.  I and J). GPX4 expression was inhibited, while ATF6 level was promoted by CLP. This was abrogated after injection with AAV9-sh-JunB (Fig.  A). These results demonstrate that downregulation of JunB improves myocardial injury in a mouse model of SIMI.

 

## Silencing of JunB activates the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway in SIMI mouse model
The activation of PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway was evaluated in the heart tissues of mice. As shown in Fig.  , CLP injury dramatically decreased the levels of p-PI3K/PI3K, p-Akt/Akt, and p-mTOR/mTOR. JunB downregulation significantly elevated their levels in the myocardial tissues. This indicates that knockdown of JunB activates the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway that is suppressed by CLP surgery.

 

## Discussion
SIMI is a life-threatening condition frequently seen in critically ill patients. Unfortunately, the lack of effective treatments for this disease contributes to high mortality rates. Previous study has indicated that JunB is dramatically upregulated in human and mouse heart tissues affected by SIMI, as well as in LPS-induced cardiomyocytes in vitro [ ]. In this study, we demonstrated that silencing of JunB alleviated LPS-induced injury in H9C2 cells and CLP-induced myocardial injury in mice. The protective effect of JunB knockdown was through activating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway.
Sepsis triggers a systemic cascade of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-6 and TNF-α) and chemokines, causing extensive cell and tissue damage in many organs, including the heart. The cardiomyocyte inflammatory response is an important pathological change in SIMI that, in turn, promotes ROS generation and cardiomyocyte death [ , , ]. During sepsis, cardiomyocytes undergo multiple models of death, including apoptosis and ferroptosis [ ]. Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of cell death characterized by excessive lipid peroxidation [ ], and growing evidence indicates that cardiomyocyte ferroptosis contributes to SIMI [ , ]. Studies have shown that JunB gene expression is correlated with the induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines [ , ]. Our results also revealed that overexpression of JunB significantly promoted the productions of IL-6 and TNF-α, and silencing of JunB downregulated their expression in LPS-induced H9C2 cells and CLP-induced SIMI mouse model, indicating the pro-inflammatory effect of JunB in SIMI. JunB has been implicated in cardiomyocyte apoptosis; however, it may either suppress or promote cardiomyocyte apoptosis, depending on the disease context [ , ]. In this study, we found that JunB contributed to cardiomyocyte apoptosis in both LPS-induced cells and CLP-treated mice. As a metabolite of lipid peroxidation, MDA accumulates during ferroptosis, while the activity of antioxidant enzyme SOD is diminished [ , ]. GPX4 is a key inhibitory factor in ferroptosis regulation [ ]. We found that GPX4 expression and SOD activity were decreased, while ROS production, MDA and Fe2+ contents were increased in LPS-treated cardiomyocytes. CLP surgery also inhibited GPX4 expression and SOD activity, and elevated MDA content in mice. This suggested that oxidative stress and ferroptosis were induced during SIMI. Moreover, our results confirmed that silencing of JunB notably suppressed oxidative stress and ferroptosis in both models. Taken together, these data demonstrated that JunB knockdown inhibited inflammation, apoptosis, oxidative stress, and ferroptosis in SIMI.
The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a multifunctional organelle responsible for protein folding and assembly. During sepsis, stress and inflammation disrupt ER homoeostasis and disturb its functions, leading to ER stress [ ]. ATF6, CHOP, and GRP78 are commonly recognized as key markers of ER stress [ ]. Studies have reported that ER-related proteins are dramatically induced in the heart of sepsis mice, and inhibition of ER stress can attenuate SIMI [ , ]. JunB has been demonstrated to regulate ER stress [ , ]. In this study, ATF6 expression was markedly upregulated in LPS-stimulated cells and SIMI mouse model, while silencing of JunB decreased its expression. This indicated that knockdown of JunB significantly attenuated ER stress.
PI3K/Akt pathway has been found to relieve SIMI through modulating cell apoptosis, ferroptosis, inflammation, and oxidative stress [ , , ]. The activation of this pathway can also suppress ER stress [ ]. Jiang et al. [ ] demonstrated that silencing of JunB activated the PI3K/Akt pathway by suppressing the transcriptional activation of MIIP. In this study, we also found knockdown of JunB induced the activation of PI3K/Akt pathway in both LPS-exposed H9C2 cardiomyocytes and myocardial tissues from CLP-induced mice. Furthermore, JunB negatively regulated the phosphorylation of mTOR, an important downstream member of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway [ ]. A PI3K/Akt-specific inhibitor LY294002 [ ] partially attenuated the protective effects of JunB silencing on the inflammation, apoptosis, oxidative stress, ferroptosis, and ER stress in LPS-stimulated cardiomyocytes. These results indicated that knockdown of JunB ameliorated SIMI, at least partially, through inducing the activation of PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway.
Although our study offers important insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, we exclusively examined on JunB-mediated regulation of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway. NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways are also implicated in the pathogenesis of SIMI [ ] and are known to be modulated by JunB [ , ]. Therefore, it is imperative to further investigate whether JunB functions in SIMI through these pathways. Additionally, our assessment of cell injury was limited to a single dose of LPS (10 µg/mL) applied for 12 h. While this dose and treatment duration were chosen based on prior researches [ , ], and Tao et al. [ ] similarly showed that 10 µg/mL LPS for 24 h induced apoptosis, inflammation, and ferroptosis in H9C2 cells, it would be more valuable to explore the effects of JunB on different LPS doses and treatment times.
In summary, this is the first study to report the role of JunB in SIMI. Through in vitro and in vivo experiments, we find that downregulation of JunB alleviates sepsis-induced myocardial inflammation, apoptosis, oxidative stress, ferroptosis, and ER stress. This is related to the activation of PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway. Thus, targeting JunB may serve as a promising strategy to relieve cardiac damage induced by sepsis.
