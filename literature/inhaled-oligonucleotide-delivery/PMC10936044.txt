## Abstract
C1q/tumor necrosis factor–related protein 9 (CTRP9) is an adipokine and has high potential as a therapeutic target. However, the role of CTRP9 in cardiovascular disease pathogenesis remains unclear. We found CTRP9 to induce HDAC7 and p38 MAPK phosphorylation via tight regulation of AMPK in vascular endothelial cells, leading to angiogenesis through increased MEF2 activity. The expression of CTRP9 and atheroprotective MEF2 was decreased in plaque tissue of atherosclerotic patients and the ventricle of post-infarction mice. CTRP9 treatment inhibited the formation of atherosclerotic plaques in ApoE KO and CTRP9 KO mice. In addition, CTRP9 induced significant ischemic injury prevention in the post-MI mice. Clinically, serum CTRP9 levels were reduced in patients with MI compared with healthy controls. In summary, CTRP9 induces a vasoprotective response via the AMPK/HDAC7/p38 MAPK pathway in vascular endothelial cells, whereas its absence can contribute to atherosclerosis and MI. Hence, CTRP9 may represent a valuable therapeutic target and biomarker in cardiovascular diseases.

## INTRODUCTION
Cardiovascular diseases, such as myocardial infarction (MI), are the number one cause of death worldwide according to the World Health Organization and constitute the deadliest pathology. According to epidemiological studies, cardiovascular disease causes 17 million deaths each year worldwide, which, by 2030, is expected to increase to more than 23 million ( ). Accumulated evidence suggests that cardiovascular disease is a metabolic disorder and is closely related to obesity. There are two types of obesity: hyperplastic and hypertrophic obesity. Modern man’s obesity is mainly hypertrophic, an increase in body weight due to enlarged adipocytes ( ). Obesity induces complex remodeling of adipose tissue that consequently results in imbalanced adipokines secreted by adipocytes; thus, obesity directly contributes to the pathogenesis of the cardiovascular disease ( ). An increase in proinflammatory adipokines (leptin) and a decrease in anti-inflammatory adipokines [C1q/tumor necrosis factor (TNF)–related protein (CTRP) family] initiated by obesity may contribute to the pathogenesis of obesity-linked disorders. In addition, dysregulation of anti-inflammatory adipokines due to fat accumulation induces a local systemic chronic inflammatory response, leading to the initiation and progression of cardiovascular disease ( , ).
Moreover, obesity regulates the phenotype of perivascular adipose tissue (PVAT) and has a pathological effect on blood vessels ( , ). As adipose tissue is distributed around blood vessels, PVAT directly interacts with blood vessels and releases various important vasoactive factors as physiological and pathological paracrine effects ( ). Here, we focused on the direct effect of adipokines secreted by the adipose tissue surrounding vascular endothelial cells.
Adiponectin, a member of the CTRP family, is the most representative adipokine and has anti-inflammatory and vasculoprotective actions that contribute to cardiovascular disease prevention and amelioration ( , ). CTRP9 is one of the CTRP families most similar to adiponectin that contains a collagen tail domain and a globular C1q-like domain in the C terminus ( ). CTRP9 inhibits the inflammatory response in vascular endothelial cells ( ), induces vasoprotective effects, and attenuates cardiac remodeling after MI via 5anadenosine monophosphate–activated protein kinase (AMPK) phosphorylation ( ).
Histone deacetylase 7 (HDAC7) is a class II HDAC with intrinsic deacetylase activity, but it also partakes in mechanisms involved in development, proliferation, and inflammation. Furthermore, HDAC7 can be transferred from the nucleus to the cytoplasm upon its phosphorylation, as a shuttling protein ( ). As a downstream partner of AMPK, HDAC7 is expressed in the vascular endothelium in early embryogenesis and plays an important role in the maintenance of vascular integrity ( , ). Phosphorylation of HDAC7 by the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in vascular endothelial cells induces the nuclear export and increases the activity of myocyte enhancer factor-2 (MEF2), leading to angiogenesis ( , ). Another downstream partner of AMPK is p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), which is phosphorylated by AMPK and was reported to increase VEGF mRNA and secretion ( , ). However, CTRP9-induced AMPK-dependent angiogenesis in vascular endothelial cells is still poorly understood.
Circulating CTRP9 was reported to be decreased by a high-fat diet, and it was identified to be significantly reduced in the blood of leptin deficiency (db/db) obesity mice ( , ). In addition, CTRP9 was suggested as a disease biomarker for type 2 diabetes mellitus ( ), but no evidence is available supporting its role as a biomarker for MI.
In this study, we showed that CTRP9 mediates HDAC7 and p38 MAPK phosphorylation via the AMPK-dependent pathway in vascular endothelial cells, which, in turn, regulates the activity of the proangiogenic transcription factor MEF2, increases the expression and secretion of VEGF, and lastly induces angiogenesis. Hence, CTRP9 holds atheroprotective effects along with MEF2, and its expression is reduced in atherosclerosis and MI ( , ). In a surgical atherosclerosis disease model (left carotid artery partial ligation model), CTRP9 knockout (KO) mice showed significant progression of atherosclerotic plaques compared with wild-type (WT) animals. On the contrary, CTRP9 treatment exhibited improvement of atherosclerotic plaques in the surgical atherosclerosis disease model of apoprotein E (ApoE) KO and CTRP9 KO mice.
In addition, CTRP9 treatment showed a significant preventive effect on heart ischemic injury in the MI-induced mouse model. Similarly, blood CTRP9 levels were significantly reduced in patients with MI compared with healthy controls. In summary, we demonstrate how CTRP9 acts as an adipokine in vascular endothelial cells to induce angiogenesis and atheroprotective effects, suggesting that it has high potential as a representative biomarker for atherosclerosis and MI.

## RESULTS

## CTRP9 regulates phosphorylation of AMPK and HDAC7 in human umbilical vein endothelial cells
CTRP9 is predominantly secreted by adipocytes and hearts, and it is one of the adipokines that play various roles in energy metabolism and inflammation inhibition ( , ). However, CTRP9 role in vascular endothelial cells is poorly understood. Here, we used human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) to verify the protein modifications in vascular endothelial cells caused by CTRP9 exposure. Treatment of HUVECs with increasing doses of CTRP9 for 15 min led to a significant increase in AMPK phosphorylation ( ). On the basis of these results, CTRP9 (2 μg/ml) was selected for further analysis of AMPK phosphorylation upon 2 to 60 min of exposure. The most significant increase in phosphorylation of AMPK was observed after 15 and 30 min of CTRP9 exposure ( ). Because AMPK is a major signaling molecule, we hypothesized that CTRP9 could induce the activation of other proteins in HUVECs. Therefore, we evaluated the phosphorylation levels of HDAC7, which is a downstream partner of AMPK and is deeply involved in the regulation of angiogenesis. The most significant increase in HDAC7 phosphorylation was observed at CTRP9 (2 μg/ml; ) and upon 15 and 30 min of incubation ( ).
 

## CTRP9-induced phosphorylation of HDAC7 promotes MEF2 activity via nuclear export
HDAC7 is a shuttling protein that, when phosphorylated by a specific stimulus, is exported out of the nucleus by binding to 14-3-3 and consequently accumulates in the cytoplasm ( ). Thus, we evaluated the localization of HDAC7 by immunocytochemistry after CTRP9 exposure. Similar to the positive control of VEGF treatment, HDAC7 was confirmed to be significantly accumulated in the cytoplasm of HUVECs after 4 hours of CTRP9 treatment ( ). It is known that the transcription factor MEF2 previously bound to HDAC7 is released and activated by the nuclear export of HDAC7 ( ). Coimmunoprecipitation assays of HDAC7 and MEF2 in HUVECs confirmed that CTRP9 treatment significantly prevented the binding of these two proteins in a time-dependent manner ( ).
 Next, we performed an MEF2C luciferase activity assay to determine whether MEF2 activity was increased as HDAC7 was translocated and released in the cytoplasm. Thus, bovine aortic endothelial cells (BAECs) were transfected with a 3× MEF2C-Luc vector and treated with VEGF or CTRP9. After 16 hours, MEF2 activity was found to be increased to a level similar to that of the positive control VEGF ( ). Furthermore, chemical inhibition of AMPK and HDAC7 phosphorylation further demonstrated that CTRP9 promoted MEF2 activity in BAECs in an AMPK- and HDAC7-dependent manner ( ).

## CTRP9 induces p38 MAPK phosphorylation, thereby regulating VEGF secretion and MEF2 activity
As one of the major downstream targets of AMPK, p38 MAPK is known to be deeply involved in the expression and secretion of VEGF in skeletal muscle cells ( , ). To verify whether CTRP9-induced AMPK activation could also trigger the phosphorylation of p38 MAPK, we performed a Western blot analysis of HUVECs after exposure to CTRP9. Overall, p38 MAPK phosphorylation was significantly and rapidly promoted by CTRP9, exhibiting a continuous increase from 2 min after CTRP9 treatment, which then decreased after 60 min of incubation ( ). Next, we investigated whether the increased phosphorylation of p38 MAPK by CTRP9 could affect the mRNA expression and secretion of VEGF. After 24 hours of CTRP9 treatment, intracellular VEGF mRNA expression was significantly increased compared with nontreated HUVECs ( ), along with the VEGF extracellular levels ( ).
 Previous studies have shown that p38 MAPK is activated by VEGF to increase MEF2 activity ( ). Therefore, we evaluated next whether p38 MAPK phosphorylation triggered by CTRP9 also regulated MEF2 activity. MEF2 activity was significantly increased after CTRP9 treatment, but cotreatment with a p38 MAPK inhibitor did not increase MEF2 activity compared with CTRP9 alone ( ).

## CTRP9-induced AMPK activation consequently regulates HDAC7 and p38 MAPK phosphorylation
Phosphorylation of class 2 HDACs and p38 MAPK is regulated by activated AMPK ( , ). To ensure that the phosphorylation of HDAC7 and p38 MAPK mediated by CTRP9 was also caused by the upstream kinase AMPK, we cotreated HUVECs with an AMPK inhibitor and CTRP9. When only CTRP9 was present, AMPK, HDAC7, and p38 MAPK were all phosphorylated in the same manner: the positive VEGF control condition; however, when the AMPK inhibitor was present, HDAC7 and p38 MAPK phosphorylation levels were significantly reduced ( ). These results confirm that CTRP9-induced AMPK activation in endothelial cells consequently promotes the phosphorylation of HDAC7 and p38 MAPK.
 

## CTRP9 induces an early angiogenic response in HUVECs
We previously identified various proangiogenic responses triggered by CTRP9-induced phosphorylation of AMPK, HDAC7, and p38 MAPK. Among them, these signals were closely related to the regulation of MEF2 activity. MEF2 is a representative transcription factor involved in angiogenesis and regulates the expression of various proangiogenic genes ( , ). To investigate the proangiogenic effects of CTRP9 in vascular endothelial cells, an in vitro angiogenesis assay was performed using HUVECs after CTRP9 treatment. First, HUVECs were found to have increased migration potential upon CTRP9 exposure ( ). Moreover, an endothelial tube forming assay further showed that the tube organization of HUVECs was significantly increased after CTRP9 treatment compared with control ( ).
 

## Inhibition of AMPK, HDAC7, and p38 MAPK by siRNA impairs CTRP9-induced angiogenesis
Next, we used tailored small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting AMPK, HDAC7, and p38 MAPK to genetically prevent the expression of these signaling proteins in HUVECs and conducted an in vitro angiogenesis assay. Two siRNA variants were prepared for each gene, and the protein level was analyzed by Western blot 24 hours after cell transfection ( ). Subsequent experiments were performed using variant #1, which showed more potent protein inhibition. We transfected HUVECs with control, AMPK, HDAC7, and p38 MAPK siRNAs for 24 hours followed by a cell migration assay. The migration ability of HUVEC was significantly reduced in cells lacking AMPK, HDAC7, and p38 MAPK compared with control cells. Furthermore, cell migration was remarkably decreased upon inhibition of AMPK, HDAC7, and p38 MAPK expression in combination with CTRP9 treatment compared with control siRNA + CTRP9 treatment cells ( ).
 Then, we transfected HUVECs with each siRNA in the same manner but seeded them on a Matrigel-coated culture plate and measured endothelial cell tube formation. In agreement with the previous results, tube formation was significantly decreased upon lack of AMPK, HDAC7, and p38 MAPK compared with control cells, and the effect was further enhanced in the presence of CTRP9 treatment. After treatment with control siRNA + CTRP9, tube formation was quite well formed, whereas after treatment with AMPK, HDAC7, and p38 MAPK siRNA + CTRP9, tube formation was only slightly branched, and almost no capillary tube was formed ( ).

## Reduction of angiogenesis by CTRP9 deficiency was proved in vivo, and CTRP9 treatment has the potential to inhibit the formation of atherosclerotic plaques
To investigate whether CTRP9 could regulate angiogenesis in vivo, CTRP9 KO mice were produced using the CRISPR-Cas9 system ( ), and we performed an in vivo Matrigel plug assay. Growth factor–reduced Matrigel was used, and supplemental vascular growth factors [VEGF, basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), and heparin] were included to induce smooth angiogenesis. Analysis of the Matrigel plug 10 days after injection showed that CTRP9 KO mice had significantly less angiogenesis than CTRP9 WT mice, as evaluated by the hemoglobin (Hb) levels ( ). Moreover, hematoxylin and eosin staining of the Matrigel plugs confirmed these findings ( ). Immunohistochemical analysis of the vascular endothelial cells and the newly formed vessels by CD31 [PECAM1 (Platelet And Endothelial Cell Adhesion Molecule 1)] staining revealed several CD31+ vessels in CTRP9 WT-derived Matrigel plugs, whereas new vessels were hardly detected in CTRP9 KO–derived Matrigel plugs ( ). To effectively observe the detailed vascular network inside the Matrigel plug, we injected fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)–dextran fluorescent dye into each mouse tail vein, and the blood vessels inside the plug were photographed through a fluorescence microscope. In agreement with the previous results, an extensive vessel network was formed in the CTRP9 WT-derived Matrigel plug, whereas it was hardly seen in the CTRP9 KO-derived Matrigel plug ( ).
 We performed QuantSeq 3′mRNA sequencing by directly isolating CTRP9 WT and KO aortic endothelium RNA to confirm the pathway regulated by CTRP9 in the endothelium. Mouse aortic endothelial cell (EC) RNA isolation method and characterization were verified by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) using markers related to each layer of blood vessels (fig. S1). MEF2 has been studied as an atheroprotective gene-regulating endothelial homeostasis in vascular endothelial cells ( ). A significant decrease in the expression of MEF2C was confirmed in CTRP9 KO aortic ECs ( ). Thus, we analyzed the expression of MEF2C using a Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) publicly available dataset (GSE43292), which comprised gene expression data of paired atheroma plaques and macroscopically intact tissues isolated from carotid endarterectomy pieces obtained from 32 hypertensive patients. Overall, MEF2 expression was significantly reduced in atheroma plaques compared with macroscopically intact tissues ( ). In this study, we found that CTRP9 regulates MEF2 activity via AMPK, HDAC7, and p38 MAPK in vascular endothelial cells.
 We performed transcriptomic analysis using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data to confirm the relationship between atherosclerosis and CTRP9. The overall expression analysis of the significant gene (fold 2, P < 0.05) in the sequencing data was represented as a heatmap (fig. S2A). As a result of RNA-seq data analysis, the significant increase in expression of 13 genes in CTRP9 KO aortic EC compared to WT among PCR array gene set for mouse atherosclerosis (QIAGEN, PAMM-038ZA) was represented as a heatmap ( ). In addition, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using RNA-seq data and confirmed a significant increase in the Hallmark_inflammatory response and hypoxia gene set in CTRP9 KO aortic EC. Reactome_smooth muscle contraction gene set was decreased explicitly in KO compared with WT ( ). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) term analysis using sequencing data was performed and shown as a count of gene plots, and a fold enrichment plot top 10 pathways were identified in the KEGG pathway. A significant increase in up-count genes was confirmed in the lipid and atherosclerosis pathways (fig. S2B). In addition, in GO analysis, the top 10 GO terms were identified, and among them, a significant increase in the up-count gene was confirmed in the 0006954 inflammatory response (fig. S2C). The overall analysis of RNA-seq data and analysis of KEGG pathway fold enrichment are indicated in figs. S2 and S3.
Hence, to determine how CTRP9 deficiency affects the development of atherosclerosis, an early pathological condition of MI, we surgically induced atherosclerotic disease in CTRP9 KO mice and evaluated them. It has been clinically established that atherosclerotic plaques are formed especially in areas where disturbed blood flow can be developed ( ). The primary pathophysiological mechanism of atherosclerosis begins with EC dysfunction induced by direct damage such as continuous disturbed blood flow and disruption of the EC lineage ( ). The left carotid artery (LCA) ligation model was used to block the blood flow by ligating the LCA and to induce early atherosclerosis by feeding them a high-fat diet. Because this model intentionally blocks and obstructs blood flow, dysfunction of vascular endothelial cells is more strongly induced as major pathogenesis of early atherosclerosis.
CTRP9 WT and KO mice were fed a high-fat diet for 3 weeks after the ligation surgery and after which were euthanized; blood samples were collected, and LCA and symmetrical right carotid artery (RCA) were separated, fixed in paraffin blocks, and sectioned. Low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein, triglycerides, and cholesterol in the blood of CTRP9 WT and KO mice were measured and compared (fig. S4A). In the surgically ligated LCA, hematoxylin and eosin staining of CTRP9 WT and KO mice showed that the atherosclerotic lesions were significantly increased in the LCA of CTRP9 KO mice ( ). Analysis of the endothelial cell marker CD31 PECAM1 and smooth muscle cell marker alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) in CTRP9 WT and KO LCA samples confirmed smooth muscle cell proliferation, a typical manifestation of atherosclerosis. Extensive proliferation of the media and neointima was also observed in CTRP9 KO LCA samples compared with CTRP9 WT LCA ( ). CTRP9 is an angiogenic adipokine that regulates vascular homeostasis and anti-inflammatory responses and is mainly secreted by adipocytes. We aimed to prove that continuous CTRP9 treatment can inhibit atherosclerotic plaque formation in obese mice induced by a high-fat diet. Hence, we directly compared with CTRP9 KO mice the plaque development using ApoE KO mice, which are widely recognized as cardiovascular/metabolic disease models. After induction of atherosclerosis, continuous intravenous CTRP9 was injected along with a high-fat diet, and the effect of CTRP9 on plaque formation was evaluated. ApoE KO mice and CTRP9 KO mice were surgically induced atherosclerosis, and then, recombinant CTRP9 (0.5 μg/g) was injected tail vein to confirm the improvement of atherosclerosis. In each group, the level of CTRP9 in the blood caused by the injection of recombinant CTRP9 was confirmed by Western blot, respectively (fig. S4B). In ApoE KO mice and CTRP9 KO mice, the formation of severe atherosclerotic plaques due to LCA ligation was verified. However, in the group treated with recombinant CTRP9 in ApoE KO mice and CTRP9 KO mice, a significant decrease in ligated LCA plaques were confirmed (white arrow: ligated LCA; ). Ligated LCA plaques were quantitatively analyzed by representing the plaque-generated area as a percentage of the total LCA area ( ). In this regard, reduction of aortic internal plaques by recombinant CTRP9 treatment was also proved in aorta oil red O staining ( ). Whole aorta plaques, including the aortic arch, were stained with oil red O staining and quantitatively analyzed by representing the area where plaques were generated among the total aorta area as a percentage ( ). Thus, it was confirmed that CTRP9 plays an important role in the atheroprotective response in vivo, which is mediated by MEF2.

## Potential of CTRP9 as a biomarker and therapeutic target for cardiovascular disease
As an adipokine circulating in our body, CTRP9 has been reported to prevent MI via AMPK-dependent and calreticulin-dependent mechanisms ( , , ). We analyzed the expression of CTRP9 and MEF2C in the GSE6580 dataset, which comprised gene expression data in the left ventricle tissue after 3, 5, 7, and 14 days of surgically induced MI in mice ( ). Overall, CTRP9 and MEF2C levels in the left ventricular tissue tended to decrease in a time-dependent manner after MI ( ). In another analysis (GSE138008), the expression of CTRP9 and MEF2 was significantly reduced in the heart of MI-induced mice compared to sham (fig. S4C). In addition, we analyzed the expression of MEF2C in the GSE66360 dataset, which comprised the gene expression patterns in CD146+ circulating endothelial cells of human patients with MI ( ). It was confirmed that the MEF2C expression was specifically reduced in CD146+ circulating endothelial cells of patients with MI compared with healthy controls ( ).
 Because CTRP9 was explicitly decreased in the mouse MI model, MI was surgically induced in the mouse, and a recovery experiment using recombinant CTRP9 was performed. After surgical ligation of the left coronary artery of the mouse, CTRP9 (1 μg/g) was injected into the jugular vein. After 24 hours, the heart was isolated, and a significant decrease in ischemic size was confirmed in MI + CTRP9 treatment compared to MI ( ). We monitored mouse magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to follow the left ventricular contractility of the MI heart and MI + CTRP9–treated hearts. As a result of MRI heart imaging of MI-induced mice and mice treated with MI + CTRP9, a significant recovery of left ventricular end-systolic contractility was observed in MI + CTRP9 (fig. S5). We investigated CTRP9 in the blood of MI-induced mice and sham mice to confirm that MI regulates CTRP9 levels. Circulating CTRP9 levels were specifically decreased by MI ( ).
To investigate the clinical correlation between cardiovascular disease and CTRP9, we first measured circulating CTRP9 levels in patients with potential coronary artery disease (CAD) and healthy controls. Next, patients with potential CAD were determined by classifying those with pathological risk factors in metabolic diseases such as hypertension, diabetes, smoking, hyperlipidemia, and obesity ( ). Among them, patients with four risk factors, including obesity, were selected (table S1). Circulating CTRP9 was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay and was significantly reduced in patients with potential CAD ( ).
Last, we compared the circulating CTRP9 levels in MI patients with those of healthy controls. We divided the patients with MI into MI stages 1 and 2 according to the vessel damage status confirmed by coronary angiography (table S2) and investigated the blood CTRP9 levels. Regardless of the MI stage, CTRP9 was significantly reduced in patients with MI compared with healthy control ( ). Blood CTRP9 levels were analyzed separately according to hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia manifestation, which are complications of MI, but no statistically significant differences were observed (fig. S6, A to C). Moreover, blood CTRP9 levels were also examined according to the presence or absence of hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia in patients with MI and healthy controls (fig. S7, A to C). CTRP9 levels showed a more noteworthy statistical significance in the presence of hypertension and hyperlipidemia but were not affected by the presence or absence of diabetes. Furthermore, blood CTRP9 levels showed a more remarkable statistical significance in males than in females (fig. S7D).
To examine the specificity and sensitivity of blood CTRP9 to predict MI prognosis, we performed an area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUROC) analysis. Comparison of healthy controls versus patients with MI stage 1 and MI stage 2 showed clinically significant specificity and sensitivity [area under curve (AUC): 0.726; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.5970 to 0.8549; ]. Healthy controls versus MI stage 2 analysis exhibited a relatively increased AUROC than MI stage 1 (AUC: 0.7738; 95% CI: 0.6358 to 0.9119) ( ).

## DISCUSSION
Important blood vessels in the human body are surrounded by PVAT, and they interact deeply through a paracrine mechanism ( ). Adipokines are directly transported from the epicardial adipose tissue to the vessel wall through a paracrine mechanism and are directly involved in cardiovascular diseases ( ). Here, we investigated the effect of CTRP9 on vascular endothelial cells, aiming to elucidate its relationship with cardiovascular diseases. Our results showed that CTRP9 increases angiogenesis in vascular endothelial cells via the AMPK-HDAC7-p38 MAPK signaling axis. In particular, CTRP9 induces HDAC7 and p38 MAPK phosphorylation in endothelial cells via AMPK activation, thereby promoting the activation of the proangiogenic transcription factor MEF2 ( to ). MEF2 has been extensively studied as a key regulator of angiogenesis and vascular homeostasis ( , , ). However, MEF2 regulation by CTRP9 in endothelial cells has not yet been studied. We confirmed that MEF2 activity is increased because of HDAC7 phosphorylation through CTRP9-induced AMPK activation ( ), similar to the angiogenic mechanism triggered by VEGF-induced HDAC7 phosphorylation ( ). According to a previous study, p38 MAPK is activated by an AMPK in muscle myoblasts to increase VEGF stability and VEGF secretion ( ). Here, CTRP9 was shown to increase VEGF extracellular secretion and mRNA expression by inducing p38 MAPK phosphorylation via AMPK activation, while p38 MAPK also regulated MEF2 activity ( ). In this regard, a decrease in the P38 MAPK kinase kinase signaling gene set was also confirmed by the aortic RNA-seq of CTRP9 KO (fig. S3D). We confirmed that CTRP9-induced HDAC7 and p38 MAPK phosphorylation and consequently enhanced angiogenesis are regulated by an AMPK-dependent pathway ( ).
Inhibition of AMPK, HDAC7, and p38 MAPK prevented angiogenesis in vitro, suggesting that they are modulators of angiogenesis. Moreover, HUVECs lacking AMPK, HDAC7, and p38 MAPK and exposed to CTRP9 did not show an angiogenic response, further demonstrating that CTRP9-induced angiogenesis occurs via the AMPK-HDAC7-p38 MAPK axis ( ). In addition, CTRP9 deficiency was shown here to inhibit angiogenesis in vivo, with angiogenesis inside the Matrigel plug of CTRP9 WT and KO mice showing a completely different pattern. Compared with CTRP9 WT, the vascular network formation was remarkably impaired in CTRP9 KO mice ( ). Hence, we demonstrated that CTRP9 significantly affects the formation of the overall blood vessel network through an AMPK-dependent signaling pathway.
MEF2 is known as a critical regulator of vascular homeostasis ( ). We confirmed a significant decrease in MEF2C expression in the aortic EC of CTRP9 KO mice ( ). Furthermore, it was verified that the expression of MEF2C was reduced in plaque tissue of atherosclerotic patients compared to adjacent tissue ( ). We noted that CTRP9 not only regulates angiogenesis in the endothelium but also may play a role as a modulator of vascular homeostasis by regulating MEF2 expression. As a result of sequencing data analysis using CTRP9 WT/KO aortic EC RNA, it was confirmed that the expression of 13 genes among the gene sets related to atherosclerosis was explicitly activated in CTRP9 KO ( ). The development of atherosclerosis is known as a vascular disease initiated by endothelial cell instability and inflammatory response ( , ). In addition, hypoxia plays a vital role in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis through specific pathogenic responses that act on endothelial cells, vascular smooth muscle cells, and macrophages ( ). It has been suggested that CTRP9 inhibits oxidized LDL-induced inflammatory response through AMPK activation in macrophages ( ). In addition, CTRP9 has been reported to improve asthma inflammation by inhibiting inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and IL-6 and to ameliorate the inflammatory response of diabetic retinopathy by inhibiting nuclear factor κB activity in db/db mice ( , ).
As a result of GSEA of our mouse aortic RNA-seq data, significant activation of the inflammatory response gene set was confirmed in CTRP9 KO ( ). Significant gene expression of the inflammatory response gene set was represented as a heatmap (fig. S3A). The activation of the hypoxia-related gene set, which plays a major role in the pathogenesis of atherosclerosis, was especially activated in CTRP9 KO, and the smooth muscle contraction gene set was considerably decreased compared to that in CTRP9 WT. Significant gene expression of the hypoxia-related gene set was represented by heatmap (fig. S3B). Activation of a reactive oxygen species pathway gene set that can induce endothelial dysfunction, LDL oxidation, and inflammatory events was identified in CTRP9 KO (fig. S3C).
Overall, transcriptomic analysis suggests that CTRP9 deficiency can cause the pathological environment of atherosclerosis, and this atherogenic environment may be derived from the inhibition of angiogenesis by CTRP9 deficiency. In addition, CTRP9 maintains vascular homeostasis and induces atheroprotective effects through MEF2.
We confirmed the immune response and metabolic phenotype caused by CTRP9 deficiency. A significant increase in neutrophils and monocytes was explicitly observed in CTRP9 KO (fig. S8A). However, no weight change was observed, and no morphological change was found in white adipose tissue (WAT) or brown adipose tissue (BAT; fig. S8, B to E). The mRNA levels of uncoupling protein 1, Cidea, Pgc1a, and Pgc1b, which are significant thermogenesis genes, were confirmed in WAT and BAT of CTRP9 WT/KO, respectively. Cidea and Pgc1b were significantly reduced in WAT of CTRP9 KO, and Cidea expression was decreased in BAT of CTRP9 KO (fig. S9).
We propose that CTRP9 deficiency may induce an atherogenic immune response and weaken the genetic function of MEF2, consequently resulting in atheroprone effects. We observed that mice with surgically induced atherosclerosis and lacking CTRP9 have significantly more atherosclerotic plaques than CTRP9 WT mice ( ).
Atherogenesis begins with the alteration of the endothelium, with immune cells infiltrating the collapsed endothelium, monocytes transforming into macrophages by infiltrating the damaged blood vessels, and foam cell formation due to lipid phagocytosis. At the same time, the proliferation of smooth muscle cells is induced, resulting in plaque formation ( ). The ligated LCA endothelial cell lineage of CTRP9 KO mice collapsed, and extensive smooth muscle layer proliferation was observed. Infiltration of CD45+ cells was also found ( ). Subsequently, the improvement of atherosclerosis by CTRP9 was verified in vivo.
We used ApoE KO mice deficient in ApoE, which is involved in blood lipid metabolism, together with CTRP9 KO mice. After surgical induction of atherosclerosis, intravenous CTRP9 injection showed a significant inhibitory effect on atherosclerotic plaque formation in both CTRP9 and ApoE KO strains ( ). Together, these findings demonstrate that CTRP9 deficiency can promote atherogenesis and that CTRP9 has great potential as a therapeutic target in atherosclerotic disease.
It has been reported that CTRP9 protects the heart from myocardial damage that can lead to MI through AMPK-dependent and calreticulin-dependent mechanisms ( , , ). It has also been reported that post-MI CTRP9 improves atrial inflammation, fibrosis, and atrial fibrillation ( ). Here, we confirmed that CTRP9 and MEF2 expression is decreased in a time-dependent manner in an MI induction mouse model ( ). Moreover, MEF2 expression in circulating endothelial cells of human patients with MI was also found to be decreased compared with that of healthy controls ( ). Therefore, CTRP9 and MEF2 levels may be inhibited by MI, which may, in turn, further support the progression of heart ischemic injury.
We confirmed the preventive effect of significant heart ischemic injury by CTRP9 injection in a recovery experiment using an MI-induced mouse model ( ). In addition, by induction of MI, blood CTRP9 was significantly decreased ( ). The preventive effect of CTRP9 on ischemic injury and the imbalance of CTRP9 caused by MI indicate the value of CTRP9 as a therapeutic target in MI.
We measured the blood CTRP9 level of patients with potential CAD to investigate when CTRP9 imbalance is caused in clinical. Blood CTRP9 levels in patients with potential CAD were significantly decreased compared to healthy controls ( ). CTRP9 has the potential as a biomarker for CAD. Last, we confirmed that the blood CTRP9 levels in patients with MI were markedly decreased compared with those of healthy controls ( ), a feature that was not dependent on MI staging. In particular, the decrease in circulating CTRP9 in patients with MI was further explicitly reduced in males, which is consistent with the presence of the male gender as a CAD risk factor ( ). AUROC analysis revealed that CTRP9 levels could effectively and with high sensitivity identify patients with MI stage 1 or stage 2 ( ). Hence, CTRP9 can represent a valuable therapeutic target and holds great potential as a biomarker for MI.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Materials
Anti-HDAC7 (#33418), anti–phospho HDAC4/5/7 (#3443), anti-AMPK (#2532), anti–phospho AMPK (#2535), anti–phospho p38 MAPK (#4511), and anti-MEF2C (#5030) antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Anti-p38 alpha/beta MAPK (sc-7972), anti-HDAC7 (sc-74563), and anti–β-actin (sc-47778) antibodies were from Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX, USA). Anti-CTRP9 (NBP2-46834) antibody was purchased from Novus Biologicals (Colorado, USA). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated anti-rabbit (111-036-003) and anti-mouse (115-036-003) secondary antibodies were purchased from Jackson ImmunoResearch (West Grove, PA, USA). Recombinant human VEGF 165 (293-VE) was purchased from R&D Systems (Minneapolis, MN, USA). Recombinant human full-length CTRP9 (00081-04-100) was purchased from Aviscera Bioscience (Sunnyvale, CA, USA). Dorsomorphin (AMPK inhibitor), TMP269 (class IIa HDAC inhibitor), and adezmapimod (SB203580, p38 MAPK inhibitor) were purchased from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, TX, USA). Calcein AM dye (C1430) was purchased from Invitrogen (Waltham, MA, USA).

## Cell culture
HUVECs were isolated using collagen I from the human umbilical cord of donated pregnant women and cultured in medium 200 supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum, large vessel endothelial supplement (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/ml). BAECs were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (B304-05; St. Louis, MO, USA) and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Corning, Corning, NY, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum, glucose (4.5 g/liter), l-glutamine, sodium pyruvate, and penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/ml). All cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified chamber containing 5% CO2.

## Western blot analysis
Cells were collected, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and lysed with lysis buffer [25 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 120 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), and 0.5% Triton X-100] supplemented with protease inhibitors (GenDEPOT, Katy, TX, USA) and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). After determining the protein concentration using Bradford Protein Assay Reagent (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), equal amounts of protein were separated on 10% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, electrically transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane, and blocked with 5% skim milk and 5% bovine serum albumin. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight with shaking at 4°C. After washing twice, the membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 hour. Last, the membranes were incubated with WESTSAVE-UP Western blotting substrate (Young in Frontier, Seoul, South Korea), and images were obtained and recorded using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad).

## Indirect immunofluorescence assay
Staining of endogenous HDAC7 in HUVECs was conducted as previously described ( ). Briefly, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min, and blocked in 1% bovine serum albumin and 1% normal goat serum in PBS for 30 min at 37°C. Incubation with the primary antibodies, HDAC7 (rabbit) was performed for 24 hours at 4°C. Bound primary antibodies were identified by incubation with an Alexa Fluor 555–conjugated goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) at room temperature for 2 hours. Cells were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Sigma-Aldrich) and mounted in Dako Floromount-G (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and examined with the EVOS M5000 Imaging System (Invitrogen).

## Coimmunoprecipitation
HUVECs were treated with CTRP9 (2 μg/ml) and incubated for 2 to 6 hours. HUVECs were lysed with lysis buffer [25 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), triton X-100 0.01%, PMSF 1 mM, and 1× phosphatase inhibitor cocktail] and incubated with rotation at 4°C for 1 hour. The whole lysate immunoprecipitation was performed according to the standard coimmunoprecipitation procedure. Briefly, whole lysates were precleared with protein A/G agarose beads (sc-2003, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) at 4°C for 1 hour. Afterward, normal IgG and HDAC7 antibodies (2 μg) were added and incubated with rotation at 4°C for 24 hours. The next day, 20 μl of Protein A/G agarose beads were added and incubated with rotation at 4°C for 1 hour. Protein A/G agarose beads were separated by centrifugation and washed. Precipitated proteins were resolved in 2× SDS buffer, and Western blot was performed. Precipitated proteins prepared using anti-HDAC7 antibody were coimmunoprecipitated with anti-MEF2C antibody ( ).

## Transient transfection
Transient transfection of HUVECs and BAECs was performed with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For luciferase reporter assays, the pGL3-3 × MEF2C-Luc plasmid was purchased from Addgene (Watertown, MA, USA), and SV-40 Renilla-Luc-plasmid was purchased from Promega (Madison, WI, USA). AMPK, HDAC7, and p38-MAPK oligo siRNAs were purchased from Bioneer (Daejun, South Korea). All transient transfections were performed in triplicate, and each independent experiment was repeated three times and quantitatively and qualitatively analyzed.

## Luciferase activity assay
BAECs were seeded and cultured at 2.5 × 104 cells per well in a 12-well plate coated with 1% gelatin. After 24 hours, pGL3-3 × MEF2C-Luc plasmid (0.3 μg per well) was transfected with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen), and Renilla-Luc (0.1 μg per well) was used as the internal control. After 16 hours, CTRP9 (2 μg/ml), VEGF (20 ng/ml), compound C (5 μM), TMP269 (10 μM), and SB203580 (10 μM) were added to the culture medium alone or in combination. After 16 hours of incubation, firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were evaluated according to the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega) protocol. Relative luciferase activity was defined as the ratio of firefly to Renilla luciferase, and the control reading was set to 1.0.

## Real-time qRT-PCR
qRT-PCR was performed according to a standard procedure. Briefly, total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) and performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA (1 μg) was reversely transcribed into cDNA with PrimeScript Reverse Transcriptase (TAKARA, Kusatsu, Japan) and oligodT primers. cDNA (100 ng) was amplified using a standard PCR protocol and TB Green Premix Ex Taq (TAKARA), in an ABI 7500 fast real-time PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The primer sequences of the human VEGF and relative control GAPDH primers were the following: VEGFA (forward: 5′-TTGCCTTGCTGCTCTACCTCCA-3′; reverse: 5′-GATGGCAGTAGCTGCGCTGATA-3′) and GAPDH (forward: 5′-GCACCGTCAAGGCTGAAGAAC-3′; reverse: 5′-TGGTGAAGACGCCAGTGGA-3′). The primer sequences of the mouse Ucp1, Cidea, Pgc1a, and Pgc1β and relative control β-actin primers were the following: Ucp1 (forward: 5′-ACTGCCACACCTCCAGTCATT-3′; reverse: 5′-CTTTGCCTCACTCAGGATTGG-3′), Cidea (forward: 5′-ATCACAACTGGCCTGGTTACG-3′; reverse: 5′-TACTACCCGGTGTCCATTTCT-3′), Pgc1a (forward: 5′-AGCCGTGACCACTGACAACGAG-3′; reverse: 5′-GCTGCATGGTTCTGAGTGCTAAG-3′), Pgc1β (forward: 5′-TCCTGTAAAAGCCCGGAGTAT-3′; reverse: 5′-GCTCTGGTAGGGGCAGTGA-3′), and β-actin (forward: 5′-CATTGCTGACAGGATGCAGAAGG-3′; reverse: 5′-TGCTGGAAGGTGGACAGTGAGG-3′).

## In vitro angiogenesis assay
HUVECs were seeded in six-well plates and cultured for endothelial cell migration assay. After 24 hours, transient transfection of control siRNA, si-AMPK, si-HDAC7, and si-p38 MAPK was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). The next day, a physical gap was created using a scratcher, and CTRP9 (2 μg/ml) was added to the culture medium. After 10 hours, the area in which the cells migrated was measured, the control group was set to 1.0, and comparative quantification was performed between the groups. For the endothelial cell tube forming assay, growth factor–reduced Matrigel (354230, Corning) was used in a 24-well plate, and 100 μl of each well was added and coated. After incubation at 37°C for 30 min, HUVECs transfected with each siRNA were seeded at 3 × 104 cells per well. After incubation for 16 hours, the cells were stained with calcein-AM dye (1 μg/ml) and photographed using EVO M5000 Imaging System (Invitrogen). The tube organization of each well was counted and analyzed by fold increase compared with a control sample.

## Experimental animals
Ctrp9 and ApoE KO mice were generated using previously described conventional CRISPR-Cas9 technology ( ). To maintain the same conditions for all animal experiments other than CTRP9 deletion, in this study, all animals used belonged to the C57BL/6N strain and were males. All mice were raised under controlled appropriate specific pathogen–free conditions at constant temperature (24° ± 1°C) and humidity (50 to 70%) and with 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycles. The animals were housed in individual sterile plastic cages (exhaust ventilated closed-system cage rack), with access to a standard sterile diet (Purina 5057). Drinking water was sterilized through a microfilter, a carbon filter, and a reverse osmosis, with an ultraviolet sterilization device. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with a protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Asan Institute for Life Sciences (2019-12-045)

## In vivo Matrigel plug assay
In vivo Matrigel plug assay was performed using CTRP9 KO and WT mice, according to previously described protocols ( ). Briefly, growth factor–reduced Matrigel was thawed overnight at 4°C and supplemented with VEGF (50 ng/ml), bFGF (20 ng/ml), and heparin (30 U/ml) ( ). Each mouse was anesthetized with isoflurane; their flanks were shaved, and the Matrigel (250 μl) was injected subcutaneously into the flanks. Matrigel incubation in vivo lasted for 10 days. FITC-dextran (250 μl at 15 mg/ml; FD70S, Sigma-Aldrich) was injected into the tail vein, and after euthanasia, the Matrigel plug was isolated. The EVOS M5000 Imaging System (Invitrogen) was used to capture the FITC-dextran fluorescence of the Matrigel plug of five random pictures, and ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/index.html) was used to quantify the fluorescent signal. For Hb measurement, the Matrigel plugs were incubated overnight at 37°C in deionized water. Then, the plug was physically smashed and centrifuged, and the supernatant was used in the QuantiChrom Hemoglobin Assay Kit (BioAssay Systems, Hayward, CA, USA). Hb content was normalized by the weight of the Matrigel plug. The Matrigel plug was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in a paraffin block. The embedded block was cut using a microtome, and then the blood vessels inside the Matrigel plug were visualized by hematoxylin and eosin staining. Moreover, the Matrigel plug slides were immunostained for CD31 (PE-CAM1) assessment, according to previously described immunohistochemical procedures ( ).

## Atherogenic animal models (LCA partial ligation model)
To evaluate the atherosclerosis pathogenesis, an LCA partial ligation model was used, as previously described ( ). Briefly, 8-week-old male mice were anesthetized by 2.5% isoflurane inhalation. The left external and internal carotid arterial branches were ligated with 6-0 silk sutures. After ligation, the mice were fed a high-fat diet (D12336, Research Diets, New Brunswick, NJ, USA) to maximize atherosclerosis development. During the high-fat diet feeding term, recombinant CTRP9 (0.5 μg/g) was injected into the tail vein every 2 days. Three weeks later, the LCA, RCA, whole aorta, and blood samples were collected to verify the atherosclerotic development.

## Oil red O staining of whole aorta
The isolated whole aorta was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and internal plaques were stained with 0.5% oil red O staining solution. Briefly, the fixed aorta was exposed to 60% propylene glycol for 3 min after washing with deionized water (D.I.W.). Then, they were sequentially exposed to 90 to 100% propylene glycol and incubated overnight at room temperature using 0.5% oil red O staining solution. The aorta was sequentially exposed to 80 to 50% propylene glycol, washed with D.I.W., and photographed under a microscope.

## MI models
To induce MI, 10-week-old male C57BL6/J mice underwent permanent ligation of the left anterior descending coronary artery. MI modeling was performed according to the previously described procedure. ( ) Briefly, C57BL/6J mice received anesthesia induced by isoflurane inhalation (3.0%). Deep anesthetized mice (loss of the paw withdrawal reflex response) were intubated into the airways with an endotracheal cannula, and mechanical ventilation equipment was used (VentElite Small Animal Ventilator, Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, Massachusetts, USA). Respiration rate was adapted to 130 respirations/min, with peak pressure maximum at 20 cm H2O. To maintain body temperature, the surgery was operated on a heating pad. During surgery, anesthesia was maintained with 1.5 to 2.0% isoflurane. A fourth intercostal thoracotomy was conducted, and the heart was exposed. The left descending coronary artery below the left atrium was ligated with an 8.0 nonresorbable nylon suture. After left anterior descending coronary artery (LAD) ligation, the fourth intercostal space was sutured over two sites with 6.0 nonresorbable nylon sutures, and the skin was sutured. After that, recombinant CTRP9 (1 μg/g) was injected through the left jugular vein, and the heart was removed 24 hours after to evaluate for ischemic injury.

## MI-induced heart triphenyltetrazolium chloride staining
Staining of MI-induced cardiac tissue was performed according to a previously described procedure. ( ) The separated heart was perfused using a Langendorff apparatus, and Krebs-Henseleit solution was used. To delineate infarcted from viable myocardium, the hearts were perfused with a 1% solution of triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 37°C at a pressure of 60 mmHg (∼5 ml over 5 min). To indicate infarction in the surviving myocardium, it was perfused with 1% TTC solution in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 37°C at a pressure of 60 mmHg (∼5 ml over 5 min). Hearts were frozen at −20°C, sliced six pieces transversely, and photographed after fix in 10% neutral buffered formaldehyde.

## Mouse aortic endothelium QuantSeq 3′ mRNA sequencing
Aortic endothelial cell isolation of CTRP9 WT and KO mice was performed according to a previously described study ( , ). Briefly, dissect the 8-week-old male CTRP9 WT/KO mice to remove the thoracic aorta and wash the aorta separated using cold DPBS. Under the dissecting microscope, a winged infusion set needle was inserted into the aorta and carefully flushed for 10 s using cold DPBS. TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) was prepared in a 5-ml syringe, connected to a winged infusion set needle, carefully flushed into the aorta for about 10 s, stopped for 10 s, and flushed again for 10 s. Then, TRIzol flushing was performed once more for identification of the endothelium, and RNA of the tunica media layer was isolated. Endothelium and tunica media–derived RNA was extracted through manual RNA isolation, and endothelium QuantSeq 3′ mRNA sequencing was performed by e-biogen (Seoul, Korea), a specialized RNA-seq company. QuantSeq 3’ mRNA sequencing is a method of isolating RNA from species in which Poly(A) exists, sequencing the 3′ untranslated region part briefly, and analyzing gene expression. QuantSeq 3′ mRNA sequencing was performed according to the previously described procedure ( ).

## GEO analysis
Three gene expression profiling datasets (GSE43292, GSE6580, GSE138008, and GSE66360) were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information GEO database and analyzed. GSE43292 comprised paired samples from 32 hypertensive patients who underwent carotid endarterectomy and had atheroma plaque tissue (stage 4 and over of the Stary classification) and distant macroscopically intact tissue ( ). GSE6580 consisted of gene expression data from left ventricular tissue (n = 5) from mice collected 3, 5, 7, and 14 days after infarction in a surgically induced MI mouse model ( ). GSE66360 consisted of gene expression data from CD146+ circulating endothelial cells from healthy controls (n = 50) and patients with MI (n = 49) ( ). The expression of CTRP9 and MEF2 in each dataset was analyzed. Statistical group comparisons were performed using Student’s t test.

## Transcriptomic analysis
Transcriptomic functional analysis was conducted using “DAVID (v6.8)” to determine the enriched KEGG and Reactome pathways ( , ). KEGG is a pool of databases covering genomes, biological pathways, diseases, drugs, and chemicals. (http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KEGG). GSEA was conducted as previously described, using non–log2-transformed RNA raw data ( ). The heatmap represents relative differences (log2), displayed by row z score calculations.

## VEGF secretion and serum CTRP9 analysis
After HUVECs were treated with increasing concentrations of CTRP9 (0.5 to 2 μg/ml), the Human VEGF ELISA kit (R&D systems) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions to measure VEGF in the cultured medium. The levels of CTRP9 in the serum were analyzed using the Human CTRP9 ELISA Kit (BioVendor, Brno, Czech Republic) and the Mouse CTRP9 ELISA Kit (cloudclone, Huston, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Clinical specimens
Blood samples were collected from 53 patients with MI and 26 healthy controls recruited at the Asan Medical Center (Seoul, Korea) between 2013 and 2017. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Asan Medical Center (approval ID: 2013-0873). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their families in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The baseline characteristics of the included patients are listed in tables S1 and S2. Patients with MI were divided according to the MI stage. Coronary angiography confirmed the MI stage based on the condition of the main cardiovascular vessels: left main coronary artery, LAD, RCA, and left circumflex artery. If there was one diseased vessel, the patient was classified as having MI stage 1, whereas if more than two diseased vessels were detected, the patient was classified as having MI stage 2.

## Statistical analysis
Each experiment was repeated three times. All data are presented as means ± SEM. Unless otherwise noted, significant numerical differences between the two groups were analyzed using Student’s t test (two-tailed). The prognostic performance of CTRP9 in MI was calculated as the AUROC using the R package pROC (V1.12.1). Differences identified as P < 0.05 were deemed statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 13.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).
